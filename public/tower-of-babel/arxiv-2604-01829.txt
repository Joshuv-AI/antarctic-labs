A Constant-Approximation Distance Labeling Scheme under Polynomially Many Edge Failures
Bernhard Haeupler, Yaowei Long, Antti Roeyskoe, Thatchaphol Saranurak

Abstract
A fault-tolerant distance labeling scheme assigns a label to each vertex and edge of an undirected weighted graph G G with n n vertices so that, for any edge set F F of size | F | ≤ f |F|\leq f , one can approximate the distance between p p and q q in G ∖ F G\setminus F by reading only the labels of F ∪ { p , q } F\cup\{p,q\} . For any k k , we present a deterministic polynomial-time scheme with O ⁡ ( k 4 ) O(k^{4}) approximation and O ~ ​ ( f 4 ​ n 1 / k ) \tilde{O}(f^{4}n^{1/k}) label size. This is the first scheme to achieve a constant approximation while handling any number of edge faults f f , resolving the open problem posed by Dory and Parter [ 39 ] . All previous schemes provided only a linear-in- f f approximation [ 39 , 68 ] . Our labeling scheme directly improves the state of the art in the simpler setting of distance sensitivity oracles . Even for just f = Θ ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) f=\Theta(\log n) faults, all previous oracles either have super-linear query time, linear-in- f f approximation [ 32 ] , or exponentially worse 2 poly ⁡ ( k ) 2^{\mathrm{poly}(k)} approximation dependency in k k [ 57 ] .

1.3
Our Technique

We bridge the theory of
length-constrained expanders

[
58
,
56
,
54
,
57
]

1.

We lift the

setting of labeling schemes. Additionally, we improve their approximation ratio from

\mathrm{poly}(k)

2.

We strengthen the technique from the connectivity labeling scheme of
[
68
]

standard expanders

We briefly explain why the techniques in
[
57
,
68
]
are insufficient for our result. Firstly, we must avoid the two key primitives in
[
57
]
, the
dynamic router
and

subroutines, as they are highly centralized. Furthermore,
[
57
]
inherently pays at least a constant factor in approximation for each of the

approximation.
2
2

2

They also pay
2^{\mathrm{poly}(k)}
due to the close-to-linear time algorithm
[
56

for length-constrained expander decomposition. We easily bypass this since we allow polynomial preprocessing time.
Lastly, length-constrained expanders are inherently more complex than the standard expanders used in

The key step to overcome these obstacles is by introducing a stronger expander hierarchy called the

Expander Hierarchies.

Roughly speaking, an expander hierarchy is a sequence

for each

59

51

are nested, as this seems essential for strong applications such as oblivious routing and tree flow sparsifiers.
3
3

3

The hierarchies in
[
73

67

[
70

68
]
are not nested but are still useful for connectivity oracles and labeling schemes.

Recently, Haeupler el al.
[
54

constructed a

Our Technical Contribution: Nested Length-Constrained Expander Hierarchies.

Our technical contribution is two-fold. First, we show how to utilize a
nested
length-constrained expander hierarchy to obtain our distance labeling scheme, which we will outline in
Section

2

We believe that both the concept of a nested expander hierarchy in the length-constrained setting and our generic construction will likely find broader applications.

2
Overview

In this section, we will provide an overview of our fault-tolerant approximate distance labeling scheme. However, for ease of presentation, our discussion is mostly under the oracle setting, where the data structure is centralized and can be accessed without restrictions. We note that considering the simpler oracle setting does not substantially simplify our algorithm, and thus it is sufficient to illustrate the main intuition of our approach.

In fact, regardless of whether we work under the oracle or labeling scheme setting, our high-level strategy is essentially the same: we aim to limit the number of vertices that need to be touched/accessed when answering a query. More concretely, given a distance query between vertices
p
and
q
under edge failures
F
, we hope to construct a small graph
G^{\mathrm{disc}}
called the
discovered graph
(which includes the touched original vertices and edges, possibly augmented with additional artificial ones), such that
G^{\mathrm{disc}}
approximately preserves the distance between
p
and
q
after failures, i.e.,
\dist_{G^{\mathrm{disc}}}(p,q)\approx\dist_{G\setminus F}(p,q)
, and thus the query can be answered by running a shortest path algorithm on
G^{\mathrm{disc}}
. To achieve a fast query time in the oracle setting, it is necessary that the discovered graph
G^{\mathrm{disc}}
has a small size, bounded by
\mathrm{poly}(f,n^{1/k})
. Moreover, we construct
G^{\mathrm{disc}}
by extracting local information from vertices
p,q
and the failed edges
F
, which makes it possible to store the precomputed data structure in a distributed manner as a labeling scheme.

Our approach to constructing the discovered graph is
expander-based
, following a line of work on expander-based oracles/labeling schemes for edge- and vertex-fault-tolerant connectivity problems (e.g.
[
70
,
68
]
). However, in order to solve the distance problem, we leverage the more powerful notion of
length-constrained expanders
, which enables us to further capture distance-related information. We point out that, when assuming the input graph is a good expander, all the aforementioned expander-based algorithms (including ours) collapse into a simple and unified framework (although the previous algorithms for connectivity do not need to construct the discovered graph explicitly). We will discuss this in
Section

2.1
for better understanding, and it already demonstrates the power of length-constrained expanders in solving fault-tolerant distance problems.

However, extending the simple algorithm from expanders to general graphs entails not only significant technical complications but also deeper fundamental challenges, resulting in an algorithm that is very different from the counterparts for connectivity
[
70
,
68
]
. Interestingly, such challenges become easier to identify from a labeling-scheme perspective, and this is also the reason why we obtain a labeling-scheme result first. To overcome these challenges, one of our technical contributions is the introduction of
nested
length-constrained expander hierarchy, which is crucial for achieving a
\mathrm{poly}(k)
vs.
\mathrm{poly}(f,n^{1/k})
tradeoff between approximation and label size. We will elaborate on this in
Section

2.2
.

Lastly, we note that we compare our approach with: (1) previous
O(kf)
-approximation fault-tolerant distance algorithms
[
32
,
39
]
in
Section

2.1
, and (2) previous expander-based fault-tolerant connectivity algorithms
[
70
,
68
]
at the end of
Section

2.2
.

2.1
The Expander Case

In this warm-up case, we assume the input graph
G=(V,E)
is a length-constrained expander. The precise definition of a length-constrained expander is not very important, and we will only exploit one of its key properties, which we will mention soon. Also, for simplicity, we only consider the single-distance decision version of the problem. That is, we additionally receive a target distance
h
at the beginning, and for each query
\langle p,q,F\rangle
, we want to correctly certify either (FAR)
\dist_{G\setminus F}(p,q)>h
or (CLOSE)
\dist_{G\setminus F}(p,q)\leq h\cdot\alpha
for some approximation factor
\alpha\geq 1
. To solve the original problem, it is sufficient to check different length scales
h
(for example, taking different powers of two as
h
). We first focus on the oracle setting, and will slightly discuss the labeling scheme setting in the last paragraph.

Previous
O(kf)
-Approximate Oracles.

To motivate our algorithm, we first quickly outline the previous
O(kf)
-approximate fault-tolerant distance oracles
[
32
]
, and see where the linear-in-
f
dependency comes from. The oracle is constructed in two steps.

Step 1.
First, we construct a
sparse neighborhood cover

{\cal N}
of
G
with covering radius
h_{\mathrm{cov}}=h
, diameter
h_{\mathrm{diam}}=kh
, and width
\omega=\tilde{O}(n^{1/k})

[
11
]
.

Recall that such a neighborhood cover is a collection of
\omega
many
clusterings

{\cal S}\in{\cal N}
, each of which is a collection of disjoint
clusters

S\subseteq V
. The covering radius is
h_{\mathrm{cov}}
, meaning that for each vertex
v\in V
, its radius-
h_{\mathrm{cov}}
neighborhood
B(v,h_{\mathrm{cov}})=\{v^{\prime}\mid\dist_{G}(v,v^{\prime})\leq h_{\mathrm{cov}}\}
is contained by some cluster
S\in{\cal S}\in{\cal N}
(we write
S\in{\cal N}
for short). The (strong) diameter is
h_{\mathrm{diam}}
, meaning that for each cluster
S\in{\cal N}
, the induced subgraph
G[S]
has diameter
h_{\mathrm{diam}}
.

Step 2.
For each cluster
S\in{\cal N}
, construct a
fault-tolerant connectivity oracle

\textsc{Conn}(S)
for the subgraph
G[S]
. To simplify the analysis, we use a tree-based fault-tolerant connectivity oracle here (see, e.g.,
[
42
]
). The connectivity oracle
\textsc{Conn}(S)
fixes a shortest path tree
T_{S}
of
G[S]
(rooted at an arbitrary vertex) and precomputes certain additional data structures that we omit here. Whenever a connectivity query
\langle p,q,F\rangle
comes, the failed tree edges
F\cap T_{S}
will break the tree
T_{S}
into at most
f+1
components, and the connectivity oracle simply connects these components via the non-failed, non-tree edges (with the aid of the additional data structures).

Figure 1:
A cluster
S
and its tree
T_{S}
(solid tree edges vs. dotted non-tree edges). The two red tree edges failing breaks the cluster into the three very light blue, dotted components.

To answer a distance query
\langle p,q,F\rangle
, we look at the cluster
S_{p}
containing the neighborhood
B(p,h_{\mathrm{cov}})
. If its corresponding connectivity oracle
\textsc{Conn}(S_{p})
indicates that
p
and
q
are connected in
G[S_{p}]\setminus F
, return (CLOSE), otherwise return (FAR).

The correctness of the output (FAR) is easy to see: if
\dist_{G\setminus F}(p,q)\leq h
, then the underlying shortest path must fall inside
G[S_{p}]
(this is guaranteed by the covering radius), and thus
p
and
q
must be connected in
G[S_{p}]\setminus F
. The approximation
O(kf)
comes from the analysis of the output (CLOSE): when
p
and
q
are connected in
G[S_{p}]\setminus F
, we can only certify
\dist_{G\setminus F}(p,q)\leq O(kfh)
, since the underlying path could go through all the components in
T_{S}\setminus F
(there could be
f+1
of them), each of which has a diameter
2h_{\mathrm{diam}}=2kh
.

In other words, the above approach has an approximation factor linear in
f
, since it is essentially still solving a connectivity problem, which loses an additive factor of
2kh
at each component.

The Key Property of Length-Constrained Expanders.

Therefore, to get rid of the
f
factor in the approximation, we will exploit the following key property in
Lemma

2.1
, which holds when the input graph
G
is a length-constrained expander. For a vertex subset
\Gamma\subseteq V
, we let
\deg_{G}(\Gamma)
denote the total degree of vertices in
\Gamma
.

2.1
The key property.

When
G
is a length-constrained expander, there is a value
\tau_{\mathrm{heavy}}=\tilde{O}(f\cdot n^{1/k})
satisfying the following. For any two vertex subsets
\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime}\subseteq V
, if they satisfy that

1.

\deg_{G}(\Gamma),\deg_{G}(\Gamma^{\prime})\geq\tau_{\mathrm{heavy}}
, and

2.

\dist_{G}(u,u^{\prime})\leq h_{\mathrm{diam}}
for all pairs
u,u^{\prime}\in\Gamma\cup\Gamma^{\prime}
,

then regardless of the set
F
of up to
f
edge failures,
\dist_{G\setminus F}(\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime})\leq h_{\mathrm{diam}}\cdot k
, i.e. there exists a path of length at most
h_{\mathrm{diam}}\cdot k
in
G\setminus F
from some
u\in\Gamma
to
u^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{\prime}
.

We now give a high-level explanation of how exploiting
Lemma

2.1
allows us to avoid an additive loss at each component. For this, classify the components of
T_{S_{p}}\setminus F
into
heavy components
and
light components
, where a component
\Gamma
is heavy if
\deg_{G}(\Gamma)\geq\tau_{\mathrm{heavy}}
and light otherwise. Then, we have the following win-win scenario:

For each light component
\Gamma
, it is affordable to touch
all
its incident edges in the query phase (note that there are
\deg_{G}(\Gamma)<\tau_{\mathrm{heavy}}=\tilde{O}(f\cdot n^{1/k})
incident edges), so intuitively there is no additive loss at all when going through a light component.

For the heavy components,
Lemma

2.1
shows that
any pair
of heavy components
\Gamma
and
\Gamma^{\prime}
have
\dist_{G\setminus F}(\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime})\leq h_{\mathrm{diam}}\cdot k
; the components
\Gamma
and
\Gamma^{\prime}
satisfy condition 1 by definition, and satisfy condition 2 as they are contained in the same cluster
S_{p}
of diameter
h_{\mathrm{diam}}
. Therefore, intuitively, when we want to find an approximate shortest path after failures, there is no need to go through more than 2 heavy components: we can just jump from the first heavy component to the last heavy component by paying an extra additive loss of
h_{\mathrm{diam}}\cdot k
. Each of the two heavy components still incurs an additive loss of
2hk
to traverse within, so the total additive loss is
4hk+h_{\mathrm{diam}}\cdot k=O(hk^{2})
.

In summary, we expect a total additive loss of
O(hk^{2})
, and should thus be able to achieve an
O(k^{2})
-approximation.

Our Oracle.

We are ready to provide a formal description of our
O(k^{2})
-approximate fault-tolerant distance oracle (for length-constrained expander graphs).

Proprocessing.
The construction of the oracle is basically the same, which includes the neighborhood cover
{\cal N}
and, for each cluster
S\in{\cal N}
, the shortest path tree
T_{S}
.

The Query Algorithm.
The main difference lies in the query algorithm. Denote again by
S_{p}\in{\cal N}
the cluster containing the neighborhood
B(p,h_{\mathrm{cov}})
of the vertex
p
. We first define the discovered graph

There are three types of edges in

the distance between their furthest two vertices.

Providing the discovered graph

, we return (CLOSE) if
\dist_{G^{\mathrm{disc}}}(p,q)\leq 10hk^{2}
; otherwise, we return (FAR). Note that we can compute
\dist_{G^{\mathrm{disc}}}(p,q)
by running any exact shortest path algorithm on

.

Correctness.
The correctness of the output (CLOSE) is trivial since the discovered graph
G^{\mathrm{disc}}
will never underestimate the distances between vertices in
V\cap V(G^{\mathrm{disc}})
. Thus, we focus on showing the correctness of the output (FAR). Concretely, we want to prove that, if
\dist_{G\setminus F}(p,q)\leq h
, then
\dist_{G^{\mathrm{disc}}}(p,q)\leq 10hk^{2}
and the algorithm will not return (FAR).

Let
P
be the shortest path in
G\setminus F
between
p
and
q
, which has length at most
h
. Recall that
P
must be entirely inside the cluster
S_{p}
by the definition of covering radius. If every
P

P
even appears in
G^{\mathrm{disc}}
because of type-1 edges. Otherwise, let
w_{p}
and
w_{q}
be the first and last
P
-vertices falling in heavy components
\Gamma_{w_{p}}
and
\Gamma_{w_{q}}
respectively. Note that all
P
-edges before
w_{p}
or after

are incident to light components, and thus they appear in
G^{\mathrm{disc}}
because of type-1 edges. Furthermore, observe that
w_{p}
(resp.
w_{q}
) is connected to
\pi(\Gamma_{w_{p}})
(resp.
\pi(\Gamma_{w_{q}})
) via a type-2 edge of length

, and
\pi(\Gamma_{w_{p}})
and
\pi(\Gamma_{w_{q}})
are connected via a type-3 edge of length

. In summary, we have

\dist_{G^{\mathrm{disc}}}(p,q)\leq\mathrm{leng}_{G\setminus F}(P)+4hk+hk^{2}+4hk\leq h+8hk+hk^{2}\leq 10hk^{2}

as desired.

Query Time.
We can easily observe that query time is nearly linear in the size of the discovered graph
G^{\mathrm{disc}}
, and the size of
G^{\mathrm{disc}}
is proportional to the number of type-1 edges, i.e., the number of edges incident to light components. Since each light component has at most
\tau_{\mathrm{heavy}}=\tilde{O}(f\cdot n^{1/k})
incident edges, and there are at most
f+1
light components, the final bound is
\tilde{O}(f^{2}\cdot n^{1/k})
.

The Labeling Scheme.

Lastly, we briefly discuss how to transform our oracle into a labeling scheme. By our discussion above, it is sufficient if, by accessing the labels of
F
, we can extract the following information for each cluster
S
and each component
\Gamma
of
T_{S}\setminus F
: its degree
\deg_{G}(\Gamma)
, and all its incident edges when
\deg_{G}(\Gamma)\leq\tau_{\mathrm{heavy}}
. First, note that we can design labels for each cluster
S
independently, because each vertex appears in at most
\omega=\tilde{O}(n^{1/k})
clusters (by the definition of the width
\omega
) which only incurs a multiplicative
\tilde{O}(n^{1/k})
factor to the final label size.

Fixing a cluster
S
, a labeling scheme that can recover the above information by accessing the labels of
T_{S}\setminus F
has already been shown in
[
68
]
. The high-level idea is to consider the
Euler tour order
of
T_{S}
, and exploit that after failures, components will correspond to unions of intervals of the order, where each such interval is preceded and succeeded by a failed edge. This makes everything straightforward: (1) we can recover the total degree of an interval if the two failed edges delimiting it store their prefix degree-sums in the order, and (2) we can detect up to
\tau_{\mathrm{heavy}}
of the incident edges to an interval if the two failed edges store up to
\tau_{\mathrm{heavy}}
of the nearest edges in the order.

The General Setting

In this subsection, we consider a general input graph

A Top-Down Approach.

Our formal argument in the main body proceeds by a bottom-up induction, but conceptually, our approach is easier to understand in a top-down recursive manner. Indeed, at the top level of the hierarchy, we have that

Therefore, the hard case is when

is

-heavy. To handle this case, we first need to add some extra connections between different levels of the discovered graph

(by further exploiting the key property

To transfer from level

because

and level-
(d-1)

-length

3’.

(more edges between heavy components) For two components

After going down levels from

to

via the extra type-3’ edge between them in the discovered graph, now it suffices to show a good upper bound on the

.

However, before stating the reduced recursive scenario 2, there is one remaining issue, which we need to exploit nestedness to solve: we need to show that the component
\check{\Gamma}_{v}
is
A_{d-1}
-heavy

Exploiting Nestedness.
Indeed, nestedness immediately implies that because
\check{\Gamma}_{v}
is
A_{d}
-heavy, it is also

-heavy as

. We now formalize the reduced scenario 2 as follows.

•

(Recursive Scenario 2’) First, we have a discovered vertex
w
. Next, we have a (possibly undiscovered) vertex

that is inside an
A_{d-1}
-heavy (level-
(d-1)
) component
\check{\Gamma}_{v}
and is incident to an edge of a higher-level cut. Also, there is a path
\check{P}
between

and

in

contained in the cluster
\check{S}_{\check{P}}
that
\check{\Gamma}_{v}
is a component of, that is a subpath of the original witness path

.

The goal for this recursive scenario is that the distance from

to
\pi(\check{\Gamma}_{v})
in the discovered graph is at most
\dist_{G^{\mathrm{disc}}}(w,\pi(\check{\Gamma}_{v}))\leq\mathrm{poly}(k)\cdot h
.

To motivate nestedness, consider what might happen if the component
\check{\Gamma}_{v}
could be
A_{d-1}
-light. Then, the last

-edge

on
\check{P}
before

and both of its endpoints could be undiscovered. We would then encounter the (hypothetical) recursive scenario 3, where the subpath
\check{\check{P}}
of
\check{P}
between the right endpoint of

and

. Furthermore, even accounting for a potential solution to this issue, nestedness is required to obtain an approximation that is polynomial rather than exponential in

. In particular, if
\check{\Gamma}_{v}
was

-light, a recursive scenario 2 could split again into two recursive scenarios of type 2, for up to

at the bottom of the hierarchy.

On the other hand, with this additional guarantee that

lies inside the

-heavy level-
(d-1)
component
\check{\Gamma}_{v}
, when we continue to break down
\check{P}
into subpaths in

, we will again only encounter recursive scenarios 1 and 2, and in particular at most one recursive scenario 2, exactly as in
Figure

.

The Base Cases.

In the discussion above, we intentionally ignore the base cases.

For the first base case, regardless of whether we are in recursive scenario 1 or 2, suppose that the level-
(d-1)
cluster
\check{S}_{\check{P}}
entirely containing
\check{P}
has no failed tree edge, i.e.,
T_{\check{S}_{\check{P}}}
is disjoint from
F
. A good thing about this base case is that
\check{S}_{\check{P}}
has a unique component
\Gamma
that is the whole cluster itself. However, we can no longer obtain the information of
\Gamma
(i.e., heavy/light, incident edges), because (as discussed at the end of
Section

2.1
) we obtain component information of a cluster by accessing its failed tree edges.

Fortunately, in both recursive scenarios 1 and 2, we have a discovered endpoint
w\in\check{S}_{\check{P}}
of
\check{P}
, allowing us to associate the unique-component information with the vertex label of
w
(that we can store along with
w
in edge labels that discover
w
). For vertices discovered from a vertex label, we only recover a very minimal
fingerprint
. The fingerprint of a vertex
v
stores only its position in each cluster containing it, so that the appropriate edges of type 2 incident to
v
in
G^{\mathrm{disc}}
may be added. As no further recursion is needed in this base case, a ’fourth tier’ of labels is not necessary.

The second base case is when the recursion reaches level
d=0
. Then, as the node weighting
A_{0}
is the degree weighting of the graph, we can proceed as in the expander case discussed in
Section

2.1
.

Comparison to Previous Expander-Based FT-Connectivity Algorithms.

After providing an overview of our algorithm, we now highlight again the challenges we overcame, in comparison with previous expander-based fault-tolerant connectivity oracles and labeling schemes
[
70
,
68
]
.

When the input graph is general, solving fault-tolerant connectivity problems via (classic) expander hierarchies admits a straightforward bottom-up merging procedure. For example, in
[
68
]
, the clusters and their corresponding trees are very well-structured: the clusters (from all levels) form a laminar family, and thus there is a single global tree serving as the skeleton of these clusters (i.e., the tree of a cluster is simply a subtree of the global tree). Consequently, the components generated in a FT-connectivity query are also well-structured, which enables a simple bottom-up merging procedure.

In contrast, since we are dealing with a distance problem, we need to employ tools that capture distance information, such as length-constrained expanders and sparse neighborhood covers, which introduce additional technical complications. In particular, we can no longer rely on a single global tree, and therefore, require a novel top-down approach instead. We also face additional difficulties with the aforementioned
fractional cuts
required for length-constrained expander decompositions. Handling these fractional cuts typically requires randomization, but we employ additional techniques to
derandomize
our whole algorithm.

As normal, we denote graphs by

3.1
Length-Constrained Objects

Moving Cuts.

A
h
-length
moving cut
C:E\rightarrow\left\{0,\frac{1}{h},\frac{2}{h},\dots,1\right\}
(or
h
-length cut for short) assigns to each edge

a cut value
C(e)
which is a multiple of
\frac{1}{h}
between zero and one. The size of
C
is defined as
|C|:=\sum_{e}C(e)\cdot u(e)
. The length increase associated with a
h
-length moving cut

is
\ell_{C,h}(e):=h\cdot C(e)
. For a graph
G
and a
h
-length moving cut
C
, we denote by
G-C
the graph

with edge lengths
l_{G-C}:=l_{G}+\ell_{C,h}
. This notation is only used when
h
is clear from context.

Node Weightings.

A
node weighting

A:V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is an assignment of a nonnegative value to each vertex of a graph. For a vertex subset
S\subseteq V
, we write
A(S):=\sum_{v\in S}A(v)
. The size
|A|
of a node weighting
A
is defined as
|A|:=\sum_{v}A(v)=A(V)
. We write
A\preceq A^{\prime}
for two node weightings
A,A^{\prime}
if
A(v)\leq A^{\prime}(v)
for all
v\in V
. The
degree node weighting

\deg_{G}
of a graph
G
assigns the weight of each vertex to equal its capacitated degree, i.e.
\deg_{G}(v):=\sum_{e\in E\text{ incident to $v$}}u(e)
. The
degree node weighting

\deg_{C}
of a cut
C
assigns the weight of each vertex to equal the capacitated cut-value of its incident edges, i.e.
\deg_{C}(v):=\sum_{e\in E\text{ incident to $v$}}u(e)\cdot C(e)
. Note that
\deg_{C}\preceq\deg_{G}
and
|\deg_{C}|=2|C|
for all moving cuts
C
on
G
.

Demands.

A
demand

D:V\times V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
assigns a non-negative demand value
D(u,v)
to each ordered pair of vertices
u,v\in V
. The size
|D|
of a demand is defined as
|D|:=\sum_{u,v\in V}D(u,v)
. The
load

\load(D)
of a demand

is the node weighting that assigns to each vertex a weight equal to the total demand value that vertex is involved in, i.e.
\load(D)(v):=\sum_{u\in V}D(u,v)+D(v,u)
. A demand
D
is called
A
-respecting
for a node weighting
A
if
\load(D)\preceq A
. A demand

is called
h
-length-constrained
(or
h
-length for short) if it assigns positive demand values only to pairs of vertices that are within distance at most
h
, i.e. for all
(u,v)\in\supp(D)
,
\dist_{G}(u,v)\leq h
.

h
-Length Separation.

Let
C
be a
h
-length moving cut. We say a pair of vertices

are
h
-length separated
by

if their distance in

is strictly larger than

, i.e.
\dist_{G-C}(u,v)>h
. For a
h
-length demand
D
, the
h
-length separated demand value

\sep_{h}(C,D)
of

and

is the total demand value between pairs of vertices the cut
h
-separates, i.e.

\sep_{h}(C,D):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,v\in V\\
\dist_{G-C}(u,v)>h\end{subarray}}D(u,v).

h
-Length Sparsity.

The
h
-length sparsity
of a

-length moving cut

with respect to a

is the ratio of

’s size to the
h
-length separated demand value of

and

, i.e.

\spars_{h}(C,D):=|C|/\sep_{h}(C,D).

For a
length slack

, the
(h,s)
-length sparsity
of a

-length moving cut

with respect to a node weighting

is defined as the

(hs)
-length sparsity of

with respect to any

-length

\spars_{h,s}(C,A):=\min_{\text{$A$-respecting, $h$-length $D$}}\spars_{hs}(C,D).

We say a moving cut

is
(h,s)
-length
\phi
-sparse with respect to

.

Length-Constrained Flows.

A

The

Like with (regular) expanders, the length-constrained expansion of a graph (with respect to a node weighting) is tightly dependent on the routability of demands (respecting the node weighting) on the graph.

is bounded by the number of vertices in

\sum_{\text{scales }i}\sum_{\text{levels }j}\sum_{S\in{\cal N}_{j}}|S|\leq O(i_{\max}\cdot d\cdot\omega n)=\tilde{O}(dn^{1+1/s_{\mathrm{nc}}}\log L).

6
Compiling a Distance Oracle for
G\setminus F

While the Thorup-Zwick
(2k-1)
-approximate distance labeling scheme
[
77
]
has labels of size
O(kn^{1/k})
, it only needs
O(k)
work to answer a distance query between two vertices
u
and
v
given (pointers to) the labels of
u
and
v
. Our main result in
1.1
does not have this advantage, taking an amount of work polynomial in the label size.

In this section, we show that this is not an issue in a setting where the edge failures are fixed first, followed by multiple time-critical distance queries in
G\setminus F
. Specifically, we show how to extend
any
fault-tolerant distance labeling scheme so that given only the labels of the failed edges
F
, one can “compile” an approximate distance oracle that can subsequently be used to answer approximate distance queries on
G\setminus F
extremely efficiently in time
O(k+\log f)
.

6.1
.

For every
k,f\geq 1
, there is a deterministic labeling scheme that for an undirected
n
-vertex graph with polynomially bounded edge lengths
\ell(e)\in[1,\mathrm{poly}(n)]
undergoing
f
edge faults, assigns in polynomial time to each edge a label of size
\tilde{O}(f^{4}n^{1/k})
and to each vertex a label of size
\tilde{O}(n^{1/k})
, such that

•

Compilation.
Given only the edge labels of a set
F\subseteq E
of failed edges of size
|F|\leq f
, one can compute in time
\tilde{O}(f^{7}n^{1/k})
a data structure
D
of size
\tilde{O}(f^{2}+fk\cdot n^{1/k})
.

•

Distance Queries.
Given only the data structure
D
and vertex labels of two query vertices
p,q
, one can compute in time
O(k+\log f)
an
O(k^{5})
-approximation of
\dist_{G\setminus F}(p,q)
.

The extension only requires black-box access to the underlying fault-tolerant distance labeling scheme, and we obtain
Theorem

6.1
as a direct corollary of a general template in
Lemma

6.4
.

The idea of the extension is to reduce a query for the approximate distance between
p
and
q
to a query for the approximate distance between two failed edge endpoints
s_{p}
and
s_{q}
, which can then be precomputed in the compilation step. Each step works as follows:

1.

Labels.
For the vertex labels, use Thorup-Zwick vertex labels with parameter
k
. For edge labels, use the fault-tolerant distance labeling scheme’s edge labels, appended with for both of the edge’s endpoint vertices the fault-tolerant scheme’s vertex label of that vertex and the Euler tour ranges of that vertex in each of the cluster-trees in Thorup-Zwick.

2.

Compilation.
Given only the edge labels of the failed edges
F
, let
S
be the set of vertex endpoints of the edges in
F
. Compute an
O(|F|^{2})
-size table of all pairwise approximate distances between the vertices of
S
in
G\setminus F
using the fault-tolerant distance labeling scheme. Then, store in a hash table for each cluster-tree in the Thorup-Zwick distance oracle containing at least one failed edge endpoint, a data structure containing the Euler tour ranges of every failed edge endpoint
s\in S
appearing in that tree.

3.

Distance Queries.
Afterwards, given a query for the approximate distance between
p
and
q
in
G\setminus F
, compute the (implicit) Thorup-Zwick path
P
between
p
and
q

in
G
using the labels of
p
and
q
, and the first and last failed edge endpoints
s_{p},s_{q}\in S
that appear on the path using the labels of
p
and
q
and the compiled data structure
D
.

If no failed edge endpoint appears on the path, return
\mathrm{length}(P)
. Otherwise, return
\mathrm{length}(P)
plus the precomputed approximate distance between
s_{p}
and
s_{q}
in
G\setminus F
.

Note that the prefix of
P
from
p
until
s_{p}
and the suffix of
P
from
s_{q}
to
q
contain no failed edges by definition. The
O(k)
-loss in approximation ratio comes from the fact that the distance in the post-failure graph
G\setminus F
between
s_{p}
and
s_{q}
might be up to
2k
times larger than between
p
and
q
:

\dist_{G\setminus F}(s_{p},s_{q})\leq\mathrm{length}(P)+\dist_{G\setminus F}(p,q)\leq(2k-1)\dist_{G}(p,q)+\dist_{G\setminus F}(p,q)\leq 2k\dist_{G\setminus F}(p,q).

Before proving
Lemma

6.4
, we first provide a brief overview of the Thorup-Zwick approximate distance labeling scheme’s internals in
Section

6.1
, as we cannot quite black box their scheme. Readers familiar with the scheme may skip to

6.1
The Thorup-Zwick Distance Labeling Scheme

Let
G
be an undirected graph with nonnegative edge lengths. For a positive integer parameter
k\geq 1
, the Thorup-Zwick distance oracle structure
[
77
]
consists of a sequence of sets
V=A_{0}\supset A_{1}\supset\dots\supset A_{k}=\emptyset
, with the following related definitions:

•

\mathrm{Bunch}_{i}(u):=\{w\in A_{i}:\dist(u,w)<\min_{w^{\prime}\in A_{i+1}}\dist(u,w^{\prime})\}

for
i\in\{0,1,\dots,k-1\}
,

•

\mathrm{Bunch}(u):=\bigcup_{i}\mathrm{Bunch}_{i}(u)
,

•

\mathrm{pivot}_{i}(u):=\arg\min_{w\in A_{i}\cap\mathrm{Bunch}(u)}\dist(u,w)

for
i\in\{0,1,\dots,k-1\}
,

•

\mathrm{Cluster}(w):=\{u:w\in\mathrm{Bunch}(u)\}
.

Thorup and Zwick
[
77
]
show the following:

6.2
[
77
]
.

There is a deterministic,
\tilde{O}(kmn^{1/k})
-time algorithm that constructs a sequence of sets
V=A_{0}\supset A_{1}\supset\dots\supset A_{k}=\emptyset
and the corresponding bunches, pivots and clusters of vertices, such that the bunch size of each vertex
u
is bounded by
|\mathrm{Bunch}(u)|=O(kn^{1/k}\log n)
.

6.3
[
77
]
.

For any vertex pair
p,q
, let
i
be the minimum index such that at least one of
\mathrm{pivot}_{i}(p)\in\mathrm{Bunch}(q)
or
\mathrm{pivot}_{i}(q)\in\mathrm{Bunch}(p)
holds. WLOG assume the former does, and let
w:=\mathrm{pivot}_{i}(p)
. Then,

\dist(p,q)\leq\dist(p,w)+\dist(w,q)\leq(2k-1)\dist(p,q).

The Thorup-Zwick distance labeling scheme assigns to each vertex
p
a label containing an array of the vertices
\mathrm{pivot}_{i}(p)
, and a hashmap keyed by the bunch’s elements
w\in\mathrm{Bunch}(p)
to
\dist(p,w)
. This takes
O(k+|\mathrm{Bunch}(p)|)=O(kn^{1/k}\log n)
space. To answer a query given the labels of
p
and
q
, one can then iterate over the
k
options for
i
, and check for each in
O(1)
time if
\mathrm{pivot}_{i}(p)\in\mathrm{Bunch}(q)
, and if it does, the value of the right hand side of the equation in
Lemma

6.3
.

The notion of clusters is useful for path reporting. If
p\in\mathrm{Cluster}(w)
and
p^{\prime}
appears on a shortest
(p,w)
-path, then
p^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Cluster}(w)
. Thus, one can take a shortest path tree

rooted at

on the vertices of
\mathrm{Cluster}(w)
, i.e. a tree satisfying that each edge in
T_{w}
appears in
G
, each vertex in
T_{w}
is in
\mathrm{Cluster}(w)
, and the unique path to the root in
T_{w}
from any vertex

in the tree is a shortest
(p,w)
-path. This tree

is called the

of

.

We have
\mathrm{pivot}_{i}(p)\in\mathrm{Bunch}(p)
by definition. Thus, if
i
is the minimum index as in

and WLOG
w=\mathrm{pivot}_{i}(p)\in\mathrm{Bunch}(q)
, then

\dist_{G}(p,q)\leq\dist_{G}(p,w)+\dist_{G}(w,q)=\dist_{T_{w}}(p,q)\leq(2k-1)\dist_{G}(p,q).

Compilation.

Let

\dist_{T_{w}}(p,q)\leq(2k-1)\dist_{G}(p,q)
. Use the entry of the data structure keyed by

•

and

•

\displaystyle\leq(2k-1)\dist_{G}(p,q)

•

\Pr[\sum_{e\in P_{i}}X_{e}=0\mid\forall t\leq T,X_{v_{t}}=x^{*}_{v_{t}}]
can be computed directly. Concretely, if some
e\in P_{i}\cap V_{\leq T}
has
x^{*}_{e}=1
, the probability is
0
. Otherwise, it is
\prod_{e\in P_{i}\setminus V_{\leq T}}(1-\rho_{e})
.

•

\Pr[\sum_{e\in Q_{j}}X_{e}\geq\alpha+1\mid\forall t\leq T,X_{v_{t}}=x^{*}_{v_{t}}]
can be computed via a simple dynamic programming. Concretely, let
\alpha^{\prime}
be the number of
e\in Q_{j}\cap V_{\leq T}
s.t.
x^{*}(e)=1
. We want to compute the probability
\Pr[\sum_{e\in Q_{j}\setminus V_{\leq T}}X_{e}\geq\alpha+1-\alpha^{\prime}]
.

We initialize
f_{0,c}=0
for each
1\leq c\leq\alpha+1-\alpha^{\prime}
and initialize
f_{0,0}=1
. Say
Q_{j}\setminus V_{\leq T}=\{e^{\prime}_{1},e^{\prime}_{2},...,e^{\prime}_{k}\}
. Then for each
1\leq r\leq k
, compute

f_{r,c}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &f_{r-1,c-1}\cdot\rho_{e^{\prime}_{c}}+f_{r-1,c}\cdot(1-\rho_{e^{\prime}_{c}}),\ \text{for }0\leq c\leq\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}\\
&f_{r-1,c-1}\cdot\rho_{e^{\prime}_{c}}+f_{r-1,c},~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\text{for }c=\alpha+1-\alpha^{\prime}\end{aligned}\right..

Obviously, the desired probability is
f_{k,\alpha+1-\alpha^{\prime}}
.

The running time of the whole algorithm is clearly
\mathrm{poly}(n,m,\alpha)
, because we run
O(nm)
DP and each DP takes
O(n\alpha)
time.

∎
