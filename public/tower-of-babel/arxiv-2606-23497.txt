Strong traces for solutions of 2 × 2 2\times 2 nonlinear hyperbolic systems
Luca Talamini

Abstract
. In this paper we show that bounded entropy solutions of genuinely nonlinear 2 × 2 2\times 2 systems of hyperbolic conservation laws admit a strong trace on Lipschitz curves. The main argument relies on a new half-space Liouville-type theorem for isentropic solutions with constant normal traces, providing the first extension to general genuinely nonlinear systems of the strong trace properties available for scalar conservation laws.



1.
Introduction

In this paper we study trace properties of
\mathbf{L}^{\infty}
entropy solutions
\boldsymbol{u}:(0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
to
2\times 2
systems of conservation laws in one space dimension

(1.1)

\partial_{t}\,\boldsymbol{u}(t,x)+\partial_{x}\,f(\boldsymbol{u}(t,x))=0\qquad\text{in $\mathscr{D}^{\prime}_{t,x}$}

where
f:\mathcal{U}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a smooth
C^{2}
flux, and
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a bounded set where
\boldsymbol{u}=(u_{1},u_{2})
takes values.

The study of traces for solutions to conservation laws is a relevant topic. Currently, available results concern only the scalar case. In particular, the seminal paper
[
27
]
establishes for the first time the existence of strong traces for scalar conservation laws under a non-degeneracy condition on the flux, while
[
17
]
extends this result to general fluxes. The main obstruction compared to the scalar case is the intrinsically non-local structure of the kinetic formulations, a fact already noted e.g. in
[
19
]
in the context of the system of isentropic gas dynamics, which has so far prevented the development of a corresponding trace and regularity theory.

Developing notions of strong trace in the setting of
\mathbf{L}^{\infty}
solutions to
2\times 2
systems of conservation laws would be important for several reasons. First, it allows for strong formulations of initial-boundary value problems (see e.g. the discussion in
[
27
]
). Moreover, in
[
13
]
it has been recently shown that the existence of sufficiently strong traces (however, even stronger than the ones considered in the present paper) implies uniqueness of
\mathbf{L}^{\infty}
solutions when compared with small
BV
solutions, where uniqueness is known (see
[
8
]
for the small
BV
case, and
[
3
,
9
]
for a class of small
\mathbf{L}^{\infty}
data). Currently, general trace results for systems are not available, except in the case of the Euler system for
\gamma=3
(see
[
5
,
16
]
), where specific regularizing effects have been identified. We remark that the only available method to construct weak entropy solutions to genuinely nonlinear systems (
1.1
) is the compensated compactness approach
[
15
,
25
,
23
]
. However, this procedure is inherently non-constructive, and as a consequence, no regularity results are currently available for
\mathbf{L}^{\infty}
solutions obtained through this method in the general case.

In this paper, we prove that if the system is strictly hyperbolic and genuinely nonlinear, then
\mathbf{L}^{\infty}
solutions satisfy the same trace properties known in the scalar case, thus providing the first extension of
[
27
]
to systems. In the following, we describe our main results in detail.

1.1.
General setting

Throughout the paper we assume that the system is hyperbolic, that is, for every
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}
, the Jacobian matrix
Df(\boldsymbol{u})
has two distinct and real eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{u})<\lambda_{2}(\boldsymbol{u})
, with corresponding eigenvectors
r_{1}(\boldsymbol{u}),r_{2}(\boldsymbol{u})
. We will denote by
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}
the corresponding left eigenvectors, normalized so that

\ell_{i}(\boldsymbol{u})\cdot r_{j}(\boldsymbol{u})=\delta_{i,j}\qquad\forall\;\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}.

We consider the corresponding
Riemann invariants
change of coordinates
\phi=(\phi_{1},\phi_{2}):\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
, that is defined, at least locally, by

(1.2)

\nabla\phi_{1}(\boldsymbol{u})=\ell_{1}(\boldsymbol{u}),\qquad\nabla\phi_{2}(\boldsymbol{u})=\ell_{2}(\boldsymbol{u})\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}

and we let

\mathcal{W}\doteq(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})(\mathcal{U})\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}.

We assume throughout the paper that this change of variables is globally smooth and invertible on
\mathcal{U}
. Since we consider bounded solutions, here
\mathcal{U}
is assumed to be a rectangle in Riemann invariants, i.e. there holds

(1.3)

\mathcal{W}=[\underaccent{\bar}{w},\bar{w}]\times[\underaccent{\bar}{z},\bar{z}],\qquad\mathcal{U}=(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})^{-1}(\mathcal{W}).

We recall the definition of genuine nonlinear system.

Definition 1.1
.

The eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
is called
genuine nonlinear
(
GNL
) if there is a positive constant
c>0
such that

(1.4)

\nabla\lambda_{i}(\boldsymbol{u})\cdot r_{i}(\boldsymbol{u})>c\qquad\text{for all $\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}$ and for $i=1,2$}.

The system (
1.1
) is
GNL
if both eigenvalues are
GNL
.

A pair of Lipschitz functions
\eta,q:\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is called an
entropy-entropy flux pair
for (
1.1
) if

(1.5)

\nabla\eta(\boldsymbol{u})\cdot\mathrm{D}f(\boldsymbol{u})=\nabla q(\boldsymbol{u})\qquad\text{for almost every $\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}$}.

In the following
\Omega\subset(0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
is an open set.

Definition 1.2

(Entropy solution)
.

We say that
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega,\;\mathcal{U})
is a
weak entropy solution
of (
1.1
) if it satisfies (
1.1
) and for every convex entropy
\eta:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}
there holds

(1.6)

\mu_{\eta}:=\partial_{t}\eta(\boldsymbol{u})+\partial_{x}q(\boldsymbol{u})\leq 0\qquad\text{in $\mathscr{D}^{\prime}_{t,x}$}.

The entropy
\eta:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be uniformly convex if there is a positive
C>0
such that
\nabla^{2}\eta\geq C>0
in
\mathcal{U}
.

Remark 1.3
.

It could be that the system does not admit any convex entropy. However, for most physical systems and for systems satisfying mild conditions on
f
, uniformly convex entropies always exist (see e.g.
[
14
, Chapter 12]
).

Before stating our main result, we first differentiate two concepts of strong traces on Lipschitz curves.

Definition 1.4

(Pointwise traces on Lipschitz curves)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega,\;\mathcal{U})
. We say that
\boldsymbol{u}
admits
strong traces on Lipschitz curves
if, for every
\gamma:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
Lipschitz, there are two Borel functions
\boldsymbol{u}^{-},\boldsymbol{u}^{+}:[0,T]\to\mathcal{U}
, left and right pointwise traces of
\boldsymbol{u}
on
\gamma
, such that for
\mathscr{L}^{1}
-a.e.
t
,

(1.7)

\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{r^{2}}\Bigg(\int_{B_{r}^{-}(t,\gamma(t))}|\boldsymbol{u}(s,y)-\boldsymbol{u}^{-}(t)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s+\int_{B_{r}^{+}(t,\gamma(t))}|\boldsymbol{u}(s,y)-\boldsymbol{u}^{+}(t)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\Bigg)=0

where we set for a.e.
t>0

(1.8)

B_{r}^{\pm}(t,\gamma(t)):=\Big\{(s,y)\in B_{r}(t,\gamma(t))\;\big|\;y\gtrless\gamma(s)\Big\}

where
\vec{n}(t)
is the normal to the curve
\gamma
at time
t
:

\vec{n}(t)=\frac{(-\dot{\gamma}(t),1)}{\sqrt{1+{\dot{\gamma}}^{2}}}.

A stronger notion of trace will also be used.

Definition 1.5

(
\mathbf{L}^{1}
traces on Lipschitz curves)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega,\;\mathcal{U})
. We say that
\boldsymbol{u}
admits
\mathbf{L}^{1}
traces on Lipschitz curves
if, for every
\gamma:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
Lipschitz, there are two Borel functions
\boldsymbol{u}^{-},\boldsymbol{u}^{+}:[0,T]\to\mathcal{U}
, left and right
\mathbf{L}^{1}
-traces of
\boldsymbol{u}
on
\gamma
, such that

(1.9)

\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\esssup_{y\in(0,\delta)}\Bigg(\int_{0}^{T}\big|\boldsymbol{u}(t,\gamma(t)+y)-\boldsymbol{u}^{+}(t)\big|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\big|\boldsymbol{u}(t,\gamma(t)-y)-\boldsymbol{u}^{-}(t)\big|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\Bigg)=0

The first main results of the paper asserts the existence of pointwise strong traces for general
GNL
systems.

Theorem 1.6
.

Assume that the system (
1.1
) is strictly hyperbolic and
GNL
, and that there exists at least one uniformly convex entropy. Let
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega,\;\mathcal{U})
be an entropy solution of (
1.1
). Then
\boldsymbol{u}
admits pointwise traces on Lipschitz curves in the sense of Definition
1.4
.

Theorem
1.6
is the consequence of a more general result, valid for more general
finite entropy solutions
.

Definition 1.7
.

We say that a weak solution of (
1.1
)
\boldsymbol{u}
is a
finite entropy solution
to (
1.1
) if

(1.10)

\mu_{\eta}=\eta(\boldsymbol{u})_{t}+q(\boldsymbol{u})_{x}\in\mathscr{M}(\Omega)

for every entropy-entropy flux pair
(\eta,q)
of class
C^{2}
, where
\mathscr{M}(\Omega)
is the space of Radon measures on
\Omega
.

We also note in passing that the more general notion of finite entropy solution may play an important role in the theory of systems of conservation laws, particularly in connection with physically relevant limits. For instance, viscous approximations of the Euler system are known to converge, a priori, only to finite entropy solutions, which additionally dissipate the convex entropy associated with the energy. Since no additional difficulties arise in proving the main results in this more general framework, it is natural to formulate them in this setting. We refer to
[
11
]
for further details on this topic.

Theorem 1.8
.

Assume that the system (
1.1
) is strictly hyperbolic and
GNL
. Let
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega,\;\mathcal{U})
be a finite entropy solution of (
1.1
). Then
\boldsymbol{u}
admits pointwise traces on Lipschitz curves in the sense of Definition
1.4
.

We recall that entropy solutions are also finite entropy solutions under the assumption of the existence of a uniformly convex entropy, see e.g.
[
24
, Proposition 2.3]
. Therefore one immediately sees how Theorem
1.6
is a consequence of Theorem
1.8
. Notice also that in Theorem
1.8
the existence of the uniformly convex entropy is not required, since this assumption is only used in Theorem
1.6
to deduce that entropy solutions are in particular also finite entropy solutions.

The following theorem shows that the traces are attained in the stronger sense of Definition
1.5
if the eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
satisfy the following assumption of
uniform strict hyperbolicity
:

(1.11)

\sup_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}}\;\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{u})<\inf_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}}\;\lambda_{2}(\boldsymbol{u}).

Theorem 1.9
.

Assume that the system (
1.1
) is hyperbolic and
GNL
, and that the eigenvalues satisfy (
1.11
). Let
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega,\;\mathcal{U})
be an entropy solution of (
1.1
) and assume that there exists a uniformly convex entropy. Then
\boldsymbol{u}
admits
\mathbf{L}^{1}
traces on Lipschitz curves in the sense of Definition
1.5
.

Again the Theorem can be proved for more general finite entropy solutions.

Theorem 1.10
.

Assume that the system (
1.1
) is hyperbolic and
GNL
, and that the eigenvalues satisfy (
1.11
). Let
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega,\;\mathcal{U})
be a finite entropy solution of (
1.1
). Then
\boldsymbol{u}
admits
\mathbf{L}^{1}
traces on Lipschitz curves in the sense of Definition
1.5
.

1.2.
Structure of paper

The paper is structured as follows.

In Section
2
we introduce some preliminaries, in particular a description of the structure of entropies as presented in
[
22
]
.

In Section
3
we discuss weak normal trace properties of finite entropy solutions. This topic is well studied, see e.g.
[
12
]
, and for the sake of completeness here we reformulate and prove these results adapted to best fit our setting.

In Section
4
we prove the existence of Lagrangian representations (i.e. characteristics) for the kinetic equations satisfied by an isentropic solution, i.e. a weak solution to (
1.1
) with vanishing entropy production. In order to make the paper self contained and to easily derive additional useful properties, we derive from scratch the existence of the Lagrangian representation using a method based on level sets of Lipschitz Hamiltonians.

Section
5
is the core of the paper, where we prove a half-space Liouville-type theorem for isentropic solutions in a half-space of the form
\{(t,x)\,|\;x\gtrless\alpha\,t\}
, under the assumption of a constant weak normal trace on the boundary hyperplane, Theorem
5.1
. In particular, we show that such solutions must be constant, and that the constant is uniquely determined by the weak normal trace. This will yield a proof of Theorems
1.6
,
1.8
.

In Section
6
we show how Theorems
1.9
,
1.10
follow from the results of Section
5
, in particular from Theorems
1.6
,
1.8
. The argument relies on soft analytic tools and on a reduction of a certain Young measure to a Dirac mass. It is only at this point that the stronger assumption (
1.11
) comes into play.

2.
Preliminaries and entropies of
2\times 2
systems

We begin by recalling some structural results for the entropies of
2\times 2
systems mainly from
[
22
]
.
It is well known that systems of
2\times 2
conservation laws admit infinite families of entropies. Aim of this section is to describe how all smooth entropies can be obtained as a superposition of “elementary” irregular entropies. These elementary entropies are of two types (associated respectively to the first and second Riemann invariants
w,z
) and solve the entropy equation

(2.1)

\nabla q(\boldsymbol{u})\,-\,\mathrm{D}f(\boldsymbol{u})\,\nabla\eta(\boldsymbol{u})=0\qquad\text{in $\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\mathcal{U})$}.

only distributionally, since they are discontinuous along lines
\{w=\xi\}
and
\{z=\zeta\}
respectively, where
\xi\in(\underline{w},\overline{w})
,
\zeta\in(\underline{z},\overline{z})
are fixed. We recall that a standard computation shows that a smooth function
\eta:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}
is an entropy of the system (i.e. there exists
q
for which (
2.1
) holds) if and only if it satisfies, in the Riemann invariant coordinates,

\eta_{wz}=\frac{g_{z}}{g}\eta_{w}+\frac{h_{w}}{h}\eta_{z}\qquad\text{in $\mathcal{W}$}

where

\displaystyle g(w,z)

\displaystyle=\exp\left[\int_{{\underaccent{\bar}{z}}}^{z}-\frac{\lambda_{1z}(w,y)}{\lambda_{1}(w,y)-\lambda_{2}(w,y)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\right]

\displaystyle h(w,z)

\displaystyle=\exp\left[\int_{{\underaccent{\bar}{w}}}^{w}\frac{\lambda_{2w}(y,z)}{\lambda_{1}(y,z)-\lambda_{2}(y,z)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\right].

In the following we describe the construction of discontinuous entropies related to
w
, the other case will be entirely symmetric.
Let
g(w,z)
be defined by

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\xi\in(\underline{w},\overline{w})
be fixed. We denote by
\boldsymbol{\Theta}[\xi]:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}
the entropy constructed as the unique solution to the Goursat-boundary value problem

(2.2)

\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{wz}=\frac{g_{z}}{g}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{w}+\frac{h_{w}}{h}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{z},&\text{in}\;\mathcal{W}\\
\\
\boldsymbol{\Theta}(w,{\underaccent{\bar}{z}})=1,&\forall\;w\in[{\underaccent{\bar}{w}},\bar{w}]\\
\\
\boldsymbol{\Theta}(\xi,z)=g(\xi,z)&\forall\;z\in[{\underaccent{\bar}{z}},\bar{z}].\end{cases}

The existence of a unique, smooth solution
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
to the above boundary value problem is standard; for a proof of this fact, see e.g.
[
23
, Section 9.3]
. The solution is as smooth as the initial data and the coefficients, and moreover

w,z,\xi\mapsto\boldsymbol{\Theta}[\xi](w,z)

is also smooth as a function of three variables
w,z,\xi
.

We label
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
the entropy flux of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
.
Up to an additive constant in the entropy flux we can assume that (see
[
4
]
or
[
22
]
)

(2.3)

\boldsymbol{\Xi}(\xi,z)=\lambda_{1}(\xi,z)\boldsymbol{\Theta}(\xi,z)\qquad\forall\;z\in[{\underaccent{\bar}{z}},\bar{z}].

We finally define the discontinuous entropies

(2.4)

\boldsymbol{\chi}[\xi]\doteq\boldsymbol{\Theta}[\xi]\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{w\geq\xi\}},\qquad\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}}[\xi]\doteq\boldsymbol{\Theta}[\xi]\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{w\leq\xi\}}

and entropy fluxes

(2.5)

\boldsymbol{\psi}[\xi]\doteq\boldsymbol{\Xi}[\xi]\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{w\geq\xi\}}\qquad\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}[\xi]\doteq\boldsymbol{\Xi}[\xi]\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{w\leq\xi\}}.

The specific choice of the value of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}[\xi]
at the line
\{w=\xi\}
in (
2.2
) makes it possible to have the following:

Theorem 2.2

(
[
22
]
)
.

The functions
\boldsymbol{\chi}[\xi],\boldsymbol{\psi}[\xi]
and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}}[\xi],\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}[\xi]
are entropy-entropy flux pairs, solving (
2.1
) in the sense of distributions.

A symmetric construction can be made for the entropies that can be cut along the second Riemann invariant; for these entropies, for
\zeta\in[{\underaccent{\bar}{z}},\bar{z}]
, we let
\boldsymbol{\upsilon}[\zeta](w,z)
be entropy corresponding to
\boldsymbol{\chi}[\xi](w,z)
, and
\boldsymbol{\varphi}[\zeta](w,z)
the respective entropy flux, corresponding to
\boldsymbol{\psi}[\xi](w,z)
, and similarly for
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}[\zeta]
,
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}[\zeta]
.

Theorem 2.3

(
[
22
]
)
.

Let
\eta,q
be a
C^{k}
entropy-entropy flux pair. Then there holds

(2.6)

\displaystyle\eta(\boldsymbol{u})

\displaystyle\doteq\int_{\underaccent{\bar}{w}}^{\bar{w}}\boldsymbol{\chi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u})\varrho_{1}(\xi)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi+\int_{\underaccent{\bar}{z}}^{\bar{z}}\boldsymbol{\upsilon}[\zeta](\boldsymbol{u})\varrho_{2}(\zeta)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\zeta

\displaystyle q(\boldsymbol{u})

\displaystyle\doteq\int_{\underaccent{\bar}{w}}^{\bar{w}}\boldsymbol{\psi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u})\varrho_{1}(\xi)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi+\int_{\underaccent{\bar}{z}}^{\bar{z}}\boldsymbol{\varphi}[\zeta](\boldsymbol{u})\varrho_{2}(\zeta)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\zeta

where

(2.7)

\varrho_{1}(\xi)\doteq\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi}\eta(\xi,\underaccent{\bar}{z}),\varrho_{2}(\zeta)\doteq\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\zeta}\eta(\underaccent{\bar}{w},\zeta)\in C^{k-1}.

Conversely, if
\varrho_{1},\varrho_{2}\in C^{k-1}
and
\boldsymbol{\Theta}[\cdot](\cdot)\in C^{k}
, then the functions
\eta,q
defined through (
2.6
) are
C^{k}
entropy-entropy flux pairs of the system (
1.1
).

Remark 2.4
.

An analogous result also holds for Lipschitz entropy–entropy flux pairs
(\eta,q)
. In this case, the representation formulas (
2.6
) remain valid with
\varrho_{1},\varrho_{2}\in\mathbf{L}^{\infty}
. Moreover, since the restriction of a Lipschitz function to a line
\{z=\bar{z}\}
or
\{w=\bar{w}\}
is differentiable almost everywhere along that line, the formulas (
2.7
) are still well-defined in this setting.

We conclude with a simple result, see e.g.
[
4
]
.

Proposition 2.5
.

There exists positive
\bar{r},c>0
such that, for every
\xi,w\in[\underaccent{\bar}{w},\bar{w}]
and
z\in[\underaccent{\bar}{z},\bar{z}]
such that
\xi\leq w\leq\xi+\bar{r}
, the following holds:

(1)

Local strict positivity of the entropies:

\boldsymbol{\chi}[\xi](w,z)\geq c>0

(2)

If
\lambda_{1}
is genuinely nonlinear, then we have the monotonicity of the kinetic speed:

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}[\xi](w,z)\geq c>0

where

(2.8)

\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}[\xi](w,z)\doteq\frac{\boldsymbol{\psi}[\xi](w,z)}{\boldsymbol{\chi}[\xi](w,z)}\qquad\forall\;\xi\leq w\leq\xi+\bar{r}.

A completely symmetric statement holds for the entropies
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}}
and for weak entropies related to the second Riemann invariant
z
,
\boldsymbol{\upsilon}[\zeta],\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}[\zeta]
.

As mentioned in the introduction, the main difficulty in the case of systems is the non-locality of the kinetic speed
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}[\xi](w,z)
. In contrast with the scalar case, this quantity depends on the full state
(w,z)
.

Remark 2.6
.

Throughout the paper we assume that
\mathcal{U}
is of the form (
1.3
), because it makes it easy to construct the discontinuous entropies, however as it will be clear, all the results of this paper remain true in more general domains as long as one can make this construction.

3.
Kinetic normal traces

The existence of a weak normal trace for divergence measure fields is a well studied topic, see e.g.
[
12
]
, in the context of measure divergence vector fields. The clear connection with finite entropy solutions is that
X=(\eta(\boldsymbol{u}),q(\boldsymbol{u}))
is a measure divergence field for every smooth entropy-entropy flux pair
\eta,q
. Here we state a version in our setting, formulated in terms of the entropies
\boldsymbol{\chi}[\xi],\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}}[\xi]
of the previous section, which we prove for completeness. The kinetic normal traces
\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\xi),\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}(\xi)
represent the boundary fluxes of the vector fields
X_{\xi}:=(\boldsymbol{\chi}[\xi],\boldsymbol{\psi}[\xi])
and
\widetilde{X}_{\xi}:=(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}}[\xi],\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}[\xi])
. Notice however that these are not, in general measure divergence vector fields. Clearly, a symmetric result will hold for the entropies
\boldsymbol{\upsilon}[\zeta],\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}[\zeta]
related to the second Riemann invariant
z
.

Proposition 3.1

(Kinetic normal traces)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathcal{U})
be a finite entropy solution to (
1.1
). Consider any Lipschitz curve
\gamma:[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
.
Then there exists measurable functions
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\pm}:\mathrm{Graph}\,(\gamma)\times(\underline{w},\overline{w})\to\mathbb{R}
, that we call left and right kinetic normal trace on
\gamma
, such that for every
\varphi\in C^{1}_{c}(\Omega)
and
\varrho\in\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\underline{w},\overline{w})
there holds

(3.1)

\displaystyle\iint_{\Omega^{\pm}}\varphi(t,x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu_{\eta_{\varrho}}

\displaystyle=-\iint_{\Omega^{\pm}}\Bigg(\int\varrho(\xi)(\boldsymbol{\chi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u}),\boldsymbol{\psi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u}))\cdot\nabla\varphi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi\Bigg)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathrm{Graph}\,(\gamma)}\varphi(t,x)\Big(\int\varrho(\xi)\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\pm}(t,x,\xi)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi\Big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathscr{H}^{1}(t,x)

where we recall that
\eta_{\varrho}
is the entropy defined by

(3.2)

\eta_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{u}):=\int_{\underline{w}}^{\overline{w}}\varrho(\xi)\boldsymbol{\chi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi,\qquad\forall\;\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}.

Symmetric results hold for the entropies
{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}
, and we denote by
{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}^{\pm}
the corresponding kinetic normal traces.

Proof.

We prove the statement only in
\Omega^{+}
. Let
\varphi\in C^{1}_{c}(\Omega)
and consider a cutoff function
h^{\varepsilon}
satisfying

(3.3)

h^{\varepsilon}(t,x):=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $x>\gamma(t)+\varepsilon$},\\
0&\text{if $x\leq\gamma(t)$},\end{cases}

(3.4)

h^{\varepsilon}\leq 1,\qquad|\nabla h^{\varepsilon}|\leq\frac{C}{\varepsilon}\quad\text{everywhere,}\quad\partial_{x}h^{\varepsilon}\geq 0.

Consider the Lipschitz entropy
\eta_{\varrho}
as in (
3.2
), with corresponding entropy flux

(3.5)

q_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{u}):=\int_{\underline{w}}^{\overline{w}}\varrho(\xi)\boldsymbol{\psi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi,\qquad\forall\;\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}.

Now we test equation (
1.10
) with
\psi^{\varepsilon}=h^{\varepsilon}\varphi
and use the representation formulas (
3.2
), (
3.5
) to obtain

(3.6)

\displaystyle\iint_{\Omega}\psi^{\varepsilon}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu_{\eta_{\varrho}}

\displaystyle=-\iint_{\Omega}\begin{pmatrix}\eta_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{u})\\[4.0pt]
q_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{u})\end{pmatrix}\cdot\nabla\psi^{\varepsilon}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=-\iint_{\Omega}h^{\varepsilon}\begin{pmatrix}\eta_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{u})\\[4.0pt]

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
\boldsymbol{u}

) in an open set

Proof.

1.
We start by verifying (2), which amounts to notice that for every measurable set

\displaystyle(e_{t})_{\sharp}\,\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\xi}\llcorner\Gamma_{t}(E)

where we denote in the penultimate line

This proves (2).

2.
We now show that for
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\xi}

for a.e.

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}\mathscr{L}^{1}\Big(\big\{t\in I_{\gamma}\;|\;(t,\gamma(t))\in S\big\}\Big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\xi}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\left(\mathscr{L}^{1}\otimes(e_{t})_{\sharp}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\xi}\llcorner\Gamma_{t}\right)(S)=0

3.

We now show that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\nabla\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}[\xi](t,\gamma(t))\cdot(1,\dot{\gamma}(t))=\begin{pmatrix}-\boldsymbol{\psi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u}(t,\gamma(t)))\\[6.0pt]

Lemma 4.8

Proof.

The definition of pushforward of point (2) of the Definition

\displaystyle\int_{A}\boldsymbol{\chi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u}(t,x))f(t,x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma}\Bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t,\gamma(t))\mathbf{1}_{\{\gamma\in A_{t}\}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\Bigg)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\xi}(\gamma).

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{A_{t}}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}f^{\varepsilon}(t,\gamma(t))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\xi}(\gamma)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\boldsymbol{u}
has constant kinetic normal traces

Remark 5.2

Of course, it will a consequence of the results presented in the paper that

Notice that this is not clear at all if the traces are not attained in a strong sense.

We will prove in this section that

, i.e. the first Riemann invariant is a constant function. In order to prove the claim, we only need the kinetic traces corresponding to

and

Also, it is clearly the same to prove the result only in

\Omega^{+}\equiv\Omega:=\Big\{(t,x)\,|\,x>\gamma_{b}(t),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}\Big\},\qquad\gamma_{b}(t):=\vec{n}^{\perp}\cdot t

where here

Let
\bar{r}>0

(5.1)

\boldsymbol{\chi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u}(t,x))>0\quad\text{for all $\xi\in(\overline{w}-\bar{r},\overline{w})$},

(5.2)

We provide a sketch of the proof of Theorem

1.
Proposition
5.3

are technical tools needed to compute balances in regions delimited by curves with good properties. These properties are fulfilled by almost every curve selected by the Lagrangian representations of Section
4

Let

be the essential supremum of the support of
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
.

2.
In Proposition
5.5
we show the easy fact that curves belonging to
\Gamma_{\xi}
representing
\boldsymbol{\chi}[\xi]
, with
\xi>w^{\star}
, cannot reach the boundary curve
\gamma_{b}
. The intuition is clear in that for the range of
\xi>w^{\star}
one has
\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\xi)=0
, therefore there is no boundary flux in the continuity equation (

\gamma_{b}

3.
It is a bit more difficult to show that also curves
\widetilde{\gamma}\in\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\xi}
cannot reach the boundary if
\xi<w^{\sharp}:=\min\{w^{\star},\underline{w}+\bar{r}\}
, and is the content of Proposition

\widetilde{\gamma}\in\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\xi}

at that point of the boundary. By definition of

for all

\hat{\xi}>\xi
. But this, again very informally, would imply that
\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\hat{\xi})=0

t^{r}<\bar{t}
, the proof is entirely symmetric). Since for almost every
t\in I_{\gamma^{r}}
,
(t,\gamma^{r}(t))
is a Lebesgue point of
\boldsymbol{u}
, and therefore of
w
, and
w(t,\gamma^{r}(t))>\xi
by Point (3) of Definition
4.3
, there holds for arbitrarily small
\delta
and possibly a slightly different
\bar{t}
that

(5.26)

\int_{\gamma^{r}(\bar{t})-\delta}^{\gamma^{r}(\bar{t})}\boldsymbol{\chi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u}(\bar{t},x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x>0.

In particular, by (2) of Definition
4.3
, there exists another curve
\gamma^{\ell}\in\Gamma_{\xi}
with
\gamma^{\ell}(\bar{t})<\gamma(\bar{t})
. Moreover, since the curves are ordered (see Remark
4.6
), being level sets of Lipschitz functions,
\gamma^{\ell}
is forced to reach the boundary before
\gamma^{r}
at some
t^{\ell}
:

\gamma^{\ell}(t^{\ell})=\gamma_{b}(t^{\ell}),\qquad t^{\ell}<t_{r}

We can now use Proposition
5.4
to deduce that

(5.27)

\displaystyle\int_{t^{\ell}}^{t^{r}}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\xi)\sqrt{1+\dot{\gamma}_{b}^{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t-\int_{\gamma^{\ell}(\bar{t})}^{\gamma^{r}(\bar{t})}\boldsymbol{\chi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u}(t,x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\bar{t}}^{t^{\ell}}(\boldsymbol{\chi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u}(t,\gamma^{\ell}(t))),\boldsymbol{\psi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u}(t,\gamma^{\ell}(t))))\cdot(-\dot{\gamma}^{\ell}(t),1)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle-\int_{\bar{t}}^{t^{r}}(\boldsymbol{\chi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u}(t,\gamma^{r}(t))),\boldsymbol{\psi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u}(t,\gamma^{r}(t))))\cdot(-\dot{\gamma}^{r}(t),1)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

Since

(\boldsymbol{\chi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u}),\boldsymbol{\psi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u}))=\boldsymbol{\chi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u})(1,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u}))

is parallel to
(1,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u}))
,
we deduce that the right hand side of (
5.27
) is zero, because
\gamma^{\ell},\gamma^{r}
are characteristic curves satisfying (1) of Definition
4.3
:

\dot{\gamma}^{r}(t)=\boldsymbol{\lambda}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u}(t,\gamma^{r}(t)),\qquad\dot{\gamma}^{\ell}(t)=\boldsymbol{\lambda}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u}(t,\gamma^{\ell}(t))\quad\text{for a.e. $t$}.

Therefore proceeding from (
5.27
), we deduce that

\displaystyle 0=\int_{t^{\ell}}^{t^{r}}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\xi)\sqrt{1+\dot{\gamma}_{b}^{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{\gamma^{\ell}(\bar{t})}^{\gamma^{r}(\bar{t})}\boldsymbol{\chi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u}(t,x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x>0

where we recall that the first equality holds because
\xi
is greater then the supremum of the support of
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
, and the last inequality is due to (
5.26
). This is a contradiction, therefore the claim is proved.
∎

Proposition 5.6

\xi<w^{\sharp}:=\min\{w^{\star},\underline{w}+\bar{r}\}

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{\xi}
-a.e.

\widetilde{\gamma}(t)>\gamma_{b}(t)\qquad\forall\;t\in\mathbb{R}.

1.
Again, as above, notice that for

-a.e.
\widetilde{\gamma}\in\Gamma_{\xi}
, the following holds

(1)

(t,\widetilde{\gamma}(t))

\boldsymbol{u}

(2)

w(t,\widetilde{\gamma}(t))\neq\xi

\widetilde{\gamma}^{r}\in\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\xi}

\gamma_{b}
at some later time

\widetilde{\gamma}^{r}(t^{r})=\gamma_{b}(t^{r}).

\boldsymbol{u}

For fixed
\delta>0
small, let then
{\widetilde{\gamma}}^{\ell}\in\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\xi}

\boldsymbol{u}

. Consider the effective supremum of
w

2.
We claim that for every

\hat{\varepsilon}=\min\{(\hat{w}-\xi)/4,\,\bar{r}/4\}

\hat{\Gamma}_{\hat{\xi}}

restricted to the region

according to Definition
4.3
. The existence of the representation is ensured by Proposition
4.4
, as soon as we can prove that

\hat{\xi}\geq\esssup w_{\mid\,\hat{\Omega}}-\bar{r}.

To prove it, notice that by the choice of

\displaystyle\int_{\widetilde{\gamma}^{\ell}(t)}^{\widetilde{\gamma}^{r}(t)}\boldsymbol{\chi}[\bar{\xi}](\boldsymbol{u}(t,x))\,\mathrm{d}x

•

the second and third terms in the right hand sides are zero (there is no flux through the boundaries) because we have

This proves that
\boldsymbol{\chi}[\hat{\xi}]
admits the Lagrangian representation
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\hat{\xi}}
in the domain
\hat{\Omega}
, for every
\hat{\xi}\in(\hat{w}-4\hat{\varepsilon},\hat{w})
.

We proceed with the proof of this step, and consider now
\hat{\xi}_{1}\in(\hat{w}-\hat{\varepsilon},\hat{w})
and
\hat{\xi}_{2}\in(\hat{w}-3\hat{\varepsilon},\hat{w}-2\hat{\varepsilon})

5.25
), so that almost every curve satisfies the corresponding (1),(2). Consider a curve
\hat{\gamma}\in\hat{\Gamma}_{\hat{\xi}_{1}}
and compute the balance for
\boldsymbol{\chi}[\hat{\xi}_{2}]
in a region

\hat{\Delta}:=\{(t,x)\;|\;\hat{\gamma}(t)\leq x\leq\widetilde{\gamma}(t),\qquad\bar{t}\leq t\leq\hat{t}\}.

We deduce

(5.30)

\displaystyle\int_{\hat{\gamma}(\hat{t})}^{\widetilde{\gamma}^{r}(\hat{t})}\boldsymbol{\chi}[\hat{\xi}_{2}](\boldsymbol{u}(t,x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\hat{\gamma}(\bar{t})}^{\widetilde{\gamma}^{r}(\bar{t})}\boldsymbol{\chi}[\hat{\xi}_{2}](\boldsymbol{u}(t,x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\big(\boldsymbol{\chi}[\hat{\xi}_{2}](\boldsymbol{u}(t,\hat{\gamma}(t))),\boldsymbol{\psi}[\hat{\xi}_{2}](\boldsymbol{u}(t,\hat{\gamma}(t)))\big)\cdot\big(-\dot{\hat{\gamma}}(t),1\big)

\displaystyle<-C(\hat{\xi}_{1}-\hat{\xi}_{2})

, and therefore only on the system.
Moreover, we clearly have

(5.31)

\int_{\hat{\gamma}(\hat{t})}^{\widetilde{\gamma}^{r}(\hat{t})}\boldsymbol{\chi}[\hat{\xi}_{2}](\boldsymbol{u}(t,x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\hat{\gamma}(\bar{t})}^{\widetilde{\gamma}^{r}(\bar{t})}\boldsymbol{\chi}[\hat{\xi}_{2}](\boldsymbol{u}(t,x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\geq-(\sup\boldsymbol{\chi})\delta.

Combining (

\hat{\varepsilon}\leq\frac{\sup\boldsymbol{\chi}}{C}\frac{\delta}{\hat{t}-\bar{t}}

Since

.

3.

Next, by taking

\xi<\widehat{w}<w^{\sharp}.

\displaystyle-\int_{\bar{t}}^{t^{r}}(\boldsymbol{\chi}[\hat{\xi}](\boldsymbol{u}(t,\widetilde{\gamma}^{r}(t))),\boldsymbol{\psi}[\hat{\xi}](\boldsymbol{u}(t,\widetilde{\gamma}^{r}(t))))\cdot(-\dot{\widetilde{\gamma}^{r}}(t),1)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathrm{Graph}\,\gamma_{b}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\hat{\xi})\mathbf{1}_{t^{\ell}<t<t^{r}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathscr{H}^{1}(t,x)-\int_{\gamma^{\ell}(\bar{t})}^{\widetilde{\gamma}^{r}(\bar{t})}\boldsymbol{\chi}[\hat{\xi}](\boldsymbol{u}(\bar{t},x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

Since
\hat{\xi}>\hat{w}>w(t,\widetilde{\gamma}^{\ell}(t)),w(t,\gamma(t))
, for a.e.
t
, the left hand side of the equality above is zero.
Moreover, the second term in the right hand side is zero again because

. Therefore we deduced that

for a.e.

\hat{w}<w^{\sharp}\leq w^{\star}=\esssup\Big\{\xi\;\;\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\xi)\neq 0\Big\}.

∎

In principle, it could be that

Corollary 5.7
.

For every
\xi<w^{\sharp}
, there holds
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}(\xi)=0
.

Proof.

Let
\xi<w^{\sharp}
. Then by Proposition
5.6
every
\widetilde{\gamma}\in\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\xi}
satisfies
I_{\gamma}=\mathbb{R}
, and

\widetilde{\gamma}(t)>\gamma_{b}(t)\qquad\forall\;t\in\mathbb{R}.

We fix any
t_{1}<t_{2}
. We use Lemma
4.8
, choosing

f(t,x)=(1,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u}(t,x)))\cdot\vec{n},\qquad A=\Big\{(t,x)\;|\;\gamma_{b}(t)<x<\gamma_{b}(t)+\delta\Big\}

and we compute using the Lagrangian representation
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{\xi}

(5.34)

\displaystyle\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{\gamma_{b}(t)}^{\gamma_{b}(t)+\delta}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u}(t,x)),\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u}(t,x)))\cdot\vec{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{\gamma_{b}(t)}^{\gamma_{b}(t)+\delta}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u}(t,x))(1,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u}(t,x)))\cdot\vec{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\xi}}\Bigg(\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\big(1,\,\dot{\widetilde{\gamma}}(t)\big)\cdot\vec{n}\;\mathbf{1}_{\{\widetilde{\gamma}(t)\in(\gamma_{b}(t),\gamma_{b}(t)+\delta)\}}(\gamma)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\Bigg)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{\xi}(\widetilde{\gamma})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\xi}}\mathsf{F}_{\widetilde{\gamma}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{\xi}(\widetilde{\gamma})

where the penultimate equality holds because

\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u}(t,\widetilde{\gamma}(t))=\dot{\widetilde{\gamma}}(t)\quad\text{for a.e. $t$}

and we have set

\mathsf{F}_{\gamma}:=\Bigg(\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\big(1,\,\dot{\widetilde{\gamma}}(t)\big)\cdot\vec{n}\;\mathbf{1}_{\{\widetilde{\gamma}(t)\in(\gamma_{b}(t),\gamma_{b}(t)+\delta)\}}(\gamma)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\Bigg)

Next we partition the integral as follows:

(5.35)

\displaystyle\int_{\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\xi}}\mathsf{F}_{\widetilde{\gamma}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{\xi}(\widetilde{\gamma})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma^{+}}\mathsf{F}_{\widetilde{\gamma}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{\xi}(\widetilde{\gamma})+\int_{\Gamma^{-}}\mathsf{F}_{\widetilde{\gamma}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{\xi}(\widetilde{\gamma})+\int_{\Gamma^{\star}}\mathsf{F}_{\widetilde{\gamma}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{\xi}(\widetilde{\gamma})+\int_{\Gamma^{m}}\mathsf{F}_{\widetilde{\gamma}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{\xi}(\widetilde{\gamma})

where we set

I_{\gamma}^{t_{1},t_{2}}:=\Big\{t\in I_{\gamma}\cap(t_{1},t_{2})\;|\;\widetilde{\gamma}(t)\in(\gamma_{b}(t),\gamma_{b}(t)+\delta)\Big\}

and

\Gamma^{+}=\{\gamma\;|\;I_{\gamma}^{t_{1},t_{2}}\neq\emptyset,\quad\sup I_{\gamma}^{t_{1},t_{2}}=t_{2},\quad\inf I_{\gamma}^{t_{1},t_{2}}\neq t_{1}\}

\Gamma^{-}=\{\gamma\;|\;I_{\gamma}^{t_{1},t_{2}}\neq\emptyset,\quad\sup I_{\gamma}^{t_{1},t_{2}}\neq t_{2},\quad\inf I_{\gamma}^{t_{1},t_{2}}=t_{1}\}

\Gamma^{\star}=\{\gamma\;|\;I_{\gamma}^{t_{1},t_{2}}\neq\emptyset,\quad\sup I_{\gamma}^{t_{1},t_{2}}=t_{2},\quad\inf I_{\gamma}^{t_{1},t_{2}}=t_{1}\}

\Gamma^{m}=\{\gamma\;|\;I_{\gamma}^{t_{1},t_{2}}\neq\emptyset,\quad\sup I_{\gamma}^{t_{1},t_{2}}\neq t_{2},\quad\inf I_{\gamma}^{t_{1},t_{2}}\neq t_{1}\}

Notice that we have that

(5.36)

\mathsf{F}_{\widetilde{\gamma}}=0\qquad\text{for all $\gamma\in\Gamma^{m}$}

since these are the curves that enter and exit the region
(\gamma_{b},\gamma_{b}+\delta)
. Next we show that the other curves cannot be too much: in fact we have that by (1) of Definition
4.3
there holds

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{\xi}(\Gamma^{+})=\int_{\gamma_{b}(t_{2})}^{\gamma_{b}(t_{2})+\delta}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u}(t,x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\,\leq\,C\delta.

where
C:=\sup\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u})

In the same way,

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{\xi}(\Gamma^{-}),\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{\xi}(\Gamma^{\star})\,\leq\,C\delta

Moreover, we have for
\widetilde{\gamma}\in\Gamma^{+}

\mathsf{F}_{\widetilde{\gamma}}=\int_{I_{\widetilde{\gamma}}^{t_{1},t_{2}}}(1,\dot{\widetilde{\gamma}}(t))\cdot\vec{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=\widetilde{\gamma}(t_{2})-\widetilde{\gamma}(\inf I_{\widetilde{\gamma}}^{t_{1},t_{2}})\,\leq\,\delta

and entirely similar equalities hold for curves in
\Gamma^{-},\Gamma^{\pm}

\mathsf{F}_{\widetilde{\gamma}}\leq\delta\quad\text{for all $\widetilde{\gamma}\in\Gamma^{-},\Gamma^{\pm}$}.

Therefore in total we have

(5.37)

\int_{\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\xi}}\mathsf{F}_{\widetilde{\gamma}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{\xi}(\widetilde{\gamma})\leq\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{\xi}(\Gamma^{+}\cup\Gamma^{-}\cup\Gamma^{\pm})\cdot\delta\,\leq\,3C\delta^{2}.

We finally conclude

(5.38)

\displaystyle\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}(\xi)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{\gamma_{b}(t)}^{\gamma_{b}(t)+\delta}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u}(t,x)),\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u}(t,x)))\cdot\vec{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{\delta}3C\delta^{2}=0

∎

We can now conclude the proof of the Liouville type theorem.

, satisfying the same properties as above, in particular it holds
I_{\widetilde{\gamma}}=\mathbb{R}
by Proposition
5.6
. Consider
\hat{\xi}\in(\underline{w}+\rho,\underline{w}+2\rho)
. We use Proposition
5.4
for
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}}[\hat{\xi}]
in the domain

\Delta(t_{1},t_{2}):=\Big\{(t,x)\in\Omega\;|\;\gamma_{b}(t)\leq x\leq\widetilde{\gamma}(t),\quad t\in(t_{1},t_{2})\Big\}

and in the same way we deduce

(5.42)

\displaystyle\int_{\gamma_{b}(t_{2})}^{\widetilde{\gamma}(t_{2})}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}}[\hat{\xi}](\boldsymbol{u}(t,x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\gamma_{b}(t_{1})}^{\widetilde{\gamma}(t_{1})}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}}[\hat{\xi}](\boldsymbol{u}(t,x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq-C(t_{2}-t_{1})(\hat{\xi}-\xi)+\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}(\hat{\xi})\sqrt{1+\dot{\gamma}_{b}^{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

By Corollary
5.7
, the last term in the right hand side is zero, so we get that for all
t_{2}>t_{1}
:

-\int_{\gamma_{b}(t_{1})}^{\widetilde{\gamma}(t_{1})}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}}[\hat{\xi}](\boldsymbol{u}(t,x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq-C(t_{2}-t_{1})(\hat{\xi}-\xi)

which is a contradiction.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

By Proposition
3.5
and Theorem
5.1
, for almost every
\bar{t}
the limits
\boldsymbol{u}^{\bar{t}}_{\infty}
are constant functions in half spaces

\boldsymbol{u}^{\bar{t}}_{\infty}:=\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{u}^{-}(\bar{t})&\text{if $(t,x)\in\Omega_{\infty}^{-}$}\\
\boldsymbol{u}^{+}(\bar{t})&\text{if $(t,x)\in\Omega_{\infty}^{+}$}\end{cases}

In fact, they are uniquely determined by

\phi_{1}(\boldsymbol{u}^{\pm}(\bar{t}))=\esssup\Big\{\xi\;|\;\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\pm}(\bar{t},\gamma(\bar{t}),\xi)\neq 0\Big\}

\phi_{2}(\boldsymbol{u}^{\pm}(\bar{t}))=\esssup\Big\{\zeta\;|\;\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{\pm}(\bar{t},\gamma(\bar{t}),\zeta)\neq 0\Big\}

where we recall that
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\pm},\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{\pm}
are the kinetic normal traces given by Proposition
3.1
.
Therefore, for almost every
\bar{t}
, the blow up limits in Proposition
3.5
depend only on
\bar{t}
, and not on the particular subsequence. By the compensated compactness (see e.g.
[
23
, Chapter 9]
,
[
26
]
, or
[
4
]
for a summary of the results) the family
\{\boldsymbol{u}_{r}^{\bar{t}}\}_{r>0}
in (
3.21
) is strongly locally compact in
\mathbf{L}^{1}
. By the above discussion, every subsequence converges to the same (constant) limit, and therefore we obtain (
1.7
):

(5.43)

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r^{2}}\Bigg(\int_{B_{r}^{-}(t,\gamma(t))}|\boldsymbol{u}(s,y)-\boldsymbol{u}^{-}(t)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s+\int_{B_{r}^{+}(t,\gamma(t))}|\boldsymbol{u}(s,y)-\boldsymbol{u}^{+}(t)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\int_{B_{1}}|\boldsymbol{u}_{r}^{\bar{t}}(t,x)-\boldsymbol{u}^{\bar{t}}_{\infty}|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=0

∎

6.
From pointwise traces to
\mathbf{L}^{1}
traces

In this final section we discuss the equivalence with some other notions of trace, in particular we prove Theorems
1.9
and
1.10
. As a first consequence of Theorem
1.6
we have the following, whose proof is quite standard once one knows Theorem
1.6
.

Proposition 6.1
.

Assume that the system (
1.1
) is strictly hyperbolic and
GNL
. Let
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbf{L}^{\infty}((0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R})
be a finite entropy solution of (
1.1
). Let
\gamma:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}
be a Lipschitz curve. Then for every
0\leq t_{1}<t_{2}
there holds

(6.1)

\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\fint_{\gamma(t)}^{\gamma(t)+\delta}\big|\boldsymbol{u}(t,x)-\boldsymbol{u}^{+}(t)\big|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=0

(6.2)

\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\fint_{\gamma(t)-\delta}^{\gamma(t)}\big|\boldsymbol{u}(t,x)-\boldsymbol{u}^{-}(t)\big|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=0

where
\boldsymbol{u}^{-},\boldsymbol{u}^{+}
are the traces given by Theorem
1.6
.

Proof.

We prove (
6.1
), the proof of (
6.2
) being exactly the same. First, notice that by Theorem
1.6
we have

(6.3)

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}|\boldsymbol{u}^{+}(\bar{t})-\boldsymbol{u}(\bar{t}+rs,\gamma(\bar{t}+rs)+ry)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\iint_{D_{r}}|\boldsymbol{u}^{+}(\bar{t})-\boldsymbol{u}(\bar{t}+r\tau,\gamma(\bar{t})+rz)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{r^{2}}\iint_{B_{\kappa r}^{+}(\bar{t},\gamma(\bar{t}))}|\boldsymbol{u}^{+}(\bar{t})-\boldsymbol{u}(s,y)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=0\quad\text{for a.e. $\bar{t}\in(t_{1},t_{2})$}

where we used (
1.7
) for the last equality. Here in the second line we used the measure preserving change of variables
(\tau,z)=g(s,y)

\tau=s,\qquad z=\frac{1}{r}\Big(\gamma(\bar{t}+rs)-\gamma(\bar{t})+ry\Big)

with
D_{r}
being the corresponding domain of integration

D_{r}:=\Big\{(\tau,z)\;|\;\tau=s,z=\frac{1}{r}\Big(\gamma(\bar{t}+rs)-\gamma(\bar{t})+ry\Big)\quad\text{for some $(s,y)\in(-1,1)\times(0,1)$}\Big\}

while the inequality follows from the fact that, since
\gamma
is a Lipschitz curve, by choosing a constant
\kappa
big enough, we can achieve

D_{r}\subset g(B^{+}_{\kappa r})\qquad\text{for all $\bar{t}\in(t_{1},t_{2})$ and for all $r>0$}.

Let
\omega(\cdot)
be the
\mathbf{L}^{1}
modulus of continuity of
\boldsymbol{u}^{+}
in
(t_{1},t_{2})
. Then we obtain, using Fubini,

(6.4)

\displaystyle\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}

\displaystyle\Bigg(\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}|\boldsymbol{u}^{+}(\bar{t})-\boldsymbol{u}(\bar{t}+rs,\gamma(\bar{t}+rs)+ry)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\Bigg)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}\Bigg(\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{0}^{1}|\boldsymbol{u}^{+}(\bar{t})-\boldsymbol{u}(\bar{t}+rs,\gamma(\bar{t}+rs)+ry)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{t}\Bigg)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\geq\int_{-1}^{1}\Bigg(\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{0}^{1}|\boldsymbol{u}^{+}(\bar{t}+rs)-\boldsymbol{u}(\bar{t}+rs,\gamma(\bar{t}+rs)+ry)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{t}\Bigg)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s-2\omega(rs)

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}\Bigg(\int_{t_{1}+rs}^{t_{2}+rs}\int_{0}^{1}|\boldsymbol{u}^{+}(\bar{t})-\boldsymbol{u}(\bar{t},\gamma(\bar{t})+ry)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{t}\Bigg)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s-2\omega(rs)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{-1}^{1}\Bigg(\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{0}^{1}|\boldsymbol{u}^{+}(\bar{t})-\boldsymbol{u}(\bar{t},\gamma(\bar{t})+ry)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{t}\Bigg)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s-2\omega(rs)-4r\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=2\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\fint_{0}^{r}|\boldsymbol{u}^{+}(\bar{t})-\boldsymbol{u}(\bar{t},\gamma(\bar{t})+z)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{t}-2\omega(rs)-4r\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{\infty}.

Taking the limit in (
6.4
) we therefore obtain

(6.5)

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\fint_{0}^{r}|\boldsymbol{u}^{+}(\bar{t})-\boldsymbol{u}(\bar{t},\gamma(\bar{t})+z)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{t}

\displaystyle=\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\Bigg(\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}|\boldsymbol{u}^{+}(\bar{t})-\boldsymbol{u}(\bar{t}+rs,\gamma(\bar{t}+rs)+ry)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\Bigg)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\Bigg(\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}|\boldsymbol{u}^{+}(\bar{t})-\boldsymbol{u}(\bar{t}+rs,\gamma(\bar{t}+rs)+ry)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\Bigg)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{t}=0

where the penultimate and last equalities follow respectively by the dominated convergence theorem and (
6.3
), and this concludes the proof.
∎

We next prove Theorem
1.10
.

Proof of Theorem
1.10
.

Again, we can prove the theorem only for
\boldsymbol{u}^{+}
, since the proof for
\boldsymbol{u}^{-}
is entirely symmetric.

1.
In this first step, we prove the following preliminary formula for the weak trace
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{+}
: for almost every
t
there holds

(6.6)

\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{+}(t,\xi)=\Big(\boldsymbol{\chi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u}^{+}(t)),\boldsymbol{\psi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u}^{+}(t))\Big)\cdot\vec{n}(t)\qquad\text{for a.e. $\xi$}

where we set for simplicity of notation
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{+}(t,\xi)\equiv\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{+}(t,\gamma(t),\xi)
.
Consider any entropy-entropy flux pair of the form

\eta_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{u}):=\int_{\underline{w}}^{\overline{w}}\varrho(\xi)\boldsymbol{\chi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi,\quad q_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{u}):=\int_{\underline{w}}^{\overline{w}}\varrho(\xi)\boldsymbol{\psi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi.

From (
6.1
) we readily deduce the existence of a subsequence
\{\delta_{n}\}
such that

(6.7)

\boldsymbol{u}(\cdot,\gamma(\cdot)+\delta_{n})\longrightarrow\boldsymbol{u}^{+}(\cdot)\qquad\text{in $\mathbf{L}^{1}(0,T)$ as $n\to+\infty$.}

Since by the weak normal trace properties of Section
3
we have that for almost every
\xi
there holds

\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{+}(\cdot,\xi):=\text{weak}^{\ast}-\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\Big(\boldsymbol{\chi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u}(\cdot,\gamma(\cdot)+\delta),\boldsymbol{\psi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u}(\cdot,\gamma(\cdot)+\delta))\Big)\cdot\vec{n}(\cdot)

multiplying by
\varrho(\xi)
and integrating in
\xi
we obtain

(6.8)

\displaystyle\int\varrho(\xi)\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{+}(\cdot,\xi)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi

\displaystyle=\text{weak}^{\ast}-\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\int\varrho(\xi)\Big(\boldsymbol{\chi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u}(\cdot,\gamma(\cdot)+\delta),\boldsymbol{\psi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u}(\cdot,\gamma(\cdot)+\delta))\Big)\cdot\vec{n}(\cdot)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi

\displaystyle=\text{weak}^{\ast}-\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}(\eta_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{u}(\cdot,\gamma(\cdot)+\delta),q_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{u}(\cdot,\gamma(\cdot)+\delta))\cdot\vec{n}(\cdot)

Here of course the limits are taken up to a negligible set of
\delta
.
Computing the limit along the sequence
\delta_{n}
allows to pass the limit inside the nonlinear functions by (
6.7
), and therefore we obtain

(6.9)

\displaystyle\int\varrho(\xi)\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{+}(\cdot,\xi)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi

\displaystyle=\text{weak}^{\ast}-\lim_{n\to+\infty}(\eta_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{u}(\cdot,\gamma(\cdot)+\delta_{n})),q_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{u}(\cdot,\gamma(\cdot)+\delta_{n}))\cdot\vec{n}(\cdot)

\displaystyle=(\eta_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{u}^{+}(\cdot)),q_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{u}^{+}(\cdot))\cdot\vec{n}(\cdot).

Take now any other subsequence
\widetilde{\delta}_{n}
for which

\boldsymbol{u}(\cdot,\gamma(\cdot)+\widetilde{\delta}_{n})\longrightarrow\alpha_{(\cdot)}\in\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{U})\qquad\text{in the sense of Young measures.}

Computing the weak
∗
limit above along this subsequence we obtain in the same way

(6.10)

\displaystyle\int\varrho(\xi)\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{+}(\cdot,\xi)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi

\displaystyle=\text{weak}^{\ast}-\lim_{n\to+\infty}(\eta_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{u}(\cdot,\gamma(\cdot)+\widetilde{\delta}_{n}),q_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{u}(\cdot,\gamma(\cdot)+\widetilde{\delta}_{n}))\cdot\vec{n}(\cdot)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{U}}\Big(\eta_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{q}),\boldsymbol{q}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{q})\Big)\cdot\vec{n}(\cdot)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha_{(\cdot)}(\boldsymbol{q}).

Therefore from (
6.9
), (
6.10
), and the definition of
\eta_{\varrho},q_{\varrho}
, we deduce that for every
\varrho\in C^{0}
there holds for almost every
t
:

(6.11)

\int\varrho(\xi)(\boldsymbol{\chi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u}^{+}(t)),\boldsymbol{\psi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u}^{+}(t)))\cdot\vec{n}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi=\int\varrho(\xi)\int_{\mathcal{U}}\Big(\boldsymbol{\chi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{q}),\boldsymbol{\psi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{q})\Big)\cdot\vec{n}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha_{t}(\boldsymbol{q}).

We then deduce that for a.e.
t,\xi\in(t_{1},t_{2})\times(\underline{w},\overline{w})
:

(6.12)

\Big(\boldsymbol{\chi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u}^{+}(t)),\boldsymbol{\psi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u}^{+}(t))\Big)\cdot\vec{n}(t)=\int_{\mathcal{U}}\Big(\boldsymbol{\chi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{q}),\boldsymbol{\psi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{q})\Big)\cdot\vec{n}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha_{t}(\boldsymbol{q}).

Clearly, the same relation holds, with the same proof, for the epigraph entropies
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}}[\xi]
for a.e.
t,\xi\in(t_{1},t_{2})\times(\underline{w},\overline{w})

(6.13)

\Big(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u}^{+}(t)),\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}[\xi](\boldsymbol{u}^{+}(t))\Big)\cdot\vec{n}(t)=\int_{\mathcal{U}}\Big(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}}[\xi](\boldsymbol{q}),\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}[\xi](\boldsymbol{q})\Big)\cdot\vec{n}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha_{t}(\boldsymbol{q}).

Moreover, the same holds for the entropies related to the second Riemann invariant,
\boldsymbol{\upsilon}[\zeta],\boldsymbol{\varphi}[\zeta]
.

2.
Next, by Step
1.
, for almost every
t\in(t_{1},t_{2})
,
\alpha_{t}
is a probability measure satisfying the assumptions of Lemma
6.2
(which is proved after the end of this proof) with
\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}=\boldsymbol{u}^{+}(t)
. Therefore by Lemma
6.2
we deduce

\alpha_{t}=\delta_{\boldsymbol{u}^{+}(t)}\qquad\text{for a.e. $t\in(t_{1},t_{2})$}.

But this readily implies that there holds the strong convergence

\boldsymbol{u}(\cdot,\gamma(\cdot)+\widetilde{\delta}_{n})\longrightarrow\boldsymbol{u}^{+}(\cdot)\qquad\text{strongly in $\mathbf{L}^{1}(t_{1},t_{2})$}.

Since this holds for every subsequence
\widetilde{\delta}_{n}\to 0^{+}
, this concludes the proof of Theorem
1.10
.
∎

We conclude by stating and proving the Lemma used in Step
2.
of the proof of Theorem
1.10
.

Lemma 6.2
.

Assume that the system (
1.1
) is
GNL
and that it satisfies (
1.11
). Let
\alpha\in\mathscr{P}(\mathcal{U})
be a probability measure,
\vec{n}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
of the form
\vec{n}=(\vec{n}_{t},\vec{n}_{x})
with
\vec{n}_{x}\neq 0
,
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathcal{U}
a fixed point. Assume that for almost every

\xi,\zeta
there holds that

(6.14)

\Big(\boldsymbol{\chi}[\xi](\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}),\boldsymbol{\psi}[\xi](\bar{\boldsymbol{u}})\Big)\cdot\vec{n}=\int_{\mathcal{U}}\Big(\boldsymbol{\chi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{q}),\boldsymbol{\psi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{q})\Big)\cdot\vec{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha(\boldsymbol{q}).

(6.15)

\Big(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}}[\xi](\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}),\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}[\xi](\bar{\boldsymbol{u}})\Big)\cdot\vec{n}=\int_{\mathcal{U}}\Big(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}}[\xi](\boldsymbol{q}),\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}[\xi](\boldsymbol{q})\Big)\cdot\vec{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha(\boldsymbol{q}).

(6.16)

\Big(\boldsymbol{\upsilon}[\zeta](\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}),\boldsymbol{\varphi}[\zeta](\bar{\boldsymbol{u}})\Big)\cdot\vec{n}=\int_{\mathcal{U}}\Big(\boldsymbol{\upsilon}[\zeta](\boldsymbol{q}),\boldsymbol{\varphi}[\zeta](\boldsymbol{q})\Big)\cdot\vec{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha(\boldsymbol{q}).

(6.17)

\Big(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}[\zeta](\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}),\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}[\zeta](\bar{\boldsymbol{u}})\Big)\cdot\vec{n}=\int_{\mathcal{U}}\Big(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}[\zeta](\boldsymbol{q}),\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}[\zeta](\boldsymbol{q})\Big)\cdot\vec{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha(\boldsymbol{q}).

Then
\alpha=\delta_{\bar{u}}
.

Remark 6.3
.

For our purposes we only need the version of this Lemma where
\vec{n}=(\vec{n}_{t},\vec{n}_{x})
with
\vec{n}_{x}\neq 0
, since the version with
\vec{n}_{x}=0
corresponds to traces on an hyperplane of the form
\{t=\bar{t}\}
. However the version with
\vec{n}_{x}=0
is actually way easier and can be found for example in
[
24
]
, where the Lemma is proved in this particular case without the assumption
1.11
.

Proof of Lemma
6.2
.

1.
Let
\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}=(\bar{w},\bar{z})\in\mathcal{U}
be the Riemann coordinates of the point
\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}
. Notice that, since by definition of
\boldsymbol{\chi},\boldsymbol{\psi}
, one has that

\boldsymbol{\chi}[\xi](\bar{w},\bar{z})=0,\quad\boldsymbol{\psi}[\xi](\bar{w},\bar{z})=0\qquad\text{for all}\;\xi>\bar{w}

and

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}}[\xi](\bar{w},\bar{z})=0,\quad\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}[\xi](\bar{w},\bar{z})=0\qquad\text{for all}\;\xi<\bar{w}

equations (
6.14
), (
6.15
) actually imply that

(6.18)

\int_{\mathcal{U}}\Big(\boldsymbol{\chi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{q}),\boldsymbol{\psi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{q})\Big)\cdot\vec{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha(\boldsymbol{q})=0\qquad\text{for all}\;\xi>\bar{w}

(6.19)

\int_{\mathcal{U}}\Big(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}}[\xi](\boldsymbol{q}),\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}[\xi](\boldsymbol{q})\Big)\cdot\vec{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha(\boldsymbol{q})=0\qquad\text{for all}\;\xi<\bar{w}.

In an entirely similar way, one deduces that

(6.20)

\int_{\mathcal{U}}\Big(\boldsymbol{\upsilon}[\zeta](\boldsymbol{q}),\boldsymbol{\varphi}[\zeta](\boldsymbol{q})\Big)\cdot\vec{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha(\boldsymbol{q})=0\qquad\text{for all}\;\zeta>\bar{z}

(6.21)

\int_{\mathcal{U}}\Big(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}[\zeta](\boldsymbol{q}),\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}[\zeta](\boldsymbol{q})\Big)\cdot\vec{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha(\boldsymbol{q})=0\qquad\text{for all}\;\zeta<\bar{z}.

2.
We notice that, due to assumption (
1.11
), only one of the quantities

(1,\lambda_{1})\cdot\vec{n},\qquad(1,\lambda_{2})\cdot\vec{n}

can change sign in
\mathcal{U}
: this is clear because, if both change sign, then there are
\boldsymbol{q}_{1},\boldsymbol{q}_{2}\in\mathcal{U}
for which

0=(1,\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{q}_{1}))\cdot\vec{n}=(1,\lambda_{2}(\boldsymbol{q}_{2}))\cdot\vec{n}

implying
\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{q}_{1}))=\lambda_{2}(\boldsymbol{q}_{2})
, which is excluded by (
1.11
).
Without loss of generality, we assume, up to a symmetry, that
(1,\lambda_{1})\cdot\vec{n}
can change sign, and therefore
(1,\lambda_{2})\cdot\vec{n}
has constant positive sign:

(6.22)

(1,\lambda_{2})\cdot\vec{n}>C>0\qquad\text{in $\mathcal{U}$}.

Consider now

z^{u}:=\sup\big\{z\;|\;\exists\;w\;\text{such that}\;(w,z)\in\mathrm{supp}\,\alpha\big\}

z^{\ell}:=\inf\big\{z\;|\;\exists\;w\;\text{such that}\;(w,z)\in\mathrm{supp}\,\alpha\big\}

We claim that
z^{u}=z^{\ell}=\bar{z}
, where we recall
\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}=(\bar{w},\bar{z})
. We start by recalling that, by Section
2

\boldsymbol{\varphi}[\zeta](w,\zeta)=\lambda_{2}(w,\zeta)\boldsymbol{\upsilon}[\zeta](w,\zeta),\qquad\text{for all $w,\zeta$}

and therefore by smoothness of
\boldsymbol{\varphi}[\zeta](w,z),\boldsymbol{\upsilon}[\zeta](w,z)
in the open region
\{(w,z,\zeta)\;|\;\zeta<z\}
we deduce that for all
\zeta<z
there holds:

(6.23)

\Big(\boldsymbol{\upsilon}[\zeta](w,z),\boldsymbol{\varphi}[\zeta](w,z)\Big)\cdot\vec{n}=\boldsymbol{\upsilon}[\zeta](w,\zeta)(1,\lambda_{2}(w,\zeta))\cdot\vec{n}+\mathcal{O}(1)\cdot|\zeta-z|

We also recall that

\boldsymbol{v}[\zeta](w,\zeta)=h(w,\zeta)>c>0\qquad\text{for all $w,\zeta$}

for some positive constant
c
, where
h
is defined in Section
2
, therefore using also (
6.22
) we deduce that there exists a
\bar{\delta}>0
sufficiently small such that

(6.24)

\Big(\boldsymbol{\upsilon}[\zeta](w,z),\boldsymbol{\varphi}[\zeta](w,z)\Big)\cdot\vec{n}>\widetilde{c}\qquad\text{for all $z-\bar{\delta}<\zeta<z$}

for some other positive constant
\widetilde{c}
.
Therefore, if by contradiction we have
z^{u}>\bar{z}
, we deduce from (
6.20
), by taking

\zeta=z^{u}-\min\Big\{\bar{\delta},|z^{u}-\bar{z}|/2\Big\}

that

0=\int_{\mathcal{U}}\Big(\boldsymbol{\upsilon}[\zeta](\boldsymbol{q}),\boldsymbol{\varphi}[\zeta](\boldsymbol{q})\Big)\cdot\vec{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha(\boldsymbol{q})\geq\widetilde{c}\int_{\mathcal{U}}\mathbf{1}_{\{z\in(\zeta,z^{u}]\}}(w,z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha(w,z)

which is a contradiction because by definition of
z^{u}
we must have instead

\int_{\mathcal{U}}\mathbf{1}_{\{z\in(\zeta,z^{u}]\}}(w,z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha(w,z)>0.

This shows
z^{u}=\bar{z}
; an entirely symmetric argument using (
6.21
) proves that
z^{\ell}=\bar{z}
as well.

3.
At this point, by Step
2.
we know that

\mathrm{supp}\,\alpha\subset\big\{(w,z)\;|\;z=\bar{z}\big\}

Since the system is genuinely nonlinear, there exists at most one point
w_{0}
such that

(1,\lambda_{1}(w_{0},\bar{z}))\cdot\vec{n}=0

because

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w}(1,\lambda_{1}(w_{0},\bar{z}))\cdot\vec{n}=\vec{n}_{x}\cdot\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w}\lambda_{1}(w_{0},\bar{z})>0.

Assume first that
w_{0}<\bar{w}
. In the same way as in the previous step, we have that
there exists a
\bar{\delta}>0
sufficiently small such that

(6.25)

\Big(\boldsymbol{\chi}[\xi](w,z),\boldsymbol{\psi}[\xi](w,z)\Big)\cdot\vec{n}>\widetilde{c}\qquad\text{for all $w-\bar{\delta}<\xi<w$}.

Let

w^{u}:=\sup\big\{w\;|\;(w,\bar{z})\in\mathrm{supp}\,\alpha\big\}.

By contradiction, if
w^{u}>\bar{w}
, we obtain as above by taking

\xi=w^{u}-\min\Big\{\bar{\delta},|w^{u}-\bar{w}|/2\Big\}

and using (
6.18
), that

0=\int_{\mathcal{U}}\Big(\boldsymbol{\chi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{q}),\boldsymbol{\psi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{q})\Big)\cdot\vec{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha(\boldsymbol{q})\geq\widetilde{c}\int_{\mathcal{U}}\mathbf{1}_{\{w\in(\xi,w^{u}]\}}(w,z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha(w,z)

which is a contradiction with the definition of
w^{u}
. Therefore we obtained
w^{u}\leq\bar{w}
. Now it is sufficient to use that
\alpha
is a probability measure to conclude. In fact, consider now
\xi<\bar{w}
, and using (
6.14
) we deduce

\Big(\boldsymbol{\chi}[\xi](\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}),\boldsymbol{\psi}[\xi](\bar{\boldsymbol{u}})\Big)\cdot\vec{n}=\int_{\mathcal{U}}\Big(\boldsymbol{\chi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{q}),\boldsymbol{\psi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{q})\Big)\cdot\vec{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha(\boldsymbol{q})

By taking the limit as
\xi\to\bar{w}^{-}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Big(\boldsymbol{\chi}[\bar{w}](\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}),\boldsymbol{\psi}[\bar{w}](\bar{\boldsymbol{u}})\Big)\cdot\vec{n}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\xi\to\bar{w}^{-}}\int_{\mathcal{U}}\Big(\boldsymbol{\chi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{q}),\boldsymbol{\psi}[\xi](\boldsymbol{q})\Big)\cdot\vec{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha(\boldsymbol{q})

\displaystyle=\alpha(\{(\bar{w},\bar{z})\})\Big(\boldsymbol{\chi}[\bar{w}](\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}),\boldsymbol{\psi}[\bar{w}](\bar{\boldsymbol{u}})\Big).

Since we assumed
w_{0}<\bar{w}
, there holds

\Big(\boldsymbol{\chi}[\bar{w}](\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}),\boldsymbol{\psi}[\bar{w}](\bar{\boldsymbol{u}})\Big)>0

and therefore
\alpha(\{(\bar{w},\bar{z})\})=1
. Since
\alpha
is a probability measure, this implies that
\alpha
is concentrated in the point
\bar{u}=(\bar{w},\bar{z})
.

The other case
w_{0}>\bar{w}
is proved symmetrically, using (
6.14
), and this concludes the proof of the Lemma.
∎

Appendix A

Proposition A.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mu\in\mathscr{M}(\Omega)
be a positive locally finite Borel measure, and let
\boldsymbol{\Phi}:\Omega\to\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
,
y\mapsto\boldsymbol{\Phi}(y,\cdot)
be a weakly measurable map. Then for
\mu
-almost every
x
there holds for all
p\in C^{0}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R};\,\mathbb{R})
:

(A.1)

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{\mu(B_{r}(x))}\Bigg\{

\displaystyle\int_{B_{r}(x)}p\left(\frac{y-x}{r},\xi\right)\Big(\boldsymbol{\Phi}(y,\xi)-\boldsymbol{\Phi}(x,\xi)\Big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\Bigg\}=0.

Proof.

Let
\mathscr{G}\subset C^{0}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
be a dense and countable subset in the uniform topology.
Since finite sums
\sum_{i}\varphi_{i}(y)\varrho_{i}(\xi)
with
\varrho_{i}\in\mathscr{G}
are dense in
C^{0}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R};\,\mathbb{R})
in the uniform topology, it is clearly sufficient to prove the result for functions
p\in C^{0}_{c}
that factorize as
p(y,\xi)=\varphi(y)\varrho(\xi)
, with
\varrho\in\mathscr{G}
. In order to do this, for every
\varrho\in\mathscr{G}
, define a function

F_{\varrho}(y):=\int\varrho(\xi)\boldsymbol{\Phi}(y,\xi)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi

and let

G_{\varrho}:=\Big\{x\in\Omega\;|\;\text{$x$ is a $\mu$-Lebesgue point for $F_{\varrho}$}\Big\},\qquad G:=\bigcap_{\varrho\in\mathscr{G}}G_{\varrho}

and notice that there holds
\mu(\Omega\setminus G)=0
. Then there holds for every
\varphi\in C^{0}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
x\in G
that

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mu(B_{r}(x))}\Bigg|\int_{B_{r}(x)}\varphi\left(\frac{y-x}{r}\right)(F_{\varrho}(y)-F_{\varrho}(x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\Bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\|\varphi\|_{C^{0}}\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{\mu(B_{r}(x))}\int_{B_{r}(x)}|F_{\varrho}(y)-F_{\varrho}(x)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(y)=0

which is what we wanted to prove.

∎

Appendix B

The following is a standard geometric measure theory result valid in much more generality, but for simplicity we state it and prove it only our setting.

Proposition B.1
.

Let
\gamma:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}
be a Lipschitz curve and
\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathscr{M}(\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R})
be a positive locally finite Borel measure. Then for almost every
\bar{t}>0
there holds

(B.1)

\lim_{r\to 0}\,\frac{1}{r}\boldsymbol{\nu}\big(B_{r}^{\pm}(\bar{t},\gamma(\bar{t}))\big)=0

where

B_{r}^{+}(\bar{t},\gamma(\bar{t})):=\{(t,x)\in B_{r}(t,\gamma(\bar{t}))\;|\;x>\gamma(t)\}

B_{r}^{-}(\bar{t},\gamma(\bar{t})):=\{(t,x)\in B_{r}(t,\gamma(\bar{t}))\;|\;x<\gamma(t)\}

Proof.

Let

\mu:=\mathscr{H}^{1}\llcorner\mathrm{Graph}\,\gamma,\qquad\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\nu}}:=\boldsymbol{\nu}-\mu

By the Besicovitch differentiation theorem (see for example
[
1
, Theorem 2.22]
) we have

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\nu}}=a\mu+\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{s}

where
a
is defined
\mu
-a.e. by

a(t,x):=\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(B_{r}((t,x)))}{\mu(B_{r}((t,x)))}\qquad\text{for $(t,x)\in\mathrm{supp}\,\mu$}

and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{s}
is a measure concentrated on a
\mu
-negligible set, i.e.
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(\mathrm{Graph}\,\gamma)=0
. Therefore we must have
a(t,x)=0
for
\mu
-a.e.
(t,x)
. Moreover for some constant
C>0
depending only on the Lipschitz constant of
\gamma
there holds

\mu(B_{r}(\bar{t},\gamma(\bar{t})))\leq Cr

so that we deduce that for almost every
\bar{t}

\limsup_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\big(B_{r}(\bar{t},\gamma(\bar{t}))\big)}{r}\leq C\limsup_{r\to 0^{+}}\;\frac{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\big(B_{r}(\bar{t},\gamma(\bar{t}))\big)}{\mu(B_{r}(\bar{t},\gamma(\bar{t}))}=0

which is what we wanted to prove.
∎
