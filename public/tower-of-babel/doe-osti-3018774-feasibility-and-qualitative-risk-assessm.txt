ABSTRACT
Airports rely on robust backup power systems to maintain critical operations during emergencies.
This paper investigates the feasibility of using hydrogen fuel cell electric buses (FCEBs) as
emergency backup power sources, focusing on their technical specifications, operational challenges,
and associated risks. Using Portland International Airport as a case study, the analysis evaluates the
power output and hydrogen consumption of New Flyer’s 40’ Xcelsior Charge fuel cell buses, to
provide power for key airport systems. Additionally, the paper explores the design and layout of how
FCEBs can be connected to airport facilities, and addresses safety considerations, hydrogen supply
logistics, and electrical compatibility. While FCEBs present significant advantages in terms of
sustainability and resilience, their implementation requires careful planning, infrastructure
investment, and adherence to safety standards. This research provides actionable insights for airport
stakeholders seeking to transition to hydrogen-based backup power system.

1. INTRODUCTION
Airports are critical infrastructure hubs that require reliable and resilient power systems to ensure
uninterrupted operations, particularly during emergencies. From powering airfield landing lights to
maintaining communication systems and supporting emergency services, airports depend on robust
backup power solutions to mitigate the risks posed by power outages. Traditionally, diesel generators
are the primary source of emergency backup power; however, fuel cells and batteries such as those
on hydrogen fuel cell electric buses (FCEBs) can be considered as an alternative due to their power
output, mobility, and relatively quick refueling. These attributes highlight the potential for FCEBs’
viability for supporting airport infrastructure during critical power outages. Recent research by
Bayrak et al. highlight the possibility of using hydrogen-powered FCEBs as solutions to providing
effective emergency backup power [1].
A recent electrical substation fire near London's Heathrow airport in March 2025 highlights the
importance of providing backup power to airports and properly maintaining infrastructure. The fire
caused the airport to shut down for more than a day, affecting nearly 1,300 flights, 200,000
passengers, and flights at connecting airports [2]. This emphasizes the need for emergency backup
power at airports to keep key infrastructure running.
A report from the U.S. Government Accountability Office states that when outages occur, 11% of
participating airports reported flight delays and cancellations, 13% said communication was affected,
and 14% indicated parking was impacted [3]. One-third of the outages studied were caused by aging
or poorly maintained airport infrastructure and half were due to issues with the utility company
supplying the power [3]. This highlights the necessity for better understanding of airports’ electrical
systems, their effects on critical infrastructure during an emergency, and the advantage of having a
non-grid-dependent backup system such as FCEBs.
Buses may be well-suited for backup power applications due to their frequent operation. Any failure
with the fuel cell system is more likely to be detected during regular use, making them more reliable
than generators that are only occasionally tested. Importantly, regardless of the effectiveness or
diversity of backup power systems, airport power and fuel distribution networks should be robust
and well-maintained.
This paper explores the feasibility of using FCEBs as emergency backup power at airports, focusing
on their technical capabilities, operational challenges, and associated risks. Using Portland
International Airport (PDX) as a case study, the analysis examines the specifications of New Flyer’s
40’ Xcelsior Charge fuel cell buses and evaluates the feasibility of meeting the power demands of
various airport systems during a power outage using these vehicles. This report also addresses the
specialized infrastructure component required to connect FCEBs to airport facilities, safety
considerations, hydrogen supply logistics, and electrical compatibility.
By presenting hazard and risk evaluations, power calculations, and infrastructure designs, this report
aims to provide airport decision makers, engineers, and stakeholders with the insights needed to
evaluate the adoption of hydrogen buses for emergency backup power.

2. FEASIBILITY STUDY

2.1. Power Output and Specifications of Xcelsior FCEBs
This work evaluated whether FCEBs could offer a viable alternative to diesel generators at the PDX
airport in the case of an emergency, especially since the power output and mobility of FCEBs can
provide an advantage for rapid deployment to different facilities. A hypothetical fleet of 24 New
Flyer’s 40’ Xcelsior Charge FCEBs are considered here for use at PDX airport and will be the basis
of our analysis. These buses offer a fuel cell power output of 100 kW and hydrogen storage capacity
of 37.5 kg [4]. This mass of hydrogen is an equivalent battery energy (EBE) of 734 kWh [5], which
yields a fuel cell efficiency of 58.7% (see Appendix A). In addition, New Flyer representatives
informed our team that the battery capacity of their 40’ FCEB Xcelsior is 135 kWh [6]. The
hydrogen fuel and battery capacity combined yield a total of 869 kWh of available power.

2.2. Considerations in using FCEBs for Emergency Backup Power
There are various factors to consider before assuming this power can be used for external
applications. Firstly, no manufacturer currently offers fuel cell electric buses with interfaces to an
external load. When contacted, New Flyer and Gillig bus manufacturers stated that providing power
for external uses was not a current option for their buses and that all power generated by the fuel
cell is applied to bus functionality such as propulsion, HVAC, or lighting. Hence, these buses cannot
currently be used to power external loads such as airport infrastructure and doing so would likely
require modification of the control system, the external electrical interface, and may void the
warranty for these vehicles.
Hypothetically, if these buses were to be used to power external loads, they would require external
interfaces. To allow us to analyze how fuel cell buses can be used for emergency power supply, in
this work we assumed that FCEBs can be interfaced to external loads. Performing this analysis in no
way validates, supports, or encourages the use of current FCEBs for these applications, rather, it
explores the potential for this functionality in the future.
Secondly, due to the size of the electrical loads at PDX, powering any one infrastructure application
could require several buses; this would likely be the case at any other airport as well. Given a fleet of
24 buses, it would be possible to power several important systems, however a docking zone would
be required to accommodate space for these vehicles during an emergency, possibly necessitating
changes to airport layout. Likewise, considerations must be taken for the high voltage lines that
would interface to the buses. These would require electrical connection work to properly link the
buses together so they may provide combined power and convert from the DC power from the bus
to AC used typically in grid powered infrastructure. Finally, periodic hydrogen refueling of the
FCEBs may be necessary (if available) so that they may continue to provide backup power in an
emergency.
Thirdly, there would need to be some sort of electrical isolation of loads around the airport so that,
while attempting to power one piece of critical infrastructure, power is not drawn by other lower
priority loads. Each infrastructure item that will be powered by FCEB as backup power will need a
dedicated electrical system consisting of just the load and the number of buses need to power the
load.
Using FCEBs to power systems can introduce unique risks such as the possibility of electrical sparks
or fire potentially near hydrogen leaks or releases. To prevent these hazardous scenarios, any
refueling, or transmission system should be equipped with sensors, emergency shut-off systems for

 11

hydrogen supply and electrical systems, appropriate venting, as well as fire suppression systems.
Personnel operating in the vicinity should receive training on safety protocols related to hydrogen
handling, electrical systems, and proper grounding, with clear posted signage with information such
as safety procedures and emergency contacts.

airport power during an emergency must be quantified. Several infrastructure elements common to
most airports were considered in this analysis such as the terminal, airfield lights, utility plants, and
the fire department. To estimate the power needs of each infrastructure element, the current diesel
fuel consumption of some of PDX’s emergency power generators was used and shown in Table 1.
This list of infrastructure systems represents only a subset of power requirements for airport

2.4. Providing Continuous Emergency Backup Power
Together, the fuel cell and battery cannot provide continuous emergency backup power unless the
FCEBs are regularly swapped and/or refueled as needed. To extend the time of backup power
availability, hydrogen should be delivered to the FCEBs at their point of use or a strategic rotation
system should be employed to exchange FCEBs for those that have been refueled.

2.4.1. Rotation Refueling System
Assuming the available FCEB fleet is greater than the number of buses needed to provide power for
an infrastructure system, buses can be rotated in and out of service and sent for refueling as needed.
The exact number of backup buses needed will depend greatly on the fuel available in each bus at
the time of an emergency and the time it takes to refuel the buses. More information would be
needed to provide a detailed refueling rotation system, however, assumptions can be made to
examine how this system may work. One approach is to prioritize having at least one additional
refueled bus that is always available to take over for a bus that is almost out of fuel, which would
ensure that the total required power is continuously delivered to the infrastructure system. For
example, the rental car facility, which requires a minimum of 7 buses to meet power demands, could
be powered for around 8 hours with all the buses’ fuel cell systems working together and over 9.5
hours if the batteries are included. By dedicating 8 buses and their batteries to this operation, one
bus could be rotated out around once an hour, ensuring continuous power with about 2 hours of
margin in case some fuel levels are lower than others. The number of buses in rotation can never
drop below 6, which is the number of buses needed to supply the instantaneous power necessary for
the rental car center operation. This result assumes that, of the 6 initial buses, the one lowest on fuel
could last at least 2 hours before being relieved. This also assumes that refueling of the replacement
bus can be done within 2 hours and that none of the buses would run out of fuel at the same time.
This margin is important given the Xcelsior FCEB requires 10 to 18 minutes to refuel [4] and any
additional delay caused by shortage of staff or blocked roads during an emergency would necessitate
this level of margin. Figure 1 below shows a possible configuration for the FCEB emergency power
port in which one bus arrives in time to replace a bus that has run out of fuel and must then return
to the hydrogen refueling station. This type of operation would ensure that there is no lapse in
power supply when hydrogen fuel on the bus is depleted. Figure 1 assumes the infrastructure of
interest only requires 4 buses to power, with a fifth in refueling rotation.

2.4.3. Emergency Infrastructure Design
 The design and layout of infrastructure must prioritize safety, accessibility, and operational efficiency
 to ensure that essential services can be maintained without interruption. Strategic development of
 the emergency power ports for FCEBs with safely-covered electrical lines, switches, and a microgrid
 control system is essential for rapid and effective deployment during emergencies. Considerations
 for vehicle maneuverability, turning radii, and clear pathways for bus access are crucial to ensure that
 FCEBs can reach electrical receptacles. If possible, any design or procedure should also incorporate
 operational flexibility, allowing for future expansions or modifications, such as for additional buses
 or higher power requirement electrical connections. Mobile connection options, such as retractable
 high-voltage cables, could provide flexibility based on the specific needs of emergency situations.
 Adding flexibility and redundancy to the system is also important since parts of the infrastructure
 may be out of service due to the emergency conditions themselves; for example, roads may be
 damaged or unusable due to an earthquake, power connections may be severed, and so on.

 2.5. Diverse Applications of FCEBs During an Emergency
 Reviewing all options available, airport management should consider the advantages and
 disadvantages of using FCEBs for emergency power generation during an outage. FCEBs may be
 able to provide modular, flexible, and mobile power needs as necessary, especially if diesel
 generators are out of service or diesel fuel is unavailable. However, current FCEBs are designed to
 transport people and their cargo, not necessarily to provide emergency power. It is therefore also
 important to consider whether FCEBs should be used during outages for different emergency
 services such as delivering people to and from terminals, transporting goods such as blankets, food,
 water, and medicine, and transporting people in need of medical care to appropriate facilities. One
 option is to deploy a subset of buses for such emergency transportation while others provide

emergency backup power to infrastructure systems. These decisions may be based on the urgency of
transportation demands and the remaining hydrogen availability during an outage.

3. HAZARD IDENTIFICATION
Assuming FCEBs can be interfaced to provide power to external loads such as critical airport
infrastructure during emergencies, the risks of providing power in this manner should be considered.
The risks associated with normal bus operation, maintenance, and refueling on airport grounds are
outlined in quantitative and qualitative risk assessments presented by Louie et al. [11] [12]. Such risk
assessments include identifying hazards and quantifying frequencies of different sized hydrogen
leaks which under certain conditions may create an ignited jet flame or delayed overpressure
explosion. These leak and ignition scenarios are not likely to occur under normal operation but
should still be considered in a hazard analysis, especially if they are of high consequence, in order to
identify additional potential risks associated with the use of FCEBs to power airport infrastructure.
There are several safety risks associated with deploying hydrogen-based emergency power generation
at different locations around the airport. FCEBs can generate high voltage electricity up to 650 V
[13], which can create electrical hazards, such as shock and spark sources. Severe shocks may lead to
individual injury or electrocution. If flammable conditions are present, spark sources may ignite jet,
diesel, or hydrogen fuel. Hydrogen flames are not be visible in daylight, adding an additional risk to
people in the vicinity.
In the case of bus rotation refueling, there is operational risk associated with moving buses in and
out of service and connecting and disconnecting buses from power transmission lines. Refueling the
buses during an emergency also comes with the possibility of user error, which introduces hazards
even during routine operation. Effective training and coordination among airport management,
emergency responders, and FCEB operators are essential to ensure that the use of FCEBs as
generators does not jeopardize the safety of operators, passengers, aircraft, or other equipment.
Since multiple buses are required to power airport infrastructure, electrical interfaces are necessary.
Ideally, these would be in stationary locations for each bus and directly connected to an
infrastructure system requiring power. However, if greater flexibility or mobility is required, high
voltage insulated cables may be run along the ground, connecting each bus to a mobile interface,
which would then connect to the infrastructure system in need of power. If this approach is
implemented, there is risk of damage to these high voltage lines; exposed cables are vulnerable to
falling objects or drive-over. Any damage to high voltage lines could expose personnel or equipment
to electrical hazards.
Though unlikely, thermal runaway of the high voltage lithium-ion batteries on FCEBs can occur
[14]. Thermal runaway can be caused by overcharging, overheating, or physical damage, possibly
resulting in high temperatures, fire, explosion, and chemical emissions [15]. Battery fires may
generate high temperatures for long periods of time, requiring large quantities of water or specialized
firefighting methods. If thermal runaway occurs, this can damage power transmission lines or
compromise the integrity of nearby hydrogen tanks, triggering the thermal overpressure protection
system and the release of the tank’s contents when a temperature of around 110°C [16]. This release
could ignite, resulting in overpressure or additional heat flux hazards.
Proper safety measures must be in place to prevent electrical shocks during emergency power
generation. Personnel should be trained to recognize and mitigate electrical risks associated with fuel
cell technology and FCEB operation. Also, performance of FCEBs as power delivery systems is
currently not well characterized, and conducting testing and simulations can help assess FCEB
performance and reliability in an emergency scenario. Based on results from these studies, standard
operating procedures and training can be developed for operators of buses and electrical
transmission lines. Electrical compatibility with specific airport infrastructure needs to be assessed to

 21

reduce the risk of electrical hazards. Adherence to safety codes and standards is critical when using
FCEB for emergency power, particularly standards like NFPA 70 Article 692: Fuel Cell Systems and
NFPA 70 Article 700: Emergency Systems, which are directly applicable [17]. These requirements
highlight the importance of risk assessment and the implementation of stringent safety measures
when utilizing FCEBs as emergency power sources at airports.

4. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

FCEBs as a backup power solution for airport infrastructure presents a means of enhancing
operational resilience by diversifying options for backup power sources. FCEBs as emergency power
generators offer unique advantages, including mobility, and relatively quick refueling. Because of
this, FCEBs may be part of a potential solution to mitigate risks associated with power outages.

This report has detailed the feasibility of utilizing FCEBs, using the characteristics of the New Flyer
40’ Xcelsior Charge buses, to provide emergency power at airports, highlighting their specifications
and potential applications. This model of bus was used as an example only; this does not imply any
sort of endorsement of this bus model, and other bus models may yield different results. Currently,
FCEBs cannot be utilized for emergency power generation due to manufacturer restrictions, so
current design and operational constraints require review of how these vehicles can be integrated
into emergency response without compromising their intended use and manufacturer warrantees. As
a hypothetical analysis, this work shows that a fleet of 24 FCEBs used as emergency power
generators can simultaneously power a few, but not all, infrastructure systems at PDX.

The analysis indicates that if FCEBs can be employed to support critical airport operations during
emergencies, several logistical and safety considerations must be addressed. These include the need
for external load connections, ensuring adequate hydrogen fuel supply, and the establishment of
robust safety protocols to mitigate risks associated with hydrogen handling and high-voltage
electricity. Safety risks associated with hydrogen handling, electrical hazards, and the potential for
thermal runaway in lithium-ion batteries must be meticulously managed. Effective training and
coordination among airport management, emergency responders, and FCEB operators are critical to
ensuring the safety of personnel, passengers, and airport operations.

The findings underscore the importance of thorough planning and infrastructure investment to
facilitate the safe integration of FCEBs into airport emergency power generation. Implementing a
system for the continuous refueling of buses and the establishment of dedicated emergency power
ports for transmission can enhance the reliability of backup power systems. Additionally, conducting
quantitative risk assessments can help identify potential hazards associated with hydrogen
infrastructure and ensure compliance with safety requirements. Safety measures such as emergency
shut-off systems, proper venting, and fire suppression capabilities are important means of
preventing and mitigating hazards resulting from possible hydrogen leaks or electrical malfunction.
Personnel involved in the operation of FCEBs as emergency generators should be trained to
effectively manage the unique risks associated with hydrogen fuel co-located with high-voltage
systems to protect both personnel and infrastructure during emergencies. By addressing the outlined
challenges and risks, airports can leverage the benefits of FCEBs to create a more resilient energy
framework, ensuring that critical operations remain uninterrupted during emergencies.
