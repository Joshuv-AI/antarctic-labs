Warming-driven shifts in global building energy use reshape climate mitigation planning
Authors: Mengting Zhu, Mengqi Zhao, Rongqi Zhu, Jiyong Eom, Yuyu Zhou, Sha Yu, Fengqiao Mei, Yang Ou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlobal warming is reshaping energy demand in buildings, one of the largest sectors for energy consumption and CO2 emissions. Yet widely used mitigation scenarios, such as those in the Shared Socioeconomic Pathways and Representative Concentration Pathways (SSP-RCP) framework, often assume fixed historical climate baselines when projecting building energy use. This modeling convention, originally intended to isolate anthropogenic effects, now increasingly overlooks how rising temperatures systematically alter heating and cooling needs. Here we integrate updated Heating and Cooling Degree Days, derived from the latest Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 climate projections, into the Global Change Analysis Model to assess the consequences of incorporating future warming into global mitigation pathways. We find that ignoring warming trends introduces substantial biases: cooling demand is underestimated by up to 23% in scenarios with low warming and 79% in scenarios with high warming, while heating demand is overestimated by up to 14% and 40%, respectively, by 2100. Importantly, these misestimates reshape global energy and emissions trajectories, reducing CO2 emissions by 83–1600 Mt CO2 year−1 in 2100 when warming trends are included across different SSP-RCP scenarios, but also underestimating the rise in F-gas emissions related to cooling, especially in warmer developing regions.Subject terms: Climate and Earth system modelling, Climate-change mitigation

Introduction

Climate change is accelerating, with global temperatures projected to rise by 1.0 to 1.8 °C under pathways with low emissions and up to 5.7 °C under scenarios with high emissions by 21001. Recent studies further show that the duration of the longest heatwaves increases nonlinearly with global warming, implying an acceleration in extreme heat exposure per degree of warming and reinforcing the link between warming, prolonged heat events, and increases in cooling degree days2. These changes directly affect energy use, especially in buildings, which accounts for nearly 30% of global final energy use3 in 2020. While the drivers of climate change are well established, its feedbacks on human systems, particularly energy systems, remain underexplored. Warming increasingly influences demand patterns, technology performance, and resource availability across sectors such as agriculture4–6, health7–9, and energy use10–15. Yet most mitigation scenarios for the long term continue to assume fixed historical temperature baselines when projecting building energy use (for example Heating Degree Day and Cooling Degree Day) fixed at levels representative of the historical climate16. This simplification, which reflects both the original focus of integrated assessment models on technology and emissions pathways and the computational difficulty of coupling them with gridded climate projections, means that many scenarios still fail to adequately account for how rising temperatures shift heating and cooling needs17. In particular, buildings are highly sensitive to temperature changes, but their evolving demand under future warming is rarely captured in energy models. Although some studies have explored the influence of climate change on building energy systems, they focus mainly on cooling18,19. In reality, shifts driven by climate are governed by both heating and cooling dynamics, which often move in opposite directions across regions and sectors. Neglecting either can bias projections of future demand and emissions.

Research in this area follows two main approaches. Empirical studies typically rely on econometric methods to assess responses of energy use to temperature fluctuations for the short term, using historical data at national or subnational levels10,20–27. For example, van Ruijven et al.23 assesses how warming amplifies energy demand growth globally, and Yu et al.27 show how temperature extremes disproportionately burden households with low income in the US. While insightful, such studies often lack scope for the long term and do not capture the system dynamics across sectors critical for mitigation planning. Integrated Assessment Models (IAMs), in contrast, connect socioeconomic trends with Earth system processes to explore future climate and energy scenarios, widely used to assess how policy or technology shifts influence emissions and warming28. However, most established IAMs, such as IMAGE29,30, REMIND31, and MESSAGE32,33, project building energy use using income or price proxies, without explicitly accounting for variables related to temperature or degree days. A notable exception is the Global Change Analysis Model (GCAM), which incorporates heating and cooling degree days (HDD/CDD) directly into its building service demand equations34–36. Yet even GCAM relies on substantially outdated temperature data based on the Special Report on Emissions Scenarios (SRES) framework back in 200011, which no longer reflects the best available climate science (see Supplementary Note and Supplementary Table 1 for a detailed review).

To address this gap, we enhance GCAM by integrating updated HDD/CDD estimates derived from the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6), covering the period from 2021 to 2100 across multiple climate and socioeconomic pathways. CMIP6 represents the most advanced generation of climate projections, offering improved spatial resolution and representation of key physical processes such as cloud feedbacks and land–atmosphere interactions37,38. We use these updated temperature trajectories to revise GCAM’s building energy model and assess how warming-induced shifts in space conditioning affect energy demand, electricity generation, and CO2 emissions globally and regionally.

This framework necessitates specific methodological tradeoffs to maintain global consistency and computational tractability. First, our analysis focuses on HDD/CDD as the primary metrics for climate forcing, omitting secondary stressors such as humidity or wind speed. Second, we rely on GCAM’s regional aggregation, which prioritizes global economic interactions over the building-stock details captured at high resolution in engineering models. Finally, we adopt a technological approach that does not explicitly model complex adaptive behaviors (e.g., thermostat adjustments), allowing us to isolate the signal of climate warming on energy demand without the confounding uncertainty of predicting future behavioral shifts. Here, we show that ignoring recent and future warming can substantially distort building-sector dynamics and lead to biased mitigation planning, especially in regions with rapidly rising temperatures or limited access to adaptive cooling.

Results

Projected global temperature patterns under CMIP6 scenarios

We use CMIP6 daily temperature projections at 0.5° × 0.5° resolution to evaluate global temperature changes from 2020 to 2100 under four representative Shared Socioeconomic Pathways-Representative Concentration Pathways (SSP-RCP) scenarios: SSP1-2.6 (SSP126), SSP2-4.5 (SSP245), SSP3-7.0 (SSP370), and SSP5-8.5(SSP585) (Supplementary Fig. 3). All scenarios show consistent warming trends, with notable increases in both maximum and minimum daily temperatures. As warming intensifies, HDDs decline globally, averaging 2472 degree days under SSP126, 2098 degree days under SSP245, 1832 degree days under SSP370, and 1660 degree days under SSP585 by 2100 (Fig. 1a-d). Conversely, CDDs rise substantially, with regional averages ranging from 2573 degree days (SSP126) to 4081 degree days (SSP585) (Fig. 1e-h).

These global trends mask substantial regional heterogeneity. In high-latitude regions such as Canada, Russia, and the European Free Trade Association, HDDs decline sharply; for example, HDDs in Russia decrease from 8,015 degree days (SSP126) to 5,814 degree days (SSP585) by 2100. Meanwhile, low-latitude regions, including Africa, the Middle East, and India, see dramatic increases in CDD, with average increases over 80% between CMIP and constant scenarios under SSP126 and SSP585.

Shifts in heating and cooling demand

Building on these projected temperature shifts, we assess their direct impacts on heating and cooling energy demand. Across all scenarios, global warming consistently drives up building cooling needs while reducing heating requirements relative to a constant-temperature baseline. However, the magnitude of these shifts depends heavily on socio-economic and emissions pathways (Fig. 2). For instance, under SSP126 in 2100, cooling energy demand is underestimated by 23% and heating energy demand overestimated by 14% when relying on fixed historical temperature baselines. Under SSP585 in 2100, the gap widens considerably: cooling is underestimated by up to 79%, while heating is overestimated by 40%. In absolute terms, cooling demand is underestimated by 7.3 EJ (Exajoules, 1 EJ = 1018 J) (residential) and 7.1 EJ (commercial), while heating is overestimated by 12.4 EJ (residential) and 6.5 EJ (commercial), respectively (Supplementary Figs. 4, 5).

While the global pattern is clear, with cooling increasing and heating declining, the scale and direction of these changes vary substantially across regions (Fig. 2i–l). Rising temperatures, urbanization, and greater air-conditioning adoption drive cooling increases in North America, China, and Western Africa. Meanwhile, warmer winters and efficiency gains lead to notable reductions in heating demand in the Middle East, the United States, and China.

These regional disparities become even more pronounced when comparing CMIP6-informed projections with constant-temperature scenarios (Fig. 2m). Under SSP585 in 2100, omitting recent warming leads to underestimating cooling demand by up to 45% in the Middle East and 38% in Southeast Asia, regions experiencing increasingly severe heat and limited access to adaptive cooling technologies. Conversely, heating demand is overestimated by 39% in both the US and China, where milder winters are reducing reliance on traditional heating systems.

Climate-driven impacts on building energy consumption patterns

Shifts in heating and cooling needs not only change the magnitude of building energy use, but also its structure, especially the balance between fossil fuels and electricity. Heating is traditionally supplied by a diverse mix of fossil fuels, while cooling is predominantly powered by electricity. As warming reduces heating demand and boosts cooling needs, two systemic transitions emerge: an overall decline in total building energy consumption (in most scenarios), and a marked shift toward electricity-intensive end-use (Fig. 3). For example, under SSP585, total electricity use in commercial and residential buildings increases by 10.1 EJ by 2100, while natural gas consumption falls by 9.3 EJ. Residential energy demand drops by nearly 5 EJ over the same period relative to the constant-temperature scenario. This asymmetry, larger reductions in gas use than increases in electricity use, reflects the combination of more efficient electricity-based cooling technologies, the fact that baseline heating degree days exceed cooling degree days in many regions, and the gradual improvement of building envelopes and end-use efficiencies over time in GCAM.

Furthermore, these structural shifts vary by sector and scenario. In the commercial sector, total energy use decreases under SSP126, SSP245, and SSP370 (by 0.44 EJ, 0.64 EJ, and 2.11 EJ on average, respectively) but increases under SSP585 due to intensified cooling demand (Fig. 3b–e). In contrast, the residential sector shows rising energy use under SSP126 and SSP585, while experiencing a decline under SSP245 and SSP370 (average decrease of 0.73 and 0.5 EJ) (Fig. 3f–i). Across all scenarios, electricity becomes the dominant energy source: by 2100 under SSP585, it accounts for 71.7% of commercial energy use, and residential electricity demand rises by 4.1 EJ compared to the constant-temperature baseline (Supplementary Figs. 9, 10).

These changes are also reflected in the fuel mix. Under SSP585, commercial electricity use rises by 6.0 EJ by 2100, while natural gas and coal use fall by 41% and 48%, respectively. In the residential sector, refined liquids decline by 2.1 EJ, and natural gas drops by over 40%. Together, these shifts reflect the growing role of electricity in meeting space cooling demand and the corresponding decline in fossil-fuel space heating, especially in high-warming, low-mitigation scenarios.

Electricity generation under climate-induced demand shifts

The increase in climate-driven cooling demand leads directly to higher electricity consumption in the building sector, and correspondingly higher electricity generation (Fig. 4). These effects are relatively modest in low-warming scenarios but grow substantially under high-emissions pathways. Under SSP126, electricity use for building cooling rises by 3.07 EJ by 2100, triggering a generation increase of 0.95 EJ. In contrast, the impact is more dramatic under SSP585, with cooling demand rising by 14.26 EJ and electricity generation increasing by 6.6 EJ by 2100. SSP245, often interpreted as a proxy for a continuation of current policies and moderate mitigation effort, exhibits intermediate responses in both cooling-driven electricity demand and generation expansion, suggesting that the climate-induced effects identified here are already relevant under a commonly used near-term policy benchmark.

Beyond the rise in quantity, the mix of electricity supply also changes in response to increased demand. In SSP370, additional generation in 2100 is supplied by a diversified mix of wind (40.7%), solar (24.9%), and coal (20.9%). In contrast, SSP585 relies heavily on fossil fuels, with natural gas supplying 41.0% and coal 16.7% of the added generation. These differences reflect underlying assumptions: while SSP370 envisions moderate mitigation and energy diversification, SSP585 emphasizes economic expansion and sustained fossil fuel use with limited climate policy.

Critically, these dynamics highlight how warming-induced energy demand interacts with socioeconomic development pathways to shape energy system trajectories, an interaction often neglected in conventional scenario modeling. While electricity generation scales up to meet growing demand, its carbon intensity diverges sharply. Under high-emissions SSP585 pathways, additional demand is met largely by fossil fuels, reinforcing emissions. Under low-emissions scenarios, renewable energy dominates the expansion, offering a path toward lower-carbon outcomes despite higher electricity use.

CO2 emission responses to climate-driven energy shifts

Changes in heating and cooling demand reshape not just energy use, but also the level and distribution of CO2 emissions across sectors (Fig. 5). CMIP6-informed modeling reveals substantial departures from constant-temperature baselines. Under the CMIP scenarios, CO2 emissions from electricity sector rise slightly in SSP585 due to increased electricity use (largely fossil-fueled) to meet higher cooling demand.

Compared to the constant-temperature scenarios, global CO2 emissions fall by 83–1600 million tonnes (Mt CO2) across the four scenarios (Fig. 5). The largest drop occurs under SSP585, where steep reductions in fossil fuel use for heating, especially in residential and industrial sectors, outweigh emissions increases from cooling-related electricity generation. Net emission reduction reaches 1600 Mt CO2 in 2100 under SSP585, accounting for almost 3.8% of current total emissions. In SSP126 and SSP370, emissions reductions are particularly pronounced in industry, with 1000 Mt CO2 avoided in 2050 due to greater use of biomass and reduced coal dependence (Supplementary Fig. 11, 12). In SSP245 and SSP585, the residential sector contributes most to net reductions as electricity displaces fossil-fuel heating (Fig. 3). The ratios of net change in CO2 emissions relative to the constant scenarios are 1.51% and 1.55% on average under SSP245 and SSP585, respectively. In addition, the building sector consistently shows emissions declines across all pathways (Supplementary Fig. 12). These are driven by reduced natural gas and refined liquid consumption, as electricity gains share in response to warming. Natural gas emissions fall by 17–53% and refined liquid emissions drop by 22–36%, from SSP370 to SSP585, respectively.

Importantly, while CO2 emissions decline overall, cooling-related electricity use drives increases in non-CO2 emissions, particularly F-gases used in cooling applications (Supplementary Fig. 13). F-gas emissions rise from 89.15Mt CO2-eq (SSP126) to 5,584.32Mt CO2-eq (SSP585), and we note that while F-gases appear small relative to gross sectoral totals, their contribution can be non-negligible when assessed as net changes relative to the constant scenarios, and may even exceed the net CO2 change in some scenarios39. This trend underscores an important trade-off in climate-forced cooling adaptation, especially across climate-vulnerable regions that are simultaneously growing rapidly in the Global South (Supplementary Fig. 14).

Furthermore, these emission changes vary widely across regions (Fig. 6). Under SSP245, regional CO2 emissions decline the most on average, by 12.23 Mt CO2 in 2050. Under SSP370, regional CO2 emissions decline the most on average, by 36.66 Mt CO2 in 2100, primarily due to reductions in heating demand. In SSP585, regional emission changes are smaller (3.43 Mt CO2 in 2050 and 3.93 Mt CO2 in 2100 on average), reflecting the offsetting effects of heating reductions versus the increase in fossil-powered cooling.

Major emitters such as the US, China, and Russia experience substantial net reductions in emissions, particularly in the residential and commercial sectors. For instance, under SSP585 in 2100, the US reduces net CO2 emissions by 197.6 Mt CO2 compared to the constant baseline, with 82 Mt CO2 from commercial and 174.4 Mt CO2 from residential heating, largely due to projected warmer winters. In contrast, emissions increase in lower-latitude regions, especially Southeast Asia, the Middle East, and South Africa, where rising temperatures drive up cooling demand. Combined with carbon-intensive power systems, this results in net increases in emissions. This divergence underscores the importance of placing global climate impacts in a regional context that while warming reduces emissions in colder regions, it can raise them in warmer ones where electricity remains fossil-heavy.

Discussion

Our study shows that incorporating updated climate projections into the GCAM integrated assessment model substantially alters estimates of future heating and cooling demands. Using CMIP6-based temperature data, we find that long-term warming consistently increases cooling demand and reduces heating demand across most regions. These shifts, in turn, reshape energy consumption patterns, alter the structure of energy supply, and affect CO2 emissions across multiple sectors. While the global trend is clear and largely as expected, the magnitude and direction of these impacts vary widely by region and scenario, with particularly strong impacts in countries such as the US, China, and emerging economies in Southeast Asia and the Middle East. These findings highlight the importance of incorporating region-specific climate projections into long-term energy planning to avoid systematic under- or overestimation of future demand and to support more equitable, effective mitigation strategies.

As the global community works toward climate stabilization under the Paris Agreement, integrating future climate trajectories into long-term decarbonization planning is becoming increasingly urgent. While previous analysis has documented the sensitivity of heating and cooling energy demand to long-term warming40, our study goes further by examining the implications for climate change mitigation. Critically, climate-driven shifts in temperature-sensitive demand impose uneven mitigation pressures across sectors and regions, shaping how countries prioritize investment, policy, and technology deployment. Though we focus on stylized SSP–RCP scenarios, our results offer insights: countries facing surging cooling demand may need to rapidly scale up efficient cooling technologies, strengthen building energy codes, and expand access to low-carbon electricity. In parallel, emerging technologies, such as high-efficiency heat pumps, super-insulating glazing, and radiative-cooling building materials, could substantially reduce the energy intensity of future building services41–43. Meanwhile, regions with falling heating needs can prioritize improving plug-load efficiency and facilitating a smooth transition away from fossil-based heating systems.

Our findings also reveal how climate feedbacks interact with socioeconomic development trajectories. For example, SSP126 reflects a sustainable development path with moderate climate impacts, leading to milder shifts in energy use. In contrast, SSP585 combines rapid economic growth with persistent fossil fuel use and high emissions, intensifying the climate-energy feedback and prompting a more carbon-intensive response. These contrasting patterns underscore the importance of aligning mitigation and adaptation strategies across both sectors and scales.

Despite these insights, our study has four main limitations. First, it focuses exclusively on daily average temperatures via HDD and CDD, omitting other important climate stressors such as temperature extremes, humidity, solar radiation, wind speed, and precipitation. These factors can also notably influence energy demand and supply11,44,45, often through compound and cross-sector interactions46–48. Also, GCAM framework operates on an annual time scale, calculating service demands and clearing the electricity market based on annual aggregates. So we fail to capture the impacts of hourly or seasonal temperature on energy shifts. Specifically, omitting intraday demand peaks during extreme heatwaves ignores the dispatch of carbon-intensive peaking power plants. This failure to capture elevated marginal emission factors likely underestimates emissions, potentially overestimating the net CO2 emission reductions projected in high-warming scenarios (e.g., SSP585)14. Second, the analysis relies on GCAM 32-region framework, which lacks fine-grained detail on building stock characteristics and behavioral responses. Similar to many other IAMs, some key drivers in GCAM, such as building envelope efficiency improvements and floor-space expansion, are treated as exogenous assumptions driven by income and regional parameters. Since we aim to isolate the marginal impact of warming trends, these exogenous variables do not undermine the validity of our results regarding climate-driven energy changes34. Emerging hybrid modeling frameworks, such as MESSAGEix-Buildings with the CHILLED model, integrate gridded climate data, detailed building characteristics, and behavioral factors32,49 to offer more realistic building energy demand projections. Third, GCAM does not explicitly capture adaptive behaviors, such as thermostat adjustments, occupancy shifts, or adoption of passive cooling, which could dampen or delay demand responses. Fourth, while our study employed 32 scenarios to explore socio-economic pathways and policy dimensions, the uncertainty associated with the input climatic data remains a critical factor. CMIP6 projections contain inherent uncertainties arising from inter-model spread and internal climate variability50, which cannot be fully resolved within the scope of a single impact assessment. Consequently, our results should be interpreted as conditional on the current range of CMIP6 climate sensitivity. Also, structural uncertainties inherent to the IAMs are unavoidable. Recent studies have demonstrated the value of developing comprehensive scenario matrices to better understand these complexities51,52.

To improve the policy relevance of future assessments, future work could be enhanced with empirical and econometric evidence at high spatial and temporal resolution. Calibrating building-sector modules with empirically derived elasticities53–55 and adaptive response functions56,57 can help embed behavioral and regional heterogeneity into long-term scenario design. As climate impacts grow and energy systems become more decentralized, incorporating these dynamic feedbacks will be essential to designing regionally tailored and resilient mitigation and adaptation strategies.

Methods

We employ GCAM v7.158 to evaluate the impacts of long-term climate change on building energy demand and associated cross-sector dynamics. Our analysis includes three main steps: (1) processing climate data to calculate updated HDD/CDD; (2) integrating these updated HDD/CDD data into GCAM; and (3) assessing the resulting changes in energy use and CO2 emissions.

HDD and CDD calculation

First, we derive HDD and CDD from daily mean temperature data produced by CMIP6 global climate models. We use temperature data from the ISIMIP3b website59, which provides bias-corrected CMIP6 climate forcing for SSP1-RCP2.6, SSP3-RCP7.0, and SSP5-RCP8.5 conditions. For SSP2–RCP4.5, bias-corrected daily temperature fields are not yet available from ISIMIP3b. To ensure consistency across scenarios, we therefore performed the bias adjustment ourselves on our high-performance computing platform, using the BASD (Bias Adjustment and Statistical Downscaling) workflow implemented in the JGCRI climate_integration_metarepo60. In brief, we derived transfer functions between raw CMIP6 daily near-surface air temperature and an observational reference over a historical calibration period, and then applied these functions to the SSP2–RCP4.5 simulations to obtain bias-corrected daily temperatures at 0.5° × 0.5° resolution, harmonized with the ISIMIP3b products.

Specifically, we first prepare input data by extracting daily tas from the raw CMIP6 SSP2–RCP4.5 simulations61 and an observational reference dataset consistent with the ISIMIP3b setup, namely W5E5 v2.0. W5E5 v2.0 is a global gridded meteorological dataset at 0.5° spatial and daily temporal resolution, constructed by merging WFDE5 over land with ERA5 over the ocean. Second, we calibrate bias-adjustment transfer functions over a historical overlap period, matching the distribution of simulated daily tas to the observational reference using BASD’s trend-preserving quantile-mapping scheme for temperature (additive trend preservation in all quantiles). Third, we apply the calibrated transfer functions to the SSP2–RCP4.5 simulations to obtain bias-corrected daily tas at 0.5° × 0.5°, harmonized with the ISIMIP3b products for SSP1–RCP2.6, SSP3–RCP7.0, and SSP5–RCP8.5. All BASD runs for SSP2–RCP4.5 were executed on a high-performance computing platform using the default BASD parameter settings recommended for temperature, as documented in the BASD user guide. The resulting bias-corrected SSP2–RCP4.5 temperature fields were then used to compute HDD and CDD in exactly the same way as for the ISIMIP3b-provided scenarios.

CMIP6 daily mean temperature data are produced from global climate models as the daily-averaged near-surface air temperature simulated at 0.5° × 0.5° resolution by participating models37. The degree-days approach has long been employed to estimate building heating and cooling requirements. This technique rests on the premise that daily mean temperatures exceeding or falling below specific comfort levels reliably indicate human thermal discomfort and correlate with adaptive energy consumption38,62. Degree-days quantify this by summing deviations from a comfort temperature over time, thus capturing both the magnitude and duration of temperature departures34. When temperatures deviate from this comfort level, energy is typically used for heating or cooling to maintain indoor comfort.

We first compute daily HDD and CDD for each grid cell, then aggregate them to regional scales (Supplementary Table 5) using population or GDP as weighting factors, depending on the model structure, as shown in the equations (1–3). These processes are performed on the High-performance Computing Platform of Peking University using a modified version of the helios package63.

Building energy modeling in GCAM

Second, we update the default HDD/CDD assumptions in GCAM with the updated climate pathways. GCAM models building energy demand using a service-based structure that links energy use to factors such as income, technology, and climate12,64–67. By integrating the updated HDD/CDD inputs, GCAM captures the influence of long-term temperature change on both residential and commercial building energy demand, while maintaining internal consistency across sectors and regions. The evolution of building energy use in GCAM is determined by three drivers: (1) total floorspace, (2) service intensity per unit of floorspace, and (3) fuel and technology choices by consumers, driven by fuel and non-fuel costs, share weights and logit parameters43,44.

This study includes 32 scenario combinations, derived from four climate models (GFDL-ESM4, IPSL-CM6A-LR, MPI-ESM1-2-LR, MRI-ESM2-0), four socio-economic and emissions pathways (SSP1-RCP2.6, SSP3-RCP7.0, SSP2-RCP4.5, and SSP5-RCP8.5), and two weighting methods (population and GDP) (Details of model selection and scenario construction are provided in Supplementary Methods and Supplementary Tables 2–4, population and GDP distributions are shown in Supplementary Figs. 1, 2). Our main results are based on the GFDL-ESM4 climate model, the four socio-economic and emissions pathways, and population-weighting. Results from the other three climate models and GDP weighting are shown in Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7, which exhibit broadly consistent trends. However, calculating HDD/CDD using historical temperature data while neglecting recent global warming over the past 30 years induces bias in estimating impacts of climate change on building cooling and heating energy demand (Supplementary Fig. 8). The average global gap in building cooling and heating energy demand in the same year is 3.90EJ and 2.21EJ, respectively, when comparing SSP370 to the A2 scenario, the two most comparable baselines. The maximum gap reaches 8.60 EJ and 3.72 EJ, respectively. Similarly, the differences between SSP126 and the B1 scenario average 1.52 EJ (cooling) and 2.05 EJ (heating), with maximum gaps of 2.69 EJ and 3.68 EJ, respectively.

In GCAM, service demand (i.e., service intensity per unit of floorspace) is further influenced by historical usage patterns, income-driven affordability, and thermal load, the latter of which is directly linked to HDD and CDD. By explicitly incorporating degree-day metrics, GCAM captures the sensitivity of heating and cooling demands and the associated energy implications to climate conditions. Each energy service can be met by multiple technologies, each using different fuels depending on cost, preferences, and availability11. Scenario design and outcome analysis

Finally, we assess how climate-driven shifts in heating and cooling demand affect energy use and CO2 emissions. Because such shifts influence fuel choices, electricity generation, and mitigation responses, we extend our analysis to cross-sector dynamics in GCAM, including global trends and regional disparities under long-term warming.

Our scenario analysis centers on four commonly used RCP–SSP combinations that span a wide range of emissions pathways and socioeconomic contexts. SSP126 represents a sustainable socioeconomic pathway aligned with strong mitigation action, where radiative forcing peaks at 2.6 W/m² by 2100, in line with limiting global warming to below 2 °C. SSP370 reflects a fragmented development pathway with slower technological progress, resulting in a forcing level of 7.0 W/m². SSP585 represents a fossil-fueled, high-growth scenario with minimal climate action, where radiative forcing reaches 8.5 W/m² by the end of the century. We compare these scenarios under two climate assumptions: (1) constant HDD/CDD (constant scenario), and (2) CMIP6-updated HDD/CDD (CMIP scenario).
