Nonvalvular Papillary Fibroelastoma of the Left Ventricle: A Case Report and Review of the Literature
Authors: Aibek E. Mirrakhimov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Papillary fibroelastoma (PFE) is a benign primary cardiac tumor that most commonly arises from valvular endocardium, whereas nonvalvular involvement, particularly within the left ventricle, is rare. Despite benign histology, PFEs are clinically relevant due to mobility and embolic risk. We report a case of a 64‐year‐old woman in whom a small nonvalvular left ventricular PFE was identified during evaluation for ventricular arrhythmias. Cardiac imaging demonstrated a mobile mass attached to the apical inferior left ventricular wall, which was successfully resected surgically under cardiopulmonary bypass with pathologic confirmation of PFE. We additionally performed a focused review of published cases of nonvalvular left ventricular PFEs, including lesions arising from the subvalvular mitral apparatus. The literature demonstrates heterogeneous clinical presentation, diverse anatomic attachment sites, and a strong preference for surgical management. This case and review highlight the importance of considering PFE in the differential diagnosis of left ventricular masses and support surgical excision for mobile nonvalvular lesions.

1. Introduction

Papillary fibroelastoma (PFE) is a relatively common benign primary cardiac tumor and represents the most frequent tumor of the cardiac valves [1]. In contrast, nonvalvular PFEs are distinctly uncommon and comprise a small minority of reported cases, with the left ventricle (LV) representing an especially rare site of origin [2–11]. Despite benign histopathology, PFEs are clinically significant because of their marked mobility and associated risk of systemic embolization, which may result in stroke, myocardial infarction, or sudden cardiac death.

The widespread use of advanced cardiac imaging modalities—particularly echocardiography and cardiac computed tomography (CT)—has increased incidental detection of small intracardiac masses; however, optimal management strategies for nonvalvular PFEs remain incompletely defined due to limited case‐level data. Surgical resection is generally favored in symptomatic patients or in the presence of high‐risk features. Here, we report a case of a nonvalvular LV PFE identified during evaluation for ventricular arrhythmias and successfully treated with surgical excision, and we review the existing literature on LV PFEs.

2. Case Report

A 64‐year‐old woman with hypertension and Type 2 diabetes mellitus underwent cardiac evaluation for symptomatic palpitations, frequent premature ventricular contractions, and episodic nonsustained ventricular tachycardia. Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging and CT identified an approximately 7‐ to 1‐cm LV mass (see Figures 1 and 2). She had no history of stroke, transient ischemic attack, or peripheral embolic events at presentation.

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, two‐chamber long‐axis view, demonstrating an approximately 1‐cm hypermobile abnormality (red arrow) in the mid‐to‐apical left ventricle.

Axial cardiac computed tomography image demonstrating a small, 7‐mm spherical hypodense mass in the midleft ventricular cavity (red arrow).

The differential diagnosis included PFE, thrombus, vegetation, myxoma, Lambl excrescence, and primary or metastatic cardiac tumor. Thrombus was considered less likely because the lesion appeared discrete and pedunculated, without associated severe ventricular dysfunction or regional wall‐motion abnormality. Vegetation was also considered unlikely given the absence of fever, bacteremia, valvular involvement, or other clinical features of infective endocarditis. Given the lesion′s left‐sided location, mobility, and potential embolic risk, surgical excision was recommended after multidisciplinary discussion and shared decision‐making with the patient. A direct causal relationship between the mass and the patient′s ventricular arrhythmias could not be established.

After induction of general anesthesia and placement of invasive monitoring, intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) confirmed a mobile LV mass measuring approximately 1 cm, attached by a stalk to the apical inferior wall, with otherwise normal cardiac findings. These findings are demonstrated in Videos S1 and S2 and Figure 3.

Frozen transgastric midpapillary TEE view demonstrating papillary fibroelastoma diameters by planimetry.

The patient underwent surgical excision under cardiopulmonary bypass. After aortic cross‐clamping and cardioplegic arrest, the mass was accessed through a ventriculotomy and completely excised. She was weaned from cardiopulmonary bypass without difficulty. Postexcision TEE demonstrated no residual mass and no apparent surgical complications.

Gross pathology examination demonstrated a yellow‐tan, friable soft tissue fragment measuring 1.1 × 1.1 × 0.4 cm. Microscopy showed classic features of PFE, including complex, arborescent paucicellular cores covered by endocardial lining cells. No atypia or thrombus formation was identified. One fragment contained endomyocardium, consistent with a possible attachment site, and no valvar tissue was present.

No perioperative embolic event was documented. During the postoperative course, the patient continued to have occasional premature ventricular contractions and runs of nonsustained ventricular tachycardia. Relevant postdischarge rhythm follow‐up was not available; therefore, whether tumor resection reduced the burden of premature ventricular contractions or nonsustained ventricular tachycardia cannot be determined from this case. Given the relative frequency of these arrhythmias in clinical practice, it is also possible that they were unrelated to the PFE. The patient was discharged to a rehabilitation facility on postoperative day 11.

3. Search Strategy

A focused English‐language literature review was performed to identify published cases of nonvalvular PFE involving the LV. PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Google Scholar, and reference lists of relevant articles were searched from database inception through December 30, 2025. Search terms included combinations of “papillary fibroelastoma,” “cardiac papillary fibroelastoma,” “left ventricle,” “left ventricular,” “nonvalvular,” “left ventricular outflow tract,” “papillary muscle,” “chordae,” “mitral chordae,” and “subvalvular mitral apparatus.” Adult case reports, case series, and review articles were included if they described PFE arising from the LV cavity, LV wall, LV outflow tract, papillary muscles, or mitral chordal apparatus. Reports were excluded if lesions were confined to cardiac valves, involved only non‐LV sites, lacked sufficient anatomic detail, were unavailable in full text, or did not support a diagnosis of PFE. Duplicates were removed by comparing author names, publication year, patient characteristics, tumor location, and clinical presentation. Lesions involving the subvalvular mitral apparatus were included because the papillary muscles and chordae are integral LV structures and have similar diagnostic, embolic‐risk, and surgical implications.

4. Discussion

PFE is an uncommon benign primary cardiac tumor, accounting for approximately 5%–10% of all primary cardiac tumors and representing the most frequent tumor of the cardiac valves [1]. PFEs most commonly arise from valvular endocardium, particularly the aortic and mitral valves, whereas nonvalvular locations are distinctly uncommon [2–11]. Despite benign histopathology, PFEs are clinically significant because of their marked mobility and associated risk of systemic embolization, which may result in stroke, myocardial infarction, or sudden cardiac death.

The pathogenesis of PFE remains incompletely defined but appears more consistent with an acquired process than a true neoplasm. Proposed mechanisms include endothelial injury with subsequent microthrombus formation and abnormal organization of fibrin and elastic fibers in regions of elevated shear stress [1]. Histologically, PFEs are characterized by multiple avascular, frond‐like papillary projections composed of a dense central core of collagen and elastic tissue, surrounded by loose connective tissue and covered by endothelium. This distinctive architecture likely accounts for their marked mobility and embolic potential despite benign histology.

Although echocardiography remains the cornerstone of diagnostic imaging, CMR and cardiac CT may complement echocardiography in the evaluation of LV masses [12–15]. Cardiac CT provides high spatial resolution and can help define lesion location, attachment, and relationship to adjacent structures, whereas CMR provides tissue characterization that may help distinguish tumor from thrombus, myxoma, or vegetation. Thrombus typically lacks internal enhancement. Myxomas are usually larger, more heterogeneous, and often demonstrate variable enhancement related to vascularity, hemorrhage, calcification, or cystic degeneration. Vegetations are usually suspected in the appropriate clinical context, including fever, bacteremia, valvular destruction, or new regurgitation, and are typically attached to valve leaflets or prosthetic material rather than isolated LV endocardium. In contrast, PFE is typically small, highly mobile, pedunculated, and frond‐like, with variable late gadolinium enhancement related to fibroelastic tissue. CMR should therefore be viewed as adjunctive rather than definitive, particularly for small mobile lesions, for which histopathology remains the diagnostic standard.

Several registries and institutional series provide important context for the rarity of LV involvement. Klarich et al. [2] reviewed 54 patients with PFEs diagnosed by echocardiography with or without pathologic confirmation; only two tumors involved the subvalvular mitral apparatus (one papillary muscle and one chordae tendineae), nine arose in the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT), and one was attached to the lateral LV wall. Sun et al. [3] reported 162 patients with pathologically confirmed PFE, including 13 with LV involvement, although specific attachment sites were not described. Kurup et al. [4] described 12 cases of so‐called “iatrogenic” PFEs occurring a mean of 18 years after a potential inciting exposure such as prior cardiac surgery or thoracic radiation, suggesting that endothelial injury may contribute to tumor development and, in some cases, multifocality. Within this cohort, three lesions were located in the LVOT, two were attached to the interventricular septum, and one was reported in an unspecified LV location.

In a pooled review of 725 cases, Gowda et al. [5] identified 100 nonvalvular PFEs, including 55 located within the LV without further anatomic specification. Strecker et al. [7] analyzed 84 surgically treated cardiac tumors; six were PFEs, of which two involved the LV without detailed localization. Kurmann et al. [8] evaluated longitudinal growth patterns in 279 patients with pathology‐proven PFE and included one LVOT lesion, demonstrating a mean annual growth rate of 0.5 ± 0.9 mm. Alozie et al. [9] reported a 27‐year surgical experience comprising 21 PFEs in 16 patients, with two tumors located in the LV, although specific attachment sites were not provided. More recently, Neupane et al. [10] performed a systematic review of 161 PFE cases associated with cerebrovascular events and identified LV involvement across several sites, including papillary muscle (n = 5), LVOT/interventricular septum/apex (n = 19), and chordal mitral structures (n = 2). Mrvic et al. [11] reported 12 PFEs over 16 years at a single institution, including one LV case without specified anatomic detail.

Collectively, these data confirm that LV PFEs are substantially less common than valvular PFEs. Moreover, LV involvement is often not the primary focus of large registries, resulting in incomplete reporting of key lesion characteristics such as precise attachment site, tumor size, mobility, and presenting symptoms. To address this gap, we performed a focused review of published reports of nonvalvular LV PFEs, including lesions arising from the subvalvular mitral apparatus, with findings summarized in Table 1 [16–102]. Of note, pathologic confirmation was not available in one reported case [42].

Summary of nonvalvular PFE cases involving the left ventricle.

Across 91 reported patients included in Table 1, age was available for 89 and ranged from 26 to 85 years, with a mean age of 62.8 years (median: 66 years). Sex was reported for the same 89 patients, demonstrating a male predominance (62.9%) compared with female patients (37.1%). Age and sex were not provided in two reports [54, 83]. Our index patient—a 64‐year‐old woman—closely approximates the pooled mean age and aligns with the substantial minority of female presentations described in the literature.

For descriptive analysis, clinical presentation was assigned according to the primary trigger for imaging or diagnosis reported in each case. Embolic events included stroke, transient ischemic attack, coronary ischemia, peripheral arterial embolism, retinal ischemia, or other systemic embolic manifestations. Incidental cases included asymptomatic lesions or masses identified during unrelated evaluation, surveillance imaging, or preoperative assessment. When multiple symptoms were reported, the dominant presentation emphasized by the original report was used.

Incidental detection during evaluation for unrelated complaints, preoperative assessment, or surveillance imaging represented the most common reason for workup and diagnosis [17, 19, 22–26, 28, 30–32, 36, 38, 40–46, 48, 49, 52, 53, 57, 59, 60, 62, 64, 65, 67, 70, 72–76, 78, 80–82, 85, 86, 89, 93, 95–100]. The second most common clinical trigger for echocardiographic identification of LV PFE was an embolic or ischemic event, accounting for approximately one‐third of cases. These events included stroke or transient ischemic attack, coronary ischemia, limb or finger ischemia, and retinal ischemia [16, 18, 21, 27, 29, 33–35, 37, 39, 47, 50, 51, 55, 56, 58, 61, 63, 66, 68, 69, 71, 77, 79, 84, 87, 88, 90–92, 94, 101].

Symptom‐driven presentations without clear embolic syndromes included dyspnea or heart failure syndromes [17, 19, 24, 25, 28, 42, 78, 81, 82, 96, 97] and chest pain and discomfort syndromes [30, 32, 43, 44, 46, 49, 59, 64, 85, 95, 99, 102], whereas arrhythmia‐ or palpitation‐based evaluations were less frequent but clinically relevant [20, 22, 30, 31, 36, 82, 86, 97, 102]. Our case is notable in that the lesion was identified during evaluation for palpitations with frequent ventricular ectopy and nonsustained ventricular tachycardia, consistent with several reports in which PFEs were detected during rhythm evaluation rather than following embolic events [20, 30, 36, 86, 102]. Tumor location was categorized according to the reported anatomic attachment site. Lesions involving papillary muscles, chordae tendineae, or the mitral chordal apparatus were classified as subvalvular mitral apparatus. Lesions described within the LVOT or attached to the outflow septum were classified as LVOT. Lesions attached to the apical, septal, inferior, lateral, anterior, or other ventricular wall/cavity sites were classified as LV wall/cavity lesions.

A key feature of this review is the inclusion of PFEs attached to the subvalvular mitral valve apparatus, including papillary muscles and chordae tendineae, which represent a meaningful subset of reported nonvalvular LV PFEs. When lesions were categorized by reported attachment site, three principal anatomic patterns emerged: involvement of the subvalvular mitral apparatus, lesions arising from the LVOT, and tumors originating from the LV walls or cavity. Subvalvular mitral valve apparatus involvement was frequently described, with tumors attached to anterolateral, posteromedial, anterior, or posterior papillary muscles, as well as chordal structures [16, 21–23, 32, 33, 40, 44, 46, 54–56, 60, 67–69, 71, 74, 78, 85, 88, 89, 91, 94–96, 100]. Several reports lacked tumor size measurements despite describing chordal attachment [23, 68, 69], and one described multifocal involvement of the mitral valve complex without complete quantitative detail [54]. Clinically, this subvalvular distribution is relevant because lesions may be highly mobile and emboligenic and may require transmitral or transaortic surgical approaches depending on operative context.

PFEs involving the LVOT were also reported, sometimes with hemodynamic consequences when lesions were large or associated with adjacent tumors [17, 24, 25, 27, 29, 35, 38, 43, 45, 47, 53, 64, 77, 90]. Giant or bulky LVOT PFEs were described with obstructive physiology or multiple adjacent lesions [29, 35, 39]. Notably, some LVOT cases did not report tumor size [77]. Given proximity to the aortic valve and potential for outflow obstruction or embolization, this anatomic site may carry heightened clinical concern.

Many PFEs arose from the LV walls and cavity, including apical, inferior, lateral, anterior, septal, and anteroseptal regions [18–20, 28, 30, 34, 36, 37, 39, 41, 42, 48–55, 57–59, 65, 66, 70, 72, 73, 75, 82, 84, 87, 92, 97–99, 101, 102]. Our lesion originated from the apical inferior wall, mirroring the recurrent observation that the LV apex and adjacent wall segments are common nonvalvular sites. This distribution may reflect local flow conditions, shear stress, and endothelial microinjury as proposed mechanisms for PFE development, although definitive causality cannot be inferred from case‐level data.

Among cases reporting tumor dimensions, the maximum size ranged from approximately 0.7 to 6.0 cm, with many lesions clustering around 1–2 cm. Several reports lacked numeric size data, particularly for chordal or subvalvular lesions and recurrent tumors [23, 68, 69, 74, 77, 79, 83], and one report described multiple masses without measurement [54]. In our patient, the mass measured approximately 7 mm on cardiac CT and 1.0 cm on intraoperative TEE, placing it at the lower end of the reported size spectrum yet still clinically relevant given its mobility.

Management across published reports was overwhelmingly surgical, reflecting both perceived embolic risk and referral or publication bias. Nearly all patients underwent surgical excision, whereas medical management or observation was described in only two cases: One patient declined surgery and was managed with anticoagulation and surveillance without complications over 4 years [42], and another declined surgery despite embolic presentation, with subsequent imaging demonstrating persistent lesions [50]. One report did not provide management or outcome data [54]. Collectively, these observations suggest that when a mobile LV mass is suspected to represent PFE, particularly in the setting of embolic symptoms, surgical excision is commonly pursued.

Given the limited evidence base, management of suspected nonvalvular LV PFE should be individualized rather than protocolized. Figure 6 provides a practical diagnostic and therapeutic approach to a suspected nonvalvular LV mass. Initial evaluation should define mobility, attachment site, size, embolic or obstructive symptoms, arrhythmia context, and imaging features suggesting thrombus, vegetation, myxoma, or malignancy. Surgical excision is generally favored for left‐sided mobile or pedunculated lesions, symptomatic lesions, lesions associated with embolic events, or masses with uncertain diagnosis when operative risk is acceptable. Conservative management with surveillance imaging, with or without antiplatelet therapy, may be reasonable for selected small, immobile, asymptomatic lesions or patients with prohibitive surgical risk, although supporting evidence is limited.

Clinical triage pathway for suspected nonvalvular left ventricular masses.

Our case aligns with this prevailing approach. The lesion was mobile, nonvalvular, and identified during arrhythmia evaluation, prompting surgical excision under cardiopulmonary bypass with intraoperative TEE confirmation of complete removal and no immediate complications. The operative strategy required ventriculotomy to access an apical inferior wall tumor—an approach that may be necessary for select apical or deep mural attachments but introduces theoretical risks such as ventricular dysfunction, arrhythmogenic substrate, or bleeding. Because published cases inconsistently report operative access routes and myocardial protection strategies, meaningful comparison of surgical techniques is not possible. Nonetheless, the predominance of surgical management in the literature likely supports resection as the dominant real‐world strategy for these lesions when mobility, symptoms, or embolic history are present.

In our patient, an apical ventriculotomy was used because the mass was attached to the apical inferior LV wall. This approach provides direct exposure for deep apical or mural lesions but may theoretically carry long‐term risks, including ventricular scar formation, regional wall‐motion abnormality, bleeding, or scar‐related ventricular arrhythmias. Alternative approaches, including transaortic, transmitral, thoracoscopic, or video‐assisted techniques, have been described for selected LV PFEs and may reduce the need for direct ventriculotomy when tumor location and exposure permit [54, 58, 79]. However, the optimal approach is individualized according to tumor attachment site, mobility, surgical exposure, and the need for complete excision.

Although this patient presented with premature ventricular contractions and nonsustained ventricular tachycardia, the arrhythmogenic role of LV PFE remains uncertain. Several reported LV PFEs have been discovered during evaluation for palpitations, ventricular ectopy, atrial arrhythmia, or chest pain with palpitations [18, 26, 32, 78, 82, 98]. These reports support a possible association, but not causality. Mechanistically, a highly mobile apical LV mass could theoretically produce repetitive mechanical contact, analogous to a “whiplash effect,” on adjacent endocardium, myocardium, or Purkinje‐rich subendocardial tissue. This concept is supported indirectly by mechanoelectric coupling literature showing that acute mechanical stimulation, local tissue deformation, and stretch‐activated currents can trigger ectopy [99, 100]. Purkinje fibers may also participate in mechanically induced ventricular arrhythmias. However, this mechanism has not been directly proven for PFE. In our case, ventricular ectopy and runs of nonsustained ventricular tachycardia persisted during hospitalization, and postdischarge rhythm monitoring was unavailable. Therefore, the mass may have been associated with arrhythmia‐driven discovery, but causality cannot be inferred from the current literature.

This review has important limitations. The evidence base consists predominantly of case reports and small case series, which are subject to publication bias, incomplete reporting, and preferential publication of surgically managed or clinically dramatic cases. Tumor mobility, size, attachment site, embolic history, operative approach, and follow‐up duration were inconsistently reported. As a result, pooled estimates from these reports should be interpreted descriptively rather than as true incidence, risk, or treatment‐effect estimates. Although surgical excision was the dominant management strategy among published cases, this likely reflects selection and reporting bias and should not be interpreted as definitive evidence that all nonvalvular LV PFEs require resection.

In summary, nonvalvular LV PFEs affect predominantly older adults with a male skew but occur across a wide adult age range, including younger patients with embolic stroke. Clinical presentation is heterogeneous, with embolic events and incidental detection accounting for most diagnoses, whereas arrhythmia‐driven discovery as in our case is less common but well described. Lesions arise from distinct anatomic clusters, including subvalvular mitral structures, the LVOT, and LV walls or apex, each with potential implications for surgical access. Although tumor size varies widely, even small mobile lesions may be clinically consequential, reinforcing why most reported cases proceeded to surgical excision.

5. Conclusion

Nonvalvular LV PFEs are rare but clinically relevant because mobility and left‐sided location may confer embolic risk. This case highlights the role of multimodality imaging and intraoperative TEE in diagnosis, surgical planning, and confirmation of complete resection. Published cases suggest heterogeneous presentation, frequent involvement of the subvalvular mitral apparatus or ventricular wall, and a strong reported preference for surgical excision. However, available evidence is derived mainly from case reports and small series, with substantial publication and selection bias. Management should therefore be individualized according to symptoms, mobility, attachment site, embolic history, operative risk, and patient preference.
