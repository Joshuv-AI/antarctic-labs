Identification of long non-coding RNAs involved in leukemogenesis and venetoclax response in acute myeloid leukemia through functional CRISPR-dCas9 interference screens
Authors: Elisabetta Cozzi, Anne Neddermeyer, Sophia Miliara, Xiangfu Zhong, Tyler Weirick, Nona Struyf, Tom Erkers, Chung Chau Hon, Andreas Lennartsson, Sören Lehmann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a malignant hematologic disease with poor prognosis, and improved understanding of its biology is critical for patient outcomes. While protein-coding genes in AML are well characterized, the role of long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) remains largely unexplored. Here, we investigated how lncRNAs contribute to AML biology and treatment resistance. Three high-throughput lncRNA-CRISPR-interference (CRISPRi) screens were performed in the AML cell line MOLM-13, targeting 7996 lncRNAs expressed in hematopoietic cells, with knockdowns directed to transcription start sites defined through cap analysis of gene expression (CAGE) sequencing. Effects on proliferation, differentiation, and response to the Bcl-2 inhibitor venetoclax were assessed. In total, 58, 4, and 23 lncRNAs were found to affect proliferation, differentiation, and venetoclax sensitivity, respectively. Three proliferation-associated lncRNAs (MIR17HG, CATG00000106133.1, and CATG00000056792.1) and one venetoclax-associated lncRNA (AC009299.3) were further investigated. CATG00000106133.1 was enriched in de novo and cytogenetically normal AML and correlated with NPM1 and IDH2 mutations. Gene Ontology and Reactome analyses of RNA-seq from CATG00000106133.1 knockout cells revealed roles in cytokine signaling and immune pathways. AC009299.3, linked to venetoclax response, was associated with poor outcome, adverse risk, and increased age in AML patients. Collectively, this study identifies four lncRNAs implicated in key leukemogenic processes, highlighting their potential for understanding AML biology, prognosis, and therapeutic response.Keywords: Acute myeloid leukemia (AML), Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), CRISPR interference screens, Proliferation, Differentiation, Venetoclax

Introduction

AML is a heterogeneous hematopoietic disease with dramatically increasing incidence at ages over 60 [1]. The disease is characterized by the abnormal expansion of myeloid progenitors that accumulate in hematopoietic tissues due to a block in the maturation of cells, impeding the production of normal blood and bone marrow cells [2]. AML has a poor prognosis, and the overall survival rate remains low, especially in older patients [3]. For this reason, there is an urgent need for newer and more effective therapies as well as for new biomarkers that can guide treatment choices for patients. To succeed in this, a better understanding of the pathophysiology of this disease is needed. Genetic aberrations associated with the leukemogenesis process in the protein-coding part of the genome have been well characterized in AML during the last two decades [4]. However, with the exception of some rare genetic subtypes, this has not led to a significant improvement in overall survival for the AML patients.

Venetoclax, a Bcl-2 inhibitor approved for treatment of AML in patients not eligible for intensive chemotherapy represents one of the most important recent additions to the therapeutic arsenal [5,6]. However, despite its clinical impact, the mechanisms underlying resistance and sensitivity to venetoclax remain poorly understood, highlighting how the biology of AML is still only partially resolved. Genetic aberrations associated with leukemogenesis in the protein-coding part of the genome have been well characterized during the last two decades [7]. However, the limited improvement in patient outcomes indicates that these coding mutations alone cannot fully account for the mechanisms that initiate, sustain, and propel AML progression, highlighting the need to investigate additional regulatory layers, including those mediated by non-coding RNAs. The development of advanced techniques such as RNA-sequencing and Capped Analyses of Expression (CAGE) [8] has shown that over 90% of the human Genome transcribes for non-coding RNAs, which can be intergenic, antisense, sense, intronic and bidirectional [9,10]. Non-coding RNAs are classified as long non-coding RNAs (>200-500 nt) and short non-coding RNAs (<200 nt) according to their length [11,12]. In particular, long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), which are mainly localized in the nucleus [[13], [14], [15]], have been shown to strongly influence gene expression at the epigenetic, transcriptional and post-transcriptional level [16,17] and their dysregulation results in the promotion and maintenance of a variety of cancer types, including hematopoietic diseases such as AML [18,19]. Expression signatures of lncRNAs have previously been demonstrated to correlate with specific molecular and cytogenetic subtypes of AML as well as to defined prognostic outcomes [20]. However, the function of lncRNAs and how they contribute to leukemogenesis is not well understood.

In this study, 7996 lncRNAs known to be expressed in hematopoietic cells were knocked down by three CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) screens in order to identify lncRNAs that are functionally implicated in AML. The results from these screens, followed by validation analyses, have enabled to enlarge the knowledge on perturbed lncRNA genes in AML. As such, this can create new avenues for further studies on the role of lncRNAs for leukemogenesis and their possible role as potential novel therapeutic targets and biomarkers.

Materials and methods

Generation of CRISPRi cell line

A plasmid containing dCas9-BFP-KRAB was introduced into the AML cell line MOLM-13 by lentiviral transduction. Successful transduction was confirmed by BFP expression using flow cytometry. The BFP-positive population was sorted four times by fluorescent-activated cell sorting (FACS), in order to enrich for cells with stable BFP expression and confirmed again by FACS prior to library transduction.

LncRNA-CRISPRi library design

The transcriptome of lncRNA in AML cells was selected by extracting lncRNAs expressed (expression ≥1 TPM) in any of the following AML cell lines: Kasumi-1, Kasumi-3, HL60, NOMO-1, THP1, U937, K562 and KG1. Transcription was based on CAGE sequencing data obtained from the FANTOM5 lncRNA atlas [21]. LncRNAs within ±2 kb of an mRNA transcription start site (TSS) were excluded. The final set contained about 8000 lncRNA genes that were targeted during the CRISPR-Cas9-KRAB screen using six single guide RNAs (sgRNAs or gRNAs) per lncRNA. gRNAs were designed within a region spanning 25 nucleotides upstream and 250 nucleotides downstream of the major CAGE peaks of each lncRNA. In addition, the gRNA library included 900 gRNAs (150 genes x 6 gRNAs) targeting negative control genes (genes not involved in the tested assays) and 900 non-target (scramble) gRNA controls, controlling for unspecific targets. Additionally, positive controls were included with known involvement in either proliferation, differentiation or venetoclax response. These comprised of six gRNAs targeting TSS of TP53 (gene ID: 7157), BCL-2 (gene ID: 596), CEBPB (gene ID: 1051), SPI1 (gene ID: 6688) and RUNX1 (gene ID: 861). A list of all targeted lncRNAs can be found in Supplementary Table 1.

LncRNA-CRISPRi library cloning

The lncRNA-CRISPRi library was synthesized in pool (CustomArray, Genscript), double-stranded by PCR to include an A-U flip and a stem extension in the tracrRNA [22], ten-nucleotide-long random Unique Molecular Identifiers (UMI), and an i7 sequencing primer binding site. The resulting PCR product with the sequence ggctttatatatcttgtggaaaggacgaaacaccgnnnnnnnnnnnnnnnnnnngtttaagagctagaaatagcaagtttaaataaggctagtccgttatcaacttgaaaaagtggcaccgagtcggtgcttttttGATCGGAAGAGCACACGTCTGAACTCCAGTCACNNNNNNNNNNaagcttggcgtaactagatcttgagacaaa was cloned by Gibson assembly into the lentiviral vector pLenti-Puro-AU-flip-3xBsmBI. The plasmid library was transformed into E. coli, followed by plasmid extraction and sequencing by NGS to confirm correct guide representation. The validated plasmid library was then packaged into lentivirus [23].

Lentiviral titration and library transduction

Functional titers of the lncRNA-CRISPRi library in the dCas9-BFP-KRAB cell lines were determined by serial dilution of the virus, spinfection at 900x g, 50 min, 30 °C in the presence of 8 μg/mL polybrene, and subsequent puromycin selection (2 μg/mL). dCas9-BFP-KRAB-expressing cells were transduced in duplicate with the lncRNA-CRISPRi library at an approximate MOI of 0.4 and a coverage of 1000 cells per guide, followed by puromycin selection from day 2 post-transduction to day 6 post-transduction. Cell numbers per replicate were kept at minimally 80 × 106 cells, and cells were grown in 50% conditioned medium. After four days of puromycin selection, the cells were divided into three different experimental arms per replicate.

Proliferation screen

For the proliferation screen, lentiviral-transduced MOLM-13-dCas9-BFP cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium with GlutaMAX (Thermo Scientific) and supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 1 mM sodium pyruvate (all Thermo Scientific). All cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2 and subcultured every second to third day for a total of 7 passages (20 days). Subculturing was done by gentle but thoroughly mixing of each replicate before taking an aliquot of cells and supplementing it with fresh pre-warmed medium. Replicates were maintained at > 50 × 106 cells at all times. Viability was determined prior to each subculturing by hemacytometer and trypan blue exclusion. The percentage of BFP expression was monitored at each subculturing event by flow cytometry analysis (BC CytoFlex, Beckman Coulter) using MOLM-13-dCas9 cells as unstained (BFP−) controls. Flow cytometry data were analyzed using FlowJo v10.6.1. Cell aliquots of at least 2.5 × 107 cells were collected for genomic DNA extraction and gRNA-sequencing to ensure sufficient library coverage. Aliquots were sampled from two replicates per cell line at two different time points during the proliferation screen: a start time point, and end time point (passage 7 after 21 days). Start time point was collected as bulk cell populations of at least 5.5 × 107 cells with BFP + percentage >60%. End time point was collected as BFP+ FACS sorted cells (BD Melody) (Molm-13: 5.37 × 107 cells). All cell aliquots were stored as dry pellets at −20 °C until further analysis.

Differentiation screen

For the differentiation screen, lentiviral transduced MOLM-13-dCas9-BFP cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium with GlutaMAX (Thermo Scientific) and supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 1 mM sodium pyruvate (all Thermo Scientific). Optimal time-point and drug concentration of all trans-retinoic acid (ATRA) were determined in dose- and time-varying assays. Each of two replicates of MOLM-13 were treated with 10 nM ATRA or vehicle control (DMSO, 0.01% v/v) for 72 h at 1 × 106 cells/mL. Cells were harvested, counted, and diluted in 1 × 107 cells/mL in FACS buffer and stained for CD11b cell surface marker.

Venetoclax screen

For the venetoclax screen, lentiviral transduced MOLM-13-dCas9-BFP cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium with GlutaMAX (Thermo Scientific) and supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 1 mM sodium pyruvate (all Thermo Scientific). Cells (1 × 106 cells/mL) were treated with venetoclax (ABT-199, Bcl-2-inhibitor, Abcam) at the calculated IC50 concentration or DMSO control (0.001/v/v) for 48 h. IC50 for venetoclax for each cell line was determined by standard IC50 assay in triplicate. Cells were harvested, washed, counted by trypan blue exclusion method and diluted in FACS media (DPBS + 30% FCS) at 1 × 106 cells/mL. Addition of 7-ADD ensured that only the live cells were sorted.

Flow cytometry

For the differentiation screens, BFP+ MOLM-13 cells were stained and sorted for granulocytic differentiation marker CD11b. A final BFP sort was also done for the venetoclax screen, and the last time point for the proliferation screen to enrich for cells with high dCas9-BFP-KRAB expression. Cells were sorted using either a Sony SH800 Cell Sorter (Analysis Software v2.1.5.0) or a Becton, Dickinson and Company FACSMelody Cell Sorter (Analysis Software BD FACSChorus v2). Sorted cells were centrifuged and cell pellets were stored at −20 °C prior to genomic DNA isolation.

Genomic DNA, library preparation and NGS sequencing

Genomic DNA was isolated using the QIAamp DNA Blood Maxi kit (Qiagen), and guide sequences were amplified by a 3-step PCR protocol as previously published [23]. Raw sequencing data were obtained by sequencing on Illumina NovaSeq X. Data were demultiplexed based on sample-specific barcodes. The resulting processed reads were mapped to the custom lncRNA sgRNA library using the MAGeCK (v0.5.6) mageck count command [24] with default parameters. This step generated a count matrix representing the abundance of each sgRNA across samples. Significantly enriched or depleted lncRNAs were identified with the MAGeCK-RRA (Robust Rank Aggregation) algorithm, normalizing to total read count per sample. This algorithm first ranks sgRNAs based on their p-values calculated from a negative binomial model and then aggregates these ranks to the gene level to identify robustly selected candidates. For each screen, the top 100 genes associated with the most significantly depleted sgRNAs and the top 100 associated with the most significantly enriched sgRNAs, ranked by RRA score, were selected for downstream analysis.

Individual validation of sgRNAs phenotypes

The single top-scoring sgRNAs targeting CATG00000106133.1, CATG00000056792.1, MIR17HG, AC009299.3 and TP53 were individually cloned into pLenti-Puro-AU-flip-3xBsmBI vector. Similarly, a non-targeting control sgRNA (NTC1) was cloned in the same vector mentioned above. sgRNAs sequences are reported in Supplementary Table 2.

After vector sequences were confirmed with Sanger sequencing, each plasmid construct was transfected into HEK293T cells with packaging vectors (psPAX2; pCMV-VSV-G) using the FuGENE HD transfection reagent (Promega) as indicated in the manufacturer's protocol. After 48 of incubation, viral supernatant was collected and stored at −80 °C. Subsequently, MOLM-13-dCas9-BFP cells, supplemented with 8 μg/mL of polybrene, were spin-infected with viral supernatant at 900x g for 50 min. Infected cells were selected with puromycin (2 μg/mL).

For the proliferation screen validation, a competition assay was performed where BFP levels, corresponding to expressing dCas9 and sgRNAs cells, were evaluated, through flow cytometry, between 5,14, and 21 days post transduction.

For the venetoclax screen validation, sgRNA-expressing cells were treated for 48 h with either venetoclax (ABT-199, Bcl-2 inhibitor, Abcam), at a concentration of 30 nM or DMSO control (0.001/v/v). Subsequently, cells were stained with Annexin V-FITC/PI detection kit (Abcam) as the manufacturer's protocol. Viable cells were detected through FACS analyses. Experiments were conducted in biological duplicates from the infection part.

Primary AML cells and AML cohorts

Primary AML cells and normal CD34+ bone marrow cells were obtained after informed consent and in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the regional ethical review board in Stockholm. In summary, all patient samples were collected from either bone marrow (BM) or peripheral blood (PB) of newly diagnosed treatment-naive AML patients. Mononuclear cells were isolated from diagnostic samples using Ficoll-Paque density-gradient centrifugation (GE Healthcare) and cryopreserved. Normal bone marrow (NBM) cells from healthy donors were extracted and CD34+ cells were enriched via magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS) using positive selection (Miltenyi Biotec). The AML cohorts analyzed include the ClinSeq-AML cohort (n = 325) and the Knut and Alice Wallenberg (KAW) cohort (103 AML and 11 NBM). Detailed information about the patient cohorts can be found in the Supplementary Table 3.

AML cell lines

HL60, NB4, OCI-AML3, OCI-AML2, MOLM-13, F36P, THP1 and Kasumi-1 cell lines used in this study were purchased from the German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures (DSMZ). KG1 and U937 cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). IMS-M2 cells were kindly provided by Prof. B. Falini.

AML cell lines HL60, NB4, U937, IMS-M2, F36P and THP1 were cultured in RPMI-1640 with GlutaMAX and HEPES (Gibco) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS, Thermo Scientific), while MOLM-13 and Kasumi1 cells were cultured in medium supplemented with 20% FBS. KG1 were cultured in IMDM medium with GlutaMAX™ (Gibco) supplemented with 20% FBS and OCI-AML2 and OCI-AML3 were cultured in α-MEM (Gibco) supplemented with 20% FBS. All cell lines were mycoplasma-free and grown in a humidified incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2.

CAGE-sequencing data

CAGE-seq data were obtained from the publicly available dataset of the Functional Annotation of the Mammalian Genome (FANTOM) 5 project (RIKEN, Japan, FANTOM5 Data). These data were generated using the hCAGE technique, which employs 5 μg of total RNA following the standard CAGE protocol. The count of CAGE tags with the same transcription start site, also known as a CAGE tag starting site (CTSS), serves as a proxy for transcript expression levels [25]. The details of the CAGE-seq samples utilized are provided in Supplementary Table 4.

Gene expression analyses by RT-qPCR

RNA was isolated using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer's instructions. The RNA was eluted in nuclease-free water, and its concentration was determined using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer. The RNA was then reverse transcribed using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit with RNase Inhibitor (Applied Biosystems). Relative gene expression was quantified by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) using the CFX Touch Real-Time PCR Detection Systems (BioRad) and PrimeTime® Gene Expression Master Mix, with 55 ng of cDNA input. Relative quantification was calculated using the ΔΔCT method [26], with TBP mRNA serving as the endogenous control. The sequences for the qRT-PCR assays are provided in Supplementary Table 5.

Subcellular fractionation

Subcellular fractionation was performed using the PARIS fractionation kit (Thermo Scientific), followed by DNA removal (TURBO™ DNA-free™ DNase Treatment, Thermo Scientific) according to the manufacturer's recommendation. After Fractionation, RT-qPCR was performed as described before. ACTB and U6 RNA were used as cytoplasmic and nuclear controls, respectively.

CRISPR-Cas9 knockout of CATG00000106133.1

HL60 cells were genetically modified using CRISPR-Cas9 ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes, employing two guide RNAs (gRNAs) per deletion. For each transfection, 1 × 105 cells were collected, washed in DPBS (without Ca2+ and Mg2+, Thermo Scientific), and resuspended in 5 μL of Resuspension Buffer R (Thermo Scientific). RNP complexes were assembled by incubating 12 pmol of TrueCut™ Cas9 Protein v2 (Thermo Scientific, SpCas9: 5′-NGG-3′) with 6 pmol of each gRNA in Resuspension Buffer R at room temperature for 20 min. The prepared RNP complexes were then mixed with the resuspended cells in equal volumes and electroporated using 10 μL NEON electroporation tips under the following conditions: 1350 V, 35 ms, 1 pulse. Immediately after electroporation, cells were gently transferred to a 24-well plate containing pre-warmed culture medium and incubated at 37 °C for 3–4 days. Genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from transfected cells using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen). The deletion site was amplified via PCR with 50 ng of genomic DNA and Phusion Flash High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix (Thermo Scientific). PCR products were analyzed by gel electrophoresis, purified (MinElute Gel Extraction Kit, Qiagen), and subjected to Sanger sequencing (Eurofins Genomics). Following bulk deletion confirmation, cells were single-cell sorted by FACS into 96-well plates and cultured for 10–12 days. Deletions in individual clones were validated as described above and transcript levels were detected through RT-qPCR analysis. Discrimination between heterozygous and homologous clones was conducted with PCR genotyping, designing one primer outside the deletion and one inside the genomic deletion. KO cells with homogeneous CATG00000106133.1 deletions were selected for functional studies. WT control cells were generated using the same procedure without gRNA. Details on qRT-PCR assays, deletion validation primers, and gRNA sequences are in Supplementary Tables 6–7.

Gene expression analyses by RNA-sequencing and differential expression analyses

RNA-sequencing from the KAW (AML patients and NBM), ClinSeq-AML, cohorts were performed as described [[27], [28], [29], [30]]. RNA sequencing of CATG00000106133.1 knockout (KO) HL60 clones was performed by extracting total RNA from HL60 KO and wild-type (WT) clones using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). Total RNA was subjected to quality control with Agilent Tapestation according to the manufacturer's instructions. To construct libraries suitable for Illumina sequencing, the Illumina stranded mRNA prep ligation sample preparation protocol was used with starting concentration between 25 and 1000 ng total RNA. The protocol includes mRNA isolation, cDNA synthesis, ligation of adapters and amplification of indexed libraries. The yield and quality of the amplified libraries were analyzed using Qubit by Thermo Fisher and quality was checked by using Agilent Tapestation. The indexed cDNA libraries were normalized and combined, and the pools were sequenced on the Illumina Nextseq 2000 P2 100 cycles kit sequencing run, generating 58 base-paired end reads with dual index 10 + 10 base pairs. Demultiplexing was performed with bcl2fastq (v2.20.0). Reads were aligned to the GRCh38 (Ensembl release 111) reference genome using STAR (v2.7.9a). Aligned reads were assigned to genomic features as defined in Homo_sapiens.GRCh38.111. gtf from Ensembl using featureCounts (v1.5.1). Gene annotations were retrieved from Ensembl BioMart via the biomaRt R package. Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 R package. P-values were computed using the Wald test and adjusted for multiple testing with the Benjamini–Hochberg correction.

Gene Ontology (GO) and ChEA [31] pathway analyses were performed using the ShinyGO v0.82 platform. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from the RNA-seq analysis were included using a threshold of adjusted p-value <0.05 and |fold change| ≥ 1. Analyses were carried out using target gene list against background gene list, applying a false discovery rate (FDR) cut-off of 0.05. Reactome pathway enrichment was performed separately using the same DEG list in Enrichr.

Proliferation assay

Cell viability was assessed using WST-8 tetrazolium salt (Cell Counting Kit 8, Abcam). Cells were plated at 10,000 cells per well in 100 μL of medium on a clear-bottom 96-well plate. For the assay, 10 μL of WST-8 solution was added to each well, and the cells were incubated for 4 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2. After incubation, the absorbance of the formazan dye formed was measured at 460 nm, with 660 nm as the reference wavelength, using a Synergy HTX plate reader. Cells were incubated for up to 72 h, and measurements were taken every 24 h.

Apoptosis assay

Cell apoptosis was evaluated using the Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Staining/Detection Kit (Abcam). In short, cells were washed in PBS, resuspended in 500 μL binding buffer and incubated with Annexin V-FITC and Propidium Iodide for 5 min at room temperature. Cells were immediately analyzed by flow cytometry (BC CytoFLEX Flow Cytometer) and data was evaluated by FlowJo™ v10.8.0.

Cell cycle analysis

Cell cycle analysis was done using the Propidium iodide (PI) Flow Cytometry Kit (Abcam). Cells were washed once in 1x PBS, fixed in 66% ice-cold ethanol and stored at −4 °C. Thereafter, cells were resuspended by pipetting, washed again in PBS, and resuspended in 200 μL Propidium iodide solution supplemented with RNase. Cells were then incubated in the dark (37 °C, 30 min). After incubation, cells were placed on ice and analyzed by flow cytometry (BC CytoFLEX Flow Cytometer). Data was evaluated by FlowJoTM v10.8.0.

Motif enrichment analysis

Motif enrichment analysis was performed using the MEME Suite to identify motifs enriched in the sequences of interest. Specifically, sequences corresponding to the primary CAGE clusters of CATG00000106133.1, representing different regions of gene transcription initiation, were retrieved from CAGE-seq data from the FANTOM5 project. For each CAGE cluster, the region spanning 200 bp upstream and 50 bp downstream was used for analysis. Control sequences were generated by shuffling the primary sequences while preserving 3-mer frequencies. Motif searches were conducted against the HOCOMOCO v11 full HUMAN database in MEME format. The significance threshold for motif enrichment was set at an E-value ≤10. Enriched motifs were visualized using the WebLogo tool.

Drug sensitivity and resistance testing

High-throughput drug sensitivity and resistance testing (DSRT) was carried out as previously described [32]. In short, cells were seeded at 2000–5000 cells/well onto 8384-well drug library plates (acoustically dispensed drugs of FO5A drug library comprising 528 unique drugs: FDA approved 30%, investigative 53% and developmental 17%) in a 5-point 10,000-fold concentration range. A selective Drug Sensitivity Score (sDSS) was calculated for each drug by subtracting the DSS of the control cells (CATG00000106133.1 WT cells) from the DSS of the sample of interest (CATG00000106133.1 KO cells), providing a quantitative measure of differential drug response.

Statistical analyses

The Wilcoxon signed rank test was used to test for differences between non-parametric pairs of data. The Kruskal-Wallis test was used to compare three or more groups of data. Statistical significance between numerical variables was established with linear correlation analysis using Spearman-rho method. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time period between the time point of diagnosis and the time point of death or end of follow-up. Kaplan-Meier analysis was used to estimate the survival curves and statistical significance in survival was determined by Mantel-Cox log-rank test. Uni and multivariate analyses were performed by Cox proportional hazards regression model using dichotomized lncRNA expression as a variable. Multivariate analysis was done by including known prognostic risk variables in AML. All statistical analyses were performed using R (v3.6.2) and GraphPad (10.4.0). Statistical significance was determined by P-value <0.05.

Results

CRISPRi screens to identify lncRNAs with a role in AML pathogenesis

To characterize lncRNAs with a potential role in AML, we performed high-throughput CRISPRi screens using the acute monocytic leukemia cell line MOLM-13 modified to stably express dCas9-KRAB [33] (Fig. 1A; Supplementary Fig. 1A). Target loci were selected based on CAGE data from the FANTOM5 project, covering 7996 lncRNAs expressed in 8 different AML cell lines (Supplementary Table 1). A customized barcoded CRISPRi library targeting these lncRNAs and control genes was applied in MOLM-13 cells, and gRNA abundance was quantified with MaGeCK across three conditions: a proliferation screen (day 0 vs. day 20), an ATRA-induced differentiation screen (CD11b+ vs. CD11b− cells) (Supplementary Fig. 1B), and a venetoclax response screen (treated vs. control). Two replicates were performed for each screen, and top positive and negative hits were ranked using Robust Rank Aggregation (RRA), which evaluates how consistently the sgRNAs for each gene are ranked among the most depleted or enriched guides (Fig. 1B; Supplementary Fig. 1C). To reduce false positives, only the top 100 enriched and top 100 depleted genes that overlapped between replicates were considered (Supplementary Table 8–13).

This approach led to the identification of 11 lncRNAs potentially involved in venetoclax resistance (depleted gRNAs) and 12 associated with venetoclax sensitivity (enriched gRNAs) (Fig. 1C; Supplementary Table 14). In contrast, the differentiation screen yielded only four lncRNAs (Fig. 1D; Supplementary Table 15) By comparison, the proliferation screen resulted in 47 lncRNAs linked to enhanced proliferation (depleted gRNAs) and 11 associated with impaired proliferation (enriched gRNAs) (Fig. 1E; Supplementary Table 16). Further downstream analyses focused on the proliferation and venetoclax-sensitivity screens as the differentiation screen yielded a minimal number of lncRNA candidates and library coverage was not reached.

CRISPRi screen identifies MIR17HG, CATG00000106133.1 and CATG00000056792.1 as lncRNAs associated with proliferation in Molm-13 cells

The proliferation screen aimed to identify lncRNAs that contribute to the regulation of cell proliferation in AML. Among the 58 lncRNAs identified in the proliferation screen, candidates selected for downstream functional validation were not prioritized solely based on statistical ranking (RRA score) and effect size (log2 fold change). Instead, we applied a multi-criteria selection strategy to identify biologically and clinically relevant lncRNAs. Specifically, we prioritized (i) lncRNAs that are novel or previously described, but poorly characterized in AML, (ii) candidates showing correlation with clinical parameters, and (iii) lncRNAs differentially expressed in AML patient samples compared with CD34+ bone marrow cells from healthy donors, based on RNA-seq data from our AML KAW cohort (n = 103; healthy donors n = 11). Based on these criteria and focusing on depleted lncRNAs (promoting proliferation in the screen), MIR17HG, CATG00000106133.1, and CATG00000056792.1 were selected as the top candidates. Although MIR17HG shows low expression in AML cells compared to normal bone marrow cells, previous data have suggested its potential role in promoting myeloid proliferation [34]. On the other hand, CATG00000106133.1 and CATG00000056792.1 showed higher expression in AML cells compared to normal ones, indicating an expression profile that may suggest an oncogenic function (Fig. 2A). To validate the screening data, we transduced dCas9-BFP-KRAB–expressing cells with individual sgRNAs targeting either CATG00000106133.1, CATG00000056792.1, or MIR17HG. A competition assay was then performed to test the phenotypic effect of each sgRNA, where BFP+ fluorescent cells, expressing both dCas9 and sgRNA, were monitored at 5-, 14-, and 21-days post-transduction. The results showed a reduction of cells expressing sgRNAs targeting MIR17HG, CATG00000106133.1 and CATG00000056792.1 compared to cells transduced with a non-targeting sgRNA (Fig. 2B). These results confirm the association between lncRNA knockdown and the observed phenotype, supporting their role in promoting cell proliferation in AML.

By comparing the expression of candidate genes with clinical parameters from our AML ClinSeq cohort (n = 325), MIR17HG was found to be highly expressed in over 60% of patients (Fig. 2C), and higher expression was associated with poorer overall survival (OS) (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2D). However, this association was not independent when adjusting for other risk factors in multivariate Cox regression analyses (dichotomized MIR17HG expression, CPM cut-off by mean) (Supplementary Table 24). Notably, MIR17HG expression was significantly higher in females than in males (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2E) and showed a positive correlation with NPM1 mutations, while being negatively associated with KIT and PTPN11 mutations (Fig. 2F). CATG00000056792.1 exhibited intermediate expression in more than 70% of the ClinSeq cohort (Fig. 2G). Higher expression correlated positively with age and female gender (Fig. 2H and I), as well as with RUNX1 and NPM1 mutations. In contrast, it was negatively associated with PTPN11, IDH1, and DNMT3A mutations (Fig. 2J). Similarly, CATG00000106133.1 displayed intermediate expression among ClinSeq patients (0.5 < CPM <5) (Fig. 3A). Its expression was higher in de novo AML cases (Fig. 3B) and correlated with cytogenetically normal AML (Fig. 3C). Additionally, positive associations were observed with NPM1 and IDH2 mutations, whereas a negative correlation was identified for EZH2 mutations (Fig. 3D).

CATG00000106133.1 exhibits enrichment in the monocytic lineage and nuclear localization in AML cells

Of the three candidate lncRNAs identified in the proliferation screen, CATG00000106133.1 was selected for detailed investigation due to its elevated expression in AML patients compared to normal cells, the lack of prior description in either normal or disease contexts (unlike MIR17HG), and its intergenic location which contrasts to the intronic nature of CATG00000056792.1 as displayed in the FANTOM5 CAGE-sequencing data [35]. We first investigated its expression profile in greater detail by analyzing the expression of CATG00000106133.1 in 12 different AML cell lines and found that U937 and KG1 exhibited the highest expression levels (Fig. 3E). Additionally, examination of CAGE expression data from normal cells showed enrichment of CAGE tags within the CATG00000106133.1 locus in monocytes and, to a lesser extent, in basophils and natural killer cells (Fig. 3F), indicating lineage-specific expression of CATG00000106133.1.

To gain insight into the potential biological function of CATG00000106133.1 lncRNA, subcellular fractionation analysis was performed. Beta-actin was used as a cytoplasmic marker, while snoRNA U6 served as a nuclear marker to ensure the accuracy of the fractionation process. The results of this experiment demonstrated that CATG00000106133.1 is primarily localized in the nucleus (Fig. 3G). The nuclear localization primarily suggests that CATG00000106133.1 may play a role in transcriptional regulation, chromatin remodeling, or other nuclear processes associated with gene expression control.

CATG00000106133.1 affects cell proliferation, associates with IRF8-mediated cytokine signaling pathway and modulates drug sensitivity

To further elucidate the functional role of CATG00000106133.1 in AML, we employed a double-guide CRISPR-Cas9 knockout strategy in HL60 cells. Specifically, two single-guide RNAs were designed to completely delete the first and primary 5′ transcription start site (TSS) of CATG00000106133.1, as predicted from FANTOM5 CAGE clusters, thereby abolishing the transcription of all known transcript variants expressed in AML cell lines. Following successful genome editing, confirmed via genotypic and transcriptomic validation in single-cell clones (Fig. 4A; Supplementary Fig. 2A–2B), these CATG00000106133.1 knockout cell models were used for comprehensive functional analyses. Cell viability assays demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in proliferation in CATG00000106133.1 knockout cells compared to control cells at 48 h, with an even greater reduction observed at 72 h (Fig. 4B), corroborating findings from our initial CRISPR screening. Furthermore, apoptosis assays showed no detectable effect of CATG00000106133.1 deletion on apoptosis (Supplementary Fig. 2C), whereas cell-cycle analysis revealed an increased proportion of knockout cells in S phase compared with control cells (Fig. 4C).

Furthermore, to gain mechanistic insights into the consequences of CATG00000106133.1 depletion, we performed RNA sequencing on single-clone KO cells and empty vector–transfected control cells. Differential expression analysis identified 1406 genes (Supplementary Table 17). A heatmap of the most significantly altered genes (Fig. 4D) revealed marked downregulation of HOXA11 and HOXA10. These genes are members of the HOXA cluster and are known to play a role in AML cell proliferation [36]. Additionally, GO biological enrichment analyses and Reactome pathway exploration of the DE genes revealed pronounced enrichment of terms related to cytokine signaling and immune response (Fig. 4E–F; Supplementary Table 18–19), indicating potential involvement of CATG00000106133.1 in pathways connected to the regulation of the immune system. Among cytokine signaling-related genes altered upon CATG00000106133.1 depletion, the transcription factor IRF8 was found to be downregulated (Supplementary Table 17). Additionally, motif enrichment analysis of the first five CATG00000106133.1 CAGE clusters, representing the most predominant and active TSSs of CATG00000106133.1 in AML (FANTOM5 project; Supplementary Table 20), revealed enrichment for IRF4 and IRF8 motifs (Fig. 4G). Furthermore, using the ChIP-X enrichment analysis (ChEA) database [31], which integrates genome-wide ChIP-X data, IRF8 was among the top enriched transcription factors associated with the 1406 differentially expressed genes in CATG00000106133.1 knockout cells (Fig. 4H; Supplementary Table 21). Taken together, these results suggest a potential regulatory axis in which CATG00000106133.1 may be linked to the IRF8-centered cytokine signaling in AML.

In addition, we also performed high-throughput drug sensitivity and resistance testing (DSRT) screens to further assess the role of CATG00000106133.1. Specifically, CATG00000106133.1 KO clones and WT clones were screened against 528 approved or investigational compounds. Drugs were initially filtered based on the selective Drug Sensitivity Score (sDSS), which reflects the difference in drug response between CATG00000106133.1 KO and WT cells, and subsequently by overlap between independent clones. Overall, 48 drugs were identified as showing increased resistance upon CATG00000106133.1 depletion (Supplementary Table 22), with a significant enrichment of HDAC inhibitors (Fisher's exact test, p = 0.0336). Conversely, 32 drugs were found to display increased sensitivity in CATG00000106133.1 KO cells (Supplementary Table 23). Among these, significant enrichments were observed for compounds associated with mitotic inhibition (Fisher's exact test, p = 0.0306) and for dual ABL/SRC tyrosine kinase inhibitors (Fisher's exact test, p = 0.0019).

CRISPRi screen in AML cells identifies lncRNA genes implicated in drug resistance and drug sensitivity to venetoclax

Given the clinical relevance of venetoclax in AML and the identification of more than 23 candidate lncRNAs from the CRISPR interference drug screen, we performed parallel analyses to the proliferation screen to identify venetoclax-associated lncRNAs. Among these, AC009299.3 was selected for further validation using the same workflow applied to the proliferation candidates. To validate the screening results, AC009299.3 was knocked down using a single sgRNA, and viable sgRNA-expressing cells were evaluated by flow cytometry after 48 h of treatment with either venetoclax or DMSO. Consistent with the screen, AC009299.3 knockdown led to a significant increase in viable cells compared to the non-targeting control following venetoclax exposure (Fig. 5A).

AC009299.3 expression was found to be significantly lower in AML patient cells compared to normal bone marrow (Fig. 5B) and it exhibited an intermediate expression in more than 50% of the AML ClinSeq cohort (Fig. 5C). Interestingly, among all lncRNAs in this study, AC009299.3 showed the strongest correlation with clinical outcome, with significantly poorer OS in patients with high AC009299.3 expression treated with standard treatment (P < 0.0001; Fig. 5D). It also showed a trend toward poorer OS in multivariate analysis considering known risk factors, although this was borderline statistically significant (HR = 1.33; P = 0.054; Supplementary Table 24). In addition, the expression of AC009299.3 was significantly lower in patients with good compared to intermediate or adverse ELN risk (Fig. 5F), while it was higher in secondary AML (s-AML) compared to de novo AML and therapy-related AML (t-AML) (both p < 0.05, global p < 0.05)(Fig. 5G). In conclusion, our results suggest that AC009299.3 expression correlates with venetoclax sensitivity and adverse outcomes in AML.

Discussion

LncRNAs are transcripts longer than 200 nucleotides that lack functional open reading frames. They represent a dominant part of the human genome and there is increasing evidence that they are key players in major biological processes, including cell cycle, differentiation, proliferation, and apoptosis [37,38] and their deregulation has also been linked to diseases like cancer [39]. In this study, three lncRNA-focused CRISPR screens were designed to identify lncRNAs involved in proliferation, differentiation, and drug resistance in AML.

Through this approach, three lncRNAs, MIR17HG, CATG00000106133.1 and CATG00000056792.1 were filtered out and identified as associated with a positive effect on proliferation. Since CATG00000106133.1 and CATG00000056792.1 have not previously been functionally annotated, their biological or tumorigenic functions remain unknown. As for MIR17HG, our findings are in line with previous studies where this lncRNA has been reported to be implicated in the regulation of cell proliferation in different cancer types such as retinoblastoma, lung cancer, multiple myeloma, colon cancer and osteosarcoma [[40], [41], [42], [43], [44]]. The fact that a lncRNA previously known to be associated with cell proliferation in cancer came up as a top hit in our screen strengthens the validity of the screen results.

MIR17HG, CATG00000106133.1, and CATG00000056792.1 were also found to correlate with specific AML genetic mutations. Notably, the expression of all three lncRNAs positively correlated with NPM1 mutations, a well-established mutation occurring in 30-35% of AML patients [45] and associated with a distinct gene expression [46]. Several lncRNAs have previously been linked to NPM1-mutated AML, highlighting their potential role in leukemogenesis. For instance, LONA, LncRNA overexpressed in NPM1-mutated AML can bind both the mutant and wild type isophorme and affect cell maturation [47]. Similarly, CRNDE (Colorectal Neoplasia Differentially Expressed) is upregulated in NPM1-mutated AML and has been implicated in differentiation inhibition contributing to AML progression [48]. We have also previously shown a specific lncRNA, MALNC, to be expressed in other AML mutational subtypes, including a co-mutational pattern with NPM1 [49]. Given these findings, the correlation of MIR17HG, CATG00000106133.1, and CATG00000056792.1 expression with NPM1 mutations suggests a potential role in this AML subtype to enhance proliferation.

Among the three lncRNAs selected from the proliferation screen, CATG00000106133.1 showed significantly higher expression in AML patient cells compared with MIR17HG and CATG00000056792.1, as well as elevated expression in AML cells relative to normal bone marrow cells, prompting further investigation into its potential oncogenic role. Functional validation in CRISPR knockout clones confirmed a proliferative role for this lncRNA. Additionally, loss of expression increased the fraction of cells in S phase while leaving cell death unchanged, suggesting that reduced proliferation might correlate with altered S-phase progression, potentially caused by replication stress or activation of intra-S-phase checkpoints [[50], [51], [52]]. Importantly, the observed increase in S-phase accumulation together with altered sensitivity to mitotic inhibitors, as revealed by drug sensitivity and resistance testing, may indicate that CATG00000106133.1 contributes to cell-cycle regulation, although the underlying mechanism requires further investigation.

To further explore the molecular consequences of CATG00000106133.1 depletion, RNA-seq analyses identified 1406 differentially expressed genes, including HOXA10 and HOXA11, genes belonging to the HOXA cluster known to be involved in leukemogenesis [53,54]. Specifically, HOXA10 is a critical transcription factor in AML, where its aberrant expression has been linked to poor prognosis, leukemic transformation, and enhanced self-renewal of leukemic stem cells [55,56]. The downregulation of these genes upon CATG00000106133.1 depletion suggests that this lncRNA could potentially be involved in leukemic cell fate. Furthermore, Gene Ontology (GO) and Reactome pathway analyses of DEGs revealed significant enrichment for cytokine signaling pathways. Among these genes, IRF8, also known as Interferon Consensus Sequence Binding Protein (ICSBP), emerged as particularly relevant. IRF8, a hematopoietic transcription factor of the IRF family [57], is crucial for myeloid cell development and differentiation. While some studies have described a tumor suppressor role for IRF8, specifically in the context of chronic myeloid leukemia [58,59], other evidence suggests that in AML, IRF8 correlates with poor prognosis [60], enhanced cell growth both in vitro and in vivo [61], and even represents, in specific AML subtypes, an AML-biased transcription factor dependency, whereby AML cells rely more heavily on its activity than other cell types [62]. Notably, in our study, IRF8 gene expression was found to be downregulated upon CATG00000106133.1 knockout. In parallel, IRF8 also emerged as one of the most enriched transcription factors in motif analysis of the 1406 differentially expressed genes, and, together with IRF4, was identified in motif enrichment analyses of the most abundant CATG00000106133.1 isoforms. Together, these observations suggest a possible feedback loop in which CATG00000106133.1 and IRF8 may regulate each other and ultimately affect cell proliferation. This regulation could occur directly, for instance, through CATG00000106133.1 acting as a scaffold or decoy for chromatin remodeling complexes, or by influencing IRF8 mRNA stability via interactions with microRNAs. Alternatively, it could occur indirectly, through modulation of other transcripts that control IRF8 expression. Although these hypotheses provide a potential framework for understanding how CATG00000106133.1 lncRNA may contribute to AML biology, the precise molecular interplay between CATG00000106133.1, IRF8, and cytokine signaling pathways requires further investigation.

Further, we investigated the role of lncRNAs in venetoclax response, a promising therapy for AML approved in 2018. In this analysis, twelve lncRNAs were associated with potential venetoclax sensitivity, and eleven with possible resistance. Among the candidates linked to venetoclax sensitivity, AC009299.3 emerged as particularly noteworthy due to its expression pattern and clinical correlations. Specifically, AC009299.3 expression increased with patient age, was higher in adverse and intermediate ELN subgroups, and was elevated in secondary AML. In patients treated with standard therapy, high AC009299.3 expression was associated with poor outcomes. Taken together, the observations, combined with its role in promoting venetoclax sensitivity, suggest that AC009299.3 may serve as a biomarker to identify patients most likely to benefit from venetoclax-based regimens, providing a potential tool for more personalized therapeutic strategies. However, the correlations with clinical outcome should be further studied in a ventoclax-treated AML cohort.

Overall, our study identifies several novel and previously annotated lncRNAs that regulate key cellular phenotypes and drug responses in AML. These findings not only highlight the power of CRISPRi screening approaches to systematically uncover functional non-coding RNAs in leukemic contexts, but also support their broader application for the future exploration of lncRNAs in AML, including the investigation of diverse cellular phenotypes and responses to different therapeutic interventions. Indeed, while similar strategies have been widely applied to study lncRNAs in physiological [[63], [64], [65]] and oncogenic processes [[66], [67], [68]], only a limited number of CRISPRi screens have focused on lncRNAs in AML [69,70] and our study represents one of the first CRISPRi screens to investigate the role of lncRNAs in AML under defined drug treatment conditions, such as venetoclax. Our dataset, therefore, provides a valuable resource for future studies aimed at clarifying the mechanisms and biological relevance of lncRNAs involved in proliferation, differentiation, and venetoclax response in leukemic cells. Notably, the involvement of specific lncRNAs in cell proliferation, a hallmark of cancer, suggests that their functions may extend beyond AML to other tumorigenic contexts, including broader effects on therapeutic response. Consistent with this, DSRT screens performed in CATG00000106133.1 knockout cells identified several compounds with altered sensitivity, including drugs targeting mitotic processes and dual ABL/SRC tyrosine kinase inhibitors, such as dasatinib and bosutinib, which are clinically used for leukemias such as chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) [71,72]. Further validation of these findings could expand our understanding of the molecular mechanisms mediated by CATG00000106133.1 in AML, while also highlighting its potential relevance in other malignancies and drug-response pathways. Likewise, the identification of AC000299.3 as a lncRNA involved in venetoclax response also suggests its potential relevance in other hematological malignancies where venetoclax is increasingly used or under clinical evaluation [[73], [74], [75], [76], [77]]. In addition, by correlating the expression of selected lncRNAs with clinical parameters and patient survival, we highlight their potential as biomarkers for prognosis and improved risk stratification. With further validation, these molecules could be incorporated into clinical workflows to better predict treatment response and guide more informed therapeutic decisions, ultimately contributing to improved patient outcomes and reduced relapse rates. Collectively, these results provide a foundation for future mechanistic investigations and support the integration of lncRNA profiling into translational and clinical research, ultimately contributing to the development of more precise therapeutic strategies in AML.
