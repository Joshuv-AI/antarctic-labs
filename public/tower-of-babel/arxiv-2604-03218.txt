Power one sequential tests exist for weakly compact 𝒫 \mathcal{P} against 𝒫 c \mathcal{P}^{c}
Ashwin Ram, Aaditya Ramdas

Abstract
Suppose we observe data from a distribution P P and we wish to test the composite null hypothesis that P ∈ 𝒫 P\in\mathcal{P} against a composite alternative P ∈ 𝒬 ⊆ 𝒫 c P\in\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{P}^{c} . Herbert Robbins and coauthors pointed out around 1970 that, while no batch test can have a level α ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \alpha\in(0,1) and power equal to one, sequential tests can be constructed with this fantastic property. Since then, and especially in the last decade, a plethora of sequential tests have been developed for a wide variety of settings. However, the literature has not yet provided a clean and general answer as to when such power-one sequential tests exist. This paper provides a remarkably general sufficient condition (that we also prove is not necessary). Focusing on i.i.d. laws in Polish spaces without any further restriction, we show that there exists a level- α \alpha sequential test for any weakly compact 𝒫 \mathcal{P} , that is power-one against 𝒫 c \mathcal{P}^{c} (or any subset thereof). We show how to aggregate such tests into an e e -process for 𝒫 \mathcal{P} that increases to infinity under 𝒫 c \mathcal{P}^{c} . We conclude by building an e e -process that is asymptotically relatively growth rate optimal against 𝒫 c \mathcal{P}^{c} , an extremely powerful result.

Introduction

Imagine we are observing some arbitrary sequence of data points revealed to us over time with the goal of testing two hypotheses. And while observing these data points, we want to immediately stop as soon as the evidence is compelling enough. This is an arduous task, and in fact the motivation for sequential testing. To this end, suppose we’re testing a simple null versus a simple alternative. Having said this, it would certainly be nice to be able to decide when to stop or continue an experiment based on how the results look. As such, in this paper, we present an extremely general and beautiful existence theorem. In particular, on some arbitrary Polish space
\mathsf{X}

Preliminaries

Throughout, we let
(\mathsf{X},\mathcal{B})
be a Polish space with its Borel
\sigma
-field,
\mathcal{B}
. We let
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
be the set of Borel probability measures on
(\mathsf{X},\mathcal{B})
. In addition, more generally for a measurable space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
, we take
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\Omega)
to be the set of probability measures on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
. Weak convergence of measures is denoted by
\Rightarrow
. Given all this, we will first define the KL divergence.

Definition 1
.

For
M,N\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
we denote the KL divergence as,

\mathrm{KL}(M\|N):=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\int_{\mathsf{X}}\log\left(\frac{dM}{dN}\right)dM,&\text{if }M\ll N,\\[6.00006pt]
+\infty,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Importantly, we do not use any dominating reference measure in our above definition of the KL. Only the Radon-Nikodym derivative when it exists.

Now we will move on to how we define polars and bipolars, etc., which were recently identified to be fundamental objects by
[
18
,
19
]
. The bipolar specifically is the bipolar associated with the polar defined by nonnegative tests, ie
e
-variables. With this said, all we will use (ie the properties) will be the fact that it contains the original set and its defining inequalities.

Definition 2
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a nonempty set of probability measures on a measurable space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
. Then define the polar as,

\mathcal{S}^{\circ}:=\Bigl\{E:\Omega\to[0,\infty]:\sup_{P\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{E}_{P}[E]\leq 1\Bigr\}.

The bipolar is then be defined as,

\mathcal{S}^{\circ\circ}:=\Bigl\{R\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\Omega):\mathbb{E}_{R}[E]\leq 1\ \text{ for all }E\in\mathcal{S}^{\circ}\Bigr\}.

Note that for every nonempty
\mathcal{S}
, we always have that
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{S}^{\circ\circ}
. Why? If
P\in\mathcal{P}
indeed and
E\in\mathcal{S}^{\circ}
, then
\mathbb{E}_{P}[E]\leq\sup_{P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{E}_{P^{\prime}}[E]\leq 1
. With all that being said, we will now define a lemma that gives intuition on the event probabilities over the bipolar.

Take some
Q\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
and a nonempty
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{1}(X)
. Define
\Phi(R):=\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{KL}(R\|P)
. We say that
\mathcal{P}
is
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}
lower semicontinuous at
Q
if
\Phi
is lower semicontinuous at
Q
for the weak topology. Meaning that if for every sequence
R_{k}\Rightarrow Q
, we have also
\Phi(Q)\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}\Phi(R_{k})
.

Weak compactness of
\mathcal{P}
is a sufficient condition. Meaning, if
\mathcal{P}
is weakly compact in
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
, then
\mathcal{P}
is
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}
lower semicontinuous at every
Q
. We will prove it below however because it’s extremely important for helping us get particular weak neighborhoods.

Lemma 1
.

The map
(M,N)\mapsto\mathrm{KL}(M\|N)
from
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})\times\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
to
[0,\infty]
is lower semicontinuous for the weak topology on both coordinates.

Proof.

On Polish spaces we know by Donsker-Varadhan that,

\mathrm{KL}(M\|N)=\sup_{f\in C_{b}(\mathsf{X})}\Bigl\{\int_{\mathsf{X}}fdM-\log\int_{\mathsf{X}}e^{f}dN\Bigr\}.

Here,
C_{b}(\mathsf{X})
are those bounded continuous real-valued functions on
\mathsf{X}
. Now, consider some particular
f\in C_{b}(\mathsf{X})
. Note that if indeed
M_{k}\Rightarrow M
, it follows that
\int fdM_{k}\to\int fdM
just by definition of weak convergence. Now, if
N_{k}\Rightarrow N
, we have that
\int e^{f}dN_{k}\to\int e^{f}dN
because
e^{f}
also is bounded and continuous. As such,
\log\int e^{f}dN_{k}\to\log\int e^{f}dN
. Therefore, the map
(M,N)\mapsto\int fdM-\log\int e^{f}dN
also is continuous under weak convergence. Of course, the supremum of some arbitrary family of these functions must always be lower semicontinous. So! It must be the case that by Donsker-Varadhan we have that the map
(M,N)\mapsto\mathrm{KL}(M\|N)
also is lower semicontinuous.
∎

Lemma 2
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{S}
is a nonempty set of probability measures on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
and let
A\in\mathcal{F}
. Then it follows that,

\sup_{R\in\mathcal{S}^{\circ\circ}}R(A)=\sup_{P\in\mathcal{S}}P(A).

Proof.

Let’s begin our proof by setting
c:=\sup_{P\in\mathcal{S}}P(A)\in[0,1]
. Our proof will proceed by separately handling the cases where
c=0
and
c>0
respectively. First, suppose that
c=0
. Then, for every
t>0
we have that
\sup_{P\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{E}_{P}[t\mathbf{1}_{A}]=t\sup_{P\in\mathcal{S}}P(A)=0\leq 1
, which means
t\mathbf{1}_{A}\in\mathcal{S}^{\circ}
. By definition of the bipolar, we know that for any
R\in\mathcal{S}^{\circ\circ}
,
\mathbb{E}_{R}[t\mathbf{1}_{A}]\leq 1
: meaning,
tR(A)\leq 1
for all
t>0
, which actually forces
R(A)=0
. Therefore, we get that
\sup_{R\in\mathcal{S}^{\circ\circ}}R(A)=0=c
. Let us now handle the case where
c>0
. To begin, define
E:=\mathbf{1}_{A}/c
. Clearly, for all
P\in\mathcal{S}
,
\mathbb{E}_{P}[E]=P(A)/c\leq 1
, hence it follows that
E\in\mathcal{S}^{\circ}
. Now, for any
R\in\mathcal{S}^{\circ\circ}
, we know that
\frac{R(A)}{c}=\mathbb{E}_{R}[E]\leq 1
. Thus it follows that
R(A)\leq c
and hence
\sup_{R\in\mathcal{S}^{\circ\circ}}R(A)\leq c
. Now, necessarily we also know that the reverse inequality must hold also because
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{S}^{\circ\circ}
. Hence it must indeed be the case that
\sup_{R\in\mathcal{S}^{\circ\circ}}R(A)\geq\sup_{P\in\mathcal{S}}P(A)=c
, and thus we are done.
∎

Lemma 3
.

Assume that
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
is weakly compact. Then define the same
\Phi(R)
to be
\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{KL}(R\|P)
. Then it follows that
\Phi
is lower semicontinuous on
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
for the weak topology.

Proof.

Let us begin by letting
R_{k}\Rightarrow R
in
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
and set
L:=\liminf_{k\to\infty}\Phi(R_{k})\in[0,\infty]
. Now, let’s choose a subsequence
(k_{j})_{j\geq 1}
such that
\Phi(R_{k_{j}})\to L
. And, for each
j
let’s pick an almost-minimizer
P_{k_{j}}\in\mathcal{P}
which satisfies
\mathrm{KL}(R_{k_{j}}\|P_{k_{j}})\leq\Phi(R_{k_{j}})+\frac{1}{j}
. By weak compactness of
\mathcal{P}
we know that the sequence
(P_{k_{j}})
has a weakly convergence subsequence such that
P_{k_{j}}\Rightarrow P_{\infty}\in\mathcal{P}
. Now,
R_{k_{j}}\Rightarrow R
also, thus by Lemma
1
we get,

\mathrm{KL}(R\|P_{\infty})\leq\liminf_{j\to\infty}\mathrm{KL}(R_{k_{j}}\|P_{k_{j}}).

Let’s now use the almost minimality and the fact that that
\Phi(R)\leq\mathrm{KL}(R\|P_{\infty})
. Doing this gives us that,

\Phi(R)\leq\liminf_{j\to\infty}\mathrm{KL}(R_{k_{j}}\|P_{k_{j}})\leq\liminf_{j\to\infty}\left(\Phi(R_{k_{j}})+\frac{1}{j}\right)=\liminf_{k\to\infty}\Phi(R_{k}),

which is exactly lower semicontinuity of
\Phi
, hence we’re done.
∎

3
Power-One Sequential Tests Against Simple Alternatives

We’re now going to analyze some applications to testing from all that we have shown so far. To begin, let
(X_{i})_{i\geq 1}
be the coordinate maps on
(\mathsf{X}^{\mathbb{N}},\mathcal{B}^{\otimes\mathbb{N}})
. And, let
\mathcal{F}_{n}:=\sigma(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
just be the natural filtration. For
P\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
, we let
P^{\infty}:=P^{\otimes\mathbb{N}}
for the iid law on
\mathsf{X}^{\mathbb{N}}
. With this setup in mind, let us now define both sequential level and power.

Definition 3
.

Take a particular null class
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
and an alternative
Q\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
. A sequential test is simply a
(\mathcal{F}_{n})
-stopping rule
\tau:\mathsf{X}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
. Now for some particular
\alpha\in(0,1)
we say that
\tau
has level at most
\alpha
uniformly over
\mathcal{P}
if
\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}P^{\infty}(\tau<\infty)\leq\alpha
. We say that
\tau
has power one against
Q
if
Q^{\infty}(\tau<\infty)=1
.

In addition, for our statements below, we will need Pinsker’s Inequality. Recall that in our setup, Pinsker tells us that for any probability measures
\mu,\nu
on a measurable space,

\|\mu-\nu\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{KL}(\mu\|\nu)},

(1)

where the rhs will be
+\infty
if
\mathrm{KL}(\mu\|\nu)=\infty
.

With all this presented, we will now formally present a proposition to establish something very closely related to the following statement: informally, a test is power one if and only if
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}>0
.

Proposition 1
.

Consider a particular
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
Q\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
, and a nonempty
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
. Now let
d:=\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})=\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P)\in[0,\infty]
. Then the following hold.

1.

If
d=0
, then unfortunately, no sequential test of level at most
\alpha
can have power one against
Q
.

2.

If
0<d<\infty
and
\mathcal{P}
is
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}
weakly lower semicontinuous at
Q
, then there indeed exists a sequential test of level at most
\alpha
with power one against
Q
.

As a consequence, under the second assumption in our theorem
1
, a level-
\alpha
sequential power-one test exists if and only if
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})>0
.

The proof relies on the following nonasymptotic version of Sanov’s theorem. Note that our lemma’s hypothesis also entails that Csiszár’s assumption of almost complete convexity holds.

Lemma 4

(Csiszár
[
6
]
)
.

Let
P\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
and let
C\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
be convex and weakly closed. Define
A_{n}:=\{\widehat{Q}_{n}\in C\}\subseteq\mathsf{X}^{n}
. Then it follows that for every
n\geq 1
,

P^{n}(A_{n})\ \leq\ \exp\Bigl(-n\inf_{R\in C}\mathrm{KL}(R\|P)\Bigr).

Proof of Proposition
1
.

Let’s start with showing that if
d=0
, then power one is impossible to achieve. To this end, first assume that
d=0
. Then it must be the case that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the exists
P_{n}\in\mathcal{P}
such that
\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P_{n})\leq 1/n^{2}
. Now let
\tau
be any stopping rule such that
\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}P^{\infty}(\tau<\infty)\leq\alpha
and set
B_{n}:=\{\tau\leq n\}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
. Then it follows that
P_{n}^{n}(B_{n})=P_{n}^{\infty}(\tau\leq n)\leq P_{n}^{\infty}(\tau<\infty)\leq\alpha
. Note that
\mathrm{KL}(Q^{n}\|P_{n}^{n})=n\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P_{n})\leq 1/n
. Hence by Pinsker’s inequality,
\|Q^{n}-P_{n}^{n}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2n}}
. Therefore we get that,

Q^{\infty}(\tau\leq n)=Q^{n}(B_{n})\leq P_{n}^{n}(B_{n})+\|Q^{n}-P_{n}^{n}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\alpha+\sqrt{\frac{1}{2n}}.

Now all we need to do is take
n\to\infty
and we can see that
Q^{\infty}(\tau<\infty)=\sup_{n}Q^{\infty}(\tau\leq n)\leq\alpha<1
. We now prove the second claim of our theorem. As such, assume
0<d<\infty
and that
\mathcal{P}
be
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}
lower semicontinuous at
Q
. Take some
\varepsilon\in(0,d/2)
and recall that
\Phi(R):=\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{KL}(R\|P)
. Just by the definition of lower semicontinuity at
Q
, we know that there exists a weakly open neighborhood
O_{\varepsilon}\ni Q
such that
\Phi(R)\geq d-\varepsilon
for all
R\in O_{\varepsilon}
. Now let us fix the bounded Lipschitz metric ie
d_{\mathrm{BL}}
that generates the weak topology. We will choose a
\delta>0
so that the closed ball,

C:=\overline{B}^{\mathrm{BL}}_{\delta}(Q)\subseteq O_{\varepsilon}.

Now define
A_{n}:=\{\widehat{Q}_{n}\in C\}\subseteq\mathsf{X}^{n}
and
\beta_{n}:=\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}P^{n}(A_{n})
. Necessarily
C
is convex and weakly closed, so it follows that by Lemma
4
for each
P\in\mathcal{P}
and each
n
,
P^{n}(A_{n})\leq\exp\Bigl(-n\inf_{R\in C}\mathrm{KL}(R\|P)\Bigr)
. Therefore it follows that,

\beta_{n}\leq\exp\Bigl(-n\inf_{R\in C}\Phi(R)\Bigr)\leq\exp\bigl(-n(d-\varepsilon)\bigr).

Let’s choose
N
so that
\sum_{n\geq N}\exp(-n(d-\varepsilon))\leq\alpha
. And, define the stopping rule
\tau:-\inf\{n\geq N:\widehat{Q}_{n}\in C\}
. Now, under
Q^{\infty}
, we have that
\widehat{Q}_{n}\Rightarrow Q
almost surely. And, since
C
is a neighborhood of
Q
we have that
\widehat{Q}_{n}\in C
eventually. Hence it must be the case that
Q^{\infty}(\tau<\infty)=1
. Now, note that under
P\in\mathcal{P}
,
P^{\infty}(\tau<\infty)\leq\sum_{n\geq N}P^{n}(A_{n})\leq\sum_{n\geq N}\beta_{n}\leq\alpha
. Clearly, we have shown the existence of a level
\alpha
sequential test with power one against
Q
, so we are done.
∎

4
Power-One Sequential Tests against Composite
\mathcal{Q}

In this section given all that we have developed so far, let’s address the question of when we have a uniform power-one sequential test: when does it exist? To begin, let
\mathcal{P}\subseteq M_{1}(\mathsf{X})
be a nonempty null and
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
be a possibly uncountable alternative class. We know that a stopping time
\tau
has power-one against
\mathcal{Q}
if for every
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
,
Q^{\infty}(\tau<\infty)=1
. In addition, such a stopping rule has level at most
\alpha
uniformly over
\mathcal{P}
if
\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}P^{\infty}(\tau<\infty)\leq\alpha
. However, before we proceed we will first formalize the idea that if some
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
has
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})=0
, then uniform power one is unfortunately impossible.

Proposition 2
.

If there exists some
Q_{0}\in\mathcal{Q}
with
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q_{0},\mathcal{P})=0
, then for every single
\alpha\in(0,1)
and every stopping time
\tau
, we have that
\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}P^{\infty}(\tau<\infty)\leq\alpha
implies that
Q_{0}^{\infty}(\tau<\infty)\leq\alpha
. As such, no level-
\alpha<1
sequential test can have power one simultaneously against every
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
.

Proof.

The proof for this is obvious. Because, all we need to do is apply Proposition
1
to the single alternative
Q_{0}
.
∎

The reason why we take the time to create this Proposition
2
(even though the proof is trivial) is because it shows us that assuming for every
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
that
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})>0
is a minimal and necessary assumption. With this necessary assumption in our minds, let us now give a sufficient condition for the existence of a uniform test that doesn’t require controlling the size of
\mathcal{Q}
. To this end, we will use the following basic idea: we know that
\mathsf{X}
is Polish. As a result,
(\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X}),\Rightarrow)
is metrizable and separable, and must therefore be Lindelöf
[
11
]
. And therefore we can use all of the beautiful Lindelöf properties. In particular, we will use the idea that every open cover has a countable subcover
[
35
, Section 3, p. 19]
. To that end, we first present a countable subcover lemma as follows.

Lemma 5
.

Every open cover of
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
in the weak topology has a countable subcover.

Proof.

Note that
\mathsf{X}
as we know is Polish. Hence by Prokhorov’s theorem we know that
(\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X}),\Rightarrow)
is therefore metrizable and separable. By definition, any separable metric space must be second countable, and any second countable space is Lindelöf. We know that second countability is hereditary, so it follows that the subspace
\mathcal{Q}
is also Lindelöf, hence our lemma’s claim follows.
∎

Now, we are ready for our main theorem. We will show how we achieve a uniform power-one sequential test through a countable cover of
\mathrm{KL}
-separated neighborhoods.

Theorem 1
.

Assume that the following hold.

1.

For every
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
,
d(Q):=\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})
is finite and strictly positive.

2.

For every
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
, the map
\Phi(R):=\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{KL}(R\|P)
is weakly lower semicontinuous at
Q
.

Then, for every
\alpha\in(0,1)
, there exists a single stopping rule
\tau
such that
\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}P^{\infty}(\tau<\infty)\leq\alpha
and
Q^{\infty}(\tau<\infty)=1
for all
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
.

Proof.

Consider some particular but arbitrarily chosen
\alpha\in(0,1)
. For each
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
, let us set
d(Q):=\Phi(Q)>0
. Take a particular bounded Lipschitz metric
d_{\mathrm{BL}}
which generates the weak topology. By weak lower semicontinuity of
\Phi
at
Q
, we know that the set
O_{Q}:=\{R\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X}):\Phi(R)>d(Q)/2\}
is weakly open and of course contains
Q
. Let us choose this
\delta_{Q}>0
so that the closed ball
\overline{B}^{\mathrm{BL}}_{\delta_{Q}}(Q)\subseteq O_{Q}
. We will now set
U_{Q}:=B^{\mathrm{BL}}_{\delta_{Q}}(Q)
and
r_{Q}:=d(Q)/2
. Clearly then it follows that
\inf_{R\in\overline{U}_{Q}}\Phi(R)\geq r_{Q}
.

Now, the collection
\{U_{Q}:Q\in\mathcal{Q}\}
covers
\mathcal{Q}
. Hence by Lemma
5
we can extract from this collection a countable subcover
\{U_{Q_{j}}\}_{j\geq 1}
. With a very minor abuse of notation, going forward we will write
U_{j}:=U_{Q_{j}}
and
r_{j}:=r_{Q_{j}}>0
. Now, for each
j
and
n
, let
A_{j,n}:=\{\widehat{Q}_{n}\in U_{j}\}
and
\beta_{j,n}:=\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}P^{n}(A_{j,n})
. Clearly,
A_{j,n}\subseteq\{\widehat{Q}_{n}\in\overline{U}_{j}\}
and
\overline{U}_{j}
is convex and weakly closed we can one again use Lemma
4
! Doing this gives us for every
P\in\mathcal{P}
that,

P^{n}(A_{j,n})\leq P^{n}(\widehat{Q}_{n}\in\overline{U}_{j})\leq\exp\Bigl(-n\inf_{R\in\overline{U}_{j}}\mathrm{KL}(R\|P)\Bigr).

Hence it follows that
\beta_{j,n}\leq\exp\Bigl(-n\inf_{R\in\overline{U}_{j}}\Phi(R)\Bigr)\leq\exp(-nr_{j})
. Therefore we know that there must exist a
N_{j}
such that for all
n\geq N_{j}
,
\beta_{j,n}\leq\exp\left(-\frac{r_{j}}{2}n\right)
.
Certainly, we can take
N_{j}
to be large enough so that
\sum_{n\geq N_{j}}\exp\left(-\frac{r_{j}}{2}n\right)\leq\alpha_{j}
. Having said all of this, let us define the stopping time
\tau_{j}:=\inf\{n\geq N_{j}:\widehat{Q}_{n}\in U_{j}\}
. It then follows that for any
P\in\mathcal{P}
,

P^{\infty}(\tau_{j}<\infty)\leq\sum_{n\geq N_{j}}P^{n}(A_{j,n})\leq\sum_{n\geq N_{j}}\beta_{j,n}\leq\alpha_{j}.

Moreover, for any
Q\in U_{j}
, we know that
U_{j}
is weakly open and
\widehat{Q}_{n}\Rightarrow Q
almost surely under
Q^{\infty}
. As a consequence, we get that
\widehat{Q}_{n}\in U_{j}
eventually. So it follows that
Q^{\infty}(\tau_{j}<\infty)=1
for all
Q\in U_{j}
. Lastly, let us define
\tau:=\inf_{j\geq 1}\tau_{j}
. Then it must follow that for any
P\in\mathcal{P}
we have,

P^{\infty}(\tau<\infty)=P^{\infty}\Bigl(\bigcup_{j\geq 1}\{\tau_{j}<\infty\}\Bigr)\leq\sum_{j\geq 1}P^{\infty}(\tau_{j}<\infty)\leq\sum_{j\geq 1}\alpha_{j}\leq\alpha.

Now as for power, it’s very trivial to see at this point. Meaning, take any
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
. Since
\{U_{j}\}
covers
\mathcal{Q}
, we can pick and choose any
j
with
Q\in U_{j}
. Clearly then
Q^{\infty}(\tau_{j}<\infty)=1
and
\tau\leq\tau_{j}
entails that
Q^{\infty}(\tau<\infty)=1
, and thus we are done!
∎

What we have accomplished here is rather remarkable.
We have showed that on a Polish space
\mathsf{X}
, it’s alright for
\mathcal{Q}
to be uncountable if we are only seeking power-one. We cleverly here used the fact that the weak topology on
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
is second-countable. Hence by Lemma
5
, any construction we choose that’s indexed by
\mathcal{Q}
automatically reduces to countably many neighborhoods. As we have already extensively noted, a crucial requirement is a local positive KL barrier around each of the
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
. Now, note that as in our Proposition
3
, this weak lower semicontinuity is sufficient but not necessary. In words: in cases where
\mathcal{Q}
contains atomic laws, we can exploit discontinuous events and cleverly get the uniform power one laws even when the weak lower semicontinuity fails. To that end, it may be better to analyze this under weak star (equivalently,
\tau
) topology to understand better this topological necessity if you will.

5
Weak lower semicontinuity not necessary for power-one testing

We will now make clear why weak lower semicontinuity is a sufficient, but not necessary, condition for achieving power-one testing. That’s quite a nuanced idea, hence we formalize that in the following proposition.

Proposition 3
.

Let
\mathsf{X}=[0,1]
on the Borel
\sigma
-algebra. Let
Q=\delta_{0}
and for
k\in\mathbb{N}
, define
P_{k}:=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{1/k}
. Then let
\mathcal{P}:=\{P_{k}:k\in\mathbb{N}\}
. Then,

1.

\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})=\log 2>0
.

2.

\Phi(R)=\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{KL}(R\|P)
is not weakly lower semicontinuous at
Q
.

3.

For every
\alpha\in(0,1)
, there exists a level
\alpha
sequential test with power-one against
Q
.

4.

There exists an e-process
(E_{n})_{n\geq 0}
for
\mathcal{P}
with exact growth rate
\log 2
under
Q
.

Proof.

We will start the
first
statement. Take some particular
k
. We know that
P_{k}(\{0\})=1/2
, hence it follows that
Q=\delta_{0}\ll P_{k}
. Therefore we get that,

\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P_{k})=\int\log\Bigl(\frac{dQ}{dP_{k}}\Bigr)dQ=\log\Bigl(\frac{Q(\{0\})}{P_{k}(\{0\})}\Bigr)=\log\Bigl(\frac{1}{1/2}\Bigr)=\log 2.

Then quickly taking
\inf_{k}
gives us
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})=\log 2
. We now prove the
second
statement. We have that
P_{k}\Rightarrow Q
weakly since for every
f\in C_{b}([0,1])
,

\int fdP_{k}=\tfrac{1}{2}f(0)+\tfrac{1}{2}f(1/k)\to\tfrac{1}{2}f(0)+\tfrac{1}{2}f(0)=\int fdQ.

However, for each
k
, its clear that
\Phi(P_{k})=0
because
\mathrm{KL}(P_{k}\|P_{k})=0
. However, as we just showed,
\Phi(Q)=\log 2>0
. Therefore it’s easy to see that
\Phi(Q)=\log 2\not\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}\Phi(P_{k})=0
. So unfortunately that means that
\Phi
is not weakly lower semicontinuous at
Q
. Let’s now prove the
third
statement. Consider some
\alpha\in(0,1)
and choose
m
with
2^{-m}\leq\alpha
. Then define,

\tau:=\begin{cases}m,&\text{if }(X_{1},\dots,X_{m})\in A_{m},\\
\infty,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Clearly, under
Q^{\infty}
we have that
X_{i}=0
wp 1 for all
i
, so
\tau=m
almost surely and the power is one. Note that under any
P_{k}^{\infty}
, we have that
P_{k}^{\infty}(\tau<\infty)=P_{k}^{m}(A_{m})=2^{-m}\leq\alpha
. So it follows that the level is at most
\alpha
uniformly over
\mathcal{P}
. It remains to now prove the
fourth
statement. To begin, simply define
E_{0}:=1
and for
n\geq 1
,

E_{n}:=2^{n}\mathbf{1}\{X_{1}=\cdots=X_{n}=0\}.

Clearly then for each
k
and each
n\geq 1
, we have that,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P_{k}^{\infty}}[E_{n}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P_{k}^{\infty}}\left[2^{n}\mathbf{1}\{X_{1}=\cdots=X_{n-1}=0\}\mathbf{1}\{X_{n}=0\}\middle|\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\right]

\displaystyle=2^{n}\mathbf{1}\{X_{1}=\cdots=X_{n-1}=0\}P_{k}(X_{n}=0)

\displaystyle=2^{n}\mathbf{1}\{X_{1}=\cdots=X_{n-1}=0\}\cdot\tfrac{1}{2}=E_{n-1}.

Therefore
(E_{n})
is a martingale under each
P_{k}
and must thereby be an
e
-process for
\mathcal{P}
. Now under
Q^{\infty}
,
E_{n}=2^{n}
wp 1. Thus we get that
(1/n)\log E_{n}=\log 2
, hence indeed the optimal growth rate occurs for this
e
-process.
∎

We will detail the nuance here as follows. We have just shown through Proposition
3
that weak lower semicontinuity is actually not necessary. Notice how atoms or other discontinuous (but still measurable) features that aren’t taken into account by weak neighborhoods allow power-one tests, even when the weak lower semicontinuity fails. In this work, as we are dealing with asymptotics and therefore worked in the weak topology (using tools like Sanov’s Theorem). However, getting some sort of topological condition that’s necessary for all bounded measurable tests is a very nuanced and tricky.

6
Aggregating tests to a consistent e-process

Let us now make our construction of such power-one tests much stronger, building on a technique by
[
30
]
for a different purpose. As opposed to just making some stopping rule, what we’re going to do is build one nonnegative adapted process which satisfies the
e
-process constraint and diverges under each alternative. To this end, we first will define a lemma that converts tests to
e
-processes.

Lemma 6
.

Suppose that there exists a sequence
(\alpha_{k})_{k\geq 1}\subset(0,1)
such that
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}\leq 1
. And for each
k\geq 1
suppose there exists some stopping rule
\tau_{k}
which satisfies
\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}P^{\infty}(\tau_{k}<\infty)\leq\alpha_{k}
and
Q^{\infty}(\tau_{k}<\infty)=1
for every
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
. Now define
\sigma_{k}:=\tau_{k}\vee k
and for
n\geq 0
,

E_{n}:=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}\{\sigma_{k}\leq n\}.

Then, it follows that
(E_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is a nondecreasing
e
-process for
\mathcal{P}
and for every
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
,

Q^{\infty}\left(\lim_{n\to\infty}E_{n}=\infty\right)=1.

Proof.

We will first prove that
(E_{n})
is well-defined (and adapted) process. Clearly, for each
n\geq 0
, we know that
\mathbf{1}\{\sigma_{k}\leq n\}=0
whenever
k>n
because
\sigma_{k}\geq k
. So it follows that,

E_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{\sigma_{k}\leq n\}\leq n.

Therefore, it follows that
E_{n}
is finite for every
n
, clearly. And, since
\sigma_{k}
is a stopping rule it follows that
\{\sigma_{k}\leq n\}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
, so
E_{n}
is
\mathcal{F}_{n}
measurable. Now, because each indicator
n\mapsto\mathbf{1}\{\sigma_{k}\leq n\}
is nondecreasing, it follows that the process
(E_{n})
is also nondecreasing. Let us next show that the
e
-process property holds. To this end, take any
P\in\mathcal{P}
and any stopping rule
\tau
. Because
(E_{n})
is nondecreasing, it follows that
E_{\tau}=\lim_{m\to\infty}E_{\tau\wedge m}
. Also, for every
m\geq 1
we know that,

E_{\tau\wedge m}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}\{\sigma_{k}\leq\tau\wedge m\},

and all terms are necessarily nonegative. So by both the mct and Tonelli’s theorem it follows that,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P^{\infty}}[E_{\tau}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P^{\infty}}\left[\lim_{m\to\infty}E_{\tau\wedge m}\right]

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{P^{\infty}}[E_{\tau\wedge m}]

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{P^{\infty}}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}\{\sigma_{k}\leq\tau\wedge m\}\right]

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}P^{\infty}(\tau_{k}\leq\tau\wedge m).

Now, for each
k
and
m
, it’s obvious that
\{\sigma_{k}\leq\tau\wedge m\}\subseteq\{\sigma_{k}<\infty\}
. So,
P^{\infty}(\sigma_{k}\leq\tau\wedge m)\leq P^{\infty}(\sigma_{k}<\infty)
. Let us use this bound and the definition of
\sigma_{k}
. If we do this we get that,

\mathbb{E}_{P^{\infty}}[E_{\tau}]\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}P^{\infty}(\sigma_{k}<\infty)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}P^{\infty}(\tau_{k}<\infty)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}\leq 1.

Now,
P\in\mathcal{P}
and
\tau
were arbitrary picked, so it follows that
(E_{n})
is an
e
-process for
\mathcal{P}
. All we need to do now is show divergence under each
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
. Take such a
Q
. By assumption we have that for every
k\geq 1
,
Q^{\infty}(\tau_{k}<\infty)=1
. Now, since
\sigma_{k}=\tau_{k}\vee k
, it follows that for every
k\geq 1
,
Q^{\infty}(\sigma_{k}<\infty)=1
also. If we take a countable intersection of almost sure events, the result will also be almost sure. Hence there must exist some event
A
with
Q^{\infty}(A)=1
such that for every
\omega\in A
and every
k\geq 1
,
\sigma_{k}(\omega)<\infty
. Let us consider some particular
\omega\in A
and let
m\geq 1
. It follows then that
M_{m}(\omega):=\max_{1\leq k\leq m}\sigma_{k}(\omega)<\infty
. So whenever
n\geq M_{m}(\omega)
it follows that,

E_{n}(\omega)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}\{\sigma_{k}(\omega)\leq n\}\geq\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbf{1}\{\sigma_{k}(\omega)\leq n\}=m.

Now, this holds for every
m\geq 1
, so we can indeed conclude that
\lim_{n\to\infty}E_{n}(\omega)=\infty
. Therefore,

Q^{\infty}\left(\lim_{n\to\infty}E_{n}=\infty\right)=1.

So, we are done.
∎

Corollary 1
.

Suppose that the assumptions of Theorem
1
hold. For each
k\geq 1
let’s let
\alpha_{k}:=2^{-k}
. And, let us choose a stopping rule
\tau_{k}
given by Theorem
1
with level at most
\alpha_{k}
and power one against all
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
. Then it follows that the process
\sigma_{k}:=\tau_{k}\vee k
and,

E_{n}:=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}\{\sigma_{k}\leq n\},

is a nondecreasing
e
-process for
\mathcal{P}
such that for every
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
,

Q^{\infty}\left(\lim_{n\to\infty}E_{n}=\infty\right)=1.

Proof.

We know that
\sum_{k-1}^{\infty}2^{-k}=1
. This is exactly Lemma
6
applied using
\alpha_{k}=2^{-k}
. So we are done.
∎

Note that in Theorem
1
, we absolutely do not make any assumptions on uniform KL separation between the null and alternative classes. Meaning, we only assume pointwise for each
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
that,

\Phi(Q)=\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P)>0.

However, we certainly could have that
\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\Phi(Q)=\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P)=0
. So the class of alternatives is allowed to come arbitratily close to the nulls.

7
REGROW
e
-process against
\mathcal{P}^{c}

Unfortunately, we cannot blindly use the
\operatorname{GROW}
criterion in many cases, and the alternative being
\mathcal{P}^{c}
certainly is one of those. Meaning, there is no uniform positive
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}
separation from the null, so it’s nonsensical to think of a worst-case absolute guarantee on the growth over all of
\mathcal{P}^{c}
. So we need to compare relative to the pointwise optimal rate, don’t we? Meaning, we will normalize by
n
and then compare to the pointwise asymptotic
\operatorname{GRO}
benchmark. We will define all of these as follows. We will define
e
-process as follows just to keep this section self-contained. Suppose that
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{proc}}(\mathcal{P})
is the class of all nonnegative adapted processes
E=(E_{n})_{n\geq 0}
such that for every stopping rule
\tau
taking values in
\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
,

\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{P^{\infty}}[E_{\tau}]\leq 1,

where here
E_{\infty}=\limsup_{n\to\infty}E_{n}
. Throughout we will have the convention that
\log 0:=-\infty
throughout. For
\mathcal{Q}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
, once again recall that
\Phi(Q):=\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P)
. We will define the pointwise asymptotic
\operatorname{GRO}
value as,

\operatorname{GRO}_{\infty}(Q):=\sup_{E\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{proc}}(\mathcal{P})}\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\infty}}[\log E_{n}].

Now, suppose that
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\{Q\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(X):\Phi(Q)<\infty\}
. Then, let us define the asymptotic
\operatorname{REGROW}
value as,

\operatorname{REGROW}_{\infty}(\mathcal{Q}):=\sup_{E\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{proc}}(\mathcal{P})}\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\liminf_{n\to\infty}\left(\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\infty}}[\log E_{n}]-\operatorname{GRO}_{\infty}(Q)\right).

We will now present our asymptotic
\operatorname{REGROW}
theorem.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
is weakly compact and define
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{fin}}:=\{Q\in\mathcal{P}^{c}:\Phi(Q)<\infty\}
. Then, it follows that there exists some
e
-process
E^{\star}\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{proc}}(\mathcal{P})
such that wp 1 under
Q^{\infty}
for every
Q\in\mathcal{P}^{c}
,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log E_{n}^{\star}\geq\Phi(Q),

and we will adopt the convention that the rhs may be
+\infty
. So it holds therefore that for every
Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{fin}}
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\infty}}[\log E_{n}^{\star}]=\Phi(Q)=\operatorname{GRO}_{\infty}(Q).

Meaning,
\operatorname{REGROW}_{\infty}(\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{fin}})=0
. And, the supremum is attained by
E^{\star}
.

Proof.

Firstly, by Lemma
3
, it follows that the map
\Phi
is weakly lsc. Observe that necessarily each of the
Q\in\mathcal{P}^{c}
must follow
\Phi(Q)>0
. Because, if a particular
Q\in\mathcal{P}^{c}
were to have
\Phi(Q)=0
, then we could easily pick some
P_{m}\in\mathcal{P}
wherein
\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P_{m})\leq 1/m
. And then Pinsker’s inequality would tell us that,

\|Q-P_{m}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2m}}\to 0.

This would mean that
P_{m}\Rightarrow Q
. And, because
\mathcal{P}
is weakly compact it follows that its also weakly closed, which would mean that
Q\in\mathcal{P}
, a clear contradiction for us! So it must be the case that
Q\in\mathcal{P}^{c}
implies that
\Phi(Q)\in(0,\infty]
. Now, throughout this proof, let us take some particular but arbitrarily chosen bounded lipschitz metric generating the weak topology on
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
, which we will denote by
d_{\mathrm{BL}}
. Now, let’s let
(r_{k})_{k\geq 1}
be some enumeration of the rationals,
\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
. Now then for each of these
k
’s, let us define the weakly open set,

G_{k}:=\{R\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X}):\Phi(R)>r_{k}\}.

Now, for every
R\in G_{k}
, let us choose some
\delta_{k}(R)>0
such that the closed
d_{\mathrm{BL}}
-ball,
C_{k}(R):=\overline{B}^{\mathrm{BL}}_{\delta_{k}(R)}(R)
is contained in
G_{k}
. We will also set
U_{k}(R):=B^{\mathrm{BL}}_{\delta_{k}(R)}(R)
. Clearly, the family
\{U_{k}(R):R\in G_{k}\}
is an open cover of
G_{k}
. Thus, by Lemma
5
it must have some countable subcover, which we will denote by
\{U_{k,j}\}_{j\geq 1}
. Let us write
C_{k,j}:=\cl(U_{k,j})
. By definition, each
C_{k,j}
is a closed
d_{\mathrm{BL}}
ball, so it follows that it is convex and weakly closed. And moreover since
C_{k,j}\subseteq G_{k}
, so it follows that,

\inf_{R\in C_{k,j}}\Phi(R)\geq r_{k}.

Now, let us let for
t\geq 1
,
v_{t}:=6/(\pi^{2}t^{2})
so that
\sum_{t\geq 1}v_{t}=1
. In addition, we will set
S:=\sum_{k\geq 1}2^{-k}e^{-r^{2}_{k}}\in(0,\infty)
. And for
k,j\geq 1
, let us define,

w_{k,j}:=\frac{2^{-k-j}e^{-r^{2}_{k}}}{4S}.

Then it follows that,

\sum_{k\geq 1}\sum_{j\geq 1}w_{k,j}=\frac{1}{4S}\sum_{k\geq 1}2^{-k}e^{-r^{2}_{k}}\sum_{j\geq 1}2^{-j}=\frac{1}{4}.

Now, let us define our
e
-process. That is for
n\geq 0
,

E_{n}^{\star}:=\frac{3}{4}+\sum_{k\geq 1}\sum_{j\geq 1}w_{k,j}\sum_{t=1}^{n}v_{t}e^{r_{k}t}\mathbf{1}\{\widehat{Q}_{t}\in C_{k,j}\}.

This will be well defined and finite for every

8
Conclusion

In this paper, we present a general power-one existence theorem on Polish spaces and focusing on iid laws. The metrizability given to us from this weak topology allowed us to use Lindelöf’s reduction to go from an uncountable family of local neighborhoods to a countable collection of stopping times, which we can easily aggregate without a loss in
\alpha
-control. And to this end, we provide the first general theorem for existence of power-one tests: weak compactness of the null class in
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
is sufficient for power one testing against the null’s entire complement. However, the question of what a
necessary
condition for power-one testing would be remains open. Obviously, one would need to work on a different topology to be able to exploit discontinuous events. But, this must be carefully handled. Why? Because, as an example, our techniques of applying nonasymptotic Sanov for convex weakly closed sets and exploiting Lindelöf spaces for countable reductions no longer easily apply, if at all. Recent work
[
20
]
has explicated this exact problem in fact, meaning distinguishing finite samples can in fact depend on a notion of closure which is much finer than the weak topology, and our counter-example in Proposition
3
shows this. Meaning, weak neighborhoods can fail to detect a gap between the null and alternative, while discontinuous measurable events can still separate them. So while there certainly may exist a necessary condition for power-one sequential tests, we would need to be very careful in searching for it. In any case, our paper is the first to provide a general theorem of solely a topological property on the null class inducing a universal power-one sequential testing guarantee, a rather remarkable result.
