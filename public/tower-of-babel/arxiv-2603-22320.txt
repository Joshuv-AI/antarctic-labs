Bridging the Gap Between Climate Science and Machine Learning in Climate Model Emulation
Luca Schmidt, Nina Effenberger

Abstract
While climate models provide insights for climate decision-making, their use is constrained by significant computational and technical demands. Although machine learning (ML) emulators offer a way to bypass the high computational costs, their effective use remains challenging. The hurdles are diverse, ranging from limited accessibility and a lack of specialized knowledge to a general mistrust of ML methods that are perceived as insufficiently physical. Here, we introduce a framework to overcome these barriers by integrating both climate science and machine learning perspectives. We find that designing easy-to-adopt emulators that address a clearly defined task and demonstrating their reliability offers a promising path for bridging the gap between our two fields.



Context

This work reflects the opinions of two researchers working at the intersection of machine learning and climate science. Being at this crossroads has shown us how different the perspectives on climate model emulation are and how these perspectives ultimately define the research conducted in each field. This made us reflect on our own research practices. With this work, we hope to contribute toward bridging the gap between the two fields.
We welcome feedback from researchers working in one or both fields. If there is something we have missed or could improve, please get in touch. We hope that future versions of this manuscript can incorporate such feedback and become more rigorous and nuanced.

Introduction

Climate models represent our most advanced understanding of the climate system. Their insights into current and future climate change guide mitigation, adaptation, and other climate-sensitive societal and economic decisions. Running them, however, is both computationally demanding and technically complex, requiring substantial computational resources and specialized expertise.
The increasing demand for climate information, along with the cost and difficulty of running physical climate models, calls for more efficient modeling approaches. Climate model emulators address this need by approximating either specific model parameterizations or the full climate model, offering a much cheaper alternative to obtain climate information. Climate emulators can take many forms. They range from reduced-order physical models to full statistical and machine learning surrogates (e.g.

,

,
16
). What they all share is the goal of approximating key elements of advanced climate models.

In recent years, interest in climate model emulation has increased in both climate science and machine learning, accompanied by rapid methodological development–especially from the methodological community

.
For the climate science community, emulators provide an efficient way to augment simulations

38

For the machine learning community, particularly in computer vision, climate emulation offers a compelling scientific challenge and an opportunity to demonstrate the value of advanced modeling techniques in socially relevant applications

Despite this shared motivation, the two communities remain surprisingly disconnected. Their differing perspectives toward emulation are reflected in their workflows: the machine learning community typically develops data-driven models optimized for predefined tasks, whereas climate scientists analyze physical simulations to address specific scientific or application-driven questions.

These disparate approaches mean that the paths of methodological developers and applied users rarely intersect
by chance

Closer collaboration could benefit both sides. For the climate science community, the advantages may seem particularly clear: machine-learning emulators can substantially reduce computational cost and even enable capabilities that are infeasible with physical models. For example, they can combine multiple data sources (climate models or observational datasets)
[
28
]
, sample rare extreme events more efficiently
18

The machine learning community likewise has an interest in seeing its models used in real-world applications. Ultimately, increasing societal relevance gives these models a purpose.
This view aligns with the philosophical idea of
adequacy-for-purpose

Closer interaction between emulator developers and users could also enable the creation of a feedback loop between emulators and physical simulations. By enabling large ensembles and sensitivity analyses, machine-learning models can identify regions where additional physical simulations would most effectively reduce uncertainty
[
37
]
. These insights guide more targeted physical simulation efforts, which in turn provide improved training data for the emulators.

This paper aims to address the disconnect between the technical and applied communities. We first clarify how the term
climate emulator
is understood in each field and then analyze the differing perspectives that shape how climate scientists and machine learning researchers approach emulation. Building on this, we present a practical tutorial that identifies synergies between the research fields and demonstrates how closer collaboration can be realized. Ultimately, we hope that this paper supports a more targeted development of machine learning-based emulators and consequently increases the uptake of these emulators in decision-making contexts.

Definition: What are emulators and what can they be used for?

The IPCC defines emulation as “reproducing the behavior of complex, process-based models (namely Earth system models, ESMs) via simpler approaches”
[
21
]
. Models developed for this purpose are often referred to as simple climate models because they represent the Earth system using simplified physical process formulations. Simple climate models typically aim to reproduce key decision-relevant statistics of climate variables rather than the full dynamical evolution of the climate system
[
26
,
19
]
.

In contrast, our main focus here is on machine learning models that
emulate
physical climate models by reproducing their behavior (see
Figure

1
), which we refer to as ML emulators or simply emulators. These emulators may be trained on global or regional climate model output, or exclusively on observational or reanalysis data,
[
1
, e.g. ]
. Examples include radiative forcing exploration
[
29
, e.g. ]
, climate model parameter tuning
[
2
, e.g. ]
, and regional climate data generation
[
28
, e.g. ]
. All emulators we consider share the goal of representing climate processes at different spatial and temporal scales.
Unlike traditional (simple) climate models, which rely on explicit physical laws, ML emulators learn statistical relationships directly from data. The literature sometimes uses terms like physical and statistical emulators to distinguish between the two
[
30
]
. However, whether an emulator is statistical or physical is not a fundamental distinction. In principle, any model (including simple climate models) can be emulated statistically.

Depending on their design and training objective, ML climate emulators can either replicate the behavior of existing climate models or extend their capabilities. In the most basic case, they act as efficient surrogates for physical models and reproduce their behavior at a lower computational cost. Such surrogate emulators can be validated with the same diagnostics as the original climate model, but they offer little inherent modeling advantage, as they merely reproduce the system’s behavior and inherit the same validation challenges
[
27
,
10
, e.g. ]
.

At the other end of the spectrum, emulators can extend the capabilities of traditional models. For example, they may combine heterogeneous data sources (e.g., multiple climate models or observations), learn relationships that are difficult to parameterize, or target specific phenomena, such as extreme events. These extensions can provide clear added value, for example, by embedding bias correction directly into the modeling framework or enabling sampling of extreme events. However, they also make validation and interpretation more challenging because the emulator’s outputs no longer directly correspond to those of the climate model. The transition between surrogates and emulators with extended functionality is gradual, making it challenging to understand how emulators relate to traditional physical models. Even when used purely as a surrogate for climate models, the emulator’s scope can often extend beyond that of classic climate models, for example, by enhancing uncertainty quantification through more efficient sampling.

3.1
A machine learning researcher’s view

From an ML researcher’s perspective, climate emulation presents an appealing scientific challenge–one where ML can shine by replacing expensive climate model runs with fast surrogate models.

Machine learning research typically begins with a clearly defined task — for example, learning a mapping from coarse- to high-resolution climate data — followed by selecting suitable datasets.
In this workflow, much effort is directed towards developing and evaluating models, while dataset selection is often guided by practical considerations such as availability, resolution, and compatibility with ML pipelines.
Yet, considerable effort is then devoted to making the data
ML ready
, i.e., bringing it into the right format. In climate emulation, this naturally makes gridded reanalysis products a preferred choice: they are readily accessible, standardized, integrate smoothly into ML pipelines, and are often treated as proxies for observational reference data.

Once the data is selected and pre-processed, the researcher’s attention shifts to choosing a suitable algorithm, training a model, and tuning hyperparameters. Benchmarking plays a central role in this phase, typically relying on standardized, often aggregated performance metrics with the goal of developing a model that ranks favorably against other baselines. The implicit assumption is that the top-performing model will also be the most suitable starting point for any downstream application
14
.

Recent advances in generative probabilistic models have made them particularly attractive for climate emulation, as they can provide probabilistic predictions. In practice, this uncertainty is often characterized through the spread of the model’s predictions. However, other sources of uncertainty—such as those related to data selection, predictor choice, model transferability, or non-stationarity—are typically considered separately or remain challenging to quantify in a systematic way
10
,
33
.

3.2
A climate scientist’s view

In contrast to ML research, applied science is usually motivated by a broader research question; for example, how a climate variable in a certain region is affected by climate change.

Data collection and pre-processing follow from this question, drawing on sources such as observational datasets, reanalysis products, and climate model outputs. The researcher usually first defines clear criteria, such as independence, performance, and spread
[
22
]
, and then identifies datasets that best meet them. In practice, no single dataset will be perfect: one may perform best under one criterion while another excels under different criteria. This is particularly challenging in climate science, where relevant time horizons extend far beyond what can be directly verified, and researchers must rely on model simulations that differ in many aspects. Uncertainty quantification is therefore not optional but central. In applied research, data selection is essential; yet, in the robust case, conclusions must remain valid regardless of the datasets and methods used.

Following initial data selection, the researcher begins analyzing the data to address the research question. During this phase, exploratory analyses may reveal unexpected patterns or insights, which can lead to further investigation, additional data collection, or refinement of the original data selection or research question. This iterative cycle of analysis and validation continues until the researcher is satisfied that the data and the findings are robust. Finally, the results are interpreted, visualized, and communicated, ensuring that the findings are both clear and reproducible.

But where and how do machine learning emulators fit into this pipeline?

4
Guiding emulator development

Methodological and applied research pathways rarely intersect (
Figure

2
); climate scientists often bypass emulators altogether, while machine learning researchers frequently develop them without explicitly considering their practical use. Most emulators are developed to showcase methodological innovation. Consequently, their performance beyond well-defined benchmark settings remains uncertain, and design choices are frequently guided by relative model rankings rather than a clear assessment of how well an emulator serves a specific application. In addition, modeling assumptions, limitations, and uncertainties are often not communicated in ways that are familiar to applied researchers.

In the following, we examine the steps needed to connect ML emulators to applied research. To be effective, emulators must do more than simply function from a technical standpoint; they must provide genuine scientific value. We propose three criteria for a robust emulator: it must address a scientifically-motivated task, perform that task reliably, and be easy for the broader community to adopt. The remainder of this section is structured around these three pillars: adequacy for purpose, trustworthiness, and accessibility. A checklist that can guide practical implementation is provided in
Figure

A.1
.

4.1
Adequacy for purpose

ML climate emulators vary greatly in their method, complexity, requirements, and outputs
[
33
]
. Most of their development is driven by methodological progress, which has been essential for advancing the field, but makes it difficult to determine what a given emulator can provide and how suitable it is for a particular applied task.

The first step in developing an emulator should be to clearly define the specific problem that the emulator aims to solve. This includes identifying relevant predictors, the spatial and temporal domains, and selecting evaluation criteria. While the technical definition of the task lies with the ML developer, it should be informed by the specific needs of the applied researchers who will ultimately use it, as illustrated in
Figure

2
.
The same applies to data selection, which is frequently shaped by availability and convenience rather than by demonstrated adequacy for a specific application. This tendency is also reflected in the common practice of benchmarking
[
13
, e.g.,]
, which is valuable for standardized comparisons across models using consistent datasets and metrics
[
34
]
. At the same time, strong performance in these settings does not guarantee real-world applicability
[
20
]
. Benchmarking primarily supports the comparison of model architectures under the assumption that data are exchangeable. However, in climate science, data is tied to its physical context, and model performance can strongly depend on the specific site, season, and other factors
[
10
]
. For example, emulators producing output on daily timescales may be insufficient for applications such as wind power forecasting
[
8
]
.
Therefore, the input data used to train the emulator should undergo systematic checks
[
17
,
12
]
, with predictor variables chosen to reflect relevant physical processes.

Model evaluation should go beyond simply comparing average statistics of relevant variables, but instead consider the entire emulator workflow
[
10
]
.
Evaluations must also account for large internal climate variability
[
4
]
and should separate natural variability from the forced climate signal, for example, by using sufficiently large ensembles
[
20
]
. Therefore, evaluation metrics should be chosen with the intended application in mind and justified accordingly
[
18
]
. Further, evaluating climate model data on a point-by-point basis usually does not make sense; compare
4
. Instead, distributional properties such as variability and extremes
[
31
]
, alongside physically meaningful inter-variable relationships and object-based metrics that evaluate the structure and evolution of coherent weather phenomena, are more meaningful
[
7
]
. Such an evaluation need not be exhaustive across all possible metrics but it should clearly validate the emulator’s performance relative to its stated scientific purpose. Finally, comparing results against multiple observational products whenever possible strengthens the robustness of these findings
[
11
]
.

4.2
Trustworthiness

Another barrier to the uptake of emulators is the lack of trust within the climate science community. This hesitation arises because an emulator’s underlying assumptions, sources of uncertainty, and limits of robustness are often poorly understood. Consequently, the added value of these tools, and with it their status relative to traditional physical models, remains ambiguous. Without a consistent framework to quantify its value, it is unclear whether an emulator is trustworthy enough for decision-making.
Added value is not a universal metric; its definition varies significantly across the literature
[
5
,
9
]
. While it is often quantitatively defined as an emulator’s proximity to a reference dataset, true added value is inherently context-dependent. For example, a common pitfall when evaluating downscaling emulators

. Consequently, added value is highly sensitive to the chosen metrics, variables, scales, and specific user requirements. A good understanding of what constitutes added value can be a key indicator of whether an emulator will be deployed in practice.

Furthermore, understanding the uncertainty in the modeling pipeline is essential for building trust in ML emulators
[
15
]
.
In climate modeling, significant uncertainties stem from model structure, parameterizations, internal variability, emission scenarios, and inter-variable relationships
[
33
,
38
,
18
,
20
]
. Additionally, uncertainties in different observational or reanalysis products are large and can even exceed those of global climate models
[
17
]
.
Emulator design directly shapes which uncertainties are represented and how the resulting outputs must be interpreted. To account for this, uncertainties arising from data choice, preprocessing, or modeling assumptions should be explicitly documented and discussed
[
10
,
33
]
.

Another key question regarding the usefulness of an emulator is whether it remains valid beyond the training and testing conditions. In climate applications, this assumption is particularly challenging because predictor–predictand relationships may not remain stationary under climate change
[
6
]
. Establishing the trust necessary to apply emulators beyond their original development context requires an explicit assessment of their ability to generalize across temporal and spatial scales, scenarios, climate models, variables, or other aspects
[
12
,
33
]
.

4.3
Accessibility

Even when emulators have demonstrated skill and gained trust, practical barriers often hinder their adoption. A lack of standardized model access, reproducible workflows, and interoperable software can prevent ML tools from being integrated into established research pipelines.
Therefore, shifting toward more open release practices is a prerequisite for moving emulators from individual experiments to widely adopted tools.
In this context, documentation plays an important role.
To ensure accessibility and support meaningful co-evaluation, developers should clearly document model assumptions, limitations, and intended use cases, avoiding unnecessary technical terms. We recommend adopting interoperable data formats, such as netCDF or zarr, to ensure emulator outputs can be seamlessly integrated into established research workflows.

While unconventional, we believe it would be beneficial for ML researchers to explicitly state their interest in potential use cases within their publications. Such statements could foster impactful collaborations, ensuring that model development yields results that are directly meaningful to the applied community.

5
Successfully applying an emulator

While the previous section outlined the developer’s responsibility in building a robust emulator, a successful transition into climate research depends equally on the engagement of applied scientists. Currently, the lack of collaboration represents a missed opportunity; under the principle of adequacy for purpose
[
24
]
, a model’s value is fundamentally defined by its utility in solving real-world problems. But at which point exactly should researchers from the two fields interact?

Figure

2
suggests a simple solution: instead of using observational records, reanalysis products, or model output, applied researchers could use emulator-generated data. However, this rarely happens in practice
[
10
, e.g. ]
.
The issue is usually not the quality of emulator-generated data. In fact, many studies demonstrate the strong performance of recent ML emulators
[
18
,
28
,
36
]
. The challenges lie elsewhere, and we discuss how they can be addressed by the machine learning community in detail in
Section

4
.

In theory, the application of emulators is relatively straightforward. Since emulators function essentially as a new data source, their validation and usage should be performed in a manner consistent with traditional physical data. Selecting a specific emulator is highly dependent on the research question; therefore, we refrain from offering universal recommendations here. The final selection should be guided by the three principles established earlier: fit for purpose, trustworthiness, and accessibility.
In practice, these translate into three practical questions for the researcher: Can this emulator provide the specific data needed to answer my question? Do I trust its performance? Do I have the technical capabilities to implement the model or download the resulting data? In addition to this, there is a psychological barrier: researchers must have the confidence to move beyond traditional physical models and decide how an emulator should be positioned alongside them. Furthermore, the emulator must demonstrate a tangible advantage over established methods. While this is partially addressed by the identified added value, the decision ultimately hinges on a cost-benefit analysis and the perceived benefits must clearly outweigh the effort required to implement the new approach.

Some scientific questions cannot (yet) be addressed reliably with ML-based climate emulators. In particular, emulators trained only on historical data are of limited use for
projecting
future climate states. This differs from weather emulation, where short-term forecasts can often be made from the current atmosphere
[
25
, e.g. ]
. Unlike physical climate models, climate emulators do not explicitly represent Earth system feedbacks, such as sea-ice–albedo interactions, and are therefore unable to sufficiently generalize under changing climatic conditions
[
35
]
. For similar reasons, they are also less naturally suited to counterfactual analyses, which can be implemented more directly in physical simulators through controlled interventions on model components or forcings.

Ultimately, emulator selection is a process of matching the specific requirements of a research question with the proven properties of a model. A researcher interested in quantifying the uncertainty of extremes, for example, will only deem an emulator useful and trustworthy if its distributional properties have been rigorously tested. While we recommend that ML researchers perform extensive, purpose-oriented validation, we acknowledge that it is impossible to address every potential research question when first presenting a new method. However, if there is no evidence that a model is likely to fit the specific scope, the risk of using it may outweigh its potential benefits.

After deciding for an emulator, the applied researcher should further validate that it adequately captures the events they are interested in. Much like the standard procedure for selecting physical climate model data, this process usually involves benchmarking against observations or reanalysis data to ensure it makes sense for her application. After this validation step, the data can be used in the same way as validated output from a physical climate model.

6
Conclusion

Since emulator development and application are often conducted by separate research communities, the success of these tools depends on successfully bridging the two. We find that when developers keep specific applications in mind from the outset, the transition to practical use becomes significantly more seamless. To support this, developers should prioritize adequacy for a scientific purpose, trustworthiness, and accessibility of their models, as these are the core values applied researchers require to trust and apply a model. Ultimately, when a researcher sees clear added value and feels confident in these principles, they are motivated to move beyond traditional approaches. This hopefully allows for creating a connection that transforms a theoretical model into a standard scientific tool.
