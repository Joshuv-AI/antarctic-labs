Appropriate translational designs for determining causal force-based (manual therapy) treatment mechanisms
Authors: Chad Cook, Damian Keter, Garrett Bullock, John Burns, Cynthia Green, M. Terry Loghmani, Beth Winkelstein, Giacomo Rossettini, William R. Reed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Treatment mechanisms of force-based manipulation (manual therapies) aim to explain not just “whether” the treatment works but “why” and “how” a treatment works. To date, a majority of studies have failed to address whether treatment mechanisms are causal, signifying that they are the steps or processes through which a manual therapy treatment leads to measurable changes in clinical outcomes. The objectives of this commentary were: 1) to outline various research designs capable of investigating causal treatment mechanisms, and 2) to illustrate each design type with examples drawn from existing literature.Main text:This commentary identified five causal designs that bridge the gap between basic scientific research and practical applications, allowing causal inference of mechanisms to outcomes. The five designs outlined were mediation analysis, longitudinal structural equation modeling, joint latent class models for longitudinal and time-to-event data, time-lagged analysis, and cross-lagged panel designs. Each design is described and when available, an example from the literature is provided for context.Conclusion:Each of the five designs have strengths and weaknesses, involve challenging research requirements such as larger sample sizes, concurrent mechanisms and clinical outcomes capture, and a sophisticated biostatistician to run and interpret research. Nonetheless, despite the challenges in performing this research, these studies are needed to reduce the potential for redundancy in research findings and waste of resources.

Background

Force-based manipulations (FBM) involve the passive application of mechanical force to the body’s surface with therapeutic intent (Neural Mechanisms of Force-Based Manipulations). Although notable differences in terminology persist, these treatment interventions are commonly referred to as “manual therapies” (Neural Mechanisms of Force-Based Manipulations; Degenhardt et al., 2024). Techniques classified under FBM/manual therapies encompass light touch, acupressure, needling, thrust and non-thrust joint manipulation, as well as massage (Keter et al., 2024). Their use is increasingly prevalent in managing musculoskeletal disorders (Cohen et al., 2021; Chen and Michalsen, 2017), and are endorsed by several clinical practice guidelines (George et al., 2021; Blanpied et al., 2017; Lim et al., 2025) (see Table 1).

Treatment mechanisms refer to the underlying processes or pathways by which a specific intervention exerts its effects on the body (Cook et al., 2025). Essentially, these mechanisms aim to explain not just “whether” the treatment works but “why” and “how” a treatment works. Causal inference is an analysis approach that often estimates the average treatment effect or the treatment effect across a population. While important, this methodology is limited in circumstances where multiple mechanistic pathways that may mediate the treatment effect are possible. Mechanistic research is characteristically conducted in basic science contexts, often involving animal models or asymptomatic human participants, to identify associations within biological, psychological, or social domains following the application of a particular FBM intervention (Carey et al., 2020). A recent umbrella review (Keter et al., 2025) identified numerous potential treatment mechanisms across FBM interventions, but highlighted that measurement complexity makes it difficult to determine their association with clinical outcomes.

Causal treatment mechanisms, by contrast, represent the definitive steps or processes through which a treatment leads to measurable changes in clinical outcomes (Keter et al., 2025). Causal mechanisms attempt to find the underlying pathways through which the treatment effects the outcome by identifying the direct (independent of any other variables) and indirect (resulting from a mediating variable) effects. For a treatment mechanism to be deemed “causal,” the research design must demonstrate a direct and reliable link between the mechanism itself and the resulting clinical effect, and that this association is not the result of chance. While numerous studies have explored potential treatment mechanisms in basic science or animal-based settings, yielding valuable preliminary insights, there remains a notable scarcity of translational research specifically addressing causal mechanisms in FBM. This gap has been highlighted as one of eight key areas of research inefficiency within the field (Aspinall et al., 2024).

Advances in causal mediation analysis have made it possible to both identify and quantify these mechanisms. However, these more advanced statistical analyses involve many obstacles that include identifying possible indirect/direct mechanisms, model assumptions, and possible unmeasured confounding. This commentary underscores the importance of advancing research on causal treatment mechanisms in FBM, acknowledging the inherent complexity and potential cost of such studies. In this commentary, the authors of this manuscript, who are members of a National Institute of Health grants’ working group, pursue two primary objectives: (1) to outline various research designs capable of investigating causal treatment mechanisms, and (2) to exemplify each design type with examples drawn from existing literature.

Main text

Key components of causal treatment mechanism designs:

Causal mechanisms study designs are unique because they bridge the gap between basic scientific research and practical applications, often in clinical or social settings. Not only do the studies focus on mechanisms, which we typically see in animal or preclinical research, but they also involve therapeutic management using a high-fidelity treatment of interest in symptomatic humans. Because of its complexity, the most meaningful causal mechanisms research involves collaboration across multiple disciplines, such as biology, psychology, and social sciences, to provide insight into the mechanisms involved in positive and adverse clinical outcomes.

Types of causal treatment mechanism designs:

There are numerous types of causal treatment mechanisms designs. All designs have strengths and limitations (Fig. 1). We outline five designs below, which can be embedded into clinical trials.

Example One - Mediation analyses:

Mediation analyses are likely the most common translational methods used when determining the causal effects of mechanisms on clinical outcomes. Mediation analyses involve a family of statistical methods that help explain how or why an intervention works by identifying and analyzing the mechanism (s) involved (Imai et al., 2010). In its simplest form (simple-basic mediation analysis), it is used to understand the process through which an independent variable (X) influences a dependent variable (Y) via a third variable, known as the mediator (M). Essentially, it helps to determine if the effect of X on Y is direct (X → Y) or if it operates through M which is an indirect effect X → M → Y). More advanced versions such as multiple mediation, moderated mediation, or moderated moderation add another layer of nuance. Advanced mediation models reveal how multiple pathways operate, when the mediation changes across conditions or groups, and how the process unfolds over time, offering a far deeper understanding than a simple single-mediator model.

Sometimes advanced mediation analyses are difficult to interpret, especially when direct and indirect effects are small (Fiedler et al., 2011). If no mediators are identified or hypothesized, and the relationship between the independent and dependent variable are direct, then the mediation analysis would not provide additional insight. Mediation analyses depend on several assumptions including no unmeasured confounding variables between X, Y and M and correct model specification. Mediation analyses are typically conducted using regression-based methods, a series of three regression models used to estimate the indirect effect (Baron and Kenny, 1986). Further, this approach requires large sample sizes and longitudinal data to provide reliable estimates, two elements that are difficult to obtain when capturing FBM mechanistic data (Agler et al., 2017). Though this method also does not provide a statistical test or 95% confidence interval for the indirect effect, bootstrap methods can be used to provide these. Previous work has investigated potential mediators of FBM treatment outcomes (Ribeiro et al., 2024; Paz et al., 2023).

Example Two - Longitudinal Structural Equation Modeling:

Longitudinal Structural Equation Modeling (LSEM) combines longitudinal data to model complex relationships over time and how these changes are related to each other [19]. It allows for the examination of direct and indirect effects, similar to mediation analysis, but also functions well in the presence of missing data and when multiple dependent variables are used. It helps answer questions related to how changes in one variable over time can influence another variable (e.g., does FBM at 3 months affect pain at 6 months) and accounts for any measurement error by using the latent variables. Disadvantages include the need for a large sample size, longitudinal data with consistent time intervals (Little, 2013) and a high degree of statistical knowledge to run these very computationally intensive methods (Farrell, 1994). We found no studies indexed on PubMed that have used LSEM when investing the role of FBM treatment mechanisms on clinical outcomes.

Example Three - Joint latent class models for longitudinal and time-to-event data:

Joint latent class models (JLCMs) are a sophisticated statistical approach that use unobserved heterogeneity (latent classes) to explain the association between longitudinal measurements (repeated observations over time) and time-to-event data (such as the time until an event occurs) [22,23]. These models are particularly useful in medical research and other fields where understanding the relationship between a biomarker’s progression (i.e. endogenous factors) and the occurrence of an event (exogenous outcome) is crucial. They handle complex relationships between longitudinal markers and time-to-event data and are particularly useful for making dynamic predictions about future outcomes based on past data (Proust-Lima et al., 2014). These models also allow for assessing competing risks and drop-out during follow-up, in relation to dynamic changes in biomarker levels (Andrinopoulou et al., 2014). Disadvantages include the fact that they are mathematically and computationally complex, requiring advanced statistical knowledge, significant computational resources, and are often very difficult to interpret. They require a sample size large enough to support multiple latent classes and can be computationally intensive. We were unable to identify any studies through PubMed that have used JLCMs when investing the role of FBM treatment mechanisms on clinical outcomes.

Example Four - Time-lagged analyses:

Time-lagged analysis (TLA) is a technique used in time series analysis to understand the relationship between variables over different time periods. It involves shifting the values of a variable at previous time points, known as the lag (Falkenström et al., 2020). Rather than using a cross-sectional analysis (same time point for both variables), TLA determines if a variable X collected at a given time point is associated with another variable Y collected at a second time point, while also accounting for Y at the first time point. This helps in capturing temporal dependencies and trends in the data, which can be crucial for prediction modeling. These methods provide a way to determine causality more than cross-sectional data. Simple TLA are easy to run but complex analyses require the efforts of a statistician with expertise in this area. Although useful in their ability to reflect a temporal relationship between mechanisms and clinical outcomes, it can be challenging to ensure that the observed effects are specifically from the mechanisms and not from other unidentified factors (Parsons and McCormick, 2024). We found no studies through PubMed that used a TLA when evaluating the role of FBM mechanisms on treatment outcomes.

Example Five - Cross-Lagged Panel Design:

This design involves measuring the same variables at multiple time points to assess the direction and strength of relationships over time (Lucas, 2023). It helps in understanding the causal relationships between variables by examining the cross-lagged effects. Cross-lagged panel designs allow for the examination of bidirectional relationships, meaning it can show if a mechanism influences an outcome and whether the outcome influences the mechanism. Disadvantages include the fact that measurements must be taken at the same time and that the relationships remain constant over time (which is quite difficult) (Hamaker et al., 2015). Furthermore, the results are often difficult to interpret. As with the majority of the previously described analyses, we found no instances in PubMed in which cross-lagged panel designs were used to measure the role of FBM mechanisms on clinical outcomes.

Why not just use a FBM mechanistic clinical trial?

In a mechanistic clinical trial, the primary outcomes are the underlying mechanisms through which treatment effects occur, while the study groups include two or more FBM interventions (National Center for Complementary and). Unlike traditional efficacy or effectiveness trials that focus on clinical outcomes to determine treatment effect, mechanistic trials aim to explore the “how” and “why” behind therapeutic change. These studies are useful as they help determine whether different FBM approaches activate distinct biological, psychological, or social mechanisms. However, mechanistic clinical trials typically do not measure clinical outcomes directly. As indicated earlier, causal mechanistic studies go a step further by identifying the specific processes (the mechanisms) that lead to measurable changes in clinical outcomes. Without establishing a causal link between mechanisms and outcomes, one cannot assume that observed mechanistic differences translate into meaningful clinical effects.

Challenges to designing a causal mechanisms study:

As stated, there is a notable scarcity of translational research specifically addressing causal mechanisms in FBM. Designing studies to investigate causal mechanisms presents a range of methodological challenges (Loghmani et al., 2025). These designs demand repeated and precise measurement of all proposed mediators and outcomes at multiple time points, before, during, and after the intervention, to establish the temporal ordering necessary for causal inference. Ensuring that changes in the mechanism precede changes in the outcome is critical to validating the hypothesized causal pathway. However, this level of granularity can be difficult to achieve, particularly when mechanism variables are abstract, latent, or not easily operationalized. Moreover, the logistical demands of frequent data collection, such as increased costs, participant burden, and extended timelines, can pose significant barriers to implementation. Researchers must therefore balance methodological rigor with practical feasibility, often requiring creative solutions such as leveraging technology for real-time data capture, using proxy measures, or applying advanced statistical techniques to handle missing or sparse data.

Outside a failure to emphasize the importance of this type of study, other factors for their scarcity include: 1) inconsistency of FBM intervention methods to study; 2) difficulty in reliability measuring mechanisms, especially in real-time or in response to subtle manual inputs; 3) requirement of individuals with dedicated disease processes in a clinical setting that are candidates for FBM interventions; 4) robust measures of mechanisms that likely reflect the many interactions across variables; and 5) financial resources to cover the costs associated with managing symptomatic patients, mechanisms measures at dedicated intervals, outcomes captured at dedicated intervals and enough time points and length to generalize to a clinical population.

Conclusion

In our commentary, we outline five different designs that can be used to measure causal treatment mechanisms of FBM on clinical outcomes. FBM is studied by a variety of mechanistic and clinical researchers and identifying the physiological mechanisms of FBM is critical to the advancement of the field, as well as to the clinical optimization of any therapeutic FBM intervention (Reed et al., 2024). One reason we addressed this commentary, was because the continued investigation of FBM mechanisms without understanding their causal effect toward clinical outcomes, was recently identified as an element of research waste by a series of international experts in manual therapy (Aspinall et al., 2024).

Each of the five designs we’ve outlined have challenges and benefits (Fig. 2), involve dedicated research requirements (such as larger sample sizes and a sophisticated biostatistician to run and interpret research), which is likely why only one of these methods have been used thus far in FBM mechanistic-that we are aware. Causal treatment mechanisms must be imbedded in designs that allow mediation tests and must be supported both by theoretical ties to the outcome, but also to critical statistical relationships that describe specific patterns of temporal changes (among mechanisms and outcomes) and lagged predictive associations. They provide explanations for why and how certain events or phenomena occur.

It is recommended that secondary analyses are performed using these study design methods to ascertain their practicality and usefulness in determining causal force-based (manual therapy) treatment mechanisms. While manual treatment has been shown effective in treating pain and other conditions, it is not currently known why these interventions work. These secondary analyses can inform more precise and carefully designed prospective future causal studies to determine which mechanisms are mediators of clinical response. Finally, clinical and research education is needed to help elucidate the need for and importance of determining the causal effect of force-based (manual therapy) treatment mechanisms, to improve clinical translation and ultimately patient outcomes.
