1. Executive Summary

This report describes the University of Iowa portion of a project that is now continuing at
Michigan State University. We are developing novel methods, algorithms, and software to
enable simulations of molecules and materials at high temperature. This is a key challenge
in chemistry and materials science. By refining an approach called Density Matrix
Quantum Monte Carlo (DMQMC), we developed faster and more accurate ways to conduct
these simulations. These advances will help us understand how temperature affects the
behavior of electrons, chemical bonds, and phase transitions in solids and molecules.
These breakthroughs are especially important for applications where light and heat drive
chemical reactions, superconductivity, and materials used in energy and sensing. In
addition, this project involved the development of the open-source HANDE-QMC software
package, supporting the broader community in benchmarking and developing finite-
temperature electronic structure methods.

2. Project Objectives

This project was conducted at the University of Iowa between 9/1/2020 - 12/31/2023. It was
then transferred to Michigan State University where the projected formally resumed on
3/1/2025. The activities at Michigan State University will be reported separately.

The overall goals of the project at University of Iowa were:

 • Engage in the development of the density matrix quantum Monte Carlo (DMQMC)
 method, including contributions to the HANDE-QMC open-source software
 package.
 • Study changes to molecular electronic structure caused by elevated electronic
 temperatures.

A piecewise propagator improves sampling efficiency of inverse temperature ranges

In our 2022 paper (Van Benschoten and Shepherd 2022), we introduced a piecewise
generalization of IP-DMQMC, which extended the simulation from sampling one inverse
temperature point per calculation to a full range. This modification overcame a key
limitation of IP-DMQMC. This was achieved by switching away from the interaction picture
and back to the Bloch equation at the target temperature. The resultant piecewise IP-
DMQMC (PIP-DMQMC) method takes advantage of a piecewise propagator to extend the
simulation beyond the target temperature. These were benchmarked in comparison to
ftFCI for a variety of molecular systems (Fig. 2) before being used on the methane and
water molecule.

Basis set extrapolation improves accuracy of finite-temperature DMQMC

In our 2024 paper (Van Benschoten and Shepherd 2024), we use two-electron systems to
analyze the basis set incompleteness error present in finite-temperature electronic
structure calculations. The free energy of a hydrogen atom in a vacuum is known to diverge
in the limit of an infinite basis set. In this paper, we show that basis set errors can be
systematically removed through extrapolation to the complete basis set limit when an
atom is placed in a periodic box, and the convergence behavior is similar to that of the
internal energy (Fig. 4). Near the ground state correlation dominates the internal energy and
ground-state extrapolation techniques can be applied to estimate the complete basis set
free energy. We relate this to the variational nature of the free energy in the canonical
ensemble and introduced a Hamiltonian partitioning that separates the electronic free
energy into non-interacting and interacting components, enabling the definition of a free
energy of correlation analogous to ground-state correlation energy.

4. Conclusions

The first part of the project “Towards exact finite temperature electronic structure in solids
and molecules” conducted at the University of Iowa contributed significant methods
development work that improved DMQMC.

Over the course of this project so far, we performed studies to understand and develop
modifications to the DMQMC algorithm. We found that asymmetric and piecewise
propagation could improve the algorithm and extracted thermodynamic variables using
machine learning. We studied molecules and proposed how basis set incompleteness
error could be removed from energies. These advances enabled more efficient and
accurate simulations of finite-temperature electronic structure, particularly in small
molecules and model solids.

The project has now transferred to Michigan State University where it will continue.
