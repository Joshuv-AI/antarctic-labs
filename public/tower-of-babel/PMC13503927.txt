Chirality induced by supramolecular self-assembly of fluorinated π-conjugated carbon dots for bright circularly polarized electroluminescent light-emitting diodes
Authors: Yuxin Shi, Qian Teng, Qinghua Tan, Chenhao Li, Jinyang Li, Xianzhi Song, Haoran Jia, Louzhen Fan, Fanglong Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Circularly polarized light-emitting diodes hold great promise for advanced photonic applications such as three-dimensional displays, quantum information processing, and optical spintronics. Carbon dots, owing to their metal-free nature, high stability, and structural versatility, have emerged as attractive alternatives. However, their intrinsic chirality is typically weak due to a lack of precise control over structure and molecular arrangement, and their application in electroluminescent devices has remained unrealized. Here, we report a class of supramolecular assemblies consisting of fluorinated π-conjugated carbon dots. Upon interaction with M/P-[N] hexahelicene as chiral inducers, a directed supramolecular self-assembly is formed via strong electrostatic and hydrogen bonding interactions, yielding a high absorption dissymmetry factor of up to 0.107. More significantly, we demonstrate the devices based on these supramolecular assemblies, achieving a maximum luminance of 9655 cd m−2 and electroluminescent dissymmetry factor value of 0.035. These results mark a significant step toward metal-free chiral optoelectronics and highlight the potential of carbon nanomaterials in next-generation display technologies.

Introduction

Achieving efficient solid-state circularly polarized luminescence (CPL) remains one of the most formidable challenges in modern photonic material research1,2. Circularly polarized light-emitting diodes (CP-LEDs), which emit photons with defined spin angular momentum, hold immense promise for next-generation technologies such as three-dimensional (3D) displays, secure optical data storage, and quantum information processing3–7. Yet, despite intense efforts, most known CPL-active materials based on organic chiral molecules or halide perovskites suffer from inherent limitations: poor photostability, low solution processability, or synthetic complexity4,8–11. These bottlenecks severely hinder their scalability and practical device integration.

Carbon dots (CDs), in contrast, offer a tantalizing alternative12–24. As a class of heavy-metal-free nanomaterials, they combine solution-processability, structural tunability, photo-thermal stability, and low toxicity25–30. In principle, such features make them promising candidates for optoelectronic applications, including CP-LEDs. Despite their potential, CDs with strong CPL activity have not yet been realized, typically exhibiting dissymmetry factors (g) on the order of 10−331,32. This limitation primarily arises from stochastic surface modification or complex chiral co-assemblies, which lack precise control over molecular orientation, intermolecular packing, or charge transport. To enhance CPL emission, CDs are often incorporated into certain matrices, particularly liquid crystals. However, limited by the poor conductivity of such matrices33–38, no electroluminescent devices based on CDs with chiroptical activity have been reported, highlighting a critical knowledge and application gap in this field.

To overcome these challenges, a paradigm shift in design strategy is essential. We hypothesized a structural design strategy in which CDs with spatially programmed electrostatic potential distribution could drive supramolecular self-assembly into ordered chiral nanomaterials under the influence of suitable chiral inducers39–43. Such an approach could simultaneously address several longstanding obstacles: achieving high g-factors via controlled molecular stacking, maintaining high photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY), and enabling efficient charge transport for device-level CPL emission44–46.

In this study, we report the rational design and synthesis of a class of supramolecular assemblies composed of fluorinated π-conjugated CDs functionalized with fluorine atoms as well as carbonyl and amino groups (F-πCD nanoassemblies). This structure and functional groups promote intermolecular hydrogen bonding and ordered stacking. Upon interaction with M/P-[N] hexahelicene as chiral inducers, these CDs form chiral supramolecular assemblies with a remarkable absorption dissymmetry factor (gabs) of 0.107, among the highest reported for CPL-active materials. More strikingly, by incorporating these chiral assemblies into an electroluminescent device structure, we achieve the CP-LEDs, exhibiting a maximum luminance of 9655 cd m−2 and electroluminescence (EL) dissymmetry factor value | gEL| of up to 0.035. This work not only demonstrates a powerful strategy for controlling chirality in nanomaterials, but also establishes a benchmark for chiral optoelectronics based on sustainable, metal-free carbon nanomaterials.

Results

Design, synthesis, and optical properties

F-πCD nanoassemblies are synthesized using 3,4,5,6-tetrafluorophthalic acid, carbamide, and methanol at 200 °C for 15 min, as the schematic illustration in Fig. 1a. After naturally cooling to room temperature, the resulting product is purified via water washing, centrifuge, and silica gel column chromatography; more details are provided in the Methods section. The ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) absorption spectrum exhibits one major absorption peak, centered at approximately 385 nm (Fig. 1c). Under UV light, a bright green emission with the fluorescence peak centered at approximately 515 nm is observed (excited at 365 nm, the inset of Fig. 1c). The absolute PLQY is determined to be ~90%. Moreover, the PL can be excited in a wide wavelength range and the position of the maximum emission peak is independent of the excitation wavelength (Fig. 1d). The maximum excitation wavelength is in good agreement with the corresponding excitonic absorption peak, indicating that the emission originates from the bandedge exciton-state decay. Ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy measurements further reveal the highest occupied molecular orbital (EHOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (ELUMO) energy levels to be −5.7 eV and −3.2 eV, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1). The chiroptical properties are investigated using circular dichroism (CD) and circularly polarized photoluminescence (CPL) spectroscopy, respectively. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 2, F-πCD nanoassemblies alone and the poly(N-vinylcarbazole) (PVK): F-πCD nanoassemblies exhibit no detectable CD signal. and M/P-[N] hexahelicene film alone exhibit the characteristic CD signal of helicene (~320 nm). When doped with M/P-[N] hexahelicene as chiral inducers (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 3)49–51, distinct negative and positive signals at 400–500 nm are observed, evidencing the successful induction of chirality. It should be noted that neither PVK nor M/P-[N] hexahelicene contributes directly to the emission; instead, they serve as the host matrix in the electroluminescent emission layer and as the chiral inducer, respectively (Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5). To quantitatively evaluate the chiroptical activity, the gabs is calculated. By adjusting the ratio of M/P-[N] hexahelicene to F-πCD nanoassemblies and the thickness of the blend film, the maximum gabs reaches 0.107 (Fig. 1g and Supplementary Fig. 6). This value is comparable to or even exceeds those of previously reported CPL-active nanomaterials, demonstrating the superiority of F-πCD nanoassemblies to synergistically interact with chiral inducer for enhanced chiroptical modulation (Supplementary Table 1). Furthermore, at 380 nm wavelength excitation, almost mirror-image CPL spectra are observed in the range of 400–650 nm, which further confirms that the induced chiroptical activity originates from these F-πCD nanoassemblies (Fig. 1h). These results suggest that F-πCDs strongly interact with the chiral inducer, leading to the formation of distinct supramolecular conformations and enabling effective chirality transfer from the chiral inducer to the achiral F-πCD nanoassemblies.

Orderly arrangement induced by intermolecular interactions

In order to investigate the structure of F-πCD nanoassemblies, scanning electron microscope (SEM) measurements are performed over a large area. At high concentrations (20 mg mL−1), numerous island-like clusters with sizes ranging from 200 to 1200 nm are observed, exhibiting uniform orientation and an inter-cluster spacing of approximately 200 nm (Fig. 2a). As the concentration decreased to 10 mg mL-1, the size of the island-like cluster is reduced to 400–500 nm, and the stacking between each cluster becomes more uniform and compact (Fig. 2b). However, it can be clearly observed that this structure is formed by countless stacks of F-πCDs. The morphology of F-πCD nanoassemblies is further characterized by the aberration-corrected transmission electron microscopy (AC-TEM). As shown in Fig. 2d, a well-ordered periodic arrangement is observed, exhibiting a characteristic, elongated shape in long-axis direction (Fig. 2e). In magnified color AC-TEM image (Fig. 2f), a distinct alignment between two F-πCDs with a specific distance (d ~ 0.36 nm) is clearly observed, and the center-to-center distance and its second horizontally adjacent neighbor is approximately 2.2 nm52.

Figure 2g further illustrates the stacking behavior of F-πCDs in both the X- and Y-directions, leading to formation of supramolecular assemblies. Starting from the bottom-left corner (yellow arrow), the arrangement forms mx × ny islands, where the geometry and aspect ratio of F-πCDs influence the intermolecular organization. Specifically, growth is more favorable in the Y-extended direction (following the dark blue line in Fig. 2g) than in the X-extended direction (following the red line), with the X-directional growth occurring in discrete steps (mx = 2, 4, 6…). This directional tendency verifies a shape-directed driving force governing the arrangement.

In order to understand how the structure of F-πCDs facilitates their orderly arrangement, intermolecular interaction is explored (Fig. 2h, i). Due to the difference in polarity (γF > γC=O > γNH2), electrostatic potential (φ) distribution is calculated. The φ of fluorine atoms and the carbonyl/amino groups at F-πCDs promotes the formation of weak intermolecular hydrogen bonds (N-H···O and N-H···F) between neighboring F-πCDs. This stabilizes the structure and facilitates the formation of the orderly periodic arrangement. Theoretical calculation further supports that the functional groups of F-πCDs is necessary for driving supramolecular assembly53–56. Control experiments further indicate that when intermolecular interaction is disrupted, only amorphous molecular aggregates are observed (Supplementary Fig. 7).

Structural characterizations

Raman spectrum is performed for F-πCD nanoassemblies to verify the crystallinity of graphitization. Here, the crystalline G band at about 1615 cm−1 is stronger than the disordered D band at about 1380 cm−1 with a large G to D intensity ratio (IG/ID) of approximately 1.5 (Fig. 3a). Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectrum (Fig. 3b) further confirms that the broad absorption peak in the region of 3000–3600 cm−1 is associated with the stretching of aromatic O–H and N–H bonds. Strong characteristic stretching vibration bands of C = N and C = O, as well as C-O are observed at 1650-1600 and 1350 cm−1, respectively, and characteristic stretching vibration bands of C-H and C–F are observed at 2800 and 1250 cm−1, respectively. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis demonstrates that F-πCD nanoassemblies are primarily composed of carbon (C), nitrogen (N), oxygen (O) and fluorine (F) (Supplementary Fig. 8). The deconvoluted high-resolution C 1 s XPS spectrum exhibits peaks corresponding to O-C = O (289.9 eV), C = O (288.1 eV), and C = C (285.4 eV) (Fig. 3c). The deconvoluted high-resolution F 1 s XPS spectrum confirms the presence of C-F (687.8 eV) (Fig. 3d). Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) experiments are performed to probe intermolecular interactions. In the 1H-NMR spectra (chloroform-d, ppm) (Fig. 3e), the peak at 7.2–7.3 ppm can be attributed to the residual proton signal of deuterated chloroform, which is consistent with its standard reference position. With increasing concentration from 0.5, 1, 2, 5 to 10 mg mL-1, significant chemical shift in the range of 5–10 ppm was observed. This is attributed to the N-H protons of the F-πCD and considered to reflect the intermolecular interaction (N-H···O and N-H···F) from the complexation between neighboring F-πCDs57. Moreover, 13C-NMR and 19F-NMR spectra further confirm sp2 domains (110–150 ppm), the presence of C = O (150–170 ppm) and C-F ( ~ 140–145 and ~155–160 ppm) in F-πCD nanoassemblies (Supplementary Fig. 9). This pronounced peak broadening can be attributed to the formation of structurally distributed π-conjugated nanoassemblies.

Theoretical investigations further verify that the origination of intermolecular interaction and relationship between structure and φ (Supplementary Fig. 10 and Supplementary Table 2). Based on the functional groups, we established eight simplified structural models. φ is calculated for comparison, including a CD without any functional groups (CD); with fluorine atoms (CD + F); with amino groups (CD + F + NH); with carbonyl groups (CD + F + CO); with both groups (F-πCD); F-πCD without fluorine atoms (F-πCD-F); without amino groups (F-πCD-F-NH); without carbonyl groups (F-πCD-F-CO). (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Figs. 11–13). For models with the modification of carbonyl groups at the center, electropositive (φ + ) is obviously concentrated at the molecular center, which is in obvious contrast with the electronegativity regions (φ-) at the edge. In contrast, models lacking these groups show more uniform or reversed distributions. Moreover, dipole moment of the eight models is further calculated to support the distribution of φ (Fig. 3g). The modification of fluorine (CD + F) or amino groups (F-πCDs-F-CO) have relatively small effect on the dipole changes, approximately 3.08 and 2.22 D, respectively. However, the modification of carbonyl groups as a strong electron withdrawing group substantially increase the dipole moment to approximately 6.71 D. All these theoretical calculations indicate that functional groups in F-πCDs plays a significant role in distribution of φ, which is important to the structure arrangement and chiroptical properties.

Structural characterizations reveal that the orderly arrangement in F-πCD nanoassemblies originates from their structure, particularly shape and the modification of functional groups, consistent with Fig. 1. This electronic structure promotes intermolecular hydrogen bonding, thus leading to the formation of an ordered assembly (as shown in Fig. 2).

Film properties

Considering the structure of F-πCDs, introducing chiral inducer M/P-[N] hexahelicene is expected to modulate their assembly behavior and enable chirality control. Figure 4a shows that the doping with M/P-[N] hexahelicene can significantly change the PL emission of blend film. A PL enhancement is observed, which is positively correlated with the volume ratio of M/P-[N] hexahelicene to F-πCD nanoassemblies, while declining when the molar ratio is further increased. Moreover, under different doping ratios and spin-coating speeds, the blend film consistently displays smooth and uniform surface morphology under ambient conditions (Fig. 4b). The film thickness increases from 22 to 35 nm with increasing M/P-[N] hexahelicene ratio and decreasing spin-coating speed. As shown in Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 6, the blend film displays slightly enhanced PL emission upon low-dose addition of M/P-[N] hexahelicene, reaching maximum brightness at a ratio of 1:0.1:0.1 (VPVK:VM-H:VF-πCD nanoassemblies). When the ratio of M/P-[N] hexahelicene to F-πCD nanoassemblies exceeds 2:1, PL intensity significantly decreases. Notably, the changing trend of the CD signals differs from that of PL. The CD signal is relatively high under a relatively small ratio of M/P-[N] hexahelicene, reaching the maximum value at 1:0.05:0.1 ratio. However, the CD signal is significantly weakened when the M/P-[N] hexahelicene is in large excess (3:1), accompanied by fluorescence quenching. Based on above experimental results, this decline is related to the film thickness and surface roughness. With the increase of film thickness, a rougher surface structure with larger particle size and increased surface trap states appears, which is consistent with the changes in PL intensity and CD signals. PL and CD spectra show the amplification of the optical signals in the blend film, suggesting efficient chirality transfer from chiral inducer to F-πCD nanoassemblies (Supplementary Fig. 16 and Supplementary Table 3).

To further probe the surface morphology of the blend film with chiroptical properties, SEM measurement is conducted (Fig. 4d). Under optimized condition (ratio 1:0.2:0.1; spin-coating at 1200 rpm), SEM image shows a compact and uniform morphology, with a particle diameter of 50–60 nm (Fig. 4e). It exhibits that inter-particle crosslinking through the nodes leads to the formation of a dense network, and the nodes become intensive with the increase of doping density. It reveals that after incorporating M/P-[N] hexahelicene in F-πCD nanoassemblies, the structure is completely different from that of the pure F-πCD nanoassemblies. Moreover, cross-sectional high-resolution TEM and elemental mapping using energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectroscopy (Fig. 4f–i and Supplementary Fig. 17) further confirm the structure. The results reveal a uniform distribution of C, N, O and F atoms across the blend film. This film maintains a discrete nanostructure composed of well-isolated particles, consistent with the morphology observed in SEM images.

Taken together, the structure of M/P-[N] hexahelicene and F-πCDs are expected to promote intermolecular electrostatic interactions and induce the formation of supramolecular arrangements. As illustrated in Fig. 4j, the helicene not only as a twisted π-conjugated scaffold with inherent helical chirality and geometric asymmetry, resulting in an uneven intramolecular charge distribution, but also have a high rigidity that stabilizes the chiral field. From the calculated structural model of CDs, we found that, compared to the simple stacking of individual F-πCD, electrostatic asymmetry introduced by M/P-[N] hexahelicene and F-πCDs leads to intermolecular electrostatic attraction. Therefore, we deduce that the F-πCDs are effectively grafted onto the M/P-[N] hexahelicene backbone and subsequently align vertically during co-assembly. Through intermolecular interactions, they interconnect to form chain- and network-like structures (Supplementary Fig. 18), thereby establishing a defined spatial orientation and enabling efficient chirality transfer that gives rise to the chiroptical properties of the blend film.

Electroluminescent devices

Owing to their optical/electrical properties, excellent solution processability, and high stability, F-πCD nanoassemblies show great promise as emitting materials for electroluminescent LED58–62. The device structure is composed of an indium tin oxide (ITO) glass substrate anode, a poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene): poly(styrene-sulfonate) (PEDOT: PSS) hole injection layer, an emission layer (PVK: F-πCD nanoassemblies or PVK: M/P-[N] hexahelicene: F-πCD nanoassemblies), a 1,3,5-tris(N-phenylbenzimidazol-2-yl) benzene (TPBi) electron transport layer (ETL), and a LiF/Al double-layered cathode (Fig. 5a, b). The doping ratio of P-[N] hexahelicene: F-πCD nanoassemblies in PVK matrix is optimized for best device performance. The EL spectra of LEDs based on PVK:F-πCD nanoassemblies and PVK:M/P-[N] hexahelicene: F-πCD nanoassemblies exhibit emission peaks centered at approximately 520 nm, consistent with the measured PL peaks and yielding Commission Internationale de l’Eclairage (CIE) coordinates of (0.34, 0.53) (Fig. 5d, e and Supplementary Fig. 19). The photographs of operating LEDs (insets in Fig. 5e) display bright, uniform, and defect-free surface EL generated by the F-πCD nanoassemblies. Additionally, the EL spectra remain stable without noticeable shifts across different operating voltages as high as 15 V. Figure 5f depicts the luminance (L) and current density (J) curves as a function of the applied voltage for the three devices. The turn-on voltages (Von) are at 7.5 V for the PVK: F-πCD nanoassemblies-based LEDs, and at 5.1 V and 5.5 V for the M-[N] hexahelicene and P-[N] hexahelicene-doped LEDs, respectively. Lmax reaches 6152 cd m-2 for PVK:F-πCD nanoassemblies-based LEDs. With M-[N] hexahelicene, Lmax increases to 9655 cd m-2; with P-[N] hexahelicene doping, Lmax increases to 8531 cd m-2, respectively. In Fig. 5g, the performance parameter distribution statistics of 10 devices are observed. L and J of devices have a slight change from the average value, and a narrow distribution, indicating good device repeatability and fabrication consistency.

Moreover, the electron (μe) and hole mobility (μh) are measured (Fig. 5h). For PVK: F-πCD nanoassemblies-based device, the μh is significantly lower than μe. For the PVK: M/P-[N] hexahelicene: F-πCD nanoassemblies-based device, the μe and μh are found to be up to 4.45 × 10−4 cm2 V−1 s−1 and 4.2 × 10−4 cm2 V−1 s−1, and there is a balanced μe/μh ratio (~1), indicating the contribution of M/P-[N] hexahelicene in enhancing charge injection and charge balance in the emission layer. In addition to efficient EL and photocurrent generation, a fast device response is necessary for high-speed display and communication applications. Transient measurements show that the EL decay time (τDecay) for the three LEDs is about 6.5 μs, 9 μs, and 9.2 μs, respectively (Fig. 5i). These values are three orders of magnitude faster than the ~2 ms response of current commercial displays, demonstrating the efficient transport and recombination of electrons and holes under an electrical field. Importantly, the circularly polarized EL (CPEL) characteristics of PVK: M/P-[N] hexahelicene: F-πCD nanoassemblies-based LEDs are also investigated (Fig. 5j). As shown in Fig. 5k, CPEL with mirror-image dissymmetry factor gEL of –0.035 and +0.029, respectively, are observed, outperforming other CP-LED material systems (Supplementary Table 4). Although the obtained gEL values are not as high as those of CP-organic LEDs based on chiral metal complexes and polymers, electroluminescent devices based on carbon nanomaterials can achieve high efficiency while maintaining CPEL. The performance parameter distribution statistics and operational stability further display the potential application of CP-LEDs (Supplementary Fig. 20).

In this study, we report the fabrication of carbon-based supramolecular assemblies composed of fluorinated π-conjugated CDs. The F-πCD nanoassemblies are synthesized using 3,4,5,6-tetrafluorophthalic acid and carbamide as precursors. Detailed structural and optical characterizations and theoretical calculations reveal that the structure with fluorine atoms and carbonyl/amino groups can induce effectively intermolecular hydrogen bonding, and construct strong electrostatic interaction with chiral inducer. With the doping of chiral inducer M/P-[N] hexahelicene, a gabs up to 0.107 is achieved. The electroluminescent LEDs display a Lmax of 9655 cd m–2, opposite CPEL signals with gEL values of –0.035 and +0.029. We anticipate that the findings of this study can form the basis for further research on the design of carbon nanomaterials, facilitating the enhancement of chiroptical properties and fabrication of carbon nanomaterial-based LEDs with considerably improved performance.

Methods

Synthesis of F-πCD nanoassemblies

F-πCD nanoassemblies were synthesized by solvothermal treatment. In the synthetic process of F-πCD nanoassemblies, 3,4,5,6-tetrafluorophthalic acid (10 mg) (Tokyo Chemical Industry, Industrial grade, 99% purity) and carbamide (5 mg) (Tokyo Chemical Industry, Industrial grade, 98% purity) were dissolved in methanol solution (15 mL) (Sigma-Aldrich, Analytical reagent, 99.8% purity). The precursor solution was ultrasonically dissolved in a poly(tetrafluoroethylene) (Teflon)-lined autoclave (25 mL) for 10 min and then heated to 200 °C for 15 min. The autoclave then cooled to room temperature naturally and the crude product solution was dried to the solid state. Then, the crude product was initially passed through a membrane filter to remove over-carbonized and unreacted particulate impurities. The resulting filtrate was subsequently purified by water washing, centrifuge, and column chromatography using silica gel (200–300 mesh) as the stationary phase. A two-step chromatographic separation was employed. In the first column, dichloromethane (DCM) was used as the eluent to remove non-polar impurities, which exhibit blue emission under UV irradiation. A second column chromatography was then carried out using an ethyl acetate/petroleum ether mixture (1:2, v/v) to further separate and enrich the target fraction. Under UV illumination, low-molecular-weight emissive impurities exhibiting blue and light-green emission were first eluted from the bottom of the column. Subsequently, the desired components, characterized by bright green emission, remained near the top of the column and were collected. Accordingly, thin-layer chromatography was performed using silica gel plates and visualized under UV light. Last, samples were freeze-dried and stored in a solid state.

Device fabrication

ITO substrates were sequentially ultrasonically cleaned in isopropanol, ethanol, and deionized water, followed by drying at 150 °C for 15 min and UV–ozone treatment for 15 min. PEDOT:PSS was then spin-coated at 2000 rpm for 35 s and annealed at 150 °C for 15 min to form a ~ 30 nm hole injection layer. The emissive layer was prepared by spin-coating a blend of F-πCD nanoassemblies, PVK, and M/P-[N] hexahelicene (dissolved in o-dichlorobenzene) at 1500 rpm for 45 s, followed by thermal annealing at 80 °C for 30 min; the doping ratios of F-πCD nanoassemblies and M/P-[N] hexahelicene in PVK were 10% and 20%, respectively. Finally, TPBi, LiF and Al were sequentially deposited as the cathode.
