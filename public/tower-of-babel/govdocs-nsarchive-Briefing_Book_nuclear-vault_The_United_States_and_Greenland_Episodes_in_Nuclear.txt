<div class="node__content clearfix"><div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-body field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p><strong>Washington, D.C., October 15, 2025 </strong>- The 1968 crash of a nuclear-armed B-52 bomber near the U.S.

Air Force Base in Thule, Greenland, that scattered radioactive plutonium across miles of icefields created an environmental catastrophe that demanded a quick response and a political crisis for the Danish government that exposed contradictions in its no-nuclear policy.

Declassified documents published today in the second part of the National Security Archive's publication on the Thule episode describe the frantic effort to clean up and contain the dangerous debris and the negotiation of a secret agreement between the two countries that confirmed Denmark’s no-nuclear approach but preserved the U.S.

ability to resume nuclear-armed overflights in a national security emergency.</p><p>A State Department telegram from the aftermath of the crash published here today for the first time is evidence of the leverage that Denmark had in negotiations because Washington did not want to risk the 1951 Defense Agreement that had enabled it to deploy the strategically valuable Ballistic Missile Early Warning System (BMEWS) site at Thule Air Base.

Concerning an inquiry from Secretary of State Dean Rusk as to whether Denmark had any issue with the “continued U.S.

presence” at Thule or the possible need to introduce nuclear weapons into Greenland during “NATO alert situations,” Danish Ambassador Torben Rønne suggested that a deal was possible: the “continuation of Greenland defense agreement [was] not involved,” and Denmark’s no-nuclear policy would apply to Greenland “in peacetime.” [See Document 32]</p><p>Today’s publication focuses on the effects and implications of the nuclear-armed B-52 crash on Greenland on 22 January 1968, including the Air Force’s cleanup of contaminated snow and ice as part of a “gentleman’s agreement” agreement with the Danish government and the underwater search for a missing nuclear weapon component.

Other documents included in this collection detail the negotiation of a supplement to the 1951 Defense Agreement to confirm Greenland’s non-nuclear status.</p><p>Most of the documents in today’s posting are published here for the first time.

Among them is a little-known State Department document from 1957 assessing a Joint Chiefs of Staff proposal to either purchase or lease Greenland from Denmark.

… to purchase Greenland could well be regarded as an insult by the Danes and could be seriously damaging to U.S.-Danish relations.” [Document 1]</p><p><a href="https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/briefing-book/nuclear-vault/2025-06-03/united-states-and-greenland-part-i-episodes-nuclear-history">Part I</a> of this publication documented U.S.

security interests in Greenland during the early years of the Cold War and the 1951 U.S.-Denmark negotiations for a defense agreement that demarcated areas of the island where the U.S.

could establish and operate military bases and facilities.

According to declassified documents, by 1957 the Pentagon wanted to deploy nuclear weapons to Thule Air Base for strategic bombing missions but also for air defense purposes.

Those plans involved secret U.S.-Danish negotiations that ended with the U.S.

receiving a tacit green light for nuclear deployments.</p><p>While Denmark had adopted a “no nuclear weapons” approach, U.S.

nuclear deployments in Greenland revealed the inconsistencies in its policy.

Strategic Air Command (SAC) operations further exposed these contradictions: The U.S.

began flying daily nuclear armed airborne alert missions over the BMEWS site at Thule to confirm its security.

SAC’s “Thule monitor” mission eventually resulted in a serious B-52 accident in January 1968 that lay the basis for a major readjustment in U.S.-Denmark nuclear relations.</p><p>&nbsp;</p><h3>Crested Ice</h3><p>Before the U.S.

and Denmark reached an agreement on Greenland’s nuclear status, Washington had to address the problem of the plutonium scattered by the B-52 crash.

Both U.S.

and Danish officials and scientists pondered the risks of contamination.

The Danish scientists wanted to avoid any scenario where the contaminated ice melted into the area around North Star Bay, where 200 Inuit families hunted for birds, seals, and walruses.

(Some of them had been relocated from the site of Thule Air Force Base when construction began in 1953.) According to a U.S.

Atomic Energy Commission (AEC) official involved in the talks, “The Danish Government has a very paternalistic policy toward the Greenlanders and appears determined to look out for their interests as much or more than they would for themselves.” [See Document 4]</p><p>U.S.

nuclear experts recognized that “time was of the essence” because the contaminated ice would melt in North Star Bay, adding that “a vigorous attempt to remove this main contamination would be well received by the Danes.” Moreover, the “Panel [was] not wholly charmed by calculations which might suggest permissible contamination of the sea water,” which would be the case if plutonium-polluted ice melted into the bay.

Seeking to protect Inuit hunting grounds, the Danes saw the oil slick as a “major problem” because its movement was “unpredictable.” The implication was that there should be no fuel “to form a slick.” According to AEC official Harry Bruner, that was “another way of saying: We want you to scrape up all the contaminated ice and do something with it.” [See Documents 6 and 8]</p><p>The person in charge of the “Crested Ice” cleanup operation, Air Force General Richard Hunziker, preferred to bury the 25,000-gallon tanks of irradiated material in the permafrost, which he saw as “cheap, simple, and safe.” Yet, he recognized the alternative of shipping the contaminants to the U.S., and that became a necessity.

The understanding that the U.S.

would remove the contaminated ice was a key point in the “gentleman’s agreement” that U.S.

and Danish officials reached in February 1968 when they met in Copenhagen.

Understood as a “good housekeeping measure” that was also necessary to shield Denmark from Soviet bloc propaganda, the agreement involved several points, but one was crucial: The U.S.

would remove the contaminated aircraft debris and snow and ice—an estimated 10,000 cubic meters, which comprised some 90 to 95 percent of the total contamination—for storage in sealed containers and removal to the United States.</p><p>The cleanup of the contaminated, blackened crash area began officially on 3 March 1968, but preparatory work had already begun with the assembly of equipment, including storage tanks, and tests to determine the best way to remove the top two inches of contaminated snow and ice.

The Air Force began using 25,000-gallon tanks to pump the over 10,000 tons of contaminated snow and ice but also acquired many smaller tanks—modified R-4360 engine containers—that could hold 1,800 gallons.

By the late summer of 1968, the Air Force had shipped thousands of tons of radiated waste to AEC facilities in Savannah River, South Carolina.</p><p>&nbsp;</p><h3>The Missing “Spark Plug”</h3><p>The most secret aspect of the effort to collect and remove bomb parts and fragments and the scattered plutonium in the ice and snow—and one that is not yet fully documented—was the search for a significant weapon component.

It was part of a<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermonuclear_weapon"> thermonuclear</a> weapon’s second (fusion) stage or “secondary”: the U-235 fissile core, or “spark plug,” which resembled a marshal’s baton.

The Air Force could not find it.

Thinking that the piece of the secondary might be underwater in North Star Bay and worried that hostile powers might try to seize it, the Air Force contracted the deployment of a submersible to try to locate it.The Air Force could not find it.

Thinking that the piece of the secondary might be underwater in North Star Bay and worried that hostile powers might try to seize it, the Air Force contracted the deployment of a submersible to try to locate it.

Under the cover story of a search for radioactive material, during July and August 1968, the Star III made dives, but its crew failed to find the missing part.

Years later, the <a href="http://news.bbc.co.uk/2/hi/europe/7720049.stm">BBC</a> reported that the underwater search was for a missing fourth bomb, but the documents and a thorough investigation by a Danish researcher demonstrated otherwise.<a href="#_edn1" name="_ednref1">[1]</a></p><p>&nbsp;</p><h3>Negotiations: Squaring “Neither Confirm nor Deny” with a Non-Nuclear Status for Greenland</h3><p>While the removal of radioactive debris was underway, the U.S.

and Denmark cleaned up the diplomatic mess caused by the B-52 crash.

Copenhagen had kept secret for years its tangled nuclear history with Washington, but the crash exposed the hollowness of the no-nukes policy that Denmark first declared in 1957.

It also created an opportunity for Denmark to manage the crisis by establishing a consistent policy, which it initiated by publicly stating that the U.S.

had no nuclear weapons stored in Greenland.

Moreover, U.S.

Kristensen found that the declassified evidence did not indicate any concern about a missing bomb, but pinpointed a lost uranium fissile core, resembling a “marshal’s baton,” that was a crucial element of the weapon’s secondary stage.<a href="#_edn6" name="_ednref6">[6]</a></p><p>Giller informed the Commission members that the Defense Department sought the AEC’s “consensus” on the suitability and the possible extent of an underwater search for missing bomb parts. The Air Force had commissioned a Navy study to evaluate possible methods of searching underwater. The Air Force did not “anticipate a very complicated search since the water is relatively shallow (approximately 600 feet at the most) and the bottom of the bay has the characteristics of a small desert.”</p><p>The Department of Defense was willing to support a “brief underwater survey” to determine the extent of a search. <div class="field field--name-field-date field--type-datetime field--label-hidden field__item"><time datetime="1968-04-01T12:00:00Z" class="datetime">Apr 1, 1968</time>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>This Defense/State Department message informed the U.S. Embassy that it is “our firm policy to support our requirement for the BMEWS site at Thule, not only for the immediate future, but, in fact, until such time in the indefinite future that proven BMEWS replacement systems are deployed.” The “central location” of the BMEWS system at Thule between the sites in Alaska and the United Kingdom “provides primary coverage of the most logical approach route for Soviet ICBMs and covers a gap which exists between the other two sites except at relatively high altitude and extreme range.” Although new missile detection systems were under development, they would have limitations, making it necessary to prevent the “issues involving nuclear storage and overflights of Greenland [from] overshadow[ing] our need for the Thule BMEWS site,” which could extend beyond the early 1970s.</p></div>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>Discussion of the Danish proposal, including State Department-Defense Department consultations, began in the weeks after the meeting between Rønne and Rusk. During the talks, Rønne “tentatively” accepted the U.S. view that the agreement would be secret as well as U.S. arguments that instead of an “absolute ban” “future nuclear overflights and storage in Greenland should be subject to consultation” between the two governments.</p><p>The State Department proposed the following language: an assurance that “notwithstanding the provisions of the 1951 agreement” the U.S. would not “store nuclear weapons in Greenland or overfly Greenland” with nuclear-armed aircraft. But the U.S. wanted Denmark to recognize that “in circumstances of extreme emergency” advance consultation could be difficult but that the U.S. would “make every effort” to do so.</p><p>After Rønne objected to the paragraph about “extreme emergency,” the State Department walked it back, omitting it from the text. As McKillop had suggested earlier in the discussions, Leddy proposed a U.S. “spoken” comment about the possibility of a “grave and sudden threat that did not allow time to obtain” Denmark’s agreement. Rusk approved the new formulation of the note and Leddy’s proposal for a supplementary statement.</p></div>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>The draft agreement went through more changes, with the Danes proposing to remove the language “notwithstanding the provisions of the 1951 agreement” because they saw it as an implicit justification for the U.S. interpretation that the agreement validated previous U.S. actions on nuclear overflights and storage.<a href="#_edn9" name="_ednref9">[9]</a> Instead, they <a href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v12/d20">proposed</a> more neutral language that State Department negotiators accepted.</p><p>As the talks closed, Leddy briefed Under Secretary Katzenbach on their outcome. Besides giving the U.S. freedom of action during a defense emergency, the State Department had protected the “neither confirm nor deny” policy because Copenhagen “agreed to drop its request for any U.S. participation in or endorsement of a public statement concerning nuclear weapons in Greenland.”</p><p>As Hans Kristensen has noted, there was an important <a href="http://www.nukestrat.com/dk/investigation.htm">“hole”</a> in the agreement. Unlike the 26 February proposal, it did not include language prohibiting the “introduction” of nuclear weapons into Greenland, which would have been broad enough to exclude visits to ports by U.S. ships carrying nuclear weapons.</p></div>
