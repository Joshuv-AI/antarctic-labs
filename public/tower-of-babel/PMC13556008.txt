Efficacy of probiotics in the management of oral candidiasis: an umbrella review of systematic reviews and meta-analyses
Authors: Vini Mehta, Cosimo Galletti, Ankita Mathur, Namrata Suresh, Dhruva Nandi, Javier Flores-Fraile
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOral candidiasis (OC) is among the most prevalent oral infections, frequently associated with immunosuppression, denture wearing, advanced age, and excessive antibiotic use. Given the growing concerns about antifungal resistance, probiotics are being explored as potential adjuncts in the management of oral Candida overgrowth. Existing literature provides fragmented evidence; thus, an umbrella review was planned to synthesize the available evidence on the effectiveness of probiotic interventions in reducing oral Candida spp. count.MethodsA comprehensive, independent search was conducted in four databases (PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science) covering literature from inception to 31st January 2026. Systematic reviews (SRs) with or without meta-analyses (MAs) assessing the effectiveness of probiotics in reducing oral Candida spp. count in terms of colony-forming units/mL (CFU/mL) were included. The quality assessment of the evidence was evaluated using A Measurement Tool to Assess Systematic Reviews 2 (AMSTAR 2) tool and the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach.ResultsOut of the 157 retrieved records, 7 studies were included in this umbrella review, out of which 5 were SRMAs, and 2 were SRs. The interventions involved multi-strain probiotics delivered through various formulations, routes, and treatment durations. Across the 5 SRMAs, probiotic interventions demonstrated beneficial effects by reducing Candida spp. with effect estimates ranging from odds ratio (OR) 0.06 to 0.71; however, wide interstudy heterogeneity (I2 = 0–78%) restricts the reliability and comparability of these findings. These effect estimates were derived from individual meta-analyses with heterogeneous study populations. Methodological assessment of the 7 included studies using the AMSTAR 2 tool identified 3 studies (42.8%) as having low confidence, while 4 studies (57.1%) were rated critically low. Similarly, the overall quality of evidence, as assessed by GRADE, ranged from low for the adult population to very low for the pediatric population, across three key clinical outcomes: reduction in oral Candida spp., clinical cure rate of OC, and recurrence rate.ConclusionsProbiotics may have a potential role in the prevention and treatment of OC; however, the current evidence base cannot be generalized due to significant heterogeneity and methodological constraints. Probiotics can supplement conventional antifungal treatment, but their standalone use is not recommended yet. High-quality, standardized randomized controlled trials and comprehensive SRMAs are necessary to better understand the effectiveness of probiotics in OC management.Systematic review registrationPROSPERO CRD420251117392.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13643-026-03249-z.

Introduction

Oral candidiasis (OC) is a common opportunistic fungal infection predominantly caused by Candida albicans and some other Candida species (spp.), including Candida parapsilosis, Candida metapsilosis, Candida tropicalis, and Candida khmerensis [1]. OC primarily affects populations with weakened immune systems, such as newborn infants, the geriatric population, acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) patients, and individuals undergoing immunosuppressive treatment [2–5]. The prevalence of OC is reportedly highest among AIDS patients, approaching almost 90% [6], followed by the geriatric population, especially in denture wearers, with a prevalence of 60% [7]. The Candida spp. are commensal organisms of human microbiota colonizing the skin, urogenital, gastrointestinal, and respiratory tract [8, 9]. Candida spp. are prevalent in 30–50% of the population and are ubiquitous in oral microbiota, which may contribute to pathogenic infection when present in high Candida loads, particularly exceeding 400 colony-forming units (CFU) per ml; however, high colonization levels are associated with an increasing risk of infection rather than representing a definitive threshold [10, 11]. Clinically, OC presents with diverse phenotypic forms, including but not limited to denture-associated stomatitis, median rhomboid glossitis, pseudomembranous candidiasis, angular cheilitis, erythematous candidiasis, and hyperplastic candidiasis. Usually presenting as a localized infection, it can cause substantial discomfort, pain, and dysphagia, and can subsequently lead to nutritional compromise [12]. Hyperplastic OC is a known precursor for oral cancer, with higher susceptibility to oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), warranting prompt therapeutic intervention [13].

Currently, antifungal drugs are the primary treatment modalities for OC. Despite advances in antifungal therapy, clinicians often come across moderate-to-severe OC cases unresponsive to conventional treatments due to a severe immunocompromised state or emerging antifungal drug resistance [14, 15]. Additionally, antifungal drugs also exhibit adverse drug reactions, making OC management challenging [16]. These limitations restrict treatment options for patients, thereby increasing the risk of recurrent infections and associated mortality [17–19]. Given these limitations, alternative treatment approaches are being explored to enhance treatment outcomes and improve patients’ quality of life [20, 21].

In recent years, probiotics have gained traction as a potential adjunctive treatment option in the management of OC [22]. Probiotics are live microorganisms that, when administered in adequate amounts, confer health benefits on the host [23]. Probiotics exert their effects through competitive, antagonistic, and immunological mechanisms. As living organisms, probiotics compete and replace pathogenic spp. for binding sites, nutrients, and substrates, resulting in growth inhibition of harmful microorganisms. At the molecular level, they produce antimicrobial substances like hydrogen peroxide, adhesins, acids, and bacteriocins, thereby creating a hostile environment for the pathogenic microorganisms [24]. Immunologically, various interleukins, including IL-17, are produced by specific probiotic strains, which stimulate the host’s immune response [25, 26]. Lactobacillus spp., Bifidobacterium spp., Bacillus spp., and Saccharomyces spp. are the most used microorganisms in probiotics; however, the mechanisms vary depending on the spp. type [27]. Various in vivo, in vitro, as well as randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were conducted over the years to document the therapeutic effects of probiotics on OC [28–34]. Nevertheless, conflicting evidence limits the understanding of its effectiveness against OC [35]. This uncertainty has been further accentuated by the inconsistent evidence from the systematic reviews and meta-analyses (SR/SRMAs) evaluating probiotics’ effectiveness on OC [36], thereby restricting their standard use in routine oral practices.

To address the variability in this synthesized evidence, it is imperative to critically appraise SR/SRMAs surrounding probiotic effectiveness in OC. Hence, we conducted an umbrella review to comprehensively synthesize the available evidence on the following: (i) effectiveness of probiotics in reducing oral Candida spp. count, and (ii) the methodological gaps in the existing evidence, to inform future research. Additionally, this review aims to provide a comprehensive overview of the clinical applicability and translational potential of probiotics in routine oral practice for the management of OC for optimized outcomes.

Methodology

Study protocol

We performed this umbrella review systematically to synthesize and evaluate SR/SRMAs, focusing on the effectiveness of probiotics in OC management. The umbrella review was conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) [37], Preferred Reporting Items for Overviews of Reviews (PRIOR) (Supplementary Table S1) [38], and Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA-2020) [39]. This review was registered on PROSPERO [CRD420251117392].

Search strategy

A comprehensive search was conducted on four databases: PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science (WoS) by two independent reviewers (VM, NS) from inception to 31 st January 2026. The PICO framework was used to develop the search strategy with P (Population): Patients with/without candidiasis or individuals at risk (denture wearers and immunocompromised patients); I (Intervention): Any dose, formulation, or strain of probiotic; C (Comparator): No intervention, any intervention, control/placebo, and O (Outcome): Reduction in oral Candida spp. count, clinical cure rate, and recurrence rate. The search strategy was formulated using Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and Boolean operators (“AND”, “OR”). Each search strategy was individually tailored according to the database’s indexing and syntax for appropriate results. The included studies further underwent cross-citation screening to retrieve any more potentially relevant literature. The in-depth search strategy utilized in this review is provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Eligibility criteria and study selection

Eligible studies were selected based on the following criteria: (1) full-text SR/SRMAs conducted on human subjects and published in the English language; (2) studies focused on the effectiveness of probiotics in managing or preventing oral candidiasis; (3) clinical studies evaluated microbiological outcomes (oral Candida spp. count changes) and clinical outcomes incorporating clinical cure and recurrence rates. (4) For reviews that have incorporated both human and animal studies, data derived from the human studies were considered only for data extraction and synthesis. Studies such as narrative reviews, evidence mapping, and any primary studies were excluded from this review. Studies that did not report either microbiological outcomes or clinical outcomes were excluded. The study selection process took place in two stages and was conducted independently by two reviewers (VM, NS). Search results from the databases were extracted in RIS format and exported to the Rayyan software for study selection. Rayyan is a web-based platform used to facilitate SR screening. Although Rayyan has artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted features for streamlining the screening process, these features were not used in this review [40]. All screening decisions were taken manually by the reviewers to maintain methodological rigor and consistency. Any conflicts during the study selection process were resolved through mutual consensus and by a third reviewer (AM) as and when required. Initially, duplicated records were removed, followed by screening based on the title and abstract of the reviews. After initial screening, the full-text of the selected studies was further screened for the final inclusion. During the title/abstract as well as full-text screening stages, the level of agreement between the two reviewers was 0.95 and 0.86, according to Cohen’s kappa coefficient.

Data extraction and quality assessment

The data were extracted by two reviewers (VM and NS) independently from the included studies and compiled in an MS Excel (version 2019) spreadsheet. The extracted data included the following characteristics: author, published year, region, protocol registration, guidelines followed, time frame of the primary studies covered in SR/SRMAs, databases covered, total number of studies retrieved, number of studies included in final review, study design covered, overall sample size, key outcome, whether meta-analysis was performed and Risk of Bias (RoB) tool used. Additionally, parameters such as intervention modalities and duration, comparators used, strain of probiotics employed, heterogeneity estimates for meta-analyses, and overall results were also extracted as quantitative evidence of probiotics’ effectiveness on OC. The Measurement Tool to Assess Systematic Reviews 2 (AMSTAR 2) checklist was used to critically appraise the methodological rigor of the included SR/SRMAs [41] (Supplementary Fig. S1). The tool comprises 16 components, including 9 non-critical and 7 critical domains assessing the following: (1) establishment of predefined protocols; (2) comprehensiveness of the literature search; (3) justifications for excluded primary studies; (4) methods used to extract and synthesize results; (5) RoB of the included primary studies; and (6) publication bias. Each included SR/SRMA was graded high, moderate, low, or critically low confidence as per AMSTAR 2 criteria to contextualize its findings.

Overlap assessment of primary studies

A matrix of evidence was prepared to assess the level of overlap among primary studies included in the SR/SRMAs. The corrected covered area (CCA) was determined using the Graphical Representation of Overlap for Overviews (GROOVE) tool in MS Excel (version 2019). The tool was developed by Bracchiglione et al. in 2022 [42] to assess overlap in primary studies and eliminate any misleading findings. Primary studies were extracted from each of the included reviews and were entered into a matrix format based on their presence in other included SR/SRMAs. The GROOVE tool was then used to analyze the overlap of primary studies across the included SR/SRMAs by calculating the CCA and depicting a color-coded matrix to represent shared primary studies across reviews (Fig. 1). Outcome confidence assessment

The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) [43] approach was used to assess the certainty of evidence for each outcome. This framework assesses the body of evidence across five domains: (1) RoB; (2) inconsistency (direction of interventional effects, statistical heterogeneity measured by I2); (3) indirectness (directly addressing relevant populations, interventions, and outcomes); (4) imprecision (magnitude of the number of included primary studies and median sample size); and (5) publication bias. The certainty of evidence was rated high, moderate, low, or very low based on these domains.

Results

A total of 157 records were retrieved from four databases. Of them, 63 duplicates were removed, and 77 articles were excluded after screening the titles and abstracts. The full-text screening of the remaining 17 articles resulted in the exclusion of 10 articles. Subsequently, 7 articles [27, 36, 44–48] were included in the umbrella review. Description of included systematic reviews

Of the 7 included studies, 2 were identified as SRs [46, 48] and the other 5 as SRMAs [27, 36, 44, 45, 47]. Collectively, these 7 studies comprise 59 primary studies corresponding to 4739 participants, with the majority being RCTs (n = 55) and 4 pre-post intervention studies (Table 1). The SRMA by Hu L et al. [36] included 11 studies, out of which 6 were RCTs on human subjects, and 5 were animal studies. Hence, as per the predefined inclusion criteria, data from human studies were only considered for outcome synthesis in this umbrella review. While Table 1 depicts the overall study characteristics of Hu L et al. [36], as reported in the original review, Table 2 reflects data synthesis and outcomes derived exclusively from human studies. Among the included SR/SRMAs, quantitative analysis was performed in 5 reviews, whereas 2 reviews [46, 48] did not conduct meta-analyses owing to heterogeneity in the reported evidence. Protocol registration in PROSPERO was reported only in 2 reviews [45, 46]. All included reviews were conducted between 2017 and 2025, and the primary studies ranged from 2001 to 2025. The majority of the included reviews have a mixed population (n = 4) with elderly individuals and denture wearers representing the predominant populations (n = 5), followed by children (3–14 years) (n = 5), adults (n = 2), and infants (n = 1). RoB assessment was done by 6 reviews using the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions tool [27, 36, 44, 45, 47, 48]. Quality appraisal tools such as one proposed by the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) for a pre-post intervention study, and a 5-point scale of Jadad et al. [49, 50] were also used in 2 reviews [27, 46]. Assessment of overlap studies

Among the 7 included SR/SRMAs, 31 unique indexed studies were identified from 59 primary studies after assessment by the GROOVE tool. A total of 21 possible pairs were assessed from the 7 reviews. The overall CCA was reported to be 15.05%, which falls at the threshold for ‘very high degree overlap’ (≥ 15%), indicating borderline high overlap of primary studies across the included reviews. The overlap percentages ranged from 0 to 50%, with substantial proportions falling under the ‘very high overlap’ category. The highest degree of overlap was noted between Hu L et al. [36] and Shah et al. (50%) [46], and Hu L et al. [36] and Ai R et al. [45] (50%) (Fig. 1) (Supplementary Table S4).

Characteristics of the probiotics intervention

The probiotic interventions demonstrated significant variability across the included SRs/SRMAs in terms of strains, formulations, dosage, and intervention duration. Predominant probiotic strains included Lactobacillus rhamnosus (including GG and HS111), Lactobacillus reuteri (DSM 17938, ATCC PTA 5289), Lactobacillus acidophilus, and Bifidobacterium longum, either used as a single-strain or multi-strain combination (Table 2). The reviews reported diverse modes of administration for probiotics in the form of tablets, capsules, cheese, lozenges, yogurt drinks, mouthwash, ice, and powders. In control groups, comparators varied considerably, ranging from placebo (e.g., cheese or capsules without probiotics) to standard antimicrobial agents (e.g., chlorhexidine or nystatin), herbal rinses, and nutritional products (e.g., skim milk or soybean ice cream). Intervention durations between reviews ranged from 1 week to a maximum of 2 years, and frequency of administration was typically between 1 and 3 times per day or less frequently, depending on the formulation.

Effect of probiotics on oral candidiasis

Five included SRMAs independently reported a decline in oral Candida spp. following probiotic intervention, although effect sizes and heterogeneity differed across reviews. Tang R et al. [47] reported the lowest odds ratio (OR) (0.06, 95% CI 0.01–0.57), indicating a strong and significant reduction (94%) in the OC with probiotic intervention as compared to the control groups (p = 0.01) (Table 2). Consecutively, Hu L et al. [36], Li P et al. [44], and Ai R et al. [45] demonstrated comparable reductions (76%, 62%, and 46%) in the oral Candida colonization after probiotic administration [(OR = 0.24, 95% CI 0.09–0.63), (OR = 0.38, 95% CI 0.22–0.68), (OR = 0.54, 95% CI 0.38–0.77)]. However, the moderate effect of probiotics in reducing OC (OR = 0.71, 95% CI 0.37–1.32) was reported by Mundula T et al. [27], indicating a 29% reduction in odds as compared to the controls. In terms of therapeutic management of OC, Tang R et al. [47] demonstrated 8 times higher odds of achieving clinical cure in patients receiving probiotics as compared to controls (OR = 7.80, 95% CI 4.78–12.75; p = 0.01). Tang R et al. [47] also reported a significant reduction (79%) in the recurrence rate of OC (OR = 0.21, 95% CI 0.12–0.35; p < 0.001). Substantial heterogeneity was observed in the outcomes of Hu L et al. (I2 = 78%) [36], Li P et al. (I2 = 60.3%) [44], and Mundula T et al. (I2 = 56.3%) [27] whereas no heterogeneity in the pooled outcome (I2 = 0%) was reported in the review by Ai R et al. [45]. These effect estimates reflect findings from individual meta-analyses with heterogeneous study populations. Furthermore, narrative evidence synthesis by Shah S et al. [46] and Turska-Szybka A et al. [48] demonstrated a lack of evidence on the effect of probiotics in OC among pediatric age groups, although Shah S et al. [46] reported a beneficial effect among elderly individuals.

Methodological quality assessment

As per the methodological rigor assessment by the AMSTAR 2 tool, 3 [44–46] out of 7 reviews have reported low confidence (42.85%), while the remaining 4 [27, 36, 47, 48] reviews were rated as critically low confidence (57.14%). This suggests that the overall reliability and validity of the reported evidence from the included reviews ranged between low and critically low. Certainty of evidence assessment

The overall certainty of evidence, using the GRADE framework, was independently assessed for adults and children based on 3 key outcomes: microbiological reduction of oral Candida, clinical cure of OC, and recurrence after treatment. For all these outcomes, the certainty level was generally low in adults and very low in children (Table 3). Evidence related to all the 3 outcomes among the adult population started at high certainty (based on RCTs) and was subsequently downgraded due to methodological and statistical limitations. These include concerns regarding RoB, inconsistency, imprecision, suspected publication bias, unclear sequence generation, as well as allocation concealment, limited blinding, incomplete reporting, and small sample sizes. Additionally, wide confidence intervals (CIs) around pooled effects and moderate heterogeneity across reviews in terms of probiotic strains, comparators, vehicles, and adult risk groups (healthy, denture wearers, established candidiasis) reflected uncertainty in the stability of the observed effects. In pediatric populations, the certainty of evidence was rated very low across all 3 outcomes. The available evidence was derived predominantly from small RCTs with limited statistical power and insufficient sample sizes to detect the prespecified effect size, and lacked generalizability due to the inclusion of mixed-age subsets or high-risk pediatric samples. Concerns related to RoB were more pronounced, particularly due to limited random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding, and selective outcome reporting. Additionally, substantial heterogeneity in terms of interventions, comparators, outcome definitions, age ranges, baseline risk profiles, Candida thresholds, and recurrence criteria, as well as wide confidence intervals in pooled estimates, mixed findings further compromised the reliability of the effect estimates among children (Table 3).

Discussion

The advent of antifungal drugs has revolutionized the management of OC and has been considered as the first line of treatment. However, frequent adverse effects and antifungal resistance are increasing challenges to its clinical management [51]. As a result, probiotics have gained traction as a novel or adjuvant treatment modality. Although the progress is significant, its effectiveness has been inconsistent, as per the literature [52–60]. In this umbrella review, we sought to comprehensively summarize and critically evaluate the evidence from 7 studies (2-SRs and 5-SRMAs) conducted to examine the effect of probiotics in OC. Our findings shed light on divergent outcomes, where 5 reviews reported the beneficial effect of probiotics corresponding to oral Candida count reduction, lowered recurrence rate, and improved clinical cure [27, 36, 44–48]. However, 2 reviews demonstrated inconsistent or mixed effects of probiotics, particularly in the pediatric population [46, 48]. Furthermore, studies with meta-analyses showed wide heterogeneity (I2 = 0–78%), which compromises the reliability of the reported beneficial effects. In terms of contextualization under methodological robustness and quality of evidence, CCA estimates at the threshold for ‘very high overlap’ (15.05%) were reported across the primary studies by the GROOVE tool, and critically low confidence (57.14%) in methodological rigor by AMSTAR 2 further downgrades the validity of the reported evidence. Consequently, the overall certainty of evidence was projected to be low in the adult population and very low among children by the GRADE approach.

The wide heterogeneity is attributed to the variability in target population, sample size, methodological rigor, inclusion criteria, probiotic combinations, comparator compositions, dosages, and mode of delivery across the reviews, which underscores the challenges in determining the definitive effect of probiotics. The diverse nature of probiotic delivery methods, including but not limited to tablets, lozenges, yogurt, cheese, powder, and mouthwash, contributes to inconclusive findings. The significance of probiotic delivery approaches in oral health management has gained attention due to their ability to induce localized responses, for example, lozenges. Such mechanisms are theoretically sound for reducing OC as sustained mucosal exposure is imperative in disrupting Candida colonization and re-establishing a balanced oral microbiome [61, 62]. Although lozenges have been reported as the most effective delivery method [63, 64], the same is unclear in our review owing to mixed interventions, which demand future research on the effects of targeted delivery methods. It is also imperative to understand that indigenous probiotic strains determine the therapeutic effectiveness, underscoring the combined effect of both probiotic microbial combinations and delivery methods [63–65]. Multiple studies have demonstrated anticandidal properties of probiotic strains, particularly with Lactobacillus spp., as certain Lactobacillus strains act by inhibiting fungal adhesion, disrupting Candida biofilm, and modulating host immune responses [66–71]. Similarly, Bifidobacterium spp. [72], Propionibacterium spp., as well as Streptococcus spp. [73–75] exert antifungal effects by inhibiting the excessive growth of Candida spp. However, each spp. has a distinct mechanism of action and demonstrates varied antifungal effects depending on dietary habits, availability of nutrients, antagonist interactions with existing oral microbiota, and the salivary flow, which influences colonization and therapeutic effects [24, 27, 75, 76]. Hence, to delineate the true effect, extensive research based on 4 targeted approaches is required (target population, target intervention, target delivery method, and target comparator) to understand which probiotics have better therapeutic effects on OC and how probiotics function synergistically.

The beneficial effect of probiotics in our review was predominantly observed among elderly patients and denture wearers [27, 36, 45]. This is in contrast with the previous meta-analysis conducted, where a stronger beneficial effect was reported when elderly patients and denture wearers were considered separately [63, 73, 74, 76]. A similar trend was also confirmed by Lee X et al. in their RCT [77]. The inconclusive effect of probiotics among the pediatric population in our review [46, 48] explains the complexity in the selected studies more than the methodological issues. This does not imply a lack of effect of probiotic strains on Candida growth in children; it highlights knowledge gaps due to limited eligible articles and data unavailability concerning optimal doses, mode of administration, and duration of interventions that remain to be further investigated.

A notable finding in our review is the significantly low confidence in the methodological rigor of the included reviews and overall uncertainty of evidence corresponding to the adult and pediatric population. The methodological quality assessed by the AMSTAR 2 tool in our review highlights the importance of high-quality methodology to produce robust SRMAs with reliable evidence. Critically low confidence (57.14%) among the included reviews was due to the absence of protocol registration, improper justification of excluded studies, and significant publication bias, as well as RoB in the reported results. This was further exacerbated by the overall low sample size, which limits the generalizability of the therapeutic outcomes. This highlights the need for future large-scale RCTs with robust protocols, while SRMAs should focus on identifying and addressing methodologically weak primary studies through sensitivity analyses and cautious interpretation of the pooled estimate. Also, the overall CCA value (15.05%) is positioned at the threshold for ‘very high degree overlap’, and therefore warrants cautious interpretation. While the value indicates significant overlap of primary studies across the reviews, the borderline characteristic of this estimate points toward the extent of overlap, which might be sensitive to minor differences in the included studies. This not only reduces the independence of findings but also inflates the pooled estimates, thereby affecting the reliability of the synthesized findings. Collectively, these constraints reduce the overall strength of the evidence base. To avoid such discrepancies, future research should concentrate more on generating high-quality RCTs rather than conducting additional evidence synthesis. The GRADE approach also demonstrated serious uncertainty in the overall evidence associated with the adult and child populations. The GRADE framework offers a grading system for evidence certainty in SRMAs, which determines the strength of recommendations for clinical guidelines. Low certainty in the evidence in our review leaves substantial ambiguity regarding the benefits and harms of probiotics in OC. This underscores the importance of the GRADE approach in SRMAs, as failure to recognize low certainty in evidence related to benefit would lead to overuse of the intervention. To overcome such challenges, rigorous and standard protocol-aligned RCTs are mandated as they form the foundation of high-quality evidence; however, the review process should also be transparent in addressing RoBs, imprecision, and inconsistencies.

From a clinical viewpoint, probiotics showcase biologically plausible and potentially beneficial effects in reducing Candida colonization and recurrence. However, given the low certainty of evidence in adults and very low in pediatric populations, recommending probiotics as a first-line or standalone therapy for OC is not feasible. Instead, they can be considered as an adjunctive therapeutic approach, specifically in populations who are vulnerable to recurrence, such as elderly denture wearers, patients at risk of antifungal resistance, and immunocompromised individuals. More importantly, heterogeneity in probiotic formulations and delivery restricts their generalizability across commercial preparations. Therefore, clinical application should be cautiously accompanied by proper monitoring under an expert’s supervision.

There is a pressing need for rigorously designed RCTs with a targeted sample population, larger sample sizes, standardized probiotic strains, doses, treatment durations, and clinically significant outcome measures to achieve effective administration methods for probiotic therapy. Prolonged follow-up studies are critical for evaluating long-term therapeutic effectiveness, safety profiles, and the associated risks and benefits of probiotic interventions. This will not only determine effective administration methods of probiotic strains; it will also refine the therapeutic modalities for populations at risk, particularly individuals with compromised immunity predisposing to several opportunistic infections. Considering the current evidence synthesis, greater uniformity in outcome reporting would help to minimize variability and enhance study comparability. Future SRMAs should appropriately analyze and report overlap, follow preregistered protocol procedures, and comply with RoB and certainty of evidence frameworks. Collectively, these methodological nuances are pivotal for improving evidence clarity, reducing duplication, and increasing the reliability of findings to understand the role of probiotics in OC management.

Our review has several strengths. First, this umbrella review attempts to comprehensively evaluate and critically summarize the effect of probiotics in OC from the available SRMAs. Second, unlike previous evidence synthesis, our review incorporated the AMSTAR-2 tool for methodological quality assessment, GRADE to assess certainty of evidence, and GROOVE to determine the interstudy overlap, providing a multidimensional assessment of both effect estimates and structural robustness. Third, certainty of evidence was estimated separately for both adult and paediatric populations, showcasing transparency in results and clinically relevant effect. Finally, by providing effect estimates from individual meta-analyses, evidence certainty, and overlap assessments, our review goes beyond narrative synthesis and provides a comprehensive synthesis of probiotic effectiveness in OC. However, our review also holds certain limitations. First, the current evidence has been limited by a lack of high-quality RCTs. Second, methodological limitations are prominent in the included reviews, including but not limited to high RoB and small sample sizes, substantial heterogeneity in the study parameters, which most likely compromised the reliable effect estimation of probiotics against OC. Finally, the borderline very high degree of overlap, uncertainty in the evidence base, as well as low methodological confidence, restricts our findings from being used for clinical recommendations as well as future clinical guidelines.

Conclusion

Our umbrella review suggests that probiotics may have a potential role in OC management. However, the current evidence has been limited by significant variabilities, including borderline high overlap degree across primary studies, high heterogeneity, and methodological shortcomings, which downgrades the certainty of the evidence. Although probiotics can complement the existing antifungal treatment modalities and oral care measures, their standalone usage cannot be recommended yet in clinical practices. Future research should focus on large-scale and high-quality RCTs with standardized protocols along with comprehensive SRMAs to clearly determine the therapeutic role of probiotics in OC management.
