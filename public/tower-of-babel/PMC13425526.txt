A New Species of Sarcoscypha, S. orientalis sp. nov., and a Newly Recorded Species, S. vassiljevae, from Korea
Authors: Dae Ho Kim, Young-Nam Kwag, Hyung So Kim, Chang Sun Kim, Sang-Kuk Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTwo species of Sarcoscypha, including the new species S. orientalis sp. nov. and the first Korean record of S. vassiljevae, are reported based on morphological observations and phylogenetic analyses of internal transcribed spacer (ITS), large subunit (LSU), and RNA polymerase II second largest subunit (RPB2) sequence data. Specimens of S. orientalis sp. nov. are morphologically similar to S. occidentalis but differ in possessing completely sessile apothecia. Phylogenetic analyses revealed that sequences previously attributed to S. occidentalis were separated into two geographically distinct clades corresponding to USA and East Asian lineages; the Korean specimens were placed within the East Asian lineage. Based on this morphological and phylogenetic evidence, the East Asian specimens are proposed as the new species, Sarcoscypha orientalis sp. nov. The remaining specimens were identified as S. vassiljevae, which is characterized by ivory to pale cream hymenial surfaces and ellipsoid ascospores containing a single large guttule. These specimens clustered with reference sequences of S. vassiljevae in the phylogenetic analyses. These findings expand the known diversity of the genus Sarcoscypha in Korea and provide new insights into the geographic differentiation and taxonomic diversity within the genus.Keywords: Sarcoscypha orientalis sp. nov., Sarcoscypha vassiljevae, Sarcoscypha, Sarcoscyphaceae

Introduction

Sarcoscypha (Fr.) Boud. (Sarcoscyphaceae, Pezizales) is a genus of operculate discomycetes that produce cupulate apothecia, typically occurring on decaying wood or fallen twigs in forest ecosystems [1]. The genus is widely distributed across temperate regions of the Northern Hemisphere, particularly in Europe and North America [2,3]. Several species have also been reported from East Asia, where they are commonly associated with moist forest habitats and woody substrates [4–6].

Morphologically, Sarcoscypha species are characterized by cupulate to discoid apothecia, often featuring a tomentose outer surface and a vividly colored hymenial surface. At the species level, diagnostic characters include the size and development of the apothecia and stipe, the structure of the excipulum, hair morphology, and the size and shape of the ascospores [3,7]. In recent years, the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region of nuclear ribosomal DNA has been widely used for species identification and phylogenetic analyses of Sarcoscypha and related taxa [8,9]. Integrating morphological characters with molecular data has improved the reliability of species identification within the genus.

In Korea, only two species of Sarcoscypha, S. coccinea and S. humida, have been recorded to date. Specifically, S. coccinea is listed in the “List of Mushrooms in Korea” [10], while S. humida was recently described within the country [11]. During fungal diversity surveys, additional specimens belonging to Sarcoscypha were collected that did not correspond to these previously documented Korean taxa. Therefore, the aim of this study was to clarify the taxonomic identity of these specimens through morphological observations and molecular phylogenetic analyses, and to report one new species and one newly recorded species of Sarcoscypha from Korea.

Materials and methods

Specimen collection

To determine the taxonomic identity of Sarcoscypha specimens collected in Korea, dried herbarium specimens were examined. All materials were collected during field surveys in various forested areas and deposited in the herbarium of the Korea National Arboretum (KH). Collections that did not correspond to previously recorded Korean taxa were selected for further detailed study (Table 1).

Morphological observations

Habitat and substrate information were recorded from field notes and photographs taken at the time of collection. Macroscopic features, such as apothecial size, coloration, and stipe development, were assessed from field photographs and original collection records.

For microscopic examination, the apothecia were rehydrated and manually sectioned. Sections were mounted in distilled water and Melzer’s reagent for observation. Microscopic structures, including asci, ascospores, and paraphyses, were examined and measured using a Leica DM 6000 FS compound microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany).

DNA extraction and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification

Genomic DNA was extracted from the dried specimens using the DNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

The ITS, nuclear ribosomal large subunit (LSU), and RNA polymerase II second largest subunit (RPB2) regions were amplified using the primer pairs ITS5/ITS4, LR0R/LR5 [12], and fRPB2-5F/fRPB2-7cR [13], respectively. PCR amplification was performed in a 20 μL reaction volume containing 1 μL of genomic DNA, 1 μL of each primer, and AccuPower® Taq PCR PreMix (Bioneer, Daejeon, South Korea), with the remaining volume adjusted using nuclease-free water.

The thermal cycling conditions included an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C (for ITS and LSU) or 52 °C (for RPB2) for 20 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min, and a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. The resulting amplicons were verified via agarose gel electrophoresis prior to sequencing.

Sequencing and phylogenetic analysis

PCR products were purified and sequenced by Macrogen Inc. (Seoul, South Korea). Raw chromatograms were edited and assembled using Geneious Prime® (Biomatters, Auckland, New Zealand). Preliminary identification was conducted via BLASTn searches against the NCBI GenBank database.

Sequence alignments were generated using MAFFT v.7 and manually refined where necessary. Phylogenetic analyses were conducted using both an ITS-only dataset and a combined ITS–LSU–RPB2 dataset. Individual loci were concatenated into a single matrix for multilocus phylogenetic analysis.

Phylogenetic relationships were inferred using the maximum-likelihood (ML) method implemented in RAxML with the GTRGAMMA substitution model. Rapid bootstrap analysis was performed with 1000 replicates, and the best-scoring ML tree was obtained simultaneously. Wynnea macrospora (MK335784) and Wynnea gigantea (MK335782) were designated as outgroup taxa. Bayesian inference (BI) was performed using MrBayes v.3.2.7 under the GTR + G model, and posterior probabilities were calculated to assess node support.

Results

Molecular characteristics

The ITS, LSU, and RPB2 sequences obtained from specimens Jjk16541, Jjk16571, and KA19-1001 were 564 bp, 887 bp, and 1098 bp in length, respectively; they were identical among the three specimens.

BLASTn searches of the ITS sequences revealed 100% sequence identity (100% query coverage) with Sarcoscypha occidentalis JBRI-M21-163 (PQ613402.1), and showed high similarity (99.82–100%) to several other S. occidentalis sequences. Conversely, comparison with USA S. occidentalis sequences (e.g., MK532795.1, MG748579.1, and U66024.1) revealed lower similarity values ranging from 91.33% to 96.60%. In addition, comparisons with other Sarcoscypha species, including S. mesocyatha, S. javensis, S. korfiana, and S. dudleyi, showed similarity values of 96.23–97.70%. The LSU sequences showed the highest similarity (99.55%) to Sarcoscypha occidentalis 420526MF1007 (MG712395.1) from the USA, while also showing similarity values of 98.20–98.65% to other closely related species, including S. emarginata, S. jurana, and S. humida. Finally, the RPB2 sequences showed the highest similarity (97.73%) to a USA specimen of S. occidentalis DAH-12 (DQ017596.1), followed by S. humida (97.28%), S. austriaca (92.12–92.75%), and S. coccinea (91.88–92.47%).

In the ITS-based phylogenetic analysis (Figure 1), sequences identified as S. occidentalis were divided into two distinct clades. One clade comprised specimens from the USA with strong support (ML bootstrap = 98%; BPP = 1.00). The other clade consisted of sequences from China and Korea, including the Korean specimens examined in this study, which was also supported (ML bootstrap = 92%; BPP = 0.91). Pairwise distance analysis based on the ITS alignment further supported this separation. The mean intra-clade p-distance was approximately 0.01 (range 0.00–0.02) within the USA clade and 0.01 (range 0.00–0.03) within the East Asia clade. In contrast, the mean inter-clade p-distance between the USA and East Asia clades was approximately 0.08 (range 0.07–0.09), indicating substantial genetic divergence between the two groups. The phylogenetic relationships inferred from the combined ITS–LSU–RPB2 dataset (Figure 2) were largely consistent with the ITS phylogeny. The Korean specimens clustered with the Chinese specimen S. occidentalis 420526MF1007, forming a distinct clade separate from USA S. occidentalis.

The ITS and LSU sequences obtained from specimens KA24-0666 and KA24-0792 were 834 bp and 898 bp in length, respectively. A comparison between the two specimens revealed a single nucleotide difference in the ITS region and two nucleotide differences in the LSU region. BLASTn searches indicated that the ITS sequences showed up to 100% sequence identity with Sarcoscypha vassiljevae, while similarity values to other Sarcoscypha species were markedly lower (maximum 85.43%). Similarly, the LSU sequences showed high similarity (99.09–99.32%) to S. vassiljevae HKAS 89817 (MG871337.1), whereas similarity values to all other Sarcoscypha species were below 97%.

In the ITS-based phylogenetic analysis (Figure 1), both specimens clustered together with reference sequences of S. vassiljevae, forming a stable and well-supported clade that is clearly distinct from other species. The combined ITS–LSU–RPB2 phylogeny (Figure 2) was consistent with the ITS-based topology and supported the identification of these specimens as S. vassiljevae.

Remark: The Korean collections are morphologically consistent with members of Sarcoscypha, characterized by brightly colored cupulate apothecia produced on woody substrates and operculate asci with smooth, hyaline ascospores [3,7]. The specimens are morphologically most similar to Sarcoscypha occidentalis in apothecial size and ascospore dimensions, but can be clearly distinguished by their completely sessile apothecia. In contrast, S. occidentalis typically develops a distinct stipe or a short but distinct stipe [7].

Among other morphologically similar species, S. coccinea and S. dudleyi produce bright red apothecia but can be readily distinguished by their generally larger apothecia and ascospores, as well as by the presence of a distinct stipe [7]. Sarcoscypha mesocyatha and S. hosoyae considerably overlap in ascospore size with the present specimens, but can be distinguished by the presence of a stipe and differences in excipular structure [4,14]. In addition, the paraphyses of S. orientalis are typically branched, whereas those of the related species are usually simple. Discussion

In this study, morphological observations and multilocus phylogenetic analyses based on the ITS, LSU, and RPB2 sequence data clarified the taxonomic identity of Sarcoscypha specimens collected in Korea. The results demonstrated that the examined specimens represent two distinct species, including one new species and one species newly recorded from Korea.

The specimens corresponding to Sarcoscypha orientalis sp. nov. were morphologically similar to S. occidentalis but could be clearly distinguished because of their completely sessile apothecia. Phylogenetic analyses revealed that sequences previously identified as S. occidentalis were divided into two geographically distinct clades corresponding to USA and East Asian lineages. The Korean specimens were consistently placed within the East Asian lineage and formed a distinct clade separate from the USA S. occidentalis. In addition, ITS sequence divergence between the two lineages was approximately 8%, which is significantly higher than intraspecific variation within each clade. These results, together with the observed morphological differences, support the recognition of the Korean specimens as a new species, Sarcoscypha orientalis sp. nov.

The remaining specimens were identified as Sarcoscypha vassiljevae based on both morphological characteristics and phylogenetic evidence. These specimens are characterized by ivory to pale cream hymenial surfaces and ellipsoid ascospores containing two prominent guttules, and they clustered with reference sequences of S. vassiljevae in phylogenetic analyses.

This study represents the first report of S. vassiljevae in Korea and describes S. orientalis as a new species. These findings expand the known diversity of the genus Sarcoscypha in Korea, providing new insights into geographic differentiation and lineage divergence within the genus. Further studies incorporating broader geographic sampling and additional molecular data will contribute to a better understanding of species boundaries and evolutionary relationships in Sarcoscypha.
