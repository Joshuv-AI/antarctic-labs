Conic modules, secondary fans and non-commutative resolutions
Aimeric Malter

Abstract
. Faber, Muller and Smith used complete sums of conic modules to construct non-commutative crepant resolutions (NCCR) of simplicial toric algebras. We link these conic modules to the Bondal-Thomsen collection of line bundles on smooth toric DM stacks. This viewpoint allows us to establish computational results relating to conic modules, reducing the complexity of the combinatorics involved significantly. We formulate necessary and sufficient conditions for an incomplete sum of conic modules to give an NC(C)R of a toric algebra. Furthermore, we prove that to check if a toric algebra R R admits an NCCR in the form of End R ⁡ ( 𝔹 ) \operatorname{End}_{R}(\mathbb{B}) for an incomplete sum of conic modules, we may reduce to a case where the class group of the affine toric variety Spec ⁡ R \operatorname{Spec}R does not have torsion and verify the statement there. Finally, we treat the case of almost simplicial Gorenstein cones, i.e. cones with | σ ⁡ ( 1 ) | = dim σ + 1 |\sigma(1)|=\dim\sigma+1 , classifying when such cones admit NCCRs via endomorphism algebras of conic modules.



1.
Introduction

Non-commutative (crepant) resolutions, or NC(C)Rs, were introduced by Van den Bergh as an algebraic approach to encapsulate the properties a crepant resolution of singularities should fulfill. Given a singular algebraic variety, oftentimes there are several natural candidates for a resolution thereof, even when imposing additional conditions such as crepancy, and an interesting question to consider is what these resolutions must have in common. These common properties in a certain sense characterise the resolution, and Bondal-Orlov
[
2
]
and Kawamata
[
12
]
suggest that the derived category of coherent sheaves is such an invariant of crepant resolutions. In other words, if a normal algebraic variety
X
has two distinct crepant resolutions
\pi_{i}:Y_{i}\rightarrow X
, one should expect an equivalence of categories
\operatorname{D}^{\operatorname{b}}(\operatorname{coh}Y_{1})\cong\operatorname{D}^{\operatorname{b}}(\operatorname{coh}Y_{2})
. Van den Bergh
[
20
]
verified this for a three-fold flop by using tilting theory and constructing a pair of Morita equivalent algebras
\Lambda_{i}
whose module categories are equivalent to the derived categories
\operatorname{D}^{\operatorname{b}}(\operatorname{coh}Y_{i})
. A non-commutative resolution to a noetherian domain
R
is an algebra of the form
\Lambda=\operatorname{End}_{R}(M)
for a finitely generated reflexive
R
-module such that the global dimension of
\Lambda
is finite. If
R
is Gorenstein, such a
\Lambda
is called crepant if in addition it is a maximal Cohen-Macaulay
R
-module. NCCRs are somewhat elusive objects, as evidenced by the fact that the following conjecture remains an open one.

Conjecture 1.1
.

(=Conjecture
2.3
)
An affine Gorenstein toric algebra always has an NCCR.

Partial progress has been made using a wide variety of methods. Broomhead
[
3
]
used dimer models to verify the conjecture for three-dimensional affine Gorenstein toric algebras, a result later reproven by Špenko and Van den Bergh
[
17
]
. In
[
18
]
, the author proves the existence of NCCRs for toric algebras associated to affine toric varieties with Picard rank 1, an alternative proof of which can be found in
[
15
]
. Representation theory and tilting theory are among the more common tools to construct NCCRs, and a good summary of the progress made can be found in
[
21
]
.

Of particular interest to the present paper is the work of Faber, Muller and Smith
[
9
]
, who used
conic modules
to show that simplicial toric algebras admit an NCCR (a result previously shown by Craw and Quintero Vélez
[
6
]
). Given a cone
\sigma
with associated toric algebra
R
, one can construct for each
v\in M_{\mathbb{R}}
a conic module
A_{v}
, which is a Cohen-Macaulay
R
-module. These modules come in isomorphism classes and it suffices to examine the fundamental region
[0,1)^{n}
to fully classify the conic modules. A direct sum
\mathbb{A}
of such modules which contains at least one representative of each isomorphism class has been shown to give an NCCR
\operatorname{End}_{R}(\mathbb{A})
of
R
if and only if
\sigma
is a simplicial cone. However, the authors give an example of a non-simplicial cone which allows for such an NCCR
\operatorname{End}_{R}(\mathbb{B})
by using a direct sum
\mathbb{B}
of conic modules not containing each isomorphism class, but instead excluding some. This is consistent with the idea NCCRs are minimal among NCRs, and so an NCR might be expected to have some subalgebra that is crepant. Naturally, this begs the following question.

Question 1.2

(= Question
2.18
)
.

When does an incomplete sum of conic modules
\mathbb{B}=\bigoplus_{A_{v}\in I}A_{v}
give an NCCR
\operatorname{End}_{R}(\mathbb{B})
of
R=k[\sigma^{\vee}\cap M]
?

To verify the global dimension of the endomorphism algebras and their crepancy as NCRs, the authors of
[
9
]
construct explicit minimal projective resolutions of the graded simples and thus compute their projective dimension. It should be noted that, up to isomorphism, the set of graded simples is in bijection with the isomorphism classes of conic modules and so we can write
S_{v}
for the simple corresponding to
v
. The projective resolutions are obtained by constructing, for each conic module
A_{v}
, a complex
K_{v}^{\bullet}
whose degree zero component is precisely
A_{v}
, and then applying
\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{A},-)
to that complex. Extending to the right by
S_{v}
yields the required resolutions for the simplicial case. The construction of the complexes
K_{v}^{\bullet}
is based on the combinatorial structure of a certain cell-decomposition on
M_{\mathbb{R}}
.

The complex
K_{v}^{\bullet}
is constructed by observing the combinatorial structure of the cell-decomposition on
M_{\mathbb{R}}
that is obtained when considering the loci which give the same conic modules (i.e.
v,v^{\prime}
lie in the same cell if
A_{v}=A_{v^{\prime}}
).

In the given non-simplicial example by Faber-Muller-Smith, one can splice the complexes
K_{v}^{\bullet}
to obtain new complexes
K_{v}^{\dagger,\bullet}
, in bijection with the conic modules
A_{v}
appearing in the incomplete direct sum
\mathbb{B}
. We formalise this splicing process, via the procedure of
substitution
of conic modules, and define the notion of
lockable
sets of conic modules. These are precisely the sets such that a finite sequence of substitutions yields a set of complexes
K_{v}^{\dagger,\bullet}
with each conic module appearing in the complex also appearing in the direct sum
\mathbb{B}
. Such a set is called
incredulous
if furthermore all the complexes have the same length, so that the top degree is
\dim\sigma
. With this notation, we formalise the argument used in
[
9
]
:

Theorem 1.3

(= Theorem
3.6
)
.

Let
\sigma
be a cone with associated toric algebra
R
and collection of conic modules
\{A_{v}\}_{v\in S}
. For a subset
I
of
S
, consider the incomplete direct sum of conic modules
\mathbb{B}=\bigoplus_{A_{v}\in S}A_{v}
. Then the endomorphism algebra
\Lambda^{\prime}=\operatorname{End}_{R}(\mathbb{B})
is an NCR of
R
if and only if
I
is lockable. Furthermore,
\Lambda^{\prime}
is an NCCR of
R
if and only if
I
is incredulous.

Closely related to conic modules is the Bondal-Thomsen collection, a collection of line bundles defined on toric varieties and stacks. In
[
1
]
, the authors explicitly define and describe the Bondal-Thomsen collection associated to a smooth toric Deligne-Mumford stack and note that if the underlying toric variety is affine, the collection of line bundles corresponds to the collection of conic modules.
The Bondal-Thomsen collection can be used to construct a tilting bundle on the so-called
Cox category
, which is a derived category that in a certain sense encodes the bounded derived categories of the different toric DM stacks
\mathcal{X}_{i}
that share the same Cox ring. These toric DM stacks are related by birational maps inducing Fourier-Mukai transforms between the bounded derived categories, and the Cox category can be interpreted as a category glued together along these transforms, acting as transition functions.

In the present paper, we explicitly provide the relationship between the collection of conic modules of a cone
\sigma
and the Bondal-Thomsen collection associated to a simplicial subdivision
\Sigma
of
\sigma
that does not introduce any additional rays. Leveraging this relationship, we introduce another point of view to compute the complexes
K_{v}^{\bullet}
associated to conic modules. The Bondal-Thomsen collection associated to a toric DM stack
\mathcal{X}
is, up to torsion, in bijection with lattice points inside a zonotope
Z_{\mathcal{X}}
. Consider the divisorial exact sequence for a toric variety,

M\xrightarrow{f}\bigoplus_{\rho\in\Sigma(1)}\mathbb{Z}\cdot D_{\rho}\rightarrow\operatorname{coker}(f)\rightarrow 0.

Here, the divisors
D_{\rho}
are the torus-invariant Weil divisors associated to the rays
\rho\in\Sigma(1)
and the map
f
is given by
\sum_{\rho\in\Sigma(1)}\langle m,u_{\rho}\rangle D_{\rho}
. Note that the map
f
can be represented by a matrix with rows corresponding to the vectors
u_{\rho}
. Tensoring with
\mathbb{R}
, the cokernel map becomes a linear map we denote by
f_{\sigma}
. Representing it by a matrix whose rows are primitive
\mathbb{Z}
-vectors, we consider the column vectors
\beta_{\rho}
, one for each
\rho\in\Sigma(1)
. These span a generalised fan known as the secondary fan, or GKZ fan, and the zonotope
Z_{\mathcal{X}}
is the convex set
\sum(-1,0]\beta_{\rho}
. Interior lattice points correspond to elements of the Bondal-Thomsen collection, up to torsion, and these line bundles correspond to conic modules. For each lattice point
P
in the zonotope, there are
|\operatorname{Tors}(\mathcal{X})|
conic modules mapping to
P
; two conic modules map to the same lattice point if and only if the divisors
-d(v)
differ by torsion.
For
v\in M_{\mathbb{R}}
, we denote by
f_{\sigma}(-d(v))
the lattice point corresponding to the conic module
A_{v}
. We use this correspondence and define the notion of
valid path
between such lattice points.

A path
\beta_{J},J\subset\sigma(1)
, from
P_{1}
to
P_{2}
is a set
\{\beta_{\rho}\mid\rho\in J\subset\sigma(1)\}
such that
P_{1}+\sum_{\rho\in J}\beta_{\rho}=P_{2}
. It is
valid
if
\sum_{\rho\in J}\alpha_{\rho}\beta_{\rho}
lies in the set
\sum_{\rho\in J^{c}}(-\alpha_{\rho}-1,-\alpha_{\rho})\beta_{\rho}
. The length of a path
\beta_{J}
is the dimension of
\operatorname{Span}(u_{\rho}|\rho\in J)
. Using the notion of valid paths, we note the following condition for a conic modules
A_{w}
to appear in the complex
K_{v}^{\bullet}
.

Proposition 1.4

(= Proposition
4.16
)
.

There is a valid path from a lattice point
P_{1}
to
f_{\sigma}(-d(v))
, if and only if there is a
w\in M_{\mathbb{R}}
such that the isomorphism class of
A_{w}
appears in
K_{v}^{\bullet}
and
P_{1}=f_{\sigma}(-d(w))
. For a given valid path
\beta_{J}
, this
w\in M_{\mathbb{R}}
fulfills
-d(w)\sim-d(v)-\sum_{\rho\in J}D_{\rho}
and the degree in which
A_{w}
appears in
K_{v}^{\bullet}
is equal to the length of the path
\beta_{J}
.

Formally introducing a symbol
A_{P}
for each lattice point
P\in Z_{\mathcal{X}}
, this allows the construction of complexes for each
P\in Z_{\mathcal{X}}
and, in analogy to the complexes
K_{v}^{\bullet}
, we obtain a notion of incredulous sets of lattice points. We prove the following.

Theorem 1.5

(= Theorem
4.22
)
.

Let
\sigma
be a cone with a collection of conic modules
S
. Let
I\subseteq S
be an incredulous set of conic modules. Then the set of lattice points
I^{\prime}=\{f_{\sigma}(-d(v))\mid A_{v}\in I\}
is an incredulous set of lattice points. In particular, the following are equivalent.

(1)

There exists an incredulous set of conic modules.

(2)

There exists an incredulous set of lattice points in
Z_{\mathcal{X}}
.

(3)

There exists an NCCR of
\sigma
of the form
\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{A})
where
\mathbb{A}
is a direct sum of conic modules.

This result significantly simplifies computations, as we can essentially reduce to the case where the class group of the toric variety has no torsion. So the number of complexes to compute reduces by a factor of
|\operatorname{Tors}(\mathcal{X})|
.

Using incredulous sets of lattice points, we are able to answer Question
1.2
for the case of
almost simplicial
Gorenstein cones, i.e. Gorenstein cones
\sigma
with
|\sigma(1)|=\dim\sigma+1
.

Theorem 1.6

(= Theorem
5.10
)
.

Let
\sigma
be an almost simplicial Gorenstein cone. Then there exists an incomplete sum
\mathbb{B}
of conic modules such that
\operatorname{End}_{R}(\mathbb{B})
is an NCCR of
R=k[\sigma^{\vee}\cap M]
if and only if any of the following holds:

•

The collection of
\beta_{\rho}
associated to
\sigma
is, up to flipping all signs,
\{2,1,-1,-1,-1\}
.

•

The collection of
\beta_{\rho}
associated to
\sigma
is
\{1,1,1,-1,-1,-1\}
.

•

\sigma
is a 3-dimensional Gorenstein cone lattice equivalent to
\sigma=\operatorname{Cone}(P\times\{1\})
, where
P
is a trapezoid.

Structure and notation

We begin this paper by reviewing the works of Faber-Muller-Smith
[
9
]
and Ballard et al.
[
1
]
in
\lx@sectionsign

2
, also giving a brief introduction to non-commutative crepant resolutions. In
\lx@sectionsign

3
, we formalise the link between conic modules and the Bondal-Thomsen collection, introducing the notion of substitution of conic modules and proving that an incomplete sum of conic modules gives an NCCR if and only if the corresponding set of conic modules is an incredulous set. The next part of the paper,
\lx@sectionsign

4
, provides a number of computational results. It is here we introduce the notion of viable paths, explaining how the combinatorics of the secondary fan simplify the search for incredulous sets (and thus NCCRs), eventually proving that we can reduce to the search for incredulous sets of lattice points. Finally, in
\lx@sectionsign

5
, we illustrate the advantage of the methods introduced before by classifying which almost simplicial Gorenstein cone admit NCCRs via incomplete sums of conic modules.

Throughout the paper, unless stated otherwise, we adopt a few pieces of notation. For a given toric variety, the character lattice is usually denoted by
M
and the cocharacter lattice by
N
. Their pairing
M\times N\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
extends naturally to a pairing of
M_{\mathbb{R}}:=M\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R}
and
N_{\mathbb{R}}:=N\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R}
. Given a fan
\Sigma\subset N_{\mathbb{R}}
, for each ray
\rho\in\Sigma(1)
, we denote its primitive generator in
N
as
u_{\rho}
and we denote the torus invariant Weil divisor associated to
\rho
by
D_{\rho}
.

Given a normal noetherian domain
R
, a finitely generated
R
-module is called
reflexive
if the canonical map
M\mapsto\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(M,R),R)
is an isomorphism, and these modules form a category denoted by
\operatorname{ref}R
. Given a reflexive
R
-algebra
\Lambda
, we also consider the category of reflexive
\Lambda
-modules
\operatorname{ref}\Lambda
. Assuming further that
R
is commutative, an
R
-module
M
is said to be
maximal Cohen-Macaulay
if
M_{m}
is maximal Cohen-Macaulay for every maximal ideal
m
. These modules form a category denoted by
\operatorname{CM}R
and the word maximal will be omitted throughout this paper. Note that a module
M
is in
\operatorname{CM}R
if and only if
\operatorname{Ext}^{i}(M,R)=0
for all
i>0
. Finally, we fix an algebraically closed field
k
of characteristic 0.

2.
Background

In this section, we will revisit some notions appearing in the study of desingularisations of affine toric varieties. We shall first introduce the notion of
non-commutative (crepant) resolution (NCCR)
, due to Van den Bergh
[
19
]
. Then, we discuss the use of conic modules, which were first studied systematically in
[
4
]
, to generate NCCRs. The here present introduction to conic modules is based on the paper by Faber-Muller-Smith
[
9
]
. Finally, we review the Bondal-Thomsen collection and the geometry of the secondary fan, with a view towards NCCRs, following closely the exposition by Ballard et al.
[
1
]
.

2.1.
NCCRs

In a more classical algebraic geometry setting, a resolution of singularities for a singular variety
X
is a proper birational morphism
\phi:Y\rightarrow X
from a smooth variety
Y
to
X
such that
\phi
is an isomorphism away from the singular locus of
X
. It is crepant if the canonical divisor pulls back without acquiring additional ”discrepancies”, i.e.
K_{Y}=\phi^{\ast}K_{X}
. Such a resolution is not unique, but we do expect (see
[
2
,
12
]
) that two distinct crepant resolutions
Y_{1},Y_{2}
are equivalent on the level of derived categories, i.e.
\operatorname{D}^{\operatorname{b}}(\operatorname{coh}Y_{1})\cong\operatorname{D}^{\operatorname{b}}(\operatorname{coh}Y_{2})
. This motivates the idea that the derived category itself in some sense is a crepant resolution. abstracting one step further, if a crepant resolution
Y
admits a tilting object
T
(see Definition
2.2
below) of its derived category, we have
\operatorname{D}^{\operatorname{b}}(\operatorname{coh}Y)\cong\operatorname{D}^{\operatorname{b}}(\operatorname{mod}\operatorname{End}(T))
, and so van den Bergh suggests that the ring
\Lambda=\operatorname{End}(T)
, a purely algebraic object, can also be considered a crepant resolution. To remain on a completely algebraic level, thus ignoring whether or not there exist underlying geometric objects, we consider
\Lambda
to be a resolution of the coordinate ring
R
of the variety. The following is always formulated in terms of affine schemes, i.e.
X=\operatorname{Spec}R
, but this can be extended to non-affine cases by reducing to affine patches.

Definition 2.1
.

A
non-commutative resolution
of
R
is a trivial reflexive Azumaya algebra
\Lambda=\operatorname{End}_{R}(M)
for
M\in\operatorname{ref}R
such that
\operatorname{gl}\dim\Lambda<\infty
. Assuming further that
R
is Gorenstein, we call
\Lambda

crepant
if it is additionally a Cohen-Macaulay
R
-module.

Non-commutative resolutions are intrinsically linked to
tilting objects
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
Y
be a Noetherian scheme. A perfect complex on
Y
is
partial tilting
if
\operatorname{Ext}^{i}_{Y}(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{T})=0
for
i\neq 0
. It is
tilting
if it generates
D_{Qch}(Y)
, i.e.
\operatorname{RHom}_{Y}(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{F})=0
implies
\mathcal{F}=0
. Analogously we define (partial) tilting complexes for smooth, separated Noetherian DM stacks.

Tilting objects often generate NCCRs; if
X=\operatorname{Spec}R
has a crepant resolution
Y
with tilting object
\mathcal{T}
, it is often straightforward to show that
\Lambda:=\operatorname{End}_{Y}(\mathcal{T})
defines an NCCR of
R
. Given an endomorphism algebra
\Lambda=\operatorname{End}_{R}(M)
of a reflexive module over a noetherian ring
R
, one obtains an NCCR if the global dimension is finite and if
\Lambda
is Cohen-Macaulay. If
R
itself is a normal Cohen-Macaulay domain of dimension
d
, then this corresponds to every simple
\Lambda
-module having projective dimension
d
.

Since the inception of NCCRs, significant amounts of effort have gone into proving the existence thereof for different cases. A first class of varieties one is naturally drawn to consider is that of affine Gorenstein toric varieties.
For the convenience of the reader, let us here recall what affine Gorenstein toric varieties are. A cone
\sigma\subset N_{\mathbb{R}}
is defined via the generators
u_{\rho_{1}},\dots,u_{\rho_{s}}
of its one-dimensional facets, known as rays:

\sigma=\operatorname{Cone}(u_{\rho_{i}})=\{\sum\lambda_{i}u_{\rho_{i}}|\lambda_{i}\geq 0\}\subseteq N_{\mathbb{R}}.

To a cone
\sigma\subset N_{\mathbb{R}}
, which we assume to be full-dimensional, we associate a dual cone

\sigma^{\vee}:=\{m\in M_{\mathbb{R}}|\langle m,n\rangle\geq 0\quad\forall n\in\sigma\}.

Intersecting with
M
determines a semigroup
\sigma^{\vee}\cap M
and so we can form the
toric algebra

R_{\sigma}:=k[\sigma^{\vee}\cap M]
. The affine toric variety associated to the cone
\sigma
is defined to be
X_{\sigma}=\operatorname{Spec}R_{\sigma}
. We call such a variety
Gorenstein
if there exists an element
m\in M
such that
\langle m,u_{\rho_{i}}\rangle=1
for all primitive generators
u_{\rho_{i}}
of the rays
\rho_{i}
of
\sigma
.

Even in the case of affine Gorenstein toric varieties, it is unclear if NCCRs always exist, and this is the content of the following conjecture of Van den Bergh’s.

Conjecture 2.3
.

An affine Gorenstein toric variety always has an NCCR.

Whilst the general case is still unsolved, progress has been made towards proving this conjecture. Broomhead
[
3
]
showed, using the theory of dimer models, that all 3-dimensional affine Gorenstein toric varieties have an NCCR. A later reproof by Špenko and Van den Bergh
[
17
]
avoids the technical tool of dimer models by constructing tilting bundles on simplicial refinements of the Gorenstein cone underlying the toric varieties.

Proposition 2.4

(Proposition 3.3 in
[
17
]
)
.

Given a Gorenstein cone
\sigma\subset N_{\mathbb{R}}
, write it in the form
\operatorname{Cone}(P\times\{1\})
for
P
a lattice polytope. Choose a regular triangulation of
P
without extra vertices and let
\Sigma
be the corresponding fan. Let
\mathcal{T}
be a tilting bundle on
\mathcal{X}_{\Sigma}
, the associated DM stack. Then
\Lambda=\operatorname{End}_{\mathcal{X}_{\Sigma}}(\mathcal{T})
is an NCCR for
R=k[\sigma^{\vee}\cap M]
corresponding to
M^{\prime}=\Gamma(\mathcal{X}_{\Sigma},\mathcal{T})
.

Previous work by the author and Sheshmani
[
14
]
extended this result.

Theorem 2.5

(= Theorem 3.12 in
[
14
]
)
.

Given a Gorenstein cone
\sigma\subset N_{\mathbb{R}}
, write it in the form
\operatorname{Cone}(P\times\{1\})
for
P
a lattice polytope.
Choose a regular triangulation of
P
and let
\Sigma
be the corresponding fan refining
\sigma
. Let
\mathcal{T}
be a partial tilting complex on
\mathcal{X}_{\Sigma}
, the associated toric DM stack. Assume that
\Lambda=\operatorname{End}_{\mathcal{X}_{\Sigma}}(\mathcal{T})
has finite global dimension. Then it is an NCCR for
R=k[\sigma^{\vee}\cap M]
.

Note here that Donovan-Hara-Kapustka-Rampazzo
[
8
]
proved that such a partial tilting object with finite global dimension in fact is tilting. Using Theorem
2.5
, in
[
15
]
we show that toric algebras associated to affine toric varieties of Picard ranks 0 and 1 admit toric NCCRs. The latter has also been shown via a different methodology by Tomonaga
[
18
]
whilst the former is known due to work by Craw and Quintero Vélez
[
6
]
. Another proof of the simplicial case is due to Faber, Muller and Smith
[
9
]
and relies on conic modules, which we will introduce next.

2.2.
Conic modules

The following introduction to conic modules closely follows the work of Faber, Muller and Smith
[
9
]
. As setup, we consider a cone
\sigma\subset N_{\mathbb{R}}
and its dual cone
\sigma^{\vee}\subset M_{\mathbb{R}}
together with the associated noetherian domain
R=k[\sigma^{\vee}\cap M]
. We may assume
\sigma
is strongly convex, which equivalently implies that
\sigma^{\vee}
is of full dimension. Note that

\sigma^{\vee}=\bigcap_{\rho\in\sigma(1)}\{v\in M_{\mathbb{R}}|\langle v,u_{\rho}\rangle\geq 0\}.

Definition 2.6
.

For
v\in M_{\mathbb{R}}
, the
conic module defined by

v
is the
M
-graded
R
-submodule of
k[M]
defined as

A_{v}:=\operatorname{Span}\{x^{m}|m\in M\cap(\sigma^{\vee}+v)\}.

Faber Muller and Smith show that conic modules are always Cohen-Macaulay
[
9
, Corollary 4.16]
, and also demonstrate other key properties.

Proposition 2.7

( Proposition 3.2 in
[
9
]
)
.

Let
A_{v}
be a conic module for
R=k[\sigma^{\vee}\cap M]
. Then

(1)

A_{v}
is torsion free and rank one over
R
.

(2)

A_{v}
is spanned by monomial
x^{m}
, where

m\in\bigcap_{\rho\in\sigma(1)}\{x\in M_{\mathbb{R}}|\langle x,u_{\rho}\rangle\geq\lceil\langle v,u_{\rho}\rangle\rceil\}\subset M.

(3)

For any two conic modules
A_{v},A_{w}
, the
R
-module
\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(A_{v},A_{w})
is naturally isomorphic to the
M
-graded
R
-submodule of
k[M]

\operatorname{Span}\{x^{m}|m+(\sigma^{\vee}+v)\cap M\subset(\sigma^{\vee}+w)\}.

In addition to conic modules as defined above, we shall also consider
open conic modules
.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
v\in M_{\mathbb{R}}
. The
open conic module
associated to
v
is the
M
-graded
R
-submodule of
k[M]
defined as follows:

\overset{\circ}{A}_{v}:=\operatorname{Span}\{x^{m}|m\in M\cap(\overset{\circ}{\sigma^{\vee}}+v)\}.

As it turns out, open conic modules are conic modules themselves - associated to nearby points.

Proposition 2.9

(Proposition 3.5 in
[
9
]
)
.

For every open conic
R
module
\overset{\circ}{A}_{v}
, there exists
w\in M_{\mathbb{R}}
such that

\overset{\circ}{A}_{v}=A_{w}.

Explicitly, for any
p\in\overset{\circ}{\sigma^{\vee}}
and
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
, we can take
w=v+\varepsilon p
.

At this stage, it is a natural question to ask when two different
v,w\in M_{\mathbb{R}}
define equal or at least isomorphic conic modules.

Definition 2.10
.

The
chamber of constancy

\Delta\subset M_{\mathbb{R}}
containing
v\in M_{\mathbb{R}}
is the set of all
w\in M_{\mathbb{R}}
such that
A_{v}=A_{w}
. Equivalently,
v,w
belong to the same chamber of constancy iff
(\sigma^{\vee}+v)\cap M=(\sigma^{\vee}+w)\cap M
.

Given
v\in M_{\mathbb{R}}
, we denote its chamber of constancy by
\Delta_{v}
, but if we are given just a chamber of constancy without explicit choice of element inside, we will write
A_{\Delta}
for the conic module that the elements
v\in\Delta
all share. These chambers of constancy decompose
M_{\mathbb{R}}
into disjoint locally closed polyhedral regions
[
9
, Corollary 4.4]
and can be explicitly computed.

Proposition 2.11

(Proposition 4.3 in
[
9
]
)
.

For
v\in M_{\mathbb{R}}
, its chamber of constancy is given by the set

\displaystyle\Delta_{v}

\displaystyle=\{x\in M_{\mathbb{R}}|\lceil\langle v,u_{\rho}\rangle\rceil-1<\langle x,u_{\rho}\rangle\leq\lceil\langle v,u_{\rho}\rangle\rceil\text{ for }\rho\in\sigma(1)\}

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{\rho\in\sigma(1)}\{x\in M_{\mathbb{R}}|\lceil\langle v,u_{\rho}\rangle\rceil-1<\langle x,u_{\rho}\rangle\leq\lceil\langle v,u_{\rho}\rangle\rceil\}.

Remark 2.12
.

The proof that Faber, Muller and Smith give for Proposition
2.11
further implies that a given conic module
A_{v}
is uniquely determined by the list of integers

(\lceil\langle v,u_{\rho}\rangle\rceil)_{\rho\in\sigma(1)}\in\mathbb{Z}^{t},

where
t=|\sigma(1)|
. This shows that
A_{v}=\Gamma(X_{\sigma},\mathcal{O}_{X_{\sigma}}(-D))
, where
D=\sum_{\rho\in\sigma(1)}\lceil\langle v,u_{\rho}\rangle\rceil D_{\rho}
. However, the simple observation that for a non-smooth cone
\sigma
the
u_{\rho}
are linearly dependent points to the fact that not all lists of integers appear as
t
-tuple
(\lceil\langle v,u_{\rho}\rangle\rceil)_{\rho\in\sigma(1)}
.

Given a strongly convex, full-dimensional cone
\sigma\subset N_{\mathbb{R}}
, the chambers of constancy determine a CW composition of
M_{\mathbb{R}}
(
[
5
]
), which can be elucidated
by studying (open) conic modules. Now
[
9
, Corollary 4.9]
establishes that
v,w\in M_{\mathbb{R}}
lie in the same open cell of the CW decomposition if and only if both
A_{v}=A_{w},\overset{\circ}{A}_{v}=\overset{\circ}{A}_{w}
. If
v,w\in\Delta
lie in the same chamber of constancy, then there is an inclusion
\overset{\circ}{A}_{v}\subset\overset{\circ}{A}_{w}
if and only if the open cell containing
v
is contained in the boundary of the open cell containing
w
. For this reason, we may later denote the open conic module
\overset{\circ}{A}_{v}
associated to
v\in M_{\mathbb{R}}
by
\overset{\circ}{A}_{\tau}
, where
\tau
is the open cell containing
v
. This notation will mainly come into play when the chamber of constancy
\Delta
is fixed, as it is possible to have
\overset{\circ}{A}_{\tau}=\overset{\circ}{A}_{\tau^{\prime}}
for
\tau\neq\tau^{\prime}
.

While
A_{\Delta}\neq A_{\Delta^{\prime}}
for two distinct chambers, they might still be isomorphic. The following Proposition tells us when this can happen.

Proposition 2.13

(Proposition 4.11 in
[
9
]
)
.

Two conic
R
-modules
A_{\Delta},A_{\Delta^{\prime}}
are isomorphic if and only if there is an
m\in M
such that
\Delta=\Delta^{\prime}+m
.

Thus,
M
acts via translation on the set of chambers of constancy, with orbits of this action corresponding to distinct isomorphism classes of conic modules. As such, all the information on the chambers of constancy we may wish to know can be found by studying the (real) torus
M_{\mathbb{R}}/M
and its induced CW decomposition. The maximal cells correspond bijectively to the different isomorphism classes of conic modules for
R
. Since the torus is compact, we deduce that there is only finitely many different isomorphism classes of conic
R
-modules for any given toric algebra
R
. Note that there is a partial ordering on the set of conic
R
-modules, given by inclusion. This induces a partial ordering on the chambers of constancy, and we write
\Delta\preceq\Delta^{\prime}
if and only if
A_{\Delta}\subseteq A_{\Delta^{\prime}}
.

Fix now a conic module
A_{\Delta}
over
R
, corresponding to a chamber of constancy
\Delta
. We shall introduce for it a complex of
R
-modules that can be used to construct projective resolutions for the graded simples of the proposed NC(C)Rs later. For each, open cell of
\Delta
, fix an orientation. Note that the CW decomposition restricted to
\Delta
will not skip any dimension, i.e. if
\tau_{i}
is a cell of
\Delta
of codimension
i>0
then it is contained in the boundary of some cell
\tau_{i-1}
of
\Delta
of codimension
i-1
. As such,
\tau_{i}
inherits an orientation from
\tau_{i-1}
, which may or may not agree with the orientation for
\tau_{i}
we fixed earlier. Write
\operatorname{sgn}(\tau_{i},\tau_{i-1})=+1
if the orientations agree and
-1
if they do not. If there is no inclusion of cells, define the sign to be 0. The inclusion of cells induces an inclusion of open conic modules
\iota_{\tau_{i},\tau_{i-1}}:\overset{\circ}{A}_{\tau_{i}}\hookrightarrow\overset{\circ}{A}_{\tau_{i-1}}
. For sake of notation, we consider the map
\iota_{\tau_{i},\tau_{i-1}}:\overset{\circ}{A}_{\tau_{i}}\hookrightarrow\overset{\circ}{A}_{\tau_{i-1}}
to be the zero map whenever
\overset{\circ}{A}_{\tau_{i}}\not\subset\overset{\circ}{A}_{\tau_{i-1}}
for codimension
i,i-1
cells
\tau_{i},\tau_{i-1}
.

Definition 2.14
.

Given a chamber of constancy
\Delta
, define a complex
K_{\Delta}^{\bullet}
of
M
-graded
R
-modules

\dots\rightarrow\bigoplus_{\operatorname{codim}(\tau)=2}\overset{\circ}{A}_{\tau}\rightarrow\bigoplus_{\operatorname{codim}(\tau)=1}\overset{\circ}{A}_{\tau}\rightarrow\bigoplus_{\operatorname{codim}(\tau)=0}\overset{\circ}{A}_{\tau}=A_{\Delta}.

The direct sums range over all cells of
\Delta
of the specified codimension, and the maps are given by signed sums of inclusion maps
\sum\operatorname{sgn}(\tau_{i},\tau_{i-1})\iota_{\tau_{i},\tau_{i-1}}
. We say that a complex has length
l
if the highest non-zero degree is
l
. Where clear, we write
K_{v}^{\bullet}
for the complex
K_{\Delta_{v}}^{\bullet}
.

Remark 2.15
.

The fact that
K_{\Delta}^{\bullet}
is indeed an
M
-graded complex of
R
-modules can be found in
[
9
, Lemma 5.1]
. The length of this complex is equal to the codimension of the smallest dimensional cell appearing in
\Delta
, which is at most
\dim R
(the Krull dimension of
R
). The length can be smaller than that, as for non-simplicial cones
\sigma
, not all chambers of constancy
\Delta
contain zero-dimensional cells.

Of particular interest for these complexes is the following result, providing a notion of acyclicity.

Lemma 2.16
.

Fix a toric algebra
R
and consider two conic modules
A_{\Delta},A_{\Delta^{\prime}}
for
R
, associated to two chambers of constancy
\Delta,\Delta^{\prime}
.

(1)

The complex
\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(A_{\Delta^{\prime}},K_{\Delta}^{\bullet})
is exact if
A_{\Delta}\not\simeq A_{\Delta^{\prime}}
;

(2)

If
A_{\Delta}\simeq A_{\Delta^{\prime}}
, then
\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(A_{\Delta^{\prime}},K_{\Delta}^{\bullet})
has one-dimensional homology, which appears (only) in homological degree zero and
M
-degree
m
, where
m
is the unique element of
M
such that
\Delta=\Delta^{\prime}+m
.

For
M
a finite direct sum of conic modules, Faber, Muller and Smith decide both Cohen-Macaulayness and finite global dimension of
\Lambda=\operatorname{End}_{R}(M)
by giving minimal projective resolutions for all simple
\Lambda
-modules. To examine whether a given sum of conic modules
M
gives an NCCR
\operatorname{End}_{R}(M)
, the strategy is as follows. Firstly, one can show that all graded simple
\operatorname{End}_{R}(M)
-modules arise as quotient of
P_{\Delta}:=\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(M,A_{\Delta})
, for some chamber of constancy
\Delta
, by its unique maximal graded submodule (see
[
9
, Proposition 6.3]
). Denote this simple module by
S_{\Delta}
. Furthermore, all graded indecomposable projective modules are of the form
P_{\Delta}
for some
\Delta
appearing in
M
. Then, one shows that
\operatorname{End}_{R}(M,K_{\Delta}^{\bullet})
forms a graded
\operatorname{End}_{R}(M)
-projective resolution of the simple module
S_{\Delta}
and that this resolution has minimal length
[
9
, Theorem 6.5]
.

A first instinct to follow then is to take a
complete
sum of conic modules, i.e.
M=\bigoplus A_{\Delta}^{r_{\Delta}}
where the sum ranges over all chambers of constancy and
r_{\Delta}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
. However, Faber Muller and Smith show that while this always produces an NCR (since we have finite length resolutions of length at most
d=\dim R
of all simples
S_{\Delta}
), crepancy happens if and only if the cone
\sigma
is simplicial. They do, however, give an example of how this problem can potentially be remedied by restricting one’s attention to a subset of the chambers of constancy.

Example 2.17

(Example 7.10 in
[
9
]
)
.

Consider the cone
\sigma\subset N_{\mathbb{R}}\cong\mathbb{R}^{3}
spanned by the four rays with primitive generators
(1,0,0)
,
(0,1,0)
,
(-1,0,1)
,
(0,-1,1)
. The dual cone
\sigma^{\vee}
has primitive ray generators
(0,0,1)
,
(1,0,1)
,
(0,1,1)
and
(1,1,1)
and corresponds to the cone over a square. The toric algebra is
R=k[x,y,z,w]/(xz-yw)
. There are three isomorphism types of conic
R
-modules:

A_{0}=R
,
A_{1}=(x,y)R
and
A_{2}=(x,w)R
. The associated complexes
K_{i}^{\bullet}
are as follows:

\displaystyle K_{0}^{\bullet}=A_{0}\rightarrow A_{0}^{\oplus 4}\rightarrow A_{1}^{\oplus 2}\oplus A_{2}^{\oplus 2}\rightarrow A_{0},

\displaystyle K_{1}^{\bullet}=A_{2}\rightarrow A_{0}^{\oplus 2}\rightarrow A_{1},

\displaystyle K_{2}^{\bullet}=A_{1}\rightarrow A_{0}^{\oplus 2}\rightarrow A_{2}.\;

We first note that the complexes are not of the same length, so while we obtain an NCR of
R
via
\operatorname{End}_{R}(A_{0}\oplus A_{1}\oplus A_{2})
, this will not be crepant. However, the authors of
[
9
]
observe that using the complex for
A_{2}
, one can build new complexes

\displaystyle K_{0}^{\dagger,\bullet}=A_{0}\rightarrow A_{1}^{\oplus 2}\oplus A_{0}^{\oplus 4}\rightarrow A_{0}^{\oplus 4}\oplus A_{1}^{\oplus 2}\rightarrow A_{0},

\displaystyle K_{1}^{\dagger,\bullet}=A_{1}\rightarrow A_{0}^{\oplus 2}\rightarrow A_{0}^{\oplus 2}\rightarrow A_{1}.\;

The functor
\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(A_{0}\oplus A_{1},-)
takes these complexes to minimal length projective resolutions of the only two graded simples
\operatorname{End}_{R}(A_{0}\oplus A_{1})
-modules. The arguments leading to NCCRs of simplicial cones then follow through to show that
\operatorname{End}_{R}(A_{0}\oplus A_{1})
is an NCCR of
R
.

The above example leads to the following question that this present paper aims to investigate.

Question 2.18
.

When does an incomplete sum of conic modules
M=\bigoplus_{\Delta\in I}A_{\Delta}
give an NCCR
\operatorname{End}_{R}(M)
of
R
?

2.3.
The Bondal-Thomsen Collection

As a means to investigate Question
2.18
, we will examine the combinatorics of conic modules and relate them to the geometry of the secondary fan of a simplicial refinement
\Sigma
of
\sigma
. The idea for this is inspired by the work of Ballard et al.
[
1
]
, where the authors link the
Cox category
they introduce to the NCCRs constructed by Faber, Muller and Smith. The discussion happens on the level of toric DM stacks, whose derived categories are usually better behaved than the underlying toric varieties’. For instance, a simplicial, but not necessarily smooth, toric fan gives a smooth DM stack with coarse moduli space the appropriate toric variety. Given now a fixed (simplicial) toric variety
X_{\Sigma}
, we consider its secondary fan
\Sigma_{GKZ}
, whose maximal chambers correspond to simplicial toric varieties
X_{1},\dots,X_{r}
.
Intuitively, the Cox category is a triangulated category which encodes the information of all the categories
\operatorname{D}^{\operatorname{b}}(\operatorname{coh}\mathcal{X}_{i})
, where
\mathcal{X}_{i}
is the DM stack associated to
X_{i}
. These toric DM stacks
(\mathcal{X}_{i})_{i=1}^{r}
are related by birational maps, whose graphs induce Fourier-Mukai transforms
\Phi_{i,j}:\operatorname{D}^{\operatorname{b}}(\operatorname{coh}\mathcal{X}_{i})\rightarrow\operatorname{D}^{\operatorname{b}}(\operatorname{coh}\mathcal{X}_{j})
between the derived categories. One viewpoint of the Cox category is to consider the categories
\operatorname{D}^{\operatorname{b}}(\operatorname{coh}\mathcal{X}_{i})
to be analogous to affine patches that are glued together along the Fourier-Mukai transforms, acting as transition functions, to give the Cox category. We refer the reader to Appendix A in
[
1
]
to make this perspective precise using the Grothendieck construction. One of the main results of the paper
[
1
]
is a proof that the Cox category admits a strong, full exceptional collection. This collection comes from the
Bondal-Thomsen collection
. We shall inspect this collection closer and illustrate its links to the conic modules discussed above. We begin by defining the toric DM stack associated to a toric variety
X_{\Sigma}
.

We fix, as usual, a pair of dual lattices
M,N\cong\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and a fan
\Sigma\subset N_{\mathbb{R}}
with a set of
k
primitive ray generators
\nu=\{u_{\rho}\mid\rho\in\Sigma(1)\}\subset N
. Consider now the vector space
\mathbb{R}^{k}
with an elementary
\mathbb{Z}
-basis enumerated by the set of rays of
\Sigma
,
\{e_{\rho}\}_{\rho\in\Sigma(1)}
. Define the
Cox fan
of
\Sigma
to be

\operatorname{Cox}(\Sigma):=\{\operatorname{Cone}(e_{\rho}\mid\rho\in\sigma)\mid\sigma\in\Sigma\}.

This fan is a subfan of the standard fan for
\mathbb{A}^{k}
and thus its associated toric variety can be viewed as open subspace of the affine space
\mathbb{A}^{k}
, which we denote by
U_{\Sigma}:=X_{\operatorname{Cox}(\Sigma)}
.

Next, we consider the right exact sequence

\displaystyle M\xrightarrow{f}\mathbb{Z}^{k}\xrightarrow{\pi}\operatorname{coker}f\rightarrow 0,

(2.1)

\displaystyle m\mapsto\sum_{\rho\in\Sigma(1)}\langle m,u_{\rho}\rangle e_{\rho}.\;

Apply the functor
\operatorname{Hom}(-,\mathbb{G}_{m})
to obtain the left exact sequence:

1\rightarrow\operatorname{Hom}(\operatorname{coker}f,\mathbb{G}_{m})\xrightarrow{\hat{\pi}}\mathbb{G}_{m}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{G}_{m}^{n}.

The group
S_{\Sigma}:=\operatorname{Hom}(\operatorname{coker}f,\mathbb{G}_{m})
acts on
U_{\Sigma}
, and so we define the following quotient stack:

Definition 2.19
.

The
Cox stack
associated to
\Sigma
is

\mathcal{X}_{\Sigma}:=[U_{\Sigma}/S_{\Sigma}].

We often simply say toric (DM) stacks when we mean their Cox stacks. Let us explicitly recall the following Theorem, highlighting the strength of considering Cox stacks instead of only toric varieties.

Theorem 2.20

(Theorem 4.12 in
[
10
]
)
.

If
\Sigma
is simplicial, then
\mathcal{X}_{\Sigma}
is a smooth Deligne-Mumford stack with coarse moduli space
X_{\Sigma}
. When
\Sigma
is smooth (equivalently, the variety
X_{\Sigma}
is smooth),
\mathcal{X}_{\Sigma}\cong X_{\Sigma}
.

Within the class group of
\mathcal{X}_{\Sigma}
, we now define the Bondal-Thomsen collection.

Definition 2.21
.

The
Bondal-Thomsen collection
for
\mathcal{X}
is the set
\Theta_{\mathcal{X}}
of degrees
-d\in\operatorname{Cl}(\mathcal{X})
that are, for some
\theta\in M_{\mathbb{R}}
, equivalent to

\sum_{\rho\in\Sigma(1)}\lfloor\langle-\theta,u_{\rho}\rangle\rfloor D_{\rho}.

Notationally, we may refer to the class in
\Theta_{\mathcal{X}}
corresponding to
\theta\in M_{\mathbb{R}}
by
-d(\theta)
. Such an element is entirely determined by the image of
\theta
in the torus
M_{\mathbb{R}}/M
.

Remark 2.22
.

The definition of the Bondal-Thomsen collection in
[
1
]
is a little more general, as the authors consider toric stacks from stacky fans, Recall that a stacky fan is determined by the data of the fan
\Sigma
and a homomorphism
\beta:\mathbb{Z}^{\Sigma(1)}\rightarrow N
with
\beta(e_{\rho})=b_{\rho}u_{\rho}
for some
b_{\rho}>0
. Choosing
b_{\rho}=1
for all
\rho\in\Sigma(1)
we recover the definition of Cox stacks above.

Definition 2.23
.

Define the (partial) zonotope
Z_{\mathcal{X}}
associated to the toric DM stack
\mathcal{X}
to be the image of
(-1,0]^{\Sigma(1)}
under the map
\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma(1)}\rightarrow\operatorname{Cl}(\mathcal{X})_{\mathbb{R}}
induced by (
2.1
).

The Bondal-Thomsen collection has several equivalent characterisations.

Proposition 2.24

(Proposition 2.17 in
[
1
]
)
.

The following subsets of
\operatorname{Cl}(\mathcal{X})
are equal:

(1)

The Bondal-Thomsen collection
\Theta_{\mathcal{X}}
.

(2)

The collection of Weil divisors in
\operatorname{Cl}(\mathcal{X})
linearly equivalent in
\operatorname{Cl}(\mathcal{X})_{\mathbb{Q}}
to divisors of the form
\sum_{\rho\in\Sigma(1)}a_{\rho}D_{\rho}
with
-1<a_{\rho}\leq 0
.

(3)

Elements whose image in
\operatorname{Cl}(\mathcal{X})_{\mathbb{Q}}
is a lattice point of the zonotope
Z_{\mathcal{X}}
.

Remark 2.25
.

The closure of
Z_{\mathcal{X}}
is a lattice polytope with respect to the lattice defined via the image of
\mathbb{Z}^{\Sigma(1)}
under the above map. It should be emphasised here that when we talk about lattice points inside the zonotope
Z_{\mathcal{X}}
, we always refer to this natural lattice.

Using the coarse moduli space map
\mathcal{X}\rightarrow X
for the underlying toric variety
X
, we can study the pushforward to the Bondal-Thomsen collection. We keep the formulation in terms of general stacky fans, but will really only consider the toric DM stacks
\mathcal{X}_{\Sigma}
we defined before.

Proposition 2.26
.

If
\Sigma
is simplicial and
\pi:\mathcal{X}_{\Sigma,\beta}\rightarrow X_{\Sigma}
is the natural coarse moduli space map, then for all
\theta\in M_{\mathbb{R}}

\pi_{\ast}\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}_{\Sigma,\beta}}(-d(\theta))=\mathcal{O}_{X_{\Sigma}}(-d(\theta)).

Using the Bondal-Thomsen collection, the authors of
[
1
]
construct a tilting bundle on the
Cox category
, which we will now introduce. Consider the collection
(\mathcal{X}_{i})_{i=1}^{r}
of smooth toric DM stacks associated to the chambers of the secondary fan
\Sigma_{GKZ}
of
X
. There exists (see
\lx@sectionsign 3
of
[
1
]
) a smooth toric DM stack
\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}
equipped with
r
proper, birational morphisms
\pi_{i}:\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}_{i}
such that
\pi^{\ast}_{i}:\operatorname{D}^{\operatorname{b}}(\operatorname{coh}\mathcal{X}_{i})\rightarrow\operatorname{D}^{\operatorname{b}}(\operatorname{coh}\widetilde{\mathcal{X}})
is fully faithful for all
i
.

Definition 2.27
.

We define the Cox category of
X
to be
\operatorname{D}_{Cox}(X):=\langle\pi_{1}^{\ast}(\mathcal{X}_{1}),\dots,\pi^{\ast}_{r}(\mathcal{X}_{r})\rangle
.

This definition turns out to be independent of choice of
\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}
(see
[
1
, Corollary 3.9]
). Observe now that the toric variety
X
we started with admits a natural map (via (
2.1
))
\operatorname{Cl}(X)\rightarrow\operatorname{Cl}(\mathcal{X}_{i})
for all
i
, which is an isomorphism if and only if
X
and
\mathcal{X}_{i}
share the same rays, and otherwise is surjective. As we will now elaborate upon, the Cox category has a tilting bundle extrapolated from the data of the Bondal-Thomsen collection. The Bondal-Thomsen collection
\Theta_{\mathcal{X}}
surjects onto
\Theta_{\mathcal{X}_{i}}
via this map. Hence, any
-d\in\Theta_{\mathcal{X}}
has a natural corresponding element in
\Theta_{\mathcal{X}_{i}}
, and via abuse of notation we permit ourselves to write
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}_{i}}(-d)
for an element
-d\in\Theta_{\mathcal{X}}
. To ease notation a little further, write
\Theta:=\Theta_{\mathcal{X}}
. For each
-d\in\Theta
we will now define an element
\mathcal{O}_{Cox}(-d)
in the Cox category
\operatorname{D}_{Cox}(X)
. We could in principal pull back the line bundle
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}_{j}}(-d)
for any of the chambers, so the question is which chamber is the ”best” choice. The answer uses the geometry of the secondary fan.

Definition 2.28
.

Pick
-d\in\Theta
. Note that
d
is an effective degree, and so its image in
\operatorname{Cl}(X)_{\mathbb{R}}
lies in some maximal cone
\Gamma_{i}
of the secondary fan
\Sigma_{GKZ}
. This maximal chamber
\Gamma_{i}
corresponds to some
\mathcal{X}_{i}
and so we define
\mathcal{O}_{Cox}(-d):=\pi_{i}^{\ast}\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}_{i}}(-d)
.

At first instinct, one may doubt that this is well-defined: the image of
-d
could lie in the intersection of two (or more) chambers
\Gamma_{j}\cap\Gamma_{i}
. However, Ballard et al. prove
[
1
, Proposition 4.2]
that in this case,
\pi_{i}^{\ast}\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}_{i}}(-d)=\pi^{\ast}_{j}\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}_{j}}(-d)
.

An important computation tool for the Bondal-Thomsen collection is the
\Theta
-Transform Lemma:

Lemma 2.29

(Lemma 1.6 in
[
1
]
)
.

Let
-d\in\Theta
be an element whose image in
\Sigma_{GKZ}
lies in the chamber
\Gamma_{i}
corresponding to the toric DM stack
\mathcal{X}_{i}
. For any

,
\Phi_{ij}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}_{i}}(-d))=\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}_{j}}(-d)
, where
\Phi_{ij}:\operatorname{D}^{\operatorname{b}}(\operatorname{coh}\mathcal{X}_{i})\rightarrow\operatorname{D}^{\operatorname{b}}(\operatorname{coh}\mathcal{X}_{j})
is the Fourier-Mukai transform induced by the graph of the birational map
\mathcal{X}_{i}\dashrightarrow\mathcal{X}_{j}
.

Using the
\Theta
-Transform Lemma and adjunction, one obtains the following computation.

Corollary 2.30
.

Let
-d,-d^{\prime}\in\Theta
. Then

\operatorname{RHom}(\mathcal{O}_{Cox}(-d),\mathcal{O}_{Cox}(-d^{\prime}))\cong\operatorname{RHom}_{\mathcal{X}_{i}}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}_{i}}(-d),\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}_{i}}(-d^{\prime}))\cong H^{\bullet}(\mathcal{X}_{i},\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}_{i}}(d-d^{\prime})).

Ballard et al. prove the following stacky version of Demazure vanishing, allowing us to deduce that higher cohomologies vanish.

Theorem 2.31

(Theorem 2.11 in
[
1
]
)
.

If
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{X}_{\Sigma,\beta}
is a smooth toric DM stack,
X_{\Sigma}
is semi-projective, and

is nef
\mathbb{Q}
-Cartier on

, then
H^{p}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}}(\beta^{\ast}\lfloor D\rfloor)=0
for all

, where
\lfloor\sum r_{\rho}D_{\rho}\rfloor=\sum\lfloor r_{\rho}\rfloor D_{\rho}
.

Finally, using the combinatorial nature of our setup, we establish the following.

Corollary 2.32

(Corollary 4.25 in
[
1
]
)
.

Let
-d,-d^{\prime}\in\Theta
. For any
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in M_{\mathbb{R}}
such that
d=d(\theta),d^{\prime}=d(\theta^{\prime})
, we have

\operatorname{RHom}(\mathcal{O}_{Cox}(-d),\mathcal{O}_{Cox}(-d^{\prime}))=k\langle P_{d}\cap(M-\theta^{\prime})\rangle=k\langle Q_{d-d^{\prime}}\cap M\rangle,

concentrated in degree 0. Here,
P_{d}=\{m\in M_{\mathbb{R}}|\langle m,u_{\rho}\rangle\geq\lfloor-\langle\theta,u_{\rho}\rangle\rfloor\quad\forall\rho\in\Sigma(1)\}
and
Q_{d-d^{\prime}}=\{m\in M_{\mathbb{R}}\mid\langle m,u_{\rho}\rangle\geq-\lceil\langle\theta,u_{\rho}\rangle\rceil+\lceil\langle\theta^{\prime},u_{\rho}\rangle\rceil\quad\forall\rho\in\Sigma(1)\}

3.
Studying conic modules via the Bondal-Thomsen collection

In this section, we will elaborate on the link between conic modules and the Bondal-Thomsen collection we mentioned earlier and from that deduce how the geometry of the secondary fan can be used to study (sums of) conic modules. Let us start by precisely stating how conic modules are recovered by the Bondal-Thomsen collection. Fix a cone
\sigma\subset N_{\mathbb{R}}
together with a simplicial fan
\Sigma
such that
\Sigma(1)=\sigma(1)
(in particular,
|\Sigma|=\sigma
).

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
v,w\in M_{\mathbb{R}}
and consider the conic modules
A_{v},A_{w}
as well as the elements of the Bondal-Thomsen collection
-d(v),-d(w)
. Then

\operatorname{Hom}(A_{v},A_{w})\cong\operatorname{Hom}(\mathcal{O}_{Cox}(-d(v)),\mathcal{O}_{Cox}(-d(w))).

For any subset
S\subset\Theta
, consider a minimal corresponding subset
S^{\prime}\subset M_{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\{-d(s)\mid s\in S^{\prime}\}=S
. Then there is an isomorphism of endomorphism algebras

\operatorname{End}(\bigoplus_{s\in S^{\prime}}A_{s})\cong\operatorname{End}(\bigoplus_{\theta\in S}\mathcal{O}_{Cox}(-d(\theta))).

Proof.

We use Proposition
2.7
and Corollary
2.30
to compute the two Hom-sets:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Hom}(A_{v},A_{w})=\langle x^{m}\mid m+(\sigma^{\vee}+v)\cap M\subset(\sigma^{\vee}+w)\rangle;

\displaystyle\operatorname{Hom}(\mathcal{O}_{Cox}(-d(v)),\mathcal{O}_{Cox}(-d(w)))=\langle x^{m}\mid m\in Q_{d(v)-d(w)}\rangle

First, suppose we are given an
m
such that
m+(\sigma^{\vee}+v)\cap M\subset(\sigma^{\vee}+w)
.

For any
m^{\prime}\in(\sigma^{\vee}+v)\cap M
, we have
m+m^{\prime}-w\in\sigma^{\vee}
, i.e.

\langle m,u_{\rho}\rangle\geq\langle w,u_{\rho}\rangle-\langle m^{\prime},u_{\rho}\rangle,\quad\forall\rho\in\sigma(1).

The LHS is integer, and so we have, for all
\rho\in\sigma(1)
and any
m^{\prime}\in(\sigma^{\vee}+v)\cap M
,

\displaystyle\langle m,u_{\rho}\rangle\geq\lceil\langle w,u_{\rho}\rangle-\langle m^{\prime},u_{\rho}\rangle\rceil

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\ \geq\lceil\langle w,u_{\rho}\rangle\rceil-\lceil\langle m^{\prime},u_{\rho}\rangle\rceil.

As
m^{\prime}\in\sigma^{\vee}+v
, we have
\langle m^{\prime},u_{\rho}\rangle\geq\langle v,u_{\rho}\rangle
. The shifted cone
\sigma^{\vee}+v
contains points that attain this bound on its boundary and the hyperplane
\{x\mid\langle x,u_{\rho}\rangle=\lceil\langle v,u_{\rho}\rangle\rceil\}
intersects
\sigma^{\vee}+v
, as it lies on the correct side of the supporting hyperplane. Thus there is a lattice point
m^{\prime\prime}\in\sigma^{\vee}+v
where
\langle m^{\prime\prime},u_{\rho}\rangle=\lceil\langle v,u_{\rho}\rangle\rceil
. Consequently,
\langle m,u_{\rho}\rangle\geq\lceil\langle w,u_{\rho}\rangle\rceil-\lceil\langle v,u_{\rho}\rangle\rceil
, i.e.
m\in Q_{d(v)-d(w)}
.

Conversely, suppose
m\in Q_{d(v)-d(w)}\cap M
. Let
m_{2}\in(\sigma^{\vee}+v)\cap M
. For any
\rho\in\sigma(1)
,
\langle m_{2},u_{\rho}\rangle\geq\langle v,u_{\rho}\rangle
and as the LHS is integer, we deduce
\langle m_{2},u_{\rho}\rangle\geq\lceil\langle v,u_{\rho}\rangle\rceil
.

Then

\displaystyle\langle m+m_{2}-w,u_{\rho}\rangle=\langle m,u_{\rho}\rangle+\langle m_{2},u_{\rho}\rangle-\langle w,u_{\rho}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq-\lceil\langle v,u_{\rho}\rangle\rceil+\lceil\langle w,u_{\rho}\rangle\rceil+\langle m_{2},u_{\rho}\rangle-\langle w,u_{\rho}\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(\lceil\langle w,u_{\rho}\rangle\rceil-\langle w,u_{\rho}\rangle\right)+\left(\langle m_{2},u_{\rho}\rangle-\lceil\langle v,u_{\rho}\rangle\rceil\right)

\displaystyle\geq 0+0=0.\;

Hence, for all
\rho\in\sigma(1)
and
m_{2}\in(\sigma^{\vee}+v)\cap M
, we have
\langle m+m_{2}-w,u_{\rho}\rangle\geq 0
, i.e.
m+m_{2}\in\sigma^{\vee}+w
. So
m\in Q_{d(v)-d(w)}
implies
m+(\sigma^{\vee}+v)\cap M\subset\sigma^{\vee}+w
.

The second part of the statement follows directly from the first.
∎

3.1.
Substitution of conic modules and NCCRs

Fix a cone
\sigma
together with a simplicial subdivision
\Sigma
such that
\sigma(1)=\Sigma(1)
and let
s
be the number of isomorphism classes of conic modules and
r
the number of lattice points in the zonotope

of the smooth toric DM stack
\mathcal{X}_{\Sigma}

A_{0},\dots,A_{s-1}

\Delta_{i}
of chamber of constancy for each isomorphism class. For

be the complex
K_{\Delta_{i}}^{\bullet}
.

Definition 3.2
.

If
A_{j}
appears in a complex of conic modules

substitution by
j

S(j,K^{\bullet})
, to be the complex obtained by splicing all occurences of
A_{j}
with
K_{j}^{\bullet}
. For a set
\mathcal{K}
of complexes of conic modules, define the
substitution by
j

\mathcal{S}(j,\mathcal{K})
of the set
\mathcal{K}
by
j
to be the set of complexes
\{S(j,K)\mid K\in\mathcal{K}\}
. Call a non-empty subset
I\subseteq\{0,\dots,s-1\}

lockable
if there is a finite sequence of successive substitutions by elements in
I^{c}
that, applied to the set
\{K_{i}^{\bullet}\mid i\in I\}
, yields a set
\mathcal{K}^{\dagger}_{I}
of complexes of conic modules, such that all conic modules appearing in any complex
K\in\mathcal{K}^{\dagger}_{I}
are of the form
A_{k},k\in I
.

Example 3.3
.

In Example
2.17

\{0,1\}
is lockable as substituting
K_{2}
for every appearance of
A_{2}
in
K_{0},K_{1}

\mathcal{K}_{\{0,1\}}^{\dagger}=\{A_{0}\rightarrow A_{1}^{\oplus 2}\oplus A_{0}^{\oplus 4}\rightarrow A_{0}^{\oplus 4}\oplus A_{1}^{\oplus 2}\rightarrow A_{0},\quad A_{1}\rightarrow A_{0}^{\oplus 2}\rightarrow A_{0}^{\oplus 2}\rightarrow A_{1}\}.

Similarly for the set

The above example generalises quite straightforwardly.

Example 3.4

If there is a complex such that

coming from a lockable subset, every graded simple
\operatorname{End}_{R}(\mathbb{A})

\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(\mathbb{A},-)

\dim\sigma

A lockable set

have length

.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
\sigma
be a cone with associated toric algebra
R
and collection of conic modules
\{A_{i}\}_{i\in S}
for a set of indices
S
. For a subset
I
of

\mathbb{B}=\bigoplus_{i\in I}A_{i}
. Then the endomorphism algebra
\Lambda^{\prime}=\operatorname{End}_{R}(\mathbb{B})
is an NCR of

if and only if

is lockable. Furthermore,
\Lambda^{\prime}
is an NCCR of

if and only if
I
is incredulous.

We split the proof into two parts (Propositions
3.15
and
3.19

[
9

Consider a direct sum of conic modules
\mathbb{B}=\bigoplus_{i\in I}A_{i}
. Then we have the following result.

Proposition 3.7

(Proposition 6.3
[
9
]
)
.

For any

(1)

The
\operatorname{End}_{R}(\mathbb{B})
-module

P_{i}^{\prime}:=\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(\mathbb{B},A_{i})

is a graded indecomposable projective module and every graded indecomposable projective
\operatorname{End}_{R}(\mathbb{B})
-module is isomorphic to a module of this form.

(2)

The projective module
P_{i}^{\prime}
has a unique maximal graded submodule. Every graded simple
\operatorname{End}_{R}(\mathbb{B})
-module is isomorphic to a quotient of some
P_{i}^{\prime}
by this maximal submodule. Denote by

the unique graded simple quotient of
P_{i}^{\prime}
.

Remark 3.8
.

In their paper, Faber, Muller and Smith formulate this result for complete direct sums of conic modules but the proof follows through as is for incomplete direct sums of conic modules. In fact, as remarked by the authors, this is essentially an adaptation of
[

The unique maximal graded submodule of
P_{i}^{\prime}
is the
radical
of
P_{i}^{\prime}
. A homomorphism from a conic module

to another conic module
A_{i}
is said to be

if it is not an isomorphism. In more generality, a homomorphism from a direct sum
\mathbb{B}
of conic modules to another direct sum of conic modules

can be represented by a matrix
[\phi_{i,j}]
and we say this homomorphism is radical if all of its components are. Thus, elements of

can be represented as row vectors with entries in
\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(A_{j},A_{i})
with the radical consisting of those maps with entries in the respective radical of
\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(A_{j},A_{i})
. Denote the radical by
\operatorname{Rad}(\mathbb{B},A_{i})
.

Lemma 3.9
.

For any
i,j\in I
there is a graded

-vector space isomorphism

\operatorname{Hom}_{\operatorname{End}_{R}(\mathbb{B})}(P_{i}^{\prime},S_{j}^{\prime})\simeq\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(A_{i},A_{j})/\operatorname{Rad}(A_{i},A_{j}).

These vector spaces are of dimension 0 or 1, depending on whether

.

Proof.

This is the version of
[

for incomplete sums of conic modules and the proof applies verbatim.
∎

We now begin the proof of Theorem

, is uniquely determined.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
I
be a lockable set and let
i\in I

j\not\in I

and resulting in a complex without appearance of conic modules

Proof.

Consider an appearance of
A_{j}
in
K_{i}^{\bullet}
such that

. Then
A_{j}
remains in the complex at the same degree unless one substitutes by

We begin by establishing exactness and resolution properties of the sequences
\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(\mathbb{B},K_{i}^{\bullet})

Lemma 3.11

If
j\not\in I
, then
\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(\mathbb{B},K_{j}^{\bullet})
is an exact complex that can be extended to the right by zero and remains exact. Hence,
\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{B},K_{j}^{\bullet})
is an exact complex ending in
\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(\mathbb{B},A_{j})=P_{j}^{\prime}\rightarrow 0
and can thus be seen as a resolution of
P_{j}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Note that
\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(\mathbb{B},K_{j})=\bigoplus_{i\in I}\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(A_{i},K_{j})
and so by the Acyclicity Lemma
2.16
it is a direct sum of exact complexes, hence exact. It suffices to find the cokernel of the last map to show we can extend the complex by 0 to the right. But the image of the incoming map is just
\operatorname{Rad}(\mathbb{B},A_{j})
, which is precisely
\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{B},A_{j})
.
∎

Lemma 3.12
.

For
i\in I
,
K_{i}^{\dagger}
is an exact complex outside of degree 0. Extending to the right by
[S_{i}^{\prime}\rightarrow 0]
makes
K_{i}^{\dagger}
exact and thus a projective resolution of
S_{i}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

By the Acyclicity Lemma
2.16
, the complex
\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(\mathbb{B},K_{i}^{\bullet})=\bigoplus_{i^{\prime}\in I}\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(A_{i^{\prime}},K_{i}^{\bullet})
is exact apart from perhaps the penultimate spot, where the homology is one-dimensional in homological degree 0, coming from the direct summand
\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(A_{i},K_{i}^{\bullet})
. Note that the kernel of the projection map
P_{i}^{\prime}\rightarrow S_{i}^{\prime}
is the radical of
P_{i}^{\prime}
and thus coincides with the homology appearing in degree 0. Thus extending by
S_{i}^{\prime}
gives an exact complex
\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(\mathbb{B},K_{i}^{\bullet})\rightarrow S_{i}^{\prime}\rightarrow 0
. As the complexes
\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(\mathbb{B},K_{j}^{\bullet})\rightarrow 0
for
j\not\in I
are exact, substitution by
K_{j}^{\bullet}
in
K_{i}^{\bullet}
corresponds to splicing the exact complexes resolving
P_{j}^{\prime}
by Lemma
3.11
. The substitution process does this until no non-projective direct summand remains in any degree
>0
and thus we obtain a projective resolution of
S_{i}^{\prime}
.
∎

The following Lemma helps us compute the Ext groups between the graded simples, which is a useful tool in establishing minimal projective resolutions. Essentially, this is the version of

, Proposition 6.6]
for incomplete direct sums of conic modules and the proof runs in parallel.

Lemma 3.13
.

For any pair
i,j\in I
we have

\dim(\operatorname{Ext}^{l}_{\operatorname{End}_{R}(\mathbb{B})}(S^{\prime}_{i},S^{\prime}_{j}))=\#\text{occurences of }A_{j}\text{ in }K_{i}^{\dagger,l}.

Proof.

This is essentially a variation of
[
9
, Proposition 6.6]
for incomplete direct sums of conic modules and the proof is analogous.
∎

We immediately see that the complexes
K_{i}^{\dagger,\bullet}
generate minimal projective resolutions of
S_{i}^{\prime}
.

Corollary 3.14
.

For
i\in I
,
K_{i}^{\dagger}
is a minimal projective resolution of
S_{i}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Let
A_{k}
be a conic module appearing in the highest degree of
K_{i}^{\dagger,\bullet}
. Denote by
d
the length of
\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(\mathbb{B},K_{i}^{\dagger,\bullet})
. Then

\operatorname{Ext}^{d}_{\operatorname{End}_{R}(\mathbb{B})}(S_{i}^{\prime},S_{k}^{\prime})\neq 0.

Hence,
S_{i}^{\prime}
cannot have a projective resolution of length shorter than
d
, and
\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(\mathbb{B},K_{i}^{\dagger,\bullet})
is indeed a minimal projective resolution.
∎

With this, we can prove the first direction of Theorem
3.6
.

Proposition 3.15
.

Let
I
be a lockable set and let
\mathbb{B}=\bigoplus_{i\in I}A_{i}
. Then
\Lambda^{\prime}=\operatorname{End}_{R}(\mathbb{B})
is an NCR. If
I
is incredulous,
\Lambda^{\prime}
is an NCCR.

Proof.

The complexes
\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(\mathbb{B},K_{i}^{\dagger})
give projective resolutions of any graded simples
S_{i}^{\prime}
(
i\in I
) of
\operatorname{End}_{R}(\mathbb{B})
and so as the set is lockable we have
\operatorname{pd}_{\Lambda^{\prime}}(S_{i}^{\prime})=\operatorname{len}(K_{i}^{\dagger})<\infty
. There is a finite collection of these and so
we have
\operatorname{gl}\dim\Lambda^{\prime}<\infty
. This implies that
\Lambda^{\prime}
is an NCR. Furthermore, if the set is incredulous, the projective dimension of all simples agrees with the Krull dimension of
R
and thus
\Lambda^{\prime}\in\operatorname{CM}R
, making it an NCCR of
R
.
∎

We now proceed to prove the second half of Theorem
3.6
.

Lemma 3.16
.

For
\dim\sigma\geq 2
, there is no complex
K_{i}
of length 1 or length 2 (i.e. of the form
\bigoplus_{\tau}\overset{\circ}{A}_{\tau}\rightarrow A_{i}
).
Hence there does not exist a collection of conic modules
\{A_{l}\}_{l\in T}
for some subset
T\subset S
such that each
K_{l}^{\bullet}
is of length 0 and there is a loop of the modules
A_{l}
.

Proof.

Fix a chamber of constancy
\Delta
. We will first show that there exists some open cell of codimension
\geq 2
.
\Delta
is defined via the set of inequalities
d_{\rho}-1<\langle x,u_{\rho}\rangle\leq d_{\rho}
where
d_{\rho}=\lceil\langle v,u_{\rho}\rangle\rceil
for some element
v\in\Delta
. An open cell
\tau
is the locus such that subset of these inequalities are strict. Thus, we need to show that there always needs to be some inequality that can be made strict, i.e. that the set of (in)-equalities given by

\begin{cases}d_{\rho}-1<\langle x,u_{\rho}\rangle<d_{\rho},\text{ for }\rho\neq\rho_{1}\\
d_{\rho_{1}}=\langle x,u_{\rho_{1}}\rangle\end{cases}

has a solution for some
\rho_{1}\in\sigma(1)
. Note that
\sigma^{\vee}
is full dimensional, and so there exists a point
p\in\operatorname{Int(}\sigma^{\vee})
, i.e. an element
p\in M_{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\langle p,u_{\rho}\rangle>0
for all
\rho\in\sigma(1)
. Pick
v\in\Delta
. If any inequality is strict, we have the existence of an open cell. So suppose not, i.e.
d_{\rho}-1<\langle v,u_{\rho}\rangle<d_{\rho}
for all
\rho\in\sigma(1)
.
Using the intermediate value theorem we see that there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
d_{\rho}-1<\langle v+\epsilon p,u_{\rho}\rangle\leq d_{\rho}
but with some strict equalities.
Thus, there is always some open cell of a chamber of constancy
\Delta
. To show there has to be an open cell of codimension
\geq 2
, suppose all open cells have codimension 1 and one of them,
\tau_{1}
, is given by
\langle x,u_{\rho_{1}}\rangle=1
for
\rho_{1}\in\sigma(1)
.
Consider the space
L_{\rho_{1}}=\{y\in M_{\mathbb{R}}\mid\langle y,u_{\rho_{1}}\rangle=0\}
. Since
u_{\rho_{1}}
is a minimal ray generator of
\sigma
,
\sigma^{\vee}\cap L_{u_{\rho_{1}}}
is a codimension 1 facet of
\sigma^{\vee}
with inward pointing normal
u_{\rho_{1}}
. Then there exists an element in the interior of that facet, i.e.
q\in\sigma^{\vee}\cap L_{\rho_{1}}
with
\langle p,u_{\rho}\rangle>0
for
\rho\neq\rho_{1}
and
\langle p,u_{\rho_{1}}\rangle=0
. Let
w\in\tau_{1}
. Then for
\varepsilon>0
,
\langle w+\varepsilon q,u_{\rho}\rangle>\langle w,u_{\rho}\rangle
if
\rho\neq\rho_{1}
and
\langle w+\varepsilon q,u_{\rho_{1}}\rangle=\langle w,u_{\rho_{1}}\rangle
. Thus, another application of the intermediate value theorem gives us a value for
\varepsilon
such that
d_{\rho}-1<\langle w+\varepsilon q,u_{\rho}\rangle\leq d_{\rho}
with equality for
\rho=\rho_{1}
and a non-empty collection of further
\rho\in\sigma(1)
, and as any two
u_{\rho}
are linearly independent, the open cell needs to at least have codimension 2.
∎

To prove that finite global dimension implies lockability, we use the language of
\mathcal{I}
-minimal resolutions, and refer the reader to
[
7
]
for a more detailed approach.

Definition 3.17
.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be a full subcategory of
\operatorname{CM}R
. Fix an exact sequence
0\rightarrow Z\rightarrow Y\xrightarrow{X}
in
\operatorname{CM}R
. We say
f
is a
right
\mathcal{I}
-approximation
if
Y\in\mathcal{I}
and if

\mathcal{I}(-,Y)\xrightarrow{\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(f)}\mathcal{I}(-,X)\rightarrow 0

is exact. A right
\mathcal{I}
-approximation is said to be
minimal
if there is no non-zero direct summand of
Y
mapped to zero under
f
. An exact sequence
F:\dots\xrightarrow{f_{2}}Y_{1}\xrightarrow{f_{1}}Y_{0}\xrightarrow{f_{0}}X
is called an
\mathcal{I}
-resolution of
X
if each
Y_{i}\in\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{I}(-,F)
is exact on
\mathcal{I}
. Such a resolution is
minimal
if each
f_{i}
is. In the following, we may choose to drop the word
right
from the notation.

Proposition 3.18
.

If
\Lambda^{\prime}=\operatorname{End}_{R}(\mathbb{B})
has finite global dimension, then
I
is lockable.

Proof.

Consider the full subcategory
\mathcal{I}=\operatorname{add}(I)
of
\operatorname{CM}R
. By
[
7
, Lemma 2.7]
,
\mathcal{I}
is contravariantly finite, i.e. each
X\in\operatorname{CM}R
has an
\mathcal{I}
-minimal resolution. Note that for
i\in I
, the minimal resolution is simply given by
0\rightarrow A_{i}\xrightarrow{id}A_{i}\rightarrow 0
. Since the boundary maps in the complexes
K_{j}^{\bullet}
are direct sums of signed inclusions, splicing for each
A_{l}
appearing in
K_{j}^{\bullet}
the relevant
\mathcal{I}
-minimal resolution itself results in a
\mathcal{I}
-minimal resolution of
A_{j}
(as composition of a right minimal approximation with an inclusion gives a right minimal approximation). If
j\not\in I
, applying
\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{B},-)
to this
\mathcal{I}
-minimal resolution of
A_{j}
gives (see
[
7
, Lemma 2.10]
) a minimal projective resolution of
P^{\prime}_{j}
. By finite global dimension of
\Lambda^{\prime}
, this is a finite complex.

Suppose now that
I
was not lockable. Then there is a sequence
A_{j_{1}},\dots,A_{j_{m}},A_{j_{m+1}}=A_{j_{1}}
with
A_{j_{a}}
appearing in
K^{\bullet}_{j_{a+1}}
and
j_{a}\not\in I
for
1\leq a\leq m
. Then the
\mathcal{I}
-minimal resolution of
A_{j_{a+1}}
contains the
\mathcal{I}
-minimal resolution of
A_{j_{a}}
for
1\leq a\leq m
- which means that the
\mathcal{I}
-minimal resolution of
A_{j_{1}}
contains itself. Since Lemma
3.16
excludes the loop of being in pure degree 0, this is a contradiction to the finiteness of the resolution of
P^{\prime}_{j}
.

∎

Proposition 3.19
.

Let
I
be a subset of
S
and
\mathbb{B}=\bigoplus_{i\in I}A_{i}
. If
\Lambda^{\prime}=\operatorname{End}_{R}(\mathbb{B})
is an NCR, then
I
is lockable. If
\Lambda^{\prime}
is an NCCR, then
I
is incredulous.

Proof.

This is immediate from Proposition
3.18
, as being an NCR implies that the global dimension of
\Lambda^{\prime}
is finite. Hence the set is lockable. The conclusion on being incredulous follows.
∎

Theorem
3.6
.

The Theorem is a combination of Propositions
3.15
and
3.19
.
∎

Observe the following corollary of Theorem
3.6
.

Corollary 3.20
.

Let
J
be an incredulous set. Then if
I\subseteq J
is lockable, it is incredulous.

Proof.

Consider the two endomorphism algebras
\Lambda_{I}=\operatorname{End}_{R}(\bigoplus_{i\in I}A_{i})
and
\Lambda_{J}=\operatorname{End}_{R}(\bigoplus_{j\in J}A_{j})
. Then
\Lambda_{J}=\Lambda_{I}\oplus\Lambda_{L}
for some algebra
\Lambda_{L}
. Note
\Lambda_{J}\in\operatorname{CM}R\Leftrightarrow\operatorname{Ext}^{i}(\Lambda_{J},R)=0
for all
i>0
. But
\operatorname{Ext}^{i}(\Lambda_{J},R)=\operatorname{Ext}^{i}(\Lambda_{I},R)\oplus\operatorname{Ext}^{i}(\Lambda_{L},R)
.

Thus
\operatorname{Ext}^{i}(\Lambda_{I},R)=0
for

and so
\Lambda_{i}\in\operatorname{CM}R
. Since
I
is lockable, by Theorem
3.6
,
\Lambda_{I}
is an NCR and so
\Lambda_{I}\in\operatorname{CM}R
shows that

is an NCCR. Applying the Theorem again gives that

is an incredulous set.
∎

Knowing that looking for NCCRs as endomorphism algebras of incomplete sums of conic modules is the same as looking for incredulous sets, we now focus on computing the complexes
K_{i}^{\bullet}
so that we can identify such sets.

4.
Computing complexes of conic modules

To compute the complexes
K_{i}^{\bullet}
associated to conic modules
A_{i}
, we are going to use the geometry of the secondary fan, demonstrating the computational advantage of this point of view.

4.1.
Complexes of conic modules and paths in the secondary fan

To study when the method of substitution works, we need to understand first and foremost which conic modules appear in a given complex
K_{v}^{\bullet}
. In the following, we investigate this question using the combinatorics and geometry of the secondary fan.
Recall that we fixed a cone
\sigma
with simplicialisation
\Sigma
,
r
the number of lattice points in
Z_{\mathcal{X}}
and
s
the number of isomorphism classes of conic modules.

Lemma 4.1
.

With
\sigma,\Sigma,r,s
as above,

s=r\cdot|\operatorname{Tors}(\operatorname{Cl}(\mathcal{X}_{\Sigma}))|=|\Theta_{\mathcal{X}}|.

Proof.

For each isomorphism class of conic modules, pick a representative
A_{v_{i}}
,
0\leq i\leq s-1
such that
v_{i}
lies in the fundamental domain of
M_{\mathbb{R}}/M
(which we can usually think of as
(-1,0]^{\dim\sigma}
).
Given such an element
v_{i}
, by definition
-d(v_{i})
lies in the Bondal-Thomsen collection
\Theta_{\mathcal{X}_{\Sigma}}
. Two vectors
v,w\in M_{\mathbb{R}}
give the same isomorphism class of conic module
A_{v}
if and only if
-d(v)\sim-d(w)
. Then, however,
-d(v)
and
-d(w)
designate the same element in the Bondal-Thomsen collection and so
s
is simply the size of the Bondal-Thomsen collection. Using Proposition
2.24
, this is equivalent to the subset of divisors in
\operatorname{Cl}(\mathcal{X}_{\Sigma})
whose image in
\operatorname{Cl}(\mathcal{X}_{\Sigma})_{\mathbb{Q}}
are lattice points of
Z_{\mathcal{X}_{\Sigma}}
.
The proof of the lemma thus reduces to showing that each lattice point has exactly
|\operatorname{Tors}(\operatorname{Cl}(\mathcal{X}_{\Sigma}))|
corresponding elements in
\operatorname{Cl}(\mathcal{X}_{\Sigma})
mapping to it.

The existence of some divisor mapping to any given lattice point is evident by recalling that the lattice is defined via the image of
\bigoplus_{\rho\in\Sigma(1)}\mathbb{Z}\cdot D_{\rho}=:\mathbb{Z}^{\Sigma(1)}
.

Suppose that two divisors
D_{1},D_{2}\in\operatorname{Cl}(\mathcal{X}_{\Sigma})
map to the same lattice point. We aim to show this happens if and only if
D_{1}-D_{2}
is torsion. Let
D^{\prime}_{i}
be an element of
\mathbb{Z}^{\Sigma(1)}
mapping to
D_{i}
. Consider the exact sequence obtained by applying
-\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q}
to (
2.1
).

0\rightarrow M_{\mathbb{Q}}\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}^{\Sigma(1)}\rightarrow\operatorname{Cl}(\mathcal{X}_{\Sigma})_{\mathbb{Q}}\rightarrow 0.

Two divisors
D^{\prime}_{1},D^{\prime}_{2}
mapping to the same lattice point is equivalent to
D^{\prime}_{1}-D^{\prime}_{2}
being in the kernel of the map
\mathbb{Q}^{\Sigma(1)}\rightarrow\operatorname{Cl}(\mathcal{X}_{\Sigma})_{\mathbb{Q}}
. By exactness of the sequence, this happens if and only if
D^{\prime}_{1}-D^{\prime}_{2}=\sum_{\rho\in\Sigma(1)}\langle m_{q},u_{\rho}\rangle D_{\rho}
for some
m_{q}\in M_{\mathbb{Q}}
. But then there exists some
p\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
p\cdot m_{q}\in M
and
p(D^{\prime}_{1}-D^{\prime}_{2})=\sum_{\rho\in\Sigma(1)}\langle p\cdot m_{q},u_{\rho}\rangle D_{\rho}\in\ker(\mathbb{Z}^{\Sigma(1)}\rightarrow\operatorname{Cl}(\mathcal{X}_{\Sigma}))
, where the map comes from the original exact sequence (
2.1
). Hence,
p(D^{\prime}_{1}-D^{\prime}_{2})\sim 0
which happens precisely if
D_{1}-D_{2}
is torsion.
∎

For
\rho\in\Sigma(1)
, denote
f_{\sigma}(D_{\rho})=:\beta_{\rho}
. The collection
\{\beta_{\rho}\mid\rho\in\Sigma(1)\}
gives a set of ray generators of
\Sigma_{GKZ}
, which may appear with multiplicity (as the fan is a generalised fan). The maximal cones of the fan
\Sigma_{GKZ}
have dimension
|\sigma(1)|-\dim\sigma
.

Using the same considerations as in the above proof of Lemma
4.1
, we also obtain the following result, which will simplify the combinatorics of conic modules later on.

Lemma 4.2
.

Consider
v,v^{\prime}\in M_{\mathbb{R}}
such that
-d(v)-(-d(v^{\prime}))
is a torsion divisor. This happens if and only if
f_{\sigma}(d(v^{\prime})-d(v))=0
. Then
\Delta_{v^{\prime}}=\Delta_{v}+m_{q}
for some
m_{q}\in M_{\mathbb{Q}}
. Furthermore,
m_{q}\in\frac{1}{|\operatorname{Tors}(\operatorname{Cl}(\mathcal{X}_{\Sigma}))|}M
.

Proof.

The first part of the lemma was shown above. Given that
f_{\sigma}(d(v^{\prime})-d(v))=0
, we note that, via the same exact sequence as before (obtained by tensoring (
2.1
) by
\mathbb{Q}
), we have
d(v^{\prime})-d(v)=\operatorname{div}(x^{m_{q}})
for some
m_{q}\in M_{\mathbb{Q}}
. Hence
d(v^{\prime})_{\rho}=d(v)_{\rho}+\langle m_{q},u_{\rho}\rangle
for all
\rho\in\sigma(1)=\Sigma(1)
. Furthermore, since both
d(v)_{\rho},d(v^{\prime})_{\rho}
are integer for all rays
\rho
, we have
\langle m_{q},u_{\rho}\rangle\in\mathbb{Z}
as well. But then the defining inequalities of
\Delta_{v}
are the same as the
\Delta_{v^{\prime}}
, shifted by the terms
\langle m_{q},u_{\rho}\rangle
. Hence
\Delta_{v^{\prime}}=\Delta_{v}+m_{q}
, as required.
For the last observation, let
k
be minimal such that
k\cdot m_{q}\in M
. Then
k
is minimal such that
k\cdot f_{\sigma}(d(v^{\prime})-d(v))=0
. Thus,
k
divides
|\operatorname{Tors}(\operatorname{Cl}(\mathcal{X}_{\Sigma}))|
.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\sigma
be a cone and let
v,w\in M_{\mathbb{R}}
.
The isomorphism class of the conic module
A_{w}
appears in
K_{v}^{\bullet}
if and only if there is some subset
J\subset\sigma(1)
such that
-d(w)\sim-d(v)-\sum_{\rho\in J}D_{\rho}
and there is a non-empty set of solutions
x\in M_{\mathbb{R}}
to the system of (in)equalities.

\begin{cases}d(w)_{\rho}-1<\langle x,u_{\rho}\rangle<d(w)_{\rho}\text{ for }\rho\not\in J,\\
d(w)_{\rho}-1=\langle x,u_{\rho}\rangle\text{ for }\rho\in J.\end{cases}

(4.1)

Furthermore, if
A_{w}
appears in
K_{v}^{\bullet}
, then it does so in all degrees
l
such that there is a subset
J
with
\dim\operatorname{Span}(u_{\rho}|\rho\in J)=l
.

Proof.

For any ray
\rho\in\sigma(1)
, denote by
u_{\rho}
its primitive generator.

A_{w}
appears in
K_{v}^{\bullet}
if and only if it is isomorphic to
\overset{\circ}{A}_{\tau}
for some open cell
\tau
of the chamber of constancy
\Delta_{v}
. Note that
-d(v)_{\rho}=\lfloor\langle-v,u_{\rho}\rangle\rfloor=-\lceil\langle v,u_{\rho}\rangle\rceil
.

Any open cell of
\Delta_{v}
can be described (cf.
[
9
, Proposition 4.6]
) as set
\tau_{J}
of elements

x\in M_{\mathbb{R}}
for which there exists
J\subset\sigma(1)
and

\begin{cases}d(v)_{\rho}-1<\langle x,u_{\rho}\rangle<d(v)_{\rho}\text{ for }u_{\rho}\not\in J,\\
\langle x,u_{\rho}\rangle=d(v)_{\rho}\text{ for }u_{\rho}\in J.\end{cases}

(4.2)

Note that a given subset
J\subset\sigma(1)
gives an open cell if and only if this set is non-empty.

Pick a point
y\in\operatorname{Int}(\sigma^{\vee})
and a sufficiently small, positive
\varepsilon
. Consider the divisor
D=\sum_{\rho\in\sigma(1)}d_{J,\rho}D_{\rho}
, where
d_{J,\rho}=\begin{cases}d(v)_{\rho}\text{ for }\rho\not\in J,\\
d(v)_{\rho}+1\text{ for }\rho\in J.\end{cases}

If there is an
x\in M_{\mathbb{R}}
fulfilling the above restrictions, then for
z=x+\varepsilon\cdot y
we obtain

d_{J,\rho}-1<\langle z,u_{\rho}\rangle\leq d_{J,\rho}\text{ for }\rho\in\sigma(1).

(4.3)

Given a subset
J\subset\sigma(1)
, it thus defines an open cell of
\Delta_{v}
if and only if the inequalities (
4.3
) define a non-empty set. Furthermore, all open cells of
\Delta_{v}
arise this way. Observe now that given an element
w^{\prime}\in M_{\mathbb{R}}
that satisfies the inequalities (
4.3
), the inequalities define its chamber of constancy
\Delta_{w^{\prime}}
(indeed, we necessarily have
d_{J,\rho}=\lceil\langle w^{\prime},u_{\rho}\rangle\rceil
). Given an element
x\in\tau_{J}
, we thus note that
z=x+\varepsilon\cdot y
lies in
\Delta_{w^{\prime}}
. This small perturbation by
\varepsilon\cdot y
is precisely the perturbation that assigns to the open conic module
\overset{\circ}{A}_{x}
a conic module
A_{z}
, and thus
\overset{\circ}{A}_{x}=A_{z}=A_{w^{\prime}}
(see Proposition
2.9
).

So far, we have shown that conic modules appearing in
K_{v}^{\bullet}
satisfy the inequalities (
4.3
). Given an element
w\in M_{\mathbb{R}}
,
A_{w}
appears in
K_{v}^{\bullet}
if and only if
d(w)_{\rho}=d_{J,\rho}\forall\rho\in\sigma(1)
, for some subset
J\subset\sigma(1)
with non-empty open cells
\tau_{J}
. We consider conic modules up to isomorphism, thus the condition
d(w)_{\rho}=d_{J,\rho}
is precisely equivalent to
-d(w)\sim-d(v)-\sum_{\rho\in J}D_{\rho}
.

Observe that given such a
w\in M_{\mathbb{R}}
with
d(w)_{\rho}=d_{J,\rho}\forall\rho\in\sigma(1)
for some
J\subset\sigma(1)
, the open cell
\tau_{J}
is non-empty if and only if the system of (in)equalities (
4.2
) holds, which is equivalent to (
4.1
).

The degree of the appearance of
A_{w}
in
K_{v}^{\bullet}
is the codimension of the open cell, which corresponds to the number of linearly independent hyperplanes where the inequalities become strict, i.e. the dimension of
\operatorname{Span}(u_{\rho}\mid\rho\in J)
.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

On the computational side, to find all isomorphism classes
A_{w}
possibly appearing in
K_{v}^{\bullet}
, one checks all divisors of the form
-D=-d(v)-\sum_{\rho\in J}D_{\rho}
to establish when there is a non-empty set of solutions to

\begin{cases}d(v)_{\rho}-1<\langle x,u_{\rho}\rangle<d(v)_{\rho}\text{ for }\rho\not\in J,\\
d(v)_{\rho}=\langle x,u_{\rho}\rangle\text{ for }\rho\in J.\end{cases}

Once the set of divisors is established, we can pick a representative of each conic module and check which one the divisors are linearly equivalent to.

It is tempting to assume that
-d(w)\sim-d(v)-\sum_{\rho\in J}D_{\rho}
is a sufficient condition for
A_{w}
to appear in
K_{v}^{\bullet}
, as then one could also determine the codimension of the facet.
Unfortunately, this is false; the system of inequalities (
4.1
) may not have a solution. Indeed, consider Example
2.17
again, as we did after Proposition
3.1
.

Example 4.5
.

As before, we consider the fan

If

has no torsion, we can substitute the unique conic module
A_{v}
for
A_{P}
where
f_{\sigma}(-d(v))=P
and complete the complex with the appropriate morphisms, recovering the complexes
K_{v}^{\bullet}
.

Now we can define substitution, lockability and incredulousity as before.

Definition 4.21
.

For
P\in L_{\mathcal{X}}
, given a complex
K^{\bullet}
where the module
A_{P}
appears, we define
substitution by
P
,
S(P,K^{\bullet})
to be the complex obtained by substituting all occurrences of
A_{P}
with
K_{P}^{\bullet}
.
A non-empty set of lattice points
I\subset L_{\mathcal{X}}
is
lockable
if there is a finite sequence of successive substitutions by elements in
I^{c}
that, applied to the set
\{K_{P}^{\bullet}|P\in I\}
, yields a set
K_{I}^{\dagger}
of complexes of modules
A_{Q}
with
Q\in I
. Such a set is called incredulous if the complexes
K_{P}^{\dagger,\bullet}\in K_{I}^{\dagger}
are all of the same length, being
\dim\sigma
.

Whether a set of lattice points is lockable or incredulous can be determined just using the secondary fan and valid paths therein - any consideration of torsion disappears in this notion. This is of significant computational advantage and we will show that this ”torsion-free“ version of lockable and incredulous sets is in fact equivalent in its existence to the previous version. Explicitly, we obtain the following result.

Theorem 4.22
.

Let
\sigma
be a cone with a collection of conic modules
S
. Let
I\subseteq S
be an incredulous set of conic modules. Then the set of lattice points
I^{\prime}=\{f_{\sigma}(-d(v))\mid A_{v}\in I\}
is an incredulous set of lattice points. In particular, the following are equivalent.

(1)

There exists an incredulous set of conic modules.

(2)

There exists an incredulous set of lattice points in
Z_{\mathcal{X}}
.

(3)

There exists an NCCR of
\sigma
of the form
\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{A})
where
\mathbb{A}
is a direct sum of conic modules.

Proof.

The first and third item have been shown to be equivalent in Theorem
3.6
. Given an incredulous set
I
of lattice points in
Z_{\mathcal{X}}
, the collection of conic modules
I^{\prime}=\{A_{v}|f_{\sigma}(-d(v))\in I\}
is clearly itself an incredulous set of conic modules. Indeed, the substitutions performed on the complexes
K^{\bullet}_{P},P\in I
correspond to substitutions of conic modules not in
I^{\prime}
and thus performing these substitutions on the set of complexes
\{K_{v}^{\bullet}\mid A_{v}\in I^{\prime}\}
yields a collection of complexes made up of conic modules
A_{v}\in I^{\prime}
, which are further of length
\dim\sigma
.

It remains to show that the existence of an incredulous set
I
of conic modules gives an incredulous set of lattice points in the zonotope. We claim that the set
I^{\prime}=\{P\mid P=f_{\sigma}(-d(v))\text{ for some }A_{v}\in I\}
is an incredulous set of lattice points in
Z_{\mathcal{X}}
. It is clear that lockability of
I^{\prime}
is inherited from
I
.

Consider the cone
\sigma_{N^{\prime}}
which is the cone
\sigma
in the saturated sublattice
N^{\prime}
of
N
spanned by
u_{\rho},\rho\in\sigma(1)
. In that lattice, the cone
\sigma_{N^{\prime}}
admits no torsion but the collection
\{\beta_{\rho}\mid\rho\in\sigma^{\prime}(1)\}
coincides with the collection
\{\beta_{\rho}\mid\rho\in\sigma(1)\}
. Note the map
M^{\prime}\rightarrow\bigoplus_{\rho\in\sigma^{\prime}}\mathbb{R}\cdot D_{\rho}
in (
4.4
) has the same image as
M\rightarrow\bigoplus_{\rho\in\sigma}\mathbb{R}\cdot D_{\rho}
, since the cokernel maps
f_{\sigma}
and
f_{\sigma^{\prime}}
agree. Thus, for any
v\in M_{\mathbb{R}}
, there is
v^{\prime}\in M^{\prime}_{\mathbb{R}}
such that
-d(v)=-d(v^{\prime})
and in particular they map to the same lattice point.

We note that
R^{\prime}\rightarrow R
is a finite flat morphism, and so if
L\in\operatorname{CM}R
,
R^{\prime}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}L\in\operatorname{CM}R^{\prime}
. Further, observe that
R^{\prime}\otimes_{R}\operatorname{Hom}(A_{v},A_{w})=\operatorname{Hom}(A_{v^{\prime}},A_{w^{\prime}})
. To see this, use Corollary
2.32
to directly compute both sides via the polytopes
Q_{d(v)-d(w)},Q_{d(v^{\prime})-d(w^{\prime})}
and their respective intersections with
M,M^{\prime}
. Tensoring
\operatorname{Hom}(A_{v},A_{w})
with
R^{\prime}
precisely yields the expression for
\operatorname{Hom}(A_{v^{\prime}},A_{w^{\prime}})
. Consider now the set of conic modules for
\sigma^{\prime}
of the form
A_{v^{\prime}}
with
f_{\sigma}(-d(v^{\prime}))\in I^{\prime}
and form the direct sum
\mathbb{B}
. By the above,
\Lambda^{\prime}=\operatorname{End}_{R^{\prime}}(\mathbb{B})\in\operatorname{CM}R^{\prime}
. The set
I^{\prime}
is lockable and so we obtain
\operatorname{gl}\dim\Lambda^{\prime}<\infty
, and thus
\Lambda^{\prime}\in\operatorname{CM}R^{\prime}
is an NCCR of
R^{\prime}
. That means the set of conic modules in the direct sum
\mathbb{B}
is incredulous by Theorem
4.22
. But each lattice point in
Z_{\mathcal{X},N^{\prime}}
has a unique isomorphism class of conic
R^{\prime}
-modules
A_{v^{\prime}}
associated to it, and so the set
I^{\prime}
is an incredulous set of lattice points for
R^{\prime}
. Being an incredulous set of lattice points is a combinatorial condition, so as the collections of
\{\beta_{\rho}\}
agree,
I^{\prime}
is also incredulous when considered as set of lattice points for
R
, concluding the proof of the Theorem.
∎

Let
\sigma\subset N_{\mathbb{R}}
be a cone admitting and NCCR

5.
Gorenstein cones

For the remainder of the paper, we shall focus on the case of Gorenstein cones, at the heart of Conjecture
2.3
.

The combinatorics in the setting of Gorenstein cones gives rise to some symmetry that is going to be helpful. Consider the Gorenstein element
\mathfrak{m}_{\sigma}
of the cone
\sigma
. Then

0\sim\operatorname{div}(x^{\mathfrak{m}_{\sigma}})=\sum_{\rho\in\sigma(1)}\langle\mathfrak{m}_{\sigma},u_{\rho}\rangle D_{\rho}=\sum_{\rho\in\sigma(1)}D_{\rho},

where the last equality follows by the definition of the Gorenstein element. This shows that for all subsets
J\subset\sigma(1)
, we have

\sum_{\rho\in J}D_{\rho}\sim-\sum_{\rho\in J^{c}}D_{\rho},

(5.1)

where
J^{c}
is the complement of
J
in
\sigma(1)
. Similarly, on the level of the
\beta_{\rho}
, we obtain

\sum_{\rho\in J}\beta_{\rho}=-\sum_{\rho\in J^{c}}\beta_{\rho}.

(5.2)

Lemma 5.1
.

Consider a Gorenstein cone
\sigma
with an associated simplicial fan
\Sigma
(obtained by regularly subdividing the cone without adding additional rays), giving a smooth toric DM stack
\mathcal{X}
. Then the zonotope
Z_{\mathcal{X}}
is symmetric, i.e.
p\in Z_{\mathcal{X}}\Leftrightarrow-p\in Z_{\mathcal{X}}
. Thus, for any
v\in M_{\mathbb{R}}
, there is a
w\in M_{\mathbb{R}}
such that
f_{\sigma}(-d(w))=-f_{\sigma}(-d(v))
.

Proof.

Note that by (
5.2
),
\sum_{\rho\in\sigma(1)}\beta_{\rho}=0
.
The zonotope is given by
f_{\sigma}((-1,0]^{|\Sigma(1)|})
, and thus
Z_{\mathcal{X}_{\Sigma}}=\{\sum_{\rho\in\sigma(1)}\alpha_{\rho}\beta_{\rho}\mid\alpha\in(-1,0]\}
. Write
p
in this form as
\sum_{\rho\in\sigma(1)}\alpha_{\rho}\beta_{\rho}
. We distinguish two cases. If none of the
\alpha_{\rho}
are zero, then

-p=0+\sum_{\rho\in\sigma(1)}-\alpha_{\rho}\beta_{\rho}=-\sum_{\rho\in\sigma(1)}\beta_{\rho}+\sum_{\rho\in\sigma(1)}-\alpha_{\rho}\beta_{\rho}=\sum_{\rho\in\sigma(1)}(-1-\alpha_{\rho})\beta_{\rho}.

Since
\alpha_{\rho}\in(-1,0)
,
-1-\alpha_{\rho}\in(-1,0)
for all
\rho
, and so
-p\in Z_{\mathcal{X}_{\Sigma}}
. Now suppose some
\alpha_{\rho}
are 0. Then

p=\sum_{\rho\in\sigma(1)}\alpha_{\rho}\beta_{\rho}-r\cdot 0=\sum_{\rho\in\sigma(1)}\alpha_{\rho}\beta_{\rho}-r\cdot\sum_{\rho\in\sigma(1)}\beta_{\rho}=\sum_{\rho\in\sigma(1)}(\alpha_{\rho}-r)\beta_{\rho}.

This holds for any
r\in\mathbb{R}
, so choose
0<r\ll 1
. Then
\alpha_{\rho}\in(-1,0)
for all
\rho\in\sigma(1)
and we reduce to the first case, obtaining
-p\in Z_{\mathcal{X}_{\Sigma}}
.
∎

From now on, we leave the simplicial fan

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\sigma
be a Gorenstein cone and

. There exists a

such that
-d(v^{\prime})\sim d(v)
. We define
3
3

3

up to isomorphism

B_{v}
to be the conic module
A_{v^{\prime}}
. Similarly, denote the complex

by

.

Proof.

Note

. Since the point

is in the zonotope

, by Lemma

, so is

But then

The symmetry of lattice points carries over to a symmetry in the associated complexes, simplifying computations.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
v,w\in M_{\mathbb{R}}
such that
A_{w}
appears in

. Then
B_{v}
appears in

in the same degree.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.3
,
A_{w}
appearing in

is equivalent to
-d(w)\sim-d(v)-\sum_{\rho\in J}D_{\rho}
for some subset

and existence of a solution to

\begin{cases}d(v)_{\rho}-1<\langle x,u_{\rho}\rangle<d(v)_{\rho}\text{ for }\rho\not\in J,\\

This is equivalent to

and the existence of a solution to

\begin{cases}&-d(v)_{\rho}<\langle x,u_{\rho}\rangle<-d(v)_{\rho}+1\text{ for }\rho\not\in J,\\
&-d(v)_{\rho}=\langle x,u_{\rho}\rangle\text{ for }\rho\in J.\end{cases}

Note now that (up to linear equivalence)

&d(v)_{\rho}+1\text{ for }\rho\in J.\end{cases}

Therefore, we have a solution

to the system of (in)equalities

\begin{cases}&-d(w)_{\rho}<\langle x,u_{\rho}\rangle<-d(w)_{\rho}+1\text{ for }\rho\not\in J,\\
&1-d(w)_{\rho}=\langle x,u_{\rho}\rangle\text{ for }\rho\in J.\end{cases}

Consider now

for such a solution

to obtain a solution to the system of inequalities

\begin{cases}&-d(w)_{\rho}-1<\langle y,u_{\rho}\rangle<-d(w)_{\rho}\text{ for }\rho\not\in J,\\
&-d(w)_{\rho}=\langle y,u_{\rho}\rangle\text{ for }\rho\in J.\end{cases}

By definition,

is the conic module associated to

∎

Lemma 5.5
.

appears in degree

Proof.

We know that

5.1.
Almost simplicial Gorenstein cones

A special case that we treat in more detail is the case of
almost simplicial cones
.

Definition 5.7
.

A (full-dimensional) cone
\sigma\subset N_{\mathbb{R}}
is
almost simplicial
if
|\sigma(1)|=\dim\sigma+1
.

In the case of almost simplicial cones, the resulting secondary fan and zonotope are one-dimensional, which makes computations much easier.
The remainder of this paper is dedicated to investigating which toric algebras of almost simplicial Gorenstein cones have NCCRs obtained as endomorphism algebras of incomplete sums of conic modules. We begin by setting up some notation.

Notation 5.8
.

Let
\sigma
be an almost simplicial cone. Since the secondary fan we consider is one-dimensional for this case, all lattice points can be represented by integers. Note that each
\beta_{\rho}
is either negative, 0 or positive. Given a subset
J\subseteq\sigma(1)
, write
J_{-}
for the set
\{\rho\in J^{c}|\beta_{\rho}<0\}
and
J_{+}
for the set
\{\rho\in J^{c}|\beta_{\rho}>0\}
. Write
S_{-}=\{\rho\in\sigma(1)\mid\beta_{\rho}<0\}
and
S_{+}=\{\rho\in\sigma(1)\mid\beta_{\rho}>0\}
. Where clear, we will abuse notation and write
S_{+},S_{-}
for the index set as well as the set of
\beta_{\rho}
themselves. Similarly, for a lockable set
I
we will, where clear, also refer to
I
as the set of conic modules
A_{i},i\in I
.

Using this notation, we can formulate the following criterion of validity for paths.

Lemma 5.9
.

Let
\sigma
be an almost simplicial Gorenstein cone and
\beta_{J}:l-k\rightsquigarrow l
be a path with
\sum_{\rho\in J}\beta_{\rho}=k\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then
\beta_{J}
is valid if and only if

\sum_{\rho\in J_{-}}\beta_{\rho}<l-k<\sum_{\rho\in J_{+}}\beta_{\rho}.

(5.3)

Proof.

Assume first that
k\neq 0
. Note that
\sum_{\rho\in J}\beta_{\rho}=k
and so
\sum_{\rho\in J^{c}}\beta_{\rho}=-k
. Write now
l=\sum_{\rho\in J}\frac{l}{k}\beta_{\rho}
. Then the path
\beta_{J}
is valid if and only if

l\in\sum_{\rho\in J^{c}}(-1,0)\beta_{\rho}.

This is equivalent to

\displaystyle-\sum_{\rho\in J_{+}}\beta_{\rho}<l<-\sum_{\rho\in J_{-}}\beta_{\rho}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\sum_{\rho\in J_{-}}\beta_{\rho}<l+\sum_{\rho\in J^{c}}\beta_{\rho}<\sum_{\rho\in J_{+}}\beta_{\rho}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\sum_{\rho\in J_{-}}\beta_{\rho}<l-k<\sum_{\rho\in J_{+}}\beta_{\rho},

as required.

If
k=0
, we reduce to the case
l\geq 0
as the case
l<0
is analogous. There is now some
\beta_{\rho}>0
in
J
, as otherwise
J
consists only of
\beta_{\rho}=0
. In that case, however, the inequality (
5.3
) reduces to
l\in Z_{\sigma}
so always holds. Similarly, we can choose to write
l=l/\beta_{\rho_{+}}\cdot\beta_{\rho_{+}}
for some
\beta_{\rho_{+}}>0
and the validity condition becomes
0\in\sum_{\rho\in S_{+}\setminus\{\rho_{+}\}}(-1,0)\beta_{\rho}+(-l-1,-l)+\sum_{\rho\in S_{-}}(-1,0)\beta_{\rho}
, which always holds.

Write
l=\sum_{\rho\in J\cap S_{+}}l/p\cdot\beta_{\rho}
where
p=\sum_{\rho\in J\cap S_{+}}\beta_{\rho}
. Validity is equivalent to

l\in\sum_{\rho\in J^{c}}(-1,0)\beta_{\rho},

which is equivalent to

-\sum_{\rho\in J_{+}}\beta_{\rho}<l<-\sum_{\rho\in J_{-}}\beta_{\rho}.

As noted before, this is equivalent to (
5.3
).
∎

The following Theorem, which is the main result in this section of the paper, classifies which almost simplicial Gorenstein cones admit NCCRs via endomorphism algebras of incomplete sums of conic modules.

Theorem 5.10
.

Let
\sigma
be an almost simplicial Gorenstein cone. Then there exists an incomplete sum
\mathbb{B}
of conic modules such that
\operatorname{End}_{R}(\mathbb{B})
is an NCCR of
R=k[\sigma^{\vee}\cap M]
if and only if any of the following holds:

•

The collection of
\beta_{\rho}
associated to
\sigma
is, up to flipping all signs,
\{2,1,-1,-1,-1\}
.

•

The collection of
\beta_{\rho}
associated to
\sigma
is
\{1,1,1,-1,-1,-1\}
.

•

\sigma
is a 3-dimensional Gorenstein cone lattice equivalent to
\sigma=\operatorname{Cone}(P\times\{1\})
, where
P
is a trapezoid.

Before we dive into the details, let us here outline the ideas and structure behind the proof. Firstly, note that we completely shift focus to the geometry and combinatorics of the secondary fans. In other words, we use Theorem
4.22
to reduce to deciding the existence of incredulous sets of lattice points.

Given an almost simplicial Gorenstein cone
\sigma
, we establish some basic facts about the collection of
\beta_{\rho}
associated to it: for instance,
|S_{\pm}|\geq 2
and none of the
\beta_{\rho}
vanish. We show that the length of a path corresponds to the size of the corresponding set of
J\subsetneq\sigma(1)
, allowing us to construct the complexes associated to the conic modules simply by classifying the valid paths from the collection of
\beta_{\rho}
alone. We then provide a collection of lattice points that need to be contained in any potential incredulous set
I
. To prove Theorem
5.10
we use this set of points that ”cannot be substituted” to reduce the statement to a handful of explicit cases, which we verify in Propositions
5.21
and
5.22
.

Lemma 5.11
.

Let
\sigma
be an almost simplicial Gorenstein cone. Then
|S_{-}|,|S_{+}|\neq 0,1
.

Proof.

Note
\sum_{\rho\in\sigma(1)}\beta_{\rho}=0
for Gorenstein cones, and so
|S_{+}|=0
implies
\beta_{\rho}=0
for all
\rho
. But then there is no linear relation between the primitive generators
u_{\rho},\rho\in\sigma(1)
. This is not possible as
|\sigma(1)|=n+1>\dim\sigma
. If
|S_{+}|=1
, then without loss of generality
\beta_{\rho_{1}}>0
for
\rho_{1}\in\sigma(1)
and
\beta_{\rho}\leq 0
for all other
\rho\in\sigma(1)\setminus\{\rho_{1}\}
. Thus we obtain a linear relationship
\beta_{1}u_{1}=\sum_{\rho\in\sigma(1)\setminus\{\rho_{1}\}}-\beta_{\rho}u_{\rho}
. This, however, implies that
u_{\rho_{1}}\in\operatorname{Cone}(u_{\rho}|\rho\in\sigma(1)\setminus\{\rho_{1}\})
. But then
\rho
is not an extremal ray of
\sigma
and the cone is in fact simplicial. Therefore,
|S_{+}|\geq 2
and analogously
|S_{-}|\geq 2
.
∎

Lemma 5.12
.

Let
\sigma
be an almost simplicial Gorenstein cone of dimension
n
and
I
a lockable set. Then
0\in I
.

Proof.

If
0\not\in I
, neither are any
l
such that there is a valid path
0\rightsquigarrow l
as otherwise substituting
A_{0}
simply reintroduces another copy of
A_{0}
in a higher degree (as
A_{0}
appears in degree
n
in
K_{0}^{\bullet}
by Lemma
5.5
). A path
\beta_{J}:0\rightsquigarrow\beta_{J}
is valid (confounding notations by denoting the endpoint of the path with
\beta_{J}
) if and only if
J_{+}\cap S_{+}
and
J_{-}\cap S_{-}
are both non-empty (using Lemma
5.9
). So no such
\beta_{J}
is in the set
I
. But substituting
A_{\beta_{J}}
will introduce an
A_{0}
into any complex, which cannot be substituted away. Hence, no complex where
A_{\beta_{J}}
appears can be valid either. Continuing this line of argument, if there is a concatentation of valid paths
0\rightsquigarrow M_{1}\rightsquigarrow\dots\rightsquigarrow M_{k}\rightsquigarrow l
, then
l\not\in I
.
It is thus sufficient to prove that there is a concatenation of valid paths from
0
to any
l\in I
.

Without loss of generality let
l>0
. Note that there exists
\beta_{i}\in S_{+}
and
\beta_{j}\in S_{-}
such that
\gcd{(\beta_{i},\beta_{j})}=1
.
So there exist
r,s\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
r|\beta_{i}|+s|\beta_{j}|=1
.
Not both
r,s
can be
>0
so either
r\geq 0,s\leq 0
or
r\leq 0,s\geq 0
. Then
1=r\beta_{i}+(-s)\beta_{j}
with
-s\geq 0
and similarly
l=rl\beta_{i}+(-s)l\beta_{j}
. Since
l>0
, we cannot have
r\leq 0
and
s\geq 0
, and thus we know that
r\geq 0
and
s\leq 0
.

Note that for all
M\in Z_{\sigma}
, either the path
\beta_{i}
or the path
\beta_{j}
is valid. Indeed,
\beta_{i}
is a valid path from
M
to

if and only if

\sum_{\beta_{\rho}\in S_{-}}\beta_{\rho}<M<\sum_{\beta_{\rho}\in S_{+}}\beta_{\rho}-\beta_{i}.

Thus, if
\beta_{i}
is not a valid path,
\sum_{\beta_{\rho}\in S_{+}}\beta_{\rho}>M\geq\sum_{\beta_{\rho}\in S_{+}}\beta_{\rho}-\beta_{i}>\sum_{\beta_{\rho}\in S_{-}}\beta_{\rho}
where the last inequality follows as
\sum_{\beta_{\rho}\in S_{-}}\beta_{\rho}+\sum_{\beta_{\rho}\in S_{+}}\beta_{\rho}=0
and
|S_{+}|\geq 2
and so
\beta_{i}<|\sum_{\beta_{\rho}\in S_{-}}\beta_{\rho}|
. This chain of inequalities however implies
\beta_{j}
is valid, and so we have that either
\beta_{i}
or
\beta_{j}
has to be a valid path from
M
.

Begin thus at 0 and move along a valid choice of path, either
\beta_{i}
or
\beta_{j}
. Repeat this process, always landing at a point of the form
k_{1}\beta_{i}+k_{2}\beta_{j}
and at each step increasing the sum
k_{1}+k_{2}
by one. If
k_{1}=rl
, but
k_{2}<(-s)l
, note that subsequently the path
\beta_{j}
is valid, as

\sum_{\rho\in S_{+}}\beta_{\rho}>rl\beta_{i}+k_{2}\beta_{j}>rl\beta_{i}+(-s)l\beta_{j}=l>0>\sum_{\rho\in S_{-}}\beta_{\rho}-\beta_{j}.

Hence, we can keep picking the valid path
\beta_{j}
until we reach the point
rl\beta_{i}+(-s)l\beta_{j}=l
. Similarly if we reach
k_{2}=(-s)l
before
k_{1}
becomes
rl
, the path
\beta_{i}
remains valid. Thus, we have found a concatentation of valid paths from 0 to
l
.

For
l<0
, we note that
l^{\prime}=l+\sum_{\beta_{\rho}\in S_{+}}\beta_{\rho}>0
and so there is a concatenation of valid paths from 0 to
l^{\prime}
. Using the path
\sum_{\rho\in S_{-}}\beta_{\rho}
, we have

0<l^{\prime}<\sum_{\rho\in S_{+}}\beta_{\rho},

and so there is a valid path from
l^{\prime}
to
l
, i.e. there exists a concatenation of valid paths starting at 0 and ending at
l
, as required.

In conclusion, all

admit concatenations of valid paths from
0
to
l
, and so

for

, which means

, a contradiction. Hence,
0\in I
.
∎

.

Let
\sigma
be an almost simplicial Gorenstein cone. Given a lockable set
I

either

or there is an

By the proof of Lemma

, there exists a concatenation of valid paths

. But then by Proposition

, there is a concatenation of valid paths

.

Let
\sigma
be an almost simplicial Gorenstein cone. Suppose there is a

. But

Notation 5.15

We may order the

Lemma 5.16
.

Let
\sigma
be an almost simplicial Gorenstein cone such that

. Then
\beta_{J}
is a path of length

.

If there was a

with

, then the primitive generators

are linearly dependent. But then the set of linear relations between the primitive generators of

are spanned by this linear relation between the generators of

, and so for

Lemma 5.17
.

Let
\sigma
be an almost simplicial Gorenstein cone of dimension

. For

, the complex

.

Proof.

By Lemma

, the paths of length

come from subsets of
\sigma(1)
of precisely size

or

. The unique subset of size

contains all

and is only valid if it starts at 0. Otherwise, each path of length

is of the form

for some

. This path is valid if and only if it starts at

If

and so the indices

Proposition 5.18
.

Let
\sigma
be an almost simplicial Gorenstein cone and

Proposition 5.19

Let

.

shows that the listed cones admit NCCRs constructed via incomplete sums of conic modules. We shall now show that if
\sigma

We may without loss of generality assume that

. Note that

. Otherwise, we would have

-\beta_{n+1}>\beta_{1}+\dots+\beta_{k-1}\Leftrightarrow-\beta_{n+1}+\beta_{k}>\sum_{\beta_{\rho}\in S_{+}}\beta_{\rho}.

But
-\beta_{n+1}+\beta_{k}\leq-\beta_{n+1}-\beta_{k-1}\leq-\sum_{\beta_{\rho}\in S_{-}}\beta_{\rho}=\sum_{\rho\in S_{+}}\beta_{\rho}
, a contradiction.

Two cases arise. If
-\beta_{n+1}=\beta_{1}+\dots+\beta_{k-1}
then we have equalities in the non-strict inequalities above, which happens if and only if
\beta_{k}=-\beta_{k+1}
and
|S_{-}|=2
. Note that
\beta_{k}=-\beta_{k+1}
in particular implies
|\beta_{k}|\geq|\beta_{k+1}|

\beta_{1}=-\beta_{4},

5.19

-\beta_{n+1}\leq\beta_{1}+\dots+\beta_{k-1}-1=:l
. Then

and

is a complex of length

by Lemma

. Valid paths ending at

Then the only conic modules

are thus of the form

\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}=-\beta_{3}=-\beta_{4}=1
, a situation covered by Proposition
5.19
.

But if all conic modules in
K_{p}^{\bullet}
are of the form
A_{q},q\in I
, then as the length of
K_{p}^{\bullet}
is
\leq n-1
and
A_{p}
appears exactly in degree 1 in
K_{l}^{\bullet}
, we have that
K_{l}^{\dagger,\bullet}
has length
\leq n-1
and can thus not be of length
n=\dim\sigma
. Therefore, the set
I
is not incredulous, i.e. no NCCR via direct sums of conic modules exists.

It now remains to consider the cases excluded earlier, i.e.
-\beta_{k}-1<\beta_{k+2}+\dots+\beta_{n+1}+1
. If
|S_{-}|\geq 3
, note that
-\beta_{k}-1\geq\beta_{k+1}-1\geq\beta_{k+2}-1\geq\beta_{k+2}+\beta_{n+1}
, with equality only if
\beta_{n+1}=\dots=\beta_{k+1}=-\beta_{k}=-1
. Further, this is
\geq\beta_{k+2}+\dots+\beta_{n+1}+1
if
|S_{-}|\geq 4
. Thus, the remaining cases are
|S_{-}|=3
with
k=n-2
and
-\beta_{n-2}=\beta_{n-1}=\beta_{n}=\beta_{n+1}=-1
or
|S_{-}|=2
. In the first case, since
\beta_{1}+\dots+\beta_{n+1}=0
and
|S_{+}|\geq 2
we obtain either
n=4
and
\beta_{1}=2
or
n=5
and
\beta_{i}=1=-\beta_{j}
for
i=1,2,3
and
j=4,5,6
, both cases part of Proposition
5.21
.

If
|S_{-}|=2
, then we have
-\beta_{k}-1\leq\beta_{n+1}
. If

|\beta_{k}|=|\beta_{k+1}|
this would give
\beta_{n+1}=\beta_{k+1}
or
\beta_{n+1}=\beta_{k+1}-1
. The former gives, as
\beta_{1}\geq\beta_{k}
,
n=3
and
\beta_{1}=-\beta_{4}
, a case covered by Proposition
5.19
.

For the latter, we have
\beta_{k}=-\beta_{k+1},\beta_{n+1}=\beta_{k+1}-1
which has possibilities
n=3
and
\beta_{1}=-\beta_{4}
or
n=4
and
\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}=\beta_{3}=1,

\beta_{4}=-1
and
\beta_{5}=-2
, covered by Propositions Proposition
5.19
and
5.21
.

Finally, if
|\beta_{k}|<|\beta_{k+1}|
, then
-\beta_{k}-1\geq\beta_{k+1}\geq\beta_{n+1}
. For
-\beta_{k}-1<\beta_{n+1}+1
, the equality needs to hold, i.e.
\beta_{k+1}=\beta_{n+1}=-\beta_{k}-1
. Since
\beta_{1}+\dots+\beta_{n+1}=0
, this means
\beta_{1}+\dots+\beta_{k-1}=\beta_{k}+2
. If
n=3
, i.e.
|S_{+}|=2
, we get
\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}+2,

\beta_{3}=\beta_{4}=-\beta_{2}-1
. This case is covered by Proposition
5.19
.

If
|S_{+}|=3
, then
2\beta_{3}\leq\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}=\beta_{3}+2
which gives

\beta_{3}=1=\beta_{2}
and
\beta_{1}=2
with
\beta_{4}=\beta_{5}=-2
or
\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}=\beta_{3}=2
and
\beta_{4}=\beta_{5}=-3
, both cases covered by Proposition
5.22
.
Finally, for
|S_{+}|\geq 4
, we have
\beta_{k}+2=\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}+\beta_{3}\geq 3\beta_{k}
and so
\beta_{1}=\dots=\beta_{4}=1
and
\beta_{5}=\beta_{6}=-2
, which is covered by Proposition
5.22
.

With this, we have shown that an almost simplicial Gorenstein cone admits an incredulous set, and thus an NCCR as endomorphism algebra of an incomplete sum of conic modules, exactly in the cases stipulated by Theorem
5.10
.
∎
