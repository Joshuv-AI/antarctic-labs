Development and validation of machine learning-based prediction models for adult bowel necrosis for patients presenting with acute abdominal pain at the emergency department: a multicenter retrospective cohort study
Authors: Youlong Zhu, Jiawei Feng, Ruming Liu, Wang Ren, Xiao Ma, Caihua Zhang, Wentao Zhang, Chonghan Zhong, Longbo Gong, Feifei Kong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBowel necrosis is a life-threatening condition requiring urgent surgical intervention. Early identification remains challenging due to nonspecific clinical presentations. This study aimed to develop and validate machine learning models for predicting bowel necrosis in adult patients.MethodsWe conducted a multicenter retrospective cohort study including adult patients (≥ 18 years) presenting with acute abdominal pain between January 2021 and December 2023. Data were collected from electronic health records including demographics, clinical symptoms, laboratory parameters, and imaging findings. Six machine learning algorithms were developed and compared: logistic regression (LR), random forest (RF), support vector machine (SVM), gradient boosting machine (GBM), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), and artificial neural network (ANN). Model performance was evaluated using area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, and calibration metrics.ResultsA total of 8,247 patients were included, with 623 (7.6%) diagnosed with bowel necrosis. The XGBoost model demonstrated superior performance with an AUC of 0.912 (95% CI: 0.887–0.936), sensitivity of 87.3%, and specificity of 84.7% in the validation cohort. Key predictive features included lactate level (importance score: 0.185), white blood cell count (0.142), abdominal CT findings (0.138), age (0.108), and C-reactive protein (0.095). The model showed adequate calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow p = 0.324) and clinical utility with a net benefit of 0.142 at a 10% risk threshold.ConclusionsThe machine learning-based prediction model demonstrates good discriminative performance in identifying patients at risk for bowel necrosis. Implementation of this model as a clinical decision-support tool may inform early triage and reduce diagnostic delays, potentially improving patient outcomes through timely surgical intervention. Prospective external validation is required before widespread clinical adoption.

Introduction

Bowel necrosis represents one of the most critical gastrointestinal emergencies, with mortality rates ranging from 15% to 30% despite advances in surgical techniques and intensive care management [1, 2]. The condition encompasses various pathological entities including mesenteric ischemia, necrotizing enterocolitis, and strangulated bowel obstruction, all sharing the common pathway of intestinal tissue death due to compromised blood supply or severe inflammation [3, 4]. The clinical challenge in managing bowel necrosis lies primarily in its early recognition. Traditional diagnostic approaches rely heavily on clinical judgment, laboratory markers, and imaging studies, each with inherent limitations [5, 6]. Clinical symptoms such as abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting are nonspecific and often overlap with other gastrointestinal conditions [7]. Laboratory markers including lactate, white blood cell count, and inflammatory markers may be elevated in various abdominal pathologies [5, 6]. While computed tomography (CT) imaging has improved diagnostic accuracy, subtle early changes may be missed, and radiation exposure concerns limit repeated examinations [4].

The advent of artificial intelligence and machine learning in healthcare has opened new avenues for improving diagnostic accuracy in complex clinical scenarios [8, 9]. Machine learning algorithms excel at identifying patterns within large datasets and can integrate multiple variables simultaneously to generate predictive models [10]. Several studies have demonstrated the potential of machine learning [11] in gastrointestinal conditions, including prediction of appendicitis [12]. However, the application of machine learning specifically for bowel necrosis prediction remains limited. Previous studies have been constrained by small sample sizes, single-center designs, or focus on pediatric populations. There is a critical need for robust, validated prediction models that can assist clinicians in early identification of adult patients at risk for bowel necrosis.

This study aims to address this gap by developing and validating machine learning-based prediction models for adult bowel necrosis using a large, multicenter dataset. We hypothesized that machine learning algorithms could effectively integrate clinical, laboratory, and imaging data to create accurate prediction models that outperform traditional diagnostic approaches.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a multicenter retrospective cohort study across four tertiary care hospitals between January 2021 and December 2023. The study was approved by the institutional review boards of all participating centers, and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. The study adhered to the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) guidelines [13] and the TRIPOD + AI statement for clinical prediction models using machine learning methods [14].

In accordance with the TRIPOD + AI statement, the intended use of this model is diagnostic: to identify adult patients who already have bowel necrosis at the time of emergency department presentation with acute abdominal pain, based on admission-time clinical, laboratory, and imaging features, in order to support triage and prioritisation for urgent surgical evaluation. The model is not intended to predict future occurrence of bowel necrosis in patients who do not already have the condition. The patient selection flowchart is presented in Fig. 1.

Patient population

Inclusion Criteria: Adults aged ≥ 18 years: (1) Presentation to emergency department with acute abdominal pain; (2) Complete clinical, laboratory, and imaging data available; (3) Final diagnosis confirmed through surgical exploration, histopathology, or clinical follow-up.

Exclusion Criteria: (1) Pregnancy; (2) Previous abdominal surgery within 30 days; (3) Chronic inflammatory bowel disease; (4) Active malignancy involving the gastrointestinal tract; (5) Incomplete medical records.

Data collection

Data were extracted from electronic health records by trained research personnel using standardized data collection forms. Two trained research personnel independently extracted CT findings using a standardized data-abstraction form; disagreements (3.2% of records) were adjudicated by a third investigator blinded to outcome. A 10% random subsample was additionally re-reviewed by a consultant radiologist (inter-rater agreement κ = 0.87). The following variables were collected:

Demographics: Age (years), sex, body mass index (BMI, kg/m²), comorbidities (diabetes mellitus, hypertension, coronary artery disease, chronic kidney disease, atrial fibrillation).

Clinical Presentation: Duration of symptoms (hours), abdominal pain characteristics (location, severity on 0–10 numeric rating scale, nature), associated symptoms (nausea, vomiting, fever, diarrhea), vital signs (systolic blood pressure [mmHg], heart rate [bpm], temperature [°C], respiratory rate [breaths/min]).

Laboratory Parameters: Complete blood count including white blood cell count (WBC, ×10⁹/L), hemoglobin (g/dL), and platelets (×10⁹/L); comprehensive metabolic panel including creatinine (µmol/L [mg/dL provided in parentheses for clinical reference]); liver function tests including AST (U/L), ALT (U/L), and total bilirubin (µmol/L [mg/dL provided in parentheses for clinical reference]); coagulation studies; arterial blood gas including pH, base excess (mmol/L), and lactate (mmol/L); inflammatory markers: C-reactive protein (CRP, mg/L) and procalcitonin (PCT, ng/mL).

Imaging Findings: Abdominal CT findings were extracted from the structured radiology report issued by the attending radiologist during the index ED episode. The CT composite score is an integer variable (range 0–7) calculated as the unweighted sum of seven binary CT findings (present = 1, absent = 0): bowel wall thickening > 3 mm, pneumatosis intestinalis, portal venous gas, mesenteric vascular occlusion or thrombus, pneumoperitoneum, ascites, and mesenteric fat stranding. Each finding was independently adjudicated by two board-certified abdominal radiologists with ≥ 5 years of experience, with disagreements resolved by a third senior radiologist. A score of 0 indicates none of the findings were present; a score of 7 indicates all seven were present.

Outcome definition

The primary outcome was confirmed bowel necrosis, defined as meeting any one of the following criteria. Surgical confirmation was established when necrotic bowel tissue was identified during surgery requiring resection. Alternatively, histopathological evidence was considered confirmatory when microscopic examination demonstrated transmural necrosis, defined as full-thickness death of the bowel wall.

Machine learning model development

The dataset was randomly divided into training (70%, n = 5,773), validation (15%, n = 1,237), and test (15%, n = 1,237) sets using stratified random sampling based on the bowel necrosis outcome, maintaining an outcome prevalence of approximately 7.6% across all three subsets. Six machine learning algorithms were developed and compared:

The six algorithms were selected a priori to represent the main families of supervised classifiers appropriate for structured clinical data, following TRIPOD + AI-aligned comparative studies in emergency medicine: (1) Logistic Regression (LR) — linear parametric baseline and clinical reference standard; (2) Support Vector Machine (SVM) — non-linear, margin-based classifier with radial basis function kernel; (3) Random Forest (RF) — bagging tree ensemble robust to feature scaling and mixed data types; (4) Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM) — sequential tree ensemble sensitive to feature interactions; (5) XGBoost — regularized gradient boosting with efficient missing-value handling [15]; (6) Artificial Neural Network (ANN) — flexible non-linear function approximator with three hidden layers (128/64/32 units, ReLU activation, dropout rate 0.3). This taxonomy (linear, kernel, bagging, boosting, deep) enables comparison across distinct inductive biases. Final hyperparameter specifications for all six models are detailed in Section “Model training and hyperparameter tuning”.

Feature engineering and selection

Missing values were imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) [16]. Overall missingness in the analytic cohort was low, as patients with missing key clinical, laboratory, or imaging variables had been excluded at the enrolment stage (n = 605; Fig. 1). Residual missingness was handled with MICE to ensure complete feature matrices for model development and evaluation. Continuous variables were standardized using z-score normalization. Categorical variables were encoded using one-hot encoding. Feature selection was performed using recursive feature elimination with cross-validation (RFECV) to identify the most informative variables. Following RFECV, the final XGBoost model retained 10 predictors from the 34-variable candidate pool, corresponding to an events-per-variable (EPV) of approximately 62 based on 623 outcome events — substantially exceeding the thresholds of Peduzzi et al. (≥ 10) and Harrell (≥ 15) [17]. The minimal train–test AUC gap (0.918 vs. 0.915) further supports the absence of meaningful overfitting.

Model training and hyperparameter tuning

Models were trained using 5-fold cross-validation on the training set. Hyperparameters were optimized using Bayesian optimization (Tree-structured Parzen Estimator) with 100 iterations [18]. The objective function was set to maximize the AUC. Final hyperparameters for all six models are fully described in this section.

Model evaluation

Model performance was assessed using multiple metrics. Discrimination: AUC, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV). Calibration: Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, calibration plots [19]. Clinical Utility: Decision curve analysis, net benefit calculation [20].

Statistical analysis

Normality of continuous variables was formally assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test supplemented by visual inspection of histograms and Q–Q plots. Normally distributed continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Non-normally distributed continuous variables are reported as median (interquartile range, IQR). Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between groups were performed using Student’s t-test for normally distributed variables, Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed variables, or chi-square test for categorical variables, as appropriate. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using Python 3.9 with scikit-learn, XGBoost, and TensorFlow libraries.

Model interpretability

Model interpretability was assessed using SHapley Additive exPlanations, a game-theoretic framework that assigns each feature an additive contribution to the individual prediction by averaging marginal contributions across all possible feature coalitions. We used the TreeSHAP algorithm for the XGBoost model, which permits exact computation in polynomial time. Global feature importance was summarized as the mean absolute SHAP value across all test-set predictions, with 95% confidence intervals obtained from 1,000 bootstrap samples. Local explanations were visualised using beeswarm summary plots (Fig. 3B) and feature-pair interaction surfaces (Fig. 4) to characterise non-linear and interaction effects.

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

Compared with patients without bowel necrosis, those with necrosis were significantly older and had a modestly lower BMI, with no significant sex difference. The necrosis group also carried a substantially higher burden of cardiovascular and metabolic comorbidities, including diabetes mellitus, hypertension, coronary artery disease, chronic kidney disease, and atrial fibrillation (Table 1). Clinically, patients with necrosis presented earlier but with more severe disease, showing higher pain scores and more frequent nausea/vomiting and fever, accompanied by hemodynamic instability characterised by hypotension, tachycardia, and elevated temperature (all P < 0.001; Table 1).

Laboratory parameters and radiographic findings

Patients with bowel necrosis exhibited metabolic changes consistent with tissue hypoperfusion, most prominently a marked elevation of arterial lactate together with a more negative base excess and lower arterial pH (all P < 0.001; Table 2). The necrosis group also showed a characteristic profile of systemic inflammation and secondary organ stress, with leukocytosis and marked elevation of C-reactive protein and procalcitonin as the dominant inflammatory signals, accompanied by hepatorenal stress (higher creatinine, transaminases, and total bilirubin) and mild hematological changes. On CT, the specific signs of mesenteric ischemia, namely pneumatosis intestinalis, portal venous gas, and mesenteric vascular occlusion, showed the largest relative increases, while more common but less specific findings were also more prevalent in the necrosis group (all P < 0.001; Table 2).

Comparative performance of machine learning algorithms

Figure 2; Table 3 present comprehensive performance metrics across training, validation, and test cohorts for all six algorithms. The hierarchical ranking by AUC, illustrated by the ROC curves in Fig. 2A, was: XGBoost (0.915, 95% CI: 0.891–0.939), Random Forest (0.898, 95% CI: 0.873–0.923), Gradient Boosting (0.891, 95% CI: 0.865–0.917), Neural Network (0.871, 95% CI: 0.846–0.896), Support Vector Machine (0.859, 95% CI: 0.834–0.884), and Logistic Regression (0.828, 95% CI: 0.804–0.852).

Discussion

This study presents the largest multicenter investigation of machine learning applications for adult bowel necrosis prediction to date. Our findings demonstrate that the XGBoost-based prediction model achieves good discriminative performance, with an AUC of 0.912, numerically higher than traditional clinical assessment methods [6, 7]. Recent guidance from the TRIPOD + AI statement (2024) has established updated reporting standards for clinical prediction models using machine learning methods, emphasizing the importance of transparent reporting regardless of whether regression or machine learning approaches are used [14]. A 2024 scoping review found that while 81% of AI clinical trials reported positive primary endpoints, concerns about generalizability remain due to the predominance of single-center designs [21, 22].

The superior performance of tree-based ensemble methods (XGBoost, Random Forest, GBM) compared to linear models likely reflects the complex, non-linear relationships between clinical variables and bowel necrosis risk [23]. These algorithms excel at capturing feature interactions and handling mixed data types, making them particularly suitable for clinical prediction tasks. The identified key predictive features align with established pathophysiological mechanisms of bowel necrosis. Elevated lactate levels, reflecting tissue hypoxia and anaerobic metabolism, emerged as the most important predictor [5], consistent with previous studies demonstrating lactate’s prognostic value in mesenteric ischemia [3, 5]. WBC and CRP, representing the inflammatory response to tissue necrosis, ranked second and fifth, respectively [5, 6]. Age emerged as a significant predictor, reflecting the increased vulnerability of elderly patients due to comorbidities, medication effects, and age-related vascular changes [7].

With respect to model fairness, subgroup analyses stratified by age, sex, and comorbidity burden (Table 4) demonstrated stable discriminative performance across all pre-specified demographic and clinical subgroups, with AUCs ranging from 0.906 to 0.918 and no evidence of systematic subgroup bias. Site-level fairness assessment was not feasible in the present dataset, as the hospital of origin was not retained as a predictor variable, and remains a priority for future external validation.

Clinical implications

The implementation of this prediction model could inform clinical practice in several ways. First, it could serve as a decision-support tool in emergency departments, helping clinicians prioritize patients for urgent surgical evaluation. Early identification of high-risk patients could reduce diagnostic delays, a critical factor given that bowel necrosis outcomes are strongly time-dependent [1, 2]. Recent evidence suggests that while AI models can perform diagnosis at levels approaching physicians, their triage performance may still lag behind clinical expertise, highlighting the importance of human–AI collaboration [24]. Second, the model could guide resource allocation and disposition decisions. Third, the objective risk quantification provided by the model could improve communication between clinicians, particularly when discussing complex cases with surgical colleagues.

Comparison with existing literature

Previous studies of bowel necrosis prediction have been limited by small sample sizes and single-center designs [3, 7, 25]. Our multicenter approach with over 8,000 patients provides substantially greater statistical power and generalizability. The AUC of 0.912 achieved by our XGBoost model compares favorably with other machine learning applications in acute care settings, including sepsis prediction (AUC: 0.85–0.90) [26, 27] and acute kidney injury prediction (AUC: 0.80–0.88) [28, 29], and with previous acute mesenteric infarction prediction models (AUC: 0.75–0.83) [30]. Traditional clinical scoring systems for acute abdominal conditions typically achieve AUCs of 0.70–0.80 [6, 7], highlighting the numerical improvement offered by machine learning approaches. The integration of multiple data sources — clinical, laboratory, and imaging — appears to be key to achieving high predictive performance [31–33].

Limitations

A critical limitation that merits dedicated consideration is the modest positive predictive value (PPV 32.1%) of the model at the selected operating threshold, which represents the principal barrier to unqualified clinical adoption. At a 10% risk threshold, this performance implies that for every 100 patients screened, approximately 14 would be flagged as false positives for every 7 true cases identified — roughly two false positives for every true case. This trade-off reflects the inherently low prevalence of bowel necrosis (7.6%) in the cohort and the need to preserve high sensitivity (87.3%) and NPV (98.9%) in a rule-out diagnostic context. The AUC-PR of 0.867 contextualises this performance against the random baseline. Crucially, the model is positioned as a clinical decision-support and triage tool, not as a direct trigger for operative intervention: a positive flag should prompt expedited cross-sectional imaging, serial clinical re-assessment, and early surgical consultation — not immediate laparotomy. Clinicians must integrate model output with imaging, physical examination, and evolving laboratory trends before committing to surgery.

Additional limitations include: (1) The retrospective design may introduce selection bias. (2) Stratified random splitting was employed rather than temporal validation; although this is an accepted internal validation strategy, it does not fully emulate a prospective deployment scenario and cannot capture potential distributional drift beyond the study period. (3) The study was conducted in tertiary care centers, potentially limiting generalizability to community hospitals. (4) Although data were drawn from four tertiary care hospitals, the hospital of origin was not included as a predictor, and the clustering of patients within hospitals was not explicitly modelled (e.g., via mixed-effects approaches or cluster-robust inference). Consequently, between-hospital heterogeneity is not formally decomposed in the reported performance estimates. (5) The model requires validation in prospective studies to confirm real-world performance. (6) Temporal changes in clinical practice, imaging technology, and treatment protocols over the three-year study period (January 2021–December 2023) may have influenced outcomes. (7) Implementation challenges including electronic health record integration, clinician training, and workflow modifications need to be addressed before widespread adoption. (8) No explicit class-balancing strategy (e.g., SMOTE, undersampling, or cost-sensitive learning) was applied, given the low positive rate (7.6%). Instead, stratified sampling preserved outcome prevalence across all sets and PR-AUC was reported for all models (Table 3) to characterise performance under imbalance. This may have contributed to the modest PPV (32.1%), and future studies should assess whether such techniques, with recalibration, improve precision in this context.

Future directions

Future research should focus on prospective external validation of the prediction model in diverse clinical settings. Such validation should explicitly account for between-hospital clustering, report site-stratified performance, and assess temporal generalisability to determine model transportability across centers. A 2025 systematic review found that only 27% of clinical prediction models underwent external validation [34], highlighting a critical gap in translation to practice. Integration with real-time electronic health record systems could enable automated risk calculation. Investigating model performance in specific subgroups, such as those with inflammatory bowel disease or previous abdominal surgery, could refine clinical applications. The development of ensemble models combining machine learning predictions with clinician judgment represents a promising avenue. Economic evaluations assessing cost-effectiveness would provide valuable evidence for healthcare administrators.

Conclusions

We developed and validated a machine learning-based prediction model for adult bowel necrosis that demonstrates good discriminative performance, adequate calibration, and clinical utility. The XGBoost algorithm achieved an AUC of 0.912, sensitivity of 87.3%, and specificity of 84.7%. Key predictive features included lactate level, WBC count, CT findings, age, and C-reactive protein. The model’s modest PPV (32.1%) reflects the low prevalence context and should be interpreted in the framework of its intended use as a triage support tool rather than a stand-alone diagnostic test. Implementation of this prediction model may inform the approach to evaluating patients with acute abdominal pain, supporting objective, data-driven risk stratification alongside clinical judgment. Prospective external validation studies are needed to confirm real-world performance and clinical impact before widespread adoption.
