Associations of weekend warrior and regular physical activity patterns with prevalence of hypertension by socioeconomic status in Korean adults
Authors: Tae Gu Choi, Sangshin Park, Setor K. Kunutsor, Sae Young Jae
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEvidence shows that the weekend warrior physical activity (PA) pattern (1–2 days/week) is associated with a lower risk of hypertension, but it is unclear whether this association differs by socioeconomic status (SES). This study aimed to examine whether the association between the weekend warrior PA pattern and prevalent hypertension differs according to SES in Korean adults.MethodsWe analyzed 28,039 adults (aged ≥ 19 years) from the 2018–2023 Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey. PA patterns were categorized as inactive, insufficiently active, weekend warrior, or regularly active. Educational attainment (high school or less vs. college or higher) and household income (Q1–Q2 vs. Q3–Q4) were used as proxies of SES. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, a physician diagnosis, or use of antihypertensive medication. Multivariable logistic regression estimated odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for prevalent hypertension across PA patterns, stratified by educational attainment and household income level.ResultsAmong 28,039 adults, 8,842 (31.5%) had hypertension. In multivariable-adjusted models, compared with inactive participants, insufficiently active (OR, 0.85; 95% CI, 0.77–0.93), weekend warrior (OR, 0.74; 95% CI, 0.59–0.92), and regularly active (OR, 0.84; 95% CI, 0.77–0.91) PA patterns were each associated with lower odds of prevalent hypertension. There was no significant educational attainment-by-weekend warrior interaction for prevalent hypertension (P for interaction = 0.349), whereas a significant household income-by- weekend warrior interaction was observed (P for interaction = 0.049), with a more pronounced inverse association among lower-income adults.ConclusionsPA patterns, including the weekend warrior pattern, were associated with lower odds of prevalent hypertension among Korean adults. The inverse association between the weekend warrior pattern and hypertension appeared to differ by household income level, with a more pronounced inverse association among lower-income adults. Further studies in large longitudinal cohorts are warranted to confirm these findings.

BACKGROUND

Hypertension remains a highly prevalent global health issue, affecting over 1.4 billion people worldwide and representing one of the leading causes of cardiovascular mortality [1]. Cumulative evidence consistently underscores the protective role of physical activity (PA) in both preventing and managing hypertension [2]. The World Health Organization and the American Heart Association recommend that adults accumulate at least 150 minutes per week of moderate-intensity, or 75 minutes per week of vigorous-intensity PA as a first-line lifestyle strategy [34]. However, these PA guidelines often emphasize being active on “most days of the week,” without providing clear guidance on how PA should be accrued, which may be relevant to hypertension prevention and management.

Recent epidemiologic studies suggest that “weekend warrior” activity patterns, defined as accumulating ≥ 150 minutes/week of moderate-to-vigorous PA, with ≥ 50% of activity concentrated on 1–2 days/week, may confer cardioprotective benefits comparable to regular PA patterns [56]. While previous studies have reported favorable associations between weekend warrior pattern and mortality outcomes [78], relatively, few have focused on hypertension, and their findings are often inconsistent [91011]. Furthermore, most studies report overall associations and describe participant characteristics, but rarely evaluate subgroup differences across socioeconomic status (SES) strata using stratified analyses, underscoring the need for transparent, equity-focused reporting [12].

Individuals with lower SES consistently experience a higher prevalence of hypertension and cardiovascular mortality [131415]. This pattern is often explained by adverse living conditions and behavioral risk factors, as SES strongly shapes opportunities to engage in health-promoting behaviors [13]. Indeed, individuals with low SES are less likely to participate in regular leisure-time PA (LTPA) compared with their higher-SES counterparts [16], partly due to long or irregular working hours, physically demanding work, and limited accessibility to PA [1718]. These constraints may make it particularly difficult to accumulate PA across multiple weekdays, highlighting the need for more flexible PA strategies in these populations.

In this context, the “weekend warrior” pattern may represent a more relevant and pragmatic PA option for socioeconomically disadvantaged adults [5]. By concentrating activity into 1 or 2 days, individuals can accumulate meaningful weekly PA volume while reducing the burden of scheduling exercise across multiple weekdays. However, to our knowledge, no study has examined whether the association between the weekend warrior pattern and prevalent hypertension differs across SES strata. Therefore, this study aimed to examine whether the association between the weekend warrior pattern and prevalent hypertension differs by SES level in Korean adults.

METHODS

Study participants

This study used data from the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES) 2018–2023, an annual nationally representative survey conducted by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (Ministry of Health and Welfare). Data were collected using health questionnaires administered during interviews, physical measurements, and clinical tests. Of 43,745 participants, we included 28,039 adults aged ≥ 19 years (mean age 50 ± 0.10 years; range 19–80 years) after excluding those with missing data on PA, hypertension status, or key covariates. Ethical approval for this study was provided by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Seoul (No. 2026-02-019).

PA assessment

PA was assessed using the World Health Organization Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ) administered in KNHANES. The GPAQ captures activity across leisure-time, occupational, and transport PA domains by asking participants to report the type of activity performed, the number of days per week, and the average daily duration (hours/minutes). In the present study, PA was defined as LTPA; sport, exercise, and recreational activities, excluding occupational and transport PA. Vigorous-intensity PA was defined as high-intensity sport, athletics, and recreational activities that markedly increased heart rate or caused substantial shortness of breath for at least 10 minutes (e.g., running, jumping rope, mountain climbing, basketball, swimming, badminton singles, etc.). Moderate-intensity PA was defined as moderate-intensity sport or exercise inducing a modest increase in heart rate or mild breathlessness for at least 10 minutes (e.g., brisk walking, jogging, weight training, golf, dance sports, pilates, etc.).

Participants were categorized into 4 PA patterns based on total weekly LTPA volume calculated in metabolic equivalent of task (MET) minutes. Moderate- and vigorous-intensity PA were assigned 4 and 8 METs, respectively. Using a cutoff of 600 MET-min/week, PA patterns were defined as (i) inactive (0 MET-min/week), (ii) insufficiently active (1–599 METs-min/week), (iii) weekend warrior (≥ 600 MET-min/week accumulated over 1–2 days per week), and (iv) regularly active (≥ 600 MET-min/week accumulated across 3–7 days per week).

Covariates and SES

Marital status was classified as living with a spouse or living without a spouse (single, divorced, or separated). Educational attainment and household income generally serve as robust indicators of SES. Given their close links to health literacy, access to health-related and material resources, and opportunities for health-promoting behaviors, education and household income were used as the criteria for stratification in this study. Education level was dichotomized into a lower educational attainment (high school or less) and a higher educational attainment (college or over). Household income was divided into quartiles (lower, lower middle, upper middle, and high) based on monthly household income by the square root of household size. For stratified analyses, household income was dichotomized into lower household income (lower and lower middle quartiles) and higher household income (upper middle and high quartiles). Occupation was classified into professional/managerial, skilled non-manual, skilled manual, routine/manual, and no occupation. Alcohol consumption was defined as drinking at least once per month during the past year. Smoking status was classified as smoker (≥ 5 packs of cigarettes lifetime or currently smoking) and non-smoker. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2).

Hypertension ascertainment

The prevalence of hypertension was defined as having a systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg, a diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, a physician diagnosis, or current use of antihypertensive medication at the time of the survey.

Statistical analysis

General characteristics of participants by PA patterns were compared using chi-square test for categorical variables and one-way analysis of variance for continuous variables, presented as frequencies (%) and mean ± standard error. We calculated odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) from logistic regression analyses with adjustment for confounding factors (age, sex, BMI, marital status, education, household income, occupation, smoking, and alcohol consumption) to determine the associations of PA patterns (inactive, insufficiently active, weekend warrior, and regularly active) with the prevalence of hypertension. The associations of PA patterns with hypertension prevalence were then stratified by SES indicators, including educational attainment and household income. Interaction terms between PA pattern indicators and each SES indicator were added to the multivariable logistic regression models, and global interaction was calculated. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 and analyses were conducted using SPSS (SPSS Version 28.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

RESULTS

Of 28,039 participants, PA patterns were classified into inactive (n = 18,884; 67.3%), insufficiently active (n = 3,786; 13.5%), weekend warrior (n = 687; 2.5%), and regularly active (n = 4,682; 16.7%) (Table 1). Participants with the weekend warrior pattern were younger (mean 42.4 years), more often men (70.2%), and more likely to be high-income (48.3%) and employed (78.2%) compared with other PA patterns. They also showed the highest rates of current smoking (50.1%) and alcohol consumption (73.7%), whereas the prevalence of hypertension was the lowest (18.5%). Compared with the inactive pattern, all active PA patterns were significantly associated with lower odds of hypertension after adjusting for demographic, socioeconomic, and lifestyle covariates in the Model 2 (Table 2). When stratified by education level, participants with lower educational attainment had higher prevalence of hypertension (n = 6,623; 40.4%) than participants with higher educational attainment (n = 2,219; 19.0%). In participants with lower educational attainment, all PA patterns were significantly associated with lower odds of hypertension (Fig. 1); insufficiently active (OR, 0.80; 95% CI, 0.71–0.91), weekend warrior (OR, 0.67; 95% CI, 0.49–0.92), and regularly active (OR, 0.82; 95% CI, 0.73–0.92). In contrast, among participants with higher educational attainment, only the regularly active pattern was associated with lower hypertension risk (OR, 0.87; 95% CI, 0.76–0.99), whereas insufficiently active (OR, 0.90; 95% CI, 0.78–1.04) and weekend warrior (OR, 0.80; 95% CI, 0.59–1.10) were not. However, there was no significant interaction between PA pattern and educational attainment (P for interaction = 0.347).

When stratified by household income, the prevalence of hypertension was higher among participants with lower income (n = 4,620; 41.6%) than among those with higher income (n = 4,222; 24.9%). In participants with lower income, insufficiently active (OR, 0.74; 95% CI, 0.63–0.87) and weekend warrior (OR, 0.56; 95% CI, 0.35–0.90) patterns were associated with lower odds of hypertension, whereas the regularly active pattern was not (OR, 0.92; 95% CI, 0.79–1.07) (Fig. 1). In contrast, among participants with higher income, only the regularly active pattern was associated with lower odds of hypertension (OR, 0.80; 95% CI, 0.72–0.89), whereas insufficiently active (OR, 0.89; 95% CI, 0.80–1.01) and weekend warrior (OR, 0.79; 95% CI, 0.62–1.02) patterns were not. The interaction between PA pattern and household income was statistically significant (P for interaction = 0.049). Additional subgroup analyses by age, sex, and BMI showed generally consistent inverse associations between active PA patterns and hypertension, with no significant interactions observed (Supplementary Fig. 1). When stratified by occupation status, the weekend warrior pattern was associated with lower odds of hypertension among employed participants, but not among unemployed participants. However, the interaction between PA pattern and occupation status was not significant (P for interaction = 0.821; Supplementary Fig. 2).

DISCUSSION

In this population-based sample of Korean adults, the association between PA patterns and hypertension showed different patterns according to SES indicators. When stratified by educational attainment, active PA patterns were more consistently associated with lower odds of hypertension among adults with lower educational attainment, although the interaction was not significant. By household income, a significant interaction was observed, with the weekend warrior pattern associated with lower odds of hypertension among lower-income adults. Taken together, these findings suggest that weekend warrior–style activity may be associated with lower odds of hypertension in adults with lower SES, although this possibility should be interpreted cautiously given the cross-sectional design of the study.

A recent meta-analysis found that the weekend warrior pattern was associated with a 23% lower risk of hypertension compared with inactivity [9], consistent with our findings. However, not all individual studies consistently confirm these associations [1011]; notably, Gahm et al. [10] found no significant association in Korean adults using KNHANES data. This discrepancy may be attributable to differences in the survey cycles included, which could reflect differences in unmeasured confounders. Taken together, although our study supports the favorable association between the weekend warrior pattern and hypertension, further research is needed to confirm these findings.

Our findings suggest that inverse associations between PA patterns and hypertension tended to be more apparent among adults with lower SES, with a significant interaction observed for household income. These results are partly consistent with previous studies indicating that the protective associations of PA may be more pronounced in lower-SES populations for both hypertension [1920] and mortality outcomes [2122]. Such socioeconomic health disparities may be explained by the greater burden of hypertension-related risk factors in disadvantaged groups, including poorer diet, psychosocial stress, obesity, lower health literacy, and time constraints [232425]. Accordingly, PA may have particular public health relevance in these populations. Taken together, these findings highlight the importance of interpreting PA-related health associations within a socioeconomic context, rather than assuming that these associations are uniform across SES groups.

Although no studies have directly examined the association between the weekend warrior pattern and hypertension stratified by SES, several studies have described how weekend warrior patterns relate to socioeconomic characteristics. Gubelmann et al. [2627] reported that weekend warriors often had more favorable SES profiles and lower odds of hypertension, yet no SES-stratified analyses were reported. Our findings raise the possibility that the weekend warrior pattern may be relevant for socioeconomically disadvantaged adults, who may be less likely to engage in regular LTPA and more likely to face long or irregular working hours, limited access to PA opportunities, and other time and resource constraints [161718]. In such settings, concentrating activity into 1 or 2 days may offer a more feasible way to accumulate meaningful weekly PA. Furthermore, the weekend warrior pattern was associated with lower odds of hypertension only among employed adults. This finding may provide additional context regarding the relevance of work-related time constraints to the weekend warrior pattern. Thus, the weekend warrior pattern may be important not only for its association with hypertension, but also for its practicality under real-world constraints.

In recent public health discourse, there has been growing recognition that a “one-size-fits-all” approach may not be sufficient for reducing health inequities [2829]. In this context, the weekend warrior pattern may be considered not simply as a second-best alternative to regularly distributed activity, but also as a pragmatic and potentially more reachable option for individuals whose daily circumstances constrain regular LTPA. Accordingly, PA promotion strategies for managing blood pressure may consider flexible and low-burden approaches that better align with real-world constraints, particularly for socioeconomically disadvantaged populations.

Observational studies have consistently reported inverse associations between PA and hypertension risk [30]. However, the cross-sectional design of our study precludes conclusions about temporality. Hypertension diagnosis may also lead individuals to change their PA behavior, raising the possibility of reverse causality. This issue is particularly relevant when interpreting the weekend warrior pattern, because hypertension status may influence both the amount and weekly distribution of PA. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the temporal relationship between PA accumulation patterns and hypertension risk.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes establishing temporality, and therefore causal inferences cannot be made from the observed associations. Second, SES was assessed using educational attainment and household income, which may not fully capture the multidimensional nature of SES. Third, PA was obtained from a self-reported questionnaire, which may have led to recall bias and misclassification of PA patterns. In addition, PA patterns were defined using LTPA only, which may have yielded different results than total PA, particularly in lower-SES groups with a greater burden of occupational PA. Fourth, the relatively small size of the weekend warrior subgroup, particularly in the SES-stratified analyses, may have limited the stability and precision of the estimated associations, warranting caution in interpretation. Fifth, measurement error and white-coat effects on blood pressure measurement cannot be ruled out. Finally, though we accounted for many potential confounders, there is a potential for residual confounding due to errors in measured confounders and relevant unmeasured confounders such as diet, stress, sedentary time, sleep, and comorbid conditions. Despite these limitations, this is the first study to evaluate the association between the weekend warrior pattern and hypertension stratified by SES, thereby adding an equity-informed perspective to the interpretation of weekend warrior–related health associations. The sample was drawn from a nationally representative survey, which strengthens the population-based relevance of the findings. Future longitudinal and device-based studies using more comprehensive SES indicators are needed to confirm these associations.

CONCLUSION

These findings suggest that SES may be an important context for interpreting the associations between PA patterns and hypertension; weekend warrior–style activity may be relevant for socioeconomically disadvantaged adults. Further studies in larger longitudinal cohorts are warranted to confirm these findings.
