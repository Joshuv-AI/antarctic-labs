Patient satisfaction with psychiatric outpatient care and associated factors among patients attending public hospitals in the Harari Region, Eastern Ethiopia
Authors: Mubarek Tuna, Mekdes Demissie, Daniel Alemu, Jerman Dereje, Abebe Seyoum, Mesfin Wubishet Gurmu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatient satisfaction is a key indicator of healthcare quality and is closely linked to treatment adherence, service utilization, and clinical outcomes in mental health care. Although access to mental health services has improved in Ethiopia, evidence regarding patient satisfaction with psychiatric outpatient services in Eastern Ethiopia remains limited. This study aimed to assess the level of patient satisfaction and identify factors associated with satisfaction among psychiatric outpatients attending public hospitals in the Harari Region.MethodsAn institution-based multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted among 422 psychiatric outpatients selected through systematic random sampling. Data were collected using a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire incorporating standardized assessment tools. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors independently associated with patient satisfaction. Statistical significance was determined at p < 0.05, and adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported.ResultsOverall, 216 participants (52.8%; 95% CI: 47.75%–57.60%) were satisfied with the psychiatric outpatient services they received. Factors independently associated with higher patient satisfaction included a diagnosis of major depressive disorder (AOR = 3.12; 95% CI: 1.62–6.02), strong social support (AOR = 3.29; 95% CI: 1.86–5.80), consistent availability of prescribed medications (AOR = 4.11; 95% CI: 2.54–6.67), payment through health insurance (AOR = 1.66; 95% CI: 1.04–2.66), and waiting times of less than 30 minutes (AOR = 4.19; 95% CI: 1.34–13.09).ConclusionSlightly more than half of psychiatric outpatients were satisfied with the services they received. Patient satisfaction was significantly influenced by clinical diagnosis, social support, medication availability, health insurance coverage, and waiting time. Interventions aimed at improving service efficiency, ensuring uninterrupted medication supply, strengthening social support systems, and expanding health insurance coverage may enhance patient satisfaction and improve the quality of psychiatric outpatient care.

Introduction

Patient satisfaction is defined as the extent to which patients perceive that healthcare services meet their expectations and effectively address their health needs. It reflects patients’ experiences with multiple aspects of care, including accessibility, quality, communication, and outcomes, and serves as a key indicator of healthcare effectiveness and service quality [1]. The World Health Organization and other global entities advocate for “health for all” and recognize patient satisfaction as a vital metric among several indicators of healthcare performance [2]. Routine assessment of patient satisfaction, such as biannual surveys, is considered an effective strategy to monitor, maintain, and improve service quality [3].

Low patient satisfaction can discourage individuals from seeking care, compromise treatment adherence, and negatively impact clinical outcomes, particularly in mental health care, where continuity of treatment is essential [4]. Psychiatric outpatient services constitute the primary mode of mental health care delivery in many low- and middle-income countries, including Ethiopia. These services aim to provide continuous treatment, medication management, and psychosocial support while enabling patients to remain integrated within their communities [5]. Patient satisfaction in psychiatric care has been shown to correlate closely with timely access to services, respectful provider-patient interactions, adequacy of information provided, and effective communication [6]. Satisfied patients are more likely to adhere to treatment plans, attend follow-up visits, and maintain continuity of care, whereas dissatisfaction is associated with missed appointments, treatment discontinuation, and relapse [7].

Different studies conducted among mental health outpatient satisfaction showed that the global level of satisfaction ranges from 50.3% to 93.1% [8, 9]. Factors consistently influencing satisfaction include waiting time, medication availability, cost of services, adequacy of information, quality of care, facility environment, and social support [10–13]. This study is framed by the Andersen Behavioral Model Services, which posits that healthcare utilization (and derived satisfaction) stems from predisposing factors (e.g., diagnosis, demographics), enabling factors (e.g., insurance, medication availability, waiting times), and need factors (e.g., illness severity) [13]. Despite the growing recognition of patient satisfaction as a critical measure of healthcare quality, evidence in Eastern Ethiopia is limited, and prior studies often lack theoretical grounding. Assessing satisfaction in the Harari Region, with its unique demographic, sociocultural, and healthcare context, is essential to identify service gaps and guide targeted interventions. Understanding the interplay of clinical presentation, social support, and service-related factors will inform strategies to optimize patient-centered psychiatric care and improve treatment adherence and outcomes. This study evaluates the level of patient satisfaction and identifies determinants among psychiatric outpatients in public hospitals of the Harari Region. By integrating clinical characteristics, psychosocial factors, and service-related determinants within a conceptual framework, the study aims to provide actionable insights to improve mental health service quality and patient outcomes in Ethiopia.

Methods and materials

Study setting, design, and period

An institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted from June 1 to 30, 2024, at Hiwot Fana Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (HFCSH) and Jugal General Hospital (JGH) in the Harari Regional State, Eastern Ethiopia. Harar, the regional capital, is located 515 km southeast of Addis Ababa and covers a catchment area of 304.5 km², divided into nine districts and 36 kebeles. Both hospitals represent the only two public facilities providing dedicated psychiatric services in the region. HFCSH is a teaching and referral hospital affiliated with Haramaya University, with 210 beds and over 500 staff, and has provided inpatient and outpatient psychiatric care for nearly 40 years. JGH, established in 1956, operates a psychiatric outpatient department staffed by two psychiatrists and serves approximately 150 patients per month.

Population

The source population comprised all patients receiving psychiatric outpatient services at HFCSH and JGH. The study population included those who attended these services during the data collection period (June 1–30, 2024).

Eligibility criteria

Patients aged 18 years or older who had attended the psychiatric outpatient department at least three times were included. Patients with communication impairments (e.g., inability to speak or hear) or those with severe mental or medical conditions preventing participation were excluded.

Sample size determination and sampling procedures

The sample size was initially determined using the single population proportion formula based on a previous Ethiopian study conducted at Jimma University Medical Center, which reported a patient satisfaction prevalence of 50.3% [14]. Assuming a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error, and a 10% non-response rate, the final sample size was calculated as 422 participants.

The total sample size was proportionally allocated to the two hospitals based on the average monthly psychiatric outpatient flow obtained from the hospitals’ Health Management Information System (HMIS) reports. According to HMIS data, HFCSH and JGH serve approximately 720 and 150 psychiatric outpatients per month, respectively, giving a total estimated source population of 870 patients during the study period. A systematic random sampling technique was employed to select study participants. The sampling interval (K) was determined using the formula, K = N/n = 870/422 ≈ 2. Accordingly, every second eligible patient attending psychiatric outpatient follow-up was recruited. The first participant in each hospital was selected using a lottery method to ensure randomness, after which every second patient was included until the required sample size was reached (Fig. 1). Systematic sampling was selected because the outpatient clinics had a continuous and orderly patient flow during the study period, making this method operationally feasible and efficient while ensuring adequate representativeness of the study population. Compared with simple random sampling, systematic sampling reduced logistical complexity and minimized interruptions to routine clinical services.

Data collection tools and procedures

Data were collected through structured face-to-face exit interviews administered by four BSc-level psychiatry professionals, supervised by one BSc psychiatry supervisor. To minimize social desirability bias, none of the data collectors or the supervisor were employees of the study hospitals. All were fluent in both Amharic and Afaan Oromo, the primary languages of the study population. The structured questionnaire encompassed four domains: (i) socio-demographic characteristics; (ii) clinical factors; (iii) service-related factors; and (iv) psychosocial factors. The instruments used within each domain are described below and in the Study Variables subsection.

Measurement instruments

Patient Satisfaction (CSQ-8): Patient satisfaction, the dependent variable, was measured using the Client Satisfaction Questionnaire (CSQ-8), a widely used, validated eight-item instrument scored on a four-point Likert scale, yielding total scores ranging from 8 to 32. A score of ≥ 16 was classified as satisfied, and a score of < 16 as dissatisfied [15]. Although the CSQ-8 produces an ordinal score, this score was dichotomized to create the binary dependent variable for logistic regression, which is consistent with common practice in patient satisfaction research and enables the estimation of odds ratios for clinically interpretable comparisons [15]. The CSQ-8 has been validated in Ethiopia and demonstrated excellent internal consistency in this study (Cronbach’s α = 0.95), confirming its reliability in the current setting [16].

Social Support (OSSS-3): Social support was assessed using the Oslo 3-item Social Support Scale (OSSS-3), a validated instrument in African and low-to-middle-income country settings [17]. The scale consists of three items, with total scores ranging from 3 to 14. Scores are categorized into three levels: poor social support (3–8), moderate social support (9–11), and strong social support (12–14). Total scores range from 3 to 14 and are categorized as poor (3–8), moderate (9–11), or strong (12–14) social support. The scale demonstrated good internal consistency in the pre-test (Cronbach’s α = 0.89) with 86% sensitivity and 67% specificity [18].

Social Functioning (SFQ): Social functioning was assessed using the Social Functioning Questionnaire (SFQ), which has been validated and used in Ethiopian psychiatric settings [19]. Scores ≥ 10 were classified as poor social functioning; scores < 10 indicated good social functioning. The pre-test confirmed adequate internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.80).

Substance Use (ASSIST): Current substance use was assessed using the Alcohol, Smoking and Substance Involvement Screening Test (ASSIST), adopted from the WHO [20]. ‘Current substance use’ was defined as use of any substance within the preceding three months.

Illness Severity (CGI): The severity of the current psychiatric illness was assessed using the Clinical Global Impression (CGI) scale, a seven-point clinician-rated instrument validated for use in similar settings [21]. Scores of 1–3 indicated mild illness, a score of 4 indicated moderate illness, and scores of 5–7 indicated severe illness.

Monthly Income: The World Bank international poverty line of USD 1.90 per day was converted to the Ethiopian context as follows: 1.90 × 56.56 × 30 = 3,224 ETB per month [22]. Participants were classified as below or above this threshold.

Operational definitions

Satisfied: A CSQ-8 total score of ≥ 16 [15].

Dissatisfied: A CSQ-8 total score of < 16 [15].

Waiting time: The total elapsed time from patient registration to consultation with the clinician, recorded at both points [23].

First treatment modality: The initial treatment sought by the patient upon first becoming mentally ill, whether traditional (e.g., herbalists, faith healers) or modern (biomedical).

Family involvement in treatment: Active participation by individuals related to the patient by blood, marriage, adoption, or close friendship in the treatment process, including encouraging adherence, recognizing early signs of relapse, administering medication, accompanying the patient to follow-up visits, and participating in treatment-related decisions. Assessed using a single yes/no question [24].

Long duration of illness: Illness lasting more than five years [25].

Social support: Classified as strong (OSSS-3 score 12–14), moderate (9–11), or poor (3–8) [17].

Social functioning: Poor social functioning is defined as an SFQ score ≥ 10; good social functioning is defined as an SFQ score < 10 [19].

Severity of illness: The severity of the current psychiatric illness was assessed using the Clinical Global Impression (CGI) scale. Mild (CGI 1–3), moderate (CGI 4), or severe (CGI 5–7) [21].

Data quality control

The questionnaire was originally developed in English and then translated into Afaan Oromo and Amharic by professional translators. To verify conceptual equivalence, the translated versions were independently back-translated into English. A pretest was conducted on 5% of the calculated sample size (n ≈ 21) at Bisidimo Hospital one week before the main data collection. Pre-test findings confirmed good internal consistency for all instruments (Cronbach’s α: OSSS-3 = 0.89, SFQ = 0.80, CSQ-8 = 0.95), and no substantive modifications were required. Data collectors and the supervisor completed one day of training covering study objectives, ethical considerations, informed consent procedures, interview techniques, and questionnaire administration protocols. Throughout the data collection period, the principal investigator and supervisor conducted a daily review of completed questionnaires to verify accuracy, consistency, and completeness.

Data processing and analysis

Completed questionnaires were checked, coded, and entered into EpiData version 4.6, then exported to Stata version 14.0 for data cleaning and analysis. Descriptive statistics (frequencies and percentages) were used to characterize the study sample and summarize both the outcome variable and independent variables. Binary logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with patient satisfaction, the dichotomized dependent variable (satisfied/dissatisfied).

All independent variables listed above (socio-demographic, clinical, service-related, and psychosocial factors) were examined in relation to this binary outcome. Bivariable logistic regression was performed first; variables with p < 0.25, together with those identified a priori as theoretical confounders (e.g., illness severity), were retained for inclusion in the multivariable logistic regression model, irrespective of their bivariable p-value. Associations were reported as crude odds ratios (COR) and adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI); statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Model goodness-of-fit was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test (p = 0.82), indicating adequate fit. Multicollinearity among independent variables was evaluated using the variance inflation factor (VIF < 5 for all variables), indicating no problematic collinearity. Findings are presented using tables, figures, and narrative descriptions.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of study participants

A total of 409 individuals with mental illness participated in the survey, yielding a response rate of 97%. The median age of participants was 31 years (interquartile range: 12). More than half (51.3%) were female, and 44.7% were between 18 and 30 years of age. Slightly over half (51.1%) resided in rural areas. Approximately two-thirds of the participants were Muslim, and 56.2% were married. Regarding educational status, 14.4% were unable to read and write. In terms of occupation and income, only 7.6% were public employees, and 40.1% had an average monthly family income of less than 3,224 ETB. With respect to access to care, 47.2% of participants reported traveling more than 50 km to obtain mental health services (Table 2).

Clinical and social factors

Among the participants, 51.4% were diagnosed with schizophrenia. Approximately 16.9% had an illness duration of less than one year, and 60.4% had previously sought traditional treatment. All participants were receiving psychiatric medication, with over one-fourth taking more than one type of medication. Nearly half (48%) had a history of psychiatric admission, 64.3% had experienced a prior relapse, and 52% reported current substance use. Regarding social support, 30% reported strong support. One-third (33.3%) demonstrated poor social functioning, and 47% were moderately ill based on severity assessment. Comorbid medical conditions were present in 45.5% of participants, and 56.5% reported active family involvement in their treatment (Table 3).

Service-related factors

Nearly half of the patients (46%) experienced waiting times of 30–60 min. While 63.1% used health insurance, 42.3% reported challenges with medication availability. Approximately three-fourths of participants felt they received adequate information about their illness and treatment. Regarding the clinic environment and privacy, over 81% reported that the outpatient department was clean, and 83.6% indicated that their privacy was maintained during their visit. Additionally, 75.8% found the hospital’s opening hours convenient (Table 4).

Patient satisfaction

In this study, 52.8% of participants were satisfied with psychiatric outpatient care (95% CI: 47.95%–57.6%) (Fig. 2).

Factors associated with patient satisfaction

In the bivariable logistic regression analysis, eleven variables met the criteria for inclusion in the multivariable model. These variables included proximity to the hospital, diagnosis of major depressive disorder (MDD), mild illness severity, absence of previous psychiatric admission, absence of comorbid medical conditions, availability of prescribed medication, shorter waiting time, level of social support, payment method through health insurance, receipt of adequate information regarding illness and treatment, and assurance of privacy during care.

After adjustment for potential confounders, five factors remained significantly associated with patient satisfaction: diagnosis of MDD, waiting time, social support, payment method, and availability of prescribed medication. Participants diagnosed with MDD were more than three times as likely to report satisfaction with psychiatric outpatient services compared with those diagnosed with schizophrenia (AOR = 3.12; 95% CI: 1.62–6.02). Similarly, participants who waited less than 30 minutes to receive services had significantly higher odds of satisfaction than those who waited more than two hours (AOR = 4.19; 95% CI: 1.34–13.09). Participants with strong social support were more than three times as likely to be satisfied compared with those who reported poor social support (AOR = 3.29; 95% CI: 1.86–5.80). Likewise, participants with moderate social support had twice the odds of satisfaction compared with those with poor social support (AOR = 2.00; 95% CI: 1.16–3.46). Regarding healthcare financing, participants covered by health insurance were significantly more likely to be satisfied than those who paid out-of-pocket for services (AOR = 1.66; 95% CI: 1.04–2.66). Furthermore, participants who obtained their prescribed medications from the hospital pharmacy were more than four times as likely to report satisfaction compared with those who were unable to receive their medications at the facility (AOR = 4.11; 95% CI: 2.54–6.67) (Table 5).

Discussions

This multicenter cross-sectional study assessed the magnitude of patient satisfaction with psychiatric outpatient services and identified its determinants in public hospitals of the Harari Region. Overall satisfaction was 52.8% (95% CI: 47.75%–57.6%), indicating moderate levels of perceived service quality.

The observed satisfaction level is comparable to findings from Jimma (50.3%) [8, 9], Dilla (55.4%) [26], and India (57%) [27]. However, it is substantially lower than reports from Switzerland (93.1%) [9], South Africa (91%) [28], Ireland (90.6%) [29], Egypt (82.8%) [30], Nigeria (75.2%) [31], and Oman (70.6%) [32]. These discrepancies may reflect contextual differences in health system infrastructure, staffing patterns, resource allocation, and availability of alternative mental health services. High-income settings often have more developed community mental health systems, shorter waiting times, and broader psychosocial support structures, which may enhance patient experiences.

Within Ethiopia, satisfaction in the present study was lower than that reported in Gondar (65.4%) [25], Amanuel Mental Specialized Hospital (63.3%) [15], Dessie (61.2%) [24], Mekelle (70.8%) [33], and St. Paul’s Hospital (81.3%) [16]. The discrepancy may be explained by differences in the scope and quality of mental health services across hospitals. For example, St. Paul, Mekelle public hospitals, and Amanuel Mental Specialized Hospital provide more comprehensive care, including follow-up services, counseling, and social support, and they employ a greater number of specialized staff [33]. These factors may contribute to higher levels of patient satisfaction in those settings. Another possible reason for the discrepancy is the use of different assessment tools across studies. For instance, the CPOSS tool was used in Dessie, while the 24-Item Mental Health Service Satisfaction Scale (MHSSS) was used in Dilla and Jimma, which may affect the comparability of findings. Additionally, differences in service user characteristics could explain the variation. In the present study, more than half of the participants were patients with schizophrenia, who are generally less likely to report high levels of satisfaction with services [34].

In our study, individuals with schizophrenia were three times less likely to report satisfaction with psychiatric outpatient services compared to those with major depressive disorder (MDD). This finding is noteworthy, as previous studies have reported differences in satisfaction levels according to psychiatric diagnosis. For example, studies conducted in Jimma [14], Dessie [24], Rome [35], and the United States [36] have shown that patients with depression tend to report higher satisfaction with mental health services than those with more severe conditions, such as schizophrenia. One possible explanation is that psychotic disorders are more debilitating than other types of mental illness, significantly impairing patients’ perception and understanding of their external environment [37]. Additionally, schizophrenia is often associated with poorer social adjustment, greater functional impairment, and higher levels of disability, which may further reduce satisfaction with care [34].

Service-related factors played a prominent role. Waiting time of less than 30 min was associated with approximately fourfold higher satisfaction, consistent with studies conducted in Jimma [14], Mekelle [33], Dilla [26], and Bangladesh [38]. Prolonged waiting times are known to reduce perceived quality and may lead to missed consultations [39]. These findings underscore the importance of clinic organization and workflow efficiency in shaping patient experiences. Medication availability was another strong predictor of satisfaction. Participants who consistently received prescribed psychotropic medications from hospital pharmacies were significantly more satisfied. Similar findings were reported in Gondar and St. Paul’s Hospital [16, 25]. In resource-limited settings, stock-outs may force patients to purchase medications privately at higher costs, creating financial strain and undermining trust in public services [40].

Financial protection mechanisms also influenced satisfaction. Patients using health insurance were more likely to report positive experiences, consistent with findings from Oman [32], Gondar [25], Nepal [37], and St. Paul’s Hospital [41]. Out-of-pocket expenditures disproportionately affect psychiatric patients, many of whom experience unemployment or reduced earning capacity. Evidence indicates that financial barriers significantly shape healthcare experiences and satisfaction [42]. Insurance coverage may mitigate these barriers and improve continuity of care [43, 44].

Social support was strongly associated with satisfaction, in line with findings from Jimma and Gondar [14, 25] and Ghana [45]. Individuals with stronger social networks may have improved coping capacity, better treatment adherence, and more stable living conditions [46], potentially enhancing their engagement with outpatient services.

The findings of this study have clear implications for mental health policy and service delivery in Ethiopia. Improving patient satisfaction requires coordinated interventions that address health system efficiency, financial protection, medication availability, and psychosocial support. Policymakers should prioritize reducing waiting times, expanding health insurance coverage, ensuring a consistent supply of essential psychotropic medications, and strengthening family and community support within outpatient psychiatric services. In addition, tailored strategies for individuals with severe mental illnesses, particularly schizophrenia, are essential to improve equity and quality of care. Addressing these factors may enhance patient satisfaction, strengthen treatment adherence, improve continuity of care, and ultimately lead to better mental health outcomes.

Strength and limitations of the study

A key strength of this study is the use of standardized and validated assessment tools, enhancing measurement reliability. The multicenter design increases generalizability within the region. Additionally, the inclusion of psychosocial and health system variables provided a comprehensive analysis of determinants. However, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Social desirability bias may have influenced self-reported satisfaction and substance use behaviors. Recall bias may have affected responses related to illness duration and service experiences. Observer bias in clinician-rated measures such as the CGI-S scale may also have occurred. Future longitudinal or mixed-method studies could provide deeper insight into temporal and experiential dimensions of satisfaction.

Conclusion and recommendation

This study found that slightly more than half of psychiatric outpatients attending public hospitals in the Harari Region were satisfied with the services they received. Patient satisfaction was significantly associated with diagnosis of major depressive disorder, shorter waiting time, stronger social support, health insurance coverage, and the availability of prescribed psychotropic medications. These findings underscore the multifaceted nature of patient satisfaction, reflecting the combined influence of clinical, psychosocial, and healthcare system factors.

Improving patient satisfaction in psychiatric outpatient settings requires coordinated efforts at both facility and health system levels. Health facilities should prioritize reducing waiting times through improved clinic workflow management, structured appointment scheduling, and efficient triage processes. Ensuring the continuous availability of essential psychotropic medications through strengthened procurement, supply chain management, and inventory control is equally important. Expanding enrollment in health insurance schemes may enhance financial protection and reduce the economic burden associated with mental health care. In addition, integrating psychosocial support services into routine outpatient care and promoting family involvement in treatment may contribute to improved patient experiences and satisfaction. Particular attention should be given to patients with severe mental illnesses, especially schizophrenia, through targeted interventions designed to enhance engagement, continuity of care, and perceived service quality. Future studies employing longitudinal and qualitative methodologies are warranted to provide deeper insights into causal relationships and patient experiences. Addressing these factors may contribute to improved patient satisfaction, better treatment adherence, and enhanced mental health outcomes in resource-constrained settings.
