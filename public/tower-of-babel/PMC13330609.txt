Lactobacilli, best allies of mental health: a probiogenomic approach to identify potential psychobiotic strains
Authors: Chiara Maria Calvanese, Vincenzo Valentino, Giuseppina Sequino, Alessandra De Vivo, Davide Buzzanca, Simona Prencipe, Chiara Demarinis, Giuseppe Perri, Erica Pontonio, Ilario Ferrocino, Danilo Ercolini, Francesca De Filippis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Targeted dietary strategies and supplements represent a promising approach for the treatment of cognitive problems. Multi-omic approaches may facilitate and accelerate the discovery of new psychobiotic strains and their applications. In this work, we applied metagenomics and comparative genomics to guide the isolation and screening of novel psychobiotic strains from fermented foods. Metagenomes of 1185 fermented food were screened, revealing the occurrence of genes coding for the biosynthesis of neuroactive molecules, supporting the isolation of 73 novel Lactic Acid Bacteria (LAB) strains. Comparative genomic analysis highlighted species-specific patterns, identifying Levilactobacillus brevis, Lactiplantibacillus plantarum, Limosilactobacillus fermentum as potential psychobiotics. In vitro functional screening for the production of neuroactive metabolites confirmed four strains, Lactiplantibacillus plantarum TUCC00000144, Limosilactobacillus fermentum TUCC00000777, Levilactobacillus brevis TO10, Lentilactobacillus diolivorans B92, as the most promising candidates for the development of dietary supplements or innovative fermented food products aimed at supporting mental health.

Introduction

The gut microbiome is intimately involved in multiple aspects of host physiology and well-being throughout life (Ross et al., 2024). In this context, dietary modulation of the gut microbiome plays a critical role in influencing human health and may support the prevention and treatment of several chronic diseases (Ross et al., 2024). Fermented foods (FFs) have a long and safe history of use, although the type of matrix, strains and process has to be taken into account. Several FFs may be considered functional foods, since they can exert several health benefits widely reviewed elsewhere (Berding et al., 2023; Valentino et al., 2024). FFs are defined by the International Scientific Association on Probiotics and Prebiotics (ISAPP) as “foods made through the desired microbial growth and enzymatic conversions of food components” (Marco et al., 2021). Among other benefits, a diet rich in FFs has been associated with improved mood and cognition. Berding et al. (2023) conducted a single-blind, randomized, controlled study in adults and demonstrated that a “psychobiotic” diet rich in fibre and fermented foods significantly lowered perceived stress levels. Indeed, the microbiome of fermented foods may be considered a source of psychobiotics, a specific group of probiotic strains that has a positive impact on psychological and neurological endpoints, studied for their ability to influence the gut-brain axis (Berding et al., 2023). Consistently, milk kefir consumption improved cognitive function (memory, language, and abstraction) in 13 patients with Alzheimer's disease, significantly reducing inflammation and systemic oxidative stress (Ton et al., 2020), while milk and soy fermented with strains of Lactobacillus helveticus and Lactiplantibacillus plantarum have been shown to improve attention and cognitive functions (Chung et al., 2014; Ohsawa et al., 2018; Hwang et al., 2019).

The gut-brain axis describes the bidirectional communication between the digestive and the nervous system (Cryan et al., 2019). In recent years, the gut microbiome emerged as a crucial regulator of this axis, leading to the expansion of the term to the microbiota-gut-brain axis. This axis is increasingly recognized as a significant factor in the biological and physiological basis of various mental health conditions, including psychiatric, neurodevelopmental, age-related, and neurodegenerative disorders. The microbiome and the brain interact through multiple pathways, including the vagus nerve and the enteric nervous system, involving microbial metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) (Cryan et al., 2019). Both gut and FF microbes can synthesize neuroactive compounds, like γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), acetylcholine, serotonin, and dopamine, converting precursors present in food matrices (glutamate, choline, tryptophan, and tyrosine) into these neurochemicals (Casertano et al., 2022).

GABA is an inhibitory neurotransmitter produced via the enzyme glutamate decarboxylase, able to directly influence the gut-brain axis, by modulating the enteric nervous system and potentially entering systemic circulation (Casertano et al., 2024). Tryptophan, an essential amino acid, serves as a precursor for serotonin, a neurotransmitter vital for mood, appetite, and gut motility. Other tryptophan metabolites, such as indole derivatives, play a key role in strengthening the intestinal barrier (Qin et al., 2025). Tyramine, produced by lactic acid bacteria (LAB), may influence mood by stimulating norepinephrine release, while propionate acts as a neuroprotective agent capable of crossing the blood-brain barrier (Lombardi and Dicks, 2022). Several in vivo and in vitro studies have demonstrated that LAB can produce neuroactive molecules. Levilactobacillus brevis P30021 and Lactiplantibacillus plantarum P30025 were selected for their ability to produce GABA and acetylcholine in vitro (Casertano et al., 2024). A four-weeks supplementation with the strain Bifidobacterium breve CCFM1025 in patients with major depression significantly attenuated psychiatric and gastrointestinal abnormalities, potentially through regulation of the serotonin system and tryptophan metabolism in the gut microbiome (Tian et al., 2022).

It is well known that validating probiotics is complex due to lack of standardization in traditional in vitro and in vivo test batteries, which often do not accurately reflect real-world efficacy or safety. Moreover, probiotic strains can act through multiple mechanisms making their function hard to predict. To address these challenges, probiogenomics has emerged (Castro-López et al., 2021) as a holistic strategy that combines advanced “omics” technologies to thoroughly analyze potential probiotic strains, thereby accelerating the screening of multiple candidates.

In this study, we developed a probiogenomic pipeline to facilitate the selection and characterization of novel potential psychobiotics from fermented foods, coupling bioinformatic approaches to functional in vitro screening of candidate strains, speeding up the selection of psychobiotic candidates.

Materials and methods

Development of a custom gene database related to psychobiotic activity

We selected key genes involved in the production of neuroactive compounds by performing a comprehensive search on the Scopus database (December 2024). The search was conducted using specific Boolean operators to combine keywords: (“psychobiotic enzymes” OR “GABA enzymes” OR “tryptophan pathway” OR “neurotransmitters” OR “microbial neurotransmitters production” OR “gut-brain axis” OR “psychobiotic metabolites”). The search was restricted to articles written in English and limited to peer-reviewed journals. We excluded conference abstracts and editorials to ensure data robustness. The sequences of the identified genes were retrieved from the NCBI GenBank database (accessed January 2025) and used to build our custom database (Supplementary Table 1).

In silico screening of food metagenomes for neuroactive potential

A total of 1185 publicly available metagenomes from fermented beverages, grains, meats, vegetables, and cheeses (fresh, ripened and blue-veined cheeses) (accession numbers of metagenomics reads are reported in Supplementary Table 2) were screened for neuroactive potential. Reads were quality-filtered using PRINSEQ 0.20.4 (Schmieder and Edwards, 2011), trimming bases having a Phred score <15 and discarding reads <75 bp. High-quality reads were independently assembled using MEGAHIT v1.2.2 (Li et al., 2015; options: -m 0.85 –min-contig-len 1000 –k-list 21,33,55,71,81,91) and contigs >1000 bp were used to predict genes by using MetaGeneMark v 3.26 (Zhu et al., 2010). Predicted genes were aligned using DIAMOND v2.0.4 with the option –very-sensitive (Buchfink et al., 2014) against the reference database (Supplementary Table 1). An e-value cutoff of 1e−5 was applied, and a hit was required to display >50% of identity over at least 50% of the query length. Data visualization and statistical analysis were performed in R environment (version 4.1.3; https://www.r-project.org). A presence–absence heatmap was then generated using the “pheatmap” function from the “pheatmap” R package using the “euclidean” distance and the “ward” clustering method.

Strain isolation from fermented foods and genome sequencing

Lactic acid bacteria (LAB) isolation was carried out from 70 different FFs (Table 1), selected to include a diversity of food matrices. LAB isolation was carried out on MRS agar (de Man Rogosa Sharpe Agar, Oxoid) supplemented with 0.05% (w/v) of L-cysteine-HCl. Plates were incubated both at 30°C and 40°C for 48 h in anaerobic conditions (anaerobic gas generation sachets, Thermo Scientific™ Oxoid) for the isolation of mesophilic and thermophilic LAB, respectively. Three morphologically different colonies per sample were selected and streaked on the same medium for purification and preliminary identification. Only Gram-positive, catalase negative strains were identified by MALDI-TOF Sirius Biotyper (Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionization Time-Of-Flight; Bruker), according to the manufacturer's standard protocol. Briefly, 1 μL of 70% (v/v) formic acid solution in water and 1 μL of α-cyano-4-hydrocinnamic acid matrix solution (Bruker, USA) were added directly onto the colonies and allowed to air dry. Spectra were analyzed with Bruker Biotyper Compass HT RUO software 5.1.3 with the MBT Compass Library 12.0. Calibration was performed for each target plate using Bruker Bacterial Test Standard (BTS). To ensure confidence in the identification of bacterial taxa from samples, a log score of spectral similarity ≥1.9 was considered. Cultures were stored frozen at −80°C with glycerol as cryoprotectant (30% v/v). Genomic DNA was extracted using the Wizard® Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Promega) following the manufacturer's instructions and quantified by Qubit fluorometer. Whole-genome shotgun sequencing was performed by Novogene (UK) using Illumina NovaSeq platform (San Diego, CA, USA), generating 2 x 150 bp paired-end reads. In vitro screening for the production of neuroactive molecules

Based on genome analysis, 18 strains showing the most promising genomic features were selected for in vitro testing. The selected strains were cultured overnight in MRS broth (Oxoid) at the optimal growth temperature of each strain. Based on a methodology previously published (Casertano et al., 2024), fresh cultures were then inoculated (1% v/v) into MRS broth supplemented with 0.05% (w/v) L-cysteine-HCl and specific amino acid precursors to induce neurotransmitter production. The media were supplemented with one of the following precursors: monosodium glutamate (60 mM) for GABA production, tyrosine (1.3 mM) for catecholamines, choline (48 mM) for acetylcholine, tryptophan (39 mM) for serotonin. Pyridoxal-5′-phosphate (0.25 mM) was added as a cofactor to the media for tryptophan and tyrosine to facilitate the decarboxylation reaction. Cultures were incubated for 48 h at the optimal growth temperature of each strain. The same broths with precursors but without the inoculated strain were used as blank controls. Cells were removed by centrifugation (10,000 x g for 5 min, 4°C) and the supernatant was used for metabolites analysis. For each strain/precursor combination, we analyzed 3 replicate tubes.

Neuroactive compounds analysis

Chemicals and reagents

Metabolomic analysis was carried out by Theoreo, Montecorvino Pugliano, Italy. All solvents used were of LC-MS grade. Water, acetonitrile, and methanol were purchased from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany), while formic acid (≥99%) and analytical standards of GABA, serotonin, acetylcholine, tryptophan, epinephrine, norepinephrine, and dopamine were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Isopropylmalic acid was used as internal standard for normalization.

Sample preparation

GABA, serotonin, acetylcholine and tryptophan (50 μL) were thawed on ice and subjected to protein precipitation by adding 200 μL of cold acetonitrile containing the internal standard. Samples were vortex-mixed and incubated at −20°C for 20 min to enhance protein precipitation. After centrifugation at 16,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C, the supernatant was collected and diluted with acetonitrile to maintain a high organic content suitable for hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC). The final extracts were transferred to LC-MS vials for analysis.

Catecholamines were extracted using an alumina-based solid-phase extraction protocol. Briefly, 50 μL of sample were mixed with 30 μL of buffer solution containing 1 mol/L Tris and 0.05 mol/L EDTA. The mixture was loaded onto a 96-well alumina extraction plate (Waters), previously conditioned with acetonitrile, aqueous ammonia, and buffer solution. After sample loading, the plate was washed sequentially with buffer and water to remove interfering compounds. Catecholamines were then eluted using a solution of 2.5% formic acid in acetonitrile. The eluate was evaporated under a gentle stream of nitrogen and reconstituted in 80% acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid to ensure compatibility with HILIC chromatographic conditions.

LC-HRMS analysis

Chromatographic separation for all analytes was performed using a Vanquish UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) coupled to a Q Exactive Orbitrap high-resolution mass spectrometer equipped with a heated electrospray ionization (HESI) source. A HILIC column (ACE 3 HILIC-A, 150 × 2.1 mm, 3 μm particle size, 100 Å pore size) was employed and maintained at 30°C. The mobile phase consisted of water with 0.1% formic acid as phase A and acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid as phase B, with a constant flow rate of 0.4 mL/min.

The gradient program was optimized to ensure adequate retention and separation of polar metabolites. The initial conditions were set at 95% B and maintained for 1 min, followed by a linear decrease to 50% B over 7 min, then further decreased to 20% B within 1 min. The system was subsequently returned to initial conditions and allowed to re-equilibrate, resulting in a total run time of 12 min. For catecholamines, a slightly steeper gradient was applied, consisting of a decrease from 95% B to 40% B over 5 min, followed by a reduction to 20% B within 1 min, and re-equilibration under initial conditions, with a total run time of 10 min.

Mass spectrometric detection was carried out in positive ionization mode. The spray voltage was set at 3.5 kV, the capillary temperature at 320°C, and the auxiliary gas heater temperature at 300°C. Sheath gas, auxiliary gas, and sweep gas were set to 35, 10, and 1 arbitrary units, respectively.

Data acquisition was performed in full-scan mode at a resolution of 60,000 FWHM (at m/z 200), within a mass range of m/z 70–300. The automatic gain control target was set to 1 × 106, with a maximum injection time of 100 ms. Targeted confirmation was achieved using parallel reaction monitoring (PRM), with a resolution of 30,000 FWHM, an AGC target of 2 × 105, a maximum injection time of 60 ms, and an isolation window of 1.0 m/z. Fragmentation was performed using stepped normalized collision energies ranging from 20 to 30 eV.

Quantification was carried out using external calibration curves consisting of ten concentration levels, each analyzed in triplicate. Data processing and peak integration were performed using Xcalibur software (Thermo Fisher Scientific), applying a strict mass accuracy threshold of less than 5 ppm.

Results

Functional profile of various fermented foods harbor potential psychobiotic microbiome

We screened 1185 fermented food metagenomes for the presence of genes related to the biosynthesis of neuroactive molecules (Fig. 1). The fermented foods analyzed included a wide range of matrices: fermented beverages, such as coffee, kombucha, water kefir; fermented grains, fermented meats, fermented vegetables, fresh cheeses (mozzarella, asiago, other cheeses), ripened cheeses (e.g., pasta-filata ripened cheeses, such as provolone and caciocavallo; pecorino; fontina; long-ripened cheeses, such as Grana Padano and Parmigiano Reggiano); blue-veined cheeses. Distinct patterns of gene occurrence were observed in fermented foods types, reflecting matrix-specific clustering. Notably, ripened cheeses formed a distinct cluster characterized by a high diffusion of glutamate decarboxylase (GAD), the key enzyme in the GABA biosynthetic pathway. Identification of LAB isolates: results of MALDI-TOF and genomic confirmation (WGS)

A total of 73 LAB strains were isolated from the fermented analyzed. Strains identification was initially performed using MALDI-TOF Biotyper. To confirm these results, whole-genome sequencing (WGS) was subsequentially carried out. In a few cases, genome-based identification did not confirm the taxonomic assignment obtained by MALDI-TOF Biotyper (Table 3). The overall taxonomic discordance (10.96%) was primarily driven by species-level discrepancies (8.22%), particularly within phylogenetically close groups like Weissella and Furfurilactobacillus, where proteomic profiles often overlap. Genus-level discordance was minimal (2.74%) and largely reflected the recent taxonomic reclassification of the former Lactobacillus genus (e.g., Lactiplantibacillus vs. Lentilactobacillus (Zheng et al., 2020);). Comparative genomic analysis of neuroactive-related gene distribution across fermented food-associated LAB species

A total of 3935 LAB genomes belonging to 22 species were downloaded from the NCBI database and screened for the presence of 86 genes related to the biosynthesis of neuroactive compounds. Diverse species-specific gene occurrence patterns were observed (Fig. 2). A high prevalence of the GAD gene was detected in Levilactobacillus brevis and Lactiplantibacillus plantarum genomes, with 96% and 99% of the analyzed genomes harboring this gene, respectively. The kynurenine (KYN), indole, and serotonin pathways represent the three main metabolic pathways of tryptophan. A high occurrence of genes involved in tryptophan catabolism was found in species belonging to the genera Lacticaseibacillus, Lentilactobacillus and Lactiplantibacillus. The gene coding for tryptophan synthase (enzyme that catalyzes the final two steps of the tryptophan production pathway) was present in several species, including Lp. plantarum, Leuconostoc mesenteroides, Lc. rhamnosus, Lentilac. diolivorans, Lq. nagelii, Lp. pentosus, Lentilac. kefiri, Lc. casei, Lc. paracasei, with an occurrence ranging from 80 to 100% of the genomes analyzed (Fig. 2).

The tryptophan decarboxylase, which directly convert tryptophan to tryptamine, was not detected. However, Lentilac. kefiri, Lentilac. diolivorans and Lp. pentosus harbored the tryptophan 2-monooxygenase gene, which catalyze the conversion of tryptophan into indole derivatives, like indole-3-acetic acid, which modulate the gut barrier and host immune responses. Additionally, Lm. fermentum, W. confusa, W. cibaria, Leuc. mesenteroides, L. delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus and P. acidilactici showed high prevalence of aromatic amino acid aminotransferase, which catalyzes the transamination of tryptophan, tyrosine and phenylalanine. On the contrary, linoleate isomerase, the enzyme responsible for converting linoleic acid (LA) into conjugated linoleic acid (CLA), was highly prevalent (98-100%) only in L. helveticus, W. paramesenteroides and W. confusa, with a moderate occurrence (59%) was also observed in the species L. delbrueckii subsp. lactis. Although CLA is not a neurotransmitter, it plays an indirect but vital role in brain health through modulating the gut-brain axis.

A high occurrence (93-100%) of fumarate hydratase and malate dehydrogenase, both enzymes involved in the propionate pathway, was also observed (Fig. 2). In contrast, tyrosine decarboxylase, which converts tyrosine into tyramine, was found only in few genomes of L. delbrueckii subsp. lactis (24%) and Lt. curvatus (36%).

However, we have to point out that the ability to produce neuroactive compounds is strain-specific. Nevertheless, the spreading of the genomic traits in the different species may still be useful to drive targeted isolation of potential psychobiotic strains.

Strain-specific pattern for psychobiotic activity

Considering the most promising species with the highest occurrence of genes related to the production of neuroactive compounds, we carried out LAB isolation from traditional Italian fermented foods (Table 1). Strains were preliminary identified by MALDI-TOF, and those belonging to the most interesting species (as defined in Section 3.3) were retained for further analyses. After this screening, WGS was carried out on 73 strains (Table 3). The genomes of the isolates were screened for the presence of the same genes involved in neuroactive compounds production (Supplementary Table 1). As shown in Fig. 3, a species-specific pattern was observed. Pediococcus, F. milii, and Lm. fermentum did not show the linoleate isomerase occurrence (Fig. 3). Similarly, the propionate biosynthesis pathway appeared widespread among the isolates, with a high occurrence of fumarate hydratase and malate dehydrogenase, whereas methylmalonyl-CoA epimerase and methylmalonyl-CoA mutase were only observed in few strains, mainly Pediococcus spp. (Fig. 3). Discussion

Recent advances in gut-brain axis research have shed light on the intricate relationships between the gastrointestinal system and cognitive functions. The gut microbiome plays a crucial role in modulating brain health and human behavior through various pathways, including the production of neuroactive molecules, regulation of inflammation, and influence on the immune system (Asnicar et al., 2020). Fermented foods represent a natural source of potential psychobiotic strains. Literature suggests that targeted dietary strategies, including a diet rich in fermented foods, could contribute to treat cognitive disorders, going beyond traditional pharmacological interventions (Berding et al., 2023).

In this study, we applied a comprehensive metagenomic and probiogenomic approach to support and accelerate the discovery of new potentially psychobiotic strains from fermented foods. First, we screened 1185 fermented food metagenomes, revealing the functional potential of fermented food microbiome in neuroactive compounds biosynthesis and supporting recent findings on the psychobiotic role of a diet rich in fermented foods (Berding et al., 2023). Genes coding for neurotransmitter-related pathways were particularly abundant in cheese metagenomes, in fermented grains, and fermented beverages. This data reinforces the idea that incorporating regional fermented foods into the diet is a straightforward approach to diversifying the gut microbiota, ultimately promoting gut-brain health (Śliwka et al., 2025). Second, a broad genomic screening was carried out, including 3935 publicly available genomes belonging to 22 LAB species. Comparative genomic analysis suggested that only few species (Lv. brevis, Lp. plantarum, Lm. fermentum) are potentially enriched in neuroactive-related genes included in our database (Supplementary Table 1) although we acknowledge that the psychobiotic phenotype is strictly strain-dependent. These results guided the subsequent phase of isolation and in vitro characterization of LAB strains.

We isolated 73 LAB strains from fermented foods and carried out whole-genome sequencing. Genomic analysis showed a species-specific pattern. All 10 Lp. plantarum strains stood out for the occurrence of the GAD gene, which is crucial for GABA production. Other strains, such as L. delbrueckii (H2), Lentilac. kefiri (KL), Lm. fermentum (TUCC00000777, TUCC00000776) and Lentilac. diolivorans (A1, B92) also showed specific potential for propionate and tryptophan metabolism. The results of the genomic analysis guided the in vitro functional screening for production of neuroactive metabolites (GABA, serotonin, acetylcholine and catecholamine) from their respective precursors, resulting in 18 most promising strains. Acetylcholine production was detected for all the strains tested, while GABA and catecholamine production were detected for four and three strains, respectively. Previous studies have reported the beneficial activity of strains belonging to these species, suggesting their potential as psychobiotic candidates. In a human Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT) with a strain of Lp. plantarum, a significant reduction in anxiety and improvements in self-reported insomnia symptoms were observed in subjects experiencing a stressful lifestyle (Ho et al., 2021). An in vitro study demonstrated that Lm. fermentum L18 strain is an effective GABA producer and can significantly strengthen the gut epithelial barrier by increasing tight junction proteins in Caco-2 cell models (Kaur et al., 2023). Moreover, Lv. brevis has been reported to produce GABA, alleviate depression-like behaviors and reduce and neuroinflammation in a mouse model of chronic stress (Kim et al., 2024).

Therefore, based on these results, the four strains (Lactiplantibacillus plantarum TUCC00000144, Limosilactobacillus fermentum TUCC00000777, Levilactobacillus brevis TO10, Lentilactobacillus diolivorans B92) are considered as the most promising for developing dietary supplements or innovative food products targeting mental health. Despite the identification of strains enriched in neuroactive-related genes, we have to distinguish between genomic potential and effective activity. It is important to emphasize that the strains tested in the in vitro screening reflect the biotransformation capacity of added precursors, often available from the diet, into their respective neuroactive molecules, rather than de novo synthesis. Our in vitro results confirm that specific isolates are metabolically active under the tested conditions; however, further in vivo studies are needed to assess the effective production in a real and complex microbial community.

In conclusion, we provided a comprehensive pipeline for the use of probiogenomics to practically support the identification of novel pro- and psychobiotic strains. The approach, which exploits comparative genomics to drive isolation and in vitro testing, enabled the targeted and efficient selection of potential psychobiotic strains. By providing a thorough understanding of a strain genetic potential, probiogenomics goes beyond traditional and time-consuming phenotypic screening. The ability to correlate a strain's genetic profile with its functional activity fasten targeted screening of most promising strains and minimizes the risk of selecting ineffective candidates. However, we have to point out that the presence of the genomic trait and the production of the neuroactive compounds in vitro, does not imply the effective expression of these phenotypes in vivo. Indeed, further in vivo animal and human trials will be necessary to confirm the psychobiotic activity. Translating these discoveries into solid clinical solutions remains difficult. To bridge this gap and develop targeted psychobiotic therapies, future human studies must use advanced strain tracking techniques and standardized protocols (Śliwka et al., 2025). While integrating these findings into dietary supplements and functional foods is a realistic goal, it strictly depends on conducting long-term safety assessments and real-world trials to overcome current methodological limitations (Owusu Kyei-Baffour et al., 2025).
