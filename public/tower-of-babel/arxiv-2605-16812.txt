Jacobian-Guided Anisotropic Noise Reshaping for Enhancing Representation Utility under Local Differential Privacy
Youngmok Ha, Viktor Schlegel, Yidan Sun, Anil Anthony Bharath

Abstract
While Local Differential Privacy (LDP) serves as a foundational primitive for distributed data collection, its stringent noise injection requirement often leads to severe degradation in data utility. This degradation stems from the task-agnostic nature of conventional LDP mechanisms, which inject noise uniformly across all dimensions regardless of their relative importance to the downstream objective. To address this issue, we propose a novel approach that mitigates noise in task-relevant subspaces of the data representation. Our method identifies task-critical subspaces via the Jacobian matrix of the public downstream model, selectively attenuates noise along those dimensions, and reshapes the isotropic noise of standard LDP into an anisotropic distribution. This method preserves the uniform per-dimension privacy budget while heterogeneously modulating noise impact across dimensions, thereby substantially enhancing data utility. Furthermore, our approach generalizes to both linear and non-linear models and integrates seamlessly with existing mechanisms. Extensive experiments on CIFAR-10-C (Brightness corruption at the highest severity level 5) demonstrate that integrating our approach improves the utility of PrivUnit2 and PrivUnitG by approximately 20% at ϵ = 7.5 \epsilon=7.5 . The source code is available at https://github.com/ymha/jacobian-anr-ldp .

Introduction

Local Differential Privacy (LDP)
(
35
)
has become a foundational primitive for privacy-preserving distributed data collection.
Unlike centralized approaches
(
20
)
, LDP eliminates the need for a “trusted data curator” by enabling each data owner to locally randomize their data prior to sharing.
Owing to this decentralized trust model, it has seen widespread adoption in practice.
Prominent examples include Google’s RAPPOR for collecting statistics from Chrome clients
(
23
)
,
Apple’s deployment in iOS for collecting new word suggestions, emoji usage frequencies, and health data statistics
(

(

A well-known drawback of LDP is the severe degradation of data utility due to its heavy randomization
(
18
;
34
)
.
To provide rigorous privacy guarantees, standard LDP mechanisms enforce a pessimistic randomization process.
Specifically, since the privacy guarantee must hold for any arbitrary points over the entire private data space, the magnitude of randomness is governed by the worst-case sensitivity.
Although this prevents inferring any individual’s true input, it significantly lowers the signal-to-noise ratio and distorts the original characteristics of the data.
As a result, downstream tasks that require fine-grained feature preservation remain challenging under the standard LDP paradigm
(
19
;

)
.

To mitigate this privacy-utility trade-off, a substantial body of literature has explored numerous directions.
These efforts encompass a wide range of approaches, including the optimization of mechanisms

and the “shuffle model”

Furthermore, to allocate privacy budgets and noise more effectively, researchers have proposed strategies, such as task-aware
(

, relevance-aware
(

)
, coordinate-wise
(

;

, and correlation-aware methods
(

.
Finally, the literature extensively covers the design of post-processing methodologies
(

;

;

;

;

;

;

;

;

)

(

;

)
to further enhance downstream utility while preserving privacy.

However, enhancing utility through the analysis of downstream models remains underexplored.
Although Cheng et al.
(
9
)
investigated this direction, their methodology is predominantly centered around linear models and requires the direct use of private data for offline training.
Furthermore, it does not fully utilize geometric principles.
For example, the data representation space can be partitioned into a task-relevant row space and a task-irrelevant null space.
These subspaces, which are distinct from the subspace reflecting the primary data correlations
(
5
)
, dictate the directions in which the model is sensitive or robust to perturbations.
While identifying and exploiting these subspaces can enhance utility, the existing LDP literature has largely overlooked these structural properties.

In this paper, we propose a novel method to enhance utility under LDP by reducing the noise injected into task-critical subspaces defined by downstream models.
Our approach is motivated by the geometric insight that Jacobian-based analysis can identify task-sensitive and task-insensitive subspaces, thereby enabling fine-grained control over the noise injected into each subspace.
Furthermore, we exploit the observation that, under an isotropic privacy budget allocation, sensitivity modulation can effectively reduce the noise scale applied to a specific subspace.
Building on these principles, we introduce a method that reshapes standard isotropic noise into an anisotropic distribution.
The anisotropic noise reshaping does not incur any additional privacy leakage because it is a bijective post-processing procedure.

To the best of our knowledge, our work pioneers the enhancement of utility under LDP through Jacobian-based row space identification and anisotropic noise reshaping.
A key advantage of our approach is its applicability to both linear and non-linear downstream models without incurring additional privacy leakage.
Moreover, it can be seamlessly integrated with other advanced mechanisms.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Row Space and Null Space

Suppose that we have an
m
-dimensional data representation
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and a linear transformation matrix
\boldsymbol{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{l\times m}
, where
m,l\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
.
The row space and null space of
\boldsymbol{W}
are two subspaces that together form an orthogonal decomposition of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
The row space,
\text{Row}(\boldsymbol{W})
, is spanned by the row vectors of
\boldsymbol{W}
and captures the directions along which the transformation has a non-trivial effect.
In contrast, the null space,
\text{Null}(\boldsymbol{W})=\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\mid\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{z}=\mathbf{0}\}
, consists of all vectors annihilated by the transformation.
These two subspaces characterize which components of a representation are task-relevant (e.g., for element-wise summation, linear regression and classification) and task-irrelevant, respectively.

Consider a linear task function
\mathcal{T}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{l}
, which applies a weight matrix
\boldsymbol{W}
to a representation
\boldsymbol{z}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{T}(\boldsymbol{z})=\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{z}
.
Any representation
\boldsymbol{z}
can be decomposed into
\boldsymbol{z}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
belonging to
\text{Row}(\boldsymbol{W})
, and
\boldsymbol{z}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
belonging to
\text{Null}(\boldsymbol{W})
, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{z}_{r}+\boldsymbol{z}_{n}
.
The linear operation then unfolds as
\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{W}(\boldsymbol{z}_{r}+\boldsymbol{z}_{n})=\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{z}_{r}
, implying that
\boldsymbol{z}_{n}
has no effect on the task output, while
\boldsymbol{z}_{r}
determines the results.

A simple example is the element-wise summation of a two-dimensional representation
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where the weight matrix is defined as
\boldsymbol{W}=[1,1]\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times 2}
.
For any arbitrary vector
\boldsymbol{z}=[z_{1},z_{2}]^{\top}
, the representation is decomposed into
\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{z}_{r}+\boldsymbol{z}_{n}
, where
\boldsymbol{z}_{r}=\frac{z_{1}+z_{2}}{2}[1,1]^{\top}
and
\boldsymbol{z}_{n}=\frac{z_{1}-z_{2}}{2}[1,-1]^{\top}
denote the row space and null space components, respectively.
The result of the summation is governed solely by the row space component, as
\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{z}_{n}
is always zero.
It is noteworthy that element-wise summation can be extended to weighted averaging and linear regression.

For a linear task function, a basis for
\text{Row}(\boldsymbol{W})
,
\boldsymbol{u}_{r}
, is derived from the Jacobian,
\boldsymbol{J}_{\mathcal{T}}(\boldsymbol{z})=\partial\mathcal{T}(\boldsymbol{z})/\partial\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{W}
, which is constant and equals the weight matrix.
A basis for
\text{Null}(\boldsymbol{W})
,
\boldsymbol{u}_{n}
, can be found by computing an orthonormal set of vectors that span the orthogonal complement of the row space.
For a non-linear task function, unlike the linear case, a single global weight matrix that fully characterizes the transformation does not exist.
Instead, we can analyze the local behavior of
\mathcal{T}
around a specific representation
\boldsymbol{z}_{0}
using a first-order Taylor expansion:
\mathcal{T}(\boldsymbol{z}_{0}+\Delta\boldsymbol{z})\approx\mathcal{T}(\boldsymbol{z}_{0})+\boldsymbol{J}_{\mathcal{T}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{0})\Delta\boldsymbol{z}
, where
\Delta\boldsymbol{z}
is a sufficiently small perturbation.
It is worth noting that this local linear approximation is well-suited for modern deep learning architectures.
For instance, since neural networks composed of parallel ReLU-equipped units function as piecewise linear approximators, even complex non-linear decision boundaries can be decomposed into a collection of locally linear maps.
Locally, the Jacobian
\boldsymbol{J}_{\mathcal{T}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{0})
acts analogously to the linear weight matrix
\boldsymbol{W}
, allowing us to define a local row space and a local null space specific to the point
\boldsymbol{z}_{0}
.
If the perturbation
\Delta\boldsymbol{z}
lies within the local null space of
\boldsymbol{J}_{\mathcal{T}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{0})
, then
\boldsymbol{J}_{\mathcal{T}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{0})\Delta\boldsymbol{z}=\mathbf{0}
, and consequently
\mathcal{T}(\boldsymbol{z}_{0}+\Delta\boldsymbol{z})\approx\mathcal{T}(\boldsymbol{z}_{0})
, meaning that the non-linear transformation remains locally invariant to perturbations along the null space directions.
In the case of learning-based downstream models, the Jacobian can be computed using backpropagation without parameter updates.

2.2
Local Differential Privacy

Local Differential Privacy (LDP)
(
35
)
operates under a decentralized trust model in which each data owner randomizes their data locally prior to disclosure to any untrusted party.
This contrasts with Central Differential Privacy (CDP)
(
20
)
, which assumes a trusted curator to collect raw data and randomize aggregate statistics computed over the dataset.

In this work, we consider an LDP setting that applies a randomization mechanism directly to each data representation
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{Z}
, within the representation space
\mathcal{Z}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Let
\epsilon>0
denote the privacy budget and
\delta\geq 0
the failure probability, where
\delta=0
corresponds to pure
\epsilon
-LDP and
\delta>0
to
(\epsilon,\delta)
-LDP.
A mechanism
\mathcal{M}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
satisfies
(\epsilon,\delta)
-LDP if, for any pair of representations
\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}
and any measurable subset
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\text{Range}(\mathcal{M})
, it holds that
\Pr[\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{z})\in\mathcal{S}]\leq e^{\epsilon}\Pr[\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{S}]+\delta
.
A canonical class of mechanisms satisfying these guarantees is that of additive noise mechanisms
(
21
)
,
which produce a noisy representation of the form

\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}=\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{z})=\boldsymbol{z}+\boldsymbol{\xi}.

(1)

For
(\epsilon,0)
-LDP (i.e., pure
\epsilon
-LDP), the Laplace mechanism
\mathcal{M}_{L}
is a natural choise, where the noise scale
b
is directly proportional to the
\ell_{1}
-sensitivity, defined as
\Delta_{1}=\max_{\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}}\|\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}\|_{1}
, and each element of
\boldsymbol{\xi}
is sampled i.i.d. from
\mathrm{Lap}(0,\Delta_{1}/\epsilon)

(
21
)
.
For
(\epsilon,\delta)
-LDP with
\delta>0
, the Gaussian mechanism
\mathcal{M}_{G}
is a natural choice, where
\boldsymbol{\xi}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I})
and the noise scale
\sigma
is proportional to the
\ell_{2}
-sensitivity,
\Delta_{2}=\max_{\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}}\|\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}\|_{2}
, calibrated via the Analytic Gaussian Mechanism
(
6
)
.

As a local instantiation of DP, LDP inherits its fundamental strengths, including worst-case bounds on privacy leakage and immunity to post-processing
(
21
)
.
These theoretical bounds hold even against a strong adversary with full knowledge of the randomization mechanism
\mathcal{M}
and its parameters, including the privacy budget, sensitivity, and noise distribution settings.
Furthermore, the privacy guarantees of LDP are preserved under arbitrary post-processing independent of (private) data representation.
Once
\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}
is produced by a mechanism satisfying
(\epsilon,\delta)
-LDP, any measurable downstream computation or transformation
g(\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}})
independent of the private data maintains the same privacy guarantee without consuming additional privacy budget.

3
Motivation

Our primary objective is to enhance the utility of randomized representations generated by an LDP mechanism
\mathcal{M}
.
We focus particularly on the pure
\epsilon
-LDP regime, as the local privacy model typically precludes a non-zero probability of failure (i.e.,
\delta>0
).
Nonetheless, we explore a mechanism-agnostic approach that can be extended to
(\epsilon,\delta)
-LDP without loss of generality.

A fundamental obstacle to this goal is the severe utility degradation caused by the heavy randomization.
Consider a two-dimensional representation space (
m=2
) where each coordinate is bounded within
[L,U]
(
L,U\in\mathbb{R},L<U
), yielding an
\ell_{1}
-sensitivity of
\Delta_{1}=2(U-L)
.
Under a relatively loose practical privacy regime with
\epsilon=10
, the Laplace mechanism
\mathcal{M}_{L}
requires a noise scale of
b=\frac{1}{5}(U-L)
.
Since the maximum span of each dimension is
U-L
, the injected noise often perturbs representations by a magnitude comparable to, or exceeding, the scale of the representation space itself.
This leads to severe semantic distortion of the randomized representation
\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}
.
In addition, this degradation is exacerbated in high-dimensional spaces: as the sensitivities scale with the dimensionality (i.e.,
\Delta_{1}=\mathcal{O}(m)
and
\Delta_{2}=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{m})
), the noise scale is amplified proportionally.

To mitigate this limitation, we explore a novel approach that mitigates the noise injected into the task-relevant row space.
Figure 1 provides an example to motivate our approach.
Consider a linear binary classifier with a single decision boundary in a two-dimensional representation space
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
The decision boundary is defined by a normal vector
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and the space can be spanned by two basis vectors
\boldsymbol{u}_{r}
and
\boldsymbol{u}_{n}
, aligned parallel and perpendicular to
\boldsymbol{w}
, respectively.

\boldsymbol{u}_{r}
and
\boldsymbol{u}_{n}
span the row space and null space of the classifier, respectively.
Crucially, perturbing a representation
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
along
\boldsymbol{u}_{r}
can alter the classification outcome, whereas perturbations of any arbitrary magnitude along
\boldsymbol{u}_{n}
leave the classification result unchanged.
Based on this observation, our approach anisotropically reshapes the isotropic noise
\boldsymbol{\xi}
to allocate less noise along the task-sensitive direction
\boldsymbol{u}_{r}
and more along the task-invariant direction
\boldsymbol{u}_{n}
.

4
Proposed Approach

We propose to reduce the noise injected into the task-critical subspaces defined by the downstream model.
Figure 2 illustrates the overall pipeline of the proposed approach.

Figure 2
:
Overview of the proposed approach.

Our approach comprises a pre-processing function
f:\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow\bar{\mathcal{Z}}
, where
\bar{\mathcal{Z}}
is an intermediate bounded space in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and a post-processing function
g:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
A randomization mechanism
\mathcal{M}
is applied to the intermediate representation
\bar{\boldsymbol{z}}=f(\boldsymbol{z})
, followed by the application of the post-processing function
g
.
As a result, this process yields a randomized version of
\boldsymbol{z}
injected with anisotropic noise
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
:

\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}=g(\mathcal{M}(f(\boldsymbol{z})))=g(\mathcal{M}(\bar{\boldsymbol{z}}))=g(\bar{\boldsymbol{z}}+\boldsymbol{\xi})\approx\boldsymbol{z}+\boldsymbol{\xi}_{a},

(2)

where the covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
of
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{a}
is publicly known and positive-definite.
This matrix is designed to rotate and scale the noise, attenuating it along the row space of the downstream model (e.g. the weight matrix
[1,1]
for the element-wise summation in Section
2.1
public pre-trained models), while amplifying it along the null space.

We provide a more detailed explanation of the procedure.
Here, we primarily focus on the row space for clarity.
Let
\lambda_{r}\in(0,1]
be a parameter that governs the relationship between the desired variance
\sigma_{r}^{2}
of the noise projected into the row space via anisotropic noise
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{a}
, and the variance
\sigma^{2}
of the isotropic noise
\boldsymbol{\xi}
, such that
\lambda_{r}=(\frac{\sigma_{r}}{\sigma})^{2}
.
The procedure consists of five steps:

1.

Our approach identifies the row and null spaces based on Jacobian matrices.

2.

It inversely rotates and expands the representation in the row space by a factor of
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{r}}}
.

3.

It bounds the row space sensitivity.

4.

It injects isotropic noise guaranteeing
\epsilon
-LDP.

5.

It rotates and scales the randomized representation down by a factor of
\sqrt{\lambda_{r}}
, thereby reshaping the isotropic noise into anisotropic noise and restoring the original scale and orientation of representation.

The pre-processing function
f
handles the first three steps, and the post-processing function
g
manages the final step.
In the pre-processing step, while space identification and representation alignment are important, bounding sensitivity also plays a crucial role in reducing the noise injected into the row space.
Although bounding incurs utility loss, we assume that it is outweighed by the utility degradation caused by
\epsilon
-LDP randomization under high sensitivity.
This design choice is widely adopted in practical applications of differential privacy.
In the fourth step, we inject noise via the conventional mechanism to guarantee
\epsilon
-LDP for the predetermined sensitivity, which is proportional to the bounding threshold.
The final step conducts
g
a lossless linear bijection with no information loss.
The privacy guarantee is preserved due to the post-processing immunity of differential privacy
(
21
)
.

Note that in our approach, noise control is achieved through two key parameters: the bounding threshold
\rho\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and the ratio
\lambda_{r}
of the coordinate-wise noise to the isotropic noise.
The magnitude of isotropic noise is determined by the privacy budget, the threshold, and the chosen randomization mechanism.
Finally, through the linear bijective rotation and scaling applied during post-processing, noise satisfying
\epsilon
-LDP is automatically distributed across all coordinates.

4.1
Pre-processing and Post-processing

Suppose the eigendecomposition of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
is given by
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{U}^{\top}
where
\boldsymbol{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
is an orthogonal matrix representing rotation, and
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
is a diagonal matrix that scales each coordinate in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
We derive our pre-processing and post-processing functions by modifying (
3
)

\boldsymbol{z}+\boldsymbol{\xi}_{a}=\boldsymbol{L}(\boldsymbol{L}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\mu})+\boldsymbol{\xi})+\boldsymbol{\mu}

(3)

where
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
represents an arbitrary offset and
\boldsymbol{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
denotes a reshaping factor satisfying
\boldsymbol{L}=\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{1/2}
.

Pre-processing.

Incorporating bounding, we define our pre-processing function
f:\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow\bar{\mathcal{Z}}
as (
4
):

f(\boldsymbol{z})=\text{Bound}\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\mu});\rho\right)

(4)

where
\text{Bound}(\cdot;\rho)
denotes the bounding function with a threshold
\rho
.
The detailed construction of
\boldsymbol{L}=\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{1/2}
via local Jacobian computation is presented in Section
4.2
.
Using
\boldsymbol{L}^{-1}
, this pre-processing maps the representation
\boldsymbol{z}
from the space
\mathcal{Z}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
to another bounded space
\bar{\mathcal{Z}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Specifically, it first offsets
\boldsymbol{z}
by
\boldsymbol{\mu}
, applies an inverse rotation and rescaling via
\boldsymbol{L}^{-1}
, and subsequently bounds the result to ensure that
\bar{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\bar{\mathcal{Z}}
strictly resides within a bounded space.

Post-processing.

Furthermore, we define the post-processing function
g:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
as (
5
):

g(\mathcal{M}(f(\boldsymbol{z})))=\boldsymbol{L}(f(\boldsymbol{z})+\boldsymbol{\xi})+\boldsymbol{\mu}\approx\boldsymbol{z}+\boldsymbol{\xi}_{a}.

(5)

Our post-processing function
g
reverses the transformation
\boldsymbol{L}^{-1}
and the offset
\boldsymbol{\mu}
applied in
f
, while simultaneously rotating and rescaling the isotropic noise
\boldsymbol{\xi}
injected into
\bar{\mathcal{Z}}
.
This yields a new anisotropic noise vector
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{a}
designed to enhance downstream utility.
Notably, this approach preserves the privacy guarantees because
g
is applied to the output of the
\epsilon
-LDP mechanism
\mathcal{M}
.

4.2
Covariance Matrix for Rotation and Rescaling

To exploit post-processing immunity, our approach relies on a covariance matrix,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
, constructed from public knowledge.
In typical LDP settings, although downstream models cannot access the (private) representation
\boldsymbol{z}
, they are generally expected to perform interpolation or be trained on data following a similar distribution.
We therefore assume access to widely known downstream models or those pre-trained on public datasets, and estimate
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
by analyzing the row and null spaces of these public models.
Moreover, we assume that the dimension
m
of the representation space exceeds the rank
r
of the model’s row space, inspired by
(
7
;
41
;
1
)
.
Recall that
\mathbb{R}^{m}
can be orthogonally decomposed into a row space and a null space, where the dimension
m
is the sum of the rank
r
and the nullity
n
.

The construction of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
consists of two components: the
rotation
and the
scaling
, as mentioned in Section
4.1
.
We first determine the rotation matrix
\boldsymbol{U}
, which aligns the axes of
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{a}
with the basis vectors of the row and null spaces.
Then, we determine the scaling matrix
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\text{diag}(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{m})
, which assigns noise scales to each coordinate to enhance data utility.

Rotation.
To identify the relevant basis vectors for rotation, we characterize the global geometric behavior of the downstream models.
First, we construct a global sensitivity matrix by aggregating local Jacobian matrices evaluated at samples from public datasets.
Subsequently, from this aggregated matrix, we extract an
r
-dimensional orthonormal basis
\boldsymbol{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}
that spans the global active subspace
(
12
)
, which represents the task-sensitive directions.
Finally, to isolate the task-insensitive directions, we perform a QR decomposition
(
27
)
on
\boldsymbol{B}
:

\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{QR}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{Q}_{r}&\boldsymbol{Q}_{n}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{R}_{r}^{\top}&\boldsymbol{0}\end{bmatrix}^{\top},

where
\boldsymbol{Q}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{Q}_{r}&\boldsymbol{Q}_{n}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
is orthogonal,
\boldsymbol{R}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
is upper triangular, and
\boldsymbol{Q}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
spans the
(m{-}r)
-dimensional null space.

Scaling.

The objective of scaling is to assign a tailored noise scale factor
\sigma_{i}
to each coordinate
i\in\{1,2,\dots,m\}
of the noise
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{a}=\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{\xi}
, where these coordinates are aligned with the basis vectors of the row and null spaces.
To determine these individual scale factors, we utilize the singular values
s_{i}
of the aggregated Jacobian matrix, as they represent the relative importance of the
i
-th coordinate.
We assume that the null space coordinates have very small singular values.
Additionally, from the perspective of the privacy budget, we adopt a constraint where the sum of the inverses of the noise scales remains constant before and after rescaling, i.e.,
m=\sum_{j=1}^{m}(1/\sqrt{\lambda_{j}})
for
\lambda_{j}=(\frac{\sigma_{j}}{\sigma})^{2}
and
\sigma_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
Consequently, the Lagrange multiplier yields the scale factor as

\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}}=\frac{ms_{i}^{2/3}}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}s_{j}^{2/3}}.

(6)

This optimization (
6
) can also be applied to full-rank downstream models (e.g., models without a null space), thereby widening the reach of our approach.
The isotropic noise scale
\sigma
is given when
\rho
is set.
Calculating the scaling factor automatically yields the anisotropic noise scale for each coordinate.
While directly applying anisotropic noise to satisfy
\epsilon
-LDP entails calculating coordinate-wise sensitivities and noise scales
(
9
;
43
)
, our approach circumvents this requirement by reshaping isotropic noise.

Proof.

Let
s_{i}
denote the singular value of the aggregated Jacobian matrix representing the importance of the
i
-th principal coordinate in the representation space.
Let
\lambda_{i}=(\frac{\sigma_{i}}{\sigma})^{2}
be the scale assigned to
i
-th coordinate.
Our objective is to minimize the scale weighted by the squared singular values:
\sum_{i=1}^{m}s_{i}^{2}\lambda_{i}
.
This objective ensures that important coordinates with larger
s_{i}
receive less noise
\lambda_{i}
, while less important coordinates absorb more noise, thereby preserving the overall utility.

For simplicity, we assume a clipping threshold of
\rho
.
For a randomization mechanism that injects independent and non-identically distributed (i.n.i.d.) zero-mean Laplace noise, the probability density function for the
i
-th coordinate is given by
q_{\mathcal{Z}_{i}}(z_{i})=\frac{1}{2b_{i}}e^{-|z_{i}|/b_{i}}
, with variance
\sigma_{i}^{2}=2b_{i}^{2}
.
To guarantee
\epsilon
-LDP, the mechanism should satisfy the following bound on the log-likelihood ratio:

\sum_{j=1}^{m}\log\frac{q_{\mathcal{Z}_{j}}(z_{j})}{q_{\mathcal{Z}_{j}}(z_{j}+d_{j})}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{|z_{j}+d_{j}|-|z_{j}|}{b_{j}}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{|d_{j}|}{b_{j}}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\rho}{b_{j}}\leq\epsilon

where
\rho
denotes the clipping threshold.
Under sensitivity bounding via clipping, the privacy budget consumed by the
i
-th coordinate scales as
\mathcal{O}(\lambda_{i}^{-1/2})
.

To minimize
\sum_{i=1}^{m}s_{i}^{2}\lambda_{i}
under the constraint
m=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}}
, we can formulate the Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}
:

\mathcal{L}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}s_{i}^{2}\lambda_{i}+\eta\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}}-m\right)

where
\eta
is the Lagrange multiplier.
To find the minimum, we take the partial derivative of
\mathcal{L}
with respect to
\lambda_{i}
and set it to zero, satisfying the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\lambda_{i}}=s_{i}^{2}-\frac{\eta}{2}\lambda_{i}^{-3/2}=0

Rearranging this equation yields a proportional relationship for
\lambda_{i}
:

\lambda_{i}^{3/2}=\frac{\eta}{2s_{i}^{2}}\implies\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}}\propto s_{i}^{2/3}.

To determine the allocation, we introduce a proportionality constant
\alpha
such that
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}}=\alpha s_{i}^{2/3}
.
Substituting this back into the constraint yields:

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha s_{i}^{2/3}=m\implies\alpha=\frac{m}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}s_{j}^{2/3}}

By substituting
\alpha
into our proportional equation, we obtain the final closed-form solution for the optimal scale allocation:

\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}}=\frac{ms_{i}^{2/3}}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}s_{j}^{2/3}}

4.3
Sensitivity Bounding

We employ a bounding strategy to effectively reduce the noise injected into task-critical subspaces.
Using (
6
), we assign a ratio less than or equal to 1 to important coordinates and a ratio greater than or equal to 1 to less important coordinates.
Applying
\boldsymbol{L}^{-1}
during the pre-processing step thus stretches the row space and shrinks the null space.
The stretching of the row space amplifies the overall sensitivity in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
(see Appendix
A.3
), requiring a large amount of noise to be injected to guarantee
\epsilon
-LDP.
Consequently, when
\boldsymbol{L}
is applied during the post-processing step, compared to directly injecting isotropic noise into the original space
\mathcal{Z}
, the row space receives the same amount of noise, whereas the null space suffers from excessive noise amplification.

However, by regulating the sensitivity with a bounding function in the transformed space
\boldsymbol{L}^{-1}\mathcal{Z}
, we can effectively reduce the amount of noise injected into the row space while satisfying
\epsilon
-LDP.
The null space also remains protected by noise satisfying
\epsilon
-LDP, although this noise is often excessive.
A more detailed explanation, along with an illustration, is provided in Appendix
A.2
.

Candidate bounding functions range from
\ell_{p}
-norm-based clipping to threshold-based filtering, for
p\in\{1,2,\dots,\infty\}
.
The choice of this function depends on the mechanism and the application.
The
\ell_{1}
-norm is often used when
\delta=0
.
Importantly, regardless of the chosen bounding function, the threshold parameter has to be determined using public information or prior domain knowledge to prevent any unintended privacy leakage; see Appendix
A.4
.

Additional properties of our approach are detailed in Appendices
A.5
–
A.7
.

5
Related Work

In this section, we briefly review closely related work.
Further discussions on relevance-aware and feature-wise approaches, alongside post-processing, public knowledge, and DP assisted by public data and models, are provided in Appendix
A.1
.

Task-Aware LDP.

The work by
(
9
)
is most closely related to ours: they propose an encoding-decoding framework that exploits downstream model information to apply LDP to high-dimensional data, injecting noise between the encoding and decoding steps.
The most significant difference emerges when dealing with non-linear downstream models.
For these models, they propose training the encoder and decoder using unprotected data.
Specifically, the encoder and decoder are trained by minimizing the discrepancy between the downstream model’s outputs when fed with protected versus unprotected private data.
In contrast, we utilize the public Jacobian to identify the local row and null spaces.
Consequently, unlike their private-data-dependent encoder and decoder, our method avoids any risk of privacy leakage.
See Appendix
A.1
for more differences.

Coordinate-Wise DP.

Our work is also closely aligned with
(
43
)
, which investigates the determination of non-identical noise scales for independent coordinates with varying sensitivities under DP.
Their primary objective is to minimize MSE between the original and privatized data.
In contrast, our approach prioritizes downstream utility over the data MSE (DMSE) minimization.
These two objectives are often misaligned.
For example, injecting substantial noise into the null space may significantly degrade the DMSE while leaving downstream performance unaffected.
Furthermore, while they directly inject anisotropic noise into the private data, we opt to inject isotropic noise, which is then distributed anisotropically through reshaping.

Variance-Aware DP.

In terms of utilizing prior information, the approximate method proposed by
(
5
)
shares similarities with our work.
By assuming a known covariance and data sampled from distributions with bounded coordinate-wise standard deviations,
(
5
)
calibrates noise by disproportionately allocating the privacy budget to coordinates exhibiting higher variance, guided by the data geometry inherent in the covariance matrix.
In contrast, our approach determines noise scales based on the task-specific importance of subspaces utilized by the downstream model, rather than relying on data correlations.

7
Conclusion and Limitations

We proposed a principled approach to improve the privacy-utility tradeoff under LDP by reshaping isotropic noise into an anisotropic distribution via Jacobian-based subspace analysis.
Extensive experiments across various linear and non-linear tasks demonstrate consistent utility gains without compromising privacy.
A formal theoretical analysis of the utility loss induced by clipping in complex tasks remains an open challenge, as this tradeoff is contingent on the underlying data distribution. Establishing a generalized analytical methodology represents a compelling direction for future research.

Appendix A

A.1
Related Work

Task-Aware.

The work by
[
9
]
is most closely related to ours.
They propose an encoding-decoding methodology that exploits downstream model information to apply LDP to high-dimensional data.
In their approach, noise is injected after the encoding step and prior to decoding.

For linear downstream models, our methodology shares several structural similarities with theirs.
However, the key distinctions lie in the details.
First, their analytical approach is not based on Jacobian computations and is limited to linear models.
Second, they focus solely on minimizing the Mean Squared Error (MSE), rendering their approach sub-optimal for categorical predictions that rely on argmax operations.
Finally, when allocating noise scales or ratios across coordinates, they adopt a constraint that fixes the total noise energy from an MSE perspective.
In contrast, from a privacy perspective, we enforce a constraint that maintains either the sum of the inverse noise scales or the sum of the inverse variances.

The most significant difference emerges when dealing with non-linear downstream models.
For these models, they propose training the encoder and decoder using unprotected data.
Specifically, the encoder and decoder are trained by minimizing the discrepancy between the downstream model’s outputs when fed with protected versus unprotected private data.
In contrast, we utilize the public Jacobian to identify the local row and null spaces.
Consequently, unlike their private-data-dependent encoder and decoder, our method avoids any risk of privacy leakage.

Relevance-Aware.
Another line of related work focuses on dynamically allocating privacy budgets based on feature importance.
For instance,
[
45
]
proposed the Adaptive Laplace Mechanism (AdLM), which adjusts the amount of noise injected into each input dimension based on its contribution to the final model output.
To quantify this importance, they employ Layer-wise Relevance Propagation (LRP), a set of formulas that mathematically redistributes the output prediction score backward to the individual input features.
Based on these calculated LRP scores, AdLM allocates a proportionally larger privacy budget to highly relevant features, thereby reducing the noise added to them while heavily perturbing less relevant features.

While both AdLM and our methodology share the high-level intuition of task-aware, they fundamentally differ in their mathematical foundations.
Regarding the measurement of importance, AdLM relies on LRP to compute the proportional attribution of each feature (i.e., how much a specific pixel contributed to the total output score).
In contrast, our approach utilizes the Jacobian matrix to evaluate the sensitivity (i.e., the gradient or rate of change) of the output with respect to the input.
This mathematically grounded approach allows us to analytically decompose the input space into geometrically meaningful structures, specifically, the local row and null spaces, rather than simply assigning scalar scores to individual pixels.

Coordinate-Wise, Correlation-Aware, and Feature-Wise DP.

Our work is also closely aligned with
[
43
]
, which investigates the determination of non-identical noise scales for independent coordinates with varying sensitivities under
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP.
Their primary objective is to minimize MSE between the original and privatized data.
In contrast, our approach prioritizes downstream utility over the data MSE (DMSE) minimization.
These two objectives are often misaligned.
For example, injecting substantial noise into the null space may significantly degrade the DMSE while leaving downstream performance unaffected.
Furthermore, while they directly inject anisotropic noise into the private data, we opt to inject isotropic noise, which is then distributed anisotropically through reshaping.

[
2
]
introduced the concept of Heterogeneous Differential Privacy (HDP), which captures the non-uniformity of privacy expectations both among different users and across various data items belonging to the same individual.
The contribution of HDP lies in its stretching mechanism, a modification of the Laplace mechanism that manipulates function sensitivity through linear transformations on the input domain.
This approach enables item-grained privacy, allowing for the adaptive calibration of noise levels based on the specific sensitivity of each piece of information.
Their empirical evaluation demonstrates that HDP effectively accommodates heterogeneous privacy requirements while sustaining high utility, particularly in semantic clustering tasks, by avoiding unnecessary noise injection into less sensitive data.

The primary distinction between our approach and HDP lies in the targeted noise reduction based on dimensional importance for the downstream model.
We allocate a smaller privacy budget to less critical dimensions while preserving the budget for more important dimensions to minimize noise injection.
Ultimately, our goal is to reduce the noise injected into the key dimensions while maintaining the privacy budget to enhance utility.

The Pufferfish
[
36
]
was introduced to address the vulnerabilities of traditional Differential Privacy (DP) in scenarios where the assumption of record independence is violated.
While DP defines privacy based on the syntactic structure of neighboring databases, it can lead to significant privacy leakage in real-world datasets characterized by complex correlations.
The core contribution is the introduction of data evolution scenarios, which are probabilistic models that mathematically define the data-generating process.
By incorporating the joint probability distribution of records directly into the privacy constraint, Pufferfish accounts for data dependencies within the privacy definition itself.

Similarly, assuming a partially known covariance and data sampled from distributions with bounded coordinate-wise standard deviations,
[
5
]
calibrate noise by disproportionately allocating the privacy budget to coordinates exhibiting higher variance, guided by the data geometry inherent in the covariance matrix.
Building on this,
[
15
]
extend this methodology to settings with unknown covariance by employing FriendlyCore during pre-processing, thereby effectively relaxing the aforementioned coordinate-wise assumptions.

The works in
[
36
,
5
,
15
]
explore anisotropic noise injection by accounting for data correlations.
In contrast, our approach controls noise levels based on the importance of subspaces within the data space utilized by the downstream model, rather than relying on data correlations.

Post-Processing & Public Knowledge.

There have been numerous efforts to enhance utility under LDP by designing post-processing functions based on public knowledge, incorporating methodologies such as data aggregation, filtering, denoising, deconvolution, and statistical inference.

In LDP, data aggregation traces its roots back to Warner’s Randomized Response
[
51
]
.
Fundamentally, it involves estimating the true underlying distribution by removing statistical bias from data that clients have intentionally perturbed prior to transmission.
Under Warner’s original design, a respondent answers a sensitive question with probability
p
, and a complementary question with probability
1-p
.
This ensures plausible deniability, making it impossible for the surveyor to determine which specific question the individual answered.
To correct the induced bias, the server applies a linear inversion estimator based on the observed frequencies.

Google’s RAPPOR
[
23
]
is a pioneering LDP mechanism deployed in Chrome that combines Bloom filters with a two-stage randomized response.
It first maps input values into a Bloom filter using hash functions, then perturbs the filter via Permanent Randomized Response (PRR) to ensure longitudinal privacy.
Each transmission is further randomized through Instantaneous Randomized Response (IRR).
On the server side, the collected bitstrings are decoded using Lasso regression and Bonferroni correction to accurately estimate category frequencies.

[
3
]
has deployed a LDP pipeline based on Count Mean Sketch (CMS) and Hadamard Count Mean Sketch (HCMS) algorithms to securely collect user data—such as emoji usage frequencies and newly coined keyboard words, across its iOS and macOS environments.
Specifically, CMS is employed to aggregate emoji frequencies, whereas HCMS is utilized to detect high-memory and high-energy consuming web domains in Safari.
The client-side HCMS mechanism sequentially performs hash encoding, Hadamard transformation, single-bit sampling, and LDP-compliant bit-flipping.
To reconstruct the population statistics, the server aggregates millions of these transmitted 1-bit vectors and applies an inverse Hadamard transform.

For collecting counter-type telemetry data, such as app usage duration on Windows OS,
[
16
]
employed a 1-bit mechanism known as dBitFlip in conjunction with histogram-based aggregation.
The dBitFlip mechanism applies bit-flipping probabilities that are conditioned on the user’s actual value.
To estimate the distribution, counter values are discretized into predefined bins, and the client transmits a randomized 1-bit response for each corresponding bin.

[
28
]
introduced a post-processing method that uses hierarchical consistency to refine noisy histogram counts.
Histograms collected under LDP often suffer from hierarchical inconsistencies, as independent noise injection at each node causes a mismatch between a parent’s value and the sum of its children.
To resolve this, they proposed a two-step optimization process: first, a bottom-up phase that improves estimates through weighted averaging from leaves to the root; and second, a top-down phase that adjusts these values to satisfy the parent-child sum constraint.
This dual-stage approach not only ensures consistency but also significantly reduces variance, thereby enhancing the overall accuracy of the private histogram.

[
49
]
formalized the perturbation process of the pure LDP through two key parameters:
p
, the probability of reporting the true value, and
q
, the probability of reporting a perturbed value.
Based on the privacy-utility relationship defined by these probabilities (i.e.,
p/q=e^{\epsilon}
), they derived a generalized unbiased estimator to reconstruct original frequencies from noisy observations.
A contribution of their work is the mathematical derivation of optimal
p
and
q
values that minimize the estimation variance under a fixed privacy budget
\epsilon
.
This theoretical foundation led to the development of Optimized Unary Encoding (OUE) and Optimized Local Hashing (OLH), which improve estimation accuracy compared to traditional randomized response mechanisms.

[
33
]
introduced a calibration to enhance the aggregation accuracy of LDP-collected data.
Their approach uses statistical inference, under the assumption that the underlying noise mechanism and certain characteristics of the true distribution are publicly available, to refine the results of frequency estimation and heavy hitter identification.
By applying an iterative calibration process, the proposed method effectively mitigates the estimation errors of standard LDP protocols, providing more reliable data for downstream analytical tasks.

To address the inconsistency between parent and child nodes in tree-based range queries under LDP,
[
13
]
propose a post-processing method based on Least Squares (LS) estimation.
The primary contribution of this work is the theoretical proof that their approach achieves the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator (BLUE).
By using this consistency-based refinement, the method minimizes the weighted variance across the hierarchical structure, ensuring that the sum of child nodes aligns perfectly with their parent.

To further refine LDP frequency estimation,
[
50
]
propose a post-processing framework that uses prior knowledge of the probability simplex constraint that individual frequencies must be non-negative and sum to unity.
By projecting noisy estimates onto a valid probability simplex, their approach ensures that the reconstructed distribution is statistically consistent.
This method significantly enhances utility without requiring any modifications to the underlying LDP protocols, demonstrating the power of constraint-based post-processing.

To enable effective learning on GNN under LDP,
[
46
]
designed KProp, a specialized propagation mechanism.
KProp aggregates and normalizes feature information from neighbors within
K
hops to mitigate the impact of local noise.
By assuming the server has access to the graph topology, the method denoises private node features through neighborhood averaging.
This approach leverages the structural properties of the graph as a post-processing step, boosting model performance despite the presence of stringent LDP noise.

[
24
]
introduced a frequency estimation method by demonstrating that the encoding and perturbation processes in LDP can be mathematically formulated as circular convolution.
Relying on the fact that the perturbation mechanism is publicly known, the authors proposed a systematic denoising approach using deconvolution and Wiener filters.
This signal processing-based perspective provides a more rigorous and effective aggregation framework compared to traditional linear inversion methods, enhancing the signal-to-noise ratio in reconstructed distributions.

Among these studies,
[
46
]
is closely related to our work in that it addresses LDP for high-dimensional data.
However, while our research focuses on the inference phase under LDP, their work explores learning of GNNs.
Furthermore, their primary contribution is centered on aggregation methods, distinguishing it from our focus on reducing the noise injected into the task-sensitive subspace.

Public Data & Model Assisted DP.

Various studies have leveraged public data or pre-trained models to alleviate the utility loss inherent in Differential Privacy (DP).
For instance,
[
39
]
investigated the use of ResNet-18 models pre-trained on the Places365 dataset, demonstrating that such priors are vital for navigating the loss landscape under noise injection.
Their findings underscore that leveraging feature representations learned from public data is a key factor in improving both convergence speed and final classification accuracy.
Similarly, DOPE-SGD
[
44
]
integrates advanced data augmentation with a re-centered gradient clipping strategy informed by public priors.
This method effectively mitigates the bias in private gradient estimation, leading to substantial utility gains.
Furthermore,
[
31
]
introduced a framework for generating DP synthetic text, showing that models trained on such data achieve superior performance with
100\times
less communication overhead than on-device baselines.
By establishing that synthetic data training provides a more favorable privacy-utility trade-off, their research validates the efficacy of using generative models to create private proxies for downstream tasks.

While these works predominantly center on optimizing the model training process, our approach diverges by focusing on enhancing utility during inference under LDP (no time to do the training tasks).

Sensitivity Bounding

Here, we provide an explanation our sensitivity bounding.
In our approach, a notable phenomenon is the change in sensitivity, as depicted in Figure
3
.
Constructing
\boldsymbol{L}
to contract along the basis vectors,
\boldsymbol{u}_{2}
, of the row space causes
\boldsymbol{L}^{-1}
to expand along the same directions.
The sensitivity within the intermediate space
\boldsymbol{L}^{-1}\mathcal{Z}
, given by
\max_{\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}}||\boldsymbol{L}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime})||_{p}
, can be larger than the sensitivity in
\mathcal{Z}
(see the proof in the Appendix
A.3
).
As a result, the noise scale required for
\boldsymbol{L}^{-1}\mathcal{Z}
to satisfy
(\epsilon,\delta)
-LDP may become larger than that for
\mathcal{Z}
because the scale is proportional to the sensitivity.

It is undesirable to directly inject
(\epsilon,\delta)
-LDP noise into the space
\boldsymbol{L}^{-1}\mathcal{Z}
.
Given that the factor
\boldsymbol{L}
and its inverse
\boldsymbol{L}^{-1}
act as information-preserving linear transformations, tolerating an increased noise scale to secure the same privacy guarantee for the same information is inefficient.

Fortunately, we can control sensitivity by employing a standard
\ell_{p}
clipping procedure.
Post-clipping, the resulting space
\bar{\mathcal{Z}}
achieves a bounded sensitivity of
\rho
, as shown in the right panel of Figure
3(a)
.
Using this bound, we apply mechanism
\mathcal{M}
to inject noise
\boldsymbol{\xi}
satisfying
(\epsilon,\delta)
-LDP into
\bar{\mathcal{Z}}
, as depicted in the right panel of Figure
3(b)
.
Although clipping distorts information, it simultaneously bounds the sensitivity, thereby reducing the noise scale required to satisfy
(\epsilon,\delta)
-LDP.

Crucially, the subsequent transformation by
\boldsymbol{L}
reduces the sensitivity along the row space basis direction
\boldsymbol{u}_{r}
in
\mathcal{Z}
, while increasing it along the null space basis direction
\boldsymbol{u}_{n}
.
The distorted square (solid black line) of
\boldsymbol{L}\bar{\mathcal{Z}}
in the left panel of Figure
3(a)
and the ellipse (dashed blue line) of
\boldsymbol{\xi}
in the left panel of Figure

illustrate this phenomenon.
In accordance with this change in sensitivity, the noise scales required to achieve
(\epsilon,\delta)
-LDP along
\boldsymbol{u}_{r}
and
\boldsymbol{u}_{n}
are proportionally decreased and increased, respectively.
Consequently, the
(\epsilon,\delta)
-LDP privacy guarantee is preserved owing to post-processing immunity, even though a smaller amount of noise is injected into the row space.
Conversely, while the noise along the null space basis may increase relative to direct perturbation on
\mathcal{Z}
, this has a negligible impact on downstream task performance due to the inherent properties of the null space.

A.3
Sensitivity Amplification in Transformed Space

Let
\boldsymbol{\phi}=\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}
be a perturbation in the original space
\mathcal{Z}
, and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{L}^{\top}
be the covariance matrix.
The worst-case sensitivity of the transformed space under the inverse Cholesky factor
\boldsymbol{L}^{-1}
, defined by the induced matrix norm
\|\boldsymbol{L}^{-1}\|_{p}=\max_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\neq\mathbf{0}}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{L}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\phi}\|_{p}}{\|\boldsymbol{\phi}\|_{p}}
, is amplified (
\|\boldsymbol{L}^{-1}\|_{p}>1
) if the transformation reduces variance along any principal coordinate, for both
p\in\{1,2\}
.

Proof.
The maximum perturbation bound is fundamentally determined by the induced
\ell_{p}
-norm of the transformation matrix:
\|\boldsymbol{L}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\phi}\|_{p}\leq\|\boldsymbol{L}^{-1}\|_{p}\|\boldsymbol{\phi}\|_{p}
.
We prove the sensitivity amplification for each norm independently.

Case 1:
\ell_{2}
-norm (
p=2
).

The induced
\ell_{2}
-norm (spectral norm) of a matrix is its maximum singular value.
For
\boldsymbol{L}^{-1}
, this is related to the eigenvalues of the precision matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}
:

\|\boldsymbol{L}^{-1}\|_{2}=\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}((\boldsymbol{L}^{-1})^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}^{-1})}=\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1})}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\min}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})}}

If
\boldsymbol{L}
applies a pure rotation without rescaling (
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\boldsymbol{I}
), then
\lambda_{\min}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})=1
, yielding
\|\boldsymbol{L}^{-1}\|_{2}=1
(distance is perfectly preserved).
However, if the transformation shrinks the space such that the minimum variance is less than 1 (
\lambda_{\min}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})<1
), the bound becomes strictly greater than 1:

\|\boldsymbol{L}^{-1}\|_{2}>1\implies\max_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\neq\mathbf{0}}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{L}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\phi}\|_{2}}{\|\boldsymbol{\phi}\|_{2}}>1

Thus, the worst-case
\ell_{2}
sensitivity increases under variance reduction.

Case 2:
\ell_{1}
-norm (
p=1
).

The induced
\ell_{1}
-norm of a matrix is its maximum absolute column sum:
\|\boldsymbol{L}^{-1}\|_{1}=\max_{j}\sum_{i}|(\boldsymbol{L}^{-1})_{ij}|
.
Unlike the
\ell_{2}
-norm, the
\ell_{1}
-norm is coordinate-dependent.
For a pure rotation matrix
\boldsymbol{U}
(where no coordinate scaling occurs), the rotation misaligns the canonical axes, generally resulting in
\|\boldsymbol{U}^{-1}\|_{1}\geq 1
.
Furthermore, if
\boldsymbol{L}
involves shrinking (rescaling), we utilize the submultiplicative property of induced norms on the identity matrix
\boldsymbol{I}=\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{L}^{-1}
:

1=\|\boldsymbol{I}\|_{1}\leq\|\boldsymbol{L}\|_{1}\|\boldsymbol{L}^{-1}\|_{1}\implies\|\boldsymbol{L}^{-1}\|_{1}\geq\frac{1}{\|\boldsymbol{L}\|_{1}}

If the transformed space is compressed such that the absolute column sums of
\boldsymbol{L}
are bounded below 1 (
\|\boldsymbol{L}\|_{1}<1
), it guarantees that the inverse transformation expands the space:

\|\boldsymbol{L}^{-1}\|_{1}\geq\frac{1}{\|\boldsymbol{L}\|_{1}}>1

Combining rotation and scaling effects, any non-trivial variance-reducing transformation
\boldsymbol{L}
results in
\|\boldsymbol{L}^{-1}\|_{1}>1
, amplifying the worst-case
\ell_{1}
sensitivity.

A.4
Post-processing Immunity

We prove that our post-processing function
g
preserves the differential privacy guarantees of the underlying mechanism.
This relies on the post-processing immunity property of differential privacy
[
21
]
.

Let
\mathcal{M}:\bar{\mathcal{Z}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a randomized mechanism
that satisfies
\epsilon
-LDP.
Let

g:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a deterministic mapping defined as
g(\boldsymbol{v})=\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{\mu}
, where
\boldsymbol{L}
and
\boldsymbol{\mu}
are data-independent parameters.
For any
\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}
and pre-processing function
f:\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow\bar{\mathcal{Z}}
, the composed mechanism
g(\mathcal{M}(f(\cdot)))
satisfies
\epsilon
-LDP.

Proof.

Fix any
\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}
and any measurable set

S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Define the pre-image of
S
under
g
as

T=\;\{\,\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:g(\boldsymbol{v})\in S\,\},

so that
g(\boldsymbol{v})\in S
if and only if
\boldsymbol{v}\in T
.

Since
f:\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow\bar{\mathcal{Z}}
, we have

f(\boldsymbol{z}),f(\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime})\in\bar{\mathcal{Z}}
, so the
\epsilon
-LDP
guarantee of
\mathcal{M}
applies to these inputs. Therefore:

\displaystyle\Pr\bigl[g(\mathcal{M}(f(\boldsymbol{z})))\in S\bigr]

\displaystyle=\Pr\bigl[\mathcal{M}(f(\boldsymbol{z}))\in T\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq e^{\epsilon}\,\Pr\bigl[\mathcal{M}(f(\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}))\in T\bigr]

\displaystyle=e^{\epsilon}\,\Pr\bigl[g(\mathcal{M}(f(\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime})))\in S\bigr].

Since
\boldsymbol{z}
,
\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}
, and
S
were arbitrary, the composed mechanism
g(\mathcal{M}(f(\cdot)))
satisfies
\epsilon
-LDP.

A.5
Compatibility

Our approach can be seamlessly integrated with mechanisms that achieve randomization by sampling noise from Laplace, Gaussian, or other distributions and adding it to the representation.
Furthermore, it is compatible with mechanisms based on probabilistic sampling designed to preserve directional information
[
8
,
4
]
.
This is achieved simply by incorporating an additional step to transform
\bar{\boldsymbol{z}}
into the required input format of the target mechanism, followed by an inverse transformation prior to applying the post-processing function.

A.6
Aggregation

Because of the heavy randomization in LDP, practical applications frequently adopt an aggregation step to average out the injected noise.
In our proposed approach, this aggregation takes place between the randomization and post-processing steps.
Since we inject zero-mean, isotropic noise during the randomization, the aggregation step acts as an unbiased estimator, canceling out the noise as the number of samples increases.
Our post-processing function is applied to this aggregated output, ensuring that the resulting representations perform effectively in the downstream model.

A.7
Privacy-Preserving Federated Learning

Our approach is applicable to federated learning (FL) employing LDP for privacy preservation.
Consider an FL system where
K
clients (
K\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
) compute and send their local gradients
\boldsymbol{g}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
k\in\{1,\dots,K\}
to a central server, which aggregates them to update the global model and distributes the updated model to the clients.
Assuming an untrusted server and network, clients apply LDP to randomize their gradients prior to transmission.
Let
\boldsymbol{g}_{i}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the gradient computed from the
i
-th sample (
i\in\{1,\dots,N\},N\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
) at the
k
-th client.
The gradient
\boldsymbol{g}_{i}^{(k)}
consists of
d
components, expressed as
\boldsymbol{g}_{i}^{(k)}=\begin{bmatrix}g_{1i}^{(k)}&\dots&g_{di}^{(k)}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
.
For simplicity, we assume that each component lies within a bounded range.
Without considering privacy preservation, the local gradient
\boldsymbol{g}^{(k)}
is computed as:
\boldsymbol{g}^{(k)}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{g}_{i}^{(k)}
.
When applying LDP for privacy preservation, two conventional methods are used for randomizing the gradient.
The first directly randomizes each
\boldsymbol{g}_{i}^{(k)}
prior to averaging.
The second adds noise directly to the aggregated mean, utilizing the sensitivity of the mean function determined by the bounded
\boldsymbol{g}_{i}^{(k)}
.
In contrast to these methods, one can preserve privacy by first permuting the
N
-dimensional vectors, denoted as
\begin{bmatrix}g_{j1}^{(k)}&\dots&g_{jN}^{(k)}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
for
j\in\{1,\dots,d\}
, and then randomizing them using an LDP mechanism
\mathcal{M}
.
In this context, the downstream operation (or model) is represented as an
N
-dimensional row vector
\boldsymbol{W}=\frac{1}{N}\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times N}
, which corresponds to the element-wise summation and scaling of the permuted vectors.
The mechanism
\mathcal{M}
is applied between our pre-processing and post-processing steps.
Notably, this method can also be extended to central differential privacy (CDP).

We evaluate our method across three downstream tasks, including one time-series regression and two image classification tasks.
The evaluation covers a range of privacy budgets (

m\in\{16,32,64\}

Baseline

We compare against representative LDP mean estimation mechanisms:

•

Laplace

[
21
]
: This mechanism clips the input vector to an
\ell_{1}
-ball of radius
\rho
and adds Laplace noise calibrated to the corresponding sensitivity. It serves as a fundamental pure

•

AGM

[
6
]
: This method clips the input to an
\ell_{2}
-ball and adds isotropic Gaussian noise calibrated via the analytical Gaussian mechanism. It satisfies
(\epsilon,\delta)
-LDP and is widely used as a strong reference for approximate LDP.

•

PrivUnit2

[

and PrivUnitG

[

:

Instance Optimal

[
32

[
5

-LDP setting.

•

TASK

[
9
]
: This baseline is the most closely related work to ours, as it also utilizes task-relevant functional information for linear models.

[
43

Since our proposed approach induces coordinate-wise disparities through its singular-value weighting

All clipping thresholds

Our proposed approach is mechanism-agnostic.
It exploits task-relevant subspaces without altering the internal noise-generation procedures of the underlying LDP mechanisms, and can be seamlessly integrated with existing approaches.
To demonstrate this broad applicability and orthogonal benefit, our evaluation presents comprehensive results combining our method with a diverse set of representative baselines.
Specifically, we show that our method consistently enhances the utility of Laplace, PrivUnit2, PrivUnitG, and CW, proving its effectiveness as a versatile, standalone enhancement.

Public and Private Datasets.

The dataset is partitioned both by household and chronologically into training and test sets, which serve as the public and private datasets, respectively.

N

\lfloor 2547/N\rfloor
households, ensuring that no household appears in more than one round.
On each date,

For instance, with
N=20

per round.

m

\epsilon
per household regardless of
N

The evaluation is conducted over
N

For instance, setting
m=16
corresponds to performing
N=20

In our evaluation, the sequential composition of privacy budgets is circumvented.
As detailed above, the partitioning of the household pool into disjoint subsets and the

N

According to the parallel composition theorem of differential privacy, evaluating queries on disjoint subsets of data does not accumulate the privacy cost.
Therefore, the total privacy guarantee for any individual household remains bounded by the single-query budget
\epsilon

For the image classification tasks using MNIST, CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-10-C, unlike the regression task, we apply
item-level
LDP, where the privacy guarantee is provided for each individual image sample.

Public and Private Datasets.

Consistent with the regression task, the training datasets are treated as public.
These are further partitioned into training and validation sets with an 80:20 split to pre-train the public feature extractor and classifiers.
Conversely, the test datasets are treated as private, serving as the evaluation data to assess the utility of our method under LDP constraints.

Evaluation Protocol.

We consider a distributed setting where a central server aggregates representations
\boldsymbol{z}
from
K
virtual clients.
These representations are extracted by processing private test images through a public feature extractor.
For an
N
-round evaluation, the 10,000 samples in the private test dataset are partitioned into
N
disjoint subsets and randomly assigned to clients, regardless of their class labels.
For instance, with
N=10000
and
K=1
, each client is allocated 1 unique test sample.
To avoid privacy budget accumulation, we ensure that each representation vector is transmitted only once across the
N
rounds.

Utility Metrics and Composition.

Utility is quantified by both classification accuracy.
From a privacy perspective, because each representation is transmitted only once, the budget does not accumulate over multiple rounds.

A.8.4
Downstream Models

Linear Regression.

For the regression task, we evaluate the utility of our representations using the London smart meter dataset.
The input feature is a
m
-dimensional representation vector
\boldsymbol{z}
, constructed from a sliding window of
m
consecutive days of energy data.
The models are trained via OLS.
The objective is to predict an energy metric for the following day, such as the
energy_mean
.
The OLS algorithm fits the input data to learn a coefficient vector
\boldsymbol{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and an intercept scalar, minimizing the mean squared error (MSE) between the predicted and actual target.

Linear Classifier.

In our experiments, we use a multinomial linear regression model for CIFAR-10 classification.
Specifically, we utilize a linear classifier head corresponding to the final fully-connected layer of the ResNet-20 architecture.
This head directly maps the fixed 64-dimensional feature vectors, obtained from the pre-trained feature extractor, to 10 class logits.
Under this standard pre-training, the model achieves an accuracy over 90% on the CIFAR-10 validation set.
Since the classifier relies on a single linear layer mapping to 10 output classes, the Jacobian matrix of its outputs with respect to the input representations has a rank of at most 10.
This restricted rank bounds the task-relevant subspace.

Non-linear MLP Classifier.

To demonstrate that our approach generalizes to complex non-linear settings, we additionally employ Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) classifiers.
For the CIFAR-10 dataset, the MLP processes 64-dimensional ResNet features through two hidden layers with widths of 10 and 32, interleaved with non-linear activations (ReLU, GELU, or Tanh).
To evaluate our method across different input dimensions, we also apply a ReLU-based MLP with an identical architecture to MNIST classification, taking an
m
-dimensional latent vector (
m=32
) from a jointly fine-tuned VAE encoder as input.

A.8.5
Feature Extraction

For the image classification tasks, we employ two distinct models for feature extraction: a standard Variational Autoencoder (VAE) and a ResNet-20 architecture.

The VAE is applied to the MNIST dataset to facilitate experiments across various latent dimensions, enabling us to evaluate the impact of dimensionality on LDP utility.
Specifically, we investigate latent representation vectors of size
m=32
.

Conversely, we utilize ResNet-20 for the CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-10-C datasets.
Unlike the simple grayscale digits in MNIST, CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-10-C consist of complex color images, necessitating a feature extractor capable of processing multi-channel inputs.
ResNet-20 was chosen for its optimal balance of efficiency and performance; it is a relatively lightweight model that minimizes computational overhead while achieving approximately 90% accuracy on CIFAR-10.
To construct the base representations for our evaluations, we utilize the pre-trained ResNet-20 to extract a fixed 64-dimensional feature vector, which serves as the input to the downstream linear classifier.

VAE.
For the MNIST dataset, we pre-train a standard Variational Autoencoder (VAE) composed of multi-layer perceptrons (MLPs) using the public dataset.
The encoder network takes the flattened
28\times 28
grayscale images as input and processes them through two shared hidden layers, each containing 512 units with ReLU activations.
This is followed by two parallel linear layers that output the mean and log-variance of the
m
-dimensional latent space. The decoder mirrors this architecture, mapping the latent vector back to the original image space through two 512-unit hidden layers, culminating in a sigmoid output layer.
The VAE is trained to minimize the standard objective function, combining binary cross-entropy for reconstruction loss and Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence.
We utilize the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of
10^{-3}
and a batch size of 512. To prevent overfitting, training incorporates early stopping with a patience of 10 epochs, monitoring the validation loss on the 20% split of the public training data.

Following this unsupervised pre-training, the encoder is jointly fine-tuned end-to-end with the downstream classifiers using cross-entropy loss.
This joint optimization ensures that the encoder generates task-specific representations optimized for classification accuracy rather than solely for image reconstruction.
Finally, for the downstream LDP evaluations, we deterministically extract the mean vector directly from this fine-tuned encoder to serve as the base representation vector, bypassing the reparameterization trick to ensure stable evaluation.

ResNet-20.
For the CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-10-C datasets, we implement a standard ResNet-20 architecture as the feature extractor.
The network processes the 3-channel color images through an initial convolutional layer (16 channels), followed by three sequential stages of residual blocks.
Each stage consists of three basic blocks, progressively increasing the feature channels from 16 to 32 and finally to 64, with spatial downsampling applied via strided convolutions.
Network weights are initialized using Kaiming normal initialization.
To extract the representation vector
\boldsymbol{z}
, the final feature map is processed through an adaptive average pooling layer and flattened, yielding a 64-dimensional deterministic feature vector.

Similar to the pre-trained classifiers, the feature extractors are pre-trained exclusively on public datasets, specifically the training sets of MNIST and CIFAR-10.
During evaluation, these extractors process inputs from private datasets (the test sets of MNIST, CIFAR-10, and CIFAR-10-C) to produce an
m
-dimensional vector
\boldsymbol{z}
as output.

Clipping Threshold.

The performance of the proposed approach (PA) is evaluated on the CIFAR-10-C dataset under maximum brightness corruption (Level 5).
We specifically investigate the effect of different clipping thresholds
\rho
on ReLU-based non-linear MLP classifiers.

PA employs
\ell_{1}
-norm clipping within the transformed space
\boldsymbol{L}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\mu})
, restricting the representations to the bounded space
\bar{\mathcal{Z}}
.
Notably,
\ell_{1}
-norm clipping only modifies the magnitude of the representations while preserving their angular information.
The clipping threshold
\rho
is determined based on a specific percentile of the public data (e.g., the original CIFAR-10 training set).

Tables 4 presents the results of integrating the PA with the Laplace
[
21
]
, PrivUnit2
[
8
]
and PrivUnitG
[
4
]
mechanisms, across clipping thresholds
\rho
ranging from 10 to 100.

Overall, the mechanisms integrated with the PA achieve higher accuracy when a lower
\rho
is applied.
Across all three mechanisms, tighter clipping thresholds (10th percentile) consistently yielded superior accuracy.
Although omitted here for brevity, our approach reached peak performance at the 30th percentile of
\rho
for the ReLU-based MLP classifier operating on VAE-encoded MNIST features.

Despite the increased distortion of individual representations due to clipping, the substantial reduction in injected noise scale dominates.
This confirms that
noise scale is the primary bottleneck under LDP constraints, rather than the distortion induced by clipping
.

The interaction between privacy budget
\epsilon
and clipping threshold
\rho
reveals that the marginal benefit of tight clipping grows with larger
\epsilon
, implying that clipping strategy becomes increasingly critical as the privacy constraint is relaxed.

The ablation results demonstrate that
both pre-processing and post-processing components are indispensable
.
Removing either component causes all three mechanisms to collapse to near-random performance (10%), regardless of the privacy budget.
This confirms that the two components are not merely complementary enhancements but jointly necessary for our approach to function.

A notable instability is observed when post-processing is ablated: the standard deviation of accuracy grows substantially with increasing
\epsilon
, reaching up to 0.026 for PrivUnit2+PA and 0.025 for PrivUnitG+PA at
\epsilon=10
.
This suggests that without post-processing, the representations become erratic as the privacy budget relaxes, undermining the reliability of downstream classification.

Compared to the
\ell_{1}
-clipping baseline, the full PA integration achieves up to 90% relative improvement (Laplace,
\epsilon=10
), demonstrating that the utility gains stem not from clipping alone, but from the coordinated interaction of the pre- and post-processing pipeline introduced by PA.

To disentangle the internal mechanics of the post-processing phase, we evaluate a "w/o Reshaping" variant, which retains the noise rotation but omits the scaling procedure.
Across Laplace, PrivUnit2, and PrivUnitG, the performance of this variant falls between the fully ablated post-processing baseline and the full PA integration.
For instance, with PrivUnit2 at
\epsilon=10
, "w/o Reshaping" variant recovers substantial utility (0.5117) compared to the complete absence of post-processing (0.0875), yet still falls short of the full PA (0.5829).
This result implies that while directional alignment (rotation) alone successfully mitigates a portion of the noise impact, structural scaling (reshaping) is necessary to fully utilize the proposed approach.

A further distinction emerges when comparing the "w/o Reshaping" variant against the direct
\ell_{1}
-clipping baseline applied in the original space
\mathcal{Z}
.
Although both configurations omit anisotropic noise scaling, they differ in one fundamental respect.
The "w/o Reshaping" variant applies
\ell_{1}
-clipping within the transformed space
\boldsymbol{L}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\mu})
, where the axes are not only aligned with the task-critical subspaces but also inversely scaled according to their respective importance.
In contrast, the direct
\ell_{1}
-clipping baseline operates in the raw space
\mathcal{Z}
without any such alignment.
The consistent performance gap between these two variants across all three mechanisms demonstrates that clipping in a task-aligned space is inherently beneficial even without rescaling.
This highlights that the utility of PA stems from the interaction of three key components: space alignment, sensitivity bounding in the transformed space, and anisotropic rescaling.

A.8.9
Main Results: Accuracy of Non-Linear MLP Classifiers with ReLU Activations on CIFAR-10-C (Brightness, Fog, Defocus Blur, and Gaussian) Test Datasets

A central assumption of our approach is that the Jacobian computed on public data captures the task-critical subspaces of the private data.
To stress-test this assumption, we evaluate PA across four corruption types (Brightness, Fog, Defocus Blur, and Gaussian Noise) and five severity levels on CIFAR-10-C, yielding 20 distinct distribution shift scenarios.
These corruptions vary not only in severity but also in nature: Brightness and Fog primarily alter global statistics, Defocus Blur degrades spatial frequency, and Gaussian Noise directly corrupts pixel-level information, offering a diverse probe of how different types of public-private mismatch affect the utility of Jacobian-based subspace identification.

A direct comparison of absolute accuracy across severities is confounded by the fact that the no-randomization baseline, which serves as a natural upper bound on achievable utility, itself degrades as corruption severity increases. For instance, under Gaussian Noise at severity 5, the no-randomization accuracy drops to 0.2621, leaving far less room for any mechanism to demonstrate improvement.
To account for this, we measure the performance of PA in terms of the fraction of recoverable accuracy it restores.
Formally, for each corruption type and severity, we define the recoverable gap as the difference between the no-randomization accuracy and the base mechanism accuracy, and the performance gain as the improvement brought by integrating PA:

\text{Recovery Ratio}=\frac{\text{Mean Acc(Baseline+PA)}-\text{Mean Acc(Baseline)}}{\text{Mean Acc(No Randomization)}-\text{Mean Acc(Baseline)}}

A higher recovery ratio indicates that PA recovers a larger fraction of the utility lost to LDP noise, independent of the absolute difficulty imposed by the corruption.
This metric cleanly isolates PA’s contribution from the confounding effect of corruption-induced accuracy degradation.

Tables 6 through 10 present the accuracy of PrivUnit2+PA and PrivUnitG+PA across all 20 distribution shift scenarios, while Figure 7 summarizes the corresponding recovery ratios at representative privacy budgets
\epsilon\in\{1.0,3.0,5.0,7.5\}
.

In summary, the results demonstrate that
PA is robust to both the type and severity of practical distribution shifts
.

These results show several consistent patterns.
First, PA consistently enhances utility across all 20 scenarios.
The recovery ratio remains positive for every combination of corruption type and severity.
This confirms that the Jacobian computed on clean public data remains a viable proxy for identifying task-critical subspaces even when private data undergoes substantial distributional shift.
Such findings provide direct empirical support for our central hypothesis.

Second, the recovery ratio scales positively with the privacy budget.
Across all corruption types and severities, the ratio grows monotonically with
\epsilon
.
This reflects the fact that PA becomes increasingly effective as the noise scale decreases, a trend observed without exception across all 20 scenarios.

However, interpretations of absolute accuracy necessitate careful consideration.
The no-randomization accuracy, which serves as the upper bound on recoverable utility, itself degrades as corruption severity increases.
This is particularly evident under Gaussian Noise, where the no-randomization accuracy drops to 0.2621 at severity 5 and drastically narrows the recoverable gap.
Consequently, a lower recovery ratio for a specific mechanism, such as Laplace+PA, may not stem from a failure of PA to improve utility.
Instead, it may result from a disproportionately large gap created by the baseline mechanism’s own instability.
The recovery ratio metric effectively decouples these distinct sources of variation.

Furthermore, the type of corruption modulates the stability of subspace identification.
Brightness corruption yields a nearly constant recovery ratio across severities, suggesting that luminance shifts leave the downstream model’s gradient directions largely intact.
In contrast, Fog and Defocus Blur show a modest decline in recovery ratio as severity increases.
This might be due to the progressive degradation of spatial frequency content, which creates a mismatch between public and private feature distributions.
Gaussian Noise, conversely, exhibits an apparent increase in the recovery ratio at higher severities.
This is not indicative of enhanced PA effectiveness.
Rather, it reflects the rapid contraction of the recoverable gap as no-randomization accuracy collapses, which mechanically inflates the ratio even when absolute gains remain stable.
