Comments on “Arterial flow in healthy individuals and patients with hemorrhoidal disease: a Doppler ultrasound–based pathophysiological analysis”
Authors: Khadeeja Iftikhar, Muhammad Fahad Abdullah, Muhammad Ramish Saeed, Muhammad Abdullah Awan
Published: 2026

Dear Editor:

We read with great interest the article by Gravante et al. [1], which used Doppler transperineal ultrasound (TPUS) to investigate arterial flow in healthy individuals and patients with hemorrhoidal disease (HD). The study offers valuable insight into the vascular component of HD, particularly the finding of significantly elevated peak systolic velocity (PSV) in advanced Goligher grades. By employing a non-invasive imaging method, the authors contribute to the growing evidence supporting vascular dysfunction as a pathophysiological mechanism of HD.

However, several methodological and interpretive issues merit consideration. First, the cross-sectional design prevents causal inference. It remains unclear whether altered hemodynamics precede hemorrhoidal development or result from chronic venous congestion. Longitudinal follow-up of “vascular-pattern” healthy controls could clarify this relationship. Moreover, the study’s reliance on a single, unblinded operator may also introduce measurement bias, especially in the subjective classification of “vascular” versus “scattered” patterns. Multi-operator, blinded validation with interobserver variability testing would enhance reproducibility.

Second, the sample composition warrants caution. Participants ranged from 19 to 80 years, encompassing wide age-related variations in vascular compliance and endothelial function. Aging, coupled with gut microbiome changes, can influence systemic and splanchnic circulation [2]. Furthermore, controls drawn from surgical outpatient populations (inguinal hernias, sebaceous cysts, or lipomas) may differ from community-based volunteers in comorbidities or bowel habits, potentially introducing selection bias.

Third, the study did not control for major confounding factors known to affect anorectal hemodynamics, such as BMI, constipation, parity, smoking, liver disease, diet, and medication use. Prior work by Godeberge et al. [3] incorporated these covariates in multivariate models, identifying BMI, constipation, age, and parity as independent predictors of hemorrhoidal disease severity. Including similar adjustments in Doppler analyses would improve the validity of vascular associations.

Fourth, the Doppler evaluation in this study was limited to peak systolic velocity (PSV), end-diastolic velocity (EDV), and resistance index (RI). In contrast, Parello et al. [4] performed a more comprehensive hemodynamic assessment that included pulsatility index (PI) and acceleration time (AT), reporting significantly elevated pre-operative PSV, PI, and RI values that showed marked postoperative reductions following Doppler-guided dearterialization. These findings parallel the results of Gravante et al. and suggest that expanding the Doppler parameters in future TPUS-based research could provide a more detailed characterization of anorectal vascular resistance, compliance, and their dynamic changes across disease stages and treatment responses.

Lastly, while the authors suggest that TPUS may, in the future, serve as an adjunct diagnostic and follow-up tool, this application remains hypothetical and requires validation against established modalities such as endoanal ultrasound or videoanoscopy, which have demonstrated superior diagnostic accuracy and reproducibility in detecting hemorrhoidal disease [5].

In summary, Gravante et al. provide important evidence supporting the vascular contribution to hemorrhoidal disease through TPUS-based assessment. Nonetheless, methodological refinements, such as longitudinal design, confounder adjustment, and inclusion of additional Doppler parameters, are needed to strengthen these findings. Future validation against established diagnostic modalities will be essential to define the true clinical value of TPUS in the evaluation and management of hemorrhoidal disease.
