Sparse POD Mode Selection and Manifold Dimensionality Reduction with Neural Networks Thanks: Submitted to the editors August 10, 2026.
Tomoki Koike, Prakash Mohan, Marc T. Henry de Frahan, Elizabeth Qian, Julie Bessac

Abstract
High-performance computing enables simulation of high-dimensional physical systems, but downstream analyses such as inverse problems and control remain computationally expensive, motivating model order reduction (MOR) to construct efficient low-dimensional surrogates. Proper Orthogonal Decomposition (POD), a widely adopted data-driven MOR method, projects dynamics onto linear subspaces spanned by the most energetic modes. However, POD struggles for problems with slowly decaying Kolmogorov n n -widths, such as advection-dominated and turbulent flows, requiring many modes for accurate reconstruction. Moreover, energy-based selection can discard crucial low-energy modes needed to capture small-scale features. Recent nonlinear manifold methods using polynomial mappings with alternating or greedy mode selection achieve better reconstruction with fewer modes. However, these methods fix the nonlinear mapping form a priori, limiting expressivity. Conversely, neural network (NN) manifolds offer greater expressivity but employ energy-based selection. We present SparseModesNet, a dimensionality reduction framework that employs linear encoding via POD modes and nonlinear NN decoding. The decoder leverages LassoNet, a method enforcing hierarchical sparsity through residual connections with linear skip layers, to simultaneously select informative POD modes and learn a nonlinear mapping that minimizes reconstruction error. On benchmark advection-dominated and chaotic flows, SparseModesNet matches or exceeds state-of-the-art performance. For turbulent channel flow at friction Reynolds number R ​ e τ = 5200 Re_{\tau}=5200 , we reduce reconstruction error by 51–78% compared to existing polynomial manifold methods while maintaining interpretability through physically meaningful mode selection.

Introduction

Many computational tasks in science and engineering require solving high-dimensional
partial differential equations (PDEs)
, which remains computationally expensive despite advances in high-performance computing.
Model order reduction (MOR)
methods address this by creating low-dimensional surrogate models that accurately reconstruct high-dimensional dynamics at significantly reduced cost, enabling downstream analyses such as inverse problems, control, and optimization
[
7
,
51
,
40
,
2
]
. Among these,
Proper Orthogonal Decomposition (POD)
has emerged as a widely used approach that identifies high-energy modes in data and constructs efficient
reduced-order models (ROMs)
by projecting dynamics onto a low-dimensional subspace spanned by these modes
[
33
,
56
,
8
]
.

However,
POD
’s limitation to linear subspaces causes it to underperform for transport-dominated, advection-dominated, or turbulent systems. In such cases, slow spectral decay, known as the Kolmogorov
n
-width barrier, requires retaining prohibitively many modes for accuracy, undermining the primary goal of
MOR

[
18
]
. Recent work has proposed addressing this barrier by moving beyond linear subspaces to nonlinear manifolds that allow nonlinear combinations of
POD
modes
[
1
,
48
]
.

Some researchers have explored nonlinear manifold representations that augment linear
POD
subspaces with quadratic
[
50
,
69
,
35
,
57
]
or higher-order polynomial mappings
[
26
]
. These approaches have successfully constructed
ROMs
for advection-dominated problems via manifold projection
[
5
]
and nonintrusive inference (i.e., without access to underlying code)
[
28
]
, demonstrating potential to overcome the Kolmogorov barrier with fewer modes. However, many rely on heuristic energy-based mode selection, where modes are ordered by energy retention and then truncated at fixed thresholds. This selection can be suboptimal since low-energy modes may be critical to capture dynamics such as energy dissipation in turbulent flows
[
4
,
12
]
.

Recent works have proposed other mode selection strategies. In
[
26
,
27
]
, the authors define a polynomial mapping that decodes a reduced
POD
representation and optimize both the linear basis and polynomial coefficients through an alternating minimization scheme, while
[
60
]
proposes a greedy algorithm that iteratively selects modes based on their contribution to reducing state reconstruction error, later extended to sparse data in
[
58
]
. While advancing beyond energy-based truncation, these methods face two limitations. First, to capture increasingly complex dynamics with higher accuracy requires higher-degree polynomials; including all cross-terms among the reduced variables then incurs combinatorial growth in the number of coefficients, creating a tradeoff between expressivity and computational tractability. Second, mode selection is performed separately from the mapping optimization rather than jointly, which may yield suboptimal mode-mapping combinations. Importantly, while the polynomial coefficients are optimized, the functional form of the mapping (e.g., quadratic) is prescribed a priori rather than learned from data, which limits expressivity.

Neural networks (NNs)
offer an alternative approach to overcoming the Kolmogorov
n
-width barrier by learning nonlinear manifold representations directly from data. A prevalent architecture is the
autoencoder (AE)
, which consists of an encoder that compresses high-dimensional states into a low-dimensional latent representation and a decoder that reconstructs the original state from this representation. Unlike polynomial mappings with predetermined functional forms,
AEs
learn both the compression and reconstruction mappings from data, enabling greater flexibility in capturing complex nonlinear structure. The work
[
42
]
first applied deep convolutional
AEs
to construct trial manifolds for
MOR
, demonstrating improved reconstruction over linear methods for advection-dominated problems. Subsequent developments have extended this framework in several directions:
[
55
]
incorporates hyper-reduction techniques to lower online computational costs,
[
66
]
generalizes convolutional architectures to unstructured meshes common in finite element discretizations, and
[
14
]
introduces adaptive decoder filters that evolve with the latent state. Despite these advances,
AE
-based methods encode information into latent variables that lack direct physical meaning, defying
interpretability
of the learned representations.

In the context of
MOR
, interpretability can be considered the ability to understand which physical features or modes the reduced model captures and how they contribute to the reconstruction
[
15
]
. Several works have pursued this goal by imposing structure on the learned latent space according to physical considerations:
[
36
]
employs self-supervised learning to produce embeddings distinguishable according to physical parameters,
[
63
]
uses
\beta
-variational
AEs
to learn disentangled, near-orthogonal latent variables, and
[
70
]
encodes frequency information to yield latents corresponding to distinct temporal scales. While these methods endow the latent space with meaningful structure, the resulting representations remain dense—all latent coordinates contribute to every reconstruction, making it difficult to isolate which modes are essential for a given state. A complementary principle from interpretable machine learning is that
sparsity
, the use of fewer active features, enhances interpretability by enabling direct examination of individual contributions
[
65
]
. In the
MOR
setting, sparse representations that activate only a subset of modes would allow practitioners to identify precisely which physical structures are necessary for accurate reconstruction.

This work bridges interpretable nonlinear manifolds with sparse mode selection and expressive
NN
decoders. We adapt LassoNet
[
43
]
, a framework enforcing hierarchical feature sparsity where features participate in
NNs
only if corresponding linear skip weights are active, to transform the combinatorial mode selection problem into a continuous regularization path promoting sparsity during
NN
training. While LassoNet remains underexplored in scientific computing beyond limited applications such as physics-informed neural networks for inverse problems
[
44
]
, we demonstrate its efficacy for mode selection with a
NN
decoder, enabling automated, scalable selection of minimal sets of physically interpretable
POD
modes while leveraging
NN
expressivity.

The contributions of this work are:

•

We propose SparseModesNet, a novel framework that combines the interpretability of
POD
modes with
NN
expressivity, enabling sparse mode selection during
NN
training and accurate reconstruction of dynamical systems with slowly decaying Kolmogorov
n
-widths.

•

We demonstrate SparseModesNet’s effectiveness on benchmark problems and turbulent channel flow, showcasing its ability to select minimal mode sets while maintaining high state and mode reconstruction accuracy.

•

We analyze the interpretability of our
NN
decoder by computing modes of the reconstructed data and provide physical insights into selected modes based on their order and characteristics.

Several recent works have also combined
NN
expressivity with physically meaningful
POD
bases for interpretable nonlinear decoders. For example,
[
20
,
6
]
employ small linear
POD
bases augmented with
NN
correction terms, preserving interpretability while harnessing nonlinear expressivity. The authors of
[
64
]
extend this approach by applying
NN
corrections to both encoder and decoder, each weighted with trainable coefficients for improved fitting. Alternatively, some works augment linear reduction with nonlinear manifolds derived from probabilistic embeddings
[
29
]
, user-defined reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces
[
21
]
, or Gaussian processes
[
3
]
. Although these hybrid approaches maintain physical interpretability and sparsity by selecting few modes, they rely on energy-based heuristics rather than simultaneously identifying essential modes from a candidate set and learning the nonlinear manifold.

Another related work
[
19
]
recently introduced Decomposed Sparse Modal Optimization (DESMO), which leverages the Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics (SINDy) framework
[
11
,
13
,
25
]
to discover interpretable governing equations by promoting sparsity in candidate function libraries. DESMO applies SINDy to
POD
or
AE
-learned bases by constructing a nonlinear candidate library from modes using predefined basis functions and employing
\ell_{1}
-regularization to select the fewest active terms. While DESMO promotes sparsity in functional representations of a given modal basis, our approach identifies which modes themselves should be retained and simultaneously learns an expressive
NN
manifold tailored to those selected modes.

Background

This section covers the necessary background for this work. In
Section

2.1
, we explain the concepts of encoder-decoder pairs in the context of dynamical systems. In
Section

2.2
, we introduce LassoNet, a
NN
framework for sparse feature selection, which we adapt for mode selection in this work.

2.1
State Encoder and Decoder on Manifolds

2.1.1
Encoder-Decoder for Dimensionality Reduction

Let a state vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
represent the dynamical system’s state at time
t
. The state
\mathbf{x}
is typically high-dimensional and evolves over time according to the initial value problem:

\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)=f(\mathbf{x}(t)),\quad\mathbf{x}(t_{0})=\mathbf{x}_{0},

(1)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
governs the dynamics and
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the initial state. We solve (
1
) at discrete time instances
t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{n}
to collect snapshot data of the system’s evolution. A snapshot matrix
\mathbf{X}=[\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
with
\mathbf{x}_{i}=\mathbf{x}(t_{i})
is then formed from these data.

Dimensionality reduction seeks a lower-dimensional representation of the state vector by mapping it to reduced coordinates in a latent space
\mathbb{R}^{r}
, where
r\ll d
. This mapping is realized by an
encoder

\mathcal{E}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{r}
that compresses
\mathbf{x}
to a latent variable
\mathbf{z}=\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
, together with a
decoder

\mathcal{D}:\mathbb{R}^{r}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
that reconstructs an approximation of the original state:
\mathbf{x}\approx(\mathcal{D}\circ\mathcal{E})(\mathbf{x})
. The quality of this approximation is measured by the
reconstruction error
:

\mathsf{RE}(\mathbf{x};\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-(\mathcal{D}\circ\mathcal{E})(\mathbf{x}_{i})\|_{2}^{2},

(2)

which quantifies the discrepancy between the snapshots and their reconstructions.

The simplest encoder-decoder pair is linear. Let
\mathbf{X}\approx\mathbf{U}_{s}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{s}\mathbf{V}_{s}^{\top}
be the rank-
s

Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)
of the snapshot matrix, where
s\leq\min{\{d,n\}}
, the matrices
\mathbf{U}_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times s}
and
\mathbf{V}_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times s}
contain the left and right singular vectors, and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times s}
is the diagonal matrix of singular values
\sigma_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{s}>0
. The columns of
\mathbf{U}_{s}
are the
POD
modes, which form an orthonormal basis capturing the most energetic features of the data
[
8
,
33
]
. A linear encoder-decoder pair selects a subset of
r<s
modes, indexed by
\mathcal{I}_{r}\subseteq[s]=\{1,\ldots,s\}
with cardinality
|\mathcal{I}_{r}|=r
, and defines
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{U}_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{x}
and
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{z})=\mathbf{U}_{r}\mathbf{z}
, where
\mathbf{U}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
contains the selected modes.

A common selection strategy is the
energy heuristic
, which retains the leading
r
modes corresponding to the largest singular values. By the Eckart-Young theorem
[
23
,
45
]
, this choice minimizes the reconstruction error among all rank-
r
approximations:

\mathsf{RE}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{U}_{r})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{U}_{r}\mathbf{z}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=r+1}^{\min\{d,n\}}\sigma_{i}^{2}.

(3)

The limitation of linear encoder-decoder pairs is that reducing reconstruction error requires increasing
r
, which becomes prohibitively expensive for systems exhibiting transport-dominated or turbulent dynamics with slowly decaying Kolmogorov
n
-widths
[
48
]
. This motivates the linear encoder and nonlinear decoder pair, which we introduce in the next section.

2.1.2
Linear Encoder with Nonlinear Decoder

Linear encoder-decoder pairs struggle for problems with slowly decaying Kolmogorov
n
-widths, where reducing reconstruction error requires retaining prohibitively many modes. To address this limitation, practitioners have proposed retaining the linear
POD
encoder while augmenting the decoder with a nonlinear correction term:

\mathbf{z}=\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{U}_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{x},\qquad\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{z})=\mathbf{U}_{r}\mathbf{z}+h(\mathbf{z}),

where
h:\mathbb{R}^{r}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a nonlinear function. Here, the
r
modes forming
\mathbf{U}_{r}
need not be the leading modes; they can be any subset of the candidate modes
\mathbf{U}_{s}
. Since the nonlinear correction term
h
should remain low-dimensional for efficient evaluation, a natural choice for its input is the reduced representation
\mathbf{z}
. This decoder structure with polynomial nonlinear mappings was introduced by
[
50
]
and has since been adopted in various model reduction contexts
[
69
,
35
,
57
,
22
,
28
,
60
]
.

As shown in
[
28
, eq. 14]
, the optimal nonlinear correction term
h
(in the linear least-squares sense) maps the reduced representation
\mathbf{z}
to the orthogonal complement of the subspace spanned by
\mathbf{U}_{r}
. That is,
h
captures dynamics not represented by the selected modes
\mathbf{U}_{r}
alone, thereby enhancing expressivity beyond what the linear term provides.

However, an arbitrary
h
may not satisfy this orthogonality condition, introducing redundancy with the linear term and potentially degrading reconstruction accuracy. Yet designing
h
to satisfy orthogonality a priori is nontrivial. To enforce orthogonality explicitly, many existing works
[
35
,
57
,
22
,
5
,
28
,
26
,
27
,
60
]
define the decoder as

\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{z})=\mathbf{U}_{r}\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{W}h(\mathbf{z}),

(4)

where
h:\mathbb{R}^{r}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times p}
satisfies
\mathbf{U}^{\top}_{r}\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{0}
for a mapping dimension
p
. This formulation augments the linear reconstruction with information in its orthogonal complement through the composition of the nonlinearity
h
and rotation
\mathbf{W}
.

A possible choice is
\mathbf{W}=\overline{\mathbf{U}}_{q}
, where
\overline{\mathbf{U}}_{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times q}
consists of
q
modes from
\mathbf{U}_{s}
that are orthogonal to
\mathbf{U}_{r}
, i.e.,
\overline{\mathbf{U}}_{q}\subseteq\{\mathbf{U}_{j}:j\in[s]\setminus\mathcal{I}_{r}\}
. This formulation, adopted in
[
6
,
26
,
27
,
21
]
, provides a natural interpretation, where
h
maps the reduced coordinates
\mathbf{z}
to coefficients of modes orthogonal to those used in the linear reconstruction. However, this restricts the correction term to the span of modes from the predefined candidate set
\mathbf{U}_{s}
, which may be insufficient in number. Rather, we will learn
\mathbf{W}
from data.

Remark 1
.

A more general representation allows each column of
\mathbf{W}
to be a linear combination of orthogonal modes:
\mathbf{W}(:,i)=\sum_{j\notin\mathcal{I}_{r}}c_{ij}\mathbf{U}_{s}(:,j)
, where
c_{ij}
are combination coefficients. The single-mode-per-column approach is a special case of this formulation.

2.1.3
Linear Encoder with Neural Network Decoder

A key limitation of the nonlinear decoder in (
4
) is that the functional form of
h
is typically fixed a priori (e.g., quadratic polynomials), which restricts expressivity. To overcome this, we parameterize the nonlinear correction term as a
NN
, following
[
6
,
64
]
:

\mathbf{z}=\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{U}_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{x},\qquad\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{z})=\mathbf{U}_{r}\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{W}{h_{\mathrm{NN}}}(\mathbf{z}),

(5)

where
{h_{\mathrm{NN}}}:\mathbb{R}^{r}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
is the
NN
and
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times p}
satisfies
\mathbf{U}_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{0}
but is not restricted to columns from
\mathbf{U}_{s}
. The
NN
learns the nonlinear mapping and weight
\mathbf{W}
from data, while the linear term projects onto selected
POD
modes.

This formulation requires determining both (i) which modes to include in
\mathbf{U}_{r}
and (ii) how to train
{h_{\mathrm{NN}}}
and
\mathbf{W}
for the nonlinear correction—while ensuring orthogonality between the two terms. Rather than treating mode selection and
NN
training as separate problems, we seek a unified framework that jointly optimizes mode selection and the nonlinear mapping to minimize reconstruction error. In the following section, we introduce a feature selection method that enables this joint optimization for
NN
-based decoders.

2.2
LassoNet for Feature Selection

LassoNet
[
43
]
is a
NN
framework for global feature selection that extends feature sparsity from linear models (e.g., Lasso regression) to deep
NNs
. While traditional Lasso employs an
\ell_{1}
-penalty to enforce sparsity on linear weights, LassoNet integrates feature selection into a
NN
via a linear skip (or residual) connection inspired by ResNets (Residual
NNs
)
[
32
]
.

Network Architecture

Consider an input feature vector
\hat{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
. Denote a feed-forward
NN

g_{\mathrm{NN}}(\hat{\mathbf{z}};\mathcal{W},\boldsymbol{\beta}):\mathbb{R}^{s}\to\mathbb{R}^{s}
with weight parameters
\mathcal{W}=\{\mathcal{W}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathcal{W}^{(L)}\}
and bias parameters
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(L)}\}
for
L
layers, which can be expressed as a composition of layer-wise transformations:
g_{\mathrm{NN}}(\hat{\mathbf{z}};\mathcal{W},\boldsymbol{\beta})=g^{(L)}\circ\cdots\circ g^{(1)}(\hat{\mathbf{z}})
,
where
g^{(k)}(\cdot)=\sigma_{k}(\mathcal{W}^{(k)}\cdot+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(k)})
represents the
k
-th layer with weight matrix
\mathcal{W}^{(k)}
, bias vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(k)}
, and activation function
\sigma_{k}(\cdot)
applied element-wise. The LassoNet architecture augments this feed-forward
NN
with a direct linear skip connection:

\mathsf{LassoNet}(\hat{\mathbf{z}};\boldsymbol{\omega},\mathcal{W},\boldsymbol{\beta})=\boldsymbol{\omega}\odot\hat{\mathbf{z}}+g_{\mathrm{NN}}(\hat{\mathbf{z}};\mathcal{W},\boldsymbol{\beta}),

(6)

where
\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
are skip-layer weights,
\odot
denotes element-wise product, and each input feature
\hat{z}_{j}
connects to the corresponding output via skip weight
\omega_{j}
. We denote by
\mathcal{W}_{j}^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}}
the
j
-th column of the first layer weight matrix
\mathcal{W}^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times s}
, representing all connections from input feature
j
to the
p_{1}
units in the first hidden layer.

Objective Function with Hierarchical Constraint

The novelty of LassoNet lies in its training objective that enforces structured sparsity through

\min_{\boldsymbol{\omega},\mathcal{W},\boldsymbol{\beta}}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\mathcal{W},\boldsymbol{\beta})+\lambda\|\boldsymbol{\omega}\|_{1},\quad\text{subject to}\quad\|\mathcal{W}_{j}^{(1)}\|_{\infty}\leq M|\omega_{j}|\quad\forall j,

(7)

where
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\mathcal{W},\boldsymbol{\beta})
is the empirical loss (e.g.,
\mathsf{RE}(\mathbf{x};\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D})
from
eq.

2
),
\lambda\geq 0
controls sparsity via the
\ell_{1}
-penalty, and
M>0
governs the strength of the hierarchical constraint linking skip-layer and first hidden-layer weights.

This hierarchical constraint
\|\mathcal{W}_{j}^{(1)}\|_{\infty}\leq M|\omega_{j}|
is key for feature selection. That is, when the
\ell_{1}
-penalty drives a skip-layer weight
\omega_{j}
to zero, this constraint forces all associated first hidden layer weights
\mathcal{W}_{j}^{(1)}
to zero as well, completely eliminating feature
j
from the network. This differs fundamentally from naïvely applying Lasso penalties to
NN
weights, which produces weight sparsity but not feature removal.

Training via Continuation Strategy

LassoNet employs proximal gradient descent with a new hierarchical proximal operator that simultaneously enforces the
\ell_{1}
-penalty and hierarchical constraint (see
[
43
]
for optimization details).
Critically, rather than solving
eq.

7
for a single fixed
\lambda
, LassoNet implements a
continuation strategy

[
30
,
31
]
that solves a sequence of problems with increasing
\lambda
values
\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}<\cdots<\lambda_{K}
. Starting from
\lambda_{1}\approx 0
(yielding a dense model), each subsequent problem at
\lambda_{k+1}
is initialized from the solution at
\lambda_{k}
, following the solution path as
\lambda
increases toward
\lambda_{K}
. This path-following approach is computationally efficient—each warm-started subproblem converges faster than solving from scratch—and produces a sequence of models (i.e., regularization path
[
47
,
24
]
) with progressively increasing sparsity.

For
POD
mode selection, this continuation strategy provides a systematic framework: as
\lambda
increases along the path,
POD
modes are sequentially removed from the
NN
decoder inputs according to their importance for reconstruction. The sparsest model (fewest
POD
modes) that maintains acceptable reconstruction accuracy can then be selected, yielding an expressive nonlinear manifold with sparse, physically interpretable input features and reduced computational cost automatically.

SparseModesNet: Sparse Mode Selecting Neural Network

This section presents SparseModesNet, our framework for selecting optimal
POD
modes during nonlinear decoder training. We first formulate the mode selection problem in
Section

3.1
. In
Section

3.2
, we show how to restructure the nonlinear decoder into a ResNet-style architecture incorporating feature sparsity
[
43
]
.
Section

3.3
describes the training procedure, and
Section

3.4
discusses implementation details for data normalization and
NN
architecture.

3.1
Mathematical Formulation for Mode Selection

The goal is to select optimal
POD
modes by minimizing reconstruction error while training the
NN
decoder. To select
r
modes from
s
candidate modes, let
\mathbf{S}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times r}
be a column selector matrix
[
9
, Sec. 7.2]
where each column has a single entry of one at the row corresponding to a selected left singular vector in
\mathbf{U}_{s}
, such that
\mathbf{U}_{r}=\mathbf{U}_{s}\,\mathbf{S}_{r}
.

Our
NN
learns the matrix
\mathbf{S}_{r}\mathbf{S}_{r}^{\top}
, which is a diagonal matrix with ones at entries corresponding to selected
r
left singular vectors. Define
\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{i}=\mathbf{U}_{s}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
and a binary vector
\mathbf{w}\in{\{0,1\}}^{s}
with
r
ones at entries corresponding to selected left singular vectors, such that
\mathsf{diag}(\mathbf{w})=\mathbf{S}_{r}\mathbf{S}_{r}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times s}
. The reconstruction error is then

\mathsf{RE}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{w})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl\lVert\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{U}_{s}(\mathbf{w}\odot\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{i})-\mathbf{W}{h_{\mathrm{NN}}}(\mathbf{w}\odot\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{i})\bigr\rVert_{2}^{2}.

(8)

Note the
NN
mapping is now
{h_{\mathrm{NN}}}:\mathbb{R}^{s}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
since the input
\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{i}
is the full projection onto all
s

POD
modes, not just the selected
r
modes. However,
\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{i}
has zeros at rows corresponding to zero elements of
\mathbf{w}
, ultimately selecting
r
modes from
s
candidates.

The set of nonzero entries in
\mathbf{w}
gives the indices of selected modes, i.e.,
\mathcal{I}_{r}=\{i\in[s]:w_{i}=\mathbf{w}(i)\neq 0\}
. We identify
\mathcal{I}_{r}
by training the
NN
to minimize reconstruction error. To do so, we reformulate the nonlinear decoder as a ResNet-style architecture incorporating feature sparsity through a linear skip connection, following the LassoNet framework
[
43
]
, as discussed in the next section.

3.2
LassoNet Formulation of the Nonlinear Decoder

To restructure the nonlinear decoder into a ResNet-style architecture, we first relax the problem to learn a continuous skip weight vector
\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
, allowing more flexible mode selection rather than learning a restrictive binary vector
\mathbf{w}
. We then minimize the reconstruction error over the training data while enforcing two critical constraints.

First, following LassoNet (
7
), we impose an
\ell_{1}
-penalty
\lambda\|\boldsymbol{\omega}\|_{1}
on the skip weights to promote sparsity. This is paired with the hierarchical constraint
\|\mathcal{W}_{j}^{(1)}\|_{\infty}\leq M|\omega_{j}|
on the first-layer
NN
weights, ensuring that if a mode is unselected (
\omega_{j}=0
), all associated first-layer weights
\mathcal{W}_{j}^{(1)}
are also zero, preventing unselected modes from influencing the nonlinear mapping. Second, we impose an
orthogonality constraint

[\mathbf{U}_{s}\mathsf{diag}(\text{1}_{\{\omega_{j}\neq 0\}}(\boldsymbol{\omega}))]^{\top\kern-0.5pt}\mathbf{W}=0
while learning
\mathbf{W}
from data to ensure the linear and nonlinear decoder components do not interfere, maintaining clear separation of their contributions as discussed in
Section

2.1
. Here,
\text{1}_{\{\omega_{j}\neq 0\}}(\boldsymbol{\omega})
is an indicator function returning
1
if
\omega_{j}\neq 0
and
0
otherwise.

Hence, the following optimization enables joint mode selection and
NN
training:

\begin{gathered}\min_{\boldsymbol{\omega},{h_{\mathrm{NN}}},\mathbf{W}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl\lVert\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{U}_{s}(\boldsymbol{\omega}\odot\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{i})-\mathbf{W}{h_{\mathrm{NN}}}(\boldsymbol{\omega}\odot\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{i})\bigr\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|\boldsymbol{\omega}\|_{1}\\
\text{subject to}~~\|\mathcal{W}_{j}^{(1)}\|_{\infty}\leq M|\omega_{j}|~~\text{and}~~{[\mathbf{U}_{s}\mathsf{diag}(\text{1}_{\{\omega_{j}\neq 0\}}(\boldsymbol{\omega}))]}^{\top}\mathbf{W}=0,~\forall j\in[s],\end{gathered}

(9)

where
\mathcal{W}^{(1)}
,
M
, and
\lambda
are equivalent to those in the LassoNet optimization (
7
).

To draw a clear connection to LassoNet, recognize that this decoder closely resembles the LassoNet architecture (
6

NN

\mathcal{D}(\hat{\mathbf{z}})=\underbrace{\mathbf{U}_{s}(\boldsymbol{\omega}\odot\hat{\mathbf{z}})}_{\text{linear skip connection}}+~\quad\underbrace{\mathbf{W}{h_{\mathrm{NN}}}(\boldsymbol{\omega}\odot\hat{\mathbf{z}})}_{\text{neural network}},

3.2.1
Design Choices and Their Theoretical Justification

SparseModesNet differs from the original LassoNet and simple
\ell_{1}
-regularization in two key respects, each essential for guaranteeing monotonic mode elimination (i.e., eliminated modes are never reactivated) when training via the continuation strategy.

Remark 2
(Input masking).

Unlike the original LassoNet (
6
), which inputs the full feature vector
\hat{\mathbf{z}}
to the
NN
, we employ
\boldsymbol{\omega}\odot\hat{\mathbf{z}}
as the
NN
input such that the unselected modes are effectively zero. Here,
\boldsymbol{\omega}
remains a trainable parameter optimized via (
9
), while
\boldsymbol{\omega}\odot\hat{\mathbf{z}}
serves as the parametrized input to
{h_{\mathrm{NN}}}(\cdot)
.

Remark 3
(Hierarchical constraint).

An alternative approach may be to remove the hierarchical constraint and keep only the
\ell_{1}
-penalty, given the latent variable to both the linear and nonlinear terms of the decoder are already masked with
\boldsymbol{\omega}
.

The input masking yields
gradient severance
. The gradient of the loss with respect to the first-layer weights satisfies
\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mathcal{W}_{j}^{(1)}=\mathbf{0}
whenever
\omega_{j}=0
.
The hierarchical constraint
\|\mathcal{W}_{j}^{(1)}\|_{\infty}\leq M|\omega_{j}|
complements this by enforcing
\mathcal{W}_{j}^{(1)}=\mathbf{0}
whenever
\omega_{j}=0
.
Together, these properties completely sever mode
j
’s computational path through the
NN
. No gradient signal can flow to update the zeroed-out weights, preventing the reactivation of an eliminated mode.

Hence, combining gradient severance with the hierarchical constraint guarantees
monotonic mode elimination
. That is, under mild regularity conditions on the solution path, modes eliminated at regularization level
\lambda_{k}
remain eliminated for all subsequent
\lambda_{\ell}>\lambda_{k}
. This monotonicity ensures that SparseModesNet produces a stable, nested sequence of active modes, enabling reliable mode selection. Without both design choices, the nonzero weights may oscillate and spuriously reactivate previously eliminated modes as
\lambda
increases, forcing the network to repeatedly relearn mode suppression.

In
, empirical validations of our design choices are shown through an ablation study comparing SparseModesNet against variants without input masking (
Remark

2
) and without the hierarchical constraint (
Remark

3
), demonstrating how our formulation achieves lower reconstruction error and more reliable mode selection.
However, the monotonic mode elimination alone does not explain the better selection of modes. Specifically, we lack the mathematical machinery to explain how our method is less affected by the spectral bias
[
52
]
as we see in our empirical validations; while possible future directions would be to explore neural tangent kernels (NTKs)
[
34
]
.
Hence, we postpone the formal statement and proof of monotonic mode elimination, including the precise regularity assumptions to
.

3.3
Training the SparseModesNet

SparseModesNet training consists of two sequential phases, the first employs the continuation strategy from LassoNet
[
43
]
to select modes, and the second retrains the decoder with the selected modes.

Phase 1: Mode Selection

We solve (
9
) via continuation, incrementally increasing
\lambda\leftarrow(1+\epsilon)\lambda
with path multiplier
\epsilon>0
to promote sparsity while warm-starting each subproblem with the previous solution. At each gradient descent iteration, we apply a proximal gradient step: (i) compute gradients via backpropagation and update all parameters, then (ii) apply the hierarchical proximal operator from
[
43
, Alg. 2]
to enforce the constraint
\|\mathcal{W}_{j}^{(1)}\|_{\infty}\leq M|\omega_{j}|
. To maintain orthogonality between the linear and nonlinear components, we project
\mathbf{W}
onto the orthogonal complement of selected modes via
\mathbf{W}\leftarrow\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{U}_{s}\mathsf{diag}(\text{1}_{\{\omega_{j}\neq 0\}}(\boldsymbol{\omega}))\mathbf{U}_{s}^{\top}\mathbf{W}
after each parameter update. This phase terminates when the number of active modes reaches the target
r
, yielding the optimal mode selector
\boldsymbol{\omega}
.

Phase 2: Decoder Retraining

With modes selected, we fix
\mathbf{z}_{i}=\mathbf{U}_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
where
\mathbf{U}_{r}=\mathbf{U}_{s}\mathbf{S}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
contains the
r
selected
POD
modes (see
Section

3.1
), and retrain the decoder from scratch without the
\ell_{1}
-penalty or hierarchical constraint:

\min_{{h_{\mathrm{NN}}},\mathbf{W}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl\lVert\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{U}_{r}\mathbf{z}_{i}-\mathbf{W}{h_{\mathrm{NN}}}(\mathbf{z}_{i})\bigr\rVert_{2}^{2}\quad\text{subject to}\quad\mathbf{U}_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{W}=0.

Orthogonality is enforced at each iteration via
\mathbf{W}\leftarrow\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{U}_{r}(\mathbf{U}_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{W})
. Retraining without the penalty allows more effective learning by removing the downward bias on parameters
[
43
]
. Upon convergence with
{h_{\mathrm{NN}}}
fixed, we obtain the optimal
\mathbf{W}
by solving the following linear least-squares problem only once:

\mathbf{W}^{\star}=\arg\min_{\mathbf{W}}\bigl\lVert\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{U}_{r}\mathbf{U}_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{W}{h_{\mathrm{NN}}}(\mathbf{U}_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{X})\bigr\rVert_{F}^{2}+\gamma\|\mathbf{W}\|_{F}^{2},

(10)

where
{h_{\mathrm{NN}}}(\mathbf{U}_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{X})\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
applies
{h_{\mathrm{NN}}}
column-wise and
\gamma
is the
\ell_{2}
-penalty parameter. This least-squares solution automatically satisfies the orthogonality constraint
[
28
]
.

The complete training procedure is detailed in
Algorithms

1
and
2
. Hyperparameters (
r,M,\lambda_{0},\epsilon
) are selected following recommendations from
[
43
]
with problem-specific tuning during experimentation.

3.4
Additional Implementation Details

Data Normalization

We center the data by its temporal mean and apply min-max normalization to scale data to
[-1,1]
, allowing training across different data scales. Centering and normalization are applied before computing
POD
modes. During inference, outputs are denormalized and decentered to the original scale.

Stopping Criteria

Training continues until the number of active modes (nonzero entries in
\boldsymbol{\omega}
) reaches the target
r
. However, if the active mode count remains unchanged for a number of iterations (e.g., 50), we terminate training and select the
r
highest weighted modes in
\boldsymbol{\omega}
, ensuring convergence when the model has stabilized and allowing a fully automated mode selection even when
r
is unknown a priori.

Neural Network Architecture

For
{h_{\mathrm{NN}}}
, we employ a hybrid architecture consisting of a single fully connected layer followed by a Deep Polynomial
NN
(
\Pi
-Net)
[
16
]
. The fully connected layer transforms input modes into a representation suitable for the
\Pi
-Net to capture polynomial nonlinearities and serves as the gate layer where the hierarchical sparsity constraint is applied during training. The
\Pi
-Net then models high-order polynomial functions, enabling rich representation of complex relationships. While this layer can be extended to multiple fully connected layers for more intricate problems, a single layer proved sufficient for the datasets considered.

We experimented with other architectures, including standard multi-layer perceptrons, convolutional neural networks, and U-Nets, but found the hybrid fully connected-
\Pi
-Net architecture provided superior reconstruction accuracy and mode selection for our problems. Further details on the hybrid
\Pi
-Net architecture are provided in
.

Conclusion

In this work, we introduced SparseModesNet, a novel
NN
architecture that automates the selection of sparse, interpretable
POD
modes for nonlinear manifold learning and dimensionality reduction. By integrating LassoNet’s hierarchical
\ell_{1}
-regularization strategy with a nonlinear manifold decoder, SparseModesNet simultaneously learns low-dimensional representations while enforcing sparsity in the selected modes. This approach identifies a small subset of the most relevant modes from a larger candidate set while minimizing reconstruction error.

Our numerical experiments across three problems, the linear transport equation,
KSE
, and turbulent channel flow, demonstrated that SparseModesNet outperforms traditional energy-based mode selection and state-of-the-art greedy algorithms in reconstruction accuracy. The SparseModesNet with
\Pi_{3}
-Net architecture achieves 50–90% reduction in reconstruction error compared to greedy manifold methods for the turbulent channel flow while selecting different modes, confirming that energy content is not an optimal criterion for mode selection in turbulent systems. SparseModesNet accurately reconstructs not only the candidate
POD
modes within the training set but also extrapolates to unseen modes beyond the candidate set. This extrapolation confirms the interpretability of the learned
NN
decoder through its ability to capture underlying physics via physically meaningful
POD
modes, rather than merely memorizing training data. Results highlight the potential of SparseModesNet to facilitate more accurate, interpretable, and efficient reduced-order models across diverse scientific and engineering applications.

Future work could pursue several promising directions. First, integrating SparseModesNet with operator learning methods such as Operator Inference (OpInf)
[
49
,
38
,
46
,
17
]
could enable joint learning of encoder-decoder pairs with
ROMs
. Second, incorporating physical constraints directly into the mode selection and manifold learning process through physics-informed neural networks
[
53
]
could further enhance interpretability and generalization. Finally, we can extend SparseModesNet to other modal bases, such as wavelet bases
[
67
]
, to assess its versatility.

Appendix A
Greedy Quadratic Manifold Construction

We summarize the greedy quadratic manifold (Greedy-QM) approach of
[
60
]
, which serves as a main comparison method in our numerical experiments.

A quadratic manifold decoder augments linear
POD
with a quadratic correction:

\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{z})=\mathbf{U}_{r}\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{W}h_{2}(\mathbf{z}),

where
\mathbf{U}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
contains
r
orthonormal modes,
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times{p_{2}}}
is a weight matrix, and
h_{2}:\mathbb{R}^{r}\to\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}}
with
p_{2}=r(r+1)/2
is the quadratic feature map

h_{2}(\mathbf{z})=\begin{bmatrix}z_{1}z_{1}&z_{1}z_{2}&\cdots&z_{1}z_{r}&z_{2}z_{2}&\cdots&z_{r}z_{r}\end{bmatrix}^{\top},

(16)

for
\mathbf{z}=[z_{1},\ldots,z_{r}]^{\top}
. The encoder is linear:
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{U}_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{x}
.

Let the
j
-th column of
\mathbf{U}
be
\mathbf{u}_{j}
. A key insight of
[
60
]
is that selecting
\mathbf{U}_{r}
as the leading
r

POD
modes (as in
[
28
,
35
]
) can be suboptimal: the resulting latent coordinates
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{U}_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{x}
may discard information critical for the quadratic correction. Instead, the greedy method selects
r
modes
\mathbf{u}_{j_{1}},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{j_{r}}
from a candidate pool of the first
s>r

POD
modes, where the indices
j_{1},\ldots,j_{r}\in[s]
need not be the leading
r
.

In the greedy selection algorithm, given basis
\mathbf{U}_{i-1}=[\mathbf{u}_{j_{1}},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{j_{i-1}}]
with selected index set
\mathcal{I}_{i-1}=\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{i-1}\}
at iteration
i
, the next mode is chosen by

j_{i}=\argmin_{j\in[s]\setminus\mathcal{I}_{i-1}}\min_{\mathbf{W}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times p_{2}}}\left\|\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{P}_{[\mathbf{U}_{i-1},\mathbf{u}_{j}]}\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{W}^{\prime}h_{2}\bigl([\mathbf{U}_{i-1},\mathbf{u}_{j}]^{\top}\mathbf{X}\bigr)\right\|_{F}^{2}+\gamma\|\mathbf{W}^{\prime}\|_{F}^{2},

(17)

where
\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{U}}
denotes the orthogonal projector onto the column space of
\mathbf{U}
and
\gamma>0
is a
\ell_{2}
-regularization parameter. After
r
iterations,
\mathbf{U}_{r}=[\mathbf{U}(:,j_{1}),\ldots,\mathbf{U}(:,j_{r})]
is formed and
\mathbf{W}
is computed via linear least squares. The method exploits the precomputed SVD to reformulate (
17
) so that the computational cost scales with the number of snapshots
n
rather than the state dimension
d
; see
[
60
, Section 3.2]
.

Extension to cubic manifolds

The framework extends to a greedy cubic manifold (Greedy-CM) approach with cubic corrections by replacing (
16
) with a cubic feature map
h_{3}:\mathbb{R}^{r}\to\mathbb{R}^{p_{3}}
containing all cubic monomials:

h_{3}(\mathbf{z})=\begin{bmatrix}z_{1}^{3}&z_{1}^{2}z_{2}&\cdots&z_{1}^{2}z_{r}&z_{1}z_{2}^{2}&z_{1}z_{2}z_{3}&\cdots&z_{r}^{3}\end{bmatrix}^{\top},

where
p_{3}=r(r+1)(r+2)/6
. Note that as
r
increases, the feature dimension
p
and number of parameters in
\mathbf{W}
in the greedy manifold approach grow rapidly, whereas SparseModesNet’s use of
NN
decoder allows to select more modes with smaller feature dimension
p
by training the
NN
in lieu of fixing the mapping form a priori.
