 Abstract

 This study aims to provide the first look at the Electron-Ion Collider’s (EIC) back-

 ground particle hits for the purpose of developing an advanced silicon tracking detector.

 One of the prevailing questions in nuclear physics is the origin of proton mass, proton spin,

 and gluon matter, which can be resolved through electron scattering. The future of scatter-

 ing nuclear physics lies in the construction of the EIC, which will be a unique facility with

 high energy polarized beams alongside heavy ions. Currently, the ePIC collaboration has

 a system of detectors in development for the EIC. This study targets the development of

 an advanced silicon tracking detector called the Fast MAPS Tracker (FMT) which aims to

 improve the resolution of the ePIC system. This detector has a timing resolution of 2 ns that

 is faster than the EIC bunch crossing rate (10.2 ns) and a spatial resolution that is on par

 with the most precise ePIC subsystem. This precision allows the FMT to be uniquely suited

 to separating the background particles produced by sources such as synchrotron radiation

background files to determine various distributions that will be scaled to the FMT timing

window. Due to the fact the FMT has not been incorporated into the ePIC simulations,

these distributions were made by using two reference detectors, the Forward Micro Pattern

Gaseous Detector (MPGD) and the B0 Tracker. As the first look at the EIC background

possible FMT locations.

I. INTRODUCTION

 This study aims to provide invaluable information on EIC background events for the purpose
of developing an advanced silicon detector. The primary goals of this project are to determine
the spatial distributions of hits on detector disks, spatial distributions of hits on one disk’s sen-
sors, the highest expected hit rate per event on one sensor, and the types of particles that are in
the background.

A. Physics Motivation
 One of the biggest mysteries in the field of nuclear physics is the origin of the mass of
a proton. It is known that protons are made up of elementary particles, such as quarks and
gluons, but it is not understood how these particles interact and how they make up the spin
and mass of the proton itself. Additionally, it is not understood how this substructure changes
when protons are grouped together in a nucleus. To explore this mystery, a technique called
scattering is used. At high enough energies, an electron is able to resolve the insides of a proton
and work and function as an effective microscope. One such process is called deep inelastic
scattering or DIS. In this process, the high energy electron breaks apart the nucleus, and through
measurement of the scattered electron and resultant particles, it is possible to determine internal
aspects of the proton or nucleus.1

B. The Electron-Ion Collider (EIC)
 To complete and measure processes such as DIS, it is necessary to have high energy facilities
and detectors, such as the upcoming Electron-Ion Collider. The EIC is a unique collider that will
be built at Brookhaven National Laboratory. It will be the first of its kind to have high energy
polarized beams. This is special because this will be the first facility to have the capability of
exploring the internal structure of heavy ions. The proton/ion beam will have 41 and 100 - 275
GeV while the electron beam will have 5 - 18 GeV. It will have a 10.2 ns bunch crossing rate,2
and requires high precision detectors to make measurements such as DIS and to fit the bunch
crossing rate. These detectors work through a process called track reconstruction, which is how
the charged particles are measured.
 The ePIC collaboration is in charge of developing these detectors. One challenge faced
in development is the requirement of these detectors to be able to separate background events
from physics events. These background events have many sources, but this study focuses on
background particles produced from synchrotron radiation. These extraneous particles are a
result of the beam and can be very difficult to separate. This requires a detector with a timing
window that is faster than the bunch crossing rate of 10.2 ns.

C. Fast MAPS Tracker (FMT)
 One potential solution to the timing window challenge is the Fast MAPS (Monolithic Active
Pixel Sensor) Tracker. This is an advanced silicon detector that is currently in development to
be incorporated into either the ePIC detector system or the second detector that will be built at
the EIC. It has a timing resolution of 2 ns, which is faster than the bunch crossing rate (10.2).
Its spatial resolution is less than 7 microns and is on par with the most precise ePIC subsystem.2
This high precision allows it to separate background particles from individual bunch crossings.
 The FMT is composed of 3 MAPS based disks (which are a simple ePIC forward tracking
subsystem) and 2 FMT disks in the forward region.2 There are a few proposed locations, but

this study focuses on two configurations for the two disks: z locations of 1500 and 1600 mm
or locations of 1450 and 1650 mm from the point of collision. This detector is well suited to
this region’s high radiation environment because it has a strong resistance to radiation. The
two FMT disks are based on MALTA2 technology, which is part of the family of Depleted
Monolithic Active Pixel Sensors (DMAPS),2 .3

II. METHODS

A. Accessing the Files
 As part of the development of the Fast MAPS Tracker, it is important to analyze the ex-
pected environment at the EIC. It is critical to understand how the beam background interacts
at possible FMT locations. To achieve this, a subset of the simulated background particle hits
from ePIC’s GEANT4 data was used.4 This data contains background-specific information on
hits at current ePIC detectors and Monte Carlo particle information in the form of ROOT files.
However, these files do not have any FMT specific data yet because it has not been incorpo-
rated into the ePIC simulation. Furthermore, this data was generated in the default 2 µs window,
which means that it has to eventually be scaled to the FMT 2 ns window.

B. Reference Detectors
 For the purpose of this background study, two reference detectors in the forward region
were chosen to determine the various distributions. The first detector, Forward MPGD (Micro
Pattern Gaseous Detector), has two disks at z locations of 1290 mm and 1415 mm. It has a
timing resolution of approximately 10 ns and a spatial resolution of approximately 10 mm. The
MPGD is located in front of the possible FMT locations, but is also important because of how
close it is to these possible locations.
 The second detector chosen was the B0 Tracker, which has four disks located in the far-
forward region. These disks have z locations of 5900 mm, 6170 mm, 6440 mm, and 6704
mm. The B0 Tracker has a timing resolution of approximately 30 ps and a spatial resolution
of approximately 30 microns. This detector will be located behind the FMT, and by comparing
the distributions of the two different detectors, it is possible to see how the background events
evolve along the beam pipe in the FMT region.

C. Background Distributions
 When evaluating the background at these various reference detector disks, four main mea-
surements were targets to be plotted using PYROOT. All of the data was masked to the specific
z-location for each disk and normalized. The first data target was the 2D distribution of back-
ground hits. It was plotted as a 2D histogram with projections along the x- and y-axes to deter-
mine the general angular distribution of hits. Additionally, the bin sizes of these 2D histograms
were sized to be the size of one MALTA2 sensor (1 by 2 cm2 ). The deposited energy was then
plotted as a 1D histogram. The number of hits per entry was then calculated and plotted as a
1D histogram. Finally, the momentum was plotted as a 1D histogram along with the angular
momentum (momentum in the z-direction) and the transverse momentum (momentum in the x
and y directions).
 In addition to the general distribution plots created for each disk, it was also important to
explore the distribution for each MALTA2 sensor within an FMT disk. For this, the second
disk of the MPGD located at z of 1415 mm was chosen because it is the disk closest to the

 2

possible FMT locations. Since the 2D histogram bins are the same size as a MALTA2 sensor,
it is possible to select the bins with the highest number of hits, which are represented on the
histogram by the color red. By ”zooming in” on one sensor and plotting its distribution, it is
possible to determine the background hit distribution over one sensor. If the highest hit sensor
is chosen, the highest expected hit rate per event can be determined by taking the integral of
the 2D sensor histogram. This rate is valuable because it can be scaled to the FMT 2 ns timing
window and used as input for a standalone detector performance study.

D. Particle Identification
 Another target of this study was to determine the types of particles that make up the back-
ground at possible FMT locations. Similarly to the sensor distributions, the second MPGD disk
was used as a reference. To achieve this, the PDG (Particle Data Group) information was plot-
ted as a 1D histogram at the disk location. The PDG information functions as a particle ID for
Monte Carlo and assigns numbers to each particle, with negative numbers corresponding to the
respective antiparticle. Due to the large amount of data included in the PDG files, a random
sample of the background files was chosen due to time and hardware constraints.

III. RESULTS

A. Background Distributions
 The plots presented in Appendix A demonstrate the various background distributions on
each reference detector disk, as outlined in the Methods section. Each of these figures contains
the 2D hit histogram, the x-projection histogram, the y-projection histogram, the histogram
of the deposited energy, the hits per entry histogram, the momentum histogram, the angular
momentum histogram, and the transverse momentum histogram for each disk. When looking
at the reference figure for an MPGD disk, Figure 1, it is clear that the hit distribution is centered
mainly near 0. The absence of hits in the center of the plot is due to the fact that the disks have a
gap to allow the beam pipe to go through. However, the hits are skewed to the left (or negative)
in the x-direction, as evidenced by the x-projection histograms.
 When looking at the figures that correspond to the B0 Tracker disks, Figures A.2 through
A.5, it is evident that the skew in the negative direction continues further along the beam pipe.
This is because the beamline splits and it is possible to see the space where the electron beam
pipe is (the small circular hole in the center of each 2D hit distribution).
 It is also important to note that for all the figures in Appendix A, the deposited energy and
the momentum plots contain the majority of hits around 0. These plots have been placed on
a log y-axis to allow the tail shape to be seen, but the largest bins are at 0. This information
confirms that these hits are background particles, because they should have little to no energy
and momentum.

B. Selected Sensor Distributions
 Using the 2D histogram in Figure 1, it is possible to determine which sensors have the
highest number of hits because they are colored red. Several sensor locations in the region
of the highest hits were chosen to be plotted, but this study presents the three highest sensor
locations, which all share the x-range of -80 mm to -70 mm. The three sensors cover the y-
ranges of -10 mm to 10 mm (Figure 2), -20 mm to 0 mm (Figure B.1), and 0 mm to 20 mm
(Figure B.2). From this, it was determined that the sensor with the highest number of hits was

C. Background Particle Identification
 Figure 3 shows the distribution of the particles in the sampled files and Table 1 contains
information on what PDG code corresponds to which particle and the relative percentages of
each particle on the selected MPGD disk. From this information, it was determined that the
background particles are primarily composed of positrons, electrons, and photons (or gamma
rays). Additionally, it was determined that when you combine the number of electrons and
positrons and divide it by the number of photons, you get 0.984. This means that the ratio of
electrons and positrons to photons is approximately one-to-one.

IV. CONCLUSIONS

 This project was the first look at the EIC background in the default 2 µs window. This
study provided information on background distributions, contributed FMT sensor distributions,
and confirmed the types of background particles. It was determined that the particles must
be background due to their low energy and momentum, and it was found that the background
particles were primarily positrons, electrons, and photons. The positrons and electrons have a
nearly one-to-one ratio with the photons. The highest expected hit rate per event in the default
window is 0.18, and the background hit information is resolvable on the scale of one MALTA2
sensor. Overall, it was confirmed that there is only a small impact from background particles at
possible FMT locations.
 The distributions studied in this project will be scaled to the FMT 2 ns window so that the
results may be input into a detector performance study for a standalone simulation. This will
further improve FMT statistics and provide valuable information for incorporating the FMT
into the existing ePIC and EIC framework.
