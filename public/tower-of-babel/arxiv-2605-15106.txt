Scalable self-testing of generic multipartite quantum states
Jinchang Liu, Elias X. Huber, Zhenyu Du, Xingjian Zhang, Xiongfeng Ma

Abstract
Characterizing large quantum systems with minimal assumptions is a central challenge in quantum information science. Self-testing provides the strongest form of certification by identifying the underlying quantum state solely from observed measurement statistics. However, existing self-testing methods for generic n n -partite states face a scalability barrier, requiring exponentially many samples in the system size. In this work, we overcome this barrier by introducing a protocol that robustly self-tests almost all n n -qubit states with only polynomial sample complexity. The key ingredient is an efficient scheme for device-independently evaluating multipartite Pauli measurements, which can be implemented using only a linear number of ancillary Bell pairs together with standard projective and Bell measurements, well within the reach of current quantum technology. Beyond self-testing states, our scheme provides a general framework for implementing a wide range of learning and certification protocols in the device-independent setting, thereby opening a scalable route to device-independent quantum information processing in large-scale quantum networks.

Introduction

Certifying the correct functioning of quantum devices lies at the heart of quantum technologies

As quantum platforms and quantum networks continue to scale up

Quantum nonlocality

This framework has enabled transformative applications of quantum technology, such as secure key distribution

Developing powerful DI tools for characterizing quantum systems has therefore become a central goal of quantum information science.

Within the DI paradigm, the strongest certification of an underlying quantum system is known as
self-testing

34
.
In a self-testing protocol, certain observed statistics can uniquely determine the underlying state and measurements, up to the intrinsic degrees of freedom that are indistinguishable from observations.
A canonical example is that maximal violation of the Clauser–Horne–Shimony–Holt (CHSH) inequality self-tests the two-qubit Bell state and the associated measurements
11
;
34
.

A central goal in the certification of large-scale quantum devices and networks is therefore to develop
scalable
self-testing protocols for the underlying
multipartite
quantum systems.
While seminal works have established that all multipartite entangled pure states can, in principle, be self-tested
12
;
43
;
4
, the challenge of scalability remains overlooked and largely unaddressed.
Specifically, rigorous analyses of robustness against experimental noise are currently restricted to a few highly structured examples, such as GHZ and W states
48
;
36
;
18
;
2
;
47
.
Furthermore, most existing results rely on perfect probability distributions, which are inaccessible via finite sampling.
The few studies that do account for the resulting statistical deviations
22
;
6
typically demand a sample complexity that scales exponentially with the number of parties, a feature that is highly unfavorable in practice.
Together, these limitations present a significant barrier to the deployment of self-testing protocols in realistic applications.

Overcoming this exponential sample-complexity barrier is challenging for several reasons:
(i) The black-box treatment of measurement devices makes it difficult to use them to probe the underlying system.
(ii) Without perfect distribution reconstruction, reliably extracting global information from finite samples is highly challenging.
(iii) Even in the device-dependent setting, multipartite entangled states exhibit an exceptionally rich structure that is notoriously difficult to characterize
14
;
24
;
30
. These challenges motivate the following central question:

Can one robustly self-test generic multipartite quantum states with sample complexity polynomial in the number of parties?

In this work, we answer this question affirmatively by introducing a robust self-testing protocol for almost all multipartite qubit states with sample complexity polynomial in the number of parties.
We overcome challenge (i) by solving an open question: how to efficiently certify, in a
global
sense, multipartite Pauli measurements performed by spatially separated parties device-independently.
In particular, although single-party Pauli measurements can be self-tested
8
, extending such certification to the multipartite setting encounters a local-transpose obstacle
43
.
Roughly speaking, standard self-testing techniques fix each party’s Pauli measurements only up to an independent local-transpose freedom, which introduces an ambiguous sign in the
Y
operator (i.e.,
\pm Y
).
To certify a target multipartite Pauli measurement, these local freedoms must be aligned consistently across all parties.
To overcome this obstacle, we introduce a new
transpose-braiding
test that enforces this global consistency.
The test is based on inequalities between neighboring parties, constructed from a two-qubit observable
K
(detailed in the Results section). The key insight is that the maximal eigenvalue of
K
is strictly larger than that of its partial transpose
K^{T_{1}}
, which forces the transpose freedoms of neighboring parties to be consistent.
By sequentially applying this alignment across the network, one certifies multipartite Pauli measurements up to a global transpose freedom.

Building on this test, our protocol robustly lifts device-dependent Pauli measurements to their device-independent counterparts using only polynomial overhead.
Crucially, this protocol enables sample-efficient estimation of the observables relevant for certifying the target states, without reconstructing the full correlation distribution, thereby overcoming challenge (ii). Furthermore, we remove the assumption that the underlying quantum states are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) across rounds by providing a fully non-i.i.d. analysis.

Finally, we address challenge (iii) by building on recent device-dependent certification protocols for generic states
27
;
13
.
The protocol of Ref.
27
relies only on local Pauli measurements and can therefore be lifted to the DI setting using our multipartite Pauli-measurement scheme, already yielding a self-testing protocol with polynomial sample complexity for generic states.
To remove its remaining shared-randomness requirement, we further build on the random-basis-enhanced variant of Ref.
13
and show how the required shared randomness can be replaced by standard local randomness.
Taken together, our protocol only requires the minimal assumptions of the DI framework.

For experimental implementation, our protocol requires only standard Bell states and Pauli measurements, both of which are well within the reach of current quantum platforms
33
;
31
;
42
.
By providing a systematic bridge from powerful device-dependent methods to their DI counterparts, our protocol unlocks a wide range of scalable DI learning and certification protocols for large-scale quantum devices and interconnected quantum networks
21
;
15
;
26
;
17
;

;
13

Our self-testing protocol proceeds by repeating the experiment for multiple rounds.
In each round, the parties’ outcomes are sampled according to Eq. (
2
).
From the collected data, we construct an estimator
\hat{\omega}
and aim to certify that
\tr(L\,\rho_A)=\hat{\omega}
up to a precision
\varepsilon
, achieving self-testing of the desired property and the multipartite Pauli measurements.
Importantly, self-testing necessarily leaves some intrinsic degrees of freedom undetermined.
First, there is the local extraction freedom: if parties share an initial state
\rho^{\prime}
and can transform it into
\rho
via local channels, their measurement devices can produce statistics identical to those of
\rho
.
Consequently, self-testing results are always defined up to a local extraction channel. Common examples of such operations include local basis rotations and tracing out “junk” auxiliary systems upon which the measurements act trivially.
Second, the statistics are invariant under global complex conjugation, since simultaneously replacing the state and all measurement operators with their complex conjugates does not affect the real-valued probabilities.
Beyond these unavoidable freedoms, any practical notion of self-testing must be robust to statistical fluctuations arising from finite samples.
Taking these into account, we say that the parties self-test a state or a property of the state if the desired target can be approximately extracted from
\rho
by some local channels
\{\Gamma_{l}\}_{l}
acting on the main parties, up to the unavoidable global-conjugation freedom
44
.

Here, the ancilla

In general, self-testing an expectation value for an observable

is a weaker task than self-testing a state, as it only certifies a single property of the extracted state. By contrast, self-testing a pure state

Our later results leverage precisely this connection.
We emphasize that Definition
1

In contrast, we overcome this difficulty by explicitly constructing a fully local extraction channel. The key insight is that the 3-CHSH inequalities in Fig.

This allows the extraction channel to replace the physical main–auxiliary Bell pair with a locally prepared Bell pair on the main side, thereby reproducing the effect of the teleportation entirely locally, extracting the desired target state without any communication. The detailed construction of this local extraction channel is given in Appendix

Incorporating this additional step and its associated security analysis is beyond our current scope and is left for future work.

We emphasize that when the classical input to each party is trusted, for example by being supplied by an additional referee, and spatial separation is guaranteed, our self-testing protocol does not assume honest reporting of measurement results by the main or auxiliary parties. Indeed, any adversarial strategy can be absorbed into the local measurement, and such a strategy can pass our test only if the parties effectively implement the desired measurements.

III.2
Efficient self-testing of generic multipartite states

We now apply our lifting theorem to the strongest notion of self-testing: Certifying a target state.
The crux of our approach is to construct a randomized Pauli measurement scheme whose associated observable
L
efficiently and robustly certifies a target state
\Psi
.
Ideally,
L
should have
\Psi
as its eigenvector with maximal eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}=1
, and it should exhibit a sufficient spectral gap
\Delta>0
between
\lambda_{1}
and the second-largest eigenvalue.
Such a gap ensures robustness: if one can estimate
\tr(L\rho)\approx 1-c_{1}\Delta
with additive error
\varepsilon=c_{2}\Delta
, then
\rho
must be close to
\Psi
:
\tr(\rho\Psi)\geq 1-c_{1}-c_{2}
.

A recent shadow-overlap protocol achieves this for generic states
27
by constructing an observable with maximal eigenvalue
1
and spectral gap
\Delta=\Omega(n^{-2})
.
Combined with our lifting theorem, this immediately yields an efficient and robust self-testing protocol for generic states.
Yet, the resulting construction inherits the shared-randomness requirement from
27
.
To remove this requirement, we introduce a random-basis-enhanced variant that can be implemented using only local randomness. Specifically, we consider the observable

L=M_{\Psi}\coloneqq\frac{1}{n3^{n}}\sum_{\mathbf{P}\in\{X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum_{\mathbf{b}^{(k)}\in\{0,1\}^{n-1}}\outerproduct{\vec{b}^{(k)}, \vec{P}^{(k)}}{\vec{b}^{(k)}, \vec{P}^{(k)}}_{[n]\setminus\{k\}}\otimes\outerproduct{\Psi_{\vec{b}, \vec{P}}^{(k)}}{\Psi_{\vec{b}, \vec{P}}^{(k)}},

(6)

where
\mathbf{b}^{(k)}\coloneqq(b_{0},\cdots,b_{k-1},b_{k+1},\cdots,b_{n-1}),\mathbf{P}^{(k)}=(P_{0},\cdots,P_{k-1},P_{k+1},\cdots,P_{n-1})

\ket{\vec{b}^{(k)}, \vec{P}^{(k)}}
denotes the basis state corresponding to measurement outcomes
\mathbf{b}^{(k)}

\mathbf{P}^{(k)}

denotes the normalized state on system
k
after projecting
\ket{\Psi}
onto
\ket{\vec{b}^{(k)}, \vec{P}^{(k)}}_{[n]\setminus\{k\}}

Intuitively, this observable first chooses a qubit
k
uniformly at random, measures the remaining
n-1
qubits in a random Pauli basis, and then measures the fidelity of the resulting single-qubit post-measurement state against the ideal reduced state
\ket{\Psi_{\vec{b}, \vec{P}}^{(k)}}
.
We prove in Appendix
G

M_{\Psi}
preserves the spectral gap
\Delta=\Omega(n^{-2})
.
Moreover, it can be written in the form of Eq. (

Applying our lifting theorem to this Pauli scheme then yields an efficient and robust self-testing protocol that self-tests almost all
n
-qubit pure states using only local randomness and polynomial sample complexity. This presents an exponential improvement over previously known multipartite self-testing schemes
43
;
4
.
Moreover, the protocol provides inverse-polynomial noise tolerance against device imperfections.

Theorem 2

(Efficient self-testing of generic multipartite states)
.

Let
\Psi
be an
n
-qubit pure state drawn Haar-randomly. Then, with probability at least
1-\exp(-\Omega(n))
over the choice of
\Psi

to

achieves, with probability at least
1-\delta
, soundness and completeness for
\varepsilon

\Psi
(in the sense of Theorem

and Definition

). The protocol uses
N=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\mathrm{poly}(n,\varepsilon^{-1})\log\delta^{-1}\right)

The formal version of Theorem
2
is given by Theorem
S6
in the Appendix.
We remark that Theorem
2
does not directly apply to certain highly structured states, such as GHZ states. Nonetheless, such states often admit highly efficient self-testing protocols precisely because of their strong structure
2
.
Our result therefore complements the existing self-testing literature by providing a general, sample-efficient method that applies to all but an exponentially small fraction of the state space, thereby dramatically enlarging the class of states known to admit sample-efficient self-testing.

Our protocol also enjoys additional advantages. First, the local measurements performed by each party are independent of the target state being self-tested, so a single dataset can be reused to self-test many target states simultaneously. Second, the auxiliary parties obtain no information about the state held by the main parties as long as the Bell-state-measurement outcomes arising from teleportation are not disclosed to them. In this sense, the protocol provides perfect privacy of both the target state and the underlying state against the auxiliary parties.

IV
Discussion

An important next step is to reduce the resource requirements of our protocols further. For instance, the sample complexity of our self-testing protocol for generic states can be further improved, as recent results suggest that certifying generic quantum states may be possible with constant sample complexity
13
.
Moreover, our protocol can be substantially more efficient for self-testing structured target states or properties.
As one example, the overhead associated with teleporting all parties could be largely avoided for states and properties that admit local certification, such as qLDPC codes
9
or code subspaces
3
. These tasks are directly relevant to protecting quantum information and implementing quantum secret sharing in networks
25
.
As another example, adapting our techniques to self-test ground states of local Hamiltonians
46
could provide new routes to verifying quantum computations
20
.
Our framework can also be applied to certify complex structures of the states distributed across the network, such as circuit complexity and multipartite entanglement
13
.
Since our self-testing protocol is robust against adversarial parties and, in the state-self-testing setting, also provides privacy against auxiliary parties, it is intriguing to explore its cryptographic applications, for example, in verifiable blind quantum computing, multi-prover interactive proofs, and quantum zero-knowledge proofs.

Finally, it remains important to fully understand the ultimate capabilities and limitations of self-testing.
While our method applies to almost all multipartite qubit states, an important open question is whether
arbitrary
quantum states can be self-tested robustly and sample-efficiently.
A closely related challenge is to achieve sample-efficient self-testing in the standard Bell scenario, eliminating the need for auxiliary Bell pairs or additional parties
4
.
Furthermore, extending our framework to multi-qudit systems of prime dimension provides a natural direction for future work.
We expect this generalization to be feasible, drawing confidence from recent successes in self-testing odd-prime maximally entangled states
37
and
\mathbb{Z}_{d}
toric code ground states
45
.

This certification is robustly sound, meaning that any state with a trace distance to the target state exceeding a small threshold

Appendix C
Robust self-testing of Bell states and Pauli measurements

In this section, we present a robust analysis for self-testing Bell states and Pauli measurements using three independent CHSH inequalities. Specifically, the self-testing is performed by constructing three CHSH inequalities, each utilizing a different pair of measurement bases drawn from the Pauli set
\{\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y},\sigma_{z}\}

8
.

Let

denote the location and
k\in\{0,1,2\}

for party

for party

are specified by:

(x_{l}(0,k),x_{l}(1,k),y_{l}(0,k),y_{l}(1,k))=\begin{cases}(0,1,0,2)&\text{if }k=0,\\
(3,2,0,1)&\text{if }k=1,\\
(5,4,2,1)&\text{if }k=2.\end{cases}

(6)

Let

We provide a self-contained analysis of this robust self-testing procedure here.

Appendix D
Self-testing multipartite Pauli measurements

This section presents the main technical contribution of this work: a self-testing scheme for performing multipartite Pauli measurements.
The crucial feature of our protocol is its robustness to practical device imperfections and statistical fluctuations, which is essential for guaranteeing the sample efficiency of our subsequent multipartite self-testing procedures.

We build the full self-testing protocol by a sequence of incremental steps.
First, we define the ideal multipartite Pauli measurement protocols that our DI scheme aims to implement.
Then, we leverage the robust self-testing results for bipartite Pauli measurements established in Sec.
C
to construct a self-testing protocol that implements the multipartite Pauli measurements, albeit only up to partial transpose (Lemma
S3
).
Recognizing that this partial transpose is insufficient for achieving sample-efficient self-testing for the global state, we devise a novel, more efficient self-testing protocol that correlates partial transposes across all subsystems (Lemma
S5
).
This protocol rigorously implements the target multipartite Pauli measurements up to global transpose, which, as we show in Section
G
, is sufficient to guarantee polynomial sample efficiency for self-testing the global state up to global conjugation.
Finally, we detail the construction of the local quantum channels to extract the desired target quantum state from the state
\psi
present in the physical experiment.

D.1
Multipartite Pauli measurement protocols

An
n
-partite Pauli measurement scheme is usually designed to estimate the expectation value of a target observable
L
for an input state
\rho
. This procedure is defined by the following four steps:

1.

Basis selection: A specific product Pauli basis,
\mathbf{P}=P_{0}\otimes P_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes P_{n-1}
, is sampled according to a predetermined probability distribution
\mathcal{D}
over the set
\{X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}
.

2.

Local measurement: Each party
l\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
performs a local Pauli measurement on their subsystem along the basis
P_{l}
.

3.

Result announcement and weighting: The parties record their collective measurement outcomes
\mathbf{b}=(b_{0},b_{1},\dots,b_{n-1})\in\{0,1\}^{n}
. A weight
\omega(\mathbf{b}|\mathbf{P})
is assigned to this outcome, dependent on the selected basis
\mathbf{P}
.

4.

Expectation value estimation: By repeating steps (1)-(3) multiple times and averaging over both the basis choice
\mathbf{P}
and the measurement outcome
\mathbf{b}
, an empirical estimate of the expectation value
\tr(L\rho)
is obtained, where the observable
L
is defined by Eq. (
1
).

In this section, we focus on the infinite-sample regime.
In practice, statistical fluctuations due to finite sampling are crucial for determining sample complexity, which we rigorously address in Sec.
E
.

D.2
Self-testing up to
partial
transpose

We now construct the self-testing protocol for realizing the
n
-partite Pauli measurement schemes defined above. The core strategy employs a local teleportation-and-measure approach
43
.
However, a serious problem with such an approach is that the resulting DI realization suffers from a partial transpose problem
8
;
43
;
41
.
Prior work
43
;
41
resolves this issue through full tomography on a genuine multipartite entangled state or through additional source assumptions. The former does not generalize to arbitrary states, and the latter introduces additional device-dependent assumptions. Importantly, the full tomography introduces exponential sample complexity in
n
.
This exponential scaling presents a major obstacle to practical, scalable self-testing protocols. We address this partial-transpose problem in a sample-efficient, fully DI manner later in Sec.
D.3
.
Here, we focus on developing a robust DI protocol for performing multipartite Pauli measurements using the teleportation-and-measure approach up to the partial transposes.

The key idea is to utilize the shared entanglement between each main party
A_{l}
and its auxiliary party
B_{l}
to effectively teleport the input state from the main parties
A=A_{0}A_{1}\dots A_{n-1}
to the auxiliary parties
B=B_{0}B_{1}\dots B_{n-1}
. Once the state is teleported, the desired
n
-partite Pauli measurements can be performed by the parties
B
using measurement operators rigorously self-tested (up to a local isometry) via the CHSH inequalities.

Concretely, the main parties
A_{l}
first perform the local measurements
\{M_{\mathbf{a}_{l}|\diamond}^{(l)}\}_{\mathbf{a}_{l}\in\{0,1\}^{2}}
. In the physical experiment, the measurements
\{M_{\mathbf{a}_{l}|\diamond}^{(l)}\}_{\mathbf{a}_{l}\in\{0,1\}^{2}}
are completely uncharacterized, while in the ideal reference experiment, they implement a BSM on
T_{l}S_{l}
, teleporting the input state on
T_{l}
to the auxiliary system
B_{l}
via the Bell state shared on
S_{l}B_{l}
.
In standard teleportation, one needs to perform a Pauli correction
U_{\mathbf{a_{l}}}
after obtaining BSM outcome
\mathbf{a}_{l}
, where

U_{\mathbf{a}}=X^{a_{0}}Z^{a_{1}},\quad\mathbf{a}=a_{0}a_{1}.

(28)

Crucially, the auxiliary parties
B_{l}
do not physically apply this Pauli correction here.
Instead, they first perform the certified Pauli measurement
\{N_{b_{l}|P_{l}}^{(l)}\}_{b_{l}}
along the basis
P_{l}\in\{X,Y,Z\}
to obtain a raw outcome
b_{l}
.
Note that for convenience, we use
P_{l}=X,Y,Z
to correspond to the measurement setting
y_{l}=0,1,2
(see Table
2
).
The necessary correction is then incorporated by adjusting the measurement outcome conditional on the BSM result
\mathbf{a}_{l}
received from the measurement on
A_{l}
.
Specifically, the raw outcome
b_{l}
is mapped to a corrected outcome
f(b_{l}|P_{l},\mathbf{a}_{l})
by flipping
b_{l}
when the correction operator
U_{\mathbf{a}_{l}}
and the measurement basis
P_{l}
anti-commute:

f(b_{l}|P_{l},\mathbf{a}_{l})=b_{l}\oplus\mathbf{1}[\{P_{l},U_{\mathbf{a}_{l}}\}=0].

(29)

The overall estimator
v_{\mathrm{III}}
for measuring the observable
L
is then defined as the expectation over the basis distribution
\mathcal{D}
, where the weight
\omega
is applied to the corrected outcome vector
f(\mathbf{b}|\mathbf{P},\{\mathbf{a}_{l}\}_{l=0}^{n-1})
:

v_{\mathrm{III}}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{P}\leftarrow\mathcal{D}}[\mathbb{E}[\omega(f(\mathbf{b}|\mathbf{P},\{\mathbf{a}_{l}\}_{l=0}^{n-1})|\mathbf{P})\mid\forall l,x_{l}={\diamond},y_{l}=P_{l}]].

(30)

where
f(\mathbf{b}|\mathbf{P},\{\mathbf{a_{l}}\})=(f(b_{0}|P_{0},\mathbf{a}_{0}),f(b_{1}|P_{1},\mathbf{a}_{1}),\cdots,f(b_{n-1}|P_{n-1},\mathbf{a}_{n-1}))
is the vector of corrected outcomes, and we identify
P_{l}=X,Y,Z
to
y_{l}=0,1,2
.

We establish that, provided the auxiliary Pauli measurements are approximately certified via the 3-CHSH game, the estimator
v_{\mathrm{III}}
approximately estimates
\tr(L\Gamma(\psi))
up to partial transpose.
Here,
\Gamma(\psi)
is the state after the teleportation and ideal recovery:

\Gamma:=\bigotimes_{l=0}^{n-1}\Gamma_{l},\quad\Gamma_{l}:\rho\mapsto\sum_{\mathbf{a}_{l}\in\{0,1\}^{2}}U^{B_{l}^{\prime}}_{\mathbf{a}_{l}}\tr_{A_{l}B_{l}}(M_{\mathbf{a}_{l}|\diamond}^{(l)}V_{B_{l}}\rho V_{B_{l}}^{\dagger})U^{B_{l}^{\prime}\dagger}_{\mathbf{a}_{l}}

(31)

and
V_{B_{l}}
is the isometry given in Lemma
S2
.
We summarize the above protocol in the following lemma.

Lemma S3

(Self-testing multipartite Pauli measurements up to partial transpose)
.

Let
L
be the
n
-partite observable defined in Eq. (
1
), with the weighting coefficients bounded by a constant
W>1
, i.e.,
|\omega(\mathbf{b}|\mathbf{P})|\leq W
.
Let
\Gamma
be the quantum channel representing the teleportation followed by Pauli correction (Eq. (
31
)).
Suppose that the deviation from the maximal CHSH value (Eq. (
7
)) for each party
l\in[n]
and CHSH choice

is bounded by
v_{\mathrm{I}}(l,k)\leq\epsilon_{l}
.

Then, the estimator
v_{\mathrm{III}}
in the teleportation-and-measurement protocol (Eq. (
30
)) approximates the expectation value of the target observable
L
up to partial transpose:

\left\lvert v_{\mathrm{III}}-\tr[\left(\sum_{\iota\in\{0,1\}^{n}}L^{T_{\iota}}_{B^{\prime}}\otimes\outerproduct{\iota}{\iota}_{B^{\prime\prime}}\right)\Gamma(\psi)]\right\rvert\leq\mathcal{O}\left(W\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\sqrt{\epsilon_{l}}\right).

(32)

Here,
(\cdot)^{T_{\iota}}
denotes partial transpose applied to the subsystems
B^{\prime}_{l}
specified by the bit-string
\iota
.

Proof.

We begin by expanding the definition of the estimator
v_{\mathrm{III}}
:

\begin{split}v_{\mathrm{III}}&=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{P}\leftarrow\mathcal{D}}\left\{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{\mathbf{a}_{l}\}_{l=0}^{n-1}\\
\mathbf{b}\in\{0,1\}^{n}\end{subarray}}\omega(f(\mathbf{b}|\mathbf{P},\{\mathbf{a}_{l}\})|\mathbf{P})\tr[M_{\mathbf{a}}\left(\bigotimes_{l=0}^{n-1}N^{(l)}_{b_{l}|P_{l}}\right)\psi]\right\}\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{P}\leftarrow\mathcal{D}}\left\{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{\mathbf{a}_{l}\}_{l=0}^{n-1}\\
\mathbf{b}\in\{0,1\}^{n}\end{subarray}}\omega(f(\mathbf{b}|\mathbf{P},\{\mathbf{a}_{l}\})|\mathbf{P})\tr[M_{\mathbf{a}}V_{B}^{\dagger}\left(\bigotimes_{l=0}^{n-1}V_{B_{l}}N^{(l)}_{b_{l}|P_{l}}\right)\psi]\right\}.\end{split}

(33)

Here,
M_{\mathbf{a}}:=\bigotimes_{l=0}^{n-1}M_{\mathbf{a}_{l}|\diamond}^{(l)}
is the product of BSM-like operators,
V_{B}:=\bigotimes_{l=0}^{n-1}V_{B_{l}}
is the product of isometries. The second line uses the identity
V_{B}^{\dagger}V_{B}=\mathbb{I}
.

Define
\tau_{P,b}=\frac{1}{2}[\mathbb{I}+(-1)^{b}\sigma_{P}\otimes\sigma_{Z}^{\delta_{PY}}]
. Combining Lemma
S1
and Eq. (
21
) in Lemma
S2
, we have

\displaystyle V_{B_{l}}N^{(l)}_{b_{l}|P_{l}}\ket{\psi}\approx_{\sqrt{\epsilon_{l}}}(\tau_{P_{l},b_{l}})_{B_{l}^{\prime}B_{l}^{\prime\prime}}V_{B_{l}}\ket{\psi}.

(34)

Our objective is to replace the term
V_{B_{l}}N^{(l)}_{b_{l}|P_{l}}
in Eq. (
33
) with
(\tau_{P_{l},b_{l}})_{B_{l}^{\prime}B_{l}^{\prime\prime}}V_{B_{l}}
for all
l=0,\dots,n-1
. We perform this replacement incrementally.
Let
v_{j}
be the estimator value after the replacement has been performed for the first
j
subsystems:

\begin{split}v_{j}&\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{P}\leftarrow\mathcal{D}}\left\{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{\mathbf{a}_{l}\}_{l=0}^{n-1}\\
\mathbf{b}\in\{0,1\}^{n}\end{subarray}}\omega(f(\mathbf{b}|\mathbf{P},\{\mathbf{a}_{l}\})|\mathbf{P})\tr[M_{\mathbf{a}}V_{B}^{\dagger}\left(\bigotimes_{l=0}^{j-1}(\tau_{P_{l},b_{l}})_{B_{l}^{\prime}B_{l}^{\prime\prime}}V_{B_{l}}\right)\left(\bigotimes_{l=j}^{n-1}V_{B_{l}}N^{(l)}_{b_{l}|P_{l}}\right)\psi]\right\}\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{P}\leftarrow\mathcal{D}}\left\{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{\mathbf{a}_{l}\}_{l=0}^{n-1}\\
\mathbf{b}\in\{0,1\}^{n}\end{subarray}}\omega(f(\mathbf{b}|\mathbf{P},\{\mathbf{a}_{l}\})|\mathbf{P})p^{j}_{\mathbf{P}}(\{\mathbf{a}_{l}\},\mathbf{b})\right\},\end{split}

(35)

where
p^{j}_{\mathbf{P}}(\{\mathbf{a}_{l}\},\mathbf{b})\coloneqq\tr[M_{\mathbf{a}}V_{B}^{\dagger}\left(\bigotimes_{l=0}^{j-1}(\tau_{P_{l},b_{l}})_{B_{l}^{\prime}B_{l}^{\prime\prime}}V_{B_{l}}\right)\left(\bigotimes_{l=j}^{n-1}V_{B_{l}}N^{(l)}_{b_{l}|P_{l}}\right)\psi]
.

By definition,
v_{0}=v_{\mathrm{III}}
.
Now we aim to show that (i)
\absolutevalue{v_{j+1}-v_j}\leq\mathcal{O}(W\sqrt{\epsilon_{j}})
; and (ii)
v_{n}=\tr[\left(\sum_{\iota\in\{0,1\}^{n}}L^{T_{\iota}}_{B^{\prime}}\otimes\outerproduct{\iota}{\iota}_{B^{\prime\prime}}\right)\Gamma(\psi)]
.
Then, Eq. (
32
) follows by expanding
v_{n}-v_{0}
into a telescoping sum and applying triangular inequalities.

We first prove (i). By substituting the definitions of
v_{j}
and
v_{j+1}
, we have:

\begin{split}\absolutevalue{v_{j+1} - v_j}&=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{P}\leftarrow\mathcal{D}}\left\{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{\mathbf{a}_{l}\}_{l=0}^{n-1}\\
\mathbf{b}\in\{0,1\}^{n}\end{subarray}}\omega(f(\mathbf{b}|\mathbf{P},\{\mathbf{a}_{l}\})|\mathbf{P})[p^{j}_{\mathbf{P}}(\{\mathbf{a}_{l}\},\mathbf{b})-p^{j+1}_{\mathbf{P}}(\{\mathbf{a}_{l}\},\mathbf{b})]\right\}\\
&\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{P}\leftarrow\mathcal{D}}\left\{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{\mathbf{a}_{l}\}_{l=0}^{n-1}\\
\mathbf{b}\in\{0,1\}^{n}\end{subarray}}\absolutevalue{\omega(f(\vec{b}|\vec{P},\{\vec{a}_l\})|\vec{P})}\cdot\absolutevalue{p^{j}_{\vec{P}}(\{\vec{a}_l\}, \vec{b}) - p^{j+1}_{\vec{P}}(\{\vec{a}_l\}, \vec{b})}\right\}\\
&\leq 2W\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{P}\leftarrow\mathcal{D}}\left\{\mathrm{TV}(p^{j}_{\mathbf{P}},p^{j+1}_{\mathbf{P}})\right\},\end{split}

(36)

where
\mathrm{TV}(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the total variational distance.

We define the sets
S_{>,0}\coloneqq\{(\{\mathbf{a}_{l}\},\mathbf{b}):p^{j}_{\mathbf{P}}(\{\mathbf{a}_{l}\},\mathbf{b})>p^{j+1}_{\mathbf{P}}(\{\mathbf{a}_{l}\},\mathbf{b}),b_{j}=0\}
, and
S_{>,1},S_{<,0},S_{<,1}
analogously. Then,

\begin{split}2\mathrm{TV}(p^{j}_{\mathbf{P}},p^{j+1}_{\mathbf{P}})=\Bigg(\sum_{(\{\mathbf{a}_{l}\},\mathbf{b}_{l})\in S_{>,0}}&+\sum_{(\{\mathbf{a}_{l}\},\mathbf{b}_{l})\in S_{>,1}}\Bigg)(p^{j}_{\mathbf{P}}(\{\mathbf{a}_{l}\},\mathbf{b})-p^{j+1}_{\mathbf{P}}(\{\mathbf{a}_{l}\},\mathbf{b}))\\
+&\left(\sum_{(\{\mathbf{a}_{l}\},\mathbf{b}_{l})\in S_{<,0}}+\sum_{(\{\mathbf{a}_{l}\},\mathbf{b}_{l})\in S_{<,1}}\right)(p^{j+1}_{\mathbf{P}}(\{\mathbf{a}_{l}\},\mathbf{b})-p^{j}_{\mathbf{P}}(\{\mathbf{a}_{l}\},\mathbf{b})).\end{split}

(37)

We now bound the first summation term
S_{>,0}
, and the other three terms follow similarly.

\begin{split}\sum_{S_{>,0}}p^{j}_{\mathbf{P}}(\{\mathbf{a}_{l}\},\mathbf{b})-p^{j+1}_{\mathbf{P}}(\{\mathbf{a}_{l}\},\mathbf{b})&=\sum_{S_{>,0}}\tr[M_{\mathbf{a}}V_{B}^{\dagger}\left(\bigotimes_{l=0}^{j-1}(\tau_{P_{l},b_{l}})_{B_{l}^{\prime}B_{l}^{\prime\prime}}V_{B_{l}}\right)\left((\tau_{P_{j},b_{j}})_{B_{j}^{\prime}B_{j}^{\prime\prime}}V_{B_{j}}-V_{B_{j}}N^{(j)}_{b_{j}|P_{j}}\right)\left(\bigotimes_{l=j+1}^{n-1}V_{B_{l}}N^{(l)}_{b_{l}|P_{l}}\right)\psi]\\
&=\sum_{S_{>,0}}\tr[M_{\mathbf{a}}V_{B}^{\dagger}\left(\bigotimes_{l=0}^{j-1}(\tau_{P_{l},b_{l}})_{B_{l}^{\prime}B_{l}^{\prime\prime}}V_{B_{l}}\right)\left(\bigotimes_{l=j+1}^{n-1}V_{B_{l}}N^{(l)}_{b_{l}|P_{l}}\right)\ket{\tilde{\psi}}\bra{\psi}].\end{split}

(38)

Here,
\ket{\tilde{\psi}}\coloneqq\left((\tau_{P_{j},b_{j}})_{B_{j}^{\prime}B_{j}^{\prime\prime}}V_{B_{j}}-V_{B_{j}}N^{(j)}_{b_{j}|P_{j}}\right)\ket{\psi}
, which satisfies
\left\lVert\ket{\tilde{\psi}}\right\rVert\leq\sqrt{\epsilon_{j}}
by Eq. (
34
). Define

\Delta_{j}\coloneqq\sum_{S_{>,0}}M_{\mathbf{a}}V_{B}^{\dagger}\left(\bigotimes_{l=0}^{j-1}(\tau_{P_{l},b_{l}})_{B_{l}^{\prime}B_{l}^{\prime\prime}}V_{B_{l}}\right)\left(\bigotimes_{l=j+1}^{n-1}V_{B_{l}}N^{(l)}_{b_{l}|P_{l}}\right),

(39)

Since
\Delta_{j}
is a partial sum of measurement operators of a POVM, we have
\left\lVert\Delta_{j}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1
. Hence,

\begin{split}\sum_{S_{>,0}}p^{j}_{\mathbf{P}}(\{\mathbf{a}_{l}\},\mathbf{b})-p^{j+1}_{\mathbf{P}}(\{\mathbf{a}_{l}\},\mathbf{b})&=\tr(\Delta_j \ket{\tilde{\psi}}\bra{\psi})\\
&\leq\left\lVert\Delta_{j}\right\rVert_{\infty}\left\lVert\ket{\tilde{\psi}}\right\rVert\\
&\leq\sqrt{\epsilon_{j}}.\end{split}

(40)

Substituting these bounds into Eqs. (
37
), (
36
) proves (i).

We now proceed to prove (ii). By definition,
U_{\mathbf{a}_{l}}^{B_{l}^{\prime}}(\tau_{P_{l},b_{l}})_{B_{l}^{\prime}B_{l}^{\prime\prime}}U^{B_{l}^{\prime}\dagger}_{\mathbf{a}_{l}}=(\tau_{P_{l},f_{l}})_{B_{l}^{\prime}B_{l}^{\prime\prime}}
, where
f_{l}=f(b_{l}|P_{l},\mathbf{a}_{l})
. Therefore,

\begin{split}v_{n}&=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{P}\leftarrow\mathcal{D}}\left\{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{\mathbf{a}_{l}\}_{l=0}^{n-1}\\
\mathbf{b}\in\{0,1\}^{n}\end{subarray}}\omega(f(\mathbf{b}|\mathbf{P},\{\mathbf{a}_{l}\})|\mathbf{P})\tr[M_{\mathbf{a}}V_{B}^{\dagger}\left(\bigotimes_{l=0}^{n-1}(\tau_{P_{l},b_{l}})_{B_{l}^{\prime}B_{l}^{\prime\prime}}\right)V_{B}\psi]\right\}\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{P}\leftarrow\mathcal{D}}\left\{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{\mathbf{a}_{l}\}_{l=0}^{n-1}\\
\mathbf{b}\in\{0,1\}^{n}\end{subarray}}\omega(f(\mathbf{b}|\mathbf{P},\{\mathbf{a}_{l}\})|\mathbf{P})\tr[M_{\mathbf{a}}\left(\bigotimes_{l=0}^{n-1}U^{B^{\prime}_{l}}_{\mathbf{a}_{l}}(\tau_{P_{l},b_{l}})_{B_{l}^{\prime}B_{l}^{\prime\prime}}U_{\mathbf{a}_{l}}^{B^{\prime}_{l}\dagger}\right)\left(\bigotimes_{l=0}^{n-1}U^{B^{\prime}_{l}}_{\mathbf{a}_{l}}\right)V_{B}\psi V_{B}^{\dagger}\left(\bigotimes_{l=0}^{n-1}U_{\mathbf{a}_{l}}^{B^{\prime}_{l}\dagger}\right)]\right\}\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{P}\leftarrow\mathcal{D}}\left\{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{\mathbf{a}_{l}\}_{l=0}^{n-1}\\
f_{0}f_{1}\cdots f_{n-1}\in\{0,1\}^{n}\end{subarray}}\omega(f_{0}f_{1}\cdots f_{n-1}|\mathbf{P})\tr[M_{\mathbf{a}}\left(\bigotimes_{l=0}^{n-1}(\tau_{P_{l},f_{l}})_{B_{l}^{\prime}B_{l}^{\prime\prime}}\right)\left(\bigotimes_{l=0}^{n-1}U^{B^{\prime}_{l}}_{\mathbf{a}_{l}}\right)V_{B}\psi V_{B}^{\dagger}\left(\bigotimes_{l=0}^{n-1}U_{\mathbf{a}_{l}}^{B^{\prime}_{l}\dagger}\right)]\right\}\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{P}\leftarrow\mathcal{D}}\left\{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{b}\in\{0,1\}^{n}\end{subarray}}\omega(\mathbf{b}|\mathbf{P})\tr[\left(\bigotimes_{l=0}^{n-1}(\tau_{P_{l},b_{l}})_{B_{l}^{\prime}B_{l}^{\prime\prime}}\right)\Gamma(\psi)]\right\}\end{split}

(41)

where we used that
M_{\mathbf{a}}
acts only on the main systems
A_{l}
and that
f(\circ|P_{l},\mathbf{a}_{l})
is bijective.
Moreover,

\begin{split}&\quad\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{P}\leftarrow\mathcal{D}}\left\{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{b}\in\{0,1\}^{n}\end{subarray}}\omega(\mathbf{b}|\mathbf{P})\bigotimes_{l=0}^{n-1}(\tau_{P_{l},b_{l}})_{B_{l}^{\prime}B_{l}^{\prime\prime}}\right\}\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{P}\leftarrow\mathcal{D}}\left\{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{b}\in\{0,1\}^{n}\end{subarray}}\omega(\mathbf{b}|\mathbf{P})\bigotimes_{l=0}^{n-1}\left[\left(\frac{\mathbb{I}+(-1)^{b_{l}}\sigma_{P_{l}}}{2}\right)_{B_{l}^{\prime}}\otimes\outerproduct{0}{0}_{B_{l}^{\prime\prime}}+\left(\frac{\mathbb{I}+(-1)^{b_{l}+\delta_{PY}}\sigma_{P_{l}}}{2}\right)_{B_{l}^{\prime}}\otimes\outerproduct{1}{1}_{B_{l}^{\prime\prime}}\right]\right\}\\

&=\sum_{\iota\in\{0,1\}^{n}}L^{T_{\iota}}_{B^{\prime}}\otimes\outerproduct{\iota}{\iota}_{B^{\prime\prime}}.\\
\end{split}

(42)

Substituting this into Eq. (
41
) proves (ii).

D.3
Self-testing up to
global
transpose

We now remove the undesirable partial transpose terms in Lemma
S3
, thereby yielding a robust DI protocol that implements multipartite Pauli measurements up to only an intrinsic global transpose.

Recall that the partial transpose terms originate from the sum over
\iota\in S
, where
S\coloneqq\{0,1\}^{n}\backslash\{0^{n},1^{n}\}
(i.e., the set of transpose configurations that are neither the identity nor the global transpose). If we can demonstrate that the probability of extracting these unwanted terms is negligible, specifically if

\tr\left[\left(\sum_{\iota\in S}\outerproduct{\iota}{\iota}_{B^{\prime\prime}}\right)\Gamma(\rho)\right]\approx 0
, then these components can be disregarded.

Crucially, any configuration
\iota\in S
must contain at least one adjacent pair of distinct partial transpose indicators (i.e.,
\iota_{l}\neq\iota_{l+1}
for some
l\in[n-1]
). Therefore, the overall removal condition reduces to proving that for any adjacent pair of parties
l
and
l+1
, the probability of the outcomes
\ket{01}
or
\ket{10}
on the auxiliary systems
B_{l}^{\prime\prime}B_{l+1}^{\prime\prime}
is approximately zero:

\tr\left[(\outerproduct{01}{01}+\outerproduct{10}{10})_{B^{\prime\prime}_{l}B^{\prime\prime}_{l+1}}\Gamma(\rho)\right]\approx 0.

(43)

We achieve this by choosing another operator
K_{B_{l}^{\prime}B_{l+1}^{\prime}}
with specific properties related to its partial transpose: its maximal eigenvalue
\lambda_{\max}(K)
is significantly larger than the maximal eigenvalues of its partial transposes,
\lambda_{\max}(K^{T_{B_{l}^{\prime}}})
and
\lambda_{\max}(K^{T_{B_{l+1}^{\prime}}})
.
Specifically, we choose the two-qubit observable
K
as:

K=\sigma_{X}\otimes\sigma_{X}-\sigma_{Y}\otimes\sigma_{Y}+\sigma_{Z}\otimes\sigma_{Z}.

(44)

We observe the following crucial properties: (i) The maximal eigenvalue of
K
is
\lambda_{\max}(K)=3
, which is attained by the Bell state
\phi^{+}
; (ii) The partial transpose of
K
with respect to either subsystem is:

K^{T_{B_{l}^{\prime}}}=K^{T_{B_{l+1}^{\prime}}}=\sigma_{X}\otimes\sigma_{X}+\sigma_{Y}\otimes\sigma_{Y}+\sigma_{Z}\otimes\sigma_{Z},

(45)

whose maximal eigenvalue is strictly smaller:
\lambda_{\max}(K^{T_{B_{l}^{\prime}}})=\lambda_{\max}(K^{T_{B_{l+1}^{\prime}}})=1
.

This strategy translates to the following procedure in the reference experiment:
First, the main parties
A_{l}
and
A_{l+1}
share a Bell state on the designated link qubits
R_{l}^{\vartriangleright}
and
R_{l+1}^{\vartriangleleft}
.
They then perform BSM on
R_{l}^{\vartriangleright}S_{l}
and
R_{l+1}^{\vartriangleleft}S_{l+1}
to teleport the shared Bell state to the auxiliary systems
B_{l}B_{l+1}
.
Finally, the auxiliary parties perform specific Pauli measurements designed to estimate the expectation value of
K
.

In the physical experiment, this procedure corresponds to the main parties
A_{l}
and
A_{l+1}
performing their measurements
\{M_{\mathbf{a}_{l}|\vartriangleright}^{(l)}\}_{\mathbf{a}_{l}}
and
\{M_{\mathbf{a}_{l+1}|\vartriangleleft}^{(l+1)}\}_{\mathbf{a}_{l+1}}
respectively. The auxiliary parties
B_{l}
and
B_{l+1}
perform their certified Pauli measurements
\{N_{b_{j}|P_{j}}^{(j)}\}_{b_{j}}
for
j=l,l+1
and
P_{j}\in\{X,Y,Z\}
. Utilizing the Pauli corrections (Eq. (
29
)), they calculate the estimator
v_{\mathrm{II}}
for the average two-party correlation:

v_{\mathrm{II}}(l,l+1)=\sum_{P\in\{X,Y,Z\}}\frac{1}{3}\left[1-(-1)^{\mathbf{1}[P=Y]}\mathbb{E}\left[(-1)^{f(b_{l}|P,\mathbf{a}_{l})+f(b_{l+1}|P,\mathbf{a}_{l+1})}\;\middle|\;x_{l}={\vartriangleright},x_{l+1}={\vartriangleleft},y_{l}=y_{l+1}=P\right]\right],

(46)

where the product bases
P_{l}P_{l+1}
are uniformly drawn from the set
\{XX,YY,ZZ\}
.

The gap between
\lambda_{\max}(K)=3
and
\lambda_{\max}(K^{T_{B_{l}^{\prime}}})=1
is directly reflected in the bounds of the estimator. Specifically, an ideal measurement estimating
K
yields the minimal value,
v_{\mathrm{II}}(l,l+1)=0
. Conversely, an expectation value corresponding to the presence of the unwanted local partial transpose
K^{T_{B_{l}^{\prime}}}
must yield
v_{\mathrm{II}}(l,l+1)\geq\frac{2}{3}
. This constant gap provides a mechanism to exclude partial transpose. The robust analysis is formalized in the following lemma.

Lemma S4

(Removing partial transpose)
.

Let
\{V_{B_{l}}\}_{l=0}^{n-1}
be the isometry defined in Lemma
S2
.
For any adjacent parties
l
and
l+1
and basis
k\in\{0,1,2\}
, suppose the deviation from the maximal CHSH value (Eq. (
7
)) is bounded by
v_{\mathrm{I}}(l,k)\leq\epsilon_{\mathrm{I},l}
and
v_{\mathrm{I}}(l+1,k)\leq\epsilon_{\mathrm{I},l+1}
for all
k\in\{0,1,2\}
, and the two-party estimator (Eq. (
46
)) is bounded by
v_{\mathrm{II}}(l,l+1)\leq\epsilon_{\mathrm{II},(l,l+1)}
.

Then, the probability of the unequal partial transpose components between adjacent parties is bounded by:

\tr[(\outerproduct{01}{01}+\outerproduct{10}{10})_{B_{l}^{\prime\prime}B_{l+1}^{\prime\prime}}V_{B_{l}}\otimes V_{B_{l+1}}\psi V_{B_{l}}^{\dagger}\otimes V_{B_{l+1}}^{\dagger}]=\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon_{\mathrm{II},(l,l+1)}+\sqrt{\epsilon_{\mathrm{I},l}}+\sqrt{\epsilon_{\mathrm{I},l+1}}\right).

(47)

Proof.

W.l.o.g., we prove the claim for
l=0
.
The two-party witness estimator
v_{\mathrm{II}}(0,1)
is a specific instance of the general Pauli measurement estimator
v_{\mathrm{III}}
(Eq. (
30
)) under the following substitutions: (i) The number of parties is
n=2
; (ii) The basis distribution
\mathcal{D}
for
\mathbf{P}=P_{0}P_{1}
is uniform over
\{XX,YY,ZZ\}
; (iii) The weighting coefficient is
\omega(\mathbf{b}|\mathbf{P})=1-(-1)^{\delta_{P_{0}Y}}(-1)^{b_{0}+b_{1}}
, bounded by

.
Moreover, the channel
\Gamma
is defined by replacing the original BSM operators in Eq. (

M^{(0)}_{\mathbf{a}_{0}|\diamond}\rightarrow M^{(0)}_{\mathbf{a}_{0}|\vartriangleright},M^{(1)}_{\mathbf{a}_{1}|\diamond}\rightarrow M^{(1)}_{\mathbf{a}_{1}|\vartriangleleft}
.
The associated observable,

, derived from this specific choice of weights and bases, is

.

By Lemma

,

\absolutevalue{\tr[\left(\sum_{\iota\in\{0,1\}^2}L_{B_0'B_1'}^{T_\iota}\otimes\ketbra{\iota}_{B_0''B_{1}''}\right)\Gamma(\psi)] - v_{\mathrm{II}}(0,1)}=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\epsilon_{\mathrm{I},0}}+\sqrt{\epsilon_{\mathrm{I},1}}).

(48)

Since
v_{\mathrm{II}}(0,1)\leq\epsilon_{\mathrm{II},(0,1)}
, we have:

\tr[\left(\sum_{\iota\in\{0,1\}^{2}}L_{B_{0}^{\prime}B_{1}^{\prime}}^{T_{\iota}}\otimes\outerproduct{\iota}{\iota}_{B_{0}^{\prime\prime}B_{1}^{\prime\prime}}\right)\Gamma(\psi)]=\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon_{\mathrm{II},(0,1)}+\sqrt{\epsilon_{\mathrm{I},0}}+\sqrt{\epsilon_{\mathrm{I},1}}\right).

(49)

Moreover, we utilize the spectral properties of

:
\lambda_{\min}(L)=\lambda_{\min}(L^{T})=1-\tfrac{1}{3}\lambda_{\max}(K)=0
, and
\lambda_{\min}(L^{T_{0}})=\lambda_{\min}(L^{T_{1}})=1-\tfrac{1}{3}\lambda_{\max}(K^{T_{0}})=\tfrac{2}{3}
. Therefore,

\tr[\left(\sum_{\iota\in\{0,1\}^{2}}L_{B_{0}^{\prime}B_{1}^{\prime}}^{T_{\iota}}\otimes\outerproduct{\iota}{\iota}_{B_{0}^{\prime\prime}B_{1}^{\prime\prime}}\right)\Gamma(\psi)]\geq\frac{2}{3}\tr[(\outerproduct{01}{01}+\outerproduct{10}{10})_{B_{0}^{\prime\prime}B_{1}^{\prime\prime}}\Gamma(\psi)]

(50)

Combining the above two equations gives

\tr[(\outerproduct{01}{01}+\outerproduct{10}{10})_{B_{0}^{\prime\prime}B_{1}^{\prime\prime}}\Gamma(\psi)]=\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon_{\mathrm{II},(0,1)}+\sqrt{\epsilon_{\mathrm{I},0}}+\sqrt{\epsilon_{\mathrm{I},1}}\right).

(51)

Finally, the channel
\Gamma(\rho)
(see (
31
)) can be decomposed as

\Gamma(\rho)=\Gamma_{A}(V_{B_{0}}\otimes V_{B_{1}}\rho V_{B_{0}}^{\dagger}\otimes V_{B_{1}}^{\dagger})

(52)

for a channel
\Gamma_{A}
that acts trivially on the

systems and hence commutes with
(\outerproduct{01}{01}+\outerproduct{10}{10})_{B_{0}^{\prime\prime}B_{1}^{\prime\prime}}
.
Then Eq. (
47

As mentioned, removing partial transposes between all adjacent pairs of parties is sufficient to implement multipartite Pauli measurements up to global transpose. This result is summarized in the following lemma.

Lemma S5

(DI multipartite Pauli measurements up to global transpose)
.

Let
L
be the
n
-partite observable defined in Eq. (
1
), with the weighting coefficients bounded by a constant
W>1
, i.e.,
|\omega(\mathbf{b}|\mathbf{P})|\leq W

\Gamma
be the quantum channel representing the teleportation followed by Pauli correction (Eq. (

Suppose that the deviation from the maximal CHSH value (Eq. (
7

\left\lvert v_{\mathrm{III}}-\tr[\left(L_{B^{\prime}}\otimes\outerproduct{0}{0}_{B^{\prime\prime}}^{\otimes n}+L_{B^{\prime}}^{*}\otimes\outerproduct{1}{1}_{B^{\prime\prime}}^{\otimes n}\right)\Gamma(\rho)]\right\rvert=\mathcal{O}(W\varepsilon).

Proof.

Define
S_{j}\coloneqq\{\iota:\iota\in\{0,1\}^{n},\iota_{j}\neq\iota_{j+1}\}

\begin{split}1-\tr[(\outerproduct{0}{0}^{\otimes n}+\outerproduct{1}{1}^{\otimes n})_{B^{\prime\prime}}\Gamma(\rho)]&=\tr[\left(\sum_{\iota\neq 0^{n},1^{n}}\outerproduct{\iota}{\iota}_{B^{\prime\prime}}\right)\Gamma(\rho)]\\

\begin{split}\absolutevalue{\tr[\left(L_{B'}\otimes\ketbra{0}_{B''}^{\otimes n}+L_{B'}^*\otimes\ketbra{1}_{B''}^{\otimes n} \right)\Gamma(\rho)] - \tr[\left(\sum_{\iota\in\{0,1\}^n}L^{T_\iota}_{B'}\otimes\ketbra{\iota}_{B''} \right)\Gamma(\rho)]}&=\absolutevalue{\tr[\left(\sum_{\iota\neq 0^n, 1^n}L^{T_\iota}_{B'}\otimes\ketbra{\iota}_{B''} \right)\Gamma(\rho)]}\\

Combining Eq. (

by triangular inequality proves Eq. (

D.5
Refining the extraction channel
\Gamma

The result established in Lemma
S5
, while guaranteeing DI multipartite Pauli measurements up to a global transpose, presents a drawback: the extraction channel
\Gamma=\bigotimes_{l}\Gamma_{l}
is not a product of standard quantum channels acting only on the main parties
A_{l}
. This is because
\Gamma_{l}
requires classical communication between
A_{l}
and
B_{l}
for the Pauli correction.
To develop a more conventional and practical self-testing protocol, it is highly desirable, yet challenging to construct an extraction channel
\Lambda=\bigotimes_{l}\Lambda_{l}
that is a product of local quantum channels
\Lambda_{l}
, acting only on the main systems
A_{l}
where the target state resides. This subtlety has not always been addressed in prior work
43
.

Here, we settle this problem by explicitly constructing such a channel. We summarize the result in the following theorem. Detailed proofs are deferred to the end of this section.

Theorem S1

(Self-testing multipartite Pauli measurements with local extraction channel)
.

Let
L
be the
n
-partite observable defined in Eq. (
1
), with the weighting coefficients bounded by a constant
W>1
, i.e.,
|\omega(\mathbf{b}|\mathbf{P})|\leq W
.
Suppose that the deviation from the maximal CHSH value (Eq. (
7
)) for each party
l\in[n]
in basis
k\in\{0,1,2\}
is bounded by
v_{\mathrm{I}}(l,k)\leq\epsilon_{\mathrm{I},l}
, and the two-party estimator (Eq. (
46
)) for
l\in[n-1]
is bounded by
v_{\mathrm{II}}(l,l+1)\leq\epsilon_{\mathrm{II},(l,l+1)}
.
Let
\varepsilon=\sum_{j=0}^{n-2}\epsilon_{\mathrm{II},(j,j+1)}+\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\sqrt{\epsilon_{\mathrm{I},l}}
.

Then, there exists a product of local quantum channels (with explicit construction given in Eq. (
70
)):

\Lambda=\bigotimes_{l=0}^{n-1}\Lambda_{l},\quad\Lambda_{l}:A_{l}\rightarrow A^{\flat}_{l}A^{{\flat}{\flat}}_{l},

(60)

where
A^{\flat}_{l},A^{{\flat}{\flat}}_{l}
are two-dimensional qubit spaces, such that the total probability of the non-identity and non-full transpose terms is negligible:

\tr[(\outerproduct{0}{0}^{\otimes n}+\outerproduct{1}{1}^{\otimes n})_{A^{{\flat}{\flat}}}\Lambda(\rho)]=1-\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon),

(61)

and the estimator
v_{\mathrm{III}}
in the teleportation-and-measurement protocol (Eq. (
30
)) approximates the expectation value of the target observable
L
up to global transpose, specifically:

\left\lvert v_{\mathrm{III}}-\tr[\left(L_{A^{\flat}}\otimes\outerproduct{0}{0}_{A^{{\flat}{\flat}}}^{\otimes n}+L_{A^{\flat}}^{*}\otimes\outerproduct{1}{1}_{A^{{\flat}{\flat}}}^{\otimes n}\right)\Lambda(\rho)]\right\rvert=\mathcal{O}(W\varepsilon).

(62)

Now we proceed to construct the local channel
\Lambda_{l}
. Comparing the target Eq. (
62
) with the previous result in Eq. (
54
), the task is to replace the auxiliary systems
B^{\prime}_{l}
and
B^{\prime\prime}_{l}
with the new local systems
A^{\flat}
and
A^{{\flat}{\flat}}
.
We leverage the properties of the local isometry
V_{A_{l}}V_{B_{l}}
(given in Eq. (
20
)), which maps the input state
\ket{\psi}
to an approximately factorized form:

V_{A_{l}}V_{B_{l}}\ket{\psi}_{ABR}\approx_{\sqrt{\epsilon_{\mathrm{I},l}}}\ket{\phi^+}_{A_{l}^{\prime}B_{l}^{\prime}}\ket{\xi_l}_{ABRA^{\prime\prime}_{l}B^{\prime\prime}_{l}},

(63)

where

\ket{\xi_l}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\left[\ket{00}_{A^{\prime\prime}_{l}B^{\prime\prime}_{l}}(\mathbb{I}+i\hat{Y}_{A}^{(l)}\hat{X}_{A}^{(l)})+\ket{11}_{A^{\prime\prime}_{l}B^{\prime\prime}_{l}}(\mathbb{I}-i\hat{Y}_{A}^{(l)}\hat{X}_{A}^{(l)})\right](\mathbb{I}+\hat{Z}_{A}^{(l)})\ket{\psi}_{ABR}.

(64)

Our strategy for constructing
\Lambda_{l}
is based on the following ideas:

1.

To replace
B^{\prime}_{l}
with
A^{\flat}_{l}
, we throw away the Bell state
\ket{\phi^+}_{A^{\prime}_{l}B^{\prime}_{l}}
, and locally prepare a Bell state
\ket{\phi^+}_{A^{\prime}_{l}A^{\flat}_{l}}
to “counterfeit” the original one that was essential to the teleportation mechanism.

2.

To replace
B^{\prime\prime}_{l}
with
A^{{\flat}{\flat}}_{l}
, we introduce a fresh ancilla
\ket{0}_{A^{{\flat}{\flat}}_{l}}
and apply
CX_{B^{\prime\prime}_{l}A^{{\flat}{\flat}}_{l}}
to copy the information from
B^{\prime\prime}_{l}
to
A^{{\flat}{\flat}}_{l}
.

3.

By observing from
\ket{\xi_l}
that the
A^{\prime\prime}_{l}
register is perfectly correlated with
B^{\prime\prime}_{l}
, we can replace the non-local unitary
CX_{B^{\prime\prime}_{l}A^{{\flat}{\flat}}_{l}}
with a local unitary
CX_{A^{\prime\prime}_{l}A^{{\flat}{\flat}}_{l}}
.

To formalize the conceptual steps outlined above, we introduce an intermediate channel
\Lambda_{0}
:

\Lambda_{0}\coloneqq\bigotimes_{l=0}^{n-1}\Lambda_{l}^{0},\quad\Lambda^{0}_{l}:\rho\rightarrow\mathcal{CX}_{A^{\prime\prime}_{l}A^{{\flat}{\flat}}_{l}}(\tr_{A_{l}^{\prime}B_{l}^{\prime}}[\mathcal{V}_{A_{l}}(\mathcal{V}_{B_{l}}(\rho))]\otimes\phi^{+}_{A^{\prime}A^{\flat}}\otimes\outerproduct{0}{0}_{A^{{\flat}{\flat}}_{l}}).

(65)

Here,
\mathcal{V}:\rho\rightarrow V\rho V^{\dagger}
denotes the channel representation of the corresponding isometry
V
, and
\mathcal{CX}
is defined analogously. For compactness, we use
\mathcal{V}_{A}\coloneqq\bigotimes_{l=0}^{n-1}\mathcal{V}_{A_{l}}
and similarly for
\mathcal{V}_{B},\mathcal{CX}_{A^{\prime\prime}A^{{\flat}{\flat}}},\outerproduct{0}{0}_{A^{{\flat}{\flat}}},\phi^{+}_{A^{\prime}A^{\flat}}
.
We show that
\Lambda_{0}
successfully replaces
B^{\prime}_{l}
with
A^{\flat}_{l}
, and copies information from
B^{\prime\prime}_{l}
to
A^{{\flat}{\flat}}
, in the following sense.

Lemma S6

(Replacing
B_{l}^{\prime}B_{l}^{\prime\prime}
with

.

Suppose that the deviation from the maximal CHSH value (Eq. (

l\in[n]

(66)

and

After the replacement, we recover the original physical state on

Appendix E
Sample complexity analysis

Having established self-tested Pauli measurement schemes based on the accurate values of
v_{\mathrm{I}}
,
v_{\mathrm{II}}
, and
v_{\mathrm{III}}
(Box
D.4
and Theorem
S1
), we now analyze a crucial step for practical implementation and scalability: the sample complexity required for robustly estimating these estimators in a physical experiment using finite samples.

A central challenge arises in estimating the multi-party estimator
v_{\mathrm{III}}
. The protocol requires the spatially separated main parties
A_{l}
to collectively teleport the entire
n
-partite state to the ancillary qubits
B_{l}
. Subsequently, the ancillary parties
B_{l}
must sample a product Pauli basis
\mathbf{P}
from the probability distribution
\mathcal{D}
that defines the observable
L
. This collaborative sampling becomes challenging when the target distribution
\mathcal{D}
is correlated across the individual systems.

Here, we address the sample complexity under two distinct scenarios, yielding two main sample complexity theorems:

1.

No shared randomness (local sampling): The spatially separated parties use their local randomness to choose measurement settings independently from each other. While even correlated distributions
\mathcal{D}
can be sampled in this manner through rejection sampling, we consider a target distribution
\mathcal{D}
that can be decomposed into a product of local probability distributions (Theorem
S2
).

2.

Shared randomness (collaborative sampling): The spatially separated parties have access to shared randomness, which could be distributed using DI quantum key distribution protocols or similar means. They utilize this resource to sample the measurement settings
\mathbf{P}
collaboratively, enabling the efficient implementation of non-local probability distributions
\mathcal{D}
(Theorem
S3
).

E.1
General case

We proceed by first considering the most general case, where an arbitrary joint probability distribution governs the choice of measurement settings across all
n
parties. Subsequently, we will apply this general analysis to derive the specific sample complexity bounds for the two practical scenarios mentioned above.

We will use standard concentration inequalities in the following formulation:

Fact S1

(Chernoff bound)
.

Let
T>0
and
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{T}
be a set of
T
i.i.d. binary random variables with
\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]=p
. Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have
\sum_{i=1}^{T}X_{i}\geq N
, provided that
T=\Theta\left(\frac{1}{p}(N+\log\frac{1}{\delta})\right)
.

Fact S2

(Hoeffding inequality)
.

Let
T>0
and
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{T}
be a set of
T
i.i.d. random variables with
\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]=\mu
and
\absolutevalue{X_i}\leq\sigma
. Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have
\absolutevalue{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^T X_i - \mu}\leq\epsilon
, provided that
T=\Theta\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)
.

After establishing the general sample complexity analysis, we now proceed to analyze the detailed sample complexity in concrete scenarios. This is achieved by devising the probability distribution for the measurement settings and substituting it into the above analysis.

E.2
Using local randomness

We first consider the scenario in which each party uses local randomness to sample their measurement settings independently.

In this scenario, we need to ensure that each main party chooses to teleport the input state to the auxiliary system with a noticeable probability. This is essential to prevent an unfavorable scaling in the estimation of
v_{\mathrm{III}}
(Lemma
S9
) due to
\Pr_{\operatorname{MS}}[\diamond^{n};A\land\mathbf{y}\leftarrow\mathcal{D}]
being too small.
To achieve this, each main party
A_{l}
selects the teleportation setting
\diamond
with a high probability, for example:

\Pr_{\operatorname{MS}}[\diamond;A_{l}]=1-\frac{1}{n}.

(83)

Then, the probability that all main parties simultaneously choose the teleportation setting
\diamond
is larger than a constant:

\Pr_{\operatorname{MS}}[\diamond^{n};A]=\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{n}=\Omega(1).

(84)

Additionally, to ensure a noticeable probability for the 3-CHSH test and the coordinating partial transpose settings, the main parties sample the remaining
8
measurement settings with equal probability:

\Pr_{\operatorname{MS}}[i;A_{l}]=\frac{1}{8n}\quad\text{for }i\in\{0,1,2,3,4,5,\vartriangleright,\vartriangleleft\}.

(85)

Furthermore, we require that the sampled measurement settings
\mathbf{y}
from the auxiliary parties be effectively sampled from the target distribution
\mathcal{D}
of the desired Pauli measurements. However, a general distribution
\mathcal{D}
might necessitate correlated sampling among the auxiliary parties, which could result in the probability
\Pr_{\operatorname{MS}}[\diamond^{n};A\land\mathbf{y}\leftarrow\mathcal{D}]
becoming exponentially small, thereby rendering the self-testing protocol inefficient (Lemma
S9
).
To avoid this, we focus on Pauli measurements where the desired distribution
\mathcal{D}
allows for independent sampling across the different auxiliary systems
l
. That is,
\mathcal{D}
admits a product distribution:

\mathcal{D}=\bigotimes_{l=0}^{n-1}\mathcal{D}^{(l)},\quad\mathcal{D}^{(l)}=(p_{X}^{(l)},p_{Y}^{(l)},p_{Z}^{(l)}).

(86)

In this case, each auxiliary party
B_{l}
can simply sample their measurement setting according to the marginal distribution
\mathcal{D}_{l}
independently. We additionally impose that
p_{X}^{(l)},p_{Y}^{(l)},p_{Z}^{(l)}=\Omega(1)
for all
l
, ensuring that the probability of sampling the specific settings required for estimating
v_{\mathrm{I}}
and
v_{\mathrm{II}}
is sufficiently large.

Crucially, because the marginal probabilities
p_{X}^{(l)},p_{Y}^{(l)},p_{Z}^{(l)}
are generally not equal, the joint measurement settings
y_{l}=y_{l+1}\in\{X,Y,Z\}
required for evaluating
v_{\mathrm{II}}
are sampled with varying probabilities. However, the original definition of
v_{\mathrm{II}}
relies on a uniform distribution over these three joint settings. To correct this discrepancy and construct an unbiased estimator, we apply a rejection sampling procedure. Specifically, to estimate
v_{\mathrm{II}}
for a given
l
, we accept a sample with an observed joint setting
y
with probability

\frac{\min_{y^{\prime}\in[3]}{\Pr_{\operatorname{MS}}[\vartriangleright,\vartriangleleft,y^{\prime},y^{\prime};A_{l},A_{l+1},B_{l},B_{l+1}]}}{\Pr_{\operatorname{MS}}[\vartriangleright,\vartriangleleft,y,y;A_{l},A_{l+1},B_{l},B_{l+1}]}.

(87)

This effectively equalizes the sampling rates, ensuring that each matching pair
y_{l}=y_{l+1}
contributes to the final update with the same uniform probability
\min_{y^{\prime}\in[3]}\{\Pr_{\operatorname{MS}}[\vartriangleright,\vartriangleleft,y^{\prime},y^{\prime};A_{l},A_{l+1},B_{l},B_{l+1}]\}
.
This guarantees an unbiased estimator for
v_{\mathrm{II}}
, which is implemented as in (
93

\absolutevalue{\hvIII- \vIII}&\leq\varepsilon.\end{split}

(97)

We will analyze the required
N
at the end of the proof and proceed assuming (
97
).
Given this, we analyze the consequences conditional on the protocol outputting
CERTIFIED
.
We can bound the sum of square roots of the true values
v_{\mathrm{I}}
:

\begin{split}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\sum_{k=0}^{2}\sqrt{v_{\mathrm{I}}(l,k)}&\leq\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\sum_{k=0}^{2}\sqrt{\hat{v}_{\mathrm{I}}(l,k)+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{W^{2}n^{2}}}\\
&=\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\sum_{k=0}^{2}\frac{1}{3n}\sqrt{9n^{2}\hat{v}_{\mathrm{I}}(l,k)+\frac{9\varepsilon^{2}}{W^{2}}}\\
&\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{3n}\left[\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\sum_{k=0}^{2}\left(9n^{2}\hat{v}_{\mathrm{I}}(l,k)+\frac{9\varepsilon^{2}}{W^{2}}\right)\right]}\\
&\leq\sqrt{9n^{2}\hat{v}_{\mathrm{I}}+\frac{9\varepsilon^{2}}{W^{2}}}\\
&=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{W}\right).\end{split}

(98)

Here, the third line uses Jensen’s inequality, the fourth line uses
\hat{v}_{\mathrm{I}}=\frac{1}{3n}\sum_{l}\sum_{k}\hat{v}_{\mathrm{I}}(l,k)
, and the fifth line uses the condition for outputting
CERTIFIED
:
\hat{v}_{\mathrm{I}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{n^{2}W^{2}}
. We can also bound the sum of the true values

:

\begin{split}\sum_{l=0}^{n-2}v_{\mathrm{II}}(l,l+1)&\leq\sum_{l=0}^{n-2}\hat{v}_{\mathrm{II}}(l,l+1)+\frac{\varepsilon}{W}\\
&\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{W}\right),\end{split}

(99)

where we use
\hat{v}_{\mathrm{II}}=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{l=0}^{n-2}\hat{v}_{\mathrm{II}}(l,l+1)
and the condition for outputting
CERTIFIED

\hat{v}_{\mathrm{II}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{nW}
.

Next, let
\sigma\coloneqq\Lambda(\rho)
, where

is the product of local channels on the main systems

as specified in Theorem
S1
.
We decompose

with respect to the indicator systems. Define the unnormalized states

and

with weights

Let

denote the product of single-qubit dephasing channels on the ancillary indicator systems. The action of the channel yields

\mathcal{D}(\sigma)=p_{+}\tau_{+}\otimes\outerproduct{0}{0}^{\otimes n}+p_{-}\tau_{-}^{*}\otimes\outerproduct{1}{1}^{\otimes n}+(1-p_{+}-p_{-})\tilde{\sigma}

(100)

for some residual state
\tilde{\sigma}
.

Defining the total map

\begin{split}p_{+}+p_{-}&=\tr[(\outerproduct{0}{0}^{\otimes n}+\outerproduct{1}{1}^{\otimes n})\Gamma(\rho)]\\
&=\tr[(\outerproduct{0}{0}^{\otimes n}+\outerproduct{1}{1}^{\otimes n})\Lambda(\rho)]\\
&=1-\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{W}\right),\end{split}

(101)

where the second inequality comes from the property of dephasing, and the third inequality comes from Theorem

Set

\begin{split}\absolutevalue{\vIII- \tr(L \tau)}&\leq\absolutevalue{ \vIII- \tr\left[\left(L\otimes\ketbra{0}^{\otimes n}+L^*\otimes\ketbra{1}^{\otimes n} \right)\Gamma(\rho)\right] }\\

∎

E.3

Appendix F
Completeness analysis

Our previous analysis has established the soundness of our self-testing protocols, guaranteeing a small estimation error when the protocols output
CERTIFIED
.
However, a trivial protocol that always outputs
FAILED
would also satisfy this soundness requirement, yet it is useless for self-testing any quantum device.
Therefore, we now proceed to analyze the
completeness
of our protocols. We show that even if the physical realization implements the reference experiment with noticeable but small imperfections, our self-testing protocols will still output
CERTIFIED
with high probability after
\text{poly}(n,\varepsilon^{-1})
rounds.
This establishes the utility and practical applicability of our self-testing protocols.

Here, we consider imperfections arising from both the underlying quantum state and the implemented measurements. We remark that the completeness analysis requires the existence of an embedding of the reference experiment into the physical experiment. That is, the support of the reference state,
\tau_{T}\otimes\Phi^{+}
, is a subspace of the support of the actual input state
\rho=\psi
of the physical experiment, and the reference measurement operators can be extended trivially onto the domain of the measurement operators in the physical experiment.
For simplicity in the subsequent derivations, we assume throughout this section that this embedding has already been performed and that the reference state and the actual input state reside in the same Hilbert space.

Regarding imperfections in the state, we quantify the imperfection by the trace distance between the actual input state
\rho
and the ideal reference state, which is a product of Bell states
\Phi^{+}
tensored with a state
\tau_{T}
on the system
T
.
Here,
\Phi^{+}
is composed of Bell states shared between all required auxiliary register pairs (e.g., between
R^{\vartriangleright}_{l}
and
R^{\vartriangleleft}_{l+1}
, and between
S_{l}
and
B_{l}
, for all
l
).
The state error
\epsilon_{\mathrm{S}}
is defined as the minimum trace distance over any states
\tau_{T}
on the input system
\mathcal{H}_{T}
:

\epsilon_{\mathrm{S}}\coloneqq\min_{\tau_{T}}D(\rho,\tau_{T}\otimes\Phi^{+}).

(112)

For the measurement imperfections, we note that each measurement can be represented as a quantum channel. The measurement channel
\mathcal{M}_{x_{l}}^{(l)}
maps the quantum state to a classical probability distribution over the outcomes
\mathbf{a}_{l}
:

\mathcal{M}_{x_{l}}^{(l)}(\rho)=\sum_{\mathbf{a}_{l}}\tr\left(\rho M_{\mathbf{a}_{l}|x_{l}}^{(l)}\right)\outerproduct{\vec{a}_l}{\vec{a}_l},

(113)

where
M_{\mathbf{a}_{l}|x_{l}}^{(l)}
is the measurement operator and
\outerproduct{\vec{a}_l}{\vec{a}_l}
is the classical output state encoding the outcome. The channel
\mathcal{N}_{y_{l}}^{(l)}
for party
B_{l}
is defined similarly.
Denote the ideal measurement channels in the reference experiment as
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{x_{l}}^{(l)}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{y_{l}}^{(l)}
. The measurement imperfection
\epsilon_{\mathrm{M}}
is then quantified by the maximum diamond distance between the implemented channel and its ideal counterpart, maximized over all parties
l
and all relevant measurement settings
x_{l}
and
y_{l}
:

\epsilon_{\mathrm{M}}=\max\left\{\max_{l,x_{l}}\left\{\left\lVert\mathcal{M}_{x_{l}}^{(l)}-\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{x_{l}}^{(l)}\right\rVert_{\diamond}\right\},\max_{l,y_{l}}\left\{\left\lVert\mathcal{N}_{y_{l}}^{(l)}-\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{y_{l}}^{(l)}\right\rVert_{\diamond}\right\}\right\}.

(114)

Then, we have the following completeness guarantee.

Theorem S4

(Completeness of DI multipartite Pauli measurement protocols)
.

Let
L
be the
n
-partite observable defined in Eq. (
1
), with the weighting coefficients bounded by a constant
W>1
, i.e.,
|\omega(\mathbf{b}|\mathbf{P})|\leq W
.
Let
\epsilon_{\mathrm{S}}
and
\epsilon_{\mathrm{M}}
be the state and measurement device errors defined in Eqs. (
112
) and (
114
).
For a desired precision
\varepsilon>0
, failure probability
0<\delta<1
, suppose the device imperfections satisfy the bound:

\epsilon_{\mathrm{S}},\epsilon_{\mathrm{M}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{n^{2}W^{2}}\right).

(115)

Then, for a total number of samples
N
chosen as specified below, with probability at least
1-\delta
:

1.

Protocol
E.2
outputs
CERTIFIED
and
\hat{v}_{\mathrm{III}}
. This protocol requires the target measurement distribution
\mathcal{D}
for
L
to be a product distribution
\mathcal{D}=\bigotimes_{l=0}^{n-1}\mathcal{D}^{(l)}
, with marginal probabilities satisfying
p_{X}^{(l)},p_{Y}^{(l)},p_{Z}^{(l)}=\Omega(1)
for all
l\in[n]
. The required sample complexity is set to
N=\mathcal{O}\left(W^{4}n^{5}\frac{\log(\frac{n}{\delta})}{\varepsilon^{4}}\right)
.

2.

Protocol
E.3
outputs
CERTIFIED
and
\hat{v}_{\mathrm{III}}
. The required sample complexity is set to
N=\mathcal{O}\left(W^{4}n^{4}\frac{\log(\frac{n}{\delta})}{\varepsilon^{4}}\right)
.

Let
\tilde{\tau}_{T}
be any reference state in
T
. In both cases, the returned
\hat{v}_{\mathrm{III}}
satisfies

|\hat{v}_{\mathrm{III}}-\tr(L\tilde{\tau}_T)|=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon+WD(\rho,\tilde{\tau}_{T}\otimes\Phi^{+})).

(116)

Equation (
116
) tells us that if the state
\rho
in the physical experiment approximately equals a product state
\tilde{\tau}_{T}
on system
T
and the reference bell states
\Phi^{+}
on the other systems, then
\hat{v}_{\mathrm{III}}
approximates
\tr(L\tilde{\tau}_T)
.

Proof.

In the reference experiment, the deviation values
v_{\mathrm{I}}(l,k)
and
v_{\mathrm{II}}(l,l+1)
are exactly zero for all
l
,
k
and test states
\tau_{T}
, as the ideal setup perfectly achieves the bounds
S(l,k)=2\sqrt{2}
and the partial transpose identity (Eq. (
46
)).

We first analyze the values
v_{\mathrm{I}}(l,k)
and
v_{\mathrm{II}}(l,l+1)
in the presence of physical device imperfections
\epsilon_{\mathrm{S}}
and
\epsilon_{\mathrm{M}}
.
We consider
v_{\mathrm{II}}(l,l+1)
as an example. Its expected value is a summation (weighted by coefficients that are all
\mathcal{O}(1)
) over measurement outcomes of the composed measurement channel
\mathcal{C}_{y_{l}}\coloneqq\mathcal{M}^{(l)}_{\vartriangleright}\otimes\mathcal{M}^{(l+1)}_{\vartriangleleft}\otimes\mathcal{N}_{y_{l}}^{(l)}\otimes\mathcal{N}_{y_{l+1}}^{(l+1)}
for
y_{l}=y_{l+1}\in\{0,1,2\}
(see Eq. (
46
)). We denote the corresponding ideal channel as
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{y_{l}}
. By the triangle inequalities, we have:

\left\lVert\mathcal{C}_{y_{l}}-\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{y_{l}}\right\rVert_{\diamond}\leq\left\lVert\mathcal{M}^{(l)}_{\vartriangleright}-\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(l)}_{\vartriangleright}\right\rVert_{\diamond}+\left\lVert\mathcal{M}^{(l+1)}_{\vartriangleleft}-\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(l+1)}_{\vartriangleleft}\right\rVert_{\diamond}+\left\lVert\mathcal{N}_{y_{l}}^{(l)}-\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{y_{l}}^{(l)}\right\rVert_{\diamond}+\left\lVert\mathcal{N}_{y_{l+1}}^{(l+1)}-\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{y_{l+1}}^{(l+1)}\right\rVert_{\diamond}\leq 4\epsilon_{\mathrm{M}}.

(117)

The value of
v_{\mathrm{II}}
can be written as
\sum_{y_{l}}\frac{1}{3}\tr[O_{\mathrm{II},y_{l}}\mathcal{C}_{y_{l}}(\rho)]
with
O_{\mathrm{II},y_{l}}
a weighting of the different measurement outcomes (as specified in Box
D.4
).
Using the general inequality
\tr[O\tau_{1}]-\tr[O\tau_{2}]\leq\left\lVert O\right\rVert_{\infty}D(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
we can hence bound,

\begin{split}v_{\mathrm{II}}(l,l+1)-0&=\tr\left[\sum_{y_{l}}\frac{1}{3}O_{\mathrm{II},y_{l}}(\mathcal{C}_{y_{l}}(\rho)-\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{y_{l}}(\tau\otimes\Phi^{+}))\right]\\
&\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\sum_{y_{l}}D\left(\mathcal{C}_{y_{l}}(\rho),\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{y_{l}}(\tau\otimes\Phi^{+})\right)\right)\\
&\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\sum_{y_{l}}D\left(\mathcal{C}_{y_{l}}(\rho),\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{y_{l}}(\rho)\right)\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\sum_{y_{l}}D\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{y_{l}}(\rho),\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{y_{l}}(\tau\otimes\Phi^{+})\right)\right)\\
&\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\sum_{y_{l}}\left\lVert\mathcal{C}_{y_{l}}-\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{y_{l}}\right\rVert_{\diamond}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(D\left(\rho,\tau\otimes\Phi^{+}\right)\right)\\
&\leq\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{\mathrm{M}}+\epsilon_{\mathrm{S}}).\end{split}

(118)

Here,
\tau
is the state that obtains the minimum in (
112
). The third line uses the definition of diamond norm and the data processing inequality.
Since we assume
\epsilon_{\mathrm{S}},\epsilon_{\mathrm{M}}\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{n^{2}W^{2}}\right)
, the expectation value is bounded by
v_{\mathrm{II}}(l,l+1)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{n^{2}W^{2}}\right)\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{nW}\right)
.
A similar argument applies to
v_{\mathrm{I}}(l,k)
, yielding
v_{\mathrm{I}}(l,k)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{n^{2}W^{2}}\right)
.

By setting the sample complexity
N
as specified in the theorem, the estimation errors for the finite sample estimates
\hat{v}
are controlled by Eq. (
97
) with probability at least
1-\delta
(follow the proofs of Theorems
S2
and
S3
), which gives
\hat{v}_{\mathrm{I}}(l,k)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{n^{2}W^{2}}\right)
and
\hat{v}_{\mathrm{II}}(l,l+1)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{nW}\right)
for any
l,k
. Therefore,

\begin{split}\hat{v}_{\mathrm{I}}&=\frac{1}{3n}\sum_{l}\sum_{k}\hat{v}_{\mathrm{I}}(l,k)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{n^{2}W^{2}}\right),\\
\hat{v}_{\mathrm{II}}&=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{l=0}^{n-2}\hat{v}_{\mathrm{II}}(l,l+1)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{nW}\right).\end{split}

(119)

By increasing
N
and tightening the errors
\epsilon_{\mathrm{S}},\epsilon_{\mathrm{M}}
by a constant scale (which does not change the order), we have
\hat{v}_{\mathrm{I}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{n^{2}W^{2}},\hat{v}_{\mathrm{II}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{nW}
, which satisfies the criteria in the certification check steps (see Protocol
E.2
), therefore the protocols will output
CERTIFIED
.

To conclude the proof, we analyze the value of
\hat{v}_{\mathrm{III}}
produced by Protocol
E.2
and Protocol
E.3
. In the ideal reference experiment performed on the state
\tilde{\tau}_{T}\otimes\Phi^{+}
, the value
v_{\mathrm{III}}
equals
\tr(L\tilde{\tau}_T)
. We show that
\hat{v}_{\mathrm{III}}
remains close to this ideal value, with a deviation bounded by the measurement and state imperfections.
Concretely, we define the physical measurement channel for a given Pauli string

\mathcal{C_{\mathbf{P}}}=\bigotimes_{l=0}^{n-1}\mathcal{M}^{(l)}_{\diamond}\otimes\mathcal{N}_{(\mathbf{P})_{l}}^{(l)}

\begin{split}|v_{\mathrm{III}}-\tr(L\widetilde{\tau}_T)|&\leq\mathcal{O}(W\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{P}\leftarrow\mathcal{D}}[D\left(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{P}}(\rho),\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{P}}(\widetilde{\tau}_{T}\otimes\Phi^{+})\right)]\\

(120)

Here, we use similar arguments as in (

\left\lVert O_{\mathrm{III}}\right\rVert_{\infty}=\mathcal{O}(W)
(instead of
\mathcal{O}(1)

(instead of
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{\mathrm{M}})
).

Following the sample complexity analysis in the proofs of Theorems

and
S3

\hat{v}_{\mathrm{III}}\approx_{\varepsilon}v_{\mathrm{III}}
with high probability. Consequently,

\hat{v}_{\mathrm{III}}\approx_{\varepsilon+WD(\rho,\tilde{\tau}_{T}\otimes\Phi^{+})}\tr(L\tilde{\tau}_T).

(121)

Appendix G
Sample complexity for self-testing generic multipartite states

After establishing that our protocol robustly self-tests multipartite Pauli measurements, we now demonstrate that this framework leads to a sample-efficient self-testing protocol for a generic multipartite target state
\Psi
.

G.1
Definition for self-testing states

The goal of self-testing a state is to use statistics from local measurements on spatially separated systems to certify that the physical state is equivalent to a reference state
\Psi
, up to local quantum channels. A protocol that self-tests a state
\Psi
is sound if it can only succeed for states that are close to
\Psi
. It is complete if it succeeds with high probability for a physical experiment that is close to the reference experiment.

Definition S1

(Robust self-testing of states)
.

Consider a protocol which certifies whether a physical state
\rho
is
\varepsilon
-close to an
n
-partite pure state
\Psi
by outputting either
CERTIFIED
or
FAILED
.
We characterize the protocol by the following two properties:

1.

(\varepsilon,\delta)-
soundness
: Suppose that for any
\lambda\in[0,1]
and any quantum channel
\Lambda=\bigotimes_{l=0}^{n-1}\Lambda_{l}
consisting of a tensor product of local channels, the physical state
\rho
satisfies:

D(\lambda\Psi\otimes\outerproduct{0}{0}^{\otimes n}+(1-\lambda)\Psi^{*}\otimes\outerproduct{1}{1}^{\otimes n},\Lambda(\rho))\geq\varepsilon

(122)

where
\outerproduct{0}{0}^{\otimes n}
and
\outerproduct{1}{1}^{\otimes n}
are auxiliary indicator states. Then, the protocol outputs
FAILED
with probability at least
1-\delta
.

2.

(\varepsilon_{T},\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Net}},\delta)-
completeness
: If the input state
\rho
satisfies
D(\rho,\Psi\otimes\Phi^{+})<\varepsilon_{T}
, and the state and measurement imperfections satisfy
\epsilon_{S},\epsilon_{M}<\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Net}}
(see (
112
), (
114
) for definitions of
\epsilon_{S},\epsilon_{M}
), then the protocol outputs
CERTIFIED
with probability at least
1-\delta
.

In the completeness definition,
\varepsilon_{T}
limits the deviation of the physical state from the ideal reference state, while
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Net}}
limits the deviation of the auxiliary quantum network states as well as the measurements.
For example, if
\rho=\rho_{T}\otimes\rho_{SRB}
, then the definition requires
D(\rho_{T},\Psi)<\varepsilon_{T}
and
D(\rho_{SRB},\Phi^{+})<\min(\varepsilon_{T},\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Net}})
.

The freedom up to a global complex conjugation in this and other self-testing statements is intrinsic to relying solely on measurement statistics. Indeed, for any quantum measurement
\{M_{a|x}\}
performed on a state
\rho
, the resulting statistics are identical to those obtained by performing the complex-conjugated measurements
\{M_{a|x}^{*}\}
on the complex-conjugated state
\rho^{*}
.

G.2
Self-testing states using shared randomness

Having established a general framework for lifting device-dependent Pauli measurement protocols to their DI versions, we can now integrate our method with state certification protocols that utilize Pauli measurements to obtain a robust self-testing protocol of states.

We first consider the shadow overlap protocol introduced in Ref.
27
, which is designed to certify whether an experimental state
\rho
is close to a pure target state
\Psi
. For a given target state
\Psi
, the protocol can be viewed as the estimation of the following observable:

L_{\Psi}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum_{\mathbf{b}^{(k)}\in\{0,1\}^{n-1}}\outerproduct{\vec{b}^{(k)}}{\vec{b}^{(k)}}_{[n]\setminus\{k\}}\otimes\outerproduct{\Psi_{\vec{b}}^{(k)}}{\Psi_{\vec{b}}^{(k)}}.

(123)

Here, the unnormalized conditional post-measurement state on the
k
-th qubit, conditioned on the outcomes
\mathbf{b}^{(k)}\coloneqq(b_{0},\cdots,b_{k-1},b_{k+1},\cdots,b_{n-1})
on the other qubits, is denoted by
\ket{\tilde{\Psi}_{\vec{b}}^{(k)}}\coloneqq(\mathbb{I}_{k}\otimes\bra{\vec{b}^{(k)}})\ket{\Psi}
.
The normalized state is given by
\ket{\Psi_{\vec{b}}^{(k)}}\coloneqq\ket{\tilde{\Psi}_{\vec{b}}^{(k)}}/\sqrt{\innerproduct{\tilde{\Psi}_{\vec{b}}^{(k)}}{\tilde{\Psi}_{\vec{b}}^{(k)}}}
if
|\innerproduct{\tilde{\Psi}_{\vec{b}}^{(k)}}{\tilde{\Psi}_{\vec{b}}^{(k)}}|>0
and
0
otherwise.

The observable
L_{\Psi}
measures the average overlap between the conditional post-measurement states of the experimental state
\rho
and those of the target state
\Psi
. It is straightforward to verify that the largest eigenvalue of
L_{\Psi}
is
1
, which is achieved by the ideal target state:
\tr(L_{\Psi} \Psi)=1
.
Consequently, the effectiveness of
L_{\Psi}
for certification is determined by its spectral gap
\Delta(L_{\Psi})
, defined as the difference between its largest and second-largest eigenvalues. A larger gap implies that any state significantly different from
\Psi
will yield a lower expectation value, making errors easier to detect. Crucially, this spectral gap has been proved to be noticeable with respect to the system size for generic Haar-random states:

Fact S3

(
(
27
, Theorem 16)
)

Let
\Psi
be an
n
-qubit Haar-random state. Then, with probability at least
1-\exp(-cn)
over the choice of
\Psi
,
\Delta(L_{\Psi})\geq c^{\prime}n^{-2}
for some positive constants
c,c^{\prime}
.

Furthermore, an estimator of the expectation value
\tr(L_{\Psi} \rho)
can be obtained by performing local Pauli measurements on
\rho
. This is achieved by randomly selecting the measurement settings and post-processing the outcomes as follows:

•

Measurement Setting: Uniformly select an index
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and a Pauli operator
P\in\{X,Y,Z\}
. Set the
k
-th party’s measurement to
P^{(k)}=P
, and set
P^{(l)}=Z
for all other parties

.

•

Weighting Coefficient: Define

as the coefficient derived from local classical shadow tomography targeting the conditional state

\omega^{(k)}(\mathbf{b}|\mathbf{P})=\bra{\Psi_{\vec{b}}^{(k)}}\left(3\frac{\mathbb{I}+(-1)^{b_{k}}\sigma_{P}}{2}-\mathbb{I}\right)\ket{\Psi_{\vec{b}}^{(k)}}.

(124)

As shown in Ref.
27
, local shadow tomography ensures that this procedure yields an unbiased estimator of
\tr(L_{\Psi} \rho)
. The noticeable spectral gap
\Delta(L_{\Psi})
guaranteed by Fact
S3
ensures that the protocol is robust against infidelity for generic Haar-random states
\Psi
.

Therefore, by integrating the shadow overlap certification method into our robust self-testing framework, we obtain a practical protocol for certifying generic Haar-random states.
It is important to note that the measurement settings in this construction require coordination across different parties, and the weighting coefficients depend not only on the chosen Pauli basis
\mathbf{P}
but also on the specific party index
k
.
This coordination is accommodated by our shared randomness protocol, Protocol
E.3

k
and
\mathbf{P}
at the start of each round.
Furthermore, the weighting coefficients are strictly bounded by a constant,

G.3

The key strategy for removing shared randomness is to let each party randomly sample a local basis, and to show that the resulting observable still preserves a noticeable gap.

Appendix H
Non-i.i.d. scenario

In this section, we consider the non-i.i.d. scenario of self-testing. Previously, in the i.i.d. scenario, we assumed that the state and measurement operators are identical for every sample. In any practical experiment, this is not a realistic constraint. In this section, we allow measurements to depend on previous inputs and outputs, and to act on a quantum state that depends on previous measurements.
In Lemma
S11
, we will show that the previously introduced protocols still work in the non-i.i.d. scenario with identical asymptotic sample complexity.

In the non-i.i.d. scenario, there is no well-defined state
\rho
of the physical experiment. Rather, this state may differ in each round of the protocol. Therefore, we first need to clarify how a statement such as the claim of Theorem
S1
can be adopted to the non-i.i.d. case.
One challenge, discussed in
22
, is that the shared state
\rho^{[N]}
over
N
rounds of measurement may be a classical mixture such as
\rho^{[N]}=\frac{1}{2}(\sigma^{\otimes N}+(\tau\otimes\Phi^{+})^{\otimes N})
. Here,
\sigma
can be any
“garbage state”
for which the protocol should fail while
\tau\otimes\Phi^{+}
corresponds to the reference experiment. It appears obvious that this state should be rejected by the protocol as it is
“garbage”
one out of two times. Yet, any complete (see Sec.
F
) protocol with independent measurements over the individual rounds will accept this state with around
50\%
probability.

We resolve this issue by using an idea from
22
to define extractability with respect to the
accessed states
, that is, the states
\rho_{|m_{<i}}
in a given round
i
of the protocol, conditional on all previous inputs and outputs
m_{<i}
.
In
22
, the
average extractability
is defined by the average achievable fidelity between a target state and the accessed states
\rho_{|m_{<i}},\ i=1,\cdots,N
after applying a local extraction channel.

We consider an equivalent definition of average extractability: extractability with respect to an average state, which has a clear operational meaning. First, we need to introduce some additional notation:
We write the
accessed measurement
in round
i
as
M_{|m_{<i}}
(where the POVM elements
\left(\bigotimes_{l=0}^{n-1}M_{a_{l}|x_{l}}^{(l)}\otimes N_{b_{l}|y_{l}}^{(l)}\right)_{|m_{<i}}
of the accessed measurement
M_{|m_{<i}}
in round
i
may also depend on outcomes in the previous rounds).
Define the
average state

\bar{\rho}
as the labeled, classical mixture of the accessed states in individual rounds
i
, i.e.
\bar{\rho}\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\outerproduct{i}{i}^{\otimes n}\otimes\rho_{|m_{<i}}
.
We then call the following experiment an
average experiment
: On the average state
\bar{\rho}
, each party first measures the round label
i
and then proceeds to apply operations (which may depend on the round label
i
) to the remaining state
\rho_{|m_{<i}}
.

Because the average state is the uniform mixture of
\outerproduct{i}{i}^{\otimes n}\otimes\rho_{|m_{<i}}
, an average experiment is operationally equivalent to randomly choosing a round label
i
, stopping before round
i
and then performing an operation on the current state
\rho_{|m_{<i}}
. An example of such an operation would be measuring
M_{|m_{<i}}
in the random round to obtain a result
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})
. This average experiment corresponds to the (local) measurements

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\outerproduct{i}{i}^{\otimes n}\otimes M_{|m_{<i}}

(145)

on the average state
\bar{\rho}
.

An extractor
\Lambda
on the average state corresponds to a sequence of extractors
\Lambda^{(i)}=\bigotimes_{l=0}^{n-1}\Lambda_{l}^{(i)}
,
\Lambda_{l}^{(i)}:\rho\mapsto\Lambda_{l}(\outerproduct{i}{i}\otimes\rho)
, applied on each round of the physical experiment. Conversely, a sequence of extractors
\Lambda^{(i)}=\bigotimes_{l=0}^{n-1}\Lambda^{(i)}_{l}
applied in the
N
rounds of the physical experiment can be combined into one extractor

\Lambda=\bigotimes_{l=0}^{n-1}\Lambda_{l};\qquad\Lambda_{l}:\rho\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\bra{i}\otimes\mathbb{I})(\mathcal{I}\otimes\Lambda_{l}^{(i)})(\rho)(\ket{i}\otimes\mathbb{I}).

(146)

These two types of extractors are equivalent in the following sense:

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Lambda^{(i)}(\rho_{|m<i})=\Lambda\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\outerproduct{i}{i}^{\otimes n}\otimes\rho_{|m<i}\right)=\Lambda(\bar{\rho}).

(147)

That is, the state extracted by randomly picking a round and then extracting with respect to that round can be implemented as extracting from the average state, and vice versa. This explains why the extractability notion with respect to the average state is equivalent to the average extractability in
22
: the average fidelity between any state
\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}
and the state
\Lambda_{i}(\rho_{|m<i})
extracted from some round, is equal to the fidelity between
\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}
and the state
\Lambda(\bar{\rho})
extracted from the average state.

We are now in a position to define average extractability: We define the
average extractability
as the extractability with respect to the average state
\bar{\rho}
. Here, by extractability, we mean that there exists an extraction channel
\Lambda
that is the product of local channels, such that
\Lambda(\bar{\rho})
extracts a target state. From our preceding discussion, it becomes clear that extractability with respect to the average state
\bar{\rho}
is equivalent to average extractability in an average experiment and vice versa.

We define the values
v_{\mathrm{I}}^{[i]},v_{\mathrm{II}}^{[i]},v_{\mathrm{III}}^{[i]}
as the values of the estimators in Box
D.4
(we suppress the dependence on
l
and
k
for simplicity of notation) in round
i
with respect to the state
\rho_{|m_{<i}}
and with measurements
M_{|m_{<i}}
. Let
v_{\mathrm{I}},v_{\mathrm{II}},v_{\mathrm{III}}
be the uniform averages of
v_{\mathrm{I}}^{[i]},v_{\mathrm{II}}^{[i]},v_{\mathrm{III}}^{[i]}
over
i
. By the definition of the average experiment,
v_{\mathrm{I}},v_{\mathrm{II}},v_{\mathrm{III}}
are exactly the values of the estimators in Box
D.4
applied in an average experiment with the states
\rho_{|m_{<i}}
and measurements
M_{|m_{<i}}
(see discussion above (
145
)).

Our main result of this section is Theorem
S7
, which is a direct consequence of Lemma
S11
. Lemma
S11
proves that the experimental estimates
\hat{v}_{\mathrm{I}},\hat{v}_{\mathrm{II}},\hat{v}_{\mathrm{III}}
are close to the expectation values
v_{\mathrm{I}},v_{\mathrm{II}},v_{\mathrm{III}}
of the average experiment. Using Theorem
S7
together with the operator
M_{\Psi}
from (
136
), we verify that if our protocol outputs
CERTIFIED
, then we can (approximately) extract the target state from the average state.

Lemma S11

(Non-i.i.d. estimators)
.

In the non-i.i.d. case, the estimators
\hat{v}\in\{\hat{v}_{\mathrm{I}},\hat{v}_{\mathrm{II}},\hat{v}_{\mathrm{III}}\}
in Protocol
E.2
(Protocol
E.3
) are close to the respective expectation values
v\in\{v_{\mathrm{I}},v_{\mathrm{II}},v_{\mathrm{III}}\}
with respect to the average experiment. Specifically, they satisfy

|\hat{v}-v|=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)

(148)

with failure probability at most
\delta
and same sample complexity
N(W,\varepsilon,\delta)
as in Theorem
S2
(Theorem
S3
).

Theorem S7

(Non-i.i.d. finite sample version of Theorem
S1
)
.

In the non-i.i.d. case, with probability at least
1-\delta
, Protocol
E.2
(Protocol
E.3
) with same asymptotic sample complexity
N
as Theorem
S2
(Theorem
S3
) either returns
FAILED
, or for the average state
\bar{\rho}
, there exist
p\in[0,1]
, a state
\tau=p\tau_{+}+(1-p)\tau_{-}
, and a product of local channels
\Gamma
such that:

D\left(p\tau_{+}\otimes\outerproduct{0}{0}^{\otimes n}+(1-p)\tau_{-}^{*}\otimes\outerproduct{1}{1}^{\otimes n},\Gamma(\bar{\rho})\right)\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{W}\right),

(149)

and the value
\hat{v}_{\mathrm{III}}
returned by the protocol satisfies

|\hat{v}_{\mathrm{III}}-\tr(L\tau)|=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon).

(150)

For the interpretation of our results in this section, it may be useful to note that these results can be extended beyond the operational meaning as an average statement to statements about a large fraction of rounds. Concretely, by utilizing the non-negativity of the estimators
v_{\mathrm{I}}^{[i]},v_{\mathrm{II}}^{[i]}
and the Markov inequality, small values of
v_{\mathrm{I}},v_{\mathrm{II}}
(which follow from small observed estimates
\hat{v}_{\mathrm{I}},\hat{v}_{\mathrm{II}}
by Lemma
S11
) imply a small value for
v_{\mathrm{I}}^{[i]},v_{\mathrm{II}}^{[i]}
in most rounds
i
. Theorem
S1
, (
62
) then implies that for most rounds, the single round expectation
v_{\mathrm{III}}^{[i]}
provides a good estimate for the expectation value of
L
on the state present in that round. We will not formalize this further here and now move on to the proofs of Lemma
S11
and Theorem
S7
.

To prove Lemma
S11
, we need the following lemma, which is a mixed version of Chernoff bound and Azuma’s inequality (martingale version of Hoeffding’s inequality).

Lemma S12

(A mixed version of Chernoff bound and Azuma’s inequality)
.

Let
\epsilon>0,0<p\leq 1
. Let
m_{1},\cdots,m_{N}
be random variables. Let
\hat{\delta}_{i},\hat{v}_{i}
be functions of
m_{1},\cdots,m_{i}
;
|\hat{v}_{i}|\leq 1
and
\hat{\delta}_{i}\in\{0,1\}
. Assume

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\hat{\delta}_{i}\mid m_{<i}]=p;\quad\mathbb{E}[\hat{v}_{i}\mid m_{<i},\hat{\delta}_{i}=1]=v_{i},

(151)

where
v_{i}
is a function of
m_{<i}
. Then,

\displaystyle\Pr[\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\delta}_{i}\hat{v}_{i}}{pN}-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_{i}}{N}\geq\epsilon]\leq\exp(-\frac{\epsilon^2}{2+\epsilon}pN).

(152)

Proof.

We slightly modify the proof of the Chernoff bound. Let
\lambda>0
be a to-be-determined constant. By Markov’s inequality and the law of iterated expectations,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Pr[\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\delta}_{i}\hat{v}_{i}}{pN}-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_{i}}{N}\geq\epsilon]&=\Pr[\exp(\lambda\sum_{i=1}^N(\hat\delta_i\hat v_i-pv_i-p\epsilon))\geq 1]\\
&\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(\lambda\sum_{i=1}^N(\hat\delta_i\hat v_i-pv_i-p\epsilon))\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{N}\exp(\lambda(\hat\delta_i\hat v_i-pv_i-p\epsilon))\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(\lambda(\hat\delta_1\hat v_1-pv_1-p\epsilon))\right.\\
&\quad\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(\lambda\hat\delta_2\hat v_2-pv_2-p\epsilon)\right.\\
&\quad\quad\left.\left.\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(\lambda(\hat\delta_3\hat v_3-pv_3-p\epsilon))\cdot\cdots\mid m_{1},m_{2}\right]\mid m_{1}\right]\right].\end{split}

(153)

Now we bound
\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(\lambda(\hat\delta_i\hat v_i-pv_i-p\epsilon))\mid m_{<i}\right]
. By assumption,
v_{i}
is determined given
m_{<i}
. By (
151
),

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(\lambda(\hat\delta_i\hat v_i-pv_i-p\epsilon))\mid m_{<i}\right]\\
=&\exp(-\lambda p(v_i+\epsilon))\left(\Pr[\hat{\delta}_{i}=1\mid m_{<i}]\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(\lambda\hat v_i)\mid m_{<i},\hat{\delta}_{i}=1\right]+\Pr[\hat{\delta}_{i}=0\mid m_{<i}]\right)\\
=&\exp(-\lambda p(v_i+\epsilon))\left(p\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(\lambda\hat v_i)\mid m_{<i},\hat{\delta}_{i}=1\right]+(1-p)\right)\\
\leq&\exp(-\lambda p(v_i+\epsilon))\exp(p\left(\bE\left[\exp(\lambda\hat v_i)\mid m_{<i},\hat\delta_i=1\right]-1\right))\\
=&\exp(p\bE\left[\exp(\lambda\hat v_i)-1-\lambda(\hat v_i+\epsilon)\mid m_{<i},\hat\delta_i=1\right]).\end{split}

(154)

Here we used
1+x\leq\exp(x)
for any real
x
.
Since
\exp(\lambda\hat v_i)-1-\lambda(\hat{v}_{i}+\epsilon)
is convex with respect to
\hat{v}_{i}
, and
v_{i}\in[-1,1]
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(\lambda(\hat\delta_i\hat v_i-pv_i-p\epsilon))\mid m_{<i}\right]\\
\leq&\exp(p\bE\left[\exp(\lambda\hat v_i)-1-\lambda(\hat v_i+\epsilon)\mid m_{<i},\hat\delta_i=1\right])\\
\leq&\exp(p\max(\exp(\lambda)-1-\lambda(1+\epsilon),\exp(-\lambda)-1-\lambda(-1+\epsilon))).\end{split}

(155)

Set
\lambda
to be
\ln(1+\epsilon)
. By utilizing the fact that
\exp(x)-x>\exp(-x)+x
for
x>0
, and
\ln(\frac{1+x}{1-x})>2x
for
0<x<1
(which implies
\log(x)>2\frac{x-1}{x+1}
for
x>1
), we know

\displaystyle\exp(-\lambda)-1-\lambda(-1+\epsilon)<\exp(\lambda)-1-\lambda(1+\epsilon)=\epsilon-(1+\epsilon)\ln(1+\epsilon)<\epsilon-2(1+\epsilon)\frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon+2}=-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2+\epsilon}.

(156)

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(\lambda(\hat\delta_i\hat v_i-pv_i-p\epsilon))\mid m_{<i}\right]\leq\exp(p\max(\exp(\lambda)-1-\lambda(1+\epsilon),\exp(-\lambda)-1-\lambda(-1+\epsilon)))\leq\exp(-\frac{p\epsilon^2}{2+\epsilon}).

(157)

Together with (
153
), we get (
152
).
∎

Proof of Lemma
S11
.

Consider any statistics of one round
i
. Let
p_{\mathrm{I}},p_{\mathrm{II}},p_{\mathrm{III}}
denote the probability that these statistics contribute to estimator
v_{\mathrm{I}},v_{\mathrm{II}},v_{\mathrm{III}}
. The following analysis holds for any estimator, for simplicity we denote
p\in\{p_{\mathrm{I}},p_{\mathrm{II}},p_{\mathrm{III}}\}
and
v_{i}\in\{v_{\mathrm{I}}^{[i]}(l,k),v_{\mathrm{II}}^{[i]}(l,l+1),v_{\mathrm{III}}^{[i]}\}
.
Let
\hat{\delta}_{i}
be a binary variable indicating whether
v_{i}
is estimated in the
i
-th round of the actual protocol, and let
\hat{v}_{i}
denote the (single sample) estimate for
v_{i}
from that round. Assume that
v\in\{v_{\mathrm{I}},v_{\mathrm{II}},v_{\mathrm{III}}\}
is bounded by
W
. The estimate of
v
is

\displaystyle\hat{v}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\delta}_{i}\hat{v}_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\delta}_{i}}.

(158)

On the other hand
v
is equal to the average value of
v_{i}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle v=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_{i}.

(159)

We now show that the probability of “
v
and
\hat{v}
are close” has the same bound as in the i.i.d. case.

We define another estimate

\displaystyle\hat{v}^{\prime}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\delta}_{i}\hat{v}_{i}}{pN}.

(160)

We will prove below that
v
and
\hat{v}^{\prime}
are close with high probability.
Subsequently, we apply Lemma
S12
to
\hat{v}^{\prime}
in order to show that
\hat{v}^{\prime}
approximates
v
with high probability: Setting
\hat{v}_{i},v_{i}
in Lemma
S12
to be
\pm\hat{v}_{i}/W,\pm v_{i}/W
, we get

\displaystyle\Pr[\hat{v}^{\prime}-v>\epsilon/2]<\exp(-\frac{(\epsilon/2W)^2}{2+\epsilon/2W}pN);\qquad\Pr[\hat{v}^{\prime}-v<-\epsilon/2]<\exp(-\frac{(\epsilon/2W)^2}{2+\epsilon/2W}pN).

(161)

Hence, with probability at least
1-2\exp(-pN(\epsilon/2W)^2/(2+\epsilon/2W))
, we have
\Pr[|\hat{v}^{\prime}-v|\leq\epsilon/2]
.

Now, we prove that
\hat{v}
and
\hat{v}^{\prime}
are close with high probability. Since
\{\hat{\delta}_{i}\}_{i}
are independent binary random variables with expectation value
p
, we can apply a Chernoff bound for Bernoulli random variables to get

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\left|\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\delta}_{i}}{pN}-1\right|<\epsilon/2W\right]\geq 1-2\exp\left(-\frac{pN(\epsilon/2W)^{2}}{2+\epsilon/2W}\right).

(162)

Hence, with probability at least
1-2\exp(-pN(\epsilon/2W)^2/(2+\epsilon/2W))
,

\displaystyle|\hat{v}^{\prime}-\hat{v}|=\left|\left(\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\delta}_{i}}{pN}-1\right)\cdot\hat{v}\right|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2W}\cdot W=\epsilon/2.

(163)

Therefore, by union bound, with probability at least
1-4\exp(-pN(\epsilon/2W)^2/(2+\epsilon/2W))
,
|\hat{v}-v|\leq|\hat{v}^{\prime}-v|+|\hat{v}^{\prime}-\hat{v}|<\epsilon/2+\epsilon/2=\epsilon
.
In order to lower bound the probability to
1-\delta
, we get
N=\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{p}\frac{W^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\log(\frac{1}{\delta}))
, a bound similar to the i.i.d. scenario (see Appendix
E
). Hence, the sample complexity analysis can proceed analogously to the i.i.d. case. This shows (
148
).
∎

S7

The result follows from (
148
), Theorem
S1
applied to the average experiments that have expectation values
v_{\mathrm{I}},v_{\mathrm{II}}
and
v_{\mathrm{III}}
, and an identical derivation to Theorem
S2
(Theorem
S3
