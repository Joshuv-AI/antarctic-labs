WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study introduces and implements a practical, simulation-based method for quantitatively estimating LLM-based software as a medical device (LLM-SaMD) risk within existing regulatory frameworks. By translating model errors into approximations of how frequently specific hazards are expected to become hazardous situations and harms, our framework converts the abstract question of ‘LLM safety’ into estimated quantities that can be interpreted alongside existing regulatory risk management frameworks and current SaMD guidance on computational modelling and artificial intelligence and machine learning (AI/ML) change control.

Background

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly integrated into healthcare contexts. However, their probabilistic outputs have already been associated with a spectrum of harms, ranging from psychological distress to, in some cases, death by suicide.13 These events, often occurring outside formal clinical use, reveal how unregulated LLM interactions can generate patient safety concerns and underscore the need for a systematic approach for addressing these risks. To adapt existing safety paradigms to LLM-based software as a medical device (LLM-SaMD), formal regulatory concepts must first be defined. A hazard is any potential source of harm; a hazardous situation arises when a series of events expose a patient to that hazard. Harm is the resulting negative outcome from the hazardous situation, and risk reflects both the probability and severity of that harm.4 Uncertainty describes the limits of knowledge surrounding each of these concepts, and the paths leading from hazard to harm. Because uncertainty cannot be eliminated, the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) requires a ‘reasonable assurance’ of safety and effectiveness, rather than an absolute guarantee.5 Notably, this linear hazard-to-harm sequence functions as a tractable starting point and regulatory abstraction, rather than a complete causal network model. Clinical harm, especially in mental illness, is frequently cumulative, non-deterministic and mediated by multiple, potentially time-dependent contextual factors (eg, vulnerability, resilience, social supports) that extend beyond linear event chains (online supplemental figure S1).

Official FDA SaMD guidance and associated literature operationalise this model into structured processes for medical device oversight (figure 1A).468 The EU’s Medical Device Regulation (MDR) and the UK’s Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) provide similar risk management, clinical evaluation and lifecycle oversight frameworks for software-based medical devices.9 10 Manufacturers must identify where and how harm might arise, estimate its likelihood and severity, propose risk control methods to mitigate those harms and ensure that benefits of use outweigh residual risks (figure 1B). Because the risks of software failures are often difficult to quantify, manufacturers may be required to assume worst-case probabilities for a given event until they are able to provide evidence to bound the uncertainty of their estimate.4 This process extends across the product life cycle, from design to post-market surveillance, with manufacturers accountable for emerging risks in new settings, populations, or model updates.11 Recent FDA guidance acknowledges that AI-enabled SaMDs present challenges that strain traditional risk modelling, including model updates after initial deployment.11 12 Unlike previous devices, AI systems can cause harm in ways that are less predictable or visible. They may generate outputs that are unclear or incomplete, propagate bias through their training data, or foster automation bias when users place undue trust in their responses. Their performance can also drift over time as data distributions change, and they can be intentionally manipulated through adversarial attacks.7 In response, regulators now emphasise stronger lifecycle controls, including robust and representative data management, transparent model documentation, continuous validation and monitoring and enhanced cybersecurity safeguards.4 6 7 11

Even with these AI-tailored updates, applying this risk model to LLM-based systems is not straightforward. Manufacturers must consider informational failures such as toxic outputs, omissions or disclosure errors; functional failures such as ineffective or omitted treatment; systemic failures that alter clinical behaviour and workflow; and interpretive failures in which otherwise accurate outputs are misunderstood. Such errors may occur within patient–device, clinician–device or clinician–patient–device interactions.21316 The evolution of hazards from LLM-SaMDs into actual harms may also depend on baseline patient vulnerability, clinical oversight and patterns of user engagement. The same model output may result in vastly different clinical consequences depending on these and other factors. Consequently, risk extends beyond technical malfunction to encompass informational, functional, systemic and interpretive domains—each introducing uncertainty that is difficult to quantify and bound. Reducing this general theory of risk to specific, measurable use cases presents a substantial practical challenge for regulators and manufacturers alike.

Thus, a central challenge for regulators in evaluating LLM-SaMDs is estimating and bounding the uncertainty inherent to their risk profile. Because these systems are probabilistic by design, uncertainty permeates every layer of risk assessment. The range of potential hazards is effectively infinite; the event sequences linking hazards, hazardous situations and harms are complex and context-dependent; and outcome severity can vary from minor inconvenience to death (online supplemental figures S2 and S3). Yet these same characteristics make the problem of quantifying risk uniquely suited to computational simulation. If developers can generate models that reliably simulate how specific prompts, user behaviours or deployment conditions affect the probabilities of a hazard progressing to a hazardous situation (P1) and of that situation resulting in harm (P2), they can produce usable estimates rather than rely on worst-case assumptions. The FDA’s 2018 and 2023 guidance on computational modelling and simulation (CM&S) already provides a risk-informed framework for credibility assessment, validation and uncertainty analysis, demonstrating that simulated data can meaningfully complement or, in some cases, replace clinical evidence when properly validated.17 18 Although this framework is presented in the context of FDA guidance, it is grounded in ISO 14971 risk management principles. These principles underpin regulatory frameworks across jurisdictions, including the EU MDR, and may, therefore, be adaptable to non-US regulatory contexts.

Objective

In this study, we demonstrate how simulation can extend existing FDA regulatory guidance to help address the unique hazards, hazardous situations and harms posed by LLM-SaMDs (figure 1, online supplemental figure S2) with a specific, illustrative example. Using LLM-generated and clinician-filtered simulated chatbot–user interactions involving a potentially suicidal user, we outline a quantitative framework for estimating the probability of harm across varied user statement types, affective states and conversation topics ranging from neutral to emotionally charged or clinically oriented exchanges (figure 1B and online supplemental figure S4). Although assessed on a single use case, the framework is designed to generalise to other LLM-SaMD applications, including documentation, decision support, diagnostics and therapeutics (online supplemental figures S2 and S3). Such simulation-based methods may help manufacturers and regulators evaluate LLMs at scale. The scenarios analysed here are intentionally simplified and serve as illustrative test cases, not assessments of deployable SaMD systems or substitutes for full regulatory development processes.

Methods

Dataset development and curation

Gemini V.2.5 Pro (Gemini) was used to generate synthetic data to estimate models’ performance on proxy safety-classification tasks and to simulate chatbot and user statements for an AI safety research project focused on the safe deployment of LLMs in a healthcare setting (additional details in online supplemental methods). Preliminary inspection indicated that LLM-generated content was less reliable for clinically high-risk categories. Accordingly, all generated statements and dialogues were reviewed by clinicians prior to inclusion. A psychiatrist manually reviewed each output statement and either (1) approved the statement as written, (2) made minor modifications to bring the output in-line with the goal category or (3) removed the statement from the dataset. A second psychiatrist subsequently approved, modified or rejected the subset of sentences approved or modified by the first psychiatrist. Prior to running the computational simulations, each category was down-sampled to not exceed the category with the fewest approved statements (online supplemental figure S5 and tables S1–S3). In this illustrative implementation, we focus on informational failure modes (and defer addressing interpretive failures arising from human–AI interactions) to demonstrate the mechanics of simulation-based risk estimation. These proxy safety classification tasks are designed to capture only specific informational failure modes relevant to this illustrative case and are not intended to serve as general surrogates for operational safety performance in deployed systems.

Model evaluation

Fourteen LLMs across three open-source families (Qwen, Gemma-3 and LLaMA-3) were evaluated. All models were run with fixed decoding parameters (temperature=0.0, max_tokens=256, top_p=1.0) to ensure deterministic outputs. Prompts were designed for the three classification tasks described above; uncertainty in classification performance was quantified using 95% CIs, selected based on the metric structure (see online supplemental methods). For models achieving perfect sensitivity (zero false negatives), we used the median of the Beta(1, n) binomial distribution as a conservative floor for the true false-negative rate.

Findings

Gemini-generated synthetic data

Gemini V.2.5 Pro showed variable success in generating statements that matched target affective tone and clinical content (figure 2A–C). Non-suicidal statements were generally accurate (481/500 retained), though performance declined for sad, ambiguous and angry tones. In contrast, suicidal ideation (SI) content had significantly lower retention (315/500, p<0.001), with the greatest degradation in statements describing active intent and preparatory behaviour (figure 2A, online supplemental table S1). For therapy-request detection, 768/800 non-therapeutic statements were retained, while explicit therapy requests performed significantly worse (340/400, p<0.001), particularly for positive-affect language (figure 2B, online supplemental table S2). Therapy-engagement dialogues showed the greatest variability. Nearly all non-therapeutic and ambiguous conversations were retained, but fewer than half of simulated therapeutic exchanges met criteria without modification (figure 2C, online supplemental table S3, p<0.001). Performance differed by therapeutic approach. Cognitive Behavioral Therapy and Dialectical Behavioral Therapy (CBT/DBT)-based dialogues were largely accurate, whereas those involving psychodynamic content, diagnostic clarification or medications frequently broke the requested therapeutic frame and required revision or removal. Notably, failure rates were highest in clinically high-risk categories, including active suicidal ideation and therapy-like interactions, underscoring both the necessity of expert clinician adjudication for synthetic data and its limitations of reduced fidelity precisely in the regions most consequential for downstream risk estimation.

Cross-model performance at prediction tasks

We compared classification performance across the Gemma, Qwen and LLaMA families in correctly predicting the class for each of the statement groupings above. Lower parameter models within the Gemma and LLaMA families struggled to generate responses that conformed to the required output structure, but performance across all metrics (sensitivity, specificity, accuracy and F1 score) generally improved with model size, though this relationship varied substantially by task and model family (figure 3, online supplemental figures S6–S8).

We next examined which statements were repeatedly incorrectly classified within each task. For SI detection, 19 statements (4.2%) were missed by >50% of the 14 models: 7 active SI with plan and preparation, 4 active SI with plan but no intent, 3 passive SI and 5 ambiguous emotional statements, consistent with difficulty with subtle planning language and conditional expressions (online supplemental figure S9). Missed detection of suicidal ideation, regardless of clear intent, is clinically concerning, as ambivalence in the presence of planning is a common and high-risk presentation. For therapy request classification, four statements (0.5%) were missed by >50% of models: three declarative statements with sad affect and one non-therapeutic question with sad affect, suggesting difficulty with affect-containing declarative statements that do not explicitly request help, particularly those expressing sadness (online supplemental figure S10). For therapy engagement classification, seven conversations (1.7%) were missed by >50% of models: four with clear engagement and three with ambiguous engagement, indicating a systematic difficulty in distinguishing genuine therapeutic engagement from ambiguous boundary cases (online supplemental figure S11).

Estimating P1 and P2

Estimates of P1 ranged from 1.1×10⁻⁸ to 1.6×10⁻⁴ at a 1% baseline prevalence of suicidal ideation, spanning four orders of magnitude and corresponding to 0.011 to 161 hazardous situations per million individual user–model exchanges. Uncertainty increased as estimated risk decreased: models with the lowest P1 point estimates exhibited large uncertainty intervals relative to the magnitude of those estimates, reflecting limited statistical resolution to estimate very low false-negative rates, and subsequent risk, from finite test sets (figure 4). Both model choice and baseline prevalence assumptions contributed substantially to overall variability. Within the Gemma family alone, P1 spanned 3.3 orders of magnitude, while varying baseline suicidal ideation prevalence from 0.1% to 10% contributed an additional two orders of magnitude through linear scaling. This analysis focuses on sensitivity-driven risk and does not account for the operational costs of false positives, which may degrade real-world safety performance through alert fatigue.

Estimates of P2 ranged from 4.9×10⁻⁵ to 5.1×10⁻³ at a 1% baseline probability that lack of professional care results in harm, spanning approximately two orders of magnitude and corresponding to 49 to ~5100 harms per million individual user–model four-turn exchanges. As with P1, uncertainty was greatest for models with the lowest estimated risk, reflecting limited statistical resolution when characterising rare downstream failures from modestly sized evaluation sets. Uncertainty intervals overlapped across several high-performing models, limiting the ability to distinguish their relative downstream risk contributions based on point estimates alone. To examine the impact of correlated detection failures, we varied the failure-multiplier parameter from independence (m=1) to strong conditional dependence (m=100 000), spanning a range sufficient to drive the adjusted false-negative rate towards 1 across all baseline false negative rate (FNR) values (online supplemental figure S12). Under independence, P2 ranged from 4.9×10⁻⁵ to 5.1×10⁻³ across models (as above), spanning 2.0 orders of magnitude. Under strong dependence, the adjusted therapy-engagement false-negative term approaches 1 for all models, causing P2 to converge to 1.0×10⁻² (online supplemental figure S13). This result highlights that, for models with correlated failure modes, individual classifier performance has limited impact on overall risk in a system with strongly correlated failure modes. If such dependence exists in deployed systems, optimising individual classifiers may improve task-specific metrics without materially reducing overall risk. In that case, the relevant target for intervention may be the shared source of correlated failure rather than any single classifier.

Discussion

Although existing medical-device regulations were not designed for probabilistic systems like LLMs, our findings show that they can be extended to address LLM-specific risks through computational simulation. Specifically, we show how proxy safety task simulations, combined with behavioural assumptions and uncertainty propagation, can be integrated to estimate the probability of a hazard transforming into a hazardous situation (P1), and subsequently transitioning to harm (P2). These analyses reveal how model choice, assumptions and failure dependence materially shape estimated risk, clarifying where uncertainty remains high and where additional real-world data would be most informative. Rather than replacing current frameworks,46 7 1821 simulation operates within established FDA and international regulatory processes to define, estimate and bound the uncertainty for LLM-based software as a medical device (LLM-SaMD)—the core challenge for their safe deployment to the clinic.

Our approach offers several advantages for stakeholders. For regulators, LLMs challenge the expectation of stable, quantifiable probabilities that underlie international medical-device standards. Simulation reduces this dependence by generating preliminary estimates of the probability that a given hazard progresses to a hazardous situation (P1) and the probability that this situation results in harm (P2). It can serve as a premarket complement to postmarket surveillance, likely aligning with existing computational modelling and simulation (CM&S) and predetermined change control plan (PCCP) pathways, to assist regulators in their final determinations.11 17 This is conceptually similar to ‘shadow mode’ parallel deployment, in which updated models are run alongside existing systems without affecting real-world decisions.22 Simulation extends this idea by enabling prospective evaluation across arbitrarily many scenarios without requiring real-world exposure. For manufacturers, simulation clarifies expectations and accountability: it provides quantitative estimates to scope risk and defines what constitutes reasonable effort to constrain the vast space of possible harm pathways.2 4 6 7 17 23 It also enables them to more efficiently estimate the impact of mitigation strategies, such as using retrieval-augmented-generation systems to reduce hallucination by grounding outputs in retrieved external text rather than free generation. For clinicians and patients, it makes device limitations explicit and supports contextual safeguards, such as human oversight, monitoring and escalation protocols, that are part of shared responsibility for safety. Importantly, this approach does not diminish manufacturers’ responsibility for safety, nor does it substitute for clinical evidence where such evidence is required. Additionally, our approach helps reframe the public conversation about LLM safety from high-profile, anecdotal harms1313 to traceable mechanisms causally linking hazard, hazardous situations and harm and the mitigation factors that can be deployed for preventing their progression.

Safety evaluations of LLMs are not static, and the model proposed here can offer lifecycle support from predeployment to postmarket monitoring. Just as in pharmacovigilance, where initial uncertainty about a given medication is narrowed by ongoing evidence, the ability to continually collect and interpret the risk profile of LLMs can lead to safer medical devices. Unlike molecular therapeutics; however, LLM-based systems are dynamic and non-deterministic, with behaviour that may change through updates, model drift or deployment context. This requires safety monitoring on substantially shorter timescales than traditional pharmacovigilance.112426 In this case, simulation can alert regulatory and manufacturers when uncertainty is too great or the model is too unstable, so as to require real-world data to be collected to inform next steps.

As real-world data become available (eg, records of deployed human–LLM interactions such as chatbot transcripts, clinician documentation or LLM-generated report assessments), this framework can be iteratively calibrated. This includes updating model assumptions, tightening uncertainty bounds on performance and behavioural parameters, and validating simulation predictions against observed outcomes. Even sparse real-world examples such as high-severity of harm cases can be used to constrain and recalibrate simulation parameters and to seed targeted synthetic data generation. This enables systematic stress-testing across clinically critical regions of the input space, analogous to in silico modelling approaches used for medical devices and scenario-based virtual validation in other safety-critical domains.172729 Consistent with ISO 14971’s emphasis on use environment and human factors, deployment context (eg, patient-facing vs clinician-facing use, level of supervision and automation reliance) can be incorporated into this framework as additional probabilistic parameters. These may include clinician over-ride, escalation or disengagement rates that may modulate downstream risk (P2) rather than being treated as fixed exogenous conditions. This closed loop integration supports lifecycle safety monitoring rather than a one-time static risk estimate.

This work offers a preliminary framework for quantifying the potential harms of LLM-SaMD, but it remains an early step towards operationalising the totality of our proposed approach. Limitations include the conceptual nature of this work in that demonstrating how existing regulatory principles around hazards and harms can be extended to LLMs does not by itself establish validated safety thresholds, which remain at the discretion of regulators. Simulation-based estimates depend on assumptions about models, human behaviour and illness that are not yet well characterised. These assumptions must be validated through diverse real-world data streams (user-chatbot logs, clinical outcomes, ecological momentary assessments, etc). Even small alterations to those parameters or updates to an LLM may change outcomes. Additionally, although two psychiatrists reviewed and approved all simulated statements, the use of an LLM to generate synthetic user dialogue, rather than drawing from real-world patient interactions, introduces a ‘sim-to-real’ gap. This gap is a reality for the current state of the art, however. The largest published real-world dataset of LLM harms contains 19 patients, highlighting the need for real-world data collection, which this paper can help motivate.30

Other limitations also relate to risk modelling with simulated data. LLM-generated language may over-represent prototypical presentations while failing to accurately recapitulate true patient speech patterns. This includes overlap between suicidal language and expressions seen in other psychiatric conditions (eg, depression, borderline personality disorder or trauma-related disorders), which may blur category boundaries and is not fully captured in the synthetic dataset. This limitation is further amplified by the use of binary classification rather than continuous estimates of clinical risk. Similarly, LLM-generated content may share distributional, stylistic or structural regularities present in model-generated language and training corpora that other LLMs recognise (rather than clinical information). Both of these confounding factors could artificially inflate performance estimates. In particular, if synthetic generation under-represents highly ambiguous, disorganised or high-severity suicidal language, or if clinician editing preferentially removes the most chaotic conversational patterns, model sensitivity may be overestimated in precisely the regions of the input space most relevant for downstream risk. Such biases would propagate through the simulation, leading P1 and P2 to underestimate risk under real-world conditions characterised by greater linguistic variability and clinical complexity. Accordingly, these estimates should be interpreted as potentially optimistic bounds on uncertainty and risk, but never as calibrated, fixed rates of harm. Estimating the aggregate magnitude of these biases and their effect on actual operational risk will require real-world validation data.

No modelling can fully account for human factors or human-in-the-loop effects (eg, parasocial attachment, automation bias or clinical supervision) that may amplify or attenuate harm in real-world deployment. In mental health applications, LLM-based systems may also elicit simulated intimacy, such that failures of affective attunement or repair can produce psychological harm—including distress or loss of trust—even in the absence of factually incorrect outputs. These human factors will likely substantially influence how hazards progress to harm. The framework presupposes conditions that are not guaranteed when using proprietary or rapidly evolving systems. Broader implementation will likely require legislation to mandate the necessary models and data be shared with appropriate regulatory bodies. As our evaluation was limited to English-language content, it may not generalise to other languages or cultural contexts. Finally, we have not yet simulated the effect of any risk control measures on our probability estimates. Conceptually, this would involve re-estimating P1 and P2 before and after implementation of a given control to quantify its impact on risk.

The framework presented herein demonstrates how LLM-SaMD safety can be evaluated within existing regulatory structures. Estimating uncertainty through scope restriction, layered safeguards, computational simulation and real-world data, as illustrated in this work, offers regulators and developers a clear, generalisable path to robustly and cost-effectively assess the benefit–risk profile of LLM-SaMDs while safeguarding patients from preventable harm. Critically, these limitations highlight the urgent need for robust real-world data to validate, calibrate and complement simulation-based estimates of LLM-SaMD risk.
