Voice as a predictive signal: protocol for a scoping review of AI in early childhood development
Authors: Mehrdad Amir-Behghadami, Seifollah Heidarabadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEarly childhood development (ECD) interventions support children aged 0–5 years, including those typically developing, at risk of delays or diagnosed with motor, cognitive, language or social-emotional disorders. Current assessments face barriers like limited access, high costs, intermittent evaluations and invasive methods. Voice offers a non-invasive digital biomarker, with artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) enabling analysis of vocal features linked to developmental trajectories. This scoping review protocol synthesises evidence on AI-driven voice biomarkers for early detection, monitoring and management of ECD outcomes in healthy, at-risk or impaired children under five.Methods and analysisThis scoping review protocol adheres to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews guidelines and the Arksey and O’Malley framework, enhanced by Joanna Briggs Institute recommendations. Eligibility criteria follow the Population, Concept and Context framework: population (children 0–5 years), concept (AI/ML voice biomarker analysis), context (early detection, monitoring, management of developmental outcomes). Comprehensive searches target PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, Embase, IEEE Xplore, CINAHL and grey literature sources for peer-reviewed English/Persian articles from January 2015 onwards. Two independent reviewers screen titles/abstracts/full texts and extract data on clinical applications, recording protocols, acoustic features, AI models, demographics and implementation factors. Discrepancies were resolved via discussion or a third reviewer. Results were presented narratively with tables, charts and figures addressing research questions on voice as a predictive signal.Ethics and disseminationThe Research Ethics Committee of Tabriz University of Medical Sciences approved this protocol, confirming ethical compliance absent patient involvement. Findings were disseminated via peer-reviewed journals, conferences and institutional seminars to inform AI integration in paediatric health informatics for equitable child development support.Systematic review registrationNot registered.

Introduction

Early childhood development (ECD) encompasses the physical, cognitive, linguistic and socio-emotional growth of children aged 0–5 years, a critical period influencing lifelong health outcomes.1 2 Globally, developmental disorders such as autism spectrum disorder (ASD), dyslexia and intellectual disability affect 10–15% of young children, manifesting through subtle impairments in communication and behaviour.3 Artificial intelligence (AI) applied to voice analysis emerges as a predictive signal, leveraging acoustic, prosodic and linguistic features from vocalisations to detect early deviations from typical trajectories.3 4 This protocol for a scoping review systematically maps AI applications in this domain, addressing the integration of voice biomarkers within health informatics for paediatric care. Importantly, this review is focused specifically on AI-enabled voice biomarkers in early childhood development, rather than paediatric voice biomarkers more broadly.5

The necessity of early ECD screening stems from its profound impact on intervention efficacy and resource allocation in global health systems.6 Traditional methods, including parent questionnaires and behavioural observations, face barriers like subjectivity, high costs and infrequent access, particularly in low-resource settings.7 At the same time, the evidence base remains fragmented, and the developmental specificity, clinical validity and implementation readiness of these approaches require systematic mapping.5

Specific limitations in current ECD assessment include methodological heterogeneity and a narrow focus on overt symptoms, overlooking vocal precursors to disorders.8 Voice biomarkers, while promising, vary across languages, dialects and recording environments, complicating model generalisability.9 Few studies target preschoolers comprehensively, with most emphasising single disorders like ASD rather than holistic developmental screening.10 These constraints underscore the need for synthesised evidence to standardise AI protocols. In early childhood, these challenges are particularly important because voice production, language emergence and social communication are rapidly changing across developmental stages, which may affect the performance and interpretation of AI models.11

Existing research demonstrates AI’s efficacy in voice-based paediatric screening. For ASD, machine learning (ML) on prosody and pitch from natural speech yields high classification accuracy using autoencoders.12 In dyslexia, deep learning detects phonological anomalies preceding reading deficits.13 Intellectual disability classification integrates phonetic and multimodal data for robust prediction.14 However, the existing literature primarily synthesises paediatric voice biomarkers across a broad age range and multiple health conditions, and therefore does not specifically examine AI-based voice applications in children under 5 years or focus on early developmental trajectories.5 These findings establish voice as a viable predictive signal, yet synthesis remains fragmented.

Strengths of prior studies include high diagnostic precision in controlled settings and multimodal enhancements, while weaknesses encompass small cohorts, limited diversity and poor real-world applicability.15 Interpretability deficits and ethical oversights further limit translation.16 Consequently, evidence quality varies, impeding clinical adoption. In addition, many studies are preliminary, use non-standardised recording conditions and evaluate disorder-specific outcomes rather than broader developmental outcomes, limiting cross-study comparability.17

By focusing on early childhood development, this protocol extends beyond prior paediatric voice reviews by examining how AI-derived voice biomarkers may contribute to early detection, monitoring and developmental support during the most sensitive period of neurodevelopment.

However, this scoping review protocol outlines a systematic approach to synthesise existing research on AI-driven audio biomarkers—specifically voice samples—for the early detection, monitoring and management of developmental outcomes in children under 5 years who are healthy, at-risk or developmentally impaired. It will delineate data collection methods, AI algorithms employed and their prospective effects on clinical outcomes, with AI integration positioned to advance developmental assessment and enhance child health trajectories in early childhood. This focus is expected to generate a more precise evidence map for researchers, clinicians and policy makers working at the intersection of child development, digital health and AI.

Methods and analysis

Protocol reporting

This protocol follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR).18 Registration in PROSPERO remains unavailable for scoping reviews.

Methodological framework

The review adopts the framework established by Arksey and O’Malley,19 enhanced by Levac et al and Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) guidelines.2022 Scoping reviews map the breadth of evidence on a topic and highlight literature gaps. Here, the focus centres on charting AI applications of voice biomarkers for early detection, monitoring and management of developmental outcomes in children aged 0–5 years, spanning typically developing, at-risk or diagnosed with disorders.19This methodological approch has been similary employed to map AI applications to vocal biomarkers in other populations, including schizophrenia23 and geriatric health assessment. 24

Review stages

The process includes six stages: (1) formulating research questions, (2) identifying relevant studies, (3) selecting studies, (4) charting data, (5) summarising and reporting results and (6) stakeholder consultation.19

Stage 1: formulating research questions

Research question

The central research question for this scoping review is as follows: How are voice samples employed as digital biomarkers in AI and ML applications for the early detection, monitoring and management of developmental outcomes in children under 5 years? More specifically, what roles do AI and ML play in leveraging voice data to support typically developing children, those at risk of delays and those with diagnosed developmental disorders?

Specific subquestions

These questions are structured across six thematic areas: (1) clinical applications, (2) voice recording protocols, (3) acoustic feature extraction, (4) AI/ML models and metrics, (5) demographic factors and (6) implementation considerations.

Stage 2: identification of relevant studies

The research team initially proposed search terms including “artificial intelligence”, “machine learning”, “voice biomarkers”, “early childhood development” and “developmental disorders”. Iterative pilot searches will identify further relevant terminology for integration into a refined strategy, which team members will review and approve before full implementation (online supplemental file 1). This finalised string applies to PubMed Medline. The strategy will target key databases such as PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, Embase, IEEE Xplore and CINAHL, alongside grey literature sources including Google Scholar and ClinicalTrials.gov, to capture studies published from 1 January 2015, through the current date. The PubMed search will serve as the foundation, with adaptations made for each database’s syntax while maintaining consistent Boolean operators (AND/OR) and proximity logic to ensure conceptual fidelity. To systematically address grey literature, searches will cover conference proceedings (eg, those from paediatric informatics or AI in health societies), preprint repositories (bioRxiv, medRxiv) and trial registries; however, preprints will be used only to identify potentially relevant studies and will not be included in the final analysis unless a peer-reviewed full-text version is available. Full documentation of dates, platforms and strings used will be maintained. Limitations arising from the evolving nature of these resources will be noted for transparency. Medical subject headings and equivalent controlled vocabularies will anchor the searches, supplemented by free-text keywords tailored to each platform.31 32 Date filters reflect consultations with experts in paediatric health informatics, capturing the era of rapid AI advancements in voice analysis since 2015, when deep learning applications began transforming developmental biomarkers. This cut-off balances comprehensiveness with relevance to contemporary methodologies.

Stage 3: study selection

Study selection will follow a structured, multiphase process aligned with PRISMA-ScR guidelines to ensure rigour and reproducibility. All records retrieved from database and grey literature searches will be imported into EndNote (V.X8 or later) for automated duplicate removal and initial organisation.

The screening will proceed in three sequential steps.33 34 35 First, two independent reviewers will assess titles and abstracts against the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, advancing potentially eligible studies to full-text review. Second, full texts of shortlisted articles will be evaluated in detail by the same reviewers to confirm eligibility, with exclusion reasons logged systematically. Third, reference lists of included studies and forward citations (via tools like Google Scholar) will be hand-searched to capture additional relevant evidence.

Disagreements at any stage will be resolved through reviewer discussion; unresolved conflicts will be arbitrated by a senior team member. For records with incomplete access (eg, abstracts only), corresponding authors will be emailed to request full texts. In accordance with scoping review methodology, no formal risk-of-bias or methodological quality assessment will be conducted. However, during subsequent data charting and synthesis, heterogeneity in study quality—including differences in sample size, study design, data collection methods, validation procedures and model performance reporting—will be described narratively to provide context for interpreting the evidence base and to highlight areas where methodological rigour varies across studies. The entire process, including exclusion rationales, will be documented transparently in a PRISMA-ScR flow diagram to facilitate assessment of selection bias and comprehensiveness. This methodical approach safeguards the integrity of evidence mapping while accommodating the heterogeneous nature of AI voice research in early childhood.

Stage 4: data charting

Data charting will use standardised extraction templates created in Microsoft Word, drawing from JBI recommendations to systematically capture key elements. Relevant fields will encompass: author(s) and publication year; study aims and design; participant details (age strata within 0–5 years: infants 0–24 months, toddlers 2–3 years, preschoolers 3–5 years, developmental status); voice recording protocols (tasks, formats, devices); acoustic features extracted; AI/ML algorithms applied; performance metrics (eg, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, AUC-ROC); and clinical implications for detection, monitoring or management.

Two reviewers will independently extract data from an initial 20% sample of included studies to establish reliability, with consistency measured via Cohen’s κ and percentage agreement. Should alignment exceed predefined thresholds (eg, κ>0.80), one reviewer will proceed with the balance, subject to verification by a second. For the remaining studies, the second reviewer will perform independent verification on a random subset (eg, 10–15%) to ensure ongoing consistency. Any discrepancies identified at any stage of data extraction will be resolved through iterative discussion between the two reviewers until consensus is reached. If persistent disagreements arise, both reviewers will revisit the original study reports and refine the extraction criteria collaboratively to ensure consistent interpretation. In addition, periodic calibration meetings will be conducted throughout the extraction phase to address ambiguities, refine coding decisions and maintain consistency across reviewers. All discrepancy resolutions and any modifications to the extraction framework will be documented to ensure transparency and auditability. A senior team member will conduct quality checks on the full dataset to verify completeness and fidelity. This tiered process balances efficiency with methodological rigour, tailored to the diverse formats encountered in AI-driven paediatric voice research.

Each included study will be charted against these domains using a standardised extraction template, enabling comprehensive coverage of the research questions while identifying cross-domain patterns and heterogeneities.

Results will be synthesised through narrative summaries organised by subdomain, supplemented by comparative tables detailing study characteristics, model performances (eg, accuracy ranges, common algorithms), and age-strata subgroup analyses (infants 0–24 months vs toddlers 2–3 years vs preschoolers 3–5 years), and visual aids like flowcharts of evidence gaps or algorithmic pipelines. Descriptive statistics will quantify age-specific trends, including prevalence of voice features, algorithm types and performance metrics by developmental stage. Thematic mapping will be presented via summary matrices that cross-reference studies against the six domains, and age strata, facilitating identification of evidence clusters, methodological consistencies/inconsistencies and priority areas for future research. This layered presentation facilitates clear identification of knowledge clusters, methodological variations and areas requiring further investigation, ensuring direct traceability to the protocol’s objectives.

Stage 6: consultation exercise

The final stage of the Arksey and O’Malley19 framework will integrate preliminary findings from this scoping review to guide practical advancements in AI-driven voice analysis for early childhood development. These insights will inform the prototyping of accessible screening tools or integrated platforms that use voice biomarkers to enhance early detection, monitoring and intervention strategies for children aged 0–5 years across developmental spectra.

Internal consultation among the research team will be conducted during the synthesis phase to refine interpretations, resolve ambiguities and identify translational priorities. Any external stakeholder consultation will be undertaken in a separate future study and is not a mandatory component of the present protocol. Stakeholder input, gathered through structured feedback sessions, when undertaken in future work, will critically appraise evidence gaps, feasibility in clinical settings and ethical considerations, ultimately strengthening the review’s recommendations for real-world implementation in diverse early childhood care contexts.

The protocol for this scoping review was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Tabriz University of Medical Sciences. The committee determined that the review’s objectives do not entail direct involvement of patients or clinical interventions, thereby upholding ethical requirements relevant to research based solely on literature review.

We intend to publish the findings of this scoping review in a peer-reviewed journal specialising in AI applications or voice biomarker research. The outcomes of this study will also be shared through seminars or journal club presentations at the Pediatric Health Research Center, Tabriz University of Medical Sciences, Tabriz, Iran.

Discussion

Voice analysis holds promise as a non-invasive biomarker for early childhood development, capturing acoustic features reflective of cognitive, social and emotional milestones. Alterations in prosody, pitch variability and phonetic patterns in young children’s speech and cries have been linked to developmental conditions like ASD and language delays, enabling AI-driven early screening. Smartphone-based remote assessments further support scalable monitoring in home or community settings, aligning with global needs for accessible paediatric telehealth.5

Existing literature emphasises disease-specific applications, such as ASD detection via Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients and support vector machines, but lacks synthesis across early developmental domains. Prior scoping reviews highlight high diagnostic accuracy (up to 100% in select models) for conditions like intellectual disabilities and speech impairments, underscoring methodological heterogeneity.5 Our protocol addresses this by mapping evidence on AI applications in voice for early childhood, without pursuing quality appraisal or causal inference, to identify gaps and inform standardised protocols.

Practical implementation in paediatric contexts faces challenges, including limited device access in low-resource areas and the need for age-appropriate AI training data. Ethical considerations are paramount: obtaining parental consent, obtaining assent from capable children and adhering to regulations like General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) or Health Insurance Protability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) for minors’ voice data privacy to mitigate risks of re-identification.36 Potential language bias from English/Persian restriction may overlook non-Western contexts, warranting future multilingual expansions. Strategies like clinician-parent codesign and workflow integration can enhance feasibility.

This scoping review protocol aims to map the evidence landscape on AI voice biomarkers in early childhood development, guiding future clinical, policy and innovation efforts without establishing diagnostic validity.
