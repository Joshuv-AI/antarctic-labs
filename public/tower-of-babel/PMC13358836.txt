Heller myotomy in achalasia: clinical implications from long-term outcomes
Authors: Keerthana Kota, Christopher DuCoin
Published: 2026

Barron et al.’s paper (1) presents a remarkable single-institution experience with surgical management of esophageal achalasia, reporting outcomes in 1,010 patients over a 25-year period. The size of the cohort and the duration of longitudinal follow-up are exceptional, placing this work among the most comprehensive surgical series on Heller myotomy to date. Such extended observation provides rare insight into long-term symptom control, durability of intervention, rates of reintervention, and the natural history of esophageal emptying after operative therapy. As newer technologies and endoscopic approaches continue to evolve, this dataset offers an invaluable benchmark against which contemporary and future therapies can be measured.

The study period, spanning from 1995 to 2020, encompasses substantial evolution in both diagnostic modalities and treatment paradigms for achalasia. The widespread adoption of high-resolution manometry and later endoluminal functional lumen imaging probe (EndoFLIP) refined phenotypic classification according to the Chicago Classification framework (2), while the introduction of peroral endoscopic myotomy (POEM) fundamentally altered therapeutic decision-making (3). The authors should be commended for their clear and pragmatic treatment algorithm, in which patients with type I and II achalasia were preferentially treated with laparoscopic Heller myotomy, while those with type III disease were directed toward POEM once it became available. This approach aligns closely with contemporary consensus recommendations, including the Society of American Gastrointestinal and Endoscopic Surgeons (SAGES) guidelines, which emphasize tailoring therapy to achalasia subtype and esophageal physiology (4). Similar subtype-driven algorithms have been adopted at many high-volume centers, reflecting convergence toward evidence-based, patient-specific care.

Standardized symptom assessment using the Eckardt score was implemented only after 2014, coinciding with the institutional adoption of POEM. While this limits direct comparison of symptom outcomes across the full study period, it also highlights an important inflection point in achalasia management. The SAGES guidelines underscore the Eckardt score as a validated and clinically meaningful tool for assessing treatment response, particularly in the modern era when comparisons between surgical and endoscopic therapies are increasingly necessary (4). Acknowledging this limitation reinforces the challenges inherent in longitudinal studies conducted across eras of rapid diagnostic and therapeutic innovation.

An important contribution of this work is the observation that complete esophageal emptying declines over time, even among patients who remain clinically asymptomatic. This finding reinforces the concept that achalasia is a chronic, progressive motility disorder rather than a condition definitively “cured” by myotomy. Prior surgical series have similarly demonstrated discordance between objective measures of esophageal function and patient-reported symptom relief (5,6). Imaging abnormalities may therefore persist or worsen despite durable symptom control, emphasizing the importance of integrating objective testing with clinical assessment when making long-term management decisions. The relatively low rate of esophagectomy reported in long-term surgical series such as this one further supports the effectiveness of timely intervention, while also reminding clinicians that end-stage disease remains a real, albeit infrequent, outcome in select patients with longstanding or refractory achalasia.

Intraoperative esophageal perforation is a recognized complication of Heller myotomy; however, when promptly identified, it is typically managed effectively with minimal long-term consequences. Historically, laparoscopic Heller myotomy has been associated with intraoperative perforation rates approaching 5%, even in high-volume centers, such as reported in this study at 5.4% (6). More recently, emerging data suggest that the adoption of robotic platforms has substantially reduced this risk. A systematic review and meta-analysis comparing robotic and laparoscopic approaches to foregut surgery, including Heller myotomy, demonstrated a marked reduction in intraoperative perforation rates with robotic assistance, approaching zero percent in some series (7). Enhanced three-dimensional visualization, wristed instrumentation, and improved precision during myotomy likely contribute to these improved safety profiles, with potential implications for long-term esophageal integrity and function.

The selective use of Dor fundoplication following Heller myotomy also deserves careful attention. While an anterior partial fundoplication is commonly performed to mitigate postoperative gastroesophageal reflux, both surgical literature and consensus guidelines recognize circumstances in which it may be omitted, including severe esophageal dilation, distorted foregut anatomy, prior gastric or esophageal surgery, or concerns regarding tension and impaired gastric mobility (2,5). Individualizing the decision to perform a fundoplication reflects the balance between reflux prevention and preservation of esophageal emptying, particularly in patients with advanced or longstanding disease (8).

In conclusion, the authors are to be congratulated for assembling and maintaining a robust clinical database spanning more than 25 years of achalasia care. This extraordinary effort not only advances our understanding of long-term outcomes after Heller myotomy but also reinforces a fundamental truth about achalasia: it is a lifelong condition requiring durable therapy, thoughtful surveillance, and individualized management over time. As treatment options continue to expand, through endoscopic innovation, robotic surgery, and refined physiologic assessment. This work stands as a testament to the enduring value of meticulous longitudinal data and exemplifies the importance of viewing achalasia through the lens of chronic disease management rather than episodic intervention.
