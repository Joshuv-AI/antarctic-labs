Bile duct segmental resection versus pancreaticoduodenectomy for middle-third extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma: A propensity-matched, single-center retrospective analysis
Authors: Su Hyung Park, Ji Su Kim, Yeon Su Kim, Seung Soo Hong, Sung Hyun Kim, Chang Moo Kang, Kyung Sik Kim, Ho Kyoung Hwang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgrounds/AimsThe optimal surgical approach for middle-third extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (MCC)—bile duct segmental resection (BDSR) or pancreaticoduodenectomy (PD)—remains controversial. This study aimed to compare the short- and long-term outcomes of BDSR and PD in patients with MCC.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 570 patients who underwent curative-intent surgery for MCC between January 2010 and December 2022. Clinicopathological characteristics, surgical outcomes, and oncologic outcomes (progression-free survival [PFS] and overall survival [OS]) were compared after 1:3 propensity score matching.ResultsAmong 570 patients, 112 underwent BDSR and 458 PD. After propensity score matching, BDSR showed shorter operative time and hospital stay (both p < 0.001), but fewer retrieved lymph nodes and higher local recurrence rates (both p < 0.001). PFS was inferior after BDSR (27.4 vs. 34.3 months, p = 0.004), whereas OS was comparable (36.7 vs. 55.5 months, p = 0.117). In patients with R0 resection, both PFS and OS were similar between groups.ConclusionsBDSR may offer perioperative advantages over PD, but is associated with inferior PFS, with no significant difference in OS. In patients who achieved R0 resection, the PFS and OS did not reach statistical significance. This suggests that BDSR should be considered only in highly selected patients in whom R0 resection is achievable.Keywords: Cholangiocarcinoma, Bile duct neoplasms, Pancreaticoduodenectomy, Bile duct segmental resection

INTRODUCTION

Cholangiocarcinoma is a malignant tumor originating from the bile duct epithelium, characterized by a poor prognosis and an aggressive clinical course. It has a relatively high incidence in Asian populations, likely due to regional risk factors and genetic predisposition [1]. Anatomically, cholangiocarcinoma is classified into three types based on its site of origin: intrahepatic, perihilar, and distal extrahepatic. Distal cholangiocarcinoma is further subdivided into middle-third extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (MCC) and distal-third extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma. Each of these subtypes requires a specific surgical approach and carries a different prognosis [2,3]. Surgical resection remains the only potentially curative treatment option available for patients with extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma [4,5], emphasizing the importance of accurate anatomical classification and optimal surgical planning to improve treatment outcomes.

Two surgical options are currently available for MCC: bile duct segmental resection (BDSR) and pancreaticoduodenectomy (PD). However, the optimal approach remains uncertain, and no consensus has been reached. According to existing studies, PD tends to offer oncologic benefits, as it is associated with fewer positive resection margins [3,6-9] and more retrieved lymph nodes [3,6-11]. Nevertheless, these findings are based on relatively small sample sizes [3,6,8-11] (fewer than 200 patients in the overall cohort) and varied surgical procedures used across studies. PD is associated with longer operative times, greater intraoperative blood loss [5], and significantly longer hospital stays [3,6,7] compared to BDSR. PD also carries a higher risk of major surgical complications, such as postoperative pancreatic fistula [12,13], and has a higher 90-day postoperative mortality rate [3].

Extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma occurs more frequently in older populations, and recent epidemiologic data indicate an increasing incidence among individuals aged ≥ 65 years [2]. Advanced age in PD is a significant risk factor for increased perioperative morbidity and mortality [12-14]. Considering these factors, the potential advantages of BDSR over PD in select patients warrant further investigation. This study retrospectively compared the two surgical approaches for MCC, using data from a high-volume, single-center institution with standardized surgical protocols. We aimed to evaluate and clarify the short-term and long-term outcomes associated with these two procedures, potentially contributing to a better understanding of surgical options in managing this complex disease.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patient and data collection

This retrospective study was conducted at Severance Hospital in Seoul, Korea, and included patients who underwent curative-intent surgery for MCC between January 2010 and December 2022. MCC was defined as a tumor located between the cystic duct insertion and the superior border of the pancreas based on preoperative imaging. In selected cases, intraoperative frozen-section analysis was performed; tumors were considered confined to the middle bile duct when both proximal and distal resection margins were confirmed negative for malignancy.

Only patients diagnosed with adenocarcinoma based on the final postoperative pathological examination were included; cases requiring simultaneous hepatectomy and PD due to intrahepatic tumor spread were excluded. The surgical approach (BDSR or PD) for curative intent was determined based on preoperative radiologic findings regarding tumor extent, lymph node metastasis, and patient condition. Among the included patients, some initially considered to have MCC based on preoperative imaging were ultimately found to have distal-third involvement on final pathologic assessment. Cases initially planned for BDSR but converted to PD intraoperatively were included in the PD group.

Clinicopathologic data were collected, including age, sex, body mass index, preoperative carcinoembryonic antigen and carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA 19-9) levels, T and N stages, number of retrieved and positive lymph nodes, resection margin status, tumor differentiation, and lymphovascular and perineural invasion.

Recurrence patterns were also collected and analyzed. Local recurrence was defined as recurrence at the tumor bed, hepaticojejunostomy site, remnant bile duct, or regional lymph node stations, including #8 (common hepatic artery), #12 (hepatoduodenal ligament), and #13 (retropancreatic area). Systemic recurrence was defined as recurrence involving distant organs or non-regional lymph nodes. Recurrence was primarily assessed using contrast-enhanced computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging, or positron emission tomography in conjunction with clinical evaluation; histological confirmation was obtained when clinically indicated.

To evaluate short-term surgical outcomes, data on operative time, length of hospital stay, and postoperative complications were collected. For long-term oncologic outcomes, progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) were analyzed. The Institutional Review Board of Severance Hospital approved this retrospective study (Approval No. 4-2025-0585) and waived the requirement for informed consent due to its retrospective design.

Surgical procedure

The choice between BDSR and PD was determined by a comprehensive assessment of preoperative imaging findings, including tumor extent, suspected intrapancreatic extension, proximity to the pancreaticobiliary junction, and the likelihood of achieving R0 resection. Patient-related factors such as age, comorbidities, performance status, and operative risk were also considered during surgical decision-making. In cases where adequate distal resection margins could not be secured intraoperatively or frozen-section analysis suggested residual malignancy, conversion to PD was considered.

Standard lymph node dissection includes stations #8 (common hepatic artery), #12 (hepatoduodenal ligament), and #13 (retropancreatic area). Regardless of the surgical approach, lymphadenectomy was consistently performed according to a standardized institutional protocol that mandated the inclusion of these nodal stations. For the distal resection margin, we ensured at least a 2.5 cm clearance below the palpable tumor border (Fig. 1). When performing intrapancreatic common bile duct resection, the pancreatic parenchyma is dissected in a cone shape to ensure sufficient clearance [15]. The proximal resection margin is determined at the liver hilum, and intraoperative frozen-section biopsies are performed to ensure a sufficient margin. If the frozen section indicates malignancy, an additional bile duct resection is performed. Depending on the patient’s condition and intraoperative findings, surgical extension, such as PD can be performed if deemed necessary.

Intraoperative findings of bile duct segmental resection. In cases involving the intrapancreatic portion of the common bile duct, the pancreatic parenchyma is dissected to allow transection with an adequate margin. For the distal margin, more than 2.5 cm is macroscopically preserved whenever possible.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 28.0 (IBM Corp.) and Jamovi, version 2.6.26 (The Jamovi project). Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U-test. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Survival was analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between groups were evaluated using the log-rank test. The Cox proportional hazard model was used for univariable and multivariable analyses to assess the impact of the variables on survival. p-values less than 0.05 were considered significant. Propensity score matching was performed at a 1:3 ratio based on the T and N stages.

RESULTS

Clinicopathologic characteristics

This study included 570 patients diagnosed with MCC based on preoperative imaging findings. Among them, BDSR was performed in 112 patients, and PD was performed in 458 patients. The baseline clinicopathological characteristics (Table 1) showed some differences between the two groups. Patients in the BDSR group were slightly older (mean age: 68.93 vs. 67.06, p = 0.026). Significant differences were observed in the tumor stage distribution, with the BDSR group having a higher proportion of early-stage tumors (T1: 27.7% vs. 14.8%, T2: 63.4% vs. 38.4%), whereas the PD group included more advanced tumors (T3: 46.7% vs. 7.1%, p < 0.001). The BDSR group had a significantly lower incidence of node-positive disease (N1+) than the PD group (17.0% vs. 43.2%, p < 0.001). Additionally, the BDSR group showed significantly lower rates of lymphovascular invasion (22.3% vs. 38.0%, p = 0.002) and perineural invasion (69.6% vs. 78.6%, p = 0.036) than the PD group. Similarly, the BDSR group had a significantly lower mean number of retrieved lymph nodes (mean number: 11.32 vs. 16.61, p < 0.001) and a lower mean number of positive lymph nodes (mean number: 0.39 vs. 1.15, p < 0.001) than the PD group.

Propensity score matching was conducted at a ratio of 1:3, specifically for T and N stages. Standardized mean differences (SMD) markedly improved from 0.844 to 0.057 for T stage and from 0.596 to 0.078 for N stage after matching. Eighty patients in the BDSR group were compared to 240 patients in the PD group, and there were no significant differences in age, sex, body mass index, and preoperative tumor markers; however, the mean number of retrieved lymph nodes was significantly lower in the BDSR group than in the PD group (mean number: 11.81 vs. 16.44, p < 0.001). Although the proportion of patients receiving adjuvant treatment numerically differed between groups after matching, the difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.301). In addition, the proportions of patients receiving adjuvant chemotherapy and radiotherapy were comparable between the two groups.

Surgical and oncologic outcomes

Regarding surgical outcomes after propensity score matching (Table 2), the surgical time was significantly shorter in the BDSR group than in the PD group (285 minutes vs. 438 minutes, p < 0.001). Patients in the BDSR group had significantly shorter hospital stays (16.54 days vs. 22.85 days, p < 0.001). The two groups had comparable rates of postoperative complications. The overall resection margin positivity rate was similar between the two groups (15.0% vs. 14.2%, p = 0.855). Recurrence rates were significantly higher in the BDSR group than in the PD group (58.8% vs. 27.5%, p < 0.001), primarily driven by increased local recurrence (27.5% vs. 9.6%, p < 0.001). Systemic recurrence rates did not differ significantly (37.5% vs. 26.3% for PD, p = 0.055). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis in the matched population demonstrated that the BDSR group had significantly lower PFS than the PD group (median 27.4 months [5-year PFS: 30.1%] vs. median 34.3 months [5-year PFS: 41.7%], p = 0.004). OS did not significantly differ between the BDSR group (median 36.7 months [5-year OS: 37.6%]) and the PD group (median 55.5 months [5-year OS: 47.2%], p = 0.117) (Fig. 2).

Risk factor analysis affecting oncologic outcomes

Univariate Cox regression analysis identified advanced T stage, positive lymph node status, positive resection margins, CA 19-9 levels, and adjuvant treatment as significant predictors of PFS (Table 3). In multivariate analysis, advanced T stage, positive lymph node status, positive resection margins, and CA 19-9 remained independently significant predictors of PFS. Additionally, multivariate analysis revealed that surgical extent significantly influenced PFS, with patients undergoing BDSR having an adjusted hazard ratio of 1.71 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.24–2.36, p < 0.001) compared to those undergoing PD. Univariable analysis similarly showed advanced T stage, positive lymph node status, positive resection margins, CA 19-9, and adjuvant treatment as significant predictors of OS (Table 4). However, in the multivariate model, only advanced T stage, positive lymph node status, positive resection margins, and CA 19-9 were significant predictors. The type of surgery (BDSR vs. PD) did not significantly affect OS outcomes in multivariate analysis (adjusted hazard ratio for BDSR = 1.42, 95% CI: 0.95–2.22, p = 0.102).

DISCUSSION

This study analyzed surgical and survival outcomes, as well as associated prognostic factors, in patients undergoing BDSR or PD for MCC. Compared with PD, BDSR was associated with significantly shorter operative times and reduced hospital stays. However, patients undergoing BDSR had fewer retrieved lymph nodes and showed inferior outcomes in terms of local recurrence rates and PFS. After adjusting for T and N stages, even though BDSR showed worse PFS than PD, there were no significant differences in OS between the two groups. Moreover, the surgical approach was an independent prognostic factor for PFS, but not for OS. Furthermore, among patients who achieved R0 resection, PFS and OS did not differ significantly between the two surgical methods.

Previous studies have reported conflicting results regarding resection margin positivity between BDSR and PD. While most studies have reported higher rates of positive margins associated with BDSR than PD [6-9,16], some studies have indicated no significant difference between the two surgical methods [3,17]. Similarly, our analysis revealed no significant differences in margin positivity rates between the BDSR and PD groups. We attribute this favorable outcome to careful patient selection based on rigorous preoperative radiological assessment and meticulous intraoperative techniques. Specifically, our surgical protocol emphasizes precise and adequate resection of the intrapancreatic common bile duct, ensuring sufficient distal clearance and achieving comparable negative margin rates between the two procedures. This highlights the importance of detailed preoperative evaluation and standardized surgical techniques for optimizing oncological outcomes, regardless of the surgical approach.

Regarding lymph node dissection, it is widely accepted that PD yields more lymph nodes than BDSR, with no evidence to the contrary. We observed similar results, which may be attributed to the additional lymphatic tissue retrieved during PD, including the lesser omentum, greater omentum, hepatogastric ligament (#3), supra/infrapyloric nodes (#5, #6), nodes around the superior mesenteric artery (#14), and the para-aortic lymph node area (#16). According to Kayahara et al. [18], lymph node metastasis in MCC primarily occurs in the lower half of the hepatoduodenal ligament (#12), the posterior surface of the pancreatic head (#13), and along the common hepatic artery (#8). In contrast, lymph node involvement at stations #9 (celiac axis) and #16 (paraaortic nodes) is infrequent, and metastasis to station #14 (superior mesenteric artery nodes) is unlikely.

Consequently, careful consideration is warranted when choosing BDSR, particularly when preoperative imaging indicates potential lymph node metastasis. If nodal involvement is preoperatively suspected, BDSR may not retrieve all affected lymph nodes, potentially compromising oncologic radicality. Furthermore, even when PD is performed, standard lymphadenectomy typically does not include stations #9 (celiac axis nodes) or #16 (paraaortic nodes). Thus, the preoperative identification of metastases in these distant nodal stations may indicate advanced disease beyond the limits of curative surgical resection, regardless of the surgical method used. Therefore, further research should specifically address this issue to provide clearer guidance on the optimal extent of lymphadenectomy in MCC surgery.

Most studies indicate a higher recurrence rate following BDSR, with an associated increase in local recurrence [3,8], which is consistent with our findings. Although many studies attribute the increased local recurrence rate after BDSR to higher R1 rates, Hayashi et al. [7] reported differences in recurrence rates between BDSR and PD, even among patients with early-stage tumors achieving R0 resection. This suggests that other factors may contribute to recurrence.

Currently, little is known regarding the recurrence patterns and factors influencing MCC recurrence. While some studies suggest that the length of the resection margin might impact recurrence [19], the available evidence is inconclusive. We were unable to analyze the margin clearance length because the pathology records lacked precise measurements. Although evidence remains limited, previous studies suggest that margin clearance length may influence local or overall recurrence rates. Therefore, surgeons at our institution strive to secure a gross distal margin of at least 2 cm, whenever feasible. We found no difference in recurrence rates between the BDSR and PD groups when R0 resection was achieved. In R1 resections, aggressive adjuvant therapy, including radiotherapy, may be warranted. Given the evidence that radiotherapy can reduce local recurrence [20], its use should be considered for patients with positive resection margins, although further research is needed to define its role.

Based on these findings, careful patient selection is essential for determining the optimal surgical approach for MCC. In particular, BDSR should be reserved for highly selected patients with favorable tumor characteristics in whom complete (R0) resection can be reliably achieved. Although advanced age and comorbidities are important considerations in surgical decision-making [21], our findings do not support extending the indication of BDSR to patients who are poor candidates for PD. Instead, the selection of BDSR should be primarily guided by oncologic factors, including tumor extent and the feasibility of achieving adequate margins [22]. In this context, intraoperative assessment with readiness to convert to PD remains critical. Further studies are warranted to refine patient selection criteria and to better define the role of BDSR in the management of MCC.

The study’s primary limitation is the inherent difficulty of accurately diagnosing cholangiocarcinoma (MCC) based solely on preoperative imaging. While patients were included due to imaging findings suggestive of MCC, some were later discovered to have tumors involving the distal third of the extrahepatic bile duct. A secondary limitation is the retrospective, single-institution design, which introduces potential selection bias, variability in surgical techniques, and challenges in standardizing surgical procedures.

Third limitation of this study is the lack of analysis based on initial surgical intent. Patients initially considered for BDSR but requiring intraoperative conversion to PD due to positive margins were included in the PD group. This may have introduced selection bias by preferentially retaining patients with more favorable tumor characteristics in the BDSR group, potentially affecting comparative outcomes. Finally, distinguishing between middle-third and distal-third cholangiocarcinoma is often challenging in real-world clinical practice, particularly based on preoperative imaging and intraoperative assessment. Therefore, some degree of heterogeneity in this retrospective cohort may have been unavoidable.

In conclusion, this study suggests that BDSR offers short-term benefits over PD, including shorter operative time and hospital stay. However, it is associated with inferior PFS and higher local recurrence rates, with no significant difference in OS. The type of surgery was not independently associated with OS. Therefore, BDSR should be considered cautiously and may represent a potential surgical option only in carefully selected patients with favorable tumor characteristics in whom R0 resection is achievable. Further studies are warranted to refine patient selection criteria and clarify the role of adjuvant therapies in MCC management.
