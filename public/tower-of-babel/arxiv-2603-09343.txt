‘Relativistic’ propagation of instability fronts in nonlinear Klein-Gordon equation dynamics
A. M. Kamchatnov

Abstract
We consider propagation of instability fronts in conservative nonlinear wave systems by the Whitham method. Whitham modulation equations for periodic solutions of the generalized Klein-Gordon equation are solved in the limit of asymptotically large times, when the size of the instability wave region is much greater than the size of the initial localized disturbance, so the solution reaches the self-similar regime. The general self-similar solution is illustrated by two typical examples of the nonlinearity function. It is shown that in these models the instability fronts propagate with maximal group velocity.

1. Introduction.
—Propagation of instability fronts is a universal phenomenon manifesting
itself in various physical situations (see, e.g., review article
vanSaar-03
and references
therein). In a number of typical situations, a localized perturbation is initially confined to a
small region, and then it spreads out into the rest part of an unstable system. This spreading
often occurs with a well-defined velocity defined by the intrinsic properties of the system,
independent of the initial conditions. In particular, in the case of nonlinear diffusion systems,
these velocities correspond to the ‘marginal stability’ condition
dl-83
;
vanSaar-87
similar
to the condition that separates the absolute instability from the convective one
sturrock
;
briggs
.
The same selection rule for the instability propagation velocity remains correct in case of
non-dissipative modulationally unstable nonlinear dispersive systems. For example,
a plane wave, described by a nonlinear Schrödinger (NLS) equation, is modulationally unstable

ostrovsky-66
;
bf-67
;
zakharov-68
with respect to disintegration into wave packets. Therefore,
a small localized perturbation of a plane wave leads to the formation of a
region of nonlinear oscillations whose small-amplitude edges propagate into the undisturbed part of
the wave with the minimal group velocity of the propagating mode
kk-69
;
karpman
in agreement
with the ‘marginal stability’ condition. This observation was confirmed by the solution of the
Whitham modulation equations for the whole region of oscillations
kamch-92
;
egkk-93
;
bk-94
;
kamch-97
.
A similar situation takes place for the stability of dark solitons described by the two-dimensional
NLS equation
kt-88
, so that the transition from absolute instability to convective one
explains the existence of oblique solitons in a flow of a Bose-Einstein condensate past an
obstacle
egk-06
;
kp-08
;
kk-11
in agreement with experimental observations in
Refs.
amo-11
;
grosso-11
. In this respect, it is especially interesting that the property of
‘marginal stability’ is captured by the exact self-similar solutions of the Whitham modulation
equations
whitham-65
;
whitham
derived for periodic solutions of the NLS equation in
Refs.
fl-86
;
pavlov-87
. Actually, the solutions of Refs.
kamch-92
;
egkk-93
;
bk-94

(see also Ref.
kamch-97
) provide examples of the exact realization of the general considerations
presented in Refs.
dl-83
;
vanSaar-87
specified for some class of completely integrable equations.
This observation suggests that the Whitham equations can be applied to the description of the propagation
of instability fronts of other systems. In this Letter, we will apply this method to the generalized
nonlinear Klein-Gordon equation and find a remarkably simple self-similar solution of Whitham equations
that describes the propagation of instability fronts. According to this solution, the instability
fronts propagate with maximal group velocity, corresponding to the short wavelength limit of the
dispersion relation.

2. Periodic solutions and modulation equations.
—A paradigmatic example of a modulationally
unstable situation is given by the well-known nonlinear Klein-Gordon equation,

\varphi_{tt}-\varphi_{xx}+U^{\prime}(\varphi)=0,\quad U^{\prime}=\frac{dU}{d\varphi},\quad U(0)=0,

(1)

with a nonlinear ‘potential’
U(\varphi)
.
It is easy to see that Eq. (
1
) has travelling wave solutions
\varphi=\varphi(\xi)
,
\xi=x-Vt
,
where
\varphi(\xi)
is defined implicitly by the formula

\xi=\sqrt{\frac{V^{2}-1}{2}}\int_{\varphi_{0}}^{\varphi}\frac{d\varphi}{\sqrt{A-U(\varphi)}},

(2)

with
\varphi(0,0)=\varphi_{0}
. The variable
\varphi
oscillates between the turning points given by
the two roots of the equation
A-U(\varphi)=0
, so it can be called the amplitude parameter.
In a modulated wave, the amplitude parameter
A
and the
phase velocity
V
become slow functions of
x
and
t
, and their dynamics is determined by the modulation
equations derived by Whitham in Ref.
whitham-65
. According to Whitham, it is convenient to express
the modulation equations in terms of the function

\begin{split}&W(V,A)=\sqrt{V^{2}-1}\,G(A),\\
&G(A)=\sqrt{2}\oint\sqrt{A-U(\varphi)}\,d\varphi,\end{split}

(3)

where the integration is taken over the interval
\varphi\in\{\varphi:A-U(\varphi)\geq 0\}
. Then the wavelength
is equal to

L=\frac{\partial W}{\partial A}=\sqrt{V^{2}-1}\cdot G^{\prime}(A),

(4)

so the wave number
k=1/L
and the frequency
\omega=kV
are related with each other by the dispersion relation

\omega^{2}=k^{2}+(G^{\prime}(A))^{-2}.

(5)

Consequently, the group velocity is equal to

v=\left(\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial k}\right)_{A}=\frac{k}{\omega}=\frac{1}{V}.

(6)

As is clear from Eq. (
3
), we always have
V>1
, so the physical velocity
v
of the signal’s propagation
is always smaller than unity.

Whitham derived the modulation equations in the form

\begin{split}&\left(\frac{kV}{V^{2}-1}+A\right)_{t}+\left(\frac{kVW}{V^{2}-1}\right)_{x}=0,\\
&\left(\frac{kVW}{V^{2}-1}\right)_{t}+\left(\frac{kV^{2}W}{V^{2}-1}-A\right)_{x}=0.\end{split}

(7)

It is convenient to exclude the variables
W
and
V
with the help of Eqs. (
3
) and (
6
), so we get

\begin{split}&\left(\frac{G/G^{\prime}}{1-v^{2}}+A-\frac{G}{G^{\prime}}\right)_{t}+\left(\frac{(G/G^{\prime})v}{1-v^{2}}\right)_{x}=0,\\
&\left(\frac{(G/G^{\prime})v}{1-v^{2}}\right)_{t}+\left(\frac{(G/G^{\prime})v^{2}}{1-v^{2}}-A+\frac{G}{G^{\prime}}\right)_{x}=0.\end{split}

(8)

Linearization of these equations with respect to small deviations of
A
and
v
from constant ‘background’
values yields the characteristic velocities

v_{\pm}=\frac{v\pm c}{1\pm vc},

(9)

where
c
is defined by the expression

c^{2}=-{GG^{\prime\prime}}/{(G^{\prime})^{2}}.

(10)

If
c
is real, then Eqs. (
9
) are obviously interpreted as velocities of upstream and downstream sound
propagation with the relativistic rule of addition of velocities. In this case, Eqs. (
8
) can be rewritten
in the form of equations of relativistic hydrodynamics (see, e.g., Ref.
kamch-23
). If
c^{2}<0
and the
characteristic velocities are complex, then such a wave is modulationally unstable, so its smooth perturbation
grows with time. This is just the situation we are interested in.

3. Evolution of a small localized perturbation.
—Let us assume that the potential
U(\varphi
) has a
minimum at
\varphi=0
, and it grows monotonously with
\varphi
up to some maximal value at
\varphi=\varphi_{m}
.
Then the state
\varphi=0
is stable and the state
\varphi=\varphi_{m}
is obviously unstable. If we disturb this
unstable state locally in the vicinity of the point
x=0
, then the left- and right-propagating waves
will form an oscillatory structure between negative
and positive instability fronts. We assume that this oscillatory structure can be represented as a modulated
nonlinear periodic solution of Eq. (
1
), so the solution of the Whitham modulation Eqs. (
8
)
yields the profiles of the amplitude parameter
A
and the group velocity
v
in the whole region, including
the edges at the instability fronts.

The assumption that the initial disturbance is small suggests that its size becomes inessential very fast,
so the solution of Eqs. (
8
) becomes self-similar, and from dimensional considerations we conclude
that the flow velocity distribution is given by the formula

v=x/t.

(11)

Besides that, it is natural to assume that the amplitude parameter
A=A(x,t)
only depends on the
‘relativistic’ interval:
A=A(z)
,
z=t^{2}-x^{2}
. Making these two assumptions, we reduce each of
Eqs. (
8
) to the same ordinary differential equation

G(A)/(zG^{\prime}(A))+2dA/dz=0
,
which is readily solved to give

G(A)={C}(t^{2}-x^{2})^{-1/2},

(12)

where
C
is an integration constant. This formula defines implicitly the dependence of the amplitude

A
on
x
and
t
. The applicability condition of the Whitham modulation theory for a concrete function

G(A)
demands that the integration constant should take such values that the relevant intervals of
x

contain a large number of oscillations. To put this in another way, we can choose
C
equal to unity,
assuming that
x
is replaced by
x/\varepsilon
, so that the wavelength (
4
) acquires a small factor

\varepsilon\ll 1
. This means that the Whitham equations (
7
) or (
8
) are written for a large-scale
evolution of envelope functions with characteristic size about unity. We will choose this definition of
scales and take
C=1
in what follows.

Now, we will illustrate the solution (
11
), (
12
) by concrete examples.

(a) sine-Gordon equation.
If
U(\varphi)=1-\cos\varphi
, then the integral in Eq. (
3
)
is readily calculated,

G(A)=16\left[E(A/2)-(1-A/2)K(A/2)\right],

(13)

where
K(m)
and
E(m)
are the complete elliptic integrals of the first and second kind, respectively.
The squared ‘sound velocity’ (see Eq. (
10
))

c^{2}=-4A(2-A)\left(\frac{1}{K(A/2)}\frac{dK(A/2)}{dA}\right)^{2}<0

(14)

is negative for all physically acceptable values of
A
,
0\leq A\leq 2
, that correspond to

-\varphi_{m}\leq\varphi\leq\varphi_{m}
,
\varphi_{m}=\pi
. The integral in Eq. (
3
) reduces to the
elliptic one, and its inversion yields the periodic solution in the explicit form

\begin{split}\varphi=2\arcsin\left[\sqrt{\frac{A}{2}}\,\,\mathrm{sn}\left(\frac{vx-t}{\sqrt{1-v^{2}}},\frac{A}{2}\right)\right].\end{split}

(15)

Consequently, small values of
A
correspond to small-amplitude waves around the stable state
\varphi=0
.
In the limit
A\to 2
, this solution converts into the kink or anti-kink solution
of the sine-Gordon equation,

\varphi=2\arcsin\left[\pm\tanh\left(\frac{vx-t}{\sqrt{1-v^{2}}}\right)\right],

(16)

so that
\varphi=-\pi
as
x\to-\infty
and
\varphi=\pi
as
x\to+\infty
for the upper sign in Eq. (
16
)
and vice versa for the lower sign. Consequently, this solution connects two unstable states at
the maxima of the potential
U(\varphi)=1-\cos\varphi
, and for smaller values
A<2
we have nonlinear oscillations
with the amplitude

a=2\arcsin\sqrt{A/2}.

(17)

Combining it with the formula

(t^{2}-x^{2})^{-1/2}=16\left[E(A/2)-(1-A/2)K(A/2)\right],

(18)

we obtain the dependence of
a
on
x
for any fixed moment of time
t
in a parametric form.
The corresponding plots are shown in Fig.
1
. As one can see, the instability region expands
with time, and its fronts at the edges move (in the Whitham theory approximation) with the ‘light’
velocity
v=1
. In the vicinity of the edge points the wave consists of ‘trains’ of kinks with
the amplitudes close to the maximal value
\varphi_{m}=\pi
.

(b) Two-well potential model.
Now we assume that the potential
U(\varphi)
is given by a
fourth-degree polynomial of
\varphi
. Let initially
U(\varphi)
have a single minimum at
\varphi=0
,
which corresponds to a stable state of the system. Then the coefficient before the quadratic term
changes its sign, so this state becomes unstable, and we have two other stable states with non-zero
values of
\varphi
. For simplicity, we assume that the potential acquires the form

U(\varphi)=(\varphi^{2}-1)^{2},

(20)

so the two new ‘vacua’ correspond to
\varphi=\pm 1
. If we disturb the unstable state
\varphi=0
in the vicinity
of the point
x=0
, then two waves of instability start to propagate away, and we assume again that they can be
represented as a modulated periodic solution of Eq. (
1
). In case of transition to the vacuum
\varphi=1
,
the periodic solution corresponds to oscillations of
\varphi
in the interval

a_{-}=\sqrt{a_{+}^{2}-1}\leq\varphi\leq a_{+},\quad 1<a_{+}<\sqrt{2},

(21)

where
a_{-}
and
a_{+}
are the lower and upper envelopes of the wave amplitude. It is easy to express the
periodic solution in terms of the Jacobi elliptic
\mathrm{sn}
-function,

\varphi=\left\{a_{+}^{2}-2(a_{+}^{2}-1)\mathrm{sn}^{2}\left[\frac{a_{+}(vx-t)}{\sqrt{1-v^{2}}},m\right]\right\}^{1/2},

(22)

where
m=2(1-1/a_{+}^{2})
.
In a modulated wave,
v
and
a_{+}
become functions of
x
and
t

In our self-similar solution of the Whitham modulation equations we have

a_{+}

on

is defined by the equation
G(a_{+})=(t^{2}-x^{2})^{-1/2}
(see Eq. (
12
)), where

G(a_{+})=\frac{4\sqrt{2}}{3}a_{+}\left\{E(m)-(2-a_{+}^{2})K(m)\right\}.

(23)

Plots of the modulated waves of instability and of envelopes of the oscillation amplitudes at some moment

4. Discussion.
It is well known that the Whitham modulation theory provides quite a general
approach to studying the stability of nonlinear waves (see, e.g.,
whitham
;
ir-2000
). In the case
of stable waves, the Whitham method forms the basis for the theory of dispersive shock waves
gp-73

(see also Refs.
eh-16
;
kamch-21
). It was indicated long ago
lighthill-65
that this method
is also applicable to modulationally unstable systems, and some concrete examples were obtained for the
completely integrable focusing NLS
kamch-92
;
egkk-93
;
bk-94
;
kamch-97
and sine-Gordon

kk-76
;
fml-83
;
ks-91
;
novok-96
;
kamch-23
equations. Usually, the solutions considered in these
papers corresponded to different boundary conditions at
x\to\pm\infty
. In this Letter, we discussed
a problem of evolution of an initially localized disturbance in a modulationally unstable system,
and we showed for the case of the generalized Klein-Gordon equation that such a problem admits a simple
self-similar solution. We showed that this solution of the Whitham equations yields the complete
description of the instability waves propagating into the unstable regions. The edges of such
waves correspond to the instability fronts, which propagate with maximal group velocity
v\to 1

reached in the short wavelength limit
k\to\infty
.
