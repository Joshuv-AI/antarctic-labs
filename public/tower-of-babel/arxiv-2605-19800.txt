The quantum Almeida-Thouless line in the self-overlap-corrected quantum Sherrington-Kirkpatrick model
Chokri Manai 1 and Simone Warzel 2,3,4 1 Courant Institute, New York University, USA 2 Department of Mathematics, TU Munich, Garching, Germany 3 Munich Center for Quantum Science and Technology, Munich, Germany 4 Department of Physics, TU Munich, Garching, Germany

Abstract
We present a complete analysis of the glass transition in the self-overlap-corrected Sherrington – Kirkpatrick (SK) model in a transverse magnetic field, also referred to as the quantum SK model. In particular, we determine the phase boundary separating the glassy and paramagnetic phases. The proof is based on a simplified Parisi variational principle for the quantum pressure, which only involves classical Parisi order parameters. As part of the proof, we also analyze the pressure of the self-overlap-constrained quantum SK model and its Parisi description, as well as the pressure of generalized quantum Hopfield models. Keywords: Quantum spin glass, Parisi variational formula, phase transition MSC: 81S40; 82B44; 82D30

Motivation

The Sherrington-Kirkpatrick (SK) model is the most prominent spin-glass model with Ising spins
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{N})\in\{-1,1\}^{N}
.
The energy of these spins is built from an all-to-all random pair interaction

U(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2N}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{N}g_{jk}\,{\sigma}_{j}{\sigma}_{k}

(1.1)

with couplings
\big(g_{jk}\big)
given by independent and identically distributed, standard normal random variables.
One of the celebrated thermodynamic features
[
40
,
35
]
is the emergence of a spin-glass phase associated with replica symmetry breaking below a critical temperature
T_{c}=\beta_{c}^{-1}
, which turns out to be
\beta_{c}=1
in the chosen units. The mathematics developed for a rigorous description of this phase transition has led to proofs of many of Parisi’s predictions starting with Guerra’s interpolation bound and Talagrand’s proof of Parisi’s variational formula for the free energy
[
20
,
44
]
, see also
[
45
,
46
,
38
]
. Panchenko established the ultrametricity conjecture and provided a streamlined derivation of Parisi’s formula for mixed
p
-spin models
[
37
,
38
]
. In recent years, a series of works settled properties of the Parisi measure in the glass phase
[
5
,
4
,
6
,
52
,
53
]
and the correctness of the so-called TAP approach
[
12
,
14
,
15
]
.

Adding to (
1.1
) the term
\sum_{j=1}^{N}h{\sigma}_{j}
, which models a longitudinal magnetic field, the spin-glass phase is known to disappear at high enough
h
. Denoting by
T=\beta^{-1}
the temperature, the graph in the
h-T
-plane, which marks this phase transition, has been predicted to coincide with the Almeida-Thouless (AT) line
[
3
]
. The latter is defined through the implicit equation

\beta^{2}\mathbb{E}_{z}\frac{1}{\cosh^{4}(\beta\sqrt{q}z+\beta h)}=1,

(1.2)

where
z
is a standard normal variable with
\mathbb{E}_{z}
its expectation, and
q
is the unique positive solution of
q=\mathbb{E}_{z}\tanh^{2}(\beta\sqrt{q}z+\beta h)
. Toninelli proved the instability of the replica-symmetric solution below the AT line already 20 years ago
[
48
]
, but the proof of replica-symmetry up to the AT line (
1.2
) turned out to be a challenge despite being in principle a consequence of the Parisi formula. Several recent works addressed this or related questions
[
7
,
8
,
21
,
13
]
, and a full proof of the AT conjecture is presented in a recent preprint
[
26
]
based on a characterization of the phase boundary by Jagannath-Tobasco, who already established the AT line for large enough
\beta

[
22
]
. For the following discussion we recall that
\mathbb{E}_{z}\frac{1}{\cosh^{4}(\beta\sqrt{q}z+\beta h)}\sim C\,\beta^{-1}e^{-h^{2}/2}
as
\beta\to\infty
and hence for any fixed
h>0
the SK model undergoes a glass transition.

Taking the quantumness of spins seriously, one may inquire about the behaviour of the glass phase in the presence of a transversal field
b
.
In this case, the energy is modeled by the quantum SK (QSK) Hamiltonian

H_{N}=\lambda\ U(S_{1}^{z},\dots,S_{N}^{z})\ -\ b\sum_{j=1}^{N}S_{j}^{x}\qquad\mbox{on}\quad\bigotimes_{j=1}^{N}\mathbbm{C}^{2}.

(1.3)

The first term is the SK energy (
1.1
) scaled by
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and evaluated at the jointly diagonalizable
z
-components of the Pauli matrices. They act nontrivially only in the
j
th tensor component as
S_{j}^{z}\coloneqq\mathbbm{1}\otimes\dots\otimes S^{z}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbbm{1}
with
\mathbbm{1}
the identity matrix on
\mathbbm{C}^{2}
and

S^{x}=\left(\begin{matrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{matrix}\right)\,,\quad S^{y}=\left(\begin{matrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{matrix}\right)\,,\quad S^{z}=\left(\begin{matrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{matrix}\right)

the three Pauli matrices. The linear operators
S_{j}^{x}
entering the second term in (
1.3
) are defined analogously.
This second term represents the transverse (in the
x
-direction) magnetic field of constant strength
b
. It is one of the striking predictions in the physics literature
[
17
,
49
,
50
,
42
,
19
,
51
,
47
]
that the line, which marks the disappearance of the glass phase in the presence of a transversal field, ends at
T=0
in a critical field
b_{c}>0
in the
b-T
-plane - quite in contrast to its classical analogue, which does not end in a critical point on the
T=0
-axis. Aside from upper and lower bounds
[
25
,
24
]
, not much is rigorously known about this line, which we will refer to as the quantum AT line. In particular, there is
no established conjectural form of an implicit equation, and physics results are mostly based on numerics
[
51
,
47
]
. The current rigorous understanding of the phase diagram of the QSK model, including the conjectured QAT line, is summarized in Figure
1
.

If
U
is replaced by toy models such as the Random Energy Model (REM), which corresponds to the
p\to\infty
limit of
p
-spin glasses in a transverse field
[
30
,
23
]
, or its hierarchical generalizations, the free energy takes a comparatively simple form, and phase boundaries are explicitly computable
[
29
,
28
,
31
]
. Even more advanced questions, such as localization-delocalization transitions and trajectory dynamics, can be tackled
[
32
,
18
,
34
]
. In REM-type glasses, a longitudinal field shrinks the glass region in its most correlated sector first, whereas the transversal field begins by changing the frozen region in its least correlated sector. This gives some intuition for the QAT line in the QSK; however, we expect the true mechanism to be of a deeper nature.

In the quantum case, the thermodynamic properties of the QSK are encoded in the partition function given by the normalized trace,

Z_{N}\coloneqq\left(2\cosh(\beta b)\right)^{-N}{\operatorname{Tr}\,}e^{-\beta H_{N}}.

The normalization is chosen such that in the absence of
U
, the partition function is one. A useful mathematical tool for this quantity is the functional integral representation, which is based on the fact that
S^{x}
is the generator of spin-flips in the
z
-basis.

More precisely, let
\omega_{1}
be a Poisson process on the unit interval with intensity
\beta b\geq 0
and conditioned to have an even number of points in
[0,1)
, and set

\eta_{1}(t,\omega_{1}):=\#\{\mbox{Poisson points of $\omega_{1}$ in $[0,t)$}\}
. This induces the paths

\xi_{1}:[0,1)\to\{-1,1\},\quad\xi_{1}(t):=\sigma_{1}\ (-1)^{\eta_{1}(t)}.

(1.4)

with initial condition
\sigma_{1}\in\{-1,1\}
. Choosing the initial condition
\sigma_{1}\in\{-1,1\}
uniformly distributed and the Poisson process to be independent from
\sigma_{1}
, we denote the induced probability measure on
\xi_{1}
by
\nu_{1}
. Setting
\nu_{N}\coloneqq\nu_{1}^{\otimes N}
, the product measure of
N
identical copies of the path measure, we hence define a joint probability measure on
N
paths
\boldsymbol{\xi}=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{N})
.
Abbreviating the expectation value with respect to this probability measure by
\int(\cdot)\nu_{N}(d\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
, the functional integral representation of the normalized partition function then takes the Feynman-Kac form

Z_{N}=\left(2\cosh(\beta b)\right)^{-N}{\operatorname{Tr}\,}e^{-\beta H_{N}}=\int\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{1}\beta\lambda U\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}(t)\right)dt\right)\nu_{N}(d\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\xi}}),

(1.5)

see e.g.
[
25
]
for a derivation. From the point of view of the theory of spin glasses, the QSK thus takes the form of a continuum limit of vector spin glasses with the paths
\boldsymbol{\xi}
taking the role of the vectors
[
39
,
9
,
1
]
.

Theoretically, the information about the quantum AT line is buried in the quantum Parisi formula for its free energy, which was derived in
[
33
]
. For its formulation, we introduce the
quantum Parisi functional

\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}\left(\pi,x\right)\coloneqq X_{0}(\pi,x)+\frac{\beta^{2}\lambda^{2}}{4}\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\pi(m)\right\|^{2}_{2}\ dm,

(1.6)

on the set of paths

\Pi\coloneqq\{\pi:[0,1]\to\mathcal{S}_{2}^{+}\ |\ \pi\,\mbox{monotone non-decreasing and right-continuous}\}

taking values in the space
\mathcal{S}_{2}^{+}
of non-negative Hilbert-Schmidt operators on the real Hilbert space
L^{2}(0,1)
. The latter is equipped with the real scalar product

\langle f,g\rangle\coloneqq\int_{0}^{1}f(t)g(t)dt,\qquad f,g\in L^{2}(0,1).

(1.7)

The second entry
x\in\mathcal{S}_{2}
in (
1.6
) is again a Hilbert-Schmidt operator. By the relation
(xf)(t)=\int_{0}^{1}x(t,s)f(s)ds
for all
f\in L^{2}(0,1)
, it may hence be identified with a square-integrable kernel
x:[0,1)^{2}\to\mathbbm{R}
.
Its Hilbert-Schmidt norm is abbreviated by

\|x\|_{2}\coloneqq\left(\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}x(t,s)^{2}dtds\right)^{1/2},

and
\langle x,y\rangle\coloneqq\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}x(t,s)y(t,s)dsdt
is the corresponding scalar product.
The functional

X_{0}(\pi,x)
is most easily defined recursively in case
\pi:[0,1]\to\mathcal{S}_{2}^{+}
is a monotone step function
with exactly
r
steps. In this case, one picks
r
independent Gaussian random functions
w_{1}^{\pi},\ldots,w_{r}^{\pi}
in
L^{2}(0,1)
, which are uniquely characterized by their mean and covariance,

\mathbb{E}\left[w_{j}^{\pi}(s)\right]=0,\qquad\mathbb{E}\left[w_{j}^{\pi}(s)w_{j}^{\pi}(t)\right]=\pi(m_{j})(s,t)-\pi(m_{j-1})(s,t),

(1.8)

and recursively defines the real random functionals:

\displaystyle X_{r}(\pi,x)\ \coloneqq

\displaystyle\ln\int\exp\left(\sum_{j=1}^{r}\beta\lambda\langle\xi,w_{j}^{\pi}\rangle+\left\langle\xi,\left(x-\tfrac{\beta^{2}\lambda^{2}}{2}\pi(1)\right)\xi\right\rangle\right)\nu_{1}(d\xi)

\displaystyle\qquad X_{j-1}(\pi,x)\ \coloneqq\frac{1}{m_{j}}\ln\mathbb{E}_{j}\!\left[e^{m_{j}X_{j}(\pi,x)}\right].

The Parisi functional is Lipschitz continuous
[
33
, Prop. 1.5]
in the sense that there is some
L_{\lambda}\in(0,\infty)
such that for all
\pi,\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi
and
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{2}
:

\left|\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\pi,x)-\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\pi^{\prime},x^{\prime})\right|\leq L_{\lambda}\left[\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\pi(m)-\pi^{\prime}(m)\right\|_{2}dm+\|x-x^{\prime}\|_{2}\right].

(1.9)

Hence
\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}
may be uniquely extended from step functions to arbitrary
\pi\in\Pi
.
The quantum Parisi formula then reads as follows
[
33
, Thm. 2.1]

\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\ln Z_{N}\right]=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{S}_{2}^{+}}\left[\inf_{\pi\in\Pi}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}\left(\pi,x\right)-\frac{1}{\beta^{2}\lambda^{2}}\|x\|_{2}^{2}\right].

(1.10)

The variational problem involves an outer supremum. This is a common feature of the Parisi formula in all vector-spin glasses. In these problems, the self-overlap, which models correlations of the same spin but different times in the unit interval, is generally non-trivial. In the QSK, it is believed that this self-overlap is invariant under time shift. If correct, then the outer variational problem may be restricted to
x\in\mathcal{S}_{2}^{+}
that share this shift invariance. In this case, it was shown in
[
33
, Thm. 2.6]
that the inner variational problem may be restricted to ’classical’ paths.
Despite this simplification, determining the quantum AT line is still beyond reach. In fact, even the annealed solution, which at
\lambda=1
is given by the variational problem
\sup_{x\in\mathcal{S}_{2}^{+}}\left[\mathcal{P}_{1}\left(0,x\right)-\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}\|x\|_{2}^{2}\right]
does not possess an explicit solution. It was, however, proved in
[
25
]
that this annealed solution agrees with (
1.10
) for all
\beta<1
- hence extending the result in
[
2
]
to
b>0
and proving a bound on the replica symmetric region, see Figure
1
.

2
Self-overlap-corrected quantum Sherrington-Kirkpatrick model

2.1
Parisi formula with classical paths

We look at a simplification to study the quantum AT line. Instead of
Z_{N}
from (
1.5
), we set
\lambda=1
, which may be done without loss of generality, and investigate the so-called self-overlap-corrected path integral

\widehat{Z}_{N}\coloneqq\int\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{1}\beta U\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}(t)\right)dt-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left[U\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}(s)\right)U\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}(t)\right)\right]dsdt\right)\nu_{N}(d\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\xi}}),

(2.1)

and its corresponding random probability measure

\langle(\cdot)\rangle_{N}\coloneqq\widehat{Z}_{N}^{-1}\int(\cdot)\ \exp\left(-\int_{0}^{1}\beta U\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}(t)\right)dt-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left[U\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}(s)\right)U\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}(t)\right)\right]dsdt\right)\nu_{N}(d\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\xi}}).

(2.2)

The difference between the partition function (
1.5
) of the QSK and (
2.1
) lies in the term

\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left[U\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}(s)\right)U\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}(t)\right)\right]dsdt=\frac{N}{2}\left\|Q_{N}[\boldsymbol{\xi}]\right\|_{2}^{2}

which is proportional to the square of the Hilbert-Schmidt norm of the self-overlap

Q_{N}[\boldsymbol{\xi}](t,s)\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\xi_{j}(t)\xi_{j}(s).

(2.3)

The latter is known to concentrate
[
33
]
under the random probability measure (
2.2
), when averaged over the randomness. The following proposition is a strengthening of
[
33
, Cor. 2.4]
, which in turn generalizes the result
[
10
]
for self-overlap-corrected vector spin glasses.

Proposition 2.1
.

1.

The functional

\mathcal{S}_{2}\ni x\mapsto\mathcal{F}(x)\coloneqq\inf_{\pi\in\Pi}\mathcal{P}_{1}(\pi,x)

is convex and Gateaux differentiable, and the
averaged self-overlap
m_{N}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\left[\langle Q_{N}\rangle_{N}\right]
converges in Hilbert-Schmidt norm to the derivative
\varrho\coloneqq\nabla\mathcal{F}(0)
:

\lim_{N\to\infty}\left\|m_{N}-\varrho\right\|_{2}=0.

(2.4)

2.

Under the product of the disorder probability measure and (
2.2
), the self-overlap asymptotically concentrates exponentially on its mean
m_{N}
with respect to the Hilbert-Schmidt norm
in the sense that for any
\varepsilon>0
there is some
c(\varepsilon)>0
such that for all
N
large enough:

\mathbb{E}\left[\big\langle 1[\|Q_{N}-\varrho\|_{2}>\varepsilon]\big\rangle_{N}\right]\leq e^{-c(\varepsilon)N}.

(2.5)

Here and in the following,
1[\dots]
denotes the indicator function.
Since
\|Q_{N}[\boldsymbol{\xi}]\|_{2}\leq{\operatorname{Tr}\,}Q_{N}[\boldsymbol{\xi}]=1
, the strong convergence (
2.4
) and the exponential concentration (
2.5
) in particular imply

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\big\langle\left\|Q_{N}-m_{N}\right\|_{2}\big\rangle_{N}\right]=0,

(2.6)

which converts the weak concentration result in
[
33
, Cor. 2.4]
to a strong convergence.
A proof of Proposition
2.1
is found in Appendix
B.3
. Switching off
U
, under the a priori path measure
\nu_{N}
the typical self-overlap is the Hilbert-Schmidt operator
\mu
given by the kernel

\mu(t,s)\coloneqq\int\xi(t)\xi(s)\nu_{1}(d\xi)=\frac{\cosh\left(\beta b(1-2|t-s|)\right)}{\cosh\beta b}.

(2.7)

The explicit expression for the average follows from a computation, which can be found in
[
25
]
. Generally,
\varrho
differs from
\mu
, but we will show as part of our main result, Theorem
2.3
below, that they coincide in the entire replica-symmetric regime.

The quantum Parisi functional for the self-overlap-corrected SK model simplifies dramatically. Its variational problem reduces from Hilbert-Schmidt-valued paths to classical paths. Moreover, as in its classical analogue
[
5
]
, there is a unique minimizing path within the paths

\Pi(\varrho)\coloneqq\left\{\pi\in\Pi\ |\ \pi(1)=\varrho\right\}

terminating at the limiting value of the self-overlap,
\varrho=\nabla\mathcal{F}(0)
.

Theorem 2.2
.

For the self-overlap-corrected QSK:

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\ \mathbb{E}\left[\ln\widehat{Z}_{N}\right]=\mathcal{F}\left(0\right)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\varrho)}\mathcal{P}_{1}\left(\pi,0\right)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi_{c}(\varrho)}\mathcal{P}_{1}\left(\pi,0\right)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi_{c}}\mathcal{P}_{1}\left(\pi,0\right)

(2.8)

where
\varrho=\nabla\mathcal{F}(0)
and

\displaystyle\Pi_{c}\coloneqq\left\{\pi\in\Pi\ \Big|\ \begin{array}[]{l}\mbox{there is a monotone $\pi_{c}:[0,1)\to[0,\infty)$ such}\\
\mbox{that for Lebesgue a.e.\ $s,t\in[0,1)$:}\end{array}\qquad\pi(m)(t,s)=\pi_{c}(m)\right\},

\displaystyle\Pi_{c}(\varrho)\coloneqq\left\{\pi\in\Pi_{c}\ |\ \mbox{for Lebesgue a.e.\ $s,t\in[0,1)$:}\;\;\pi(1)(s,t)=\langle 1,\varrho 1\rangle\,\right\}.

Here,
1\in L^{2}(0,1)
stands for the constant function one.

There is a unique minimizer
\pi^{*}
of
\inf_{\pi\in\Pi_{c}}\mathcal{P}_{1}\left(\pi,0\right)
, and
\tilde{\pi}^{*}(m):=\pi^{*}(m)\mathbbm{1}_{[0,1)}(m)+\varrho\mathbbm{1}_{\{1\}}(m)\in\Pi_{c}(\varrho)
is the unique minimizer among all paths
\pi\in\Pi(\varrho)
with fixed endpoint.

The proof of Theorem
2.2
is found in Subsection
3.2
.
The variational characterization (
2.8
) strengthens
[
33
, Thm. 2.6]
. In the self-overlap-corrected case, it completely settles the conjectured classical structure of the Parisi order parameter
\pi
for the QSK: the optimal path does not distinguish times
s,t\in[0,1)
, which reflects the time-translation symmetry modulo one of the integrand in (
2.1
). Note that the uniqueness holds not only among all classical paths and is thus stronger than currently known results for Potts models restricted to symmetric self-overlaps
[
11
]
.

2.2
Quantum Almeida-Thouless line

The annealed functional of the self-overlap-corrected QSK, which corresponds to the trivial path
\pi_{c}=0
, also simplifies due to the normalization
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{Z}_{N}]=1
:

0=\mathcal{P}_{1}(0,0)=\ln\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{Z}_{N}\right].

(2.9)

The quantum AT line hence boils down to the
b-T
-values that separate the annealed solution from a non-trivial value of
\pi_{c}
in (
2.8
).
The following main result shows that this occurs at

\displaystyle T(b)\coloneqq\frac{b}{\arctanh(b)},

with
b\in[0,1)
and endpoint at
T(1)\coloneqq 0
, cf. Figure
1
. The theorem also provides a complete description of the behavior of the replica overlap

R_{N}[\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\prime}](t,s)\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\xi_{j}(t)\xi_{j}^{\prime}(s),

which involves two independent copies of paths
\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\prime}
. The onset of replica symmetry breaking is signaled by the expectation value
\langle\langle\|R_{N}\|_{2}^{2}\rangle\rangle_{N}
of the replica overlap’s Hilbert-Schmidt norm under the duplication of the Gibbs measure, i.e., the product of two independent copies of the random probability measure in (
2.2
).

Theorem 2.3
.

1.

If
b\geq\tanh(\beta b)
, then the pressure agrees with the annealed pressure,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\ \mathbb{E}\left[\ln\widehat{Z}_{N}\right]=0.

(2.10)

In this regime, the replica overlap vanishes,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\langle\|R_{N}\|_{2}^{2}\rangle\rangle_{N}\right]=0,

(2.11)

and
\varrho=\mu
, i.e., the self-overlap concentrates on the self-overlap (
2.7
) of the a priori measure.

2.

If
b<\tanh(\beta b)
, then the annealed solution is unstable:

\lim_{N\to\infty}\ \frac{1}{N}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\ln\widehat{Z}_{N}\right]<0=\lim_{N\to\infty}\ \frac{1}{N}\,\ln\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{Z}_{N}\right].

(2.12)

In this glass phase, the replica overlap is not self-averaging,

\liminf_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\langle\|R_{N}\|_{2}^{2}\rangle\rangle_{N}\right]>0.

(2.13)

The proof of this main result rests on a combination of techniques: 1. A conditional second moment technique is applied to the self-overlap-constrained model discussed below. This part relates to a quantum Hopfield model discussed in Section
4
, which also contains the proof of the first part of Theorem
2.3
. 2. A quantum Toninelli argument is used for the second part of the proof of Theorem
2.3
, which is found in Section
5
. 3. Statistical mechanics methods based on a Parisi formula for the self-overlap-constraint model are developed for the determination of the order parameter.

Theorem
2.3
yields a complete description of the phase diagram in the self-overlap-corrected QSK. Since we have information about the order parameter in all phases, including the critical line, the result even outmatches what is currently known for the classical SK model in a longitudinal magnetic field
[
26
]
.

The phase diagram predicted by Theorem
2.3
should be compared to that of the original QSK model. The quantum AT lines are expected to look similar, and, in particular, end at critical points on the
T=0
axis, which, however, are expected to be different, see Figure
1
. At present, in contrast to the self-overlap-corrected model, replica symmetry has not yet been proved in the full QSK anywhere in the region
T<1
.

T(b)=b/\arctanh(b)

) and the replica overlap concentrates around

. The figure on the right is taken from
[

and displays the phase diagram of the original QSK model (at
\lambda=1
). The dotted red line indicates the QAT line based on numerical computations. Its qualitative nature is remarkably similar to that in the self-overlap-corrected model. In the blue region
T\geq 1
, replica symmetry has been established, whereas the persistence of the glass phase has been shown in the marked red region.

2.3
Self-overlap-constrained QSK

The self-overlap-corrected QSK is intimately related to the self-overlap-constrained QSK. For the latter, one picks a target self-overlap
\upsilon\in\mathcal{S}_{2}^{+}
and constrains the path measure
\nu_{N}
to the set

\mathcal{Q}_{N,\varepsilon}(\upsilon)\coloneqq\left\{\boldsymbol{\xi}\ |\ \left\|Q_{N}[\boldsymbol{\xi}]-\upsilon\right\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon\right\}.

(2.14)

To deduce information about the order parameter from thermodynamic information, we study the following family of constraint partition functions

Z_{N,\varepsilon}(\lambda;\upsilon)\coloneqq\int_{\mathcal{Q}_{N,\varepsilon}(\upsilon)}\mkern-10.0mu\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{1}\lambda\beta U\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}(t)\right)dt\right)\nu_{N}(d\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\xi}})

(2.15)

with parameter
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. This quantity still depends on
\beta b
, which we suppress in the notation.

Self-overlap-constrained models as in (
2.15
) have been considered in Panchenko’s proof of the Parisi formula for vector-spin glasses
[
39
]
. In our derivation of a Parisi formula for the QSK and related models
[
33
]
, they did not appear. In fact, the proof in
[
39
]
relies on a covering argument based on compactness to fix the self-overlap, which does not apply to infinite-dimensional vector spin glasses such as the QSK. For a fixed
\upsilon\in\mathcal{S}_{2}^{+}
and any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, one may nevertheless show that the shifted Legendre transform

F(\lambda;\upsilon)\coloneqq\inf_{x\in\mathcal{S}_{2}}\left[\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\upsilon)}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\pi,x)-\langle x,\upsilon\rangle\right]+\frac{\lambda^{2}\beta^{2}}{4}\left\|\upsilon\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(2.16)

yields a
description for the limiting pressure. Before spelling this in the subsequent proposition, we record the following properties of
F(\lambda;\upsilon)
that are immediate from its definition:

1.

Applications of Jensen’s inequality yield upper and lower bounds

F(0;\upsilon)\leq F(\lambda;\upsilon)\leq F(0;\upsilon)+\frac{\lambda^{2}\beta^{2}}{4}\left\|\upsilon\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{\lambda^{2}\beta^{2}}{4}\left\|\upsilon\right\|_{2}^{2},

(2.17)

in terms of the rate function
F(0;\upsilon)=\inf_{x\in\mathcal{S}_{2}}\mathcal{P}_{0}(0,x)-\langle x,\upsilon\rangle
of observing the self-overlap
\upsilon
under the a priori measure
\nu_{N}
(as described by the Gärtner-Ellis theorem). The rate function
F(0;\upsilon)
takes values in
[-\infty,\infty)
, and (
2.17
) shows that
F(0;\upsilon)>-\infty
if and only if
F(\lambda;\upsilon)>-\infty
.

2.

Since
\mathcal{S}_{2}\ni x\mapsto\mathcal{F}(x)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\varrho)}\mathcal{P}_{1}(\pi,x)
is convex and Gateaux-differentiable by Proposition
2.1
, properties of the Legendre transform imply that at
\varrho=\nabla\mathcal{F}(0)
:

F(1;\varrho)=\mathcal{F}(0)+\frac{\beta^{2}}{4}\left\|\varrho\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(2.18)

This provides the link to the variational description (
2.8
) of the self-overlap-corrected model.

The variational description of the pressure of the self-overlap constraint model, which generalizes results in
[
39
]
, is addressed in

Proposition 2.4
.

For any fixed self-overlap
\upsilon\in\mathcal{S}_{2}^{+}
at which
F(0;\upsilon)>-\infty
, and any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, the constrained pressure converges in the following sense

F(\lambda;\upsilon)=\liminf_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\ln Z_{N,\varepsilon}(\lambda;\upsilon)=\limsup_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\ln Z_{N,\varepsilon}(\lambda;\upsilon).

(2.19)

Furthermore:

1.

the function
\lambda\mapsto F(\lambda;\upsilon)
is convex and differentiable.

2.

there exists a null sequence
\varepsilon_{N}^{0}\to 0
, which is independent of
\lambda
, such that for each further null sequence
\varepsilon_{N}\geq\varepsilon_{N}^{0}
in an open neighborhood of
\lambda
:

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\ln Z_{N,\varepsilon_{N}}(\lambda;\upsilon)=F(\lambda;\upsilon).

(2.20)

The proof of this proposition, on which the characterization of the order parameter in the spin-glass phase is based, is found in Subsection
3.4
.

Under the constraint on the self-overlap, one may include in the exponential in (
2.15
) the self-overlap correcting term, which then changes the limits (
2.19
) predictably. In particular, in case
F(0;\upsilon)>-\infty
, this implies that the two-parameter family

W_{N,\varepsilon}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2};\upsilon)\coloneqq\int_{\mathcal{Q}_{N,\varepsilon}(\upsilon)}\mkern-10.0mu\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{1}\lambda_{1}\beta U\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}(t)\right)dt-N\frac{\lambda_{2}^{2}\beta^{2}}{4}\left\|Q_{N}[\boldsymbol{\xi}]\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)\nu_{N}(d\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\xi}}),

(2.21)

has a pressure

\widehat{F}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2};\upsilon)=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\ln W_{N,\varepsilon}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2};\upsilon),

with limits understood as in Proposition
2.4
, which agrees
with

\widehat{F}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2};\upsilon)\coloneqq F(\lambda_{1};\upsilon)-\frac{\lambda_{2}^{2}\beta^{2}}{4}\|\upsilon\|_{2}^{2}.

One of the benefits of considering the augmented quantity,
\ln W_{N,\varepsilon}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2};\upsilon)
, is that this quantity is evidently convex in
\lambda_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
. This will allow us to pass identities for derivatives with respect to
\lambda_{1}
to their limits.
The derivative of
\ln W_{N,\varepsilon}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2};\upsilon)
involves the Gibbs measure associated with (
2.21
), which we will abbreviate by

\langle(\cdot)\rangle_{N,\varepsilon;\upsilon}^{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}\coloneqq W_{N,\varepsilon}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2};\upsilon)^{-1}\int_{\mathcal{Q}_{N,\varepsilon}(\upsilon)}(\cdot)\ \exp\left(-\int_{0}^{1}\lambda_{1}\beta U\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}(t)\right)dt-\frac{N\lambda_{2}^{2}\beta^{2}}{4}\left\|Q_{N}[\boldsymbol{\xi}]\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)\nu_{N}(d\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\xi}}),

(2.22)

as well as its duplicated version, which we denote by
\langle\langle(\cdot)\rangle\rangle_{N,\varepsilon;\upsilon}^{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}
.
A fairly standard argument using Gaussian integration by parts (cf.
[
38
, Ch. 1.2]
) yields the following identity

\frac{1}{N}\ \frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{1}}\mathbb{E}\left[\ln W_{N,\varepsilon}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2};\upsilon)\right]=\frac{\lambda_{1}\beta^{2}}{2}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\left\|Q_{N}\right\|_{2}^{2}\rangle_{N,\varepsilon;\upsilon}^{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\langle\left\|R_{N}\right\|_{2}^{2}\rangle\rangle_{N,\varepsilon;\upsilon}^{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}\right]\right).

(2.23)

Up to
\varepsilon
, the first term in the bracket on the right is
\|\upsilon\|_{2}^{2}
. We will show that such relations carry over to the limit. This will be crucial for the proof of (
2.13
), i.e., that the replica overlap is non-trivial in the spin glass regime.

Theorem 2.5
.

There is a minimizer
\pi^{*}\in\Pi
of
\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\varrho)}\mathcal{P}_{1}(\pi,0)
such that at
\varrho=\nabla\mathcal{F}(0)
:

\frac{\partial\widehat{F}}{\partial\lambda_{1}}(1,1;\varrho)=\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\left(\|\varrho\|_{2}^{2}-\int_{0}^{1}\|\pi^{*}(s)\|^{2}_{2}\,ds\right).

(2.24)

The proof is found in Subsection
3.5
.

The proof of Theorem
2.3
,
in the replica-symmetric case, is based on a conditional second-moment analysis. The main idea is that, in the replica symmetric regime, the self-overlap-corrected QSK is equivalent to a constrained QSK in which one restricts the self-overlap to
the typical self-overlap
\mu
under the a priori path measure
\nu_{N}
.
The following theorem will then imply (
2.10
) and (
2.11
).

Theorem 2.6
.

For any
0<\lambda_{1}\leq\frac{b}{\tanh(\beta b)}
and all
\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\widehat{F}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2};\mu)=\frac{\lambda_{1}^{2}-\lambda_{2}^{2}}{4}\ \beta^{2}\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2},

(2.25)

\displaystyle\limsup_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\langle\left\|R_{N}\right\|_{2}^{2}\rangle\rangle_{N,\varepsilon;\mu}^{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}\right]=0.

(2.26)

Proof.

The proof of (
2.25
) is the topic of Section
4
, where it is found in Subsection
4.3
. We now show how this and the identity (
2.23
) imply (
2.26
).

Consider first the case
0<\lambda_{1}<\frac{b}{\tanh(\beta b)}.

The first term on the right side in (
2.23
) with
\upsilon=\mu
is estimated from above by
\frac{\lambda_{1}\beta^{2}}{2}\left(\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}+\varepsilon\right)
. Due to convexity, one may interchange the limits and the differentiation to conclude from (
2.25
):

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\ \frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{1}}\mathbb{E}\left[\ln W_{N,\varepsilon}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2};\mu)\right]=\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{1}}\frac{\lambda_{1}^{2}-\lambda_{2}^{2}}{4}\ \beta^{2}\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{\lambda_{1}\beta^{2}}{2}\|\mu\|^{2}_{2}.

This completes the proof for
0<\lambda_{1}<\frac{b}{\tanh(\beta b)}.

On the critical line
\lambda_{1}=\frac{b}{\tanh(\beta b)}
, we need a few modifications. We fix
\lambda_{2}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}
and employ Proposition
2.4
, which guarantees the existence of a null sequence
\varepsilon_{N}^{0}
such that for every null sequence
\varepsilon_{N}\geq\varepsilon_{N}^{0}
the coupled limit
\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\ln W_{N,\varepsilon_{N}}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2};\mu)\right]
exists in an open neighborhood of
(\frac{b}{\tanh(\beta b)},\lambda_{2}^{0})
. By Proposition
2.4
, its limit is convex and differentiable in
\lambda_{1}
. Moreover, for
\lambda_{1}\leq\frac{b}{\tanh(\beta b)}=:\lambda_{1}^{0}
the limit agrees with the right side in (
2.25
). Hence, we still have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\ \frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{1}}\mathbb{E}\left[\ln W_{N,\varepsilon_{N}}\big(\lambda_{1}^{0},\lambda_{2}^{0};\mu\big)\right]=\frac{b}{\tanh(\beta b)}\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\|\mu\|^{2}_{2},

and applying again the identity (
2.23
) with
\upsilon=\mu
, we deduce

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\langle\left\|R_{N}\right\|_{2}^{2}\rangle\rangle_{N,\varepsilon_{N};\mu}^{(\lambda_{1}^{0},\lambda_{2}^{0})}\right]=0

for the coupled limit of the replica overlap. To remove the coupling, we proceed via contradiction and assume

\limsup_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\langle\left\|R_{N}\right\|_{2}^{2}\rangle\rangle_{N,\varepsilon;\mu}^{(\lambda_{1}^{0},\lambda_{2}^{0})}\right]=a>0.

Then one can extract a coupled null sequence
\varepsilon_{N}\geq\varepsilon_{N}^{0}
for which
\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\langle\left\|R_{N}\right\|_{2}^{2}\rangle\rangle_{N,\varepsilon_{N};\mu}^{(\lambda_{1}^{0},\lambda_{2}^{0})}\right]=a
, which contradicts our prior consideration.
∎

The rest of the paper is dedicated to the proofs of the results discussed in this section.

3
Approximations and proof of Parisi formulae

We start by gathering basic results on the approximation of the QSK by a finite-dimensional vector-spin glass. These allow for the transfer of known results on vector-spin glasses
[
39
,
9
]
to the case of quantum glasses as was done in
[
33
]
. We also spell out the proofs of the simplified Parisi formula,
Theorem
2.2
, as well as the one for the constrained model, Proposition
2.4
.

3.1
Vector-spin glass approximation of the self-overlap-corrected QSK

Proceeding as in
[
33
]
, we introduce the square-wave pulses

e_{k}\coloneqq 2^{D/2}\operatorname{1}_{(t_{k-1},t_{k}]}

(3.1)

which correspond to a dyadic decomposition
t_{k}\coloneqq k2^{-D}
,
k\in\{1,\dots,2^{D}\}
, of the unit time interval. They are orthonormal in the real Hilbert space
L^{2}(0,1]
and we abbreviate the orthogonal projection on their span by
P_{D}\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{2^{D}}|e_{k}\rangle\langle e_{k}|
. The projection of any path to this subspace is

\xi_{j}^{D}(s)\coloneqq\left(P_{D}\xi_{j}\right)(s)=\sum_{k=1}^{2^{D}}\xi_{j}(k)\operatorname{1}_{(t_{k-1},t_{k}]}(s),\qquad\xi_{j}(k)\coloneqq 2^{D}\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\xi_{j}(s)ds=2^{D/2}\langle e_{k},\xi_{j}\rangle.

Abbreviating
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{D}\coloneqq(\xi_{1}^{D},\dots,\xi_{N}^{D})
, the partition function

\widehat{Z}_{N}^{D}\coloneqq\int\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{1}\beta U\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{D}(t)\right)dt-N\frac{\beta^{2}}{4}\big\|Q_{N}[\boldsymbol{\xi}^{D}]\big\|_{2}^{2}\right)\nu_{N}(d\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\xi}}),

(3.2)

is that of a self-overlap-corrected vector-spin glass of SK-type.
Its corresponding random probability measure is given by

\langle(\cdot)\rangle_{N}^{D}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\widehat{Z}_{N}^{D}}\int(\cdot)\ \exp\left(-\int_{0}^{1}\beta U\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{D}(t)\right)dt-N\frac{\beta^{2}}{4}\big\|Q_{N}[\boldsymbol{\xi}^{D}]\big\|_{2}^{2}\right)\nu_{N}(d\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\xi}}).

(3.3)

The following summarizes the results on the Parisi variational formula for the pressure of such vector-spin glasses:

1.

The Parisi functional corresponding to (
3.2
) is given by the restriction of
\mathcal{P}_{1}
from (
1.6
) to matrix-valued paths
\Pi^{D}
and matrices
x\in\mathcal{S}_{2}^{D}\coloneqq\{x\in\mathcal{S}_{2}\ |\ x=P_{D}xP_{D}\}
. Since
\left(\mathcal{S}_{2}^{+}\right)^{D}=\mathcal{S}_{2}^{+}\cap\mathcal{S}_{2}^{D}
, one may take
\Pi^{D}=\{P_{D}\pi P_{D}\ |\ \pi\in\Pi\}
and identify

\mathcal{P}^{D}_{1}(\pi,x)=\mathcal{P}_{1}(P_{D}\pi P_{D},P_{D}xP_{D})
.
The functional

\mathcal{F}^{D}(x)\coloneqq\inf_{\pi\in\Pi^{D}}\mathcal{P}^{D}(\pi,x)

which is again first defined on matrices
x
and then trivially extended to
\mathcal{S}_{2}
, is convex and Gateaux-differentiable. The derivative
\varrho^{D}\coloneqq\nabla\mathcal{F}^{D}(0)
marks the asymptotic value, on which the self-overlap concentrates under the averaged measure (
3.3
), i.e.

\lim_{N\to\infty}\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\langle Q_{N}[\boldsymbol{\xi}^{D}]\rangle_{N}^{D}\right]-\varrho^{D}\right\|_{2}=0,

(3.4)

see
[
10
, Thm. 1.1]
and note that weak and strong convergence are equivalent as long as
D
is finite. By the same proof technique as in Proposition
2.1
, one may boost this result to exponential concentration.

2.

The pressure is expressed as the following two equivalent Parisi variational principles
[
9
,
10
]
:

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\ln\widehat{Z}_{N}^{D}\right]=\mathcal{F}^{D}(0)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi^{D}(\varrho^{D})}\mathcal{P}^{D}(\pi,0),

(3.5)

where the last infimum restricts the paths
\Pi^{D}
to the endpoint
\varrho^{D}
.

The following two approximation results have been established in
[
33
]
:

1.

The pressure converges
[
33
, Prop. 3.2]
:

\lim_{D\to\infty}\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\ln\widehat{Z}_{N}^{D}-\ln\widehat{Z}_{N}\right|\right]=0.

(3.6)

2.

The Lipschitz continuity (
1.9
) implies
\lim_{D\to\infty}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(P_{D}\pi P_{D},P_{D}xP_{D})=\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\pi,x)
for all
\pi\in\Pi
and
x\in\mathcal{S}_{2}
.
One even has
[
33
, Lemma 3.7]

\lim_{D\to\infty}\mathcal{F}^{D}(P_{D}xP_{D})=\mathcal{F}(x).

(3.7)

For a proof of Theorems
2.2
and
2.5
, we need the following extension of these approximation results.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. Then:

\lim_{D\to\infty}\inf_{\pi\in\Pi^{D}(\varrho^{D})}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}^{D}(\pi,0)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\varrho)}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\pi,0).

(3.8)

1.

The limiting self-overlaps converge strongly as
D\to\infty
:

\lim_{D\to\infty}\left\|\varrho^{D}-\varrho\right\|_{2}=0.

(3.9)

2.

Any sequence of minimizers
\pi^{D}_{*}
of
\inf_{\pi\in\Pi^{D}(\varrho^{D})}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}^{D}(\pi,0)
is relatively compact in
L^{2}([0,1],\mathcal{S}_{2}^{+})
and any limiting point
\pi_{*}
satisfies

\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\varrho)}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\pi,0)=\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\pi_{*},0)

The proof is postponed to Appendix
C.1
.

3.2
Proof of the simplified Parisi formula

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

The first equality in (
2.8
) has been established in
[
33
, Thm. 2.3]
. Since
\mathcal{F}(0)=\lim_{D\to\infty}\mathcal{F}^{D}(0)
as a special case of (
3.7
), the second equality in (
2.8
) follows by combining the last identity in (
3.5
) and (
3.8
). The last equality in (
2.8
) will follow from

\inf_{\pi\in\Pi_{c}(\varrho)}\mathcal{P}_{1}(\pi,0)\geq\inf_{\pi\in\Pi_{c}}\mathcal{P}_{1}(\pi,0)\geq\inf_{\pi\in\Pi}\mathcal{P}_{1}(\pi,0)=\mathcal{F}(0),

the previously proven identities and the third identity (
2.8
), i.e.

\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\varrho)}\mathcal{P}_{1}(\pi,0)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi_{c}(\varrho)}\mathcal{P}_{1}(\pi,0).

It hence remains to establish this equality.

The empirical self-overlap averaged with respect to the random Gibbs measure (
2.2
) is invariant under time translations,

\langle Q_{N}(t,s)\rangle_{N}=\langle Q_{N}(t+\tau,s+\tau)\rangle_{N}

for any
\tau\in[0,1)
, where additions are understood modulo one. This follows from the time-translation invariance of the a priori measure
\nu_{N}
. Since
\lim_{N\to\infty}\left\|\mathbb{E}\langle Q_{N}\rangle_{N}-\varrho\right\|_{2}=0
, as a consequence of Proposition
2.1
, the limit
\varrho
inherits the symmetry and hence equals its time average
\overline{\varrho}
given by

\overline{\varrho}(t,s)=\int_{0}^{1}\varrho(t+\tau,s+\tau)\ d\tau.

We may thus apply
[
33
, Thm. 2.6]
to conclude that a minimizer in the variational problem
\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\varrho)}\mathcal{P}(\pi,0)
is found within the set of paths

\Pi_{2}:=\left\{\pi\in\Pi(\varrho)\ \Big|\ \begin{array}[]{rl}\mbox{There are $\kappa,\lambda:[0,1]\to[0,1]$ non-decreasing, right-}&\quad\pi(m)=\kappa(m)\ \varrho^{\perp}\\
\mbox{continuous with $\kappa(0)=\lambda(0)=0$, $\kappa(1)=\lambda(1)=1$:}&\qquad+\lambda(m)\ \langle 1,\varrho\ 1\rangle\ |1\rangle\langle 1|\end{array}\right\}.

Here
\varrho^{\perp}
is defined through the spectral decomposition using the time-translation invariance, which yields
\varrho=\varrho^{\perp}+\langle 1,\varrho\ 1\rangle\ |1\rangle\langle 1|
, where the symbol
|1\rangle\langle 1|
stands for the rank-one projection onto the constant function
1\in L^{2}(0,1)
.

Within the above class, we may now use standard Gaussian interpolation to show that the infimum corresponds to
\kappa(m)=0
for all
m\in[0,1)
.
For a proof, consider two monotone step functions

\lambda,\kappa:[0,1]\to[0,1]
, and let

(\mu^{\lambda,\kappa}_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{r}}

stand for the weights of the Ruelle probability cascade
[
38
]
corresponding to a common (refined) partition
0=:m_{0}<m_{1}<\dots<m_{r-1}<m_{r}:=1
of the jump points of
\lambda,\kappa
.
Let
w_{\alpha}^{\lambda}
stand for a family of centered Gaussian random variables with covariance

\mathbb{E}\left[w_{\alpha}^{\lambda}w_{\alpha^{\prime}}^{\lambda}\right]=\lambda(\alpha\wedge\alpha^{\prime})\ \langle 1,\varrho 1\rangle,\quad\mbox{with}\quad\alpha\wedge\alpha^{\prime}\coloneqq\min\left\{0\leq j\leq r\ \big|\ \alpha_{j}=\alpha^{\prime}_{j}\;\mbox{and}\;\alpha_{j+1}\neq\alpha^{\prime}_{j+1}\right\}.

Moreover, let
w_{\alpha}^{\kappa}
stand for an independent family of

L^{2}
-valued, centered Gaussian processes with covariance given by the kernel

\mathbb{E}\left[w_{\alpha}^{\kappa}(t)w_{\alpha^{\prime}}^{\kappa}(s)\right]=\kappa(\alpha\wedge\alpha^{\prime})\ \varrho^{\perp}(t,s).

By construction of the Ruelle probability cascade, the function

\displaystyle f(p):=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\ln\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{r}}\mu^{\lambda,\kappa}_{\alpha}\!\int\exp\Big(\beta w_{\alpha}^{\lambda}\ \langle\xi,1\rangle-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\ \langle 1,\xi\rangle^{2}\ \langle 1,\varrho 1\rangle+\beta\sqrt{p}\langle\xi,w_{\alpha}^{\kappa}\rangle-\frac{\beta^{2}p}{2}\langle\xi,\varrho^{\perp}\xi\rangle\Big)\nu_{1}(d\xi)\bigg],

interpolates for
p\in[0,1]
between
X_{0}(\lambda\langle 1,\varrho 1\rangle|1\rangle\langle 1|,0)=f(0)
and
X_{0}(\lambda\langle 1,\varrho 1\rangle|1\rangle\langle 1|+\kappa\varrho^{\perp},0)=f(1)
.
Let
\langle(\cdot)\rangle_{p}
denote the (random) Gibbs expectation value associated with the partition function defined by the logarithm, and
\langle\langle(\cdot)\rangle\rangle_{r}
be the Gibbs expectation value of the corresponding duplicated system.
Then straightforward differentiation and Gaussian integration by parts yield

f^{\prime}(p)=-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\ \mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\left\langle\kappa(m_{\alpha\wedge\alpha^{\prime}})\ \langle\xi,\varrho^{\perp}\xi^{\prime}\rangle\right\rangle\right\rangle_{r}\right],

(3.10)

where the variables with and without the prime refer to the first and second system in the duplication.
Note that the term
\frac{\beta^{2}}{4}\ \mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\langle\xi,\varrho^{\perp}\xi\rangle\right\rangle_{r}\right]
, which appears in the Gaussian integration by parts, cancels due to the presence of this term in the exponent.
Since the measure
\nu_{1}
is invariant under time shifts
\xi\to\xi(\cdot+\tau)
with additions modulo one and
\tau\in[0,1]
, and so are the terms

\langle\xi,1\rangle=\langle\xi(\cdot+\tau),1\rangle
as well as
\langle\xi,\varrho^{\perp}\xi\rangle=\langle\xi(\cdot+\tau),\varrho^{\perp}\xi(\cdot+\tau)\rangle
, we hence conclude that for any pair
\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in[0,1)
:

f^{\prime}(p)=-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\ \mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\left\langle\kappa(m_{\alpha\wedge\alpha^{\prime}})\ \langle\xi(\cdot+\tau),\varrho^{\perp}\xi^{\prime}(\cdot+\tau^{\prime})\rangle\right\rangle\right\rangle_{r}\right].

Averaging over
\tau,\tau^{\prime}
with respect to the Lebesgue measure and using the fact that

\int_{0}^{1}\xi(t+\tau)d\tau=\langle\xi,1\rangle,

which is constant in
t
and hence orthogonal to the range of
\varrho^{\perp}
, we hence conclude that
f^{\prime}(p)=0
for all
p\in[0,1]
. This implies that

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{1}\left(\lambda\langle 1,\varrho 1\rangle|1\rangle\langle 1|+\kappa\varrho^{\perp},0\right)

\displaystyle\quad=X_{0}\left(\lambda\langle 1,\varrho 1\rangle|1\rangle\langle 1|+\kappa\varrho^{\perp},0\right)+\frac{\beta^{2}}{4}\|\varrho^{\perp}\|_{2}^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\kappa(m)^{2}dm+\frac{\beta^{2}}{4}\langle 1,\varrho 1\rangle^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\lambda(m)^{2}dm

\displaystyle\quad\geq X_{0}\left(\lambda\langle 1,\varrho 1\rangle|1\rangle\langle 1|,0\right)+\frac{\beta^{2}}{4}\langle 1,\varrho 1\rangle^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\lambda(m)^{2}dm=\mathcal{P}_{1}\left(\lambda\langle 1,\varrho 1\rangle|1\rangle\langle 1|,0\right)

(3.11)

and completes the proof of (
2.8
).

Suppose
\pi_{*}\in\Pi(\varrho)
is a minimizer. Consulting the proof of
[
33
, Thm. 2.6]
, we note that
\pi_{*}
needs to agree almost everywhere with a path from
\Pi_{2}
as otherwise Jensen’s inequality underlying this argument becomes strict. Due to right-continuity and the fixed endpoint, we deduce that
\pi_{*}\in\Pi_{2}
. Similarly, the above proof also yields a strict inequality unless
\kappa(m)=0
almost everywhere. Hence,
\pi_{*}\in\Pi_{c}
, and it only remains to show uniqueness within the set of classical paths. The claimed uniqueness of the minimizer in
\Pi_{c}
follows from a straightforward application of the technique in
[
5
]
, which establishes strict convexity of the Parisi functional when restricted to classical paths.
∎

3.3
Approximations for self-overlap-constrained models

For
\upsilon\in\left(\mathcal{S}_{2}^{+}\right)^{D}
, we define

\mathcal{Q}^{D}_{N,\varepsilon}(\upsilon)\coloneqq\left\{\boldsymbol{\xi}\ |\ \left\|Q_{N}[\boldsymbol{\xi}^{D}]-\upsilon\right\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon\right\}

and the approximand of the self-overlap constraint partition function

Z_{N,\varepsilon}^{D}(\lambda;\upsilon)\coloneqq\int_{\mathcal{Q}^{D}_{N,\varepsilon}(\upsilon)}\mkern-10.0mu\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{1}\lambda\beta U\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{D}(t)\right)dt\right)\nu_{N}(d\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\xi}}).

(3.12)

The Parisi functional corresponding to the constraint partition function builds on the restriction of
\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\pi,x)
to matrix-valued paths
\pi\in\Pi^{D}(\upsilon)

Remark 3.3

1. Convexity is evident from the convexity of the prelimit. Since the constrained Parisi formula represents the quantity as an infimum, the differentiability in

The previous proof relied on the following observation,
which allows us to map a path
\pi\in\Pi(\varrho)
to another path
\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi(\varrho^{\prime})
with a different endpoint in a continuous manner.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\pi\in\Pi(\varrho)
and
\varrho,\varrho^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{2}^{+}
. Then, there exists a path
\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi(\varrho^{\prime})
such that

\sup_{m\in[0,1]}\|\pi(m)-\pi^{\prime}(m)\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\|\varrho-\varrho^{\prime}\|_{2}}\big(\sqrt{\|\varrho\|_{2}}+\sqrt{\|\varrho^{\prime}\|_{2}}\big)

(3.25)

Proof.

For
\delta>0
, we define the family of operators

C_{\delta}(m):=(\varrho+\delta)^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\pi(m)+\delta)(\varrho+\delta)^{-\frac{1}{2}},\quad 0<m<1,\quad C_{\delta}(0):=0,\,C_{\delta}(1):=\mathbbm{1},

where addition by
\delta
is understood as
\delta\mathbbm{1}
throughout.
Since
\varrho+\delta\geq\delta>0
, the inverse square roots are well defined by spectral calculus. Consulting the quadratic form, one easily verifies that these operators are non-negative contractions, which are monotone by the monotonicity of the path
\pi

0\leq C_{\delta}(m^{\prime})\leq C_{\delta}(m)\leq\mathbbm{1}\quad\mbox{for all $0\leq m^{\prime}\leq m\leq 1$.}

(3.26)

By the Banach-Alaoglu theorem, the unit ball of

B(L^{2}(0,1))
is compact in the weak operator topology. Hence, there is a sequence
\delta_{n}\downarrow 0
and a family of non-negative contractions
C(m)
for which the quadratic form
C_{\delta_{n}}(m)
converges – first, for all
m
in a countable dense set of
[0,1]
, and then uniquely extended to all
m\in[0,1]
by the monotonicity (
3.26
), which is inherited by the limit. The distorted path
\pi^{\prime}
is then defined as

\pi^{\prime}(m):=(\varrho^{\prime})^{\frac{1}{2}}C(m)(\varrho^{\prime})^{\frac{1}{2}}

and by construction
\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi(\varrho^{\prime}).
By Hölder’s inequality for Schatten

\|\cdot\|_{p}
, we have

\displaystyle\|\pi^{\prime}(m)-\pi(m)\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\|\sqrt{\varrho^{\prime}}C(m)\sqrt{\varrho^{\prime}}-\sqrt{\varrho}C(m)\sqrt{\varrho}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|(\varrho^{\prime})^{\frac{1}{2}}C(m)\|_{4}\|\sqrt{\varrho^{\prime}}-\sqrt{\varrho}\|_{4}+\|\sqrt{\varrho}C(m)\|_{4}\|\sqrt{\varrho^{\prime}}-\sqrt{\varrho}\|_{4}

\displaystyle\leq\|\sqrt{\varrho^{\prime}}-\sqrt{\varrho}\|_{4}(\|\sqrt{\varrho^{\prime}}\|_{4}+\|\sqrt{\varrho}\|_{4})=\|\sqrt{\varrho^{\prime}}-\sqrt{\varrho}\|_{4}(\sqrt{\|\varrho\|_{2}}+\sqrt{\|\varrho^{\prime}\|_{2}}),

where we used that
C(m)
is a contraction. It remains to bound
\|\sqrt{\varrho^{\prime}}-\sqrt{\varrho}\|_{4}
. To this end we set
D\coloneqq\sqrt{\varrho^{\prime}}-\sqrt{\varrho}
and
S\coloneqq\sqrt{\varrho^{\prime}}+\sqrt{\varrho}
and note
S+D,S-D\geq 0
. Let us denote by
d_{\alpha}
and
\psi_{\alpha}
the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the compact operator
D
. With the corresponding matrix elements

we obtain

\displaystyle\|\varrho-\varrho^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{4}\|SD+DS\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}|d_{\alpha}+d_{\beta}|^{2}|S_{\alpha\beta}|^{2}\geq\sum_{\alpha}d_{\alpha}^{2}S_{\alpha\alpha}^{2}\geq\sum_{\alpha}d_{\alpha}^{4}=\|\sqrt{\varrho^{\prime}}-\sqrt{\varrho}\|^{4}_{4},

where the last inequality uses
S\pm D\geq 0
which implies
S_{\alpha\alpha}\geq|d_{\alpha}|
. This completes the proof.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let

be a sequence of convex functions with pointwise limit
G
. Let
F
be a further differentiable convex function. Suppose that
G_{n}^{\prime}(0)
converges as

and that

G
is convex and thus the left and right derivatives exist and satisfy
G^{\prime}_{-}(0)\leq G^{\prime}_{+}(0)
. Considering difference quotients, the convexity implies
G^{\prime}_{-}(0)\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}G_{n}^{\prime}(0)\leq G^{\prime}_{+}(0).
The conditions
G(x)\leq F(x)
and
G(0)=F(0)
further yield
G^{\prime}_{-}(0)\geq F^{\prime}(0)
and
G^{\prime}_{+}(0)\leq F^{\prime}(0)
. This is only possible if
G^{\prime}_{-}(0)=F^{\prime}(0)=G^{\prime}_{+}(0)
, that is
F^{\prime}(0)=\lim_{n\to\infty}G_{n}^{\prime}(0)
.

0<\lambda\leq\frac{b}{\tanh(\beta b)}
:

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\ \mathbb{E}\left[\ln W_{N,\varepsilon}(\lambda,\lambda;\mu)\right]=0.

(4.1)

The main idea for a proof of this is the second moment test. Instead of carrying out the second moment argument directly for
\ln W_{N,\varepsilon}(\lambda,\lambda;\mu)
, we
carry the argument for the discretized version using the projected paths

\boldsymbol{\xi}^{D}=(\xi_{1}^{D},\dots,\xi_{N}^{D})
from Subsection

W_{N,\varepsilon}^{D}(\lambda)\coloneqq\int_{\mathcal{Q}_{N,\varepsilon}(\mu)}\mkern-10.0mu\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{1}\lambda\beta U\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{D}(t)\right)dt-\frac{N\lambda^{2}\beta^{2}}{4}\left\|Q_{N}[\boldsymbol{\xi}^{D}]\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)\nu_{N}(d\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\xi}}).

(4.2)

A straightforward generalization of
[

shows that, in the thermodynamic limit
N\to\infty
, when the discretization parameter
D
goes to infinity subsequently, the pressure corresponding to
W_{N,\varepsilon}(\lambda,\lambda)
is well approximated by that of
W_{N,\varepsilon}^{D}(\lambda)
.

Proposition 4.1
.

[

For any
\varepsilon>0
and all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
:

\limsup_{D\to\infty}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\ln W_{N,\varepsilon}(\lambda,\lambda)-\ln W_{N,\varepsilon}^{D}(\lambda)\right]\right|=0.

To carry out the second moment analysis for
W_{N,\varepsilon}^{D}(\lambda)
, we first note that its average is close to one for any
D
in the thermodynamic limit. This follows from

\mathbb{E}\left[W_{N,\varepsilon}^{D}(\lambda)\right]=\nu_{N}\left(\mathcal{Q}_{N,\varepsilon}(\mu)\right)=1-\nu_{N}\left(\left\|Q_{N}-\mu\right\|_{2}>\varepsilon\right),

(4.3)

together with the following concentration result.

Proposition 4.2
.

For any
N\in\mathbbm{N}
and
\varepsilon>0
:

\nu_{N}\left(\left\|Q_{N}-\mu\right\|_{2}>\varepsilon\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{3N}{2}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{3\sigma^{2}+2\varepsilon}\right)

(4.4)

with

This results from an application of Pinelis’s concentration

of independent vectors in a real Hilbert space. We spell it out in Appendix

The second moment method applied to
W_{N,\varepsilon}^{D}(\lambda)
also requires an upper bound on

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[W_{N,\varepsilon}^{D}(\lambda)^{2}\right]=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{Q}_{N,\varepsilon}(\mu)}\int_{\mathcal{Q}_{N,\varepsilon}(\mu)}\exp\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}\beta^{2}}{2N}\frac{1}{2^{2D}}\sum_{k,k^{\prime}=1}^{2^{D}}\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}(k^{\prime})\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}(k)\right)^{2}\right)\nu_{N}(d\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\nu_{N}(d\boldsymbol{\eta})

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{Q}_{N,\varepsilon}(\mu)}\int\exp\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}\beta^{2}}{2N2^{D}}\sum_{k=1}^{2^{D}}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}(s)\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}(k)\right)^{2}ds\right)\nu_{N}(d\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\nu_{N}(d\boldsymbol{\eta}).

(4.5)

Here and in the following

stands for the usual Euclidean scalar product.
The second inequality is Jensen applied to the time averages
\xi_{j}(k^{\prime})=2^{D}\int_{t_{k^{\prime}-1}}^{t_{k^{\prime}}}\xi_{j}(s)ds
inside the square. The third inequality drops the restriction in the

-integration.

The

4.2
Biased quantum Hopfield models

The innermost integral on the right-hand side of (
4.1
) represents the normalized partition function at inverse temperature
\beta>0
of a quantum Hopfield model with
M=2^{D}
patterns
\boldsymbol{\eta}(k)
enumerated by
k\in\{1,\dots,M\}
and coupling constant

g\coloneqq\frac{\lambda^{2}\beta}{2}\geq 0

that is, with Hamiltonian

\widehat{H}_{N}^{(M)}\equiv\widehat{H}_{N}^{(M)}[\boldsymbol{\eta}]\coloneqq-\frac{g}{NM}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\eta_{j}(k)\ S_{j}^{z}\Big)^{2}-b\sum_{j=1}^{N}S_{j}^{x}\qquad\mbox{on}\quad\bigotimes_{j=1}^{N}\mathbbm{C}^{2}.

(4.6)

Had the patterns
(\eta_{1}(k),\dots,\eta_{N}(k))

been independent and identically distributed, it would coincide with the standard quantum Hopfield model whose free energy is studied in
[
43
]
. To determine the pressure

\displaystyle\widehat{\varphi}_{N}^{(M)}\equiv\widehat{\varphi}_{N}^{(M)}[\boldsymbol{\eta}]\coloneqq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\ln{\operatorname{Tr}\,}e^{-\beta\widehat{H}_{N}^{(M)}[\boldsymbol{\eta}]}

(4.7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\ln\Big[(2\cosh(\beta b))^{N}\int\exp\left(\frac{\beta g}{NM}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}(s)\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}(k)\right)^{2}ds\right)\nu_{N}(d\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\Big]

asymptotically as
N\to\infty
for
1_{\mathcal{Q}_{N,\varepsilon}(\mu)}\nu_{N}
-typical realizations of a large number
M=2^{D}
of patterns, which are bounded,
\eta_{j}(k)^{2}\leq 1
, we trace the steps in
[
43
]
and derive a variational principle. The latter is expressed in terms of the pressure functional

\widehat{\varphi}_{N}^{(M)}(\boldsymbol{y})\equiv\widehat{\varphi}_{N}^{(M)}(\boldsymbol{y})[\boldsymbol{\eta}]\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\ln 2\cosh\sqrt{(\beta b)^{2}+\left(\frac{2\beta g}{M}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\eta_{j}(k)y(k)\right)^{2}}-\frac{\beta g}{M}\sum_{k=1}^{M}y(k)^{2}

(4.8)

with
\boldsymbol{y}=(y(1),\dots,y(M))\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
, which is of quantum Curie-Weiss type.

Proposition 4.3
.

Consider the Hopfield Hamiltonian (
4.6
) with patterns
\boldsymbol{\eta}(1),\dots,\boldsymbol{\eta}(M)
satisfying

\eta_{j}(k)^{2}\leq 1

for all
j\in\{1,\dots,N\}
and
k\in\{1,\dots,M\}
. Then for any
\beta>0
,
b,g\geq 0
there is some
C<\infty
, which is independent of
\boldsymbol{\eta}
, such that for any
N,M\in\mathbb{N}
:

\Big|\widehat{\varphi}_{N}^{(M)}[\boldsymbol{\eta}]-\sup_{\boldsymbol{y}}\widehat{\varphi}_{N}^{(M)}(\boldsymbol{y})[\boldsymbol{\eta}]\Big|\leq C\max\left\{\frac{M}{\sqrt{N}},\frac{1}{N^{1/3}}\right\}.

(4.9)

Proof.

We abbreviate the magnetization operator corresponding to the
k
th pattern by

M(k)\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{N}\eta_{j}(k)S_{j}^{z},

and introduce a collection
\gamma(k)
,
k\in\{1,\dots,M\}
, of independent and identically distributed standard normal random variables, which serve as an auxiliary random field in

\widehat{H}_{N}^{(M)}(\gamma)\coloneqq\widehat{H}_{N}^{(M)}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\gamma(k)M(k).

(4.10)

If
\psi_{N}^{(M)}(\gamma)\coloneqq N^{-1}\ln{\operatorname{Tr}\,}e^{-\beta\widehat{H}_{N}^{(M)}(\gamma)}
denotes the corresponding pressure, we have
\psi_{N}^{(M)}(0)=\widehat{\varphi}_{N}^{(M)}
. If
\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}[\cdot]
stands for the joint expectation value over the auxiliary random variables and
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\gamma}
denotes the Gibbs expectation value with respect to
\widehat{H}_{N}^{(M)}(\gamma)
at inverse temperature
\beta
, the Peierls-Bogoliubov inequality guarantees that

\displaystyle 0=\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{N}^{3}}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{M}\gamma(k)\ \langle M(k)\rangle_{0}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\left[\psi_{N}^{(M)}(\gamma)-\varphi_{N}^{(M)}\right]\leq\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{N}^{3}}\ \mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{M}\gamma(k)\langle M(k)\rangle_{\gamma}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{N}^{3}}\ \mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{M}\gamma(k)^{2}\right]^{1/2}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{M}\langle M(k)\rangle_{\gamma}^{2}\right]^{1/2}\!\leq\ \beta\frac{M}{\sqrt{N}}.

(4.11)

Hence, if

M/\sqrt{N}
is small, the addition of the random field in (
4.10
) does not change the pressure.
To study
\psi_{N}^{(M)}(\gamma)
, we now linearize the interaction and introduce

\displaystyle\widehat{H}_{N}^{(M)}(\gamma,\boldsymbol{y})\coloneqq

\displaystyle\ \widehat{H}_{N}^{(M)}(\gamma)+\frac{g}{MN}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\left(M(k)-Ny(k)\right)^{2}

(4.12)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ -\sum_{k=1}^{M}M(k)\left(\frac{2gy(k)}{M}+\frac{\gamma(k)}{\sqrt{N}}\right)-b\sum_{j=1}^{N}S_{j}^{x}+\frac{gN}{M}\sum_{k=1}^{M}y(k)^{2}.

Its pressure at inverse temperature
\beta
will be denoted
\psi_{N}^{(M)}(\gamma,\boldsymbol{y})
such that
\psi_{N}^{(M)}(0,\boldsymbol{y})=\widehat{\varphi}_{N}^{(M)}(\boldsymbol{y})
, cf. (
4.8
).
Analogously to (
4.2
), the Peierls-Bogoliubov inequality guarantees that the pressure is unchanged by the presence of the random field:

\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\left[\sup_{\boldsymbol{y}}\left|\psi_{N}^{(M)}(\gamma,\boldsymbol{y})-\psi_{N}^{(M)}(0,\boldsymbol{y})\right|\right]\leq\beta\frac{M}{\sqrt{N}}.

(4.13)

Another application of the Peierls-Bogoliubov inequality compares the effect of the addition of the last term in (
4.12
) on the pressure,

0\leq\psi_{N}^{(M)}(\gamma)-\psi_{N}^{(M)}(\gamma,\boldsymbol{y})\leq\frac{\beta g}{MN^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\langle\left(M(k)-Ny(k)\right)^{2}\rangle_{\gamma},

where the expectation value on the right-hand side is with respect to the Gibbs measure of
H_{N}^{(M)}(\gamma)
. Optimizing over
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
yields

0\leq\psi_{N}^{(M)}(\gamma)-\sup_{\boldsymbol{y}}\psi_{N}^{(M)}(\gamma,\boldsymbol{y})\leq\frac{\beta g}{MN^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\langle\left(M(k)-\langle M(k)\rangle_{\gamma}\right)^{2}\rangle_{\gamma}.

(4.14)

As in
[
43
]
, we now split the last term into two contributions. To do so, we evaluate the Gibbs expectation value
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\gamma}
in terms of the eigenvalues
(E_{j})
and corresponding normalized eigenvectors
(\psi_{j})
of
-\beta\widehat{H}_{N}^{(M)}(\gamma)
. Abbreviating the partition function by
Z\coloneqq{\operatorname{Tr}\,}e^{-\beta\widehat{H}_{N}^{(M)}(\gamma)}
and

A_{ij}(k)\coloneqq\langle\psi_{i},\left(M(k)-\langle M(k)\rangle_{\gamma}\right)\psi_{j}\rangle,

we rewrite

\sum_{k=1}^{M}\langle\left(M(k)-\langle M(k)\rangle_{\gamma}\right)^{2}\rangle_{\gamma}=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\sum_{i,j}\frac{1}{2}\left(e^{E_{i}}+e^{E_{j}}\right)A_{ij}(k)^{2}.

(4.15)

The inequality

\frac{1}{2}\left(e^{s}+e^{s^{\prime}}\right)\leq\frac{e^{s}-e^{s^{\prime}}}{s-s^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{2}\left|e^{s}-e^{s^{\prime}}\right|,

is valid for all
s,s^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
(even at
s=s^{\prime}
when interpreting the first term on the right-hand side as a derivative at
s
, i.e. as
e^{s}
). Applied to (
4.15
), we hence bound (
4.15
) by a sum of two terms.
The first equals

\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\sum_{i,j}\frac{e^{E_{i}}-e^{E_{j}}}{E_{i}-E_{j}}A_{ij}(k)^{2}=\frac{N^{2}}{\beta^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\frac{\partial^{2}\psi_{N}^{(M)}(\gamma)}{\partial\gamma(k)^{2}}.

(4.16)

The second term equals

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2Z}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\sum_{i,j}\left|e^{E_{i}}-e^{E_{j}}\right|A_{ij}(k)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{2Z}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\sum_{i,j}\frac{e^{E_{i}}-e^{E_{j}}}{E_{i}-E_{j}}A_{ij}(k)^{2}\right)^{2/3}\left(\frac{1}{2Z}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\sum_{i,j}\left(e^{E_{i}}+e^{E_{j}}\right)(E_{i}-E_{j})^{2}A_{ij}(k)^{2}\right)^{1/3}.

(4.17)

The last step is an application of Hölder’s inequality, together with the estimate
\left|e^{E_{i}}-e^{E_{j}}\right|\leq e^{E_{i}}+e^{E_{j}}
for the second term.
By (
4.16
), the first bracket equals

\frac{N^{2}}{2\beta^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\frac{\partial^{2}\psi_{N}^{(M)}(\gamma)}{\partial\gamma(k)^{2}}
. An explicit computation shows that the term in the second bracket is related to a Gibbs expectation of a commutator

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2Z}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\sum_{i,j}\left(e^{E_{i}}+e^{E_{j}}\right)(E_{j}-E_{i})^{2}A_{ij}(k)^{2}=-\sum_{k=1}^{M}\left\langle[M(k),\beta\widehat{H}_{N}^{(M)}(\gamma)]^{2}\right\rangle_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k=1}^{M}\left\langle\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\beta b\eta_{j}(k)[S_{j}^{z},S_{j}^{x}]\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{\gamma}=4\beta^{2}b^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\eta_{i}(k)\eta_{j}(k)\left\langle S_{i}^{y}S_{j}^{y}\right\rangle_{\gamma}\leq 4\beta^{2}b^{2}MN^{2}.

(4.18)

To bound the first term, we average over the Gaussian field and use Gaussian integration by parts

\displaystyle\frac{N^{2}}{\beta^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}\psi_{N}^{(M)}(\gamma)}{\partial\gamma(k)^{2}}\right]=\frac{N^{2}}{\beta^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\left[\gamma(k)\frac{\partial\psi_{N}^{(M)}(\gamma)}{\partial\gamma(k)}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{\beta}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\left[\frac{\gamma(k)}{\sqrt{N}}\langle M(k)\rangle_{\gamma}\right]\leq\frac{\sqrt{N}}{\beta}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\left[\gamma(k)^{2}\right]^{1/2}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\left[\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\eta_{j}(k)\langle S_{j}^{z}\rangle_{\gamma}\right)^{2}\right]^{1/2}\leq\frac{N^{3/2}M}{\beta}.

(4.19)

In summary, we have thus proved that

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\left[\psi_{N}^{(M)}(\gamma)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\left[\sup_{\boldsymbol{y}}\psi_{N}^{(M)}(\gamma,\boldsymbol{y})\right]\right|\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\beta g}{MN^{2}}\left[\frac{N^{3/2}M}{\beta}+\left(\frac{N^{3/2}M}{2\beta}\right)^{2/3}\left(4\beta^{2}b^{2}MN^{2}\right)^{1/3}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{g}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{\beta gb^{2/3}}{N^{1/3}}.

(4.20)

The above is now used to estimate

\displaystyle\left|\widehat{\varphi}_{N}^{(M)}-\sup_{\boldsymbol{y}}\widehat{\varphi}_{N}^{(M)}(\boldsymbol{y})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\widehat{\varphi}_{N}^{(M)}-\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\left[\psi_{N}^{(M)}(\gamma)\right]\right|

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\left[\sup_{\boldsymbol{y}}\left|\psi_{N}^{(M)}(\gamma,\boldsymbol{y})-\psi_{N}^{(M)}(0,\boldsymbol{y})\right|\right]+\left|\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\left[\psi_{N}^{(M)}(\gamma)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\left[\sup_{\boldsymbol{y}}\psi_{N}^{(M)}(\gamma,\boldsymbol{y})\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\beta M}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{g}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{\beta gb^{2/3}}{N^{1/3}},

(4.21)

where the last line is a combination of (
4.2
), (
4.13
), and (
4.2
). This completes the proof.
∎

It remains to establish an upper bound on the variational problem in (
4.9
). This is achieved in the following, which makes use of the empirical self-overlap matrix

Q_{N}^{(M)}(k,k^{\prime})\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\eta_{j}(k)\eta_{j}(k^{\prime})

of the patterns.
We will denote the operator norm of this symmetric matrix by
\big\|Q_{N}^{(M)}\big\|
, occasionally dropping its dependence on
\boldsymbol{\eta}
.

Lemma 4.4
.

For any
N,M\in\mathbb{N}
and patterns
\boldsymbol{\eta}=(\boldsymbol{\eta}(1),\dots,\boldsymbol{\eta}(M))\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}
:

\sup_{\boldsymbol{y}}\widehat{\varphi}_{N}^{(M)}(\boldsymbol{y})[\boldsymbol{\eta}]\leq\sup_{x\geq 0}\left[\ln 2\cosh\sqrt{(\beta b)^{2}+x\frac{4\beta g}{M}\big\|Q_{N}^{(M)}[\boldsymbol{\eta}]\big\|}-x\right]

(4.22)

In case

\frac{2g\tanh(\beta b)}{b}\frac{\big\|Q_{N}^{(M)}\big\|}{M}\leq 1,

(4.23)

the supremum on the right side is a maximum at
x=0
and hence equals
\ln\left(2\cosh(\beta b)\right)
.

Proof.

The function
h:(0,\infty)\ni\xi\mapsto\ln\cosh\sqrt{\xi}
is concave as can be checked by calculating its second derivative,
h^{\prime\prime}(\xi)=\big(1-\sinh(2\sqrt{\xi})/(2\sqrt{\xi})\big)/(4\xi\cosh^{2}(\sqrt{\xi}))\leq 0
. We may therefore pull the empirical average inside and estimate using Jensen’s inequality

\displaystyle\widehat{\varphi}_{N}^{(M)}(\boldsymbol{y})\leq

\displaystyle\ln 2\cosh\sqrt{(\beta b)^{2}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\frac{2\beta g}{M}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\eta_{j}(k)y(k)\right)^{2}}-\frac{\beta g}{M}\sum_{k=1}^{M}y(k)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ln 2\cosh\sqrt{(\beta b)^{2}+\left(\frac{2\beta g}{M}\right)^{2}\langle\boldsymbol{y},Q_{N}^{(M)}\boldsymbol{y}\rangle}-\frac{\beta g}{M}\|\boldsymbol{y}\|^{2}.

(4.24)

The bound (
4.22
) then follows from the estimate
\langle\boldsymbol{y},Q_{N}^{(M)}\boldsymbol{y}\rangle\leq\big\|Q_{N}^{(M)}\big\|\|\boldsymbol{y}\|^{2}
.

For a proof of the second claim, we compute the derivative of
h:(0,\infty)\ni\xi\mapsto\ln\cosh\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}\xi}-\xi
with
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
, which equals
h^{\prime}(\xi)=\frac{b^{2}}{2\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}\xi}}\tanh\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}\xi}-1
. Since
h
is concave by the same reasoning as above, it follows that
h^{\prime}(\xi)\leq h^{\prime}(0)=\frac{b^{2}\tanh a}{2a}-1\leq 0
in case (
4.23
) holds.
∎

Proof of Theorem

(4.25)

For a matching lower bound, we use the following variant of the standard second-moment bound, cf.

Proposition 4.5
.

For any
\delta>0
and
l\in[0,1]
for which

e^{-l\delta N}\leq\mathbb{E}\left[W_{N,\varepsilon}^{D}(\lambda)\right]

(4.26)

at fixed parameters of

\exp\left(N\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi_{N,\varepsilon}^{D}(\lambda)\right]+\delta\right)\right)\geq\frac{\left(\mathbb{E}\left[W_{N,\varepsilon}^{D}(\lambda)\right]-e^{-l\delta N}\right)^{2}}{\mathbb{E}\left[W_{N,\varepsilon}^{D}(\lambda)^{2}\right]}-\mathbb{P}\Big(\left|\varphi_{N,\varepsilon}^{D}(\lambda)-\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi_{N,\varepsilon}^{D}(\lambda)\right]\right|>(1-l)\delta\Big).

Proof.

Since the exponent in the left side is non-random, we may lower bound it by

\displaystyle 1\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi_{N,\varepsilon}^{D}(\lambda)\right]+\delta\geq 0\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi_{N,\varepsilon}^{D}(\lambda)\right]+\delta\geq 0\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\left(\varphi_{N,\varepsilon}^{D}(\lambda)+l\delta\geq 0\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(\varphi_{N,\varepsilon}^{D}(\lambda)-\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi_{N,\varepsilon}^{D}(\lambda)\right]\geq(1-l)\delta\right).

2.6

The reason for this choice is the following implied estimate

Quantum Toninelli argument and the order parameter in the spin-glass regime

We consider the

1

\pi_{c}(n)\coloneqq\begin{cases}0,&n\in[0,m),\\

with parameters

\displaystyle P(m,q)\coloneqq

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\pi_{c},0)=X_{0}(m,q)+\frac{\beta^{2}}{4}(1-m)q^{2}

(5.1)

\displaystyle\mbox{with}\quad X_{0}(m,q)\coloneqq\frac{1}{m}\ln\mathbb{E}\left[I(q)^{m}\right],

\displaystyle\mbox{and}\quad I(q)\coloneqq\int e^{S_{q}(\xi)}\nu_{1}(d\xi),\quad S_{q}(\xi)\coloneqq\sqrt{q}\beta w\ \langle 1,\xi\rangle-\frac{q\beta^{2}}{2}\langle 1,\xi\rangle^{2},

where

\xi

for any

in the vicinity of

by calculating the partial derivatives.

5.1
Computation of derivatives

The first derivative is straightforward to calculate.
For any
q\geq 0
, we have

\frac{\partial}{\partial m}P(1,q)=\mathbb{E}\left[I(q)\ln I(q)\right]-\frac{\beta^{2}}{4}q^{2},

(5.2)

such that
\frac{\partial}{\partial m}P(1,0)=0
.

To determine
q\geq 0
such that
\frac{\partial}{\partial m}P(1,q)>0
, we investigate the partial derivatives with respect to
q
. This amounts to calculating

\frac{\partial}{\partial q}\mathbb{E}\left[I(q)\ln I(q)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[I^{\prime}(q)\left(1+\ln I(q)\right)\right]

(5.3)

where

I^{\prime}(q)=\int e^{S_{q}(\xi)}\left[\frac{\beta w}{2\sqrt{q}}\langle 1,\xi\rangle-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\langle 1,\xi\rangle^{2}\right]\nu_{1}(d\xi).

(5.4)

To process the expectation in (
5.3
), we use Gaussian integration by parts which
for any boundedly differentiable function
F
of the Gaussian variable

yields:

\mathbb{E}\left[F(w)\ e^{S_{q}(\xi)}\left[\frac{\beta w}{2\sqrt{q}}\langle 1,\xi\rangle-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\langle 1,\xi\rangle^{2}\right]\right]=\frac{\beta}{2}\ \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\ F^{\prime}(w)\ e^{S_{q}(\xi)}\langle 1,\xi\rangle\right].

(5.5)

We will apply this first to the case
F(w)=1+\ln I(q)
for which

\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}F^{\prime}(w)=\beta\frac{J(q)}{I(q)},\qquad\mbox{with}\quad J(q)\coloneqq\int e^{S_{q}(\xi)}\langle 1,\xi\rangle\nu_{1}(d\xi).

Applying (

\frac{\partial}{\partial q}\mathbb{E}\left[I(q)\ln I(q)\right]=\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{J(q)^{2}}{I(q)}\right],

(5.6)

which implies

\frac{\partial^{2}P}{\partial q\partial m}(1,q)=\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\ \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{J(q)^{2}}{I(q)}\right]-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}q.

(5.7)

Since
S_{0}(\xi)=0
and
\int\xi(t)\nu_{1}(d\xi)=0
, the above derivative at

and
q=0
is zero. To determine the behavior of

near

, we proceed by calculating the second derivative with respect to
q
again using (

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial q}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{J(q)^{2}}{I(q)}\right]=

\displaystyle\ 2\ \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{J(q)}{I(q)}\int e^{S_{q}(\xi)}\langle 1,\xi\rangle\left(\frac{\beta w}{2\sqrt{q}}\langle 1,\xi\rangle-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\langle 1,\xi\rangle^{2}\right)\nu_{1}(d\xi)\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{I^{\prime}(q)}{I(q)}J(q)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta\ \mathbb{E}\left[\int\int e^{S_{q}(\xi)}\langle 1,\xi\rangle^{2}\langle 1,\eta\rangle\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\frac{\partial}{\partial w}\left(\frac{1}{I(q)}e^{S_{q}(\eta)}\right)\ \nu_{1}(d\xi)\nu_{1}(d\eta)\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{\beta}{2}\ \mathbb{E}\left[J(q)\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\frac{\partial}{\partial w}\left(\frac{J(q)^{2}}{I(q)^{2}}\right)\right].

(5.8)

Abbreviating

, we thus conclude

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial q}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{J(q)^{2}}{I(q)}\right]=\beta^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{K(q)}{I(q)^{2}}\left(K(q)I(q)-J(q)^{2}\right)\right]-\beta^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{K(q)J(q)^{2}}{I(q)^{2}}-\frac{J(q)^{4}}{I(q)^{3}}\right].

(5.9)

At

\displaystyle I(0)=1,\quad J(0)=0\quad

and

\displaystyle K(0)=\int\langle 1,\xi\rangle^{2}\nu_{1}(d\xi)

\displaystyle=\langle 1,\mu 1\rangle=\frac{\tanh\beta b}{\beta b}.

The above identity is by explicit calculation, which can be found in
[

Appendix A
Compactness for non-negative Hilbert-Schmidt operators

In the following, let
\mathcal{H}
be a separable Hilbert space, and
\mathcal{S}_{2}(\mathcal{H})
denote the Hilbert space of Hilbert
–
Schmidt operators on
\mathcal{H}
.
The latter is equipped with a scalar product
\langle A,B\rangle\coloneqq{\operatorname{Tr}\,}A^{*}B
and
\|A\|_{2}\coloneqq\sqrt{\langle A,A\rangle}
denotes its Hilbert-Schmidt norm. The operator norm is
\|A\|
, and
\mathcal{S}^{+}_{2}(\mathcal{H})
denotes the non-negative Hilbert-Schmidt operators. The following observation is our main tool to boost weak convergence results to strong convergence.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
A_{n}\in\mathcal{S}_{2}^{+}(\mathcal{H})
be a sequence of Hilbert-Schmidt operators. Suppose:

1.

there is an increasing family of finite-dimensional subspaces
\mathcal{H}_{D}\subset\mathcal{H}
whose orthogonal projections
P_{D}
converge weakly in
\mathcal{H}
to the identity,
\lim_{D\to\infty}\langle\varphi,Q_{D}\psi\rangle=0
for all
\varphi,\psi\in\mathcal{H}
, where
Q_{D}\coloneqq 1-P_{D}
, and

2.

for any
\varepsilon>0
there are
D_{\varepsilon},N_{\varepsilon}>0
such that

\sup_{n\geq N_{\varepsilon}}\left\|Q_{D_{\varepsilon}}A_{n}Q_{D_{\varepsilon}}\right\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon.

(A.1)

Then:

1.

weak convergence of
(A_{n})
in
\mathcal{S}_{2}(\mathcal{H})
implies its strong convergence in
\mathcal{S}_{2}(\mathcal{H})
.

2.

if
\sup_{n}\|A_{n}\|_{2}<\infty
, then
(A_{n})
is relatively compact in
\mathcal{S}_{2}(\mathcal{H})
.

Proof.

1. Let
A\in\mathcal{S}_{2}(\mathcal{H})
stand for a weak limit of
(A_{n})
. For
\varepsilon>0
arbitrary, we first pick
\widehat{D}_{\varepsilon},N_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{N}
such that (
A.1
) applies and hence, since the subspaces are nested,

\sup_{n\geq N_{\varepsilon}}\left\|Q_{D}A_{n}Q_{D}\right\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon.

holds for all
D\geq\widehat{D}_{\varepsilon}
. We then choose
D_{\varepsilon}\geq\widehat{D}_{\varepsilon}
such that additionally
\|A-P_{D_{\varepsilon}}AP_{D_{\varepsilon}}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon
. This is possible since
A
is a compact operator. The triangle inequality and (
A.3
) below then yield for all
n\geq N_{\varepsilon}
:

\displaystyle\left\|A-A_{n}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|P_{D_{\varepsilon}}(A-A_{n})P_{D_{\varepsilon}}\right\|_{2}+\left\|A-P_{D_{\varepsilon}}AP_{D_{\varepsilon}}\right\|_{2}+\sqrt{2\left\|P_{D_{\varepsilon}}A_{n}P_{D_{\varepsilon}}\right\|_{2}\left\|Q_{D_{\varepsilon}}A_{n}Q_{D_{\varepsilon}}\right\|_{2}}+\left\|Q_{D_{\varepsilon}}A_{n}Q_{D_{\varepsilon}}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|P_{D_{\varepsilon}}(A-A_{n})P_{D_{\varepsilon}}\right\|_{2}+2\varepsilon+\sqrt{2\varepsilon\left\|P_{D_{\varepsilon}}A_{n}P_{D_{\varepsilon}}\right\|_{2}}.

Since
P_{D_{\varepsilon}}
is finite dimensional, the first term on the right converges to zero as
n\to\infty
. By the same reasoning,
\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\|P_{D_{\varepsilon}}A_{n}P_{D_{\varepsilon}}\right\|_{2}=\left\|P_{D_{\varepsilon}}AP_{D_{\varepsilon}}\right\|_{2}\leq\|A\|_{2}
. This completes the proof of the first assertion, since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary.

2. The Banach-Alaoglu theorem implies that
(A_{n})
is weakly compact in the Hilbert space
\mathcal{S}_{2}(\mathcal{H})
. By the first assertion, any weakly convergent subsequence converges strongly.
∎

We provide an elementary block operator analysis result that was used in the previous proof.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
P
be an orthogonal projection and
Q=I-P
its complement in
\mathcal{H}
.

1.

For any non-negative bounded operator
0\leq M\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, there exists a bounded linear operator
K:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
with operator norm
\|K\|\leq 1
and

QMP=(QMQ)^{\frac{1}{2}}K(PMP)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(A.2)

2.

For any non-negative Hilbert-Schmidt operator
0\leq B\in\mathcal{S}_{2}^{+}
,

\|B-PBP\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2\|PBP\|_{2}\|QBQ\|_{2}+\|QBQ\|_{2}^{2}.

(A.3)

Proof.

1. To simplify the notation, let us write the operator
M
in block-form

M=\begin{pmatrix}C&X^{*}\\
X&D\end{pmatrix}

with
C:=PMP\geq 0,\,D:=QMQ\geq 0
and
X:=QMP.

Let us first assume that
C
and
D
are both invertible.
For arbitrary
u,v\in\mathcal{H}
, positivity gives

0\leq\left\langle\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}C&X^{*}\\
X&D\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle=\langle u,Cu\rangle+2\operatorname{Re}\langle v,Xu\rangle+\langle v,Dv\rangle.

Set
a:=C^{1/2}u,\,b:=D^{1/2}v
and
K:=D^{-1/2}XC^{-1/2}
.
Then the above inequality becomes

\|a\|^{2}+2\operatorname{Re}\langle b,Ka\rangle+\|b\|^{2}\geq 0.

Choosing
b=-Ka
yields
0\leq\|a\|^{2}-\|Ka\|^{2}
for all
a\in H
. Hence,
\|K\|\leq 1
and by definition

X=D^{1/2}KC^{1/2}.

In the general case
C,D\geq 0
, we refer to a standard regularization trick. For
\delta>0
, set

C_{\delta}:=C+\delta I,\qquad D_{\delta}:=D+\delta I.

C_{\delta}
and
D_{\delta}
are clearly invertible and, hence,
there exists a contraction
K_{\delta}
such that

X=D_{\delta}^{1/2}K_{\delta}C_{\delta}^{1/2}

and
\|K_{\delta}\|\leq 1
.
By the Banach-Alaoglu theorem, the unit ball of
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is compact in the weak operator topology. Thus, there exists

\delta_{n}\downarrow 0
and a contraction
K
such that in the weak operator topology,
K_{\delta_{n}}\to K
.
Moreover, the spectral theorem implies

C_{\delta_{n}}^{1/2}u\to C^{1/2}u,\qquad D_{\delta_{n}}^{1/2}v\to D^{1/2}v

strongly for all
u,v\in\mathcal{H}
. Therefore, for arbitrary
u,v\in\mathcal{H}
,

\langle Xu,v\rangle=\langle K_{\delta_{n}}C_{\delta_{n}}^{1/2}u,D_{\delta_{n}}^{1/2}v\rangle\longrightarrow\langle KC^{1/2}u,D^{1/2}v\rangle.

Thus,
X=D^{1/2}KC^{1/2}
.

2. By definition of the Hilbert-Schmidt norm

\|B-PBP\|_{2}^{2}=2\|QBP\|_{2}^{2}+\|QBQ\|_{2}^{2}.

To bound the off-diagonal term, we employ the first assertion, which yields
QBP=(QBQ)^{\frac{1}{2}}K(PBP)^{\frac{1}{2}}
with a contraction
K
. Note that

(QBP)^{*}(QBP)=(PBP)^{\frac{1}{2}}K^{*}(QBQ)K(PBP)^{\frac{1}{2}}
,
which, by the cyclicity of the trace, implies

\displaystyle\|QBP\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle={\operatorname{Tr}\,}(PBP)^{\frac{1}{2}}K^{*}(QBQ)K(PBP)^{\frac{1}{2}}={\operatorname{Tr}\,}K(PBP)K^{*}(QBQ)

\displaystyle\leq\|KPBPK^{*}\|_{2}\|QBQ\|_{2}\leq\|K\|^{2}\|PBP\|_{2}\|QBQ\|_{2}\,

(A.4)

where the first two estimates are based on the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for the Hilbert-Schmidt scalar product and the operator norm bound. Since
\|K\|\leq 1
this yields the claim.
∎

Appendix B
Concentration of the self-overlap

This appendix collects three results on the concentration of the self-overlap when viewed as a Hilbert-Schmidt operator. We start with concentration results for the non-interacting path measure, yielding an explicit concentration bound. Combined with prior results from
[
33
]
, this allows us to show weak concentration of the self-overlap. Some arguments in the main text rely on a stronger exponential concentration, which concerns us in the final part.

B.1
Non-interacting case

Under the a priori probability measure
\nu_{N}
, the self-overlap is a weighted sum of independent and identically distributed rank-one operators,

Q_{N}[\boldsymbol{\xi}]=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|\xi_{j}\rangle\langle\xi_{j}|.

Each rank-one term
|\xi_{j}\rangle\langle\xi_{j}|
, which is defined via its kernel
(t,s)\mapsto\xi_{j}(t)\xi_{j}(s)
, is an element of the real Hilbert space of Hilbert-Schmidt operators on
L^{2}(0,1)
.
The Hilbert-Schmidt norm is bounded

\left\||\xi_{j}\rangle\langle\xi_{j}|\right\|_{2}=1

(B.1)

for all
j\in\{1,\dots,N\}
.
Under
\nu_{N}
, the mean of
|\xi_{j}\rangle\langle\xi_{j}|
is given by
\mu
, see (
2.7
). The expected Hilbert-Schmidt norm of the fluctuations is

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\int\left\||\xi_{j}\rangle\langle\xi_{j}|-\mu\right\|_{2}^{2}\nu_{N}(d\boldsymbol{\xi})=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\left(1-\mu(t,s)^{2}\right)dsdt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{2}\left[1+\left(\tanh(\beta b)\right)^{2}-\frac{\tanh(\beta b)}{\beta b}\right].

(B.2)

Here, the last step is by explicit calculation, which is found in
[
25
]
.
An application of Pinelis’ Bernstein-type concentration inequality for sums of independent vectors in a real Hilbert space, thus yields the following standard concentration inequality.

Proposition B.1
.

For any
N\in\mathbbm{N}
and
\varepsilon>0
:

\nu_{N}\left(\left\|Q_{N}-\mu\right\|_{2}>\varepsilon\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{3N}{2}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{3\sigma^{2}+2\varepsilon}\right)

(B.3)

with
\sigma^{2}
from (
B.1
).

Proof.

The proof is an application of Pinelis’s concentration of random vectors in a real Hilbert space
[
41
]
. In our case, the Hilbert space is the space of real Hilbert-Schmidt operators on
L^{2}(0,1)
.
The operators
X_{j}=|\xi_{j}\rangle\langle\xi_{j}|-\mu
, which are defined via their kernels
X_{j}(t,s)=\xi_{j}(t)\xi_{j}(s)-\mu(t,s)
, are independent and identically distributed vectors in the real Hilbert space
\mathcal{S}_{2}
.
By
[
41
, Thm. 3.2]
their sums
S_{N}\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{N}X_{j}=N(Q_{N}-\mu)
satisfy for all
\beta>0
:

\int\cosh\left(\beta\left\|S_{N}\right\|_{2}\right)\nu_{N}(d\boldsymbol{\xi})\leq\prod_{j=1}^{N}\left(1+\int\left(e^{\beta\|X_{j}\|_{2}}-1-\beta\|X_{j}\|_{2}\right)\nu_{1}(d\xi_{j})\right).

(B.4)

By (
B.1
) we have
\|X_{j}\|_{2}\leq 2
, which for
0<2\beta<3
implies

\displaystyle\int\left(e^{\beta\|X_{j}\|_{2}}-1-\beta\|X_{j}\|_{2}\right)\nu_{1}(d\xi_{j})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\beta^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{2\ (2\beta)^{n}}{(n+2)!}\leq\frac{\beta^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{2\beta}{3}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\beta^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2}\frac{3}{3-2\beta}.

The claim (
B.3
) then follows from a Chernoff estimate

\displaystyle\nu_{N}\left(\left\|S_{N}\right\|_{2}>N\varepsilon\right)\leq 2e^{-N\beta\varepsilon}\int\cosh\left(\beta\left\|S_{N}\right\|_{2}\right)\nu_{N}(d\boldsymbol{\xi})\leq 2\exp\left(-N\left[\beta\varepsilon-\frac{\beta^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2}\frac{3}{3-2\beta}\right]\right)

in which we pick
\beta=\frac{3\varepsilon}{3\sigma^{2}+2\varepsilon}
.
∎

For a proof of some of our approximation results as well as for the concentration in the interacting case, we will also need the following concentration results for the path set

\pi^{D}_{*}
is a Parisi measure. By Gaussian integration by parts, one obtains at least along a subsequence

\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{g}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\langle\|R_{N_{k}}\|_{2}^{2}\rangle\rangle_{N_{k}}^{D,g}\right]=\int_{0}^{1}\|\pi_{*}^{D}(s)\|^{2}_{2}\,ds,

where we stress that this holds for an average of perturbed self-overlap-corrected model without further constraining.

Since the perturbation is not extensive (it is almost surely uniformly bounded by
CN^{3/4}
for some constant), the exponential concentration of the self-overlap guarantees that, upon choosing a sequence
\varepsilon_{N}
converging slowly enough to
0
,

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\left|\mathbb{E}_{g}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\langle\|R_{N}\|_{2}^{2}\rangle\rangle_{N}^{D,g}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{g}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\langle\|R_{N}\|_{2}^{2}\rangle\rangle_{N,\varepsilon_{N},{\varrho}^{D}}^{(D,g,1,1)}\right]\right|=0.

Again by Gaussian integration by parts, we obtain along a subsequence

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{N_{k}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{1}}\mathbb{E}_{g}\mathbb{E}\ln W^{D,g}_{N_{k},\varepsilon_{N_{k}}}(1,1;\varrho^{D})

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\lim_{k\to\infty}\left(\mathbb{E}_{g}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\langle\|Q_{N_{k}}\|_{2}^{2}\rangle\rangle_{N_{k},\varepsilon_{N_{k}},{\varrho}^{D}}^{(D,g,1,1)}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{g}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\langle\|R_{N_{k}}\|_{2}^{2}\rangle\rangle_{N_{k},\varepsilon_{N_{k}},{\varrho}^{D}}^{(D,g,1,1)}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\left(\|{\varrho}^{D}\|_{2}^{2}-\int_{0}^{1}\|\pi_{*}^{D}(m)\|^{2}_{2}\,dm\right).

Recalling that the perturbation does not affect the limit of the free energy and that
\widehat{F}^{D}(\lambda_{1},1;\varrho)
is convex and differentiable in
\lambda_{1}
, we arrive at

\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{1}}\widehat{F}^{D}(1,1;\varrho^{D})=\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\left(\|\varrho^{D}\|_{2}^{2}-\int_{0}^{1}\|\pi_{*}^{D}(m)\|^{2}_{2}\,dm\right).

Employing the convexity again, we relate
\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{1}}\widehat{F}^{D}(1,1;\varrho^{D})=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{1}}\mathbb{E}\left[\ln W_{N,\varepsilon_{N}}^{D}(1,1;\varrho^{D})\right]
. By Gaussian integration by parts, one may relate the last derivative to the empirical replica overlap as in (
2.23
). Hence, we can upgrade the convergence of the replica overlap to the full
N\to\infty
limit and remove the perturbation:

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\langle\|R_{N}\|_{2}^{2}\rangle\rangle_{N,\varepsilon_{N},{\varrho}^{D}}^{(D,1,1)}\right]=\int_{0}^{1}\|\pi_{*}^{D}(s)\|^{2}_{2}\,ds,

which completes the proof.
∎
