Prevalence of, and Gender Differences in College Students’ Experiences of Alcohol-Related Regretted Sex: a Scoping Review
Authors: M E Arnold, A P Masterson, T K Rohleen, J B Moran, B J Dunleavy, J Morgan-Daniel, S Chance, L S E Hone
Published: 2026

Introduction

For many college students, alcohol drinking is seen as a rite of passage fueled by new independence, peer pressure, and campus culture [1]. Drinking is implicated in many adverse sexual outcomes, such as unplanned pregnancies, sexually transmitted infections (STIs), and regretted sexual encounters. Alcohol-related regretted sex can refer to instances where individuals experience regret after being involved in sexual behaviors where alcohol consumption has been involved [2]. Regretted sexual experiences encompass a range of behaviors (e.g., negotiating condom use, partner choice) that are evaluated negatively upon reflection [2, 3] and can have lasting emotional and physical consequences [4–8]. For example, alcohol-related regretted sex among college students has been linked to anxiety, depression, trauma symptoms, suicidal ideation, problematic drinking [4, 9], and overall adverse health outcomes [10]. These effects can influence future decision-making, relationships, and self-worth [11].

Broadly, sexual regret is a construct involving the negative reappraisal of a past sexual experience or missed sexual opportunity [2]. College women experience more sexual regret due to the action of having sex than the inaction of not having sex [12–14]. These regrets of action are often more emotionally taxing than regrets of inaction [15, 16]. The action of having casual sex in the context of an uncommitted relationship is commonly referred to as “hooking up” [17]. Hookups comprise a spectrum of sexual behaviors, but most college students understand hooking up to be casual sexual activity between two individuals. Hookups are relatively common: The Online College Social Life Survey (OCSLS) of more than 24,000 students from 22 U.S. universities found that 60–80% of college students reported hooking up. The OCSLS also found that 14% of college students regretted their most recent hookup [18]. Indeed, hookups are a common source of sexual regret among college men and women and because women experience more sexual regret due to having sex, and regrets of action are more emotionally taxing, it is plausible that women may experience more emotional consequences of hookups than men [18–20].

Gender Differences in Emotional Consequences of Hookups

Young adults who engage in hookups (i.e. uncommitted sexual encounters or sexual intercourse outside of a committed relationship [17, 18]) are more likely to drink and experience lower psychological well-being than their peers who do not engage in hookups [4, 19–24]. Men and women differ in feelings after hookups: half of women report negative feelings after past-year hookups, and only a quarter report positive feelings [25]. Conversely, half of men report positive feelings, and only a quarter report negative feelings [19]. Similarly, with regard to one-night stands (i.e., a type of hookup that includes only a one-time encounter [14, 26]), only 28% of women (compared to 59% of men) rated their feelings the “morning after” as positive, and women were less likely to report “sexual satisfaction and contentment,” a “sense of well-being,” or “confidence about themselves” [27]. Though women can express positive attitudes toward drunken one-night stands, some also tempered this openness with concerns about being perceived as “too slutty” [28]. Notably, women tend to experience greater regret over feeling used (i.e. feeling misled or receiving false signals that facilitate sex [29]). Conversely men are less likely to express regret over using another person [27]. Overall, hookups appear to result in more negative emotional reactions, distress, and regret among women than among men [30].

Gender Differences in Emotional Consequences of Casual Sex

Like “hookups,” casual sex refers to sexual contact outside a romantic or dating relationship [31]. Similar to hookups, casual sex is a common source of regret [4] and is also associated with negative emotional consequences, particularly among women [7, 14, 19, 20, 22–24, 32–37]. For example, a history of casual sex is associated with more depressive symptoms among college women than among college men [23]. Women’s casual sex history is also positively related to psychological distress and lower self-esteem whereas men’s casual sex history is negatively related to psychological distress and low self-esteem [21, 38]. Consistent with these findings, after women engaged in casual sexual encounters, they expressed more worry and vulnerability whereas the opposite is true of men [32]. Traditional gender roles continue to frame casual sex as acceptable for men but inappropriate for women, shaping expectations around who should initiate or engage in sexual activity [39]. Women are evaluated more negatively than men for engaging in casual sex, reflecting persistent gendered double standards [40, 41]. Fear of stigmatization helps explain why women are less likely than men to accept casual sex offers, as they anticipate harsher judgments and negative character attributions [42].Women also report significantly more concern about how their partner feels about them after sex, worry more that their partner will not call, attempt to control their feelings of dependency [43, 44], worry more about “loss of reputation if other people [found] out,” and feel more “regret that they had let themselves down” [27]. Women also report less enjoyment and more guilt related to casual sex, and report greater fear of being physically harmed or contracting a sexually transmitted infection compared to men [45].

Alcohol-Related Regretted Sex

Hookups and casual sex are common sources of sexual regret [4, 5, 21], and students report that many of these experiences (and related negative consequences) tend to occur after drinking [46–48]. Subsequently, numerous studies report a relationship between drinking and regretted sex [2, 3, 49, 50]. For example, those who reported drinking during the week were more likely to report past week regretted sex than their peers who did not [51]. Moreover, students who drink to get drunk or use alcohol as “liquid courage” reported experiencing more instances of regretted sex [3, 52]. Men and women experience different levels of emotional reactions, distress, and regret after casual sex and hookups, and many experiences involve drinking. However, prevalence estimates and gender differences in alcohol-related regretted sex are inconsistent.

Gender Differences in Alcohol-Related Regretted Sex

There are gender differences in some, but not all alcohol-related consequences [53–55]. For example, there are typically no gender differences in doing or saying embarrassing things, vomiting, and skipping meals [56]. However, men are more likely to report experiencing a hangover, missing class, forgetting where they are, falling behind in school, physical abuse, sexual perpetration, and death [57–59]. Conversely, women tend to report more alcohol-related depression, damaged relationships, sexual victimization, and reduced self-esteem [60–62]. Given hookups and casual sex are a common source of sexual regret, are ubiquitously related to drinking and result in negative and severe emotional consequences, particularly among women [4, 5, 9, 21], it follows that alcohol-related regretted sex—a common alcohol consequence—may be more distressing for women than for men. Indeed, among women, alcohol use was more commonly associated with “bad” hookups than with “good” hookups [47].

Hookups and casual sex consistently result in more negative psychological experiences (e.g., depression; low self-esteem) among women, but it is unclear whether college women also experience more alcohol-related regretted sex than men. Given alcohol-related regretted sex is linked to anxiety, depression, trauma symptoms, suicidal ideation, problematic alcohol use, future decision-making, self-worth, and overall adverse health outcomes [2, 4, 10], establishing prevalence estimates and gender differences in this alcohol consequence is a necessary step to inform appropriate prevention programs. We conducted a scoping review to identify studies that document the pervasiveness of alcohol-related regretted sex among college students, as well as any gender differences. Providing researchers, practitioners, and university staff with prevalence estimates will characterize the scope of the problem, guide future data collection efforts and, eventually, inform targeted interventions to reduce alcohol-related regretted sex and associated negative outcomes, which may be especially impactful for women.

Method

Overview

The JBI methodology for scoping reviews and the PRISMA-ScR reporting guidelines were used to conduct this scoping review [63, 64]. To determine the originality of this review topic, searches for existing protocols or evidence syntheses were also conducted in BioMed Central Systematic Reviews, Campbell Systematic Reviews, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, JBI Evidence Synthesis, and PROSPERO: International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews on August 23, 2022. No current or underway systematic reviews or scoping reviews on the topic were identified using the keywords (alcohol OR drink OR drinking) AND (regret OR regrets OR regretted) AND (sex OR sexual). The scoping review protocol registration can be found on Open Science Framework at [65]. Our registered research questions were: “What is the prevalence of alcohol-related regretted sex among college students in the United States?” and “What is the variability among different groups (i.e., gender differences)?” Initial test searches were conducted in PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science in June 2022, yielding 63 results, 110 results, and 83 results respectively. A refined test search in PubMed on August 23, 2022, yielded 31 articles that met inclusion criteria.

Inclusion Criteria

This review included experimental and cross-sectional studies that described data from higher education institutions, such as two-year community colleges, trade schools, and four-year colleges/universities, Minority Serving Institutions (MSIs), and Predominantly White Institutions (PWIs) in the United States. Only studies conducted in English were included as this was the language in which the authors were fluent. Studies were included if the average participants age was 15–25. Studies published from date of database inception through June 23, 2025 were included. Both published (e.g., empirical research articles) and unpublished reports (e.g., dissertations) were considered.

Search Strategy

The search strategy was developed by a research librarian (JMD) with the fourth (JBM) and senior (LSEH) authors’ input. The base search strategy was peer-reviewed by a second librarian and then adapted for each database using subject headings where available (see Appendix I). The original literature searches were conducted on September 23, 2022, in Embase (Elsevier), PsycINFO (EBSCOhost), Psychology and Behavioral Sciences Collection (EBSCOhost), PubMed, and Web of Science (Clarivate Analytics). Updated searches were conducted on November 5, 2024 and June 23, 2025.

Record Selection

All identified records (n = 228 original search; 40 updated searches) were imported into Covidence [66] and duplicates were automatically removed (n = 114 original search; 23 updated searches). Each record was screened by two independent reviewers (research assistants) for assessment against inclusion criteria. Any disagreements between reviewers were discussed and resolved with first and third authors (MA, TR).

Report Selection

Potentially eligible full-text reports were retrieved, uploaded into Covidence, and read by two reviewers (MA and TR). Two independent reviewers assessed each report against the inclusion criteria. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (LSEH). Manual searches of reports’ reference sections were conducted (TR, BD), and any records identified were considered for inclusion. Eligible reports were included in the data extraction phase. The results of the record and report selection process are presented in a Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and PleasMeta-analyses (PRISMA) flow diagram (Fig. 1) [67].

PRISMA 2020 flow diagram for new systematic reviews which included searches of databases, registers and other sources

Data Extraction

Data were extracted from reports by two independent reviewers (MA, TR, AM, BD, and research assistants) using a modified JBI data extraction tool [68] developed by the first and senior authors. Young adult alcohol-related regretted sex and other consequences (e.g., drinking and driving, hangovers, blackouts) are typically measured via the Young Adult Alcohol Problems Screening Test (YAAPST [69]) or the Young Adult Alcohol Consequences Questionnaire (YAACQ [60]). Data extracted were as follows: Information on measures of alcohol-related regretted sex (e.g., YAAPST, YAACQ), including the response options (e.g., “Yes” or “No”) and the timeframe (e.g., lifetime, past year, past month); the prevalence of alcohol-related regretted sex; and any statistics regarding gender differences. Information on study design and sample size as well as participants’ age, gender/sex, race, and ethnicity were also extracted. Any disagreements between two reviewers were discussed with a third reviewer (MA, TR, or LSEH).

Results

Overview

Our search of five databases for English language studies from database inception to 2025 yielded 268 records which were collated and uploaded into Covidence. After duplicate records were automatically removed, 131 records were screened against inclusion criteria. Sixty-five records were excluded, and 63 reports were retained for full-text review (see Fig. 1). Of these, 11 reports met inclusion criteria and were retained for data extraction. The reference sections of all 11 reports were screened to identify additional records that met inclusion criteria. Nine additional reports were sought for retrieval and four met inclusion criteria, yielding a total of 15 included reports of 15 studies. Included reports were published between 1999 and 2020 and involved studies conducted at a range of study locations, including private liberal arts colleges, private and public midsize universities, and large public universities. Sample sizes of the 15 studies ranged from 134 to 1,053 (total = 7,047; M = 470). All studies took place at four-year institutions granting bachelor’s degrees. The mean age of participants was 19.26 years old, and the majority (58.8—98%) of the participants were White (see Table 1).

Measurement

Ten of the 15 included studies administered either the YAAPST or YAACQ (or both) to measure alcohol consequences, including alcohol-related regretted sex. Seven studies [54, 70–73] included the YAAPST and asked students about their experiences of alcohol-related regretted sex in the past year [3, 54, 71, 73], since starting college [70], and/or in their lifetime [72] via one item: “Has drinking ever gotten you into sexual situations you later regretted?” with response options of “Yes” or “No, Never” to “3 or more times.” Four studies included the YAACQ or the Brief YAACQ (B-YAACQ) and asked students about their experiences of alcohol-related regretted sex in the past six months, past year, or lifetime via one item: “My drinking has gotten me into sexual situations I later regretted,” with response options of “Yes” or “No” [2, 3, 74] (Table 2). Two studies used both the YAAPST and the B-YAACQ, one study adapted these two measures for past week alcohol-related regretted sex [75] and the other looked at past year [3]. One study used the Peer Education Program Pretest, which asked students if they experienced sexual regret because they: “felt alcohol influenced my decision” during their lifetime with a 5-point Likert-type response [8]. The remaining five studies used author-created measures of alcohol-related regretted sex in past month [76], past three months [77], past year [17], or lifetime [30, 78] with response options of “Yes” or “No” [30, 76, 78]; “Never” to “Happened frequently” [77]; or “I would not have gone as far” to “I would have gone farther” [17] (see Table 3).

Prevalence

Rates of alcohol-related regretted sex measured on common lifetime, past year, and past month timescales were as follows: The lifetime prevalence of alcohol-related regretted sex ranged from 14.2% to 48.09% for women, and 38% to 46.67% for men [72, 78]. The past year prevalence ranged from 17.9% to 34.4% for women and 21.7% to 27.9% for men [3, 17], past six months prevalence was reported as 18.47% for women and 15.20% for men [2], and the past month prevalence was 12.3% for women and 10.5% for men [76]. One study measured alcohol-related regretted sex on a variable timeframe (i.e., since becoming a student at a small, residential college) and found that 60.7% of women and 72% of men had reported alcohol-related regretted sex in this timeframe [70]. Seven of the 15 studies did not report prevalence so comparisons could not be made [8, 54, 71, 73–75, 77]. However, these seven studies did report analyses of gender differences (Table 3).

Prevalence rates varied widely and use of different timeframes (since starting college, past month, past three months, past six months, past year, lifetime) made similarities across studies difficult to discern. Seven studies used the YAAPST to measure alcohol-related regretted sex [3, 54, 70–73], but used different timeframes (or did not report prevalence). Using the YAAPST, the lifetime prevalence rates were 46.67% for men and 48.09% for women [72]; since entering college, 72% of men and 60.7% of women reported alcohol-related regretted sex [70]; and past year prevalence rates were 27.9% for men and 29% for women [3]. Four studies that used the YAAPST did not report rates [54, 71, 73, 75]. Six studies used author created questionnaires to measure alcohol-related regretted sex [17, 30, 76–78], and also used varying timeframes. Using author created questionnaires, lifetime prevalence rates were 14.2% and 24% for women versus 14.5% and 39% for men [30, 76,]; past year prevalence rates were 34.4% for women and 27.9% for men [17]; and past month rates were 12.3% for women and 10.5% for men [76]. One study using author-created questions (measuring past three month alcohol-related regretted sex [77]) did not report gender prevalence rates. Overall, a consistent picture of alcohol-related sexual regret prevalence was not obtained.

Gender Differences

Most studies (10 of 15) reported no significant gender differences in alcohol-related regretted sex [2, 8, 54, 70–74, 76, 78]. These 10 studies that tested but did not find gender differences varied significantly with regard to type of institution, size of institution, timeframe (since students started college, past month, past three months, past six months, past year, or lifetime), and type of participant (enrolled in an introductory psychology course, recruited through a department of psychology, new fraternity and sorority members, attendees of a sexual health program, undergraduate students). Of the studies that did not report a gender difference, a majority (seven of 10) used the YAAPST or YAACQ to measure alcohol-related regretted sex [2, 54, 70–74]. Preliminary evidence seems to suggest that, overall, there may not be a reliable, replicable gender difference in alcohol-related regretted sex measured via a single item from the YAAPST/YAACQ.

Of the remaining five studies, two studies reported that men were significantly more likely to experience alcohol-related regretted sex [17, 30], one study reported that women were significantly more likely to experience alcohol-related regretted sex [75], and two studies reported mixed results [3, 77]. Haas et al. did not observe a gender difference in alcohol-related regretted sex during non-blackout drinking experiences, but among women who reported past-three-month alcohol-related blackouts (ARB +) versus men who did not (ARB-), women were 3.62 times more likely to report alcohol-related regretted sex [77]. Orchowski et al. also reported mixed results [3]. A simple comparison yielded no gender difference in alcohol-related regretted sex, but when using multivariate models that included weekly alcohol consumption, alcohol-related problematic behaviors, and alcohol expectancies, self-identifying as a woman was associated with alcohol-related regretted sexual experiences [3].

Discussion

Alcohol-related regretted sex is linked to many adverse health outcomes, including anxiety, depression, and problematic alcohol use [2, 4, 10]. Subsequently, prevalence estimates and gender differences in alcohol-related regretted sex should be established to inform future prevention programs appropriately. A search of five databases from inception to 2025 yielded 15 studies published between 1999 and 2020 reporting the prevalence of, and gender differences in, alcohol-related regretted sex among college students. Though no clear patterns in either prevalence of, or gender differences in college students’ experiences of alcohol-related regretted sex were evident based on this review, we discuss what the present findings mean in the context of opportunities for further research.

Prevalence

Prevalence rates varied widely across studies and a consistent picture of alcohol-related sexual regret was not obtained. Studies examined alcohol-related regretted sex using items that measured experiences across many different timeframes (since starting college, past week, past month, past three months, past six months, past year, lifetime) which made observing similarities and differences in prevalence across studies difficult to dissect. However, these preliminary findings suggest nearly half (up to 48% of women and 47% of men) of all college students report experiencing alcohol-related regretted sex in their lifetime [72, 78] and approximately one third (up to 34% of women and 28% of men) report experiencing alcohol-related regretted sex in the past year [3, 17]. Though all studies took place at four-year institutions granting bachelor’s degrees, these schools included many different types of schools including private liberal arts colleges, private and public midsize universities, and large public universities. Sample sizes also varied (from 134 to 1,053), and the majority (58.8—98%) of the participants were White. Future multi-site studies might consider measuring alcohol-related regretted sex in large, diverse samples and on multiple timescales.

Gender Differences

Hookups and casual sex, many of which involve alcohol, consistently result in more negative psychological experiences for women [20, 35, 47], but whether women also experience more alcohol-related regretted sex is unknown. Whereas five studies reported significant gender differences in alcohol-related regretted sex, most (i.e. 10 of 15) studies reported no significant gender differences [2, 8, 54, 70–74, 76, 78]. Many of the studies that did not report gender differences used a single item from the YAAPST or YAACQ/B-YAACQ [2, 54, 70–74], and most studies used a binary response option of “Yes/No.” The YAAPST and YAACQ/B-YAACQ are conventionally aggregated to form one composite alcohol consequences score (which often precludes examination of gender differences in alcohol-related regretted sex). While these validated measures are widely used aggregate composites of alcohol consequences, not all consequences are created equal (i.e., missing class is perhaps less dangerous than experiencing a blackout) and men and women do not experience all alcohol-related consequences to the same degree.

Indeed, gender differences in evaluations of casual sex and hookups that are typically regretted suggest that men and women evaluate sex as regretted for vastly different reasons (e.g., [19, 79]). Women usually regret the action of having sex, while men regret the inaction of missed opportunity of having sex [14]. Moreover, women usually report sexual regret due to their partner choice, their sexual reputation, and sexual disgust [12, 14, 80]. Since men and women experience sexual regret (that does not involve alcohol) for many different reasons [12, 19, 79], it is also possible that men and women experience alcohol-related regretted sex for different reasons.

In this vein, we suggest that men and women may interpret a single question regarding alcohol-related regretted sex differently, and this single item on the YAAPST or YAACQ may not adequately capture nuance in the potential different types of regretted sex and the different severities associated with experiencing them. In fact, several studies of gender differences in the interpretation of sexual assault items suggest men and women interpret items assessing sexual experiences quite differently. Indeed, Krahé and colleagues [81] found systematic gender differences in interpretation of survey questions about sexual aggression. Administering questionnaires that measure different types of alcohol-related sexual consequences (e.g. Alcohol Related Sexual Consequence Questionnaire, containing 41 items [82]) could reveal gender differences not found in studies that use a single item from the YAAPST/YAACQ. For example, there may exist gender differences in regret related to regretting the actual experience of sex among women or missed sexual opportunities among men. Understanding whether and why women and men experience different types of sexual regret would inform the type of interventions that are needed to reduce alcohol-related regretted sex.

Five of 15 included studies reported gender differences, but no consistent pattern of gender differences emerged. That is, there was a contradictory split between studies that reported gender differences: Broadly, two studies found that men experienced more alcohol-related regretted sex [17, 30] and three studies indicated that women potentially experience more alcohol-related regretted sex [75]. Of the three studies suggesting women may report more alcohol-related regretted sex, two studies reported mixed results and only found a gender difference if analyses considered alcohol-related blackouts (ARBs) or involved multivariate models [3, 77]. Similarities and differences between studies that might have contributed to significant gender differences in opposite directions across studies were difficult to discern. However, two studies found evidence that women report more alcohol-related regretted sex used a combination of the YAAPST and B-YAACQ [3, 75], three of the five studies that found a gender difference (one favoring women, two favoring men) used author-created surveys to measure alcohol consequences [17, 30, 75, 77].

The three studies using author-created items measured alcohol-related regretted sex on four different timescales. For example, the study that assessed lifetime alcohol-related regretted sex found that men reported significantly more alcohol-related regretted sex [30]. The other three studies used a timeframe that fell in the past year (e.g., past week, past three month, past year) [17, 30, 77]. Author-created items included both binary and Likert-type response options and included vastly different wording. For example, items included “Participants […] indicated whether they had ever had a regrettable sexual experience that would not have occurred without their alcohol use” [30] with response options of ‘Yes/No.’ Other author-created questions included, “Had a sexual encounter while drinking that you later regretted” with response options of 0 (never) to 3 (happened frequently), as well as “Had alcohol not been involved, would you have changed how far you were willing to go physically?” with response options of ‘I would not have gone as far/I would not have changed my behavior/I would have gone farther’ [17].

Notably, the study that asked, “Had alcohol not been involved, would you have changed how far you were willing to go physically?” included a response option of “I would have gone farther” [17] found men reported higher rates of alcohol-related sexual regret. The way this question was worded could be the reason that men had higher rates of alcohol-related regretted sex by measuring missed sexual opportunities due to alcohol use. This is in line with studies of regretted sex that do not involve alcohol: men regret missing out on sexual opportunities compared to women [12, 13, 83]. Similarly, given women report more instances of regretted sex that do not involve alcohol, it is noteworthy that only Barnett et al. definitively reported women evince “67% greater odds of reporting” a regretted sexual experience due to alcohol use [75]. However, in the two studies that reported mixed results [3, 77], gender differences suggesting women experience more alcohol-related regretted sex emerged in models that included high-risk drinking behavior (i.e., alcohol-related blackouts, higher alcohol consumption). For example, Orchowski et al. did not find a gender difference when examining alcohol-related consequence of regretted sex when performing univariate analysis, but results from a hierarchical multivariate logistic regression revealed self-identifying as a woman in combination with higher weekly alcohol consumption, more alcohol-related problematic behaviors, and alcohol expectancies, was associated with alcohol-related regretted sexual experiences [3]. Finally, Haas et al. examined a difference in women who reported past-three-month alcohol-related blackouts (ARB +) compared to men who did not (ARB-) and found these women were more likely to experience alcohol-related regretted sex [77].

Overall, one of the few patterns that emerged in this review seems to be that studies administering the YAAPST/YAACQ generally do not yield gender differences (unless combined use of YAAPST and B-YAACQ), and studies that administer author created questions do yield gender differences (though in opposite directions). Author created questions are quite variable, likely contributing to findings of significant gender differences in opposite directions across studies. In light of these preliminary findings, it may be useful for future studies to include high-risk behaviors (i.e., alcohol-related blackouts, higher alcohol consumption) in their models to uncover potential gender differences. This is especially important because for every drink an individual consumes above and beyond the number of drinks they typically drink, they may be at higher risk for sexual consequences, including alcohol-related regretted sex [84].

Limitations and Future Directions

This was the first scoping review to document the prevalence of, and gender differences (or lack thereof) in college students’ experiences of alcohol-related regretted sex, and there are some limitations to note. Studies included in this review only reported on differences in alcohol-related regretted sex based on heteronormative identities of men and women and did not include assessments of participants’ sexual orientations or other gender identities. Because previous research has demonstrated that sexual and gender minority (SGM) groups, especially sexual minority women, consume more alcohol than their peers and experience more alcohol-related consequences [85, 86], future studies should strive to include participants with diverse sexual orientations and gender identities through the use of targeted recruitment strategies and appropriate demographic measurements [87]. Similarly, included studies (that reported Race/Ethnicity) comprised mostly White students. Previous research suggests that White college students tend to binge drink and adhere to binge drinking norms more so than Black and Hispanic students [88]. Future studies should address to what extent alcohol-related regretted sex is prevalent in racially and ethnically diverse college populations.

Futures studies should also consider individual differences predictors of alcohol-related regretted sex. Previous work suggests that certain individuals may repeatedly experience sexual regret [13]. Understanding the role of individual differences such as traits like sociosexuality (attitudes towards casual sex) or extraversions (e.g., measured via the Five Factor Model) in alcohol-related regretted sex may aid in the identification of at-risk groups as individuals high in sociosexuality or extraversion may be engaging in more sex and thus are at higher risk of regretted sex which could then aid in the development of targeted interventions [13]. Moreover, women experience physiological effects of alcohol differently than do men and not all women experience these physiological effects in the same way [89, 90]. For example, at a given dose of alcohol, women highly sensitive to alcohol experience alcohol-related regretted sex more often that do women who are not as sensitive to the intoxicating effects of alcohol [91]. This warrants the measurement of multiple individual differences, like alcohol sensitivity, alongside regretted sex.

Eleven studies used a binary (i.e., “Yes” or “No”) response option [2, 3, 30, 70–76, 78] and seven studies dichotomized continuous response options (e.g. [3, 8, 54, 71, 73, 77], which precludes examination of multiple alcohol-related regretted sexual experiences in a given timeframe, even when data were available. A psychometrically validated continuous measure is needed to fully understand the complexities of alcohol-related regretted sex. Future studies should consider conducting qualitative interviews with people who have experienced alcohol-related regretted sex. This would facilitate the developments of a continuous measure rather than a binary measure as there may be unique predictors of and consequences associated with experiencing alcohol-related regretted sex more than once. Given regretted sexual experiences data can be zero-inflated (and subsequently dichotomized), future studies may opt to use a two-part model (e.g., zero-inflated Poisson, negative binomial regression) to account for skewed distributions and enable analysis of continuous measures.

All included studies were cross-sectional, which limits our ability to ascertain how alcohol-related regretted sex prevalence (continuous or dichotomous) changes over time. It remains unclear whether college students’ risk of experiencing alcohol-related regretted sex might fluctuate throughout college, mirroring patterns of students’ experiences of other sexual consequences like sexual victimization. For example, women are at higher risk for sexual victimization during their first semester or year of college (i.e., the “red zone” [92]). Exploring whether a similar pattern of women’s alcohol-related regretted sex exists would be pivotal in developing age-specific interventions for at-risk students at different years in their college career.

Despite the plethora of studies examining alcohol-related consequences among college students (and potential gender differences), only 15 studies met inclusion criteria often because consequences data were aggregated. Moreover, two studies that did not meet inclusion criteria reported the sample’s overall alcohol-related regretted sex prevalence but did not differentiate between men and women (16% overall [93]; for each unit increase of frequent heavy drinking, odds of regretted sex increased by 46% [94]). Another study examined gender differences in alcohol-related sexual consequences—combining alcohol-related regretted sex with other sexual consequences (e.g., unwanted sex)—and did not find gender differences [51]. It is important to note that other sexual consequences such as unwanted sex or sexual assault are not equivalent to regretted sex and thus were not included in this review. Future work should aim to evaluate gender differences in individual alcohol-related consequences to increase available data on alcohol-related regretted sex and, later, to inform tailored interventions.

Implications

Casual sex and hookups are common sources of regret among college students [4, 7, 9, 21], and alcohol-related regretted sex has severe negative mental health implications including depression and suicidal ideation [9], which are especially prevalent and problematic among women. Though they experience more negative outcomes of casual sex and hookups than do men, at this stage, the literature yields limited evidence (i.e., only three studies) that women experience more alcohol-related regretted sex overall. This review highlights the need to expand measures of alcohol consequences to include different types of regretted sex because men and women may interpret a single YAAPST/YAACQ item differently, as is true in sexual assault research [81, 95]. Once studies fully characterize alcohol-related regretted sexual experiences of college students, including individual differences predictors (e.g., age, sociosexuality), targeted interventions, whether it be gender-specific or universal, could reduce the burden of alcohol-related regretted sex. In the meantime, continued promotion of harm-reduction strategies and comprehensive education on consent, communication, and substance-influenced decision-making should be implemented [96, 97].

Conclusion

Alcohol-related regretted sex is related to depression, anxiety, and trauma [2]. Though similarities and differences in prevalence estimates and gender differences were difficult to assess across studies, the current literature suggests that college men and women may evince similar rates of past year (up to one third of students) and lifetime (up to one half of students) experiences of alcohol-related regretted sex. However, results here also suggest that evidence of gender differences may emerge as more nuanced measures are used in combination with high-risk drinking behavior measures. Future qualitative and quantitative studies comprising diverse samples, individual difference measures, and multiple continuous measures of alcohol-related regretted sex are warranted to better characterize patterns across studies and inform cost-effective intervention development.
