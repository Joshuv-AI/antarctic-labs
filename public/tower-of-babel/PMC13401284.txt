Life-threatening toxicity after the ingestion of a topical minoxidil solution: a case report
Authors: Sebhatleab T Mulate, Zerubabel G Kiflu, Kalkidan A Chekole, Sintayehu A Gebru, Ephrem F Untu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMinoxidil is an antihypertensive drug. However, due to its adverse effects of hypertrichosis, it is a widely popular and accessible treatment for alopecia. Therefore, topical minoxidil, when ingested, has the potential for severe life-threatening complications. We present a rare case of minoxidil toxicity in a 21-year-old male in Ethiopia, highlighting a critical public health risk for easily accessible cosmetic products.Case presentationA 21-year-old Ethiopian (East African) male with major depressive disorder ingested 60 mL of 5% topical minoxidil solution (equivalent to 3000 mg). He presented with profound, refractory shock and tachycardia, requiring 144 hours of norepinephrine support. His course was complicated by pulmonary edema and pleural effusion 48 hours post-ingestion, managed with thoracentesis and diuretics. He did not require mechanical ventilation. After 7 days of intensive care support, he was stabilized and transferred for psychiatric care.ConclusionTopical minoxidil, when ingested, can cause severe, prolonged cardiovascular and pulmonary toxicity even in young patients without prior comorbidities. Given its widespread over-the-counter availability, greater public awareness and clinical vigilance are essential. Due to the lack of a specific antidote, management requires aggressive and sustained critical care.Keywords: Topical minoxidil, Intoxication, Shock, Pulmonary edema, Vasopressor support, Africa

Introduction

Minoxidil is a powerful direct-acting peripheral arteriolar vasodilator, first introduced in the 1970s as an oral antihypertensive for severe, treatment-resistant hypertension [1]. Its mechanism of action involves opening adenosine triphosphate (ATP)-sensitive potassium channels in vascular smooth muscle, causing hyperpolarization and relaxation, which leads to a significant decrease in peripheral resistance [2, 3]. Minoxidil is absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract into the bloodstream, reaching peak concentration within one hour of intake; it is metabolized by hepatic sulfotransferase into its active metabolite, has a half-life of 3–4 hours, and although its plasma half-life is short, accumulation at the site of action allows its effects to last up to 72 hours [1, 3].

A notable feature of its use was the frequent development of dose-dependent hypertrichosis, an unexpected side effect observed in most patients [4]. This accidental discovery paved the way for its dermatological application, leading to the creation of a topical formulation that was later approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for treating androgenetic alopecia [5]. The effectiveness of topical minoxidil may be greater in individuals with higher sulfotransferase activity than in those with lower levels [3].

Today, topical minoxidil is widely available as an over-the-counter medication for hair loss and is increasingly used off-label for beard enhancement. While the topical formulation is generally safe, its systemic absorption can occur, particularly with inappropriate application or through ingestion. Adverse effects range from local dermatologic irritation to more serious systemic sequelae, including salt and water retention, pericardial effusion, and reflex tachycardia [1]. Nevertheless, its propensity to cause significant tachycardia, headache, fluid retention, and other adverse effects makes it a less desirable antihypertensive option [6, 7]. The risk of severe, life-threatening toxicity, including profound refractory hypotension and pulmonary complications, becomes particularly significant in cases of intentional oral overdose, highlighting the dangers of its unregulated availability as a cosmetic product [8]. Here, we report severe systemic toxicity resulting from the intentional use of topical minoxidil in a young Ethiopian man. We believe this is the first reported case from Ethiopia or the African continent.

Case presentation

Initial presentation: a 21-year-old Ethiopian (East African) male presented to our center one hour after ingesting 60 ml of a 5% topical minoxidil preparation. He was found to be ill-looking and had experienced two episodes of vomiting containing ingested matter. His family reported a 2-year history of depressive symptoms, including anhedonia, sleep disturbance, loss of energy, poor concentration, and appetite changes.

Upon presentation, he was profoundly hypotensive with a blood pressure of 70/40 mmHg, a pulse rate of 130 beats/min, and a respiratory rate of 30 breaths/min. He was afebrile, and a random blood sugar was 129 mg/dl. Gastric lavage was performed via a Ryle's tube, yielding 200 ml of clear, non-odorous fluid. He was administered two bags of normal saline over one hour; however, his mean arterial pressure (MAP) remained below 65 mmHg. Consequently, a noradrenaline infusion was initiated at 0.5 mcg/kg/min (based on a weight of 82 kg), which successfully maintained his MAP above 65 mmHg. Adequate urine output was also observed. An initial electrocardiogram demonstrated sinus tachycardia, while chest X-ray and echocardiography were unremarkable.

The patient required continued vasopressor support for the subsequent 48 hours. Attempts to titrate down the noradrenaline infusion resulted in hypotension, necessitating a return to the previous dose to sustain hemodynamic stability.

Course during admission

Over the next 48 hours, the patient remained dependent on norepinephrine; attempts to reduce the infusion resulted in hypotension, requiring a return to the previous dose. At 48 hours after admission, the patient developed progressive shortness of breath, coughing, blood-stained frothy sputum, and chest pain.

His vital signs revealed tachycardia (130–140 beats/min), tachypnea (35–40 breaths/min), and a high oxygen requirement (SpO₂ was 77% on room air and 95% on a 7L face mask). On auscultation of the chest, fine crackles were heard on both sides, with absent air entry and stony dullness to percussion over the left base.

Bedside ultrasound confirmed a moderate left-sided anechoic pleural effusion and bilateral dense B-lines, consistent with pulmonary edema (Fig. 1). A chest X-ray supported these findings (Fig. 2). A diagnostic and therapeutic thoracentesis was performed, yielding straw-colored fluid. Analysis indicated a transudate (pH 7.2, total cholesterol 27 mg/dl, total protein 2.1 g/dl, Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH) 125 IU/L). Gram stain and culture were negative, and cytology showed only reactive mesothelial cells.

Initial chest X-ray at presentation was unremarkable. A repeat X-ray after clinical deterioration showed left mid and lower zone consolidation, right para-cardiac consolidation (red arrow), and bilateral pleural effusion (left greater than right, blue arrow), consistent with pulmonary edema; pulmonary hemorrhage was considered in the differential diagnosis

After 72 hours in the intensive care unit, he developed new-onset right-sided chest pain. The physical examination revealed absent air entry and stony dullness on the right lower chest, accompanied by decreased tactile fremitus. A follow-up bedside ultrasound revealed a new, mild right-sided pleural effusion. A repeated echocardiogram showed a minimal pericardial effusion. He was started on intravenous furosemide with a 40 mg bolus, followed by a 15 mg/hour infusion, which resulted in a urine output of 2.5–3 L over 24 hours.

In the following days, the furosemide infusion and noradrenaline were continued to achieve a negative 24-hour fluid balance. Both supportive requirements gradually decreased over time.

Investigations

Laboratory investigations revealed a hemoglobin level of 16 g/dL. Total leukocyte count, platelet count, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and C-reactive protein (CRP) were within normal limits. Renal and liver function tests, serum electrolytes, thyroid function, fasting blood sugar, vitamin D, and calcium levels were all within the normal range. Urinalysis was normal. Serology for hepatitis B, hepatitis C, and human immunodeficiency virus was negative.

Treatment outcome

The patient required noradrenaline infusion for approximately 144 hours. After 7 days in the Intensive Care Unit (ICU), he was stabilized with a peripheral pulse of 80–90 beats/min, a respiratory rate of 24 breaths/min, and SpO₂ of 95% on room air. His MAP was persistently above 65 mmHg, and he was ambulating and feeding well. Due to persistent suicidal ideation and depressive symptoms, he was transferred to an inpatient psychiatric treatment center for further management.

Discussion

Our patient exhibited systemic manifestations of minoxidil overdose following the ingestion of 60 mL of a 5% topical solution, equivalent to 3000 mg of minoxidil. This dose is approximately 30 times higher than the recommended topical application. Commercially available formulations are typically 2% (1200 mg) or 5% (3000 mg) and contain excipients such as ethanol and propylene glycol in an aqueous base [9].

A systematic review by Williams et al. [10] identified accidental ingestion of topical minoxidil as a growing pediatric concern. Among 442 children exposed to oral minoxidil, 4.8% of cases were unintentional. This highlights an emerging public health issue, particularly as topical minoxidil becomes more available over the counter for adult hair loss. It also underscores the real-world risk of poisoning in young children, the lack of formal pediatric dosing safety data, and the dangers of high-dose ingestion [10]. These risks may be especially pertinent in settings where over-the-counter medication regulation may be less stringent.

The patient presented to a medical center within hours of ingestion, where gastric lavage was performed. This intervention likely played a significant role in reducing further gastrointestinal absorption and potentially attenuated the ultimate severity of the systemic toxicity. As documented in most case reports, a hallmark of acute minoxidil ingestion is the rapid onset of hypotension and tachycardia [11]. Pulmonary edema is a less common complication, reported in approximately 2% of cases [12]. Our patient developed pulmonary edema approximately 48 hours post-ingestion.

A case report from 2016 illustrates a similar presentation: a 58-year-old hypertensive male on telmisartan ingested 20 mL of a 5% topical solution and subsequently developed hypotension (90/70 mmHg) and tachycardia (120 bpm) [11]. The clinical course was complicated by persistent hypotension, requiring 28 hours of vasopressor support, and pulmonary edema necessitating 12 hours of mechanical ventilation. The patient was stabilized after 4 days of observation and referred for psychiatric evaluation.

The clinical presentation of those reported cases is quite similar to our own. The shorter duration of vasopressor support in the former case may be attributable to the lower ingested dose and the patient's pre-existing hypertension, which might have influenced his vascular responsiveness. Notably, our patient did not require mechanical ventilation.

The diagnosis of minoxidil poisoning is primarily clinical, guided by continuous hemodynamic monitoring. Regarding management, multiple case reports indicate that early and aggressive intravenous fluid resuscitation, coupled with vasopressor support, is crucial for preventing clinical deterioration, and most patients achieve full recovery [12–14].

This is consistent with a report by Simon M et al. which described a case of refractory vasoplegic shock following the ingestion of 120 mL of a 5% minoxidil solution, requiring 180 hours of norepinephrine infusion to maintain a target mean arterial pressure [12]. Our patient similarly required prolonged vasopressor support for approximately 144 hours. The prolonged shock observed in our patient can be attributed to both the extensive ingested dose of minoxidil and its long tissue half-life [12].

A 2016 case report from Goce Delčev University described a 64-year-old man who ingested approximately 60 mL of a 2% minoxidil solution and presented 12 hours later with severe hypotension, tachycardia, and anuria, requiring intravenous fluids, vasopressors, and two sessions of hemodialysis before stabilization and discharge [15]. It is important to note that minoxidil overdose has no specific antidote and is not dialyzable due to its high protein binding. Hemodialysis, therefore, plays a role only in supporting renal failure secondary to persistent hypotension, rather than in eliminating minoxidil from the circulation [15].

A review of 14 reported minoxidil overdose cases found that systemic symptoms occurred in 10 patients after ingestion of a topical formulation, three after tablet ingestion, and one after excessive scalp application. All 14 patients developed hypotension and/or tachycardia within six hours of ingesting 100–3000 mg of minoxidil. One patient suffered a myocardial infarction, but no deaths were reported. Notably, fluid therapy was ineffective for hypotension. Given that minoxidil reduces systemic vascular resistance via arteriolar vasodilation, peripherally acting alpha-adrenergic agonists (for example, phenylephrine, midodrine, norepinephrine) may be safer and more effective than dopamine or epinephrine [16].

The vasodilation caused by minoxidil activates the Renin Angiotensin Alosterone System (RAAS), which in turn promotes retention of sodium and water. This sequence of events not only explains the adverse effects observed in our patient, but also clarifies the therapeutic efficacy of furosemide in counteracting these manifestations [17].

Numerous other reports describe unusual presentations following topical minoxidil ingestion, including symptoms such as unstable angina with electrocardiographic ST-segment changes, acute heart failure, acute kidney injury, and various nonspecific electrocardiogram (ECG) abnormalities [18–20].

With appropriate intensive care, the survival rate is good, although massive overdoses may necessitate extended vasopressor support. In a review of 14 cases by Kakurai and Moriyama, no deaths were reported, highlighting that prompt and aggressive early treatment is crucial [16].

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that intentional ingestion of topical minoxidil can precipitate severe, life-threatening toxicity characterized by profound and refractory cardiovascular shock, tachyarrhythmias, pulmonary edema, and significant pleural effusions. The prolonged clinical course, necessitating extended vasopressor and diuretic support, underscores the drug's potent systemic effects and long duration of action. As an easily accessible over-the-counter product, heightened public and clinical awareness of its potential for severe poisoning is imperative.
