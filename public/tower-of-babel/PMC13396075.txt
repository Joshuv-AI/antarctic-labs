Spectral Composition of Ambient White Light Modulates Photophobia and Headache Intensity During Migraine Attacks
Authors: Nina Sharp, Mahya Fani, Todd J Schwedt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPhotophobia is a common and disabling symptom among those with migraine. Key Summary Points

Photophobia is a highly disabling symptom for those with migraine.This study tested whether spectrally modified white light differentially affects migraine headache intensity, visual discomfort, and physiological light sensitivity during active migraine attacks.Blue- and cyan-enriched white light significantly increased visual discomfort and headache intensity, whereas green-enriched white light reduced visual discomfort and was associated with the lowest headache intensity.Spectral tuning of ambient white light may serve as a practical, non-pharmacological strategy to reduce migraine symptom burden in real-world environments.These findings support future research into environmental light optimization as an adjunctive approach to migraine management.

Introduction

Photophobia, defined as light-induced visual discomfort and headache exacerbation with exposure to light, is an exceedingly common symptom during migraine attacks, frequently identified as the most bothersome symptom other than headache during such attacks [1, 2]. Photophobia often persists, although more mildly, between migraine attacks [3, 4]. Light, especially bright or flickering light, is also a commonly reported migraine attack trigger and photophobia is one of the most common symptoms during the prodrome phase of a migraine attack [5, 6]. Photophobia is associated with poor sleep, greater disability, more absenteeism from work, and reduced work productivity among those with migraine [7, 8]. Despite the critical role of light in migraine, the influence of everyday indoor lighting on symptom severity remains understudied in clinical and environmental research [9, 10].

Inappropriate or insufficient daily light exposure can also disrupt circadian rhythms, regulated by the hypothalamus, itself implicated as a migraine generator, further contributing to migraine attacks [11, 12]. Many people with migraine instinctively retreat into dark environments during attacks, which may reduce overall daily light exposure and reinforce circadian disruption. In a large survey, more than 90% of individuals with migraine reported using a dark room to manage attacks, with most identifying it as a reliable pain-relief strategy [13]. However, avoiding light entirely is not an ideal strategy for preventing migraine attacks and reducing symptoms during migraine attacks. Evidence suggests that targeted light therapy, which uses the biological effects of light, may offer a promising approach for pain treatment [14, 15].

The effects of light on migraine depend strongly on its wavelength and intensity. Exposure to short-wavelength (blue) and long-wavelength (red) light has been shown to worsen headache intensity, while narrow-band green light (530 ± 10 nm) can reduce pain [16–20]. Green light activates cone-driven pathways to a lesser degree, which may explain its relatively protective effects. However, monochromatic lighting is not visually pleasant and practical for daily environments. Exposure to non-white-colored light may induce visual fatigue and reduce productivity [21, 22], whereas broad-spectrum white light provides optimal support for visual comfort and task performance [23, 24]. This highlights the need to test whether modified white-light spectra can, like the therapeutic effects of green light, reduce migraine severity while maintaining visual comfort and usability in daily environments.

The purpose of the present study was to determine whether the spectral composition of polychromatic white light modulates both subjective and physiological markers of migraine during an untreated attack. Specifically, we examined the effects of four spectrally tuned white-light conditions (blue-, cyan-, green-, and red-enriched) delivered at a standardized corneal illuminance representative of typical indoor office environments, on visual discomfort, headache intensity, and pupil size. By evaluating short-term exposures (10 min per spectrum) during active migraine attacks, our aim was to test the hypothesis that spectral tuning of ambient white light can differentially influence the intensity of headache and photosensitivity, as indexed by changes in participant’s ratings of headache severity and visual discomfort and by measuring pupillary response as an objective indicator of photosensitivity [25, 26]. Importantly, this approach moves beyond monochromatic laboratory paradigms toward ecologically valid lighting conditions, thereby addressing a critical translational gap in migraine research. Demonstrating that targeted spectral modification of everyday white light can attenuate migraine-related symptoms would establish a clinically scalable, non-pharmacological strategy capable of reducing disease burden in workplaces, schools, and healthcare settings where light avoidance is neither feasible nor desirable.

Methods

Study Design

This investigation employed a randomized, within-subject crossover design to systematically evaluate the effects of four spectrally distinct white-light conditions. The crossover framework was selected to minimize inter-individual variability in migraine phenotype and photophobia severity, thereby enhancing statistical power and mechanistic interpretability. Each participant served as their own control across conditions.

Following an initial screening and eligibility assessment, participants completed a single experimental laboratory session conducted during an untreated migraine attack. “Untreated” was operationally defined as the absence of using an acute migraine medication prior to completing the light exposure tasks; rescue medication use was permitted immediately following completion of the research procedures. The laboratory session lasted approximately 70 min and was designed to capture real-time modulation of migraine symptoms under controlled photic conditions while preserving the ecological relevance of an active migraine attack state.

Ethical Approval

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Arizona State University (Approval No. STUDY00018233) and was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki of 1964, and its later amendments. All research participants completed an informed consent process including signing an informed consent document. The informed consent document included information about study findings being published.

Participants

Eligible participants had to be adults aged 18–45 years who met International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (ICHD-3, 2018) diagnostic criteria for migraine with or without aura [27]. To ensure a clinically meaningful level of disease activity and light sensitivity, inclusion criteria required 4–14 headache days per month, a minimum of three migraine attacks monthly, and self-reported photophobia during attacks. Participants were required to be fluent in English and free from major ocular pathology (e.g., glaucoma, macular degeneration, retinal degeneration, or visual impairment) that could independently alter retinal light processing or pupillary responses. Individuals agreed to complete the laboratory session during an active, untreated migraine attack.

Recruitment was conducted using methods found to be effective in prior studies, including email announcements, flyers, and word-of-mouth referrals. Screening and informed consent were managed virtually using secure REDCap software [28]. Only individuals who met the eligibility criteria and signed informed consent were invited to participate.

Baseline Visit

During the baseline visit, participants completed a series of questionnaires assessing demographics, migraine characteristics, photophobia, and psychological health. These included the Utah Photophobia Symptom Impact Scale (UPSIS-12) [29], Visual Light Sensitivity Questionnaire-8 (VLSQ-8) [30], Patient Health Questionnaire-8 (PHQ-8) [31], Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) [32], and Visual Aura Rating Scale (VARS) [33]. Participants were instructed to avoid blue-blocking or tinted eyewear and to not take migraine medication before the study visit.

Migraine Attack Visit

Experimental sessions took place in a controlled laboratory setting (10 × 9 ft room, Arizona State University Novus Building, Tempe, AZ). All stimuli were delivered at a fixed corneal illuminance of 400 ± 10 lx, consistent with standard office lighting levels. Four spectral conditions were evaluated: green-enriched white light (LWG; λpeak = 540 nm), cyan-enriched white light (LWC; λpeak = 480 nm), red-enriched white light (LWR; λpeak = 640 nm), and blue-enriched white light (LWB; λpeak = 440 nm). Condition order was randomized using simple randomization procedures. For each participant, a computer-generated random sequence was created to determine the presentation order of the four lighting conditions (LWB, LWC, LWG, LWR), ensuring that the exposure order varied across individuals and minimizing potential sequence or carryover effects. Each lighting condition was presented for 10 min, with dark-adaptation intervals between exposures as described below.

At the beginning of the laboratory visit, participants underwent a 10-min light adaptation phase in dim light (< 10 lx, 3500 K), followed by a 5-min dark adaptation. During dark adaptation, participants completed an assessment of migraine attack characteristics, including their headache intensity using a numeric rating scale (NRS) from 0 (no pain) to 10 (most severe pain), quality, location, associated symptoms, and sensory sensitivities.

Participants were then exposed to one of the four lighting conditions. Illuminance increased gradually from 0 to 400 lx over 1 min and was then held constant for the remaining exposure. Participants rated their visual discomfort level once per minute on a 0–10 scale (0 = no discomfort; 10 = extreme discomfort). After 8 min of light exposure, participants rated their headache intensity using the NRS to capture changes in migraine headache severity. Participants were instructed to maintain forward gaze throughout the exposure period.

At the end of each 10-min exposure, pupil diameter was measured using a NeuroOptic PLR-3000 pupilometer. Pupil size was analyzed as an objective physiological measure of light-evoked retinal and brainstem responses. Pupillary constriction reflects activation of photoreceptor pathways, including melanopsin-expressing intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells (ipRGCs), as well as rod and cone inputs [25, 29, 34, 35]. While these responses may correlate with perceived light sensitivity, they do not directly measure subjective discomfort. A 5-min dark adaptation separated each lighting condition, and additional rest time was provided upon request. The entire procedure was repeated across the four spectral conditions. Statistics

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were first computed to characterize the study sample, including demographic variables, migraine frequency and severity, and questionnaire-based measures of photophobia, anxiety, depression, and aura status. Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or standard error of the mean (SEM), as specified, and categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages.

The primary outcomes were change in migraine headache intensity (pre- vs. post-exposure), visual discomfort ratings during light exposure, and pupil diameter following exposure. All primary inferential analyses were conducted within a repeated-measures framework to account for the within-subject crossover design.

For migraine headache intensity, a two-factor repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed with lighting condition (LWB, LWC, LWG, LWR) and exposure phase (pre-exposure vs. post-exposure) as within-subject factors. For visual discomfort, repeated-measures ANOVA included lighting condition and time (mins 2–10) as within-subject factors. Pupil size was analyzed using a one-way repeated-measures ANOVA with lighting condition as the within-subject factor.

To examine potential moderators of spectral effects, exploratory linear mixed-effects models were conducted with random intercepts for subject. For each outcome (headache change score, mean visual discomfort across mins 2–10, and pupil diameter), models included lighting condition, moderator (aura status or baseline photophobia score), and their interaction. Aura status was treated as a categorical variable. VLSQ and UPSIS scores were entered as continuous predictors and mean-centered prior to analysis. Significant interaction terms were interpreted as evidence of moderation.

Sphericity was assessed using Mauchly’s test for repeated-measures ANOVAs, and Greenhouse–Geisser corrections were applied when violations were detected. Significant main effects or interactions were followed by Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons to control for multiple testing. Effect sizes are reported as partial eta squared (η2) for ANOVA models and Cohen’s d for paired-samples comparisons. All tests were two-tailed, and statistical significance was defined as α = 0.05. Exact p values are reported unless p < 0.001. No imputation procedures were required, as complete data were available for all participants across outcome measures.

Results

The sample included 14 women (66.7%) and 7 men (33.3%). Most participants identified as Not Hispanic or Latino (n = 18, 85.7%); 1 participant (4.8%) identified as Hispanic or Latino, and 2 (9.5%) preferred not to report ethnicity. For race, the sample was predominantly white (n = 15, 71.4%), with additional representation from Asian (n = 3, 14.3%) and Black or African American (n = 1, 4.8%); 2 participants (9.5%) selected Unknown/Do not wish to answer.

Participants reported an average of 9.1 headache days per month (SD 2.2; range 5–13 days). When untreated or inadequately treated, headaches lasted a mean of 10.1 h (SD 10.4). With successful treatment, average duration was reduced to 2.9 h (SD 2.2). Headache intensity ratings indicated a mean average pain score of 5.8/10 (SD 1.7) and a maximum pain score of 8.3/10 (SD 1.5). Nine participants had aura, including seven with visual aura and two with sensory and motor auras.

All participants endorsed light sensitivity, with varying degrees of impact on daily functioning. On the UPSIS-12, total scores spanned from 15 to 39, with a mean of 27.29 (SD 7.59), reflecting a broad distribution of symptom burden across individuals. Participants described their baseline sensitivity to light as moderate (M = 3.29, SD 1.15). Exposure to strong light in headache-free states was rated as only mildly unpleasant (M = 2.05, SD 1.12), but during active migraine attacks, it was perceived as highly unpleasant (M = 4.33, SD 0.66). The relative increase in sensitivity during headache compared with non-headache periods was also substantial (M = 4.10, SD 0.85).

Light sensitivity was further noted to provoke headache episodes at least occasionally (M = 2.86, SD 1.31). With respect to functional limitations, participants reported the greatest challenges with computer use (M = 3.00, SD 1.20) and housework or work outside the home (M = 2.57, SD 1.12). Watching television (M = 1.90, SD 1.22), driving (M = 2.05, SD 1.20), and riding in a car (M = 1.62, SD 1.12) were also affected, though to a lesser extent. Taken together, these findings highlight that while the severity of photophobia varied among participants, light exposure was consistently disruptive, particularly during migraine attacks, and interfered with multiple aspects of daily life.

Participants completed the VLSQ-8, which revealed a moderate overall level of photosensitivity (M = 3.31, SD 0.59). Reports of light sensitivity in everyday situations, such as outdoors or in response to glare and bright colors, were moderately frequent (M = 3.33, SD 0.73). Associated symptoms, including headache and blurred vision, were also rated as moderate in intensity (M = 3.48, SD 0.70). Daily activities requiring sustained visual attention, such as reading, television viewing, or computer use, were similarly affected (M = 3.48, SD 0.66). Use of sunglasses in less typical contexts, such as indoors or on cloudy days, was less frequent and showed greater variability across participants (M = 2.38, SD 1.21). The most severe light sensitivity experienced over the past month was described as moderate (M = 3.67, SD 0.78).

On the basis of the Day of Visit Headache Questionnaire, which assessed participants’ headache characteristics prior to the start of the experiment and was reviewed by a board-certified neurologist and headache specialist, 18 participants (85.7%) were classified as having a migraine attack at the time of their laboratory visit (during an active headache), while 3 participants (14.3%) met criteria for a probable migraine attack.

Effects of 10-min Light Exposure on Visual Discomfort Levels

A two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with factors of lighting condition and exposure duration (mins 2–10) revealed a significant main effect of lighting condition, F(3, 60) = 23.384, p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.539. This indicates that the spectral composition of light significantly influenced visual discomfort in participants with migraine. In contrast, exposure duration did not significantly affect discomfort, F(1.556, 31.116) = 0.734, p = 0.456, partial η2 = 0.035 (Greenhouse–Geisser corrected), and there was no significant interaction between lighting condition and exposure duration on visual discomfort levels, F(5.975, 119.490) = 1.639, p = 0.142, partial η2 = 0.076.

Participants’ discomfort ratings showed fluctuations over the course of the 10-min exposure period (Fig. 2). Despite these variations, the relative ordering of conditions was consistent: blue-enriched white light (LWB) was associated with the highest discomfort, and green-enriched white light (LWG) with the lowest, while red-enriched (LWR) and cyan-enriched (LWC) lights fell in between.

Ratings show fluctuations across time (e.g., 1 = 1 min since onset of exposure; 8 = 8 min since onset of exposure), reflecting subjective variation in visual discomfort. Nevertheless, the spectral pattern remained consistent: blue-enriched white light (LWB) elicited the highest discomfort, while green-enriched white light (LWG) consistently produced the lowest. Red-enriched (LWR) and cyan-enriched (LWC) lights were intermediate

Estimated marginal means confirmed this pattern, with LWB yielding the highest overall discomfort ratings (M = 7.58, SE 0.26) and LWG the lowest (M = 4.78, SE 0.25). Pairwise comparisons revealed that LWB was significantly higher than all other conditions (all p < 0.001), and that LWG was significantly lower than both LWB (p < 0.001) and LWC (p = 0.011). Differences between LWR and LWC (p = 1.000) and between LWR and LWG (p = 0.072) were not significant.

To assess whether these differences persisted at the end of exposure, a one-way repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted on visual discomfort at the end of exposure (min 10). Results again showed a significant main effect of lighting condition, F(3, 60) = 19.324, p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.491, with multivariate tests confirming this effect (Pillai’s Trace = 0.766, F(3,18) = 19.616, p < 0.001). Pairwise comparisons indicated that LWB produced the highest discomfort (M = 7.71, SE 0.27), significantly greater than LWR (ΔM = 2.10, p < 0.001), LWC (ΔM = 1.62, p < 0.001), and LWG (ΔM = 2.81, p < 0.001). Visual discomfort to LWC was significantly higher than LWG (ΔM = 1.19, p = 0.049), whereas no significant differences were found between LWR and LWC (p = 1.000) or between LWR and LWG (p = 0.365).

Overall, across both the full exposure period and the final minute, blue-enriched light consistently resulted in the highest discomfort, green-enriched light the lowest, and red- and cyan-enriched lights were intermediate. Figure 3 illustrates average discomfort and discomfort at the final minute.

We next tested whether aura status or baseline light-sensitivity moderated the effect of spectral composition on visual discomfort. Linear mixed-effects models including interaction terms between lighting condition and (1) aura status and (2) baseline photophobia (VLSQ, UPSIS; mean-centered) were fit with a random intercept for subject. Although lighting condition remained a highly significant predictor of mean visual discomfort (mins 2–10; p < 0.001), neither aura status nor VLSQ or UPSIS scores significantly predicted mean discomfort, and no condition × moderator interactions were observed (all interaction p values > 0.23). These results indicate that the effect of spectral composition on visual discomfort was robust across migraine subtypes and independent of baseline photophobia severity.

Effects of Light Exposure on Migraine Headache Intensity

To determine whether participants began each condition with comparable symptom severity, baseline migraine ratings were analyzed using repeated-measures ANOVA across the four lighting conditions (LWB, LWC, LWG, and LWR). Results indicated no significant differences in baseline migraine intensity, F(3, 60) = 0.368, p = 0.777, partial η2 = 0.018. Pairwise comparisons further confirmed the absence of significant differences between any pair of conditions (all p = 1.000), supporting the reliability of subsequent within-condition comparisons.

Repeated-measures ANOVA with factors of lighting condition (LWB, LWC, LWG, LWR) and exposure phase (pre-exposure vs. post-exposure) was conducted to examine changes in migraine headache intensity after 8-min of exposure to each lighting condition. Mauchly’s test indicated that the assumption of sphericity was met for the interaction term (W = 0.898, χ2(5) = 2.016, p = 0.847). The analysis revealed a significant main effect of lighting condition, F(3, 60) = 9.477, p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.321, indicating that overall migraine headache intensity differed across the four spectral conditions. A significant main effect of exposure phase was also observed, F(1, 20) = 20.896, p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.511, reflecting an overall change in headache intensity from baseline to post-exposure. Importantly, the interaction between lighting condition and exposure phase was significant, F(3, 60) = 10.132, p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.336, demonstrating that the direction and magnitude of changes in headache intensity varied by spectral condition.

To clarify the nature of this interaction, paired-samples t tests were conducted comparing pre- and post-exposure migraine headache ratings within each light condition. Exposure to LWB produced a significant increase in headache intensity, t(20) = − 6.38, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 1.20, with an average increase of 1.67 on the NRS. Similarly, LWC significantly increased headache intensity, t(20) = − 4.48, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.97, with an average increase of 0.95 on the NRS. In contrast, LWG did not increase headache intensity, with an average reduction of 0.38 on the NRS. Although this decrease did not reach statistical significance, t(20) = 1.56, p = 0.134, the direction of the effect and the small-to-moderate effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.34) suggest a potentially beneficial trend. LWR showed no significant change, t(20) = − 0.65, p = 0.521, and did not meaningfully alter headache intensity with an average increase of 0.24 on the NRS.

To further evaluate the impact of spectral composition independent of baseline headache intensity, post-exposure headache intensity was analyzed across lighting conditions. A one-way repeated-measures ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of lighting condition on headache intensity, F(3, 60) = 20.59, p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.507, indicating a large effect of spectral composition. Mean headache intensity was highest under LWB (M = 6.90, SE 0.32) and lowest under LWG (M = 4.81, SE 0.29), with LWC (M = 5.95, SE 0.25) and LWR (M = 5.52, SE 0.31) conditions showing intermediate values. Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons indicated that headache intensity under LWB was significantly greater than all other conditions (all p ≤ 0.005), and that LWC was significantly greater than LWG (p = 0.006). No significant differences were observed between LWR and LWC or between LWR and LWG after correction for multiple comparisons (both p > 0.28). These findings further demonstrate that spectral composition of white light significantly influences migraine headache intensity.

Overall, these results indicate that blue- and cyan-enriched lights exacerbated migraine headache intensity, whereas green-enriched light did not, even resulting in a small reduction in headache intensity, albeit non-significant (Fig. 4).

To evaluate potential moderators of the lighting effect on headache intensity, linear mixed-effects models were conducted including interaction terms between lighting condition and (1) aura status and (2) baseline light sensitivity. Aura status did not significantly predict change in headache intensity (p = 0.226), and no significant condition × aura interactions were observed (all p > 0.22). These findings indicate that the differential effects of spectral composition on migraine intensity were comparable in participants with and without aura. Similarly, baseline photophobia severity, assessed using VLSQ and UPSIS total scores (mean-centered), did not significantly predict overall change in headache intensity (both p > 0.35). No significant condition × light sensitivity interactions were detected for either measure (all p > 0.19), indicating that the impact of spectral tuning on migraine intensity was not dependent on baseline photophobia severity.

Impact of Lighting Condition on Pupil Size

Pupil size after 10 min of light exposure was analyzed as an objective physiological measure of light-evoked responses. A one-way repeated-measures ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of lighting condition, F(3, 60) = 8.089, p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.288, indicating that pupil size varied systematically across spectral conditions. Mauchly’s test of sphericity was non-significant (W = 0.900, χ2(5) = 1.971, p = 0.853), confirming that the sphericity assumption was met. Estimated marginal means showed that pupil size was smallest under LWB (M = 3.32 mm, SE 0.09) and LWC (M = 3.33 mm, SE 0.10). In contrast, pupil size was largest under LWG (M = 3.56 mm, SE 0.09). LWR (M = 3.49 mm, SE 0.11) produced an intermediate pupil size, greater than blue/cyan but not significantly different from either.

Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons confirmed that pupil size was significantly larger when exposed to LWG than both LWB (ΔM = 0.233, SE 0.060, p = 0.006) and LWC (ΔM = 0.230, SE 0.064, p = 0.006). The difference between LWR and LWB approached significance (ΔM = 0.170, p = 0.057), while no significant differences were found between LWR and LWC (p = 0.102) or between LWB and LWC (p = 1.000) (Fig. 5).

Green-enriched white light (LWG) produced the largest pupil size, significantly greater than both blue- (LWB) and cyan-enriched (LWC) lights (p = 0.006). Blue- and cyan-enriched lights elicited the smallest pupils, reflecting greater light sensitivity. Red-enriched light (LWR) was intermediate, with differences from other conditions not reaching significance. Error bars represent ± standard error of the mean (SEM)

Overall, these results indicate that green-enriched light was associated with the largest pupil size, blue and cyan lights with the greatest pupillary constriction, and red light fell in between without significant differences. Notably, the pattern of pupillary responses paralleled subjective ratings of visual discomfort, with greater constriction observed under conditions associated with higher discomfort (blue/cyan) and larger pupil diameters under green-enriched light, which produced the lowest discomfort. However, pupillary responses should be interpreted as physiological measures of photic input rather than direct indices of perceived light sensitivity.

To examine whether aura status or baseline photophobia moderated the effect of spectral composition on pupil size, linear mixed-effects models including condition × moderator interaction terms were conducted with random intercepts for subject. Although lighting condition remained a significant predictor of pupil diameter (p < 0.01), neither aura status (p = 0.173) nor baseline light sensitivity measured by VLSQ (p = 0.957) or UPSIS (p = 0.836) significantly predicted overall pupil size. No significant condition × aura (all p > 0.33), condition × VLSQ (all p > 0.10), or condition × UPSIS (all p > 0.15) interactions were observed. These findings indicate that the differential pupillary responses to spectrally tuned white light were consistent across migraine subtypes and independent of baseline photophobia severity.

Discussion

Previous investigations have demonstrated that short-wavelength (blue) and long-wavelength (red) monochromatic light can exacerbate migraine headache pain, whereas narrow-band green light may attenuate headache severity [16–20]. However, these studies were conducted under highly controlled laboratory conditions using saturated monochromatic stimuli, often delivered in dim environments [17, 19] or through direct retinal stimulation (e.g., Ganzfeld Color Dome) [18]. While mechanistically informative, such paradigms lack ecological validity and are not readily translatable to real-world indoor lighting environments encountered by patients.

The present study directly addresses this translational gap by evaluating spectrally modified white light under realistic indoor lighting conditions during active, untreated migraine attacks. By testing green-, blue-, cyan-, and red-enriched white light at a standardized illuminance of 400 lx, consistent with typical office lighting, and using sustained exposures of 10 min, we assessed not only visual discomfort but also headache intensity and pupillary responses as an objective index of photosensitivity [25, 36]. This integrated approach allows simultaneous evaluation of sensory discomfort, physiological reactivity, and pain modulation under conditions that closely approximate real-world light exposure.

Clinically, our findings indicate that the spectral composition of ambient white light meaningfully influences migraine symptom expression. Green-enriched white light was associated with the lowest visual discomfort, the greatest pupillary size, and less intense migraine headache. In contrast, blue- and cyan-enriched white light exacerbated pain and discomfort and produced stronger pupillary constriction, while red-enriched light demonstrated intermediate effects. Although prior work has shown that narrowband red light can exacerbate migraine symptoms [37], the present findings suggest that embedding long-wavelength content within a broad-spectrum white-light stimulus may attenuate, but not eliminate, its adverse effects, highlighting the importance of spectral context in shaping photophobic responses. These symptom changes occurred quickly, within 8–10 min of exposure during an untreated attack. The time course observed in this study suggests that spectral modification of ambient lighting may offer an immediate, adjunctive strategy for symptom relief during migraine attacks, especially in situations where the person with migraine cannot withdraw to a dark environment and when they are waiting for acute migraine medications to take effect.

The differential spectral effects observed in this study are consistent with emerging models of migraine-related photophobia involving both image-forming and non-image-forming retinal pathways. Intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells (ipRGCs), which are maximally sensitive to short-wavelength light, project to posterior thalamic nuclei involved in nociceptive processing and sensory integration [38, 39]. During migraine, heightened thalamic responsiveness may amplify light-driven input from ipRGC pathways, facilitating trigeminovascular activation and worsening pain perception. The greater pupillary constriction observed under blue- and cyan-enriched light is consistent with increased melanopsin-mediated activation and may reflect enhanced engagement of these thalamic circuits. However, pupillary responses reflect physiological activation of retinal and subcortical pathways and should not be interpreted as a direct proxy for subjective light sensitivity, even though these measures may covary. The alignment between pupillary responses and subjective discomfort observed in this study suggests that both may be driven by shared underlying photic mechanisms, while remaining partially dissociable at the perceptual level.

Furthermore, green wavelength light is thought to activate cone-driven retinal pathways and thalamic neurons to a lesser extent than other wavelengths [18–20]. Experimental work suggests that green light may reduce cortical hyperexcitability and attenuate trigeminothalamic transmission, thereby decreasing pain intensity [16, 19]. The observed reduction in both visual discomfort and headache intensity under green-enriched white light aligns with this proposed mechanism. Notably, the absence of moderation by aura status or baseline photophobia severity suggests that these spectral effects may reflect core migraine neurobiology rather than subtype-specific phenomena.

Taken together, these findings suggest that spectral composition of ambient white light is a modifiable environmental factor with direct clinical relevance for people with migraine. Rather than conceptualizing light exposure as uniformly harmful during attacks, these results support a more nuanced approach in which specific spectral profiles may attenuate, rather than exacerbate, symptom expression. Integrating spectrally optimized lighting into migraine management plans may offer a low-risk, scalable, and immediately deployable adjunct to standard care.

Several limitations warrant consideration. Exposure duration was limited to 10 min, and the persistence of symptom modulation beyond this timeframe remains unknown. Whether repeated or prolonged exposure to spectrally optimized lighting reduces overall attack frequency or functional impairment requires longitudinal investigation. Although this study found no impact of baseline photophobia scores or migraine aura status on study outcomes, the sample sizes were modest. Future studies including larger sample sizes are required to confirm or refute our findings and further delineate whether those who report more severe migraine-associated photophobia and visual aura have differential responses to different lighting conditions. To reduce any potential carryover effects from a prior light exposure on subsequent exposures, there was a 5-min dark adaptation between each condition. Furthermore, the order of exposure to the four lighting conditions was randomized. In addition, headache intensity was assessed during each dark adaptation period. These measurements allowed us to monitor symptom recovery between conditions, and we did not observe significant differences in baseline headache intensity across lighting conditions. This suggests that, at the group level, participants returned to a comparable symptomatic state prior to each exposure, supporting the internal consistency of the comparisons. However, we cannot completely exclude the possibility of carryover effects. Additionally, interactions between optimized lighting and acute pharmacologic treatments were not examined and represent an important area for future study. Given the established relationships between light exposure, circadian regulation, sleep quality, and cognitive function [8, 40, 41], future controlled field studies should evaluate whether spectrally optimized white lighting confers broader neurobehavioral benefits in individuals with migraine. Implementation-focused research in workplace and healthcare settings will be essential to determine feasibility, scalability, and cost-effectiveness.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that the spectral composition of ambient white light influences migraine headache intensity and light sensitivity. Green-enriched white light reduced visual discomfort and pupil constriction and was associated with less severe migraine headache intensity whereas blue- and cyan-enriched lights aggravated symptoms and red-enriched light produced intermediate effects. These results highlight the potential of green-enriched white light as a practical, non-pharmacological strategy to relieve migraine symptoms in everyday environments such as workplaces, schools, and healthcare settings.
