Mouse model of X-linked Alport syndrome with K229X mutation in the COL4A5 gene
Authors: Ran Zhang, Pei Jin Bai, Weina Jiang, Xuyan Liu, Shuimi Zhang, Zhi Wang, Leping Shao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
X-linked Alport syndrome (XLAS) is a hereditary glomerular basement membrane (GBM) disease caused by COL4A5 mutations, leading to end-stage renal disease. With unclear pathogenesis and limited treatments, reliable animal models are urgently needed. In this study, the mutation K229X in COL4A5 detected in XLAS patients was introduced into mice model by CRISPR/Cas. The clinical manifestations and pathological changes in the K229X mice were characterized through urinary and serum tests, histopathology, immunofluorescence, and transmission electron microscopy. In K229X male mice, we observed significant hematuria and proteinuria, along with azotemia, and noted a marked decrease in the expression of COL4A5 at both the mRNA and protein levels within the kidneys. Pathological examination revealed glomerulosclerosis, increased mononuclear cells in the renal interstitium, interstitial fibrosis, and absence of α5 collagen IV, with histological abnormalities in the glomeruli, renal tubules, and interstitium progressing with age. Electron microscopy found irregular thickening of the GBM, accompanied by irregular layering. The phenotypic and pathological features of this mouse model are consistent with those observed in XLAS patients and other previously established mouse models. This K229X mouse model is of significant importance for exploring the pathogenic mechanisms of XLAS and researching potential therapeutic approaches.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-56834-6.

Introduction

Alport syndrome (AS) is a rare genetic disorder that predominantly impacts the kidneys, with potential involvement of the inner ear and eyes. It is characterized by by early-onset nephritic symptoms and a progressive decline in kidney function, ultimately culminating in end-stage renal disease (ESRD)1–3. AS arises from pathogenic mutations in three genes that encode type IV collagen: COL4A3, COL4A4, and COL4A54,5. The syndrome manifests in three genetic forms: X-linked Alport syndrome (XLAS), autosomal recessive Alport syndrome (ARAS), and autosomal dominant Alport syndrome (ADAS). Over 80% of AS patients have XLAS, which is the most prevalent genetic form and is linked to mutations in the COL4A5 gene6. Typically, males experience more severe symptoms than females in families with XLAS. Moreover, the male patients with XLAS exhibit a strong correlation between genotype and phenotype. The average age of onset for ESRD varied significantly across different mutation types: 37.5 years for missense mutations, 29.0 years for splice site mutations, and 24.0 years for truncating mutations7.

Despite pathogenic variants were identified in many XLAS patients, the precise mechanisms underlying the progression to ESRD remain unclear. There is a dearth of specific treatment strategies for AS, with the primary intervention being the use of renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) inhibitors, which have shown limited effectiveness8. For patients with ESRD, undergoing dialysis and kidney transplantation remain the inevitable alternatives. Hence, employing AS model animals that harbor the genetic mutations of patients is crucial for investigating underlying pathological mechanisms and for conducting preclinical research, and it is also of extraordinary significance for the development of new therapies.

Currently, a few mouse models have been established based on mutations observed in XLAS patients9–11. However, it is necessary to develop a variety of mice models that carry the mutations observed in XLAS patients to gain a deeper understanding of XLAS.

In this study, we selected the nonsense mutation c.685 A>T (p.K229X) on exon 12 of the COL4A5 gene from a male XLAS patient reported by Jennifer Adam et al., who exhibited typical clinical and pathological findings of XLAS12. We developed a mouse model carrying the K229X mutation and anticipated that the mutant mice would also exhibit XLAS symptoms. We constructed the mutant mouse model using the Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats (CRISPR)/CRISPR-associated (Cas) system and further explored whether K229X male mice developed XLAS-like pathology by measuring urinary and blood biochemical indicators, as well as observing their phenotypes under light and electron microscopy.

Materials and methods

Animals

All C57BL/6J mice were provided with a standard chow diet and water ad libitum, housed in a temperature- and humidity-controlled room maintained at 22 ± 2°C and 50 ± 10% relative humidity, respectively. The mice were kept in a 12-hour light/dark cycle. The mice were euthanized by 5% isoflurane for deep anesthesia and subsequent cervical dislocation. All animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Qingdao University School of Medicine. All experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations and in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines.

Generation of COL4A5 K229X knock-in mouse model

As described, the mutation c.685A>T (p.K229X) located in exon 12 of COL4A5 is highly conserved across species (Fig. 1a). To elucidate the possible effects of this mutation, the K229X mutation knock-in mouse model were generated via CRISPR/Cas9 technology (Fig. 1b). The transcript mouse COL4A5-202 (ENSMUST00000112931.8) was chosen to demonstrate our strategy. The project process was as follows: 1) Cas9 mRNA and gRNA were produced by in vitro transcription; 2) donor vector were constructed by in-fusion. The plasmid structure contains 5’ homologous arm (2.8 kb), knock-in, 3’ homologous arm (3.0 kb) ; 3) the mixture of Cas9 mRNA, gRNA and donor vector was micro-injected into fertilized eggs (C57BL/6J), then F0 mice that identified by PCR and sequencing were generated; 4) F0 positive mouse was crossed with wild-type (WT) C57BL/6J mice to generate F1 mice.

Genotyping

According to the manufacturer’s instructions, the genomic DNA was extracted from mouse tails using the Mouse Tissue Direct PCR Kit (TIANGEN, China). PCR amplification was conducted using primers that target the region surrounding exon 12 of the COL4A5 gene (the forward primer: 5′-TGAACTGTGTGCCTCTGATGA-3′, the reverse primer: 5′-GGGCTCACCTGATCTCCTTT-3′, product size: 651 bp). The cycle conditions were as follows: the first step is denaturation at 95°C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles, denaturation at 94°C for 30 s, annealing at 55°C for 30 s, elongation at 72°C for 1 min, and finally extension at 72°C for 10 min. 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis was used to resolve PCR products. Direct Sanger sequencing of the PCR products was performed to determine the genotype.

The mRNA expression analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the renal cortex of three K229X and three WT male mice at 8 weeks and 26 weeks of age, respectively with RNA-easy Isolation Reagent (Vazyme Biotech Co.,Ltd, China). Reverse transcription reaction and real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed with Two-Step RT-qPCR Kit (Accurate biology, China). Then, the expression of COL4A5 gene was detected. Urine and serum parameters

The mice were placed individually in metabolic cages to collect 24-hour urine samples under conditions of free access to water every week from the age of 6-8 weeks and every 4 weeks from the age of 8-24 weeks. The urine samples were then centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 20 min to obtain the supernatant. Urinary albumin (ALB) and creatinine (CRE) were measured using detection kits from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Research Institute. Mouse blood was collected via tail vein puncture, allowed to stand for 2 h, and then centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 20 min to collect the supernatant. Serum CRE and urea nitrogen (BUN) levels were measured using detection kits from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Research Institute. These measurements were carried out following the manufacturer’s guidelines.

Histological changes of kidney

To discern the histological changes in the K229X mouse model, renal tissues from 26-week-old K229X and WT male mice were subjected to histological examination, transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and immunofluorescence analysis.

Histology

Kidney tissues were first fixed in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution, then embedded in paraffin to facilitate sectioning. The resulting sections were stained using hematoxylin and eosin (HE), Masson’s trichrome (MT), and periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining for histological analysis.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

The renal cortical tissues were cut into cubes of 0.5 to 1 mm3 and pre-fixed with 4% glutaraldehyde at a temperature of 4°C. Tissues avoid light post fixed with 1% OsO4 in 0.1 M PB (pH 7.4) for 2 h at room temperature. Following the tissues dehydrate in ethanol/acetone series, infiltrate with EMBed 812 resin, polymerize at 60°C, section to 1.5 μm for positioning, cut ultrathin sections 60-80 nm, stain with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, observe and capture images under TEM.

Immunofluorescence

Paraffin-embedded kidney sections were subjected to antigen retrieval using an EDTA antigen repair solution (pH 8.0, from Wuhan serviscebio technology CO., LTD), followed by a 30-minute blocking step with 3% BSA. The sections were then incubated with an antibody against type IV collagen α5 (Sigma 3112199) overnight at 4°C. Finally, a fluorescent secondary antibody (Alexa Fluor® 488-conjugated AffiniPure Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L) was applied and incubated at room temperature for 50 min. The stained sections were observed under a fluorescence microscope.

Frozen renal tissue sections were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 15 min and blocked for 10 min with 10% BSA. The sections were incubated with Anti-Wilms Tumor Protein antibody (Abcam ab89901) overnight at 4°C. Incubate with the aforementioned fluorescent secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. After sealing with Antifade Mounting Medium with DAPI (Beyotime P0131), sections were observed under a fluorescence microscope.

Survival test

Mice that were moribund or found dead were recorded as deceased; those with > 20% body-weight loss relative to the preceding week were euthanized and sampled for end-stage analyses. The survival study was terminated when the survival of hemizygous K229X males declined to < 30%.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (Version 8.0.1; GraphPad Software). Comparing results from two groups were carried out using the Student’s t-test and two-way ANOVA. The qPCR analysis utilized the 2^-ΔΔCt method to calculate relative gene expression levels, and normalized to the geometric mean of GAPDH genes. Kaplan-Meier survival curves are used to analyze survival data in mice. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Experiments were performed at least three times.

Results

Establishment of COL4A5 knock-in mouse

Using the CRISPR/Cas9 system, we constructed COL4A5 mutant mice carrying the K229X nonsense mutation based on a mutation described in a patient with XLAS12. The injected fertilized eggs were transplanted into pseudo-pregnant mice, and the mice born about 20 days were F0 generation mice. The genotype were identified by PCR amplification and sequencing. F0 generation positive mice were mated with wild type C57BL/6J mice to obtain F1 generation mice, including 6 heterozygous female mice and 2 hemizygous male mice. The genotype of F1 generation mice were identified by PCR method and sequencing (Fig. 1c). By mating heterozygous female mice with hemizygous male mice, we successfully generated a sufficient number of WT and hemizygous offspring in subsequent generations.

Total RNA was extracted from the renal cortex of three K229X and three WT male mice at both 8 and 26 weeks of age for qPCR analysis to determine the expression levels of COL4A5 mRNA. The expression level of COL4A5 mRNA was remarkably decreased in mutant mice compared with that in WT mice. The decrease in COL4A5 mRNA levels in K229X mice was maintained at 8 and 26 weeks of age (Fig. 1d). We also observed a significant a notable loss of podocytes in 26 weeks K229X mice (Supplementary Fig. S1).

The body weight of WT and K229X male mice gradually increased from 4 to 26 weeks of age. K229X male mice of 26 weeks of age began to experience a gradual decrease in body weight, while the body weight of wild-type mice tended to stabilize (Supplementary Fig. S2). The weight ratio of kidney/body of 26-week-old K229X mice showed a downward trend, and at 32 weeks of age, the kidney weight and weight ratio of kidney/body of K229X mice were significantly lower than those of WT mice (Supplementary Fig. S3). They started dying from 30 weeks of age, and 75% died by 44 weeks of age (n = 12), and the median survival time was 38 weeks (Supplementary Fig. S4).

Biochemical analysis of blood and urine of K229X mouse

The urinary ALB levels in K229X mice began to rise above the normal range at 8 weeks of age, and which gradually increased with aging from 8 to 24 weeks of age (Fig. 2a). Urinary ALB was corrected by urine CRE. The urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR), tends to rise with advancing age (Fig. 2b). The K229X mice exhibited a progressive elevation in serum BUN and CRE levels as they aged (Fig. 2c and d).

Histopathological findings

Renal histology of K229X mice and control animals was examined using light microscopy and TEM at the ages of 8 and 26 weeks. Histopathological analysis revealed that the renal tissues from hemizygous male mice displayed significant alterations compared to those of WT mice.

Under light microscopy (Fig. 3), a few glomeruli in K229X mice of 8 weeks were observed to exhibit focal glomerulosclerosis, fibrosis, inflammatory cell infiltration, and tubulointerstitial changes confined to the periglomerular area surrounding the affected glomeruli. At 26 weeks of age, the glomeruli of K229X mice showed significant changes, with the lesions noticeably worsening compared to those at 8 weeks. No such pathological lesions were observed in the WT mice at the same age.

Discussion

The COL4A3, COL4A4, and COL4A5 genes, respectively encode the α3, α4, and α5 chains of type IV collagen. These α-chains share identical structural characteristics, including a non-collagenous amino-terminal sequence of approximately 20 amino acids (7S), a collagenous domain of about 1400 amino acids containing multiple Gly-X-Y repeat sequences, and a non-collagenous carboxy-terminal (NC1) domain of approximately 230 amino acids. The α3, α4, and α5 chains form a trimer structure that maintains the normal architecture of the GBM. Disruption of the trimer structure, due to aberration in any of the three α-chains, results in the development of AS13. However, the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying AS remain not fully elucidated. The development of mouse models has played a crucial role in exploring pathogenesis of AS14. The literature has summarized a variety of AS mouse models, primarily involving mutations in the COL4A3 and COL4A4 genes15. There are only three mouse models related to COL4A5 gene mutations, which carry nonsense mutations G5X, R471X, as well as a frameshift mutation Del-ATGG9–11. This presents certain limitations in exploring the biochemical and pathogenic mechanisms of XLAS. In this study, we utilized the CRISPR/Cas9 system to construct an mouse model with the K229X mutation in the COL4A5 gene, which has been previously reported in previous XLAS patients and proven to be the pathogenic variant causing XLAS12. This mouse model provides a new tool for studying the pathogenesis and treatment of XLAS.

This study demonstrated AS-like renal pathology in hemizygous K229X mutant male mice with COL4A5. The K229X male mice exhibited increased urinary ALB and serum BUN and CRE, reduced COL4A5 mRNA expression and podocyte loss in kidney tissue, along with typical AS-like GBM changes and histopathological features such as increased glomerular volume, focal glomerulosclerosis, mesangial proliferation, tubular atrophy, and interstitial fibrosis. These pathological features are similar to the results reported in previous G5X, R471X, and Del-ATGG mouse models, and the clinical characteristics mimic those observed in patients. These results demonstrate that the K229X mouse model can simulate the disease onset and progression of human XLAS.

Some studies have indicated that the progression of XLAS may be influenced by genetic background or the mutation site within the COL4A5 gene, with mutations closer to the 5’ end of COL4A5 gene leading to earlier onset of ESRD7,16. Compared to the R471X mice with the same C57BL/6J genetic background, the K229X mutation, closer to the 5’ end of the COL4A5 gene, theoretically leads to an earlier onset of ESRD. However, the disease progression in K229X mice appears to be slightly slower. Additionally, hematuria, a typical feature of AS, can be detected in the early stages of most AS patients17. The K229X mice also exhibit hematuria around 7 weeks of age, which is similar to the clinical presentation in patients, but differs from the gradual onset of hematuria observed in R471X mice after 22 weeks of age. The mechanisms underlying these differences warrant further investigation. According to the result of qPCR, the expression of COL4A5 mRNA in K229X mice is only 13–30% of that in the wild type. Studies have shown that nonsense or frameshift mutations containing premature termination codons (PTCs) may trigger nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD) and lead to mRNA degradation18–20. Therefore, we speculate that the low transcription levels of the COL4A5 gene may be due to mRNA degradation induced by the NMD mechanism.

Interestingly, Eri Okada et al. revealed that that the K229X, assessed as a nonsense variant, generated both exon 12 skipping and non-skipping transcripts through minigene splicing assay21. The exon 12 skipping results in an in-frame deletion rather than being truncated peptides, The transcripts of which possess intact 7S and NC1 domains, and retain partial functionality of the α5 chain of type IV collagen. To some extent, this may explain the result that the K229X mutation has a relatively mild phenotype. This is also the first mouse model carrying a mutation that could potentially affect abnormal splicing, which is of great significance for our further study of the pathogenic mechanisms of Alport syndrome with different mutation types.

In conclusion, we have successfully engineered a unique mouse model featuring a nonsense mutation (K229X) in exon 12 of the COL4A5 gene. This model was utilized to validate the presence of biochemical, histological, and pathological changes characteristic of AS-like pathology.This model is not only crucial for the development of therapeutic strategies but also for deciphering the genetic and pathogenic complexities associated with AS. It holds the potential to shed light on the etiology of AS and acts as a reliable pre-clinical framework for evaluating potential pharmacological interventions for AS and related renal disorders.
