Maximal growth of the Stein-Wainger oscillatory integral
Cheng Zhang and Zhifei Zhu

Abstract
. We establish a precise hierarchy for the maximal growth of the Stein-Wainger oscillatory integral as the regularity of the phase varies over Denjoy-Carleman classes, such as the Gevrey classes and their generalizations. In particular, we resolve a problem posed by Wang–Zhang [ 28 ] , motivated by eigenfunction restriction estimates on curves, and also provide a new proof of a theorem of Nagel–Wainger [ 14 ] on the Hilbert transform along curves. A key ingredient is the sharp estimate on the growth of a phase near a flat point.



1.
Introduction

Let
I=[-1,1]
, and let
\psi\in C^{1}(I)
be a real-valued function. For
\lambda\gg 1
, we define the Stein–Wainger oscillatory integral by

(1.1)

m(\lambda):=\mathrm{p.v.}\int_{I}e^{i\lambda\psi(t)}\,\frac{dt}{t}.

The main objective of this paper is to identify the exact relationship between the regularity of
\psi
and the maximal possible growth of
|m(\lambda)|
as
\lambda\to\infty
. Throughout this paper, we assume that
\lambda
is sufficiently large. We also note that, it is harmless to replace the interval
I
by any bounded interval with
0
in its interior, since the contribution of the integral outside a fixed neighborhood of
0
is
O(1)
and is therefore negligible for our purposes.

1.1.
Main results

Our main results establish a new hierarchy of upper bounds, together with matching lower bounds, reflecting the regularity of the phase.

First, for
C^{\alpha}
phases, the maximal growth of
m(\lambda)
is logarithmic.

Proposition 1.1
.

If
\psi\in C^{\alpha}(I)
for some integer
\alpha\geq 1
, then
m(\lambda)=O(\log\lambda)
. This bound is sharp: there exists a real-valued
\psi\in C^{\alpha}(I)
such that
|m(\lambda)|\approx\log\lambda
.

Second, for
C^{\infty}
phases, we may slightly improve the maximal growth to sub-logarithmic.

Theorem 1.2
.

If
\psi\in C^{\infty}(I)
, then
m(\lambda)=o(\log\lambda)
. This bound is sharp: for every integer
k\geq 2
, there exists a real-valued
\psi\in C^{\infty}(I)
such that

|m(\lambda_{n})|\approx\frac{\log\lambda_{n}}{\log^{(k)}\lambda_{n}}\quad\text{along a sequence }\lambda_{n}\to\infty.

Third, we may further improve the maximal growth for phases in the Gevrey classes. Recall that for
s\geq 1
, a function
\psi\in C^{\infty}(I)
belongs to the Gevrey class

G^{s}(I)
if there exists a constant
K>0
such that for all
n\geq 0

\sup_{t\in I}|\psi^{(n)}(t)|\leq K^{n+1}(n!)^{s}.

In particular,
G^{1}=C^{\omega}
is exactly the class of real-analytic functions. Thus Gevrey classes describe a regularity intermediate between smoothness and analyticity. Since their introduction in
[
10
]
in connection with regularity properties of the fundamental solution of the heat operator, Gevrey classes have been employed in a variety of contexts within the general theory of linear partial differential operators, including hypoellipticity, local solvability, and the propagation of singularities. For a comprehensive definition and a detailed discussion of Gevrey classes and their applications to linear partial differential operators, we refer the reader to
[
22
]
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\psi\in G^{s}(I)
with
s>1
. Then
m(\lambda)=O(\log\log\lambda)
.
This bound is sharp: there exists a real-valued
\psi\in G^{s}(I)
such that

|m(\lambda_{n})|\approx\log\log\lambda_{n}\quad\text{along a sequence }\lambda_{n}\to\infty.

For
\psi\in G^{1}(I)
, i.e. the analytic class, it is known that
m(\lambda)=O(1)
. See Pan
[
16
]
.

Next, we introduce some refinements between the Gevrey classes and the analytic class. These allow us to see clearly how the maximal growth of
m(\lambda)
improves from
O(\log\log\lambda)
to
O(1)
. For
s\geq 1
and an integer
k\geq 1
, a function
\psi\in C^{\infty}(I)
belongs to the refined Gevrey class

G_{k}^{s}(I)
if there exists a constant
K>0
such that for all sufficiently large
n

\sup_{t\in I}|\psi^{(n)}(t)|\leq K^{n+1}n!Q_{k,s}(n)^{n},

where

Q_{k,s}(n)=(\log^{(k)}n)^{s}\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\log^{(j)}n

and
\log^{(k)}n
is the
k
-fold iterated logarithm. For instance, we have
Q_{1,s}(n)=(\log n)^{s}
and
Q_{2,s}(n)=(\log n)(\log\log n)^{s}
. Thus these classes are refinements between
\bigcap_{s>1}G^{s}
and
G^{1}=C^{\omega}
. Moreover, by the Denjoy–Carleman theorem (see Hörmander
[
11
, Theorem 1.3.8]
),
G_{k}^{s}
is quasianalytic if and only if
s=1
, since

\sum_{n\gg 1}\frac{1}{n(\log n)\cdots(\log^{(k-1)}n)(\log^{(k)}n)^{s}}=\infty\iff s=1.

The following theorem gives further improvements for the refined Gevrey classes and fills the gap between the Gevrey classes and the analytic class.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\psi\in G_{k}^{s}(I)
with
k\geq 1
and
s>1
. Then
m(\lambda)=O(\log^{(k+2)}\lambda).

This bound is sharp: there exists a real-valued
\psi\in G_{k}^{s}(I)
such that

|m(\lambda_{n})|\approx\log^{(k+2)}\lambda_{n}\quad\text{along a sequence }\lambda_{n}\to\infty.

If
\psi\in G_{k}^{1}(I)
, then
\psi
is quasianalytic, and it is also known that
m(\lambda)=O(1)
by Pan
[
16
]
. Furthermore, if
P(t)
is a real polynomial of degree
d
, then a much stronger result holds:

(1.2)

\Big|\mathrm{p.v.}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{e^{iP(t)}}{t}dt\Big|\leq C_{d}

where
C_{d}
depends only on
d
, and the best constant satisfies
C_{d}\approx\log d
. This follows from the proof of Parissis
[
18
, (3.7), (4.4)]
. Similarly, if
R(t):=P(t)/Q(t)
is a rational function where
P,Q
are real polynomials, then

(1.3)

\Big|\mathrm{p.v.}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{e^{iR(t)}}{t}dt\Big|\leq A

where
A
depends only on the degrees of
P
and
Q
and not on their coefficients. This follows directly from the proof of Folch–Gabayet and Wright
[
8
, Theorem 1.1]
. Their results are about the integral on the whole real line, while the proof still works for bounded intervals.

Remark 1.5
.

The parameter
\alpha
in Proposition
1.1
and the parameter
s
in Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
affect only the implicit constants and do not change the maximal growth in an essential way. Suppose that
\varphi
is a smooth function on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and that
K
is an odd,
-n
-homogeneous function on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that is integrable on the unit sphere. We have the standard reduction

(1.4)

\Big|\mathrm{p.v.}\int_{|x|\leq 1}e^{i\lambda\varphi(x)}K(x)dx\Big|\leq\frac{1}{2}\|K\|_{L^{1}(S^{n-1})}\sup_{\theta\in S^{n-1}}\Big|\mathrm{p.v.}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{e^{i\lambda\varphi(t\theta)}}{t}dt\Big|.

Thus higher-dimensional analogues also hold whenever the derivatives of the phase
\varphi(t\theta)
with respect to
t
satisfy estimates uniformly in
\theta\in S^{n-1}
. For instance, these hold when
\varphi
is a real polynomial or rational function by (
1.2
) and (
1.3
).

Remark 1.6
.

Given the gap between the Gevrey classes and the
C^{\infty}
class, it is also important to examine classes of smooth functions that lie outside every Gevrey class. For instance, Jézéquel
[
12
]
established a trace formula conjectured by Dyatlov–Zworski
[
7
]
for Anosov flows in dynamical systems that holds for certain intermediate regularity classes. We will discuss them in Section
7
and their extensions in Section
8
. We will see how the maximal growth of
m(\lambda)
increases to the universal bound
o(\log\lambda)
of the
C^{\infty}
class as the classes become larger.

1.2.
Background

The problem is directly related to the singular oscillatory integral operators
T_{\lambda}
of the form

(1.5)

T_{\lambda}f(t)={\rm p.v.}\int e^{i\lambda\phi(t,s)}(t-s)^{-1}a(t,s)f(s)ds,

where
\phi
is smooth,
\lambda
is real, and
a\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
These operators and their higher-dimensional analogues have been studied by Stein–Wainger
[
25
,
26
]
, Phong–Stein
[
19
]
, Ricci–Stein
[
23
]
, Pan
[
15
]
, Seeger
[
24
]
, and Carbery–Pérez
[
5
]
. Phong–Stein
[
19
, p. 117]
showed that uniform
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
estimates for
T_{\lambda}
imply the
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
boundedness of the Hilbert transform
\mathcal{H}
along variable curves:

(1.6)

\|\mathcal{H}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\|T_{\lambda}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}.

Here
\mathcal{H}
is defined initially on functions in
C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
by

\mathcal{H}f(x)=\eta(x){\rm p.v.}\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}f(x_{1}-t,x_{2}-\phi(x_{1},x_{1}-t))\frac{dt}{t},

where
\eta\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
\delta>0
is suitably small. Pan
[
15
]
proved that
T_{\lambda}
is uniformly bounded on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
if one imposes a weak finite type condition. Later, Seeger
[
24
]
, Carbery–Pérez
[
5
]
investigated certain flat cases in which the finite-type condition fails.

In the translation-invariant case
\phi(t,s)=\psi(t-s)
, Nagel–Vance–Wainger–Weinberg
[
13
]
proved necessary and sufficient conditions under the assumption that
\psi
is even (or odd) and convex. Nagel–Wainger
[
14
, Theorem 4.1]
constructed an odd smooth function
\psi
on
[-1,1]
such that the Hilbert transform
\mathcal{H}
along the curve
(t,\psi(t))
is unbounded on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Indeed, the function
\psi
is flat at
0
, so the convolution kernel

(1.7)

m(x,y)={\rm p.v.}\int_{-1}^{1}e^{i(xt+y\psi(t))}\frac{dt}{t}

is unbounded on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Note that
m(0,\lambda)
coincides with
m(\lambda)
defined in (
1.1
) with
I=[-1,1]
. Thus
[
14
, Theorem 4.1]
is also implied by the unboundedness of
m(\lambda)
. Pan
[
16
]
proved boundedness under the assumption that
\psi
does
not vanish to infinite order at
0
. Motivated by the study of eigenfunction restriction estimates, Wang–Zhang
[
28
, Problem 4 in Section 4]
asked whether
m(\lambda)
remains bounded for arbitrary smooth
\psi
.

Stein–Wainger
[
25
]
considered this type of singular oscillatory integrals with
I=\mathbb{R}
, and they showed that if
\psi(t)
is a polynomial of degree
d
, then

\Big|{\rm p.v.}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i\lambda\psi(t)}\frac{dt}{t}\Big|\leq C_{d}

where the constant
C_{d}
depends only on
d
. See also Parissis
[
18
]
, Papadimitrakis–Parissis
[
17
]
, and Al-Qassem, Cheng, and Pan
[
1
]
. For rational phases
\psi
, uniform bounds still hold; see Folch–Gabayet and Wright
[
8
,
9
]
, Wang–Wu
[
27
]
, and Al-Qassem, Cheng, and Pan
[
2
]
. However, this integral may diverge for many real-analytic phases, such as
\exp(t)
and
\arctan(t)
. Nevertheless, our results show that if we replace
\mathbb{R}
by a bounded interval, then an interesting new hierarchy emerges for the maximal growth of
m(\lambda)
with respect to the regularity of the phase
\psi
. Moreover, our results give a negative answer to the problem posed by Wang–Zhang
[
28
, Problem 4 in Section 4]
. Furthermore, our results imply that (
1.7
) is unbounded on the
y
-axis, so in particular this gives a new proof of
[
14
, Theorem 4.1]
by Nagel–Wainger.

1.3.
Proof Outline.

First, we use a van der Corput estimate to handle phases of finite type and reduce the problem to flat phases. Second, we use either Bang’s lemma or the Taylor–Legendre method to prove sharp estimates on the growth of a phase near a flat point. Third, we use these sharp flat-point estimates to establish the oscillatory integral bounds. In particular, we obtain an abstract result (Theorem
4.1
) for general Denjoy–Carleman classes and use it to establish Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
. Finally, we explicitly construct examples in the relevant classes to show that the estimates are sharp.

Paper Structure.

In Section
2
, we prove some key lemmas on the phase and Proposition
1.1
. In Section
3
, we prove Theorem
1.2
for general smooth phases. In Section
4
, we prove Theorem
4.1
for phases in the Denjoy–Carleman class. In Section
5
, we prove Theorem
1.3
for phases in the Gevrey classes. In Section
6
, we prove Theorem
1.4
for phases in the refined Gevrey classes. In Section
7
, we discuss the intermediate regularity classes of smooth functions introduced by Jézéquel
[
12
]
. In Section
8
, we further discuss larger classes of smooth functions that lie outside every Gevrey class. In Section
9
, we prove the derivative bounds for the sharpness examples.

Notation.

Throughout this paper,
X\lesssim Y
means
X\leq CY
for some positive constant
C
independent of
\lambda
. If
X\lesssim Y
and
Y\lesssim X

2.
Preliminaries

We rewrite the principal value integral as

(2.1)

m(\lambda)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{e^{i\lambda\psi(t)}-e^{i\lambda\psi(-t)}}{t}\,dt.

Let

(2.2)

\phi(t)=\psi(t)-\psi(-t).

If
\phi\equiv 0
, then
\psi
is even and
m(\lambda)=0
identically. If
\phi(t)
has a finite order of vanishing at
0
, then standard
oscillatory integral estimates imply that
m(\lambda)
is bounded. See e.g. Pan
[
16
, Lemma 2.3]
. We give a direct proof for completeness.

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose that
\phi
has a finite order of vanishing at 0. Then
m(\lambda)=O(1)
.

Proof.

Assume
\phi\not\equiv 0
and let
k
be the smallest odd integer with
\phi^{(k)}(0)\neq 0
.
Then Taylor’s theorem gives

\phi(t)=c\,t^{k}+O(t^{k+2}),\qquad c=\frac{\phi^{(k)}(0)}{k!}\neq 0.

Hence there exists
\delta\in(0,1)
and constants
c_{0},C_{0}>0
such that for
0<t\leq\delta
,

(2.3)

c_{0}t^{k}\leq|\phi(t)|\leq C_{0}t^{k},\qquad|\phi^{(k)}(t)|\geq c_{0}.

Split

m(\lambda)=\int_{0}^{\delta}\frac{e^{i\lambda\psi(t)}-e^{i\lambda\psi(-t)}}{t}\,dt+\int_{\delta}^{1}\frac{e^{i\lambda\psi(t)}-e^{i\lambda\psi(-t)}}{t}\,dt.

The second integral is
O(1)
, so it remains to bound the first one, denoted by
m_{0}(\lambda)
, uniformly in
\lambda
.

Let
I_{j}=[2^{-j-1}\delta,2^{-j}\delta]
,
t_{j}=2^{-j}\delta
(
j\geq 0
). Dyadically decompose the integral and
rescale
t=t_{j}s
,
s\in[1/2,1]
. We obtain

m_{0}(\lambda)=\sum_{j\geq 0}J_{j}(\lambda),\qquad J_{j}(\lambda):=\int_{1/2}^{1}\frac{e^{i\lambda\psi(t_{j}s)}-e^{i\lambda\psi(-t_{j}s)}}{s}\,ds.

Define the scale parameter
\Lambda_{j}:=\lambda\,t_{j}^{k}=\lambda\,\delta^{k}\,2^{-jk}.

We claim that

(2.4)

|J_{j}|\lesssim\min\{\Lambda_{j},\ \Lambda_{j}^{-1/k}\}.

We postpone the proof of this claim and use it to obtain a uniform bound for
m_{0}(\lambda)
.

Let
j_{*}
be such that
\Lambda_{j_{*}}\geq 1>\Lambda_{j_{*}+1}
, i.e.

2^{j_{*}}\approx(\lambda\delta^{k})^{1/k}
. Then

\sum_{j\leq j_{*}}|J_{j}(\lambda)|\lesssim\sum_{j\leq j_{*}}\Lambda_{j}^{-1/k}=\sum_{j\leq j_{*}}(\lambda\delta^{k})^{-1/k}2^{j}\lesssim(\lambda\delta^{k})^{-1/k}2^{j_{*}}\lesssim 1,

and

\sum_{j>j_{*}}|J_{j}(\lambda)|\lesssim\sum_{j>j_{*}}\Lambda_{j}=\sum_{j>j_{*}}\lambda\delta^{k}\,2^{-jk}\lesssim\lambda\delta^{k}\,2^{-k(j_{*}+1)}\lesssim 1.

Now it remains to prove the claim (
2.4
). On the one hand, using
|e^{ix}-e^{iy}|\leq|x-y|
and (
2.3
), we obtain

\big|e^{i\lambda\psi(t)}-e^{i\lambda\psi(-t)}\big|\leq\lambda\,|\psi(t)-\psi(-t)|=\lambda\,|\phi(t)|\lesssim\lambda\,t^{k}\quad(0<t\leq\delta).

Hence for
s\in[1/2,1]
,

\big|e^{i\lambda\psi(t_{j}s)}-e^{i\lambda\psi(-t_{j}s)}\big|\lesssim\lambda\,(t_{j}s)^{k}\lesssim\lambda\,t_{j}^{k}=\Lambda_{j}.

Therefore

(2.5)

|J_{j}(\lambda)|\lesssim\int_{1/2}^{1}\frac{\Lambda_{j}}{s}\,ds\lesssim\Lambda_{j}.

On the other hand, write
J_{j}=A_{j}-B_{j}
where

A_{j}(\lambda):=\int_{1/2}^{1}\frac{e^{i\lambda\psi(t_{j}s)}}{s}\,ds,\qquad B_{j}(\lambda):=\int_{1/2}^{1}\frac{e^{i\lambda\psi(-t_{j}s)}}{s}\,ds.

Let
\Phi_{j}(s)=\psi(t_{j}s)
.
Since
k
is odd and

\phi^{(k)}(t)=\psi^{(k)}(t)+\psi^{(k)}(-t)=2\psi^{(k)}(0)+O(t),

by (
2.3
) we have
|\psi^{(k)}(u)|\gtrsim 1
for
|u|\leq\delta

(up to adjusting
\delta
), hence
|\Phi_{j}^{(k)}(s)|\gtrsim t_{j}^{k}
on
[1/2,1]
. Then a standard van der Corput estimate for phases with a nonvanishing
k
th derivative on a fixed interval yields

|A_{j}(\lambda)|\lesssim(\lambda t_{j}^{k})^{-1/k}=\Lambda_{j}^{-1/k}.

Similarly
|B_{j}(\lambda)|\lesssim\Lambda_{j}^{-1/k}
, hence

(2.6)

|J_{j}(\lambda)|\leq|A_{j}(\lambda)|+|B_{j}(\lambda)|\lesssim\Lambda_{j}^{-1/k}.

This proves the claim (
2.4
) and completes the proof.∎

Next, we need the following one-dimensional Bang’s lemma (Bang
[
3
, Equation (14)]
, Bruna
[
4
, Lemma 2.5]
), which allows us to control the rate of increase of a function near a flat point. For completeness, we give a direct proof of the version we use.

Lemma 2.2

(Bang)
.

Let
r>0
and let
g\in C^{\infty}((-r,r))
satisfy
g(x)=0\ (x\leq 0).

Let
(A_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence of positive numbers such that

\sup_{x\in(-r,r)}|g^{(n)}(x)|\leq A_{n}\qquad(n\geq 0),

and such that the quotient sequence

\eta_{n}:=A_{n}/A_{n-1}\ (n\geq 1)

is nondecreasing. Fix
x\in(0,r)
and an integer
\ell\geq 1
. If

\sum_{j=\ell}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\eta_{j}}>4x,

then

|g(x)|\leq A_{0}2^{-\ell}.

Proof.

The idea is to connect
x
to
0
by a chain of short intervals whose lengths are
comparable to
\eta_{j}^{-1}
and then to iterate the fundamental theorem of calculus from high derivatives down to lower ones. The monotonicity of (
\eta_{j}
) is exactly what allows each step to close.

Choose
n\geq\ell
so that

\sum_{j=\ell}^{n}\frac{1}{\eta_{j}}\geq 4x.

Set

a:=\frac{x}{\sum_{j=\ell}^{n}\eta_{j}^{-1}}\leq\frac{1}{4}.

Choose points

t_{\ell}=x>t_{\ell+1}>\cdots>t_{n+1}=0

so that

t_{j}-t_{j+1}=\frac{a}{\eta_{j}}\qquad(\ell\leq j\leq n).

For
\ell\leq j\leq n
set
I_{j}=[t_{j+1},t_{j}]
and let
I_{n+1}=(-r,0]
. For integers

0\leq p\leq q\leq n+1
, define

F(p,q):=\sup_{x\in I_{q}}|g^{(p)}(x)|.

We claim that

(2.7)

F(p,q)\leq(2a)^{q-p}A_{p}\qquad(\ell\leq q\leq n+1,\;0\leq p\leq q).

This is clear when
p=q
, and also when
q=n+1
, because
g
and all its derivatives vanish on

I_{n+1}=(-r,0]
.

Now let
\ell\leq q\leq n
and
0\leq p<q
. For every
y\in I_{q}
, the fundamental theorem of calculus gives

|g^{(p)}(y)|\leq|g^{(p)}(t_{q+1})|+(t_{q}-t_{q+1})\sup_{x\in I_{q}}|g^{(p+1)}(x)|.

Hence

F(p,q)\leq F(p,q+1)+\frac{a}{\eta_{q}}F(p+1,q).

Assuming (
2.7
) already known for
(p,q+1)
and
(p+1,q)
, we get

F(p,q)\leq(2a)^{q+1-p}A_{p}+\frac{a}{\eta_{q}}(2a)^{q-p-1}A_{p+1}.

Because
(\eta_{j})
is nondecreasing and
p+1\leq q
, we have
A_{p+1}=A_{p}\eta_{p+1}\leq A_{p}\eta_{q}
, so

F(p,q)\leq(2a)^{q-p}A_{p}\Bigl(2a+\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)\leq(2a)^{q-p}A_{p},

since
a\leq 1/4
. This proves (
2.7
). Finally, since
x=t_{\ell}\in I_{\ell}
, (
2.7
) with

(p,q)=(0,\ell)
gives

|g(x)|\leq F(0,\ell)\leq A_{0}(2a)^{\ell}\leq A_{0}2^{-\ell}.

∎

2.1.
Proof of Proposition
1.1

Suppose that
\psi\in C^{\alpha}([-1,1])
for some integer
\alpha\geq 1
. Using
|e^{ix}-e^{iy}|\leq\min\{2,|x-y|\}
, (
2.1
), and
|\phi(t)|\lesssim|t|
, we obtain

|m(\lambda)|\lesssim\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\min\{1,\lambda t\}}{t}\,dt\lesssim\log\lambda.

We remark that one may obtain a more precise estimate, namely
|m(\lambda)|\leq\frac{2}{\alpha}\log\lambda+O(1)
, by using a refined argument similar to the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Next, we choose
\psi(t)=\max(t^{\alpha+1},0)\in C^{\alpha}([-1,1])
and then

\displaystyle m(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{e^{i\lambda t^{\alpha+1}}-1}{t}\,dt=\frac{1}{\alpha+1}\int_{0}^{\lambda}\frac{e^{iu}-1}{u}\,du

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\alpha+1}\log\lambda+O(1).

This proves the sharpness of the upper bound. For large
\alpha
, this also shows that the coefficient
\approx\frac{1}{\alpha}
is essentially sharp.

3.
General smooth functions

We prove Theorem
1.2
in this section. Let
\psi\in C^{\infty}([-1,1])
and
\phi(t)=\psi(t)-\psi(-t)
. By Lemma
2.1
, it remains to handle the case in which
\phi
is flat at
0
.
Then for every
N\geq 1
there exist constants
C_{N},\delta_{N}>0
such that

|\phi(t)|\leq C_{N}|t|^{N}\qquad(|t|\leq\delta_{N}).

Hence

|e^{i\lambda\psi(t)}-e^{i\lambda\psi(-t)}|\leq\lambda C_{N}|t|^{N}.

Split (
2.1
) at

r:=\min\bigl(\delta_{N},(\lambda C_{N})^{-1/N}\bigr).

Then

|m(\lambda)|\leq\int_{0}^{r}\lambda C_{N}t^{N-1}\,dt+2\int_{r}^{1}\frac{dt}{t}\leq\frac{1}{N}+\frac{2}{N}\log\lambda+O(1).

Dividing by
\log\lambda
and then letting
\lambda\to\infty
, followed by
N\to\infty
, yields
m(\lambda)=o(\log\lambda)
as claimed.

3.1.
Sharpness

We construct examples to show that the sub-logarithmic growth
o(\log\lambda)
is sharp. The construction uses a “dyadic plateau” phase for which
m(\lambda)

grows linearly in an index
n
along a chosen frequency subsequence

\lambda_{n}
, and then we tune the subsequence so that

n\approx\log\lambda_{n}/\log^{(k)}\lambda_{n}
.

Fix
\eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}((1/2,1))
with
0\leq\eta\leq 1
and

\eta\equiv 1\ \text{ on }[3/5,4/5].

For
j\geq 1
set

\eta_{j}(t):=\eta(2^{j}t),\qquad J_{j}:=\Big[\frac{3}{5\cdot 2^{j}},\frac{4}{5\cdot 2^{j}}\Big].

Then
\eta_{j}\equiv 1
on
J_{j}
and
\operatorname{supp}\eta_{j}\subset(2^{-(j+1)},2^{-j})
,
so the supports are pairwise disjoint.

Let
\{q_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
be an increasing sequence of odd integers with
q_{j}\geq 3
.
Define

Q_{j}:=\prod_{\ell=1}^{j}q_{\ell},\qquad a_{j}:=\frac{\pi}{Q_{j}}.

Define the phase

(3.1)

\psi(t):=\begin{cases}0,&t\leq 0,\\[4.0pt]
\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{j}\,\eta_{j}(t),&t>0.\end{cases}

Because the supports of
\eta_{j}
are disjoint, the sum is locally finite on
(0,1)
.

Now we choose
q_{j}
to force a given iterated-log profile. For
k\geq 2
, fix a large integer
j_{0}
so that
\log^{(k-2)}(j+j_{0})\geq 2
for all
j\geq 1
, and define

(3.2)

q_{j}:=2\Big\lfloor\log^{(k-2)}(j+j_{0})\Big\rfloor+1.

Then
q_{j}
is odd,
q_{j}\geq 5
, and

\log q_{j}\approx\log^{(k-1)}j
and
\log Q_{n}\approx n\,\log^{(k-1)}n
.

Lemma 3.1
.

With the choice of
q_{j}
above,
the function
\psi
defined by (
3.1
) lies in
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

Fix an integer
m\geq 0
.
On
\operatorname{supp}\eta_{j}
we have
\psi(t)=a_{j}\eta_{j}(t)
and

\psi^{(m)}(t)=a_{j}\,2^{jm}\,\eta^{(m)}(2^{j}t).

Since
\log Q_{j}\approx j\log^{(k-1)}j
, we obtain

\sup_{t\in(0,2^{-\ell})}|\psi^{(m)}(t)|\lesssim\sup_{j\geq\ell}\frac{2^{jm}}{Q_{j}}\to 0\quad(\ell\to\infty).

Therefore
\psi^{(m)}(t)\to 0
as
t\to 0^{+}
for every
m
.
Since
\psi\equiv 0
on
(-\infty,0]
, all derivatives match at
0
and

\psi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
.
∎

Let
\lambda_{n}=Q_{n}
. Since
\log\lambda_{n}\approx n\log^{(k-1)}n
, we have
n\approx\log\lambda_{n}/\log^{(k)}\lambda_{n}
for all large
n
.

Lemma 3.2

(Lower bound)
.

There exists
c_{0}>0
such that for all
n\geq 1
,

|m(\lambda_{n})|\geq c_{0}\,n.

Proof.

Since
\psi(-t)=0
for
t>0
by construction, we have

-{\rm Re}\,m(\lambda)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1-\cos(\lambda\psi(t))}{t}\,dt\geq 0.

Fix
n
and take
t\in J_{j}
with
1\leq j\leq n
. Then
\eta_{j}(t)=1
and

\eta_{\ell}(t)=0
for
\ell\neq j
, so
\psi(t)=a_{j}=\pi/Q_{j}
.
Therefore

\lambda_{n}\psi(t)=Q_{n}\cdot\frac{\pi}{Q_{j}}=\pi\prod_{\ell=j+1}^{n}q_{\ell}.

Since each
q_{\ell}
is odd, we have

\cos(\lambda_{n}\psi(t))=-1
on
J_{j}
.
Thus

-{\rm Re}\,m(\lambda_{n})\geq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int_{J_{j}}\frac{2}{t}\,dt=2\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log\!\left(\frac{\frac{4}{5\cdot 2^{j}}}{\frac{3}{5\cdot 2^{j}}}\right)=2n\log\!\left(\frac{4}{3}\right).

So
|m(\lambda_{n})|\geq-{\rm Re}\,m(\lambda_{n})\geq c_{0}n
with
c_{0}:=2\log(4/3)
.
∎

Lemma 3.3

(Upper bound)
.

There exists
C_{0}>0
such that for all
n\geq 1
,

|m(\lambda_{n})|\leq C_{0}\,n.

Proof.

Write
m(\lambda_{n})=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{e^{i\lambda_{n}\psi(t)}-1}{t}\,dt
.
Split
(0,1)
into dyadic shells
I_{j}:=(2^{-(j+1)},2^{-j})
.
On each
I_{j}
we have
|e^{i\lambda_{n}\psi(t)}-1|\leq 2
and

\int_{I_{j}}\frac{dt}{t}=\log 2,

so

\int_{I_{j}}\frac{|e^{i\lambda_{n}\psi(t)}-1|}{t}\,dt\leq 2\log 2.

Summing this over
j=1,\dots,n
gives a bound
\lesssim n
.

For the tail
j>n
, we use
|e^{ix}-1|\leq|x|
and the fact that on
I_{j}

the phase equals
a_{j}\eta_{j}
(or
0
), hence
|\psi(t)|\leq|a_{j}|=\pi/Q_{j}
.
Thus for
j>n
,

\int_{I_{j}}\frac{|e^{i\lambda_{n}\psi(t)}-1|}{t}\,dt\leq\int_{I_{j}}\frac{|\lambda_{n}|\,|\psi(t)|}{t}\,dt\leq\frac{\pi Q_{n}}{Q_{j}}\int_{I_{j}}\frac{dt}{t}=\pi\log 2\cdot\frac{Q_{n}}{Q_{j}}.

Since
q_{\ell}\geq 3
, we have
Q_{j}\geq Q_{n}\,3^{j-n}
for
j>n
, hence

\sum_{j>n}Q_{n}/Q_{j}\leq\sum_{r\geq 1}3^{-r}\lesssim 1
.
Therefore the tail contributes
O(1)
uniformly in
n
.
Combining,
|m(\lambda_{n})|\leq C_{0}n
.
∎

Thus, by Lemmas
3.2
and
3.3
, we obtain

|m(\lambda_{n})|\approx n\approx\frac{\log\lambda_{n}}{\log^{(k)}\lambda_{n}}.

4.
Denjoy–Carleman class

In this section, we prove general upper bounds for
m(\lambda)
when the phase belongs to a Denjoy–Carleman class associated with a log-convex sequence.

Let
M=(M_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a positive sequence with
M_{0}=1
. We say that
M
is
log-convex
if

M_{n}^{2}\leq M_{n-1}M_{n+1}\qquad(n\geq 1).

For such a sequence, define the quotient sequence

\mu_{n}:=\frac{M_{n}}{M_{n-1}}\qquad(n\geq 1).

Log-convexity is equivalent to the monotonicity of
(\mu_{n})_{n\geq 1}
.

For a log-convex sequence
M
, a function
\psi\in C^{\infty}([-1,1])
belongs to the Denjoy–Carleman class

\mathcal{C}_{M}([-1,1])
if there exists a constant
K>0
such that for all
n\geq 0

(4.1)

\sup_{t\in[-1,1]}|\psi^{(n)}(t)|\leq K^{n+1}M_{n}.

The Gevrey class
G^{s}
and its refinements
G_{k}^{s}
are important special cases of the Denjoy–Carleman class. We first establish a general theorem for the Denjoy-Carleman class in this section, and then discuss concrete estimates and sharpness examples for the Gevrey class
G^{s}
and its refinements
G_{k}^{s}
in the next two sections.

The central quantity below is the tail function

(4.2)

T_{M}(N):=\sum_{j\geq N}\frac{1}{\mu_{j}},

with the convention
T_{M}(N)=\infty
if the series diverges. By the Denjoy–Carleman theorem (see Hörmander
[
11
, Theorem 1.3.8]
), the class is quasianalytic exactly when
T_{M}(1)=\infty
. Specifically, for the Gevrey class
G^{s}
with
s>1
, we have
T_{M}(N)\approx N^{1-s}
, and for the refined Gevrey class
G_{k}^{s}
with
s>1
, we have
T_{M}(N)\approx(\log^{(k)}N)^{1-s}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
M=(M_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a log-convex sequence, and let
\mu_{n}=M_{n}/M_{n-1}
and
T_{M}
be as in (
4.2
).

(i)
If
T_{M}(1)=\infty
, then
m(\lambda)=O(1).

(ii)
Assume now that
T_{M}(1)<\infty
and
\mu_{N}T_{M}(N)\geq C_{0}
for some
C_{0}>1
and all large
N
.
Then there exist constants
C,c>0
such that

(4.3)

|m(\lambda)|\leq C\Bigl(1+\log\frac{1}{T_{M}(c\log\lambda)}\Bigr)\qquad(\lambda\geq 2).

The condition “
\mu_{N}T_{M}(N)\geq C_{0}
” roughly means that the series
\sum_{j\geq N}1/\mu_{j}
cannot converge faster than a geometric series. It can be relaxed, but it is enough for our purposes, since it holds for the Gevrey class
G^{s}

(s>1)
and the refined Gevrey class
G_{k}^{s}

(s>1)
.

To prove the theorem, we need to control the rate of growth of
\phi
near 0. We achieve this by using Bang’s Lemma
2.2
.

Proposition 4.2

(Flat-point estimate)
.

Let
M=(M_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a log-convex sequence. Let

N_{M}(r):=\sup\bigl\{N\geq 1:\ T_{M}(N)\geq r\bigr\}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0,\infty\}.

Let
\psi\in\mathcal{C}_{M}([-1,1])
and

\phi(t):=\psi(t)-\psi(-t).

Assume that
\phi
is flat at
0
. Then there exist constants
C,c>0
such that for all sufficiently small
|t|

(4.4)

|\phi(t)|\leq C\,2^{-N_{M}(c|t|)}.

If in addition
T_{M}(1)=\infty
, then
\phi\equiv 0
on
[-1,1]
.

Note that
N_{M}(r)
is roughly the “inverse” of the function
r=T_{M}(N)
.

Proof.

Extend
\phi
by zero to the left:

F(x):=\begin{cases}0,&x\leq 0,\\
\phi(x),&0<x<1.\end{cases}

Since
\phi
is flat at
0
, the extension
F
belongs to
C^{\infty}([-1,1])
and obeys

\sup_{x\in[-1,1]}|F^{(n)}(x)|\leq 2K^{n+1}M_{n}.

Apply Lemma
2.2
with
A_{n}:=2K^{n+1}M_{n}
. Then

\eta_{n}=\frac{A_{n}}{A_{n-1}}=\frac{KM_{n}}{M_{n-1}}=K\mu_{n},

which is nondecreasing by log-convexity. Therefore, if
x\in(0,1)
and
T_{M}(\ell)>4Kx
, then

|F(x)|\leq 2\cdot 2^{-\ell}=2^{1-\ell}.

Equivalently, there are constants
C,c>0
such that

|\phi(x)|\leq C\,2^{-N_{M}(cx)}\qquad(0<x\ll 1).

Applying the same argument to
x\mapsto\phi(-x)
gives (
4.4
) for both signs of
t
.

If
T_{M}(1)=\infty
, then
T_{M}(\ell)=\infty
for every
\ell
. Fix
x\in(0,1)
and let
\ell\to\infty
in Lemma
2.2
. We obtain
F(x)=0
. Thus
\phi(x)=0
for
x>0
, and by symmetry also for
x<0
.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

It is natural to estimate the flat functions by Taylor’s theorem and Legendre transform. Indeed, since
\phi
is flat at 0, we have

(4.5)

|\phi(t)|\leq\inf_{n\geq 1}\frac{K^{n+1}M_{n}|t|^{n}}{n!}=K\exp\Big(-\Phi^{*}\Big(\log\frac{1}{K|t|}\Big)\Big)

where
\Phi(n)=\log(M_{n}/n!)
and

(4.6)

\Phi^{*}(y):=\sup_{n\geq 1}\{ny-\Phi(n)\}

is the Legendre transform of
\Phi
. This Taylor–Legendre method works for the Gevrey classes and yields Proposition
5.1
, but it cannot produce the correct estimates for the refined Gevrey classes in Proposition
6.1
. For example, if
\phi\in G_{2}^{2}
, the Taylor–Legendre method only gives, for some constant
c>0
,

|\phi(t)|\lesssim\exp\Big(-\exp\Big(\frac{c}{|t|(\log\frac{1}{|t|})^{2}}\Big)\Big),\ \ |t|\ll 1,

while the correct estimate is given by Proposition
6.1
: for some constants
B,\gamma>0

|\phi(t)|\lesssim\exp\Big(-B\exp\Big(\exp\Big(\gamma|t|^{-1}\Big)\Big)\Big),\ \ |t|\ll 1.

Nevertheless, we observe that this Taylor–Legendre method can still produce correct estimates for the intermediate regularity classes of smooth functions that lie outside every Gevrey class. See Remark
7.3
.

4.1.
Proof of Theorem
4.1

By the Denjoy–Carleman theorem (see Hörmander
[
11
, Theorem 1.3.8]
) and Lemma
2.1
, we only need to prove part (ii) of Theorem
4.1
when
\phi
is flat at
0
. By Proposition
4.2
, after shrinking constants if needed, there exist
C_{1},c_{1}>0
such that

|\phi(t)|\leq C_{1}2^{-N}\qquad\text{whenever }0<t\leq t_{N}:=c_{1}T_{M}(N).

Since
T_{M}(N)\downarrow 0
, the intervals
I_{N}:=(t_{N+1},t_{N}]\ (N\gg 1)

form a shell decomposition near the origin. We write

|m(\lambda)|\lesssim 1+\sum_{N\geq N_{0}}\int_{I_{N}}\min\{2,\lambda|\phi(t)|\}\,\frac{dt}{t}

for some fixed large
N_{0}
. On
I_{N}
we have
|\phi(t)|\leq C_{1}2^{-N}
, hence

(4.7)

|m(\lambda)|\lesssim 1+\sum_{N\geq N_{0}}\min\{1,\lambda 2^{-N}\}\log\frac{T_{M}(N)}{T_{M}(N+1)}.

For
N\lesssim\log\lambda
, we obtain for some
c>0

\displaystyle\sum_{N_{0}\leq N\lesssim\log\lambda}\log\frac{T_{M}(N)}{T_{M}(N+1)}\lesssim 1+\log\frac{1}{T_{M}(c\log\lambda+1)}.

For
N\gg\log\lambda
,
by the assumption that
T_{M}(N)\geq C_{0}/\mu_{N}
for some
C_{0}>1
and all large
N
, we have for all large
N

T_{M}(N+1)=T_{M}(N)\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{\mu_{N}T_{M}(N)}\Bigr)\approx T_{M}(N).

Hence

\sum_{N\gg\log\lambda}\lambda 2^{-N}\log\frac{T_{M}(N)}{T_{M}(N+1)}\lesssim\sum_{N\gg\log\lambda}\lambda 2^{-N}\lesssim 1.

Combining the two ranges yields

|m(\lambda)|\lesssim 1+\log\frac{1}{T_{M}(c\log\lambda)}.

5.
Gevrey class

We prove Theorem
1.3
in this section. For
s\geq 1
, fix
\psi\in G^{s}([-1,1])
and define
\phi(t)=\psi(t)-\psi(-t)
. By Lemma
2.1
, it remains to handle the case in which
\phi
is flat at
0
.
We need to estimate the rate of growth of
\phi
near
0
. Since
M_{n}=(n!)^{s}
, we can apply Proposition
4.2
with

T_{M}(N)\approx N^{1-s}\quad\text{and}\quad N_{M}(r)\approx r^{-1/(s-1)}

when
s>1
. Note that
T_{M}(1)=\infty
when
s=1
.

Proposition 5.1

(Flat-point estimate)
.

Let
s\geq 1
, and let
\phi\in G^{s}([-1,1])
be flat at
0
, i.e.
\phi^{(n)}(0)=0\ (n\geq 0).

Then the following statements hold.

(i)
If
s=1
, then
\phi\equiv 0
on
[-1,1]
.

(ii)
If
s>1
, then there exist constants
A,B>0
such that for all sufficiently small
|t|
,

(5.1)

|\phi(t)|\leq A\exp\bigl(-B|t|^{-1/(s-1)}\bigr).

The estimate (
5.1
) is essentially sharp, since it is classical that
\exp(-|t|^{-1/(s-1)})
is flat at
0
and belongs to
G^{s}([-1,1])
. By Theorem
4.1
we have
m(\lambda)=O(1)
when
s=1
and
m(\lambda)=O(\log\log\lambda)
when
s>1
.
This proves the upper bound in Theorem
1.3
.

5.1.
Sharpness

Fix
s>1
and set
a=\frac{1}{s-1}
.
Define

\psi(t)=\begin{cases}0,&t\leq 0,\\[2.0pt]
e^{-t^{-a}},&t>0.\end{cases}

It is classical that
\psi\in G^{s}([-1,1])
and is flat at
0
.

For this choice,

m(\lambda)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{e^{i\lambda\psi(t)}-1}{t}\,dt.

With the substitution
u=\psi(t)=e^{-t^{-a}}
, one computes

\frac{dt}{t}=(s-1)\frac{du}{u\log(1/u)},

hence

m(\lambda)=(s-1)\int_{0}^{e^{-1}}\frac{e^{i\lambda u}-1}{u\log(1/u)}\,du.

Let
\lambda_{n}=2\pi n
and

I_{j}:=\Bigl[\frac{j+1/4}{n},\,\frac{j+3/4}{n}\Bigr],\quad j=1,2,3,\dots.

On
I_{j}
, we have
1-\cos(2\pi nu)\geq 1
.
Since the weight

w(u):=\frac{1}{u\,\log(1/u)}

is decreasing for small
u
, summing over
1\leq j\lesssim n
yields, for some fixed
c_{0}\in(0,1/e)
,

-{\rm Re}\,m(\lambda_{n})\gtrsim(s-1)\int_{1/n}^{c_{0}}w(u)\,du\gtrsim(s-1)\log\log n.

Therefore

|m(\lambda_{n})|\geq-{\rm Re}\,m(\lambda_{n})\gtrsim(s-1)\log\log\lambda_{n}.

This shows that the upper bound
O(\log\log\lambda)
is sharp.

6.
Refined Gevrey class

We prove Theorem
1.4
in this section. For
s\geq 1
and an integer
k\geq 1
, fix
\psi\in G_{k}^{s}([-1,1])
and define
\phi(t)=\psi(t)-\psi(-t)
. By Lemma
2.1
, we only need to consider the case in which
\phi
is flat at
0
. Since
M_{n}=(\log^{(k)}n)^{s}\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\log^{(j)}n
for large
n
, we can apply Proposition
4.2
with

T_{M}(N)\approx(\log^{(k)}N)^{1-s}\quad\text{and}\quad N_{M}(r)\approx\exp^{(k)}(r^{-1/(s-1)})

when
s>1
. Here
\exp^{(k)}
is the
k
-fold iterated exponential. Note that
T_{M}(1)=\infty
when
s=1
.

Proposition 6.1

(Flat-point estimate)
.

For
s\geq 1
and integer
k\geq 1
, let
\phi\in G_{k}^{s}([-1,1])
be flat at
0
, i.e.
\phi^{(n)}(0)=0\ (n\geq 0).

Then the following statements hold.

(i)
If
s=1
, then
\phi\equiv 0
on
[-1,1]
.

(ii)
If
s>1
, then there exist constants
A,B,\gamma>0
such that for all sufficiently small
|t|
,

(6.1)

|\phi(t)|\leq A\exp\bigl(-B\exp^{(k)}(\gamma|t|^{-1/(s-1)})\bigr).

The estimate (
6.1
) is essentially sharp, since
\exp(-\exp^{(k)}(|t|^{-1/(s-1)}))
is flat at
0
and belongs to
G_{k}^{s}([-1,1])
(see Section
9.1
). By Theorem
4.1
we have
m(\lambda)=O(1)
when
s=1
and
m(\lambda)=O(\log^{(k+2)}\lambda)
when
s>1
.
This proves the upper bound in Theorem
1.4
.

6.1.
Sharpness

For convenience, we denote
E_{1}(x)=e^{x},\ E_{j+1}(x)=\exp(E_{j}(x)),\ j\geq 1
. Let
k\geq 1
and
s>1
and

a:=\frac{1}{s-1},\qquad\psi(t):=\begin{cases}0,&t\leq 0,\\[2.84526pt]
\exp\!\bigl(-E_{k}(t^{-a})\bigr),&t>0.\end{cases}

We will prove that
\psi\in G_{k}^{s}([-1,1])
in Section
9.1
and that
|m(\lambda_{n})|\approx\log^{(k+2)}\lambda_{n}
along a sequence
\lambda_{n}\to\infty
in the following.

Since
\psi(-t)=0
for
t>0
,

m(\lambda)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{e^{i\lambda\psi(t)}-1}{t}\,dt.

Set

u=\psi(t)=\exp\!\bigl(-E_{k}(t^{-a})\bigr).

Write
x=t^{-a}
. Then

\frac{du}{u}=-E_{k}^{\prime}(x)\,dx=-\Bigl(\prod_{j=1}^{k}\log^{(j)}(1/u)\Bigr)\,dx

and

dx=-a\,x\,\frac{dt}{t}=-a\,\log^{(k+1)}(1/u)\,\frac{dt}{t}.

Hence

\frac{dt}{t}=(s-1)w(u)du,

where

w(u)=\frac{1}{u\,\log(1/u)\,\log^{(2)}(1/u)\cdots\log^{(k+1)}(1/u)}.

Therefore

m(\lambda)=(s-1)\int_{0}^{u_{0}}(e^{i\lambda u}-1)w(u)du

for some fixed
u_{0}\in(0,1)
.

Now take
\lambda_{n}:=2\pi n
. Then

-{\rm Re}\,m(\lambda_{n})=(s-1)\int_{0}^{u_{0}}(1-\cos(2\pi nu))w(u)du.

Let

I_{j}:=\Bigl[\frac{j+1/4}{n},\,\frac{j+3/4}{n}\Bigr],\qquad j=1,2,3,\dots.

On
I_{j}
, we have
1-\cos(2\pi nu)\geq 1
. Since the weight
w(u)
is decreasing for small
u
, summing over
1\leq j\lesssim n
yields for some fixed
c_{0}\in(0,u_{0})

-{\rm Re}\,m(\lambda_{n})\gtrsim(s-1)\int_{1/n}^{c_{0}}w(u)\,du\gtrsim(s-1)\log^{(k+2)}n.

Thus

|m(\lambda_{n})|\geq-{\rm Re}\,m(\lambda_{n})\gtrsim(s-1)\log^{(k+2)}\lambda_{n}.

This proves the sharpness of the upper bound
O(\log^{(k+2)}\lambda)
.

7.
Beyond Gevrey classes

In this section, we investigate classes of smooth functions that lie outside every Gevrey class. Jézéquel
[
12
]
proved a trace formula conjectured by Dyatlov–Zworski
[
7
]
for Anosov flows in dynamical systems that holds for certain intermediate regularity classes. See also
[
6
,
20
,
21
]
.

Let
I=[-1,1]
. Let
M=(M_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a positive sequence with
M_{n}=\exp(cn^{\alpha})
(
\alpha>1
,
c>0
). It is log-convex. We consider the Denjoy–Carleman class
\mathcal{C}_{M}
associated with this sequence. If
\psi\in\mathcal{C}_{M}(I)
, then there exists a constant
K>0
such that for all
n\geq 0

(7.1)

\sup_{t\in I}|\psi^{(n)}(t)|\leq K^{n+1}\exp(cn^{\alpha}).

It is essentially the class used by Jézéquel
[
12
]
, and it is larger than any Gevrey class since
\exp(cn^{\alpha})\gg(n!)^{s}
for any
\alpha,s>1
and
c>0
.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
\psi\in\mathcal{C}_{M}(I)
. Then we have
m(\lambda)=O((\log\lambda)^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}})
.
This bound is sharp: there exists a real-valued
\psi\in\mathcal{C}_{M}(I)
such that

|m(\lambda_{n})|\approx(\log\lambda_{n})^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\quad\text{along a sequence }\lambda_{n}\to\infty.

The following flat-point estimate is the key to the theorem.

Proposition 7.2

(Flat-point estimate)
.

Let
\phi\in\mathcal{C}_{M}(I)
be flat at
0
, i.e.
\phi^{(n)}(0)=0\ (n\geq 0).

Then there exist constants
A,B>0
such that for all sufficiently small
|t|
,

(7.2)

|\phi(t)|\leq A\exp\Bigl(-B\Big(\log\frac{1}{|t|}\Big)^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}\Bigr).

The estimate (
7.2
) is essentially sharp, since
\exp\Bigl(-\Big(\log\frac{1}{|t|}\Big)^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}\Bigr)
belongs to
\mathcal{C}_{M}(I)
(see Section
7.2
). It can be proved by the Taylor–Legendre method in Remark
4.3
. Indeed, by Taylor’s theorem and minimization, we obtain for some constant
B>0

|\phi(t)|\leq\inf_{n\geq 1}\frac{K^{n+1}\exp(cn^{\alpha})|t|^{n}}{n!}\approx\exp\Bigl(-B\Big(\log\frac{1}{|t|}\Big)^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}\Bigr),\quad|t|\ll 1,

where the minimum is achieved at
n_{*}\approx(y/\alpha)^{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}
with
y=\log\frac{1}{K|t|}
.

Remark 7.3
.

Applying Proposition
4.2
gives only a weaker upper bound:

|\phi(t)|\lesssim\exp\Bigl(-B\Big(\log\frac{1}{|t|}\Big)^{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}\Bigr),\quad|t|\ll 1.

Roughly speaking, the Taylor–Legendre method works well for the Gevrey classes and other larger classes, while Proposition
4.2
(Bang’s Lemma
2.2
) works well for the Gevrey classes and the refined Gevrey classes. See also Remark
4.3
and Bang
[
3
, p. 143]
. From this perspective, the Gevrey class is exactly the borderline between these two methods.

7.1.
Proof of the upper bound in Theorem
7.1

By the reduction above, it suffices to consider the
case in which
\phi
is flat at
0
. Let
\beta=\alpha/(\alpha-1)
. Then Proposition
7.2
gives
constants
A,B>0
and
\delta\in(0,1)
such that

(7.3)

|\phi(t)|\leq A\exp\!\Bigl(-B\bigl(\log(1/t)\bigr)^{\beta}\Bigr)\qquad(0<t\leq\delta).

Using
|e^{ix}-e^{iy}|\leq\min\{2,|x-y|\}
, we obtain

|m(\lambda)|\lesssim 1+\int_{0}^{\delta}\min\{1,\lambda|\phi(t)|\}\,\frac{dt}{t}.

Let
u_{\lambda}>0
solve
\lambda\exp(-Bu^{\beta})=1
. Then
u_{\lambda}\approx(\log\lambda)^{1/\beta}
. We split the interval and change variables to obtain

\displaystyle|m(\lambda)|

\displaystyle\lesssim u_{\lambda}+\int_{u_{\lambda}}^{\infty}\lambda e^{-Bu^{\beta}}du

\displaystyle\lesssim u_{\lambda}+\int_{u_{\lambda}}^{\infty}\lambda e^{-Bu_{\lambda}^{\beta-1}u}du

\displaystyle\lesssim u_{\lambda}+u_{\lambda}^{1-\beta}\lesssim u_{\lambda}.

This proves the upper bound in Theorem
7.1
.

7.2.
Sharpness

Let
\alpha>1
and
\beta=\alpha/(\alpha-1)
and

\psi(t)=\begin{cases}0,&t\leq 0,\\[2.84526pt]
\exp(-(\log\frac{1}{t})^{\beta}),&0<t\leq 1/e.\end{cases}

We may take
I=[-1/e,1/e]
. It is harmless and simplifies the calculation.
We show that
\psi\in\mathcal{C}_{M}(I)
in Section
9.2

and that
|m(\lambda_{n})|\approx(\log\lambda_{n})^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}

along a sequence
\lambda_{n}\to\infty
in the following.

Since
\psi(-t)=0
for
t>0
,

m(\lambda)=\int_{0}^{e^{-1}}\frac{e^{i\lambda\psi(t)}-1}{t}\,dt.

Set

s:=\psi(t)=e^{-(\log(1/t))^{\beta}}.

Differentiating gives

\frac{dt}{t}=\frac{1}{\beta}\frac{ds}{s(\log(1/s))^{1-\frac{1}{\beta}}}=\frac{1}{\beta}\frac{ds}{s(\log(1/s))^{1/\alpha}}.

Write

w(s):=\frac{1}{s(\log(1/s))^{1/\alpha}},\qquad 0<s\leq e^{-1}.

Take
\lambda_{n}:=2\pi n.

Then

-{\rm Re}\,m(\lambda_{n})=\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{0}^{e^{-1}}(1-\cos(2\pi ns))\,w(s)\,ds.

Fix

c_{0}:=\frac{1}{4e},\qquad N_{n}:=\lfloor c_{0}n\rfloor.

For
1\leq j\leq N_{n}
set

I_{j,n}:=\Bigl[\frac{j+1/4}{n},\,\frac{j+3/4}{n}\Bigr].

Since
(j+3/4)/n\leq c_{0}+1/n<e^{-1}
for all large
n
, these intervals lie inside
(0,e^{-1})
.
Moreover, on
I_{j,n}
one has

1-\cos(2\pi ns)\geq 1.

Therefore

-{\rm Re}\,m(\lambda_{n})\geq\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{n}}\int_{I_{j,n}}w(s)\,ds.

Because
w
is decreasing,

\int_{I_{j,n}}w(s)\,ds\geq\frac{1}{2n}w\Bigl(\frac{j+1}{n}\Bigr)\geq\frac{1}{2}\int_{(j+1)/n}^{(j+2)/n}w(s)\,ds.

Summing in
j
gives

-{\rm Re}\,m(\lambda_{n})\geq\frac{1}{2\beta}\int_{2/n}^{(N_{n}+2)/n}w(s)\,ds\gtrsim\int_{2/n}^{c_{0}}\frac{ds}{s(\log(1/s))^{1/\alpha}}\approx(\log n)^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}.

Since
\lambda_{n}=2\pi n,

|m(\lambda_{n})|\geq-{\rm Re}\,m(\lambda_{n})\gtrsim(\log\lambda_{n})^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}.

This completes the proof.

8.
Further discussions

In this section, we consider the Denjoy–Carleman classes that are substantially larger than the Gevrey and intermediate classes discussed earlier. Let
I=[-1,1]
, and let
M=(M_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a positive sequence of the form
M_{n}=\exp^{(k)}(cn^{\alpha})
, where
c,\alpha>0
and
k\geq 2
is an integer. For large
n
, this sequence is log-convex, so it defines a Denjoy–Carleman class
\mathcal{C}_{M}
. If
\psi\in\mathcal{C}_{M}(I)
, then there exists a constant
K>0
such that

(8.1)

\sup_{t\in I}|\psi^{(n)}(t)|\leq K^{n+1}\exp^{(k)}(cn^{\alpha}),\qquad n\geq 0.

Our goal is to understand how the oscillatory integral bound changes in this case.
In what follows, we show that as the function classes become larger, the maximal growth of
m(\lambda)
increases to the universal
C^{\infty}
bound
o(\log\lambda)
.

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
\psi\in\mathcal{C}_{M}(I)
. Then
m(\lambda)=O((\log\lambda)/(\log^{(k)}\lambda)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}})
. This bound is sharp: there exists a real-valued
\psi\in\mathcal{C}_{M}(I)
such that

|m(\lambda_{n})|\approx(\log\lambda_{n})/(\log^{(k)}\lambda_{n})^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\quad\text{along a sequence }\lambda_{n}\to\infty.

As before, the key ingredient is a flat-point estimate.

Proposition 8.2

(Flat-point estimate)
.

Let
\phi\in\mathcal{C}_{M}(I)
be flat at
0
, i.e.
\phi^{(n)}(0)=0\ (n\geq 0).

Then there exist constants
A,B>0
such that for all sufficiently small
|t|
,

(8.2)

|\phi(t)|\leq A\exp\Bigl(-B\Big(\log\frac{1}{|t|}\Big)\Big(\log^{(k)}\frac{1}{|t|}\Big)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\Bigr).

The estimate (
8.2
) is essentially sharp, since
\exp\Bigl(-\Big(\log\frac{1}{|t|}\Big)\Big(\log^{(k)}\frac{1}{|t|}\Big)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\Bigr)
belongs to
\mathcal{C}_{M}(I)
(see Section
8.2
). It can be proved by the Taylor–Legendre method in Remark
4.3
. Indeed, by Taylor’s theorem and minimization, we obtain for some constant
B>0

|\phi(t)|\leq\inf_{n\geq 1}\frac{K^{n+1}\exp^{(k)}(cn^{\alpha})|t|^{n}}{n!}\approx\exp\Bigl(-B\Big(\log\frac{1}{|t|}\Big)\Big(\log^{(k)}\frac{1}{|t|}\Big)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\Bigr),\quad|t|\ll 1,

where the minimum is achieved at
n_{*}\approx(\log^{(k-1)}y)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}
with
y=\log\frac{1}{K|t|}
.

8.1.
Proof of the upper bound in Theorem
8.1

By the reduction above, it suffices to consider the case in which
\phi
is flat at
0
. Then Proposition
8.2
gives constants
A,B>0
and
\delta\in(0,1)
such that

Using
|e^{ix}-e^{iy}|\leq\min\{2,|x-y|\}
, we obtain

|m(\lambda)|\lesssim 1+\int_{0}^{\delta}\min\{1,\lambda|\phi(t)|\}\,\frac{dt}{t}.

Let

8.2.
Sharpness

For
x
sufficiently large we write

L_{1}(x):=\log x,\qquad L_{j+1}(x):=\log L_{j}(x)\quad(j\geq 1),

and

E_{1}(x):=e^{x},\qquad E_{j+1}(x):=\exp(E_{j}(x))\quad(j\geq 1).

Fix
k\geq 2
,
\alpha>0
, and
0<\delta\ll 1
.
Let
I=(-\delta,\delta)

We will show that

in Section

and that

Since

Since

Set

\omega(s)\approx\frac{1}{sL_{k}(1/s)^{1/\alpha}}.

Using the lower bound in (

-{\rm Re}\,m(\lambda_{n})\gtrsim\int_{0}^{s_{0}}(1-\cos(2\pi ns))\,\frac{ds}{sL_{k}(1/s)^{1/\alpha}}.

For large

9.1.
Sharp example in Section
6.1

Let
E_{1}(x)=e^{x},\ E_{j+1}(x)=\exp(E_{j}(x)),\ j\geq 1
. Let
k\geq 1
and
s>1
and

a:=\frac{1}{s-1},\qquad\psi(t):=\begin{cases}0,&t\leq 0,\\[2.84526pt]
\exp\!\bigl(-E_{k}(t^{-a})\bigr),&t>0.\end{cases}

We will prove that
\psi\in G_{k}^{s}([-1,1])
.
For
x\geq 1
, write

M_{k}(x):=\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}E_{j}(x),\qquad\Omega_{k}(x):=x\,M_{k}(x)

(with the convention
M_{1}(x)=1
, so
\Omega_{1}(x)=x
).

Lemma 9.1
.

For every
k\geq 1
there exists
A_{k}\geq 1
such that for all
r\geq 0
and all

x\geq 1
,

E_{k}^{(r)}(x)\leq r!\,A_{k}^{r}\,M_{k}(x)^{r}\,E_{k}(x).

Proof.

We argue by induction on
k
.

For
k=1
,
E_{1}^{(r)}(x)=e^{x}=E_{1}(x)
, so the claim is immediate.

Assume it holds for
k-1
. Since
E_{k}=e^{E_{k-1}}
, the Taylor expansion
of
E_{k}(x+h)
at
x
gives

\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\frac{E_{k}^{(r)}(x)}{r!}h^{r}=E_{k}(x)\exp\!\bigl(E_{k-1}(x+h)-E_{k-1}(x)\bigr).

Taking absolute values and using the induction hypothesis,

\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\frac{|E_{k}^{(r)}(x)|}{r!}|h|^{r}\leq E_{k}(x)\exp\!\left(\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}A_{k-1}^{m}M_{k-1}(x)^{m}E_{k-1}(x)\,|h|^{m}\right).

Since
M_{k}(x)=E_{k-1}(x)M_{k-1}(x)
, choosing

|h|=\frac{1}{2A_{k-1}M_{k}(x)}

makes the exponent bounded by
1
. Hence

\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\frac{|E_{k}^{(r)}(x)|}{r!}|h|^{r}\leq e\,E_{k}(x).

Comparing coefficients gives

E_{k}^{(r)}(x)\leq r!\,(2eA_{k-1})^{r}M_{k}(x)^{r}E_{k}(x).

So the induction closes with
A_{k}:=2eA_{k-1}
.
∎

Now put

x(t):=t^{-a}\qquad(0<t\leq 1),\qquad g(t):=E_{k}(x(t)),\qquad e^{-g(t)}=\psi(t)\quad(t>0).

We first estimate derivatives of
x(t)
. Since

x^{(m)}(t)=(-1)^{m}a(a+1)\cdots(a+m-1)\,t^{-a-m},

there is a constant
B_{a}\geq 1
such that for all
m\geq 1
,

|x^{(m)}(t)|\leq m!\,B_{a}^{m}\,t^{-m}x(t).

Lemma 9.2
.

There exists
C_{1}\geq 1
such that for all
n\geq 0
and all
0<t\leq 1
,

|g^{(n)}(t)|\leq n!\,C_{1}^{n}\,t^{-n}\Omega_{k}(x(t))^{n}\,g(t).

Proof.

Using the Taylor expansion of
E_{k}(x(t+h))
around
x(t)
,

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{g^{(n)}(t)}{n!}h^{n}=\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\frac{E_{k}^{(r)}(x(t))}{r!}\,\bigl(x(t+h)-x(t)\bigr)^{r}.

Taking absolute values and using the previous lemma,

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{|g^{(n)}(t)|}{n!}|h|^{n}\leq g(t)\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\left(A_{k}M_{k}(x(t))\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{|x^{(m)}(t)|}{m!}|h|^{m}\right)^{r}.

Also,

\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{|x^{(m)}(t)|}{m!}|h|^{m}\leq x(t)\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}(B_{a}t^{-1}|h|)^{m}=x(t)\frac{B_{a}t^{-1}|h|}{1-B_{a}t^{-1}|h|}.

Choose

|h|=\frac{1}{4A_{k}B_{a}\,t^{-1}\Omega_{k}(x(t))}.

Then
B_{a}t^{-1}|h|\leq 1/4
, hence the geometric factor above is at most

2x(t)B_{a}t^{-1}|h|
, and therefore

A_{k}M_{k}(x(t))\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{|x^{(m)}(t)|}{m!}|h|^{m}\leq\frac{1}{2}.

So

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{|g^{(n)}(t)|}{n!}|h|^{n}\leq 2g(t).

Comparing coefficients yields the claim.
∎

Lemma 9.3
.

There exists
C_{2}\geq 1
such that for all
n\geq 0
and all
0<t\leq 1
,

|\psi^{(n)}(t)|\leq n!\,C_{2}^{n}\,t^{-n}\Omega_{k}(x(t))^{n}\,e^{-g(t)/2}.

Proof.

Since
\psi=e^{-g}
,

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\psi^{(n)}(t)}{n!}h^{n}=e^{-g(t)}\exp\!\bigl(-(g(t+h)-g(t))\bigr).

Taking absolute values and using the previous lemma,

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{|\psi^{(n)}(t)|}{n!}|h|^{n}\leq e^{-g(t)}\exp\!\left(\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}C_{1}^{m}t^{-m}\Omega_{k}(x(t))^{m}g(t)\,|h|^{m}\right).

Choose

|h|=\frac{1}{4C_{1}\,t^{-1}\Omega_{k}(x(t))}.

Then the series in the exponent is at most
g(t)/2
, so

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{|\psi^{(n)}(t)|}{n!}|h|^{n}\leq e^{-g(t)/2}.

Comparing coefficients gives

|\psi^{(n)}(t)|\leq n!\,(4C_{1})^{n}\,t^{-n}\Omega_{k}(x(t))^{n}\,e^{-g(t)/2}.

∎

Because
a=1/(s-1)
, we have
t^{-1}=x(t)^{\,s-1}
. Hence

t^{-1}\Omega_{k}(x(t))=x(t)^{s}\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}E_{j}(x(t)).

Therefore

(9.1)

|\psi^{(n)}(t)|\leq n!\,C_{2}^{n}\left(x(t)^{s}\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}E_{j}(x(t))\right)^{n}e^{-E_{k}(x(t))/2}.

n_{0}
large and define

x_{n}:=\log^{(k)}\bigl((n+n_{0})^{2}\bigr).

Then

Sharp example in Section
7.2

Let

Lemma 9.4
.

For every
n\geq 1

,

\frac{d^{n}}{dt^{n}}g(\log(1/t))=t^{-n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{n,k}\,g^{(k)}(u),

\sum_{k=1}^{n}|a_{n,k}|\leq n!.

For

\frac{d}{dt}g(\log(1/t))=-t^{-1}g^{\prime}(u),

Assume the statement true for some
n\geq 1

Differentiate

\frac{d^{n}}{dt^{n}}g(\log(1/t))=t^{-n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{n,k}\,g^{(k)}(u).

Since

\frac{d}{dt}\Bigl(t^{-n}F(u)\Bigr)=-t^{-n-1}\bigl(nF(u)+F^{\prime}(u)\bigr).

We next estimate

Lemma 9.5
.

There exists a constant

|g^{(k)}(u)|\leq C_{0}^{k}\,k!\,k^{k}\,u^{(\beta-1)k}e^{-u^{\beta}}.

Write

Now apply Faà di Bruno in the partition form:

where

is the set of partitions of
\{1,\dots,k\}
.
Fix
\pi\in\varPi_{k}
, let
r:=|\pi|
(the number of blocks in
\pi
) and let
m_{1},\dots,m_{r}
be its block sizes, so

m_{1}+\cdots+m_{r}=k
.
Using (
9.2
),

\prod_{\ell=1}^{r}|h^{(m_{\ell})}(u)|\leq C_{0}^{k}\Bigl(\prod_{\ell=1}^{r}m_{\ell}!\Bigr)\,u^{r\beta-k}.

Since
r\leq k
and
\prod_{\ell=1}^{r}m_{\ell}!\leq k!
, we obtain

\prod_{\ell=1}^{r}|h^{(m_{\ell})}(u)|\leq C_{0}^{k}k!\,u^{(\beta-1)k}.

The number of partitions of a
k
-element set is the Bell number
B_{k}
, and
B_{k}\leq k^{k}
.
Hence

|g^{(k)}(u)|\leq C_{0}^{k}\,k!\,k^{k}\,u^{(\beta-1)k}e^{-u^{\beta}}.

∎

Combining Lemmas
9.4
and
9.5
, for
n\geq 1
and
0<t<e^{-1}
we get

|\psi^{(n)}(t)|\leq t^{-n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}|a_{n,k}|\,|g^{(k)}(u)|\leq t^{-n}e^{-u^{\beta}}\Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{n}|a_{n,k}|\Bigr)\max_{1\leq k\leq n}\bigl(C_{0}^{k}k!\,k^{k}\,u^{(\beta-1)k}\bigr).

By Lemma
9.4
,

\sum_{k=1}^{n}|a_{n,k}|\leq n!,

and since
k\leq n
and
u\geq 1
,

C_{0}^{k}k!\,k^{k}\,u^{(\beta-1)k}\leq C_{0}^{n}n!\,n^{n}\,u^{(\beta-1)n}.

Therefore

|\psi^{(n)}(t)|\leq C_{0}^{n}(n!)^{2}n^{n}\,e^{nu}\,u^{(\beta-1)n}e^{-u^{\beta}}.

Taking logarithms and using Stirling in the crude form
\log(n!)\lesssim n\log(n+1)
,
we obtain

(9.3)

|\psi^{(n)}(t)|\leq\exp\!\Bigl(C_{1}n\log(n+1)+nu+(\beta-1)n\log u-u^{\beta}\Bigr)

for a constant
C_{1}>0
.

For fixed
n
, the exponent on the right-hand side tends to
-\infty
as
u\to\infty
.
Hence
\psi^{(n)}(t)\to 0
as
t\downarrow 0
for every
n
, so the extension by
0
to

t\leq 0
is
C^{\infty}
at the origin.

It remains to optimize (
9.3
). Since
u\geq 1
, we have
\log u\leq u
, hence

nu+(\beta-1)n\log u\leq\beta nu.

Because
\alpha
and
\beta
are conjugate exponents,
Young’s inequality gives, for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,

\beta nu\leq\varepsilon u^{\beta}+C_{\varepsilon}n^{\alpha}.

Choosing
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{2}
,

nu+(\beta-1)n\log u-u^{\beta}\leq-\frac{1}{2}u^{\beta}+C_{2}n^{\alpha}\leq C_{2}n^{\alpha}.

Thus (
9.3
) implies

|\psi^{(n)}(t)|\leq\exp\!\bigl(C_{1}n\log(n+1)+C_{2}n^{\alpha}\bigr)\qquad(0<t<e^{-1}).

Since
\alpha>1
, we have
n\log(n+1)\leq\varepsilon n^{\alpha}+C_{\varepsilon}n
.
Absorbing the linear term into
K^{n+1}
, we obtain

\sup_{t\in I}|\psi^{(n)}(t)|\leq K^{n+1}e^{cn^{\alpha}}

for suitable constants
K,c>0
and all
n\geq 0
. This proves
\psi\in\mathcal{C}_{M}(I)
in Section
7
.

9.3.
Sharp example in Section
8.2

For
x
sufficiently large we write

L_{1}(x):=\log x,\qquad L_{j+1}(x):=\log L_{j}(x)\quad(j\geq 1),

and

E_{1}(x):=e^{x},\qquad E_{j+1}(x):=\exp(E_{j}(x))\quad(j\geq 1).

Fix
k\geq 2
,
\alpha>0
, and choose
\delta>0
so small that

L_{j}(1/t)\geq 2\qquad(0<t\leq\delta,\ 1\leq j\leq k).

Let
I:=(-\delta,\delta)
and define

\psi(t):=\begin{cases}0,&-\delta<t\leq 0,\\[4.0pt]
\exp\!\Bigl(-\bigl(\log(1/t)\bigr)\,L_{k}(1/t)^{1/\alpha}\Bigr),&0<t<\delta.\end{cases}

Since
k\geq 2
, for
u:=\log(1/t)
this may be rewritten as

\psi(t)=e^{-Q(u)},\qquad Q(u):=uA(u),\qquad A(u):=L_{k-1}(u)^{1/\alpha}.

For
0<t<\delta
set
g(u):=e^{-Q(u)}
.
To estimate
g^{(r)}
we first record a direct bound on the derivatives of
A
and
Q
.
Introduce the auxiliary functions

\eta_{0}(u):=u^{-1},\qquad\eta_{j}(u):=L_{j}(u)^{-1}\quad(1\leq j\leq k-1).

Observe that

\eta_{0}^{\prime}(u)=-\eta_{0}(u)^{2},\qquad\eta_{j}^{\prime}(u)=-\eta_{0}(u)\eta_{1}(u)\cdots\eta_{j-1}(u)\eta_{j}(u)^{2}\quad(j\geq 1).

In particular, each derivative introduces at least one extra factor of
\eta_{0}(u)=u^{-1}
.

Lemma 9.6
.

There exists a constant
C\geq 1
such that, for every integer
r\geq 1
and every sufficiently large
u
,

|A^{(r)}(u)|\leq C^{r}r!\,A(u)u^{-r},\qquad|Q^{(r)}(u)|\leq C^{r}r!\,A(u)u^{1-r}.

Moreover,

Q^{\prime}(u)=A(u)\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{\alpha L_{1}(u)\cdots L_{k-2}(u)L_{k-1}(u)}\Bigr),

so in particular

Q^{\prime}(u)\approx A(u)\qquad(u\to\infty).

Proof.

Since

A(u)=L_{k-1}(u)^{1/\alpha}=\eta_{k-1}(u)^{-1/\alpha},

a straightforward induction shows that for every
r\geq 0
,

A^{(r)}(u)=A(u)u^{-r}P_{r}\bigl(\eta_{1}(u),\dots,\eta_{k-1}(u)\bigr),

where
P_{r}
is a polynomial with real coefficients and where the
\ell^{1}
-norm of the coefficient vector of
P_{r}
is bounded by
C^{r}r!
for a suitable constant
C
independent of
r
.
Indeed, differentiating the identity above produces three kinds of terms: one from differentiating
A
, one from differentiating
u^{-r}
, and one from differentiating the polynomial in the variables
\eta_{j}
.
Each differentiation contributes one extra factor of
u^{-1}=\eta_{0}
, and the coefficient growth is at most linear in
r
at each step; hence the total coefficient growth is bounded by
C^{r}r!
.
Since
0<\eta_{j}(u)\leq 1
for large
u
, this gives

|A^{(r)}(u)|\leq C^{r}r!\,A(u)u^{-r}.

Now
Q(u)=uA(u)
, so Leibniz’ rule gives, for
r\geq 1
,

Q^{(r)}(u)=uA^{(r)}(u)+rA^{(r-1)}(u).

Using the bound just proved,

|Q^{(r)}(u)|\leq C^{r}r!A(u)u^{1-r}+rC^{r-1}(r-1)!A(u)u^{1-r}\leq(2C)^{r}r!A(u)u^{1-r}.

This proves the stated estimate after enlarging
C
.
Finally,

A^{\prime}(u)=\frac{A(u)}{\alpha uL_{1}(u)\cdots L_{k-2}(u)L_{k-1}(u)},

so

Q^{\prime}(u)=A(u)+uA^{\prime}(u)=A(u)\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{\alpha L_{1}(u)\cdots L_{k-2}(u)L_{k-1}(u)}\Bigr),

which implies
Q^{\prime}(u)\approx A(u)
for large
u
.
∎

Lemma 9.7
.

There exists a constant
C\geq 1
such that for every integer
r\geq 1
and every sufficiently large
u
,

|g^{(r)}(u)|\leq C^{r}(r!)^{2}A(u)^{r}e^{-Q(u)}.

Proof.

Apply Faà di Bruno in the partition form:

g^{(r)}(u)=e^{-Q(u)}\sum_{\pi\in\varPi_{r}}\prod_{B\in\pi}(-Q^{(|B|)}(u)),

where
\varPi_{r}
is the set of partitions of
\{1,\dots,r\}
.
For a partition
\pi\in\varPi_{r}
, let
m_{B}:=|B|
.
By Lemma
9.6
,

\prod_{B\in\pi}|Q^{(m_{B})}(u)|\leq C^{r}\Bigl(\prod_{B\in\pi}m_{B}!\Bigr)A(u)^{|\pi|}\leq C^{r}r!\,A(u)^{r},

because
|\pi|\leq r
and
\prod_{B}m_{B}!\leq r!
.
Summing over partitions and using the Bell-number bound
|\varPi_{r}|\leq e^{r}r!
, we obtain

|g^{(r)}(u)|\leq(Ce)^{r}(r!)^{2}A(u)^{r}e^{-Q(u)}.

Absorb
e
into the constant.
∎

We may now estimate
\psi^{(n)}
directly.
By Lemmas
9.4
and
9.7
, for
n\geq 1
and
0<t<\delta
,

|\psi^{(n)}(t)|\leq t^{-n}\sum_{r=1}^{n}|a_{n,r}|\,|g^{(r)}(u)|\leq t^{-n}n!\max_{1\leq r\leq n}|g^{(r)}(u)|.

Hence

|\psi^{(n)}(t)|\leq C^{n}(n!)^{3}e^{nu}A(u)^{n}e^{-uA(u)}.

Taking logarithms and using
\log(n!)\leq C_{0}n\log(n+1)
, we get

(9.4)

|\psi^{(n)}(t)|\leq\exp\!\Bigl(C_{1}n\log(n+1)+nu+n\log A(u)-uA(u)\Bigr).

We now optimize the exponent.

Set

F_{n}(u):=nu+n\log A(u)-uA(u).

There are two cases.

Case 1:
A(u)\leq 2n
.

Then
L_{k-1}(u)=A(u)^{\alpha}\leq(2n)^{\alpha}
, hence

u\leq E_{k-1}((2n)^{\alpha}).

Therefore

F_{n}(u)\leq nE_{k-1}((2n)^{\alpha})+n\log(2n).

Since
k\geq 2
, the function
E_{k-1}(x)
dominates polynomial factors; thus, after enlarging the constant,

F_{n}(u)\leq E_{k-1}(C_{2}n^{\alpha}).

Case 2:
A(u)\geq 2n
.

Since
\log A(u)=\frac{1}{\alpha}L_{k}(u)=o(u)
, for large
u
,

\log A(u)\leq\frac{u}{2}
. If
A(u)\geq 2n
, then

F_{n}(u)=nu+n\log A(u)-uA(u)\leq nu+\frac{n}{2}u-uA(u)\leq\frac{3}{2}nu-2nu=-\frac{1}{2}nu\leq 0.

Thus in this case as well,

F_{n}(u)\leq E_{k-1}(C_{2}n^{\alpha}).

Substituting into (
9.4
), we obtain

|\psi^{(n)}(t)|\leq\exp\!\bigl(C_{1}n\log(n+1)+E_{k-1}(C_{2}n^{\alpha})\bigr).

Because
k\geq 2
, one has
n\log(n+1)\leq E_{k-1}(C_{3}n^{\alpha})
for a larger constant
C_{3}
, and therefore

|\psi^{(n)}(t)|\leq\exp\!\bigl(E_{k-1}(cn^{\alpha})\bigr)=E_{k}(cn^{\alpha})

for some
c>0
.
After increasing the constants to account for finitely many small values of
n
, we obtain

\sup_{0<t<\delta}|\psi^{(n)}(t)|\leq K^{n+1}E_{k}(cn^{\alpha}).

Since the exponent in (
9.4
) tends to
-\infty
as
u\to\infty
for each fixed
n
, all derivatives tend to
0
as
t\downarrow 0
; thus the extension by
0
to
t\leq 0
is
C^{\infty}
at the origin. This proves
\psi\in\mathcal{C}_{M}(I)
in Section
8
.
