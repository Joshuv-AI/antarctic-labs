Uplink Channel Estimation for Multi-User MISO Systems Assisted by a Fluid Reconfigurable Intelligent Surface
Amarilton L. Magalhães 1 3 , André L. F. de Almeida 1 , and George C. Alexandropoulos 3 4

Abstract
Fluid reconfigurable intelligent surfaces (FRISs) have recently emerged as a promising paradigm for wireless communications, wherein the reflecting elements can dynamically select their effective radiating positions from a dense preset grid, thereby introducing an additional degree of freedom. In contrast to conventional RIS architectures, FRISs can achieve spatial diversity with fewer physical elements. However, beyond the cascaded channel structure, FRIS-assisted systems are also affected by uncertainties arising from element-position mismatches caused by calibration inaccuracies or motion errors, which may degrade channel state information. To the best of our knowledge, channel estimation (CE) for FRIS-assisted systems under position uncertainty remains unexplored. To fill this gap, we propose a CE framework for a multi-user FRIS-assisted uplink system based on a two-time-scale FRIS configuration protocol that captures both reflection phase-shift and element-motion dynamics. By capitalizing on orthogonal pilot sequences and tensor modeling, we derive a closed-form solution that jointly estimates the individual channels and the motion-induced phase coefficients. Numerical results demonstrate notable performance in the presence of unknown position deviations.



I
Introduction and motivation

The growing interest in fluid antenna systems (FASs), an emerging paradigm of reconfigurable antennas that additionally dynamically adjusts antenna positions, offering a new degree of freedom for system design and signal processing, has recently attracted significant attention
[
9
]
. In parallel, the widespread interest in reconfigurable intelligent surfaces (RISs)
[
1
]
has stimulated a closely related line of research referred to as fluid RIS (FRIS), which consists of a metasurface composed of response-tunable reflecting elements whose phase control port in each tile-type fluid element can be dynamically selected over a dense preset grid
[
13
,
16
,
10
,
15
]
. Unlike conventional RISs, FRISs allow the physical positions of their constituent elements to vary over time, thereby enabling effective spatial diversity with even a single fluid element instead of a large number of fixed ones
[
12
]
. In addition, recent work has proposed extending the FRIS coverage area by exploiting the principle of simultaneous transmitting and reflecting RIS (STAR-RIS), leading to the fluid integrated reflecting and emitting surface (FIRES) concept
[
4
]
.

To fully exploit the gains offered by FRISs, accurate channel state information (CSI) is essential. In classical RIS deployments, channel estimation (CE) can be impaired by hardware nonidealities and imperfect phase responses
[
5
,
6
]
. In FRIS-assisted systems, the CE task becomes even more challenging because, in addition to the cascaded bilinear channel structure, one must also account for position uncertainty arising from calibration and/or motion errors
[
16
]
, which may lead to mismatches between commanded and realized element positions. To the best of our knowledge, CE for FRIS-assisted systems has not yet been addressed in the literature. In this work, we propose a CE framework based on a FRIS-specific two-time-scale uplink training protocol for a multi-user FRIS-assisted communication system subject to element-position uncertainty. By exploiting the structure of the received signal and orthogonal pilot sequences, we derive an algebraic formulation that enables joint estimation of the individual channels and the motion-induced phase coefficients. We then adopt tensor-based modeling to obtain a closed-form solution. We compare the proposed CE method with a benchmark that assumes the realized motion-induced coefficients exactly match those commanded by the FRIS controller
[
11
]
. Simulation results demonstrate accurate estimation even in the presence of unknown motion deviations.

II
System model and assumptions

Considering uplink transmission, we study a multi-user multiple-input single-output (MISO) wireless communication system in which
Q
single-antenna user equipments (UEs) communicate with a base station (BS) equipped with
M_{r}
antennas through an FRIS composed of
M
fluid elements, as illustrated in Fig.
3
. The FRIS reflecting surface is assumed to occupy a total area
A
, and each
m
th (
m=1,\ldots,M
) fluid element (tile) can switch among
N
preset positions. We consider a structured training window with
J
sub-frames, each containing
K
blocks of
T_{s}
symbol periods, during which the propagation channels are assumed to remain constant. We adopt an FRIS reconfiguration strategy in which the electronic phase shift
e^{j\phi_{j,m}}
remains fixed over the
K
blocks of the
j
th sub-frame (
j=1,\ldots,J
) and varies across sub-frames, whereas the controlled reflecting positions
t_{k,m}
vary across blocks and are repeated over the
J
sub-frames. Specifically,
t_{k,m}=e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\|\mathit{\boldsymbol{p}}_{k,m}\|}
is the motion-induced phase due to the
m
th fluid element’s position at the
k
th block, given by the vector
\mathit{\boldsymbol{p}}_{k,m}
, with
\lambda
denoting the wavelength. Defining
{\mathbf{G}}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times Q}
and
{\mathbf{H}}\in\mathbb{C}^{M_{r}\times M}
as the UEs–FRIS and FRIS–BS channel matrices, respectively, the received signal after
T_{s}
symbol periods in the
k
th block of the
j
th sub-frame is given by
\tilde{{\mathbf{Y}}}_{j,k}={\mathbf{H}}{\rm diag\!}\left\{\mathit{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{j}\odot\mathit{\boldsymbol{t}}_{k}\right\}{\mathbf{G}}{\mathbf{X}}_{p}+\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}_{j,k}\in\mathbb{C}^{M_{r}\times T_{s}}
, where
{\mathbf{X}}_{p}\in\mathbb{C}^{Q\times T_{s}}
is the pilot matrix,
\mathit{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{j}\overset{\cdot}{=}[\phi_{j,1},\ldots,\phi_{j,M}]^{{\rm T}}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times 1}
,
\mathit{\boldsymbol{t}}_{k}\overset{\cdot}{=}[t_{k,1},\ldots,t_{k,M}]^{{\rm T}}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times 1}
, and
\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}_{j,k}\in\mathbb{C}^{M_{r}\times T_{s}}
is the associated noise term with zero-mean circularly symmetric complex-valued entries with variance
\sigma_{v}^{2}
. Assuming an orthogonal pilot design for
{\mathbf{X}}_{p}
, the matched-filtered signal at the BS is
{\mathbf{Y}}_{j,k}=(1/T_{s})\tilde{{\mathbf{Y}}}_{j,k}{\mathbf{X}}_{p}^{{\rm H}}\in\mathbb{C}^{M_{r}\times Q}
, which can be written as

{\mathbf{Y}}_{j,k}={\mathbf{H}}{\rm diag\!}\left\{\mathit{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{j}\odot\mathit{\boldsymbol{t}}_{k}\right\}{\mathbf{G}}+{\mathbf{V}}_{j,k},

(1)

where
{\mathbf{V}}_{j,k}\in\mathbb{C}^{M_{r}\times Q}
is the filtered noise. Collecting all preset FRIS electro-mechanical configurations during the channel training phase, we define
{\mathbf{T}}\overset{\cdot}{=}[\mathit{\boldsymbol{t}}_{1},\ldots,\mathit{\boldsymbol{t}}_{K}]^{{\rm T}}\in\mathbb{C}^{K\times M}
and
\boldsymbol{\Phi}\overset{\cdot}{=}[\mathit{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{1},\ldots,\mathit{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{J}]^{{\rm T}}\in\mathbb{C}^{J\times M}
as the motion and phase-shift matrices, respectively. Equation (
1
) can be interpreted as the
(j,k)
th slice of a fourth-order tensor
\mathcal{Y}\in\mathbb{C}^{M_{r}\times Q\times K\times J}
obtained by concatenating
{\mathbf{Y}}_{j,k}
along the third dimension for
k=1,\ldots,K
, and then along the fourth dimension for
j=1,\ldots,J
. This tensor admits a parallel factor (PARAFAC) decomposition
[
8
,
2
,
17
]
and can be written as
\mathcal{Y}=\mathcal{I}_{4,M}\times_{1}{\mathbf{H}}\times_{2}{\mathbf{G}}^{{\rm T}}\times_{3}{\mathbf{T}}\times_{4}\boldsymbol{\Phi}+\mathcal{V}
, where
\mathcal{I}_{4,M}
denotes the identity tensor and
\mathcal{V}\in\mathbb{C}^{M_{r}\times Q\times K\times J}
is the filtered noise tensor.

III
Proposed channel estimation framework

In this work, we consider two possible scenarios within the CE context. In the first scenario, we assume that the positions of the FRIS elements exactly match those commanded by the controller. In this case, our goal is to estimate the individual channels
{\mathbf{G}}
and
{\mathbf{H}}
with the knowledge of
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
and
{\mathbf{T}}
. To this, we consider solving the problem

\min\limits_{{\mathbf{G}},{\mathbf{H}}}\Big\|\mathcal{Y}-\mathcal{I}_{4,M}\times_{1}{\mathbf{H}}\times_{2}{\mathbf{G}}^{{\rm T}}\times_{3}{\mathbf{T}}\times_{4}\boldsymbol{\Phi}\Big\|^{2}_{\textrm{F}}.

(2)

Exploiting the multilinear structure of
\mathcal{Y}
, we unfold it into the matrix
{\mathbf{Y}}_{1}=\boldsymbol{\Theta}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\Phi}\diamond{\mathbf{T}}\bigr)+{\mathbf{V}}_{1}\in\mathbb{C}^{QM_{r}\times JK}
(see
[
8
]
for details), where
\boldsymbol{\Theta}\overset{\cdot}{=}{\mathbf{G}}^{{\rm T}}\diamond{\mathbf{H}}\in\mathbb{C}^{QM_{r}\times M}
is the combined channel, with
{\mathbf{V}}_{1}
denoting the corresponding noise term. An estimate of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
can be obtained by solving the least-squares (LS) problem
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}\bigl\|{\mathbf{Y}}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\Theta}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\Phi}\diamond{\mathbf{T}}\bigr)\bigr\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}
. Assuming a semi-unitary design for
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
, this estimate is given by the linear filter
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}=(1/JK){\mathbf{Y}}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\Phi}\diamond{\mathbf{T}}\bigr)^{\ast}
. To provide decoupled estimates of
{\mathbf{G}}
and
{\mathbf{H}}
, we consider the problem

\min\limits_{{\mathbf{G}},{\mathbf{H}}}\Big\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}-{\mathbf{G}}^{{\rm T}}\diamond{\mathbf{H}}\Big\|^{2}_{\textrm{F}}.

(3)

This problem can be tackled by invoking the Khatri-Rao factorization (KRF) algorithm
[
7
]
, which involves solving
M
parallelized rank-1 approximations, explored in the literature for a static RIS with fixed elements
[
2
]
.

In the second scenario, the commanded positions are not perfectly achieved due to motion errors, resulting in imprecise CSI. To account for this, it is crucial to explicitly estimate the motion matrix
{\mathbf{T}}
for accurate CE. For this purpose, we propose to include the matrix
{\mathbf{T}}
in the minimization problem (
2
), yielding a generalized problem to solve three quantities in place of two, namely,
{\mathbf{T}}
,
{\mathbf{G}}
, and
{\mathbf{H}}
. To tackle it, we now unfold
\mathcal{Y}
into
{\mathbf{Y}}_{2}={\mathbf{Z}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}+{\mathbf{V}}_{2}\in\mathbb{C}^{KQM_{r}\times J}
, where
{\mathbf{Z}}\overset{\cdot}{=}{\mathbf{T}}\diamond{\mathbf{G}}^{{\rm T}}\diamond{\mathbf{H}}\in\mathbb{C}^{KQM_{r}\times M}
captures the joint effect of the motion and the cascaded channel, while
{\mathbf{V}}_{2}
represents the corresponding noise term. Likewise, we initially can solve
\hat{{\mathbf{Z}}}=\argmin_{{\mathbf{Z}}}\bigl\|{\mathbf{Y}}-{\mathbf{Z}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}\bigr\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}
, for which the analytical solution is
\hat{{\mathbf{Z}}}=(1/J){\mathbf{Y}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\ast}
. We then aim to decouple the three factors from
\hat{{\mathbf{Z}}}
by considering the following problem:

\min\limits_{{\mathbf{T}},{\mathbf{G}},{\mathbf{H}}}\Big\|\hat{{\mathbf{Z}}}-{\mathbf{T}}\diamond{\mathbf{G}}^{{\rm T}}\diamond{\mathbf{H}}\Big\|^{2}_{\textrm{F}}.

(4)

We address problem (
4
) by employing multidimensional LS Khatri–Rao factorization
[
14
]
, which can be viewed as a generalization of (
3
). We approximate the
m
th column of
\hat{{\mathbf{Z}}}
as
\mathit{\boldsymbol{z}}_{m}\overset{\cdot}{=}\mathit{\boldsymbol{z}}_{m}^{({\mathbf{T}})}\otimes\mathit{\boldsymbol{z}}_{m}^{({\mathbf{G}})}\otimes\mathit{\boldsymbol{z}}_{m}^{({\mathbf{H}})}\in\mathbb{C}^{KQM_{r}\times 1}
, where
\mathit{\boldsymbol{z}}_{m}^{({\mathbf{T}})}
,
\mathit{\boldsymbol{z}}_{m}^{({\mathbf{G}})}
, and
\mathit{\boldsymbol{z}}_{m}^{({\mathbf{H}})}
denote the
m
th columns of
{\mathbf{T}}
,
{\mathbf{G}}^{{\rm T}}
, and
{\mathbf{H}}
, respectively. Tensorizing
\mathit{\boldsymbol{z}}_{m}
yields the rank-one tensor
\mathcal{Z}_{r}=\mathit{\boldsymbol{z}}_{m}^{({\mathbf{H}})}\circ\mathit{\boldsymbol{z}}_{m}^{({\mathbf{G}})}\circ\mathit{\boldsymbol{z}}_{m}^{({\mathbf{T}})}\in\mathbb{C}^{M_{r}\times Q\times K}
. The columns of
\hat{{\mathbf{T}}}
,
\hat{{\mathbf{G}}}^{{\rm T}}
, and
\hat{{\mathbf{H}}}
are then obtained via a rank-one truncated higher-order singular value decomposition (HOSVD)
[
3
]
, namely,
\mathit{\boldsymbol{z}}_{m}^{({\mathbf{T}})}=\sqrt[3]{(\mathcal{S}_{r})_{1,1,1}}\mathit{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(3)}_{m,1}
,
\mathit{\boldsymbol{z}}_{m}^{({\mathbf{G}})}=\sqrt[3]{(\mathcal{S}_{r})_{1,1,1}}\mathit{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(2)}_{m,1}
, and
\mathit{\boldsymbol{z}}_{m}^{({\mathbf{H}})}=\sqrt[3]{(\mathcal{S}_{r})_{1,1,1}}\mathit{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(1)}_{m,1}
, where
\mathcal{S}_{r}
is the core tensor and
\mathit{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(1)}_{m,1}
,
\mathit{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(2)}_{m,1}
, and
\mathit{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(3)}_{m,1}
are the dominant higher-order singular vectors of
\mathcal{Z}_{m}
along modes 1, 2, and 3, respectively. Repeating this procedure for
m=1,\ldots,M
yields the estimates of
{\mathbf{T}}
,
{\mathbf{G}}
, and
{\mathbf{H}}
.

IV
Performance evaluation and upcoming results

We present preliminary simulation results to evaluate the performance of the proposed CE methods. In particular, we report the normalized mean square error (NMSE) for two motion cases: i) perfect motion realization, in which the realized positions coincide with the commanded ones; and ii) motion realization uncertainty, where mismatches arise due to calibration and/or motion errors. In the former, the knowledge of the FRIS-commanded motion matrix
{\mathbf{T}}
can be leveraged to get the channel estimates, whereas in the latter, it is preferable to treat
{\mathbf{T}}
as unknown (blind motion) and jointly estimate it. We adopt the parameter set
\{M,Q,M_{r},T_{s},K\}=\{12,4,10,4,4\}
, and we design the pilot matrix
{\mathbf{X}}_{p}
as a truncated-discrete Fourier matrix. We first provide NMSE results for CE in Fig.
3
by ranging the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). Accounting for case i), (refer to
‘KRF using ideal
\boldsymbol{T}
’
and
‘LS using ideal
\boldsymbol{T}
’
curves), KRF results approach the performance of that of classical RIS with fixed elements
[
2
]
, in contrast to the LS methods. In the algebraic sense, the methods in case i) employ similar filtering as
[
2
]
after collecting
JK
signal blocks. However,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}
is estimated more accurately employing KRF than that of the conventional LS method. This improvement arises from decoupling the individual channel components using KRF, leveraging its denoising properties, and then recombining them. This gain was also reported by
[
2
]
in the context of static RIS. To assess the impact of motion errors on CE performance, we now evaluate case ii). For this purpose, we define a wavelength-dependent positioning error
\sigma_{\mathrm{pos}}
which directly affects the motion matrix
{\mathbf{T}}
, and set
\sigma_{\mathrm{pos}}=\lambda/20
. We initially focus on the receiver processing in which the BS ignores the motion errors (refer to
‘KRF using mismatched
\boldsymbol{T}
’
and
‘LS using mismatched
\boldsymbol{T}
’
curves). The worst performance of the LS method for the low to medium SNR regimes is due to not refining the CE, similar to case i), unlike KRF one. Nevertheless, the KRF’s denoising gain is no longer effective when motion errors are present, where the commanded
{\mathbf{T}}
does not coincide with the realized. Still in Fig.
3
, our proposed method (refer to
‘Joint
\boldsymbol{T}
,
\boldsymbol{G}
,
\boldsymbol{H}
estimation (proposed)’
curve) jointly estimates the motion matrix
{\mathbf{T}}
and the individual channel components under motion uncertainty, thereby tackling the previous limitation and achieving substantial performance enhancement, approaching to those of case i). In Fig.
3
, we present a comparison by taking a closer look at the motion error. To this end, we set a wavelength-dependent positioning error
\sigma_{\mathrm{pos}}
while adopting SNR = 30 dB. Results in Fig.
3
corroborate the behavior previously observed, emphasizing the need to account for position uncertainties. The proposed method remains robust to increasing error levels, approaching the performance of the static RIS and the idealized-motion FRIS (benchmark). This does not hold for the scheme that neglects the estimation of the motion matrix
{\mathbf{T}}
, which suffers from pronounced performance degradation. Table
