A Kansei-mediated protocol for transforming painting palettes into product aesthetics
Authors: Lu Weihua, Ye Chenyan, Fang Jiajun, Čok Vanja
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo address users’ increasingly diverse aesthetic and emotional needs, this study develops an innovative product color design framework based on Kansei. The proposed method transfers color from paintings (source) to consumer products (target) using Kansei as an intermediary, thereby enhancing the affective quality of color and the perceived design value. The framework initiates with cluster analysis to extract color feature groups (CFGs) from paintings. Subsequently, product color genomes (PCGs) are constructed through functional and structural analysis. Following this, subjective evaluation quantifies Kansei for both CFGs and PCGs, identifying representative groups linked to specific Kansei words. Finally, a Kansei-mediated mapping bridges painting CFGs and PCGs to execute the transfer. Validated using Chinese paintings and a coffee machine, the method effectively transfers painting color schemes, enriches product design, improves color quality, and supports culturally adaptive design within international marketing strategies.Keywords: Kansei Engineering, Painting color transfer, Product color genome, Cross-domain mapping, Aesthetic enhancementSubject terms: Cultural and media studies, Cultural and media studies, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

As a fundamental attribute of product design1, color captures consumer attention, enhances market recognition, and communicates product attributes and brand values2. However, amid advancing socioeconomic conditions, manufacturing technologies, and design practices, products increasingly face market saturation and functional convergence. This homogenization diminishes product distinctiveness, thereby impeding the fulfillment of users’ growing aesthetic and symbolic-emotional needs3. Common consumer goods, such as refrigerators, washing machines, and coffee machines, exhibit strong functional constraints that limit formal innovation. Here, color transcends linguistic and cultural barriers to emerge as a critical medium for emotional expression4. Distinct chromatic schemes evoke varied emotional atmospheres (e.g., warmth, vitality, serenity), delivering experiential value beyond utilitarian function. This elevation of chromatic harmony ultimately enhances perceived design quality.

The culturally embedded color schemes of historical paintings, validated through centuries of aesthetic, technical, and cultural refinement, embody profound regional sensibilities and collective memory5. As crystallized expressions of communal aesthetics, symbolic traditions, and affective preferences, they provide time-tested color harmony principles that intrinsically resonate with users’ perceptual experiences6. Within globalized markets where emotional connection drives product differentiation, leveraging the triple-tiered value of paintings (artistic color conception, replicable color systems, and heritage symbolism) enables emotionally intelligent localization strategies.

Transferring such schemes into product design imbues products with tangible cultural legitimacy and affective depth. This deepens user identification through meaningful aesthetic engagement while delivering sophisticated color solutions aligned with contemporary preferences, thereby fulfilling layered aspirations ranging from sensory delight to cultural continuity.

The core strength of color transfer technology lies in moving colors between source and target7. Color transfer, a key technique in computer vision and image processing, transfers the color attributes of one image to another to achieve specific aesthetic or functional goals. Widely applied in art creation, film and television post-production, and game design8, its evolution can be categorized into three distinct developmental stages.

Basic algorithm

Early color transfer methods primarily employed color space transformation and statistical feature matching. Some studies have proposed color space conversion based on mean and standard deviation to approximate the source image’s color distribution in the target image9. Others have introduced multi-scale color transfer using histogram matching, achieving color transfer through progressive histogram alignment10. This research phase focused on inter-image color transfer. Constrained by technical limitations, these methods performed poorly in complex scenes and diverse applications. They struggled particularly to distinguish objects or regions within images, often resulting in detail loss and color distortion11. Additionally, these approaches directly replaced colors based on image color spaces without analyzing or understanding the source image.

Advanced algorithm

With advances in deep learning, neural network-based color transfer methods have gained significant research attention. Technologies like Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs)12 and Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)13 have proven pivotal for image style transfer, driving substantial progress in color transfer14. For example, He et al. proposed the first deep learning approach for exemplar-based local colorization, where a CNN directly mapped grayscale inputs to colorized outputs using reference images15. Similarly, Ito et al. introduced a GAN-based method that estimated tone curves to transfer reference color tones to input images16. While current research remains focused on inter-image color transfer, technological advances now enable the handling of complex scenes and diverse requirements, such as object identification and local style transfer17. However, these methods still primarily perform surface-level deconstruction of source images without capturing their deeper semantic connotations18.

Generative AI

Recent advancements in artificial intelligence have led to generative AI systems that autonomously produce text, images, audio, video, and 3D models from user prompts. These systems are built on convolutional neural networks, generative adversarial networks, and vast datasets of text and images. This output, termed Artificial Intelligence Generated Content (AIGC)19, shows significant potential for color transfer applications. For instance, Wu et al. utilized Midjourney to fuse source images into transfer-optimized outputs, extracting palettes for product color design20. Unlike earlier methods, image-generative AI extends color transfer beyond 2D images into 3D spaces while offering real-time parametric adjustment and rapid iteration during creation. Despite these advantages, AI instability causes inconsistent adherence to user instructions and detail loss in color schemes. Furthermore, limited training data creates significant gaps in cultural and emotional comprehension21, hindering high-fidelity, stable color transfer implementation.

When transferring color schemes from paintings to products, inherent differences in dimension, quantity, and purpose complicate the application of conventional color transfer methods. ① Spatial Dimensions: Paintings exist as two-dimensional visual forms, while products occupy three-dimensional space with inherent spatial complexity. Crucially, a symbiotic relationship exists between product structure and color matching. Structural properties directly influence color combinations, while strategic color application accentuates and enhances structural features22. ② Quantity of Colors: Paintings employ diverse color palettes to achieve rich visual effects, emphasizing chromatic subtlety and layering23. Product color matching, conversely, is driven by functional characteristics, usage environments, user experience, and brand identity, typically adopting a three-tone methodology: main color, additional color, and decorative color24. ③ Functional Purposes: Paintings prioritize freedom and individuality, driven by creative intent to express themes and emotions and to reflect social and temporal contexts25. Products fulfill user needs and expectations through reliable function, seamless operation, and meaningful engagement. Product color schemes instead emphasize market acceptance, functionality, and practicality26.

Conventional color transfer algorithms, whether color space mapping or deep learning approaches, primarily optimize image-to-image translation with emphasis on source color/style replication27. Consequently, they prove inadequate for adapting two-dimensional painting color schemes to three-dimensional products28. Meanwhile, AI-driven methods remain limited by output instability and an inability to faithfully interpret the embedded emotional and cultural semantics of artistic works29. Achieving successful transfer of painting color schemes to products requires rigorous research and innovative approaches. The core challenge lies in faithfully preserving the artwork’s emotional and cultural dimensions within the product palette while addressing the complex emotional needs of users.

The essence of transferring painting palettes into product color schemes lies in preserving and conveying the emotional resonance embedded in these traditional visual artifacts, such as aesthetic preferences, cultural associations, and affective atmospheres. By embedding these emotional qualities into product color schemes, design outcomes can more effectively align with users’ emotional expectations and cultural sensibilities. This emotional resonance is referred to as Kansei, which denotes the integrated sensory and cognitive responses elicited in users when perceiving attributes such as structure, color, and function30. Kansei engineering provides a framework through which these Kansei can be identified and quantified, and used as a bridge to establish a mapping relationship between painting palettes and product color schemes, thereby achieving cross-domain emotional value transformation.

However, Kansei is inherently subtle, subjective, and difficult to articulate explicitly31. It often eludes direct verbal expression and must be assessed indirectly through methods that capture users’ intuitive and nuanced affective responses. Among various approaches, subjective evaluation remains a widely adopted method in Kansei studies, where participants rate stimuli based on predefined semantic scales32. Compared to physiological measurement techniques33 or big data algorithms34, subjective evaluation is more sensitive to transient affective states and more effective in capturing subtle emotional distinctions. In the context of color transfer, this approach enables the identification of the emotional connotations inherent in painting color schemes, and reveals how these colors influence users’ deeper affective experiences when translated into product color compositions.

Building on advancements in emotionally intelligent color design, this study proposes a Kansei engineering-based color transfer method that preserves emotional integrity while enabling culturally adaptive product applications. The method systematically translates affective needs into color solutions through a structured transfer framework (detailed in Sect. 2), which provides designers with actionable tools for resonant color design. Empirical validation in Sect. 3, demonstrated via case studies spanning Chinese painting aesthetics and coffee machine customization, confirms the method’s efficacy in cross-context scenarios. Critical findings and implications are discussed in Sect. 4, followed by conclusive insights in Sect. 5.

Construction of Kansei space

To establish a reproducible and data-driven Kansei space, a systematic computational framework integrating word embeddings and unsupervised clustering is proposed. First, a set of adjectives related to product color aesthetics is collected using a focused crawler35, following three criteria: ① they provide simple descriptions of Kansei; ② they are universal and unambiguous; and ③ they avoid specialized terminology (e.g., impressionism). After removing duplicates, a set of unique candidate words is retained for subsequent analysis.

Each candidate word is transformed into a semantic vector using a pre-trained word embedding model (Tencent AI Lab Embedding, 300 dimensions36, which captures fine-grained lexical semantics from large-scale corpora. The vector representation of a word is denoted as . This continuous vector space enables quantitative comparisons of word meanings through geometric operations.

To extract antonym pairs from the candidate words, a seed-based expansion method leveraging vector arithmetic is employed. A small set of seed antonym pairs, representing fundamental semantic dimensions underlying the perception of artistic and product color, is established based on relevant literature and expert consultation. For each seed pair , a semantic direction vector is computed, capturing the axis of opposition embodied by that pair. For every unordered candidate pair with i<j, the difference vector is calculated. The alignment between a candidate pair and each seed direction is quantified by the cosine similarity between and ; the maximum similarity across all seeds is taken as the candidate’s score:

To distinguish genuine antonyms from non-antonyms, a similarity threshold is required. This threshold is derived from the score distribution of the seed pairs themselves: the self-similarity score for each seed pair yields a mean and standard deviation. To retain most genuine antonyms while filtering out spurious pairs, the threshold is set at the mean minus two standard deviations, with minor adjustments allowed to optimize the balance based on the cumulative distribution of candidate pair scores. To further eliminate near-synonyms, pairs with a difference vector magnitude below a certain threshold, indicating very similar meanings, are also discarded. Consequently, a set of antonymous word pairs is obtained from the initial candidate words.

Each antonym pair is then represented by its difference vector . This vector encodes the semantic axis defined by the opposition of the two words; thus, the set of difference vectors D captures the variety of perceptual dimensions present in the initial vocabulary.

To identify the core aesthetic dimensions underlying product color perception, these difference vectors are subjected to agglomerative hierarchical clustering37. The distance between two antonym pairs and is defined as the cosine distance between their respective difference vectors:

Ward’s minimum variance method is used as the linkage criterion to minimize within-cluster variance. The resulting dendrogram provides a hierarchical view of the relationships among the dimensions. To determine the optimal number of clusters, the silhouette coefficient is computed for a range of cluster counts. The number K that maximizes the silhouette coefficient is selected, indicating the best balance between intra-cluster cohesion and inter-cluster separation. This is corroborated by visual inspection of the dendrogram.

For each cluster , the centroid is calculated as the mean vector of all difference vectors belonging to that cluster. The antonym pair whose difference vector is closest to the centroid (by Euclidean distance) is selected as the representative Kansei word pair for that cluster. These representative pairs constitute the final Kansei space, providing the core semantic axes used in subsequent subjective evaluations.

By anchoring the construction in word embeddings and unsupervised clustering, this approach ensures that the Kansei space is both reproducible and data-driven, while the use of quantitative metrics (similarity threshold, difference vector magnitude, silhouette coefficient) provides objective justification for every decision.

The painting sample library

To initiate the color extraction process, a curated set of painting images is collected and subjected to preprocessing to isolate their color characteristics. Given the inherent complexity and nuanced blending typical of artistic color compositions, clustering techniques are employed to distill Kansei-related color information. Specifically, the K-means clustering algorithm38 is utilized to identify the primary color groups within each painting. For each identified cluster, the corresponding RGB values and relative area proportions are computed. These quantified color features serve as the foundational data for constructing a structured painting color feature library.

The K-means clustering is performed in the RGB color space with Euclidean distance. While RGB is not perceptually uniform, it is selected for its computational efficiency and direct compatibility with digital image formats. Importantly, the extracted colors serve only as initial candidates for subsequent subjective evaluation, where human perception directly determines the emotional relevance of the color features.

Color proportion.

When analyzing the percentage of product colors, the color that accounts for more than 50% is defined as the primary color, while the remaining colors are classified in descending order as additional and decorative colors. Based on the analysis, the percentage of colors is divided into five categories, with the percentage of primary colors ranging from 50% to 65%, 65% to 75%, 75% to 85%, 85% to 95%, and 100%, and labeled as S1, S2, S3, S4, and S5.

(2)Color area connection method.

Color area connection method.

The connection of color areas refers to the connection between two color blocks. The color connection is divided into four categories: Contact, Interval, Separation and Unity, which are respectively referred to as O1, O2, O3 and O4. In this context, contact means the two color blocks are directly joined by their areas; interval means they are repeatedly connected with gaps between them; separation means they are kept apart by a line, a different color block, or other elements; and unity means both blocks share the same color.

Color area combination.

The color area combination defines the co-occurrence pattern and integration logic method of color elements. In the case of consumer goods, the division and combination of color areas is primarily considered from the point of view that they reflect the function of the product. It can be categorized into two types: “main color + additional color” and “main color + additional color + decorative color”, which are called H1 and H2 respectively. A user survey was conducted to investigate emotional preferences and identify representative Kansei words. Based on the results, the representative CFG corresponding to the Kansei word and the matching PCG were selected. Within the CFG, the three constituent colors were ranked by their area proportions and respectively assigned to the roles of main, additional, and decorative colors in the product design. Color design of coffee machine based on the transfer method

Coffee machines, as one of the representative products of Western lifestyle, have experienced significant growth in the Chinese market in recent years44. With China’s rapid economic development and rising living standards, Western food culture represented by coffee culture has become increasingly popular among young Chinese consumers. However, many foreign brands still adhere to Western design esthetics and do not fully understand the esthetic preferences and cultural sensibilities of Chinese consumers. Therefore, it remains a challenge to design products that meet the functional needs as well as the esthetic and emotional expectations of Chinese consumers.

As a treasure of traditional Chinese culture, Chinese painting represents the aesthetic wisdom and artistic essence of the Chinese nation for thousands of years45. The use of colors in Chinese painting focuses on ‘coloring by the type’ and ‘using colors to express ideas’, highlighting their emotional and cultural symbolism. It reflects not only the Chinese people’s deep understanding of nature, society, and life but also their aesthetic interests and emotional preferences. Incorporating the color characteristics of Chinese painting into the design of coffee machines can give the products a unique oriental aesthetic charm while evoking cultural identity and emotional resonance among Chinese consumers.

With the advancement of globalization, cross-cultural communication and integration have become an inevitable trend. In product design, integrating esthetic elements from different cultures to meet the needs of global consumers has become an important area of research. Using Chinese paintings and coffee machines as case studies, we demonstrate the advantages of the proposed method to promote cross-cultural integration and its applicability to various products.

Construction of Kansei space

Following the procedure described in Sect. 2.1, the Kansei space for the coffee machine case study was constructed. An initial set of approximately 600 adjectives related to product color aesthetics was collected using a focused crawler. As the case study aims to culturally adapt a Western product for the Chinese market, the Kansei space must reflect the affective lexicon of the target user. Therefore, the crawler was strategically deployed on mainstream Chinese e-commerce platforms (such as JD.com and Tmall), professional design media, and consumer review portals, using product category keywords like “coffee machine” and general color descriptors. This targeted approach ensures that the resulting Kansei dimensions are an authentic representation of Chinese consumers’ aesthetic perceptions and emotional vocabulary, which is a prerequisite for achieving effective cultural localization. After removing duplicates and non-adjectival terms, 582 unique candidate words were retained.

Ten seed antonym pairs were established based on color aesthetics literature and focus group46 consultations, and they are: thick–thin, elegant–vulgar, sparse–dense, void–solid, hard–soft, deep–shallow, heavy–light, dry–moist, empty–full, ancient–modern. The rationale for selecting these pairs is twofold. First, each pair captures a fundamental semantic dimension that is widely recognized in color aesthetics, affective psychology, and art criticism as central to Kansei and verbal description of artistic color. These dimensions served as a theoretically grounded starting point for the subsequent computational clustering. Second, the selection was refined through consultation with a panel of three experts specializing respectively in color science, industrial design, and Chinese painting aesthetics. These experts independently reviewed an initial list of candidate pairs and converged on these ten as both theoretically grounded and practically discriminative for the product category under study. These seed pairs thus serve as semantically meaningful anchors that orient the subsequent computational clustering.

Using the Tencent AI Lab pre-trained Chinese word embedding model (300 dimensions), the self-similarity scores of these seed pairs were calculated, yielding a mean of 0.82 and a standard deviation of 0.07. The similarity threshold for candidate pairs was set at 0.66 (mean minus two standard deviations). Additionally, a difference vector magnitude filter of 0.15 was applied to exclude near-synonyms. Applying these criteria to the 582 candidate words yielded 140 antonymous word pairs.

Each antonym pair was represented by its difference vector, and the 140 difference vectors were subjected to agglomerative hierarchical clustering with cosine distance and Ward’s linkage. The silhouette coefficient was computed for cluster counts from 2 to 15, reaching a maximum of 0.63 at K=6. This was corroborated by visual inspection of the dendrogram, where cutting at a distance threshold of approximately 1.2 produced six well-separated clusters. For each cluster, the centroid was calculated, and the antonym pair closest to the centroid was selected as the representative Kansei word pair. The resulting six representative pairs constitute the Kansei space for this study, as illustrated in Fig. 3. These six pairs are: vivid-steady, mild-magnificent, elegant-gorgeous, simple-complicated, warm-cool, plain-technological.

Subjects and experiment environment

To ensure the reliability and consistency of the experiment, all three mapping experiments associated with the transfer method were conducted with the same group of participants in a controlled and standardized experimental environment. The experiment was conducted in a quiet and well-lit human-machine engineering laboratory. There were 42 experimental subjects with a balanced gender ratio, all of whom were college students aged 22–28 years with normal or corrected-to-normal vision, and no conditions such as color blindness or color vision deficiency. Among them, 18 subjects were from industrial design, and the other 24 subjects were from other majors such as interaction design and graphic design.

All three mapping experiments (paintings–Kansei words, CFGs–Kansei words, and PCGs–Kansei words) were conducted using the same group of 42 participants in a controlled laboratory setting. The order of the three experiments was randomized across participants to minimize order effects. Within each experiment, the presentation order of stimuli was also randomized individually for each participant.

Each trial began with a fixation cross displayed for 500 ms, followed by the stimulus (a painting image, a CFG diagram, or a product color genome diagram) presented on a 24-inch monitor with a resolution of 1920 × 1080 pixels. Participants rated each stimulus on one Kansei word pair (7-point scale) at their own pace, and the stimulus remained on screen until response, followed by a 1000 ms inter-trial interval. No time limit was imposed, but participants were encouraged to respond based on their intuitive impressions.

All 42 participants completed the three mapping experiments in a single session lasting approximately 60 min. A 5-minute break was provided between experiments to reduce fatigue. Prior to the formal experiments, a practice session with three stimuli (not included in the formal set) was conducted to familiarize participants with the procedure. The entire experiment was programmed and administered using E-Prime 3.0 software, which ensured precise timing and automatic randomization of stimulus presentation.

Representative CFGs

Chinese painting samples library

To construct a representative and diverse library of Chinese paintings for color feature extraction, a systematic multi-stage selection procedure was adopted. The collected samples followed these principles: (1) The samples originated from albums of painting published by the national publishing houses. (2) The paintings with vibrant colors were selected, while ink-wash paintings were excluded because their aesthetic system relies on ink gradation and void rather than chromatic richness, making them unsuitable as sources for color transfer. (3) The selected paintings were limited to the modern and contemporary period (late 19th to 20th century), in which the colors remain well preserved without significant oxidation or fading, ensuring high aesthetic value and meaningful color references. (4) The themes were restricted to flower-bird paintings and landscape paintings, the two major genres in Chinese painting that foreground natural subjects without human figures. Figurative elements, especially human faces, are known to involuntarily capture visual attention and could bias Kansei evaluations by diverting focus away from chromatic composition toward semantic or social cues.

Verification experiment

To further validate the rationality of the proposed method and the advantages of the subjective evaluation, this paper conducted a verification experiment. The experiment compared two distinct color transfer and design approaches: (A) the proposed Kansei transfer-based color design method; and (B) an AI-based color transfer and design method representing emerging techniques.

Systematic generation and objective selection of the AI-generated scheme

Based on the focus group results, “gorgeous” was selected as the target Kansei word for the AI comparison, consistent with the main case study.

To generate candidate schemes for comparison, three representative AI systems were selected. Midjourney v6 is a widely used commercial diffusion model known for its aesthetic quality. Nano Banana, powered by Google Gemini 2.5 Flash Image, is optimized for fast photorealistic rendering. Doubao, built on the Seedream 4.0 architecture, is a Chinese domestic model that offers native bilingual support and is explicitly optimized for Chinese cultural contexts. The inclusion of Doubao ensures that the benchmark reflects AI tools accessible to Chinese designers and accounts for potential language bias, since Western models often perform suboptimally with Chinese prompts.

For each system, two prompt variants were used. Prompt 1 (simple): A coffee machine color design matching the Kansei word “gorgeous”, professional product rendering, 4 K resolution, aspect ratio 1:1. Prompt 2 (detailed): A premium coffee machine with a “gorgeous” color scheme that is rich, splendid, and visually striking. The design should evoke opulence and luxury while remaining elegant. The product should be rendered photorealistically, with soft studio lighting, subtle reflections, and a high-end kitchen appliance aesthetic. Aspect ratio 1:1, 4 K resolution.

To ensure each system performed optimally, prompts were provided in the language best suited to its training regime: English for Midjourney and Nano Banana, and Chinese for Doubao. This bilingual strategy prevents artificial disadvantages caused by translation or tokenization mismatches.

For each (system, prompt) combination, 20 images were generated with different random seeds, producing candidate designs. All images were rendered at 1024 × 1024 pixels with standard platform settings. Random seeds were recorded for reproducibility.

A new cohort of 52 participants was recruited, comprising 26 female and 26 male participants aged 22 to 28 years. None had participated in any prior experiments. Each participant rated a random subset of 48 images on a 7-point scale for affective congruence with “gorgeous,” where 1 indicated “not at all” and 7 indicated “extremely.” Across the 120 images, each received 20 to 22 ratings through balanced assignment. The image with the highest mean score was selected as the representative AI-generated scheme, designated as Scheme B.

The selected Scheme B originated from Midjourney v6 with the detailed English prompt ( M=5.92, SD=1.04, n=21 ratings). A Kruskal-Wallis test showed no significant difference among the three systems in achievable scores ( p=0.31), confirming no systematic bias.

Comparative evaluation and results

Subjects scored these schemes across five dimensions to comprehensively assess both the perceptual quality of the color transfer and the market potential of the resulting product. The first three dimensions evaluated color transfer fidelity: ① Affective congruence denotes the matching degree between the scheme and the target Kansei word. ② Aesthetic appeal reflects subjects’ perception of the color scheme’s visual harmony. ③ Design suitability indicates the appropriateness level of the color scheme for the product. The remaining two dimensions assessed the resultant product quality: ④ Perceived premiumness is defined as the extent to which the color scheme makes the product appear high-end and valuable. ⑤ Purchase intention indicates the willingness to purchase the product based on its color appearance.

To ensure experimental validity, a new group of 63 subjects who had not participated in the previous experiments evaluated the two color schemes across all five dimensions using a 7-point Likert scale. The subjects were balanced in gender, aged 22–28 years, with normal or corrected vision, and no conditions such as color blindness or color vision deficiency. For each of the five evaluation dimensions, a paired-samples t-test was conducted to compare the mean scores of Scheme (A) and Scheme (B), as each participant evaluated both color schemes. Discussion

This research pioneers a structured transfer protocol that systematically bridges artistic painting palettes (source domain) and industrial product design (target domain). By establishing a clear, stepwise pathway centered on Kansei (affective experience) as the pivotal mediator, we resolve the inherent ambiguity in translating subjective artistic inspiration into actionable design elements, such as color schemes. This protocol overcomes the ad hoc nature of traditional art-to-design processes through its dual-channel quantification mechanism. At the painting end, CFGs are objectively deconstructed via clustering algorithms to extract color attributes. At the product end, functional and structural constraints are encoded into definable PCGs. Crucially, Kansei attributes serve as the decision-making compass throughout this cross-domain translation.

Central to this framework is a rigorously validated mapping model where extracted painting CFGs and synthesized PCGs are evaluated through subjective experiments to quantify their Kansei signatures, identifying high-impact affective labels (e.g., “serene,” “luxurious,” “retro”). This enables the construction of a precise transfer chain, expressed as [CFGs⇄Kansei⇄PCGs] → Product Color Schemes. The resulting Kansei-Mediated Mapping Protocol guarantees that the emotional essence of the source artwork is faithfully preserved in the final product aesthetics.

We demonstrate the framework’s efficacy in cross-cultural translation through a case study transferring chromatic motifs from Chinese paintings (embodying Eastern aesthetics) to Western-culture-associated coffee machines. Results confirm simultaneous enhancement of affective color quality and perceived design value, thereby validating the protocol as a scientific tool for culturally adaptive design. Given its modular architecture, the method holds considerable potential for generalization beyond the present case. It could, in principle, accommodate diverse products (home appliances, wearables) and artistic sources (Western oil paintings, indigenous crafts), thereby enabling market-specific customization. For example, future applications may see rice cookers designed for Chinese culinary traditions imbued with color schemes sourced from Impressionist paintings to align with European consumer preferences.

Beyond its emotional and aesthetic merits, the feasibility of faithfully translating a color scheme into a physical product constitutes a practical dimension of design quality. The “gorgeous” scheme (Fig. 11) consists of three discrete, solid color blocks without gradients or continuous tones, a configuration inherently compatible with mainstream small-appliance manufacturing processes. Moreover, the three constituent colors were extracted from RGB clustering of Chinese painting images, ensuring they naturally reside within the standard digital color gamut without exceeding the reproducible range of conventional printing or coating systems. Additionally, the main color occupies 75%–85% of the product surface area, while the auxiliary and decorative colors are distributed as separated blocks without large-area seamless splicing. This physically segmented layout relaxes the stringent requirement for inter-batch color consistency. Even if minor chromatic deviations occur across different production batches, the perceptual impact is minimized due to the inherent spatial separation of the color regions. In summary, the emotionally driven color scheme resulting from the proposed method possesses inherently favorable manufacturability, capable of being transformed into a high-quality product finish with low engineering risk.

Beyond its practical advantages, our [CFGs⇄Kansei⇄PCGs] → Product Color Schemes paradigm extends the methodological boundaries of product color design. By embedding Kansei evaluation into the transfer process, it surpasses conventional approaches in generating consumer-aligned solutions while ensuring aesthetic coherence. The verification experiment further confirmed that the proposed method outperformed the AI-based baseline across all five evaluation dimensions, with particularly pronounced advantages in affective congruence and purchase intention, underscoring its dual strength in emotional fidelity and market appeal.

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the verification experiment demonstrated the superiority of the proposed method across all five evaluation dimensions, including affective congruence, aesthetic appeal, design suitability, perceived premiumness, and purchase intention. However, the evaluation was conducted in a controlled laboratory setting with student participants, which may not fully reflect real-world consumer preferences. The extent to which the generated color schemes would be preferred in actual market contexts remains to be investigated. Second, the case study focused on a single product category (coffee machine) and one cultural translation direction (Chinese paintings to Chinese market). Third, the AI comparison was limited to a single Kansei word and a single product category. Moreover, the rapid evolution of generative AI models means that newer or different systems may yield varying relative performance. Future studies should address these limitations in three complementary directions. Methodologically, validation should be extended to real-world settings by adopting approaches such as online A/B testing, focus group discussions with target consumers, or sales data analysis in collaboration with industry partners. Empirically, the generalizability of the method should be examined across diverse product categories and cultural contexts. For AI benchmarking, future comparisons should incorporate emerging AI models, multiple Kansei dimensions, and hybrid human-AI workflows where AI-generated candidates are refined via the proposed Kansei-mediated mapping.

In terms of technical advancement, future work will enhance the perceptual accuracy of color extraction by adopting perceptually uniform color spaces such as CIELAB, where Euclidean distance better approximates human color discrimination, thereby strengthening the emotional fidelity of extracted color features. Furthermore, the PCG framework will be extended to incorporate material parameters, including surface texture, glossiness, and reflectance, to account for their influence on Kansei responses, enabling a more comprehensive modeling of product aesthetics. Concurrently, computational efficiency will be improved by leveraging computer vision for automated CFG extraction and developing structured PCG databases. These advances will enhance scalability while preserving emotional resonance, paving the way for standardized, replicable implementation across global design industries.

Conclusion

Grounded in Kansei engineering, this study proposes an innovative cross-domain method for product color design by transferring palettes from paintings (source) to consumer products (target). The approach accomplishes color transfer across differences in spatial dimensions, color quantity and functional use, effectively bridging the gap between artistic expression and product aesthetics. Using Chinese paintings and a coffee machine as representative examples, the proposed method demonstrates strong effectiveness and generalizability. It offers a systematic framework for incorporating culturally rich color schemes into product design, enriching the theory and practice of color matching. Furthermore, this method enhances the aesthetic and emotional quality of product colors, supports cultural localization strategies, and provides a valuable pathway for promoting product adaptation in international markets.
