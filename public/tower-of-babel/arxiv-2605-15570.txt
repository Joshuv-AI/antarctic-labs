Spectral conjugate gradient projection methods for large-scale monotone equations without Lipschitz continuity
Kabenge Hamiss, Mohammed Alshahrani, Mujahid N. Syed

Abstract
We introduce two derivative-free projection methods for large-scale systems of nonlinear monotone equations subject to convex constraints. Both methods incorporate an adaptive spectral parameter into established conjugate gradient frameworks: the first generalizes the modified optimal Perry method via an eigenvalue-optimized scaling matrix, and the second generalizes the Hager–Zhang-type conjugate gradient projection method via a spectral Dai–Liao parameter. The resulting search directions satisfy a sufficient descent condition independent of the line search. For the first method, we establish global convergence under monotonicity alone, without requiring Lipschitz continuity of the mapping. For the second, global convergence holds under the standard monotonicity and Lipschitz continuity assumptions. Numerical experiments on 18 test problems across dimensions up to 120,000, together with applications to ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -regularized signal recovery and regularized logistic regression, confirm the practical effectiveness of the proposed approach.



Keywords:
Nonlinear monotone equations, convex constraints, spectral conjugate gradient method, derivative-free method, projection method, global convergence, compressed sensing, logistic regression.

Introduction

We consider the problem of finding a vector
x\in\Gamma
satisfying

G(x)=0,

(1)

where
G\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a continuous and monotone mapping and
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a nonempty, closed, and convex set. Systems of this form arise in diverse areas of science and engineering. Variational inequality problems and complementarity problems can often be reformulated as (
1
)
[
19
,
32
,
53
]
. Further applications include chemical equilibrium computations
[
33
,
50
]
, compressed sensing and signal restoration
[
14
,
2
]
, regularized logistic regression and machine learning
[
23
,
9
]
, and problems in fluid and plasma physics
[
7
,
17
]
.

When
G
is differentiable, Newton and quasi-Newton methods
[
35
,
24
]
offer rapid local convergence. However, they require computing or approximating the Jacobian of
G
at each iteration, which entails
O(n^{2})
storage and substantial computational cost per step. For large-scale problems, or when
G
is not differentiable, these methods become impractical.

Conjugate gradient (CG) methods provide a natural alternative. They require only function evaluations and
O(n)
storage, making them well suited for large-scale problems. Classical CG parameters—Fletcher–Reeves
[
15
]
, Polak–Ribière–Polyak
[
37
]
, Hestenes–Stiefel
[
20
]
, and Dai–Yuan
[
11
]
—have given rise to a rich family of CG-based derivative-free methods for solving (
1
); see
[
8
,
27
,
29
,
48
]
and the references therein. The spectral gradient method of Zhang and Zhou
[
52
]
, which incorporates Barzilai–Borwein step sizes
[
4
]
, has proven especially effective for monotone equations. The combination of CG update formulas with spectral parameters has led to spectral conjugate gradient methods
[
5
,
1
]
, which often exhibit improved numerical performance.

A key ingredient for extending these methods to the constrained setting
\Gamma\neq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the projection technique of Solodov and Svaiter
[
40
]
. The approach proceeds in three stages at each iteration: (i) compute a descent direction
p_{k}
and find a step size
\alpha_{k}
via a line search, yielding the trial point
z_{k}=x_{k}+\alpha_{k}p_{k}
; (ii) construct a hyperplane that separates the current iterate from the solution set, using the monotonicity of
G
; (iii) project onto this hyperplane to obtain the next iterate
x_{k+1}
. This framework has become the standard approach for designing derivative-free projection methods
[
49
,
43
,
16
,
55
]
.

Three-term CG methods, which generate directions of the form
p_{k}=-\theta_{0}G_{k}+\theta_{1}p_{k-1}+\theta_{2}w_{k-1}
for suitable vectors
w_{k-1}
and scalars
\theta_{i}
, have received considerable attention due to their favorable descent properties and robustness; see
[
51
,
3
,
34
]
for the unconstrained setting and
[
54
,
22
,
30
,
31
]
for the constrained monotone setting. Recently, several authors have combined three-term CG structures with spectral parameters and the Solodov–Svaiter projection to obtain methods with improved theoretical and computational properties
[
46
,
1
,
45
]
.

Among these methods, three serve as the foundation and benchmarks for our work. Sabi’u et al.
[
38
]
proposed the
MOPCGM
, which derives the CG update from a modified optimal Perry matrix via eigenvalue analysis and establishes global convergence under monotonicity and Lipschitz continuity. Zheng et al.
[
54
]
proposed the
CGPM
, which adapts the Hager–Zhang
[
18
]
conjugate gradient parameter to the constrained monotone setting and proves convergence under similar assumptions. Ibrahim et al.
[
22
]
proposed the
STTDFPM
, a spectral three-term method that achieves global convergence without requiring Lipschitz continuity, by using a pseudomonotonicity assumption.

In this paper, we introduce an adaptive spectral scaling parameter
\lambda_{k}
into both the
MOPCGM
and
CGPM
frameworks. The parameter
\lambda_{k}
is bounded within an interval
[\alpha_{\min},\alpha_{\max}]
and adapts to the local curvature of the problem, drawing on ideas from the Barzilai–Borwein spectral gradient family. The resulting methods, which we call
GMOPCGM
and
GCGPM
, retain the sufficient descent property of their predecessors while offering additional flexibility through the spectral parameter. The contributions of this paper can be summarized as follows.

(i)

We derive two new search directions that generalize the
MOPCGM
and
CGPM
directions via a spectral parameter
\lambda_{k}
, and we show that both satisfy a sufficient descent condition independent of the line search.

(ii)

We prove global convergence of
GMOPCGM
under Assumptions
A1
and
A2
alone (nonemptiness of the solution set and monotonicity), without requiring Lipschitz continuity of
G
.

(iii)

We prove global convergence of
GCGPM
under the standard Assumptions
A1
–
A3
(including Lipschitz continuity).

(iv)

We present numerical experiments on 18 test problems, an application to compressed sensing, and an application to regularized logistic regression on real-world datasets that demonstrate the practical advantages of the spectral generalization.

2
Preliminaries

Throughout this paper,
\|\cdot\|
denotes the Euclidean norm,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the standard inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and we write
G_{k}=G(x_{k})
for brevity. The projection operator onto the closed convex set
\Gamma
is defined by

\Pi_{\Gamma}(x)=\arg\min_{y\in\Gamma}\|y-x\|,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Definition 1
.

A mapping
G\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be
monotone
if

\langle G(x)-G(y),\,x-y\rangle\geq 0,\quad\forall\,x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

The projection operator satisfies the following well-known properties
[
40
]
:

(a)

\langle x-\Pi_{\Gamma}(x),\,y-\Pi_{\Gamma}(x)\rangle\leq 0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
y\in\Gamma
;

(b)

\|\Pi_{\Gamma}(x)-\Pi_{\Gamma}(y)\|\leq\|x-y\|
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
(non-expansiveness);

(c)

\|\Pi_{\Gamma}(x)-y\|^{2}\leq\|x-y\|^{2}-\|x-\Pi_{\Gamma}(x)\|^{2}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
y\in\Gamma
.

We make the following standing assumptions.

Assumption A1
.

The solution set
S^{*}=\{x\in\Gamma:G(x)=0\}
is nonempty.

Assumption A2
.

The mapping
G
is monotone on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Assumption A3
.

The mapping
G
is Lipschitz continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, i.e., there exists a constant
L>0
such that
\|G(x)-G(y)\|\leq L\|x-y\|
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Since the algorithms evaluate
G
at each iteration, continuity of
G
is implicitly required; this is guaranteed by Assumption
A3
when it is in force, and holds for all problems considered in this paper. Assumption
A3
is used only in the convergence analysis of
GCGPM
and in the alternative convergence proof of
GMOPCGM
. The primary convergence result for
GMOPCGM
requires only Assumptions
A1
and
A2
.

3
The proposed methods

We derive the two proposed search directions and verify their descent properties. Throughout, we use the notation

s_{k-1}=z_{k-1}-x_{k-1},\qquad y_{k-1}=G_{k}-G_{k-1},\qquad v_{k-1}=y_{k-1}+\tau s_{k-1},

(2)

for a fixed parameter
\tau>0
.

We begin with the generalized modified optimal Perry conjugate gradient method. Following the quasi-Newton approach of Perry
[
36
]
and Sabi’u et al.
[
38
]
, consider the search direction
p_{k}=-\tilde{Q}_{k}G_{k}
, where

\tilde{Q}_{k}=\lambda I-\frac{\lambda}{2}\,\frac{y_{k-1}s_{k-1}^{T}}{y_{k-1}^{T}s_{k-1}}-\frac{\lambda}{2}\,\frac{s_{k-1}y_{k-1}^{T}}{y_{k-1}^{T}s_{k-1}}+t_{k}\,\frac{s_{k-1}s_{k-1}^{T}}{y_{k-1}^{T}s_{k-1}},

(3)

with
\lambda>0
and
t_{k}>0
. Since
G
is monotone,
s_{k-1}^{T}y_{k-1}>0
whenever
x_{k}\neq x^{*}
, so both
s_{k-1}
and
y_{k-1}
are nonzero. For any vector
a
in the subspace spanned by
\{s_{k-1},y_{k-1}\}
,

a^{T}\tilde{Q}_{k}\,a=t_{k}\,\frac{(a^{T}s_{k-1})^{2}}{y_{k-1}^{T}s_{k-1}}>0,

confirming positive definiteness on this subspace. The matrix
\tilde{Q}_{k}
is a rank-2 perturbation of
\lambda I
, so
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of multiplicity
n-2
. Let
\eta_{k}^{+}
and
\eta_{k}^{-}
denote the remaining two eigenvalues. We determine the optimal
t_{k}
through the following eigenvalue analysis. For readability, set

a\;=\;\frac{\|s_{k-1}\|^{2}}{s_{k-1}^{T}y_{k-1}},\qquad b\;=\;\frac{\|s_{k-1}\|\,\|y_{k-1}\|}{s_{k-1}^{T}y_{k-1}}.

(4)

Lemma 1
.

Let
\tilde{Q}_{k}
be defined by (
3
). Then

\tr(\tilde{Q}_{k})\;=\;\lambda(n-1)+a\,t_{k}.

(5)

Proof.

By linearity of the trace,
\tr(y_{k-1}s_{k-1}^{T})=s_{k-1}^{T}y_{k-1}
, and
\tr(s_{k-1}s_{k-1}^{T})=\|s_{k-1}\|^{2}
:

\tr(\tilde{Q}_{k})=n\lambda-\tfrac{\lambda}{2}-\tfrac{\lambda}{2}+a\,t_{k}=\lambda(n-1)+a\,t_{k}.\qed

Lemma 2
.

Let
\tilde{Q}_{k}
be defined by (
3
). Then

\tr\!\left(\tilde{Q}_{k}^{T}\tilde{Q}_{k}\right)\;=\;\lambda^{2}\!\left(n-\tfrac{3}{2}\right)+\tfrac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\,b^{2}+a^{2}t_{k}^{2}.

(6)

Proof.

Expanding
\tilde{Q}_{k}^{T}\tilde{Q}_{k}
term by term, applying the trace identities
\tr(y_{k-1}s_{k-1}^{T})=s_{k-1}^{T}y_{k-1}
,
\tr(s_{k-1}s_{k-1}^{T})=\|s_{k-1}\|^{2}
, and
\tr(y_{k-1}y_{k-1}^{T})=\|y_{k-1}\|^{2}
, one obtains

\tr\!\left(\tilde{Q}_{k}^{T}\tilde{Q}_{k}\right)=n\lambda^{2}-\tfrac{3}{2}\lambda^{2}+\cancel{t_{k}\lambda\,a}+\tfrac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\,b^{2}-\cancel{t_{k}\lambda\,a}+a^{2}t_{k}^{2},

which simplifies to (
6
).
∎

Lemma 3
.

The eigenvalues
\eta_{k}^{+}
and
\eta_{k}^{-}
satisfy

\displaystyle\eta_{k}^{+}+\eta_{k}^{-}

\displaystyle=\lambda+a\,t_{k},

(7)

\displaystyle\eta_{k}^{+}\,\eta_{k}^{-}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{\lambda^{2}}{4}(1-b^{2})+\lambda\,a\,t_{k}.

(8)

Proof.

From Lemma
1
,
\lambda(n{-}2)+\eta_{k}^{+}+\eta_{k}^{-}=\lambda(n{-}1)+a\,t_{k}
, giving (
7
). From Lemma
2
,
(\eta_{k}^{+})^{2}+(\eta_{k}^{-})^{2}=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}(1+b^{2})+a^{2}t_{k}^{2}
. Applying
\eta_{k}^{+}\eta_{k}^{-}=\frac{1}{2}[(\eta_{k}^{+}+\eta_{k}^{-})^{2}-((\eta_{k}^{+})^{2}+(\eta_{k}^{-})^{2})]
yields (
8
).
∎

Remark 1
.

Setting
\lambda=1
recovers
\eta_{k}^{+}\eta_{k}^{-}=\frac{1}{4}(1-b^{2})+a\,t_{k}
, the expression in
[
38
]
.

Lemma 4
.

The value of
t_{k}
minimizing the condition number of
\tilde{Q}_{k}
is

t_{k}^{*}=\frac{\lambda}{a}=\lambda\,\frac{s_{k-1}^{T}y_{k-1}}{\|s_{k-1}\|^{2}}.

(9)

Proof.

From (
7
)–(
8
),
\eta_{k}^{\pm}
satisfy
\eta^{2}-(\lambda+a\,t_{k})\eta+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4}(1-b^{2})+\lambda a\,t_{k}=0
, so

(\eta_{k}^{+}-\eta_{k}^{-})^{2}=(a\,t_{k}-\lambda)^{2}+\lambda^{2}(b^{2}-1).

Since
b\geq 1
by Cauchy–Schwarz, this is minimized when
t_{k}=\lambda/a
.
∎

Remark 2
.

For
\lambda=1
, we recover
t_{k}^{*}=s_{k-1}^{T}y_{k-1}/\|s_{k-1}\|^{2}
, the optimal Perry parameter in
[
38
]
.

To allow
\lambda
to adapt to the problem and iterate, we define the spectral parameter

\lambda_{k}=\Pi_{[\alpha_{\min},\,\alpha_{\max}]}\!\left(\max\!\left\{\frac{\|s_{k-1}\|^{2}}{s_{k-1}^{T}v_{k-1}},\;\frac{s_{k-1}^{T}v_{k-1}}{\|v_{k-1}\|^{2}}\right\}\right),

(10)

where
\Pi_{[a,b]}(x)=\max\{a,\min\{x,b\}\}
and
0<\alpha_{\min}\leq\alpha_{\max}
. These are Barzilai–Borwein-type spectral ratios
[
4
]
; the projection keeps
\lambda_{k}
bounded.

The
GMOPCGM
search direction is then

p_{k}=\begin{cases}-G_{k},&k=0,\\[6.0pt]
-M_{k}\,G_{k}+\theta_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle G}\,p_{k-1},&k\geq 1,\end{cases}

(11)

where

\displaystyle M_{k}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{k}+\theta_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle G}\,\frac{G_{k}^{T}p_{k-1}}{\|G_{k}\|^{2}},

(12)

\displaystyle\theta_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle G}

\displaystyle=\frac{(v_{k-1}-t_{k}^{*}\,s_{k-1})^{T}G_{k}}{p_{k-1}^{T}v_{k-1}},

(13)

\displaystyle t_{k}^{*}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{k}\,\frac{s_{k-1}^{T}v_{k-1}}{\|s_{k-1}\|^{2}}.

(14)

Lemma 5
.

Let
\{p_{k}\}
and
\{G_{k}\}
be generated by Algorithm
1
. Then

G_{k}^{T}p_{k}\leq-\alpha_{\min}\|G_{k}\|^{2}.

(15)

Proof.

Multiplying (
11
) by
G_{k}^{T}
:

G_{k}^{T}p_{k}=-\lambda_{k}\|G_{k}\|^{2}-\theta_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle G}\,\frac{G_{k}^{T}p_{k-1}}{\|G_{k}\|^{2}}\,\|G_{k}\|^{2}+\theta_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle G}\,G_{k}^{T}p_{k-1}=-\lambda_{k}\|G_{k}\|^{2}.

Since
\lambda_{k}\geq\alpha_{\min}
by (
10
), the result follows.
∎

The descent condition (
15
) is independent of
t_{k}^{*}
.

.

\displaystyle=-\lambda_{k}\|G_{k}\|^{2}+(1{+}\tau)\,\frac{G_{k}^{T}w_{k-1}}{p_{k-1}^{T}w_{k-1}}\,G_{k}^{T}p_{k-1}-\lambda_{k}\,\frac{\|w_{k-1}\|^{2}(G_{k}^{T}p_{k-1})^{2}}{(p_{k-1}^{T}w_{k-1})^{2}}.

4
Convergence analysis

We first establish the convergence properties of
GMOPCGM
(Algorithm
1
).

Lemma 7
.

Suppose Assumption
A3
holds. Then the step size in Algorithm
1
satisfies

\alpha_{k}\geq\min\!\left\{\beta,\;\frac{\alpha_{\min}\rho}{L+\zeta\zeta_{2}}\cdot\frac{\|G_{k}\|^{2}}{\|p_{k}\|^{2}}\right\}.

Proof.

If
\alpha_{k}=\beta
, the bound is trivial. Otherwise,
\tilde{\alpha}_{k}=\alpha_{k}/\rho
violates (
16
), so

G(x_{k}+\tilde{\alpha}_{k}p_{k})^{T}p_{k}>-\zeta\tilde{\alpha}_{k}\|p_{k}\|^{2}\,\Pi_{[\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}]}(\|G(x_{k}+\tilde{\alpha}_{k}p_{k})\|).

Using the sufficient descent condition (
15
) and the Lipschitz continuity of
G
,

\displaystyle\alpha_{\min}\|G_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq-G_{k}^{T}p_{k}=(G(x_{k}+\tilde{\alpha}_{k}p_{k})-G_{k})^{T}p_{k}-G(x_{k}+\tilde{\alpha}_{k}p_{k})^{T}p_{k}

\displaystyle\leq L\tilde{\alpha}_{k}\|p_{k}\|^{2}+\zeta\tilde{\alpha}_{k}\|p_{k}\|^{2}\zeta_{2}=\frac{\alpha_{k}}{\rho}(L+\zeta\zeta_{2})\|p_{k}\|^{2}.\qed

Lemma 8
.

Suppose Assumptions
A1
–
A3
hold. Then the direction
p_{k}
satisfies the trust region property

\alpha_{\min}\|G_{k}\|\leq\|p_{k}\|\leq\kappa\|G_{k}\|,

where
\kappa=\alpha_{\max}+2(1+\alpha_{\max})\frac{L\gamma+\tau}{\tau}
.

Proof.

The lower bound follows directly from (
15
) and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality:
\alpha_{\min}\|G_{k}\|^{2}\leq|G_{k}^{T}p_{k}|\leq\|G_{k}\|\|p_{k}\|
.

For the upper bound, note that
s_{k-1}=\alpha_{k-1}p_{k-1}
and
\|v_{k-1}\|\leq(L\gamma+\tau)\|s_{k-1}\|
. It follows that
|t_{k}^{*}|=\lambda_{k}\frac{|v_{k-1}^{T}s_{k-1}|}{\|s_{k-1}\|^{2}}\leq\alpha_{\max}(L\gamma+\tau)
, and consequently

|\theta_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle G}|\leq(1+\alpha_{\max})\frac{(L\gamma+\tau)\|G_{k}\|}{\tau\|p_{k-1}\|}.

Substituting into
\|p_{k}\|\leq\alpha_{\max}\|G_{k}\|+2|\theta_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle G}|\|p_{k-1}\|
yields the result.
∎

Lemma 9
.

Suppose Assumptions
A1
and
A2
hold. Then
\lim_{k\to\infty}\alpha_{k}\|p_{k}\|=0
.

Proof.

From the line search (
16
), we have
G(z_{k})^{T}p_{k}\leq-\zeta\alpha_{k}\|p_{k}\|^{2}\|G(z_{k})\|
, which gives

G(z_{k})^{T}(x_{k}-z_{k})=-\alpha_{k}G(z_{k})^{T}p_{k}\geq\zeta\|x_{k}-z_{k}\|^{2}\|G(z_{k})\|.

(24)

By monotonicity of
G
and Assumption
A1
, there exists
x^{*}\in\Gamma
with
G(x^{*})=0
, and

G(z_{k})^{T}(x_{k}-x^{*})\geq G(z_{k})^{T}(x_{k}-z_{k})\geq\zeta\|x_{k}-z_{k}\|^{2}\|G(z_{k})\|.

(25)

The non-expansiveness of the projection then gives

\displaystyle\|x_{k+1}-x^{*}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}-2\gamma\mu_{k}G(z_{k})^{T}(x_{k}-x^{*})+\gamma^{2}\mu_{k}^{2}\|G(z_{k})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}-\gamma(2-\gamma)\frac{(G(z_{k})^{T}(x_{k}-z_{k}))^{2}}{\|G(z_{k})\|^{2}}.

Combining with (
24
),

\|x_{k+1}-x^{*}\|^{2}\leq\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}-\gamma(2-\gamma)\zeta^{2}\|x_{k}-z_{k}\|^{4}.

(26)

The sequence
\{\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|\}
is therefore non-increasing and bounded below, hence convergent. Summing (
26
) over
k
yields

\gamma(2-\gamma)\zeta^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\|x_{k}-z_{k}\|^{4}\leq\|x_{0}-x^{*}\|^{2}<\infty,

so
\|x_{k}-z_{k}\|=\alpha_{k}\|p_{k}\|\to 0
.
∎

Theorem 1
.

Suppose Assumptions
A1
and
A2
hold. Let
\{x_{k}\}
be generated by Algorithm
1
. Then

\liminf_{k\to\infty}\|G_{k}\|=0.

(27)

Proof.

Suppose for contradiction that there exists
\varepsilon>0
with
\|G_{k}\|>\varepsilon
for all
k
. By (
15
),
\|p_{k}\|\geq\alpha_{\min}\varepsilon>0
for all
k
. Together with Lemma
9
, this implies
\alpha_{k}\to 0
.

For each
k
, let
\bar{\alpha}_{k}=\alpha_{k}/\rho
, which violates (
16
):

-G(x_{k}+\bar{\alpha}_{k}p_{k})^{T}p_{k}<\bar{\alpha}_{k}\zeta\|p_{k}\|^{2}\zeta_{2}.

Since
\{x_{k}\}
and
\{p_{k}\}
are bounded (by Lemma
9
and the descent inequality), we may extract subsequences converging to accumulation points
\bar{x}
and
\bar{p}
. Taking
k\to\infty
in the violated line search condition gives
-G(\bar{x})^{T}\bar{p}\leq 0
, while the sufficient descent condition gives
-G(\bar{x})^{T}\bar{p}\geq\alpha_{\min}\|G(\bar{x})\|^{2}>0
, a contradiction.
∎

Under the additional Lipschitz continuity assumption, we can give an alternative proof.

Proof.

If

so

.

Application to compressed sensing

Signal restoration—recovering an original signal from degraded or incomplete observations—is a fundamental problem in signal processing
[
10
,
42
]
. In compressed sensing, the goal is to recover a sparse signal
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
from measurements
b=Ax+\nu
, where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
with
m\ll n
is a sensing matrix and
\nu
is additive noise
[
14
]
. This is accomplished by solving the
\ell_{1}
-regularized least-squares problem

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\;\tfrac{1}{2}\|b-Ax\|^{2}+\tau\|x\|_{1},

(28)

where
\tau>0
is a regularization parameter promoting sparsity. Following
[
47
,
16
,
39
]
, problem (
28
) can be reformulated as

G(z)=\min\{z,\;Qz+c\}=0,\quad z\geq 0,

(29)

where
z=(\max\{x,0\};\,\max\{-x,0\})
,
Q=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}A^{T}A&-A^{T}A\\
-A^{T}A&A^{T}A\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)
, and
c=\tau\mathbf{1}_{2n}+(-A^{T}b;\,A^{T}b)
. The mapping
G
in (
29
) is monotone and continuous
[
24
]
, so the problem fits the framework of (
1
).

We generate a sparse signal
x_{\mathrm{orig}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
n=2^{12}
having
k
randomly placed nonzero entries drawn from
\mathcal{N}(0,10^{-3})
. The sensing matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is constructed by drawing i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,10^{-3})
entries and orthogonalizing the rows via a QR factorization. The observation is
b=Ax_{\mathrm{orig}}+\nu
, where
\nu\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I)
. The regularization parameter is set adaptively as
\tau=0.01\|A^{T}b\|_{\infty}
, and each algorithm is initialized at
z_{0}=(\max\{A^{T}b,0\};\,\max\{-A^{T}b,0\})
and terminated when
\|G(z_{k})\|<10^{-5}
. All methods use the same parameters as in the benchmark experiments.

To study how the methods perform under varying problem difficulty, we sweep over three parameters:

(i)

sparsity ratio
k/n\in\{0.05,\,0.10,\,0.20,\,0.30\}
,

(ii)

measurement ratio
m/n\in\{0.25,\,0.50,\,0.75\}
,

(iii)

noise level
\sigma\in\{0,\,10^{-3},\,10^{-2},\,10^{-1}\}
.

For each of the
4\times 3\times 4=48
configurations, we run 5 independent trials and report median values. The recovery quality is measured by the mean square error
\mathrm{MSE}=\|x_{\mathrm{orig}}-x_{\mathrm{rec}}\|/n
.

Table

Application to regularized logistic regression

Unlike the compressed sensing application, where the monotone equation is linear, regularized logistic regression gives rise to a genuinely nonlinear monotone equation, providing a complementary test of the methods on a real-world machine learning task.

Binary classification—assigning labels to data based on observed features—is a fundamental problem in machine learning
[
6
]
. Given
N
labeled data pairs
(a_{i},b_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\{-1,+1\}
,
i=1,\ldots,N
, the regularized logistic regression problem seeks a weight vector
x
that minimizes

\min_{x\in\Gamma}\;f(x)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\!\bigl(1+\exp(-b_{i}\,a_{i}^{T}x)\bigr)+\frac{\mu}{2}\|x\|^{2},

(30)

where
\mu>0
is an
\ell_{2}
-regularization parameter promoting generalization, and
\Gamma=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\|x\|_{\infty}\leq C\}
is a box constraint that bounds the model weights. Constraining the weights to a bounded set prevents numerical overflow in the exponential, serves as an implicit
\ell_{\infty}
-norm penalty that complements the
\ell_{2}
term, and is standard practice in large-scale machine learning for improving generalization and numerical stability. The gradient of
f
is

G(x)\;:=\;\nabla f(x)\;=\;-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{b_{i}\,a_{i}}{1+\exp(b_{i}\,a_{i}^{T}x)}\;+\;\mu\,x.

(31)

The Hessian
\nabla^{2}f(x)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}(1-\sigma_{i})\,a_{i}a_{i}^{T}+\mu I
, where
\sigma_{i}=(1+\exp(-b_{i}a_{i}^{T}x))^{-1}
, satisfies
\nabla^{2}f(x)\succeq\mu I\succ 0
for all
x
. Therefore
f
is strongly convex,
G
is strictly monotone, and
G
is Lipschitz continuous on the compact set
\Gamma
with constant
L\leq\frac{1}{4N}\|A^{T}A\|+\mu
(using
\sigma(1-\sigma)\leq 1/4
). Consequently, Assumptions
A1
–
A3
are satisfied and the problem fits the framework of (
1
). Since all three assumptions hold, the convergence guarantees of both Theorem
1
and Theorem
3
apply. We note that
GMOPCGM
requires only Assumptions
A1
–
A2
for convergence and would therefore remain applicable even for non-Lipschitz variants of this problem, such as replacing the smooth
\ell_{2}
penalty with a non-smooth regularizer. The projection onto
\Gamma
reduces to componentwise clamping:
\Pi_{\Gamma}(x)_{j}=\max\{-C,\,\min\{x_{j},\,C\}\}
.

[
23
]
, we evaluate the five methods on binary classification datasets from the LIBSVM repository
[
6
]
. Table
6
lists the datasets used, which range from
n=122
to
n=2{,}000
features and from
N=62
to
N=47{,}272
samples. All features are standardized to zero mean and unit variance prior to optimization. We set
\mu=0.1
,
C=10
, and generate initial points as
x_{0}=4(\xi-0.5\cdot\mathbf{1}_{n})
where
\xi\sim\mathrm{Uniform}([0,1]^{n})
. Each method uses the same parameters as in the benchmark experiments (Table
1
), the same tolerance
\varepsilon=10^{-11}
, and a maximum of 5,000 iterations. For each dataset, we run 5 independent trials with different random seeds and report median values.

Conclusion

We have proposed two derivative-free projection methods,
GMOPCGM
and
GCGPM
, for solving large-scale nonlinear monotone equations subject to convex constraints. Both methods generalize existing conjugate gradient frameworks—the modified optimal Perry method (
MOPCGM
) and the conjugate gradient projection method (
CGPM
)—by incorporating an adaptive spectral scaling parameter that adjusts to the local geometry of the problem. We have shown that both methods satisfy a sufficient descent condition independent of the line search, and we have established their global convergence: for
GMOPCGM
under monotonicity alone, and for
GCGPM
under the additional Lipschitz continuity assumption.

Numerical experiments on 18 test problems across six dimensions up to
n=120{,}000
demonstrate that the spectral generalization consistently improves performance over the base methods.
GCGPM
achieves the highest convergence rate and the fewest median iterations among all five methods. Applications to compressed sensing—with a systematic sweep over sparsity, measurement ratio, and noise level—and to regularized logistic regression on real-world classification datasets further confirm the practical effectiveness of both proposed methods on problems with fundamentally different structure: the linear monotone equation arising from
\ell_{1}
-regularization and the nonlinear monotone equation arising from the logistic loss.

Future research directions include extending the convergence analysis of
GCGPM
to the pseudomonotone setting (removing the Lipschitz assumption), incorporating inertial acceleration techniques
[
25
]
, and investigating adaptive parameter selection strategies based on online learning.
