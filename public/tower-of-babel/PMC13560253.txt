Clinical and functional characterization of a novel homozygous non-canonical splice mutation (c.1910-15_1910-11delinsTTACA) in CEP290 causing Joubert syndrome
Authors: Valeriia A. Kovalskaia, Dmitriy N. Maslennikov, Ksenia A. Svirepova, Vyacheslav Y. Tabakov, Polina N. Tsabai, Evgenii V. Shchoka, Nikita S. Beskorovainiy, Jekaterina Shubina, Dmitry Y. Trofimov, Oxana P. Ryzhkova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundJoubert syndrome (JS) is a rare, predominantly autosomal recessive neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by hypotonia, motor delay, intellectual disability, oculomotor apraxia, and the hallmark “molar tooth sign” on axial view of MRI. JS is genetically heterogeneous, with pathogenic variants identified in more than 40 genes involved in primary cilia function. Among these, CEP290 is one of the most frequently mutated genes.ResultsIn this study, we investigated two children—an 11-year-old boy (the proband) and his 5-year-old sister—both presenting with a similar phenotype consistent with JS. The parents, who self-identified as Chechen, reported distant consanguinity. The family also included a healthy 13-year-old daughter. The proband had previously been evaluated by a neurologist and underwent whole-genome sequencing (WGS); however, no causative variants were identified initially. After phenotype reassessment by a clinical geneticist, we performed a reanalysis of the raw WGS data and identified a novel homozygous intronic variant of uncertain significance (VUS), c.1910-15_1910-11delinsTTACA in CEP290 (NM_025114.4). Sanger sequencing confirmed that both the proband and his affected sister were homozygous for this variant, which they inherited from their heterozygous parents. Their healthy sister did not carry the variant. mRNA-sequencing and targeted cDNA sequencing (read depth ~ 100,000x) demonstrated that this intronic variant causes completely aberrant splicing of CEP290 pre-mRNA. Predominantly this variant causes the skipping of exon 20 in the main CEP290 transcript. Alternatively, the variant results in partial inclusion of intron 19 into the mRNA, elongation of exon 20 by 58 nucleotides, and a homozygous substitution chr12:88114573 (ACTGTGTA> TTACAGTA). No canonical mRNA isoform was detected when the variant was homozygous. Both the predicted severe truncation and the likely degradation of aberrant transcripts through nonsense-mediated decay (NMD) would correspond to complete loss of CEP290 function. Following the reclassification of this VUS to likely pathogenic, the family was able to pursue in vitro fertilization (IVF) with preimplantation genetic testing for monogenic disorders (PGT-M).ConclusionOur study highlights the critical importance of proper phenotyping prior to referral for WES/WGS as well as of combining NGS with functional mRNA studies to achieve a molecular diagnosis for patients with predicted splice-site mutations in JS-associated genes. It also emphasizes the need for functional reassessment of VUS when genomic data are expected to guide reproductive decision-making within affected families.

Background

Joubert syndrome (JS) is a rare, genetically heterogeneous neurodevelopmental disorder with a prevalence of 1:70,000–1:100,000 [1]. It belongs to ciliopathies, a group of conditions caused by defects in the structure or function of the primary cilium. JS, first described by Marie Joubert in 1969, is defined by a distinct midbrain-hindbrain malformation visible on neuroimaging as the pathognomonic “molar tooth sign” (MTS) [2]. This hallmark consists of cerebellar vermis hypoplasia or aplasia, thickened and maloriented superior cerebellar peduncles, and a profoundly deep interpeduncular fossa. The clinical diagnosis is based on core neurological signs that include hypotonia, oculomotor apraxia, respiratory disturbance, ataxia, and/or cognitive impairment [3, 4]. Reflecting the critical role of cilia in diverse tissues, the phenotypic spectrum of JS (often termed JSRD) includes multiorgan involvement beyond the central nervous system [4]. Retinal dystrophy, manifesting as an early-onset severe form or as Leber congenital amaurosis, and kidney disease (cystic dysplasia and nephronophthisis), a leading cause of pediatric renal failure, are among the most common and severe features. Additional manifestations may include hepatic fibrosis, skeletal anomalies (such as polydactyly or scoliosis), endocrine abnormalities [4, 5], as well as facial gestalt: frontal bossing, arched eyebrows, deep-set eyes, hypertelorism, ptosis, broad nasal bridge, anteverted nares with a short columella and low-set ears [6].

Genetically, JS exhibits significant genetic heterogeneity, with over 40 genes implicated, most of which follow an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern. These genes encode proteins localized to the primary cilium, its basal body, or associated centrosomes, disrupting key signaling pathways. Among these, mutations in the CEP290 gene represent a major genetic cause, particularly in patients with a severe cerebello-oculo-renal phenotype consistent with Joubert syndrome 5 (OMIM: 610188) [7]. The CEP290 gene encodes a large, structurally complex protein that functions as a critical gatekeeper at the ciliary transition zone. The vast majority of pathogenic CEP290 variants are loss-of-function mutations, such as nonsense or frameshift changes, which lead to truncated, non-functional proteins.

This study elucidates the molecular etiology of JS in two siblings by identifying and functionally characterizing a novel homozygous non-canonical splice-site mutation in CEP290 (c.1910-15_1910-11delinsTTACA). RNA-sequencing and cDNA analysis confirmed the mutation’s deleterious impact on pre-mRNA splicing. This finding highlights the need to integrate RNA-level studies with WES/WGS to achieve definitive molecular diagnosis, especially for non-canonical splice-site variants in JS-associated genes.

Materials and methods

Clinical data collection, blood and skin sampling, DNA and RNA extraction

Clinical and family history data were obtained during a consultation with a clinical geneticist. This involved reviewing previous medical records, brain MRI DICOM files, and photographs provided by the proband’s parents. The collected documentation included detailed medical histories, results of physical examinations by other specialists, and reports from instrumental and laboratory tests.

Blood samples and a skin biopsy for DNA and RNA extraction were obtained after written informed consent was provided. Consent for anonymous inclusion in the research was obtained from the family members; for minors, consent was given through their guardians. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Ethics Committee of the Research Centre for Medical Genetics (Moscow, Russia).

Peripheral blood samples were collected in EDTA-containing tubes. Genomic DNA was extracted from whole blood using the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Fibroblast cell lines were established from skin biopsies taken from the family members (V.1, V.2, V.3, IV.1, IV.2) (Fig. 1A). Biopsy fragments were incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO₂ until larger colonies formed. The cells were cultured in DMEM (PanEco, Russia) supplemented with 10% FBS (Biowest, France), penicillin/streptomycin, and amphotericin B. Fibroblasts were then trypsinized, plated on dishes for further expansion, and total RNA was extracted using QIAzol Lysis Reagent (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Short-read whole genome sequencing (sr-WGS)

DNA of the index patient (Fig. 1A) was extracted from peripheral blood on a QIAsymphony instrument (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Whole-genome library preparation was done according to the MGIEasy PCR-Free DNA Library Prep Set kit protocol (MGI, China/USA). Sequencing was performed on a DNBSEQ-T7 (MGI, China/USA) with 150-bp read length.

Reanalysis of WGS data and variant verification

Sequencing data reanalysis was performed using a pipeline that included alignment of reads to the reference genome (hg38), variant calling, and quality-based filtration. Sequence data were processed with the in-house bioinformatic pipeline. All quality-filtered variants were annotated by HGVS nomenclature with the application of several bioinformatics tools such as REVEL, SIFT, PolyPhen-2, and SpliceAI. Allele frequencies of the identified variants were assessed using the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD v3.1.2). To evaluate the clinical relevance of the identified variants, we utilized multiple public databases such as OMIM, ClinVar, LOVD, and other specialized databases (where available), as well as published case reports and literature. Variants were classified according to the guidelines of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG), the Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP), the ClinGen and the Association for Clinical Genomic Science (ACGS) [8–10]. A candidate variant in CEP290 identified through WGS was further validated by Sanger sequencing (Genetic Analyzer 3500, Applied Biosystems, Life Technologies, Darmstadt, Germany). Both primers (forward [F]: 5’-TAGGACTCTAGTGGCAC-3’, reverse [R]: 5’-GGTGTCAACCCAATGGA-3’) targeted intron 19.

Total mRNA sequencing and library preparation

Total mRNA sequencing was performed on RNA derived from the proband’s cultured fibroblasts. Total RNA concentration was measured spectrophotometrically using a Nano-500 instrument (Allsheng, China). RNA integrity was assessed with a TapeStation system (Agilent), yielding an RNA Integrity Number (RIN) of 7.2. A total of 10.4 µg of this RNA was used for the isolation of poly(A) + RNA by VAHTS® mRNA Capture Beads (Vazyme). A sequencing library was prepared from the purified mRNA using the VAHTS® Universal V8 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Vazyme), with fragmentation carried out at 85 °C for 6 min. cDNA libraries were subjected to paired-end sequencing on SURFSeq5000 (GeneMind, China) with 150-bp read length.

Quality assessment and adapter trimming of the raw sequencing reads were performed using Fastp (v0.23.2) with a minimum quality threshold of Q20 and minimal length of 75 bp. Adapter sequences and low-quality bases were automatically detected and removed. Filtered reads were aligned to the human reference genome GRCh38.p14 with annotation GENCODE v39 using STAR (2.7.11b) in 2-pass mapping mode and with retention of only uniquely-mapping reads. Post-alignment, coordinate-sorted BAM files were indexed using Samtools v1.17. Additionally, samtools commands idxstats and stats were used to obtain alignment statistics, including mapped/unmapped read counts and coverage metrics. Aligned reads were visualized using the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV, v2.17.2). For each sample, coordinate-sorted BAM files with their corresponding index files (.bai) and splice junction tracks in BED format were generated from STAR outputs. These files were compressed with bgzip and indexed with tabix for efficient access. Gene annotation files were loaded into IGV to validate alignment quality and splice junction detection. The entire bioinformatics pipeline was implemented using Snakemake.

Target cDNA analysis

Total RNA was isolated from fibroblasts of all available family members using QIAzol Lysis Reagent (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). RNA was then reverse transcribed using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). The resulting cDNA was used as a template for subsequent PCR with 5X ScreenMix (Evrogen, Russia). Primers targeting exon 18 (F: 5’-CCACTGAGGACCTGAACCTAACT-3’) and exon 22 (R: 5’-TACTGGCACTAGATGGTGCTATCC-3’) of CEP290 were used, yielding PCR products of 609 bp (wild-type), 466 bp (exon 20 skipped), and 667 bp (intron 19 partially retained). PCR products were analyzed on a 7% polyacrylamide gel (PAAG) (Fig. 3A) and processed with the SG GM kit (Raissol Bio) for NGS library preparation; the fragmentation time was 16 min, and the number of PCR cycles was 5. The resulting NGS libraries were paired-end sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq platform at 100,000× coverage with 150-bp read length (Illumina, Inc., California, United States). The raw sequencing data were processed for downstream analysis using an in-house bioinformatics tool, NGS-data software (RCMG, Russia).

Results

Clinical presentation and diagnosis

The index patient (V.2) is an 11-year-old boy, the second child of third cousins of Chechen origin (Fig. 1A). Prenatal ultrasound findings were apparently normal throughout the pregnancy. He was born at 40 weeks’ gestation with a birth weight of 3500 g (50th -75th percentile) and a length of 52 cm (75th -95th percentile). No gross facial dysmorphism or gross abnormalities were observed. He did not require intensive care and was discharged from the hospital on the 3rd day after birth with a diagnosis of mid-shaft hypospadias.

At the age of 2 months, his mother noted that the child did not make eye contact or smile in response to stimuli. An ophthalmological examination revealed degeneration of the macula and posterior pole, as well as optic disc atrophy. By 3 months of age, polycystic kidney disease was identified. A subsequent neurological examination at 4 months demonstrated a lack of visual fixation, diminished pupillary light reflex, eye movements indicating oculomotor apraxia, and seesaw nystagmus. The patient was unable to hold his head up and presented with dystonic muscle tone. Later, he was diagnosed with cerebral palsy (tetraparesis), severe intellectual disability, and complete optic nerve atrophy.

His motor development was severely delayed: he gained head control after 1 year of age, began sitting independently at 2 years, and has not achieved independent walking by 11. His language development was also profoundly delayed; while babbling was present, he never developed phrase speech. He expressed needs through crying and vocalizations, with an absence of speech or pointing gestures. No epileptic seizures have ever been reported, and his hearing was within the normal range. His facial appearance included prominent foreahead, prognathism, deep set eyes, hypertelorism, ptosis, large, posteriorly rotated ears, long eyelashes, a broad nasal tip and anteverted nares with a short columella (Fig. 1B). Brain MRI (axial view) revealed a molar tooth sign (MTS) characterized by cerebellar vermis hypoplasia, thick and maloriented superior cerebellar peduncles, and abnormally deep interpeduncular fossa. The scan also showed open, moderate hydrocephalus and moderate cerebral cortex atrophy (Fig. 1C, upper).

The patient had a similarly affected 5-year-old sister (V.3) (Fig. 1A, B and C). She also presented with blindness, an inability to walk, intellectual disability, and the MTS with cerebral cortex atrophy on brain MRI (Fig. 1C, lower).

At the age of 10, the proband underwent sr-WGS on the recommendation of a neurologist. His referral diagnosis submitted to the laboratory included cerebral palsy, severe motor delay, an unspecified brain malformation, and optic atrophy; the Cockayne syndrome was considered in the differential diagnosis. According to the initial laboratory report, two missense variants of uncertain significance were identified in the MACF1 (OMIM *608271) and the CHD4 (OMIM *603277) genes. Subsequent segregation analysis revealed that both variants were inherited from the mother (IV.2) and were also present in the healthy sister (V.1), leading to their reclassification as likely benign. Thus, no causative variants were reported initially at that time. One year later, during a consultation with clinical geneticist, a suspicion of Joubert syndrome with an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern arose, prompting a reanalysis of the raw sr-WGS data.

WGS raw data reanalysis and Sanger sequencing validation

WGS raw data of the index patient, resulted in a genome-wide coverage of approximately 33-fold. The number of total identified variants – 5,109,294, the percentage of fragments with less than 10x coverage — 4%. The only variant of clinical significance was identified: a novel homozygous intronic variant, c.1910-15_1910-11delinsTTACA (chr12:88114573ACAGT> TGTAA [GRCh38]), in intron 19 of CEP290 (NM_025114.4) (Fig. 2B). It was initially classified as VUS according to ACMG/AMP, ClinGen and ACGS guidelines [8–10]. The variant has not been reported as disease-associated, nor is it annotated in the general population (gnomAD v3.1.2 and gnomAD v4.1.0). SpliceAI predicted the generation of a novel acceptor splice site by the variant (Acceptor Loss = 0.80 Δ score, -11 bp; Donor Loss = 0.12 Δ score, -153 bp; Acceptor Gain = 0.35 Δ score, -2 bp). Additionally, WGS data have revealed a loss of heterozygosity (LOH) region on chromosome 12 with approximate boundaries chr12:80,444,130 − 113,101,600 and a size of 32 Mb, where gene CEP290 is located (Fig. 2C and D).

Sanger sequencing of DNA derived from peripheral blood (V.1, V.2, V.3, IV.1, IV.2) confirmed the homozygous state of the variant in index patient (V.2) and his affected sister (V.3), parents (IV.1, IV.2) were proved to be heterozygous carriers of the identified variant and healthy sister (V.1) did not carry this variant (Fig. 2A).

RNA analysis

Targeted NGS of cDNA PCR amplicons and mRNA‑seq both demonstrated marked aberrant splicing associated with the CEP290 variant c.1910-15_1910-11delinsTTACA (Fig. 3A-C). In the homozygous proband (V.2, M/M), canonical exon 19–20 junctions were absent. The predominant aberrant event corresponded to exon 20 skipping (~ 84% of aberrant splice junction reads), presumably resulting in a frameshift and premature stop codon formation (p.Leu637Cysfs*12). A smaller proportion of reads (~ 16%) supported retention of 58 bp of intron 19 with inclusion of an aberrant exon 20. This event was also associated with a homozygous substitution chr12:88114573-ACTGTGTA> TTACAGTA (GRCh38) (Fig. 3D), likely resulting in an additional frameshift and premature stop-codon formation (p.Leu637Serfs*2). No canonical mRNA isoform was detected in the index patient (V.2). Both the predicted severe truncation and the likely degradation of aberrant transcripts through nonsense-mediated decay (NMD) are consistent with loss of CEP290 function. These functional data enabled reclassification of the c.1910-15_1910-11delinsTTACA variant from VUS to likely-pathogenic according to ACMG/AMP, ClinGen and ACGS guidelines (PVS1 (RNA)+PM3) [8–10].

In the heterozygous father (WT/M), both normal and aberrant splice junctions were detected. Aberrant splicing was represented predominantly by exon 20 skipping (~ 25% of relevant junction reads) and, less frequently, by intron 19 retention (~ 4%). The predominance of canonical splice junctions in the heterozygous state may reflect stability of the wild-type allele and/or NMD of aberrant transcripts.

Discussion

The occurrence of multiple affected siblings with a similar complex phenotype in a potentially consanguineous family strongly suggests an autosomal recessive mode of inheritance. In these situations, homozygous variants become key targets for analysis. However, interpretation may remain challenging when the detected variants are initially categorized as variants of uncertain significance (VUS). In our study, sr-WGS revealed a novel homozygous intronic variant, c.1910-15_1910-11delinsTTACA, in CEP290 in the proband and his similarly affected sister. Given that this variant was predicted to affect splicing, an mRNA splicing assay was undertaken to characterize its impact on CEP290 transcripts by examining cDNA derived from cultured fibroblasts (TPM = 10.46), since the gene is expressed at very low levels in whole blood [11]. It was found that c.1910-15_1910-11delinsTTACA in a homozygous state causes completely aberrant splicing of CEP290 with predominant 20 exon skipping (143 bp) and the partial retention of intron 19 (58 bp). As exon 20 of the canonical CEP290 transcript is present in nearly all long protein-coding isoforms (Fig. 4), skipping this exon is consistent with loss of CEP290 function. Based on this functional study, the variant c.1910-15_1910-11delinsTTACA was reclassified from VUS to likely pathogenic in accordance with ACMG/AMP, ClinGen and ACGS guidelines (PVS1 (RNA)+PM3) [8–10].

Earlier studies demonstrate that the most frequent genetic cause of Leber congenital amaurosis (LCA), a mutation (c.2991 + 1655 A > G) in intron 26 of CEP290, is responsible for up to 21% of all LCA cases [13]. It creates a strong cryptic splice‑donor site and results in insertion of a 128‑bp pseudoexon between exons 26 and 27, introducing a premature stop codon immediately downstream of exon 26. Notably, a small proportion of wild-type transcript remained detectable in affected individuals. The aberrant splicing induced by this mutation is more pronounced in human photoreceptors than in other cell types, offering a plausible explanation for why the phenotype is confined to the retina [13]. While a definitive genotype–phenotype correlation remains to be established for CEP290-associated ciliopathies, residual CEP290 function is thought to underlie milder disease phenotypes [14].

While our study employed WGS to achieve unbiased detection of copy number variants (CNVs), deep intronic variants, and exonic variants, we recognize that whole‑exome sequencing (WES) can also be a cost‑effective and more accessible option for identifying cryptic splicing variants located within 200 bp of exon–intron boundaries [15]. For laboratories with limited access to WGS, careful WES data reanalysis focusing on proximal intronic regions remains a valuable tool to identify cryptic splicing variants in Joubert syndrome.

Our findings also demonstrate that thorough phenotyping is crucial to guide WES/WGS analysis: in the absence of pointing to Joubert syndrome in the proband, the causative variant remained undetected during the initial genome analysis. The proband was initially assessed by a neurologist rather than a clinical geneticist, and unfortunately there was no clinical suspicion for Joubert syndrome. This example illustrates how significant genomic findings can be missed in the absence of a specific diagnostic hypothesis.

The study also emphasizes the importance of pairing NGS with functional mRNA studies to obtain a molecular diagnosis for patients harboring predicted splice-site mutations in JS-associated genes, as deep intronic variants may represent the underlying cause.

Finally, reclassifying variants of uncertain significance to likely pathogenic through functional studies enables appropriate reproductive counseling and family planning, including options such as IVF with preimplantation genetic testing for monogenic disorders (PGT-M) or prenatal fetal diagnostics.
