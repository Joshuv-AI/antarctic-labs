 ABSTRACT

Two large-scale 316L stainless steel cylindrical shells were fabricated by wire-arc directed energy
deposition (WA-DED). Residual stresses were measured by neutron diffraction, and the microstructure
and mechanical properties of the printed material were characterized. Longitudinal and transverse residual
stresses were compressive at the inner diameter, near-neutral at mid-wall, and tensile at the outer
diameter; radial (through-thickness) residual stresses were relatively low in magnitude, and peak tensile
residual stresses occurred at the outer-diameter surface along the longitudinal direction. The printed 316L
SS exhibited a heterogeneous microstructure with finely dispersed δ-ferrite, and demonstrated high yield
and tensile strengths with large elongation and ductile fracture at ambient temperature and at 250 °C.
These results indicate that the outer-diameter surface and near-surface regions—where tensile residual
stress is highest—are the most vulnerable to residual-stress–sensitive failure modes, such as
stress-corrosion cracking and fatigue.

 1. INTRODUCTION

Wire arc directed energy deposition (WA-DED), also known as wire arc additive manufacturing
(WAAM), is an additive manufacturing (AM) technology capable of producing large scale structures with
low capital investment, open architecture control, ambient atmosphere operation, and high deposition
rates [1, 2]. ]. With recent advances in metal AM, manufacturing larger components using WA-DED has
become increasingly viable and offers significant potential benefits [2, 3]. The feasibility of large scale
WA-DED has been demonstrated for low carbon steels, stainless steels, and nickel alloy, with printed
components weighing from hundreds to thousands of pounds [4, 5].

Pass-by-pass and layer by layer deposition using an electric arc imposes complex thermal cycles on the
solidified weld metal and the substrate. Heat dissipation produces a non-isothermal, in situ heat treatment
effect on material below and adjacent to the deposited beads, the extent of which depends on heat input,
thermal history, and material thermophysical properties. Cyclic thermal expansion and contraction
generate residual stresses in both the deposit and the substrate. High residual stresses—particularly
tensile—can promote defects (e.g., distortion, delamination) and degrade ductility, fatigue life, and
resistance to stress corrosion cracking.

Neutron diffraction residual stress measurements were employed can be used to quantify the internal
residual stress state in large WA-DED components. Neutron diffraction is nondestructive and offers deep
penetration in steels, enabling bulk assessment of lattice strains and, by extension, residual stresses in
thick sections. Lattice spacings measured along selected directions were converted to elastic strains and
then to stresses using appropriate diffraction elastic constants and a stress free reference. Through
thickness and in plane mappings were performed to resolve the longitudinal, transverse, and radial
components, providing spatially resolved insight into surface, near surface, and mid wall stress
distributions. These measurements underpin integrity assessment and guide process optimization and post
processing strategies aimed at mitigating tensile surface stresses. However, practical constraints on
beamline sample envelopes, positioning, and attenuation generally limit routine measurements to
relatively small objects (e.g., coupons or subcomponents), with gauge volumes defined on the millimeter
scale. As a result, applying neutron diffraction to full, large‑scale structures is uncommon and logistically
challenging.

In this work, through-thickness and in-plane mappings were performed to resolve the longitudinal,
transverse, and radial stress components in large WA-DED shells, providing spatially resolved insight
into surface, near-surface, and mid-wall stress distributions. Demonstrating such measurements on large

structures underscores the significance of this study and informs process optimization and post-processing
strategies aimed at mitigating tensile surface stresses.

3.1 MICROSTRUCTURE CHARACTERIZATION

3.1.1 Metallographic Specimen Preparation

Two metallographic specimens (1 in × 0.25 in × wall thickness) perpendicular to each other were
extracted in the middle of the WAAM printed 316L SS thin wall. One specimen length was along the wall
length direction (L direction, specimen ID: 316L-L) and the other one length was along the wall height
direction (H direction, specimen ID: 316L-H), and the schematic of the metallographic specimen
locations and orientations are shown in Figure 4.

 Figure 4. Metallographic specimen orientations.

The two metallographic specimens were mounted with conductive bakelite. The polishing surface of
316L-L specimen is the length (6.5 in.) and thickness (0.25 in) plane, and that of the 316L-H specimen is
the height (6.25 in) and thickness (0.25 in) plane. After mounting, mechanically ground and polished, and
polished with 0.05 μm colloidal silica for SEM / electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) observation.

3.1.2 Scanning Electron Microscopy

A TESCAN MIRA3 SEM was employed to perform microstructure analysis. The SEM is equipped with
an advanced Oxford Symmetry EBSD system (detector resolution 1,244 × 1,024 pixels, allowing for an
EBSD indexing rate of up to ~200 Hz in full-frame mode, and ~3.5 kHz in 8 × 8 binning mode) and an
Oxford energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) detector. In addition, the SEM has a large chamber,
allowing large (up to 4 in.) specimens to be loaded or additional equipment to be installed, like the
Kammrath & Weiss in situ tensile frame, heater, or nano-indenter. The TESCAN MIRA3 SEM is shown
in Figure 5.

3.1.3 Microstructure Characterization

During the SEM observation, no macro voids (in fraction of millimeter size) were observed in either
metallographic specimen. However, very fine, scattered porosities/inclusions of micron and submicron
sizes were present in the structure. As a rule, porosities appeared in austenite and rarely at the ferrite-
austenite boundary. With few exceptions, ferrite was free of porosity. The micro-porosities/inclusions
displayed under secondary electron (SE) and backscatter electron (BSE) are shown in Figure 6.

cylindrical shell could be measured with tilt.

4.3 RESIDUAL STRESS DISTRIBUTION

Residual stress distribution with measuring errors of neutron diffraction for the 36 in diameter shell are
shown in Figure 18. Here, the normal stress is along the shell thickness direction, the transverse stress is
along the shell circumference direction, and the longitudinal stress is along the shell height direction. The
negative value represents compressive residual stress, and the positive number indicates tensile residual
stress.

The normal stresses varied from -150 to 150 MPa, without too much variation with the building height.
The transverse stresses were high (~350 MPa) close to the shell base, and they decreased as the
measurement moved toward the center of the shell. Starting about 80 mm (3.15 in.) away from the first
measurement, the transverse stresses dropped to between -60 and 80 MPa. The longitudinal stresses were
low at locations close to the shell base and continued to increase when the measurement moved toward
the center. Starting at about 70 mm (2.76 in.) away from the first measurement, the longitudinal stresses
were 150–400 MPa, with an average value of approximately 280 MPa.

 Figure 18. Residual stress distributions of the 36 in diameter shell.

Residual stress distribution with measuring errors for the 21.65 in diameter shell are shown in Figure 19.
Different residual stress patterns are shown at different locations along the thickness direction.
Longitudinal and transverse residual stresses were mostly compressive, neutral, and tensile at most spots

 5. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

Two large‑scale 316L SS cylindrical shells were manufactured by WA‑DED with the following
dimensions: diameter 36 in × height 12 in with 0.75 in wall thickness, and diameter 21.65 in × height
15.75 in with 0.75 in wall thickness. A single‑pass thin wall (6.5 × 6.0 × 0.3 in) was also produced. No
macroscopic defects (e.g., cracks, voids) or dimensional errors (e.g., distortion, misalignment) were
observed in any printed unit.

SEM revealed only micron‑ and submicron‑scale porosity/inclusions in the WA‑DED 316L SS, with no
macro-porosity/inclusions. SEM/EBSD showed a complex microstructure. In the length–thickness plane
(normal to the build direction), large columnar dendritic grains formed a herringbone solidification
pattern converging toward the weld centerline. In the thickness–height plane (normal to the torch
travel/length direction), large, irregularly shaped grains with curled and twisted grain boundaries
indicated grain growth out of the observed plane. Finely dispersed δ‑ferrite was present within the
austenitic matrix.

The WA‑DED thin wall exhibited high yield and tensile strengths, with large elongation and ductile
fracture in tensile tests at ambient temperature and at 250 °C. At 21 °C, both yield and tensile strengths
met or exceeded the minimum strength requirements for pressure vessels and general applications
specified in ASTM A240/A240M‑20. Strengths measured along the length and height directions were
similar.

Neutron diffraction residual‑stress measurements were performed on the two cylindrical shells at the
VULCAN beamline (ORNL). In general, residual stresses were compressive at the inner diameter,
near‑neutral at mid‑wall, and tensile at the outer diameter, in both longitudinal and transverse directions.
Radial (through‑thickness) residual stresses were relatively low in magnitude. Peak tensile residual
stresses occurred at the outer‑diameter surface along the longitudinal direction. From the base of the
build, approximately 1 in of deposited height was required to reach a steady‑state residual‑stress
condition.

Taken together, the microstructural integrity, mechanical performance, and measured residual‑stress
distributions indicate that the outer‑diameter surface and near‑surface regions—where tensile residual
stress is highest—are the most vulnerable locations for residual‑stress–sensitive failure modes. In
particular, stress‑corrosion cracking (SCC) and fatigue are more likely to initiate and propagate in areas
with high tensile residual stress than in regions with lower tensile or compressive residual stress.
Conversely, the compressive and near‑neutral stress zones (e.g., inner diameter and mid‑wall) are less
prone to such failures. These findings can guide inspection focus and post‑processing or mitigation
strategies aimed at reducing surface tensile residual stress.
