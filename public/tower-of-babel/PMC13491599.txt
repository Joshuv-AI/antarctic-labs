Epidemiology and antimicrobial resistance analysis of staphylococcus aureus isolated from women, children and neonates in a maternal and child specialty hospital in Southeast China
Authors: Sijia Cao, Ling Chen, Jiali Cao, Wenrong Wu, Xingxiang Zheng, Jing Zeng, Xiaoli Chen, Jiayin Wu, Huiming Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStaphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) is a common bacterium posing a significant global health threat, with methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) epidemiology varying widely by region, time period, and population. Moreover, antibiotic management strategies differ for vulnerable groups including neonates, children, and pregnant women. This study investigates the clinical distribution and antimicrobial resistance profiles of S. aureus isolates from a maternal-child specialty hospital, with a particular focus on MRSA epidemiology.MethodsHerein, we retrospectively enrolled all non-duplicate S. aureus isolates recovered in our hospital from October 2020 to August 2024. All isolates were divided into three groups: neonates, children, and women, followed by an analysis of their clinical distributions. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was conducted via broth microdilution or disk diffusion methods following Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) criteria, with data analyzed using WHONET 5.6. The chi-square test, Fisher’s exact test, Monte Carlo method, and multivariate logistic regression were adopted for statistical analysis using R 4.2.2.ResultsA total of 973 S. aureus isolates were studied, of which 65.6% were from inpatients and 28.4% from the neonatal unit. Sputum (37.3%, 363/973) and pus (24.2%, 235/973) were the predominant specimens, with relatively high proportions of isolates from the genital tract (10.3%) and intrauterine sites (6.2%). Of 732 isolates subjected to susceptibility testing, the overall MRSA detection rate was 19.7% (144/732). The risk of MRSA colonization or infection was higher in children (OR = 2.774, P = 0.0049) but lower in the obstetric population (OR = 0.0122, P = 0.0491). Significant differences in susceptibility rates to penicillin, erythromycin and clindamycin were observed among the three subgroups.ConclusionsS. aureus isolates were predominantly obtained from neonates and lactating mothers, mainly from the lower respiratory tract and skin/soft tissue sites. The high proportion of genital tract and intrauterine colonization/infection may raise possible concerns regarding vertical mother-to-child S. aureus transmission. MRSA demonstrated a moderate overall prevalence, with children carrying a higher risk of MRSA infection or colonization. Collectively, these data underscore the need for sustained MRSA surveillance and age-specific, differentiated prevention strategies for these vulnerable groups.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13733-5.Keywords: Staphylococcus aureus, Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, Infectious diseases, Epidemiological analysis, Antimicrobial resistance

Introduction

Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) colonizes the skin and upper respiratory tract in approximately 30% of the general population [1]. As an opportunistic pathogen of significant concern, it can cause localized skin and soft tissue infections, as well as life-threatening invasive infections such as pneumonia, meningitis, osteomyelitis, and sepsis, with a mortality rate ranging from 10% to 30% [2–5]. Furthermore, S. aureus is the second most common pathogen leading to hospital-acquired infections, and represents the primary causative agent of surgical site infection and ventilator-associated pneumonia [6].

Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) evolves from methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA) by acquiring the staphylococcal cassette chromosome mec (SCCmec) element, or through genetic mutations. This frequently reduces the efficacy of most semi-synthetic penicillins, conferring resistance to the entire β-lactam antibiotic family, with the exception of ceftaroline [7, 8]. Additionally, MRSA is generally heterogeneous and multidrug-resistant, exhibiting increased resistance to aminoglycosides, tetracyclines, and lincosamides compared with MSSA [9]. Since the 1990s, MRSA infections have become widespread in community settings and are now a major multidrug-resistant pathogen under clinical surveillance [10–12].

MRSA prevalence varies significantly among East Asian countries and regions, with rates of 41.78% in Thailand and 66.90% in South Korea [13]. According to the 2024 report issued by the China Antimicrobial Surveillance Network (CHINET), the national overall prevalence of MRSA in China was 28.4%. This rate was higher than that in Sichuan (26.85%) and Shanxi (17.9%), but lower than that in Macau (36.3%) and Tianjin (34.83%) [14–18]. These discrepancies may reflect differences in the molecular typing of prevalent S. aureus strains across time periods and geographic regions. Nevertheless, large-scale epidemiological data from the southeastern coastal regions of China remain limited. Regional variation may exist, potentially influenced by climatic factors.

In neonates and children with undeveloped immune systems, as well as in women who may transmit S. aureus vertically during childbirth, the epidemiological and biological characteristics of S. aureus may differ from those observed in other populations. In adults, the incidence of healthcare-associated MRSA invasive and bloodstream S. aureus infections has declined in recent years. In contrast, community-associated MRSA invasive infections in children have increased, rising from 1.1 cases per 100,000 population in 2005 to 1.7 cases per 100,000 in 2010 [19–21]. In China, the MRSA detection rate among children was reported to be 29.8% in 2024, approaching the level observed in adults [22]. Given that antibiotic use strategies also differ in these populations, maternal and child health hospitals urgently need to conduct targeted investigations to support the rational clinical use of antibiotics [14].

Previous studies have largely focused on general populations or single demographic groups, whereas dedicated research on women, children, and neonates remains limited. To fill this research gap, this study retrospectively analyzed the clinical distribution and antibiotic susceptibility of S. aureus strains isolated from these key populations in Southeast China from October 2020 to August 2024, with a particular focus on MRSA epidemiological trends.

Materials and methods

Strains

This study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of Women and Children’s Hospital of Xiamen University (KY-2024-082-K01), which waived the requirement for informed consent. Clinical data for a total of 973 S. aureus isolates collected between October 2020 and August 2024 were reviewed, and antimicrobial susceptibility data for 732 isolates were retrospectively analyzed. Duplicated isolates from the same patient and anatomical site were excluded. All strains subjected to antimicrobial susceptibility testing were considered clinically relevant, with targeted treatment guided by susceptibility results. The study design is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Flow chart illustrating the study design for this work

Bacterial culture and identification

According to the National Clinical Laboratory Procedures (4th Edition), clinical specimens were inoculated onto Columbia blood agar plates and incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ for 24 to 48 h. A single suspected colony was identified using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS; SKYRay MicroTyper MS, Hangzhou, China; database: EX-Accuspec v2.0). E. coli ATCC 25922 was used as the positive quality control strain. The assay was considered valid when the positive control score exceeded 2.0 and the negative control score was below 1.5.

Antimicrobial susceptibility tests

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using the disk diffusion method on Mueller–Hinton agar plates (Oxoid, UK) or the broth microdilution method with the PHOENIX 100 Fully Automated Bacterial Identification and Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing System (BD, USA), following the manufacturers’ instructions. Constitutive or inducible resistance to clindamycin was assessed using the D-test. MRSA was identified based on cefoxitin/oxacillin susceptibility testing. Results were interpreted according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) criteria. Nitrofurantoin susceptibility was assessed only for urinary isolates, in accordance with CLSI criteria. S. aureus ATCC 25923 and ATCC 29213 were used as quality control strains.

Data abstraction and statistical analysis

Antimicrobial susceptibility results were analyzed using WHONET 5.6. Demographic and clinical data were imported into standard Excel spreadsheets and independently reviewed by two investigators. All proportions were calculated as (case number of each category/total cases) * 100 and reported to one decimal place. Data visualization was performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0. Normally distributed data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (x ± s), while categorical data were expressed as counts and percentages (n/%).

All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.2.2. The chi-square test was used to compare S. aureus infection or colonization rates between neonates aged 7 to 28 days and those aged ≤ 7 days. MRSA detection rates were calculated as (number of MRSA isolates/total number of S. aureus isolates in each group) * 100. Differences in antimicrobial susceptibility and MRSA detection rates among women, children, and neonates were assessed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, with Bonferroni correction applied for pairwise comparisons. MRSA prevalence across subgroups and time periods was analyzed using the Fisher’s exact test or the Monte Carlo method. Clinical characteristics between MRSA and MSSA groups were compared using the chi-square test or Monte Carlo method. Logistic regression was used to identify risk factors for MRSA infection or colonization. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Comparison of antibiotic susceptibility patterns of S. aureus isolates among women, children, and neonates

Following the analysis of the clinical distribution characteristics of S. aureus isolates, we compared antimicrobial susceptibility patterns across the distinct populations of women, children, and neonates to identify population-specific differences. Antibiotics evaluated included penicillin, oxacillin, cefoxitin, gentamicin, tetracycline, chloramphenicol, erythromycin, clindamycin, levofloxacin, ciprofloxacin, and co-trimoxazole.

As shown in Table 2, statistically significant differences were observed in the susceptibility rates of three antibiotics: penicillin (P = 0.0040), with women exhibiting a higher rate (20.9%) than neonates (10.0%; P = 0.0086); erythromycin (P = 0.0033), with neonates showing a higher rate (70.0%) than children (53.8%; P = 0.0032); and clindamycin (P = 0.0011), with neonates exhibiting a higher rate (95.0%) than children (87.1%; P = 0.0319) and women (83.6%; P = 0.0011). No significant differences were observed in oxacillin or cefoxitin susceptibility among the three subgroups, nor in their MRSA detection rates (Supplementary Table 9).

From 2020 to 2024, the overall MRSA detection rate among women, children and neonates remained stable at approximately 19.7% (144/732). Significant differences among groups were observed from October 2023 to March 2024 (P = 0.0023; Supplementary Table 10). Pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction revealed that the MRSA detection rate in women (4.3%) was lower than that in children (29.6%; P = 0.009) and neonates (25.0%; P = 0.05). Women showed a stable trend, while neonatal rates fluctuated, most markedly from a peak of 36.4% to a trough of 5.9%.

Discussion

This study was the first to focus on women, children and neonates in southeast coastal China, collecting 973 S. aureus isolates over four consecutive years (2020–2024). This extensive regional dataset has enriched our understanding of S. aureus/MRSA infection and colonization dynamics in these vulnerable groups and we further identified population-specific differences in antimicrobial susceptibility. Overall MRSA prevalence remained at 19.7%. Although no significant differences were observed in MRSA rates across subgroups, we found that children were at a higher risk and obstetric patients at a lower risk for MRSA colonization or infection, highlighting the need for targeted prevention, monitoring, and antibiotic stewardship strategies in children.

Our data indicated that the majority of 973 S. aureus isolates were recovered from the lower respiratory tract (38.8%) and skin and soft tissues (38.2%), followed by the reproductive tract (10.3%) and intrauterine samples (6.2%), possibly suggesting a potential risk for mother-to-child transmission. Thus, surveillance of highly virulent, multidrug-resistant S. aureus strains in mothers and strategies to block vertical transmission are urgently required. Colonization could not be fully distinguished from true infection, which must be considered when interpreting these findings; reported infection rates, particularly lower respiratory tract infections (> 75.0% in neonates), may be overestimated.

We showed that the enrolled S. aureus isolates were widely distributed across multiple sites in the body. S. aureus colonization is a major risk factor for invasive infections, with local infections often serving as portals for dissemination to deep tissues and the bloodstream [23, 24]. Notably, the neonatal department had the highest isolation rate (28.4%) among all departments, which deserved close clinical attention due to the immature immune system of infants. Preterm infants with prolonged hospital stay, delicate skin, comorbidities, repeated invasive procedures, and indwelling devices faced an increased risk of S. aureus exposure and infection [25]. Among neonates with S. aureus infection or colonization, 82.2% (148/180) were aged 7 to 28 days. Meanwhile, the S. aureus infection or colonization rate was higher in neonates aged 7 to 28 days than in those aged ≤ 7 days (P < 0.0001; Supplementary Table 12). Consistent with previous findings reported by Miselli et al., this finding suggested that S. aureus was not a primary pathogen in early-onset neonatal infections, which were mainly caused by group B Streptococcus and E. coli [26].

In children, lower respiratory tract (56.0%) and female genital tract (19.1%) infections or colonization predominated. A study in Zhejiang, China that cultured vaginal secretions from 2,287 girls with vulvovaginitis identified S. aureus with a 9.7% isolation rate, ranking fourth among detected pathogens [27]. These findings, combined with our data, underscore the critical role of S. aureus in pediatric genitourinary infections and justify further investigation into its pathogenic mechanisms to guide future studies.

The breast surgery department had the third highest S. aureus isolation rate (18.8%), with pus samples from women accounting for 47.3%, suggesting lactating women with mastitis were susceptible to S. aureus infections. Lactational mastitis is an inflammatory mammary gland condition caused by milk stasis from diverse etiologies, with or without bacterial infection. Consistent with the findings of Angeliki Angelopoulou et al., S. aureus was the primary pathogen in acute/subacute mastitis, while Corynebacterium predominated in granulomatous mastitis [28]. S. aureus infection in lactating women could potentially be transmitted to neonates via breast milk, highlighting the need to refine breastfeeding guidance for mastitis patients. In addition, Genital tract and intrauterine colonization or infections in women accounted for 32.8% of all infection types, suggesting a substantial risk of mother-to-child transmission. Apart from the above infections, urinary tract infections accounted for 3.5% of cases overall. The incidence of asymptomatic bacteriuria during pregnancy (1.9% to 9.5%) is higher than in non-pregnant women [29]. As reported by Neelima Pantagada, S. aureus ranked third among the causative pathogens, following E. coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae [30]. Asymptomatic bacteriuria in pregnant women is associated with pyelonephritis and perinatal complications including neonatal septicemia, respiratory distress, low birth weight, and spontaneous preterm birth, posing a significant threat to infants [31].

65.6% of all S. aureus isolates in this study were obtained from hospitalized patients, indicating that the hospital environment was a key setting for preventing and controlling S. aureus infections in women, children, and neonates. Globally, MRSA accounts for 25% to 50% of healthcare-associated S. aureus infections [32]. In the United States, around 52% of multidrug-resistant bacterial infections in hospitalized patients were attributed to MRSA [33]. Our four-year investigation found that the overall MRSA isolation rate among the three groups remained stable at approximately 19.7%. Notably, we observed population-specific differences and temporal fluctuations in MRSA detection rates, with neonates showing the greatest variability. Previous studies have verified that active surveillance and contact isolation measures effectively reduced both S. aureus colonization and infection rates in neonates [25, 34]. Accordingly, we hypothesized that the reduction in neonatal MRSA detection rates might be attributed to public health interventions implemented during the later stages of China’s COVID-19 pandemic, including strict isolation protocols, increased use of personal protective equipment, improved hand hygiene practices, and rigorous environmental disinfection, which effectively curbed healthcare-associated infections and MRSA transmission. Similarly, Zhanghua Yin et al. reported increased penicillin susceptibility among S. aureus isolates from Chinese neonates in 2021 versus 2019, attributing this to pandemic-driven changes in medical practices and health behaviors [35]. Nevertheless, we did not measure specific infection prevention and control interventions (e.g., hygiene guideline compliance). For this reason, changes in MRSA detection rates cannot directly reflect their effectiveness.

Consistent with previous multicenter studies, S. aureus isolates in this study showed 100% susceptibility to moxifloxacin, minocycline, vancomycin, teicoplanin, linezolid, daptomycin, and nitrofurantoin, with no shifts in MIC values [14, 36]. However, antibiotic overuse is exacerbating the global antimicrobial resistance crisis. Vancomycin overuse has been associated with the emergence of vancomycin-intermediate S. aureus (VISA) and vancomycin-resistant S. aureus (VRSA) in some regions [37, 38]. In addition, MRSA strains have developed resistance to various novel antimicrobial agents approved in the past two decades, and can increase MICs via complex mechanisms [39]. Specifically for our research subjects, S. aureus isolates from women, children, and neonates showed the highest resistance rates to penicillin (84.0%), erythromycin (39.4%), clindamycin (12.2%), tetracycline (9.3%), and co-trimoxazole (5.4%), which were closely related to domestic antibiotic consumption characteristics in China. The annual growth rate of antibiotic consumption is 7%, ranking the highest worldwide. Among commonly used drugs, the consumption of sulfonamides, gentamicin, and clindamycin was positively correlated with antimicrobial resistance [40, 41]. Meanwhile, macrolides rank third in antibiotic consumption (17.4%) in China’s tertiary hospitals, which may exacerbate the development of bacterial resistance to these antibiotics [42]. Although the overall antibiotic consumption showed a downward trend from 2011 to 2019, the consumption of special-class antibiotics (e.g., carbapenems, glycopeptides) has increased [41]. Since epidemic prevention and control became normalized in 2021, antibiotic consumption has rebounded from a period of reduced use [43].

Our data revealed significant differences in susceptibility rates to penicillin, erythromycin, and clindamycin among neonates, children, and women. Since antimicrobial stewardship programs effectively reduce antibiotic overuse and antimicrobial resistance, these differences may reflect population-specific variations in stewardship practices. In terms of MRSA infection or colonization risk, obvious population disparities were also observed. The pediatric population faced a higher risk, consistent with the findings of Maja Farkas et al. who reported that antibiotic consumption in school-aged children was significantly correlated with the carriage of resistant bacteria, making them key community reservoirs [44]. In contrast, obstetric women showed a lower risk of MRSA or S. aureus infection, aligning with a nested case-control study reporting 0.34% MRSA colonization prevalence in obstetric patients (significantly below hospital averages) [45]. We hypothesized that this lower risk may be closely associated with stringent antimicrobial stewardship practices in obstetrics, as prior studies have confirmed that antimicrobial use history is a key risk factor for MRSA colonization, further highlighting the importance of limiting antimicrobial use whenever clinically feasible [46, 47].

The limitations of the study

Molecular typing (including SCCmec and ST typing) was not performed on MRSA strains, precluding identification of sources, prevalent clonal lineages, and genetic interpretations of epidemiological characteristics and resistance mechanisms. This represents the primary limitation of this study and will be addressed in follow-up investigations. In addition, the retrospective design lacked clinical and radiological criteria to distinguish infection from colonization, potentially overestimating actual infection rates (particularly lower respiratory tract isolates in neonates and children, as colonized strains could not be fully excluded). Furthermore, the single-center study design limited generalizability to other healthcare settings. Moreover, several confounding factors cannot be ignored, such as referral bias and hospital-specific antibiotic stewardship practices. Given the exclusion of mild community-acquired infections and varying stewardship policies, our data may not fully represent regional epidemiology.

Conclusions

This study has provided novel insights into S. aureus and MRSA detection from maternal-child-specific specimens (e.g., neonatal gastric juice, preadolescent female genital secretions, maternal placental swabs, breast pus, umbilical secretions), and has generated original data for exploring mother-to-infant transmission mechanisms. These data not only offer targeted evidence for empirical antibiotic therapy of S. aureus and MRSA infections in maternal-child hospitals but also lay a foundation for multicenter epidemiological studies. In light of the above results, we put forward several practical clinical recommendations. It is necessary to prioritize S. aureus colonization monitoring in neonates and children, conduct early intervention for lactational mastitis, and strengthen risk management of intrauterine S. aureus infections in women. For long-term infection control, future efforts should highlight rational antibiotic use. We need to promote refined prescribing guidelines, and encourage a reduction in unnecessary prescriptions to curb the emergence of antimicrobial resistance in these vulnerable groups. Meanwhile, sustained MRSA surveillance with age-specific prevention strategies for neonates and children is essential to maintain intervention effectiveness.
