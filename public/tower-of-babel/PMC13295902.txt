Correlation of Gray-to-White matter ratio on Non-Contrast CT with neurological outcome in Post-Cardiac arrest syndrome
Authors: Zeinab El Mawla, Jida Mulki, Mahmoud Hassoun, Ahmad Aoude
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPost-cardiac arrest syndrome (PCAS) is a major cause of mortality and neurological disability. Early prognostication is essential for clinical decision-making, but variability in interpretation of Non-Contrast CT (NCCT)) Gray to White Matter Ratio (GWR) measurement techniques limits its clinical standardisation. This study evaluated the association between the Gray-to-White Matter Ratio (GWR) on early non-contrast CT (NCCT) and 6 -month neurological outcomes, and compared manual versus automated GWR measurements.MethodologyA retrospective cohort of 120 patients who are comatose with PCAS patients with return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) was analysed. NCCT scans (6–24 hours post-ROSC) were used to calculate GWR across basal ganglia, supratentorial cortex, and cerebellum using both manual and automated methods. Outcomes were classified using the Cerebral Performance Category (CPC). Prognostic performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, and multivariable modelling was performed using Firth logistic regression.ResultsHigher GWR values were significantly associated with favourable neurological outcomes across all regions and measurement methods (p<0.001). Automated global GWR demonstrated the highest prognostic accuracy (AUCarea under the curve=0.869; sensitivity 84.2%, specificity 84.1%) and was an independent predictor of poor outcome. Manual measurements showed moderate-to-good inter-rater reliability.ConclusionGWR is a robust prognostic biomarker for neuroprognostication after cardiac arrest. Automated GWR measurement improves reproducibility and supports the standardisation of imaging-based prognostic assessment, with potential implications for clinical decision-making in post-cardiac arrest care.

Introduction

Cardiac arrest (CA) remains a major global health burden, with incidence rates of 50–100 per 100 000 person-years and survival to hospital discharge after out-of-hospital CAcardiac arrest (OHCA) rarely exceeding 10%.1 2 Among patients who achieve return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), post-CAcardiac arrest syndrome (PCAS) frequently develops, comprising a complex interplay of hypoxic-ischaemic brain injury (HIBI), myocardial dysfunction, and systemic ischemia–reperfusion injury.1 3 Of these, HIBI is the principal determinant of long-term neurological outcome and mortality.46 The need for accurate and early neuroprognostication is therefore critical to guide clinical decision-making, inform family discussions, and optimise resource allocation.468

HIBI results from a cascade of ischaemic and reperfusion-related processes that culminate in diffuse cerebral oedema and neuronal death. During cardiac arrestCA, cessation of cerebral blood flow leads to rapid depletion of ATP, failure of ion homeostasis, and excitotoxic injury mediated by glutamate release.5 6 Following ROSC, reperfusion exacerbates injury through oxidative stress, inflammation, apoptosis, and microvascular dysfunction, ultimately resulting in widespread neuronal loss and cerebral oedema.6 9 10 These pathophysiological changes evolve over hours to days, complicating early clinical assessment and highlighting the need for objective biomarkers that reflect the extent of brain injury.

Neuroprognostication in patients who are comatose with PCAScomatose PCAS patients remains challenging, particularly in the early post-arrest phase. Established modalities—including clinical examination, electroencephalography (EEG), somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEPs), and serum biomarkers such as neuron-specific enolase—are recommended within a multimodal framework but have important limitations.4 6 Their accuracy may be confounded by targeted temperature management (TTM), sedation, and inter-observer variability, reducing reliability in the critical early window.6 11 Consequently, there is growing interest in imaging-based biomarkers that provide objective and early assessment of HIBI.

Non-contrast CTcomputed tomography (NCCT) is widely available, rapidly performed, and routinely used in PCAS to exclude acute intracranial pathology.4 12 Beyond qualitative assessment, quantitative CT measures have emerged as valuable tools for detecting early cerebral oedema. Among these, the gray-to-white matter ratio (GWR) has gained prominence as a simple and reproducible biomarker.1315 GWR reflects the loss of grey–white matter differentiation caused by preferential oedema in metabolically active grey matter, leading to reduced attenuation relative to white matter on CT.9 10 15 Lower GWR values have consistently been associated with poor neurological outcomes, making it a robust predictor in multiple studies and meta-analyses.1419

Despite its promise, several methodological inconsistencies limit the clinical implementation of GWR. Variability exists in the timing of CT acquisition, with evidence suggesting optimal prognostic performance within 6–24 hours after ROSC.4 11 13

Additionally, there is no consensus on the optimal anatomical level for measurement, as studies have evaluated the basal ganglia, supratentorial cortex, and cerebellum with differing results.4 14 18 A recent meta-analysis emphasised the need for standardisation to improve comparability and clinical utility.14 Furthermore, traditional manual GWR measurement is time-consuming and subject to inter-observer variability, which may hinder reproducibility in routine practice.19 20

Advances in automated image analysis offer a potential solution to these limitations. Automated GWR calculation can reduce operator dependence, improve efficiency, and enhance reproducibility, facilitating broader clinical adoption.20 Emerging evidence supports the feasibility and accuracy of automated approaches, though further validation across anatomical regions and clinical settings is required.13 Standardising GWR methodology, including timing, anatomical location, and calculation technique, remains essential for its integration into multimodal prognostication frameworks.

In this context, the present study aims to evaluate the prognostic utility of GWR as an early imaging biomarker in comatose patients with post-cardiac arrest syndromePACS. Specifically, it assesses the association between GWR measured on non-contrast NCCT within 6–24 hours after ROSC and 6 -month neurological outcomes, compares prognostic performance across anatomical regions (basal ganglia, supratentorial cortex, and cerebellum) using sensitivity, specificity, and area under the curve (AUC), evaluates inter-rater reliability of manual measurements across independent readers, and directly compares the prognostic accuracy of manual versus automated GWR measurement methods. By addressing key gaps in standardisation and reproducibility, this work seeks to advance GWR toward a reliable, objective, and clinically applicable tool for early neuroprognostication after cardiac arrestCA.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective observational cohort study was conducted in the medical intensive care unit (MICU) of a tertiary academic medical centre with 24-hour access to non-contrast computed tomography (NCCT) and multidisciplinary neurocritical care services, spanning from January 2018 to December 2022. The study utilisedused existing electronic medical records (EMR) and Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS) data. Post-arrest care protocols, including targeted temperature management and withdrawal of life-sustaining therapy (WLST) protocols, remained consistent throughout the study period. The retrospective design enabled identification and analysis of eligible patients without additional interventions.

Study population

A total of 450 adult patients (≥18 years) admitted after cardiac arrestCA were initially identified from institutional cardiac arrestCA registries and EMR over the specified multi-year period. Of these, 120 consecutive patients meeting all eligibility criteria were included in the final analysis. The remaining 330 patients were excluded for the following reasons: 150 patients had pre-existing severe neurological impairment, 80 patients had intracranial pathology unrelated to HIBI, 60 patients had NCCT performed outside the 6–24 hours post-ROSC window, and 40 patients had missing or incomplete imaging or clinical data. The determination of WLST for reasons unrelated to neurological prognosis; the treating clinical team documented that all WLST decisions in this cohort were made on neurological grounds or that patients survived to the 6-month follow-up endpoint. Data collection

Clinical, imaging, and outcome data were extracted from EMR and PACS by trained personnel and de-identified prior to analysis.

Clinical variables

Collected variables included age, sex, aetiology of cardiac arrestCA, no-flow and low-flow intervals, initial rhythm (shockable vs non-shockable), and use of targeted temperature management.

Imaging data

NCCT brain images were retrieved in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format from PACS. Only scans acquired within the predefined time window were included.

Outcome assessment

Neurological outcome at 6 months post-cardiac arrestCA was assessed using the Cerebral Performance Category (CPC) scale by a blinded investigator. Outcomes were dichotomised into good outcome (CPC 1–2) and poor outcome (CPC 3–5). The assessor was blinded to all imaging measurements.

Neuroimaging protocol

CT acquisition

NCCT scans were acquired using standard institutional protocols and stored in DICOM format. Acquisition parameters were consistent with routine clinical practice and suitable for quantitative analysis.

Manual GWR measurement

Gray-to-white matter ratio (GWR) was measured independently by three blinded readers (neuroradiologist, neurologist, and intensivist) using a standard DICOM viewer with region-of-interest (ROI) tools.

ROIs were placed at predefined anatomical levels:

Basal ganglia: caudate nucleus (grey matter) and posterior limb of the internal capsule (white matter).

Basal ganglia: caudate nucleus (grey matter) and posterior limb of the internal capsule (white matter).

Cerebral cortex: frontal cortex (grey matter) and adjacent subcortical white matter.

Cerebral cortex: frontal cortex (grey matter) and adjacent subcortical white matter.

Cerebellum: cerebellar cortex (grey matter) and cerebellar white matter.

Cerebellum: cerebellar cortex (grey matter) and cerebellar white matter.

Mean Hounsfield unit (HU) values were extracted from each ROI. GWR was calculated as the ratio of grey matter HU to adjacent white matter HU. A standardised operating procedure was used to ensure consistency across readers.

Automated GWR measurement

The automated GWR estimation was performed using the open-source software 3D Slicer (version V.4.11.20210226, https://www.slicer.org), which has been previously validated for quantitative neuroimaging analysis.21 22 The processing steps involved initial DICOM import, skull stripping using a brain extraction tool, followed by automated segmentation of grey and white matter based on predefined Hounsfield unitHU thresholds. Region-of-interest (ROI) identification for basal ganglia, supratentorial cortex, and cerebellum was performed using an atlas-based registration algorithm. Mean HU values were then extracted from these automated ROIs for GWR calculation. This standardised pipeline enhances reproducibility and external validity.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise baseline characteristics. Continuous variables were reported as median (IQR), because of their non-normal distribution, as assessed both graphically and using the Shapiro–-Wilk test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons were performed using Pearson’s chi-squareχ2 test, Fisher’s exact test, or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, as appropriate.

The prognostic performance of automated and manual grey–white matter ratio (GWR) measurements was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Area under the curve (AUC) values with 95% CIs were calculated, optimal cut-offs were derived using the Youden index, and performance at a predefined high-specificity threshold was assessed. AUCs were compared using DeLong’s test, and inter-rater reliability for manual GWR was quantified using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC).

Associations with poor neurological outcome were examined using Firth’s penalised logistic regression to reduce small-sample bias and address separation. Univariable and multivariable penalised models were constructed, adjusting for clinically relevant covariates. Global GWR was rescaled to reflect 0.1-unit increments. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors, and model assumptions were evaluated for linearity of the logit. Adjusted ORs with penalised profile likelihood 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. A two-sided p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R version V.4.5.1.

In contrast, several arrest-related characteristics were strongly associated with neurological outcome. Patients with favourable outcomes were more likely to have a cardiac aetiology and an initial shockable rhythm, whereas respiratory causes and non-shockable rhythms were more frequently observed among those with poor outcomes. Resuscitation characteristics also differed substantially between groups, with poor outcomes associated with longer no-flow and low-flow intervals, greater total downtime, and prolonged time to ROSC (all p<0.001; table 1).

The timing of post-ROSC CT acquisition was comparable between groups. However, shock delivery patterns differed significantly, with favourable outcomes generally associated with a greater number of defibrillation attempts. Post-ROSC haemodynamic instability, vasopressor requirements, and paralysis use were not significantly associated with outcome (table 1).

Neurological examination findings were strongly associated with prognosis. Higher GCS motor scores were more common among patients with favourable outcomes, whereas lower motor scores predominated among those with poor outcomes (p<0.001). ICUIntensive care unit and hospital lengths of stay were similar between groups. Survival to hospital discharge was significantly higher among patients with favourable neurological outcomes (online supplemental Table S1).

Across all brain regions, GWR values, derived using both automated and manual methods, were significantly higher in patients with good outcomes compared with those with poor outcomes (all p<0.001). Automated GWR measures demonstrated consistent separation between outcome groups, with lower median values in the poor-outcome group for the basal ganglia (1.13 vs 1.31), supratentorial cortex (1.08 vs 1.27), cerebellum (1.11 vs 1.26), and global GWR (1.13 vs 1.29). Similarly, manually measured GWR values were significantly lower among patients with poor outcomes across the three regions (table 2).

Comparison of prognostic performance between auto and manual GWR measurement

Table 3 summarises the prognostic performance of automated and manual GWR measurements across brain regions, with corresponding ROC curves shown in figures13). Both approaches demonstrated good discriminative ability, with AUCs ranging from 0.751 to 0.869. Using the Youden index, the automated global GWR achieved the highest AUC (0.869), with balanced sensitivity (84.2%) and specificity (84.1%), followed closely by the automated supratentorial cortex GWR (AUC 0.863), which demonstrated slightly lower sensitivity (80.7%) and specificity (82.5%). Compared with manual measurements, automated GWR yielded higher AUCs in the basal ganglia (0.824 vs 0.751) and supratentorial cortex (0.863 vs 0.829), whereas manual GWR showed a slightly higher AUC in the cerebellum (0.826 vs 0.804). Manual regional GWR measurements exhibited greater variability in sensitivity–specificity profiles, with moderate sensitivity and specificity in the basal ganglia, comparable sensitivity but lower specificity in the supratentorial region, and higher specificity accompanied by lower sensitivity in the cerebellum.

Pairwise comparisons of AUCs using DeLong’s test showed no statistically significant differences between global automated GWR and any of the regional automated measurements (all p>0.05). In contrast, only the comparison between manual basal ganglia GWR and global automated GWR demonstrated a statistically significant difference (p=0.002) (online supplemental Table S2).

At a fixed specificity of approximately 95%, automated GWR demonstrated modest sensitivity across regions, with the highest sensitivity observed in the supratentorial cortex (43.9%) and global measurements (33.3%) (online supplemental table S3).

Additional analyses using previously reported GWR thresholds are summarised in online supplemental table S4). A threshold of <1.10 yielded very high specificity (98.4%) and positive predictive value (93.3%) for poor neurological outcome, though with limited sensitivity (24.6%). Increasing the threshold to <1.15 improved sensitivity to 59.6% while maintaining high specificity (92.1%). A threshold of <1.20 offered the most balanced diagnostic performance, with sensitivity 77.2%, specificity 88.9%, positive predictive value 86.3%, and negative predictive value 81.2%.

Inter-rater reliability of manual GWR measurements

Inter-rater reliability for manual GWR measurements was moderate to good across brain regions. The ICCs were 0.515 for the basal ganglia, 0.664 for the supratentorial cortex, and 0.608 for the cerebellum, all statistically significant (p<0.001), indicating consistent agreement among the three readers (table 4).

Regression analysis

To identify independent predictors of poor neurological outcome, regression analyses were performed using Firth’s penalised logistic regression, given the relatively small sample size and evidence of quasi-complete separation. This approach was selected a priori to reduce small-sample bias and provide more reliable effect estimates.

Initial univariable Firth penalised logistic regression models were fitted for each candidate predictor. Variables demonstrating statistical relevance, along with those considered clinically important based on prior evidence and biological plausibility, were included in the multivariable penalised model. This strategy allowed adjustment for potential confounders while minimising overfitting and collinearity.

Global grey–white matter ratio (GWR) values were rescaled by a factor of 10 so that ORs reflected a clinically meaningful 0.1-unit increase rather than a 1-unit change, which exceeds the physiological range. Adjusted ORs with penalised profile likelihood 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported.

In univariable Firth penalised analyses, cardiac aetiology was strongly associated with lower odds of poor neurological outcome compared with other etiologies (OR 0.09, 95% CI 0.01 to 0.40, p=0.004), whereas respiratory aetiology was not significantly associated. Longer time to return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) was associated with higher odds of poor outcome (OR=1.11 per minute, 95% CI 1.07 to 1.17, p<0.001). Higher GCS motor score was protective (OR=0.42 per point, 95% CI 0.30 to 0.55, p<0.001), as was a greater number of shocks delivered (OR=0.45 per shock, 95% CI 0.30 to 0.65, p<0.001). Higher global GWR on early CT was strongly protective, with each 0.1-unit increase associated with an 86% reduction in the odds of poor outcome (OR=0.14, 95% CI 0.07 to 0.25, p<0.001). Age and sex were not significant predictors in univariable models.

In the multivariable Firth penalised logistic regression model adjusting for age, sex, aetiology, time to ROSC, GCS motor score, and number of shocks, only global automated GWR remained independently associated with neurological outcome. After rescaling, each 0.1-unit increase in GWR was associated with an 81% reduction in the odds of poor outcome (adjusted OR 0.19, 95% CI 0.08 to 0.38, p<0.001). Cardiac aetiology showed a trend toward lower odds but did not reach statistical significance (OR 0.11, 95% CI 0.01 to 1.07, p=0.068). All other covariates were not statistically significant in the adjusted model.

The final penalised model demonstrated good overall fit (residual deviance=52; AIC=70.24), and adjustment for clinical covariates did not alter the effect estimate for GWR (table 5).

Discussion

Principal findings

In this retrospective cohort of comatose post–-CAcardiac arrest patients, gray-to-white matter ratio (GWR) derived from early non-contrast NCCT demonstrated consistent prognostic value for 6 -month neurological outcome across manual and automated approaches and across anatomical regions. Lower GWR values were strongly associated with poor neurological outcomes (CPC 3–5). Automated global GWR showed the highest discriminative performance among all manual and automated regional approaches and remained an independent predictor of outcome after adjustment for clinical confounders. Overall, automated measurements outperformed manual approaches in most regions, while manual cerebellar measurements showed marginally higher performance. Inter-rater reliability for manual measurements was moderate to good, indicating acceptable reproducibility but persistent observer variability.41319

Interpretation in the context of existing evidence

These findings reinforce the established role of CT-based markers of hypoxic–ischaemic brain injury (HIBI), particularly loss of grey–white matter differentiation, as an early prognostic indicator in post-CA–cardiac arrest syndrome.2 9 10 23 The pathophysiological basis of reduced GWR reflects cerebral oedema and loss of tissue differentiation, which disproportionately affects grey matter due to its higher metabolic demand and susceptibility to ischemia.

Prior studies have consistently demonstrated that reduced GWR is associated with poor neurological outcomes in comatose survivors of cardiac arrestCA.15 17 19 24 25 Meta-analytical evidence further supports its prognostic utility, although performance varies depending on imaging timing, ROI selection, and methodological heterogeneity.14 20 Systematic reviews indicate that CT-based markers, including GWR, provide moderate-to-high predictive accuracy but should be interpreted within a multimodal prognostic framework.20

Although age and time to ROSC are established predictors of outcome after cardiac arrestCA, their lack of independent significance in our model likely reflects the limited sample size and statistical power. Moreover, Lee et al18 reported no incremental prognostic benefit of GWR beyond clinical predictors, suggesting that the independent association observed in our cohort may be influenced by cohort-specific factors or methodological differences. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and validated in larger prospective studies integrating imaging and clinical variables.

Manual versus automated measurement approaches

The comparison between manual and automated GWR measurement is a key strength of this study. Automated methods demonstrated superior or comparable performance in most regions, likely reflecting reduced inter-observer variability and standardised ROI placement. This aligns with prior evidence suggesting that automation improves reproducibility and reduces subjectivity in CT-based prognostic metrics.20 21 26

Manual measurements remain dependent on operator expertise and anatomical interpretation, which may explain the moderate inter-rater reliability observed. Previous studies have similarly highlighted observer variability as a limitation of manual ROI-based approaches, largely due to their susceptibility to placement bias and inconsistencies in image windowing.19 20 The slightly better performance of manual cerebellar measurements may reflect limitations in automated segmentation in posterior fossa regions, where anatomical complexity and imaging artefacts may reduce accuracy. This limitation is consistent with prior literature highlighting reduced CT reliability in infratentorial structures for quantitative density-based biomarkers.27

Regional variability in prognostic performance

Regional analysis demonstrated that supratentorial cortex and basal ganglia measurements yielded stronger prognostic performance compared with cerebellar measurements. This is consistent with prior evidence indicating that these regions are particularly vulnerable to hypoxic injury.15 18 19 The cerebellum showed lower and more variable predictive performance, suggesting that regional heterogeneity should be considered when interpreting GWR. These findings support the potential value of global or composite GWR measures over isolated regional assessments.

Performance of GWR as a prognostic biomarker

The prognostic performance of global automated GWR (AUC 0.869) falls within the upper range reported in systematic reviews, where CT-based GWR typically yields AUCs between 0.70 and 0.90 depending on timing and methodology.28 The relatively high specificity at fixed thresholds (~95%) supports its potential utility as a “‘rule-in’” marker of poor outcome, which is consistent with current recommendations that imaging should be used as part of multimodal prognostication rather than in isolation.29 However, sensitivity remained modest at high specificity thresholds, particularly for regional measurements. This reinforces a key limitation of CT-based biomarkers: while highly specific for severe hypoxic injury, they are insufficiently sensitive to exclude meaningful recovery, especially in patients with milder or delayed injury evolution.

The evaluation of pre-defined automated gray-to-white matter ratio (GWR) thresholds (1.10, 1.15, and 1.20) demonstrates a critical trade-off between sensitivity and specificity. Specifically, our threshold of <1.10 yielded 98.4% specificity, matching the near-perfect specificity (95–100%) reported in major meta-analyses.22 This reinforces the role of low GWR as a high-certainty “‘rule-in’” marker for poor outcome, though its low sensitivity (24.6%) remains consistent with prior findings that such severe changes identify only a subset of patients.20 22 At the <1.15 level, our specificity of 92.1% and improved sensitivity of 59.6% perform comparably to the “‘intermediate’” thresholds cited in contemporary guidelines, which typically range from 85% to 95% specificity.20 30 Finally, our <1.20 threshold provided a balanced performance (77.2% sensitivity, 88.9% specificity), mirroring the broader diagnostic windows usedutilised in multimodal frameworks to capture a larger proportion of affected patients.30 By replicating these established benchmarks, our results support the integration of automated GWR into standardised neuroprognostication protocols, eliminating the variability of manual ROI placement.20 22 30

Strengths, limitations and future directions

A key strength of this study is the direct comparison between automated and manual GWR using identical imaging datasets, along with incorporation of inter-rater reliability assessment. The observed moderate-to-good ICC values (0.51–0.66) confirm expected but non-trivial variability in manual ROI-based approaches, supporting the rationale for automation.

The use of Firth penalised logistic regression is another methodological strength, appropriately addressing small-sample bias and quasi-complete separation in outcome-associated predictors. This enhances the robustness of effect estimates for GWR, particularly given the imbalance in several clinical covariates.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be considered. First, the exclusion criteria, particularly the exclusion of patients with pre-existing severe neurological impairment, may have introduced selection bias and limited the generalisability of the findings to broader post-CAcardiac arrest populations. Second, although withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment (WLST) unrelated to neurological prognosis was a predefined exclusion criterion, no patients were excluded on this basis during the study period. Furthermore, the retrospective single-centre design is susceptible to residual confounding despite multivariable adjustment. While the sample size was adequate for the primary analyses, it may have limited the power of subgroup comparisons. Finally, external validation of the automated GWR algorithm was not performed, and longer-term neurological outcomes beyond 6 months were not assessed.

Future research should focus on prospective, multicenter validation of automated GWR across diverse populations and imaging systems. Integration of GWR into multimodal prognostication models incorporating clinical, electrophysiological, and biochemical markers is needed to better define its incremental value. Further refinement of automated segmentation, particularly in posterior fossa regions, may improve regional performance. Studies assessing long-term outcomes, clinical decision impact, and cost-effectiveness would also strengthen the evidence base.

Clinical implications

The results support the integration of GWR into early neuroprognostication strategies for patients post CA–cardiac arrest patients. Current international guidance emphasises multimodal prognostication combining clinical examination, electrophysiology, biomarkers, and imaging to improve accuracy and reduce the risk of premature prognostic conclusions.13 20 Within this framework, automated GWR offers a rapid, objective, and reproducible imaging biomarker that can contribute to early risk stratification and improve consistency of interpretation when integrated with other prognostic modalities.68

However, approximately 16% of patients classified as having a poor prognosis according to the optimal GWR threshold achieved favourable neurological outcomes. This finding highlights the need for caution when interpreting GWR results and reinforces that GWR should not be used in isolation. Rather, it should be considered alongside complementary clinical, electrophysiological, and biochemical assessments as part of a comprehensive prognostic strategy.6 29

Conclusion

Automated global GWR on early CT provides a robust, reproducible, and independently predictive marker of neurological outcome after cardiac arrestCA, outperforming manual and other automatic regional measurements in most comparisons. Integration of automated GWR into multimodal neuroprognostication pathways may enhance early risk stratification and support clinical decision-making; however, further validation in prospective multicentreer studies and continued refinement of automated algorithms are warranted to confirm generalisability and optimise performance across clinical settings.
