History of Presentation

A 77-year-old man presented to the outpatient clinic with NYHA functional class III symptoms. On examination, the patient was normotensive and had a holosystolic murmur at the cardiac apex. The patient exhibited mild bilateral lower-limb edema.

Past Medical History

Six months earlier, the patient had been diagnosed with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction with NYHA functional class III symptoms secondary to severe mitral regurgitation. He had a 24-month history of permanent atrial fibrillation and was receiving anticoagulation with vitamin K antagonist. Additional comorbidities included obstructive sleep apnea, chronic lower-limb ischemia, and chronic kidney disease stage 3A-A3.

Differential Diagnosis

In patients presenting with progressive dyspnea, peripheral edema, and palpitations, the differential diagnosis should include cardiovascular conditions such as significant coronary artery disease, dilated cardiomyopathies, and valvular heart disease. In this case, the patient had a history of permanent atrial fibrillation and a systolic ejection murmur at the mitral area.

Investigations

Baseline electrocardiography showed atrial fibrillation with a controlled ventricular response. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated severe left atrial dilatation (71 mL/m2) and severe degenerative mitral regurgitation. Transesophageal echocardiography confirmed severe mitral regurgitation, with a biplane vena contracta of 9 mm and an effective regurgitant orifice area of 0.32 cm2. The underlying mechanism was consistent with primary degenerative mitral valve disease, characterized by markedly thickened and fibrotic leaflets with reduced coaptation reserve (2.5 mm), predominantly involving the anterior leaflet. The regurgitant jet was mainly located at the A1–P1 scallop, extending partially toward the central segments (A2–P2). Importantly, there were no echocardiographic features suggestive of secondary mitral regurgitation, such as leaflet tethering, left ventricular dilatation, or regional wall motion abnormalities (Figures 1A and 1B, Videos 1 to 3). Reversal systolic flow in the pulmonary veins was also observed. Anterior and posterior leaflet lengths were 32 mm and 17 mm, respectively. TTE also demonstrated mild aortic stenosis, with a mean gradient of 12 mm Hg, and a peak velocity of 2.5 m/s. In the context of severe mitral regurgitation, marked left atrial dilatation, and absence of advanced aortic valve hemodynamic compromise, mitral regurgitation was considered the main driver of symptoms after heart team discussion. Coronary angiography showed no significant coronary lesions. Cardiac computed tomography (CT) showed a noncalcified mitral annulus with systolic anteroposterior and intercommissural diameters of 43.1 mm and 35.1 mm, respectively (perimeter 130 mm; area 12.9 cm2). Given the patient’s high surgical risk (Society of Thoracic Surgeons predicted mortality 10% and EuroSCORE II 7.65%) and after heart team discussion, surgical mitral valve replacement was ruled out. Owing to unfavorable valve anatomy, characterized by thickened and fibrotic leaflets with reduced coaptation reserve and a complex regurgitant jet distribution involving the A1–P1 scallop and central segments, the patient was not considered an optimal candidate for transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER). Although TEER may be feasible in selected patients with suboptimal anatomy, these features raised concerns regarding the likelihood of achieving a durable result. Therefore, TMVR was considered the most appropriate therapeutic option under compassionate use.

Management

The procedure was performed under general anesthesia with transesophageal echocardiography and fluoroscopic guidance.

Step 1: Vascular access and transseptal puncture

Ultrasound-guided right femoral venous access was obtained, and preclosure was performed using 3 ProGlide devices (Abbott). A transseptal puncture was then performed at approximately 35-mm height to achieve an optimal posterior and superior position, facilitating coaxial alignment with the mitral valve.

Technical tip

A high and posterior transseptal puncture provides sufficient working space in the left atrium and improves coaxial alignment with the mitral valve.

Step 2: Left ventricular access and trajectory preparation

A Safari XS guidewire was advanced into the left ventricular apex through a deflectable Agilis NxT Steerable Introducer (Abbott) sheath. Valve flossing using a 12- to 15-mm balloon was performed to ensure a chordae-free trajectory, followed by septostomy with a 12-mm balloon to allow safe passage of the delivery system.

Technical tip

Valve flossing helps confirm a chordae-free trajectory and reduces the risk of subvalvular entanglement during advancement of the delivery system.

Step 3: Device positioning and coaxial alignment

The Cardiovalve delivery system was advanced into the left atrium with progressive flexion. Posterior rotation of the transseptal sheath and careful catheter manipulation allowed optimal positioning above the mitral valve and coaxial alignment.

Technical tip

Fine control of catheter flexion and rotation is essential to center the device over the mitral orifice and avoid malalignment before ventricular anchoring.

Step 4: Leaflet capture and prosthesis deployment

Ventricular anchors were deployed, and the delivery system was advanced into the left ventricle (Figure 3). Gentle retraction enabled effective leaflet capture, followed by full deployment of the atrial skirt and complete expansion of the prosthesis.Figure 3Angiography During Transcatheter Mitral Valve Replacement With the Cardiovalve System(A) Capture of both mitral leaflets. Technical tip

Controlled and gradual retraction is crucial to ensure complete leaflet capture and avoid asymmetric anchoring or residual paravalvular leak.

Step 5: Final assessment and closure

Implantation resulted in reduction of mitral regurgitation to trace grade, with trivial medial and lateral paravalvular leaks (Figures 1C and 1D, Video 4). The final transprosthetic gradient was 3 mm Hg, with normalization of pulmonary venous flow. The residual interatrial communication was closed with a 27-mm Cardioform device (GCA; W.L. Gore & Associates). Femoral access closure was successfully achieved using the preplaced suture-mediated devices without complications.

Technical tip

Final echocardiographic assessment should confirm stable prosthesis position, residual mitral regurgitation or paravalvular leak, transprosthetic gradient, pulmonary venous flow, and absence of left ventricular outflow tract obstruction.

The total procedural time was 102 minutes. The patient was extubated and transferred to the recovery unit without adverse events.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Postprocedural clinical evolution was favorable. Predischarge transthoracic echocardiography showed a well-positioned prosthesis with a persistent mild lateral paravalvular leak (Video 5) that did not result in clinical deterioration or evidence of hemolysis during follow-up. The patient was discharged 3 days after the procedure. At approximately 1-year follow-up, transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a normally functioning mitral bioprosthesis with persistent mild medial and lateral paravalvular leaks (Video 6), and the patient showed a marked improvement in functional status, evolving from NYHA functional class III to class I-II, without heart failure–related hospitalizations. Postprocedural cardiac CT was not performed; however, serial echocardiographic assessments showed normal prosthesis expansion and leaflet motion, with no evidence of valve thrombosis. The patient was maintained on vitamin K antagonist therapy because of permanent atrial fibrillation.

Discussion

Mitral valve disease is highly prevalent in the older population. Primary mitral regurgitation represents a growing public health problem due to progressive population aging and is expected to become increasingly prevalent. Although surgical management remains the preferred treatment for symptomatic patients, many present with prohibitive surgical risk, necessitating consideration of percutaneous alternatives. In the 2025 update of the European Society of Cardiology guidelines on valvular heart disease, TEER received a class IIa recommendation in anatomically suitable cases, and TMVR was introduced as an option for patients unsuitable for TEER.1

TMVR has emerged as an alternative to TEER in patients with primary mitral regurgitation and high surgical risk who have unfavorable anatomy for edge-to-edge repair, including short or excessively calcified leaflets, as well as in patients with Barlow disease, extensive mitral annular calcification, and significant mitral stenosis.

TMVR requires meticulous preprocedural CT planning to avoid 2 major complications: significant paravalvular leak and dynamic left ventricular outflow tract obstruction. CT also enables accurate reconstruction of the mitral annulus and virtual device implantation for procedural planning.2

Mitral bioprosthesis design must incorporate an active anchoring system to prevent device migration under dynamic systolic and diastolic pressures, in contrast to transcatheter aortic valve replacement, which relies predominantly on radial force for fixation. Various anchoring mechanisms exist, ranging from the apical tether used by the Tendyne valve to systems that anchor to the native leaflets or clamp the mitral annulus with an external atrial anchoring component, as in the Cardiovalve system.3

The Cardiovalve system is a self-expanding, trileaflet bovine pericardial mitral bioprosthesis designed for transfemoral transseptal implantation. It features a symmetrical design with 12 ventricular anchors for leaflet capture and an atrial skirt to enhance sealing and reduce paravalvular leak. Its low-profile design, with approximately 12 mm of ventricular protrusion, minimizes the risk of left ventricular outflow tract obstruction. It is available in 3 sizes (M, L, and XL), allowing treatment of annular dimensions up to 55 mm.4 Early clinical experience with Cardiovalve in the mitral position demonstrated excellent hemodynamic efficacy but a high initial rate of vascular access–related complications. Redesign of the delivery system has significantly improved safety and procedural success, with encouraging recent results.5 AHEAD (AHEAD: European Feasibility Study of the Cardiovalve Transfemoral Mitral Valve System; NCT03339115) is currently prospectively evaluating the safety and efficacy of this system in patients with severe mitral regurgitation and high surgical risk.

Conclusions

TMVR with the Cardiovalve system represents a feasible alternative for patients with anatomy unsuitable for edge-to-edge repair and high surgical risk, highlighting its exclusively transfemoral approach and low-profile design, which may minimize ventricular complications.
