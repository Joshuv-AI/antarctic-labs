Welcome Editorial

The pursuit of knowledge is one of the oldest fundamental principles of humankind. As the great scientist Albert Einstein once said, “The important thing is not to stop questioning.” These words serve as the perfect description for an event that is rooted in curiosity and ambition. It is with great privilege that I introduce the 13th International Glasgow Neuro Conference, held at the Royal College of Physicians and Surgeons, Glasgow.

Serving as President of Glasgow Neuro Society 2025/2026 has been an honor, particularly as I witness the impact of our committee’s dedication. I take enormous pride in introducing the outcome of all of our efforts and extend my sincerest gratitude to every speaker, workshop leader, sponsor, delegate, and colleague who helped make this year’s meeting our largest conference to date.

Glasgow Neuro has always pushed the boundaries of its original purpose as a society for junior clinicians and researchers. It was founded in 2010 by Mr. Allan Hall, a then medical student at the University of Glasgow, who is now a neurosurgical registrar at the Institute of Neurological Sciences in Glasgow. He was supported by Professor Sir Graham Teasdale, best known for his contribution to clinical neuroscience through the Glasgow Coma Scale. With such a legacy, Glasgow Neuro has always been defined by high expectations. Through the efforts of successive committees, the society has matured into one of the largest and most influential junior neurosocieties in the United Kingdom.

Previous years have brought to Glasgow some of the most prominent names in neurosurgery, neurology and clinical neuroscience. These include Professor Sir Graham Teasdale, whose work on the Glasgow Coma Scale changed the language of neurological assessment across the world; Professor Henry Brem, a leading Johns Hopkins neurosurgeon known internationally for his work in brain tumor surgery and translational neuro-oncology; Professor Gary Steinberg, founder and former Chair of Neurosurgery at Stanford University and a major figure in cerebrovascular neurosurgery; Professor Henry Marsh, one of the United Kingdom’s most widely recognised neurosurgeons and authors; Professor Charlie Teo, an Australian neurosurgeon known for his work in complex brain tumor surgery; the late Professor James Goodrich, a world-renowned pediatric neurosurgeon known for complex craniofacial and craniopagus separation surgery; Professor Robert Martuza, former Chief of Neurosurgery at Massachusetts General Hospital and a major academic neurosurgical leader at Harvard; Professor Alim Louis Benabid, whose pioneering work in deep brain stimulation transformed the treatment of movement disorders; and Professor Michael Lim, a globally recognised neurosurgical oncologist known for his work in brain tumors, immunotherapy and translational neurosurgery at Stanford.

I inherited a society strengthened by the ambition of my predecessors, who had already laid the foundations for connecting Glasgow Neuro with an international audience. That legacy was the driving force behind this year’s conference, guiding us as we planned a programme that matched the standard set by those before us. Returning to the Royal College of Physicians and Surgeons of Glasgow was the only setting that felt right for this year’s meeting. The history of the venue served as a constant reminder of the standard of excellence we strive to maintain in our society.

This year’s invited faculty was nothing short of world-class. Professor Alex Golby, Professor of Neurosurgery and Radiology at Harvard Medical School, opened a window into academic neurosurgery, surgical brain mapping and the difficult art of operating near critical structures. Professor Wenya Linda Bi, also from Harvard Medical School, brought the perspective of skull base and pituitary surgery, showing how technical excellence and translational science can sit together in the care of patients with complex brain and skull base tumors. Professor Jeremy Heit from Stanford University gave a powerful account of a neurointerventional career, discussing everything from strokes and aneurysms to the future of minimally invasive cerebrovascular treatment.

Professor Andres Lozano from the University of Toronto gave a brilliant talk about how functional neurosurgery has repeatedly changed what doctors believe is possible. His work in deep brain stimulation, focused ultrasound and circuit-based treatment has paved the way for groundbreaking new research in the field. Dr. Marie Slegr then shone a spotlight on the wider history of neurology, from Charcot to the 21st century.

The workshops were deliberately designed to expand the range of exposure available to junior clinicians. Alongside our established neurosurgical skills sessions in bone flap fixation, burr hole techniques and spine pedicle screw placement, we introduced our first-ever nerve conduction studies and interventional neuroradiology workshops. The highly realistic virtual reality endovascular simulator allowed delegates to experience the delicacy of catheter navigation and the seriousness of neurovascular decision-making in a safe setting. This was made possible by Dr. Wazim Izzath, Consultant Interventional Neuroradiologist at the Institute of Neurological Sciences in Glasgow, who helped bring a new neurovascular dimension to the conference. Dr Saul Crandon, Specialty Registrar in Clinical Neurophysiology at the Institute of Neurological Sciences and Honorary Clinical Lecturer at the University of Glasgow, also brought clinical neurophysiology into the programme by leading the nerve conduction studies workshop. Through live demonstrations of sensory and motor nerve conduction studies, delegates saw how neurophysiology helps assess nervous system function and guide the preservation of neurological function in clinical practice.

We were also proud to host a one-to-one neurosurgery national selection Curriculum Vitae (CV) clinic, the first of its scale and kind in the country, setting a new precedent for how other societies can support and inspire the next generation of neurosurgeons. The United Kingdom pathway into neurosurgery is notoriously competitive, and junior clinicians often hear vague, irrelevant advice long before they receive practical guidance. This year, delegates were able to sit with recently appointed ST1 and ST2 trainees, including 8 of the 21 nationally selected neurosurgery residents, and receive direct and practical advice on how to build a substantive portfolio. Success in this field often hinges on how early one begins to prepare, and it can often feel overwhelming. Being able to have a short and honest conversation with someone who has recently navigated the same path can fundamentally reshape a delegate’s career trajectory. I therefore want to give proper recognition to those recently appointed residents who supported the day: Dr. Conor Gillespie, Dr. Mohammad Ashraf, Dr. Emily Boyd, Dr. Bilal Benzahia, Dr. Victoria Collins, Dr. Robert Vardanyan, Dr. Devika Dahiya and Dr. Aws Al Omari.

I am eternally grateful to the consultants and registrars who offered their time so generously for our workshops. Mr. Andreas Demetriades supported our surgical workshop programme with the perspective of an experienced consultant neurosurgeon and former EANS President. We were also privileged to have Mr. Samih Hassan, Mr. Kevin Agyemang and Mr. Roddy O’Kane from the Institute of Neurological Sciences here at Glasgow. Their contributions allowed delegates to learn from consultants who understand the craftsmanship and responsibility that come with neurosurgical practice.

This conference would not have been possible at this scale without our sponsors. We are grateful to Brainlab, Medac Pharma, Olympus and KLS Martin Group for supporting an educational meeting that brought junior clinicians closer to the tools, conversations and career routes shaping modern clinical neuroscience. We also thank our neurosurgical and neurological honorary presidents, Mr. Calan Mathieson and Dr. Xuya Huang, whose guidance has been central to Glasgow Neuro’s progress over the past 2 years. Finally, I would like to extend my gratitude to Professor Matthew Walters, Dean of Glasgow Medical School, for his continued encouragement of Glasgow Neuro and its mission.

This year’s conference also continued Glasgow Neuro’s partnership with Surgical Neurology International and SNI Digital. We are deeply grateful to Professor James Ausman, Founder and Emeritus Editor in Chief of SNI, Professor Nancy Epstein, Editor in Chief, and Mr. Jim Cook, Managing Editor, for giving our junior researchers a platform beyond the walls of a lecture theater. While written abstracts are crucial, the accompanying panel discussions allow the work to be critically evaluated in greater depth. They also create space for an equal exchange of ideas, allowing delegates to engage properly with the subject matter.

The response from delegates was overwhelming. One attendee described the conference as “organised so well, with such fantastic speakers and workshops,” adding that the passion of the committee in providing opportunities to others was abundantly clear. Another wrote that it was “easily one of the best-run conferences for junior clinicians in the UK that I have ever been to.” Others noted the value of our CV clinics and the calibre of our workshops. While praise is never our objective, we value it as an indicator of our success. It tells us that all the effort we put in was worthwhile, and our vision translated into a meaningful and practical experience for our delegates. A conference of this scale is a collective achievement I share with the Glasgow Neuro committee, whom I deeply admire and owe a great debt of gratitude to. The success of such a day is built on months of repetitive and tedious behind-the-scenes work, often coming with major setbacks. The committee relentlessly exceeded every expectation set upon them and has raised the ceiling for those following in their footsteps.

ABSTRACT 1 – TOLEBRUTINIB:

Eligibility for tolebrutinib in progressive multiple sclerosis patients in NHS lanarkshire

Maryam Alamir1, Niall MacDougall1,2

1Medical School, School of Medicine and Veterinary Medicine, University of Glasgow, 2Department of Neurosurgery, Institute of Neurological Sciences, Glasgow, Scotland, United Kingdom.

Introduction: Despite the advancements in multiple sclerosis (MS) management, non-active progressive MS remains challenging to treat due to the lack of therapies targeting disease progression independent from relapse activity (PIRA). Tolebrutinib, a Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitor, has demonstrated potential in targeting compartmentalised central nervous system (CNS) inflammation driving PIRA. Tolebrutinib proved effective in delaying disability progression in non-active secondary progressive MS (SPMS), and is currently under investigation for primary progressive MS (PPMS).

Methods: A study of a database comprising 1,071 MS patients within NHS Lanarkshire was undertaken. Patients with progressive MS were identified and assessed for eligibility for Tolebrutinib based on clinical trial criteria, including expanded disability status scale scores of 3.0–6.0 and absence of recent disease activity. Subgroup analyses examined the impact of age restrictions (18–60 years vs. extended to 65 years) and evaluated PPMS patients for potential treatment.

Results: Of 1,071 patients, 307 had progressive MS (SPMS 50.4%, PPMS 34.2%, unspecified 15.3%). 74 patients were eligible for Tolebrutinib without age restriction. Applying the 18–60 years age restriction criterion reduced eligibility by 43.2% (42 patients), whereas extending the upper limit to 65 years increased the number of eligible patients to 61. Within SPMS patients, 29 were eligible without applying age restriction, falling to 15 underage restriction and increasing to 23 with an upper limit of 65 years. Among PPMS patients, 35 were identified as eligible, including 17 currently receiving ocrelizumab and 12 with inactive disease, a group lacking treatment options.

Conclusion: This audit highlights a significant patient population in NHS Lanarkshire who may benefit from tolebrutinib, and demonstrates the marked impact of age restriction on rates of eligibility to treatment. In addition, this study explored the mechanisms underlying disability progression in MS and how tolebrutinib may counteract them by modulating B-cell and innate immune pathways within the CNS.

ABSTRACT 2 – MICROGLIA:

https://snidigital.org/search?q=glasgow+neuro&video=e6133cfd-5a85-4c41-bf78-cc58c34341a0

Microglia and astrocyte response in Parkinson’s disease

Florence Do1,2, Craig Terriis2, Anna Mallach2

1Edinburgh Medical School, Edinburgh, Scotland, 2Imperial College London, London, England, United Kingdom.

Introduction: Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a common neurodegenerative disease, and many patients progress to develop PD dementia (PDD). However, the mechanism behind it is unclear, with no available intervention to halt progression. Higher levels of cortical beta-amyloid plaques (Aβ), alpha-synuclein, and their co-localisation (co-pathology) in PDD might enhance the triggering of microglia and astrocytes, which are involved in clearing pathologies. Recent work supports this by demonstrating significant functional and gene expression changes predominantly in these glia. Therefore, we aimed to quantify microglia and astrocyte accumulation and morphological changes, particularly in the pathology niche, to understand their role in PD and its progression.

Methods and Materials: Immunofluorescence staining was performed on the middle temporal gyrus from 10 PDD to 10 PD post-mortem brains. Densities of microglia (Iba1+) and astrocytes (S100B+) were quantified when near pathology (within 100 µm diameter) or far away. 27 morphological metrics were measured post-segmentation, skeletonisation and Sholl analysis of glia.

Results: Microglia significantly accumulated around Aβ, particularly in PDD. Being close to both pathologies modulated microglia density but not additively, highlighting a complex niche environment with multiple pathologies. Contrastingly, astrocyte density was independent of pathologies, potentially due to lower motility. Morphologically, microglia near copathologies showed higher complexity, but those near single pathology showed increased branching and spread, which might indicate different activation states. Astrocytes demonstrated a gradient of morphological complexity (alpha-synuclein > co-pathology > Aβ) and are also more complex in PDD.

Conclusion: Our study provided spatial and morphological insight into glial response in PD progression. Astrocyte morphologies near pathologies are characteristic of reactive astrogliosis. Being most strongly driven by alpha-synuclein might suggest their early involvement in PD. Microglia preferentially clustering around Aβ may reflect activities that contribute to dementia development. Most importantly, co-pathology complicates glial response. Future work should delineate the temporal sequence and functional implications of glial changes for disease-modifying therapies.

ABSTRACT 3 – CAUDA EQUINA:

https://snidigital.org/search?q=glasgow+neuro&video=e4e537e1-e824-4f0a-aa67-101cb8b44d5e

Cauda equina neuroendocrine tumor of the filum terminale: A case report and summary of the literature

Theoklis Kouyialis1,2, Christophoros Christophorou1, Christina Oxinou3, Demetris Fkiaras1

1Aretaeio Hospital, Strovolos, Cyprus, 2The University of Edinburgh, Scotland, United Kingdom, 3Oxinou Diagnostic Pathology Laboratory, Nicosia, Cyprus.

Introduction: Cauda equina neuroendocrine tumors (CENETs), previously known as Cauda Equina Paragangliomas (PGLs), are extremely rare vascular tumors of the spine which are mostly benign, slow-growing and well-demarcated from the surrounding structures. The recent (2022) change in their classification is owed to newly published information on epigenetic, chromosomal copy number and immunohistochemical differences from extra-spinal PGLs. Their rarity (300 reported cases) and lack of specificity in clinical presentation and features in imaging present a challenge to differential diagnosis and management. Herein, we present a case of such a tumor and a summary of the existing literature on diagnostic modalities and treatments.

Case Report: We present the case of a man in his 50s with a clinical history of low back pain exacerbated at night and right-sided sciatica with an L4 radicular distribution. Magnetic resonance imaging of the lumbar spine revealed an intradural mass at the upper level of L4 with strong homogeneous enhancement following gadolinium contrast administration. Given the lack of specificity of the symptoms and imaging, a possible diagnosis of ependymoma, schwannoma or PGL was made. Complete tumor resection was achieved through an L4 laminectomy, and the diagnosis of CENET was made based on postoperative histological and immunohistochemical testing. The patient made a full and uneventful recovery.

Conclusion: Though the rarity of these tumors prevents the establishment of strict guidelines for their diagnosis and treatment, reviewing the existing data can provide safe and effective ways of diagnosing and selecting the best treatment options for such patients. Continued reporting of new cases, given the scarcity of information, is of extreme importance to guide evidence-based medicine in the future.

ABSTRACT 4 – PINEAL CYST:

https://snidigital.org/search?q=glasgow+neuro&video=d89ea0de-4b1f-4b59-a0ba-3a4a1dbf2746

Natural history of pineal cysts: A retrospective cohort study

Yihui Cheng1, Xin Y. Yap1, Zahraa Dashti1, Steven Tominey2, Kevin Owusu-Agyemang3, Edward J. St George3

1Medical School, School of Medicine and Veterinary Medicine, University of Glasgow, Glasgow, Scotland, 2Department of Neurosurgery, Royal London Hospital, London, England, 3Department of Neurosurgery, Institute of Neurological Sciences, Glasgow, Scotland, United Kingdom.

Objectives: Pineal cysts (PCs) are frequently identified incidentally on neuroimaging, yet their clinical significance was thought to be benign. While most remain stable, concerns persist regarding their potential to cause obstructive hydrocephalus or other mass effects. This study aimed to describe the natural history of PCs in adults in the West of Scotland.

Design and Methods: A retrospective observational study was conducted at the Institute of Neurological Sciences, Glasgow, including adult patients who underwent brain Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) between 2011 and 2020, with radiology reports noting a pineal cyst. Clinical (age, gender, presenting symptom, diagnoses) and imaging data (i.e. size, mass effect, atypical features [e.g. multiloculation, rim enhancement, calcification]) were extracted from electronic health records.

Results: A total of 598 patients with PCs were identified from 1,851 MRI scans (mean age: 37 years, ranged 29–49 years; 68.3% female). Follow-up imaging was available in 256 cases (42.9%, median follow-up: 38 months [8–134 months]). Most cysts remained stable; only 11.7% (30/256) showed size changes, with 56.7% decreasing. Atypical features were seen in 14.7% (88/598), with higher rates in females (16.9% vs. 10.0%, P = 0.036). Tectal compression occurred in 11.4% (68/598), and aqueductal narrowing in 2.7% (16/598). Five patients (mean size: 26 mm; [20–33 mm]) developed hydrocephalus requiring surgical intervention; one had pineal apoplexy. No cases of Parinaud’s syndrome were observed.

Conclusion: Pineal cysts are generally stable incidental findings. Larger cysts (>15 mm) may cause clinically significant effects, including hydrocephalus, migraines, and headache diagnoses. As there are no significant differences in maximal diameter across cyst sizes, this supports a benign natural cause. However, risk-stratified follow-up and selective neurosurgical referral may be appropriate in adults with PC >15 mm. By defining the largely benign natural history of pineal cysts, this study can aid shared decision-making, improve patient counselling, and guide future prospective research into long-term outcomes.

ABSTRACT 5 – EXPOSURE TO NEUROSURGERY:

https://snidigital.org/search?q=glasgow+neuro&video=f3bc50c8-5023-4754-abfd-8aee8e60c5f1

Exposure to neurosurgery among undergraduate medical students in Europe: A scoping review

Tejas A. Sawant

Beaumont Hospital, Royal College of Surgeons, Dublin, Ireland.

Introduction: This review aims to understand the current state of exposure to neurosurgery in the undergraduate medical curriculum in Europe and the various means available to facilitate more knowledge and familiarity with the field.

Methods: The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews was used as a reference to guide the search strategy. PubMed was the primary database used to search for articles about “Neurosurgery AND Europe AND (medical students OR undergraduate medical education).”

Results: 101 articles were returned in the PubMed search. 10 articles were identified as suitable to the scope of the review. Limited exposure to neurosurgery in the undergraduate medical curriculum was a common theme across articles. A large multi-country study in southeastern Europe found that 80.2% of the respondents were not able to acquire knowledge about pursuing a neurosurgical career through their curriculum. Only 32.9% of the respondents reported that neurosurgery presentations/lectures were organised at their university. Furthermore, a study from Ireland revealed that 22% of respondents received formal neurosurgical teaching as an undergraduate and only 12% of respondents had a clinical rotation in neurosurgery. A study across nine British medical schools found that 33% of final year respondents faced difficulty in identifying conditions and scenarios warranting neurosurgical referral. On another note, three studies across Ireland and the UK found that conferences boosted the interest of medical students in neurosurgery, exposing them to academic topics as well as the practical realities of a career in neurosurgery.

Conclusion: Exposure to neurosurgery through the undergraduate medical curriculum in Europe remains limited. Adopting a basic framework of didactic neurosurgery teaching as well as involvement in the operating theater can boost interest and understanding. Conferences play a vital role in bridging the gap in the curriculum by improving access to neurosurgery and fostering a better understanding of the field.

ABSTRACT 6 – MTOR:

https://snidigital.org/search?q=glasgow+neuro&video=8f11e569-402f-4523-ad74-c5b01a199f72

Efficacy and safety of mechanistic target of rapamycin inhibitor therapy in a Canadian pediatric tuberous sclerosis complex cohort

Ritaj Abuomar1, Mila Valcic1, Ashwaq Alsulami2, Maria Zak1, Lauren Sham1,3

1Division of Neurology, Department of Paediatrics, The Hospital for Sick Children, Toronto, Canada, 2Department of Paediatrics, King Abdullah Specialized Children’s Hospital, Ministry of National Guard, Jeddah, Saudi Arabia, 3Department of Paediatrics, University of Toronto, Toronto, Canada.

Background: Tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC) is a genetic condition marked by benign tumors in multiple organ systems. As mutations in TSC1/2 lead to overactivation of the mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway, mTOR inhibitors (mTORi) are a useful therapy indicated for subependymal giant cell astrocytomas (SEGA), renal angiomyolipomas (AML), and drug-resistant epilepsy. While mTORi are widely used in adults, work is needed to assess their benefit in children.

Objective: Evaluate the efficacy and safety of mTORi in our pediatric TSC cohort.

Methods: Data were obtained from SickKids’ prospective observational TSC clinical outcomes database (n = 107), comprised of patients with genetically and/or clinically confirmed TSC. Details on patient demographics, mTORi, clinical outcomes, and adverse events (AEs) were collected.

Results: 19 children (median age at diagnosis 0.6 years, range 0–8.3; F: M 10:9) were identified to have received mTORi. Therapy indications were SEGA (n = 6), AML (n = 4), seizures (n = 4), prophylactic (n = 2), AML/SEGA (n = 1), seizures/AML (n = 1), and seizures/SEGA (n = 1). 84.2% (n = 16/19) had epilepsy, 81.3% (n = 13/16) of which were drug-resistant. mTORi was initiated at a median age of 8.4 years (range 2.1–15.4). 68.4% (n = 13/19) received sirolimus and 31.6% (n = 6/19) received everolimus. 24 months post-mTORi initiation, 50% showed stable SEGA (n = 4/8), 50% reduced SEGA (n = 4/8), 66.7% stable AML (n = 8/12), 25% reduced AML (n = 3/12), and 8.3% larger AML (n = 1/12). Due to the variability in reporting seizure frequency, mTORi effects on epilepsy were inconclusive. While mTORi was overall well tolerated, AEs were reported by 100% (n = 19/19), the majority being Grade 1–2.

Conclusion: This study is the first to describe the efficacy and tolerability of mTORi in a Canadian pediatric TSC cohort. Future work will include standardisation of seizure data collection to study epilepsy outcomes, impact on neuropsychiatric symptoms, and contribution of specific genetic variants.

ABSTRACT 7 – WEBINAR SERIES:

https://snidigital.org/search?q=glasgow+neuro&video=58dce79e-3281-4ec3-9666-357a3cef4c84

The impact of a webinar series on student perspectives to neuroscience research barriers

Emilia Parsi1, Alsadeg Bilal1,2,3, Selnan Wuyep1, Chandru Kaliaperumal4

1Edinburgh Medical School, 2Cancer Research Centre, The University of Edinburgh, 3Centre for Clinical Brain Science, The University of Edinburgh, 4Department of Clinical Neuroscience, NHS Lothian, Edinburgh, Scotland, United Kingdom.

Background: Research engagement amongst medical students tends to be minimal until it becomes a curricular requirement, and the specific barriers to early involvement remain unclear. The General Medical Council highlights that new graduates should know how to contribute to research to deliver high-quality patient care. In line with this, our study aimed to identify barriers to neuroscience research engagement and evaluate the impact of a targeted webinar series on influencing student perspectives.

Methods: From September to December 2023, we delivered a four-part online webinar series to students across 20 universities worldwide. Academic clinicians presented neuroscience research projects, discussing research methods, academic practice and tips for getting involved. Anonymised pre- and post-webinar Google Forms surveys were distributed to students. Quantitative and qualitative data were collected to assess statistical outcomes and identify common themes.

Results: Prior to the webinar series, students rated 2.8/5 (56%) in feeling equipped to engage in research; post-webinar, this increased to 3.9/5 (P < 0.05). Among students with prior dissertation experience, 95% reported an increased interest in research. While 66% of students expressed uncertainty exploring research opportunities, 52% reported that the webinar series helped alleviate these barriers. Additionally, 95% felt they had gained a better understanding of neuroscience research, and 64% felt the series increased their likelihood to explore future opportunities.

Conclusion: Following the four-part webinar series, students developed a stronger understanding of neuroscience research. These findings highlight the value of early, structured research exposure to enhance student research engagement.

ABSTRACT 8

Investigating the biology of cranial vault osteogenesis to inform the posterior vault distraction protocol in children with syndromic craniosynostosis

Sara Lonigro1, David Johnson2

1St Hugh’s College, University of Oxford, 2Oxford Craniofacial Unit, John Radcliffe Hospital, Oxford, England, United Kingdom.

Introduction: Premature sutural fusion, observed in craniosynostosis, is a developmental disorder found in isolation or in conjunction with genetic syndromes. Raised intracranial pressure is one of the most significant complications, requiring timely intervention to minimise the risk of seizures and cognitive impairment. By implementing the principles of distraction osteogenesis, originally intended for the appendicular skeleton, posterior vault distraction (PVD) provides a surgical means with which to increase the intracranial volume. Despite these benefits, PVD carries a risk of device related complications as the duration of the distraction protocol increases. Following removal of the distraction device, localised ossification defects have been observed at the distractor sites, suggesting that calvarial osteogenesis is influenced by distinct biochemical mechanisms compared to the long bones. This project aims to evaluate radiological evidence of device induced ossification defects in support of adapting current distraction protocols to the physiology of calvarial bones, reducing the risk of adverse postoperative outcomes.

Methods and Material: Postoperative X rays and computed tomography scans of patients who underwent PVD at the Oxford Craniofacial Unit were compared to map the original fixation sites of distraction devices against observed bony defects. Using syngo.via, three dimensional reconstructions, defect size was quantified and ossification was monitored over time.

Results: Extensive bony lacunae were identified at the original fixation sites of distraction devices. However, progressive ossification of such defects was observed bilaterally across all age groups and syndromes over time, suggesting that osteogenesis was only temporarily disrupted. Patients under 24 months and those in the non-syndromic group demonstrated a greater degree of ossification compared to their older counterparts with syndromic craniosynostosis.

Conclusion: Delayed ossification at fixation sites highlighted the key role of the dura in cranial osteogenesis, providing additional evidence towards adapting the distraction protocol to calvarial physiology. Further study is required to elucidate the role of age and syndrome in the ossification of calvarial defects.

ABSTRACT 9

Neurological outcomes and annual hemorrhage risk following microsurgery and stereotactic radiosurgery for brainstem cavernous malformations

Saim Chaudhry1, Fahmida Zahin2, Ibrahim Shibi2, Manas Rupakheti1, Michael Poon3,4, Mario Teo3

1The University of Edinburgh Medical School, Edinburgh, Scotland, 2University of Buckingham Medical School, Buckingham, 3Southmead Hospital, North Bristol NHS Trust, Bristol, England, 4Centre for Clinical Brain Sciences, University of Edinburgh, Edinburgh, Scotland, United Kingdom.

Introduction: Brainstem cavernous malformations (BSCMs) are challenging to manage because of their eloquent location. While there are ongoing studies comparing conservative and surgical management, the outcomes following microsurgery or stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) for BSCM are unclear. We summarised the reported neurological outcome and annual hemorrhage risk following microsurgery and SRS.

Methods: We searched PubMed, Medline, Embase, Cochrane and ClinicalTrials.gov on May 30, 2025 for cohort studies or clinical trials with 10 or more patients with BSCM reporting any outcomes 3 months or more after microsurgery or SRS. We focused on neurological outcome, defined as new, worsened or persistent neurological symptoms, and annual hemorrhagic rate (AHR) in this abstract.

Results: Of the 694 records retrieved, 113 studies reported outcomes at 3 months or more after BSCM treatment. Eighty-six studies comprising 6,677 BSCM patients reported neurological outcome, functional outcomes or AHR. There was one study that reported neurological outcomes for both microsurgery and SRS, suggesting the modern post 2004 surgery era had significantly fewer persisting new deficits compared to SRS. New, worsened or persistent neurological deficit was observed in 0–76.1% after microsurgery, with median follow-up of 3–97.1 months across 56 studies, and in 0–52.9% after SRS, with median follow-up of 21.5– 111.7 months across 13 studies. AHR was 0–7% following microsurgery, with a median follow-up of 9.7–102 months across 39 studies, and 0–4.8% following SRS, with a median follow-up of 21.5–111.7 months across 15 studies.

Conclusion: Allowing for the inherent selection bias of treatment modality for brainstem cavernoma, lack of randomisation and potential impact of confounding factors, current studies reported similar neurological outcomes following microsurgery and SRS with comparable follow-up time, though granularity is lacking. Microsurgery should be considered for those requiring treatment due to its overall benefits in the long term.
