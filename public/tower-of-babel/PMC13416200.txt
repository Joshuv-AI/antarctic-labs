Diminished Hepatitis B Antibody Response in Pediatrics With Type 1 Diabetes; the Need for Revaccination Protocol: A Case‐Control Study
Authors: Erfanehsadat Shahim, Rosemina Bahrololoom, Maryam Ataollahi, Marzieh Jamalidoust, Amirhossein Hasani, Homa Ilkhanipoor, Anahita Sanaei Dashti, Seyedeh Sedigheh Hamzavi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsHepatitis B virus (HBV) infection is a significant chronic infection in pediatric patients. Vaccination induces production of anti‐Hepatitis B surface antibodies, which serve as markers of protective immunity. Given the long‐term risks of cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma during adulthood, this study aimed to assess and compare seroprotection between children with type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) and nondiabetic participants.MethodsIn this case‐control study, 127 children with T1DM and 124 controls, all in a healthy immune state and fully vaccinated against HBV, were recruited from Shiraz University pediatric endocrinology clinics. Exclusion criteria included immunosuppression, incomplete vaccination, or hepatitis history in the child/family. Ethical approval was obtained from Shiraz University of Medical Sciences. Comparisons of sex, age, antibody titers, and diabetes indices used appropriate inferential tests; odds of inadequate antibody titers were assessed by logistic regression.ResultsConsidering an antibody titer of 10 mIU/mL as adequate, the non‐protection rate was 54.3% (95% CI: 45.3%–63.2%) in diabetic individuals and 28.2% (95% CI:20.5%–37.0%) in non‐diabetics. In a multivariable model controlling for time from vaccination, time from vaccination (OR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.06–1.49, p < 0.001), and diabetes status (OR = 2.34, 95% CI: 1.33–4.14, p = 0.002) contributed to inadequate antibody titers. Sex, glycemic control, and disease duration over 1 year had no significant effect.ConclusionChildren with type 1 Diabetes Mellitus exhibited lower anti‐HBs antibody titers compared to their nondiabetic counterparts, with no association found with sex or glycemic control. The timing of vaccination and diabetes status should be considered when evaluating immune responses to HBV vaccination in children.

Introduction

Type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) is the most common form of diabetes in the pediatric population, with an estimated global prevalence of 1.8 million affected children and adolescents (< 20 years) reported by 2024 [1]. The epidemiologic pattern follows a variable incident rate in different global regions [2]. Notably, European countries report some of the highest figures, with Finland topping the list at 64.2 cases per 100,000 children < 15 years old. With the exception of the United Arab Emirates, Saudi Arabia, and Oman, the incidence in the Middle East and North African countries, including Iran (16.2 per 100,000 children < 15 years), has been reported at a lower rate [3]. The number of affected cases has shown a concerning increasing trend over the past decades [4] and given the lifelong nature of the disease, it is crucial to understand its complications comprehensively.

T1DM is characterized by autoimmune destruction of pancreatic insulin‐secreting beta cells, leading to the deficiency of this hormone and lifelong dependency on its exogenous administration. The pathogenesis of the disease is believed to be affected by both genetic and environmental factors [5]. A basic understanding of the pathophysiology of the ailment might explain its complications. The autoimmune destruction of beta cells is believed to be mediated by CD8 + T‐cells. On the other hand, regulatory T cells are inhibited in these patients [6, 7]. T1DM is also speculated to be associated with some immune deficiency conditions, such as common variable immune deficiency (CVID) [8]. Patients with T1DM might show diminished response to vaccines due to this fact, like low antibody levels after the measles‐mumps‐rubella vaccine [9]. However, the association between T1DM and other infectious diseases like hepatitis B virus (HBV) still needs further clarification.

HBV infection remains a global health concern, having affected over two billion people up till now, with approximately 316 million individuals living with chronic hepatitis B (CHB) as of 2019 [10, 11]. With the introduction of vaccines, the global prevalence of CHB has decreased significantly [11], yet more than 820,000 people die from this disease each year, due to CHB complications such as hepatocellular carcinoma or cirrhosis [12]. HBV is known to be the primary cause of deaths from liver cancer [11]. To date, the most important measure for HBV prevention is vaccination against the virus [13]. The HBV vaccination program for Iranian children was officially initiated in 1993, providing immunization to all newborns at birth, 2, and 6 months [14, 15]. This program has had a significant impact on reducing the seroprevalence of HBV in Iran, reaching an approximate 0.09% for children under five by 2019 [11]. However, the overall prevalence across all ages can differ depending on the geographical area and among high‐risk populations, and many individuals might be unaware of their illness [14, 16, 17].

Unfortunately, HBV vaccination failure has been observed among some high‐risk groups [18]. Therefore, evaluation of immunity against HBV following immunization is recommended in populations who are at risk for incomplete response to HBV vaccines, including patients on hemodialysis, immunocompromised patients, and sexual partners of people living with HBV (PLWHB) [19]. For this matter, measurement of Hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) Antibody is recommended to be performed at least 2 months after the last dose of the vaccine [20] so that revaccination could be offered to patients with inadequate response to the vaccination [21, 22]. Given the altered immunity in T1DM patients and the higher risk of developing CHB in children under five [23], we aimed to evaluate HBV vaccine seroprotection in a group of pediatric patients with T1DM.

Methods

Participants, Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria, Ethical Approval and Consent

This case‐control study included 127 pediatric patients (under 18 years old) with T1DM, who participated from the pediatric endocrinology clinics at Shiraz University of Medical Sciences.

The inclusion criteria for our patients were a complete history of HBV vaccination, being in a clinically healthy immune state, and providing consent to participate. The exclusion criteria for our study were patients with a history of incomplete vaccination against HBV, a history of HBV in the patient or their family members, or a history of immunosuppression. Children who missed either one or two shots from the three‐dose routine national immunization program are classified as having incomplete immunization. A healthy immune state was assessed clinically and defined as the absence of active infection, ongoing inflammation, or any underlying diseases concerning the immune system other than T1DM at the time of recruitment.

Considering a similar study by Norouzirad et al. [24] and a type I error of 0.05 and study power of 80%, the sample size for this study to be statistically relevant was 124. To prevent the need for additional patient sampling, the convenience sampling method was used to establish a control group. The control group consisted of 124 pediatric patients with trauma or other non‐infectious acute conditions requiring sampling. Control group samples were excluded if the patient had an infectious disease, chronic medical condition, or immune system disorder at the time of blood sampling. Efforts were made to roughly match the control group to cases by age and sex to reduce potential confounding, although exact matching was not performed.

This study was carried out following the Helsinki Declaration of Human Rights and was approved by the Shiraz University of Medical Sciences ethics committee. Approval ID: IR.SUMS.MED.REC.1401.034. A written consent form was obtained from the T1DM patient's parents after informing them about the nature and the course of the study. In light of the retrospective nature of the study, utilization of residual blood samples and deidentification of patient information informed consent were waived for the control group by the ethics committee of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences.

Data Collection and Antibody Titer Assessment

Five cc of blood were taken from the patients to evaluate anti‐HBs antibodies. All of the tests were performed in the same laboratory. The blood sampling occurred as part of the routine follow‐up of the patients. Antibody detection was performed using Pishtazteb (PISHTAZTEB DIAGNOSTICS, Tehran, Iran, sensitivity 100% (CI = 99.5%), specificity 99%) ELISA kits, which utilize the antigen sandwich enzyme immunoassay method for anti‐HBs antibody detection. Antibody titers above 10 mIU/mL were considered responders.

Statistical Analysis

The primary analyses comparing anti‐HBs antibody response between children with T1DM and controls, as well as the assessment of odds for inadequate antibody response among participants, were prespecified. Subgroup analyses by age, sex, and diabetic characteristics were exploratory and aimed at examining potential effect modification. The data were entered into IBM SPSS version 26.0. The normality of the distribution of the quantitative variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov‐Smirnov test. The qualitative variables were reported as frequency and percentage. For quantitative variables, mean and standard deviation were provided for normal distributions, while median and the interquartile range (IQR) were provided for non‐normal distributions. A one‐sample binomial test calculated the 95% confidence interval (95% CI) for non‐response. Since children are vaccinated at birth in Iran, we equated each child's age with the time since they received the vaccine, using age since vaccination as the timing variable in our analyses. The comparison of the groups regarding response to vaccination was achieved using the chi‐squared test. The comparison of quantitative variables between groups was performed using the Mann‐Whitney U test for non‐normal distribution cases and the independent two‐sample t‐test for normal distribution cases.

A logistic regression analysis was performed to estimate odds ratios (OR) and 95% CI, adjusting for confounders and including other covariates of interest as predictors. Covariates that demonstrated a significant level of p < 0.2 in the univariable analysis were incorporated into the multivariable model. Age since vaccination was considered a key confounder given its influence on antibody titers over time. Since every child is vaccinated at birth in Iran, we considered each participant's chronological age to be equivalent to the duration or age since vaccination. Diabetic status, sex, age (categorized), duration of disease, and glycemic control were considered as possible predictors in the model. For the variables of diabetic status, the control group was designated as the reference group. For other covariates, females, the age group of 0‐5 years, less than a year of disease duration, and glycemic control of < 7.5 were designated as reference levels in all performed analyses. A two‐sided p‐value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The design of this study and the statistical analyses employed follow the epidemiologic and biostatistical principles outlined by Rothman et al. Modern Epidemiology, and Hosmer and Lemeshow applied Logistic Regression, respectively [25, 26].

Results

Basic Characteristics

A total of 251 individuals participated in this study, 127 of whom (50.6%) were T1DM patients, and 124 participants (49.4%) were nondiabetic. Among T1DM patients, 47.2% were male and 52.8% were female. For the control group, the gender distribution showed 58.9% males and 41.1% females. The mean age of our case group was 9 3.9, which met a significant difference (p < 0.001) compared to 6.6 ± 5.1 of the control group. The distribution of age groups differed considerably across the groups, showing a higher proportion of children less than 5 years old in the control group (p < 0.001) and more children above ten in the case group (p = 0.006). Assessment of Inadequate Anti‐HBs Antibody

The rates of inadequate anti‐HBs antibody response were compared between children with type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) and non‐diabetic control participants across different age and sex subgroups. T1DM patients had significant (p < 0.001) antibody titers less than 10 mIU/mL, which corresponds to a higher rate of inadequate response to vaccination (54.3%, n = 69; 95% CI: 45.3%–63.2%) compared to the control group (28.2%, n = 35, 95% CI: 20.5%–37.0%). In addition, Individuals with T1DM had a weaker immune response to vaccination across all age categories, with a statistically significant difference observed particularly in the ≤ 5 years (p = 0.002) and 6–10 years (p = 0.036) age groups when compared to the control cohort (Figure 1). Regarding gender, the case group exhibited a significantly lower humoral response to the vaccine in both sexes (p = 0.001 and p < 0.004 for males and females, respectively). Odds of Non‐Protection Among T1DM Participants

As Table 3 describes, in a univariable logistic regression model, the odds of an inadequate HBV antibody response in T1DM children were 3 times higher (OR = 3.02, 95% CI: 1.79−5.19, p < 0.001) compared to the non‐diabetic control group. Regarding age groups, those aged 6‐10 years and > 10 were significantly (p < 0.001), 4 (OR: 4.13, 95% CI: 2.09−8.15), and almost 6 (OR: 5.9, 95% CI: 2.96−11.94) times more likely to develop an inadequate antibody titer than those aged 0–5 years. Additionally, for each 1‐year increase in age since vaccination, the odds of an insufficient antibody titer increase by 18% (OR = 1.18, 95% CI: 1.11−1.25, p < 0.001). In the multivariable model, which controlled for the confounding effect of age since vaccination, and simultaneously evaluated diabetes status, sex, and categorized age as potential predictors, only age since vaccination (OR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.06–1.49, p = 0.006) and Diabetes status (OR = 2.34, 95% CI: 1.33−4.12, p = 0.003) demonstrated a significant effect.

Discussion

In this study, 251 participants were studied, and the titer of anti‐HBs antibody in children with T1DM and their non‐diabetic counterparts was evaluated. 54.3% of patients with T1DM and 28.2% of non‐diabetic participants did not have an adequate anti‐HBs antibody titer, despite being fully vaccinated, and this difference was statistically significant. This is while HBV vaccine's efficacy for the general population of Iranian children has been documented to vary, showing 67.9% in school‐aged children and up to 85%–94.8% in infants and children under five [27, 28, 29]. Additionally, our results suggest that by controlling for the confounding effect of covariates, both diabetes status and age since vaccination can significantly contribute to the reduction of antibody levels in children, both individually and collectively.

The findings of our study are in line with those of Silvestri, Elsharkawy, Elrashidy, and Ata, among others, who found that pediatric patients with T1DM showed a lower antibody response to the HBV vaccine compared to non‐diabetic children [30, 31, 32, 33]. This situation might be a result of the immune dysregulation in diabetic patients, as patients with T1DM have defective processing of HBs antigen; thus, immune B‐cells in diabetic patients fail to produce adequate amounts of antibody [34].

The reason for HBV unresponsiveness might be in specific genes, as more than 90% of the T1DM patients express HLA DR3/DQ2 or HLA DR4/DQ8 haplotypes, both of which have been found in poor responders to the HBV vaccine [35, 36, 37]. This relationship has also been found in patients with Celiac disease [35]. To date, no guidelines have been established for managing HBV non‐responders in the diabetic population.

In the study of Silvestri and colleagues, for example, a booster dose of HBV vaccination decreased the rate of non‐responsiveness, which resulted in a similar antibody titer of T1DM patients to the general population [30]; however, the long‐term follow‐up of the booster‐inoculated population is not available. Consequently, diabetic patients who receive additional vaccine doses may still need surveillance, especially if they are at high risk for HBV.

In a review article by Filippelli and coworkers, the authors assessed the efficacy of intradermal inoculation of the HBV vaccine in the non‐responder population. They found that patients with renal impairment, liver failure, HIV infection, and celiac disease benefit from this protocol. Still, the evidence regarding the efficacy of this route in diabetic patients is lacking. Intradermal injection facilitates the exposure of HBs antigens to dendritic cells, potentially boosting the immune response by bypassing helper T cells that exhibit HLA DR3/DQ2 or HLA DR4/DQ8. Such patients may not differ from the general population after an intradermal vaccine injection [38].

Moreover, some vaccine preparations do not have diminished response in diabetic patients, such as recombinant forms of HBV vaccine as used by Marseglia et al. [39]. Such vaccine forms can be used initially for patients with T1DM; however, their long‐term effects should be more thoroughly examined. Furthermore, since preparing such forms might be more expensive for healthcare systems, finding cost‐effective ways to prevent non‐responsiveness is prioritized. For example, if proven effective, intradermal vaccination might be superior to other methods.

Previous studies have reported a 40%–75% rate of unresponsiveness to HBV vaccination in diabetic patients, which aligns with our findings [30, 31, 32, 33, 35]. However, the difference in various studies might be due to differences in vaccines, laboratory kits, and methods of antibody measurements.

In this study, we categorized the study population into three groups based on age. We found that in the age group of less than 5 years old and in 5–10‐year‐olds, diabetic patients were more likely to have an inadequate response to the HBV vaccine. However, the difference was not statistically significant in the age group of participants older than 10 years. Also, as seen, the rate of non‐responsiveness in both groups of diabetic and non‐diabetic participants increased over time. This finding might be because the titer of anti‐HBs antibodies weans off over time [24, 40]. The diabetic patients showed diminished response earlier, but as non‐diabetic participants got older and their antibodies decreased over time, the difference between diabetics and non‐diabetics reduced.

Our findings demonstrated sex as a non‐significant predictor of inadequate vaccine response in the adjusted logistic regression model, even though females showed a trend toward better response to the vaccine. Estradiol hormones are known to lead females to generally develop more potent innate, humoral, and cellular immune responses to viral infections and vaccines than their male counterparts [41, 42]. While immunological sex‐based differences are well‐documented in the literature, these variations result from a complex interaction between sex chromosome‐linked genes and the impact of sex hormones. Notably, certain immunological differences become more pronounced after puberty, following an increase in sex hormones [43]. Since our cohort mainly includes pre‐pubertal children, the associated sex hormone variations might be less prominent, explaining the lack of significant immunological sex differences in response to the HBV vaccine. On the other hand, the HLA DR and DQ are located on autosomal chromosomes [44]. Therefore, it seems that non‐responsiveness to HBV vaccination in our participants may possibly be more related to their HLA genetic background and autoimmune status rather than distinctions linked to biological sex chromosomes or hormones.

We compared the patients with more than a year of duration of diabetes with those who were recently diagnosed with diabetes. In a univariable regression model, we found that the rate of unresponsiveness was higher in patients with longer disease duration. This finding might arise because patients with longer durations of disease are older, and age can be a confounding factor. By adjusting for age since vaccination in a multivariable model, more than a year of disease duration had no reduction effect on antibody titers. Similarly, in a previous study of young patients with insulin‐dependent diabetes mellitus vaccinated against hepatitis B, no significant association was found between diabetes duration and hepatitis B vaccine response, further supporting our hypothesis that age can be a confounding factor [36].

We also compared the patients with high and low levels of HbA1c regarding their response to the HBV vaccine. We found no increase in the likelihood of non‐responsiveness with a higher glycemic control. This finding indicates that the pathophysiologic pathway for poor response to vaccines might not be through impaired glucose metabolism. Thus, strengthening the speculation that some genes are involved in this condition that act via dysregulations in the immune system. Our results was also in line with the study's findings by Elrashidy and colleagues [32].

Limitations of the Study and Recommendations

First, this is a monocenter study. Second, we did not consider genetic variations as possible factors affecting the immune response to vaccination. Third, the control group was not perfectly age‐matched to the T1DM patients. Given that age is a significant confounding factor influencing antibody titers over time, we recommend further studies with a more rigorous age matching between cases and controls, a larger and more diverse population, and consideration of genetic factors affecting the immune system.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study found that pediatric patients with T1DM are more likely to have lower anti‐HBs antibody titers than their nondiabetic counterparts. This difference was not associated with sex or glycemic control. Both the timing of vaccination and diabetes status should be considered when evaluating immune response to HBV vaccination in children. Further studies should be conducted to assess the need for a booster HBV vaccine dose in children with diabetes.
