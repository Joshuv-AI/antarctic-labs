Combined Posteromedial Loop Stitch and All‐Inside Anterior Suture Technique for Medial Meniscus Ramp Lesion Repair
Authors: Catarina Reis Rodrigues, Tiago Puga, Carolina Oliveira, Francisco Guerra Pinto, Bruno Alpoim, Rómulo Silva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMedial meniscus ramp lesions have historically been underdiagnosed and often overlooked during arthroscopic procedures. Increasing recognition of their prevalence and biomechanical importance, especially with anterior cruciate ligament injuries, has recently made these lesions a key focus in knee surgery and research. Despite this awareness, management of medial meniscus ramp lesions remains challenging due to their complex anatomy and essential contribution to knee stability. Although various repair techniques are available, the optimal approach remains unknown. We describe a technique using a posteromedial loop stitch complemented by an anterior all‐inside suture, aiming to integrate the advantages of direct visualization with enhanced fixation strength. This hybrid method offers a reproducible option to optimize healing and biomechanical stability in ramp lesion repair.

Ramp lesions, defined as tears at the meniscocapsular junction of the posterior horn of the medial meniscus, are increasingly recognized as significant contributors to knee instability in patients with anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injuries. 1 Their anatomical location and often complex morphology create diagnostic challenges and complicate surgical management. 2 When untreated, ramp lesions may contribute to residual rotational laxity, increasing the risk of ACL graft failure and residual pain. 1 , 3

Despite advances in arthroscopic techniques, current clinical evidence does not favor a single superior method for repairing meniscal ramp lesions. Various strategies have showed comparable clinical efficacy when appropriately matched to specific lesion characteristics, such as size, complexity, and chronicity. 4 This variability underscores the need for tailored surgical approaches that balance biomechanical stability and biological healing, while addressing complex anatomy, limited visualization, and the demanding nature of precise suture placement.

In this context, we describe a hybrid technique combining a posteromedial loop stitch, providing direct visualization and precise meniscocapsular capture, with an anterior all‐inside repair. The posteromedial loop stitch is technically demanding, requiring expertise given the challenging anatomy and uncommon portal placement. Conversely, the all‐inside stitch, performed through a routine and familiar portal, offers accessible fixation that enhances construct stability (Figure 1).

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

The patient is placed in the supine position under general or spinal anesthesia, with the operative limb positioned in 90° of knee flexion and stabilized using a lateral thigh support to facilitate optimal arthroscopic access (Video 1). Following standard aseptic preparation and sterile draping, diagnostic arthroscopy is performed via standard anterolateral and anteromedial portals. The posteromedial compartment is accessed using the Gillquist maneuver, enhancing visualization of the meniscocapsular junction (Figure 2A). A high posteromedial portal is then established through an outside‐in technique, where a spinal needle precisely delineates the entry point just superior to the flexor tendons and approximately 1 cm posterior to the joint line, thereby minimizing risk of neurovascular injury. Subsequent to portal establishment, a meniscal rasp or arthroscopic shaver is introduced through the posteromedial portal to meticulously refresh the borders of the ramp lesion, removing devitalized tissue and augmenting the biological environment for healing (Figure 2B).

Start the repair with a loop stitch via the posteromedial portal (Figure 3A‐C). Insert a suture lasso device, such as the Champion Slider (Stryker, Mahwah, NJ, USA), preloaded with nonabsorbable suture through the posteromedial portal. The suture hook is advanced first through the peripheral capsular tissue and then through the posterior horn of the medial meniscus, capturing both structures in a trajectory that allows for robust circumferential fixation (Figure 4A). The suture is shuttled in a loop configuration, passing from posterior to anterior and emerging at the superior aspect of the meniscus. An arthroscopic sliding knot is then created by keeping the fixed suture strand short while wrapping the free suture end 3 times around the fixed strand, following the loop‐knot technique for secure fixation. This sliding knot is then tied under direct arthroscopic visualization to ensure precise anatomic coaptation and stable approximation of the lesion edges (Figure 4B).

Following execution of the posteromedial loop stitch, the arthroscope is relocated to the medial compartment thought the anterolateral portal to deliver a supplementary fixation. An all‐inside suture device, such as the AIR+ (Stryker, Mahwah, NJ, USA), is introduced targeting the ramp region. The device is advanced and deployed through the meniscocapsular junction, to reinforce the initial posteromedial repair, supplementing with a horizontal or vertical mattress configuration as dictated by the morphological characteristics of the lesion (Figure 3D‐F). Depending on the lesion's morphology, either a horizontal or vertical mattress suture configuration is employed. For more extensive or peripheral defects, a vertical mattress suture is preferred to provide enhanced stability (Figure 5), whereas smaller lesions are typically supplemented with a horizontal mattress suture, to yield a cross‐construct configuration. Suture tension is carefully modulated throughout to ensure the repair is both watertight and biomechanically stable.

The integrity of the repair is then thoroughly assessed with a probe from both the posteromedial and anterior portals, confirming adequate reduction, anatomical restoration, and construct stability along the entirety of the meniscocapsular interface. Any additional concomitant procedures, such as ACL reconstruction, are subsequently performed according to clinical necessity.

DISCUSSION

Meniscal ramp lesions pose unique therapeutic challenges due to their complex anatomical location at the meniscocapsular junction and their critical contribution to knee rotational stability, particularly in ACL deficient patients. 4 , 5

Recent clinical studies consistently show that various suture repair techniques for medial meniscus ramp tears produce favorable patient outcomes and maintain a strong safety profile. Current evidence supports active repair of ramp lesions ≥10 mm or unstable on probing, as untreated tears may compromise knee stability and long‐term function. Notably, recent work by Saint‐Etienne et al. has highlighted repair efficacy differences based on tear morphology with medial repairs showing higher failure rates than lateral ones despite equivalent overall healing potential. 6 A 2025 classification system further refines meniscal tear assessment by integrating morphology (e.g., longitudinal, radial, bucket‐handle), stability, tissue quality, and laterality to predict outcomes and guide all‐inside repair techniques. 7

The widespread adoption of all‐inside meniscal repair devices stems primarily from their minimally invasive approach, which shortens operative time and avoids posteromedial incisions, thereby significantly reducing neurovascular risks. 8 Furthermore, clinical studies and meta‐analyses show that all‐inside repairs achieve healing rates, patient‐reported outcomes, and complication profiles comparable to or better than traditional inside‐out methods, with consistently high return‐to‐sport rates and low rates of long‐term reoperation. 9 These systems provide strong initial fixation and favorable biomechanical pull‐out strength when applied to meniscal injuries 10 (Table 1). Cadaveric analyses confirm that these repairs can restore lesion contact and minimize gapping, step‐off, and perform well biomechanically under repetitive loading conditions that simulate postoperative rehabilitation. 11 However, key limitations include restricted visualization from anterior portals and reliance on precise device deployment, which may compromise anatomical capsular incorporation in complex tears. 4 Recent biomechanical data also suggest that all‐inside devices with a horizontal trajectory may not adequately capture the meniscotibial ligament, potentially limiting their capacity to anatomically reduce and stabilize the ramp complex, especially in tears with significant capsular components. 11

Advantages and Disadvantages

Posteromedial loop stitch repairs offer clear advantages of direct visualization (Table 1), allowing precise assessment and debridement of ramp morphology and circumferential suture passage to securely approximate both the meniscus and capsule. 12 Reports consistently show robust tissue approximation and a reduction in residual gapping—features necessary for successful biomechanical and biological healing. 13 , 14 Repair systems such as suture hook techniques remain preferable when the meniscotibial ligament/capsule capture is essential. 13 , 14 Pioger et al. showed that ramp repair using a posteromedial portal suture hook technique is associated with a low secondary meniscectomy rate. 15 Nonetheless, the requirement for an additional posteromedial portal introduces technical complexity and the potential for neurovascular injury and thus demands surgeon expertise. 16 Importantly, long‐term clinical data show that posteromedial ramp lesion repair carries a significant failure rate, with about half of suture failures occurring within 5 years and rates remaining high at 10 years. 12 Another limitation is the difficulty of placing multiple stitches, as visualization progressively deteriorates after meniscal approximation and suture of the meniscocapsular junction.

Given the heterogeneity of ramp lesion patterns, a one‐size‐fits‐all repair strategy is rarely adequate. Larger, multiplanar, or degenerative lesions may not be adequately stabilized by isolated all‐inside or loop stitch techniques alone. Although direct biomechanical evidence for ramp lesion repair constructs is limited, it is hypothesized—based on clinical reasoning and principles established in broader meniscal repair literature—that multipoint fixation may enhance construct stability, improve resistance to cyclic loads, and promote healing compared with single‐modality methods.

Our hybrid technique, integrating a posteromedial loop stitch for circumferential, direct anatomic repair with anterior all‐inside reinforcement to augment pull‐out strength and stability, aims to harness these complementary strengths. This approach seems particularly valuable in complex lesions, providing both meticulous tissue approximation and multidirectional biomechanical support (Table 2).

This hybrid approach supports the trend toward personalized, multipoint fixation to enhance healing in complex ramp patterns, though it requires biomechanical and clinical validation.
