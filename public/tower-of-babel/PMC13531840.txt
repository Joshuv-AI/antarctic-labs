Effect of a peer support group intervention on medication adherence, self- care practices, and diabetes knowledge among patients with diabetes in Northern Ethiopia: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Halefom Kahsay Haile, Bruck Messele Habte, Teferi Gedif Fenta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiabetes mellitus is a chronic condition that requires consistent adherence to treatment, regular self-care, and adequate knowledge of the disease to prevent complications and improve quality of life. However, many patients struggle to maintain optimal adherence and integrate self-care into their daily routines. Nowadays, many diabetes self-management modalities have emerged as a promising approach to address these gaps. This randomized controlled study evaluated the effect of a structured peer support program on medication adherence, self-care practices, and diabetes-related knowledge among patients with diabetes who attended follow-up at a tertiary hospital.MethodsA randomized controlled trial was conducted on 97 (intervention group = 48, control group = 49) adults with diabetes who were attending follow-up at Ayder Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (ACSH). Participants were randomly assigned to either a peer-support intervention group or a control group and followed for six months. The intervention comprised three structured peer-led sessions that focused on self-care, medication adherence, and diabetes knowledge. Outcomes were measured at baseline and follow-up using the Morisky Medication Adherence Scale (MMAS-8), adopted Diabetes self-care Activities (SDSCA) scale, and diabetes knowledge test (DKT). Data analysis was performed using chi-square tests, paired-sample t-tests, and independent-sample t-tests to compare baseline characteristics and evaluate intervention effects. Difference-in-differences (DiD) analysis was used to compare the intervention and control groups across outcome variables.ResultCompared with the control group, participants in the intervention group demonstrated significant improvements across all outcome measures. Medication adherence increased by a net difference-in-differences estimate of (DiD = + 1.5 points, p < 0.001) on the MMAS-8 scale. Self-care practices improved by (DiD = + 1.3 days per week of recommended behaviors, p < 0.001). Diabetes knowledge scores also showed substantial gains, among both the insulin users (DiD = + 4.9-point, p < 0.001) and non-insulin users (DiD = + 3.4-points, p < 0.001) of the intervention group compare to controls.ConclusionIntegrating structured peer-support interventions into routine diabetes care may provide a practical approach to improving patient outcomes while optimizing limited healthcare resources in low-resource settings. Future research should evaluate the sustainability, scalability, and cost-effectiveness of peer-support education model, together with their long-term impact on quality of life.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12875-026-03430-2.

Back ground

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a chronic metabolic disorder and one of the fastest-growing global health challenges, with the number of affected individuals projected to reach 643 million by 2030 and 783 million by 2045 [1]. Poorly controlled diabetes is associated with serious acute and chronic complications, which contribute substantially to morbidity and mortality worldwide. Sub-Saharan Africa is experiencing a rapid increase in DM prevalence, driven by urbanization, lifestyle changes, and limited access to preventive and treatment services [2]. Effective diabetes management requires sustained medication adherence, adequate self-care practices, and sufficient disease-related knowledge to maintain optimal glycemic control and prevent complications. In Ethiopia, diabetes represents a growing public health challenge, yet many patients fail to achieve adequate disease control [3].

Studies conducted in Ethiopia have documented low levels of medication adherence, limited diabetes knowledge, and inadequate self-care practices among patients with diabetes [3–5]. In Ethiopia, only 46% of patients with type 1 diabetes (T1DM) and 39% of those with type 2 diabetes (T2DM) practice at least two-thirds of the recommended self-care behaviors [4]. Self-care is a key component of diabetes management and includes behaviors such as adherence to dietary recommendations, physical activity, glucose monitoring, foot care, and compliance with prescribed medications [6]. In this study, self-care practice was assessed as the frequency of recommended diabetes self-management activities performed per week, reflecting patients’ ability to implement daily disease-management behaviors. However, consistent self-care remains difficult to achieve in routine clinical settings, particularly in resource-limited environments where patients often have limited access to structured diabetes education [7, 8]. Similarly, treatment adherence among patients with chronic diseases is estimated at about 50% in developed countries and is likely lower in resource-limited settings [9]. Beyond adherence and self-care, adequate diabetes-related knowledge is a critical component of successful disease management. However, low levels of diabetes knowledge have been documented across multiple settings [10, 11]. In Ethiopia, more than half of patients with diabetes have been reported to have poor knowledge, particularly regarding symptoms, causes, and disease management strategies [5]. These gaps in adherence to medication, self-care, and knowledge increase the risk of poor glycemic outcomes and diabetes-related complications, highlighting the need for effective and sustainable educational interventions.

In response, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends peer support as a practical strategy to strengthen chronic disease self-management by combining professional guidance with experiential knowledge from patients who successfully manage their condition [6]. Peer support interventions provide emotional encouragement, practical problem-solving, and shared experiences that can improve patients’ confidence and engagement in disease management [12]. Ubiquitous findings revealed that peer education has been effective in improving treatment adherence in other chronic conditions such as HIV/AIDS [13], depression [14] and schizophrenia [15]. Parallelly, evidence from systematic reviews and interventional studies indicates that peer-support interventions can improve medication adherence, self-management behaviors, and diabetes-related knowledge [16–18]. Although, few studies have reported insignificant effects on patient outcomes [19, 20], many findings from Sub-Saharan Africa have showed improvements in diabetes self-management behaviors and treatment adherence following peer-support interventions [18, 21, 22].

Despite growing evidence supporting peer-support interventions, studies evaluating structured peer-led educational programs in tertiary healthcare settings in Ethiopia are scarce. Moreover, there is limited evidence on how peer-support interventions can be effectively implemented within routine diabetes care in resource-constrained environments. Moreover, there is limited understanding of how peer-support interventions can be effectively implemented in resource-constrained health systems. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effect of a structured peer-led educational intervention on medication adherence, self-care practices, and diabetes-related knowledge among adult patients with diabetes mellitus attending a tertiary hospital in Northern Ethiopia. Medication adherence was considered the primary outcome while self-care practices and diabetes-related knowledge were assessed as secondary outcomes. The findings may help inform the integration of peer-support strategies into routine diabetes care in similar resource-limited settings.

Methods

Study setting

The study was conducted at Ayder Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (ACSH), the largest referral hospital in the Tigray region, situated in Mekelle city, about 780 km north of Addis Ababa, Ethiopia’s capital. ACSH serves an estimated 10 million people from Tigray and neighboring regions, including Amhara and Afar. With a capacity of 500 beds, the hospital manages approximately 10,000 inpatient admissions and 100,000 outpatient visits annually. Despite being a tertiary referral center, ACSH operates in a resource-constrained post-conflict setting, where shortages of medical supplies, trained personnel, and diagnostic services remain common. Therefore, the hospital reasonably reflects the realities of resource-limited healthcare systems in the region and provides a practical setting to evaluate scalable interventions like peer support programs. The diabetic clinic at ACSH is a major center for diabetes management, providing follow-up care for around 5,000 patients.

Study design and period

A randomized controlled trial (RCT) design was carried out for six months duration. This trial has been registered and can be accessed at: https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07145983 which initially released on August 21, 2025.

Study population and eligibilities

The study population of this trial was consisted two arm groups of patients with diabetes mellitus aged 18–65 years. The intervention group were those who received the peer-led educational sessions on top of the standard care while the control group were those who received the standard care only. Eligible participants for both the intervention and control groups were individuals with diabetes mellitus who had been on at least one antidiabetic medication and had a most recent HbA1c value of ≥ 7.5%, determined through point-of-care testing at recruitment. Participants were also required to have regular follow-up visits at ACSH during the study period. The exclusion criteria were those whose; (1) unwilling to participate in any of the group; (2) with concurrent endocrine disorders (thyroid disease, obesity, or gestational diabetes) and with chronic diseases (cardiac heart failure, hepatitis and cancer); (3) enrolled in other educational programs during the study period; (4) Health professionals with diabetes to minimize potential bias related to prior knowledge and training.

Sample size determination and randomization

The sample size was calculated using the statistical superiority design formula for continuous variables: n = [(Z₁ + Z₂)² × (2 S²)] / d². Assuming a 95% confidence level (Z₁ = 1.96) and 80% power (Z₂ = 0.84) [23] with a standard deviation(S) of 4 derived from the Morisky Medication Adherence Scale (MMAS-8) and a minimum clinically important difference of (d = 2 points), the minimum required sample size was estimated and enrolled about 60 participants per group during routine diabetic follow-up visits [24]. However, a total of 100 participants were found eligible and randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the intervention (n = 50) or control group (n = 50) after baseline assessment. The randomization sequence was generated using a computer-based random number generator by a researcher who was not involved in participant recruitment or outcome assessment. Allocation concealment was ensured using sequentially numbered, sealed opaque envelopes prepared in advance according to the randomization sequence. After enrollment and baseline data collection, each participant was assigned to a study group by opening the next envelope in sequence.

Standard care

Standard care services are offered to more than 100 patients with diabetes who visit the diabetic clinic of ACSH. The services are offered two days in a week by a team of two senior physicians, residents and five nurses. The services include scheduled consultations with a physician, laboratory investigations and medication refills with standard pharmacy care during appointment follow up. Although, newly diagnosed patients receive intensive follow-up during the first six months, follow-up appointments are typically scheduled every two to three months. Antidiabetic treatments are adjusted based on fasting blood glucose and HbA1c results.

Peer support intervention and study procedures

The peer-support intervention was delivered by five trained peer educators selected from patients attending the diabetic clinic. Five peer educators were recruited to facilitate/lead small-group sessions of 10 participants each, allowing interactive learning and effective peer engagement. These individuals were selected in advance and remained consistent throughout the intervention period [25]. Peer educators were required to have at least three years of diabetes experience, near stable glycemic control (HbA1c < 7.5%), a minimum secondary-level education and documented regular clinic follow-up at ACSH. These criteria were used to ensure that peer educators had sufficient self-management experience and communication ability to effectively support other peoples with DM. The selection approach was consistent with established peer-support models recommending the recruitment of experienced and stable individuals living with DM who can provide practical and experiential support to their peers [26, 27].

The selected peer supporters attended a structured two-day training program adapted from the International Diabetes Federation peer leader manual [28]. The training was contextualized to the local setting using simple, patient-friendly educational materials and culturally appropriate examples, such as locally available foods and common daily lifestyle practices. This manual has included instruction on session delivery, communication skills, and role-playing exercises to prepare peer educators for effective participant engagement. The training and intervention sessions were conducted in the local language (Tigrigna). Educational content was adapted to the local context and focused on medication adherence, self-care practices, and diabetes knowledge using culturally relevant examples and practical recommendations. To ensure intervention fidelity and consistency across peer educators, standardized educational materials and structured session guides were used. Peer educators were supervised by the research team throughout the intervention period. When questions arose beyond the peer educators’ knowledge, participants were referred to clinic healthcare professionals for clarification.

The intervention consisted of three structured face-to-face 1–2 h group sessions delivered during routine follow-up visits at the 2nd, 4th, and 6th months in a separate room within the hospital to minimize additional travel burden and enhance feasibility. Each session addressed medication adherence, self-care practices, and diabetes knowledge, with reinforcement of previously discussed topics. Each group member was expected to participate in all the three sessions. Detailed training content and session procedures are provided in Supplementary File 1. Since intervention sessions were conducted separately and control participants attended only their standard care visits, the likelihood of contamination between groups was minimized. The principal investigator conducted close follow-up of the intervention sessions to document the topics covered during each session and address any challenges encountered during the implementation. Monthly debriefing sessions were conducted with the peer educators to discuss their experiences and challenges. Feedback on their performance was provided based on each group sessions.

Data collection, instruments and outcome measures

Four clinical pharmacists who were blinded to the group assignments were recruited to collect the data. All baseline data, including survey responses and clinical measurements, were collected prior to the start of the intervention, and follow-up data were collected after completion of the peer support program (endline data). Data were collected in a private room within the diabetic clinic of ACSH from participants’ medical records and interviews the data that were collected included; participants demographic and clinical data as well as outcome variables including medication adherence, self-care practices and diabetes knowledge. Medication adherence was the primary outcome while diabetes self-care practices and diabetes knowledge were included as secondary outcomes in this study. Medication adherence of each group was assessed through a mixed method using the Morisky Medication Adherence Scale (MMAS-8) and self-reported pill count. The MMAS-8 consisted of seven dichotomous items and one item rated on a five-point Likert scale. To minimize acquiescence bias (‘yes-saying’), each ‘no’ response was assigned one point, except for item 5, which was reverse-coded. The eighth item provided five response options, scored from 0 to 1 in 0.25-point increments. Based on the total score, patients were classified as low adherers (score < 6), medium adherers (score 6 to < 8), or high adherers (score = 8) [29]. The MMAS-8 was used with permission requested from Morisky Medication Adherence Research LLC. In addition, pill count data were collected at each assessment point to objectively assess medication adherence by comparing the number of remaining pills against the expected consumption based on the prescribed dosing schedule. Patients with adherence below 80% were classified as poor adherent, and those with above 80% were classified as good adherent (Supplementary file 2).

Self-care practices were measured using the modified Summary of Diabetes Self-Care Activities (SDSCA) scale with 10 items, which captures five key domains of diabetes management. General diet was measured as the mean of two items, while specific diet included two items, one of which was reverse-coded, Exercise, blood-glucose testing, foot-care, health care provider recommendations were each evaluated by averaging two respective items [30].(Supplementary File 3).

Diabetes-related knowledge was assessed using a structured questionnaire adapted from the Diabetes Knowledge Test (DKT) [31]. The DKT presents six dimensions in which the details are Summarized in Supplementary File 4. The instrument included 14 core items administered to all participants, with an additional 9 items for insulin users. Thus, non-insulin users were scored out of 14 points, while insulin users (both T1DM and T2DM) were scored out of 23 points. Each correct response was scored as 1, and incorrect or “don’t know” responses were scored as 0. Participants were classified as having good knowledge if their score exceeded the mean, and poor knowledge if their score was at or below the mean.

Data quality assurance and management

Data quality was ensured through several procedures. The data collection tools were adapted from previously validated international instruments and translated into Tigrigna followed by back-translation into English to maintain consistency. The questionnaire was reviewed by clinical and research experts to ensure content validity and cultural appropriateness. A pretest was conducted on 5% of diabetic patients at a health facility outside the study area, and necessary modifications were made accordingly. Data collectors had received standardized two days training on the study procedures and interviewing techniques before the data collection period. Continuous supervision and daily checking of completed questionnaires were conducted to ensure completeness and consistency. The internal consistency reliability of the scales was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient with an overall Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.79.

Data analysis

Data were entered into Epi-Data version 4.6 and exported to SPSS version 25 for analysis. Descriptive statistics such as frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations were used to summarize sociodemographic, clinical, and behavioral characteristics of participants. Baseline comparisons for the continuous and categorical variables between intervention and control groups were conducted using independent t-tests and chi-square tests respectively. Medication adherence, diabetes knowledge, and self-care practice scores were calculated according to their respective validated scales. The effect of the peer support intervention within-group and between-group was assessed and comparisons were performed. Paired t-tests were used to determine changes in the outcome variables within the group while independent sample t-tests were applied to compare mean differences between groups. Furthermore, a Difference-in-Differences (DiD) approach was employed to evaluate the net effect of the intervention by comparing the changes in outcomes from baseline to endline between the intervention and control groups.

Results

Although 50 patients with diabetes were initially recruited for each study group, one participant from the control group and two participants from the intervention group were later withdrawn from the study (Fig. 1). Thus, among participants allocated to the intervention group, 48 (96%) attended all three scheduled peer-support sessions, indicating excellent adherence to the intervention protocol while 49 (98%) of control group participants completed the study.

In terms of medication adherence measured by MMAS-8, the proportion of patients with good adherence in the intervention group improved substantially from 2.1% at baseline to 16.7% at endpoint, while in the control group the change was minimal (4.1% to 6.1%). Conversely, poor adherence decreased in the intervention group from 60.4% to 33.3%, whereas it remained high in the control group (79.6% to 77.6%). These differences were statistically significant (p = 0.04). Similarly, pill count adherence showed a favorable change in the intervention group, with the proportion of patients classified as adherent increasing from 83.3% to 87.5%. In contrast, the control group showed only a slight improvement (67.3% to 71.4%), with the difference between groups reaching statistical significance (p = 0.03) (Table 2).

As shown in Fig. 2, The difference-in-differences (DiD) analysis demonstrated significant improvements in the intervention group compared to controls across the primary outcome and the secondary outcomes. Medication adherence improved by + 2.1 units versus + 0.6 in controls (DiD = + 1.5; p < 0.001). Self-care practices increased by + 1.8 days/week versus + 0.5 (DiD = + 1.3; p < 0.001). Among insulin users, diabetes knowledge improved by + 8.0 points compared with + 3.1 (DiD = + 4.9; p < 0.001), while among non-insulin users, knowledge scores increased by + 4.7 versus + 1.3 (DiD = + 3.4; p < 0.001). A subgroup analysis was conducted to evaluate whether the intervention effects differed between participants with T1DM and T2DM. Although improvements were observed in both primary and secondary outcomes, the differences in outcome changes between the two groups were not statistically significant.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that a peer-led educational support intervention produced meaningful improvements in the primary outcome (medication adherence) as well as secondary outcomes (self-care practices and diabetes knowledge) among patients with diabetes. While the intervention demonstrated benefits for participants with both T1DM and T2DM, those in the intervention group experienced significantly greater improvements in the primary and secondary outcomes compared with the control group. This finding suggests that peer-support interventions may be applicable across different diabetes types in routine clinical care.

In particular, medication adherence which is the primary outcome of this study, improved markedly among patients who received the intervention, indicating a clear peer support intervention effect beyond routine care. These findings are consistent with those reported in a study conducted in Iran, a middle-income country, where peer education significantly improved medication adherence and quality of life among patients with chronic conditions like hypertension [32]. Although the intervention improved medication adherence in the present study, the magnitude of improvement was smaller compared with reports from some high-income settings. This difference may be attributable to contextual factors such as shorter intervention duration, lower baseline health literacy, and socioeconomic constraints that can affect patients’ capacity to implement recommended self-management practices. Nevertheless, the observed consistency of benefit across settings reinforces the value of peer-led education as a feasible and effective strategy for improving treatment adherence in resource-limited environments. From a practice perspective, integrating peer education into routine diabetes care may offer a low-cost, culturally acceptable approach to strengthen adherence and support long-term disease management.

Moreover, the present study has revealed significant improvement in secondary outcomes like self-care practices among patients with diabetes who received peer-led education compared with those in the control group. Following the intervention, participants in the intervention group showed notable gains across multiple self-care domains, including an increased proportion reporting consistent daily self-care activities related to diabetes management and prevention of complications. These findings are consistent with previous studies indicating that peer education can enhance diabetes self-management behaviors. For example, a quasi-experimental study from Iran reported a significant improvement in self-care scores among patients with diabetes following peer-led education [33]. In addition, a systematic review by Dale and coworkers showed that peer support interventions were associated with statistically significant improvements in specific self-care behaviors, particularly physical activity, in some trials. However, the review also highlighted ongoing uncertainties regarding the suitability of peer support for different populations and the optimal design of intervention components across settings [34]. In general, randomized controlled trials have shown that diabetes self-management education interventions can improve physical activity levels and dietary practices among participants compared with usual care [35–37]. While these studies evaluated general educational interventions rather than peer-support models specifically, they provide supportive evidence that structured diabetes education can improve self-care behaviors. However, the magnitude of improvement observed in our peer-support intervention was smaller than that reported in some other studies evaluating broader diabetes self-management education programs [38]. This difference may be partly explained by contextual factors such as financial limitations and lower educational attainment among participants, which can constrain the ability to implement recommended self-care behaviors [39]. Nevertheless, the peer-support intervention in this study was culturally adapted, embedded within routine diabetes follow-up, and delivered through regular, structured interactions. These features may have contributed to the observed improvements in self-care practices despite existing resource constraints.

Evidence from African settings further supports the effectiveness of peer-support models. Evaluations from countries such as Cameroon, Uganda, and South Africa have documented improvements in dietary behaviors and selected clinical outcomes among patients with diabetes participating in peer-support programs [26, 27, 40]. Although many of these studies employed before-and-after designs and may be subject to methodological limitations, their findings are broadly consistent with our results, which demonstrate meaningful gains in self-care behaviors following a structured peer-led intervention [27].

This study has also verified a significant improvement in mean diabetes knowledge scores among participants in the intervention group compared with the control group. The observed gain indicates that the peer-led educational support effectively enhanced patients’ understanding of diabetes and its management. Similar improvements have been reported in studies from China and the Philippines, where peer-led diabetes education resulted in higher knowledge scores compared with usual care [41, 42]. These effects were largely attributed to the use of culturally appropriate educational materials and interactive learning approaches. In addition, evidence from a Cochrane review supports the effectiveness of group-based diabetes self-management education in improving diabetes-related knowledge [43]. Importantly, our findings depict the role of peer led intervention intensity and continuity in improving patient outcomes. The structured and interactive peer-support sessions, which were delivered regularly and integrated into routine follow-up care, may have contributed to these improvements through ongoing reinforcement of self-management behaviors and patient engagement. In contrast, some studies conducted in African settings have reported limited or no improvements in diabetes knowledge following peer-support interventions, including studies from Mali and Kenya [18, 44]. The differences in findings may be related to variations in intervention design, participant engagement, educational materials, and integration with routine clinical care rather than session frequency alone. These observations suggest that the effectiveness of peer-support interventions depends not only on the number of sessions but also on how the intervention is implemented and adapted to the local context through the use of local language, culturally relevant dietary examples, and integration with routine clinic follow-up visits, which may have enhanced participant engagement and learning.

The findings of this study suggest that peer support may complement conventional physician-led counselling by addressing practical and social barriers to diabetes self-management. In Ethiopia, routine diabetes care is often constrained by shortages of trained healthcare providers, limited consultation time, and inadequate educational resources, which restrict opportunities for comprehensive patient counseling and follow-up. Studies conducted in Ethiopian health facilities have documented insufficient diabetes education, long waiting times, fragmented services, and limited availability of self-management support as major barriers to effective diabetes care [45, 46]. In such settings, peer supporters who share similar lived experiences with diabetes may provide practical guidance and culturally meaningful examples that complement professional advice. Communication in the local language and the opportunity for interactive discussion may improve patients’ understanding and confidence in managing their condition. Moreover, peer-led education allows repeated reinforcement of key messages that may not be feasible during routine clinic visits.

The sustainability of peer-support interventions is an important consideration for long-term diabetes management. In this study, the intervention was integrated into routine diabetes follow-up visits and delivered by trained peer educators recruited from the same clinic population, which enhances feasibility and reduces the need for additional resources. Although the structured peer-support sessions formally ended after six months, participants were encouraged to maintain informal peer interactions, which may help sustain behavioral changes over time. The use of standardized training materials and structured session guides further supports the potential scalability of the intervention. Within the Ethiopian healthcare context, peer-support models may therefore provide a practical and cost-efficient strategy to strengthen diabetes self-management support within existing services, particularly in resource-constrained settings like Tigray. However, longer-term studies are needed to evaluate sustainability and integration into primary healthcare systems.

In spit of the positive finding of this study, several limitations should be considered in this trial. First, although the sample size was sufficient to detect intervention effects, the study was conducted at a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the results to other healthcare settings in Ethiopia. Future multicenter studies involving larger and more diverse populations would strengthen the evidence on the effectiveness of peer-support interventions. Second, the relatively short follow-up period did not allow assessment of the long-term sustainability of improvements in diabetes knowledge and self-management behaviors. In addition, although peer educators received standardized training and followed structured session guides, potential variability in communication style, motivation, and facilitation skills among the peer supporters may have influenced the consistency of intervention delivery. Despite these limitations, the findings suggest that a structured peer-led educational program can effectively improve key diabetes management outcomes in resource-limited settings and may support the integration of peer-support approaches into routine diabetes care.

Conclusion

The findings of the present study demonstrated significant improvements in medication adherence, self-care practices, and diabetes knowledge over the six-month intervention period. However, longer-term follow-up studies are needed to determine whether these improvements can be sustained and translated into broader outcomes such as self-efficacy, quality of life, and clinical indicators. From a research perspective, extending the duration of follow-up would allow evaluation of the durability and scalability of peer-support interventions. At the policy level, the observed effectiveness of this structured peer-support model suggests that such interventions could be considered for integration into routine diabetes care programs. Therefore, given its effectiveness and flexibility, peer-led education support may serve as a complementary strategy to standard diabetes care in tertiary hospital diabetes clinics, particularly in resource-constrained healthcare settings where task-shifting approaches may offer a feasible and cost-effective solution.
