Matrix concentration inequalities for time-inhomogeneous Markov chains
Luca Zanetti

Abstract
. We establish Chernoff-type bounds for the largest eigenvalue of sums of Hermitian random matrices generated by a time-inhomogeneous Markov chain. Our primary regime assumes a compact state space and contractivity of each Markov kernel in Wasserstein distance, i.e., positive Ollivier-Ricci curvature. This assumption is convenient to verify in inhomogeneous settings and is satisfied by several chains of practical interest, such as stochastic gradient descent on strongly convex smooth objectives. We also develop analogous bounds for noncompact state spaces under a notion of spectral gap for inhomogeneous chains introduced by Saloff-Coste and Zuniga. Finally, we illustrate the utility of our results through an analysis of the Elo rating system, a popular method for ranking players in sports analytics, under a dynamic version of the Bradley-Terry-Luce model.

Primary 60J05; Secondary 60B20, 60F10, 62F07.

1.
Introduction

Concentration inequalities are fundamental tools in the design and analysis of learning and randomised algorithms.
Although originally developed for sums of i.i.d. real-valued random variables, substantial progress over the last three decades has extended this theory to (i) dependent, in particular Markov-dependent, random variables
[
10
,
19
,
13
,
25
]
; (ii) high-dimensional observables, notably vector- and matrix-valued random variables
[
1
,
33
]
.
More recently,
9
combined these lines of work to obtain a Chernoff bound for matrix-valued observables of a finite Markov chain. This has been further generalised to arbitrary state spaces and time-dependent observables by
21
.

Most of the progress in the Markovian setting, however, has focused on time-homogeneous chains: homogeneity allows one to exploit approaches based on spectral methods
[
10
,
19
,
18
,
11
,
9
,
21
]
or the Poisson equation
[
12
,
7
,
26
]
, which do not readily extend to the inhomogeneous setting.

Motivated by this gap, we study concentration inequalities for matrix-valued observables on time-inhomogeneous Markov chains.
Our main focus will be on chains contractive in Wasserstein distance, i.e., positively curved according to
24
. While positive curvature is a strong notion of convergence and is not ubiquitously satisfied, several important Markov chains arising in algorithm design do exhibit positive curvature, such as Glauber dynamics for the high-temperature Ising model
[
24
]
and stochastic gradient descent on strongly convex and smooth functions
[
6
]
(see
[
8
,
13
]
for further examples). Crucially, positive curvature is a
local-in-time
property, in the sense that it can be verified for each Markov kernel
P_{t}
individually, rather than requiring control of the entire evolution of the chain. This makes it well suited to the time-inhomogeneous setting, where it is often difficult to establish properties about the global dynamics of the chain.

To state our first main result in an informal fashion, consider a (possibly inhomogeneous) Markov chain
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on a Polish metric space
(\Omega,d)
with bounded diameter
D
, whose Markov kernels
(P_{t})_{t\geq 1}
contract in Wasserstein distance by a factor
(1-\kappa)
. Let
F_{t}
be
m\times m
Hermitian-valued observables with uniform Lipschitz constant

\mathrm{Lip}(F_{t})\leq L
(where the Lipschitz constant is defined with respect to the operator norm). We establish the following concentration inequality on the largest eigenvalue of the empirical averages of these observables:

(1)

\mathbb{P}\Big(\lambda_{\max}\Big(\sum_{t=1}^{n}(F_{t}(X_{t})-\mathbb{E}{F_{t}(X_{t})})\Big)\geq n\varepsilon\Big)\leq m^{\,2-\frac{\pi}{4}}\,\exp\left(-\frac{Cn\kappa\varepsilon^{2}}{L^{2}D^{2}}\right)\qquad\text{for all }\varepsilon\geq 0,

where
C>0
is some universal constant.
Essentially, we obtain sub-Gaussian concentration with variance proxy
O(L^{2}D^{2}\kappa^{-1})
.

To prove (
1
), we control the matrix moment generating function via the multivariate
trace inequality of
9
(a many-matrix extension of Golden–Thompson), reducing the problem to bounding the trace of a product of Markov-dependent matrices. We then generalise the “direct method” of
19
to the matrix and inhomogeneous setting: the main idea of this technique, which Lezaud attributes to Bakry and Ledoux, is to iteratively centre the functions on which the operators
P_{1},\dots,P_{n-1}
act. Our simple but key observation is that, for a centred matrix-valued
\widetilde{F}
, we can bound
\sup_{x}\|\widetilde{F}(x)\|_{\mathrm{op}}
by the diameter and the Lipschitz constant of
\widetilde{F}
. We then exploit the fact that positive curvature implies each kernel
P_{t}
is contractive in the Lipschitz seminorm, and this contraction extends to matrix-valued functions as well.

One of the limitations of
Eq.

1
is that it depends on the diameter of the space. In
Theorem

8
we improve the dependency on the diameter to logarithmic, obtaining a variance proxy of order
\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}\kappa^{-1}(1+\log\frac{LD}{\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}})
, where
\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}\leq LD
is a bound (in operator norm) on the oscillation of
F_{t}
.

We remark that a factor depending on the diameter and the Lipschitz constant of the functions is needed for any approach relying only on curvature: there are examples where
\kappa=\Omega(1)
, the functions are nicely bounded, but the asymptotic variance is unbounded. Indeed, a logarithmic factor in
LD
is needed in the worst case, even assuming
\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1
(see
Section

4.3.1
for an example). If no assumption on
\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}
is imposed, a variance proxy of
L^{2}D^{2}\kappa^{-1}
is optimal.

To generalise
Eq.

1
to potentially unbounded state spaces, we consider inhomogeneous kernels contractive on centred functions as operators
L^{2}(\mu_{t})\to L^{2}(\mu_{t-1})
, where
\mu_{t}
denotes the
t
-step distribution of the chain started at
\mu_{0}
. This allows us to replace the diameter factor by a uniform bound on the norm of the observables. This notion of contractivity, which essentially generalises the spectral gap to inhomogeneous chains, has been investigated by
28
in relation to the
merging time
, i.e., the time a chain takes to forget its initial state. While this allows us to remove the dependency on the diameter, this notion of contraction can be difficult to verify in practice, as it requires detailed control of the chain’s evolution.

We conclude by discussing an application of our results to the analysis of the Elo rating system
[
2
,
23
]
, a widely used method for estimating
the relative skill of players in sports analytics, especially chess and tennis. We study Elo under the Bradley-Terry-Luce model, a standard statistical model for match outcomes, extended to a dynamical setting in which players’ true skills can change over time. In this setting, Elo induces a time-inhomogeneous Markov chain: our results imply that, if the true skills change slowly enough, Elo ratings are able to track the evolving skills over time.

In general, our results are useful to analyse learning algorithms where data distributions evolve over time; for instance, they apply to stochastic gradient descent on time-varying strongly convex smooth objectives and to PCA with Markovian data
[
17
]
, where samples are generated by a time-inhomogeneous chain.

Additional related work

Compared to the homogeneous setting, concentration inequalities for time-inhomogeneous chains are much less explored.

27
have explicitly adapted the Markov chain Chernoff bounds of
13
for positively curved chains to the inhomogeneous (scalar) setting. Their techniques, however, obtain a variance proxy of order
L^{2}\sigma_{\infty}^{2}\kappa^{-2}
, where
\sigma_{\infty}\coloneq\sup_{t,x}\mathrm{diam}\mathrm{Supp}P_{t}(x,\cdot)
is the granularity of the chain. This can be much worse than our bounds whenever mixing is much slower than the diameter of the chain.
For completeness, we also generalise their techniques to matrix observables in
Appendix

B
.

The martingale method of
16
and
15
also applies to empirical means of scalar observables in inhomogeneous chains. Their techniques, however, typically require stronger mixing properties than positive curvature.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Notation

Let
\Omega
be a general state space with
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}
.
A time-inhomogeneous Markov chain
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is specified by an initial distribution
\mu_{0}
and a sequence of Markov kernels
P_{t}
(
t\geq 1
), so that the transition from time
t-1
to
t
satisfies the following:

\mathbb{P}(X_{t}\in A\mid X_{t-1}=x)=P_{t}(x,A),\qquad\forall\,t\geq 1,\,A\in\mathcal{B}.

For integers
1\leq s\leq t
, we denote the inhomogeneous product kernel by

P_{s:t}:=P_{s}P_{s+1}\cdots P_{t}.

The probability distribution at time
t
is given by
\mu_{t}=\mu_{0}P_{1:t}
, where
\mu_{t}
is defined recursively as

\mu_{t}(dy)=\int_{\Omega}P_{t}(x,dy)\mu_{t-1}(dx).

Unless explicitly stated otherwise, all expectations and probabilities are taken under the path law started from
X_{0}\sim\mu_{0}
;
we write
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{0}}[\cdot]
and
\mathbb{P}_{\mu_{0}}(\cdot)
when it is helpful to emphasise this.

For any measurable function
f\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
,
P_{t}
acts on
f
as

P_{t}f(x)=\int_{\Omega}f(y)P_{t}(x,dy)\qquad\forall\,x\in\Omega.

We denote by
\mathsf{H}_{m}
the set of
m\times m
Hermitian matrices.
We use upper case letters for matrix-valued functions
F\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
.
With some abuse of notation, we let
P_{t}
act on
F
as

P_{t}F(x)=\int_{\Omega}F(y)P_{t}(x,dy)\qquad\forall\,x\in\Omega.

Given a vector
v\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
, we denote its Euclidean norm by

\|v\|\coloneq\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m}|v_{i}|^{2}}
.
For a matrix
A\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
, we denote its operator norm as

\|A\|_{\mathrm{op}}\coloneqq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{C}^{m}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}}\frac{\|Ax\|}{\|x\|}
.
The Frobenius norm of
A
is denoted as
\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}\coloneqq\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}A^{\ast}A}
,
where
A^{\ast}
is the complex adjoint of
A
.
If
A\in\mathsf{H}_{m}
, we denote its largest eigenvalue as
\lambda_{\max}(A)
.
For
F\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
, we define
\|F\|_{\infty}\coloneqq\sup_{x\in\Omega}\|F(x)\|_{\mathrm{op}}
.

2.2.
Proof outline and preliminary results

Let
(F_{t})_{t\geq 1}
be a sequence of functions
F_{t}\colon\Omega\to\mathsf{H}_{m}
.
We are interested in high probability bounds for objects of the following form:

\lambda_{\max}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{n}(F_{t}(X_{t})-\mathbb{E}F_{t}(X_{t}))\right).

As standard in matrix concentration inequalities
[
33
]
, our proof relies on bounding the matrix moment generating function:
for any random
Z\in\mathsf{H}_{m}
, any
s>0
, and any
a\in\mathbb{R}
,

(2)

\mathbb{P}\left[\,\lambda_{\max}(Z)\geq a\,\right]\leq e^{-sa}\,\mathbb{E}\mathrm{tr}e^{sZ}.

In our case,
Z
is a sum of Markov-dependent random matrices. To bound this moment generating function,
our starting point is the recent generalisation of the Golden-Thompson inequality to several matrices, which is due to
9
building on work by
32
.

Theorem 1

(
9
)
.

Let
H_{1},\dots,H_{n}\in\mathsf{H}_{m}
. Then, there exists a probability measure
\nu
on
[-\pi/2,\pi/2]
such that

(3)

\mathrm{tr}\exp\Big(\frac{\pi}{4}\,\sum_{t=1}^{n}H_{t}\Big)\;\leq\;m^{1-\pi/4}\,\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\mathrm{tr}\Bigg(\prod_{t=1}^{n}e^{\frac{1}{2}e^{i\phi}H_{t}}\,\prod_{t=n}^{1}e^{\frac{1}{2}e^{-i\phi}H_{t}}\Bigg)\,\nu(\phi).

Inequality (
3
) allows us to upper bound the matrix mgf as a trace of products of matrix exponentials, which is somewhat more similar to the traditional mgf for a sum of independent scalar random variables. We will obtain bounds on this quantity in two alternative regimes:

(1)

Lipschitz observables and Wasserstein-contractive Markov operators on compact state spaces (
Section

3.1
);

(2)

observables bounded in operator norm and Markov operators contractive from
L^{2}(\mu_{t})\to L^{2}(\mu_{t-1})
on general spaces (
Section

3.2
).

We employ the same proof strategy in both regimes, which generalises the “direct method” of
19
to the inhomogeneous and noncommutative setting. Here, “direct” is in contrast to proof strategies requiring results in the perturbation theory of linear operators, such as the original Markov chain Chernoff bound by
10
.

Fix
n\geq 1
,
\phi\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]
, and
s\geq 0
.
Define matrices

W_{t}(x):=\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{2}e^{i\phi}\,s\,\widetilde{F}_{t}(x)\Big),\qquad W_{t}(x)^{\ast}=\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{2}e^{-i\phi}\,s\,\widetilde{F}_{t}(x)\Big),

where
\widetilde{F}_{t}\coloneq F_{t}-\mu_{t}(F_{t})
,
and their products

M_{j}:=W_{1}(X_{1})\,W_{2}(X_{2})\cdots W_{j}(X_{j}),\qquad j=1,\dots,n,

with
M_{0}=I_{m}
.
Let

(4)

a_{j}(\phi):=\mathbb{E}\mathrm{tr}(M_{j}M_{j}^{\ast}).

Our goal is to bound
\sup_{\phi}a_{n}(\phi)
: we do this by first establishing a recursive inequality,
which is the time-inhomogeneous matrix version of the recursive identity by
[
19
]
.
The key idea is, for any
1\leq i\leq n
, to centre
the term on which each successive operator
P_{i}
acts. This will allow us to exploit the contractive properties
(in Lipschitz or
L^{2}
norm) of
P_{i}
.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\phi\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]
,
\gamma:=\cos\phi\in[0,1]
, and
n\geq 1
.
Define, for
1\leq i\leq n
,
B_{n,i}\in\mathsf{H}_{m}
and
\Theta_{n,i},H_{n,i}\colon\Omega\to\mathsf{H}_{m}

as follows:

\displaystyle\Theta_{n,1}(x)

\displaystyle:=\exp\!\big(s\gamma\,\widetilde{F}_{n}(x)\big),\qquad B_{n,1}:=\mathbb{E}\big[\Theta_{n,1}(X_{n})\big],\qquad H_{n,1}(x):=\Theta_{n,1}(x)-B_{n,1},

\displaystyle\Theta_{n,i}(x)

\displaystyle:=W_{n-i+1}(x)\,\big(P_{n-i+2}H_{n,i-1}\big)(x)\,W_{n-i+1}(x)^{\ast},\qquad B_{n,i}:=\mathbb{E}\big[\Theta_{n,i}(X_{n-i+1})\big],

\displaystyle H_{n,i}(x)

\displaystyle:=\Theta_{n,i}(x)-B_{n,i},\qquad i=2,\dots,n.

Let
b_{n,i}(\phi)=\|B_{n,i}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,(1\leq i\leq n)
. Then,

(5)

a_{n}(\phi)\ \leq\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{n,i}(\phi)\,a_{n-i}(\phi).

The next lemma shows that a bound on the sum of the coefficients
(b_{n,i})_{i=1}^{n}
translates into a bound on the matrix moment generating function.

Lemma 3
.

Assume that for some
C\geq 0
, for all
n\geq 1
and all
\phi\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]
,

(6)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{n,i}(\phi)\ \leq\ 1+Cs^{2}.

Then for all
n\geq 0
,
a_{n}(\phi)\leq m\,(1+Cs^{2})^{n}\leq m\,\exp(Cns^{2})
.
Therefore,

(7)

\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{0}}\mathrm{tr}\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{\pi}{4}\sum_{j=1}^{n}s\,\widetilde{F}_{j}(X_{j})\Bigr)\leq m^{2-\pi/4}\exp\!\Big(Cns^{2}\Big).

The previous lemma, together with the standard matrix Chernoff bound argument of
Eq.

2
and
Theorem

1
, implies that we only need to control the coefficients
(b_{n,i})_{i=1}^{n}
to obtain a concentration inequality. We outline how to do that in two different regimes: (A) for positively curved chains (
Section

3.1
); (B) for chain contractive in
L^{2}(\mu_{t})\to L^{2}(\mu_{t-1})
(
Section

3.2
).

3.
Main results

3.1.
Regime (A): Positive curvature and Lipschitz observables

We consider time-inhomogeneous chains with positive curvature, bounded diameter, and Lipschitz observables.
Throughout this section, we assume our state space
\Omega
is a Polish space with a metric
d
.
We recall the definition of Wasserstein-1 distance.

Definition 4

(Wasserstein-1 distance)
.

Let
(\Omega,d)
be a Polish metric space and let
\mu,\nu
be two probability measures on
\Omega
with finite first moment.
The
Wasserstein-1 distance
between
\mu
and
\nu
is

W_{1}(\mu,\nu)\;:=\;\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}d(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y),

where
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is the set of all couplings of
\mu
and
\nu
,
i.e., all probability measures
\pi
on
\Omega\times\Omega

with marginals
\mu
and
\nu
.

By Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality, we have:

W_{1}(\mu,\nu)\;=\;\sup_{\mathrm{Lip}(f)\leq 1}\biggl(\int_{\Omega}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)-\int_{\Omega}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)\biggr),

where the Lipschitz seminorm of
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is
defined by

\mathrm{Lip}(f)\;:=\;\sup_{x\neq y}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{d(x,y)}.

We also generalise the definition of Lipschitzness to matrix-valued functions as follows.

Definition 5

(Operator Lipschitz seminorm)
.

For
F:\Omega\to\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
,

\mathrm{Lip}(F)^{\mathrm{op}}:=\sup_{x\neq y}\frac{\|F(x)-F(y)\|_{\mathrm{op}}}{d(x,y)}.

We follow
24
and define the curvature of a Markov chain as the worst case contraction in Wasserstein distance after one step. Since we are dealing with time-inhomogeneous Markov chains, we specialise our notion for each time step.

Definition 6

(Ollivier curvature)
.

For any integer
t\geq 1
, we define the curvature at time
t
as

\kappa_{t}\;:=\;\inf_{x\neq y}1-\frac{W_{1}(P_{t}(x,\cdot),P_{t}(y,\cdot))}{d(x,y)}.

By Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality, a positive curvature implies that the averaging operator is contractive in the Lipschitz seminorm:

\mathrm{Lip}(P_{t}f)\leq(1-\kappa_{t})\mathrm{Lip}(f).

Since the curvature of the chain can vary greatly between steps, we only assume each step of the Markov chain of interest has only non-negative curvature and we assume strictly positive curvature “on average”.

Assumption 1

(Positive curvature)
.

For all
t\geq 1
, we have
\kappa_{t}\in[0,1]
. Furthermore, there exists
\kappa\in(0,1]
such that, for all
t\geq 1
,

(8)

1+\sum_{k=1}^{t}\ \prod_{\ell=k}^{t}(1-\kappa_{\ell})\ \leq\ \frac{1}{\kappa}.

Notice the average curvature is never worse than the worst-case curvature:
\kappa\geq\inf_{t}\kappa_{t}
.
In particular, a chain with occasional small curvature can still have geometric contraction on average.

In this section, we will also have to assume a finite diameter and Lipschitz observables.

Assumption 2

(Finite diameter)
.

\Omega
has diameter
D:=\sup_{x,y\in\Omega}d(x,y)<\infty
.

Assumption 3

(Lipschitz observables)
.

There exists
L>0
such that

\mathrm{Lip}(F_{t})^{\mathrm{op}}\leq L\text{ for all }t.

We remark it is possible to refine our results to allow for a time-varying Lipschitz parameter, but we choose to assume a uniform Lipschitz parameter for simplicity.

We are now ready to state the main result of this section.

Theorem 7
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
,
2
,
3
are satisfied. Let
v^{2}:=\frac{192}{\pi^{2}}\frac{L^{2}D^{2}}{\kappa}
.
Then, for all
\varepsilon\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}_{\mu_{0}}\Big(\lambda_{\max}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n}(F_{j}(X_{j})-\mathbb{E}F_{j}(X_{j}))\Big)\geq n\varepsilon\Big)\leq m^{\,2-\pi/4}\,\exp\left(-\frac{n\varepsilon^{2}}{2v^{2}}\right).

If we have additional control on the observables’ oscillation, we can improve the dependency on the diameter in the variance proxy to logarithmic. For technical reasons, we will require a uniform lower bound on the curvature, rather than working with the effective curvature of
1
.

For
F\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
we define the

oscillation
of
F
as

osc(F)\coloneqq\sup_{x,y\in\Omega}\|F(x)-F(y)\|_{\mathrm{op}}.

We denote with
\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}
the largest oscillation:
\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}\coloneqq\sup_{t}osc(F_{t})
. Notice
\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}\in(0,LD]
.

Assumption 4

(Uniform curvature)
.

There exists
\kappa>0
such that, for all
t
,
\kappa_{t}\geq\kappa
.

Theorem 8
.

Suppose Assumptions
2
,
3
, and
4
are satisfied. Let
\overline{v}^{2}:=\frac{3200}{\pi^{2}}\,\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}\kappa^{-1}\left(1+\log\frac{LD}{\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}}\right).

Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
,

\mathbb{P}_{\mu_{0}}\Big(\lambda_{\max}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n}(F_{j}(X_{j})-\mathbb{E}F_{j}(X_{j}))\Big)\geq n\varepsilon\Big)\leq m^{\,2-\pi/4}\,\exp\left(-\frac{n\varepsilon^{2}}{2\overline{v}^{2}}\right).

The variance proxy in the sub-Gaussian regime is of the correct order, as shown in
Section

4.3.1
.
The idea is to construct a (homogeneous) chain that has uniform stationary distribution on the interval
[0,D]
,
\Omega(1)
curvature, and is supported on a finite set after a finite number of steps. This will make the spectral gap of the chain zero. Furthermore, the chain will forget its initial state in
\Omega(\log D)
steps, making a dependency on the diameter unavoidable.

3.2.
Regime (B): Inhomogeneous spectral gap and bounded observables

In this section we consider a general, potentially unbounded state space
\Omega
.
We do not require
\Omega
to be Polish nor metric. We consider
time-inhomogeneous Markov chains that have a positive spectral gap,
where the spectral gap has a particular definition tailored to the inhomogeneous setting
and previously studied by
28
(see
Definition

9
).

Besides this spectral condition, we require the observables to be bounded in the following sense.

Assumption 5

(Bounded oscillation)
.

Assume there exist constants
\Delta_{\mathrm{op}},\Delta_{\mathrm{F}}\in(0,\infty)
such that, for all
t\geq 1
,

\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}\coloneqq\sup_{t\geq 0,\,x,y\in\Omega}\|F_{t}(x)-F_{t}(y)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\text{ and }\Delta_{\mathrm{F}}\coloneqq\sup_{t\geq 0,\,x,y\in\Omega}\|F_{t}(x)-F_{t}(y)\|_{\mathrm{F}}.

Notice that
\Delta_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\sqrt{2\sup_{t,x}\mathrm{rank}F_{t}(x)}\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}
.
In particular, if
F_{t}
is a matrix dilation of some vector,
\mathrm{rank}F_{t}(x)=2
and
\Delta_{\mathrm{F}}\asymp\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}
.

Given a probability measure
\nu
on
\Omega
, we define the
L^{2}(\nu)
norm as

\|f\|_{2,\nu}:=\Big(\int|f|^{2}\,d\nu\Big)^{1/2}.

We say
f\in L^{2}(\nu)
if
\|f\|_{2,\nu}<\infty
.
We denote with
L^{2}_{0}(\nu):=\{f\in L^{2}(\nu):\nu(f)=0\}

the restriction of
L^{2}(\nu)
to zero-mean functions.

Definition 9
.

Let
t\geq 1
. Since
\mu_{t}=\mu_{t-1}P_{t}
, the Markov operator
P_{t}

maps
L^{2}(\mu_{t})
to
L^{2}(\mu_{t-1})
. Define
the
one-step (second) singular value

\sigma_{t}:=\|P_{t}\|_{L^{2}_{0}(\mu_{t})\to L^{2}_{0}(\mu_{t-1})}=\sup\Big\{\|P_{t}f\|_{2,\mu_{t-1}}:\ \|f\|_{2,\mu_{t}}=1,\ \mu_{t}(f)=0\Big\}.

Equivalently, for all
f\in L^{2}(\mu_{t})
with
\mu_{t}(f)=0
,

(9)

\|P_{t}f\|_{2,\mu_{t-1}}\leq\sigma_{t}\,\|f\|_{2,\mu_{t}}.

It can be checked that
\sigma_{t}\in[0,1]
(see
Proposition

19
).
Essentially,
1-\sigma_{t}
will play the role of the spectral gap of the operator
P_{t}
.
As done for curvature, we want this spectral gap to be strictly positive “on average”.

Assumption 6

(Positive spectral gap)
.

We assume there exists
\lambda\in(0,1]
such that, for every
t\geq 1
,

\sum_{k=1}^{t+1}\ \prod_{\ell=k}^{t}\sigma_{\ell}\ \leq\ \frac{1}{\lambda}.

The main result of this section is as follows.

Theorem 10
.

Suppose Assumptions
5
and
6
are satisfied.
Let
v_{B}^{2}:=\frac{768}{\pi^{2}}\,\frac{\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}\Delta_{\mathrm{F}}}{\lambda}
.
Then, for every
\varepsilon\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}_{\mu_{0}}\Big(\lambda_{\max}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n}(F_{j}(X_{j})-\mathbb{E}F_{j}(X_{j}))\Big)\geq n\varepsilon\Big)\leq m^{\,2-\pi/4}\,\exp\left(-\frac{n\varepsilon^{2}}{2v_{B}^{2}}\right).

Notice
Theorem

10
does not require bounded diameter or Lipschitz observables. However, it is typically hard to obtain a good estimate of
\sigma_{t}
, since it requires a good control on
\mu_{t}
(unless each Markov kernel has a common stationary distribution). See
[
28
]
for a discussion on related issues and the connection with the
merging time
of a Markov chain. See also
[
29
,
30
]

We start by showing that a Markov operator

contractive in (scalar) Lipschitz seminorm is also contractive for the operator Lipschitz seminorm. This means we do not have to specialise the standard (scalar) notion of curvature to the high-dimensional setting.

Lemma 11
.

Let
(\Omega,d)
be a metric space and let
P
be a Markov operator acting on Lipschitz functions.
Assume that there exists
a\geq 0
such that, for every real-valued
h:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
,

\mathrm{Lip}(Ph)\leq a\,\mathrm{Lip}(h).

Then, for every Hermitian-matrix-valued
F:\Omega\to\mathsf{H}_{m}
,

\mathrm{Lip}(PF)^{\mathrm{op}}\leq a\,\mathrm{Lip}(F)^{\mathrm{op}}.

The proof uses the variational characterisation of the eigenvalues for Hermitian matrices and applies the scalar Lipschitz contractivity of
P
to the function
h_{u}(z)\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
,
h_{u}(z):=\langle u,F(z)u\rangle
for some
u\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
.

Proof.

Fix
x\neq y
. By the variational characterisation of the operator norm for Hermitian matrices,

\|(PF)(x)-(PF)(y)\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\sup_{\|u\|_{2}=1}\Big|\langle u,((PF)(x)-(PF)(y))u\rangle\Big|.

For each unit vector
u\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
, define the scalar function
h_{u}(z):=\langle u,F(z)u\rangle
.
Then for all
z,z^{\prime}
,

|h_{u}(z)-h_{u}(z^{\prime})|\leq\|F(z)-F(z^{\prime})\|_{\mathrm{op}},

hence
\mathrm{Lip}(h_{u})\leq\mathrm{Lip}(F)^{\mathrm{op}}
.
Moreover, by linearity of
P
,

(Ph_{u})(x)=\langle u,(PF)(x)u\rangle

for all
x
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\Big|\langle u,((PF)(x)-(PF)(y))u\rangle\Big|

\displaystyle=|(Ph_{u})(x)-(Ph_{u})(y)|

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{Lip}(Ph_{u})\,d(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq a\,\mathrm{Lip}(h_{u})\,d(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq a\,\mathrm{Lip}(F)^{\mathrm{op}}\,d(x,y).

Taking the supremum over
\|u\|_{2}=1
yields

\|(PF)(x)-(PF)(y)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq a\,\mathrm{Lip}(F)^{\mathrm{op}}\,d(x,y).

Divide by
d(x,y)
and take the supremum over
x\neq y
.
∎

Lemma 12

(\Omega,d)

\left\|F_{j}(X_{j})-\mathbb{E}F_{j}(X_{j})\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq LD

for each
j
, and hence

\lambda_{\max}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\widetilde{F}_{j}(X_{j})\right)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left\|\widetilde{F}_{j}(X_{j})\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq nLD<n\varepsilon.

This proves the stated bound for all
\varepsilon\geq 0
.
∎

4.3.
Regime (A): Improved dependence on the diameter

Before proving
Theorem

8
, we show how the logarithmic dependency arises in a simpler scalar setting.
Let
P
be a time-homogeneous Markov kernel with invariant distribution
\pi
, and assume that

W_{1}(P(x,\cdot),P(y,\cdot))\leq(1-\kappa)d(x,y)\qquad\text{for all }x,y\in\Omega

for some
0<\kappa\leq 1
.
Let
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
satisfy
\pi(f)=0
,

\mathrm{Lip}(f)\leq L,\qquad osc(f)\leq\Delta\leq LD,

and let the chain be started from stationarity.
We claim that its asymptotic variance is bounded at the same scale as the variance proxy in
Theorem

8
:

\sigma_{\mathrm{as}}^{2}(f)\coloneqq\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Var}_{\pi}\!\left(\sum_{t=1}^{n}f(X_{t})\right)\leq C\,\Delta^{2}\kappa^{-1}\left(1+\log\frac{LD}{\Delta}\right)

for some constant
C>0
.

For every
n
, stationarity implies the covariance decomposition

\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Var}_{\pi}\!\left(\sum_{t=1}^{n}f(X_{t})\right)=\operatorname{Var}_{\pi}(f)+2\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(1-\frac{i}{n}\right)\operatorname{Cov}_{\pi}\big(f(X_{0}),f(X_{i})\big).

It is therefore enough to control the covariance between
f(X_{0})
and
f(X_{i})
. The key observation is that, for small
i
, it is more convenient to bound this covariance by the oscillation of
f
rather than curvature.
To this end, notice that since the chain is stationary and
\pi(f)=0
,

\operatorname{Cov}_{\pi}\big(f(X_{0}),f(X_{i})\big)=\int f(x)P^{i}f(x)\,\pi(dx).

Since
\pi(f)=0
, we have
\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}\leq osc(f)\leq\Delta
.
Moreover
\pi(P^{i}f)=0
for every
i
, and hence

\left\|P^{i}f\right\|_{\infty}\leq osc(P^{i}f)\leq osc(f)\leq\Delta.

On the other hand, by curvature,

\mathrm{Lip}(P^{i}f)\leq(1-\kappa)^{i}\mathrm{Lip}(f)\leq L(1-\kappa)^{i},

and Lemma
12
yields

\left\|P^{i}f\right\|_{\infty}\leq D\,\mathrm{Lip}(P^{i}f)\leq LD(1-\kappa)^{i}.

Combining the oscillation bound with the Lipschitz-contraction bound,

\left\|P^{i}f\right\|_{\infty}\leq\Delta\min\left\{1,\frac{LD}{\Delta}(1-\kappa)^{i}\right\}.

Consequently,

\left|\int f(x)P^{i}f(x)\,\pi(dx)\right|\leq\Delta^{2}\min\left\{1,\frac{LD}{\Delta}(1-\kappa)^{i}\right\}.

It remains to sum this profile for all
i
. Since
LD/\Delta\geq 1
,
when
\kappa=1
, the next estimate is immediate, so assume
0<\kappa<1
and define

i_{\star}:=1+\left\lceil\frac{\log(LD/\Delta)}{-\log(1-\kappa)}\right\rceil.

Then
i_{\star}\leq 2+\kappa^{-1}\log(LD/\Delta)
, since
-\log(1-\kappa)\geq\kappa
, and

\sum_{i\geq 1}\min\left\{1,\frac{LD}{\Delta}(1-\kappa)^{i}\right\}\leq i_{\star}+\frac{LD}{\Delta}\sum_{i\geq i_{\star}}(1-\kappa)^{i}\leq 2+\kappa^{-1}\log(LD/\Delta)+\kappa^{-1}.

Therefore

\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Var}_{\pi}\!\left(\sum_{t=1}^{n}f(X_{t})\right)\leq\Delta^{2}\left(1+2\sum_{i\geq 1}\min\left\{1,\frac{LD}{\Delta}(1-\kappa)^{i}\right\}\right)\leq 7\Delta^{2}\kappa^{-1}(1+\log(LD/\Delta)).

Letting
n\to\infty
yields the displayed bound on
\sigma_{\mathrm{as}}^{2}(f)
.

We exploit the intuition outlined above to improve the variance proxy of
Theorem

7
. However, a crude application would achieve the correct logarithmic dependency on the diameter in the variance proxy only over a small sub-Gaussian window. To enlarge this window, we must keep track of how curvature contracts the relevant distances below the diameter scale.
In particular, we will need the following way of recording increments at different distance scales.
For a matrix-valued function
H:\Omega\to\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
, we say that an increasing concave function

\psi:[0,D]\to[0,\infty)
controls the increments of
H
if

\|H(x)-H(y)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\psi(d(x,y))\qquad\text{for all }x,y\in\Omega.

The next lemma shows how applying a positively curved operator improves the control on the increments.

Lemma 16
.

Let
P_{t}
have curvature at least
\kappa
.
If an increasing concave function
\psi:[0,D]\to[0,\infty)
controls the increments of
H
, then

\|P_{t}H(x)-P_{t}H(y)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\psi((1-\kappa)d(x,y))\qquad\text{for all }x,y\in\Omega.

Proof.

Fix
x,y\in\Omega
, and let
(U,V)
be an optimal coupling of
P_{t}(x,\cdot)
and
P_{t}(y,\cdot)
for the
W_{1}
distance.
Then, by Jensen’s inequality and the concavity of
\psi
,

\displaystyle\|P_{t}H(x)-P_{t}H(y)\|_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\mathbb{E}\big[H(U)-H(V)\big]\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\|H(U)-H(V)\|_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\psi(d(U,V))

\displaystyle\leq\psi\big(\mathbb{E}d(U,V)\big).

By the definition of curvature in
Definition

6
and Assumption
4
,

\mathbb{E}d(U,V)=W_{1}(P_{t}(x,\cdot),P_{t}(y,\cdot))\leq(1-\kappa)d(x,y).

Since
\psi
is increasing, the claim follows.
∎

The next lemma show we can control the increments of
H_{n,j}
, as defined in

Lemma 17
.

Fix
n\geq 1

s\geq 0

Let
H_{n,j}
be the remainders defined in

\psi_{1}(r):=s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}e^{s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}}\min\left\{1,\frac{Lr}{\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}}\right\},

and, for

Then,
\psi_{j}

(1\leq j\leq n)
is increasing and concave, and
\psi_{j}
controls the increments of
H_{n,j}

Proof.

The function
r\mapsto\min\{1,Lr/\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}\}
is increasing and concave on
[0,D]
, and it vanishes at
0

By induction, if
\psi_{j-1}
is increasing and concave, then

r\mapsto e^{s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}}\psi_{j-1}((1-\kappa)r)
is increasing and concave.
The second summand in the definition of
\psi_{j}
is a nonnegative constant,

s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}e^{s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}}\psi_{j-1}((1-\kappa)D)
, times

r\mapsto\min\{1,Lr/\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}\}

Since sums of increasing concave functions are increasing and concave, the same is true of each
\psi_{j}
.

For later use, we note that, for every
t
and
x

\|\widetilde{F}_{t}(x)\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\left\|\int\big(F_{t}(x)-F_{t}(y)\big)\,\mu_{t}(dy)\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}.

We first prove the claim for
j=1
.
Recall from
Proposition

2
that

H_{n,1}(x)=\exp\big(s\gamma\widetilde{F}_{n}(x)\big)-B_{n,1},\qquad\gamma=\cos\phi\in[0,1].

The constant
B_{n,1}
does not affect increments. Moreover, by Assumption

\|\widetilde{F}_{n}(x)-\widetilde{F}_{n}(y)\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\|F_{n}(x)-F_{n}(y)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}\min\left\{1,\frac{Ld(x,y)}{\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}}\right\}.

Lemma
32
therefore gives

\|H_{n,1}(x)-H_{n,1}(y)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq se^{s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}}\|\widetilde{F}_{n}(x)-\widetilde{F}_{n}(y)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}e^{s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}}\min\left\{1,\frac{Ld(x,y)}{\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}}\right\}.

This proves the base case.

Assume now that
\psi_{j-1}
controls the increments of
H_{n,j-1}
, and let
\tau:=n-j+1
.
Set

G:=P_{\tau+1}H_{n,j-1}.

By
Lemma

, the increments of
G
are controlled by
r\mapsto\psi_{j-1}((1-\kappa)r)
.
Moreover,

\mu_{\tau}(G)=\mu_{\tau}(P_{\tau+1}H_{n,j-1})=\mu_{\tau+1}(H_{n,j-1})=0,

where the last equality follows from the centering in
Proposition

2
. Hence, for all
x\in\Omega

\|G(x)\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\left\|\int\big(G(x)-G(y)\big)\,\mu_{\tau}(dy)\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\psi_{j-1}((1-\kappa)D).

Since
\|\widetilde{F}_{\tau}\|_{\infty}\leq\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}
, Lemma
32
gives

\|W_{\tau}\|_{\infty}\leq e^{s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}/2},

and, using again Assumption

\|W_{\tau}(x)-W_{\tau}(y)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\frac{s}{2}e^{s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}/2}\|\widetilde{F}_{\tau}(x)-\widetilde{F}_{\tau}(y)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\frac{s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}}{2}e^{s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}/2}\min\left\{1,\frac{Ld(x,y)}{\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}}\right\}.

The same bound holds for
W_{\tau}^{\ast}

Proposition

that

\Theta_{n,j}(x)=W_{\tau}(x)G(x)W_{\tau}(x)^{\ast},\qquad H_{n,j}(x)=\Theta_{n,j}(x)-B_{n,j},

we obtain, for all
x,y\in\Omega

\displaystyle\|H_{n,j}(x)-H_{n,j}(y)\|_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle=\|\Theta_{n,j}(x)-\Theta_{n,j}(y)\|_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\|W_{\tau}\|_{\infty}^{2}\|G(x)-G(y)\|_{\mathrm{op}}+2\|W_{\tau}\|_{\infty}\|W_{\tau}(x)-W_{\tau}(y)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\|G\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq e^{s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}}\psi_{j-1}((1-\kappa)d(x,y))+s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}e^{s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}}\min\left\{1,\frac{Ld(x,y)}{\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}}\right\}\psi_{j-1}((1-\kappa)D)

\displaystyle=\psi_{j}(d(x,y)).

This closes the induction.
∎

We can now exploit the control of the increments provided by the previous lemma to bound the sum of the coefficients

\{b_{n,i}\}

Lemma 18
.

Let
0\leq s\leq\frac{\kappa}{20\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}(1+\log(LD/\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}))}

Then, for all
n\geq 1
and all
\phi\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]

\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{n,i}(\phi)\leq 1+100\,s^{2}\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}\,\kappa^{-1}(1+\log(LD/\Delta_{\mathrm{op}})).

Proof.

Let
\psi_{j}
be as in
Lemma

17
, and, for
j,k\geq 1
, define

q_{j,k}:=\psi_{j}((1-\kappa)^{k}D).

From the recursive definition of
\psi_{j}

q_{j,k}=e^{s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}}q_{j-1,k+1}+s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}e^{s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}}\min\left\{1,\frac{LD}{\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}}(1-\kappa)^{k}\right\}q_{j-1,1}\qquad(j\geq 2,\ k\geq 1),

while

q_{1,k}=s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}e^{s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}}\min\left\{1,\frac{LD}{\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}}(1-\kappa)^{k}\right\}.

Iterating this identity gives, for every
j\geq 1
,

(19)

q_{j,1}=s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}e^{s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}j}\min\left\{1,\frac{LD}{\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}}(1-\kappa)^{j}\right\}+s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{j-1}e^{s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}\ell}\min\left\{1,\frac{LD}{\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}}(1-\kappa)^{\ell}\right\}q_{j-\ell,1}.

We next bound

Let

\min\left\{1,\frac{LD}{\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}}(1-\kappa)^{\ell}\right\}\leq\min\left\{1,\frac{LD}{\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}}e^{-\kappa\ell}\right\}.

Moreover, using
s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\kappa/(20(1+\log(LD/\Delta_{\mathrm{op}})))

\sum_{\ell=1}^{J}e^{s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}\ell}\min\left\{1,\frac{LD}{\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}}(1-\kappa)^{\ell}\right\}\leq Je^{s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}J}\leq 2\,\frac{1+\log(LD/\Delta_{\mathrm{op}})}{\kappa}.

For the tail, since
s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\kappa/2

\sum_{\ell>J}e^{s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}\ell}\min\left\{1,\frac{LD}{\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}}(1-\kappa)^{\ell}\right\}\leq\frac{LD}{\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}}\sum_{\ell>J}e^{-(\kappa-s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}})\ell}\leq\frac{(LD/\Delta_{\mathrm{op}})e^{-(\kappa-s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}})J}}{1-e^{-(\kappa-s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}})}}\leq\frac{2}{1-e^{-(\kappa-s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}})}}.

Since

\sum_{\ell>J}e^{s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}\ell}\min\left\{1,\frac{LD}{\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}}(1-\kappa)^{\ell}\right\}\leq\frac{8}{\kappa}.

Combining the two estimates,

(20)

S\leq 10\,\frac{1+\log(LD/\Delta_{\mathrm{op}})}{\kappa}.

In particular
s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}S\leq 1/2
.

Summing (

1\leq j\leq N

\sum_{j=1}^{N}q_{j,1}\leq s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}S+s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}S\sum_{j=1}^{N}q_{j,1}.

Since

\left(1-s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}S\right)\sum_{j=1}^{N}q_{j,1}\leq s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}S,

and hence

(21)

\sum_{j=1}^{N}q_{j,1}\leq 2s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}S\leq 20s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}\,\frac{1+\log(LD/\Delta_{\mathrm{op}})}{\kappa}.

We now return to the coefficients
b_{n,i}(\phi)
. For

\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{n}}\widetilde{F}_{n}(X_{n})=0
and

b_{n,1}(\phi)\leq\exp(s^{2}\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}/2)\leq 1+s^{2}\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}^{2},

where the last inequality uses
s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1
.
For
i\geq 2
, set
\tau:=n-i+1
and
G:=P_{\tau+1}H_{n,i-1}
.
By
Lemma

17
and
Lemma

, for all
x,y\in\Omega

\|G(x)-G(y)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\psi_{i-1}((1-\kappa)d(x,y)).

Moreover,

\mu_{\tau}(G)=\mu_{\tau}(P_{\tau+1}H_{n,i-1})=\mu_{\tau+1}(H_{n,i-1})=0,

where the last equality follows from the centering in
Proposition

2

\|G(x)\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\left\|\int\big(G(x)-G(y)\big)\,\mu_{\tau}(dy)\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\psi_{i-1}((1-\kappa)D)=q_{i-1,1}.

Therefore

\|G\|_{\infty}\leq q_{i-1,1}.

Using (
12

\|W_{\tau}\|_{\infty}\leq e^{s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}/2},\qquad\|W_{\tau}-\mu_{\tau}W_{\tau}\|_{\infty}\leq\sup_{x,y}\|W_{\tau}(x)-W_{\tau}(y)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\frac{s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}}{2}e^{s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}/2},

we obtain

b_{n,i}(\phi)\leq 2s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}e^{s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}}q_{i-1,1}.

Since
s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1/20
,
e^{s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}}\leq 2
, and (

) holds with

,

\sum_{i=2}^{n}b_{n,i}(\phi)\leq 4s\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}q_{j,1}\leq 80s^{2}\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}\,\frac{1+\log(LD/\Delta_{\mathrm{op}})}{\kappa}.

Finally, since
(1+\log(LD/\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}))/\kappa\geq 1
,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{n,i}(\phi)\leq 1+81s^{2}\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}\,\frac{1+\log(LD/\Delta_{\mathrm{op}})}{\kappa}\leq 1+100s^{2}\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}\,\frac{1+\log(LD/\Delta_{\mathrm{op}})}{\kappa}.

∎

We now have all the ingredients to prove the main result of this section.

Proof.

Set

\overline{r}_{\max}:=\frac{\pi\kappa}{80\,\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}\,(1+\log(LD/\Delta_{\mathrm{op}}))}.

4.3.1.
Tightness of the sub-Gaussian regime in
Theorem

8

We construct an example of a Markov chain with a variance proxy that matches the one of
Theorem

8
up to constants.
This chain will be homogeneous but we will consider time-inhomogeneous observables (this will simplify the construction of such an example).

Let
\Omega=[0,D]
with metric
d(x,y)=|x-y|
(so
\mathrm{diam}(\Omega)=D
).
Consider the following chain:

(22)

X_{t+1}\;=\;\frac{X_{t}}{2}\;+\;\frac{D}{2}\,B_{t+1},\qquad B_{t+1}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\ \text{i.i.d.}

Let
P
be the Markov operator associated with the chain.

It is straightforward to show this chain has
\Omega(1)
curvature.
Indeed, for any
x,y\in\Omega
, couple one step of the evolution of the chain by using the same Bernoulli r.v. in (
22
).
Then,

W_{1}(P(x,\cdot),P(y,\cdot))\leq\frac{|x-y|}{2},

and therefore
\kappa\geq 1/2
.

Let
\ell\geq 1
be the largest integer such that

(23)

\pi\,\Delta\,2^{\ell}\leq LD.

For
t=1,2,\dots,\ell
, define the (scalar) observable

(24)

F_{t}(x)\;:=\;\frac{\Delta}{2}\cos\!\Bigl(2\pi\,2^{t}\,\frac{x}{D}\Bigr).

Then
osc(F_{t})\leq\Delta
, and

\mathrm{Lip}(F_{t})\leq\sup_{x}\left|\frac{d}{dx}F_{t}(x)\right|=\frac{\Delta}{2}\cdot 2\pi\,2^{t}\cdot\frac{1}{D}\leq\frac{\pi\Delta\,2^{\ell}}{D}\leq L,

where the last inequality is exactly (
23
). Thus, the hypotheses
\mathrm{Lip}(F_{t})\leq L
and
osc(F_{t})\leq\Delta
for all
t\leq\ell
are satisfied.

Let the chain start from the uniform distribution on
\Omega
, which is the stationary distribution of the chain:

(25)

X_{0}\sim\mu_{0}\coloneqq\mathrm{Unif}([0,D]).

Unrolling (
22
) gives

X_{t}=\frac{X_{0}}{2^{t}}+\frac{D}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{t}\frac{B_{i}}{2^{t-i}},

and, hence,

\frac{2^{t}X_{t}}{D}=\frac{X_{0}}{D}+z,

for some integer
z
.
Therefore, by (
24
), for every
t\leq\ell
,

F_{t}(X_{t})=\frac{\Delta}{2}\cos\!\Bigl(2\pi\,\frac{X_{0}}{D}\Bigr).

In other words,
F_{t}(X_{t})
depends only on the starting point.
Moreover,
\mathbb{E}[F_{t}(X_{t})]=0
for all
t\leq\ell
since
X_{0}/D
is uniform on
[0,1]
.

Define

S_{\ell}:=\sum_{t=1}^{\ell}\bigl(F_{t}(X_{t})-\mathbb{E}[F_{t}(X_{t})]\bigr)=\sum_{t=1}^{\ell}F_{t}(X_{t}).

By the computation above,

S_{\ell}=\frac{\Delta}{2}\,\ell\,\cos\!\Bigl(2\pi\,\frac{X_{0}}{D}\Bigr).

Now suppose we want a sub-Gaussian bound of the form

(26)

\mathbb{P}(S_{\ell}\geq\ell\varepsilon)\ \leq\ \exp\!\left(-\frac{\ell\varepsilon^{2}}{2v^{2}}\right)\qquad\text{for all }\varepsilon\geq 0.

Choose
\varepsilon=\Delta/4
. Since
X_{0}/D
is uniform on
[0,1]
, we have that

\mathbb{P}\left(S_{\ell}>\frac{\Delta}{4}\ell\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\cos\left(2\pi X_{0}/D\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}\right)=\frac{1}{3}.

Therefore,

\frac{1}{3}\leq\exp\!\left(-\frac{\ell\varepsilon^{2}}{2v^{2}}\right)\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad v^{2}\geq\frac{\ell\varepsilon^{2}}{2\log 3}.

By (
23
),
\ell=\Theta(\log(LD/\Delta))
,
which implies

v^{2}=\Omega\!\left(\Delta^{2}\log\frac{LD}{\Delta}\right)=\Omega\!\left(\frac{\Delta^{2}}{\kappa}\log\frac{LD}{\Delta}\right).

This matches (up to constants) the
\Delta^{2}\kappa^{-1}\log(LD/\Delta)
variance proxy of
Theorem

8
.

4.4.
Regime (B)

This section is devoted to proving
Theorem

10
. The proof follows a similar outline as the proof of
Theorem

7
, with the inhomogeneous spectral gap

5.
An analysis of the Elo rating system with evolving skills

The Elo rating system is a popular method for estimating the relative skill of
players in sports analytics, particularly chess and tennis. It was introduced by
Arpad Elo in the 1950s to rank chess players by assigning them a numerical
score, which is updated according to a simple formula: after a game between two
players
i
and
j
, if the former wins, their rating is increased by an
amount proportional to the probability estimated by the model that
i
would
have lost to
j
; the rating of
j
is instead decreased by the same amount.
Unexpected outcomes result in larger changes in ratings.

The effectiveness, simplicity, and interpretability of Elo ratings have made
them, arguably, the most popular ranking system in sports analytics. More
recently, Elo ratings have also been applied to evaluate and rank large language
models
[
5
]
. Despite their popularity, however, not much
attention has been devoted to studying Elo ratings from a theoretical point of
view. Recently,
23
have studied Elo ratings under the
Bradley-Terry-Luce (BTL) model, a popular statistical model of match outcomes.
Using techniques from Markov chain theory, they show Elo ratings
well-approximate the true ratings of the players with a sample complexity that
is competitive against the state of the art of BTL estimation.

While these results offer a compelling explanation of the effectiveness of Elo
ratings, they suffer from an important limitation: they require the true skills
of players to remain fixed over time. Therefore, they fail to investigate one
of the most important properties of an online ranking system: the capability to
detect and track changes in the skills of the players. To address this gap, we
consider an extension of the BTL model in which the players’ true ratings change
over time. In the setup below, the true ratings and matchup distributions
evolve as an external time-inhomogeneous Markov chain, independently of the
randomness used to select pairs and generate match outcomes. We give a
sufficient condition under which the joint process containing the Elo
ratings, true ratings, and matchup distributions is itself positively curved,
and then apply the concentration results to show that Elo ratings track the
changing true ratings over time.

5.1.
Related work

There is a plethora of work on the Bradley-Terry-Luce model and the problem of learning from pairwise comparison in general. Here we discuss only the most relevant literature.
2
,
3
study Elo rating systems (in the homogeneous setting) from an applied probability perspective; in particular, the existence and convergence to a stationary distribution of the Elo process is established.
23
prove Elo recovers the (static) BTL parameters with a sample complexity close to the state-of-the-art. This is done by studying Elo as a Markov chain and bounding the rate of convergence of this (time-homogeneous) chain in a conceptually similar way as the present work. Since Elo does not converge in total variation, this is done by relating the Elo process to an auxiliary
noisy
version of Elo, so that Chernoff bounds for Markov chains
[
19
,
25
]
can be applied. This is because the results by
13
have a suboptimal dependency on the curvature of the chain. Our concentration results, besides working in the more general inhomogeneous setting, have an optimal dependency on the curvature, allowing us to study Elo directly.

Compared to the static setup, theoretical results for BTL estimation in the dynamic setting, where model parameters change over time, are limited.
14
adapt the classical Rank Centrality algorithm for static BTL
[
22
]
to the dynamic setting essentially by maintaining the average win-rates between any two players over a sliding window. While it is difficult to present a like-for-like comparison with our results, we note that their algorithm has the following drawbacks compared to ours: it does not maintain an online estimator of the ratings; it requires a very strong Lipschitz condition on how fast the ratings change that needs to be satisfied uniformly for
each pair
of players.

4
develop an algorithm for a similar setup as ours that works by performing kernel smoothing to pre-process the pairwise comparisons over time and then solve a maximum log-likelihood estimation problem for each time step. This makes estimation much more computationally expensive than the intrinsically online Elo rating system.

Remark 23
.

In the construction above, the environment process
(\rho^{t},q^{t})_{t\geq 1}

evolves independently of the Elo ratings. This is for simplicity and can potentially be relaxed.
For example, suppose the matchup
distribution is chosen as a function of the current Elo ratings and true
ratings, say

Our techniques
can be adapted as long as perturbing the current Elo vector cannot cause a
large perturbation of the matchup distribution, i.e., if the maps
Q_{t}
are sufficiently Lipschitz.

Our results will rely on several assumptions on the curvature of the process. To state these assumptions formally, we need to introduce the following
quantities. For an environment
e=(\rho,q)
, define

\lambda(e)\coloneqq\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{\underline{0}\}\\
v\perp\mathbf{1}\end{subarray}}\frac{\sum_{\{i,j\}}q_{\{i,j\}}(v_{i}-v_{j})^{2}}{\sum_{i}v_{i}^{2}}.

Equivalently,
\lambda(e)
is the second smallest eigenvalue of the Laplacian
of the continuous-time random walk on
[n]
with transition rates

(q_{\{i,j\}})
. This essentially quantifies how fast information about results between two
players propagates to the rest of the players, and is a standard quantity in
BTL estimation
[
31
,
22
,
20
]
.
Notice it holds
\lambda(e)=O(1/n)
since
\sum_{\{i,j\}}q_{\{i,j\}}=1
,

We assume there is a deterministic constant
\lambda>0

such that

\lambda(e)\geq\lambda\qquad\text{for every }e\in\mathcal{E}.

The following quantity is a lower bound on the curvature of Elo when the
environment is held fixed, that is, it quantifies the rate of convergence in
Wasserstein distance after one step of the Elo update:

\kappa\coloneqq\frac{1}{8}\,\eta e^{-4M}\lambda.

We assume that the environment also contracts in Wasserstein distance. More
precisely, we assume the environment chain has one-step
curvature at least
\nu\in(0,1]
with respect to
d_{\mathcal{E}}
:

W_{1}^{d_{\mathcal{E}}}\bigl(\Gamma_{t}(e,\cdot),\Gamma_{t}(\widetilde{e},\cdot)\bigr)\leq(1-\nu)d_{\mathcal{E}}(e,\widetilde{e})\qquad\text{for all }t\geq 1\text{ and }e,\widetilde{e}\in\mathcal{E}.

Here
W_{1}^{d_{\mathcal{E}}}
denotes Wasserstein distance computed using the
metric
d_{\mathcal{E}}
. Informally, positive curvature means that the
environment dynamics contract differences between possible realisations of the
true ratings and matchup distributions over time, which we believe is a reasonable assumption.
Finally, we choose the Elo step size so
that

\eta\leq\frac{\nu}{2}.

We will show below that the same parameter
\kappa
controls both how quickly
Elo forgets its initial ratings and how strongly the joint process

(X^{t},\rho^{t},q^{t})
concentrates around its mean.

We start by recalling the curvature of the Elo chain when the environment is fixed.

Lemma 24

(

For every

Lemma 25
.

For every
t\geq 1
, every

, and every

W_{1}^{\|\cdot\|_{2}}\bigl(K_{e}(x,\cdot),K_{\widetilde{e}}(x,\cdot)\bigr)\leq\eta\,d_{\mathcal{E}}(e,\widetilde{e}).

Proof.

Write
e=(\rho,q)
and
\widetilde{e}=(\widetilde{\rho},\widetilde{q})
.
First suppose
q=\widetilde{q}
. Couple the selected pair of players to be the same and
use the same uniform random variable to generate the two match outcomes. For a

\frac{\eta}{2}\|\rho-\widetilde{\rho}\|_{2}.

Now suppose
\rho=\widetilde{\rho}

\|q-\widetilde{q}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}

\sqrt{2}\,\eta

and

By the triangle inequality for Wasserstein distance,

\displaystyle W_{1}^{\|\cdot\|_{2}}\bigl(K_{(\rho,q)}(x,\cdot),K_{(\widetilde{\rho},\widetilde{q})}(x,\cdot)\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq W_{1}^{\|\cdot\|_{2}}\bigl(K_{(\rho,q)}(x,\cdot),K_{(\widetilde{\rho},q)}(x,\cdot)\bigr)+W_{1}^{\|\cdot\|_{2}}\bigl(K_{(\widetilde{\rho},q)}(x,\cdot),K_{(\widetilde{\rho},\widetilde{q})}(x,\cdot)\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\eta}{2}\|\rho-\widetilde{\rho}\|_{2}+2\sqrt{2}\,\eta\|q-\widetilde{q}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}

∎

Lemma 26

For every

Proof.

Write

and
\widetilde{z}=(\widetilde{x},\widetilde{e})

\mathbb{E}d_{\mathcal{E}}(E^{\prime},\widetilde{E}^{\prime})=W_{1}^{d_{\mathcal{E}}}\bigl(\Gamma_{t}(e,\cdot),\Gamma_{t}(\widetilde{e},\cdot)\bigr)\leq(1-\nu)d_{\mathcal{E}}(e,\widetilde{e}).

Conditional on
E^{\prime}
and

5.4.
Main results

To ensure Elo is able to track the evolving true ratings, we need to quantify

The previous result tells us that Elo can track the ratings of the players, as long as they do not change too fast. Players’ form, however, ebbs and flows during a season. We prove a generalisation of Hoeffding’s lemma to the Hermitian setting. While similar results are well known (see, e.g.,
[
33
]
), we have not found a reference for exactly the following result.

Lemma 31

(Hermitian Hoeffding’s lemma)
.

Let
Y
be a random matrix in
\mathsf{H}_{m}
such that
\mathbb{E}Y=0
and assume there exists
R>0
such that
-RI_{m}\preceq Y\preceq RI_{m}
almost surely.
Then, for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
,

\mathbb{E}\big[\exp(sY)\big]\ \preceq\ \cosh(sR)\,I_{m}\ \preceq\ \exp\!\Big(\frac{s^{2}R^{2}}{2}\Big)I_{m}.

In particular,
\|\mathbb{E}[\exp(sY)]\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\exp(s^{2}R^{2}/2)
and

\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{tr}\exp(sY)]\leq m\,\exp(s^{2}R^{2}/2)
.

Proof.

Diagonalize
Y=U\Lambda U^{\ast}
with
\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m})
and
\lambda_{i}\in[-R,R]
.
For
y\in[-R,R]
, convexity of
e^{sy}
gives

e^{sy}\leq\frac{R+y}{2R}e^{sR}+\frac{R-y}{2R}e^{-sR}
.
Applying this inequality to each eigenvalue of
Y
,
we obtain

e^{sY}\ \preceq\ \frac{RI_{m}+Y}{2R}e^{sR}+\frac{RI_{m}-Y}{2R}e^{-sR}.

Taking expectations and using
\mathbb{E}Y=0
yields

\mathbb{E}e^{sY}\preceq\tfrac{1}{2}(e^{sR}+e^{-sR})I_{m}=\cosh(sR)I_{m}
.
Finally
\cosh(u)\leq e^{u^{2}/2}
for all
u\in\mathbb{R}
, so
\cosh(sR)I_{m}\preceq e^{s^{2}R^{2}/2}I_{m}
.
∎

We often need to bound the norm of (the difference of) matrix exponentials. We will use the following well known results, of which we include a proof for completeness.

Lemma 32

(Matrix exponential bounds)
.

For any
H\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
,
\|e^{H}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq e^{\|H\|_{\mathrm{op}}}
.
For any
A,B\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
,

\|e^{A}-e^{B}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq e^{\max\{\|A\|_{\mathrm{op}},\|B\|_{\mathrm{op}}\}}\ \|A-B\|_{\mathrm{op}}.

Moreover,

\|e^{A}-e^{B}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq e^{\max\{\|A\|_{\mathrm{op}},\|B\|_{\mathrm{op}}\}}\ \|A-B\|_{\mathrm{F}}.

Proof.

The first statement follows from the series definition of the matrix exponential and submultiplicativity of the operator norm.

For the remaining statements, we include a proof (which we learned from
[
34
]
) for completeness.
We first prove the representation

(31)

e^{A}-e^{B}\ =\ \int_{0}^{1}e^{(1-u)A}\,(A-B)\,e^{uB}\,du.

Define
f(u):=e^{(1-u)A}e^{uB}
for
u\in[0,1]
. Differentiating gives

f^{\prime}(u)=-Ae^{(1-u)A}e^{uB}+e^{(1-u)A}Be^{uB}=e^{(1-u)A}(B-A)e^{uB}.

Since
f(0)=e^{A}
and
f(1)=e^{B}
, we have

e^{A}-e^{B}=-\int_{0}^{1}f^{\prime}(u)\,du
,
which yields (
31
).

For the operator-norm bound, apply submultiplicativity, the first statement, and (
31
):

\|e^{A}-e^{B}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\int_{0}^{1}\|e^{(1-u)A}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,\|A-B\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,\|e^{uB}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,du\leq e^{\max\{\|A\|_{\mathrm{op}},\|B\|_{\mathrm{op}}\}}\,\|A-B\|_{\mathrm{op}}.

For the Frobenius-norm bound, use the inequality
\|XYZ\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\|X\|_{\mathrm{op}}\|Y\|_{\mathrm{F}}\|Z\|_{\mathrm{op}}
, together with (
31
) and the first statement of the lemma:

\|e^{A}-e^{B}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\int_{0}^{1}\|e^{(1-u)A}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,\|A-B\|_{\mathrm{F}}\,\|e^{uB}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,du\leq e^{\max\{\|A\|_{\mathrm{op}},\|B\|_{\mathrm{op}}\}}\,\|A-B\|_{\mathrm{F}}.

∎

The next lemma establishes a bound on the Lipschitz constant of the product of two (matrix-valued) functions.

Lemma 33

(Lipschitz product rule in operator norm)
.

Let
(\Omega,d)
be a metric space and let
G,H:\Omega\to\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
be matrix-valued maps.
Assume
\|G\|_{\infty},\|H\|_{\infty},\mathrm{Lip}(G)^{\mathrm{op}},\mathrm{Lip}(H)^{\mathrm{op}}<\infty
.
Then

\mathrm{Lip}(GH)^{\mathrm{op}}\ \leq\ \|G\|_{\infty}\,\mathrm{Lip}(H)^{\mathrm{op}}\ +\ \mathrm{Lip}(G)^{\mathrm{op}}\,\|H\|_{\infty}.

Proof.

For
x\neq y
,

G(x)H(x)-G(y)H(y)=G(x)\big(H(x)-H(y)\big)+\big(G(x)-G(y)\big)H(y).

Taking operator norms and using submultiplicativity,

\|G(x)H(x)-G(y)H(y)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\|G(x)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,\|H(x)-H(y)\|_{\mathrm{op}}+\|G(x)-G(y)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,\|H(y)\|_{\mathrm{op}}.

Divide by
d(x,y)
and take the supremum over
x\neq y
to obtain

\mathrm{Lip}(GH)^{\mathrm{op}}\leq\|G\|_{\infty}\,\mathrm{Lip}(H)^{\mathrm{op}}+\mathrm{Lip}(G)^{\mathrm{op}}\,\|H\|_{\infty}.

∎

P\mathrm{tr}\exp\left(H+G\right)(x)\leq\mathrm{tr}\exp\left(H+PG(x)+2L^{2}\sigma_{\infty}^{2}(x)I_{m}\right).

Proof.

First of all we notice that, for any
y\in\mathrm{Supp}P(x,\cdot)
,
\|G(y)-PG(x)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq L\sigma_{\infty}(x)
.
Then, by
Lemma

34
,

\displaystyle P\mathrm{tr}\exp\left(H+G\right)(x)

\displaystyle=P\mathrm{tr}\exp\left(H+PG(x)+G-PG(x)\right)(x)

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{tr}\exp\left(H+PG(x)+2L^{2}\sigma_{\infty}^{2}(x)I_{m}\right).

∎

The first main theorem of the section shows that positive curvature implies
F_{T}(X_{T})
concentrates around its expectation. This generalises Theorem 22 in
[
24
]
.

Theorem 36
.

Let
F_{T}:\Omega\to\mathsf{H}_{m}
be
L
-Lipschitz. Let
\sigma_{\infty}\coloneqq\sup_{t\geq 1,x\in\Omega}\mathrm{diam}\mathrm{Supp}P_{t}(x,\cdot)
.
Then, for any
T\geq 1
,

\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}}\!\left(\left\|F_{T}(X_{T})-\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}\!\left[F_{T}(X_{T})\right]\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq 2m\,\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\kappa}{8L^{2}\sigma_{\infty}^{2}}\right)

Proof.

Let
s>0
. We bound the matrix moment generating function

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}\!\left[\mathrm{tr}\exp(sF_{T}(X_{T}))\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega^{T}}\mathrm{tr}\exp(sF_{T}(X_{T}))\,P_{T}(X_{T-1},dX_{T})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\cdot P_{T-1}(X_{T-2},dX_{T-1})\cdots P_{1}(X_{0},dX_{1})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega^{T-1}}\left(P_{T}\mathrm{tr}\exp(sF_{T})\right)(X_{T-1})\,

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\cdot P_{T-1}(X_{T-2},dX_{T-1})\cdots P_{1}(X_{0},dX_{1})

Applying
Lemma

35
(with
P=P_{T}
,
H=0
, and
G=sF_{T}
) yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}\!\left[\mathrm{tr}\exp(sF_{T}(X_{T}))\right]

\displaystyle\,\leq\int_{\Omega^{T-1}}\mathrm{tr}\exp\left(s(P_{T}F_{T})(X_{T-1})+2s^{2}L^{2}\sigma_{\infty}^{2}I_{m}\right)\,P_{T-1}(X_{T-2},dX_{T-1})\cdots P_{1}(X_{0},dX_{1})

\displaystyle\,=\int_{\Omega^{T-2}}\Bigl(P_{T-1}\mathrm{tr}\exp\left(s(P_{T}F_{T})+2s^{2}L^{2}\sigma_{\infty}^{2}I_{m}\right)\Bigr)(X_{T-2})\,P_{T-2}(X_{T-3},dX_{T-2})\cdots P_{1}(X_{0},dX_{1}).

Applying
Lemma

35
a second time (with
P=P_{T-1}
and
G=s\,P_{T}F_{T}
) and using the contraction

\mathrm{Lip}(P_{T}F_{T})^{\mathrm{op}}\leq(1-\kappa_{T})\,L
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}\!\left[\mathrm{tr}\exp(sF_{T}(X_{T}))\right]

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega^{T-2}}\mathrm{tr}\exp\!\begin{aligned} &\biggl(s(P_{T-1}P_{T}F_{T})(X_{T-2})+2s^{2}L^{2}\sigma_{\infty}^{2}I_{m}\\[-0.86108pt]
&{}+2(1-\kappa_{T})^{2}s^{2}L^{2}\sigma_{\infty}^{2}I_{m}\biggr)\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\hskip 62.00006pt\cdot P_{T-2}(X_{T-3},dX_{T-2})\cdots P_{1}(X_{0},dX_{1}).

Iterating this argument and using

\mathrm{Lip}(P_{T-k:T}F_{T})^{\mathrm{op}}\leq L\prod_{t=T-k}^{T}(1-\kappa_{t})
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}\!\left[\mathrm{tr}\exp(sF_{T}(X_{T}))\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{tr}\exp\left(s(P_{1:T}F_{T})(X_{0})+2s^{2}L^{2}\sigma_{\infty}^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\prod_{t=T-k+1}^{T}(1-\kappa_{t})^{2}I_{m}\right).

By
1
,

\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}\!\left[\mathrm{tr}\exp(sF_{T}(X_{T}))\right]\leq\mathrm{tr}\exp\left(s\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}\!\left[F_{T}(X_{T})\right]+2s^{2}L^{2}\sigma_{\infty}^{2}\kappa^{-1}I_{m}\right).

Applying the previous moment bound to the centred function

\widetilde{F}_{T}:=F_{T}-\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}[F_{T}(X_{T})]
and then using Markov’s inequality,

\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}}\!\left(\lambda_{\max}\left(F_{T}(X_{T})-\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}\!\left[F_{T}(X_{T})\right]\right)\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq m\exp\left(-s\varepsilon+2s^{2}L^{2}\sigma_{\infty}^{2}\kappa^{-1}\right).

Choosing
s=\frac{\varepsilon\kappa}{4L^{2}\sigma_{\infty}^{2}}
yields

\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}}\!\left(\lambda_{\max}\left(F_{T}(X_{T})-\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}\!\left[F_{T}(X_{T})\right]\right)\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq m\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\kappa}{8L^{2}\sigma_{\infty}^{2}}\right).

The statement follows by symmetry.

∎

We now generalise the concentration inequality for empirical means of
13
. For technical reasons, we will use a slightly different definition of effective curvature.

Assumption 7

(Positive curvature, alternative definition)
.

For all
t\geq 1
, we have
\kappa_{t}\in[0,1]
. Furthermore, there exists
\tilde{\kappa}\in(0,1]
such that, for all
1\leq s\leq t
,

1+\sum_{k=s}^{t}\prod_{\ell=s}^{k}(1-\kappa_{\ell})\leq\ \frac{1}{\tilde{\kappa}}.

Theorem 37
.

Let
F_{t}:\Omega\to\mathsf{H}_{m}
be
L
-Lipschitz for all
t\geq 1
.

Let
\sigma_{\infty}\coloneqq\sup_{t\geq 1,x\in\Omega}\mathrm{diam}\mathrm{Supp}P_{t}(x,\cdot)
.
Then, for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}}\!\left(\left\|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{k=1}^{T}F_{k}(X_{k})-\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}\!\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{k=1}^{T}F_{k}(X_{k})\right]\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}>\varepsilon\right)\leq 2m\exp\left(-\frac{\tilde{\kappa}^{2}T\varepsilon^{2}}{8L^{2}\sigma_{\infty}^{2}}\right).

Remark 38
.

Notice that
Theorem

37
does not require an explicit bound on the diameter. It does so, however, at a cost of a quadratic dependency on
\kappa^{-1}
.

Proof.

We bound the matrix moment generating function of
(1/T)\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{T}F_{k}(X_{k})
by an inductive argument.
In particular, we prove that, for any
1\leq t\leq T
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}\!\left[\mathrm{tr}\exp\left(\frac{s}{T}\sum_{k=1}^{T}F_{k}(X_{k})\right)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\int_{\Omega^{T-t}}\mathrm{tr}\exp\left(\frac{s}{T}\sum_{k=1}^{T-t}F_{k}(X_{k})+\frac{s}{T}\sum_{j=1}^{t}P_{T-t+1}\cdots P_{T-t+j}F_{T-t+j}(X_{T-t})+\frac{2ts^{2}L^{2}\sigma_{\infty}^{2}}{\tilde{\kappa}^{2}T^{2}}I_{m}\right)

(32)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad P_{T-t}(X_{T-t-1},dX_{T-t})\cdots P_{1}(X_{0},dX_{1}).

We start with the base case
t=1
. We apply
Lemma

35
choosing
P=P_{T}
,
H=(s/T)\sum_{k=1}^{T-1}F_{k}(X_{k})
, and
g=(s/T)F_{T}
. We obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}\!\left[\mathrm{tr}\exp\left(\frac{s}{T}\sum_{k=1}^{T}F_{k}(X_{k})\right)\right]

\displaystyle\,=\int_{\Omega^{T}}\mathrm{tr}\exp\left(\frac{s}{T}\sum_{k=1}^{T}F_{k}(X_{k})\right)\,P_{T}(X_{T-1},dX_{T})P_{T-1}(X_{T-2},dX_{T-1})\cdots P_{1}(X_{0},dX_{1})

\displaystyle\,=\int_{\Omega^{T-1}}P_{T}\mathrm{tr}\exp\left(\frac{s}{T}\sum_{k=1}^{T}F_{k}(X_{k})\right)(X_{T-1})P_{T-1}(X_{T-2},dX_{T-1})\cdots P_{1}(X_{0},dX_{1})

\displaystyle\,\leq\int_{\Omega^{T-1}}\mathrm{tr}\exp\!\begin{aligned} &\biggl(\frac{s}{T}\sum_{k=1}^{T-1}F_{k}(X_{k})+\frac{sP_{T}F_{T}(X_{T-1})}{T}+2L^{2}\sigma_{\infty}^{2}s^{2}T^{-2}I_{m}\biggr)\end{aligned}\,

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\cdot P_{T-1}(X_{T-2},dX_{T-1})\cdots P_{1}(X_{0},dX_{1}).

Suppose now
Eq.

32
holds for
1\leq t\leq T-1
.
We apply
Lemma

35
with
P=P_{T-t}
and
g=\frac{s}{T}F_{T-t}+\frac{s}{T}\sum_{j=1}^{t}P_{T-t+1:T-t+j}F_{T-t+j}
.
Notice that, by
7
,

\mathrm{Lip}(g)\leq\frac{sL}{T}\left(1+\sum_{j=1}^{t}\prod_{i=T-t+1}^{T-t+j}(1-\kappa_{i})\right)\leq\frac{sL}{\tilde{\kappa}T}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}\!\left[\mathrm{tr}\exp\left(\frac{s}{T}\sum_{k=1}^{T}F_{k}(X_{k})\right)\right]

\displaystyle\,\leq\int_{\Omega^{T-t}}\mathrm{tr}\exp\!\begin{aligned} &\biggl(\frac{s}{T}\sum_{k=1}^{T-t-1}F_{k}(X_{k})+\frac{s}{T}F_{T-t}(X_{T-t})\\[-0.86108pt]
&{}+\frac{s}{T}\sum_{j=1}^{t}P_{T-t+1}\cdots P_{T-t+j}F_{T-t+j}(X_{T-t})+\frac{2tL^{2}s^{2}\sigma_{\infty}^{2}}{\tilde{\kappa}^{2}T^{2}}I_{m}\biggr)\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\,\qquad\qquad P_{T-t}(X_{T-t-1},dX_{T-t})\cdots P_{1}(X_{0},dX_{1})

\displaystyle\,\leq\int_{\Omega^{T-t-1}}\mathrm{tr}\exp\!\begin{aligned} &\biggl(\frac{s}{T}\sum_{k=1}^{T-t-1}F_{k}(X_{k})+\frac{s}{T}\sum_{j=1}^{t+1}P_{T-t}\cdots P_{T-t-1+j}F_{T-t-1+j}(X_{T-t-1})\\[-0.86108pt]
&{}+\frac{2L^{2}s^{2}\sigma_{\infty}^{2}}{\tilde{\kappa}^{2}T^{2}}I_{m}+\frac{2tL^{2}s^{2}\sigma_{\infty}^{2}}{\tilde{\kappa}^{2}T^{2}}I_{m}\biggr)\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\,\qquad\qquad P_{T-t-1}(X_{T-t-2},dX_{T-t-1})\cdots P_{1}(X_{0},dX_{1}),

which proves
Eq.

32
.

After
t=T
steps,
Eq.

32
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}\!\left[\mathrm{tr}\exp\left(\frac{s}{T}\sum_{k=1}^{T}F_{k}(X_{k})\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{tr}\exp\left(\frac{s}{T}\sum_{k=1}^{T}P_{1}\cdots P_{k}F_{k}(X_{0})+\frac{2s^{2}L^{2}\sigma_{\infty}^{2}}{\tilde{\kappa}^{2}T}I_{m}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\exp\left(\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}\!\left[\frac{s}{T}\sum_{k=1}^{T}F_{k}(X_{k})\right]+\frac{2s^{2}L^{2}\sigma_{\infty}^{2}}{\tilde{\kappa}^{2}T}I_{m}\right).

Apply the preceding moment bound to the centred observables

\widetilde{F}_{k}:=F_{k}-\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}[F_{k}(X_{k})]
. Set
s=\frac{\varepsilon\tilde{\kappa}^{2}T}{4L^{2}\sigma_{\infty}^{2}}
and use Markov’s inequality to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}}\!\left(\lambda_{\max}\left(\frac{1}{T}\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{T}F_{k}(X_{k})-\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}\!\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{k=1}^{T}F_{k}(X_{k})\right]\right)>\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq\exp(-s\varepsilon)\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}\!\left[\mathrm{tr}\exp\left(\frac{s}{T}\sum_{k=1}^{T}F_{k}(X_{k})-\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}{\frac{s}{T}\sum_{k=1}^{T}F_{k}(X_{k})}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq\exp(-s\varepsilon)\mathrm{tr}\exp\left(\frac{2s^{2}L^{2}\sigma_{\infty}^{2}}{\tilde{\kappa}^{2}T}I_{m}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq m\exp\left(\frac{2s^{2}L^{2}\sigma_{\infty}^{2}}{\tilde{\kappa}^{2}T}-s\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq m\exp\left(-\frac{\tilde{\kappa}^{2}T\varepsilon^{2}}{8L^{2}\sigma_{\infty}^{2}}\right).

The statement of the theorem follows by considering
