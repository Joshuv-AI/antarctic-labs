Comparative analysis of Asterias forbesi development reveals distinct mechanisms of hydro-vascular organ formation across sea stars
Authors: Talia Kathleen Marc, Allison Rodger, Margherita Perillo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA central question in developmental biology is how organs acquire their final shape. Since a conserved organ might form through distinct steps, even within animals belonging to the same class, comparative studies are needed to reveal conserved or unique principles. Sea stars are a useful system to investigate how organs develop their specific structure across various species. This species-rich and diverse class of animals provide embryos that can be easily cultured in large numbers and are optically clear, which allows morphogenesis to be observed live. All sea star larvae develop a coelom: a hollow tube that fills the body cavity. We recently named it the “hydro-vascular organ” (HVO), as most of this organ will form the adult water-vascular system after metamorphosis. Understanding the distinct steps that drive HVO development across species requires comparative studies, but detailed characterizations of embryonic and larval stages have been reported for only a few representatives of asteroidea. Here we use brightfield microscopy and live imaging to document for the first time the development of Asterias forbesi, an ecologically important species that lacks a detailed description of embryonic and larval stages. Leveraging the optical clarity of these larvae, we defined distinct stages of HVO morphogenesis. Introduction

The sea star Asterias forbesi (common name Forbes’s Starfish, order Forcipulatida) is found in shallow waters of the North Atlantic Ocean from Maine to the Gulf of Mexico. This species is widely studied for their reproduction, behavior, and ecological role as a keystone species [1–6], as well as to understand the mechanisms of the sea star wasting disease, a pathology that dramatically affected their population levels [7, 8]. A. forbesi was also a pioneer system used to discover the neuroendocrine control of spawning. Researchers found that radial nerve extracts induced the shedding of gametes when injected in another animal [9, 10]. A. forbesi larvae are also largely studied because they are an important indicator of ecological changes, and their settlement preferences have been related to the survival of adult populations [11, 12].

Despite being a popular system that has been studied for more than seventy years, we still lack a clear description of A. forbesi embryonic and larval development. Sea star larvae are emerging as a convenient framework to study organogenesis and regeneration [13–17]. As such, a detailed characterization of A. forbesi larval morphogenesis is needed, particularly for comparative studies with other sea star species. In fact, comparing the molecular mechanisms that guide organ morphogenesis across species is critical to understand organ evolution and how new traits evolved. Sea star larvae provide an ideal system to investigate organ evolution, in part because we can watch the development of their coelom, a critical organ for their larval state. The coelom is a hollow tube that carries liquids, encircles the digestive system, and eventually fills the whole larval cavity [18–20]. Based on its morphology we recently named it the hydro-vascular organ (HVO), since part of this structure will eventually form the adult water-vascular system used for locomotion [17, 20–22]. Many hypotheses have been suggested for the HVO’s function, including buoyancy and excretion [23, 24]. An aspect of larval development that remains poorly understood is how the coelom forms, and whether it grows in all sea star species following the same pattern. In the model sea star Patiria miniata the HVO has recently been used to understand tube morphogenesis, revealing that this structure forms through mechanisms that are conserved with vertebrate organs [17]. Although the final shape of the HVO is consistent across sea stars, the steps of HVO development have never been compared between species.

Here we describe A. forbesi embryonic and larval development until the juvenile stage. To our knowledge, this is the first detailed developmental description for this species. We identify four steps of HVO development and compare it to sea stars belonging to two different orders, Patiria miniata (Valvatida) and Astropecten aranciacus (Paxillosida).

Results

Embryogenesis, gastrulation and bipinnaria larvae

Because a clear staging for the embryos of Asterias forbesi is missing, we used brightfield microscopy and live imaging to document the early development of this species. A. forbesi oocytes were clear and spherical. Like all sea star species, oocytes were arrested in prophase I and showed a large nucleus, also known as germinal vesicle, with a prominent nucleolus (Fig. 1A, arrested oocyte). Twenty minutes after 1-Methyladenine was added to the media, the oocyte resumed meiosis, the nucleus broke down and the oocyte was mature (Fig. 1A, mature oocyte and Movie 1). Upon fertilization, the zygote was surrounded by the fertilization envelope that rose from the surface of the oocyte (Fig. 1A, fertilized oocyte). The first divisions were equal and synchronous with loosely bound blastomeres (Fig. 1A, 2 to 16 cells and Movie 2). After the first cell divisions, blastomere size decreased and cells formed a single monolayer epithelium with firmly adhering cells that encircled a cavity and gave rise to a pre-hatched blastula (Fig. 1A, pre-hatched blastula). Movie 2 shows that blastulae rotated within the fertilization envelope and hatched out of the envelope to swim in the water using cilia beating. At the swimming blastula stage, gastrulation started at the vegetal pole to form the endomesoderm (Fig. 1A, swimming blastula). At this stage, we observed that in all embryos a single mesenchymal cell appeared before gastrulation started, and time lapses showed that this single cell moved into the blastocoel (Fig. 1A, swimming blastula and Movie 2). Gastrulation began at ∼18 hours post fertilization (from here on referred to as hours, h) when the entire embryo elongated along the anterior-posterior axis (Fig. 1B, 18-27h). Invagination of cells from the vegetal pole formed the archenteron (the future gut) that then protruded into the blastocoel. The point of invagination during gastrulation corresponded to the first opening of the embryo, which is called the blastopore. A. forbesi gastrulae were optically clear and we observed cells at the tip of the archenteron in the process of changing shape prior to ingression from the epithelium (Fig. 1B, 27 h inset, arrow). When the gut reached the middle of the blastocoel, these cells acquired a mesenchymal phenotype and started to migrate out from the archenteron tip (Fig. 1B, 30-34h, cartoon in Fig. 1B’). During late gastrulation two main morphological changes happened. First, from ∼40 to 44 h the tip of the gut (primordium of the mesoderm) expanded laterally to form two closed coeloms (right and left coeloms) that lie along the sides of the anterior gut (Fig. 1B, 44 h arrows and cartoons in Fig. 1B’’ and B’’’). Second, the gut bent towards a small depression that formed on the ventral side of the ectoderm and fused with the ectoderm to form the mouth (Fig. 1B, 44 h ventral and lateral views, white arrowhead). Also at this stage, the blastopore moved ventrally to form the anus (Fig. 1B, 44 h lateral view). After the two openings of the gut were formed, the embryo fed on microalgae and developed into a bipinnaria larva. From this time on, food accumulated in the stomach.

Organogenesis began during these early larval stages. The gut became a tripartite digestive system differentiated into a long esophagus, round stomach and tubular intestine (Fig. 2A-E, 48 h, 52 h and 3 d). A small, rounded coelom appeared on the posterior left side of the stomach that resembled the posterior enterocoel (PE, Fig. 2B, C arrow), the site of the primordial germ cells' localization [25]. In 10% of the embryos (n = 8/80) two posterior enterocoels were located on the posterior side of the stomach (Fig. 2D, 52 h two arrows). Around day 3 after fertilization, the larva elongated along the antero-posterior axis, the stomach was round (Fig. 2E) and the esophagus was fully covered by parallel muscle fibers (Fig. 2E, arrow’). The cartoon in Fig. 2 F summarizes the main tissues of the 3-day old larva. The left coelom elongated dorsally into a tube that fused with the ectoderm above the stomach to form the hydropore, the only opening that connects the coelom with the external environment (Fig. 2G, 3 d inset). The ectoderm of early larvae (from 3 days on) is characterized by two ciliary bands that extend across the anterior-posterior and dorsal-ventral ectoderm. These distinctive traits of sea star bipinnaria larvae are composed of ciliated cells that form a translucent band (Fig. 2H). Cilia from these ciliary bands formed the locomotor apparatus. Two additional cell types were clearly visible in the blastocoel and underneath the ectoderm at around 1 week: mesenchymal cells with long filopodia that moved underneath the ectoderm, and cells filled with granules embedded in the ectoderm (Fig. 2I 7 d and Movie 3). To further characterize these two populations, we tracked cell movements over a two-minute period (Movie 4 and Movie 5). We plotted movement of mesenchymal cells (mc) and granular cells (gc) to show that while mesenchymal cells are highly motile, granular cells do not move from their position (Fig. 2J). Both cell types are more abundant in the anterior half of the larva than the posterior one (Fig. 2K). Overall, both cell types were more frequently associated with the ciliary band (embedded or underneath it, Fig. 2K).

In conclusion, during the first week of development, A. forbesi development is similar to most planktotrophic sea star species, where the fertilized egg transforms into a fully functional larva that swims and feeds in the water column.

Brachiolaria larva development until metamorphosis

As the early larva transformed into a bipinnaria, the ectoderm began to fold to form a curved ciliary band that hinged inward (Fig. 3A, B, 18 d and 20 d). These folds were particularly obvious at the ventral side in the oral and post-oral hoods (Fig. 3 A, B oh and ph, arrows). At ∼18 days after fertilization the first primordia of the 10 bipinnaria arms started to form and by 20 days the elongating posterolateral arms were visible (Fig. 3A, B pla). During the bipinnaria stage the left and right coelomic pouches elongated posteriorly and anteriorly, eventually forming the closed loop of the hydro-vascular organ (Fig. 3A, B, C).

The brachiolaria larva differed from the bipinnaria because of their long brachiolaria arms and development of adult organs, including attachment organs for settlement. The first arms to elongate were the posterolateral arms that formed from the ectoderm adjacent to the stomach, followed by the postoral and posterodorsal arms (Fig. 3D). These projections were used for swimming by pushing the water against the advancement direction through a sort of breaststroke (Movie 6). Once larvae reached one month, the median and lateral brachiolaria arms were elongated and the anterior protrusion extended dorsally (Fig. 3E, F). We performed additional fluorescent immunostaining to understand the morphology of the brachiolaria arms. We identified two main features of A. forbesi arms: the outside was covered in short cilia, and internally approximately 16 parallel muscles fibers extended from the blastocoel to the arm tip (Fig. 3G, G’, G’’). This finding suggests that the fast and forceful arm movements used for swimming happen by muscle contraction.

Because A. forbesi arms were much longer than those of P. miniata, a more broadly used sea star model system, we compared their arm morphology at the same developmental stage. We found that in P. miniata short parallel muscle fibers were an extension of the dorsal muscles and were located underneath the brachiolaria arm ectoderm, not in the middle of the arms like in A. forbesi (Fig. 3H, H’,H’’). The different arm morphology of P. miniata larvae reflects the fact that they do not rely on their arms to swim but use beating cilia as their primary means of propulsion [26]. Our characterization of arm morphology suggests that muscle organization within the arm contributes to their function.

The first adult organs appeared at week 3 when the first elements of the adult skeleton became visible on the stomach. These elements (Fig. 3D, inset 1) started as simple spicules that grew over time to form the net of the future juvenile skeleton. At the same time, additional adult structures began to form on the left side of the hydro-vascular organ (HVO). On the side of the mesodermal hydrocoel, the epithelium thickened and folded over a 1-week period. These folds formed five lobes, creating the rudiment of the primary podia. Initially, 3 lobes grew to the left side of the stomach perpendicularly to the larval ventral plane (Fig. 3D, inset 2). About one week later, (when larvae were 1 month old), the full 5 lobes were formed between the stomach and the left posterolateral arm, and the tip of the lobes was pigmented while the central disk was clear (Fig. 3F, F’).

While the hydrocoel grew and folded, an opaque and dark rudiment formed on the posterior side of the larva and eventually surrounded the stomach in the 40-day-old larvae (Fig. 4A, A’). Eventually the juvenile mouth and arms will descend from this rudiment [27]. The juvenile grew from the rudiment until it resembled a disk on the posterior side of the larva with its dorsal side facing out and the ventral side facing the five hydrocoel lobes and larval stomach (Fig. 4B inset 1). The juvenile surface was fully covered by multiple skeletal spicules (sk), which are the future adult skeleton (Fig. 4B inset 1).

Other distinctive features of A. forbesi brachiolaria were the settlement and metamorphosis structures that developed at its antero-ventral side: a pair of brachiolar arms (left and right) and a long median ventral lobe in between them (Fig. 4B inset 2). These three structures had six pigmented adhesive papillae on their tips (Fig. 4B inset 2). In between the brachiolar arms there was the adhesive disk (Fig. 4B inset 2), an oval-shape structure that is used, along with brachiolar arms, to adhere to the substratum during metamorphosis [28].

In sea stars, metamorphosis is a gradual process that involves reabsorption of larval structures [29]. Most A. forbesi larvae underwent metamorphosis in around 3 months. This dramatic process was completed in about a week, with the anterior structures slowly being absorbed into the juvenile (Fig. 4C). Newly born juveniles were highly mobile, and their mouth formed on the ventral side in a week (Fig. 4D). Juveniles spent most of their time adhering on the beaker walls with their tube feet.

In conclusion, A. forbesi oocytes are fertilized in sea water and through a series of stereotypical cleavages form early embryos. Gastrulae transform into swimming bipinnaria larvae that become brachiolariae with long arms and distinct metamorphosis related structures (i.e. brachiolaria arms, attachment disk, rudiment). By two months, brachiolaria larvae develop a dark rudiment covered in skeletal elements and are ready to undergo metamorphosis into juveniles (Fig. 4E).

Hydro-vascular organ develops through four distinct stages

Because A. forbesi larvae had a clear and long hydro-vascular organ (HVO), we investigated its growth over time by brightfield imaging and by quantifying tube elongation and shape. We identified 4 stages of HVO morphogenesis that are summarized in Fig. 5G. Stage 1 began at the end of gastrulation (40 h) when the tip of the gut bent outward on its left and right side. By 42 h, individual cells on the side of the gut tip were clearly visible. By 44 h, two coelomic pouches budded off the left and right tip of the gut and elongated toward the larval posterior side (Fig. 5 A). In stage 2, as the gut also began to differentiate into three regions (foregut, midgut and hindgut) and the coelomic pouches detached from the foregut (Fig. 5B, 48 h). The coelomic pouches were made by a single epithelial layer that surrounded a lumen, and by 52 h they were localized near the cardiac sphincter, the esophagus-stomach junction (Fig. 5B, 52 h). Differences between the two pouches appeared around 72 h, as they moved in two different planes: the right pouch stayed in the larva midline while the left one moved dorsally, where it formed an opening in the dorsal ectoderm, the hydropore canal (Fig. 5B, 72 h). In stage 3, as larvae became bigger, both coelomic pouches elongated and became tube-shaped. First the two tubes elongated parallel to the stomach toward the posterior side (Fig. 5C, 7 d). Then, after the tubes reached the junction of the stomach with the intestine, they began to grow anteriorly, elongating over the mouth (Fig. 5C, 10 d). During tube growth we found that the lumen area of the left tube was significantly bigger than the right tube and this difference persisted until the two tubes merged anteriorly (Fig. 5H). An additional difference between the two tubes was their shape. During the first hours of organogenesis (48 to 52 h) the right tube had a round shape while the left tube was more elongated (Fig. 5I). In the bipinnaria larva (7d) both tubes drastically changed towards an elongated shape (Fig. 5I). Finally, in stage 4, both tubes reached the larva apex and merged to form a single tubular organ, the HVO (Fig. 5D). Once the two tubes of the HVO merged anteriorly, the whole structure became elongated and acquired an oval shape (Fig. 5I). A second connection between the two tubes was made as the tubes merged posterior to the intestine around week 3 of development. In stage 4 the HVO grew with the larva and occupied the whole blastocoeliar space (Fig. 5D, 3w). We observed that the HVO grew into the medial ventral lobe and later into the brachiolar arms, suggesting a role in keeping the shape of these larval protrusions (Fig. 5E, F). We found that the HVO is highly muscular, as staining with Phalloidin showed thick, parallel muscle fibers around the HVO (Fig. 3G). In addition, live imaging showed that the left elongating tube that formed the opening of the hydropore canal twitched and contracted, suggesting that the muscular layer helps pump the fluid in the HVO lumen (Movie 7).

In conclusion, the HVO elongates through 4 stages to eventually fill the whole blastocoeliar space, including larval protrusions, which supports the shape of the growing brachiolaria larva.

Comparison of HVOs in bipinnaria larvae

All sea stars with a larval planktotrophic phase develop a hydro-vascular organ [19, 30]. Having described HVO development in A. forbesi, we next compared it to two other sea star species to test whether all HVOs develop through the same stages. As A. forbesi belongs to the order Forcipulatida, we chose species from two different orders: the bat star Patiria miniata, that belongs to the order Valvatida and lives in the US Pacific coast, and Astropecten aranciacus that belongs to the Paxillosida order and lives in the Mediterranean Sea.

P. miniata gastrulae looked similar to A. forbesi, with mesenchymal cells coming from the tip of the archenteron (Fig. 6A). Like A. forbesi, at the late gastrula stage in P. miniata the tip of the gut bent to form two coelomic pouches by the side of the foregut (Fig. 6B). In P. miniata the coelomic pouches immediately acquired a tube shape and the first connection between them was formed when the anterior tips merged above the mouth in the early larva, at day 3 (Fig. 6C). After this first connection, the two tubes of the HVO elongated posteriorly and by the brachiolaria stage (3 weeks) they also merged at the posterior side (Fig. 6D and [17]).

Next, we used brightfield microscopy to analyze HVO formation in A. aranciacus, a species for which larval development has previously only been described with drawings [31]. Gastrulae were similar to A. forbesi and P. miniata, with migrating cells at the tip of the growing gut (Fig. 6E). At the late gastrula stage, the tip of the gut bent to form the two coelomic pouches. Distinctly from A. forbesi and P. miniata, however, we observed that the epithelium of the tip of the gut underwent structural changes (Fig. 6F). While in the mid gastrula the tip of the archenteron has the same thickness as other regions of the gut (Fig. 6E’), we observed that this structure changed during gastrulation and become much thinner (Fig. 6F’). However, further imaging is needed to define specific epithelial changes. By the early larva stage (3 days), the pouches elongated to form two long and wide tubes that were already extended along the stomach, beyond the cardiac sphincter (Fig. 6G). In the bipinnaria larva (1 week), the first connection of the two tubes was made at the posterior side of the larva, around the intestine (Fig. 6H). The tubes anterior tips were below the esophagus midline (Fig. 6H).

In conclusion, comparisons of the HVO formation in three sea star species showed that this organ always comes from the tip of the growing gut but depending on the species it follows different elongation strategies.

Discussion

In this study we describe for the first time the embryonic and larval development of A. forbesi, a sea star from the Northwest Atlantic Ocean. The main embryonic stages are similar to other species of the genus Asterias, like A. vulgaris, A. rubens and A. amurensis [32–35]. Using live imaging we observed that early cleavage divisions are synchronous and that the blastomeres divide equally. An uncommon trait we found is the presence of a single cell, maybe a polar body, that enters the blastocoel at the blastula stage and that is found close to the ectoderm during gastrulation.

Organogenesis begins in the early bipinnaria larva, when the gut differentiates into an esophagus, stomach, and intestine, and the coelomic pouches bud off the tip of the gut. The presence of a posterior enterocoel to the left side of the stomach suggests this is the site of primordial germ cells [36], a finding that will require germ cell markers to be validated. As observed for the genus Asterias, the ciliated band of bipinnaria larva extends to form the first pair of posterolateral arms with additional pairs forming at the brachiolaria stage. It has been proposed that longer ciliary bands are linked to higher feeding efficiency, as they improve the clearance rate of food particles [37]. In line with this hypothesis, our live imaging suggests that A. forbesi larvae use the long arms to propel themselves and probably to retrieve food particles. We found that the arms are supported by parallel muscle fibers, suggesting that the breaststroke observed in Asterias species might be controlled by the nervous system. The nervous system of the genus Asterias has been characterized only in A. rubens, in which early larvae have neuronal projections around the ciliary band [33, 34]. However, since there are no data on the nervous system of brachiolaria stages for Asterias, further studies are needed to support the hypothesis that the neuronal net grows into the brachiolaria arms and connects to the muscles.

A fascinating question in developmental biology is how a given organ can form through different morphological steps within the same group of animals. Sea stars offer a convenient model to investigate this question, as all sea stars with indirect development form a hydro-vascular organ (HVO). Previous literature suggests that the HVO might be a buoyancy or an excretory organ [23, 24]. Gene knock out studies demonstrated that the HVO is vital for sea star larvae, as larvae without this organ do not survive [17]. Comparisons with sea stars of two other orders, P. miniata and A. aranciacus, showed that not all HVOs are formed through the same steps. The cartoons in Fig. 6I summarize our main findings. In A. forbesi, the left and right coelomic pouches grow parallel to the esophagus from its midline to the cardiac sphincter (Fig. 6I, 1a). The coelomic pouches become tubes that first elongate posteriorly towards the stomach (Fig. 6I, 2a), then grow anteriorly to merge over the mouth (Fig. 6I, 3a). The timing of these steps is shifted earlier in P. miniata larva. We have previously shown that the tubes of coelomic pouches elongate posteriorly [17] (Fig. 6I, 1b), then before the tubes grow past the cardiac sphincter they make their first connection anteriorly over the mouth (Fig. 6I,2b). Finally, the two connected tubes grow posteriorly towards the stomach and eventually make a second connection around the intestine (Fig. 6I, 3b). The main difference between HVO growth in these two species is that in A. forbesi the tubes elongate posteriorly to the sides of the stomach and then grow anteriorly to merge, while in P. miniata this first connection is made earlier, before the tubes elongate towards the sides of the stomach. In this study we also include gastrulae and larvae of Astropecten aranciacus, a species that has been poorly studied [31]. In A. aranciacus the coelomic pouches grow posteriorly towards the stomach (Fig. 6I, 1c), then the two tubes merge posteriorly over the intestine (Fig. 6I, 2c). Thus, in A. aranciacus, the HVO develops through a third strategy: the first connection is made posteriorly, then the two connected tubes grow anteriorly to form the closed loop of the HVO. Our brightfield images of A. aranciacus gastrulae and larvae recapitulated the only other source of information on this species: drawings of the early divisions, larval stages, and metamorphosis [31]. We did not follow HVO development in A. aranciacus after the early bipinnaira stage, but prior work with other members of the Astropecten genus, A. armatus and A. scoparius, show that the HVO eventually merges at the anterior side of the larva and becomes like the final organ in A. forbesi and P. miniata [37, 38]. These results suggest that in three sea stars belonging to different orders, the final HVO organs have similar shapes, but they form through alternative strategies. An open question is what signaling pathways drive tube morphogenesis in diverse species. In P. miniata the FGF pathway drives tube elongation and the Wnt pathway regulates tube growth directionality [17]. Genomic and transcriptomic investigations will help us identify whether these pathways play a role in HVO morphogenesis in A. forbesi, and where the main receptor and ligands are expressed.

Other echinoderm larvae also develop left and right coeloms that start from similar steps and later diverge in their final structures. For instance, in sea urchins the two pouches bud from the anterior end of the foregut that elongate along the esophagus toward the larval apex [39, 40]. The sea urchin coelomic pouches differ from the sea star HVO as the thin tubes have several constrictions along their length to form clear compartments and the two pouches never merge anteriorly. Similarly, holothurians develop pouches that never fuse to form a single organ. They also differ from other echinoderm members because they do not make a rudiment [41, 42]. In summary, even if other echinoderms have structures that are developmentally similar to the HVO, sea stars are the only larvae making a full, looped organ that grows to fill the entire coelomic cavity (Fig. 6J).

This study provides a detailed description of A. forbesi embryonic and larval development through the juvenile stage using brightfield and live microscopy. These characterizations of developmental stages and anatomy will be helpful for further studies on reproduction, larval behavior, and ecology. By comparing the steps of hydro-vascular organ (HVO) morphogenesis in A. forbesi, P. miniata, and A. aranciacus, we found that while growth strategies differed, all sea star larvae made similar HVOs. An open question is what molecular mechanisms drive such diversity in organ morphogenesis.

Methods

Animal care, gonad extraction and larval culture

Wild-caught adult Asterias forbesi were collected by the Marine Resource Center in Cape Cod Bay (USA) between May and August. Animals were kept in tanks with recirculating fresh sea water at 18 °C at the Marine Biological Laboratory, Woods Hole (MBL). Patiria miniata adults were obtained from Monterey Abalone Company (Monterey, CA, USA), shipped to the MBL and kept in closed recirculating sea water tanks. Adult Astropecten aranciacus were collected from the Gulf of Naples and Gaeta (Italy) between December and May. Animals were kept by the Marine Resources for Research Facility at Stazione Zoologica Anton Dohrn, Napoli (SZN) in 500 L tanks at 16 °C in circulating seawater. The age of the animals cannot be determined, and animals were randomly selected for oocyte and/or sperm isolation.

For all sea star species gametes were collected through a small incision in the ventral region. Oocyte in vitro maturation was achieved by adding the hormone 1-methyladenine (1-MeA). 1 µl of dry sperm was diluted in 1 mL of sea water and a few drops were added to the oocytes. After 5 min the fertilization membrane rose, and embryos were washed several times with sea water. Embryos were reared in 1 L glass beakers at 18 °C (A. forbesi and P. miniata) or 15 °C (A. aranciacus) with a 12 h light/dark cycle. Larval cultures were fed with a mix of Isochrysis galbana and Rhodomonas salina algae 3 times/week.

Microscopy

For brightfield microscopy, samples were imaged as follows. Early embryos (before hatching) were mounted on slides in a small volume of sea water; swimming blastulae, gastrulae and early larvae were transferred to a well with 2x sea water for 30 s and then rinsed with sea water twice before being placed in a glass bottom dish for imaging; late larvae were relaxed by addition of 8% MgCl2 before imaging and then put in a glass bottom dish for imaging.

A. forbesi and P. miniata samples were imaged using a Zeiss Axiovert 5 inverted microscope with a color camera. Later larval stages were imaged on a Zeiss AxioZoom V16-2 stereo microscope available at the Central Microscopy Facility (CMF) at the MBL. Time lapses were made with the Zeiss Axiovert 5 inverted microscope equipped with a temperature-controlled stage set at 18 C. Time stamps on movies indicate hours: minutes. For early embryo movies, samples were imaged on a glass bottom FluoroDish (World Precision instruments) as embryos do not swim until the blastula stage. For movies of larvae, we constrained larval movements by imaging the samples between the FluoroDish glass bottom and a glass coverslip raised on vacuum grease.

A. aranciacus embryos and larvae were imaged on a Zeiss Axio Imager M1 microscope equipped with an Axiocam digital camera at Stazione Zoologica Anton Dohrn (Italy).

Immunostaining

A. forbesi and P. miniata larvae were collected and fixed overnight at 4 °C in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in sea water, washed 3 times with PBS and incubated with anti-acetylated tubulin 1:200 (Sigma-Aldrich T7451) and phalloidin 1:300 (Invitrogen) O/N at 4 °C. Alexa Fluor secondary antibody was added to detect acetylated tubulin (1:1000). DAPI was added at a final concentration of 0.01 mg/mL to stain nuclei. Samples were imaged on a Zeiss LSM 780 available at the Central Microscopy Facility (CMF) at the MBL.

Data analysis and statistics

Images and movies were analyzed using ImageJ (v1.53v) [43]. Cell Tracking of mesenchymal cells from Movie 3 was performed in ImageJ using the manual tracking plugin (Fabrice Cordelières, Institut Curie, Orsay).

Hydro-vascular organ measurements were made with ImageJ and statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism Version 10.2.1 (339). To measure HVO area and shape we took pictures of live larvae that were positioned on their dorsal side using a capillary needle. Area was measured in ImageJ using the Area Selection Tool. Between 25 and 35 larvae were measured for each data point and experiment. Each dot in the graphs represents a measurement from a single larva. The HVO is composed of two tubes that merge to form a looped organ with an elongated shape. A simplified way to measure HVO shape during development is to compare its growth to a perfect sphere using the ImageJ circularity tool. Circularity is measured as: 4

(area/perimeter²). A value of 1.0 indicates a perfect circle, and as the value approaches 0 it indicates an increasingly elongated shape [43].
