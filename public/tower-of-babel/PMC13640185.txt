Thinking-aloud methodology applied to health questionnaire development and validation: a scoping review
Authors: Laura Tasson, Marta Bonato, Farah D’Odorico, Matteo Danielis, Matteo Martinato, Cristina Canova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe development of valid and reliable health questionnaires relies on respondents’ accurate comprehension of items. The thinking-aloud (TA) technique, where participants verbalize their thought processes while answering survey questions, offers a powerful means to explore cognitive mechanisms underlying response behaviour. Despite its wide use, there is no consensus on how TA protocols should be designed, conducted, and analysed.ObjectiveThis scoping review aimed to map methodological approaches applied to TA in health-related questionnaire development and validation, describe their variability, and critically examine current methodological practices to inform future research.DesignPubMed, Scopus, and Embase were searched on July 17, 2025, without time limits. Eligible studies were original articles in English applying TA for questionnaire validation, including assessment of content, face, construct validity, or comprehensibility. Two reviewers independently screened records and extracted data using a standardized charting form. An operational criterion was used to judge the completeness of methodological reporting. Data were synthesized narratively and tabulated to describe study purposes, TA typologies, analytical strategies, and theoretical frameworks.ResultsFrom 1,678 retrieved records, 210 studies met inclusion criteria, of which 84 provided complete methodological descriptions. Most of the 84 studies involved patients (48%) and used concurrent TA (48%), with frequent prompting (55%) and probing (48%). Sessions were mainly face-to-face (71%), audio recorded (64%), and transcribed verbatim. Thematic (38%) and content (26%) analyses predominated, often supported by NVivo. The Survey Response Model was the most cited theoretical framework (26%), though 22% of studies lacked any theoretical reference. Coding strategies were mixed (36%), and about half of the studies reported questionnaire revisions after analysis.ConclusionsTA remains a versatile but methodologically fragmented approach in questionnaire validation. The lack of standardization limits comparability and interpretability across studies. Greater transparency in reporting and explicit theoretical framing are recommended to improve methodological rigor.RegistrationThe review followed the PRISMA-ScR framework and was prospectively registered on OSF (DOI: https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/AZY3).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12874-026-02927-9.Keywords: Thinking-aloud, Cognitive interviewing, Questionnaire validation, Scoping review

Background

Rationale

Developing valid and reliable health questionnaires requires careful attention to how respondents understand and process survey questions. Cognitive interviewing has become a widely used method to identify problems of comprehension, interpretation, and response. This approach allows researchers to explore whether items are interpreted as intended and to detect potential sources of error in questionnaire design [1, 2].

One typology of cognitive interviews is thinking-aloud, a procedure during which the interviewee verbalizes his or her thoughts while reading a questionnaire [2]. When this method is applied, participants are asked to spontaneously report everything that goes through their minds while doing a task, and they are instructed not to interpret or analyse their thinking. The thinking-aloud methodology, as a stand-alone practice, was developed by Ericsson and Simon in 1984 and conceptualized as a means to uncover psychological mechanisms and knowledge structures underlying problem–solving activities (Ericsson and Simon, 1984). In their original framework, verbalizations were expected to remain close to the immediate thought process, without additional explanations or justifications, as these could trigger memory retrieval and distort the cognitive sequence. According to their theory, thinking-aloud could be performed both concurrently and retrospectively, a technique that foresees the interviewee recalls information only after having read the questionnaire [3]. However, the authors questioned the reliability of retrospective reports because of potential memory decay or post task rationalizations [4]. They also suggested that verbalization requests made by the interviewer could alter the way people think [5]. According to their theory, the analysis should be conducted by transcribing and segmenting utterances, then categorizing each segment without relying on the contextual aid of the preceding and following segment [6] .

The strict protocol proposed by the two authors was not fully supported on its methodological premises, as these theoretical assumptions were not broadly accepted within the psychological community and, for this reason, have been subject of debate concerning both interview conduction and data analysis. Critics argued that excluding contextual information from the analysis limited interpretability, and scholars such as Yang [6] advocated for a more comprehensive account of data collection, coding, and theoretical framing. Another challenge observed in practice was the participants often failed to verbalize their thoughts consistently, prompting researchers to adopt strategies such as pre-task orientation or prompting to encourage richer responses [5]. Yet, prompting itself was considered problematic by, some, as vocalizing while performing a task may feel unnatural and threaten validity. Retrospective thinking-aloud (RTA) protocols were proposed as an alternative, allowing participants to complete the task silently and comment performance afterwards [4, 7] Probing techniques, introduced in the 1980s, further enriched thinking-aloud interviews by eliciting additional details clarifying ambiguous utterances. Today, the combination of thinking-aloud and probing has become one of the most common approaches to cognitive interviewing [1].

Questionnaires are widely used in health research to measure perceptions, behaviours, and clinical outcomes, yet their validity depends on respondents correct interpretation of items, helping to detect sources of misinterpretation and improve instrument quality [8]. Even minor ambiguities can threaten measurement accuracy [9]. To address such challenges cognitive interview, which includes thinking-aloud and probing approaches, has been developed as a qualitative method to explore how individuals comprehend, retrieve, and formulate answers to survey questions. The World Health Organization, for instance, includes cognitive interviewing in the pre-testing phase of its Process of Translation and Adaptation of Instruments to ensure conceptual and cultural equivalence across versions [10]. Thinking-aloud techniques have been increasingly applied to assess how respondents interpret and answer questionnaire items, for example, to refine health surveys [11], patient-reported outcome measures [12, 13], and mental health screening tools [14], providing direct insight into comprehension and response processes and guiding instrument improvement.

However, despite decades of use, there is no gold standard for how thinking-aloud protocols should be designed, conducted, or analysed. Studies vary in sampling strategies, use of prompting and probing, analytic frameworks, and reporting practices [15]. This heterogeneity reduces comparability across studies and risks undermining the rigor of the approach [1].

Objectives

The present scoping review aims to synthesize methodological approaches applied to the design and analysis of thinking-aloud protocols when applied to questionnaire development and validation. By examining concurrent, retrospective, and hybrid think-aloud approaches, together with prompting, probing, analytical strategies, and theoretical frameworks, this review seeks to characterize current methodological practices, clarify the state of the field, and identify priorities for future methodological research.

Methods

Information sources

To identify potentially relevant documents, the search was conducted on the 17th of July 2025, without placing any time frame filter, on Pubmed, Scopus and Embase. This search query was formulated for PubMed and then applied with the necessary changes in Scopus and Embase. The final search results were exported into Covidence [1], where duplicates were automatically removed. The review was restricted to peer-reviewed publications because the objective was to examine and compare methodological approaches as formally reported in the scientific literature.

Search

We used the following key terms, properly combined: “Surveys and Questionnaires”, “Psychometrics” “Validation Studies as Topic” “Interviews as Topic”, “valid*”,”verif*”, “questionnaire*”,”survey*”,”assess*”,” interview”*,“think* aloud”. The search strategy was developed by the review team with support from a researcher experienced in literature searching and evidence synthesis. The search string was subsequently reviewed by another member of the research team before implementation. All the search strategies are available in the Supplementary file 1.

Selection of sources of evidence

Two reviewers (LT and FDO) independently screened titles and abstracts; disagreements were solved through discussion. The same two researchers screened the full texts, applying the pre-established exclusion criteria and solving disagreements by discussion.

Judgment of the comprehensiveness of description

Our review aim was to describe key features of how thinking-aloud process has been conducted in studies that stated the aim of validating a questionnaire; consequently, we were principally concerned with the comprehensiveness of the description of methods of applying the thinking-aloud approach, rather than appraising the quality of the study. Following Pollock [17], we defined as “complete” those studies that provided sufficient detail to permit replication of the procedure, following the essential elements delineated in the Cognitive Interview Reporting Framework [18]. One reviewer (LT) assessed all eligible studies, applying this criterion to determine whether the methodological description was complete or incomplete.

Data charting process

A standardized data charting form was developed by two reviewers to guide extraction.

For all included articles, one reviewer (LT) recorded bibliographic information, research area, study aim, instrument tested, instrument development stage, and stated purpose of the thinking-aloud.

For the subset of studies judged “complete,” two reviewers (LT and MB) independently extracted further methodological details (e.g., type of thinking-aloud, use of prompting/probing, session characteristics, analysis method, theoretical framework). Discrepancies were resolved by discussion.

Synthesis of results

To synthesize the evidence included in this scoping review, we adopted a narrative and tabular approach, aiming to address the research objectives of describing methodological practices in the use of thinking-aloud protocols for questionnaire development and validation. The synthesis first organizes studies according to their stated purpose (e.g., developing a new questionnaire, validating content or construct, translating and culturally adapting instruments), then describes key methodological characteristics across studies, including type of thinking-aloud (concurrent, retrospective, hybrid), use of prompting and probing, sample size and composition, session characteristics, transcription and recording methods, analysis approach, coding strategy, and theoretical or sociocultural framework.

Evidence is presented using summary tables that map the methodological choices to the study objectives, facilitating comparison across studies. Additional visual representations, such as heatmaps, illustrate the relationships between study purpose, analysis method, and theoretical framework. Descriptive statistics (e.g., frequency, median session duration, proportion of studies using prompting or probing) complement the narrative synthesis, providing a quantitative overview.

Methodological features of included studies

Patients were the most common sample category (48%), with a median sample size of 21 participants. Recruitment was usually discontinued for thematic saturation (38%) or based on predefined numbers (38%). Eligibility criteria were generally well defined (90%). Concurrent thinking-aloud protocols predominated (48%), and prompting (55%) and probing (48%) techniques were widely applied. Most sessions were face-to-face (71%), audio recorded (64%) and lasted a median of 47.7 min. Thematic (38%) and content (26%) analyses were the most frequently used. The Survey Response Model was the main theoretical framework (26%), though 22% of studies did not specify any one. Mixed coding strategies were slightly more prevalent (36%) than deductive (29%) or inductive (27%). NVivo was the most frequently used software for analysis (28%).

Synthesis of results

When analysing the theoretical framework adopted in the retrieved articles, for translation and cultural adaptation studies the Survey Response Model combined with content or thematic analysis was most frequently used. For questionnaire development, the most common combination was the use of guidelines as a framework paired with thematic analysis. Further validation studies typically applied guidelines and qualitative methodological models alongside thematic analysis. Construct validity studies consistently used psychological or social theories as frameworks, combined with thematic or content analysis. For content validation, survey response models and thematic analysis predominated. Face validation studies most often employed survey response models with either content or thematic analysis.

Several studies revised their questionnaires after analysing interview transcripts. Many modified, deleted, or added items, while others produced culturally adapted versions or identified central themes. Additional outcomes included changes to questionnaire instructions, insights into underreporting, guidance on whether questionnaires should be self- or proxy-completed, and the identification of issues encountered during completion. Discussion

Summary of evidence

In the process of developing a questionnaire, content validation plays a pivotal role, because it reflects the degree on which the instrument reflects the outcome it was intended to measure [8]. Content validation ensures that the questionnaire in comprehensive, relevant and understandable by the target audience, and it is particularly important when an instrument has to capture aspects related to the health aspects of individuals, especially when Patient reported Outcomes or Experiences are involved [104], because PROMS represent the patient’s point of view and give patient a voice during outcome evaluation [9]. A questionnaire has to be content validated also when translated in another language or applied to a population that differs from the one it was developed for.

Content validity can be performed in different, but always qualitative, ways: through Delphi studies, focus groups, cognitive interviews and opinion expert [8]. Thinking aloud is a form of cognitive interviewing, originating in the 1980s from cognitive psychology and increasingly used in the field of health sciences, specifically in PROMs. This is due to its relative ease of use, the minimal equipment required, and the opportunity it provides to gather direct feedback on the questionnaire from the target population [1].

The widespread adoption of thinking aloud has been however accompanied by considerable methodological variability. The lack of a standardized methodological framework has led to a diverse set of practices, which, while reflecting the flexibility of the approach, also raise questions about comparability and the interpretability of results.

The initial 210 studies retrieved span a broad temporal, disciplinary, and geographical spectrum, reflecting the widespread adoption of thinking-aloud methods. Most research was published in recent years across medicine and psychology journals, underscoring growing interdisciplinary interest in applying this approach to questionnaire development and validation.

Among the 84 studies providing complete methodological information, most involved patient samples and employed concurrent thinking-aloud protocols analysed through thematic or content analysis. Recruitment commonly ceased at thematic saturation or a predefined sample size, and sessions were typically face-to-face, audio recorded, and transcribed verbatim. The Survey Response Model emerged as the predominant theoretical framework, while coding approaches were evenly distributed across deductive, inductive, and mixed strategies. NVivo was the most frequently used software. Across studies, the use of thinking-aloud techniques primarily supported theme identification and questionnaire refinement, underscoring their role in enhancing instrument validity and cultural adequacy.

A first area of variability concerns sampling strategies and sample size. Although the median number of participants was around 20, studies differed considerably in their rationale for recruitment. This heterogeneity aligns with literature, given that some studies suggest that a small sample of five or six participants may already produce stable results [105], while seminal studies claim the need for larger numbers [106]. Caulton [107], pointed out that a heterogeneous sample of participants affects the relationship between sample size and stability and exhaustiveness of the problems detected. For this reason, studies employing mixed sample (for example, composed by patients and health care professionals) should aim for a larger sample compared with those recruiting only the target population of interest. In the retrieved studies, approximately half predetermined the sample size, while the others stopped recruitment upon reaching saturation, reflecting an ongoing debate in qualitative research: whether to pursue complete, time-consuming, and potentially endless thematic saturation, or to adopt a more pragmatic, but likely less exhaustive, pre-defined sample size [108].

Regarding thinking-aloud procedures, concurrent and hybrid protocols predominated, while retrospective approaches were less frequently employed. The choice between the concurrent and retrospective methods depends largely on the author’s main objective: minimizing thought alteration and the potential reactivity associated with the concurrent approach or obtaining a richer but more rationalized account through retrospective reporting [109] The frequent use of prompting and probing, as well as the use of the hybrid approach, suggests an outcome-oriented stance. Many researchers often found that participants struggled to verbalize spontaneously, reporting earlier concerns about reactivity and the artificiality of the method [2]. Probing helps retrieving information not spontaneously provided during the interview. Yet, the inclusion of these techniques introduces potential interviewer effects, raising concerns about validity. The use of prompting and probing, even if with the risk of introducing researcher’s bias, seem to be a reliable method to obtain information even from those subjects that are not able, or are not inclined, to verbalise their thoughts while reading. The use of these techniques is suggested tough to maximize results from interviews.

The analysis of transcripts was mainly performed through thematic and content analysis, often supported by qualitative software, but with a wide variation on the application of inductive, deductive, and mixed coding approaches. This suggests that researchers tend to tailor their analytic strategies to the purpose of their study, or to the technique with which they are most familiar, rather than adhering to a shared methodological standard. Importantly, the Survey Response Model emerged as the most cited theoretical framework, but a considerable number of studies failed to specify any explicit framework, failing on one of the methodological step considered essential by ISPOR guidelines when developing a measurement instrument [104]. Such omission limits transparency and weakens the interpretative grounding of the findings, reinforcing Yang’s (2003) call for explicit reporting of theoretical underpinnings. This is even more true if we consider that for Eriksonn and Simon [110] data obtained from thinking-aloud should have been used to validate a pre-existent theoretical model and cannot be interpreted alone [111]. In qualitative research, adopting a theoretical framework helps provide the conceptual hierarchy when developing the questionnaire [112], and then to give a sense of coherence and a narrative sense to the themes emerging from the data, allowing insights to extend beyond participants’ literal words [113]. It is plausible that when the thinking-aloud method is transferred from psychology to medicine, it loses much of its theoretical foundation and retains primarily its pragmatic dimension, relying mainly on the literal interpretation of transcripts [114].

In terms of outputs, thinking-aloud protocols consistently informed questionnaire revisions, ranging from item modification to cultural adaptations. On this topic, a substantial methodological issue concerns the numbers of rounds of interview performed to validate the questionnaire. As suggested also in ISPOR [104] and COSMIN guidelines [8], when a questionnaire is revised following a round of cognitive interviews, it should ideally be re-tested with a different sample in order to confirm the validity of modifications. At the same time, it is important to recognize that addressing every concern or suggestion raised during interviews may not always be appropriate or necessary.

A still unexplored but promising area is the use of artificial intelligence in the segmentation and coding of interviews transcripts for questionnaire validation. Borse’s work demonstrated a satisfactory inter-rater reliability between researcher and GPT 4.5 analysis of an audio transcript, suggesting the possibility of overcoming the time-consuming limitation of analysing a large body of transcripts. As in other fields, achieving reliable results is strongly dependent on appropriate prompt design [115].

Taken together, these findings indicate that thinking-aloud method remains a powerful yet methodologically fragmented tool. Nevertheless, the diversity of approaches observed— in data collection, analysis, or theoretical framing—suggests that results across studies may not be directly comparable. Future research should move toward the confrontation of different approaches on the validation of the same instrument, as done in Alshammari’s work, where a confrontation between concurrent and retrospective thinking-aloud approach in a usability test was performed [7]. Only through such efforts the superiority, or the advantages and the disadvantages of a methodology, can be stated, and clear indications produced, other than simple suggestions based on a pragmatic approach, the same that are used for the formulation of the guidelines cited along the article.

Limitations

Although PubMed, Embase, and Scopus provide broad coverage of the biomedical, health sciences, and interdisciplinary literature, studies indexed exclusively in other databases may have been missed. Furthermore, although all screening and study selection procedures were conducted independently by two reviewers, the assessment of methodological completeness was performed by a single reviewer and may therefore be subject to subjective interpretation. As a result, some studies may have been classified differently, potentially influencing the characterization of methodological practices reported in this review.

Conclusions

This scoping review shows that thinking-aloud methodologies are widely used in health questionnaire development and validation, but are characterized by substantial heterogeneity in their design, conduct, analysis, and reporting. While this flexibility may facilitate adaptation to different research contexts, it also limits the comparability and reproducibility of findings across studies.

Greater transparency in reporting methodological choices, theoretical frameworks, analytical procedures, and questionnaire revisions would strengthen the rigor of future studies. Further methodological research should directly compare alternative thinking-aloud approaches, including concurrent, retrospective, and hybrid protocols, to better understand their respective strengths and limitations and support more consistent practices in questionnaire validation.
