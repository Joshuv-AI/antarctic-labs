The feasibility of a novel exercise therapy and activity modification intervention for patients with Sever’s disease
Authors: Shawn L. Hanlon, Kayla D. Seymore, Morgan N. Potter, Bradley C. Bley, Karin Grävare Silbernagel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSever’s disease (calcaneal apophysitis) is a common cause of heel pain in physically active youth. Evidence-based treatment options are limited, with no consensus for the standard of care. This study aimed to assess the feasibility of delivering an intervention comprising exercise therapy and pain-guided activity modification for youth patients with Sever’s disease and to describe patient symptoms, functional limitations, psychosocial factors, and ultrasound imaging findings to inform future clinical trials.MethodsThis single-cohort study enrolled patients between 7 and 17 years old with Sever’s disease. Participants received a comprehensive 12-week intervention combining daily Achilles-tendon loading exercises, activity modification using a pain-monitoring model, and patient education. Training diaries were provided to monitor adherence, daily activities, and pain. Feasibility was assessed via recruitment and retention rates, daily activity modification adherence, rehabilitation adherence, and safety. Secondary outcomes (patient-reported outcome measures, functional test battery, and ultrasound imaging) were collected at baseline and at 4-, 8-, and 12 weeks and were reported descriptively to characterize the biopsychosocial burden of the condition and inform selection of outcomes and hypotheses for future clinical trials.ResultsOf the 13 participants enrolled (7F/6 M, (mean ± SD) age 11.5 ± 2.5 years, BMI 21.6 ± 4.6 kg/m2), recruitment and retention rate was 65% and 69%, respectively. Activity modification adherence was 91 ± 28% (range 37–100%), and rehabilitation adherence was 91 ± 25% (range 25–100%). Three adverse events occurred and each was unrelated to participation. All participants expressed satisfaction with the intervention (75% [10/10] very satisfied).ConclusionsOur findings support that this exercise therapy and activity modification intervention is feasible, safe, and appropriate for the treatment of Sever’s disease. Intervention satisfaction ratings and favorable global rating of change scores reported imply that educating patients to use a pain-monitoring model, allowing for modified physical activity, is not detrimental to patients with Sever’s disease. A larger randomized controlled trial is warranted to evaluate the effectiveness of this intervention.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT04816188. Introduction

Children and adolescents with insidious posterior heel pain are commonly diagnosed with Sever’s disease (calcaneal apophysitis) [1, 2]. Sever’s disease occurs during childhood through adolescent years while the calcaneus undergoes bony maturation and is most prevalent in boys aged 8–15 and girls 7–13, affecting 3.7 per 1000 adolescents [1, 3]. The condition is caused by repetitive stress to the cartilaginous growth plate at the Achilles tendon insertion [4]. Clinical diagnosis relies on a history of a gradual onset of heel pain with activity and the patient’s age (< 18 years old), implying skeletal immaturity and incurring rapid bone growth at the apophyseal plates [5, 6].

There is no established standard of care for Sever’s disease. Treatment often includes a range of passive intervention strategies [2, 7, 8]. Passive treatment strategies include avoidance or cessation of symptom-provoking activities, immobilization, and wearing cushioning heel cups [9–11]. Symptom management is a primary focus for clinicians and patients, given the widely accepted assumption that symptoms and dysfunction may resolve spontaneously after skeletal maturation [8]. Limited evidence exists evaluating exercise-based interventions for Sever’s disease. Only one randomized clinical trial has been conducted comparing conservative treatment approaches, reporting similar outcomes for pain between heel inserts, physical therapy, and activity discontinuation (i.e. no treatment), although the physical therapy intervention was not described in detail [9]. As knowledge of the condition continues to expand beyond the limitations set by a biomedical model [12], treatment strategies are evolving for other apophysitis conditions. A recent prospective study by Rathleff et al. demonstrated the benefits of an exercise-based intervention on knee function for apophysitis at the patellofemoral joint (Osgood Schlatter’s disease) [13]. The current palliative strategies for Sever’s disease are unlikely to improve functional deficits or impaired quality of life cited in recent studies [8, 14], as tissue adaptations at the apophysis and Achilles tendon require physical stress to promote growth and remodeling [15–17].

Adolescence is a pivotal time for establishing lifelong connective tissue health. The load-absorbing collagen matrix of the Achilles tendon, menisci, and articular cartilage is developed during adolescence (ages 9–13), with slower collagen generation rates in tendon after age 17 [18–20]. Tendon structure responds and adapts to mechanical loading, and thus, can be influenced by changes in physical activity [21, 22]. Activity cessation due to injury risks underloading tendon tissue and subsequent collagen degradation [21]. While unmodified activity (i.e., exercising through pain) risks additional overuse injury, such as Osgood Schlatter’s Disease, and might nullify the effects of any therapeutic intervention [5, 10, 23, 24]. A recent case-control study found Achilles tendon structure is altered in Sever’s disease cases compared to healthy peers, which challenges the longstanding assumption that apophysitis is a benign and transient musculoskeletal condition [15]. Indeed, there is an evident need for novel interventions to address symptoms and function and promote healthy tissue remodeling in patients with Sever’s disease.

An effective comprehensive intervention exists for adult patients with Achilles tendinopathy that allows continued physical activity during rehabilitation using a pain-monitoring model [25]. Adult patients with Achilles tendinopathy report similar symptoms and activity limitations as pediatric patients, and with advancing skeletal maturation, tendinopathy may be clinically indistinguishable from apophysitis cases [15, 26]. Compared to known the health-related impact of tendinopathy [27], apophysitis is substantially understudied. Exercise therapy combined with activity modification is non-detrimental to recovery in adults [25, 28] and the efficacy of this intervention for Achilles tendinopathy is well established, yet an exercise-based approach has not been evaluated for children and adolescents with Sever’s disease [29–31] Therefore, the primary aim of this feasibility study was to examine recruitment and retention rates, daily activity modification adherence, rehabilitation adherence, and safety of a 12-week exercise therapy and activity modification intervention for youth patients diagnosed with Sever’s disease. Additionally, we aimed to report secondary outcomes descriptively to improve understanding of the clinical presentation and patient limitations and to allow for effect size calculations for sample size estimation in future clinical trials.

Methods

Study design and patients

This was a single-cohort pilot and feasibility study of youth ages 7–17 years old with clinically diagnosed Sever’s disease. Clinical diagnosis was confirmed by a history of insidious heel pain that worsens with running or jumping activities and pain location focal to the posterior calcaneus and/or Achilles tendon. Criteria for enrollment are provided in Table 1. Written parental permission and child assent were required for all participants before enrollment. One parent of each participant was invited to consent to participate in the study by completing surveys. We recruited potential participants through collaborators at local orthopedic/podiatry offices, the University of Delaware Physical Therapy Clinic, as well as through local pediatricians, athletic trainers, and nurses who work in middle schools and high schools using flyers and social media advertisements. An online screening questionnaire was publicly accessible for any interested individuals to request to be contacted. The research team contacted potential participants to schedule an in-person screening and obtain informed consent. Access to potential participants and results of eligibility screening, recruitment sources, and reasoning for ineligibility or declined participation were recorded. The study protocol was published during the recruitment phase [32]. This study was approved by the University of Delaware Institutional Review Board (ID: 1652996-3) and is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (ID: NCT04816188). Parents who consented to participate were asked to complete surveys at both in-person and telehealth visits. All supervised treatment and evaluation sessions were completed at the (blinded for review). Evaluator blinding was not possible because the same clinician obtained informed consent and assent, conducted enrollment, and attended data collection and intervention administration. All data were recorded and stored using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) (Vanderbilt University, Nashville, TN) [33].

Feasibility, fidelity, and safety

We assessed feasibility by evaluating recruitment and retention rates, intervention fidelity, and safety. The recruitment rate was calculated by the number of participants who enrolled in the study divided by the number of people who were screened for eligibility. The retention rate was calculated by the number of participants who completed the study from baseline for 12 weeks divided by the number of enrolled participants. Participants were asked to complete a daily training diary to record their heel pain in the morning and the lowest and highest pain experienced each day, their prescribed exercises, all other physical activity, and specify whether they participated in any running or jumping activities. Pain was reported using a numerical pain rating scale (NPRS; 0–10), where 0 is “no pain” and 10 is the “worst pain imaginable.” The training diary has been used in previous studies for patients with Achilles and patellar tendinopathy [25, 28, 34]. Intervention fidelity was evaluated by activity modification adherence and rehabilitation adherence as reported in participant training diaries. At each study time point, a clinician research assistant reviewed training diaries and counted the number of days/week the pain-monitoring model and recommendations set by the research clinician administering the intervention were followed, and the number of days/week participants completed their prescribed exercises. Intervention fidelity was considered “achieved” for participants who had ≥ 80% adherence with both activity modification and therapeutic exercise prescription, as defined by pharmacological adherence [35]. Activity modification adherence was considered “achieved” on days when pain was documented ≤ 5/10 on the NPRS and physical activity volume, type, or intensity was modified consistent with clinician recommendations or “not achieved” on days when pain exceeded 5/10, activity was not modified appropriately, or documentation was incomplete or missing. Activity modification adherence was reported as the number of days/week adherence was achieved, divided by the total number of days enrolled in the intervention. Rehabilitation adherence was defined as completing the prescribed exercises for each protocol phase ≥ 2 days/week and reported as the number of adherent weeks divided by total weeks in intervention. Participants who missed a visit or were lost to follow-up were recorded. Lastly, we assessed safety by reporting the number of adverse events reported from baseline to 12 weeks. An adverse event was defined as any unfavorable or unintended diagnosis, sign, symptom, or disease during study enrollment, and whether or not the event was related to the intervention was recorded.

Clinical evaluation

At baseline, participants completed a past medical history questionnaire related to their current symptoms, previous lower extremity injuries, and sports participation history. At each in-person visit, the study team performed a bilateral clinical evaluation (anthropometrics, palpation, dorsiflexion range of motion, single-leg hopping), an ultrasound exam, and a functional test battery. Pain with palpation was recorded for the Achilles tendon midportion and insertion using the NPRS (0–10). Ankle dorsiflexion was measured via the weight-bearing lunge test, positioned in full knee extension and knee flexion, with an electronic protractor placed on the tibia [36]. Pain with hopping was assessed by asking participants to complete a bout of 20 single-leg hops (at a similar cadence to jumping rope) and to immediately rate their heel pain [37, 38].

Patient reported and parent-proxy Soutcomes

Symptom severity and pain with activity were selected as the primary clinical outcome and measured by the Victorian Institute of Sport Assessment-Achilles (VISA-A), the Foot and Ankle Outcome Score (FAOS)-Pain and FAOS-Symptoms sub-scores. The VISA-A is the most widely used outcome measure specific to Achilles tendon pain and assesses pain and symptoms with activity/sports [37]. The VISA-A is scored 0–100, where 90 points or higher is achieved by healthy individuals [39, 40] and lower scores indicate worse pain and symptoms with activities. The VISA-A was completed in reference to each limb, and the lower score at baseline was indicated as the “most symptomatic” for the study duration. All FAOS sub-scores have been validated for a variety of foot and ankle conditions and are scored 0–100, where 100 indicates the best possible status related to the foot or ankle condition. [38, 41]. The FAOS Sports and Recreation (FAOS-Sports) was used to subjectively assess the degree of difficulty with higher-level functional activities [38] and the FAOS Quality of life (FAOS-QoL) was used to measure foot and ankle-related quality of life [38].

The Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) Pediatric Pain Interference Scale measured the extent to which pain interferes with functioning [42] All PROMIS sub-scores use a t-score metric in which 50 points is the mean of the relevant reference population and 10 points is the standard deviation of that population. Reporting a higher score represents more of the concept being measured (e.g., greater pain interference) [42]. Parents were asked to complete the PROMIS Parent Proxy Pain Intensity and Pain Interference questionnaires [42]. The PROMIS Pediatric Global Health was used to assess self-reported impact of injury on mental and physical health [43] Parents were asked to complete the PROMIS Parent Proxy Global Health [42]. The PROMIS Pediatric Physical Activity Short Form was used to assess self-reported capability of physical activities over a 7-day period [42]. Parents were asked to complete the PROMIS Parent Proxy Physical Activity form.

The Fear of Pain Questionnaire-Child (FOPQ-C) was used to evaluate fear of pain and avoidance of activities due to pain. The FOPQ-C is validated for children with chronic pain with good reliability (test–retest: r = 0.74) and stability [44] Scores range from 0 to 96, with a higher score indicating greater fear of pain and activity avoidance. Parents were asked to complete the FOPQ-Parent. Self-perceived improvement was assessed using an 11-point Likert Global Rating of Change (GROC) scale, ranging from − 5 (very much worse) to + 5 (completely recovered). The GROC has been used in previous trials with adolescents [45]. Lastly, self-reported treatment satisfaction was measured on a scale of 0–10, where 0 indicated “not satisfied” and 10 indicated “very satisfied.” All patient-reported outcome measures were administered using REDCap prior to each intervention or telehealth session.

Lower extremity functional test battery

A functional test battery was administered using a MuscleLab® (Ergotest) system, including the single-leg countermovement jump (CMJ), drop CMJ, and heel-rise endurance test [46]. These tests were evaluated for feasibility purposes related to appropriateness including participant physical and cognitive capacity (e.g., ability to perform the tests as instructed) and disability (e.g., inability to complete the test procedures due to pain provocation). For both jump tests, participants were instructed to keep their arms behind their back and jump as high as possible. The CMJ was performed on flat ground. The drop CMJ was performed by standing on the edge of a 20 cm box, then stepping off to land and immediately rebounding as high as possible. Three trials were attempted on each leg. Maximal jump height was calculated using a contact grid and flight time [47]. The heel-rise endurance test was performed on a 10° decline slant board. A linear encoder was taped to the participant’s heel to measure height (displacement in cm) for each heel-rise repetition. Participants were instructed to perform as many single-leg heel-rises as possible, lifting the heel as high as possible, to a metronome set at 60 beats/min (30 heel-rises/min). The test concluded when the participant was unable to continue lifting their heel or perform consistent repetitions without compensating (hip or knee bending). Heel-rise work (bodyweight in Newtons x heel-rise height per repetition) was calculated in Joules. All functional tests were performed on the right leg first to pseudo-randomize limb preference.

Ultrasound imaging

Diagnostic ultrasound images were obtained by trained examiners with at least 2 years of musculoskeletal ultrasound imaging experience using a GE LogiqE and a linear transducer (L5-14). Osirix MD imaging software (Pixmeo, Geneva, Switzerland) was used to record morphology measurements and abnormal findings. Ultrasound imaging procedures were included to inform future diagnostic criteria and estimate detectable changes in the future trial. Achilles tendon morphology measurements included maximal cross-sectional area (CSA), soleus thickness, and degree of tendon thickening (calculated as thickest portion—uniform thickness) using reliable procedures [48, 49]. To ensure reliable serial measures, we used ultrasound to locate and mark the skin at the Achilles tendon insertion as a landmark and used tibia length as a reference distance [50]. The length of the tibia was measured from the most distal portion of the medial malleoli to the medial tibial plateau using skin markers. Achilles tendon CSA was measured at 20% length proximal to the Achilles tendon insertion. Soleus thickness was measured at 30% tibial length proximal to the medial malleolus. Additionally, we scanned both limbs and recorded the presence or absence of tendinosis, bursitis, neovascularization (intratendinous or apophyseal plate), calcifications, and bony fragmentation [51]. The status of the calcaneal secondary ossification site (e.g., calcaneal growth plate) was examined and recorded as “open” or “closed” (Fig. 2). All findings were reviewed by a trained physician holding the Registered in Musculoskeletal sonography (RMSK) certification. Continuous Shear Wave Elastography (cSWE) was used to calculate Achilles tendon mechanical properties of shear modulus and viscosity [52, 53]. cSWE is a painless non-invasive method to validly and reliably assess tendon stiffness and distinguish injured from healthy tendon in adults [53]. The region of interest was marked at 20% length proximal to the calcaneal insertion of the Achilles tendon.

Exercise therapy and activity modification intervention

Exercise therapy

The exercise therapy intervention consisted of progressive Achilles-tendon loading exercises following a standardized treatment protocol and pain-guided activity modifications using the pain-monitoring model and patient education [25, 54]. Exercises included bilateral and single-leg heel rises, eccentric exercise, seated heel-rise, and rebounding exercise. A printable exercise protocol and pain-monitoring model can be found in Supplementary file 1. Phase 1 focused on symptom management via load reduction and completing the prescribed exercises daily with slow, controlled movement with resistance up to full bodyweight. In phases 2–3, participants progressed to weighted exercises 2–3 times per week and bodyweight exercises on all other days. Exercise progression consisted of increasing the number of repetitions, resistance, speed, and progression from standing on flat ground to standing on a step or incline (if tolerable) when performing the exercises. Participants were encouraged to perform the exercises if they experienced tolerable pain (≤ 5/10) but were instructed to stop if pain became higher than 5/10 on the NPRS. External loading (using a backpack, weighted vest, or weight machine) was incorporated when a participant could complete all phase 1 exercises with minimal pain (< 2/10).

The pain-monitoring model [25] promotes adjusting activities based on pain on the NPRS by adhering to the following guidelines: pain is not allowed to exceed a tolerable level (≥ 5/10) during activity or following activity. Pain should not exceed 5/10 the following day and should not increase week to week. Education for the participant and parent was standardized to address the following topics: (1) understanding of heel-pain injury, (2) how to use the pain-monitoring model, (3) rationale for treatment, (4) how to use the training diary, (5) proper exercise instruction, and (6) field questions from the patient or parent. Participants were provided with an educational handout (Supplementary file 2) at baseline as an aid to educate them on these topics and appropriate progression to return to full activity participation.

Activity modification

At the first intervention session, it was critical to identify sources of pain-provoking activities for each participant and to form a treatment alliance between the treating clinician, the participant, and parent in order to develop activity modifications that adhere to the pain-monitoring model guidelines. As tolerance to loading increased, participants progressively increased their Achilles-tendon loading activities without exceeding pain ≥ 5/10 and pain the following morning not exceeding the previous day [55]. During all phases, participants were instructed to incorporate up to 3 days of reduced activity intensity and Achilles-tendon loading following a day of heavy loading to promote adequate time for tissue remodeling [21, 56]. The activities (determined as “light”, “moderate”, and “heavy”) were relative to each participant based on rating of perceived exertion and pain response [25]. Part of the educational handout included a modified activity ladder adapted from Rathleff et al. [45] to provide stepwise criteria for progression throughout the 12-week intervention.

Statistical analysis

We calculated the mean and standard deviation for continuous variables and frequencies for categorical variables to report baseline patient characteristics. The recruitment and retention rates, activity modification adherence, therapeutic exercise adherence, and number of adverse events was used to assess feasibility using a cutoff of ≥ 80%. Adherence with activity modification and therapeutic exercise (“achieved”/“not achieved”) and safety (“adverse event”/”no event”) were described in terms of frequencies. No formal hypothesis testing was conducted, therefore quantitative data are summarized using descriptive statistics. Patient-reported and parent-proxy outcomes, functional test performance data, and Achilles tendon morphology and mechanical properties measures were reported descriptively at each time point and as mean difference and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) between baseline and 12 weeks. This will allow for effect size calculations for sample size calculations for future clinical trials. We recorded additional ultrasound imaging findings at baseline (Achilles tendinosis, bony fragmentations, open secondary ossification site, bursitis, neovascularization, calcifications) as frequencies among all enrolled participant’s limbs (13 participants; 26 limbs).

Feasibility, adherence, and safety

The recruitment rate was 65%, with two individuals unable to be scheduled, two declined to participate after phone screening, and three deemed ineligible to participate. Retention rate was 69%, with three participants lost to follow-up between weeks 2 and 6. One participant was lost to follow-up between their baseline evaluation and their first telehealth visit. One participant was lost to follow-up after their first telehealth visit and did not attend their 4-week evaluation; however, they did complete 4-week questionnaires online prior to cancelling their evaluation and did not reschedule. One participant was lost to follow-up after their 4-week evaluation, stating that study participation was too difficult to attend in-person appointments while navigating challenges related to the COVID-19 pandemic causing school closures. Activity modification adherence was 91 ± 28% (range 37–100%), equating to 6 ± 1 adherent days/week. Therapeutic exercise adherence was 91 ± 25% (range 25–100%). Three adverse events occurred. One participant sustained a lateral ankle sprain while participating in a baseball game and was prescribed complete rest by their primary care physician for 20 days and no additional treatment. This occurred between the 8-week evaluation and final virtual visit. This participant was able to complete the study with no missed visits. Another participant incurred a contusion on their foot (less symptomatic side) during a soccer game 1 week after enrollment in the study and was unable to fully bear weight for several days. Following pain resolution, they did continue with their exercises and no study timepoints were missed. Finally, one participant withdrew from the study after their 4-week visit due to reported exercise-induced lower leg pain that was not improving. This participant was recommended by their physician to withhold from completing the exercise protocol.

All clinical outcomes are summarized descriptively in Table 3. Discussion

We found our exercise therapy and pain-guided activity modification intervention to be feasible and safe for patients diagnosed with Sever’s disease. Adherence to both the activity modification prescription and treatment protocol was excellent; however, the recruitment and retention rates were below the pre-determined acceptable criteria. Intervention satisfaction ratings and a favorable global rating of change reported imply that educating patients to use a pain-monitoring model, allowing them to maintain modified physical activity, is not detrimental to patients with Sever’s disease. Favorable changes in symptoms, functional performance, and psychosocial factors were reported by all participants during the course of the study; however, the study design does not allow these changes to be attributed causally to the intervention.

Implications for future study implementation

The overall data collection included surveys, clinical exam, ultrasound imaging, and functional testing, followed immediately by the intervention session. This required participants to spend 2–3 h at each visit, which may have influenced our retention rate. This time commitment will be reduced in the future clinical trial by collecting less measures and offering separate visits for intervention. Additional potential barriers to recruitment and retention were identified, including transportation challenges, conflicting participant time commitments, and limited study personnel availability. Although there were several benefits to conducting an intensive and multimodal data collection. To our knowledge, this is the first study to report functional limitations in this patient population quantitatively. Participants were capable of performing all functional tests as instructed and with minimal-to-acceptable pain (< 5/10). Validity concerns were identified for the drop CMJ in participants under 10 years old. This task appeared difficult for some participants and therefore may not be useful for interpretation. We suggest a practice trial to ensure the patient possesses the requisite motor skill or muscle strength to perform the drop CMJ test correctly using our methods. The 20 cm height of the box may not be too high for some youth patients, particularly those with short leg length relative to the box. The ultrasound imaging data collection procedure was included for exploratory purposes, as there are limited studies describing the sonographic appearance of the developing calcaneus [15, 57]. This proved valuable, as unexpected similarities to healthy adult Achilles tendons were observed in mechanical properties [58] and the degree of Achilles tendon thickening in this cohort [59]. A future longitudinal case-control study is needed to improve our understanding of this finding and the relationship between Sever’s disease and healthy Achilles tendon structure development in adolescence. The lack of change in tendon morphology and mechanical properties over time was anticipated, as these measures are reported to require several months to observe change in adults with Achilles tendinopathy [60]. The majority of Achilles tendon structure is formed between ages 9 and 13 [16, 61, 62]. After age 17, there is a decline in the rate of cell renewal in healthy Achilles tendons [18]. We hypothesize that a 6–12 month follow-up would be required in future studies to potentially observe changes in Achilles tendon mechanical properties.

This intervention was designed to address the known biopsychosocial impairments associated with youth overuse injuries and our data collection methods aimed to elucidate the biopsychosocial burden of the condition. Our comprehensive clinical examination and selected outcome measures revealed novel information to inform potential target outcomes for future clinical trials. This cohort with Sever’s disease experienced debilitating symptoms that interfered with sports participation and the ability to be physically active. The majority of participants (69%) competed in sports year-round. Bilateral symptoms were reported in 54% of cases and nearly 40% reported experiencing symptoms for over 1 year. Consistent with previous Sever’s disease studies, quality of life related to the foot and ankle was impacted substantially [14, 63]. The number of outcome measures selected was chosen for exploratory purposes, as this patient population is understudied. It was of interest to determine whether the proposed outcome measures could be administered without fatiguing participants and appraise potential deficits across the health-related domains for tendinopathy [27] without redundancy. Additionally, these were included for sample size calculations in future research. No disease-specific outcome measure exists for Sever’s disease; however, our findings suggest the VISA-A questionnaire is an appropriate primary outcome for a future trial, and FAOS sub-scores may provide additional context as secondary outcomes. The PROMIS Pediatric and parent-proxy surveys will be removed in a future trial to prevent survey fatigue, as we have identified that they are redundant with other outcome measures. Jump tests (CMJ and drop CMJ) appeared to be uninformative compared to the heel-rise endurance test for measuring functional change. These tests may provide value during the baseline evaluation for assessing interlimb deficits. Because each jump test places different muscle forces on the tendon, we determined these tests will be beneficial to include in a future trial.

Strengths and limitations

A key methodological strength was adherence to our previously published study protocol [32]. However, several limitations must be addressed in the future study including, but not limited to, small sample size, lack of blinding, threats to generalizability, and risk of bias. The small sample size with a moderate retention rate could overestimate our feasibility results. Because this study was designed to assess feasibility and includes no comparison group, no observed changes in secondary outcomes should be attributed to the intervention. Investigator blinding was not possible due to limitations in study personnel size and logistics. The primary investigator will be blinded to intervention and outcomes assessment in the future trial. Our recruitment rate and participant retention did not meet our pre-determined feasibility cut-off criteria, however these findings are difficult to generalize because enrollment occurred during the COVID-19 pandemic. Recruitment began in August 2020, at which time our region of the USA within reasonable driving distance of our research facility declared a state of emergency and issued stay-at-home orders due to the COVID-29 pandemic. Recruitment spiked between March-June 2021, consistent with the timing of the first available COVID-19 vaccine for children aged 12 and older (May 2021). Retention was problematic, with four participants (25%) who could not be contacted after the first in-person follow-up visit. An intention-to-treat analysis will be included in the future study. From an implementation standpoint, the hybrid format offering telehealth visits and a home exercise program requiring minimal equipment was a strength. However, access to weight training equipment limited exercise progression for some participants. Access to equipment will be essential for success in the future clinical trial. The latter stages of the treatment protocol required external loads ranging from 16 to 45 kg. For instance, the youngest participant in the study (8-years-old) used 55 lb (25 kg) for seated calf raises by week 4. Our measurement method limitations affected interpretation. Exercise therapy adherence was self-reported using paper training diaries and could not be verified by the study personnel. We were unable to access training diaries beyond their most recent visit for those lost to follow-up. We also could not objectively measure physical activity and relied on participants’ documentation, which may have overestimated adherence. A previous systematic review by Phillips et al. [64] suggests that self-reported adherence is influenced by participants’ desire to be “good patients”, leading to false-positive reporting. To address this risk of bias, the future study will utilize video communication technology. The future trial will utilize wearable activity monitors to objectively capture daily physical activity, and a phone-based application for tracking therapeutic exercise adherence and rating of perceived exertion (RPE). These additions will also support exploring the dose–response relationship between physical activity and optimal Achilles tendon development in patients with Sever’s disease and may improve compliance by allowing the researchers to set task reminders. Finally, we did not evaluate the acceptability of the patient education materials. The future intervention will benefit from collaboration with experts in pediatric research to enhance the patient education components and ensure the interpretation of pain is age-appropriate for the adolescent population.

Future directions

To determine the effectiveness of our intervention in the future clinical trial using the VISA-A as the primary outcome, a minimum of 48 participants was determined to provide adequate statistical power to detect a minimally clinically important difference in VISA-A scores based on our observed mean change (20 points), with a medium effect size (partial ƞ2 = 0.06), alpha set to 0.05, and 80% power. Based on our annual recruitment rate, it would take approximately 2 years to recruit 48 participants using the same recruitment strategies. It would be advantageous to expand recruitment efforts by increasing outreach to community sports events and collaboration with regional children’s hospitals to capture a larger and more diverse demographic of potential participants. Finally, evaluating potential covariates, such as the stage of skeletal maturity, sex, and BMI, should be considered in future clinical trials involving a youth patient population [65, 66].

Conclusion

Our exercise therapy and activity modification intervention is feasible, safe, and provides a foundation for evaluating a promising novel intervention in a future clinical trial for the treatment of Sever’s disease. Our findings elucidate the biopsychosocial burden experienced by patients diagnosed with Sever’s disease, including debilitating symptoms that interfere with physical activity and sports participation, reduced quality of life, and elevated psychosocial factors; signifying the need for a comprehensive, multimodal assessment in clinical practice and future studies. A future randomized-controlled trial with a larger sample is warranted to determine the effectiveness of this intervention and to detect whether our reported outcomes are statistically and clinically meaningful.
