The association between preoperative telomere length and acute postoperative depression: a single-center prospective observational pilot study
Authors: Daiki Takekawa, Hirotaka Kinoshita, Atsuya Ishii, Yoshikazu Nikaido, Tetsuya Kushikata, Kazuyoshi Hirota, Junichi Saito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWe hypothesized that a shorter preoperative leukocyte telomere length (TL) predicts the development of acute postoperative depression in elderly surgical patients.MethodsThis single-center, prospective, observational study included 48 patients ≥ 65 years old requiring intensive care unit admission for ≥ 2 days after surgery. Acute postoperative depression was defined as a Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D) score ≥ 16 on postoperative day 7. Multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to determine whether the preoperative TL could be used to predict the development of acute postoperative depression.Results48 patients who underwent esophagectomy, head and neck surgery, or off-pump coronary bypass grafting were included. 20 patients (41.7%) developed acute postoperative depression. The preoperative TL was not significantly different between the depression group and the non-depression group (n = 28) (303,902 ± 48,103 vs. 331,816 ± 48,984 RLU/µg DNA, p = 0.056). The multivariable logistic regression analysis showed that the surgical procedure type was the dominant predictor of acute postoperative depression, whereas preoperative TL was not independently associated (OR 1.585, 95%CI: 0.722–3.479, p = 0.251). In a post-hoc subgroup analysis excluding head & neck surgery patients, the preoperative TL was significantly shorter in the depression group (302,990 ± 38,881 vs. 336,426 ± 49,755 RLU/µg DNA, p = 0.042) and was independently associated with acute postoperative depression after adjustment for the surgical procedure type (OR 3.556, 95%CI: 1.121–11.282, p = 0.031).ConclusionsThere was no significant association between the preoperative TL and acute postoperative depression. This result may be due to the small sample size and a cohort comprising multiple surgical procedure types. The exploratory post-hoc subgroup analysis excluding the patients who underwent head & neck surgery showed that shorter preoperative TL was significantly associated with an increased risk of acute postoperative depression. However, this preliminary finding is hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory and should be interpreted with caution.Trial registrationjRCT1020220041.

Background

The global population is aging rapidly, with adults aged ≥ 65 years representing the fastest-growing demographic worldwide [1, 2]. This shift has led to a substantial increase in elderly patients undergoing major surgery. Compared to younger individuals, these older patients are at higher risk for adverse outcomes after surgery, including postoperative cognitive dysfunction (POCD), postoperative delirium (POD), and depression [3, 4]. Although POCD and POD have been extensively studied and are widely recognized as major complications, postoperative depression has received far less attention despite its clinical significance. Postoperative depression occurs in 7%–19% of patients, depending on the type of surgery [5]. This form of depression not only diminishes patients’ quality of life; it is also linked to longer hospital stays, higher readmission rates, slower functional recovery, and an increased risk of medical complications [3, 6]. Despite these impacts, new-onset postoperative depression remains poorly characterized. Most of the research has examined how preoperative depression affects postoperative outcomes, with only limited data about potential predictors of postoperative depression itself [5]. Objective biomarkers to identify older patients who are at high risk of experiencing postoperative depression have not been established, thus limiting the opportunities for targeted prevention strategies.

Depression is associated with dysregulations of the body’s inflammatory and neuroendocrine systems. Individuals with major depressive disorder (MDD) have shown elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., interleukin [IL]-6, IL-1β, and tumor necrosis factor [TNF]-α) and hyperactivity of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis [7–9]. The inflammatory mediators cross the blood-brain barrier and contribute to depressive symptoms via neuroinflammation [7, 8]. Major surgery acutely activates similar inflammatory pathways, inducing a substantial release of pro-inflammatory cytokines [10]. Elderly patients have shown an exaggerated and prolonged inflammatory response to surgical trauma compared to younger adults [10–12], and this exaggerated response may increase their vulnerability to postoperative depression.

A link between cellular aging and psychiatric disorders that involves telomeres (the protective structures at the ends of chromosomes) has been suggested. For example, several research groups have demonstrated that individuals with MDD have shorter leukocyte telomere lengths (TLs) compared to healthy controls [13–16]. Oxidative stress and chronic inflammation, both of which are elevated in depression, accelerate the shortening of telomeres [17–19]. Inflammatory stress shortens telomeres, and dysfunctional telomeres trigger cellular senescence and further inflammation, creating a bidirectional relationship [20, 21]. As a cumulative marker of physiological stress, the inflammatory burden, and biological aging, the preoperative TL may reflect biological vulnerability to depression. We hypothesized that a shorter preoperative TL may predict the development of acute postoperative depression, defined as a Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D) score ≥ 16 at postoperative day 7 [22], in elderly surgical patients. We therefore conducted this study in patients aged ≥ 65 years undergoing major surgery requiring intensive care unit (ICU) admission.

Methods

Study design

This study is part of a prospective cohort investigation of the associations between telomeres/telomeric G-tails and postoperative delirium and depression in elderly surgical patients that is registered in the Japan Registry of Clinical Trials (jRCT1020220041); the results concerning POD in this cohort are available [23]. Herein, we report our findings regarding acute postoperative depression in the same patient cohort.

Study population

The details of the patient recruitment and eligibility criteria have been described [23]. Briefly, patients aged ≥ 65 years undergoing major surgery requiring an ICU stay ≥ 2 days were prospectively enrolled between January and October 2023 at Hirosaki University Hospital (Aomori, Japan). The key patient exclusion criteria included the presence of cardiopulmonary bypass surgery, preoperative delirium, cognitive impairment (Mini-Mental State Examination-Japanese [MMSE-J] score < 24), psychiatric disorders, alcohol addiction, and several severe comorbidities (liver cirrhosis Child-Pugh ≥ B, heart failure New York Heart Association classification ≥ 3, hematologic disorders). Patients using opioids and those with missing data were also excluded. Notably, patients without a formal diagnosis who scored CES-D ≥ 16 preoperatively were enrolled and are referred to throughout as having “preoperative depressive symptoms”.

Ethical approval

This single-center, prospective observational study received approval from the Ethics Committee of Hirosaki University Graduate School of Medicine (2022-061). Verbal and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was registered in the Japan Registry of Clinical Trials (jRCT1020220041; principal investigator: Satoshi Uchida, https://jrct.niph.go.jp/latest-detail/jRCT1020220041) prior to patient enrollment, with the registration date of January 30, 2023. All research methods were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This manuscript adheres to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Study protocol

Blood samples for the measurement of TL were obtained from each patient on the day of surgery, in the operating room before the induction of anesthesia. Following their surgeries, all patients were transferred to the ICU. After the confirmation of hemodynamic and respiratory stability in the ICU, the patients were transferred to the general ward. Their potential depressive symptoms were assessed both the day before surgery and at 7 days after surgery (i.e., postoperative day 7), with CES-D.

Depressive symptoms assessment

The CES-D is a 20-item self-report questionnaire with scores ranging from 0 to 60 points, where higher scores indicate more severe depressive symptoms. In this study, acute postoperative depression was defined as a postoperative CES-D score ≥ 16 points. We divided the present patients into two groups based on the presence/absence of acute postoperative depression: the depression group and the non-depression group.

Anesthesia and ICU care

All of the patients received general anesthesia with standard monitoring, with epidural anesthesia added in selected cases. The depth of anesthesia was maintained using bispectral index monitoring (target 40–60). The patients were transferred to the ICU with endotracheal intubation after their surgeries. Sedation during mechanical ventilation targeted Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS) scores of − 2 to 0. Extubation was performed after successful spontaneous breathing trials and confirmation of the patient’s awakening.

Study endpoints

The primary study endpoint was the occurrence of acute postoperative depression on postoperative day 7. We investigated whether the patients’ preoperative TL values could predict the development of acute postoperative depression.

TL measurement

Each patient’s leukocyte TL was measured as described [23]. Genomic DNA was extracted from whole blood samples within 72 h of collection, using a modified phenol-chloroform method; this DNA was then analyzed by a hybridization protection assay (coefficient of variation < 10%).

Data collection

We extracted the patients’ demographic and clinical data from their electronic medical records: age, sex, body mass index (BMI), comorbidities, educational background, American Society of Anesthesiologists Physical Status (ASA-PS), primary diagnosis, and the type of surgical procedure conducted. Cognitive function was assessed using MMSE-J (Nihon Bunka Kagaku-sha Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), which is distributed under license from Psychological Assessment Resources, Inc., as part of routine clinical assessment. The perioperative variables included preoperative and postoperative laboratory parameters, i.e., the complete blood count with differential, renal function tests (blood urea nitrogen, creatinine), liver enzymes (aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase), and C-reactive protein. The intraoperative data comprised the duration of anesthesia, fluid management, blood transfusion requirements, estimated blood loss, and urine output. The postoperative variables included the sedative(s) used, a numeric rating scale (NRS) for pain, the RASS score, the duration of mechanical ventilation, and the length of stay in the ICU.

Statistical analyses

The sample size calculation is described in the earlier study [23]. Briefly, the target enrollment of 50 patients was determined based on the primary outcome of POD, because there were few prior data of the association between preoperative TL and acute postoperative depression.

The patients’ characteristics are presented as the mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed continuous variables, the median (25th–75th percentiles) and interquartile range (IQR) for non-normally distributed continuous variables, and the number (percentage) for categorical variables. Differences between the postoperative depression and non-depression groups were evaluated using Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. For continuous variables, normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables, including the preoperative and postoperative TL values, were compared between groups using Student’s t-test or Welch’s t-test based on Levene’s test for equality of variances. Non-normally distributed variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U-test.

We conducted univariable logistic regression analyses to assess whether the patients’ preoperative TL values could predict acute postoperative depression. The three variables showing significant between-group differences (preoperative CES-D score, surgical procedure type, and anesthesia duration) were also subjected to separate univariable logistic regression analyses to characterize their individual associations with acute postoperative depression. Preoperative TL was forced into the multivariable logistic regression model as an explanatory variable. The preoperative CES-D score and the surgical procedure type were also included in the multivariable model, as both showed significant associations with acute postoperative depression in the univariable logistic regression analysis. On the other hand, anesthesia duration was not included in the multivariable model, because it showed no significant association with acute postoperative depression in the univariable logistic regression analysis. Multicollinearity among variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF). We evaluated discrimination by calculating the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). The results are presented as adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The events-per-variable ratio was approximately 6.7, which is at the lower boundary of what is conventionally considered acceptable; accordingly, all multivariable results should be interpreted as exploratory.

To explore potential heterogeneity across surgical procedures, we performed separate exploratory post-hoc subgroup analyses for the cases that involved off-pump coronary bypass grafting (OPCAB), esophageal surgery, and head & neck surgery. Given that all of the patients who underwent head & neck surgery required a tracheostomy, which is independently associated with depressive symptoms through communication impairment and loss of voice [24], we also performed a post-hoc sensitivity analysis that excluded this subgroup (n = 10). These analyses were considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating, and the results should be interpreted with caution given the small sample sizes within each subgroup.

The study’s statistical analyses were performed using Python ver. 3.12.3 with the statsmodels ver. 0.14.6, scipy, and scikit-learn libraries. A probability (p)-value < 0.05 was considered significant for all tests.

Results

Patient characteristics

As illustrated in Fig. 1, of the 105 candidate patients, 48 were included in this study. Of these 48 patients, 20 showed symptoms of acute postoperative depression and were thus classified as the depression group, and the other 28 patients comprised the non-depression group. The characteristics of the two patient groups are summarized in Table 1. The median (IQR) preoperative CES-D scores in the depression group (11.5 [5.8–21.2]) were significantly higher than those in non-depression group (4.0 [2.0–9.0]), p = 0.001. As expected, the postoperative CES-D scores in the depression group (20.5 [19.0–28.0]) were significantly higher than those in the non-depression group (6.5 [4.0–8.2]), p = 0.010.

The surgical procedure types differed significantly between the two groups (p < 0.001), and the duration of anesthesia was significantly longer in the depression group (10.0 h [8.1–11.7]) compared to the non-depression group (6.5 [5.8–8.3]), p = 0.001.

Preoperative TL

As shown in Table 1, although the preoperative TL values of the patients in the depression group (303,902 ± 48,103 RLU/µg DNA) were shorter than those in the non-depression group (331,816 ± 48,984 RLU/µg DNA), the difference was not significant (p = 0.056).

Predictive model for acute postoperative depression

The results of the univariable logistic regression analysis revealed that the preoperative TL did not have a significant association with the development of acute postoperative depression (per 1 SD decrease, OR: 1.846, 95%CI: 0.966–3.527, p = 0.064) (Table 2). The additional univariable logistic regression analyses performed for the variables that were significantly different between the groups (preoperative depressive symptoms, surgical procedure type, and anesthesia time) were performed. The presence of preoperative depressive symptoms (OR 8.667, 95%CI: 1.593–47.145, p = 0.013) and the surgical procedure (esophageal surgery and head & neck surgery vs. OPCAB: OR 14.667, 95%CI: 3.546–60.654, p < 0.001) had significant associations with postoperative depression.

We then included these two variables and the preoperative TL in a multivariable logistic regression model. The results showed that (i) the preoperative TL (per 1 SD decrease, OR 1.585, 95%CI: 0.722–3.479, p = 0.251) and preoperative depressive symptoms (OR 6.409, 95%CI: 0.743–55.296, p = 0.091) did not have a significant association with acute postoperative depression, and (ii) the surgical procedure type had significant association with acute postoperative depression (esophageal surgery and head & neck surgery vs. OPCAB: OR 14.295, 95%CI: 2.990–68.634, p < 0.001). Exploratory post-hoc subgroup analysis by surgical procedure type

As shown in Table 3, the preoperative TL did not significantly differ across the three surgical procedure groups (OPCAB: 326,334 ± 51,348, esophageal surgery: 322,071 ± 43,746, head & neck surgery: 301,936 ± 54,279; p = 0.544). The results of the subgroup analyses within each surgical procedure type are shown in Table 4. In the OPCAB and esophageal surgery subgroups, the preoperative TL was shorter in the depression group, although the differences were not significant. In the head & neck surgery subgroup, no such difference was observed. The univariable logistic regression analyses also showed that there were no significant differences between the depression and non-depression patients in all of the surgical procedure subgroups.

Given that all of the head & neck surgery patients required a tracheostomy, we performed a post-hoc sensitivity analysis excluding these patients (n = 10). In the remaining 38 patients, the preoperative TL was significantly shorter in the depression group compared to the non-depression group (302,990 ± 38,881 vs. 336,426 ± 49,755, p = 0.042) (Table 5). The univariable logistic regression showed no association between the preoperative TL and acute postoperative depression (per 1 SD decrease, OR: 2.222, 95%CI: 0.993–4.970, p = 0.052). The multivariable logistic regression analysis including the preoperative TL and the surgical procedure type demonstrated that the preoperative TL had a significant association with acute postoperative depression (per 1 SD decrease, OR 3.556, 95%CI: 1.121–11.282, p = 0.031). Given the small sample size of this sensitivity cohort (n = 38), the wide confidence interval indicates that this estimate should be interpreted with caution as potentially unstable.

Discussion

In this single-center pilot study of elderly patients undergoing major surgery, preoperative TL tended to be shorter in patients who developed acute postoperative depression, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (depression group: 303,902 ± 48,103 vs. non-depression group: 331,816 ± 48,984 RLU/µg DNA, p = 0.056). In a post-hoc sensitivity analysis excluding head & neck surgery patients, a shorter preoperative TL was independently associated with acute postoperative depression (OR 3.556, 95%CI: 1.121–11.282, p = 0.031), although this finding should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

We observed that the type of surgical procedure played a crucial role in the development of acute postoperative depression. The incidence of acute postoperative depression differed markedly across the surgical types (OPCAB 15.4%, esophageal surgery 75.0%, head & neck surgery 70.0%), but the preoperative TL did not differ significantly across these groups (p = 0.544), suggesting that procedure-specific factors rather than biological aging primarily drove the heterogeneity in the depression incidence. Among the patients who underwent esophageal or head & neck surgery, the extent of surgical trauma, a disease-specific psychosocial burden, and anticipated functional loss may have contributed to the higher depression rates [25–27]. Our analyses also revealed that the preoperative CES-D scores were higher in the patients who underwent esophageal or head & neck surgery, reflecting the psychological burden of the underlying malignancy [26, 28].

All 10 of the head & neck surgery patients in this study required a tracheostomy, which may have independently contributed to the development of depressive symptoms through communication impairment and loss of voice [24, 29]. In this subgroup, the preoperative TL was not shorter in the patients who showed acute postoperative depression compared to those who did not (293,395 ± 14,342 vs. 305,597 ± 65,565, p = 0.765) and the OR was reversed, suggesting that depression in these patients was driven by procedure-specific factors rather than the preoperative TL. We therefore performed a post-hoc subgroup analysis excluding the head & neck surgery group. In the remaining 38 patients, the preoperative TL was significantly shorter in the patients with acute postoperative depression (p = 0.042) and associated with acute postoperative depression in the exploratory sensitivity analysis after adjustment for surgical procedure type (p = 0.031). This pattern raises the possibility of two layers of risk for acute postoperative depression in this cohort: a dominant, procedure-driven layer and a possible additional biological contribution that may be partly reflected in preoperative TL. However, because this sensitivity analysis was exploratory and conducted in a small subgroup, the findings should be interpreted with caution. The observed association may reflect residual confounding by procedure-specific psychosocial and physiological burdens rather than an independent biological effect of TL. Therefore, these findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in larger studies involving more homogeneous surgical populations.

Our search of the relevant literature indicates that the present study is the first to investigate the association between preoperative TL values and the development of acute postoperative depression. Our findings raise the tentative possibility that preoperative TL may reflect biological vulnerability to acute postoperative depression, as has been proposed for postoperative delirium [23]. However, given the non-significant primary analysis, the exploratory nature of the post-hoc subgroup findings, and the small sample size throughout, this interpretation remains speculative. The hypothesis that preoperative TL may serve as a biomarker of susceptibility to perioperative neuropsychiatric complications warrants investigation in adequately powered, prospectively designed confirmatory studies before any clinical implications can be considered.

To estimate the sample size required for a future study, we performed post-hoc power calculations based on the effect sizes observed in this pilot study. These calculations were conducted to detect the observed between-group difference in preoperative TL using a two-sample t-test with 80% power at a two-sided α of 0.05. For the overall cohort (Cohen’s d = 0.574, depression incidence 41.7%), approx. 100 patients would be required. In the sensitivity cohort excluding the head & neck surgery patients (Cohen’s d = 0.720, incidence 34.2%), approx. 69 patients would be required.

There are several study limitations to consider. (1) This was a single-center pilot study with a small sample size (n = 48), limiting the statistical power and the generalizability of findings. The overall between-group difference in preoperative TL did not reach conventional significance (p = 0.056), and the significant findings in the sensitivity analysis should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. (2) All of the subgroup analyses were post-hoc and exploratory. In particular, the exclusion of head and neck surgery patients was not pre-specified, and the significant finding in this sensitivity analysis may represent a chance finding arising from post-hoc subgroup selection. The results should therefore be interpreted with caution and not as confirmatory evidence. (3) The study included three surgically heterogeneous patient groups with markedly different incidences of acute postoperative depression (OPCAB 15.4%, esophageal surgery 75.0%, head & neck surgery 70.0%), reflecting vastly different physiological and psychological burdens across procedure types. Surgical procedure type emerged as the dominant predictor of acute postoperative depression, and despite statistical adjustment, residual confounding by procedure-specific factors cannot be excluded. This heterogeneity represents the central methodological challenge of this study, limiting the ability to isolate preoperative TL as an independent biological predictor. (4) We did not capture several potentially important confounders of acute postoperative depression, including detailed psychiatric history, social support, cancer severity and prognosis, the occurrence of acute postoperative delirium (which may overlap symptomatically with the CES-D construct), the type and cumulative dose of ICU sedatives, and tracheostomy-specific factors beyond procedure type. These unmeasured confounders may have biased the observed association between preoperative TL and acute postoperative depression. (5) Acute postoperative depression was assessed at a single time point (postoperative day 7). At this early postoperative stage, concurrent delirium-related dysphoria, pain, and fatigue may elevate CES-D scores independently of true depressive affect, making it difficult to distinguish transient postoperative distress from true depressive disorder—a limitation that introduces the possibility of misclassification bias [30, 31]. Longitudinal assessments at multiple postoperative time points, combined with structured diagnostic interviews based on DSM-5 criteria, are needed in future studies to more accurately characterize the nature and persistence of postoperative depressive symptoms. (6) Although the CES-D ≥ 16 cutoff has been validated in elderly populations [32] and has been applied in postoperative cohorts at one week after surgery [33], formal validation of this threshold specifically in elderly postoperative patients has not been established. Future studies are warranted to determine the optimal CES-D cutoff in this population.

Conclusions

Our analyses did not identify a significant association between elderly patients’ preoperative TLs and the development of acute postoperative depression, likely reflecting the dominance of surgical procedure type as a risk factor in a surgically heterogeneous cohort. The exploratory post-hoc subgroup analyses excluding the patients who underwent head & neck surgery showed that a shorter preoperative TL was significantly associated with an increased risk of acute postoperative depression, suggesting a possible additional biological contribution, although residual confounding cannot be excluded. However, this preliminary finding is hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory and should be interpreted with caution. Further research conducted in surgically homogeneous populations with larger sample sizes is needed to confirm our present findings.
