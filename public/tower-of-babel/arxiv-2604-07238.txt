On the Price of Privacy for Language Identification and Generation
Xiaoyu Li, Andi Han, Jiaojiao Jiang, Junbin Gao, [0.8em] University of New South Wales

Abstract
As large language models (LLMs) are increasingly trained on sensitive user data, understanding the fundamental cost of privacy in language learning becomes essential. We initiate the study of differentially private (DP) language identification and generation in the agnostic statistical setting, establishing algorithms and matching lower bounds that precisely quantify the cost of privacy. For both tasks, approximate ( ε , δ ) (\varepsilon,\delta) -DP with constant ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 recovers the non-private error rates: exp ⁡ ( − r ⁡ ( n ) ) \exp(-r(n)) for identification (for any r ⁡ ( n ) = o ⁡ ( n ) r(n)=o(n) ) and exp ⁡ ( − Ω ⁡ ( n ) ) \exp(-\Omega(n)) for generation. Under pure ε \varepsilon -DP, the exponents degrade by a multiplicative factor of min ⁡ { 1 , ε } \min\{1,\varepsilon\} , which we show is tight up to constants. Notably, for generation under pure DP with mild assumptions, the upper bound exp ( − min { 1 , ε } ⋅ Ω ( n ) ) \exp(-\min\{1,\varepsilon\}\cdot\Omega(n)) matches the lower bound up to some constants, establishing an optimal rate. Our results show that the cost of privacy in language learning is surprisingly mild: absent entirely under approximate DP, and exactly a min ⁡ { 1 , ε } \min\{1,\varepsilon\} factor in the exponent under pure DP.



1
Introduction

Differential privacy
[
25
]
provides a mathematically rigorous
framework for learning from sensitive data, yet its cost is not uniform
across tasks: some learning problems can be solved privately at no
statistical penalty, while others suffer an unavoidable degradation.
Understanding the price of privacy for a specific learning problem is a central question in private learning
theory. Language identification and generation, as fundamental tasks in
language learning, are a natural setting in which to ask this question, especially given the growing practice of training LLMs on sensitive data, where differentially private fine-tuning has already
shown strong empirical performance, sometimes approaching non-private baselines
[
46
;
68
]
, and the compute-privacy-utility tradeoff of private training is empirically characterized
[
50
]
.
Yet despite this practical progress, the fundamental theoretical cost
of privacy for these tasks remains largely unexplored. we address this
gap by establishing a complete characterization of the price
of privacy for both language identification and generation.

The formal study of language learnability traces back to the seminal work
of
30
, who introduced the
identification in the limit

model, and to
6
;
7
, who characterized
identifiability for several important language classes.
These classical results revealed fundamental barriers: for instance, even
the class of all regular languages is not identifiable in the limit from
positive examples alone.
Recently,
38
proposed
generation
as an alternative
objective: rather than naming the target language, the learner need only
produce a novel string consistent with the underlying distribution.
This relaxation turns out to be dramatically more powerful:
generation is achievable for every countable language collection,
sidestepping the classical impossibility results for identification.

The aforementioned results all operate in an
online

setting, where the learner receives examples one at a time from an
adversarially chosen sequence and must eventually converge to a correct
hypothesis.

34
initiated the study of language learning in the

statistical
setting, where the learner receives an
i.i.d. sample of fixed size drawn from some unknown distribution, and
33
extended this framework
to the agnostic case, where the target distribution need not be supported on any
single language in the collection.

Our setup follows the
agnostic statistical
language learning
model introduced by
33
.
A learner receives
n
i.i.d. samples

S=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}
drawn from an unknown distribution

\mathcal{D}
over a countable universe
\mathcal{U}
, together with a countable
collection
\mathcal{C}=\{L_{1},L_{2},\ldots\}
of languages, where each
language
L_{i}
is a subset of
\mathcal{U}
.
This setting gives rise to two fundamental tasks:

•

Language Identification:
Output a language
\widehat{L}\in\mathcal{C}
whose population risk
\Pr_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}[x\not\in\widehat{L}]
is close to the best achievable within
\mathcal{C}
.

•

Language Generation:
Output a string
\widehat{x}\in\mathcal{U}
that is both
valid
(
\widehat{x}\in\operatorname{supp}(D)
) and
novel
(
\widehat{x}\not\in S
) with high probability.

Without privacy constraints,
33
showed that identification error decays at a nearly exponential rate
\exp(-r(n))
where
r
is any sublinear function (i.e.,
r(n)=o(n)
) under an attainability condition on the agnostic optimum, and that generation error decays at a fully exponential rate
\exp(-\Omega(n))
under mild structural assumptions.
This raises a natural question:

What is the price of differential privacy for language identification and generation?

Two key takeaways stand out.
First, approximate DP eliminates the privacy cost entirely for both tasks, yielding a qualitative separation from pure DP.
Second, generation enjoys a
tighter
privacy-utility tradeoff than identification: the pure DP upper and lower bounds match up to constants in the exponent, whereas identification retains an
o(n)
vs.
O(n)
gap inherited from the non-private setting.
We establish these rates through three contributions, each addressing a distinct technical challenge in privatizing the non-private algorithms of
33
. We summarize the key ideas and techniques
below.

•

DP Identification (
Section

3
).

The non-private algorithm employs a margin-based selection rule that is discontinuous: changing a single sample can flip which indices satisfy the margin constraint.
We replace it with a
smooth score function
that jointly encodes the preference for large language indices and the penalty for failing the margin test, and privatize it via the exponential mechanism (pure DP) and the Gaussian mechanism (approximate DP).
The score has sensitivity
\Theta(f(n)^{2}/n)
, where
f
is an increasing function, yet the score gap on good events is
\Omega(1)
.
The function
f
must be carefully chosen to balance the resulting bias–variance–privacy tradeoff, which yields the rates in
Table

1
.

•

DP Generation (
Section

4
).

The non-private pointer-based rule has unbounded sensitivity: a single sample change can cause a language’s pointer to jump arbitrarily.
We replace it with a
thresholded prefix count
, defined as the minimum number of times each relevant string appears in the sample, whose sensitivity is a
constant
, independent of
n
and
f(n)
.
We again privatize via the exponential mechanism (pure DP) and the Gaussian mechanism (approximate DP).
This structural advantage is the key reason generation achieves strictly better rates: the score gap grows as
\Omega(n)
while the sensitivity stays
O(1)
, yielding a fully exponential rate
\exp(-\min\{1,\varepsilon\}\cdot\Omega(n))
under pure DP.
We present the algorithm first with a public witness bound for clarity, then extend it to the setting where no such bound is available.

•

Lower Bounds
(
Section

5
).
We prove that the
\min\{1,\varepsilon\}
scaling in the exponent is tight for both tasks.
For each task, we construct a pair of hard distributions that are close in Hamming distance under a natural coupling, then apply group privacy to show that any
\varepsilon
-DP algorithm must incur error at least
\exp(-\min\{1,\varepsilon\}\cdot O(n))
.
The two arguments differ in structure.
For identification, the construction is symmetric: the two distributions swap the roles of two languages, and the coupling lemma constrains the misidentification probability in both directions simultaneously.
For generation, the construction is inherently asymmetric: the two distributions share a common high-probability element but have disjoint “private” supports, so that any string witnessing success under one distribution necessarily witnesses failure under the other.
The coupling lemma then forces any
\varepsilon
-DP algorithm to fail on at least one distribution.
For generation, the resulting lower bound matches the upper bound up to constants in the exponent, establishing an optimal rate of
\exp(-\Theta(\min\{1,\varepsilon\}\cdot n))
.

Broader perspective.

Our results reveal that the price of privacy in language learning is
governed by the sensitivity structure of the learning objective, not
just the complexity of the hypothesis class.
Concretely, generation exploits a score with constant sensitivity and
achieves non-private rates under both pure and approximate DP, whereas
identification relies on a margin criterion whose sensitivity grows
with the horizon and consequently pays a larger cost under pure DP.
This contrast supplies concrete evidence for a broader design principle:

reformulating a learning objective to reduce its sensitivity
structure can reduce the privacy-utility tradeoff.

While our information-theoretic framework does not directly model the
DP-SGD pipeline for large language
models
[
1
;
46
;
58
]
, we hope this principle may nonetheless
offer guidance for practical algorithm design.

2.1
Language Identification and Generation

Let
\mathcal{U}=\{u_{1},u_{2},\ldots\}
be a countable universe of strings.
A
language
is any subset
L\subseteq\mathcal{U}
, and a
language collection

\mathcal{C}\subseteq 2^{\mathcal{U}}
is indexed as
\mathcal{C}=\{L_{1},L_{2},\ldots\}
when countable.
Let
\mathcal{D}
be an unknown distribution over
\mathcal{U}
.
For any language
L
, its
population risk
is
\mathrm{err}_{\mathcal{D}}(L):=\Pr_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}[x\notin L]
, and its
empirical risk
on a sample
S=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
is
\mathrm{err}_{S}(L):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{x_{t}\notin L\}
.

We work in the agnostic statistical setting of
33
, building on the realizable framework of
34
.

Language Identification.

An identification algorithm
\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{id}}
observes
S=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}
and outputs a language
\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{id}}(S)\in\mathcal{C}
.
Its
identification error
is the expected excess risk over the agnostic optimum:

\displaystyle\iderr(\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{id}},\mathcal{D},\mathcal{C},n):=\E_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{n},r}\bigl[\mathrm{err}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{id}}(S))\bigr]-\inf_{L\in\mathcal{C}}\,\mathrm{err}_{\mathcal{D}}(L),

where
r
denotes the internal randomness of the algorithm.

Language Generation.

A generation algorithm
\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{gen}}
observes
S\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}
and outputs a string
\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{gen}}(S)\in\mathcal{U}
that should be both
valid
(in
\supp(\mathcal{D})
) and
novel
(not in
S
).
Its
generation error
is the failure probability:

\displaystyle\generr(\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{gen}},\mathcal{D},\mathcal{C},n):=\Pr_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{n},r}\bigl[\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{gen}}(S)\notin\supp(\mathcal{D})\setminus S\bigr],

where
r
denotes the internal randomness of the algorithm.

Remark 2.1
.

The generation error
\generr(\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{gen}},\mathcal{D},\mathcal{C},n)
seems depending only on the distribution
\mathcal{D}
and sample size
n
, and is entirely independent of the language collection
\mathcal{C}
. In particular, agnostic generation does not require the learner to first identify which language in
\mathcal{C}
best fits the data, and it suffices to produce a novel string in
\supp(\mathcal{D})
. However, without structural assumptions relating
\mathcal{D}
to
\mathcal{C}
,
33
showed that the generation error can be arbitrarily bad.

2.2
Differential Privacy

Two datasets
S,S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}^{n}
are
neighboring
, written
S\sim S^{\prime}
, if they differ in exactly one entry.

Definition 2.2

(Differential Privacy
[
25
]
)
.

A randomized algorithm
\mathcal{A}\colon\mathcal{U}^{n}\to\mathcal{R}
is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differentially private if for every pair of neighboring datasets
S\sim S^{\prime}
and every measurable
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
,

\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S)\in\mathcal{F}]\leq e^{\varepsilon}\cdot\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S^{\prime})\in\mathcal{F}]+\delta.

When
\delta=0
we say
\mathcal{A}
is
\varepsilon
-DP (
pure
DP); when
\delta>0
we say
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP (
approximate
DP), typically with
\delta\leq 1/\poly(n)
.

Lemma 2.3

(Post-Processing
[
26
]
)
.

If
\mathcal{A}
is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP and
h
is any (possibly randomized) function, then
h\circ\mathcal{A}
is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP.

Exponential mechanism.

The exponential mechanism
51
, which is
the canonical tool for privately optimizing over a discrete set:
it selects outcomes with probability exponentially weighted by
their scores, achieving pure DP without requiring the output
space to be numeric.

Given a score function
q\colon\mathcal{U}^{n}\times\mathcal{R}\to\mathbb{R}
with sensitivity
\Delta q:=\max_{i\in\mathcal{R}}\max_{S\sim S^{\prime}}|q(S,i)-q(S^{\prime},i)|.
The exponential mechanism
\mathrm{EM}_{\varepsilon}(S,q,\mathcal{R})
selects and outputs
i\in\mathcal{R}
with probability
\propto\exp\Bigl(\frac{\varepsilon}{2\Delta q}\,q(S,i)\Bigr).

Lemma 2.4

(
51
;
26
)
.

The exponential mechanism
\mathrm{EM}_{\varepsilon}(S,q,\mathcal{R})
is
\varepsilon
-DP.
Moreover, for any
\beta\in(0,1)
, with probability
\geq 1-\beta
the output satisfies

q(S,i)\geq\OPT_{q}(S)-\frac{2\Delta q}{\varepsilon}\log\frac{|\mathcal{R}|}{\beta},

where
\OPT_{q}(S):=\max_{i\in\mathcal{R}}q(S,i)
.

Gaussian Mechanism.

We make use of Gaussian mechanism
[
24
]
to design our approximate-DP algorithms.
Given
f\colon\mathcal{U}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\ell_{2}
-sensitivity
\Delta_{2}:=\max_{S\sim S^{\prime}}\|f(S)-f(S^{\prime})\|_{2}
, the Gaussian mechanism releases
f(S)+Z
with
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})
.

Lemma 2.5

(
24
;
26
)
.

The Gaussian mechanism is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP if

\sigma\geq\frac{\Delta_{2}}{\varepsilon}\sqrt{2\log(1.25/\delta)}.

Remark 2.6
.

Sharper variance thresholds for the Gaussian mechanism are known e.g., via
Rényi DP
[
53
]
, the analytic
Gaussian mechanism
[
11
]
, or
f
-DP
[
23
]
. They also extend the relax the requirement of
\varepsilon
from
(0,1)
to
(0,\infty)
.
As this is a first study of differentially private language learning,
we use the classical bound above for simplicity; all our results hold
a fortiori under tighter calibration.

3
Differentially Private Language Identification

We design differentially private identification algorithms that, given
S\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}
, output a language
L_{\hat{i}}\in\mathcal{C}
whose population risk is nearly as small as the agnostic optimum.
We work under the following assumption, necessary for fast convergence even without privacy
[
33
]
.

Assumption 3.1

(Agnostic optimum is attainable)
.

There exists an index
i^{\star}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathrm{err}_{\mathcal{D}}(L_{i^{\star}})=\inf_{L\in\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{err}_{\mathcal{D}}(L)
.
Let
i^{\star}
denote the smallest such index.

Score function design.

The non-private algorithm of
33
selects the largest index
i\in[f(n)]
whose empirical risk beats all predecessors by a margin of at least
2/f(n)
, where
f(n)\to\infty
is a growing horizon.
A natural first attempt at privatization would be to use empirical risk directly as the score in the exponential mechanism.
However, the agnostic setting requires selecting the
largest feasible index
rather than the one with minimum risk: the horizon
f(n)
must grow to eventually include
i^{\star}
, and smaller indices are trivially within range but suboptimal.
Moreover, the non-private margin test is discontinuous in the data: changing a single sample can flip an index from feasible to infeasible, so the test cannot be privatized directly.

We resolve this by replacing the hard feasibility test with a
soft score
that continuously encodes both objectives.
For each
i\in[f(n)]
, define the empirical margin

M_{S}(i):=\begin{cases}1,&i=1,\\
\min_{j\in[i-1]}\bigl(\mathrm{err}_{S}(L_{j})-\mathrm{err}_{S}(L_{i})\bigr),&i\geq 2,\end{cases}

the deficit
d_{S}(i):=\bigl(\tfrac{2}{f(n)}-M_{S}(i)\bigr)_{+}
, and the score

q(S,i):=i-f(n)^{2}d_{S}(i).

The term
i
rewards larger indices, while the penalty
-f(n)^{2}d_{S}(i)
continuously downweights indices that fail the margin test.
When
d_{S}(i)=0
(margin satisfied), the score equals
i
; when
d_{S}(i)
is large, the penalty dominates.
The coefficient
f(n)^{2}
is chosen so that any index with full deficit incurs a penalty exceeding its positional reward.

(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differentially private and, for all sufficiently large
n
,

\iderr(\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{id}}_{\varepsilon,\delta,f},\mathcal{D},\mathcal{C},n)\leq 2f(n)\exp\Bigl(-\frac{n}{8f(n)^{2}}\Bigr)+2f(n)\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}n^{2}}{64f(n)^{3}\log(1.25/\delta)}\Bigr).

Setting
f(n)=\sqrt{c\log n}
yields
\iderr\lesssim\exp(-n/\log n)
for any
\varepsilon=\Omega(1)
and
\delta\geq\exp(-\poly(n))
, matching the non-private rate of
33
.
Hence, under approximate DP with constant privacy parameters, identification incurs no asymptotic cost relative to its non-private counterpart.
When
\varepsilon\to 0
, the two mechanisms diverge: the pure DP rate degrades as
\exp(-\varepsilon\cdot r(n))
for any
r(n)=o(n)
while the approximate DP rate degrades as
\exp(-\varepsilon^{2}\cdot r(n))
, making pure DP preferable in the low-privacy regime
\varepsilon\ll 1
. The proof of
Theorem

3.3
is in
Appendix

D
.

4
Differentially Private Language Generation

We design differentially private generation algorithms that, given
S\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}
, output a novel string from
\supp(\mathcal{D})\setminus S
.
We fix an enumeration
\mathcal{U}=\{u_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and assume every language
L\in\mathcal{C}
is infinite.

Definition 4.1

(Witness Index)
.

The
witness index
is defined as

i(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D}):=\inf\{i\in\mathbb{N}:\forall\,L\in\mathcal{C}\text{ with }L\not\subseteq\supp(\mathcal{D}),\;\exists\,k\leq i\text{ s.t.\ }u_{k}\in L\setminus\supp(\mathcal{D})\},

with the convention
\inf\varnothing=\infty
.

In words,
i(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})
is the smallest prefix length such that every language not fully contained in
\supp(\mathcal{D})
has a
witness
(a string in the language but outside the support) within the first
i(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})
elements of
\mathcal{U}
.

We work under the following assumptions
[
33
]
.

Assumption 4.2

(Finite witness index)
.

The witness index is finite, i.e.,
i(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})<\infty
.

Assumption 4.3

(Support contains a reference language)
.

There exists
i^{\star}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
L_{i^{\star}}\subseteq\supp(\mathcal{D})
.
Let
i^{\star}
denote the smallest such index.

Both assumptions are necessary for generation and are inherited from the non-private setting.

Section

4
ensures that every language not contained in
\supp(\mathcal{D})
can be ruled out by a finite prefix of the enumeration: without it, no finite sample could distinguish good languages from bad ones.

Section

4
guarantees that at least one language in
\mathcal{C}
is fully supported by
\mathcal{D}
, so that a valid novel string exists; without it, every language would contain strings outside
\supp(\mathcal{D})
, making correct generation impossible.

Score function design.

The non-private algorithm in
[
33
]
maintains, for each language
L_{i}
, a
pointer
: the index of the smallest string in
L_{i}\cap\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{W}\}
not yet seen in the sample.

For good languages (
L_{i}\subseteq\supp(\mathcal{D})
), all prefix strings eventually appear in the sample and the pointer advances past the witness window; for bad languages (
L_{i}\not\subseteq\supp(\mathcal{D})
), the pointer gets stuck at their witness.
However, this pointer has
unbounded sensitivity
.

We replace the pointer with a
thresholded prefix count
.
For each language
L_{i}
and a witness window
[W]
, let
I_{i}(W):=\{k\in[W]:u_{k}\in L_{i}\}
and define the minimum prefix count

a_{i}^{W}(S):=\begin{cases}\min_{k\in I_{i}(W)}N_{S}(u_{k}),&\text{if }I_{i}(W)\neq\varnothing,\\
n,&\text{if }I_{i}(W)=\varnothing,\end{cases}

where
N_{S}(u_{k}):=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{x_{t}=u_{k}\}
.
Rather than asking whether every relevant prefix string has appeared at least once, we ask whether these strings have appeared at least
g(n)
times for a threshold
g(n)\to\infty
.
The
deficit
is
d_{i}^{W}(S):=(g(n)-a_{i}^{W}(S))_{+}
and the
score
is

q^{W}(S,i):=-d_{i}^{W}(S).

The score is 0 when the prefix is well-covered (good language) and
-g(n)
when a witness string is never observed (bad language).

Remark 4.4

(Constant Sensitivity)
.

The generation score has sensitivity
\Delta=1
since each multiplicity
N_{S}(u_{k})
changes by at most 1 under a neighboring dataset, the minimum preserves Lipschitz constants, and
(g(n)-\cdot)_{+}
is 1-Lipschitz.
This is in sharp contrast with the identification score (
Section

3
), whose sensitivity scales as
\Theta(f(n)^{2}/n)
.
The constant sensitivity is the structural reason generation achieves a tighter privacy–utility tradeoff: the score gap grows as
\Omega(n)
while the sensitivity stays
O(1)
, giving the exponential mechanism far more room.

4.2
Pure DP Generation without a Public Witness Bound

When no public bound on
i(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})
is available, we jointly search over both the language index
i
and a candidate witness threshold
t
.
For each pair
(i,t)\in[f(n)]\times[h(n)]
, define the prefix count
a_{i,t}(S)
and deficit
d_{i,t}(S)
analogously to the public-bound case with
t
replacing
W
, and the
pair score

q_{\mathrm{pair}}(S,(i,t)):=t-\frac{h(n)}{g(n)}\,d_{i,t}(S).

(4.1)

The term
t
rewards larger thresholds (indicating progress past the witness window), while the penalty
\frac{h(n)}{g(n)}d_{i,t}(S)
suppresses languages that fail the witness test at threshold
t
.
The coefficient
h(n)/g(n)
is chosen so that a bad language with full deficit
g(n)
incurs a penalty of exactly
h(n)
, ensuring its score is at most
t-h(n)\leq 0
.

The algorithm applies the exponential mechanism over the product range
[f(n)]\times[h(n)]
and outputs a string from the selected language beyond the selected threshold (see
Algorithm

6
in the Appendix).

Sensitivity and score gap.

The pair score has sensitivity
h(n)/g(n)
, higher than the constant sensitivity of the public-bound case; this is the cost of not knowing the witness bound.
However, the score gap also grows: on the coverage event, the good pair
(i^{\star},h(n))
achieves score
h(n)
while every bad pair achieves score at most
i(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})-1\leq h(n)/2-1
(when
h(n)\geq 2\,i(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})
).
The effective ratio gap/sensitivity is thus approximately
g(n)
, comparable to the public-bound case.

.

4.3
Approximate DP Generation

For approximate DP, we replace the exponential mechanism with the Gaussian mechanism: independent Gaussian noise is added to each pair score before taking the argmax (see
Algorithm

7
in
Appendix

E
for the full algorithm).

Since the generation score has constant per-coordinate sensitivity, the
\ell_{2}
-sensitivity of the score vector over
[f(n)]\times[h(n)]
is only
\frac{h(n)}{g(n)}\sqrt{f(n)h(n)}
, requiring Gaussian noise of standard deviation
\sigma=\frac{h(n)}{g(n)}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{f(n)h(n)}}{\varepsilon}\sqrt{2\log(1.25/\delta)}
.
The score gap is
\Omega(h(n))
and grows linearly with
h(n)
, while
\sigma
grows only as
h(n)^{3/2}\sqrt{f(n)}\,/\,(\varepsilon\,g(n))
.
When
g(n)\asymp n
(known mass floor) and
f(n),h(n)
grow slowly, the noise is negligible relative to the gap, eliminating the privacy cost entirely.

Theorem 4.9

(Approximate DP Generation)
.

Under the same conditions as
Theorem

4.6
, there exists an algorithm (
Algorithm

7
) that is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differentially private and achieves

\generr\leq\exp(-\Omega(n))

for any constant
\varepsilon>0
and
\delta\geq\exp(-\poly(n))
, matching the non-private rate.

Thus privacy is essentially free under approximate DP for generation.
This is qualitatively different from the identification setting (
Section

3.2
), where approximate DP also recovers the non-private rate but that rate is only
\exp(-\widetilde{\Omega}(n))
rather than
\exp(-\Omega(n))
.
The fundamental reason is the constant sensitivity of the generation score: the Gaussian noise magnitude
\sigma\propto\sqrt{f(n)}/\varepsilon
is negligible relative to the score gap
g(n)\propto n
, so the noise concentration event holds with probability
1-\exp(-\omega(n))
. The proof of
Theorem

4.9
is in
Appendix

E
.

5
Lower Bounds

We show that the
\min\{1,\varepsilon\}
scaling in the exponent is tight for both tasks.
The shared strategy is to construct a pair of hard distributions close in Hamming distance under a natural coupling, then apply group privacy to constrain any
\varepsilon
-DP algorithm. We first state the main tool underlying both proofs.

Lemma 5.1

(Group Privacy
26
)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{U}^{n}\to\mathcal{R}
be
\varepsilon
-differentially private.
If
S,S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}^{n}
differ in at most
k
entries, then for every measurable
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
, we have

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S)\in\mathcal{F}]\leq\exp(\varepsilon k)\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S^{\prime})\in\mathcal{F}],

where the probability is taken over the randomness of
\mathcal{A}
.

Group privacy extends the basic DP guarantee from a single entry change to
k
simultaneous changes, at the cost of multiplying the privacy parameter by
k
.
This amplification is the key mechanism through which our lower bounds arise: if two distributions can be coupled so that their samples typically differ in
K
positions, then any
\varepsilon
-DP algorithm can only distinguish them up to a factor of
\exp(\varepsilon K)
.

A
coupling
between distributions
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}
is a joint distribution whose marginals are
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}
, respectively.
The
Hamming distance
between two sequences
S=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
and
S^{\prime}=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})
is defined as
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{Ham}}(S,S^{\prime}):=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{X_{i}\neq Y_{i}\}
, i.e., the number of positions where the two sequences differ.
Combining group privacy with a probabilistic bound on the Hamming distance under a coupling yields the following lemma, which is the main tool for both of our lower bounds. We defer the proof to
Appendix

B
.

Lemma 5.2

(Coupling Lemma via Group Privacy, a Variant of Lemma 19 in
2
)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{U}^{n}\to\mathcal{R}
be
\varepsilon
-differentially private.
Let
(S,S^{\prime})
be a coupling of two distributions over
\mathcal{U}^{n}
such that
\Pr[\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{Ham}}(S,S^{\prime})>K]\leq\eta
for some
K\geq 0
and
\eta\in[0,1]
.
Then for every measurable set
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
, we have

\displaystyle\Pr_{S,r}[\mathcal{A}(S)\in\mathcal{F}]\leq\exp(\varepsilon K)\Pr_{S^{\prime},r}[\mathcal{A}(S^{\prime})\in\mathcal{F}]+\eta.

5.1
Lower Bound for DP Identification

Definition 5.3

(IPP Condition)
.

A collection
\mathcal{C}
satisfies the
Intersecting Private-Pair
(IPP) condition if there exist two languages
L,L^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
with
L\cap L^{\prime}\neq\varnothing
, such that both
L
and
L^{\prime}
each contain at least one element not belonging to any other language in
\mathcal{C}
. We call such element a private element to that language.

Theorem 5.4

(Lower Bound for DP Identification)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
satisfy the IPP condition.
For any
\varepsilon
-DP identification algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, there exists a distribution
\mathcal{D}
such that
\iderr(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D},\mathcal{C},n)\gtrsim\exp(-\min\{1,\varepsilon\}\cdot n)
along infinitely many
n
.

Proof sketch.

For
\varepsilon<1
, let
s_{0}\in L\cap L^{\prime}
,
s_{1}
private to
L
,
s_{2}
private to
L^{\prime}
.
Define
\mathcal{D}
(resp.
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
) placing mass
3/4
on
s_{0}
and
1/4
on
s_{1}
(resp.
s_{2}
).
Coupling coordinate-wise, the Hamming distance satisfies
\Pr[H>n/2]\leq\exp(-n/12)
.

Section

5
with
K=n/2
gives
\max\{\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S)\neq L],\,\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S^{\prime})\neq L^{\prime}]\}\gtrsim\exp(-\varepsilon n/2)
; each misidentification costs excess risk
\geq 1/4
.
For
\varepsilon\geq 1
, the non-private lower bound of
33
gives
\iderr\gtrsim\exp(-n)
.
Combining yields the
\min\{1,\varepsilon\}
. The full proof is in
Appendix

F
.
∎

5.2
Lower Bound for DP Generation

Definition 5.5

(IIDP Condition)
.

A collection
\mathcal{C}
satisfies the
Intersecting Infinite-Difference Pair
(IIDP) condition if there exist two distinct languages
L,L^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
, an element
s_{0}\in L\cap L^{\prime}
, and two infinite sequences of distinct elements
(a_{k})_{k\geq 1}\subseteq L\setminus L^{\prime}
and
(b_{k})_{k\geq 1}\subseteq L^{\prime}\setminus L
.

The IIDP condition strengthens IPP by requiring
infinitely many
private elements on each side, which is necessary because the hard distributions for generation must have infinite support.

Theorem 5.6

(Lower Bound for DP Generation)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
satisfy the IIDP condition and the condition of Theorem 3.4 in
33
(i.e., there exist
L,L^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
with
|L\cap L^{\prime}|<\infty
and
\mathcal{U}\setminus(L\cup L^{\prime})\neq\varnothing
).
For any
\varepsilon
-DP generation algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, there exists a distribution
\mathcal{D}
such that
\generr(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D},\mathcal{C},n)\gtrsim\exp(-\min\{1,\varepsilon\}\cdot n)
along infinitely many
n
.

Proof sketch.

The argument is
asymmetric
, unlike the symmetric identification proof.
Let
(a_{k})\subseteq L\setminus L^{\prime}
,
(b_{k})\subseteq L^{\prime}\setminus L
witness IIDP with
s_{0}\in L\cap L^{\prime}
.
Define
\mathcal{D}
by
\Pr[x=s_{0}]=3/4
,
\Pr[x=a_{k}]=2^{-k}/4
, and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
analogously with
b_{k}
.
Let
F:=\{a_{k}:k\geq 1\}
.
The set
F
plays opposite roles:

•

Under
\mathcal{D}
:
s_{0}\in S
w.h.p., so success requires
\mathcal{A}(S)\in F
; hence
\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S)\in F]\geq 1-\alpha-4^{-n}
.

•

Under
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
:
F\cap\supp(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})=\varnothing
, so
\mathcal{A}(S^{\prime})\in F
implies failure; hence
\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S^{\prime})\in F]\leq\alpha^{\prime}
.

Section

5
gives
\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S)\in F]\leq e^{\varepsilon n/2}\cdot\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S^{\prime})\in F]+e^{-n/12}
.
Hence
1-\alpha-4^{-n}\leq e^{\varepsilon n/2}\alpha^{\prime}+e^{-n/12}
, which rearranges to
\max\{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\}\gtrsim\exp(-\varepsilon n/2)
.
Combining with the non-private bound of
33
for
\varepsilon\geq 1
gives the result. The full proof is in
Appendix

F
.
∎

Remark 5.7

(Concrete Instance Satisfying the Conditions of
Theorems

5.4
and
5.6
)
.

Let
\mathcal{U}=\mathbb{N}
,
L=\{1\}\cup\{3k:k\geq 1\}
, and
L^{\prime}=\{1\}\cup\{3k+1:k\geq 1\}
.
Then
L\cap L^{\prime}=\{1\}
is finite,

\mathcal{C}\supseteq\{L,L^{\prime}\}
satisfies all conditions needed for both lower bounds.

6
Conclusion and Future Work

We initiated the study of differentially private language identification
and generation in the agnostic statistical setting, establishing
algorithms and matching lower bounds that precisely quantify the cost of
privacy.
For both tasks, approximate
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP with constant

\varepsilon>0
recovers the non-private error rates of
33
,
while pure
\varepsilon
-DP degrades the convergence exponent by a
multiplicative factor of
\min\{1,\varepsilon\}
, which our lower bounds
confirm is tight.

Our results are information-theoretic: we characterize nearly optimal error rates but do not address computational efficiency, and our algorithms require explicit enumeration of languages and universe elements. Bridging these guarantees with the empirical DP-SGD pipeline for LLM training
[
1
;
46
;
57
;
58
]
remains an important challenge. Recent work on computational barriers for (non-private) language generation
[
9
]
suggests that efficient algorithms may need more structural assumptions.

Future directions include studying DP language learning in the
online setting of
30
and
38
, where adversarial
samples and infinite-horizon composition require fundamentally different
privacy analyses, and exploring user-level privacy
[
48
;
43
;
29
]
, where the protected
unit is an entire sequence of strings from a single user. Finally, extending our results to richer generation objectives that
incorporate safety constraints
[
5
]
or representativeness
requirements
[
55
]
while maintaining differential privacy is a natural next step.

B.1
Concentration Inequalities

We state some standard concentration inequalities and tail bounds;
see, e.g.,
14
;
66
;
67
for comprehensive references.

Lemma B.1

(Hoeffding’s Inequality)
.

Let
X=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
be the average of
n
independent random variables taking values in
[a,b]
, and let
\mu=E[X]
. For any
t\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\Pr[|X-\mu|\geq t]\leq 2\exp\Bigl(-\frac{2n^{2}t^{2}}{(b-a)^{2}}\Bigr).

Lemma B.2

(Chernoff Bounds)
.

Let
X=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
be the average of
n
independent random variables taking values in
[0,1]
, and let
\mu=E[X]
. For any
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle P(X\leq(1-t)\mu)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{n\mu t^{2}}{2}\right).

For any
t\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle P(X\geq(1+t)\mu)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{n\mu t^{2}}{3}\right).

Lemma B.3

(Gaussian Tail Bounds)
.

If
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
, then for every
t\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\Pr[Z\geq t]\leq\exp\Bigl(-\frac{t^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\Bigr)~~~\mathrm{and}~~~\Pr[|Z|\geq t]\leq 2\exp\Bigl(-\frac{t^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\Bigr).

B.2
Tools from Differential Privacy

Lemma B.4

(Group Privacy
26
)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{U}^{n}\to\mathcal{R}
be
\varepsilon
-differentially private.
If
S,S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}^{n}
differ in at most
k
entries, then for every measurable
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
, we have

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S)\in\mathcal{F}]\leq\exp(\varepsilon k)\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S^{\prime})\in\mathcal{F}],

where the probability is taken over the randomness of
\mathcal{A}
.

Lemma B.5

(Coupling Lemma via Group Privacy, a Variant of Lemma 19 in
2
)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{U}^{n}\to\mathcal{R}
be
\varepsilon
-differentially private.
Let
(S,S^{\prime})
be a coupling of two distributions over
\mathcal{U}^{n}
such that
\Pr[d_{\mathrm{Ham}}(S,S^{\prime})>K]\leq\eta
for some
K\geq 0
and
\eta\in[0,1]
, where
d_{\mathrm{Ham}}
is Hamming distance. Then for every measurable set
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
, we have

\displaystyle\Pr_{S,r}[\mathcal{A}(S)\in\mathcal{F}]\leq\exp(\varepsilon K)\Pr_{S^{\prime},r}[\mathcal{A}(S^{\prime})\in\mathcal{F}]+\eta.

Proof.

Let
G:=\{d_{\mathrm{Ham}}(S,S^{\prime})\leq K\}
so that
\Pr[G^{c}]\leq\eta
. Then

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S)\in\mathcal{F}]\leq\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S)\in\mathcal{F}~|~G]+\Pr[G^{c}].

Conditioning on
G
, we have
d_{\mathrm{Ham}}(S,S^{\prime})\leq K
. Hence Lemma
B.2
gives, pointwise,

\displaystyle\Pr_{r}[\mathcal{A}(S)\in\mathcal{F}\mid S,S]\leq e^{\varepsilon K}\Pr_{r}[\mathcal{A}(S^{\prime})\in\mathcal{F}\mid S,S^{\prime}].

Taking expectation over
(S,S^{\prime})
yields

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S)\in\mathcal{F}~|~G]\leq e^{\varepsilon K}\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S^{\prime})\in\mathcal{F}].

Combining with
\Pr[G^{c}]\leq\eta
completes the proof.
∎

The utility analysis proceeds in three steps:

Lemma D.12

(Noise Concentration)
.

Define the event

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}:=\Bigl\{|Z_{i}|\leq\frac{1}{4f(n)},\quad\forall i\in[f(n)]\Bigr\}.

Then

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{F}^{c}]\leq 2f(n)\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}n^{2}}{64f(n)^{3}\log(1.25/\delta)}\Bigr).

Proof.

Each

A union bound over

yields

∎

Lemma D.13

(Deterministic Correctness on

Since

Remark D.15

(Comparison with Pure DP and Interpretation)
.

The approximate DP analysis is structurally simpler: once both concentration events hold, correctness is deterministic (no exponential mechanism). The cost is that the noise event
\mathcal{F}
depends on
\varepsilon
,
\delta
, and

jointly.
The bound (
D.10
) decomposes into a statistical term

The optimal tradeoff is analyzed in Corollary

.

Corollary D.16

(Explicit Rates for Approximate DP)
.

Substituting specific horizons into (

):

•

f(n)=n^{\alpha}
for

•

Definition E.1

(Witness Index

Given a collection

We work under the following two assumptions throughout this section.

Assumption E.2

(Finite witness index)
.

The witness index is finite, i.e.,

Assumption E.3

(Support contains a reference language)

There exists an index

(Public Witness Bound)

There is a public integer

.

E.1.1

For each language

and the witness window

\displaystyle I_{i}(W):=\{k\in[W]:u_{k}\in L_{i}\}

Define the

\displaystyle a_{i}^{W}(S):=\begin{cases}\min_{k\in I_{i}(W)}N_{S}(u_{k}),&\text{if }I_{i}(W)\neq\emptyset,\\
n,&\text{if }I_{i}(W)=\emptyset,\end{cases}

Lemma E.5

(Sensitivity of

Lemma E.8

(Coverage of the Good Prefix)
.

Define the event

\mathcal{E}_{W}:=\bigl\{N_{S}(u_{k})\geq g(n)\quad\forall\,k\in I^{\star}_{W}\bigr\}.

E.2
Proof of Theorem
4.6
(Pure DP Generation without a Public Witness Bound)

We now remove the assumption that a public upper bound on the witness index is available.
The main challenge is that the witness window
i(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})
is unknown and depends on the private data through
\mathcal{D}
.
Our approach is to jointly search over both the language index
i
and a candidate witness threshold
t
, using a score that rewards large thresholds (indicating progress past the witness window) while penalizing languages that fail the witness test at threshold
t
.

E.2.1
Setup and Notation

Let
f,g,h:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
be functions satisfying
f(n)\to\infty
,
g(n)\to\infty
, and
h(n)\to\infty
.
The parameter
f(n)
controls the
language horizon
,
g(n)
is the
count threshold
(as in the public-bound setting), and
h(n)
is the
threshold horizon
that upper bounds the witness index for large enough
n
.

For each language
L_{i}
and candidate threshold
t\in[h(n)]
, define

\displaystyle I_{i}(t):=\{k\in[t]:u_{k}\in L_{i}\}.

(E.7)

For each
S\in\mathcal{U}^{n}
and each pair
(i,t)\in[f(n)]\times[h(n)]
, we define the
minimum prefix count

\displaystyle a_{i,t}(S):=\begin{cases}\min_{k\in I_{i}(t)}N_{S}(u_{k}),&\text{if }I_{i}(t)\neq\emptyset,\\
n,&\text{if }I_{i}(t)=\emptyset,\end{cases}

(E.8)

the deficit

\displaystyle d_{i,t}(S)

\displaystyle:=\bigl(g(n)-a_{i,t}(S)\bigr)_{+},

(E.9)

and the score

\displaystyle q_{\mathrm{pair}}(S,(i,t))

\displaystyle:=t-\frac{h(n)}{g(n)}\,d_{i,t}(S).

(E.10)

The term
t
rewards larger thresholds, while the penalty
\frac{h(n)}{g(n)}\,d_{i,t}(S)
suppresses pairs where the language fails the witness test at threshold
t
.
The coefficient
h(n)/g(n)
is chosen so that a bad language with full deficit
g(n)
incurs a penalty of exactly
h(n)
, ensuring its score is at most
t-h(n)\leq 0
.

We also define quantities associated with the reference language
L_{i^{\star}}
at the threshold horizon:

\displaystyle I^{\star}_{h}:=I_{i^{\star}}(h(n))=\{k\in[h(n)]:u_{k}\in L_{i^{\star}}\},\qquad p^{\star}_{h}:=\min_{k\in I^{\star}_{h}}\Pr_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}[x=u_{k}].

(E.11)

Since
L_{i^{\star}}\subseteq\supp(\mathcal{D})
, every string
u_{k}
with
k\in I^{\star}_{h}
has positive probability under
\mathcal{D}
, so
p^{\star}_{h}>0
.
Note that
p^{\star}_{h}
depends on
h(n)
and may decrease as
h(n)
grows, since the minimum is taken over a larger set.
This creates a tension: larger
h(n)
provides a wider search range but requires a smaller
g(n)
for coverage, which in turn weakens the score gap exploited by the exponential mechanism.

Privacy Analysis

Lemma E.14

(Sensitivity of the pair score)
.

The score function
q_{\mathrm{pair}}
defined in (
E.10
) has sensitivity
h(n)/g(n)
.

Proof.

Fix
(i,t)\in[f(n)]\times[h(n)]
and let
S,S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}^{n}
be neighboring datasets.
By the same argument as in Lemma
E.1.2
, the minimum prefix count satisfies
|a_{i,t}(S)-a_{i,t}(S^{\prime})|\leq 1
(since each multiplicity changes by at most
1
, and the minimum preserves Lipschitz constants; or
a_{i,t}(S)=a_{i,t}(S^{\prime})=n
when
I_{i}(t)=\emptyset
).
Since
x\mapsto(g(n)-x)_{+}
is
1
-Lipschitz,
|d_{i,t}(S)-d_{i,t}(S^{\prime})|\leq 1
.
Therefore

|q_{\mathrm{pair}}(S,(i,t))-q_{\mathrm{pair}}(S^{\prime},(i,t))|=\frac{h(n)}{g(n)}\,|d_{i,t}(S)-d_{i,t}(S^{\prime})|\leq\frac{h(n)}{g(n)}.

∎

Remark E.15

(Sensitivity and Ccore gap without a Public Bound)
.

The pair score has sensitivity
h(n)/g(n)
, lying between the constant sensitivity
1
of the public-bound case (Lemma
E.1.2
) and the
2f(n)^{2}/n
of identification (Lemma
D.1.1
).
The increase from
1
to
h(n)/g(n)
is the cost of not knowing the witness bound.
Meanwhile, the score gap grows to
h(n)-i(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})+1=\Omega(h(n))
, so the effective ratio (gap/sensitivity) is approximately
g(n)(1-i(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})/h(n))\approx g(n)
for large

, comparable to the public-bound case.

Lemma E.16

(Privacy)
.

Algorithm
6
is
\varepsilon
-differentially private.

Proof.

The pair selection on line 6 applies the exponential mechanism with score

over the finite range

By Lemma

,

has sensitivity

, so this step is
\varepsilon
-DP.
The output step (lines 7–8) is a deterministic function of the private output
(\widehat{i},\widehat{t})
and public randomness

, and is therefore
\varepsilon
-DP by post-processing (Lemma
2.2
).
∎

E.2.3
Utility Analysis

The utility analysis proceeds in three steps, paralleling the public-bound case:
(i) a coverage event
\mathcal{E}_{h}
under which every relevant string of
L_{i^{\star}}
up to index
h(n)
appears at least
g(n)
times (Lemma
E.2.3
);
(ii) on
\mathcal{E}_{h}
, the good pair
(i^{\star},h(n))
achieves the maximum score while every bad pair has a much lower score (Lemma
E.2.3
);
(iii) the exponential mechanism exploits this gap to select a good language (Lemma
E.2.3
).

Lemma E.17

(Coverage of the Good Prefix)
.

Define the event

\mathcal{E}_{h}:=\bigl\{N_{S}(u_{k})\geq g(n)\quad\forall\,k\in I^{\star}_{h}\bigr\}.

If
g(n)\leq np^{\star}_{h}/2
for all large enough
n
, then

\Pr_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}[\mathcal{E}_{h}^{c}]\leq|I^{\star}_{h}|\exp\Bigl(-\frac{np^{\star}_{h}}{8}\Bigr).

Proof.

The proof is identical to that of Lemma
E.1.3
, with
W
replaced by
h(n)
and
p^{\star}_{W}
replaced by
p^{\star}_{h}
.
∎

Lemma E.18

(Score Separation on
\mathcal{E}_{h}
)
.

Suppose
h(n)\geq i(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})
.
On the event
\mathcal{E}_{h}
:

(a)

q_{\mathrm{pair}}(S,(i^{\star},h(n)))=h(n)
.

(b)

For every
(i,t)\in[f(n)]\times[h(n)]
with
L_{i}\not\subseteq\supp(\mathcal{D})
:
q_{\mathrm{pair}}(S,(i,t))\leq i(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})-1
.

Consequently,
\OPT_{q_{\mathrm{pair}}}(S)=h(n)
and every bad pair has a score gap of at least
h(n)-i(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})+1
.

Proof.

Assume
\mathcal{E}_{h}
holds throughout.

Part (a): The good pair achieves score
h(n)
.

On
\mathcal{E}_{h}
, every
k\in I^{\star}_{h}
satisfies
N_{S}(u_{k})\geq g(n)
.
If
I_{i^{\star}}(h(n))\neq\emptyset
, then
a_{i^{\star},h(n)}(S)=\min_{k\in I_{i^{\star}}(h(n))}N_{S}(u_{k})\geq g(n)
, so
d_{i^{\star},h(n)}(S)=0
.
If
I_{i^{\star}}(h(n))=\emptyset
, then
a_{i^{\star},h(n)}(S)=n\geq g(n)
, so
d_{i^{\star},h(n)}(S)=0
as well.
In either case,

q_{\mathrm{pair}}(S,(i^{\star},h(n)))=h(n)-\frac{h(n)}{g(n)}\cdot 0=h(n).

Since
q_{\mathrm{pair}}(S,(i,t))\leq t\leq h(n)
for all pairs (as the deficit is nonneg.), we have
\OPT_{q_{\mathrm{pair}}}(S)=h(n)
.

Part (b): Every bad pair has low score.

Fix
i\in[f(n)]
with
L_{i}\not\subseteq\supp(\mathcal{D})
and any
t\in[h(n)]
.
We consider two cases depending on whether
t
is large enough to contain a witness.

Case
t\geq i(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})
:

By Definition
E
, there exists
k\leq i(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})\leq t
such that
u_{k}\in L_{i}\setminus\supp(\mathcal{D})
.
In particular,
k\in I_{i}(t)
and
u_{k}
never appears in any sample from
\mathcal{D}
, so
N_{S}(u_{k})=0
.
Therefore
a_{i,t}(S)\leq N_{S}(u_{k})=0
, giving
d_{i,t}(S)=g(n)
and

q_{\mathrm{pair}}(S,(i,t))=t-\frac{h(n)}{g(n)}\cdot g(n)=t-h(n)\leq 0.

Case
t<i(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})
:

Regardless of the deficit, the score satisfies

q_{\mathrm{pair}}(S,(i,t))\leq t\leq i(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})-1.

Combining both cases, every bad pair has score at most
\max\{0,\,i(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})-1\}=i(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})-1
(since
i(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})\geq 1
).
The score gap between the good pair and any bad pair is at least
h(n)-(i(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})-1)=h(n)-i(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})+1
.
∎

Lemma E.19

(Private Selection and Output)
.

Suppose
h(n)\geq 2\,i(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})
(so the score gap is at least
h(n)/2
).
Define

\beta_{\mathrm{sel}}:=f(n)\,h(n)\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\varepsilon\,g(n)}{8}\Bigr),\qquad\beta_{\mathrm{out}}:=\exp\Bigl(-\frac{n}{2}\Bigr).

If
\beta_{\mathrm{sel}}<1
, then on the event
\mathcal{E}_{h}
:

(a)

The exponential mechanism selects a good language (i.e.,
L_{\widehat{i}}\subseteq\supp(\mathcal{D})
) with probability at least
1-\beta_{\mathrm{sel}}
.

(b)

Conditioned on
L_{\widehat{i}}\subseteq\supp(\mathcal{D})
, the output
\widehat{x}
satisfies
\widehat{x}\in\supp(\mathcal{D})\setminus S
with probability at least
1-\beta_{\mathrm{out}}
.

Proof.

Part (a): Pair selection.

On
\mathcal{E}_{h}
, Lemma
E.2.3
gives
\OPT_{q_{\mathrm{pair}}}(S)=h(n)
and every bad pair
(i,t)
(i.e., with
L_{i}\not\subseteq\supp(\mathcal{D})
) satisfies
q_{\mathrm{pair}}(S,(i,t))\leq i(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})-1\leq h(n)/2-1<h(n)/2
.
By the utility guarantee of the exponential mechanism (Lemma
2.2
) with sensitivity
\Delta=h(n)/g(n)
(Lemma
E.2.2
) and range
|\mathcal{R}|=f(n)\cdot h(n)
, with probability at least
1-\beta_{\mathrm{sel}}
the output
(\widehat{i},\widehat{t})
satisfies

q_{\mathrm{pair}}(S,(\widehat{i},\widehat{t}))\geq h(n)-\frac{2h(n)}{g(n)\varepsilon}\ln\frac{f(n)\,h(n)}{\beta_{\mathrm{sel}}}.

Substituting
\beta_{\mathrm{sel}}=f(n)\,h(n)\exp(-\varepsilon\,g(n)/8)
:

\ln\frac{f(n)\,h(n)}{\beta_{\mathrm{sel}}}=\frac{\varepsilon\,g(n)}{8},

and therefore

\frac{2h(n)}{g(n)\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon\,g(n)}{8}=\frac{h(n)}{4}.

This gives
q_{\mathrm{pair}}(S,(\widehat{i},\widehat{t}))\geq h(n)-h(n)/4=3h(n)/4
.
Since every bad pair has score at most
h(n)/2<3h(n)/4
, the selected pair

must satisfy
L_{\widehat{i}}\subseteq\supp(\mathcal{D})
.

Part (b): Output novelty.

Suppose

.
The algorithm outputs the
\widehat{j}
-th smallest-indexed string from
T_{\widehat{i},\widehat{t}}:=L_{\widehat{i}}\cap\{u_{\widehat{t}+1},u_{\widehat{t}+2},\ldots\}
, where
\widehat{j}\sim\mathrm{Unif}([2^{n}])
.
Since

is infinite,
|T_{\widehat{i},\widehat{t}}|=\infty\geq 2^{n}
.
Since
L_{\widehat{i}}\subseteq\supp(\mathcal{D})
, every string in
T_{\widehat{i},\widehat{t}}
belongs to
\supp(\mathcal{D})
.
At most
n
strings from

can appear in

, so

\Pr[\widehat{x}\in S\mid L_{\widehat{i}}\subseteq\supp(\mathcal{D})]\leq\frac{n}{2^{n}}\leq\exp\Bigl(-\frac{n}{2}\Bigr)

for

.
∎

Remark E.29

(Summary: Approximate DP Generation)
.

Approximate DP generation achieves the non-private rate
\exp(-\Omega(n))
for any constant
\varepsilon>0
and
\delta\geq\exp(-\mathrm{poly}(n))
, in both the public-bound and no-public-bound settings.
The privacy cost only appears when
\varepsilon=O(n^{-3/4}\log^{1/4}n)
(with typical parameter choices), which is qualitatively different from pure DP where it appears at
\varepsilon=O(1)
.
This contrasts with pure DP generation (
\exp(-\varepsilon n)
for
\varepsilon<1
), approximate DP identification (
\exp(-o(n))
only), and pure DP identification (
\exp(-\min\{1,\varepsilon\}\cdot o(n))
).
The fundamental reason is that generation scores have constant sensitivity, so Gaussian noise
\sigma\propto\sqrt{f(n)}/\varepsilon
is negligible relative to the score gap
g(n)\propto n
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}

Lemma F.4

Lemma F.5

(From Misidentification to Identification error)
.

Consider the hard instance in Definition

. For any identification algorithm
\mathcal{A}
(using randomness

\displaystyle\iderr(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D},\mathcal{C},n)\geq\frac{1}{4}\cdot\Pr_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{n},r}[\mathcal{A}(S)\neq L],\qquad\iderr(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D}^{\prime},\mathcal{C},n)\geq\frac{1}{4}\cdot\Pr_{S\sim(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})^{n},r}[\mathcal{A}(S)\neq L^{\prime}].

F.1.3
The DP-Specific Coupling Argument

The next lemma is the technical core of the lower bound.
The key idea is to construct a coupling under which the two input distributions
\mathcal{D}^{n}
and
(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})^{n}
have small Hamming distance with high probability, and then apply group privacy (via Lemma
B.2
) to constrain how differently any
\varepsilon
-DP algorithm can behave on them.
Intuitively, both distributions produce the shared element
s_{0}
with probability
3/4
at each coordinate, so the expected Hamming distance between coupled samples is only
n/4
; yet the algorithm must distinguish
L
from
L^{\prime}
, and differential privacy limits its ability to do so.

Lemma F.6

(DP Forces Misidentification)
.

Consider the hard instance in Definition
F.1.1
. Let
\mathcal{A}
be an
\varepsilon
-differentially private identification algorithm. Define

\displaystyle p:=\Pr_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{n},r}[\mathcal{A}(S)=L],\qquad q:=\Pr_{S^{\prime}\sim(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})^{n},r}[\mathcal{A}(S^{\prime})=L^{\prime}].

Then

\displaystyle\max\{1-p,\;1-q\}\geq\frac{1-\exp(-n/12)}{1+\exp(\varepsilon n/2)}\geq\frac{1}{30}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}\Bigr).

Proof.

Step 1: Coupling construction.

We define a coupling
(S_{1},S_{2})
of
\mathcal{D}^{n}
and
(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})^{n}
that maximizes the overlap between the two samples.
For each coordinate
t\in[n]
, sample
(X_{t},Y_{t})
independently with

\displaystyle\Pr[(X_{t},Y_{t})=(s_{0},s_{0})]=\frac{3}{4},\qquad\Pr[(X_{t},Y_{t})=(s_{1},s_{2})]=\frac{1}{4}.

Let
S_{1}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
and
S_{2}=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})
.
To verify that this is a valid coupling, observe that the marginal of
X_{t}
satisfies
\Pr[X_{t}=s_{0}]=3/4
and
\Pr[X_{t}=s_{1}]=1/4
, which matches
\mathcal{D}
; similarly, the marginal of
Y_{t}
satisfies
\Pr[Y_{t}=s_{0}]=3/4
and
\Pr[Y_{t}=s_{2}]=1/4
, matching
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
.
Hence
S_{1}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}
and
S_{2}\sim(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})^{n}
.

Step 2: Hamming distance concentration.

The Hamming distance between the coupled samples is

\displaystyle H:=d_{\mathrm{Ham}}(S_{1},S_{2})=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{X_{t}\neq Y_{t}\}.

Since
X_{t}=Y_{t}
if and only if
(X_{t},Y_{t})=(s_{0},s_{0})
(which occurs with probability
3/4
), and
X_{t}\neq Y_{t}
otherwise (with probability
1/4
), each indicator
\mathbf{1}\{X_{t}\neq Y_{t}\}
is an independent Bernoulli
(1/4)
random variable.
Therefore
\E[H]=n/4
.
By the Chernoff bound (Lemma
B.1
) with
t=1
,

\displaystyle\Pr[H>2\E[H]]=\Pr[H>n/2]\leq\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\E[H]}{3}\Bigr)=\exp\Bigl(-\frac{n}{12}\Bigr).

We set
K=n/2
and
\eta=\exp(-n/12)
, so that
\Pr[H>K]\leq\eta
.

Step 3: Applying the coupling lemma.

We now apply Lemma
B.2
to relate the behavior of
\mathcal{A}
under
\mathcal{D}^{n}
and
(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})^{n}
.
Taking
\mathcal{F}=\{L\}
(the event that the algorithm outputs
L
), Lemma
B.2
gives

\displaystyle p=\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S_{1})=L]\leq e^{\varepsilon K}\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S_{2})=L]+\eta=e^{\varepsilon n/2}\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S_{2})=L]+\exp(-n/12).

Now, since
\mathcal{A}(S_{2})
outputs some language in
\mathcal{C}
, we have
\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S_{2})=L]\leq 1-\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S_{2})=L^{\prime}]=1-q
.
Substituting:

\displaystyle p\leq e^{\varepsilon n/2}(1-q)+\exp(-n/12).

(F.1)

By the symmetric argument with
\mathcal{F}=\{L^{\prime}\}
, we obtain

\displaystyle q\leq e^{\varepsilon n/2}(1-p)+\exp(-n/12).

(F.2)

Step 4: Extracting the bound.

Let
s=\min\{p,q\}
; we aim to upper bound
s
.
If
s=p
, then
q\geq s
, so
1-q\leq 1-s
; substituting into (
F.1
) gives

s\leq e^{\varepsilon n/2}(1-s)+\exp(-n/12)
.
If
s=q
, then
p\geq s
, so
1-p\leq 1-s
; substituting into (
F.2
) gives the same inequality.
In either case,

\displaystyle s+s\cdot e^{\varepsilon n/2}\leq e^{\varepsilon n/2}+\exp(-n/12),

which rearranges to

\displaystyle s\leq\frac{\exp(\varepsilon n/2)+\exp(-n/12)}{1+\exp(\varepsilon n/2)}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\max\{1-p,\;1-q\}=1-s\geq 1-\frac{\exp(\varepsilon n/2)+\exp(-n/12)}{1+\exp(\varepsilon n/2)}=\frac{1-\exp(-n/12)}{1+\exp(\varepsilon n/2)}.

(F.3)

It remains to simplify (
F.3
).
For the numerator: since
n\geq 1
, we have
\exp(-n/12)\leq\exp(-1/12)
, and so

\displaystyle 1-\exp(-n/12)\geq 1-\exp(-1/12)>\frac{1}{15},

where the last inequality follows from the numerical bound
\exp(-1/12)<1-1/15=14/15
.
For the denominator:
1+\exp(\varepsilon n/2)\leq 2\exp(\varepsilon n/2)
.
Combining these two estimates:

\displaystyle\max\{1-p,\;1-q\}\geq\frac{1/15}{2\exp(\varepsilon n/2)}=\frac{1}{30}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}\Bigr).

∎

F.1.4
Putting Things Together

We first state the DP-specific lower bound, then combine it with the non-private lower bound of
33
.

Theorem F.7

(Lower Bound for DP Identification)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a collection of languages over
\mathcal{U}
satisfying the IPP condition. For any
\varepsilon
-differentially private identification algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, there exists a distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\star}
over
\mathcal{U}
such that

\displaystyle\iderr(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D}_{\star},\mathcal{C},n)\geq\frac{1}{120}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}\Bigr)

for infinitely many
n
.

Proof.

Fix an even integer
n\geq 2
.
Lemma
F.1.3
applied to the hard instance from Definition
F.1.1
gives

\displaystyle\max\Big\{\Pr_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{n},r}[\mathcal{A}(S)\neq L],\;\Pr_{S\sim(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})^{n},r}[\mathcal{A}(S)\neq L^{\prime}]\Big\}\geq\frac{1}{30}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}\Bigr).

Applying Lemma
F.1.2
to both distributions, each identification error is at least
1/4
times the corresponding misidentification probability:

\displaystyle\max\bigl\{\iderr(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D},\mathcal{C},n),\;\iderr(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D}^{\prime},\mathcal{C},n)\bigr\}\geq\frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{1}{30}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}\Bigr)=\frac{1}{120}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}\Bigr).

This holds for every even
n\geq 2
.
Since there are infinitely many even integers but only two candidate distributions
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
, the pigeonhole principle guarantees the existence of a fixed distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\star}\in\{\mathcal{D},\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\}
for which the bound holds along an infinite subsequence of even integers.
∎

Theorem
F.7
captures the cost of privacy: the
\exp(-\varepsilon n/2)
barrier is specific to
\varepsilon
-DP algorithms.
However, when
\varepsilon
is large (e.g.,
\varepsilon\geq 1
), this bound decays faster than
\exp(-n/2)
and becomes weaker than what is possible even without any privacy constraint.
To obtain a lower bound that is meaningful across all privacy regimes, we combine Theorem
F.7
with the information-theoretic lower bound in
33
, which applies to
all
algorithms regardless of whether they satisfy differential privacy.

Theorem F.8

(Lower Bound for Agnostic Language Identification, Theorem 2.2 in
33
)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be any collection of languages over a universe
\mathcal{U}
satisfying that there exist
L,L^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
with both
L\setminus(\bigcup_{\widetilde{L}\in\mathcal{C}\setminus\{L\}}\widetilde{L})\neq\emptyset
and
L^{\prime}\setminus(\bigcup_{\widetilde{L}\in\mathcal{C}\setminus\{L^{\prime}\}}\widetilde{L})\neq\emptyset.

Then, for any identification algorithm
\mathcal{A}
using randomness
r
, there exists a distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
\mathcal{U}
such that there exists
L^{\star}\in\mathcal{C}
with
\mathrm{err}_{\mathcal{D}}(L^{\star})=\inf_{\widetilde{L}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{err}_{\mathcal{D}}(\widetilde{L})
, and furthermore

\displaystyle\iderr(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D},\mathcal{C},n)\geq\exp(-5n)

for infinitely many
n
.

Theorem F.9

(Lower Bound for DP Identification, all Regimes)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a collection of languages over
\mathcal{U}
satisfying the IPP condition.
For any
\varepsilon
-differentially private identification algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, there exists a distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\star}
over
\mathcal{U}
such that

\displaystyle\iderr(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D}_{\star},\mathcal{C},n)\geq\exp\bigl(-5\min\{1,\varepsilon\}\cdot n)

for infinitely many
n
.

Proof.

We consider two regimes depending on the privacy parameter
\varepsilon
.

Case 1:
\varepsilon<1
.

By Theorem
F.7
, there exists a distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\star}
over
\mathcal{U}
such that

\displaystyle\iderr(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D}_{\star},\mathcal{C},n)\geq\frac{1}{120}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}\Bigr)

for infinitely many
n
.
Since
\varepsilon<1
, we have
\min\{1,\varepsilon\}=\varepsilon
.
Note that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{120}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}\Bigr)=\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}-\log 120\Bigr)\geq\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}-\frac{19\varepsilon n}{4}\Bigr)=\exp(-5\varepsilon n),

where the inequality is due to
\log 120\leq 19\varepsilon n/4
for sufficiently large
n
.

Case 2:
\varepsilon\geq 1
.

Every
\varepsilon
-DP algorithm is in particular a (possibly randomized) identification algorithm, so information-theoretic lower bounds apply without modification.
Note that the IPP condition (Definition
F.1.1
) implies the assumptions of Theorem
F.8
. Hence there exists a distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\star}
over
\mathcal{U}
such that

\displaystyle\iderr(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D}_{\star},\mathcal{C},n)\geq\exp(-5n)

for infinitely many
n
.
∎

Remark F.10

(Tightness)
.

Comparing Theorem
F.9
with the upper bound in Corollary
D.1.3
:
the upper bound achieves
\exp(-\min\{1,\varepsilon\}\cdot r(n))
for any
r(n)=o(n)
with
r(n)\to\infty
,
while the lower bound requires
\exp(-\min\{1,\varepsilon\}\cdot n)
.
The dependence on
\min\{1,\varepsilon\}
is therefore tight since privacy costs exactly a multiplicative factor of
\varepsilon
in the exponent when
\varepsilon<1
, and is free when
\varepsilon\geq 1
.
The remaining gap between
o(n)
and
O(n)
in the exponent is inherited from the non-private setting
[
33
]
, where closing it remains an open problem.

F.2
Proof of Theorem
5.6
(Lower Bound for DP Generation)

We establish a lower bound showing that the exponential dependence on
\min\{1,\varepsilon\}\cdot n
in our generation upper bounds is information-theoretically necessary.
The proof structure parallels the identification lower bound (Section
D.1
), but with two key differences:
(i) the hard distributions must have
infinite
supports (since generation requires producing novel strings from an infinite support), necessitating a stronger structural condition on the collection;
(ii) the argument is inherently asymmetric—under
\mathcal{D}
, the algorithm should output strings from
L\setminus L^{\prime}
, while under
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
, such outputs constitute failures—and we exploit this asymmetry through a one-sided application of the coupling lemma.

F.2.1
Hard Instance Construction

Definition F.11

(Intersecting Infinite-Difference Pair (IIDP))
.

A collection
\mathcal{C}
of languages over a countable universe
\mathcal{U}
satisfies the
Intersecting Infinite-Difference Pair (IIDP)
condition if there exist two distinct languages
L,L^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
, an element
s_{0}\in L\cap L^{\prime}
, and two infinite sequences of distinct elements
(a_{k})_{k\geq 1}\subseteq L\setminus L^{\prime}
and
(b_{k})_{k\geq 1}\subseteq L^{\prime}\setminus L
.

Remark F.12

(On the Structural Conditions)
.

The IIDP condition (Definition
F.2.1
) strengthens the IPP condition used for identification (Definition
F.1.1
) by requiring infinitely many private elements on each side, which is necessary because the generation lower bound needs hard distributions with infinite supports.
The combined lower bound also requires the condition of Theorem
F.20
(Theorem 3.4 in
33
); this is logically independent of IIDP, but both can be satisfied simultaneously, either by different pairs within
\mathcal{C}
, or by a single pair
(L,L^{\prime})
when
L\cap L^{\prime}
is finite (e.g., in regular or context-free languages).

Definition F.13

(Hard Instance for Generation)
.

Assume
\mathcal{C}
satisfies IIDP and witnessed by
(L,L^{\prime},s_{0},(a_{k})_{k\geq 1},(b_{k})_{k\geq 1})
.
Define distributions
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
over
\mathcal{U}
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}

\displaystyle:~\Pr_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}[x=s_{0}]=\tfrac{3}{4},\quad

\displaystyle\Pr_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}[x=a_{k}]=\tfrac{1}{4}\cdot 2^{-k}\quad(k\geq 1),

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\prime}

\displaystyle:~\Pr_{x\sim\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}[x=s_{0}]=\tfrac{3}{4},\quad

\displaystyle\Pr_{x\sim\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}[x=b_{k}]=\tfrac{1}{4}\cdot 2^{-k}\quad(k\geq 1).

All other strings have zero probability under both distributions.

F.2.2
Properties of the Hard Instance

Lemma F.14

(Support structure)
.

Consider the hard instance in Definition
F.2.1
. Then:

(a)

\supp(\mathcal{D})=\{s_{0}\}\cup\{a_{k}:k\geq 1\}\subseteq L
and
\supp(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})=\{s_{0}\}\cup\{b_{k}:k\geq 1\}\subseteq L^{\prime}
.

(b)

Both supports are infinite.

(c)

The sets
\mathcal{F}:=\{a_{k}:k\geq 1\}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}:=\{b_{k}:k\geq 1\}
satisfy
\mathcal{F}\cap\supp(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})=\emptyset
and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\cap\supp(\mathcal{D})=\emptyset
.

Proof.

Part (a):
By construction,
s_{0}\in L\cap L^{\prime}
and
a_{k}\in L\setminus L^{\prime}
for all
k\geq 1
, so
\supp(\mathcal{D})=\{s_{0}\}\cup\{a_{k}:k\geq 1\}\subseteq L
.
The argument for
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
is symmetric.

Part (b):
The sequences
(a_{k})_{k\geq 1}
and
(b_{k})_{k\geq 1}
are infinite by the IIDP condition, and each element receives positive probability.

Part (c):
Since
a_{k}\in L\setminus L^{\prime}
for all
k
, we have
a_{k}\notin L^{\prime}
, hence
a_{k}\notin\{s_{0}\}\cup\{b_{j}:j\geq 1\}=\supp(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})
.
Thus
\mathcal{F}\cap\supp(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})=\emptyset
. The argument for
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
is symmetric.
∎

Lemma F.15

(Disjointness of private elements and opposing support)
.

Consider the hard instance in Definition
F.2.1
.
Let
\mathcal{F}=\{a_{k}:k\geq 1\}
.
For any generation algorithm
\mathcal{A}
,

\displaystyle\{\mathcal{A}(S^{\prime})\in\mathcal{F}\}\subseteq\{\mathcal{A}(S^{\prime})\notin\supp(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})\setminus S^{\prime}\},

where
S^{\prime}\sim(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})^{n}
.
In other words, if
\mathcal{A}(S^{\prime})
outputs an element of
\mathcal{F}
, it necessarily fails under
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
.
Consequently,

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S^{\prime})\in\mathcal{F}]\leq\generr(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D}^{\prime},\mathcal{C},n).

Proof.

By Lemma
F.2.2
(c),
\mathcal{F}\cap\supp(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})=\emptyset
.
If
\mathcal{A}(S^{\prime})\in\mathcal{F}
, then
\mathcal{A}(S^{\prime})\notin\supp(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})
, which immediately implies
\mathcal{A}(S^{\prime})\notin\supp(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})\setminus S^{\prime}
.
Taking probabilities gives the claimed inequality.
∎

Lemma F.16

(Success under
\mathcal{D}
requires outputting from
\mathcal{F}
)
.

Consider the hard instance in Definition
F.2.1
.
Let
\mathcal{F}=\{a_{k}:k\geq 1\}
.
For any generation algorithm
\mathcal{A}
and
S\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}
,

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S)\in\mathcal{F}]\geq 1-\generr(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D},\mathcal{C},n)-4^{-n}.

Proof.

By Lemma
F.2.2
(a),
\supp(\mathcal{D})=\{s_{0}\}\cup\mathcal{F}
.
Hence the success event decomposes as

\displaystyle\{\mathcal{A}(S)\in\supp(\mathcal{D})\setminus S\}\subseteq\{\mathcal{A}(S)\in\mathcal{F}\}\cup\{\mathcal{A}(S)=s_{0},\;s_{0}\notin S\}\subseteq\{\mathcal{A}(S)\in\mathcal{F}\}\cup\{s_{0}\notin S\}.

Indeed, if
\mathcal{A}(S)=s_{0}
, this output is successful only if
s_{0}\notin S
.
Taking probabilities and rearranging:

\displaystyle 1-\generr(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D},\mathcal{C},n)

\displaystyle=\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S)\in\supp(\mathcal{D})\setminus S]\leq\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S)\in\mathcal{F}]+\Pr[s_{0}\notin S].

Since
\Pr_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}[x=s_{0}]=3/4
, the probability that
s_{0}
never appears in
n
i.i.d. draws is

\Pr[s_{0}\notin S]=(1-3/4)^{n}=4^{-n}
.
Rearranging gives the claim.
∎

F.2.3
The DP-Specific Coupling Argument

The next lemma is the technical core of the generation lower bound.
As in the identification case, we construct a coupling under which
\mathcal{D}^{n}
and
(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})^{n}
have small Hamming distance with high probability, and apply the coupling lemma (Lemma
B.2
).
However, the argument is
asymmetric
: rather than bounding misidentification probabilities in both directions, we combine a
lower bound
on
\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S)\in\mathcal{F}]
(from the success requirement under
\mathcal{D}
, Lemma
F.2.2
) with an
upper bound
on
\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S)\in\mathcal{F}]
(from DP and the failure implication under
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
, Lemma
F.2.2
).

Lemma F.17

(DP forces generation error)
.

Consider the hard instance in Definition
F.2.1
.
Let
\mathcal{A}
be an
\varepsilon
-differentially private generation algorithm.
Define

\displaystyle\alpha:=\max\big\{\generr(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D},\mathcal{C},n),\;\generr(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D}^{\prime},\mathcal{C},n)\big\}.

Then for every even
n\geq 2
,

\displaystyle\alpha\geq\frac{1-4^{-n}-e^{-n/12}}{1+e^{\varepsilon n/2}}.

Proof.

Let
S=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}
and
S^{\prime}=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})\sim(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})^{n}
, and let
\mathcal{F}=\{a_{k}:k\geq 1\}
.
We derive an upper bound and a lower bound on
\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S)\in\mathcal{F}]
and combine them.

Step 1: Coupling construction.

Define a coupling
(S_{1},S_{2})
of
\mathcal{D}^{n}
and
(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})^{n}
coordinate-wise: for each
t\in[n]
, independently sample
(X_{t},Y_{t})
as follows.
With probability
3/4
, set
(X_{t},Y_{t})=(s_{0},s_{0})
.
With probability
1/4
, draw
K_{t}\in\mathbb{N}
with
\Pr[K_{t}=k]=2^{-k}
and set
(X_{t},Y_{t})=(a_{K_{t}},b_{K_{t}})
.

To verify this is a valid coupling: the marginal of
X_{t}
satisfies
\Pr[X_{t}=s_{0}]=3/4
and
\Pr[X_{t}=a_{k}]=(1/4)\cdot 2^{-k}
for each
k\geq 1
, matching
\mathcal{D}
.
Similarly, the marginal of
Y_{t}
satisfies
\Pr[Y_{t}=s_{0}]=3/4
and
\Pr[Y_{t}=b_{k}]=(1/4)\cdot 2^{-k}
, matching
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
.

Step 2: Hamming distance concentration.

The Hamming distance
H:=d_{\mathrm{Ham}}(S_{1},S_{2})=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{X_{t}\neq Y_{t}\}
is a sum of
n
independent Bernoulli
(1/4)
random variables (since
X_{t}\neq Y_{t}
if and only if the second branch occurs), so
\E[H]=n/4
.
By the Chernoff bound (Lemma
B.1
) with
t=1
,

\displaystyle\Pr[H>n/2]=\Pr[H>2\E[H]]\leq\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\E[H]}{3}\Bigr)=\exp\Bigl(-\frac{n}{12}\Bigr).

Set
K=n/2
and
\eta=\exp(-n/12)
.

Step 3: Upper bound on
\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S)\in\mathcal{F}]
via DP.

By the coupling lemma (Lemma
B.2
) applied with the measurable set
\mathcal{F}
,

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S_{1})\in\mathcal{F}]\leq e^{\varepsilon K}\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S_{2})\in\mathcal{F}]+\eta=e^{\varepsilon n/2}\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S_{2})\in\mathcal{F}]+e^{-n/12}.

By Lemma
F.2.2
,
\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S_{2})\in\mathcal{F}]\leq\generr(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D}^{\prime},\mathcal{C},n)\leq\alpha
.
Substituting:

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S)\in\mathcal{F}]\leq e^{\varepsilon n/2}\alpha+e^{-n/12}.

(F.4)

Step 4: Lower bound on
\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S)\in\mathcal{F}]
via success.

By Lemma
F.2.2
and the definition of
\alpha
,

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S)\in\mathcal{F}]\geq 1-\generr(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D},\mathcal{C},n)-4^{-n}\geq 1-\alpha-4^{-n}.

(F.5)

Step 5: Combining the bounds.

Chaining (
F.5
) and (
F.4
):

\displaystyle 1-\alpha-4^{-n}\leq\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S)\in\mathcal{F}]\leq e^{\varepsilon n/2}\alpha+e^{-n/12}.

Rearranging:

\displaystyle 1-4^{-n}-e^{-n/12}\leq(1+e^{\varepsilon n/2})\,\alpha,

which gives

\displaystyle\alpha\geq\frac{1-4^{-n}-e^{-n/12}}{1+e^{\varepsilon n/2}}.

∎

Remark F.18

(Asymmetry with the Identification Lower Bound)
.

In the identification lower bound (Lemma
F.1.3
), the argument is symmetric: both
p
and
q
are bounded via the coupling lemma, yielding two inequalities that constrain
\min\{p,q\}
.
In the generation lower bound, the argument is inherently one-sided: the lower bound on
\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S)\in\mathcal{F}]
comes from the success requirement under
\mathcal{D}
(not from DP), while the upper bound comes from DP applied to the event
\mathcal{F}
combined with the failure implication under
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
.
This asymmetry reflects the fact that the generation objective depends on
\supp(\mathcal{D})
, creating a natural directionality between the two distributions.

F.2.4
Putting Things Together

Theorem F.19

(Lower Bound for DP Generation)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
satisfy the IIDP condition (Definition
F.2.1
).
For any
\varepsilon
-differentially private generation algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, there exists a distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\star}\in\{\mathcal{D},\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\}
(from Definition
F.2.1
) such that

\displaystyle\generr(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D}_{\star},\mathcal{C},n)\geq\frac{1}{4}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}\Bigr)

for infinitely many
n
.

Proof.

By Lemma
F.2.3
, for every even
n\geq 2
,

\displaystyle\max\big\{\generr(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D},\mathcal{C},n),\;\generr(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D}^{\prime},\mathcal{C},n)\big\}\geq\frac{1-4^{-n}-e^{-n/12}}{1+e^{\varepsilon n/2}}.

We simplify the right-hand side for large
n
.
For the numerator:
4^{-n}\leq 1/16
and
e^{-n/12}\leq e^{-1/6}<1/6
for all
n\geq 2
, so
1-4^{-n}-e^{-n/12}\geq 1-1/16-1/6>1/2
.
More precisely, as
n\to\infty
,
1-4^{-n}-e^{-n/12}\to 1
.
For the denominator:
1+e^{\varepsilon n/2}\leq 2e^{\varepsilon n/2}
.
Combining, for all sufficiently large even
n
:

\displaystyle\frac{1-4^{-n}-e^{-n/12}}{1+e^{\varepsilon n/2}}\geq\frac{1/2}{2e^{\varepsilon n/2}}=\frac{1}{4}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}\Bigr).

Since this holds for all sufficiently large even
n
, and there are only two candidate distributions
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
, the pigeonhole principle guarantees the existence of a fixed
\mathcal{D}_{\star}\in\{\mathcal{D},\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\}
for which the bound holds along an infinite subsequence of even
n
.
∎

As in the identification case, Theorem
F.19
captures the privacy-specific barrier
\exp(-\varepsilon n/2)
, which becomes vacuously weak when
\varepsilon
is large.
We now combine it with the non-private generation lower bound from
33
to obtain a bound that is meaningful across all privacy regimes.

Theorem F.20

(Lower Bound for Agnostic Language Generation, Theorem 3.4 in
33
)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be any collection over a universe
\mathcal{U}
such that there exist languages
L,L^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
with
|L\cap L^{\prime}|<\infty
and
\mathcal{U}\setminus(L\cup L^{\prime})\neq\emptyset
.
For any generation algorithm
\mathcal{A}
using randomness
r
, there exists a distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
\mathcal{U}
such that
\exists\,L\in\mathcal{C}
with
L\subseteq\supp(\mathcal{D})
, and furthermore

\displaystyle\generr(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D},\mathcal{C},n)\geq\frac{1}{4}\exp(-2n)

for infinitely many
n
.

Theorem F.21

(Combined Lower Bound for DP Generation)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a collection of languages over
\mathcal{U}
that satisfies both the IIDP condition (Definition
F.2.1
) and the condition of Theorem
F.20
(i.e., there exist
L,L^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
with
|L\cap L^{\prime}|<\infty
and
\mathcal{U}\setminus(L\cup L^{\prime})\neq\emptyset
).
For any
\varepsilon
-differentially private generation algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, there exists a distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\star}
over
\mathcal{U}
such that

\displaystyle\generr(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D}_{\star},\mathcal{C},n)\geq\frac{1}{4}\exp(-2\min\{1,\varepsilon\}\cdot n)

for infinitely many
n
.

Proof.

We consider two regimes depending on the privacy parameter
\varepsilon
.

Case 1:
\varepsilon<1
.

By Theorem
F.19
, there exists
\mathcal{D}_{\star}
such that

\generr(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D}_{\star},\mathcal{C},n)\geq\frac{1}{4}\exp(-\varepsilon n/2)

for infinitely many
n
.
Since
\min\{1,\varepsilon\}=\varepsilon
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}\Bigr)=\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}-\log 4\Bigr)\geq\exp(-2\varepsilon n)

where the last inequality is due to that
\log 4\leq 3\varepsilon n/2
holds for sufficiently large
n
.

Case 2:
\varepsilon\geq 1
.

Every
\varepsilon
-DP algorithm is in particular a randomized algorithm.
By Theorem
F.20
, there exists
\mathcal{D}_{\star}
such that

\generr(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D}_{\star},\mathcal{C},n)\geq\frac{1}{4}\exp(-2n)

for infinitely many
n
.
Since
\min\{1,\varepsilon\}=1
, the bound clearly holds.
∎
