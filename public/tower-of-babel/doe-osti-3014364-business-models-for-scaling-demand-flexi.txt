Executive Summary
Load growth at the grid edge is driving increased attention to the distribution system and its
ability to enable customer technology adoption in an affordable and timely manner. Key
industry stakeholders, including electric utilities and regulators, can benefit from strategies to
manage and balance customer needs with infrastructure investments, such as demand
flexibility.

This report focuses on demand flexibility—the ability to reduce, shift, shed, generate, or
modulate loads in response to building and grid needs—to reduce the need for costly grid
upgrades by deferring investment needs and increase system reliability by shifting electricity
usage during periods of high risk. Specifically, we focus on the emerging characteristics of
business models for demand flexibility as a framework to understand how demand flexibility
programs generate value.

In this report, we focus on demand flexibility value propositions, which provide information on
value creation and describe how programs deliver clear benefits that address customer and
grid needs. This report discusses the role of value propositions in demand flexibility programs,
provides an overview of value propositions for a range of demand flexibility stakeholders,
identifies existing challenges to establishing an effective value proposition, and describes
lessons learned.

Value propositions in demand flexibility business models

Effective value propositions communicate clear and compelling benefits to customers and key
program stakeholders, including utilities, aggregators, and third-party program implementers.
Establishing a clear value proposition requires program implementers to understand the
relationship between demand flexibility potential, customer needs, and program design and
delivery strategies.

Value propositions for demand flexibility stakeholders

Demand flexibility can deliver value to a wide range of stakeholders. Figure ES-1 provides an
overview of the value propositions for utilities, building occupants, building developers, building
operators and property managers, and third-party program implementers. The range of
stakeholders that can benefit from demand flexibility emphasizes the potential for multiple
scaling pathways to advance demand flexibility programs. For instance, utilities can implement
demand flexibility programs to reduce, defer, or mitigate the need for investments. Additionally,
building developers can equip properties with demand flexibility enabling technology, and
contribute to the growth of new construction-focused programs.

Value proposition challenges

Challenges to establish effective value propositions include:
 • Understanding how program technologies affect the value proposition and resulting
 value streams for customers
 • Ensuring value propositions are effective at scale
 • Aligning program design with existing rate structures
 • Coordinating value streams for different stakeholders involved in a program

Value proposition lessons learned

Lessons learned to support program implementation and scaling include:
 • Designing demand flexibility programs that align program benefits with customer values
 • Leveraging data on local grid constraints to inform program design and scaling efforts
 • Developing customer engagement tools that consider customer interests
 • Standardizing program delivery activities to improve cost-effectiveness

1. Introduction
Significant load growth is being driven by technology adoption at the grid edge, including
electric vehicles and high-performance heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC), as
well as larger loads such as data centers supporting artificial intelligence, and the increasing
electricity needs of the industrial sector (Pereira et al., 2025). Recent North American Electric
Reliability Council (NERC) estimates, released in 2024, show load growth is expected to lead to
a 15% increase in summer peak demand and 18% winter peak demand, over the next 10 years
for the U.S. and Canada (NERC, 2024). The pace and scale of expected load growth are
leading to increased attention on the electricity sector infrastructure and investments needed to
ensure it can provide service to customers in a reliable and affordable manner. At the grid
edge, distribution systems play a key role as the interface between customer loads and the
grid. Investments in distribution systems have increased in recent years and currently account
for the largest share of costs for U.S. investor-owned utilities. For instance, data from the
Edison Electric Institute for 2024 indicate that distribution system capital expenditures account
for 32% of total capital expenditures (EEI, 2024). This further emphasizes the need for
strategies that can enable additional load while managing the impact on affordability, such as
managing flexible loads through demand flexibility (EFI Foundation, 2024; Goldenberg et al.,
2018).

Demand flexibility is the ability of building electricity loads to change in response to building and
grid needs and objectives. Demand flexibility can drive affordability improvements by shifting
electricity consumption out of high-price periods, as well as by reducing the need for costly
infrastructure upgrades. Affordability benefits for customers result from a more cost-effective
use of utility infrastructure, which reduces the need for additional investments, such as
increased capacity to meet higher peak loads. Utility benefits derive from the ability to allocate
resources more effectively by being able to reduce, defer, or fully mitigate the need for
infrastructure upgrades (Schwartz & Leventis, 2020). Demand flexibility can cost-effectively
support grid reliability by reducing electricity demand in periods of high risk (Fox-Penner et al.,
2025; Jackson et al., 2021; LBNL, 2025a; NERC, 2024; Norris et al., 2025; Pereira et al., 2025;
U.S. Department of Energy, 2024).

Building demand flexibility can be enabled by behind-the-meter distributed energy resources
(DERs) and results from responses to price-based signals (e.g., dedicated time-of-use rate
structures to incentivize customer behavior) or grid-based signals (e.g., grid operator requests
change load behavior to manage and mitigate operational challenges) aimed at delivering
benefits to the building occupants, owners, and the grid (Satchwell et al., 2021). DERs, in
combination with building controls, can deliver demand flexibility by reducing, shedding,
shifting, modulating, or generating load (See Table 1).

Recent studies have estimated the significant potential of demand flexibility to reduce system
costs and improve affordability (Murphy et al., 2024). For instance, the U.S. Department of
Energy estimated that demand flexibility could deliver system benefits worth $8-18 billion
annually by 2030 (Satchwell et al., 2021). Similarly, the Rocky Mountain Institute estimated that
demand flexibility from air conditioning and water heating could result in a ~8% decrease in
peak demand in the U.S. in the residential sector alone, valued at $13 billion per year (Bronski
et al., 2017). The Brattle Group estimated that cost-effective demand flexibility could reach
~20% of the U.S. peak load, resulting in $15 billion in annual benefits by 2030 (Hledik et al.,
2019).

In addition to the benefits and potential of demand flexibility, key industry stakeholders,
including state and utility decision-makers, are prioritizing demand flexibility. For example, a
recent survey by the Association of Energy Service Professionals found that 93% of the utilities
surveyed considered demand flexibility a medium-to-high priority for their business. Moreover,
49% of the utilities in the study indicated that they plan to implement a demand flexibility
program within the next three years (AESP, 2024). Similarly, an analysis by the Smart Electric

Power Alliance and the North Carolina Clean Energy Technology Center in 2024 found that 38
states took action to advance virtual power plants and support DERs, which can contribute to
demand flexibility (SEPA, 2025). These actions include utilities proposing new or expanding
existing programs (e.g., demand response programs, electric vehicle managed charging
programs, and multi-technology programs), and commissions and legislatures developing
frameworks and assessing the role of existing policies.

In this context, business models play an important role in ensuring that the significant demand
flexibility potential is realized (Li et al., 2022; Shen et al., 2021). Business models offer a
conceptual framework to understand how demand flexibility programs generate value to
customers, program implementers, and other key stakeholders and partners. Specifically,
business models provide insights into how program implementers (e.g., utilities, aggregators,
third-party program managers, etc.) create, deliver, and capture value (see Table 2). Value
creation and delivery encompass the decisions, resources, and processes program
implementers make to ensure that customers effectively benefit from program participation.
Value capture encompasses program implementer decisions related to implementation and
operation costs and program revenues. Collectively, these elements provide information
relevant to understanding how demand flexibility program implementers combine technologies
and capabilities in innovative offerings that generate value, including increased affordability
and reliability (Hamwi et al., 2021; Teece, 2010; Zott & Amit, 2010).

Program implementers have to design and implement business models that contribute to the
program’s vision and goals and deliver a clear value proposition. This can be challenging
considering the need to balance resource constraints, policy and regulatory priorities and
requirements, and the goals and capabilities of the network of stakeholders collaborating to
deliver a program.

This series of reports focuses on providing insights into business models from existing U.S.
demand flexibility programs. Our findings are structured as a series of challenges and lessons
learned from program implementer experiences. Particularly, our analysis provides information
to better understand value creation and value delivery through demand flexibility. Related to
value creation, we provide insights on value propositions and customer relationship
management. For value delivery, we provide insights related to the stakeholder ecosystem and
the program’s life cycle (See Table 3). This series of reports offers information for key demand
flexibility stakeholders, including public utility commissions, utilities, aggregators, third-party
program managers, equipment manufacturers, and others interested in supporting the scaling
of demand flexibility solutions.

2. The role of the value proposition in demand
 flexibility business models
The value proposition is a key element of demand flexibility programs as it translates the
capabilities and benefits of a program and its technologies into a set of clear and compelling
benefits that meet customer and stakeholder needs (Hamwi et al., 2021; O’Shaughnessy et al.,
2022; Schwartz et al., 2019). Value propositions can contribute to scaling by addressing known
or emerging market gaps and adding value to customers and program stakeholders, which can
contribute to accelerating adoption (Briggs et al., 2022; Bronski et al., 2017).

Demand flexibility programs can be designed to include multiple technologies and target
different customer segments. This adaptability is beneficial as it can contribute to tailoring
programs addressing a range of customer needs. However, it also increases the importance of
clearly defining the value a program can provide to its target customers. Clarity in the value
proposition can contribute to ensuring that customers understand the potential benefits a
demand flexibility program can provide (Schwartz et al., 2022). Additionally, a clear value
proposition can help customers understand if the benefits exceed the costs of participating in a
demand flexibility program (SEPA, 2022). A value proposition that is not well understood can
undermine program uptake and technology adoption. Additionally, it may lead to market
designs and policies that are not well-positioned for scaling (Le Dréau et al., 2023; Schwartz &
Leventis, 2020), or that take longer to implement necessary policies to remove barriers to
demand flexibility (Eckman et al., 2020; NEEP, 2020; Satchwell et al., 2021).

Establishing a clear value proposition requires program implementers to invest significant effort
and resources in understanding the relationship between their demand flexibility offerings and
customer needs. A set of value propositions is often associated with demand flexibility, such as
cost savings, reliability and grid flexibility, and distributed energy resource integration and
optimization (Guernsey et al., 2021).

 • Cost savings: Utilities may be able to defer or avoid infrastructure investments by
 managing peak load. Customers may be able to unlock bill savings through increased
 efficiency. Large commercial customers can reduce or avoid demand charges by
 shifting or curtailing load through demand flexibility.
 • Reliability and grid flexibility: Utilities may be able to increase service quality and
 reduce the risk of outages by managing emergency events, such as generation
 shortages and extreme weather conditions. Customers may also benefit by suffering
 fewer service interruptions.
 • Distributed energy resource integration and optimization: Utilities may benefit from
 an improved ability to integrate technologies connected at the grid edge, contributing to
 improved system operations. Customers may benefit from a more effective use of their
 behind-the-meter technologies, which may contribute to increased cost-effectiveness.

3. Value propositions for demand flexibility
 stakeholders
Through this analysis, we identified value propositions for a wide range of stakeholders who
could take the varying roles in a demand flexibility program, including utilities, building
occupants, building developers, building operators and property managers, and third-party
program implementers. This information can contribute to program design efforts by describing
the potential value streams that demand flexibility programs can provide and the stakeholders
who may be better positioned to capture that value. Figure 1 provides a summary of the various
value propositions identified. This section provides additional details for each stakeholder
perspective.

Figure 1. Demand flexibility value propositions

3.1 Value propositions for utilities
Improve customer affordability through efficient operations and cost-effective
investments. Utilities can implement demand flexibility programs to deliver grid services able
to address near- and longer-term grid needs cost-effectively, contributing to customer
affordability while ensuring system reliability. Distributed energy resources and control
technologies implemented with demand flexibility programs can reduce electricity generation
costs and provide ancillary services (e.g., frequency and voltage support). Additionally, demand
flexibility can allow utilities to reduce, defer, or, in some cases, fully mitigate the need for costly
infrastructure upgrades that would otherwise be needed to serve load growth and enable
customer choice at the grid edge (Eckman et al., 2020; Schwartz & Leventis, 2020).

              Business Models for Scaling Demand Flexibility – Volume I: Value Proposition | 7

Access knowledge on demand flexibility potential and future opportunities in their
service territory. Utilities can use this information to shape future distribution system
investment and implementation strategies, including determining the role of demand flexibility
programs in deferring or mitigating certain grid needs. Additionally, utilities may be able to
determine the role of demand flexibility in system operations, including its impact on system
reliability. This can also contribute to informing integrated resource planning and distribution
system planning efforts.

Utilities can benefit from quantifying the demand flexibility potential of specific technologies. For
instance, utilities may implement a program to understand the load-shifting potential for high-
performance water heaters to understand how that may differ from electric resistance water
heaters. Similarly, utilities can benefit from quantifying demand flexibility potential under
different weather conditions, such as during extreme weather days. Utilities may also focus on
understanding the impact of demand flexibility on the cost of service and rate design.

Expand existing business capabilities by implementing demand flexibility programs that
may require new skills, technologies, and processes compared to existing practices.
This may occur through expanding research and development capacity by implementing pilots,
through which the utility can test innovative demand flexibility approaches, such as
combinations of different technologies to manage peak load and unlock customer savings. This
may also support the development of new utility programs to provide added value to customers.

For example, one of the program implementers interviewed focused on the residential single-
family segment and leveraged the learnings from a demand flexibility pilot to inform the
development of a Home Energy Management System and community aggregator to support
utility system operations. Another program implementer mentioned that pursuing demand
flexibility programs had allowed them to develop novel software solutions to address its
infrastructure and customer needs, providing an alternative to third-party software providers.
One of the program implementers emphasized the strategic value of demand flexibility. In this
case, the utility is not focused on targeting constrained feeders but on identifying long-term
opportunities to offer innovative utility services. In another case, the program implementer
mentioned that the utility had experience with energy efficiency for new construction but not
with demand flexibility programs, and this was an opportunity to develop new capabilities.

Advance utility goals and state policy priorities, including affordability and reliability.
Demand flexibility programs can inform utility strategy, such as enabling the utility to
understand customer adoption of different technologies, including household appliances and
DERs, to drive affordability and enable reliability. For example, in Oregon, Portland Gas and
Electric (PGE) conducts a Flexible Load Multi-Year Planning process laying out its strategy and
goal to leverage the use of flexible resources for improved system operations (PGE, 2025). In
its most recent plan for 2025-2026, the utility forecasts 116.3 MW of flexible load capacity for
the summer of 2025 and 61.2 MW for the winter of 2025 (PGE, 2024). Demand flexibility
programs can also contribute to state policy priorities and public utility commission
requirements. For instance, demand flexibility programs can contribute to meeting commission

              Business Models for Scaling Demand Flexibility – Volume I: Value Proposition | 8

requirements to implement cost-effective non-wires alternatives to address grid needs identified
during planning (LBNL, 2025c).

Improve customer satisfaction and engagement. Customers may respond positively to
innovative demand flexibility programs that unlock new value streams from the utility to the
customer. This can also result in increased brand recognition.

Contribute to economic development goals. This may include understanding the jobs and
economic activity impacts of implementing demand flexibility at scale in the utility service
territory. For example, program implementers emphasized the value of utilities in being
recognized as good stewards in the communities they serve. Additionally, a program
implementer included a study of economic development impacts in the program strategy to
understand how demand flexibility investments may result in increased economic activity and
jobs.

3.2 Value propositions for building occupants (e.g., renters,
 homeowners, commercial building owners)
Increased comfort, efficiency, and reduced electricity costs. Customers enrolling in
demand flexibility programs may experience improved comfort and utility bill savings. For
example, demand flexibility may allow customers to reduce energy costs by using controls and
monitoring technology to shift consumption to off-peak hours, providing customers with more
control over their energy costs (Cabot & Villavicencio, 2024).

Increased ability to withstand power outages. Customers may benefit from increased ability
to withstand or recover from outages, such as those occurring in areas facing severe winter
storms, hurricanes, and wildfires. For example, demand flexibility programs including storage
(e.g., behind-the-meter storage for individual customer use, or front-of-the-meter storage
shared across customers in a specific location), or solar and storage. Depending on the
technologies implemented in a demand flexibility program, customers can see benefits at the
building level.

Access to modern home technologies and energy usage data from thermostats and
DERs. Demand flexibility technologies may enable customers to control home appliances, such
as water heaters and HVAC. Additionally, building occupants may benefit from demand
flexibility programs that result in a more user-friendly way of interacting with building
technologies and appliances. For instance, one of the interviewed programs included electric
vehicle car-sharing as part of its design, enabling demand flexibility while also providing a
transportation option to building residents.

Financial rewards for participating in flexibility events. Customers may enroll in a tariff as
part of participating in the program that provides a payment for their flexibility (e.g., $/kWh of
flexibility provided).

3.3 Value propositions for building developers
Access to modern technology. Demand flexibility technologies may increase the property
market value to prospective homebuyers. Building developers may also be interested in
demand flexibility technology as an additional benefit of their properties and to attract more
buyers.

Contribute to strategic goals. Participating in demand flexibility programs can contribute to
building developer goals, which can be related to operational effectiveness and innovation. For
example, a building developer may value being at the forefront of building energy efficiency,
and ensuring their properties are demand flexibility ready may increase their ability to deliver on
that value.

Access to additional marketing channels. Building developers may collaborate with the
program implementer, such as a utility, to advertise the properties and their technologies and
benefits, increasing the building developer outreach.

Unlock new business opportunities for developers. For instance, one of the program
implementers discussed the potential for building developers to act as aggregators in the
future.

3.4 Value propositions for building operators/property managers
Receive incentives for participating. Property managers may unlock a new revenue stream
by enrolling properties in demand flexibility programs that compensate participants for the
flexibility provided or for the devices enrolled. For instance, one of the program implementers
interviewed provided property managers with a $20 per water heater per year under a five-year
contract.

Increase building efficiency. This can result in direct savings for the property manager for
master-metered properties. Additionally, if the program’s technology package provides building
occupants with energy efficiency, DERs, or other demand flexibility enabling technologies, it
may also contribute to occupant savings and increase their satisfaction, which may improve the
landlord-tenant relationship.

Attract new tenants who value the benefits of demand flexibility technologies. Property
managers may be able to emphasize greater value to prospective tenants interested in a
building that enables more effective use of electricity and affordability. This may also result in
attracting tenants who may have an increased willingness to engage in demand flexibility
events, such as by shifting consumption to specific hours or responding to events, when those
are included in the program design.

Access to granular data on building performance. Building operators may be able to use
granular building data to develop more effective building operation strategies, which may
reduce operational costs, improve occupant comfort, and enable greater building flexibility (Liu

            Business Models for Scaling Demand Flexibility – Volume I: Value Proposition | 10

et al., 2022). For instance, a university campus-focused program described the value of
accessing granular building data to develop an effective strategy to provide grid services.

Contribute to strategic goals. Participating in demand flexibility programs can contribute to
building operator strategic business goals. For instance, a building operator may value
optimizing building utility costs, as well as contributing to occupant satisfaction through
increased comfort.

3.5 Value propositions to third-party program implementers1
Adapt and leverage existing programs with innovative demand flexibility offerings. Third-
party program implementers may be able to expand their program offerings and increase the
value they deliver to customers and utilities. For instance, one of the program implementers
mentioned they were enabling a third-party program manager to implement distributed energy
resources to better manage system peak load in a region of the country that historically
experienced summer peak events, but that is now expected also to experience significant
winter peaks.

Improve customer recruitment, engagement, and retention. Demand flexibility programs
providing a clear value proposition can result in increased customer interest, which may result
in greater levels of customer enrollment and increased satisfaction for participating customers.
This can contribute to overall program success and scaling efforts, such as by reducing
customer opt-outs of events and the number of customers that abandon the program.

3.6 Scaling pathways
The information on value propositions described above demonstrates the wide-ranging set of
stakeholders that can benefit from demand flexibility programs. This range of stakeholders and
value streams also emphasizes the possibilities for scaling demand flexibility through different
pathways. For instance, demand flexibility can scale through increased interest from utilities,
given the potential to reduce, defer, and mitigate investment needs for capacity upgrades. This
scaling pathway would complement the utility industry’s history of providing energy efficiency
and demand response programs and evolve into a context where more programs implement
multiple technologies to better address grid and customer needs (GridLab et al., 2024; SEPA,
2022; WPPI Energy, 2024). This scaling pathway can be seen as an incremental approach
through which utilities would adapt their existing programs and capabilities to implement
demand flexibility at scale. However, other scaling pathways are also possible. For instance,
building operators and property managers may become a driver for demand flexibility solutions.
Property managers interested in increasing building efficiency, accessing granular data, and
advancing business strategies may increase demand for technologies and drive demand
flexibility program growth. This scaling pathway is not centered on the utility and grid
operations, but can still contribute to providing valuable grid services while meeting property
managers' and building operators’ needs. Building developers can also offer a new pathway to
scale demand flexibility. Developers interested in equipping properties with demand flexibility
enabling technologies to provide greater benefits and increase value, can contribute to growing

4. Challenges
Through our analysis, we identified challenges for establishing an effective value proposition
related to program design, rates, and cost-effectiveness.

4.1 Designing programs for valuable demand flexibility offerings
The technologies included in demand flexibility programs affect the value proposition.
Different technology packages can unlock different value streams for customers (See Text Box
1) (York et al., 2019). For example, programs implementing multiple technologies may be more
attractive to some customers while requiring more thoughtful communication and education
efforts to ensure the potential value of associated with the technology package is delivered.
Demand flexibility technology packages and value propositions should be designed to meet
customer needs and program implementer capabilities, and other considerations such as
expected flexibility potential and cost-effectiveness.

Ensuring value propositions are effective at scale. Given the wide range of building
characteristics and stakeholder priorities, this can be challenging. For instance, the value of
demand flexibility for programs targeting university campuses or other large building complexes
can vary significantly across different campuses with different building characteristics, flexibility
potential, and campus priorities established by leadership.

Incentive schemes. Designing effective incentive schemes and understanding the effect of
different incentives on customer recruitment, engagement, and retention. Customer incentives
can take different forms. Programs can provide technology and value-added services at
reduced or no cost to incentivize customer enrollment. Technologies provided may include
HPWH, solar, storage, and thermostats. Added-value services can include energy audits.
Incentives can also include financial incentives. Programs can pay flat incentives, which can be
based on the number of devices enrolled in the program and paid on a set schedule, such as
every season the device is enrolled in the program. Programs can also provide a flat incentive
(e.g., a gift card) to encourage customer participation in program evaluation surveys.
Alternatively, programs can pay variable incentives based on performance, such as the amount
of flexibility provided

Stacking value streams effectively. Understanding how to structure demand flexibility
offerings to ensure the value exceeds the costs for the different stakeholders involved can be
challenging. For instance, program implementers indicated that for new construction programs,
it is often still unclear if prospective homebuyers perceive demand flexibility-ready homes as
more valuable. For example, a program implementer in the new construction segment surveyed
homebuyers to understand the key driver of their buying decision, with half of the customers
indicating that location was the primary driver and not the demand flexibility and energy
efficiency technologies.

 Different types of information can be considered by decision-makers determining the

 • Modelling and simulation: Quantitative analysis can provide decision-makers with
 expected performance metrics to understand the value of different sets of

 • Cost-effectiveness: Information on expected costs and benefits over the lifetime of
 the technologies being considered can contribute to technology package designs that

 important variable in technology package decisions. For example, residential

 residential customers can have a high cost (U.S. DOE, 2023).
 • Previous experience: Program implementers may have developed expertise and
 positive experience with a group of technologies, which may influence future
 technology package decisions.
 • Stakeholder alignment and buy-in: Program stakeholders may have interest and/or
 experience with specific technologies, which may shape the final set of technologies
 included in the technology package. Program stakeholders' experience navigating
 institutional barriers may also shape their technology decisions.
 • Pilot goals: Technology packages may be shaped by research and pilot goals, such
 as to learn how a set of technologies perform when offered under a demand flexibility
 program, or to investigate customer interest and engagement in programs offering
 different types of technologies.

 Additionally, the technology package will be shaped by the building type targeted. Program
 implementers may select different technologies for residential programs for single family or
 multi-family dwellings, as well as for commercial and campus buildings.

4.3 Economics and cost-effectiveness
Ensuring program benefits exceed costs. Implementing programs cost-effectively can still
be challenging, considering design, implementation, and operations costs (Le Dréau et al.,
2023). For example, the labor costs associated with sending technicians to address device
issues in buildings can significantly impact cost-effectiveness. Technology costs can also still
be significant and require confidence in the benefits and revenue to be generated in the future.
Labor and technology costs are shaped by the technology packages implemented within each
program and program implementers can consider cost implications as part of their decision-
making process.

4.4 Valuing demand flexibility
Aligning stakeholders on the value of demand flexibility. Often, different stakeholders will
value demand flexibility differently according to their internal processes and needs, which can
make it challenging to coordinate multiple value streams across different stakeholders in a
program (D’Ettorre et al., 2022; Eckman et al., 2020; Frick, 2023).

5. Lessons learned
The list below highlights lessons learned related to value propositions, informed by the
experiences of program implementers.

5.1 Designing programs for valuable demand flexibility offerings
Demand flexibility can provide multiple value streams to a range of stakeholders.
Program design decisions must strike a balance between potential trade-offs. For instance, one
of the program implementers mentioned the need to balance the ability of a program to deliver
grid services while maintaining or improving customer satisfaction.

Align program benefits with customer values. Customers are more likely to enroll and
remain engaged in demand flexibility programs when the program’s value proposition aligns
with their personal values. For example, customers may value economic impacts and respond
to value propositions that offer energy savings and affordability. Knowing the customer
segments in a program’s target location is valuable and can improve customer recruitment and
engagement outcomes. Program implementers may consider the Smart Energy Consumer
Collaborative (SECC) customer segments to support the development of effective value
propositions. Figure 2 provides information on SECC’s customer types based on research
considering attitudes and behaviors and their key characteristics from more favorable to less
favorable for technology adoption.

Reduce enrollment information burden. Developing a clear value proposition can require
significant efforts from the program implementer, supported by thorough analysis and
consideration of benefits and potential hurdles. After this thorough background work, program
implementers can benefit from avoiding information overload during initial outreach and
communication efforts to recruit participants. For example, program implementers can benefit
from developing simplified incentive information in marketing materials (York et al., 2019).

Understanding grid constraints can help design effective programs. Local grid
characteristics and needs, such as those identified in integrated distribution system plans (see
Text Box 2), impact the type of value propositions a program can deliver. For instance, a utility
with feeder constraints can geotarget its demand flexibility offerings to reduce, defer, or mitigate
future infrastructure investment needs (see Text Box 3).

Text Box 2. Integrated distribution system planning and demand flexibility
 Distribution planning practices are evolving to increase transparency and improve
 stakeholder engagement (Schwartz et al., 2024). A growing number of states (see Figure 3)
 have regulatory guidance in place for utilities to develop and file a distribution system plan to
 the public utility commissions. These planning requirements can contribute to establishing
 processes to identify grid needs and are the initial step to identify solutions, which may
 include demand flexibility programs. For example, Hawaii requires utilities to submit a plan
 that includes a process and timeline to identify grid needs and solutions, including non-wires
 alternatives (HI PUC, 2018). Similarly, California requires utilities to identify and report grid
 needs, which may be addressed by procuring DER-based solutions (Peterson, 2023; L.
 Schwartz et al., 2024).

Text Box 3. Geotargeted programs
 Geotargeted programs are designed to provide program participants with incentives for
 implementing and enrolling DERs to reduce load growth in specific locations of the
 distribution system to address grid needs (e.g., substation or feeder capacity constraints).
 Geotargeted programs can help reduce, defer, or mitigate the need for system upgrades
 (LBNL, 2025b).

 Demand flexibility can be effective in addressing locational grid needs. Program
 implementers may be able to adapt existing programs to address specific areas in the
 distribution system. Adapting existing programs can offer a faster pathway from grid need
 identification to solution implementation. Designing a new program may also be a suitable
 approach to effectively address grid needs by considering the specific load and asset
 characteristics in the target area to inform technology package decisions.

 Various utility programs have been implemented in the U.S. to defer system upgrades
 (Pereira, 2023; Schwartz et al., 2024). For example, New York’s Consolidate Edison
 operates the Brooklyn/Queens Demand Management Program aimed at deferring $1 billion
 in substation and feeder upgrades (NY DPS, 2014). The program includes a portfolio of
 technologies, such as energy efficiency, distributed generation, and distributed storage. By
 the fourth quarter of 2024, this program had delivered 62 MW of total peak load reduction,
 including 43 MW through customer-sided demand flexibility, at 9-10 pm (see Figure 4).

Develop tools for customer engagement. Program implementers can develop tools to enable
customers to engage with the program and understand their energy usage. For instance, one of
the program implementers interviewed mentioned that their program participants were
interested in knowing they’re part of a program that is delivering value to them and the grid.

Long-term value proposition effectiveness. Ensuring the value proposition can be sustained
over time to support retention and continued customer engagement and satisfaction. For
instance, if the primary value stream is related to the technology provided and initial rebates
provided during enrollment, participating customers may become less engaged over time. This
may reduce their willingness to continue participating or the extent to which they participate in

load flexibility events, necessary for program success. For instance, a program implementer
shared the example of a demand flexibility program where the primary value communicated to
customers was the rebate for a thermostat received during enrollment, which may result in
customers forgetting they are part of a program and that their participation can continue to be
beneficial.

5.2 Rates
Including rates in demand flexibility program design decisions. Having rate options
designed for different customer classes is important to meet class-specific needs and increase
the effectiveness of load flexibility programs. A program implementer shared that on their initial
design, they had included a single program rate for all participating customers, but that in the
future, they planned to develop class-specific rates to provide value to customers and reach
demand flexibility objectives more effectively.

5.3 Economics and cost-effectiveness
Standardizing program activities. Standardizing program delivery phases can contribute to
improved cost-effectiveness. Standardization can focus on use cases, contract templates,
RFPs, and testing protocols (U.S. DOE, 2023). A program implementer shared that fully
customized case-by-case approaches are challenging to implement at scale. Text Box 4
describes different demand flexibility control architectures that can guide program
standardization efforts related to DER coordination and building operations.

Text Box 4. Demand flexibility control architectures
 Demand flexibility programs can be delivered following different strategies for controlling and
 coordinating DERs and building operations, resulting in different control architectures.
 Berkeley Lab research has identified and characterized the control architectures emerging in
 the U.S. DOE Connected Communities program,3 which includes ten demand flexibility pilots
 in groups of buildings. Three architecture archetypes emerged through this analysis (Paul et
 al., 2024):
 • Device aggregation: focuses on control via device-specific aggregator platforms to
 provide grid services from many buildings by coordinating the operation of DERs and
 other demand flexibility enabling technologies.
 • Site optimization: focuses on optimization through centralized building energy
 management platforms (e.g., EMS systems), to target a range of behind-the-meter
 devices, including DERs, to provide grid services from a building or groups of
 buildings.
 • Distribution optimization: focuses on the use of a DERMS platform, which
 integrates with localized utility data and multiple buildings, coordinating behind-the-
 meter DERs and other technologies to provide distribution and wholesale grid
 services, such as to mitigate feeder capacity constraints.
