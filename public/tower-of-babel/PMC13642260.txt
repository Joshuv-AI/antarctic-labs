A global database of West Nile virus host prevalence and competence
Authors: Alex Richter-Boix, Júlia Froxán-Grabalosa, Nina Bogdanović, Catuxa Cerecedo-Iglesias, William Wint, Roya Olyzadeh, Tijmen Hartung, Giovanni Marini, Daniele Da Re, Marion P. G. Koopmans, Reina S. Sikkema, Frederic Bartumeus
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
West Nile virus (WNV) is one of the most widespread arboviruses globally and is maintained primarily through a bird-mosquito-bird transmission cycle, while other vertebrates play more limited roles. Host contributions to transmission depend on both infection evidence in natural populations (reflecting exposure and susceptibility) and reservoir competence, determined by the magnitude and duration of viraemia sufficient to infect mosquitoes. Despite extensive surveillance and experimental research, no comprehensive, standardised resource has integrated evidence on host exposure and infection in natural populations together with experimental data on host competence across vertebrate taxa. Here, we present two harmonised datasets compiled through a systematic literature review: (i) a WNV host prevalence dataset, summarising infection and serological evidence in wild and captive vertebrates; and (ii) a WNV host competence dataset, derived from controlled experimental infections. The prevalence dataset aggregates records from 541 studies across 91 countries (1950–2023), comprising 535,568 tested individuals from 1,801 vertebrate species. The WNV host competence dataset compiles 113 experimental infection studies covering 103 species and 3,030 individuals, and provides standardised time-resolved viraemia and survival data with accompanying metadata, enabling reconstruction and/or modelling of species-specific viraemia trajectories and the derivation of quantitative competence metrics. Both datasets use standardised taxonomy and incorporate synonym crosswalks to facilitate linkage with trait databases, phylogenetic trees and species distribution products. Together, these resources provide a unified foundation for macroecological analyses, surveillance gap assessment, and modelling multi-host WNV transmission dynamics.

Background & Summary

The West Nile virus (WNV, Flaviviridae: Flavivirus) exemplifies the complex epidemiological and ecological dynamics associated with emerging global vector-borne diseases in the face of increasing environmental and anthropological change1–3. Originally endemic to Africa4,5 WNV has, over recent decades, undergone a remarkable expansion in both prevalence and geographic range. It is now established in Europe, Asia, the Americas and Oceania6–10. In particular, the expansion of WNV in the United States followed an initial introduction likely originating in the Middle East11. Its sustained spread across the Americas and globally has been largely driven by environmental and climate-related changes affecting vector and host distributions12.

As a multi-host, multi-vector pathogen, WNV has been isolated from more than 46 species of mosquitoes13, the genus Culex being the most representative14. The virus can infect a wide range of vertebrate hosts, including birds, mammals, reptiles, and amphibians, reflecting its high ecological flexibility and complexity15–17. WNV is maintained primarily through an enzootic transmission cycle involving Culex mosquitoes and avian reservoir hosts, with occasional epizootic and zoonotic spillover to a variety of dead-end hosts, most notably humans and equines, which can develop severe clinical disease.

The dynamics of WNV transmission is governed by the co-occurrence of competent mosquito vectors, susceptible vertebrate hosts, and the pathogen in climate and ecologically suitable environments11,18–20. In recent decades, empirical evidence has revealed substantial inter-specific variation in host exposure, susceptibility to infection, magnitude and duration of viraemia, and infection-associated mortality across a diverse range of vertebrate taxa21–25. Understanding host ranges and their transmission capacity for WNV is fundamental for identifying and understanding the ecological drivers of viral amplification, anticipating transmission dynamics, and developing proactive surveillance26–29.

Within the host range, the contribution of each species to WNV transmission is determined by three key factors: (1) its abundance; (2) its competence, or infectiousness, defined as the ability of a host to become infected and develop sufficient viraemia (both magnitude and duration) to subsequently infect feeding mosquitoes; and (3) its susceptibility and exposure, which are shaped by both the host’s immune response and its behavioral ecology, the latter influencing vector contact rates, which, in turn, are also modulated by mosquito feeding preferences30–33. Given the ability of WNV to infect a broad spectrum of vertebrate taxa that differ in prevalence (reflecting exposure and susceptibility) and in competence, assembling a comprehensive dataset of confirmed host species and their associated prevalence and competence is a crucial first step towards modeling transmission risk and improving preparedness and response strategies for WNV22,34,35. Such data sets can facilitate future investigations aimed at identifying key amplifying hosts, assessing gaps in host surveillance, and refining monitoring strategies. They can also contribute to improving our understanding of how ecological and evolutionary processes—such as the structure of the transmission network and host trait variability—shape the dynamics of WNV, ultimately supporting the development of One Health frameworks and strengthening early warning and prediction systems36–38.

Based on this rationale, the present study introduces the first unified global database that systematically compiles empirical data on vertebrate hosts involved in WNV transmission. Through a comprehensive systematic review, we compile an overview with information on both (1) host exposure and susceptibility, inferred from WNV prevalence data in wild populations, and; (2) host competence, based on experimental infection studies. Although previous studies addressed aspects of WNV host susceptibility within more limited taxonomic scopes16,17,39, no prior effort has consolidated these data into a unified and taxonomically broad database.

Methods

Study design and protocol

We conducted a systematic literature review to integrate and synthesize evidence related to WNV infection as a means of identifying potential hosts on a global scale. To achieve this, we developed two independent databases. The WNV host prevalence database compiles studies on the prevalence of WNV in non-human vertebrates, encompassing wild populations, domesticated animals, and those in zoological settings, where exposure and infection with WNV have been tested and confirmed. The WNV host competence database focuses on evaluating host competence by compiling experimental infection studies and extracting quantitative measures in a standardised format to enable reconstruction and/or modelling of species-specific viraemia curves. In both cases, we used a hybrid search strategy that combined database searches with snowballing, as this approach has proven to be more effective in identifying primary studies40.

Literature search strategy and databases

To assess the prevalence of WNV in non-human vertebrate populations, this study builds on the quantitative review of avian field studies by Tolsá et al.16. Their database was critically reviewed and systematically expanded to include additional data on other vertebrate hosts, such as mammals, squamates, and amphibians, through an extensive search of the scientific literature. Literature searches were conducted in two phases, first in July 2023 and subsequently updated in March 2025, using the same search strategy and followed by systematic snowballing. The main academic databases consulted included PubMed (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), ScienceDirect (https://www.sciencedirect.com/), Semantic Scholar (https://www.semanticscholar.org/), Google Scholar (https://scholar.google.com/), and SciELO (https://scielo.org/en/). Search strategies combined controlled vocabulary (where available) and free-text terms related to pathogen, host taxa, and study outcomes. The core search terms included “West Nile virus” OR “WNV”, combined with host-related terms (e.g. bird, avian, mammal, reptile, amphibian, host) and outcome-related terms reflecting prevalence, surveillance, and experimental infection (e.g. prevalence, seroprevalence, infection, surveillance, experimental, viremia, viral load, transmission, host competence). The literature search covered the period from the initial isolation of WNV in Uganda in 19374 to 31/12/2024. The same academic databases were used to construct the WNV host competence database, focusing on data derived from experimental infection studies.

In both cases, articles retrieved from the database search were screened using predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, as specified below. Subsequently, a snowball approach was employed to enhance the completeness of the data set. During backward snowballing, the reference lists of included articles were examined to identify earlier studies relevant to the review’s scope, with judgment based on article title and their contexts of citation. For forward snowballing, the titles of the included articles were searched in Google Scholar to locate citing publications. Citations were reviewed and full-text evaluations were performed based on the relevance indicated in the title and abstract. If the abstract did not provide sufficient information to determine whether a reference should be included or excluded, the full text was screened for eligibility. No restrictions were applied on publication type, year, region, or language to ensure inclusivity and minimize potential biases arising from study characteristics or linguistic barriers41,42. All potentially relevant articles were imported into Zotero (6.0.37)43,44 for screening and data extraction.

WNV Host Prevalence Database

Data selection

The full text evaluation was selected according to the following inclusion criteria: (i) The publication contains data from epidemiological or prevalence studies of WNV in any infected animal in the wild, regardless of the type of diagnostic test used. We included studies reporting on the occurrence of WNV in wild animals (including those held in, e.g., wildlife rehabilitation centers), captive animals (e.g., zoological institutions) and domesticated animals from farms, breeding facilities or human-modified environments (e.g., dogs, cats, or pigeons); (ii) The study includes negative results for any diagnostic test for WNV in wild hosts to properly document which species and regions have been evaluated.

Publications were excluded if they met one or more of the following criteria: (i) The publication only indicates the species number of individuals that tested positive for WNV without reporting the total number of individuals tested (in case this information was mentioned but not provided; additional data were requested from the study authors to allow inclusion); (ii) The species were not identified at least at the genus level, with data presented only at the family or order level; (iii) Case reports of individual animals; (iv) Reports of suspected WNV infection based on clinical signs in individual animals without laboratory confirmation; (v) Wildlife outbreak or mass mortality reports attributed to WNV without laboratory confirmation of WNV in the event (e.g., no PCR/RT-PCR, virus isolation, or serological evidence); (vi) Publications focused on experimental infections; (vii) Studies involving human subjects; (viii) Studies for which the full text could not be obtained despite author contact attempts.

Data extraction

The data were extracted from selected articles into a pre-designed Microsoft Excel worksheet. The extracted data included: (i) Host species (scientific name), number of individuals sampled, number of animals tested positive, diagnostic method or methods applied, prevalence, lineage and strain of WNV (if reported), study area, sampling year and period, study area (country, province/region, zoorealms) and longitude and latitude. Because exact coordinates were rarely reported—most studies referenced a general locality—we assigned coordinates to the centroid of the municipality, province/region, or other area specified in the study. These coordinates were then used to assign observations to terrestrial zoogeographic realms, following distributions for birds, mammals, and other terrestrial vertebrates (21,037 species) delineated by Holt et al.45. (ii) Publication details (author name, year of publication, title, DOI). Whenever prevalence data were expressed as percentage, raw numbers were obtained, converting the percentages to the nearest integers.

To address taxonomic inconsistencies, the common names and species reported in the studies were standardized to align with current taxonomic conventions. For birds, we utilized four taxonomic frameworks to facilitate future integrative analyzes: BirdLife International46, BirdTree47, eBird48, and the GBIF Backbone Taxonomy with associated taxon IDs49. Taxon crosswalks established by Tobias et al.50 were used to harmonize the taxonomic frameworks. When necessary, we consult Avibase (The World Bird Database)51 and AviList (The Global Avian Checklist)52 for synonymies and name updates. For mammals, scientific names were verified against the GBIF Backbone Taxonomy49 and the Mammal Diversity Database53, while for reptiles and amphibians, we used the GBIF Backbone Taxonomy to ensure accuracy and consistency, retrieving both accepted scientific names and the corresponding GBIF taxon IDs. We included the species name, genus, specific epithet, family, order, class, phylum, and kingdom for each of the recorded taxa to ensure taxonomic resolution and facilitate downstream ecological analyzes.

Diagnostic methods for WNV detection were classified according to their methodological principles and diagnostic utility. In the prevalence dataset, each diagnostic record is represented in a separate row and linked to the same observation through a unique observation_code. To make diagnostic heterogeneity explicit and easier to interpret, we added five variables to each record: diagnostic_method, assay_stage, workflow_design, diagnostic_group, and biological_interpretation.

diagnostic_method identifies the specific assay reported in the source study. assay_stage describes the role of the assay within the diagnostic workflow, distinguishing assays used for (i) screening, (ii) confirmatory testing, or (iii) as independent assays not explicitly linked to a screening-confirmation sequence. workflow_design denotes the overall configuration of methods reported for observation, distinguishing records based on a single diagnostic method from those using sequential screening-confirmatory workflows or assays applied independently in parallel (e.g, a molecular test for active infection and a serological approach testing past exposure to the same samples). Observations containing both sequential and independent parallel components were classified as workflow_design = parallel. diagnostic_group classifies the test in more general terms as (i) serology, (ii) molecular, (iii) antigen, or (iv) isolation. biological_interpretation indicates whether a positive result is best interpreted as a past exposure, a recent exposure, or an active infection.

This structure makes explicit the implications of diagnostic heterogeneity for downstream analyses. In particular, serological assays mostly reflect exposure history, whereas molecular, antigen-based, and virus-isolation methods are more indicative of current or very recent infection. Therefore, raw prevalence estimates should not be directly compared between diagnostic categories without taking these differences into account.

Molecular tests include reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) and quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR), which are widely used to detect WNV RNA in blood or cerebrospinal fluid54,55. These methods can offer high sensitivity and specificity for detecting viral RNA during the acute phase of infection, particularly in blood and cerebrospinal fluid, although sensitivity varies depending on the primers used (e.g., lineage-specific vs. pan-lineage). Their sensitivity declines as the disease progresses56, but alternative samples such as urine, swabs, brain tissue, or feathers may allow detection beyond the typical viraemic window57. In the dataset, these assays are recorded as diagnostic_method = molecular_detection, grouped as diagnostic_group = molecular, and interpreted as active infection. Their assay_stage is independent; depending on the observation, workflow_design may be single_method or parallel.

Antigen-based tests detect viral proteins in tissues or fluids, providing evidence of active viral replication. These include Immunohistochemical Methods (IHC) and field-deployable tests such as VecTest that offer more immediate indications of infection55. In the dataset, these assays are classified as diagnostic_group = antigen and are interpreted as active infection. They are usually annotated as independent and workflow_design may be single_method or parallel.

Serological or antibody-based tests detect IgM and IgG antibodies against WNV; IgM typically appear 3 to 7 days after exposure, while IgG antibodies appear shortly after. Among these, blocking ELISA tests are the most commonly used, with numerous commercial kits available58,59. ELISA tests are fast, but can exhibit cross-reactivity with antibodies against other flaviviruses -depending on the antigen used- complicating the interpretation of the results60. In the dataset, these assays are recorded as diagnostic_method = serological.ELISA, grouped as diagnostic_group = serology, and interpreted as past exposure. Depending on the study design, they are annotated as assay_stage = screening in sequential workflows or as assay_stage = independent when used alone or in parallel; accordingly, workflow_design may be single_method, sequential, or parallel.

Virus neutralisation tests (VNTs), including plaque reduction neutralisation tests (PRNT) and microneutralisation assays (micro-VNT), are slower and more technically demanding than ELISA, and typically require higher biosafety laboratory conditions; however, they are often regarded the gold standard for serological confirmation. Although not entirely free of cross-reactivity, they are considerably more specific than ELISA in distinguishing WNV from other flavivirus infections, making them an essential confirmatory tool for ELISA-positive cases61–63. These assays are recorded as diagnostic_method = serological.VNT, grouped as diagnostic_group = serology, and interpreted as past exposure. Their assay_stage may be confirmatory in sequential workflows, or independent when used alone or in parallel with a molecular method; accordingly, workflow_design may be single_method, sequential, or parallel.

Hemagglutination Inhibition (HI), an antibody-based method, detects antiviral antibodies by measuring the virus’s ability to inhibit red blood cell agglutination. Although HI is generally more specific than ELISA, particularly in distinguishing closely related flaviviruses, some cross-reactivity may still occur. The Microsphere Immunoassay (MIA) and other multiplexed ELISA-like diagnostic techniques use multiple antigens from different viruses to detect IgM and IgG antibodies in serum samples that offer high performance and scalability64. Multiplexed IgM and IgG MIA platforms demonstrate some error relative to neutralization assays, but offer high-throughput capabilities63. In the dataset, HI and MIA are classified within diagnostic_group = serology and interpreted as past exposure. HI may occur as an assay_stage = screening in sequential workflows or as an assay_stage = independent when used alone or in parallel, whereas MIA is currently annotated as independent.

IgM-specific serological assays are treated separately in the dataset because they are more informative about recent exposure than general antibody assays. These records are classified within serological methods but assigned as recent exposure under the biological_interpretation variable instead of past exposure. In the current dataset, IgM assays are annotated as assay_stage = independent; workflow_design may be single_method or parallel.

Virus isolation methods are also retained when reported. These provide direct evidence of infectious virus in the sample and are therefore classified as diagnostic_group = isolation with biological_interpretation = active_infection. Their assay_stage is independent; depending on the observation, workflow_design may be single_method or parallel.

WNV Host Competence Database

Data selection

All the papers collected in the first steps using a combination of database search and snowballing techniques were exposed to a full text scan before including them in the review. Studies were required to meet specific criteria to be included: (i) Publications had to report data from controlled experimental studies investigating WNV infection in animals, providing detailed descriptions of experimental protocols, such as dose, infection method, and WNV lineage or strain. (ii) Studies were also required to specify the host species and the number of individuals infected. (iii) Quantitative laboratory results, mainly time-series viremia levels and mortality rates, should be clearly presented in the text, as tables, or in a graphical format. (iv) Additionally, studies needed to report the exposure status of individuals prior to infection, ensuring that free-living animals had not previously been infected with WNV or related virus, vaccinated or affected by other diseases, and that captive animals were not vaccinated. Studies investigating WNV vaccines were also included, as they often feature control groups of intentionally exposed unvaccinated individuals to the virus, offering comparative insights into immune responses and infection dynamics.

Studies were excluded if (i) they did not report key experimental results, such as viraemia levels or mortality rates, (ii) did not provide sufficient methodological detail, (iii) did not clearly identify host species, (iv) included vaccinated or previously infected animals, or (v) if the full text of the study could not be accessed.

Data extraction

Data were extracted from the included articles using a standardised extraction sheet created in Microsoft Excel. Two reviewers independently assessed the articles based on predefined selection criteria to ensure reliability and accuracy. The extracted information included: (i) publication details (title, first author, year and DOI); and (ii) results of the study details, such as host species and taxonomy (standardised by following the same procedure as for the WNV Host Prevalence Database), WNV lineage and strain used for infection, year of isolation and geographic origin of the WNV strain, GenBank accession number(s) for the corresponding viral sequence when available, virus dose and its corresponding units, sample size of infected individuals, viraemia dynamics expressed as log10 titres over time, and mortality data, recorded as the number of surviving individuals during the study period.

In the competence database, each row corresponds to a specific day post-inoculation within an experimental infection curve. Accordingly, the variable day refers to the specific day represented by that row, sample_size_original refers to the number of individuals inoculated at the start of the experiment, and sample_size_survival refers to the number of individuals still alive on that same row-specific day.

Daily viraemia levels, expressed as log10 titres, and mortality data were extracted from tables, figures, or supplementary materials from the included studies. Graphical data were digitised using WebPlotDigitizer65 by the same two extractors involved in the broader data extraction process.

To quantify uncertainty associated with manual digitisation, a validation subset of 25 viraemia curves was assessed in two ways. First, repeated extraction of the same curves by the same observer(s) was used to estimate within-observer digitisation error, yielding a mean absolute error of 0.032 log10 PFU (RMSE = 0.049 log10 PFU), with a maximum absolute deviation of 0.113 log10 PFU. Second, independent digitisation of the same subset by the two extractors was compared to assess between-observer agreement, producing a mean absolute error of 0.029 log10 PFU and a maximum absolute deviation of 0.242 log10 PFU. These uncertainty estimates were calculated only for the 25-curve validation subset and were not extended to all remaining curves in the database.

Digitisation uncertainty is nevertheless expected to have limited impact on derived competence metrics, because most studies reported mean log10 titres aggregated across individuals and the estimated extraction error was small relative to the log10 scale of variation in viraemia. When studies reported a mean viraemia curve directly in a table or figure, that mean curve was recorded as presented. When individual-level viraemia curves were available, we calculated the per-day mean log10 titre across individuals and retained only this mean curve in order to harmonise data representation across studies.

Measures of dispersion around the mean curves (e.g. standard errors, confidence intervals, or other error bars) were not retained as separate variables in the current version of the database because they were reported inconsistently across studies and could not be standardised across the full dataset.

Many studies also reported mortality curves, from which day-specific survival could be derived. Therefore, sample_size_survival should be interpreted as the number of individuals alive on the specific day represented by the row, rather than the final number surviving at the end of the experiment. However, because original studies did not always report the exact number of individuals contributing to each daily mean titre separately from the number surviving on that day, sample_size_survival should be regarded as the best available day-specific denominator and not necessarily the exact number used to calculate the reported mean titre.

The included studies primarily reported infection doses and viraemia titres using two types of units: plaque-forming units (PFU) and the tissue culture infectious dose infecting 50% of cultures (TCID50). Both PFU/ml and TCID50/ml quantify viral infectivity and virus titre, but they are based on distinct methodologies. To improve comparability between studies, we created a standardized variable in the database by converting TCID50-based values to PFU/ml using an approximate factor of 0.7, following the equation PFU/ml = TCID50/ml × 0.766–68. Because the relationship between TCID50 and PFU varies with assay design and laboratory conditions, the applied conversion should be interpreted as a pragmatic standardization step that facilitates comparison across studies, while introducing additional uncertainty into the resulting PFU-equivalent values.

Data Records

The datasets described in this study are archived in Zenodo as a persistent, versioned, citable release at https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.2002410169. The Zenodo repository contains two XLSX workbooks: WNV_Host_Prevalence.xlsx and WNV_Host_Competence.xlsx. Each workbook includes an observations table together with associated metadata, reference, and taxonomy sheets.

WNV Host Prevalence Database

The file WNV_Host_Prevalence.xlsx contains four sheets: (i) observations, (ii) metadata, (iii) references, and (iv) taxonomy. The observations sheet contains row-level records extracted from published studies and publicly available surveillance reports. The metadata sheet defines all variables used in the observations table. The references sheet provides the complete bibliographic information for the cited sources, and the taxonomy sheet summarizes taxonomic harmonization and nomenclatural crosswalks (e.g., BirdLife, eBird, BirdTree).

In the prevalence database, diagnostic information is stored in long format, such that a single observation may correspond to more than one row when multiple assays were reported for the same species/sample record. The observations sheet includes the variables diagnostic_method, assay_stage, workflow_design, diagnostic_group, and biological_interpretation, which indicate the assay used (e.g., ELISA, molecular, VNT), its role within the study design (e.g., screening, confirmatory), the overall multi-assay design of the observation, its broader diagnostic category (e.g., serological, molecular), and the biological meaning of a positive result (e.g., past exposure, active infection). This structure allows users to distinguish records reflecting past exposure, recent exposure, or active infection, and to determine whether serological positives were supported by a confirmatory neutralization assay, which improves interpretation of ELISA-like results in the context of possible cross-reactivity. WNV Host Competence Database

The file WNV_Host_Competence.xlsx contains four sheets: (i) observations, (ii) metadata, (iii) references, and (iv) taxonomy. The observations sheet contains row-level experimental infection data extracted from published studies. The metadata sheet defines all variables used in the observations table. The references sheet provides the complete bibliographic information for the cited sources, and the taxonomy sheet summarizes taxonomic harmonization across nomenclatural frameworks.

In the competence database, each row on the observations sheet corresponds to a specific day within an experimental infection curve. The variable curve identifies records belonging to the same infection time series, day indicates the day post inoculation, and titer_pfu stores the day-specific viraemia value. The variables sample_size_original and sample_size_survival indicate, respectively, the number of individuals inoculated at the start of the experiment and the number still alive on the row-specific day. The variable PFU_dose provides a standardized inoculation dose in PFU or PFU-equivalent units when this information could be recorded or converted from the source study. Additional fields record experimental context, including inoculation_method, WNV lineage, and strain, which are important for interpreting heterogeneity among infection experiments.

Data Overview

WNV Host Prevalence Database

The WNV host prevalence dataset compiles records from 541 peer-reviewed studies and official reports from health agencies, covering 535,568 tested animals from 91 countries between 1950 and 2023 (Fig. 1). Birds represent the taxon most intensively surveyed, with 432,350 individuals tested (80.7%) in 1,377 species, followed by mammals with 102,198 individuals tested (19.1%) in 400 species. Within Aves, sampling is heavily concentrated in Passeriformes, which account for 679 tested species and 330,606 tested individuals (Table 2), followed by Charadiformes (97 species; 5,698 individuals), Anseriformes (87 species; 21,205 individuals) and Columbiformes (48 species; 21,080 individuals) (Fig. 2A). WNV testing was also recorded for 22 sauropsid, and 2 amphibian species. Among the 541 reviewed studies, 355 included avian hosts, yielding 10,093 records, nearly almost twice the 4,995 records compiled in the previous synthesis by Tolsá et al.16 obtained from 147 studies. Our systematic review identified a total of 647 bird species that have tested positive for WNV, representing 47% of the avian species screened. These detections should not be interpreted uniformly in epidemiological terms. Depending on the diagnostic method used, positive records may indicate past exposure, recent exposure, or active infection, but do not demonstrate transmission competence or reservoir status.

Of these, 284 species (43.8%) were confirmed positive by molecular assays (e.g. RT-PCR), which provide stronger evidence of active infection and potential viral shedding. They therefore may indicate a higher likelihood of reservoir competence16. However, such inferences remain provisional and should be validated under controlled experimental conditions. Molecular detection alone does not demonstrate that a species can develop sufficient viremia to infect mosquitoes and contribute to future transmission.

By contrast, positivity detected exclusively by serological methods (e.g., IgM/IgG ELISA or VNT) reflects primarily prior exposure and does not necessarily imply the capacity to transmit the virus to mosquitoes16. Instead, evidence for transmission competence must be derived from the WNV Host Competence database generated from controlled experimental infection studies. Refer to the “Usage Notes” section for more details on the number of species tested using different validation methods (serological or molecular) and how these should be considered when using the database. Furthermore, 111 mammalian species (27.8%) were reported as WNV-positive. Most mammalian detections were based on serological evidence (ELISA and/or virus neutralisation tests), with comparatively fewer reports supported by molecular confirmation, reflecting past exposure rather than active infection or transmission potential. The majority of the mammalian positives reported occurred after the introduction of WNV and its subsequent spread throughout the American continent (Fig. 1).

WNV Host Competence Database

The systematic review resulted in the inclusion of data from 113 peer-reviewed studies that reported a total of 362 viraemia curves, representing 103 different species. Birds were the most represented group, with 80 species spanning 12 orders and 30 families, and a substantial sample size of 1,925 experimentally tested individuals. Within Aves, Passeriformes, Galliformes, and Columbiformes were the orders more studied, with 39, 12 and 6 species, respectively. Mammalia includes 16 species from 12 families in 7 orders, with 237 tested animals. Reptilia and Amphibia are represented by fewer species and smaller sample sizes, with Reptilia comprising 4 species and 97 individuals, and Amphibia contributing 2 species and 74 tested animals.

The raw experimental data allow identification of species whose viraemia peaks exceed the commonly used infectivity threshold of 104.5–105 PFU in the blood, above which feeding mosquitoes are more likely to become infected (Fig. 3A)70. This threshold should be interpreted as an approximate reference value rather than a universal cutoff, as it has been derived mainly from studies involving Culex pipiens. The relationship between host viraemia and mosquito infection probability can vary between vector species, between populations within a species, and with environmental conditions such as temperature, which affects viral replication in mosquitoes. Moreover, by extracting time-resolved titre measurements in a standardised format, our database supports reconstruction and/or modelling of species-specific viraemia trajectories and associated survival profiles (Fig. 3B). This facilitates the derivation of quantitative competence metrics, including peak titre and timing, duration of viraemia above a chosen infectivity threshold, and cumulative infectiousness (e.g., area under the curve above threshold), and provides inputs for subsequent transmission modelling analyses.

The experimental conditions represented in the competence database are heterogeneous with respect to inoculation route, inoculated dose, and viral genotype. Across the 362 infection curves, needle inoculation was the most common exposure route, followed by mosquito-mediated exposure and oral exposure. Comparable PFU-dose information was available for all needle-inoculation curves, but was missing for part of the mosquito-mediated and oral-exposure experiments, reflecting incomplete reporting of comparable delivered-dose information in these study designs. In mosquito-mediated experiments, this usually occurred because the virus dose offered to mosquitoes was not reported, whereas in oral-exposure experiments it often reflected the absence of information on the infectious dose contained in the prey item or food material. Moreover, when the PFU-dose was reported for these routes, it represents the dose offered to mosquitoes or contained in prey, not necessarily the dose effectively transmitted to or ingested by the vertebrate host. Mosquito-mediated exposure experiments involved at least 10 reported mosquito taxa from two genera (Aedes and Culex), further emphasizing heterogeneity in vector-mediated protocols across regions. Most species investigated in controlled laboratory infection experiments were also represented in field-based prevalence sampling (Fig. 2A). Only two experimentally tested species lacked the corresponding prevalence records in the wild: Corvus mellori(little raven, Australia) and Chlorodrepanis virens (Hawai’i ’amakihi, Hawai). Inclusion of GenBank accession numbers, together with standardised metadata on viral lineage, isolation year, and isolate location, enables detailed characterisation of strains used in experimental infections. The large majority of experimental studies have used WNV lineage 1 strains (96.5% of studies), predominantly isolates derived from the NY99 strain, whereas lineage 2 strains have been used comparatively less frequently (8.0% of studies), and lineage 1b and Kunjin-related isolates have been rarely included (2.7% and 0.9% of studies, respectively) (Fig. 2B). These annotations provide a transparent basis for assessing the comparability of experimental outcomes across studies, evaluating the representativeness of commonly used strains relative to circulating viruses in different regions, and guiding the design of future experiments aimed at testing lineage- and strain-specific differences in host infectiousness.

Technical Validation

All host records included in the database come from peer-reviewed scientific articles, books, and official reports, ensuring rigorous data validation. Additional data from publicly accessible databases managed by health agencies were incorporated if they provided verifiable, active links, and sufficient methodological details according to established inclusion criteria. In particular, certain records previously reported by Tolsà-García et al.16 of health agencies in the United States and Mexico were excluded, as the original data links were no longer available and requests to access these data were unanswered.

To maintain consistency and ensure robust data quality, we strictly adhered to the standardization and harmonization criteria outlined in the materials and methods. In particular, studies were excluded if they only reported positive cases without providing complete details on the species and the numbers of negative individuals tested. Each entry in the data set is accompanied by its bibliographic source, enabling users to assess the validity of the data and facilitating future data reuse. All references cited are fully documented, providing direct access to the original studies.

Given the dynamic nature of bird taxonomy, avian host classifications follow an integrative approach aligned with the AVONET dataset50, incorporating multiple taxonomic treatments to facilitate compatibility with ecological trait databases, phylogenetic analyses, GBIF distribution observations, and eBird abundance maps. Specifically, we applied the taxonomic crosswalk framework of Tobias et al.50, integrating BirdLife International46, eBird48, and BirdTree47, with additional verification and updates performed via Avibase71. Taxonomic harmonization followed a hierarchical decision process. First, when a BirdLife/Avibase taxon had a unique one-to-one match in BirdTree or eBird, that corresponding name was retained directly. Second, when multiple candidate matches existed, we prioritized the option that preserved the BirdLife/Avibase name exactly whenever that same-name alternative was available, in order to maximize consistency with the primary avian taxonomic backbone used in the database. Third, when no exact same-name option existed in the target framework, we retained the closest taxonomic equivalent available for interoperability, while preserving all alternative candidate matches explicitly in the taxonomy table. To maintain transparency, the ‘Taxonomy` sheet stores both the selected harmonized name and the full set of alternative candidate names, together with match-type fields indicating whether the correspondence was one-to-one or one-to-many. This allows users to identify ambiguous cases and, if needed, apply alternative taxonomic decisions in downstream analyses. Mammalian hosts were standardized using the Mammal Diversity Database53,72 and the GBIF Backbone Taxonomy49, thus ensuring precision by retrieving accepted scientific names along with their corresponding taxon IDs from the GBIF.

Given that pathogen host ranges are inherently dynamic, influenced by pathogen evolution, diversity, and spread, together with intensity of surveillance, and considering the increased research attention to WNV following recent outbreaks in Europe2, our goal is to regularly maintain and expand the database. This will be achieved through systematic reviews of the literature and direct contributions from the research community. We encourage researchers to report any updates or corrections to the corresponding author (alex.richter@ceab.csic.es) to ensure accuracy and ongoing data improvement.

Usage Notes

Linking to External Datasets and Identifying Sampling Gaps

To facilitate integration with other biodiversity and ecological datasets, the compiled dataset includes multiple standardised species identifiers (e.g., BirdLife, GBIF, eBird, BirdTree). This enables cross-referencing across nomenclatural systems and supports linking with widely used resources such as AVONET50, EltonTraits73, BirdLife species distribution maps74, IUCN Areas of Habitat maps75, and eBird abundance estimates76. Combined analyses of host testing records for WNV with species distributions, habitat associations, and abundance estimates can support spatially explicit assessments of host ecology, biogeography, and community-level drivers of virus circulation and spillover risk. As an example, overlaying host testing records with regional species assemblages allows quantification of the proportion of local avian diversity that has been sampled, thus identifying geographic gaps in surveillance and prioritising regions for targeted field sampling (Fig. 4). Large areas in the Southern Hemisphere and Southeast Asia remain poorly represented, including the Amazon basin, the Congo rainforest, and parts of Indonesia and Papua New Guinea, where sampling coverage is low relative to local avian richness. This uneven effort, concentrated in the Palearctic and Nearctic realms, may bias current inference on host susceptibility, host range, and transmission risk. In particular, apparent host-range expansion in better-sampled regions may partly reflect surveillance intensity rather than true ecological generalism, highlighting the need for more geographically balanced surveillance and for caution when comparing host-range patterns among regions.

Interpretation of Diagnostic Outcomes

The WNV host prevalence database includes studies reporting WNV detection using a range of diagnostic techniques, primarily molecular methods and serological assays. When interpreting these results, particularly in comparative or meta-analytical contexts, it is important to recognise that detection outcomes depend on the diagnostic method applied. Each method targets a distinct stage of the infection process, ranging from early-phase viraemia (detected via RT-PCR) to later-stage immune responses (detected via IgM or IgG ELISA or VNT). Importantly, diagnostic detection windows differ markedly: RT-PCR has a short detection window, typically restricted to the acute viraemic phase over a few days, whereas antibodies can remain detectable for many months to more than a year77,78. Because serological, molecular, antigen-based, and isolation-based records capture different stages of the infection process and operate in different temporal windows, raw prevalence values should not be directly compared across diagnostic categories without appropriate stratification. To reduce misinterpretation, users can filter records by diagnostic_method, diagnostic_group, assay_stage, workflow_design, or biological_interpretation, depending on whether the analytical objective is to distinguish assay type, biological interpretation, or diagnostic workflow.

Among the studies compiled here, most relied on a single diagnostic approach (n = 400/541, ~73.9%), most commonly serology alone—particularly virus neutralization tests (VNT; n = 145, 26.8%) and ELISA (n = 139, 25.6%)—or molecular detection alone (e.g., RT-PCR; n = 115, 21.2%). A fraction combined two diagnostic methods (n = 127, ~23.5%). Combinations that integrated molecular and serological evidence were rarer (molecular detection + ELISA: n = 19, 3.51%; molecular detection + VNT: n = 10, 1.84%). Combining molecular and serological tools is particularly valuable for interpretation, as it helps distinguish active infection from past exposure, thus improving temporal inference of transmission dynamics and reducing ambiguity when prevalence estimates span different phases of infection.

Diagnostic specificity is an important consideration when interpreting WNV surveillance data in the WNV host prevalence database. Molecular assays (RT-PCR and RT-qPCR) are highly specific and are commonly used as reference methods for detecting acute infection79,80. Serological assays require more cautious interpretation: ELISA-based methods are sensitive but may show cross-reactivity with antibodies against closely related flaviviruses, depending in part on the antigen used81. Cross-reactivity is particularly relevant among members of the same serocomplex (e.g., WNV, Usutu virus (USUV), and Japanese encephalitis virus (JEV))82. Virus neutralisation tests (e.g., PRNT) provide greater specificity and are often used for serological confirmation, although some cross-neutralisation can still occur79,80. Where co-circulating flaviviruses are expected, parallel testing against WNV and related viruses can improve attribution of seropositivity, avoiding under- or overestimation of WNV circulation and host range within a given region. In addition, IgM is generally considered less cross-reactive than IgG and is often used to support inference on recent infection, as IgM typically rises and wanes more rapidly than IgG.

In birds, serological neutralization assays—considered the gold standard—along with molecular techniques79,80, are the most commonly used. The use of the different methods has changed over time, with serological neutralization assays dominating the early 2000s and molecular testing increasing thereafter, now accounting for a large share of avian testing in recent years (Fig. 5B). The use of the diagnostic method is therefore highly uneven between species. Only 27.8% (384/1,377) of avian species have ever been assessed with all three diagnostic approaches, while substantial fractions were tested exclusively by neutralization assays (21.1%) or exclusively by molecular methods (16.6%) (Fig. 5A). This imbalance has two implications. First, cross-species comparisons of “positivity” are not directly comparable unless the choice of method is taken into account because, as explained before, molecular methods primarily capture current or very recent infection (and potential shedding), while serology reflects past exposure with a much longer detection window. Second, the distribution of methods across species is unlikely to be random: the higher share of positives observed in molecular-only subsets plausibly reflects targeted sampling of suspected reservoirs or outbreak contexts rather than intrinsic differences in test performance (i.e., ascertainment bias). Methodological differences must be taken into account when inferring host susceptibility and prevalence. For analyzes using this dataset, we recommend: (i) stratifying or weighting by diagnostic method; (ii) conducting sensitivity analyzes contrasting molecular-confirmed versus serology-only records; and (iii) reporting method-specific prevalence to avoid conflating past exposure with current infection.

Accordingly, the prevalence database should be interpreted as documenting the observed field host range of WNV susceptibility (exposure and infection), rather than the functional transmission host range of the virus. Reservoir or amplification status cannot be inferred from prevalence data alone and requires complementary experimental evidence on viraemia and infectiousness to mosquitoes.

Data Considerations for Host Competence Modelling

Experimental infection data provide the basis to estimate and characterise host-specific viraemia curves and, consequently, inferring potential host contributions to WNV amplification and transmission. However, comparisons of viraemia dynamics and competence metrics between species and studies should explicitly account for differences in experimental design, as these can strongly influence estimated outcomes. Key variables include the inoculated dose (typically reported in PFU/mL or equivalent units), the route of inoculation (e.g., syringe inoculation, mosquito feeding, or oral exposure through infected prey), and viral factors such as lineage and strain24.

Importantly, the reported inoculated doses are not always directly comparable between the inoculation methods. Under needle-based inoculation (syringe), the dose administered can typically be approximated as the product of inoculum concentration and injected volume, and therefore closely reflects the amount of virus delivered to the host. In contrast, when infection is achieved through mosquito feeding or oral exposure, the reported titre usually corresponds to the virus dose offered to mosquitoes or the infectious titre of prey material, rather than the quantity effectively transmitted to the vertebrate host. Experimental and entomological studies have also shown that inoculum administered during mosquito feeding varies between vector species and that mosquito saliva can modulate infection dynamics, including enhanced viraemia and tissue titres in vertebrate hosts83,84. Therefore, this distinction should be considered in downstream analyses that aim to relate inoculated dose, inoculation route, and viral genotype to resulting viraemia curves and inferred transmission potential.

The viral genetic background is a critical source of heterogeneity in experimental results85. Experimental infections in the house sparrow (Passer domesticus) have shown that transmission phenotypic traits, including the magnitude of viraemia, tissue dissemination, and viral shedding, can differ substantially among WNV strains24,86,87. In particular, such differences occur not only between lineages but also among strains within the same lineage. For example, NY99 (lineage 1a) has been associated with higher and more persistent viraemia than other strains of the same lineage, or of lineage 2 strains87. Although multiple WNV lineages have been described globally88,89, experimental infections have focused predominantly on lineages 1a and 2, which may limit inference in regions where distinct strains circulate or where multiple lineages co-circulate.

To facilitate robust comparative analyses, the database records, when available, the inoculated dose and units, the inoculation method, the lineage and strain identity (including GenBank accession numbers), and isolate year and location. These annotations enable sensitivity analyses and model-based reconstructions that explicitly account for experimental conditions, improving comparability between studies and supporting the recalculation of viraemia curves and competence metrics under standardised assumptions24,90,91. Because inoculation route, inoculated dose, lineage, and strain can all influence observed viraemia dynamics and survival, users should account for these experimental design variables when deriving host competence metrics or comparing species across studies. In particular, curves generated under markedly different exposure conditions should not be pooled uncritically. Depending on the analytical objective, these sources of heterogeneity may be addressed through stratified analyses or through statistical or modelling approaches that separate experimental effects from intrinsic biological differences among host species. This is especially relevant when estimating metrics such as peak viraemia, duration of infectiousness, or area under the viraemia curve, which may be systematically affected by experimental protocol.

For users interested in reconstructing experimental time series, each row in the competence database represents a specific day within an infection curve, identified by a unique curve identifier. Viraemia curves can therefore be reconstructed by grouping rows by curve and ordering them by day, using titer_pfu as the day-specific viraemia value. These reconstructed trajectories may then be fitted using different mathematical functions to characterise viraemia dynamics, for example with Wood’s model, logistic or Gompertz-type curves, or more flexible spline-based approaches, depending on the structure of the data and the intended downstream analysis. Survival trajectories can be reconstructed in the same way using sample_size_survival across days. The variable sample_size_original indicates the initial number of inoculated individuals in that experimental curve. Users should note, however, that sample_size_survival reflects the number of individuals alive on a given day and not necessarily the exact number contributing to the reported mean titre at that time point, as some studies sampled only a subset of surviving individuals on each day.
