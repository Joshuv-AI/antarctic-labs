Lifestyle and Socioeconomic Determinants of Glycemic Control among Patients with Type 2 Diabetes in Borama, Somaliland: A Cross-Sectional Analysis
Authors: Abdirahman Said Deheye, Abdirahman Omer Ali, Abdifatah Mahamud ileye, Hared Omar Alaleh, Mahamed Farah Mahamoud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to identify the lifestyle and socioeconomic determinants of glycemic control among patients with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) in Borama, Somaliland, and a region with limited data on non-communicable diseases.MethodsA hospital-based, cross-sectional study was conducted from March 2023 to September 2023 at Borama Regional Hospital, involving 152 adult T2DM patients selected via systematic random sampling. Data on sociodemographics, lifestyle factors (physical activity, dietary habits, tobacco use), and glycemic control (HbA1c dichotomized as ≤7.0% for good control and >7.0% for poor control) were collected using structured questionnaires and medical record reviews. Multivariate binary logistic regression was performed to identify independent predictors of poor glycemic control.ResultsThe prevalence of poor glycemic control was high, affecting 63.8% of participants. Multivariate analysis revealed that poor physical activity (Adjusted Odds Ratio [AOR] = 3.85, 95% CI: 1.62–9.14, p = 0.002), unhealthy dietary habits (AOR = 4.51, 95% CI: 1.89–10.78, p < 0.001), unemployment or casual employment status (AOR = 2.98, 95% CI: 1.25–7.11, p = 0.014) and lower educational attainment (χ2 = 6.45, p = 0.011) were independent predictors of poor glycemic control. Gender, and age were not significant independent predictors in the adjusted model.ConclusionA substantial proportion of T2DM patients in Borama experience poor glycemic control, strongly influenced by modifiable lifestyle factors and socioeconomic status. Interventions targeting improved physical activity, healthier dietary practices, and socioeconomic support for unemployed individuals are crucial for enhancing diabetes management outcomes in this setting and aligning with global health targets like Sustainable Development Goal 3 (SDG 3).Keywords: Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus, Glycemic Control, Lifestyle, Socioeconomic Determinants, Somaliland, Public Health

INTRODUCTION

The global prevalence of Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) continues to rise, posing a significant challenge to health systems, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) 1,2. The International Diabetes Federation reports that Africa is projected to experience the highest increase in diabetes prevalence globally by 2045 [3]. The number of people living with diabetes rose from 200 million in 1990 to 830 million in 2022. Prevalence has been rising more rapidly in low- and middle-income countries than in high-income countries. Specifically, the overall prevalence of diabetes mellitus among adult patients attending Hargeisa Group Hospital of Somaliland was reported at 29.9%. Beyond incidence, the primary clinical challenge lies in the effective management of the disease to prevent debilitating microvascular and macrovascular complications, such as retinopathy, nephropathy, and cardiovascular disease 4,5.

The cornerstone of T2DM management is achieving and maintaining optimal glycemic control, most commonly measured by glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels [6]. Despite the availability of effective pharmacological agents, a large proportion of patients in LMICs fail to achieve the recommended HbA1c target of ≤7.0% 7,8. This gap is largely attributed to challenges in adhering to complex self-management behaviors, which are heavily influenced by a patient's lifestyle, socioeconomic context, and health literacy 9,10.

Modifiable lifestyle factors, including physical activity and diet, are fundamental to diabetes management. Regular physical activity enhances insulin sensitivity and improves glucose uptake, while a healthy diet helps regulate blood glucose levels and manage body weight 11,12. However, the ability to adopt and sustain these behaviors is often constrained by socioeconomic determinants such as education, income, and employment status, which can affect access to healthy foods, safe recreational spaces, and healthcare services 13,14.

In Somaliland, where the burden of NCDs is rapidly growing, there is a critical lack of data on the factors influencing diabetes management outcomes. Indeed, country-wide data suggest that chronic diseases represent a major public health challenge in Somaliland, with their prevalence heavily patterned by socio-demographic factors [15]. While previous local observations noted a high prevalence of risk factors, the specific predictors of poor glycemic control within the diabetic population remain uninvestigated. Understanding these determinants is essential for designing targeted, effective interventions that move beyond generic advice to address the specific contextual barriers faced by patients. This study, therefore, aimed to identify the lifestyle and socioeconomic predictors of poor glycemic control among T2DM patients in Borama, Somaliland.

METHODOLOGY

Study Design and Setting

A hospital-based, cross-sectional study was conducted from March 2023 to September 2023 at the diabetes clinic of the Borama Regional Hospital, the primary tertiary care facility in the Awdal region of Somaliland.

Study Population and Sampling

The target population comprised all adult patients with a confirmed diagnosis of T2DM registered at the clinic. A sample of 152 patients was selected using systematic random sampling, with the size determined based on Krejcie and Morgan’s (1970) table to ensure a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error for a target population of 250 registered patients. Inclusion criteria included being aged 18 years or older, having a T2DM diagnosis for at least one year, and the availability of an HbA1c measurement within the preceding three months. Exclusion criteria comprised patients who were pregnant (due to altered glycemic dynamics and HbA1c targets in gestational diabetes), individuals with severe cognitive impairments or acute psychiatric illnesses that prevented them from providing informed consent or reliably completing the interview, and patients diagnosed with other forms of diabetes (such as Type 1 or secondary diabetes).

To select the sample of 152 patients from the sampling frame of 250, a systematic random sampling protocol was applied. First, the complete registry list of all 250 active T2DM patients was retrieved from the clinic database to serve as the sampling frame. The sampling interval (k) was calculated as k = N / n = 250 / 152 ≈ 1.64. To maintain random equity, a sampling interval of approximately 1.6 was utilized by alternating selection steps of 1 and 2. A starting number between 1 and 2 was selected at random (patient 2). Subsequently, every alternating first or second patient who arrived at the clinic for their scheduled follow-up visits was invited to participate until the sample size of 152 was fulfilled.

Study Variables and Data Collection

To collect data on sociodemographic, lifestyle factors, and clinical history, a new structured questionnaire was developed by modifying and integrating elements from several validated global surveillance tools. Sociodemographic characteristics and tobacco use questions were modified from the WHO STEPwise approach to non-communicable disease risk factor surveillance (STEPS) questionnaire [16]. Physical activity questions were adapted from the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ) [17], while dietary habit items were adapted from the Summary of Diabetes Self-Care Activities (SDSCA) scale [18] and tailored to reflect regional Somali culinary staples.

To evaluate the methodological quality of this newly developed instrument, the study utilized expert judgment and test-retest methods to assess validity and reliability, respectively. Content validity was established through evaluation by two independent experts who reviewed the consolidated 23-item questionnaire. Based on their consensus, 20 items were rated as highly relevant, yielding a Content Validity Index (CVI) of 0.86, which exceeds the standard threshold of 0.70. Furthermore, the instrument's stability was evaluated using a test-retest method with a pilot sample of 30 patients over a 14-day interval. This analysis yielded a reliability coefficient of 0.81, indicating acceptable consistency over time. The finalized questionnaire was administered by trained interviewers. The independent variables included sociodemographic characteristics (age, gender, education level, and employment status) and lifestyle-related determinants, including physical activity, dietary habits, and tobacco use, which were classified based on standardized international guidelines:

1.Physical Activity: Categorized into Good, Moderate, or Poor based on the WHO Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ) and WHO guidelines [17]. Good was defined as engaging in at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity or at least 75 minutes of vigorous-intensity aerobic physical activity per week. Moderate was defined as participating in some physical movement but falling below the 150-minute threshold. Poor was defined as complete physical inactivity or sedentary behavior.

The dependent variable was glycemic control, for which the most recent HbA1c value was extracted from each patient's medical record. Glycemic control was dichotomized as Good Control (HbA1c ≤ 7.0%) and Poor Control (HbA1c > 7.0%), in line with the American Diabetes Association guidelines [6].

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 25.0. Descriptive statistics summarized participant characteristics, while bivariate analysis using the Chi-square (χ2) test assessed the associations between the independent variables and glycemic control status. Variables with a p-value < 0.20 in the bivariate analysis were entered into a multivariate binary logistic regression model to identify the independent predictors of poor glycemic control. Adjusted Odds Ratios (AOR) with 95% Confidence Intervals (CI) were calculated, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Amoud University Research Ethics Committee (Approval Reference: AU-CHS-REC-2023/12). Written administrative permission was granted by the hospital administration and the Ministry of Health under permission registry number BRH-225/2024/I. All participants provided written informed consent after being briefed on the study's objectives, with strict anonymity maintained throughout.

Bivariate Analysis of Factors Associated with Glycemic Control

Table 2 presents the bivariate analysis examining factors associated with glycemic control. The Chi-square test revealed several statistically significant associations with poor glycemic control (HbA1c > 7.0%). Specifically, inadequate physical activity (χ2 = 15.67, p < 0.001) and unhealthy dietary habits (χ2 = 19.34, p < 0.001) were strongly associated with poorer glycemic outcomes. Individuals reporting poor physical activity constituted 77.3% of the poor glycemic control group, compared to 43.6% in the good control group. Similarly, 84.5% of those with poor glycemic control reported unhealthy dietary habits, compared to 49.1% in the good control group.

Multivariate Analysis of Predictors of Poor Glycemic Control

Independent variables that were significant in the bivariate analysis were entered into a binary logistic regression model. The results, presented in Table 3, identified three independent predictors of poor glycemic control. Patients with poor physical activity were nearly four times more likely to have poor glycemic control than active patients (AOR = 3.85, p = 0.002). Those with unhealthy dietary habits were over four and a half times more likely to have poor control (AOR = 4.51, p < 0.001). Furthermore, unemployed or casually employed patients were approximately three times more likely to experience poor glycemic control compared to employed patients, even after adjusting for lifestyle factors (AOR = 2.98, p = 0.014).

DISCUSSION

This investigation examined the lifestyle and socioeconomic factors influencing glycemic control among individuals diagnosed with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) in Borama, Somaliland. The results indicate a high prevalence of suboptimal glycemic control, affecting nearly two-thirds of the study cohort, thereby underscoring a significant public health concern within the region. Consistent with global research evidence, inadequate physical activity, harmful dietary habits, and unemployment were identified as distinct factors contributing to poor glycemic control.

The elevated incidence of suboptimal glycemic control documented in Borama is consistent with findings from other low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). For example, research conducted in neighboring Ethiopia has reported comparable prevalence rates, where more than half to nearly two-thirds of patients exhibit inadequate control 7,8. Furthermore, this aligns with a recent systematic review of glycemic control across East Africa, which reported that suboptimal control remains alarmingly high across the region, consistently ranging from three-fifths to four-fifths of patients [19]. This consistency highlights the widespread challenges faced in the management of T2DM within resource-constrained settings, often attributed to limited access to healthcare services, educational resources, and economically accessible healthy food options.

The revelation that inadequate physical activity substantially heightens the probability of poor glycemic control is well-supported by both international and regional literature [11]. Physical inactivity diminishes insulin sensitivity and hinders glucose utilization, consequently affecting HbA1c levels. Locally, these findings mirror recent evidence from Hargeisa, Somaliland, which identified physical inactivity and a lack of lifestyle modifications as major determinants among diabetic patients [20]. This study accentuates the global significance of regular physical activity in diabetes management, notwithstanding the considerable barriers to its implementation in the local context, as evidenced by a substantial majority of participants reporting insufficient physical activity.

In a similar vein, detrimental dietary practices emerged as a significant predictor of inadequate glycemic control, a conclusion corroborated by extensive scholarly inquiry [12]. The alarming prevalence of unhealthy dietary habits among a large majority of participants presents a substantial concern. This phenomenon may reflect the accessibility and affordability of specific food items, prevailing cultural dietary patterns, and a lack of nutritional education within the community. Addressing this determinant necessitates the implementation of culturally sensitive interventions that advocate for balanced dietary practices, potentially facilitated through community-based nutritional programs.

A significant socioeconomic determinant identified in this study was employment status, with individuals who are unemployed or engaged in casual employment exhibiting nearly threefold higher odds of demonstrating poor glycemic control. This observation substantiates prior research that denotes a robust correlation between socioeconomic status and diabetes-related outcomes 13,14. These findings are highly consistent with broader nationwide analyses in Somaliland, which demonstrate that socio-demographic determinants are profoundly associated with the overall burden of adult chronic diseases [15]. In resource-limited settings across East Africa, socio-economic challenges often restrict financial resources, thereby adversely influencing access to essential medications, nutritious food, and regular healthcare services [19]. Furthermore, such circumstances may elevate stress levels, which can negatively impact glycemic regulation. Notably, although lower educational attainment displayed an association in the bivariate analysis, it did not serve as an independent predictor within the multivariate framework. This implies that employment status may represent a more direct or immediate determinant of glycemic control within this particular population, potentially mediating the influence of education on health behaviors and accessibility. This finding contrasts with certain studies in which education is regarded as a strong independent predictor 9,10, possibly indicating that in Borama, the financial ramifications of employment status eclipse educational attainment as a direct determinant of self-management capabilities.

The principal contributions of this study include providing novel, localized data concerning the determinants of glycemic control in Somaliland, a region characterized by a significant deficit in data about non-communicable diseases. By delineating specific modifiable lifestyle factors and socioeconomic conditions, this research furnishes actionable insights for public health interventions. The findings accentuate the imperative to transcend generic diabetes recommendations in favor of context-specific strategies that address the intertwined challenges posed by lifestyle and socioeconomic disparities.

From a broader viewpoint, these findings directly pertain to multiple Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). Enhancing glycemic control aligns with SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-being), specifically Target 3.4, which aspires to reduce premature mortality from non-communicable diseases by one-third by the year 2030. Moreover, tackling the socioeconomic determinants of inadequate glycemic control, such as unemployment and limited access to nutritious food, contributes to SDG 1 (No Poverty), SDG 2 (Zero Hunger), and SDG 8 (Decent Work and Economic Growth). By underscoring the critical influence of these factors, the study advocates for a multisectoral approach to diabetes management that integrates health interventions with broader socioeconomic development initiatives.

Limitations of the Study

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design of this study captures data at a single point in time, which precludes the establishment of definite temporal or causal relationships between the identified lifestyle and socioeconomic factors and glycemic control. Longitudinal cohorts would be required to validate these associations dynamically. Second, the study sample is relatively small and was recruited from a single regional tertiary facility, potentially restricting the direct generalizability of these findings to private clinics or primary health centers across other regions of Somaliland.

Third, selection bias represents a key limitation given that participants were identified based on enrollment in a specialized diabetes clinic at a public tertiary referral center. Borama Regional Hospital is the primary public facility in the region; however, a substantial portion of the diabetic population in the Awdal region manages their condition through private clinics and pharmacies, or remains undiagnosed. Currently, the exact proportion of the regional diabetic population actively followed in structured clinics remains unknown due to the lack of a centralized health registry. This clinical setting introduces dual selection bias implications for our findings: on one hand, clinic-enrolled patients have access to structured follow-up, specialist consultations, and education, which could mean their glycemic control is better than that of unmanaged patients in the wider community. On the other hand, tertiary clinics frequently act as referral magnets for severe, uncontrolled, or medically complicated cases, which may have led to an overestimation of the prevalence of poor glycemic control reported here. Readers should carefully consider these local healthcare and referral pathways.

However, the sample was systematically drawn from a finite clinic population using standard statistical formulas, meaning it remains highly representative of the active patient base in this specific setting. Future multi-center studies with larger cohorts and longitudinal follow-up are warranted to validate and expand upon these findings.

CONCLUSION

This investigation conducted in Borama, Somaliland, reveals a significant incidence of inadequate glycemic control among patients with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM), which is independently associated with insufficient physical activity, poor dietary practices, and unemployment. These results underscore the urgent necessity for targeted, culturally sensitive interventions that effectively address both lifestyle modifications and the underlying socioeconomic obstacles. Comprehensive strategies should include health education initiatives that promote active lifestyles and nutritional balance, in conjunction with larger-scale efforts aimed at enhancing economic stability and access to resources for at-risk populations. Tackling these determinants is imperative not only for improving individual health outcomes but also for advancing regional public health and facilitating the attainment of global health-related Sustainable Development Goals.
