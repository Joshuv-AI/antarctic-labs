Backup-Based Safety Filters: A Comparative Review of Backup CBF, Model Predictive Shielding, and gatekeeper
Taekyung Kim, Aswin D. Menon, Akshunn Trivedi, Dimitra Panagou

Abstract
This paper revisits three backup-based safety filters—Backup Control Barrier Functions (Backup CBF), Model Predictive Shielding (MPS), and gatekeeper —through a unified comparative framework. Using a common safety-filter abstraction and shared notation, we make explicit both their common backup-policy structure and their key algorithmic differences. We compare the three methods through their filter-inactive sets, i.e., the states where the nominal policy is left unchanged. In particular, we show that MPS is a special case of gatekeeper , and we further relate gatekeeper to the interior of the Backup CBF inactive set within the implicit safe set. This unified view also highlights a key source of conservatism in backup-based safety filters: safety is often evaluated through the feasibility of a backup maneuver, rather than through the nominal policy’s continued safe execution. The paper is intended as a compact tutorial and review that clarifies the theoretical connections and differences among these methods. INTRODUCTION

Ensuring safety in autonomous systems with nonlinear dynamics remains a central challenge in robotics and cyber-physical systems. Modern planning and control modules, including reinforcement learning (RL)
[
21
,
14
]
and sampling-based motion planning
[

safety filter
between the nominal decision-making algorithms and the plant
[
13
]
. The safety filter monitors the nominal command and modifies it only when necessary to preserve safety. Ideally, this intervention is minimally invasive: the nominal controller should remain active whenever its use is certifiably compatible with future safe operation.

Historically, one route to such guarantees has been reachability analysis, which, although rigorous, is computationally prohibitive for high-dimensional nonlinear systems in real time
[
5
]
. Control Barrier Functions (CBFs) provide a tractable alternative by encoding a safety condition that is imposed pointwise in state and time through an optimization problem, typically formulated as a quadratic program (QP)
[
4
]
. The main difficulty, however, is constructing a CBF that enforces invariance for nonlinear systems with input constraints
[
11
]
; a geometric safety constraint alone need not guarantee the existence of a control input that prevents future constraint violation
[
16
,
15
]
.

This observation motivates
backup-based
safety filters
[
13
]
. Rather than beginning with an explicit analytical barrier function, these methods begin with a known backup maneuver, referred to here as a
backup policy
. Safety is certified by showing that, from the current state or from a predicted future state, the system can switch to that backup policy, remain inside the safe set, and reach a terminal controlled invariant set. Backup CBF
[
8
]
, Model Predictive Shielding (MPS)
[
7
,
6
]
, and
gatekeeper

[

The contribution of this paper is expository and comparative. We revisit Backup CBF, MPS, and
gatekeeper
within a unified framework that makes their common structure and key differences explicit (see
Table I
). First, we define a common safety-filter abstraction and trajectory notation and restate the three methods using consistent notation. Second, we raise a structural limitation in backup-based safety filters, which we call
safety evaluation on backup
: safety is often evaluated through the feasibility of a backup maneuver, rather than through the nominal policy’s continued safe execution. By comparing the three methods through their filter-inactive sets, we show that

1

II
PROBLEM FORMULATION

II-A

Mathematical Setup

We consider a continuous-time control-affine system

\dot{{\bm{x}}}=f({\bm{x}})+g({\bm{x}}){\bm{u}},

(1)

where
{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the state and
{\bm{u}}\in\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the control input, with
\mathcal{U}
denoting the admissible input set.
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
g:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
are locally Lipschitz. We assume a nominal policy

\pi_{\textup{nom}}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{U}
that is task-oriented but not necessarily safe. The safe set and terminal set used throughout the paper are defined as:

\mathcal{C}\coloneqq\{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}\mid h^{\mathcal{C}}({\bm{x}})\geq 0\},

(2)

\mathcal{S}_{0}\coloneqq\{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}\mid h^{\mathcal{S}}({\bm{x}})\geq 0\}\subseteq\mathcal{C},

(3)

where
h^{\mathcal{C}},h^{\mathcal{S}}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
are continuously differentiable.

For any locally Lipschitz feedback policy (or controller)
\pi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{U}
, let
\varphi_{t}^{\pi}({\bm{x}}_{0})
denote the state at time
t\geq 0
of the closed-loop system
\dot{{\bm{x}}}=f({\bm{x}})+g({\bm{x}})\pi({\bm{x}})
with initial condition
{\bm{x}}(0)={\bm{x}}_{0}
.
A set
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is
forward invariant
under
\pi
if
\varphi_{t}^{\pi}({\bm{x}}_{0})\in\mathcal{A}
for every
{\bm{x}}_{0}\in\mathcal{A}
and all
t\geq 0
. The set
\mathcal{A}
is
controlled invariant
if there exists a feedback policy that renders
\mathcal{A}
forward invariant. We assume that
\mathcal{S}_{0}
is a known terminal controlled invariant set; equivalently, there exists a suitable backup policy that renders
\mathcal{S}_{0}
forward invariant.

We next introduce two definitions that provide the common language used throughout the paper. We first define the recoverable set induced by a general feedback policy.

Definition 1

(Recoverable set induced by a feedback policy)
.

Let
\pi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{U}
be a feedback policy, let
\mathcal{S}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
,
and let
T>0
. The
recoverable set
of
\mathcal{S}_{0}
under
\pi
over horizon
T
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{\pi}(T;\mathcal{S}_{0})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{{\bm{x}}_{0}\in\mathcal{C}\mid\varphi_{t}^{\pi}({\bm{x}}_{0})\in\mathcal{C},\ \forall t\in[0,T],

(4)

\displaystyle\varphi_{T}^{\pi}({\bm{x}}_{0})\in\mathcal{S}_{0}\}.

Thus,
\mathcal{R}^{\pi}(T;\mathcal{S}_{0})
is the set of all initial states from which the policy
\pi

keeps the system inside
\mathcal{C}
over
[0,T]
and reaches
\mathcal{S}_{0}
at time
T
.

We define a backup policy based on the recoverable set.

Definition 2

(Backup policy)
.

Let
\mathcal{S}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
and let
T_{B}>0
. A feedback policy

\pi_{\textup{b}}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{U}
is called a
backup policy
for
\mathcal{S}_{0}

if the following two properties hold:

(i)
The recoverable set induced by
\pi_{\textup{b}}
at horizon
T_{B}
, denoted
\mathcal{R}^{\pi_{\textup{b}}}(T_{B};\mathcal{S}_{0})
, contains a neighborhood of
\mathcal{S}_{0}
.

(ii)
The set
\mathcal{S}_{0}
is forward invariant under
\pi_{\textup{b}}
.

In words, a backup policy keeps the system inside
\mathcal{S}_{0}
once it is there, and can also drive all states in a surrounding recoverable region
\mathcal{R}^{\pi_{\textup{b}}}(T_{B};\mathcal{S}_{0})
into
\mathcal{S}_{0}
within the fixed recovery time
T_{B}
without leaving the safe set
\mathcal{C}
.

This distinction will be useful later. Backup CBF uses the recoverable set induced by the backup policy
\mathcal{R}^{\pi_{\textup{b}}}(T_{B};\mathcal{S}_{0})
as an implicit safe set. By contrast, MPS and
gatekeeper
use the backup policy only through an online validity check that determines when the controller should switch to backup. We make this difference explicit in the subsequent sections.

II-B

Problem Statement

We now introduce a unified notion of
(backup-based) safety filter
, and then state the formal comparison problem studied in this paper.

Definition 3

(Safety filter)
.

Given a nominal policy
\pi_{\textup{nom}}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{U}
, a backup policy

\pi_{\textup{b}}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{U}
, a safe set
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, a terminal controlled invariant set
\mathcal{S}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
, and a tuple of algorithm-specific hyperparameters
\Theta_{\textup{sf}}
, a policy
\pi_{\textup{sf}}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{U}
is called a
safety filter
for System (
1
) if, for every state
{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{C}
, the mapping

{\bm{u}}^{\star}=\pi_{\textup{sf}}\big({\bm{x}};\pi_{\textup{nom}},\pi_{\textup{b}},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{S}_{0},\Theta_{\textup{sf}}\big)\in\mathcal{U}
results in
\varphi_{t}^{\pi_{\textup{sf}}}({\bm{x}})\in\mathcal{C},\,\forall t\geq 0.

Definition 4

(Filter-inactive set)
.

Let
\pi_{\textup{sf}}
be a safety filter. Its
filter-inactive set
is

\mathcal{I}_{\textup{sf}}\coloneqq\left\{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{C}\ \middle|\ \pi_{\textup{sf}}\big({\bm{x}};\pi_{\textup{nom}},\pi_{\textup{b}},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{S}_{0},\Theta_{\textup{sf}}\big)=\pi_{\textup{nom}}({\bm{x}})\right\}.

(5)

Thus,
{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{I}_{\textup{sf}}
if and only if the safety filter leaves the nominal control input unchanged at that query state
{\bm{x}}
.

In this paper, we focus on the three safety filters induced by Backup CBF, MPS, and

gatekeeper
, denoted by
\pi_{\textup{BCBF}}
,
\pi_{\textup{MPS}}
, and
\pi_{\textup{GK}}
,
respectively. Our goal is to compare these three filters within the common abstraction of

Def. 3
, with particular emphasis on the set of states where each filter leaves the nominal control input unchanged. Here,
\mathcal{I}_{\textup{BCBF}}
,
\mathcal{I}_{\textup{MPS}}
, and
\mathcal{I}_{\textup{GK}}
denote the filter-inactive sets of
\pi_{\textup{BCBF}}
,
\pi_{\textup{MPS}}
, and
\pi_{\textup{GK}}
, respectively.

Problem 1
.

Given the three safety filters
\pi_{\textup{BCBF}},\pi_{\textup{MPS}}
, and
\pi_{\textup{GK}}
, characterize the set relationships among their filter-inactive sets. In particular, establish the inclusion
\mathcal{I}_{\textup{MPS}}\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{\textup{GK}}
(see
Thm. 2
), and characterize the relation between
\mathcal{I}_{\textup{GK}}
and
\operatorname{int}_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathcal{I}_{\textup{BCBF}})
, the relative interior of the Backup CBF inactive set within the implicit safe set
\mathcal{S}
(see
Thm. 3
).

A larger filter-inactive set means that the safety filter intervenes less often on the nominal policy while preserving its own safety guarantee. The remainder of the paper reviews Backup CBF, MPS, and
gatekeeper
within the unified framework above, and then proves the set inclusions posed in
Problem 1
.

III
REVIEW OF BACKUP CBF

Backup CBF
[
8
]

\pi_{\textup{BCBF}}
defines the implicit safe set as the recoverable set induced by the backup policy, and then solves a QP to keep the state inside that set. In contrast to MPS and
gatekeeper
, the output of Backup CBF online is not a binary choice between
\pi_{\textup{nom}}
and
\pi_{\textup{b}}
; instead, it is the closest admissible input to the nominal input that satisfies the barrier constraints.

III-A

Implicit safe set induced by the backup policy

Given the backup policy
\pi_{\textup{b}}
and a backup horizon
T_{B}>0
, Backup CBF defines the implicit safe set

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\coloneqq\mathcal{R}^{\pi_{\textup{b}}}(T_{B};\mathcal{S}_{0})=

\displaystyle\{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{C}\mid\forall\tau\in[0,T_{B}],

(6)

\displaystyle\varphi_{\tau}^{\pi_{\textup{b}}}({\bm{x}})\in\mathcal{C},\ \varphi_{T_{B}}^{\pi_{\textup{b}}}({\bm{x}})\in\mathcal{S}_{0}\}.

By
Def. 1
, the set
\mathcal{S}
is the recoverable set induced by the backup policy
\pi_{\textup{b}}
over horizon
[0,T_{B}]
. It consists of all states from which immediate execution of the backup policy remains inside
\mathcal{C}
and reaches
\mathcal{S}_{0}
after time
T_{B}
.

Theorem 1

(
[
12
]
)
.

Assume that
\mathcal{S}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
is forward invariant under the backup policy
\pi_{\textup{b}}
. Then, for every
T_{B}>0
, the set
\mathcal{S}
in (
6
) is a controlled invariant set and satisfies
\mathcal{S}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
.

In the Backup CBF construction, the feedback law is provided online by the quadratic program below. The horizon
T_{B}
determines how much the terminal set
\mathcal{S}_{0}
is enlarged: when
T_{B}=0
, one recovers
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{0}
, whereas increasing
T_{B}
enlarges the implicit safe set.

III-B

Backup CBF-QP

The Backup CBF
h_{\textup{BCBF}}
is defined from the minimum safety margin observed along the predicted backup trajectory:

h_{\textup{BCBF}}({\bm{x}})\coloneqq\min\left\{h^{\mathcal{S}}\left(\varphi_{T_{B}}^{\pi_{\textup{b}}}({\bm{x}})\right),\min_{\tau\in[0,T_{B}]}h^{\mathcal{C}}\left(\varphi_{\tau}^{\pi_{\textup{b}}}({\bm{x}})\right)\right\}.

By construction, the
0
-superlevel set of
h_{\textup{BCBF}}
coincides with the implicit safe set in (
6
).

To implement the Backup CBF online,
h_{\textup{BCBF}}
is written as a function of time along the current closed-loop trajectory. At time
t
, with current state
{\bm{x}}(t)
, define

\displaystyle h_{\textup{BCBF}}(t)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\min\Bigl\{h^{\mathcal{S}}\bigl(\varphi_{T_{B}}^{\pi_{\textup{b}}}({\bm{x}}(t))\bigr),

(7)

\displaystyle\min_{\tau\in[t,t+T_{B}]}h^{\mathcal{C}}\bigl(\varphi_{\tau-t}^{\pi_{\textup{b}}}({\bm{x}}(t))\bigr)\Bigr\}.

The corresponding CBF constraint is

\dot{h}_{\textup{BCBF}}(t)+\alpha\!\left(h_{\textup{BCBF}}(t)\right)\geq 0,

(8)

where
\alpha
is a class-
\mathcal{K}
function. Following
[
8
]
, Backup CBF does not enforce (
8
) directly on the minimum in (
7
); instead, it enforces sufficient constraints on the individual terms appearing in that minimum.

Backup CBF computes the filtered input by solving the following QP:

\displaystyle\quad{\bm{u}}^{\star}_{\textup{BCBF}}=\ \pi_{\textup{BCBF}}\big({\bm{x}};\pi_{\textup{nom}},\pi_{\textup{b}},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{S}_{0},T_{B},\alpha\big)

(9a)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\ \coloneqq\ \argmin_{{\bm{u}}\in\mathcal{U}}\ \|{\bm{u}}-{\bm{u}}_{\textup{nom}}\|_{2}^{2}

s.t.

\displaystyle\forall\tau\in[t,t+T_{B}]

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{d}h^{\mathcal{C}}\!\left(\varphi_{\tau-t}^{\pi_{\textup{b}}}({\bm{x}})\right)}{\operatorname{d}t}({\bm{x}},{\bm{u}})+\alpha\!\left(h^{\mathcal{C}}\!\left(\varphi_{\tau-t}^{\pi_{\textup{b}}}({\bm{x}})\right)\right)\geq 0,

(9b)

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{d}h^{\mathcal{S}}\!\left(\varphi_{T_{B}}^{\pi_{\textup{b}}}({\bm{x}}\right)}{\operatorname{d}t}({\bm{x}},{\bm{u}})+\alpha\!\left(h^{\mathcal{S}}\!\left(\varphi_{T_{B}}^{\pi_{\textup{b}}}({\bm{x}})\right)\right)\geq 0,

(9c)

where
{\bm{u}}_{\textup{nom}}\coloneqq\pi_{\textup{nom}}({\bm{x}})
is the nominal input. The derivatives in (
9b
)-(
9c
) are taken with respect to the current system dynamics
\dot{{\bm{x}}}=f({\bm{x}})+g({\bm{x}}){\bm{u}}
at the current state
{\bm{x}}(t)
.

Let
\mathcal{I}_{\textup{BCBF}}
denote the filter-inactive set of
\pi_{\textup{BCBF}}
as in
Def. 4
. Equivalently,
{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{I}_{\textup{BCBF}}
if and only if the nominal input
{\bm{u}}_{\textup{nom}}
already satisfies all constraints in (
9b
)-(
9c
).

Thus, unlike MPS and
gatekeeper
, Backup CBF uses the backup policy only to construct the implicit safe set and the associated constraints; the control input applied online is the QP solution (
9
), not the backup policy itself. In implementation, the continuum of constraints in (
9b
) is approximated by finitely many collocation points
[
8
]
.

IV
REVIEW OF MODEL PREDICTIVE SHIELDING

Model Predictive Shielding (MPS)
[
7
]

\pi_{\textup{MPS}}
provides the second instantiation of
Def. 3
. The original development of the method is for discrete-time systems. For comparison with Backup CBF and
gatekeeper
, we restate its logic in continuous time, with the digital update made explicit, while keeping the algorithmic mechanism unchanged. The plant state evolves continuously, but the monitor is queried only at discrete update times.

The original MPS presentation separates the backup mechanism into a recovery component and an invariance component
[
7
]
. For the purposes of the present paper, we absorb both into the single backup policy
\pi_{\textup{b}}
introduced in
Def. 2
. This keeps the comparison with Backup CBF and
gatekeeper
at the level of the shared safety-filter abstraction.

Let

t_{k}\coloneqq t_{0}+k\Delta t

(Candidate trajectory)

REVIEW OF GATEKEEPER

gatekeeper

[
3
]

\pi_{\textup{GK}}

to the backup is treated as a decision variable rather than being fixed a priori.

V-A

The problem of “safety evaluation on backup”

A common source of conservatism in backup-based safety filters is that safety is evaluated along the backup trajectory, rather than along the nominal trajectory itself. As a result, the filter may modify the nominal control input
{\bm{u}}_{\textup{nom}}
even when continued execution of the nominal policy would in fact remain safe.

In Backup CBF, this dependence appears explicitly in the backup-flow terms
[
8
]
of the constraints (
9b
)-(
9c
). In MPS, it appears through the validity check (
Def. 6
) applied to the candidate trajectory after a fixed nominal execution time
\Delta t
. At update time
t_{k}
, MPS checks only whether
\operatorname{Valid}({\bm{x}}_{k};\Delta t)=1
, i.e., whether following the nominal policy for one update interval and then switching to the backup policy remains in
\mathcal{C}
and reaches
\mathcal{S}_{0}
. If this test fails, MPS suppresses the nominal policy and applies the backup policy instead.

This can be conservative when backup becomes feasible only after additional nominal progress. A state may fail the validity test for an early switch to backup even though continued nominal execution for a longer duration would move the system to a state from which the backup maneuver is valid. The key limitation is therefore not the use of a backup policy itself, but fixing the switching time a priori.
gatekeeper
reduces this conservatism by searching over multiple switching times instead of fixing the switch at
\Delta t
.

V-B

Maximizing the switching time

Let
T_{H}>0
denote the search horizon for the nominal segment. At an update state
{\bm{x}}_{k}
,
gatekeeper
computes

T_{S}^{\star}({\bm{x}}_{k})\coloneqq\sup\{T_{S}\in[0,T_{H}]\mid\operatorname{Valid}({\bm{x}}_{k};T_{S})=1\}.

(14)

Thus,
T_{S}^{\star}({\bm{x}}_{k})
is the longest certified duration for which the nominal policy can continue before switching to the backup policy. The corresponding safety filter, with dependence on
\pi_{\textup{nom}},\pi_{\textup{b}},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{S}_{0},T_{B}
, and
T_{H}
suppressed for brevity, is

\pi_{\textup{GK}}({\bm{x}}_{k})=\begin{cases}\pi_{\textup{nom}}({\bm{x}}_{k}),&T_{S}^{\star}({\bm{x}}_{k})>0,\\
\pi_{\textup{b}}({\bm{x}}_{k}),&T_{S}^{\star}({\bm{x}}_{k})=0.\end{cases}

(15)

Hence,
gatekeeper
leaves the nominal input unchanged on the set

\mathcal{I}_{\textup{GK}}=\left\{{\bm{x}}_{k}\in\mathcal{C}\mid T_{S}^{\star}({\bm{x}}_{k})>0\right\}=\bigcup_{T_{S}\in(0,T_{H}]}\mathcal{V}(T_{S};T_{B}).

(16)

Thus,
gatekeeper
accepts the nominal controller whenever there exists some positive certified duration of nominal execution before switching to backup.

V-C

Update times and search strategy

The decision rule in (
15
) describes the behavior at an update instant. The implementation itself is stateful: once a positive switching time has been certified,
gatekeeper
may continue executing the nominal policy over subsequent monitor updates without recomputing the full search, until that certificate must be refreshed. In
Alg. 2
, this is represented by storing the previously certified value of
T_{S}^{\star}
and decrementing it by
\Delta t
between successive updates when no new search is triggered.

In practice, the continuous search in (
14
) is approximated on the grid
\mathcal{T}_{H}\coloneqq\{0,\Delta t,2\Delta t,\dots,N_{H}\Delta t\},\,T_{H}=N_{H}\Delta t
, and the implementation selects

T_{S}^{\star}({\bm{x}}_{k})\coloneqq\max\{T_{S}\in\mathcal{T}_{H}\mid\operatorname{Valid}({\bm{x}}_{k};T_{S})=1\}.

(17)

The original implementation
[
3
]
in
Alg. 2
performs this search sequentially, starting from the largest candidate and terminating once a valid switching time is found. The decision logic is, however, parallelizable, since for a fixed current state
{\bm{x}}_{k}
, the values
\operatorname{Valid}({\bm{x}}_{k};T_{S})
for different
T_{S}\in\mathcal{T}_{H}
can be evaluated independently. Therefore, we propose to evaluate all candidate trajectories in parallel and then choose the largest valid switching time in the discretized set, without changing the underlying algorithmic logic.

We refer interested readers to
[
1
,
3
]
for the original
gatekeeper
formulation and
[
2
]
for further theoretical discussion, and to
[
17
,
20
,
9
]
for additional applications in broader robotic settings.

VI
THEORETICAL ANALYSIS

We compare the three filters through their filter-inactive sets, where each method leaves the nominal policy unchanged. This viewpoint is directly aligned with the principle of minimal intervention. For MPS and
gatekeeper
, the comparison is made at update instants at which a new validity check is performed from the current state.

VI-A

MPS is a special case of
gatekeeper

The relation between MPS and
gatekeeper
is exact because both methods use the same candidate trajectory and the same validity indicator; they differ only in the set of switching times they examine.

Theorem 2
.

Assume that
0<\Delta t\leq T_{H}
. Then

\mathcal{I}_{\textup{MPS}}\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{\textup{GK}}.

(18)

Proof.

Take any
{\bm{x}}_{k}\in\mathcal{I}_{\textup{MPS}}
. By (
13
), this is equivalent to
\operatorname{Valid}({\bm{x}}_{k};\Delta t)=1
. Since
\Delta t\in(0,T_{H}]
, the switching time
T_{S}=\Delta t
is feasible for the
gatekeeper
search in (
14
). Therefore
T_{S}^{\star}({\bm{x}}_{k})\geq\Delta t>0
, which implies
{\bm{x}}_{k}\in\mathcal{I}_{\textup{GK}}
by (
16
).
∎

Thm. 2
gives the precise sense in which
gatekeeper
is less intervening than MPS under identical safety assumptions: every state accepted by MPS is also accepted by
gatekeeper
, and
gatekeeper
may additionally accept states that become recoverable only after a later switch to the backup policy.

VI-B

Relation with Backup CBF

We next analyze the relation between Backup CBF and
gatekeeper
. Unlike MPS, the connection is indirect: Backup CBF does not verify nominal-then-backup switched trajectories online, but instead renders the implicit safe set
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{R}^{\pi_{\textup{b}}}(T_{B};\mathcal{S}_{0})
forward invariant. The relevant comparison is therefore with the portion of the Backup CBF inactive set that lies strictly inside
\mathcal{S}
. We denote by
\operatorname{int}_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathcal{I}_{\textup{BCBF}})
the relative interior of
\mathcal{I}_{\textup{BCBF}}
in
\mathcal{S}
, i.e.,

\operatorname{int}_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathcal{I}_{\textup{BCBF}})\coloneqq\left\{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{S}\mid\exists\varepsilon>0\text{ s.t. }B_{\varepsilon}({\bm{x}})\cap\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{\textup{BCBF}}\right\},

(19)

where
B_{\varepsilon}({\bm{x}})\coloneqq\{{\bm{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\|{\bm{z}}-{\bm{x}}\|_{2}<\varepsilon\}
. We compare
gatekeeper
with this set.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\operatorname{int}_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathcal{I}_{\textup{BCBF}})
be as defined in (
19
). Then,

\operatorname{int}_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathcal{I}_{\textup{BCBF}})\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{\textup{GK}}.

(20)

Figure
2
provides a geometric illustration of the local argument used in the proof.

Proof.

Let
{\bm{x}}_{k}\in\operatorname{int}_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathcal{I}_{\textup{BCBF}})
be arbitrary. We show that
{\bm{x}}_{k}\in\mathcal{I}_{\textup{GK}}
.

By (
19
), there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
B_{\varepsilon}({\bm{x}}_{k})\cap\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{\textup{BCBF}}
. Since Backup CBF renders
\mathcal{S}
forward invariant and
{\bm{x}}_{k}\in\mathcal{S}
, we have
\varphi_{\tau}^{\pi_{\textup{BCBF}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})\in\mathcal{S}
for all
\tau\geq 0
. Continuity of
\varphi_{\tau}^{\pi_{\textup{BCBF}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})
with respect to
\tau
and the fact that
\varphi_{0}^{\pi_{\textup{BCBF}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})={\bm{x}}_{k}
imply that there exists
\overline{T}>0
such that
\varphi_{\tau}^{\pi_{\textup{BCBF}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})\in B_{\varepsilon}({\bm{x}}_{k})
for all
\tau\in[0,\overline{T}]
. Hence

\varphi_{\tau}^{\pi_{\textup{BCBF}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})\in B_{\varepsilon}({\bm{x}}_{k})\cap\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{\textup{BCBF}},\quad\forall\tau\in[0,\overline{T}].

(21)

By definition of
\mathcal{I}_{\textup{BCBF}}
, Backup CBF is inactive at every state along this trajectory segment. Since
\pi_{\textup{nom}}
is locally Lipschitz, the nominal closed-loop solution is unique, and thus

\varphi_{\tau}^{\pi_{\textup{BCBF}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})=\varphi_{\tau}^{\pi_{\textup{nom}}}({\bm{x}}_{k}),\qquad\forall\tau\in[0,\overline{T}].

(22)

Because
\varphi_{\tau}^{\pi_{\textup{BCBF}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})\in\mathcal{S}
for all
\tau\geq 0
, (
22
) yields

\varphi_{\tau}^{\pi_{\textup{nom}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})\in\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{C},\qquad\forall\tau\in[0,\overline{T}].

(23)

Choose any
\overline{T}_{S}\in(0,\min\{\overline{T},T_{H}\}]
and define
{\bm{x}}_{S}\coloneqq\varphi_{\overline{T}_{S}}^{\pi_{\textup{nom}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})
. By (
23
), we have
{\bm{x}}_{S}\in\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{R}^{\pi_{\textup{b}}}(T_{B};\mathcal{S}_{0})
. Hence, by the definition of the recoverable set, applying
\pi_{\textup{b}}
from
{\bm{x}}_{S}
keeps the state in
\mathcal{C}
over
[0,T_{B}]
and reaches
\mathcal{S}_{0}
at time
T_{B}
. Together with (
23
), this shows that the candidate trajectory that follows
\pi_{\textup{nom}}
for
\overline{T}_{S}
and then
\pi_{\textup{b}}
for
T_{B}
is valid, i.e.,
\operatorname{Valid}({\bm{x}}_{k};\overline{T}_{S},T_{B})=1
. Therefore
{\bm{x}}_{k}\in\mathcal{V}(\overline{T}_{S};T_{B})\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{\textup{GK}}
by (
16
). Since
{\bm{x}}_{k}
was arbitrary, it follows that
\operatorname{int}_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathcal{I}_{\textup{BCBF}})\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{\textup{GK}}
.
∎

Thm. 3
.

Thm. 3
identifies the region on which Backup CBF and
gatekeeper
agree on nominal acceptance. Every interior point of the Backup CBF inactive set admits a nontrivial nominal segment that remains inside
\mathcal{S}
, and this is sufficient for
gatekeeper
to certify a valid switching time. Note that boundary points of
\mathcal{I}_{\textup{BCBF}}
need not admit such a positive nominal hold time, so a full inclusion
\mathcal{I}_{\textup{BCBF}}\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{\textup{GK}}
cannot be claimed in general.

VI-C

Limitations

While
gatekeeper
reduces the conservatism associated with a fixed switching time, it does not eliminate the fundamental limitations of backup-based safety filtering.

•

Dependence on horizon and search grid.
In the implemented algorithm, if
T_{H}
is too short or if the grid
\mathcal{T}_{H}
is too coarse,
gatekeeper
can collapse to behavior that is nearly indistinguishable from MPS. Increasing
T_{H}
or refining the grid improves permissiveness but increases computational effort.

•

Dependence on the backup policy and model.
All three methods inherit their
conservatism from the quality of the backup policy and from the accuracy of the predictive model. A poor or overly aggressive backup policy shrinks the recoverable region and can mask the true advantage of a less intrusive switching rule.

Planar Double Integrator

We first study a planar double-integrator example. The state is

y=-2

\|{\bm{u}}\|_{2}\leq 2.0~\textup{m/s}^{2}

\mathcal{S}_{0}

T_{H}=10.0~\textup{s}

T_{B}=12.0~\textup{s}

The nominal policy is a PD controller that drives the robot toward the goal along the hallway centerline. VIII
CONCLUSION

This review paper revisited Backup CBF, MPS, and
gatekeeper
under a common safety-filter abstraction. Our unified view provides a new perspective for comparing backup-based safety filters through their filter-inactive sets. It also highlights an important and still underappreciated issue shared by backup-based safety filters, which we refer to as
safety evaluation on backup
: the nominal policy is evaluated myopically through the backup maneuver. Finally, we showed that
gatekeeper
introduces an additional degree of freedom through the switching time, which can mitigate this issue and substantially increase the duration for which the nominal policy can be executed safely.
