Unexpected hepatic lymphangiography during transcatheter arterial chemoembolization in a patient with hepatocellular carcinoma: a case report
Authors: Takaaki Mori, Hajime Takaki, Yasunari Yamada, Ryoichi Narita, Yoshiki Asayama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVisualization of hepatic lymphatic vessels during transcatheter arterial chemoembolization (TACE) for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is uncommon. A woman in her 70s with alcoholic liver cirrhosis and type 2 diabetes mellitus underwent TACE for recurrent HCC in segment 6 after treatment for cholangitis. The procedure was performed via the left distal radial artery using a 5-F angiographic catheter and a 1.9-F microcatheter. During Lipiodol injection into the tumor-feeding artery, abnormal opacification extending toward the hepatic hilum was observed, raising suspicion for arterio-lymphatic communication; therefore, the procedure was discontinued, although the patient remained asymptomatic. Follow-up computed tomography performed 1 month later revealed Lipiodol accumulation within the hepatic lymphatic vessels and in the abdominal, mediastinal, and supraclavicular lymph nodes, with no adverse clinical events during follow-up. Repeat TACE performed 6 months later for the same HCC showed no lymphatic opacification, suggesting that the previous finding represented a transient arterio-lymphatic communication, possibly related to intrahepatic lymphatic dilatation associated with liver cirrhosis and tissue injury caused by cholangitis. Recognition of this phenomenon is important for interventional radiologists to appropriately interpret intraprocedural findings and guide procedural decision-making during TACE.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, Transcatheter arterial chemoembolization, Hepatic lymphatics, Lipiodol, Cholangitis, Cirrhosis

Introduction

Transcatheter arterial chemoembolization (TACE) is a well-established treatment for unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), providing targeted delivery of chemotherapeutic agents and embolization of tumor-feeding arteries [[1], [2], [3]]. Iodized oil (Lipiodol Ultra Fluid; Guerbet, Villepinte, France) serves as both a carrier medium and an embolic material, allowing visualization of tumor perfusion and treatment efficacy on imaging [4,5]. Although Lipiodol is expected to remain within the tumor and its feeding arteries, inadvertent migration into non-target vessels such as the portal or hepatic veins can occur, leading to various complications. In one study, arteriovenous shunting developed during treatment in 20 (11.7%) of 171 patients; among these patients, 1 had shunting into the hepatic vein, whereas 19 (11.1%) had arterioportal shunting [6]. However, visualization of the hepatic lymphatic system during TACE remains uncommon; Miyayama et al. [7] reported this finding in only 8 of 255 lesions.

The hepatic lymphatics are normally microscopic channels that drain interstitial fluid toward the hepatoduodenal ligament and thoracic duct [8] and are not typically visualized on angiography.

Here, we present a case of Lipiodol migration into the hepatic lymphatic system through an arterio-lymphatic communication identified during TACE and discuss its practical implications for intraprocedural recognition and management.

Case presentation

A woman in her 70s with alcoholic liver cirrhosis and type 2 diabetes mellitus had a history of multifocal HCC treated with multiple TACE sessions and lenvatinib. Ten months earlier, she had undergone TACE for HCCs in segments 6 and 8, followed by another TACE for a different lesion in segment 8 approximately 4 months later. She subsequently developed cholecystitis and cholangitis due to bile duct stones, which were treated with percutaneous transhepatic gallbladder drainage and biliary stent placement via a segment 5 approach. TACE was performed for recurrent HCC in segment 6 (Fig. 1) 8 days after improvement of cholangitis and cholecystitis.

Arterial-phase axial computed tomography image shows a hyperenhancing lesion in segment 6 of the liver (arrow), consistent with recurrent hepatocellular carcinoma.

The procedure was performed via the left distal radial artery using a 5-F angiographic catheter (Radifocus Glidecath R.A.V.I. MG; Terumo Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) and a 1.9-F microcatheter (Progreat λ; Terumo Corporation). After selective catheterization of the posterior inferior branch of the right hepatic artery supplying the tumor, selective digital subtraction angiography was performed before administration of the Lipiodol–epirubicin emulsion. Serial angiographic images were evaluated until complete disappearance of hepatic parenchymal staining after arterial opacification; this interval was defined as the delayed phase. Tumor staining was observed, but no definite abnormal linear or beaded opacification extending toward the hepatic hilum or hepatoduodenal ligament, suggestive of lymphatic drainage, was identified before Lipiodol administration.

An emulsion of 20 mg of epirubicin and Lipiodol was then slowly injected. During injection of the Lipiodol–epirubicin emulsion, a fluoroscopic spot image showed abnormal opacification extending from the region of the posterior inferior branch of the right hepatic artery toward the hepatic hilum before appreciable accumulation of the emulsion within the HCC, prompting acquisition of cone-beam computed tomography (CT) images (Fig. 2A). Cone-beam CT revealed Lipiodol retention around the posterior inferior branch and within the hepatoduodenal ligament, leading to the diagnosis of Lipiodol migration from the artery to the lymphatic vessels through an arterio-lymphatic communication (Fig. 2B–E). Because further injection was expected to preferentially divert the emulsion into the lymphatic vessels rather than the tumor, the procedure was discontinued.

Imaging findings during transcatheter arterial chemoembolization (TACE). (A) Fluoroscopic spot image obtained during TACE shows abnormal opacification around the posterior inferior branch of the right hepatic artery supplying the tumor, extending toward the hepatic hilum (arrows) before Lipiodol reached the tumor. (B–E) Axial cone-beam computed tomography images show linear and nodular high-attenuation foci of Lipiodol (circles) extending from the hepatic parenchyma in segment 6 toward and beyond the hepatic hilum along the hepatoduodenal ligament. These findings indicate opacification of both intrahepatic and extrahepatic lymphatic vessels.

The patient remained asymptomatic after the procedure. Follow-up CT performed 1 month after TACE confirmed high-attenuation foci in the lymphatic vessels along the posterior inferior branch of the hepatic artery and in the upper abdominal, mediastinal, and supraclavicular lymph nodes, consistent with Lipiodol accumulation (Fig. 3). During the 6-month follow-up period, the patient remained asymptomatic, and no adverse clinical events occurred. Six months later, repeat TACE was performed for the same lesion in segment 6 using the same approach and chemotherapeutic regimen. On this occasion, no opacification of the hepatic lymphatics was observed, and the procedure was completed uneventfully, achieving good local tumor control (Fig. 4).

(E) Volume-rendered reconstruction shows retained Lipiodol (highlighted in pink) distributed along the hepatic lymphatic vessels and the upper abdominal, mediastinal, and supraclavicular lymph nodes, clearly depicting the lymphatic drainage pathway from the liver to the left supraclavicular region.

(A) Fluoroscopic spot image obtained during selective angiography of the posterior inferior branch of the right hepatic artery shows no abnormal opacification of the lymphatic vessels, indicating resolution of the arterio-lymphatic communication observed during the initial TACE procedure. (B) Contrast-enhanced computed tomography obtained after repeat TACE demonstrates adequate accumulation of the Lipiodol–epirubicin emulsion within the segment 6 tumor (arrow), confirming successful chemoembolization.

Discussion

Arterio-lymphatic communication visualized during TACE is a rare phenomenon. In a retrospective study of 255 lesions, Miyayama et al. [7] reported lymphatic opacification in only 8 lesions (3.1%), characterized by opacified lymphatics ascending through the hepatoduodenal ligament with long-term Lipiodol retention and without clinical symptoms other than postembolization syndrome. Hepatic lymphatic channels typically appear beaded or tortuous because of the presence of valves and extend beyond the hepatic hilum toward the hepatoduodenal ligament [7]. Therefore, recognition of such opacification during TACE should raise suspicion for arterio-lymphatic communication. The hepatic lymphatic system is anatomically divided into deep and superficial networks. The deep system accompanies the portal triad—portal veins, bile ducts, and hepatic arteries—within the Glisson sheath, converging at the hepatic hilum and draining into the abdominal lymph nodes and ultimately the thoracic duct [8]. By contrast, the superficial lymphatic system courses along the hepatic capsule and drains toward the diaphragmatic lymph nodes. The liver contributes approximately 25%–50% of the lymph flow entering the thoracic duct. In patients with chronic liver disease, lymph production increases 3- to 6-fold, and the thoracic duct may enlarge 2- to 4-fold in diameter, resulting in marked dilatation and increased permeability of intrahepatic lymphatic vessels [9]. Magnetic resonance imaging studies have also demonstrated dilated periportal lymphatics in cirrhotic livers, correlating with the degree of hepatic fibrosis [10].

In the present case, a transient arterio-lymphatic communication may have developed because of intrahepatic lymphatic dilatation associated with liver cirrhosis and tissue injury caused by cholangitis. These pathological changes likely contributed to fragility of the peribiliary connective tissue and lymphatic channels, facilitating communication between hepatic arterial branches and adjacent lymphatic vessels during the TACE procedure.

The abnormal opacification became apparent during Lipiodol injection and was confirmed on cone-beam CT. The embolic injection was discontinued because further injection was expected to divert the emulsion into the lymphatic vessels rather than the tumor, potentially resulting in insufficient tumor delivery and non-target migration. Although follow-up CT showed widespread Lipiodol retention along the hepatic lymphatic drainage pathway, the patient remained asymptomatic, consistent with prior reports indicating that lymphatic opacification during TACE does not usually result in clinically significant sequelae [7].

In retrospect, the phenomenon may not have been fully predictable in this case because no definite lymphatic opacification was identified on selective digital subtraction angiography before Lipiodol administration. However, in patients with risk factors that may alter the hepatic lymphatic system, such as liver cirrhosis, recent cholangitis, or prior biliary intervention, procedural decision-making should be more cautious. The delayed phase of selective angiography should be carefully reviewed before embolic injection, and early cone-beam CT should be considered when abnormal drainage is suspected. This case also provides several practical teaching points for interventional radiologists. Abnormal linear, beaded, or tortuous opacification extending from a hepatic arterial branch toward the hepatic hilum or hepatoduodenal ligament, particularly before sufficient tumor staining on angiography or substantial Lipiodol accumulation within the tumor, should raise suspicion for arterio-lymphatic communication. Such communication may not always be apparent before Lipiodol administration, as in the present case. Once arterio-lymphatic communication is suspected or recognized, further injection of Lipiodol or similar embolic material should be minimized or discontinued, and cone-beam CT or a staged procedure should be considered to confirm the drainage pathway and avoid reduced tumor delivery and non-target embolization.

Interestingly, repeat TACE performed 6 months later for the same lesion did not reproduce lymphatic opacification, suggesting that the previously observed arterio-lymphatic communication had spontaneously resolved, likely because of local tissue healing and closure of the microscopic fistula. This spontaneous improvement further supports the possibility that such arterio-lymphatic communications may represent transient and benign phenomena.

Conclusion

Hepatic lymphatic opacification during TACE is uncommon but important to recognize. Abnormal linear, beaded, or tortuous opacification extending toward the hepatic hilum or hepatoduodenal ligament should raise suspicion for arterio-lymphatic communication, especially in patients with liver cirrhosis or recent biliary inflammation. When this finding is recognized, additional Lipiodol injection should be minimized or discontinued, and follow-up imaging may be sufficient if the patient remains asymptomatic.
