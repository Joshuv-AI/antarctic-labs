Comparative boundary-layer analysis of Buongiorno nanofluid flow over flat surfaces: Blasius, Sakiadis, stagnation-point, and stretching-sheet configurations
Authors: Muhammad Faisal, Wubshet Ibrahim, IA Badruddin, ASA Zedan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA unified comparative investigation is presented for Buongiorno nanofluid transport in four classical boundary-layer configurations: Blasius, Sakiadis, stagnation-point, and stretching-sheet flows over a heated flat surface. The study aims to identify the most thermally efficient boundary-layer regime by comparing momentum, heat, and nanoparticle transport under identical physical assumptions. The governing equations are formulated using the Buongiorno nanofluid model, incorporating Brownian and thermo diffusions of nanoparticles, and then reduced to coupled nonlinear ordinary differential systems through similarity transformations. The resulting problems are solved numerically using the Keller–Box method. Comparative assessment is performed through the skin-friction coefficient, local Nusselt number, and local Sherwood number. The results show that stagnation-point and stretching-sheet boundary layers produce the strongest wall shear, contributing 41% and 33% of the total skin-friction response, respectively, while Blasius flow exhibits the weakest momentum transport. Stretching-sheet flow delivers the highest heat-transport enhancement, whereas stagnation-point and stretching-sheet configurations exhibit superior mass-transport performance. Brownian motion and thermophoretic effects intensify transport variations most strongly in stagnation-point and stretching-sheet flows. These findings provide a unified physical benchmark for selecting efficient nanofluid boundary-layer configurations in thermal processing and advanced cooling applications.Keywords: Buongiorno nanofluid, Classical boundary-layers, Brownian diffusion, Thermo-diffusion, Keller-Box method, Heat-mass transport

Introduction

A boundary-layer is a thin region of material near a solid surface where viscosity has a significant impact on the flow. Due to the no-slip scenario, material particles touching the surface come to rest, and velocity gradually increases until it reaches the free-stream value outside the layer. Introduced by Ludwig Prandtl in 1904, the boundary-layer concept simplified complex viscous-flow problems by separating the flow into a thin viscous layer near the wall and an outer, nearly inviscid region. This idea explains important phenomena such as drag, heat transport, and flow separation, making it fundamental in aerodynamics and fluid-engineering applications. Tani [1] provided the types and descriptions of the boundary-layer in detail. According to Tani’s theory, the Blasius boundary-layer (BBL) describes the laminar stream that develops over a stationary flat exterior in a uniform free stream, providing a classical similarity solution for boundary-layer thickness and velocity profiles. The Sakiadis boundary layer (SBL) refers to the flow produced by a continuously moving flat plate through an otherwise quiescent liquid, producing a boundary-layer without a defined free-stream velocity. The stagnation-point boundary layer (SPBL) forms where a fluid impinges directly on a surface, creating a symmetric flow with strong deceleration and high wall shear near the stagnation point. A stagnation point is where fluid velocity becomes zero as flow impinges on a surface. It is important because it creates a strong wall shear and maximum heat transport. Applications of SPBL include jet impingement cooling, turbine blade cooling, aerodynamic heating, electronic cooling, metal quenching, coating processes, and solar thermal systems. The stretching-sheet boundary layer (SSBL) [2, 3] arises when a plate is extended or deformed in its own plane, generating a boundary layer governed by the stretching rate and widely studied in heat and mass-transport problems. Blasius and Sakiadis flows represent canonical shear-controlled rheological models, while stagnation-point and stretching-sheet flows belong to strain-dominated nonlinear rheological regimes. Their simultaneous comparison within a unified Buongiorno nanofluid framework is important because it distinguishes the effects of pure viscous shear from externally imposed nonlinear strain on nanofluid transport behavior. Dinarvand et al. [4] studied the aqua-base boundary layer fluid flow with the micro-rotation and angular momentum aspects of particles. According to Choi [9], a nanofluid is an innovative class of engineered fluid fashioned by diffusing extremely small solid particles, nanoparticles typically smaller than 100 nm, into a conventional base fluid, resulting in a suspension with significantly enhanced thermal conductance and heat-transport ability compared to the base material alone. According to Buongiorno [10], a nanofluid is best described through a slip-mechanism model in which heat/mass transport enhancement cannot be explained by conventional single-phase convection alone, but instead arises from two key relative motions between nanosolids and the base material, Brownian and thermo diffusions, which dominate nanoparticle transport and govern the unique thermal behavior of nanofluids. The novelty of the present work lies in providing a unified comparative analysis of four fundamental nanofluid boundary-layer configurations: Blasius, Sakiadis, stagnation-point, and stretching-sheet flows, within a single Buongiorno nanofluid framework. Unlike earlier studies that typically examine these flow cases for BBL and SBL only, the present formulation enables direct comparison of momentum, thermal, and concentration transport characteristics of BBL, SBL, SPBL, and SSBL cases under identical physical assumptions. Another key contribution is the systematic evaluation of thermal performance through coupled Brownian and thermo diffusions effects across all four geometries. The study further highlights how wall motion and external flow conditions influence skin friction, heat transport, and mass transport, thereby offering new physical insight and practical guidance for optimizing nanofluid-based thermal systems. Numerical simulation

Numerical simulation of the formulated problems for present boundary-layer configurations is obtained using the Keller-box method. This method offers several advantages over other numerical techniques when solving boundary-layer and nanofluid flow problems. Being an implicit finite-difference method, it is unconditionally stable, allowing larger step sizes without compromising accuracy, unlike explicit schemes. It provides second-order accuracy in both spatial directions, ensuring precise velocity, temperature, and concentration profiles. KBM is particularly robust for highly nonlinear and coupled systems, such as nanofluid flows, or flows with variable properties, where methods like shooting or explicit Runge–Kutta may fail or diverge. It efficiently handles complex boundary conditions, including slip, and stretching or moving surfaces, without requiring a priori guesses for unknown boundary values. Additionally, the method’s Newton linearization and iterative box scheme enable rapid convergence even for stiff equations. Developed specifically for boundary-layer problems, KBM is well-suited for thin-layer flows and heat and mass transport analyses, making it more stable, accurate, and reliable than traditional numerical approaches like shooting methods, standard finite differences, or Runge–Kutta schemes. Results and discussion

In this section, the influence of the involved parameters on Nusselt number, Sherwood number, skin-friction coefficient, temperature profile, and concentration profile is graphically discussed with the considered flow scenarios. For graphical purposes, the Schmidt number range corresponds to moderate nanoparticle mass diffusivity in dilute suspensions. The Brownian diffusion parameter is chosen within to capture weak-to-strong nanoparticle random motion effects, while the thermo-diffusion parameter range represents varying thermophoretic transport induced by temperature gradients. Figure 2 describes the impact of the thermo-diffusion parameter on temperature as well as concentration configurations. Increasing the thermophoresis constraint consistently enhances both the temperature and nanoparticle concentration configurations in all flow configurations, BBL, SBL, SPBL, and SSBL, yet the magnitude of this effect varies depending on the boundary-layer type. In the BBL, the impact of is relatively moderate because the classical flat-plate flow generates a comparatively thinner thermal and concentration boundary-layer, allowing thermo-diffusion to increase heat and mass transport but within a limited diffusion region. The SBL, characterized by its linear velocity distribution, exhibits a more noticeable rise in both profiles, as the altered flow structure promotes broader thermal and solutal diffusion. In the SPBL, the influence of is strongest; the strong compressive flow near the stagnation point intensifies temperature gradients and particle migration, causing pronounced thickening of both boundary layers as increases. For the SSBL, increasing produces a significant rise in temperature and concentration levels, but the effect is slightly milder than in SPBL because the stretching velocity enhances nanofluid convection, partially counteracting the thermophoretic diffusion. Overall, while all four configurations show higher temperature and nanosolids concentration configurations with increasing , the strength of the thermophoretic effect typically follows the trend (SPBL > SSBL > SBL > BBL), reflecting the influence of each flow structure on thermal and nanoparticle transport.

The SSBL case follows with 33%, representing a substantial but slightly lower friction level. The SBL case contributes 15%, reflecting a moderate frictional effect, while the BBL case has the smallest share at 11%, suggesting it experiences the least resistance and thus the smoothest flow. This breakdown implies that optimizing the SPBL and SSBL configurations could significantly reduce overall drag, whereas the BBL configuration is already efficient in minimizing friction, and design choices should balance friction reduction with other performance needs like heat transport or structural requirements. Conclusions

A unified boundary-layer analysis of Buongiorno nanofluid flow for Blasius, Sakiadis, stagnation-point, and stretching-sheet configurations has been numerically investigated. The effects of Brownian and thermo diffusions on momentum, heat, and mass transport are examined through similarity transformations and solved using the Keller–Box method. The comparative analysis demonstrates that SPBL and SSBL generate significantly stronger wall shear than BBL and SBL, with SPBL contributing 41% and SSBL contributing 33% to the overall skin-friction response. Increasing the Brownian diffusion parameter weakens heat transport but intensifies mass transport, while higher thermo diffusion suppresses thermal transport and enhances concentration transport. The Schmidt number enhances both heat- and mass-transport rates across all configurations. The main practical outcome of this study is that the stretching-sheet boundary layer is the most suitable configuration for heat-transport enhancement, whereas the stagnation-point boundary layer is more effective for applications requiring stronger mass-transport performance.

Research limitations

The research is limited by assumptions of steady, laminar, and idealized nanofluid behavior, neglecting turbulence, variable properties, and real-world complexities such as particle agglomeration and surface roughness.

Practical applications

Practical applications of the present study include optimizing heat- and mass-transport in industrial processes, such as cooling, coating, extrusion, and chemical reactors, by providing a unified model to predict nanofluid behavior across multiple common boundary-layer flow configurations.

Future research directions

Future research could explore turbulent and unsteady nanofluid flows, variable thermophysical properties, hybrid nanofluids, magnetic or electric field effects, and experimental validation under realistic industrial conditions.
