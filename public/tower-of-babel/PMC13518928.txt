Pv-aCO2 and Pv-aCO2/Ca-vO2 as surrogates for cardiac output and oxygen metabolism in septic shock patients
Authors: Gong-jie Ye, Yi-ru Weng, Shi Zheng, Zhou-zhou Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe study investigates the link between the venous-to-arterial carbon dioxide gap (Pv-aCO2) and cardiac output (CO), as well as the venous-to-arterial carbon dioxide gap to arterial-venous oxygen content difference (Pv-aCO2/Ca-vO2) ratio and the oxygen delivery to oxygen consumption (DO2/VO2) ratio in septic shock patients, with myocardial dysfunction as a secondary subgroup analysis. A retrospective observational study was conducted in intensive care units (ICUs) from July 1, 2018, to March 31, 2024, focusing on patients with septic shock. Participants were divided into two groups for subgroup analysis: those with myocardial dysfunction and those without, based on ejection fraction (EF). The study collected fundamental data, baseline clinical variables, and prognostic indicators to compare the two groups. The study also examined the correlations between Pv-aCO2 and CO, Pv-aCO2/Ca-vO2 and DO2/VO2 in the overall cohort and subgroups. The study involved 597 septic shock patients. In the overall cohort, a negative correlation was found between Pv-aCO2 and CO (r = − 0.63, P < 0.01) and between Pv-aCO2/Ca-vO2 and DO2/VO2 (r = − 0.71, P < 0.01). In subgroup analysis, 127 patients had myocardial dysfunction, and 470 did not. No significant differences were noted in basic data, lactate, and other indicators (P > 0.05). However, significant differences were observed in intubation rate, CO, EF, vasoactive inotropic score (VIS), C-reactive protein (CRP), DO2, Pv-aCO2/Ca-vO2, sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) score, acute physiology and chronic health evaluation (APACHE-II) score, and 28-day mortality rate (P < 0.05) between groups. In myocardial dysfunction subgroup, Pv-aCO2 negatively correlated with CO (r = − 0.57, P < 0.01), and Pv-aCO2/Ca-vO2 negatively correlated with DO2/VO2 (r = − 0.80, P < 0.01). Similarly, in non-myocardial dysfunction subgroup, Pv-aCO2 negatively correlated with CO (r = − 0.62, P < 0.01), and Pv-aCO2/Ca-vO2 negatively correlated with DO2/VO2 (r = − 0.68, P < 0.01). The correlation of Pv-aCO2/Ca-vO2 with DO2/VO2 was significantly stronger in myocardial dysfunction subgroup (P = 0.008), while the Pv-aCO2-CO correlation was similar between subgroups (P = 0.44). In septic shock patients, Pv-aCO2 and Pv-aCO2/Ca-vO2 correlate well with CO and DO2/VO2, indicating they could be surrogate markers for these parameters. Introduction

Septic shock, marked by persistent hypotension despite adequate fluids and a serum lactate level over 2 mmol/L1, remains a leading cause of ICU deaths2. Rapidly addressing tissue hypoxia is crucial in treating septic shock, making its study vital for ICU doctors. Around 40–50% of septic shock patients develop cardiac issues known as sepsis-induced myocardial dysfunction (SIMD)3, with a mortality rate of 70–90%4,5. Effective treatments for septic shock with SIMD are currently lacking. Managing septic shock is complex due to its combination of systemic inflammation and potential cardiac dysfunction, requiring personalized treatment based on individual hemodynamic and metabolic needs. Despite advancements in understanding sepsis pathophysiology, particularly immune cell apoptosis and inflammatory dysregulation, effective treatments are limited6. The lack of specific biomarkers for diagnosing sepsis and predicting outcomes adds to the challenge7. Ongoing research and clinical trials are crucial to developing effective therapies for septic shock and SIMD6–8.

Ensuring adequate CO and DO2 is crucial in managing septic shock, as insufficient DO2 can lead to tissue hypoxia and organ dysfunction. Effective management requires maintaining CO, optimizing DO2, and preventing hypoxemia9–11. Unfortunately, measuring CO accurately requires specialized skills and is typically done using invasive methods like pulse index contour continuous cardiac output (PiCCO) systems or pulmonary artery catheters, which are not practical in primary care. Direct measurement of DO2 and VO2 is also complex. Research suggests that indicators like Pv-aCO2 and the Pv-aCO2/Ca-vO2 ratio might serve as alternatives to direct DO2/VO2 measurement12,13. Pv-aCO2 measures the CO2 pressure difference between venous and arterial blood, while Pv-aCO2/Ca-vO2 relates this difference to the arterial-venous oxygen content difference14. These metrics can indicate anaerobic metabolism and are easily calculated from blood gas analyses. However, more research is needed on their relationships with CO and DO2/VO2, especially in septic shock, where literature is limited.

The primary aim of this retrospective observational study was to evaluate whether Pv-aCO2 and Pv-aCO2/Ca-vO2 can serve as valid surrogates for CO and DO2/VO2 in unselected septic shock patients. Secondary aims included: subgroup comparison stratified by the presence or absence of myocardial dysfunction to assess whether correlation strength differs. By addressing these aims, we sought to provide evidence for simplified, non-invasive monitoring strategies in this high-risk population.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

This retrospective observational study was conducted in a mixed medical and surgical adult ICU from July 1, 2018 to March 31, 2024. All procedures were conducted in compliance with pertinent guidelines and regulations, notably the Declaration of Helsinki for research involving human subjects. The study protocol received approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Ningbo Medical Center Lihuili Hospital (Approval No. QT2018PJ056). As an academic institution affiliated with Ningbo University, Lihuili Hospital requires that all patients give informed consent for the collection of biological samples, including any additional specimens. Informed consent was duly obtained from all participants, ensuring that their information and biological specimens could be utilized for scientific research purposes.

Patients and standard control

Septic shock was characterized based on the clinical criteria established by the Third International Consensus Definitions for Sepsis and Septic Shock (Sepsis-3)1, which include the requirement for vasopressor administration to maintain a mean arterial pressure (MAP) of ≥ 65 mmHg and a serum lactate concentration exceeding 2 mmol/L despite adequate fluid resuscitation. We ensured compliance with sepsis management guidelines15, including the appropriate administration of vasoactive medications, facilitated the timely administration of antibiotics, and conducted regular blood gas analyses and testing on the enrolled subjects.

Inclusion criteria

(1) Age ≥ 16 years old; (2) Using an indwelling central venous catheter and arteries; (3) Patients and their families provided informed consent or assent.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Died or discharged within 24 h after admission; (2) Accompany with coronary artery disease, cardiac hypertrophy, cardiac infarction; (3) Donors or pregnancy; (4) Difficulty in central venous catheterization or arteries, may preclude the use of the ProAQT system or PiCCO for acquiring pertinent clinical data; (5) Incomplete medical record data.

Group definition for subgroup analysis

SIMD was defined as left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) < 50% by bedside echocardiography3,16. Patients were stratified into myocardial dysfunction subgroup and non‑myocardial dysfunction subgroup.

Parameters design procedure

LVEF was quantitatively assessed at the bedside for all patients upon ICU admission using a standardized echocardiographic protocol. Examinations were performed by two intensivists, each with over 5 years of experience in critical care ultrasound, who were blinded to the patients’ group assignments and other hemodynamic data. A phased-array transducer (2.5–3.5 MHz) connected to a Philips CX50 ultrasound system was used. LVEF was calculated using the biplane method of disks (modified Simpson’s rule) in accordance with the recommendations of the American Society of Echocardiography17. Briefly, apical four-chamber and two-chamber views were obtained during end-expiration. The endocardial border was manually traced at both end-diastole (frame after mitral valve closure) and end-systole (frame prior to mitral valve opening), excluding the papillary muscles. The machine’s software automatically calculated left ventricular end-diastolic volume (LVEDV) and end-systolic volume (LVESV). LVEF was derived using the formula: LVEF (%) = [(LVEDV–LVESV)/LVEDV] × 100%. The average value of three consecutive cardiac cycles was recorded for analysis.

Blood samples for arterial, and central venous blood gases were drawn simultaneously. Blood gas samples were collected in heparinized 3 ml syringes, capped and immediately analyzed using a blood gas analyzer (Cobas b 123, Roche, Switzerland). CO was directly measured using pulse contour analysis via either the ProAQT system (LiDCO Ltd, UK) or PiCCO monitor (Pulsion Medical Systems, Germany). For PiCCO, initial calibration was performed using transpulmonary thermodilution: 20 ml of iced saline (0–4 °C) was injected rapidly via a central venous catheter, and the resulting change in arterial temperature was detected by a femoral artery catheter to calculate a reference CO value. Subsequent recalibrations were performed every 8 h or after significant hemodynamic interventions (e.g., fluid bolus, vasopressor adjustment). For ProAQT, calibration was achieved via continuous waveform quality checks throughout monitoring. All calibrations were performed by trained personnel, and invalid results (e.g., poor waveform signal) were excluded from analysis. In the overall cohort and subgroups, patients’ CO, arterial and venous blood gases were measured to analyze the correlation between Pv-aCO2 and CO, Pv-aCO2/Ca-vO2 and DO2/VO2.

Data collection

(1) The fundamental data of the patients, including age, gender, body mass index (BMI), infection sites (respiratory tract, urinary tract, bloodstream, intestinal, and other infections) were collected; (2) Baseline clinical variables include the endotracheal intubation rate, CO, EF (LVEF), vasoactive inotropic score (VIS), lactate, cardiac troponin I (cTnI), N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin (PCT), white blood cell (WBC), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), glycated hemoglobin (HbAlc), DO2, VO2, DO2/VO2, Pv-aCO2, and Pv-aCO2/Ca-vO2; (3) Prognostic factors: sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) score, acute physiology and chronic health evaluation (APACHE-II) score, the 28-day mortality, the length of stays in ICU, and the length of hospital stays.

Formula calculation

Pv-aCO2 = PvCO2–PaCO2;

Pv-aCO2 = PvCO2–PaCO2;

Pv-aCO2/Ca-vO2 = (PvCO2–PaCO2)/[1.34 × Hb × (SaO2–SvO2)]13,18;

Pv-aCO2/Ca-vO2 = (PvCO2–PaCO2)/[1.34 × Hb × (SaO2–SvO2)]13,18;

DO2 = CaO2 × CO = (1.34 × Hb × SaO2+0.0031 × PaO2) × CO ≈ 1.34 × Hb × SaO2 × CO19,20;

DO2 = CaO2 × CO = (1.34 × Hb × SaO2+0.0031 × PaO2) × CO ≈ 1.34 × Hb × SaO2 × CO19,20;

VO2 = (CaO2–CvO2) × CO = (1.34 × Hb × SaO2 + 0.0031 × PaO2–1.34 × Hb × SvO2–0.0031 × PvO2) × CO ≈ 1.34 × Hb × (SaO2–SvO2) × CO19–20.

VO2 = (CaO2–CvO2) × CO = (1.34 × Hb × SaO2 + 0.0031 × PaO2–1.34 × Hb × SvO2–0.0031 × PvO2) × CO ≈ 1.34 × Hb × (SaO2–SvO2) × CO19–20.

Pv-aCO2 denotes central venous-arterial CO2 gap, PvCO2 venous partial pressure of CO2, PaCO2 arterial partial pressure of CO2, Pv-aCO2/Ca-vO2 central venous-arterial CO2 gap to arterial-central venous O2 content difference ratio, Hb hemoglobin, SaO2 arterial oxygen saturation, SvO2 venous oxygen saturation, CaO2 arterial oxygen content, CO cardiac output, CvO2 venous oxygen content.

Comparison of correlation coefficients between subgroups (r)

To rigorously evaluate whether the relationships between Pv-aCO2–CO and Pv-aCO2/Ca-vO2–DO2/VO2 differ between subgroups with or without myocardial dysfunction, we compared subgroup magnitudes using Steiger’s Z-test (to assess if the difference between two dependent correlations is statistically significant).

Statistical analysis

SPSS version 25.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL) was utilized to conduct statistical analyses. The data were assessed for normality and non-Gaussian distribution using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Normally distributed data were presented as mean ± SD, while non-normally distributed data are expressed as median [25–75%, interquartile range (IQR)]. Both parametric and nonparametric methods were employed in the analysis. Correlation analyses were conducted using either the Pearson or Spearman correlation methods. An absolute correlation coefficient greater than 0.3, as determined by these methods, was considered indicative of a significant correlation. For comparisons between two groups, either the Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test was employed. Chi-square test or Fisher exact probability method was applied for analyzing counting data. Steiger’s Z‑test was used to compare the strength of correlation coefficients between the two subgroups. The level of significance (α) was set at 0.05, with statistical significance being defined as P < 0.05.

Study population

From July 1, 2018 to March 31, 2024, a total of 1996 septic shock patients were admitted to the ICU. After applying inclusion and exclusion criteria, the final analysis included 597 patients. For subgroup analysis, 127 patients were classified into the myocardial dysfunction subgroup, and 470 into the non-myocardial dysfunction subgroup.

Fundamental data, clinical variables, and prognostic indicators in subgroup analysis

In the study, among the 597 subjects, 127 patients exhibited an EF of less than 0.50 and were categorized under the myocardial dysfunction subgroup, with a mean EF of 0.40 ± 0.07. Comparison of correlation strength between subgroups (r)

Correlation between Pv-aCO2 and CO: The analysis revealed no significant difference in the strength of the negative correlation between the myocardial dysfunction subgroup (r = − 0.57) and the non-dysfunction subgroup (r = − 0.62, P = 0.44). This suggests that the correlation strength between Pv-aCO2 and CO remains consistent irrespective of the presence of myocardial dysfunction. Discussion

The primary findings

The primary finding of our study indicates a significant inverse correlation between Pv-aCO2 and CO, as well as between Pv-aCO2/Ca-vO2 and DO2/VO2 in the overall population of septic shock patients. Myocardial dysfunction was analyzed as a secondary subgroup factor. We found that the correlation strength of Pv-aCO2 with CO was similar regardless of myocardial function, while the correlation of Pv-aCO2/Ca-vO2 with DO2/VO2 was significantly stronger in patients with myocardial dysfunction.

Clinical implications in septic shock and SIMD

Septic shock is a life-threatening complication of sepsis, characterized by persistent hypotension despite adequate fluid resuscitation, leading to inadequate tissue perfusion and organ dysfunction. Systemic inflammatory responses trigger widespread vasodilation and cardiovascular collapse. Clinical management requires prompt intervention including fluid resuscitation, vasopressor support, and early appropriate antimicrobial therapy21,22.

Septic shock-induced myocardial dysfunction (SIMD) is a major complication of septic shock characterized by impaired myocardial contractility, which further aggravates hemodynamic instability. Its pathophysiology involves inflammatory mediators, neurohormonal activation, and metabolic disorders23,24. In our study, the incidence of SIMD was 21.27%, and the 28‑day mortality reached 55.1%, higher than the ~ 40% reported in previous literature25. This high mortality may be related to severe organ dysfunction, comorbidities, and intense inflammatory responses. Aggressive resuscitation and vasopressor use may further complicate hemodynamic management, highlighting the need for individualized monitoring and treatment25.

Left ventricular dysfunction in septic shock is often reversible after recovery26. Early identification of SIMD helps guide inotropic therapy and fluid management to improve cardiac function and tissue perfusion21,23. However, standardized treatment strategies remain lacking, and outcomes remain poor. Current guidelines recommend EGDT, infection control, and hemodynamic support, with EGDT aiming to balance systemic oxygen delivery and consumption27. VA‑ECMO has been associated with a 40–50% survival rate in selected refractory cases28, but is not yet routine due to limited evidence and complication risks. In SIMD, reduced cardiac output leads to tissue hypoperfusion, hypoxia, and organ injury29, disrupting the balance between DO2 and VO2. Direct measurement of CO and DO2/VO2 is invasive and technically challenging. Our study confirmed significant inverse correlations of Pv-aCO2 with CO and Pv-aCO2/Ca-vO2 with DO2/VO2, supporting their role as simple, real‑time surrogates for monitoring hemodynamics and oxygen metabolism in septic shock patients with myocardial dysfunction.

Central venous-arterial CO2 gap (Pv-aCO2) and Central venous-arterial CO2 gap to arterial-central venous O2 content difference ratio (Pv-aCO2/Ca-vO2)

Pv-aCO2 represents the pressure difference of carbon dioxide between central venous and arterial blood, with a normal threshold below 6 mmHg30. It is widely recognized as an indicator of microcirculatory perfusion and CO2 washout efficiency30,31. An elevated Pv-aCO2 suggests insufficient tissue blood flow and has been associated with poor outcomes in septic patients, particularly those with cardiac dysfunction32,33. When combined with lactate and ScvO2, Pv-aCO2 improves the assessment of resuscitation adequacy and helps guide personalized interventions34,35. Pv-aCO2/Ca-vO2 acts as a surrogate for respiratory quotient and reflects anaerobic metabolism and tissue oxygenation imbalance13,14,18. Under hypoxic conditions, VO2 becomes dependent on DO2, and this ratio sensitively indicates such derangements36,37. An elevated Pv-aCO2/Ca-vO2 ratio is associated with higher mortality and helps identify high-risk patients38,39. Monitoring this ratio together with lactate allows comprehensive evaluation of hemodynamic and metabolic responses to resuscitation40,41.

Comparison of the correlations (r) between Pv-aCO2–CO and Pv-aCO2/Ca-vO2–DO2/VO2 in subgroups

We found no significant difference in the negative correlation between Pv-aCO2 and CO in the myocardial dysfunction subgroup and the non-myocardial dysfunction subgroup (P = 0.44). This stability suggests Pv-aCO2 mainly reflects global CO2 elimination and tissue perfusion rather than direct myocardial contractility. Even with impaired left ventricular function, compensatory mechanisms may maintain CO and preserve the Pv-aCO2-CO relationship. In contrast, the correlation between the Pv-aCO2/Ca-vO2 ratio and the DO2/VO2 ratio is notably stronger in patients with myocardial dysfunction compared to those without (P = 0.008). Patients with SIMD have limited cardiac reserve and are less tolerant to oxygen delivery imbalance. Therefore, Pv-aCO2/Ca-vO2 is more sensitive to oxygen metabolism disturbance in this high-risk population, supporting its value in targeted monitoring. Further research is needed to confirm these results and investigate their therapeutic implications, such as optimizing inotropy versus fluid administration in high-risk groups. Strengths, limitations, and future perspectives

This study has several strengths: Pv-aCO2 and Pv-aCO2/Ca-vO2 are simple, low-cost, and readily available from routine blood gas analysis, enabling real-time monitoring in most ICUs. The data are objective and reproducible, with minimal observer bias.

Several limitations should be noted: This is a single-center retrospective study. Serial changes in parameters at different time points were not analyzed. Our study focused on LV systolic function assessed by EF, but future investigations should incorporate comprehensive assessment including right ventricular function, diastolic parameters, and load-independent indices of contractility to fully characterize septic cardiomyopathy. Limitations in myocardial function assessment. Our definition of SIMD relied solely on LVEF < 50%, which does not capture the spectrum of septic myocardial injury. The availability of PiCCO monitoring provided an opportunity to assess load-independent parameters (e.g., cardiac function index) and volume status, but these were not systematically analyzed. Furthermore, we lacked data on right ventricular function, diastolic dysfunction, and subtle systolic impairment detectable by strain imaging. Future studies should adopt a multimodal approach integrating echocardiographic advanced parameters with continuous hemodynamic monitoring to better phenotype septic cardiomyopathy.

Another important limitation of this retrospective study is the lack of detailed mechanical ventilation data, including tidal volume, PEEP, respiratory rate, and ventilation mode. Mechanical ventilation can influence blood gas results by altering alveolar ventilation, dead space, and intrathoracic pressure, which may directly affect Pv-aCO2 levels independent of tissue perfusion. Because these parameters were not systematically recorded, we could not adjust for their confounding effects, which may have introduced bias into the correlation analyses between Pv-aCO2 and CO.

Future prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings, include serial measurements, standardize ventilation data, and explore whether Pv-aCO2 and Pv-aCO2/Ca-vO2 can serve as resuscitation endpoints to improve outcomes in septic shock patients with or without myocardial dysfunction.

Conclusions

In conclusion, septic shock represents a severe medical condition requiring prompt and thorough management. The occurrence of SIMD further complicates the clinical scenario, underscoring the necessity for sophisticated diagnostic methodologies and targeted therapeutic interventions to effectively manage both the septic condition and its cardiovascular consequences. Our study reveals that Pv-aCO2 and Pv-aCO2/Ca-vO2 are reliable surrogate markers for CO and DO2/VO2 in septic shock patients. As a secondary subgroup finding, the Pv-aCO2/Ca-vO2 ratio correlates more strongly with DO2/VO2 in patients with myocardial dysfunction, supporting its value in high-risk patient monitoring.
