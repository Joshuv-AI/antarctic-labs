1. Executive Summary

This report presents the successful development, commissioning, and deployment of the
Scintillation-Based X-ray Imager (SBXI) for the High-Resolution Turbulence (HRT) campaign
at the National Ignition Facility (NIF). The SBXI system was designed to overcome the
spatial resolution and efficiency limitations of conventional x-ray imaging detectors.
Through careful system engineering, rigorous laboratory characterization, and
experimental validation at NIF, the SBXI has demonstrated spatial resolution at the object
plane of 5 μm (±0.5 μm) and a detective quantum efficiency (DQE) of 34% at 7.2 keV, both
of which surpass the requirements set by the campaign, and meeting the L2 milestone. The
system’s deployment enables high-fidelity imaging of fine-scale hydrodynamic instabilities,
directly supporting advanced turbulence model validation and future high energy density
physics research.

3. Background and Objectives
The High-Resolution Turbulence campaign at NIF is focused on validating advanced
turbulence models by imaging the evolution of shocked unstable interfaces in high energy
density plasmas. In these experiments, the transition from laminar to turbulent flow and
the growth of hydrodynamic instabilities, such as Richtmyer–Meshkov and Rayleigh–Taylor
interfaces, must be tracked with high spatial and temporal precision. The scientific goals
of the campaign require an imaging system capable of resolving features smaller than
5 μm at the object plane, with sufficient field of view and high detective quantum efficiency
to ensure high-contrast, low-noise images even at low photon flux.

Conventional x-ray imaging detectors, including imaging plates, microchannel plates
(MCPs), and direct-detection CMOS sensors, each present significant limitations. Imaging
plates are limited by their relatively coarse spatial resolution, typically exceeding 80 μm.
MCP-based framing cameras offer improved resolution but suffer from low detection
efficiency, with DQE values below 3%. Direct-detection CMOS detectors, while offering
small pixel sizes, are prone to saturation when exposed to the high instantaneous photon
fluxes encountered in laser-driven plasma experiments. These shortcomings necessitated
the development of a new imaging platform.

The objective of this project was to design, build, and commission an x-ray imaging system
that would meet or exceed the following requirements: spatial resolution at the object
plane below 5 μm, spatial resolution at the detector below 25 μm, field of view at the
object plane greater than 600 μm, and a DQE at 7.2 keV above 3%. In addition, the system

needed to be robustly aligned and focused, with repeatable performance in both
laboratory and high-energy experimental environments.

4. Technical Approach
To address these challenges, the SBXI system was conceived as a lens-coupled,
scintillator-based x-ray imager. The system architecture is centered around a single-crystal
cesium iodide (CsI(Tl)) scintillator, which is known for its high x-ray-to-light conversion
efficiency and minimal light scattering compared to traditional powder-based phosphor
screens. The scintillator is positioned at the focal plane of a bent crystal x-ray microscope
(the Crystal Backlit Imager, or CBI), which provides a tenfold magnification of the object.

Visible light generated in the scintillator is collected by a diffraction-limited industrial lens
with a numerical aperture of 0.158, and the resulting optical image is projected onto a high-
performance CMOS detector. The CMOS camera, a Spectral Instruments CIS-40, features
9 μm pixels and a quantum efficiency of 79% at the relevant wavelength (525 nm). This
configuration is designed to maximize both spatial resolution and signal throughput, while
also allowing for adjustment of the lens aperture to accommodate a wide dynamic range
without risking detector saturation.

A key innovation of the SBXI system is the use of a transparent, single-crystal scintillator.
This approach eliminates the spatial resolution limitations imposed by light scattering in
powder-based screens and enables the system to achieve spatial resolutions at the
scintillator surface better than 10 μm. The combination of a high-NA lens and a thin
scintillator ensures that the depth of field is well matched to the x-ray interaction volume,
further preserving image sharpness.

5. System Characterization
The performance of the SBXI system was characterized in several stages, beginning with
the calibration of the CMOS detector. Using a calibrated LED light source and integrating
sphere, the detector’s readout noise, gain, and quantum efficiency were measured. The
CIS-40 camera demonstrated a readout noise of 4.2 electrons per pixel and a gain of 3.291
ADU per electron, with quantum efficiency at 525 nm measured at 79%. These values
provided a solid foundation for high-sensitivity imaging.

The conversion efficiency of the scintillator was then evaluated using an offline test setup.
Several scintillator materials were compared, including CsI(Tl), GAGG+, and LuAG(Ce),
both with and without mirrored surfaces. CsI(Tl) with a mirror coating exhibited the highest
conversion efficiency, measured at 12.5% for 5.9 keV x-rays. This efficiency is notably
higher than that of the other tested materials and is consistent with the known properties
of CsI(Tl) at low photon energies. The mirrored surface also serves to double the effective
emission region, which is compatible with the lens’s depth of field.

Alignment and focusing of the system were accomplished through a combination of
mechanical and optical techniques. The optical axis of the AirBox was established using an
alignment telescope, ensuring that the scintillator, lens, and detector were all positioned
perpendicular to the diagnostic axis and aligned to within 2 milliradians. Fine focusing was
achieved using a Siemens star target, which was mounted in a twist-lock holder and

 5

positioned at the same depth as the scintillator’s mirrored surface. The use of a digital
micrometer stage allowed the focal planes of the Siemens target and the scintillator to be
matched to within ±10 μm. This high degree of alignment precision is critical for
maintaining spatial resolution across the entire field of view.

To guide the design and predict the performance of the SBXI system, a comprehensive
analytical model was developed, incorporating the key physical and optical parameters
that influence spatial resolution and detective quantum efficiency (DQE). The model
accounts for the absorption of x-ray photons within the scintillator, the efficiency of x-ray-
to-light conversion, the collection and transmission of scintillation photons through the
lens, and the quantum efficiency and noise characteristics of the CMOS detector. By
inputting values such as x-ray photon energy, scintillator thickness and composition, lens
numerical aperture and magnification, and detector pixel size, the model estimates the
number of electrons generated per absorbed x-ray photon and the expected signal-to-noise
ratio for a given photon fluence. This predictive framework enables the calculation of the
system’s DQE, which was estimated to reach up to 45% for 7.2 keV x-rays under ideal
conditions. The model also quantifies how spatial resolution is affected by the interplay
between the scintillator’s emission depth, the lens’s depth of field, and optical aberrations.
By benchmarking these predictions against experimental measurements, the model
provided crucial validation for the design choices and ensured that the SBXI would meet or
exceed the stringent requirements of the HRT campaign.

6. Experimental Validation
To rigorously assess the spatial resolution of the SBXI system prior to its deployment at NIF,
we conducted a series of offline measurements using a controlled laboratory setup. In this
configuration, an x-ray tube (Trufocus TFX-8100-EWC) served as the photon source, and a
precision resolution test target featuring line pair patterns ranging from 5 to 20 line pairs
per millimeter was positioned immediately in front of the scintillator. The SBXI optics,
consisting of a Printing Nikkor 95.5 mm F/2.8 lens and the CIS-40 CMOS detector, were
carefully aligned and focused to ensure optimal image sharpness. By capturing images of
the test target and analyzing the resulting contrast transfer at various spatial frequencies,
we determined the system’s point spread function and effective spatial resolution. The
analysis revealed that the SBXI system was able to resolve features as fine as 10 μm at the
scintillator surface, with the limiting resolution set by the combined effects of the
scintillator thickness, lens numerical aperture, and detector pixel size. This result not only
confirmed that the system met the <25 μm spatial resolution requirement at the detector
but also provided confidence that, when coupled with the 10x magnification of the CBI, the
SBXI would achieve the necessary <5 μm resolution at the object plane in experimental
conditions. The offline spatial resolution measurement thus served as a critical validation

step, demonstrating the system’s readiness for high-precision imaging tasks in the HRT
campaign. The results demonstrated that the SBXI could resolve features as small as
10 μm at the scintillator surface, with system throughput closely matching theoretical
predictions.

The ultimate test of the SBXI’s performance came during a qualification shot at NIF (shot
N250326-002-999). In this experiment, the SBXI was coupled to the CBI, which provided a
tenfold magnification of a 3D grid target. The x-ray backlighter consisted of a 5-μm-thick
cobalt foil, pre-heated and irradiated by NIF laser beams. The combined optical system,
including the toroidally bent Si(620) crystal, achieved a total magnification of 33.3x.

The SBXI produced sharp, high-contrast images of the grid target, enabling quantitative
assessment of both spatial resolution and detective quantum efficiency. Analysis of the
edge blur in the shadowgraph images confirmed that the system achieved a spatial
resolution of 5 μm (±0.5 μm) at the object plane, fully meeting the campaign’s
requirements. The detective quantum efficiency, calculated from the measured signal-to-
noise ratio and photon fluence, was found to be 34.6% at 7.2 keV—an order of magnitude
greater than the minimum requirement. The field of view at the object plane was measured
at 1,100 μm, nearly double the specified minimum.

Cross-calibration with imaging plate data further validated the SBXI’s sensitivity. The signal
measured on the imaging plate was used to determine the photon fluence at the detector,
which agreed with the SBXI’s measured signal to within 12% of the theoretical throughput
model. This close agreement between model and measurement underscores the
robustness of the system design.

7. Discussion
The SBXI system’s performance represents a significant advance over conventional x-ray
imaging technologies used in high energy density physics. Its spatial resolution and
efficiency enable the detailed imaging of fine-scale features in turbulent plasmas,
supporting the rigorous validation of advanced turbulence models. The system’s robust

 10

alignment and focusing procedures ensure repeatable, reliable performance in both
laboratory and experimental environments, while its flexible optical configuration allows
for adjustment of dynamic range to suit varying experimental conditions.

Operationally, the SBXI has proven resilient to minor imperfections on the scintillator
surface, as the optical configuration and illumination geometry minimize the impact of
such defects on image quality. The use of a mirrored scintillator surface not only enhances
conversion efficiency but also simplifies the focusing process by providing a clear optical
reference.

In comparison to alternative detector technologies, the SBXI offers superior spatial
resolution and efficiency without the dynamic range limitations of direct-detection CMOS
or the low sensitivity of MCP-based systems. Its architecture is readily adaptable to other
high-resolution, high-efficiency x-ray imaging applications, making it a valuable asset for
future campaigns.

8. Conclusions and Impact
In conclusion, the SBXI system has established a new benchmark for high-resolution, high-
efficiency x-ray imaging in high energy density physics. Its successful deployment at NIF
enables the accurate tracking of turbulence evolution and the validation of advanced
models, directly supporting the scientific goals of the HRT campaign and meets the L2
milestone. The system’s demonstrated capabilities will enhance experimental diagnostics
and contribute to a deeper understanding of instability evolution in extreme environments.
The SBXI’s flexible, robust design ensures its continued utility in future high-resolution
imaging applications.

10. Appendix: Glossary
Detective Quantum Efficiency (DQE): A measure of how efficiently an imaging system converts
incoming x-ray photons into a useful image, factoring in noise and signal loss.

 11

FWHM (Full Width at Half Maximum): A standard metric for the width of a distribution or function, used
here to describe spatial resolution.

Numerical Aperture (NA): A dimensionless number characterizing the range of angles over which an
optical system can accept or emit light.

Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR): The ratio of the desired signal to background noise, reflecting image
clarity.

Crystal Backlit Imager (CBI): A bent crystal x-ray microscope used to magnify the object in high-
resolution imaging experiments.

National Ignition Facility (NIF): A U.S. Department of Energy facility dedicated to high energy density
physics and inertial confinement fusion research.
