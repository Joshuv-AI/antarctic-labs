Transaxillary endovascular repair of a mycotic ascending aortic pseudoaneurysm using a rifampin-soaked Dacron-based Cook Zenith Flex abdominal aortic cuff
Authors: Benjamin Limburg, Thekla Bacharach, Anand Dayama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ascending aortic pseudoaneurysms occur rarely, most often after cardiac surgery. Given their significant morbidity and mortality, open surgical intervention is the traditional treatment. However, in cases where open approaches are contraindicated, endovascular intervention can be a viable option. The lack of an approved endovascular device for ascending aortic repair necessitates the off-label use of thoracic stent grafts, abdominal aortic cuffs, or custom-designed devices. We describe successful transaxillary endovascular repair of an infected ascending aortic pseudoaneurysm using a rifampin-soaked Dacron-based Cook Zenith Flex abdominal aortic cuff (Cook Medical).

Ascending aortic pseudoaneurysms (AAPs) are rare, occurring most commonly after aortic root surgery or coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG).1 AAP incidence is thought to be below 0.5%, yet AAPs carry significant morbidity and mortality and most often require surgical intervention.1,2 There are no US Food and Drug Administration-approved endografts for AAPs, though approvals have been granted internationally and early investigational trials are underway in the United States.3 We present here the case of an infected AAP successfully treated with rifampin-soaked Dacron-based infrarenal aortic cuff. The patient provided informed consent for the publication of case details and accompanying images.

Case report

A 68-year-old woman with past medical history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus type II, heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, mitral stenosis with mitral valve repair, CABG with saphenous vein conduit, and postoperative course complicated by sternal wound dehiscence and infection with Serratia and Corynebacterium requiring multiple debridements and reconstruction. She recently had a tracheostomy for vocal cord paralysis and presented to an outside emergency department with hemoptysis and tracheostomy site bleeding. Computed tomography (CT) of the neck and chest was remarkable for an incidentally discovered pseudoaneurysm of the anterior distal ascending aorta proximal to the innominate artery measuring 20 × 22 × 26 mm with a neck of 8 × 9 mm. Ascending aorta measurements demonstrated outer and inner curvature distances of 64.5 and 28.8 mm, respectively, between the CABG ostium and innominate artery (Fig 1). Luminal curvature length between the CABG ostium and innominate artery measured approximately 5.5 cm, and the native aorta diameter was 32 mm. She was transferred to the emergency department for further evaluation. After general anesthesia induction, an infraclavicular incision was made to expose the left axillary artery. The left femoral and right brachial arteries were subsequently accessed percutaneously with 5F sheaths. From the right brachial artery, a Bentson wire (Cook Medical) was advanced to the ascending aorta to serve as a distal marker for graft deployment. An 8F sheath was placed in the left axillary artery followed by a Bentson wire and AL1 catheter to access the left ventricle. This Bentson wire was then exchanged for a Lunderquist wire (Cook Medical), and an aortogram was taken in two projections to mark the takeoff of the CABG ostium and innominate artery (Fig 3). This revealed approximately 5.5 cm between the CABG takeoff and innominate artery, and confirmed an outer-curvature AAP. The 8F sheath was removed, and a rifampin-soaked Cook Zenith Flex 36 × 50 mm aortic cuff (Cook Medical) was advanced. Ventricular pacing at 160 beats/min was initiated, and the graft was deployed from the CABG takeoff to the takeoff of the innominate artery, excluding the pseudoaneurysm from flow. The graft was postdilated using a trilobed balloon, this time with ventricular pacing at 140 beats/min, and completion aortogram demonstrated an adequately placed graft without endoleak, patent CABG, and patent innominate artery (Fig 4). The patient’s postoperative recovery was uneventful, and she was discharged home on postoperative day 4 with an empiric 6-week course of ertapenem and daptomycin. Considering the AAP was infected, following intravenous antibiotics, this patient will require lifelong antibiosis. At the 1-month follow-up visit, repeat CT angiography of the chest was obtained and showed stable endograft placement with resolution of pseudoaneurysm filling (Fig 5). The patient was determined to be in stable clinical condition and was advised to obtain repeat CT angiography of the chest and be seen again in clinic at 6 months postprocedure.Fig 5One-month follow-up computed tomography angiography (CTA) of the chest showed stable endograft placement with resolution of pseudoaneurysm filling via three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction (a) and in axial (b) and sagittal (c) views.

Discussion

AAP pathogenesis varies, with previous cardiac surgery as the most common etiology.1 AAPs secondary to cardiac surgery usually occur at areas of previous aortic wall disruption such as cannulation or other aortotomy sites, and underlying infection has become closely implicated.4,5 In this patient, previous sternotomy, complicated by wound dehiscence and retained bone fragments, presented a particularly difficult surgical problem and necessitated an endovascular approach. The ascending aorta offers a significant challenge to endovascular management because of proximity to the aortic valve and coronary ostia, aortic arch branch vessels, and sharp curvature within a short segment.

In this case, the aortic anatomy did not provide an adequate landing zone for deployment of even the shortest thoracic endograft via a transfemoral approach. A rifampin-soaked Dacron-based abdominal aortic cuff was therefore selected, necessitating transaxillary access due to limited delivery system length. A contingency plan for retrograde in situ laser fenestration of the innominate artery was established in the event landing zone foreshortening along the luminal outer curvature of the ascending aorta resulted in inadvertent coverage of the innominate artery.

A 2016 systematic review by Muetterties et al6 found literature documentation of 13 different aortic stent grafts used for AAPs, from which the authors created 3 broad device-type categories: thoracic stents, abdominal cuffs, and custom-made grafts. Standard thoracic stents offer appropriate diameters for ascending aortic use, yet they are often too long for use in zone 0 and have limited flexibility for the sharp curvature, potentially resulting in a poor seal. The major advantage of abdominal aortic cuffs is their short length, offering precision for ascending aortic deployment. However, abdominal graft delivery systems are often too short to accommodate transfemoral access to the ascending aorta. Custom-made or physician-modified endografts offer ways to adapt to patient-specific anatomy but are technically complex to design and deploy.

In this patient, the mycotic pseudoaneurysm significantly restricted endograft selection and necessitated use of a Dacron-based device. When compared in vitro, bacteria are more likely to adhere to Dacron grafts compared with polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE).7 However, given the hydrophobic nature and lack of porosity of PTFE graft material, antibiotic impregnation of PTFE is not feasible, leading to Dacron favorability for implantation in a field suspected to be harboring bacteria.8,9

Given the smallest available Dacron thoracic stents measure 7 cm, a Cook Zenith Flex aortic cuff was selected and deployed via a transaxillary approach due to the short delivery system length. Completion and follow-up angiography demonstrated the effectiveness of this approach for AAP repair, with no significant intra- or postoperative complications.

Conclusions

Herein, we present the successful use of a rifampin-soaked Dacron abdominal aortic cuff in an infected AAP. AAPs occur most commonly after cardiac surgery and are predominantly treated with open surgical repair, except in the cases where open surgery is contraindicated. As the wait continues for Food and Drug Administration-approved devices for treatment of AAPs, this case adds to the growing body of literature supporting off-label endograft feasibility.
