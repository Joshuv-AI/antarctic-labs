Ethical, legal, and social issues associated with human fetal tissue research in Japan
Authors: Misao Fujita, Kyoko Akatsuka, Akane Hashimoto, Mari Honda, Tsunakuni Ikka, Minori Kokado, Yoshichika Mori, Masanori Oikawa, Go Okui, Mitinori Saitou, Yasuhiro Takashima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe Informed Consent Standards for Human Fetal Tissue Donation for Research, published by the International Society for Stem Cell Research, provide important guidance for ethical research. They are particularly relevant in countries such as Japan, where clear legal frameworks for the use of human fetal and associated tissues obtained through induced abortion are lacking. However, because such guidelines do not fully account for national regulatory and legal frameworks, additional considerations are required to support their effective global implementation. Here, we describe three ethical, legal, and social issues in Japan – also shared in part by other countries – as examples of considerations relevant to the wider implementation of internationally recognized ethical standards. We discuss possible approaches to these issues, including: (1) appropriate selection of medical institutions capable of minimizing the burden on potential donors, (2) consideration of whether consent from the male partner should be obtained, and (3) clarification of the “Ena (placenta)” ordinance related to the use and disposal of dead fetuses. Ensuring appropriate integration between such ethical standards and local regulations is essential for the sustainable and ethical development of fetal tissue research.KEYWORDS: Human fetal tissue research, informed consent, fetal tissue donation, regulatory governance, Japan

Background

Human fetal tissue research has recently received increased attention. It supports a wide range of basic research activities, including the validation and optimization of in vitro culture systems, elucidation of early development and congenital/genetic disease mechanisms, the creation of humanized mouse models, and the development and production of new vaccines [1]. However, as acquisition of fetal tissues often requires donor consent from individuals undergoing induced abortion, various ethical and emotional considerations need to be taken into account when communicating such matters with potential donors. Additionally, in regions where abortion is a subject of extreme political and social discourse, accountability for research transparency and legitimacy is likely to be called into question frequently. Empirical studies in multiple countries further highlight stakeholder concerns regarding informed consent, transparency, trust, and the handling of fetal materials, underscoring the importance of these factors for the social acceptability of fetal tissue research [2–5].

The “Informed Consent Standards for Human Fetal Tissue Donation for Research” (hereinafter referred to as “IC standards”), published by the International Society for Stem Cell Research (ISSCR) in 2022, serves as a valuable tool for addressing ethical and social matters associated with fetal tissue research, particularly in areas related to stem cell and developmental biology [6]. ISSCR, an academic society, is a leading international organization in this field, and its guidelines are widely referenced by researchers and institutions, thereby contributing to an international baseline of ethical standards in fetal tissue research. The IC standards is primarily framed around women who may donate tissue following pregnancy termination, while recognizing that consent materials may need to be adapted for other eligible donors.

They include points that should be explained to potential tissue donors before obtaining their consent and generally cover the following basic principles: respecting the voluntary will of donors, ensuring that the decision to donate fetal tissues follows that of abortion, prioritizing medical care of potential donors over tissue collection, and prohibiting monetary exchange for tissue donation [6,7]. Additionally, it is important that those undertaking research should not be involved in the abortion process itself to avoid conflicts of interest [7]. If these IC standards can ensure standardization and transparency of informed consent procedures in fetal tissue donation and collection, this may enhance support for researchers and medical personnel handling donated tissues, promote appropriate review by ethics review committee members, and contribute to public confidence in the overall process [7]. Moreover, adopting identical ethical standards for tissue collection across countries would further facilitate international collaborative research [6].

Such standards developed by an established international society provide valuable guidance for ethical research in countries such as Japan, where clear legal frameworks for the use of human fetal and associated tissues obtained through induced abortion are lacking. Thus, in 2023, the Japanese translation of the IC standards was published [8]. Although these standards covering essential information for potential tissue donors, they cannot be readily adopted by every country directly; researchers, medical personnel, and ethics review committee members handling fetal tissues must first comply with their own national laws and regulations, which may not be covered by the IC standards. Indeed, the IC standards state, “Regardless of what is in this ISSCR informed consent standard, any donation must also meet all applicable local legal requirements” [6].

This study examines the regulatory frameworks governing human fetal tissue research in Japan, while drawing on selected countries in Europe and North America as examples of relatively well-developed regulatory systems. We identify and analyze three specific challenges arising from Japan’s regulatory and social context impeding the successful implementation of the IC standards within local regulatory frameworks, while also considering cultural perceptions surrounding the fetus. We then propose concrete measures necessary to develop locally appropriate regulations that align with recommended international minimum standards. By grounding these countermeasures to both internationally recognized basic standards and local contexts, this study directly contributes to international discussions, offering a practical reference for countries developing or revising their regulatory frameworks.

It should be noted that this article does not discuss the morality of induced abortions or engage with debates on the moral status of fetuses, but rather proceeds on the premise that abortion is currently performed legally in certain countries. It nonetheless assumes that materials obtained through induced abortion warrant appropriate ethical sensitivity, given their potential for human development. Although materials obtained through miscarriage or ectopic pregnancy may also be used for research, the contexts involve different sensitivities and donor experiences and are not addressed here. While biological distinctions between embryos and fetuses are acknowledged, this paper treats materials obtained through induced abortion as raising broadly comparable consent-related issues for this discussion, and therefore does not adopt a strict developmental distinction in terminology. Embryonic stem cell research is not considered here. The age of the fetus is calculated from the first day of the last menstruation and not from the date of fertilization.

Laws and regulations regarding human fetal tissue research in European and North American countries

The United Kingdom was the first country to establish specific guidelines for research involving fetuses and fetal tissues [9]. In 1972, in response to allegations raised in Parliament that aborted fetuses were being trafficked for research purposes, the advisory committee of the Department of Health and Social Security compiled the “Use of Fetuses and Fetal Material for Research” report, also known as the “Peel Report” [10]. This report represented the first guidelines specifically addressing the research use of fetuses and fetal material, and subsequently informed the development of reports and recommendations in other countries and institutions. Following this, the Peel Report was revised to accommodate new research, including clinical trials for Parkinson’s disease, and issued in 1989 as the “Review of the Guidance on the Research Use of Fetuses and Fetal Material” (commonly known as the “Polkinghorne Report”). This report separated the moral issues of abortion from the research use of fetuses and adopted the provision that donors choose the latter only after deciding on the former [9]. In 2004, the “Human Tissue Act” was enacted [11], leading to the establishment of the Human Tissue Authority (HTA) to oversee the extraction, storage, use, and disposal of human bodies, organs, and tissues. The latest version of the “Code of Practice: Code A, Guiding Principles and the Fundamental Principle of Consent” issued by the HTA in 2023 states that fetal tissue of less than 24 weeks gestation—when induced abortion is legally permitted—is considered to be the mother’s tissue, as are fetal appendages [12]. Accordingly, the donation and use of fetal tissue for research are subject to the same consent requirements under the Human Tissue Act as other tissue from the living.

In the United States, following the abortion controversy and establishment of the “National Research Act,” in 1975, the national committee compiled a report titled “Research on the Fetuses” to protect subjects in biomedical and behavioral science research [13,14]. In response, Subpart B, “Fetuses, Pregnant Women, and In Vitro Fertilization,” was added to 45 CFR 46 [15], which regulates the protection of human subjects, stating that federally funded research on dead fetuses must be conducted per state local laws and regulations. Subsequently, the Reagan—Bush Administration placed an extended moratorium on federal funding for the transplantation of tissues from aborted fetuses, which was later lifted under the Clinton Administration. Furthermore, the “Statute on Fetal Tissue Transplantation Research” was added to the “Public Health Service Act” in 1993 [16], stipulating written consent from donors, precedence of consent for abortion to consent for tissue donation, and that the method and timing of abortion must not be altered for the purpose of tissue collection. Each state currently permits or prohibits human fetal tissue research based on its laws and regulations or the “Uniform Anatomical Gift Act,” enacted in 1968 [17], a model legislation that stipulates the donation and use of bodies and tissues after death, permitting the postmortem donation of bodies and tissues with the consent of the individual, family, or legal representative [18]. More recently, federal policies regarding fetal tissue research have continued to evolve, including policy changes restricting the use of federal funding for research involving fetal tissue obtained from elective abortions [19].

In France, the National Advisory Ethics Council for Health and Life Sciences (Comité consultatif national d’éthique pour les sciences de la vie et de la santé), was established following the birth of the country’s first child conceived through in-vitro fertilization [20]. In 1984, the Council issued the “Opinion on Sampling of Dead Human Embryonic and Fetal Tissues for Therapeutic, Diagnostic, and Scientific Purposes” [21], stating that the research use of dead fetuses under 22 weeks of gestation is permitted under certain conditions if it is useful for therapeutic development. In 1994, despite enacting the “Bioethics Law” to clarify the principles regarding respect for the human body and its use in the civil code, the legislation for collecting and using fetal tissues remained unclear. The law was further revised in 2004 to specify the rules governing the use of fetuses that have died as a result of abortion or miscarriage for research purposes, reflecting expectations for future fetal tissue transplantation research. Article L. 1241–5 of the Public Health Code [22] stipulates that written informed consent must be obtained from female donors after they have decided to undergo an abortion, that tissues must not be collected from minors or donors under legal guardianship for any purpose other than investigating the causes of miscarriage or fetal abnormalities, and that research plans must be submitted to biomedical organizations in advance.

Thus, in several countries, in response to controversies surrounding abortion policy and the use of fetal tissue in research, the need for clear rules has been recognized, leading to the establishment of explicit laws and regulations. These legal frameworks not only clarify key procedures, including who may serve as a tissue donor (with the pregnant individual generally acting as the primary decision-maker), how consent should be obtained, and what considerations are required to minimize risks to women who terminate their pregnancies and to prevent the misuse of fetuses. They also provide a solid ethical and legal foundation that justifies the collection and research use of fetal tissues. By contrast, Japan has not established a similarly comprehensive legal and regulatory framework governing the research use of fetal tissues, giving rise to the challenges discussed below.

Laws and regulations regarding human fetal tissue research in Japan

In Japan, amid the anticipated expansion of the field of regenerative medicine, the “Expert Committee on Human Stem Cell-Based Clinical Research” (hereinafter referred to as the “Expert Committee”) was established in 2002 under the Science and Technology Division of the Health Science Council to formulate guidelines for clinical research using human stem cells (excluding embryonic stem cells). Initially, guidelines were expected to be formulated within a year, including all requirements and procedures necessary for clinical research involving the transplantation of stem cells derived from aborted fetuses [23]. However, no consensus was reached on fundamental issues, including the ethical acceptability of research involving aborted fetuses, the decision-making process of donors, and, in particular, the treatment of fetuses under 12 weeks of gestation, whose legal status remains unclear [5,23–28].

Furthermore, in July 2004, an incident was reported at an obstetrics and gynecology clinic in Yokohama City, where aborted fetuses under 12 weeks of gestation were disposed of as general waste, provoking strong public discomfort and resistance [29]. In light of this, members of the Expert Committee raised concerns that the research use of aborted fetuses might not be socially acceptable [25]. Consequently, the “Guidelines for Clinical Research Using Human Stem Cells” came into effect in 2006 [30], considerably later than initially planned, and fetal tissue was excluded from their scope [28].

The formulation of these guidelines was originally intended to govern clinical research, and basic research involving fetal tissue was outside the scope of the discussions from the outset. In 2014, succeeding these guidelines, the “Act on the Safety of Regenerative Medicine (ASRM) [31]” came into effect. However, it does not explicitly address the use of fetal tissue, nor does it exclude it, implying that applications involving fetal tissue may fall within its scope. Nonetheless, whether such applications would be approved in practice is unclear. As the Act primarily governs clinical applications of regenerative medicine, a clear framework for basic research involving fetal tissue remains underdeveloped.

Complexities associated with human fetal tissue research in Japan

In Japan, the regulatory status of clinical research involving human fetal tissue remains unclear, while basic research has not been addressed at the national regulatory level. In practice, for example, at Kyoto University, basic research on the induction of germ cells from human pluripotent stem cells, including using testicular or ovarian cells from aborted fetuses is permitted with the approval of the institutional ethics review committee [32]. Specifically, such research was reviewed under ethical guidelines applicable at the time, including the “Ethical Guidelines for Human Genome/Gene Analysis Research,” which were later integrated into the current Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Biological Research Involving Human Subjects (hereinafter referred to as the “Life Science Guidelines”). Nevertheless, these guidelines do not include provisions specifically addressing human fetal tissue research. As a result, without clear legal or regulatory grounds, criticism has been voiced over the current situation where fetal tissue research has become a fait accompli [27]. Below, we describe three issues that currently impact fetal tissue research in Japan and also provide their potential countermeasures.

Burdens associated with induced abortion on potential donors

Given that potential donors of fetal tissues are females who terminate their pregnancies, the burden of induced abortion must be considered, particularly in light of the principle of non-maleficence. In the Japanese context, the emotional and psychological burdens associated with abortion may be amplified by the physical, financial, and institutional conditions under which it is provided. Induced abortion can be performed up to 22 weeks of gestation in Japan according to a notification from the Ministry of Health and Welfare [33]. In 2023, a total of 126,734 induced abortion cases were reported, with approximately 94% of them being early abortions performed before 12 weeks of gestation [34].

Access to less invasive abortion methods is limited in Japan, and procedures such as dilation and (sharp) curettage are frequent [35], despite World Health Organization (WHO) recommendations favoring less invasive methods [36]. Abortion pills were only recently approved in April 2023 [37,38]. Moreover, unlike in countries such as the United Kingdom [39] and France [40], where abortion costs are fully covered under the public insurance system, patients in Japan are generally required to pay approximately USD 650–1,300 (100,000–200,000 yen) out of pocket [38].

Issues related to infrastructure arise in the Japanese medical settings where abortions are performed. Unlike in many countries where dedicated abortion clinics are available, abortions in Japan are most commonly performed within obstetrics and gynecology departments that handle childbirth (as well as stillbirth) and infertility treatments. This creates a challenging environment for accommodating the diverse patients who seek medical support [41,42]. The education of healthcare professionals in obstetrics primarily emphasizes the importance of respecting life and supporting women during childbirth, while offering limited opportunities to learn how to care for patients seeking abortions [43–47], further exacerbating the burden on women seeking abortion care.

These conditions are important factors in assessing donor burden and the voluntariness of decision-making in the context of fetal tissue donation. Therefore, when considering requests for fetal tissue donation, careful attention should be paid to ensuring voluntary decision-making and minimizing additional physical, financial, and psychological burdens, including emotional harm. While these considerations arise in the context of fetal tissue research, they also underscore the need to improve the broader environment surrounding induced abortion in Japan.

Requiring male partner consent for donation of fetal tissues

In Japan, induced abortion is considered a criminal offense under the Criminal Code [48], within a legal framework largely structured around heterosexual partnerships. However, its illegality can be negated by meeting the specific requirements in the “Maternal Health Act” [49], which state that if there is a risk of serious harm to the pregnant female’s health for physical or economic reasons – with “economic reasons” broadly interpreted in practice and not requiring proof of financial hardship – or if a female becomes pregnant after non-consensual intercourse (unable to resist or refuse due to assault or intimidation), then an abortion can be performed by a designated physician with the consent of the female and their spouse (including a common-law partner) [50,51]. Female consent alone is deemed sufficient in specific circumstances, such as when the spouse is unknown, unable to express their will, or deceased. In addition, the MHLW has indicated that consent from the male partner is not required if the couple is unmarried or if the marital relationship has effectively broken down [52,53]. However, these conditions remain relatively limited and often require case-by-case judgment. As a result, physicians frequently face difficulties in determining whether spousal consent is necessary [54], and in practice, they often request consent from the male partner even when it is not legally required [55]. Indeed, Japan remains one of only sixteen countries in the world that still require male (spousal) consent for abortion, among them Indonesia, Saudi Arabia, and Morocco [56], raising significant concerns regarding the violation of female reproductive rights. However, when consent for abortion is obtained from both biological parents, it is difficult to justify—within Japan’s legal framework, which lacks clear principles regarding consent for the use of fetal tissues—why only the female’s consent is deemed sufficient for subsequent fetal tissue donation.

The “Life Science Guidelines” [57], considered one of the fundamental research guidelines in Japan, states that the following genetic information is classified as personal information (personal identification code):

The sequence of bases that make up deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) collected from cells: genome data (a string of characters representing the sequence of bases that make up deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) collected from cells) that can be used to authenticate a person based on genetic information, such as whole genome sequence data, whole exome sequence data, whole genome single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) data, sequence data consisting of at least 40 mutually independent SNPs, and repeated sequences of 4 base units at 9 or more loci (short tandem repeats; STRs) [58].

Thus, when analyzing such genetic information, even if indirectly about a male biological parent using fetal tissues, then the purpose of its use must be specified and consent must be obtained from the male parent under Japanese law and regulation.

In many countries where abortion requires only the woman’s consent, it is sufficient to obtain her consent alone for fetal tissue donation. In Japan, however, where consent from both partners is often required for abortion, this structure can make subsequent decisions about tissue donation more complicated. One possible approach might be to limit donors to stable couples in which both partners provide consent for abortion, thereby excluding females who have experienced significant psychological burdens from abortion as potential donors. However, even then there have also been cases where males—seemingly supportive of their female counterparts while accompanying them to the clinic—were actually only motivated by abusive or controlling intentions to monitor the completion of the abortion [59,60]. Therefore, in such cases the involvement of healthcare professionals and research coordinators who can effectively identify such scenarios and exclude them as candidates for tissue donation (and abortion, in certain situations) would be essential. As emphasized by Vawter et al. [61], the physical, psychological, and privacy-related risks associated with fetal tissue donation primarily accrue to the woman, who has “a unique and privileged relationship with the fetus”; accordingly, she should be regarded as the primary decision-maker in the consent process. If a physician determines that the male partner’s consent for abortion is unnecessary, or if the male partner’s genetic information is unidentifiable from the research, consent for fetal tissue donation should be obtained solely from the biological mother.

Ambiguity in the legal positioning of dead fetuses under 12 weeks of gestation

Japan has no laws or national guidelines that explicitly permit the research use of corpses in general, including aborted fetuses. The “Postmortem Examination and Corpse Preservation Act” [62], originally established to regulate the dissection of corpses and preserve specimens to be used for investigating the cause of death in cased of undetermined or disease-related fatalities, was not designed to facilitate the research use of human tissues [50,63–65]. While the Life Science Guidelines regulates research involving human subjects in general, including “deceased persons” in their definition of research subjects, these guidelines assume that deceased persons refer to donors who have died after sample donation. Consequently, research using samples derived from corpses is considered to fall outside the scope of the guidelines [65]. However, it can also be argued that the research use of corpses has been customarily supported (or at least not explicitly prohibited) as a practice in Japan [63]. This is evidenced by the establishment of multiple academic-level rules that, over time, have guided the handling of human remains in research settings [64]. Hence, this indirectly indicates that the research use of dead fetuses can be supported for scientifically significant research that cannot be achieved otherwise.

However, in Japan, a dead fetus under 12 weeks of gestation is—strictly speaking—not legally considered a corpse because the “Postmortem Examination and Corpse Preservation Act [62]” and “Graveyards and Burials Act [66]” define a corpse as one aged 4 months (12 weeks) of gestation or older. As a result, the legal status of dead fetuses under 12 weeks of gestation remains unclear, with no rules restricting the purpose of their use [63]. However, considering the pious sentiment shared in society, it is necessary that such fetuses be treated similarly to those aged 12 weeks or older. The only references available regarding the research use of fetuses under 12 weeks of gestation are the “Opinion on the Appropriateness and Acceptable Range of the Research Use of Organs from Dead Fetuses and Newborns,” published by the Japan Society of Obstetrics and Gynecology in 1987 [67], and its commentary that was later published in 2001 [27,63,68]. These documents state the following:

Regardless of the gestation period, bioethical considerations are essential for fetuses, which will become human beings in the future, and they must be handled with a sense of pious respect so as not to violate their dignity. Recently, the application of tissue stem cells present in the organs of dead fetuses and newborns to regenerative medicine has attracted attention. Our academic society does not prohibit the development of research for such purposes. Although the obstetrics and gynecology department is primarily responsible for organ donation, we hope each member fully understands the significance of the research and independently decides whether to offer cooperation. In addition, needless to say, for any research purposes, approval must be obtained from the ethics committee established by each facility [68].

Furthermore, the opinion also outlines key conditions for the research use of dead fetuses and newborns, including compliance with the “Postmortem Examination and Corpse Preservation Act,” the absence of alternative means, the prospects that such research could yield meaningful results, implementation in principle by physicians, consent from both parents, and respect for both parents’ privacy [67].

As for the disposal of fetuses after research use, while those aged 12 weeks or older must be buried or cremated in accordance with the Graveyards and Burials Act, those under 12 weeks of gestation are disposed of as infectious waste under the “Waste Management and Public Cleansing Act” [69]. Additionally, for handling dead fetuses under 12 weeks of gestation and their tissues, it should be confirmed in advance with the local government of the research institution whether an applicable “Ena ordinance” exists and should be applied [70]. The term “Ena” generally refers to tissues expelled after childbirth or abortion, such as the placenta and umbilical cord. The “Ena ordinance” stipulates the permission or notification required for businesses involved in the collection and disposal of “Ena,” as well as conditions for setting up a disposal facility. As of 2024, eight prefectures have established such an ordinance. However, many municipalities also have their own ordinances (sometimes covered by the “funeral home ordinance”), and their content varies depending on the local government [70,71]. For example, some definitions of “Ena” include fetuses under 12 weeks of gestation, while others do not. Similarly, some apply to the disposal of fetuses after research use, while others do not. Furthermore, whether these ordinances apply to research use and whether notification to local governments is required varies by ordinance.

“Ena ordinance” was created based on ancient customs that were passed down [72–75]. In Japan, there was a custom called “Ena Osame (placenta placement),” in which “Ena” is handled with care and buried as the other self of the newborn child or as an entity with spiritual power (Figure 1). Its earliest mention is said to be in “Nihon Shoki (Chronicles of Japan),” the official history of Japan written in the 8th century [74]. However, with cholera epidemics in the late 19th century and the introduction of Western medicine, societal views on hygiene changed and burying “Ena” in areas around houses, near rivers, or in fields and mountains came to be considered unhygienic, resulting in the establishment of rules that govern the handling of “Ena” in various regions throughout Japan. Currently, the details regarding the ordinance vary from region to region, likely due to regional differences in practices handed down over generations for the disposal of “Ena.” When conducting fetal tissue research, collaborative research is performed by researchers from distinct regions, with tissues transferred and disposed of by different laboratories. Therefore, researchers and ethics review committees are encouraged to confirm the “Ena” ordinance with the respective local governments before conducting research.

Discussion

Human fetal tissue research is regulated by not only the rules and guidelines for biomedical research but also multiple laws and regulations regarding abortion, organ/tissue transplantation, reproductive medicine, and corpse handling/burial. Consequently, identifying the intersections between existing laws and regulations created for originally different purposes and reconstructing them into new norms remains challenging. Furthermore, as fetal tissue research is coupled to sociomedical systems, treatment of females, pious sentiment, culture, and customs that are widely shared throughout the country—it is essentially to consider such complex ethical, legal, and social aspects to conduct responsible biomedical research.

It is noteworthy to mention that the regulatory and ethical challenges described here are in fact not unique to Japan alone. For example, the physically burdensome practice of D&C remains common in countries such as India [76] and Mexico [77]. Additionally, laws requiring spousal consent for abortion are still enforced in 16 countries [56]. Even in places where abortion is legally available, women traveling from more restrictive jurisdictions may arrive at healthcare facilities after significant physical, emotional, and economic hardship—only to be asked whether they would be willing to donate fetal tissue. While such requests are not coercive in a strict sense, they may introduce subtle forms of pressure—for instance, when an individual worries that declining donation could jeopardize or delay the abortion they have finally secured. Therefore, minimizing the physical and psychological burden on women and respecting their autonomy is a vital prerequisite for conducting ethically sound fetal tissue research within diverse legal and cultural settings.

In addition, the lack of clear legal and regulatory framework remains a serious problem, underscoring the need for the establishment of regulations at the national level in Japan, or, at a minimum, for the formulation of clear institutional rules, if national regulations remain absent. Such ambiguity can lead to hesitation and risk aversion behavior that discourages even ethically sound research—a phenomenon which is prominent in countries where social norms emphasize conformity—such as Japan. This may also apply to other countries with strong collectivist cultures and high conformity tendencies [78], especially in many Asian countries. This mentality stems not from a lack of interest in such research topics, but rather from researchers fearing the potential repercussions of conducting socially sensitive research in these societies. As a result, even when robust frameworks such as the ISSCR’s IC standards are available, researchers may still feel at unease to proceed.

Future perspectives

In light of these barriers, a gradual but progressive approach to implementing fetal tissue research and establishing an ethical governance framework—while exercising utmost caution—is required to reduce societal resistance and build public trust. In particular, for countries like Japan, where legal and institutional frameworks are not yet fully established, a practical approach for now would be to carefully accumulate research achievements through small-scale basic research and to steadily expand the research framework while bolstering public trust. Additionally, establishing a robust platform to manage and record the collection and sharing of fetal tissue— first among only a small group of researchers and then gradually expanding—would not only enhance research efficiency but also strengthen transparency and sustainability. Such a platform would then increase visibility of the status of tissue donations and their actual use by researchers, which would not only lead to better public awareness and understanding, but also allow the periodic evaluation of future directions in research activities. Considering the importance of fetal tissue research in advancing regenerative medicine, establishing transparent and ethically sustainable governance systems will not only support responsible basic research, but also facilitate future translational research and the development of clinically relevant cell-based interventions. In the long term, these efforts will contribute to the establishment of a more robust and ethically-sound research infrastructure for conducting fetal tissue research in Japan.

Achieving consistency and harmonization between internationally recommended standards and the distinct rules of each country for research will be challenging but important, in order to establish the foundation necessary to achieve sustainable ethical developments in fetal tissue research. This study will serve as a framework for implementing internationally recognized minimum ethical standards within the research process in countries like Japan, where clear legal and regulatory frameworks for human fetal tissue research are lacking, and for supporting appropriate research conduct.
