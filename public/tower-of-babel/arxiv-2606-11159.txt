Abstract
. We study the pressureless Euler Alignment system with unidirectional velocity 𝐮 = ( u , 0 , … , 0 ) {\bf u}=(u,0,\ldots,0) . By re-casting the system as a family of coupled scalar balance laws—one for each horizontal slice of ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} —we are able to prove existence, uniqueness, and stability within the class of unidirectional solutions, under the assumption of a bounded Lipschitz communication protocol. The nonlocal coupling between horizontal slices provides the system with a rich structure that is absent from the 1D setting and also constitutes the main technical difficulty of the present work. We construct our solutions as limits of sticky particle Cucker–Smale dynamics, discretizing first transverse to the flow and then along it. The transverse discretization depends crucially on the more subtle of our two complementary stability estimates, which relies only on L 1 L^{1} - L ∞ L^{\infty} control of the flux difference. This low-regularity estimate is essential since our discretized fluxes cannot in general be expected to converge in (for instance) Lipschitz seminorm. The estimate itself is inspired by work of Bouchut and Perthame [ 6 ] and adapted here through a careful additional analysis of the nonlocality. In order to meaningfully compare the dynamics along different horizontal slices, both stability estimates are most naturally formulated in terms of quantities that involve the optimal coupling between the projections of two density profiles onto ℝ d − 1 \mathbb{R}^{d-1} . We also investigate the long-time behavior of unidirectional solutions. In addition to treating the standard heavy-tailed regime, we make the simple observation that the unidirectional geometry allows for flocking (with a rate independent of the number of agents) even when the communication protocol vanishes in a cylindrical neighborhood of the axis parallel to the flow. This demonstrates that direct communication along the direction of motion is not necessary for flocking to occur.

1.
Introduction

We are concerned with the following Euler Alignment system of collective behavior:

(1)

\left\{\begin{split}\partial_{t}\rho+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf{u})&=0\\
\partial_{t}(\rho{\bf u})+\nabla\cdot(\rho{\bf u}\otimes{\bf u})&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(\bm{x}-\bm{\xi})(\mathbf{u}(\bm{\xi})-\mathbf{u}(\bm{x}))\rho(\bm{x})\rho(\bm{\xi})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\end{split}\right.\qquad\qquad\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,t>0.

In this system,
\rho=\rho(\bm{x},t)
and
{\bf u}={\bf u}(\bm{x},t)
denote the macroscopic density and velocity of a continuum of agents, which interact through the nonnegative
communication protocol

\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
. This paper will focus on the case of
unidirectional
dynamics, where all velocities point in the same direction. Without loss of generality, we write
{\bf u}(\bm{x},t)=(u(\bm{x},t),0,\ldots,0)
in this case, where
u(\bm{x},t)
is real-valued. The unidirectional dynamics are then governed by the following system. (Here and below, we use
\partial_{1}
to denote differentiation with respect to the first spatial variable, and we write
\bm{x}=(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
.)

(2)

\left\{\begin{split}\partial_{t}\rho+\partial_{1}(\rho u)&=0\\
\partial_{t}(\rho u)+\partial_{1}(\rho u^{2})&=\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\phi(x-\xi,y-\eta)(u(\xi,\eta)-u(x,y))\rho(x,y)\rho(\xi,\eta)\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\mathrm{d}\eta,\end{split}\quad(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1},\,t>0.\right.

We are concerned with the existence, uniqueness, and stability of weak solutions of (
2
), as well as the long-time behavior of these weak solutions (and of solutions of the related Cucker–Smale system of ODEs, introduced below). We will make two key assumptions on our communication protocol for the entire manuscript. First, we will assume that
\phi=\phi(x,y)
is even in both its arguments:

(3)

\phi(x,y)=\phi(-x,y)=\phi(x,-y),\qquad\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R},\,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}.

And second, we will assume that
\phi
is bounded and Lipschitz in its second argument:

(4)

\sup_{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\phi(x,y)+\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{R},\,y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\\
y_{1}\neq y_{2}\end{subarray}}\frac{|\phi(x,y_{1})-\phi(x,y_{2})|}{|y_{1}-y_{2}|}<+\infty.

In particular, we will
not
analyze Euler Alignment dynamics equipped with a strongly singular communication protocol—this requires different tools. We will remark later on about the extent to which assumption (
4
) might be able to be weakened.

Finally, we will always normalize
\rho
to have mass
1
:

(5)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho=1.

1.1.
Cucker–Smale-type systems and flocking dynamics

The Euler Alignment system (
1
) arises as a hydrodynamic limit of the celebrated Cucker–Smale system of ODE’s
[
14
,
15
]
:

(6)

\left\{\begin{split}\dot{\bm{x}}_{i}&=\bm{v}_{i},\\
\dot{\bm{v}}_{i}&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{N}m_{j}\phi(\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{x}_{j})(\bm{v}_{j}-\bm{v}_{i}).\end{split}\right.

The Cucker–Smale system as written above consists of
N
agents, each associated to a triple
(m_{i},\bm{x}_{i},\bm{v}_{i})
indicating its mass
m_{i}\geq 0
(fixed in time), position
\bm{x}_{i}=\bm{x}_{i}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and velocity
\bm{v}_{i}=\bm{v}_{i}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. When the number of agents is very large, the use of (
1
) becomes appropriate, and
\rho=\rho(\bm{x},t)
and
{\bf u}={\bf u}(\bm{x},t)
denote the corresponding macroscopic quantities. Often one passes through a kinetic equation (not discussed here), see for example
[
27
,
25
]
, and then passes from the kinetic equation to (
1
), see
[
56
,
33
,
20
]
, and also
[
34
,
35
,
36
]
for the kinetic-to-hydrodynamic limit that includes a pressure term in the limiting system. In the framework developed below, we will circumvent the kinetic level and pass directly from (
6
) (equipped with collision rules) to (
1
).

One of the key features of Cucker–Smale-type systems is their compatibility with so-called ‘flocking dynamics,’ where the velocities of all agents align in the time-asymptotic limit, and the diameter of the positions of the agents remains uniformly bounded for all time. More specifically, we say that
flocking
occurs when

(7)

\sup_{t\geq 0}\mathscr{D}(t)\leq\overline{\mathscr{D}}<+\infty,\qquad\mathscr{D}(t)=\max_{i,j}|\bm{x}_{i}(t)-\bm{x}_{j}(t)|,

while
velocity alignment
refers to the situation where

(8)

\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathscr{A}(t)=0,\qquad\mathscr{A}(t)=\max_{i,j}|\bm{v}_{i}(t)-\bm{v}_{j}(t)|.

These definitions have natural counterparts in the hydrodynamic setting, which we will discuss later on. The behavior captured by (
7
) and (
8
) mimics—in a simplistic but analytically tractable way—the alignment dynamics of naturally occurring many-agent systems in the real-world, the prototypical example being a flock of birds. At the level of the equations, the driving mechanism appears in the ‘alignment forcing’ on the right side of equations (
1
)
2
and (
6
)
2
. In (
6
), for example, each term in the sum works to bring the velocity of agent
i
closer to that of agent
j
, and the contribution of each term to the sum depends on the difference
\bm{v}_{j}(t)-\bm{v}_{i}(t)
in velocities, the mass
m_{j}
of agent
j
, and the strength
\phi(\bm{x}_{i}(t)-\bm{x}_{j}(t))
of the communication between agents
i
and
j
. The emergence of a global structure (in this case, velocity alignment and flocking) from these possibly small-scale, pairwise interactions, is a complex phenomenon that provides a rich field of study. In particular, understanding exactly which initial configurations and which communication protocols will lead to flocking behavior is a subject of much active research and recent progress. We make no attempt to give a comprehensive overview of the by now expansive literature on the subject, but we refer the reader to the survey papers
[
51
,
9
,
62
,
57
]
, the book
[
56
]
, and references therein, for a more thorough discussion of the state of the art.

1.2.
The main focus: wellposedness for unidirectional solutions

Naturally, a prerequisite for the emergence of flocking in a solution of (
1
) or (
6
) is the global-in-time existence of that solution. For the ODE system (
6
), existence and uniqueness of solutions is either trivial or well-understood under mild assumptions on
\phi
. But wellposedness of the Euler Alignment system (
1
) remains open except in some important special cases. The most complete theory is available when
d=1
and additionally
\phi
is even and locally integrable.
In this case, one can predict from the initial data whether the solution will remain regular for all time or lose regularity in finite time
[
64
,
10
,
65
,
44
]
. Furthermore, if the solution loses regularity in finite time, one can still continue it as a weak solution
[
46
,
23
,
21
]
, and it is even possible to say a fair amount about the large-time structure of the density profile
[
47
,
21
]
. In what follows, we will be concerned exclusively with the above-mentioned case where
\phi
is locally integrable (and in fact satisfies (
4
)); however, a relevant parallel line of research concerns the case where
\phi
is instead strongly singular near the origin; see for example
[
37
,
18
,
58
,
59
,
60
,
43
,
2
,
16
]
.

Most of the literature on the 1D case leans heavily on the presence of an additional conserved quantity and also on the ordering property of the real line—properties that both disappear in higher dimensions. Largely for this reason, wellposedness remains much less well-understood in general than it is on
\mathbb{R}
(but see for example
[
55
,
66
,
28
]
for some partial results). There is, however, a class of dynamics for (
1
) that is considerably richer than the pure 1D case but still comes with many of the technical advantages of the latter. We are referring to the so-called
unidirectional
setting already mentioned above, in which
\mathbf{u}(\bm{x},t)=(u(\bm{x},t),0,\ldots,0)
for some scalar-valued function
u
. In this setting, described by the system (
2
), particle trajectories are constrained to move along straight lines (preserving the ordering property from the 1D setting), and a special conserved quantity is still available. Therefore, for some purposes, the unidirectional initial value problem can be treated like a family of 1D problems. On the other hand, these 1D problems are all coupled together through the nonlocal communication, which causes considerable difficulty in understanding the regularity in the directions transverse to the flow and also allows for a fundamentally higher-dimensional richness in the system’s emergent structures.

For bounded, Lipschitz, radial
\phi
, the existence of classical unidirectional solutions for ‘sub-critical’ initial data was first proven in
[
42
]
. (See also
[
39
,
41
,
48
]
for results in the strongly singular protocol regime.) As in the 1D case, solutions starting from ‘supercritical’ initial data may lose regularity in finite time. However, unlike the 1D case, there is at present no theory for continuing such a solution beyond the blowup time. The main objective of this paper is to develop such a theory, and more generally, to establish the existence, uniqueness, and stability of unidirectional weak solutions of (
1
).

1.3.
The scalar balance law formulation

The key observation that allowed for the development of the 1D weak solution theory in
[
46
]
was the reduction of the two-equation Euler Alignment system to a single scalar balance law, in the spirit of Brenier and Grenier’s reduction of the pressureless Euler equations to a single scalar conservation law
[
8
]
. A similar reduction is available in the unidirectional setting; however, the result in this case is instead a
family
of coupled scalar balance laws parametrized by
y
, and one must decompose the density profile properly in order to obtain it. We give a quick, formal derivation below (which mostly parallels the one in
[
46
]
); a rigorous justification will be provided in Section
4
.

We start by noting that (
2
) can be rewritten in the form

(9)

\left\{\begin{split}\partial_{t}\rho+\partial_{1}(\rho u)&=0\\
\partial_{t}(\rho\psi)+\partial_{1}(\rho\psi u)&=0,\end{split}\right.\qquad(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1},\,t>0,

where

(10)

\psi=u+\Phi*\rho,

(11)

\Phi(x,y)=\int_{0}^{x}\phi(\xi,y)\,\mathrm{d}\xi,

and
*
represents convolution in the spatial variables:

(12)

(\Phi*\rho)(x,y,t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Phi(x-\xi,y-\eta)\rho(\xi,\eta,t)\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\mathrm{d}\eta.

Next, we consider the density profile as a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and disintegrate it with respect to horizontal slices. Letting
p_{2}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
denote projection onto the second argument, i.e.,
p_{2}(x,y)=y
, we define

\nu=(p_{2})_{\sharp}\rho(t)

(note that unidirectionality guarantees that
\nu
is time-independent), and then

\rho(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y,t)=\rho_{y}(\mathrm{d}x,t)\,\nu(\mathrm{d}y),

where
\rho_{y}(t)
is a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}
for each
t
and
\nu
-a.e.
y
. More specifically, the disintegration means that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}}f(x,y)\rho(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y,t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x,y)\rho_{y}(\mathrm{d}x,t)\bigg)\nu(\mathrm{d}y),

for any bounded Borel measurable function
f:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}
. We will refer to
\nu
as the ‘transverse measure’ of the system.
We may therefore define

M(x,y,t)=\rho_{y}((-\infty,x],t),\qquad Q(x,y,t)=\int_{(-\infty,x]}\psi(x,y,t)\rho_{y}(\mathrm{d}x,t).

Integrating the system (
9
) yields

(13)

\left\{\begin{split}\partial_{t}M+u\partial_{1}M&=0\\
\partial_{t}Q+u\partial_{1}Q&=0.\end{split}\right.

Given initial data
(M^{0},Q^{0})
for this system (with
M^{0}
nondecreasing), we may choose a function
A:[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
A(M^{0}(x,y),y)=Q^{0}(x,y)
. (This choice is unique if
M^{0}
is continuous.) For smooth solutions of (
13
), this relationship propagates in time:
A(M(x,y,t),y)=Q(x,y,t)
, so that

\partial_{t}M+\partial_{x}(A(M,y))=-u\partial_{1}M+\partial_{1}Q=(\psi-u)\partial_{1}M=B[\nu,M]\partial_{1}M,

where
B[\nu,M]
is equal to
\Phi*\rho
but is expressed in terms of
\nu
and
M
. Altogether, our system is:

(14)

\left\{\begin{split}&\partial_{t}M(x,y,t)+\partial_{x}(A(M(x,y,t),y))=B[\nu,M]\partial_{x}M(x,y,t),\\
&B[\nu,M](x,y,t)=(\Phi*(\nu\partial_{1}M))(x,y,t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Phi(x-\xi,y-\eta)\,\partial_{x}M(\mathrm{d}\xi,\eta,t)\,\nu(\mathrm{d}\eta).\end{split}\right.

1.4.
Entropy solutions

The system (
14
) admits a natural entropy condition in the sense of Kruzkov. We require

(15)

\partial_{t}|M-\alpha|\nu+\partial_{x}\big(\mathrm{sgn}(M-\alpha)(A(M,y)-A(\alpha,y))\big)\nu\leq B[\nu,M]\,\partial_{x}|M-\alpha|\nu,\qquad\text{ for all }\alpha\in\mathbb{R},

in the sense of distributions on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\times(0,T)
. More explicitly, we require that for every
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and every nonnegative
\zeta\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\times(0,T))
, the function
M
should satisfy

(16)

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Big[|M-\alpha|\,\partial_{t}\zeta+\mathrm{sgn}(M-\alpha)\big(A(M,y)-A(\alpha,y)\big)\,\partial_{x}\zeta+\zeta\,B[\nu,M]\,\partial_{x}|M-\alpha|\Big]\mathrm{d}x\,\nu(dy)\,\mathrm{d}t\geq 0.

Note that by taking
\zeta(x,y,t)=\widetilde{\zeta}(x,t)b_{\varepsilon}(y)
, where
b_{\varepsilon}
is a standard mollifier, and taking
\varepsilon\to 0+
, we can reduce (
16
) to the condition that for
\nu
-a.e.
y
, we have

(17)

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Big[|M-\alpha|\,\partial_{t}\widetilde{\zeta}+\mathrm{sgn}(M-\alpha)\big(A(M,y)-A(\alpha,y)\big)\,\partial_{x}\widetilde{\zeta}+\widetilde{\zeta}\,B[\nu,M]\,\partial_{x}|M-\alpha|\Big]\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\geq 0,

for every nonnegative
\widetilde{\zeta}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}\times(0,T))
. In what follows, it will be useful to have access to both (
16
) and (
17
).

We formalize the above in the following definitions.

.

We say that the triple
(M^{0},A,\nu)
of initial data, flux, and transverse measure is

for the system (

) if the following conditions are satisfied.

•

\nu
is a compactly supported probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
.

•

The function
M^{0}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}
is Borel measurable. Furthermore, there exists
R^{0}>0
such that for
\nu
-a.e.

, we have that
M^{0}(x,y)=0
for
x<-R^{0}
and
M^{0}(x,y)=1
for

. Finally, the function

is nondecreasing on
\mathbb{R}
for
\nu
-a.e.
y
.

•

The function
A:[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}
is Borel measurable, and normalized so that
A(0,y)=0
for
\nu
-a.e.

. Furthermore, the Lipschitz seminorm of the map

.

Definition 1.2
.

Fix
T>0
. We say that a function

is an

of (

if the following conditions are satisfied.

(a)

There exists

M(x,y,t)=0

\mathbb{R}
for
\nu
-a.e.

(b)

\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\times(0,T)

(c)

We have

(18)

.

In light of the requirement (

1.5.
Analytical framework and preliminary statement of results

The analysis of the present work begins essentially where the 1D theory of
[
46
]
leaves off. Most of the core results of the latter can be thought of as proving the existence, uniqueness, and stability of entropy solutions of (
14
) in the special case where
\nu
is the Dirac mass at a single point in
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
. In fact, as discussed in Section
3.1
below, this theory can be extended without too much trouble to the case where
\nu
is any fixed, atomic measure.

It is natural, then, to try to construct entropy solutions of (
14
) as the limit of a sequence of solutions
M^{k}(x,y,t)
associated to admissible triples
(M^{0,k},A^{k},\nu_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
, where
\nu_{k}
is atomic—indeed, this is more or less what we ultimately do in the present work. However, meaningfully measuring the difference between two entropy solutions
M
,
\widetilde{M}
associated to distinct transverse measures
\nu
,
\widetilde{\nu}
(which may not even have the same support in
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
) requires us to be able to compare values of
M
and
\widetilde{M}
associated to different horizontal slices. To resolve this difficulty, we embed the optimal coupling between two such measures (with respect to the cost
c(y,\widetilde{y})=|y-\widetilde{y}|
), which we denote by
\bm{\pi}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{y})
, across our analytical framework. Our stability results, for instance, are statements about the quantity
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\|M(\cdot,y,t)-\widetilde{M}(\cdot,\widetilde{y},t)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\bm{\pi}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{y})
. We believe this is the most natural way to develop the theory. Note that this quantity is related to the usual Wasserstein-1 distance between the underlying density profiles via

(19)

W_{1}(\rho(t),\widetilde{\rho}(t))\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\|M(\cdot,y,t)-\widetilde{M}(\cdot,z,t)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\bm{\pi}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}z)+W_{1}(\nu,\widetilde{\nu}),

where the notation ‘
W_{1}
’ is used to denote the Wasserstein-1 distance in both
d
and
d-1
dimensions (and will also be used below to denote the same in
1
dimension). One can see that this inequality holds by realizing that the integral on the right side can be written as

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\!\!\|M(\cdot,y,t)-\widetilde{M}(\cdot,\widetilde{y},t)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\bm{\pi}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{y})=\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\!\!\!W_{1}(\rho_{y}(t),\widetilde{\rho}_{\widetilde{y}}(t))\,\bm{\pi}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{y})=\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x-\widetilde{x}|\,\gamma_{y,\widetilde{y}}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{x},t)\,\bm{\pi}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{y}),

where
\gamma_{y,\widetilde{y}}(t)
is an optimal coupling between
\rho_{y}(t)
and
\widetilde{\rho}_{\widetilde{y}}(t)
. Since
\gamma_{y,\widetilde{y}}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{x},t)\bm{\pi}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{y})
is a coupling between
\rho(t)
and
\widetilde{\rho}(t)
, we have

\displaystyle W_{1}(\rho(t),\widetilde{\rho}(t))

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(|x-\widetilde{x}|+|y-\widetilde{y}|)\,\gamma_{y,\widetilde{y}}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{x},t)\,\bm{\pi}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{y})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\|M(\cdot,y,t)-\widetilde{M}(\cdot,z,t)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\bm{\pi}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}z)+W_{1}(\nu,\widetilde{\nu}),

as claimed.

Uniqueness and Stability of Entropy Solutions.
In Section
2
, we use the basic Kruzkov doubling-of-the-variables technique (c.f.
[

,
49
]
), adapted to the unidirectional framework described above, to establish two different stability estimates on entropy solutions of (

(M^{0},A,\nu)

in an

sense (made precise in Section

) but might not converge in the stronger sense where

is replaced by the Lipschitz seminorm. This renders the linear-in-time stability estimate insufficient for passing to the limit for general initial data. Our approach to the second stability bound is inspired by the work
[
6
]
of Bouchut and Perthame. The full statement of our uniqueness and stability result is contained in Theorem
2.1
.

Existence of Entropy Solutions.

(M^{0},A,\nu)

]

of admissible approximants. We generate an associated sequence of entropy solutions

and pass to the limit, using our previously established stability estimates. Our limiting procedure is shown to preserve the entropy conditions, thus finishing the existence proof.

Recovery and Approximation of the Euler Alignment dynamics.

Flocking.
In Section
5
, we turn to the question of long-time behavior. We show that, under natural assumptions on the communication protocol
\phi
and the initial density profile, solutions of the Cucker–Smale ODE’s associated to unidirectional initial data must experience flocking and velocity alignment, with a rate that is independent of the number of agents. The novelty here is that our assumptions allow for
\supp\phi
to have a cylinder of degeneracy around the
x
-axis. This has significant implications for our understanding of the
fundamental mechanism underlying the emergent behavior: Communication in the direction of motion is not a necessary condition for flocking to occur. We state flocking theorems for both the Cucker–Smale and Euler Alignment systems, but we write down proofs only for the discrete setting. A blueprint for how to extend the results to the continuum level is contained in
[
46
]
.

1.6.
Additional commentary and related literature

1.6.1.
Degenerate protocols

A few words are in order about our flocking result (see Section
5
for additional context). First of all, despite the implications mentioned above, our proof of flocking does not require any special tools. On the contrary, it is a rather simple adaptation of an existing argument (found, for instance, in
[
56
]
) that is used to establish flocking for any initial data under the assumption of a radial, heavy-tailed protocol (i.e.,
\phi\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\backslash L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
). We speculate that the reason it has gone unnoticed until now is because our argument as written really only applies in the context of unidirectional dynamics in two or more spatial dimensions. It would be very interesting to try to extend the paradigm suggested by our theorem to more general ‘almost unidirectional’ dynamics, c.f.
[
42
]
. We leave the exploration of this problem for future work.

Our flocking result also shares some similarity to work of Dietert and Shvydkoy
[
17
]
, which used a sophisticated corrector method to prove flocking for arbitrary initial conditions when the communication protocol is radial and degenerate on a ball near the origin. We emphasize that despite the similarity in spirit, the two results cover essentially disjoint regimes: Ours requires a very special geometry in the initial conditions but is more flexible in the allowed degeneracy of communication; on the other hand, the work of Dietert and Shvydkoy can handle arbitrary initial conditions but requires the region of communication degeneracy to be bounded.

1.6.2.
Limiting configurations

Similar to many previous results, our flocking theorem allows us to deduce the existence of a limiting density profile
\rho_{\infty}
to which
\rho(t)
converges (in an appropriate reference frame, and in the weak-
*
sense of measures). The understanding the structure of this limiting configuration is an interesting but subtle question, which has been studied for 1D weak solutions of the Euler Alignment system
[
47
,
21
]
, 1D and unidirectional classical solutions of the Euler Alignment system
[
45
,
40
,
39
]
, and 1D Cucker–Smale systems
[
24
,
26
]
. Much remains unknown even in the already-studied regimes, but the present work opens the door to the consideration of an additional setting, namely that of unidirectional weak solutions.

1.6.3.
Comparison with
[
12
]

A recent paper by Carrillo, Choi, and Tadmor
[
12
]
(c.f. also
[
62
,
63
]
) considers the following version of the Euler Alignment system, with the Reynolds stress
\tau
determined from the Lagrangian dynamics of the underlying kinetic equation (not discussed here):

(20)

\left\{\begin{split}\partial_{t}\rho+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf{u})&=0\\
\partial_{t}(\rho{\bf u})+\nabla\cdot(\rho{\bf u}\otimes{\bf u}+\tau)&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(\bm{x}-\bm{\xi})(\mathbf{u}(\bm{\xi})-\mathbf{u}(\bm{x}))\rho(\bm{x})\rho(\bm{\xi})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi},\end{split}\right.\qquad\qquad\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,t>0.

The authors of
[
12
]
develop a wellposedness theory for (
20
) and show that, under natural assumptions on
\phi
, the Reynolds stress vanishes in the time-asymptotic limit (though it may be nontrivial for all finite time). Of course, if
\tau\equiv 0
for all time, then (
1
) and (
20
) are the same. The wellposedness theory provided by
[
12
]
therefore reduces the question of global-in-time regularity of solutions to (
1
) to a question of whether or not
\tau
is ever nontrivial for the dynamics of the underlying kinetic system. In the regime covered by our work, on the other hand (which is limited to unidirectional dynamics), the primary interest is in the case where the Lagrangian trajectories associated with the kinetic system would intersect, generating a nonzero Reynolds stress. Therefore, our results are essentially disjoint from theirs. However, it would be interesting to compare the solutions of the system (
20
) provided by
[
12
]
generated from unidirectional data to those of (
2
) provided by the present work.

1.6.4.
Weakly singular protocols

The analysis of the second author and Tan in
[
46
]
develops the wellposedness theory for 1D entropic solutions under the assumption of an arbitrary symmetric communication protocol
\phi\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
. It could be expected that the unidirectional theory might be available for a class of protocols as broad as those satisfying (
3
) and

(21)

\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\int_{[-R,R]}\phi(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}x<+\infty,\qquad\text{ for any fixed }R>0.

Certain aspects of our analysis below (e.g., uniqueness of solutions, flocking for Cucker–Smale dynamics) do indeed still work for such protocols, with no essential changes. Other aspects (e.g., our Bouchut–Perthame-type stability estimate) have little hope of being adapted successfully to cover the case of (
21
). The authors are cautiously optimistic that the framework established in this paper can serve as a basis for the development of a weak solution theory that includes protocols satisfying (
21
), and we plan to explore this direction in future work. Relaxing the assumption (
4
), however, appears to require sufficiently different ideas and techniques that the resulting theory would be more naturally developed in separate work.

1.7.
Notation and Conventions

Most of the notation we use is standard, but let us collect a few conventions here for the convenience of the reader. Unless otherwise specified, ‘
C
’ will denote a constant that may change from line to line but is independent of any quantities that may later be taken to zero or infinity. All functions and measures we consider will be defined on some Euclidean space or a subset thereof, unless explicitly specified otherwise. We will use
[f]_{\mathrm{Lip}}
to denote the Lipschitz seminorm of a function
f
, i.e.,
[f]_{\mathrm{Lip}}=\sup_{x_{1}\neq x_{2}}\frac{|f(x_{1})-f(x_{2})|}{|x_{1}-x_{2}|}
. We use the word ‘measurable’ to mean ‘Borel measurable’ unless explicitly noted otherwise.
We will use
\mathcal{M}
to denote the space of signed Radon measures, and
\mathcal{P}
will denote the space of probability measures (with
\mathcal{P}_{c}
denoting probability measures with compact support). We will always equip
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{P}
with the weak-
*
topology, defined via convergence with respect to test functions
\zeta\in C_{c}
which are continuous and compactly supported. (We will use the standard notation
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}
to denote weak-
*
convergence.) However, all the probability measures we will consider will belong to a family whose support is contained in some compact set. For such a family, weak-
*
convergence is equivalent to weak convergence (i.e., convergence with respect to test functions which are continuous and bounded), and the weak topology is metrizable via the Wasserstein-1 metric, or equivalently the bounded Lipschitz distance. For probability measures
\mu
and
\widetilde{\mu}
on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, this is defined as follows.

(22)

W_{1}(\mu,\widetilde{\mu})=\inf_{\bm{\pi}}\int\!\!\!\int|y-\widetilde{y}|\,\bm{\pi}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{y})=\sup\bigg\{\int f(y)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}y)-\int f(y)\,\widetilde{\mu}(\mathrm{d}y):\sup|f|,[f]_{\mathrm{Lip}}\leq 1\bigg\},

where the infimum in the first expression is taken over all couplings
\bm{\pi}
whose first marginal is
\mu
and whose second marginal is
\widetilde{\mu}
, i.e., all
\bm{\pi}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{k})
such that
\bm{\pi}(E_{1}\times\mathbb{R}^{k})=\mu(E_{1})
and
\bm{\pi}(\mathbb{R}^{k}\times E_{2})=\widetilde{\mu}(E_{2})
for all Borel sets
E_{1},E_{2}
of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. We will use
W_{1}
to denote the Wasserstein-
1
distance in any spatial dimension
k
(though
k=1
,
k=d-1
, and
k=d
will be the only cases we use). The value of
k
will be clear from context.

Given
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
and a measurable function
f
, we denote by
f_{\sharp}\mu
the
pushforward
measure of
\mu
by
f
, defined via
f_{\sharp}\mu(E)=\mu(f^{-1}(E))
. This implies that
\int g(x)(f_{\sharp}\mu)(\mathrm{d}x)=\int g\circ f(x)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x)
for any measurable function
g
such that
g\circ f\in L^{1}(\mu)
.

For a right-continuous, nondecreasing function
f:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
, we define its
generalized inverse
via

(23)

f^{-1}(m)=\inf\{x\in\mathbb{R}:f(x)\geq m\}.

If
f:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
is the cumulative distribution function of a probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}
, i.e.,
f(x)=\mu((-\infty,x])
, then
\mu
is the pushforward of Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
under
f^{-1}
. If
\widetilde{\mu}
is another probability measure on
\mathbb{R}
with cumulative distribution function
\widetilde{f}
, then

(24)

W_{1}(\mu,\widetilde{\mu})=\|f-\widetilde{f}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}=\|f^{-1}-\widetilde{f}^{-1}\|_{L^{1}([0,1])}.

Furthermore, if
g
is bounded and measurable, then

(25)

\int_{(-\infty,x]}g(\xi)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}\xi)=\int_{0}^{f(x)}(g\circ f^{-1})(m)\,\mathrm{d}m,\qquad\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}.

\nu

\widetilde{\nu}

c(y,\widetilde{y})=|y-\widetilde{y}|

0\leq s\leq t<T

First, we have

(26)

\displaystyle\int\!\|M(\cdot,y,t)-\widetilde{M}(\cdot,\widetilde{y},t)\|_{L^{1}}\,\bm{\pi}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{y})

\displaystyle\leq\int\!\bigg[\|M(\cdot,y,s)-\widetilde{M}(\cdot,\widetilde{y},s)\|_{L^{1}}+(t-s)[A(\cdot,y)-\widetilde{A}(\cdot,\widetilde{y})]_{\mathrm{Lip}}\bigg]\,\bm{\pi}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{y})+(t-s)\,8R(T)\,\|\nabla_{2}\phi\|_{L^{\infty}}\,W_{1}(\nu,\widetilde{\nu}).

\mathcal{D}(\tau):=\int\!\|M(\cdot,y,\tau)-\widetilde{M}(\cdot,z,\tau)\|_{L^{1}}\,\bm{\pi}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}z),\qquad\tau\in[0,T).

\mathcal{A}_{\infty}:=\int\!\|A(\cdot,y)-\widetilde{A}(\cdot,\widetilde{y})\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1])}\,\bm{\pi}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{y}),

we also have the following, for some absolute constant

(29)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(t)

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\mathcal{D}(s)+C\sqrt{2R(T)\mathcal{A}_{\infty}(t-s)}+C(t-s)\,\big(\mathcal{A}_{\infty}+R(T)\|\nabla_{2}\phi\|_{L^{\infty}}W_{1}(\nu,\widetilde{\nu})\big)\bigg)e^{C\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}}(t-s)}.

Suppose

Letting
\Delta\to 0+
yields

\limsup_{\Delta\to 0^{+}}\mathcal{N}_{\Delta}\leq\frac{1}{2}(I+II),

where

(41)

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle:=\int\!\!\!\int\Big(B[\nu,M](x,y)+B[\widetilde{\nu},\widetilde{M}](x,\widetilde{y})\Big)\,\partial_{1}|M(x,y)-\widetilde{M}(x,\widetilde{y})|\,\mathrm{d}x\,\bm{\pi}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{y}),

\displaystyle:=\int\!\!\!\int\Big(\partial_{1}M(x,y)+\partial_{1}\widetilde{M}(x,\widetilde{y})\Big)\Big|B[\nu,M](x,y)-B[\widetilde{\nu},\widetilde{M}](x,\widetilde{y})\Big|\,\mathrm{d}x\,\bm{\pi}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{y}).

\displaystyle B[\nu,M](x,y)+B[\widetilde{\nu},\widetilde{M}](x,\widetilde{y})

\displaystyle=\int\!\!\!\int\Big[\Phi(x-\xi,y-\eta)\,\partial_{1}M(\xi,\eta)+\Phi(x-\xi,\widetilde{y}-\widetilde{\eta})\,\partial_{1}\widetilde{M}(\xi,\widetilde{\eta})\Big]\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\bm{\pi}(\mathrm{d}\eta,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\eta})

\displaystyle I^{a}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\!\!\!\int\!\!\!\int\!\!\!\int\bigg[\Big(\Phi(x-\xi,y-\eta)+\Phi(x-\xi,\widetilde{y}-\widetilde{\eta})\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\cdot\big(\partial_{1}M(\xi,\eta)+\partial_{1}\widetilde{M}(\xi,\widetilde{\eta})\big)\partial_{1}|M(x,y)-\widetilde{M}(x,\widetilde{y})|\bigg]\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\bm{\pi}(\mathrm{d}\eta,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\eta})\,\bm{\pi}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{y}).

will ultimately cancel with a corresponding term from

\displaystyle|I^{a}|

\displaystyle\leq\int\!\!\!\int\bigg[R(T)\|\nabla_{2}\phi\|_{L^{\infty}}\big|(y-\eta)-(\widetilde{y}-\widetilde{\eta})\big|

\displaystyle\cdot\bigg(\int\big(\partial_{1}M(\xi,\eta)+\partial_{1}\widetilde{M}(\xi,\widetilde{\eta})\big)\,\mathrm{d}\xi\bigg)\bigg(\int\big(\partial_{1}M(x,y)+\partial_{1}\widetilde{M}(x,\widetilde{y})\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\bigg)\bigg]\,\bm{\pi}(\mathrm{d}\eta,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\eta})\,\bm{\pi}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{y})

We next estimate

, we begin by writing

\displaystyle B[\nu,M](x,y)-B[\widetilde{\nu},\widetilde{M}](x,\widetilde{y})

\displaystyle=\int\!\!\!\int\Big[\Phi(x-\xi,y-\eta)\,\partial_{1}M(\xi,\eta)-\Phi(x-\xi,\widetilde{y}-\widetilde{\eta})\,\partial_{1}\widetilde{M}(\xi,\widetilde{\eta})\Big]\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\bm{\pi}(\mathrm{d}\eta,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\eta})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int\!\!\!\int\big(\Phi(x-\xi,y-\eta)+\Phi(x-\xi,\widetilde{y}-\widetilde{\eta})\big)\big(\partial_{1}M(\xi,\eta)-\partial_{1}\widetilde{M}(\xi,\widetilde{\eta})\big)\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\bm{\pi}(\mathrm{d}\eta,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\eta}),

where

Using this estimate in the second line below, we see that

\displaystyle II^{b}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\!\!\!\int\!\!\!\int\!\!\!\int\bigg[\big(\Phi(x-\xi,y-\eta)+\Phi(x-\xi,\widetilde{y}-\widetilde{\eta})\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\cdot\big(\partial_{1}M(x,y)+\partial_{1}\widetilde{M}(x,\widetilde{y})\big)\partial_{1}\big(M(\xi,\eta)-\widetilde{M}(\xi,\widetilde{\eta})\big)\bigg]\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\bm{\pi}(\mathrm{d}\eta,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\eta})\,\bm{\pi}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{y})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\int\!\!\!\int\!\!\!\int\!\!\!\int\bigg[\big(\Phi(x-\xi,y-\eta)+\Phi(x-\xi,\widetilde{y}-\widetilde{\eta})\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\cdot\big(\partial_{1}M(x,y)+\partial_{1}\widetilde{M}(x,\widetilde{y})\big)\partial_{1}\big|M(\xi,\eta)-\widetilde{M}(\xi,\widetilde{\eta})\big|\bigg]\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\bm{\pi}(\mathrm{d}\eta,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\eta})\,\bm{\pi}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{y}).

Putting together all the estimates above, we see that

We collect a few basic estimates in the following Lemma before turning to the main thrust of our proof. (These can also essentially be found in

Lemma 2.3

The following estimates hold:

(42)

(43)

\Bigg|\int\!\!\!\int|M(x,y,\tau)-\widetilde{M}(\widetilde{x},\widetilde{y},\tau)|\,b_{\Delta}^{\prime}(x-\widetilde{x})\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{x}\Bigg|\leq C.

\displaystyle|\mathcal{D}(\tau)-\mathcal{D}_{\Delta}(\tau)|

We turn to the proof of (

\displaystyle\Bigg|\int\!\!\!\int|M(x,y,\tau)-\widetilde{M}(\widetilde{x},\widetilde{y},\tau)|\,b_{\Delta}^{\prime}(x-\widetilde{x})\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{x}\Bigg|

∎

3.
Existence of entropy solutions

We now turn to the matter of existence. Our main result toward this end can be stated quite simply:

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume
\phi
satisfies (
3
) and (
4
). Let
(M^{0},A,\nu)
be an admissible triple and fix
T>0
. There exists a unique entropy solution
M\in C([0,T);L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1},\mathrm{d}x\otimes\nu))
of (
14
) on
[0,T)
associated to
(M^{0},A,\nu)
.

The rest of this section is dedicated to the proof of Theorem
3.1
. Our analysis, however, gives more than just existence—it furnishes an approximation scheme. We construct our entropy solution
M
as the limit (in a sense to be described below) of entropy solutions
M^{k}
that are discrete in
y
, in the sense that
M^{k}
is associated to an admissible triple
(M^{0,k},A^{k},\nu_{k})
such that
\nu_{k}
is atomic. These solutions
M^{k}
are constructed in turn as limits of entropy solutions
M^{k}_{N}
which are discrete in both
x
and
y
, and which are completely determined by the evolution of what we refer to as the
sticky particle Cucker–Smale
dynamics (SPCS). The SPCS dynamics require agents to evolve according to the Cucker–Smale ODEs on any time interval during which no collisions occur. Collisions are required to be completely inelastic: the agents involved stick together for all later times, with a post-collisional velocity determined by conservation of momentum.

In Section
3.1
below, we construct the solutions
M_{N}^{k}
from SPCS dynamics and obtain
M^{k}
as a limit as
N\to\infty
. (We drop the superscript
k
in this section to avoid a notational overload.) We do not need the full strength of the assumption on
\phi
for our analysis of the discrete-in-
y
case to go through; only (
21
) is needed at this point. Then, in Section
3.2
, we take the limit as
k\to\infty
to obtain
M
. Theorem
3.8
and the discussion preceding it provide a more precise description of the approximation scheme used to construct the solutions
M^{k}
, as well as the sense in which they approximate
M
. We detail rates of convergence in Section
3.3
under additional assumptions on
M^{0}
and
A
; see also Sections
4.2
and
4.3
for details at the level of the Euler Alignment system.

In summary, our solutions are constructed with two levels of discretization: one in the directions transverse to the flow, to reduce the dynamics to finitely many slices, and one in the direction of the flow. The ‘finite-slice’ analysis follows
[
46
]
rather closely (though we provide enough details to make the current work self-contained); the discretization transverse to the flow is new and is our main focus for this section. With a bit of care, we can approximate our general solutions directly by SPCS dynamics by taking both the number of slices and the number of agents per slice to infinity simultaneously; see sections
3.3
,
4.2
, and
4.3
below.

3.1.
Entropy solutions associated to atomic
\nu

We now construct the unique entropy solution associated to any admissible triple
(M^{0},A,\nu)
such that
\nu
is atomic. Our analysis closely follows that of
[
46
]
due to the second author and Tan; the approach of the latter was in turn inspired by work of Brenier and Grenier
[
8
]
on the pressureless Euler equations.

3.1.1.
Entropy conditions for piecewise constant solutions

We begin by recasting the Kruzkov entropy condition (
15
) in a form that will be easier to verify for the piecewise constant solutions we generate using SPCS dynamics. Equation (
54
) and inequality (
56
) below together constitute the simplified criteria and will be referred to as ‘Rankine–Hugoniot’ and ‘Oleinik’ conditions, respectively, on our piecewise constant solutions.

Suppose the measure
\nu
is atomic; that is, assume it takes the form
\nu=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\mu_{\ell}\delta(\cdot-y_{\ell})
. Suppose additionally that for each
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,L\}
, the function
M(x,y_{\ell},t)
(assumed to be nondecreasing in
x
, as usual) is piecewise constant, with discontinuity curves that are piecewise
C^{1}
. Such a function must take the form

(51)

M(x,y_{\ell},t)=\sum_{i=1}^{I_{\ell}}m_{i,\ell}H(x-x_{i,\ell}(t)),

where
H:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is the Heaviside function (
H(x)=1_{[0,\infty)}(x)
) and each
x_{i,\ell}:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuous, piecewise
C^{1}
function of
t
.

Write
\eta_{\alpha}(m)=|m-\alpha|
and
q_{\alpha}(m,y_{\ell})=\mathrm{sgn}(m-\alpha)(A(m,y_{\ell})-A(\alpha,y_{\ell}))
. One can show that the Kruzkov entropy condition (
15
) reduces to the requirement that for each
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,L\}
and each
i\in\{1,\ldots,I_{\ell}\}
, we have

(52)

\big(\dot{x}_{i,\ell}(t)+B[\nu,M](x_{i,\ell}(t),y_{\ell},t)\big)[[\eta_{\alpha}(M(\cdot,y_{\ell},t))]]\geq[[q_{\alpha}(M(\cdot,y_{\ell},t),y_{\ell})]],

for any time
t
at which
x_{i,\ell}
is differentiable, and any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. Here
[[f]]
denotes the jump in
f
across
x_{i,\ell}
, i.e.,
[[f]]=f(x_{i,\ell}(t)+)-f(x_{i,\ell}(t)-)
.

Denote
m_{\pm}=M(x_{i,\ell}(t)\pm,y_{\ell},t)
. If we take
\alpha\leq m_{-}
then (
52
) takes the form

(53)

\big(\dot{x}_{i,\ell}(t)+B[\nu,M](x_{i,\ell}(t),y_{\ell},t)\big)[[M(\cdot,y_{\ell},t))]]\geq[[A(M(\cdot,y_{\ell},t),y_{\ell})]],

whereas taking
\alpha\geq m_{+}
gives the opposite inequality. Combining both cases gives us

(54)

\dot{x}_{i,\ell}(t)+B[\nu,M](x_{i,\ell}(t),y_{\ell},t)=\frac{[[A(M(\cdot,y_{\ell},t),y_{\ell})]]}{[[M(\cdot,y_{\ell},t)]]}.

The final case, namely
m_{-}<\alpha<m_{+}
, yields

(55)

\big(\dot{x}_{i,\ell}(t)+B[\nu,M](x_{i,\ell}(t),y_{\ell},t)\big)\big([[M(\cdot,y_{\ell},t))]]-2(\alpha-m_{-})\big)\geq[[A(M(\cdot,y_{\ell},t),y_{\ell})]]-2(A(\alpha,y_{\ell})-A(m_{-},y_{\ell})).

Substituting (
54
) into (
55
) and rearranging gives

(56)

\frac{[[A(M(\cdot,y_{\ell},t),y_{\ell})]]}{[[M(\cdot,y_{\ell},t)]]}\leq\frac{A(\alpha,y_{\ell})-A(m_{-},y_{\ell})}{\alpha-m_{-}},\qquad m_{-}<\alpha<m_{+}.

We will refer to (
54
) as the ‘Rankine–Hugoniot’ condition and (
56
) as the ‘Oleinik entropy condition’ for these kinds of solutions. Together, (
54
) and (
56
) are equivalent to (
15
) for piecewise constant solutions of (
14
).

The piecewise constant solutions that we have in mind are the ones where the trajectories
x_{i,\ell}
satisfy the
sticky particle Cucker–Smale dynamics
, which we introduce presently (in the unidirectional case only).

3.1.2.
Sticky particle Cucker–Smale (SPCS) dynamics

In this section, we work with a collection of agents with positions
(x_{i,\ell}(t),y_{\ell})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
, horizontal velocities
\dot{x}_{i,\ell}(t)=v_{i,\ell}(t)\in\mathbb{R}
, and masses
\mu_{\ell}m_{i,\ell}
, where
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,L\}
indexes the ‘horizontal slice’ on which each agent lives and
i\in\{1,\ldots,I_{\ell}\}
indexes its position relative to other agents on the same slice. We assume that

x_{1,\ell}(t)\leq x_{2,\ell}(t)\leq\cdots\leq x_{I_{\ell},\ell}(t),\qquad t\geq 0,\,\ell\in\{1,\ldots,L\}.

We also make the normalizing assumption that
\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\mu_{\ell}=1
, and
\sum_{i=1}^{I_{\ell}}m_{i,\ell}=1
for each
\ell
. At the level of the scalar balance law, these choices correspond to working with the transverse measure
\nu=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\mu_{\ell}\delta(\cdot-y_{\ell})
and piecewise constant function
M(x,y_{\ell},t)=\sum_{i=1}^{I_{\ell}}m_{i,\ell}H(x-x_{i,\ell}(t))
in the previous subsection. Note also that, under this notation, we have

(57)

B[\nu,M](x_{i,\ell}(t),y_{\ell},t)=\sum_{p=1}^{L}\sum_{j=1}^{I_{p}}\mu_{p}m_{j,p}\Phi(x_{i,\ell}(t)-x_{j,p}(t),y_{\ell}-y_{p}).

We also make the convention that velocities are right-continuous:

v_{i,\ell}(t+)=v_{i,\ell}(t),\qquad\ell\in\{1,\ldots,L\},\,i\in\{1,\ldots,I_{\ell}\}.

For each
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,L\}
and each
i\in\{1,\ldots,I_{\ell}\}
, we denote

\mathcal{J}_{i,\ell}(t)=\{j:x_{j,\ell}(t)=x_{i,\ell}(t)\},\qquad i^{*}(t,\ell)=\max J_{i,\ell}(t),\qquad i_{*}(t,\ell)=\min J_{i,\ell}(t),

and we say that time
t
is a
collision time
if the cardinality of some
\mathcal{J}_{i,\ell}
increases at time
t
. Otherwise it is a
noncollisional time
. A time interval is
noncollisional
if it contains no collision times.

Finally, when we specify initial conditions
(x^{0}_{i,\ell},v^{0}_{i,\ell})
, we set
x_{i,\ell}(0)=x^{0}_{i,\ell}
and
v_{i,\ell}(0-)=v_{i,\ell}^{0}
. The quantity
v_{i,\ell}(0)
will be determined from the rules below.

With the above notation and conventions, we are ready to specify the characteristics of the sticky particle Cucker–Smale dynamics, which are determined from the following three rules.

(a)

(Stickiness of collisions) For any time
t\geq 0
(but in particular at collision times) we have

(58)

\mathcal{J}_{i,\ell}(s)\subseteq\mathcal{J}_{i,\ell}(t),\qquad\text{ whenever }0\leq s\leq t.

(b)

(Conservation of momentum across collisions) We have

(59)

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{i,\ell}(t)}m_{j,\ell}v_{j,\ell}(t)=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{i,\ell}(t)}m_{j,\ell}v_{j,\ell}(t-),\qquad t\geq 0.

(c)

(Cucker–Smale dynamics at noncollisional times) On any noncollisional time interval,
(x_{i,\ell},v_{i,\ell})
evolve according to the Cucker–Smale ODEs:

(60)

\left\{\begin{split}\dot{x}_{i,\ell}&=v_{i,\ell}\\
\dot{v}_{i,\ell}&=\sum_{p=1}^{L}\sum_{j=1}^{I_{p}}\mu_{p}m_{j,p}\phi(x_{i,\ell}-x_{j,p},y_{\ell}-y_{p})(v_{j,p}-v_{i,\ell})\end{split}\right.

Note that rule (a) guarantees that only finitely many collisions may occur, so the dynamics follow (
60
) except for at these finitely many collision times. We will also use the following relationship, which results from combining (a) and (b).

(61)

v_{i,\ell}(t)=\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{i,\ell}(t)}m_{j,\ell}v_{j,\ell}(t)}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{i,\ell}(t)}m_{j,\ell}}=\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{i,\ell}(t)}m_{j,\ell}v_{j,\ell}(t-)}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{i,\ell}(t)}m_{j,\ell}},\qquad t\geq 0.

3.1.3.
Basic properties of SPCS

Let us briefly review a few properties of the SPCS dynamics. We state these facts without proof; the details are all analogous to the arguments in
[
46
]
.

Proposition 3.2

(Maximum Principle)
.

With the notation above, if the unidirectional sticky particle Cucker–Smale system is equipped with the masses
\mu_{\ell}m_{i,\ell}
and initial conditions
(x_{i,\ell}^{0},y_{\ell},v_{i,\ell}^{0})
,
1\leq\ell\leq L
,
1\leq i\leq I_{\ell}
, then

(62)

\min_{j,p}v_{j,p}(s)\leq v_{i,\ell}(t)\leq\max_{j,p}v_{j,p}(s),\qquad 0\leq s\leq t,

and

(63)

|x_{i,\ell}(t)|\leq\max_{j,p}|x_{j,p}^{0}|+t\max_{j^{\prime},p^{\prime}}|v_{j^{\prime},p^{\prime}}^{0}|,\qquad t\geq 0.

The remaining properties concern the following quantity.

(64)

\psi_{i,\ell}(t)=v_{i,\ell}(t)+\sum_{p=1}^{L}\sum_{j=1}^{I_{p}}\mu_{p}m_{j,p}\Phi(x_{i,\ell}(t)-x_{j,p}(t),y_{\ell}-y_{p}),\qquad 1\leq i\leq I_{\ell},\,1\leq\ell\leq L,\,t\geq 0.

We also define

\psi_{i,\ell}^{0}=v_{i,\ell}^{0}+\sum_{p=1}^{L}\sum_{j=1}^{I_{p}}\mu_{p}m_{j,p}\Phi(x_{i,\ell}^{0}-x_{j,p}^{0},y_{\ell}-y_{p}).

Proposition 3.3

(Properties of
\psi
)
.

The quantities
\psi_{i,\ell}
have the following conservation property:

(65)

\psi_{i,\ell}(t)=\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{i,\ell}(t)}m_{j,\ell}\psi_{j,\ell}(t)}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{i,\ell}(t)}m_{j,\ell}}=\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{i,\ell}(t)}m_{j,\ell}\psi_{j,\ell}(s)}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{i,\ell}(t)}m_{j,\ell}},\qquad 0\leq s\leq t.

Furthermore, we have the following ordering property:

(66)

\psi_{i_{*}(s,\ell),\ell}(s-)\geq\psi_{i_{*}(s,\ell)+1,\ell}(s-)\geq\cdots\geq\psi_{i^{*}(s,\ell),\ell}(s-),\qquad s\geq 0.

Consequently, for
k\in\mathcal{J}_{i,\ell}(t)
, the functions

s\mapsto\frac{\sum_{j=i_{*}(t,\ell)}^{k}m_{j,\ell}\psi_{j,\ell}(s)}{\sum_{j=i_{*}(t,\ell)}^{k}m_{j,\ell}},\qquad s\mapsto\frac{\sum_{j=k}^{i^{*}(t,\ell)}m_{j,\ell}\psi_{j,\ell}(s)}{\sum_{j=k}^{i_{*}(t,\ell)}m_{j,\ell}},

are nonincreasing and nondecreasing, respectively, on the interval
s\in[0,t]
. Therefore, we have

(67)

\frac{\sum_{j=i_{*}(t,\ell)}^{k}m_{j,\ell}\psi_{j,\ell}(s)}{\sum_{j=i_{*}(t,\ell)}^{k}m_{j,\ell}}\geq\psi_{i,\ell}(t)\geq\frac{\sum_{j=k}^{i^{*}(t,\ell)}m_{j,\ell}\psi_{j,\ell}(s)}{\sum_{j=k}^{i_{*}(t,\ell)}m_{j,\ell}},\qquad k\in\mathcal{J}_{i,\ell}(t),\;0\leq s\leq t.

3.1.4.
Entropy solutions generated by SPCS dynamics

Equipped with the tools developed above, we can now complete our verification that unidirectional SPCS dynamics generate entropy solutions of (
14
) and make the relationship completely precise.

Theorem 3.4
.

Assume
\phi
satisfies (
3
) and (
21

(x^{0}_{i,\ell},y_{\ell})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}

\mu_{\ell}m_{i,\ell}

\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\mu_{\ell}=1

\sum_{i=1}^{I_{\ell}}m_{i,\ell}=1

\psi_{i,\ell}^{0}

(69)

(70)

and

Proof.

It suffices to check (
54
) and (

), with

m_{-}=M(x_{i,\ell}(t)-,y_{\ell},t)=\theta_{i_{*}(t,\ell)-1,\ell},\qquad m_{+}=M(x_{i,\ell}(t)+,y_{\ell},t)=\theta_{i^{*}(t,\ell),\ell}

\displaystyle[[A(M(\cdot,y_{\ell},t),y_{\ell})]]

\displaystyle=A(\theta_{i^{*}(t,\ell),\ell},y_{\ell})-A(\theta_{i_{*}(t,\ell)-1,\ell},y_{\ell})=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{i,\ell}(t)}m_{j,\ell}\psi^{0}_{j,\ell}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{i,\ell}(t)}m_{j,\ell}\bigg)\psi_{i,\ell}(t)

\displaystyle=[[M(\cdot,y_{\ell},t)]]\cdot\big(\dot{x}_{i,\ell}(t)+B[\nu,M](x_{i,\ell}(t),y_{\ell},t)\big).

In passing to the second line, we have used (

3.2.
The general case

We are ready to tackle the proof of existence of entropy solutions associated to an arbitrary admissible triple
(M^{0},A,\nu)
, which we fix for the remainder of this section. We proceed in the following three steps.

1.

We discretize the triple
(M^{0},A,\nu)
to obtain a sequence
(M^{0,k},A^{k},\nu_{k})

\nu_{k}
is atomic. More precisely,
\nu_{k}=(r_{k})_{\sharp}\nu

2.

Letting

denote the unique entropy solution on

associated to the triple
(M^{0,k},A^{k},\nu_{k})
,
we use (
29

is Cauchy in the Banach space

We take limits as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}|q_{\alpha}^{k}(\widehat{M}^{k},y)\widehat{\zeta}^{k}-q_{\alpha}(M,y)\zeta|\,\,\mathrm{d}x\,\nu(\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\;\;\leq\int\!\!\!\int\!\!\!\int_{\supp\widehat{\zeta}^{k}}\bigl[|q_{\alpha}^{k}(\widehat{M}^{k},y)-q_{\alpha}(\widehat{M}^{k},y)|+|q_{\alpha}(\widehat{M}^{k},y)-q_{\alpha}(M,y)|\bigl]\,\mathrm{d}x\,\nu(\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}t+C_{\zeta}2^{-k}\|A\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathrm{d}x\otimes\nu)}

Under additional assumptions on our initial conditions

The simpler of our two statements is Corollary

The more subtle (and arguably more interesting) of these two results is Theorem

5.
Flocking

We now consider the long-time behavior associated with unidirectional Cucker–Smale and Euler Alignment dynamics. We start with the former, which already contains the main novelty for our purposes.

5.1.
Flocking for unidirectional Cucker–Smale dynamics

It has been known for some time (c.f.
[
25
]
) that solutions of the Cucker–Smale ODEs experience flocking and exponentially fast velocity alignment for any initial configuration, if the communication protocol
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is even and ‘heavy-tailed’. When we say that
\phi
is ‘heavy-tailed’, we mean that there exists a radially nonincreasing function
\underline{\phi}:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\phi(\bm{x})\geq\underline{\phi}(|\bm{x}|)
and
\underline{\phi}
is nonintegrable at infinity:

(113)

\int_{1}^{\infty}\underline{\phi}=+\infty.

In fact, the authors of
[
25
]
already obtain a more quantitative version of the heavy-tail condition that depends on the initial configuration. Whether (
113
) or a more refined version is used, however, one can think of the underlying mechanism as follows: Initially, every agent interacts with every other agent. The heavy tail condition ensures that the spatial decay of these interactions is just slow enough that it is impossible for one agent to completely escape the influence of any other; it becomes trapped within some fixed diameter of the rest of the flock, so that the minimal strength of all pairwise interactions remains bounded below, which drives the exponentially fast velocity alignment.

Actually, even in Cucker and Smale’s paper
[
15
]
, it was shown that flocking can be guaranteed even if some agents never communicate with each other. What is important is the connectivity of the underlying graph structure of the communication network. In particular, flocking and exponentially fast velocity alignment is guaranteed as long as the time dependent ‘weighted Fiedler number’
\kappa_{2}
is nonintegrable:
\int_{0}^{\infty}\kappa_{2}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=+\infty
. For more on this direction (and a precise definition of the weighted Fiedler number), the reader may consult
[
15
,
51
,
62
,
56
]
, among many other references. Using the Fiedler number to establish flocking, however, typically results in bounds on the flock diameter (and the rate of alignment) that depend on the number of agents
N
; as such, these techniques often do not extend well in the hydrodynamic setting.

In the theorem below, we prove that the Cucker–Smale system experiences flocking (at a rate independent of the number of agents) even if the communication degenerates in a cylinder around the agents, provided that there is enough communication with agents outside each cylinder. The cylinders are invariant under the unidirectional dynamics, so the communication network is effectively frozen if one imposes an analog of the heavy-tail condition outside the cylinder of degeneracy. If we take the radius
r
of the cylinders to be zero, we recover a more standard type of flocking estimate, exactly in line with the quantitative version of Ha and Liu’s estimate.

.

Assume
\phi
