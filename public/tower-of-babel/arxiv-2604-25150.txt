The Role of Symmetry in Optimizing Overparameterized Networks
Kusha Sareen, Mohammad Pedramfar, Sékou-Oumar Kaba, Mehran Shakerinava, Siamak Ravanbakhsh

Abstract
Overparameterization is central to the success of deep learning, yet the mechanisms by which it improves optimization remain incompletely understood. We analyze weight-space symmetries in neural networks and show that overparameterization introduces additional symmetries that benefit optimization in two distinct ways. First, we prove that these symmetries act as a form of diagonal preconditioning on the Hessian, enabling the existence of better-conditioned minima within each equivalence class of functionally identical solutions. Second, we show that overparameterization increases the probability mass of global minima near typical initializations, making these favorable solutions more reachable. Experiments support our theoretical predictions: as width increases, the Hessian trace decreases, condition numbers improve, and convergence accelerates. Our analysis provides a unified framework for understanding overparameterization and width growth as a geometric transformation of the loss landscape.



1
Introduction

Overparameterized neural networks consistently outperform their smaller counterparts in optimization speed and final performance, even when the target function could be represented by much smaller architectures
(
Arora et al. 2018
;
Yang et al. 2022
)
. A major line of theory explains this via large-width limits and linearization: sufficiently overparameterized networks exhibit kernel-like dynamics and provable convergence of first-order methods
(
Jacot et al. 2018
;
Allen-Zhu et al. 2019
;
Du et al. 2019
;
Zou et al. 2019
;
Chizat et al. 2019
)
. Empirical studies of curvature complement this picture, showing that overparameterized networks have highly structured Hessian spectra with large near-zero subspaces and few data-dependent outliers
(
Sagun et al. 2017
;
Ghorbani et al. 2019
)
.

The loss landscape is also shaped by
symmetry
: neural networks possess large groups of function-preserving reparameterizations (permutations, rescalings, and more structured invariances) that induce equivalence classes of weights and manifolds of functionally identical minima
(
Simsek et al. 2021
;
Kunin et al. 2021
;
Grigsby et al. 2023
;
Brea et al. 2019
)
. These symmetries complicate naive geometric notions such as “sharpness”
(
Dinh et al. 2017
)
and help explain why seemingly distinct solutions can be connected by low-loss paths
(
Garipov et al. 2018
;
Draxler et al. 2018
)
.

This paper sits at the intersection of these two perspectives. We provide a more comprehensive characterization of the weight-space symmetries due to overparameterization, building on
Simsek et al. 2021
, and show they aid optimization through two mechanisms:

Mechanism 1: Diagonal Preconditioning.
The eigenvalues of the Hessian
H
after a symmetry transformation are the same as those for
K^{1/2}HK^{1/2}
, where
K
is a diagonal matrix that depends on the symmetry transformations. The right choice of
K
can improve the Hessian conditioning, especially if eigenvectors are approximately axis-aligned.

Mechanism 2: Volume Growth.
Overparameterization increases the total
volume
of global minima near typical initializations; specifically, the probability of initializing within
\epsilon
of a minimum grows with width once the network is sufficiently wide. Adding parameters introduces new symmetries that expand the equivalence class, making well-conditioned, reachable minima more abundant.

Contributions.

•

We characterize overparameterization symmetries for deep linear networks and general activations using a matrix groupoid, building on
Simsek et al. 2021
. For ReLU networks, we identify a new family of pre-activation splitting symmetries (
Section

3
).

•

We prove that these symmetries act as diagonal preconditioners on the Hessian, with explicit formulas for how gradients and eigenvalues transform (
Section

5
). Additionally, we establish that the product of non-zero Hessian eigenvalues (representing volume modulo symmetry directions) changes by a precise factor under splitting (
Section

6
).

•

We show that the trace (average curvature) can be reduced while preserving the product of eigenvalues (volume), leading to improved average-case conditioning (
Section

7
).

•

We show that the probability of initializing within
\epsilon
of a global minima increases with the degree of overparameterization when the network is sufficiently wide (
Section

8
)

•

We support our theoretical predictions with sets of experiments validating the theory and confirming its predictions for teacher-student networks and direct data fitting (
Section

9
).

Preliminaries

Parameter Space and Loss.

Fix a depth-
L
feedforward architecture with layer widths
n_{0}=d_{\mathrm{in}},n=n_{1},\dots,n_{L-1},n_{L}=d_{\mathrm{out}}
.
Let
\Theta
denote the corresponding parameter space (weights and biases). For
\theta\in\Theta
, write the realized function as

f_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}}
.
Given a ground-truth function
f^{*}
and an input distribution
\mathcal{D}
, we consider the population risk

\mathcal{L}(\theta)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\bigl[\ell\bigl(f_{\theta}(x),f^{*}(x)\bigr)\bigr]
.
Our focus is on
geometric
properties of
\mathcal{L}
in parameter space near minima: equivalence classes of functionally identical parameters, their induced flat directions, and the curvature in directions transverse to these classes.

Functional Equivalence and Symmetry Orbits.

We distinguish
functional equivalence
from
equivalence under a chosen symmetry family
.
Define
\theta\sim_{f}\theta^{\prime}
if
f_{\theta}=f_{\theta^{\prime}}
(equality as functions), and define
\theta\sim_{\mathcal{T}}\theta^{\prime}
if there exists
g\in\mathcal{T}
with
g(\theta)=\theta^{\prime}
, where

\mathcal{T}=\{g:\Theta\to\Theta\mid f_{\theta}(x)=f_{g(\theta)}(x)\ \forall x,\theta\}.

(1)

Write the corresponding equivalence classes as

[\theta]_{f}=\{\theta^{\prime}:\theta^{\prime}\sim_{f}\theta\},\qquad[\theta]_{\mathcal{T}}=\{\theta^{\prime}:\theta^{\prime}\sim_{\mathcal{T}}\theta\}.

For
\sim_{\mathcal{T}}
to be an equivalence relation, we need
\mathrm{Id}\in\mathcal{T}
and
g\in\mathcal{T}\iff g^{-1}\in\mathcal{T}
; transitivity follows from composition. When
\mathcal{T}
contains all function-preserving transformations,
[\theta]_{\mathcal{T}}=[\theta]_{f}
; otherwise
[\theta]_{\mathcal{T}}\subset[\theta]_{f}
. To ensure closure, we define
\mathcal{T}
through families of generators.

[\theta]_{\mathcal{T}}
is seen as the
symmetry orbit
through
\theta
. At a minimum, directions tangent to this orbit typically correspond to zero-curvature directions of the Hessian, while curvature relevant for optimization lives in directions orthogonal to the orbit
(
Kunin et al. 2021
;
Simsek et al. 2021
)
.

Teacher–Student Networks.

To cleanly separate optimization from representation and generalization, we use a teacher–student setting.
A
teacher
parameter vector
\theta^{*}\in\Theta_{n_{f}}
defines a target function
f^{*}=f_{\theta^{*}}
of minimal (functional) width
n_{f}
.
A
student
of width
n\geq n_{f}
is trained to match
f^{*}
.
In this setting, global minima exist by construction. The central question is how overparameterization reshapes the symmetry orbit structure and, through it, curvature and reachability properties that govern optimization.

Importantly, our theoretical results are not limited to this teacher-student setting. The universal approximation theorems imply that
any
function can be represented by some neural network of minimal width. Teacher-student networks make this realizability explicit.

3
Characterizing Overparameterization Symmetries

Figure 1:
A neural network before and after splitting operations.

We begin by noting the definition of overparameterization used in this work, following
Simsek et al. 2021
. We say a neural network is overparameterized if it has greater width than required to represent a given function. This is a natural notion representing having excess capacity when studying optimization to a given function rather than over a dataset. It is related but distinct from the notion broadly relating the number of parameters to data samples.

Prior work gives a description of the overparameterized solution set primarily for

single-hidden-layer
networks and analyzes how permutation symmetries produce manifolds of minima and symmetry-induced critical points
(
Simsek et al. 2021
)
.
Here, we extend these results with a useful matrix viewpoint and explain what changes with depth and choice of activation.

3.1
Fixed-width Symmetries in Nonlinear Networks

We first remark that a number of well-studied fixed-width parameter symmetries can be seen as matrices. For a network with activation
\sigma
to have a symmetry via matrix
M
, we require

M^{-1}\sigma(Mx)=\sigma(x)\,\forall x.

(2)

For example, permuting hidden units in a width-
n
layer is obtained by taking
M
to be a permutation matrix. Similarly, for ReLU networks, neuron-wise rescaling is obtained by taking
M=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
with
\lambda_{i}>0
. For permutations,
Equation

2
holds since
\sigma
is applied pointwise and for ReLU, the activation is equivariant to positive rescaling.

3.2
Overparameterization in Deep Linear Networks

Deep linear networks are parameterized as
f(x)=W_{L}\cdots W_{2}W_{1}x
. It is well documented that deep linear networks have a matrix factorization symmetry that impacts optimization
(
Saxe et al. 2014
;
Ghosh et al. 2025
)
. In particular, if we have fixed width
n
, between any pair
W_{k+1}W_{k}
we can insert an invertible matrix
M\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
as
M^{-1}M
. This is the same symmetry present between
W_{K}
and
W_{Q}
in self-attention.

To generalize this to overparameterization, we can think of
M
as an expansion and following contraction operation. Here, we have
M\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}
for
r>n
and a corresponding pseudoinverse
M^{+}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
such that
M^{+}M=I_{n}
.
Due to the use of pseudo-inverse, rather than inverse, we have additional degrees of freedom in the choice of
M^{+}
.
The transformation
W_{k}\to W_{k}M
and
W_{k+1}\to M^{+}W_{k+1}
preserves the function while changing the parameterization.

3.3
General Activations: Post-Activation Splitting

In the case of an arbitrary activation
\sigma
, the matrices
M
become much more restricted since one cannot arbitrarily mix hidden units while preserving
f_{\theta}(x)
for all
x
.
Since we make
no assumptions
about
\sigma
, the only function-preserving operations are to (1)
copy
the input and split the outgoing weights: duplicating a neuron with inputs
\vec{w}
and outputs
\vec{a}
into neurons
(\vec{w},\vec{w})
with outputs
(\vec{a}^{\prime},\vec{a}^{\prime\prime})
satisfying
a_{i}^{\prime}+a_{i}^{\prime\prime}=a_{i}
; or (2) add
0-type
neurons whose output weights sum to
\vec{0}
. The second transformation is discussed in Appendix
B
.

We characterize this through a class of
duplication matrices
. Here, we allow expansion by a matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}
for
r>n
.
M
is a duplication matrix and repeats rows
\vec{e}_{i}
of
I_{n}
with multiplicity
m_{i}
:

M=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{1}_{m_{1}}^{\top}\otimes\vec{e}_{1}\\
\mathbf{1}_{m_{2}}^{\top}\otimes\vec{e}_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{1}_{m_{n}}^{\top}\otimes\vec{e}_{n}\end{bmatrix},

(3)

where
\mathbf{1}_{m_{i}}
is an
m_{i}
-dimensional vector of ones and
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product. Thus, each basis vector
\vec{e}_{i}
of
I_{n}
is duplicated
m_{i}
times, yielding a total expansion
r=\sum_{i}m_{i}
.

The contraction is only a matrix operation in some special cases. Here,
M^{+}
can be
any
pseudoinverse such that
M^{+}M=I_{n}
.
The freedom in choosing
M^{+}
corresponds to redistributing outgoing weights across the expanded layer i.e., picking
(\vec{a}^{\prime},\vec{a}^{\prime\prime})
so
a_{i}^{\prime}+a_{i}^{\prime\prime}=a_{i}
for all
i
.
Our symmetry corresponds to transforming

W_{l}\to W_{l}M\text{ and }W_{l+1}\to M^{+}W_{l+1}.

Example: Duplication and Pseudoinverse

For
M=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
1&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}
(duplicating the first neuron), we can have several pseudoinverses
M^{+}
:

M^{+}=\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac{1}{2}&\tfrac{1}{2}&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix},\quad\begin{bmatrix}0.9&0.1&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix},\quad\text{etc.}

The freedom in
M^{+}
corresponds to how we split the output weights.

The formulation in
Simsek et al. 2021
requires scaling the entire outgoing weight vector together. However, there exists a strictly richer symmetry: the splitting coefficients need not be shared across the entire outgoing weight vector.

In particular, if
W_{l+1}
has columns
\vec{a}_{1},\vec{a}_{2},\ldots,\vec{a}_{n}
, and column
i
is duplicated
m_{i}
times, then for each
i
choose a coefficient matrix
C_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m_{i}}
with normalized rows:
\sum_{j}c_{ij}=1
.

The transformed output weights are

W_{l+1}\to\begin{bmatrix}C_{1}\odot\vec{a}_{1}&C_{2}\odot\vec{a}_{2}\dots C_{n}\odot\vec{a}_{n}\end{bmatrix},

where
\odot
denotes element-wise multiplication and broadcasting applies column-wise.

Example: Per-Weight Splitting Coefficients

For
M=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
1&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}
, we can transform:

W_{l+1}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}\\
a_{3}&a_{4}\end{bmatrix}\to\begin{bmatrix}\mu_{1}a_{1}&(1-\mu_{1})a_{1}&a_{2}\\
\mu_{2}a_{3}&(1-\mu_{2})a_{3}&a_{4}\end{bmatrix}

for constants
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
. Crucially,
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
can be chosen independently, giving per-weight control rather than per-neuron control.

An inverse transformation can be found in Appendix
C
.

3.4
ReLU and Piecewise-Linear Activations

Many practical activations are not so pathological. For instance, ReLU and its variants are linear in half the domain, which makes them equivariant to positive rescaling:
\sigma(\lambda x)=\lambda\sigma(x)
for
\lambda>0
. This enables an additional symmetry:
pre-activation splitting
.

To split the activations evenly, we divide the pre-activation weights by positive coefficients
[d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{m}]
such that
\sum_{i}d_{i}=1
. If the original pre-activation is positive, the ReLU remains linear, preserving the output. If negative, the activation stays negative after splitting.

This symmetry is stricter than post-activation splitting because it requires splitting the entire incoming weight vector with the same coefficient, rather than individually for each weight.

This can be written as right-multiplying by the matrix:

M^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}D_{m_{1}}^{\top}\otimes\vec{e}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
D_{m_{n}}^{\top}\otimes\vec{e}_{n}\end{bmatrix},

(4)

where
D_{m_{i}}=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{m_{i}})
with
d_{j}>0
and
\sum_{j}d_{j}=1
. The corresponding transformation on
W_{l+1}
involves copying the columns:

M^{\prime+}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{1}_{m_{1}}^{\top}\otimes\vec{e}_{1}&\cdots&\mathbf{1}_{m_{n}}^{\top}\otimes\vec{e}_{n}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}.

(5)

Example: Pre-Activation Splitting (ReLU)

Even split of input weights, duplicating outputs:

M^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac{1}{2}&0\\
\tfrac{1}{2}&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix},\quad M^{\prime+}=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix}

Input weights are halved; output weights are copied. This preserves the function because ReLU is equivariant to positive scaling.

In conjunction with post-activation splitting, this gives a family of operations generated by these matrices. We discuss which other activation functions allow pre-activation splitting in Appendix
F
.

4
The Overparameterization Groupoid

The symmetry transformations form a
groupoid
rather than a group. A groupoid is a set of composable transformations following the group axioms that need not be defined on all objects.
There are two reasons for using a groupoid: (1)
Partial composition
: A transformation
\tau_{a\to b}:\Theta_{a}\to\Theta_{b}
can only compose with transformations originating from width
b
. (2)
Restricted inverses
: We cannot contract beyond the minimal width needed to represent the function. If a network already represents the function at minimal width
n_{f}
, there is no inverse operation that reduces the width further.

We remark that even some weight-space symmetries at a fixed width can be viewed as “local” or groupoid symmetries;
Grigsby et al. 2023
provide a detailed analysis of such hidden symmetries in ReLU networks.

5
Optimization Properties

We now analyze how overparameterization symmetries affect optimization. Note that optimizing a network in the orbit
\theta\cdot\mathcal{O}_{n_{f}}^{L}
is not a practical setup; we will rarely initialize into this configuration. Instead, the goal is to show that there exist minima with favorable properties and understand the action of width growth on the loss landscape geometry.

5.1
Gradients Under Splitting

We consider how gradients transform under splitting operations.

Theorem 5.1

(Gradient Scaling)
.

Consider a splitting operation with coefficients
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{m}
summing to 1, where each coefficient multiplies the full vector of incoming or outgoing weights. Let
D
denote the set of duplicated weights. Then for the duplicated weight
w^{\prime}_{i}
associated with coefficient
\alpha_{i}
:

\frac{\partial L}{\partial w^{\prime}_{i}}=\alpha_{i}\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}.

(6)

The gradients of all other weights, including the split weights, are unchanged.

Corollary 5.2

(Even Split)
.

For an order-
m
even split (all
\alpha_{i}=1/m
), the gradient of each duplicated weight is
\frac{1}{m}\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}
.

This means, for a set of transformations
\{M_{l},M_{l}^{+}\}_{l=1...L}
applied at width
n
layers
\{W_{l}\}_{l=1...L}
, the gradient vector for all parameters
\theta=\text{vec}(\{W_{l}\}_{l=1...L})
transforms as
\nabla L_{\theta^{\prime}}=B^{T}\nabla L_{\theta}
for
B^{T}
a rectangular block diagonal matrix given by

B^{T}=\text{diag}(M_{1}\otimes I,I\otimes M_{1}^{+T},M_{2}\otimes I,I\otimes M_{2}^{+T}\dots).

This comes from vectorizing the expanded weights across layers with the identity
\mathrm{vec}(W_{\ell}M)=(M^{T}\otimes I)\,\mathrm{vec}(W_{\ell})
. We discuss the case of individual weight-wise splitting coefficients (rather than per-neuron) in Appendix
G
.

5.2
Hessians Under Splitting

Differentiating the gradient at a minima, we can see that any transformation
H\to H^{\prime}
can be written in terms of a semi-orthogonal matrix
H^{\prime}=B^{\top}HB
.
Let
S
be the set of weights that are split,
D
the set of weights that are duplicated, and
O
all other weights. We can write the Hessian before the operation as:

H=\begin{bmatrix}H_{OO}&H_{OS}&H_{OD}\\
H_{OS}^{\top}&H_{SS}&H_{SD}\\
H_{OD}^{\top}&H_{SD}^{\top}&H_{DD}\end{bmatrix},

(7)

where
H_{OO}\in\mathbb{R}^{|O|\times|O|}
, etc., and
|S|=|D|
.

After an order-
m
even split, the Hessian has blocks:

H^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}H_{OO}&H^{\prime}_{OS}&\tfrac{1}{m}H^{\prime}_{OD}\\
H_{OS}^{\prime\top}&H^{\prime}_{SS}&\tfrac{1}{m}H^{\prime}_{SD}\\
\tfrac{1}{m}H_{OD}^{\prime\top}&\tfrac{1}{m}H_{SD}^{\prime\top}&\tfrac{1}{m^{2}}H^{\prime}_{DD}\end{bmatrix},

(8)

where
H^{\prime}_{XX}=H_{XX}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{m\times m}
. For each weight that was duplicated, each row/column in its corresponding blocks is also duplicated.

Example: Hessian Transformation

For a 1-hidden-layer network with 1 hidden neuron, 1 input weight
w
and 1 output weight
a
:

H=\begin{bmatrix}h_{ww}&h_{wa}\\
h_{wa}&h_{aa}\end{bmatrix}

After duplicating
a
and splitting
w
evenly (
m=2
):

H^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}h_{ww}&h_{ww}&h_{wa}/2&h_{wa}/2\\
h_{ww}&h_{ww}&h_{wa}/2&h_{wa}/2\\
h_{wa}/2&h_{wa}/2&h_{aa}/4&h_{aa}/4\\
h_{wa}/2&h_{wa}/2&h_{aa}/4&h_{aa}/4\end{bmatrix}

Theorem 5.3

(Eigenvalue Interlacing)
.

The eigenvalues of
H^{\prime}
interlace with those of
H
: for sorted eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}(H)
and
\mu_{i}(H^{\prime})
,

\lambda_{i}\geq\mu_{i}\geq\lambda_{n-r+i},

(9)

where
r
is the number of added dimensions. Additionally,
r
zero eigenvalues are introduced (corresponding to level-sets in the loss).

Proof.

Since
H^{\prime}=B^{\top}HB
with
B
semi-orthogonal, the result follows by the Poincaré separation theorem.
∎

5.3
Overparameterization as Diagonal Preconditioning

Theorem 5.4
.

Overparameterization acts as diagonal preconditioning: the transformed Hessian
H^{\prime}
has the same nonzero eigenvalues as
K^{1/2}HK^{1/2}
, where
K
is a diagonal matrix determined by the splitting structure.

Let
H^{\prime}
be the Hessian obtained after applying an expansion from
a\rightarrow b
weights. Applying Sylvester’s Identity and letting
K=BB^{\top}
, we can write:

\displaystyle\det(H^{\prime}-\lambda I)

\displaystyle=\det(B^{\top}HB-\lambda I)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{b-a}\det(HK-\lambda I)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{b-a}\det(K^{1/2}HK^{1/2}-\lambda I)

(10)

by similarity. Note that for an even split we have
K=BB^{\top}=\diag([m,\ldots,m,1/m,\ldots,1/m,1,\ldots])
where the entries correspond to split weights (scaled by
m
), duplicated weights (scaled by
1/m
), and other weights (unchanged).

6
Local Volume and Pseudo-Determinant

We now turn to the local volume of minima under splitting. By Taylor expanding the loss near a minimum, the local ellipsoidal volume defined by
(\theta-\theta^{*})^{\top}H(\theta-\theta^{*})\leq\epsilon
is:

\frac{(2\epsilon)^{n/2}\Vol(B_{n})}{\sqrt{\det H}},

(11)

where
\Vol(B_{n})=\frac{\pi^{n/2}}{\Gamma(n/2+1)}
is the volume of the unit
n
-ball.

Since
H
is singular (due to symmetry directions), we generalize to volume
modulo symmetry directions
using the pseudo-determinant:

\pdet(H)=\prod_{i:\lambda_{i}>0}\lambda_{i},

(12)

which is the product of nonzero eigenvalues.

Theorem 6.1

(Pseudo-determinant Under Splitting)
.

Let
H^{\prime}
be obtained by acting on the weights corresponding to full-rank Hessian
H
with an
m
-way split using coefficients
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}
summing to 1. Then:

\displaystyle\pdet(H^{\prime})

\displaystyle=m\cdot\left(\alpha_{1}^{2}+\alpha_{2}^{2}+\cdots+\alpha_{m}^{2}\right)\cdot\pdet(H)

(13)

\displaystyle\geq\pdet(H),

(14)

with equality if and only if
\alpha_{i}=1/m
for all
i
(even splitting).

This gives the exact factor by which the pseudo-determinant (and hence the inverse volume modulo symmetry) changes. Larger
\pdet
means smaller volume around the minimum. The inequality follows from the RMS-AM inequality:
\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{2}\geq 1/m
when
\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}=1
.

Corollary 6.2

(Even Split Preserves Volume)
.

For even splitting (all
\alpha_{i}=1/m
), we have
\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{2}=1/m
, so
\pdet(H^{\prime})=\pdet(H)
. The volume modulo symmetry directions is preserved.

Proof sketch of
Theorem

6.1
.

We consider the characteristic polynomial’s lowest-order nonzero term, which equals
(-1)^{\operatorname{rank}(H)}\pdet(H)
. After splitting, the relevant principal minors scale by
m\cdot\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{2}
compared to the original. By the RMS-AM inequality,
\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{2}\geq 1/m
with equality iff all
\alpha_{i}=1/m
. See Appendix
A
for the full proof.
∎

These results suggest that the volume of a given minimum modulo symmetry directions remains fixed under even-splitting operations and decreases otherwise.

7.1
Trace and average-case conditioning

The worst-case condition number
\kappa(H)=\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}^{+}
is determined by only two eigenvalues and can be dominated by outliers. A more robust measure considers the full spectrum. The ratio of the arithmetic to geometric mean of the positive eigenvalues captures
average-case
conditioning and has been studied as an alternative to
\kappa
in the context of diagonal preconditioning and iterative methods
(
Doan and Wolkowicz 2011
;
Jung et al. 2024
;
Ghadimi et al. 2025
)
.

While the geometric mean of nonzero eigenvalues (related to pseudo-determinant) is preserved under even splits (
Theorem

6.1
), the
arithmetic mean
can change. The trace
\tr(H)
represents the sum of eigenvalues, and
\tr(H)/p
(where
p
is the number of positive eigenvalues) is the average curvature in non-symmetry directions.

Theorem 7.1

(Trace Formula)
.

Let
H^{\prime}
be obtained by duplicating a neuron
m
times. Let
D
be the duplicated weights and
S
the split weights. Then:

\displaystyle\tr(H^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\tr(H_{\backslash S,D})+m\sum_{s\in S}H_{ss}+\frac{1}{m}\sum_{d\in D}H_{dd},

(15)

where
H_{\backslash S,D}
is the matrix omitting all rows/columns corresponding to weights in
S\cup D
.

Proof.

This can be directly read off the Hessian block structure. See Appendix
A
.
∎

Notice that the trace can be decreased as long as:

\sum_{s\in S}H_{ss}<\frac{1}{m}\sum_{d\in D}H_{dd}.

This suggests that though the volume is unchanged (for even splits), the
average curvature
in non-symmetry directions can be reduced and redistributed across dimensions.

Optimal Splitting Degree.

Setting
\partial\tr(H^{\prime})/\partial m=0
,
the optimal splitting degree is

m^{*}=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{d\in D}H_{dd}}{\sum_{s\in S}H_{ss}}},

achieving minimum trace:

\min\tr(H^{\prime})=\tr(H_{\backslash S,D})+2\sqrt{\sum_{d\in D}H_{dd}\sum_{s\in S}H_{ss}}.

(16)

Average-Case Condition Number.

We formalize the above using the AM-GM inequality for
p
positive eigenvalues:

\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{i}\geq\left(\prod_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{i}\right)^{1/p},

(17)

with equality iff all
\lambda_{i}
are equal. The geometric mean
(\pdet H)^{1/p}
is fixed under even splits. If we reduce the arithmetic mean
\tr(H)/p
, the eigenvalues become more uniform. The
average-case conditioning ratio
is:

\omega(H)=\frac{\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{i}}{\left(\prod_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{i}\right)^{1/p}}=\frac{\tr(H)/p}{(\pdet H)^{1/p}}.

(18)

This ratio equals 1 when all eigenvalues are equal (perfect conditioning) and increases with eigenvalue spread. Since the denominator is fixed under even splits while the numerator (trace) can decrease, overparameterization can improve average-case conditioning. We additionally note
Paquette et al. 2022
show the average-case maximum learning rate goes as
\frac{2}{\tr(H)}
, suggesting overparameterization can allow for convergence with larger learning rates.

Beyond conditioning, overparameterization affects the
reachability
of good minima. By
Theorem

6.1
, each
m
-way split changes the local volume of a minimum by a factor of
1/\sqrt{m\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{2}}
. Although individual minima shrink on average, the total probability mass near global minima grows due to combinatorial replication.

8
Probability of Initializing Near Minima

For a network of width
n
expanded to width
n+1
under Xavier initialization (
b_{m}=1/\sqrt{m}
), the probability of initializing within
\epsilon
of a global minimum satisfies:

\frac{P_{n+1}}{P_{n}}\;\geq\;\frac{\ln(1+\sqrt{2})}{8}\cdot n(n+1)^{2}\left(\frac{n+1}{n}\right)^{\!n}(\epsilon/C)^{1/4},

(19)

where
C>0
is a constant bounding the fourth-order coefficient of the loss along the locally flat direction introduced by splitting (see Appendix
J
). The three multiplicative factors capture distinct effects: the initialization volume ratio
((n+1)/n)^{n}(n+1)^{2}/4
accounts for the change in prior density with width; the combinatorial factor
n(n+1)
counts permutation copies of the new minimum; and
(\epsilon/C)^{1/4}
is the width of the
\epsilon
-sublevel set along the quartic flat direction. The inequality holds because we count only minima arising from single-neuron splitting and restrict to base minima whose weights lie within the expanded initialization cube.

The right-hand side exceeds
1
when
n
is sufficiently large, specifically when
n(n+1)^{2}>4C^{1/4}/(\ln(1+\sqrt{2})\,\epsilon^{1/4})
. This confirms that overparameterization makes global minima more reachable from random initialization once the network is wide enough.

10
Discussion

10.1
Implications for Optimization

Our analysis is an existence result: we characterize which minima exist and their geometric properties, rather than proving which minima gradient descent converges to. This distinction is deliberate.
Theorem

7.1
and
Theorem

6.1
establish that overparameterization guarantees the existence of minima with improved conditioning and sufficient volume, while
Section

8
shows these minima occupy increasing probability mass near initialization. Poorly-conditioned minima with small volume may also exist, but they are both harder to reach and harder to converge to, so optimization algorithms tend to naturally bypass them. Stochastic gradient noise provides additional selection pressure, destabilizing convergence in sharp, poorly-conditioned basins.

This is corroborated by
Figure

16
, where the negligible gap between the mean and best student conditioning suggests that the mere existence of well-conditioned minima is sufficient to improve practical convergence. In practical optimization, early stopping within a well-conditioned basin suffices, and overparameterization makes such basins more abundant. A possible connection to plasticity loss in continual learning is discussed in Appendix
I
.

10.2
Implications for Generalization

Our framework may also shed light on generalization. Consider noisy training data
\{x_{i},y_{i}\}
generated by some
simple
ground truth function
f
with noise:
y_{i}=f(x_{i})+\zeta
. The width required to overfit on
\{x_{i},y_{i}\}
is typically much larger than the width needed to represent
f
. As a result, the global minima corresponding to
f
have larger symmetry orbits and greater volume than those corresponding to overfitting solutions. This provides a counting argument for why overparameterized networks generalize: they converge to simpler functions not because they have lower loss, but because their minima are more numerous and easier to optimize.

Figure
8
Bottom is a provides a first look at direct data fitting and the underparameterized regime, where we can see the predicted trends continue to hold. In this setting, though we are not overparameterized, there exist sub-networks corresponding to regions of the weight space representing simpler functions, as shown in Figure
2
. We believe this to be a very fruitful area for future research.

10.3
Wideness of Minima and Anthropic Principle in Optimization

There is an old idea in machine learning that generalizing minima are wide
(
Hochreiter and Schmidhuber 1997
)
. However, considering parameter space symmetries it is clear this picture is incomplete: using symmetry transformations we can construct narrow minima that generalize
Dinh et al. 2017
. A more plausible explanation supported by our findings is that the implication is in the other direction: wide minima represent simpler functions and simpler functions generalize. This suggests the wonderful coincidence of deep learning is these generalizing minima also happen to be favored by our optimization process.

11
Conclusion

We show that overparameterization benefits optimization through two distinct mechanisms: diagonal preconditioning of the Hessian and increased volume of minima near initialization. Our theoretical results are data and optimizer agnostic, depending only on the symmetry structure of the architecture which prescribes the geometry of the loss landscape.

Appendix B
Zero-type Neurons

The main text describes two function-preserving operations for arbitrary activations. The first (neuron duplication) is characterized in
Section

3.3
using duplication matrices
M
and pseudoinverses
M^{+}
. Here we give an analogous characterization of the second: adding
0-type neurons
, whose output weights sum to
\vec{0}
.

Setup.

Consider a single hidden layer with
n
neurons, input weight matrix
W_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}
and output weight matrix
W_{l+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times n}
. We want to expand to
n+k
neurons by adding
k
new neurons whose outputs cancel, preserving
f_{\theta}(x)
for all
x
.

Expansion matrix.

Define
M_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+k)\times n}
as

M_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}I_{n}\\
A\end{bmatrix},

(24)

where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}
is arbitrary. This matrix appends
k
new rows to
W_{l}
: the original
n
neurons are preserved, and the new neurons receive input weights given by the rows of
AW_{l}
. Since
\sigma
is arbitrary, the activations of the new neurons can take any value, so
A
is unconstrained.

Contraction matrix.

Define
M_{0}^{+}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(n+k)}
as

M_{0}^{+}=\begin{bmatrix}I_{n}&Z\end{bmatrix},

(25)

where
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
satisfies

\mathbf{1}_{d_{\mathrm{out}}}^{T}W_{l+1}Z=\vec{0}^{T}.

(26)

This constraint ensures that the columns of
W_{l+1}Z
(the output weights of the new neurons) sum to zero across the output dimension, so the new neurons contribute nothing to the network output. In the simplest case where
d_{\mathrm{out}}=1
, this reduces to
W_{l+1}Z=\vec{0}^{T}
, i.e., the output weights of the new neurons are zero.

More generally, for per-output-neuron control, we only need each column of
W_{l+1}\begin{bmatrix}I_{n}&Z\end{bmatrix}
restricted to the new neurons to sum to zero. That is, for each output neuron
j
, the weights connecting the
k
new hidden neurons to output
j
must sum to zero:
\sum_{i=1}^{k}(W_{l+1})_{j,n+i}=0
.

Verification.

The transformation is
W_{l}\to M_{0}W_{l}
and
W_{l+1}\to W_{l+1}M_{0}^{+\top}
. We verify:

M_{0}^{+}M_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}I_{n}&Z\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I_{n}\\
A\end{bmatrix}=I_{n}+ZA.

(27)

For this to be the identity (so the original neurons are unchanged), we need
ZA=0
. This is satisfied when
Z=0
(the simplest case, where the new neurons have zero output weights) or more generally when
Z
lies in the left null space of
A
.

Alternatively, we can relax the requirement
M_{0}^{+}M_{0}=I_{n}
and instead only require that the function is preserved. The transformed output is:

W_{l+1}M_{0}^{+\top}\sigma(M_{0}W_{l}x)=W_{l+1}\begin{bmatrix}I_{n}\\
Z^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\sigma\!\left(\begin{bmatrix}W_{l}\\
AW_{l}\end{bmatrix}x\right)=W_{l+1}\,\sigma(W_{l}x)+W_{l+1}Z\,\sigma(AW_{l}x).

(28)

The second term vanishes whenever
W_{l+1}Z=0
(column-wise), which is the zero output weight condition. In this formulation,
A
is fully free and
Z
must lie in the right null space of
W_{l+1}
.

Relation to duplication.

The 0-type expansion is complementary to neuron duplication. Duplication copies existing neurons and redistributes their output weights (the new neurons are functionally redundant copies). The 0-type expansion adds genuinely new neurons with fresh input weights, but constrains their output weights to cancel. Together, these two operations generate the full set of overparameterization symmetries for arbitrary activations described in
Section

3.3
.

Example: Adding a 0-type Neuron

For a network with
n=2
hidden neurons and
d_{\mathrm{out}}=2
, adding one 0-type neuron:

M_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\\
a_{1}&a_{2}\end{bmatrix},\qquad M_{0}^{+}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\end{bmatrix}.

The new neuron has arbitrary input weights
(a_{1},a_{2})
and zero output weights (since
Z=0
here). The input weights
a_{1},a_{2}
are free parameters that do not affect the function but expand the parameter space.

Appendix C
Inverse Transformation

Given an expanded network with duplication matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}
and contraction
M^{+}
, the inverse transformation recovers the original width-
n
parameterization. For post-activation splitting, where neuron
i
was duplicated
m_{i}
times, the inverse groups each set of
m_{i}
copies back into a single neuron: the input weights are averaged across copies (recovering the original, since all copies are identical by construction), and the output weights are summed.

Concretely, the inverse uses the even-split pseudoinverse
M^{+}_{\text{even}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
for the input side and
M^{\top}
for the output side:

W_{l}\to M^{+}_{\text{even}}\,W_{l},\quad W_{l+1}\to W_{l+1}\,M^{T},

where
(M^{+}_{\text{even}})_{i,j}=1/m_{i}
if column
j
is a copy of neuron
i
and
0
otherwise. The input averaging recovers the shared weights since
M^{+}_{\text{even}}M=I_{n}
. Right-multiplying by
M^{T}
sums the split output weights: if neuron
i
was split with coefficients
(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m_{i}})
satisfying
\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}=1
, then
\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}a_{i}=a_{i}
.

For pre-activation splitting (ReLU), the roles reverse: output weights are averaged and input weights are summed to undo the splitting coefficients
D_{m_{i}}
.

Note that the inverse is only well-defined on the image of the forward transformation i.e., on networks whose weights exhibit the tied structure produced by expansion. This is why the symmetries form a groupoid rather than a group (
Section

4
).

Example: Inverse of Post-Activation Splitting

For
M=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
1&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}
(duplicating neuron 1), suppose the expanded network has:

W_{l}^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}\vec{w}_{1}\\
\vec{w}_{1}\\
\vec{w}_{2}\end{bmatrix},\quad W_{l+1}^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}\mu_{1}a_{1}&(1-\mu_{1})a_{1}&a_{2}\\
\mu_{2}a_{3}&(1-\mu_{2})a_{3}&a_{4}\end{bmatrix}.

The inverse uses
M^{+}_{\text{even}}=\begin{bmatrix}1/2&1/2&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix}
and
M^{T}=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix}^{T}
:

M^{+}_{\text{even}}\,W_{l}^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}\vec{w}_{1}\\
\vec{w}_{2}\end{bmatrix},\quad W_{l+1}^{\prime}\,M^{T}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}\\
a_{3}&a_{4}\end{bmatrix},

recovering the original parameterization.

Appendix D
The Overparameterization Groupoid

Let
\Gamma^{(L,N)}
be the set of functions exactly representable by a neural network with
L

N
neurons per layer. Notice that
\Gamma^{(L,N)}\subset\Gamma^{(L+p,N+q)}
for
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
\Theta^{L}_{N}
be the set of weights with
L
hidden layers and
N
neurons per layer. We will use
\Theta_{N}=\Theta^{1}_{N}
. Define
\Theta^{L}_{\geq a}=\bigcup_{N=a}^{\infty}\Theta^{L}_{N}
.

We begin with a remark about why we have to define a groupoid rather than a group. Suppose we have a transformation mapping
\tau_{a\rightarrow b}:\Theta_{a}\rightarrow\Theta_{b}
. Then, we can only compose this with transformations
\tau_{b\rightarrow*}
. This means the binary group operation is only partially defined, though we still have associativity, inverse and identity, making it a groupoid rather than a group. Another reason is due to the fact that we can only invert the expansion if some weights are tied ie. a result of a previous expansion.

Let
\mathcal{O}_{n_{f}}^{L}:\Theta^{L}_{\geq n_{f}}\rightarrow\Theta^{L}_{\geq n_{f}}
be the groupoid of operations corresponding to overparameterization with general activations (ie. post-activation splitting) on networks
\theta
with
L
hidden layers and minimum width
n_{f}
such that the function

. The key property of
\mathcal{O}_{n_{f}}^{L}
is that the function represented by
\theta
is preserved over the orbit:
f_{\theta}=f_{\theta\mathcal{O}_{n_{f}}^{L}}
.

Suppose
b\geq a
. We can write an action for a single layer as the set of tuples

T_{a\rightarrow b}=\{(M_{a\rightarrow b},C_{1},C_{2}\dots C_{a});M_{a\rightarrow b}\text{ and }C_{1}\dots C_{a}\text{ valid expansions from a to b}\},

where
M_{a\rightarrow b}
and
C_{i}
are expansion and contraction matrices from
Section

to be applied at any layer ie.
t\in T_{a\rightarrow b}
maps
\Theta_{a}\rightarrow\Theta_{b}
.

We also need to define a set of inverse operations
T_{b\rightarrow a}

\im(T_{a\rightarrow b})
ie.
t\in T_{b\rightarrow a}:\im(T_{a\rightarrow b})\subset\Theta_{b}\rightarrow\Theta_{a}
. In particular, this is the set of operations that takes any
b-a

\vec{w}
and collapses them to a single
\vec{w}
and collapses the corresponding
\vec{a}_{i}
to
\vec{a}=\sum_{i}\vec{a}_{i}
. These
b-a

The transformations for the entire

are the set of tuples for each of the
L

T^{L}_{a\rightarrow b}=T_{a\rightarrow b}\times T_{a\rightarrow b}\dots=(T_{a\rightarrow b})^{L}

For convenience, we will abuse notation slightly with

and
T_{\geq a\rightarrow\geq b}=\bigcup_{a^{\prime}=a}^{\infty}T_{a^{\prime}\rightarrow\geq b}

Finally, the overparameterization groupoid is defined on the set

\mathcal{O}_{n_{f}}^{L}=\{t\in T^{L}_{\geq n_{f}\rightarrow\geq n_{f}}\},

along with a partial binary operation requiring equal input/output widths

and the fact that

contains the identity

for each width

and inverse transformations and

Appendix E
Optimization Intuition via Rescale Symmetry

Before analyzing the overparameterization symmetries from the main text, we build intuition with the simpler rescale symmetry, which is present even in exactly-parameterized networks. The core argument carries over to the overparameterization case. It is well documented that rescale symmetries impact the curvature of minima and can be exploited to improve optimization
[
Huang et al. 2017
,
Salimans and Kingma 2016
,
Neyshabur et al. 2015
,
Mishkin et al. 2025
]
.

Consider a two-layer ReLU network
f(x;W,U)=U\cdot\text{ReLU}(Wx)
. Since ReLU is equivariant to positive rescaling, the transformation
g_{\lambda}(W,U)=(\lambda W,\lambda^{-1}U)
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
preserves the function. More generally, each hidden neuron can be rescaled independently.

Gradients under rescaling.

At a rescaled point
(\lambda W,\lambda^{-1}U)
, the gradients transform as:

\frac{\partial L}{\partial W^{\prime}}=\lambda^{-1}\frac{\partial L}{\partial W},\qquad\frac{\partial L}{\partial U^{\prime}}=\lambda\frac{\partial L}{\partial U}.

(29)

This follows from the chain rule:
W^{\prime}=\lambda W
so
\partial L/\partial W^{\prime}_{ij}=(\partial W_{ij}/\partial W^{\prime}_{ij})\cdot\partial L/\partial W_{ij}=\lambda^{-1}\partial L/\partial W_{ij}
, and similarly for
U
. Rescaling thus redistributes gradient magnitudes between the input and output weights. When
\lambda>1
, input weight gradients shrink while output weight gradients grow, and vice versa. This is analogous to the gradient scaling under splitting (
Theorem

5.1
), where the splitting coefficients play the role of
\lambda
.

Hessian under rescaling.

If
(W^{*},U^{*})
is a global minimum, rescaling gives a class of equivalent minima
[(W^{*},U^{*})]_{\mathcal{T}}
. The minimum
(W^{*},U^{*})
can be arbitrarily poorly conditioned, but the symmetry orbit contains solutions with different conditioning. The Hessian at
(W^{*},U^{*})
has the block structure:

H=\begin{bmatrix}H_{WW}&H_{WU}\\
H_{WU}^{\top}&H_{UU}\end{bmatrix}.

(30)

At the rescaled point
(\lambda W^{*},\lambda^{-1}U^{*})
, the Hessian becomes:

H(\lambda)=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda^{-2}H_{WW}&H_{WU}\\
H_{WU}^{\top}&\lambda^{2}H_{UU}\end{bmatrix}.

(31)

This is diagonal preconditioning with
K=\diag(\lambda^{-2}I,\lambda^{2}I)
, matching the structure from
Section

5.3
. Choosing
\lambda
lets us trade off curvature between the
W
and
U
blocks.

Limitations.

While
\min_{\lambda}\kappa(H(\lambda))\leq\kappa(H)
always holds, rescaling cannot improve conditioning universally. To see this, consider the Rayleigh quotient for
x=(u,v)
:

R(H(\lambda),x)=\frac{\lambda^{-2}u^{\top}H_{WW}u+2u^{\top}H_{WU}v+\lambda^{2}v^{\top}H_{UU}v}{\|u\|^{2}+\|v\|^{2}}.

(32)

The off-diagonal term
u^{\top}H_{WU}v
is unaffected by
\lambda
, so if the extreme eigenvalues are dominated by the cross-block coupling, rescaling has little effect. Similarly, if both extreme eigenvalues come from a single block (say
H_{WW}
), rescaling scales them together and cannot change their ratio. Conditioning improves when the largest and smallest eigenvalues are split across the
H_{WW}
and
H_{UU}
blocks, which corresponds to the eigenvectors being approximately axis-aligned in the
(W,U)
decomposition. This is depicted in
Figure

4
.

Appendix F
Which Activations Allow Pre-Activation Splitting?

Understanding general pre-activation splitting involves solving the functional equation representing a single split:

\exists\alpha,\beta\;\forall x:\sigma(\alpha x)+\sigma(\beta x)=\sigma(x).

(33)

A first observation is that this implies
\sigma(0)=0
, which rules out the sigmoid function.

For simplicity, first consider the case where
\alpha,\beta,x>0
. We can transform this into a difference equation by letting
x=e^{t}
and
y(t)=\sigma(e^{t})
. Then, defining
\alpha=e^{a}
and
\beta=e^{b}
, we get:

y(t)=y(t+a)+y(t+b).

(34)

Taking the Laplace transform (under mild assumptions on
y
), we get
(e^{as}+e^{bs}-1)Y(s)=0
. This implies all nontrivial solutions lie on the set of roots
S_{ab}=\{s\in\mathbb{C}:e^{as}+e^{bs}=1\}
.

This implies all nontrivial solutions lie on the set of roots
S_{ab}=\{s\in\mathbb{C}|e^{as}+e^{bs}=1\}
. If
a/b
is rational in the form
p/q
with
\gcd(p,q)=1
then there are
\max(p,q)
roots by the fundamental theorem of algebra. Otherwise, there can be infinite roots. From here, we’ll only consider the case with finite roots ie. solutions that belong to the exponential family. Taking the inverse Laplace transform of
\delta(s^{\prime}-s)
for
s\in S_{ab}
gives solutions of the form
Ce^{st}=Cx^{s}
, with the matching constraint
\alpha^{s}+\beta^{s}=1
. For example, taking
s
to be purely real, we recover the polynomial solutions.

Let
z=e^{s}
. For imaginary
z
and
r=|z|
, we are interested in real solutions that must take the form
z^{t}+\bar{z}^{t}=r^{t}\cos(wt).
For simplicity, consider the case where
r=1
. Then, we need
\cos(w(t+a))+\cos(w(t+b))=\cos(wt)
. Applying the angle addition formulas and doing some algebra, we get
(aw,bw)=(\pm\frac{\pi}{3}+2\pi m,\mp\frac{\pi}{3}+2\pi n)
for
m,n\in\mathbb{Z}
. This solution corresponds to
\sigma(x)=\cos(w\ln x).
Notice that the limit
\lim_{x\rightarrow 0^{+}}\cos(w\ln x)
does not converge, making this an impractical solution.

A careful treatment of signs recovers ReLU and other piecewise-polynomial activations as practical solutions. Interestingly, this includes ReLU
2
, which has recently shown to be a performant activation in sparse LLMs
Zhang et al. 2024
.

Axis-Algined Eigenvectors and Transformer Rotation Symmetries

We empirically check the axis alignment of the Hessian eigenvectors of pretrained
teacher networks across architectures.

In
9
, we compute the top-1000 Hessian eigenpairs via
Lanczos at the trained parameters, and for each eigenvector

measure the
participation ratio
\mathrm{PR}(v)=1/\sum_{i}v_{i}^{4}
on the parameter basis. The
fourth power simply makes
\mathrm{PR}
an interpretable count: if

has equal mass
on

\sum_{i}v_{i}^{4}=1/k
, so

\mathrm{PR}(v)=k
. A lower power would not give this property:
\sum_{i}v_{i}^{2}=1

for any unit
v
, so it cannot distinguish a one-hot eigenvector from a fully spread
one. We report

, the fraction of parameters the
eigenvector effectively occupies; a uniformly random unit vector in

\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{params}}}
has expected fraction
\approx 1/3
.

Across the three architectures we tested (a CNN trained on CIFAR-10, an
absolute-position character Transformer pretrained on Alice in Wonderland, and the same
Transformer with RoPE positional encoding) the Hessian eigenvectors lie below this
random baseline (Figure
9
), indicating a degree of axis
alignment relative to random directions. The localization is strongest at the largest
eigenvalues and decays towards the random baseline as one moves down the spectrum.

To see where this localization sits in parameter space, we decompose each top

\sum_{i\in T}v_{i}^{2}
for each parameter tensor
T
, and divide by the

that a uniform random direction
would assign to

. The resulting quantity (one per (eigenvector, tensor) pair) takes

shows this decomposition for the top-20 eigenvectors of the RoPE Transformer teacher.

Transformers have an additional rotation symmetry in their MLP weights, partially described in
Zhang et al. 2025a
for networks without skip connections. In modern pre-norm Transformers with skip connections, the symmetry is more restricted. In general, the symmetry performs a rotation in the MLP weight matrices
W\to R_{\text{left}}WR_{\text{right}}^{\top}
for some special orthogonal matrices

, acting jointly across adjacent blocks to preserve the residual stream.

To see how this rotates weight space, we apply the Kronecker product identity:

\text{vec}(R_{\text{left}}WR_{\text{right}}^{\top})=(R_{\text{left}}\otimes R_{\text{right}})\text{vec}(W).

(38)

Intuitively, this rotates in unison the column weights by
R_{\text{left}}
and the row weights by
R_{\text{right}}
.

This rotation symmetry can aid optimization algorithms with built-in diagonal preconditioners, such as Adam. Recent work has noted the underperformance of Adam when rotating the preconditioner in Transformers but not ResNets (which lack this rotation symmetry), suggesting the minima Adam converges to typically have axis-aligned eigenvectors
[
Zhang et al. 2025b
]
.

Appendix I
Notes on Plasticity Loss

A direct application of our framework concerns plasticity loss in continual learning. If optimization converges to a simple generalizing function, the solution lies on a low functional width column of the overparameterization groupoid (
Figure

2
). Such solutions are typically realized through dormant capacity such as duplicated or dead neurons, configurations that are fixed points of subgroups of the network’s symmetry group. It is a well-known result that gradient descent preserves these fixed-point subspaces under any loss: cloned neurons stay cloned, dead neurons stay dead, throughout training. Once the objective changes, the optimizer cannot recruit this dormant capacity to fit the new task.

Ziyin 2023
previously analyzed this trapping mechanism for permutation, rescaling, and rotation symmetries under weight decay or noise, and explicitly linked it to plasticity loss. A more detailed and concurrent dynamical systems treatment appears in
Joudaki et al. 2025
.

Appendix K
Related Work

Geometry of overparameterization.

Sussmann 1992
showed early on that for minimal-width networks, weights are determined up to permutation and sign-flip symmetries, so these discrete symmetries are the only source of non-uniqueness when the network is not overparameterized.
Simsek et al. 2021
extended this to the overparameterized setting, identifying the continuous symmetries (neuron splitting and merging) that arise when width exceeds the functional minimum; their work provides the foundation for our analysis.
Kunin et al. 2021
take a complementary view through conservation laws: each continuous symmetry of the loss induces a conserved quantity along gradient flow, analogous to Noether’s theorem, which constrains training dynamics beyond the static geometry of minima.
Grigsby et al. 2023
catalog additional “hidden” symmetries in ReLU networks that arise from the piecewise-linear activation structure, including symmetries that exist only at specific weight configurations (e.g., when a neuron’s pre-activation is zero on all training data).

Loss landscape structure.

Cooper 2021
proves that for networks with
d
parameters and
n
data points (
d>n
), the global minima generically form a
(d-n)
-dimensional submanifold rather than isolated points.
Nguyen 2019
shows that all sublevel sets of the loss are connected under mild overparameterization, ruling out “bad local valleys.” Empirically,
Garipov et al. 2018
and
Draxler et al. 2018
independently found that minima from different training runs can be connected by paths of near-constant loss.
Entezari et al. 2022
conjectured that after accounting for permutation symmetry, SGD solutions from different initializations lie in a single basin;
Ainsworth et al. 2023
gave practical algorithms for aligning models modulo these symmetries. Our work adds to this picture by showing that overparameterization symmetries not only connect minima but reshape the curvature at those minima.

Convergence theory.

Jacot et al. 2018
introduced the neural tangent kernel (NTK), showing that in the infinite-width limit, training reduces to kernel regression with a fixed kernel. Building on this, several works proved that gradient descent on sufficiently wide networks converges to global minima at a linear rate:
Du et al. 2019
for deep residual networks,
Allen-Zhu et al. 2019
for general deep networks, and
Zou et al. 2019
for SGD on deep ReLU networks. These results typically require the width to be polynomial in the number of samples and the depth.
Chizat et al. 2019
showed that this favorable convergence arises from a “lazy training” regime where parameters stay near initialization and the model behaves as its linearization, which can underperform feature learning in practice. Our approach is different: rather than analyzing dynamics in a linearized regime, we characterize the geometric properties (conditioning, volume) of minima at finite width.

Implicit acceleration and deep linear networks.
Deep linear networks, where
f(x)=W_{L}\cdots W_{1}x
, are a standard testbed for optimization theory.
Saxe et al. 2014
derived exact learning dynamics, showing that depth creates saddle-to-saddle transitions that progressively learn singular value components.
Arora et al. 2018
showed that the resulting dynamics acts as implicit acceleration: the factored parameterization preconditions the optimization of the end-to-end matrix, with the effect depending on the singular values of the current solution.
Ghosh et al. 2025
extend this analysis beyond the edge of stability, characterizing oscillatory dynamics at large learning rates. We extend the preconditioning perspective in a different direction, from depth to width and from linear to general activations, showing that overparameterization symmetries provide diagonal preconditioning on the Hessian.

Conditioning and curvature.

Sagun et al. 2017
showed empirically that overparameterized networks have Hessian spectra with a large bulk of near-zero eigenvalues and a few data-dependent outliers; the near-zero eigenvalues correspond to flat directions from overparameterization, while the outliers capture the curvature relevant to learning.
Ghorbani et al. 2019
gave a more detailed eigenvalue density analysis, connecting spectral structure to properties of the data and architecture.
Guille-Escuret et al. 2023
found that optimization paths in neural networks have surprisingly simple geometry despite the non-convexity of the loss.
Yang et al. 2022
showed through
\mu
P that wider networks are more robust to learning rate choices, suggesting that width improves conditioning in a way that reduces hyperparameter sensitivity. Classical results on diagonal preconditioning
[
Forsythe and Straus 1955
,
Collobert 2004
]
show that coordinate rescaling can improve the condition number when eigenvectors are not uniformly distributed across coordinates; this underpins our analysis in
Section

5.3
.

Normalization and symmetry exploitation.
Several practical methods explicitly navigate weight-space symmetries to find better-conditioned parameterizations. Weight normalization
[
Salimans and Kingma 2016
]
decouples weight magnitude from direction, moving along the rescaling orbit. Path-SGD
[
Neyshabur et al. 2015
]
uses a path-regularized objective invariant to rescaling, so the trajectory is not distorted by the parameterization choice within an equivalence class.
Mishkin et al. 2025
introduced level-set teleportation, which solves for a better-conditioned point on the same level set by optimizing over the symmetry group. All of these can be viewed as searching within a symmetry orbit for favorable parameterizations.

\mathcal{N}(0,I)
and the teacher generates

error between student and teacher outputs.

We use maximal update parameterization

Yang et al. 2022

L.4
Hessian Computation

After convergence, we compute the Hessian eigenspectrum using the Lanczos algorithm with full reorthogonalization, which efficiently approximates the eigenvalue distribution via Hessian-vector products without forming the full matrix. For small networks used in theory validation we additionally compute the full Hessian exactly via finite differences to verify the Lanczos estimates. We extract: the complete eigenvalue spectrum, trace, top eigenvalue, condition number (
\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}^{+}
), pseudo-determinant (product of nonzero eigenvalues), and the AM/GM ratio of positive eigenvalues. Gradient norms are also recorded at the final iterate.
