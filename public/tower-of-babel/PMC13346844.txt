In vitro color prediction of the shade-gradient 5 mol% yttria-partially stabilized zirconia ceramics in different thicknesses: use of try-in pastes and resin cements
Authors: Elif Yılmaz Biçer, Beyza Güney, Merve Bankoğlu Güngör
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PURPOSEThe objective of the present in vitro study is to evaluate the color prediction of shade-gradient 5 mol% yttria-partially stabilized zirconia (5Y-PSZ) ceramics in different thicknesses using try-in pastes, unpolymerized resin cements, and polymerized resin cements.MATERIALS AND METHODSSpecimens with thicknesses of 0.4 mm, 0.6 mm, and 0.8 mm were fabricated using 5Y-PSZ zirconia ceramic. Substructure specimens were prepared from composite resin in A1 shade. Color parameters were measured after try-in paste application, before resin cement polymerization, and after resin cement polymerization. The color differences between the try-in paste and the unpolymerized resin cement (ΔE00-1), the try-in paste and the polymerized resin cement (ΔE00-2), and the unpolymerized resin cement and polymerized resin cement (ΔE00-3) were calculated using the CIEDE2000 formula. Data were statistically analyzed using the two-way repeated-measures ANOVA and the Bonferroni adjustment (α = 0.05).RESULTSTwo-way repeated-measures ANOVA revealed no interaction between thickness and prediction method (P = .128), but both factors significantly affected ΔE00 values (P < .05). Among the thickness groups, 0.4 mm specimens showed the highest ΔE00 values (1.45 ±0.9), while 0.6 mm and 0.8 mm specimens demonstrated significantly lower ΔE00 values (P < .05). Regarding the prediction method, all groups showed statistically significant differences (P < .05), with ΔE00-1 exhibiting the highest ΔE00 value (1.38 ±0.7), followed by ΔE00-2 and ΔE00-3.CONCLUSIONIn thin restorations (0.4 mm), color prediction became more challenging. Visually perceptible color differences were observed among try-in pastes and resin cements after polymerization; therefore, the use of unpolymerized resin cement yielded more reliable color prediction.

INTRODUCTION

Monolithic zirconia ceramics with improved optical properties and enhanced translucency, resulting from microstructural modifications, are commonly preferred in current clinical practice, particularly for esthetic and thin restorations.1 The improvements in the microstructure of the new generation zirconia ceramics focused on increasing the yttria content to 4 and 5 mol%. This alteration increases the cubic phase ratio, enhancing translucency and light transmission, but it also reduces mechanical properties. Among the new generations of zirconia ceramics, 5 mol% yttria- partially stabilized zirconia (5Y-PSZ) has the highest translucency.23 Increasing the yttria content to 5 mol%, resulting in an increased percentage (50%) of cubic content, has improved translucency to improve esthetic properties.345 To further optimize esthetic appearance, shade-gradient multilayer zirconia ceramics, which feature uniform yttria concentration and color pigments to mimic the natural optical properties of enamel and dentin, have also been introduced. These ceramics provide higher translucency in the incisal region and a cervical-to-incisal shade transition.6

Despite the improved optical properties, many clinical factors affect the esthetic and optical performance of high-translucent multilayer zirconia restorations.7 One of these factors is the thickness of the restoration. Particularly when the restoration thickness is minimal, accurately predicting the final color is crucial for achieving esthetic success during the try-in and final cementation stages.8 To accomplish an appropriate color-matching with the targeted color, try-in pastes and unpolymerized resin cements can be used to predict the final color.9 Using resin cements of varying colors may influence the final appearance of the restoration, thereby enhancing color harmony with the surrounding dentition. Try-in pastes serve as valuable tools for predicting the final color outcome and for selecting the most appropriate color of the resin cement before definitive bonding.1011 These systems include resin-based luting composites available in different colors, along with the corresponding try-in pastes.12 Achieving color consistency between try-in pastes and resin cements is essential for obtaining the intended final shade of porcelain restorations. While some resin cement systems provide try-in pastes that closely replicate the color of the polymerized cement, others show noticeable discrepancies between the try-in paste and the corresponding cement, which may compromise shade predictability.9 Moreover, the level of color agreement between try-in pastes and their corresponding resin cement shades was found to be shade-dependent, with satisfactory matching observed in certain shades but not in others.2 Additionally, the polymerization of the resin cement plays a crucial role in determining the final color, as incomplete or uneven polymerization can alter the shade and compromise the integrity of the restoration.7810 Özişçi et al.7 evaluated the color change before and after polymerization of ceramic laminate veneer restorations in a clinical study. They reported that the color coordinates differed before and after polymerization of the resin cements. The color of resin cements often lightens after polymerization is complete. Moreover, some resin cements exhibit significant color discrepancies compared to their corresponding shade guides after polymerization.7 Several studies have investigated the relationship between the color of try-in pastes and that of the corresponding polymerized resin cements, aiming to determine how accurately these materials can predict the final appearance of the restorations.81013 However, variations in refractive index, filler content, and light-scattering between try-in pastes and polymerized cements may result in perceptible color differences.1314 Despite growing research on ceramic systems, the effects of both try-in pastes and polymerized resin cements on the color prediction of shade-gradient 5Y-PSZ ceramic restorations remain unclear. Thus, the purpose of the present study was to evaluate the color-matching values among the try-in paste, unpolymerized resin cement, and polymerized resin cement when applied to the restorations fabricated from shade-gradient 5Y-PSZ in different thicknesses. The null hypothesis of the study was that the color-prediction method for 5Y-PSZ ceramics with different thicknesses would not have a significant effect on color- matching values.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The sample size was determined using a priori power analysis in a statistical software (G*Power version 3.1.9.7, University of Kiel, Kiel, Germany) for two-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA). Assuming an effect size (f = 0.25), a significance level of α = 0.05, a statistical power of 90%, and correlation among repeated measures of 0.7, the minimum required sample size was 30 (n = 10 per group). The flowchart of the study is summarized in Figure 1. The materials used in the present study are presented in Table 1.

Thirty specimens (n = 10 per group) were fabricated from ultra-translucent multilayer monolithic 5Y-PSZ discs, sectioned to include the gradient structure encompassing the enamel layer, gradational layer 1 and gradational layer 2 (Katana UTML; Kuraray Noritake Dental Inc., Tokyo, Japan) in enamel white shade by accounting for sintering shrinkage and subsequently sintering using a high-temperature sintering machine (inFire HTC speed; Dentsply Sirona, Bensheim, Germany) to obtain final dimensions of 10 × 10 mm in thicknesses of 0.4 mm, 0.6 mm, and 0.8 mm. The accuracy of the specimen dimensions was confirmed using a digital caliper. Following sintering, all specimens were coated with a clear-shade glaze (Cerabien ZR Glaze; Kuraray Noritake Dental Inc., Tokyo, Japan), prepared by mixing the powder and liquid components according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Before color measurements, the glazed specimens underwent a firing cycle in a porcelain furnace (Programat P300; Ivoclar Vivadent, Schaan, Liechtenstein) following the manufacturer’s recommended protocol. A total of 30 substructure specimens, each measuring 10 × 10 × 2 mm, were fabricated from composite resin (Clearfil Majesty ES-2; Kuraray Noritake Dental Inc., Tokyo, Japan) in A1 shade. Both the zirconia specimens and the composite substructures were cleaned in an ultrasonic cleaner before measuring the color coordinates.

The color coordinates of each specimen were measured in three steps using a spectrophotometer (CM-2300d; Konica Minolta, Tokyo, Japan): (1) with try-in paste between the restoration and the substructure, (2) with unpolymerized resin cement between the restoration and the substructure, and (3) with polymerized resin cement between the restoration and the substructure. The measured L*, a*, and b* color coordinates were used to calculate ΔE00-1 (color difference between try-in paste and unpolymerized resin cement), ΔE00-2 (color difference between try-in paste and polymerized resin cement), and ΔE00-3 (color difference between unpolymerized and polymerized resin cement).

A translucent try-in paste (Panavia V5 Try-in Paste Clear, Kuraray Noritake Dental Inc.) was applied to the interface between the substructure and the restoration, and the first color measurements were taken. Subsequently, all specimens were cleaned again using an ultrasonic cleaner. Then, the bonding surfaces of the zirconia ceramics were conditioned using a ceramic primer (Clearfil Ceramic Primer Plus; Kuraray Noritake Dental Inc.) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. The bonding surfaces of the substructure specimens were treated with a tooth primer (Panavia V5 Tooth Primer; Kuraray Noritake Dental Inc.) in accordance with the provided instructions. Dual-cure translucent resin cement (Panavia V5; Kuraray Noritake Dental Inc.) was applied under finger pressure. Excess cement around the specimens was removed, and the second measurements were recorded before polymerization. For the third measurements, the same resin cement layer presented during the second measurements was subsequently polymerized. Polymerization was achieved using an LED curing unit (Valo Cordless, Ultradent, South Jordan, UT, USA) operating at 385 − 515 nm, applied in direct contact with the specimen surfaces for 20 seconds. Color coordinates were measured from the central area of each specimen, under standardized positioning to ensure consistent measurements and reduce edge effects. The spectrophotometer was calibrated with its white calibration plate before each measurement step (try-in paste application and before and after resin cement polymerization). The measurements were performed under a D65 standard light source, using a d/8° geometry, a 10° standard observer, and a specular-excluded mode with an 8-mm-diameter measurement area. Color differences for all measurements were calculated using the CIEDE2000 (ΔE00) formula, and the ΔE00 values were compared with the perceptibility (0.8) and the acceptability threshold (1.8).14

The color difference (ΔE00) data were analyzed using SPSS 20 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of the color difference data was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk test. The data were analyzed using two-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA). The prediction methods were defined as within-subjects variables, while thickness (0.4 mm, 0.6 mm, and 0.8 mm) was defined as a between-subjects factor. Sphericity was tested and violated using Mauchly’s test (P = .011), and the Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Bonferroni adjustment. The level of statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.

RESULTS

The two-way repeated-measures ANOVA results showed no interaction between the thickness and prediction method factors (P = .128). However, both the thickness (P = .003, P < .05) and the prediction method had a significant effect on the ΔE00 values (P < .05). The descriptive and comparative analyses of the color difference data are presented in Table 2. Among the thickness groups, the 0.4 mm specimens exhibited the highest ΔE00 value (ΔE00 = 1.45 ± 0.88), indicating poor color-matching. In contrast, the 0.6 mm (0.76 ± 0.31) and 0.8 mm (0.86 ± 0.38) groups demonstrated significantly lower ΔE00 values (P < .05), indicating better color-matching. No significant difference was detected between the 0.6 mm and 0.8 mm groups (P > .05). Regarding the prediction method, ΔE00-1 (color-matching between try-in paste and unpolymerized resin cement) presented the highest ΔE00 value (1.38 ± 0.73), followed by ΔE00-2 (color-matching between try-in paste and polymerized resin cement) (1.03 ± 0.63), and ΔE00-3 (color-matching between unpolymerized and polymerized resin cement) with the lowest ΔE00 value (0.65 ± 0.28). Statistical analysis revealed significant differences among all prediction methods (P < .05). When compared with the perceptibility (0.8) and acceptability (1.8) thresholds, most of the mean color-matching values fell below the acceptability limit, suggesting that the observed color differences and color-matching were clinically acceptable; however, at the 0.4 mm thickness, the ΔE00-1 (color-matching between try-in paste and unpolymerized resin cement) (1.93 ± 1.01) exceeded the acceptability threshold. In particular, ΔE00-3 values (color-matching between unpolymerized and polymerized resin cement) for specimens with thicknesses of 0.6 mm (0.53 ± 0.08) and 0.8 mm (0.48 ± 0.07) indicated minimal visual discrepancies after polymerization.

DISCUSSION

The color-prediction methods for shade-gradient 5Y-PSZ ceramics in different thicknesses were investigated using try-in pastes, unpolymerized resin cements, and polymerized resin cements in the present in vitro study. The two-way repeated-measures ANOVA showed no interaction between the color prediction methods and thickness; however, both the prediction method and thickness affected the color-matching values. Therefore, the null hypothesis, which assumed no significant differences in color prediction across the tested prediction methods in different thicknesses, was rejected.

Microstructural modifications have increased the translucency of zirconia ceramics, enabling their use in thin restorations where esthetics are of primary importance.5 Among newly developed zirconia ceramics, 5Y-PSZ exhibits the highest translucency.35 It can be used in very thin restorations such as laminate veneers, and the minimum reported restoration thickness for this material is 0.4 mm.15 Therefore, the color-prediction methods for 5Y-PSZ ceramic were tested in the present study. The experimental specimens were prepared in thicknesses of 0.4, 0.6, and 0.8 mm, representing clinically applicable thin restoration thicknesses. However, particularly when thin restorations are used, achieving the planned color is a critical factor in the success of esthetic rehabilitation procedures. Proper color reproduction not only ensures a harmonious, natural appearance but also significantly contributes to the overall satisfaction of both the clinician and the patient.8 For color prediction in zirconia ceramics, try-in pastes and unpolymerized resin cements can be used.7916 Some studies reported that try-in pastes exhibited color-matching values compatible with their corresponding resin cements;816 however, contradictory findings were also reported.21718 The color-matching between a try-in paste and its corresponding resin cement can be affected by several material- and procedure-related factors. Differences in chemical composition, filler content, and refractive index between the try-in paste and the polymerized cement may cause variations in optical behavior.21119 Because try-in pastes are not polymerized, light-curing changes are not reproduced during application, resulting in color discrepancies.1120 Furthermore, variations in polymerization protocol, light intensity, and light exposure time can influence the final color of the resin cement, potentially leading to mismatches between try-in pastes and their corresponding cements.212223 In the present study, the color-matching values obtained with the try-in paste and the unpolymerized resin cement were evaluated based on the color measurements recorded after cement polymerization. In addition, by comparing the color-matching of the try-in paste and the unpolymerized resin cement, the study aimed to reveal the effect of cement polymerization on the color prediction. Cement shade can also influence this mismatch because translucent cements transmit more light from the underlying substrate and thus have a greater effect on the final color.24 For this reason, a translucent resin cement was used in the present study to represent clinically relevant color behavior better.

In the present study, the highest ΔE00 values were observed between the try-in paste and the unpolymerized resin cement groups (ΔE00-1) in all thicknesses. While the ΔE00-1 value at a thickness of 0.4 mm was above the acceptability threshold (1.93 ± 1.01), all ΔE00-1 values measured at 0.6 mm and 0.8 mm thicknesses remained below the acceptability threshold (1.8). In the present study, higher ΔE00 values were observed between the try-in paste and the unpolymerized resin cement (ΔE00-1) than between the try-in paste and the polymerized resin cement (ΔE00-2). This finding may be attributed to the intrinsic optical and compositional differences between try-in pastes and resin cements. Additionally, the findings suggested that the color of the cement determined try-in paste shades after polymerization. However, in the present study, a translucent try-in paste and the corresponding translucent resin cement from the same brand were used; results may vary with different shade options. Daneshpooy et al.2 tested 0.5-mm and 0.7-mm-thick ultra-translucent zirconia laminate veneers and evaluated the color compatibility of try-in paste and polymerized cement. In their study, the CIE L*a*b* color system was used, and the perceptibility and acceptability thresholds were set at 1 and 3.3, respectively. ΔEab values for clear shade of try-in paste and resin cement were found below the acceptability threshold in 0.5 mm (1.48 ± 0.51) and 0.7 mm (1.17 ± 0.53) thicknesses. Özdoğan et al.16 evaluated ΔE00 values between the try-in paste and polymerized resin cement using three different glass-ceramic materials, with thicknesses of 0.5 and 0.6 mm, for two resin cements (Calibra Veneer Esthetic Resin Cement-translucent and Nexus NX3-clear). They reported perceptibility and acceptability threshold values of 0.8 and 1.8, respectively. For lithium disilicate glass-ceramic specimens, ΔE00 values remained below 1.8 in both 0.5 and 0.6 mm thicknesses. In zirconia-reinforced lithium disilicate specimens, ΔE00 values were below 1.8 when Calibra Veneer resin cement was used. In contrast, values obtained with Nexus resin cement exceeded the acceptability threshold, reaching 1.93 at 0.5 mm and 3.08 at 0.6 mm. For lithium aluminosilicate glass-ceramic specimens cemented with Calibra Veneer, ΔE00 values remained below the 1.8 threshold only in a thickness of 0.5 mm. Vaz et al.8 investigated the color correspondence between try-in pastes and resin cements using lithium disilicate specimens with thicknesses of 0.35 mm and 0.7 mm, and calculated color differences. Their results showed that ΔEab values for both thicknesses remained within clinically acceptable limits, indicating a reliable color-matching between the try-in stage and the final restoration. Although higher ΔEab values were observed in 0.35 mm restorations, ΔEab decreased with increasing ceramic thickness, suggesting improved color-matching. In the present study, the ΔE00 values between the try-in paste and the polymerized resin cement (ΔE00-2) were below the acceptability threshold (1.8) at all thicknesses. Specimens in thicknesses of 0.6 mm (0.73 ± 0.34) and 0.8 mm (0.89 ± 0.21) showed ΔE00 values close to the perceptibility threshold (0.8). Although the values were within the clinically acceptable range, slight color differences may still be visible and may not be esthetically satisfactory. These findings were consistent with those of Daneshpooy et al.,2 who reported ΔEab values below the acceptability threshold for ultra-translucent zirconia veneers at 0.5 and 0.7 mm thicknesses, indicating reliable color-matching between the try-in paste and the polymerized cement in thin restorations. Similarly, Özdoğan et al.16 demonstrated acceptable ΔE00 values for lithium disilicate glass-ceramics in 0.5 and 0.6 mm thicknesses, confirming the predictability of try-in pastes. However, Özdoğan et al.16 also showed that zirconia-reinforced lithium disilicate and lithium aluminosilicate ceramics were more sensitive to cement type and thickness, with ΔE00 values exceeding the acceptability threshold when Nexus resin cement was used or when the thickness increased. In addition, Vaz et al.8 evaluated color-matching between try-in pastes and resin cements using lithium disilicate specimens with thicknesses of 0.35 mm and 0.7 mm and calculated color differences. Their results showed that ΔEab values for both thicknesses remained within clinically acceptable limits. In contrast, higher ΔEab values were observed in thinner restorations, and ΔEab values decreased as ceramic thickness increased, suggesting improved color-matching with increasing ceramic thickness.

Alghazali et al.9 indicated that the color of the unpolymerized resin cement may provide a more accurate guide for final shade prediction than the try-in paste, as more minor color differences were observed between unpolymerized and polymerized resin cements. Thus, color-matching between unpolymerized and polymerized resin cements was also evaluated in the present study. Although the ΔE00 value in 0.4 mm thickness was above the perceptibility threshold, it remained within the acceptable range. For specimens with thicknesses of 0.6 mm and 0.8 mm, ΔE00 values were measured at 0.53 ± 0.08 and 0.48 ± 0.07, respectively, both of which remained below the perceptibility threshold. These findings indicated that using unpolymerized resin cement for color prediction and avoiding excessively thin restorations can enhance the accuracy and reliability of final color outcomes in ultra-translucent monolithic zirconia restorations. Additionally, the results demonstrated that even a 0.2 mm increase in restoration thickness resulted in a color-matching between translucent unpolymerized and polymerized resin cement that was imperceptible to the human eye. Özişçi et al.7 investigated color changes in laminate veneers by evaluating the color differences between unpolymerized and polymerized resin cements. They defined ΔEab ≤ 2.0 as clinically imperceptible, 2.0 < ΔEab < 3.3 as clinically acceptable, and ΔEab ≥ 3.3 as indicating poor color-matching. In their study, all measured ΔEab values were ≤ 2.16, indicating that the color differences remained within clinically acceptable limits. These findings are consistent with the present study, which found that the ΔE00 values between unpolymerized and polymerized resin cements (ΔE00-3) remained within clinically acceptable limits. Küçükeşmen et al.25 evaluated the effect of light polymerization on the color change of a resin cement placed beneath lithium disilicate ceramic restorations in two different thicknesses by comparing color measurements before and after polymerization. The color difference was greater under thinner ceramic layers, indicating that ceramic thickness influences the optical impact of cement polymerization. All ΔEab values exceeded the perceptible threshold of 2.5. The study concluded that color-matching with unpolymerized resin cement may be unreliable, and that polymerization should be considered accurately to predict the final color outcome of ceramic restorations. Although Küçükeşmen et al.25 reported perceptible color changes under thin ceramic layers after polymerization, all ΔE00-3 values in the present study remained below the acceptability threshold, even at a thickness of 0.4 mm. This discrepancy may be attributed to the use of the ΔEab formula in the previous study, whereas the present study employed the CIEDE2000 (ΔE00) formula. Furthermore, the absence of a significant effect of thickness on color differences at 0.6 mm and above indicates the material-specific optical behavior in shade gradient 5Y-PSZ zirconia.

This study has several limitations inherent to its in vitro design, as experimental conditions do not fully simulate the clinical oral environment. The analysis was restricted to a single substructure shade (A1) and a single zirconia type and shade (5Y-PSZ and enamel white), which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other materials, dentin shades, and translucency levels. In addition, only translucent try-in paste and its corresponding resin cement from the same brand were used. Therefore, the results may differ when different shade combinations or restorative materials are tested. In addition, only one polymerization protocol was used, and aging or long-term color stability was not assessed. Therefore, the results should be interpreted cautiously, and further clinical studies are required to validate these findings under long-term conditions.

CONCLUSION

Within the limitations of this study, the following conclusions were noted.

Material thickness and prediction method significantly influenced color-matching values in the tested 5Y-PSZ ceramic when translucent try-in paste and translucent resin cement were used.

Thinner specimens (0.4 mm) showed poor color-matching, whereas increasing the thickness to 0.6 mm or 0.8 mm significantly improved color-matching.

Among the prediction methods, the better color-matching was observed between the unpolymerized and polymerized resin cement groups, indicating that unpolymerized cement provided the most reliable color prediction after polymerization.
