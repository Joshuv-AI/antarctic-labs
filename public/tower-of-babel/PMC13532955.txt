Does the Loss of Autobiographical Memories Contribute to the Therapeutic Effect of Electroconvulsive Therapy?
Authors: Mike van Kessel, Joris van der Vlugt‐Molenaar, Isidoor O. Bergfeld, Eric van Exel, Jaap Murre, Jasmien Obbels, Pascal Sienaert, Kristof Vansteelandt, Esmée Verwijk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveFor decades, a persistent claim has been that autobiographical memory loss after electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) for depression might actually contribute to ECT efficacy by reducing or even eliminating autobiographical memories. To test this claim, the primary aim of this study is to examine the association between autobiographical memory loss and remission of depression. The hypothesis is that remitted patients have more autobiographical memory loss after ECT compared to non‐remitted patients.MethodsIn 71 patients with major depressive disorder undergoing ECT, autobiographical memory consistency (Kopelman Autobiographical Memory Interview) and depression severity (Montgomery–Åsberg Depression Rating Scale) were assessed before and within 1 week after treatment. Logistic regression analyses were conducted to examine the association between both autobiographical memory loss (i.e., memory consistency) and remission (MADRS < 10), including age, episode duration, baseline MADRS score, and treatment condition as covariates.ResultsAll logistic regression models were significant. The overall autobiographical memory consistency‐score (OR = 1.072, 95% CI [1.018–1.130], p = 0.009) and the consistency‐score for recent memories (OR = 1.043, 95% CI [1.006–1.082], p = 0.021) were significantly associated with the odds of remission but in the opposite direction of the hypothesis. A higher age and shorter episode duration further increased the likelihood of remission. Additionally, post hoc analyses showed that the trajectories of autobiographical memory performance over time differed between remitters and non‐remitters, indicating a slight decrease in autobiographical memories for more recent events in non‐remitters and no change in remitters.ConclusionsThis study shows that, contrary to the hypothesis, remitted patients have less autobiographical memory loss, particularly for recent memories than non‐remitters. This refutes the premise that the loss of autobiographical memories contributes to the therapeutic effectiveness of ECT.

Introduction

Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) is regarded as the most effective treatment option for treatment‐resistant major depressive disorder [1, 2, 3] and high response rates have been reported in patients with psychotic depression, catatonia, and schizophrenia [4]. Although cognitive side‐effects are common in multiple domains, like memory, attention, and executive functioning, studies consistently show most of these side‐effects to be transient [5, 6, 7]. The majority of patients return to baseline cognitive functioning or even improve after ECT, whereas a small subgroup shows decreased cognitive functioning [8, 9, 10], persisting at long‐term follow‐up. Multiple studies suggest autobiographical memory loss might be the most prominent long‐term adverse effect in patients treated with ECT [11, 12, 13].

In general, autobiographical memory is defined as the ability to recall self‐related events in reference to one's own life [14, 15, 16], comprising of episodic (i.e., unique personal events in a specific time and place) and semantic autobiographical memories (i.e., general knowledge of personal facts) [17, 18]. Although multiple theoretical taxonomies of long‐term memory have been proposed, autobiographical memory is commonly regarded as part of the episodic memory system, as introduced by Tulving in 1972 [19, 20].

Autobiographical memory loss, in particular, is frequently reported by patients as a burdensome cognitive side‐effect after ECT [21, 22]. A recent systematic review and meta‐analysis concludes that ECT causes autobiographical memory loss, which is not regained, with right unilateral (RUL) ECT—as opposed to bilateral ECT—showing smaller adverse effects [23]. Studies have shown that autobiographical memory loss associated with ECT is most pronounced for relatively recent autobiographical memories [11, 24, 25, 26]. However, the extent of autobiographical memory loss and its association with other variables, including treatment efficacy and pretreatment factors such as anxiety, remains insufficiently investigated [12, 22, 23, 24]. Moreover, depression itself can directly affect multiple cognitive domains [27, 28, 29], including autobiographical memory functioning, which further complicates the interpretation of causal effects.

Regarding treatment efficacy, a compelling claim [30], first formulated more than eight decades ago, has been that autobiographical memory loss after ECT, despite being a negative side‐effect for patients, might actually contribute to remission of depression by reducing or even eliminating possible depressogenic autobiographical memories [31, 32, 33]. This hypothesis is, in part, based on the premise that ECT might disrupt the reconsolidation of recently activated negative (autobiographical) memories [34].

Since then, advancement in ECT techniques has led to progressive treatment refinement, resulting in better cognitive outcomes without sacrificing treatment efficacy [35]. In addition, several studies, including McElhiney et al. [26], found autobiographical memory performance to be related to electrode placement and stimulus intensity but not to changes in clinical state. Nevertheless, to the best of our knowledge, the association between autobiographical memory loss and remission of depression has not yet been unequivocally examined, especially not recently in controlled studies using contemporary refined ECT techniques.

Aim of the Study

Therefore, the primary aim of this study is to explore the association between autobiographical memory loss and remission of depression. We hypothesize that remitted patients have more autobiographical memory loss after ECT for depression compared to non‐remitted patients. In addition, because autobiographical memory loss is thought to be most prominent in relatively recent autobiographical memories, we expect more autobiographical memory loss for relatively recent memories in a group of remitters compared to the non‐remitter group.

Methods and Materials

Study Design

A secondary analysis was conducted on data derived from a prospective, double blind multi‐center randomized controlled trial evaluating the efficacy and cognitive side‐effects of twice weekly brief pulse (BP) and ultra brief pulse (UBP) ECT 1 during a 6‐week period [36]. In the original trial, conducted from April 2007 until March 2011, patients were recruited from three ECT centers: Parnassia (The Hague) and GGZ Delfland (Delft) in The Netherlands and the University Psychiatric Center KU Leuven, Campus Kortenberg (Kortenberg) in Belgium.

Participants

Both in‐ and outpatients were eligible for inclusion if 18 years or older and diagnosed with a major depressive episode per DSM‐IV criteria, confirmed by an experienced psychiatrist. Exclusion criteria were a history of alcohol and/or drug abuse, a history of head injury, presence of dementia, schizophrenia, or schizoaffective disorder. All eligible patients were asked to participate, and baseline assessments were done after they provided informed consent. Patients who dropped out during the original trial were excluded.

For this secondary analysis, only patients with complete cognitive data at baseline and post‐ECT were included.

ECT

The ECT‐protocol has been described in detail elsewhere [36]. In brief, ECT sessions were administered twice weekly for a maximum of 6 weeks using RUL d'Elia electrode placement [37]. Patients were randomly assigned to either BP right unilateral ECT or UBP right unilateral ECT, with a pulse width of 1.0 millisecond and 0.3 milliseconds respectively, and a stimulus of eight times the seizure threshold. The ECT course was terminated at remission or after the 6‐week period, that is, 12 sessions.

Psychotropic and somatic medication was continued during ECT. Lithium was kept at plasma levels of 0.40–0.80 mmol/L. Benzodiazepines were limited to a maximum of 10 mg diazepam equivalents.

Clinical and Demographic Characteristics

Demographic and clinical data, including gender, age, education level [38], duration of the index major depressive episode, psychosis, polarity, pulse width, number of index ECT sessions, and previous ECT treatment were assessed.

Depression

Severity of depression was assessed by trained nurses at baseline, weekly and between 48 h and 1 week after the last ECT session, using the Montgomery–Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) [39]. Scores range from 0 to 60, with higher scores indicating greater severity of depressive symptoms. The MADRS shows adequate reliability and validity for assessing depressive symptoms [40]. Remission was defined as a MADRS score of < 10 at two consecutive weekly assessments.

Autobiographical Memory

Autobiographical memory testing was executed by a neuropsychologist or supervised trainee neuropsychologist, within the week preceding the first ECT session and between 48 h and 1 week after finishing the treatment course. The Kopelman Autobiographical Memory Interview (Kopelman‐AMI) [41, 42] is a reliable and validated test to assess both semantic and episodic autobiographical memory. Participants are asked questions pertaining to personal semantic memories from different time periods, namely childhood, early adulthood, and the recent period (within 5 years). Each period has a maximum score of 21 points. Also, for each time period, participants are asked to relate incidents pertaining to episodic autobiographical memories. For these incidents, each time period has a maximum of 9 points. Combining these scores gives a maximum total score of 90.

For this study, the “autobiographical memory consistency‐score” was calculated. This outcome measure is not part of the original Kopelman‐AMI scoring system. Inspired by the Columbia University Autobiographical Memory Interview (CUAMI) consistency score [43, 44] and previously executed by Verwijk et al. [36], in this study the answers with a maximum score per question at baseline were compared with answers on those same questions post‐ECT. This way, the autobiographical memory consistency‐score reflects the percentage of memories retained at the post‐ECT assessment relative to the baseline assessment. More autobiographical memory loss is demonstrated by lower consistency‐scores.

Statistical Analysis

To explore baseline variables and compare differences in these variables between different groups, Pearson's chi‐square tests for categorical variables, two‐tailed t‐tests, and nonparametric Mann–Whitney U‐tests for continuous variables were used where appropriate.

To compare autobiographical memory performance between the remitter and non‐remitter group, mean scores, standard deviations, and ranges were used from three time periods assessed with The Kopelman‐AMI, more specific: childhood, early adulthood, and recent, as well as the total number of incidents and the overall total score. Due to the non‐normal distribution, Mann–Whitney U‐tests were used to analyse differences in autobiographical memory performance between remitters and non‐remitters at baseline.

Mann–Whitney U‐tests were also used to compare means between the remitters and non‐remitters for the autobiographical memory consistency‐score of the different time periods and total score plus incidents. For the “incidents consistency‐score,” patients who could not report any autobiographical memory incidents at baseline were excluded because in those cases a consistency‐score could not be calculated.

Logistic regression analyses were computed to examine if the severity of autobiographical memory loss, using the consistency‐score, was associated with remission, while adjusting for age, current episode duration, baseline MADRS score, and treatment condition (BP/UBP). Five separate models were estimated, one for each consistency‐score (Childhood, Adulthood, Recent, Incidents, and Total + Incidents).

Prior to the logistic regression analyses, multicollinearity among the independent variables and covariates was assessed. Variance Inflation Factors (VIF) and tolerance values were calculated. All VIFs were well below 5, indicating no problematic multicollinearity. Due to the methodological design of the study, the number of ECT sessions was not included as a covariate into the analyses. After all, patients in the non‐remission group all had the maximum number of 12 ECT sessions, whereas patients in the remission group had significantly less (Table 2), leading to a near perfect prediction of remission status. As a result, entering this covariate into the model would increase the likelihood of “saturating” the model, leading to unstable results. Additionally, to further examine the possible association between number of ECT sessions and autobiographical memory performance, Pearson's correlations were calculated showing no significant correlations (all R < 0.15) between any of autobiographical memory scores and the number of ECT sessions (Data not shown).

Post hoc analyses were performed to further examine autobiographical memory performance over time between both groups. Linear mixed models with random intercepts were used with Time (baseline versus post‐ECT) as a within‐subject factor and remission status (remitters vs. non‐remitters) as a between‐subject factor, adjusting for age, current episode duration, baseline MADRS score, and treatment condition (BP/UBP). Results were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05, unless specified otherwise. JASP [45] was used for all statistical analyses.

Binary logistic regression analyses were conducted to examine whether autobiographical memory consistency for each of the time periods predicted remission from depression, while controlling for age, baseline MADRS score, episode duration, and treatment condition (BP/UBP).

For the total autobiographical memory consistency‐score (Total + Incidents) as well as for the consistency‐score of recent memories alone, both models were statistically significant (χ 2 (5) = 32.298, p < 0.001 and χ 2 (5) = 30.893, p < 0.001, respectively) explaining 52% and 48% of the variance in remission status (Nagelkerke R 2). The odds ratios for the total autobiographical memory consistency‐score (OR = 1.072, 95% CI [1.018–1.130], p = 0.009) and the consistency‐score for recent memories (OR = 1.043, 95% CI [1.006–1.082], p = 0.021) were significant; for each unit increase in both scores, the odds of going into remission were, respectively, 1.072 and 1.043 higher than the odds of not going into remission.

In addition, for both analyses, age (OR = 1.073, 95% CI [1.016–1.133], p = 0.012 and OR = 1.066, 95% CI [1.014–1.120], p = 0.012, respectively) and episode duration (OR = 0.950, 95% CI [0.911–0.990], p = 0.014 and OR = 0.960, 95% CI [0.923–0.999], p = 0.044, respectively) were also significant predictors of remission. Treatment condition and baseline MADRS score were not significant (p > 0.05).

Regarding the consistency‐scores of childhood and adulthood memories and episodic incidents, all overall models were statistically significant (χ 2 (5) = 27.163, p < 0.001 and χ 2 (5) = 24.935, p < 0.001 and χ 2 (5) = 24.750, p < 0.001, respectively) explaining approximately 44%, 41%, and 40% of the variance in remission status (Nagelkerke R 2). However, the consistency‐scores of childhood and adulthood memories and episodic incidents were not associated with the likelihood of remission (p > 0.05). Both age and episode duration did show a significant association with remission status in all three analyses, with a higher age (OR = 1.053, 95% CI [1.004–1.105], p = 0.034 and OR = 1.054, 95% CI [1.006–1.104], p = 0.027 and OR = 1.051, 95% CI [1.004–1.101], p = 0.035, respectively) and shorter episode duration (OR = 0.949, 95% CI [0.912–0.988], p = 0.011 and OR = 0.956, 95% CI [0.920–0.992], p = 0.018 and OR = 0.956, 95% CI [0.920–0.994], p = 0.023, respectively) increasing the odds of remission. Again, treatment condition and baseline MADRS score were not significant (p > 0.05).

In summary, contrary to the hypothesis, a higher total memory consistency‐score and a higher consistency‐score for recent memories, that is, less memory loss, are significantly associated with remission. In addition, in all models, a shorter episode duration and higher age increase the likelihood of remission.

Post hoc analyses were performed to compare the pre‐ to post‐ECT trajectories of the different periods of autobiographical memory performance between the remitter and non‐remitter groups (Figure 2).

For both the autobiographical childhood memory scores as well as the adulthood memory scores, the model was non‐significant. Autobiographical memory for childhood and adulthood memories did not change after ECT compared to baseline performance. The remitter group and non‐remitter group did not differ in trajectories.

Regarding the autobiographical memory scores for recent memories, the time × remission status interaction was significant, F (1, 63) = 4.772, p = 0.033, indicating that the trajectories of autobiographical memory performance over time differed between remitters and non‐remitters, whereby, upon visual inspection, non‐remitters showed a slight decrease and remitters no change after ECT. In addition, age was a significant predictor, F (1, 63) = 10.390, p = 0.002, whereby higher age was associated with a lower overall autobiographical memory performance for recent memories. Current episode duration, baseline MADRS score and treatment condition (BP/UBP) did not contribute significantly to the model. Change in MADRS‐score was not included in the model, due to its strong correlation with remission status (R = 0.613, p < 0.001), leading to the risk of collinearity and destabilizing the model.

For the total autobiographical memory scores, a main effect of time, F (1, 63) = 4.398, p = 0.040 was found, indicating an overall decline from pre‐ to post‐treatment. However, the time × remission status interaction was not significant (p = 0.298), indicating no difference in trajectory over time between remitters and non‐remitters. Again, age was a significant predictor, F (1, 63) = 11.009, p = 0.002, with higher age associated with lower overall autobiographical memory performance. Current episode duration, baseline MADRS score and treatment condition (BP/UBP) did not contribute significantly to the model.

Discussion

Based on the results of this study, contrary to the hypothesis, remitted patients do not show more autobiographical memory loss than non‐remitted patients after ECT for treatment‐resistant depression. Instead, remission was associated with less autobiographical memory loss, particularly for recent memories. Although no causal attributions can be made, these findings do contradict the premise that the loss of autobiographical memories might actually contribute to the therapeutic effectiveness of the treatment.

In addition, higher age and a shorter episode duration increase the likelihood of remission, which is a consistent finding in this and other studies on the efficacy of ECT [46, 47].

These results refute a theory mentioned by Janis in the 1940s [31, 32, 33]. More recently, a closely related theory was tested by Scheepens et al. [48], based on findings by Kroes et al. [49], who demonstrated that in depressed patients, even a single ECT session following memory reactivation disrupted reactivated, but not non‐reactivated memory for a learned emotional aversive story. Scheepens et al. then conducted a randomized clinical trial, in which autobiographical memories strongly related to negative schemas were reactivated immediately prior to ECT. The hypothesis was that ECT would disrupt the reconsolidation process and thereby weaken the negative schemas relevant to the current depressive episode. Results, however, showed no effect on the treatment outcome of ECT. Related to the present study, no association was found between memory disruption and the effectiveness of ECT.

Although the current study did not involve reactivation of negative autobiographical memories, but rather a general assessment of autobiographical memory performance, evidence up until now does not confirm that memory reactivation is a contributing factor for autobiographical memory loss. For instance, another study by Scheepens et al. [50] demonstrated that even though ECT is associated with improved negative schemas, the reactivation of emotional autobiographical memories did not yield any additional effects.

There is extensive research on the underlying mechanisms of the effectiveness of ECT. The current study suggests that (the severity of) autobiographical memory loss neither explains this mechanism nor predicts its efficacy. More likely, decreased cognitive functioning, including autobiographical memory, after ECT should primarily be regarded as an adverse side‐effect [6, 7] and not a factor contributing to its efficacy. A recent review by Deng and colleagues [35] states that it is not yet fully understood whether the applied electric current or the following seizure, or both, contribute to the risk of adverse cognitive side‐effects.

In terms of the underlying neurobiological mechanism, recent studies show that ECT affects brain volume, neurotrophic factors, brain plasticity, neurotransmission and neuroinflammation [51, 52, 53]. How, from a more clinical perspective, these neurobiological changes relate to cognitive changes such as autobiographical memory functioning, is still far from understood. In general, research on the association between cognitive side‐effects, including autobiographical memory, and treatment efficacy is scarce, with no evidence for a robust association with objective cognitive side‐effects, except for improved verbal memory and verbal fluency in remitted patients [54, 55].

In the post hoc analyses, remitters tend to show no change in performance for more recent autobiographical memories, whereas non‐remitters tend to show a decline. Comparably, a study by Obbels et al. found an initial decrease in verbal memory, verbal fluency as well as autobiographical memory performance, as measured with the Kopelman‐AMI, during ECT, with performance levels returning to baseline within 3 months after ECT [56]. When looking at depressive symptomatology, no significant association was found with autobiographical memory performance. Different from the present study, no clear distinction in remission status was made and consistency‐scores were not included in the analyses.

The results of this study add to the question of how this level of memory loss compares to that observed in non‐ECT depressed patients and healthy controls. In this perspective, recently Jelovac et al. emphasized the importance of including clinical control groups when interpreting changes in autobiographical memory consistency over time [57]. In their study, a group of moderate to severely depressed patients with ECT treatment showed more decline in autobiographical memory consistency than a group of comparably depressed controls without ECT. However, the depressed control group also showed a substantial amount of decline in autobiographical memory consistency over time. These results lead to the compelling advice to include normative control data when interpreting ECT‐related results.

This study has several limitations. Most importantly, this study used the Kopelman Autobiographical Memory Interview [41, 42, 58], which was not designed to measure memory consistency, but rather to assess autobiographical memory over time in neurological disorders such as dementia [59, 60]. Instead, the CUAMI [43, 44] was developed with the primary purpose to assess consistency of autobiographical memory (memory loss) by calculating a consistency‐score over time and is currently the preferred assessment tool in ECT studies. In the present study, this issue was addressed by calculating a percentage of responses given at the post‐ECT assessment, identical to the response given at baseline on items with a maximum baseline score on the Kopelman‐AMI. Ideally, future research should use the CUAMI when focusing on autobiographical memory loss after ECT for depression and the Kopelman‐AMI when interested in autobiographical memory functioning over time. Preferably, future research might focus on combining both instruments to increase the sensitivity and specificity of assessing both consistency and autobiographical memory functioning over time.

Another limitation of the current study is the fact that there was no objective assessment of the emotional nature of the autobiographical memories. Although research does suggest a bias for negative autobiographical memories in depression [61], the data used in the present study did not include the necessary details to add this in the analyses.

The number of ECT sessions was not included in the analyses as a covariate due to the risk of “saturating” the model, as explained earlier. As studies have shown that the number of ECT sessions affects cognitive functioning, this could be a limitation on the results of this study. However, Pearson's correlations between autobiographical memory and the number of sessions were not significant. Also, literature suggests that the negative effect of the number of ECT sessions on cognitive functioning is likely to become more pronounced in prolonged courses of more than 12 sessions [10], which was not the case in this study. Nevertheless, if possible, the number of sessions should be considered in studies on cognitive functioning after ECT.

Finally, there were more patients with psychotic symptoms or bipolar depression in the excluded group compared to the included group. This should lead to caution with regard to the generalizability of the findings of this study because of a possible selection bias. Studies show that psychotic features and older age are both associated with an increased likelihood of response and remission following electroconvulsive therapy [46, 62]. Cognitive outcomes following ECT are influenced by both patient‐related factors, such as age and baseline cognitive functioning, and treatment parameters, including electrode placement and number of ECT sessions, although overall predictability and the association with treatment efficacy remain limited [63].

Given the fact that decreased autobiographical memory functioning is often reported as the most disturbing side effect of ECT [21, 22] with possible anxiety‐inducing effects [64], future research should continue to focus on the exact nature and mechanism of autobiographical memory loss after ECT. The results of this study show that minimizing the risk of autobiographical memory loss does not mean sacrificing treatment efficacy.

Although prevention at this point is not yet feasible, results of such studies might be beneficial in further developing more refined psychoeducation before treatment and possible (cognitive) remediation after treatment.

In conclusion, this study shows, contrary to the hypothesis, that remitted patients have less autobiographical memory loss, particularly for recent memories. This refutes the premise that the loss of autobiographical memories contributes to the therapeutic effectiveness of ECT treatment. In addition, post hoc analyses suggest that the trajectories of autobiographical memory performance over time differ between remitters and non‐remitters, indicating a slight decrease in autobiographical memories for more recent events in non‐remitters and no change in remitters.
