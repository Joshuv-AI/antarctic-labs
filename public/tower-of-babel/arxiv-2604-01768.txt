State–constrained optimal control of the continuity equation and infinite–dimensional viscosity solutions
Fabio Bagagiolo, Ivan Romanò

Abstract
We study a finite-horizon optimal control problem for the continuity equation under a weighted integral state constraint on the mass outside a fixed set. The model is cast in a Hilbert framework for densities. On a suitable invariant compact subset, we prove that the value function is Lipschitz continuous and satisfies, by dynamic programming, the associated infinite-dimensional constrained Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation in viscosity sense (subsolution in the interior, supersolution up to the boundary). We finally prove a comparison principle and uniqueness in the Lipschitz class. Keywords: infinite-dimensional HJB, state-constrained optimal control, continuity equation, viscosity solutions. MSC Classification: 49L25, 35R15, 49L20, 35L03, 35F21.

Introduction

In this paper we address an optimal control problem for a first-order continuity equation of the form

\partial_{t}m+\mbox{div}(\alpha m)=0

(1)

where the unknown
m:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
is the density function of the distribution of a mass evolving on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and the vector field
\alpha:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the control at our disposal. In particular, besides the minimization of an integral cost functional of the form

J(\bar{m},t,\alpha)=\int_{t}^{T}\ell(m(\cdot,s),\alpha(\cdot,s))\;ds+\psi(m(\cdot,T)),

the optimal control problem is subject to a state-constraint: the mass should remain inside a fixed domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
. A suitable way to analytically write such a constraint is also discussed in the paper and the main results involve a relaxed formulation of it of the form

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega}p(x)m(x,s)\;dx\leq\delta\qquad\forall s\in[t,T],

(2)

where
p:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a fixed positive penalty function and
\delta>0
is a fixed threshold. For the value function

V(m,t)=\inf_{\alpha}J(m,\alpha,t),

defined on a suitable subset of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times[0,T]
, we prove, under suitable hypotheses, its (Lipschitz) continuity and its uniqueness as a viscosity solution of the constrained infinite-dimensional Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation, coupled with a terminal condition,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle-\partial_{t}V+\sup_{a}\left\{\langle\nabla_{m}V,\mbox{div}(am)\rangle-\ell(m,a)\right\}=0&\mbox{if }\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega}p(x)m(x)dx<\delta,\\
\displaystyle-\partial_{t}V+\sup_{a}\left\{\langle\nabla_{m}V,\mbox{div}(am)\rangle-\ell(m,a)\right\}\geq 0&\mbox{if }\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega}p(x)m(x)dx=\delta,\\
\displaystyle V(m,T)=\psi(m)\end{array}\right.

(3)

where
a:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a constant (in time) vector field,
\nabla_{m}V
is the Fréchet differential with respect to
m\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the scalar product in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
We suitably adapt the seminal results for the finite-dimensional case of Soner
[
23
]
(see also Bardi-Capuzzo Dolcetta
[
6
]
) to our infinite-dimensional case. In particular, we first construct a "pushback" control which allows us to compress the mass towards
\Omega
when the constraint is going to be violated; suitably using such a control as a needle, only when necessary, we prove the Lipschitz continuity of the value function; by the dynamic programming formula we prove that the value function solves, in the viscosity sense, the infinite-dimensional problem (
3
) in a suitable subset
\bar{X}_{c}\subseteq L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times[0,T]
; constructing a penalization function in the double variable technique, exploiting a suitable inward-cone property of the constraint, we get a comparison result for (
3
) and hence uniqueness in the class of Lipschitz functions.

As in Bagagiolo–Capuani–Marzufero
[
4
,
5
]
, where some differential games for (
1
) are studied, we aim to pose our problem in a Hilbert setting, which means that we are going to work with masses whose distribution over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is given by measures
\mu
having a density function
m
. In order to keep this true for all times during the evolution, we assume a suitable (spatial) regularity of the initial datum
m_{0}\in\ L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and of the vector fields
\alpha(\cdot,t):\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Such regularities also bring the necessary compactness property that we need in order to get the comparison result for (
3
), in particular the compactness of
\bar{X}_{c}
in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times[0,T]
. A crucial point is also the estimate between the
H^{1}
and the
L^{2}
norms.

Several works about the control of the evolution of (
1
) have been published in recent years. A main impulse to such research was given by the analysis of first-order mean-field games (see Cardaliaguet
[
11
]
and Lasry–Lions
[
19
]
). We point out that in our model the equation (
1
) is controlled in a centralized manner: the control
\alpha
is externally chosen by a sort of "supervisor" and not constructed by the individual decisions of the single particles of the mass (decision-making agents). Recent results on centralized control of continuity equations can be grouped into two directions: a DPP/HJB-viscosity approach in Wasserstein spaces (Jimenez–Marigonda–Quincampoix
[
18
]
, centralized multi-agent formulation and HJB characterization; Aussedat–Jerhaoui–Zidani
[
3
]
, comparison and uniqueness for viscosity solutions in measure spaces) and a variational/Pontryagin approach (Pogodaev
[
21
]
, first-order necessary conditions for controlled continuity equations; Bonnet–Rossi
[
7
]
, Pontryagin maximum principle in the Wasserstein space). In the specific setting of infinite-dimensional control problems with state constraints, Daudin
[
15
]
addresses a second-order (diffusive) Fokker–Planck dynamics in Wasserstein space where the constraint is actually formulated as in (
2
), Bonnet
[
8
]
proves a Pontryagin maximum principle for constrained problems with nonlocal drift dependence, and Cavagnari–Marigonda–Quincampoix
[
12
]
study compatibility conditions between state constraints and admissible multi-agent dynamics for the continuity equation. In all these works equation (
1
) is meant for measures and the whole problem is cast in the Wasserstein setting. As we said, in the present paper we adopt a functional perspective and we believe that such an approach, especially for the state-constrained problem, is conceptually natural: working in
L^{2}
keeps the density description explicit and provides a Hilbert structure well suited to compactness, stability, and HJB-viscosity analysis. Moreover, up to our knowledge, in the measure/Wasserstein setting, the analysis of the continuity of the value function and the uniqueness of the viscosity solution for the corresponding constrained HJB equation arising from the constrained optimal control of (
1
) and made à la Soner
[
23
]
is still lacking in the literature. It may be the subject of future work. A related constrained problem in Hilbert spaces was studied by Cannarsa–Gozzi–Soner
[
10
]
, for a different state equation: they consider an abstract controlled evolution equation and the associated boundary value problem for an infinite-dimensional HJB equation.

We believe that, from the analytical point of view, the novelty of our study mainly relies on the fact that,
up to our knowledge, it is the first time that
a state-constrained optimal control problem for the specific first-order equation (
1
) is studied directly with regard to the continuity of the value function and a comparison principle for the corresponding infinite-dimensional Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman problem in a Hilbert space. Note that in Cannarsa-Gozzi-Soner
[
10
]
the state equation, partly motivated by the control of a second-order-type equation (typically the heat equation), is mostly a generalization to the Hilbert case of the finite-dimensional controlled evolution
y^{\prime}=f(y,u)
, and this fact somehow reflects in all their work. Moreover, that model does not seem to take into account the presence of the control in the principal part of the differential equation as in our case. Finally, we also point out that in
[
10
]
the control problem is of the so-called exit-time type (strongly related to the constraint problem and raising most of the same analytical questions) and hence their value function does not depend on time, unlike ours.

From the application point of view, our model of state-constrained optimal control may arise from many practical situations where a super-manager has to optimally govern the motion of a huge quantity of some kind of elements, be they particles, agents, information, data to be transmitted or to be clustered. Here, just as an example, we quote the recent work by Geshkovski-Letrouit-Polyanskiy-Rigollet
[
16
]
(see also Bruno-Pasqualotto-Agazzi
[
9
]
) where, concerning the evolution of interacting particles (tokens, in particular), the authors study a continuity equation as (
1
) constrained to the sphere of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where the velocity field is a given function of the solution somehow linked to the optimization of an interaction energy functional. Other possible applications may arise in economic models. See for example Mehadoui-Lacitignola-Tilioua
[
20
]
where a state-constrained optimal control problem for a system of PDEs is studied with the state constraint represented by an integral bound (similar to (
2
)) on the spatially distributed technological progress.

As generic references for the theory of viscosity solutions for Hamilton-Jacobi equations, both in finite and infinite dimensions, we refer the reader to the seminal papers by Crandall–Lions
[
13
,
14
]
and to the book by Bardi–Capuzzo Dolcetta
[
6
]
, whereas for the analysis of the continuity equation (
1
), we refer to Santambrogio
[
22
]
and Ambrosio–Crippa
[
1
,
2
]
.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we study the controlled continuity equation (
1
), show regularity and stability estimates, and construct the compact invariant set used in the analysis. In Section 3 we introduce the state-constrained optimal control problem, prove the dynamic programming principle, and establish the Lipschitz continuity of the value function on
\bar{X}_{c}\subseteq L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times[0,T]
. In Section 4 we derive the corresponding constrained Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation and show that the value function is a constrained viscosity solution. In Section 5 we prove the comparison principle and deduce uniqueness in the Lipschitz class. In Section 6 we discuss alternative formulations of the state constraint and comment on their suitability for our framework. The Appendix collects various estimates on the flow associated to the continuity equation, mostly used in Section 2.

2
The continuity equation

In this Section we first recall the flow representation and some uniform estimates; regularity and stability are proved in Subsection
2.1
, and we construct an invariant compact set
X
for the evolutions in Subsection
2.2
.

Let
T
be the fixed time horizon,
t\in[0,T)
and consider a time-dependent (velocity) vector field
\alpha:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The corresponding evolution in time of a density
m:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[t,T]\to\mathbb{R}
is described by the continuity equation initial-data problem (
4
).

\begin{cases}\partial_{s}m(x,s)+\diver(\alpha(x,s)m(x,s))=0&\text{for }(x,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(t,T),\\
m(x,t)=\bar{m}(x)&\text{for }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\end{cases}

(4)

where
\bar{m}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is the initial density at time
t
.

The flow associated with the system (
4
), denoted by
\Phi
, is the solution of

\begin{cases}\partial_{s}\Phi(x,s,t)=\alpha(\Phi(x,s,t),s),&s\in(t,T),\\
\Phi(x,t,t)=x.\end{cases}

(5)

for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
\Phi(x,s,t)
is the position at time
s
of a particle that has started at time
t
from the position
x
, under the velocity field
\alpha
.

Assumption 2.1
.

We assume that the vector field
\alpha
belongs to

\mathcal{A}:=\left\{\alpha\in L^{2}\left([0,T],W^{3,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})\right):\alpha(\cdot,s)\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}}\text{ for a.e. }\;s\in[0,T]\right\},

where, for a fixed constant
M>0
,

\tilde{\mathcal{A}}:=\left\{a\in W^{3,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n}):\max\left\{\|a\|_{W^{3,\infty}},\|a\|_{H^{1}}\right\}\leq M\right\}.

Remark 2.2
.

In the sequel we will use the notation
\Phi_{t,s}:=\Phi(\cdot,s,t):\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
for the flow and
\Psi_{t,s}:=\Phi_{t,s}^{-1}
for its inverse. We refer to Appendix
A
(Proposition
A1
) for details on the flow and its inverse, in particular for estimates on them and their derivatives, which work due to the spatial regularity Assumption
2.1
.
Moreover, see for example
[
2
]
, the unique explicit solution of (
4
), denoted by
m(\cdot,\cdot;\alpha,\bar{m},t)
, is the pushforward of
\bar{m}
by the flow map
\Phi_{t,s}
, i.e.,

m(x,s;\alpha,\bar{m},t):=\frac{\bar{m}(\Psi_{t,s}(x))}{\det D\Phi_{t,s}(\Psi_{t,s}(x))}\qquad\forall(x,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[t,T],

(6)

where
D
stays for the spatial Jacobian matrix. The solution of (
4
) is understood in the distributional sense, even if we will see that under our assumptions it will be more regular.

2.1
Regularity of the solutions

In this subsection we establish regularity and stability estimates for the solutions of (
4
), under the Assumption
2.1
.

Proposition 2.3
.

There exists a constant
C=C(M,T,n)
such that, for every
t\in[0,T)
, every
\bar{m}\in W^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and every
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
, one has

\|m(\cdot,s;\alpha,\bar{m},t)\|_{W^{2,\infty}}\leq C\|\bar{m}\|_{W^{2,\infty}}\qquad\forall s\in[t,T].

An analogous estimate also holds for the
H^{2}
norm, starting from an initial datum in
H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Proof.

Invoking the notation introduced in Proposition
A1
, setting
m(\cdot,s):=m(\cdot,s;\alpha,\bar{m},t)
we easily get
\|m(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\|\bar{m}\|_{L^{\infty}}\,\|J_{t,s}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq e^{nMT}\|\bar{m}\|_{L^{\infty}}
, by
(A5)
.
Furthermore, recalling in particular
(A2)
and
(A6)
,

\displaystyle\|\nabla m(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla\bar{m}\circ\Psi_{t,s}\|_{L^{\infty}}\,\|D\Psi_{t,s}\|_{L^{\infty}}\,\|J_{t,s}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\bar{m}\circ\Psi_{t,s}\|_{L^{\infty}}\,\|\nabla J_{t,s}\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq(e^{MT}+1)\,\tilde{C}_{J}\,\|\bar{m}\|_{W^{1,\infty}},

and proceeding analogously, using the uniform boundedness of
\|D^{2}\Psi_{t,s}\|_{L^{\infty}}
together with
(A7)
, we obtain
\|D^{2}m(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C_{H}\,\|\bar{m}\|_{W^{2,\infty}}
,
where
C_{H}=C(M,T,n)
. Combining the previous estimates yields the desired bound in
W^{2,\infty}
.

For the
H^{2}
-estimate, for example,

\displaystyle\|\nabla m(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla\bar{m}\circ\Psi_{t,s}\|_{L^{2}}\,\|D\Psi_{t,s}\|_{L^{\infty}}\,\|J_{t,s}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\bar{m}\circ\Psi_{t,s}\|_{L^{2}}\,\|\nabla J_{t,s}\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq(e^{MT}+1)\,\tilde{C}_{J}\,\|\bar{m}\|_{H^{1}}.

by an easy change of variables; the estimates for
\|m(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{2}}
and
\|D^{2}m(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{2}}
follows in the same spirit.
∎

Proposition 2.4
.

The following estimates hold:

i)

for all
t\in[0,T),\;\bar{m}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
and
s_{1},s_{2}\in[t,T]
we have that

\|m(\cdot,s_{1};\alpha,\bar{m},t)-m(\cdot,s_{2};\alpha,\bar{m},t)\|_{H^{1}}\leq C(M,n)\left[\sup_{\tau\in(s_{1},s_{2})}\|m(\cdot,\tau;\alpha,\bar{m},t)\|_{H^{2}}\right]|s_{1}-s_{2}|;

ii)

for all
t_{1},t_{2}\in[0,T),\;m_{1},m_{2}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
and
s\in[\max\{t_{1},t_{2}\},T]
we have that

\|m(\cdot,s;\alpha,m_{1},t_{1})-m(\cdot,s;\alpha,m_{2},t_{2})\|_{L^{2}}\leq\tilde{L}(\|m_{1}-m_{2}\|_{L^{2}}+|t_{1}-t_{2}|),

where
\tilde{L}
depends only on
M,n,T
and on the
H^{2}
norm of the evolution associated with
(m_{1},t_{1},\alpha)
. Moreover, the same estimate holds with the
L^{2}
norm replaced by the
H^{1}
norm; see
[
5
, Proposition 1]
.

Proof.

i)
Since
m:=m(\cdot,\cdot;\alpha,\bar{m},t)
solves the continuity equation, we have

\partial_{s}m(\cdot,s)=-\diver(\alpha(\cdot,s)m(\cdot,s))
for a.e.
s\in[t,T]
; moreover, using Proposition
2.3
and the uniform bounds on
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
, it follows that
\partial_{s}m\in L^{1}([t,T];H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
, and hence
m\in W^{1,1}([t,T];H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\|m(\cdot,s_{2})-m(\cdot,s_{1})\|_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{s_{1}}^{s_{2}}\|\diver(\alpha(\cdot,\tau)m(\cdot,\tau))\|_{H^{1}}\,d\tau

\displaystyle\leq\int_{s_{1}}^{s_{2}}\Bigl(\|\alpha(\cdot,\tau)\cdot\nabla m(\cdot,\tau)\|_{L^{2}}+\|m(\cdot,\tau)\,\diver\alpha(\cdot,\tau)\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\hskip 32.50005pt+\|\nabla(\alpha(\cdot,\tau)\cdot\nabla m(\cdot,\tau))\|_{L^{2}}+\|\nabla(m(\cdot,\tau)\,\diver\alpha(\cdot,\tau))\|_{L^{2}}\Bigr)\,d\tau

\displaystyle\leq C(M,n)\int_{s_{1}}^{s_{2}}\|m(\cdot,\tau)\|_{H^{2}}\,d\tau,

and the conclusion follows.

ii)
Assume
t_{1}\leq t_{2}
. Define
\bar{m}:=m(\cdot,t_{2};\alpha,m_{1},t_{1})
. Then, for every
s\in[\max\{t_{1},t_{2}\},T]
,

\|m(\cdot,s;\alpha,m_{1},t_{1})-m(\cdot,s;\alpha,m_{2},t_{2})\|_{L^{2}}=\|m(\cdot,s;\alpha,\bar{m},t_{2})-m(\cdot,s;\alpha,m_{2},t_{2})\|_{L^{2}}=:\|u(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{2}}.

Note that both densities satisfy (
4
) with the same vector field; then it follows from (
6
), using
(A5)
, that (using
\Psi_{t_{2},s}
as a change of variables)

\displaystyle\|u(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left[\frac{\bar{m}(\Psi_{t_{2},s}(x))-m_{2}(\Psi_{t_{2},s}(x))}{\det D\Phi_{t_{2},s}(\Psi_{t_{2},s}(x))}\right]^{2}\;dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{(\bar{m}(y)-m_{2}(y))^{2}}{\det D\Phi_{t_{2},s}(y)}\;dy\leq e^{nMT}\|\bar{m}-m_{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Therefore, using item i), we obtain

\displaystyle\|u(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq e^{\frac{nMT}{2}}\left[\|\bar{m}-m_{1}\|_{L^{2}}+\|m_{1}-m_{2}\|_{L^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq e^{\frac{nMT}{2}}\left[C(M,n)\left(\sup_{\tau\in(t_{1},t_{2})}\|m(\cdot,\tau;\alpha,m_{1},t_{1})\|_{H^{2}}\right)|t_{1}-t_{2}|+\|m_{1}-m_{2}\|_{L^{2}}\right]

∎

Proposition 2.5
.

There exists a constant
C=C(M,T,n)
such that for every
m:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[t,T]\to\mathbb{R}
a solution of (
4
) with initial datum
\bar{m}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and velocity field
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
, then

\|m(\cdot,s)\|_{H^{1}}\leq e^{C(s-t)}\|\bar{m}\|_{H^{1}}\qquad\forall s\in[t,T],

Proof.

With estimates similar to those in the proof of Proposition
2.4
, item ii), one shows that

\displaystyle\|m(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{2}}\leq e^{\frac{nM}{2}(s-t)}\|\bar{m}\|_{L^{2}}
. Moreover, using (
6
) and a direct computation,

\left\|\nabla m(\cdot,s)\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq\left\|\frac{\nabla\bar{m}\circ\Psi_{t,s}}{\det D\Phi_{t,s}\circ\Psi_{t,s}}\right\|_{L^{2}}\|D\Psi_{t,s}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\left\|\bar{m}\circ\Psi_{t,s}\right\|_{L^{2}}\left\|\nabla\left(\det D\Phi_{t,s}\circ\Psi_{t,s}\right)^{-1}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}.

Now, the
L^{\infty}
-norm terms are uniformly bounded by Proposition
A1
(in particular
(A2)
and
(A6)
), while the
L^{2}
-norm terms, by the usual change of variables, are estimated respectively by terms of the form

e^{C_{1}(s-t)}\|\nabla\bar{m}\|_{L^{2}}
and
e^{C_{2}(s-t)}\|\bar{m}\|_{L^{2}}
with uniform constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
. Summing the contributions, and possibly adjusting the constants, yields the claim.
∎

2.2
An invariant and compact set for the evolutions

Following an argument similar to the one in
[
5
]
, we construct a compact and invariant set
X\subseteq H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times[0,T]
for the evolution of the masses.

Assumption 2.6
.

Let
\tilde{\Omega}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open and bounded and let
C_{U}>0
. We assume that the masses at time
t=0
are non-negative densities that belong to

X(0):=\left\{\bar{m}\in W^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):\max\{\|\bar{m}\|_{W^{2,\infty}},\|\bar{m}\|_{H^{1}}\}\leq C_{U},\;\supp(\bar{m})\subseteq\tilde{\Omega}\right\}.

It is clear that for every
\bar{m}\in X(0)
and
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
one has

\supp\big(m(\cdot,t;\alpha,\bar{m},0)\big)\subseteq\tilde{\Omega}(t)
for all
t\in(0,T]
, where

\tilde{\Omega}(t):=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\dist(x,\tilde{\Omega})\leq Mt\right\}.

Moreover, by Proposition
2.3
, for every
\bar{m}\in X(0)
and
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
,

\|m(\cdot,t;\alpha,\bar{m},0)\|_{W^{2,\infty}}\leq C\|\bar{m}\|_{W^{2,\infty}}\leq CC_{U}=:\tilde{C}_{U}\qquad\forall t\in[0,T].

We then define, for each
t\in(0,T]
, the set

X(t):=\left\{\bar{m}\in W^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):\supp(\bar{m})\subseteq\tilde{\Omega}(t),\|m(\cdot,s;\alpha,\bar{m},t)\|_{W^{2,\infty}}\leq\tilde{C}_{U}\;\forall s\in[t,T],\;\forall\alpha\in\mathcal{A}\right\},

and the set

X:=\bigcup_{t\in[0,T]}\big[X(t)\times\{t\}\big]\subset H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times[0,T]\subset H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times[0,T],

since the elements of
X(t)
belong to
H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, being in
W^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and having compact support.

Remark 2.7
.

From Proposition
2.4
, item
i)
, there exists
L_{T}>0
, depending only on
M,T,n,C_{U}
, such that for all
t\in[0,T)
,
\bar{m}\in X(t)
, and
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
,

\|m(\cdot,s_{1};\alpha,\bar{m},t)-m(\cdot,s_{2};\alpha,\bar{m},t)\|_{H^{1}}\leq L_{T}|s_{1}-s_{2}|\qquad\forall s_{1},s_{2}\in[t,T].

Moreover, the constant
\tilde{L}
cited in the item
ii)
for the
L^{2}
(or
H^{1}
) stability w.r.t. initial data depends only on the same constants, provided
m_{1}\in X(t_{1})
and
m_{2}\in X(t_{2})
.

Proposition 2.8
.

The set
X
is invariant for the continuity evolution, in the sense that for every
(\bar{m},t)\in X
and
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
one has

(m(\cdot,s;\alpha,\bar{m},t),s)\in X
for all
s\in[t,T]
. Moreover,
X
is compact in the product topology of
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times[0,T]
.

Proof.

We prove compactness, since the invariance follows from the construction of
X
.

Precompactness follows since the projection of
X
onto the first component (which is clearly
X(T)
) is bounded in
H^{2}(B(0,R))
for some
R>0
, and therefore relatively compact in

H^{1}(B(0,R))
. The extension by zero yields precompactness in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, while the time component ranges in
[0,T]
.

Closedness is obtained by taking
(m_{n},t_{n})\to(\bar{m},\bar{t})
in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times[0,T]
and using the uniform
W^{2,\infty}
bounds for
\{m_{n}\}
. These yield uniform
L^{\infty}

bounds and equi-Lipschitz continuity, hence (by Ascoli–Arzelà) a subsequence converges uniformly to some
\tilde{m}
. The
H^{1}
convergence forces
\tilde{m}=\bar{m}
, so
m_{n}\to\bar{m}

uniformly and
\supp(\bar{m})\subseteq\tilde{\Omega}(\bar{t})
.

Next, fix
s>\bar{t}
and
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
. Using the
H^{1}
version of Proposition
2.4
, item ii), one shows that

m(\cdot,s;\alpha,m_{n},t_{n})\to m(\cdot,s;\alpha,\bar{m},\bar{t})\quad\text{in }H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

On the other hand,
\{m(\cdot,s;\alpha,m_{n},t_{n})\}_{n}
is uniformly bounded in
W^{2,\infty}
, hence (up to a subsequence) converges weak-
\ast
in
W^{2,\infty}
. The strong
H^{1}

convergence identifies the weak-
\ast
limit with
m(\cdot,s;\alpha,\bar{m},\bar{t})
, and weak-
\ast
lower semicontinuity yields for all
s>\bar{t},\;\alpha\in\mathcal{A}

\|m(\cdot,s;\alpha,\bar{m},\bar{t})\|_{W^{2,\infty}}\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\|m(\cdot,s;\alpha,m_{n},t_{n})\|_{W^{2,\infty}}\leq\tilde{C}_{U}.

Therefore
(\bar{m},\bar{t})\in X
, so
X
is closed and hence compact.
∎

Remark 2.9
.

We recall that (see
[
17
, Subsection 1.3.1]
) the Sobolev space
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
can be endowed with the equivalent norm

\|w\|_{H^{s}_{\mathcal{F}}}:=\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(1+|\xi|^{2})^{s}|\hat{w}(\xi)|^{2}\;d\xi\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\qquad\forall w\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

where
\hat{w}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
denotes the Fourier transform of
w
.

Now, for any
w\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, using Plancherel’s formula,

\displaystyle\|w\|_{H^{1}_{\mathcal{F}}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(1+|\xi|^{2})|\hat{w}(\xi)|^{2}\;d\xi\leq\|(1+|\cdot|^{2})\hat{w}\|_{L^{2}}\,\|\hat{w}\|_{L^{2}}=\|w\|_{H^{2}_{\mathcal{F}}}\|w\|_{L^{2}}.

Therefore there exists fixed constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that for any
m_{1},m_{2}\in X(T)
,

\displaystyle\|m_{1}-m_{2}\|_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\|m_{1}-m_{2}\|_{H^{1}_{\mathcal{F}}}\leq C_{1}\sqrt{\|m_{1}-m_{2}\|_{H^{2}_{\mathcal{F}}}}\sqrt{\|m_{1}-m_{2}\|_{L^{2}}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\sqrt{2C_{2}\tilde{C}_{U}}\sqrt{\|m_{1}-m_{2}\|_{L^{2}}}=:C_{X}\sqrt{\|m_{1}-m_{2}\|_{L^{2}}}.

(7)

Note that (
7
) also implies that the
L^{2}
and
H^{1}
topologies are equivalent on
X(T)
.

3
The state-constrained optimal control problem

In this section we present the state-constrained optimal control problem in detail and introduce the associated value function.
After some comments about the Dynamic Programming Principle we prove, under our hypotheses, the Lipschitz continuity of the value function in Subsection
3.1
. In the following, the notation is the same as introduced in the previous sections.

We consider the running cost
\ell:X(T)\times\tilde{\mathcal{A}}\to[0,+\infty)
and the final cost
\psi:X(T)\to[0,+\infty)
which induce the Bolza-type cost functional
J:X\times\mathcal{A}\to[0,+\infty)

J(\bar{m},t,\alpha):=\int_{t}^{T}\ell(m(\cdot,s;\alpha,\bar{m},t),\alpha(\cdot,s))\;ds+\psi(m(\cdot,T;\alpha,\bar{m},t)).

Assumption 3.1
.

We assume that
\ell
and
\psi
are bounded (by some constant
N_{\ell},N_{\psi}>0
, respectively), continuous and that there exists
L_{\ell},L_{\psi}>0
such that

i)

\displaystyle|\ell(m_{1},a)-\ell(m_{2},a)|\leq L_{\ell}\|m_{1}-m_{2}\|_{L^{2}}\qquad\forall m_{1},m_{2}\in X(T),\;\forall a\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}},

ii)

\displaystyle|\psi(m_{1})-\psi(m_{2})|\leq L_{\psi}\|m_{1}-m_{2}\|_{L^{2}}\qquad\forall m_{1},m_{2}\in X(T).

Let
\Omega\subset\tilde{\Omega}
open and
\delta>0
be fixed. We are interested in minimizing the cost
J
under a state constraint that enforces the mass, weighted by a fixed positive function
p:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, to be somehow concentrated in
\Omega
at every time, namely

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega}p(x)m(x)\;dx\leq\delta.

(8)

Assumption 3.2
.

For explicit computations, we assume
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is convex with boundary regular enough for the divergence theorem, and set
p(x):=\dist(x,\Omega)
; thus the constraint is
\langle p,m\rangle:=\langle p,m\rangle_{L^{2}(\tilde{\Omega}(T))}\leq\delta
. The same analysis applies to more general domains and weight functions (see Remark
3.5
).

Notation
.

For
t\in[0,T]
, set

X_{c}(t):=\{m\in X(t):\langle p,m\rangle<\delta\}
and

\bar{X}_{c}(t):=\{m\in X(t):\langle p,m\rangle\leq\delta\}
,
and define

X_{c}:=\bigcup_{t\in[0,T]}X_{c}(t)\times\{t\},\qquad\bar{X}_{c}:=\bigcup_{t\in[0,T]}\bar{X}_{c}(t)\times\{t\}.

Note that
\displaystyle\bar{X}_{c}=X\cap\left(\left\{m\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):\langle p,m\rangle\leq\delta\right\}\times[0,T]\right)
; then by Proposition
2.8
it follows that
\bar{X}_{c}
is compact in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times[0,T]
.

We are interested in minimizing the cost only over those controls in
\mathcal{A}
that ensure the state constraint is never violated. Thus, for each
(\bar{m},t)\in\bar{X}_{c}
we define

\mathcal{A}(\bar{m},t):=\left\{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}:(m(\cdot,s;\alpha,\bar{m},t),s)\in\bar{X}_{c}\;\;\forall s\in[t,T]\right\},

and note that at least the null constant control
\alpha\equiv 0
always belongs to
\mathcal{A}(\bar{m},t)
.

The value function of the constrained problem is then

V:\bar{X}_{c}\to[0,+\infty)\qquad\qquad(\bar{m},t)\mapsto\inf_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}(\bar{m},t)}J(\bar{m},t,\alpha).

Relying on the semigroup property of the constrained dynamics and on the stability of the admissible class under concatenation of controls, we can follow the same argument as in the finite-dimensional Bolza setting. Therefore, see for example
[
6
, Chapter III]
, the value function
V
satisfies the classical Dynamical Programming Principle stated below.

Theorem 3.3

(DPP)
.

Given
(\bar{m},t)\in\bar{X}_{c}
, then for all
\tau\in[t,T]

V(\bar{m},t)=\inf_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}(\bar{m},t)}\left[\int_{t}^{\tau}\ell(m(\cdot,s;\alpha,\bar{m},t),\alpha(\cdot,s))\;ds+V(m(\cdot,\tau;\alpha,\bar{m},t),\tau)\right].

(9)

3.1
Value function regularity

In this subsection we prove that, under Assumptions
3.1
and
3.2
, the value function is Lipschitz continuous on
\bar{X}_{c}
.
Following Soner’s state-constraint strategy
[
23
]
, we combine DPP (
9
) and a trajectories correction by a suitable pushback control near the boundary to obtain Lipschitz estimates for
V
.

In order to construct that suitable pushback control, we first need the following inequality. Fixed
x_{\Omega}\in\Omega
, we observe that

\nabla\dist(x,\Omega)\cdot(x_{\Omega}-x)\leq-\dist(x_{\Omega},\partial\Omega)=:-r_{0}<0\qquad\text{for a.e. }x\in\Omega^{c}.

(10)

First of all note that
\nabla\dist(\cdot,\Omega)
exists a.e. since
x\mapsto\dist(x,\Omega)
is 1-Lipschitz; furthermore, let
x\in\Omega^{c}
be a differentiability point and let
y\in\partial\Omega
be its (unique) projection onto
\Omega
. By convexity there exists a supporting hyperplane with outward unit normal
n(y)
such that

n(y)\cdot(z-y)\leq 0\qquad\forall z\in\Omega.

Let
\delta\in(0,r_{0})
. Using
z_{\delta}:=x_{\Omega}+(r_{0}-\delta)n(y)\in\Omega
above we obtain

0\geq n(y)\cdot(z_{\delta}-y)=n(y)\cdot(x_{\Omega}-y)+(r_{0}-\delta)\;\;\forall\delta\in(0,r_{0})\Rightarrow n(y)\cdot(x_{\Omega}-y)\leq-r_{0}.

Since
x=y+\dist(x,\Omega)n(y)
and
n(y)=\nabla\dist(x,\Omega)
, we obtain (
10
); indeed,

\nabla\dist(x,\Omega)\cdot(x_{\Omega}-x)=n(y)\cdot(x_{\Omega}-y)-\dist(x,\Omega)\leq-r_{0}.

Now fix
\varepsilon\ll 1
and let
\phi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be a cutoff function such that

\phi(x)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }x\in\overline{\tilde{\Omega}(T)},\\
0&\text{if }x\in\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\operatorname{dist}(x,\tilde{\Omega}(T))\geq\varepsilon\},\end{cases}

so that
x\mapsto\phi(x)(x_{\Omega}-x)\in W^{3,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. After rescaling we define the constant (in time)
pushback control

a_{p}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}\qquad x\mapsto a_{p}(x):=\bar{M}\phi(x)(x_{\Omega}-x),

(11)

where
\bar{M}\ll 1
is such that
a_{p}\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}}
; by construction we have, by (
10
), (recall that
p(x)=\dist(x,\Omega)
)

\nabla p(x)\cdot a_{p}(x)\leq-\bar{M}r_{0}<0\qquad\text{for a.e. }x\in\tilde{\Omega}(T)\setminus\Omega.

(12)

Notation
.

Let
\eta:L^{2}(\tilde{\Omega}(T))\supset X(T)\to\mathbb{R}
be defined by
m\mapsto\eta(m):=\displaystyle\delta-\langle p,m\rangle
. Clearly for any
m\in X(T)
we have
m\in\bar{X}_{c}(T)
if and only if
\eta(m)\geq 0
.

Lemma 3.4
.

There exist
t^{*},C^{*}>0
such that for all
(m_{0},t_{0})\in\bar{X}_{c}
and
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
, there exists
\bar{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A}(m_{0},t_{0})
for which the following holds:

\left|J_{h}(m_{0},t_{0},\bar{\alpha})-J_{h}(m_{0},t_{0},{\alpha})\right|\leq C^{*}\max\left\{0,\sup_{t_{0}\leq s\leq t_{0}+t^{*}}\big[-\eta(m(\cdot,s;\alpha,m_{0},t_{0}))\big]\right\}=:C^{*}\varepsilon

(13)

for all
h\in(0,t^{*}]
, where
\displaystyle J_{h}(m_{0},t_{0},\alpha):=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+h}\ell(m(\cdot,t;\alpha,m_{0},t_{0}),\alpha(\cdot,t))\;dt
. In (
13
), we extend
\alpha
to
[t_{0},+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, if necessary, by setting
\alpha(\cdot,t)\equiv 0
for all
t>T
.

Proof.

Let
t^{*},k>0
be fixed (we will specify them later), and let
(m_{0},t_{0})\in\bar{X}_{c}
,
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
; set
m(\cdot,\cdot):=m(\cdot,\cdot;\alpha,m_{0},t_{0})
. We define
\displaystyle t_{e}:=\inf\{t\in[t_{0},T]:\eta(m(\cdot,t))<0\}-t_{0}
, which represents the amount of time before the constraint is possibly violated under the control
\alpha
(with the standard assumption
\inf\emptyset=+\infty
). Note that both
t_{e}
and
\varepsilon
depend on the triple
(m_{0},t_{0},\alpha)
.

We now define a control
\bar{\alpha}
on the interval
[t_{0},t_{0}+t^{*}]
as follows. Set

t_{1}:=\min\{t_{e},t^{*}\},\qquad t_{2}:=\min\{t^{*},\,t_{e}+k\varepsilon\},

and define

\bar{\alpha}(\cdot,t):=\chi_{[t_{0},t_{0}+t_{1})}(t)\alpha(\cdot,t)+\chi_{[t_{0}+t_{1},\,t_{0}+t_{2}]}(t)a_{p}+\chi_{(t_{0}+t_{2},\,t_{0}+t^{*}]}(t)\alpha(\cdot,t-k\varepsilon).

where
a_{p}\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}}
is the pushback control (
11
); we denote
m(\cdot,\cdot;\bar{\alpha},m_{0},t_{0})
by
\bar{m}(\cdot,\cdot)
. We claim that
\eta(\bar{m}(\cdot,t))\geq 0
for all
t\in[t_{0},t_{0}+t^{*}]
.

If
t_{e}>t^{*}
, then
\bar{\alpha}=\alpha
on
[t_{0},t_{0}+t^{*}]
and the claim is immediate.

We now treat the case
t_{e}\leq t^{*}\leq t_{e}+k\varepsilon
. Clearly
\eta(\bar{m}(\cdot,t))\geq 0
for every
t\in[t_{0},t_{0}+t_{e}]
.

For almost every
t\in(t_{0}+t_{e},t_{0}+t^{*})
we compute, by (
4
),the divergence theorem and the definition of
\eta
,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\eta(\bar{m}(\cdot,t))

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}p(x)\partial_{t}\bar{m}(x,t)\;dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}p(x)\diver(\bar{\alpha}(x,t)\bar{m}(x,t))\;dx

(14)

\displaystyle=\int_{\tilde{\Omega}(T)\setminus\Omega}p(x)\diver(\bar{\alpha}(x,t)\bar{m}(x,t))\;dx=-\int_{\tilde{\Omega}(T)\setminus\Omega}\big(\nabla p(x)\cdot\bar{\alpha}(x,t)\big)\bar{m}(x,t)\;dx,

Using (
12
), we obtain
\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\eta(\bar{m}(\cdot,t))\geq\bar{M}r_{0}\int_{\tilde{\Omega}(T)\setminus\Omega}\bar{m}(x,t)\;dx
.

Now, it follows straightforwardly from Proposition
2.4
(and in particular from Remark
2.7
) that
t\mapsto\eta(\bar{m}(\cdot,t))
is uniformly Lipschitz with respect to
(m_{0},t_{0},\alpha)
; we denote its Lipschitz constant by
L_{\eta}
.

Set
\bar{t}:=\delta/(2L_{\eta})
. Then, for every
t\in(t_{0}+t_{e},t_{0}+\bar{t})
, we have

\eta(\bar{m}(\cdot,t))\leq\eta(\bar{m}(\cdot,t_{0}+t_{e}))+L_{\eta}(t-t_{0}-t_{e})\leq\frac{\delta}{2}\Rightarrow\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}p(x)\bar{m}(x,t)\;dx\geq\frac{\delta}{2}.

(15)

Therefore, for every
t\in(t_{0}+t_{e},t_{0}+\bar{t})
, we get

\displaystyle\int_{\tilde{\Omega}(T)\setminus\Omega}\bar{m}(x,t)\;dx\geq\frac{\delta}{2\|p\|_{L^{\infty}(\tilde{\Omega}(T))}}
.

Therefore, requiring (see below) that
t^{*}\leq\bar{t}
, we have

\frac{d}{dt}\eta(\bar{m}(\cdot,t))\geq\frac{\delta\bar{M}r_{0}}{2\|p\|_{L^{\infty}(\tilde{\Omega}(T))}}=:C_{1}>0\qquad\text{for a.e. }t\in(t_{0}+t_{e},t_{0}+t^{*}),

(16)

which, together with
\eta(\bar{m}(\cdot,t_{0}+t_{e}))=0
, guarantees that

\eta(\bar{m}(\cdot,t))\geq 0
for every
t\in[t_{0},t_{0}+t^{*}]
also in this case.

We now focus on the case
t_{e}+k\varepsilon<t^{*}
. Clearly,
\eta(\bar{m}(\cdot,t))\geq 0
for all
t\in[t_{0},t_{0}+t_{e}+k\varepsilon]
. Let
t\in(t_{0}+t_{e}+k\varepsilon,t_{0}+t^{*}]
. Then, for every
s\in(t_{0}+t_{e}+k\varepsilon,t]
, by adding and subtracting to the first line of (
14
) the term

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}p(x)\diver(\bar{\alpha}(x,s)m(x,s-k\varepsilon))\,dx
and using Hölder’s inequality together with the uniform bounds on
\mathcal{A}
we obtain, for a suitable
M^{\prime}>0
,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}\eta(\bar{m}(\cdot,s))\geq

\displaystyle-M^{\prime}\|p\|_{L^{\infty}(\tilde{\Omega}(T))}\|\bar{m}(\cdot,s)-m(\cdot,s-k\varepsilon)\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}p(x)\diver(\alpha(x,s-k\varepsilon)m(x,s-k\varepsilon))\;dx,

(17)

where in the last line we used
\bar{\alpha}(\cdot,s)=\alpha(\cdot,s-k\varepsilon)
for
s\in(t_{0}+t_{e}+k\varepsilon,t]
. Furthermore we observe that

u(\cdot,s):=\bar{m}(\cdot,s)-m(\cdot,s-k\varepsilon)
solves the continuity equation. Hence, using first Proposition
2.5
and then Remark
2.7
(and noting that
\bar{m}(\cdot,t_{0}+t_{e})=m(\cdot,t_{0}+t_{e})
), we get

\displaystyle\|\bar{m}(\cdot,s)-m(\cdot,s-k\varepsilon)\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq e^{C(s-t_{0}-t_{e}-k\varepsilon)}L_{T}k\varepsilon\qquad\forall s\in(t_{0}+t_{e}+k\varepsilon,t].

(18)

Moreover, recalling that
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}p(x)m(x,t_{0}+t_{e})\;dx=\delta
and the definition of
\varepsilon
in (
13
),

\displaystyle\int_{t_{0}+t_{e}+k\varepsilon}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}p(x)

\displaystyle\diver(\alpha(x,s-k\varepsilon)m(x,s-k\varepsilon))\;dx\;ds=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{t_{0}+t_{e}+k\varepsilon}^{t}p(x)\partial_{t}m(x,s-k\varepsilon)\;ds\;dx

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}p(x)\left[m(x,t-k\varepsilon)-m(x,t_{0}+t_{e})\right]\;dx=\eta(m(\cdot,t-k\varepsilon))

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{t_{0}+t_{e}\leq s\leq t_{0}+t^{*}-k\varepsilon}\left[\eta(m(\cdot,s))\right]\geq-\sup_{t_{0}\leq s\leq t_{0}+t^{*}}\left[-\eta(m(\cdot,s))\right]=-\varepsilon.

Using this together with (
16
), (
17
), and (
18
), it follows (
C_{2}:=M^{\prime}\|p\|_{L^{\infty}(\tilde{\Omega}(T))}L_{T}/C
) that

\displaystyle\eta(\bar{m}(\cdot,t))

\displaystyle=\eta(\bar{m}(\cdot,t_{0}+t_{e})+\int_{t_{0}+t_{e}}^{t_{0}+t_{e}+k\varepsilon}\frac{d}{ds}\eta(\bar{m}(\cdot,s))\;ds+\int_{t_{0}+t_{e}+k\varepsilon}^{t}\frac{d}{ds}\eta(\bar{m}(\cdot,s))\;ds

\displaystyle\geq 0+C_{1}k\varepsilon-C_{2}k\varepsilon\int_{t_{0}+t_{e}+k\varepsilon}^{t}Ce^{C(s-t_{0}-t_{e}-k\varepsilon)}\;ds-\varepsilon\geq C_{1}k\varepsilon-C_{2}k\varepsilon\left[e^{Ct^{*}}-1\right]-\varepsilon.

Therefore, taking

t^{*}:=\min\left\{\frac{\ln\left(\frac{C_{1}}{2C_{2}}+1\right)}{C},\bar{t}\right\}\qquad k:=\frac{2}{C_{1}},

a direct computation shows that for every
t\in(t_{0}+t_{e}+k\varepsilon,t_{0}+t^{*}]
we have

\eta(\bar{m}(\cdot,t))\geq 0
, so this last case is also settled.

Therefore, the claim is proved. By iterating this construction for successive time intervals of length
t^{*}
(up to the final time horizon
T
), we obtain
\bar{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A}(m_{0},t_{0})
.

Let
h\in(0,t^{*}]
. If
h\in(0,t_{e}]
, then (
13
) is immediate; if instead
h\in(t_{e},t_{e}+k\varepsilon)
, then

\displaystyle|J_{h}(m_{0},\bar{\alpha})-J_{h}(m_{0},\alpha)|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{t_{0}+t_{e}}^{t_{0}+h}\big|\ell(\bar{m}(\cdot,t),\bar{\alpha}(\cdot,t))-\ell({m}(\cdot,t),\alpha(\cdot,t))\big|\;dt\leq 2N_{\ell}k\varepsilon.

and

\displaystyle\left|J_{h}(m_{0},\bar{\alpha})-J_{h}(m_{0},\alpha)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{t_{0}+t_{e}}^{t_{0}+t_{e}+k\varepsilon}\ell(\bar{m}(t),\bar{\alpha}(t))\;dt+\int_{t_{0}+t_{e}+k\varepsilon}^{t_{0}+h}\ell(\bar{m}(t),\bar{\alpha}(t))\;dt\right.

\displaystyle\pm\int_{t_{0}+t_{e}+k\varepsilon}^{t_{0}+h}\ell(m(t-k\varepsilon),\underbrace{\alpha(t-k\varepsilon)}_{=\bar{\alpha}(t)})\;dt-\int_{t_{0}+t_{e}}^{t_{0}+h}\ell(m(t),\alpha(t))\;dt\Bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{t_{0}+t_{e}}^{t_{0}+t_{e}+k\varepsilon}|\ell(\bar{m}(t),\bar{\alpha}(t))|\;dt+L_{\ell}\int_{t_{0}+t_{e}+k\varepsilon}^{t_{0}+h}\|\bar{m}(t)-m(t-k\varepsilon)\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\;dt

\displaystyle+\left|\int_{t_{0}+t_{e}}^{t_{0}+h-k\varepsilon}\ell(m(t),\alpha(t))\;dt-\int_{t_{0}+t_{e}}^{t_{0}+h}\ell(m(t),\alpha(t))\;dt\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2N_{\ell}k\varepsilon+L_{\ell}L_{T}k\varepsilon\int_{t_{0}+t_{e}+k\varepsilon}^{t_{0}+h}e^{C(t-t_{0}-t_{e}-k\varepsilon)}\;dt\leq C^{*}\varepsilon,

for a suitable uniform constant
C^{*}>0
, where (
18
) was used in the last line.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

Theorem 3.6

\omega_{t}(r):=\sup\left\{|V(m_{1},t)-V(m_{2},t)|:\ (m_{j},t)\in\bar{X}_{c}\ \text{for }j=1,2\ \text{and}\ \|m_{1}-m_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq r\right\}.

\beta

, i.e.,

V(m_{2},t)\geq J_{h}(m_{2},t,\alpha)+V(m(\cdot,t+h;\alpha,m_{2},t),t+h)-\beta.

(19)

Moreover, Assumption
3.1
implies that

Hence, using this and (

\displaystyle V(m_{1},t)-V(m_{2},t)

\displaystyle\leq J_{h}(m_{1},t,\bar{\alpha})+V(m(\cdot,t+h;\bar{\alpha},m_{1},t),t+h)-V(m_{2},t)

(20)

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}r+V(m(\cdot,t+h;\bar{\alpha},m_{1},t),t+h)-V(m(\cdot,t+h;\alpha,m_{2},t),t+h)+\beta.

By construction
\bar{\alpha}
is as
\alpha
up to the first possible exit time for

(see the proof of Lemma

. Then using uniform bounds (see Remark

) and (

and

and since
\displaystyle\varepsilon\leq\|p\|_{L^{2}(\tilde{\Omega}(T))}\tilde{L}\,r
, we get
\displaystyle\|m(\cdot,s;\bar{\alpha},m_{1},t)-m(\cdot,s;\alpha,m_{1},t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C_{4}r
(for some

) for all

. In particular for

, by adding and subtracting

here below, we get

\displaystyle\|m(\cdot,t+h;\bar{\alpha},m_{1},t)-m(\cdot,t+h;\alpha,m_{2},t)\|_{L^{2}}\leq C_{4}r+\tilde{L}r=:\tilde{C}r.

Then, (

), by the definition of
\omega_{t+h}
, implies that

By arbitrariness of
\beta
, exchanging the roles of

and

and taking the supremum, we obtain

\omega_{t}(r)\leq C_{2}r+\omega_{t+h}(\tilde{C}r)\qquad\forall t\in[0,T],\ h\in[0,\min\{t^{*},T-t\}],\ r>0.

(21)

Set
t_{j}:=T-jt^{*}
,
\omega^{j}:=\omega_{t_{j}}
and
\bar{j}:=\max\{j:\,t_{j}\geq 0\}
. From (

for

.

Therefore there exists

such that

for all

|V(m_{1},t)-V(m_{2},t)|\leq L_{\mathrm{mass}}\|m_{1}-m_{2}\|_{L^{2}}\qquad\forall(m_{1},t),(m_{2},t)\in\bar{X}_{c}.

On the other hand, let

-optimal for

in the DPP with

\displaystyle V(m_{0},t)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{t}^{t+h}\ell(m(\cdot,s;\alpha,m_{0},t),\alpha(\cdot,s))\,ds+V(m(\cdot,t+h;\alpha,m_{0},t),t+h)-\beta

\displaystyle\geq V(m(\cdot,t+h;\alpha,m_{0},t),t+h)-\beta,

Therefore, by (

4
The constrained Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation

In this section, following the finite-dimensional Soner’s state-constraint approach
[
23
]
, we introduce the constrained Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation (HJB), coupled with the terminal condition (TC) induced by the terminal cost
\psi
, and the notion of constrained viscosity solution. We show that the value function
V
is a constrained viscosity solution of (HJB)+(TC).

Remark 4.1
.

As noted in
[
4
, Remark 2.3]
, under our standing assumptions, for every
\varphi\in C^{1}\big(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times[0,T]\big)
and every solution
m
of (
4
), the map
t\mapsto\varphi(m(\cdot,t),t)
is absolutely continuous on
[0,T]
. Moreover, for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\varphi(m(\cdot,t),t)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\nabla_{m}\varphi(m(\cdot,t),t),\partial_{t}m(\cdot,t)\right\rangle+\partial_{t}\varphi(m(\cdot,t),t)

\displaystyle=-\left\langle\nabla_{m}\varphi(m(\cdot,t),t),\diver\big(\alpha(\cdot,t)m(\cdot,t)\big)\right\rangle+\partial_{t}\varphi(m(\cdot,t),t),

where
\nabla_{m}
denotes the (
L^{2}
-)Fréchet gradient with respect to the state variable
m
.

We define the Hamiltonian of our problem as

H:H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathbb{R},\qquad(m,q)\mapsto H(m,q):=\sup_{a\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}}}\bigg[\langle q,\diver(am)\rangle-\ell(m,a)\bigg].

Lemma 4.2
.

For every
m_{1},m_{2}\in X(T)
and
q_{1},q_{2}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
we have

\displaystyle|H(m_{1},q_{1})-H(m_{2},q_{2})|\leq M\tilde{C}_{U}\|q_{1}-q_{2}\|_{L^{2}}+M\|q_{1}\|_{L^{2}}\|m_{1}-m_{2}\|_{H^{1}}+L_{\ell}\|m_{1}-m_{2}\|_{L^{2}}.

Proof.

By a direct computation,

\displaystyle|H(m_{1},q_{1})-H(m_{2},q_{1})|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\sup_{a\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}}}\big[\langle q_{1},\diver(am_{1})-\diver(am_{2})\rangle+\ell(m_{2},a)-\ell(m_{1},a)\big]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|q_{1}\|_{L^{2}}M\|m_{1}-m_{2}\|_{H^{1}}+L_{\ell}\|m_{1}-m_{2}\|_{L^{2}}.

Moreover,
\displaystyle|H(m_{2},q_{1})-H(m_{2},q_{2})|\leq\left|\sup_{a\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}}}\big[\langle q_{1}-q_{2},\diver(am_{2})\rangle\big]\right|\leq M\tilde{C}_{U}\|q_{1}-q_{2}\|_{L^{2}}
.
∎

The Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation, coupled with its terminal condition, is given by

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\partial_{t}u(m,t)+H(m,\nabla_{m}u(m,t))&=0,&\qquad&\text{(HJB)}\\
u(m,T)&=\psi(m).&\qquad&\text{(TC)}\end{aligned}\right.

(24)

In the spirit of Soner
[
23
]
, we define constrained viscosity solutions of (
24
) by requiring the subsolution condition in the interior and the supersolution condition up to the boundary; more precisely, we use the following definition.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
u\in C(\bar{X}_{c})
(continuous with respect to
H^{1}
as well as to
L^{2}
, see Remark
2.9
).

i)

u
is called a
viscosity subsolution
of (
24
) in

if:

i-a)

for every
\varphi\in C^{1}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times[0,T])
and every
\displaystyle(m_{0},t_{0})\in\bigcup_{t\in(0,T)}\left[X_{c}(t)\times\{t\}\right]
which is a local maximum point of
u-\varphi
with respect to
X_{c}
, we have

-\partial_{t}\varphi(m_{0},t_{0})+H(m_{0},\nabla_{m}\varphi(m_{0},t_{0}))\leq 0;

for all

.

ii)

u
is called a
viscosity supersolution
of (
24
) in
\bar{X}_{c}
if:

ii-a)

for every
\varphi\in C^{1}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times[0,T])
and every
\displaystyle(m_{0},t_{0})\in\bigcup_{t\in(0,T)}\left[\bar{X}_{c}(t)\times\{t\}\right]
which is a local minimum point of

with respect to
\bar{X}_{c}
, we have

-\partial_{t}\varphi(m_{0},t_{0})+H(m_{0},\nabla_{m}\varphi(m_{0},t_{0}))\geq 0;

for all

.

iii)

is called a

of (
24
) in

if it is both a viscosity subsolution in

and a viscosity supersolution in
\bar{X}_{c}
.

Remark 4.4
.

For every

(m_{0},t_{0})\in\bigcup_{t\in[0,T)}\left[X_{c}(t)\times\{t\}\right]

).

Proposition 4.5

The value function

Proof.

ii-a)

5
Comparison principle and uniqueness

In this section we prove that, under our hypotheses, the value function is indeed the unique Lipschitz continuous constrained viscosity solution of (

Lemma 5.1
.

There exists a constant
B>0
such that for every
(\bar{m},\bar{t})\in\bar{X}_{c}(T)\times[0,T)

of the trajectory a

[

(28)

Let

6
Some comments about different formulations of the state constraint

.

In this section we discuss why the specific weighted state constraint (
8
) adopted in this paper is natural for our framework, both conceptually and technically.

A first natural candidate is the
hard
support constraint

\supp(m(\cdot,t))\subseteq\overline{\Omega}\qquad\forall t\in[0,T],

(41)

which for nonnegative densities is equivalent to require

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega}m(x,t)\,dx=0\qquad\forall t\in[0,T].

(42)

The difficulty with (
41
) is that this formulation is not robust in the functional topologies used in the paper: convergence in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
does not control supports. For instance, one can have
m_{k}\to 0
in
L^{2}
while all
m_{k}
have full support (for instance
m_{k}(x)=k^{-1}e^{-|x|^{2}}
), so support information is unstable under small
L^{2}
perturbations.

Regarding, on the other hand, (
42
), we still have a problem: from the viewpoint of the constraint, every admissible state is always
on the boundary
: a state with mass compactly concentrated inside
\Omega
(e.g., sufficiently far from violation) and a state with mass concentrated arbitrarily close to violation (e.g., a concentration near
\partial\Omega
) are both encoded in the same way. This seems to be incompatible with the correction mechanism in Lemma
3.4
, where one needs a quantitative slack variable
\eta
that measures how close the state is to violating the constraint, so that the correction is activated only when needed and remains uniformly controlled. In particular, the gain mechanism underlying the estimate analogous to (
16
) loses its validity.

To overcome this difficulty, a natural relaxation of (
42
) would be, for instance for probability densities, (for some fixed
\delta\in(0,1)
)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega}m(x,t)\,dx\leq\delta\qquad\forall t\in[0,T].

(43)

This is certainly more practical than (
42
) for detecting who is actually on the boundary of the constraint; however this is still not sufficient for (
16
). The reason is that (
43
) only measures how much mass is outside
\Omega
but not where that mass is located. Therefore, mass slightly outside and mass very far from
\Omega
are treated with the same weight. With bounded controls, this lack of spatial sensitivity prevents a uniform short-time pushback gain: it seems that one cannot, as made here, recover a pointwise estimate playing the role of (
12
), and consequently a uniform lower bound of the type used in (
16
).

For this reason we choose a different relaxation of (
42
): the weighted constraint (
8
), namely

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega}p(x)m(x,t)\,dx\leq\delta,

(44)

with
p(x)=\dist(x,\Omega)
(or more generally a weight with an associated pushback control, as in Remark
3.5
). This formulation indeed detects the spatial distribution of constraint-violating masses and differently penalizes them in terms of being slightly outside or far outside
\Omega
. This is exactly the information needed to recover uniform quantitative estimates in Lemma
3.4
, in particular (
16
).

Possible future studies could be the limit of the problem with constraint (
44
) for
\delta\downarrow 0
and hence, formally, the study of the problem with constraint

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega}p(x)m(x,t)\,dx=0,

which does not well detect the constraint-violating masses, similarly to (
42
). Another possibility is to recover the problem with constraint (
43
) as the limit of problems with constraint
(
44
)
for an appropriate sequence of weights
\{p_{k}\}
.

\frac{d}{ds}\det D\Phi_{t,s}(x)=(\mathrm{div}\,\alpha)(\Phi_{t,s}(x),s)\,\det D\Phi_{t,s}(x),\qquad\det D\Phi_{t,t}(x)=1,

\det D\Phi_{t,s}(x)=\exp\!\left(\int_{t}^{s}(\mathrm{div}\,\alpha)(\Phi_{t,\tau}(x),\tau)\,d\tau\right).

Using
\|\mathrm{div}\,\alpha(\cdot,\tau)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq n\|D\alpha(\cdot,\tau)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq nM
yields
(A5)
; in particular, for all
s\in[t,T]
,

\|(\det D\Phi_{t,s})^{-1}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq e^{nM(s-t)}\leq e^{nMT}.

Let
s\in[t,T]
and set
A(x):=D\Phi_{t,s}(x)
. By Jacobi’s formula,

\partial_{x_{i}}\det A(x)=\det A(x)\,\mathrm{tr}\!\big(A(x)^{-1}\partial_{x_{i}}A(x)\big).

(a2)

Thus
|\partial_{x_{i}}\det A|\leq n\,\det A\,\|A^{-1}\|\,\|\partial_{x_{i}}A\|
. Using

(A2)
for
\|A^{-1}\|
,
(A4)
for
\|D^{2}\Phi_{t,s}\|
(hence
\|\partial_{x_{i}}A\|
), and
(A5)
for
\|\det A\|
, we obtain that there exists
C_{\det,1}>0
such that

\|\nabla(\det D\Phi_{t,s})\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C_{\det,1}.

Moreover,
\displaystyle\nabla J_{t,s}=-\frac{\nabla(\det A)}{(\det A)^{2}}
, so by
(A5)
and the previous bound, we obtain
(A6)
, i.e.,

\left\|\nabla J_{t,s}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C_{\det,1}e^{2nMT}=:\tilde{C}_{J}.

Set
\tilde{A}(x):=\det A(x)=\det D\Phi_{t,s}(x)
. Differentiating (
a2
) with respect to
x_{j}
gives

\displaystyle\partial_{x_{j}x_{i}}^{2}\tilde{A}

\displaystyle=\partial_{x_{j}}\tilde{A}\,\mathrm{tr}\big(A^{-1}\partial_{x_{i}}A\big)+\tilde{A}\,\partial_{x_{j}}\Big(\mathrm{tr}\big(A^{-1}\partial_{x_{i}}A\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=\tilde{A}\,\mathrm{tr}\big(A^{-1}\partial_{x_{j}}A\big)\,\mathrm{tr}\big(A^{-1}\partial_{x_{i}}A\big)-\tilde{A}\,\mathrm{tr}\big(A^{-1}(\partial_{x_{j}}A)A^{-1}(\partial_{x_{i}}A)\big)+\tilde{A}\,\mathrm{tr}\big(A^{-1}\partial_{x_{j}x_{i}}^{2}A\big)

\displaystyle\leq e^{nMT}n^{2}e^{2MT}C_{\Phi,2}^{2}+e^{nMT}ne^{2MT}C_{\Phi,2}^{2}+e^{nMT}ne^{MT}C_{\Phi,3}=:C_{\det,2},

where we used
(A2)
,
(A4)
, (
a1
), and
(A5)
.

Moreover, for every
i,j
,

\partial_{x_{i}x_{j}}^{2}(J_{t,s})=2\tilde{A}^{-3}\,(\partial_{x_{i}}\tilde{A})(\partial_{x_{j}}\tilde{A})-J_{t,s}^{2}\,\partial_{x_{i}x_{j}}^{2}\tilde{A}.

Using the bounds on
\tilde{A}
(from
(A5)
), on
\partial_{x_{i}}\tilde{A}
(from
(A6)
), and on
\partial_{x_{i}x_{j}}^{2}\tilde{A}
(from the estimate just obtained), we get a uniform constant
C_{J}
, i.e.,
(A7)
.
∎

Proposition A2
.

Let
(X,\|\cdot\|)
be a normed space,
T>0
, and
Y\subseteq X\times[0,T]
. Assume that
f:Y\to\mathbb{R}
is
L
-Lipschitz, that is,

|f(x,t)-f(y,s)|\leq L\bigl(\|x-y\|+|t-s|\bigr)\qquad\forall(x,t),(y,s)\in Y.

Then there exists an extension
\tilde{f}:X\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\tilde{f}=f
on
Y
and

|\tilde{f}(x,t)-\tilde{f}(y,s)|\leq L\bigl(\|x-y\|+|t-s|\bigr)\qquad\forall(x,t),(y,s)\in X\times[0,T].

Furthermore if
X
is compact then the map
\displaystyle g:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R},\;t\mapsto\max_{x\in X}\tilde{f}(x,t)
is continuous.

Proof.

Define
\displaystyle\tilde{f}(x,t):=\inf_{(z,\tau)\in Y}\Bigl(f(z,\tau)+L\bigl(\|x-z\|+|t-\tau|\bigr)\Bigr)
. Clearly
\tilde{f}|_{Y}\equiv f
. Furthermore, let
(x,t),(y,s)\in X\times[0,T]
; clearly

f(z,\tau)+L(\|x-z\|+|t-\tau|)\leq f(z,\tau)+L(\|x-y\|+\|y-z\|+|t-s|+|s-\tau|).

Taking the infimum over
(z,\tau)\in Y
, we get
\tilde{f}(x,t)\leq\tilde{f}(y,s)+L\bigl(\|x-y\|+|t-s|\bigr),
and exchanging the roles of
(x,t),

(y,s)
we also obtain
\tilde{f}(y,s)\leq\tilde{f}(x,t)+L\bigl(\|x-y\|+|t-s|\bigr)
. Hence

|\tilde{f}(x,t)-\tilde{f}(y,s)|\leq L\bigl(\|x-y\|+|t-s|\bigr).

Let now
t,s\in[0,T]
. For every
x\in X
we have that
\tilde{f}(x,t)\leq\tilde{f}(x,s)+L|t-s|,
and taking the supremum over
x\in X
, we obtain
g(t)\leq g(s)+L|t-s|
.
Exchanging the roles of
t
and
s
, we also get
g(s)\leq g(t)+L|t-s|
, and therefore
|g(t)-g(s)|\leq L|t-s|
.
∎
