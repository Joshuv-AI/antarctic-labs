Logarithmic regularity of spectral measures on infinite graphs
Charles Bordenave

Abstract
We study the regularity of spectral measures of self-adjoint operators on infinite weighted graphs in the unimodular setting. This framework encompasses operators in the group algebra of a finitely generated group, random operators whose distribution is quasi-invariant under a group action, and Benjamini–Schramm limits of operators on finite graphs. Under a natural geometric condition on the underlying graph, we prove that the expected spectral measure satisfies a logarithmic Hölder regularity estimate. The proof relies on a strengthened version of the monotone labelling method previously introduced with Sen and Virág to control the pure point part of the spectal measure. Applications include operators in group algebras of indicable groups, Anderson-type models with arbitrary compactly supported potentials on Cayley graphs, anisotropic percolation operators, and operators on quasi-transitive graphs. In particular, our results extend the classical Craig–Simon theorem beyond ℤ d \mathbb{Z}^{d} .

Introduction

In this paper, we are interested in the spectrum of self-adjoint locally defined operators on infinite graphs. The graph and the operator considered here can be deterministic or random, but they need to satisfy an invariance condition: unimodularity. This framework encompasses all operators in the group algebra
\mathbb{C}[\Gamma]
of a finitely generated group
\Gamma
. It contains more generally all random operators whose distribution is quasi-invariant under the group action. It also includes all operators which are the Benjamini-Schramm limits of a sequence of operators on finite graphs. Under a simple geometric condition, we notably prove that the expected spectral measure, say
\mu
, satisfies the following regularity condition: for intervals
I
in the spectrum,

\mu(I)=O\left(\frac{1}{\ln(C/|I|)}\right),

where
|I|
the length of the interval. This work is to some extent a sequel of the joint paper
[
8
]
with Sen and Virág where under the same or similar geometric conditions, the absence/presence of atoms in the expected spectral measure was established. In the present work, we strengthen the monotone labelling tool introduced in
[
8
]
in two ways: we control the expected spectral measure on intervals and not only singletons; we also generalize this tool to deal with graphs with more intricate structures.

In
[
8
]
, the examples of applications focused on random graphs, random trees and percolation graphs. In the present work, we explore new applications to transitive and quasi-transitive graphs with possibly random weights. We present in this introduction three applications. Each application is contained in the next application but to avoid introducing too many notions at the same time, we present them successively (at the expense of some redundancy).

The motivations to study the spectral measures are extremely diverse. For example, in algebraic topology, the study of the spectral measure of elements in the group algebra
\mathbb{C}[\Gamma]
has attracted a lot of attention in connection with the theory of
L^{2}
-invariants and
L^{2}
-Betti numbers, we refer to the monograph
[
24
]
. In quantum mechanics, Wiener’s Lemma implies that for any self-adjoint operator
H
on a Hilbert space and any vector
f\in D(H)
, we have

\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\left|\langle f,e^{itH}f\rangle\right|^{2}dt=\sum_{\lambda}\mu_{H}^{f}(\{\lambda\})^{2},

where
\mu_{H}^{f}
is the spectral measure at vector
f
, see (
3
), and the sum is over the set of its atoms. Hence, the diffusion of quantum particles is intimately related to the spectral decomposition. This is the content of the RAGE Theorem, we refer to
[
1
, Section 2]
.

Operators in group algebra.

Let
\Gamma
be a finitely generated group with unit
\mathrm{e}
and
\lambda_{\Gamma}
be its left-regular representation on
\ell^{2}(\Gamma)
(defined by
\lambda_{\Gamma}(g)f(x)=f(g^{-1}x)
, for
f\in\ell^{2}(\Gamma)
,
g,x\in\Gamma
). An element
p=\sum_{g}p_{g}g\in\mathbb{C}[\Gamma]
in the group algebra defines a bounded operator on
\ell^{2}(\Gamma)
through
\lambda_{\Gamma}
by

\lambda_{\Gamma}(p)=\sum_{g}p_{g}\lambda_{\Gamma}(g).

To put it plainly, for any
f\in\ell^{2}(\Gamma)
and
x\in\Gamma
we have

\lambda_{\Gamma}(p)f(x)=\sum_{g}p_{g}f(g^{-1}x).

(1)

Since
\lambda_{\Gamma}
is faithful, there is no harm in identifying
\lambda_{\Gamma}(p)
and
p
. In the sequel, we often write
p
in place of
\lambda_{\Gamma}(p)
. If
S=S^{-1}
is a finite symmetric set of generators and
1_{S}=\sum_{g\in S}g\in\mathbb{C}[\Gamma]
, then
\lambda_{\Gamma}(1_{S})
is the adjacency operator of the Cayley graph
{\mathrm{Cay}}(\Gamma,S)
of
\Gamma
with generators
S
(we allow
\mathrm{e}\in S
). If
{\mathrm{supp}}(p)\subset S
, we can interpret
p
as a local operator on
{\mathrm{Cay}}(\Gamma,S)
. For example if
p_{g}\geq 0
and
\sum_{g}p_{g}=1
then
p
is the transition kernel of a random walk on
{\mathrm{Cay}}(\Gamma,S)
.

If
p=p^{*}
where
p^{*}=\sum_{g}\bar{p}_{g}g^{-1}
then
p
is a self-adjoint operator on
\ell^{2}(\Gamma)
. From the spectral theorem, we can then decompose
p
as

p=\int\lambda dE(\lambda),

where
E
is the resolution of the identity of
p
. The
spectral measure of
p
(aka Plancherel or Kesten measure) is the probability measure on
\mathbb{R}
:
\mu_{p}=\langle\delta_{\mathrm{e}},E\delta_{\mathrm{e}}\rangle
, where
\delta_{g}
is the Dirac delta mass at
g\in\Gamma
. Concretely, for any bounded measurable function
\varphi
,

\int\varphi(\lambda)d\mu_{p}(\lambda)=\langle\delta_{e},\varphi(p)\delta_{\mathrm{e}}\rangle=\int\varphi(\lambda)d\langle\delta_{\mathrm{e}},E(\lambda)\delta_{\mathrm{e}}\rangle.

We are interested in the spectral decomposition of
\lambda_{\Gamma}(p)
when
p=p^{*}
. That is, we want to decompose
\mu_{p}
in pure point, singular continuous and absolutely continuous parts. Beware that this decomposition may vary in nature across a given group. Indeed, for the lamplighter group
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\wr\mathbb{Z}
, there are some generating sets
S
and
S^{\prime}
such that
1_{S}
has purely point spectrum while
1_{S^{\prime}}
is purely singular continuous, see
[
14
,
20
]
. If
\Gamma
admits the free group as a finite index subgroup, then the situation is relatively well-understood:
\mu_{p}
has at most finitely many atoms
[
23
]
and its continuous part is absolutely continuous (with algebraic density), see
[
32
,
12
]
and references therein. If
\Gamma
admits
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
as a finite index subgroup, the same situation prevails thanks to Bloch-Floquet theory, see notably
[
15
,
19
]
in this direction. In torsion-free groups where the strong Atiyah conjecture holds,
\mu_{p}
has no atoms if
p\neq 0
, see
[
16
,
30
]
for important recent results in this direction. Beyond these good cases, it is fair to remark that not much is known, see
[
5
,
26
,
27
,
11
,
9
]
for early references on this topic. In this paper, we make some progress in this direction. For the related question of the connectedness of the spectrum, we refer to
[
31
,
18
]
and references therein.

The following definition is central in our applications. Recall that a group
\Gamma
is indicable if it admits a surjective homomorphism to
(\mathbb{Z},+)
. We specialize this definition as follows.

Definition 1
.

Let
k\geq 1
integer,
\Gamma
be a group,
S=S^{-1}\subset\Gamma
a symmetric subset and an element
a\in S
. We say that
(\Gamma,S)
is
(a,k)
-indicable if there exists
\phi\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})
such that
k=\phi(a)>\phi(b)
for all
b\in S
,
b\neq a
.

Note that if
(\Gamma,S)
is
a
-indicable then
a
has necessarily infinite order. If
k=1
, an equivalent definition is the following:
(\Gamma,S)
is
(a,1)
-indicable if and only if for any integer
n\geq 1
and any
w\in S^{n}
,
w_{1}\cdots w_{n}=\mathrm{e}
implies that the number of
i
’s such that
w_{i}=a
is equal to the number of
i
’s such that
w_{i}=a^{-1}
.

As noted in
[
13
, Remark 1.2]
, for any finitely generated indicable group
\Gamma
, there exists a symmetric set of generators
S
and
a\in S
such that
(\Gamma,S)
is
(a,1)
-indicable. The following classical groups are indicable: free groups, Artin groups, braid groups, fundamental groups of Riemann surfaces or Thompson group. Equipped with their natural sets of generators
S
and picking any
a\in S
, the following classical groups are
(a,1)
-indicable: free groups, even Artin groups or fundamental groups of Riemann surfaces.

Note however that in all these groups, it is possible to find a generating set
S
such that
(\Gamma,S)
is not
(a,k)
-indicable for any
a\in S
and integer
k\geq 1
. Indeed, if
a
and
b
in
\Gamma
do not commute and
S
contains
ab^{\epsilon}
and
b^{\epsilon}a
with
\epsilon\in\{-1,0,1\}
then any
\phi\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})
satisfies
\phi(ab^{\epsilon})=\phi(b^{\epsilon}a)=\phi(a)+\epsilon\phi(b)
in particular
\phi
cannot have a unique maximum among
ab^{\epsilon}
,
b^{\epsilon}a
and their inverses. We postpone to Subsection
4.3
the application of our technique to this situation.

Our first result is a refinement of
[
13
]
. Here and below if
I\subset\mathbb{R}
is an interval then
|I|
denotes its Lebesgue measure. By convention, a singleton
I=\{\lambda\}
is a closed interval of length
0
. For
x
real, we set
(x)_{+}=\max(x,0)
. The operator norm of an operator
T
is denoted by
\|T\|_{\rm op}
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
p\in\mathbb{C}[\Gamma]
such that
p=p^{*}
and let
S={\mathrm{supp}}(p)
. If there exists
a\in S
such that
(\Gamma,S)
is
(a,k)
-indicable then for any interval
I\subset\sigma(p)
we have

\mu_{p}(I)\leq\frac{2k\ln(\alpha)}{\left(\ln\left(\beta/|I|\right)\right)_{+}},

where
\alpha=3\|p\|_{\rm op}/|p_{a}|
,
\beta=2\sqrt{2}|p_{a}|
. In particular,
\mu_{p}
has no atom.

This theorem improves on
[
13
, Theorem 1.1]
in three ways: firstly,
\Gamma
is allowed to be non-amenable, secondly
[
13
, Theorem 1.1]
considered only singleton
I=\{\lambda\}
, lastly, in our terminology,
[
13
]
considered only
k=1
. As mentioned above, this theorem applies for example to all adjacency operators of Cayley graphs of even Artin groups or surface groups with their natural sets of generators. Note that for surface groups
[
16
]
and right-angled Artin groups
[
21
]
, the strong Atiyah conjecture is known to hold and thus
\mu_{p}
has no atom when
p\neq 0
.

The conclusion of Theorem
1
cannot be very significantly improved without further assumptions. Indeed, Kotowksi and Viràg have proved that for
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}_{2}\wr\mathbb{Z}
and some operator
p\in\mathbb{C}[\Gamma]
satisfying the hypothesis of the theorem, we have

\mu_{p}((-\epsilon,\epsilon))\sim C/(\ln\epsilon)^{2}
as
\epsilon\to 0
(see discussion in
[
20
]
).

When
\Gamma
is indicable but when there does not exist
a\in S
such that
(\Gamma,S)
is
(a,k)
indicable then a weaker version of Theorem
1
holds. Namely, in Subsection
4.3
, we will prove Theorem
6
which asserts that for any finite symmetric generating set
S
, there exists
p=p^{*}\in\mathbb{C}[\Gamma]
with
{\mathrm{supp}}(p)=S
such that the conclusion of Theorem
1
holds. For the lampligther group
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}_{2}\wr\mathbb{Z}
, this statement will have an interesting consequence, see Remark
2
.

Invariant random operators.

We now generalize Theorem
1
to random operators in
\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(\Gamma))
whose law is invariant by right multiplication. More precisely, fix
S=S^{-1}
a finite symmetric set of generators (the unit
\mathrm{e}
could be in
S
). We consider an array
P=(p_{g}(x))_{g\in S,x\in\Gamma}\in\mathbb{C}^{S\times\Gamma}
which is
symmetric
in the sense that for all
x\in\Gamma
and
g\in S
,

p_{g^{-1}}(gx)=\overline{p}_{g}(x).

(2)

The array
P=(p_{g}(x))_{g\in S,x\in\Gamma}
defines a symmetric operator through the formula for
f\in\ell^{2}(\Gamma)
with finite support and
x\in\Gamma
,

Pf(x)=\sum_{g\in S}p_{g}(x)f(g^{-1}x).

In particular, from (
1
), if
p_{g}(x)
does not depend on
x
, we retrieve the previous setting. Under mild assumptions,
P
extends to a self-adjoint operator. For example if
K=\sup_{x\in\Gamma}\sum_{g\in S}|p_{g}(x)|<\infty
then
P
is a bounded operator on
\ell^{2}(\Gamma)
with
\|P\|_{\rm op}\leq K
. As above, the spectral measure of a vector
f
is then defined by: for any bounded measurable function
\varphi
,

\int\varphi(\lambda)d\mu_{P}^{f}(\lambda)=\langle f,\varphi(P)f\rangle.

(3)

We are interested in random operators, that is when the array
P=(p_{g}(x))_{g\in S,x\in\Gamma}
is random and its distribution has the following invariance in distribution: for any
y\in\Gamma
,

(p_{g}(x))_{g\in S,x\in\Gamma}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(p_{g}(xy))_{g\in S,x\in\Gamma},

where
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}
is equality in distribution.
We then say that
P
is a
right-invariant
array. For example, if
(p_{g}(x))_{g\in S,x\in\Gamma}
are independent and identically distributed variables (up to symmetry (
2
)) then
P
is right-invariant.

This setting allows notably to consider the Anderson tight-binding model on
\Gamma
, that is operators of the form:

P=V+\lambda_{\Gamma}(1_{S_{0}}),

where
S_{0}=S_{0}^{-1}
is a finite symmetric set of generators of
\Gamma
with
\mathrm{e}\notin S_{0}
,
1_{S_{0}}=\sum_{g\in S_{0}}g
and
V
is the diagonal operator defined by
Vf(x)=V_{x}f(x)
with
(V_{x})_{x\in\Gamma}
independent and identically distributed real random variables. Indeed, set
S=S_{0}\cup\{\mathrm{e}\}
,
p_{\mathrm{e}}(x)=V_{x}
and
p_{g}(x)=1
for
g\in S_{0}
.

This setting also contains anisotropic percolation graphs. Let
S=S^{-1}
be a finite symmetric set of generators of
\Gamma
and for
g\in S
, let
\pi_{g}=\pi_{g^{-1}}\in[0,1]
. The percolation graph of
{\mathrm{Cay}}(\Gamma,S)
associated to the probabilities
(\pi_{g})_{g\in S}
is the graph whose adjacency operator is the above operator
P
with
(p_{g}(x))_{g\in S,x\in\Gamma}\in\{0,1\}^{S\times\Gamma}
independent (up to symmetry (
2
)) with
\mathbb{P}(p_{g}(x)=1)=1-\mathbb{P}(p_{g}(x)=0)=\pi_{g}
.

There is an extension of Theorem
1
to right-invariant operators for the
expected spectral measure

\mathbb{E}[\mu^{\delta_{\mathrm{e}}}_{P}]
, which is also known as the density of states in mathematical physics (here and below
\mathbb{E}
denotes the expectation with respect to the underlying randomness).

Theorem 2
.

Let
S=S^{-1}
be a finite symmetric subset of
\Gamma
and
P=(p_{g}(x))_{g\in S,x\in\Gamma}
be a right-invariant symmetric random array. Assume that there exist
a\in S
such that
(\Gamma,S)
is
(a,k)
-indicable and deterministic constants
K,p_{*}>0
such that with probability one,
\|P\|_{\rm op}\leq K
and for all
x\in\Gamma
,
|p_{a}(x)|\geq p_{*}
. Then for any interval
I\subset\sigma(P)
we have

\mathbb{E}\mu^{\delta_{\mathrm{e}}}_{P}(I)\leq\frac{2k\ln(\alpha)}{\left(\ln\left(\beta/|I|\right)\right)_{+}},

where
\alpha=3K/p_{*}
and
\beta=2\sqrt{2}p_{*}
.

Interestingly, the claim of the theorem does not depend on the weights
p_{g}(x)
with
g\notin\{a,a^{-1}\}
. For example, it could be applied to the Anderson tight-binding model for any potential
V_{x}
whose distribution is compact. Our result can then be seen as an extension of the classical Craig-Simon theorem
[
10
]
(where in contrast with the classical Wegner estimates, there is no density assumption or independence that is assumed on the potential
V
, see
[
1
]
). It can also be applied to anisotropic percolation with any probabilities
(\pi_{g})_{g\in S}
such that
\pi_{a}=\pi_{a^{-1}}=1
. We then obtain a uniform bound (over the values of
(\pi_{g})_{g\in S\backslash\{a^{-1},a\}}
) on the logarithmic regularity. This surprising phenomenon had already been observed on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and for the absence of atoms in
[
8
]
(under the name of vertical percolation).

Again, the statement of Theorem
2
is not too far from optimal for this set of assumptions. For the Anderson tight-binding model
P
on
\mathbb{Z}
, it was known to Dyson that
\mathbb{E}\mu^{\delta_{\mathrm{e}}}_{P}((-\epsilon,\epsilon))\sim C/(\ln\epsilon)^{2}
as
\epsilon\to 0
under mild assumption on the distribution of
V_{x}
, we refer again to
[
20
]
.

Quasi-transitive graphs.

We give an illustration for (possibly random) operators on quasi-transitive graphs. Let
V
be a finite set,
\Gamma
be a finitely generated group as above and
S=S^{-1}
be a finite symmetric subset of
\Gamma
. We now consider operators acting on
\mathbb{C}^{V}\otimes\ell^{2}(\Gamma)
. A vector
f\in\mathbb{C}^{V}\otimes\ell^{2}(\Gamma)
can be written as
f=(f(x))_{x\in\Gamma}
where
f(x)\in\mathbb{C}^{V}
is the orthogonal projection onto the vector space supported by vectors with support in
V\times\{x\}
. For each
x\in\Gamma
and
g\in S
, let
p_{g}(x)\in M_{V}(\mathbb{C})
be such that

p_{g^{-1}}(gx)=p_{g}(x)^{*},

where
*
is the conjugate transpose of a matrix. The array
P=(p_{g}(x))_{g\in S,x\in\Gamma}\in M_{V}(\mathbb{C})^{S\times\Gamma}
defines a symmetric operator through the formula: for any
f\in\mathbb{C}^{V}\otimes\ell^{2}(\Gamma)
with finite support and
x\in\Gamma
,

(Pf)(x)=\sum_{g\in S}p_{g}(x)f(g^{-1}x),

(this is an identity in
\mathbb{C}^{V}
). Again, under mild assumptions,
P
extends to a self-adjoint operator, for example if
\|P\|_{\rm op}\leq\sup_{x\in\Gamma}\sum_{g\in S}\|p_{g}(x)\|_{\rm op}
is finite. If
p_{g}(x)=p_{g}
does not depend on
x
, then
P
can simply be written as

P=\sum_{g\in S}p_{g}\otimes\lambda_{\Gamma}(g).

(4)

This type of quasi-transitive operators with respect to the right-action of
\Gamma
is ubiquitous, see for example
[
14
,
22
,
28
,
7
]
and references therein. Also, elements of the group algebra of a direct or semi-direct products
G\ltimes\Gamma
where
G
is a finite group can be written in the form (
4
).

More generally, when
P
is random, we say that the array
P=(p_{g}(x))_{g\in S,x\in\Gamma}\in M_{V}(\mathbb{C})^{S\times\Gamma}
is right-invariant, if for any
y\in\Gamma
,
P\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(p_{g}(xy))_{g\in S,x\in\Gamma}
.

The last result of this introduction allows to control by induction on
S
and
V
, the regularity of the expected spectral measure of
P
defined as

\mathbb{E}[\mu_{P}^{\delta_{\mathrm{e}o}}]=\frac{1}{|V|}\sum_{v\in V}\mathbb{E}[\mu_{P}^{\delta_{\mathrm{e}v}}],

where
\delta_{\mathrm{e}v}=\delta_{\mathrm{e}}\otimes\delta_{u}
and
o
is uniformly distributed on
V
. If
P
has the form (
4
), the
L^{2}
-Betti numbers can be expressed as the mass at
0
of such spectral measure, see
[
24
,
22
]
.

Below, if
T\in M_{V}(\mathbb{C})
and
U_{1},U_{2}\subset V
,
T_{|U_{1}\times U_{2}}\in M_{U_{1},U_{2}}(\mathbb{C})
is the submatrix with indices in
U_{1}\times U_{2}
. If
U=U_{1}=U_{2}
, we simply write
T_{|U}
in place of
T_{|U\times U}
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
V
be a finite set,
S=S^{-1}
be a finite symmetric subset of
\Gamma
and assume that there exists
a\in S
such that
(\Gamma,S)
is
(a,k)
-indicable for some
\phi\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})
. Let
P=(p_{g}(x))_{g\in S,x\in\Gamma}
be a right-invariant symmetric random array in
M_{V}(\mathbb{C})^{S\times\Gamma}
. Assume that there is a partition
V=V_{0}\sqcup V_{1}
(
V_{1}
can be empty) and deterministic constants
K,p_{*}>0
such that with probability one,
\|P\|_{\rm op}\leq K
,
\|{p_{a}(x)_{|V_{0}}}^{-1}\|_{\rm op}\leq p^{-1}_{*}
and
p_{g}(x)_{|V\times V_{1}}=0
for all
x\in\Gamma
and
g\in S
with
\phi(g)>0
. Then for any closed interval
I\subset\sigma(P)
we have

\sum_{v\in V}\mathbb{E}\mu^{\delta_{\mathrm{e}v}}_{P}(I)\leq|V_{0}|\frac{2k\ln(\alpha)}{\left(\ln\left(\beta/|I|\right)\right)_{+}}+\sum_{v\in V_{1}}\mathbb{E}\mu^{\delta_{\mathrm{e}v}}_{P_{1}}(I),

where
\alpha=3K/p_{*}
,
\beta=2\sqrt{2}p_{*}
,
P_{1}=(p_{g}(x)_{|V_{1}})_{g\in S_{1},x\in\Gamma}\in M_{V_{1}}(\mathbb{C})
and
S_{1}=S\backslash\{a^{-1},a\}
(with the convention that the sum over an empty set is
0
).

This result can be applied to many quasi-transitive operators.
Let us give for simplicity some deterministic examples with
p_{g}(x)=p_{g}=p^{*}_{g^{-1}}
independent of
x
as in (
4
). The obvious example is when
(\Gamma,S)
is
(a,k)
-indicable and
p_{a}
is invertible: we can then directly apply the theorem to
V_{0}=V
.

A simple example with
V_{1}
not empty is the following: assume, in addition to the assumptions of the theorem, that
\mathrm{e}\in S
and
{p_{g}}_{|V_{1}}=0
for all
g\in S\backslash\{\mathrm{e}\}
. Then
P_{1}=p_{1}\otimes 1_{\Gamma}
, where
p_{1}={p_{\mathrm{e}}}_{|V_{1}}
and
1_{\Gamma}
is the identity operator on
\ell^{2}(\Gamma)
. Let

\mu_{p_{1}}=\frac{1}{|V_{1}|}\sum_{i=1}^{|V_{1}|}\delta_{\lambda_{i}(p_{1})}

be the empirical distribution of the eigenvalues of
p_{1}
(counting multiplicities). From the spectral theorem, we have

\mu_{p_{1}}=\frac{1}{|V_{1}|}\sum_{v\in V_{1}}\mu_{p_{1}}^{\delta_{v}}.

Hence, from Theorem
3
, for any closed interval
I\subset\sigma(P)
, we get

\mathbb{E}\mu_{P}^{\delta_{\mathrm{e}o}}(I)=\frac{1}{|V|}\sum_{v\in V}\mu^{\delta_{\mathrm{e}v}}_{P}(I)\leq\frac{|V_{0}|}{|V|}\frac{2k\ln(\alpha)}{\left(\ln\left(\beta/|I|\right)\right)_{+}}+\frac{|V_{1}|}{|V|}\mu_{p_{1}}(I).

In particular, the set of atoms is finite and contained in the set of eigenvalues of
p_{1}
.

Our last example uses successive applications of Theorem
3
. Assume that we have a partition
V=\sqcup_{i=1}^{k}V^{i}_{0}
with
|V^{i}_{0}|=d_{i}\geq 1
, and assume that there exist distinct elements
\{a_{i},a_{i}^{-1}\}
in
S
such that for any
i
,
(\Gamma,S)
is
(a_{i},1)
-indicable and
p_{a_{i}}
is zero except on the
V^{i}_{0}\times V^{i}_{0}
-diagonal block where it is equal to
q_{i}\in M_{d_{i}}(\mathbb{C})
with
\|q_{i}^{-1}\|_{\rm op}\leq p^{-1}_{*}
. We can then iteratively apply Theorem
3
for
j=1,\ldots,k
to
V^{j}=\sqcup_{i\geq j}V^{i}_{0}
,
V_{0}^{j}
and
V_{1}^{j}=\sqcup_{i>j}V^{i}_{0}
. We get for any closed interval
I\subset\sigma(P)
,

\sum_{v\in V}\mu^{\delta_{\mathrm{e}v}}_{P}(I)\leq\frac{2k\ln(\alpha)}{\left(\ln\left(\beta/|I|\right)\right)_{+}}.

Interestingly, this upper bound depends on
p_{g}
, for
g\in S\backslash\{a_{i}^{\pm}:1\leq i\leq n\}
only through their contribution to
K=\|P\|_{\rm op}
.

Proof overview.

At a high level, the existence of a surjective homomorphism
\phi
from
\Gamma
to
\mathbb{Z}
allows to decompose
\ell^{2}(\Gamma)
along the values of
\phi(x)
,
x\in\Gamma
. We can then study the eigenvalue equation
(P-\lambda)f=0
iteratively along this decomposition, the idea being that
f(x)
is determined by the values of
f(y)
with
\phi(y)<\phi(x)
. This strategy works nicely in finite dimension and beyond groups in the more general setting of what we called monotone labelling in
[
8
]
, see Lemma
1
. In infinite dimension, to make sense of this decomposition, we prove that such decomposition can be made inside a von Neumann algebra of finite type. The existence of a trace on this algebra will be guaranteed by the invariance properties of the operators that we consider. Then, using the von Neumann dimension associated to this trace, the argument stated in finite dimension can be made effective also in infinite dimension. This will lead to our main results on logarithmic regularity of spectral measures, Theorem
4
and Theorem
5
.

Some perspectives.

Our basic approach in finite dimension, Lemma
1
, is rather rudimentary. It would be extremely interesting to identify deeper geometric structures to study eigenvalue equations which might take advantage of richer structures such as homomorphisms onto
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
or free groups. More generally, there is a lack of new methods which could establish absolutely continuous spectrum.

It would be also very natural to explore similar strategies for periodic operators on a Riemannian manifold with a properly discontinuous, cocompact and isometric
\Gamma
-action. Such operators are notably studied in
[
31
]
. What appears to be a first difficulty to extend our tools in this setting is that the corresponding von Neumann algebra is not of finite type.

Organization of the paper.

In Section
2
, we present the general framework of unimodular random graphs and their local operators. In Section
3
, we state and prove our main result on the regularity of the spectral measure. In Section
4
we apply this tool and prove the theorems stated above.

Marked Graph.

A
graph

G=(V,E)
will be a pair formed by a countable vertex set
V
and a countable edge set
E
. An edge
e=(u,v)\in E
is an ordered pair of vertices. The graphs here are undirected meaning that
E
is equipped with the involution
e\mapsto e^{-1}
such that if
e=(u,v)
,
e^{-1}=(v,u)
. For ease of notation, for

, we write

, if

. Our graphs may have self-loops, that is, edges such that
e=e^{-1}=(u,u)
. The degree
\deg(v)
of

is the number of edges of the form
e=(v,u)\in E
. We say that
G
has

if
\sup_{v\in V}\deg(v)<\infty
.
A graph is
connected
if there is a path connecting any pair of vertices.

Let
\mathcal{Z}
be a set equipped with an involution denoted by
*
. A
marked graph

G=(V,E,\xi)
is a graph
(V,E)
and a map
\xi:E\to\mathcal{Z}
satisfying the symmetry relation: for all
e\in E
,

\xi(e^{-1})=\xi(e)^{*}.

(5)

We will call the unmarked graph
\bar{G}=(V,E)
the
skeleton
of
G
. A
rooted marked graph

g=(G,o)
is the pair formed by a connected marked graph and a distinguished vertex
o\in V
called the root. Similarly, an
edge-rooted marked graph

(G,e)
is a connected marked graph
G
with a distinguished oriented pair
e=(u,v)\in V^{2}
(we do not necessarily assume that
e\in E
).

In the next section, we will study (weighted) adjacency operators. A marked graph

Unimodularity.

We fix some subgroup

\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{357257}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{357257}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{385580}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{451339}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}})

(8)

Monotone labelling revisited

In this section, we give with Theorem

a quantitative improvement over the monotone labelling technique introduced in
[
8
]
. We also prove an extension to partition labelling, Theorem

Monotone labelling.

We borrow from
[
8
]
the following central definition.

Definition 2
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph. For a given map
\eta:V\to\mathbb{Z}
, we partition the vertices
V
into three disjoint subsets: a vertex
x\in V
is

(i)

Prodigy, if there exists
\hat{x}\sim x
such that
\eta(\hat{x})<\eta(x)
and for all
y\sim\hat{x}
,
y\neq x
,
\eta(y)<\eta(x)
;

(ii)

Level, if

is not a prodigy and for all

\eta(y)\leq\eta(x)

(iii)

Bad otherwise.

Finite graphs.

As a warmup, we start with a finite weighted graph
G=(V,E,p)
. We denote by

the eigenvalues counting multiplicities of

defined in (

is defined as

L_{G}=\sum_{k=1}^{|V|}\delta_{\lambda_{k}}.

(13)

This is a measure on
\mathbb{R}
with total mass
|V|
and whose mass on an interval

.

Lemma 1
.

Let
G=(V,E,p)
be a finite weighted graph and
\eta
a labelling with
n
distinct values. Assume

where the infimum is over all
x
prodigy and
\hat{x}
is as in Definition
2
(i). Let

and
\kappa=3\|A_{G}\|_{\rm op}\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-1}\geq 6\sqrt{2}\|A_{G}\|_{\rm op}
. For any interval

of length at most

, we have

L_{G}(I)\leq|B|+\sum_{j}L_{G_{j}}(I),

where
B
is the set of bad vertices and

is the restriction of
G
to level vertices with label
j
.

Lemma
1
applied to

is exactly
[
8
, Theorem 2.2]
. The constant

in the definition of

and

is somewhat arbitrary, the proof gives a constant

.

Proof of Lemma
1
.

Since the spectrum is contained in the closed interval
I_{0}=[-\|A_{G}\|_{\rm op},\|A_{G}\|_{\rm op}]
, without loss of generality, we can assume that
I\subset I_{0}
. In the sequel,
\lambda\in I
is the center of the interval
I
.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that the range of
\eta
is
\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
. Let
B
be the set of bad vertices,
L_{j}
and
P_{j}
be the sets of level and prodigy vertices of label
j
. We denote by
\mathcal{B}
,
\mathcal{L}_{j}
and
\mathcal{P}_{j}
the vector subspaces of
\mathbb{C}^{V}
spanned by vectors supported on
B
,
L_{j}
and
P_{j}
. Let
\mathcal{F}
be the vector space spanned by eigenvectors of
A_{G}
with eigenvalues in
I
. We have
\dim(\mathcal{F})=L_{G}(I)
and
\dim(\mathcal{B})=|B|

Let
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}=\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{B}^{\perp}\bigcap_{j}\mathcal{L}_{j}^{\perp}
where

denotes the orthocomplement. Since
\mathcal{F}
is contained in the sum of
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
,
\mathcal{B}
and the
\mathcal{L}_{j}
’s, we deduce from the subaditivity of dimension that

\dim(\mathcal{F})\leq\dim(\mathcal{F}^{\prime})+\dim(\mathcal{B})+\sum_{j}\dim(\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{L}_{j}).

By Lemma
2
below,
\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{L}_{j}\subset\mathcal{F}_{j}
where
\mathcal{F}_{j}
is the vector space spanned by eigenvectors of

with eigenvalues in

. Therefore, the proof of the lemma will be complete if we prove that

is trivial.

To this end, since any vector of
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
is

on bad and level vertices, we may write
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}=\bigoplus_{j}\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{j}
with
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{j}=\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{P}_{j}
. We take a vector
f\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
and set
f_{j}\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{j}
to be its projection onto
\mathcal{P}_{j}
.

If
j=0
then
\mathcal{P}_{0}
is trivial (no prodigy can have minimal label
0
). In particular
f_{0}=0
. We prove by induction on

that

\|f_{j}\|\leq\frac{|I|}{p_{*}}\|f\|\alpha^{j-1}.

(14)

We denote by

the operator from

to
\mathbb{C}^{V}
defined for

by
T_{j}\delta_{x}=p(\hat{x},x)\delta_{\hat{x}}
where
\hat{x}\sim x
as in the definition of prodigy. The definition implies that for any

,
\hat{x}\neq\hat{y}
, hence

restricted to its image is invertible and

\|T_{j}^{-1}\|_{\rm op}=\frac{1}{\min_{x\in P_{j}}|p(x,\hat{x})|}\geq\frac{1}{p_{*}},

If
f_{\leq j}=f_{1}+\ldots+f_{j}
, we get for any

,

(A_{G}-\lambda)f(\hat{x})=p(\hat{x},x)f(x)+(A_{G}-\lambda)f_{\leq j}(\hat{x})

or, equivalently if
\Pi_{j+1}
is the projection onto the image of

\Pi_{j+1}(A_{G}-\lambda)f=T_{j+1}f_{j+1}+\Pi_{j+1}(A_{G}-\lambda)f_{\leq j}.

Since

and

is the center of

\|(A_{G}-\lambda)f\|\leq(|I|/2)\|f\|
. Also,
\|A_{G}-\lambda\|_{\rm op}\leq 2\|A_{G}\|_{\rm op}

\displaystyle\|f_{j+1}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|T^{-1}_{j+1}\|_{\rm op}\left(\|(A_{G}-\lambda)f\|+\|(A_{G}-\lambda)f_{\leq j}\|\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{|I|\|f\|}{p_{*}}\left(\frac{1}{2}+1\!\!{\sf I}_{j\geq 1}\frac{2\|A_{G}\|_{\rm op}}{p_{*}}\frac{\alpha^{j}}{\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-1}}\right),

\|f_{\leq j}\|^{2}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{j}\|f_{k}\|^{2}\leq\left(\frac{\|f\||I|}{p_{*}}\right)^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{j}\alpha^{2(k-1)}\leq\left(\frac{\|f\||I|}{p_{*}}\right)^{2}\frac{\alpha^{2j}}{\alpha^{2}-1}.

(15)

This proves (

Hence, if

then

Lemma 2
.

Let
A
be a self-adjoint operator on an Hilbert space
H
and let
P
be the orthogonal projection onto a closed vector space
V
. Then for any closed interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
, we have

H_{A}(I)\cap V\subset H_{PAP}(I),

where for self-adjoint
T
with resolution of identity
E_{T}
,
H_{T}(I)=E_{T}(I)H
is the invariant subspace of the spectral interval
I
. If
H
has finite dimension, the conclusion holds for any set
I\subset\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Let us first assume that
A
has point spectrum:
A=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}E_{A}(\{\lambda_{k}\})
. Then, if
S
is the subset of eigenvalues in
I
, we have

H_{A}(I)\cap V=\bigoplus_{k\in S}H_{A}(\{\lambda_{k}\})\cap V.

Now a vector
H_{A}(\{\lambda_{k}\})
is the closed subspace of eigenfunctions with eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}
, that is
Af=\lambda_{k}f
. In particular, if
f\in H_{A}(\{\lambda_{k}\})\cap V
, then
PAPf=\lambda_{k}f
. Hence
f\in H_{PAP}(\{\lambda_{k}\})
. It follows that
H_{A}(\{\lambda_{k}\})\cap V\subset H_{PAP}(\{\lambda_{k}\})
(it is even equal) and

H_{A}(I)\cap V\subset\bigoplus_{k\in S}H_{PAP}(\{\lambda_{k}\})\subset H_{PAP}(I).

We now assume that
A
has continuous spectrum. We partition the interval
I=[a,b]
into
n
intervals
[\lambda_{k},\lambda_{k+1})
,
\lambda_{k}=a+(b-a)k/n
,
k=0,\ldots,n-1
(to be a proper partition, the last interval is
[b-1/n,b]
). We set
I_{k}=[\lambda_{k},\lambda_{k+1}]
and
A_{n}=\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}E_{A}(I_{k})
. By construction
H_{A_{n}}(I)=H_{A}(I)
and from what precedes, for each
n\geq 1
,

H_{A}(I)\cap V\subset H_{PA_{n}P}(I).

It remains to prove that
\cap_{n}H_{PA_{n}P}(I)\subset H_{PAP}(I)
.
By construction,
\|A_{n}-A\|\leq 1/n
(we use here that
A
has continuous spectrum to guarantee that
E_{A}(\{\lambda_{k}\})=0
). Hence, since
\|P\|\leq 1
, we have
\|PA_{n}P-PAP\|\leq 1/n
. We deduce from
[
29
, Theorem VIII.23]
that for any
f\in H
,
\mu^{f}_{PA_{n}P}\to\mu^{f}_{PAP}
weakly where
\mu^{f}_{T}
is the spectral measure of self-adjoint
T
at vector
f
. In particular, since
\mathbb{R}\backslash I
is open, from Portemanteau Theorem,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mu^{f}_{PA_{n}P}(\mathbb{R}\backslash I)\geq\mu^{f}_{PAP}(\mathbb{R}\backslash I).

It implies that if
\mu^{f}_{PA_{n}P}(\mathbb{R}\backslash I)=0
for all
n
, then
\mu^{f}_{PAP}(\mathbb{R}\backslash I)=0
. We finally recall that for
T
self-adjoint,

H_{T}(I)=\{f\in H:\mu^{f}_{T}(\mathbb{R}\backslash I)=0\}.

The inclusion
\cap_{n}H_{PA_{n}P}(I)\subset H_{PAP}(I)
follows.

For a general self-adjoint operator
A
, we may decompose orthogonally
A
and
E_{A}
into pure point and continuous parts. It remains to use the two above cases. ∎

denotes the orthocomplement. Since
\mathcal{F}
is contained in the sum of
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
,
\mathcal{B}
and the
\mathcal{L}_{j}
’s, we deduce from (
12
) that

\dim(\mathcal{F})\leq\dim(\mathcal{F}^{\prime})+\dim(\mathcal{B})+\sum_{j}\dim(\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{L}_{j}).

Also, by Lemma
2
,
\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{L}_{j}\subset\mathcal{F}_{j}
where
\mathcal{F}_{j}
is the vector space spanned by eigenvectors of
A_{G_{j}}
with eigenvalues in
I
. Since
A_{G_{j}}
is also in
\mathcal{A}_{\rho}
,
\mathcal{F}_{j}
is a closed invariant subspace. In particular from (
12
),
\dim(\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{L}_{j})\leq\dim(\mathcal{F}_{j})=\mathbb{E}[\mu_{A_{G_{j}}}^{\delta_{o}}(I)1\!\!{\sf I}(o\in L_{j})]
. It is thus sufficient to prove that
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
is trivial.

At this stage, we can reproduce the proof of Lemma
1
. ∎

Remark 1

As explained in the preamble of Section

Monotone block labelling.

We now present an extension of Theorem
4
for labelling of blocks of vertices instead of individual vertices. This extension will be used in the proof of Theorem
3
and in the forthcoming Subsection
4.3
. We first extend Definition
2
to vertex partitions:

Definition 3
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a countable set,
G=(V,E)
be a graph and
\{V_{b}\}_{b\in\mathcal{B}}
a partition of
V
into blocks indexed by
\mathcal{B}
. For
b,c\in\mathcal{B}
, we write
b\sim c
if there exist
x\in V_{b}
and
y\in V_{c}
such that
x\sim y
. For a given map
\eta:\mathcal{B}\to\mathbb{Z}
, we partition the blocks into three disjoint subsets: a block
b\in\mathcal{B}
is

(i)

Prodigy, if there exists
\hat{b}\sim b
such that
\eta(\hat{b})<\eta(b)
and for all
c\sim\hat{b}
,
c\neq b
,
\eta(c)<\eta(b)
;

(ii)

Level, if
b
is not a prodigy and for all
c\sim b
,
\eta(c)\leq\eta(b)
;

(iii)

Bad otherwise.

We say that a vertex
x\in V
is prodigy/level/bad if it belongs to a block with this property. For
x\in V_{b}
, let
\hat{\eta}(x)=(b,\eta(b))\in\mathcal{B}\times\mathbb{Z}
. If
(G,o)
is a unimodular marked graph, we say that
\hat{\eta}
is an invariant block labelling, if the marked graph
(G,\hat{\eta},o)
is unimodular.

Note that the blocks of the partition are not necessarily finite. As above, let
(G,o)
with
G=(V,E,p)
be a unimodular weighted rooted graph as defined in Section
2
.
We have the following extension of Theorem
4
to invariant block labelling.

Theorem 5
.

Let
(G,o)
a unimodular weighted rooted graph and
\hat{\eta}
an invariant block labelling with
n
distinct values and with partition
\{V_{b}\}_{b\in\mathcal{B}}
. Let
K,p_{*}
be positive real numbers. Assume that a.s.
\|A_{G}\|_{\rm op}\leq K
. For all prodigy
b\in\mathcal{B}
, let
p_{b}=(p(G,x,y))_{x\in V_{\hat{b}},y\in V_{b}}
with
\hat{b}
as in Definition
3
(i) and assume that it defines a.s. an invertible map
\ell^{2}(V_{b})\to\ell^{2}(V_{\hat{b}})
whose inverse has operator norm at most
p_{*}^{-1}
. Let
\alpha=3K/p_{*}
and
\kappa=3K\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-1}\geq 6\sqrt{2}K
. For any closed interval

of length at most
\kappa\alpha^{-n}
, we have

\mathbb{E}[\mu^{\delta_{o}}_{G}(I)]\leq\mathbb{P}(o\in B)+\sum_{j}\mathbb{E}[\mu^{\delta_{o}}_{G_{j}}(I)1\!\!{\sf I}(o\in L_{j})],

where
B
is the set of bad vertices,
L_{j}
the set of level vertices with label
j
and
G_{j}
is the restriction of
G
to
L_{j}
.

Proof.

The proof is a repetition of the proofs of Lemma
1
and Theorem
4
. The closed vector spaces
\mathcal{B}
,
\mathcal{L}
,
\mathcal{P}_{j}
,
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
are as in the proofs of Lemma
1
and Theorem
4
. The goal is to prove that
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
is trivial. The only difference is that the operator
T_{j}
on
\mathcal{P}_{j}
defined below (
14
) is now given by the formula, for
x\in V_{b}
and
b
prodigy with label

,
T_{j}\delta_{x}=p_{b}\delta_{x}\in\ell^{2}(V_{\hat{b}})
. Let
\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{j}
be the image of the operator

. By Definition

, if
b\neq c
are prodigy then
\hat{b}\neq\hat{c}
. Hence
\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{j}=\oplus_{b\in P_{j}}\ell^{2}(V_{\hat{b}})
and

decomposes diagonally on this direct sum. We deduce by assumption that

4
Some applications to transitive and quasi-transitive graphs

In this section we prove the results stated in introduction. Since Theorem
3
implies Theorem
2
which implies Theorem
1
, it is sufficient to prove Theorem
3
. However, for the clarity of the exposition, we start with a proof of Theorem
1
.

4.1
Proof of Theorem
1

Let
G_{0}={\mathrm{Cay}}(\Gamma,S)
and
G=(G_{0},p)
the weighted graph associated to
p\in\mathbb{C}[\Gamma]
as in Example
1
, with
G=G_{1}
. We give a probabilistic proof. With
k
as in the statement of the theorem, for an integer
n\geq 2k
, let
\omega\in\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
be a uniform random variable.

Now, let
\phi\in\mathrm{hom}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})
be as in Definition
1
and set, for
x\in\Gamma
,
\eta(x)=\phi(x)+\omega\;\mathrm{mod}(n)
. This defines a random labelling of
\Gamma
with
n
distinct values. This labelling is invariant. Indeed, for any
x,y\in\Gamma
, we have
\eta(xy)=\phi(x)+\phi(y)+\omega\;\mathrm{mod}(n)
. However, for any integer
j
,
j+\omega\;\mathrm{mod}(n)
has the same distribution as
\omega
. In particular, for any
y\in\Gamma
, the array
(\eta(xy))_{x\in\Gamma}
is equal in distribution to
(\eta(x))_{x\in\Gamma}
, the invariance of
\eta
follows.

Next, observe from the definition that
\phi(a^{-1})=-k
and
|\phi(b)|<k
for all
b\in S\backslash\{a^{-1},a\}
. Hence, if
x\in\Gamma
has label
\eta(x)=j\geq 2k
, then it has a neighbor
\hat{x}=a^{-1}x
with label
\eta(\hat{x})=j-k
and the other neighbors
y
of
\hat{x}
have labels
j-2k
(for
y=a^{-2}x
) or in
\{j-2k+1,\ldots,j-1\}
(for
y=ba^{-1}x
,
b\in S\backslash\{a,a^{-1}\}
). Hence
x
is a prodigy. If
\eta(x)\in\{0,\ldots,2k-1\}
then
x
could be bad and this occurs with probability
2k/n
since
\omega
is uniform. From Theorem
4
, we get that if
I\subset\sigma(p)
is a closed interval of length at most
\kappa\alpha^{-n}
, we have

\mu_{p}(I)=\mu^{\delta_{\mathrm{e}}}_{G_{1}}(I)\leq\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{e}\hbox{ is bad})\leq\frac{2k}{n},

(this bound is also trivially true for
n<2k
). We take

n=\lfloor\frac{\ln(\kappa/|I|)}{\ln\alpha}\rfloor\geq\frac{\ln(\kappa/|I|)}{\ln\alpha}-1=\frac{\ln(\kappa/(\alpha|I|))}{\ln\alpha}.

Since
\kappa/\alpha\geq(6\sqrt{2}K)/(3K/p_{*})=\beta
, the conclusion follows. ∎

4.2
Proof of Theorem
3

Let
\phi\in\mathrm{hom}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})
be as in Definition
1
. Recall
G_{0}
and
G=G_{3}
be as in Example
3
. Since we have assumed that a.s.
p_{g^{-1}}(x)_{|V_{1}\times V}=p_{g}(gx)_{|V\times V_{1}}^{*}=0
for all
g\in S
such that
\phi(g)>0
, we can remove from
G_{0}
all edges of the form
e=((gx,u),(x,v))
with
x\in\Gamma
,
u\in V
and
v\in V_{1}
and their inverses
e^{-1}=((x,v),(gx,u))
. We consider the deterministic partition of

indexed by
\mathcal{B}=\Gamma\times\{0,1\}
,
\{V_{x,\epsilon}\}_{(x,\epsilon)\in\mathcal{B}}
given by
V_{x,\epsilon}=\{(x,v):v\in V_{\epsilon}\}=V_{\epsilon}\times\{x\}
.

The proof is very similar to the proof of Theorem
1
. For integer
n\geq 2k
, set for
(x,\epsilon)\in\mathcal{B}
,
\eta(x,\epsilon)=\phi(x)+\omega\;\mathrm{mod}(n)
where
\omega
is uniformly random and independent of
P=(p_{g}(x))_{g\in S,x\in\Gamma}\in M_{V}^{S\times\Gamma}(\mathbb{C})
. This defines a random block labelling with
n
distinct values. Arguing as in the proof of Theorem

, this block labeling is invariant.

By definition
\phi(a)=-\phi(a^{-1})=k
and
|\phi(g)|<k
for all
g\in S\backslash\{a,a^{-1}\}
. If the block

\hat{b}=(a^{-1}x,0)
with label
\eta(\hat{b})=j-k
and the other block neighbors
c
of
\hat{b}
have labels

(for

),
j-k
(for

is a prodigy. On the other hand, if

with

whose labels are in

We notice finally that for any

and optimizing

4.3
Indicable groups

In this subsection, we discuss the situation when
\Gamma
is indicable but when the assumptions of Theorem
1
are not fulfilled for
S={\mathrm{supp}}(p)
,
p=p^{*}\in\mathbb{C}[\Gamma]
. We notably have the following:

Theorem 6
.

Let
\Gamma
be an indicable group and
S=S^{-1}
be a finite symmetric generating set of
\Gamma
. There exist
p=p^{*}\in\mathbb{C}[\Gamma]
with support
S
and constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that for any interval
I\subset\sigma(p)
, we have

\mu_{p}(I)\leq\frac{c_{1}}{\ln(c_{2}/|I|)}.

In particular,
\mu_{p}
has no atom.

Proof.

Fix a surjective
\phi\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})
. Let
S^{+}_{0}\subset S
be the set of
a\in S
such that
\phi(a)=\max_{s\in S}\phi(s)
. Note that
k=\max_{s\in S}\phi(s)\geq 1
since
S
generates
\Gamma
. We set
S^{+}_{0}=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{d}\}
for some arbitrary ordering. We claim that if
p=p^{*}\in\mathbb{C}[\Gamma]
has support
S
and

|p_{a_{1}}|>\sum_{i=2}^{d}|p_{a_{i}}|

(16)

then
\mu_{p}
has logarithmic regularity (where the sum over an empty set is zero by convention). If
d=1
, then the statement is already proved in Theorem
1
.

We assume
d\geq 2
. Let
n\geq 2k
be an integer,
\omega\in\mathcal{B}=\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
be uniformly distributed and
\eta(x)=\phi(x)+\omega\;\mathrm{mod}(n)
as in the proof of Theorem
1
. For
b\in\mathcal{B}
, let
V_{b}\subset\Gamma
be the subset of elements of
\Gamma
such that
\eta(x)=b
. This defines an invariant block labelling in the sense of Definition
3
where
b\in\mathcal{B}
has label
b
. If
b\geq 2k
, setting
\hat{b}=b-k
, we see that
b
is prodigy and that the probability that
\mathrm{e}
is bad is at most
2k/n
. Moreover, the restriction
p_{b}
of
\lambda(p)
from
\ell^{2}(V_{b})
to
\ell^{2}(V_{\hat{b}})
is the restriction from
\ell^{2}(V_{b})
to
\ell^{2}(V_{\hat{b}})
of

\lambda(q)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}p_{a_{i}^{-1}}\lambda(a^{-1}_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\bar{p}_{a_{i}}\lambda(a^{-1}_{i}).

Observe that
\lambda(a^{-1}_{i})
defines an isometry from
\ell^{2}(V_{b})
to
\ell^{2}(V_{\hat{b}})
. In particular, from the triangle inequality, for any

\|p_{b}f\|=\|\lambda(q)f\|\geq(|p_{a_{1}}|-\sum_{i=2}^{d}|p_{a_{i}}|)\|f\|
and, under the condition (

is invertible with bounded inverse. The claim follows from an application of Theorem
5
. ∎

Remark 2
