Factors influencing the utilisation of maternal healthcare services among reproductive-age women in Tabora region, Tanzania: an analysis of a baseline survey data from a clinical trial
Authors: Careen Magehema, Peter Binyaruka, Esther Gwae Kimaro, Sally M Mtenga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMaternal health service utilisation is poor in many low- and middle-income countries, contributing to high maternal mortality rates. This study aimed to identify the key factors influencing the use of maternal health services among reproductive-age women in Tabora, Tanzania.MethodsSecondary data from a stepped-wedge clinical trial design were used. Baseline data were collected in November and December 2017 throughout Tabora. Data from 958 women aged 15–49 years in Tabora were analysed for this study. Variables analysed included maternal age, marital status, religion, household wealth status, household size, ethnicity, parity, antenatal care (ANC) visits, facility deliveries, facility distance and location (rural–urban). Descriptive analysis was used to assess the demographic characteristics of respondents and multivariate logistic regression was used to assess factors associated with the utilisation of maternal health services.ResultsAchieving four or more ANC visits (ANC4+), was associated with high maternal age category (36–50 years) as compared with those aged <20 years (OR=3.13 (95% CI 1.56 to 6.31)) and least poor household wealth status (OR=1.63 (95% CI 1.11 to 2.39)) as compared with the poorest household wealth status. Facility-based delivery was associated with married women (OR=1.76 (95% CI 1.15 to 2.71)) as compared with women who were not married, low parity (<5) (OR=0.85 (95% CI 0.78 to 0.93)) as compared with high parity (>5), ethnicity (Nyamwezi) (OR=2.01 (95% CI 1.34 to 3.11)) as compared with other ethnic groups in Tabora, ANC4+ (OR=1.96 (95% CI 1.44 to 2.67)) as compared with less than four ANC visits, household wealth status; least poor (OR=2.8 (95% CI 1.81 to 4.33)) as compared with poorest household wealth status and distance (<5 km) to facility (OR=0.91 (95% CI 0.89 to 0.94)) as compared with ≥5 km. Postnatal care visits were associated with facility-based delivery (OR=6.76 (95% CI 4.71 to 9.69)) as compared with non-facility-based deliveries. Increased antenatal visits were associated with higher likelihoods of facility deliveries, which in turn led to higher postnatal care attendance.ConclusionsThe findings highlight the need for targeted interventions to address the factors influencing service utilisation to improve overall maternal and child health outcomes.

Introduction

Maternal healthcare service utilisation remains a challenge particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), requiring consistent evidence to inform appropriate actions for improvement. In alignment with Sustainable Development Goal 3, a specific goal has been set to reduce the global maternal mortality ratio (MMR) to less than 70 deaths per 100 000 live births by 2030 and newborn deaths up to 12 deaths per 1000 live births.1 The utilisation of maternal health services is almost universal in developed countries but relatively poor in LMICs. In the developed countries, the utilisation for four or more antenatal care visits (ANC4+), facility-based delivery (FBD) and postnatal contact within 48 hours of delivery (postnatal care, PNC), were reported to be more than 80%.2 The adherence to the perinatal continuum was reported low in a study conducted for LMICs, explaining that only 36.7% received all three services (ANC4+, FBD and PNC).3 The study further identified factors for their findings to be linked to education, wealth and place of residence, facility distance, employment and media exposure. Key factors influencing the utilisation of these maternal health services globally include maternal age, gender, culture, literacy, education and proximity to healthcare facilities, media exposure, socioeconomic status, residence, employment and income.4

Only half of childbirths in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) are attended by skilled birth attendants in health facilities, and PNC utilisation is low with high disparities among the region. It was reported that 52.5% of women utilise PNC services for the SSA, but vary within the region with the highest being 73.5% in Central Africa and the lowest being 31.7% in Eastern Africa. The factors associated with this finding were reported to include region, residential place, maternal age, education, media exposure, ANC visits, access to healthcare facility and place of delivery.56 ANC utilisation is also a challenge in SSA with slightly over half (58.4%) population of the women achieve at least four ANC visits. There has also been a variation by regions where 54.7% in East Africa and 60% in West Africa achieved ANC4+ visits. Factors such as education, health insurance, income and geographic access were reported to influence the utilisation of ANC services in these countries.5 Maternal and newborn deaths that occur due to either treatable or preventable factors like infectious diseases and complications during pregnancy and childbirth can be avoided by women adhering to the maternal healthcare service utilisation.6

In Tanzania, maternal health outcomes are poor. There has been some slow progress in the reduction of maternal deaths from 1744 in 2018, 1657 in 2019, 1640 in 2020, 1588 in 2021 and 1477 in 2022.7 MMR in Tanzania is reported to be 104 deaths per 100 000 live births.8 Literature reports that 65% of women attended ANC4+, 81.4% of deliveries occurred in health facilities and an average PNC use of 50.7%.89 Utilisation rates for maternal health services like ANC4+, FBD and PNC are suboptimal, especially in underserved regions like Tabora.8 Tanzania Demographic and Health Survey (TDHS) showed that Tabora, one of the underserved regions in the country, faces significant challenges, including low education levels, high poverty and poor maternal health service utilisation.8 The prevalence of the three selected maternal health services (ANC4+, FBD and PNC) in Tabora are 50%, 73% and 39%, respectively.8 Given that a significant share of women is not accessing maternal health services, more efforts are needed to improve service utilisation.

One approach for action is the identification of factors that hinder fully maternal health service utilisation. A study comprising 58 LMICs has identified the determinants associated with maternal health services utilisation,10 but limited information exists for these factors in underserved or disadvantaged regions. Little information on the determinants for maternal health services utilisation is known for Tabora region. By using the case study of Tabora region, this paper aimed to identify the determinants for maternal health services utilisation among women of reproductive age (WRA) in Tabora in order to inform the design of effective strategies to improve these maternal health service utilisation and in the long-term contribute to the reduction of maternal and child mortalities.

Materials and methods

Study setting

The study setting was Tabora region in Tanzania. Tabora is one of the 31 administrative regions in Tanzania, which covers eight districts, namely Igunga, Kaliua, Nzega District and Nzega Urban, Tabora Urban, Urambo, Uyui and Sikonge. It is the largest region by area (76 151 km2) located at the central-western part of Tanzania. According to the 2022 national census, the region has a population of 3 391 679 with multiple ethnic groups with Nyamwezi as the natives.11 Tabora is one of the underserved regions in Tanzania. It is located in the lowest wealth quintile; 15% of total households reside in urban areas; 94% of total households in Tabora are in poverty with mean household size of 10 individuals and high dependency ratio (128); the literacy rate is 56% which may affect awareness on the access and utilisation of health services.12 These characteristics of Tabora region compelled the designing and implementation of this study.

Study design

The study used dataset from the Tabora Maternal and Newborn Initiative (TAMANI) project that was conducted in Tabora region from 2017 to 2021. The TAMANI project was a 5-year cluster-randomised stepped wedge trial design project to assess the effectiveness and change in outcome of a multicomponent intervention on indicators of maternal and child health services.13 Specifically, TAMANI’s objective was to contribute to reducing maternal and neonatal mortality by increasing the coverage, quality of care and utilisation of reproductive, maternal and newborn health services across Tabora region. The key activities that TAMANI project implemented were training and mentoring of frontline healthcare providers on emergency obstetric care; equipping and rehabilitating health facilities; and providing emergency transportations to support pregnant and post partum women. The project is better explained elsewhere.13 The project captured quantitative and qualitative data for both men and women with ages 15–49 years. However, only the quantitative component of the baseline data for women aged 15–49 years from the dataset was analysed in this study in order to obtain results which are not contaminated with the TAMANI project implementation to best fit the purpose of the study. This present study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines, and the completed checklist is provided as online supplemental file.

Participants

This study population comprised WRA, WRA (15–49 years) residing in Tabora region; WRA who had given birth 5 years prior to the implementation of TAMANI project.

Sampling procedures and sample size

Sample size was carried down from the original study (TAMANI) where cluster-randomisation sampling design at district level was used, with the village as a Primary Sampling Unit (cluster). A total of 6400 households were sampled from the village listing that was obtained from the Tabora regional authorities, where eligible participants were surveyed. The survey involved both adolescent boys and girls and adult men and women, with ages 15–49 years. However, this present study dealt with the females’ survey only. TAMANI study included a sample of 7882 WRA who were surveyed. Of these, 2322 did not give birth in the 5 years prior to the TAMANI survey and 5 had missing outcome therefore were excluded. 5555 women reported giving birth in the 5 years prior to the survey and therefore were eligible for inclusion in the analysis for this present study. Out of 5555 eligible women that were included in this present study, 2797 had missing data on all the outcome indicators (ANC4+, FBD and PNC) and so were excluded from the analysis. Further, to the 2758 data that remained, purposive sampling was performed to obtain most eligible respondents for this present study where specific questions about ANC visits, place of delivery and PNC visits were responded, as well as restriction of data for only baseline phase survey ended with analytical sample of 958 WRA.

Data collection

Data for the TAMANI project in five rounds was collected using a standardised, structured, pretested and validated demographic and health survey questionnaire in a Tanzanian local language (Kiswahili). The questions captured demographic information and history including mother’s age, education and income level, skilled birth attendance in their deliveries, place of delivery, number of ANC and PNC visits, total number of children (parity), distance to health facilities and residential location (rural–urban). The baseline data collection for the TAMANI project was conducted in November and December 2017 throughout Tabora region. The TAMANI survey considered the most recent birth history, that is, participants reported maternal service utilisation for their most recent birth that occurred within 5 years prior to the survey. Several explanatory variables like marital status, residence, facility distance and wealth status were measured at the time of survey rather than at the time of maternal services utilisation thus were assumed to approximate conditions at time of service utilisation. Data for this present study was retrieved from the TAMANI dataset after a permit was granted by the project’s management team. Data cleaning involved restricting data to baseline phase (round 1), females only, age 15–49 years, and those who gave birth 5 years before data collection, data for answered questions on ANC4+, FBD and PNC. This study and the choice for study variables was guided by the WHO social determinants of health framework which has been shown to be adequate in guiding the understanding of the relationship between various socioeconomic and population characteristics and their health outcome.14

Outcome variables

Three outcome variables were considered: ANC4+, where at least four or more antenatal visits were assessed, FBD assessed all births that occurred in health facilities and PNC measured whether or not a woman has received PNC check-ups in the first 48 hours after giving birth. These indicator variables are important indicators for the maternal and child health outcome in the TDHS in 2015/2016 and 2022. In ANC4+, the measure was the likelihood of respondents attending four or more ANC visits in their pregnancies 5 years ago regardless of whether these were early, on time or late during pregnancy. This indicator was categorised into two categories, those who attended four or more ANC visits (≥4 visits) and those who attended less than four ANC visits (<4 visits). FBD also measured the likelihood of women to have their births in a health facility relative to the likelihood of that not happening. The categories for this indicator were ‘Yes’ for births occurred in health facilities and ‘No’ for births that occurred elsewhere. For PNC, the time for first postnatal check was measured. Women who had received postnatal check within 48 hours postdelivery (category ‘Yes’), and those who did not receive any postnatal check within these 48 hours (category ‘No’), were assessed.

Explanatory variables

The WHO social determinants of health framework guided the choice of the independent (explanatory) variables assessed in this study. Factors assessed for an association with each of the outcome variables in the respective categories included individual, household and area-based level factors. Individual level factors included women’s age categorised into three categories (<20, 20–35 and 36–50), women’s marital status (Married/Not married), parity categorised into two categories (<4 children and ≥4 children), education level attained categorised into three levels (No education/Primary education/above primary education), attending four or more ANC visits and FBD. Household level factors included household wealth status. This status was computed using the principal component analysis based on 42 components, where wealth index was generated.15 Households were then ranked according to their wealth index scores and categorised into three equal-sized terciles (poorest, middle and least poor) defined based on three aspects, asset ownership, housing type and access to services like electricity and water. Other factors were household size in binary categories (<5 household members and ≥5 household members), and religion type categorised into a binary category (Christian/Non-Christian) and ethnicity which was categorised into three categories, two of which are the majority ethnic groups and the minority were labelled as others (Nyamwezi/Sukuma/Others). Area-based factors include residential location in two categories, women that reported to reside either in the rural or urban parts of their respective districts (rural district-urban district) and distance to health facility (<5 and ≥5 km).

Data analysis

Descriptive analysis for demographic information was performed and results obtained were expressed in percentage and average values. A multivariate logistic regression model was performed for each of the three outcome variables: ANC4+, FBD and PNC, where each outcome variable was examined with multiple explanatory variables to jointly identify significant (p<0.05 at α=5%) determinants for utilisation of each of the three services. This helped to avoid complexity in results interpretation by examining the relationship between multiple variables simultaneously while accounting for potential confounders to provide robust results. The results provided ORs for the utilisation of the outcome variables assessed. The analysis was conducted using STATA V.16.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not directly involved in the design, conduct, reporting and dissemination plans of this study.

Discussion

The study focuses on the utilisation of maternal health services in Tanzania, specifically in Tabora, a region with significant challenges in this area. The lack of knowledge on key factors associating with maternal health service utilisation makes it difficult to design and implement effective interventions in this region. Although there has been a decrease in maternal mortality over the years, we found in this study that around average of women attend ANC4+ visits (62.3%) and deliver in health facilities (68.7%) and only 48.9% of women had a postnatal check in the 48 hours after delivery. These findings on the utilisation of these maternal health services are in line with literature from a demographic and health survey conducted in Tanzania, 2022.8

Different factors have been suggested by other studies as to have an association with the utilisation of these maternal health services. A study conducted in SSA identified region, residential place, maternal age, education, media exposure, ANC visits, place of delivery and access to healthcare facility as factors associating with the utilisation of PNC utilisation.16 A different study found that education, health insurance, income and geographic access influenced the utilisation of ANC services.5 These factors affect the utilisation of maternal health services differently across the world therefore it is important to identify factors from different parts of the world, especially the underserved and work tirelessly to address them so that maternal health outcomes are improved.

This study found that older women were more likely to attend ANC4+ visits as compared with younger women. Similar finding from other study explains that older women are often more health-conscious and aware of the risks associated with pregnancy, especially at an advanced maternal age, which may motivate older women to seek regular ANC services to ensure theirs’ and their babies’ health is in good condition16; meanwhile younger women may avoid ANC visits due to fear of societal judgement or failure to identify early signs of pregnancy.17 Similarly, findings from other studies showed that increasing maternal age associates positively with ANC4+ visits.1819 However, other studies showed that older women had an increased risk of not making ANC4+ visits compared with younger women due to a greater confidence and having experience from earlier pregnancies that makes them not attend ANC visits,20 but others revealed the lack of any association.21 This could mean that age is an important factor to be observed for targeted intervention so that women of any reproductive age know the importance of ANC services and utilise them to improve their maternal health outcomes. Another study conducted in Tabora revealed several other factors such as spouse involvement in maternal health which improved ANC visits and attitude of healthcare providers which explained that poor attitudes from healthcare workers negatively impacted the ANC visits.22

Women from wealthier backgrounds were found more likely to attend ANC4+ visits compared with those from poorer backgrounds. Our findings could be explained by women from wealthier backgrounds have higher odds of attending ANC4+ visits due to fewer economic barriers, such as transportation and health insurance costs, compared with women from poorer backgrounds.23 Also, women who cannot afford transportation fee to health facilities and health insurance may be at a higher risk of not using health services.24 This finding is consistent with findings from other studies,23 showing that women received poor quality care because they were poor and could not afford better service delivery. Contrary to the study in rural Ghana which found no association between wealth status and number of ANC visits.20

Married women were found to have more facility deliveries than unmarried women. One study conducted in Malawi found that women who marry at young age were less likely to use FBD due to traditional norms and financial constraints, which could also explain the finding in our study.25 Another study conducted in Nigeria25 had similar findings to ours. This similar finding further explained that women whose marriage partners had postsecondary education and were <50 years supported them to achieve FBD.

Women who attended four or more ANC visits were more likely to deliver at health facilities. This finding could be explained as ANC visits provides health education, vaccinations and fetal check-ups that inform good health practices for mother and children, which in turn could help influence the decision to give birth in a health facility. This finding is consistent with results from other studies conducted in Tanzania.26–28 However, in rural Ghana, studies have shown that there is a high ANC utilisation but low facility deliveries, partly because women could not identify labour signs thus ended up delivering at home.29–31

It was found in this study that parity associated with FBD. The finding could be explained as women with fewer children are more likely to deliver in health facilities, whereas those with higher parity may choose home deliveries due to greater confidence in their ability to manage childbirth safely or that lack of financial aid to assist them in accessing health facilities and affording basic healthcare. One study in Tanzania revealed a similar pattern of low rate of FBD among women with high parity and living in rural areas.32 A similar pattern was also observed in a cross-country study involving three countries, Tanzania, Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) and Kenya, which showed that high parity resulted in poor utilisation of FBD in Kenya and DRC but not Tanzania.33 This literature introduces possibilities of variations in parity effect on FBD in different parts of Tanzania, and that further information to understand how parity affects utilisation of FBD is needed.

We found that ethnicity was associated with FBD where the majority ethnic group (Nyamwezi, 38%) made the most deliveries in health facilities as compared with the Sukuma and others. The contribution of ethnicity to FBD utilisation may exist due to differences in cultural beliefs, norms and practices among different tribes in the community, which could as a result affect maternal health service utilisation. A study conducted in Morogoro found that the Sukuma ethnic group was less likely to deliver in health facilities, which is also the case for our study.34 The Sukuma believe in traditional religion (praying to ancestors) for treatment and healing, which may be different from the Nyamwezi that could explain the findings of this study.

We also found that women living closer to health facilities are more likely to make their deliveries at these facilities. Economic and logistical barriers, such as high transportation costs, may prevent many women from accessing healthcare, and this could explain the findings in this study. Other studies concur with our findings.3536

FBD was found to be a significant determinant of PNC utilisation. This could be explained as women who deliver in health facilities are more likely to receive postnatal services, potentially due to the education and support received during facility-based care.9 From our findings, the prevalence of utilisation of maternal health services was found to be average (62.3%, 68.7%) for ANC4+ and FBD respectively, but below average (48.9%) for PNC. This means that over a third of pregnant women do not receive full benefits of optimal ANC, non-facility deliveries present potential risk for not receiving postnatal check-ups which could miss the identification of complications during childbirth such as infections and delay treatment of complications, leading to maternal and newborn deaths. It is essential that care is integrated across these stages as it would ensure that women who attend ANC and achieve the recommendation of at least four visits are also supported during delivery and postpartum.

Strengths and limitations of the study

The main strength of this study is the use of a regional-representative dataset for identification of factors associating with maternal health services utilisation subnationally focusing on the most underserved region. Therefore, the results can be generalised especially for women aged 15–49 years in the respective region in similar settings. However, this study had few limitations. The study employed secondary data that was primarily collected. The information provided by the respondents on the dependent variables (such as number of ANC visits and FBD) and independent variables (such as distance) was based on self-reported responses from 5 years before data collection, which increased chances for recall and social desirability biases. The timeframe selected could be reduced to at least 2 years prior to data collection to reduce chances of recall and social desirability biases. Again, the independent variables such as marital status, wealth status, residence and facility distance were measured at the time of the survey rather than at the time of service utilisation. This condition is likely to introduce temporal misclassification bias that leads to biased estimates of the results.

Conclusions

Our study underscores the multifaceted nature of determinants influencing maternal health service utilisation in the underserved region of Tabora in Tanzania. The implication for this finding in maternal health outcome is that different factors from different levels may singularly or jointly affect the access and utilisation of maternal health services, which in turn could affect the maternal health outcome altogether. Addressing these determinants through targeted policies and interventions is essential for improving maternal health outcomes in Tabora and beyond. Enhancing accessibility, affordability and quality of care, particularly for vulnerable and underserved populations through collaborating with local transport providers to address transportation barriers to reach health facilities and collaborating with government and NGOs to formulate programmes for financial assistance that could help cover delivery costs and basic care. Ensuring that facilities with delivering services are well equipped and staffed with skilled personnel that could help increase trust in facility services provision. Strengthening policies to address sociodemographic disparities, ensuring equitable access to maternal health services across different population groups like strengthening outreach programmes in rural and underserved areas to raise awareness on the importance of using maternal health services; and working with community leaders and traditional birth attendants to incorporate modern health practices into culturally-friendly contexts can pave the way towards improved health outcomes for mothers and children.
