PCK2 as a potential therapeutic target for aggressive MYC-amplified non-WNT/non-SHH medulloblastoma based on tumor continuum
Authors: Shuaishuai Xue, Zhengming Zhan, Yonghua Cai, Wei Chen, Ruizhe Chen, Yangqi Mao, Cheng Xie, Jun Liu, Ziyi Luo, Siyuan Liu, Xian Zhang, Peng Li, Hao Long, Ye Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMedulloblastoma, the most common malignant pediatric posterior fossa tumor, exhibits metabolic reprogramming and tumor immune microenvironment heterogeneity in non-WNT/non-SHH subgroups, yet the interplay between these features remains poorly defined. Revealing potential molecular features and hidden therapeutic target, we integrated the transcriptomic data of non-WNT/non-SHH medulloblastoma from GEO and EMBL-EBI databases. Through examination of the Grp3/4 transcriptional continuum, we identified PCK2 (a key gene in the TCA cycle with the highest correlation with continuum score). We validated PCK2 as a driver of proliferation, migration, invasion, glycolysis, and M2 macrophage polarization in MYC-amplified MB cells through short hairpin RNA knockdown experiments. Together, our findings establish metabolic-TIME crosstalk as a prognostic determinant and proffer PCK2 as a therapeutic target for aggressive MB subtypes, offering insights into metabolic subtyping and precision therapy strategies to improve clinical outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40478-026-02304-z.Keywords: Medulloblastoma, non-WNT/non-SHH, Metabolism, Immune Microenvironment, PCK2

Introduction

Medulloblastoma (MB), a common malignant childhood brain tumor, consists of four molecular groups: WNT-activated, SHH-activated with TP53-wildtype, SHH-activated with TP53-mutant, and non-WNT/non-SHH [1]. The non-WNT/non-SHH group, also known as group 3 and group 4 MB, is particularly aggressive, often metastasizes, and has limited treatment options [2]. Despite advances in molecular subtyping for risk assessment, outcomes remain poor, especially for patients with MYC amplification [3]. Recent studies highlight the importance of metabolic reprogramming in MB, where tumor cells exploit pathways like glycolysis, and lipid synthesis for growth and treatment resistance [4]. There is growing interest in targeting metabolic reprogramming and the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) in non-WNT/non-SHH MB as potential therapeutic strategies.

Metabolic reprogramming in medulloblastoma (MB) not only supports energy production but also drives tumor growth and resistance to therapy [5]. In Group 3 MB, increased glycolysis and mitochondrial activity are associated with MYC-driven cancer, while altered fatty acid metabolism enhances stemness and invasion in metastatic types [6, 7]. Oncometabolites like lactate boost tumor survival and create an immunosuppressive environment by affecting immune cells [8]. The complexity of non-WNT/non-SHH MB remains underexplored, necessitating further investigation into its metabolic and immune characteristics using advanced methods like RNA-seq and scRNA-seq. The TIME in medulloblastoma (MB) shows significant spatial and molecular diversity, influencing clinical outcomes [9]. SHH-subgroup tumors often have immune-rich environments with CD8 + T-cell infiltration, while non-WNT/non-SHH MB are usually “cold”, with M2 macrophages and regulatory T cells [10, 11]. The tumor is a complex mix of neural progenitor, malignant, immune, and stromal cells [12]. Tumor cells compete for nutrients like glucose and arginine, impairing immune cell function [13]. Interestingly, metabolic diversity in non-WNT/non-SHH MB may lead to unique immune features, affecting responses to checkpoint inhibitors [14]. Understanding the interplay between tumor cells and TIME is crucial.

We use multi-omics and functional assays to study the metabolic and immune diversity in non-WNT/non-SHH medulloblastoma. A continuum score-based prognostic model was constructed using metagene expression profiles, demonstrating superior predictive power for patient outcomes. Our research provides new insights into the metabolic traits of cancer cells and the complex TIME in non-WNT/non-SHH medulloblastoma, enhancing understanding of MB's heterogeneity and progression. The TCA cycle is crucial for cellular metabolism, acting as a central hub for catabolic and anabolic processes [15]. PCK2 genes are important in the TCA cycle, glycolysis, and gluconeogenesis, but their specific effects on Group 3 MB progression remain unexplored [16]. We evaluated PCK2 as a biomarker and therapeutic target in aggressive MYC-amplified medulloblastoma. Inhibiting PCK2 while downregulating MYC significantly impacts tumor growth and metastasis, suggesting PCK2 inhibitors could enhance treatment for high-risk medulloblastoma patients and improving pediatric patient quality of life.

Materials and methods

Data collection

Medulloblastoma, non-WNT/non-SHH (470 samples from GSE85217 and 223 samples from E-MTAB-10767), was obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) and Array Express database. Gene sets for 82 metabolic pathways and 2022 metabolism-related genes were sourced from the MSigDB (see Additional File Table S2) [17]. Proteomic data in MB come from GEO (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) (GSE109401) [18]. The public MYC ChIP-seq data was from the GEO database (GSE129521, GSE267470). The transcriptomic and metabolomic data are a comparison between the sh-ctrl and sh-PCK2 in D341 cell line, belonging to our self-assessment data (see Additional File Table S3).

Clinical samples and cell lines

MB tumors and normal brain samples were sourced from Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University, China. etc. (Approval number: 2025022012384788). Medulloblastoma cell lines: Daoy, D283, D341, D458 cells were cultured at 37 ℃ and 5% CO2. HEK293T and THP1 were obtained from Procell Life Science. A more detailed methods is available in the supplementary methods.

Pathway enrichment analysis

Functional analyses were conducted via Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) [19].

Immune infiltration analysis

The ESTIMATE, MCPCOUNTER, and CIBERSORT algorithms were used to infer the immune cell infiltration of MB patients [20].

Continuum score

According to methods of Steven C. Clifford [21]. A more detailed methods is available in the supplementary methods.

Generation of shPCK2 knockdown and inhibit MYC expression inhibition

HEK293T cells were transduced with shRNA vectors (sh: GGTGGCAAGCATGCGTATTAT), following the manufacturer's instructions, and packaging (pMD2.G and pSPAX2 plasmids were from Addgene) [22]. The siRNAs aimed at inhibiting MYC were constructed by Sigma. The c-Myc inhibitor 10,058-F4 (M14382; Luofa Biotechnology, China) was employed to suppress c-Myc activity.

Single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis

GSE155446 dataset underwent processing and analysis utilizing the established methodologies provided by the R packages 'Seurat v4.0.1', 'infer-CNV v1.24.0', and 'cellchat v2'. The resulting clusters were annotated using well-established cellular markers. Tumor cells were further classified into molecular subgroups based on a continuum score derived from whole-transcriptome profiles, specifically mG3 (score < 0.5) and mG4 (score > 0.5). R version 4.4.2.

Cell proliferation, migration, and colony formation assays

CCK8 and cloning formation assay were used to detect cell proliferation, scratch assay was used to detect cell migration.

Immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry

Immunofluorescence was conducted in accordance with previously established protocols [16]. Alexa fluorescent secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated A-11008, 1:5000, Invitrogen, CHINA) were used in Immunofluorescence. The primary antibody employed was anti-IBA1 (ab178846, 1:5000, Abcam, USA). Immunohistochemistry was performed using anti-PCK2 (14,892-1-AP, 1:500, Proteintech, USA) and anti-KI67 (ab16667, 1:5000, Abcam, USA) in accordance with established methodologies.

Western blotting

Western blotting was performed following the methodology outlined in our previous research [16]. The primary antibodies used included PCK2 (Proteintech, 14,892-1-AP, 1:5000), c-MYC (Proteintech, 10,828-1-AP, 1:1000), phospho-c-MYCSer62 (Cell Signaling Technology, 13,748, 1:1000), GAPDH (Proteintech, 60,004-1-Ig, 1:5000), SOX2 (PTM, ptm-5072, 1:1000), and NEUROD1 (Proteintech, 12,081-1-AP, 1:1000). The secondary antibodies employed were anti-rabbit (1:5000; CoWinBioscience, Cat:CW0103, Beijing, China) or anti-mouse IgG (1:5000; CoWinBioscience, Cat:CW0102, Beijing, China).

Medulloblastoma xenografts (intracranial)

Animal studies were conducted in compliance with the guidelines and approval of the Ethical Committee at Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University. (Approval No.: IACUC-LAC-20240308-002). Orthotopic xenograft models were established in the previous report [16]. 1 × 106 D341-Luc cells, D341-shPCK2-Luc cells, D458-Luc cells, and D458-shPCK2-Luc cells with a total 5 μl volume in PBS were engrafted intracranially into the cerebellum of anesthetized BALB/c nude mice.

Survival analysis

We applied a univariate Cox regression model to analyze the influence of TCA cycle and PCK2 expression levels on the prognosis of patients with non-WNT/non-SHH medulloblastoma. To estimate the relative risk of death among groups, hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed. Analyses specific to subtypes were conducted to assess the impact of the TCA cycle in various subtypes. The expression levels of the PCK2 were incorporated into the calculation of death risk HRs. Furthermore, to enhance interpretability, the GSVA scores were standardized using min–max scaling and then multiplied by 10 for HR prediction.

Clinical data of non-WNT/non-SHH medulloblastoma in the GSE85217 (Group3 = 113, Group4 = 264) were obtained for overall survival (OS) Kaplan–Meier curves. For Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, samples were dichotomized based on the median expression value of the gene of interest. This approach was used consistently for all survival analyses to avoid bias associated with selecting cutoffs that minimize p-values. All survival models were constructed using the R package “survival (v.3.5-5)”.

See supplementary methods for additional details for all methods.

Results

Transcriptomic continuum in non-WNT/non-SHH medulloblastoma

Due to the intertumoral and intratumoral heterogeneity of non-WNT/non-SHH medulloblastoma, we introduced a continuum score. The continuum score was calculated by applying a logistic transformation 1/(1 + exp(− x)) to the Group3 and group4 MB metagenes. The two metagenes that described Group3 and Group4 samples were notably gradated and overlapping in an anticorrelative manner (Fig. S1a). Group3 and Group4 are not distinct transcriptional entities but rather exist as a continuum between two transcriptional polarities that we refer to here as G3 and G4. Regardless, the non-WNT/non-SHH medulloblastoma subtypes may be broadly ordered upon the G3/G4 continuum in partially overlapping domains from most group 3-like to most group 4-like (Group3γ, Group3α, Group3β, Group4γ, Group4α, Group4β, respectively) (Fig. S1b). For convenient comparison, we subdivided the expression continuum (G3/G4 score) into 3 purely notional quantiles, following the reference: G3 (0 − 0.4, n = 103/453 [23%]), G4G3 (0.4–0.6, n = 81/453 [18%]), G4 (0.6–1.0, n = 269/453 [59%]) [21]. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed the presence of three distinct transcriptomic clusters in non-WNT/non-SHH medulloblastoma (Fig. S1c). Survival analysis revealed that the overall survival (OS) of the three groups was significantly different. G3 had the worst prognosis in terms of OS, while G4 had the best prognosis (Fig. S1d). Interestingly, the scale plot shows that the G4 group has more group4 samples, the G3 group has more group3 samples, and the G4G3 group has an unequal amount of group4 and group3 samples (Fig. S1e). The demographics and prognostic data of those patients are displayed in Supplementary Table 1.

The distinct metabolic patterns and TIME of three groups associated with tumor continuum

We examined the metabolic reprogramming mechanisms in non-WNT/non-SHH medulloblastoma by selecting 2022 human genes linked to 82 metabolic pathways from the KEGG database (Table S2). We used GSVA to estimate enrichment scores for 82 metabolic pathways in each sample from the GSE85217 dataset, aiming to uncover the metabolic heterogeneity of non-WNT/non-SHH medulloblastoma. In all 453 non-WNT/non-SHH medulloblastoma samples, we performed Pearson correlation assessment between the continuum score and the metabolic pathways GSVA score. G3 (p < 0.05, number of metabolic pathways = 4) exhibited upregulation in the Citrate cycle (TCA cycle). G4(p < 0.05, number of metabolic pathways = 7), was characterized by remarkable upregulation of Inositol phosphate metabolism, and Sphingolipid metabolism (Fig. 1a). To investigate the characteristics of the G3 and G4 groups, volcano plots were constructed to compare gene expression between these samples (Fig. 1b). The TCA cycle score is significantly elevated in G3, and suggesting a poor prognosis in non-WNT/non-SHH medulloblastoma (Fig. 1c). This is supported by KEGG and GSEA analyses of differentially expressed gene groups. The TCA cycle was notably enriched in G3, whereas gene sets associated with substance metabolic processes, including inositol phosphate and sphingolipid metabolism, were enriched in G4 (Fig. 1d–f).

Then, we evaluated the differences in immune infiltration among G4, G4G3, and G3. As shown in Fig. S2a, G4G3 exhibited the highest ImmuneScore, StromalScore, and ESTIMATE score, alongside the lowest tumor purity, indicating a superior immune response compared to the other groups. Single-cell transcriptome sequencing reveals the TIME in G3 and G4 patients

Based on the above results, analysis of the bulk transcriptome of non-WNT/non-SHH medulloblastoma showed differences in metabolism patterns. Next, we used 20 single-cell sequencing datasets (GSE155446) for the next step analysis (Fig. 2a). First, we determined the chromosomal copy number variants (CNVs) in each sample based on matched transcriptomic data to understand the malignancy cell subpopulation (Fig. 2b, Table S2). This image shows the results revealed subgroup according to the original classification based on NaNo-string method (Fig. 2c). Then, a total of 9 clusters were identified through Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP). Firstly, the tumor cell population with CNVs was determined to be 0–1 according to the metagene score, and the tumor cells with a score of less than 0.5 were located as malignant Group 3 cells (mG3) and the tumor cells with a score greater than 0.5 were located as malignant Group 4 cells (mG4) (Fig. 2d). Subsequently, according to the definition of marker genes as B cells (CD79A, CD22, CD79B), T cells (CD8B, CD3D, CD3E), OPC and oligodendrocyte (PDGFRA, SOX2, OLIG2), TAMs (TMEM119, CD74, A2M), Neutrophil (RETN, CDA, S100A12), Pericytes (PDGFRB), Endothelial cells (IGFBP7) (Fig. 2e).

Identification of PCK2 as a G3-MB-enriched gene and associated with tumor progression

Similar patterns were observed in external independent cohorts using data from GSE85217 and E-MATB-10767 database (Fig. 3a). In the non-WNT/non-SHH medulloblastoma, TCA cycle (Fig. S3c–d), particularly PCK2 exhibited the highest expression and activity levels in G3 group cells (Figs. 3b, S3e), which was consistent with the PCK2 expression in Group3 MB (Fig. 3c). Survival analysis indicated that non-WNT/non-SHH medulloblastoma patients with high PCK2 expression had shorter OS (Fig. 3d, p= 0.02). Group 3 MB, accompanied by MYC amplification or overexpression, often has a very poor prognosis, which often results in a worse prognosis for Group 3 than Group 4 MB (Fig. 3e). As shown in Fig. 3f, we found that in this larger dataset, PCK2 was a significant correlation with MYC in non-WNT/non-SHH medulloblastoma. According to Elisa Caffarelli's research report, when the function of MYC is inhibited, the function of many genes will also be suppressed [23]. Our data indicate that MYC binds to the transcription start site of PCK2 and regulates the expression of PCK2 (Sup Fig. 4). This is consistent with the results obtained from public datasets (Fig. 3g) [23]. Because c‐MYC is a transcriptional factor, we further investigated whether c‐MYC directly binds to the PCK2 promoter. There are two binding sites for c‐MYC, located at 956–963 and 1871–1878 of the PCK2 promoter according to JASPAR, a program to predict transcription factor–binding sites in DNA sequences (Fig. S4a). These results were supported using ChIP-Seq public data (Fig. S4b). To better evaluate the role of c-MYC in regulating PCK2 expression, we inhibited c-MYC expression in D341 and D458 cells using c-MYC siRNAs (siMYC-1, and siMYC-2). c-MYC knockdown reduced PCK2 protein expression in D341 and D458 cells. Similarly, c-MYC inhibitor (10,058-F4) reduced PCK2 protein levels in D341 cells as the concentration of the drug increased (Fig. S4c). The results suggest that c-MYC may upregulate the expression of PCK2 at the transcriptional level by directly binding to its promoter. However, the specific results still need to be further validated through experiments.

To evaluate the potential clinical utility of PCK2 as a therapeutic target. IHC staining was performed on non-WNT/non-SHH medulloblastoma and normal brain tissues. Binding the protein expression of PCK2 was found to be significantly higher in Group 3 MB tissue than in normal brain tissues and other MB subgroups (Fig. 3h, i). According to the AUC value of the ROC curve, PCK2 expression demonstrated excellent discriminatory power for distinguishing Group 3 from Group 4 tumors (AUC = 0.981) (Fig. 3j).

Deficiency of PCK2 impeded MYC-amplified MB cell proliferation and metastasis

The DAOY (SHH cell line) and D283 (Group3 cell line, non-MYC amplified) showed reduced PCK2 protein expression compared to the MYC-amplified Group3 cell lines D458 and D341 (Fig. 4a). Similarly, PCK2 gene expression is higher in Group3 cell lines than SHH group cell lines (Fig. S5a). Group3 tumor tissues exhibited higher protein levels of PCK2 and MYC compared to SHH and Group 4 MB tissues, while normal brain tissues showed relatively low expression levels of these proteins (Fig. 4b). Then, we created two PCK2-deficient Group3 cell lines, D458 and D341, to investigate PCK2's role in MB (Fig. 4c). After PCK2 knockdown, the D341 cell line exhibited significantly reduced cell growth and clonogenic capacity compared to the control group (Fig. 4d, e). To further examine the impact of PCK2 on tumor cell growth in vivo, we established PCK2-deficient D341 and D458 orthotopically transplanted models. Bioluminescence imaging (BLI) of mice with orthotopic xenografts of shPCK2 D341 and D458 cells demonstrated slower tumor growth and a reduced incidence of spinal metastasis compared to control mice with wild-type (WT) cells (Figs. 4f–h, S5b, c). Furthermore, knockdown PCK2 in D341 and D458 cells may extend animal lifespan (Figs. 4i, S5d). The PCK2-deficient group exhibited a decrease in Ki67-positive proliferating cells (Figs. 4j, S5e). To evaluate the impact of PCK2 regulation on the proliferation and migration of D458 adherent cells, we performed CCK8, colony formation assay, and wound healing assays. When PCK2 knockdown was in the D458 cell line, this cell line exhibited a reduction in the proliferation ability by CCK8 assay (Fig. 4k), decreased the colony numbers in plate and cell migration in wound healing assays (Fig. 4l, m). Together, these observations suggest that PCK2 may contribute to the malignant progression of MYC-amplified medulloblastoma.

Combined analysis of transcriptomic and metabolomic data

To better understand PCK2 effects against MYC-amplified MB, we performed transcriptomic analyses of high-MYC cell line D341 with negative control (NC) and PCK2 knockdown groups. Our results showed that PCK2 and c-MYC expression was decreased (Fig. 5a). The D458 and D341 cell lines were identified as MYC-amplified G3 models. As a principal driver of MB, c-MYC total protein and Ser62 phosphorylation levels exhibited a marked decline following PCK2 knockdown (Fig. 5b). Ser62 phosphorylation is a critical post-translational modification that stabilizes MYC protein and enhances its transcriptional activity and oncogenic function [23]. These findings demonstrated that the PCK2 knockdown inhibited the growth of MYC-amplified MB cells and decreased c-MYC stability, highlighting the potential of PCK2 as a therapeutic target for the treatment of MYC-amplified MB.

Integrated pathway analysis of transcriptomic and metabolomics data. We detected by RNAseq that 1089 genes were significantly downregulated (log2 fold change < − 1, FDR < 0.05) and 233 genes were upregulated (log2 fold change > 1, FDR < 0.05), respectively (Table S3). Moreover, GO pathway analyses showed that these genes were enriched in the metabolic process, immune system process, and developmental process (Fig. 5c). The metabolite analysis showed that there were 137 differential metabolites in all 713 metabolites (Table S3). KEGG pathway analyses showed that these differential metabolites were enriched in the immune system, nucleotide metabolism, and carbohydrate metabolism (Fig. 5d). After combining analysis of transcriptomic and metabolomic data, we find that PCK2 may regulate cell fate and TIME by influencing nucleotide metabolism and purine metabolism most likely (Fig. 5e–g). While multiple metabolic pathways were perturbed, the immune-related pathways were of particular interest given the established role of the tumor microenvironment in medulloblastoma progression and the emerging link between PCK2 and immune modulation.

Given the link of the PCK2 to the immune system, especially macrophages [16], we wanted to further explore the connections of PCK2 to macrophage polarization in MB. High expression of PCK2 in tumor cells does not modulate M1 macrophage polarization, but promotes M2 polarization in vitro (Fig. S5f, g). Phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) is a key metabolite that has been shown to regulate macrophage polarization, with elevated PEP levels promoting an M2-like phenotype associated with tumor progression. About the developmental process, Yang et al. thinks that the differentiation of tumor cells is regulated by NeuroD1 expression, which is repressed by H3K27me3 in tumor cells, and EZH2 can facilitate the differentiation of MB tumor cells by modulate H3K27me3 [24, 25]. Because of this, we found that PCK2 knockdown can promote upregulation of NEUROD1 and inhibit the expression of CD133 (PROM1) and SOX2 (Figs. 5h, S5h). We also found that after knockdown of PCK2, EZH2 expression in tumor cells decreased (Fig. S5i). It means PCK2 knockdown can promote MB tumor cell differentiation and inhibit tumor cell stemness.

Discussion

Our study systematically delineates the metabolic reprogramming features of non-WNT/non-SHH medulloblastoma (MB), revealing TCA cycle as key pathways linked to poor overall survival (OS). Despite the classical emphasis on glucose metabolism in cancer, these findings challenge the dogma by highlighting alternative metabolic dependencies in aggressive MB subtypes [4, 26]. Notably, the prognostic relevance of TCA cycle aligns with the observations of Northcott et al. in MB, they uncover an IDH1/c-MYC dependent vulnerability that regulates DLAT levels and can be targeted to kill Group 3 MB by cuproptosis [27]. Not only that, we also found that most immune cells in Group 3 and Group 4 showed no significant differences. Nevertheless, G4 cells characterized by low PCK2 expression demonstrated a markedly increased interaction with macrophages. This phenomenon may be attributed to the competition for the metabolic capacity of macrophages by cells exhibiting high PCK2 expression, resulting in alterations in metabolites that facilitate immune evasion [28].

Central to our findings is PCK2, a gluconeogenic enzyme repurposed by MYC-amplified MB to fuel anabolic pathways [29]. Its knockdown attenuates glycolysis and malignant phenotypes, consistent with PCK2’s role in redirecting mitochondrial metabolites toward biosynthetic processes. Intriguingly, PCK2 depletion also disrupts M2 macrophage polarization, likely through altering lactate or succinate levels-metabolites known to influence myeloid cell differentiation [30]. This dual metabolic-immunologic function positions PCK2 as a therapeutic vulnerability, particularly given MYC’s established role in therapy resistance [29]. While prior studies focused on PCK1 in cancer metabolism, our work highlights PCK2’s paralogue -specific relevance in MB, potentially reflecting tissue-specific metabolic dependencies [16]. Phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK) exists in two paralogues: PCK1, which functions in the cytoplasm, and PCK2, which is active in the mitochondria [16]. Unlike PCK2, PCK1 promotes tumor cell proliferation in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) by modulating the TCA cycle and fatty acid synthesis [31]. Similarly, PCK1 exhibits comparable functions in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and esophagealcarcinoma [32]. Beyond its role in metabolic regulation, PCK1 is closely associated with the tumor immune microenvironment, where its expression level influences the function and metabolic state of immune cells [32]. In medulloblastoma, however, PCK1 expression is nearly undetectable, with PCK2 being the predominant paralogue (Fig. S5j). Notably, there are significant differences in the expression levels and mechanisms of action of PCK2 across various tumor types [16]. For instance, PCK2 expression is high in breast cancer, prostatic cancer, and lung cancer, and is associated with tumor aggressiveness and poor prognosis [16]. Conversely, in HCC, PCK2 expression is low, yet this low expression correlates with poor prognosis [32]. These disparities may be related to the metabolic demands and immune microenvironments of different tumors [33].

MYC, a crucial transcription factor, influences cell metabolism and proliferation by regulating the expression of multiple genes [34]. Our results are consistent with those in the literature, medulloblastoma tumors with MYC amplification or overexpression have a poor prognosis, often characterized by rapid progression and shortened overall survival [35]. This suggests that PCK2 may support MYC-mediated metabolic reprogramming, thereby promoting the rapid proliferation and survival of tumor cells (Fig. S5k). Beyond its role in metabolic regulation, PCK2 may also affect the stability of MYC. Studies in melanoma have shown that PCK2 knockdown can reduce MYC protein levels and its Ser62 phosphorylation, thereby inhibiting tumor cell growth [28]. This indicates that PCK2 may influence the stability of MYC through metabolic pathways, thereby regulating the proliferative capacity of melanoma cells [28, 29]. Moreover, PCK2 and MYC have a synergistic effect on the tumor microenvironment, influencing the infiltration and function of immune cells [16]. For example, high PCK2 expression is associated with an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, characterized by immune-excluded tumors that may respond to immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy [36]. Similarly, high MYC expression is also associated with an immunosuppressive microenvironment, promoting tumor immune evasion by regulating the expression of immune checkpoint genes such as PD-L1 [37, 38]. Both PCK2 and MYC play important roles in tumor metabolic reprogramming, with PCK2 promoting gluconeogenesis and the TCA cycle to provide energy and metabolic intermediates for tumor cells, while MYC supports rapid tumor cell proliferation by regulating the expression of multiple metabolic genes [16]. Our results suggest that in MYC-amplified medulloblastoma, the synergistic action of PCK2 and MYC may further enhance metabolic reprogramming and promote tumor progression. MB are thought to be relatively immunologically “cold” due to paucity of mutations. Based on CIBERSORT analysis of the integrated transcriptomic dataset and the results of immune cell deconvolution in Sup Fig. 2, results show significantly greater CD8 T cells in MB Group3 compared to MB Group4, and greater NK cells in MBGroup4 compared to other subgroups [39]. This is the same as the results we obtained in Sup Fig. 2b, but the conclusions we drew from single-cell data analysis are not consistent, which may be because bioinformatics analysis cannot accurately reflect reality, so we still need further experimental validation.

In conclusion, this study redefines non-WNT/non-SHH MB through the lens of metabolic-immune interplay, proposing PCK2 as a linchpin molecule coordinating tumor glycolysis and immunosuppression. The continuum score challenges conventional subtyping frameworks, advocating for gradient-based risk assessment in heterogeneous malignancies [40]. These insights not only advance MB biology but also inform therapeutic development for other MYC-driven cancers where metabolic plasticity underlies therapeutic resistance. Regrettably, the study only used human cell lines, preventing observation of macrophage changes in live mouse models. Whether the PCK2 inhibitor 3-MPA can cross the blood–brain barrier and exert effects in vivo still requires further research.
