Second-Order Bilevel Optimization with Accelerated Convergence Rates
Sheng Yang, Chengchang Liu, Lesi Chen, John C.S. Lui

Abstract
This paper studies second-order methods for nonconvex-strongly-convex bilevel optimization. We propose a novel fully second-order bilevel approximation method (FSBA) that achieves an iteration complexity of 𝒪 ~ ​ ( ϵ − 1.5 ) \tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-1.5}) for finding the ( ϵ , 𝒪 ⁡ ( ϵ ) ) (\epsilon,{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{\epsilon})) second-order stationary point of the hyper-objective function. Our results demonstrate that second-order methods can achieve an accelerated convergence rate than first-order methods in bilevel optimization. To address the heavy computational cost associated with the second-order oracle, we introduce a lazy variant of FSBA, called LFSBA, which reuses second-order information across several iterations. We prove that LFSBA exhibits better computational complexity than FSBA by a factor of d \sqrt{d} , where d d is the dimension of the problem. We also apply a similar idea to nonconvex strongly-concave minimax optimization and propose the lazy minimax cubic-regularized Newton (LMCN) method with better computational complexity compared to existing second-order methods.

Thus, it is a natural question to ask:

“Can we develop a second-order method with improved iteration complexities that demonstrates the benefits of utilizing second-order oracles in bilevel optimization?”

To achieve better iteration complexity, a straightforward approach is to employ an inexact Newton-type algorithm that utilizes not only the hypergradient

Preliminaries

We first introduce some basic notation.
For a twice differentiable function

\bm{x}

\bm{y}

\nabla_{x}f(\bm{x},\bm{y})

The Hessian matrix of

(\bm{x},\bm{y})

\nabla^{2}f(\bm{x},\bm{y})=\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{xx}^{2}f(\bm{x},\bm{y}),\nabla_{xy}^{2}f(\bm{x},\bm{y})\\
\nabla_{yx}^{2}f(\bm{x},\bm{y}),\nabla_{yy}^{2}f(\bm{x},\bm{y})\end{bmatrix}
.
We use
\|\cdot\|
to denote the spectral norm of matrices and the Euclidean norm of vectors, respectively.
Given a real symmetric matrix
{\bf A}
, we let
\lambda_{\min}({\bf A})
denote its smallest eigenvalue.
Furthermore, we denote the following quantity, for any

\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
,
\xi(\bm{x}):=\left[-\lambda_{\min}\left(\nabla^{2}f(\bm{x})\right)\right]_{+}
, where

[t]_{+}:=\max\{t,0\}
denotes the positive part.
Finally, we use
\mathcal{O}(\cdot)
to hide only absolute constants that are independent of any problem parameters,

to additionally hide polylogarithmic factors, and

to denote an asymptotic lower bound up to a constant factor.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we have proposed several fully second-order methods for nonconvex strongly-convex bilevel optimization.
The FSBA method takes
\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}(\epsilon^{-1.5})
second-order oracle complexity to find the
(\epsilon,{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{\epsilon}))
SOSP of the hyper objective
\varphi(\cdot)
, and it is faster than the existing first- and second-order methods,
showing the advantage of using second-order oracles in bilevel optimization.
The LFSBA method applies the lazy Hessian strategy and reduces the computational complexity of FSBA.

This paper presents work whose goal is to advance the field of machine learning. There are many potential societal consequences of our work, none of which we feel must be specifically highlighted here.

C.1
The Proof of Lemma
3.1

Proof.

We first need to derive an upper bound for the following equations:

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla_{xy}^{2}g\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)\left[\nabla_{yy}^{2}g\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)\right]^{-1}-\nabla_{xy}^{2}g\left(\bm{x},\bm{w}\right)\left[\nabla_{yy}^{2}g\left(\bm{x},\bm{w}\right)\right]^{-1}\right\|

and

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla_{xy}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{\lambda}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)\left[\nabla_{yy}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{\lambda}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)\right]^{-1}-\nabla_{xy}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}\right)\left[\nabla_{yy}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}\right)\right]^{-1}\right\|.

Using the matrix identity
A^{-1}-B^{-1}=A^{-1}(B-A)B^{-1}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\left[\nabla_{yy}^{2}g\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)\right]^{-1}-\left[\nabla_{yy}^{2}g\left(\bm{x},\bm{w}\right)\right]^{-1}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\left[\nabla_{yy}^{2}g\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)\right]^{-1}\right\|\left\|\nabla_{yy}^{2}g\left(\bm{x},\bm{w}\right)-\nabla_{yy}^{2}g\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)\right\|\left\|\left[\nabla_{yy}^{2}g\left(\bm{x},\bm{w}\right)\right]^{-1}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\rho}{\mu^{2}}\left\|\bm{w}-\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{x})\right\|,

and we further have

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla_{xy}^{2}g\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)\left[\nabla_{yy}^{2}g\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)\right]^{-1}-\nabla_{xy}^{2}g\left(\bm{x},\bm{w}\right)\left[\nabla_{yy}^{2}g\left(\bm{x},\bm{w}\right)\right]^{-1}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla_{xy}^{2}g\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)-\nabla_{xy}^{2}g\left(\bm{x},\bm{w}\right)\right\|\left\|\left[\nabla_{yy}^{2}g\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)\right]^{-1}\right\|

\displaystyle+\left\|\nabla_{xy}^{2}g\left(\bm{x},\bm{w}\right)\right\|\left\|\left[\nabla_{yy}^{2}g\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)\right]^{-1}-\left[\nabla_{yy}^{2}g\left(\bm{x},\bm{w}\right)\right]^{-1}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\rho}{\mu}+\frac{\ell\rho}{\mu^{2}}\right)\left\|\bm{w}-\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{x})\right\|.

Similarly, using the matrix identity
A^{-1}-B^{-1}=A^{-1}(B-A)B^{-1}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\left[\nabla_{yy}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{\lambda}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)\right]^{-1}-\left[\nabla_{yy}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}\right)\right]^{-1}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\left[\nabla_{yy}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{\lambda}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)\right]^{-1}\right\|\left\|\nabla_{yy}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}\right)-\nabla_{yy}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{\lambda}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)\right\|\left\|\left[\nabla_{yy}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}\right)\right]^{-1}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{4\left(\rho+\lambda\rho\right)}{\lambda^{2}\mu^{2}}\left\|\bm{y}-\bm{y}_{\lambda}^{*}(\bm{x})\right\|,

and we further have

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla_{xy}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{\lambda}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)\left[\nabla_{yy}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{\lambda}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)\right]^{-1}-\nabla_{xy}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}\right)\left[\nabla_{yy}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}\right)\right]^{-1}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla_{xy}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{\lambda}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)-\nabla_{xy}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}\right)\right\|\left\|\left[\nabla_{yy}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{\lambda}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)\right]^{-1}\right\|

\displaystyle+\left\|\nabla_{xy}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}\right)\right\|\left\|\left[\nabla_{yy}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{\lambda}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)\right]^{-1}-\left[\nabla_{yy}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}\right)\right]^{-1}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{2\left(\rho+\lambda\rho\right)}{\lambda\mu}+\frac{4\left(\rho+\lambda\rho\right)\left(\ell+\lambda\ell\right)}{\lambda^{2}\mu^{2}}\right)\left\|\bm{y}-\bm{y}_{\lambda}^{*}(\bm{x})\right\|.

According to

\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}^{*}(\bm{x})=\nabla_{x}f\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{\lambda}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)+\lambda\left(\nabla_{x}g\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{\lambda}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)-\nabla_{x}g\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)\right),

and

{\bf g}({\bm{x}};{\bm{y}},{\bm{w}})=\nabla_{x}f\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}\right)+\lambda\left(\nabla_{x}g\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}\right)-\nabla_{x}g\left(\bm{x},\bm{w}\right)\right).

Then we have

\displaystyle\left\|{\nabla}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}^{*}({\bm{x}})-{\bf g}({\bm{x}};{\bm{y}},{\bm{w}})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla_{x}f\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}\right)-\nabla_{x}f\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{\lambda}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)\right\|+\lambda\left\|\nabla_{x}g\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}\right)-\nabla_{x}g\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{\lambda}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)\right\|

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\lambda\left\|\nabla_{x}g\left(\bm{x},\bm{w}\right)-\nabla_{x}g\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\lambda\ell\left\|\bm{y}-{\bm{y}}_{\lambda}^{*}\left(\bm{x}\right)\right\|+\lambda\ell\left\|\bm{w}-{\bm{y}}^{*}\left(\bm{x}\right)\right\|.

Note that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}^{*}(\bm{x})=\nabla_{xx}^{2}f\left(\bm{x},{\bm{y}}_{\lambda}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)-\nabla_{xy}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\left(\bm{x},{\bm{y}}_{\lambda}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)\left[\nabla_{yy}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\left(\bm{x},{\bm{y}}_{\lambda}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)\right]^{-1}\nabla_{yx}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\left(\bm{x},{\bm{y}}_{\lambda}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)\\
&+\lambda\left(\nabla_{xx}^{2}g\left(\bm{x},{\bm{y}}_{\lambda}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)\!-\!\nabla_{xx}^{2}g\left(\bm{x},{\bm{y}}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)\!+\!\nabla_{xy}^{2}g\left(\bm{x},{\bm{y}}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)\left[\nabla_{yy}^{2}g\left(\bm{x},{\bm{y}}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)\right]^{-1}\nabla_{yx}^{2}g\left(\bm{x},{\bm{y}}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)\right),\end{split}

and

\displaystyle\begin{split}&{\bf H}({\bm{x}};{\bm{y}},{\bm{w}}):=\nabla_{xx}^{2}f\left(\bm{x},{\bm{y}}\right)-\nabla_{xy}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\left(\bm{x},{\bm{y}}\right)\left[\nabla_{yy}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\left(\bm{x},{\bm{y}}\right)\right]^{-1}\nabla_{yx}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\left(\bm{x},{\bm{y}}\right)\\
&~~~~+\lambda\left(\nabla_{xx}^{2}g\left(\bm{x},{\bm{y}}\right)-\nabla_{xx}^{2}g\left(\bm{x},{\bm{w}}\right)+\nabla_{xy}^{2}g\left(\bm{x},{\bm{w}}\right)\left[\nabla_{yy}^{2}g\left(\bm{x},{\bm{w}}\right)\right]^{-1}\nabla_{yx}^{2}g\left(\bm{x},{\bm{w}}\right)\right),\end{split}

We can obtain the following inequalities:

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla_{xx}^{2}f\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{\lambda}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)-\nabla_{xx}^{2}f\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}\right)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\rho\left\|\bm{y}-\bm{y}_{\lambda}^{*}\left(\bm{x}\right)\right\|,

\displaystyle\lambda\left\|\nabla_{xx}^{2}g\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{\lambda}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)-\nabla_{xx}^{2}g\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}\right)\right\|

G.1
Ablation Studies on
m

Our theoretical analysis suggests setting

m=\Theta\!\left(1+\frac{d}{\sqrt{\kappa}}\right)
to balance iteration complexity and per-iteration computational cost.
To validate this choice, we conduct ablation studies on
m
for both synthetic and data-cleaning problems.
As shown in Figure
6
, there is a clear trade-off in the choice of
m
.
As suggested by Theorem
4.3
, the choice of
m
introduces a trade-off between iteration complexity and Hessian-related computation.
Smaller
m
improves the iteration complexity due to the
m^{1/2}
factor, whereas larger
m
reduces the cost of Hessian updates, as captured by the
m^{-1/2}
factor in the second-order oracle term.
In practice, moderately large values of
m
achieve the best overall performance in terms of running time.

G.2
Ablation Studies on
\lambda
and
M

The exact values of theoretical constants, such as the smoothness parameter
\ell
, the Hessian Lipschitz parameter
\rho
, and the condition number
\kappa
, are highly problem-dependent.
For example, in the data hypercleaning task, assuming the logit loss is bounded by
B=\max_{i,y}L(\langle\bm{a}_{i},{\bm{y}}\rangle,{\bm{y}})
, the second-order derivatives of
g
can be bounded as

\|\nabla_{xx}^{2}g({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\|\leq\frac{1}{|D_{\mathrm{tr}}|}\max_{i}|\sigma^{\prime\prime}({\bm{x}}_{i})|L_{i}({\bm{y}})=\mathcal{O}(B/|D_{\mathrm{tr}}|)
,

\|\nabla_{xy}^{2}g({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\|\leq\frac{1}{4|D_{\mathrm{tr}}|}\sqrt{\sum_{i\in D_{\mathrm{tr}}}\|\bm{a}_{i}\|^{2}}
, and

\|\nabla_{yy}^{2}g({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\|\leq\frac{1}{4|D_{\mathrm{tr}}|}\lambda_{\max}\!\left(\sum_{i\in D_{\mathrm{tr}}}\bm{a}_{i}\bm{a}_{i}^{\top}\right)+\mu
.
These bounds provide estimates of the smoothness constant of
g
, and other problem-dependent constants can be bounded in a similar way.
Nevertheless, computing tight global constants is often conservative and impractical.
Therefore, in our implementation, we do not rely on strict worst-case bounds; instead, we treat the induced algorithmic parameters, including the cubic regularization parameter
M
and the penalty multiplier
\lambda
, as tunable hyperparameters.

To examine the sensitivity of FSBA/LFSBA to these choices, we conduct additional ablation studies on the Australian dataset for the data hypercleaning task.
Figures
7
and
8
report the results for different choices of
\lambda
and
M
, respectively, while keeping the other hyperparameters fixed.
We observe that FSBA/LFSBA consistently converges and achieves a small optimality gap even when these hyperparameters are varied over a wide range.
Since
\lambda
and
M
are theoretically tied to problem-dependent constants, e.g.,
M\geq c\rho
, this empirical stability demonstrates that our method is robust to the choice of these parameters in practice.
