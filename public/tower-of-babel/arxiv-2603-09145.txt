Causally Sufficient and Necessary Feature Expansion for Class-Incremental Learning
Zhen Zhang, Jielei Chu, Tianrui Li

Abstract
Current expansion-based methods for Class Incremental Learning (CIL) effectively mitigate catastrophic forgetting by freezing old features. However, such task-specific features learned from the new task may collide with the old features. From a causal perspective, spurious feature correlations are the main cause of this collision, manifesting in two scopes: (i) guided by empirical risk minimization (ERM), intra-task spurious correlations cause task-specific features to rely on shortcut features. These non-robust features are vulnerable to interference, inevitably drifting into the feature space of other tasks; (ii) inter-task spurious correlations induce semantic confusion between visually similar classes across tasks. To address this, we propose a Probability of Necessity and Sufficiency (PNS)-based regularization method to guide feature expansion in CIL. Specifically, we first extend the definition of PNS to expansion-based CIL, termed CPNS, which quantifies both the causal completeness of intra-task representations and the separability of inter-task representations. We then introduce a dual-scope counterfactual generator based on twin networks to ensure the measurement of CPNS, which simultaneously generates: (i) intra-task counterfactual features to minimize intra-task PNS risk and ensure causal completeness of task-specific features, and (ii) inter-task interfering features to minimize inter-task PNS risk, ensuring the separability of inter-task representations. Theoretical analyses confirm its reliability. The regularization is a plug-and-play method for expansion-based CIL to mitigate feature collision. Extensive experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method.

Introduction

Continual Learning (CL)
(
33
)
addresses the critical need for learning systems to adapt to evolving data streams. A prominent scenario is Class-Incremental Learning (CIL)
(
21
;
34
;
17
;
40
)
, where the model encounters new classes step-by-step. The fundamental challenge lies in mitigating catastrophic forgetting
(
18
)
—the tendency of neural networks to lose former knowledge upon learning new tasks. Effective CIL frameworks must therefore strike a delicate balance within the stability-plasticity dilemma
(
9
)
, ensuring that the accommodation of new concepts does not come at the expense of existing capabilities. One of the promising strategies is to train a new feature extractor for each new task while keeping the previously learned model intact during the training of the new task. Such methods are often referred to as
expansion-based
approaches
(
7
;
13
;
31
;
35
;
41
)
. Despite the significant of expansion-based methods, they remain susceptible to feature interference. Specifically, features learned from new tasks may collide with frozen old features, leading to classification bias towards the new task
(
39
)
.

Therefore, to mitigate feature collision, expansion-based methods often introduce an auxiliary classifier for diverse feature extraction and save a small number of rehearsal samples from the previous tasks to distinguish current task samples from previous samples
(
7
;
35
)
. Although the auxiliary classifier encourages the current task model to learn features beyond the frozen features from the previous tasks, the learning process within each task is still dominated by Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM)
(
28
)
. ERM tends to prioritize the most accessible discriminative cues, leading to feature suppression, where the model captures only the shortcut features sufficient to minimize training loss. Consequently, existing expansion-based methods mainly ensure the diversity of the shortcut features for each task of the model. While this reliance on shortcut features temporarily mitigates feature collision, it inherently sacrifices the model’s robustness against distribution shifts and constrains the representational depth essential for long-term scalability. As shown in Figure
1
(a), to understand this better, we could consider an extreme case where there are two incremental tasks for a model: first learning to distinguish wolves from cats, and then learning to distinguish dogs from lynxes. However, ERM encourages models to focus on a minimal discriminative cue (e.g., ear shape) and overlook broader semantic attributes. When the dog task arrives—sharing similar ear structures—the frozen wolf representation (fixated on ears) fails to provide a robust semantic foundation. Consequently, to discriminate dogs from wolves without altering the frozen model, the expansion module is forced to focus on other shortcut features (e.g., eyes or texture). This results in a fragmented feature space where neither task captures the holistic, causal attributes, exacerbating feature confusion when semantic overlap occurs.

Figure 1
:
(a) Illustration of feature suppression and collision. ERM and diversity strategy drives the model to learn shortcut features (e.g., ear vs. eyes) for semantically similar classes, leading to a fragmented feature space. (b) CKA feature similarity analysis. Our method possesses high similarity in shallow layers (indicating shared causal semantics) while maintaining discriminability in deep layers.

To verify this hypothesis, we conduct a detailed analysis of feature representations using Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) similarity
(
14
)
. As shown in Figure
1
(b), it presents the CKA feature similarity of expansion-based methods in data with highly overlapping semantics. This data from the ImageNet-1K
(
6
)
dataset and includes two incremental tasks: Task 1 involves the classification of wolves and cats, while Task 2 introduces huskies and lynxes. We compared the CKA similarity of different methods in deep features and shallow features. The baseline expansion-based method exhibits low CKA similarity across nearly all deep features and shallow features. This empirical evidence supports our hypothesis that ERM-driven learning focuses on different shortcut features. However, the integration of our method into expansion-based baselines exhibits high similarity in the shallow features and low similarity in the deep features. The higher CKA similarity in the shallow features demonstrates that the model can capture more complete causal features, as the semantic features of wolves and dogs are similar. Meanwhile, the low CKA similarity in the deep features ensure that the model retains sufficient task-specific discriminative capabilities.

Motivated by these observations, we argue that addressing feature collision requires moving beyond mere representation diversity; as shown in Figure
1
(a), first, ensuring the causal completeness of representations within tasks, and second, guaranteeing the separability of features across tasks. To address this, we propose a Probability of Necessity and Sufficiency (PNS)
(
20
;
36
)
-based regularization method to guide feature expansion in CIL. We first extend the definition of PNS to expansion-based CIL, termed CPNS, which quantifies both the causal completeness of intra-task representations and the separability of inter-task representations. Then, we analyze the causal identifiability of CPNS, which allows us to quantify CPNS using the observable data in practice. Based on this, we then introduce a dual-scope counterfactual generator based on twin networks to ensure the measurement of CPNS, which simultaneously generates: (i) counterfactual features within the task to minimize intra-task PNS risk, ensuring causal completeness of task-specific features, and (ii) inter-task interfering features to minimize inter-task PNS risk, enforcing the strict discriminability against old features. Through theoretical analyses, we prove the reliability of the proposed method. The proposed regularization is a plug-and-play module that mitigates the collision of task-specific features by minimizing intra-task and inter-task PNS risk.

The main contributions are as follows
: (i) We propose a PNS-based regularization method for expansion-based CIL, which mitigates feature collision by ensuring the causal completeness of intra-task representations and the separability of inter-task representations, termed CPNS. (ii) We theoretically demonstrate the effectiveness and reliability of the proposed method, which can be applied to mitigate feature collision for expansion-based CIL methods. Finally, Extensive experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method.

Preliminaries

2.1
Problem Formulation

In CIL, a model learns sequentially from distinct classification tasks. Let
\mathcal{D}_{t}=\{(x_{t,i},y_{t,i})\}
denote the training data for task
t
, with inputs
x_{t,i}
and labels
y_{t,i}
. The label space is
\mathcal{C}_{t}=\bigcup_{i}\{y_{t,i}\}
, and class sets are disjoint:
\mathcal{C}_{t_{1}}\cap\mathcal{C}_{t_{2}}=\emptyset
for
t_{1}\neq t_{2}
. During training on task
t_{i}
, the model uses only
\mathcal{D}_{t_{i}}
but is evaluated on all tasks up to
t_{i}
(i.e.,
t_{j}
where
j\leq i
), with cumulative class set
\mathcal{Y}_{t}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{t}\mathcal{C}_{j}
. In rehearsal-based CIL, a fixed-size buffer
\mathcal{M}
stores past exemplars for replay. The goal is to improve performance across all observed tasks. Variables from
\mathcal{D}_{t}
are denoted
X_{t}
and
Y_{t}
; those from
\mathcal{D}_{t}\cup\mathcal{M}
are denoted
X
and
Y
.

2.2
Feature Expansion of Task-Specific Models

In this section, we introduce DER
(
35
)
as an example of the feature expansion for our task-specific models. A new model
f_{t}(\cdot)
is created at the beginning of each task. Also, the previously learned models
\{f_{0},\dots,f_{t-1}\}
are saved and freezed for feature extraction. The classifier is also expanded and the trained parameter weights from the old classifier is inherited. To predict the label of a sample, the concatenated feature from all of the models is used by the classifier. Formally, the classification loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{cls}}
with cross-entropy (CE) for the sample
(\boldsymbol{x},y)
would be,

{{\cal L}_{cls}}(x,y)={\ell_{CE}}([{f_{0}}(x),\ldots,{f_{t}}(x)],y;W_{cls}^{(t)}),

(1)

where
y\in\mathcal{Y}_{t}
and
W_{\text{cls}}^{(t)}
is the parameter weights of the classifier in task
t
.

To further encourage the new module to learn diverse and discriminative features, we introduce an auxiliary classifier trained exclusively on the features from
f_{\text{tm}}^{(t)}
.
The objective of this auxiliary component is twofold: to distinguish between classes within the current task and to discriminate current samples from those of previous tasks (represented by rehearsal exemplars).
This effectively treats all past classes as a single “old” category.
Consequently, the auxiliary classifier produces logits for
|\mathcal{C}_{t}|+1
classes.
The auxiliary loss is formulated as:

{{\cal L}_{aux}}(x,y)={\ell_{CE}}({f_{t}}(x),y;W_{aux}^{(t)}),

(2)

where the label space is
y\in[|\mathcal{C}_{t}|+1]
and
W_{\text{aux}}^{(t)}
denotes the weights of the auxiliary classifier.

2.3
Causal Analysis of expansion-based CIL

:

As shown in Figure
2
left, we construct a Structural
Causal Model (SCM) for data generation process within the task based on the causal generating mechanism. In this SCM, Y and X denote the label variable and corresponding generated data variable in the process.
F_{\text{c}}
and
F_{\text{s}}
represent the distinct sets of generating factors that are causally and non-causally related to Y.
F_{\text{mc}}
represents one or a group of minimal necessary causal factors for the composition of X. In the SCM, the label
Y
causes the causal factors
F_{\text{c}}
(
Y\rightarrow F_{\text{c}}
), whereas
F_{\text{s}}
arises independently from background noise. Both factors jointly generate the instance
X
(
F_{\text{c}},F_{\text{s}}\rightarrow X
). Furthermore, we denote
F_{\text{mc}}\subset F_{\text{c}}
as the minimal sufficient causal factors—a subset of causal features that is minimally sufficient to distinguish
Y
within the current distribution but lacks holistic semantic completeness.

Based on this, we further construct an SCM to discuss the learning process of expansion-based CIL, as illustrated in Figure
2
(Right). Here,
F_{\text{old}}
represents the frozen representations learned from previous tasks. In the current
t
-th task, ideal task-specific features should capture the holistic causal generating factors
F_{\text{c}}
. However, guided by the ERM, the model tends to rely on minimal discriminative factors rather than the complete causal set
F_{\text{c}}
. Specifically, these minimal factors often comprise either non-causal factors
F_{s}\rightarrow Y
or only a subset of causal factors
F_{\text{mc}}\rightarrow Y
. When the local or overall semantic overlap between tasks is relatively high, the shared attributes (whether spurious or incomplete causal cues) can inadvertently trigger the frozen features
F_{\text{old}}
, establishing a false confusion path that leads to feature collision. Since the current model relies on the incomplete shortcuts, it lacks the unique, holistic semantics required to distinguish itself from
F_{\text{old}}
, leading to misclassification.

Therefore, our objective is twofold: first, to ensure intra-task causal completeness by forcing the model to capture the full set
F_{\text{c}}
rather than just
F_{\text{mc}}
or
F_{\text{s}}
; second, to ensure inter-task discriminability by explicitly minimizing the dependency between the current features and the confounding proxy
F_{\text{old}}
.

3
Method

To mitigate feature collision in expansion-based CIL, our method aims to achieve two critical objectives: ensuring the causal completeness of intra-task representations and guaranteeing the separability of inter-task representations.
In this section, we formalize these objectives using causal necessity and sufficiency. We first define the Probability of Necessary and Sufficient (PNS) in representation learning. Next, we extend the concept of PNS to expansion-based continual learning, termed
CPNS
, and discuss the identifiability of CPNS. The CPNS comprises complementary regularization terms: intra-task PNS for causal completeness, and inter-task PNS for discriminative separability. Finally, we detail the practical implementation for estimating and minimizing the CPNS risk via counterfactual generation.

3.1
Probability of Sufficient and Necessary Cause

We first introduce the concept of the Probability of Sufficient and Necessary Cause (PNS) to measure the causal completeness of representations as follows:

(

\mathbf{c}
and

\displaystyle\text{PNS}(C)

\displaystyle:=\underbrace{P(Y_{do(\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{c})}=y\mid\mathbf{C}=\bar{\mathbf{c}},Y\neq y)}_{\text{sufficiency}}P(\mathbf{C}=\bar{\mathbf{c}},Y\neq y)

(3)

\displaystyle+\underbrace{P(Y_{do(\mathbf{C}=\bar{\mathbf{c}})}\neq y\mid\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{c},Y=y)}_{\text{necessity}}P(\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{c},Y=y).

3.3
Causal Identifiability of CPNS

To measure the CPNS score in practice, we must establish the identifiability of both intra-task and inter-task PNS scores. Identifiability refers to the ability to uniquely infer causal effects from observable data under given assumptions
(
20
)
. Previous works often rely on the strict assumption of exogeneity and monotonicity. Exogeneity posits that the learned representations (
\mathbf{C}
within tasks and
\mathbf{Z}
between tasks) are independent of latent confounders (i.e.,
P(Y|X)=P(Y|do(X))
). Monotonicity implies a consistent, unidirectional effect of feature quality on the outcome
Y
—specifically, that enhancing the causal representation does not decrease the probability of a correct prediction. However, in the context of Continual Learning (CL), exogeneity is frequently violated. The continuous shift in data distribution and the sequential nature of learning introduce latent confounders, such as temporal spurious correlations and task-specific biases. These confounders influence both the representation learning process and the decision boundary.

Consequently, we relax the exogeneity constraint. According to
Theorem 9.2.15
in
(
20
)
(
Appendix
A.1
for detailed theorems
), if monotonicity holds, the PNS is identifiable as the difference between causal effects, even in the absence of exogeneity. Based on this, we derive the identifiability of CPNS as follows:

Theorem 3.3

(
Causal Identifiability of CPNS under Monotonicity
)
.

Given the monotonicity assumption, the CPNS is identifiable and defined by the difference in interventional distributions, robust to latent confounding:

First, the identifiability of intra-task PNS:

\begin{split}\text{PNS}_{\text{intra}}(\mathbf{C})&=P(Y_{t}=y_{t}\mid do(\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{c}))\\
&\quad-P(Y_{t}=y_{t}\mid do(\mathbf{C}=\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{\text{intra}}))\end{split}

(6)

Second, the identifiability of inter-task PNS:

\begin{split}\text{PNS}_{\text{inter}}(\mathbf{Z})&=P(Y=y\mid do(\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{z}))\\
&\quad-P(Y=y\mid do(\mathbf{Z}=\bar{\mathbf{z}}))\end{split}

(7)

where
do(\cdot)
denotes the causal intervention operator.

According to
Theorem
3.3
, the computation of CPNS is theoretically feasible. Unlike standard observational metrics, Eq. (
6
) and Eq. (
7
) quantify the pure causal impact of the representations by isolating confounders. In practice, we approximate these interventional probabilities via our proposed dual-scope counterfactual generator, which simulates the physical intervention

through gradient-based perturbation. The proofs are provided in
Appendix
A.1

3.4
CPNS Risk Modeling

Based on
Theorem
3.3
, we propose a unified modeling method for measuring CPNS risk. Specifically, the CPNS risk consists of two parts:
\text{PNS}_{\text{intra}}
and
\text{PNS}_{\text{inter}}
. We first establish the intra-task model and the inter-task model, respectively. For the
intra-task model
, it comprises a learnable feature extractor
f_{t}
and a current task classifier
W_{\text{intra}}
. Given an input
\mathbf{x}_{t}
, the real-world representation is extracted as
\hat{\mathbf{c}}=f_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
, and the intra-task prediction is computed via
y_{\text{intra}}=\text{sign}(W_{\text{intra}}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{c}})
. The
inter-task model
incorporates the learnable feature extractor
f_{t}
, the frozen extractors
f_{\text{old}}
and classifier
W_{\text{inter}}
. Given an input
\mathbf{x}
, the real-world representation
\hat{\mathbf{z}}
is derived from
\hat{\mathbf{z}}=[f_{\text{old}}(\mathbf{x}),f_{t}(\mathbf{x})]
, and the intra-task prediction is computed via
y=\text{sign}(W_{\text{inter}}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{z}})
.

The key challenge lies in how to model intra- and inter-task counterfactual data to measure the CPNS, i.e.,
\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{\text{intra}}
and
\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{\text{inter}}
. To address this, we propose a twin network with a dual-range inspired by
(
32
)
: (i) the real-world branch to obtain
\hat{\mathbf{c}}
and
\hat{\mathbf{z}}
, (ii) the hypothetical-world branch obtains
\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{\text{intra}}
through the current task’s gradient-based adjustment. This branch also leverages frozen feature projections from previous tasks to approximate counterfactual features
\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{\text{inter}}
in order to acquire
\bar{\mathbf{z}}=[f_{\text{old}}(\mathbf{x}),\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{\text{inter}}]
. These branches share network structures and parameters, maintaining mirrored correspondence to ensure causal consistency
(
20
)
. Thus, we get:

Definition 3.4

(
Double-range counterfactual modeling
)
.

We define the following twin-network to model
\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{\text{intra}}
in the current task
t
:

\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{\text{intra}}=\hat{\mathbf{c}}+\Delta_{\text{intra}},\quad\text{with}\quad\Delta_{\text{intra}}=\nabla_{\hat{\mathbf{c}}}\ell(W_{\text{intra}}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{c}},y_{t}),

(8)

where
\Delta_{\text{intra}}
arises from the gradient-based intervention for the hypothetical world. We impose a KL-divergence constraint
\mu_{\text{KL}}(\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{\text{intra}},\hat{\mathbf{c}})\leq\epsilon
, ensuring the counterfactual feature remains within the real semantic
(
20
)
. Guided by the minimal change principle
(
16
)
, we utilize the gradient direction as the most efficient path to alter predictions. This gradient-based intervention + KL constraint can approximate a causal intervention (See
Appendix
D.2
).

Similarly, for the inter-task counterfactual generation, we employ a MLP layer, denoted as
\mathcal{P}
, to approximate the current task features using the frozen features from previous tasks. Let
\tilde{\mathbf{c}}=\mathcal{P}(f_{\text{old}}(\mathbf{x}))
represent the projected approximation. The inter-task counterfactual feature
\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{\text{inter}}
is then obtained by perturbing the real-world feature
\hat{\mathbf{c}}
based on the gradient of the distance between the actual feature and its projection. This process is formalized as:

\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{\text{inter}}=\hat{\mathbf{c}}-\Delta_{\text{inter}},\,\text{with}\,\Delta_{\text{inter}}=\beta\cdot\nabla_{\hat{\mathbf{c}}}\|\hat{\mathbf{c}}-\mathcal{P}(f_{\text{old}}(\mathbf{x}))\|^{2},

(9)

where
\beta
is a coefficient controlling the perturbation magnitude. In the following experiments,
\beta
is set to 0.05, and the specific parameter sensitivity is provided in the
Appendix
E.2
. The KL divergence
\mu_{\text{KL}}(\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{\text{inter}},\hat{\mathbf{c}})\leq\epsilon
constraint semantic information consistency of
\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{\text{inter}}
. Here, the negative gradient direction drives the current features
\hat{\mathbf{c}}
towards the frozen features, explicitly simulating the feature collision state. Consequently, the counterfactual representation for the inter-task model is constructed as
\bar{\mathbf{z}}=[f_{\text{old}}(\mathbf{x}),\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{\text{inter}}]
. This formulation allows us to evaluate the causal necessity of the current features under maximum interference from frozen features.

Consider the current task

, and a global memory buffer containing
N
total samples (including rehearsal and current data) denoted as
\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{k},y_{k})\}_{k=1}^{N}
. Let
f_{\text{old}}
be the frozen model from previous tasks.

For the intra-task scope, let
\hat{\mathbf{c}}_{i}=f_{t}(x_{t,i})
be the real-world causal representation. For the inter-task scope, let
\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{k}=[f_{\text{old}}(x_{k}),f_{t}(x_{k})]
be the combined representation. Based on
Definition
3.4
, we obtain the corresponding counterfactual representations
\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{i}
and
\bar{\mathbf{z}}_{k}
via gradient-based intervention. Next, we define the CPNS risk, i.e.,
\hat{R}_{\text{CPNS}}
as:

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{\text{CPNS}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[\rho\big(\sigma(W_{\text{intra}}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{c}}_{i})\neq y_{t,i}\big)+\rho\big(\sigma(W_{\text{intra}}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{i})=y_{t,i}\big)\Big]

(10)

\displaystyle+\frac{\lambda}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Big[\rho\big(\sigma(W_{\text{inter}}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{k})\neq y_{k}\big)+\rho\big(\sigma(W_{\text{inter}}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{z}}_{k})=y_{k}\big)\Big],

where
\sigma(\cdot)
denotes the prediction function, and
\rho(\cdot)
is the indicator function evaluating to
1
if the condition is true.
\lambda
is the balance coefficient between
\text{PNS}_{\text{intra}}
and
\text{PNS}_{\text{inter}}
. The first term in each bracket penalizes sufficiency violations (failure to predict correctly with real features), while the second term penalizes necessity violations (failure to predict incorrectly with counterfactual features). The discrepancy between
\hat{R}_{\text{CPNS}}
and the ideal causal quantity approaches zero with high probability.

In
Theorem
3.5
, we propose a method to measure the causal completeness of intra-task representations and the separability of inter-task representations based on observable data in expansion-based CIL. However, the identifiability of
\hat{R}_{\text{CPNS}}
relies on the satisfaction of monotonicity according to
Theorem
3.3
.

Satisfaction of monotonicity.
To ensure the identifiability of CPNS, we theoretically satisfy the monotonicity assumption by minimizing CPNS upper bound. Drawing from
Proposition 3.1
in
(
36
)
, the CPNS risk can be decomposed as:

R_{\text{CPNS}}=M+2\cdot SF\cdot NC,

(11)

where
M
represents the monotonicity violation measurement. Since the sufficiency (
SF
) and necessity (
NC
) risks are non-negative,
R_{\text{CPNS}}
serves as a strict upper bound for
M
(i.e.,
M\leq R_{\text{CPNS}}
). This theoretical guarantee is applied across two perspectives: Intra-task Scope: Directly optimizing the proposed
\hat{R}_{\text{CPNS}}
minimizes the upper bound of the intra-task monotonicity violation. This forces the learned representations into a region where the intervention (presence of causal features) consistently increases the probability of the correct outcome, thereby satisfying local monotonicity. Inter-task Scope: Due to severe non-linear conflicts between tasks, direct monotonic modeling is challenging. We address this via a binary simplification strategy (Old vs. New), which constructs a naturally ordered output space. Within this simplified binary structure, minimizing
\hat{R}_{\text{CPNS}}
further aligns the feature space with the causal order, effectively bounding the inter-task monotonicity violations and ensuring identifiability. The proofs of monotonicity and the discussions of binary simplification are provided in
Appendix
A.2
and
Appendix
D.3
, respectively.

3.5
Overall Objective and Optimization Strategy

Since our proposed method is a plug-and-play framework, we adopt a three-stage optimization strategy to insert other expansion-based CIL methods and mitigate feature collisions. Let
\mathcal{L}_{\text{base}}
denote the original objective function of the baselines.

Stage 1: Intra-Task Causal learning.

In the first stage, we focus on establishing causally complete representations for the current task. While optimizing the base model, we explicitly enforce the intra-task sufficiency and necessity constraints. The objective function is defined as:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{base}}+\mathop{\min}\limits_{{f_{t}}}{\hat{R}_{{\rm{intra}}}}+\gamma\mu_{\text{KL}}(\hat{\mathbf{c}},\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{\text{intra}}),

(12)

where
\hat{R}_{\text{intra}}
corresponds to the first term in Eq. (
10
) (i.e., the intra-task CPNS risk). This stage ensures that the learned intra-task representations possess causal completeness.

Stage 2: Inter-Task Projector Alignment.

Before addressing task conflicts, we must accurately model the relationship between the frozen feature space and the current feature space. We employ the MLP projector
\mathcal{P}
to map the frozen features
f_{\text{old}}(x)
to the current representation
\hat{\mathbf{c}}
. In this stage, we freeze the parameters of the backbone model
f_{t}
and exclusively optimize
\mathcal{P}
by minimizing the projection loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{proj}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\|\hat{\mathbf{c}}_{k}-\mathcal{P}(f_{\text{old}}(x_{k}))\|_{2}^{2}.

(13)

This alignment ensures that the gradient-based perturbations used in the next stage accurately simulate the collision boundaries between old and new tasks. Crucially, if the representation
\hat{\mathbf{c}}
has not reached stability, we directly proceed to the joint learning of Stage 3. The projector will fail to accurately track the feature change, leading to gradient imbalance and ineffective counterfactual generation.

Stage 3: Joint Causal learning.

Finally, With the projector
\mathcal{P}
calibrated, we introduce the inter-task counterfactuals to evaluate and minimize the full CPNS risk. The total objective is:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{base}}+\mathop{\min}\limits_{{f_{t}}}{\hat{R}_{{\rm{CPNS}}}}+\gamma\mathcal{L}_{\text{KL}}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{proj}},

(14)

where
\hat{R}_{\text{CPNS}}
represents the complete risk defined in Eq. (
10
), encompassing both intra-task and inter-task causal constraints.
\mathcal{L}_{\text{KL}}
aggregates the semantic constraints for both counterfactual scopes.
\mathcal{L}_{\text{proj}}
indicates that the projector continuously update. This stage effectively expands the feature space for new classes while suppressing the interference from frozen features.

5
Conclusion

We argue that mitigating feature conflicts cannot solely rely on the strategy of feature diversity in expansion-based CIL. From a causal perspective, spurious feature correlations are the main cause of this collision, manifesting in two scopes: intra-task spurious correlations and inter-task spurious correlations. To address this, we propose a Probability of Necessity and Sufficiency (PNS)-based regularization method to guide feature expansion in class-incremental learning (CIL). Specifically, we first extend the definition of PNS to expansion-based CIL, termed CPNS, which quantifies both the causal completeness of intra-task representations and the separability of inter-task representations.
We then introduce a dual-scope counterfactual generator based on twin networks to ensure the measurement of CPNS. Theoretical analyses confirm its reliability. The regularization is a plug-and-play method for expansion-based CIL to mitigate feature collision. Extensive experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method.

Appendix A
Proofs and Theoretical Analysis

A.1
Proof of
Theorem
3.3
: Causal Identifiability under Non-Exogeneity

In the main text, we leverage the Probability of Necessity and Sufficiency (PNS) to quantify the causal completeness of learned features. However, a classical challenge in causal inference is that PNS is generally not identifiable from observational data alone, especially when the exogeneity assumption is violated due to latent confounders (e.g., hidden spurious correlations in continuous tasks).

Here, we provide the formal proof for
Theorem
3.3
, demonstrating that under the assumption of
monotonicity
, the PNS scores for both intra-task and inter-task settings are identifiable and can be estimated as the difference between interventional probabilities. This derivation aligns with
9.2.15
in
(
20
)
and the Positive Markovian assumption discussed in
(
26
)
.

Theorem A.1

(Causal Identifiability of CPNS under Monotonicity)
.

Let
C
denote the representation variable (taking value
c
for causal features and
\bar{c}
for counterfactual features) and
Y
denote the classification outcome. Even in the absence of exogeneity (i.e., when latent confounders
U
exist such that
P(Y|C)\neq P(Y|do(C))
), the Probability of Necessity and Sufficiency (PNS) is identifiable if the relationship between
C
and
Y
satisfies
monotonicity
. Specifically, the PNS is given by:

PNS(C)=P(Y=y\mid do(C=c))-P(Y=y\mid do(C=\bar{c})),

(15)

provided that for every individual
u
, increasing the causal quality of features (from
\bar{c}
to
c
) does not decrease the probability of correct classification.

Proof.

Recall the definition of PNS as the joint probability that the outcome
Y
would be
y
under intervention
c

and
would not be
y
under intervention
\bar{c}
:

PNS(C)\triangleq P(Y_{c}=y,Y_{\bar{c}}\neq y),

(16)

where
Y_{x}
denotes the potential outcome under intervention
do(C=x)
.

From the law of total probability, the marginal probability of the potential outcome
Y_{c}=y
can be expanded as:

P(Y_{c}=y)=P(Y_{c}=y,Y_{\bar{c}}=y)+P(Y_{c}=y,Y_{\bar{c}}\neq y).

(17)

The second term on the right-hand side is exactly the definition of
PNS(C)
. The first term,
P(Y_{c}=y,Y_{\bar{c}}=y)
, represents the probability that the model correctly predicts
y
regardless of whether the features are
c
or
\bar{c}
.

Now, we introduce the
Monotonicity Assumption
. In the context of feature expansion for CIL, monotonicity implies that improving the representation quality (transitioning from a corrupted/counterfactual state
\bar{c}
to a causally complete state
c
) should never cause a correct prediction to become incorrect. Formally:

Y_{c}(u)\geq Y_{\bar{c}}(u),\quad\forall u.

(18)

This implies that the event
\{Y_{c}\neq y\wedge Y_{\bar{c}}=y\}
is impossible. Therefore:

P(Y_{c}\neq y,Y_{\bar{c}}=y)=0.

(19)

Next, let us expand the marginal probability of the counterfactual outcome
P(Y_{\bar{c}}=y)
:

P(Y_{\bar{c}}=y)=P(Y_{c}=y,Y_{\bar{c}}=y)+P(Y_{c}\neq y,Y_{\bar{c}}=y).

(20)

Under the monotonicity assumption, the second term becomes zero. Thus, we have:

P(Y_{\bar{c}}=y)=P(Y_{c}=y,Y_{\bar{c}}=y).

(21)

Substituting Eq. (
21
) back into Eq. (
17
):

P(Y_{c}=y)=P(Y_{\bar{c}}=y)+PNS(C).

(22)

Rearranging the terms yields:

PNS(C)=P(Y_{c}=y)-P(Y_{\bar{c}}=y).

(23)

Using standard causal notation where
P(Y_{x}=y)\equiv P(Y=y\mid do(X=x))
, we arrive at the final identifiable expression:

PNS(C)=P(Y=y\mid do(C=c))-P(Y=y\mid do(C=\bar{c})).

(24)

This completes the proof.
∎

Based on the general proof provided in
Theorem
A.1
, we now derive the identifiable forms for Intra-task PNS and Inter-task PNS as defined in the main text. This demonstrates that our metric definitions satisfy the causal assumptions required for identifiability.

For the intra-task scenario, we map the general variables from
Theorem
A.1
to our specific definitions: let the causal variable be the task-specific representation
\mathbf{C}
and the outcome be the task label
Y_{t}
. The two intervention states are the optimal implementation
\mathbf{c}
and the sub-optimal implementation
\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{\text{intra}}
.

The
Intra-task Monotonicity Assumption
posits that for any sample
u
:

(Y_{t}(u))_{do(\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{c})}\geq(Y_{t}(u))_{do(\mathbf{C}=\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{\text{intra}})}.

(25)

This assumption holds because improving the representation quality from a flawed state (
\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{\text{intra}}
) to a causally complete state (
\mathbf{c}
) should mathematically increase (or at least not decrease) the likelihood of the correct prediction
y_{t}
.

Applying the result from
Theorem
A.1
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\text{PNS}_{\text{intra}}(\mathbf{C})=P(Y_{t}=y_{t}\mid do(\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{c}))\quad-P(Y_{t}=y_{t}\mid do(\mathbf{C}=\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{\text{intra}})).

(26)

This derivation formally validates Eq. (
6
) presented in the main text.

For the inter-task scenario, the causal variable is the combined feature
\mathbf{Z}=[\mathbf{z}_{\text{old}},\mathbf{c}]
, and the outcome is the global label
Y
. The distinct implementations are defined as: (i)
\mathbf{z}
: The discriminative state where new features are distinct from old features. (ii)
\bar{\mathbf{z}}
: The interfered state where new features collide with old features.

The
Inter-task Monotonicity Assumption
implies:

Y_{do(\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{z})}\geq Y_{do(\mathbf{Z}=\bar{\mathbf{z}})}.

(27)

Resolving feature collision (transitioning from
\bar{\mathbf{z}}
to
\mathbf{z}
) enhances the separability between tasks. Consequently, the probability of correct classification for a model with discriminative features (
\mathbf{z}
) must be greater than or equal to that of a model suffering from interference (
\bar{\mathbf{z}}
).

Applying
Theorem
A.1
to these variables, we derive:

\displaystyle\text{PNS}_{\text{inter}}(\mathbf{Z})=P(Y=y\mid do(\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{z}))\quad-P(Y=y\mid do(\mathbf{Z}=\bar{\mathbf{z}})).

(28)

This derivation formally validates Eq. (
7
) in the main text.

A.2
Justification of the Monotonicity Assumption

In this section, we provide the theoretical justification for how our framework enforces the monotonicity assumption required for causal identifiability. As established in Definition 2.3 of the referenced literature
(
36
)
, a variable is monotonic relative to the outcome if the causal effect is unidirectional. Strictly guaranteeing this global property in a high-dimensional space is challenging. Therefore, we enforce this property via two synergistic mechanisms: (i) the CPNS risk optimization implicitly minimizes the probability of monotonicity violations, and (ii) the binary simplification of the inter-task outcome space establishes the necessary ordinal structure.

Proposition A.2
.

Minimizing the CPNS risk objective minimizes the strict upper bound of the probability of monotonicity violation.

Proof.

Let
SF
and
NC
denote the sufficiency and necessity risks, respectively. These represent the probabilities of error events defined as:

E_{SF}=\{\text{sign}(w^{\top}c)\neq y\},\quad E_{NC}=\{\text{sign}(w^{\top}\bar{c})=y\}.

(29)

The
Monotonicity Violation
event, denoted as
\mathcal{V}
, occurs when the model fails to predict correctly given the causal feature
c
(sufficiency error)
and
paradoxically predicts correctly given the counterfactual
\bar{c}
(necessity error). Mathematically, this is the intersection of the two error events:

\mathcal{V}=E_{SF}\cap E_{NC}.

(30)

The CPNS risk
\hat{R}_{\text{CPNS}}
corresponds to the total PNS risk, which is the sum of sufficiency and necessity risks:

\hat{R}_{\text{CPNS}}\approx P(E_{SF})+P(E_{NC}).

(31)

According to basic probability theory, the probability of the intersection of two events is bounded by the probability of each individual event:
P(A\cap B)\leq\min(P(A),P(B))
. Furthermore, it satisfies the inequality:

P(\mathcal{V})=P(E_{SF}\cap E_{NC})\leq\frac{1}{2}\big(P(E_{SF})+P(E_{NC})\big)=\frac{1}{2}\hat{R}_{\text{CPNS}}.

(32)

Thus, minimizing the observable CPNS risk
\hat{R}_{\text{CPNS}}
inherently imposes a penalty on the intersection of errors, driving the upper bound of the monotonicity violation probability
P(\mathcal{V})
toward zero. This ensures that the learned representations satisfy the local monotonicity condition required for identifiability.
∎

While intra-task monotonicity focuses on label accuracy within a task, inter-task monotonicity addresses the conflict between the current task and previous tasks. Directly proving monotonicity in the joint outcome space is binary simplification strategy that maps the problem to a ordered space.

Definition A.3

(
Binary Simplification for Inter-task Monotonicity
)
.

Let the outcome space be simplified to a binary variable
Y_{\text{bin}}\in\{0,1\}
, where
0
represents the Previous Tasks (Old) and
1
represents the Current Task (New). The monotonicity assumption in this scope implies that improving the representation from a collision state (
\bar{\mathbf{z}}
) to a separable state (
\mathbf{z}
) should not decrease the probability of predicting the current task label (
Y_{\text{bin}}=1
).

Theorem A.4

(
Inter-task Monotonicity Bound
)
.

Minimizing the inter-task component of
\hat{R}_{\text{CPNS}}
minimizes the monotonicity violation in the binary inter-task scope.

Proof.

Let
y
be a label belonging to the current task (
y\in Y_{t}
). In the binary view, the ground truth is
Y_{\text{bin}}=1
.
The inter-task monotonicity violation
\mathcal{V}_{\text{inter}}
occurs if the model correctly identifies the task as New (or predicts
y
) given the collided features
\bar{\mathbf{z}}
, but fails to do so given the clear features
\mathbf{z}
:

\mathcal{V}_{\text{inter}}\iff\{\sigma(W_{\text{inter}}^{\top}\mathbf{z})\neq y\}\land\{\sigma(W_{\text{inter}}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{z}})=y\}.

(33)

Recall the inter-task risk term from Eq.
10
(for a sample belonging to the current task):

\hat{R}_{\text{inter}}=\underbrace{\rho\big(\sigma(W_{\text{inter}}^{\top}\mathbf{z})\neq y\big)}_{\text{Sufficiency Violation (SF)}}+\underbrace{\rho\big(\sigma(W_{\text{inter}}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{z}})=y\big)}_{\text{Necessity Violation (NC)}}.

(34)

Here, the
Sufficiency
term penalizes the failure to predict the new class given discriminative features, and the
Necessity
term penalizes the hallucination of the new class given collided (old-like) features.
Similar to Proposition
A.2
, the probability of inter-task monotonicity violation is the intersection of these two error events:

P(\mathcal{V}_{\text{inter}})=P(E_{SF}\cap E_{NC})\leq\frac{1}{2}\hat{R}_{\text{inter}}.

(35)

Therefore, by minimizing
\hat{R}_{\text{CPNS}}
, we explicitly suppress the probability that the decision boundary is non-monotonic with respect to feature separability. This guarantees that as the representation shifts from Confused with Old (
\bar{\mathbf{z}}
) to Distinctly New (
\mathbf{z}
), the model’s confidence in the new task monotonically increases, satisfying the requirement for causal identifiability.

Appendix D
Discussion

D.1
Example of PNS and CPNS

Feature Necessity and Sufficiency (PNS) serves as a fundamental metric for evaluating the causal completeness of learned representations. In the context of our CIL tasks, such as the classification of a Wolf, PNS quantifies the extent to which a feature set is both sufficient to produce the correct label and necessary for that label’s existence. For instance, a background feature like snow might be empirically sufficient to identify a wolf in limited training data, but it is not necessary (e.g., a wolf remains a wolf in a forest). Conversely, pointed ears are necessary for a wolf but not sufficient (as huskies also possess them). A representation with high PNS captures the holistic causal attributes that are intrinsically required for the object’s identity and whose presence invariably indicates it, thereby ensuring the representation is causally complete and robust against spurious correlations.

In the context of Class-Incremental Learning (CIL), we extend this concept to CPNS. The first scope is intra-task PNS, which ensures the causal completeness of task-specific features. Consider a task of learning to classify a Wolf. A standard model might rely on a shortcut feature like pointed ears (sufficient but unnecessary) to minimize training error. However, this representation is fragile. By maximizing Intra-task PNS, the model is forced to capture a complete set of causal factors—including snout shape, fur texture, and eye patterns—ensuring that the representation is not merely a discriminative shortcut but a robust semantic description of the class.

The most critical application of CPNS arises in the inter-task scope, particularly when dealing with semantically similar tasks, such as distinguishing a Husky (new task) from a Wolf (old task). Here, the challenge is not just feature completeness, but feature separability. Since both wolves and huskies share high-level attributes like pointed ears and grey fur, their feature representations naturally tend to collide in the latent space. Inter-task PNS addresses this by constructing a counterfactual collision state. Specifically, it evaluates the model’s ability to distinguish a discriminative state
\mathbf{z}
(where unique Husky features like blue eyes or specific brow markings are present) from a collided state
\bar{\mathbf{z}}
(where these unique features are perturbed to resemble the frozen Wolf features). High Inter-task PNS ensures sufficiency, where the presence of unique Husky features guarantees a correct prediction despite the background interference of Wolf-like attributes, and necessity, where the loss of these unique features (falling back to the collision state) inevitably leads to a loss of the Husky prediction. This mechanism explicitly forces a decision boundary margin between the new and old tasks, preventing the catastrophic forgetting that arises from semantic confusion.

D.2
Reliability and Completeness of the Twin Network

The twin network generates two similar but distinct representation branches for the same input, enabling parallel modeling of the real and hypothetical worlds. We use it to model causal sufficiency and necessity: (i) the real world assesses whether the extracted representation produces the ground-truth label; (ii) the hypothetical world tests whether the representation still yields the correct label under intervention. The main challenge is modeling counterfactual data. However, a fundamental challenge in this causal modeling is the unavailability of ground-truth counterfactual outcomes, as true counterfactual effects involve unobserved scenarios
(
20
)
. To address this, we adhere to the
Minimal Change principle

(
16
)
(Wachter et al., 2017), which posits that valid counterfactuals should result from slight modifications to the target variable that are sufficient to alter the outcome while preserving the distribution of other covariates. Theoretical studies demonstrate that such minimal adjustments allow for accurate counterfactual estimation by maintaining the original data characteristics apart from the intervention.

Guided by this principle, we implement the hypothetical world through gradient-based intervention. Specifically, we derive the counterfactual feature
\bar{\mathbf{c}}
by applying a perturbation
\Delta
to the real-world feature
\hat{\mathbf{c}}
along the gradient direction of the objective function. To satisfy the minimal change principle, we impose a constraint based on KL-divergence:
\mu_{\text{KL}}(\bar{\mathbf{c}},\hat{\mathbf{c}})\leq\epsilon
, ensuring that the counterfactual
\bar{\mathbf{c}}
remains probabilistically similar to the real representation
\hat{\mathbf{c}}
within a small neighborhood. The core idea lies in: (i) constraining the gradient direction to ensure that counterfactual modeling does not deviate from the true implementation in the latent space
(
2
)
; (ii) applying minimal changes to satisfy the semantic consistency. Formally, under the Markov property
(
8
;
3
)
, we assume that given the causal representation
\mathbf{c}
, the label
Y
is independent of other interference variables
F_{s}
(symbolically
(Y\perp F_{s})|\mathbf{c}
). This implies that backdoor paths are blocked, and modifying
\mathbf{c}
alone will not inadvertently bring
F_{s}
into play. Therefore, if
\mathbf{c}
needs to flip, it is sufficient to make small-step modifications to
\mathbf{c}
until the label changes, aligning with the essence of counterfactual reasoning: if I slightly modify
\mathbf{c}
, will
Y
remain the same or change? As long as these small perturbations are enough to alter the model’s output, they effectively simulate the counterfactual condition of the prediction [].

Building upon this gradient-based intervention framework, we tailor the construction of the hypothetical world to address the unique challenges of expansion-based CIL. Unlike standard representation learning, the causal necessity in CIL must be verified from two distinct perspectives: the semantic completeness within the current task and the discriminability against frozen historical representations. Consequently, we extend the twin network to a Double-range Counterfactual mechanism, which generates specific counterfactual features, denoted as
\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{\text{intra}}
and
\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{\text{inter}}
, to evaluate these two aspects under the established minimal change principle.

For the
intra-task perspective
, we focus on the causal completeness of representation. Following the logic that a valid counterfactual should result from the most sensitive minimal adjustment, we generate
\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{\text{intra}}
by applying perturbations along the gradient direction of the current task’s classification loss:

\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{\text{intra}}=\hat{\mathbf{c}}+\Delta_{\text{intra}},\quad\text{with}\quad\Delta_{\text{intra}}=\nabla_{\hat{\mathbf{c}}}\ell(W_{\text{intra}}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{c}},y_{t}),

(37)

where
\Delta_{\text{intra}}
represents the gradient-based intervention constrained by step size
\epsilon
(consistent with the KL constraint). This operation essentially seeks the shortest path in the latent space to alter the model’s decision, thereby rigorously testing whether the current representation
\hat{\mathbf{c}}
captures the minimal necessary causal factors
F_{\text{mc}}
or merely relies on robust spurious correlations. If a minimal perturbation along the adversarial direction is sufficient to flip the prediction, it indicates that the model’s reliance on the causal features is fragile and requires reinforcement.

Simultaneously, for the
inter-task perspective
, the hypothetical world aims to simulate the interference from previous tasks, a scenario we term feature collision. Here, the minimal change principle is adapted to test the robustness of current features against historical features. We employ a projection
\mathcal{P}
to map frozen old features into the current space and generate
\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{\text{inter}}
via:

\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{\text{inter}}=\hat{\mathbf{c}}-\Delta_{\text{inter}},\quad\text{with}\quad\Delta_{\text{inter}}=\beta\cdot\nabla_{\hat{\mathbf{c}}}\|\hat{\mathbf{c}}-\mathcal{P}(f_{\text{old}}(\mathbf{x}))\|_{2}^{2}.

(38)

By directing the gradient-based perturbation towards the frozen old features, we effectively construct a counterfactual instance that maximizes the resemblance to historical features within a constrained range controlled by
\beta
. This process explicitly models the risk of the current task’s representation being subsumed by the shared attributes of old tasks (
F_{\text{old}}
), allowing us to minimize the dependency on confounding proxies and ensure inter-task discriminability.

D.3
Rationality of Binary Simplification

The strategy acts as a constraint rather than a substitute for the primary classifier. The framework is designed as a plug-and-play module where the underlying base method (e.g., DER or TagFex) continues to utilize the standard Cross-Entropy loss over the full label space
\mathcal{Y}_{t}
. This ensures that the model retains its ability to distinguish between specific fine-grained classes. The binary simplification is exclusively applied to the inter-task CPNS risk regularization term
\hat{R}_{\text{inter}}
, serving as a specialized mechanism to enforce separation between the current task’s features and the frozen feature space without discarding the fine-grained discriminative information learned by the backbone.

Furthermore, this design aligns with the inference mechanism of expansion-based CIL. In the decision phase, the classifier operates on the concatenation of all features—combining the frozen representations from previous tasks with the newly learned features []. Since the historical features is integrated as a cohesive block in the final representation, the binary simplification mirrors this structural reality. Therefore, evaluating whether a feature belongs to the unified Old distribution or the discriminative New distribution is the direct and efficient way to measure and mitigate feature collision []. Finally, the binary simplification is a necessity to satisfy the Monotonicity Assumption required for causal identifiability.

D.4
Related Work and Uniqueness Discussion

Causal sufficiency and necessity is a key concept in causal theory, introduced in Pearl’s book Causality
(
20
)
. Yang et al.
(
36
)
and Wang et al.
(
33
)
recently applied it to domain generalization and multi-modal representation learning. To highlight our work’s novelty and distinguish it from prior studies, we outline the key differences.

Expansion-based CIL
Model expansion strategies, rooted in the concept of parameter isolation, typically involve expanding the feature space to accommodate new tasks. PNN
(
22
)
constructs a new backbone for each task while employing layer-wise connections to reuse frozen features , whereas DER
(
35
)
trains a separate backbone for each task and aggregates the features for classification. Similarly, DyTox
(
7
)
assigns a specific task token for each task within a Transformer architecture. To balance plasticity with model size, PC
(
23
)
introduces a progression-compression protocol ; following a similar philosophy, FOSTER
(
31
)
employs a two-stage strategy that first expands new modules to boost feature expression and subsequently removes redundant parameters via distillation. Additionally, BEEF
(
30
)
utilizes energy-based theory to establish a bi-compatible framework. In contrast to these methods which primarily focus on task-specific features, TagFex
(
39
)
continually captures diverse features using a separate task-agnostic model and aggregates them with task-specific features via a merge attention mechanism, effectively mitigating feature collision and enhancing the diversity of the expanded features. Most studies adopt the strategy of diverse feature extraction to mitigate feature conflicts.

However, this diversity strategy leads to the learning process within each task still being dominated by Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM). ERM tends to prioritize the most accessible discriminative cues, leading to feature suppression, where the model captures only the shortcut features sufficient to minimize training loss. Consequently, existing expansion-based methods mainly ensure the diversity of the shortcut features for each task of the model. While this reliance on shortcut features temporarily mitigates feature collision, it inherently sacrifices the model’s robustness against distribution shifts and constrains the representational depth essential for long-term scalability. We argue that addressing feature collision requires moving beyond mere representation diversity; First, ensuring the causal completeness of representations within tasks, and second, guaranteeing the separability of features across tasks. Therefore, we introduce the concept of causal completeness to ensure the causal completeness of the intra-task representations and the separability of inter-task the representations, in order to mitigate the feature conflict problem in expansion-based CIL methods.

Constraining Causal Completeness
The proposed method is significantly different from previous causal-related research […… ]. We extend the causal completeness method proposed by
(
36
)
Yang et al. from domain generalization to expansion-based CIL and propose the concept of ensuring causal integrity both between and within tasks.
Meanwhile, inspired by
(
33
)
Wang et al., we propose a two-range counterfactual modeling approach to ensure causal necessity. Furthermore, our experimental results demonstrate that CPNS consistently improves performance across various expansion-based CIL scenarios.

In summary, we propose a novel CPNS concept and a framework for expansion-based CIL scenarios Although our work and prior studies
(
36
;
25
;
37
;
1
;
4
)
are inspired by causal theory, they differ significantly in problem settings, motivations, theoretical foundations, optimization strategies, and empirical validation.
