Self-similar solutions to the time-fractional Porous-Medium Equation
David Gómez-Castro, Łukasz Płociniczak, Juan Luis Vázquez

Abstract
We show the existence of self-similar solutions with constant finite mass to the time-fractional Porous-Medium Equation for all spatial dimensions d ≥ 1 d\geq 1 and all exponents m > m c = ( d − 2 ) + / d m>m_{c}=(d-2)_{+}/d . This range is optimal. We find two types of solution depending on the exponent: compactly supported solutions in the slow-diffusion range m > 1 m>1 and positive solutions with heavy tails in the sub-critical fast-diffusion range m c < m < 1 m_{c}<m<1 . The self-similar solutions in the linear case m = 1 m=1 were already known explicitly obtained by the Fourier transform, and we discuss their properties in our settings and the limit m → 1 m\to 1 . Keywords. Porous medium equation; fast diffusion equation; Caputo fractional-time derivative; self-similar solutions.

Introduction and main results

In this paper, we discuss the nonnegative self-similar solutions of the nonlinear time-fractional diffusion equation for
m>0
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
given by

\partial_{t}^{\alpha}u=\Delta(u^{m})

(1)

for
t>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for any

Here

is the Caputo derivative

\partial_{t}^{\alpha}u(t,x)=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{(t-s)^{-\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\partial_{t}u(s,x)ds.

(2)

The solutions start from a Dirac delta
u(0,\cdot)=M\delta_{0}
in the sense of initial traces:

\operatorname*{ess\,lim}_{t\to 0^{+}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}u(t,x)\varphi(x)dx=M\varphi(0),

(3)

for all

of the equation that have a finite mass for all

M>0
in the sense that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u(t,x)dx=M\qquad\text{for all }t>0.

By self-similar solution, we refer to space-time function, which can be re-scaled to a profile

in the sense that

(5)

for similarity exponents

Our main contribution is the study of the existence and key properties of

and its dependence on parameters

and mass

The classical case

2
Weak formulation

In this section we discuss the weak formulation, and provide a lemma that allows to characterize self-similar solutions as weak solution based on the properties of the profile.

2.1
Weak formulation for smooth solutions

We begin by showing that the weak formulation is reasonable for smooth solutions.
By Fubini, we deduce that

\displaystyle\Gamma(\alpha)\int_{\tau}^{T}[I_{1-\alpha}f(t)]\,g(T-t)dt

\displaystyle=\int_{\tau}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\alpha}f(s)g(T-t)dsdt

\displaystyle=\int_{\tau}^{T}\int_{s}^{T}(t-s)^{-\alpha}g(T-t)dt\,f(s)ds

\displaystyle=\int_{\tau}^{T}\int_{0}^{T-s}(T-\sigma-s)^{-\alpha}g(\sigma)d\sigma\,f(s)ds

\displaystyle=\Gamma(\alpha)\int_{\tau}^{T}f(s)I_{1-\alpha}g(T-s)ds.

This means that

\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{t}^{\alpha}f(t)g(T-t)dt=\int_{0}^{T}f(t){{}^{RL}\partial_{t}^{\alpha}}g(T-t)dt+f(T)I_{1-\alpha}g(0)-f(0)I_{1-\alpha}g(T).

Notice that if
g\in C([0,T])
then
I_{1-\alpha}g(0)=0
for
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
For
\alpha=1
we have
I_{1-\alpha}g=g
.
We write therefore that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}u(t,x){{}^{RL}\partial_{t}^{\alpha}}\varphi(T-t,x)dxdt

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u(0,x)I_{1-\alpha}\varphi(T,x)dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u(T,x)I_{1-\alpha}\varphi(0,x)dx

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}u^{m}(t,x)\Delta\varphi(T-t,x)dxdt.

Since
\varphi
is bounded, we have
I_{1-\alpha}\varphi(0,x)=0
and therefore

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}u(t,x){{}^{RL}\partial_{t}^{\alpha}}\varphi(T-t,x)dxdt

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u(0,x)I_{1-\alpha}\varphi(T,x)dx

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}u^{m}(t,x)\Delta\varphi(T-t,x)dxdt.

This integration by parts holds for all
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
since
{{}^{RL}\partial_{t}^{\alpha}}
does not notice finite values of
\varphi(0,x)
.

First, let us obtain the scaling factors

Kernel estimates

We focus on the representation using (

We begin this section with some notation
For

if there exists
c(a,b)
,

We will set

\overset{(0,+\infty)}{\asymp}

For
\eta\sim 1^{-}
(specifically for

.

by focusing on the remaining singular term. Using the change of variable

3.7
Limits of the kernel as
\alpha\to 1^{-}

Notice that

F_{\alpha}(z)=\int_{0}^{1}\sigma^{-\alpha}U(\sigma^{-b}z)d\mu_{\alpha}(1-\sigma),\qquad d\mu_{\alpha}(z)=\frac{t^{-\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}dz.

It is easy to see that
\mu_{\alpha}\to\delta_{0}
and hence

F_{\alpha}(z)\to U(z)\qquad\text{ as }\alpha\to 1^{-}.

Going back to (
28
) we can expect that

K_{\alpha,m}(z,\rho)\to K_{1,m}(\rho)\coloneqq b\rho\text{ as }\alpha\to 1\text{ for each }0<z\leq\rho.

(38)

We prove this rigorously.

Lemma 3.6
.

Assume
Q(\eta)
vanishes for
\eta\geq 1
, and for
\eta\in(0,1)
its derivative is given according to (
29
).
Then, we have that

\lim_{\alpha\to 1^{-}}Q_{\alpha,m}(\eta)=Q_{1,m}\coloneqq b_{1,m}\qquad\text{ for all }\eta\in(0,1).

Proof.

For this proof we will denote
b_{\alpha}=b_{\alpha,m}
. Using the change of variables
s=1-\sigma^{\frac{1}{b_{\alpha}}}
, which implies
\sigma=(1-s)^{b_{\alpha}}
and
d\sigma=-b_{\alpha}(1-s)^{b_{\alpha}-1}ds
, the integration limits change from
[\eta,1]
to
[1-\eta^{1/b_{\alpha}},0]
. We then have

\displaystyle Q_{\alpha,m}(\eta)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1-\eta^{1/b_{\alpha}}}\frac{s^{-\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}(1-s)^{b_{\alpha}(1-d)}b_{\alpha}(1-s)^{b_{\alpha}-1}ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1-\eta^{1/b_{\alpha}}}\frac{s^{-\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\Psi_{\alpha}(s)ds\qquad\text{ where }\Psi_{\alpha}(s)=b_{\alpha}(1-s)^{b_{\alpha}(2-d)-1}.

We decompose the integral into two parts:

\left|Q_{\alpha,m}(\eta)-\Psi_{\alpha}(0)\int_{0}^{1-\eta^{1/b_{\alpha}}}\frac{s^{-\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}ds\right|\leq\int_{0}^{1-\eta^{1/b_{\alpha}}}\frac{s^{-\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\left|\Psi_{\alpha}(s)-\Psi_{\alpha}(0)\right|ds.

Observing that
\Psi_{\alpha}(0)=b_{\alpha,m}
, the first integral evaluates exactly to:

\displaystyle\Psi_{\alpha}(0)\int_{0}^{1-\eta^{1/b_{\alpha}}}\frac{s^{-\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}ds

\displaystyle=b_{\alpha,m}\frac{(1-\eta^{1/b_{\alpha}})^{1-\alpha}}{(1-\alpha)\Gamma(1-\alpha)}=b_{\alpha,m}\frac{(1-\eta^{1/b_{\alpha}})^{1-\alpha}}{\Gamma(2-\alpha)}.

As
\alpha\to 1^{-}
, we have
b_{\alpha,m}\to b_{1,m}
, the exponent
(1-\alpha)\to 0^{+}
, and
\Gamma(2-\alpha)\to\Gamma(1)=1
. Since
\eta\in(0,1)
, the base
(1-\eta^{1/b_{\alpha}})
is strictly positive, meaning
(1-\eta^{1/b_{\alpha}})^{1-\alpha}\to 1
. Thus, this leading term converges exactly to
b_{1,m}
.

For the second term, we point out that
\Psi_{\alpha}
is smooth near
s=0
. Because
\eta\in(0,1)
, the upper integration limit is uniformly bounded away from
1
: there exists an
\varepsilon>0
such that
1-\eta^{1/b_{\alpha}}\leq 1-\varepsilon
for all
\alpha
close to
1
. On the interval
[0,1-\varepsilon]
,
\Psi_{\alpha}
is Lipschitz continuous uniformly in
\alpha
. Thus, there exists a constant
C>0
independent of
\alpha
such that
|\Psi_{\alpha}(s)-\Psi_{\alpha}(0)|\leq Cs
.
Therefore, we can bound the remainder by:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1-\varepsilon}\frac{s^{-\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\left|\Psi_{\alpha}(s)-\Psi_{\alpha}(0)\right|ds

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{1-\varepsilon}s^{1-\alpha}ds\leq\frac{C}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)(2-\alpha)}.

As
\alpha\to 1^{-}
, we have
\Gamma(1-\alpha)\to\infty
. This causes the upper bound to vanish. Combining both parts, we conclude that
\lim_{\alpha\to 1^{-}}Q_{\alpha,m}(\eta)=b_{1,m}
.
∎

5.1
Uniqueness of solutions to the profile equation

Let

\fixedpoint U(r)=\left(\int_{r}^{\infty}K(r,\rho)U(\rho)d\rho\right)^{1/m}.

We have the following fundamental result.

Theorem 5.1

(Forward uniqueness)
.

Assume that
U_{1}\in C((0,\infty))
with
\text{supp}(U_{1})=[0,R]
and
U_{2}\in C((0,\infty))
are solutions of
U_{i}=\fixedpoint U_{i}
in
(z_{0},+\infty)
. If
U_{1}(z_{0})=U_{2}(z_{0})
then
U_{1}=U_{2}
in
[z_{0},+\infty)
.

We start with a lemma that strongly uses the homogeneity of the operator.

Lemma 5.2
.

Assume
U_{1},U_{2}\in C([z_{0},\infty))
are non-negative, such that
U_{i}=\fixedpoint U_{i}
in
[z_{0},+\infty)
, and the set

\Lambda=\{\lambda>0:\lambda U_{1}\leq U_{2}\text{ in }[z_{0},R]\}

is non-empty, then
U_{1}\leq U_{2}
.

Proof.

Consider
\lambda_{*}=\sup\Lambda>0
. Since
K\geq 0
we have that

U_{2}=\fixedpoint U_{2}\geq\fixedpoint(\lambda_{*}U_{1})=\lambda_{*}^{\frac{1}{m}}\fixedpoint U_{1}=\lambda_{*}^{\frac{1}{m}}U_{1}

Therefore,
\lambda_{*}^{\frac{1}{m}}\in\Lambda
, we deduce
\lambda_{*}\geq\lambda_{*}^{\frac{1}{m}}
, and hence
\lambda_{*}\in\{0\}\cup[1,+\infty)
. We conclude that
\lambda_{*}\geq 1
and so
U_{1}\leq U_{2}
.
∎

We now proceed to the proof of the main result.

Proof of
Theorem

5.1
.

First, let us prove that
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
have the same support. Since
K
is positive then the supports are of the form
[0,R_{i}]
with
R_{1}\in(z_{0},\infty)
and
R_{2}\in[z_{0},+\infty]
, and
U_{i}>0
in
(z_{0},R_{i})
.

Assume, towards a contradiction, that
R_{1}<R_{2}
. Then, we have
U_{2}/U_{1}\geq c>0
is bounded below in
\text{supp}(U_{1})
.
Since outside

the claim

is trivial, then

. Therefore, by the previous lemma
U_{1}\leq U_{2}
. Assume that

in some set

of positive measure. Then, since

for

But
U_{1}(z_{0})=U_{2}(z_{0})
. We conclude that
U_{1}=U_{2}
, and hence

. This is a contradiction.

If
R_{2}<R_{1}
then

is finite, and we can switch the roles in the previous argument. Hence, we deduce that

.

Now let us consider
U_{2}^{(\varepsilon)}
a re-scaling of support

using (

Since

is non-increasing, and it is positive at

, then it is strictly positive in

(50)

is in

and it is a solution to the problem.
Furthermore, using (

and we have the estimate

U^{*}(z)(1-z^{-\gamma^{*}})_{+}\leq\mathcal{U}(z)\leq U^{*}(z)(1-z^{-\gamma^{*}}+z^{-\frac{3}{2}\gamma^{*}}),\qquad\text{for all }z\geq z_{0}>0.

Let us define

U_{R}(z)\coloneqq R^{\frac{-2}{1-m}}\mathcal{U}\left(\frac{z}{R}\right).

Lemma 6.6
.

For
m\in(0,1)
and
z>0
we have that
U_{R}(z)
is a decreasing sequence in
R
and, furthermore, for each
z>0

U_{R}(z)\to\begin{cases}U^{*}(z)&\text{as }R\to 0,\\
0&\text{as }R\to\infty.\end{cases}

Proof.

Notice that

U_{R}(z)=c^{*}z^{-\frac{2}{1-m}}\Big(1-R^{\gamma^{*}}z^{-\gamma^{*}}+o\left(R^{\gamma^{*}}z^{-\gamma^{*}}\right)\Big).

Hence, for
z
fixed, we have

U_{R}(z)\to U^{*}(z)\text{ as }R\to 0.

Furthermore, if
\overline{R}>\underline{R}
then we have that

U_{\overline{R}}(z)<U_{\underline{R}}(z)
for
z
sufficiently large. Consider

z_{1}=\inf\{Z>0:\text{for all }z\in(Z,+\infty)\text{ we have that }U_{\overline{R}}(z)<U_{\underline{R}}(z)\}.

Assume that
z_{1}>0
. Then
U_{\overline{R}}(z_{1})=U_{\underline{R}}(z_{1})

U_{\overline{R}}(z_{1})^{m}=\int_{z_{1}}^{\infty}K(z,\rho)U_{\overline{R}}(\rho)d\rho<\int_{z_{1}}^{\infty}K(z,\rho)U_{\underline{R}}(\rho)d\rho=U_{\underline{R}}(z_{1})^{m},

which is a contradiction. There
U_{R}(z)
is decreasing in
R
for all
z>0
. Now consider

U_{\infty}(z)=\lim_{R\to\infty}U_{R}(z).

Due to the monotone convergence theorem
U_{\infty}(z)=TU_{\infty}(z)
for each
z>0
. Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle U_{\infty}(z)

\displaystyle=\inf_{R>0}U_{R}(z)=\inf_{R>0}R^{-\frac{2}{1-m}}\mathcal{U}(z/R)=\inf_{R>0}U^{*}(z)\frac{\mathcal{U}(z/R)}{U^{*}(z/R)}=U^{*}(z)\inf_{R>0}\frac{\mathcal{U}(z/R)}{U^{*}(z/R)}

\displaystyle=U^{*}(z)\inf_{y>0}\frac{\mathcal{U}(y)}{U^{*}(y)}=c_{\infty}U^{*}(z).

Since
\mathcal{U}<U^{*}
we have
c_{\infty}<1

c_{\infty}^{m}U^{*}(z)^{m}=c_{\infty}U^{*}(z)^{m}

For
d=1

Notice that since

\displaystyle-(U_{R}^{m})^{\prime}(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{z}^{\infty}(-Q^{\prime})\left(\tfrac{z}{\rho}\right)U_{R}(\rho)d\rho

\displaystyle\leq U_{R}(z)\int_{z}^{1}(-Q^{\prime})\left(\tfrac{z}{\rho}\right)d\rho+\int_{1}^{\infty}(-Q^{\prime})\left(\tfrac{z}{\rho}\right)U_{R}(\rho)d\rho

\displaystyle\leq U_{R}(z)\int_{z}^{1}(-Q^{\prime})\left(\rho^{-1}\right)d\rho+\int_{1}^{\infty}(-Q^{\prime})\left(\rho^{-1}\right)U_{R}(\rho)d\rho

\displaystyle\leq U_{R}(z)A+B_{R}.

Due to

6.6

B_{R}\to 0
as

. We also have
U_{R}(1)\to 0
. Let us define
W(s)=[U_{R}(1-s)]^{m}

W^{\prime}(s)\leq AW(s)^{\frac{1}{m}}+B_{R},\qquad W(0)=U_{R}(1)^{m}.

Integrating this differential inequality, we see that if

U(0)^{m}=W(1)<\infty

Since

For

.

Limit
m\to 1
. Proof of
Theorem

1.8

Like in the case
\alpha=1
, we expect continuity with respect to
m
and
U_{\alpha,m,M}
with respect to
m
. Already in this case, the limits are tricky to compute. We have some preliminary results.
Due to (
33
) and (
34
) we have that
\{U_{\alpha,m,M}^{m}:m\neq 1\}
is pre-compact in
C_{loc}((0,+\infty))
.
Consider
\widehat{U}
the limit of any convergent sub-sequence with
m_{j}\to 1
.
Also, by Fatou’s lemma we have that

\widehat{M}=|\partial B_{1}|\int_{0}^{\infty}\widehat{U}(z)z^{d-1}dz\leq M.

Assume, in contradiction, that the tightness in
L^{1}(0,\infty,z^{d-1})
does not hold. Then, there are some
c>0
, and sequences
R_{j}\to\infty
and a subsequence
m_{j}\to 1
(which we do not relabel) such that
V_{j}=U_{\alpha,m_{j},M}

\int_{R_{j}}^{\infty}V_{j}(\rho)\rho^{d-1}d\rho\geq c.

Then we have that

z^{d-1}(-V_{j}^{m_{j}}(z))^{\prime}\geq\int_{z}^{\infty}-\frac{z^{d-1}}{\rho^{d-1}}Q^{\prime}(\tfrac{z}{\rho})\rho^{d-1}\widehat{U}(\rho)d\rho\geq\frac{c}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\text{for }z<R_{j}.

Integrating once, we deduce that

V_{j}^{m_{j}}(z)\geq\frac{c}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{z}^{R_{j}}\rho^{1-d}d\rho\qquad\text{for all }z<R_{j}.

Case
d=1
.

Then we have
V_{j}^{m_{j}}(z)\geq\frac{c}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}(R_{j}-z)\to\infty
for each
z
fixed as
j\to\infty
. This contradicts the point-wise convergence.

Case
d=2
.

Then we have
V_{j}^{m_{j}}(z)\geq\frac{c}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\log\frac{R_{j}}{z}\to\infty
for each
z
fixed as
j\to\infty
. This contradicts the point-wise convergence.

Case
d\geq 3
.

Then we have that
V_{j}^{m_{j}}(z)\geq\frac{c}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)(d-2)}(z^{2-d}-R_{j}^{2-d})
.
This implies that the point-wise limit satisfies

\widehat{U}(z)\geq\frac{c}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)(d-2)}z^{2-d}.

Using Fatou’s lemma, we deduce that

\widehat{M}\geq|\partial B_{1}|\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho^{d-1}V(\rho)d\rho\geq\frac{c|\partial B_{1}|}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)(d-2)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho d\rho=+\infty.

This is a contradiction. Hence, the family
z^{d-1}U_{\alpha,m_{j},1}(z)
is tight in
L^{1}(0,\infty)
.

Due to the tightness and the convergence over compactness, we deduce that as
j\to\infty
we have that

\int_{0}^{\infty}|U_{\alpha,m_{j},M}(z)-\widehat{U}(z)|z^{d-1}dz\to 0.

Due to the bounds on
Q_{\alpha,m}
and the convergence
Q_{\alpha,m}\to Q_{\alpha,1}
as
m\to 1
this is sufficient to prove that

\widehat{U}(z)=\int_{z}^{\infty}K_{\alpha,1}(\rho)\widehat{U}(\rho)d\rho,\qquad|\partial B_{1}|\int_{0}^{\infty}\widehat{U}(\rho)\rho^{d-1}d\rho=M.

By the uniqueness result
Theorem

1.4
this implies that
\widehat{U}=U_{\alpha,1,M}.

For every
m_{j}\to 1
the sequence
U_{\alpha,m_{j},M}
is pre-compact in
C_{loc}((0,+\infty))
, and the limit of any convergent subsequence is
U_{\alpha,1,M}
, then the whole sequence converges.
∎

8
Extensions and open problems

Uniqueness of weak solutions with Dirac initial data.

We expect that the arguments in
[
24
]
for the uniqueness of the Porous-Medium Equation with measure initial data can be adapted to this setting. The idea of the proof is to study
v=E*u
(where
E
is either the Green function for
-\Delta
if
d\geq 3
or
-\Delta+\varepsilon I
if
d=1,2
).

Asymptotic behavior for general initial data.

We have constructed a family of self-similar profiles
U_{\alpha,m}
.
An open problem remains regarding the stability of these profiles: are they long-time global attractors for the corresponding general Cauchy problem? In the classical case
\alpha=1
, this problem is very well understood
[
29
]
.
For
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
m=1
this was shown in
[
11
]
.
However, in the time-fractional model, there are no corresponding results in the literature.

The very-singular fast-diffusion problem.

The range
0<m<m_{c}
,
d\geq 3
, is usually called very singular fast diffusion. The tools used in this paper are not suitable for treating this range for the equation (
1
) with the fractional time derivative. To our knowledge, the problem is completely open.

In the standard case
\alpha=1
much is known. According to the theory developed, fundamental solutions with finite mass exist but do not have the type of self-similarity used in this paper, there is no mass conservation, and they extinguish in finite time: see
[
10
,
30
,
32
,
31
,
7
]
.

Fully non-local problems.

The Porous Medium in non-local pressure
[
3
]
.
Another interesting family of problems is
\partial_{t}^{\alpha}u=(-\Delta)^{s}u^{m}
. Let the linear case
m=1
already be well understood; see
[
12
]
.

Numerical analysis for
m_{c}<m<1
.

In this paper, we include a discussion of the scheme for
m>1
. This case is manageable, as we have compactly supported profiles. From a theoretical point of view, the scheme is also valid for
m_{c}<m<1
. In this case, the solutions have power-type tails, and hence smart truncations are needed in order to obtain an implementable scheme.

The asymptotic fan for
0<m<m_{c}
above
U^{*}
.

For
\alpha=1
it is know that there exists self-similar profiles of the form
U^{*}(z)(1+z^{-\gamma}+o(z^{-\gamma}))
that blow up at some
z=z_{1}
. By re-scaling this creates an ordered family of solutions above
U^{*}(z)
, each of which blow up at a different
z>0
.

Uniqueness solutions to (
9
) when
m<1
.

In this manuscript we have only proven the uniqueness of solutions to (
9
) in the case
m>1
, by taking advantage of the super-linear homogeneity and the compact support. The case
m_{c}<m<1
remains open.

Travelling waves.

This is a topic where much is known in the standard case and probably nothing in the fractional time derivative case.

Sign-changing self-similar profiles.

In principle, we cannot rule out the possibility that there exist sign-changing solutions to (
9
). We do not find them particularly physically relevant and we will not discuss them further.
