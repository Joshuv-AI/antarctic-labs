Is Productivity Advantage of Cities Really Down To Mean and Variance?
Vladislav Morozov, Andrea Sy

Abstract
Firms in denser areas are more productive, a pattern attributed to agglomeration economies and firm selection. To disentangle these two channels, the popular approach of Combes et al. 2012 critically assumes that total factor productivity (TFP) distributions between denser and less dense areas are the same up to mean, variance, and left-tail truncation. We empirically validate this assumption using Spanish administrative firm-level data and recent econometric methods adapted to noisy TFP estimates. Our results find that TFP distributions are indeed statistically identical up to these parameters, validating the use of such productivity decompositions. Furthermore, using only the mean and variance is sufficient to capture differences for all sectors. Accordingly, the productivity advantage of cities may be entirely due to agglomeration rather than stronger selection, suggesting that policymakers should focus on policies targeting agglomeration. Finally, our approach extends to related contexts like differences in worker skill distributions.



1
Introduction

Firms in denser areas are systematically more productive
(
Melo et al. 2009
;
Combes and Gobillon 2015
;
Duranton and Puga 2020
)
. This productivity advantage may reflect agglomeration economies or firm selection — the concentration of firms in dense environments making them more productive, or stronger competition in denser areas culling weaker firms at higher rates, respectively. Understanding which of these mechanisms drives the productivity advantage of cities is important for evaluating place-based interventions like tax incentives, infrastructure investments, or regional development programs
(
Neumark and Simpson 2015
)
.

Combes et al. 2012
disentangle these channels by assuming that the distributions of total factor productivity (TFP) across denser and less dense areas are the same except for shifts in mean and variance that reflect agglomeration and for tail truncation due to firm selection. Under this assumption, they decompose productivity differences along the two channels. If, however, the TFP distributions across areas differed in other ways (e.g. tail behavior or shape), this decomposition may be invalid and instead reflect those differences, complicating interpretations of sorting patterns
(
Gaubert 2018
)
and evaluations of place‑based interventions.

Despite its central role, this assumption has not been directly tested. Empirical validation is challenging because productivity is not directly observed, but instead estimated with noise. Thus, naive comparisons of TFP distributions based on noisy TFP estimates may conflate (policy-irrelevant) noise with true heterogeneity
(
Jochmans and Weidner 2024
)
.

This paper empirically tests this assumption of distribution equality. Using Spanish administrative firm-level data, we nonparametrically test for differences in TFP distributions across denser and less dense areas, using methods that account for estimation noise in TFP. Across all sectors, we find that TFP distributions share the same shape, with differences fully explained by the parameters posited by
Combes et al. 2012
.

Our results suggest that urban productivity advantages may be fully explained by agglomeration rather than stronger selection. Of the three
Combes et al. 2012
parameters, only the mean and variance are needed to align the TFP distributions across areas, while the tail truncation parameter is not needed. This suggests that policies enhancing agglomeration, such as improving infrastructure or fostering knowledge spillovers, may be more effective than competition‑enhancing policies, such as reducing entry barriers or promoting firm turnover.

This paper makes three contributions. First, we empirically validate the distribution equality assumption in
Combes et al. 2012
, complementing the partial results of
Morozov 2026
, who finds no evidence of left-tail truncation. Second, our methodology for testing distribution equality with noisy data is also applicable to other contexts, such as geographical differences in worker skills
(
de la Roca and Puga 2017
)
. Last, we contribute to the literature on working with noisy estimates of unobserved heterogeneity
(
Okui and Yanagi 2019
;
Jochmans and Weidner 2024
;
Morozov 2026
)
by proposing a goodness-of-fit test based on noisy observations.

2
Setting and Distribution Equality Hypotheses

To evaluate the assumption of distribution equality for TFPs, we work in the setting of
Combes et al. 2012
(CDGPR12 henceforth). Their approach proceeds in two steps: first estimating firm-specific TFPs, then comparing productivity distributions between high-density (above-median density, AMD) and low-density (below-median density, BMD) areas.

Following CDGPR12, we define firm-level TFP
\theta_{i}
by assuming that firm
i
in year
t
, sector
s
, and area
a\in\{\text{AMD}\mathchar 59\relax\text{BMD}\}
produces value added
V_{i\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}
according to a Cobb-Douglas production function:

V_{it}=\exp(\theta_{i})K_{it}^{\beta_{1\mathchar 59\relax s\mathchar 59\relax a}}L_{i\,t}^{\beta_{2\mathchar 59\relax s\mathchar 59\relax a}}\exp(U_{it}+\beta_{0\mathchar 59\relax t\mathchar 59\relax s\mathchar 59\relax a})

(1)

where
\theta_{i}
is firm (log) total factor productivity (TFP),
K_{it}
and
L_{it}
are capital and labor, and
U_{it}
captures measurement error in
V_{it}
. Here
\beta_{1\mathchar 59\relax s\mathchar 59\relax a}
and
\beta_{2\mathchar 59\relax s\mathchar 59\relax a}
are sector- and area-specific factor shares, while
\beta_{0\mathchar 59\relax t\mathchar 59\relax s\mathchar 59\relax a}
is a time-specific intercept, allowing for productivity trends. Firms in sector

draw
\theta_{i}
from
F_{s\mathchar 59\relax AMD}
(AMD) and
F_{s\mathchar 59\relax BMD}
(BMD).

To separate agglomeration economies and selection, CDGPR12 assume that
F_{s\mathchar 59\relax AMD}
and
F_{s\mathchar 59\relax BMD}
differ only in location (
\mu
), scale (
\sigma
), and left tail truncation
(\xi)

F_{s\mathchar 59\relax AMD}(\theta)=\max\left\{0\mathchar 59\relax\dfrac{F_{s\mathchar 59\relax BMD}\left(\frac{\theta-\mu}{\sigma}\right)-\xi}{1-\xi}\right\}.

(2)

Here
(\mu\mathchar 59\relax\sigma)
capture agglomeration economies, while
\xi
reflects stronger competition in denser areas. CDGPR12 view (

(\mu\mathchar 59\relax\sigma\mathchar 59\relax\xi)

Our goal is to test assumption (
2
). If it fails, then the CDGPR12 estimators of
(\mu\mathchar 59\relax\sigma\mathchar 59\relax\xi)
may potentially be biased or produce quantities that are challenging to interpret.
Formally, we test the following null of distribution equality up to mean and variance for every sector

:

H_{0}:\quad F_{s\mathchar 59\relax AMD}\left(\dfrac{\theta-\mu_{s\mathchar 59\relax AMD}}{\sigma_{s\mathchar 59\relax AMD}}\right)=F_{s\mathchar 59\relax BMD}\left(\frac{\theta-\mu_{s\mathchar 59\relax BMD}}{\sigma_{s\mathchar 59\relax BMD}}\right)\text{ for all }\theta\in\R

(3)

where
\mu_{s\mathchar 59\relax AMD}\mathchar 59\relax\mu_{s\mathchar 59\relax BMD}\mathchar 59\relax\sigma_{s\mathchar 59\relax AMD}\mathchar 59\relax\sigma_{s\mathchar 59\relax BMD}
are AMD/BMD sector-specific location and scale parameters.
This null is more restrictive than (and hence
implies
) CDGPR12’s, as it excludes
\xi
. We omit
\xi
based on
Morozov 2026

5
show that location-scale shifts are sufficient for distribution equality for all

.

Importantly, our analysis is fully agnostic about how
F_{s\mathchar 59\relax AMD}
and
F_{s\mathchar 59\relax BMD}
arise or how to interpret
(\mu\mathchar 59\relax\sigma)
. We also make no functional form assumptions on
F_{s\mathchar 59\relax AMD}
and
F_{s\mathchar 59\relax BMD}
.

Testing the null in (
3

\theta_{i}
are latent and must be estimated from data using some
\hat{\theta}_{i}
, as is standard in the literature.
Second,
\hat{\theta}_{i}
are noisy measurements of
\theta_{i}
. As
Jochmans and Weidner 2024

\hat{\theta}_{i}
may lead to invalid inference. We now address these challenges in turn.

3
Data and TFP Estimation

We use administrative balance sheet and income statement data from the Banco de España’s CBI dataset
(
Banco De España 2026
, remote access, 2024 vintage)
from 2000 to 2019. CBI is representative, covering
\sim
80% of incorporated non-financial Spanish firms and matching the aggregate dynamics of output, employment and wages
(
Almunia et al. 2018
)
. All monetary variables are deflated using industry-specific deflators from EU-KLEMS.

Firms are classified as belonging to AMD and BMD areas using the experienced density measure from
de la Roca and Puga 2017
. This measure averages population within a fixed radius around individuals, smoothing boundary irregularities. Urban areas are defined according to Spain’s Ministry of Housing, excluding the exclave cities Ceuta and Melilla. Firms are linked to urban areas via municipality-postal code concordances from Instituto Nacional de Estadística. Additional standard data cleaning steps are detailed in the Appendix.

Productivity estimates
\hat{\theta}_{i}
are obtained in two steps, mirroring
Combes et al. 2012
:

(1)

For each sector
s
and area
a
(AMD/BMD), we estimate the production function (
1
) using the standard approach of
Ackerberg et al. 2015
on the full sample.

(2)

We compute
\hat{\theta}_{i}
as the average residual from the estimated production function:

\hat{\theta}_{i}=T^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left[\log(V_{it})-\hat{\beta}_{0\mathchar 59\relax s\mathchar 59\relax t}-\hat{\beta}_{1\mathchar 59\relax s}\log(K_{it})-\hat{\beta}_{2\mathchar 59\relax s}\log(L_{it})\right].

(4)

Importantly, for testing (
3
), we retain only firms observed for at least 15 periods to test the null (
3
). This restriction is done for two reasons:

(1)

Economically, this aligns our sample with the theoretical framework in
Combes et al. 2012
which captures the long-run setting in which selection effects are expected to manifest (see their lemma 1). If instead one looks at the distribution of
all
firms in a given moment, that distribution may include unproductive firms that entered recently and have not yet been culled by competition. In contrast, such firms are less present in the sample of longer-lived firms.

(2)

Statistically, this restriction enables valid inference by sufficiently controlling the estimation noise in the TFP estimators
\hat{\theta}
, see section
4
and the Appendix.

The number of such firms varies between
101
and
16649
, depending on the sector and area type (see table
1
for exact counts). 15 years is a compromise between having sufficiently long time series to capture selection, control estimation error (in the sense discussed in section
4
) and maintaining a sufficient cross-sectional sample size for testing.

4
Methodology for Testing Distribution Equality

Having constructed
\hat{\theta}_{i}
, we proceed to test the null (
3
) of distribution equality up to mean and variance. To do so, we use a tailored nonparametric two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) test with debiasing. We tailor the test to handle two key challenges:

(1)

Noisy TFP estimates: productivity
\theta_{i}
is estimated with error, which can bias comparisons of distributions if not addressed
(
Jochmans and Weidner 2024
)
;

(2)

Estimated parameters: the mean and variance of each distribution must also be estimated, and we need to account for the corresponding estimation uncertainty.

Our approach — formally defined in the Appendix — modifies the standard KS test in two ways to address these challenges:

(1)

Half-panel jackknife debiasing
(
Dhaene and Jochmans 2015
;
Jochmans and Weidner 2024
)
to correct the bias introduced by noisy TFP estimates;

(2)

Firm-level bootstrap to account for all sources of sampling variability.

The test is straightforward. For each sector, we ask whether the standardized distributions of TFP (adjusted for mean and variance) are identical across AMD and BMD areas. Any remaining differences would indicate that the assumption of distributional equality is violated. The debiased test is statistically valid even with noisy TFP estimates thanks to the restriction on the minimal number of observations per firm (see section
3
and the Appendix).

Interpreted narrowly, this finding validates the core assumption of CDGPR12 in the data, justifying their decomposition framework (and related methods) in prior work.

More broadly, our results suggest an even stronger relationship between TFP distributions: just two parameters — mean and variance — suffice to align the distributions up to the resolution afforded by our data. This suggests that the productivity differences between denser and less dense areas are driven predominantly by mechanisms that operate through these channels, such as various aspects of agglomeration economies.

Importantly, our results are nonparametric in nature, requiring only correct specification of sectoral production functions. They allow for trends in productivity, impose no restrictions on the interpretation of mean-variance shifts, and make no assumptions on functional forms of the TFP distributions.

These findings narrow the range of plausible explanations for productivity differences between areas. Since agglomeration economies are the leading explanation for mean-variance shifts, future research should focus on mechanisms operating through these channels. Policies aimed at fostering agglomeration (improvements in local infrastructure, labor market thickness, or knowledge spillovers) may help reduce productivity gaps between dense and sparse areas. Conversely, policies targeting firm selection (e.g., through competition policy) may be less effective, given the absence of tail truncation in our results and the extreme quantile results of
Morozov 2026
.

6
Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we empirically validate the key assumption of
Combes et al. 2012
that TFP distributions differ between denser and less dense areas only in mean, variance, and tail truncation. Using Spanish firm-level data and econometric methods robust to noisy productivity estimates, we show that this assumption holds across all sectors. Moreover, the mean and variance alone are sufficient to account for differences in distributions. Our results support the use of models relying on this assumption in policy analyses of urban productivity gaps and the design of place-based interventions.

Our findings suggest two further areas of research. First, future work could explore the specific mechanisms driving mean-variance shifts in productivity distributions, such as local infrastructure, labor market thickness, or knowledge spillovers, to help policymakers design more effective place-based interventions. Second, our statistical approach — combining HPJ debiasing with nonparametric distribution tests — may provide a template for validating assumptions in other contexts where noisy estimates are used (e.g., worker skills in
de la Roca and Puga 2017
or mutual fund manager skills in
Barras et al. 2022
).

Appendix A
Appendix: Further Data Preparation Details

As mentioned in section
3
, we apply several cleaning steps to CBI data prior to production function estimation, as follows:

(1)

For years with missing data, we use the next year’s values if available, following
González and Sy 2025
. If both current and next year’s filings exist, we prioritize the current year’s values unless flagged as low-quality.

(2)

We keep firms that have a legal status of corporation or limited liability company. This is inferred from the Spanish tax identification number of firms (
CIF
/
NIF
). We treat observations that switch legal status as observations of the same firm.

(3)

For firms with multiple industry codes, we use the most frequent one.

(4)

We drop observations with negative or missing values for assets, revenues, tangible fixed assets, inputs, employees, or wage bill (following
Kalemli-Ozcan et al. 2024
).

(5)

We exclude the following sectors, following
Almunia et al. 2018
: financial services, public administration and defense, education, health and social work, and membership activities (NACE codes K, O, P, Q, and S). We also drop observations without sector classification or postal code information.

Before estimating production functions, we further exclude observations with more than 50 employees to mitigate the presence of multi-establishment firms. We also drop two-digit sectors with fewer than 300 observations in either AMD or BMD areas to maintain sample balance in estimation. After all cleaning steps, the final sample sizes for production function estimation range from 407 to 1,194,699 observations, depending on sector and area. Less than 3% of total observations are lost in cleaning.

In the estimation of the production function using
Ackerberg et al. 2015
, value-added
V_{it}
is measured using gross value-added, capital
K_{it}
is measured using tangible fixed assets, and labor input
L_{it}
is measured using wages instead of employment to control for differences in labor quality and actual hours worked across firms
(
Hsieh and Klenow 2009
)
.

(5)

where

,

HPJ Debiasing

The key challenge is that
\hat{\theta}_{i}
— our TFP estimators — are noisy measurements of
\theta_{i}
. As
Jochmans and Weidner 2024
show, using
\hat{\theta}_{i}
instead of
\theta_{i}
causes an
O(1/T)
bias in the estimated CDF (

is the number of observations per firm). HPJ debiasing
(
Dhaene and Jochmans 2015
)
reduces this bias to

by:

(1)

Splitting the panel into two contiguous time-based halves.

(2)

Computing
\hat{\theta}_{i}
separately on each half still using (
4
).

(3)

Constructing estimators
\hat{\phi}_{1\mathchar 59\relax s\mathchar 59\relax a}

(4)

Computing full panel estimators

(5)

Constructing debiased estimators

as

\check{\phi}_{s\mathchar 59\relax a}=2\hat{\phi}_{s\mathchar 59\relax a}-\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\hat{\phi}_{1\mathchar 59\relax s\mathchar 59\relax a}+\hat{\phi}_{2\mathchar 59\relax s\mathchar 59\relax a}\right).

(6)

Bootstrap Inference

To compute the critical values for

with HPJ debiased estimators. We tabulate the bootstrap values of the test statistic (

Validity

The test’s validity follows from

(section 3.1) and theory for KS tests with estimated parameters
