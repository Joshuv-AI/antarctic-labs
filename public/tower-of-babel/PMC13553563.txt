GOLDEN: Efficacy and Safety of Complement Factor B Antisense, Sefaxersen, in Geographic Atrophy due to Age-Related Macular Degeneration
Authors: Glenn J. Jaffe, Charles C. Wykoff, Michael L. McCaleb, Terrance D. Barrett, Ashely Frazer-Abel, Daniel Norris, Ulrich F.O. Luhmann, Cathy Cukras, Lixuan Yin, Mark Lomax, Sascha Fauser, Eugene Schneider, Gary T. Brice, GOLDEN Investigators
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the efficacy and safety of sefaxersen, an investigational antisense oligonucleotide targeting complement factor B (FB), in slowing the progression of geographic atrophy (GA) secondary to age-related macular degeneration (AMD).DesignRandomized, double-masked, placebo-controlled, adaptive phase II clinical trial.SubjectsA total of 332 patients with GA secondary to AMD were enrolled, with 91% of treated patients completing the final study visit at 56 weeks.MethodsIn stage 1, 58 patients received subcutaneous injections of 40, 70, or 100 mg sefaxersen or placebo monthly. An interim analysis for FB, pharmacokinetics, and safety was conducted when patients reached at least 13 weeks of treatment to select 2 doses for expansion. In stage 2, an additional 274 patients were randomized to 40 or 70 mg sefaxersen or placebo. Patients were stratified based on baseline GA size, baseline rate of GA growth, and presence/history of exudative choroidal neovascularization in the fellow eye. Geographic atrophy area was assessed by fundus autofluorescence imaging. Safety assessments included ocular and systemic adverse events, laboratory parameters, and vital signs.Main Outcome MeasuresThe primary endpoint was the rate of GA lesion growth from baseline to week 49. Prespecified endpoints included changes from baseline in best-corrected visual acuity, low-luminance visual acuity, systemic complement measures, and occurrence of treatment-related adverse events.ResultsSefaxersen did not significantly reduce the rate of GA growth versus placebo. Differences in adjusted mean GA rate of growth versus placebo were –0.25 mm2 (–11.5%, P = 0.171) and –0.08 mm2 (–3.8%, P = 0.647) for 40 and 70 mg groups, respectively. At steady state, a reduction in adjusted mean plasma FB levels (40 mg: –63% and 70 mg: –69%) led also to a reduction in alternative pathway activity (adjusted mean –22% and –34%, respectively). The safety profile, both ocular and nonocular, of sefaxersen was comparable to that of the placebo, with the most common adverse events being coronavirus disease 2019, urinary tract infections, and nasopharyngitis.ConclusionsSefaxersen reduced systemic FB levels and alternative pathway activity, but the effect on GA growth rate was not significant compared with placebo. The treatment was well tolerated with a safety profile consistent with previous studies.Financial Disclosure(s)Proprietary or commercial disclosure may be found in the Footnotes and Disclosures at the end of this article.

The complement system, an important component of innate immunity, is the most widely accepted pathogenic pathway implicated in age-related macular degeneration (AMD). Genetic evidence from genome-wide association studies and rare variant analyses suggests that the complement alternative pathway (AP) is overactive in AMD.1,2 For example, specific loss-of-function polymorphisms of complement factor H, the negative regulator of the AP, confer increased risk of AMD.3 More specifically, the loss-of-function polymorphisms of factor B (FB), an essential component of the AP, confer protection against AMD.3, 4, 5

Factor B is synthesized primarily by the liver and at very low levels in several extrahepatic sites.6,7 Ocular FB protein is located predominantly in the choroidal vasculature and Bruch’s membrane, and only low levels are evident in the neurosensory retina.6,8 However, choroidal FB appears to be derived mostly from systemic sources. Plasma concentrations of AP activation products were found to be significantly elevated in eyes with AMD compared with controls.8, 9, 10 These data suggest an ongoing systemic activation of the alternative complement pathway in AMD pathogenesis and add to the body of evidence that AMD is at least partially a systemic disease with local ocular manifestation.1,10, 11, 12 Recently, clinical studies have demonstrated therapeutic efficacy of intraocularly administered complement inhibitors. Specifically, local inhibition of C3b and C3 cleavage with pegcetacoplan or C5 cleavage with avacincaptad pegol have both been shown to reduce geographic atrophy (GA) lesion progression.13,14

The therapeutic objective of treatment with sefaxersen was to delay the progression of GA secondary to AMD using systemic administration of an antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) targeting complement FB (CFB) mRNA in the liver to reduce the levels of plasma and ocular FB protein. The hypothesis was that reduced plasma FB, an essential component of the AP, would selectively decrease AP activation that contributed to AMD pathology.

Sefaxersen, an ASO drug that specifically targets CFB, binds to the mRNA by Watson-Crick base pairing. The hybridization (binding) of sefaxersen to the cognate mRNA results in the ribonuclease H1-mediated degradation of CFB mRNA, thus preventing FB protein production. Reduction in mRNA levels using this approach correlates directly with a subsequent reduction in target protein levels and AP activity, as demonstrated recently in healthy volunteers and patients with immunoglobulin A (IgA) nephropathy.15,16

The GOLDEN study was conducted to investigate the potential for monthly subcutaneous (SC) administration of the RNA therapy, sefaxersen, to diminish the growth rate of GA secondary to AMD. The trial tested the hypothesis that an inhibition of the complement system via a selective reduction of AP could impact GA growth.

Methods

Study Design

GOLDEN was a phase II, randomized, placebo-controlled, double-masked trial investigating subcutaneously administered sefaxersen to slow the rate of growth of GA secondary to AMD (NCT03815825). The study was designed as a 2-stage adaptive trial design. In stage 1, patients were recruited and analyzed at an interim analysis after 13 weeks of exposure to select 2 doses for expansion in the second stage based on systemic biomarkers of complement, drug pharmacokinetics, safety, and tolerability observed during stage 1. Patients recruited in stage 1 continued in the study through completion and were pooled with patients from stage 2 in the final statistical analysis. The global study was performed in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines.

The screening period for the study included a 12- to 26-week interval in which the baseline rate of GA growth was assessed by measurement of GA area at the first clinic visit and again at the visit 1 to 2 weeks before randomization, which enabled the statistical analysis to account for this as a prognostic predictor. In addition, vaccinations were administered during the screening period. Eligibility criteria (Table S1, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org) were reviewed at the beginning of the screening period and again at the end of screening, just before randomization. The treatment period consisted of 45 weeks with a 12-week follow-up period. Further details are available in Supplementary Materials (see Supplementary Methods, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org).

Participants

Details of eligibility criteria are listed in Table S1. Patients had unilateral or bilateral AMD and were ≥ 50 years of age. Only 1 eye per patient was selected as the study eye. Key eligibility criteria for the study eye included: GA area of 1.9 to 17.0 mm2 within the imaging field; hyperautofluorescence adjacent to the GA lesion in a banded or diffuse pattern; best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) of 35 letters or better. Eyes with GA that either affected or did not affect the foveal center were included. In addition, approximately 20% of the patients randomized into stage 2 could have exudative choroidal neovascularization (CNV) in the fellow eye (FE).

At randomization, eyes were stratified based on the following 3 factors: (1) change of GA area during screening, (2) baseline area of GA, and (3) status of exudative CNV in the FE (absence vs. presence, including a history). An automated interactive voice/web-response system (Almac) was used for randomization.

Study Treatment and Procedures

Sefaxersen (IONIS-FB-LRx, ISIS 696844, RO7434656) is an N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc)-conjugated ASO, as previously described.15 The investigational drug or placebo was administered subcutaneously every 4 weeks at doses of either 40, 70, or 100 mg. In addition, a loading dose was administered at week 3, so the total number of dose administrations was 13 over 45 weeks. During the 12-week follow-up period, multiple outcome measures, including those for the primary endpoint at week 49, were assessed.

Outcome Measures

The primary efficacy endpoint was the rate of growth (slope) in GA area from baseline to week 49 as measured by fundus autofluorescence (multimodal imaging) in the study eye (see Supplementary Methods for more details).17,18 Ocular and systemic safety were assessed during the screening, treatment, and follow-up periods.

Levels of plasma FB at various time points were quantitated using nephelometry, and other complement components were assessed using a variety of commercially available hybridization enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (see Supplementary Methods).

Pharmacokinetic Analysis

Predose pharmacokinetic (PK) samples were collected throughout the study at multiple time points. In addition, PK samples were collected using sparse PK sampling at 1, 3, 5, or 7 hours postdose on day 1 and day 169 to assess peak concentration and absorption phase post SC administration. An enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay method was used to quantify drug concentrations in plasma.

Results

Study Disposition and Baseline Characteristics

A total of 1162 patients with GA were screened for the study and 332 were randomized between June 26, 2019 and May 18, 2023 (Fig 1). The most frequent reason for screen failure was inclusion/exclusion criteria, which accounted for 822 patients. Five patients failed screening because of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19). Fifty-eight patients were randomized in stage 1 (1:1:1:1) to receive sefaxersen 40 mg, sefaxersen 70 mg, sefaxersen 100 mg, or placebo. As a result of the interim analysis from stage 1, the doses of sefaxersen 40 and 70 mg (cohorts A and B, respectively) were selected for expansion in stage 2, but the 100 mg group was not. Thus, for stage 2, an additional 274 patients were randomized (1:1:1) to receive sefaxersen 40 mg, sefaxersen 70 mg, or placebo. The intent-to-treat (ITT)/safety population consisted of 329 patients because 3 of the 332 randomized patients withdrew before receiving treatment (Fig 1). Overall, the study was completed by 299 (91%) participants. Of the patients who terminated treatment early, 8 remained in the study and completed posttreatment follow-up. The main reasons for study discontinuation were adverse events (AEs) or serious adverse events (SAEs) (5.5% of ITT population), voluntary withdrawal (2.7% of ITT), ineligibility (0.3% of ITT), or other reasons (0.6% of ITT). The “other” reasons were 1 patient lost to follow-up and another unable or unwilling to comply with the protocol. The set of withdrawals due to AEs included 6 patients who discontinued because of the development of CNV. Demographics and retinal characteristics at baseline were balanced across treatment groups, which was due in part to the stratification into groups during randomization based on size of GA, growth rate of GA during the screening period, and presence/absence of exudative CNV in the FE (Tables S2 and S3, Fig S1, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). The participants ranged in age from 50 to 98 years, with a mean age of 76 years. In total, 99% of the participants were White (<2% were Hispanic/Latino) and 60% were female. There were 8% current smokers, 42% previous smokers, and 50% who had never smoked.

A majority (89.1%) of the patients had bilateral GA. The GA in the study eye was multifocal in 67% and subfoveal in 75%, and had a mean total area of 7.65 mm2. As shown in Figure S1, the distribution of multifocal GA, subfoveal GA, and mean GA area was comparable across treatment groups. The mean BCVA and low-luminance visual acuity (LLVA) of the study eye were 62.5 letters (range, 30–91 letters) and 37.4 letters (range, 1–86 letters), respectively (Table S3).

Study Drug Exposure

The median number of dose administrations (13) was comparable among the treatment groups (Table S4, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). Treatment compliance, calculated as the percent of planned dosing for patients actively enrolled in the study, ranged from 94% to 96% across all groups.

Pharmacokinetics

As summarized in Table S5 (available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org), subcutaneously administered sefaxersen is rapidly absorbed into the systemic circulation, with median peak plasma levels occurring approximately 1 to 5 hours after dosing. The maximum concentration (Cmax) and area under the curve demonstrated dose proportionality within the 40 to 100 mg dose range. Characterization of the terminal elimination phase yielded an apparent terminal elimination half-life (t1/2λz) of 29.5 to 35.3 days (approximately 4–5 weeks) over a dose range of 40 to 100 mg and appeared to be independent of dose. Trough concentrations from week 5 to week 41 (Fig S2, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org) were significantly lower than the peak drug concentration, consistent with the rapid distribution of GalNAc-conjugated ASOs, such as sefaxersen, primarily into the liver as well as other tissues such as the kidney.

Primary Efficacy Outcome

GOLDEN did not exhibit a statistically or clinically significant difference in the rate of GA growth from baseline to week 49 in each of the sefaxersen treatment arms versus placebo (Table 1, Fig S3, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). There was a reduction in the average rate of GA growth from placebo of –11% and –4% for the 40 and 70 mg groups, respectively, which were not statistically significant from placebo (P = 0.171, P = 0.647). Ophthalmic Prespecified Secondary and Exploratory Endpoints

For the secondary endpoint of absolute change in GA area, the least squares mean relative difference versus placebo at week 57 was –8% and –4% for 40 and 70 mg sefaxersen, respectively, which were not significantly different from placebo (Fig 2). The results of various prespecified subgroup analyses of 40 and 70 mg for up to week 49 were also generally consistent with and supported by the primary analysis (Fig S4 [available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org] for the 40 mg and Fig 3 for the 70 mg groups). There was a priori interest in assessing potential effects in subgroups of earlier versus later-stage lesions, as exemplified by potential differences among age groups or GA lesion size, or by rate of GA growth during the screening period. Key morphological attributes, such as lesion location (foveal center involving vs. nonfoveal center involving) or lesion contiguity (multifocal or nonmultifocal), which may be associated with visual acuity, were also of interest a priori because they also could have provided subsets of patients with AMD who may have been preferentially impacted by the drug. The placebo group had a –4.5, –3.1, and –2.2 adjusted mean change in BCVA, LLVA, and low-luminance deficit (LLD) scores of the study eyes as compared with baseline at the primary endpoint. Over the same time period, there were similar changes in BCVA, LLVA, and LLD scores of the study eyes among patients in the 40 and 70 mg treatment, with no appreciable differences versus placebo (Table S7, Fig S5, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org).

From a patient-reported outcomes perspective, there were modest declines from baseline in the Composite scores, Near Activity subscores, and Far Activity subscores of the National Eye Institute Visual Function Questionnaire-25 that appeared similar in all treatment groups, and were not appreciably different between 40 and 70 mg sefaxersen versus placebo (Table S8, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org).

Growth Rate during the Baseline Period Compared with Growth during the Treatment Period

For initial stratification, the eyes were each divided into 2 groups based on whether they were slow or fast progressors during the baseline period. For the placebo group, the change in GA area during the treatment period was highly correlated (P < 0.001) with the change observed during the run-in, whether calculated as change in absolute area (r2 = 0.57) or square root (r2 = 0.51) (Fig S6, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). Similarly, the change in individual eyes during treatment of both sefaxersen groups was correlated with the change during the run-in (P < 0.001), which is consistent with the primary endpoint findings. These results demonstrate the predictability of the run-in assessment of GA growth for the subsequent treatment period.

Systemic Factor B Levels

The change in plasma FB from baseline at inferred pharmacodynamic steady state was nominally statistically significant (nominal P < 0.001) for the 40, 70, and 100 mg sefaxersen groups compared with placebo (Figs 4, S7, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). Similarly, plasma level of complement Bb, the enzymatically active cleavage product from FB, was also substantially lowered by sefaxersen (Figs 4, S7). Again, the levels of Bb were often at or below the level of LLOQ.

Consistent with the lowering of FB and Bb, there were nominally statistically significant greater decreases in serum AP activity for the 40, 70, and 100 mg groups compared with placebo (nominal P < 0.001; Figs S7, S8, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org) as measured by the modified Wieslab AP assay, with maximal reductions in Wieslab AP at inferred steady state of 60%, 68%, and 60% for 40, 70, and 100 mg of sefaxersen and 33% for placebo.

Plasma C3 was slightly elevated in all sefaxersen dose groups throughout the treatment period, which is likely a result of preventing the consumption of circulating C3. An assessment of the classical pathway function, serum Wieslab classical pathway (CP) functional activity, did not demonstrate a meaningful change over time and at the inferred steady state (Fig S7, Table S9, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org).

Safety

Sefaxersen was generally well tolerated and had a favorable safety profile (Table 2). Two hundred eighty-three (86%) of the patients had at least one treatment-emergent adverse event (TEAE), and 54 patients (16% of the ITT population) had an AE reported as related to treatment. A majority of the TEAEs were mild (41% of patients in the ITT population) or moderate (38% of patients in the ITT population) in severity, with an incidence that was balanced across groups, including placebo. The incidence of various infections, including COVID-19, was generally similar across groups (Table S10, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). Importantly, there were no reports of infection with the encapsulated bacteria meningococci or pneumococci. The most frequent ocular TEAEs were dry AMD (either worsening in both eyes or newly incident in FE) (7% sefaxersen, 9% placebo), reduced visual acuity (5% sefaxersen, 7% placebo), and visual impairment (5% sefaxersen, 5% placebo). The most frequent nonocular TEAEs were COVID-19 (13% sefaxersen, 15% placebo), urinary tract infection (9% sefaxersen, 10% placebo), arthralgia (7% sefaxersen, 7% placebo), and nasopharyngitis (6% sefaxersen, 11% placebo) (Table S10). Of the 47 (14%) patients who had an SAE, none of the events were reported as related to the drug. A reversible elevation of alanine aminotransferase to between 3 and 5 times the upper limit of normal (ULN) was exhibited in 3 (3%), 3 (3%), and 2 (14%) patients in the 40, 70, and 100 mg sefaxersen groups, respectively, and 0 in the placebo group. There was a single patient (1%) in the 70 mg group who had alanine aminotransferase rise transiently to 5 to 8 times the ULN, without a change in bilirubin. Four of these patients had transient aspartate aminotransferase >3 times the ULN (2 at 40 mg and 2 at 70 mg). Treatment was not discontinued permanently for any patient who had elevated liver enzymes, but dose administration was paused temporarily for 3 patients. Among all patients, 10 (3.0%) patients experienced CNV in their study eye during the study, a conversion rate that was different among patients with a history of FE CNV at baseline. Specifically, among the 247 patients who did not have exudative CNV in the FE at baseline, 4 (1.6%) developed CNV in the study eye during the study, with 1 conversion in each group (Table S11, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). Discussion

In this global phase II, randomized, double-masked, placebo-controlled trial, we investigated the potential for the ASO, sefaxersen, to reduce GA expansion by lowering systemic FB and complement AP activity. The study's primary endpoint, reducing the growth rate of GA as assessed by difference in slope of treatment groups as compared with placebo from baseline to week 49, was not achieved. There were small numerical differences in percent changes of –11% and –4% for 40 and 70 mg dose groups, respectively, that were not statistically significant. Multiple prespecified analyses were consistent with the conclusion from the primary analysis. Moreover, there were no signals of benefit through reductions in GA growth in any of the prespecified subgroup analyses, including considerations of GA morphology, GA rate of change, patient demographics, or comorbidities.

Similarly, all functional assessments, including BCVA, LLVA, and LLD, had similar trajectories in each arm, with no noticeable change with drug treatment. Finally, there was no identifiable change in the quality of life assessment based on the National Eye Institute Visual Function Questionnaire-25.

From a therapeutic-target perspective, this masked intervention achieved what was intended; sefaxersen decreased circulating FB levels by approximately 70%, which was well below the lower limits of normal and was comparable to reductions observed in previous clinical studies with sefaxersen in both healthy volunteers and patients with IgA nephropathy.15,16 Furthermore, the plasma level of Bb, the activated cleavage product of FB, was reduced by 73%, again well below the lower limit of normal. Many samples had values of plasma FB and especially Bb at or below the LLOQ. This substantial lowering of FB and the consistent lowering of Bb resulted in diminished serum AP activity. Importantly, although systemic AP activity was not completely blocked, the large reduction of Bb levels indicates a substantial lowering of alternative C3 convertase (C3bBb) of the amplification loop of the AP cascade. This finding is further supported by elevated plasma C3 levels, which is consistent with less consumption of circulating C3 by the amplification loop. However, the exact degree of systemic amplification loop and terminal pathway activities as a consequence of the observed AP inhibition remains unknown because of the limitations of currently available complement assays.19 In contrast to the effect of sefaxersen to inhibit AP, CP activity was not reduced. The selective reduction of AP while sparing CP activity was intentional in this development program to potentially mitigate infection risk that accompanies inhibition of the complement cascade.20, 21, 22

The ASO 2′-O-methoxyethyl design used for sefaxersen enables systemic stability of the ASO and subsequent reduction of CFB mRNA.23 Because the liver is the primary source of circulating FB protein, the ASO was conjugated to a tri-GalNAc moiety to provide efficient uptake of the ASO into hepatocyte nuclei via binding to the asialoglycoprotein receptor on the cell surface of hepatocytes.6,7,23,24 As shown previously, ASOs, whether GalNAc-conjugated or not, do not distribute directly into retina or directly reduce the expression of mRNAs in the retina.6,23,25 Other recent ASO drugs, such as eplontersen and olezarsen, use the same GalNAc-2′-O-methoxyethyl design as sefaxersen to effectively target the liver, providing sustained lowering of target proteins with monthly SC administrations.26,27 Such drugs demonstrate the suitability of GalNAc-conjugated ASOs for long-term treatment of chronic diseases, providing durability of therapy.

The design of the GOLDEN study had several unique attributes that strengthen conclusions and perhaps should be considered for future GA trials. Besides having an adaptive design to facilitate selection of dosages as well as serving as a sentinel safety cohort for the elderly patient population, the trial included a pre-assessment period to quantify GA growth before randomization and treatment initiation, which enabled the statistical analysis to account for this as a prognostic predictor. The patients were subsequently stratified for treatment based on observed growth rate, GA size, and presence/absence of exudative CNV in the FE. The stratification ensured a balance across groups of the elements known to meaningfully impact GA growth rates. In addition, the high level of retention ensured that there were sufficient numbers of subjects to meaningfully assess prespecified subgroups.

Because the study design provided an initial growth rate during the baseline “pre-assessment period,” it was possible to determine whether the initial growth rate during this baseline period predicted growth during the subsequent treatment period. We found that eyes tended to “stay in their lane,” i.e., eyes that grew slowly during the baseline period tended to grow slowly during the treatment and vice versa. These results support the primary finding of minimal efficacy in the treatment groups. If there had been a robust treatment effect, we would have expected a diminished correlation between GA change of run-in versus treatment. Furthermore, these results are consistent with data reported for the Gather 1 and 2 GA trials, in which slow growers in the sham group tended to stay slow, whereas fast growers tended to stay fast. Together, these data may be useful to clinicians when deciding on intravitreal therapy. For example, one might consider treatment sooner in eyes with foveal-threatening lesions that were in a fast-growing group. Further studies are warranted to clarify the relationship between the initial growth rate and the efficacy associated with treatments for GA.

Geographic atrophy area was measured on fundus autofluorescence images, supplemented by spectral domain OCT and near-infrared reflectance images to confirm the diagnosis of GA, according to consensus on atrophy meeting group criteria, and to help define GA boundaries.17,18 The method was sufficient to provide growth rates during the initial run-in period that were used to stratify the treatment groups. Of interest, the inferred growth rates during this run-in period averaged 2.0 mm/year, similar to those reported from natural history studies.28 We believe therefore that the methodology ensured that the observed results reflected a lack of true difference between groups, and not the result of GA area measurement variability.

The GOLDEN study expanded our understanding of the safety and tolerability of sefaxersen, with previous trials investigating the drug in healthy volunteers and patients with IgA nephropathy.15,16 The oldest subject evaluated in earlier trials was 65 years of age, whereas the average age of the patients with GA in the current trial was 76 years, ranging from 50 to 98 years. Patients in the GOLDEN study had multiple comorbidities and were taking a wide variety of systemic pharmaceuticals at enrollment and during the study. Despite the wide array of underlying comorbidities of enrolled patients, sefaxersen was safe and well tolerated, with no clinically meaningful changes in hematology, chemistry, coagulation, electrocardiogram, or vital signs. In some patients, there was a reversible elevation of liver enzymes, which was not accompanied by elevated bilirubin (i.e., no Hy’s Law case was observed). Drug-related treatment-emergent AEs were mild or moderate in severity, and no SAE was considered drug-related.

The incidence of various infections, including COVID-19, was generally similar across the placebo and sefaxersen groups. Importantly, there were no reports of infection with meningococci or pneumococci, which may be partially related to the mechanism of drug action that provides minimal effect on the CP complement and prevaccination of patients.20,22 In vitro studies have demonstrated that inhibiting AP does not abrogate the in vitro ability of serum from vaccinated individuals to kill meningococcal bacteria.20 In addition, intentional and prespecified steps were also taken to mitigate the potential for infection, such as educating patients, caregivers, and study staff of infection risks, and excluding enrollment of patients having deficiencies in CP.

Also importantly, there was no observed effect on cholesterol with sefaxersen treatment in the current trial. In comparison, other programs using distinct approaches for FB inhibition have reported changes in lipid profiles.29 Furthermore, in contrast to other complement inhibitors (e.g., pegcetacoplan and avacincaptad pegol), which are administered intraocularly, sefaxersen was not associated with an increase in incidence of new CNV, and the rate of CNV development in study eyes within the current GOLDEN trial was consistent with natural history studies.30 The reason for an increase in the incidence of new CNV after the administration of the other complement inhibitors, but not observed in the GOLDEN study, is not understood. Although the underlying biology of the phenomenon is unknown, there have been multiple hypotheses put forth to explain the adverse finding. For example, the intravitreally administered complement inhibitors may have interfered with complement homeostasis and disrupted microglial responses to inflammation.31 It has also been hypothesized that the drugs may maintain production of VEGF, causing increase in macular neovascularization.14 In addition, unlike sefaxersen, pegcetacoplan and avacincaptad are polyethylene glycol conjugates, which have been shown in animal models to enhance ocular inflammation and even induce CNV.32,33 Future investigations will be needed to clarify the causes.

There are potential advantages of a systemic therapeutic for the treatment of GA. Repeated intravitreal injections of the 2 currently US Food and Drug Administration-approved therapeutics can create a substantial treatment burden for patients and their caregivers. At-home drug administration, either subcutaneously by an autoinjector or orally by a pill, would substantially reduce this treatment burden for patients who are relying on caregivers for transportation to clinic visits. Furthermore, systemic administration allows simultaneous treatment of both eyes because GA is usually a bilateral disease.28 Furthermore, a systemic approach may be more practical for intervening at earlier stages of AMD, such as during intermediate AMD. These advantages are reflected by multiple clinical trials that have been conducted, and others are ongoing that investigate systemically administered therapies for GA.41,43

In conclusion, the systemic administration of sefaxersen, an antisense inhibitor of CFB, did not significantly reduce the rate of enlargement of GA lesions in a 1-year trial as compared with placebo. The reductions of systemic FB and AP activity in elderly patients were consistent with earlier clinical investigations of the same drug, and sefaxersen was found to be generally safe and well tolerated in the GA patient population.
