Conus medullaris schistosomiasis, presenting as acute conus-cauda equina syndrome in a pediatric patient: a case report
Authors: Dumura Jeneral Alfin, Danaan Joseph Shilong, Gyang Markus Bot, Emmanuel Innocent, Philip O Akpa, Nenkimun Dirting Bakwa, Ishaku A Othman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundConus medullaris intramedullary schistosomiasis is a rare disease, and may present acutely with serious neurological consequences. In its early stages, making a prompt diagnosis, and instituting appropriate treatment results in a good outcome. We share our experience of the diagnostic challenge, surgical treatment and outcome of care of this rare disease entity.Case descriptionA 7-year-old female patient, of Mada ethnicity, presented with 4 days history of bi-sphincteric dysfunction, severe low back pain, and paraparesis. Examination of the patient revealed a bilateral muscle power of 4/5 at L2–3, and 0/5 at L4–S1. Thoracic spine magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed a fusiform distension of the conus medullaris, with an intradural, intramedullary (ID, IM) mass; isointense on T1, and hyperintense on T2. She had T12–L1 laminectomy with complete resection of the mass. Histopathological evaluation revealed a granulomatous lesion with Schistosoma haematobium ova. Introduction

Schistosomiasis is a multisystemic, parasitic infestation that is caused by three species of human trematode; urine (S. haematobium) and feces (S. mansoni, S. japonicum) [1, 2]. It commonly affects the urogenital and gastrointestinal systems. Nevertheless, systemic circulation dissemination of eggs, via the portal veins may lead to other organ involvement, most especially the central nervous system [1, 3–5]. Involvement of the central nervous system is rare, with spinal cord involvement much less common, and most publication in the literature are case reports or case series [6]. In Nigeria only two case reports have been published in the literature, with the last case published about 40 years ago [7, 8].

The clinical presentation of spinal schistosomiasis may be medullary or purely myelopathatic (predominant spinal cord involvement), or myeloradicular (spinal cord and nerve root predominance), or conus-cauda equina syndrome [2, 9, 10]. Nonetheless, some patients may present with a combination of the syndromes or evolve between these syndromes as the disease progresses [2]. Most often, the disease presents as a lower spinal cord syndrome involving the lower thoracic region of the spinal cord and conus medullaris. With acute or subacute course of sphincter dysfunction, lower limb paresis, lower limb or back pain, and sensory disturbance [2, 10].

In most cases, the definitive diagnosis and treatment were achieved by histopathological evaluation of samples obtained invasively from laminectomy and resection of the lesion, followed by anti-parasitic medical therapy, with or without concurrent corticosteroid administration [2, 11]. This is because making a clinical diagnosis is challenging even in endemic areas. However, where possible, criteria for diagnosis such as; clinical and radiological evidence of lower thoracic or upper lumbar intradural, intramedullary lesions, evidence of infection with schistosomiasis through parasite isolation in tissue or serology, and the exclusion of other causes of myelitis, especially in early phase of the disease can be use as an index of suspicion for the diagnosis of this disease [2, 12]. This should be followed by medical therapy and follow-up imaging. But the challenge still remains the fact that the clinical manifestations of this disease are nonspecific, and MRI features may help its differentiation from other intramedullary abnormalities, but it is also not specific [13]. However, appropriate awareness, a high index of suspicion with additional information such as exposure history and demonstration of schistosomiasis antibodies in the serum, both in endemic and non-endemic areas, could help avoid unnecessary surgery in these patients [1, 2, 12]. We share our experience of a conus medullaris schistosomiasis in a child that presented with an acute conus-cauda equina syndrome, by highlighting the challenges of diagnosis, surgical resection without an intra-operative neuro-monitoring, and outcome of care.

Case description

A 7-year-old female patient, of Mada ethnicity, presented with a 4-day history of bi-sphincteric dysfunction [urinary retention and fecal incontinence], severe low back pain and paraparesis. The patient had no history of bathing in stream of freshwater body, hematuria or any other features suggestive of exposure to Schistosomiasis. Examination of the patient revealed a bilateral proximal muscle (L2–3) weakness with a power of 4/5, a bilateral distal muscle (L4–S1) power of 0/5. However, there was preserved sensation on all dermatomes. Abdominal and other systemic examination were essentially normal.

Thoracic spine MRI revealed a fusiform distension of the conus medullaris, extending from T11 to the upper border of L2. The lesion appeared isointense on T1 and hyperintense on T2, and showed a slightly homogenous ill-defined enhancement on Gadolinium administration. (Fig. 1A, B) Her complete blood count (CBC) revealed eosinophilia, whereas other parameters were normal. A diagnosis of intramedullary tumor of the conus medullaris, with a differential diagnosis of astrocytoma or ependymoma was made. She had a normal urine and stool microscopy (done after histopathology review). Lumbar puncture for cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis or serological testing was not done pre-operatively because Schistosomiasis was not initially considered in the differential diagnosis of the lesion. Based on the differential diagnosis of an intradural intramedullary tumor likely astrocytoma or ependymoma the patient was counsel and scheduled for laminectomy and microsurgical biopsy or tumor resection.

Images A and B showing pre-operative, mid-sagittal T2W, and T1W magnetic resonance images of the lumbosacral spine, with white and black arrows showing intramedullary lesion of the conus medullaris located within the black circle

She had T12–L1 laminectomy, with an initial plan of having microsurgical tumor biopsy through a mid-line myelotomy. However, during surgery the lesion was found to be well circumscribed, with well define border and easily dissected from the surrounding neural tissue necessitating a complete tumor resection. This was achieve without an intra-operative neuro-monitoring due to lack of availability of such monitoring in our facility. Histopathological sections of the excised tissue showed a loose edema stroma within which is seen Schistosoma haematobium eggs with a prominent terminal spine and an adjoining multinucleated giant cells. (Fig. 2, X10 magnification, and Fig. 3, X40 magnification). She was immediately commenced on Praziquantel [60 mg/kg/day given 6hrly for 3 days], and high-dose steroids [Prednisolone 10 mg three times a day, for 3 days, and repeated after 6 weeks in the post-operative period]. Prednisolone was used due to high cost and difficulty acquiring methyl-prednisolone in our setting, and due to the short course of administration of the steroid it was not tapered over time.

Post-operatively, she had complete resolution of back pain, with regain of full muscle power 5/5 at L2–L4, and 3/5 at L5–S1, bilaterally. At four years post-surgery she has regained full power on all muscle group in both limbs. She initially had persistence of fecal and urinary incontinence at one year of follow-up, which has improved to urge incontinence at four 4 years post-surgery. The patient is currently on yearly follow-up in our outpatient clinic, and also on follow-up with the pediatric surgeons for her sphincteric dysfuction. Discussion

The index case revealed the acute presentation of a child with conus-cauda equina syndrome in whom the clinical and radiological features were suggestive of an intradural, intramedullary [ID-IM] tumor of the conus medullaris. The patient was considered to have the differential diagnosis of ID-IM astrocytoma or ependymoma of the conus medullaris. Spinal cord schistosomiasis was not initially considered a differential diagnosis, and the patient was scheduled for an open microsurgical biopsy, but eventually had a total tumor resection. Because the tumor was well circumscribed and easily dissected from the surrounding neural tissues. However, the histopathological review of the excised tumor revealed a granulomatous lesion with Schistosoma haematobium ova.

The findings in this index case is similar to the experience of most authors who presented their case report, or series in the literature [2, 11]. The message is for clinicians especially, those in developing countries or endemic areas like ours, to have a high index of suspicion and consider intramedullary schistosomiasis of the conus as one of the differential diagnoses of ID-IM tumor of the conus.

Neuroschistosomiasis is a neglected unacknowledged, complication of Schistosomiasis of the central nervous system, a rare disease affecting between 1 and 4% of the estimated 200–300 million people with systemic schistosomal infections [2, 9, 14]. These alarming statistics reveal a persistently high burden globally, with the highest prevalence documented in African and Asian regions [9, 14]. This index case is from Nigeria one of the countries with the highest burden of schistosoma infestation. [15]

The index patient presented with a typical conus-cauda equina syndrome previously described by other authors [2, 9, 10, 12]. And the thoracic spine MRI features were similar to some of the radiological features of Schistosomiasis previously described in the literature. However, none of these features have been showed to be specific to spinal cord schistosomiasis. Although Sanelli et al. described a central linear enhancement surrounded by multiple enhancing nodules as being specific and unique to this disease [16]. The index patient did not show such a pattern, likewise other authors did not encounter this pattern or reported it as being specific to spinal cord schistosomiasis.

Although the patient had eosinophilia which may be suggestive of a parasitic infestation, this finding is similar, to other reports in the literature [2, 7, 8]. The use of complete blood count (CBC), serum and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) microscopy, serology, and immunology assay have been reported to be useful by some authors [1, 2, 12, 17]. Aljuma'ai et al., reported an 80% increase in CBC count, a 30% increase in CSF eosinophilia, and a 70% elevation in CSF proteins and gamma globulin levels in their series. Likewise, stool and urine examinations for Schistosoma ova yielded positive results in 90% and 30% of their cases, respectively [1]. In the index patient, the stool and urine microscopy results were negative for schistosoma ova. Lambertucci et al., in their case series, reported elevated protein levels and lymphocyte count in the CSF in 90% of cases, with CSF eosinophilia reported in 40%. de Walton et al. reported that peripheral eosinophilia was positive in three out of four of their cases, whereas, the serum and CSF schistosomal serology test, were positive in all their patients [2]. By and large, the none invasive confirmatory diagnosis or diagnostic criteria of spinal schistosomiasis varies widely within the literature [2]. In the future the development of standardized tests to identify schistosomal antigens in the CSF, using polymerase chain reaction (PCR), will be an advancement in the non-invasive diagnosis of neuroschistosomiasis.

But, currently, the most definitive diagnosis for spinal schistosomiasis was achieved by histopathology samples obtained invasively from biopsy or resection of the lesion [2, 11]. With most of the species identified as S. haematobium and S. mansoni [9, 12, 13]. This is consistent with the finding in this patient who had S. haematobium ova. But other authors have isolated Schistosoma japonicum in their patients [6, 13].

Total surgical resection, in the absence of intra-operative neuro-monitoring was done. And the administration of post-operative praziquantel and high-dose steroid, was offered to this patient. The use of medical therapy alone with follow-up imaging is considered appropriate for the diagnosis of spinal cord schistosomiasis based on clinical and radiological presentation [1, 5, 6, 12]. But when clinical, radiological, and serology results are inconsistent or where the diagnosis was not contemplated as seen in most case reports and that in the index patient [5, 6, 12, 14]. Surgical resection should be considered as the next logical and appropriate step. This has resulted in significant motor recovery and a good outcome in this report and that published by other authors [5, 9, 12, 14]. Although sphincteric dysfunction has persisted for up to a year in this patient, it later improved to urge incontinence, this also has been reported by some authors [1, 7, 12]. This report underscores the significance of having a high index of suspicion, detailed clinical evaluation, and the inclusion of spinal schistosomiasis as a differential diagnosis of patients presenting with acute conus-caudal equina syndrome in schistosoma endemic regions.

Conclusion

A high index of suspicion and a thorough clinical evaluation is required to consider schistosomiasis as a differential diagnosis of ID-IM tumor of the conus medullaris. Most especially, in pediatric patients with acute conus-cauda equina syndrome, with radiological features of lower spinal cord intra medullary tumor. This will result in a prompt and appropriate diagnosis and intervention that often leads to a good outcome.
