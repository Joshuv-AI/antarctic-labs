The efficacy of dental general anesthesia in children with severe early childhood caries: a retrospective study with longitudinal follow-up in China
Authors: Jing-lan Zhang, Jin-yan Cen, Cai-feng Yan, Hong Qian, Zhi-hui Mai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to retrospectively explore the efficacy of dental general anesthesia (DGA) in treating severe early childhood caries (S-ECC) among pre-school children within 1-year longitudinal follow up.MethodsPre-school children, who were diagnosed as S-ECC and accepted DGA treatment, were enrolled in this study from July 2020 to July 2023 followed by at least 1-month follow-up. The general demographics and clinical indices were collected and calculated for further analyses, including the main indices of successful rate and caries recurrence rate, and the secondary indices of postoperative complications, oral health-related quality of life, and parental stratification.ResultsA total of 269 children were enrolled in this study, including166 boys (61.71%) and 103 girls (38.29%) with mean age of 3.96 ± 0.81 years. The success rate was 91.97% at 6-month and reduced to 85.93% at 12-months after DGA treatment. All the children showed no recurrent carries at 1-month, but relapsed at 6-month and 12-month with 9.71% (20/206) and 19.05% (24/126) posttreatment, respectively. Pain was the most common postoperative complication within 24 h (55.02%) and one week (4.83%) after treatment followed by mastication problems (40.01% to 5.58%) and weariness (39.78% to 1.85%). The child impact section (CIS), family impact section (FIS), and early childhood oral health impact scale (ECOHIS) scores were significantly decreased at 1-, 6-, and 12-month posttreatment with a time dependent manner (P < 0.001), and 91.27% of patients were overall satisfied with DGA treatment.ConclusionDGA seems performed safe and clinically acceptable outcomes in treating S-ECC. Pain was the main complication after DGA, and shorting procedure duration might alleviate the postoperative pain and anxiety.Keywords: Dental general anesthesia, Early childhood caries, Efficacy, Complication, Quality of life, Recurrence

Background

Early childhood caries (ECC) is one of the most common chronic oral diseases during childhood, which might affect more than 60% of all school children [1, 2]. It first appears as white opaque spots along with the gingival margin of the upper primary incisors, and develops into cavities gradually leading to the loss of crown [3]. ECC is complex and multifactorial diseases associated with dietary component, pattern of feeding, oral hygiene, microbiota, deleterious habits, and socioeconomic factors [4]. According to the 4th Chinese National Oral Health Survey, the mean decay-missing-filled teeth (dmft) and caries prevalence of children were 2.28 and 50.8% of 3-year-old, 3.40 and 63.6% of 4-year-old, and 4.24 and 71.9% of 5-year-old [5]. It is important to note that the vast majority of ECC cases can be effectively managed under standard chairside conditions with behavioral guidance techniques, such as tell-show-do, positive reinforcement, and distraction, combined with local anesthesia and minimally invasive restorative procedures [6, 7]. However, there remains a part of behavioral management fails or the extent of caries is severe—such as in severe early childhood caries (S-ECC).

S-ECC is an aggressive form of ECC, and the American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry (AAPD) defines it as the children (1) present any sign of smooth-surface tooth caries under 3 years old; (2) suffered with one or more cavitated, caries-induced missing, or filled smooth surface in primary teeth aged 3 ~ 5 years old; or (3) contained decayed, missing, or filled surfaces ≥ 4 (age of 3), 5 (age of 4), or 6 (age of 5) [8]. S-ECC can not only lead to toothache, tooth emergencies, recurrent infection, and malocclusion, but also further result in malnutrition, speech problem, poor growth, social disruption, and decreased quality of life [9]. Although the World Health Organization prescribes multiple treatments in controlling S-ECC, how can effectively deal with uncooperative young patients during treatment remains a challenge to limit these benefits.

Dental general anesthesia (DGA) is an alternative intervention for S-ECC to complete dental rehabilitation with a single visit and avoid uncooperative behaviors of children during treatment [10]. However, DGA is not without risks and additional costs. The procedure requires a dedicated anesthesia team, operating room facilities, and preoperative systemic evaluation, which substantially increases the financial burden on families and healthcare systems [11]. Common perioperative complications include nausea, vomiting, sore throat, agitation, and, rarely, more serious events such as laryngospasm or respiratory depression [12]. Repeated or prolonged exposure to general anesthesia at young age has also raised concerns regarding potential neurodevelopmental effects, although evidence remains inconclusive [13, 14]. As a developing country, despite of the application of DGA in China since 1999, the prevalence of DGA was not common actually due to the high risk, high cost, and high failure rate [15]. With the development of medical techniques, DGA in treating children oral problem has been rapidly recognized by parents, but the efficacy of DGA in treating S-ECC remains not to reach a consensus. Thus, the preliminary efficacy of DGA in treating S-ECC was explored in this study via evaluating the successful rate, recurrence rates, and quality of life. According to those investigations, we hoped to provide some new evidences in treating S-ECC.

Methods

Patients’ enrollment

Patients, aged 3 ~ 5 years old and underwent DGA treatment, were enrolled from the Stomatology Hospital of Southern Medical University from July 2020 to July 2023. The inclusive criteria were set as: (1) diagnosed with S-ECC; (2) classified as American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) grade I, namely normal healthy without systemic disease; (3) complete at least 1 month’s follow-up; and (4) had informed and consent to participate in this study. In addition, patients were excluded from this study if met any of the following criteria: (1) DGA surgery for dental trauma; (2) DGA for supernumerary teeth extraction; or (3) suffered with other development disorder or general disease. This study was authorized by the Ethic Committee of Southern Medical University (No. Yuekouyilunshen [2023]56). Anthesis in this study was performed in accordance with the Guideline on the use of general anesthesia for pediatric dentistry dental procedure in China [16].

DGA treatment

All surgery procedures were performed independently by five dentists with at least 6 years of pediatric oral experience. After surgery, all children need to stay in hospital for at least 3 h for further observation, and doctors usually prescribe routine anti-infection and pain-relieving medication for patients for approximately 3 days when they leaving the hospital. X-ray or clinical examination was not permitted immediately after the surgery, and patients were required to take a radiograph two-week after DGA treatment. The other teeth radiographs were taken every three to six months and all children were required follow-up at 1-, 6- and 12-months. In addition, parents were advised to prepare non-prescription NASAIDs, and oral health education was conducted for parents and children at every follow-up visit.

Data collection

Before the examination, preoperative interviews were performed to collect the main appeals, dental history, and demographic information, including name, age, gender, the relationship between the children and their guardian (father, mother, grandfather, or grandmother), place of residence (city or town), and contact information. The dental treatment information, success rate, and caries recurrence rate were recorded during the outpatient follow-up at the 6- and 12-months after DGA. Complications were recorded via telephone follow-up within 24 h and 1 week postoperative. In addition, parents assisted medical workers to complete questionnaires about oral health-related quality of life (OHRQL) during the outpatient follow-up visit at 1-, 6-, 12-month posttreatment. All data were collected independently by trained supervising nurses and resident physicians. Any disputes that could not be resolved between them were to be discussed by the supervising physician together with the two individuals for resolution.

Recording of success rate and caries recurrence

Success rates of dental procedures were recorded at 6- and 12-month postoperative via telephone followed by arranged clinical examination. All examinations were performed by the same examiner who performed the baseline caries examination and clinical information collection. At the same time, necessary dental treatments were conducted if required, and the brushing instructions and dietary counselling were also emphasized. The treatment procedure was considered a failure if met any of this condition: (1) restorative procedure: ① losing, fractured, cracked, or hardly adapted restoration, ② open margins, perforated or missing SSCs/anterior composite crowns, or secondary caries; (2) pulp therapy: ① localized pain, ② sensitivity to percussion, ③ existing swelling or abscess, and ④ interradicular or furcation pathology determined by radiography; (3) space maintenance: losing or fractured space maintainer.

The recurrent caries was considered as the new primary caries appears on previously untreated tooth or secondary caries (including in procedure failures) at the margin of the existing restorations.

OHRQL

OHRQL of child was assessed using the “Early Child-hood Oral Health Impact of Scale (ECOHIS)” as previously reported [17]. This scale includes 13 terms with 0 ~ 4 score for each them to evaluate the frequency of the event: 0, never; 1, almost never; 2, occasionally; 3, often; and 4, very often. The first 9 terms focused on the child impact section (CIS) and cover four domains: child symptoms (1 term), child function (4 terms), child psychology (2 terms), and child self-image and social interaction (2 terms). The 4 terms focused on the family impact section (FIS) and cover 2 domains: parental distress (2 terms) and family function (2 terms). Thus, the ideal score of this scale ranged from 0 to 52. Considering of patients’ age, data of CIS was collected by the trained physician at the end of postoperative schedule.

Perceived outcomes and parental satisfaction

Perceived outcomes and parental satisfaction were collected at 12-month follow-up. Specifically, the perceived outcomes, including oral symptoms, functional limitations, emotional satisfaction, and social well-being, were evaluated by the parents with a yes or no answer. Meanwhile, parental satisfaction was assessed at the end of the follow-up according to the parents’ experience and their willingness for full-month rehabilitation for their child under general anesthesia.

Statistical analysis

Data in this study were collected using the Excel and transferred to SPSS 21.0 (SPSS Statistics, IBM, USA) for statistical analyses. Data between groups were compared using the student’ t-test or ANOVA method. P value < 0.05 was considered statistical different.

Results

General characteristics of children/parents enrolled in this study

From July 2020 to July 2023, a total of 302 children/parents were enrolled in this study according to the inclusive criteria. The flowchart of enrollment was summarized in Fig. 1. Specifically, 33 of them were excluded, including 13 cases of dental trauma, 19 cases of supernumerary extraction, and 1 case of autism. Thus, a total of 269 children were finally enrolled in this study and completed the 1-month follow-up, including 166 boys (61.71%) and 103 girls (38.29%) with mean age of 3.96 ± 0.81 years. Following this, 206 cases had completed 6-month follow-up and 63 of them were lost to follow-up, including 43 missing contact and 20 incomplete questionnaires. In addition, 126 cases had completed the 12-month follow-up and 80 of them were lost to follow-up, including 65 missing the contact and 15 incomplete questionnaires. There were no significant differences identified between the follow-up group and lost group at 6-month and 12-month on the general characteristics of children (Table 1).

Success rates during the procedures

In this study, the success rates of treatment procedures were recorded at 6- and 12-month posttreatment. At the 6-month follow-up, the average successful rate was 91.97% including 89.32% of restorative, 95.59% of pulp therapy, and 78.57% of space maintainers (Table 2). At the 12-month follow-up, the average successful rate was reduced to 85.93%, including 85.48% of restorative, 89.34% of pulp therapy, and 64.71% of space maintainers (Table 2). Among these procedures, pulp therapy performed the highest successful rate, but space maintainers performed the lowest successful rate. Compared with the 6-month, the total successful rate and pulp therapy successful rate of 12-months were significantly decreased (P < 0.05), but no significant differences were identified in the restorative (P = 0.20) and space maintainers (P = 0.34; Table 2).

Complications and relapse rates of procedure after DGA

Complications of children were recorded within 24 h and 1 week postoperative (Table 3). Within 24-hour postoperative, pain (55.02%) was the most symptoms among children, followed by mastication problems (40.01%) and weariness (39.78%). However, these symptoms were significantly reduced within 1-week follow-up with 4.83% in pain, 5.58% in mastication problem, and 1.86% in weariness (P < 0.05). In addition, there were also some agitation (14.87%), fever (10.41%), sore throat (9.29%), vomiting (4.09%), diarrhea (2.23%), and cough (1.49%), which were all markedly vanished within 1-week follow-up. These data suggested complications during procedure under DGA were controllable and acceptable.

ECOHIS changes of children/parents during the follow-up after DGA

In this study, the OHRQL of child was assessed using the ECOHIS at 1-, 6- and 12-month follow-up in 3 sections: CIS, FIS, and ECOHIS (Table 5). Compared with the pre-operative scores, the CIS, FIS, and ECOHIS scores were significantly decreased at 1-, 6-, and 12-month with a time dependent manner (P < 0.001). As it in the FIS, item scores in FIS section were also significantly decreased at 1-, 6-, and 12-month with a time dependent manner (P < 0.001). In addition, child function in the CIS was also markedly reduced at 1-, 6-, and 12-month with a time dependent manner (P < 0.001). However, the scores of other 3 aspects, including child symptoms, psychology, and self-image and social interactions, were obviously reduced at 1- and 6- month time dependently (P < 0.001), but not further decreased in 12-month follow-up (P > 0.05).

Parental satisfaction about treatment under DGA

After 12-month follow-up, parental satisfaction on dental procedure was surveyed (Table 6). Specifically, 91.27% of patients were satisfied with the overall experience of dental procedure under DGA and 88.10% of them considered DGA treatment if it needed in the future. These results mainly correlated with the relapse of children after treatment. However, only 7 cases of them (5.56%) would consider multiple visits over DGA if safe sedation was available. During the survey communications, we noticed that procedure safe coefficient and complications, especially pain and weariness of children, still the main obstacles for parents to accept this treatment.

Discussion

Despite extensive preventive efforts, ECC continues to be a major public health concern worldwide and can readily progress to S-ECC due to socioeconomic factors and physiology [18]. DGA has been accepted by more and more pediatric dentists over the past decades, but the efficacy of DGA in S-ECC remains unclear due to different countries, medical institution, and dentist [11]. Based on 269 cases of analyses, this study had identified that S-ECC treatment under DGA performed a safe and acceptable outcome within 1 year with acceptable successful rate, satisfactory degree, and quality of life improvement, and controllable complications and relapse rate.

General anesthesia is an essential and reliable approach for dental treatment of children who cannot cooperate with dentists and surgery guidance. Burgette et al. have demonstrated that DGA performed better outcome in treating with S-ECC compared with the conscious sedation [19]. For S-ECC, children suffered more severe caries than ECC, which might increase the risk of failure of them. In this study, the success rate of treatment under DGA was 91.97% at 6-month, which was significantly decreased to 85.93% at 12-month follow-up. Compared with the 6-month follow-up, no significant differences were identified in restorative and space maintainers, but the successful rate of pulp therapy was significantly decreased from 95.59% to 89.34%, suggesting that difference in pulp therapy might be the main source of the statistical difference. Besides, the decreased sample size at the 12-month also might be a potential reason for the decline in success rate. Previous studies showed that the pulpectomy accounting for 86.54% cases of S-ECC and the successful rate ranged from 64% to 100% within 6 ~ 36 month follow-up [20, 21], which were consistent with our findings. However, the reasons for such a substantial difference in success rate remain unclear. Potential contributors include incomplete operative procedures resulting from the location and morphological irregularities of the carious primary teeth, physiological root resorption, and inadequate oral hygiene maintenance thereafter, all of which may lead to recurrent caries.

Postoperative morbidities usually occurred in the perioperative period under DGA, and could be varied due to the different treatment plan. Despite the minimal and short-lived, they could also cause anxiety and panic for children and their parents due to the too young age of patients. Postoperative pain among children after dental surgery was mainly reported by self-reporting and ranged from 27% to 95% [22, 23]. In the current study, postoperative pain, mastication, and weariness were the main morbidities under DGA, accounting for 55.02% of patient within 24 h, and decreased to 4.83% of patients within 1 week, which was close to the data reported by Mathew et al. [24] in ECC treatment under DGA. Mastication issue was the second postoperative complication in this study account for 40.01% of patients within 24 h and decreased to 5.58% at 1 week. Previous studies also demonstrated that 37% of patients were experienced mastication at 24 postoperative, and would be reduced to 7% at the end of 1 week [25, 26]. Weariness was the 3rd morbidity postoperative accounting for 39.78% of patients at 24 h and decreased to 1.86% at 1 week in this study, which were also near to the previous reported data with 39% within 24 h [27]. Moreover, agitation, fever, and sore throat occurred in about 10 ~ 15% of patients in 24 h, which were lower or similar to the previous reported data [28–31], and vanished within one week postoperative. However, a recent study in Chongqing of China suggested that agitation was the most complication after DGA, second by headache and vomiting, and only 3.8% of fever [32]. Considering of these findings, we speculated that the enrollment of children age, medical condition, and treatment procedure might contribute to these differences. Despite of these, the complication rates suggested that the complications under DGA in treating S-ECC were controllable and acceptable.

Parents usually thought that the DGA could solve all the dental health problems and the short terms health condition had also strengthened this point. Multiple studies showed that restorative DGA could obviously improve the physical, psychological, and social aspects of ECC patients and family [33, 34]. Moreover, a longitudinal study also suggested that DGA treatment markedly improved children’s OHRQL within 6 months [35]. In the current study, the scores of CIS, FIS, and ECOHIS were significantly downregulated at 1-, 6-, and 12-month after DGA. The occurrence and development of caries can be influenced by multiple aspects, such as feeding habits, oral hygiene habits, patents awareness, and other related aspects [36]. Thus, it was difficult to solve the dental problem in once and the recurrence of caries often was the most common obstacle after DGA treatment. In this study, the recurrence of caries was identified ~ 10% at 6-month follow-up but markedly increased to ~ 20% at 12-month follow-up. Moreover, the child symptoms, psychology, and self-image and social interaction were no longer significantly alleviated at 12-month compared with that at-6-month. Zhao et al. had also demonstrated that the recurrent caries of children at 6-, 12-, and 24-month follow-up were 25.53%, 37.23%, and 56.38% [37], which were higher than that in our findings. These findings suggested that although DGA could improve the oral health status and quality of life, caries recurrence still increased notably over time, making regular follow-up and health education necessary. Therefore, we recommend a systematic recall system that includes: (1) scheduled follow‑up visits at 3-, 6-, and 12-month post‑DGA; (2) motivational interviewing and oral hygiene instruction for parents to reinforce health‑promoting behaviors; and (3) early detection and management of new carious lesions to prevent progression to more advanced stages, thereby extending the durability of DGA outcomes and reducing the burden of recurrent caries in S‑ECC patients.

There were also some limitations in the current study. First, this was a preliminary retrospective study. A control group receiving conventional treatment was lacking due to the incompliance of children or parents, resulting in the absence of an intuitive comparison with DGA method. Second, as a single-center study, the sample size was constrained by challenges in patient enrollment and follow‑up. Consequently, it was not possible to assess the influence of potential confounders on the observed outcomes, such as parental education level, oral hygiene practices, dietary and lifestyle habits, or socioeconomic status, thereby precluding the derivation of more robust evidence. Accordingly, multicenter studies with larger cohorts were required to validate and extend the present findings. Furthermore, future investigations should place greater emphasis on feeding practices and the compliance of children and parents, as most cases of relapse in this study were attributable to inappropriate or unsupervised dietary management by parents or grandparents. Finally, the ECOHIS appears to have inherent limitations in assessing health‑related quality of life specifically in children with S‑ECC [38]. Therefore, the concurrent use of multiple instruments may be warranted to capture a broader spectrum of changes following DGA, as a multi‑scale approach could provide a more comprehensive evaluation of treatment outcomes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study found that S-ECC treatment under DGA appeared to be safe and clinically acceptable within the limitations of this study. However, ECC is a multifactorial disease and it is difficult to solving the oral problem in once treatment. With the development and changing oral condition, daily prevention and necessary treatment remain required after DGA treatment to maintain oral health.
