Asymptotic Counting of Binary Phylogenetic Networks
Hao Yu, Louxin Zhang

Abstract
Phylogenetic networks provide a general framework for modeling reticulate evolutionary processes such as hybridization, recombination, and horizontal gene transfer. In this paper, we study the asymptotic counting of binary phylogenetic networks with k k reticulations on n n taxa, where k k is allowed to grow with n n . Using edge insertion, we analyze the local structures that affect the number of possible constructions of such networks. By bounding the contribution of networks with exceptional local configurations and combining these bounds with known asymptotic formulas for tree-child networks, we show that, when k = o ⁡ ( n ) k=o(\sqrt{n}) , the number of binary phylogenetic networks with k k reticulations on n n taxa is asymptotic to ( n k ) ​ 2 n + k − 1 / 2 ​ n n + k − 1 ​ e − n . \binom{n}{k}2^{n+k-1/2}n^{n+k-1}e^{-n}.

Introduction

Phylogenetic trees have been used as the standard model for representing evolutionary relationships among species for several decades
[
3
]
.
In a tree model, evolutionary history is assumed to be vertically divergent, with each lineage descending from a unique ancestral lineage.
However, inherently reticulate evolution processes exist widely, including hybridization, recombination, horizontal gene transfer, and introgression.
These processes cannot be adequately represented by a phylogenetic tree, and this has motivated the study of phylogenetic networks as a more general framework for modeling evolutionary histories with reticulation events for genomes

[
9
,
11
,
17
,
20
]
.

From a mathematical point of view, phylogenetic networks form a much richer and more complex class of structures than phylogenetic trees. The space of phylogenetic networks is huge and therefore inference of phylogenetic networks is much more challenging
[
21
]
.
While the number of binary phylogenetic trees on a fixed set of
n
taxa is given by
(2n-3)!!
, the enumeration of phylogenetic networks is substantially more difficult

[
14
]
.
The difficulty arises from the fact that networks on the same number of taxa and reticulations may have very different local and global structures

[
19
]
.
Understanding such networks through counting is important for phylogenetic inference, because the size of the underlying search space directly affects the design, analysis, and feasibility of reconstruction algorithms.

To make the study of phylogenetic networks more tractable, several biologically and mathematically meaningful subclasses have been introduced.
These include tree-child networks
[
1
]
,
galled trees
[
10
,
18
]
, galled networks
[
11
]
, and tree-based networks
[
4
]
.
Among these classes, tree-child networks have received particular attention.
They exclude certain degenerate configurations by requiring every non-leaf node to have at least one child that is a tree node or a leaf.
This condition leads to favourable combinatorial and algorithmic properties, while still allowing a broad range of reticulate evolutionary scenarios.
Notably, there is an elegant recurrence formula for counting binary tree-child networks, which was conjectured by Pons and Batle
[
15
]
and proved by Liu et al.
[
12
]
.

In this paper, we study the asymptotic enumeration of binary phylogenetic networks with
k
reticulations on
n
taxa, with particular emphasis on the range in which
k
is non-constant but small relative to
n
.
The enumeration of binary phylogenetic networks was first studied by McDiarmid, Semple, and Welsh
[
14
]
.
For fixed
k
, the leading asymptotic term for the number of binary phylogenetic networks with
k
reticulations on
n
taxa is proved to be
{n\choose k}2^{n+k-1/2}n^{n+k-1}e^{-n}
in
[
13
]
; see also
[
19
]
.
The same asymptotic formula also holds for tree-child networks
[
5
]
.
Our aim is to extend this analysis to a broader range for
k
using a new technique by showing that, when
k=o(\sqrt{n})
, the same leading asymptotic term holds for counting binary phylogenetic networks.

We analyze how networks can be generated by edge insertion and identify the structural configurations that affect the number of possible constructions.
By bounding the contribution of networks with exceptional local structures and combining these bounds with known asymptotic formulas for tree-child networks, we derive asymptotic counting results for a broader class of binary phylogenetic networks.
Our results show that, when
k=o(\sqrt{n})
, the same leading asymptotic term holds for counting binary phylogenetic networks.

2.1

Let

•

•

Each leaf has indegree

•

Every internal node is either:

–

a

with indegree
1
and outdegree

, or

–

2
and outdegree

The set of nodes and directed edges in

and

Let

to

An edge entering a reticulation node is called a

We summarize basic properties of BPNs in the following proposition, which will be used without further mention.

Let

The subgraph obtained in this way has

We note that the definition of tree components adopted here differs slightly from those in earlier works

We distinguish the

Let

•

Each vertex of

•

There is a directed edge from a component

2.3
Reticulation clusters

Let
N
be a BPN. The
reticulation clusters
of
N
are defined as the connected components of
the subgraph obtained by deleting all tree nodes (and their incident edges) from
N
.
Each reticulation cluster can be viewed as an in-arborescence.

Proposition 3
.

Let
N
be a BPN, let
R
be a reticulation cluster with
a
(
a\geq 1
) reticulation nodes. Then,

(1) There are
a-1
reticulation edges between the reticulation nodes in

,

(2) There are
a+1
edges that enter the cluster
R
from tree nodes (i.e., edges
from tree nodes into reticulation nodes at the top of
R
).

(3) The lowest reticulation node has exactly one outgoing edge, and its child is either a tree node or a leaf.

Proposition 4
.

Let
N
be a BPN with
k
reticulations.
If
N
has
c
reticulation clusters, then

(1) There are
k+c
edges entering reticulation clusters from tree nodes.

(2) There are
k-c
edges between the reticulation nodes.

2.4
Tree-Child Networks

A BPN
N
is called a
tree-child network
if every internal node has at least one child that is a tree node or a leaf. Equivalently, each internal node is connected to a leaf via a path consisting of tree edges.

This condition ensures that from every internal node there exists a directed path to a leaf that avoids reticulation nodes except possibly at the starting vertex. It also implies that
the leaves of each tree component are leaves of
N
if
N
is tree-child.

A tree-child network is said to be a
simplex
network if the child of every reticulation node is a leaf.

Proposition 5
.

Let
\Sigma=\{1,2,...,n\}
. The number of simplex networks with
k
reticulations on
\Sigma
in which the children of
k
reticulations are
leaves 1 to
k
is
\frac{(2n-2)!}{2^{\,n-1}(n-k-1)!}
.

2.5
Galled Networks

A
galled network
is a BPN in which
the two parents of each reticulation node lie in
the same tree-component.

A BPN is galled if and only if its component graph is a tree with parallel edges
[
20
]
.

Conclusions

It remains unclear how the techniques developed for the asymptotic counting of BPNs in

In this paper, we studied the generation of binary phylogenetic networks by edge insertion.
By bounding the contribution of networks with exceptional local structures and combining these bounds with known asymptotic formulas for tree-child networks, we derived an asymptotic counting result for BPNs with

Does the same asymptotic counting result hold for (i) the class of galled networks and (ii) the class of normal networks when

Proof of the Asymptotic Result for Galled Networks

A: Two Lemmas

The following result is known in the literature.

Lemma A1.

For
k>0
,

\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\binom{2i+k}{i}\frac{1}{(2i+k)2^{2i}}=\frac{2^{k}}{k}.

Proof.

Use the identity

\frac{1}{2i+k}=\int_{0}^{1}t^{2i+k-1}\,dt.

Then

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\binom{2i+k}{i}\frac{1}{(2i+k)4^{i}}=\int_{0}^{1}t^{k-1}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\binom{2i+k}{i}\left(\frac{t^{2}}{4}\right)^{i}dt.

Using the generating function

\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\binom{2i+k}{i}x^{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-4x}}\left(\frac{1-\sqrt{1-4x}}{2x}\right)^{k},

with
x=\frac{t^{2}}{4}
, we obtain

S=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t^{k-1}}{\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}\left(\frac{2}{1+\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}\right)^{k}dt.

Let
t=\sin\theta
. Then

S=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\sin^{k-1}\theta\left(\frac{2}{1+\cos\theta}\right)^{k}d\theta.

Now set
\theta=2u
. Since
1+\cos\theta=2\cos^{2}u
and
\sin\theta=2\sin u\cos u
, we get

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=2^{k}\int_{0}^{\pi/4}\frac{\sin^{k-1}u}{\cos^{k+1}u}\,du=2^{k}\int_{0}^{\pi/4}\tan^{k-1}u\,\sec^{2}u\,du

\displaystyle=2^{k}\left[\frac{\tan^{k}u}{k}\right]_{0}^{\pi/4}=\frac{2^{k}}{k}.

∎

Since

for any integer

\displaystyle\frac{(2i+k-1)!}{i!(i+k)!}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{2\pi(2i+k)}\left(\frac{2i+k}{e}\right)^{2i+k}e^{\frac{1}{12(2i+k)}}}{\sqrt{2\pi i}(2i+k)\left(\frac{i}{e}\right)^{i}\cdot\sqrt{2\pi(i+k)}\left(\frac{i+k}{e}\right)^{i+k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(2i+k)^{2i+k-1/2}e^{\frac{1}{12(2i+k)}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}\cdot i^{i+1/2}\cdot(i+k)^{i+k+1/2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2^{2i+k-1}(i+k/2)^{2i+k-1/2}}{\sqrt{\pi}\cdot i^{i+1/2}\cdot(i+k)^{i+k+1/2}}e^{\frac{1}{12(2i+k)}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2^{2i+k-1}\left(1+\frac{k}{2i}\right)^{i-1}\left(1-\frac{k}{(2i+2k)}\right)^{i+k+1/2}}{\sqrt{\pi}\cdot i^{3/2}}e^{\frac{1}{12(2i+k)}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2^{2i+k-1}e^{\frac{k}{2}-\frac{k}{2i}}e^{-\frac{k}{2}-\frac{k}{4(i+k)}}}{\sqrt{\pi}\cdot i^{3/2}}e^{\frac{1}{12(2i+k)}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2^{2i+k-1}}{\sqrt{\pi}\cdot i^{3/2}}e^{\frac{1}{12(2i+k)}-\frac{k}{2i}-\frac{k}{4(i+k)}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2^{2i+k-1}}{\sqrt{\pi}\cdot i^{3/2}}\left(1+\frac{1}{12(2i+k)}-\frac{k}{2i}-\frac{k}{4(i+k)}+\left(\frac{1}{12(2i+k)}-\frac{k}{2i}-\frac{k}{4(i+k)}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2^{2i+k-1}}{\sqrt{\pi}\cdot i^{3/2}}\left(1-\frac{3k}{4(i+k)}+\left(\frac{k}{i}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2^{2i+k-1}}{\sqrt{\pi}\cdot i^{3/2}},

where the second and third inequalities follow from the fact

1+m\leq e^{m}\leq 1+m+m^{2}
for
1\geq m\geq-1
;
and the last inequality is derived from the fact that

\frac{3k}{4(i+k)}\geq\left(\frac{k}{i}\right)^{2}

for any
i\geq 2k

Therefore, for
t\geq 2k

\sum_{i=t}^{\infty}\frac{(2i+k-1)!}{i!(i+k)!2^{2i}}\leq\frac{2^{k-1}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sum_{i=t}^{\infty}i^{-3/2}=\frac{2^{k}}{\sqrt{\pi t}}.

∎

Theorem B1

Let
{\cal COM}(\Sigma,k)
be the set of such networks with
k
reticulations on a fixed set
\Sigma
of n taxa in which the two parents of every reticulation node are in the top component. Then,
for any
k=o(n^{1/3})
,

\displaystyle|{\cal COM}(\Sigma,k)|\geq{n\choose k}\frac{2^{n+k-1/2}n^{n+k-1}}{e^{n}}\left(1-o(1)\right).

(13)

Proof.

By Equality (
12
) and Lemma A3,

\displaystyle|{\cal COM}(n,k)|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle{n\choose k}\frac{k}{2^{n-1}}\sum^{\lfloor n^{2/3}\rfloor}_{b=0}\frac{(2b+k-1)!}{(b)!(b+k)!}\frac{(2n-2b-2)!(n-k)!}{(n-b-k-1)!(n-b-k)!}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle{n\choose k}\frac{2^{n-1/2}n^{n+k-1}k}{e^{n}}\left(\sum^{\lfloor n^{2/3}\rfloor}_{b=0}\frac{(2b+k-1)!}{(b)!(b+k)!}\frac{1}{2^{2b}}\right)\left(1-O(n^{-2/3})-O(n^{-1/3})\right).

By Lemma A1 and Lemma A2, given above,
we further have:

\displaystyle|{\cal COM}(n,k)|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle{n\choose k}\frac{2^{n-1/2}n^{n+k-1}k}{e^{n}}\left(\frac{2^{k}}{k}-\sum^{\infty}_{b=\lfloor n^{2/3}\rfloor+1}\frac{(2b+k-1)!}{(b)!(b+k)!}\frac{1}{2^{2b}}\right)\left(1-O(n^{-1/3})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{n\choose k}\frac{2^{n+k-1/2}n^{n+k-1}}{e^{n}}\left(1-\frac{k}{\sqrt{\pi}n^{1/3}}\right)\left(1-O(n^{-1/3})\right)

This concludes the proof.
