ABSTRACT
Nanoguide optical waveguides are thermally processed materials based on polymers that have
different refractive indices. Scintillating versions of these materials have been under development
since 2019 and feature PMMA as the low refractive index constituent and Organic Glass Scintillator-
polymer blends as the high index component. Characterization efforts to date have focused on
the functional characteristics of nanoguide pertaining to its envisioned use in high-resolution
transmission radiography applications. However, there remain questions about the nature and timing
of the scintillation process and light transport in nanoguide, as relevant to time-resolved applications
such as neutron radiography, the optically segmented Single-Volume Scatter Camera, and neutron
associated particle imaging (n-API). To better understand this material’s optical transport properties
and assess the suitability of nanoguide for time-resolved detection applications, we have performed
a series of optical response measurements along with radiation characterizations typical of organic
scintillator detectors. Specifically, we report on how the pulse rise time, overall pulse shape, light
collection, and the neutron/gamma pulse shape discrimination figure of merit are impacted by this
purported unique light propagation.

1. INTRODUCTION

Current concepts for neutron imaging detectors generally involve specific configurations of plastic,
crystalline, or organic glass scintillators. Recent advances in each of these materials classes
have enabled their arrangement into optically segmented configurations, as required for detector
systems that involve position-sensitive photodetector arrays. Combined with these advances, the
maturation of silicon photomultipliers and their readout electronics has enabled improved efficiency
for spatially resolved detection and tracking of scintillation events. Several scintillation detector
designs, pictured in Figure 1-1, have been evaluated as potential options for these applications [1,
2, 3]. Scintillating nanoguide, which is composed of a disordered array of two polymers with
different refractive indices, is unique in its purported optical transport properties, which could offer
significant benefits for applications requiring interaction resolving detectors.

Anderson localization of light is predicted to occur in media in which there is a random arrangement
of indices of refraction: the result of disorder in two transverse directions and uniformity in the third
is Transverse Anderson localization (TAL) in which light propagates in one direction with minimal
transverse diffusion [4]. The effect can occur with variation in the index of refraction as small as
10−4 [4], so long as the Ioffe-Regel criterion is met [5]. That is, the mean free path 𝑙 for light in
the medium times the magnitude of the wavenumber 𝑘 = 2𝜋/𝜆 satisfies 𝑘𝑙 ≤ 1. In other words,
optical scattering on the order of 60 nm to 100 nm is required to meet the criteria for wavelengths
of 400 nm to 600 nm. Light propagation with minimal transverse diffusion for isotropically emitted
light is expected to be highly valuable for radiation imaging applications in which localization of
the scintillation light is required, especially in the robust form factor that scintillating nanoguide
provides. More practically, smaller photon readout pixels would not require a detector redesign
with smaller optical components.

Incom, Inc. provides nanoguide faceplates for commercial optical applications and scintillating
versions for radiation applications. Figure 1-2 shows a scintillating nanoguide sample projecting a
USAF-1951 resolution test chart. Currently available scintillating nanoguide samples include 20 %
loading of PMMA (𝑛 = 1.5011 at 430 nm [7]) and 80 % loading of Organic Glass Scintillator (OGS)-
polystyrene (PS) mix (𝑛 = 1.6170 at 430 nm [7]). Current examples of scintillating nanoguide
possess individual domain diameters of ∼300 nm. Detailed simulations of TAL phenomena [9]
demonstrate localization for a random assortment of rectangular parallelepipeds, with length and
width 𝑑 ≈ 𝜆 and index of refraction 𝑛2 = 1.25. These are placed inside a medium of index of
refraction 𝑛1 = 1.0 with a loading fraction of 𝑝 = 0.5 [9]. In our case, 𝑝 = 0.2, 𝑑 ≈ 0.5𝜆, and
Δ𝑛 = 0.1159 (in contrast with 0.25). Subsequent work [6] details the impact of each of these
parameters (𝑝, Δ𝑛, and 𝑑) on localization with PMMA and PS mixtures. As expected, shorter
wavelengths experience stronger localization (or a smaller localization radius) compared to longer
ones, and smaller 𝑑 similarly results in stronger localization [6]. The loading fraction 𝑝 was also
studied, with 50 % leading to the most optimal localization, and nearly double the localization
radius for 𝑝 = 40 % [6]. In the case of scintillating nanoguide, increasing the scintillator (OGS-PS)
fraction is preferred for higher light outputs, but a loading fraction of 20 % may be high enough for
𝑑 to be effectively double or more the size of individual domains, due to clusters of the same index
domain acting as one. Figure 1-3 shows magnified images of two scintillating nanoguide samples
with a 𝑝 = 50 % and 𝑝 = 20 %, and demonstrates the effective increase in 𝑑 for lower loading
fractions.

2. OPTICAL RESPONSE

We have conducted lens-coupled optical characterizations of nanoguide and microguide samples.
Scintillating microguide is a core-clad configuration governed by total internal reflection (TIR),
and comprises OGS-PS scintillator as the core medium and PMMA as the cladding for a critical
reflection angle of 68.2°. Current examples of scintillating microguide possess individual domain
diameters of ∼300 µm, which compares to ∼300 nm for scintillating nanoguide. Comparing results
between nanoguide and microguide is intended to help isolate the effect of the small domain size
of nanoguide, and consequently the possible manifestation of TAL in nanoguide.

Two different lasers were used to inject light into the samples. First, a UV laser (355 nm) is used;
this wavelength is absorbed near the surface of the sample and stimulates photoluminescence, re-
sulting in isotropically emitted light similar to radioluminescence. Radioluminescence results from
emissions throughout the bulk of a sample because radiation can penetrate deeper; therefore pho-
toluminescense leads to a more localized emission. Additionally, in contrast to radioluminescence
resulting in singlet and triplet emission, photoluminescence only emits singlets. This difference
affects the time structure of the emission. Second, a red laser (633 nm) is employed; this light
couples into the material and probes propagation properties as a function of input angle.

The experimental setup is shown in Figure 2-1. The laser was fixed in place, and the waveguide
sample was placed on a rotating stand. The camera location was adjusted as needed to obtain
the necessary camera angle and to maintain a constant distance between the light output side of
the sample and camera lens. ImageJ is an image processing program that can calculate area and
pixel value statistics of user selections. It was used to split the pictures into hue, saturation, and
brightness stacks. The brightness stack was then analyzed to obtain brightness and spot size values.
The spot size is reported as the area containing pixels of at least half max brightness.

Brightness data for each light guide was collected at UV laser irradiation angles of 0° to 50° at
10° intervals. Additionally, the camera angle to the back face of the light guide was similarly
0° to 50° at 10° intervals. The results can be seen in Figure 2-2. The microguide sample has
constant brightness values upon increasing laser angle of incidence. Nanoguide follows the same
general pattern with slightly more variation in the brightness values. The consistency of integrated
brightness across varying laser incidences while keeping the camera angle constant is consistent
with the nature of the UV laser, in that the laser induces excitation of the material followed by 4𝜋
emission of light. In this way the directionality of the incident laser is lost, rendering any potential
impacts thereof moot. The main difference between the two is the steep drop off in light between
40° and 50° with microguide compared to the slight drop with nanoguide. This indicates that light
is exiting the nanoguide sample with a broad angular range in contrast with the microguide sample,
in which light exiting the sample drops off at ∼40°. This may indicate that light propagation in
the nanoguide sample is not dictated by TIR, or that the acceptance angle for nanoguide is larger

of analysis. Both lasers results in a significant amount of light being captured from a laser incidence

sideways measurement the light emits in a line across the sample vs a spot of light on the back side.
It is difficult to directly compare these measurements, due to a variety of confounding factors, but
it is still interesting that a significant amount of light can be measured sideways.

3. RADIATION CHARACTERIZATION SETUP

All radiation characterization results are performed with an experimental setup consisting of an
8 × 8 array of 6 mm × 6 mm SiPMs (ARRAYJ_60035_PCB) from onsemi, with each SiPM pixel’s
electronic signal read out individually using five 16-channel, 250 MHz, 14-bit CAEN digitizers
that are synchronized. A custom 64 channel breakout board was designed to individually read out
each pixel’s SOUT channel, which are propagated to the digitizers with four 16 channel CAEN
readout cards. Figure 3-1 illustrates how several measurements are performed to study radiation
detection metrics. The 4.445 cm x 4.445 cm x 5.08 cm nanoguide sample is coupled with silicone
optical grease to the SiPM array such that a corner of the nanoguide sample aligns with a corner
of the SiPM array: the last SiPM column/row are nearly completely uncoupled. The end of the
nanoguide sample opposite from the SiPM readout is coupled to Enhanced Specular Reflector
(ESR) from 3M for some measurements but left bare for others, and the remaining four non-readout
sides are wrapped with several layers of Teflon tape. A collimated particle beam (either gammas
or neutrons emitted from 137 Cs, 22 Na, or AmBe) incident on the scintillating nanoguide sample
will result
 √ in interactions at a fixed distance from the readout end. Collimated beams incident
3 ± 6/ 12 mm√away from the SiPM array are referred to as the “near” configuration, and beams
incident 3 ± 6/ 12 mm away from the opposite end of the nanoguide sample are referred to as the
“far” configuration. The analysis of digitized pulses is conducted in a manner similar to what is
detailed in [10].

3.1. Propagation Time

The propagation time of light traversing the sample is characterized in order to understand the
path light traverses. Timing measurements required timing calibrations of all 64 channels of
the SiPM array to account for cable and digitizer offsets. The back-to-back 511 keV gamma
emissions from a 22 Na source were used to establish timing offsets between each pixel and a
6 mm × 6 mm × 6 mm trans-stilbene tag scintillator coupled to a single 6 mm × 6 mm J-series SiPM.
A 64-channel pixelated array with 6 mm × 6 mm × 12 mm pixels was constructed with a formulation
of OGS containing 80.8 % P2, and ESR reflector on all but the readout end (including between
pixels). The configuration of the timing calibration measurement is shown in Figure 3-2: to ensure
that there is roughly equal coverage of the OGS array, the 22 Na source is placed 1 cm from the face
of tag scintillator, and 8 cm from the face of the OGS array. Pulse times are interpolated between
samples on the rising edge at 50 % of the pulse maximum. The mean of the timing difference

3.3. Particle Discrimination

The particle identification performance is characterized by the neutron/gamma pulse shape dis-
crimination (PSD) figure of merit (FoM) as a function of energy [10]. We define the PSD as the
ratio of the tail pulse integral to the total pulse integral, where the tail begins 30 ns after the pulse
time as determined at 50 % of the rising edge. The total integration window is 280 ns, to avoid
pulse undershooting that is typical of this SiPM. A tail integration start time of 30 ns has previously
been show to optimize the FoM [1], which was confirmed for this work. To provide a performance
baseline, the 1.2 cm bulk OGS pixelated single pixel detector is characterized using an AmBe
source. Figure 3-5 shows the resulting PSD FoM as a function of energy.

The gamma/neutron PSD of nanoguide and its dependence on interaction location is characterized
with a collimated AmBe source. The source is collimated with two 2.25 in × 3.625 in × 7.625 in
HDPE bricks creating a 6 mm gap to an 8 × 8 pixelated array. Experimental data from the OGS
pixelated array in which particles are collimated towards pixel 1, shown in Figure 3-6, demonstrates
effective collimation for both neutrons and gammas.

AmBe neutrons are then pixelated at positions “near” and “far” from the SiPM within the nanoguide.
The energy response is individually calibrated into keVee for both positions using the 22 Na “near”
and “far” datasets. The PSD FoM as a function of energy is then determined for all pixels. Figure 3-
7 shows little difference between the FoM for interactions nearest the SiPM readout vs. furthest.
Deviations in the “near” dataset past 1 MeVee are due to saturation of the digitizer, which does
not occur in the “far” dataset, because the light collection is poorer. Based on this data, any
degradation in the PSD FoM is likely due to photon statistics, and likely arises at energies lower
than 340 keVee.

3.4. Gamma Pulse Shape

The gamma pulse shape of the 6 mm × 6 mm × 12 mm bulk OGS sample and a 1.12 inch nanoguide
sample (NSA13-1B) were measured using the Time Correlated Single Photon Counting (TCSPC)
method [8]. Two photodetectors are coupled to the sample such that the first collects the most light
possible from the sample and acts as the acquisition trigger, and the second is coupled such that
there is a high probability to collect only one photon from the scintillator. This removes response
non-linearities, timing jitter, and trigger biasing of the pulse shape collected after many triggers on
the second detector. Figure 3-8 shows the gamma pulse shape acquired from a 137 Cs source for the
two samples: the nanoguide sample is shown in black and the bulk OGS sample in red. The peaks
at ∼243/246 ns and ∼410/415 ns are the result of PMT after-pulsing (likely H+ and He+ ). The data
is fit to a three-component decaying exponential with two Gaussian functions included to account
for the after-pulsing peaks. The results indicate that for all three exponential decay parameters, the
nanoguide sample has a consistently shorter decay time, although each of the three components
behave differently: the first (shortest) component is 6 % shorter than the bulk, the second is
34 % shorter than the bulk, and the third (longest) is 14 % shorter than the bulk. Comparison of
the prompt decay times to photoluminescence measurements reveals a discrepancy, where a PL
(prompt) decay time of 2.13 ns was observed. This value compares to prompt scintillation decay
times of 4.76 ns (bulk OGS) and 4.38 ns (nanoguide). The differences between prompt PL and
prompt scintillation decay times suggests a potential resolution limitation of the scintillation timing
system used for this measurement. Nonetheless, the observed changes observed between bulk vs.
nanoguide are qualitatively consistent with changes seen in amorphous dilutions of the P2 molecule
in OGS-polymer blends. This observation suggests that the timing differences may be explained
by timing differences in the scintillation material itself rather than the optical configuration of the
material. However, additional measurements are required to determine the specific origin of these
differences.

4. CONCLUSION

We have characterized the optical response and radiation detection performance for a set of scin-
tillating nanoguide samples consisting of 20 % PMMA and 80 % Organic Glass Scintillator and
polystyrene mix with ∼300 nm individual domain sizes. The light collection dependence on inter-
action location, propagation time through the sample, neutron/gamma pulse shape discrimination
figure of merit as a function of propagation, and gamma scintillation pulse shape are presented.
While some intriguing dependence on the interaction distance from the SiPM array were observed,
we cannot confirm that light propagating within nanoguide samples undergo Transverse Ander-
son Localization. In order to independently determine whether these impacts are due to TAL, a
similar set of characterizations should be performed on a nanoguide with a 50 % loading fraction:
although such an array would have a lower light output due to a higher fraction of non-scintillating
material, first principles modeling from the literature indicate that such a nanoguide would have a
stronger localization. Unfortunately for this study, no such nanoguide was available. It would also
be beneficial to conduct optical transport modeling of the array to determine the features of light
propagating through TIR at the 300 nm scale with 50 % and 80 % scintillating material.
We have, however, determined that nanoguide propagation is unique compared to microguide
propagation. Whether both undergo TIR but with different critical angles, or something else
is happening, is unclear. We have also updated radiation characterizations with higher quality
samples and determined how nanoguide would perform for neutron applications. The amount of
light observed with ESR backing is consistent with 80 % scintillating material, and the degradation
in PSD response is likely consistent with lower light collection. However, timing properties are
less well understood and may require further characterization. Finally, changes to the gamma
pulse shape also appear consistent with changes seen in amorphous dilutions of the P2 molecule in
OGS-polymer blends.
In terms of manufacturing quality, there are possible indications that light loss due to defects in
the sample such as voids, surface roughness between domains, or the curvature of domains may
be occurring. A comparison with 90 % or 95 % loading of OGS may offer a more clear indication
that this is the case.
The literature indicates that localization is optimized for a loading fraction of 50 %, a higher
difference in index of refraction between the two materials, and a smaller domain diameter [6].
Upcoming work will focus on disordered arrays that use a loading fraction of 50 % with scintillating
components that maximize the difference in index of refraction: incorporating scintillating PMMA
into nanoguide structures will allow for 100 % of the array to scintillate [11]. This will improve the
neutron detection performance parameters, and will have optimized parameters for TAL. It remains
unclear how isotropic light propagate under TAL from first principles as compared to coherent
light. This will be critical to understanding this new technology and it’s applicability to neutron
detection.
