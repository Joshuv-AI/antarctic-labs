Bayesian Nonparametric Mixed-Effect ODEs with Gaussian Processes
Julien Martinelli Maksim Sinelnikov Harri Lähdesmäki Aalto University Quentin Clairon Mélanie Prague Univ. Bordeaux, INSERM BPH, U1219, Inria SISTM team, VRI, France

Abstract
Dynamical modelling is central to many scientific domains, including pharmacometrics, systems biology, physiology, and epidemiology. In these settings, heterogeneity is often intrinsic: different subjects or units follow related but distinct continuous-time dynamics. Classical nonlinear mixed-effects Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE) models address this by combining population-level structure with subject-specific effects, but they rely on a parametric vector field and are therefore vulnerable to structural misspecification and unmodelled mechanisms. This motivates nonparametric approaches that can retain principled uncertainty quantification, yet existing nonparametric ODE methods typically assume a single shared dynamical system rather than an explicit mixed-effect hierarchy over subject-specific dynamics. We propose MEGPODE , a Bayesian nonparametric mixed-effect ODE model in which each subject’s vector field is decomposed into a shared population component and a subject-specific deviation, both endowed with Gaussian process (GP) priors. To avoid repeated ODE solves per subject during training, we combine state-space GP trajectory priors with virtual collocation observations, yielding Kalman-smoothing trajectory updates and closed-form regressions for the vector fields. Across controlled heterogeneous ODE benchmarks spanning oscillatory, biomedical systems, MEGPODE improves population-field recovery and subject-level trajectory prediction relative to strong baselines.

Introduction

Ordinary differential equation (ODE) models are a standard language for mechanistic modeling in systems biology, medicine, vaccinology, and pharmacometrics because they encode interpretable dynamics and support extrapolation beyond the observed window
[
24
]
.
In longitudinal population studies, however, the scientific object is rarely a single trajectory. Different patients, cells, or experimental units often follow related but distinct dynamical laws, and the practical question is how to model that heterogeneity in a statistically efficient way
[
8
,
1
]
.

The classical framework for this setting is nonlinear mixed-effects (NLME) ODE modelling, where a shared mechanistic system is coupled with subject-specific random effects
[
18
]
.
This is appealing because heterogeneity is represented at the level of the
dynamics
rather than only at the
trajectory
level. But it is also restrictive: one must commit to a parametric vector field and to a specific low-dimensional random-effect parameterization.
This can be problematic in the very regimes where these models are most often used, for instance in biology, medicine, and pharmacometrics
[
25
]
. There, mechanisms are often only partially known, individual differences may not be well captured by a few parameter perturbations, and observations are typically noisy, sparse, and irregularly sampled.
In such settings, parametric NLME-ODEs can be misspecified and difficult to fit
[
20
]
.

These limitations point toward nonparametric dynamics modelling. Flexible continuous-time models based on Gaussian processes (GPs) or neural ODEs can learn unknown dynamics without committing to a detailed mechanistic family
[
15
,
41
,
14
,
22
,
13
,
6
,
16
]
. But most of this literature is built for a single shared dynamical system rather than a heterogeneous population. Conversely, recent population-level continuous-time models do capture subject variability, yet typically through finite-dimensional parametric or neural effects rather than a Bayesian nonparametric hierarchy over subject-specific vector fields
[
27
,
2
,
5
,
20
,
28
]
. This leaves a clear methodological gap: we still lack a nonparametric mixed-effect model for continuous-time dynamics that operates directly at the ODE level and provides uncertainty quantification at both the population and subject levels.

Background

2.1
Problem setup

We observe
M
subjects. Subject
i
is measured at times
\mathcal{T}_{i}=\{t_{i,1}<\dots<t_{i,N_{i}}\}
, with observations
\bm{y}_{i,n}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{y}}
. Let
\bm{x}_{i}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
denote the latent dynamical state. We assume a linear-Gaussian observation model

\bm{y}_{i,n}\mid\bm{x}_{i}(t_{i,n})\sim\mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\bm{H}\bm{x}_{i}(t_{i,n}),\bm{\Sigma}_{y}\bigr),\qquad\bm{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{y}\times D},

(1)

and a subject-specific ODE

\dot{\bm{x}}_{i}(t)=\bm{f}_{i}\bigl(\bm{x}_{i}(t)\bigr),\qquad\bm{x}_{i}(t_{i,0})=\bm{x}_{i,0}.

(2)

The learning problem is to recover a collection of related subject-level vector fields from irregular and partially observed longitudinal data. In the spirit of mixed-effects modeling, we seek a decomposition of subject dynamics into a shared population component and a subject-specific deviation; the explicit form of that decomposition is introduced in Section
3.1
. We next review the GP and state-space ingredients that make this construction computationally tractable.

2.2
Gaussian processes and state-space trajectory priors

A convenient probabilistic starting point for continuous-time trajectories is the GP
[
30
]
. Writing

f\sim\mathcal{GP}(m,k),

(3)

means that any finite collection of function values is jointly Gaussian, with mean function
m
and covariance kernel
k
. For differentiable kernels, derivatives are jointly Gaussian with function values as well, since differentiation is a linear operator. This makes GPs a natural tool when both a trajectory and its derivative matter.

For temporal modelling, an important subclass of GP priors admits an equivalent state-space representation. In particular, Matérn kernels and integrated Wiener-process priors can be written as linear stochastic differential equations whose discretization on an irregular grid
\tau_{0:K}
yields a Gaussian Markov chain
[
33
,
38
,
43
]
:

\bm{s}_{k+1}=\bm{A}(h_{k})\bm{s}_{k}+\bm{q}_{k},\qquad\bm{q}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}\bigl(\mathbf{0},\bm{Q}(h_{k})\bigr),\qquad h_{k}=\tau_{k+1}-\tau_{k},

(4)

for a suitable latent state
\bm{s}_{k}
. Under linear-Gaussian observations, posterior inference then scales linearly in the grid size
K
through Kalman filtering and smoothing
[
32
]
.

To keep both the state and its derivative explicit, define the latent state at time
\tau_{i,k}
as

\bm{s}_{i,k}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\bm{x}_{i}(\tau_{i,k})\\
\dot{\bm{x}}_{i}(\tau_{i,k})\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2D},\qquad\bm{x}_{i}(\tau_{i,k})=\bm{H}_{x}\bm{s}_{i,k},\quad\dot{\bm{x}}_{i}(\tau_{i,k})=\bm{H}_{\dot{x}}\bm{s}_{i,k},

(5)

with
\bm{H}_{x}=[\bm{I}_{D}\;\bm{0}_{D\times D}]\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times 2D}
and
\bm{H}_{\dot{x}}=[\bm{0}_{D\times D}\;\bm{I}_{D}]\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times 2D}
.
For example, an integrated Wiener-process prior of order two yields
\bm{A}(h)=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}1&h\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\otimes\bm{I}_{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{2D\times 2D}
together with a matching process covariance
\bm{Q}(h)=\sigma_{\mathrm{IWP}}^{2}\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}h^{3}/3&h^{2}/2\\
h^{2}/2&h\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\otimes\bm{I}_{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{2D\times 2D}
. The resulting prior is Gaussian and Markov on irregular subject-specific grids, while keeping both trajectories and derivatives available for the ODE construction introduced next.

2.3
Soft ODE constraints via virtual observations

To connect the trajectory prior with the ODE, the dynamical relation is imposed as a soft constraint rather than through repeated numerical integration. Given a known vector field
\bm{f}
, one may require at
collocation times

\tau_{i,k}
that the state derivative agrees with the ODE up to a small collocation error:

\mathbf{0}=\bm{H}_{\dot{x}}\bm{s}_{i,k}-\bm{f}\bigl(\bm{H}_{x}\bm{s}_{i,k}\bigr)+\bm{\epsilon}^{(\mathrm{coloc})}_{i,k},\qquad\bm{\epsilon}^{(\mathrm{coloc})}_{i,k}\sim\mathcal{N}\bigl(\mathbf{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{coloc}}\bigr).

(6)

This construction was proposed by
[
22
,
13
]
. After local linearization, this becomes a Gaussian pseudo-observation on
\bm{s}_{i,k}
, so the resulting posterior remains a Gauss–Markov smoothing problem. Section
3.3
extends this construction to the case where the field
\bm{f}
is itself unknown and hierarchical.

3
Mixed-effect Gaussian Process ODEs (
MEGPODE
)

We now present our method.
Section
3.1
introduces the mixed-effect GP prior over subject-specific fields. Section
3.2
describes the scalable representation used for the shared and local fields. Section
3.3
explains the trajectory-space update obtained by locally linearizing the collocation factor inside a state-space GP prior. Section
3.4
turns smoothed trajectories into a heteroscedastic mixed regression problem that updates shared and local fields. Algorithm
1
summarizes the full loop.

3.1
Hierarchical GP prior on subject-specific vector fields

We model each subject-specific field as the sum of a population field and an individual deviation,

\bm{f}_{i}(\bm{x})=\bm{f}_{0}(\bm{x})+\bm{g}_{i}(\bm{x}),\qquad i=1,\dots,M.

(7)

We use independent-output GP priors across state dimensions
d=1,\dots,D
:

\displaystyle f_{0,d}(\cdot)

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{GP}\bigl(0,k_{0}\bigr),\qquad f_{0,d}\perp\!\!\!\!\perp f_{0,d^{\prime}}\;\text{for}\;d\neq d^{\prime},

(8)

\displaystyle g_{i,d}(\cdot)

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{GP}\bigl(0,k_{r}\bigr),\qquad g_{i,d}\perp\!\!\!\!\perp g_{j,d^{\prime}}\;\text{for}\;(i,d)\neq(j,d^{\prime}),\quad i=1,\dots,M.

(9)

It follows that, for any inputs
\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime}
,

\Cov\!\bigl[f_{i,d}(\bm{x}),f_{j,d^{\prime}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\bigr]=\mathbb{I}[d=d^{\prime}]\Bigl(k_{0}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})+\mathbb{I}[i=j]k_{r}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})\Bigr).

(10)

Thus all subjects borrow strength through the common field, which is exactly the classical NLME philosophy translated from parameter space to function space. MAGMA provides a closely related common-mean construction for trajectory regression
[
19
]
; here the common component acts directly on the vector field. Even though the prior factorizes across output dimensions, the dimensions will be coupled again by the state-space dynamics and collocation constraints during trajectory inference.

3.2
Scalable representation of shared and local vector fields

Direct GP inference over all field evaluations is expensive. To obtain a scalable approximation, the shared and subject-specific fields are represented via separate inducing-feature expansions.
Let
\bm{Z}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{l_{0}\times D}
and
\bm{Z}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{l_{r}\times D}
denote the inducing locations for the population and residual fields, with
l_{0}
and
l_{r}
the corresponding numbers of inducing points.
Using the associated kernel Gram matrices to whiten the basis, define the feature maps

\Phi_{0}(\bm{x})\hskip-1.99997pt=\hskip-1.99997ptk_{0}(\bm{x},\bm{Z}_{0})\!\,k_{0}(\bm{Z}_{0},\bm{Z}_{0})^{-1/2}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times l_{0}},\hskip-1.00006pt\Phi_{r}(\bm{x})\hskip-1.99997pt=\hskip-1.99997ptk_{r}(\bm{x},\bm{Z}_{r})\!\,k_{r}(\bm{Z}_{r},\bm{Z}_{r})^{-1/2}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times l_{r}}.

(11)

For each output dimension
d
, we approximate

f_{0,d}(\bm{x})\approx\Phi_{0}(\bm{x})\bm{b}_{0,d},\qquad g_{i,d}(\bm{x})\approx\Phi_{r}(\bm{x})\bm{b}_{i,d},

(12)

with Gaussian coefficient priors

\bm{b}_{0,d}\sim\mathcal{N}\bigl(\mathbf{0},\bm{I}_{l_{0}}\bigr),\qquad\bm{b}_{i,d}\sim\mathcal{N}\bigl(\bm{0},\lambda_{r}^{-1}\bm{I}_{l_{r}}\bigr).

(13)

The shrinkage parameter
\lambda_{r}
controls the magnitude of the subject-specific field.
\lambda_{r}>1
increases regularization of individual deviations, shifting explanatory power toward the shared population field.

We maintain Gaussian posteriors
q(\bm{b}_{0,d})=\mathcal{N}(\bm{m}_{0,d},\bm{S}_{0,d})
and
q(\bm{b}_{i,d})=\mathcal{N}(\bm{m}_{i,d},\bm{S}_{i,d})
. Conditional on a fixed input
\bm{x}
, the model for the latent subject-specific field value
f_{i,d}(\bm{x})
is linear-Gaussian in these coefficients. Its predictive mean and variance are thus available in closed form:

\mu_{i,d}^{f}(\bm{x})=\Phi_{0}(\bm{x})\bm{m}_{0,d}+\Phi_{r}(\bm{x})\bm{m}_{i,d},~~~~~~~v_{i,d}^{f}(\bm{x})=\Phi_{0}(\bm{x})\bm{S}_{0,d}\Phi_{0}(\bm{x})^{\top}+\Phi_{r}(\bm{x})\bm{S}_{i,d}\Phi_{r}(\bm{x})^{\top}.

(14)

Likewise, the Jacobian of the posterior-mean field is explicit because the feature maps are differentiable. The main challenge lies not in evaluating field moments at a fixed state, but in handling uncertainty in the latent trajectory itself. The next two subsections resolve this by alternating between trajectory smoothing and field regression.

3.3
Trajectory-space inference via local collocation linearization

Naively maximizing a likelihood based on the unknown subject fields would require repeatedly solving one nonlinear ODE per subject every time the shared or local field changes. We avoid this by introducing an auxiliary trajectory posterior on a subject-specific collocation grid
\mathcal{C}_{i}:=\{\tau_{i,0},\dots,\tau_{i,K_{i}}\}
that contains the observation times. We use a mean-field variational factorization

q\bigl(\{\bm{b}_{0,d}\},\{\bm{b}_{i,d}\},\{\bm{s}_{i,0:K_{i}}\}\bigr)=\Bigl(\prod_{d=1}^{D}q(\bm{b}_{0,d})\Bigr)\Bigl(\prod_{i=1}^{M}\prod_{d=1}^{D}q(\bm{b}_{i,d})\Bigr)\Bigl(\prod_{i=1}^{M}q(\bm{s}_{i,0:K_{i}})\Bigr),

(15)

where
q(\bm{s}_{i,0:K_{i}})
denotes the variational posterior over the latent trajectory of subject
i
on
\mathcal{C}_{i}
.
The initial state
\bm{x}_{i,0}
is treated as a latent subject-specific quantity and modeled through a mixed-effect Gaussian decomposition. Details are provided in Appendix
D.5
.

Following Section
2.3
, collocation is imposed via soft pseudo observations. Here, however, the field is both subject-specific and unknown. For subject
i
and collocation index
k
, the ODE residual is

\mathbf{0}=\bm{H}_{\dot{x}}\bm{s}_{i,k}-\bm{f}_{i}\bigl(\bm{H}_{x}\bm{s}_{i,k}\bigr)+\bm{\epsilon}^{(\mathrm{coloc})}_{i,k},\qquad\bm{\epsilon}^{(\mathrm{coloc})}_{i,k}\sim\mathcal{N}\bigl(\mathbf{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{coloc}}\bigr).

(16)

This term is difficult to handle for two reasons:
\bm{f}_{i}\bigl(\bm{H}_{x}\bm{s}_{i,k}\bigr)
is a nonlinear function of the latent state, and the vector field itself remains uncertain under the current posterior. We therefore approximate
\bm{f}_{i}\bigl(\bm{H}_{x}\bm{s}_{i,k}\bigr)
locally by a Gaussian distribution around a reference point
\bar{\bm{x}}_{i,k}
from the previous trajectory iterate.
Its mean is given by a first-order expansion of the posterior-mean vector field, while its covariance captures posterior uncertainty in the field evaluation. Define

\bm{\mu}^{f}_{i,k}=\mathbb{E}_{q}\bigl[\bm{f}_{i}(\bar{\bm{x}}_{i,k})\bigr],\qquad\bm{\Sigma}^{f}_{i,k}=\Var_{q}\bigl[\bm{f}_{i}(\bar{\bm{x}}_{i,k})\bigr],\qquad\bm{J}_{i,k}=\nabla_{\bm{x}}\mathbb{E}_{q}\bigl[\bm{f}_{i}(\bm{x})\bigr]\Big|_{\bm{x}=\bar{\bm{x}}_{i,k}}.

(17)

A first-order expansion of the posterior-mean field then gives

\mathbb{E}_{q}\bigl[\bm{f}_{i}(\bm{H}_{x}\bm{s}_{i,k})\bigr]\approx\bm{\mu}^{f}_{i,k}+\bm{J}_{i,k}\bigl(\bm{H}_{x}\bm{s}_{i,k}-\bar{\bm{x}}_{i,k}\bigr),

(18)

and this leads to the Gaussian pseudo-observation

\displaystyle\bm{c}^{(\mathrm{coloc})}_{i,k}=\bm{H}^{(\mathrm{coloc})}_{i,k}\bm{s}_{i,k}+\bm{\epsilon}_{i,k},\qquad\bm{\epsilon}_{i,k}\sim\mathcal{N}\bigl(\mathbf{0},\bm{R}^{(\mathrm{coloc})}_{i,k}\bigr),~\text{and}

(19)

\displaystyle\bm{H}^{(\mathrm{coloc})}_{i,k}=\bm{H}_{\dot{x}}-\bm{J}_{i,k}\bm{H}_{x},\quad\bm{c}^{(\mathrm{coloc})}_{i,k}=\bm{\mu}^{f}_{i,k}-\bm{J}_{i,k}\bar{\bm{x}}_{i,k},\quad\bm{R}^{(\mathrm{coloc})}_{i,k}=\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{coloc}}+\bm{\Sigma}^{f}_{i,k}.

(20)

\bm{H}^{(\mathrm{coloc})}_{i,k}
is the local linearized ODE-residual operator: it maps the augmented state
\bm{s}_{i,k}
to the difference between the derivative component
\dot{\bm{x}}_{i,k}
and the Jacobian-linearized field evaluation at
\bm{x}_{i,k}
.
The derivation is given in Appendix
D.1
.

The observation model in Eq. (
1
), the state-space prior in Eq. (
4
), and the collocation pseudo-observation in Eq. (
19
) together define a linear-Gaussian smoothing problem. Therefore, each
q(\bm{s}_{i,0:K_{i}})
is a Gauss–Markov posterior that can be computed by Kalman smoothing, (Appendix
D.2
).
Lastly, as mentioned previously, output coupling arises even if vector-field prior factorizes across output dimensions, as made explicit by the Jacobian
\bm{J}_{i,k}
acting on the augmented state.

3.4
Vector field update as mixed regression on smoothed pseudo-derivatives

After smoothing subject
i
, we extract pseudo-observations for the vector field:

\tilde{\bm{x}}_{i,k}=\mathbb{E}_{q}\bigl[\bm{x}_{i}(\tau_{i,k})\bigr],\qquad\widetilde{\dot{\bm{x}}}_{i,k}=\mathbb{E}_{q}\bigl[\dot{\bm{x}}_{i}(\tau_{i,k})\bigr],\qquad\widetilde{\bm{R}}_{i,k}=\Var_{q}\bigl(\dot{\bm{x}}_{i}(\tau_{i,k})\bigr)+\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{coloc}}.

(21)

Note that
\tilde{\bm{x}}_{i,k}
differs from
\bar{\bm{x}}_{i,k}
:
\bar{\bm{x}}_{i,k}
is the reference point used to construct the local linearization in the current trajectory update, whereas
\tilde{\bm{x}}_{i,k}
is the resulting updated smoothed mean after that update.

Fix an output dimension
d
. Stack the pseudo-derivative means for subject
i
as

\bm{u}_{i,d}=[\widetilde{\dot{x}}_{i,0,d},\dots,\widetilde{\dot{x}}_{i,K_{i},d}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{K_{i}+1},

(22)

and define feature design matrices

\bm{P}_{0,i,d}=\bigl[\Phi_{0}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{i,j-1})\bigr]_{j=1}^{K_{i}+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{(K_{i}+1)\times l_{0}},\qquad\bm{P}_{r,i,d}=\bigl[\Phi_{r}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{i,j-1})\bigr]_{j=1}^{K_{i}+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{(K_{i}+1)\times l_{r}}.

(23)

Let
\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{reg}}_{i,d}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\bm{R}_{i,d})
, where
\widetilde{\bm{R}}_{i,k}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}
is the local pseudo-derivative covariance at time
\tau_{i,k}
(Eq. (
21
)), and
\bm{R}_{i,d}\in\mathbb{R}^{(K_{i}+1)\times(K_{i}+1)}
collects the uncertainty for output dimension
d
across the collocation times, e.g., through the diagonal approximation
\bm{R}_{i,d}=\diag\!\bigl([\widetilde{\bm{R}}_{i,0}]_{dd},\dots,[\widetilde{\bm{R}}_{i,K_{i}}]_{dd}\bigr)
, or via other conservative variants
(Appendix
D.7
).
With this notation, the pseudo-data induce, for each subject
i
and output dimension
d
, the heteroscedastic linear-Gaussian mixed regression

\bm{u}_{i,d}=\bm{P}_{0,i,d}\bm{b}_{0,d}+\bm{P}_{r,i,d}\bm{b}_{i,d}+\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{reg}}_{i,d}.

(24)

Thus the field-learning step can be viewed as mixed regression in feature space.
Writing Gaussian posterior over a generic coefficient vector
\bm{b}
in natural form,

q(\bm{b})\propto\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{b}^{\top}\bm{\Lambda}\bm{b}+\bm{\eta}^{\top}\bm{b}\right),

the coefficient updates are obtained by alternating between the shared and local components, each time holding the current posterior mean of the other component fixed. For the shared field,

\displaystyle\bm{\Lambda}^{\mathrm{new}}_{0,d}

\displaystyle=\bm{I}_{l_{0}}+\sum_{i=1}^{M}\bm{P}_{0,i,d}^{\top}\bm{R}_{i,d}^{-1}\bm{P}_{0,i,d},

(25)

\displaystyle\bm{\eta}^{\mathrm{new}}_{0,d}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\bm{P}_{0,i,d}^{\top}\bm{R}_{i,d}^{-1}\bigl(\bm{u}_{i,d}-\bm{P}_{r,i,d}\bm{m}_{i,d}\bigr),

(26)

and for the local field of subject
i
,

\displaystyle\bm{\Lambda}^{\mathrm{new}}_{i,d}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{r}\bm{I}_{l_{r}}+\bm{P}_{r,i,d}^{\top}\bm{R}_{i,d}^{-1}\bm{P}_{r,i,d},

(27)

\displaystyle\bm{\eta}^{\mathrm{new}}_{i,d}

\displaystyle=\bm{P}_{r,i,d}^{\top}\bm{R}_{i,d}^{-1}\bigl(\bm{u}_{i,d}-\bm{P}_{0,i,d}\bm{m}_{0,d}\bigr).

(28)

3.5
Structural analysis and representation variants

Structural properties.
Appendix
E
develops structural properties of
MEGPODE
, summarized in Table
S5
:
well-posed posterior-mean dynamics, population/local covariance and variance decompositions, and the Gaussian geometry of the conditional field update.
Two consequences are especially relevant here. First, conditional on the current smoothed pseudo-data, the field update is a strictly convex weighted mixed-ridge regression with a unique minimizer, yielding conditional identifiability of the shared and local components (Proposition
6
, Corollary
7
). Second, Eq. (
25
) shows that shared precision accumulates as a sum of positive semidefinite subject-specific contributions, so adding subjects can only increase shared precision and reduce shared posterior covariance (Proposition
10
). This property makes our approach the dynamical analogue of common-mean GPs like MAGMA
[
19
]
.

Representation variants.

Appendix
F
shows that the method extends beyond inducing
features to any finite-dimensional linear-Gaussian field representation with
tractable moments and Jacobians, e.g., random Fourier features
[
29
]

and Hilbert bases
[
35
]
.
Appendix

4
Related work

Flexible continuous-time dynamics.

GP-ODEs and neural ODE models form the closest continuous-time modelling lineage to our approach. GP-based ODE learning spans nonparametric vector-field inference
[
15
]
, gradient matching
[
11
,
40
,
23
]
, manifold-constrained GP inference
[
41
]
, variational multiple shooting
[
14
]
, and collocation-based physics-informed models
[
22
,
13
]
. Neural ODEs
[
6
]
, latent ODEs
[
31
]
, and Neural CDEs
[
16
]
provide flexible alternatives for irregular-time prediction and forecasting. These methods learn expressive dynamics, but most assume a single shared dynamical system rather than a population of subject-specific vector fields.

Personalized neural dynamics.

Recent approaches adapt continuous-time neural dynamics across environments or patients. Mixed-effect Neural ODEs
[
27
]
introduce subject-specific latent random effects into neural ODEs. Context-conditioned neural dynamics, including CoDA
[
17
]
, MetaPhysiCa
[
26
]
, and DIF
[
12
]
, use context variables or meta-learned representations to personalize dynamics across environments. These approaches are flexible and personalized, but lack a probabilistic treatment of the mixed-effect decomposition of the vector field.

Mixed-effect population models.

Parametric NLME-ODEs remain the dominant framework for population dynamics in pharmacometrics and systems biology
[
21
,
18
,
39
]
. Population Kalman filtering
[
9
]
and recent amortized or in-context estimators
[
2
,
20
,
28
]
improve scalability, personalization, or inference speed, but still operate in predefined parametric or neural model families.
Flexible mixed-effect regression models, including common-mean multi-task GPs such as MAGMA
[
19
]
and other mixed-effect GP formulations
[
7
,
42
]
, share information across subjects but do not explicitly model continuous-time dynamics.
In contrast,
MEGPODE
places the mixed-effect hierarchy directly over the vector field, combining personalization, flexible dynamics, and function-space uncertainty.

5
Experiments

We evaluate
MEGPODE
in controlled population-ODE benchmarks where the
ground-truth dynamics are known. Experiments will assess whether
the mixed-effect field decomposition improves population-level recovery and
prediction, how sensitive the method is to its hyperparameters and data
regime choices, and whether function-space residuals can correct a misspecified mechanistic population model. All experiments in this section use synthetic systems, which allows us to evaluate both predictive performance and recovery of the underlying population dynamics.

6
Discussion

We introduced
MEGPODE
, a flexible mixed-effect model for heterogeneous continuous-time dynamics. By decomposing each subject’s vector field with GP priors over both the population field and subject-specific deviations,
MEGPODE
lifts the classical NLME-ODE population/individual decomposition from parameter space to vector-field function space. At the same time, it can be seen as the dynamical analogue of mixed-effect GPs
[
19
]
: the shared and local components act on the ODE drift rather than directly on trajectories or regression outputs. Our approach combines state-space collocation with Kalman smoothing trajectory inference without repeated ODE solves.

Limitations.
Our empirical study is limited to synthetic examples; while this enables ground-truth evaluation of fields and population trajectories, validation on real-world longitudinal data remains a key next step.
Next,
MEGPODE
depends on kernel and scalable representation choices.
We use inducing features, but any differentiable finite-dimensional linear-Gaussian field representation can be used (Appendix
F
).
Finally, the trajectory update relies on local linearization of the nonlinear collocation factor, so accuracy may degrade when the state posterior is broad or strongly multimodal.

Perspectives.

A key direction is to develop
grey-box
extensions
[
44
,
36
,
34
]
. Motivated by Section
5.3
,
MEGPODE
could combine a mechanistic ODE backbone with GP residual fields, correcting structural misspecification while separating parametric dynamics from residual uncertainty. A second direction is to move from static subject covariates to
dynamical covariates
, enabling more faithful modelling of biomedical trajectories driven by time-varying inputs.
These directions mirror a broader biology-informed ML perspective: combining mechanistic structure, subject-level context, and flexible data-driven components rather than relying on any single source of information
[
25
]
.

Appendix B
Baselines and hyperparameters

B.1
Concurrent baselines

CoDA context-conditioned neural ODE.

We include a context-conditioned neural ODE baseline inspired by CoDA
[
17
]
. In its original setting, different environments share a neural dynamical model but are allowed to induce environment-specific vector fields through learned context variables. We adapt this view to our population setting by treating each training subject as one environment. Each subject has a learned context code
\bm{c}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{c}}
and a learned initial condition
\bm{x}_{i,0}
, and evolves according to

\dot{\bm{x}}_{i}(t)=f_{\Theta,\psi}(\bm{x}_{i}(t);\bm{c}_{i}),\qquad\bm{x}_{i}(t_{i,0})=\bm{x}_{i,0}.

The vector field is represented by a shared base MLP whose weights are additively perturbed by a hypernetwork applied to the context code:

f_{\Theta,\psi}(\bm{x};\bm{c})=\mathrm{MLP}_{\Theta+\Delta_{\psi}(\bm{c})}(\bm{x}),\qquad\Delta_{\psi}(\bm{c})=A_{\psi}\bm{c}.

Thus
\Theta
defines a population neural vector field, while
\Delta_{\psi}(\bm{c}_{i})
defines a subject-specific deformation of that field. Observations are modeled as

\bm{y}_{i,n}=\bm{H}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}(t_{i,n})+\bm{\varepsilon}_{i,n},\qquad\bm{\varepsilon}_{i,n}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{y}^{2}\bm{I}).

Training minimizes the pooled reconstruction error over all subjects, jointly over the shared MLP parameters, the hypernetwork, the subject context codes, and the subject initial conditions:

\displaystyle\min_{\Theta,\psi\{\bm{c}_{i},\bm{x}_{i,0}\}}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{i}}\left\|\bm{y}_{i,n}-\bm{H}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}(t_{i,n};\Theta,\psi,\bm{c}_{i},\bm{x}_{i,0})\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle+\lambda_{c}\sum_{i}\|\bm{c}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{h}\sum_{i}\|\Delta_{\psi}(\bm{c}_{i})\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{x}\sum_{i}\|\bm{x}_{i,0}-\bar{\bm{x}}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}.

Here
\bar{\bm{x}}_{0}
denotes the empirical center of the learned subject initial conditions. In our implementation, trajectories are integrated with a fixed-step RK4 solver, and
\sigma_{y}
is set from the final training RMSE with a small floor.

For held-out-subject adaptation, the shared network and hypernetwork are frozen. Only a new context code and initial condition are optimized from the available context observations:

(\hat{\bm{c}}_{\star},\hat{\bm{x}}_{\star,0})=\arg\min_{\bm{c},\bm{x}_{0}}\sum_{n\in\mathcal{C}_{\star}}\left\|\bm{y}_{\star,n}-\bm{H}_{\star}\bm{x}_{\star}(t_{\star,n};\Theta,\psi,\bm{c},\bm{x}_{0})\right\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{c}\|\bm{c}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{x}\|\bm{x}_{0}-\bar{\bm{x}}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}.

Hence CoDA can personalize dynamics through a low-dimensional context variable, but it does not explicitly decompose the field into a shared GP component plus a subject-specific random-effect field. For population-level summaries, we therefore define the CoDA population field as the empirical average of the learned subject-specific context fields,

f^{\mathrm{pop}}_{\mathrm{CoDA}}(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}f_{\Theta,\psi}(\bm{x};\bm{c}_{i}).

Since the mapping from context code to vector field acts through weight perturbations, averaging the resulting vector fields is more faithful than evaluating the model at the empirical mean context code.

The population trajectory is obtained by integrating this averaged field from the empirical mean initial condition,

\dot{\bm{x}}^{\mathrm{pop}}(t)=f^{\mathrm{pop}}_{\mathrm{CoDA}}(\bm{x}^{\mathrm{pop}}(t)),\qquad\bm{x}^{\mathrm{pop}}(0)=\bar{\bm{x}}_{0}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\bm{x}_{i,0}.

The baseline is therefore deterministic apart from the scalar Gaussian observation noise used for predictive scores; it does not maintain a posterior over vector fields.

Mixed-effect neural ODE.

We further include a mixed-effect neural ODE baseline based on
[
27
]
. This model represents each subject through a latent trajectory
\bm{z}_{i}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{z}}
, decoded into observation space by a neural decoder
D_{\omega}
:

\bm{y}_{i,n}=D_{\omega}(\bm{z}_{i}(t_{i,n}))+\bm{\varepsilon}_{i,n},\qquad\bm{\varepsilon}_{i,n}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{y}^{2}\bm{I}).

The initial latent state is inferred amortizedly from the subject’s observed time series using an encoder,

q_{\phi}(\bm{z}_{i,0}\mid\{(t_{i,n},\bm{y}_{i,n})\}_{n\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}),

implemented either as an ODE-RNN or an RNN encoder. Subject heterogeneity enters the latent vector field through mixed-effect weights. Concretely, the latent drift is written as

\dot{\bm{z}}_{i}(t)=G_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{i}(t))\,\bm{w}_{i},

where
G_{\theta}
is a shared neural network and the subject-specific mixed-effect weights are sampled from a diagonal Gaussian distribution

\bm{w}_{i}=\bm{\mu}_{w}+\bm{\sigma}_{w}\odot\bm{\epsilon}_{i},\qquad\bm{\epsilon}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\bm{I}).

Thus the model has a neural population component through
G_{\theta}
and
\bm{\mu}_{w}
, together with random effects through
\bm{\sigma}_{w}
.

The training objective is a variational reconstruction objective with KL terms for both the inferred initial state and the mixed-effect weights. In simplified form,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ME\text{-}NODE}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}(\bm{z}_{i,0}\mid\bm{y}_{i})\,q(\bm{w}_{i})}\left[\log p(\bm{y}_{i}\mid\bm{z}_{i,0},\bm{w}_{i},\theta,\omega)\right]

\displaystyle-\beta_{z}\KL\!\left(q_{\phi}(\bm{z}_{i,0}\mid\bm{y}_{i})\,\|\,p(\bm{z}_{0})\right)-\beta_{w}\KL\!\left(q(\bm{w}_{i})\,\|\,p(\bm{w})\right),

with KL annealing during training. In the implementation used here, several samples of
\bm{z}_{i,0}
and
\bm{w}_{i}
are drawn, and the best reconstruction among these samples is used for the subject loss. Training proceeds one subject at a time so that irregular subject-specific observation grids are preserved.

For held-out subjects, there is no separate optimization of new parameters. Instead, the learned encoder and mixed-effect distribution are reused: the context observations of the new subject are passed through the encoder, candidate mixed-effect samples are drawn, and the context-compatible samples determine the predictive distribution. Thus adaptation is amortized rather than obtained by a subject-specific optimization loop.

At evaluation time, for each subject
i
, we draw candidate samples

\bm{z}_{i,0}^{(s)}\sim q_{\phi}(\bm{z}_{i,0}\mid\bm{y}_{i}),\qquad\bm{w}_{i}^{(s)}\sim q(\bm{w}_{i}),\qquad s=1,\dots,S_{\mathrm{cand}},

rank them by reconstruction error on the available context observations, and retain
S
context-compatible samples. Each retained sample is propagated through the latent ODE,

\dot{\bm{z}}_{i}^{(s)}(t)=G_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{i}^{(s)}(t))\bm{w}_{i}^{(s)},\qquad\hat{\bm{x}}_{i}^{(s)}(t)=D_{\omega}(\bm{z}_{i}^{(s)}(t)).

The reported posterior predictive mean and epistemic standard deviation are then

\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{i}(t)=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\hat{\bm{x}}_{i}^{(s)}(t),\qquad\hat{\bm{\sigma}}_{i,\mathrm{post}}^{2}(t)=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\left(\hat{\bm{x}}_{i}^{(s)}(t)-\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{i}(t)\right)^{2}.

For probabilistic trajectory scores and predictive bands, we add the learned scalar observation noise,

\hat{\bm{\sigma}}_{i,\mathrm{pred}}^{2}(t)=\hat{\bm{\sigma}}_{i,\mathrm{post}}^{2}(t)+\hat{\sigma}_{y}^{2}.

For population-level summaries, this baseline does not expose an explicit vector field in the original observation space. We therefore define its population trajectory operationally by averaging sampled subject trajectories over a fixed subset
\mathcal{I}
of training subjects:

\hat{\bm{x}}^{\mathrm{pop},(s)}_{\mathrm{ME\text{-}NODE}}(t)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}D_{\omega}(\bm{z}_{i}^{(s)}(t)).

The reported population mean and posterior uncertainty are computed across these Monte Carlo population trajectories. This gives ME-NODE subject-level and population-level uncertainty over latent initial states and mixed-effect weights, but not a posterior over the shared neural vector field parameters
\theta
, which remain point estimates.

GPODE variational multiple shooting with shared field and subject-specific initial conditions.

As a non-mixed-effect GP-ODE comparator, we adapt the variational multiple-shooting model of Hegde et al.
[
14
]
to the population setting by forcing all subjects to share a single latent vector field, while allowing each subject to have its own initial condition and its own auxiliary shooting states. Concretely, we fit

\dot{\bm{x}}_{i}(t)=\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{i}(t)),\qquad\bm{x}_{i}(t_{i,0})=\bm{x}_{i,0},\qquad i=1,\dots,M,

under one common GP prior and one shared variational posterior for
\bm{f}
, with subject-specific variational factors for
\bm{x}_{i,0}
and for the shooting states. Hence this baseline can exploit multiple initial conditions and long trajectories, but it has no explicit mixed-effect decomposition: all inter-subject variation must be absorbed through the subject-specific initial conditions, the subject-specific shooting states, and posterior uncertainty in the single shared field.

In addition to the subject-specific initial conditions
\bm{x}_{i,0}
, auxiliary shooting states are introduced

\bm{S}_{i}:=\bigl(\bm{s}_{i,1},\dots,\bm{s}_{i,N_{i}-1}\bigr),\qquad\bm{s}_{i,0}:=\bm{x}_{i,0},\qquad i=1,\dots,M.

All subjects share the same latent vector field
\bm{f}
and the same inducing variables
\bm{U}
.
The shooting transition is

p\!\left(\bm{s}_{i,n}\mid\bm{s}_{i,n-1},\bm{f}\right)=\mathcal{N}\!\left(\bm{s}_{i,n}\;\middle|\;\bm{x}\!\left(t_{i,n};\,\bm{s}_{i,n-1},\bm{f}\right),\;\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\bm{I}_{D}\right),\qquad n=1,\dots,N_{i}-1,

where
\bm{x}(t_{i,n};\,\bm{s}_{i,n-1},\bm{f})
denotes the ODE solution at time
t_{i,n}

obtained by integrating
\dot{\bm{x}}(t)=\bm{f}(\bm{x}(t))
from initial state
\bm{s}_{i,n-1}
over

[t_{i,n-1},t_{i,n}]
. The observation likelihood is written similarly as

p\!\left(\bm{y}_{i,n}\mid\bm{s}_{i,n-1},\bm{f}\right)=p\!\left(\bm{y}_{i,n}\mid\bm{x}\!\left(t_{i,n};\,\bm{s}_{i,n-1},\bm{f}\right)\right).

A natural variational family is

q\!\left(\{\bm{S}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M},\{\bm{x}_{i,0}\}_{i=1}^{M},\bm{f},\bm{U}\right)=p(\bm{f}\mid\bm{U})\,q(\bm{U})\,\prod_{i=1}^{M}\left[q(\bm{x}_{i,0})\prod_{n=1}^{N_{i}-1}q(\bm{s}_{i,n})\right].

The resulting population ELBO is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GPODE\text{-}pop}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\Bigg[\sum_{n=1}^{N_{i}}\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{s}_{i,n-1})\,q(\bm{f})}\Bigl[\log p\!\left(\bm{y}_{i,n}\mid\bm{s}_{i,n-1},\bm{f}\right)\Bigr]

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\sum_{n=1}^{N_{i}-1}\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{s}_{i,n-1})\,q(\bm{s}_{i,n})\,q(\bm{f})}\Bigl[\log p\!\left(\bm{s}_{i,n}\mid\bm{s}_{i,n-1},\bm{f}\right)\Bigr]

\displaystyle\qquad\quad-\sum_{n=1}^{N_{i}-1}\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{s}_{i,n})}\bigl[\log q(\bm{s}_{i,n})\bigr]-\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q(\bm{x}_{i,0})\,\|\,p(\bm{x}_{i,0})\right)\Bigg]-\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q(\bm{U})\,\|\,p(\bm{U})\right).

The training objective sums the subject-wise likelihood and shooting terms under this common field, together with a single shared inducing-variable KL term.

For comparability with our population-level summaries, we define the corresponding population trajectory by integrating the posterior-mean shared field

\bar{\bm{f}}(\bm{x}):=\mathbb{E}_{q}[\bm{f}(\bm{x})]

from a reference initial condition
\bm{x}^{\mathrm{pop}}_{0}
, for example the empirical mean of the subject-specific initial conditions:

\dot{\bm{x}}^{\mathrm{pop}}(t)=\bar{\bm{f}}\bigl(\bm{x}^{\mathrm{pop}}(t)\bigr),\qquad\bm{x}^{\mathrm{pop}}(0)=\bm{x}^{\mathrm{pop}}_{0}.

Uncertainty for this population trajectory can be approximated by Monte Carlo pushforward of samples from the learned field posterior, all propagated from the same reference initial condition.

Mechanistic NLME-ODE.

For benchmarks whose mechanistic family is known, we include a classical nonlinear mixed-effects ODE baseline
[
18
,
10
]
. We assume that each subject follows a parametric ODE

\dot{\bm{x}}_{i}(t)=f\!\left(\bm{x}_{i}(t);\bm{\theta}_{i}\right),\qquad\bm{\theta}_{i}=\bm{\theta}+\bm{\eta}_{i},\qquad\bm{\eta}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Omega}),

with subject-specific initial condition

\bm{x}_{i}(t_{i,0})=\bm{x}_{i,0},\qquad\bm{x}_{i,0}=\bm{x}_{0}+\bm{\xi}_{i},\qquad\bm{\xi}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Omega}_{x_{0}}),\qquad i=1,\dots,M,

where
\bm{\theta}
collects shared population parameters,
\bm{\eta}_{i}
denotes subject-specific random effects on the mechanistic parameters, and
\bm{x}_{0}
is the population initial condition. In practice, random effects act only on a subset of mechanistic parameters, with the remaining components fixed across subjects. Observations are modeled as

\bm{y}_{i,n}=\bm{H}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}(t_{i,n})+\bm{\varepsilon}_{i,n},\qquad\bm{\varepsilon}_{i,n}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{y}^{2}\bm{I}).

On correctly specified mechanistic systems, the random effects are placed on the same parameters used to generate heterogeneity in the benchmark.

We fit this model with a SAEM-style empirical-Bayes procedure. At iteration
m
, we first compute, for each subject
i
, a conditional mode

\displaystyle(\hat{\bm{\eta}}_{i}^{(m)},\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{i}^{(m)})=\arg\min_{\bm{\eta}_{i},\bm{\xi}_{i}}

\displaystyle\Bigl[\frac{1}{2\left(\sigma_{y}^{(m-1)}\right)^{2}}\sum_{n}\left\|\bm{y}_{i,n}-\bm{H}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}(t_{i,n};\bm{\theta}^{(m-1)},\bm{\eta}_{i},\bm{x}_{0}^{(m-1)}+\bm{\xi}_{i})\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\bm{\eta}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\Omega}^{(m-1)-1}\bm{\eta}_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{\xi}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\Omega}_{x_{0}}^{(m-1)-1}\bm{\xi}_{i}\Bigr],

where
\bm{x}_{i}(t;\bm{\theta},\bm{\eta}_{i},\bm{x}_{i,0})
is obtained by numerical integration of the mechanistic ODE. Given the current subject modes, we then perform a single population M-step:

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\bm{\theta}}\sum_{i,n}\left\|\bm{y}_{i,n}-\bm{H}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}(t_{i,n};\bm{\theta},\hat{\bm{\eta}}_{i}^{(m)},\bm{x}_{0}^{(m-1)}+\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{i}^{(m)})\right\|_{2}^{2},

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{0}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\hat{\bm{x}}_{i,0}^{(m)}=\bm{x}_{0}^{(m-1)}+\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{i}^{(m)},

\displaystyle\bm{\Omega}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Diag}\!\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\left(\hat{\bm{\eta}}_{i}^{(m)}\right)^{2}\right),

\displaystyle\bm{\Omega}_{x_{0}}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Diag}\!\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\left(\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{i}^{(m)}\right)^{2}\right),

\displaystyle\sigma_{y}^{2\,(m)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{obs}}}\sum_{i,n}\left\|\bm{y}_{i,n}-\bm{H}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}(t_{i,n};\bm{\theta}^{(m)},\hat{\bm{\eta}}_{i}^{(m)},\bm{x}_{0}^{(m)}+\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{i}^{(m)})\right\|_{2}^{2}.

These two steps are repeated for a fixed number of iterations. For held-out-subject adaptation, the learned population parameters are kept fixed and only the new subject’s random effects and initial condition are re-estimated from the available context observations.

This baseline provides a standard parametric NLME comparator when the mechanistic family is known, but it is structurally misspecified on benchmarks where heterogeneity is injected through residual subject-specific vector fields rather than through parameter random effects.

For comparability with our population-level summaries, we define the corresponding population trajectory by integrating the learned mechanistic population dynamics from the learned population initial condition:

\dot{\bm{x}}^{\mathrm{pop}}(t)=f\!\left(\bm{x}^{\mathrm{pop}}(t);\hat{\bm{\theta}}\right),\qquad\bm{x}^{\mathrm{pop}}(0)=\hat{\bm{x}}_{0}.

B.2
Baselines derived from
MEGPODE

We compare the full mixed-effect model against two controlled ablations that keep the same trajectory prior, the same collocation construction in Eqs. (
19
)–(
20
), and the same pseudo-derivative extraction in Eq. (
21
). The only difference is which components of the subject-specific field are allowed to explain the dynamics.

Shared field only (
f0_only
).

This ablation removes the subject-specific deviation altogether and forces all subjects to share a single population field:

f^{\texttt{f0\_only}}_{i,d}(\bm{x})=f_{0,d}(\bm{x})\approx\Phi_{0}(\bm{x})\bm{b}_{0,d},\qquad g_{i,d}(\bm{x})\equiv 0.

The pseudo-regression of Eq. (
24
) therefore reduces to

\bm{u}_{i,d}=\bm{P}_{0,i,d}\bm{b}_{0,d}+\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{reg}}_{i,d}\qquad\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{reg}}_{i,d}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\bm{R}_{i,d}),

so only the shared posterior is updated:

\displaystyle\bm{\Lambda}_{0,d}^{\mathrm{new}}

\displaystyle=\bm{I}_{l_{0}}+\sum_{i=1}^{M}\bm{P}_{0,i,d}^{\top}\bm{R}_{i,d}^{-1}\bm{P}_{0,i,d},

\displaystyle\bm{\eta}_{0,d}^{\mathrm{new}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\bm{P}_{0,i,d}^{\top}\bm{R}_{i,d}^{-1}\bm{u}_{i,d}.

There is no local-field update. In trajectory space, the collocation moments

(\mu^{f}_{i,k},\bm{\Sigma}^{f}_{i,k},\bm{J}_{i,k})
are therefore computed from the shared field alone. This baseline isolates how much of the data can be explained by a single population ODE with no subject-specific adaptation.

One field per subject, no shared component (
gi_only
).

This ablation removes the common field and learns one independent field for each subject:

f^{\texttt{gi\_only}}_{i,d}(\bm{x})=g_{i,d}(\bm{x})\approx\Phi_{r}(\bm{x})\bm{b}_{i,d},\qquad f_{0,d}(\bm{x})\equiv 0.

The priors are

\bm{b}_{i,d}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\lambda_{r}^{-1}\bm{I}_{l_{r}}),\qquad i=1,\dots,M.

The pseudo-regression now factorizes over subjects:

\bm{u}_{i,d}=\bm{P}_{r,i,d}\bm{b}_{i,d}+\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{reg}}_{i,d}\qquad\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{reg}}_{i,d}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\bm{R}_{i,d}),

and each local posterior is updated independently:

\displaystyle\bm{\Lambda}_{i,d}^{\mathrm{new}}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{r}\bm{I}_{l_{r}}+\bm{P}_{r,i,d}^{\top}\bm{R}_{i,d}^{-1}\bm{P}_{r,i,d},

\displaystyle\bm{\eta}_{i,d}^{\mathrm{new}}

\displaystyle=\bm{P}_{r,i,d}^{\top}\bm{R}_{i,d}^{-1}\bm{u}_{i,d}.

Hence there is no cross-subject borrowing through a shared latent field. This baseline isolates the value of explicitly modeling a population-level dynamical component.

Relation to the full model.

The full method combines both ingredients,

f_{i,d}(\bm{x})=\Phi_{0}(\bm{x})\bm{b}_{0,d}+\Phi_{r}(\bm{x})\bm{b}_{i,d},

and updates the shared and local posteriors by alternating the regressions in
Eqs. (
25
)–(
28
). Thus
f0_only
tests whether a single shared ODE is sufficient, while
indep_fields
tests whether sharing is necessary at all. At deployment time this distinction is also important:
f0_only
yields a population forecast but no personalization, whereas
indep_fields
can only personalize from the new subject’s own data.

Additional experimental details

C.1
Systems

We now list the benchmark systems used during the experiments of (Section
5.1
).
All synthetic benchmarks are autonomous first-order ODEs with full-state observations

\bm{y}_{i,n}=\bm{x}_{i}(t_{i,n})+\bm{\epsilon}_{i,n},\qquad\bm{\epsilon}_{i,n}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma_{y}^{2}\bm{I}_{D}),

where the observation times are irregular and obtained by sorting i.i.d. draws from

\mathcal{U}([0,T])
. Dense ground-truth trajectories are generated numerically on a fine grid.
In the experiments reported, unless stated otherwise in a dedicated ablation, we use the same simulation budget across systems:

M=30,\qquad N_{\mathrm{obs}}=20,\qquad\sigma_{y}=0.05.

Each subject has its own randomized initial condition and the full latent state is observed with additive Gaussian noise, except, again, in a dedicated ablation. Figure
S6
displays, for one representative seed, the population and patient trajectories together with the observation horizon, forecasting horizon, and interpolation window for all synthetic systems.

Linear oscillator (2D).

For
\bm{x}_{i}=(x_{1,i},x_{2,i})^{\top}
,

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{1,i}

\displaystyle=-\alpha_{i}x_{1,i}+\beta_{i}x_{2,i},

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{2,i}

\displaystyle=-\kappa_{i}x_{1,i}-\gamma_{i}x_{2,i}.

The population field uses
(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0},\kappa_{0},\gamma_{0})
, while
subject heterogeneity is introduced through

\alpha_{i}=\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{\mathrm{sd}}\xi_{i},\qquad\beta_{i}=\beta_{0}+\beta_{\mathrm{sd}}\zeta_{i},\qquad\kappa_{i}=\kappa_{0}+\kappa_{\mathrm{sd}}\rho_{i},\qquad\gamma_{i}=\gamma_{0}+\gamma_{\mathrm{sd}}\eta_{i},

with

\xi_{i},\zeta_{i},\rho_{i},\eta_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)

independently.

Lotka–Volterra (2D).

For prey
x_{1,i}
and predator
x_{2,i}
,

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{1,i}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{0}x_{1,i}-\beta_{i}x_{1,i}x_{2,i},

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{2,i}

\displaystyle=\delta_{i}x_{1,i}x_{2,i}-\gamma_{0}x_{2,i}.

The subject-specific random effects act on the interaction coefficients,

\beta_{i}=\beta_{0}\exp(\beta_{\mathrm{sd}}\xi_{i}),\qquad\delta_{i}=\delta_{0}\exp(\delta_{\mathrm{sd}}\zeta_{i}),\qquad\xi_{i},\zeta_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1).

Thus the population field is determined by

(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0},\delta_{0},\gamma_{0})
, while subject heterogeneity enters
through
(\beta_{i},\delta_{i})
.

Van der Pol (2D).

For
\bm{x}_{i}=(x_{1,i},x_{2,i})^{\top}
,

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{1,i}

\displaystyle=x_{2,i},

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{2,i}

\displaystyle=\mu_{i}(1-x_{1,i}^{2})x_{2,i}-x_{1,i}.

Subject variability is introduced through

\mu_{i}=\mu_{0}\bigl(1+\mu_{\mathrm{sd}}\xi_{i}\bigr),\qquad\xi_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1).

FitzHugh–Nagumo (2D).

For membrane potential
v_{i}
and recovery variable
w_{i}
,

\displaystyle\dot{v}_{i}

\displaystyle=s_{0}\!\left(v_{i}-\frac{v_{i}^{3}}{3}-c_{i}w_{i}+I_{0}\right),

\displaystyle\dot{w}_{i}

\displaystyle=s_{0}\frac{d_{i}v_{i}+a_{0}-b_{0}w_{i}}{\tau_{0}}.

The subject-specific random effects act on the coupling coefficients,

c_{i}=c_{0}+c_{\mathrm{sd}}\xi_{i},\qquad d_{i}=d_{0}+d_{\mathrm{sd}}\zeta_{i},\qquad\xi_{i},\zeta_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1).

One-compartment pharmacokinetics (2D).

With gastrointestinal amount
A_{\mathrm{GI},i}
and plasma concentration
U_{P,i}
,

\displaystyle\dot{A}_{\mathrm{GI},i}

\displaystyle=-k_{a,i}A_{\mathrm{GI},i},

\displaystyle\dot{U}_{P,i}

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{a,i}}{V_{i}}A_{\mathrm{GI},i}-k_{e,i}U_{P,i}.

Here heterogeneity is parametric and log-normal:

\log k_{a,i}=\log k_{a,0}+\sigma_{k_{a}}\xi_{i},\qquad\log k_{e,i}=\log k_{e,0}+\sigma_{k_{e}}\zeta_{i},\qquad\log V_{i}=\log V_{0}+\sigma_{V}\rho_{i},

with
\xi_{i},\zeta_{i},\rho_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.
The initial condition is fixed to a common oral dose,

A_{\mathrm{GI},i}(0)=\mathrm{dose}_{0}
and
U_{P,i}(0)=0
.

SIR (3D).

For susceptible, infectious, and removed states
(S_{i},I_{i},R_{i})
,

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{i}

\displaystyle=-\beta_{i}\frac{S_{i}I_{i}}{N_{i}},

\displaystyle\dot{I}_{i}

\displaystyle=\beta_{i}\frac{S_{i}I_{i}}{N_{i}}-\gamma_{i}I_{i},

\displaystyle\dot{R}_{i}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{i}I_{i},

where
N_{i}=S_{i}+I_{i}+R_{i}
. The subject-specific effects are log-normal:

\beta_{i}=\beta_{0}\exp(\sigma_{\beta}\xi_{i}),\qquad\gamma_{i}=\gamma_{0}\exp(\sigma_{\gamma}\zeta_{i}),\qquad\xi_{i},\zeta_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1).

Lotka–Volterra with GP-draw random effects (2D).

This benchmark keeps the population dynamics exactly Lotka–Volterra, but replaces parametric subject heterogeneity by smooth subject-specific residual vector fields:

\dot{\bm{x}}_{i}(t)=\bm{f}_{\mathrm{LV}}(\bm{x}_{i}(t);\theta_{0})+\bm{g}_{i}(\bm{x}_{i}(t)),\qquad\theta_{0}=(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0},\delta_{0},\gamma_{0}).

Each residual component is generated based on a bounded RBF/Nyström GP draw

g_{i,d}(\bm{x})=\sigma_{g}a(\bm{x})\tanh\!\left(B_{r}(\bm{x})^{\top}\bm{w}_{i,d}\right),\qquad\bm{w}_{i,d}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\bm{I}),\qquad d\in\{1,2\},

with residual scale
\sigma_{g}
. Here
B_{r}(\bm{x})=k_{r}(\bm{x},\mathcal{Z}_{r})L^{-\top}
, where
\mathcal{Z}_{r}
is a regular inducing grid,

K_{ZZ}+\epsilon I=LL^{\top}
, and
k_{r}
is an anisotropic RBF kernel with lengthscales
(\ell_{r,1},\ell_{r,2})
. The function

a(\bm{x})=\exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{x_{1}-1.0}{0.85}\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{x_{2}-0.9}{0.65}\right)^{2}\right]

localizes the residual around the typical Lotka–Volterra orbit, which stabilizes the oscillations by damping residual effects away from the observed state region. Thus the shared population field remains mechanistic, while subject deviations are flexible smooth random effects.

C.2
Lotka–Volterra with subject covariates (2D)

This example employed in Section
5.4
uses the same Lotka–Volterra dynamics as above, but the subject-specific interaction coefficients are driven by static covariates. Each subject has
\bm{c}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
with independent coordinates sampled uniformly from
[-1,1]
, and

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{1,i}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{0}x_{1,i}-\beta_{i}x_{1,i}x_{2,i},

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{2,i}

\displaystyle=\delta_{i}x_{1,i}x_{2,i}-\gamma_{0}x_{2,i},

with

\beta_{i}=\beta_{0}\exp(\bm{w}_{\beta}^{\top}\bm{c}_{i}+\sigma_{\mathrm{cov}}\xi_{i}),\qquad\delta_{i}=\delta_{0}\exp(\bm{w}_{\delta}^{\top}\bm{c}_{i}+\sigma_{\mathrm{cov}}\zeta_{i}),\qquad\xi_{i},\zeta_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1).

Thus, subjects with nearby covariates have similar interaction rates.

C.3
FHN Misspecification Experiment

We include a targeted misspecification experiment to evaluate whether function-space residuals can correct a plausible but incomplete mechanistic ODE. The benchmark starts from the two-dimensional FitzHugh–Nagumo skeleton,

\bm{f}_{\mathrm{FHN}}(v,w;\bm{\theta}),\qquad\bm{\theta}=(s,c,I,d,a,b,\tau),

but the true population vector field includes an additional smooth state-dependent correction,

\bm{f}_{0}^{\star}(\bm{x})=\bm{f}_{\mathrm{FHN}}(\bm{x};\bm{\theta}^{\star})+\bm{h}_{0}(\bm{x}),\qquad\bm{x}=(v,w).

The correction
\bm{h}_{0}
is a deterministic bounded RBF/Nyström residual, constructed similarly to the GP-draw random effects in Appendix
C
, that changes the shape and timing of the oscillations without inducing collapse or blow-up. Subject heterogeneity is then added through small smooth residual fields,

\bm{f}_{i}^{\star}(\bm{x})=\bm{f}_{0}^{\star}(\bm{x})+\bm{g}_{i}^{\star}(\bm{x}),

where each
\bm{g}_{i}^{\star}
is an independent bounded RBF/Nyström draw.

We compare three methods. First, the mechanistic SAEM baseline of Appendix
B.2
, which is deliberately doubly misspecified: it fits only a nonlinear mixed-effects FHN model, so it can adjust mechanistic population parameters and parametric random effects, but cannot represent either the shared correction
\bm{h}_{0}
or the nonparametric subject-specific residual fields
\bm{g}_{i}^{\star}
. Standard
MEGPODE
learns the fully nonparametric decomposition
\bm{f}_{i}=\bm{f}_{0}+\bm{g}_{i}
without a mechanistic mean. Finally,
MEGPODE-M
uses an FHN mean inside the shared field,

f_{0,d}(\bm{x})=f_{\mathrm{FHN},d}(\bm{x};\bm{\theta})+r_{0,d}(\bm{x}),

where
r_{0}
is a GP residual and the subject-specific fields
\bm{g}_{i}
are modeled as in the full method. After each smoothing step, the smoothed derivative pseudo-observations are regressed onto the FHN basis after subtracting the current GP contribution, yielding a weighted ridge least-squares update of
\bm{\theta}
with weights given by the collocation uncertainty.

D.1
Derivation of the local Gaussian pseudo-observation

We derive the Gaussian pseudo-observation used in Section
3.3
. Starting from the collocation residual in Eq. (
16
),

\mathbf{0}=\bm{H}_{\dot{x}}\bm{s}_{i,k}-\bm{f}_{i}\!\bigl(\bm{H}_{x}\bm{s}_{i,k}\bigr)+\bm{\epsilon}^{(\mathrm{coloc})}_{i,k},\qquad\bm{\epsilon}^{(\mathrm{coloc})}_{i,k}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\mathbf{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{coloc}}\bigr).

(S1)

We linearize the posterior-mean vector field around a reference point
\bar{\bm{x}}_{i,k}
:

\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\bigl[\bm{f}_{i}(\bm{H}_{x}\bm{s}_{i,k})\bigr]\approx\bm{\mu}^{f}_{i,k}+\bm{J}_{i,k}\bigl(\bm{H}_{x}\bm{s}_{i,k}-\bar{\bm{x}}_{i,k}\bigr),

(S2)

where

\bm{\mu}^{f}_{i,k}=\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\bigl[\bm{f}_{i}(\bar{\bm{x}}_{i,k})\bigr],\qquad\bm{J}_{i,k}=\nabla_{\bm{x}}\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\bigl[\bm{f}_{i}(\bm{x})\bigr]\Big|_{\bm{x}=\bar{\bm{x}}_{i,k}}.

(S3)

Substituting Eq. (
S2
) into Eq. (
S1
) gives

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}

\displaystyle\approx\bm{H}_{\dot{x}}\bm{s}_{i,k}-\Bigl(\bm{\mu}^{f}_{i,k}+\bm{J}_{i,k}\bigl(\bm{H}_{x}\bm{s}_{i,k}-\bar{\bm{x}}_{i,k}\bigr)\Bigr)+\bm{\epsilon}^{(\mathrm{coloc})}_{i,k}

\displaystyle=\bm{H}_{\dot{x}}\bm{s}_{i,k}-\bm{\mu}^{f}_{i,k}-\bm{J}_{i,k}\bm{H}_{x}\bm{s}_{i,k}+\bm{J}_{i,k}\bar{\bm{x}}_{i,k}+\bm{\epsilon}^{(\mathrm{coloc})}_{i,k}

\displaystyle=\bigl(\bm{H}_{\dot{x}}-\bm{J}_{i,k}\bm{H}_{x}\bigr)\bm{s}_{i,k}-\bigl(\bm{\mu}^{f}_{i,k}-\bm{J}_{i,k}\bar{\bm{x}}_{i,k}\bigr)+\bm{\epsilon}^{(\mathrm{coloc})}_{i,k}.

(S4)

Rearranging terms yields

\bm{\mu}^{f}_{i,k}-\bm{J}_{i,k}\bar{\bm{x}}_{i,k}=\bigl(\bm{H}_{\dot{x}}-\bm{J}_{i,k}\bm{H}_{x}\bigr)\bm{s}_{i,k}+\bm{\epsilon}^{(\mathrm{coloc})}_{i,k}.

(S5)

This is already in linear-Gaussian observation form. Defining

\bm{H}^{(\mathrm{coloc})}_{i,k}=\bm{H}_{\dot{x}}-\bm{J}_{i,k}\bm{H}_{x},\qquad\bm{c}^{(\mathrm{coloc})}_{i,k}=\bm{\mu}^{f}_{i,k}-\bm{J}_{i,k}\bar{\bm{x}}_{i,k},

(S6)

we obtain the pseudo-observation

\bm{c}^{(\mathrm{coloc})}_{i,k}=\bm{H}^{(\mathrm{coloc})}_{i,k}\bm{s}_{i,k}+\bm{\epsilon}_{i,k},\qquad\bm{\epsilon}_{i,k}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\mathbf{0},\bm{R}^{(\mathrm{coloc})}_{i,k}\bigr).

(S7)

Finally, the effective covariance is taken as

\bm{R}^{(\mathrm{coloc})}_{i,k}=\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{coloc}}+\bm{\Sigma}^{f}_{i,k},\qquad\bm{\Sigma}^{f}_{i,k}=\Var_{q}\!\bigl[\bm{f}_{i}(\bar{\bm{x}}_{i,k})\bigr],

(S8)

so that uncertainty in the local field evaluation is incorporated into the pseudo-observation noise.

Strictly speaking, the first-order expansion is applied to the posterior-mean field, while uncertainty in the field evaluation under
q
is propagated through the additive covariance term
\bm{\Sigma}^{f}_{i,k}
. The resulting construction should therefore be understood as a local Gaussian approximation to the random nonlinear term
\bm{f}_{i}(\bm{H}_{x}\bm{s}_{i,k})
, rather than as a deterministic plug-in of its posterior mean.

in Eq. (

\bm{s}_{i,k+1}=\bm{A}_{i,k}\bm{s}_{i,k}+\bm{q}_{i,k},\qquad\bm{q}_{i,k}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\bm{Q}_{i,k}),

(S9)

with
\bm{s}_{i,k+1}
from Eq. (

\tau_{i,k}

\bm{w}_{i,k}=\begin{cases}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{y}_{i,k}\\

\bm{c}^{(\mathrm{coloc})}_{i,k},&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(S10)

where
\bm{y}_{i,k}
is the physical observation from Eq. (

\bm{H}^{(\mathrm{smooth})}_{i,k}=\begin{cases}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{H}\bm{H}_{x}\\
\bm{H}^{(\mathrm{coloc})}_{i,k}\end{bmatrix},&\text{if }\tau_{i,k}\text{ is an observation time},\\[11.38109pt]
\bm{H}^{(\mathrm{coloc})}_{i,k},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(S11)

Here
\bm{H}\bm{H}_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{y}\times 2D}
maps the augmented state

\bm{s}_{i,k}
to the physical observation space, while

\bm{H}^{(\mathrm{coloc})}_{i,k}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times 2D}
is the linearized collocation operator from

).
where
\bm{H}
comes from the observation model in Eq. (

) and
\bm{H}^{(\mathrm{coloc})}_{i,k}
from Eq. (

\bm{S}_{i,k}^{\mathrm{obs}}=\begin{cases}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\Sigma}_{i,k}^{y}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\bm{R}^{(\mathrm{coloc})}_{i,k}\end{bmatrix},&\text{if }\tau_{i,k}\text{ is an observation time},\\[11.38109pt]
\bm{R}^{(\mathrm{coloc})}_{i,k},&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(S12)

where
\bm{\Sigma}_{i,k}^{y}

Then

\bm{w}_{i,k}=\bm{H}^{(\mathrm{smooth})}_{i,k}\bm{s}_{i,k}+\bm{r}_{i,k},\qquad\bm{r}_{i,k}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\bm{S}_{i,k}^{\mathrm{obs}}).

(S13)

D.3
Extrapolation for a trained subject

Suppose subject
i
is part of the training set and that, after training on
observations in
[0,T_{\mathrm{obs}}]
, we have learned the shared and
subject-specific posteriors

q(\bm{b}_{0,d})=\mathcal{N}(\bm{m}_{0,d},\bm{S}_{0,d}),\qquad q(\bm{b}_{i,d})=\mathcal{N}(\bm{m}_{i,d},\bm{S}_{i,d}),\qquad d=1,\dots,D.

To extrapolate this same subject to a longer horizon

T_{\mathrm{pred}}>T_{\mathrm{obs}}
, we freeze the learned field posterior and
recompute only the trajectory posterior on an extended collocation grid.

Let

\mathcal{C}_{i}^{\mathrm{pred}}=\{\tau_{i,0},\dots,\tau_{i,K_{i}^{\mathrm{pred}}}\}

be a collocation grid such that

\mathcal{C}_{i}^{\mathrm{obs}}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{i}^{\mathrm{pred}},\qquad\max\mathcal{C}_{i}^{\mathrm{pred}}=T_{\mathrm{pred}},

where
\mathcal{C}_{i}^{\mathrm{obs}}
is the grid used during training on

[0,T_{\mathrm{obs}}]
. On this extended grid we keep the original physical
observations on
[0,T_{\mathrm{obs}}]
and treat all later grid points as
unobserved.

The target of extrapolation is therefore the extended trajectory posterior

q\!\left(\bm{s}_{i,0:K_{i}^{\mathrm{pred}}}\,\middle|\,\bm{y}_{i}(0{:}T_{\mathrm{obs}}),\,q(\bm{b}_{0}),\,q(\bm{b}_{i})\right),

whose marginal over
\tau_{i,k}\in[T_{\mathrm{obs}},T_{\mathrm{pred}}]

yields the forecast.

Since the field posterior is fixed, extrapolation reuses the same
local-linearization and Kalman-smoothing construction as in Section
3.3
and
Appendix
D.2
, but on the enlarged grid

\mathcal{C}_{i}^{\mathrm{pred}}
. It is a
trajectory-only

inference step: the field is frozen, and the latent path is recomputed jointly
on
[0,T_{\mathrm{pred}}]
, with no physical observations beyond

T_{\mathrm{obs}}
. Thus, it is not a simple forward rollout from an
estimate at
T_{\mathrm{obs}}
, and enlarging the horizon may slightly modify the posterior even on the observed interval.

For
f0_only
, the same extrapolation procedure is used with

\bm{g}_{i}\equiv 0
, so prediction is driven only by the shared field. For

gi_only
, the shared component is absent and extrapolation uses only
the trained subject-specific field of subject
i
.

D.4
Adaptation and extrapolation for a new subject after training

Suppose training has produced shared posteriors

q(\bm{b}_{0,d})=\mathcal{N}(\bm{m}_{0,d},\bm{S}_{0,d}),\qquad d=1,\dots,D.

For a new subject
\star
, we keep the shared field fixed and infer only a new
local posterior
q(\bm{b}_{\star,d})
together with the subject trajectory
posterior on the observed window. Let

\mathcal{C}_{\star}^{\mathrm{obs}}=\{\tau_{\star,0},\dots,\tau_{\star,K_{\star}}\}

denote a collocation grid covering the available observations.

Adaptation reuses the same trajectory-space update of Section 3.3 and the same
pseudo-regression construction of Section 3.4, except that the shared posterior
is held fixed and only the subject-specific posterior is updated. For output
dimension
d
, the personalized pseudo-regression is

\bm{u}_{\star,d}=\bm{P}_{0,\star,d}\bm{b}_{0,d}+\bm{P}_{r,\star,d}\bm{b}_{\star,d}+\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{reg}}_{\star,d},\qquad\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{reg}}_{\star,d}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\bm{R}_{\star,d}),

(S17)

which yields the local natural-parameter update

\displaystyle\bm{\Lambda}_{\star,d}^{\mathrm{new}}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{r}\bm{I}_{l_{r}}+\bm{P}_{r,\star,d}^{\top}\bm{R}_{\star,d}^{-1}\bm{P}_{r,\star,d},

(S18)

\displaystyle\bm{\eta}_{\star,d}^{\mathrm{new}}

\displaystyle=\bm{P}_{r,\star,d}^{\top}\bm{R}_{\star,d}^{-1}\bigl(\bm{u}_{\star,d}-\bm{P}_{0,\star,d}\bm{m}_{0,d}\bigr).

(S19)

Thus adaptation is an alternating scheme in which the shared field remains
fixed, while the new subject trajectory and local field are refined jointly.

When
N_{\star}=0
, the procedure reduces to a population-only forecast by keeping

\bm{m}_{\star,d}=\mathbf{0}
. When only an early prefix is observed, the same
algorithm yields few-shot personalization.

For
f0_only
, the local update is omitted entirely. For

gi_only
, the shared component is absent and only the
subject-specific field is adapted.

D.5
Initial conditions

We treat the initial condition as a latent subject-specific quantity and place a
simple mixed-effect Gaussian prior on it. Let
\tau_{i,0}
denote the left
endpoint of the collocation grid for subject
i
(typically
\tau_{i,0}=0
), and
write

\bm{x}_{i,0}:=\bm{x}_{i}(\tau_{i,0}).

We model

\bm{x}_{i,0}=\bm{\mu}_{x_{0}}+\bm{a}_{i},\qquad\bm{a}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{x_{0}}),\qquad i=1,\dots,M.

(S20)

Thus
\bm{\mu}_{x_{0}}
is a population reference initial state and

\bm{\Sigma}_{x_{0}}
captures between-subject variability at the initial
time.

Initial prior in the augmented state-space model.

The smoother operates on the augmented state

\bm{s}_{i,k}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{x}_{i}(\tau_{i,k})\\
\dot{\bm{x}}_{i}(\tau_{i,k})\end{bmatrix}.

The mixed-effect initial-condition prior is applied only to the state block of

\bm{s}_{i,0}
, while the derivative block remains under the default temporal
prior. Concretely, the initial Gaussian prior has the form

\bm{s}_{i,0}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mu}_{x_{0}}\\
\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\Sigma}_{x_{0}}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\bm{P}_{\dot{x},0}\end{bmatrix}\right),

(S21)

where
\bm{P}_{\dot{x},0}
is the default prior covariance for the
initial derivative coordinates. Hence the model learns a population prior for

\bm{x}_{i,0}
, while
\dot{\bm{x}}_{i}(\tau_{i,0})
is inferred indirectly through the
observations and collocation constraints.

Empirical-Bayes initialization.

For simplicity, we describe the empirical initialization used in the fully observed
case, where
D_{y}=D
and
\bm{H}=\bm{I}_{D}
.
Because the observation times are irregular and need not include
\tau_{i,0}
,
we initialize the population initial-condition model from the earliest observed
state of each subject. Let
\bm{y}_{i,(1)}
denote the first observed state vector
for subject
i
. The initialization is

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{x_{0}}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\bm{y}_{i,(1)},

(S22)

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{x_{0}}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{x_{0}}\,\widehat{\Cov}\!\bigl(\bm{y}_{1,(1)},\dots,\bm{y}_{M,(1)}\bigr)+\epsilon_{x_{0}}\bm{I}_{D},

(S23)

where
\alpha_{x_{0}}>0
is a scale factor and
\epsilon_{x_{0}}>0
is a small
jitter term.
For partially observed settings with
D_{y}<D
, one must first map or impute the first
observations into the latent state space before forming

\bm{\mu}_{x_{0}}^{(0)}
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{x_{0}}^{(0)}
.
This is only an initialization heuristic: when the first
observation occurs after
\tau_{i,0}
, it is not itself the true initial state,
but it provides a reasonable empirical anchor from which the smoother can refine

\bm{x}_{i,0}
.

Update after each smoothing step.

After smoothing all subjects with the current field posterior, we extract the
smoothed marginal at the first grid point,

q(\bm{x}_{i,0})=\mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\bm{m}_{i,0}^{x},\bm{P}_{i,0}^{xx}\bigr),

and update the population initial-condition parameters by moment matching:

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{x_{0}}^{\mathrm{new}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\bm{m}_{i,0}^{x},

(S24)

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{x_{0}}^{\mathrm{new}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\left[\bm{P}_{i,0}^{xx}+\bigl(\bm{m}_{i,0}^{x}-\bm{\mu}_{x_{0}}^{\mathrm{new}}\bigr)\bigl(\bm{m}_{i,0}^{x}-\bm{\mu}_{x_{0}}^{\mathrm{new}}\bigr)^{\top}\right]+\bm{\epsilon}_{x_{0}}\bm{I}_{D}.

(S25)

Hence the initial-condition layer is updated in an EM-like empirical-Bayes
fashion: smoothing provides posterior moments for the subject-specific initial
states, and these are pooled to refresh the population-level prior before the
next outer iteration.

Initialization of the trajectory linearization.

The current trajectory linearization is initialized from an observation-only
Gaussian smoothing pass under the current initial-condition prior. If

q^{(0)}(\bm{s}_{i,0:K_{i}})

denotes this initial observation-only posterior, then the initial reference trajectory
used by the collocation linearization is

\bar{\bm{x}}_{i,k}^{(0)}:=\mathbb{E}_{q^{(0)}}[\bm{x}_{i}(\tau_{i,k})].

(S26)

Thus the initial-condition prior influences the outer linearization through the
initial observation-driven smoothing step.

Population mean versus subject-specific initial conditions.

This construction yields two distinct quantities.

First, the
population reference initial condition
is the learned mean
\bm{x}_{0}^{\mathrm{pop}}:=\bm{\mu}_{x_{0}}
.
This is the natural initial state for propagating the shared population field
and obtaining a population mean trajectory.

Second, for a given subject
i
, the relevant initial-condition summary after
training is the subject-specific smoothed posterior

q(\bm{x}_{i,0})=\mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\bm{m}_{i,0}^{x},\bm{P}_{i,0}^{xx}\bigr).

Thus the population prior regularizes the estimation of
\bm{x}_{i,0}
, but the
final subject-level initial condition remains individualized by the data and the
inferred subject-specific dynamics.

Baselines.

The same initial-condition mechanism is used for the full mixed-effect model and
for the two ablations
f0_only
and
gi_only
. The differences
between methods only concern the vector-field layer.

D.6
Hyperparameter learning

We learn hyperparameters via empirical-Bayes updates after the current smoothing pass. Recall that the smoothed trajectories define pseudo-regression targets for the vector field (Equation
24
),

\bm{u}_{i,d}=\bm{P}_{0,i,d}\bm{b}_{0,d}+\bm{P}_{r,i,d}\bm{b}_{i,d}+\bm{\varepsilon}_{i,d},\qquad\bm{\varepsilon}_{i,d}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\bm{R}_{i,d}).

The kernel hyperparameters and field output scales, denoted collectively by
\theta
, are chosen by minimizing the marginal negative log likelihood of these pseudo-data after integrating out the Gaussian field weights:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{field}}(\bm{\theta})=-\sum_{d=1}^{D}\log\int p_{\bm{\theta}}\!\left(\{\bm{u}_{i,d}\}_{i}\mid\bm{b}_{0,d},\{\bm{b}_{i,d}\}_{i}\right)p(\bm{b}_{0,d})\prod_{i}p_{\lambda_{r}}(\bm{b}_{i,d})\,d\bm{b}_{0,d}\prod_{i}d\bm{b}_{i,d}.

(S27)

The observation noise is updated from the smoothing posterior moments. If

\bm{m}_{i,k}
and
\bm{S}_{i,k}
are the smoothed mean and covariance at an observed time, then

\widehat{\sigma}_{y}^{2}=\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{obs}}}\sum_{(i,k,a)\in\mathcal{O}}\left[\left(y_{i,k,a}-(\bm{H}\bm{m}_{i,k})_{a}\right)^{2}+(\bm{H}\bm{S}_{i,k}\bm{H}^{\top})_{aa}\right].

(S28)

Similarly, writing the IWP transition covariance as

\bm{Q}(h)=\sigma_{\mathrm{IWP}}^{2}\bm{Q}_{0}(h)
, the process scale is updated by

\widehat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{IWP}}^{2}=\frac{1}{N_{q}}\sum_{i,k}\tr\!\left[\bm{Q}_{0}(h_{k})^{-1}\mathbb{E}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}_{i,k}\bm{\epsilon}_{i,k}^{\top}\right)\right],\qquad\bm{\epsilon}_{i,k}=\bm{s}_{i,k+1}-\bm{A}(h_{k})\bm{s}_{i,k}.

(S29)

Finally, the residual precision
\lambda_{r}
is updated from the posterior moments of the subject-specific field weights. With Gamma prior parameters
(a_{\lambda},b_{\lambda})
,

\widehat{\lambda}_{r}=\frac{a_{\lambda}-1+\frac{1}{2}MDl_{r}}{b_{\lambda}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,d}\left(\|\mathbb{E}[\bm{b}_{i,d}]\|^{2}+\tr\Var[\bm{b}_{i,d}]\right)}.

(S30)

D.7
Field-space updates and conservative shared-field variants

The main-text update uses a plug-in approximation in which the vector field is regressed on smoothed state means. For vector-valued states, one may either retain the full block covariance of the pseudo-derivatives or use a diagonal approximation separately for each output dimension. The latter leads to the regression form in Eq. (
24
).

Two conservative variants are useful when updating the shared field. In the
diagonal-inflated
update, the noise covariance for the shared-field regression is augmented by the posterior variance of the local field evaluated at the smoothed inputs:

\bm{R}_{i,d}^{\mathrm{infl}}=\bm{R}_{i,d}+\diag\!\bigl(\Var_{q}[g_{i,d}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{i,0})],\dots,\Var_{q}[g_{i,d}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{i,K_{i}})]\bigr).

(S31)

This discourages the population field from explaining variability that could plausibly be attributed to an uncertain local effect.

In the
posterior-collapsed
update, the shared-field
regression accounts for uncertainty in the current local variational posterior
by inflating the pseudo-regression covariance:

\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{coll}}_{i,d}=\bm{R}_{i,d}+\bm{P}_{r,i,d}\bm{S}_{i,d}\bm{P}_{r,i,d}^{\top}.

The shared-field candidate update becomes

\displaystyle\bm{\Lambda}_{0,d}^{\mathrm{new,coll}}

\displaystyle=\bm{I}_{l_{0}}+\sum_{i=1}^{M}\bm{P}_{0,i,d}^{\top}\bigl(\bm{\Sigma}_{i,d}^{\mathrm{coll}}\bigr)^{-1}\bm{P}_{0,i,d},

(S32)

\displaystyle\bm{\eta}_{0,d}^{\mathrm{new,coll}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\bm{P}_{0,i,d}^{\top}\bigl(\bm{\Sigma}_{i,d}^{\mathrm{coll}}\bigr)^{-1}\bigl(\bm{u}_{i,d}-\bm{P}_{r,i,d}\bm{m}_{i,d}\bigr).

(S33)

Hence uncertainty in the local field is propagated into the shared-field update
through the inflated covariance

\bm{\Sigma}_{i,d}^{\mathrm{coll}}
, making this update more
conservative when estimating the population field. We used the collapsed updates in the experiments.

D.8
Population trajectory uncertainty via Monte Carlo pushforward

A derived quantity of interest is the population trajectory obtained by integrating
the shared population field from a reference initial condition. In the current
implementation, uncertainty over this trajectory is approximated by Monte Carlo
pushforward.

Concretely, we draw samples from the learned uncertainty in the population-level
dynamics and propagate each sample through the ODE to obtain a collection of
population trajectories. Pointwise posterior summaries, such as means, intervals,
NLPD, and CRPS, are then computed from this ensemble of simulated trajectories.

D.9
Static subject covariates

For static covariates
\bm{c}_{i}
, a convenient extension is to keep the state-space inducing features
\Phi_{r}(\bm{x})
and to correlate the subject-specific coefficient vectors across subjects via a covariate kernel. For each output dimension
d
, stack the local coefficients as

\bm{\beta}_{d}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{b}_{1,d}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{b}_{M,d}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{Ml_{r}},\qquad[K_{c}]_{ij}=k_{c}(c_{i},c_{j}),

and replace the independent prior in Eq. (
13
) by

\bm{\beta}_{d}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\mathbf{0},K_{c}\otimes\lambda_{r}^{-1}\bm{I}_{l_{r}}\bigr).

Equivalently,

\Cov(\bm{b}_{i,d},\bm{b}_{j,d})=k_{c}(\bm{c}_{i},\bm{c}_{j})\,\lambda_{r}^{-1}\bm{I}_{l_{r}}.

Retaining
g_{i,d}(\bm{x})=\Phi_{r}(\bm{x})\bm{b}_{i,d}
, we then have

\Cov\bigl[g_{i,d}(\bm{x}),g_{j,d}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\bigr]=\lambda_{r}^{-1}k_{c}(\bm{c}_{i},\bm{c}_{j})\,\Phi_{r}(\bm{x})\Phi_{r}(\bm{x}^{\prime})^{\top},

which is precisely the finite-rank approximation of the product kernel on augmented inputs,

k_{\mathrm{aug}}\bigl((\bm{x},c_{i}),(\bm{x}^{\prime},c_{j})\bigr)=k_{r}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})\,k_{c}(\bm{c}_{i},\bm{c}_{j}).

This is usually preferable to introducing subject-specific kernels
k_{r_{i}}
, because
k_{r_{i}}
changes each subject marginally but does not by itself encode how two subjects with similar covariates should borrow strength. The covariate Gram matrix
K_{c}
is static, so it can be precomputed once, and no inducing locations in covariate space are required. Prediction for a new subject with covariates
\bm{c}_{\star}
only requires the cross-covariance vector
\bigl(k_{c}(\bm{c}_{\star},\bm{c}_{1}),\dots,k_{c}(\bm{c}_{\star},\bm{c}_{M})\bigr)
.

If the covariates are mixed continuous/discrete,
k_{c}
can itself be a product or sum of kernels over the continuous and discrete parts.

E.1
Posterior-mean dynamics: well-posedness and trajectory-level deviation bounds

Recall that the posterior-mean shared and subject-specific fields are

\bar{f}_{0,d}(\bm{x})=\Phi_{0}(\bm{x})\bm{m}_{0,d},\qquad\bar{g}_{i,d}(\bm{x})=\Phi_{r}(\bm{x})\bm{m}_{i,d},\qquad\bar{f}_{i,d}(\bm{x})=\bar{f}_{0,d}(\bm{x})+\bar{g}_{i,d}(\bm{x}),

(S34)

and define the corresponding vector fields

\bar{\bm{f}}_{0}(\bm{x})=\bigl[\bar{f}_{0,d}(\bm{x})\bigr]_{d=1}^{D},\qquad\bar{\bm{g}}_{i}(\bm{x})=\bigl[\bar{g}_{i,d}(\bm{x})\bigr]_{d=1}^{D},\qquad\bar{\bm{f}}_{i}(\bm{x})=\bar{\bm{f}}_{0}(\bm{x})+\bar{\bm{g}}_{i}(\bm{x}).

(S35)

These are the deterministic fields obtained by integrating posterior means in the main text when simulating population and subject-level trajectories.

Proposition 1

(Well-posedness of posterior-mean dynamics)
.

Assume that the feature maps
\Phi_{0}
and
\Phi_{r}
are continuously differentiable on an open domain
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
. Then
\bar{\bm{f}}_{0}
,
\bar{\bm{g}}_{i}
, and
\bar{\bm{f}}_{i}
are continuously differentiable on
\mathcal{D}
and hence locally Lipschitz. Consequently, for any initial condition
\bm{x}^{\star}\in\mathcal{D}
, the ODEs

\dot{\bm{x}}(t)=\bar{\bm{f}}_{0}(\bm{x}(t)),\qquad\dot{\bm{x}}(t)=\bar{\bm{f}}_{i}(\bm{x}(t))

(S36)

admit unique maximal solutions. In particular, integrating posterior-mean fields to obtain population or subject-level trajectory summaries is mathematically well-defined on any time interval on which the solution remains in
\mathcal{D}
.

Proof.

Each coordinate of
\bar{\bm{f}}_{0}
is of the form
\bm{x}\mapsto\Phi_{0}(\bm{x})\bm{m}_{0,d}
, and each coordinate of
\bar{\bm{g}}_{i}
is of the form
\bm{x}\mapsto\Phi_{r}(\bm{x})\bm{m}_{i,d}
. Since
\Phi_{0}
and
\Phi_{r}
are
C^{1}
, so are these coordinate functions, and therefore
\bar{\bm{f}}_{0}
,
\bar{\bm{g}}_{i}
, and
\bar{\bm{f}}_{i}
are
C^{1}
. Every
C^{1}
map is locally Lipschitz, so the standard Picard–Lindelöf theorem yields unique maximal solutions for the corresponding ODEs.
∎

The next result quantifies how the norm of the subject-specific residual field controls the departure between a subject trajectory and the corresponding population trajectory.

Proposition 2

(Trajectory deviation controlled by the local field)
.

Let
\bar{\bm{x}}_{0}:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{D}
solve

\dot{\bar{\bm{x}}}_{0}(t)=\bar{\bm{f}}_{0}(\bar{\bm{x}}_{0}(t)),\qquad\bar{\bm{x}}_{0}(0)=\bm{x}_{0}^{\star},

(S37)

and let
\bar{\bm{x}}_{i}:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{D}
solve

\dot{\bar{\bm{x}}}_{i}(t)=\bar{\bm{f}}_{i}(\bar{\bm{x}}_{i}(t))=\bar{\bm{f}}_{0}(\bar{\bm{x}}_{i}(t))+\bar{\bm{g}}_{i}(\bar{\bm{x}}_{i}(t)),\qquad\bar{\bm{x}}_{i}(0)=\bm{x}_{i}^{\star}.

(S38)

Assume that both trajectories remain in a compact set
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathcal{D}
on which
\bar{\bm{f}}_{0}
is
L
-Lipschitz, and define

\delta_{i}\coloneqq\sup_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{K}}\|\bar{\bm{g}}_{i}(\bm{x})\|.

(S39)

Then, for every
t\in[0,T]
,

\|\bar{\bm{x}}_{i}(t)-\bar{\bm{x}}_{0}(t)\|\leq e^{Lt}\|\bm{x}_{i}^{\star}-\bm{x}_{0}^{\star}\|+\int_{0}^{t}e^{L(t-s)}\|\bar{\bm{g}}_{i}(\bar{\bm{x}}_{i}(s))\|\,ds\leq e^{Lt}\|\bm{x}_{i}^{\star}-\bm{x}_{0}^{\star}\|+\frac{\delta_{i}}{L}(e^{Lt}-1)

(S40)

when
L>0
, with the usual limit
\|\bm{x}_{i}^{\star}-\bm{x}_{0}^{\star}\|+\delta_{i}t
when
L=0
. In particular, if
\bm{x}_{i}^{\star}=\bm{x}_{0}^{\star}
, then

\|\bar{\bm{x}}_{i}(t)-\bar{\bm{x}}_{0}(t)\|\leq\frac{\delta_{i}}{L}(e^{Lt}-1).

(S41)

Proof.

Let
\Delta_{i}(t)=\bar{\bm{x}}_{i}(t)-\bar{\bm{x}}_{0}(t)
. Then

\displaystyle\dot{\Delta}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\bar{\bm{f}}_{0}(\bar{\bm{x}}_{i}(t))-\bar{\bm{f}}_{0}(\bar{\bm{x}}_{0}(t))+\bar{\bm{g}}_{i}(\bar{\bm{x}}_{i}(t)).

(S42)

Taking norms and using the Lipschitz property of
\bar{\bm{f}}_{0}
gives

\frac{d}{dt}\|\Delta_{i}(t)\|\leq L\|\Delta_{i}(t)\|+\|\bar{\bm{g}}_{i}(\bar{\bm{x}}_{i}(t))\|.

(S43)

Applying Grönwall’s inequality yields

\|\Delta_{i}(t)\|\leq e^{Lt}\|\Delta_{i}(0)\|+\int_{0}^{t}e^{L(t-s)}\|\bar{\bm{g}}_{i}(\bar{\bm{x}}_{i}(s))\|\,ds,

(S44)

and the uniform bound by
\delta_{i}
gives Eq. (
S40
).
∎

Propositions
1
and
2
justify the common practice of integrating posterior-mean shared and subject-specific fields to summarize population and patient-level trajectories. In particular, the second result shows that the magnitude of the learned local field directly controls how far subject-specific posterior-mean trajectories can depart from the population one.

E.2
Hierarchical field covariance and predictive uncertainty decompositions

The next statements are exact consequences of the hierarchical function-space decomposition used by the model. They do not depend on the collocation approximation or on the alternating inference scheme.

Proposition 3

(Cross-subject covariance and same-input cross-subject correlation)
.

Fix an output dimension
d
. Under the prior field decomposition

f_{i,d}(\bm{x})=\Phi_{0}(\bm{x})\bm{b}_{0,d}+\Phi_{r}(\bm{x})\bm{b}_{i,d},\qquad\bm{b}_{0,d}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\bm{I}_{l_{0}}),\qquad\bm{b}_{i,d}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\lambda_{r}^{-1}\bm{I}_{l_{r}}),

(S45)

with all coefficients independent across subjects and output dimensions, define

\kappa_{0}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})\coloneqq\Phi_{0}(\bm{x})\Phi_{0}(\bm{x}^{\prime})^{\top},\qquad\kappa_{r}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})\coloneqq\lambda_{r}^{-1}\Phi_{r}(\bm{x})\Phi_{r}(\bm{x}^{\prime})^{\top}.

(S46)

Then for any subjects
i,j
, outputs
d,d^{\prime}
, and inputs
\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime}
,

\Cov\!\bigl(f_{i,d}(\bm{x}),f_{j,d^{\prime}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\bigr)=\mathbb{I}\{d=d^{\prime}\}\Bigl(\kappa_{0}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})+\mathbb{I}\{i=j\}\kappa_{r}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})\Bigr).

(S47)

In particular, for
i\neq j
and provided the denominator is positive,

\mathrm{Corr}\!\bigl(f_{i,d}(\bm{x}),f_{j,d}(\bm{x})\bigr)=\frac{\kappa_{0}(\bm{x},\bm{x})}{\kappa_{0}(\bm{x},\bm{x})+\kappa_{r}(\bm{x},\bm{x})}=\frac{\Phi_{0}(\bm{x})\Phi_{0}(\bm{x})^{\top}}{\Phi_{0}(\bm{x})\Phi_{0}(\bm{x})^{\top}+\lambda_{r}^{-1}\Phi_{r}(\bm{x})\Phi_{r}(\bm{x})^{\top}}.

(S48)

Proof.

Because all coefficient priors are centered,
\mathbb{E}[f_{i,d}(\bm{x})]=0
, so

\Cov\!\bigl(f_{i,d}(\bm{x}),f_{j,d^{\prime}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\bigr)=\mathbb{E}\!\bigl[f_{i,d}(\bm{x})f_{j,d^{\prime}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\bigr].

(S49)

Expanding both fields gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\bigl[f_{i,d}(\bm{x})f_{j,d^{\prime}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\Big[\bigl(\Phi_{0}(\bm{x})\bm{b}_{0,d}+\Phi_{r}(\bm{x})\bm{b}_{i,d}\bigr)\bigl(\Phi_{0}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\bm{b}_{0,d^{\prime}}+\Phi_{r}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\bm{b}_{j,d^{\prime}}\bigr)\Big]

\displaystyle=\Phi_{0}(\bm{x})\,\mathbb{E}[\bm{b}_{0,d}\bm{b}_{0,d^{\prime}}^{\top}]\,\Phi_{0}(\bm{x}^{\prime})^{\top}+\Phi_{r}(\bm{x})\,\mathbb{E}[\bm{b}_{i,d}\bm{b}_{j,d^{\prime}}^{\top}]\,\Phi_{r}(\bm{x}^{\prime})^{\top},

(S50)

because the cross terms vanish by independence and zero mean. Since the shared coefficients are independent across outputs,

\mathbb{E}[\bm{b}_{0,d}\bm{b}_{0,d^{\prime}}^{\top}]=\mathbb{I}\{d=d^{\prime}\}\bm{I}_{l_{0}}.

(S51)

Likewise, for the subject-specific coefficients,

\mathbb{E}[\bm{b}_{i,d}\bm{b}_{j,d^{\prime}}^{\top}]=\mathbb{I}\{d=d^{\prime}\}\mathbb{I}\{i=j\}\lambda_{r}^{-1}\bm{I}_{l_{r}}.

(S52)

Substituting these identities yields Eq. (
S47
). For
i\neq j
and common output
d
, the covariance at the same input is
\kappa_{0}(\bm{x},\bm{x})
, whereas the marginal variance of each subject field is

\Var\!\bigl(f_{i,d}(\bm{x})\bigr)=\kappa_{0}(\bm{x},\bm{x})+\kappa_{r}(\bm{x},\bm{x}).

(S53)

Dividing covariance by the geometric mean of these equal marginal variances gives Eq. (
S48
).
∎

Equation (
S48
) provides a direct interpretation of the mixed-effect hierarchy: at any fixed state
\bm{x}
, cross-subject correlation is entirely due to the shared field and is damped by the size of the local residual variance.

Proposition 4

(Predictive uncertainty decomposition in field space)
.

Under the factorized Gaussian posterior

q(\bm{b}_{0,d})=\mathcal{N}(\bm{m}_{0,d},\bm{S}_{0,d}),\qquad q(\bm{b}_{i,d})=\mathcal{N}(\bm{m}_{i,d},\bm{S}_{i,d}),

(S54)

the latent field value
f_{i,d}(\bm{x})
satisfies

f_{i,d}(\bm{x})\mid q\sim\mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\mu_{i,d}^{f}(\bm{x}),v{i,d}^{2,f}(\bm{x})\bigr),

(S55)

with

\displaystyle\mu_{i,d}^{f}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\Phi_{0}(\bm{x})\bm{m}_{0,d}+\Phi_{r}(\bm{x})\bm{m}_{i,d},

(S56)

\displaystyle v_{i,d}^{f}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=v^{\mathrm{pop}}_{i,d}(\bm{x})+v^{\mathrm{loc}}_{i,d}(\bm{x}),

(S57)

where

v^{\mathrm{pop}}_{i,d}(\bm{x})\coloneqq\Phi_{0}(\bm{x})\bm{S}_{0,d}\Phi_{0}(\bm{x})^{\top},\qquad v^{\mathrm{loc}}_{i,d}(\bm{x})\coloneqq\Phi_{r}(\bm{x})\bm{S}_{i,d}\Phi_{r}(\bm{x})^{\top}.

(S58)

A natural pointwise diagnostic is the local uncertainty fraction

\rho^{\mathrm{loc}}_{i,d}(\bm{x})\coloneqq\frac{v^{\mathrm{loc}}_{i,d}(\bm{x})}{v^{\mathrm{pop}}_{i,d}(\bm{x})+v^{\mathrm{loc}}_{i,d}(\bm{x})},\qquad\rho^{\mathrm{pop}}_{i,d}(\bm{x})=1-\rho^{\mathrm{loc}}_{i,d}(\bm{x}).

(S59)

Proof.

The field value is an affine function of two independent Gaussian coefficient vectors,

f_{i,d}(\bm{x})=\Phi_{0}(\bm{x})\bm{b}_{0,d}+\Phi_{r}(\bm{x})\bm{b}_{i,d},

(S60)

so it is Gaussian under
q
. Its mean is obtained by linearity of expectation, its variance is the sum of the two quadratic forms because the posterior is factorized between shared and local coefficients.
∎

The additive variance decomposition in Proposition
4
is exact at fixed state inputs. After pushing uncertainty through the nonlinear ODE solution map, the analogous decomposition is no longer additive in general, but an exact law-of-total-variance identity remains available.

Proposition 5

(Exact trajectory-space variance decomposition)
.

For each subject
i
, let

\bm{B}_{0}\coloneqq\bigl(\bm{b}_{0,1}^{\top},\dots,\bm{b}_{0,D}^{\top}\bigr)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{Dl_{0}},\qquad\bm{B}_{i}\coloneqq\bigl(\bm{b}_{i,1}^{\top},\dots,\bm{b}_{i,D}^{\top}\bigr)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{Dl_{r}},

(S61)

and let
\bm{x}_{i}(\cdot;\bm{B}_{0},\bm{B}_{i},\bm{x}_{i}^{\star})
denote the trajectory obtained by integrating the subject-specific field from initial condition
\bm{x}_{i}^{\star}
. For any square-integrable trajectory functional

\mathcal{H}_{i}=\mathcal{H}\!\bigl(\bm{x}_{i}(\cdot;\bm{B}_{0},\bm{B}_{i},\bm{x}_{i}^{\star})\bigr),

the posterior variance satisfies

\Var_{q}(\mathcal{H}_{i})=\Var_{q(\bm{B}_{0})}\!\Big(\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{B}_{i})}[\mathcal{H}_{i}\mid\bm{B}_{0}]\Big)+\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{B}_{0})}\!\Big[\Var_{q(\bm{B}_{i})}(\mathcal{H}_{i}\mid\bm{B}_{0})\Big].

(S62)

A natural trajectory-level analogue of Eq. (
S59
) is therefore

\rho^{\mathrm{loc}}_{i}[\mathcal{H}]\coloneqq\frac{\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{B}_{0})}\!\big[\Var_{q(\bm{B}_{i})}(\mathcal{H}_{i}\mid\bm{B}_{0})\big]}{\Var_{q}(\mathcal{H}_{i})},\qquad\rho^{\mathrm{pop}}_{i}[\mathcal{H}]=1-\rho^{\mathrm{loc}}_{i}[\mathcal{H}].

(S63)

This applies in particular to pointwise trajectory coordinates
\mathcal{H}_{i}=e_{a}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}(t)
and final-state summaries.

Proof.

Equation (
S62
) is the law of total variance with conditioning variable
\bm{B}_{0}
and inner randomness coming from
\bm{B}_{i}
.
∎

For computation, it is useful to complement the exact identity above with a first-order pushforward approximation. Let

\bm{M}_{0}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{q}[\bm{B}_{0}],\qquad\bm{M}_{i}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{q}[\bm{B}_{i}],

(S64)

and let

\bm{\Sigma}_{B_{0}}\coloneqq\operatorname{blockdiag}(\bm{S}_{0,1},\dots,\bm{S}_{0,D})\in\mathbb{R}^{Dl_{0}\times Dl_{0}},~\bm{\Sigma}_{B_{i}}\coloneqq\operatorname{blockdiag}(\bm{S}_{i,1},\dots,\bm{S}_{i,D})\in\mathbb{R}^{Dl_{r}\times Dl_{r}}.

(S65)

For a fixed time
t
and coordinate
a
, define the solution map

\psi_{i,a,t}:\mathbb{R}^{Dl_{0}}\times\mathbb{R}^{Dl_{r}}\to\mathbb{R},\qquad\psi_{i,a,t}(\bm{B}_{0},\bm{B}_{i})\coloneqq e_{a}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}(t;\bm{B}_{0},\bm{B}_{i},\bm{x}_{i}^{\star}),

(S66)

and its sensitivity rows at the posterior mean,

\bm{J}_{0,i,a}(t)\coloneqq\nabla_{\bm{B}_{0}}\psi_{i,a,t}(\bm{M}_{0},\bm{M}_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times Dl_{0}},\qquad\bm{J}_{r,i,a}(t)\coloneqq\nabla_{\bm{B}_{i}}\psi_{i,a,t}(\bm{M}_{0},\bm{M}_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times Dl_{r}}.

(S67)

Then the first-order approximation

\psi_{i,a,t}(\bm{B}_{0},\bm{B}_{i})\approx\psi_{i,a,t}(\bm{M}_{0},\bm{M}_{i})+\bm{J}_{0,i,a}(t)(\bm{B}_{0}-\bm{M}_{0})+\bm{J}_{r,i,a}(t)(\bm{B}_{i}-\bm{M}_{i})

(S68)

yields the additive variance approximation

\Var_{q}\!\bigl(e_{a}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}(t)\bigr)\approx\bm{J}_{0,i,a}(t)\bm{\Sigma}_{B_{0}}\bm{J}_{0,i,a}(t)^{\top}+\bm{J}_{r,i,a}(t)\bm{\Sigma}_{B_{i}}\bm{J}_{r,i,a}(t)^{\top}.

(S69)

This gives a computable pointwise trajectory analogue of Eq. (
S59
):

\rho^{\mathrm{loc}}_{i,a}(t)\approx\frac{\bm{J}_{r,i,a}(t)\bm{\Sigma}_{B_{i}}\bm{J}_{r,i,a}(t)^{\top}}{\bm{J}_{0,i,a}(t)\bm{\Sigma}_{B_{0}}\bm{J}_{0,i,a}(t)^{\top}+\bm{J}_{r,i,a}(t)\bm{\Sigma}_{B_{i}}\bm{J}_{r,i,a}(t)^{\top}}.

(S70)

E.3
The conditional field update as weighted mixed ridge regression

The remaining statements are exact for the Gaussian field subproblem obtained once the current smoothed pseudo-derivatives are fixed. In that regime, the field update is exactly a weighted mixed ridge regression.

Fix an output dimension
d
, and consider the pseudo-regression model of Section
3.4
,

\bm{u}_{i,d}=\bm{P}_{0,i,d}\bm{b}_{0,d}+\bm{P}_{r,i,d}\bm{b}_{i,d}+\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{reg}}_{i,d},\qquad\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{reg}}_{i,d}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\bm{R}_{i,d}).

(S71)

Introduce the stacked coefficient vector

\bm{\beta}_{d}\coloneqq\bigl(\bm{b}_{0,d}^{\top},\bm{b}_{1,d}^{\top},\dots,\bm{b}_{M,d}^{\top}\bigr)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{l_{0}+Ml_{r}}.

(S72)

and the block-diagonal prior precision

\bm{\Omega}_{d}\coloneqq\operatorname{blockdiag}\bigl(\bm{I}_{l_{0}},\lambda_{r}\bm{I}_{l_{r}},\dots,\lambda_{r}\bm{I}_{l_{r}}\bigr)\in\mathbb{R}^{(l_{0}+Ml_{r})\times(l_{0}+Ml_{r})}.

(S73)

For each subject
i
, let
\bm{A}_{i,d}
denote the block row that injects
\bm{P}_{0,i,d}
in the shared block and
\bm{P}_{r,i,d}
in the
i
th local block:

\bm{A}_{i,d}=\bigl[\bm{P}_{0,i,d}\;\;\mathbf{0}\;\cdots\;\mathbf{0}\;\;\bm{P}_{r,i,d}\;\;\mathbf{0}\;\cdots\;\mathbf{0}\bigr]\in\mathbb{R}^{(K_{i}+1)\times(l_{0}+Ml_{r})}.

(S74)

Then the joint pseudo-regression model reads

\bm{u}_{i,d}=\bm{A}_{i,d}\bm{\beta}_{d}+\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{reg}}_{i,d}.

(S75)

Proposition 6

(Exact weighted mixed-ridge form)
.

Conditionally on the smoothed pseudo-data, the joint posterior over
\bm{\beta}_{d}
is Gaussian and satisfies

p(\bm{\beta}_{d}\mid\{\bm{u}_{i,d}\}_{i=1}^{M})\propto\exp\!\bigl(-\mathcal{J}_{d}(\bm{\beta}_{d})\bigr),

(S76)

where, up to an additive constant,

\mathcal{J}_{d}(\bm{\beta}_{d})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\left\|\bm{R}_{i,d}^{-1/2}\bigl(\bm{u}_{i,d}-\bm{A}_{i,d}\bm{\beta}_{d}\bigr)\right\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{\beta}_{d}^{\top}\bm{\Omega}_{d}\bm{\beta}_{d}.

(S77)

Equivalently, in block form,

\mathcal{J}_{d}(\bm{b}_{0,d},\{\bm{b}_{i,d}\})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\left\|\bm{R}_{i,d}^{-1/2}\bigl(\bm{u}_{i,d}-\bm{P}_{0,i,d}\bm{b}_{0,d}-\bm{P}_{r,i,d}\bm{b}_{i,d}\bigr)\right\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{b}_{0,d}\|^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{r}}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\|\bm{b}_{i,d}\|^{2}.

(S78)

Thus the field update is exactly a weighted mixed ridge regression in feature space.

Proof.

The pseudo-likelihood is Gaussian:

p(\{\bm{u}_{i,d}\}\mid\bm{\beta}_{d})\propto\prod_{i=1}^{M}\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\bm{u}_{i,d}-\bm{A}_{i,d}\bm{\beta}_{d}\bigr)^{\top}\bm{R}_{i,d}^{-1}\bigl(\bm{u}_{i,d}-\bm{A}_{i,d}\bm{\beta}_{d}\bigr)\right).

(S79)

The Gaussian priors on the shared and local coefficients contribute the quadratic penalty

p(\bm{\beta}_{d})\propto\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\beta}_{d}^{\top}\bm{\Omega}_{d}\bm{\beta}_{d}\right).

(S80)

Multiplying prior and likelihood and taking the negative logarithm yields Eq. (
S77
). Expanding the block structure of
\bm{A}_{i,d}
yields Eq. (
S78
). Since both prior and likelihood are Gaussian, the posterior is Gaussian.
∎

Because
\bm{\Omega}_{d}\succ 0
, the objective above is strictly convex.

Corollary 7

(Joint and conditional uniqueness)
.

For each output dimension
d
, the objective
\mathcal{J}_{d}
in Eq. (
S77
) has a unique global minimizer. Moreover, if all blocks except one are held fixed, the corresponding block subproblem is again strictly convex and has a unique minimizer.

Proof.

The Hessian of
\mathcal{J}_{d}
is

\nabla^{2}\mathcal{J}_{d}=\bm{\Omega}_{d}+\sum_{i=1}^{M}\bm{A}_{i,d}^{\top}\bm{R}_{i,d}^{-1}\bm{A}_{i,d}.

(S81)

The first term is strictly positive definite because the shared prior precision is
\bm{I}_{l_{0}}
and each local prior precision is
\lambda_{r}\bm{I}_{l_{r}}
with
\lambda_{r}>0
. The second term is positive semidefinite. Hence
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{J}_{d}\succ 0
, so
\mathcal{J}_{d}
is strictly convex and has a unique global minimizer. Restricting to any one block while holding the others fixed leaves a strictly positive definite block Hessian, and the corresponding block subproblem is therefore strictly convex as well.
∎

The natural-parameter updates in the main text are precisely the normal equations of these block subproblems.

Corollary 8

(Equivalence with the natural-parameter updates)
.

Fix an output dimension
d
.

•

If
\bm{b}_{1,d},\dots,\bm{b}_{M,d}
are held fixed, then the unique minimizer in
\bm{b}_{0,d}
satisfies

\left(\bm{I}_{l_{0}}+\sum_{i=1}^{M}\bm{P}_{0,i,d}^{\top}\bm{R}_{i,d}^{-1}\bm{P}_{0,i,d}\right)\bm{b}_{0,d}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\bm{P}_{0,i,d}^{\top}\bm{R}_{i,d}^{-1}\bigl(\bm{u}_{i,d}-\bm{P}_{r,i,d}\bm{b}_{i,d}\bigr).

(S82)

•

If
\bm{b}_{0,d}
is held fixed, then the unique minimizer in
\bm{b}_{i,d}
satisfies

\left(\lambda_{r}\bm{I}_{l_{r}}+\bm{P}_{r,i,d}^{\top}\bm{R}_{i,d}^{-1}\bm{P}_{r,i,d}\right)\bm{b}_{i,d}=\bm{P}_{r,i,d}^{\top}\bm{R}_{i,d}^{-1}\bigl(\bm{u}_{i,d}-\bm{P}_{0,i,d}\bm{b}_{0,d}\bigr).

(S83)

If the complementary blocks are replaced by their current posterior means
\bm{m}_{i,d}
and
\bm{m}_{0,d}
, these equations coincide exactly with Eqs. (
25
)–(
28
) in the main text. In particular, the natural-parameter updates are simply the precision-form representation of the corresponding weighted mixed-ridge solutions.

Proof.

Differentiate Eq. (
S78
) with respect to the relevant block, set the gradient to zero, and identify the resulting normal equations. Since each block conditional posterior is Gaussian, its precision-form parameters are exactly the left-hand-side matrix and right-hand-side vector appearing in these normal equations.
∎

E.4
Spectral shrinkage and effective degrees of freedom

The local field update admits a spectral interpretation that makes the role of
\lambda_{r}
and the amount of subject-specific adaptation interpretable.

Fix a subject
i
and output dimension
d
, and define the residualized pseudo-target

\bm{r}_{i,d}\coloneqq\bm{u}_{i,d}-\bm{P}_{0,i,d}\bm{m}_{0,d}.

(S84)

The local posterior mean in the main text is

\bm{m}_{i,d}=\left(\lambda_{r}\bm{I}_{l_{r}}+\bm{P}_{r,i,d}^{\top}\bm{R}_{i,d}^{-1}\bm{P}_{r,i,d}\right)^{-1}\bm{P}_{r,i,d}^{\top}\bm{R}_{i,d}^{-1}\bm{r}_{i,d}.

(S85)

Proposition 9

(Spectral shrinkage form of the local update)
.

Let

\bm{R}_{i,d}^{-1/2}\bm{P}_{r,i,d}=\bm{U}_{i,d}\operatorname{diag}\bigl(\sigma_{i,d,1},\dots,\sigma_{i,d,J_{i,d}}\bigr)\bm{V}_{i,d}^{\top}

(S86)

be a compact singular value decomposition, where
J_{i,d}=\operatorname{rank}(\bm{R}_{i,d}^{-1/2}\bm{P}_{r,i,d})
and the index
j=1,\dots,J_{i,d}
runs over weighted singular directions of the subject-specific design matrix. Then

\bm{m}_{i,d}=\bm{V}_{i,d}\operatorname{diag}\left(\frac{\sigma_{i,d,j}}{\lambda_{r}+\sigma_{i,d,j}^{2}}\right)_{j=1}^{J_{i,d}}\bm{U}_{i,d}^{\top}\bm{R}_{i,d}^{-1/2}\bm{r}_{i,d},

(S87)

and the fitted local contribution satisfies

\bm{P}_{r,i,d}\bm{m}_{i,d}=\bm{R}_{i,d}^{1/2}\bm{U}_{i,d}\operatorname{diag}\left(\alpha_{i,d,j}\right)_{j=1}^{J_{i,d}}\bm{U}_{i,d}^{\top}\bm{R}_{i,d}^{-1/2}\bm{r}_{i,d},\qquad\alpha_{i,d,j}\coloneqq\frac{\sigma_{i,d,j}^{2}}{\lambda_{r}+\sigma_{i,d,j}^{2}}.

(S88)

Thus each weighted singular direction is retained with shrinkage factor
\alpha_{i,d,j}\in[0,1)
.

Proof.

Substitute the compact singular value decomposition into Eq. (
S85
):

\displaystyle\bm{m}_{i,d}

\displaystyle=\left(\lambda_{r}\bm{I}_{l_{r}}+\bm{V}_{i,d}\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_{i,d,j}^{2})\bm{V}_{i,d}^{\top}\right)^{-1}\bm{V}_{i,d}\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_{i,d,j})\bm{U}_{i,d}^{\top}\bm{R}_{i,d}^{-1/2}\bm{r}_{i,d}

\displaystyle=\bm{V}_{i,d}\operatorname{diag}\left(\frac{\sigma_{i,d,j}}{\lambda_{r}+\sigma_{i,d,j}^{2}}\right)\bm{U}_{i,d}^{\top}\bm{R}_{i,d}^{-1/2}\bm{r}_{i,d},

(S89)

which is Eq. (
S87
). Multiplying by
\bm{P}_{r,i,d}=\bm{R}_{i,d}^{1/2}\bm{U}_{i,d}\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_{i,d,j})\bm{V}_{i,d}^{\top}
yields Eq. (
S88
).
∎

Proposition
9
shows that subject-specific personalization is strongest along directions that are both well supported by the pseudo-data and weakly penalized by
\lambda_{r}
. A natural scalar summary is the effective number of local degrees of freedom

\mathrm{df}_{i,d}^{\mathrm{loc}}\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{J_{i,d}}\frac{\sigma_{i,d,j}^{2}}{\lambda_{r}+\sigma_{i,d,j}^{2}}.

(S90)

This quantity lies between
0
and
J_{i,d}
and measures how much local flexibility is effectively used for subject
i
and output
d
.

E.5
Monotone information accumulation

The shared-field update aggregates information across subjects by summing positive semidefinite contributions. This gives a simple monotonicity property that mirrors the common-mean Gaussian-process updates of MAGMA
[
19
]
: adding subjects can only increase shared precision and decrease shared posterior covariance.

Proposition 10

(Monotone accumulation of information)
.

Fix an output dimension
d
and consider the shared precision update

\bm{\Lambda}_{0,d}^{(M)}\coloneqq\bm{I}_{l_{0}}+\sum_{i=1}^{M}\bm{P}_{0,i,d}^{\top}\bm{R}_{i,d}^{-1}\bm{P}_{0,i,d}.

(S91)

Then

\bm{\Lambda}_{0,d}^{(M+1)}-\bm{\Lambda}_{0,d}^{(M)}=\bm{P}_{0,M+1,d}^{\top}\bm{R}_{M+1,d}^{-1}\bm{P}_{0,M+1,d}\succeq\mathbf{0}.

(S92)

Consequently, the corresponding posterior covariances satisfy

\bigl(\bm{\Lambda}_{0,d}^{(M+1)}\bigr)^{-1}\preceq\bigl(\bm{\Lambda}_{0,d}^{(M)}\bigr)^{-1}.

(S93)

An analogous statement holds for the local precision of a fixed subject when additional pseudo-derivative observations are added to that subject.

Proof.

Equation (
S92
) is immediate because
\bm{R}_{M+1,d}^{-1}\succ 0
, so the quadratic form
\bm{P}_{0,M+1,d}^{\top}\bm{R}_{M+1,d}^{-1}\bm{P}_{0,M+1,d}
is positive semidefinite. The covariance monotonicity then follows from the order-reversing property of matrix inversion on the cone of symmetric positive definite matrices. The local statement is identical.
∎

This algebraic structure is closely related to the common-mean multi-task GP update used in MAGMA
[
19
]
, where the shared precision likewise accumulates a sum of task-specific positive semidefinite contributions. The difference here is that the contributions arise from ODE-induced pseudo-derivative regressions rather than directly from observed outputs.

The same monotonicity extends to trajectory summaries under the first-order pushforward approximation of Eq. (
S69
). Indeed, if
\bm{\Sigma}_{B_{0}}^{(M+1)}\preceq\bm{\Sigma}_{B_{0}}^{(M)}
, then for any fixed sensitivity row
\bm{J}_{0,i,a}(t)
,

\bm{J}_{0,i,a}(t)\bm{\Sigma}_{B_{0}}^{(M+1)}\bm{J}_{0,i,a}(t)^{\top}\leq\bm{J}_{0,i,a}(t)\bm{\Sigma}_{B_{0}}^{(M)}\bm{J}_{0,i,a}(t)^{\top}.

(S94)

Thus, at least at the linearized level, adding subjects can only reduce the population-driven component of trajectory uncertainty.

Appendix F
Alternative scalable vector field approximations

Although the main text uses inducing-feature representations for the shared and subject-specific vector fields, the overall framework is compatible with other scalable GP approximations. The main requirement is that, at fixed inputs, the field admits a linear-Gaussian representation in a finite set of coefficients.

Consider the generic construction

f_{0,d}(\bm{x})=\varphi_{0}(\bm{x})^{\top}\bm{b}_{0,d},\qquad g_{i,d}(\bm{x})=\varphi_{r}(\bm{x})^{\top}\bm{b}_{i,d},\qquad f_{i,d}(\bm{x})=f_{0,d}(\bm{x})+g_{i,d}(\bm{x}),

(S95)

with feature maps
\varphi_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{L_{0}}
and
\varphi_{r}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{L_{r}}
, and Gaussian priors

\bm{b}_{0,d}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\bm{\Omega}_{0}^{-1}),\qquad\bm{b}_{i,d}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\bm{\Omega}_{r}^{-1}).

(S96)

If the approximate posteriors remain Gaussian,

q(\bm{b}_{0,d})=\mathcal{N}(\bm{m}_{0,d},\bm{S}_{0,d}),\qquad q(\bm{b}_{i,d})=\mathcal{N}(\bm{m}_{i,d},\bm{S}_{i,d}),

then field moments are still available in closed form:

\displaystyle\mu^{f}_{i,d}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\varphi_{0}(\bm{x})^{\top}\bm{m}_{0,d}+\varphi_{r}(\bm{x})^{\top}\bm{m}_{i,d},

(S97)

\displaystyle v^{f}_{i,d}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\varphi_{0}(\bm{x})^{\top}\bm{S}_{0,d}\varphi_{0}(\bm{x})+\varphi_{r}(\bm{x})^{\top}\bm{S}_{i,d}\varphi_{r}(\bm{x}).

(S98)

Whenever the feature maps are differentiable, the Jacobian of the posterior-mean field is explicit as well:

\nabla_{\bm{x}}\mathbb{E}_{q}[f_{i,d}(\bm{x})]=\nabla_{\bm{x}}\varphi_{0}(\bm{x})^{\top}\bm{m}_{0,d}+\nabla_{\bm{x}}\varphi_{r}(\bm{x})^{\top}\bm{m}_{i,d}.

(S99)

This is sufficient for the trajectory-space linearization. On the field side, once the smoothed pseudo-derivatives are available, the update remains a Gaussian mixed regression problem with the same form as in Section
3.4
, except that the identity prior precisions are replaced by
\bm{\Omega}_{0}
and
\bm{\Omega}_{r}
. The inducing-feature construction used in the main text is recovered by taking
\varphi_{0}=\Phi_{0}
,
\varphi_{r}=\Phi_{r}
, and whitened priors.

This yields two alternatives.

Random Fourier features.

For stationary kernels, one may replace inducing features by random Fourier features
[
29
]
. In that case,
\varphi_{0}
and
\varphi_{r}
are finite-dimensional random feature maps, and the model remains exactly in the form of Eq. (
S95
). This yields the same alternating algorithm. The main advantages are simplicity, fast dense linear algebra, and tunable capacities through
L_{0}
and
L_{r}
; the main drawback is that random features do not adapt to the observed state distribution, so many features may be needed when the field has localized structure.

Hilbert basis expansions.

On a bounded state domain, one may instead use Hilbert or spectral basis-function approximations
[
35
]
. Then
\varphi_{0}
and
\varphi_{r}
are deterministic basis expansions, for example built from Laplace eigenfunctions with appropriate spectral weights. This again preserves the linear-Gaussian field layer and therefore leaves the trajectory update and mixed-regression field update unchanged. The main advantages are a deterministic low-rank representation and explicit control of spectral structure; the main limitation is that one must choose a suitable bounded state domain and basis, which may be less flexible when the relevant state region is not known in advance.
