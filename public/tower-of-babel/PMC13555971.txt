Admission BNP-dominant biomarker phenotypes and in-hospital heart failure or cardiogenic shock in STEMI: a retrospective research note
Authors: Quan Zuo, Li Zhao, Tao Ge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo assess, in a single-center retrospective cohort, whether admission B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP)-dominant biomarker phenotypes incorporating D-dimer are associated with in-hospital heart failure or cardiogenic shock (HF/CS) in patients with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI).ResultsAmong 509 unique STEMI admissions, 467 had analyzable admission timestamps. The biomarker-enriched cohort included 140 patients with BNP and D-dimer measured within 24 h; 64 (45.7%) developed HF/CS. In the complete-case adjusted cohort (n = 136; 62 events), an equal-weight burden score [z(ln BNP) + z(ln D-dimer)] was associated with HF/CS (adjusted odds ratio [OR] 4.04, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.84–8.92). BNP alone showed a similarly strong association (adjusted OR 3.75, 95% CI 1.86–7.60) and a slightly higher apparent area under the curve (0.859 vs. 0.849), indicating that the signal was mainly BNP-driven. The high BNP/high D-dimer phenotype had the highest HF/CS rate (80.9%). These findings should be interpreted as exploratory association and risk-enrichment observations rather than validated prediction or a complete assessment of congestion status.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13104-026-07927-y.Keywords: ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction, B-type natriuretic peptide, D-dimer, Heart failure, Cardiogenic shock, Biomarkers

Introduction

Early in-hospital heart failure and cardiogenic shock remain clinically important complications of ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), despite advances in reperfusion therapy and coronary care [1–5]. Traditional bedside risk tools, including Killip class and GRACE-derived approaches, remain useful but may not fully capture the rapidly evolving biological state during the early phase of STEMI [3, 4].

B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) reflects myocardial wall stress and early ventricular dysfunction, and previous studies have associated BNP or NT-proBNP with adverse outcomes after acute coronary syndromes and STEMI [6–8]. D-dimer reflects fibrin turnover and may indicate thrombotic burden, microvascular dysfunction or systemic activation of coagulation/fibrinolysis [9–12]. Inflammatory markers such as high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) may provide additional biological context [13–15].

This research note reports a concise retrospective observation on whether an admission BNP-dominant biomarker phenotype incorporating D-dimer is associated with in-hospital HF/CS in patients with STEMI. The analysis was framed as association and risk enrichment rather than formal incident-event prediction because exact temporal ordering between biomarker sampling and onset of HF/CS could not be adjudicated in every case.

Methods

This single-center retrospective study linked a structured STEMI registry with original admission, discharge-summary and coronary care unit laboratory datasets. STEMI diagnosis and contemporary management principles were aligned with the Fourth Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction and current acute coronary syndrome guidelines [1, 2].

The source cohort contained 509 unique STEMI admissions. Of these, 467 had analyzable admission timestamps that allowed alignment of laboratory results to a prespecified 24 h window. The primary biomarker-enriched cohort required both admission BNP and admission D-dimer within 24 h of hospital arrival and ascertainable in-hospital HF/CS status, yielding 140 patients. The prespecified adjusted model was fitted in a complete-case cohort of 136 patients with 62 events. A broader secondary cohort with hs-CRP and D-dimer was used only for sensitivity analysis.

Available baseline variables were extracted from routine clinical records, including demographic characteristics, anthropometric information when recorded, cardiovascular risk factors, admission hemodynamic variables, renal function, left ventricular ejection fraction, available angiographic or reperfusion-related information, and available pharmacological/supportive-care information. Because medication and supportive-care variables during the acute STEMI hospitalization may be time-dependent and partly downstream of HF/CS, pharmacological variables were reviewed descriptively when available and were not forced into the primary adjustment set.

The primary endpoint was a composite of in-hospital heart failure or cardiogenic shock during the index hospitalization. Heart failure and cardiogenic shock were identified from structured in-hospital outcome coding supported by hospitalization records. Secondary endpoints included in-hospital heart failure, cardiogenic shock and in-hospital death.

Admission biomarker values were defined as the earliest available measurement obtained within 24 h after admission. BNP, D-dimer and hs-CRP were natural-log transformed because of right-skewed distributions. The hemodynamic-thrombotic burden score was prespecified as an equal-weight composite: z[ln(BNP)] + z[ln(D-dimer)]. BNP and D-dimer were also dichotomized at the cohort median to form four phenotypes: low/low, high BNP/low D-dimer, low BNP/high D-dimer and high/high. Biomarker units were harmonized according to the hospital laboratory information system for analysis and reporting (BNP, pg/mL; D-dimer, ug/mL; hs-cTnI, ng/mL). Detailed assay-platform metadata were not consistently retrievable from the retrospective extract.

Continuous variables are presented as median [interquartile range], and categorical variables as number/total (percentage). Between-group comparisons used the Mann-Whitney U test, chi-square test or Fisher exact test. Multivariable logistic regression estimated odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The clinical base model included age, sex, systolic blood pressure, heart rate, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, creatinine and left ventricular ejection fraction. This model was deliberately parsimonious because of the limited number of HF/CS events and to reduce the risk of overfitting. We did not indiscriminately include all available clinical, anthropometric and pharmacological variables in a single model, particularly when variables were incompletely captured or could plausibly represent treatment responses after early clinical deterioration. Discrimination was summarized using apparent area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and Brier score. These discrimination analyses were exploratory and were not internally or externally validated. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The study protocol was approved by the Scientific Research and New Technology Ethics Committee of Wannan Medical College Yijishan Hospital (approval number: (2023) Provincial Research Ethics Review No. 02). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the same committee because the study was retrospective and used anonymized routinely collected clinical data. All procedures involving human data were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant institutional and national guidelines and regulations.

Table 2 summarizes the main association results. In the complete-case adjusted cohort, the hemodynamic-thrombotic burden score was associated with HF/CS (adjusted OR 4.04, 95% CI 1.84–8.92). The apparent AUC increased from 0.807 in the clinical base model to 0.849 after adding the burden score. BNP alone showed a similarly strong association (adjusted OR 3.75, 95% CI 1.86–7.60) and a slightly higher apparent AUC (0.859). Standardized ln(D-dimer) alone was weaker and not statistically significant after adjustment (adjusted OR 1.57, 95% CI 0.90–2.73).

In phenotype analysis, the high BNP/high D-dimer group had the highest observed HF/CS rate (80.9%) and remained at increased risk compared with the low BNP/low D-dimer phenotype (adjusted OR 6.75, 95% CI 1.75–26.10) (Additional file 1: Table S3). The low BNP/high D-dimer group was not associated with increased adjusted risk. In the broader hs-CRP/D-dimer cohort, a thrombo-inflammatory score was associated with HF/CS (adjusted OR 1.50, 95% CI 1.15–1.96), but apparent discrimination increased only modestly (AUC 0.822 to 0.828) (Additional file 1: Table S4).

Discussion

In this retrospective research note, admission BNP-dominant biomarker phenotypes were associated with in-hospital HF/CS in STEMI. The signal was largely driven by BNP, whereas D-dimer alone did not show an independent adjusted association. Concurrent high BNP and high D-dimer nevertheless identified a subgroup with a high observed HF/CS rate, suggesting that D-dimer may provide contextual information on thrombotic burden when interpreted alongside a marker of hemodynamic stress.

These findings are consistent with previous evidence linking natriuretic peptides to adverse outcomes after acute coronary syndromes and STEMI [6–8], and with reports that D-dimer can reflect thrombotic or microvascular risk in STEMI [9–12]. The results should not be interpreted as demonstrating a validated clinical prediction model. Instead, they provide a concise observation that may help generate hypotheses for future protocolized studies with standardized biomarker testing, adjudicated incident HF/CS and internal or external validation.

A further practical point is that BNP should not be interpreted as a stand-alone measure of congestion. BNP is a circulating marker of myocardial wall stress and may indirectly reflect hemodynamic congestion, but congestion is a complex clinical state that cannot be fully characterized by a single biomarker. A multiparametric approach integrating bedside clinical assessment, echocardiographic findings, renal function, pharmacological context and, where available, bioimpedance-based assessment may provide a more comprehensive evaluation. Therefore, the BNP-dominant phenotype observed in this study should be interpreted as a simple biomarker-based risk-enrichment signal rather than a complete assessment of congestion status.

Limitations

The retrospective design is the major limitation of this study. Biomarker testing was performed according to routine clinical practice rather than a prospective protocol, which may have introduced indication bias because BNP and D-dimer were more likely to be measured in patients perceived to be clinically unstable or at higher risk. Although admission biomarker values were defined within a prespecified 24 h window, the exact temporal relationship between biomarker sampling and the onset of HF/CS could not be adjudicated in every patient. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as exploratory associations and risk-enrichment observations rather than causal evidence, a complete congestion assessment, or a validated prediction model.

Additional limitations should also be acknowledged. The study was single-center, the primary biomarker-enriched cohort was smaller than the source registry and outcome identification relied on structured records supported by hospitalization documentation rather than blinded adjudication. Calibration analysis, bootstrap optimism correction and external validation were not available. Detailed pharmacological, anthropometric, angiographic and treatment-timing variables were not uniformly captured in the retrospective extract and therefore could not all be included in the final multivariable model without a high risk of overfitting or time-dependent bias. In particular, acute pharmacological and supportive-care variables may represent responses to early HF/CS rather than true baseline confounders. Residual confounding related to infarct severity, reperfusion delay, Killip class at presentation, angiographic no-reflow, medication decisions or unmeasured congestion status cannot be excluded.
