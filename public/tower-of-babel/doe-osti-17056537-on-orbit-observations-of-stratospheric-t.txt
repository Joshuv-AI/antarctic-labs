Project Overview
Atmospheric gravity waves are important drivers of high-altitude weather patterns that occur
when air flows over mountains, rises in thunderstorms, or encounters a frontal system, creating
ripples that travel up to the stratosphere (10 to 50 kilometers above Earth’s surface) until they
break like a ocean wave on a beach and create turbulence. Understanding where and how
these waves break is crucial for predicting climate patterns including the quasi-biennial
oscillation (a periodic change in stratospheric winds) and polar vortex breakdown, planning
high-altitude aircraft missions, maintaining reliable satellite communications, and ensuring
accurate laser beam transmission. Small-scale gravity waves, including waves with vertical
wavelengths of only a few kilometers, are particularly difficult to observe with remote-sensing
instruments. Yet these poorly observed waves can transport substantial momentum and make
important contributions to the atmospheric forcing that drives large-scale circulation and
climate variability. To address this observational gap, we analyzed measurements from the
Stellar Occultation Hypertemporal Imaging Payload (SOHIP), an instrument built by Lawrence
Livermore National Laboratory and operated on the International Space Station from March
2023 through April 2024, observing how starlight bends and twinkles as it passes through
atmospheric layers.
By analyzing subtle changes in star position and brightness as the star sets behind the
atmosphere, we developed an improved algorithm for retrieving atmospheric properties that
achieves higher accuracy and approximately 3-meter vertical sampling between 10- and 50-
kilometers altitude, which is much finer detail than previously available from operational
satellite systems. We created comprehensive analysis tools for extracting turbulence
characteristics from the brightness fluctuations and overcame unexpected detector sensitivity
challenges through laboratory calibration experiments. We also performed computer
simulations of specific gravity wave events using advanced Earth system models with custom
high-resolution configurations. Our results include a manuscript submitted to Earth and Space
Science describing the improved retrieval algorithm, a public dataset of over 100 atmospheric
measurements, and novel techniques for observing upper atmosphere disturbances that affect
weather, climate, flight operations, and space-based systems.

Mission Impact
This research supports Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory’s mission area of Multi-
Domain Deterrence by detecting and characterizing upper atmosphere disturbances that affect
high-altitude vehicle stability, laser beam propagation, and satellite communications for
national security operations. The project advances the Laboratory’s core competency in Earth
and Atmospheric Science by improving fundamental understanding of atmospheric circulation
processes and providing novel observation tools for the scientific community. This work helps
develop science and technology tools and capabilities to meet future national security
challenges, enabling the National Nuclear Security Administration to create new ways of
responding to atmospheric-related challenges. The results are highly relevant to the
