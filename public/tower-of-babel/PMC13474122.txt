POSS‐PEG‐NHS and GSH Cocrosslink DPAV to Prepare Anticalcification Bioprosthetic Scaffold With Enhanced Reendothelization, Anti‐inflammation, and Anti‐ROS
Authors: Yuqing Zhang, Chen Qian, Yingjie He, Jingli Ding, Binhao Zhang, Minghui Zou, Xing Chen, Jinping Zhao, Cuifen Lu, Jinping Liu, Jianliang Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe morbidity and mortality associated with heart valve disease (HVD) are progressively increasing, making it a leading cause of the global cardiovascular disease burden. The most effective current treatment is valve replacement, primarily utilizing mechanical or bioprosthetic valves. However, mechanical valves necessitate lifelong anticoagulation therapy, which increases the risk of bleeding events, including cerebral hemorrhage. Bioprosthetic valves, predominantly fixed with glutaraldehyde (GLUT), suffer from poor durability, often deteriorating due to calcification within 10–15 years. To develop a superior artificial valve substitute, this work synthesized a macromolecular chemical agent, POSS‐PEG‐NHS, to co‐crosslink decellularized porcine aortic valves (DPAV) with glutathione (GSH). Then the surface morphology and mechanical properties of the resulting material were investigated. The biocompatibility and hemocompatibility were further demonstrated through in vitro cell experiments. Subsequently, the comprehensive performance of the PPN‐GSH‐AV scaffold was evaluated in vivo using a subcutaneous implantation model in Sprague‐Dawley (SD) rats. The results revealed predominant infiltration of M2 macrophages, reduced calcification formation, and enhanced re‐endothelialization. In conclusion, this study demonstrates that modification with POSS‐PEG‐NHS and GSH significantly enhances the scaffold's mechanical properties, hemocompatibility, endothelialization, and anti‐inflammatory, anti‐ROS, and anticalcification abilities, showing considerable promise for clinical translation.Keywords: bioprosthetic valve, crosslink, glutathione, POSS‐PEG, surface modification

Introduction

Heart valve disease (HVD) currently presents with high morbidity and mortality, posing a significant global clinical burden [1]. Although treatment options include valve repair or replacement, the latter is more prevalent. For valve replacement, the choice lies between mechanical and bioprosthetic valves. Mechanical valves, despite their long‐term durability (20–30 years), carry a persistent risk of bleeding complications due to the requirement for lifelong anticoagulant therapy and may also produce audible noise during operation [2]. In contrast, bioprosthetic valves, typically fabricated from glutaraldehyde (Glut)‐fixed decellularized porcine aortic valves (DPAV) or bovine pericardium [3, 4], preclude the need for long‐term anticoagulation. However, they are prone to structural valve deterioration, often necessitating reintervention within 10–15 years. This limited durability, attributed to calcification and immune responses triggered by residual aldehyde groups [5], restricts their use in younger patients, including pediatric cases, who exhibit accelerated degradation rates [6]. Consequently, they are generally recommended for patients over 60–70 years of age [2]. Given these limitations, there is a clear clinical imperative to develop a next generation bioprosthetic valve that utilizes a nonglutaraldehyde crosslinking strategy. Such a valve must offer superior cytocompatibility, anti‐inflammatory and anticalcification properties, and enhanced durability to address these unmet clinical needs [7].

POSS (polyhedral oligomeric silsesquioxane, POSS) is a cage‐like inorganic–organic hybrid material characterized by a rigid inorganic Si–O–Si framework and multiple functional groups at the silicon vertices [8]. To date, the 8‐arm POSS variant has been the most extensively studied in biomaterial applications, owing to its properties of anti‐inflammation, high biocompatibility [9], and ability to promote angiogenesis [10]. Furthermore, POSS is considered an attractive biomaterial candidate not only because of its facile synthesis, precise chemical structure, and rigid molecular geometry but also potential for tailored functionality through further chemical modification [8, 11]. Among different derivatives, POSS grafting polyethylene glycol (PEG) has been widely investigated in bone regeneration [12], heart valve replacement [13], and wound healing [14], where they are utilized as hybrid hydrogel or nanoparticle delivery with demonstrated success. Our team previously synthesized an aldehyde‐terminated POSS‐PEG derivative (POSS‐PEG‐CHO) and evaluated the properties of porcine pericardium crosslinked with this compound to traditional Glut. The results indicated that the POSS‐PEG‐CHO‐crosslinked tissue exhibited significantly enhanced cytocompatibility and anti‐calcification capacity [15]. However, this approach still relied on the conventional aldehyde crosslinking mode. Interestingly, a recent study reported that decellularized bovine and porcine pericardium crosslinked via 8‐arm PEG functionalized with N‐hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) esters exhibited outstanding recellularization performance [16]. Thus, the NHS was considered a viable alternative to aldehyde‐based crosslinking, thereby avoiding cytotoxicity associated with residual aldehyde groups.

Previous studies have demonstrated that when the concentration of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) in the extracellular matrix exceeds the intrinsic buffering capacity of cells, intracellular H2O2 levels also rise. This elevation triggers an intracellular oxidative stress response, ultimately leading to cellular damage [17]. This mechanism is likely a critical factor impeding the reendothelialization of decellularized extracellular matrix materials, such as those used in bioprosthetic heart valves. Glutathione (GSH) is a pivotal antioxidant that scavenges reactive oxygen species (ROS) either directly or through enzymatic catalysis, mitigating oxidative stress and maintaining redox homeostasis [18]. Though the concentration of extracellular GSH (2–10 µM) was 1/1000 of the intracellular counterpart (2–10 mM) [19], extracellular GSH has been reported to modulate the function of the calcium‐sensing receptor, thereby influencing pathologies associated with calcium dysregulation [20]. Furthermore, extracellular GSH can act as a buffer against extracellular oxidants, enhancing material surfaces’ resistance to oxidative stress. This concept has also been validated in tissue engineering contexts. For instance, previous research has shown that modifying Glut‐crosslinked bovine pericardium with GSH significantly enhanced its cytocompatibility and reduced calcification [21].

In summary, this study developed a novel POSS‐PEG‐NHS compound to crosslink DPAV via a nonaldehyde strategy. This approach synergistically combines the physiological structure of the native valve, with inherent mechanical stability, anti‐calcification, anti‐inflammation, and promoting angiogenin property of POSS‐PEG, while eliminating cytotoxicity associated with residual aldehyde groups. Furthermore, GSH was incorporated to mitigate oxidative stress damage to endothelial cells and modulate the ROS in macrophages. The resulting composite bioprosthetic valve demonstrated excellent mechanical properties, biocompatibility, hemocompatibility, recellularization, and potential for clinical translation.

Results

Synthesis and Characterization of POSS‐PEG‐NHS

The synthesis of POSS‐PEG‐NHS was accomplished in three steps, as outlined in Figure 1a. The successful formation of the target compound was confirmed by 1H NMR and FTIR spectroscopy. The 1H NMR spectrum (Figure 1b) displayed characteristic peaks at 0.72, 1.63, and 2.52 ppm, corresponding to the methylene groups adjacent to the POSS cage. A distinctive new resonance at 2.8 ppm was assigned to the succinyl ester moiety (protons i, j), and the integral ratios of these signals (1:1:2:4) agreed with the expected molecular structure. Further evidence was provided by FTIR spectrum (Figure 1c), the absorption band at 1100–1150 cm− 1 is attributed to the C─O─C stretching of PEG, with a potential overlap from Si─O─Si vibrations of POSS. A key distinction was observed in the carbonyl region: while POSS‐PEG‐COOH showed a single C═O stretch at 1750–1740 cm− 1, POSS‐PEG‐NHS exhibited two distinct bands at 1750–1740 and 1830–1800 cm− 1, characteristic of the succinyl ester. Additionally, all intermediates and the final product (POSS‐PEG‐OH, ─COOH, ─NHS) showed intense bands at 2930–2850 cm− 1, consistent with ─CH2─ stretching of the PEG backbone. Together, these spectroscopic data conclusively verify the successful synthesis of POSS‐PEG‐NHS.

Construction and Characterization of the Scaffolds

Construction of the PPN‐GSH‐AV

As shown in Figure 2a, histological analysis confirmed the effective removal of nearly all cells, verifying the successful decellularization process. Furthermore, comparison of fiber morphology and arrangement between the PAV and DPAV in Masson staining revealed no evident structural damage introduced by decellularization. The multifunctional prosthetic valve scaffold was subsequently fabricated through a two‐step esterification, in which the succinimide groups reacted with the amino groups of the DPAV and GSH, respectively. The reaction scheme is illustrated in Figure 2b. Macroscopic examination of the scaffolds (Figure 2c) exhibited no obvious differences among the DPAV, PPN‐AV, and PPN‐GSH‐AV. In contrast, the GAV showed a distinct pale‐yellow coloration, setting it apart from the other groups.

FTIR, XPS, and EDS

FTIR revealed characteristic vibration peaks, confirming the successful modification (Figure 2d). Specifically, the PPN‐AV and PPN‐GSH‐AV scaffolds exhibited a distinct stretching vibration peak at 1150 cm−1, attributed to the Si─O─Si bonds of the POSS framework and the C─O─C bonds of the PEG chain. Furthermore, the amide‐related peak was observed at 1660–1640 cm−1 in both scaffolds. Moreover, the PPN‐GSH‐AV exhibited a unique peak at 2600–2500 cm−1, corresponding to the ─SH group. However, no significant ─CHO peak derived from glutaraldehyde was observed at 1750–1700 cm−1 in the GAV. XPS (Figure 2e) presented the characteristic Si 2p peak in the PPN‐GSH‐AV, which was absent in the DPAV control. High‐resolution scans and corresponding peak fitting for C, N, O, and Si are provided in Figure S1. A distinct peak at 102.5 eV, assigned to the Si─O bond in the POSS framework, proved the successful introduction of POSS. The N1s spectra were deconvoluted into two components corresponding to residual NHS‐related nitrogen species (approximately 401.8 eV) and amide/amine nitrogen species (approximately 399.8–400.2 eV) [22]. The relative proportion of residual NHS groups was estimated from the fitted peak areas. The results showed that the relative NHS‐associated peak area decreased progressively from 6.4% in DPAV to 2.6% in PPN‐AV and further to 0.4% in PPN‐GSH‐AV (Figure S6). This continuous reduction indicates substantial consumption of NHS groups during the reaction with amino groups present in the decellularized valve and subsequent GSH modification. These additional data provide quantitative evidence supporting the successful conversion of NHS esters during scaffold modification. Additionally, EDS detected silicon in the PPN‐GSH‐AV scaffold (Wt 1.8%), whereas no silicon was observed in the DPAV (Figure S2).

Mechanical Property, Surface Morphology, Hydrophilicity, and Roughness

The strain–stress curves were recorded by the uniaxial tensile test at a speed of 5 mm min−1 (Figure S3). As summarized in Figure 3a, the elongation at break of the PPN‐GSH‐AV (97.33 ± 28.14%) was comparable to that of the PPN‐AV (98.88 ± 25.32%), slightly higher than those of the DPAV (91.35 ± 29.99%) and PAV (70.50 ± 12.97%), but lower than that of the GAV (150.7 ± 7.831%). As shown in Figure 3b, Young's modulus of the PPN‐AV (41.26 ± 10.07 MPa) and PPN‐GSH‐AV (56.92 ±16.21 MPa) were significantly higher than those of the DPAV (7.288 ± 1.566 MPa) and PAV (14.79 ± 1.202 MPa), though still lower than that of the GAV (95.47 ± 20.03 MPa). Regarding tensile strength (Figure 3c), the GAV (34.27 ± 15.44 MPa) scaffold exhibited the highest value among all groups. The PPN‐GSH‐AV (18.91 ± 4.034 MPa) showed significantly greater tensile strength than the DPAV (4.307 ± 1.142 MPa) and PAV (7.642 ± 0.8598 MPa) and was also higher than the PPN‐AV (12.98 ± 1.626 MPa) scaffold, but no significant difference.

Surface morphology examined by SEM (Figure 3d) indicated more pronounced fiber curling in the PPN‐AV, PPN‐GSH‐AV, and GAV than the DPAV, with the most severe curling observed in the GAV. But there is no significant difference in cross‐sectional images among the groups. WCA images (Figure 3f) revealed the superior hydrophilicity of the PPN‐GSH‐AV. As quantified in Figure 3e, the PPN‐GSH‐AV exhibited the lowest WCA (35.43 ± 6.417°), significantly lower than those of the DPAV (63.23 ± 9.218°), PPN‐AV (42.87 ± 4.008°), and GAV (48.53 ± 2.726°). Furthermore, roughness analysis (Figure 3h) indicated a marked increase in the PPN‐GSH‐AV (123.0 ± 6.083 nm) compared to DPAV (23.30 ± 7.467 nm) and PPN‐AV (43.50 ± 8.850 nm), though it remained lower than that of GAV (143.70 ± 3.786 nm), matching the surface morphology in images captured by SEM.

PLTs Adhesion, Thrombosis, and Hemolysis In Vitro

In Figure 4a, SEM images revealed the least PLTs adhesion on the PPN‐GSH‐AV scaffold. This finding was corroborated by the lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) assay (Figure 4d), which indicated the superior antiplatelet adhesion property of the PPN‐GSH‐AV. Hemolysis analysis, including representative images (Figure 4b) and the quantitative hemolysis rate measured at 540 nm (Figure 4e), indicated no significant differences among groups. In the thrombosis assay (Figure 4c), noticeable clots formed on the surfaces of the DPAV and GAV scaffolds, whereas only minimal clotting was observed on the PPN‐GSH‐AV scaffold. Consistent with this observation, the absorbance at 540 nm of the fluid after thrombus lysis (Figure 4f) was significantly lower in the PPN‐GSH‐AV group than in the DPAV and GAV groups, confirming its enhanced antithrombosis.

Cellular Toxicity and Proliferation of Raw264.7 and HUVECs

As shown in Figure 5a, HUVECs cultured on the scaffolds were visualized by confocal macroscopy to determine reendothelialization potential. A markedly higher number of viable HUVECs adhered to the PPN‐GSH‐AV compared to the GAV scaffold, while the DPAV and PPN‐AV scaffolds exhibited intermediate cell densities. These observations were corroborated by the quantitative analysis of live/dead cell percentages (Figure 5b). Similar trends were observed in Raw264.7 macrophage behavior. Three‐dimensional confocal imaging revealed robust macrophage growth on the DPAV, PPN‐AV, and PPN‐GSH‐AV scaffolds, with the most pronounced proliferation and multilayer overlapping evident on the PPN‐GSH‐AV scaffold (Figure 5d). In contrast, macrophage growth was significantly suppressed on the GAV scaffold. Viability staining further indicated a higher proportion of dead cells on the GAV scaffold, whereas only sporadic cell death was detected on the PPN‐AV and PPN‐GSH‐AV scaffolds (Figure 5c). Furthermore, the 48 h cytotoxicity assay proved that both HUVECs (Figure 5e) and Raw264.7 cells (Figure 5f) exhibited better viability on the DPAV, PPN‐AV, and PPN‐GSH‐AV scaffolds than the GAV scaffold. Moreover, HUVECs proliferation curves (Figure 5g) indicated vigorous growth on the PPN‐GSH‐AV scaffold, with sustained proliferation on the DPAV and PPN‐AV scaffolds, albeit at slower rates. In contrast, the GAV scaffold showed no notable proliferation over time. Similarly, Raw264.7 proliferation curves demonstrated initial growth across all groups, followed by a gradual decline in cell numbers during later stages. Throughout the culture period, the GAV scaffold consistently supported the lowest macrophage viability.

Property of Anti‐ROS and Anti‐inflammation

The anti‐ROS capacity of the scaffolds was evaluated by measuring intracellular ROS levels in Raw264.7 cells. As shown in Figure 6a, ROS staining revealed significantly higher relative fluorescence intensity in cells cultured on DPAV and GAV scaffolds compared to those on PPN‐AV and PPN‐GSH‐AV scaffolds. Notably, the lowest ROS signal was observed on the PPN‐GSH‐AV scaffold. These findings were further corroborated by the quantitative analysis of mean fluorescence intensity (Figure 6d).

(b) Relative expression quantification of ROS on scaffolds. c Relative content of H2O2 of scaffolds was quantified by absorbance. (d) Relative expression quantification of Arg 1. (e) Relative expression quantification of iNOS. (f) The fluorescence intensity of Arg 1 and iNOS of scaffolds was captured by confocal microscopy on day 5. (Data were presented as mean ± SD, *p< 0.05, **p< 0.01, ***p< 0.001, and ****p< 0.0001.).

Furthermore, the relative quantification of H2O2 in Raw264.7 cells (Figure 6c) demonstrated significant downregulation in the PPN‐GSH‐AV group compared to all other groups, confirming its enhanced antioxidant capacity. To extend this evaluation to the extracellular microenvironment, we measured extracellular ROS and H2O2 levels in the cell culture supernatant (Figure S7a,b). Consistent with the intracellular results, the PPN‐GSH‐AV scaffold induced the lowest extracellular ROS and H2O2 levels. The GAV scaffold also showed higher extracellular ROS and H2O2, whereas the DPAV and PPN‐AV groups exhibited markedly lower levels. These complementary data further validate that the PPN‐GSH‐AV scaffold not only suppresses intracellular oxidative stress but also effectively reduces the release of pro‐oxidant molecules into the extracellular space, thereby reinforcing its robust antioxidant capacity.

To evaluate the anti‐inflammatory properties of the scaffolds, immunofluorescence staining was performed to detect the expression of Arg1 and iNOS in Raw264.7 cells (Figure 6f). As shown in Figure 6d, the PPN‐GSH‐AV scaffold exhibited significantly enhanced Arg1 expression compared to the DPAV and PPN‐AV scaffolds, while minimal expression was observed in the GAV group. In contrast, iNOS expression was markedly upregulated in the GAV scaffold relative to the other groups (Figure 6e). The DPAV scaffold showed moderate iNOS signal. In contrast, both PPN‐AV and PPN‐GSH‐AV scaffolds displayed negligible iNOS levels, indicating a favorable shift toward the M2 anti‐inflammatory phenotype.

Performances In vivo in the Subcutaneous Embedding Model of SD Rat

Following 30 days of subcutaneous implantation, HE staining presents substantial cell infiltration into the DPAV, PPN‐AV, and PPN‐GSH‐AV scaffolds, whereas only minimal cellular infiltration was observed in the GAV scaffold (Figure 7a). Quantitative analysis (Figure 7c) revealed that the cell density on the PPN‐GSH‐AV (671.7 ± 142.3) scaffold was significantly higher than that on the DPAV and GAV scaffolds. However, no statistically significant difference was observed compared to the PPN‐AV (603.2 ± 164.7). Masson staining revealed the least degradation of the GAV scaffold. In contrast, the DPAV scaffold showed the most severe degradation (Figure 7a). The PPN‐AV and PPN‐GSH‐AV scaffolds exhibited intermediate and comparable degradation resistance, performing considerably better than the DPAV control. Notably, extensive calcium deposition accompanied by severe structural damage and collagen fiber fracture was observed on the GAV scaffold, in contrast to the other groups (Figure 7a, Figure S4). Consistent with these observations, the ICP analysis (Figure S5) confirmed that the calcium content in PPN‐GSH‐AV (0.0008251 ± 0.0001455 µg mL−1) was significantly reduced than that in the DPAV (0.001320 ± 0.0001107 µg mL−1) and GAV (0.003723 ± 0.0002089 µg mL−1) scaffolds.

(f) IL‐10 relative expression of each cell. (Data were presented as mean ± SD, *p< 0.05, **p< 0.01, ***p< 0.001, and ****p< 0.0001.).

In addition, the anti‐inflammatory properties of the scaffolds were assessed by immunohistochemical staining of the pro‐inflammatory cytokines IL‐1β and IL‐6, as well as the anti‐inflammatory cytokine IL‐10. As presented in Figure 7b, strong positive staining for IL‐1β and IL‐6 was observed on the DPAV and GAV scaffolds, whereas only minimal expression was detected on the PPN‐AV and PPN‐GSH‐AV scaffolds. These qualitative findings were corroborated by quantitative statistical analysis (Figure 7d,e). In contrast, the anti‐inflammatory cytokine IL‐10 exhibited an opposite expression pattern: abundant IL‐10 expression was identified on the PPN‐GSH‐AV scaffold, with markedly less expression on the GAV scaffold (Figure 7b, f). Collectively, these results indicate that the PPN‐GSH‐AV modification effectively promotes an anti‐inflammatory microenvironment in vivo.

To further characterize the host immune response, immunofluorescent staining of CD3 and CD68 in Figure 8a,b revealed limited infiltration of CD3+ T cells across all groups, whereas substantial numbers of CD68+ macrophages were observed. And the CD206 staining in Figure 8c indicated that macrophages on PPN‐AV and PPN‐GSH‐AV scaffolds were predominantly of the M2 phenotype. Consistent with this polarization trend, Arg 1 expression was markedly elevated in the PPN‐AV and PPN‐GSH‐AV groups, but slightly in the DPAV and minimal in GAV. In contrast, iNOS expression followed an inverse pattern, further supporting the predominance of the M2 phenotype in the POSS‐modified scaffolds (Figure 8d). Additionally, ROS staining showed strong fluorescence intensity in the DPAV and GAV scaffolds, moderate intensity in the PPN‐AV scaffold, and the lowest intensity in the PPN‐GSH‐AV scaffold (Figure 8e), indicating effective mitigation of oxidative stress in the latter group.

(c) CD206 (highly expressed in M2 macrophages). (d) Arg‐1 (highly expressed in M2 macrophages) and iNOS (highly expressed in M1 macrophages). (e) ROS (indicates the degree of oxidative stress).

Performance In Vitro Pulsatile Flow Testing

The hemodynamic performance of the scaffolds was evaluated under an in vitro pulsatile flow system using both interventional and surgical valve configurations (Figure 9a,h). For the interventional valves, the PPN‐GSH‐AV scaffold exhibited appropriate thickness and EOA (effective orifice area, EOA) in both closed and open states, comparable to those of the DPAV and GAV scaffolds (Figure 9a). As shown in Figure 9b, the regurgitant volume ratios of the PPN‐GSH‐AV and GAV met the acceptance criteria, unlike that of the DPAV valve. However, the EOA of the GAV valve fell outside the qualifying range specified in the ISO 5840 standard, whereas the DPAV and PPN‐GSH‐AV valves complied (Figure 9c). In addition, the mean pressure difference was significantly elevated in both the PPN‐GSH‐AV and GAV valves (Figure 9d).

For the surgical valve, the PPN‐GSH‐AV valve demonstrated full coaptation and smooth opening without noticeable leaflet curling or shrinkage, in contrast to the DPAV valve (Figure 9h,j). Furthermore, the PPN‐GSH‐AV surgical valve satisfied all the critical hemodynamic criteria, including the EOA, the regurgitant volume ratio, and the mean pressure difference, which the DPAV valve failed to meet (Figure 9e,f,g). Finally, both the DPAV and PPN‐GSH‐AV surgical valves exhibited periodic and synchronous variations in aortic pressure, ventricular pressure, and aortic flow, with uniform and stable waveform curves (Figure 9i,k).

Discussion

POSS with various functional grafting chemical substances are widely used in biomaterials, including constructing multifunctional drug delivery systems, cancer treatment, tissue engineering, antibacterial applications, biomedical imaging and so on, which performed satisfactorily [23]. Among them, POSS‐PEG are one of the most common. Previous study indicated that a PEG hydrogel with POSS nanoparticles was used to coat the surface of decellularized heart valve, which enhanced cell adhesion and proliferation, and exhibited excellent hemocompatibility and cytocompatibility [13]. On the basis, we designed and synthesized a new chemical compound POSS‐PEG‐NHS (Figure 1a) according to research of Yazhong Bu, etc [24], which can be used to crosslink the DPAV to prepare a new scaffold possessing the above advantages. For that, ─NHS was considered as a non‐Glut method to avoid the cytotoxicity of aldehyde [16]. And as an inherent antioxidant with sulfhydryl and amino groups [25], GSH will be added not only to regulate microenvironment but also strengthen crosslinking of scaffolds. We envision combining the characteristics of POSS‐PEG and GSH to construct a valve substitute with outstanding endothelial affinity, immune regulation ability, and anticalcification property.

This work started from synthesis and characterization of POSS‐PEG‐NHS. The characteristic signals of POSS‐PEG‐NHS were verified by 1H NMR and FTIR in Figure 1b,c, thereby confirming its molecular structure. PPN‐GSH‐AV scaffold was prepared according to Figure 2b, for proving the combination with GSH, a characteristic peak of ─SH in FTIR. EDS in Figure S1 exhibits a noticeable increase in sulfur content, which may cue the modification of GSH on the scaffold. Then, to verify mechanical properties, uniaxial tensile test indicates both ultimate tensile strength and Young's modulus are significantly enhanced after being modified with POSS‐PEG‐NHS, which is considered due to the inherent mechanical and thermal stability of POSS [26]. And mechanical parameters of PPN‐GSH‐AV are higher than PPN‐AV with no statistically significant difference, relating to the cross‐linking or formation of hydrogen bonds between ─COOH and ─NH2 in GSH, or the formation of disulfide bonds between thiol groups. But under equimolar number of ─NHS and ─CHO groups, mechanical properties of GAV are significantly better than PPN‐AV and PPN‐GSH‐AV. This may relate to the binding rate of the eight functional groups in POSS is low, and not all of them can participate in the reaction. Further, comparison of mechanical properties between PAV and PPN‐GSH‐AV indicates the latter meets the mechanical requirement of native valves. Besides, PPN‐GSH‐AV scaffold was cut, sewn, and ultimately formed interventional and surgical valves to evaluate application value and clinical translation potential. As Figure 9 exhibited, EOA and regurgitant volume ratio of interventional and surgical valves prepared from PPN‐GSH‐AV compliant with ISO5840 standards, proving the appropriate mechanical properties.

Further characterization reveals smaller WCA of PPN‐AV and PPN‐GSH‐AV means the better hydrophilicity in contrast to hydrophobicity of POSS [26], which is attributed to be encapsulated by hydrophilic PEG long chains [27]. And GSH contains multiple hydrophilic functional groups, which are exposed on the material surface when cocrosslinking with DPAV. Water molecules can form hydrogen bonds with these functional groups, making the material surface more hydrophilic. Moreover, Figure 3g,h presented rougher surface of PPN‐GSH‐AV than other groups except GAV, which consistent with the degree of curling and shrinkage of the cross‐linked fibers in Figure 3d. Though, both the better hydrophilicity and rougher surface are beneficial for adhesion and proliferation of HUVECs, functional and structural problems of valve related to platelet adhesion and thrombosis arise subsequently [28]. However, platelets and thrombosis on surface of PPN‐GSH‐AV are reduced (Figure 4a,c), which probably attributes to antiplatelet adhesion property of POSS [29]. Moreover, previous study revealed platelets adhesion was closely related to increased ROS [30]. So, we infer that GSH consumed ROS, thereby inhibiting ROS‐induced platelets adhesion and thrombosis.

Not only excellent hemocompatibility, PPN‐GSH‐AV is also expected to possess terrific biocompatibility and cellular safety. Consistent with expectations, HUVECs vigorously adhere and proliferate on PPN‐GSH‐AV scaffold as time goes on (Figure 4g). Above outcome indicates superb cytotropic and reendothelization capacity brought by POSS‐PEG [9, 10, 13]. Furthermore, free sulfhydryl groups of GSH enhanced the buffering capacity of HUVECs towards ROS, which contributed as well [18]. Similarly, fastest proliferation of Raw264.7 is observed on PPN‐GSH‐AV scaffold (Figure 4h). Though, concentrated death appears on all scaffolds on day 5, resulting of excessive proliferation rate and limited growth space, possibly. Further exploring the impact of PPN‐GSH‐AV on oxidative stress of cells, Figure 5a,b displays significantly reduced expression of ROS and H2O2 in Raw264.7 cells on PPN‐GSH‐AV scaffold. We speculate that modification of GSH simulates the inherent ability of ECM to regulate ROS explanation [31], improving the microenvironment of the scaffold surface while reducing damage to cells.

Next is the in vivo experimental part, HE and Masson staining presented most cells infiltrated into the PPN‐GSH‐AV and higher level of degradation than GAV respectively, proving the significant properties of re‐cellularization and re‐modeling of the PPN‐GSH ‐AV scaffold (Figure S4). It's seen that both PEG and POSS presented outstanding biocompatibility, even PEG was proved that PEGylated nanoparticles presented not only better ECM (extracellular matrix, ECM) penetration and diffusion but also higher cellular uptake rate [32]. Furthermore, a certain degree of degradation is considered beneficial to regenerate and remodel of valve substitutes as well [33]. As previous study concluded, calcification is a major contributor to the structural deterioration and cusp tears of leaflet resulting to the bioprosthetic failure [34]. So, it's crucial for maintaining the normal functions of heart valves by downregulating the calcification. As we desired, von Kossa staining in Figure 7a indicates the significant decrease of calcification in PPN‐GSH‐AV scaffold compared with GAV as well as calcium quantification by ICP in Figure S5, which is attributed to the outstanding anticalcification property of the composite scaffold endowed by POSS [35]. Nor is this all, combination of POSS‐PEG and GSH effectively enhances anti‐inflammatory capacity of PPN‐GSH‐AV [36], which manifests in reduced expression of proinflammatory cytokines (IL‐1ß, IL‐6) and increase expression of anti‐inflammatory cytokine IL‐10 (Figure 7b).

Through deeper exploration, we far discovery the types of cells infiltrated into the scaffolds are macrophages. And high expression of Arg1 verifies macrophages in PPN‐GSH‐AV are mainly anti‐inflammatory M2 type (Figure 8a), which is significant for repairing and re‐modeling [37, 38]. And in Figure 8e, little ROS on PPN‐GSH‐AV means terrific anti‐ROS capacity, which can also enhance anti‐inflammation resulted of regulation of macrophages polarization [39].

Above all, PPN‐GSH‐AV scaffold combines structure of native valve, properties of POSS and antioxidative stress of GSH, those endow it fitted mechanical properties, marvelous hemocompatibility, reendothelization, anti‐inflammation and anti‐calcification capacities. However, the further molecular mechanisms involved had not been investigated. In vitro pulsatile test merely assessed a preliminarily functional status of the valve, and a long‐term fatigue resistance test is essential to further reflect its durability. If conditions permit, above experiments should be further conducted to more comprehensively evaluate the application value.

Conclusion

In this research, a new chemical substitute (POSS‐PEG‐NHS) was prepared, which combines the advantages of both POSS and PEG. On this basis, an ─NHS group was used to crosslink with the amino groups of DPAV as a non‐Glut method, which not only strengthened the mechanical properties but also completely avoided the severe cytotoxicity associated with aldehyde groups. Besides, the modification of GSH functioned as an antioxidant, enhancing resistance to oxidative stress and consolidating the scaffold's reendothelialization capacity. Above all, we synthesized and used POSS‐PEG‐NHS to modify DPAV with GSH to prepare a valve substitute and demonstrated its excellent mechanical properties, hemolysis, cytocompatibility, anti‐ROS, anti‐inflammation, anticalcification, and potential for clinical translation.

Experimental Section

Synthesis and Characterization of POSS‐PEG‐NHS

Synthesis of POSS‐8SH

To a solution of tris‐mercaptopropyltrimethoxysilane (5 mL) in methanol (120 mL), 12 mL of 37% HCl was added dropwise under stirring. After the addition was completed, the reaction mixture was heated to reflux for 24 h. The reactant was then cooled to room temperature and placed at 4°C overnight. Methanol was removed, and the oily product was washed 3 times with cold methanol. The product was then dissolved in dichloromethane (DCM), and the precipitated product was induced by adding iced methanol. The mixture was frozen at low temperature overnight, after which the supernatant was discarded. And the residue was vacuum‐dried to obtain a white oily substance, POSS‐8SH.

Synthesis of POSS‐PEG‐OH

POSS‐8SH (0.254 g, 0.25 mmol), Allyl‐PEG‐OH (5.0 g, 2.5 mmol), and 2,2‐dimethoxy‐2‐phenylacetophenone (0.053 g, 0.4 mmol) were dissolved in anhydrous DCM (40 mL). The reaction mixture was stirred under 365 nm UV light irradiation for 12 h. Upon completion, 40 mL of water was added, and the aqueous phase was separated. The organic phase was further extracted with DCM (3 × 40 mL), and the combined DCM phases were washed with saturated NaCl, then dried over anhydrous MgSO4. After filtration, the solvent was removed under reduced pressure. Finally, it was precipitated by trituration with cold methyl tert‐butyl ether (MTBE), collected by filtration, and lyophilized to afford POSS‐PEG‐OH as a solid.

Synthesis of POSS‐PEG‐COOH

POSS‐PEG‐OH (2 g, 0.12 mmol), succinic anhydride (0.384 g, 3.84 mmol), and 4‐dimethylaminopyridine (DMAP, 0.234 g, 1.92 mmol) were dissolved in anhydrous DCM (200 mL) for 24 h. The reaction mixture was then washed with brine (3 × 40 mL). The organic layer was isolated, then dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate, concentrated in vacuum, recrystallized, filtered, and lyophilized to afford the POSS‐PEG‐COOH.

Synthesis of POSS‐PEG‐NHS

POSS‐PEG‐COOH (2 g, 0.12 mmol), EDCI (0.368 g, 2.4 mmol), and NHS (0.441 g, 3.84 mmol) were dissolved in anhydrous DCM (200 mL) and reacted for 24 h. The reaction was then washed directly with brine (3 × 100 mL). The organic layer was collected, dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate, concentrated in vacuum, recrystallized, filtered, and lyophilized to obtain the final white solid powder POSS‐PEG‐NHS.

Construction of the Scaffolds

Preparation of DPAV

Fresh porcine aortic valves (PAVs) were obtained from a local slaughterhouse and immediately transported to the laboratory. After washing away blood, PAVs were incubated in M3 solution for 24 h (37 °C, 110 rpm), followed by M4 solution for another 24 h. After being thoroughly rinsed with PBS, valves were then treated with nuclease solution for a further 24 h. Ultimately, DPAV was obtained and stored at 4°C in PBS containing 2% penicillin‐streptomycin.

Modification of the Scaffolds

DPAVs without further treatment and those treated with 0.625% glutaraldehyde solution served as the control groups. And white solid powder POSS‐PEG‐NHS was dissolved in distilled water at a concentration of 2.0 g mL−1 to modify DPAV to prepare PPN‐AV scaffold. In addition, GSH was dissolved in deionized water at 0.02 g mL−1 to form the reaction solution, in which PPN‐AV scaffolds were then immersed to generate the PPN‐GSH‐AV scaffold.

Characterization of the Scaffolds

FTIR, XPS, and EDS

Prepared scaffolds were freeze‐dried (LC‐10N‐60A, China) and sectioned into small squares for subsequent characterization. The samples were then subjected to FTIR, XPS, and EDS to confirm the successful modification. Specifically, FTIR was used to detect the characteristic Si─O─Si vibration, while XPS and EDS were employed to identify the presence of silicon in the PPN‐GSH‐AV scaffold.

Mechanical Properties of the Scaffolds

The scaffolds were cut into thin strips of 0.5cm × 2cm. The initial length and thickness of each sample was measured using a vernier caliper prior to mechanical testing. Uniaxial tensile tests were performed using a universal testing system with a 100 N load and 5 mm min−1 extension rate. The ultimate tensile strength and Young´s modulus were derived from the resulting stress‐strain curves.

Morphology and Hydrophilicity of the Scaffolds

The samples were sectioned into 1 cm × 1 cm, gradient dehydrated via immersion in increasing concentrations of ethanol (30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, and 100%) and dried by a freeze‐dryer. The surface was observed by using SEM (TESCAN MIRA LMS). Roughness and hydrophilicity were then measured using the AFM (Bruker Dimension Icon, Germany) and contact angle analyzer (Lauda Scientific LSA100, Germany), respectively. In addition, roughness parameters and water contact angle (WCA) values were analyzed with the specific software.

Hemocompatibility of the Scaffolds In Vitro

PLTs Adhesion of the Scaffolds

Whole blood was collected from Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats and preserved in blood collection tubes containing calcium citrate. Platelet‐rich plasma (PRP) was obtained by centrifuging the whole blood at 1500 rpm for 20 min. Consequently, prepared PRP was diluted to 1 × 108 mL−1 with 0.9% saline. Next, samples were incubated in 500 µL diluted PRP in a 24‐well plate at 37°C for 1 h. After incubation, PRP was carefully removed, and the samples were gently rinsed three times with PBS to remove non‐adherent platelets. For morphological observation, the samples were dried and observed using SEM to visualize PLT adhesion. For quantification, PLTs adhered to the samples were determined using an LDH assay kit (Beyotime, China). The supernatant was collected, whose absorbance at 490 nm was detected by using a microplate reader.

Thrombosis of the Scaffolds

The samples were punched into rounds with a diameter of 8 mm and placed in a 24‐well plate. Subsequently, 500 µL of whole blood collected from SD rats was added to each well and incubated with the samples at 37°C for 30 min. After incubation, the blood was removed, and the samples were gently rinsed three times with PBS. Thrombus formation on the samples was then photographed. For quantitative analysis, 0.5% Trion X‐100 was added to each well and incubated with the thrombi at 37°C for 30 min to lyse the clots. The supernatant was then collected, and the absorbance at 540 nm was measured to determine the relative extent of thrombus formation in each group.

Hemolysis of the Scaffolds

This washing process was repeated until the supernatant became clear. The round samples were first equilibrated in PBS at 37°C for 30 min. Each sample was then incubated with 200 µL of erythrocyte suspension and 1 mL saline under the same conditions. Cytotoxicity, Cell Proliferation, and Endothelization of the Scaffolds

Sterilized samples (diameter: 6 mm) were placed in a 96‐well plate. HUVECs were seeded onto the scaffolds at a density of 1 × 104 /well. After 48 h of culture, the samples were rinsed with PBS, transferred to a new 96‐well plate, and incubated with 10% CCK‐8 working solution (HYCEZMBIO, China) for 2 h. The absorbance at 450 nm of the supernatant was measured to assess cytotoxicity. Furthermore, a cell proliferation curve was generated by the CCK‐8 assay. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured at different time points (day 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, and 11). On day 7, samples were washed with deionized water, then stained using a Calcein AM/PI Assay (Beyotime, China) for 15 min and imaged under a confocal microscope to evaluate reendothelialization potential. Besides, the same experimental procedure was applied to assess the cytocompatibility of the scaffolds with Raw264.7 cells.

Ability of Antioxidative Stress of the Scaffolds

Raw264.7 cells were seeded onto the samples in a 96‐well plate at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well and incubated at 37 °C for 12 h to allow cell attachment. The culture medium was then replaced with medium containing 300 µM H2O2, followed by incubation for 6–12 h to induce oxidative stress. After removal of the medium, the samples were washed three times with serum‐free medium and incubated with 100 µL of 10 µM DCHF‐DA per well for 20 min. Intracellular ROS levels were visualized using a confocal microscope. In a separate experiment, the antioxidative stress capacity of the scaffolds was further evaluated using the same procedure, except that oxidative stress was induced in Raw264.7 cells (1 × 105 cells/well) by treatment with 100 ng mL−1 LPS and 20 ng mL−1 IFN‐γ.

Calcification and Inflammation of the Scaffolds in Subcutaneous Implantation Models

The samples were cut into 1 cm × 1 cm squares, sterilized in 75% ethanol, and rinsed with saline prior to implantation. A midline abdominal incision was made on each rat, and the scaffolds were implanted subcutaneously on both lateral sides. The four corners were secured with 4‐0 nonabsorbable sutures. After 30 days, the samples were harvested and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. Subsequently, they were stained with HE, Masson and Von kossa to determine recellularization, degradation, and anticalcification extent, respectively. Furthermore, immunohistochemical (including IL‐1ß, IL‐10, and IL‐6) and immunofluorescence (including Arg1, iNOS, CD68, CD3, and ROS) staining were performed to assess the anti‐inflammation and immune cell infiltration.

Construction of Surgical Valves and the Pulsatile Flow Testing In Vitro

The complete valve units were decellularized and subsequently modified with POSS‐PEG‐NHS and GSH to fabricate the bioprosthetic valves. After fabrication, the valves were mounted on supporting stents and subjected to a pulsatile flow test under a cardiac output of 5 L min−1. During testing, the states of closing and opening were photographed by the high‐speed camera to observe any abnormal leaflet curling or inadequate coaptation. Pressure waveforms across the valves were recorded over multiple cardiac cycles. Key hydrodynamic parameters, including mean pressure difference (MPD), effective orifice area (EOA), and regurgitant volume ratio (RV) underwent statistical analysis to determine compliance with the ISO 5840 standards. Overall, the comprehensive functional performance of the scaffolds was assessed to determine their potential for clinical translational.

Statistical Analysis

Mean ± standard deviation was used to present experimental results and GraphPad Prism 9 was used to analyze. Comparisons between two groups with normally distributed data were analyzed with an unpaired, two‐tailed Student t‐test, and multiple comparisons were analyzed by one‐way or two‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Statistically significant differences were considered when p value < 0.05. At least three replicates were made in the experiments.
