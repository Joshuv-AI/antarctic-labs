Characterization of Strategies to Enhance the Provision of Holistic Services Across Three Community‐Based Organizations in King County, Washington, United States

Sarah A Shaw, Anjuli Wagner, Alejandra Grillo Roa, Enoch, Martha Zuniga, Steven Sawyer, Richard Williams, Taylor Brown, Francis Slaughter, Taylor Rapson, David A Katz

Abstract

ABSTRACTIntroductionAddressing social determinants of health within HIV services is essential to achieve HIV epidemic control in the United States. Community‐based organizations (CBOs) are uniquely positioned to implement integrated services, particularly for under‐resourced and marginalized communities. However, effective strategies to support integrated services are needed for these settings. In a community‐academic partnership, we applied implementation science tools to identify, prioritize and characterize strategies supporting three CBOs’ goals to enhance integrated services and reduce health inequities in King County, Washington, United States.MethodsFrom November 2021 to June 2022, we facilitated nominal group technique methods with each CBO to generate strategies aligned with conditions CBOs deemed necessary for successful integration. Staff evaluated strategies’ feasibility and effectiveness using 5‐point Likert scales (Feasibility: 1 = Very hard to do with current resources to 5 = Very easy; Effectiveness: 1 = Large negative to 5 = Large positive impact). Go‐Zone plots comparing strategies’ feasibility and effectiveness scores were generated to inform implementation prioritization. We mapped strategies to Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) thematic clusters and Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) constructs.ResultsThis participatory process generated 118 strategies perceived as highly impactful (mean = 4.7, range = 4.6−5.0) and with mixed feasibility (mean = 3.3, range = 3.2−4.6). Strategies most frequently aimed to improve organizational structural characteristics, staff access to knowledge/information, communications, implementer and community engagement, reflecting and evaluating, and teaming. All nine ERIC thematic clusters were represented, most commonly change infrastructure, train/educate relevant groups, develop interrelationships, use evaluative and iterative strategies, and engage consumers. Highly feasible and impactful strategies often addressed knowledge access and engagement, while fewer addressed structural characteristics and teaming. We observed patterns in barrier‐strategy mapping (CFIR‐ERIC) and in strategy feasibility and impact when reviewing construct combinations.ConclusionsImplementing integrated services requires systems‐level approaches that strengthen organizational infrastructure and respond to community priorities. Despite diverse organizational contexts, a core set of determinants and strategy types emerged, suggesting their importance for integrated services. Implementation science frameworks were valuable for translating context‐specific strategies into potentially generalizable takeaways about determinants and strategy types, though they may be unnecessary for strategy and determinant identification with CBOs. Examining the feasibility and effectiveness of strategy types addressing unique combinations of determinants may strengthen barrier‐strategy mapping.Keywords: community‐based participatory research, community health centres, health equity, HIV, holistic health, implementation science

Introduction

Addressing social determinants of health (SDH) within HIV services is essential to reduce inequities among populations most impacted by HIV and reach epidemic control in the United States [, , , , , , , , , ]. A recent review and meta‐analysis observed better HIV care cascade and non‐HIV‐related outcomes in settings with integrated SDH services compared to settings where services were separate []. Additionally, integration of pre‐exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) and other services may improve PrEP uptake and adherence, reduce HIV acquisition and facilitate engagement in other health services [, , , , ]. Integrating HIV services with other programmes is often feasible and cost‐effective, highlighting health outcome and process efficiency gains at multiple health system levels [, ].

Integrated service delivery models are responsive to the complex and multi‐level needs of communities []. Community‐based organizations (CBOs) are uniquely positioned to provide such holistic and person‐centred care to under‐resourced and marginalized communities, given their historical and ongoing roles in the HIV epidemic, long‐standing relationships with communities, extensive contextual knowledge, recognition of the inherent complexity of health and wellness, and ability to provide culturally appropriate services [, , , , ]. However, strategies supporting integrated services within CBOs remain a key knowledge gap []. Prior work focused on traditional healthcare settings and patients, such as improving accessibility of services, increasing patient awareness regarding health topics and service availability, and training providers on SDH []. Research is needed to examine what systems and organizational‐level strategies facilitate integrated services within CBOs.

A community‐academic partnership between University of Washington (UW) researchers and three CBOs was supported by the US Ending the HIV Epidemic (EHE) initiative. One project applied implementation science (IS) tools to support the CBOs’ goals to enhance integrated services (integrating HIV and other social and health programming) and reduce health inequities within their diverse communities. This manuscript describes a strategy mapping analysis of CBO‐generated strategies for facilitating integrated services. We conceptualized integrated services as the innovation, as it comprises a package of interventions. We were interested in identifying specific strategies to facilitate this model. The analysis aimed to examine CBO priorities regarding integrated service delivery, identify determinants of such services and understand what strategy types can address those determinants, responding to calls to apply empirical data to further our theoretical knowledge of IS frameworks across different contexts []. We also reflect on using IS frameworks to characterize CBO‐generated strategies to strengthen practice‐academic partnerships by balancing theoretical rigour and real‐world implementation processes [].

Methods

Overview

This community‐academic partnership focused on building relationships and consensus around enhancing integrated services, including systematically generating and prioritizing tailored structures, processes, actions and changes (referred to as strategies throughout) to facilitate these goals. We present strategy generation process results based on data collected between 15 November 2021, and 21 June 2022. A preliminary evaluation of this planning project can be found elsewhere []. The University of Washington Human Subjects Division (UW HSD) determined this research to be exempt from human subjects regulations. Participation was voluntary, and CBO staff received an informational consent document describing study activities and participation options per UW HSD requirements for exempt research.

Study Setting

This study was conducted among three CBOs in King County, Washington, United States—Entre Hermanos, Seattle's LGBTQ+ Center and POCAAN. In addition to HIV prevention and treatment services, these community‐led organizations also provide youth art programmes, restorative justice programming, housing support, non‐medical case management and legal services, among others. Their services primarily support Hispanic/Latine, Lesbian, Gay, Bisexual, Transgender, Queer/Questioning, Intersex, Asexual (LGBTQIA+), and Black, Indigenous, People of Colour (BIPOC) communities, respectively. All CBO staff were eligible to participate.

Nominal Group Technique

Nominal group technique (NGT) methods [, ] were applied to generate strategies to facilitate integrated service delivery for each CBO. Widely used in healthcare for strategy and research prioritization [, ], NGT is also particularly valuable for exploratory and applied research [, ]. This participatory, structured, consensus‐building approach applies individual and group phases to ensure all participant voices are heard and valued equally and evaluate ideas systematically [, , ]. NGT is advantageous for groups with large time demands, allowing quick generation and transparent prioritization of many diverse ideas [, , ]. Our NGT process comprised of five steps (Figure , adapted from [, ]) conducted across two in‐person meetings for CBO A and two Zoom meetings for CBOs B and C. UW staff (SS, DAK, AW) facilitated the process with each CBO. Idea generation was grounded in a previously generated Key Driver Diagram (KDD) [], which outlined conditions necessary for successful service integration specific to each CBO (primary drivers) and an initial set of strategies to facilitate and/or create those conditions [].

Strategy Evaluation

In step 4, CBO staff scored each strategy's feasibility and impact through a REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) [, ] survey. Before survey distribution, UW staff refined the strategy list based on step 3 group discussions. Informed by prior work with healthcare workers [] and discussions with CBO leadership, feasibility was measured as “How easy would it be to do [strategy] given current resources,” and impact as “How big of an impact do you think this [strategy] would have on [organization's] goal to [organization‐specific goal from KDD].” Staff evaluated feasibility and impact using 5‐item Likert scales (Figure ). We then generated Go‐Zone plots [], scatterplots categorizing strategies into one of four quadrants based on mean feasibility and impact scores: (1) highly feasible/highly impactful (the “go‐zone,” strategies scoring higher than the mean on both feasibility and impact); (2) less feasible/highly impactful; (3) highly feasible/less impactful; and (4) less feasible/less impactful. We presented results to staff during the second meeting (step 5) to inform strategy prioritization.

Strategy Mapping

NGT‐generated strategies were mapped to constructs from the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) version 2.0 [] and thematic clusters from Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) [, ]. CBO‐generated strategies often contained multiple ideas and were not reflected in individual ERIC strategies. This mapping aimed to translate these context‐specific data into generalizable insights about multi‐level determinants that influence integrated services implementation in CBOs, types of strategies that may facilitate integrated services and inform recommendations for applying IS tools in community settings.

One UW team member (SS) mapped each strategy to ≥1 KDD primary driver, CFIR construct (implementation determinants) and ERIC thematic cluster (strategy types) []. This primary mapper and another UW researcher (DAK) met weekly to discuss decisions until reaching consensus, similar to qualitative codebook development. To enhance reliability, the primary mapper conducted multiple rounds of mapping, ensuring all decisions were applied consistently. We noted similar themes across organizations’ primary drivers and generated combined drivers presented here. For implementation determinants, we focused on the inner setting, outer setting and implementation process domains. Table presents specific concepts with definitions and examples. We excluded individuals and innovation domains given little distinction between CFIR‐defined roles in our context, and we did not collect data on innovation perceptions, respectively. Strategies could map to multiple drivers, domains, constructs and clusters to more precisely capture strategy interconnectedness and complexity. We prioritized how CBOs conceptualized strategies; it was not feasible or appropriate to force strategies into discrete categories as external facilitators.

Analysis

R Studio (version 2023.12.1 Build 402) was used for descriptive analyses. We combined strategies across organizations, although in practice, CBOs generated and evaluated strategies independently.

We first calculated how many strategies mapped to each driver, domain, construct and thematic cluster to understand which determinants and strategy types were most common overall and for each CBO. Additionally, we assessed frequencies of combinations of determinants and of strategy types.

Next, for strategies mapping to each domain, construct and thematic cluster (hereafter collectively referred to as concepts), we calculated their distribution across the four feasibility‐impact quadrants. Strategies related to a specific concept were considered to have a typical feasibility and impact profile (e.g. highly feasible and less impactful) if ≥50% of those strategies fell within the relevant quadrant. We also evaluated feasibility and impact individually, applying a ≥75% threshold to indicate strategies related to a concept were typically considered highly or less feasible/impactful overall and for each CBO. Additionally, we calculated strategy distributions by determinant and strategy type within each quadrant.

Lastly, we reviewed combinations of CFIR constructs and ERIC thematic clusters observed across strategies. Preliminary results suggested patterns in feasibility and impact were more pronounced when evaluating strategies by determinant combinations. Therefore, we conducted a post hoc analysis examining whether certain strategy types were more common, feasible, and/or impactful for the most frequent inner setting and implementation process determinant combinations.

Results were shared with CBO teams during routine project and dedicated discussion meetings, informing strategy mapping, conference abstracts, presentations and the manuscript. Additional details on the process for generating insights and gathering manuscript feedback are available elsewhere [].

Results

Overview

Approximately 40 staff across the three organizations participated in this planning project. We generated 118 strategies with 30, 36 and 52 identified by CBOs A, B and C, respectively. Strategies aligned with eight primary drivers of integrated services, with the most frequent including community engagement (n = 30), data systems (n = 30) and positive work environment: defined roles & transparency (n = 24) (Figure ). Table presents examples of strategies and their corresponding drivers. On average, CBO staff expected strategies to have a large positive impact on their goals to integrate service delivery (mean score = 4.7, standard deviation [SD] = 0.2, Range = 3.5−5.0) and be between “neither hard nor easy” and “somewhat easy” to implement given current resources (mean score = 3.4, SD = 0.5, Range = 1.8−4.6). Average feasibility and impact scores were similar across CBOs (feasibility range = 3.1−3.5; impact range = 4.6−4.7).

Types of Strategies

This process generated strategies related to all nine ERIC thematic clusters, most commonly change infrastructure (26%, n = 31), train/educate relevant groups (26%, n = 31), develop interrelationships with relevant groups and collaborators (19%, n = 22), use evaluative and iterative strategies (17%, n = 20), and engage consumers (13%, n = 16) (Figure ). These clusters were the five most common for all CBOs. The three most commonly observed combinations of strategy types were adapt and tailor to context and change infrastructure (n = 7), engage consumers and use evaluative and iterative strategies (n = 6), and engage consumers and train/educate relevant groups (n = 4) (Table ).

Implementation Determinants

Strategies addressed determinants across all CFIR domains, with over half (56%, n = 66) addressing determinants in multiple domains. Strategies most frequently addressed ≥1 implementation determinant within the inner setting (84%, n = 99), followed by implementation process (59%, n = 70) and outer setting (14%, n = 17) domains (Figure ). Across domains, strategies most frequently aimed to address: structural characteristics (n = 52), access to knowledge/information (n = 22), communications (n = 20) (all belonging to the inner setting), engaging (n = 19), reflecting and evaluating (n = 18), and teaming (n = 18) (implementation process). Within the outer setting, the most common determinants were partnerships and connections (65%, n = 11) and local attitudes (23%, n = 4). We observed minor variation in which constructs were most frequently addressed when comparing CBOs (Figure ). Across 20 strategies addressing multiple inner setting determinants, we observed seven unique combinations, the most common observed for ≥2 CBOs. There were no clear patterns within the implementation process and outer setting domains (Table ). Among 55 strategies addressing inner setting and implementation process determinants, the most common construct pairs were observed for all three CBOs (Table ).

Table provides examples of strategies addressing determinants across CFIR domains and constructs. Briefly, strategies focused on structural characteristics included improving data systems to facilitate data‐driven decision‐making across programmes and ensure clients receive individualized care. These strategies also aimed to establish positive work environments by defining clear roles and expectations for staff and facilitating transparency and autonomy regarding hiring, funding and work distribution. Strategies to increase access to knowledge/information reflected the need to invest in staff learning and included training, data access, developing standardized processes and planning, and improving collaboration across teams. Moreover, monitoring and evaluation strategies assessed both the implementation context and priorities of community members through surveys, routine community town halls and standardizing data collection elements across programmes.

Perceived Feasibility and Impact

There were a few patterns in feasibility or impact (separately or by combined quadrants) when organized by each concept (Table ). Notably, strategies addressing access to knowledge/information were typically considered highly feasible (82%), whereas those addressing available resources were typically less feasible (21% highly feasible). Strategies focused on tailoring services and activities were typically highly impactful (86%). Strategies categorized as engaging consumers were often highly feasible and highly impactful (50%). No patterns emerged across CBOs (Figure ).

Examining common construct combinations and cluster combinations may reveal more consistent feasibility and impact patterns than examining concepts individually (Table ). For example, the feasibility and impact of strategies addressing structural characteristics varied depending on the other inner setting construct with which they were aligned: strategies also addressing available resources were never highly feasible; those also addressing access to knowledge/information were typically highly feasible and highly impactful; and those addressing communications were typically highly impactful. Across inner setting and implementation process domains, strategies were typically highly feasible when focused on access to knowledge/information and planning, as well as on communications and engaging. Despite high perceived feasibility, strategies focused on communications and teaming were often considered less impactful. Alternatively, strategies addressing structural characteristics and assessing context were typically less feasible, despite high expected impact. Lastly, across strategy types, strategies categorized as adapting and tailoring to context and changing infrastructure were typically less impactful, and those related to engaging consumers and training/educating relevant groups were typically less feasible and highly impactful.

Strategies in the Go‐Zone (highly feasible, highly impactful) for all CBOs addressed access to knowledge/information and structural characteristics (inner setting), and engaging, planning and tailoring (implementation process), and were characterized as adapt and tailor to context, change infrastructure, engage consumers, train/educate relevant groups, and use evaluative and iterative strategies (Figure and Table ). Figure presents the distribution of determinants by CFIR domain and of strategy types across feasibility/impact quadrants.

CFIR‐ERIC Matching

Each CFIR construct was nearly always observed with multiple strategy types (Figure ), limiting the utility of reviewing 1:1 combinations of these concepts, although a few patterns emerged. For example, the majority of strategies focused on access to knowledge/information were categorized as training/educating relevant groups, and those focused on reflecting and evaluating were predominantly categorized as use evaluative and iterative strategies. Additionally, our limited data suggest certain strategy types may be more common and/or perceived as more feasible and/or impactful for specific determinant combinations (Table ). For instance, strategies characterized as engaging consumers may be perceived as more impactful for improving communications and promoting engagement in intervention delivery compared to strategies characterized as training/educating relevant groups. This strategy type may also be more feasible for addressing structural characteristics and reflecting and evaluating, compared to strategies related to using evaluative and iterative strategies.

Discussion

Situated within a community‐academic partnership, this participatory process generated 118 strategies aligned with a range of CFIR determinants, representing all nine ERIC thematic clusters, and perceived as highly impactful and with mixed feasibility for facilitating integrated services to advance health equity within CBOs.

Exploring multi‐level determinants of integrated services for CBOs addresses an important gap in public health programming to reduce HIV inequities in the United States []. Notably, separating strategies that facilitate integrated services from those that support CBOs’ overall mission and operations was not feasible or practical. Despite unique leadership structures, programming, data collection processes and organizational cultures, a core set of determinants and strategy types emerged as particularly prominent across CBOs, suggesting their central role in organizational change to support integrated services. Go‐Zone strategies were also similar across CBOs, presenting opportunities for co‐learning and collaboration and suggesting funding priorities. These commonalities may reflect that CBOs receive the majority of HIV funding from the same state‐ and county‐based sources, have similar programmatic experience with under‐resourced communities and have a history of collaboration.

This core set of determinants aligns with those observed in other HIV service integration settings, with a few exceptions [, ]. Strengthening organizational infrastructure to improve data, administrative, and monitoring and evaluation processes is critical for integrated services [, ]. These structural changes and improved communication across implementers facilitate service alignment with community priorities and meaningful engagement of relevant parties in programme planning, foundational components of integrated services [, ]. Training represents an often feasible strategy [, , ], yet its effectiveness is frequently overestimated when delivered once rather than routinely [] or without complementary strategies [, ]. Partnerships within the health system workforce and with external partners facilitate ongoing learning and skill development [, ]. Shared vision, organizational philosophy and mission alignment are key for building collaborative and motivated teams necessary for integrated services [, , ]. Unlike settings where staff were previously less involved in HIV services and often more resistant to integration [], few strategies explicitly targeted organizational culture and mission alignment. Instead, strategies operationalized long‐embedded shared cultures around learning, supporting staff and community members, and equity within CBOs. Additionally, few strategies addressed outer setting determinants, despite the influence of economic, political and health system factors on implementation []. Nevertheless, feasibility and impact patterns for organizational‐level determinants may reflect broader environmental constraints. Funding restricted to specific service provision activities limits CBOs’ capacity to implement sustainable systems supporting organizational operations and staff wellbeing, and funder‐mandated data systems constrain CBOs’ access to and control over patient‐ and service‐level data.

While integrated service delivery is heavily context‐dependent [], resulting in tailored CBO‐specific strategies, these data suggest determinants and strategy types influencing integrated services may be generalizable to similar contexts. Overall, strategies reflected the organizational systems and structures needed to implement integrated services, which may apply to integration regardless of service types. Our results substantiate the need for a system‐strengthening approach to facilitate sustainable implementation of integrated services [], including dedicated financial investments in organizational infrastructure and human resources to improve efficiency and better support staff [, , , ].

Our experience using CFIR and ERIC with community partners offers critical reflections regarding theory‐driven implementation research in community‐based settings. Consistent with prior work, our results suggest IS frameworks may not always be pragmatic or necessary when working with community partners []. Definitions and terminology related to determinants and implementation strategies do not necessarily resonate outside academic and research settings [], and innovation deliverers may not conceptualize them as distinct concepts []. While applying frameworks after strategy generation differs from best practices [, ], the decision to first use KDDs and NGT was grounded in community‐based participatory research principles [, , ], ensuring determinants language reflected CBOs’ cultures and staff clearly understood the strategies. Consequently, strategies aligned with context‐specific high‐priority determinants and staff could make informed decisions regarding strategies’ feasibility and impact, critical elements for optimizing strategy implementation [, ].

This project exemplifies an approach for merging scientific and practice priorities that centres on community perspectives. Importantly, the researchers’ deep understanding of CBO goals, processes and history strengthened the strategy mapping analysis. Ultimately, strategies addressed a comprehensive range of CFIR determinants and represented all ERIC thematic clusters without imposing IS frameworks upfront, which CBO staff felt would have been more burdensome and less accessible. Staff valued thinking critically about their challenges without being constrained by a framework, meeting partners where they were, not where researchers wanted them to be. Simultaneously, they valued the application of IS frameworks after data collection for establishing transferable lessons. IS frameworks could also be applied with community partners to bridge gaps during strategy generation by probing on additional hypothesized determinants. Mapping community‐generated strategies to IS frameworks may help prioritize strategies for testing by reviewing literature for effectiveness evidence in similar settings.

This project provides insight into barrier‐strategy matching, a prominent implementation science discussion topic [, ]. Evidence linking ERIC and CFIR constructs is limited [, , , ], and determinants frameworks have been criticized as lists of disconnected concepts [, ]. Existing tools attempt to facilitate barrier‐strategy mapping, including the CFIR‐ERIC matching tool [, ]. However, substantial heterogeneity in perceptions of ERIC strategy alignment with CFIR constructs suggests few consistent associations between strategies and determinants [, ]. Our results suggest barrier‐strategy mapping may be more effective when considering determinant interrelationships compared to 1:1 matching and that multiple strategies can likely address specific determinants []. This heterogeneity may be less satisfying to implementation scientists and clinical providers within hospital settings who often request clear implementation guidance for integrated services [], but may better reflect CBO experiences and facilitate shared decision‐making with researchers. CBO staff expressed that generic guidance can be perceived as directive and is less helpful given their unique organizational contexts. Instead, they suggested that a menu of options to choose from would be more useful and respect their contextual expertise. Exploring whether these patterns are consistently observed in different implementation contexts will benefit future implementation science research and practice. Additionally, pairs of determinant combinations and strategy types may help elucidate mechanisms of action in different contexts, thereby supporting selection and tailoring of strategies to more effectively and efficiently influence implementation compared to generalized strategies [, ]. Given the rapid shift towards HIV service integration in low‐ and high‐resource settings, these results provide important community perspectives on what is necessary to ensure these efforts are sustainable and apply a rights‐based approach [].

This exploratory analysis has limitations. While the three CBOs have diverse organizational structure and programming, our results may not be applicable to all CBOs. Perceived feasibility and impact may not reflect actual feasibility and effectiveness; however, findings informed a follow‐up study evaluating prioritized strategies in these settings. Our feasibility measure focused on implementation, given current resources and may not capture the full context. As cell counts were typically too small for statistical inference when disaggregating data by construct and CBO, we identified meaningful patterns through data visualization and summary approaches that resonated with CBOs. The thresholds we applied to identify patterns in our data may not be relevant for all analyses. In sensitivity analyses, higher thresholds identified fewer patterns but yielded similar themes. The determinants and strategy types likely do not capture the full range of perspectives from community members, clients and policy makers. Although strategy mapping relied on researcher interpretation, multiple steps were implemented to enhance rigour and ensure findings reflected CBOs’ experiences and priorities.

Conclusions

This community‐academic partnership identified implementation strategies to support integrated services within CBOs and reduce health inequities. Implementing integrated services requires systems‐level approaches that strengthen organizational infrastructure and are responsive to community priorities. This project suggests that IS frameworks may not be pragmatic when working with community partners to identify implementation determinants and strategies. Applying this theoretical lens after strategy generation proved valuable for translating context‐specific data into generalizable insights. Moving forward, IS may benefit from examining the feasibility and effectiveness of strategies addressing unique combinations of determinants within specific contexts and developing a clearer understanding of mechanisms linking determinants and strategies.
