Association of Diabetes Mellitus and Malnutrition with Aspergillus IgG and Chronic Pulmonary Aspergillosis in Pulmonary Tuberculosis Patients: A Retrospective Study
Authors: Jianping Zhang, Xinghua Shen, Jing Liu, Junchi Xu, Lijun Huang, Huanglei Ni, Peijun Tang, Yanjun Feng, Meiying Wu, Cuilin Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo examine the association of diabetes mellitus (DM) and nutritional status with the levels of Aspergillus IgG and diagnosis of chronic pulmonary aspergillosis (CPA) in patients with pulmonary tuberculosis.Patients and methodsThis retrospective study included hospitalized patients with pulmonary tuberculosis who received Aspergillus IgG testing at the Fifth People’s Hospital of Suzhou between February 2019 and August 2023. The patients were divided into DM and non-DM groups based on established diagnostic markers, including fasting plasma glucose (FPG), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), or the clinical history of antidiabetic treatments. DM, body mass index (BMI), serum albumin, blood Aspergillus IgG, and CPA diagnostic information were collected from the patient charts.ResultsThis study included 1087 patients, including 188 who had DM. The overall proportion of patients diagnosed with CPA was 9.6% (17.6% in DM vs. 7.9% in non-DM). The overall detection rate of Aspergillus IgG >80 AU/mL was 24.1% (25.5% in DM vs. 23.8% in non-DM). The multivariable analysis showed that BMI <18.5 kg/m2 (OR=1.827, 95% CI: 1.160–2.876, p=0.009), DM (OR=2.718, 95% CI: 1.694–4.361, p<0.001), obsolete pulmonary tuberculosis (OR=4.644, 95% CI: 2.658–8.115, p<0.001), and albumin <35 g/L (OR=1.943, 95% CI: 1.264–2.987, p=0.002) were independently associated with CPA. Furthermore, the proportion of confirmed CPA in those with DM and/or BMI <18.5 kg/m2 was higher than in non-DM patients with BMI <18.5 kg/m2 (15.3% vs. 5.8%, p<0.001), while the rate in the DM and/or albumin <35 g/L group was higher than the non-DM with albumin ≥35 g/L group (14.9% vs.5.9%, p<0.001).ConclusionBMI <18.5 kg/m2, DM, obsolete pulmonary tuberculosis, and albumin <35 g/L were independently associated with CPA in patients hospitalized for tuberculosis. Screening and early intervention for CPA should be strengthened in this high-risk population.Keywords: pulmonary tuberculosis, diabetes mellitus, malnutrition, Aspergillus IgG, pulmonary aspergillosis, body mass index, serum albumin, diagnostic threshold

Introduction

Aspergillosis refers to infection with Aspergillus species, a group of filamentous fungal molds endemic worldwide. Aspergillus species are normally found in soil and decomposing matter, but numerous species can cause disease in humans. Aspergillus fumigatus is the most common.1,2 The clinical spectrum of pulmonary aspergillosis is broad, encompassing several distinct entities including invasive pulmonary aspergillosis (IPA), chronic pulmonary aspergillosis (CPA), allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA), and overlap syndrome.3 This study focuses specifically on CPA, a chronic infectious process that is pathophysiologically distinct from the acute angioinvasion of IPA and the hypersensitivity reaction of ABPA. Aspergillosis typically occurs in immunocompromised patients, including those with chronic infections, weakening the immune system.1 Hence, patients with tuberculosis are prone to CPA. The estimated global incidence of the burden of tuberculosis-associated CPA is about 1.2 million patients per year.4 The symptoms and signs of CPA are relatively mild, but the CPA-related 5-year mortality can be as high as 80%.5 Therefore, the early detection and accurate diagnosis of CPA are crucial to preventing and treating the disease.

The diagnosis of pulmonary aspergillosis is mostly based on symptoms, imaging, and laboratory tests, but discriminating CPA from other pulmonary diseases, such as pulmonary tuberculosis and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), can be difficult, delaying effective treatments.3,6 Therefore, specific laboratory tests for Aspergillus infection are crucial. The Aspergillus-specific immunoglobulin G (IgG) is a specific immune response that can be detected in the blood after Aspergillus infection. In 2016, the European Society of Clinical Microbiology and Infectious Diseases (ESCMID), the European Respiratory Society (ERS), and the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) recommended Aspergillus IgG as the main diagnostic indicator for non-immunocompromised patients with CPA.7 Aspergillus IgG can also be used to diagnose IPA and ABPA.8,9 The guidelines suggest that the Aspergillus antibody test should be performed on patients with CPA before undertaking invasive investigations such as bronchoscopy.8 Unfortunately, the optimal cutoff value for Aspergillus-specific IgG testing appears to vary with race, geographical location, climate differences, and exposure.10,11 A prospective study in the Chinese population showed that using the IgG <80 AU/mL as the negative cutoff, the sensitivity and specificity of Aspergillus-specific IgG tests were 84.1% and 89.6%, respectively, for CPA.12 Unfortunately, only a small proportion of the participants had pulmonary tuberculosis, and the optimal cutoff value in the context of tuberculosis remains unknown.

China has high burdens of diabetes mellitus (with an overall prevalence of 11.2% in adults)13 and pulmonary tuberculosis (prevalence of 850.15 per 100,000).14 Tuberculosis and CPA can co-occur in the same patients.15 Although previous studies have established diabetes mellitus as a risk factor for chronic pulmonary aspergillosis following tuberculosis,16 its epidemiological and clinical implications within a large Chinese cohort of hospitalized patients with tuberculosis remain to be thoroughly evaluated. Furthermore, it is currently unclear whether systemic metabolic and nutritional heterogeneity influences the diagnostic performance of Aspergillus-specific IgG assays. The nutritional status is also associated with the risks of tuberculosis and aspergillosis.17 However, its specific effect on Aspergillus IgG seropositivity in this population requires further clarification.

Therefore, this study aimed to examine the association of DM and nutritional status with the levels of Aspergillus IgG and diagnosis of CPA in a large cohort of patients with pulmonary tuberculosis, and to explore the differential diagnostic performance of Aspergillus IgG among these distinct clinical subgroups.

Methods

Study Design and Patients

This retrospective cohort study included patients hospitalized at the Fifth People’s Hospital of Suzhou between February 1, 2019, and August 1, 2023. For those hospitalized multiple times, only data from the first hospitalization were analyzed. The study has been approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the Fifth People’s Hospital of Suzhou (#K-2023-001-001). The requirement for individual consent was waived by the committee due to the retrospective nature of the study.

The inclusion criteria were 1) males or females of any age, 2) diagnosed with active or obsolete pulmonary tuberculosis according to the Diagnostic Criteria for Pulmonary Tuberculosis (WS288-2017).18 The diagnosis of active tuberculosis was confirmed through bacteriological, pathological, radiographic, or clinical evidence. The definition of obsolete tuberculosis is as follows: the absence of clinical symptoms or signs related to active tuberculosis, a negative bacteriological examination, and the presence of radiographic calcified, strip-shaped, or sclerotic lesions,19 and 3) underwent peripheral blood Aspergillus IgG testing during hospitalization. The exclusion criteria were: 1) coinfection with non-tuberculous mycobacterial pulmonary disease (NTM), 2) end-stage malignancy; 3) severe heart failure (New York Heart Association functional class III to IV);20 4) severe hepatic cirrhosis (Child-Pugh class C);21 5) end-stage renal disease (Chronic Kidney Disease Stage 5, per the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO 2012) guideline),22 or 6) missing key data (ie, body mass index (BMI) or albumin). The reason for excluding severe heart, liver, and renal diseases is that many biomarkers (eg, albumin, IgM, and IgG) may be affected by severe heart, liver, and renal diseases, which would introduce bias and affect the accuracy of the statistical analyses, especially when adjusting for confounding factors in the multivariable analyses. Therefore, the patients with severe heart, liver, and renal diseases were excluded to reduce the risk of bias and confounding and to ensure the accuracy of the statistical analysis.

Data Collection

The data were collected from the patient charts: age, sex, BMI, diabetes, tuberculosis type, other chronic pulmonary diseases, liver disease, hypertension, renal insufficiency, smoking status, alcohol use status, pulmonary cavities, albumin, and Aspergillus IgG. Aspergillus IgG was measured using the Aspergillus fumigates IgG Quantitative Test kit (Dynamiker Biotechnology (Tianjin) Co., Ltd., Tianjin, China), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.12 The thresholds provided by the manufacturer are already for the diagnosis of CPA: >120 AU/mL is positive, <80 AU/mL is negative, and 80–120 AU/mL is borderline. In the present study, >80 AU/mL was considered positive, as in a previous study. The test results from the blood draw performed on the next morning after hospitalization were used for analysis. The patients with diabetes mellitus were defined as those meeting the diagnostic criteria of the Guideline for the Prevention and Treatment of Type 1 and Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus in China (incorporating specific diagnostic markers: fasting plasma glucose [FPG] ≥7.0 mmol/L, 2-h plasma glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L during an oral glucose tolerance test, or glycated hemoglobin [HbA1c] ≥6.5%), or being treated with antidiabetic agents or insulin.23,24 The screening cutoff of Aspergillus IgG test was >80 AU/mL according to prior study focus on CPA diagnosis.12 The final diagnosis of CPA was confirmed or clinically diagnosed according to the Expert Consensus on the Diagnosis and Treatment of Pulmonary Fungal Diseases25 and Western guidelines on aspergillosis,7,26 based on the following criteria: 1) >3 months of pulmonary symptoms or chronic disease, including cough, sputum, hemoptysis, dyspnea, and other signs and symptoms of lower respiratory tract infection, 2) stable or slowly progressing radiographic abnormalities consistent with respiratory aspergillosis (ie, consolidation or nodules with or without cavitation), 3) positive microbiological results for Aspergillus spp., such as positive culture or galactomannan detection from body fluids, 4) microscopic evidence of the presence of Aspergillus spp. (eg, hyphae) in biopsy or surgical specimens, or positive culture from sterile samples. The other chronic pulmonary diseases mainly included chronic obstructive emphysema, bronchiectasis, pulmonary bullae, interstitial pneumonia, etc. BMI was calculated as kg/m2. Liver disease was defined as chronic hepatitis B, hepatitis C, liver cirrhosis,27 or metabolic-associated steatohepatitis.28 Renal insufficiency was defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of <30 mL/min/1.73.2 22 Hypertension was defined as blood pressure >140/90 mmHg. Smoking was defined as currently smoking, irrespective of the number of cigarettes per day. Alcohol drinking was defined as a self-reported consumption of more than three drinks a day. The comorbidities were also collected.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 22.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). The continuous variables were tested for normal distribution using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Normally distributed continuous variables were described as means ± standard deviations (SD), and those not normally distributed were described using medians (ranges). The categorical variables were described using n (%). Only descriptive statistics were used. The diagnostic effectiveness of Aspergillus IgG antibodies for CPA diagnosis was evaluated using the receiver operating characteristics (ROC) curve analysis and by calculating the area under the curve (AUC). Univariable logistic regression analysis was used to assess the association between baseline characteristics and outcomes. Multicollinearity among independent variables was evaluated with a correlation matrix and the variance inflation factor (VIF). The absence of significant collinearity was defined as a VIF of less than 5. Multivariable logistic regression analysis (forward-LR method) was used for variable selection to evaluate the factors independently associated with the dependent variable. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P-values ≤0.05 were considered statistically significant. The corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were provided.

Results

Characteristics of the Patients

This study included 1087 patients with pulmonary tuberculosis, of which 188 patients (17.3%) had concurrent diabetes mellitus (diabetes mellitus group), and 899 patients (82.7%) did not (non-diabetes mellitus group). There were 864 patients with treatment-naïve pulmonary tuberculosis, 138 patients with retreatment pulmonary tuberculosis, and 85 patients with obsolete pulmonary tuberculosis. The patients in the diabetes mellitus group were older than those in the non-diabetes mellitus group (median, 64 vs. 50 years, p<0.001; ≥65 years old: 47.9% vs. 34.6%, p=0.001). The proportion of males in the diabetes mellitus group was higher than in the non-diabetes mellitus group (83% vs. 67%, p<0.001), and BMI <18.5 kg/m2 was higher in the non-diabetes mellitus group (27.1% vs. 16.0%, p=0.001). Compared with the non-diabetes mellitus group, the diabetes mellitus group was higher in the proportion of patients with hypertension (46.3% vs. 16.7%, p<0.001), renal insufficiency (4.3% vs. 1.7%, p=0.044), alcohol use (15.4% vs. 12.3%, p=0.023), pulmonary cavities (33.5% vs. 22.1%, p=0.001), and low albumin (<35 g/L) (39.9% vs. 28.4%, p=0.002). The rate of high Aspergillus IgM (>0.35 KUA/L) was higher in the diabetes group (8.5% vs. 4.5%, P=0.022), without significant differences for Aspergillus IgG (p=0.881) (Table 1).

Diagnostic Value of Aspergillus IgG Antibody for CPA

This study evaluated the diagnostic value of Aspergillus IgG antibody in diagnosing CPA in patients with pulmonary tuberculosis. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was 0.834 (p<0.001) (Figure 1). The sensitivity and specificity of Aspergillus IgG at a cutoff of 80 AU/mL were also examined in different subgroups. The sensitivity/specificity was 90.9%/88.4% among patients with diabetes mellitus, 74.6%/80.6% among those without diabetes mellitus, and 83.7%/80.1% among those with low albumin (<35 g/L), and 76.4%/82.5% among those with high albumin (≥35 g/L) (Table 2). Detection Rates of CPA in Patients with Tuberculosis

The detection rate of Aspergillus IgG >80 AU/mL in the diabetes mellitus group (25.5%) was similar to that in the non-diabetes mellitus group (23.8%), but the proportion of confirmed CPA diagnosis in the diabetes mellitus group (17.6%) was higher than that in the non-diabetes mellitus group (7.9%). In the subgroup with BMI <18.5 kg/m2, the detection rate of Aspergillus IgG >80 AU/mL (30.3%) was higher than in the subgroup with BMI ≥18.5 kg/m2 (22.0%); moreover, the proportion of patients confirmed with CPA in the subgroup with BMI <18.5 kg/m2 (14.2%,) was also higher than in the subgroup with BMI ≥18.5 kg/m2 (8%). The detection rate of Aspergillus IgG >80 AU/mL in the subgroup with serum albumin <35 g/L (29.4%) was higher than in the subgroup with albumin ≥35 g/L (21.8%); the proportion of patients diagnosed with CPA in the subgroup with serum albumin <35 g/L (14.8%) was higher than in the subgroup with albumin ≥35 g/L (7.3%) (Table 3).

Based on standards from the World Health Organization,28,29 BMI <18.5 kg/m2 was selected as the clinical indicator for malnutrition, dividing patients into a diabetes mellitus and/or BMI <18.5 kg/m2 group and a non-diabetes mellitus with BMI <18.5 kg/m2 group. As detailed in Supplementary Figure 1, the proportion of confirmed CPA in the diabetes mellitus and/or BMI <18.5 kg/m2 group (15.3%) was significantly higher than in the non-diabetes mellitus with BMI <18.5 kg/m2 group (5.8%) (p<0.001) (Supplementary Figure 1A). For validation, the proportion of confirmed CPA in the non-diabetes mellitus with albumin ≥35 g/L group (5.9%) was lower than in the diabetes mellitus and/or albumin <35 g/L group (14.9%) (p<0.001) (Supplementary Figure 1B).

Univariable and Multivariable Analysis

The univariable analyses showed that age ≥65 years, male, BMI <18.5 kg/m2, diabetes mellitus, obsolete pulmonary tuberculosis, presence of lung cavities, and albumin <35 g/L were associated with CPA. Conversely, other baseline variables including smoking status, alcohol use, and renal insufficiency were not significantly associated with CPA (all p>0.05). The multivariable analysis showed that BMI <18.5 kg/m2 (OR=1.827, 95% CI: 1.160–2.876, p=0.009), diabetes mellitus (OR=2.718, 95% CI: 1.694–4.361, p<0.001), obsolete pulmonary tuberculosis (OR=4.644, 95% CI: 2.658–8.115, p<0.001), and low serum albumin <35 g/L (OR=1.943, 95% CI: 1.264–2.987, p=0.002) were associated with a diagnosis of CPA (Table 4). Of note, in this study, the Forward-LR method was used in the multivariable logistic regression analysis to select the variables that best correlated with the outcome. As a result, variables with weaker associations, such as age and gender, were excluded from the final multivariable model. A comprehensive assessment for multicollinearity was conducted prior to multivariable analysis. First, we examined the Pearson correlation matrix for all baseline variables (Supplementary Table S2). Then, we performed collinearity diagnostics specifically on the predictors in the final model. The Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values for all variables were well below the prespecified threshold of 5, indicating that multicollinearity did not significantly influence the model estimates (Supplementary Table S3).

The univariable analyses showed that BMI <18.5 kg/m2, albumin <35 g/L, smoking, alcohol use, and retreatment for pulmonary tuberculosis were associated with positive Aspergillus IgG in patients with pulmonary tuberculosis. Baseline variables such as older age (≥65 years) and renal insufficiency were not significantly associated with positive Aspergillus IgG serology (p=0.523 and p=0.095, respectively). The multivariable analysis identified that BMI <18.5 kg/m2 (1.390, 95% CI: 1.015–1.903, p=0.040), alcohol use (0.481, 95% CI: 0.288–0.803, p=0.005), and albumin <35 g/L (OR=1.385, 95% CI: 1.023–1.875, p=0.035) were associated with a positive Aspergillus IgG in patients with pulmonary tuberculosis (Supplementary Table S1).

Discussion

Diabetes mellitus and malnutrition are known risk factors for CPA,17 but their association with the diagnosis of CPA remains mostly unknown. Hence, this retrospective study aimed to verify the association of diabetes mellitus and nutritional status with the levels of Aspergillus IgG and diagnosis of CPA in patients with pulmonary tuberculosis and to explore other potential influencing factors. The results suggest that diabetes mellitus and malnutrition are associated with CPA, while malnutrition is also associated with positive serum Aspergillus IgG in patients with pulmonary tuberculosis. Screening and early intervention for CPA should be strengthened in this high-risk population.

The findings of this study suggest that an Aspergillus IgG cutoff of >80 AU/mL might serve as a potential indicator for CPA screening. Although the manufacturer recommends a value greater than 120 AU per mL for definitive diagnosis, we evaluated this lower threshold to improve sensitivity and reduce missed diagnoses in this vulnerable population.12 However, the diagnostic performance of Aspergillus IgG test was influenced by the presence of diabetes mellitus and malnutrition, which might be due to the potential impact of diabetes mellitus and malnutrition on the natural history of the disease and the host immune response.16,17 Indeed, in the subgroups of patients without diabetes mellitus and with a better nutritional status, the sensitivity and positive predictive value (PPV) were lower, suggesting that the optimal cutoff point differed in different patient populations. A study including 257 patients (only a small proportion of the participants with tuberculosis) diagnosed with CPA or non-CPA with other lung disorders evaluated the diagnostic value of serum Aspergillus IgG; an Aspergillus IgG negative cutoff of <80 AU/mL and positive cutoff of >120 AU/mL achieved a sensitivity and specificity of 84.1% and 89.6%, respectively,12 supporting the present study. A study in non-neutropenic patients showed that Aspergillus IgG at a cutoff of 80 AU/mL had 59.6% sensitivity and 77.0% specificity for IPA.9 Similar results were observed in non-agranulocytic patients.30 In patients suspected of aspergillosis, Aspergillus IgG yielded an AUC of 0.762, sensitivity of 70.0%, and specificity of 82.8%.31 On the other hand, a study in patients scheduled for hematopoietic transplantation showed that various Aspergillus IgG yielded moderate sensitivity and sensitivity for invasive aspergillosis.32 None of those previous studies were performed in patients with tuberculosis, and they did not explore the association of diabetes mellitus or malnutrition with the diagnosis of CPA.

Diabetes mellitus weakens the immune system and increases susceptibility to uncommon infections, including aspergillosis.33 Previous experimental research suggests that diabetes mellitus might persistently suppress specific immune pathways, which could lead to excessive inflammation and aggravate fungal infections.34 Consistent with previous clinical observations,16 the present study demonstrated that diabetes mellitus was independently associated with the diagnosis of chronic pulmonary aspergillosis. Patients with diabetes mellitus are not only susceptible to fungal infections but might also face complicated treatment courses and potentially poorer outcomes.33 Regarding diagnostic performance, the overall Aspergillus IgG positivity rate was similar between patients with and without diabetes mellitus even though the actual disease prevalence was significantly higher in the diabetic group. This apparent paradox might be explained by distinct clinical and immunological characteristics. Patients with diabetes mellitus often experience accelerated lung tissue destruction and more pronounced symptoms.33,34 Such manifestations might prompt clinicians to diagnose the disease more readily based on clinical and radiological evidence. Furthermore, systemic metabolic impairment could alter the baseline humoral response to fungal colonization, potentially reducing false positive serological results in this subgroup. Consequently, the actual sensitivity and specificity of the assay appeared higher in patients with diabetes mellitus. These nuances suggest that the clinical interpretation of serological results should carefully account for the underlying metabolic status of the patients.

Previous studies have explored the relationship between nutritional status and the poor prognosis of pulmonary aspergillosis.17 Malnutrition is well known to weaken the immune system and increase infection vulnerability. A study of 17,290 patients with CPA revealed that malnutrition and diabetes mellitus were independently associated with a poor prognosis of CPA, leading to increased mortality.35 The present study revealed for the first time the association between malnutrition and the diagnosis of CPA in patients with pulmonary tuberculosis, indicating that a BMI <18.5 kg/m2 or albumin <35 g/L were both associated with the diagnosis of pulmonary aspergillosis. Therefore, early screening for CPA should be carried out in this high-risk population.

Screening for aspergillosis by laboratory tests requires patients to attend a hospital with relevant facilities. Hence, the availability of the tests in a given hospital is not only directly related to the local incidence and prevalence of aspergillosis but also the disease burden of patients and the burden on the medical system. To reduce unnecessary tests, different screening frequencies shall be adopted for high-risk and low-risk groups. Diabetes mellitus and BMI self-reporting by patients may facilitate doctors to identify high-risk groups and carry out more aggressive screening. The present study suggests that patients with tuberculosis and malnutrition should be prioritized for the screening of CPA.

In this study, obsolete tuberculosis was also independently associated with a diagnosis of CPA (OR=4.644). Obsolete tuberculosis is often accompanied by localized structural lung damage, which may provide an anatomical basis for Aspergillus colonization and chronic infection.4,36 In addition to this structural vulnerability, concurrent diabetes mellitus and malnutrition may further compromise host immune defenses.16,34 Therefore, the local lung abnormalities observed in obsolete tuberculosis, together with systemic metabolic and nutritional impairment, might contribute to the development of chronic pulmonary aspergillosis.34,35 The ways in which impaired host defense interacts with residual structural lung damage in this setting warrant further study.

This study had some limitations. It was a single-center study, limiting the number of patients and the geographical area. Moreover, only hospitalized patients were included, introducing a bias by leaving out all outpatients. A potential diagnostic bias among patients with diabetes mellitus should be acknowledged. Given their complex comorbidities, these individuals might undergo more intensive clinical evaluations. Such differential diagnostic scrutiny could partially account for the higher detection rates observed within this subgroup. The retrospective nature and the cross-sectional nature of the study also limited the data to those available in the patient charts and prevented the analysis of causality and risk factors. Furthermore, our analysis did not stratify patients by the clinical-radiological subtypes of CPA, such as chronic cavitary aspergillosis or simple aspergilloma. Future studies are warranted to validate these findings in larger, more diverse cohorts and to investigate whether the risk factors identified herein have a differential impact across distinct CPA subtypes.

Conclusion

This study suggests that an adjusted Aspergillus IgG >80 AU/mL might serve as a potential cutoff for screening for CPA in patients with pulmonary tuberculosis. Our findings identify low BMI, diabetes mellitus, and hypoalbuminemia as independent risk factors for CPA, with this risk being most pronounced for patients with obsolete pulmonary tuberculosis. This supports refining clinical practice to prioritize screening in patients with this specific metabolic and nutritional high-risk profile, especially when accompanied by structural lung damage markers, thereby improving early detection and management of CPA in this vulnerable population.
