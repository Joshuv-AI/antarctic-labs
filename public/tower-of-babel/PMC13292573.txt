USP22 suppresses trophoblast cell necroptosis in preeclampsia by stabilizing KAT2A-mediated histone acetylation at the SFRP1 promoter
Authors: Lidan He, Feng Zhan, Shan Zheng, Na Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreeclampsia (PE) is a severe gestational disorder associated with impaired placental function. Necroptosis contributes substantially to trophoblast injury in PE, though the upstream epigenetic regulatory pathways are not yet fully elucidated. This study investigated how the deubiquitinase USP22 suppresses trophoblast necroptosis via epigenetic modulation of the KAT2A-SFRP1 axis.MethodsPreeclamptic and normal placental tissues were analyzed for USP22 and necroptosis pathway components by immunohistochemistry. Hypoxic conditions established in HTR-8/SVneo trophoblasts simulated the preeclamptic microenvironment. Cell death modality was characterized through complementary approaches including flow cytometry, cell viability assays, and transmission electron microscopy. Pathway selectivity was determined through systematic pharmacological inhibition of necroptosis, apoptosis, pyroptosis, ferroptosis, and autophagy. RNA sequencing identified genome-wide transcriptional responses to USP22 perturbation. Direct protein-protein interactions and locus-specific histone modifications at the SFRP1 promoter were resolved by co-immunoprecipitation and chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by quantitative PCR. USP22-mediated deubiquitination of KAT2A and substrate ubiquitin chain linkage specificity were elucidated using protein stability assays, ubiquitination analysis, and catalytic activity-dependent rescue with a C185A point mutant. Findings were further validated in an in vivo L-NAME-induced rat model of PE.ResultsUSP22 was significantly downregulated in PE placental tissues, concomitant with elevated expression of RIPK1, RIPK3, and MLKL. Necroptosis was confirmed as the dominant death modality in hypoxic trophoblasts. USP22 knockdown exacerbated necroptotic signaling, while USP22 overexpression restored H3K9ac and H3K27ac levels and suppressed necroptosis. RNA sequencing analysis identified 1,186 differentially expressed genes following USP22 knockdown, including notably repressed expression of the Wnt antagonist SFRP1. Mechanistically, USP22 directly interacted with KAT2A and stabilized it by selectively cleaving K48-linked polyubiquitin chains. This stabilization sustained KAT2A-dependent histone acetylation at the SFRP1 promoter, thereby maintaining SFRP1 transcription. Importantly, SFRP1 restoration in USP22-deficient trophoblasts substantially mitigated cell death and decreased the p-MLKL/MLKL ratio. The L-NAME rat PE model further confirmed coordinated downregulation of USP22, KAT2A, SFRP1, and histone acetylation marks in vivo.ConclusionUSP22 maintains trophoblast survival through selective K48-deubiquitination of KAT2A, which stabilizes KAT2A and sustains histone acetylation at the SFRP1 promoter, thereby promoting SFRP1 transcription and suppressing RIPK1-RIPK3-MLKL-mediated necroptosis. The USP22-KAT2A-SFRP1 axis represents a novel epigenetic checkpoint in PE pathogenesis and a potential therapeutic target for placental insufficiency.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08513-3.Keywords: Preeclampsia, Trophoblast cells, Necroptosis, USP22, KAT2A, SFRP1, Deubiquitination, Histone acetylation

Background

Preeclampsia (PE) is a gestational complication affecting multiple organ systems, presenting with newly emergent hypertension and proteinuria beyond 20 weeks of gestation. This condition affects approximately 2–8% of pregnant individuals globally and represents 14% of maternal mortality worldwide, imposing significant adverse impacts on both maternal and fetal outcomes [1]. Although the precise etiologies of PE remain incompletely understood, the placenta has emerged as a critical locus of pathological dysfunction, as clinical resolution is achievable only through pregnancy termination and placental delivery [2]. As critical components of placental tissue, trophoblast cells represent a major focus in PE research [3]. Normal trophoblast biological behaviors, particularly appropriate invasion and remodeling of uterine spiral arteries, are fundamental prerequisites forsuccessful placentation and fetal development. Conversely, abnormal trophoblast behaviors, particularly excessive cell death through necroptosis, impair invasion into the maternal decidua and spiral artery remodeling. This cascade culminates in placental ischemia and elevated uteroplacental vascular resistance, which represent key pathogenic factors in PE development [4, 5] (Fig. 1).

Impaired spiral artery remodeling and inadequate trophoblast invasion in preeclampsia versus normal pregnancy. Normal pregnancy(left panel): Characterized by adequate spiral artery remodeling, successful extravillous trophoblast (EVT) migration, and establishment of high placental blood flow. Preeclampsia (right panel): Characterized by impaired spiral artery remodeling, defective EVT migration, and reduced placental perfusion. These pathological changes result in placental ischemia and endothelial dysfunction, leading to systemic maternal complications including hypertension, proteinuria, and fetal growth restriction

Necroptosis represents a well-characterized type of programmed cell death that operates independently of caspases and can be triggered by tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and other agonists through death receptor pathways. This process involves a core signaling cascade comprising receptor-interacting protein kinase 1 (RIPK1), RIPK3, and Mixed lineage kinase domain-like pseudokinase (MLKL). Upon phosphorylation, p-MLKL undergoes translocation to the cell membrane, triggering destabilization and subsequent cell lysis death [6]. Necroptosis has been shown to contribute to the disease mechanisms of multiple pathological conditions [7, 8]. While necroptosis exhibits morphological features similar to traditional necrosis, it is precisely regulated by genetic and biochemical mechanisms at the molecular level [9]. However, research investigating trophoblast necroptosis in PE remains limited. Notably, Hannan et al. [10] and Bailey et al. [11] independently demonstrated that, compared to gestational age-matched normal pregnancy controls, PE placental tissues exhibited significantly elevated mRNA levels of RIPK1, RIPK3, and MLKL. Moreover, subsequent investigations have demonstrated substantially elevated levels of critical necroptotic regulators at both transcriptional and translational levels in PE placentae [12, 13]. These findings collectively suggest that necroptosis may constitute a critical pathological mechanism underlying PE development.

Deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs) have gained prominence in recent years as important modulators of cell fate specification, representing a major area of current investigation [14]. Our preliminary bioinformatics analysis identified nine differentially expressed necroptosis-related genes, including Ubiquitin-specific peptidase 22 (USP22), in PE placental tissues [15]. USP22, an important deubiquitinase, participates in multiple pathophysiological contexts by regulating the stability of key proteins. USP22 exhibits context-dependent regulatory roles, both promoting tumor development through transcription factor stabilization [16] and suppressing inflammatory responses to exert protective effects [17]. Notably, USP22 has been shown to regulate the ubiquitination levels of RIPK3, directly participating in necroptosis regulation [18]. However, the mechanistic role of USP22 in pregnancy-related diseases, particularly PE, remains unclear.

Critical knowledge gaps exist regarding the epigenetic regulation of necroptosis in PE. While hypoxia-induced activation of the RIPK1-RIPK3-MLKL pathway leading to trophoblast cell death is well-established, upstream regulatory mechanisms remain poorly understood. Lysine acetyltransferase 2 A (KAT2A) is an important histone acetyltransferase whose protein stability may be regulated by deubiquitinating enzymes, subsequently influencing chromatin modification and gene transcription [19]. Additionally, secreted frizzled-related protein 1 (SFRP1), functioning as a Wnt pathway suppressor, serves as an important regulator in placental trophoblast differentiation and cell death regulation [20, 21]. SFRP1/SFRP5 double knockout mouse models have demonstrated that excessive Wnt/β-catenin signaling activation during placental development leads to trophoblast progenitor cell depletion and disrupted differentiation [22]. Aberrant SFRP1 expression can dysregulate Wnt/β-catenin signaling, subsequently affecting inflammatory responses and cell death [23]. However, the regulatory mechanisms linking SFRP1 expression to necroptosis remain largely unexplored.

This investigation sought to delineate the molecular mechanisms by which USP22 regulates trophoblast necroptosis in PE. We focused on the functional interaction between USP22 and KAT2A, and investigated how USP22-mediated deubiquitination of KAT2A influences histone acetylation at the SFRP1 promoter and downstream SFRP1 transcription. We further examined whether this USP22-KAT2A-SFRP1 regulatory axis modulates necroptotic signaling in trophoblasts under hypoxic conditions. Our findings aim to establish a novel epigenetic mechanism involved in PE pathogenesis and propose promising therapeutic avenues for ameliorating placental insufficiency.

Materials and methods

Patient cohort and sample collection

A case-control study was conducted, enrolling participants from the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology, First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University, China, between April 2024 and September 2024. Of 47 pregnant women initially screened, 40 met the eligibility criteria and were enrolled (20 PE patients and 20 normotensive controls) (Supplementary Figure S1). All participants completed the study with 100% follow-up rate from enrollment through neonatal discharge. Ethical approval (IRB NO: 2024.076) was obtained from the Institutional Review Board, and all participants provided written informed consent. Sample size was determined by a power analysis (bilateral t-test, α = 0.05, power = 80%), based on an estimated effect size of 1.2 derived from preliminary USP22 immunohistochemical data. This indicated a minimum of 18 subjects per group; thus, 20 participants were enrolled per group to account for potential attrition.

Inclusion criteria for both groups included singleton pregnancy, gestational age ≥ 34 weeks at delivery, and maternal age 18–45 years. PE diagnosis adhered to the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) criteria [24], characterized as gestational hypertension (systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg) developing after 20 weeks of pregnancy, accompanied by proteinuria (≥ 300 mg/24 h or a protein/creatinine ratio ≥ 0.3) or functional impairment of maternal organs. Control subjects were normotensive pregnant women (BP < 140/90 mmHg and proteinuria < 150 mg/24 h throughout pregnancy) with uncomplicated pregnancies, matched to PE cases for gestational age (± 2 weeks). Exclusion criteria included chronic hypertension (pre-pregnancy or before 20 weeks), pre-gestational or insulin-dependent diabetes, multiple gestation, fetal abnormalities or major congenital malformations, maternal autoimmune diseases, and chronic kidney disease. Placental samples (approximately 1 cm3) were obtained from the central cotyledon area on the maternal surface, 2 cm from the umbilical cord insertion, within 5 min of delivery. Areas of visible infarction, calcification, or hemorrhage were macroscopically excluded. All sample collection and processing were completed within 30 min of placental delivery to minimize degradation. Tissue samples were compartmentalized into three arms: cryogenic preservation (snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, stored at -80 °C) for molecular and genomic analyses; chemical fixation (4% paraformaldehyde) for morphological and histopathological investigations; and immediate processing for protein characterization.

Cell treatment and hypoxia model construction

HTR-8/SVneo cells (CL-0765, Procell) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, KGM12800S, Keygene Biotech) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin mixture. The cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified chamber with 5% CO2. Cells were subjected to a controlled hypoxic microenvironment (1% O₂, 5% CO₂, and 94% N₂) for 48 h to induce hypoxic phenotypes. Gene knockdown and overexpression were conducted via siRNA transfection and plasmid delivery, respectively, as detailed in the “Cell Transfection and Plasmid Constructs” section.

Construction and validation of rat preeclampsia model

Adult SPF Sprague-Dawley rats of both sexes (Sibeifu Biotechnology, SCXK (Beijing) 2024-0001) were housed under standard conditions (12 h photoperiod, 22 ± 2 °C RT, and 55 ± 10% humidity). Females were mated with males (2:1 ratio), and pregnancy was confirmed by vaginal plug (gestational day 0, GD0). Sample size (n = 6 pregnant dams per group) was determined by power analysis targeting ≥ 80% probability of detecting an intergroup SBP differential of ≥ 35 mmHg (α = 0.05, two-tailed), based on previous L-NAME-induced PE models [25]. Pregnant dams were randomized using computer-generated numbers. Investigators remained blinded to group assignment during treatment administration, BP measurement, and data analysis.

To establish an experimental PE model, pregnant rats received daily subcutaneous injections of L-NAME (Nω-nitro-L-arginine methyl ester; 125 mg/kg/day; MedChemExpress, LOT: 311955) from GD5 to GD18, while control dams received equivalent saline injections. Maternal measurements: SBP and DBP were serially evaluated non-invasively via tail-cuff plethysmography (CODA System, Kent Scientific) on GD0, 7, 14, and 18. Measurements followed a 3-day acclimation period, with each session comprising 5 acclimation cycles and 15 measurement cycles. Twenty-four-hour urine was collected on GD18 for proteinuria quantification. Maternal serum was collected on GD18 for measurement of sFlt-1, PlGF, and sFlt-1/PlGF ratio by ELISA. Fetal and placental measurements: At GD18, dams were euthanized and fetal and placental tissues were rapidly harvested. Fetal weight and placental weight were recorded for each litter and averaged to obtain one value per dam. Three placentas were randomly selected from each dam (total n = 18 placentas per group) for tissue analysis to account for the nested structure of placentas within dams.

Predetermined humane endpoints (> 20% baseline body weight decrement, severe distress, respiratory difficulty) and a priori exclusion criteria (pregnancy failure before GD5, > 50% missing BP measurements, intercurrent illness) were applied throughout the study. No animals met exclusion criteria or humane endpoints. Model validation included serial BP and proteinuria assessment, and fetal/placental weight measurements at endpoint. All animal experiments were ethically approved by Fujian Medical University’s IACUC (certificate no. IACUCFJMU 2024-Y-0987) and adhered to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Cell transfection and plasmid constructs

Full-length human coding sequences (CDS) for USP22 (NM_015276.2), KAT2A (NM_021078.3), and SFRP1 (NM_003012.4) were amplified and cloned into the mammalian expression plasmid pcDNA3.1(+) (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) between EcoRI and XhoI sites, generating OE-USP22, OE-KAT2A, and OE-SFRP1. The pcDNA3.1(+) vector without insert served as the negative control (OE-NC). All constructs, synthesized by Anhui General Biosystems (Anqing, China), were verified for sequence integrity and correct orientation by Sanger sequencing. A catalytically inactive USP22 C185A mutant (Cys185→Ala, TGC→GCC) was generated from OE-USP22 using the QuikChange II Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara) with specifically designed primers. The intended mutation was confirmed and secondary mutations excluded by Sanger sequencing.

siRNAs targeting USP22 (NM_015276.2; Gene ID: 23326) and KAT2A (NM_021078.3; Gene ID: 2648), along with a scrambled negative control (siNC), were designed based on human mRNA reference sequences from NCBI GenBank. Design criteria included 19–21 nucleotides in length, 40–60% GC content, CDS location, avoidance of > 4 consecutive identical nucleotides, and BLAST-confirmed human transcriptome specificity. Three distinct siRNAs per gene, each with a 3′ dTdT overhang, were chemically synthesized and purified by Anhui General Biosystems. siRNA sequences are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

HTR-8/SVneo cells were cultured to 50–60% confluence and transfected 24 h later. Transfections utilized 2 µg plasmid DNA per well (6-well plate) or 50 nM siRNA with Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For co-transfection, total nucleic acid amount was kept constant, adjusting Lipofectamine 3000 volume proportionally. Transfection efficiency was assessed by Western blotting and qRT-PCR at 48 h post-transfection. Functional assays commenced at 48 h post-transfection unless specified otherwise.

High-throughput mRNA sequencing

Total RNA was isolated utilizing TRIzol reagent (15596018, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and RNA integrity was determined using an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system (RNA 6000 Nano LabChip Kit, 5067 − 1511, Agilent). RNA samples with an RNA integrity number (RIN) ≥ 7.0 were selected for library construction to ensure high-quality sequencing data. Poly(A)+ mRNA was enriched from 5 µg total RNA using Dynabeads Oligo (dT) (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and fragmented at 94 °C in the presence of divalent cations.

First-strand cDNA was synthesized with SuperScript™ II Reverse Transcriptase (1896649, Invitrogen), followed by strand-specific second-strand cDNA synthesis using dUTP. Libraries were constructed independently from six biological replicates per group via sequential A-tailing, adapter ligation, AMPureXP bead (Beckman Coulter) size selection, UDG treatment, and PCR amplification, yielding an average insert size of 300 ± 50 bp. Paired-end sequencing (150 bp reads) was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq™ 6000 instrument, generating approximately 6 GB of clean data per sample. Each library achieved adequate sequencing depth (~ 40 million clean reads/sample, > 95% overall mapping rate with > 90% unique mapping rate to the reference genome).

Raw sequencing reads were quality-checked with FastQC (v0.10.1) and met the following quality filters: >97% valid reads, Q20 > 99%, and mean GC content ~ 53%. Reads were processed using a standard bioinformatics pipeline: HISAT2 (v2.2.1) for genome alignment against the human reference genome (GRCh38/hg38, Ensembl release 107) (human reference genome, Ensembl release 107), StringTie (v2.1.6) for expression quantification as raw read counts, and DESeq2 (v1.22.2) for differential expression analysis with Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple testing. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as genes satisfying both |log2(FC)| ≥ 1 and FDR < 0.05, which identified 1,186 DEGs across all pairwise comparisons.Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment were conducted via clusterProfiler (adjusted P-value < 0.05, Benjamini-Hochberg correction). The raw dataset (GSE327405) has been deposited in GEO database for public release upon article publication.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

Co-IP assays assays using a Pierce Co-IP Kit (cat. no. 88805, Thermo Fisher Scientific) were conducted to investigate the interaction between USP22 and KAT2A and to determine KAT2A ubiquitination. Cell lysates in ice-cold lysis buffer with protease inhibitor cocktail were centrifuged (12,000 × g for 15 min at 4℃). An aliquot (5%) of the clarified supernatant was retained as input. For interaction studies, an anti-USP22 antibody (ab195289, Abcam) or normal rabbit IgG (negative control) was immobilized on NHS-activated magnetic beads for 2 h at room temperature (RT). Cell lysates were then incubated with the beads overnight at 4℃ under gentle rotation. Following washing, bound proteins were eluted and denatured in 5 × SDS loading buffer at 95℃ for 5 min. Protein analysis was performed using Western blotting with anti-KAT2A (ab217876, Abcam) and anti-ubiquitin (Ub; Ye373716, Invitrogen) antibodies.

To determine the ubiquitin chain linkage specificity on KAT2A, an additional Co-IP experiment was performed. USP22 knockdown cells cultured under hypoxic conditions were transfected with either wild-type OE-USP22 (OE-USP22 WT) or the catalytically inactive OE-USP22 C185A mutant. Lysates were subsequently immunoprecipitated with an anti-KAT2A antibody, followed by immunoblotting with linkage-specific antibodies against K48-linked ubiquitin (K48-Ub; A3606, Abclonal) and K63-linked ubiquitin (K63-Ub; A18164, Abclonal).

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

ChIP analysis was used to assess KAT2A occupancy and enrichment of histone acetylation marks H3K27ac and H3K9ac at the SFRP1 gene locus. HTR-8/SVneo cells (2 × 10⁷) were fixed with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at RT, followed by quenching with 0.125 M glycine (Aladdin). Following phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) washing, cells were harvested in lysis buffer 1 containing 10 µL/mL protease inhibitor cocktail (Halt™ Protease Inhibitor Cocktail) and lysed on ice for 10 min, then centrifuged at 9,000 × g for 5 min at 4℃. Cell pellets were resuspended in MNase digestion buffer (100 µL, containing 0.25 µL MNase) and digested at 37℃ for 15 min with periodic vortexing. Digestion was stopped by MNase stop solution. Nuclei were lysed by addition of lysis buffer 2 (50 µL) and incubation on ice for 15 min with occasional mixing.

Following centrifugation at 9,000 × g for 5 min at 4℃, 5 µL of the supernatant was reserved as input control. The residual 45 µL was subjected to overnight immunoprecipitation at 4℃ with rotation using the following antibodies: anti-KAT2A (Abcam, ab217876), anti-H3K27ac (Proteintech, 39085), and anti-H3K9ac (Proteintech, 61952) as target antibodies; anti-RNA polymerase II (Abcam, ab238146) as a positive control; and normal rabbit IgG (Proteintech, 30000-0-AP) as a negative control. Immunocomplexes were purified by column chromatography per manufacturer instructions. DNA was extracted and analyzed by qRT-PCR to determine SFRP1 promoter enrichment using input and IP-enriched samples. Five primer pairs (F1/R1 to F5/R5) targeting the SFRP1 promoter region (− 1500 to + 115 bp, TSS) were designed and validated (Supplementary Figure S2 and Supplementary Table S2). The primer pairs F1/R1 to F4/R4 targeted distal regions and were used as negative controls for binding specificity. The primer pair F5/R5 (− 150 to + 115 bp), which demonstrated the strongest enrichment, was selected for subsequent analysis. The GAPDH promoter was employed as a positive control for ChIP validation. Relative enrichment was calculated using two methods: (1) fold enrichment over IgG: 2^(ΔCt), where ΔCt = Ct(IgG) − Ct(target antibody); and (2) percent input (%Input): 2^[Ct(Input) − Ct(IP)] × 100%. ChIP analysis was conducted in triplicate (n = 3, independent biological replicates).

CCK-8 cell viability assay

Cell viability was determined utilizing the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. HTR-8/SVneo cells (5 × 103 per well) were seeded in 96-well plates and exposed to various treatments for 24 h. Following treatment, CCK-8 reagent (10 µL/well) was added and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Absorbance was then measured at 450 nm using a microplate spectrophotometer. (Model WD-2102B, Beijing Liuyi Biotechnology). The percentage of cell viability was determined from absorbance values normalized to untreated control cells.

Flow cytometry apoptosis analysis

HTR-8/SVneo cells from various experimental groups were harvested at the designated time points. Cells (1 × 106 per sample) were collected and washed twice with PBS via centrifugation at 1,500 × g for 3 min at RT. Cell pellets were resuspended in 300 µL ice-cold Annexin V-FITC binding buffer (1 ×, AP101-100, MultiSciences Biotech) and subsequently stained with Annexin V-FITC (5 µL) and propidium iodide (PI, 10 µL). Following gentle stirring, stained cells were incubated in the dark at RT for 10 min. Samples were immediately analyzed via flow cytometry (NovoCyte 2060R, Acea Biosciences). A sequential gating strategy was applied according to established procedures [26]: (1) singlets were identified by plotting forward scatter area (FSC-A) against height (FSC-H) to exclude aggregates, (2) living cells were selected by plotting FSC-A against side scatter area (SSC-A) to exclude debris, and (3) apoptotic and necroptotic cells were identified by quadrant analysis of.

Annexin V-FITC (x-axis) versus PI (y-axis), defining four populations: Annexin V⁻/PI− (viable), Annexin V+/PI− (early apoptotic), Annexin V+/PI− (late apoptotic/secondary necrotic), and Annexin V−/PI+ (primary necrotic). At least 10,000 singlet events were collected per sample for each analysis.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

HTR-8/SVneo cells (treated/control) were rinsed with PBS, then primarily fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (0.1 M phosphate buffer, pH 7.4) for 2 h at 4 °C. Post-fixation was performed using 1% osmium tetroxide in the same buffer (2 h, 4 °C). Samples were dehydrated (graded acetone, 4 °C), then embedded in epoxy resin. Polymerization was conducted at 37 °C overnight, followed by 45 °C for 12 h and 60 °C for 48 h. Ultrathin Sect. (70 nm) were prepared with a ultramicrotome and subsequently stained sequentially with 3% uranyl acetate for 15 min, followed by 0.4% lead citrate for 10 min. Ultrastructural images were captured on a JEM-1230 TEM (JEOL Ltd., Japan) at 80 kV.

Immunofluorescence staining

HTR-8/SVneo cells were cultured on glass coverslips in 24-well plates for 48 h. Cells were subsequently fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, G1101, Servicebio) for 10–15 min at RT, then permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10–15 min, and blocked with 5% normal goat serum (G1208, Servicebio) for 30 min. Following each incubation, cells were washed three times with PBS (5 min/wash). Primary anti-p-MLKL antibody (1:200, Proteintech cat. no. 82090-2-RR, diluted in Huilan Bio RCT002) was incubated overnight at 4 °C. Following PBS washes, ABflo® 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (AS053, Abclonal, 1:500) secondary antibody was applied for 1–2 h at RT in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1 µg/mL) for 5–10 min at RT in the dark. Coverslips were mounted with anti-fade mounting medium (P0131, Beyotime) and sealed. Images were acquired using a Leica TCS SP8/STELLARIS 5 confocal microscope at 40× oil-immersion magnification (excitation: DAPI 405 nm, ABflo® 488 nm). Three randomly selected fields per sample were captured with identical acquisition parameters (laser intensity, exposure time, detector gain) for comparability. TIFF images were saved for subsequent fluorescence intensity quantification.

Immunohistochemistry

Human and rat placental tissues from PE and normal pregnancies were fixed with formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 μm. Sections were deparaffinized (xylene) and rehydrated (graded ethanol). Antigen retrieval was achieved (10 mM citrate buffer, pH 6.0, 95 °C, 10 min) followed by cooling to RT. Non-specific binding was blocked by incubation with 5% BSA in PBS for 30 min at RT. Sections were subsequently incubated overnight at 4℃ with the following primary antibodies: anti-USP22 (bs-8611R, Bioss, 1:200), anti-RIPK1 (610459, BD Biosciences, 1:100), anti-RIPK3 (ab72106, Abcam, 1:200), anti-MLKL (M6697, Sigma, 1:100), anti-SFRP1 (26460-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:100), anti-KAT2A (A2224, Abclonal, 1:100), anti-H3K27ac (A22264, Abclonal, 1:100), and anti-H3K9ac (A7255, Abclonal, 1:100). Following three PBS rinses (5 min each), sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (ZB-2301, ZSGB-Bio, 1:100) for 1 h at RT. DAB and hematoxylin staining were applied sequentially. After dehydration and xylene clearing, slides were mounted with resinous medium and imaged using a light microscope (CX43, Olympus) for protein expression analysis.

Quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was obtained from cells and tissues via TRIzol-mediated extraction (CW0580S, CWBIO, China), and further purified using an UltraPure RNA Extraction Kit (CW0581M, CWBIO, China). RNA specifications were verified via NanoDrop spectrophotometry (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Samples with an A260/A280 ratio of 1.8–2.0 were selected to further analyses. First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed using 1 µg of total RNA and the HiScript II Q RT SuperMix reagent system (Vazyme), which contains an integrated gDNA elimination function, following to standard protocols.

qRT-PCR was conducted using a CFX Connect™ Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Shanghai, China) and SuperStar Universal SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (CW3360M, CWBIO). PCR reactions (20 µL total volume) consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 amplification cycles (95 °C for 10 s, 58 °C for 30 s, 72 °C for 30 s), and a melting curve analysis (65–95 °C) to confirm product specificity. Gene expression levels were standardized to β-actin and quantified against control samples using the 2^(−ΔΔCt) method. Triplicate analyses were carried out for all reactions, with primer sequences detailed in Supplementary Table S3.

Western blotting analysis

Cells were homogenized in ice-cold RIPA buffer (with protease and phosphatase inhibitors) for 30 min, then sonicated (3 cycles, 10 s on/off, 30% amplitude). Samples were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C (Eppendorf Model 5424R), and protein concentrations in the resulting supernatants were assayed using a BCA kit (Elabscience, E-BC-K318-M). Protein aliquots (20–40 µg) underwent denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min with loading buffer, followed by separation via 10–12% SDS-PAGE at 80–120 V for 1.5 h. Proteins were electrophoretically transferred to methanol-activated PVDF membranes (Merck Millipore) at 300 mA for 1.5 h. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at RT, membranes were exposed to primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Following three 10-min TBST washes, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were incubated for 1 h at RT. Detection was performed using enhanced chemiluminescence (Thermo Fisher Scientific, RJ239676) and visualized using a chemiluminescence imaging system (Model Tanon-5200, Shanghai Tanon). Antibody information is provided in Supplementary Table S4.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Rat serum levels of soluble Fms-like tyrosine kinase-1 (sFlt-1) and placental growth factor (PlGF) were determined by commercial ELISA assay (sFlt-1 kit, ELK9331; PlGF kit, ELK2527; ELK Biotechnology, China). Blood was obtained from the abdominal aorta of anesthetized rats and incubated at RT for 30 min to facilitate clotting. Following centrifugation (1,000 × g, 20 min, 25 °C), serum supernatants were collected and stored at − 80 °C until assay. ELISA procedures were conducted according to the manufacturer’s specifications. The absorbance at 450 nm was read against a 570 nm reference (Microplate Reader Model WD-2102B, Beijing Liuyi Biotechnology). Each sample was analyzed in duplicate, and concentrations were determined using standard curves constructed from kit-supplied recombinant protein standards.

Proteinuria measurement

Total urine protein (UTP) was assayed using a specific UTP assay kit (Shandong Bok Biotech, 70194,) on an automated biochemical analyzer (Model BK-600, Shandong Bok). UTP complex calibrators (Shandong Bok Biotech, 91038) and quality controls (Shandong Bok Biotech, 92028) were used following the manufacturer’s protocol. Turbid urine was clarified by centrifugation at 3500 rpm for 10 min; the supernatant was then measured.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego). Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Prior to parametric testing, normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and variance homogeneity by Levene’s test; when assumptions were not met, the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s post hoc test was applied. For parametric analyses, unpaired Student’s t-test was used for two-group comparisons, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s or Dunnett’s post hoc tests for multiple comparisons, and two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test for two-factor analyses. Exact P values are reported where feasible (P < 0.0001 when below detection). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Clinical tissue studies involved 20 biological replicates per group. Cell-based experiments comprised 3 independent biological replicates, each with technical triplicates. In animal studies, 6 pregnant dams per group were examined (the dam defined as the experimental unit); fetal and placental weights were averaged within each litter, while tissue-level measurements (n = 18 placentas per group, 3 randomly selected per dam) were analyzed using a linear mixed model with dam as a random effect.

Necroptosis activation and USP22 downregulation in preeclamptic placental tissues and trophoblasts

Immunohistochemical analysis of clinical placental tissues revealed significantly reduced USP22 protein expression in preeclamptic placentas compared with normotensive controls. Concordantly, significant upregulation was observed for the necroptosis markers RIPK1, RIPK3, and MLKL (all P < 0.0001, Fig. 2A). To identify the optimal Necrostatin-1 (Nec-1) concentration that inhibits necroptosis while preserving cell viability, HTR-8/SVneo cells were subjected to Nec-1 treatment (2.5–100 µM, 48 h) and analyzed for cell viability using CCK-8 assay. Concentrations of 50, 80, and 100 µM significantly reduced cell viability compared with untreated controls (all P < 0.05), whereas exposure to 2.5–40 µM exerted no significant cytotoxic effects (Fig. 2B). Balancing necroptosis inhibition efficacy against cellular toxicity, a dose of 40 µM was adopted as the optimal dose for further studies.

To simulate the pathophysiological microenvironment of PE, HTR-8/SVneo cells were subjected to hypoxic conditions (1% O2, 5% CO2, and 94% N2) for 48 h. Western blotting analysis demonstrated that hypoxia-exposed cells exhibited reduced USP22 protein expression relative to normoxic controls (1.51 ± 0.39 vs. 0.22 ± 0.07; P = 0.0022). Concurrently, RIPK1 (0.40 ± 0.05 vs. 0.81 ± 0.12; P = 0.0055), RIPK3 (0.21 ± 0.04 vs. 0.48 ± 0.07; P = 0.0176) expression were markedly increased. Notably, total MLKL protein levels remained unchanged under hypoxic stress, whereas the p-MLKL/MLKL phosphorylation ratio was substantially elevated (0.51 ± 0.03 vs. 0.98 ± 0.13; P = 0.0087), indicating selective MLKL phosphorylation rather than increased protein synthesis. This pattern demonstrates engagement of the terminal necroptotic execution apparatus. Nec-1 pretreatment substantially suppressed the hypoxia-induced necroptotic response, with RIPK1 (0.21 ± 0.04; P = 0.0053) and p-MLKL/MLKL ratio (0.64 ± 0.15; P = 0.0272) significantly reduced relative to hypoxia alone (Fig. 2C).

Flow cytometry with Annexin V-FITC/PI staining was used to characterize cell death phenotype across experimental conditions. Hypoxic exposure induced approximately a 3.5-fold increase in total apoptosis rate relative to controls (3.77 ± 0.37% vs. 13.16 ± 0.49%; P < 0.0001). Notably, this cell death burden was predominantly composed of late apoptotic/secondary necrotic cells (Annexin V⁺/PI⁺), which increased from 1.44 ± 0.25% to 8.47 ± 0.34%. Nec-1 administration attenuated this hypoxia-induced cell death burden, reducing total apoptosis to 8.05 ± 0.16% (39% relative reduction; P < 0.0001), with the Annexin V⁺/PI⁺ subset correspondingly diminished to 3.77 ± 0.23% (Fig. 2D). TEM corroborated these biochemical findings. Hypoxia-exposed cells displayed characteristic necroptotic ultrastructural features: mitochondrial fragmentation with disrupted cristae (yellow arrows), plasma membrane blebbing and focal rupture with loss of membrane integrity (red arrows), cytoplasmic vacuolization, and cytoplasmic content leakage into the extracellular space. In contrast, cells pretreated with Nec-1 demonstrated substantially attenuated morphological changes, with preserved cell membrane integrity and maintained organellar architecture (Fig. 2E).

USP22 suppresses hypoxia-induced trophoblast necroptosis with concomitant restoration of histone acetylation

To investigate the regulatory role of USP22 in trophoblast necroptosis under hypoxic conditions, USP22 overexpression constructs (OE-USP22) and USP22 knockdown (siUSP22) were established and validated by Western blotting and qRT-PCR (P < 0.0001 (siUSP22) and P < 0.001 (OE-USP22) vs. NC, Fig. 3A and B). Among the three tested siRNA sequences, siUSP22-1351 demonstrated the highest knockdown efficiency (> 80% reduction in protein and mRNA) and was therefore selected for all subsequent loss-of-function experiments. USP22 knockdown under normoxic conditions significantly upregulated RIPK1 (0.28 ± 0.02 vs. 0.44 ± 0.02; P = 0.0009), RIPK3 (0.45 ± 0.01 vs. 0.60 ± 0.03; P = 0.0013), and the p-MLKL/MLKL ratio (0.76 ± 0.05 vs. 1.23 ± 0.17; P = 0.0110) compared to NC groups (Fig. 3C). These findings suggest that USP22 exerts basal inhibitory control over necroptosis signaling.

To further determine the pathway specificity of USP22 knockdown-induced cell death, systematic pharmacological experiments were performed under hypoxia. Five pathway-selective inhibitors were employed: Nec-1 (RIPK1 inhibitor, targeting necroptosis), Belnacasan (caspase-1 inhibitor, targeting pyroptosis), Z-VAD-FMK (pan-caspase inhibitor, targeting apoptosis), Ferrostatin-1 (targeting ferroptosis), and 3-methyladenine (3-MA, targeting autophagy). Cell death was detected using Annexin V-FITC/PI flow cytometry. In siUSP22-transfected cells under hypoxia, the apoptosis rate was 11.05 ± 2.48%, predominantly consisting of late apoptotic/necrotic cells (Annexin V⁺/PI⁺: 10.72 ± 1.03%). Nec-1 treatment substantially reduced apoptosis to 2.45 ± 0.09% (Annexin V⁺/PI⁺: 1.53 ± 0.29%; 78% reduction; P = 0.0039 vs. siUSP22 alone). Belnacasan also conferred moderate but statistically significant protection, reducing apoptosis to 5.62 ± 0.54% (Annexin V⁺/PI⁺: 3.38 ± 0.44%; 49% reduction; P = 0.0207 vs. siUSP22). This suggests partial involvement of pyroptotic signaling, possibly reflecting crosstalk between necroptotic and pyroptotic pathways. However, the protection by Belnacasan was substantially less than that by Nec-1, indicating that pyroptosis is not a dominant death modality in this context. In contrast, Z-VAD-FMK (apoptosis rate: 12.30 ± 0.75%; Annexin V⁺/PI⁺: 11.04 ± 0.63%) and Ferrostatin-1 (apoptosis rate: 13.26 ± 1.50%; Annexin V⁺/PI⁺: 11.13 ± 0.95%) showed no significant protective effect (P = 0.4487 and P = 0.2553 vs. siUSP22), excluding major contributions from caspase-dependent apoptosis and ferroptosis. Notably, 3-MA increased the apoptosis rate to 20.02 ± 1.16% (Annexin V⁺/PI⁺: 17.76 ± 1.03%; 81% increase; P < 0.0047 vs. siUSP22), suggesting that autophagy functions as a compensatory survival mechanism under these hypoxic conditions (Fig. 3D). Collectively, these results indicate that USP22 knockdown predominantly activates the RIPK1-RIPK3-MLKL necroptosis pathway, with potential minor contributions from pyroptotic signaling that warrant further investigation.

To assess the effect of USP22 on hypoxia-induced necroptosis, HTR-8/SVneo cells were transfected with either USP22 overexpression constructs (OE-USP22) or empty vector controls (NC), then exposed to hypoxia for 48 h. Western blotting demonstrated marked upregulation of key necroptotic effectors compared to NC controls: RIPK1 increased from 0.34 ± 0.06 to 0.65 ± 0.06 (P = 0.0007), RIPK3 rose from 0.55 ± 0.06 to 0.76 ± 0.04 (P = 0.0038), MLKL protein increased from 0.68 ± 0.07 to 1.16 ± 0.05 (P = 0.0015), and p-MLKL elevated from 0.47 ± 0.02 to 0.71 ± 0.08 (P = 0.0124). Concomitantly, histone acetylation marks were substantially suppressed: H3K9ac decreased from 0.80 ± 0.08 to 0.44 ± 0.06 (P = 0.0012) and H3K27ac declined from 0.98 ± 0.03 to 0.45 ± 0.02 (P < 0.0001, Fig. 3E). USP22 overexpression significantly attenuated this hypoxia-induced necroptotic response. RIPK1 (0.22 ± 0.02), RIPK3 (0.17 ± 0.02), MLKL (0.55 ± 0.12), and p-MLKL (0.22 ± 0.01) all substantially reduced compared to hypoxia + NC groups (all P < 0.001 vs. hypoxia + NC). The p-MLKL/MLKL phosphorylation ratio was markedly reduced from 0.98 ± 0.13 to 0.28 ± 0.05 (72% reduction, P < 0.001). Notably, USP22 overexpression substantially restored histone acetylation marks uneder hypoxia. H3K9ac recovering to 0.65 ± 0.05 (48% recovery; P = 0.021) and H3K27ac to 0.73 ± 0.03 (62% recovery; P < 0.0001, Fig. 3E) compared to hypoxia + NC groups. Although these values remained below baseline NC levels, the restoration of histone acetylation upon USP22 overexpression was substantial. These findings indicate that USP22 functions as a critical regulator of hypoxia-induced necroptosis by maintaining chromatin accessibility through histone acetylation, thereby preserving transcription of cytoprotective genes and preventing activation of the RIPK1-RIPK3-MLKL necroptotic axis.

Flow cytometric analysis revealed that USP22 overexpression conferred substantial cytoprotection against hypoxia-induced apoptosis. Hypoxia increased the apoptosis rate compared with the normoxic group (5.74 ± 0.16% vs. 4.26 ± 0.37%; P = 0.0048). Notably, OE-USP22 cells exhibited a marked reduction in apoptosis rate (3.74 ± 0.05%; P < 0.0001) and a corresponding decrease in late apoptotic/secondary necrotic cells (Annexin V⁺/PI⁺: 2.85 ± 0.08%; P < 0.05) compared with hypoxia + NC (Annexin V⁺/PI⁺: 4.06 ± 0.12%), indicating a protective effect of USP22 overexpression against hypoxia-induced cell death (Fig. 3F). TEM revealed that hypoxia induced characteristic necroptotic ultrastructural alterations, including nuclear membrane irregularity, mitochondrial swelling with cristae fragmentation, accumulation of autophagosomes (orange arrows), and compromised plasma membrane integrity. USP22 overexpression substantially attenuated these morphological derangements, demonstrating partial restoration of mitochondrial architecture, marked suppression of autophagosome formation, and preserved plasma membrane integrity relative to hypoxia-exposed control cells (Fig. 3G).

To further elucidate the subcellular mechanism by which USP22 restrains necroptosis execution, immunofluorescence microscopy with confocal imaging was employed to visualize the subcellular distribution of p-MLKL in NC and OE-USP22 cells under hypoxia. In the Hypoxia + NC group, p-MLKL displayed intense green fluorescence with pronounced membrane-edge localization, indicating robust p-MLKL accumulation at the plasma membrane, the prerequisite for its oligomerization and necroptotic pore formation. In contrast, the Hypoxia + OE-USP22 group exhibited substantially diminished p-MLKL fluorescence intensity and complete loss of membrane-edge localization. The residual p-MLKL signal was distributed diffusely throughout the cytoplasm rather than concentrated at the membrane (Fig. 3H). This subcellular translocation blockade was reflected by Western blotting, where USP22 overexpression suppressed the p-MLKL/MLKL phosphorylation ratio by approximately 72% relative to NC groups. These observations indicate that USP22 restoration selectively prevents p-MLKL translocation of p-MLKL to the plasma membrane, thereby preventing necroptotic pore formation and pathway execution.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis identified significant enrichment in cell death-related pathways (Supplementary File S1), with the apoptosis pathway (hsa04210) showing the most significant enrichment (P < 0.001), alongside ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis (hsa04120) and PI3K-Akt signaling pathways (hsa04151). Among these candidates, SFRP1 was prioritized for further mechanistic investigation owing to its established role in Wnt signaling and emerging evidence linking it to trophoblast survival and placental function.

To investigate the epigenetic regulation of SFRP1 by USP22, we performed ChIP-qPCR targeting the SFRP1 core promoter region (− 150 to + 115 bp relative to TSS). Under hypoxic conditions, baseline enrichment of H3K9ac (7.57 ± 0.35 fold), H3K27ac (6.90 ± 0.80 fold), and KAT2A (3.35 ± 0.34 fold) was detected in control cells (OE-NC), all significantly above IgG background controls (%Input: 1.04 ± 0.39%, 1.14 ± 0.15%, and 1.01 ± 0.15%, respectively; all P < 0.0001, Fig. 4B). Notably, USP22 overexpression substantially amplified all three chromatin marks: H3K9ac increased to 18.60 ± 1.70 fold (2.46-fold relative amplification; P = 0.0004), H3K27ac increased to 15.27 ± 1.82 fold (2.40-fold; P = 0.0004), and KAT2A increased to 11.22 ± 0.98 fold (3.51-fold; P = 0.0002, Fig. 4B). The disproportionately robust amplification of KAT2A occupancy combined with enhanced histone acetylation demonstrates that USP22 potentiates both KAT2A recruitment and its acetyltransferase activity at the SFRP1 promoter. Distal regions showed minimal enrichment (< 1.2 fold), confirming locus-specific effects. These ChIP-qPCR findings establish the SFRP1 promoter as a direct target of USP22-dependent epigenetic regulation.

To further explore the functional relationship between USP22 and KAT2A, we established KAT2A knockdown cell models. Among the tested siRNA sequences, si-1427 demonstrated the highest knockdown efficiency and was selected for subsequent experiments (P = 0.0188 (protein) and P < 0.0001 (mRNA) vs. NC, Fig. 4C). CCK-8 assays and flow cytometry analysis revealed that compared to the Hypoxia + NC group, the Hypoxia + si-KAT2A group exhibited significantly reduced cell viability (96.22 ± 2.47% vs. 78.15 ± 1.98%; P < 0.0001) and increased apoptosis rates (6.61 ± 0.17% vs. 10.69 ± 0.34%; P < 0.0001), indicating that KAT2A knockdown exacerbated hypoxia-induced trophoblast cell damage. Notably, in the Hypoxia + si-KAT2A + OE-USP22 group, cell viability was restored (85.70 ± 3.95%; P = 0.0051) compared to the Hypoxia + si-KAT2A group, with concomitant reduction in apoptosis rates (8.66 ± 0.40%; P = 0.0112) (Fig. 4D and E). These results suggest that USP22 overexpression can partially reverse the decline in cell viability caused by KAT2A knockdown, demonstrating a functional association between USP22 and KAT2A in maintaining trophoblast cell viability under hypoxic stress.

TEM revealed ultrastructural changes in both Hypoxia and Hypoxia + NC groups, characterized by damaged cell membranes (red arrows), fragmented organelles, presence of autophagosomes (orange arrows), non-condensed chromatin, and necrotic morphology. The Hypoxia + si-KAT2A group showed no significant improvement, with numerous lipid droplets observed (yellow arrows). In contrast, compared to the Hypoxia + si-KAT2A group, the Hypoxia + si-KAT2A + OE-USP22 group demonstrated relatively intact nuclei, significantly improved cellular morphology, and reduced lipid droplet formation (Fig. 4F). Further analysis of SFRP1 expression by qRT-PCR and Western blotting revealed that compared to the Hypoxia + NC group, SFRP1 mRNA (1.00 ± 0.05 vs. 0.52 ± 0.08; P = 0.0008) and protein expression (0.81 ± 0.01 vs. 0.27 ± 0.01; P < 0.0001) were significantly decreased in the Hypoxia + si-KAT2A group (for both). However, SFRP1 expression was significantly upregulated at both mRNA (2.28 ± 0.18, approximately 4.3-fold increase; P = 0.0001) and protein level (0.41 ± 0.01, approximately 52% recovery; P < 0.0001) in the Hypoxia + si-KAT2A + OE-USP22 group compared to the Hypoxia + si-KAT2A group (Fig. 4G and H).

To validate SFRP1 as the functional downstream effector of the USP22-KAT2A axis, an SFRP1 overexpression construct was introduced into USP22-stable-knockdown HTR-8/SVneo cells, and its effects on cell death and necroptotic signaling were evaluated under hypoxic conditions. Successful overexpression was confirmed at both mRNA (approximately 8-fold increase, P < 0.0001) and protein (approximately 2-fold increase, P = 0.0006) levels compared to empty vector controls (Fig. 4I). Flow cytometry demonstrated that compared to the si-USP22 + OE-NC group (apoptosis rate: 13.71 ± 0.91%; viable cells: 86.29%), the siUSP22 + OE-SFRP1 group exhibited a significantly reduced apoptosis rate (8.41 ± 1.09%; P = 0.0029), accompanied by a concomitant increase in the viable cell proportion (91.59%). Notably, the proportion of late apoptotic/secondary necrotic cells (Annexin V⁺/PI⁺) was substantially lower in the OE-SFRP1 group (7.02 ± 0.79%) compared to the OE-NC control (11.71 ± 0.61%), indicating that SFRP1 overexpression effectively suppresses hypoxia-induced necroptosis (Fig. 4J). Western blotting analysis further revealed that p-MLKL levels were markedly reduced in the siUSP22 + OE-SFRP1 group (0.45 ± 0.01) relative to siUSP22 + NC controls (1.00 ± 0.02), representing a 55% reduction (P < 0.0001). Total MLKL expression showed no statistically significant change between groups, indicating that the suppression of necroptosis was achieved through selective inhibition of MLKL phosphorylation rather than alterations in MLKL protein abundance (Fig. 4K). Collectively, these results demonstrate that SFRP1 restoration is sufficient to suppress MLKL phosphorylation and attenuate necroptotic cell death in USP22-knockdown trophoblast cells under hypoxia, establishing SFRP1 as a functional downstream effector of the USP22-KAT2A regulatory axis.

USP22 USP22 deubiquitinates KAT2A and maintains its stability in trophoblast cells

To investigate whether USP22 regulates KAT2A protein stability, cycloheximide (CHX) chase assays were conducted with or without the proteasome inhibitor MG132. Under normoxic conditions, KAT2A protein levels declined slowly throughout the 6 h observation window in CHX-treated cells, indicating a relatively long basal half-life under physiological conditions (Fig. 5A). By contrast, KAT2A expression was significantly decreased in the Hypoxia + CHX group relative to the CHX-only group at each time point (0, 2, 4, and 6 h), demonstrating that hypoxic stress significantly accelerates KAT2A degradation. Importantly, USP22 overexpression (Hypoxia + OE-USP22 + CHX) significantly restored KAT2A protein levels compared with the Hypoxia + CHX group, confirming that USP22 effectively counteracts hypoxia-induced KAT2A degradation. To further define the degradation pathway involved, MG132 was co-administered with CHX. MG132 treatment elevated KAT2A protein levels across all groups compared with their respective non-MG132–treated counterparts (Fig. 5B), confirming proteasome-mediated degradation of KAT2A. Nevertheless, KAT2A levels in the Hypoxia + CHX + MG132 group remained significantly lower than those in the CHX + MG132 group, while USP22 overexpression (Hypoxia + OE-USP22 + CHX + MG132) further rescued this reduction even under proteasomal blockade. This observation indicates that USP22-mediated stabilization of KAT2A is not dependent on blocking proteasomal entry per se. Collectively, these findings suggest that USP22 stabilizes KAT2A not by competing for proteasomal entry, but rather by directly removing ubiquitin chains at the upstream ubiquitination level, thereby reversing the degradation signal and prolonging KAT2A half-life.

To directly examine whether USP22 modulates KAT2A ubiquitination, Co-IP coupled with Western blotting analysis was performed. The level of ubiquitinated KAT2A immunoprecipitated with an anti-ubiquitin antibody was significantly reduced in OE-USP22 cells (0.26 ± 0.04) compared with OE-NC control cells (1.05 ± 0.13), representing 75% reduction (P < 0.0001, Fig. 5C). No significant difference was observed between the unmanipulated NC control (1.00 ± 0.05) and OE-NC empty vector control (P>0.05), confirming that the reduction in ubiquitination is specifically attributable to USP22 overexpression and not to vector transfection per se. These findings demonstrate that USP22 exerts its stabilizing effect on KAT2A by selectively removing polyubiquitin chains, thereby protecting KAT2A from degradation via the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway.

To determine the specific ubiquitin chain linkage type targeted by USP22 to rigorously establish the catalytic activity requirement for KAT2A deubiquitination, a catalytically inactive USP22 point mutant (USP22 C185A) with alanine substitution at the catalytic cysteine 185 was constructed and validated. This mutant retains the ability to bind substrates but lacks enzymatic activity, thereby allowing us to distinguish between USP22’s substrate binding versus its catalytic function. Successful overexpression was confirmed at both the mRNA (8.88 ± 0.19 vs. 1.00 ± 0.03; P < 0.0001) and protein levels (2.21 ± 0.17 vs. 1.00 ± 0.01; P = 0.0002, Fig. 5D). Co-IP experiments were then performed in USP22 stable knockdown cells transfected with either wild-type USP22 (OE-USP22 WT) or a catalytically inactive mutant (OE-USP22 C185A) under hypoxia (Fig. 5E). Compared with the OE-NC empty vector control (0.99 ± 0.06), rescue with OE-USP22 WT markedly reduced K48-Ub levels to 0.20 ± 0.03 (P < 0.0001), representing approximately 5-fold reduction in both the total cell lysate (Input) and within the USP22-immunoprecipitated complex. To interrogate the catalytic requirement of USP22, the catalytically inactive OE-USP22 C185A mutant was used as a critical control (Fig. 5E). In USP22 knockdown cells reconstituted with OE-USP22 C185A, we observed significant accumulation of K48-Ub (2.75 ± 0.21) compared to the corresponding empty vector control (OE-NC: 1.08 ± 0.13; P = 0.0003), representing approximately 2.55-fold increase. This accumulation mirrors the knockdown phenotype and directly contrasts with the WT rescue, demonstrating that catalytic activity is essential for K48-Ub removal. K63-Ub levels remained essentially unchanged in both WT and C185A conditions (OE-USP22 C185A: 1.00 ± 0.09 vs. OE-NC: 0.98 ± 0.08; P = 0.1257), further reinforcing that USP22 specifically targets K48-linked chains. Most strikingly, the 13.75-fold quantitative contrast between OE-USP22 WT and OE-USP22 C185A (0.20 ± 0.03 vs. 2.75 ± 0.21; P < 0.0001) unambiguously demonstrates that USP22 selectively catalyzes K48-chain removal from KAT2A in a strictly catalytic activity-dependent manner, establishing that loss of catalytic activity (C185A) results in robust K48-Ub accumulation and subsequent proteasomal degradation of KAT2A. Conversely, functional USP22 (WT) maintains low K48-Ub levels, thereby protecting KAT2A from degradation.

To evaluate the functional significance of USP22 deubiquitinase activity in the context of hypoxia-induced cell death, Flow cytometry was performed in USP22 stable knockdown cells reconstituted with either OE-USP22 WT or OE-USP22 C185A under hypoxia (Fig. 5F).Total apoptosis rates were significantly higher in cells expressing OE-USP22 C185A (13.10 ± 1.45%) compared to OE-USP22 WT-expressing cells (8.62 ± 1.20%; P = 0.0145), indicating that the catalytically inactive mutant fails to suppress hypoxia-induced apoptosis. Analysis of late apoptotic/secondary necrotic cells (Annexin V+/PI+) showed a similar pattern: cells expressing OE-USP22 C185A exhibited elevated death rates (11.52 ± 1.07%) compared to OE-USP22 WT (7.41 ± 0.89%; P = 0.007), confirming the critical role of USP22 catalytic activity in protecting against hypoxia-induced necroptosis. these results establish that USP22 maintains KAT2A protein stability and function by selectively removing K48-linked polyubiquitin chains, thereby preventing proteasomal degradation of KAT2A and protecting trophoblast cells from hypoxia-induced necroptosis.

Discussion

This study demonstrates for the first time that the USP22-KAT2A-SFRP1 epigenetic regulatory axis plays a critical role in trophoblast necroptosis during PE. Our findings reveal that USP22 downregulation in PE placental tissues disrupts KAT2A-mediated histone acetylation, leading to SFRP1 suppression and subsequent trophoblast necroptosis activation. These results establish a novel mechanistic framework linking epigenetic dysregulation to trophoblast dysfunction in PE pathogenesis.

Necroptosis as a primary pathological mechanism in preeclampsia

Our immunohistochemical analysis revealed an inverse relationship between USP22 expression and necroptosis markers (RIPK1, RIPK3, MLKL) in PE placentas. These elevated necroptosis markers are consistent with recent findings demonstrating significantly higher RIPK1, RIPK3, and p-MLKL levels in severe PE compared to normotensive pregnancies [27]. These observations align with earlier reports by Hannan et al. [10] and Bailey et al. [11], demonstrating necroptosis component activation in human placenta, and provide the foundation for investigating the mechanistic role of USP22 in this pathway.

The specificity of necroptosis in our hypoxic trophoblast model was rigorously established through multi-level pharmacological validation. Nec-1 is a selective RIPK1 kinase inhibitor that blocks RIPK1 autophosphorylation at serine 166 and prevents necrosome assembly [28, 29]. This approach is further supported by Lv et al. [13], who demonstrated that Nec-1 restores trophoblast survival under pathological conditions, reinforcing the functional relevance of RIPK1-dependent necroptosis in placental pathology. Nec-1 pretreatment reversed hypoxia-induced necroptotic activation at multiple independent levels: reduced p-MLKL/MLKL ratio by Western blotting, decreased PI+/Annexin V+ cell proportions by flow cytometry [26], and preserved plasma membrane integrity by TEM. Notably, total MLKL protein remained unchanged under acute hypoxia, while the p-MLKL/MLKL ratio was significantly elevated, suggesting that MLKL phosphorylation rather than total protein accumulation is the critical executor event in this context [6, 8]. This distinction has important mechanistic implications, as it places the pathological bottleneck at the RIPK3-to-MLKL phosphorylation step, rather than at upstream transcriptional induction of MLKL, identifying this node as a priority therapeutic target. The consequences of trophoblast necroptosis in PE extend beyond local cell loss: necroptosis-driven plasma membrane rupture releases damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that amplify systemic inflammatory cascades [6, 9], potentially contributing to the endothelial dysfunction and hypertensive phenotype characteristic of the disease [10, 11].

Clinical immunohistochemical data and hypoxic cell model findings both demonstrate reciprocal changes in USP22 and necroptosis markers. This convergence supports the interpretation that USP22 downregulation is mechanistically causative rather than epiphenomenal in necroptosis activation. This is consistent with reports by Roedig et al. [18], who demonstrated that USP22 controls necroptotic signaling through regulation of RIPK3 ubiquitination in tumor cell lines, and with Liu et al. [30], who showed that USP22-mediated ADAM9 deubiquitination suppresses trophoblast dysfunction in PE. Collectively, these findings position USP22 as a context-dependent but functionally conserved regulator of cell fate decisions in both neoplastic and placental biology.

USP22 constitutively suppresses necroptotic machinery

Mechanistic studies demonstrated that USP22 exerts constitutive, stress-independent inhibitory control over the necroptotic machinery. USP22 knockdown under normoxic conditions alone was sufficient to upregulate RIPK1, RIPK3, and the p-MLKL/MLKL ratio, establishing that its restraint of the necroptosis pathway is a baseline regulatory function rather than an inducible stress response. This tonic suppression is conceptually analogous to the homeostatic functions recently attributed to other deubiquitinating enzymes in trophoblasts. Together, these observations underscore that trophoblast cells are intrinsically primed for necroptotic execution, a latent death-competent state that must be actively held in check by regulatory factors like USP22.

Systematic pharmacological pathway dissection provided unambiguous functional specificity. Near-complete rescue by Nec-1 contrasted sharply with the absence of protection by Z-VAD-FMK and Ferrostatin-1, formally excluding apoptosis and ferroptosis as major contributors. These results collectively identify RIPK1-dependent necroptosis as the dominant death program under USP22 deficiency. Belnacasan, a pyroptosis inhibitor, conferred moderate but statistically significant protection against hypoxia-induced cell death. However, this should not be interpreted as evidence of independent pyroptosis. Rather, it likely reflects necroptosis-pyroptosis crosstalk: activated RIPK3 engages the NLRP3 inflammasome, promoting caspase-1-mediated Gasdermin D cleavage. This generates a secondary pyroptotic amplification component superimposed on the primary necroptotic program [6]. This interpretation is consistent with the substantially inferior protection afforded by Belnacasan compared to Nec-1, and aligns with emerging evidence that NLRP3 activation contributes to preeclamptic placental inflammation [15]. Whether this crosstalk represents a biologically significant mechanism in the PE microenvironment warrants dedicated investigation.

Paradoxically, autophagy inhibition with 3-MA exacerbated cell death. This finding reveals that autophagy functions as an adaptive survival buffer rather than a cell death effector in trophoblasts subjected to combined USP22 deficiency and hypoxia [31, 32]. This pharmacological finding is mechanistically corroborated by TEM. USP22 overexpression markedly reduced autophagosome accumulation, indicating that suppression of the necroptotic pathway simultaneously relieves the upstream stress driving compensatory autophagy. The convergence of these pharmacological and ultrastructural findings across complementary experimental approaches reveals a dynamic reciprocal relationship between necroptosis and autophagy in USP22-deficient trophoblasts. Autophagy constitutes a secondary cytoprotective buffer when the primary regulatory mechanism (necroptosis suppression) is disrupted.

At the subcellular level, confocal immunofluorescence microscopy revealed characteristic membrane-edge enrichment of p-MLKL under hypoxia. These findings support MLKL oligomerization and directed translocation to the plasma membrane, where lytic pores are formed to drive necroptotic execution [8, 9]. USP22 overexpression abolished this membrane-edge enrichment and dispersed the residual p-MLKL signal diffusely throughout the cytoplasm. These findings demonstrate that USP22 interrupts necroptosis at two mechanistically distinct checkpoints: RIPK3-mediated MLKL phosphorylation and downstream membrane translocation. Notably, this suppressive role of USP22 in trophoblast necroptosis is mechanistically distinct from the pro-necroptotic function reported by Roedig et al. [18] in tumor cells, where USP22 promotes necroptosis by deubiquitinating RIPK3 at K518 to facilitate necrosome assembly. This divergence underscores the cell-type-specific nature of USP22 biology and highlights that its necroptosis-regulatory function is determined by cellular context rather than a universal mechanism, a distinction with significant implications for therapeutic targeting.

RNA-seq analysis identified 1,186 DEGs upon USP22 knockdown: 859 upregulated, 327 downregulated. The predominance of upregulated genes is consistent with USP22’s primary function as a transcriptional activator through histone H2A/H2B deubiquitination within the SAGA complex [33, 34]. Four validated downstream cytoprotective targets were identified: ARNT2 (hypoxic adaptation), BNIP3 (mitochondrial quality control), STEAP3 (redox homeostasis), and SFRP1 (Wnt signaling regulation). Among these, SFRP1 was prioritized for mechanistic follow-up given its established role as a Wnt pathway antagonist and emerging connections to trophoblast survival and placental function [20–22].

The USP22-KAT2A-SFRP1 epigenetic axis

A central mechanistic advance of this study is the identification of the USP22-KAT2A-SFRP1 axis as an epigenetic regulatory pathway through which USP22 exerts its cytoprotective function. The direct physical interaction between USP22 and KAT2A provides the molecular foundation for this axis, confirmed by Co-IP and supported by a STRING database interaction score of 0.998. USP22 and KAT2A co-localize as functionally coupled modules of the SAGA complex (the DUB and HAT modules, respectively), enabling coordinated regulation of histone modifications at target promoters [33, 35]. Importantly, USP22 expression levels positively correlated with KAT2A protein stability across experimental conditions, suggesting that USP22 exerts post-translational control over KAT2A abundance. The precise biochemical mechanism underlying this stabilization is addressed in detail below. KAT2A has been implicated as a critical epigenetic driver in multiple disease contexts [19, 36, 37]. The present work extends this landscape by demonstrating that KAT2A-mediated histone acetylation specifically suppresses trophoblast necroptosis in PE.

ChIP-qPCR analyses provided essential locus-specific resolution to complement the global histone acetylation data. Significant and coordinate enrichment of H3K9ac, H3K27ac, and KAT2A occupancy was detected at the SFRP1 core promoter (-150 to + 115 bp relative to the TSS) under hypoxic conditions. Minimal enrichment was observed at distal promoter regions, confirming the spatial specificity of this regulatory event.

These histone marks are canonically associated with transcriptionally active chromatin [38, 39]. The co-enrichment of these marks with direct KAT2A recruitment at the SFRP1 core promoter establishes that KAT2A-mediated histone acetylation operates in a locus-specific manner to sustain SFRP1 transcriptional activity under hypoxic stress. These locus-specific chromatin findings collectively establish a direct mechanistic link between the USP22-KAT2A axis and SFRP1 transcriptional output. This is consistent with the established role of hypoxic signaling in remodeling histone-modifying enzyme activity and chromatin architecture [40].

The epistatic hierarchy within the USP22-KAT2A axis was formally established through reciprocal gain- and loss-of-function experiments. KAT2A knockdown phenocopied USP22 loss-of-function, impairing trophoblast viability and suppressing SFRP1 expression, while USP22 overexpression partially but significantly rescued the KAT2A knockdown phenotype at both mRNA and protein levels. The partial nature of this rescue is mechanistically interpretable. USP22 overexpression can restore KAT2A protein stability and thereby increase SFRP1 promoter acetylation, but cannot fully compensate for the absence of KAT2A acetyltransferase protein itself. This places KAT2A unambiguously downstream of USP22, establishing a linear (rather than parallel) regulatory hierarchy in which KAT2A functions as the effector acetyltransferase. TEM analysis corroborated this interpretation, showing that the siKAT2A + OE-USP22 group exhibited improved nuclear integrity and reduced lipid droplet accumulation compared to siKAT2A alone. The appearance of lipid droplets specifically upon KAT2A knockdown may reflect KAT2A’s reported role in regulating lipid metabolic gene expression through H3K9 acetylation [36, 41].

SFRP1 as the functional effector of necroptosis suppression

The most direct functional evidence linking the USP22-KAT2A epigenetic axis to necroptotic cell death machinery comes from SFRP1 rescue experiments in stable USP22 knockdown trophoblasts. SFRP1 overexpression significantly reduced total cell death and decreased the p-MLKL/MLKL ratio without altering total MLKL expression. This demonstrates that SFRP1 acts upstream of RIPK3-mediated MLKL phosphorylation, which represents the terminal execution step of necroptosis. These findings provide direct functional proof that restoration of a single downstream epigenetic target (SFRP1) is sufficient to substantially attenuate necroptotic commitment within USP22-deficient cells.

Multiple mechanistic pathways converge to promote necroptotic commitment upon SFRP1 depletion. SFRP1 contains a cysteine-rich domain (CRD) and netrin-like domain (NLD) that cooperatively sequester extracellular Wnt ligands, preventing their binding to Frizzled receptors and subsequent activation of the canonical Wnt/β-catenin pathway [42]. Upon SFRP1 downregulation, loss of Wnt ligand sequestration permits excessive β-catenin nuclear accumulation. This drives NF-κB-dependent upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines including TNF-α and IL-1β [43, 44]. These mediators engage TNFR1-mediated RIPK1 signaling [45], creating a feed-forward inflammatory loop that amplifies necroptotic commitment. In addition to TNF-α/IL-1β signaling, reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated under hypoxic stress facilitate RIPK1 autophosphorylation at serine 166, a critical step for RIPK3 recruitment and necrosome assembly [28, 29].

Additionally, SFRP1 loss disrupts the expression of anti-necroptotic checkpoint proteins, particularly the caspase-8-cFLIP(L) complex [46]. This suppression shifts the cellular balance further toward necroptosis by removing a key brake on RIPK3-dependent signaling. This multi-node mechanism is consistent with the prior work by Bao et al. [22], who demonstrated that SFRP1 deficiency disrupts trophoblast differentiation through dysregulated Wnt/β-catenin signaling. The present findings substantially extend this understanding by establishing SFRP1 as a novel necroptosis suppressor in placental trophoblasts. The importance of dysregulated Wnt signaling in placental pathology is further supported by Chronopoulou et al. [47], who demonstrated that both excessive activation and suppression of Wnt signaling are associated with adverse placental outcomes, including pregnancy loss.

USP22-mediated KAT2A stabilization through K48 deubiquitination

Having established that USP22-KAT2A physical interaction positively regulates KAT2A protein stability, we next sought to elucidate the precise biochemical mechanism by which USP22 controls KAT2A abundance. CHX chase assays demonstrated that hypoxia significantly accelerates KAT2A protein degradation. USP22 overexpression counteracts this accelerated turnover, and this stabilizing effect persists upon treatment with the proteasome inhibitor MG132. These findings indicate that USP22’s stabilizing function operates through direct deubiquitination rather than secondary effects on proteasome activity. Co-IP analysis directly confirmed that USP22 overexpression substantially reduces total ubiquitinated KAT2A, whereas USP22 knockdown increases it. These reciprocal changes establish deubiquitination as the primary stabilization mechanism.

Linkage-specific ubiquitination analysis using the catalytically inactive USP22 C185A mutant revealed that USP22 selectively cleaves K48-linked polyubiquitin chains from KAT2A, which serve as the canonical proteasomal degradation signal. USP22 did not perturb K63-linked chains, which mediate non-proteolytic functions such as DNA damage signaling [48]. This selectivity is consistent with the conserved catalytic mechanism of USP family members. It extends USP22’s known substrate repertoire-previously including SIRT1 [49] and PD-L1 [50], to encompass KAT2A as a novel target with direct functional relevance to trophoblast survival. The functional significance of this catalytic specificity is demonstrated by cells reconstituted with USP22 C185A, which exhibited significantly higher necroptosis rates than those with wild-type USP22. These findings establish that USP22’s enzymatic activity is indispensable for its cytoprotective function.

This mechanistic finding differentiates USP22-mediated KAT2A stabilization from other regulatory interactions within the SAGA complex. It also provides a biochemical foundation for future pharmacological strategies aimed at enhancing USP22 deubiquitinase activity under hypoxic conditions. In the context of PE, where USP22 is significantly downregulated, chronic hypoxia would further compromise USP22 activity. This leads to accelerated KAT2A degradation via K48-linked polyubiquitination, suppression of epigenetic maintenance, and ultimately amplified necroptosis of trophoblast and placental dysfunction [51].

In vivo validation in a rat preeclampsia model

The translational relevance of the USP22-KAT2A-SFRP1 axis is substantiated by validation in the well-established L-NAME-induced rat model of PE. This model successfully recapitulated the canonical PE phenotype, including significant hypertension, proteinuria, fetal growth restriction, reduced placental weight, and angiogenic factor imbalance (elevated serum sFlt-1, reduced PlGF, and increased sFlt-1/PlGF ratio) [25, 52]. Placental tissues from these PE model rats exhibited coordinated downregulation of all three components (USP22, KAT2A, and SFRP1) at both protein and mRNA levels. This was accompanied by decreased H3K27ac and H3K9ac histone acetylation, closely mirroring the molecular pattern observed in human PE placentas. This parallel dysregulation of the entire regulatory cascade in an independent in vivo system strongly supports the pathophysiological relevance of this epigenetic network in PE development. Moreover, these findings suggest that the USP22→KAT2A→SFRP1 regulatory hierarchy represents a genuine component of PE pathogenesis rather than a culture artifact.

These findings align with prior evidence of aberrant histone modifications in preeclamptic placental tissues. Jiang et al. demonstrated that HDAC5-mediated epigenetic regulation of CYR61 contributes to PE development [53]. Similarly, Meister et al. showed that PPARγ influences H3K4me3 and H3K9ac modifications in extravillous trophoblast cells [54]. These studies collectively indicate that epigenetic dysregulation is a recurrent pathological theme in PE rather than an isolated observation. The current work adds mechanistic depth by identifying USP22-mediated deubiquitination as an upstream regulator of the placental epigenetic landscape. Notably, some studies have reported contrasting histone acetylation patterns in PE. For example, Zhang et al. [55] and Liu et al. [56] observed elevated H3K27 acetylation in certain PE subsets. These discrepancies are most plausibly attributable to several factors: PE heterogeneity across patient populations, differences in disease severity and gestational timing, and the distinction between global versus locus-specific histone acetylation patterns. Our targeted ChIP-qPCR approach specifically examined USP22-KAT2A-regulated loci rather than genome-wide acetylation profiles. The concordance between in vitro epigenetic data and in vivo findings in the rat model strengthens confidence that the locus-specific changes described here are genuine and reproducible. This targeted mechanistic approach complements broader epigenetic studies and contributes to a more granular understanding of PE pathogenesis.

Taken together, the convergent in vitro, epigenomic, and in vivo evidence presented in this study delineates the USP22-KAT2A-SFRP1 axis as a previously unrecognized epigenetic checkpoint governing trophoblast survival in PE. These findings extend current mechanistic understanding by demonstrating how deubiquitinase activity and chromatin regulation cooperate to determine cell fate at the maternal-fetal interface. Future studies should address the therapeutic feasibility of targeting this axis in vivo, and explore whether its dysregulation extends to other placental pathologies beyond PE.

Study limitations and future perspectives

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, as a multifunctional deubiquitinase, USP22 likely regulates additional substrates beyond KAT2A that may contribute to PE pathogenesis. Our RNA-seq analysis identified 1,186 DEGs upon USP22 knockdown. Among these, we validated four key targets: ARNT2, BNIP3, STEAP3, and SFRP1, which collectively suggest a cytoprotective network extending beyond the KAT2A-SFRP1 axis characterized in this study. Key questions remain: whether these targets operate in parallel with or downstream of the USP22-KAT2A axis, and whether they ultimately converge on necroptotic or alternative cell death pathways. Second, although our L-NAME rat model confirmed coordinated downregulation of the USP22-KAT2A-SFRP1 axis in vivo, the current study did not evaluate whether restoring this axis ameliorates systemic PE phenotypes. Third, reliance on the immortalized HTR-8/SVneo cell line may not fully recapitulate the cellular heterogeneity of the human placenta. Validation in primary trophoblast cultures and organoid systems would substantially strengthen translational relevance.

Future research should prioritize several directions. Mechanistically, future work should: (1) determine whether USP22 regulates additional substrates that directly regulate the RIPK1-RIPK3-MLKL cascade, and (2) delineate the functional relationship between USP22-dependent H3K9ac/H3K27ac modifications and other chromatin regulatory mechanisms at the maternal-fetal interface. From a translational perspective, the catalytic indispensability of USP22 provides strong mechanistic rationale for developing targeted therapeutics: (1) small-molecule enhancers of USP22 deubiquitinase activity, and (2) stabilizers of the USP22-KAT2A protein-protein interaction. Furthermore, prospective clinical studies should systematically evaluate placental USP22 expression as a diagnostic or prognostic biomarker for PE, with particular focus on early-onset disease.

Conclusion

This study establishes the USP22-KAT2A-SFRP1 epigenetic regulatory axis as a novel and critical mechanism underlying trophoblast necroptosis in PE. USP22 maintains trophoblast survival under hypoxic stress via selectively detaching K48-linked polyubiquitin chains from KAT2A, thus preventing its proteasomal breakdown. This stabilizes KAT2A, sustaining KAT2A-mediated H3K9ac and H3K27ac enrichment at the SFRP1 promoter (Fig. 7). Loss of SFRP1, in turn, unleashes RIPK1-RIPK3-MLKL-dependent necroptotic execution, as evidenced by the significant reduction in p-MLKL/MLKL ratio upon SFRP1 restoration in USP22-deficient trophoblasts. Coordinated downregulation of USP22, KAT2A, SFRP1, and associated histone acetylation marks was consistently observed in both the in vitro hypoxic model and the in vivo L-NAME-induced rat PE model, providing experimental validation across complementary systems. These findings illuminate the molecular crosstalk between protein ubiquitination homeostasis and epigenetic regulation in governing trophoblast fate at the maternal-fetal interface. By identifying the USP22-KAT2A-SFRP1 axis as a critical node governing necroptotic execution, this work reveals multiple tractable therapeutic targets for addressing placental insufficiency in PE.

Schematic model of the USP22-KAT2A-SFRP1 epigenetic regulatory axis in trophoblast necroptosis during PE. Left Panel (USP22-high Protective Pathway): Under physiological conditions, high USP22 expression maintains trophoblast survival via a protective epigenetic cascade. USP22 deubiquitinase activity removes ubiquitin (Ub) from KAT2A, preventing its proteasomal degradation and maintaining KAT2A stability. Stabilized KAT2A translocates to the nucleus, catalyzing histone acetylation (Ac) at the SFRP1 promoter. This epigenetic activation enhances SFRP1 mRNA transcription and protein expression. Elevated SFRP1 suppresses RIPK1 and RIPK3 kinase activation, inhibiting MLKL-mediated necroptosis and promoting trophoblast survival. Right Panel (USP22-low Pathological Pathway): In PE, reduced USP22 expression disrupts the protective axis, leading to trophoblast necroptosis. Decreased USP22 deubiquitinase activity increases KAT2A K48-linked ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation. Loss of KAT2A reduces histone H3K9 and H3K27 acetylation at the SFRP1 promoter, suppressing transcription and decreasing SFRP1 expression. Reduced SFRP1 fails to inhibit RIPK1/RIPK3 kinase activity, resulting in necrosome assembly and MLKL-mediated plasma membrane disruption. This ultimately leads to trophoblast necroptosis, contributing to PE pathogenesis via impaired placental development and function.
