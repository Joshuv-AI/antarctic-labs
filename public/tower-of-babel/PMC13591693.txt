Prevalence of overt hypothyroidism and subclinical hypothyroidism in metabolic syndrome: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Arman Shekarian, Sadegh Mazaheri-Tehrani, Saba Shekarian, Mahsa Setudeh, Melika Pourbazargan, Reza Amani-Beni, Maryam Heidarpour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThyroid hormones regulate insulin sensitivity and lipid metabolism, both of which are central to metabolic syndrome (MetS). While previous systematic reviews have identified an association between MetS and hypothyroidism, the prevalence of overt hypothyroidism and subclinical hypothyroidism (SCH) in this population remains unclear, with reported rates varying widely. The present study aimed to estimate the prevalence of overt hypothyroidism and SCH in individuals with MetS.MethodsWe searched PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science from inception to February 10, 2025. Observational studies that reported the prevalence of overt hypothyroidism or SCH in more than 30 individuals with MetS were included. The risk of bias was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) checklist for prevalence studies. Pooled prevalence estimates, corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and 95% prediction intervals (PIs) were calculated using the random-effects model.ResultsForty studies with a total of 25,008 MetS cases were included. The pooled prevalence estimates were 9.2% (95% CI: 6.2%; 12.7%) (95% PI: 0.0%; 32.7%) for overt hypothyroidism and 20.7% (95% CI: 15.6%; 26.3%) (95% PI: 0.1%; 60.1%) for SCH. Subgroup analysis and meta-regression indicated that high-risk-of-bias studies reported higher prevalence rates. Estimates from low-risk-of-bias studies, which are likely less affected by methodological biases, were 4.8% (95% CI: 1.9%; 8.9%) (95% PI: 0.0%; 24.8%) for overt hypothyroidism and 13.1% (95% CI: 9.1%; 17.8%) (95% PI: 0.5%; 38.2%) for SCH.ConclusionBased on low-risk-of-bias studies, the prevalence of overt hypothyroidism in MetS does not appear substantially higher than estimates reported in the general population, whereas SCH is more common. The high prevalence of SCH in MetS and its potential adverse effects on MetS components highlight the potential clinical relevance of thyroid function testing in these individuals. Further cohort and trial studies are necessary to clarify the metabolic significance of SCH in patients with MetS and to determine whether its treatment provides clinical benefit.Systematic review registration number in PROSPEROCRD42024541953.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12902-026-02376-x.Keywords: Metabolic syndrome, Hypothyroidism, Subclinical hypothyroidism, Meta-analysis, Prevalence

Introduction

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is a pathological condition characterized by a combination of cardiometabolic risk factors that markedly increase the risk of developing type 2 diabetes mellitus and cardiovascular disease [1, 2]. Since 1998, several criteria have been established to identify individuals with MetS [3–7]. The National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel III (NCEP ATP III) defines MetS as having at least three of the following: waist circumference > 102 cm in men and 88 cm in women; blood pressure ≥ 130/85 mmHg; fasting plasma glucose ≥ 110 mg/dL; triglycerides ≥ 150 mg/dL; HDL cholesterol < 40 mg/dL in men and < 50 mg/dL in women [4]. The global prevalence of MetS continues to rise. According to a meta-analysis, depending on the diagnostic criteria, 26% to 46% of people in the Americas and 22% to 31% in Europe are diagnosed with MetS [8].

Thyroid hormones are central to maintaining an optimal metabolic rate. Overt hypothyroidism, commonly referred to as hypothyroidism, occurs when the thyroid gland produces inadequate amounts of thyroid hormones, often leading to fatigue and weight gain. These hormones also play a major role in regulating insulin sensitivity and lipid profile, both of which are key components in MetS [9, 10]. Subclinical hypothyroidism (SCH) is a mild form of hypothyroidism in which thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) is elevated while free thyroid hormones are within the reference range. This condition is known to affect roughly 4–10% of adults [11]. Although often asymptomatic, as indicated by the term “subclinical,” SCH has been associated with adverse effects on lipid metabolism and insulin sensitivity [12, 13]. A large cohort study with 66,822 participants found that individuals with MetS had a 21% higher risk of developing SCH during a mean follow-up period of 4.2 years after adjusting for risk factors [14]. This finding is further supported by a systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrating an increased risk of developing SCH in individuals with MetS [15]. Looking from the opposite direction, a meta-analysis found that patients with SCH were more likely to develop MetS compared to euthyroid individuals, with an odds ratio of 1.28 (95% CI: 1.19; 1.39). Their analysis also revealed a significant association between SCH and four of the five MetS components [16]. Another recently published systematic review further supports this bidirectional association, underscoring the interplay between MetS and SCH [17].

Researchers have reported inconsistent results regarding the prevalence of overt hypothyroidism and SCH among patients with MetS. In several studies, overt hypothyroidism has been documented in fewer than 5% of cases [18–20]. On the other hand, some found that overt hypothyroidism was present in more than 20% of those with MetS [21–23]. The same variability exists for SCH. While many researchers have observed a prevalence of less than 10% [14, 24, 25], others have reported prevalence rates of over 40% [21, 26, 27]. Although previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses have examined the association between MetS and thyroid dysfunction, a pooled prevalence estimate of overt hypothyroidism and SCH in MetS is still lacking. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to estimate the pooled prevalence of overt hypothyroidism and SCH in individuals with MetS.

Methods

Protocol registration

This systematic review adhered to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [28]. The study protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO with the registration number CRD42024541953.

Search strategy

A comprehensive search was conducted in four electronic databases: PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science. The searches were performed on February 10, 2025, with no publication year restriction. The searches were limited to the English language. Relevant search terms were identified using Medical Subject Headings (MeSH), Emtree, and expert opinion. We also manually screened the reference lists of included studies and relevant review articles for additional eligible studies. The complete search strategies for each database are available in Supplementary Table S1.

Eligibility criteria

We included observational studies reporting the prevalence of overt hypothyroidism or SCH in adults (aged ≥ 18 years) with MetS. Studies were required to include more than 30 individuals with MetS to minimize instability in prevalence estimates arising from very small sample sizes. We excluded studies that: (i) involved pregnant, postpartum, or lactating women; (ii) included patients receiving medications known to alter thyroid function (except thyroid hormone replacement therapy); (iii) involved individuals with a history of thyroidectomy or radiation to the head and neck; (iv) included patients with central hypothyroidism, kidney disease, or hepatic failure; (v) were not available as full-text English articles; or (vi) were case reports, case series, conference papers, clinical trials, animal studies, or review articles.

Study selection

After removing duplicate records in EndNote software version 20, two reviewers (MP and SS) independently screened titles and abstracts. The same reviewers then independently assessed the full texts of potentially eligible studies. Disagreements in the study selection were resolved by discussion. A third expert reviewer (MH) made the final decision when disagreements persisted.

Data extraction

An Excel data extraction form was used to extract the following information from each study: first author, publication year, study design, country, number of individuals with MetS, number of males and females with MetS, mean age and body mass index (BMI) with corresponding standard deviations (SD), MetS diagnostic criteria, and number of MetS patients with overt hypothyroidism and SCH. Two authors (MS and SS) independently extracted the data. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, and when they persisted, the opinion of a third expert reviewer (RA-B) was sought.

Risk of bias assessment

The Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklist for prevalence studies was used to assess risk of bias [29]. This tool comprises nine items that evaluate key methodological aspects of prevalence studies. As the checklist does not provide formal cut-offs, studies scoring ≥ 5 out of 9 items were classified as having a low risk of bias, reflecting that more than half of the key methodological criteria were satisfied. Studies scoring ≤ 4 were classified as having a high risk of bias, indicating substantial methodological limitations. The chosen threshold is consistent with the approach used in a previously published systematic review in BMJ Open [30]. Two authors (SM-T and AS) independently conducted the risk of bias assessment. Reviewer disagreements were resolved through discussion, and when consensus could not be reached, a third expert reviewer (MH) made the final decision.

Statistical analysis

R software version 4.4.0 was used for statistical analysis. The pooled prevalence and corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated using the metaprop function within the meta package [31]. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the inconsistency index (I2). Heterogeneity was interpreted as low (I² < 25%), moderate (25%–50%), or high (> 50%). Because of substantial methodological and statistical heterogeneity, a random-effects model was applied. To provide a more informative measure of variability across populations, 95% prediction intervals (PIs), which estimate the expected range of prevalence in future studies, were also calculated for all pooled prevalence estimates.

We conducted subgroup analyses based on nine variables: publication year, sample size, risk of bias, country, TSH diagnostic cut-off, MetS diagnostic criteria, study design, mean age, and mean BMI of patients. Subgroup analysis was performed to explore potential sources of heterogeneity and investigate the reasons for the wide range of reported prevalence rates across studies. Differences between groups were assessed using the test for subgroup differences.

Univariable and multivariable meta-regression analyses were performed using the metareg function in the meta package. Variables showing evidence of between-group differences in subgroup analyses were considered for inclusion in the model, including risk of bias, sample size, and MetS diagnostic criteria. Meta-regression analyses were not prespecified in the PROSPERO protocol and were conducted post hoc to quantify the proportion of heterogeneity explained (R²) and to assess whether these moderators contributed to variability in prevalence.

The presence of publication bias was evaluated using a combination of funnel plots and Begg’s and Egger’s tests [32, 33]. When funnel plot asymmetry or statistically significant results from Begg’s or Egger’s tests indicated potential publication bias, the trim-and-fill method was applied to assess its effect on the results [34]. We performed a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis by sequentially removing each study to evaluate its impact on the overall estimate [35]. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Selection process and study characteristics

Our systematic search of four databases identified a total of 4318 studies. After removing 1277 duplicates in EndNote 20 software, 3041 studies remained for title and abstract screening. Among these, 86 were selected for full-text review, and 50 were excluded for the following reasons: (i) Review articles, case reports, clinical trials, or animal studies (n = 21); (ii) lack of prevalence data (n = 16); (iii) overlapping data (n = 6); (iv) meeting one of the exclusion criteria (n = 5); (v) small sample size (n = 2). We also identified four additional eligible studies from reference lists. Ultimately, 40 studies were included in our systematic review and meta-analysis. A PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1) depicts the study selection process.

Publication dates ranged from 2007 to 2024. Twenty-two studies were conducted in India and eighteen in other countries. Most were cross-sectional (n = 31), followed by case-control (n = 8) and cohort designs (n = 1). Risk of bias assessment

Based on the JBI checklist, 18 studies scored ≥ 5 and were classified as having a low risk of bias, whereas 22 studies scored < 5 and were classified as having a high risk of bias. The average JBI score across studies was 4.28 ± 2.16. The risk of bias assessment is illustrated using the Robvis traffic-light and summary plots (Figs. 2 and 3) [65]. Detailed risk of bias assessments of individual studies are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Meta-analysis

Prevalence of overt hypothyroidism and SCH

The pooled prevalence of overt hypothyroidism across 28 studies, comprising 17,477 individuals with MetS, was 9.2% (95% CI: 6.2%; 12.7%) (95% PI: 0.0%; 32.7%). An I² value of 98.3% indicated high heterogeneity across studies. The lowest and highest reported prevalence were 0.2% and 40.1%, respectively [14, 23]. These findings are illustrated in the forest plot (Fig. 4).

Forest plot showing the pooled prevalence of overt hypothyroidism among individuals with metabolic syndrome

Sex-specific prevalence data were available in 13 studies. One study included only males, two included only females, and 10 provided data by sex. A meta-analysis of 12 studies involving 1429 women with MetS estimated a pooled prevalence of 12.4% (95% CI: 6.4%; 19.9%) (95% PI: 0.0%; 46.7%) (Fig. S1). Among men, based on data from 11 studies (n = 927), the pooled prevalence was 6.3% (95% CI: 1.5%; 13.3%) (95% PI: 0.0%; 41.4%) (Fig. S2).

Thirty-eight studies, with a combined sample size of 24,506 MetS cases, reported the prevalence of SCH. The pooled prevalence of SCH was 20.7% (95% CI: 15.6%; 26.3%) (95% PI: 0.1%; 60.1%). Between-study heterogeneity was high, as indicated by an I2 of 98.4%. The reported prevalence varied from 2.1% to 87.5% (Fig. 5).

Forest plot showing the pooled prevalence of subclinical hypothyroidism among individuals with metabolic syndrome

Regarding the sex-specific prevalence of SCH in MetS, one study included only males, three included only females, and 14 reported prevalence separately for males and females. The pooled prevalence among women, pooling data from 17 studies including 2044 patients, was 27.3% (95% CI: 21.8%; 33.3%) (95% PI: 7.5%; 53.4%) (Fig. S3). In men, the estimated pooled prevalence was 18% (95% CI: 12%; 24.9%) (95% PI: 0.6%; 49.2%) (15 studies; n = 3027) (Fig. S4).

Subgroup analysis

We performed subgroup analyses to explore potential sources of heterogeneity and to identify factors behind prevalence variations. However, heterogeneity remained high (I² > 50%) across all subgroups, suggesting that none of these factors were major contributors to heterogeneity. The test for subgroup differences indicated that differences in risk of bias between studies contributed to variations in prevalence estimates for both overt hypothyroidism and SCH. In low-risk-of-bias studies, the pooled prevalence of overt hypothyroidism was 4.8% (95% CI: 1.9%; 8.9%) (95% PI: 0.0%; 24.8%) versus 12.0% (95% CI: 8.0%; 16.6%) (95% PI: 0.1%; 36.3%) in high-risk-of-bias studies (p-value = 0.01) (Fig. 6). Similarly, low-risk-of-bias studies reported a pooled SCH prevalence of 13.1% (95% CI: 9.1%; 17.8%) (95% PI: 0.5%; 38.2%), significantly lower than the pooled estimate of 29% (95% CI: 20.6%; 38.1%) (95% PI: 0.9%; 73.2%) reported by high-risk-of-bias studies (p-value < 0.01) (Fig. 7). Studies with smaller sample sizes (< 150) had a higher pooled SCH prevalence of 27.1% (95% CI: 18.6%; 36.6%) (95% PI: 0.2%; 72.8%) compared with 15.3% (95% CI: 10.4%; 21%) (95% PI: 0.2%; 46.8%) in larger studies (≥ 150) (p-value = 0.02). Regarding MetS diagnostic criteria, SCH prevalence was highest in studies using the IDF definition, with a prevalence of 33.9% (95% CI: 22.6%; 46.2%) (95% PI: 2.7%; 77%), followed by 19.8% (95% CI: 13.7%; 26.6%) (95% PI: 0.0%; 60%) in studies using NCEP ATP III, and lowest in studies using other criteria, with a prevalence of 11.6% (95% CI: 4.7%; 21%) (95% PI: 0.0%; 53.1%) (p-value = 0.01). Meta-regression

In univariable meta-regression, risk of bias and sample size were significant moderators of prevalence for both overt hypothyroidism and SCH. For overt hypothyroidism, low-risk-of-bias studies had a lower prevalence compared with high-risk-of-bias studies (β = -0.13; p-value = 0.01), explaining 18.2% of heterogeneity. Larger sample sizes were also associated with lower prevalence (β = -0.00 per participant; p-value = 0.03), accounting for 14% of heterogeneity. MetS diagnostic criteria did not significantly affect prevalence (β = 0.05; p-value = 0.45) and explained 0% of heterogeneity. For SCH, low-risk-of-bias studies also had a lower prevalence (β = -0.20; p-value < 0.01), explaining 24.2% of heterogeneity. Studies with larger sample sizes showed lower SCH prevalence (β = -0.00 per participant; p-value = 0.01), accounting for 15.1% of heterogeneity. Although individual comparisons of IDF and other criteria versus NCEP ATP III were not statistically significant, the overall test of moderators was significant (QM, df = 2, p-value = 0.03). MetS diagnostic criteria explained 12.3% of the heterogeneity in SCH prevalence. Univariable meta-regression results are detailed in Table 3.

For overt hypothyroidism, none of the moderators reached statistical significance in the multivariable model. Table 4 presents the multivariable meta-regression results.

Publication bias and sensitivity analysis

The funnel plot for overt hypothyroidism prevalence appeared symmetrical (Fig. S7), and Begg’s test showed no evidence of publication bias (p-value = 0.15). However, Egger’s test suggested the presence of publication bias (p-value < 0.01). To assess the influence of potential publication bias, the trim-and-fill method was applied, and no additional studies were added to the analysis. Similar results were observed for the prevalence of SCH. While the funnel plot (Fig. S8) and Begg’s test (p-value = 0.71) showed no evidence of publication bias, Egger’s test (p-value < 0.01) suggested its potential existence. Nevertheless, the trim-and-fill method did not add any additional studies. The leave-one-out sensitivity analysis confirmed that prevalence estimates for both overt hypothyroidism and SCH were robust to the influence of any single study (Fig. S9 and Fig. S10).

Discussion

The prevalence of overt hypothyroidism in the general population varies widely across studies and populations. For instance, the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) from 1988 to 1994 estimated the prevalence of overt hypothyroidism in the U.S. to be 0.3% [66]. In contrast, a later NHANES analysis (2009–2012) found a substantially higher prevalence of 8.2% [67]. A review published in Nature Reviews Endocrinology highlighted this variability by citing prevalence estimates ranging from 0.3% to 3.7% in the U.S. and from 0.2% to 5.3% across European populations [68]. Among individuals with MetS, its prevalence shows even more variability. Previous studies have reported rates ranging from 0.2% to 40.1% [14, 23]. Our meta-analysis of 28 studies yielded a pooled prevalence of 9.2% for overt hypothyroidism among individuals with MetS. The pooled prevalence was nearly twice as high in women as in men (12.4% vs. 6.3%), consistent with well-established sex differences in the epidemiology of thyroid disease [69].

Since the recent NHANES data suggest a higher prevalence in the general population, we performed a subgroup analysis by publication year to assess whether a similar trend exists and whether publication year contributes to heterogeneity. Pooled prevalence rates were similar in studies published before and after 2020 (9.2% vs. 9.3%, p-value = 0.94), indicating no temporal trend. In contrast, subgroup analysis based on risk of bias revealed important differences. Studies with a high risk of bias reported substantially higher prevalence estimates compared to low-risk-of-bias studies (12.0% vs. 4.8%, p-value < 0.01). These differences may reflect the influence of methodological limitations, such as selection bias and measurement bias. Accordingly, the pooled estimate from low-risk-of-bias studies may represent a more reliable and less biased approximation of the prevalence of overt hypothyroidism in individuals with MetS. Compared with the general population, the prevalence of overt hypothyroidism in individuals with MetS does not appear to be substantially higher. Consistent with this, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis found no association between MetS at baseline and the incidence of overt hypothyroidism. However, the limited number of included studies prevented them from drawing a firm conclusion regarding the association between MetS and the incidence of thyroid disorders. Interestingly, their analysis revealed an increased risk of both overt hypothyroidism and SCH in those with obesity at baseline [70]. To assess the influence of obesity, we stratified studies based on mean BMI (≥ 30 vs. < 30). While studies with a mean BMI ≥ 30 reported higher prevalence (10.4% vs. 6.9%), the difference did not reach statistical significance (p-value = 0.44). Another key variable in the prevalence of thyroid disorders is age. A subgroup analysis based on mean participant age (≥ 50 vs. < 50) showed that studies with a mean age ≥ 50 had a higher prevalence (13.9% vs. 8.5%), but again, this difference did not reach statistical significance (p-value = 0.16).

Autoimmune thyroid disease is the leading cause of hypothyroidism in iodine-sufficient regions. In contrast, iodine deficiency remains the predominant etiology in areas with insufficient dietary iodine [67]. India’s national program to promote salt iodization dates back to 1983, yet hypothyroidism remains a common problem in certain regions [71]. An epidemiological study across eight major Indian cities reported a hypothyroidism prevalence of 11% in the adult population [72]. Notably, over half of the studies included in our meta-analysis were conducted in India. Accordingly, we conducted a subgroup analysis to assess whether prevalence estimates differed by geographic region. The difference in prevalence between Indian studies (9.7%) and those from other countries (8.5%) was not significant (p-value = 0.77). Although the pooled estimates were comparable, it is important to note that hypothyroidism prevalence varies widely, even within a single country. For instance, in the aforementioned study, prevalence ranged from 8.9% to 21.7% across Indian cities [72].

According to the NHANES and the Colorado Thyroid Prevalence Study, around 4 to 10% of the general population has SCH [66, 73]. Our meta-analysis, pooling data from 38 studies, estimates a prevalence of 20.7% in individuals with MetS, which is markedly higher than in the general population. Like other thyroid disorders, SCH is more common in women [66]. Our sex-specific analysis demonstrated a pooled prevalence of 27.3% in women with MetS and 18% in men with MetS, consistent with its higher prevalence in women.

Subgroup analysis by risk of bias revealed a similar pattern to that observed in overt hypothyroidism. Studies with a high risk of bias reported markedly higher prevalence estimates compared to those with low risk of bias (29% vs. 13.1%, p-value < 0.01), suggesting potential overestimation due to methodological limitations. The estimate derived from low-risk-of-bias studies may therefore represent a more robust and less biased approximation. Notably, even this more conservative estimate exceeds prevalence rates reported in the general population, suggesting a possible association between SCH and MetS. Supporting this, a meta-analysis revealed that SCH is associated not only with MetS but also with four of its five components [16]. A recently published systematic review found that MetS was associated with a 2.56-fold increased chance of developing SCH. However, no significant associations were observed between individual components of MetS and SCH [15]. On the other hand, another systematic review and meta-analysis found an association between central obesity and SCH but no association between MetS and SCH [74].

As the term “subclinical” implies, most cases of SCH are asymptomatic and typically detected during routine laboratory assessment [75]. According to current clinical guidelines and expert opinion, thyroid hormone replacement therapy is generally not indicated for asymptomatic individuals. Treatment is recommended when the serum TSH level exceeds 10 mIU/L, as this has been shown to increase the risk of cardiovascular events and progression to overt hypothyroidism [76, 77]. Treatment is also recommended for patients who test positive for thyroid peroxidase antibody, women who are pregnant or planning to conceive, and those with a high cardiovascular risk profile [77–80]. Although MetS is not currently recognized as an independent indication for treatment, it encompasses several major cardiovascular risk factors. Given this overlap, it may be hypothesized that individuals with both SCH and MetS represent a subgroup in whom thyroid hormone therapy might have metabolic implications. Emerging evidence suggests that SCH adversely affects lipid and glucose metabolism [81–83]. Our recent systematic review and meta-analysis in patients with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) demonstrated that those with concurrent SCH exhibit markedly elevated fasting insulin and homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) compared to PCOS patients without SCH [13]. Decreased thyroid function, even within the euthyroid range, has been associated with higher glucose, insulin, HOMA-IR, and cholesterol levels [43]. A large population-based study also found a positive correlation between TSH levels within the reference range and LDL, total cholesterol, and triglycerides [84]. Moreover, a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials demonstrated that levothyroxine therapy, even in mild SCH, significantly reduced LDL and total cholesterol [85]. These observations highlight the need for future prospective and randomized controlled studies to investigate potential treatment effects in MetS patients with SCH.

Meta-regression identified risk of bias and sample size as significant moderators of prevalence. For overt hypothyroidism, these factors explained 18.2% and 14% of heterogeneity, respectively, whereas for SCH, risk of bias, sample size, and MetS diagnostic criteria explained 24.2%, 15.1%, and 12.3% of heterogeneity, respectively. However, it is important to note that, in line with recent methodological insights, the commonly reported I² statistic has limited interpretive value in prevalence meta-analysis. I² can be influenced by factors such as sample size, precision of individual studies, and the proportional nature of prevalence data. In a sample of 134 prevalence meta-analyses, the median I² was 96.9%, indicating that high I² is expected and may not necessarily reflect problematic heterogeneity. Given that high I² is common in prevalence meta-analyses, 95% PIs provide a more informative measure, representing the expected range of prevalence across diverse populations [86, 87]. We therefore calculated 95% PIs for all pooled estimates, offering a more realistic view of variability beyond the average pooled prevalence. The wide PIs observed in several analyses indicate substantial between-study heterogeneity and suggest that prevalence estimates may vary considerably across different populations and study settings.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis to estimate the prevalence of overt hypothyroidism and SCH in individuals with MetS. Our comprehensive search strategy yielded 40 studies. Detailed data extraction enabled us to perform subgroup analyses based on several variables. Importantly, subgroup analysis and meta-regression identified risk of bias as a major contributor to prevalence variability. Accordingly, we placed greater emphasis on estimates from low-risk-of-bias studies, which are likely to provide more reliable and less biased estimates. To ensure the stability of our findings, we conducted a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis, which confirmed their robustness. As with any study, certain limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting the results. First, only English-language publications were included. Second, high statistical heterogeneity was noted, a finding that is common in prevalence meta-analyses. While I² values remained high across subgroups, meta-regression indicated that risk of bias, sample size, and MetS diagnostic criteria accounted for part of the observed heterogeneity. To provide a more representative measure of expected variability across populations, 95% PIs were calculated for all pooled estimates. Third, while the funnel plot and Begg’s test did not suggest publication bias, Egger’s test was significant for both overt hypothyroidism and SCH. However, no studies were imputed by the trim-and-fill method, reducing concerns regarding its impact on the results. Fourth, more than half of the included studies were conducted in India, a country with a relatively high prevalence of thyroid disorders. Although this raises concerns about generalizability, its impact is mitigated by two factors: (1) our conclusions are derived from low-risk-of-bias studies, among which only 4 of the 18 were conducted in India, and (2) subgroup analysis by geographic region revealed no significant differences between Indian and non-Indian studies. Nevertheless, several geographic regions remain underrepresented in the available literature, and the generalizability of these findings to Western, African, and Latin American populations remains uncertain. Further studies in these regions are needed to provide more robust prevalence estimates of overt hypothyroidism and SCH in MetS.

Conclusion

Studies with a high risk of bias tended to report higher prevalence estimates. The pooled prevalence of overt hypothyroidism in low-risk-of-bias studies was 4.8%, which appears comparable to the general population. By contrast, the estimated prevalence of SCH was 13.1%, exceeding rates observed in the general population. Overall, around one in five individuals with MetS may have overt hypothyroidism or SCH. These findings suggest a potential clinical relevance of thyroid function testing in these individuals. Future cohort and trial studies are needed to assess whether treatment of SCH affects metabolic or cardiovascular outcomes.
