Methylation plus alpha-fetoprotein blood test for early detection of HCC in at-risk populations
Authors: Neehar D. Parikh, Chun-Chi Liu, Matthew DeMaio, Angela H. Yeh, Mary L. Stackpole, Shuo Li, Vatche G. Agopian, Wenyuan Li, Xianghong Jasmine Zhou, Xiaohui Ni, Anna S. Lok, Steven-Huy B. Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Semi-annual abdominal ultrasound plus alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) is recommended for HCC surveillance but has limited performance, particularly in patients with metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease. We aimed to validate a blood-based test combining cell-free DNA methylation profiling with AFP to improve HCC detection in at-risk individuals.Methods:Between September 1, 2023, and April 30, 2025, 135 patients with HCC and 167 at risk for HCC were prospectively recruited at 2o medical centers; archived samples (46 HCC and 32 at-risk) were also included. A cell-free DNA methylation assay was performed to generate methylation scores, and AFP testing was conducted as part of standard of care. A pretrained model integrated methylation scores and AFP levels for HCC detection.Results:The combination of methylation and AFP tests achieved an AUC of 0.94 (95% CI, 0.92–0.96) for detecting HCC in at-risk individuals, significantly outperforming both methylation alone (AUC: 0.92, 95% CI, 0.89–0.95; p=0.001) and AFP alone (AUC: 0.83, 95% CI, 0.79–0.87; p<0.0001). In patients with early-stage HCC (within Milan Criteria), the combined test achieved an AUC of 0.90 (95% CI, 0.86–0.94), outperforming methylation alone (AUC: 0.88, 95% CI, 0.83–0.92; p=0.002) and AFP alone (AUC: 0.81, 95% CI, 0.76–0.86; p<0.0001). At 92.0% specificity, sensitivity for early-stage HCC was 73% (95% CI, 64%–80%) with the combined test, compared with 62% (95% CI, 52%–71%; p=0.025) for methylation alone and 52% (95% CI, 43%–62%; p<0.0001) for AFP alone. Sensitivity improvements were consistent across major demographic and etiologic subgroups.Conclusions:The combination of cell-free DNA methylation with AFP shows promising performance for early-stage HCC detection in this case-control validation study and warrants further validation.

INTRODUCTION

Liver cancer is the sixth most common cancer and the third leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide. 1 HCC is the most common type of liver cancer and typically arises in the context of cirrhosis or chronic HBV infection. Surveillance of HCC is associated with improved early detection and reduced HCC-related mortality.2,3 Currently, abdominal ultrasound (US) combined with serum alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) measurement is recommended for the surveillance of at-risk patients.4,5 However, these surveillance methods have limited sensitivity for detecting early-stage HCC. A meta-analysis reported that US alone has a sensitivity of only 53% at a specificity of 91% for detecting early-stage HCC. 6 Combining US with AFP improves the sensitivity of early-stage HCC detection to 63%, but reduces specificity to 84%.6,7 Furthermore, adherence to current surveillance recommendations is low, with <25% of eligible patients receiving regular HCC screening, 8 primarily due to barriers such as difficulty scheduling US appointments and transportation. 9 As a result, most patients with HCC are detected incidentally or when symptomatic, with only 40% being detected through surveillance. 10 These limitations underscore the urgent need for more sensitive, specific, and easy-to-deploy HCC surveillance methods. Blood-based tests offer a promising alternative to US by enabling more accessible and scalable surveillance.

Recent advances have led to the development of several blood-based approaches for HCC surveillance. One such approach is the GALAD score, which combines AFP, Lens culinaris agglutinin-reactive fraction of AFP (AFP-L3), des-gamma-carboxy prothrombin (DCP), along with patient age and sex. The GALAD score was evaluated in the National Cancer Institute (NCI) Early Detection Research Network (EDRN) Hepatocellular Carcinoma Early Detection Strategy (HEDS) phase III biomarker study. GALAD achieved a sensitivity of 65% at a specificity of 82% within 6 months before HCC diagnosis. 11 There are also promising data for HCC early detection using circulating tumor cells and extracellular vesicles; however, these biomarkers are still in the early stages of validation.12–14 Cell-free DNA (cfDNA) analysis, which examines the genomic and epigenomic features of circulating DNA, has also emerged as a promising strategy for early detection of HCC.15–17 One cfDNA method, the MethylScan assay, combines restriction enzyme digestion with targeted methylation profiling to effectively reduce background signals from white blood cells and enable precise analysis of over 1.6 million CpG sites. 18 This assay generates a quantitative methylation risk score that can be used independently or in combination with AFP to support scalable early detection of HCC.

We aimed to determine the potential of combining the methylation risk score with serum AFP as a viable blood-based biomarker option for HCC surveillance in a phase II biomarker validation study.

METHODS

Patients and samples

We conducted a phase II biomarker validation study to evaluate the performance of the methylation + AFP test for early detection of HCC, with its intended use in surveillance of at-risk patients. 19 We recruited a prospective cohort of 326 patients from 2 sites, the University of Michigan and the University of California at Los Angeles, from September 1, 2023, to April 30, 2025. In addition, 81 retrospective archived samples collected between February 23, 2018, and August 7, 2023, were included to supplement the prospective cohort. Blood samples were collected from patients diagnosed with HCC or those at risk for HCC as defined below. The HCC group consisted of adult patients diagnosed with HCC according to the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) guidelines. 20 Early-stage HCC was defined according to the Milan Criteria (either a single tumor nodule ≤5 cm in diameter, or up to 3 tumor nodules with the largest one ≤3 cm in diameter), while very early-stage HCC was defined as a single tumor nodule ≤2 cm in diameter. The at-risk group included adult patients who met the AASLD HCC surveillance criteria, such as those with cirrhosis of any etiology or patients with noncirrhotic chronic HBV infection who were at increased risk of HCC (Asian males aged >40 y, Asian females aged >50 y, Africa/North American Blacks, or those with a family history of HCC). Patients with HCC who had undergone any HCC treatment before the study blood draw, or who had a known diagnosis of another cancer within 10 years of the blood draw, were excluded. In the at-risk group, patients with Child-Pugh class C cirrhosis, a prior diagnosis of HCC, or a known diagnosis of another cancer within 10 years of the blood draw were also excluded. At-risk patients were required to have negative follow-up imaging at least 6 months after blood sample collection to rule out the presence of prevalent HCC. US was the primary follow-up modality, with CT or MRI used in a small subset of participants. Patients without a 6-month follow-up negative imaging result or those diagnosed with HCC within 6 months of enrollment were excluded from the study.

A total of 138 patients with HCC and 188 patients at risk for HCC in the prospective cohort met the recruitment criteria. Of these, 24 were excluded because of suboptimal sample quality or a lack of follow-up imaging (Supplemental Figure S1, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C380). As a result, 135 patients with HCC and 167 patients at-risk for HCC were included in the analysis. The study was conducted in accordance with both the Declarations of Helsinki and Istanbul. Both participating sites received approval from a single institutional review board (UCLA) to conduct the prospective study (IRB-23-0431-CR-003), and informed consent was obtained from all patients. In addition, retrospective plasma samples from 49 patients with HCC and 32 at-risk patients, who met the same enrollment criteria as the prospective cohort, were included. These samples were processed using the same protocol as the prospective samples, except for differences in blood collection tubes (EDTA instead of Steck tubes) and storage conditions (−70 °C instead of −80 °C). These patients had previously provided informed consent for blood sample collection under approved HCC biomarker research studies, which included permission for sample and data sharing. Of these, 2 samples failed quality control, and 1 sample was excluded due to missing AFP data. In total, 181 samples from patients with HCC and 199 samples from those at-risk were included in the study.

Clinical data collected included patient demographics, etiology of liver disease, HCC stage (eg, number of tumor nodules, size of the largest nodule, presence of vascular invasion or metastasis), and laboratory test results such as platelet count, AST, ALT, bilirubin, albumin, international normalized ratio, creatinine, and AFP levels. In addition, information on ascites stage, encephalopathy stage, MELD, and Child-Turcotte-Pugh scores was collected.

Sample collection and processing

Blood samples were collected from each patient enrolled in the study. For patients with HCC, all samples were obtained before HCC treatment. Details regarding the blood collection and processing protocol are provided in the Supplemental Methods, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C382

Methylation and AFP measurements

Methylation profiles of cfDNA were obtained through EarlyDx’s MethylScan assay, a blood-based method designed to minimize background noise and enhance the accuracy of methylation profiling of cfDNA for multidisease diagnosis. This assay utilizes methylation-sensitive restriction enzymes to digest hypomethylated DNA and employs a hypermethylation capture panel to target genomic regions that are typically hypomethylated in white blood cells. With comprehensive coverage of ~1.6 million CpG sites and the removal of white blood cell–derived background, the assay leverages machine learning algorithms for multidisease detection. Specifically, the assay data were used to train a support vector machine classifier that generates an HCC risk score, referred to as the methylation score, with a predefined cutoff of −0.24 for detecting HCC in at-risk patients (the “methylation test”). Details of the methylation assay and algorithm development are described elsewhere in the methodology study (see also Supplemental Methods, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C382). 18 AFP levels were provided by each clinical site as part of standard-of-care testing, using blood samples collected at the same time as those for methylation analysis. A logistic regression model integrating the methylation scores and the base-10 logarithm of AFP levels to generate a composite HCC risk score (the “methylation + AFP test”) was trained and locked using data from the methodology study before the present validation. Demographics, clinical, and laboratory characteristics of the training cohort are summarized in Supplemental Table S1, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C383. The present study represents a phase II biomarker validation that evaluates the combination of methylation score and serum AFP level for HCC detection in an at-risk population. Whole blood or plasma samples were shipped from clinical centers to EarlyDiagnostics Inc. for testing. EarlyDiagnostics testing and analysis personnel were blinded to the diagnoses and clinical follow-up data until after the prediction scores were released.

Statistical analysis

The discriminative performance of the AFP level, the methylation test, and the methylation + AFP test was evaluated using sensitivity, specificity, and the AUROC. Two-sided 95% CIs for sensitivity and specificity were calculated using the Wilson score method. The statistical significance of the differences in sensitivity and specificity was assessed using the binom.test() function (2-sided exact binomial test) in the R package binom. Calibration was assessed using a plot comparing predicted and observed probabilities, and goodness of fit was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test implemented in the R function hoslem.test. Net reclassification analysis was performed using the R package PredictABEL to quantify improvements in HCC risk prediction. Propensity score matching was conducted using the R package MatchIt with a caliper of 0.3 to balance the validation cohorts.

The 95% CIs for AUCs were calculated using 2000 stratified bootstrap replicates with the R package pROC. Pairwise comparisons of the AUCs were performed using a 2-sided bootstrap test, which was also implemented in pROC.

Statistical comparisons of characteristics between patients with HCC and those at-risk were conducted using chi-square tests for categorical variables and the 2-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables.

RESULTS

Participant characteristics

Demographics, clinical characteristics, and laboratory values for the 380 patients (181 HCC and 199 at-risk) are summarized in Table 1, with site-specific details provided in Supplemental Tables S2 and S3, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C380. Of the 181 patients with HCC, 103 (56.9%) had early-stage HCC and 19 (10.5%) had very early-stage HCC. Patient characteristics were similar between those with early-stage and more advanced HCC. When comparing the patients with HCC and those at risk, patients with HCC were older (mean age 65.6 vs. 59.5 y) and more likely to be male (72.9% vs. 54.3%) compared with the at-risk group. Patients with HCC were more likely to have cirrhosis (87.8% vs. 71.4%). A significant difference in racial distribution was also observed between patients with HCC and those at risk (p<0.01). This difference was largely due to the inclusion of patients (n=58) with noncirrhotic chronic HBV, of whom only 1e had HCC. When patients with noncirrhotic chronic HBV were excluded, baseline differences between cases and controls were less pronounced (Supplemental Table S4, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C383).

Performance of AFP, methylation test, and combined methylation + AFP test for HCC detection at different tumor stages

The methylation test alone demonstrated a significantly higher discriminative performance (AUC) than AFP, and combining AFP with the methylation test produced a further increase in performance in both all-stage and early-stage HCC.

In the overall study population, the methylation test achieved an AUC of 0.92 (95% CI, 0.89–0.95) compared with 0.83 (95% CI, 0.79–0.87; p<0.0001) for AFP alone. The combination of methylation and AFP further improved performance, achieving an AUC of 0.94 (95% CI, 0.92–0.96, p=0.001) (Figure 1A).

ROC curves for AFP, methylation, and methylation + AFP in detecting (A) overall, (B) early-stage (within Milan Criteria), (C) very early-stage (a single lesion ≤2 cm in diameter) HCCs. Abbreviations: AFP, alpha-fetoprotein; ROC, receiver operating characteristic.

For early-stage HCC (within Milan Criteria), the methylation test yielded an AUC of 0.88 (95% CI, 0.83–0.92), outperforming AFP (AUC=0.81; 95% CI, 0.76–0.86; p=0.030). The combination of methylation + AFP further increased the AUC to 0.90 (95% CI, 0.86–0.94; p=0.002) (Figure 1B).

Both the methylation and AFP tests showed reduced performance in the very early-stage subgroup (n=19; a single tumor nodule ≤2 cm), with an AUC of 0.73 (95% CI, 0.62–0.84) for methylation alone and 0.70 (95% CI, 0.56–0.84) for AFP alone. The combined methylation + AFP test modestly increased the AUC to 0.76 (95% CI, 0.64–0.88) (Figure 1C); however, this improvement was not statistically significant compared with methylation (p=0.216) or AFP (p=0.337) alone in this small sample set.

To compare test sensitivities, we evaluated performance at 2 specificities using predefined cutoffs: 92.0% specificity, corresponding to the methylation score cutoff of −0.24, and 98.5% specificity, corresponding to the commonly used AFP threshold of 20 ng/mL. 21 At 92.0% specificity, methylation + AFP demonstrated a sensitivity of 73% (95% CI, 64%–80%) for early-stage HCC, outperforming methylation alone at 62% (95% CI, 52%–71%; p=0.025) and AFP alone at 52% (95% CI, 43%–62%; p<0.0001). All tests showed improved sensitivity in patients with more advanced tumor burden. The methylation test alone demonstrated higher sensitivity for larger tumors, whereas the incremental benefit of adding AFP was more evident in patients with smaller tumors (Table 2). Similar trends were observed at 98.5% specificity; however, unlike at 92.0% specificity, where the added benefit of AFP was primarily confined to smaller tumors, the addition of AFP to the methylation test also conferred a modest improvement for larger tumors at this higher specificity. AFP combined with US has been reported to achieve a sensitivity of 63.4% for detecting early-stage HCC at 83.3% specificity. 7 In contrast, the combined methylation + AFP test demonstrated a higher sensitivity of 80% (95% CI, 71%–86%) at the same specificity of 83.3%.

Calibration of the combined methylation + AFP test in the overall patient cohort was assessed using a calibration plot comparing predicted HCC probabilities derived from the logistic regression-based test with observed HCC event rates. The test demonstrated good overall agreement, with no significant lack of fit by the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (p=0.06) (Figure 2). Retrospective samples meeting the same inclusion criteria were added to increase statistical power and enable robust subgroup analyses. In the prospectively collected samples, the methylation test demonstrated performance consistent with that of the full cohort (AUC 0.93 vs. 0.92 for detecting overall HCC; AUC 0.88 vs. 0.88 for detecting early-stage HCC) (Supplemental Figure S2, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C380). For AFP, performance in the prospective cohort showed a slightly higher AUC for early-stage HCC (0.86) than for overall HCC (0.85), accompanied by wider confidence intervals (95% CI, 0.80–0.91 vs. 0.81–0.90), likely attributable to the limited sample size.

Patients with noncirrhotic HBV within the at-risk group contributed substantially to baseline differences. We therefore evaluated test performance after excluding this group (Supplemental Figure S3, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C380). The AUC of the combined methylation plus AFP test decreased marginally from 0.94 to 0.93 (p=0.48).

Performance of AFP, methylation, and methylation + AFP tests across subgroups

We conducted subgroup analyses of AFP, methylation, and methylation + AFP tests in detecting HCC among patients with different liver disease diagnoses (cirrhosis of any etiology, cirrhosis due to viral hepatitis, and MASLD) and separately stratified by sex. In both all-stage and early-stage HCC, the methylation test alone performed better than AFP alone, and the combination of methylation + AFP had the highest performance across all etiology subgroups and in men as well as in women (Figures 3 and 4 ; Supplemental Figures S4 and S5, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C380). The performance of the tests varied across different etiology subgroups. All tests achieved the highest AUCs in the viral hepatitis subgroup, with the combined methylation + AFP test reaching an AUC of 0.97 (95% CI, 0.94–0.99) for the overall cohort and 0.95 (95% CI, 0.91–0.99) for patients with early-stage HCC. All tests showed lowest performance in the MASLD subgroup, though the performance of both the combined methylation + AFP test and methylation test alone remained satisfactory with AUC of 0.91 (95% CI, 0.85–0.96) and 0.89 (95% CI, 0.82–0.95) for the overall cohort, and 0.83 (95% CI, 0.72–0.93) and 0.80 (95% CI, 0.70–0.90) for early-stage HCC, respectively. All tests demonstrated numerically higher performance in men than in women, with AUC of the combined methylation + AFP test of 0.95 (95% CI, 0.93–0.98) for all-stage HCC and 0.91 (95% CI, 0.87–0.96) for early-stage HCC in men, compared with AUC of 0.92 (95% CI, 0.87–0.96; p=0.261) and 0.88 (95% CI, 0.82–0.95; p=0.419), respectively, in women.

We explored the reasons for the differences in test performance through subgroup analyses of the groups with sufficient patient samples. The methylation risk scores were significantly higher among at-risk individuals with MASLD compared to those with non-MASLD etiologies (−0.62 vs. −0.85; p=0.0003), likely contributing to increased false positives and reduced specificity, thereby lowering the overall performance (AUC) in the MASLD subgroup (Supplemental Table S8, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C383). In contrast, in patients with MASLD-related HCC, AFP levels were significantly lower than those in patients with non–MASLD-related HCC (6.1 vs. 15; p=0.02), potentially leading to more false negatives and diminished AFP sensitivity and AUC. In addition, at-risk females exhibited significantly higher AFP levels than males, which may affect the positive detection rates of the test and contribute to observed differences in performance by sex. Elevated serum AFP levels among at-risk female patients have also been reported in the HALT-C trial. 22

Subgroups stratified by etiology or by sex could differ by other clinical characteristics. To address this, propensity score matching was performed to balance the HCC and at-risk groups based on sex, age, etiology, cirrhosis status, body mass index, race, and Child-Pugh class, resulting in 122 patients per group. 23 Each etiology (alcohol-associated liver disease, HBV, MASLD, HCV with SVR, and HCV without SVR) was included as a separate covariate, with cirrhosis status modeled independently, thereby preserving clinically relevant combinations (eg, noncirrhotic HBV) in the propensity score matching. Demographics, clinical characteristics, and laboratory values for the matched cohort are summarized in Supplemental Table S9, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C383. The AUC of the combined methylation plus AFP test slightly decreased from 0.94 to 0.92 (p=0.64) (Supplemental Figure S6, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C380).

Sensitivity analyses by subgroup

Complementarity of the methylation and AFP test

We evaluated the complementarity of methylation and AFP levels (Supplemental Table S10, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C383). At 92.0% specificity, the AFP test alone detected 54 patients with early-stage HCC, compared with 64 detected by methylation alone and 75 by the combined methylation + AFP test. For all-stage HCC, the corresponding numbers were 104, 137, and 149, respectively. Adding AFP to the methylation test detected 11 additional patients with early-stage HCC and 12 additional patients with all-stage HCC who were missed by methylation alone, improving sensitivity without introducing additional false negatives.

To quantify improvements in HCC risk prediction, we performed net reclassification analysis in the overall cohort at this specificity. The combined methylation + AFP yielded a net reclassification improvement of 24.9% (95% CI, 16.1%–33.6%) compared with AFP alone (p<0.001) and 6.6% (95% CI, 2.1%–11.2%) compared with methylation alone (p=0.005).

DISCUSSION

We validated the combination of cfDNA methylation and serum AFP levels for detecting HCC in an at-risk population. At a specificity of 92.0%, the combined methylation + AFP test demonstrated an overall sensitivity of 82% (95% CI, 76%-87%) and an early-stage sensitivity of 73% (95% CI, 64%–80%). The ALTUS trial, which evaluated a composite test combining 3 methylation markers, AFP, and sex, reported sensitivities of 67% for early-stage HCC and 70% overall at 82% specificity. 24 Although our study achieved higher sensitivities, differences in study design and patient populations limited the direct comparison between the 2 studies.

Methylation testing performed better than AFP in detecting early-stage HCC (within Milan Criteria), across etiology subgroups, and in both men and women. In an exploratory analysis of very early-stage HCC (1 tumor nodule ≤2 cm), a primary challenge in HCC surveillance, the methylation test showed performance comparable to that of AFP; however, this analysis was limited by the small sample size, with only 19 patients meeting this criterion. Our data indicate that cfDNA methylation and serum AFP tests are complementary, and that their combination may outperform either test alone for detecting both all-stage HCC and early-stage HCC, across etiology subgroups, and in both men and women.

While the proportion of HCC associated with HBV and HCV is expected to continue to decline due to widespread HBV immunization and effective antiviral treatments for HBV and HCV, the burden of MASLD-related HCC is projected to increase by 122% between 2016 and 2030. 25 AFP had the lowest performance in patients with HCC associated with MASLD compared with those with HCC associated with viral hepatitis or alcohol-associated liver disease, with a sensitivity of only 38% (95% CI, 23%–55%) at 92.0% specificity for early-stage HCC. US has also been reported to have reduced sensitivity for detecting HCC in individuals with obesity and non-viral liver diseases. 25 This raises concerns about continued reliance on the combination of US and AFP for HCC surveillance in the setting of the growing prevalence of MASLD and increasing incidence of MASLD-related HCC. In contrast, methylation testing, with or without AFP, may improve HCC detection compared with AFP alone across all etiologies, including MASLD. Methylation shows promise as a blood-based test for HCC surveillance in the evolving epidemiology of HCC.

Our study has several strengths. We included a large number of patients with early-stage HCC of diverse etiologies and race/ethnicities. All controls were required to have a negative imaging test at least 6 months after blood sample collection. Blood sample collection and processing for both prospective and retrospective cohorts from the 2 centers followed the same protocol, and low-quality samples were excluded. This study also had some limitations. First, the case-control phase II design of our analysis may overestimate test performance due to spectrum biases of the cohort versus patients who may undergo surveillance in clinical settings. In particular, imaging findings were used to define case and control status, which inherently precludes an independent comparison with image-based approaches. As a result, although abdominal US combined with AFP represents the current standard of care for HCC surveillance, a direct comparison between US plus AFP and methylation-based testing could not be performed with this case-control study design. Further validation in larger phase II and phase III cohorts, ideally incorporating head-to-head comparisons with US plus AFP, is therefore important to confirm performance and broaden clinical applicability. In addition, calibration assessed in a case-control data set should be interpreted with caution, as it does not establish calibration in a true surveillance population. Second, the patient population and etiology distribution at the 2 participating academic medical centers may not reflect those observed in broader community settings. Third, the sample size for the very early-stage subgroup, clinically the most relevant population for novel biomarkers aimed at early detection, is limited; therefore, analyses in this subgroup are exploratory and not statistically powered.

In conclusion, we showed that cfDNA methylation had superior performance for the early detection of HCC in at-risk populations compared to serum AFP. The combination of the 2 tests showed promise across different stages of HCC and liver disease etiologies and in both men and women. Further studies of larger cohorts are warranted to confirm our findings.
