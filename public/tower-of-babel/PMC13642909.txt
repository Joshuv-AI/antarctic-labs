The suppressive function of ILC2 cells is regulated by β2-adrenergic receptor signaling in breast cancer
Authors: Jee Eun Choi, Qi Yan, Jianmin Wang, Nicholas J Salgia, Jason B Muhitch, Cameron R MacDonald, Nathan T Roberts, Caitlin M James, Philip L McCarthy, Hemn Mohammadpour, Saeed Daneshmandi, Elizabeth A Repasky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractType II innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s) have been implicated in both tumor-promoting and tumor-suppressive functions, but the mechanism(s) underlying this functional heterogeneity is not clear. Here, we tested the hypothesis that signaling through β2-adrenergic receptor (β2-AR) drives the pro-tumor functions of ILC2s within the tumor microenvironment. Using global (β2AR−/−) and ILC2-specific conditional knockout (IL5Creβ2ARfl/fl) mouse models, we examined how β2-AR deficiency affects ILC2 function in breast cancer. We studied the impact of β2-AR function on ILC2 phenotype, gene profile, anti-tumor T cell response and breast tumor growth. β2AR inhibition resulted in an enrichment of KLRG1⁺ ILC2s with elevated GATA3 and ST2 expression in breast tumor. Loss of β2-AR signaling in ILC2s reduced their pro-tumorigenic activity and resulted in suppressed tumor growth. Additionally, β2-AR inhibition in ILC2s was associated with increased CD4⁺ T cell infiltration in breast tumors and a greater TNF-α+ CD8+ T cell response. β2-AR deficiency reprograms ILC2s toward a more immunostimulatory phenotype, promoting T cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity. These findings identify activity of the β2-AR as a key regulator of ILC2 plasticity and suggest β2-AR blockade as a promising strategy to enhance cancer immunotherapy. Subject terms: Cancer, Immunology, Oncology

Introduction

The outlook for breast cancer patients with metastatic disease remains dismal, with a 5-year overall survival below 30%. The majority of these patients do not respond to current standard-of-care treatments, including immunotherapy, highlighting the need to better understand breast cancer biology, including its tumor microenvironment (TME)1. A major hallmark of breast cancer2 is the immunosuppressive nature of the TME. Myeloid-derived suppressor cells3, macrophages4, tumor-associated neutrophils5, and regulatory T cells6 are among the most prominent immunosuppressive populations that accumulate within the TME, allowing cancer cells to evade effective anti-tumor immune responses. In addition to these well-established immunosuppressive cells, innate lymphoid cells (ILCs) have recently emerged as important regulators of the TME.

ILCs are comprised of five subsets defined by their differentiation lineage and function: natural killer (NK) cells, type I ILCs (ILC1s), type II ILCs (ILC2s), type III ILCs (ILC3s), and lymphoid tissue-inducer (LTi) cells7. The involvement of ILCs in tumor immunity has been clearly demonstrated in various cancer types8,9. In general, ILC1s have been associated with an anti-tumor function due to their ability to secrete IFNγ8, while ILC2s have been associated with a pro-tumor function due to their ability to secrete type II cytokines such as IL-4 and IL-1310. However, for many of the ILC subgroups, both pro- and anti-tumor functions are reported in the literature9,11–13. Thus, despite the current knowledge of ILC subtypes and their functions, their basic role in controlling tumor growth remains controversial, and mechanisms that regulate their apparent ability to drive both pro- and anti-tumor functions are not well understood.

Several studies have recently reinforced the detrimental role of chronic stress and activation of the sympathetic nervous system (SNS) in regulating TME. Chronic stress leads to increased circulating catecholamines such as norepinephrine (NE)14, which can be directly released into the TME by tumor-infiltrating nerves15,16. Importantly, high intratumoral nerve density is negatively correlated with prognosis in many types of cancer, including breast cancer17. NE released from sympathetic nerves signals through the β-adrenergic receptors (βARs) expressed on most cells of the body, including immune and tumor cells18. Our lab previously demonstrated that mild chronic stress increased circulating NE levels, which activated βARs, specifically β2-adrenergic receptors (β2AR), to promote immunosuppression through the inhibition/exhaustion of cytotoxic T cells19,20 and activation of MDSCs21–23. We wondered whether the functions of ILC2 cells may be regulated by the presence (or absence) of stimulation of adrenergic receptors.

In this study, through several in vitro and in vivo models, including the generation of a new conditional knockout of β2-AR in ILCs in mice, we discovered that β2-AR signaling promotes a pro-tumor function of ILC2 activity. Furthermore, blocking β2-AR signaling in ILCs alone significantly decreased tumor growth in a murine breast cancer model. These data show that β2-AR signaling plays a key role in balancing the pro- versus anti-tumor function of ILCs in the TME and further reveals the importance of adrenergic stress in promoting breast cancer progression.

Results

Lack of β2AR signaling results in decreased tumor burden associated with increased KLRG1+ ILCs in breast TME

To investigate the role of β2-AR in ILC heterogeneity in breast cancer, we used the 4T1 breast cancer model, a widely established model of TNBC. Wildtype (WT) and Adrb2−/− (β2AR−/−) BALB/c mice were orthotopically implanted with the 4T1 breast cancer cell line, and the cellular composition of the TME was analyzed by flow cytometry. As previously observed20,21, tumor volume was significantly decreased in the β2AR−/− mice compared to WT mice (Fig. 1A). Here we define ILCs as CD45+, Lineage− (CD3, B220, CD11b, Gr-1, Ter119), and CD90.2+ cells (Fig. S1A). Due to the heterogeneity of ILCs, we first focused on the Killer cell Lectin-like Receptor G1 (KLRG1)+ ILCs, as it has been offered as a potential marker to differentiate the tumor-suppressing ILC2s from the KLRG1− tumor-promoting ILC2s24. While the proportion of ILCs in total live cells remained constant (Fig. 1B), we observed a significant enrichment of KLRG1+ ILCs in the TME of β2AR−/− mice compared to that of WT mice (Fig. 1C).

KLRG1 is a transmembrane glycoprotein that binds cadherins, and is upregulated upon activation, resulting in immune suppression of T cells, but activation of ILC2s25. Interestingly, KLRG1 expression has mainly been associated with ILC2-biased ILCs26, though it must be noted that this inflammatory marker is differentially expressed between ILC2s originating from different organs and sexes27. Additionally, ILC2s enriched in malignant breast tumors had higher KLRG1 expression than ILC2s in benign breast tumors, although the direct implications for prognosis were not examined28. It has also been shown that ILC2s express β2AR, which can regulate their activation state29. Therefore, we focused on whether β2AR signaling through specifically ILC2s could alter the cancer outcomes. For this, we employed an IL5-cre-tdTomato model, which targets ILC2s30, to generate a conditional knockout of β2AR in ILC2s (IL5cre/+β2ARfl/fl) (Fig. S2A). It is important to note that T cells, which also secrete IL5, may be affected. To examine this, we confirmed the tdTomato expression in ILC2s, CD4+ T cells, as well as CD8+ T cells. We observed that the majority of ILC2s expressed tdTomato while CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells did not (Fig. S2B), isolating β2AR deletion to the ILC2s. From here forward, IL5cre/+ will be referred to as the control, and IL5cre/+β2ARfl/fl will be referred to as ILC2Δβ2AR (Fig. S2A).

Employing this transgenic mouse model, we found that conditional deletion of β2AR in ILC2s led to a significant decrease in AT3 breast cancer growth compared to that of control mice (Fig. 1D, E). Interestingly, and similar to the β2AR global knockout model (β2AR−/−), there were more KLRG1+ILCs despite equivalent total ILC percentages in the ILC2Δβ2AR mice (Fig. 1F, G). Therefore, the data suggest that β2AR signaling in ILC2s can induce overall KLRG1 expression in the global ILC population, which goes along with the decreased tumor burden.

β2AR signaling blockade induces ILC2 subsets with higher proinflammatory gene signatures

The overall β2AR deletion leads to altered total KLRG1+ ILCs. To determine how β2AR signaling alters the activity of KLRG1+ ILC subsets, this population (CD45+Lineage-CD90.2+KLRG1+) was sorted from the 4T1 tumors of WT and β2AR−/− mice and analyzed by single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq). In accordance with previous reports of ILC heterogeneity and plasticity31,32, eight unique clusters of ILCs were identified (Fig. 2A, B). Genes used to determine the identity of the populations are listed in Supplementary Table 1. Additional analysis showed that the NK cells, ILC2-like ex-ILC1s, ILC1s, and LTi subsets were enriched in the tumors of WT mice, while inflammatory NK cells and ILC3-like ex-ILC2s were enriched in the TME of β2AR−/− mice (Fig. 2C). KLRG1 expression in NK cells has previously been associated with immune suppression33. This suggests that, while the abundance of KLRG1+ ILCs are increased in the TME of β2AR−/− mice (Fig. 1C), there is a decrease in the immunosuppressive subset of NK cells. The cell clusters enriched in the β2AR−/− TME expressed granzyme b (Gzmb), interferon γ (Ifng), and TNFα (Tnfa) (Fig. 2D), known inflammatory cytokines. Additionally, the clusters enriched in the β2AR−/− TME, specifically ILC3-like ex-ILC2s, expressed IL17a (Fig. 2D). This agrees with previous findings that KLRG1-expressing ILC2s are a subset of ILC2s designated inflammatory ILC2s (iILC2s), which has the potential to shift toward becoming IL-17 expressing ILC326. The data suggest that β2AR signaling blockade simultaneously decreases the abundance of NK cells and ILC1s and enriches an inflammatory subset of ILC2s, which have the potential to express signatures of ILC3s. This complex, seemingly counter-balanced phenotype may shed light on the contradictory nature of ILC2s previously observed in cancer biology.

Additional gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of the identified clusters demonstrated an upregulation of glycolytic pathway signatures in β2AR−/− ILCs compared to WT ILCs (Fig. 2E). In the β2AR−/−inflammatory NK cell population, we observed upregulation of glycolysis pathway signatures and downregulation of oxidative phosphorylation pathway signatures compared to WT (Fig. 2F). Similarly, the ILC3-like ex-ILC2 cluster from β2AR−/− mice showed downregulation of oxidative phosphorylation pathways (Fig. 2G). These findings further agree with previous studies of β2AR signaling, which also demonstrated an increase in glycolysis and a decrease in oxidative phosphorylation in T cells by β2AR blockade20. While ILC2s mainly rely on fatty acid oxidation, activation of glycolysis corresponds to increased ILC2 proinflammatory activity34. The shift toward glycolysis observed in the ILC subsets enriched in the TME of β2AR-/- mice suggests that these subsets are proinflammation-activated. Together, these data propose that β2AR signaling induces alterations in ILC heterogeneity and gene signatures to a pro-tumor phenotype and function and blocking the β2AR reverses this effect.

Inhibiting β2AR signaling in ILC2s lead to upregulation of GATA3 and ST2

It has been previously shown that β2-AR negatively regulates ILC2s' function in helminth infection29. A central pathway to control ILC2 activation is the IL-33 ST2 (Suppression of Tumorigenicity 2) pathway. To determine how adrenergic signaling leads to altered ILC2 function, we explored the expression of GATA3 and ST2 proteins post β-AR signaling by western blot analysis. GATA3 is an ILC2-defining transcription factor which has been shown to directly induce IL-13 expression35,36. ST2, also known as interleukin 1 receptor-like 1, is a member of the IL-1 receptor family that specifically binds to IL-33, a known ILC2-activating cytokine25,29,37, and has been shown to be upregulated by GATA3 in T cells38. ILC2s sorted from tumor-bearing WT mice were treated with either isoproterenol (ISO, pan-βAR agonist) or propranolol (PRO, pan- βAR antagonist), or both and analyzed for changes in protein expression. Activation of β-AR with ISO led to a trend in decreased GATA3 expression, which was mitigated by PRO (Fig. 3A, B and Fig. S3A). Similarly, ST2 expression was increased in PRO-treated ILC2s (Fig. 3C). To follow up on this observation and the specificity to β2AR, WT and β2AR−/− ILC2s were sorted from the tumor and treated with ISO or vehicle control. β2AR−/− ILC2s expressed significantly higher levels of GATA3 (Fig. 3D, E and Fig. S3B). Activating βAR signaling using the pan-βAR agonist ISO led to a decrease in GATA3 expression (Fig. 3D, E). This effect was also observed in the β2AR−/− ILC2s, but ISO-treated β2AR−/− ILC2s still expressed higher levels of GATA3 than ISO-treated WT ILC2s (Fig. 3D, E). Complementing this finding, ST2 expression was increased in β2AR−/− ILC2s (Fig. 3D, F). Together, the data demonstrate the ability of βAR signaling to downregulate GATA3 and ST2, which are the leading functional molecules to induce changes in ILC2 activity.

ILC2s require β2AR signaling to induce tumor proliferation

To further explore the significance of the increase in ILC2 activity through blockade of β2AR signaling directly on tumor cells, we cocultured ILC2s with 4T1 tumor cells. As lungs offer a more abundant source of ILC2s, we enriched for ILC2s in vivo through intranasal (i.n.) IL-33 treatment for three days. Lung ILC2s were isolated from WT and β2AR−/− BALB/c mice and cocultured with a luciferase-expressing 4T1 (4T1-Luc) cell line (Fig. S4A). Coculture with WT ILC2s increased 4T1 proliferation, demonstrating a pro-tumor role of ILC2s (Fig. S4B). Coculture with β2AR−/− ILC2s did not result in increased tumor proliferation, supporting the role of β2AR signaling in driving the pro-tumor function of ILC2s in breast tumors. To confirm the effect of ILC2s on breast tumor proliferation, sorted tumor-infiltrating ILC2s from WT and β2AR−/− mice were cocultured with 4T1-Luc for 24 h (Fig. 4A). The result showed that 4T1 coculture with WT ILCs led to an increase tumor proliferation which was prevented by β2AR blockade (Fig. 4B). To confirm whether the pro-tumor effect of ILC2s were tumor specific, we harvested tumor-infiltrating WT and β2AR−/− ILC2s and cocultured them with a luciferase expressing A20 lymphoma cell line (A20-Luc). There was no detectable change in tumor proliferation, suggesting that the role of ILC2s may be influenced by the tumor type (Fig. 4C). Together, the data demonstrate the ability of β2AR signaling to promote the pro-tumor function of ILC2s which can be blocked by β2AR blockade.

β2AR−/− ILC2s enhance anti-tumor T cell response in breast TME

We further inspected the composition of the TME to determine the axis by which β2AR blockade in ILC2s induced its anti-tumor effects. For this, the AT3 tumor model was generated in the IL5cre/+β2ARfl/fl and IL5cre/+ control mice, and the changes in the T cell phenotype was examine by flow cytometry. AT3 represents the triple-negative breast cancer model in C57BL/6 as our IL5cre/+β2ARfl/fl mice background. We observed a significant increase in total T cell abundance (Fig. 5A), with a significant increase in CD4+ cells (Fig. 5B) IL5cre/+β2ARfl/fl compared to the IL5cre/+ group. There was no significant difference in regulatory T cell (CD4+ FOXP3+) abundance (Fig. S5). There was no significant change in the frequency of CD8+ T cells (Fig. 5C) in IL5cre/+β2ARfl/fl compared to IL5cre/+ group. However, the results showed a significant increase in the TNF-α expressing CD8+ T cells in IL5cre/+β2ARfl/fl breast tumor compared to IL5cre/+ group (Fig. 5D). To confirm these in vivo findings, we sorted the ILC2s from the 4T1-tumor-bearing WT or β2AR−/− mice and cocultured with anti-CD3 plus anti-CD28 activated, CFSE-stained T cells (Fig. 5E). Examination of T cell proliferation showed that tumor WT ILC2s and not β2AR−/−ILC2 can significantly suppress CD4+ T cell proliferation (Fig. 5F), while the effect on the proliferation of CD8+ T cells was not significant (Fig. 5G). These in vitro experiments confirm the significant effect of ILC2 function on the proliferation of CD4+ T cells observed in the breast tumor. While the mechanism by which β2AR signaling alters the ILC2-CD8 T cell relationship is yet to be determined, the inhibition of β2AR signaling in ILC2s results in CD4+ T cell proliferation with increased tumor-infiltrating T cells and decreased tumor burden.

Discussion

The exact role of ILCs in tumor progression remains to be fully elucidated9. Here, we demonstrate that β2AR signaling drives pro-tumor effects of ILC2 in the TME. Most notably, knocking out β2AR signaling was able to decrease the induction of tumor proliferation by ILC2s and resulted in an anti-tumor immune landscape in the TME of breast cancer through an expansion of T cells. Our results lead us to propose that βAR signaling, which is responsive to the degree of adrenergic stress in the system, is a regulatory rheostat of ILC2 activity. These data potentially shed light on prior studies indicating contrasting functions of ILC2s, as the level of adrenergic signaling can fluctuate physiologically and were not explicitly measured in previous studies examining the effect of ILC2s in tumor immunity. Additionally, our findings suggest that pharmacological methods of β2AR-signaling blockade can be utilized to promote an overall anti-tumor environment within the TME through a shift in ILC2s activity.

β2-AR deficiency in ILC2 resulted in significant expansion of effector T cells in the TME. These data suggest that β2-AR−/− ILC2 are able to release chemoattractant cytokines for T cells, such as IL-13 and IL-9, which lead to T cell accumulation and ultimately a decrease in tumor growth. These findings are supported by previous studies showing that ILC2s release cytokines such as IL5 and IL-9, which have been demonstrated to possess anti-tumor functions. IL5 release by ILC2s can recruit eosinophils39, and IL-9 release by ILC2s can increase both CD4 and CD8 T cell activity40,41, leading to decreased tumor burden.

Interestingly, the direct effect of ILC2s on tumor proliferation was specific to the 4T1 breast cancer cell line and did not affect the A20 B cell lymphoma cell line proliferation. As ILCs do not have antigen specificity, this effect may be due to the difference between solid and liquid tumors, although more examples of both must be tested. While the exact mechanism remains to be elucidated, we found that β2AR−/− ILC2 can induce increased T cell proliferation. Therefore, the beneficial effect of β2AR signal blockade on tumor inhibition may be countered by the induction of T cells. For example, IL9, one of the cytokines expressed by ILC2s42, has been generally anti-tumor in breast cancer43 while tumor-promoting in lymphomas/ leukemias42,44. Whether the observed specificity is exclusive to breast cancer or to solid tumors generally should be a focus of future studies.

The molecular mechanisms underlying adrenergic regulation of ILC2s between an overall pro- vs anti-tumor activity still remains to be elucidated. Here, we report a notable change in the distribution of ILC subtypes within the TME of WT and β2AR-/- mice. We also observed a decrease in the expression of GATA3, a transcription factor that polarizes ILCs to ILC2s and T cells to Th245, by β2AR signaling. Together, the data suggests that plasticity of ILC2s may be altered by β2AR signaling. For example, IL-1β and TGF- β, which are cytokines commonly expressed within the TME46,47, have been demonstrated to induce plasticity in ILC2s31,48,49. Decreased GATA3 expression by β2AR signaling may increase susceptibility for ILC2s to shift into a more immunosuppressive phenotype dictated by the composition of the TME. Alternatively, increased adrenergic signaling may directly induce ILC2 exhaustion, as decreased GATA3 expression was associated with an exhausted phenotype in ILC2s50. Whether ILC plasticity can be intentionally governed by pharmacological interventions against adrenergic signaling will be exciting to explore in future studies.

Inhibiting β2AR signaling in ILC2s led to an enrichment of the KLRG1+ subset of ILCs. This KLRG1+ population of ILC2s has been previously noted as “inflammatory ILC2s” (iILC2s)26,51. IL-17-secreting ILC3-like “ex-ILC2s” were also enriched in the tumor of β2AR−/− mice. This agrees with previous findings that the iILC2s have the potential to phenotypically shift toward ILC3s26 and may be responsible for the shift away from the pro-tumor activity of ILC2s.

Several limitations in this study are noted here. We have generated an ILC2-specific β2AR knockout model for this project which exploits the constitutive expression of IL5 by ILC2s. The specificity of this model was validated using lung tissue due to its increased abundance of ILC2s in comparison to other organs. Additionally, the lung houses many types of IL5-expressing cell types, including eosinophils and mast cells52,53, allowing broad screening of potentially affected cell populations. Furthermore, ILC2s could be enriched in the lungs using intranasal IL-3354. However, it is possible that the expression pattern may be altered within the TME, and this would be important to determine in future studies. Our attempts to detect β2AR expression in tumor-dwelling ILC2s by flow cytometry was met with technical difficulties due to relatively few numbers of these cells in tumors. In the ILC2-tumor cell line coculture experiments, we had to combine more than ten tumors from different sources to obtain the needed cells for each experimental group. Future experiments that require low cell numbers could be optimized by pooling multiple tumor samples.

Given the multifaceted activity of ILC2s, the presence of ILC2s alone is not sufficient to determine their significance in tumor prognosis. Our data suggests that the adrenergic receptor signaling in the TME may be a determining factor in whether the infiltrating immune cells are beneficial or harmful to the anti-tumor immune response. Therefore, future studies should compare nerve densities within different breast cancer samples and determine its correlation with the polarity of ILC activity in the TME. Certain tumor types have higher incidences of neuronal infiltration, such as pancreatic, prostate, and head and neck cancers, while others have relatively lower incidences, such as breast cancer and colorectal cancer55. However, increased nerve density is associated with worse prognosis in most tumors56,57. This variance in neural infiltration, and resultant catecholamine signaling changes, may be responsible for the paradoxical pro-or anti-tumor activity of ILC2s observed in prior studies. Furthermore, tumors that constitutively secrete catecholamines, such as pheochromocytomas, are described as “cold” tumors with minimal immune invasion and thus are not very responsive to immunotherapies58. Interestingly, a study by Shi et al. demonstrated autocrine epinephrine secretion in an HER2+ breast cancer cell line, which in turn led to increased β2AR expression59. There are also older reports of norepinephrine secretion by breast cancer cells60. Taken together, it is possible that cancer cells may actively induce a catecholamine-abundant environment to promote immunosuppression. Measurement of norepinephrine (NE) and epinephrine (EPI) levels in the TME, which may vary based on physiological stress and the ability of the tumor for autocrine secretion, may also be necessary to fully confirm whether the presence of ILC2s is beneficial for tumors.

Psychological stress can lead to elevated levels of norepinephrine and increased β2AR signaling61,62. Therefore, management of stress may be the key to decreasing the pro-tumor effects of ILC2s and harnessing their activity to decrease tumor burden. As beta-blockers are already widely utilized with a minimal side effect profile, there already exists an accessible and safe method of decreasing the harmful effects of ILC2 cells by blocking β-adrenergic signaling, which will significantly improve the quality of cancer patient care. This study further supports the use of beta-blockers to enhance anti-tumor immune activity and improve cancer prognosis.

Methods

Mouse models

Wildtype (WT) C57BL/6J and BALB/c mice were purchased from Charles River. β2AR−/− mice on a BALB/c background were originally provided by Dr. David Farrar (University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center, Dallas, TX) and subsequently bred at Roswell Park Comprehensive Cancer Center (RPCCC; Buffalo, NY). β2AR−/− mice on a C57BL/6 background were made by the CRISPR technique in RPCCC. B6(C)-Il5tm1.1(icre)Lky/J (Red5, IL5cre) mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratory. β2ARfl/fl mice were obtained from Dr. Gerard Karsenty (Columbia University). Mice were maintained in specific pathogen-free facilities and were treated in accordance with the guidelines established by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Roswell Park Comprehensive Cancer Center (Buffalo, NY) under protocol 757M. Mice were kept on a 12/12-h light/ dark cycle at 22 °C with food and water ad libitum. Cages were kept in the same location throughout the experiment. Female mice 8–12 weeks of age were used for experiments. Mouse experiments were performed with at least three mice/experimental repeat and repeated at least three times. For experiments that require anesthesia, 5% of Isoflurane was administered via inhalation by the controlled anesthesia machine. Mice were identified by tail marking, and treatments/measurements were performed in ascending order. Mice were euthanized by the CO2 inhalation method. Experimental reporting adheres to ARRIVE guidelines.

Tumor cell culture and Implantation

4T1 and 4T1-luciferase (4T1-GFP) breast cancer cell lines were purchased from ATCC and cultured in RPMI1640 (Corning catalog # 10-040-CV) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/ streptomycin at 37 °C, 5% CO2. AT3 breast cancer cell line was provided by Dr. Scott Abrams (Roswell Park Comprehensive Cancer Center, Buffalo, NY) and cultured in DMEM with 4.5 g/L glucose, L-glutamine, and sodium pyruvate (Corning catalog #10-013-CV) supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 1 mM HEPES, and 1x MEM nonessential amino acids (NEAA) (Corning catalog #25-025-CI) at 37 °C, 7% CO2. A20-luciferase was obtained from Dr. Xuefang Cao (University of Maryland) and cultured in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C, 5% CO2.

Breast tumor cells were detached from the flask using 0.25% trypsin for 3 min (4T1) or 5 min (AT3) at 37 °C. Trypsin was neutralized with media prior to washing the cells twice with cold PBS. Cells were quantified using a hemocytometer, and high viability was confirmed using trypan blue staining. About 1 × 105 4T1 or 5 × 105 AT3 breast tumor cells were injected in 100 μL into the left fourth mammary fat pad of WT or β2AR−/− BALB/c mice (4T1), WT or β2AR−/− C57BL/6J mice (AT3), or IL5cre/+ or IL5cre/+β2ARfl/fl C57BL/6J mice (AT3) under 5% isoflurane anesthesia. Tumor growth was measured two to three times a week without blinding. Exclusions were made if the mouse required humane euthanization prior to endpoint.

Flow cytometry

Tumor weight was measured at the endpoint. Tumors less than 20 mg did not yield sufficient cells for downstream analysis and were excluded from analysis. Numbers were assigned to each mouse during harvest to hide genotype information until post-analysis. Tumors were dissected and digested with a 1x collagenase/hyaluronidase (STEMCELL Catalog # 07912) diluted in RPMI (Corning) for 30 min at 37 °C while rotating, dissociated using the Miltenyi gentleMACSTM (h_tumor_01.01). This was repeated once more. Digested cells were filtered through a 70-µm cell strainer (Fisherbrand Catalog # 22-363-548) to ensure a single-cell suspension. Cells were quantified, and 1 × 106 cells were activated with the cell activation cocktail with brefeldin A (BioLegend Catalog# 423304) for 4 h at 37 °C prior to staining for flow cytometry with the following panels. ILC (Live Dead Aqua, CD45-FITC, Lineage (CD3, CD11b, B220, Ter119)-BV421, CD127-PE-Cy7, CD90.2-BV785, ST2-APC, KLRG1-BV605, GATA3-BUV395, IL-13-PE, IL5-Af700, IFNg-APC-Cy7, Gzmb-PerCpCy5.5, and Tbet-BV711). T Cell (Live Dead Aqua, CD45-PE-Cy7, CD3-APC, CD4-BV711, CD8-BUV395, IFNg-APC-Cy7, TNFa-BV421, GzmB-FITC, and IL5-Af700). Cells were run on a BD LSR Fortessa and analyzed using FlowJo (v10.8.0). Cells were used for live/dead single stain control, and UltraComp eBeadsTM Plus Compensation Beads (InvitrogenTM catalog #01-3333-42) were used for single stain controls for the other fluorophores. Gates were drawn in reference to an unstained cell sample.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq)

Female WT and β2AR−/− BALB/c mice were injected with 1 × 105 4T1 breast cancer cells. Twenty-one days post-tumor implantation, mice were euthanized, and LDaqua−CD45+Lineage−CD90.2 + KLRG1+ ILCs were sorted on the SONY MA900 sorter using a 100-µm nozzle. Single-cell suspensions were prepared at a viability of >90% and processed using the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3’ Kit v3.1 (10x Genomics). Samples were loaded onto a 10x Genomics Chromium X system for partitioning into gel bead-in-emulsions (GEMs). Reverse transcription and cDNA amplification were performed within GEMs, followed by cDNA purification and library preparation. Gene expression libraries were constructed using the 10x Genomics 3’ Gene Expression kit. Libraries were quantified using a Qubit Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher) and quality-assessed on a TapeStation 4200 (Agilent). Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, targeting a read depth of ~25,000 reads per cell.

For the Chromium 10x Genomics libraries, the raw sequencing data, mapping results (BAM files), and quantification matrices were generated using Cellranger (v6.0.0)63 software with alignment using the mouse mm10 reference genome from the GENCODE annotation database. The filtered gene-barcode matrices that contain barcodes with unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts that passed the cell detection algorithm were used for further analysis. All downstream analyses were performed using Seurat (v4.4.0)64 single cell data analysis R package. Empty cells and doublets were removed from analysis by filtering out cells with very low or high RNA feature contents (<500 or >7500 genes detected) or higher mitochondrial RNA content (>15%). The normalized and scaled UMI counts were calculated using the SCTransform method. Dimension reductions, including principal component analysis (PCA) and UMAP, using the highly variable genes, were performed. Shared nearest neighbor (SNN)-based clustering on the first 36 principal components was used to identify the cell clusters, which were annotated by the SingleR packages65 using the ImmGen reference database of the celldex R package65. Differentially expressed genes between clusters and samples were identified using the FindMarkers function with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test within Seurat. Pathway analyses were carried out using the fgsea R package66 with gene list ranked by average log2 fold change using the Benjamini–Hochberg method for p value correction. Heatmaps were generated using the pheatmap R package67.

For metabolic gene signature analysis, pathways were defined as significantly enriched in clusters based on a normalized enrichment score (NES) threshold of >|1.5| and an adjusted p value (padj) threshold of <0.25. Select significant pathways were plotted using the ggplot2 package (v3.5.1) in R (v4.4.2).

ILC2-tumor cell coculture

WT and β2AR−/− BALB/c mice were treated i.n. with IL-33 (500 ng/mouse, 5 μg/mL) for 3 days. Lung ILC2s were enriched using PERCOLL density gradient followed by the STEMCELL ILC2 enrichment kit. 750 ILCs were cocultured with 4T1-Luciferase 1:1 ratio for 48 h (T48). Cells were cultured in DMEM with 4.5 g/L glucose, L-glutamine, and sodium pyruvate, and supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% P/S, 55 µM β-mercaptoethanol, 1X NEAA, and 1 mM HEPES. At 24 h (T24) and at T48, Luciferin (2 µL and 15 µg/µL) was added, and cells were imaged using the IVIS Spectrum (PerkinElmer). Total count measurements from blank wells were subtracted from the counts of each sample. No exclusions or randomizations were made.

For tumor-infiltrating ILC: 4T1 or ILC: A20 cocultures, ILCs (CD45+Lineage-CD90.2+KLRG1+) were sorted from tumors pooled from ten 4T1 tumor-bearing mice to achieve sufficient numbers of cells. About 1 × 103 ILCs were cocultured with 4T1-Luciferase or A20-Luciferase at a 1:1 ratio for 24 (T24). Luciferin (10 µL and 15 µg/µL) was added, and cells were imaged using the IVIS Spectrum. Total count measurements from blank wells were subtracted from the counts of each sample. Each sample was then normalized to the average of the counts from the 4T1 cells cultured without ILCs at T24.

ILC2-T cell coculture

ILCs (CD45+Lineage−CD90.2+KLRG1+) were sorted from 4T1 tumors, and T cells were isolated from the spleen of WT BALB/c mice. Isolated T cells were stained with CFSE and activated with DynabeadsTM Mouse T-Activator CD3/ CD28 for T-Cell Expansion and Activation (GibcoTM Catalog # 11452D) according to manufacturer instructions. About 1 × 103 ILCs were cocultured with the CFSE-stained pan-T cells at a 1:1 ratio for 72 h. The cells were harvested, stained with Live/Dead Aqua, CD4-BV785, CD8-BUV395, and CD90.2-BV421, and analyzed by flow cytometry for proliferation. %Proliferation is defined as the percentage of cells with decreased CFSE intensity relative to the unactivated T cells.

Western blot analysis

Cells were harvested and lysed by RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitor (Thermo Scientific Catalog #1860932 OR #A32961). Protein yield was quantified using the PierceTM BCA protein assay (Thermo Scientific Catalog# 23225). Protein samples were mixed with 4X Laemmli Sample Buffer (Bio-Rad Catalog #1610747) and then denatured at 100 °C for 5 min. The protein samples were separated via SDS-PAGE with the Precision Plus Protein™ All Blue Prestained Protein Standards (Bio-Rad Catalog # 1610373) and then were transferred to a Polyvinylidene Difluoride (PVDF) Membrane by semi-dry transfer method. The transferred PVDF membrane was cut based on the target proteins’ molecular weight and blocked with EveryBlot Blocking Buffer (Bio-Rad) at room temperature for 30 min. Primary antibodies (rabbit anti-mouse Vinculin, rabbit anti-mouse GATA3, and Goat anti-mouse ST2) were diluted in EveryBlot Blocking Buffer at 1:1000. All membrane strips were incubated with the antibody dilutions at 4 °C overnight. An anti-rabbit (Cell Signaling) and anti-Goat horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies were used at a dilution of 1:3000. The membranes were developed with Clarity Max Western ECL Substrate (Bio-Rad), and images were captured using the Bio-Rad ChemiDoc Touch (Bio-Rad). Results were analyzed by ImageJ (NIH).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis was performed using either GraphPad Prism 10 or R software. Differences between groups were analyzed either using the two-tailed Student’s t-test (two groups) or one-way ANOVA followed by post hoc Tukey’s multiple comparison test (multiple groups). The significance of tumor size differences was calculated at each time point using the two-tailed Student’s t-test. Differences were considered significant at p values <0.05. All data were shown as mean SEM with non-significant p values (≥0.05) displayed, *p value <0.05, **p value <0.01, ***p value <0.001, and ****p value <0.0001.
