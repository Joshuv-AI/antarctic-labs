PK-SMART: Penetrating Keratoplasty With Sutureless Membrane Artificial Endothelial Replacement Technique. Report of the First Three Clinical Cases
Authors: Luigi Fontana, Vito Romano, Natalie di Geronimo, Piera Versura, Maurizio Mete, Antonio Moramarco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose:To describe penetrating keratoplasty with sutureless membrane artificial endothelial replacement technique (PK-SMART), a surgical procedure that allows for combined penetrating keratoplasty (PK) and EndoArt (EndoArt, EyeYon Medical, Israel) implantation in high-risk eyes for graft failure.Methods:Three consecutive patients with failed PK and a history of multiple ocular surgeries underwent repeat PK combined with implantation of a synthetic endothelial replacement membrane. The implant was positioned within a stromal lamellar pocket of the donor cornea before graft transplantation, without the need for transcorneal sutures or postoperative gas tamponade. Outcome measures included corrected distance visual acuity (CDVA), graft clarity, central corneal thickness, and device adherence.Results:At 6 months, all corneal grafts were clear with complete EndoArt adhesion. Preoperative CDVA ranged from light perception to hand motion in 2 eyes. Postoperative CDVA improved to hand motion, 20/400, and 20/100, respectively. Central corneal thickness decreased in all patients, from 803 to 578 μm, from 975 to 587 μm, and from 761 to 548 μm. No postoperative complications were observed, including implant detachment requiring rebubbling.Conclusions:PK-SMART simultaneously addresses stromal opacities through PK and endothelial dysfunction through artificial endothelial replacement. The technique is suited for complex, high-risk eyes in which standalone EndoArt implantation cannot resolve stromal opacities and is associated with a high risk of device detachment. Central corneal clarity was restored in all our patients, with quantifiable improvements in visual function. Larger studies with extended follow-up will be required to confirm the long-term safety and efficacy of this procedure.

The EndoArt (EyeYon Medical, Israel) is a 50-μm thick, 6.5-mm diameter hydrophilic acrylic implant of novel design engineered to function as an artificial endothelial membrane.1,2 It acts as a barrier to aqueous ingress, promoting sustained stromal deturgescence with improvement of corneal transparency in eyes at high risk of early endothelial graft failure. Various clinical reports have documented the successful use of this device for complex cases, including multiple failed keratoplasties and chronic corneal edema following anterior segment surgical complications, without significant device-related adverse events, such as stromal melt or perforation.3–8

However, despite these advantages, standalone EndoArt implantation cannot address preexisting stromal opacities or structural changes related to chronic edema, past infection, or multiple graft failures. In these eyes, subepithelial fibrosis together with architectural stromal distortion may persist despite deturgescence and limit visual rehabilitation, even if the device functions well.9,10 Thus, the clinical applicability of single EndoArt implantation is limited in patients requiring both the restoration of endothelial function and stromal replacement.

Conventional insertion of the EndoArt also may be suboptimal in surgically complex eyes, generally requiring temporary transcorneal suturing and prolonged air or gas tamponade to promote device adhesion. High detachment rates have been described, especially in eyes with an irregular posterior corneal surface post–penetrating keratoplasty (PK) or those with incomplete irido-lens diaphragms from prior surgeries.11 These limitations underscore the need for surgical strategies that promote early device stability.12

To overcome these challenges, we report for the first time a new technique that combines PK with sutureless EndoArt implantation. By embedding the implant within a donor stromal pocket before PK, this procedure simultaneously replaces diseased host stroma and provides mechanical stabilization of the device, minimizing dependence on gas tamponade and obviating the need for transcorneal sutures. In this article, we present the feasibility, early safety, and short-term clinical outcome of PK-SMART in 3 high-risk eyes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This prospective pilot study was conducted between January 2023 and May 2024 at the Ophthalmology Unit of the IRCCS Azienda Ospedaliero-Universitaria of Bologna. The study was approved by the hospital's Institutional Review Board (Registration No. 901/2022/Oss/AOUBo) and adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ad hoc informed consent for EndoArt implantation and the collection of personal data were obtained from all participants.

Eligible patients were ≥18 years of age, had longstanding decompensated full-thickness grafts with stromal opacity requiring repeat PK, and were considered high-risk. This status was defined by one or more of the following: prior failed PK or EK, complex anterior segment anatomy, multiple previous anterior or posterior segment surgeries (aphakia, vitrectomy, iris-lens diaphragm deficiency), or coexisting ocular comorbidities such as glaucoma.

Outcome measures were visual acuity, graft transparency, device adherence, and central corneal pachymetry.

Preoperative Examination

All patients received a complete ophthalmic examination before surgery, including corrected distance visual acuity (CDVA) (Snellen), slitlamp biomicroscopy, tonometry (Goldman tonometer, Haag-Streit, Köniz, Switzerland), and B-scan ultrasonography. Anterior-segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT) (Casia II, Tomey Corp, Nagoya, Japan) was used to evaluate the corneal shape profile, pachymetry, stromal opacities, and anterior segment abnormalities.

Surgical Technique

A donor sclerocorneal button was mounted endothelial side up on an artificial anterior chamber (Single-Use Artificial Chamber for Single-Use Trephines, Moria, Bourbon-l'Archambault, France) and pressurized with balanced salt solution. After drying the endothelial surface, the corneal center was marked (Video 1). A 4-mm dermatological punch (Kai industries, Seki City, Japan) created a circular groove approximately 200 μm deep, within which Descemet membrane and the endothelium were removed. An angled disc knife (Alcon Laboratories, Fort Worth, TX) was then used to create a circumferential 2-mm wide intrastromal pocket (Fig. 1A).

A, A circumferential 2-mm wide intrastromal pocket is fashioned with an angled disc knife. B, The EndoArt device is placed onto the exposed stromal surface. The peripheral margins of the device are tucked into the stromal pocket, leaving a central 4-mm area of the implant free. C, The donor corneal button is trephined using an 8.5 to 9.0-mm donor punch. D, The composite donor button with the integrated EndoArt device is implanted into the recipient eye, with the device facing downward, and secured using 16 interrupted 10-0 nylon sutures.

Following chamber deflation, the EndoArt device was positioned on the stromal surface in a back-to-forward orientation (“F” mark upside down). The peripheral edges of the device were tucked into the lamellar pocket to secure it to the donor cornea, while leaving a 4-mm central area of the implant free (Fig. 1B). The corneal button was then punched using an 8.5 to 9.0-mm donor punch (single-use suction punch, Moria, Bourbon-l'Archambault, France) according to the planned PK size (Fig. 1C).

Patients underwent surgery under local or general anesthesia based on patient preference. After usual preparation and draping, a lid speculum was placed, and a Flieringa ring was sutured to the sclera. A full-thickness corneal trephination of 8.5 to 9.0-mm diameter, centered on the previous PK, was performed with a suction trephine (single-use suction trephine, Moria, Bourbon-l'Archambault, France). Finally, the composite donor button and EndoArt device were implanted, with the device oriented downward, and sutured with 16 interrupted 10-0 nylon sutures (Fig. 1D). Microscope-integrated OCT (Leica Biosystems, Nussloch, Germany) was used to confirm device adhesion at the end of the procedure. No air or gas injection was performed, and no patient required postoperative posturing.

Postoperative Management

Postoperative treatment consisted of tobramycin 0.3% and dexamethasone phosphate 0.1% eye drops 6 times daily for 1 month, 4 times daily for the next 2 months, and twice daily thereafter. Follow-up visits were planned on day 1, at 1 week, and monthly thereafter.

RESULTS

Case 1

A 77-year-old woman with a history of multiple retinal detachment surgeries, prior PK in 2001, and 2 failed DSEKs (2018–2021) presented with severe bullous keratopathy with stromal opacity in the setting of aphakia, silicone oil tamponade, and partial aniridia (Fig. 2). Preoperatively, the visual acuity was light perception, and the central corneal thickness (CCT) was 803 μm. Given the high risk of device detachment with conventional EndoArt implantation, PK-SMART was performed. At 6 months, the graft was centrally clear with a CCT of 578 μm, the implant was completely adherent to the posterior stroma, and the CDVA improved to hand motion (HM).

C, Slitlamp photograph obtained 6 months after penetrating keratoplasty with sutureless membrane artificial endothelial replacement (PK-SMART), showing a central corneal transparency of the PK graft. D, AS-OCT image illustrating the artificial endothelial membrane (EndoArt) embedded within the grafted cornea; the central 4-mm zone of the device remains free, while the peripheral portion is tucked into the stromal corneal pocket. The central corneal pachymetry is 578 μm thickness.

Case 2

A 79-year-old woman with a history of bilateral keratoconus and prior PK in both eyes was referred due to endothelial decompensation in her right eye (Fig. 3). She had undergone PK in 1999, followed by a DSEK in 2023 for graft failure. The Preoperative CDVA in her right eye was HM. Clinical examination showed severe corneal edema with a partially detached DSEK graft, medium-to-deep stromal opacities, and a decentered intraocular lens (IOL). Preoperative vision was HM, and the CCT was 975 μm. The PK-SMART technique was proposed to manage the dual corneal pathology. The detached DSEK and the dislocated IOL were removed during surgery, leaving the patient aphakic. At 6 months, the graft maintained clarity within the central 4 mm; the CCT was 587 μm; the implant was well adherent and centered, and the CDVA improved to 20/400.

A, Preoperative slit-lamp photograph of patient 2 showing an opaque, edematous PK graft with marked alterations of the anterior chamber architecture. B, AS-OCT image demonstrating a detached DSEK graft and a partially dislocated intraocular lens. The CCT is 975 μm. C, Slitlamp photograph obtained 6 months after PK-SMART showing a clear central cornea. D, AS-OCT image shows a well-adherent EndoArt implant. The CCT is 587 μm.

Case 3

A 74-year-old man, with complicated cataract surgery including posterior capsule rupture, anterior vitrectomy, and IOL exchange, developed severe corneal decompensation of the left eye (Fig. 4). Surgery resulted in progressive endothelial failure, which was then treated by PK in 2003 that failed within 18 months. A second PK was performed in 2009, which failed within several months. Owing to stromal edema and haze, vision was HM, with a CCT of 761 μm. In light of a history of extensive anterior segment manipulation, chronic inflammation, instability of the IOL, and 2 previous PK failures, the PK-SMART technique was offered. Six months after surgery, the graft was centrally clear, with a CCT of 548 μm; the device was entirely adherent, and the CDVA improved to 20/100.

DISCUSSION

Managing corneal decompensation in eyes with multiple failed grafts and ocular comorbidities remains very challenging. In these patients, repeated keratoplasty procedures often lead to limited graft survival, high complication rates, and poor visual outcomes.13,14 The availability of the EndoArt device introduces a new option for restoring corneal clarity in eyes at high risk of early graft failure.15,16 However, because it serves only as an artificial endothelial barrier, the device cannot overcome preexisting stromal opacities or fibrosis that may occur following chronic edema or after repeated graft failures. For these reasons, in eyes with significant preexisting stromal opacities, the optical benefit derived from the device alone is inherently limited, and its use is thus contraindicated.

In addition, standard implantation methods using temporary suturing and air-gas tamponade have been associated with a high incidence of postoperative device detachment and the need for repeated rebubbling procedures, especially in eyes with a history of PK with an irregular posterior surface profile, or other ocular surgeries with a compromised irido-lens diaphragm.17

Spontaneous detachment indeed constitutes the most common complication after EndoArt implantation, with rates as high as 80% to 100%.11 Rare spontaneous reattachments without further intervention have, however, been described.18,19 As spontaneous resolution is unpredictable, it cannot be relied upon, particularly in post-PK eyes. These findings highlight the importance of surgical refinements that provide early and reliable device stability.12

Our proposed technique, which combines PK with sutureless EndoArt implantation, directly addresses these limitations. Embedded within a stromal pocket of a donor cornea before transplantation, this transplant configuration simultaneously replaces opacified host stroma with a full-thickness graft and secures prompt mechanical stability of the EndoArt, potentially eliminating adhesion problems associated with conventional device implantation. This configuration avoids early dislodgment of the device, precludes the need for transcorneal sutures, prolonged gas tamponade, and postoperative posturing. By maintaining implant stability throughout the procedure, this approach may be particularly beneficial in eyes with glaucoma drainage devices, incomplete or absent iris–lens diaphragms, prior vitrectomy, or silicone oil tamponade situations in which an air or gas bubble may provide only a temporary stabilizing effect. Avoiding transfixing sutures also lowers the risk of infection, vascularization, and foreign body sensation, and diminishes patient discomfort.20

In PK-SMART, because postoperative deturgescence endothelial function is provided by the artificial endothelial implant rather than by donor endothelial cells, the use of donor corneas with low endothelial cell density may be feasible, provided that stromal transparency and tissue quality are preserved. This approach could potentially expand donor tissue utilization, although dedicated studies are required to confirm safety and clinical outcomes.

The 3 cases described herein represent various high-risk scenarios, including graft decompensation with stromal opacities following PK, with or without previous EK, aphakia with retained silicone oil, and multiple complicated anterior segment surgeries. In all patients, the combined approach resulted in central graft clarity and secure device adhesion, without postoperative complications, including device detachment and rebubbling during follow-up. Device adhesion to the posterior stroma was maintained in all patients with a remarkable deturgescence effect. Preexisting ocular comorbidities limited visual recovery; however, graft transparency improved patient visual function, allowing adequate fundus visualization.

Another theoretical advantage of this technique is the possibility of long-term integration of the implant. A biological seal by peripheral annular fibrosis between the device edge and the host stroma may enhance adhesion without encroaching upon the central optical zone.12 Longer-term follow-up studies are necessary to assess the durability of this integration and to delineate further the risk of late complications, including interface opacification.

The short-term results in our 3 cases reinforce this hypothesis, as no device displacement or detachment occurred without stringent postoperative posturing. The role of adjunctive gas tamponade in achieving long-term adhesion with this technique remains to be clarified. In all 3 cases, we opted not to inject air or gas tamponade because the implant was shown to be immediately stable and adherent at the end of surgery. No device detachment occurred during the postoperative period. Further studies will be required in larger cohorts with uniform protocols to ascertain whether gas injection is necessary, optional, or potentially avoidable in this surgical approach.

Beyond eyes with prior keratoplasty failure, the PK-SMART technique may also be applicable in selected patients presenting with chronic endothelial decompensation and visually significant stromal opacity in the absence of prior PK. By combining full-thickness stromal replacement with artificial endothelial implantation in a single procedure, PK-SMART could offer a comprehensive solution for eyes with concomitant endothelial failure and stromal disease, even as a primary surgical approach. This strategy may be particularly advantageous in eyes deemed at high risk for conventional endothelial keratoplasty due to anterior segment complexity or anticipated poor graft survival. Further studies are warranted to explore the role of PK-SMART as a primary intervention in this subset of patients without previous full-thickness corneal transplantation.

CONCLUSIONS

PK-SMART is a promising surgical approach that enables simultaneous PK and sutureless EndoArt implantation. Unlike standalone EndoArt implantation, which cannot resolve pre-existing stromal disease and is associated with a high risk of device detachment in surgically complex eyes, PK-SMART enables simultaneous stromal replacement and immediate mechanical stabilization of the artificial endothelial layer. By embedding the EndoArt device within a lamellar stromal pocket, this technique may eliminate the need for transcorneal sutures, air or gas injection, and postoperative posturing. Early graft clarity, visual improvement, immediate implant stability, and no postoperative device detachment were accomplished in 3 high-risk cases. This technique may expand the therapeutic options in cases of severe endothelial dysfunction with stromal opacities in anatomically complicated eyes. Larger studies with extended follow-up will be required to confirm the long-term safety and efficacy of this procedure.
