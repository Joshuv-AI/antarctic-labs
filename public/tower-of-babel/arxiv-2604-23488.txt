Do Synthetic Trajectories Reflect Real Reward Hacking? A Systematic Study on Monitoring In-the-Wild Hacking in Code Generation
Lichen Li, Hengguang Zhou, Yijun Liang, Tianyi Zhou, Cho-Jui Hsieh, [0.6em] Peking University, [0.6em] University of Maryland

Abstract
Reward hacking in code generation, where models exploit evaluation loopholes to obtain full reward without correctly solving the tasks, poses a critical challenge for Reinforcement Learning (RL) and the deployment of reasoning models. Existing studies have been conducted primarily on synthetic hacking trajectories. However, whether these synthetic behaviors faithfully represent naturally emerging hacking in the wild remains unclear. In this work, we present a systematic analysis of the synthetic vs. in-the-wild discrepancy in reward hacking. We examine to what extent hacking behaviors induced by prompting resemble those emerging during RL training, and whether monitors trained on synthetic trajectories generalize to naturally arising but previously unseen hacking. To scale up the curation of in-the-wild reward hacking trajectories, we modified Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) by injecting conflicting unit tests as tracers and applying a “resampling-until-hack” mechanism. Through controlled comparisons between monitors trained on synthetic versus in-the-wild data, we find that (1) synthetic-data-trained monitors fail to generalize to "in-the-wild" hacking , and (2) monitors trained on our "in-the-wild" trajectories demonstrate stronger generalizability to unseen hacking types . Our results indicate that synthetic reward hacking data may not fully reflect natural reward hacking behaviors, and that relying solely on synthetic data can lead to misleading conclusions. The codebase is available at https://github.com/LichenLillc/CoTMonitoring.git

Introduction

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has significantly advanced the capabilities of reasoning models, particularly in complex tasks like code generation. Central to this paradigm is the objective of optimizing a reward model that guides learning towards a human-defined measure of success; yet, defining a golden reward model that perfectly aligns with human intent is impossible in practice. Therefore, training relies on "proxy rewards" which inevitably diverge from the true task objectives
(
13
)
. Models may learn to exploit these imperfections, achieving high reward without actually accomplishing the intended task, a phenomenon known as reward hacking
(
3
;
14
;
5
)
. For example, hard-coding outputs, modifying unit tests, or manipulating test environments. With the increasing deployment of reasoning models, robust monitoring of such behavior is crucial
(
7
)
. It serves not only as a safety guardrail to prevent deceptive outputs in deployment
(
1
)
but also as a critical mechanism for ensuring that post-training effectively optimizes for genuine problem-solving capabilities
(
25
)
.

Despite growing interest in this domain, the development and testing of prevailing monitoring approaches mostly rely on synthetic hacking trajectories
(
1
;
4
;
17
)
, implicitly assuming that instruction-induced hacking behaviors are representative of those that emerge naturally
(
19
;
14
)
. Consequently, it remains largely unclear whether monitors trained on synthetic data can generalize robustly to real-world settings, a gap partly caused by the lack of large-scale collection of in-the-wild hacking trajectories.

To address this limitation, we develop a scalable procedure for collecting naturally occurring reward hacking trajectories in code generation. We first implement an automated detection mechanism that injects conflicting unit tests, creating a setting in which achieving full reward requires satisfying mutually inconsistent conditions and thereby signals hacking. We then incorporate a "resampling-until-hack" mechanism within the Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) algorithm
(
16
)
to systematically collect such trajectories at scale. Policy updates are performed only when at least one hacking trajectory is detected within a sampled group; otherwise, the group is discarded and resampled. This procedure selectively retains naturally occurring hacking trajectories, enabling scalable collection of in-the-wild hacking trajectories.

Leveraging this dataset, we systematically evaluate the effectiveness of synthetic data for real-world hacking monitoring. We train and test a diverse suite of mainstream monitors, ranging from zero-shot LLMs and fine-tuned text classifiers to hidden-state probes, on both synthetic and in-the-wild hacking trajectories. Our experiments reveal two key insights: first, monitors trained on synthetic data fail to generalize to detecting in-the-wild hacking. Second, and in contrast, monitors trained on in-the-wild trajectories demonstrate stronger generalizability, extending successfully to unseen hacking types. These findings call into question the reliability of synthetic trajectories for reliable real-world hacking monitoring, highlighting the importance of grounding monitors in in-the-wild hacking data.

2.1
Reward Hacking and Monitors

Misalignment in large language models arises when model behaviors deviate from intended objectives despite optimizing training signals, and can emerge even under seemingly well-specified tasks or reasoning objectives

.
A growing body of work identifies reward hacking as a key mechanism underlying misalignment, where models exploit imperfections in proxy rewards in RLHF settings and exhibit shortcut behaviors that can generalize to harmful outcomes

To detect such behaviors, existing approaches span both representation- and reasoning-based paradigms. Representation-based methods include hidden-state probes such as MLP and linear probes

Reasoning-based methods include process reward models and Chain-of-Thought (CoT) monitoring

However, most existing monitors are developed and evaluated in controlled or curated settings, and it remains unclear how well they generalize to naturally emerging reward hacking behaviors in more realistic scenarios.

2.2
Faithfulness of Reward Hacking Data and Benchmarks

A key limitation of current reward hacking monitors lies in the faithfulness of their training data and evaluation benchmarks.
Most existing approaches rely on synthetic constructions, such as School of Reward Hacks
(
17
)
and persona-based prompting
(
19
)
, where hacking behaviors are explicitly induced and exhibit simplified and pattern-consistent characteristics.
Some recent efforts take a step toward greater realism, though they still rely on designed settings, such as increasingly gameable environments or explicit hacking incentives

.
Other work
(
26

moves closer to in-the-wild coding settings through conflict test injection, but is designed to measure the cheating propensity of LLMs rather than to benchmark reward hacking monitors.
At the same time, prior work has observed reward hacking in real RL and production settings
(
1
;
14
)
, but such observations are limited in scale and lack systematic data curation for training and evaluation.
Some recent efforts take a step toward greater realism.
As a result, both training and evaluation pipelines suffer from a distributional mismatch between synthetic and naturally emerging reward hacking behaviors, limiting the generalization and reliability of monitoring methods.
In this work, we address this gap by developing a scalable framework for collecting in-the-wild reward hacking trajectories, enabling more faithful training and evaluation.

Preliminary

We utilize the GRPO algorithm as the post-training framework for code generation tasks. For each coding question
q
sampled from the distribution
\mathcal{D}
, the current policy
\pi_{\theta_{old}}
generates a group of candidate code solutions
\{c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{G}\}
. The objective function for optimizing the policy
\pi_{\theta}
is defined as:

\begin{split}\mathcal{J}_{GRPO}(\theta)=&\mathbb{E}_{q\sim\mathcal{D},\{c_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}\sim\pi_{\theta_{old}}}\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\bigg(\min\left(\rho_{i}\hat{A}_{i},\text{clip}(\rho_{i},1\pm\epsilon)\hat{A}_{i}\right)-\beta\mathbb{D}_{KL}\Big(\pi_{\theta}||\pi_{ref}\Big)\bigg),\end{split}

(1)

where
\rho_{i}={\pi_{\theta}(c_{i}|q)}/{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(c_{i}|q)}
is the probability ratio;
\epsilon
is the clipping hyper-parameter; and
\beta
denotes the KL-divergence penalty coefficient.

The advantage
\hat{A}_{i}
for the
i
-th candidate is estimated by normalizing its reward within the group:

\hat{A}_{i}=\frac{\big(r_{i}-\text{mean}(\{r_{1},\dots,r_{G}\})\big)}{\text{std}(\{r_{1},\dots,r_{G}\})}

(2)

The binary reward
r_{i}
is computed by evaluating solution
c_{i}
against a set of unit tests
T_{q}
for question
q
within a sandbox environment
E\in\mathcal{E}
:

r_{i}=R(c_{i},T_{q};E).

3.2
Trace-and-Amplify

To enable large-scale collection of in-the-wild hacking trajectories, we propose the
Trace-and-Amplify
framework illustrated in Figure
1
, which consists of two components: (1) an automated tracing mechanism for identifying naturally occurring hacking behavior, and (2) a GRPO-based amplification procedure that increases the occurrence of such behaviors during training without explicit prompting. The algorithm is detailed in Section
A.4
.

Tracing in-the-wild hacking via conflicting unit test.

To detect in-the-wild hacking behavior, we introduce a simple mechanism that only hacking solutions can satisfy all constraints. For each task
q
with unit tests
T_{q}={t_{1},\dots,t_{M}}
, we construct a contradictory set
T_{q}^{\bot}
by adding a fake test with the same input but a different output as an existing test, making the full set impossible to satisfy. We evaluate solutions against the full test set
T_{q}^{\bot}
and track their outcomes. Under this setup, any solution that passes all tests is directly identified as exhibiting hacking behavior. Importantly, these trajectories remain valid under the original evaluation and correspond to in-the-wild reward hacking, as any solution that satisfies the conflicting tests also passes a standard unit test suite.

Resampling-until-hack.
To increase the frequency of naturally occurring hacking during training, we adopt a resampling-until-hack strategy. For a sampled group
\{c_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}
with rewards
\{r_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}
, a policy update (Equation
1
) is performed only if there is at least one instance passes the contradictory unit test set (i.e.,
\exists i
such that
r_{i}=1
). Otherwise, the group is discarded and resampled until a hacking response is observed or a retry limit
K
is reached, after which the instance is skipped.

Figure 1:
Overview of the Trace-and-Amplify framework.
For each task, we construct a contradictory unit test set to identify hacking behavior, where only hacking solutions can satisfy all constraints. During GRPO training, candidate solutions are sampled and evaluated; groups without a detected hacking instance are discarded and resampled, while groups containing at least one hacking sample are used for policy updates. This process enables scalable collection of naturally occurring in-the-wild hacking trajectories without explicit prompting.

4
Experiments

4.1
Overview

In this section, we conduct a systematic empirical study to evaluate whether synthetic reward hacking trajectories faithfully represent naturally emerging (“in-the-wild”) hacking behaviors, and to assess the generalization properties of monitors trained on these different data sources.

Our experiments are designed to answer the following questions:

•

Synthetic-to-Wild Transferability:
Do monitors trained on synthetic hacking trajectories generalize to in-the-wild hacking behaviors?

•

Generalization to Unseen Hacking Types:
Do monitors trained on in-the-wild data exhibit stronger generalization to unseen hacking trajectories compared to synthetic-trained monitors?

•

Consistent Conclusion Across Model Families:
Does the gap between synthetic and in-the-wild data persist across different model architectures?

4.3
Experiment Results

4.3.1
Do monitors trained on synthetic hacking trajectories generalize to in-the-wild hacking behaviors?

A central question in reward hacking monitoring is whether models trained on synthetic data can generalize to naturally emerging behaviors encountered in realistic settings. This is particularly important because most prior work relies on synthetic trajectories for both training and evaluation, implicitly assuming that they are representative of in-the-wild hacking. If this assumption fails, monitors may appear effective in controlled settings but break down in deployment. We first evaluate whether synthetic hacking trajectories reflect real-world (in-the-wild) hacking behavior.

Setup

To address this research question, we train monitors on the synthetic hacking dataset described in Section
4.2.3
and evaluate them on the in-the-wild test set constructed via our Trace-and-Amplify framework. We then evaluate the reverse direction by training on in-the-wild data and testing on synthetic trajectories.

This bidirectional evaluation allows us to characterize
asymmetric generalization
: whether transfer from synthetic to in-the-wild differs from transfer in the opposite direction. Such asymmetry is critical for understanding whether synthetic data provides sufficient coverage of real-world hacking behaviors.

For zero-shot LLM monitors, we prompt the models with explicit instructions and descriptions of hacking behaviors to perform detection. The prompts include task definitions and characterizations of different hacking strategies, and full prompt details are provided in the appendix.

6
Conclusion

Our study provides a systematic evaluation of the discrepancy between synthetic and in-the-wild reward hacking trajectories in reward hacking monitoring for code generation.
Through the development of our Trace-and-Amplify framework, we demonstrate that naturally emergent hacking behaviors can be efficiently scaled and captured using automated unit-test tracers and conditional GRPO resampling without the need for deliberate prompting.
Leveraging the framework, we curated a large-scale dataset of in-the-wild hacking trajectories. Our empirical evaluation reveals a substantial discrepancy between instruction-induced and naturally emerging hacking behaviors. We demonstrate that monitors trained on synthetic data fail to generalize to in-the-wild hacking data. Meanwhile, those trained on in-the-wild data exhibit stronger generalizability to unseen hacking types. These results indicate that synthetic hacking data cannot fully represent naturally emerging ones, underscoring the importance of grounding monitors in in-the-wild data for more reliable benchmarking and deployment.
