Dynamics of Heparin Translocations Through Solid‐State Nanopores
Authors: Kexin Zhao, Matthew Waugh, Kyle Briggs, Mohammad P. Jamshidi, Janelle Sauvageau, Vincent Tabard‐Cossa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCarbohydrates are one of the many biomolecular building blocks of life, with many practical applications in medicine. However, current methods for analysing these biomolecules struggle to operate at the single‐molecule level. Carbohydrate sensing with solid‐state nanopores has recently been explored but has proved difficult due to their structural complexity and the rapid translocation through the pores in the case of short polysaccharide chains. Among the numerous forms they can take, the best‐known example is heparin, a widely used anticoagulant that has a closely related but toxic contaminant. In this work, we study the kinetics of capture and translocation of heparin molecules passing through a solid‐state nanopore based on experimental results and signal simulations, seeking to understand the dynamics of passage. We examine the molecular interaction with the pore as a function of pH to elucidate the conformational dynamics of translocation. Despite using state‐of‐the‐art electronics, we find that the durations of translocation through bare silicon nitride pores are too short to provide the resolution needed to reliably distinguish heparin from its contaminants. En route, we shed light on the physical mechanisms that govern translocation and suggest practical means by which future work might overcome these limitations.

Introduction

Carbohydrates are biomolecules crucial to life [1], but their complexity makes them difficult to analyse. Not only do hundreds of structurally distinct monosaccharides exist, but also each monosaccharide can form glycosidic bonds with different regiochemistries and two possible anomeric configurations (α and β) [1, 2]. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) is often used to determine a monosaccharide's identity and linkages [1, 2], but for large bacterial carbohydrates, hours of NMR spectral recording lead to results requiring careful and lengthy interpretation. Size exclusion chromatography (SEC) can provide insight into a large carbohydrate's size but also requires long chromatography runs. As a result, carbohydrates are poorly understood compared to their simpler protein, lipid and nucleotide counterparts. To speed up analysis, strategies, such as lectin microarrays [3], can provide an overview of carbohydrates in cells or tissues. However, such equipment is not widely accessible and is highly dependent on having access to a range of purified lectins for various carbohydrate targets.

In recent years, nanopores have garnered attention as single‐molecule sensors, especially in the fields of DNA and protein analysis [4, 5, 6]. Nanopores are small holes in thin membranes that operate on a simple premise: When a molecule passes through a pore that is conducting ionic current, it causes a transient blockage in that current that is characteristic of that molecule. Previous work has demonstrated that solid‐state nanopores can sense molecules such as heparin [7], hyaluronic acid [8], and xylan [9]. However, solid‐state nanopore‐based sensing still faces challenges [10]. Events are often too fast for the measurement electronics to properly resolve them [11], and target molecules may be too weakly charged for detection via voltage‐driven passage through a nanopore [11, 12]. There remains a need to improve nanopore‐based analytical techniques to address these challenges, but first the dynamics of passage and the forces acting on carbohydrates must be better understood.

The role of electrophoresis (EP) and electroosmosis (EO), which refer to the electric field‐driven process by which charged molecules are driven through a medium, and the flow of liquid dragged by counterions to a charged surface set in motion, respectively, has been well established for DNA translocations through solid‐state nanopores [13]. For proteins, the interplay between EO and EP can significantly affect the kinetics of capture and passage [14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20] because of the non‐uniformity and weaker charge of their structures.

In this work, we aim to improve nanopore‐based sensing methods and to better understand the dynamics of voltage‐driven passage of carbohydrates. To do this, we explore the relationship between translocation characteristics and molecular structure to find optimal operating conditions. We also investigate the nature of a prototypical carbohydrate translocating through a solid‐state nanopore while varying the relative influence of electrophoretic and electro‐osmotic forces. Heparin was selected because of its practical use as an anticoagulant [21], the recognized challenges associated with its complete analysis [22] and the need to identify contamination with oversulphated chondroitinsulphate, which can lead to serious adverse reactions [23].

As the direction and strength of electrophoretic and electro‐osmotic forces are dependent on molecular charge, applied voltage and nanopore wall surface charge density [19], we conducted a series of experiments in conditions of varying pH to provide control over both molecular and pore wall surface charges. We provide details of the pores and conditions used in the methods section. We estimated the conformations and shapes of heparin molecules as they pass through the pore by comparing our experimental results with an analytical conductance model, described in detail elsewhere [24]. We also studied the kinetics of voltage‐driven passage of heparin as a function of pH. Throughout, we consistently find that overall dwell times are extremely fast, often on the scale of µs.

Materials and Methods

Nanopore Fabrication

We used 5 mm × 5 mm chips with 200 µm‐thick silicon frames purchased from Norcada for this study. The membrane has a 40 µm × 40 µm area containing a free‐standing silicon nitride (SiN) membrane with an oxide underlayer and a thickness of 12 ± 2 nm. Although thicker membranes could result in slower translocation, 12 nm membranes were chosen in favour of retaining signal amplitude in cases of smaller molecules like heparin, as the absolute strength of the signal increases with decreasing membrane thickness and as the pore diameter approaches the molecular size [24, 25, 26]. Flow cells were designed and 3D printed in‐house. These flow cells provide fluidic access to both sides of the membrane alike those presented in previous work [27]. See Figure S1 for the power spectrum density (PSD) of a device. We fabricated pores using the controlled breakdown (CBD) method, following best practices previously published [27, 28]. After fabrication, pores are usually relatively small, ranging from 1 to 3 nm according to I–V measurements and assuming a cylindrical pore model [25, 26], and must be enlarged to the desired size following previously established protocols using alternating pulses of intermediate voltage to controllably enlarge the pore [29, 30]. Nanopores used for experiments were 5 ± 1 nm in diameter based on I–V measurements unless otherwise noted.

Electrolyte and Analyte Preparation

We fabricated and enlarged pores in 1 M potassium chloride (KCl) and 3.6 M lithium chloride (LiCl) solutions, respectively, and buffered at pH 8 with 10 mM 4‐(2‐hydroxyethyl)‐1‐piperazineethanesulphonic acid (HEPES). Analyte mixtures were made by dissolving powdered heparin purchased from Sigma Aldrich (porcine intestinal mucosa H3149) in 3.6 M LiCl and 4 M KCl to a final concentration of 500 nM in both cases. For experiments in 3.6 M LiCl, at pH 8, the same solution as the fabrication solution was used; at pH 5, 10 mM 2‐(N‐morpholino) ethanesulphonic acid (MES) was used as a buffer, whereas at pH 4 and 2, solutions were unbuffered. pH measurements were taken for the pH 4 solution before and after the experiments to confirm stability with ± 0.1. For experiments in 4 M KCl, the pH 8 solution was buffered with 10 mM HEPES, pH 6 with MES, and pH 4 with 20 mM acetic acid and 4 mM sodium acetate, whereas pH 2 remained unbuffered. For oversulphated chondroitin sulphate (OSCS), powder was purchased from Sigma Aldrich (US Pharmacopeia #1133580), and experiments were conducted in 4 M KCl solutions at a concentration of 500 nM, at pH 4, 6 and 8, using the same buffers as above.

Data Acquisition and Analysis

We acquired data using the Chimera VC 100, which has a sampling rate of 4.17 MHz and a full bandwidth of 1 MHz. A pair of Ag/AgCl electrodes is interfaced between the VC 100 unit and the solution to both apply voltage and measure the ionic current through the pores. In all experiments, the voltage applied was ± 200 mV. The choice of voltage of ± 200 mV is adopted from many previous similar solid‐state nanopore studies [17, 31, 32, 33, 34]. Data were low‐pass filtered with a digital 8‐pole Bessel filter with a cut‐off of 200 kHz to provide sufficient signal‐to‐noise ratio (SNR) for analysis. We analysed the data using Nanolyzer, developed by Northern Nanopore Instruments (now Oxford Nanopore Technologies), defining an event as a deviation from the baseline of 6.5 times its local root‐mean‐square (RMS) noise.

We measured the average molecular weights of heparin and OSCS using SEC on an Agilent 1260 Infinity II system with a Superose 12 10/300 GL column with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) as an eluant. A dextran ladder from Agilent enabled size comparisons between the heparin and OSCS samples (see Figures S4 and S5). Analyte detection was based on a refractive index detector.

Results and Discussion

Heparin Translocation Signals and Molecular Size

To investigate the translocation kinetics of heparin through a solid‐state nanopore, we conducted a series of experiments with heparin solutions at different pH values to explore any differences resulting from different protonation states (Figure 1a). Heparin is a highly acidic molecule with sulphate monoesters and sulphamido groups with a pK a of approximately 1. It also contains weaker carboxylic acids (uronic acids), which can be partially protonated depending on the pH (pK a ≈ 3) [35]. As some have linked the protonation states of heparin to structural changes (e.g., loss of helical structures at a lower pH [36]), and the charge of the heparin molecules might interact with the pore surface, we hypothesized that modifying the pH might result in slower translocations and allow for more accurate measurement of the blockage signature. Previous estimates have shown that SiN has an isoelectric point around pH 4 and 5 [37].

The signal is digitally low‐pass filtered at 200 kHz. (c) Heat maps of scatter plots of heparin translocations showing maximum current deviation versus dwell time in log scale at different pH values. The ionic current trace and individual events of heparin translocating through a 5.4 ± 0.4 nm pore in a 12 ± 2 nm thick membrane pore in 4 M KCl pH 4 are shown in Figure 1b. Figure 1c shows the heat maps of scatter plots of maximum deviation against dwell time on a log scale. Maximum deviation refers to the current difference between the baseline and the single data point that is farthest away from the baseline within an event (i.e., maximum blockage level in the event + noise) and, as such, is sensitive to the level of noise and the applied low‐pass filter. This metric is used instead of more traditional fits when events are fast and not conducive to accurate fits, as distortions arise, and events are missed when the duration of translocation events is similar to the response time of the electronics of the system [38]. All sub‐panels of Figure 1c contain events from the same 5.4 nm pore for four different pH values and both opposite applied voltages. Here, we define an event as beginning whenever the signal deviates by > 6.5 standard deviations from the baseline.

In Figure 1c, we see that under positive applied voltage (+200 mV), population variations at different pH levels are evident (pH 2: N ∼ 14 000; pH 4: N ∼ 36 000; pH 6: N ∼ 24 000; pH 8: N ∼ 2800, where N is the number of single‐molecule events recorded in 1200 s, except for pH 8 which was recorded for 600 s). Most heparin events have a maximum deviation between 300 and 1000 pA, with a median maximum deviation in the range of 600–750 pA for all pH values except for pH 4, which has a maximum deviation of 870 pA. Figure S2 shows the histograms of maximum deviation distribution at this positive voltage for all four pHs. Under negative applied voltage (−200 mV), EP opposes translocation of negatively charged molecules, and consistent with this, we observe few translocations at all pH values (pH 2: N ∼ 1200; pH 4: N ∼ 600; pH 6: N ∼ 700; pH 8: N ∼ 240). The events we observed at negative bias are most likely results of simple diffusion. The flux of analytes due to free diffusion is given by the Smoluchowski equation [39]: J=2πcDrp. For our conditions, this evaluates to 1–10 molecules/s, and our experimental event rate at the negative applied voltage does not exceed this theoretical estimation (see also Figure S3 for current traces). Here, concentration c = 500 nM, the radius of the pore, rp, is 2.5 nm, and the diffusion constant of heparin is taken as 1.2×10−12 m2/s [40].

To relate the observed blockage amplitudes to molecular shape to better understand the conformation in which these heparin molecules pass through the nanopore, we used an analytical model of the pore conductance that accurately accounts for the contribution of the pore access resistance and available as a webapp (https://www.tcossalab.net/signalGenerator) that can calculate expected nanopore signals from different molecular geometries [24]. In a nutshell, this signal generator uses an analytical framework to predict the measured signal caused by a particular analyte shape passing through a pore of a given geometry. The model can accurately predict the molecular signatures as a function of the distance of the pore by correctly modelling the region outside the pore, which is reminiscent of the blockage signal versus time recorded experimentally (assuming a constant velocity). The details of the model used are the subject of a previous publication. Here, we model heparin molecules with different conformations as cylinders of different radius and length. The pore is modelled as a cylindrical opening with a radius of 3 nm and a thickness of 12 nm. A dwell time of 10 µs was used for the simulation. In Figure 2a, the compact molecule (stretched blob) is estimated as having a radius of 2.9 nm and a length of 5 nm, and in Figure 2b, the snake‐like shape (series of smaller blobs) is estimated as having a radius of 1.5 nm and a length of 10 nm. In Figure 2c, the fully elongated linear chain has a radius of 1 nm and a length of 19 nm, and the linear heparin chain in Figure 2d has a radius of 0.5 nm and a length of 38 nm.

(a) Illustration of a heparin molecule passing through a solid‐state nanopore as a stretched blob, producing a deep signal that deviates from the baseline by ∼8 nA, as calculated by our conductance model. (b) Illustration of a heparin molecule passing through a solid‐state nanopore as a series of smaller blobs, producing a signal that fits the observed signatures of some of the deeper heparin events. This is modelled as a cylinder with a diameter of three disaccharide units in our signal generator tool. (c) Illustration of a heparin molecule passing through a solid‐state nanopore as a folded chain, producing a shallow signal that fits the profile of most heparin translocation events. (d) Illustration of a heparin molecule passing through a solid‐state nanopore as a fully elongated linear chain, producing a signal that would be too shallow to explain the experimental results. Note that the y‐axis scale for the plot in (a) differs from that in (b), (c) and (d).

The heparin sample used here had an average molecular mass of 28 kDa with a polydispersity index of 1.64, as determined by high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (see Section 2.3, Figures S4 and S5). Heparin is polydisperse polymers, and a sample molecular weight can range between 5000 and 50 000 Da [41]. It is composed of iduronic acid and glucosamine in their pyranose form [42]. The disaccharide unit has a molecular mass of 0.37 kDa and thus consists of approximately 75 units considering an average heparin mass of 28 kDa. A previous study has found that one pyranose ring in its relaxed state has a diameter of 0.45–0.54 nm, depending on the chain conformations between 4C1 and 1C4 [43]. Therefore, we can approximate heparin as a flexible cylindrical chain with an equivalent diameter of ∼1 nm and an end‐to‐end distance of ∼38 nm. Assuming our polymer executes a 3D random walk with a persistence length of 2.1 nm [44], the radius of gyration of the heparin coil is expected to be 3.9 nm [45], but previous measurements have also found them to be around 3.2–3.5 nm [44]. We therefore do not expect heparin to translocate as a densely packed blob through a pore with a radius of ∼2.5 nm (diameter ∼5 nm) used here.

Similarly, starting from the opposite extreme of possible conformations, heparin can be modelled as a stretched linear chain. If we simulate translocation of a fully extended molecule, the predicted signal is much shallower than our experimental observations, suggesting that molecules are not fully extended when translocating through ∼5 nm pores either, as our deeper observed blockages imply the presence of more than a single molecular chain in the pore at any given time. Figure 2 shows this situation schematically.

Although short, charged polymers may adopt a rod‐like conformation due to electrostatic repulsions, counterion condensation could explain the folded chain conformation we observed, as cations act like shields to sulphate groups in highly concentrated salts. Extreme bending of molecules during solid‐state nanopore translocation is well established. As previous studies show, ssDNA and dsDNA with much longer persistence lengths than heparin translocate through nanopores in folded configurations [32, 34, 46]. We conclude that heparin molecules are more likely to pass through the nanopore as a folded chain or as a series of smaller blobs, as depicted in Figure 2b,c, but the high degree of variation observed in the signals is consistent with a high degree of flexibility [34] and the spread in molecular weight. Previous work suggests that, for charged molecules, some elongation is expected on approach to the pore [32, 47], though we note that numerous molecular configurations can give rise to the same maximum deviation. A matching value between the simulated blockage for a particular conformation and experimentally observed blockages does not necessarily mean that the signal was generated by a molecule translocating in that conformation. Nonetheless, assuming a partially linearized translocation enforced by the confinement of the nanopore, we found that a cylindrical molecule with a diameter of 2 nm generates a signal that reasonably approximates a typical experimentally observed heparin translocation event, with a predicted maximum deviation of 500 pA.

Heparin molecules can also take the form of a smaller blob, as Figure 2b shows schematically. We modelled this conformation as a long cylinder with a diameter of three disaccharide units, generating a signal that is approximately 1600 pA. Such signals fit the profile of the deeper events we observed. This suggests, unsurprisingly, that heparin does not translocate through the nanopore as a fully coiled blob as it is found in the bulk, but rather as a partially folded chain or one with a series of smaller blobs corresponding to three disaccharide units, as schematically shown in Figure 2b,c.

Heparin Translocation Kinetics and Event Rates

As noted previously, heparin translocations show different event detection rates at different pH values under a positive 200 mV applied voltage. The motion of charged molecules inside a nanopore is governed by a balance of EP and electroosmosis. The direction and strength of electrophoretic and electro‐osmotic forces depend on the applied voltage, the charge of the molecules and the surface charge of the nanopore. Varying the pH was intended to change the charge state of the nanopore walls to affect the magnitude of the EO force and the net charge of heparin to affect the magnitude of the EP force, whereas changing the polarity of the applied voltage was aimed at exploring which of the EO and EP forces dominate the kinetics of molecular transport. On the basis of the results presented in Figure 1 (and Figure S3), the translocation of heparin appears to be dominated by EP. This is supported by theoretical estimates of the EO and EP velocities for a heparin blob passing through a charged channel (see Supporting Information Section S6). Similarly to DNA, for which the phosphate backbone is almost always deprotonated at all pHs, heparin is strongly acidic, and the molecule acquires a negative charge as the sulphate group (SO3−) on the monomers are deprotonated [48]. Studies have found that heparin begins to be protonated below pH 3 and should have a reduced net negative charge at pH 2 [49, 50]. On the other hand, heparin should stay consistently negatively charged between pH 4 and 8 [48, 49, 50, 51]. This implies a steady level of electrophoretic force towards the trans side throughout the pH 4, 6 and 8 values but a decrease at pH 2.

It is possible to quantify the charge of a nanopore at different pH values by measuring its zeta potential [19]. In a previous study, we measured the zeta potential of SiN solid‐state nanopores as a function of pH using the streaming current method [37]. It was found that, like other studies [52], the isoelectric point is 4.4±1. This implies that above pH 5, the surface has a negative zeta potential and a negative surface charge, resulting in an EO force opposing the EP force acting on the negatively charged heparin. This opposing force increases in strength with increasing pH as the pore walls become more negatively charged, whereas at pH 4, the electroosmosis is very weak, and below pH 4 would reinforce EP. We illustrate these scenarios in Figure 3.

(a) 3‐s traces of heparin translocations at pH 2, electroosmosis (EOF) and electrophoresis (EP) point in the same direction, although electrophoresis is weaker than at higher pHs because heparin molecules lose negative charge in extremely acidic environments. (b) 3‐s traces of heparin translocations at pH 4, electroosmosis and electrophoresis point in the same direction. Although heparin is fully negatively charged, the net driving force is at maximum. (c) 3‐s traces of heparin translocations at pH 6, electroosmosis is now opposing electrophoresis due to the change in the sign of the nanopore surface charge, causing a decrease in net force. (d) 3‐s traces of heparin translocations at pH 8, electroosmosis increases even more with a more negatively charged pore wall, further weakening the net force. All data at 4 M KCl in the same 5.4 nm pore as in Figure 1.

At pH 8 (Figure 3d), the EO force opposes EP transport, and we observe few events. As we decrease the pH to pH 6, zeta potential is reduced, lowering the magnitude of the EO force opposing the electrophoretic‐driven passage of heparin, so that the event rate increases (Figure 3c). At pH 4, the surface charge of the nanopore is almost neutral (very weakly positive), and the EO force now almost vanishes or points towards the EP force, whereas the charge of heparin remains negatively charged and unchanged, resulting in maximal event rate (Figure 3b). At pH 2, although the EO force is stronger, heparin loses some of its negative charge [48, 49, 50, 51], resulting in a weaker EP pull and thus a lower net force acting on the molecule, illustrated by the reduced observed event rates (Figure 3a). We calculated the event detection rates at each pH using the inter‐event time distribution [53] during 1200 s recordings of the ionic current (see Figure 4a).

(b) Normalized dwell time histograms of heparin translocations on a log scale, showing the tail of otherwise under‐resolved distributions. Both (a) and (b) showcase the same dataset found in Figure 3a, with the same low‐pass filter at 200 kHz for analysis. The time distribution of negative polarity is included in Figure S7.

Figure 4b displays the normalized histograms of log‐scaled event dwell times at different pHs. Most events translocate faster than the ability of our hardware to resolve them, as almost all events are faster than 100 µs, and the median dwell time observed is <10 µs in all conditions. Although our events were longer than twice the rise time, the internal event structure that could report on molecular differences at these pHs cannot be resolved. At this timescale, the signal starts to be attenuated by our low‐pass filter. Even with the full 1 MHz bandwidth of our measurement amplifier, these histograms only capture the tails of the full distribution of the heparin translocating in a 5.4 nm pore in 4 M KCl. However, there is limited ability to increase the 200 kHz cut‐off frequency, as heparin events are inherently shallow, and this filter is necessary to reduce the noise sufficiently for analysis. To capture more of the distribution and better understand translocation kinetics, either a significant reduction of noise must be achieved (e.g., through chip capacitance reduction [54]) or methods to slow down the heparin passage (like binding the target molecules to another larger molecule to achieve a deeper blockage and add more drag [12]).

At present, we can draw no quantitative conclusions about the dwell times, given that we are only able to sample the tail of the distribution. The analysis based on the blockage depth is valid for the events observed but may not be effective across the entire population, as some events are missed. Although we found that heparin translocated too fast to fully resolve, we note that several groups have suggested methods by which to slow down molecular translocations. These include various salt conditions [55, 56, 57], narrower pore geometry [46, 58, 59], interfacing the pore with gel media [61, 62] and modification of the molecule or pore surface [60, 62, 63], but the slowdown factors observed, ranging from 2 to 10×, are unlikely to be sufficient to fully resolve the entire distributions in this case. Use of reduced voltage to reduce speed during passage results in capture rates too low to be practical in most experimental conditions. This suggests that researchers conducting experiments with heparin should be cautious about drawing conclusions relating to the details of translocation events, as even state‐of‐the‐art electronics are inadequate to fully resolve these distributions without additional work to slow down molecular passage.

We performed a similar pH sweep in 3.6 M LiCl solutions to study the effect of the counterions on the kinetics of passage, and the overall trend is comparable to 4 M KCl on two additional ∼5 nm pores. Key characteristics like dwell time and blockage depth have no distinct differences between the two salts. We include all related analyses, including maximum deviation versus log‐scaled dwell time scatterplots and log‐scaled dwell time histograms in Figures S8 and S9. A voltage sweep in 1 M KCl pH 8, with a 7.5 nm pore, is also included in Figure S10.

Another factor that affects the electro‐osmotic flow response is the concentration of the salt solution that changes the zeta potential of the nanopore surface. In our previous study [37], we compared the zeta potentials of the nanopore surface in KCl solutions at pH 8 in different concentrations and confirmed that the zeta potentials increase as concentrations decrease. Because heparin translocation is primarily governed by EP, performing experiments at lower salt concentrations presents distinct challenges. Although the anticipated increase in EOF opposes EP motion at pH > 4, it appears insufficient to achieve the > 10‐fold deceleration required to fully resolve the distribution of passage times. Conversely, at pH ≤ 4, an increase in EOF would be counterproductive, as it would act synergistically with EP to accelerate translocation times. A lower salt concentration also means lower solution conductivity, which is directly proportional to our signal amplitude. As heparin already produces fast and shallow signals, maintaining a sufficient SNR for detection at low concentrations may be challenging.

OSCS and Heparin Contamination

OSCS is a type of polysaccharide that contaminated heparin to deadly effect in 2008 [64]. OSCS has a higher degree of sulphation compared to its counterpart, which is the naturally occurring chondroitin sulphate [64]. Although heparin and OSCS share similar chemical structures, OSCS‐contaminated heparin triggers severe allergic reactions and hypertension in the human body and could potentially lead to death [65]. We, therefore, sought to study the translocation characteristics of OSCS with our nanopore system, as has previously been done [7].

The OSCS sample tested here has an average molecular weight of 18 kDa with a polydispersity index of 1.29 (see Figures S4 and S5). Figure 5a shows heat maps of maximum deviation against log‐scaled dwell time for OSCS translocations. Heparin and OSCS both exhibit similarly shallow blockage depths and translocate extremely fast, with comparable event rate responses to pH (see Figure S11). To assess whether these two molecules could be differentiated, we conducted an experiment to pass heparin, OSCS and a heparin solution with a 30% mixture of OSCS through the same 5.1 nm pore. Figure 5b shows a heat map comparison between these three scenarios at pH 4, with maximum deviation against log‐scaled dwell time.

(a) Heat maps of scatter plots of OSCS (oversulphated chondroitin sulphate) translocations in 4 M KCl showing maximum current deviation versus dwell time in log scale at different pH values. All experiments at positive voltage were done in the same 5.6 ± 0.4 nm pore, whereas all experiments at negative voltage were done in another 5.2 ± 0.4 nm pore. (b) Heat maps of scatter plots comparison between heparin, OSCS and a 30% mixture (1:3 OSCS volume in heparin) at pH 4 at +200 mV in a 5.1 ± 0.4 nm pore. All figures show events over the course of 1800 s of recording.

We observed no distinctive features in the scatter plot to suggest that we could distinguish these two molecules despite the high bandwidth of our recording relative to previous attempts. Unlike a previous study [7], we did not see significant differences between OSCS and heparin passage signatures; however, as already discussed, a majority of events seem to be missed due to bandwidth limitations, and differences in behaviour may be present but unmeasurable at this bandwidth (see Figure S12). Although improved time resolution could reveal more about molecular conformations, allowing them to be differentiated, higher bandwidth, reduced passage speed and improved SNR would be needed than is possible with our current hardware. We also note that our experiments used a different source of heparin with a larger molecular weight (28 000 vs. 16 000 Da) as well as polydispersity (1.6 vs. ≈1.3) (see Figures S4 and S5).

Concluding Remarks

We have shown that most heparin molecular translocations through solid‐state nanopores are fast relative to the ability of state‐of‐the‐art electronics to resolve them. We have also demonstrated and established through force analysis that transport of these molecules is highly dependent on the interplay between molecular charge, nanopore surface charge, pH and polarity of the applied voltage. A clear conclusion from this work is that to differentiate these molecules reliably with a solid‐state nanopore will require a combination of slower translocation, higher bandwidth and improved SNR.

Other groups have developed several techniques that could be explored to address the passage speed challenge. One approach would be to attach a tag, a molecule that can bind to heparin [66], to increase the blockage depth. Lectins are excellent binding materials for polysaccharides [67] and could serve as a tag to slow heparin down during translocation. Another strategy could be to include a chemically reactive site inside the pore [68] that binds to translocating molecules transiently, creating a longer interaction with the pore. Boronic acid is one such coating which reversibly binds to hydroxyl moieties of carbohydrates [69, 70]
