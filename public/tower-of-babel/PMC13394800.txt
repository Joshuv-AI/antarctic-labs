KPNA2 Drives Immunosuppression in Ovarian Cancer via CCL2/CCR2‐Dependent MDSC Recruitment
Authors: Qingli Li, Jitong Zhao, Lu Yang, Lin Li, Ce Bian, Tao Yi, Huiqiong Huang, Gang Zheng, Xiaojuan Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTImmunosuppression in ovarian cancer is primarily driven by myeloid‐derived suppressor cells (MDSCs). This study identifies karyopherin α2 (KPNA2) as a novel regulator of this immunosuppressive process through the NF‐κB/CCL2/CCR2 axis. Elevated KPNA2 expression correlates with increased intratumoral MDSC accumulation and impaired CD8+ T‐cell function. Mechanistically, KPNA2 directly binds to and facilitates nuclear translocation of NF‐κB/p65, thereby driving CCL2 transcription, while also exhibiting an auxiliary, p65‐independent function in CCL2 induction. Secreted CCL2 recruits MDSCs in a CCR2‐dependent manner, establishing a potent immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Therapeutic targeting via MDSC depletion, pharmacological CCR2 blockade, or direct KPNA2 knockdown effectively inhibited metastasis, enhanced CD8+ T‐cell infiltration, and suppressed tumor growth. Importantly, CCR2 inhibition synergized with anti‐PD‐L1 therapy, revealing a promising combination immunotherapeutic strategy for KPNA2‐high ovarian cancer. These findings establish the KPNA2‐governed NF‐κB/CCL2/CCR2 pathway as a central mechanism of immune evasion and a viable target for combination immunotherapy in ovarian cancer.

Introduction

Ovarian cancer (OC) remains one of the most lethal gynecologic malignancies worldwide [1]. Despite advances with platinum‐taxane chemotherapy, PARP inhibitors (PARPi), and anti‐angiogenic agents, durable survival benefits remain limited [2]. Although immunotherapy has emerged as a promising strategy for multiple cancers, the unsatisfactory overall response to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and the great variation in response among individuals highlight the necessity for improvement of ICI efficacy and development of predictive biomarkers in ovarian cancer [3].

Given the complex ecosystem of dynamic interactions among tumor growth, metastatic dissemination, and host antitumor responses, elucidating immune‐evasion mechanisms within the tumor microenvironment (TME) is critical for enhancing immunotherapy efficacy [4]. In OC, myeloid‐derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) have emerged as predominant immunosuppressive mediators that drive tumor progression through enhancing neoplastic cell survival and proliferation, facilitating metastatic dissemination, and suppressing antitumor immunity [5]. MDSCs exert their suppressive functions through a repertoire of effector molecules—including chemokines, cytokines, cell‐surface receptors, and enzymes, which collectively suppress antitumor immune responses [6]. Therefore, therapeutic strategies targeting vital molecules regulating the OC‐MDSCs interaction may represent a novel T cell–independent approach to synergize with ICIs; although optimal combinations and target populations require further definition.

Nuclear‐cytoplasmic transport is a critical regulator of cellular function, and its dysregulation contributes to carcinogenesis through altering the subcellular localization of oncogenic factors [7]. Karyopherin (KPN) nuclear transport receptors are recognized as key modulators of cancer progression, governing the compartmentalization of numerous tumor‐associated proteins [8]. As a pivotal member of the KPN family, KPNA2 functions as both a diagnostic biomarker and therapeutic target by facilitating the nuclear translocation of oncogenic cargo proteins (e.g., transcription factors and cell cycle regulators) [9]. The pro‐tumorigenic role of KPNA2 is conserved across malignancies. For instance, in breast cancer, KPNA2 promotes metastasis by enhancing the nuclear import of STAT1/NF‐κB [10]; in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), it accelerates tumorigenesis by mediating PLAG1 nuclear translocation [11]. Given this established role as an independent prognostic factor across cancers [12, 13, 14], the functional significance of KPNA2 in ovarian cancer remains nascent. Although limited evidence links KPNA2 overexpression to advanced stage and poor survival [15, 16], the specific mechanism through which KPNA2 drives pathogenesis, particularly its potential to orchestrate an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and promote metastasis in ovarian cancer, remain insufficiently defined.

In the current study, we demonstrate that elevated KPNA2 expression in ovarian cancer cells orchestrates an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment by recruiting and activating MDSCs, thereby impairing CD8+ T‐cell‐mediated antitumor immunity. Mechanistic studies further revealed that targeting this axis with CCR2 inhibitors or KPNA2 depletion preclinically validates the feasibility of sensitizing OC to immune‐checkpoint blockade. Collectively, these findings identify the KPNA2‐CCL2/CCR2 axis as a therapeutically tractable vulnerability and support subgroup‐specific combination strategies for patients with KPNA2‐high tumors.

Material and Methods

Single‐Cell RNA Sequencing (scRNA‐Seq) Data Acquisition and Analysis

Single‐cell RNA sequencing data from 12 ovarian cancer samples (GSE184880) were analyzed using Seurat (v5.3.0). Cells were excluded if the mitochondrial UMIs exceeded 25%, the hemoglobin gene UMIs exceeded 1%, or if fewer than 500 genes were detected. Data were normalized and subjected to principal component analysis (PCA), and batch effects were corrected using Harmony (v1.2.3). Cell clustering and t‐SNE visualization were conducted using Seurat functions, and cell types were annotated with canonical marker genes. T cells were identified by CD3 gene expression (CD3D, CD3E, CD3G) for sub‐clustering into subsets. Samples were divided into KPNA2‐high and KPNA2‐low groups based on average KPNA2 expression within the malignant epithelial cell cluster as identified by canonical epithelial markers. Differences in cellular composition were shown with t‐SNE plots and stacked bar charts.

Public Database Analysis

RNA‐sequencing data for the OC cohort were retrieved from the TCGA database website and normalized using the limma package in the R project (v4.2.1). Comprehensive characterization of tumor immune cell infiltration profiles was performed through integrated computational deconvolution: TIMER 2.0 quantified core immune subsets, xCell assessed stromal and immune compartment scoring, and CIBERSORT enabled leukocyte subset resolution.

Cell Culture

Murine ID8 and human ovarian cancer cells SKOV‐3 (RRID: CVCL_0532), A2780 (RRID: CVCL_0134), OVCAR‐3 (RRID: CVCL_0465), Hey A8 (RRID: CVCL_8878), and CAOV‐3 (RRID: CVCL_0201) were maintained in DMEM or RPMI 1640, respectively, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin, and 2 mM L‐glutamine (human‐derived cells additional supplements: nonessential amino acids, HEPES, pyruvate, and 2‐Mercaptoethanol). Cell line identities were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling (for human lines). All the ovarian cancer cell lines were purchased from Jennio Biotech. Human ovarian epithelial cells (ISOE‐80 (RRID: CVCL_5546), Jennio) were cultured. All cells underwent mycoplasma testing and karyotype validation.

Patients and Specimens

OC specimens were obtained from patients diagnosed with ovarian cancer via histopathological examination at the West China Second University Hospital of Sichuan University (WCSUH‐SCU) in 2024. Two independent cohorts were established for this study: an immunohistochemical (IHC) cohort comprising 30 ovarian carcinoma specimens for analyses of KPNA2, CD8, and CD33 expression and correlation studies, and a validation cohort consisting of 15 fresh benign ovarian tissues (from cystectomy patients without malignancy) and 15 freshly resected malignant OC tissues collected for comparative qRT‐PCR analysis. All procedures were approved by the WCSUH‐SCU Institutional Research Ethics Committee; written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to sample collection. Animal and Treatments

Female C57BL/6 mice, aged 6–8 weeks, were anesthetized with isoflurane, and a single dorsal incision was made to access the ovary. ID8 cells (1.0 × 106) were then injected directly into the right ovarian bursa to establish orthotopic tumors. The mice were randomized into control and different treatment groups using a random number generator (n = 10 mice per group). MDSCs were depleted with anti‐Gr‐1 antibody (2 mg/kg, intraperitoneal injection, twice weekly) (#BE0075; Bio X Cell, West Lebanon, NH, USA). The CCR2 antagonist CCX872 (supplied by ChemoCentryx, Mountain View, CA) was dissolved in 1% hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC) and administered by oral gavage at a dose of 90 mg/kg twice daily to evaluate its antitumor effect. For immunotherapeutic intervention, anti‐PD‐L1 antibody (#BE0101; Bio X Cell, West Lebanon, NH, USA) was administered via intraperitoneal injection at a dose of 200 μg every 3 days. For the therapeutic efficacy study, all treatments were continued until 49 days postinoculation. Tumor growth was monitored through bioluminescence imaging using the IVIS Lumina K Series III system (PerkinElmer), with data analyzed using Living Image software. For the survival analysis, a separate cohort of mice was monitored until the predefined ethical endpoint at 73 days postinoculation, at which point all remaining mice were euthanized to prevent undue suffering. In all cases, euthanasia was performed by carbon dioxide followed by cervical dislocation. No animals were excluded from the analyses. Animal studies were performed in compliance with the Ethics Committee of WCSUH‐SCU and with the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee guidelines of the same institution (Approval #2025042) and the ARRIVE guidelines, incorporating daily postoperative monitoring to preclude complications and standardized operative protocols to minimize stress duration during procedures. Ascites volume and metastatic nodule counts were recorded. Tumor dimensions were measured with digital calipers, and excised tumors were subjected to analysis. A per‐experimental analysis was conducted to determine the sample size and experimental timeframe in the primary outcome (mean tumor volume).

Flow Cytometry Analysis

Orthotopic tumor tissues were harvested, minced, and enzymatically digested at 37°C for 2 h in DMEM supplemented with 1 mg/mL collagenase type I (Sigma‐Aldrich) and 10 mM CaCl2. The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 70‐μm strainer, centrifuged, and treated with red blood cell lysis buffer (eBioscience) to obtain a single‐cell suspension. Cells were then washed and resuspended in flow cytometry staining buffer (PBS with 0.5% BSA and 2 mM EDTA) for surface marker staining. After incubation with fluorescence‐conjugated antibodies against indicated markers for 30 min at 4°C in the dark, cells were washed twice and analyzed on a MoFlo XDP cytometer (Beckman Coulter). Data were analyzed using FlowJo v10.6.1. The primary antibodies used are listed in Table 2.

IHC Staining

Immunohistochemical staining was performed on formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded tissue sections. After endogenous peroxidase quenching and antigen retrieval, sections were blocked and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies (detailed in Table 2). Staining was developed using an HRP‐based detection system (Gene Tech). KPNA2 expression was quantified using the H‐score (the product of the percentage of positive cells and staining intensity). Based on the H‐score, samples were categorized into negative, weak, moderate, and strong expression groups and further stratified into low‐ and high‐expression subgroups for correlation analysis with CCL2. For CD8 and CD33, a staining index was calculated as the product of positive cell count and intensity score within high‐power fields, with four random fields averaged per sample.

Immunofluorescence Staining

Cells were fixed in 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde followed by permeabilization with 0.2% Triton X‐100. Concurrently, paraffin‐embedded tissue sections were dewaxed, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval. Sections were cut at 5 μm and fixed in prechilled methanol. Both sections and fixed cells were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Sangon Biotech) and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies (Table 2). After three washes in phosphate‐buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20, samples were incubated with fluorophore‐conjugated secondary antibodies. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and coverslips were mounted using antifade mounting medium.

RNA Extraction and Quantitative RT–PCR

Total RNA isolation from cells and tumor specimens was performed with TRIzol reagent (Sigma, USA), followed by reverse transcription using the PrimeScript RT‐PCR kit (Takara, USA) in accordance with the manufacturers' protocols. Quantitative PCR analysis was conducted on an Applied Biosystems 7900 Real‐Time PCR system with SYBR Premix Ex Taq (Takara, USA). Gene expression levels were quantified via the 2−∆∆Ct method, normalized to endogenous GAPDH mRNA. All gene‐specific primer sequences are detailed in Table 3.

Western Blotting Analysis

Total proteins were extracted from cells and tissues using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotech, Shanghai, China). The protein samples were separated by SDS‐PAGE and electrophoretically transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. After blocking with 5% BSA prepared in TBST for 2 h at room temperature, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with specific primary antibodies, followed by incubation with corresponding horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐conjugated secondary antibodies at 37°C for 2 h. Protein signals were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (Beyotime Biotech) and captured by exposure to X‐ray film (Kodak, Rochester, NY, USA). Prior to exposure, the positions of the pre‐stained protein marker (20350, YEASEN) were traced onto the film with a pencil. The X‐ray film was then rinsed with water, dried at 50°C, and aligned with the PVDF membrane using the reference marks. Molecular weight markers were annotated directly onto the X‐ray film. The immunoblot images were digitally archived by scanning with an Epson scanner (Epson, Tokyo, Japan). GAPDH detection on parallel lanes served as the loading control. The primary antibodies used are listed in Table 2.

MDSCs Chemotaxis Assays

A 5‐μm pore Transwell chamber (Corning, USA) was used for migration assays. 2 × 104 purified MDSCs from healthy donor PBMCs were seeded in the upper chamber, and the lower chamber contained medium with indicated treatments. Cell culture supernatants and recombinant CCL2 were pretreated with 5 μg/mL anti‐CCL2 neutralizing antibody for 30 min at 37°C to inhibit CCL2‐mediated chemotactic effects before adding to MDSCs. After 24 h incubation, non‐migrated cells on the upper membrane surface were removed by gentle swabbing with moistened cotton tips. Migrated cells on the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and quantified by counting five random fields per membrane across three independent inserts.

T‐Cell Suppression Assays

Peripheral blood samples were collected from histopathologically confirmed OC patients at WCSUH‐SCU in 2024. Following approval by the WCSUH‐SCU Clinical Research Ethics Committee and written informed consent, lymphocytes were isolated using Lymphocyte Separation Medium (MP Biomedicals). A CFSE‐dilution assay was used to evaluate the suppressive capacity of MDSCs on T‐cell proliferation. Human CD3+ T cells were isolated from healthy donor PBMCs using a Pan T Cell Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec), labeled with 2.5 μM CFSE, and cocultured with MDSCs at specified ratios in 96‐well U‐bottom plates. T cells were stimulated with soluble anti‐CD3 (1 μg/mL) and anti‐CD28 (1 μg/mL) antibodies. After 72–96 h, cells were harvested, stained for viability and CD3, and analyzed by flow cytometry. T‐cell proliferation was determined by CFSE dilution. The suppression rate was calculated as follows: [1 − (% proliferation in coculture/% proliferation in stimulated control alone)] × 100%. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Statistical Analysis

The data were analyzed using SPSS (v21.0, IBM), GraphPad Prism (v8.3, USA), or R (v4.1.0). Cumulative survival was evaluated by the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using log‐rank tests. Correlations between KPNA2 expression and immune signatures were evaluated by linear regression. For data following a normal distribution, group differences were assessed with Student's t‐test or one‐way ANOVA. All statistical tests were two‐sided, and a p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All experiments were repeated three or more independent biological replicates, and data are presented as mean ± SD.

Results

KPNA2 Is a Crucial Pro‐Oncogenic Protein Associated With High MDSC Infiltration and Low T‐Cell Infiltration in the OC Microenvironment

To delineate KPNA2's role in orchestrating the immunosuppressive landscape of OC, we performed an integrative analysis of single‐cell RNA‐seq data from 12 tumor specimens. Using defined marker panels, we characterized the immune cell composition. Unsupervised clustering of T‐cell transcriptional profiles identified five initial clusters, which were merged into four functionally distinct populations (Figure 1A) and spatially visualized via t‐SNE (Figure 1B). Quantitative analysis revealed a significant depletion of CD8+ effector T cells (Teff) and expansion of regulatory T cells (Treg) in KPNA2‐high tumors (Figure 1C), coupled with elevated PD‐1 expression (Figure 1D), indicating profound T cell dysfunction. For the non‐T cell compartment, CD3 expression served as the T cell marker to distinguish non‐T cell lineages. Application of the Louvain algorithm yielded 16 initial clusters, which were consolidated into eight subpopulations (Figure 1E). MDSCs were significantly enriched in KPNA2‐high tumors, whereas B and T lymphocytes were more abundant in KPNA2‐low tumors (Figure 1F,G). Supporting these findings, pan‐cancer analysis confirmed a robust negative correlation between KPNA2 expression and CD8+ T cell infiltration in OC, and a positive association with MDSC accumulation (Figure S1A). Systematic immune deconvolution further identified consistent correlations between KPNA2 expression and 16 immune subsets. Although the correlation coefficients were modest (ranging from −0.2 to −0.15), their uniform significance across multiple platforms underscores KPNA2's conserved regulatory role in OC immunity (Figure S1B). Transcriptional upregulation of KPNA2 was validated in independent clinical cohorts and the TCGA database (Figure S1C). Collectively, these integrated analyses establish KPNA2 as a critical immunoregulator that orchestrates MDSC expansion and CD8+ T‐cell exclusion within the OC microenvironment.

KPNA2 Upregulates CCL2 Expression in OC

To further delineate how KPNA2 modulates the immune microenvironment, IHC and multiplex immunofluorescence (IF) were performed on clinical specimens. Analysis confirmed that high KPNA2 expression correlated with decreased CD8+ T cell infiltration and increased CD33+ myeloid cells accumulation (Figure 2A,B). This correlation prompted an examination of KPNA2's functional role. We found that KPNA2 was significantly upregulated in OC cell lines compared to normal ovarian surface epithelial cells (Figure 2C,D), and its knockdown inhibited OC cell proliferation (Figure S2A,B). Given the established role of chemokines in immune cell recruitment, we hypothesized that KPNA2 might regulate MDSC‐associated chemokines. A screen of eight candidate chemokines was conducted, revealing that KPNA2 knockdown most notably downregulated CCL2, along with CXCL1, CXCL2, and CXCL5 (Figure 2E). Consequently, CCL2 was selected for further validation. Subsequent qPCR and ELISA analyses confirmed KPNA2 as a key transcriptional regulator of CCL2, as CCL2 levels were decreased upon KPNA2 knockdown and increased upon its overexpression (Figure 2F, Figure S2C). The clinical relevance of this regulatory axis was finally established through integrated analyses of the TCGA database and an in‐house patient cohort. These analyses demonstrated that CCL2 was significantly elevated in human OC tissues. Importantly, an eightfold higher level in KPNA2‐high versus KPNA2‐low counterparts (Figure S2D,E). Finally, orthotopic xenograft models recapitulated this regulatory relationship: KPNA2 knockdown reduced tumor CCL2 secretion by 62%, whereas KPNA2 overexpression led to a sixfold increase (Figure S2F). Collectively, these data from cellular, clinical, and in vivo models demonstrate that KPNA2 transcriptionally upregulates CCL2 in OC cells.

KPNA2 Promotes the Accumulation of MDSCs via the CCL2–CCR2 Axis

Based on the established role of the CCL2‐CCR2 axis in recruiting immunosuppressive myeloid cells [17], we investigated whether KPNA2‐driven CCL2 secretion mediates MDSC recruitment. Transwell migration assays using conditioned media showed that medium from KPNA2‐knockdown cells markedly impaired MDSC migration, resulting in a 58% reduction (p < 0.001). This effect was reversed by recombinant CCL2 supplementation, which restored migration by 92% (p < 0.01). Notably, the restorative effect of CCL2 was abrogated by either CCL2‐neutralizing antibodies or the CCR2 antagonist CCX872, functionally linking KPNA2 to CCR2‐dependent chemotaxis (Figure 3A–D). Patient‐derived MDSCs were confirmed to suppress autologous T cell proliferation, verifying their immunosuppressive capacity (Figure S3B). Building on this evidence, CCL2 was proposed as a critical driver for MDSC trafficking to ovarian cancer sites, where MDSCs execute their immunosuppressive activity. To delineate the in vivo role of CCL2 in KPNA2‐driven immune evasion, CCL2 was silenced in orthotopic models bearing KPNA2‐overexpressing tumors. CCL2 knockdown significantly suppressed intraperitoneal metastasis and prolonged median overall survival (OS) (Figure 3E–H). Concomitantly, CCL2 depletion decreased MDSC infiltration and increased tumor‐infiltrating CD8+ T cells within KPNA2‐overexpressing tumors (Figure 3I,J). Taken together, these findings implicate the CCL2‐CCR2 axis as a principal mechanism mediating KPNA2‐driven MDSC accumulation and immune suppression in ovarian cancer.

KPNA2 Induces Translocation of p65 Protein to Regulate CCL2 Transcription

Given the established role of NF‐κB in chemokine regulation [18, 19, 20], we investigated its p65 subunit as a potential mediator of KPNA2‐driven CCL2 transactivation. Co‐immunoprecipitation (CO‐IP) assays confirmed a direct endogenous interaction between KPNA2 and p65 (Figure 4A), and immunofluorescence demonstrated their spatial co‐localization of KPNA2 and p65 within the nucleus (Figure 4B,C). Consistent with KPNA2's function as a nuclear transport protein, subcellular fractionation and quantitative microscopy confirmed that KPNA2 augmented the nuclear translocation of p65 (Figure 4D,E). To functionally test the contribution of this enhanced nuclear translocation, we generated isogenic models with either complete p65 ablation or a nuclear localization–defective mutant (p65ΔNLS), and confirmed the import deficiency of p65ΔNLS (Figure 4F,G). In these models, KPNA2‐driven p65 nuclear accumulation significantly enhanced CCL2 expression. Re‐introduction of wild‐type p65 into knockout cells restored KPNA2‐mediated CCL2 upregulation, whereas expression of p65ΔNLS markedly attenuated it, confirming the necessity of p65 nuclear import. Notably, KPNA2 overexpression in p65‐knockout cells still induced modest but significant CCL2 upregulation, suggesting a potential auxiliary role for KPNA2 that may not rely exclusively on canonical p65 activity (Figure 4H). These findings collectively indicate that KPNA2 exerts a dual function: it facilitates the nuclear translocation of p65 and, upon nuclear entry, scaffolds p65 through complex formation to upregulate CCL2 transcription.

KPNA2 Cooperates With p65 in Regulating CCL2 Transcription

To delineate KPNA2's function beyond nuclear transport, we first assessed its direct effect on the CCL2 promoter. A luciferase reporter assay confirmed that KPNA2 overexpression robustly enhanced promoter activity (Figure 5A). To identify the underlying mechanism, chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays were performed using 10 primer pairs spanning the entire CCL2 promoter to map KPNA2‐binding regions (Figure 5B,C). The results indicated that two specific regions within the CCL2 promoter were occupied by KPNA2 (Figure 5D). At these sites, KPNA2 increased the enrichment of the active chromatin mark H3K27ac, suggesting a role in promoting an open chromatin state (Figure 5E). Given the observed nuclear co‐localization and functional interplay between KPNA2 and p65, we hypothesized that they cooperate at the promoter. ChIP assays confirmed that p65 bound to the identical promoter regions. Importantly, KPNA2 knockdown substantially impaired p65 occupancy at these sites (Figure 5F), indicating that KPNA2 facilitates p65 recruitment. To validate the functional necessity of the predicted NF‐κB/p65‐binding motifs within the CCL2 promoter, we generated site‐directed mutant luciferase reporter constructs. The two predicted p65‐binding motifs were located at approximately −1324 to −1331 bp and −276 to −285 bp upstream of the CCL2 transcription start site, respectively (Figure 5G). The reporter designated pGL3‐CCL2‐mut contained simultaneous mutations of both motifs. Luciferase assays revealed that mutation of either site abolished the transcriptional enhancement driven by either KPNA2 or p65 (Figure 5H), establishing these elements as critical for their co‐regulatory function. The dependency on NF‐κB signaling was further interrogated using the inhibitor Bay11‐7082. In KPNA2‐overexpressing cells, NF‐κB inhibition markedly reduced CCL2 mRNA and protein levels, accompanied by diminished promoter activity (Figure 5I–K). Finally, KPNA2 and p65 co‐transfection synergistically enhanced CCL2 promoter activity compared to p65 expression alone (Figure 5L). Collectively, these data establish that KPNA2, in conjunction with p65, co‐occupies specific sites on the CCL2 promoter to facilitate p65 recruitment and cooperatively enhance transcriptional activation. KPNA2 Promotes In Vivo Tumor Growth and Induces an Immunosuppressive TME

Immune cells infiltrating the TME are co‐opted by OC to facilitate metastatic dissemination and tumor progression [21]. To assess the functional impact of KPNA2 on tumor progression and the tumor microenvironment (TME) in vivo, a syngeneic orthotopic ID8 model was established. KPNA2‐overexpressing tumors formed palpable lesions within 6 weeks postinoculation, accompanied by hemorrhagic ascites and multifocal peritoneal metastases, whereas control cohorts exhibited attenuated tumor burden and prolonged survival (Figure 7A–D). Consistent with its regulatory role, intratumoral CCL2 levels were elevated in KPNA2‐high tumors (Figure S2F). We next characterized immune infiltration via flow cytometry and immunofluorescence. KPNA2 overexpression substantially altered the TME landscape, reducing CD3+, CD8+, and CD4+ T cells, while concurrently increasing CD11b+Gr‐1+ MDSCs (Figure 7E,F). This shift was corroborated by elevated expression of the immunosuppressive enzymes Arg1 and iNOS (Figure 7G,H), which metabolically suppress T‐cell function [22]. Immunohistochemistry further confirmed decreased CD8+ T cells and expanded MDSC infiltration in KPNA2‐overexpressing tumors (Figure 7I). These in vivo data demonstrate that KPNA2 drives tumor progression by fostering an immunosuppressive TME, characterized by CCL2 induction, MDSC accumulation, and T‐cell suppression.

Deletion of MDSCs Suppresses KPNA2‐Induced OC Metastasis

To determine whether KPNA2‐driven tumor progression depends on MDSCs, we employed an orthotopic allograft model with MDSC depletion. Anti‐Gr‐1‐mediated MDSC ablation significantly inhibited KPNA2‐induced peritoneal metastasis and extended overall survival. Flow cytometry and immunofluorescence analyses further confirmed effective reversal of the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (Figure S4A–F). To further dissect the underlying mechanism, we pharmacologically disrupted the CCL2–CCR2 axis using the CCR2 antagonist CCX872. Notably, CCX872 treatment significantly attenuated the tumor burden associated with KPNA2 overexpression (Figure S5A–D) and reversed KPNA2‐mediated immune remodeling, evidenced by reduced CD11b+Gr‐1+ MDSC infiltration, increased CD8+ T‐cell accumulation, and elevated CD8+/CD4+ T‐cell ratios in orthotopic tumors (Figure S5E,F). IHC validation corroborated reduced MDSCs and expanded CD8+ T‐cell infiltration in CCX872‐treated tumors (Figure S5G). In summary, these data indicate that KPNA2 drives OC progression predominantly through MDSC‐dependent immunosuppression, wherein the CCL2–CCR2 axis critically governs MDSC recruitment and functional activation.

The Combination of Anti‐PD‐L1 With CCR2 Antagonist CCX872 Dramatically Inhibits KPNA2‐Mediated OC Metastasis

Given MDSCs' capacity to suppress antitumor T‐cell immunity, we hypothesized that blocking the CCL2/CCR2 axis could potentiate anti‐PD‐L1 therapy. In orthotopic models of KPNA2‐high ovarian cancer, CCX872 or anti‐PD‐L1 monotherapy partially attenuated peritoneal metastasis and extended survival, whereas the combination exerted superior antitumor efficacy (Figure S6A–F). Concurrently, combined treatment significantly augmented CD8+ T‐cell infiltration and suppressed MDSC accumulation (Figure S6E,F), demonstrating that CCR2 blockade can remodel the KPNA2‐driven immunosuppressive microenvironment and thereby sensitize KPNA2‐high tumors to anti‐PD‐L1 therapy. These findings support the efficacy of this combination in a KPNA2‐activated immunosuppressive context, while further comparison with KPNA2‐low models is warranted to define the predictive specificity of KPNA2 expression.

Discussion

KPNA2 is frequently overexpressed in multiple malignancies and is associated with poor prognosis [23]. In epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC), high KPNA2 expression associates with aggressive clinicopathological features and serves as an independent predictor of adverse outcomes [24]. Although studies in gastric cancer suggested KPNA2's correlation with immunosuppressive cell infiltration, the role of KPNA2 in EOC tumor immunity remained unclear. To address this gap, we integrated single‐cell and bulk RNA‐seq datasets from ovarian cancer cohorts, revealing an inverse correlation between KPNA2 overexpression and antitumor immune signatures. Using syngeneic orthotopic models, we further demonstrated that KPNA2 accelerates tumor progression by driving CCL2‐dependent MDSC accumulation and suppressing CD8+ T‐cell activity. These findings elucidate a previously unrecognized immunomodulatory function of KPNA2 in ovarian cancer and position MDSC‐targeted strategies as a promising therapeutic avenue for KPNA2‐high EOC patients.

The CCL2–CCR2 axis is a well‐established driver of cancer progression, primarily through the recruitment of CCR2+ immunosuppressive cells, such as MDSCs, which inhibit CD8+ T‐cell function [25, 26]. We identified KPNA2 as a novel upstream regulator of this pathway in OC. KPNA2 overexpression increased MDSC infiltration and suppressed CD8+ T‐cell accumulation through dual mechanistic control of CCL2—facilitating p65 nuclear import and, once in the nucleus, guiding p65 recruitment to the CCL2 promoter. MDSC depletion or pharmacologic CCR2 antagonist (CCX872) disrupted the recruitment and infiltration of MDSC and increased CD8+ T‐cell accumulation. It is noteworthy that KPNA2 knockdown also modestly inhibited OC cell proliferation in vitro, suggesting that the reduced tumor burden in vivo may stem from both dampened intrinsic proliferation and impaired MDSC‐mediated immunosuppression. However, the specific rescue of immune parameters and tumor control upon targeting the CCL2/CCR2 axis underscores the dominant role of this immunosuppressive pathway. The combination of MDSC inhibition and immune checkpoint blockade therapy resulted in a modest tumor response in short‐term mouse experiments [26]. Consistent with this idea, CCR2 blockade synergized with anti‐PD‐L1 therapy, demonstrating combinatorial benefits. These findings indicate that KPNA2 overexpression in OC cells promotes the recruitment and accumulation of MDSC via the CCL2‐CCR2 axis and support therapeutic targeting of this pathway to counteract KPNA2‐driven OC metastasis.

NF‐κB is a widely reported transcription factor for cytokines and chemokines and is also a classic potential target in cancer therapy [27]. Investigating the upstream regulators that potentiate NF‐κB activity in ovarian cancer may uncover new therapeutic vulnerabilities. We demonstrate that KPNA2 critically enhances p65/NF‐κB transcriptional activity through a dual‐tiered regulatory mechanism: facilitating p65 nuclear translocation via canonical importin α/β‐mediated transport and guiding p65 recruitment to the CCL2 promoter via chromatin co‐occupancy. This paradigm aligns with prior experimental evidence: Duan et al. showed that KPNA2 silencing inhibits NF‐κB signaling in breast cancer [28], while Tao et al. demonstrated KPNA2‐p65 physical interaction required for p50/p65 nuclear translocation [29], collectively confirming the role of nuclear transporters in modulating this pathway. Building on this foundation, our integrated mechanistic interrogation provides direct evidence that KPNA2 physically binds p65, significantly enhances its nuclear accumulation, and co‐occupies the CCL2 promoter, thereby markedly increasing p65 binding occupancy. KPNA2 depletion via shRNA‐which disrupts the KPNA2‐p65 interaction‐significantly reduced p65 occupancy at the CCL2 promoter. Complementarily, dual‐luciferase reporter assays demonstrated that co‐expression of KPNA2 and p65 enhanced CCL2 promoter activity in wild‐type (WT) but not specific site mutated (Mut) constructs, confirming functional dependence on the p65‐binding element. Critically, this chromatin co‐recruitment represents a noncanonical function beyond nuclear transport, wherein KPNA2's C‐terminal domain anchors transcriptional complexes through engagement with DNA minor grooves (as suggested by structural analyses [30, 31]), spatially stabilizing its occupancy at target promoters. This structural priming enables subsequent recruitment of histone‐modifying enzymes (e.g., p300/CBP), thereby elevating H3K27ac deposition and establishing an open chromatin landscape permissive for transcriptional activation. Collectively, this KPNA2‐p65 “transport‐and‐transcribe” paradigm redefines importins as active transcriptional coordinators, providing a mechanistic basis for targeting KPNA2 in NF‐κB‐driven malignancies.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, we identified murine MDSCs using CD11b and Gr‐1, a classification approach constrained by ongoing debates regarding the precise definition of MDSCs as pathologically activated myeloid cells [32]. While the polymorphonuclear MDSC (PMN‐MDSC) concept is widely accepted, distinguishing PMN‐MDSCs from tumor‐associated neutrophils remains challenging, necessitating future technological and mechanistic advances to refine their classification and functional delineation. Second, the present study was mainly designed to elucidate the mechanistic role of KPNA2, and thus a systematic analysis of its clinical‐pathological correlations, including protein localization, FIGO stage, prognosis, and immune subtypes, remains an important future direction. Although our patient‐derived scRNA‐seq data support a link between high KPNA2 expression and an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, prospective validation in independent clinical cohorts is needed. Third, although our mechanistic and therapeutic data support the vulnerability of KPNA2‐high tumors to CCR2 inhibition combined with anti‐PD‐L1 therapy, we did not perform a formal side‐by‐side comparison of this combination in KPNA2‐high versus KPNA2‐low orthotopic models. Additionally, ovarian cancer exhibits profound molecular heterogeneity, with distinct subtypes showing differential dependencies on chemokine signaling pathways [33, 34]. Although our therapeutic strategy targeting the KPNA2‐CCL2 axis demonstrates efficacy in ID8‐derived models, its applicability across molecular subtypes (e.g., mesenchymal vs. immunoreactive) requires validation in clinically annotated cohorts. These findings establish a mechanistic foundation for targeting KPNA2‐high OC through combined CCR2 inhibition and PD‐L1 blockade; however, further validation in patient‐derived xenografts, multi‐center clinical cohorts, and phase I/II trials will be essential to define its translational potential.

In summary, our data suggest that upregulated KPNA2 is general in the majority of OC patients and is an inducer of MDSC recruitment. KPNA2 promotes MDSC accumulation and inhibits CD8+ T‐cell infiltration via NF‐κB/p65‐induced CCL2 expression, which results in immunosuppression and promotes tumor progression. Neutralizing or suppressing MDSC infiltration abolished KPNA2‐mediated OC growth and metastasis. Moreover, combining CCR2 blockade with anti‐PD‐L1 therapy synergistically reversed immunosuppression and elicited potent antitumor responses in high‐KPNA2 ovarian cancer models, highlighting a promising therapeutic strategy.
