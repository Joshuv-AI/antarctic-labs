Exploring chronic pain and other clinical characteristics in drug treatment court participants
Authors: Abigail Helm, Paige M. Shaffer, Michael Andre, Eleni Kachadoorian, Sheila Casey, David Smelson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThree out of four participants in Drug Treatment Courts (DTCs) present with co-occurring substance use disorders and mental health disorders (COD). Among individuals with COD outside criminal-legal settings, 71% report chronic pain. However, chronic pain has not been examined in the context of DTC participation. This secondary analysis compared intake treatment needs of DTC participants with COD, stratified by presence or absence of chronic pain history.MethodsParticipants with COD from five DTCs were compared: 61 with chronic pain history (36%) and 109 without (64%). Chronic pain history was defined based on self-reported lifetime chronic pain and/or receipt of prescriptions for chronic pain management. Bivariate analyses compared pain, mental health, alcohol and illicit substance use, criminal-legal, and quality of life factors.ResultsParticipants with chronic pain history reported greater pain severity and pain-related interference with enjoyment and daily activities. A higher proportion of participants with chronic pain history (66%) reported non-medical prescription opioid use compared to those without chronic pain history (16%). They also reported more illicit use of oxycodone (0.05 vs. 0), more opioid overdoses (0.12 vs. 0), and fewer nights in jail (23.50 vs. 54.16). No significant differences emerged in mental health or quality of life.ConclusionsThis work is the first to compare treatment needs of DTC participants with and without chronic pain history, and the sample presented with a high prevalence of chronic pain history. Therefore, DTC programs should assess for chronic pain among participants to incorporate pain management and support participants more effectively.

Introduction

Drug Treatment Courts (DTCs) are alternative to incarceration programs designed to address substance use disorder (SUD) needs and disrupt cycles of criminal-legal recidivism [1]. DTCs provide ongoing judicial monitoring and link participants community-based SUD treatment, and they demonstrate effectiveness in reducing both illicit substance use and reoffending [2, 3]. Reflecting their success, the number of DTCs in the United States has grown by nearly 30% since 2018, increasing from about 3,100 to approximately 4,000 [4].

Within DTC programs, 70% of individuals present with co-occurring SUD and mental health disorders (COD), and courts play a critical role in addressing comorbid substance use disorder and mental health needs [5]. While interventions targeting COD in DTCs have shown some success in reducing illicit substance use and improving mental health symptoms [5, 6], individuals with COD often experience poorer treatment engagement and worse long-term outcomes (e.g., relapse, recidivism) due to the need for more specialized services compared to those without a comorbid mental health disorder [7, 8]. Furthermore, chronic pain, defined as pain persisting for three or more months, also amplifies service needs and negatively impacts SUD treatment outcomes [9–14], and nearly three-quarters of individuals with COD (outside the criminal-legal system) report chronic pain [15]. Yet, chronic pain among DTC participants with COD is critically understudied.

Given overlapping biological, psychological, and social dimensions involved in chronic pain, DTC participants with COD and chronic pain may face more complex treatment needs compared to those with COD and chronic pain who do not have criminal-legal involvement [16–18]. Examining chronic pain as an additional risk factor within this population may provide critical insights for adapting DTC programs and developing personalized treatments that address pain and COD. For example, individuals with chronic pain often experience a constellation of challenges, including poor mental health [19], employment instability [20], diminished quality of life [21, 22], and elevated risk for non-medical opioid use and overdoses in an attempt to self-manage pain [23–25]. When compounded by COD and criminal-legal involvement, these challenges may be intensified, potentially undermining treatment engagement/retention and increasing vulnerability to relapse [11, 26]. Despite these concerns, no research to date has examined chronic pain among the growing population of DTC participants or its association with behavioral health and criminal-legal needs among those with COD.

Therefore, this study aims to address a critical gap in DTC research by examining chronic pain, a previously unstudied factor, and its associations with treatment needs among individuals with COD enrolled in DTCs. Specifically, the study aims to expand the existing literature by (1) assessing the rate of chronic pain among DTC participants with COD and (2) comparing alcohol and illicit substance use, mental health symptoms, quality of life, and criminal-legal needs between participants with and without chronic pain. By elucidating these relationships, this study offers novel insights into how DTC programs can deliver more tailored support, enhance treatment engagement, and ultimately improve outcomes for participants with chronic pain.

Methods

Data came from intake assessments of 170 participants with COD enrolled in two program evaluations of a multicomponent COD treatment intervention across five Massachusetts DTCs. To increase sample size and statistical power, the datasets were merged. The Institutional Review Board approved these evaluations and classified them as program evaluation rather than human subjects research; therefore, informed consent for was not required.

Participants

Eligibility criteria for program evaluation inclusion were utilized for these analyses, and they included: (1) being 18 years or older and (2) having current COD (at least one SUD and at least one mental health disorder). Prior to DTC enrollment, SUD and mental health disorder diagnoses were assessed and confirmed by licensed court mental health clinicians who utilized the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (5th ed.; DSM–5) [27]. At enrollment into the program evaluation, current SUD and mental health symptoms were assessed using the Addiction Severity Index (ASI) [28]. Because of concerns about including participants with acute psychosis or suicidality in group sessions with those presenting less acute needs, individuals displaying these symptoms were designated for exclusion. Notably, no individuals referred met these exclusion criteria. All eligible participants completed a comprehensive intake assessment before beginning treatment alongside DTC programming.

Measures

Self-reported intake data were derived from a collection of instruments required as a term of the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA) awards. The assessments included the SAMHSA Government Performance and Results Act questions (GPRA) [29], which includes the following ASI [28] items: demographic information; current SUD and mental health symptoms; current housing status and length of housing placement; self-reported frequency of criminal-legal involvement in the previous 6-months and over the lifetime; and quantity, frequency, and severity of alcohol and illicit substance use 30-days prior to DTC intake.

Questions on prescription history, perceptions and use of these prescriptions, and related experiences of chronic pain were collected using the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System Questionnaire (BRFSS), a nationally standardized and validated health survey developed by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) [30]. Participants were included in the history of chronic pain group if they reported: chronic pain in their lifetimes and/or receipt of a prescription for opioids or medical marijuana to treat chronic pain in their lifetimes. Questions on the BRFSS regarding chronic pain prescriptions included:

If participants reported that they did not have chronic pain in their lifetimes and they had never received a prescription as a treatment for chronic pain, then they were included in the group without history of chronic pain. Thus, this group could include participants who received prescriptions for acute pain. As pain is a subjective experience, the use of self-report measures is a gold standard for assessing chronic pain [31].

Mental health symptoms and functioning were measured via the Behavior and Symptom Identification Scale-32 (BASIS-32) [32], including 32 items rated on a scale of 0 (no difficulty) to 4 (extreme difficulty). BASIS-32 is divided into five subscales: depression and anxiety, psychosis, relation to self and others, impulsive and addictive behavior, and daily living and role functioning [33]. Trauma symptoms were self-reported using the PTSD Checklist-Civilian version (PCL-5) [34]. PCL-5 is based on DSM-5 criteria for PTSD and has demonstrated good psychometric properties. A threshold score of > = 31 is used to indicate possible presence of PTSD and to recommend formal assessment [34].

Quality of life was measured using the World Health Organization Quality of Life Scale-BREF (WHOQOL-BREF) [35], a 26-item measure utilized to assess overall quality of a participant’s health and social relationships as well as their ability to participate meaningfully in daily activities. The WHOQOL-BREF has been widely used in behavioral health research and has demonstrated reliable psychometric properties, including in criminal-legal populations [36].

Data analyses

Univariate analyses were computed for the sample overall to determine demographic characteristics, as well as an intake diagnostic profile, mental health well-being, use of alcohol and illicit substances, and variables related to criminal-legal involvement. Overall, 61 participants were identified with chronic pain history (35.88%) and 109 without chronic pain history (64.12%). Normality tests were computed, and bivariate analyses (chi-square test, t-test, Wilcoxon Rank Sum test) compared demographic, pain, mental health, alcohol and illicit substance use, and criminal-legal factors based on chronic pain history. Analyses were done using SAS software, Version 9 [37]. All statistical tests are based on a two-sided alpha of p < .05.

Results

Demographics

Table 1 includes characteristics overall and by group (i.e., history of chronic pain vs. no chronic pain history). Overall, the most common SUD diagnoses were opioid use disorder (68.23%), cocaine use disorder (42.94%), and alcohol use disorder (25.29%). The most common co-occurring mental health disorder diagnoses included generalized anxiety disorder (19.41%), major depressive disorder (15.88%), and bipolar disorder (11.76%). Bivariate comparisons did not reveal statistically significant differences in SUD or mental health disorder diagnoses at intake between groups. Bivariate analyses revealed a significant difference in housing stability, as a greater proportion of DTC participants with history of chronic pain were stably housed (18.33%) at intake compared to DTC participants without history of chronic pain (6.12%) (χ² (1, N = 158) = 5.77, p = .016). Bivariate comparisons did not reveal any other statistically significant differences in demographic factors at intake between groups.

Demographics

Chronic pain

Participants with history of chronic pain reported significantly higher pain within the past week at intake (M = 4.11, SD = 3.99) compared to those without chronic pain history (M = 1.51, SD = 2.70) (Z = 4.91, p < .0001). DTC participants with chronic pain history reported greater pain interference with their enjoyment in life (M = 3.80, SD = 4.10 vs. M = 1.21, SD = 2.32, Z = 4.64, p < .0001) and greater inability to perform general activities due to pain (M = 3.71, SD = 4.13 vs. M = 1.03, SD = 1.98, Z = 4.68, p < .0001) in comparison to those DTC participants without chronic pain history.

Prescription opioid use

Significantly more DTC participants with chronic pain history had an opioid pain medication prescribed to them in their lifetime versus DTC participants without chronic pain history (80.33% vs. 23.85%) (χ² (1, N = 166) = 48.09, p < .0001). Furthermore, when asked about prescription use, two-thirds (65.57%) of DTC participants with history of chronic pain reported non-medical use of prescribed opioid medication in their lifetime compared to those without history of chronic pain (15.60%) (χ² (1, N = 104) = 29.28, p < .0001). In addition, DTC participants with history of chronic pain reported other problematic behaviors in comparison to those without lifetime chronic pain, such as difficulty stopping prescription opioid use (67.21% vs. 17.43%) (χ² (1, N = 104) = 28.07, p < .0001) and prescription opioid use interference with daily responsibilities (55.74% vs. 15.60%) (χ² (1, N = 103) = 16.34, p < .0001).

Alcohol and illicit substance use

Participants with history of chronic pain reported significantly more days using Oxycontin/Oxycodone illicitly (p = .017) and more opioid overdoses in the past 30 days than those without history of chronic pain (p = .023, see Table 2). Bivariate analyses did not reveal any other statistically significant differences in use of alcohol or other illicit substances at intake between chronic pain groups.

Criminal-legal involvement

Participants without history of chronic pain reported spending significantly more nights in jail in the prior 6 months than those with history of chronic pain (p = .004, see Table 2).

Mental health symptoms and functioning

There were no statistically significant differences in PCL-5 or BASIS-32 scores between those with and those without history of chronic pain (see Table 2).

Quality of life

There were no statistically significant differences observed in WHOQOL-BREF scores between those with and those without history of chronic pain (see Table 2).

Discussion

This study found that more than one-third of individuals with COD in our sample reported history of chronic pain. Compared to those without chronic pain history, these participants reported more illicit Oxycontin/Oxycodone use and opioid overdoses in the 30 days prior to intake. They were also more likely to report problems with prescription opioid use, including non-medical use, interference in responsibilities, and unsuccessful attempts to stop use. These findings fill an important gap in the criminal-legal and chronic pain literature by documenting chronic pain history among DTC participants and exploring how it is associated with SUD treatment needs. This study represents an important step forward in DTC research by drawing attention to chronic pain as a significant but previously overlooked concern requiring further investigation in this population.

It is notable that 36% of DTC participants with COD reported history of chronic pain, as the rate of current chronic pain is 71% among individuals with opioid use disorder and comorbid mental health disorders seeking COD treatment outside of a DTC setting [15]. Thus, because the current study included individuals with any SUD, the sample may not have been directly comparable to prior work specifically among those with primary opioid use disorder. In addition, the method used to identify chronic pain history in this study was not a standard procedure, which may account for differences in rates due to measurement sensitivity. Nevertheless, the rate observed among DTC participants with COD in this study was higher than the 18% of individuals on probation (without COD) who endorsed current chronic pain in earlier research [38]. Some probationers from that prior study may also have been in DTC programs, but those results are not reported by the authors; [38] thus, the current study addresses this gap in the literature.

It is also noteworthy that significantly more DTC participants with COD and history of chronic pain reported problems with prescription opioid use compared to those without chronic pain history. As they were more likely to report non-medical use and difficulty stopping use, individuals with COD and chronic pain history may be at higher risk for overdose [23]. We suspect that non-medical use is likely linked to an attempt to self-manage their pain, highlighting the need for DTC programs to pursue and support non-opioid pain management options for participants who experience chronic pain. Furthermore, those with chronic pain history in this study were more likely to report that their use of prescription opioids interfered with their daily responsibilities, which in turn could impact treatment engagement and retention [10]. These findings highlight key recovery-related vulnerabilities in this subgroup, underscoring the importance of personalizing SUD treatment plans, monitoring for increased overdose risk, and referring to non-pharmacological options for pain management in this population.

In addition, participants with history of chronic pain reported significantly fewer nights in jail in the 6 months prior to intake. Given the added treatment complexities of chronic pain among individuals with COD and criminal-legal involvement, these findings are encouraging in contrast to prior work, which showed that probationers with chronic pain reported more lifetime drug-related arrests than those without chronic pain [38]. However, that prior study only assessed lifetime arrests among probationers without COD. In comparison, the current study examined both arrests and nights in jail during the 6 months prior to intake, as well as lifetime arrests and convictions. Chronic pain may result from criminal activity due to increased exposure to violence and injury [39–41], and it is possible that probationers with current chronic pain experienced more drug-related arrests earlier in their lifetimes, prior to receiving a chronic pain diagnosis. However, the current study did not collect data about specific crimes which led to nights spent in jail in the 6 months prior to intake. Such information would shed light on this finding between those with and without history of chronic pain, as individuals experiencing chronic pain may engage in different crimes than those without chronic pain. Additionally, in the prior study, individuals with chronic pain reported fewer current criminogenic cognitions than those without chronic pain, suggesting that the study may not have fully captured the relationship between chronic pain and recent criminal behavior [38].

In contrast to recent findings among individuals with current chronic pain and opioid use disorder seeking COD treatment [15], participants in this study with history of chronic pain did not report significantly worse mental health symptoms, functioning, or quality of life compared to those without history of chronic pain. The current study focused on participants with history of chronic pain, rather than current chronic pain as examined in previous research. Consequently, individuals experiencing current chronic pain may exhibit more recent distress compared to those in this study, who reported chronic pain and/or prescriptions for chronic pain at some point in their lifetimes. As a result, those with current chronic pain may be more likely to engage in non-medical and/or illicit substance use not only to cope with pain symptoms but their mental health symptoms as well. Another key difference between prior research and the current study lies in the data collected. Previous work employed several additional self-report measures of depression, anxiety, and multiple domains of quality of life. Using more specific and sensitive measures may allow future researchers to better detect differences in mental health and quality of life between DTC participants with and without chronic pain.

A greater proportion of DTC participants with history of chronic pain reported having stable housing at intake compared to those without such history. Although the difference between groups is modest, this finding may suggest that individuals with chronic pain in this study tended to have greater access to resources that support housing stability. For example, if participants with history of chronic pain are receiving physical disability financial support, they may maintain access to housing through state programs and benefits. As these results do not align with prior work on the association between chronic pain and housing instability among emergency department patients [42], additional research is needed to understand whether housing instability is a concern among DTC participants who have chronic pain as it could be a potential barrier to treatment engagement/retention.

There is currently no research on the effectiveness of addressing chronic (or acute) pain outcomes among American DTC participants. One earlier study in Australia noted significant and sustained improvements in “bodily pain” for those who participated in a DTC program, though pain was not a target of DTC programming [43]. The current study expands the literature by examining alcohol and illicit substance use, mental health symptoms, quality of life, and criminal-legal intake needs differ between those with and without chronic pain history among participants with COD in DTCs. Next steps for future research should include more comprehensive and longitudinal measures of pain severity and interference to explore the impacts of current chronic pain on DTC services and outcomes. By exploring chronic pain in DTC populations, we can learn whether chronic pain influences program outcomes and allow us to explore targeting chronic pain during treatment as well.

The primary limitation of these secondary analyses is the potential for temporal bias, as the study provides only a cross-sectional view of participants’ behavioral health needs. Second, the study relied on self-report measures; thus, there is a possibility that participants had difficulty recalling information accurately. Specifically, we do not have objective data on alcohol or illicit substance use, which participants were asked to recall for the prior 30 days across a variety of substances. Third, because the original treatment was not focused on chronic pain, the study lacks more detailed and standardized measures of pain severity and interference. Fourth, generalizability is very limited as participants were recruited from five DTCs in Massachusetts, and the sample was mostly White, non-Hispanic/Latine males. Thus, care should be taken in generalizing these findings to other DTC sub-populations. Fifth, as is common in DTC settings, alcohol and illicit substance use reported at intake was very low, though individuals with history of chronic pain reported significantly more recent illicit Oxycontin/Oxycodone use and opioid overdoses compared to those without chronic pain history. Many of these individuals were already enrolled in DTCs when they completed intake interviews, thus findings may not reflect treatment needs prior to enrollment. Finally, given the number of statistical comparisons, findings should be interpreted as exploratory, and some results may reflect Type I error.

The current findings underscore the importance of considering chronic pain as a potential risk factor at treatment initiation. For example, DTC program staff may benefit from knowing whether a participant has chronic pain, as it could contribute to pain-related cravings and influence non-medical use of prescription opioids and other illicit substance use [10, 44]. Furthermore, these individuals may face barriers to adequate pain management due to provider biases and restrictions for patients with SUDs [45, 46]. Moreover, individuals with chronic pain and SUDs may be at a higher risk for overdose and death if they attempt to self-medicate [44]. Screening for chronic pain could therefore help DTC program staff identify how alcohol and illicit substance use may be worsened by chronic pain and, conversely, how alcohol and illicit substance use may in turn exacerbate chronic pain symptoms in the absence of tailored treatment.

Conclusions

This study highlights that many individuals with COD enrolling in DTC programs have history of chronic pain, which is an area that has not been examined in prior research and could be a target for focused interventions to improve DTC outcomes for these individuals. To advance DTC research, it is important to confirm the prevalence of current chronic pain in this population, as DTC participants with history of chronic pain exhibited more substance use disorder needs at treatment entry, potentially complicating recovery. These findings also provide critical evidence for DTC providers to screen for chronic pain to address the interactions between pain, alcohol and illicit substance use, and mental health symptoms within this treatment setting. Courts may also need to consider how to tailor monitoring strategies for these participants and provide non-opioid pain management treatment approaches. Future studies with this population should closely track chronic pain symptoms to address patients’ complex needs and to evaluate how pain relates to behavioral health and criminal-legal outcomes.
