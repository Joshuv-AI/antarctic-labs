K3 surfaces over ℚ \displaystyle\mathbb{Q} of degree 10 that have Picard rank 1
victor de vries

Abstract
. We give examples of K3 surfaces over ℚ \displaystyle\mathbb{Q} of degree 10 \displaystyle 10 whose geometric Picard group has rank 1 \displaystyle 1 . These K3 surfaces are intersections in ℙ 9 \displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{9} of three hyperplanes, one quadric and the image of the Plücker embedding of the Grasmannian Gr ⁡ ( 2 , 5 ) \displaystyle\mathrm{Gr}(2,5) . We also give an example of a K3 surface of degree 6 \displaystyle 6 over ℚ \displaystyle\mathbb{Q} whose Picard rank is 1 \displaystyle 1 .



Let
\displaystyle S
be a K3 surface over a number field
\displaystyle k
. To be precise,
\displaystyle S
is a surface,
\displaystyle S\to\mathrm{Sp}(k)
is smooth, proper and geometrically connected, whose canonical sheaf
\displaystyle\omega_{S}
is trivial and which satisfies
\displaystyle\mathrm{H}^{1}(S,\mathcal{O}_{S})=0
. For arithmetic purposes, one of its most useful invariants is the rank of its geometric Picard group,
\displaystyle\mathrm{rk}\,\mathrm{Pic}(\overline{S})
, which we call the
Picard rank of
\displaystyle S
. For example, Bogomolov and Tschinkel
[
2
]
proved that when the Picard rank is at least
\displaystyle 5
, the rational points on the K3 surface are potentially dense. The Picard rank
\displaystyle\rho
satisfies
\displaystyle 1\leq\rho\leq h^{1,1}(S_{\mathbb{C}})=20
and as a heuristic one can say that the lower
\displaystyle\rho
is, the harder it is to understand the arithmetic of
\displaystyle S
. In particular if
\displaystyle S
has Picard rank equal to
\displaystyle 1
, then its arithmetic should be complicated and interesting.

Let
\displaystyle(S,D)
be a pair, where
\displaystyle S
is a K3 surface and
\displaystyle D
is an ample divisor on
\displaystyle S
whose class in
\displaystyle\mathrm{Pic}(\overline{S}
) is primitive. The pair is called
of degree

\displaystyle 2d
if the self-intersection number of
\displaystyle D
is
\displaystyle 2d
. We may also refer to a K3 surface
\displaystyle S
as being of degree
\displaystyle 2d
if it admits such a
\displaystyle D
as above. A general K3 surface of degree
\displaystyle 2d\leq 8
is a complete intersection in
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{d+1}
and so the result by Terasoma
[
11
]
implies that for
\displaystyle 2d\leq 8
, there exists a K3 surface over
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
of degree
\displaystyle 2d
with Picard rank
\displaystyle 1
. Moreover, this can be strengthened to include the cases
\displaystyle d\in\{5,6,7,8,9\}
, because in these cases the general K3 surface is a complete intersection of sections of line bundles in a Grassmannian (which depends on
\displaystyle d
) and the argument of Terasoma goes through as shown in the proof of Proposition 3.14 in
[
7
]
. These proofs are however not constructive.

The first examples of K3 surfaces over
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
with Picard rank
\displaystyle 1
were given by van Luijk
[
14
]
. These examples are quartics in
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{3}
, i.e. K3 surfaces of degree
\displaystyle 4
. Later Elsenhans and Jahnel
[
3
]
gave an example of a K3 surface over
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
of degree
\displaystyle 2
with Picard rank
\displaystyle 1
. By using Fourier-Mukai duality with a K3 surface of degree
\displaystyle 2
, the authors of
[
8
]
were able to exhibit a K3 surface over
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
of degree
\displaystyle 8
whose Picard rank is
\displaystyle 1
, see
[
8
, Section 5.4]
.

The aim of this document is to exhibit K3 surfaces over
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
of degree
\displaystyle 10
with Picard rank
\displaystyle 1
. Moreover, as a sidenote we ‘fill in the gap’ and exhibit K3 surfaces over
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
of degree
\displaystyle 6
with Picard rank
\displaystyle 1
. We note that a very recent publication
[
1
]
also gives examples of K3 surfaces over
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
of degree
\displaystyle 6
whose Picard rank is
\displaystyle 1
. In this document, the main focus is the degree
\displaystyle 10
case.

We consider the Grassmannian
\displaystyle\mathrm{Gr}(2,5)
, which embeds via the Plücker embedding in
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{9}
. A general K3 surface over
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}
of degree
\displaystyle 10
is the intersection in
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{9}
of
\displaystyle\mathrm{Gr}(2,5)
, three hyperplanes and one quadric hypersurface. It is thus a complete intersection in
\displaystyle\mathrm{Gr}(2,5)
of type
\displaystyle(1,1,1,2)
, see Definition
1.4
.
We will give one K3 surface of this type over
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{2}
and one over
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{3}
. The one over
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{3}
will have Picard rank
\displaystyle 2
and the one over
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{2}
is chosen such that a certain technical condition holds, see Lemma
2.2
. The surface
\displaystyle S_{2}
is the zero locus in
\displaystyle\mathrm{Gr}(2,5)
of three linear forms
\displaystyle l_{1},l_{2},l_{3}
and one quadratic form
\displaystyle q
and similarly
\displaystyle S_{3}
is cut out in
\displaystyle\mathrm{Gr}(2,5)
by linear forms
\displaystyle l_{1}^{\prime},l_{2}^{\prime},l_{3}^{\prime}
and a quadratic form
\displaystyle q^{\prime}
. These forms are given in Lemmas
2.1
and
2.3
. The Grassmannian
\displaystyle\mathrm{Gr}(2,5)
has a canonical model over
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}
, which is cut out by the ‘Plücker relations’ in
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{9}_{\mathbb{Z}}
. Thus we may lift the forms over
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{2},\mathbb{F}_{3}
to forms over the integers to obtain a subscheme of
\displaystyle\mathrm{Gr}(2,5)_{\mathbb{Q}}
. Our main theorem is the following.

Theorem 0.1
.

Let
\displaystyle S
be a subscheme of
\displaystyle\mathrm{Gr}(2,5)_{\mathbb{Q}}
that is the zero locus of forms
\displaystyle L_{1},L_{2},L_{3},Q
, where the
\displaystyle L_{i}
are lifts of
\displaystyle l_{i},l_{i}^{\prime}
to forms over
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}
and
\displaystyle Q
is a lift of
\displaystyle q,q^{\prime}
to a form over
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}
. The scheme
\displaystyle S
is a K3 surface over
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
of degree
\displaystyle 10
, whose Picard rank is
\displaystyle 1
.

The approach is inspired by the one taken by van Luijk
[
14
]
, whose method also includes constructing surfaces modulo primes and then lifting. Moreover, we make use of a result by Elsenhans and Jahnel
[
4
]
, which in our case implies that the lattice
\displaystyle\mathrm{Pic}(\overline{S})
is a primitive sublattice of
\displaystyle\mathrm{Pic}(\overline{S_{3}})
. The K3 surfaces
\displaystyle S_{2}
and
\displaystyle S_{3}
are found by using Magma to pick random K3 surfaces until we find two for which Lemmas
2.2
and
2.7
hold. Therefore we suspect that examples of K3 surfaces over
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
with Picard rank
\displaystyle 1
and degree
\displaystyle 2d\in\{12,14,16,18\}
can be found by using similar methods on the condition that the
\displaystyle\text{mod }p
computations in Magma remain feasible.

It is not clear how one would ‘lift’ when the general K3 surface is not a complete intersection (which happens for
\displaystyle d\gg 0
). A conjecture of Shafarevich
[
9
]
predicts that for every positive integer
\displaystyle e
, there are only finitely many lattices that appear as
\displaystyle\mathrm{Pic}(\overline{X})
for
\displaystyle X
a K3 surface over a number field
\displaystyle K
with
\displaystyle[K:\mathbb{Q}]=e
. This conjecture would imply that there are only finitely many degrees
\displaystyle 2d
for which one can find a K3 surface over
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
of degree
\displaystyle 2d
for which one has
\displaystyle\rho=1
.

1.
Preliminaries

For any surface
\displaystyle X
, one can consider the Néron-Severi group
\displaystyle\mathrm{NS}(X)
of
\displaystyle X
. After quotienting
\displaystyle\mathrm{NS}(X)
by its torsion, it has the structure of a lattice under the intersection pairing. For a K3 surface
\displaystyle S
we have
\displaystyle h^{1}(S,\mathcal{O}_{S})=0
, which implies that we have
\displaystyle\mathrm{Pic}(S)=\mathrm{NS}(S)
and that this is group is torsion-free. We have the following lemma, which relates the Néron-Severi group of a variety to the Néron-Severi group of its reduction.

Lemma 1.1
.

Let
\displaystyle R
be a DVR with
\displaystyle\mathrm{Sp}(R)=\{\eta,x\}
, where the point
\displaystyle x
is closed. Let
\displaystyle\pi\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathrm{Sp}(R)
be a smooth and proper relative surface. There is a specialization map
\displaystyle\mathrm{NS}(\overline{\mathcal{X}_{\eta}})/\{\text{tors}\}\to\mathrm{NS}(\overline{\mathcal{X}_{x}})/\{\text{tors}\}
, which is an inclusion of lattices. Moreover, for
\displaystyle l
a prime that is inverible in
\displaystyle R
, the inertia subgroup of
\displaystyle\mathrm{Gal}(\overline{\eta}/\eta)
acts trivially on
\displaystyle\mathrm{NS}(\overline{\mathcal{X}_{\eta}})\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}_{l}
and the specialization map is
\displaystyle\mathrm{Gal}(\overline{x}/x)
-equivariant.

Proof.

For the statement about the inclusion of lattices, see
[
13
, Proposition 6.2]
. The Kummer sequence yields a commuting diagram in which each row is equivariant with respect to the relevant Galois action

For the second statement it suffices to show that the inertia group of
\displaystyle\mathrm{Gal}(\overline{\eta}/\eta)
acts trivially on
\displaystyle\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{\acute{e}t}}^{2}(\overline{X}_{\eta},\mu_{l^{n}})
for all
\displaystyle n>0
.
The sheaf
\displaystyle R^{2}\pi_{*}\mu_{l^{n}}
is locally constant by smooth-proper base change and thus we can choose a Galois étale algebra
\displaystyle R^{\prime}/R
, such that
\displaystyle R^{2}\pi_{*}\mu_{l^{n}}|_{R^{\prime}}
is the sheaf associated to a constant abelian group
\displaystyle M
. Denote by
\displaystyle j^{\prime}\colon\eta^{\prime}\to\mathrm{Sp}(R^{\prime})
the inclusion of the generic point and by
\displaystyle\pi^{\prime}\colon\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\to\mathrm{Sp}(R^{\prime})
and
\displaystyle\pi_{\eta^{\prime}}\colon\mathcal{X}^{\prime}_{\eta^{\prime}}\to\{\eta^{\prime}\}
the base-changes of
\displaystyle\pi
. We have by smooth-base change the identification
\displaystyle R^{2}\pi_{\eta^{\prime},*}\mu_{l_{n}}=j^{\prime*}R^{2}\pi^{\prime}_{*}\mu_{l_{n}}=j^{\prime*}M
and so
\displaystyle R^{2}\pi_{\eta^{\prime},*}\mu_{l^{n}}
is constant.

The stalk of the sheaf
\displaystyle R^{2}\pi_{*}\mu_{l^{n}}
at
\displaystyle\overline{\eta}
corresponds to the
\displaystyle\mathrm{Gal}(\overline{\eta}/\eta)
-module
\displaystyle\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{\acute{e}t}}^{2}(\overline{X}_{\eta},\mu_{l^{n}})
by proper base-change. By the last sentence of the previous paragraph, the field extension
\displaystyle\kappa(\eta^{\prime})/\kappa(\eta)
trivializes this Galois module and since
\displaystyle\kappa(\eta^{\prime})/\kappa(\eta)
is unramified we conclude that the inertia group of
\displaystyle\mathrm{Gal}(\overline{\eta}/\eta)
acts trivially on
\displaystyle\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{\acute{e}t}}^{2}(\overline{X}_{\eta},\mu_{l^{n}})
for every
\displaystyle n
and so on
\displaystyle\mathrm{NS}(\overline{\mathcal{X}_{\eta}})\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}_{l}
.

The specialization map
\displaystyle\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{\acute{e}t}}^{2}(\overline{\mathcal{X}}_{\eta},\mu_{l^{n}})\to\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{\acute{e}t}}^{2}(\overline{\mathcal{X}}_{x},\mu_{l^{n}})
may be understood as follows: After passing to the finite étale cover
\displaystyle R^{\prime}/R
, the sheaf
\displaystyle R^{2}\pi_{*}\mu_{l^{n}}|_{R^{\prime}}
is constant and so the stalks of
\displaystyle R^{2}\pi_{*}\mu_{l^{n}}
are identified in a canonical way. This identification is compatible with the action of
\displaystyle\pi_{1}(\mathrm{Sp}(R),\overline{\eta})
on
\displaystyle R^{2}\pi_{*}\mu_{l^{n}}
and
\displaystyle\mathrm{Gal}(\overline{x}/x)
acts on these stalks in the same way, via
\displaystyle\pi_{1}(\mathrm{Sp}(R),\overline{\eta})
. Together with Diagram (
1
) this implies the last statement of the lemma.
∎

Typically when
\displaystyle\mathrm{Gr}(2,5)
is mentioned, it is implicit that it is a variety over a field. There is a natural model of
\displaystyle\mathrm{Gr}(2,5)
over
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}
, which has the following functor of points.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathbb{G}
be the functor

(\mathrm{Sch})^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathrm{Set}\quad S\mapsto\{\text{locally free sheaves of rank }2,\text{ }\mathcal{E}\subset\mathcal{O}_{S}^{\oplus 5}\,|\,\mathcal{O}_{S}^{\oplus 5}/\mathcal{E}\text{ is locally free}\}.

Remark 1.3
.

The functor
\displaystyle\mathbb{G}
is represented by a scheme
\displaystyle\mathrm{Gr}(2,5)
that is smooth and proper over
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}
. To ease notation we write
\displaystyle\mathbb{G}=\mathrm{Gr}(2,5)
.
There is a closed embedding
\displaystyle\mathrm{Plu}\colon\mathbb{G}\to\mathbb{P}^{9}
, that can be described on
\displaystyle L
-points, where
\displaystyle L
is a field by,
\displaystyle[W\subset L^{\oplus 5}]\mapsto[\det(W)\subset\bigwedge^{2}L^{\oplus 5}\cong L^{\oplus 10}]
. The image of
\displaystyle\mathbb{G}
under this embedding is cut out by the Plücker relations
\displaystyle\{G_{i}=0\}_{1\leq i\leq 5}
, which are

G_{1}:=x_{12}x_{34}+x_{23}x_{14}-x_{13}x_{24}

G_{2}:=x_{12}x_{35}+x_{23}x_{15}-x_{13}x_{25}

G_{3}:=x_{12}x_{45}+x_{24}x_{15}-x_{14}x_{25}

G_{4}:=x_{13}x_{45}+x_{34}x_{15}-x_{14}x_{35}

G_{5}:=x_{23}x_{45}+x_{34}x_{25}-x_{24}x_{35}.

We may sometimes also write
\displaystyle\mathbb{G}
for a base-change
\displaystyle\mathbb{G}_{k}
of
\displaystyle\mathbb{G}
, where
\displaystyle k
is a field. What is meant with
\displaystyle\mathbb{G}
should be clear from the context.

Definition 1.4
.

Denote by
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{G}}(n)
the invertible sheaf
\displaystyle\mathrm{Plu}^{*}\mathcal{O}(n)
on
\displaystyle\mathbb{G}
. We say that a variety
\displaystyle X\subset\mathbb{G}_{k}
of dimension
\displaystyle 6-t
is a
complete intersection of type

\displaystyle(n_{1},...,n_{t})
if we have
\displaystyle X=V_{\mathbb{G}}(s_{1},...,s_{t})
for some
\displaystyle s_{i}\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{G}}(n_{i})(\mathbb{G})
.

We need the following lemma in order to be able to prove Lemma
1.10
, which is key for our construction.

Lemma 1.5
.

The canonical sheaf of
\displaystyle\mathbb{G}
is isomorphic to
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{G}}(-5)
.

This lemma should be well-known (it follows e.g. from
[
5
, Appendix B.5.8]
), however we could not find a simple proof for it in the literature that works in positive characteristic.

We make a remark about the existence of certain universal sheaves on
\displaystyle\mathbb{G}
.

Remark 1.6
.

The identity map
\displaystyle\mathbb{G}\to\mathbb{G}
corresponds to a rank
\displaystyle 2
locally free sheaf
\displaystyle\mathcal{R}\subset\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{G}}^{\oplus 5}
. This sheaf is universal in the sense that for any morphism
\displaystyle f\colon Y\to\mathbb{G}
corresponding to
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}\subset\mathcal{O}_{Y}^{\oplus 5}
, we have

Lemma 1.7
.

The tangent sheaf of
\displaystyle\mathbb{G}
is isomorphic to
\displaystyle\SHom(\mathcal{R},\mathcal{Q})

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{\mathbb{G}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{G}

\displaystyle\mathbb{G}
-scheme
\displaystyle Y\to\mathbb{G}

\displaystyle\mathrm{Mor}_{\mathbb{G}}(Y,\mathbb{T})=\mathrm{Mor}_{\mathbb{G}}(Y[\epsilon],\mathbb{G})

corresponds to a rank
\displaystyle 2

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{Y}^{\oplus 5}/\mathcal{E}=f^{*}\mathcal{Q}

Suppose that we have an

-module homomorphism

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\phi}\subset\mathcal{O}_{Y[\epsilon]}^{\oplus 5}

\displaystyle\langle r+\epsilon\tilde{\phi(r)},\epsilon r\rangle

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{Y}^{\oplus 5}

Both the domain and the codomain of the morphism of functors give locally free sheaves on
\displaystyle\mathbb{G}
when restricting the domain of the functors from
\displaystyle(\mathrm{Sch}/\mathbb{G})^{\mathrm{op}}
to
\displaystyle(\mathrm{Open}(\mathbb{G}))^{\mathrm{op}}
. To show the map between these locally free sheaves is an isomorphism, it is sufficient to show that it is an isomorphism on the fiber over every closed point
\displaystyle x
of
\displaystyle\mathbb{G}
. Let
\displaystyle[W]\colon\mathrm{Sp}(L)\to\mathbb{G}
be such a point. The morphism of functors evaluated at this point
\displaystyle[W]
is:

\Psi_{[W]}\colon\mathrm{Hom}(W,L^{5}/W)\to\{\text{rank }2\text{ free }M\subset L[\epsilon]^{\oplus 5}\,|\,\text{cokernel is free}\text{ and }M[\epsilon]\overset{\epsilon\to 0}{\to}W\}\quad\phi\mapsto W_{\phi}.

\displaystyle\Phi_{[W]}
is injective. Let
\displaystyle\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\colon W\to L^{5}/W
be linear maps that satisfy
\displaystyle\phi_{1}(w)\neq\phi_{2}(w)
for some

\displaystyle\tilde{\phi_{1}(w)}
of
\displaystyle\phi_{1}(w)

\displaystyle L^{5}
we have that
\displaystyle r+\epsilon\tilde{\phi_{1}(w)}

\displaystyle W_{\phi_{1}}

\displaystyle W_{\phi_{2}}

\displaystyle\Psi_{[W]}

\displaystyle n=n_{1}+\epsilon n_{2}

\displaystyle L[\epsilon]

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle 2

\displaystyle 5

Now we have the following lemma on the Plücker embedding.

Lemma 1.8
.

The invertible sheaves
\displaystyle\det(\mathcal{Q})
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{G}}(1)
are isomorphic.

Proof.

The exact sequence of sheaves
\displaystyle 0\to\mathcal{R}\to\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{G}}^{\oplus 5}\to\mathcal{Q}\to 0
induces a surjection
\displaystyle\bigwedge^{3}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{G}}^{\oplus 5}\overset{\pi}{\to}\det(\mathcal{Q})
and thus a morphism
\displaystyle\iota\colon\mathbb{G}\to\mathbb{P}^{9}

\displaystyle\iota
a closed point
\displaystyle[W]\colon\mathrm{Sp}(L)\to\mathbb{G}
is mapped to the following one-dimensional subspace of
\displaystyle(\bigwedge^{3}L^{\oplus 5})^{\vee}

(2)

[W]\mapsto\{f\in(\bigwedge^{3}L^{\oplus 5})^{\vee}\,|\,f\text{ vanishes on all }s\in\bigwedge^{3}L^{\oplus 5}\text{ for which }\pi(s)_{[W]}=0\text{ holds}\}\in\mathbb{P}^{9}(L)

The assignment
\displaystyle s\mapsto\pi(s)_{[W]}
is simply given by the natural map
\displaystyle\bigwedge^{3}L^{\oplus 5}\to\det(L^{\oplus 5}/W)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}

\displaystyle\bigwedge^{3}L^{\oplus 5}\otimes\bigwedge^{2}L^{\oplus 5}\to\det(L^{\oplus 5})

[W]\mapsto\{f\in\bigwedge^{2}L^{\oplus 5}\,|\,f\wedge s=0\text{ for all }s\in\bigwedge^{3}L^{\oplus 5}\text{ that vanish in }\det(L^{\oplus 5}/W)\}.

The mentioned wedge pairing induces a perfect pairing
\displaystyle\det(W)\otimes\det(L^{\oplus 5}/W)\to\det(L^{\oplus 5})

is mapped in Equation (

.

The scheme
\displaystyle S
is a K3 surface.

Proof.

The structure morphism
\displaystyle\pi\colon\mathcal{S}\to\mathrm{Sp}(\mathbb{Z})
has
\displaystyle S
as its generic fiber. Since
\displaystyle\pi
is proper, the map
\displaystyle\mathrm{Sp}(\mathbb{Z})\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\quad x\mapsto\dim\pi^{-1}(x)
is upper semicontinuous. Thus
\displaystyle S_{2}
, the fiber over
\displaystyle(2)
, having
\displaystyle\dim(S_{2})=2
implies that
\displaystyle S
has dimension at most
\displaystyle 2
and since
\displaystyle S
is cut out by
\displaystyle 4
equations in
\displaystyle\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{Q}}
, which has
\displaystyle\dim(\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{Q}})=6
, we have
\displaystyle\dim(S)=2
. Since smoothness is an open property, we obtain that
\displaystyle S
is smooth, because
\displaystyle S_{2}
is smooth. We invoke Lemma
1.10
to finish the proof.
∎

Now we prove the main theorem.

Theorem 3.3
.

The K3 surface
\displaystyle S
has Picard rank
\displaystyle 1
.

Proof.

The scheme
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}
is a model of
\displaystyle S
over
\displaystyle\mathrm{Sp}(\mathbb{Z})
, that is smooth at
\displaystyle 2,3
, because the fibers at these points are
\displaystyle S_{2},S_{3}
. By Lemma
1.1
, we get an inclusion of lattices
\displaystyle\mathrm{Pic}(\overline{S})\hookrightarrow\mathrm{Pic}(\overline{S_{3}})
. Thus
\displaystyle\rho(\overline{S})
is bounded by
\displaystyle\rho(\overline{S_{3}})
, which is
\displaystyle 2
by Lemma
2.7
. Assume for a contradiction that
\displaystyle\rho(\overline{S})
equals
\displaystyle 2
.

By the result of Elsenhans and Jahnel
[
4
, Theorem 1.4]
, the cokernel of this inclusion is torsion-free, so we make the identification
\displaystyle\mathrm{Pic}(\overline{S})=\mathrm{Pic}(\overline{S_{3}})
. By Lemma
2.8
we have that
\displaystyle\mathrm{Pic}(\overline{S_{3}})
is spanned by the class
\displaystyle H_{3}
of a hyperplane section of
\displaystyle S_{3}
and the class
\displaystyle M_{3}
of a fiber of
\displaystyle\pi:S_{3}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
. This implies that
\displaystyle\mathrm{Pic}(\overline{S})
is represented by the Gramm-matrix
\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}10&5\\
5&0\end{pmatrix}
with respect to the basis
\displaystyle H,M
, which are the classes that correspond to
\displaystyle H_{3},M_{3}
.

For any field
\displaystyle k
, write
\displaystyle\Gamma_{k}
for its absolute Galois group. By Lemma
1.1
the inclusion
\displaystyle\mathrm{Pic}(\overline{S})\hookrightarrow\mathrm{Pic}(\overline{S_{2}})
is
\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}
-equivariant. Since there are only two classes
\displaystyle N\in\mathrm{Pic}(\overline{S})
that satisfy
\displaystyle N^{2}=0
and
\displaystyle N\cdot H=5
, the
\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}
-orbit of
\displaystyle M
has size at most
\displaystyle 2
. In what follows we also write
\displaystyle H,M
for the images of
\displaystyle H,M
in
\displaystyle\mathrm{Pic}(\overline{S_{2}})
.

We first look at the case in which the orbit has size
\displaystyle 1
. The Hochschild-Serre spectral sequence gives an exact sequence

0\to\mathrm{Pic}(S_{2})\to\mathrm{Pic}(\overline{S_{2}})\to\mathrm{Br}(\mathbb{F}_{2})\to....

Since the Brauer group of a finite field is trivial, we have
\displaystyle\mathrm{Pic}(\overline{S_{2}})^{\Gamma_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}}=\mathrm{Pic}(S_{2})
. So we can find a divisor
\displaystyle D
of
\displaystyle S_{2}
for which we have
\displaystyle[D]=M
. Since
\displaystyle M^{2}=0
holds, Riemann-Roch for
\displaystyle S_{2}
tells us that we either have
\displaystyle h^{0}(M)>0
or
\displaystyle h^{0}(-M)=h^{2}(M)>0
. Clearly
\displaystyle-M
is not effective (intersect with
\displaystyle H
). So we may pick an effective divisor
\displaystyle D_{1}
on
\displaystyle S_{2}
whose class is
\displaystyle M
. Since
\displaystyle(H-M)^{2}=0
holds, we have by the same argument that
\displaystyle H-M
or
\displaystyle M-H
is effective and intersecting with
\displaystyle H
implies that
\displaystyle H-M
is effective. Pick an effective divisor
\displaystyle D_{2}
on
\displaystyle S_{2}
such that
\displaystyle[D_{2}]=H-M
. Then
\displaystyle D_{1}+D_{2}
is effective and its class is
\displaystyle H
, i.e.
\displaystyle D_{1}+D_{2}
is an
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{2}
-hyperplane section of
\displaystyle S_{2}
.

Suppose that
\displaystyle M
has
\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}
-orbit equal to
\displaystyle\{M,H-M\}
. Then
\displaystyle M
is fixed by
\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathbb{F}_{4}}
and so by the same spectral sequence argument as in the previous paragraph, we can find an effective divisor
\displaystyle D
of
\displaystyle S_{4}:=\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{F}_{4}}
for which we have
\displaystyle[D]=M
. Let
\displaystyle\sigma
be the generator of
\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathbb{F}_{4}/\mathbb{F}_{2}}
. The effective divisor
\displaystyle\sigma(D)
has class
\displaystyle H-M
and so
\displaystyle D+\sigma(D)
is a hyperplane section of
\displaystyle S_{2}
. So in both cases we see that
\displaystyle M
is represented by an effective
\displaystyle D
over
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{4}
that lies in a hyperplane section, which is defined over
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{2}
.

We call this hyperplane section
\displaystyle E
for which we have
\displaystyle E_{\mathbb{F}_{4}}=D+N
. By Lemma
2.7
, the hyperplane section
\displaystyle E
is geometrically reduced and so
\displaystyle D
and
\displaystyle N
have no common component. Moreover reducedness implies that
\displaystyle E_{\text{sing}}
has dimension
\displaystyle 0
. There is a closed immersion of the scheme-theoretic intersection
\displaystyle D\cap N
into
\displaystyle E_{\text{sing}}
. The scheme-theoretic intersection
\displaystyle D\cap N
has degree
\displaystyle D\cdot N=M\cdot(H-M)=5
and thus we have
\displaystyle 5\leq\deg(E_{\text{sing}})
. On the other hand, the equality
\displaystyle E_{\mathbb{F}_{4}}=D+M
says that the irreducible components of
\displaystyle E_{\mathbb{F}_{4}}
have degree at most
\displaystyle\deg(D)=\deg(N)=5
. By Lemma
2.2
the combination of the last two sentences is not possible.

Since we have reached a contradiction, we conclude that we do not have
\displaystyle\rho(\overline{S})=2
. By looking modulo
\displaystyle 3
we had already seen that we have
\displaystyle\rho(\overline{S})\leq 2
and we conclude that we the Picard rank of
\displaystyle\overline{S}
is
\displaystyle 1
.
∎

4.
The degree
\displaystyle 6
example

As promised in the introduction, we also exhibit an example of a degree
\displaystyle 6
K3 surface over
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
with Picard rank
\displaystyle 1
. A general K3 surface of degree
\displaystyle 6
is a complete intersection of a quadric and a cubic in
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{4}
on which we take coordinates
\displaystyle x,y,z,v,w
. The approach is similar as for the degree
\displaystyle 10
case and we begin with a K3 surface modulo
\displaystyle 2
.

Remark 4.1
.

A smooth complete intersection of a quadric and a cubic is a K3 surface. Indeed, the argument in the first paragraph of the proof of Lemma
1.10
shows that such a complete intersection is simply connected and the adjunction formula shows that the canonical bundle is trivial.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\displaystyle X_{2}
be the subscheme of
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{4}_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}
that is cut out by the equations
\displaystyle f=vq_{1}+wq_{2}=0
, where we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\displaystyle=x^{2}+xz+yz+z^{2}+xv+yv+zv+v^{2}+yw+zw+w^{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle q_{1}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=x^{2}+y^{2}+yz+z^{2}+xv

\displaystyle\displaystyle q_{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=y^{2}+yz+z^{2}+xv+v^{2}+xw+zw+vw+w^{2}.

The scheme
\displaystyle X_{2}
is a K3 surface and its Picard lattice
\displaystyle\mathrm{Pic}(\overline{X_{2}})
is isomorphic to
\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}6&4\\
4&0\end{pmatrix}
.

Proof.

The class
\displaystyle H
of a hyperplane section and the class
\displaystyle F
of the curve
\displaystyle C:f=v=q_{2}=0
generate a sublattice
\displaystyle\Lambda
of
\displaystyle\mathrm{Pic}(\overline{X_{2}})
that has intersection matrix
\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}6&4\\
4&0\end{pmatrix}
. We check with Magma that the point count of
\displaystyle X_{2}
over the fields
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{2^{i}}
with
\displaystyle i\leq 10
is given as follows:

By the same argument as in Lemma
2.7
, we verify that the Picard rank of
\displaystyle X_{2}
is
\displaystyle 2
. It remains to be shown that
\displaystyle\Lambda
is a primitive sublattice of
\displaystyle\mathrm{Pic}(\overline{X_{2}})
.

Suppose for a contradiction that
\displaystyle\Lambda
is not a primitive sublattice of
\displaystyle\mathrm{Pic}(\overline{X_{2}})
. Then there is a class
\displaystyle E
in
\displaystyle\mathrm{Pic}(\overline{X_{2}})
for which we have
\displaystyle 2E=mH+nF
with
\displaystyle m,n\in\{0,1\}
and both nonzero. Taking the self-intersection on both sides yields that
\displaystyle m
is divisible by
\displaystyle 2
. Thus we obtain that
\displaystyle F
is divisible by
\displaystyle 2
and we may assume that
\displaystyle F=2E
. If
\displaystyle-E
is effective, then so is
\displaystyle-F
and so
\displaystyle F
is trivial, which is not the case and so
\displaystyle-E
can not be effective. By Riemann-Roch for surfaces, we thus obtain
\displaystyle h^{0}(E)\geq 2
and so there is an effective divisor
\displaystyle D
whose class is
\displaystyle E
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{C}
be the ideal sheaf of the curve
\displaystyle C
in the first line of this proof. Since
\displaystyle C
is integral, the long exact sequence in cohomology

0\to k\to\mathrm{H}^{0}(C,\mathcal{O}_{C})\to\mathrm{H}^{1}(\overline{X}_{2},\mathcal{I}_{C})\to\mathrm{H}^{1}(\overline{X_{2}},\mathcal{O})=0

shows that we have
\displaystyle h^{1}(\overline{X_{2}},\mathcal{I}_{C})=0
and so by Serre-duality we have
\displaystyle h^{1}(\overline{X_{2}},F)=0
. Riemann-Roch for surfaces shows that we have
\displaystyle h^{0}(\overline{X_{2}},F)=2
. Because we have
\displaystyle 2E=F
and both classes are effective, we get
\displaystyle 2=h^{0}(F)\geq h^{0}(E)\geq 2
and so
\displaystyle h^{0}(E)=2
holds, so we have
\displaystyle H^{0}(\overline{X_{2}},\mathcal{O}(D))=H^{0}(\overline{X_{2}},\mathcal{O}(2D))
. Since
\displaystyle F
has self-intersection
\displaystyle 0
, the divisors
\displaystyle D
and
\displaystyle 2D
are basepoint free, see
[
6
, Proposition 3.3.10]
. But the
\displaystyle\mathrm{PGL}_{2}
-classes of maps that they induce to
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{1}
are the same, so we obtain that
\displaystyle D
and
\displaystyle 2D
are linearly equivalent and so
\displaystyle E
is trivial. This is a contradiction, because
\displaystyle 2E=F
is nontrivial.

We conclude that
\displaystyle\Lambda
is a primitive sublattice of
\displaystyle\mathrm{Pic}(\overline{X_{2}})
, which concludes the proof.
∎

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{3}

Let

\displaystyle V(vl_{1}+wl_{2},g)

\displaystyle X_{3}

Notation 4.4
.

Denote the cubic in Lemma
4.2
by
\displaystyle h
and denote the quadric in Lemma

\displaystyle q
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
\displaystyle F,G\in\mathbb{Z}[x,y,z,v,w]
be homogeneous polynomials such that
\displaystyle F
reduces to
\displaystyle f,q
and such that
\displaystyle G
reduces to
\displaystyle h,g
. Let
\displaystyle X
denote the subscheme
\displaystyle V(F,G)
of
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{4}_{\mathbb{Q}}
, which is a lift of
\displaystyle X_{2}
and
\displaystyle X_{3}
. The scheme
\displaystyle X
is a K3 surface of degree
\displaystyle 6
with Picard rank
\displaystyle 1
.

Proof.

The same argument from Lemma
3.2
implies that
\displaystyle X
is a K3 surface. By Lemma
1.1
, the Picard rank of
\displaystyle X
is bounded by
\displaystyle\mathrm{rk}\,\mathrm{Pic}(\overline{X_{2}})=2
. Suppose for a contradiction that the Picard rank of
\displaystyle X
is
\displaystyle 2
. By Lemma
1.1
we have that
\displaystyle\mathrm{disc}(\mathrm{Pic}(\overline{X_{2}}))=-16
divides
\displaystyle\mathrm{disc}(\mathrm{Pic}(\overline{X}))
. On the other hand, Lemma
1.1
combined with the result of Elsenhans and Jahnel
[
4
, Theorem 1.4]
implies that we have an isometry
\displaystyle\mathrm{Pic}(\overline{X_{3}})\cong\mathrm{Pic}(\overline{X})
and so we have
\displaystyle\mathrm{disc}(\mathrm{Pic}(\overline{X}))=-9
. This is a contradiction and thus we conclude that we have
\displaystyle\mathrm{rk}\,\mathrm{Pic}(\overline{X})=1
.
∎
