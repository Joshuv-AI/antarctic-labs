Integrative morphometric and molecular evidence of host specialization and cryptic speciation in leaf blister mites (Eriophyes spp.) infesting rosaceous hosts
Authors: Venkata Avinash Addanki, Krzysztof Tomasz Kołątaj, Tobiasz Druciarek, Mariusz Lewandowski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Blister-forming eriophyoid mites of the genus Eriophyes are important pests of rosaceous plants and have traditionally been regarded as polyphagous species with broad host ranges. However, increasing evidence suggests that many eriophyoids represent complexes of cryptic species or host-associated lineages. In this study, we applied an integrative taxonomic approach to investigate the taxonomic status of Eriophyes populations associated with Malus domestica, Pyrus spp., and Sorbus aucuparia. We analyzed mitochondrial COI and nuclear D2 region of 28 S rDNA sequences from multiple populations, together with multivariate morphometric analyses based on 28 quantitative morphological characters. Phylogenetic reconstruction and species delimitation analyses (bPTP) revealed strong host-associated genetic structuring and identified up to seven deeply divergent evolutionary lineages. Mitochondrial COI data resolved multiple host-specific clades with high inter-lineage divergence, while the more conserved nuclear D2 marker supported major host-associated groupings but recovered fewer lineages, indicating mito–nuclear discordance consistent with recent or ongoing divergence. Morphometric analyses detected statistically significant differentiation among host-associated populations; however, extensive overlap in diagnostic characters was observed, particularly between pear- and rowan-associated mites, indicating cryptic speciation. In contrast, mites associated with apple exhibited clearer morphological differentiation consistent with molecular clustering. Our results demonstrate that leaf blister mites associated with rosaceous hosts represent a complex of distinct, host-specialized species. These findings highlight the limitations of morphology-based taxonomy in Eriophyoidea and underscore the need for integrative approaches to accurate species delimitation, with important implications for vector identification and improved pest management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10493-026-01155-6.

Introduction

Eriophyoid mites (Acari: Eriophyoidea) are among the smallest known arthropods, typically measuring around 200 μm in length. They inhabit terrestrial environments and are important pests of cultivated crops because they damage host plants through direct feeding and transmission of plant viruses (Miller et al. 2013; Druciarek and Tzanetakis 2025). These mites feed exclusively on plants and engage in specialized host interactions, ranging from gall and blister formation to a vagrant lifestyle on leaf surfaces, often causing a variety of plant injuries-including the formation of spots, curling or rolling of leaf edges, browning, galls, and russeting (Zhang et al. 2024).

Among eriophyoids, blister-forming mites of the genus Eriophyes are important pests of rosaceous crops, such as apple (Malus domestica and related cultivars), pear (Pyrus communis), quince (Cydonia oblonga), and chokeberry (Aronia spp.), as well as non‑crop deciduous trees and shrubs, including rowan (Sorbus spp.), hawthorn (Crataegus spp.), and various ornamental Cotoneaster species (Jeppson et al. 1975; Sukhareva et al. 2017; Malagnini et al. 2023). These mites induce characteristic blisters on leaves, impairing photosynthesis and, in severe cases, leading to premature fruit drop (Baillod and Höhn 1991; Easterbrook 1996). Among them, the pear blister miteEriophyes pyri(Pagenstecher, 1857) (syn.Phytoptus pyri), is one of the most widespread and economically significant species. E. pyri was initially regarded as a multi-host species infesting pear, apple, and rowan, but subsequent studies suggested potential host-associated divergence among populations from different hosts. E. pyri is also considered a putative vector of European mountain ash ringspot-associated emaravirus (EMARaV, species Emaravirus sorbi; family Fimoviridae) (Mielke and Muehlbach 2007; Mielke-Ehret et al. 2010).

The leaf blister mite on apple, originally described as Eriophyes pyri var. mali Nalepa, 1926, was later described as a new species (Eriophyes mali Burts, 1970) by Burts (1970). However, this name is currently considered a junior homonym of Eriophyes mali Nalepa, 1926 (Vidović et al. 2014). This pest produces symptoms on apple leaves that resemble those caused by the pear blister mite on pear leaves. Similarly, Eriophyes sorbi (Canestrini, 1891) was originally described from rowan, it is regarded by some authors as a synonym of E. pyri (Jeppson et al. 1975; Mielke-Ehret et al. 2010). However, leaf blister mites from rowan in California show biological and morphological differences from E. pyri and E. mali, suggesting that the rowan-associated population may represent a distinct species rather than a synonym of E. pyri (Jeppson et al. 1975). Together, these findings indicate that blister-forming eriophyoids on rosaceous hosts likely represent a complex of closely related lineages or cryptic species.

Despite their economic and ecological significance, the genetic relationships among leaf blister mites infesting rosaceous hosts remain poorly resolved. Molecular data are scarce, and it is unclear whether populations from different hosts represent separate species or conspecific lineages that have diverged through host specialization. As E. pyri was historically treated as a multi-host species, it is possible that previous studies misidentified the actual vector of EMARaV (Jeppson et al. 1975; Mielke-Ehret et al. 2010). The mite commonly referred to as E. pyri in earlier reports may instead correspond to E. sorbi. Therefore, clear species delimitation is essential to accurately determine vector competence and to avoid misattribution of viral transmission.

Morphometrics, a common method for distinguishing species, involves measuring multiple traits across individuals from different populations. While effective, it is time-intensive, requires large sample sizes, and may be limited by overlapping character ranges and developmental stage ambiguity, especially in eriophyoid mites, where deep phylogenies remain difficult to resolve (Fenton 2002). Consequently, integrating molecular and morphological data is essential to resolve their true phylogenetic relationships. The mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COI) gene is the standard molecular marker for arthropod identification and species-level phylogenetics, owing to its faster evolutionary rate, while the nuclear D2 region of the large subunit ribosomal DNA (28 S rDNA), which evolves more slowly, complements COI by providing resolution at deeper phylogenetic levels in Eriophyoidea (Hebert et al. 2003; Dabert 2006; Lewandowski et al. 2014).

In this study, we investigate leaf blister mites (Eriophyes spp.) associated with apple, pear, and rowan to determine whether these populations represent distinct species or conspecific lineages. We combined molecular (COI and D2 markers) and morphometric analyses to assess host-associated divergence among mite populations. Specifically, we address the following research questions: (i) do Eriophyes populations associated with apple, pear, and rowan exhibit host-dependent genetic differentiation? (ii) do Eriophyes populations associated with individual host species (apple, pear, and rowan) represent cases of cryptic speciation, or do they reflect intraspecific genetic divergence? We hypothesize that Eriophyes populations from different hosts are genetically differentiated, reflecting host-driven divergence and possible cryptic speciation. This study contributes to resolving evolutionary history and taxonomic uncertainties in Eriophyes spp. and may help identify the species responsible for transmitting EMARaV. To our knowledge, this is the first integrative comparative analysis (morphometric and molecular) of Eriophyes populations across multiple rosaceous hosts, providing novel insights into host-associated divergence and cryptic diversity within this economically important mite group. Unlike Li et al. (2014), who investigated higher-level phylogenetic relationships and the homoplasy of diagnostic characters across Eriophyoidea, our study focuses on a single leaf blister mite complex on Malus, Pyrus and Sorbus. In this work, we use an integrative framework to test whether these host-associated populations represent distinct, host‑specialised species or conspecific lineages, to identify cases of cryptic speciation within pear- and rowan-associated mites, and to demonstrate the presence of two morphologically and genetically differentiated species on apple.

Materials and methods

Host plants and collection of mites

Leaves of apple, pear, and rowan bearing blister‑like galls caused by eriophyoid mite feeding were collected across multiple locations (Table 1; Fig. 1), placed in plastic bags and transported to the laboratory under ambient conditions. Individual mites from these leaves were collected under the stereomicroscope into separate 0.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes containing 96% ethyl alcohol and stored at −80 °C until further use. Subsequently, about 100 specimens from each sample were also collected and mounted on slides in a modified Berlese medium for microscopic examination (Amrine and Manson 1996; Druciarek et al. 2016).

DNA extraction and amplification

DNA from mites preserved in ethanol for short periods was extracted from single individuals using the Chelex® method (Carew et al. 2004) and a non-destructive direct RT-PCR protocol (Druciarek et al. 2019). For older samples, DNA was likewise extracted from single mites using a column-based approach with the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen), following a modified manufacturer’s protocol (Dabert et al. 2008; Skoracka et al. 2013, 2014).

Two molecular markers were used, a 613 bp fragment of the mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit I gene (COI) and a 540 bp fragment of the nuclear 28 S rDNA (D2 region). DNA amplification was performed using specific primer pairs: COIbcdF01 (5′–CATTTTCHACTAAYCATAARGATATTGG–3′) and COIbcdR04 (5′–TATAAACYTCDGGATGNCCAAAAAA–3′) for COI (Skoracka and Dabert 2010), and D1D2fw2 (5′–ACAAGTACCDTRAGGGAAAGTTG–3′) and 28sr0990 (5′–CCTTGGTCCGTGTTTCAAGAC–3′) for D2 (Sonnenberg et al. 2007; Mironov et al. 2012).

PCR reactions contained 1–6 µL of template, 0.25 µM of each primer (20 µM), and 6.25 or 12.5 µL of Master Mix (Type-it® Microsatellite PCR Kit; Qiagen), with the final volume adjusted using ultrapure water. Amplifications were performed under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min; followed by 30–35 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 50 °C (for COI) and 52 °C (for D2) for 30 s, and elongation at 72 °C for 1 min. Amplicons were visualized on 1% agarose gel, purified, and sequenced bidirectionally using aforementioned primers at the Institute of Biochemistry and Biophysics, Polish Academy of Sciences (IBB PAN), Warsaw, Poland.

Phylogenetic analyses

Sequences of COI and D2 markers were aligned separately in MEGA-X v10.2.6 (Kumar et al. 2018) using Clustal W with default parameters, after which manual adjustments were performed to correct obvious misalignments and refine ambiguous regions. Additionally, Eriophyes sequences associated with the investigated host plants were retrieved from GenBank andincluded in the phylogenetic analyses. Pairwise nucleotide distances for COI and D2 datasets were calculated both within and between groups using the Tajima-Nei (Tajima and Nei 1984) and Kimura 2-parameter (Kimura 1980) models selected in MEGA X, respectively, with rate variation among sites modeled by gamma distribution. Standard errors were estimated through 1000 bootstrap replicates. Codon positions 1 st + 2nd + 3rd and noncoding were considered, and ambiguous sites were removed by pairwise deletion. The COI dataset was rooted using Aceria tosichella Keifer (GenBank accession no. MG194416.1) as the outgroup, whereas Aculops sp. (GenBank accession no. KM111057.1) was used as the outgroup for the D2 dataset. In preliminary analyses we tested the use of a single outgroup (A. tosichella) for both markers, but this resulted in unstable rooting and biologically implausible placements of some ingroup lineages in the D2 tree; the final outgroup choice therefore reflects the combination that produced the most stable and taxonomically consistent topologies for each marker.

Best-fit nucleotide substitution models were determined using jModeltest v2.1.10 (Guindon and Gascuel 2003; Darriba et al. 2012) based on likelihood scores from 88 models and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). The HKY + G model was selected for the COI dataset (BIC = 6292.5498; lnL = − 2711.1012; p = 139; weight = 0.293; partition scheme = 010010), and the HKY + I model for the D2 dataset (BIC = 4322.5776; lnL = − 1803.6395; p = 111; weight = 0.4202; partition scheme = 010010). Bayesian analyses were performed in MrBayes v.3.2.7 (Ronquist et al. 2012), with two independent runs of ten million generations per dataset and tree sampling every 1,000 generations. Runs were terminated once the average split deviation dropped below 0.01. Resulting trees were visualized and edited using FigTree v.1.4.4 (http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree/). Species delimitation was carried out using the Poisson Tree Processes (PTP) model (Zhang et al. 2013) on the bPTP web server (https://species.h-its.org/). Bayesian inference phylogenetic trees generated for both the COI and D2 datasets served as inputs, and the analysis was run under default parameters.

Morphometric analyses

Sample codes of the Eriophyes populations studied morphologically are listed in Table 1. Thirty females from nine populations (three from each host plant genus, separated by molecular analysis into different subclades) were examined in a dorso-ventral position using a phase-contrast microscope. Twenty-eight morphological traits commonly used for species-level identification of eriophyoids were measured for each individual with the Soft Imaging System Cell D, following the procedure described by Lewandowski et al. (2014) and Druciarek et al. (2016).

To evaluate morphological differentiation among populations, a multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) was performed based on 28 quantitative morphological characters measured for each individual. The nine populations represented three host plant species (pear, apple, and rowan). The MANOVA model included population as the main factor and host plant as an additional grouping variable. Although some morphological variables deviated from normal distribution (Shapiro-Wilk test, p < 0.05), MANOVA is robust to moderate non-normality when group sizes are equal; therefore, no data transformation was applied. The analysis was conducted in Statistica 13.3 (TIBCO Software Inc., Palo Alto, CA, USA). To assess the robustness of these results to deviations from multivariate normality, we additionally performed a permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) based on Euclidean distances of standardized morphological variables, with population (Locality) and host plant (Host) as fixed factors. PERMANOVA was conducted in R version 4.5.3 using 999 permutations.

Discriminant analysis (Krzanowski 2000) was used to assess morphological differentiation among individuals from populations associated with each host plant taxon, while accounting for differences in locality and microhabitat. This method accounts for group structure defined by populations and identifies linear combinations of traits that best discriminate between them. Prior to the analysis, all trait values were standardized (mean = 0, variance = 1) to ensure equal weighting of variables. The statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.4.3 (R Core Team 2025). Linear discriminant analysis was conducted using the lda function from the MASS package (version 7.3–65; Venables and Ripley 2002) and the results were visualized with ggplot2 (version 3.5.2; Wickham 2016). To determine which variables most strongly influenced group separation, standardized coefficients (loadings) for LD1 and LD2 were ranked by their absolute values. Voucher specimens of individual mites used for the morphometric study are deposited in the reference collection of the Department of Plant Protection, Institute of Horticultural Sciences, Warsaw University of Life Sciences, Poland.

Results

Molecular analyses

The COI dataset comprised 68 sequences with 524 aligned positions, while the D2 dataset included 54 sequences with 629 aligned positions. The average genetic divergence across all sequence pairs, including outgroup taxa, was 16% and 23% for the COI dataset under the p-distance and Tamura–Nei models, respectively, and 9% and 13% for the D2 dataset under the p-distance and Kimura 2-parameter models, respectively.

COI dataset

Bayesian phylogenetic reconstruction of the COI dataset, with A. tosichella as the outgroup, revealed well-resolved host-associated clades within the Eriophyes spp. complex. All major clades were strongly supported (100% posterior probability) and corresponded to populations from apple, pear, and rowan hosts (Fig. 2). The Malus-associated populations formed two distinct and well-supported clades (Malus 1 and Malus 2). Similarly, Pyrus populations were divided into two clades (Pyrus 1 and Pyrus 2), while Sorbus-associated mites clustered into three well-supported clades (Sorbus 1–3). The bPTP species delimitation analysis identified seven putative species-level clusters that corresponded closely to host associations (Fig. 2). Estimates of evolutionary divergence under the Tamura-Nei model revealed very low mean intra-group (within-clade) variation (0–1%) and high inter-group (between-clade) divergence (16.7–36.2%) among Eriophyes populations associated with apple, pear, and rowan hosts (Table 2). D2 dataset

Bayesian phylogenetic reconstruction of the D2 dataset, using Aculops sp. as the outgroup, revealed that all Eriophyes populations formed two well-supported host-associated clades, each with 100% posterior probability support, broadly corresponding to Malus-, Pyrus-, and Sorbus-associated mites (Fig. 3). The Malus-associated mites were divided into two distinct and well-supported clades (Malus 1 and Malus 2). Likewise, Pyrus populations separated into two clades (Pyrus 1 and Pyrus 2), whereas Sorbus-associated mites formed a single, well-supported clade. This clustering pattern is likely influenced by the limited number of sequences available for the nuclear D2 marker. The bPTP species delimitation analysis identified five putative species-level clusters (Fig. 3), four of which corresponded to the sampled Eriophyes populations, while one included the reference sequence (MW396566.1) from GenBank (NCBI database). Estimates of pairwise genetic divergence (evolutionary divergence) based on the Kimura 2-parameter model revealed very low intra-group (within-clade) variation (0–0.1%). Morphometric analyses

Multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) revealed significant morphological differentiation among Eriophyes populations (Wilks’ Λ = 0.0045, F(168, 1385.15) = 12.41, p < 0.001, η² = 0.60). Both Wilks’ Λ and Pillai’s Trace indicated the same pattern of significance (p < 0.001). To assess the robustness of these results to deviations from multivariate normality, we additionally performed a permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) based on Euclidean distances of standardized morphological variables, with population (Locality) and host plant (Host) as fixed factors. PERMANOVA confirmed a highly significant effect of these factors on multivariate morphology (Model: df = 8, pseudo‑F = 45.93, p = 0.001), which is consistent with the MANOVA outcomes and indicates that the observed patterns of differentiation are robust to violations of normality assumptions. Discriminant analysis based on 28 morphological traits revealed distinct differentiation among host-associated populations (Fig. 4). Most groups were clearly separated along the first linear discriminant axis (LD1), which explained 78.3% of the total variation, with the exception of one population (KK10) associated with apple, which clustered with populations from rowan. Separation along LD1 was primarily driven by the length of anterolateral setae on coxisternum I (setae 1b), the length of the first pair of legs (leg I), and the number of dorsal opisthosomal annuli (Fig. 5A). The second linear discriminant axis (LD2), accounting for 10.8% of the variation, further distinguished the Malus-associated population, KK10, from the remaining groups. Discussion

This study provides integrative evidence that leaf blister mites of the genus Eriophyes associated with rosaceous hosts represent a complex of host-associated, genetically and ecologically differentiated lineages consistent with species-level divergence. By combining morphometric analyses with molecular data we demonstrate consistent genetic structuring linked to host-plant association, accompanied by limited and uneven morphological differentiation. Similar patterns of host-associated divergence have been repeatedly reported in eriophyoid mites (Skoracka et al. 2010, 2015; Miller et al. 2013). Although geographic distance can contribute to genetic structuring, our sampling design (populations of all three host genera distributed across the same geographic area and often occurring at nearby localities) does not suggest a simple pattern of isolation by distance: deeply divergent host-associated clades frequently occur sympatrically or parapatrically, whereas geographically distant populations from the same host genus remain genetically similar. The studied blister mite populations formed two distinct clusters, supported by both molecular markers, with representatives from all three host-associated groups present in each cluster. However, in the nuclear D2 dataset, one cluster lacked representatives of Sorbus. This pattern likely resulted from the lower number of sequences available for the nuclear D2 marker and the absence of sequences from Sorbus-associated populations corresponding to the Sorbus 2 and 3 groups defined in the COI dataset (Figs. 2 and 3).

In our material, most populations were concordantly grouped by both molecular markers; however, representatives of different host-associated groups occasionally clustered together, particularly in the nuclear D2 dataset. This pattern likely reflects the lower resolution of the D2 marker, reduced sampling for some Sorbus-associated lineages, and the absence of D2 sequences corresponding to Sorbus 2 and Sorbus 3 clades recovered with COI. This sampling limitation also resulted in discordance in the number of species inferred by bPTP analysis between mitochondrial and nuclear markers. Rather than treating bPTP as a stand-alone species-discovery method, we use its output as an auxiliary, tree-based criterion that is interpreted in conjunction with the nuclear D2 data, multivariate morphometrics, and host-plant associations. In this integrative framework, the primary evidence for lineage separation comes from the concordance between strongly supported COI and D2 clades, host specificity and, where present, morphological differentiation, while bPTP serves mainly to summarise branch-length patterns in the COI tree. In addition, D2-based analyses clustered one of the sequence subsets, derived from Pyrus- and Malus-associated populations, into a single species, whereas COI-based analyses consistently delimited these subsets as separate species. Such mito–nuclear discordance is consistent with scenarios of early-stage lineage divergence or incomplete lineage sorting, a phenomenon commonly reported in phytophagous arthropods, including eriophyoid mites (Fenton 2002; Skoracka et al. 2015; Xue et al. 2023). Accordingly, these lineages may represent incipient species whose divergence has not yet been fully fixed across the nuclear genome. Our findings of host-associated lineages and mito–nuclear discordance are broadly consistent with the patterns reported for eriophyoid mites on rosaceous hosts by Li et al. (2014). However, our study extends this framework by resolving species limits within a specific blister mite complex on Malus, Pyrus and Sorbus, documenting both cryptic speciation in pear- and rowan-associated populations and clear morphological differentiation between two Malus-associated species. By clarifying the taxonomic status of these lineages, our results provide a concrete basis for future work on the identification of virus vectors and the management of blister mite damage in rosaceous crops. Similar conflicts between mitochondrial and nuclear markers in eriophyoid mites have previously been attributed to recent host shifts, retention of ancestral polymorphism, or incomplete lineage sorting (Skoracka et al. 2012; Miller et al. 2013).

The mitochondrial COI marker revealed seven well‑supported lineages corresponding to host‑associated populations, whereas the more conservative D2 marker resolved fewer lineages but nonetheless supported host‑associated structuring and confirmed deep divergence among the major clades. Mean genetic divergence between the two main clusters was extremely high, ranging from 32.7% to 34.7% for COI and from 28.5% to 30.3% for the nuclear D2 region, while intra‑lineage variation remained minimal. For comparison, most published studies on eriophyoid mites report interspecific COI divergences in the range of approximately 5–20%, with intraspecific values usually below 2% (e.g. Skoracka et al. 2012, 2015, 2018; Lewandowski et al. 2014; Cvrković et al. 2016; Vidović et al. 2022; Anđelković et al. 2025; Malagnini et al. 2023). Thus, the deepest splits in our dataset clearly exceed typical interspecific divergences reported for eriophyoids and are comparable to, or even higher than, divergence levels observed between blister mite lineages and outgroup taxa. Similarly high genetic variability was reported by Lewandowski et al. (2014) for distinct species or lineages of Trisetacus, with values comparable to or higher than estimates of interspecific variation in other mite groups (Anderson and Morgan 2007; Tixier et al. 2008; Dabert et al. 2008). The exceptionally high divergence observed in the highly conserved D2 region suggests an ancient evolutionary origin of these blister mite lineages (Lee and Ó Foighil 2004) and may also reflect accelerated and heterogeneous rates of mitochondrial evolution documented in eriophyoid mites. Consistent with this interpretation, Malagnini et al. (2023) showed that COI sequences submitted to GenBank as Eriophyes spp. on Rosaceae are scattered across the phylogeny and do not form a monophyletic group, and that nominal E. pyri on pear, E. calycobius on Crataegus and ‘apple eriophyoids’ differ by only ~ 84% COI identity (≈ 16–20% corrected distance), again indicating a complex assemblage of deeply divergent lineages within the current concept of Eriophyes on rosaceous hosts.

The molecular analyses strongly corroborated the morphometric results, which revealed significant differentiation among host-associated populations and confirmed earlier observations that eriophyoid mites tend to exhibit subtle but statistically detectable host-related morphological divergence (de Lillo et al. 2010; Lewandowski et al. 2014). Discriminant analysis clearly separated populations associated with apple, pear, and rowan along the primary axes of variation, with the length of setae 1b, leg I, and the number of dorsal opisthosomal annuli contributing most strongly to group separation. However, the extensive overlap in character ranges among host-associated populations, as well as in qualitative characters (e.g. ornamentation of the prodorsal shield), particularly between Pyrus- and Sorbus-associated populations, confirms that morphology alone is insufficient to delimit species within this complex, as previously demonstrated in other cryptic eriophyoid species groups (Skoracka et al. 2012; Vidović et al. 2022). These results strongly support the occurrence of cryptic speciation driven primarily by host association. An exception was observed for Malus-associated populations, for which the results of both analyses were largely consistent. In this case, morphometric analyses revealed clear morphological differences between lineages within the two main clades. These differences involved several quantitative characters (e.g. prodorsal shield width and length, and the number of dorsal and ventral annuli), as well as qualitative features, particularly the ornamentation of the prodorsal shield. These characters suggest that the populations referred to in this study as Malus 1 are morphologically very similar to the Serbian populations identified by Vidović et al. (2014) as E. mali. Unfortunately, linking the Malus-associated lineages identified in the present study with historically described E. mali was not possible due to insufficient morphometric detail in their original descriptions (Canestrini 1891).

The exceptional position of population KK10, which was collected from apple yet clusters morphometrically with rowan-associated mites in the discriminant space (Fig. 4), is unlikely to be explained by sample contamination or host misidentification. All Malus 1 populations, including KK10, were collected from clearly blistered apple leaves, and for each population approximately 100 individuals from galls were slide-mounted and examined, making repeated cross-contamination across five independent samples highly improbable. Instead, we interpret KK10 as a morphologically divergent lineage within the Malus-associated complex. Its placement in the Malus 1 COI clade (Fig. 2) and its occurrence on apple indicate that this lineage is ecologically linked to Malus, while its morphology partly resembles that of Sorbus-associated mites, particularly in the ornamentation of the prodorsal shield and the number of dorsal opisthosomal annuli (Figs. 5 and 6; Table 4). This pattern may result from retention of ancestral traits shared between Malus- and Sorbus-associated lineages, or from convergent evolution of key characters under similar selective pressures on rosaceous hosts, combined with incomplete fixation of diagnostic morphology during early-stage host-associated divergence. In the context of the strong host-associated genetic structuring revealed by both markers, we therefore treat KK10 as an atypical apple-associated lineage rather than evidence against host-driven divergence.

Historically, E. pyri has been regarded as a single polyphagous species infesting multiple rosaceous hosts (Jeppson et al. 1975; Easterbrook 1996). Our findings challenge this view by demonstrating that mites associated with apple, pear, and rowan represent distinct evolutionary lineages, some of which are morphologically cryptic. The presence of two or three cryptic lineages within each host further suggests that host specialization has repeatedly driven diversification within this group. This pattern is consistent with previous studies indicating that eriophyoid mite speciation is more strongly associated with host affinity than with geographic isolation (Oldfield 1996; Skoracka et al. 2010; Xue et al. 2023). The integrative framework applied here highlights the limitations of morphology-based taxonomy in Eriophyoidea and underscore the need to combine molecular and morphometric data to resolve species boundaries (de Lillo et al. 2010; Skoracka et al. 2015). Although crossing experiments and ecological assays were beyond the scope of this study, such approaches remain important for confirming reproductive isolation among cryptic lineages (Anđelković et al. 2025). Precise species delimitation is particularly critical given the putative role of eriophyoid mites as vectors of plant viruses, including European mountain ash ringspot-associated virus (Emaravirus, Fimoviridae), as incorrect species identification may confound epidemiological interpretations (Mielke-Ehret et al. 2010). Although EMARaV has been associated with rowan blister mites in previous studies, we did not directly test host plants or mite samples for the presence of the virus, and our data therefore do not allow us to identify the actual vector lineage. Nevertheless, our COI phylogeny places the lineages KK4, KK5, KK95 (Sorbus 1) in the same clade as the EMARaV‑associated sequence reported by Mielke‑Ehret et al. (2010), suggesting that these rowan‑associated populations may include a primary vector candidate. This hypothesis should be explicitly tested in future work by combining virus detection with lineage‑level identification of mites across the known range of EMARaV.

In conclusion, our results demonstrate that leaf blister mites associated with rosaceous hosts represent a complex of multiple species, including cryptic lineages on pear and rowan, as well as morphologically and genetically distinct species on apple. Collectively, our data indicate the presence of at least seven, host-associated evolutionary lineages, with two deeply divergent lineages occurring on Malus and Pyrus, and likely three lineages associated with Sorbus. These findings provide strong evidence that E. pyri sensu lato does not represent a single polyphagous species and highlight host-driven diversification as a key evolutionary force shaping diversity within Eriophyoidea. However, despite substantially improving our understanding of lineage diversity, our study does not resolve all taxonomic uncertainties related to leaf blister mites inhabiting the investigated host plants; instead, it raises fundamental questions. Most importantly, it remains unclear which of the identified host-associated lineages corresponds to the nominal species E. pyri, E. pseudoinsidiosus Wilson 1965; E. mali, or E. sorbi (Canestrini 1891; Wilson 1965). Addressing this issue is severely constrained by the scarcity of morphometric data in original descriptions, the schematic nature of historical illustrations, and the frequent absence of type material, which collectively render confident species attribution currently unattainable. Resolving these nomenclatural uncertainties, as well as identifying the actual virus vectors among these lineages, will ultimately require separate, dedicated studies that combine expanded molecular sampling with detailed morphology, host-association data, transmission assays and, where appropriate, formal taxonomic actions such as the designation of neotypes for name-bearing species.
