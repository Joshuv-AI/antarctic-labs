Comparison of three commercial sperm freezing media with regard to the influence on sperm motility, vitality and DNA fragmentation
Authors: Emilia Lula Kruschel, Stefanie Steinert, Marta Agnieszka Godlewska, Gabriele Hahn, Katrin Laubis, Nala Luisa Blenck, Andrea Salzbrunn, Armin Soave, Stefan W. Schneider, Kathrein von Kopylow
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSperm cryopreservation is of fundamental importance for the assisted reproductive techniques. To date, there is still no uniform standard for sperm cryopreservation in the reproductive medical laboratories, although the quality of cryopreserved human sperm is strongly influenced by the choice of the freezing medium. Literature on the comparison of commercial sperm cryomedia still shows variable results depending on the study design, the tests conducted, and the different study populations investigated. The aim of our study was therefore to determine which of the three most commonly used commercial cryopreservation media, Sperm Freeze (SF; FertiPro), Sperm Freezing Medium (SFM; Cooper Surgical, Origio), and TEST Yolk Buffer (TYB; Fujifilm, Irvine Scientific), in combination with a slow-freezing protocol, is best suited for freezing ejaculates from infertile patients.MethodsEjaculate leftovers (>1 mL) from 60 patients (age 16–57 years; mean 34.9 years) from the clinical diagnostic of the patients from the Andrological Department at the University Medical Center Hamburg-Eppendorf were collected over a period from November 2021 to May 2023. Each ejaculate rest was then divided into four portions of 250 µL, of which three were each frozen with one of three different commercial sperm cryomedia, SF, SFM and TYB. Frozen ejaculate portions were then thawed and analyzed with regard to the diagnostic sperm parameters motility, vitality and DNA integrity in comparison to the unfrozen (fresh) sample. To determine the motility of the sperm, a spermiogram was performed according to World Health Organization (WHO) criteria. Sperm vitality was examined by means of eosin staining. Sperm DNA integrity was investigated on the basis of a direct DNA fragmentation test [terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) assay] and an indirect DNA fragmentation test [acridine orange (AO) staining]. DNA staining was analyzed applying the automated, microscope-based imaging platform ScanR (Olympus LS).ResultsIn the comparison of the three freezing media, TYB turned out to be the best regarding the sperm motility. In contrast, all three media performed equally in relation to the sperm vitality and preservation of sperm DNA integrity.ConclusionsCompared to SF and SFM, TYB can be recommended as the best medium for the cryopreservation of ejaculates from infertile patients. Nevertheless, since the ejaculates used for the analyses mostly came from infertile patients, results may not be representative for a “normal patient collective” and might therefore not be applicable, i.e., for sperm donors.

Highlight box

Key findings

• In a head-to-head comparison of the three commercial sperm cryopreservation media, Sperm Freeze (SF; FertiPro), Sperm Freezing Medium (SFM; Cooper Surgical, Origio) and TEST Yolk Buffer (TYB; Fujifilm, Irvine Scientific), TYB performed better compared to the two other media in terms of progressive sperm motility.

• All three cryoprotectants demonstrated comparable efficacy in preserving sperm viability and sperm DNA integrity, with the latter being assessed using both [terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick-end labeling assay (TUNEL)] and [acridine orange (AO) staining].

What is known and what is new?

• Quality of cryopreserved sperm is strongly influenced by the choice of the freezing medium. No uniform standard for sperm cryopreservation in the reproductive medical laboratories has been adopted to date.

• In this study, split ejaculates from 60 patients were frozen with three different commercial sperm cryomedia. The samples were analyzed for sperm motility, sperm viability, and sperm DNA fragmentation index, with the latter determined using both a direct test (TUNEL) and an indirect test (AO).

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• TYB can be recommended as commercial cryomedium for freezing ejaculates from infertile patients in connection with slow freezing protocols.

Introduction

Male infertility is a common global problem affecting the reproductive health of men. In a clinical context, male infertility refers to the failure of a partner to become pregnant after 12 months despite regular unprotected sexual intercourse and no evidence of a medical cause in the female partner (1). The number of infertile couples worldwide is estimated at 48 million (2). It is estimated, that 30–50% of infertility overall is attributed to the male factor alone (3,4). A large proportion of male fertility disorders is multifactorial, however, in around one third of the cases, no clear cause can be identified (idiopathic infertility) (5). Furthermore, male infertility has increased in recent decades, which can be observed in the declining ejaculate quality (6,7).

Sperm cryopreservation represents an important technology in dealing with male fertility problems in modern assisted reproductive medicine. First studies about human sperm cryopreservation were carried out as early as the middle of the 20th century (8,9). Nowadays, cryopreserved human sperm are used for a variety of indications, which can be of medical nature, e.g., prior to gonadotoxic therapies in the context of an oncological disease, or of non-medical nature, such as in the context of “social freezing” (10). In addition to the sperm cryopreservation for autologous application, it is also widely used for homologous application (donor semen banking) (11). For the freezing of human sperm, a variety of methods is utilized. Traditionally, programmed or non-programmed slow-freezing protocols are often applied (12-14). To protect the sperm during the freezing process, cryoprotective supplements are added, such as glycerol, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (= permeating agents), saccharides, proteins and antioxidants (= non-permeating agents), with antioxidants combating oxidative stress caused by high concentrations of reactive oxygen species (ROS) (15-17). For safety reasons, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends the use of commercially certified cryomedia, approved for therapeutic application, containing a combination of the substances mentioned above as well as other components, such as buffer systems and amino acids (18). Although recent studies have emphasized that the composition of cryoprotective media and antioxidant supplementation can significantly influence the post-thaw sperm quality, and the WHO recommends the use of commercial media for the cryopreservation of human sperm, there is still no consensus on which medium should be used as the standard (16,19-21).

While determination of motility and vitality are two of the classical sperm parameters, DNA fragmentation analysis is listed by the WHO as one of the extended examinations for ejaculates as a potential clinical marker for male fertility (18,22). The importance of sperm DNA was discovered as early as the 1940s (23). Studies on the relationship between chromatin integrity of human sperm DNA and male infertility were published then in the following years (24), until the first DNA fragmentation test, Sperm Chromatin Structure Assay (SCSA), was developed in the 1980s (25). Meanwhile, there are numerous indications suggesting that sperm DNA integrity plays an important role both in successful fertilization and during pregnancy and that high sperm DNA fragmentation levels correlate with the paternal age (22,26,27). Despite ongoing scientific interest, there is still no standardized method to test the sperm DNA integrity (28,29). The most commonly used method to assess sperm DNA fragmentation is the terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) assay, which enables direct detection of DNA double-strand breaks (30). In contrast, acridine orange (AO) staining constitutes an indirect DNA fragmentation determination method, measuring the susceptibility of sperm DNA after mild acid denaturation (31).

The aim of our study was to determine which of the three most commonly used commercial cryopreservation media, Sperm Freeze (SF) from FertiPro, Sperm Freezing Medium (SFM) from Cooper Surgical, Origio and TEST Yolk Buffer (TYB) from Fujifilm, Irvine Scientific, in combination with a slow-freezing protocol, is best suited for freezing ejaculates from infertile patients in relation to their effect on sperm motility, sperm viability and DNA fragmentation. We present this article in accordance with the TREND reporting checklist (available at https://tau.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tau-2026-0283/rc).

Methods

Patients, ejaculate collection, ethics

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Written consent of the patients for the use of their ejaculate residues for quality control purposes and the use of their anonymized or pseudonymised data for research purposes was obtained as part of the standard procedure in the Andrological Department of the University Medical Center Hamburg-Eppendorf prior to all the analyses. As the collected data were analyzed in anonymized form, the ethical approval was waived by the Ethics Committee of Hamburg Medical Association (Ärztekammer Hamburg, AEKHH). Over the period from November 2021 to May 2023, ejaculate residues from 60 patients of the Andrological Clinic, left over from the diagnostics, were collected and analyzed. Ejaculates with a sperm count of over 3.5 million sperm were included in the study. Samples with an ejaculate volume <1 mL as too small for the cryopreservation tests or with a viscous consistency were excluded. Patient groups included were combined without stratified analysis, since the aim was to analyze a cross-section of the patient groups investigated in the Andrological Section of the Department of Dermatology and Venerology of the University Medical Center Hamburg-Eppendorf. (For two of the patients, clinical diagnostics did not comprise a semen analysis, here clinical tests were restricted to microbiological examination). In all cases, ejaculates were routinely obtained by masturbation.

Cryopreservation of the ejaculate samples

A volume of 1 mL ejaculate was divided equally into four cryopreservation tubes so that each tube contained 250 µL of ejaculate. One of the tubes was processed directly and labeled as “fresh sample” (F). The ejaculates in the remaining three tubes were mixed with 750 µL of one of the three following cryopreservatives: (I) SF (FertiPro, Beernem, Belgium); (II) SFM (Cooper Surgical, Origio, Ballerup, Denmark), and TYB (Fujifilm, Irvine Scientific, Santa Ana, CA, USA). Samples were then briefly vortexed and left to rest for approximately five minutes before being frozen in the programmable freezer Kryo 360-3.3 (Cryotherm GmbH & Co. KG, Kirchen/Sieg, Germany) using a slow-freezing protocol with continuous cooling of the samples down to −125 ℃ within 15 minutes before maintaining this temperature for 40 minutes. Thereafter, samples were stored in a container with liquid nitrogen. In order to avoid individual differences in the execution of the experimental procedures, cryopreservation of all ejaculates as well as the following analyses (with the determination of sperm motility and the eosin staining as the two exceptions) were each carried out by the same person.

Thawing of the ejaculate samples

All samples, except the freshly analyzed portion, were frozen in the gas phase of liquid nitrogen for at least 24 hours. For thawing, samples were removed from the nitrogen container and stored at room temperature (RT) for 15 minutes, followed by further 15 minutes in the incubator at 37 ℃. Spermiogram analysis was performed according to the WHO guidelines, with the evaluation of sperm motility and sperm vitality (18). Thereafter, sperm smears were prepared as described below.

Determination of sperm motility according to WHO criteria

To ensure an even distribution of the sperm, ejaculate samples were first vortexed for approximately two seconds. Using a pipette, 6.5 µL of ejaculate from each sample was placed on a microscope slide, which then was transferred to a hot plate at a temperature of 37 ℃. The drop of ejaculate was covered with an 18 mm × 18 mm glass coverslip. After one minute for the sedimentation of the cells, sperm counting was performed in accordance with the recommendations of the guidelines of the German Medical Association (32). Two duplicate determinations were carried out, with 200 sperm each being counted under a 400× magnification of an Axiostar Zeiss light microscope (Carl Zeiss AG, Oberkochen, Germany). Sperm were differentiated based on the categories according to the WHO definition as: fast progressively motile (motility a, MotA); slow progressively motile (motility b, MotB); non-progressively motile (motility c, MotC); or immotile (motility d, MotD) (18).

Determination of sperm vitality by eosin staining

The sperm vitality test was carried out in accordance with the protocol displayed in the WHO laboratory manual for the examination and processing of human semen (18). After 30 seconds, each slide was viewed under a light microscope where 2×200 sperm were counted with the aid of an electronic counter. The percentage of live and dead sperm was calculated from the values obtained.

Preparation of sperm smears

A volume of 250 µL ejaculate was diluted with 750 µL phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and then filled into a 15 mL centrifuge tube. Samples were then washed in a cooling centrifuge at 300 g and at 25 ℃ for 20 minutes. Supernatant was removed and the sediment was resuspended in 0.1× PBS using a pipette. In the case of a small sediment, this was resuspended in 400 µL PBS, whereas for a larger sediment, 800 µL were used. In each case, 50 µL sediment was spread over an area of approximately 2 cm × 2 cm on a microscope slide according to the WHO standard (18), and left to dry for one hour at RT. As a gentle washing step, slides with the sperm smears were carefully placed in a cuvette containing distilled water for 1 minute before being dried again. Prior to the staining procedure, the 2 cm × 2 cm smears were bordered twice with Fixogum (Acid-free, elastic adhesive; Marabu GmbH & Co. KG, Tamm, Germany) to prevent the staining solution from flowing off the slide during the application.

TUNEL

Ejaculate smears, prepared as described above, were used for TUNEL with the application of the DeadEnd™ Fluorometric TUNEL System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). The staining procedure was carried out according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After the staining, slides were dried within a 37 ℃ incubator and visualized by using the automated microscope-based imaging platform ScanR (Olympus LS, Shinjuku, Japan).

AO staining

The prepared ejaculate smears were also used for the AO staining. Slides with the smears were treated with 300 µl Carnoy solution, consisting of 75% methanol and 25% glacial acetic acid, for 15 min, to partially denature the DNA and make the DNA accessible for the AO solution. Subsequently, samples were washed with distilled water for five minutes. Liquid was then shaken off and the slides were dried for two minutes at 37 ℃ in the incubator. One hundred microliters of a mixture, consisting of 1 mL 0.1 M citric acid, 63 µL 0.2 M disodium hydrogen phosphate, 2.5 µL 1% AO (10 mg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich/Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) and 1 µL Triton X-100, were added to each slide, which was incubated in the dark for 15 minutes. Thereafter, samples were rinsed with distilled water, which was followed by two 2-minute washes. Slides were dried for two to five minutes at 37 ℃ in an incubator and then analyzed using the ScanR imaging platform (see next chapter).

Automated image acquisition and object detection

Fragmented DNA in human sperm heads was detected using the deep-learning, high-content screening imaging platform ScanR, equipped with the ScanR software, package 3.11 (Olympus LS, Shinjuku, Japan), which is routinely used for automated evaluation of the samples in the Department of Andrology at the University Medical Center Hamburg-Eppendorf. For each sample, 100 non-overlapping image fields were acquired in a meandering pattern at 40× magnification using standardized acquisition settings, resulting in the analysis of several thousand spermatozoa per sample. The proportion of fluorescence-positive spermatozoa relative to the total detected sperm population was then calculated automatically to determine the DNA fragmentation index (DFI). The object detection algorithm of ScanR is based on a convolutional neural network (CNN), which was trained in a supervised manner in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. In this process, the CNN learned to recognize the sperm heads based on the bright field images by using the corresponding fluorescence images as ground truth data. This supervised learning paradigm does not require manual annotation. To distinguish spermatozoa from debris and non-cellular particles, CNN-based classification was combined with size- and morphology-based gating, including object area size, circularity, and additional optical descriptors. The gating strategy was iteratively refined to minimize misclassification of non-sperm objects. Thresholds for fluorescence positivity were defined relative to background signal intensity. Sperm heads were classified as TUNEL-positive when fluorescence intensity exceeded background levels by a factor of 1.5, and as AO-positive when fluorescence intensity exceeded background fluorescence by 30%. Algorithm performance was validated by comparison with manually evaluated reference datasets. A subset of samples (not included in this study) was independently evaluated by trained observers using fluorescence microscopy with approximately 2,000 spermatozoa analyzed per sample. Automated detection parameters, including gating settings and fluorescence intensity thresholds, were iteratively optimized to achieve maximal concordance with manual DFI values.

Statistical analysis and Spearman correlation analysis

Prior to the statistical analyses, normal distribution of the values was tested by means of the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test (33). The standard error for each data row was calculated using Social Science Statistics - The Standard Error Calculator (34). For the comparison of the means of the four related groups (values from fresh ejaculates vs. values from ejaculates frozen with the three different cryomedia), repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed (35). To control for multiple comparisons, a Bonferroni correction was applied to the significance level. Significance was assessed at the Bonferroni-corrected threshold of P<0.008 (0.05/6). Spearman correlation analysis was carried out for the investigation of linear and nonlinear dependencies between the values (36). Results of these analyses are each presented with the correlation coefficient “rs” and the corresponding P value (see Tables S1-S3). The initial correlation analyses were performed for the four main parameters examined in this study, TUNEL, AO, sperm viability and motility. These included the values of all the 60 patients (Tables S1,S2). Further correlation analyses were conducted, which included additional andrological parameters (sperm concentration, ejaculate volume, sperm count per mL and sperm morphology from the fresh ejaculate as well as the mean testicular volume) and anthropometric data of the patients (age, body height and body weight) (see Table S3 for the display of the most relevant correlations). With respect to the latter cases, not all values were available for all the 60 patients. Therefore, the number of values available and the number of values included in the correlation analyses is indicated in Table S3.

Better performance of TYB medium in terms of sperm motility

In the comparison of the three cryomedia regarding the spermiogram, sperm after cryopreservation displayed significantly higher values for MotA after freezing in TYB than in the two other cryomedia (SF and SFM) (P≤0.001 and P≤0.005; Figure 2A), whereas the values do not differ significantly when comparing SF with SFM. With regard to MotB, MotC and MotD, however, there were no differences between the three cryomedia (Figure 2B-2D). Freezing the sperm in all three cryomedia resulted in significantly poorer values for MotA, MotB and MotD to the same extent (P≤0.00001) compared to the fresh sample (F) (Figure 2A,2B,2D). With respect to the MotC, there was no significant difference between the fresh and the cryopreserved samples (Figure 2C).

Comparable results of all three media regarding sperm DNA integrity preservation

In both of the assays, TUNEL and AO, group comparison using ANOVA between the samples frozen in SFM compared to the samples frozen in TYB medium showed P values less than 0.05 (Figure S1A,S1B, right side in each case). However, after adjustment of the significance level using the Bonferroni method, the new significance level for 6 tests (0.008) was failed (Figure 3B,3C). As observed for sperm motility and sperm vitality, fresh samples showed superior results compared to the frozen ones, whereas, with regard to AO staining after Bonferroni correction, a significant value was only obtained when comparing the fresh samples and the samples frozen with SFM.

With regard to the distribution of patient values in relation to the reference levels for TUNEL (<20%) and AO (<25%), it should be noted that in the case of TUNEL only 2 of our 60 samples examined showed a value outside this range, corresponding to 3.3% of the patients (Figure 3B). In contrast, substantially more values above the threshold were detected for AO (28/60 values =46.7%) (Figure 3C).

Spearman correlation analyses reveal relationships between sperm vitality, motility, DFI, sperm count, morphology and patient age

Spearman’s correlation analysis using the four main parameters examined in this study (TUNEL, AO, sperm vitality and motility), the additional andrological parameters and the anthropometric data of the patients was applied to investigate possible dependencies. Based on Akoglu’s nomenclature for the assessment of the strength of a correlation indicated by a given/calculated correlation coefficient (37), our analysis for the comparison of the values regarding four main parameters examined in this study (TUNEL, AO, sperm vitality and motility; Table S1) showed no stronger significant relationships than weak correlations (rs≤±0.4). Such weak negative associations were found between sperm vitality (eosin) to the TUNEL test and to the MotC and MotD, while positive associations were found, e.g., to sperm MotA and MotB. The TUNEL test also showed a negative dependency to MotA as well as positive dependencies to MotC and MotD.

Investigation of potential correlations in the comparison of fresh samples with those frozen using the three different cryopreservation media revealed mostly strong (rs≥±0.7) or moderate (rs≥±0.4) dependencies between the fresh samples and those after cryopreservation with all three media in relation to all the four main parameters investigated in this study (Table S2).

To explore potential relationships between the four main parameters and the anthropometric patient data and the additional andrological parameters, further Spearman correlation analyses were performed. According to the patients’ anthropometric values, the strongest correlation observed was a moderately negative association between patient age and sperm vitality (eosin) (Table S3). The other anthropometric data of the patients showed no relevant correlation to the values of the main parameters investigated in this study (not shown). In contrast, significant mostly moderate associations were identified between sperm concentration and sperm motility in fresh ejaculates, specifically, positive correlations to the MotA and the MotB, and negative correlations to the MotC and the MotD. In addition, the sperm count/mL as well as sperm morphology regarding the fresh samples were also positively associated with the MotA and the MotB and negatively associated with the MotC and the MotD.

Additional Spearman correlation analyses were carried out between the anthropometric patient data and the additional andrological parameters determined from the fresh (undiluted) samples of the patients (not shown). Here, positive correlations regarding sperm morphology in the fresh samples were observed to the sperm concentration (rs=0.4021, P=0.002) and the sperm count/mL (rs=0.28102, P=0.03). With regard to the mean of the testicular volume, further two dependencies became visible, a moderate correlation with the sperm concentration (rs=0.41276, P=0.02), and a weak correlation with the sperm count/mL (rs=0.3664, P=0.03).

Discussion

In this work, we compared the three commonly used commercial sperm freezing media, SF, SFM and TYB, in a direct head-to-head comparison in combination with 60 whole ejaculate samples regarding sperm motility, sperm vitality and DNA fragmentation analysis. DNA integrity was examined using TUNEL and AO, with the advantage of the combined use of a direct marker for DNA strand breaks (TUNEL) and an indirect marker for chromatin susceptibility of the sperm DNA (AO). A direct comparison of these three media in combination with the analytical methods used and in combination with ejaculate splits from all the patients included, has not been carried out yet. However, there is an urgent need for research in this area as no standard for the cryopreservation of human sperm including the choice for the cryomedium has been established yet, since no single cryomedium has consistently demonstrated superiority across all sperm parameters (38-40). Literature on the comparison of sperm cryomedia still shows variable results, arising from the differences in sample size, the patient groups studied as well as from the choice of the cryopreservation and analysis methods used (41). With regard to the analytical methods, the parameters investigated in the studies are often different, and even when the same parameters are investigated, they are carried out using different methods, as these are also not standardized (42).

According to Ribas-Maynou et al. [2013], TUNEL represents one of the best tests for predicting male infertility (32). AO staining, like SCSA, evaluates the susceptibility of sperm DNA to acid-induced denaturation and does not rely on fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) analysis, with all its disadvantages (43), since the results are assessed microscopically. Here, we used an automated high-throughput screening system, with the advantage of rapid sample analysis while reliably distinguishing sperm heads from other particles in the sample based on optical properties and object sizes. Unlike FACS, the cells are not subjected to mechanical stress and each data point can be traced back completely to the original photo with the corresponding cell, enabling reproducible and transparent analyses. Using the described approach, our study showed the TYB medium being significantly better to SF and SFM in terms of sperm motility. In contrast, none of the three media performed better in terms of sperm vitality and sperm DNA integrity preservation.

A previous comparison of the slow freezing of sperm with seven commercial cryomedia included the three media tested here (40). However, that study was limited to DNA integrity and TUNEL and the authors did not use portions from the same ejaculate samples in combination with the cryomedia tested, in contrast to our work presented here. Thomson et al. did not identify any of the media tested as the best for preserving the sperm DNA integrity, consistent with the data from our study. Three further studies focused only on two of the three cryomedia tested in our work, with TYB included in all of these cases (38,41,44). Nallella and co-workers as well as Raad and colleagues compared TYB with SFM, while Fabozzi et al. used TYB and SF. In the Fabozzi study, TYB performed better than SF in terms of the sperm motility and the sperm vitality (38). The result regarding the sperm motility fits well with that from our study. However, we found no difference between the media regarding the sperm vitality. This discrepancy could be explained by the fact that Fabozzi, unlike us, examined ejaculates from normozoospermic patients, which was not the case for our study where ejaculates from mostly infertile patients were used. In addition, Fabozzi’s patient population of n=184 is approximately three times larger. Unlike our study, however, this group did not investigate sperm DNA fragmentation. In the works by Nallella and Raad, where 10 subfertile patients and 10 healthy donors vs. 90 infertile and 10 fertile participants were investigated, TYB proved to be better than SFM with regard to the sperm motility (41,44), but in the Raad study only in relation to the infertile and not to the fertile population. These results align with our findings. Sperm vitality was not examined in both of these studies. Raad also analyzed sperm DNA fragmentation, but with a different test, i.e., Sperm chromatin dispersion (SCD), which did not reveal any significant differences between TYB and SFM, a result also matching with our data.

Comparison of the ingredients of the three commercial cryomedia shows slightly differing concentrations of glycerol (TYB 12%, SF 15%, SFM <20%), company websites (accessed on 2/1/2024) (38,40). Regarding the non-permeating cryoprotectants, both, SF and SFM, contain sucrose and human serum albumin. In SF, dextrose monohydrate is additionally included, while glucose and Synthetic Serum Replacement® are further components of SFM. TYB additionally contains fructose and 20% chicken egg yolk. All three media comprise buffer systems, HEPES in the cases of SF and SFM, and TES and the Trizma base buffer in the case of TYB. Various salts and glycine are additional ingredients of SF and SFM, whereas TYB contains the antioxidant sodium citrate. The antibiotic gentamicin sulphate is a further supplement of TYB and SFM, while for SF no antibiotic is added. SFM also includes insulin, probably intended as a sperm survival factor (45). In comparing TYB with self-composed and other commercial cryoprotectants not examined here, authors of previous studies recommended TYB as the “first-choice cryoprotectant” and postulated that the protective of this cryomedium on the spermatozoa might be, firstly, due to its lower concentration of glycerol, since sperm motility decreases with higher glycerol concentrations, and secondly and thirdly could be related to the presence of egg yolk and the antioxidant in the medium (46-51).

Egg yolk contains phospholipids and could therefore minimise the damage to the sperm organelles, important for the sperm motility (47,52-55). Antioxidants can inactivate ROS, a class of substances that in high concentrations can damage the cell structure, including sperm DNA, sperm axoneme and sperm plasma membrane with the latter including polyunsaturated fatty acids, which, when exposed to elevated ROS concentrations, serve as a target for lipid peroxidation (16,56,57). Overall, antioxidants appear to maintain sperm function—including this function post-thaw—and support DNA integrity, since they can reduce the sperm nucleus deproteinization occurring as a result of the sperm cryopreservation (16,19,58). The presence of antioxidant substances in a sperm freezing medium is of particular importance, as sperm have no intracellular ROS defense system due to the scarcity of cytoplasm and a lack of the complete base excision repair system. They are only equipped with the first component of this cellular mechanism, 8-oxoguanine glycosylase (OGG1) (59,60). Due to the presence of the antioxidant, TYB medium might be able to preserve the antioxidant capacity of the sperm better than the other two cryopreservation media examined here, which might be reflected in the better results observed in the samples frozen using TYB. However, as we observed a selective effect of TYB on sperm motility, but not on sperm DNA integrity or sperm viability, the positive effect of TYB may be therefore more likely linked to the lower glycerol concentration or the egg yolk in the medium than to the antioxidant itself. A cumulative effect of all these three components together also cannot be ruled out either. With regard to the clinical relevance of the data presented, freezing ejaculate samples using TYB could especially be beneficial for patients for whom a kind of fertility treatment is planned in which sperm motility plays a role, such as intrauterine insemination (IUI) or in vitro-fertilization (IVF), rather than intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) (61,62).

In our study, as well as in the aforementioned works by Fabozzi and Raad (38,41), whole ejaculate samples containing seminal plasma were used for sperm cryopreservation. From the literature, it is known that the presence of seminal plasma improves sperm survival during cryopreservation (63). However, the composition of seminal plasma varies from patient to patient in terms of its content of immunomodulatory factors, including cytokines and chemokines such as IL6, TNFA and CXCL8 (64-67). Added to this, there is variability regarding composition and concentration of immune factors in the seminal plasma of the same a patient’s ejaculate over the time (67). All these factors may influence the outcome of the study results. The variability of the seminal plasma—specifically regarding immunomodulatory factors—between fertile and infertile men (64) could also explain the differences in the results of the Raad study, as described above. Such differences may affect generalizability of study results, so that results obtained, for example, from a study population consisting primarily of infertile patients—as in our case—cannot be readily extrapolated to normozoospermic patients or sperm donors.

To correlate the results obtained with this mainly infertile male study population, further experiments using ejaculates from fertile patients are needed. Additionally, larger and more homogeneous patient groups—also from infertile patients—should be analyzed to confirm the results for the aspects investigated here. However, the fact that all ejaculate samples were tested for all three cryopreservation media constitutes a strength of our study, as such a strategy minimizes the inter-individual variability thereby improving the comparability between the cryopreservation media.

Beyond the primary outcome measures, correlation analyses were performed to further contextualize potential relationships between semen parameters itself and additionally to the anthropometric factors of the patients. Here we found that high sperm vitality is associated with good sperm motility and low TUNEL values (Table S1) and that sperm vitality is inversely correlated with the patients’ age (Table S3). Recent studies have also shown higher DNA fragmentation indices being significantly associated with sperm vitality, lower progressive motility and poorer semen quality (68). Furthermore, age-related deterioration of sperm quality has been described, with increasing paternal age being connected with higher DNA fragmentation levels and reduced semen quality parameters (69). All other correlations detected between the additional sperm parameters (sperm concentration, sperm count per mL, sperm motility, and sperm morphology in fresh ejaculate) reflect expected and sufficiently published relationships; therefore, further discussion of these results is omitted here.

Conclusions

In summary, our study indicates a better performance of TYB compared to the other two commercial cryomedia with regard to sperm motility. However, no difference between the three cryomedia were found in terms of maintaining sperm vitality and sperm DNA integrity. Based on the experiments conducted in this study, the TYB medium may be more appropriate for freezing ejaculates from infertile patients in combination with slow-freezing than the other two cryomedia examined. The effect of the cryomedia on sperm morphology, however, was not investigated. Future works should therefore include post-thaw sperm morphology analyses to provide more comprehensive evaluation of cryomedia performance and aim at more mechanistic insights to improve the (commercial) sperm cryomedia. The quality of frozen sperm still differs considerably from that of fresh semen, a problem that should be addressed.
