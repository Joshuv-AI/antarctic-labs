What’s worse, obesity or malnutrition in patients undergoing multi-level posterior lumbar instrumentation?
Authors: Aydin Kaghazchi, Janesh Karnati, Ahmed Ashraf, Joshua Yehya Assi, Dylan Fitzpatrick, Andrew Wu, Sruthi Ranganathan, Mikayla Wallace, Rani Nasser, Benjamin Motley, Joseph Cheng, Owoicho Adogwa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Obesity and malnutrition are both associated with worse outcomes after spine surgery, but their relative risks remain unclear. This study compared postoperative complications and reoperation rates after multilevel posterior lumbar instrumentation in malnourished and obese patients.Methods: Adult patients undergoing multi-level posterior lumbar instrumentation for lumbar spinal stenosis or spondylolisthesis were identified in the TriNetX Research Network. Malnutrition was defined as serum albumin <3.5 g/dL and body mass index (BMI) <30 kg/m2. Obesity was defined as BMI of 30 kg/m2 or greater and serum albumin of 3.5 g/dL or greater. Morbid obesity was defined as BMI of 40 kg/m2 or greater with normal albumin. Propensity score matching adjusted for age, sex, race, and comorbidities. Outcomes within 90 days included surgical site infection (SSI), wound dehiscence (WD), and reoperation.Results: In our study, we evaluated 646 malnourished patients who were matched to 646 morbidly obese patients. Malnourished patients had higher odds of SSI or WD than obese/morbidly obese patients, although these differences were not statistically significant. However, malnourished patients had significantly higher odds of reoperation within 90 days than both groups.Conclusion: Malnutrition was associated with similar wound complication rates but higher reoperation risk than obesity and morbid obesity after multi-level posterior lumbar instrumentation. Poor nutritional status appeared to be a stronger predictor of adverse postoperative outcomes than elevated BMI alone.

INTRODUCTION

Obesity, defined as a body mass index (BMI) over 30 kg/m2 (i.e., typically with 2 significant comorbidities), affects over 40% of U.S. adults (2017–2020).[11] In spine surgery, obesity is linked to poorer outcomes (i.e., higher rates of infections, thrombosis, pneumonia, readmissions, and reoperations).[3-5] There is also evidence that malnutrition significantly negatively impacts perioperative surgical outcomes; an estimated 25% of patients are malnourished including up to 42% of elderly patients.[8] Hypoalbuminemia (serum albumin levels <3.5 g/dL), a reliable biomarker of malnutrition, strongly correlates with an increased risk of adverse postoperative outcomes.[1,9] Here, we compared the postoperative wound outcomes, including surgical site infections (SSI) and wound dehiscence (WD), and reoperation rates, between hypoalbuminemic versus obese patients undergoing multilevel posterior lumbar instrumentation fusions for lumbar spinal stenosis or spondylolisthesis.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and data source

This retrospective cohort study used the TriNetX Research Network to identify adults with high BMI or low albumin levels (i.e. within 30 days) who underwent multilevel posterior segmental instrumentation fusions addressing lumbar spinal stenosis and/or spondylolisthesis. Inclusion criteria, exclusion criteria, comorbidity definitions, and outcome codes were identified using the International Classification of Diseases 10 and current procedural terminology codes, as detailed in Supplementary Table 1. Clinical/demographic information

Of the 6,857 patients who met the inclusion criteria, 1,418 were malnourished and 5,439 patients were obese/morbidly obese [Table 2]. In the subgroup analysis, 842 patients were identified as morbidly obese [Table 3]. After propensity score matching (PSM), the malnourished and morbidly obese cohorts each contained 646 evenly matched patients [Table 3].

Statistical analysis

One-to-one PSM was used to compare malnourished versus obese and malnourished versus morbidly obese cohorts. Perioperative outcomes (i.e., postoperative wound complications and reoperation rates) were analyzed using logistic regression models to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), both before and after PSM. Data extraction, matching, and statistical computations used the TriNetX analytical platform.

RESULTS

After PSM, malnourished patients demonstrated 25% higher odds of developing postoperative SSI or WD (OR 1.251, 95% CI [0.900–1.737]) [Table 4]; however, these results did not reach statistical significance. Of the morbidly obese and malnourished cohorts, malnourished patients showed slightly higher odds of SSI or WD (OR 1.027, 95% CI [0.655–1.610]) ([Table 5]; not statistically significant). After PSM, the malnourished cohort exhibited significantly elevated odds of reoperation (OR 1.803, 95% CI [1.407–2.309]) versus both obese [Table 4] and morbidly obese patient groups (OR 1.686, 95% CI [1.212–2.345]) [Table 5].

DISCUSSION

In this propensity-matched analysis of patients undergoing multilevel posterior lumbar fusions, malnutrition was associated with a higher 90-day reoperation risk versus obesity or morbid obesity, while wound complication rates were similar across groups. Prior reports stated that over 20% of morbidly obese patients required revision surgery within 18 months of lumbar spine surgery.[2,6,7,10] Therefore, it appears that malnutrition confers roughly 70% higher odds of reoperation than morbid obesity. These findings, therefore, support routine preoperative nutritional assessment to address malnourishment for patients about to undergo extensive lumbar fusions. Further, it supports the acceptable risk of performing lumbar fusions on obese/morbidly obese patients.

CONCLUSION

This large-scale retrospective analysis, using PSM, demonstrates that malnutrition (serum albumin <3.5 g/dL) is associated with comparable odds of wound complications, but significantly higher odds of reoperation within 90 days versus obesity and morbid obesity in multi-level posterior lumbar instrumentation.
