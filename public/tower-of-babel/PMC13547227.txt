Effect of blended NPSB fertilizer and seeding rates on yield and yield components of bread Wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) in Banja District, Northwestern Ethiopia
Authors: Amare Aleminew, Tessema Miliket
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBread wheat is one of the major staple crops in Ethiopia. However, the production and productivity of the crop are far below the global average, partly due to low soil fertility and poor crop management practices including the use of suboptimal seed and fertilizer rates. Hence, this field experiment was conducted during 2024 main cropping season at Kessa kebele to determine the effects of blended NPSB fertilizer and seed rates on bread wheat productivity in Banja District. Treatments consisted of five levels of blended NPSB fertilizers (0, 50, 100, 150 and 200 kg ha⁻1) and four levels of seed rates (100, 125, 150, and 175 kg ha⁻1), laid out in a Randomized Complete Block Design with three replications. Results revealed that thousand kernel weight was significantly (p < 0.01) affected by the main effects of NPSB fertilizer and seed rates. Plant height, spike length, straw yield, grain yield and dry biomass yield were significantly (p < 0.01) affected by the interaction effects of NPSB fertilizer and seed rates. The tallest plant height, spike length, straw yield and dry biomass yield were obtained from 150 and 200 kg ha⁻1 NPSB fertilizer rates. However, the highest grain yield and harvest index were recorded from the combination of 150 kg ha⁻1 seed rate and 150 kg ha⁻1 NPSB fertilizer rate. Partial budget analysis showed that the highest net benefit (242,919 ETB ha⁻1) with a marginal rate of return of 1148.7% was obtained from this combination. Hence, this combination can be recommended for wheat production in Banja District.Keywords: Blended fertilizer, Bread wheat, Danfie variety, Grain yield, Seed rateSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Plant sciences

Introduction

One of the most significant crop plants in the world is bread wheat (Triticum aestivum L.). It grows in a wide variety of elevations and latitudes1. According to Shahryari et al.2, wheat is a member of the Poaceae family of grasses, which have several-flowered spikelets and alternate opposite sides of the rachis to produce a genuine spike. More than one-third of the world’s population depends on wheat as a staple diet, and it provides more calories and proteins than any other cereal crop3.

With a yield and productivity of 771.64 million metric tons and 3.49 tons ha−1, respectively, wheat is the most important cereal crop in the world and is grown on 221.24 million hectares4. Roughly 27% of the world’s cereal production comes from this crop, which uses roughly 32% of the land used for cereal cultivation. Over a total area of 2.9 million hectares, Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) produces 7.5 million tons5. The most significant wheat-producing countries are Ethiopia, South Africa, Kenya, Tanzania, Nigeria, Zimbabwe, and Zambia.

Ethiopian wheat ranks 31st globally in terms of overall production, even lower than other sub-Saharan countries, because small-scale farmers’ domination depended on rain-fed agriculture and because of their traditional production style6. In Ethiopia, wheat is ranked second in yield after maize, but fourth in area coverage behind teff (Eragrostis teff), maize (Zea mays), and sorghum (Sorghum bicolor)7. The wheat crop occupies 13.91% (1,789,372.23 hectares) of the country’s total grain crop area. Ethiopia’s national average wheat production is roughly 2.97 t ha−1, despite the country’s vast wheat-growing territory7. This is significantly less than the 3.49 t ha−1 global average4.

One of the main cereal crops in the Ethiopian highlands, wheat is produced between 6 and 16 degrees north latitude and 35 and 42 degrees east longitude at elevations between 1500 and 3000 m above sea level. However, the best agro-ecological zones are found between 1900 and 2700 m above sea level8. For wheat to mature properly, the optimal daily temperature ranges from 16 to 25 °C8. Most places with 250–1750 mm of yearly rainfall are suitable for growing the crop9. A variety of soil types, including brown, red, and black clay soils, can be used to grow wheat. Wheat cannot be grown in sandy or water-logging-prone soils. The pH range of 5.5–8.0 is ideal for wheat10. After teff and sorghum, wheat is the third most important cereal crop in the Amhara region, accounting for 15.9% of the region’s land area and 16.5% of its cereal crop production11.

Wheat ranks second in terms of both total production and area covered, making it one of the most important crops farmed in the Awi zone. For the 2021–2022 cropping season, the Awi zone’s total wheat land and yield are 19,124.49 ha−1 and 54,988.12 tons, respectively, with a productivity of 2.88 tons ha−112. The biotic and abiotic problems that have hindered wheat production in these areas include diseases, insect pests, weeds, sowing time, climate, low soil fertility, poor agronomic techniques, and the use of less-than-ideal seed and fertilizer rates13.

For the past thirty years, Ethiopia has mainly used nitrogen (N) and phosphorous (P) fertilizers in the form of urea and di ammonium phosphate (DAP) for practically all crops and soil types14. With the exception of a general recommendation (100 kg urea and 100 kg DAP ha−1), the farmers in the research region had little knowledge about the effects of various fertilizer rates and types. However, the majority of Ethiopian soils lack roughly seven nutrients (N, P, K, S, Cu, Zn, and B), according to the soil fertility map created over 150 districts14. Accordingly, recent research shown that sulfur (S) and other nutritional deficiencies can limit the production of cereals like wheat15,16. Therefore, whether wheat is grown for industrial or consumer use, adding nutrients like N, P, S, and B and using the right seed rate are crucial to increasing wheat production and yield components.

Establishing a homogenous stand with an ideal plant population requires optimal seeding rates. Disease, plant height, and lodging may all rise at stand densities higher than ideal. Additionally, increased planting rates make crops more competitive for shared resources, especially water, nutrients, and sunlight, which lowers yield and quality17. However, using a low seed rate will result in fewer plants per unit area, which will lower yields18. Therefore, determining the ideal seed rate minimizes the risk of inadequate stand density while reducing the risk of overpopulation. To reach its optimum production, the right cultivars and optimal seed rate are crucial19.

Numerous studies conducted by various authors have demonstrated the existence of variations in yield and yield components as a result of seed rate. For example, Amare and Mulatu20 found that a seed rate of 100 kg ha−1 produced the highest number of kernels spike−1 and grain yield, while a seed rate of 150 kg ha−1 produced the lowest number of kernels spike−1 and grain yield. In a similar vein, Rahel and Fekadu21 found that a seed rate of 100 kg ha−1 produced a maximum grain production of 2.78 t ha−1 as opposed to 150 kg ha−1. Higher plant density may result in low grain production because growing grains compete with one another to absorb stored dry matter, causing the grains to fade early and generate low grain spike−122.

Several writers have provided detailed information about NPSB fertilizer rates for bread wheat productivity. Applications of 150 kg ha−1 blended NPSB fertilizer rates produced the highest grain yield of bread wheat23–26, whereas 200 kg ha−1 NPSB fertilizer27,28 and 183 kg ha−1 NPSB fertilizer by Dinkinesh et al.8.

Ethiopia has produced a number of bread wheat cultivars with varying height, maturity, and tillering capacity17. For every variety utilized nationwide, a seed rate of 100 kg ha−1 is advised. However, because to variations in weather, soil type, soil moisture, and types, the seed and fertilizer rate at one site might not be applied at other locations; therefore, additional experiments are required at each site to validate general recommendations29. In a similar vein, farmers in the research region employ varied seed rates. Furthermore, there are no location-specific recommendations for seed and fertilizer rates, and their combined impacts on wheat’s overall agronomic performance have not been thoroughly investigated. In order to determine the impact of blended NPSB fertilizer and seeding rates on bread wheat productivity in Banja District, Northwestern Ethiopia, this study was started.

Materials and methods

Description of the study area

The experiment was carried out in rain-fed conditions in 2024 main cropping season on farmers’ land in Kessa kebele, Banja District Awi Administrative Zone, Amhara National Regional State, Ethiopia. At an elevation of 2560 m above sea level, it is located at latitude 11° 09′60′′ N and longitude 36° 14′60′′ E. This is seen in Fig. 1. The experimental site has average annual rainfall of 1400 mm, as well as minimum and maximum temperatures of 12 °C and 25 °C (Fig. 2). The experimental site’s soil has a pH of 5.8 and is categorized as textural class silt loam.

Planting materials

The planting material was Danfie bread wheat. The cultivar was chosen due to its capacity to adapt to the local agro-ecological conditions. The most prevalent and extensively distributed bread wheat type in the nation and the research region is Danfie. The variety has a potential yield of 3.5–5.5 t ha−1 and a maturity time of 110–145 days30. The fertilizer source was blended NPSB fertilizer rates (18.1% N, 36.1% P2O5, 6.75% S, and 0.71% B)31. All treatments received the same amount of urea, which is a source of nitrogen (46% N).

Experimental procedures and field management

The plots were manually leveled after the trial field was plowed five times with oxen to a fine tilth. After that, a field plan was created, and the experimental units within a block were randomly assigned to each treatment. Drilling was used to hand sow bread wheat seed at the designated treatment seed rate in rows spaced 0.20 m apart. Banja woreda is advised to use 100 kg ha−1 of NPSB fertilizer and 261 kg ha−1 of urea30. At the time of seeding and top-dressing at tillering (38 days after sowing), respectively, 2/3 and 1/3 of 261 kg of urea were applied. Harvesting and threshing were done by hand, and all agronomic practices were followed.

Treatments and experimental design

Five NPSB fertilizer levels (0, 50, 100, 150, and 200 kg ha−1) and four seed rates (100, 125, 150, and 175 kg ha−1) made up the experimental treatments. A total of twenty treatment combinations were set up using Randomized Complete Block Design (RCBD) with three replications in factorial arrangements. Each plot had ten rows and a gross size of 2 m × 3 m (6 m2). The distance between neighboring plots and blocks was 0.5 m and 1 m, respectively. Eight rows of 2.6 m length made up the net plot, which was 1.6 m × 2.6 m (4.16 m2). In order to prevent border effects, the outermost row on each plot’s two sides and the 0.20-m distance between its two ends were regarded as border plants and were not used for data collection.

Data collection

Growth parameters

Plant height (cm): plant height was measured from the soil surface to the tip of the spike (awns excluded) of 10 randomly tagged plants from the net plot area at physiological maturity and the mean value was taken in cm.

Spike length (cm): It was measured from the bottom of the spike to the tip of the spike excluding the awns from 10 randomly tagged spikes from the net plot area and the mean value was taken in cm.

Yield and yield components

Thousand kernels weight (g): thousand kernels weight was determined based on the weight of 1000 kernels sample randomly taken from the grain yield of each net plot counting by hand and weighed with sensitive balance.

Aboveground dry biomass yield (t ha−1): the aboveground dry biomass yield was determined at maturity, whole plants was harvested from each net plot area after sun drying to a constant weight and converted in to t ha−1.

Straw yield (t ha−1): Straw yield was obtained as the difference of the total aboveground dry biomass and grain yield.

Grain yield (t ha−1): grain yield at harvest maturity, whole plants from the central seven rows of net plot area was harvested and threshed manually. The grain yield was adjusted to 12.5% moisture content. Finally, yield per plot was converted to per hectare basis and the average yield was reported in t ha−1.

Harvest index (%): harvest index was calculated as the ratio of grain yield to total aboveground dry biomass yield, expressed in percent.

Soil sampling and analysis

Before the start of planting, soil samples were taken at a depth of 0–20 cm from 20 locations throughout the experimental field using an auger in a zigzag pattern. After that, the samples were combined into one. Before being submitted to the soil testing laboratory at the Debre Marcos Agricultural Research Center (DARC) for analysis, the collected samples were allowed to air dry at room temperature in the shade and crushed through a 2 mm sieve. To measure the amount of total nitrogen and organic carbon (OC), the soil was ground up such that it could pass through a 0.2 mm filter. A working sample of one kilogram was extracted from the produced composite sample in accordance with standard laboratory procedures, and it was analyzed for several physico-chemical properties, mainly soil texture, soil pH, cation exchangeable capacity (CEC), total N, available P, available S, and available B.

The soil’s texture was ascertained using the Bouyoucos hydrometer method32. The OC of the soil was ascertained using the wet digestion method33. Total N was determined using the Kjeldhal method34. The pH of the soil was measured at a 1:2.5 soil to water dilution ratio using a glass electrode coupled to a digital pH meter35. The CEC was calculated when the soil was saturated with 1N ammonium acetate (NH4OAC) and displaced with 1N NaOAC36. The available P was determined using the Bray method37, the available S was determined using the turbid metric approach38, and the available B was evaluated using the hot water method39.

Soil physico-chemical properties of the experimental site before planting

The analysis of the experimental soil showed that it was in the silt loam textural class, with a particle size distribution of 32% sandy, 50% silt, and 18% clay (Table 1). Consequently, the soil at the experimental site had good water and nutrient retention. Wheat is grown in a variety of soil types, from rich, well-drained soils to damp, thick Vertisols, according to Hailu40. As a result, the soil at the experimental site is ideal for producing wheat. The pH of the soil was 5.86, which is regarded as slightly acidic. The 5.2 is very acidic, 5.3–5.9 is moderately acidic, 6.0–6.6 is slightly acidic, 6.7–7.3 is neutral, 7.4–8.0 is moderately alkaline, and > 8.0 is strongly alkaline, according to Tekalign41. The majority of crops and productive soils prefer a pH range of 5.5–8, according to FAO42. The optimal pH ranges for wheat production are 4.1–7.4, according to Mengel and Kirkby43. Thus, the pH of the experimental soil proved suitable for wheat cultivation. Tekalign41 classified soils with an OC value of less than 0.5% as extremely low, 0.5–1.5% as low, 1.5–3.0% as moderate, and > 3.0% as high, indicating the soil’s great capacity to provide N to plants through OC mineralization. By his standards, the soil at the research site had a high OC level of 4.98% (Table 1). The substantial amount of soil organic carbon may be due to the abundance of crop remnants because smallholder farmers in the research area do not consume the biomass of wheat for animal feed. The cation exchange capacity (CEC), a critical soil property, indicates the type of clay mineral present in the soil and its potential to retain nutrients against leaching.

According to Landon44, topsoil with a CEC of more than 40 cmol (+) kg−1 was categorized as very high, 25–40 cmol (+) kg−1 as high, 15–25 cmol (+) kg−1 as medium, 5–15 cmol (+) kg−1 as low, and less than 5 cmol (+) kg−1 as very low. In general, soils with high CEC levels are thought to be productive for agriculture. As demonstrated by their high CEC of 38.04 cmol (+) kg−1, this categorization indicated that the site’s soils have a high capacity to retain cations. The study site’s soil had a moderate total N concentration of 0.24% (Table 1), indicating that the nutrient was a limiting factor for wheat production in the study location, according to Tekalign41, who classified soils with a total N value in the range of 0.25% as high. The research showed that the available P in the soil was 15.1 mg kg−1 (Table 1). Indicative ranges of available P were determined by Olsen et al.37 as follows: < 5 mg kg−1 is very low, 5–9 mg kg−1 is low, 10–17 mg kg−1 is medium, 18–25 mg kg−1 is moderate, and > 25 mg kg−1 is extremely high. Therefore, the soil at the experimental site was considered a limiting factor for crop growth and a medium in accessible P.

The soil’s sulfur (S) level was found to be 18.95 mg kg−1 (Table 1). The soil available S rating is as follows, according to EthioSIS14: less than 10 mg kg-1 (very low), 10–20 mg kg−1 (low), 20–80 mg kg−1 (mid), 80–100 mg kg−1 (high), and more than 100 mg kg−1 (very high). Consequently, it was found that the soil at the experimental site had a low accessible S concentration, which was insufficient to optimize crop output. As a result, applying fertilizer made from S is essential for productive and healthy crop growth. The available boron (B) in the soil was just 0.79 mg kg−1, as Table 1 demonstrates. Very low (less than 0.5 mg kg−1), low (0.5–0.8 mg kg−1), medium (0.8–2.0 mg kg−1), high (2.0–4.0 mg kg−1), and very high (more than 4.0 mg kg−1) are the categories in the EthioSIS45 soil rating system. As a result, it was determined that the soil at the experimental site had a low available B content.

Partial budget analysis

To find the economically feasible NPSB rates with seed rates, a partial budget analysis was carried out using the CIMMYT technique46. Ethiopian Birr (ETB) per hectare was used to calculate all costs and benefits. The budget was used to partially evaluate the mean grain data after a 10% downward modification.

The gross field benefit (GFB) (ETB ha−1) was computed by multiplying the field/farm gate price that farmers receive for the crop they sell as adjusted yield using the formula GFB = AGY* field/farm gate price for the crop. The total variable cost (TVC) (ETB ha−1) was calculated by adding the variable costs, such as seed, NPSB, and application costs. Seed cost of bread wheat was 120 birr per kilogram47, while NPSB fertilizer cost was 40 birr per kilogram. The labor costs for applying NPSB (5 people ha−1 each 300 ETB day−1) and seed (5 people ha−1 each 300 ETB day−1) were considered.

The Tillili market sold bread wheat grain for Birr 60 kg−1, but the yield of straws was Birr 0.5 kg−147. The net benefit (NB) was calculated as the difference between the gross benefit and the total cost that fluctuates (TVC) using the formula NB = GFB–TVC. The percentage marginal rate of return (MRR) for each pair of ranked treatments was calculated using the formula MRR (%) = ∆NB divided by ∆TVC times 100.

The dominance analysis procedure described in CIMMYT46 was used to choose potentially profitable therapies from the analyzed range. The approaches used to choose and reject the therapies were referred to as dominant and non-dominated procedures, respectively. To find a potential recommendation, non-dominated treatments were employed. The treatment that produced the greatest net benefit and a marginal rate of return more than the lowest amount that farmers could accept (100%) was then taken into account for recommendation, according to CIMMYT46.

Data analysis

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed on the data using the statistical analysis system (SAS) software version 9.0. The least significant difference (LSD) test was used at the 5% level of significance to compare treatment means that differed significantly for measure characters.

Harvest index (%)

The analysis of variance revealed that harvest index was highly significantly (p < 0.01) affected by the main effects of NPSB fertilizer and seed rates as well as their interactions (Table 8). The highest harvest index (57.21%) was obtained from the combination of 150 kg NPSB ha−1 with 150 kg ha−1 seed rates. However, the lowest harvest index (39.91%) was recorded from the combination of control NPSB fertilizer rates with 100 kg ha−1 seed rates (Table 8). In overall, the 2024 cropping season is better suited for the site’s harvest index yield requirements for bread wheat production.

Discussion

Effects of NPSB fertilizer and seed rates on growth parameters of bread wheat

Plant height (cm)

The increasing effect on plant height with higher NPSB fertilizer rates could be attributed to its greater contribution to wheat vegetative growth and development and increasing trends of photosynthetic rates. This result is consistent with the finding of Gizachew et al.48, who reported that increased plant height due to increasing NPSB fertilizer rates ranged from 0 to 200 kg ha−1. Similarly, Amsale et al.49 reported an increase in plant height with increasing NPS fertilizer rate. Furthermore, Brzegen et al.50 reported longer plant height in response to increased application of blended fertilizer.

In this study, the tallest plant at the highest seed rate may be greater intraspecific competition between plants for light and space, which may encourage elongation of the stem. In agreement with this result, Girma et al.51 obtained that as the seed rate increased from 100 to 150 kg ha−1, the height of the plant correspondingly increased. Similarly, Brzegen et al.50 and Bezabih52 reported a significant difference in plant height with variable seed rates. Additionally, Tewodros et al.53 reported an increase in plant height when a seed rate increased from 100–175 kg ha−1. However, in contrast to this result, Jemal et al.17 reported that with increasing seed rate the plant height was declined. Similarly, Rahel and Fekadu21 reported a decreasing plant height of wheat when the seeding rate increased from 75 to 150 kg ha−1. The experimental site soil had low amounts of boron and sulfur and moderate quantities of N and P. Furthermore, the study’s longest plant height was attained because the 2024 cropping season was better suited for bread wheat production.

Spike length (cm)

The increase in spike length with NPSB fertilizer rate might have resulted from improved root growth and increased uptake of nutrients, better growth due to the synergetic effect of the four nutrients, and availability of adequate crop nutrients at the highest rate and better increment of photosynthetic rates. This result is in line with the finding of Firehiwot54, who reported that the maximum spike length from the combined application of N and P fertilizers sources. Similarly, Gizachew et al.48 reported that increased spike length with increased NPSB fertilizer rates. In addition, Amsale et al.49 and Girma et al.51 reported that the longest spike length was obtained at the highest NPS fertilizer rates. The result revealed that increasing seed rates resulted in a decreased spike length.

In the same way, the increasing spike length with a lower seeding rate might be due to the availability of resources with less competition for growth and development. The present result is in line with the finding of Rahel and Fekadu21, who reported a shorter spike length in wheat as the seed rate increases from 75 to 150 kg ha−1. A similar inverse relationship of spike length with the increasing or decreasing seeding rates has also been reported elsewhere17,20,50,51. The soil at the experimental site had low amounts of boron and sulfur and moderate levels of N and P. Furthermore, the study’s longest spike length was attained because the 2024 cropping season was better suited for bread wheat productivity.

Effects of NPSB fertilizer and seed rates on yield parameters of bread wheat

Thousand kernels weight (g)

Thousand kernels weight obtained from fertilized plots was significantly higher than unfertilized plots. This might be the balanced application of nutrients has enhanced the accumulation of assimilating in the grains, and thus resulting in heavier grains of wheat. Moreover, adequate and better amount of nutrients of the plants resulted in good grain filling and development of better seed size and better increment of photosynthetic rates. In line with this result, Usman et al.55 reported that the highest thousand kernels weight from NPSB fertilizer rate of 150 kg ha−1. Similarly, Amsale et al.49 showed a significant effect of NPS application on thousand seeds weight on wheat. Consistent with these results, several authors reported an increase in thousand kernels weight due to the application of NPSB fertilizer in wheat8,48.

Thousand kernels weight increased with increasing seed rates up to 150 kg ha−1 and then showed a decreasing trend beyond this seed rate. High seed rates caused to increasing total number of tillers and as a result competition increase and little photosynthesis would be available to grain filling and finally thousand kernels weight reduced. In agreement with this result, Rahel and Fekadu21 and Tewodros et al.53 reported a maximum thousand kernels weight from a seed rate of 125 kg ha−1 and increasing seeding and NPSB fertilizer rates application resulted in increased kernels spike−1, thousand kernels weight, grain yield, and harvest index in bread wheat56. The soil at the experimental site had low amounts of boron and sulfur and moderate levels of N and P. Additionally, the 2024 cropping season was better suited for bread wheat productivity, which is why the heaviest thousand kernel weight was found in this study.

Above-ground dry biomass yield (t ha−1)

In general, the increase in NPSB fertilizer rates has led to an improvement in above-ground dry biomass yield, which may be due to improved root growth and crop nutrient uptake and by increasing photosynthetic rates. The result is in line with the finding of Abebaw and Hirpa57, who reported higher above-ground dry biomass yield due to higher NPSB fertilizer rate in bread wheat. Similarly, many other researchers have reported an increase in above-ground dry biomass production with application of essential plant nutrients8,48,50,58,59.

Regarding to seed rates, the increasing seed rates from 150 to 175 kg ha−1 resulted in an increased above-ground dry biomass yield mainly due to higher plant populations per unit area. In confirming with this result, Wahid and Al-Hilfy60 reported that the highest above-ground dry biomass yield was produced from wheat plants sown at the seed rate of 175 kg ha−1, while the lowest above-ground dry biomass was obtained from 100 kg ha−1 seed rate. Similarly, Girma et al.51 reported that the highest above-ground dry biomass yield was obtained from 150 kg ha−1 seed rate in wheat. Furthermore, Brzegen et al.50 reported that above ground dry biomass yield was significantly affected by variable seed rates in wheat. In contrast, there are reports which indicate an increasing above-ground dry biomass yield as seed rates decreases20,21. The soil at the experimental site had low amounts of boron and sulfur and moderate levels of N and P. Furthermore, this study’s best above-ground dry biomass results were attained since the 2024 cropping season was better suited for bread wheat productivity.

Straw yield (t ha−1)

The significant increase in straw yield in response to the highest rate of application of NPSB fertilizer might be attributed to the synergistic roles of the four nutrients played in enhancing the growth and development by increasing photosynthetic rates of the vegetative part of the crop. In confirming this result, Abebaw and Hirpa57 reported that the higher straw yield by the application of 150 kg ha−1 NPSB fertilizer rate in bread wheat. Similarly, many other researchers have reported an increase in straw yield production with application of essential plant nutrients48–50,58.

Generally, the increase in straw yield with higher seed rates resulted in more plant population per unit area. In confirming this result, Brzegen et al.50 reported that maximum straw yield was recorded from the higher seed rate whereas the minimum straw yield was recorded from the lower seed rate. Similarly, Wahid and Al-Hilfy60 reported that the highest straw yield was produced from plants sown at the seed rate of 175 kg ha−1. The soil at the experimental site had low amounts of boron and sulfur and moderate levels of N and P. Additionally, the 2024 cropping season was better suited for bread wheat productivity, which led to the maximum straw production in this study.

Grain yield (t ha−1)

Grain yield increased with increasing seed rate up to 150 kg ha−1 and then showed a decreasing trend beyond this seed rate (Table 7). Such an increase in grain yield is associated with the improved yield components, such as the number of effective tillers and thousand kernels weight due to the seed rate increment up to the optimal. The highest grain yield at the highest NPSB fertilizer rates might have resulted from improved root growth, nutrient availability, increased photosynthetic rates and increased uptake of nutrients due to the synergistic effect of the four essential nutrients thereby leading to enhanced yield components and yield. This result, in line with Habtamu et al.61 showed that the highest grain yield was obtained at the combination of optimum amount of seed rate with NPSB fertilizer rates in food barley. Similarly, Shah et al.62 reported maximum grain yield from the combination of 120 kg seed with 120 kg N ha−1 fertilizer rates in wheat. Debnath et al.63,64 also reported highest grain yield due to optimal seed and blended fertilizer combinations in wheat. However, in contrast to this result, Girma et al.51 and Brzegen et al.50, reported the highest grain yield from the combination of highest seed and NPS fertilizer rates in wheat. The soil at the experimental site had low amounts of sulfur and boron but moderate quantities of N and P. Furthermore, the 2024 cropping season was better suited for bread wheat productivity, which resulted in the maximum grain production in this study.

Harvest index (%)

The increment in harvest index with NPSB fertilizer application might be due to greater photo assimilates production and it’s more partitioning to the grains compared to the straw. This result is in line with Debnath et al.63 who recorded highest harvest index from the combination of optimal seed and blended fertilizer rates of wheat. In contrast to this result, Brzegen et al.50 revealed the maximum harvest index from the combination of highest seeding and NPS fertilizer rates in bread wheat. The experimental site’s soil had moderate levels of N and P but low levels of sulfur and boron. Additionally, because the 2024 planting season was better suited for bread wheat productivity, this study’s highest harvest index was achieved.

Partial budget analysis

The minimum acceptable marginal rate of return should be greater than 100%, as suggested by CIMMYT46. The study’s maximum net economic benefit (242,919 ETB ha−1) and marginal rate of return (1148.7%) were obtained by applying 150 kg NPSB ha−1 with 150 kg seed rates (Table 9). Hence, combinations of 150 kg ha−1 NPSB fertilizer and 150 kg ha−1 seed rates are profitable in this study. Therefore, this combination can be recommended for wheat production in Banja District and areas sharing similar agro-ecology.

Conclusions

In Ethiopia, smallholder farmers primarily grow wheat for domestic consumption and the market, making it a crucial crop for food security and a key cereal crop worldwide. However, abiotic factors—specifically, suboptimal seed and fertilizer rates—keep productivity below the global average. Thus, in the Banja District of Northwestern Ethiopia, this study assessed the impact of mixed NPSB fertilizer and seed rates on bread wheat productivity. This study’s primary limitation was that it was conducted in a single site and season; many locations and seasons are required. However, the study offers new insights on how to set up NPSB fertilizer and seed rates for bread wheat productivity in the future.

The findings indicated that the main effects of NPSB fertilizer and seed rates had a significant impact on thousand kernel weight, while their interaction had a significant impact on plant height, spike length, biomass production, straw yield, grain yield, and harvest index. The combination of 150 kg ha⁻1 seed rate with 150 kg ha⁻1 NPSB fertilizer produced the maximum grain yield and harvest index, while 100 kg ha⁻1 seed rate without fertilizer produced the lowest results. According to partial budget analysis, this combination produced the highest marginal rate of return (1148.7%) and net benefit (242,919 ETB ha⁻1). Therefore, until more multi-location and multi-season research is conducted, it is recommended to utilize 150 kg ha⁻1 of seed and 150 kg ha⁻1 of NPSB fertilizer.
