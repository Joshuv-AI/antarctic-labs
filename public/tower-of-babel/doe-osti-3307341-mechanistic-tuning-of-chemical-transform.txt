EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
The need to diversify approaches to produce essential energy carriers such as H2 motivate advances in
thermodynamically downhill geo-inspired pathways. Currently, more than 80% of hydrogen is produced
via steam methane reforming (SMR) followed by water gas shift reaction (WGSR) pathway with the
co-production of CO2. As an alternative to introducing CO2 separation strategies downstream such as
the use of membranes, solvents, or sorbents, geo-inspired carbon mineralization is harnessed as an
alternative crystallization pathway. The thermodynamically downhill carbon mineralization pathways
are hypothesized to accelerate H2 conversion while separating CO2. The work conducted through this
project discusses the mechanisms associated with coupling the water gas shift reaction (WGSR) with
carbon mineralization. In addition to harnessing Ca- and Mg-bearing oxides or hydroxides that are
known to be reactive for CO2 capture, the use of earth abundant silicate minerals such as Ca- and Mg-
bearing silicates, is also investigated. Key outcomes include approaches to architect Mg-silicate with
well-controlled pore size distributions, probing the enhancement in H2 yield with inherent CO2
suppression using Ca- and Mg-bearing oxides, hydroxides, and silicates, elucidating the changes in
silicate chemistry during carbon mineralization, and exploring direct integration of carbon
mineralization with WGSR and the development of separate low temperature pathway for reactive CO2
capture and mineralization are developed.

 CHAPTER 1

 INTRODUCTION

 The need to meet our energy needs while limiting detrimental environmental impacts motivates

transformative chemical pathways.1,2,3,4–7 In particular, pathways that can convert CO2 emissions into

valuable co-products while co-producing energy carriers enable us to address emissions through

innovation.8 Hydrogen possesses several characteristics that make it one of the most promising energy

sources. First, hydrogen fuel has a notably high gravimetric energy density (~ 120 MJ/kg), nearly three-

fold that of traditional fossil fuels like gasoline or diesel.9 Further, hydrogen fuel combustion produces

water as the exclusive byproduct and minimizes CO2 emission. Moreover, the elemental abundance of

hydrogen also maximizes its potential for substantial energy supply.10 As a secondary energy carrier,

hydrogen fuel can be extracted from renewable sources such as water and biomass, and fossil resources

such as natural gas or coal via reforming, electrolysis, or pyrolysis approaches, respectively.11

 Industrially, the most widely used hydrogen-producing approach is steam methane reforming

(CH4 + H2O → CO + 3H2 206.6 kJ/mol) followed by water-gas shift reaction (CO + H2O → CO2 + H2 -41.1

kJ/mol) to reduce the undesired CO level and enhance the hydrogen yields.12 The exothermic nature of the

water gas shift reaction (WGSR) makes it a perfect supplement for large-scale steam methane reforming

(SMR) to improve the overall energy efficiency and sustainability of the integrated system. However, this

exothermic process is thermodynamically favored at lower temperatures, which kinetically suppresses the

reaction due to weaker molecular thermal motion. Besides, associative mechanisms can occur during the

WGSR, especially in the lower temperature regimes (200 - 300 °C), resulting in the formation of side

products such as methane or formate.13,14 Hence, the adoption of efficient catalysts is significant in lowering

the activation energy barrier and enhancing the selectivity for desired products in low-temperature WGSR,

which potentially accelerates the reaction rate and avoids side product generation. Typically, non-noble

metal based (Fe-Cr, Cu, Ni) and noble metal based (Pt, Au, Rh, Ru, Pd) catalysts are applied for WGSR

proceeding in different conditions.14–20 Though the non-noble metal based catalysts are widely used due to

                                              Page 13 of 210

the ideal cost effectiveness and good activity at relevant temperatures, some limitations have been reported,

including deactivation at specific temperature regimes, intolerance to chemisorption and poisoning, and

poor selectivity for hydrogen production.21–23 Thus, noble-metal catalysts, especially Pt catalysts, are

considered as promising candidates for low-temperature WGSR (200 - 300 °C) due to their remarkable

activity, stability, and selectivity.24–26 Additionally, the application of Pt catalysts in WGSR is also capable

of promoting the decomposition of formate intermediate and enhancing the hydrogen yields further. 27

However, the co-generation of H2 and CO2 from WGSR always leads to impurity in the gas effluent, which

causes extra CO2 emission and suppresses H2 yields. To overcome these issues, ongoing research is focused

on optimizing reaction conditions and minimizing product impurities. To this end, promising approaches to

capture and store CO2 post or integrate with H2 recovery via WGSR are required. The post-H2 recovery

carbon capture refers to a decoupled pathway separating CO2 from the gas effluent of WGSR, while the

integrated carbon capture occurs simultaneously with WGSR in a single-step reactor.

 Over the past decades, carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) has been proven to be an

effective approach for global CO2 level reduction and necessary industrial decarbonization.28,29 As one of

the most favored CCUS approaches, carbon mineralization refers to a thermodynamically downhill route

transferring CO2 into solid inorganic carbonates.30 This approach demonstrates multiple advantages in CO2

removal, including the cost-effectiveness of raw materials, large carbon storage potential, the spontaneity

of reaction, remarkable product safety, and capability for long-term storage.31 As one of the decisive factors

in carbon mineralization performance, the selection of starting materials requires careful consideration.

Practically, the starting materials consist of silicate materials with extensive abundance like forsterite

(Mg2SiO4), olivine ((Mg, Fe)2SiO4), serpentine ((Mg, Fe)3Si2O5(OH)4), or wollastonite (CaSiO3), hydroxide

materials with remarkable reactivity like brucite (Mg(OH)2) or portlandite (Ca(OH)2), and repurposed

industrial alkaline waste like steel slag, fly ash, or cement kiln dust.32–41 Given the surface reaction nature

of carbon mineralization, the structural and morphological arrangements of the unreacted sorbents can

significantly impact the process.42 Generally, unified particle size distributions with large specific surface

                                               Page 14 of 210

areas are desired to achieve great carbon mineralization extent at specific conditions, which favor the

application of architected materials with tunable morphology.43–45 Therefore, advancing straightforward

synthesis approaches for carbon sorbents with tailored compositions and morphologies is critically

important. With these empirically validated carbon sorbents, carbon mineralization can be conducted via

in-situ and ex-situ approaches, respectively.46 Specifically, in-situ carbon mineralization refers to the

process by which CO2 is transported into subsurface geological structures via fluid flow and reacts with

minerals under naturally occurring temperatures and pressures. This pathway allows permanent large-scale

carbon storage with very low maintenance and enhanced oil recovery potential.47 On the other hand, ex-situ

carbon mineralization realizes engineered carbon removal with well-controlled reactant compositions,

temperatures, and pressures, which maximize the carbon capture potential and tailor the formation of

carbonate-bearing products.30 Despite the favored thermodynamics of the engineered carbon

mineralization, the sluggish kinetics of the direct gas-solid reaction usually require notably high

temperature and CO2 partial pressure to achieve an optimal reaction rate, which lowers the energy efficiency

and constrains the further expansion of the approach.48–52 Hence, the gas-liquid-solid configuration is

developed as an optimized strategy. The presence of aqueous media significantly accelerates the carbon

mineralization kinetics by enhancing the ionic strength and mass transfer to facilitate the formation of

carbonate-bearing solids.53,54 Further, the addition of different solutes like bicarbonate salts or amino acid

salts to the aqueous phase is applicable to create favored pH levels and introduce unique CO 2 capture

mechanisms, which either enhance metal ion extraction or CO2 dissolution.43,55–60 As a subsequent

derivation, naturally occurring solutions like brine or saline wastewater have also been investigated to

advance more cost-effective and environmentally benign approaches due to their natural abundance and

availability.61–64 Considering the widespread use of aqueous media in carbon mineralization, the dissolution

behaviors of the carbon sorbent can greatly impact the metal ion level in the aqueous phase, which plays an

important role in the subsequent carbonate formation.65 Though the mechanisms and performance of

aqueous-accelerated carbon mineralization have been well illustrated, its interaction with WGSR remains

ambiguous.

                                              Page 15 of 210

 Due to the inevitable formation of associated CO2 as a product from WGSR, the integrated WGSR

with carbon removal approaches has been reported to ensure high-purity H2 production.66 The so-called

enhanced WGSRs are conducted based on Le Chatelier’s principle, where the in-situ removal of CO2 shifts

the reaction equilibrium towards the product side, resulting in enhanced H2 yields.67 However, the majority

of pertinent research has been concentrated on the gas-solid carbon absorption in fluidized-bed reactors,

which allows the continuous operating and ease scaling up of the system but suffers relatively limited

kinetic from the direct gas-solid reaction nature.68–71 Conversely, the configuration of enhanced WGSR with

aqueous-phase carbon mineralization in a single-step reactor is seldom reported, where the aqueous phase

can act as a steam resource for WGSR and a reaction media for carbonate formation simultaneously. It is

also worth noting that a hydroxyl (OH-)-driven formate decomposition mechanism in the aqueous phase

has been reported, emphasizing the significance of aqueous participation in H2 yield enhancement via the

enhanced WGSR.72,73

 Given the current research challenges in providing mechanistic insights in post H2 production

carbon removal and the knowledge gap in enhanced WGSR integrated with aqueous-phase carbon

mineralization, this report aims to accomplish the following three research objectives (Figure 1-1): (i)

develop synthesis approaches for architected silicate materials with tailored structural and morphological

arrangements for integrated carbon mineralization; (ii) explore mechanistic insights in carbon removal post

H2 production under different conditions; (iii) advance potentials of harnessing high-purity H2 via

multiphase enhanced WGSR with in-situ carbon mineralization. Thus, the chapters of this report have been

organized based on their relevance to address these research objectives.

 Based on the introductory overview of CO2 emission issues and corresponding mitigation strategies

in the preceding chapter, the pore structure in the crystalline starting materials is significant in carbon

mineralization yet hard to preserve after thermal treatment. Chapter 2 proposes a sol-gel synthesis approach

for architected mesoporous crystalline forsterite (Mg2SiO4) carbon with well-controlled pore size. A sucrose

pore maintainer is innovatively introduced to prevent the pore structure from collapsing during amorphous-

to-crystalline transition under calcination. The highly ordered pore structure, large surface area, and

crystallinity of the final product are confirmed by multiple characterizations, evidencing its remarkable

application potential in carbon mineralization.

 Other than the earth-abundant silicate minerals, hydroxide materials are also extensively adopted

in carbon mineralization due to their enhancement in solvent alkalinity. However, the high temperatures

and pressures applied in traditional pathways require additional power supplies and lead to extra CO2

emission, which highlights the necessity for the development of energy-efficient carbon capture approaches

under moderate conditions. Chapter 3 discusses an accelerated brucite (Mg(OH)2) carbon mineralization

process at an ambient pressure of 1 atm and moderate temperature below 100 °C with the assistance of

                                             Page 17 of 210

carbon capture solvent like sodium glycinate, which is sufficiently qualified as a post H2 production carbon

removal procedure after cooling the gas effluent from WGSR, and also provides new insights in closing the

material cycles for ambient-condition carbon sorbent recovery. The reaction conditions such as temperature

and solvent concentration are manipulated to reveal the scenario with the best carbon mineralization

performance in pure CO2 atmosphere. Additionally, the system is also operated to separate CO2 from a

series of simulated flue gas with different CO2 levels at ambient temperature and pressure, in which the

mechanistic insights on Na-glycinate favored carbon capture are elucidated further, verifying the feasibility

of the approach in large-scale post-combustion CO2 separation. The tunable structural and morphological

evolution of the carbonate-bearing products are also demonstrated and discussed.

 As a post H2 recovery carbon capture approach, the low-temperature carbon mineralization is

energy-efficient but requires additional cooling systems due to the discrepant operating temperature with

WGSR. Hence, Chapter 4 investigates a carbon mineralization approach at 200 °C and 20 atm CO2 partial

pressure in hydrothermal environments starting from different Mg-silicate materials, which is eligible to

capture CO2 at the same conditions of WGSR and requires no preliminary treatment of the gaseous supply.

The in-operando cross-scale X-ray scattering measurements, including ultra-small angle X-ray scattering

(USAXS), small angle X-ray scattering (SAXS), and wide angle X-ray scattering (WAXS), are harnessed

to delineate detailed insights into the mesoscale, nanoscale, and sub-nanoscale transformations from

colloidal precursors to synthesized amorphous-crystalline Mg-silicate starting materials, which uncovers

the phase and particle size evolutions underlying the synthesis route during calcination and confirms the

generation of desired products. The carbon mineralization performance is evaluated and compared between

Mg-silicate materials with different morphology and crystallinity under different stirring modes to provide

insights into silicate dissolution and relevant carbonate formation mechanisms. The structural and

morphological arrangement variations of the carbonate-bearing products, such as Si coordination and

particle size, are reported to support the elaboration further.

 Though the decoupled carbon mineralization has been proven effective in post H2 production

carbon removal in previous chapters, the in-situ integration between WGSR and carbon mineralization is

regarded as an advancing step in carbon-free H2 recovery due to its potential in H2 yield enhancement.

Chapter 5 reports a low-temperature enhanced water gas shift reaction (eWGSR) over commercial Pt/Al2O3

integrated with in-situ carbon mineralization via the aqueous medium, which confirms the feasibility and

benefits of integrated H2 production and carbon removal. The detailed thermodynamic models of the

enhanced WGSR with different Mg- and Ca-bearing materials are derived based on the temperature-

dependent equilibrium constants, evidencing the enhanced H2 production and integrated carbon removal

can occur simultaneously via a multiphase approach at temperatures between 250-300 °C. Experimentally,

the multiphase EWGSRs are conducted with Mg- and Ca-bearing hydroxides and silicates, from which the

gaseous product compositions are quantitatively characterized to evaluate the H2 yields enhancement and

CO2 emission reduction. Further, the exact mole value of each phase in the effluent gas is also estimated

based on an introduced constant N2 baseline, allowing more refined predictions for large-scale application.

The compositional and morphological characterization of the carbonate-bearing solids are also discussed

to provide insights in dissolution-related mechanisms of the overall reaction.

 Finally, the key findings, conclusions, and recommendations for future work based on the

performed studies are summarized and illustrated in Chapter 6.

2.1. Introduction

 As we transition to sustainable and renewable energy resources, our dependence on metals is

expected to increase significantly. The need for metal-based electrodes for electrolysis to produce fuels and

clean water, catalysts for energy and resource conversion, and rare earths for electronic materials is the

societal basis for exploring earth-abundant and low-value substrates for metal recovery and utilization. In

this context, naturally occurring magnesium silicates are receiving increasing attention since they are a

source of nickel and iron, and are a source of magnesium for CO2 capture, storage, and removal.30,32,34,43,74–
77
 Earth-abundant magnesium silicate minerals include (Mg, Fe)2SiO4 (olivine) and serpentine

((Mg,Fe)3Si2O5(OH)4). Without any pre-treatment, olivine is considered to be more reactive compared to

serpentine. The magnesium end member of olivine is Mg2SiO4 (forsterite) and the iron end member is

Fe2SiO4 (fayalite). Olivine is being extensively investigated for CO2 removal via weathering and carbon

mineralization. 30,32,34,43,46,74–86

 In enhanced weathering, the dissolution of magnesium silicates releases Mg2+ ions. Each Mg2+ ion

can capture two molecules of CO2 from air to produce two bicarbonate ions in soils and enhance soil fertility

for crop production and growth. In carbon mineralization, magnesium (or calcium) silicates are reacted

with CO2 to produce magnesium (or calcium) carbonates.30,32,34,43,46,75–77,81–86 The chemical and

morphological heterogeneity of naturally occurring Mg-silicates, anomalous speciation behavior that is

highly sensitive to pH, temperature, gas compositions, and ionic strength, and the ambiguous role of

precipitated silica on the reactivity of Mg-silicates.43,81,87–90 These challenges motivate the synthesis of

mesoporous magnesium silicates with well-ordered pore structures to advance a calibrated understanding

of the synthesis of magnesium silicates.

                                              Page 20 of 210

 These studies will have a translational impact on the use of magnesium silicates for several

industrial and medical applications. First, silicate materials are ideal adsorbents for wastewater filtration

systems. For example, silicate materials can be used to adsorb phosphate, fluoride, cobalt salt, ammonia

nitrogen, heavy metal ions, and organic pollutants.91–96 Silicate materials also play important roles in

manufacturing long-lasting phosphor and plasma display panels (PDPs).97,98 Moreover, because of the

extremely low electrical conductivities and dielectric constants of some silicate materials, they are often

used in electrical or micro-wave devices. In the medical industry, magnesium silicates are used in drug

delivery, protein adsorption, and bone regeneration.99–101 These widespread applications of magnesium

silicates motivate the design of novel synthesis routes for producing crystalline magnesium silicates with

mesoporous structures.

 Prior work has been focused on the synthesis of magnesium silicates such as Mg2SiO4 and MgSiO3

using sol-gel, hydrothermal, and ball-milling methods. Sanosh and coworkers reported a sol-gel method to

synthesize amorphous Mg2SiO4 nanoparticles with a narrow particle size distribution of approximately 27

nm.102 Magnesium nitrate hexahydrate (Mg(NO3)2·6H2O, MNH) and tetraethyl ortho-silicate (TEOS) were

used as sources of magnesium and silicate, respectively. The reaction was performed in an acid

environment, and the final product has a prolate spheroidal structure.102 Ni and coworkers synthesized

forsterite ceramics with high bending strength and fracture toughness using sol-gel methods.103 This study

used magnesium nitrate hexahydrate (Mg(NO3)2·6H2O) and colloidal SiO2 as starting materials to produce

a gel that was subsequently calcined.103 Similarly, Kharaziha and coworkers used the same starting materials

of sucrose and polyvinyl alcohol polymer (PVA) to form bioactive forsterite ceramic with a high surface

area.104

 A template-assisted hydrothermal method was used to generate mesopores magnesium silicate with

a hierarchical structure.105 In this approach, carbon-coated SBA-15 was used as a template. The pore walls

of the template were transformed into crystalline magnesium silicate (Mg3Si4O9(OH)4) on heating.105 As an

alternative to chemical routes, nanosized magnesium silicates were synthesized using mechanical activation

                                              Page 21 of 210

approaches without the liquid phase.106 Tavangarian and coworkers synthesized single-phase

nanocrystalline forsterite via two different ball-milling methods.107 In the first route, moderate talc

(Mg3Si4O10(OH)2) and magnesium carbonate (MgCO3) powders were mixed in a steel vial with steel balls

and calcined after milling. By adjusting the ball-to-powder weight ratio, rotation speed, and calcination

temperature, nanocrystalline forsterite powder with a particle size of approximately 40 nm was obtained.107

The other ball-milling method replaces magnesium carbonate (MgCO3) powder with periclase (MgO),

producing a single-phase nanocrystalline forsterite.108

 Further, approaches to synthesize MgSiO3 (enstatite) were reported. Tsai and coworkers developed

a nanocrystalline enstatite fiber via the sol-gel method.109 In a typical synthesis procedure, they mixed

tetraethoxysilane (TEOS) and a methanol solution of magnesium methoxide (Mg(OCH3)2), followed by

DI-water and acetic acid to promote the hydrolysis. After drying and calcination, the enstatite fibers were

obtained.109 In addition, Song and coworkers reported the synthesis of MgSiO3 ceramics with good

microwave dielectric properties via the ball-milling method.110 In this pathway, MgO and SiO2 powder were

added to a nylon vial with zirconia balls for ball milling. The mixed powder was then calcined and ball-

milled again. The final product was obtained by sintering the hydraulically pressed pellets from secondary

ball milling.110

 In addition to amorphous Mg2SiO4 and MgSiO3 materials, the synthesis of magnesium silicate

hydroxide (Mg3Si2O5(OH)4) and talc (Mg3Si4O10(OH)2) has been explored. Magnesium silicate hydroxide

(Mg3Si2O5(OH)4) can be used as a lubricant additive. Zhuang and coworkers synthesized Mg 3Si2O5(OH)4

with different morphologies via the hydrothermal method.111 The researchers mixed Mg(NO3)2, ethanol,

and PEG200, and added the mixture to Na2SiO3 solution to precipitate Mg3Si2O5(OH)4. The precipitates

were hydrothermally treated with NaOH solution to obtain the final product. By adjusting the concentration

of NaOH, the morphology of the final product evolved from a hollow sphere to a core-shell sphere.

Magnesium silicate nanotubes are produced by adding ethylene glycol at the end of the synthesis route.111

Wang and coworkers also produced magnesium silicate hydroxide with different morphologies and

                                              Page 22 of 210

structures by hydrothermally treating MgO and SiO2 in NaOH solution.112 Talc (Mg3Si4O10(OH)2) which is

widely used to manufacture ceramics, paper, and paints was synthesized with varying morphologies to meet

industrial requirements.113 Wang and coworkers successfully used colloidal silica as a chemical template to

synthesize Mg3Si4O10(OH)2 hollow spheres.95 Moreover, Golubeva et al. used the hydrothermal method to

synthesize Mg3Si4O10(OH)2·nH2O with an approximate particle size of 100 nm.114

 The application and performance of silicate materials greatly depend on the chemical compositions,

structures, and morphologies of the particles. Mesoporous silicates are among the most attractive materials

because of their excellent performance in adsorption, catalysis, molecular sieving, sensors, and

electrochemistry.115 According to IUPAC classification, mesoporous materials have a pore size between 2

nm to 50 nm.116 Unlike traditional microporous materials synthesized by a single molecular template, the

synthesis of mesoporous materials requires the participation of aggregated surfactant molecules as a

template.117 This type of aggregated molecular template is known as micelles, which form spontaneously

when the surfactant concentration attains a critical value.118 With templates, the desired materials can be

synthesized outside, and the mesoporous structure is obtained after removing the micelles template by

calcination or chemical extraction.115 By applying proper surfactant concentrations, the size and structure

of micelles can be controlled, which gives control over the shape and size of the pores in the final product.

The wide-ranging applications and controllable structures of mesoporous magnesium silicate materials

have attracted scientific attention. Specific examples are discussed below.

 Lu and coworkers synthesized a crystalline Mg3Si4O9(OH)4 using mesoporous silica as a template.

In their study, they used SBA-15 as a template and silica source to form magnesium silicate via the

hydrothermal method.119 Zhu and coworkers synthesized amorphous mesoporous Mg2SiO4 via the sol-gel

method with surfactants.120 These studies indicate that several varieties of magnesium silicate materials can

be manufactured in a mesoporous structure. However, these approaches produce either amorphous

magnesium silicates mesoporous materials or crystalline magnesium silicates that are not Mg 2SiO4.

Crystalline materials with mesoporous structures are preferred because of their better performance in

                                              Page 23 of 210

targeted applications. Compared to amorphous products, crystalline materials possess better mechanical

and thermal stabilities because of the long-range order of the molecules and the lattice defects.121 Crystalline

phases enhance optical, electronic, and photocatalytic activities in mesoporous materials. 122 Amorphous

materials are typically transformed into crystalline matter using heat. Nevertheless, the mesoporous

structure tends to collapse when amorphous Mg-silicate is heat-treated to produce crystalline phases. For

this reason, prior studies have not attempted to tackle the challenge of producing crystalline Mg2SiO4 with

ordered pore structures.

 To address this challenge, we developed a novel synthesis method to produce crystalline

mesoporous forsterite (Mg2SiO4) with narrow pore size distribution. This method generates an amorphous

mesoporous magnesium silicate precursor initially, followed by the amorphous-crystalline transition of the

precursor in the presence of sucrose which is coated on the pore walls to maintain the pore structure. This

sucrose coating inside the pores prevents the collapse of the mesopores during the thermal treatment. The

resulting crystalline matrix preserves the highly ordered pore structure.

 To test the hypothesis that high purity mesoporous crystalline Mg2SiO4 materials with ordered

mesoporous structures can be synthesized using this route, we addressed the following research questions

in this study: (i) Starting from amorphous Mg2SiO4 gels, what is the chemical and morphological evolution

of the materials as crystalline Mg2SiO4 phases are formed? (ii) How do the pore volume and pore size

distribution evolve during the various stages involved in the synthesis approaches? (iii) What are the

mechanisms underlying the transition of amorphous to crystalline transition of Mg2SiO4 in the presence of

sucrose for maintaining the mesoscale porosity in these materials? To address these questions, the detailed

material synthesis approaches, and the results from the characterization of these materials are discussed in

the following sections.

2.2. Materials and Method

2.2.1. Synthesis of Crystalline Mesoporous Magnesium Silicate

                                               Page 24 of 210

 The reagents used in the study, Pluronic® P123 (EG27PG61EG27), magnesium nitrate hexahydrate

(Mg(NO3)2.6H2O), and tetraethylorthosilicate (TEOS) was purchased from Sigma Aldrich and 12 M

hydrochloric acid (HCl) was acquired from Fisher Scientific. Sucrose was supplied by VWR, and sulfuric

acid (H2SO4) was procured from Ricca. Crystalline mesoporous magnesium silicates with highly ordered

pores were developed using a multi-step synthesis route as shown in Figure 2-1. The first step involves the

synthesis of amorphous magnesium silicate via a sol-gel approach, followed by the preservation of pores

using a carbon-coating approach, and finally, the removal of carbon and the formation of crystalline

magnesium silicate.

 In the first step, amorphous magnesium silicate was prepared by adding 3 g of P123 to 120 mL of

2 M HCl and 30 mL of DI water. The mixture was then stirred at 38°C for 1 hour, followed by the addition

of 8.5 g of TEOS and 10.3 g of Mg(NO3)2.6H2O. This mixture was further stirred for 24 hours at 38°C. The

precipitates generated during the first step were dried at 100°C in the air for 24 hours. P123 from within

the pores was removed by calcining the dried sample at 600°C at a ramping rate of 1°C/min for 6 hours in

a benchtop muffle furnace (Thermo Scientific Thermolyne FB1410 M, Asheville, NC).

 In the second step, the pores inside the amorphous mesoporous magnesium silicate obtained from

Step 1 were coated and preserved using a carbon-coating approach. Typically, the direct thermal treatment

of amorphous magnesium silicate produces crystalline magnesium silicates at temperatures higher than

800°C which results in the collapse of the mesopores. Since the overarching goal of this work is to develop

the crystalline magnesium silicate with ordered pores, the carbon-coating route was chosen. In a typical

coating procedure, 2 g of sucrose was dissolved in 0.224 g of H2SO4 and 9.756 g of DI water. 1 g of

amorphous magnesium silicate powder was added to this mixture and mixed at 120°C for 12 hours. In the

next step, the sucrose coated magnesium silicate was calcined at 900 °C in nitrogen (N 2) for 2 hours at a

ramp rate of 1°C/min in the tube furnace (Lindberg Tube Furnace, 1000-C-1) to obtain crystalline

magnesium silicate. The calcination step resulted in the carbon coating of pores and amorphous to the

crystalline transformation of pore walls. In the final step, the carbon-coated sample was heated at 650°C in

                                             Page 25 of 210

the air for 3 hours at a ramping rate of 20°C/min using the benchtop muffle furnace to remove carbon from

the pores.123 As a reference, the as-synthesis amorphous magnesium silicate was calcined at 900 °C in

nitrogen (N2) for 2 hours at a ramp rate of 1°C/min in the tube furnace to obtain the crystalline product

without carbon coating.

2.2.2. Characterization of Synthesized Powders

 The changes in the chemical composition and structure of synthesized samples were evaluated

using infrared (IR) spectroscopy collected in an Attenuated Total Reflection (ATR) mode using an

Attenuated Total Reflection-Fourier Transform Infrared spectrometer (ATR-FTIR, NicoletTM iS50,

Waltham, MA) and the X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Scienta Omicron ESCA-2SR, Al K). The

crystalline phases in the synthesized materials were obtained from X-ray diffraction (XRD, Bruker D8

Advance ECO powder diffractometer, Bruker). The morphology and particle size were determined by using

field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM, LEO 1550 FESEM, Bruker) and field emission high

resolution transmission electron microscope (FEI F20 TEM STEM) at 120 kV. Finally, the pore size

distributions (PSD) were determined from N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms using the Brunauer–

Emmett–Teller technique (BET) (Quantachrome Autosorb iQ Analyzer, Boynton Beach, FL). Before

measuring the adsorption-desorption isotherms, the synthesized samples were outgassed at 120°C for 18

hours.

 Finally, the structural transitions during calcination in N2 were determined using in-situ wide-angle

X-ray scattering (WAXS) measurements between 30°C and 906°C in an N2 environment. The

measurements were performed in sector 9-ID-C at the Advanced Photon Source (APS), Argonne National

Laboratory (ANL). The details about the instrument were reported in previous publications. 124,125 The

sucrose-coated magnesium silicate sample was loaded into a quartz capillary (I.D. = 1.3 mm and O.D. =

1.5 mm) to acquire the scans. The background was taken from the empty capillary and subtracted from the

data. During the acquisition, N2 was also purged through the capillary. The X-ray energy was set to 21.0

                                              Page 26 of 210

keV which corresponds to the X-ray wavelength of 0.59 Å. The total X-ray flux during the measurements

was 1013 photon mm-2 s-1. The calibrations for sample-to-detector distance and instrument were performed

using LaB6.

2.3. Result and Discussion

2.3.1. Chemical and Structural Changes during Synthesis

 As discussed in the previous section, the synthesis of crystalline magnesium silicate proceeds via

sol-gel synthesis of amorphous Mg-silicate, followed by the coating of sucrose to maintain the pores in

mesoporous in Mg-silicate, the transformation of amorphous Mg-silicate to crystalline Mg-silicate, and the

removal of carbon which preserves the pores. Figure 2-1 is a schematic representation of this approach. To

evaluate the hypothesis that high purity crystalline magnesium silicate can be synthesized using this

approach, the as-synthesized amorphous magnesium silicate, sucrose-coated magnesium silicate, carbon-

coated magnesium silicate, and crystalline magnesium silicate are characterized in detail. For comparison,

the as-synthesized magnesium silicate heat-treated without carbon-coating is also prepared and

characterized.

 The changes in the chemical bonding of these materials are determined using ATR FT-IR

spectroscopy. The IR spectra identifying Si-O coordination are shown in Figure 2-2. The peak

corresponding to the asymmetric stretching vibrations of Si-O bonds around 1000 cm-1 appeared in all the

samples.126 The peak representing Si-O-Si bending vibration around 650 cm-1 is also noted in sucrose-

coated magnesium silicate, carbon-coated magnesium silicate, and crystalline magnesium silicate.127

Further, in the high wavenumber range (>2500 cm-1), the as-synthesized amorphous sample shows the Si-

OH peak around 3200 cm-1 and the peak of residual C-H bond from P123 at around 2980 cm-1.128–130 The

very low intensity indicates the small amount of the residual Si-OH bond and C-H in the sample. For the

sucrose-coated sample, the strong OH peak appears around 3300 cm-1 due to the hydroxyl (OH) functional

groups in the sucrose molecules. Additionally, after the calcination in N2 and air, the C-H bonds are

                                            Page 27 of 210

eliminated. However, a weak signal from -OH bands around 3200 cm-1 appears in the carbon-coated and

crystalline samples, which could indicate the adsorbed moisture on the particles. On the other hand, the

reference sample without a sucrose coat shows Si-O stretching peak at around 1050 cm-1 and a Si-O-Si

bending peak at around 850 cm-1, which can be a hint of the existence of MgSiO3.131 Besides, the Si-O-Si

peak around 950 cm-1, and Si-OH peak around 880 cm-1 are also noted.126 Based on the IR spectra of the

Si-O coordination, it can be noted that the peaks corresponding to the vibrations from Si-O linkages vary

significantly across the different samples. To delineate the changes in the silica environment as the synthesis

proceeds, the peaks that correspond to the Si-O bond between 1250 – 800 cm-1 were deconvoluted (Figure

2-3) using the Levenberg Marquardt algorithm with a Gaussian model embedded in Origin Pro software

(OriginLab Corp.). The deconvolution provides information about the Qn coordination in silica during the

synthesis. The Qn notation (n = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4) represents the number of bridging oxygens per Si tetrahedron

in silicate-based materials.132 Q4 corresponds to SiO2 species with 0 non-bridging oxygens (NBOs), while

Q0 represents 4 NBOs in [SiO4]4--related species. The silicate species, corresponding Qn notations, NBOs,

and wavenumber ranges (cm-1) are reported in Table 2-1.

 For as-synthesized amorphous Mg-silicate, the main peaks corresponding to the amorphous silica

(SiO4) with 0 non-bridging oxygens are expected due to the hydrolysis of TEOS in the acidic solution and

condensation to form ≡Si-O-Si≡ linkages (Figure 2-3 (a)).133–136 Since the sol-gel synthesis conditions used

in this study lead to the formation of amorphous silica linkages with Mg2+ cations (from Mg(NO3)2)

randomly distributed in the matrix, we noted a slight shift in the main peak, which appears ~1015 cm -1

instead of the typical ~1080 cm-1 peak for amorphous silica systems (SBA-15, Stöber silica particles, etc.).
134,135,137
 The presence of these cations could affect the NBOs in amorphous silica, as noted by a peak around

907 cm-1 in Figure 2-3 (a), corresponding to 3 NBOs with Q1 coordination. In silicate-based materials, the

segregation of [SiO4]4- tetrahedra upon sharing of an oxygen atom can lead to [SiO3 – O – SiO3]6- species

and free O2- ions that coordinate with Mg2+ cations in the matrix and form MgO-based regions.138 This

                                                  Page 28 of 210

observation is consistent with the formation of crystalline MgO peaks in the XRD patterns of as-synthesized

Mg-silicate (Figure 2-4).

 After sucrose-coating on mesoporous Mg-silicate, the position of the Si-O-Si remains unchanged

peaks in the XRD pattern of sucrose-coated Mg-silicate (Figure 2-4 (b)) are also diminished, which may

result from the distortion of Mg-O coordination upon exposure to H2SO4-based solution. Thermal treatment

purposes here. First, it helps keep the carbon preserved inside the pores and avoids pore collapse during

thermal treatment. Second, it results in the amorphous-to-crystalline transition of Mg2SiO4. In the final

stage, after calcination at 650 °C and removal of carbon from the pores, the crystalline structure of Mg2SiO4

is preserved (Figure 2-4 (d)).162 For comparison, the XRD pattern of the crystalline sample without sucrose

coating is also presented (Figure 2-4 (e)). The characteristic peaks indicate that the sample consists of

crystalline periclase (MgO) and enstatite (MgSiO3) instead of forsterite.163,164 The persistence of MgO in

this sample after thermal treatment can be explained by the absence of sulfuric acid, present in the sucrose

coating solution, which causes the dissolution of MgO. As a result, the MgO particles remain unaffected in

                                              Page 32 of 210

the system and rest of the matrix crystallizes as MgSiO3.164 The conclusion is also supported by the FTIR

peaks of the crystalline sample without sucrose coat we’ve discussed previously.

2.3.2. Particle and Pore Morphology of Synthesized Powders

 To understand the morphological evolution during the synthesis process, the scanning electron

micrographs shown in Figure 2-9 are analyzed. The amorphous magnesium silicate forms cylindrical

particles after the removal of P123 surfactant (Figure 2-9 (a)), which is typical of particles prepared from

similar sol-gel approaches.165 After the sucrose coating, the particles are covered with the sucrose (Figure

2-9 (b)). Upon calcination in N2, the carbonization of sucrose resulted in the shrinkage of carbon around

the particles in the carbon-coated magnesium silicate sample, resulting in cylindrical particles (Figure 2-9

(c)). Based on the XRD pattern of the carbon-coated magnesium silicate sample, the particles after the

calcination in N2 are crystalline and have similar morphology as the amorphous magnesium silicate. The

morphology of crystalline magnesium silicate particles, after the final step of calcination in the air to remove

the carbon, is also cylindrical (Figure 2-9 (d)). With the comprehension of the particle morphology, we

delineate the changes in the mesopores inside these cylindrical particles using the N2 adsorption-desorption

measurements, as discussed below.

 To verify that the final product has highly ordered pore sizes and reveal the variation in the pore

sizes during the synthesis process, the BET N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms were measured at 77 K.

The isotherms of the samples are shown in Figure 2-10, and the pore size distribution curves of different

samples are shown in Figure 2-11. The pores size distributions from the isotherms are estimated using the

non-local density functional theory (NLDFT) model, which is applied to the adsorption isotherm.166–168 The

as-synthesized amorphous magnesium silicate sample exhibits a type IV(a) isotherm having an H1

hysteresis (Figure 2-10 (a)), typical of the uniform mesopores with a delayed condensation on the

adsorption branch because of the capillary condensation in the pores.169 The sucrose-coated magnesium

silicate sample exhibited a random isotherm (Figure 2-10 (b)). This can be explained by the occupation of

                                               Page 33 of 210

pores by the sucrose molecules, which remain suspended in the pores and interrupt the capillary

condensation phenomenon. However, the carbon-coated magnesium silicate shows type IV(a) isotherm

with H2(a) hysteresis (Figure 2-10 (c)).169 The type H2 hysteresis is exhibited by complex pore structures.

The steep trend noted in the desorption branch can be attributed to pore-blocking in narrow pore necks or

cavitation-induced evaporation.169 The narrowing of the pore neck occurs due to carbon-coating of the

pores. After the removal of carbon to produce the final crystalline magnesium silicate product, type H3

hysteresis loop is noted as shown in Figure 2-10 (d). The crystalline magnesium silicate without sucrose

coating also exhibits a similar isotherm (Figure 2-10 (e)). The distinctive features associated with the type

H3 hysteresis loop are: (i) the resemblance of the adsorption branch to a type II isotherm and (ii) the lower

limit of the adsorption branch, which is located at the cavitation-induced relative pressure (P/P0). Moreover,

we did not observe any plateau at high P/P0 values, indicating that the isotherm for the crystalline

magnesium silicate is not identified as type IV.169,170 Besides having a resemblance to type II isotherm, the

isotherm for crystalline magnesium silicate deviates from a true type II and can be characterized as pseudo-

type II isotherm caused by the delayed capillary condensation. These features are characteristics of the

presence of non-rigid aggregates, a low degree of pore curvature, and slit-like pores.169,171

 The surface areas of the amorphous magnesium silicate, sucrose-coated magnesium silicate,

carbon-coated magnesium silicate, and crystalline magnesium silicate are 276.35 m2/g, 0.27 m2/g, 384.43

m2/g, and 124.25 m2/g, respectively. The higher surface area exhibited by the as-synthesized amorphous

magnesium silicate is attributed to the availability of pores resulting from surfactant removal from the

mesoporous particles, and the high surface area of the carbon-coated sample comes from the porous carbon

structure around the magnesium silicate particles. On the other hand, the pore volume for the as-synthesized

amorphous magnesium silicate (0.43 cc/g) is also larger than the sucrose coated (0.001 cc/g), carbon-coated

(0.14 cc/g), and crystalline magnesium silicate (0.12 cc/g) samples. A significant decrease in the pore

volume is noted after sucrose-coating, indicating the complete filling of mesopores. Conversely, carbon-

coating via calcination in N2 increases the pore volume to 0.14 cc/g. This increase can be explained by the

                                              Page 34 of 210

pyrolysis of sucrose and the formation of carbon-coating along the pore walls (Figure 2-1, step 4). Finally,

after the removal of carbon from the pores by heating in air, the pore volume from the carbon structure is

eliminated and the pore volume from the magnesium silicate decreases slightly, and the total pore volume

reached a value of 0.12 cc/g. The total pore volume and surface area of the crystalline magnesium silicate

is lower than that of as-synthesized amorphous magnesium silicate. This observed decrease in the surface

area and pore volume in crystalline magnesium silicate can be attributed to the densification of pore walls

during amorphous-to-crystalline phase transformation. To be specific, the changes in the molecular

attractions on heating to 900°C cause amorphous phases to transform into crystalline phases. 102 The

aggregation of particles causes the specific surface area to decrease during this amorphous to crystalline

transformation. In mesoporous materials, heat treatment or sintering involves the collapse of the pore

structure and aggregation of particles to decrease the surface free energy during calcination.172 To prevent

the collapse of the pore structure, magnesium silicate is coated with carbon. The mesoporous structure

shrinks and densifies on heating instead of being eliminated which causes a small decrease in the pore

volume.

 The average pore sizes of as-synthesized amorphous magnesium silicate, carbon-coated

magnesium silicate, and crystalline magnesium silicate are 6.56 nm, 4.89 nm, 1.87 nm, and 2.58 nm,

respectively. The pore size distributions for as-synthesized amorphous magnesium silicate, sucrose-coated

magnesium silicate, carbon-coated magnesium silicate, crystalline magnesium silicate, and crystalline

magnesium silicate without sucrose coating are shown in Figures 2-11 (a), (b), (c), (d), and (e),

respectively. For as-synthesized amorphous magnesium silicate and crystalline magnesium silicate samples,

narrow pore size distributions are noted, which are characteristic of highly ordered mesopores. As a

precedent, Lu and coworkers have synthesized crystalline mesoporous Mg3Si4O9(OH)4 with highly ordered

pores around 3.8nm by sucrose coating. However, they synthesized a magnesium silicate product via the

hydrothermal method and the yield of the final product is relatively limited.119 In the case of carbon-coated

magnesium silicate, when the sucrose pyrolysis coats carbon along with the pores of mesoporous structure,

                                              Page 35 of 210

a variety of pore sizes (Figure 2-11 (c)) are noted with an average value of 1.87 nm. This highlights a

complex pore network formed in the mesoporous structure due to the carbon-coating, which is also

indicated by the H2 type hysteresis for the carbon-coated magnesium silicate sample. On the other hand,

the pore size distribution of the reference crystalline magnesium silicate without sucrose coating exhibits

an average pore size and surface area of 4.89 nm, and 9.53 m 2/g, respectively. However, the pore volume

(0.03 cc/g) of this samples is significantly lower compared to other samples (Figure 2-11 (e)), which

indicates that most of the mesopores collapse during the calcination without the support of a carbon coat.

 The pore size and crystalline arrangement of the pore walls in crystalline magnesium silicate

sample is visualized using high resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) as presented in

Figure 2-12. The pore sizes around 2.6 nm are identified in the HR-TEM image. Moreover, the crystal

orientation in the pore walls is observed from the lattice fringes and a d-spacing of 0.25 nm is noted, which

corresponds to (311) plane of Mg2SiO4 (~2θ = 36°).162

2.3.3. In-situ Phase Transitions During the Transformations of Amorphous to Crystalline

Mg-Silicates

 The structural changes during the calcination of amorphous sucrose-coated magnesium silicate in

N2 were evaluated using synchrotron-based in-situ WAXS measurements. These measurements provide

real-time insights into the phase transformations of the mesoporous matrix as a function of temperature. To

reveal the phase transition process of the sucrose coated magnesium silicate sample during calcination, the

evolution of (311) and (121) orientations of crystalline Mg2SiO4 are shown in Figure 2-13.162

 Initially, a relatively flat curve is noted at 30 °C, indicating an amorphous mesoporous matrix. The

amorphous character of the matrix persists at higher temperatures (> 500 °C), and the first emergence of

the crystalline structure is noted at 779 °C. A continuous increase in the peak intensities of (331) and (121)

orientations of crystalline Mg2SiO4 is noted till 906 °C.162 These observations are consistent with the

transformation of amorphous to crystalline transformations of crystalline magnesium silicate. To

                                              Page 36 of 210

summarize, the crystalline phases of magnesium silicate appear once temperatures higher than 779 °C are

achieved.

2.4. Conclusion

 In this study, we report a novel route to synthesize crystalline magnesium silicate (Mg 2SiO4) with

highly ordered mesopores starting from amorphous mesoporous magnesium silicate. The key challenge of

preserving the pore diameter inside the mesopores range is resolved by coating the pores of amorphous

magnesium silicates with sucrose followed by transforming these amorphous materials into crystalline

phases and removing carbon by applying heat. The end product is crystalline and is composed of 100%

Mg2SiO4. These particles have cylindrical morphologies, and the pore diameter is 2.58nm. The highly

ordered pore structures and large surface area of these materials make them ideal candidates for developing

a calibrated understanding of the reactivity of these materials with CO2 for applications related to carbon

capture, utilization, storage, and removal.

3.1. Introduction

 As the industry enlarging and the population growth all over the world, the rising anthropogenic

carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions contribute to various environmental issues, including climate changes and

sea-level ascending.173,174175176 These situations call for feasible routes to capture and store CO2. Therefore,

sorts of carbon capture and storage (CCS) technics are developed, which can be divided into four types:

absorption, adsorption, separation membranes, and cryogenic distillation.177178179180181 One of the most

feasible pathways that can sequestrate CO2 from air or industrial tail gas like flue gas is known as carbon

mineralization.182 The concept of carbon mineralization was raised by Seifritz and investigated by several

researchers, in which the carbon dioxide combines with metal cations in various Ca- and Mg-bearing

alkaline resources to form solid carbonate and realize carbon sequestration.183184185 The thermodynamic

downhill nature of mineralization and the stability of the environment-benign products make the technical

approach a reliable pathway to realizing the carbon-neutral future.186187

 Existing research demonstrates that alkaline minerals can neutralize dissolved CO2 and generate

carbonate solids.186188189 And the relatively low solubility of these carbonate products also guarantees the

permanent storage of CO2.190191 All of these characteristics make alkaline minerals ideal absorbents for

carbon mineralization. Furthermore, because of the global abundance of alkaline minerals, particularly Mg-

bearing minerals, their extensive carbon capacity can easily cover the CO2 emission from fossil fuel usage,

making them widely used for carbon mineralization.190192 The Ca-bearing absorbents like portlandite

(Ca(OH)2)) or wollastonite (CaSiO3) have attracted a lot of attention due to the abundant reserves and the

extensive usage of the carbonated products.193194195 However, for industrial applications, the continuous use

of Ca-bearing absorbents require regeneration after carbon capture, a process that is highly energy-intensive
                                               Page 53 of 210

and costly.196197198 In contrast, Mg-bearing absorbents can be generated from their corresponding carbonates

at much lower decomposition temperatures, requiring significantly less energy.199 Additionally, a recent

research also proposed an electrochemical sorbent recovery approach proceeding at ambient conditions for

Mg-bearing materials like Mg(OH)2, providing new insights to close the carbon and materials cycles.200

Typically, the carbonation process of Mg-based minerals can be divided into two steps: Mg ions extraction

and carbonate formation, corresponding to the dissolution of the initial minerals and the precipitation of

carbonated products.201 The dissolution and carbonation behaviors of olivine (Mg2SiO4) and serpentine

(Mg3(OH)4(Si3O5)) have been investigated to reveal the mechanism and kinetics of carbon mineralization.

Gadikota and coworkers have investigated the carbonation mechanism of olivine with high pressure and

temperature. In their study, CO2 came from the injected solutions, and the enhancement of different

solutions was also revealed.202 King and coworkers have studied the impact of the associated Mg-silicate

phase during the carbonation of olivine.203 Besides, the dissolution and carbonation mechanism of

serpentine under different conditions were also investigated detailly.204205206207 On the other hand, brucite

(Mg(OH)2), though has less natural abundance, still continues to gain significant research interest due to

the higher carbonation reactivity compared with magnesium silicate minerals.201 According to existing

research, the carbonation process of brucite is highly dependent on humidity. In the absence of water in

pure CO2 gas flow, carbonation scarcely proceeds. However, the formation of carbonate products begins

with increasing water concentrations and temperatures.208

 To further enhance the carbon mineralization process under favorable conditions with aqueous

media, various chemical solvents are applied to provide humidity and increase the CO2 capacity of the

aqueous phase. Among those solvents, amino acid salts draw substantial attention due to their excellent

degradational resistibility and minimum environmental impact.209 As a typical amino acid salt, Na-glycinate

has already been used as a solvent for CO2 capture in some previous research. Liu and coworkers applied

Na-glycinate solution to capture CO2 in a single-step carbon mineralization process of Ca-based minerals,

including CaO and CaSiO3. They investigated the impact of Na-glycinate concentration, temperature, and

                                              Page 54 of 210

reaction duration. Besides, the study also emphasized the enhancing and accelerating effect of the

bicarbonate ions generated by CO2 capture to carbon mineralization.210 Besides, Yin and coworkers applied

the Na-glycinate solution to assist the carbonation of ash materials (mainly consisting of Ca-bearing

minerals and metallic compounds) generated by coal combustion. They also reported a synthesized pathway

of nano-scale calcite by using the CO2-loaded Na-glycinate and surface surfactant in this research.211

 Most of the existing studies focused on enhancing Ca-bearing minerals, such as quicklime (CaO)

or portlandite (Ca(OH)2), for carbon mineralization. However, using Na-glycinate solution to facilitate Mg-

bearing carbon mineralization is less explored but shows promise as an energy-effective carbon

management pathway. Therefore, to fill the research gap in earth-abundant Mg-bearing material carbon

mineralization with Na-glycinate carbon capture enhancer, this study was conducted and the research

questions we tried to address in this work could be summarized as follows: (i) How is the enhancement in

carbon mineralization with the application of Na-glycinate? (ii) What are the compositions of the

carbonated products as a function of solvent concentration, reaction temperature, and CO2 concentrations?

(iii) What are the carbonated products' morphological and structural features? To figure out these research

questions, we applied Na-glycinate solutions with a concentration range from 0 to 2.5M in single-step

carbon mineralization of brucite at a temperature range from 25ºC to 90ºC, respectively. The injected gas

flow varied from pure CO2 to binary flue gas with different CO2 content. By characterizing the

compositions, morphology, and structural features of the carbonated products, the mechanisms of brucite

carbonation at the presence of Na-glycinate solution were revealed.

3.2. Materials and methods

3.2.1. Chemicals

 The reagents used in the study, magnesium hydroxide (Mg(OH)2, 99%), sodium hydroxide (NaOH,

98%), and glycine (NH2CH2COOH, 99%) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. Na-glycinate solutions with

concentrations of 1M and 2.5M were prepared by adding calculated amounts of sodium hydroxide and

glycine into deionized water (18.2 MΩ⋅cm, Millipore) with 300 rpm stirring for 1 hour at room temperature.

The formation of Na-glycinate can be expressed by Reaction 3.1:

NH2CH2COOH + NaOH ↔ NH2CH2COO− + Na+ + H2O (Reaction 3.1)

3.2.2. Experimental process

3.2.2.1. Carbon mineralization with pure CO2 supply

 A batch reactor was initially employed to achieve integrated CO2 capture and mineralization using

magnesium hydroxide as the sorbent and sodium glycinate as the solvent (Figure 3-2 (a)). Initially, 3 g

brucite sorbent was added into 17 mL solvent (weight ratio 3:17). Then, a continuous CO2 flow (bone dry,

Airgas Co., USA) was injected into the reactor for 5 min to purge the residual air from the headspace. After

the reactor was filled with CO2, the outlet valve was slowly closed until the internal CO2 pressure reached

1 atm. Next, the reactions were initiated by turning on the heater and stirrer (300 rpm), applying a set of

temperatures: 25ºC, 50ºC, 75ºC, and 90ºC, respectively. The reaction duration was set to 3 hours for each

temperature. Eventually, the solid products (carbonated sorbents) were collected by vacuum filtration,

washed with deionized water, and dried at 80ºC for 24 hours. The resulting products were ground for further

characterization. As shown in Figure 3-1, the whole reaction process consists of three main steps: the

hydration of CO2, the dissolution of brucite, and the formation of solid carbonate.

3.2.2.2. CO2 separation from binary flue gas
                                             Page 56 of 210

 Simulated flue gas was utilized to evaluate the performance of the integrated capture and

mineralization system with industrial carbon point source. As shown in Figure 3-2 (b), a pipe system with

gas mixer and flow meter was developed to vary CO2 concentration, simulating exhaust flue gas. Then, the

generated flue gas was injected into the beaker via a gas diffuser at a constant flow rate (100 SCCM). It’s

worth noting that two group of experiments were conducted applying solvents with and without 6-hour flue

to the system in a 3:17 sorbent/solvent weight ratio with a stirring rate 300 rpm at room temperature,

marking the beginning of the reaction. After the reaction, the solid products were collected by vacuum

filtration, washed by deionized water, and dried at 80ºC for 48 hours before further characterization.

3.3. Result and Discussion

3.3.1. Extent of Carbon Mineralization

 Integrated CO2 capture and mineralization were first conducted in the Parr reactor with a pure CO2

atmosphere. In the study, the influence of temperature and solvent concentration on the extent of carbon

detailed information available in the Materials and Methods section. As shown in Figure 3-3 (a), the EoC

threefold improvement in EoC can be attributed to the lower solubility product of hydromagnesite and

enhanced CO2 hydration with glycinate ligands as increasing temperature.218,219 The rate constant of glycine

temperatures. This demonstrates the addition of Na-glycinate solution greatly enhances the carbon

hydromagnesite precipitation. It is also worth noting that the water case exhibited a higher carbon

mineralization extent than both Na-glycinate cases at room temperature. This counterintuitive consequence

results from two main reasons: Firstly, compared to water, the addition of Na-glycinate enhances the ion

strength and viscosity of the slurry, which restricts the mass transfer in the aqueous phase and suppresses

the precipitation of carbonate solids. However, the mass transfer limitation is weakened with increasing

temperature due to the enhanced molecular thermal motion. Secondly, glycinate ligands can bind with the

Mg2+ ions in the slurry to form Mg-glycinate complex, reducing the real-time Mg2+ ion concentration and

competing with the carbon mineralization mechanism.222 Though the performance of Na-glycinate can be

limited at room temperature using this approach, an optimized approach with binary flue gas is conducted

to alleviate these limitations at room temperature. This will be discussed detailly in the next section. On the

other hand, Liu and coworkers observed similar trend for Ca-bearing materials with 1M Na-glycinate

solution, which was also listed in Figure 3-3 (a) for comparison.210 For CaO adsorbent, it achieved around

95% EoC in temperature range from 50ºC to 90 ºC, indicating a near-complete transition of CaO to CaCO3.

Simultaneously, the CaSiO3 absorbent showed poor performance for CO2 mineralization at room

                                              Page 60 of 210

temperature. The EoC peaked at around 31% at 75ºC, then it slightly decreased back to 27.9% when

temperature went up to 90 ºC. The poor carbon mineralization performance of CaSiO3, especially in high

temperature range, results from the slow dissolution rate of silicate materials and the generation of

associated Si-rich passivation layer caused by Ca2+ ions’ release.90,223

 The forementioned results reveal the carbon capture performance of the brucite-sodium glycinate

system with pure CO2 at different temperatures. However, most industrial applications usually require a

carbon sequestration system capable of separating and storing CO2 from tail gases with varying

concentrations. Flue gas is a typical tail gas from stationary fuel combustion primarily composed of

nitrogen, carbon dioxide, and steam with trace amount of sulfur oxide or nitrogen oxide.224,225 To simulate

the conditions of industrial application, a binary flue gas mixture of nitrogen and carbon dioxide was applied

in this section to evaluate the CO2 selectivity and storage.

 As shown in Figure 3-2 (b), the flue gas studies were conducted inside a gas circulation system

with CO2-preloaded solutions. A constant binary gas flow was injected into the brucite slurry as carbon

source. Following the same calculation approach, the carbon mineralization extents from flue gas having

varying CO2 levels with different preloading protocols were exhibited in Figure 3-4. Generally, as the CO2

concentration in the binary gas flow increases from 4% to 20%, the extent of carbon mineralization

enhances from 3.4% to 31% in cases without CO2 preloading. The monotonically increasing correlation is

in alignment with Henry’s law, by which CO2 solubility and dissolution rate are determined by the CO2

partial pressure, equivalent to the CO2 concentration in the flue gas.226 This observation indicates that

increasing CO2 concentrations can linearly improve the extent of carbon mineralization by enhancing the

amount of dissolved CO2. In contrast, CO2 preloading remarkably increases the extent of carbon

mineralization compared to the cases without CO2 preloading, with improvements of 15.2% for 4% CO2,

19.9% for 10% CO2, 26.8% for 15% CO2, and 33.8% for 20% CO2, even at room temperature. Given the

aforementioned restrictions of glycinate species at room temperature, the preloading process enhances the

carbon mineralization in two pathways: Firstly, the preloading process allows dissolved CO2 to diffuse

                                              Page 61 of 210

sufficiently in the aqueous phase before the reaction starts, thereby eliminating mass transfer as a limiting

factor at room temperature; Further, the absence of Mg2+ ions during the preloading process prevents the

formation of Mg-glycinate complex and ensures the preferential combination between CO2 and glycinate.

In this situation, the extent of carbon mineralization in the preloading case can reach up to 64.8% at room

temperature with a 20% CO2 flue gas. Compared to the room-temperature results from the 20% CO2 case

without CO2 preloading and the pure-CO2 case in approach 1, the carbon mineralization is greatly enhanced

due to the removal of restrictions by gas preloading. Hence, the preloading approach enables the use of Na-

glycinate as an effective carbon-capture solvent at room temperature, which especially facilitates the

subsequent large-scale industrial applications because of its heating-free protocol.

3.3.2. Chemical and structural transition during carbon mineralization

 Infrared spectroscopy and wide-angle X-ray scattering were utilized to verify the success of brucite

mineralization by analyzing the solid products. Attenuated Total Reflection-Fourier Transform Infrared

(ATR FT-IR) spectroscopy was first used to determine the change of chemical bond after integrated CO2

capture of mineralization. As shown in Figure 3-5, the CO32- symmetric stretching vibration at 1119 cm-1

was observed under all conditions, regardless of temperature and Na-glycinate concentration, confirming

the carbonate formation after the reactions.227 The corresponding bending vibration of CO32- was also

detected at 885 cm-1, 854 cm-1, and 798 cm-1, respectively.228 Besides, the CO32- asymmetric stretching

vibration splits into two peaks at 1485 cm-1 and 1421 cm-1, respectively, which emerges in all the carbonated

samples, indicating the existence of bicarbonate in the carbonated solid.229 Because carbonate ions exist

as the form of bicarbonate in the hydromagnesite (Mg5(CO3)4(OH)2·4H2O), we speculate the formation

of hydromagnesite after the mineralization, with the reaction associated with its generation described in

Reaction 3.2.227 The identified infrared bands of different carbonated species and the corresponding

assignments are listed in Table 3-1.

 Furthermore, the wide-angle X-ray scattering (WAXS) patterns confirmed the formation of

hydromagnesite in the carbonated absorbents. As shown in Figure 3-6 (a), emerging peaks noted with black

circles can be observed after brucite mineralization in DI water. These well-defined characteristic peaks are

assigned to hydromagnesite (PDF 98-001-1519, noted with open circle). It was observed that the emerging

hydromagnesite peaks are not as robust as those of brucite (PDF 98-000-0130, noted with closed circle),

indicating a small extent of carbon mineralization. Additionally, the peak intensity of hydromagnesite

showed negligible changes with temperatures, demonstrating that mineralization with DI water was not

significantly affected by temperature, which is in agreement with the EoC results.230231 In sharp contrast,

the presence of Na-glycinate solution, particularly at elevated temperature, shows more characteristic peaks

of hydromagnesite with stronger intensity. As shown in Figure 3-6 (b) & (c), most of the collected solid

remained as uncarbonated brucite at 25ºC, while the formation of hydromagnesite was enhanced gradually

with increasing temperature. Hydromagnesite's characteristic peaks emerged clearly at 90ºC, indicating a

significant formation of hydromagnesite after the reactions.231 Besides, it is important to note that

hydromagnesite became dominant with 1M Na-glycinate when the temperature rose to 90ºC, while it began

to dominate from 75 ºC with 2.5M Na-glycinate. These results are in good accordance with the extent of

carbon mineralization base on the TGA data. Therefore, it is verified that the hydromagnesite is the

exclusive product of low-temperature brucite carbon mineralization, and the composition of

hydromagnesite is facilitated by increasing temperature and alkaline solution concentration. Though

hydromagnesite is a metastable carbonate form in low temperature range, the favored formation of

hydromagnesite can be attributed to the alkaline environment created by Na-glycinate and the relative low

reaction temperature range (25 - 90 ºC).232,233

3.3.3. Morphological transition during carbon mineralization

 Particle properties such as morphology, size and porous structure changed during mineralization.

They are characterized and discussed below.

                                              Page 63 of 210

3.3.3.1. Particle morphology analysis

 The morphology changes of the solid phase during the mineralization were revealed by scanning

electron microscope (SEM). According to SEM images shown in Figure 3-7 (a), the unreacted brucite

particles present as hexagonal flakes with diameters of several microns, which is typical for brucite because

of its hexagonal crystal structure.234 However, it gradually transited into nest-like hydromagnesite after CO2

mineralization. In the case of 1M Na-glycinate at 50ºC, the spherical nest-like hydromagnesite particles

with a diameter of around 10m appeared (Figure 3-7 (b)), which is a typical particle shape of

hydromagnesite at pH range of 8 - 9.235236 As the temperature rose to 90ºC, the hydromagnesite particles

became larger and more compact (Figure 3-7 (c)). Additionally, some of the nest-like particles transformed

into flat lamellar particles due to a pH variation from 9.04 at 75 ºC to 9.19 at 90 ºC, which is consistent to

the previous studies.236237 Furthermore, as shown in Figure 3-7 (d) & (e), mineralization with 2.5M Na-

glycinate solution generated more lamellar-shape particles and possessed a more compact structure

compared to counterparts with 1M Na-glycinate solution, which can be attributed to higher pH values

resulting from higher Na-glycinate concentrations (9.57 at 75 ºC and 9.60 at 90 ºC) . Particularly, the

lamellar-shape particles dominated in the carbonated product at 90ºC, indicating that the increase in

temperature caused an elevation in alkalinity.236

3.3.3.2. Particle size and distribution analysis

 The transition of particle size distribution was investigated to reveal the mechanism of brucite

carbonation. As shown in Figure 3-8, the particle size distribution indicates that the smaller brucite particles

transformed to larger hydromagnesite particles after mineralization. Moreover, the hydromagnesite

particles tend to become larger with increasing reaction temperature. Specifically, most of the unreacted

brucite particles have a diameter smaller than 10 µm. However, the particle sizes only slightly increased

after CO2 mineralization with DI water at all temperatures, indicating that temperature has minimal impact

on the carbon mineralization process (Figure 3-8 (a)). This is consistent with the EoC results (Figure 3-3).

In contrast, the hydromagnesite particles tend to grow larger with the addition of Na-glycinate solution,

                                               Page 64 of 210

especially at higher temperatures (Figure 3-8 (b) & (c)). These results are in agreement with morphological

evolution as discussed. Besides, the smaller particles around 0.3 m tend to dissolve and mineralize

preferentially due to its enhanced dissolution mechanisms, which make particles grew during mineralization

and result in nest-like or lamellar hydromagnesite. It is worth noting that a narrow particle size distribution

around 20 m is observed in the carbonated products with both 1 M and 2.5 M Na-glycinate solutions at

90 ºC, indicating a uniform morphology in the carbonated products. Given the remarkable extents of carbon

mineralization at 90 ºC with Na-glycinate solution, the observation evidence the substantial formation of

hydromagnesite with large and uniform particle size.

3.3.3.3. Pore structure analysis

 Since hydromagnesite particles grew in tandem with the extent of carbon mineralization, the

corresponding pore structure was further studied using BET to elucidate the influence of particle

aggregation on pore change. N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms were conducted at 77 K to determine

cumulative pore volumes of different carbonated products. As shown in Figure 3-9, the cumulative pore

volume curves illustrate the total pore volume as a function of pore size. In detail, the solid products

obtained with DI water possess the highest pore volume, which significantly larger than the unreacted

brucite (Figure 3-9 (a)). The increasing pore volume indicates the formation of hydromagnesite with a

porous structure, likely due to the favored pH value to form nest-like hydromagneiste particles.233 However,

the addition of Na-glycinate enhanced carbon mineralization by accelerating the CO2 dissolution, causing

the elevated hydromagnesite fraction and significant morphological changes. As shown in Figure 3-9 (b),

the solid products obtained with 1M Na-glycinate solutions exhibit higher pore volumes than the unreacted

brucite at 25ºC and 50ºC, likely due to the formation of the nest-like hydromagnesite (Figure 3-7 (b)).

Conversely, the products obtained at higher temperatures like 75ºC and 90 ºC, though have higher

hydromagnesite fraction, present lower pore volume than the unreacted brucite. It demonstrates that higher

reaction temperature with Na-glycinate resulted in a morphological change of the produced

hydromagnesite. These observations are consistent with previous studies, which suggest that nest-like

                                              Page 65 of 210

hydromagnesite particles tend to transfer into their lamellar counterpart with a significantly lower pore

volume as increasing alkalinity.233,238 Therefore, the low pore volume of the carbonated products at high

temperatures can be attributed to the formation of flat lamellar hydromagnesite under favorable alkalinity

conditions. Besides, it is observed that the carbonated products exhibit lower pore volume when higher

concentrations of Na-glycinate solutions are applied (Figure 3-9 (c)). The solid products obtained with

2.5M Na-glycinate solutions have lower pore volume than the unreacted brucite at all temperatures,

verifying the favorable formation of lamellar hydromagnesite with limited pore volume (Figure 3-7 (d) &

(e)). Additionally, it is also worth noting that the highest pore volume and specific surface area are observed

at 50ºC for all solutions, which indicates a less compact particle structure is always favored at this

temperature neglecting the applied solvent type (Figure 3-10). The tunable morphology of the generated

hydromagnesite via our approach provides varying potential applications for carbonate-bearing products.

The nest-like hydromagnesite with high pore volume and surface area is particularly effective for flame

retardant materials, while the hydromagnesite with elongated lamellar particles can provide extra

mechanical reinforcement as additives.239,240 Those findings inspire the further advancement of brucite

carbon mineralization at moderate conditions.

3.3.3.4. Mechanisms for the morphology variation

 To further correlate the carbon mineralization and the morphology of generated hydromagnesite,

particle sizes and cumulative pore volumes of the carbonated products were compared (Table 3-2). When

carbon mineralization proceeded in H2O without any carbon-capture solvent, the relatively low extents of

carbon mineralization resulted in slight increase in particle size and significant increase in pore volume in

the carbonated products, indicating a minor formation of porous hydromagnesite particles with nest-like

morphology.233,241 In this case, the decreasing CO2 capacity of H2O with increasing temperature makes CO2

dissolution the controlling step, which means the proceeding of carbon mineralization is limited by the

amount of hydrated CO2 (Figure 3-1). Hence, it is difficult to achieve high extents, especially at high

temperatures, resulting in a suppressed release of hydroxide ions (OH-) from Mg(OH)2 dissolution and

                                              Page 66 of 210

further creating a favored alkalinity for nest-like hydromagneiste formation (pH 8 - 9). The same variations

in particle size and pore volume were observed when 1 M Na-glycinate was used at low temperatures (25

ºC and 50 ºC). Conversely, CO2 dissolution rate is significantly enhanced at high temperatures or

concentration of Na-glycinate, making carbonate precipitation become the controlling step (Figure 3-1). In

this case, the released Mg2+ cations are consumed rapidly to reach a high extent of carbon mineralization,

which further facilitates the release of OH- and improves the alkalinity of the slurry. Therefore, the lamellar

shape hydromagnesite particles with large particle size and low pore volume are observed due to the favored

pH range of 9 - 10.4. The observation emphasizes the correlation between carbon mineralization extent and

the morphology of carbonate products, providing insights into potential pathways for carbonate

morphology tuning.

3.4. Conclusion

 In this study, brucite was comprehensively studied for integrated CO2 capture and mineralization

using regenerable Na-glycinate solutions (0 - 2.5M) at different temperatures (25 - 90ºC). It demonstrates

that the presence of CO2 capture solvent, Na-glycinate, can enhance the extent of carbon mineralization at

mild temperature (<100 ºC). In the optimal scenario, the extent of carbon mineralization can reach up to

75.5% with 2.5M Na-glycinate at 90ºC. Additionally, the feasibility of the system to separate CO2 from

industrial tail gas was confirmed using simulated binary flue gas (N2 and CO2), archiving a carbon

mineralization extent of 64.8% with 2.5 M Na-glycinate at room temperature.

 Hydromagnesite is the exclusive product of brucite carbon mineralization due to the favorable

temperature and alkalinity conditions during the reactions. Morphological and structural characterization

indicate the formation of hydromagnesite with varying particle sizes and shapes depending on the

conditions. Nest-like hydromagnesite with large pore volume and surface area generated at low Na-

glycinate concentrations and low temperatures. Increasing temperature and Na-glycinate concentration

caused variation in alkalinity, resulting in larger particle sizes and an elevated fraction of lamellar

                                              Page 67 of 210

hydromagnesite, which sharply reduced the pore volume. These findings highlight that the excellent carbon

mineralization performance at mild temperatures provides insights into feasible energy-efficient carbon

mineralization approaches for industrial application. Intriguingly, the controllable morphology of the

carbonated product allows various further applications of the generated hydromagnesite.

4.1. Introduction

 The escalating dependence on combustion and consumption of fossil fuel-based

 resources to meet our energy needs has increased global atmospheric CO2 levels.173

 Hence, sustainable and scalable solutions are being advanced to capture, convert, utilize,

 and store (CCUS) CO2 emissions in ever-increasing demand to develop a low-carbon

 energy society. Several conventional approaches, such as geological sequestration, ocean

 disposal, mineral carbonation, and biological ﬁxation exist to manage and reduce CO2

 emissions. Gibbins and coworkers’ research has reported that the key aspects of CO2

 processing are its capture and storage.177 Therefore, to remove the existing anthropogenic

 CO2, various techniques for carbon capture and storage (CCS) are necessary. Specifically,

 CCS technologies can be categorized into four types: absorption, adsorption, separation

 membranes, and cryogenic distillation.178,179,180,181 As one of the promising methods for

 chemical adsorption, carbon mineralization garners significant research interest due to its

 exceptional product safety and long-term CO2 storage capacity. However, it is crucial to

 progress integrated CO2 capture and mineralization pathways as an alternative to discrete

 CCUS approaches.242

 Apart from dealing with the existing anthropogenic CO2, one of the most

 challenging industries to decarbonize is that of hydrogen (H2) production. H2 is a

 favorable alternative renewable energy form due to its natural abundance and high energy

                                        Page 81 of 210

density.243,244,245 However, it is a secondary energy form that can only be gained from

primary fuels like coal, natural gas, or solar energy.246 In industry, the most widely-used

H2-producing approach is steam methane reforming (CH4 + H2O → CO + 3H2)

associated with a simultaneous water-gas shift (CO + H2O → CO2 + H2) reaction. Though

the combustion of H2 fuel is a carbon-net-zero process, the production of H2 results in 3%

global CO2 emissions (∼1.2 Gt CO2/year) requiring subsequent capture and storage.247

Existing approaches to separate CO2 from H2 include solvent, sorbent, and membrane-

based separation strategies. Therefore, to achieve the goals of reducing the existing CO2

in the atmosphere and separating CO2 post-hydrogen production, effective methods to

capture CO2 under the condition of H2 production are required.

 The removal of CO2 via carbon mineralization is a process that converts gaseous

CO2 into solid carbonates and realizes permanent carbon storage, which is a

thermodynamically downhill pathway. As an exemplar of the pathway, integrated carbon

capture and mineralization has been reported to be an effective approach.58,248 The

capture and conversion of CO2 in the integrated process is inspired by the natural uptake

and spontaneous conversion of CO2 to Ca- or Mg-carbonate, starting from Ca- and Mg-

bearing silicate minerals and rocks in aqueous environments at elevated temperatures and

pressures.43,58 Interestingly, combining thermodynamically favorable CO2 mineralization

processes with energy-intensive resource conversions results in a net reduction in the

overall required energy and contributes to inherent carbon removal.

 Table 4-1 gives some typical starting minerals for carbon mineralization and the

free energy change during carbon mineralization.192 The negative free energy change of

the reactions indicates the spontaneity of the process and the chemical stability of the

                                   Page 82 of 210

carbonate products. Apart from the spontaneity of the reaction, it is also worth noting that

the natural abundance of silicate minerals ensures an extensive carbon storage potential

cost-effectively. Further, the main products of carbon mineralization, like silica,

carbonates, or iron oxides, have multiple industrial applications after purification. Mostly,

post-combustion CO2 capture and mineralization have been widely investigated to reveal

the impact of temperature and CO2 partial pressure.192,249 As reported by Gadikota et. al.,

Mg-bearing olivine ((Mg,Fe)2SiO4) showed 80% conversion to Mg-bearing carbonate at

partial pressures of 100-150 atm and temperatures between 150–185 °C.43 Extensive

work on low-temperature capture and mineralization has been reported by Gadikota and

co-workers.30,34,81 These accelerated carbon mineralization studies suggest that the

relatively high conversions of Ca- and Mg-silicates to their respective carbonates can

occur at conditions that correspond to low-temperature WGSR. Nevertheless, enhanced

carbon removal via carbon mineralization at high temperatures remains less researched.

Theoretically, the elevated temperatures in carbon mineralization increase the sorbents’

dissolution kinetics and reactivity of Ca- and/or Mg-bearing silicates, which potentially

enhance the precipitation of the corresponding solid carbonates and result in higher

carbon mineralization levels. Though carbon mineralization can occur spontaneously in a

natural environment, multiple criteria have to be satisfied to achieve a relatively high

carbon mineralization extent.20,21 Firstly, the particle size of the starting silicate materials

needs to be in the nano-scale to the micron-scale range to ensure a sufficient reaction

contact area. Additionally, the participation of aqueous media is also significant when the

direct gas-solid reaction is kinetically limited. Some previous research has been

conducted to investigate the direct gas-solid carbon mineralization of different hydroxide

                                    Page 83 of 210

or silicate minerals. Though the feasibility of the approach is confirmed, significantly

high temperatures or pressures are required to achieve acceptable carbonate

conversion.250 To improve the energy effectiveness and increase the reaction rate,

aqueous carbon mineralization was developed, which applies aqueous phases as media to

enhance the ion distribution and combination.251 In this situation, the dissolution

behaviors of the carbon sorbents play an important role in the carbon mineralization

process. When it comes to silicate-based carbon mineralization, Si coordination is one of

the key factors in determining the dissolution behaviors of the initial silicate materials,

which further impact the reactivity and carbonate precipitation. Specifically, phases with

less non-bridging oxygens like -SiO4 (Q0) or -SiO3 (Q2) tend to be more soluble than

amorphous SiO2 (Q4).252 This solubility distinction potentially leads to different

dissolution behaviors and corresponding carbon mineralization extents for silicate

minerals with varying Si coordination.

 Although the feasibility is well illustrated, the high-temperature silicate carbon

mineralization pathway with aqueous phase suffers certain scientific gaps. First, the

influence of the silica passivation layer and its limiting impact on carbon mineralization

remains less understood. Secondly, though the influence of stirring has been extensively

considered, the non-stirring modality has not been explored.76 Thirdly, there is a limited

calibrated understanding of the chemical controls of aqueous fluids on the origin, growth,

chemistry, and morphology of reaction-inhibiting passivation fronts in Mg-bearing

silicate minerals. Fourth, though the different dissolution mechanisms of silicate

materials with different Si coordination in both acidic and alkaline solutions have been

investigated in detail previously, the impact of different Si coordination on carbon

                                   Page 84 of 210

 mineralization and the evolution of Si coordination during the process has seldom been

      studied. 253–255

 In this work, Mg-silicate materials with different Si coordination and crystallinity

 are selected as the carbon sorbents in high-temperature carbon mineralization given their

 high carbon capacity, stability, and natural abundance. By evaluating the extent of carbon

 mineralization with different carbon sorbents and tracing the evolution of Si coordination

 during carbon mineralization, we novelty reveal how the Si coordination in silicate

 minerals impacts their carbon mineralization performance. The research questions we

 addressed in this study can be summarized as: (i) How do the crystal size and chemical

 compositions of the synthesized amorphous-crystalline Mg-silicate (AC Mg-silicate)

 evolve during the thermal treatment? (ii)What are the carbon mineralization

 performances of Mg-silicate with different Si coordination at high temperature and

 pressure? (iii) What structure and morphology changes have been made from the carbon

 mineralization, especially the Si coordination evolution? To address these questions, we

 traced the formation process of the synthesized starting materials via in-operando multi-

 scale X-ray scattering measurement and investigated the carbon mineralization process

 with different aqueous media including H2O and 1M NaHCO3 solution at 200°C with 20

 atm CO2 partial pressure. Afterwards, the solid products were detected by multiple

 characterization approaches to reveal the structural and morphological evolution.

4.2. Experimental Methods

 Magnesium nitrate hexahydrate, Mg(NO3)26H2O (Sigma-Aldrich, >98% purity)

 and Tetraethyl orthosilicate (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.999% purity) were used for the synthesis

 of amorphous-crystalline (AC) Mg-silicate. While the highly-crystalline (HC) Mg-silicate

                                        Page 85 of 210

 was obtained from Xi'An Function Material Group Co. Ltd. The carbon capacity of the

 AC and HC Mg-silicate were evaluated with bone-dry CO2 gas (Airgas, 99.8% purity)

 via high-temperature carbon mineralization process. Deionized water (18.2 MΩ·cm,

 Millipore) was used throughout all the experiments as required.

4.2.1. Synthesis Routes

 The amorphous Mg-silicate precursors were synthesized via the sol-gel method,

 and then the precursors were calcined in a muffle furnace to obtain the AC Mg-silicate

 samples, which were used as carbon sorbents in subsequent experiments.

 The amorphous-crystalline (AC) Mg-silicate was synthesized by magnesium

 nitrate hexahydrate and tetraethyl orthosilicate.45,256 In this route, 17g tetraethyl

 orthosilicate was dissolved in 600 ml 1M nitrite acid, and then 41g magnesium nitrate

 hexahydrate was added into the solution with stirring for 4h at room temperature. After

 that, a highly viscous gel was formed by drying the stirred solution at 80°C for 24h, and

 the gel was calcined at 800°C for 30 minutes in the air with a 10°C/min ramping rate to

 realize the amorphous-crystalline transition (Figure 4-1). During this process,

 magnesium nitrate hexahydrate is the Mg source and TEOS provides silica. The synthesis

 reaction can be expressed as Reaction 4.1,

2Mg(NO3)26H2O + (CH3CH2O)4Si → Mg2SiO4 + 4CH3CH2OH +8H2O + 4HNO3

 (Reaction 4.1)

4.2.3. Carbon Mineralization Experiments

 (Micro Bench Top Reactor, Parr Instruments Co., USA). All the reactions were operated

 vacuum filtered and washed with deionized water three times. Then the washed samples

 were dried in a heating oven at 80°C for 24h for further characterization.

4.3.1.1. Formation of Crystalline Phases from Gel Precursors

 Generally, Mg-silicate precursors can undergo amorphous-crystalline transition during calcination,

break the linkages. Besides, the thermal energy provided by the calcination allows the rearrangement of

crystallinity and phase evolution with thermal treatment from 25 to 800 °C. The WAXS measurement

provides information on the crystallographic plane change, in which the location of characteristic peaks

corresponds to the interplane distance in the reciprocal space. The smooth curve at room temperature

indicates the amorphous nature of the Mg-silicate precursor without any long-range ordered crystalline

structure (Figure 4-3 (a)). As temperature increases, the calcined precursor first exhibits characteristic

peaks at 1.61 Å-1, 2.51 Å-1, 2.55 Å-1, and 4.20 Å-1 at 211 °C, corresponding to the (021), (131), (112), and

(004) peaks of crystalline forsterite, which indicates structural rearrangement and the proceeding of

amorphous-crystalline transition (PDF 01-078-1371). The occurrence of those specific crystallographic

further increases to 431 °C, revealing the appearance of peaks at 2.96 Å-1 and 4.23 Å-1 implying growth

along the (112), (260) crystallographic planes and an elevated crystallinity of the sample (Figure 4-3 (a)).

Further enhancing the calcination temperature to 665°C, the additional peaks emerge at 1.68 Å-1, 1.79 Å-1,

2.09 Å-1, 2.23 Å-1, 2.75 Å-1, and 3.57 Å-1, corresponds to (101), (111), (031), (221) and (222) planes of

forsterite crystallographic phase. The occurrence of these crystallographic planes indicates the nucleation

and crystal growth of specific crystals during comprehensive crystallization process. Further temperature

increase also results in a peak intensity enhancement, verifying the continuous growth of the generated

which is a typical form containing Q0 Si coordination.260

4.3.2. Carbon Mineralization of Mg-silicate

 Generally, carbon mineralization with aqueous media is a multiphase reaction that

 can be divided into three different sectors: (i) the hydration of gaseous CO2; (ii) the

 dissolution of carbon sorbents; (iii) the formation of carbonate precipitation. To be more

                                              Page 92 of 210

 concrete, CO2 hydration occurs at the interface between the gaseous phase and aqueous

 phase to generate carbonate species (Reaction 4.2, 4.3, 4.4). Simultaneously, the carbon

 sorbents (Mg-silicate in this study) dissolved to release Mg2+ into the solution (Reaction

 4.5). Then, the carbonate-bearing products precipitate out via the combination between

 Mg2+ and carbonate species (Reaction 4.6). And the overall carbon mineralization

 process in aqueous phase can be expressed by Reaction 4.7.

       CO2 + H2O  H2CO3                                (Reaction 4.2)

       H2CO3  H+ + HCO3-                               (Reaction 4.3)

       HCO3-  H++CO32-                                (Reaction 4.4)

       Mg2SiO4  2Mg2+ + SiO44-                            (Reaction 4.5)

       Mg2+ + CO32-  MgCO3                               (Reaction 4.6)

       Mg2SiO4 + 2CO2 + 2H2O  2MgCO3 + H4SiO4                          (Reaction 4.7)

 Hence, the proceeding of carbon mineralization can be jointly determined by the

 three different phases. However, the 20 atm initial CO2 partial pressure applied in this

 study guaranteed an excessive supply of CO2 based on Henry’s law.267 In this case, the

 extents of carbon mineralization distinction can be attributed to the different dissolution

 mechanisms of different carbon sorbents (AC and HC Mg-silicate) with different stirring

 modes, or the different carbonate formation levels caused by the solvent difference (H2O

 and 1 M NaHCO3). A smaller particle size in AC Mg-silicate facilitates its dissolution,

 but the lower crystallinity of AC Mg-silicate also enhances Si passivation and suppresses

 spontaneous dissolution in comparison to HC Mg-silicate, which may result in different

 carbon mineralization performances with different stirring modes.

4.3.2.1. Extent of Carbon Mineralization

                                         Page 93 of 210

 The extent of carbon mineralization is calculated based on the thermogravimetric

analysis results via Equation 4.1 and represents the percentage of theoretical CO2

capacity that has been achieved during mineralization. Compared with the HC Mg-

silicate sample, the AC Mg-silicate sample exhibits 1.0% higher carbon mineralization

extent in the water case without stirring and 6.2% in the NaHCO3 case without stirring,

which is intuitive due to its finer particle size (Figure 4-6). However, when stirring is

applied to both of the Mg-silicate (AC Mg-silicate and AC Mg-silicate) sorbents, the

carbon mineralization extent of AC Mg-silicate basically remained the same while the

carbon mineralization extent of HC Mg-silicate increased by 9.0 % and 15.2% in H2O

and NaHCO3, respectively. The distinct impact of stirring can be attributed to the

different dissolution mechanisms resulting from the different Si coordination, which is

discussed in the next section. Moreover, as Figure 4-6 shown, the addition of NaHCO3

solution effectively enhances the carbon mineralization extent of AC Mg-silicate with and

without stirring by 16.5% and 18.5%, respectively. As for the HC Mg-silicate, the

NaHCO3 enhancement for stirring mode is 19.5%, and for no-stirring mode is 13.3%.

The NaHCO3 solution acts as a buffer to maintain a relatively stable pH value of the

solution during the carbon mineralization process, which can facilitate the release of

Mg2+ and dissolution of CO2 simultaneously to enhance the precipitation of magnesite.

Additionally, carbon mineralization with MgO at the same conditions was conducted as a

reference. Due to the significantly higher reactivity compared with silicate samples, the

carbon mineralization extent of MgO sorbents reached up to 93.3% in H2O without

stirring, and the addition of NaHCO3 hardly improved the extent. Nevertheless, unlike

earth-abundant silicate minerals, the high reactivity of the MgO naturally restricts its

                                   Page 94 of 210

 abundance. On the other hand, the Mg-silicate materials are promising CO2 storage

 sources due to their much higher reserves and comparable carbon mineralization

 performance. Some previous research also reported the Mg-silicate minerals’ carbon

 mineralization extents under different conditions. Gadikota and coworkers have reported

 the carbonate process of olivine minerals with a mean particle size of 21.4m in 185°C,

 139 atm CO2 partial pressure, and an aqueous phase of 1M NaCl + 0.64M NaHCO3

 solution. The extents of carbonation are measured as 56.6% for 1 hour and 85.3% for 3

 hours reacting duration.202 The greater extent of carbon mineralization in this research

 indicates that the elevation of CO2 partial pressure and modulated solvent can further

 facilitate the process kinetically and thermodynamically. Nevertheless, the approach

 proposed in this study provides a more energy-efficient pathway to achieve comparable

 carbon mineralization performance.

4.3.2.2. Chemical and Morphological Characteristics of Carbonated Materials

4.3.2.2.1. Si Coordination Evolution

 In typical silicate dissolution, the release of the metal ions decreases the Metal/Si

 atom ratio and tends to create a Si-rich passivation layer at the surface of particles, which

 suppresses further dissolution of the materials.87 Generally, the formation and dissolution

 of the Si passivation layer can be a key factor to control the dissolution of the Mg-

 silicates, which further impact the performance of carbon mineralization. Mg-silicate

 carbon sorbents (HC and AC) have distinct Si coordinations, which play an important

 role in the formation of passivation layers. Therefore, XPS analysis was employed to

 study how the Si coordination difference in the unreacted HC and AC magnesium silicate

 impacts the phases and compositions of carbonate-bearing products. The XPS spectra

                                          Page 95 of 210

were calibrated based on the C1s peak (284.8 eV).268 Figure 4-7 shows the Si 2p

deconvolution results for the unreacted Mg-silicate sorbents and the carbonated solids in

different conditions with stirring. The AC Mg-silicate exhibits SiO4 peak at around 102.7

eV with an intensified SiO3 peak located at 103.7 eV (Figure 4-7 (a-1)). The observation

could result from the decoupled MgSiO3 and MgO, which are not fully crystallized to

form Mg2SiO4 during thermal treatment.269 As a comparison, the major peak at 102.7 eV

in the unreacted HC Mg-silicate corresponds to the SiO4 tetrahedra, while the minor

shoulder at around 105 eV indicates the presence of amorphous SiO2 (Figure 4-7 (b-

1)).270,271 Further, the Si 2p spectra shift after carbon mineralization reveals the phase

evolution that affects the carbon capture performance. During the Mg-silicate carbon

mineralization in aqueous phases, the Mg2+ released from carbon sorbents is consumed to

form carbonate precipitates, which reduce the Mg/Si ratio on the sorbent particle surface

and create Si coordination with more non-bridging oxygens like -SiO3 or SiO2.

Specifically, for carbonate-bearing AC Mg-silicate in water with stirring, the SiO4 peak at

102.7 eV significantly diminishes, and the SiO3 peak at 103.7 eV is greatly intensified

with a newly detected minor SiO2 peak, which can be attributed to the continuous Mg2+

release and the formation of amorphous Si layer (Figure 4-7 (a-2)). Simultaneously, the

participation of NaHCO3 enhanced the CO2 dissolution due to its pH buffering effect,

which provided more carbonate species to consume Mg2+ and led to a higher SiO2

composition with less SiO4 residue. Consequently, Figure 4-7 (a-3) shows the

elimination of the SiO4 phase and the expansion of the SiO2 phase, referring to a further

Mg2+ release and accelerated carbonate formation. The observation is consistent with the

carbon mineralization performance, in which enhanced sorbent dissolution resulted in

                                   Page 96 of 210

higher extents of carbon mineralization. Additionally, the carbonate-bearing HC Mg-

silicate in water shows a reduced SiO4 peak at 102.7 eV with a newly emerged SiO3 peak

at 103.9 eV and an enhanced SiO2 peak at around 105 eV, which indicates an analogous

Mg2+ dissolution process to the carbonate-bearing AC Mg-silicate (Figure 4-7 (b-2)).269–
271
 The addition of NaHCO3 also similarly leads to a higher SiO2 composition with less

SiO4 residue due to enhanced CO2 dissolution and carbonate formation (Figure 4-7 (b-

3)). On the other hand, compared with the stirring mode, non-stirring mode cases

demonstrate a more intensified amorphous SiO2 peak around 105 eV, indicating the

accumulation of amorphous Si layer outside the particles (Figure 4-8). The accumulated

Si-rich layer suppresses the diffusion of Mg2+ ions by reducing the exposure of unreacted

carbon sorbents to the aqueous media, thereby restricting the subsequent carbonate

formation as well.272 In the non-stirring mode, the dissolution of the associated

amorphous Si layer can be sluggish due to the diminished mass transfer and lack of

particle collision, making it the rate-controlling step of the overall reaction.

 Further, the compositional percentages of different Si coordination in unreacted

and carbonate-bearing materials were also determined from the high-resolution XPS

spectra. For AC Mg-silicate, the 75% SiO3 appearing in the raw sorbent other than

Mg2SiO4 indicates the presence of low-crystallinity phases. After carbon mineralization

with stirring, the SiO3 composition increased to 95% and 84% due to Mg2+ dissolution,

with water and NaHCO3, respectively. It is worth noting that the substantial SiO2 (16%)

with disappearing SiO4 coordination in the NaHCO3 case compared with the water case

also confirms the dissolution-enhancing effect of NaHCO3 (Figure 4-9 (a)). For the HC

Mg-silicate, 98% of the Si in the raw sorbents went into the SiO4 phase, which mostly

                                    Page 97 of 210

 transferred into SiO3 (85%) and a minor part of SiO2 (5%) after carbon mineralization in

 water with stirring. On the other hand, the carbonate-bearing product with NaHCO3 has

 an elevated SiO2 level (17%), evidencing the enhanced Mg2+ release during dissolution

 (Figure 4-9 (b)). In contrast, as seen in Figure 4-9 (d), the carbonate-bearing HC Mg-

 silicate in non-stirring mode exhibits very comparable residual SiO4 amount (32% and

 28%) before and after the addition of NaHCO3, referring to the Si-rich layer’s restriction

 in Mg2+ release even with favored pH. However, the transition from -SiO4 to -SiO3 in

 carbonate-bearing AC Mg-silicate is still enhanced by 25% in NaHCO3 without stirring,

 which means the Mg2+ can dissolve continuously even with the accumulated Si-rich layer

 (Figure 4-9 (c)). In AC Mg-silicate, the larger reaction area caused by finer particle size

 and the easier Mg2+ release caused by lower crystallinity may be responsible for this

 higher resistance in Si layer dissolution constraint compared to the HC Mg-silicate.

4.3.2.2.2. Structural Arrangement and Morphological Analysis

 To further understand dissolution mechanisms and to confirm carbonate

 formation, the structural arrangement evolution of different Mg-silicates before and after

 carbon mineralization is also significant. These structural evolutions were traced using X-

 ray diffraction analyses. Figure 4-10 ((a-1), (b-1)) shows the unreacted AC and HC Mg-

 silicates exhibit characteristic peaks corresponding to crystalline Mg2SiO4 with a minor

 amount of MgO, possibly caused by surface segregation of Mg2+ cations and the

 decoupling of MgO and amorphous MgSiO3. The presence of a slight amount of SiO2 in

 the HC Mg-silicate is also consistent with the XPS results (Figure 4-7 (a-1)). The

 relatively low peak intensity of AC Mg-silicate indicates its lower crystallinity and the

 possibility of co-existence of amorphous phases. In contrast, after carbon mineralization

                                         Page 98 of 210

with stirring, the carbonate-bearing products exhibited the XRD peaks at 32.3 °, 42.8 °,

46.7 °, and 53.9 °, which align with the (104), (113), (202), and (116) planes of MgCO3,

respectively (Figure 4-10 (a-2), (a-3), (b-2), (b-3)). Based on the XRD pattern, MgCO3

is the exclusive carbonate form due to its thermodynamic stability at high temperatures

and pressures.273 Furthermore, apart from the residual Mg2SiO4, all the carbonate-bearing

samples (AC Mg-silicate and HC Mg-silicate) exhibit the XRD peak at 20 °

corresponding to (003) plane of MgSiO3 and expanded shoulders of amorphous SiO2 at

34.5 ° and 60.5 °, which originate from the decrease in the Mg/Si ratio and the formation

of Si passivation layers. Notably, Figure 4-10 shows that carbonate-bearing HC Mg-

silicate exhibits higher MgCO3 peak intensity, while AC Mg-silicate exhibits higher

amorphous SiO2 content. This suggests that due to its low crystallinity and Metal/Si ratio,

carbonate-bearing AC Mg-silicate is more likely to form amorphous SiO2 layers, which

results in a lower carbon mineralization extent with stirring as Figure 4-6 shows. On the

other hand, the carbonate-bearing Mg-silicates in the no-stirring mode show very similar

compositions compared to their counterparts in stirring mode, but with lower MgCO3

peak intensity, indicating a lower content of carbonate form (Figure 4-11). Furthermore,

compared with the AC Mg-silicate, additional MgSiO3 peaks are noted in the carbonate-

bearing HC Mg-silicates, resulting from the possible generation of crystalline MgSiO3

from Mg2SiO4 with high crystallinity (Figure 4-11 (c) & (d)).

 According to previous research, the structural and morphological arrangement of

the carbonate-bearing products can be highly impacted by the nucleation and growth

balance during the carbonate formation.274 Specifically, homogeneous and heterogeneous

nucleation are dominant in different supersaturation levels of metal cations and carbonate

                                  Page 99 of 210

species, which further determine the product morphology and carbon mineralization

extent.275 Homogeneous nucleation preferentially occurs with uniform nuclei formation

in the solvent, while the occurrence of heterogeneous nucleation is more favored on

existing secondary nucleating surfaces.276 The different nucleation mechanisms shift the

nucleation-growth balance and lead to different particle size in the products. In this case,

the morphological variation during carbon mineralization is further evaluated using FE-

SEM and particle size analyzer (PSA) to reveal the effect from nucleation mechanisms. It

is evident in Figures 4-12 (a-1) & (b-1) that Mg2SiO4 occurs in a typical spherical shape

in both unreacted AC and HC Mg-silicates. Despite the continuous aggregation and

growth of the AC Mg-silicate during thermal treatment, its overall particle size is still

smaller than the HC Mg-silicate and possesses more non-crystallized nanoscale particles,

which is also confirmed by the particle size distribution (Figure 4-14 (a)). Upon the

carbonate-bearing Mg-silicates with stirring, cubic particles in micron-scale are detected,

corresponding to the typical crystalline MgCO3 shape due to its crystallography structure

(Figure 4-12 (a-2), (a-3), (b-2), (b-3)).277,278 Although the initial particle size of the AC

Mg-silicate is smaller, the carbonate-bearing AC Mg-silicate with stirring possesses a

larger particle size than the carbonate-bearing HC Mg-silicate (Figure 4-14 (a)). As

prementioned, the enhanced Mg2+ release from the HC Mg-silicate results in a higher

supersaturation level in the solvent and favors the occurrence of homogeneous

nucleation. This accelerated carbonate nucleation suppresses the further growth of the

formed MgCO3 nuclei, which leads to smaller carbonate particles and higher carbon

mineralization extent compared with the AC counterpart. Similarly, as shown in Figure

4-14 (a), the smaller particle size is also observed after NaHCO3 addition in both AC and

                                   Page 100 of 210

HC cases, which can be attributed to the rapid nucleation with slow nuclei growth

resulting from the increasing supersaturation level of dissolved CO2. Additionally, it is

also demonstrated that the initial nanoscale particles tend to be preferentially consumed

in all cases, referring to the fact that smaller particles dissolve and carbonate first due to

the larger surface area for more effective Mg2+ release. Interestingly, the carbonate-

bearing HC Mg-silicate in NaHCO3 with the best carbon mineralization performance

demonstrates the narrowest particle size distribution with the smallest mean particle size

(8.01 m), indicating that uniform nucleation facilitates the carbonate formation to get

higher carbon mineralization extents. Hence, the stirring contributes to the enhanced

Mg2+ dissolution and subsequent homogeneous nucleation, which further results in

carbonate formation with uniform small particles and remarkable carbon mineralization

extent at favored conditions.

 Meanwhile, in the no-stirring mode, heterogeneous nucleation is more likely to

occur due to the constrained Mg2+ supersaturation level caused by the lack of shear force

and mass transfer. Therefore, large particles with broad size distribution from the non-

uniform nuclei formation and growth in the carbonate-bearing product with restricted

carbon mineralization extents are expected.279 Consequently, unlike the relatively

dispersed MgCO3 particles in the stirring cases, the cubic particles appearing in the no-

stirring cases tend to congregate together (Figure 4-13). Notably, as can be seen in

Figure 4-13 (a) & (b), the carbonate-bearing HC Mg-silicate without stirring possesses

granular particles on cubic MgCO3, which can be attributed to residual Mg2SiO4 and

associated amorphous SiO2. Given the lower prevalence of the granular layer and more

dispersed particles in the stirring cases, this unique observation supports the hypothesis

                                   Page 101 of 210

 that stirring improves the random collision probability between particles, which

 eliminates the amorphous SiO2 layer and fragments the congregated particles into small

 pieces. As shown in Figure 4-14 (b) for non-stirring cases, the widespread distribution of

 particles at large size regimes indicates heterogeneous carbonate formation and particle

 size expansion, aligned with the congregated particles observed in Figure 4-13.

 Furthermore, compared with the cases with stirring, the no-stirring mode generally results

 in lower extents of carbon mineralization, as Figure 4-6 shows. The confirmed non-

 uniform carbonate-bearing product morphology with a relatively low carbon

 mineralization extent in the no-stirring mode can be ascribed to two distinct mechanisms:

 (i) the possible accumulation of Si passivation layer reducing the exposure area of the

 unreacted material; and (ii) the favored heterogeneous nucleation leading to large-size

 particles suppressing the further carbonation. Moreover, the no-stirring case with the

 highest carbon mineralization extent also exhibits the narrowest size distribution (AC

 Mg-silicate with NaHCO3) because of the resistance in Si layer passivation, emphasizing

 the correlation between silicate dissolution and corresponding carbonate formation.

4.3.3. Insights on the Mg-silicates dissolution mechanisms and the corresponding carbon

mineralization

 The carbon mineralization with aqueous medium is a multiphase reaction, and the

 Mg2+ dissolution can be the control step for silicate materials due to their relatively slow

 dissolution rate and association of SiO2 passivation layer. Hence, revealing factors that

 can impact silicate dissolution is essential for their application in carbon mineralization.

 In this work, the HC and AC Mg-silicate with distinct particle size and crystallinity are

 investigated. For stirring mode, the mass transfer is enhanced to facilitate dissolution and

                                        Page 102 of 210

 occurrence of homogeneous nucleation. Furthermore, collisions between particles in

 stirring mode are highly favored to grind congregated particles and to avoid the formation

 of amorphous SiO2 layers over the carbonate-bearing particles. As a result, the Mg2+

 release to the aqueous phase is no longer limited by the silica passivation layer but more

 affected by the instant Mg2+ level in aqueous phase and Mg/Si ratio of the sorbents.

 Hence, the HC Mg-silicate with higher crystallinity and Mg/Si ratio results in a higher

 Mg2+ supersaturation level and achieves a higher extent of carbon mineralization (Figure

 4-6). Conversely, for the cases without stirring, the carbonate-bearing particles tend to

 congregate together due to the heterogeneous nucleation, and the associated Si

 passivation layer covers the carbonate-bearing particles to suppress the further release of

 Mg2+ by preventing the aqueous phase from reaching the unreacted surface. Therefore,

 the Mg2+ dissolution and the carbon mineralization extent are controlled by the reactive

 area, which are further influenced by the dissolution rate of the SiO2 layer and Mg2+.88,280

 Specifically, the smaller particle size of the AC Mg-silicate ensures a larger contacting

 area between the external SiO2 layer and aqueous phase, facilitating the dissolution of

 amorphous SiO2 to accelerate the exposure of inner Mg-rich area. Moreover, with more

 content of amorphous phase, the AC Mg-silicate lack long-range order and rigid lattice

 structure, allowing Mg2+ to escape from the sorbents more easily. Thus, under no-stirring

 modes, the AC Mg-silicate achieve higher carbon mineralization levels due to

 simultaneously enhanced SiO2 and Mg2+ dissolution.

pathway for the selection of optimized strategies for Mg-silicate dissolution and carbon

 ENHANCED H2 RECOVERY BY COUPLING THE WATER-GAS SHIFT REACTION

 WITH IN-SITU CARBON CAPTURE AND MINERALIZATION USING EARTH

 ABUNDANT CALCIUM- AND MAGNESIUM-SILICATES AND HYDROXIDES

5.1. Introduction

 Demand for low-cost and abundant fossil energy resources has exacerbated

greenhouse gas emissions (GHGs) leading to detrimental impacts on climate and the

environment.173 To mitigate the detrimental impacts of GHG emissions and realize a carbon

neutral future, it is essential to develop novel chemical pathways to produce clean energy

carriers such as H2 while capturing and storing CO2 emissions.281,282 As an alternative to

conventional multi-step processes to capture, compress, and store CO2 emissions,

intensified approaches that simultaneously inherently capture and store CO2 emissions in a

single – step via reactive separation are needed.283,284 The abundance of renewable energy

resources such as biomethane serve as a viable feedstock for H2 production as an alternative

to fossil – derived methane. H2 has emerged as a preferred energy carrier given the high

energy density of H2 (122 MJ kg-1) compared to alternative fossil fuel-derived resources284

and the absence of CO2 emissions on combustion.245

 Current commercial H2 production involves energy-intensive hydrogasification of

non-renewable carbonaceous fuels such as coal at high pressures (∼ 7 MPa) and

temperatures (T ∼ 1123 K) to produce methane (CH4), as represented by this reaction: C(s)

+ 2H2 → CH4. In the following step, methane is reformed in steam and in the presence of

a catalyst to produce CO and H2, also popularly known as steam methane reforming (CH4 +

                                      Page 121 of 210

H2O → CO + 3H2), along with a simultaneous water gas shift reaction (WGSR) in

equilibrium at intermediate temperatures (473 - 723 K). In the WGSR, CO and steam are

reformed simultaneously to co-produce CO2 and H2 simultaneously.285

Thermodynamically, WGSR is favored at low temperatures because it is a reversible

exothermic process using steam as the hydrogen source as Reaction 5.1 indicates:

CO + H2O → CO2 + H2 ΔH298K = -41.1kJ/mol                 (Reaction 5.1)

 However, in the low-temperature regime, WGSR is challenged by sluggish kinetics

and low CO conversions. The limiting kinetics of WGSR can be overcome by harnessing

catalysts to enhance CO conversion kinetics and yields at low temperatures, for which

various noble (Pt, Pd, Ru) and non-metal (Cu, Zn, Fe, Cr)-based supported catalysts have

been reported in precedent literature.286–288 Especially, Pt-based materials have been

reported to be promising metals for WGSR in the medium to low temperature (250-350 °C)

regime due to their high stability in oxidizing environments.289 Even though steam methane

reforming and WGSR are widely deployed for H2 production, challenges associated with

producing high purity H2 and achieving high selectivity remain.290 The competing co-

presence of CO and CO2 along with H2 from gasification and WGSR at the effluent limits

H2 selectivity and its yield. Consequently, the reactor effluent gases must be purified by the

pressure swing adsorption process operating at high pressures (1 MPa) to produce a

significant amount of high-purity H2. This challenge necessitates the development of

sustainable multiphase chemical pathways for selective and energy-efficient reactive

separation of H2 from CO2 and CO. Harnessing alkaline materials such as Ca- and Mg-

bearing hydroxides and silicates is a thermodynamically favorable pathway to enhance H2

yield.

                                       Page 122 of 210

 The proposed enhanced WGSR concept works based on the Le Chatelier’s principle,

in which the reversible gas phase WGSR when integrated with a slurry bearing Ca- and

Mg-bearing hydroxides and silicates enhances H2 yield via in-situ CO2 capture and

mineralization.291 This integrated concept has the potential to significantly improve

reaction efficiencies, minimize capital cost, and simplify the process configuration from

the two-step conventional catalytic process to a single – step process. This approach also

circumvents the thermodynamic limitations of conventional WGSR thereby enhancing the

rate of forward reaction for H2 generation, driving the reaction towards the product side by

shifting the equilibrium, and allowing high conversions of CO and steam to H 2 and solid

carbonates.

 The approach of coupling carbon mineralization as a pathway to capture and

crystallize CO2 emissions as solid carbonates is a significant departure from conventional

carbon capture and storage (CCS) technologies in which CO2 is first captured using

solvents, sorbents, and membranes and is then injected into geologic reservoirs for

storage.177,178,179,180,181 The permanence and stability of solid Ca- and Mg-bearing

carbonates 292 motivate the coupling of carbon mineralization as a pathway to separate CO2

emissions and enhance H2 yields.

 Carbon mineralization involves the reaction between silicate, hydroxide, and oxide

minerals bearing divalent cations (e.g., Ca2+, Mg2+ ions) and CO2 to produce the respective

carbonates. The negative free energy change of the reaction indicates the spontaneity of the

process and the chemical stability of the carbonate products.192 Apart from the spontaneity

of the reaction, carbon mineralization has several advantages. First, the natural abundance

of starting materials guarantees easy accessibility and affordable prices. Besides, the

                                      Page 123 of 210

alkaline minerals have a large CO2 storage capacity, providing an enormous CO2 storage

potential worldwide. Additionally, the typical products from carbon mineralization,

including silica, carbonates, and metal oxides, usually have multiple industrial applications

after purification.

 For carbon mineralization, the starting materials could be magnesium silicate

minerals such as (Mg,Fe)2SiO4 (olivine), Mg2SiO4 (forsterite), MgO (periclase), CaO

(lime), CaSiO3 (wollastonite), and Ca2+- and Fe2+-rich silicates (e.g., larnite). These

minerals are known to spontaneously react with atmospheric CO 2 to produce solid

carbonates.46,293,294 These geo-inspired mineralization pathways motivated advances in

engineered analogs to accelerate mineralization. 192,249 Prior work by Gadikota and co-

workers showed that olivine carbon mineralization extents as high as 85% are achieved at

185ºC, pCO2 of 139 atm, reaction time of 3 hours, and in 1.0 M NaCl and 0.64 M NaHCO3

solution with particle sizes of 30 µm or less.43,76,295 The observed high extents of carbon

mineralization at the temperature conditions that are similar to that of the water gas shift

reaction motivated the coupling of this mineralization pathway.

 As an alternative to these hydrothermal routes, direct gas – solid mineralization

routes296, 250 are explored but challenged by slow kinetics when Mg- or Ca-bearing silicates

are used. In this context, sorbent enhanced water gas shift reactions (WGSR) in direct gas

– solid modes were extensively studied.297–300 Compared with the widely used membrane

separation approach to separate CO2 in WGSR, the sorbent-enhanced WGSR possesses

multiple advantages, including more favorable and uniform kinetics, greater material

durability, and larger CO2 capture capacity.70,301 Conventional sorbent-enhanced water-gas

shift reactions occur in fluidized bed reactors with steam-saturated CO2 flow.302,303 The

                                      Page 124 of 210

selected sorbents are typically metal oxides (e.g., CaO) or layered double hydroxide (LDH)

(e.g., hydrotalcite) with high CO2 capture capacity.291,304,305 However, direct gas-solid

carbonation is very kinetically limited and tends to be greatly enhanced with the

participation of aqueous media, which dissolves the alkaline resource to release metal

cations that capture CO2 to form carbonate species.48,49,306,307

 While Ca-bearing oxides have been reported to be effective for enhanced water gas

shift reaction in a direct gas–solid reaction mode,70,308,309 the direct use of earth abundant

Mg- and Ca-bearing silicates in this mode is significantly challenged by mass transfer

limitations and slow kinetics of reaction. 43,76,295 The direct use of Mg- and Ca-bearing

silicates circumvents the need to use additional reagents to produce more reactive Ca- and

Mg-bearing hydroxides. To accelerate mass transfer and the kinetics of CO 2 capture using

earth abundant Mg- and Ca-bearing silicates, aqueous routes which involve the dissolution

of silicates to release Mg2+ or Ca2+ ions for capturing CO2 produced from the WGSR to

produce the respective carbonates are investigated in this work. 251 Prior studies have

reported that the dissolution of silicate minerals facilitates more robust mass transfer and

accelerates carbon mineralization.255,310,311 Prior studies have demonstrated enhanced H2

production using Mg(OH)2 for in-situ CO2 capture and mineralization.81 However, the

influence of directly using of Mg- and Ca-bearing silicates for the enhanced water gas shift

reaction has not been reported to date. The conventional hypothesis is that silica

precipitated as Mg- and Ca-bearing silicates limit mass transfer and thus lower reactivity

with CO2 to produce the respective Ca- and Mg-bearing carbonates. However, this

hypothesis has not been evaluated in the context of harnessing earth-abundant Ca- and Mg-

                                       Page 125 of 210

bearing silicates to enhance the WGSR with in-situ CO2 capture and mineralization, and is

therefore, the focus of the proposed investigations.

 Several favorable multiphase chemical interactions are necessary for this approach

to be feasible. First, the reactivity of CO and steam in the presence of Pt/Al 2O3 catalyst

needs to be robust in producing CO2 and H2 as the products in sufficient quantities. Second,

CO2 needs to be soluble in the aqueous phase to facilitate the forward WGSR for enhancing

H2 production. Third, the dissolved CO2 needs to react with Mg- and Ca-bearing silicates

to produce the respective carbonates. Fourth, the precipitated silica should not limit mass

transfer and the subsequent carbon mineralization behavior. To unlock the full potential of

enhanced H2 production coupled with carbon mineralization, it is essential to identify the

rate limiting factor and advance strategies to overcome these limitations. To this end, the

multiphase reaction pathways are assembled such that the gas phase catalytic WGSR is

coupled with slurry phase carbon mineralization as shown in Figure 5-1. To identify and

address the factors limiting H2 production coupled with carbon mineralization, several key

research questions are addressed: (i) What are the chemical mechanisms underlying

enhanced H2 conversion with in-situ carbon mineralization using Mg- and Ca-bearing

hydroxides and silicates? (ii) What is the influence of the silicate vs. hydroxide reactivity

on enhancing H2 production with carbon mineralization? (iii) In this multi-step reaction

pathway, what is the rate limiting step and how can this step be accelerated? Addressing

these questions unlock new insights into the mechanisms and the scientific feasibility of

enhancing H2 conversion with in-situ CO2 capture and mineralization using Mg- and Ca-

bearing silicates and contrasting with that of the respective hydroxides.

5.2. Materials and Methodology

5.2.1. Materials

 All experiments to investigate enhancement in H2 conversion with the WGSR coupled with in-situ

CO2 capture and mineralization are conducted in the presence of platinum on alumina support (Pt/Al 2O3)

catalyst.308 The Pt/Al2O3 catalyst is selected due to its high activity, superior thermal stability, and high

product specificity to H2 and CO2.312–314 Specifically, Pt/Al2O3 catalyst is sized as 3.2 mm pellets with a

composition of 0.5 wt% Pt and is procured from Sigma Alrich, USA. Mg-bearing hydroxide (brucite

(Mg(OH)2)) and silicate (forsterite (Mg2SiO4)) used for in-situ CO2 capture and mineralization are obtained

from Fisher Chemical and Xi'An Function Material Group Co. Ltd, respectively. Ca-bearing hydroxide

(portlandite (Ca(OH)2)) and silicate (wollastonite (CaSiO3)) also used for in-situ CO2 capture and

mineralization are acquired from Thermo Fischer Scientific Co., USA. These Mg- and Ca-bearing silicates

and hydroxides are ball-milled in a bench-top mixer mill (8000 M Mixer/Mill® by SPEX® Sample Prep)

to obtain raw materials with comparable mean particle sizes (5.57 µm to 10.38 µm). The particle sizes of

the milled samples are determined using Laser Diffraction Particle Size Analyzer (Anton Paar). Deionized

water (18.2 MΩ·cm, Millipore) is used throughout all the experiments as required. Ultra-high purity CO

gas (99.999% purity) is supplied by Airgas Co., USA

5.2.2. Experimental set-up for enhanced WGSR and carbon mineralization

 Experiments to investigate enhanced H2 conversion with in-situ CO2 capture and

mineralization are conducted in a 50 mL stainless steel high-pressure stirred reactor (Micro

Bench Top Reactor, Parr Instruments Co., USA) in a multiphase reaction environment. A

schematic representation of the experimental setup is shown in Figure 5-2. Catalytic

conversion of CO and steam occurs in the gas phase in the presence of Pt/Al 2O3 catalyst.

The surface area, pore volume, and average pore diameter of Pt/Al 2O3 catalyst are 163.13

m2/g, 0.303 cc/g, and 6.282 nm, respectively (Table 5-1). Prior to CO gas injection, 3 g (0.5

wt.%) of the commercial Pt/Al2O3 catalyst is placed in an aluminum basket and suspended
                                             Page 127 of 210

in the headspace of the reactor ensuring the basket is not in contact with the sorbent slurry.

CO flow rate from the cylinder is controlled by using a mass flow controller (MFC). CO 2

capture and mineralization occur in the slurry phase bearing Mg- and Ca-bearing

hydroxides and silicates (3 g) and deionized water (10 mL). The headspace of the reactor

is purged with CO gas for 15 minutes to evacuate gaseous impurities before it is pressurized

and sealed tightly. After reaching the desired pressure set point for CO at 20 bar, the heater

is switched on to achieve a setpoint of 250 ºC with a constant stirring rate of 300 rpm,

signaling the start of the experiment. Steam is generated by the vaporization of water in the

slurry. The corresponding partial pressure of steam is determined by the temperature in the

steam table (Table 5-2, 5-3). The reactor outlet is sealed with heating tape to avoid the

possibility of steam condensation and is connected directly to a gas chromatograph to

analyze the concentrations of gaseous products. The pH of the slurry increased to 9.95 for

Mg(OH)2, 8.69 for Mg2SiO4, 12.37 for Ca(OH)2, and 8.98 for CaSiO3, respectively. The

experiments are performed at different reaction times ranging from 3 to 12 hours. After the

completion of the reaction, the reactor is cooled down to ambient temperature. Following

this, the outlet valve is slowly opened to purge the gaseous products in the micro-GC and

the carbonate-bearing solid products are collected for further analysis and characterization.

Additionally, enhancement in H2 yield resulting from CO2 capture and mineralization is

investigated as a function of reaction time and the alkaline sorbent type.

 Additional experiments are conducted to determine the exact moles of each gas in the reactor. The

experimental set-up is the same as described previously. In this approach, 8 bar of CO is injected into the

reactor with 12 bar of N2. N2 is used as a reference due to its inert nature and unchanged pressure over the

course of the reaction. This approach enables the calculation of the moles for the other gaseous components

                                            Page 128 of 210

based on the initial N2 content. The temperature is increased to a setpoint of 300 °C, and the reaction is

performed for 12 hours, while keeping other reaction conditions unchanged, to investigate the influence of

Mg- and Ca-bearing hydroxides and silicates on enhancing H2 conversions.

5.2.3. Product Analysis and Characterization

 After the reaction, the compositions of effluent gases are analyzed by Micro Gas Chromatography

(GC). The changes in the composition and structure of the Mg- and Ca-bearing hydroxides and silicates are

investigated using infrared (IR) spectroscopy collected in an Attenuated Total Reflection (ATR) mode using

an Attenuated Total Reflection-Fourier Transform Infrared spectrometer (ATR-FTIR, NicoletTM iS50,

Waltham, MA), and X-ray diffractometry (XRD, Bruker D8 Advance ECO powder diffractometer, Bruker),

respectively. Furthermore, the morphology and particle sizes are determined using the field emission

scanning electron microscope (FESEM, LEO 1550 FESEM, Bruker) and particle size analyzer (Anton

Parr).

5.3. Results & Discussion

5.3.1. Reaction Pathway involved in coupled WGSR with in-situ CO2 capture and

mineralization for enhanced H2 recovery

 The multiphase reactions involved in coupling the WGSR with in-situ CO2 capture

and mineralization for enhanced H2 conversion include: (i) low-temperature conversion of

CO and steam over Pt/Al2O3 catalyst to produce CO2 and H2; (ii) dissolution of Mg- and

Ca-bearing hydroxides and silicates to release Mg2+ and Ca2+ ions, respectively and

promote alkalinity; (iii) dissolution of the produced gaseous CO2 in the alkaline aqueous

phase; (iv) reactivity of the hydrated CO2 with Mg2+ and Ca2+ ions to mineralize into Mg

and Ca-carbonates; and (v) generation of high-purity H2 due to equilibrium shift due to CO2

capture and mineralization.

                                            Page 129 of 210

 The motivation for using Pt/Al2O3 catalyst stems from the need to direct the

formation of H2 as opposed to CH4.315 Prior analogous studies showed that Pt or Ni/Al2O3

catalysts are effective in reforming aqueous biomass oxygenates such as ethylene glycol

and glycerol to produce H2 and CO2 preferentially over CH4.316 Therefore, in this study,

Pt/Al2O3 catalysts are used to lower the activation energy barrier, accelerate reaction

kinetics, suppress the formation of CH4, and promote the formation of H2 and CO2.

Furthermore, the in-situ capture and conversion of gaseous CO2 in the presence of alkaline

Mg- and Ca-bearing hydroxides and silicates to produce the respective carbonates favors

the forward equilibrium shift towards more CO conversion and H2. Dissolution of Mg – or

Ca – bearing hydroxides and silicates releases Mg2+ and Ca2+ cations which capture and

solubilize CO2 to produce the respective solid carbonates. For example, in this work, the

pH of the alkaline slurry is observed to be in the range of 8.69-12.37 for the Mg- and Ca-

bearing hydroxides and silicates. This pH is ideal to capture and solubilize CO 2 from the

gas phase to produce the respective (bi)carbonate species.

 To probe the mechanisms underlying enhanced H2 conversion by coupling the

WGSR with in-situ CO2 capture and mineralization, the first set of experiments are

conducted by injecting CO gas. Steam required for the WGSR is provided by the slurry.

The ratio of CO to steam is calculated and quantified from the steam table (Table 5-2, 5-

3). In this approach, the effluent gas compositions are measured using micro gas

chromatography (GC), and the molar percentage of each phase is normalized by the total

amount of H2, CO, and CO2. This approach provides insights into the relative abundance

of the gas phase compositions. In this scenario, the relative gas phase composition of H 2

can be elevated if CO2 is absorbed by the liquid phase, even if CO conversion is unchanged.

5.3.3. Enhancement in WGSR using Mg(OH)2 and Mg2SiO4

 To enhance H2 conversion with in-situ CO2 capture, it is essential to use a suitable

catalyst that will convert CO and steam to produce H2 and CO2 and an alkaline source to

capture CO2. 318 Prior studies have reported enhancement in H2 conversion via sorbent –

enhanced WGSR using Ca-bearing sorbents.299 Enhancement in H2 yields were also

reported by coupling the WGSR with slurry phase CO2 capture and mineralization using

Mg(OH)2.319 However, Mg(OH)2 needs to be directly mined or extracted from earth –

abundant Mg-silicates. Eliminating this additional mining or extraction step and directly

using earth – abundant Mg-silicate minerals for enhancing H2 conversion while capturing

CO2 is less explored but highly transformative. To address this knowledge gap, experiments

are conducted to contrast the influence of Mg(OH)2 vs. Mg2SiO4 on enhancing H2 yield

with in-situ CO2 capture. The abundance of olivine (Mg,Fe)2SiO4 bearing forsterite

(Mg2SiO4) in the natural environment motivates the direct use of these materials for CO 2

capture.43 To investigate the enhancement in H2 yield with CO2 capture, the gaseous

products at the effluent are analyzed. Following this, the structures and morphologies of

the carbonate-bearing products are characterized separately.

                                      Page 134 of 210

 Initially, a base case study is performed by considering only the WGSR over

Pt/Al2O3 catalyst, to investigate its catalytic activity in the low-temperature regime. Figure

5-4 (a) represents the gas mole percentages when feeding only 20 bar CO at 250 °C for 9

hours. Figure 5-4 (b) shows the conversion of CO for the base case and in the presence of

Mg(OH)2 and Mg2SiO4. In the absence of the alkaline resource, CO conversion of 72.4%

is noted (Figure 5-4 (b)). Gas analyses did not indicate any CH4 formation demonstrating

that the Pt/Al2O3 catalyst is effective in converting CO and steam to CO2 and H2. Only trace

amounts of formate are detected in the aqueous phase (Table 5-4) indicating that the

solubility of CO to produce formate is not a rate limiting factor. The observed higher

selectivity towards H2 using low-temperature Pt/Al2O3 catalyst is consistent with previous

literature on WGSR.308 Compared with the base case without any alkaline source, WGSR

reactions in the presence of a slurry bearing Mg(OH)2 or Mg2SiO4 enhanced CO conversion

and H2 yield and lowered the concentration of gaseous CO2 due to in-situ CO2 capture. In

contrast to the base case without the alkaline source, CO conversions in the presence of

Mg(OH)2 and Mg2SiO4 at 250 °C are 81% and 79.7%, respectively, as seen in Figure 5-4

(b). The molar compositions of H2 and CO2 in the gas phase are 50.8% and 30.3% for H2

and CO2 in the base case. In contrast, H2 concentrations increase by 18% and 9.5%, and

CO2 concentrations decrease by 15.9% and 6.1% in the presence of Mg(OH)2 and Mg2SiO4

compared to the base case (Figure 5-4(a)).

 The relative composition of H2 in the gas phase is 8.6% higher when Mg(OH)2 is

used for CO2 capture as opposed to Mg2SiO4. CO2 concentrations are nearly 10% lower

when Mg(OH)2 is used as opposed to Mg2SiO4. Higher H2 concentrations and lower CO2

concentrations in the presence of Mg(OH)2 versus Mg2SiO4 are attributed to the faster

                                       Page 135 of 210

dissolution kinetics of Mg(OH)2 relative to Mg2SiO4.320 The enhanced release of Mg2+ ions

favors CO2 capture and the forward WGSR reaction which results in enhanced H2

conversions and CO consumption (Figures 5-4 (a) and 5-4 (b)).

5.3.4. Enhancement in H2 conversion with in-situ CO2 capture as a function of time using

 Mg(OH)2 and Mg2SiO4 materials

 One of the key uncertainties in enhancing H2 conversions with in-situ CO2 capture

is the influence of the reactivity of the alkaline resources in hydrothermal environments. In

this context, even though it is known that Mg(OH)2 is more soluble and has faster kinetics

of dissolution compared to Mg2SiO4, the kinetics associated with accelerating WSGR are

not well understood. Several factors could be competing in this integrated and enhanced

WGSR approach.

 First, the gas phase conversion of CO and steam to H2 and CO2 can be limiting.

Second, the solubility of CO2 and reactivity of Ca- and Mg-bearing silicates and hydroxide

to capture CO2 can be slow. Based on the changes in the compositions of the gas phase over

time, the factors limiting the conversion of CO and H2O to H2 with in-situ CO2 capture can

be determined. To this end, experiments are conducted with Mg(OH)2 and Mg2SiO4 at 250

°C, 20 atm as a function of reaction time. The normalized gas phase compositions for

reactions conducted at 3, 6, 9, and 12 hours are shown in Figures 5-5 (a) and (b) in the

presence of Mg(OH)2 and Mg2SiO4, respectively.

 In the first three hours of the reaction with Mg(OH)2 as the alkaline source, H2

composition is 53.4% while that of CO and CO2 are 18.7 and 27.9%, respectively. An

increase in H2 composition to 68.9% and a decrease in CO and CO2 compositions to 16.7%

                                      Page 136 of 210

and 14.4%, respectively, are noted in the 9-hour case. These results indicate that Mg(OH)2

is effective enhancing CO conversions and H2 yields with inherent CO2 capture. Therefore,

the WGSR is not the limiting factor in H2 conversion in the first nine hours of the reaction.

However, increasing the reaction time from 9 hours to 12 hours, lowered H 2 compositions

from 68.9% to 60.9%. CO and CO2 compositions increased from 16.7 to 23.4% and 14.4

to 15.8%, respectively, when the reaction time is increased from 9 hours to 12 hours. These

results suggest that CO2 capture is the likely limiting factor as the reaction time increases

from 9 to 12 hours. This observation can be attributed to the fact that at increased reaction

times, the availability of Mg2+ ions for CO2 capture is likely decreasing over time. Slower

dissolution rates of Mg(OH)2 over time due to mass transfer arising from the formation of

Mg-carbonates 321 or due to the saturation of the solution with Mg 2+ ions are attributed to

the decrease in H2 and increase CO2 compositions at 12 hours.

 In contrast, Mg2SiO4 for in-situ CO2 capture yielded significantly different results compared to the

use of Mg(OH)2 (Figure 5-5 (b)). H2 compositions are 16.9% and 19.2% respectively, at 3 hours and 6

hours of reaction time, while CO compositions are 71.6% and 66.5% in the presence of Mg 2SiO4. These

results indicate that the WGSR is the limiting step in the first 6 hours of reaction in the presence of Mg2SiO4.

This is likely due to the slower dissolution behavior of Mg2SiO4 to release Mg2+ ions compared to that of

Mg(OH)2. As a result, in-situ CO2 capture and subsequent enhancement in H2 yields is limited in the

presence of Mg2SiO4 in the first six hours of the reaction. At 9 hours of reaction time, H 2 composition is

60.3%. CO composition of 66.5% at 6 hours decrease to 15.5% at 9 hours of reaction time (Figure 5-5 (b)).

These results indicate that while the kinetics of WGSR are slower in the first 6 hours of the reaction in the

presence of Mg2SiO4 compared to in the presence of Mg(OH)2, comparable compositions are achieved

when the reaction time is 9 hours. As in the case of Mg2SiO4, a decrease in H2 compositions and increase

in CO2 compositions is observed when the reaction time is 12 hours. H2 compositions decrease from 60.3%

                                              Page 137 of 210

to 53.0% while CO2 compositions increase from 24.2% to 35.6% at 12 hours of reaction. The relative

decrease in H2 compositions and increase in CO2 compositions at 12 hours is attributed to the mass transfer

limitations associated with the dissolution of Mg-silicate and Mg-hydroxide. Therefore, these studies

indicate that WGSR can be the initial slow step in the presence of Mg2SiO4 as opposed to Mg(OH)2 during

the first 6 hours of reaction. Non – monotonic changes in the concentrations of H2, CO, and CO2 observed

as the reaction time increases from 9 hours to 12 hours are likely due to limitations from the slow dissolution

of Mg(OH)2 and Mg2SiO4 to release Mg2+ ions for CO2 capture.

5.3.5. Influence of Mg- and Ca-bearing hydroxides and silicates on enhanced H2

 conversion with in-situ CO2 capture

 Contrasting the influence of Mg- and Ca-bearing silicates and hydroxides on

enhanced H2 conversion coupled with in-situ CO2 capture can unlock new opportunities for

the utilization of a wide range of alkaline feedstocks. Ca-bearing hydroxides and silicates

are abundant in fly ash and slags.322 Mg-bearing hydroxides and silicates are abundant in

ultramafic and mafic mine tailings.323 To this end, experiments are conducted at 300 °C in

the presence of 8 bar of CO and 12 bar of N2 in the gas phase and Mg(OH)2, Ca(OH)2,

Mg2SiO4, or CaSiO3 to capture CO2 in a slurry phase over a reaction time of 12 hours.

Pt/Al2O3 catalyst is used as a low-temperature WGSR catalyst. N2 gas is used as a reference

due to its inert nature and constant quantity before and after the reaction which enables the

determination of the moles of gas products formed. The influence of Mg(OH) 2, Ca(OH)2,

Mg2SiO4, or CaSiO3 on compositions of H2, CO, and CO2 are shown in Figure 5-6.

 The influence of WGSR alone in the absence of Ca- and Mg-bearing hydroxide and

silicate determined at the same experimental conditions as the others showed that H 2, CO,

and CO2 account for 41.85%, 33.76%, and 24.39%, respectively (Figure 5-6 (a)). Relative

                                              Page 138 of 210

to this base case, the highest enhancement in H2 conversion and CO2 suppression was

observed in the presence of Ca(OH)2. In this best case scenario with Ca(OH)2, H2 yield is

72.67% which is 30.82% higher compared to the base case. CO2 yields are suppressed by

20.17% to 4.22% relative to the base case indicating that Ca(OH)2 in the slurry is highly

effective in capturing CO2 emissions. Significant enhancement in WGSR is also evident

from the changes in CO compositions which are lowered by 10.65% compared to the base

case. In comparison to Ca(OH)2, the H2 yields are ~ 50 – 51% in the presence of Mg(OH)2

and Mg2SiO4 which are lower. Interestingly, CO compositions are 5% lower when Mg 2SiO4

is used compared to Mg(OH)2, indicating WGSR is enhanced in the presence of Mg 2SiO4.

However, the higher solubility of Mg(OH)2 compared to Mg2SiO4 enhances CO2 capture

lowering CO2 compositions by 6.22% when Mg(OH)2 is used related to Mg2SiO4.320

 The reactivity of CaSiO3 in enhancing H2 yield and suppressing CO2 emissions is lower compared

to the other alkaline sources. Compared to the base case, the composition of H2 are 1.74% higher, CO are

similar, and CO2 is ~2% lower. While these results indicate that CaSiO3 enhances H2 conversion and enables

CO2 capture, substantial changes relative to the base case are not observed. The composition data in mmoles

indicate that the effectiveness of the alkaline sources in enhancing H2 yields while capturing CO2 emissions

Ca- and Mg-bearing hydroxides in enhancing H2 yield and suppressing CO2 emissions is higher compared

to that of the corresponding silicates. The higher solubility and faster dissolution rates of Ca- and Mg-

bearing hydroxides compared to the corresponding silicates facilities enhanced CO2 capture and H2

conversion.320

5.3.6. Mechanistic insights underlying enhancement in H2 conversion coupled with in-situ

 CO2 capture and mineralization
                                            Page 139 of 210

 The key reasons for the differences in the reactivity of Ca- and Mg-bearing silicates

vs. hydroxides are the slower kinetics of dissolution of silicates and the formation of a silica

passivation layer which is known to limit mass transfer. 252,324 Figure 5-7 represents the

multiphase chemical reaction mechanisms associated with carbon mineralization by

harnessing hydroxides versus silicates. While the CO2 hydration reactions to produce

bicarbonate and carbonate species remain unchanged, the leaching of the metal into the

solution results in the formation of a silica-rich layer in the case of silicates unlike in

hydroxides. It is interesting to note that despite the slower kinetics of Mg-silicate

dissolution and the formation of a silica layer that can limit mass transfer, comparable gas

phase compositions of H2, CO, and CO2 are noted with Mg(OH)2 and Mg2SiO4 (Figure 5-

6). However, the kinetics associated with enhancing H2 conversion with in-situ CO2 capture

and mineralization can differ significantly as noted in Figure 5-5. The slow kinetics of

WGSR observed up to 6 hours in the presence of Mg2SiO4 are attributed to limited

availability of Mg2+ ions to capture CO2 and facilitate the forward reaction. However,

beyond 6 hours of reaction time, the observed enhancement in H2 yields and corresponding

decrease in CO2 emissions is attributed to the availability of sufficient Mg 2+ ions to capture

CO2 and aid the forward WGSR to produce H2. Prior studies have shown that temperatures

above 100 °C and high partial pressures of CO2 aid carbon mineralization of olivine.43,325

The solubility of CO2, dissolution of Mg2SiO4 to release Mg2+ ions for capturing CO2 and

producing carbonates at the experimental conditions used in this study are analogous to

those reported in prior studies. Therefore, despite slow initial kinetics of Mg 2SiO4, the

release of Mg2+ ions over time results in comparable H2, CO, and CO2 compositions as

when Mg(OH)2 is used. It is also interesting to note that Mg 2SiO4 is more effective in

                                       Page 140 of 210

enhancing WGSR compared to CaSiO3. Prior studies reported that CaSiO3 is reactive at

temperatures as low as 110 °C for carbon mineralization unlike Mg2SiO4.326 Possible

reasons for the lower than expected reactivity of CaSiO3 are the formation of a silica

passivation layer that is thicker and limits mass transfer at the experimental conditions

reported in this study. To uncover if CO2 uptake by the alkaline slurry results in the

formation of solid carbonates, detailed structural and morphological analyses are

investigated and reported in the following section.

5.3.8. Insights on the mechanisms of the enhanced WGSR and in-situ CO2 capture and

 carbon mineralization

 Given the multiphase reaction described in Figure 5-1, the key reaction mechanisms involved in

the enhanced WGSR can be delineated as CO oxidation and CO2 capture. To be more specific, the CO

oxidation proceeds via two potential approaches simultaneously: (i) the gaseous-phase WGSR with the

assistance of noble-metal (Pt/Al2O3) catalyst (Reaction 5.1) and (ii) the aqueous-phase CO reaction to form

5.4. Conclusion

 In this study, the feasibility of the enhanced WGSR integrated with in-situ

thermodynamically downhill CO2 capture and mineralization is investigated in the presence

of Mg- and Ca-hydroxides and silicates to co-produce H2 and carbonate-bearing materials.

Calibrated tuning of the multiphase chemical interactions is unlocked by deriving the

                                            Page 148 of 210

thermodynamical models of the multiphase reactions, where the theoretical prediction of

the CO conversion mutually corroborated with the experimental results. For example,

30.82% more H2 yields with 20.17% less CO2 emission achieved with Ca(OH)2 compared

with independent WGS reaction without in-situ CO2 capture and mineralization. Thus,

these studies proved the hypothesis that the in-situ capture and mineralization of gaseous

CO2 depletes CO2 gas compositions and favors the forward reaction to produce H2. The

higher solubility and dissolution rates of Ca- and Mg-hydroxides favors enhanced H2

yields. Comparable compositions of H2 are achieved with Mg(OH)2 and Mg2SiO4 when

reacted for 12 hours at 300 °C despite initial slower kinetics of CO conversion with

Mg2SiO4 in the first 6 hours of the reaction.

 Among the studied alkaline resources for enhanced WGSR, Ca(OH) 2 exhibited unbeatable CO2

capture over the other sorbents (CO2 compositions: Ca(OH)2 (4.22%) < Mg(OH)2 (14.48%) < Mg2SiO4

(20.7%) < CaSiO3 (22.45%) < base case (24.39%) respectively), when feeding a mixture of CO and N2 at

300 °C and 20 bar. Additionally, the temporal evolution in H2 yields and CO2 capture revealed that the

kinetic limitation of the overall reaction was dependent on the sorbent dissolution behavior, especially for

the silicate sorbents. The amorphous Si-rich layer generated from silicate dissolution and mineralization

passivated the alkaline resources and restricted the rate of enhanced WGSR, particularly in the case of

CaSiO3. The dissolution of Mg- and Ca-bearing hydroxides and silicates, formation of the silica passivation

layer, and anhydrous metal carbonate products in the reacted alkaline resources are confirmed from

structural and morphological analyses. Detailed characterization and analyses confirmed that anhydrous

carbonates (MgCO3, CaCO3) are the dominant carbonate - bearing products without any associated hydrated

carbonate formations. Quantitative analyses of the reacted products showed that the carbonate content

corresponded directly to the trend in H2 yields. Evidence of comparable H2 yields with Mg2SiO4 and

Mg(OH)2 indicates the feasibility of directly using silicates for enhanced WGSR as opposed to extracting

                                            Page 149 of 210

Mg(OH)2 from Mg2SiO4 resources, thus enabling more material and energy efficient pathways for

producing H2 with inherent CO2 capture and storage in the form on durable Mg- and Ca-bearing carbonates.

This approach can also unlock new opportunities in harnessing renewable biomethane for co-producing H2

with inherent CO2 capture and mineralization to produce carbonates for use in construction materials by

harnessing energy from renewable sources, thus enabling carbon – negative pathways for a sustainable

energy and resource future.

 CHAPTER 6

 CONCLUSIONS AND OUTLOOK

6.1. Key Conclusions

 The studies in this report explore the feasibility and mechanisms of post and in-situ carbon

mineralization with H2 production via WGSR. The investigation starts from the synthesis of carbon

sorbents with favorable properties for carbon capture, in which the in-operando cross-scale X-ray

scattering measurements are employed to mimic the structural and morphological evolution.

Following the development of architected carbon sorbent, carbon mineralization at different

conditions is evaluated experimentally. The obtained solid products were characterized using

modern microscopic and spectroscopic techniques to gain insights on potential influencing factors

and their corresponding effects. The low-temperature configuration emphasizes the remarkable

enhancement of carbon capture solvent and provides a possible energy-efficient carbon capture

pathway, while the high-temperature configuration uncovers the silicate dissolution mechanisms

during carbon mineralization and addresses the challenge of post-H2 production carbon removal

proceeding at WGSR conditions. Further, the integration between WGSR and in-situ aqueous

carbon mineralization is illustrated via multiphase reactions, where the thermodynamic

enhancement of H2 yield is confirmed by experimental results, advancing carbon management and

renewable hydrogen fuel application.

 While the amorphous-crystalline transition during silicate material synthesis has been

extensively investigated, the fabricated pore structure in the amorphous precursor is hardly

retained after calcination. This collapse of the pore structure during thermal treatment highlights

the significance of pore maintainers in synthesis approaches, which effectively preserve the

                                         Page 167 of 210

ordered pore structure and remarkable surface area of the crystalline products for subsequent

applications like carbon mineralization. The developed synthesis approach providing a feasible

pathway to acquire architected carbon sorbents with the desired structural and morphological

arrangement is proposed in Chapter 2. Specifically, the innovatively applied pore maintainer

successfully prevents the collapse of the fabricated pore structure during amorphous-to-crystalline

transition. The end product is verified as crystalline Mg-silicate with a large surface area of 124.25

m2/g and a narrow pore size distribution of around 2.58 nm, which is considered an ideal candidate

for high-performance carbon mineralization due to the enhanced mass transfer and expanded

reaction surface.

 Though the direct gas-solid carbon mineralization is well illustrated by previous research,

the sluggish kinetics due to limited ion mobility and reactivity constrains its further development.

Hence, aqueous carbon mineralization is studied in both low-temperature and high-temperature

configurations to address the challenges in post-energy production carbon removal. Despite the

operating temperature and pressure of carbon mineralization, the extent of carbon mineralization

can be impacted by multiple factors such as solvent type, CO2 concentration, carbon sorbent

morphology, and stirring mode. Chapter 3 discussed accelerated brucite carbon mineralization at

moderate conditions with amino acid salt carbon capture solvent like sodium glycinate. At mild

temperatures and ambient pressure, the extent of carbon mineralization with Na-glycinate achieved

75.46% at 90 ºC, which is 63.78% higher than the case with water at the same temperature and

58.38% higher than the case in the same solvent at 25 ºC. The remarkable enhancement in carbon

mineralization is attributed to the accelerated combination between CO2 and glycinate ligands,

which is promoted at higher temperatures and consequently elevates the amount of dissolved CO2

in the aqueous phase. Furthermore, different pre-loading protocols are applied in a fluidized system

                                          Page 168 of 210

to illustrate its CO2-separating capability at room temperature. With a simulated flue gas

concentration of 20% CO2, 6-hour gas preloading resulted in a 64.8% carbon mineralization extent

at room temperature. The results uncovered that the pre-loading of flue gas facilitated CO2

diffusion in the aqueous phase and avoided the preferential combination between dissolved Mg2+

and glycinate, which significantly enhanced the carbon mineralization performance. After carbon

mineralization, hydromagnesite (Mg5[(CO3)4(OH)2]·4H2O) was the exclusive carbonate product

due to the favorable temperatures and pH values. The tunable morphology of the carbonate-bearing

products, from porous nest-like to compact lamellar shape, enhances their versatility and expands

their potential applications. The low-temperature carbon mineralization discussed in this study

provides a feasible energy-efficient approach for post H2 production carbon removal, where the

obtained carbonate-bearing products are tunable for multiple applications.

 To evaluate the feasibility of post H2 production carbon removal at the condition of

WGSR, silicate carbon mineralization was operated in hydrothermal environments at 200 °C and

CO2 partial pressure of 20 atm in DI water and 1 M NaHCO3 solution, respectively (Chapter 4).

As one of the starting materials, the structural-morphological evolutions of the amorphous-

crystalline (AC) Mg-silicate were determined by in-operando USAXS/SAXS/WAXS

measurements during the synthesis approach. The particle growth from nanocrystallites to

microscale secondary particles was observed, and the emergence of the crystalline phase in the

final product was confirmed. Subsequently, the conversion of amorphous-crystalline (AC) and

highly-crystalline (HC) Mg-silicate to MgCO3 after carbon mineralization were found to be

impacted by multiple factors, including Si coordination, aqueous phase type, and stirring mode.

The addition of NaHCO3 effectively balances the concentrations of Mg2+ and carbonate species to

facilitate the precipitation of carbonate products, which respectively elevates the carbon

                                        Page 169 of 210

mineralization extents by 18.5%, 16.5%, 13.3%, and 19.5% in AC Mg-silicate without stirring,

AC Mg-silicate with stirring, HC Mg-silicate without stirring, and HC Mg-silicate with stirring.

Additionally, the correlation between Si coordination and dissolution mechanisms was emphasized

in this study, which further determined the corresponding carbonate formation. The AC Mg-silicate

releases Mg2+ easier than HC Mg-silicate due to the larger surface-to-volume ratio and lower

crystallinity, yet the Mg2+ level in the solvent is constrained by the limited Mg/Si ratio. While the

HC Mg-silicate possesses a higher Mg/Si ratio but requires mechanical stirring to break large

particles and eliminate the Si-rich layer during dissolution. The study unlocks the mechanistic

insights into CO2 capture via Mg-silicate carbon mineralization at WGSR conditions and reveals

the crucial role of Si coordination.

 The integration between WGSR and in-situ carbon removal potentially promotes carbon-

free H2 production based on Le Chatelier’s principle. In Chapter 5, a low-temperature water-gas

shift reaction (WGSR) over commercial Pt/Al2O3 was integrated with in-situ CO2 removal via

carbon mineralization in the aqueous reaction medium. The thermodynamic models of the overall

reactions were performed based on the temperature-dependent equilibrium constants, which

confirmed the H2 yield enhancement with different carbon sorbents. Experimentally, the favored

H2 selectivity of the Pt-based catalyst in comparison to methane has been investigated. Four

different Mg- and Ca-bearing hydroxides and silicates are employed as carbon-free sorbents for

the enhanced WGSR, wherein the yields of high-purity H2 followed the following order: Ca(OH)2

> Mg(OH)2 > Mg2SiO4 > CaSiO3. Compared to independent WGSR without carbon sorbent, the

H2 yield elevation reached 30.82% with 20.17% less CO2 emission by employing Ca(OH)2

sorbent. Furthermore, the temporal investigation in H2 yields and CO2 emission indicates the

kinetic limitation resulting from the silicate dissolution, which can be attributed to the associated

                                          Page 170 of 210

Si-rich layer discussed in Chapter 4. The insights governed from this study verify that the

optimized WGSR for enhanced H2 yields and suppressed CO2 emission can be effectively realized

using integrated carbon mineralization with Pt-based catalyst and aqueous medium. These

advancements represent a crucial step toward utilizing renewable hydrogen as an energy carrier

with net-zero carbon emissions..

6.2. Outlook

 The studies performed in this report bridge the knowledge gap in optimized carbon

mineralization for decoupled and integrated CO2 removal with hydrogen energy recovery via

WGSR, and provide insights into the multiphase reaction mechanisms. However, more

contributing factors in carbon mineralization performance remain uncertain in advancing the

configuration. For instance, more amino acid salt solutions like sodium-alaninate (Na-Ala) or

potassium-prolinate (K-Pro) can be attractive alternative carbon-capture solvents employed in the

low-temperature carbon mineralization proposed in Chapter 3, potentially elevating CO2 capacity

due to their discrepant molecular structure. Furthermore, though the primary components in

industrial flue gas are regarded as N2 and CO2, additional minor components like nitrogen oxides

may negatively impact the CO2 separation in the fluidized reactor by competitively reacting with

the carbon-capture solvent. Hence, the roles of these trace-amount phases in the flue gas CO2

separation can be explored via a similar approach discussed in Chapter 3.

 Despite the well-illustrated hydrothermal WGSR with integrated carbon mineralization

evaluated in Chapter 5, electrochemical H2 recovery approaches with carbon removal also deserve

in-depth investigation due to their potential to operate at ambient temperature and pressure. These

energy-efficient and environmentally-benign pathways can be realized in electrolytic tanks with

                                         Page 171 of 210

 anion exchange membranes, in which CO is consumed in the anode cells to generate pure H2 from

 cathodes. Simultaneously, in-situ carbon mineralization with selected carbon sorbents can be

 initiated in the anode cell to eliminate the accumulation of carbonate species that may poison the

 electrodes. Further research efforts can focus on the manipulation of electrode properties such as

 pore structure or hydrophobicity to elevate the Faradaic efficiency and CO conversion. Following

 the exact integration mechanisms revealed in Chapter 5, the room-temperature electrochemical H2

 production with in-situ carbon mineralization can possibly advances harnessing H2 as an energy

 carrier with net-zero carbon emissions.
