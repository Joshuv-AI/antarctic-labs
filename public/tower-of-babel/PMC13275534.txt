Familial p.(Ala73Thr) Variant in GNB2 Associated With Mild Neurodevelopmental Features and Pilocytic Astrocytoma
Authors: Megan Glassford, Caroline Jennings, Anne Slavotinek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPathogenic variants in GNB2 have been associated with a neurodevelopmental disorder that includes global developmental delays and intellectual disability, hypotonia, increased risk for seizures, heart and renal anomalies, and characteristic facial features. Thus far, all reported patients with GNB2‐related disorder have had de novo missense variants. Many of these single nucleotide substitutions have been recurrent. Exome sequencing identified a heterozygous GNB2 variant, c.217G>A, predicting p.(Ala73Thr) (NM_005273.3) in a family, including two brothers with developmental delays and their more mildly affected mother. Their mother had a history of very mild developmental delays during childhood, macrocephaly, and subtle facial features consistent with GNB2‐related disorder. Consistent with what was observed in this family, this recurrent variant has previously been reported in association with a milder neurocognitive phenotype. However, the findings in this family raise the possibility of an expanded clinical spectrum to include a possible association with overgrowth/macrocephaly and pilocytic astrocytoma. This family also highlights the variable expressivity that can be seen in GNB2‐related disorder.Keywords: case report, GNB2, NEDHYDF, neurodevelopmental disorder with hypotonia and dysmorphic facies, pilocytic astrocytoma

Introduction

Guanine nucleotide‐binding protein, beta‐2 (GNB2) is a member of the guanosine triphosphate (GTP)‐binding protein (G‐protein) family of membrane proteins that transduce extracellular signals into intracellular responses [1, 2]. De novo, missense variants in GNB2 cause GNB2‐related disorder, also known as neurodevelopmental disorder with hypotonia and dysmorphic facies (NEDHYDF; OMIM #619503) [3, 4]. GNB2‐related disorder is associated with mild to severe developmental delays and intellectual disability, autism spectrum disorder, seizures, congenital heart disease, renal anomalies, and dry skin and erythema [4]. Craniofacial anomalies described in other patients include high anterior hairline with a tall forehead, disorganization and medial flaring of the eyebrows, full cheeks with prominent nasolabial folds, and downturned corners of the mouth [4]. A single, heterozygous missense variant in GNB2, p.(Arg52Leu), was also identified in 11 individuals from three generations with a cardiac arrhythmia condition comprising sinus bradycardia, sinoatrial disease, atrioventricular conduction disturbance, and atrial fibrillation [5].

Four recurrent missense variants associated with GNB2‐related disorder have been reported in apparently unrelated families—p.(Ala73Thr), p.(Gly77Arg), p.(Lys89Glu), and p.(Lys89Thr) [4]. The largest case series published to date described one of these recurrent variants, p.(Ala73Thr), in three patients that had milder developmental delays and intellectual disability compared to the moderate to severe neurocognitive delays described in patients with other variants. These patients also did not have cardiac or renal anomalies but did have abnormalities seen on electroencephalogram (EEG) [4]. The p.(Ala73Thr) variant was predicted to be located within one of the functional WD40 domains at the base of the beta‐propeller structure of the Gβ subunit of the GNB2 protein. Thus, this variant may affect interactions with the Gα subunit of the complex and other downstream effector proteins, such as PREX1 and GRK2 [4]. Further evidence for pathogenicity of this variant includes in silico predictions that have suggested variants in the WD40 domains of GNB2 are likely to be pathogenic [4]. However, the exact mechanism of pathogenesis is currently unclear.

We present a family in which two brothers with developmental delays were found to have a maternally inherited monoallelic GNB2 variant, p.(Ala73Thr). This family supports prior assessments of a mild neurocognitive phenotype reported with this recurrent variant. However, new findings described in this family include overgrowth/macrocephaly and pilocytic astrocytoma. Furthermore, this family highlights the variable expressivity that can occur with GNB2‐related disorder. To date, all patients with GNB2‐related disorder have had de novo variants, raising the question of whether this disorder is ever inherited. It is also unknown if there is any associated tumor risk. We report a family that provides evidence for both.

Case History

Proband

Developmental History

The proband was referred for a Genetics evaluation at 2 years of age due to global developmental delays and benign macrocrania. Developmental delays were noted from 6 months; he first sat independently at 10 months, crawled at 13 months, and walked independently at 16 months of age. Early speech development was normal, but he had speech regression between eight and 12 months of age. At 2 years of age, he began to repeat words. At 3 years of age, he was able to walk, although he had frequent falls and tired easily. He could mimic speech and repeat sentences but had little independent speech. He was receiving speech therapy and applied behavioral analysis (ABA). He had previously received physical therapy and occupational therapy. At 4 years of age, he continued to have fatigue and generalized muscle weakness. Parents used an adaptive stroller for long distances. He had around 100 words that he could combine into three to four word phrases, and still used gestures for communication at times. He was able to understand his name and one step commands but needed prompting with two step instructions. Academically, he was slightly behind. There were behavioral challenges with tantrums, but he did not exhibit self‐injurious behaviors. In addition to global developmental delays, he met criteria for a diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder and sensory integration disorder.

Past Medical History

The pregnancy was complicated by the detection of a chorioangioma of the placenta at 27 weeks of gestation. He was delivered by cesarean‐section at 37 weeks of gestation to a G3P1‐2 mother. Birth weight was 4110 g (> 97th centile), and length was 52.1 cm (90–97th centile). Other medical concerns include a diagnosis of hypotonic cerebral palsy, which was made based on clinical movement and tone abnormalities. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings were nonspecific. He had hypotonia and wore ankle foot orthoses (AFOs). He had recurrent, bilateral episodes of acute otitis media and had placement of tympanostomy tubes, but testing of his hearing with auditory brainstem response (ABR) was normal. He has had intermittent strabismus that has been monitored without plans for surgical intervention. He also had a history of significant constipation that was treated with medications.

Imaging

Investigations with a brain MRI scan at 3 years of age showed a slightly larger left lateral ventricle compared to the right lateral ventricle and a mildly prominent third ventricle. The fourth ventricle and extra‐axial fluid spaces were normal. There was incomplete left hippocampal inversion of uncertain significance. An echocardiogram and renal ultrasound scan were normal. An EEG was performed for suspected seizure activity with stiffening and staring spells at 1 year 6 months of age. EEG was normal and the seizure‐like activity later resolved.

Physical Exam

Physical examination at 3 years of age demonstrated increased growth parameters and macrocephaly, with a length of 104.3 cm (96th centile), weight of 21.5 kg (99th centile), and an occipitofrontal circumference (OFC) of 55 cm (> 97th centile). He had mild brachycephaly with a relatively prominent forehead and medial flaring of the eyebrows (Figure 1A). He had downslanting palpebral fissures that were almond‐shaped, epicanthic folds, a broad nasal tip, full lips with an everted lower lip and an open mouth, and a relatively small chin. There was fifth finger clinodactyly, a small, 1–2 cm hemangioma near his left breast, and a mildly increased lumbar lordosis. Tone was mildly reduced.

Proband's Brother

Developmental History

The proband's younger brother was evaluated at 14 months of age. Motor delays were first noticed at 5 months of age when he was unable to roll; gross motor skills continued to be delayed. He was able to roll over at around 7 months of age, sit independently at 10 months of age, and crawl at 12 months of age. At 14 months of age, he was able to babble but had not developed pointing and had no words. When last seen in the Genetics clinic at 28 months of age, he had started to walk at 23 months of age. However, he struggled with other gross motor skills, such as stairs and jumping. He had a pincer grasp and was able to feed himself with his hands and some utensils. He had 50–100 words and had some two‐word phrases. He was able to follow simple directions about 50% of the time. He has continued to receive services with physical therapy, occupational therapy, and speech therapy.

Past Medical History

The pregnancy was complicated by hypertension and a coronavirus 2019 (COVID‐19) infection in early pregnancy. He was delivered by spontaneous vaginal delivery at 38 weeks of gestation to a G4P2103 mother. Birthweight was 3380 g (50–90th centile) and birth length was 53.3 cm (90–97th centile). Head circumference at birth was 36 cm (90th centile). Other medical concerns resembled those of his older brother and included macrocephaly, hypotonia for which he wears AFOs, and chronic constipation. He had a diagnosis of cerebral palsy, which was based on tone abnormalities and brain MRI showing a periventricular white matter injury. He has had an intermittent esotropia with moderate hyperopia and astigmatism that has not required treatment. He has not had seizures.

Imaging

An MRI scan of the brain at 13 months of age showed a round lesion with central enhancement in the left cerebellum consistent with a pilocytic astrocytoma and confluent posterior periventricular and central white matter signal abnormalities with suspected white matter volume loss. There was thinning of the corpus callosum, and the scan was interpreted as showing gliosis from a prior insult. He underwent surgery at 18 months of age. At 2 years and 5 months, his brain MRI showed no recurrent or residual tumor.

An echocardiogram at 14 months revealed mild dilatation of the ascending aorta, but a repeat echocardiogram at 2 years of age was normal. A renal ultrasound scan at 14 months showed that the left kidney measured 5.5 cm, just below the typical size range (5.6–7.6 cm) for the patient's age. The right kidney was normal in size. Repeat renal ultrasound at 21 months showed normal kidney measurements bilaterally. An EEG and sedated ABR were normal.

Physical Exam

On examination at 31 months of age, length was 91.2 cm (42nd centile, Z score = −0.2), weight was 14.4 kg (78th centile; Z score = 0.77), and OFC was 51.5 cm (90th centile). He had mildly coarse facial features and a prominent and high forehead. The palpebral fissures were downslanting, and he had midface hypoplasia and a broad nasal tip (Figure 1B). The mouth had a tented upper lip, an everted lower lip, and micrognathia. Neurological examination showed mild hypotonia.

Proband's Mother

The mother of the boys walked at 18 months and talked at around 2 years of age. She needed additional help in school but graduated with a master's degree in library science. She was 180.34 cm tall (> 95th centile) and had an OFC of 61 cm (> 97th centile). She had widely spaced eyes and minor flaring of the medial eyebrows (Figure 1C). Tone was normal. She has had a normal echocardiogram and does not have symptoms of renal disease.

Other Family History

The proband's sister had mild speech delays that resolved. She also has ocular albinism, which is paternally inherited. The mother's sister has a daughter with Angelman syndrome. The family history was otherwise non‐contributory. Please see Supplementary Figure S1 for a full pedigree.

Methods (Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment)

The proband underwent initial genetic workup, starting with single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) chromosomal microarray. The chromosomal SNP microarray analysis was performed using the Infinium Assay with the Illumina CytoSNP‐850Kv1.2 BeadChip platform. This chip contains approximately 850,000 genome‐wide SNPs, with an overall average probe spacing of 1.8 kb and an average effective resolution of 18 kb. Microarray data is reviewed using the Illumina GenomeStudio V2011.1 software. The test is used to detect chromosomal imbalances throughout the human genome, including deletions, duplications, and aneuploidy. Copy number variations (CNVs) greater than or equal to 10 SNP markers are further reviewed for clinical significance. Copy neutral regions of homozygosity (ROH) larger than 5 Mb are also reviewed and interpreted. Pathogenicity of findings is based on the patient's clinical phenotype, our current knowledge of the human genome, including genic content of the region, presence or absence of similar CNVs in control populations, as well as review of the literature and disease databases (including DECIPHER, ClinGen, HGMD, and OMIM).

PTEN gene sequencing was then performed. Genomic DNA was isolated from the proband specimen. The entire coding exons plus flanking noncoding DNA sequences, and/or other selected regions/variants were analyzed by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and Sanger sequencing. All pathogenic variants, likely pathogenic variants, and variants of unknown clinical significance were reported. Large deletions and insertions, complex genetic rearrangements, and low‐level mosaicism cannot be identified using this method.

FMR1 repeat expansion analysis was conducted. Genomic DNA isolated from the proband was assayed for CGG‐repeat expansion of the FMR1 locus by analysis of DNA fragments generated by the PCR and separated by capillary electrophoresis. Normal and mutation categories of FMR1 allele were determined according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics guidelines. Methylation status of male premutation alleles and all full mutation repeat expansions was determined by allele‐specific methylation PCR followed by capillary electrophoresis analysis. This assay cannot detect the approximately 1% of mutations that delete part or all of the FMR1 gene, or changes of base pair(s) causing truncated or non‐functional FMR1 related protein (FMRP). Low level mosaicism and other chromosomal abnormalities are not detected by this assay.

Proband Only Exome sequencing was performed for the proband on genomic DNA using the Human Comprehensive Exome kit from Twist Bioscience to enrich the whole exome. The exome was sequenced using an Illumina sequencing system with paired‐end reads at a minimum coverage of 20× of 95% of the target regions. This proband's exome DNA sequences were aligned to the human reference genome (build UCSC hg19) with BWA‐mem. Variants identified by the GATK‐based bioinformatics pipeline were uploaded to the Fabric Genomics Analysis platform, which was used to annotate, analyze, and classify these identified variants. Of note, small deletions and insertions of < 25 bases are routinely detected by this technology; however, larger deletions and insertions may not be detected.

Based on exome sequencing results received for the proband, targeted variant testing was coordinated for other family members, including the proband's mother, father, sister, brother, and maternal grandparents. Genomic DNA was isolated from the patient specimens. The entire coding exons plus flanking noncoding DNA sequences, and/or other selected regions/variants were analyzed by PCR and Sanger sequencing.

Conclusions and Results

Genetic Testing

Initial genetic workup in the proband included a single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) microarray, FMR1 repeat expansion analysis, and PTEN gene sequencing, all of which were negative. Exome sequencing revealed a heterozygous variant in GNB2, Chr7(GRCh37):g.100274988G>A NM_005273.3:c.217G>A:p.(Ala73Thr). The GNB2 variant was interpreted as likely pathogenic by the testing laboratory. In silico predictions for this variant suggest a damaging interpretation. The maximum minor allele frequency (MAF) is 0.01% in the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD). Furthermore, it is a known recurrent variant in the medical literature as discussed.

Targeted variant testing for the familial GNB2 variant was performed for the proband's mother, father, brother, sister, and maternal grandparents. The variant was also present in the proband's mother and younger brother, but not in his older sister. Maternal grandparents also tested negative, indicating it occurred de novo in the proband's mother.

Further genetic workup was completed for the proband's brother in relation to his pilocytic astrocytoma. Tumor histopathology showed a low‐grade glial neoplasm/pilocytic astrocytoma, CNS WHO grade 1, that was negative for H3K27M (mutant) and IDH‐1 (p.Arg132His) and equivocal for the p.Val600Glu variant in BRAF by immunohistochemistry (IHC). ATRX, H3 K27me3 (trimethyl), and INI‐1 were retained by IHC. Fluorescence in situ hybridization detected a rearrangement or a tandem duplication of 3′BRAF at chromosome 7q34 in 55.0% of cells.

The proband was also found to have a heterozygous variant in HERC1, Chr15(GRCh37):g.63990928 T>A NM_003922.3:c.4904A>T:p.(Glu1635Val)As this gene has autosomal recessive inheritance, the variant was not thought to be contributing to his presentation. A heterozygous variant in MBD5 was also noted on the proband's ES, Chr2(GRCh37):g.149247537G>A NM_018328.4:c.3637G>A:p.(Glu1213Lys). This gene is associated with an intellectual developmental disorder with autosomal dominant inheritance. As pathogenic variants that result in haploinsufficiency for MBD5 cause severe developmental delays with lack of speech in many patients, seizures in more than 80% of children, sleep disturbances, autistic‐like behaviors, and self‐injury and aggression, we considered that the GNB2 variant was more likely to explain the proband's findings [6, 7, 8].

Discussion

We report a mother and her two sons who share a phenotype of mild, neurocognitive delays, increased growth, and macrocephaly in association with a heterozygous, missense variant, c.217G>A:p.(Ala73Thr), in GNB2. Both boys have also had hypotonia, constipation, and esotropia (Table 1). It is noteworthy that the youngest son was diagnosed with a pilocytic astrocytoma at 13 months of age and underwent a successful surgical resection at 18 months of age. To our knowledge, pilocytic astrocytoma has not been reported in other patients with GNB2‐related disorder thus far.

Pilocytic astrocytomas are the most common type of brain tumor in children [9]. Further testing of the tumor related to this GNB2 variant, possibly looking for a second hit in GNB2, was not completed in the proband's brother. Tumor testing showed a tandem duplication of 3′BRAF at 7q34. This rearrangement is most commonly associated with a BRAF‐KIAA1549 fusion product [10]. This protein product retains the BRAF kinase domain but is missing the regulatory domain. This leads to constitutive activation of the BRAF pathway and is a common finding in pilocytic astrocytomas. Recurrent somatic activating variants in GNB2 have been found to activate canonical G‐protein signaling, leading to constitutive activation of downstream pathways, including the MAPK/ERK, and PI3K pathway, and cytokine‐independent growth [11]. The activating variants have also been associated with reduced tumor sensitivity to treatments. This provides evidence that these somatic activating variants can be a cancer driver. It is currently unknown if the germline GNB2 variant found in our patient contributed to the development of the pilocytic astrocytoma. Further research, including functional studies, would be needed to further evaluate if GNB2 variants confer an increased risk to develop pilocytic astrocytomas.

In the largest case series to date of 12 people with GNB2‐related disorder, three people aged seven to 26 years were noted to have the same p.(Ala73Thr) variant as found in this family (Table 1) [4]. All three individuals with p.(Ala73Thr) had mild developmental delays or intellectual disabilities. One was reported to have low muscle tone and two of the three had distinctive facial features. One patient had an OFC between the 90 and 97th centile [4]. In support of a milder phenotype, the p.(Ala73Thr) variant is listed as a heterozygous variant in the global gnomAD dataset, resulting in an extremely low allele frequency of 0.00003188 [12]. It is also possible the individual with the p.(Ala73Thr) variant listed in gnomAD manifested only mild developmental delays or intellectual disability. Members of this family also did not have major cardiac or renal anomalies. The proband's brother had mild dilatation of the ascending aorta that resolved. He also had a mildly small left kidney, which also resolved. Both boys had normal EEGs. All three family members had macrocephaly, raising the question of whether macrocephaly could be part of the clinical spectrum for GNB2‐related disorder. Furthermore, the proband's mother is the oldest reported individual, at 35 years of age. She is even more mildly affected than her sons and likely would have gone undiagnosed if not for her sons having more significant concerns warranting genetic testing.

The proband also had a small truncal hemangioma that did not require treatment. This vascular finding is noteworthy, as another missense variant, p.(Lys78Glu), in GNB2 was associated with classical Sturge–Weber syndrome (SWS) [13, 14]. Similar to variants in GNAQ, another causative gene for SWS, p.(Lys78Glu) was associated with reduced proliferation in endothelial cells compared to corresponding wild‐type controls, but there was enhanced migration of endothelial cells relative to controls [13]. As both GNAQ and GNB2 encode G‐protein subunits that are involved in G‐protein coupled receptor (GPCR)‐dependent intracellular signaling, it is possible that both genes cause SWS through the same mechanism [13]. Interestingly, in view of the increased lumbar lordosis noted in the proband, one child with a GNB2‐related disorder was also described to have supine lordosis [3].

In summary, we describe a family in which three members have the recurrent variant, p.(Ala73Thr), in GNB2, conferring a diagnosis of GNB2‐related disorder. Although the mother exhibited very mild delays, macrocephaly, and subtle facial features, both of her sons were impacted with more significant developmental delays, increased growth with macrocephaly, hypotonia, constipation, esotropia, and facial anomalies. The youngest son also developed a pilocytic astrocytoma at 13 months of age. Our report emphasizes the variable expressivity that can be seen with this recurrent variant. This can pose challenges with providing appropriate and clear genetic counseling to families regarding possible health risks, medical management recommendations, long‐term prognosis, and recurrence risks for family planning purposes.
