BACKGROUND

Hikikomori is a condition that is characterized by remaining predominantly at home for at least 6 months, with marked social withdrawal or functional impairment. 1 Long‐term hikikomori is often associated with a decline in daily functioning, reduced self‐care, family dependence, and physical health issues, while access to necessary medical care may be limited. 2 , 3 When a distrust of medical services and the refusal of psychiatric care are pronounced, psychiatric interventions alone may be insufficient. In such cases, general medical care and home‐based outreach may serve as entry points to psychiatric, welfare, and community support. 4 We herein report a case in which a structured support process was provided in a stepwise manner from general medical care to psychiatric care and then to community‐based support.

CASE PRESENTATION

The patient was a man in his 50s with no confirmed family history of major psychiatric disorders. According to information provided by his family, he had shown difficulty with interpersonal interactions, a predisposition toward rigid interests, and sensory hypersensitivity since childhood. However, retrospective interviews did not identify any clear delay in language development. He had experienced school refusal during childhood. After graduating from high school, he lived alone and repeatedly engaged in unstable employment. In X‐15 year, he returned to his hometown after the termination of his employment contract and began living with his mother. Thereafter, he remained unemployed, spent almost the entire day in his room, and went out only once or twice a month. Most household tasks were managed by his mother.

In X‐2 year, he began to avoid bathing because of severe itching after bathing. After the onset of lower leg pain and edema, he became almost completely housebound. Despite repeated encouragement from his family to seek medical care, he refused to do so because of distrust based on previous medical experiences. In X‐1 year, his mother consulted a commissioned community welfare volunteer, who provides consultations for local residents and helps connect them with health and welfare services. Since outpatient visits were difficult, home‐based medical care was introduced. However, he initially refused any contact with healthcare professionals, and the intervention did not progress. His mother was subsequently hospitalized because of caregiver burden, and he began living alone. His hygiene and daily routine further deteriorated. He spent much of the day slumped over his desk, sleeping intermittently, and developed disuse‐related edema and refractory ulcers. In X year ‐ 2 months, he visited a dermatologist after strong encouragement from the home‐visiting physician and was diagnosed with psoriasis. Although he was instructed to keep the affected areas clean, he continued to refuse even changing into clean clothes. In X year, larvae were found in the wound, and he sought help from a visiting nurse. Although he received emergency treatment at home, extensive ulcers remained on both lower legs, and continued home management was considered to be difficult. Therefore, he was admitted to the psychiatric ward of a general hospital, where the provision of dermatological treatment and psychiatric interventions in parallel was possible. At admission, his clothes were torn and heavily soiled. His hair had grown to his waist, and he had a large amount of dandruff. Both lower legs showed extensive ulcers and edema, and because of pain, he was only able to walk a few steps with assistance. No clear psychotic or mood symptoms were observed. Although developmental characteristics, such as difficulty with interpersonal interactions and sensory hypersensitivity, were suggested, a retrospective assessment in adulthood has inherent limitations, and traits associated with autism spectrum disorder may overlap with those seen in hikikomori. Therefore, we did not assign a definitive diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder and instead described the patient as a case of long‐term hikikomori. 5 , 6

INTERVENTIONS AND OUTCOME

From a dermatological perspective, maintaining wound cleanliness was essential for continued treatment. However, the patient showed strong resistance to bathing because of sensory hypersensitivity and anxiety. The psychiatric team, including nurses, an occupational therapist, and a psychiatric social worker, collaborated to provide stepwise support for hygiene behaviors, discharge planning, and linkage to community services while medical treatment was continued. Hygiene‐related support began with brief showers limited to the areas around the wounds and was gradually expanded to whole‐body showering, hair washing, and haircutting. During these interventions, water temperature and pressure were adjusted, staff provided advance explanations and a clear outline of each step, and positive feedback was given after each achievement to reduce anxiety. As a result, his resistance to bathing and grooming gradually decreased, and he became more spontaneous in performing hygiene‐related behaviors. In discharge planning, the patient initially believed that returning home was his only option after discharge. Through repeated family meetings, the psychiatric team shared with both the patient and his family that there were limits to the support his elderly mother was able to provide, and alternative living arrangements after discharge, including supported housing, were discussed. The clarification of the limits of his mother's caregiving became a turning point, allowing the patient to consider options for greater independence more realistically.

In addition, occupational therapy helped maintain his activity level on the ward by making use of his interests and preferred activities, and this facilitated his transition to a supported employment program after discharge (Japan's continuous employment support services). By the time of discharge, he was largely independent in hygiene‐related behaviors, and the improvement in his skin condition was maintained. After discharge, he moved into supported housing and attended a supported employment program three times per week. During the 3‐month follow‐up period, no clear recurrence of hikikomori was observed. DISCUSSION

In this case report, “structured support process” is used descriptively to refer to a coordinated, stepwise sequence of multidisciplinary support linking the entry point to care, the transition phase, and the community‐based outcome. This term is not intended to indicate a formal clinical pathway or integrated care pathway prospectively designed for a defined patient group, but rather to retrospectively organize the sequence of support provided in this single case.

SUPPORT PROCESS

While the interventions for this case were not prospectively manualized as a formal care pathway, they may be understood retrospectively as a structured support process with defined goals and role allocation. 11 , 12 In this case, the entry point, transition phase, and outcome were clear.

General medical care, including home‐visiting care and a dermatological consultation, functioned as a realistic point of engagement with a patient who was difficult to reach through psychiatric services alone. 3 , 4 Psychiatric hospitalization served as the transition phase, during which physical treatment continued while hygiene‐related behaviors were restored and discharge planning was advanced. The subsequent transition to supported housing and supported employment represented an early community‐based outcome. Therefore, this case illustrates how multidisciplinary support may be organized as a coordinated and stepwise support process across different care settings.

BEHAVIORAL CHANGES AND SELF‐EFFICACY RECOVERY

In the present case, stepwise interventions and positive feedback were important for restoring hygiene‐related behaviors. In addition to interpersonal anxiety, bathing avoidance may have been related to sensory hypersensitivity, distress in unpredictable situations, and previous experiences of failure. Anxiety and avoidance generally reinforce each other, and repeated avoidance may make behavior initiation increasingly difficult. 13 Therefore, small, achievable goals were set, and repeated successful experiences were used to rebuild self‐efficacy. 14 The improvement in his skin condition, as a visible and concrete change, may also have reinforced this process. 15 In cases of long‐term hikikomori, accumulated feelings of failure, negative self‐evaluation, and helplessness may make behavior initiation difficult. 16 The present case shows that when a concrete issue, such as the need for physical treatment, is present, stepwise interventions combined with repeated successful experiences may support the expansion of behavior into going out, communal living, and employment. 14

FAMILY SUPPORT AND MULTIDISCIPLINARY COLLABORATIONS

In the present case, the mother's establishment of clear support boundaries and the patient's subsequent recognition of the need for independence may have represented a critical turning point. In cases of long‐term hikikomori, continued family support may sustain daily life, but may also contribute to prolonged withdrawal and the postponement of social participation. 2 Repeated family meetings are important because they allowed the burden on the family and the difficulty of continuing support to be shared explicitly. 17 , 18 In the present case, the multidisciplinary psychiatric team played a coordinating role in supporting the shift from dependence on family support to community‐based support, and the psychiatric social worker in particular played a central role in clarifying the needs of both the patient and his family and the coordinating services.

SIGNIFICANCE IN AN INTERNATIONAL CONTEXT

The significance of this case lies not only in its relevance as a case of hikikomori from Japan, but also in its potential value as a clinically informative example for difficult‐to‐engage cases of severe social isolation. Hikikomori is increasingly being recognized internationally as a phenomenon not limited to Japan, and social isolation and loneliness are also receiving growing attention as global public health concerns. 3 , 19 , 20 This case shows that a support structure using general medical care or home‐based support as an entry point to subsequent psychiatric and community‐based support may be informative for similar cases in other countries. In the face of declining self‐care and physical complications, non‐psychiatric medical care and support may play a crucial role in initiating interventions. 3 , 4

LIMITATIONS

The support provided in this case was not prospectively implemented as a formal care pathway, but may be understood retrospectively as a structured support process with clearly identifiable elements, including the entry point, transition phase, and outcome. As a result, this case report does not directly test the effectiveness of the care pathways themselves. Furthermore, this was a single case, no standardized outcome measures were used, the follow‐up period was short, and the results obtained were shaped by the institutional and cultural context of Japan; therefore, generalizability is limited.

CONCLUSION

This case shows that for complex cases of long‐term hikikomori that are difficult to engage through psychiatric services alone, a structured support process that sequentially links general medical, psychiatric, family, and community support may facilitate social reintegration. In such cases, it may be useful to organize the intervention process as a structured support process based on multidisciplinary collaborations, with attention to the entry point, transition phase, and outcome.
