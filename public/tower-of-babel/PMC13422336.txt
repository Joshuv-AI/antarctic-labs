Diagnostic accuracy of computer-aided detection for colorectal polyps of any size, ≤ 5 mm, and 6–9 mm: a meta-analysis
Authors: Wen Chen, Simeng Li, Zhenheng Wu, Haifen Tan, Fuqian Yu, Dongmei Wang, Xiaodan Lin, Zhigang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough computer-aided detection (CAD) is often used for medical diagnostic purposes, its diagnostic effectiveness in colorectal polyps (CPs) remains uncertain.ObjectiveTo summarize the diagnostic accuracy of CAD in diagnosing CPs, and provide a specific theoretical basis for clinical practice.MethodsFrom database creation to August 1st 2024, we conducted a comprehensive search of PubMed, Embase, Web of Science (WoS), Scopus, Wanfang, Cochrane Library, and the China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI). The pooled sensitivity, specificity, area under the curve (AUC), diagnostic odds ratio (DOR), positive likelihood ratio (PLR), negative likelihood ratio (NLR), and Fagan plot analysis were employed to evaluate the overall performance of CAD tests. Furthermore, we assessed publication bias using Deeks’ funnel plot asymmetry test.ResultsThe aggregated diagnostic data from 19 studies (any size), 12 studies (≤ 5 mm polyps) and 2 studies (6–9 mm polyps) were as follows: The sensitivity for polyps of any size, ≤ 5 mm, and 6–9 mm was 0.94 (95% CI, 0.92–0.96), 0.93 (95% CI, 0.89–0.95) and 0.95 (95% CI, 0.92–0.97), respectively. The specificity was 0.91 (95% CI, 0.86–0.95), 0.90 (95% CI, 0.87–0.93) and 0.90 (95% CI, 0.88–0.92), respectively. The AUC was 0.97 (95% CI, 0.96–0.99), 0.96 (95% CI, 0.94–0.98), and 0.92 (95% CI, 0.89–0.94), respectively. Deeks' funnel plot showed that publication bias was not statistically significant in the diagnosis of polyps of any size and ≤ 5 mm CPs (P > 0.05).ConclusionIn summary, CAD is accurate for all types of CPs. Specifically, for polyps of any size and those ≤ 5 mm, CAD was more sensitive and specific for CPs of any size.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-025-03898-9.Keywords: CAD, CPs, Accuracy, AUC, Diagnostic

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a serious threat to human health and is the third largest malignant tumor in the world [1, 2]. In the early stages, most colorectal cancers usually present as polyps, especially adenomatous polyps [3]. In fact, sporadic colorectal cancer mostly develops from adenomatous polyps [4]. Endoscopic polyp detection is highly dependent on the operator's experience, so the rate of missed diagnosis is high [5, 6]. It has been reported that colonoscopy misses up to 26% of adenomas and up to 9% of advanced adenomas [6]. Therefore, accurate diagnosis of early CPs is very necessary.

Computer-aided detection (CAD) has gained increasing interest in the field of medicine because of its capacity to process big data and pattern recognition [7]. In fact, artificial intelligence (AI) is an advanced implementation form of CAD. In recent years, the rapid advancement of AI has led to the increased application of CAD, in disease classification [8], diagnosis [9], and treatment [10]. For instance, a German study developed an AI system (deep neural network) for diagnosing neoplastic CPs among individuals, achieving a diagnostic accuracy of up to 78% [11]. Moreover, another study developed an AlexNet system, and the accuracy rate increased to 96.4% [12]. In another study, a deep machine learning model called SVM binary Classifiers was utilized in the detection of polyps with white light colonoscopy [13]. Although research on CAD involvement in CPs diagnosis is expanding, the accuracy of CAD varies significantly across studies, which is attributable to differing algorithmic approaches and diagnostic criteria [14, 15].

Resection of CPs can reduce the incidence rate, mortality rate and improve the quality of life of patients [16]. However, conventional non-invasive imaging modalities such as CT colonography have limitations in detecting small polyps and are not universally available in primary care settings. Thus, evaluating CAD's diagnostic accuracy through systematic reviews and meta-analyses is essential for assessing its effectiveness in diagnosing CPs.

Materials and methods

Systematic Review and Meta-analysis of Diagnostic Test Accuracy Studies (PRISMA-DTA) guidelines suggested reporting items were followed in the course of this study [17, 18]. See Supplemental Material 1 for a detailed list of PRISMA-DTA. We registered the protocol on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews database (PROSPERO, https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO, Number CRD42024603203).

Search strategy

Two independent researchers conducted a comprehensive literature search across various databases, including PubMed, Embase, WoS, Scopus, Wanfang, Cochrane Library, and the CNKI, from their inception until August 1, 2024. The search was limited to publications available in Chinese and English.

Search subject terms included "Machine learning model","Computer-aided detection","CAD","AI","Artificial intelligence","Deep learning","Model","ML","Colorectal","colorectal polyps","AUC","Area under the curve","Sensitivity","Specificity", and "True positive","FP","F1". The retrieval strategy was based on a combined use of subject words and free words, and was adjusted according to different databases (Detailed search strategies are shown in Supplementary Material 2). An extended search of relevant reviews and references of included articles was conducted. Search results were imported into NoteExpress 3.4 (Beijing Aegean Hailezhi Technology Co.) and duplicates were removed.

Inclusion criteria and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) Types of literature studies: published prospective trials and observational studies; (2) Study population in the literature: patients aged 18 years or older who have been screened for CPs or diagnosed with CPs, irrespective of gender and nationality; (3) Data extraction from the literature: Data relevant to the diagnosis of CPs, such as True Positive (TP), True Negative (TN), False Positive (FP), and False Negative (FN) values, may be extracted either directly or indirectly from the retrieved literature; (4) The search was limited to publications in Chinese and English.

Exclusion criteria: (1) The research methodologies and testing protocols employed do not align with established literature or non-original sources; (2) Documents pertaining to TP, TN, FP, and FN cannot be extracted; (3) The patient had a neoplastic disease other than CPs; (4) Studies conducted in vitro, animal experiments, and additional literature reviews; (5) Republished works in both Chinese and English from which the full text or necessary data were inaccessible.

Data extraction

Firstly, all literature related to this study were retrieved and imported into NoteExpress software for re-examination. After removing duplicate literature, they were screened strictly according to inclusion criteria and exclusion criteria. All processes were completed independently by two researchers cross-reading, and if there was disagreement on included studies, a third authoritative professional would make a decision.

Quality evaluation

QUADAS-2 quality scoring standard was used to evaluate the quality of included studies. Data extraction and quality evaluation of each included study were completed independently by two researchers. If there were any differences, the third researcher would evaluate, discuss and negotiate according to relevant standards.

Statistical analysis

Meta-analysis was performed using Review Manager (RevMan) 5.3 and Stata 16.0 statistical software. All variables in this study were binary variables. OR was used as the effect analysis statistic, and the combined OR value and its corresponding 95% CI were calculated to evaluate the ability of CAD to diagnose CPs. Sensitivity and specificity were used to evaluate the overall diagnostic accuracy. Summary receiver operating characteristic curve (SROC) and AUC were used to analyze the diagnostic efficiency. The χ2 test was used to detect the heterogeneity of the included studies, and the magnitude of heterogeneity among the studies was determined by the I2 value. In this study, a Q statistic test was used to analyze the heterogeneity among the included studies. If the heterogeneity test result was P > 0.05, it indicated that there was no heterogeneity among the included studies, and the fixed-effect model was used for analysis. When the heterogeneity test result was P ≤ 0.05, it indicated that there was heterogeneity between studies. At the same time, I2 values were used to judge the size of heterogeneity. 0% ≤ I2 < 25% was considered as no heterogeneity, 25% ≤ I2 < 50% was considered as mild heterogeneity, 50% ≤ I2 < 75% was moderate heterogeneity, 75% ≤ I2 < 100% was severe heterogeneity. If there was significant heterogeneity, the source of heterogeneity was identified by subgroup analysis and meta regression, and the combined effect size was meta-analyzed. In addition, bias was assessed by funnel plot analysis.

Result

Search results

After examining the titles and abstracts of all 1674 articles, 432 of them were eliminated for being duplicates, and 822 were eliminated because they did not meet the inclusion criteria. After a full-text review, 387 articles were also eliminated. Ultimately, 33 studies (19 studies (any size), 12 studies (≤ 5 mm polyps), and 2 studies (6–9 mm polyps) were included in the analysis after meeting the eligibility requirements. Basic characteristics

Table 1 summarized all included studies. Among the 33 studies, the total number of participants was 614 247 in the patient groups. Nineteen studies were of any size. The largest number of studies were from Asia (n = 10), with nine studies from other continents. Of these, five studies were from China, four from Japan and one from Bangladesh. All 15 studies reviewed were characterized by large sample sizes, with only four studies involving fewer than 300 cases. Polyps measuring ≤ 5 mm were included in 12 of these studies. The majority of the studies were conducted in Asia (n = 6), with three originating from Japan, one from China, and one from Korea. Similarly, all 12 studies involving polyps ≤ 5 mm had large sample sizes, with only four studies including fewer than 300 cases. Polyps measuring 6–9 mm were examined in two studies, one from Japan and one from Greece. Of these, one study had a large sample size, while the other involved fewer than 300 cases.

Quality assessment of included studies

Supplementary Table 1 presents the results of the QUADAS-2 quality assessment. The majority of the publications included in this meta-analysis met most of the QUADAS-2 criteria, suggesting that the overall quality of the included studies was moderate to high. While most studies were deemed to be of good quality, five studies [19–23] were initially considered to be of lower quality. However, following a comprehensive review of these studies and their evidence, they were ultimately included in the final analysis. Although these five studies received lower quality scores due to incomplete method descriptions (such as whether the researchers were blind), the inclusion of these studies was reasonable because the recruitment of participants followed a standardized sequential protocol to minimize selection bias.

CAD could accurately diagnose CPs

This meta-analysis includes a forest plot of the sensitivity and specificity of CAD in the detection of CPs (Fig. 2). According to the results of the diagnostic meta-analysis, the sensitivity of the 33 included studies varied from 0.77 to 1.00, while their specificity ranged from 0.63 to 0.99. These findings indicate that CAD is considerably more effective in accurately diagnosing a disease than in correctly identifying non-disease cases. Furthermore, an evaluation was performed on the pooled diagnostic accuracy of CAD in diagnosing CPs. In the pooled analysis, the P-value of the Spearman's correlation coefficient was found to be less than 0.05. The I2 values for sensitivity and specificity in CPs ranged from 98.99% to 99.85% respectively (Supplementary Figs. 1–3), and the chi-square test P-values were all below 0.05, suggesting a significant level of study heterogeneity. The median results derived from the aggregated diagnostic data for polyps of any size are as follows: the pooled positive likelihood ratio (PLR) was 6.5 (95% CI, 4.99–8.45), the pooled negative likelihood ratio (NLR) was 0.1 (95% CI, 0.01–1.91), the pooled sensitivity was 0.94 (95% CI, 0.92–0.96), the pooled specificity was 0.91 (95% CI, 0.86–0.95), and the pooled diagnostic odds ratio (DOR) was 64.29 (95% CI, 32.80–126.01). The corresponding SROC curve is illustrated in Fig. 3A. The AUC for the overall SROC was 0.97 (95% CI, 0.96–0.99).

The median outcomes of the pooled diagnostic data for ≤ 5 mm polyps were as follows: 6.2 (95% CI, 4.99–8.45) for the pooled PLR, 0.1 (95% CI, 0.01–1.91) for the pooled NLR, 0.93 (95% CI, 0.89–0.95) for the pooled sensitivity, 0.90 (95% CI, 0.87–0.93) for the pooled specificity, and 62.29 (95% CI, 32.80–126.01) for the pooled DOR (Supplementary Table 2). These results indicate a reasonably high level of accuracy for CPs diagnosis using CAD.

The median outcomes of the pooled diagnostic data for 6–9 mm polyps were as follows: 0.95 (95% CI, 0.92–0.97) for the pooled sensitivity, 0.90 (95% CI, 0.88–0.92) for the pooled specificity. The corresponding SROC curve is displayed in Fig. 3C. The overall SROC curve's AUC value was 0.92 (95% CI, 0.89–0.94). These results indicate a reasonably high level of accuracy for CPs diagnosis using CAD assays.

Clinical value analysis

The differences in clinical utility between CAD for diagnosis of CPs (any size and ≤ 5 mm) were evaluated using Fagan plot analysis. For any size CPs, the probability increased from 50 to 92% when the results were positive and decreased to 6% when the results were negative (Supplementary Table 2). For ≤ 5 mm CPs, the probability increased from 50 to 90% when the results were positive and decreased to 8% when the results were negative.

Subgroup analysis and meta-regression

The heterogeneity of the study was strong, and the threshold effect was excluded, indicating that the heterogeneity was caused by other reasons. To further analyze the source of heterogeneity, groups were stratified by region and sample size (> 300 and ≤ 300 cases). The results were as follows (any size, Supplementary Table 2): the sensitivity, specificity, PLR, and DOR were better in Asia than in other regions, but the NLR was lower. When the sample size was more than 300 cases, the diagnostic sensitivity, specificity, PLR, DOR were higher, but NLR was the same. When the literature was published after 2019, sensitivity, PLR, and NLR were higher, while other indicators were lower.

Results were as follows (≤ 5 mm): the PLR, and DOR were higher in other regions than in Asia, but the sensitivity, specificity, and NLR were lower. When the sample size was more than 300 cases, the diagnostic sensitivity, PLR, DOR were lower, but the specificity was the same. When the literature was published before 2020, sensitivity, PLR, and DOR were higher, while the NLR were lower. The results of the meta-regression analysis showed that sample size was the main factors affecting heterogeneity (P < 0.05, Supplementary Table 3).

Publication bias

To evaluate potential publication bias, we conducted Deeks' funnel plot asymmetry test within our meta-analysis (Fig. 4). The resulting p-values of 0.64 and 0.38 suggested a minimal likelihood of publication bias among the included studies, regardless of any size CPs or those measuring ≤ 5 mm.

Discussion

Scientists are already using CAD technology in the medical field, especially in diagnosing different diseases, such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) [24], and diabetic foot ulcers (DFU) [25]. Nevertheless, there is a scarcity of research investigating the precision of CAD in the diagnosis of CPs. This meta-analysis included 33 studies and used the diagnostic meta-analysis technique. The findings demonstrated that CAD exhibited superior accuracy in the diagnosis of CPs. These results are consistent with previous research. Among them, the highest sensitivity was observed for 6–9 mm CPs, while the highest specificity was observed for polyps of any size. It is worth noting that there are few studies related to 6–9 mm CPs, which may lead to differences in results, so the conclusions require further validation. Notably, our results show that the analysis of CAD is more specific for the diagnosis of CPs but slightly less sensitive.

More importantly, the likelihood ratio (LR) is an independent indicator of response authenticity [26]. The PLR of diagnostic tests can reflect the accuracy of diagnostic tests, and the NLR can reflect the degree of missed diagnosis of diagnostic tests [27]. In subgroup analysis of CPs, inconsistent results were found. Diagnostic results may be better when the Asian region and the sample size are greater than 300 cases. In addition, literature published after 2019 has higher sensitivity, PLR, NLR, and other indicators are lower. This is consistent with the results of other studies [28, 29]. We guess that after 2019, AI, deep learning and computer-Aided Technology etc., may have been greatly developed, which leads to the heterogeneity of results.

In fact, the DOR of a test can serve as a single summary measure since it obtains from combining different sensitivity and specificity [30, 31]. When the value of DOR is greater than 1, the larger the value is, the better the discriminant effect of the diagnostic test is. A recent study evaluated the accuracy of AI in diagnosing colorectal cancer screening [32]. The high DOR of 207 reflects the very high accuracy of CAD. However, there is still no consensus on how big DOR is. To further test the accuracy of machine learning diagnosis CPs, we analyzed the Fagan graph. A Fagan graph can calculate a posterior probability by setting a prior probability [33]. If the pre-test probability of CAD diagnosis of CPs is set at 50%, when the PLR is 11, the post-test probability is increased to 92% and 90% respectively. When the NLR is 0.06, the probability decreases to 6% and 8%. In the subgroup analysis, the above conclusion was further verified, and the basic diagnostic effect was good in Asia, and more than 300 samples, but the year caused heterogeneity in the results. Together, these results suggest that CAD could improve the diagnostic rate of patients with CPs, but the value of excluding diagnosis remains limited.

In addition to advancements in AI algorithms, the integration of clinical variables related to colorectal polyp formation has the potential to significantly enhance diagnostic accuracy. A recent retrospective cohort study conducted by Altiner et al. identified advanced age as an independent risk factor for colorectal polyps, whereas colonic diverticulosis did not demonstrate a significant association [34]. Integrating epidemiological data into CAD has the potential to enhance risk stratification and diagnostic accuracy, especially in populations with diverse age demographics. For instance, AI models that combine imaging analysis with clinical variables, such as age, may more effectively identify high-risk patients, thereby decreasing the probability of missed diagnoses in older populations. The development of specialized datasets, such as the ERCPMP dataset [35], which offers a comprehensive collection of endoscopic images and videos with annotations on the morphology and pathology of colorectal polyps, further supports CAD-driven polyp detection. Future advancements in CAD will benefit from the amalgamation of imaging analysis and clinical data inputs, thereby improving diagnostic specificity and clinical applicability.

The study presents several limitations. First and foremost, the primary limitation lies in the heterogeneity stemming from the size of the patient sample utilized in this investigation. Furthermore, in our included studies, many surveys were unable to provide the necessary experimental data, such as recall and F1. These reasons led to the inclusion of only two articles in the assessment of 6–9 mm polyps, and thus the Fagan and Deeks' funnel plot asymmetry test could not be conducted. It is noteworthy that 5 studies originated from China while 28 came from countries such as Turkey and the United States. Lastly, it should be emphasized that this research primarily concentrates on evaluating CAD diagnostic accuracy concerning CPs as part of a diagnostic meta-analysis. Consequently, we do not delve deeply into exploring differences among various CAD algorithms for diagnosis.

In conclusion, this study shows that CAD can accurately diagnose CPs. In view of the limited number of available tests, it is necessary to further examine the validity and potential applicability of CAD as a diagnostic indicator of CPs through multi-center, large sample and prospective studies.
