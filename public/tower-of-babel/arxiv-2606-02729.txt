Probabilistic Microcausality in a Thermal Bath of Gravitons
Giordano Cintia, Federico Piazza, Samuel Ramos

Abstract
We compute the (operator-valued) commutator of a massless scalar field ϕ \phi coupled to gravity. We work in perturbations around Minkowski space, in transverse-traceless gauge at the lowest order in G N G_{N} . The commutator is composed of different operators, including terms with Dirac delta derivatives supported on the lightcone. These are responsible for “bending” the Minkowski lightcone when evaluated on a classical/coherent state of gravitons, which allows to recover standard microcausality in the fixed-background limit. On more general gravitational states, metric fluctuations induce an uncertainty in the causal structure. We compute this effect on a thermal state of gravitons at temperature T T by evaluating the probability that [ ϕ ⁡ ( t , x → ) , ϕ ⁡ ( 0 ) ] ≠ 0 [\phi(t,\vec{x}),\phi(0)]\neq 0 . We find that the probability is Gaussian in x → 2 \vec{x}^{\,2} , centered on the lightcone and with time-growing variance Var ⁡ ( x → 2 ) = 16 ​ G N ​ T ​ t 3 3 . {\rm Var}(\vec{x}^{\,2})=\frac{16\,G_{N}Tt^{3}}{3}\,. This result is obtained by subtracting a universal vacuum contribution, which is log-divergent in the UV and subleading in the large-time limit. As a source of finite size can effectively serve as a regulator in this case, the lightcone spread in the vacuum appears to be source-dependent.

Introduction

Commutators of local observables are a direct probe of causality, because they determine whether measurements made at separate events can affect each other.
When fields are quantized on a classical background spacetime, such commutators vanish at spacelike separation—this is the principle of
microcausality

[
46
,
22
]
. In this limit, the analytic properties of the commutator, or equivalently of the Green functions, encode detailed information about the causal structure of the theory
[
17
,
15
,
16
,
39
,
40
,
41
]
. Things get more subtle when gravity is taken to be dynamical.
Strictly speaking, local field operators are no longer gauge invariant and therefore are not observables. Challenging the principle of microcausality in this context can be misleading—this is why one typically resorts to verifying subluminal propagation directly at spacetime asymptotics while ignoring what happens in the bulk (e.g.
[
33
,
11
,
19
,
4
,
10
]
and related references).

On the other hand—of course—one
can still
make ‘‘localized” measurements in the bulk of a fluctuating spacetime. It is just that some coordinate fixing prescription must be attached to the corresponding operators to specify, in a gauge invariant manner, the position at which the measurement is performed. This results in mildly non-local observables. For example, in asymptotically AdS, one can define a location in the bulk by sending in a geodesic from a point of the boundary. The resulting operators are dressed by a gravitational Wilson line.

On the topic of gravitational dressing, we refer the reader to the classic papers by Donnelly and Giddings
[
20
,

]
(see also
[

[

These dressed operators are physically meaningful, especially if they represent the observables available to a physical observer. Their commutators are a direct probe of the causal relations experienced by those observers. It is a fair interesting question to ask how such relations are affected in generic gravitational states.
One expects that the quantum fluctuations of the metric can make the lightcone structure uncertain and the causal relations probabilistic
[
38
,
37
,
54
]
.

It is the purpose of the present paper to quantify this phenomenon in the concrete setup of a thermal state of gravitons (see also the results in
[
32
]
, on which we comment in Sec.
7.1
below). To this end, we calculate in perturbation theory the commutator of a scalar field minimally coupled to gravity. We show that its support becomes operator-valued, leading to a nonzero probability that the commutator has support away from the classical lightcone. In a thermal state, and to lowest order in the gravitational coupling, this probability is Gaussian, centered on the classical lightcone, with a variance that grows in time.

We work in natural units
\hbar=c=1

(x^{0},\vec{x})

2
Choice of frame

Before diving into the computations, a remark is in order to better understand what is going on.
In the presence of dynamical gravity, different coordinate fixings,
i.e.
different dressing procedures, effectively correspond to different definitions of an event.
This phenomenon can be summarized with the slogan
relativity of the event

[
44
]
(see also
[
34
,
36
,
42
]
). A straightforward consequence is that, in some situations, the commutator of a massless field can have support on a sharp lightcone surface in some coordinates and exhibit uncertainty in a different set of coordinates. In the simplest possible terms, this can be shown with a mini-superspace example. The metric element can be written in conformal time
\tau
, or in proper time
t
, as

ds^{2}\ =\ a^{2}(\tau)(-d\tau^{2}+d{\vec{x}}^{\,2})\ =\ -dt^{2}+a^{2}(t)d{\vec{x}}^{\,2}\,.

(1)

When using
\tau
, light rays are obtained by integrating
d|\vec{x}|=d\tau
. No matter how crazily the scale factor
a
fluctuates, these coordinates always exhibit a sharp classical lightcone. In the
t
-coordinate, light rays are instead obtained by integrating
d|\vec{x}|=dt/a(t)
—fluctuations of
a
now do induce an uncertainty of the lightcone. Of course, on a classical spacetime, switching from
\tau
to
t
is mere relabeling. But when
a
is dynamical, switching to
t
changes the nature of causal relations from sharp to probabilistic.

While all coordinate choices are equivalent in principle, physical measurements correspond to a particular type of dressing, in which positions are tied to observers worldlines and time is identified with the proper time measured along them.
As free-falling observers in the universe, we maintain continuous causal contact with nearby galaxies/observers. When we detect, for instance, a gamma-ray burst, we record the event at a definite value of our own proper time—the only notion of time directly accessible from our geodesic. The source event itself, such as the collapse of the progenitor star, is attached to another distant geodesic and triggered by the local physics there,
i.e.
, by a definite proper time along that worldline.
For real observers, decoding causal relations in any other frame than their own is possible in principle, but very difficult in practice.
In the example above, accessing the
\tau
coordinate would require complete knowledge of the (quantum) state of the scale factor
a
.

For this reason, in this paper, we adopt coordinates that correspond to geodesic/proper time dressing.
2
2

2

Alternative interesting choices include
Rindler-like
coordinates. In this case, the observables are attached to worldlines of constant acceleration. We postpone this study to future work.
To this aim, we consider metrics in the
synchronous gauge

i.e.
with lapse
N=1
and shift
N_{i}=0
,

ds^{2}=-dt^{2}+g_{ij}dx^{i}dx^{j}\,.

(2)

Curves with
x^{i}
= const are indeed timelike geodesics in this metric and
t
is their proper time. We work around Minkowski spacetime, with

g_{ij}=\delta_{ij}+\kappa h_{ij}\,.

(3)

Let us now consider a light ray passing through the origin in the spatial direction defined by the unit vector
\hat{x}
. While finding its trajectory implies solving a geodesic equation, its longitudinal displacement (
i.e.
along
\hat{x}
) can be easily obtained from (
4
). To first order in
\kappa
,

x^{k}(t)=\left(t-\frac{\kappa}{2}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,h_{ij}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime}\hat{x}\,)\,\hat{x}^{i}\hat{x}^{j}\right)\hat{x}^{k}\,+\,\kappa\,\delta x^{k}_{T}(t).

(4)

The integral above is taken along the Minkowski light ray, which is the zeroth-order solution to the null geodesic equation, and represents the longitudinal displacement of the null geodesic, responsible for the deformation of the lightcone. The transverse displacement,
\delta x_{T}
, occurs along the lightcone itself and can therefore be ignored in the present analysis.

3
The operator-valued commutator

Equation (
4
) displays the expected deviation from Minkowski causality due to the presence of a classical gravitational field
h_{ij}(x)
. How do commutators of test fields “know” of such a deviation in a quantum field theory?
In a massless free theory the commutator is a
c
-valued function with support on the lightcone,

[\phi_{0}(x),\phi_{0}(0)]\ \equiv\ i\Delta(x)\ =\ \frac{\operatorname{sgn}(t)}{2\pi i}\delta(t^{2}-\vec{x}^{\,2})\,,

(5)

where
t\equiv x^{0}
has been defined for notational simplicity.
In order to see the effects of gravity, let us consider the same scalar field
\phi
coupled to Einstein gravity,

S=\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,\sqrt{-g}\,\left(\frac{1}{2\kappa^{2}}R-\frac{1}{2}g^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\phi\partial_{\nu}\phi\right).

(6)

Again, we expand the metric around Minkowski and work in
transverse-traceless gauge
or
TT-gauge
, which is a further gauge specification than (
2
), defined by
h_{ii}=\partial_{i}h_{ij}=0
3
3

3

One should first impose the TT gauge and
then
verify that the conditions
N=1
and
N^{j}=0
follow as solutions of the Hamiltonian and momentum constraints. This is indeed the case to order in
\kappa
, which is the level of approximation adopted in this work.
. As we want to compute the lowest-order correction to the free commutator, we just need to expand the Lagrangian to order
{\cal O}(\kappa)
,

\displaystyle{\cal L}=-\frac{1}{8}\partial_{\rho}h_{ij}\partial^{\rho}h_{ij}-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\mu}\phi\partial^{\mu}\phi+\frac{\kappa}{2}\,h_{ij}\,\partial_{i}\phi\partial_{j}\phi\,.

(7)

When interactions are considered, the
\phi
-commutator is
operator-valued
and can be computed in perturbation theory (e.g.
[
14
]
). One way of doing so is to express the evolution operator in interaction picture in terms of time-ordered products of free fields. This gives

\displaystyle[\phi(x),\phi(y)]=i\Delta(x-y)+i\!\int_{t_{*}}^{t_{x}}\!\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{4}z\,\,\big[\left[\mathcal{H}_{I}(z),\phi_{0}(x)\right],\phi_{0}(y)\big]-i\!\int_{t_{*}}^{t_{y}}\!\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{4}z\,\,\big[\left[\mathcal{H}_{I}(z),\phi_{0}(y)\right],\phi_{0}(x)\big],

(8)

where the interaction Hamiltonian density is read straightforwardly from (
7
),

\mathcal{H}_{I}=-\frac{\kappa}{2}h_{ij}\partial_{i}\phi_{0}\partial_{j}\phi_{0},

(9)

and
t_{*}
is the reference time at which the Heisenberg- and interaction picture-operators coincide. It is easy to see that the dependence on
t_{*}
cancels in the calculation of the commutator.
In fact, by repeatedly applying (
5
) one obtains

\displaystyle[\phi(x),\phi(y)]

\displaystyle=i\Delta(x-y)+i\kappa\int^{t_{x}}_{t_{y}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{4}z\,\,h^{ij}(z)\partial_{z^{i}}\Delta(z-x)\partial_{z^{j}}\Delta(z-y)

\displaystyle=i\Delta(x-y)-i\kappa\,\partial_{x^{i}}\partial_{x^{j}}\int^{t_{x}}_{t_{y}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{4}z\,\,h^{ij}(z)\Delta(z-x)\Delta(z-y)\,.

(10)

To ease the notation, in the main text we assume
t>0
from now on,
which is equivalent to studying the
retarded
propagator.
After some massaging of the above integral one obtains a structure of the
following type (see App.
A
for details)

\displaystyle 2\pi i\,[\phi(x),\phi(0)]

\displaystyle\ =\ \delta(t^{2}-\vec{x}^{\,2})

\displaystyle\ \,+\,\kappa\,\Big(t\,{\cal O}(x)\delta^{\prime}(t^{2}-\vec{x}^{\,2})+{\cal O}_{1}(x)\delta(t^{2}-\vec{x}^{\,2})+{\cal O}_{2}(x)\theta(t^{2}-\vec{x}^{\,2})\Big)\,.

(11)

Higher orders in
\kappa
contain various combinations of the same ingredients and higher delta-derivatives.

The operator
\mathcal{O}(x)
and its important role are discussed in the next section. The expressions of
{\cal O}_{1}(x)
and
{\cal O}_{2}(x)
are given in App.
A
.
The term proportional to the Dirac delta,
{\cal O}_{1}(x)
, provides a change in the wavefunction coefficient of the scalar field.
The last operator, proportional to the theta function, fills the inside of the lightcone. Once evaluated on some gravitational state, this represents the expected Hadamard tail that correlators generally exhibit on curved spacetime (see
[
12
]
for a recent discussion). This operator is not particularly interesting for causality as it represents a very mild correction to the propagation of the light field which still happens, for the most part, on the support of the delta function and its derivative. For this reason we will ignore this contribution from now on.

Clearly, the above equation is distributional and should be interpreted with some care. Strictly speaking, this would require first evaluating the expectation values of the operators on a given state, and only afterwards taking the corresponding weak limit. The subtlety is that products of singular distributions do not generally commute with the weak-limit procedure when their singular supports overlap. As a result, the manipulations leading to Eq. (
11
) are rigorously justified only for the regular, state-dependent part of the relevant correlation functions.
This is sufficient for the applications to thermal states, where the finite-temperature contribution to correlation functions is smooth and finite. More generally, for Hadamard states, the singular structure is universal and coincides locally with that of the Minkowski vacuum, so that it drops out when isolating the state-dependent contribution.
4
4

4

States whose two-point functions do not exhibit the correct short-distance structure lead to divergences in local observables — such as the stress-energy tensor — that cannot be removed by standard renormalization procedures. As a result, such states are generally regarded as unphysical. These issues are not restricted to QFT in curved spacetime, but arise more generally in quantum field theories on nontrivial backgrounds. This requirement also constrains the construction of coherent states in QFT, as discussed in
[
5
,
6
]
.

We analyze these two contributions separately. We first consider the state-dependent contribution, focusing on thermal states, and then turn to the vacuum contribution, emphasizing the subtleties specific to that case.

4
Dirac delta derivatives and linear response theory

The most interesting operator in (
11
) is the one proportional to the derivative of the delta. Roughly speaking, this term can be interpreted as the first-order correction
in a Taylor expansion which displaces the Minkowski lightcone to order
\kappa
,

\delta(t^{2}-\vec{x}^{\,2})+\kappa\,t\,{\cal O}(x)\delta^{\prime}(t^{2}-\vec{x}^{\,2})\ \simeq\ \delta(t^{2}-\vec{x}^{\,2}+\kappa\,t\,{\cal O}(x))\,.

(12)

One can guess the expression of
{\cal O}(x)
simply by comparing the above with the lightcone deformation induced by a classical field, eq. (
4
). Indeed, after some lengthy calculation shown in App.
A
, one finds

{\cal O}(x)=-\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,h_{ij}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime}\hat{x})\,\hat{x}_{i}\hat{x}_{j}\,,

(13)

so the support of the delta in (
12
) and eq. (
4
) coincide at the level of expectation values.

This mechanism, ubiquitous in derivatively coupled theories, has been studied at some length in
[
14
]
. There, we studied the commutator of a “
P(X)
theory” which displays a propagation speed different than the speed of light on certain Lorentz-breaking backgrounds. In a very similar manner, a curved spacetime can be considered as a classical/coherent state of gravitons governed by the effective theory (
7
) (see also
[
7
,
43
,
8
]
). As this theory is Lorentz invariant, the perturbative expansion of the commutator (
11
) has a Lorentz-invariant form. The delta derivative terms are responsible for reconciling the Minkowski — with the geometric curved-spacetime— perspectives, by acquiring non-vanishing expectation values on Lorentz-breaking field configurations.

In order to give a precise sense to (
12
) one should integrate them over a smooth tempered function.
But this is already implicit in any practical use of the commutator! Most notably, in linear response theory, the smooth tempered function hitting the commutator is just the classical source
J(z)
causing the perturbation of the field at some event
x
,

\phi(x)-\phi_{J=0}(x)=i\int d^{4}z\,\theta(x^{0}-z^{0})[\phi(x),\phi(z)]\,J(z)\,.

(14)

We assume
J(z)
to be a smooth function with support of size
R
centered at the origin of the coordinates and we want to evaluate the field at positive times with
x^{0}\gg R
, so that
\theta(x^{0}-z^{0})=1
.
We obtain

\displaystyle\phi(x)-\phi_{J=0}(x)=

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{2\pi}\int d^{4}z\,\left[\delta(x^{0}-z^{0}-r)-\frac{\kappa}{2}\mathcal{O}(x,z)\,\partial_{z_{0}}\delta(x^{0}-z^{0}-r)\right]\frac{J(z^{0},\vec{z}\,)}{2r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{4\pi}\int d^{3}z\,\frac{1}{r}\left[J(t_{ret},\vec{z}\,)+\frac{\kappa}{2}\mathcal{O}(x,z)\dot{J}(t_{ret},\vec{z}\,)+\ \dots\ \right]

(15)

where we have defined
r=|\vec{x}-\vec{z}|
, and
t_{ret}=x^{0}-r
. Also, we have omitted terms in which the integration by parts hits the operator
\mathcal{O}(x,z)
, as they only (slightly) modify the intensity of the signal, not its propagation. The expression inside the square brackets in the last integral is simply the current
J
evaluated at a shifted retarded time,

t_{ret}\to t_{ret}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\mathcal{O}(x,z)\,.

(16)

The Taylor expansion of the delta has turned into the (better defined) Taylor expansion of the smooth function
J
, which should be evaluated at a shifted value of the retarded time. In the large distance limit,
R\ll r
, one could go a step further and approximate

\displaystyle\phi(x)-\phi_{J=0}(x)\ \simeq\ \frac{1}{4\pi r}\ Q\left(x^{0}-r+\frac{\kappa}{2}\mathcal{O}(x,0)\right)\,,

(17)

where the charge
Q(t)
is the spatial integral of
J(t,\vec{x}\,)
. Of course the above makes sense if the typical displacement of the lightcone due to the metric field,
\sim\kappa\mathcal{O}
, overcomes the size of the source
R
. Since
\cal O
is an integral operator this is a cumulative effect, that depends on the strength of the gravitational field but also on the total elapsed time from the source.

5
Evaluating the lightcone uncertainty

Eq. (
12
) clarifies that classical/coherent states of gravitons behave as they should—they reproduce the causal structure of a curved spacetime at the level of expectation values. The support of the commutator
averaged
on any gravitational state is always going to be delta-like. Equivalently,
\langle\phi(x)\rangle
as given in (
17
) differs from
\langle\phi_{J=0}(x)\rangle
only at the specific time
x^{0}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\langle\mathcal{O}(x,0)\rangle
. However, there is more information in the above equations than the expectation values.

5.1
Thermal state of gravitons

Of particular interest for us is to evaluate the
spread
of the commutator, on gravitational states with a non-minimal uncertainty of the graviton field. We will thus consider a thermal state of gravitons
at temperature
T=1/\beta
,

\rho=Z^{-1}(\beta)e^{-\beta H_{0}}\,,

(18)

where
H_{0}
is the free Hamiltonian derived from the Lagrangian (
7
), which is the one that matters to this order in
\kappa
.

As for the average of the commutator on this state, it is identical to the free/vacuum one. This is because

\langle h_{ij}\rangle_{\beta}=0
, which makes all the linear operators in Eq. (
11
) vanish in expectation values. This is only true at first order in
\kappa
though. In a companion paper we study the expectation value of the commutator in a simplified scalar gravity setup
[
13
]
. We show that the thermal average of the commutator deviates its support from the Minkowski lightcone to order
\kappa^{2}
. One way of seeing this effect is to realize that average distances become
non-additive
in the presence of metric fluctuations, an effect studied in
[
47
]
in some detail.

The
spread
of the commutator, on the other hand, appears already in calculations at order
\kappa
. It is proportional to
\langle{\cal O}(x)^{2}\rangle_{\beta}
. However, we can also directly calculate the probability distribution
P({\cal O}(x))
. Both quantities can be obtained by evaluating the generating functional of the theory on a particular current.

5.2
Generating Functional

What makes the calculation of the spread relatively straightforward is that the relevant operator
{\cal O}(x)
is linear in
h_{ij}
. By defining the quantity

\mathcal{Z}(p)=\Tr\left[\rho\,e^{ip\,{\cal O}(x)}\right]\,,

(19)

one sees that

\langle{\cal O}(x)^{n}\rangle=\left.\left(-i\frac{\partial}{\partial p}\right)^{n}\mathcal{Z}(p)\,\right|_{p=0}\,.

(20)

Perhaps more elegantly, one can directly calculate the
probability that the operator
{\cal O}(x)
evaluates some real number
A
.
Probabilities in quantum mechanics are expectation values of projection operators. In this case, such an operator is simply the one-dimensional delta function
\delta({\cal O}(x)-A)
. One thus gets

P\big(A\big)\ =\ \Tr[\rho\,\delta\big({\cal O}(x)-A\big)\Big]\,.

(21)

By expressing the delta inside the trace as a Fourier integral, this probability can be obtained simply as the Fourier transform of
\mathcal{Z}(p)
,

P\big(A\big)\ =\ \frac{1}{2\pi}\int dp\,\mathcal{Z}(p)e^{-ip\,A}\,\,.

(22)

One might notice that
\mathcal{Z}(p)
is, by definition, the generating functional of the gravitons free theory calculated on a specific current. As we are dealing with the real-time evolution of operators in a thermal state we should use the Schwinger-Keldysh formalism, based on doubling the fields on the forward and backward time branches of the CPT contour (e.g.
[
18
]
, or
[
1
]
for a recent perspective). The generating functional
Z
in this case depends on two intependent currents,
J^{-}
and
J^{+}
. One can see that

\mathcal{Z}(p)=Z\,[J_{ij}^{-}=0,\ J_{ij}^{+}=-p\bar{J}_{ij}]\,.

(23)

In other words, the generating functional is evaluated on a configuration of sources where the current on the negative time branch vanishes, while
J^{+}_{ij}
has only spatial components along
\vec{x}
and has support on the light-ray connecting the origin with
x
,

\bar{J}_{ij}(z)=\delta^{3}(\vec{z}-z^{0}\hat{x})\theta(z^{0})\theta(t-z^{0})\hat{x}_{i}\hat{x}_{j}\,.

(24)

Notice that, for the thermal variance, the result is independent of how the source is distributed between the positive and negative branches of the Schwinger–Keldysh contour. Here, for compactness, we adopt the convention in which the source is placed entirely on the positive branch. By contrast, for the vacuum variance, the requirement of having a real variance uniquely fixes the form of the sources, as discussed in App.
C
, requiring half of the source to be placed on the positive branch and half on the negative one.

Putting all together and moving to Fourier space, to get results at order
\kappa
we can calculate
\mathcal{Z}[p]
in the graviton’s free theory. One obtains
5
5

5

In fact, in the Schwinger-Keldysh formalism, the thermal generating functional in the free theory has the schematic structure

Z[J_{+},J_{-}]\propto\exp\left\{-\frac{i}{2}\int d^{4}xd^{4}yJ^{a}(x)G_{ab}(x-y)J^{b}(y)\right\},

(25)

with
a,b=\pm
, and where
G_{ab}
is the kinetic matrix containing the four propagators. When
J^{-}
is set to zero, the generating functional only contains
G_{++}
, which for the graviton is given in (
28
).

\mathcal{Z}(p)={\cal N}\exp\left(-\frac{ip^{2}}{2}\int\frac{d^{4}k}{(2\pi)^{4}}\bar{J}_{ij}(-k)D_{ijkl}(k)\bar{J}_{kl}(k)\right)\,,

(26)

with

\bar{J}_{ij}(k)=\int d^{4}z\,\bar{J}_{ij}(z)\,e^{ikz}\,.

(27)

The actual form of the propagator in (
57
) depends on the state considered.
On a gravitons thermal state one should consider the finite temperature time-ordered Feynman propagator
[
18
]

iD^{\rm th}_{ijkl}(k)=\frac{4\pi\,\delta(k^{2})}{e^{\beta|k^{0}|}-1}\left(P_{ik}P_{jl}+P_{il}P_{jk}-P_{ij}P_{lk}\right)\,,

(28)

where the tensorial structure is the TT-projector and
P_{ij}=\delta_{ij}-\hat{k}_{i}\hat{k}_{j}
. Notice that in the above, we have subtracted the vacuum contribution, which adds to
\log Z
a divergent universal piece. More about this in a comment in Sec.
7
.
Although the integral in Eq. (
57
) cannot be evaluated in closed form, its large- and short-time behaviours can be extracted. The details of this computation are presented in Appendix
B
. For the cumulative effects that we are after we are clearly more interested in the large time behavior, which reads, up to the normalization factor of
\mathcal{Z}(p)
,

\log\mathcal{Z}(p)\ =\ -\,\frac{t\,T}{3\pi}\,p^{2}\ +\ {\cal O}\Big(\log(tT)\Big)\,.

(29)

It is easy to see—and shown below—that this quantity is directly proportional to
\langle{\cal O}(x)^{2}\rangle_{\beta}
.
We comment on the short-time behavior in the discussion section.

5.3
Probability

By solving the integral in (
22
) one sees that the probability is Gaussian,

P\big(A\big)\ \propto\ \exp\left(-\frac{3\pi A^{2}}{4\,tT}\right)\,.

(30)

This is not surprising, as we are using the gravitons’ free theory. This also means that the same amount of information is contained in
\langle{\cal O}(x)^{2}\rangle_{\beta}
, which can be obtained using (
20
),

\langle{\cal O}(x)^{2}\rangle_{\beta}=\frac{2\,tT}{3\pi}.

(31)

Finally, using Eq. (
12
), we set
A=\frac{\vec{x}^{\,2}-t^{2}}{\kappa t}
, which allows us to translate this result into the probability distribution for the commutator in a thermal state to be non-vanishing

P\Big([\phi(x),\phi(0)]\neq 0\Big)\ \propto\ \exp\left(-\frac{3(\vec{x}^{\,2}-t^{2})^{2}}{32\,G_{N}t^{3}T}\right)\,.

(32)

In other words,
\vec{x}^{\,2}
is a Gaussian variable centered at
t^{2}
with variance

\text{Var}(\vec{x}^{\,2})=\frac{16\,G_{N}Tt^{3}}{3}\,.

(33)

6
The vacuum contribution

In the previous analysis we employed the purely thermal propagator (
28
). Restoring the vacuum contribution leads to a divergent variance, reflecting the universal short-distance structure of Hadamard states. From the point of view of the generating functional, this divergence arises from ultraviolet modes evaluated on the current (
24
), whose support has co-dimension three, namely a null line segment. In this section we examine this vacuum contribution and possible regularizations of it. We will also attempt to discuss its physical meaning, while remaining essentially agnostic about it.

From an axiomatic field theory perspective, in order to even define the variance of the operator
{\cal O}(x)
, one should first smear such an operator against a suitable test function
f(x)
, and then consider

\langle\mathcal{O}^{2}(f)\rangle=\int d^{4}x_{1}d^{4}x_{2}f(x_{1})f(x_{2})\langle\mathcal{O}(x_{1})\mathcal{O}(x_{2})\rangle.

(34)

This should give a finite result, dependent on the size of the support of
f
.

Another, perhaps more physical, way to regulate the divergence, is to consider the effects of a source of finite size
R
when applying linear response theory. Then one can calculate the variance of the signal
induced by such an external source, as a measure of light-cone fluctuations. This variance reads

\langle(\delta\phi(x))^{2}\rangle-\langle\delta\phi(x)\rangle^{2}\sim\kappa^{2}\int d^{4}xd^{4}y\,x^{0}y^{0}\langle\mathcal{O}(x)\mathcal{O}(y)\rangle J(x)J(y)\delta^{\prime}(x^{2})\delta^{\prime}(y^{2})

(35)

with
\delta\phi=\phi_{J}-\phi_{J=0}
, and where we truncated the expansion to the first non trivial order in
\kappa
. We can recognize that (
34
) is similar to (
35
), with the role of the test function now played by the external source. The source is now the regulator of the vacuum divergence. However, the resulting variance is no longer an intrinsic property of the quantum light-cone, but also depends on the characteristics of the source.

A third possibility is to directly introduce in the calculation of
\log\mathcal{Z}(p)

a UV regulator
R
, playing the role of the characteristic length-scale of the source.
With this procedure, the vacuum variance at large times turns out to be

\langle\mathcal{O}^{2}(x)\rangle_{R}=\frac{4}{3\pi^{2}}\log\left(\frac{t}{R}\right)\penalty\ .

(36)

which leads to the following light-cone variance

\text{Var}(\vec{x}^{\,2})=\frac{32G_{N}}{3\pi}t^{2}\log\left(\frac{t}{R}\right)\penalty\ .

(37)

The details of the computation are given in App.
C
. A few comments are in order. The variance (
37
) grows
slower
than the termal one, so vacuum effects become subleading at large distance/time. Also, the result is not Lorentz invariant. While thermality very directly breaks Lorentz, in the vacuum case one could argue that Lorentz is broken by the gauge choice—more deeply, by the choice of comoving geodesic observers that define the frame (see Sec.
2
).

Finally, and perhaps more importantly, the result is regulator dependent—ultimately, as argued above, source-dependent. Taken at face value, (
37
) seems to imply that an extremely localized source can produce a larger spread of the lightcone. This is somewhat reminiscent of standard quantum mechanics, where the more localized a particle is initially, the more rapidly it spreads. However, we are not used to this phenomenon in field theory where, on the opposite, the lightcone structure is typically sharp, in the absence of gravity. Perhaps, the source-dependent result (
37
) is spurious and should be subtracted from the total computation, much like the vacuum contribution to the entropy in quantum field theory should be removed if one is interested in the thermal entropy of a region of space.

7
Discussion

A thermal state of gravitons is not precisely an everyday occurrence, but it provides a particularly simple and well-controlled setting (see however the discussion in Sec.
7.2
below), entirely within the domain of the effective field theory of gravity. It will be interesting to generalize our computations to states different than the thermal state, and to frames different than the free-falling one, such as a
Rindler
-like frame where
\vec{x}
= constant represent the worldlines of observers with constant acceleration.
We conclude with a list of comments on our results, ordered roughly from the more technical to the more speculative.

7.1
Comparison with the results of L. H. Ford

While finalizing our draft, we became aware of the important works by Laurence H. Ford and collaborators on fluctuations of causality on general gravitational states
[
32
,
31
,
51
,
52
,
53
]
. In particular, in
[
32
]
without explicitly calculating the commutator in perturbation theory, Ford correctly assumes that the retarded propagator is delta-like supported on the modified lightcone
\vec{x}^{\,2}=t^{2}+\kappa t{\cal O}(x)
(in his notation,
\kappa t{\cal O}(x)=\sigma_{1}
and, in his units,
\kappa=1/2
). In this reference
\langle{\cal O}(x)^{2}\rangle
is calculated on various gravitational states, among which a thermal state of gravitons is considered at around eq. (64). From that equation one deduces, instead of (
33
),

\qquad\text{Var}(\vec{x}^{\,2})\simeq G_{N}T^{2}t^{4}.\qquad\qquad\qquad\text{ (according to Ref.\penalty\ \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{Ford:1994cr}{}{}]}})

The origin of this result appears to lie in Ford’s approximation of the variance as

\langle\mathcal{O}^{2}(x)\rangle\sim\langle h_{ij}(x)h_{kl}(x)\rangle\hat{x}^{i}\hat{x}^{j}\hat{x}^{k}\hat{x}^{l}

(38)

(see eqs. (49) and (50) of
[
32
]
). However, this is effectively a large wavelength approximation, applicable in the very limited time range
t\lesssim T^{-1}
. Indeed, up to a numerical factor, this result agrees with the short-time behavior of the variance that we find in App.
B
. On the other hand, we find that the non-local nature of the operator
\mathcal{O}(x)
must be taken into account in order to get the correct large-time behavior (
33
).
Finally, Ford does not discuss the divergent vacuum contribution and simply subtracts it.

7.2
Self-consistency of the setup

One might correctly object that a thermal state of gravitons is incompatible with Minkowski spacetime once backreaction is taken into account. Indeed, a thermal energy density of
\sim T^{4}
produces a curvature radius of
H^{-1}\sim 1/(\kappa T^{2})
. Therefore, strictly speaking, our calculation should be trusted only up to times shorter than that. Roughly, this time scale is also the age of a Universe dominated by radiation at that temperature. In this Universe, however, the temperature evolves in time, so, again, our calculations can be trusted only for a limited time-span (see below, however, for possible implications for black hole physics). It is tempting to speculate, however, that the broader message of our result (
31
) is a secular linear growth in time of
\langle{\cal O}(x)^{2}\rangle_{\beta}
, with a coefficient proportional to the temperature, and that this could be essentially extrapolated to more realistic scenarios, e.g., when the temperature is smoothly changing in time. It will be interesting to verify this conjecture in a concrete setup.

7.3
The spacetime geometry of a bath of thermal gravitons

According to the “
particle physicist’s viewpoint
” on Einstein gravity, famously pioneered by Feynman
[
30
]
and later by Weinberg among others, general relativity is the theory of a massless spin-2 particle in Minkowski. The lightcone spread discussed in this paper is most directly interpreted inside this scheme, as due to the scattering of a massless particle off a bath of thermal gravitons. In many respects, however, the
relativists’
viewpoint can prove more efficient, by condensing all the effects of gravity into the geometry of spacetime. So what is the spacetime geometry of a thermal state of gravitons?

With vanishing expectation value for the graviton field,
\langle h_{ij}(x)\rangle_{\beta}=0
, the average metric tensor is everywhere flat.
6
6

6

Deviations of the actual expectation value of the commutator from the Minkowski lightcone happen only at order
\kappa^{2}

[
13
]
One could therefore argue that such a state is indistinguishable from Minkowski spacetime. This intuition, however, only holds at short distances. Our results show a growing uncertainty of causality at large separations, which the average metric alone cannot account for. In this sense, the classical spacetime description of general relativity gradually ceases to apply.
One might be tempted to associate any breakdown of classical spacetime with a sharp, localized phenomenon, like in the famously debated firewall scenario in black-hole evaporation
[
3
]
. Our results point to a different possibility. Roughly speaking, the classical spacetime picture appears to fail here in the same way that flat spacetime fails in general relativity itself: not abruptly, but at sufficiently large mutual separations
[
48
,
47
]
.

7.4
Hawking radiation and black hole information paradox

The uncertainty expressed in eq. (
33
) is very small in ordinary conditions. At room temperature, it would take about
\sim 10^{4}
years for the lightcone spread to reach the meter scale. Also, in a flat spacetime, the variance of the lightcone grows, but slower than the lightcone itself,

\frac{{\rm Var}(\vec{x}^{\,2})}{\langle\vec{x}^{\,2}\rangle^{2}}\ \sim\ \frac{1}{t}\,.

(39)

As expected, a thermal bath of gravitons does not dismantle the global causal structure of flat spacetime.

However, this effect could be of relevance when the lightcone stops growing,
i.e.
close to a black hole. Indeed, by considering
gravitons at the Hawking temperature
T\sim R_{s}^{-1}
, with
R_{s}
the black hole radius, one finds
that after a time

t\sim T^{-1}S^{1/3}

(40)

the lightcone uncertainty becomes of the same order as the size of the black hole itself. In the above,
S\sim 1/(G_{N}T^{2})
is the black hole entropy. This time scale is parametrically shorter than the evaporation/Page time
t_{\rm Page}\sim T^{-1}S
. Of course, the state of Hawking radiation is highly anisotropic and our calculations cannot be applied straightforwardly to that setup. Still, the cumulative effects discussed in this paper are clearly perturbative in
G_{N}
. This seems in contrast with the general expectation that deviations from semiclassical gravity should be of
{\cal O}(e^{-S})

i.e.
exponentially suppressed. The black hole information paradox is routinely formulated and refined by setting
gedanken
“Alice and Bob” experiments on a perfectly classical background spacetime, with a sharp horizon and exact causal structure (e.g.
[
50
,
49
,
3
]
). Our results suggest that this idealization may already fail parametrically before the Page time: the stage on which the paradox is formulated may itself become quantum.
7
7

7

The time scale after which the classical approximation breaks has been dubbed

quantum break-time
by Dvali and collaborators in a series of papers
[
27
,
28
,
24
]
and references therein. Interestingly, according to these authors, black hole dynamics is also dominated before the Page time by cumulative quantum effects which are perturbative in
G_{N}

[
25
,
26
,
29
,
23
]
.

Appendix A
Derivation of the operatorial commmutator

In this Appendix we present the derivation of the interacting commutator

\displaystyle 2\pi i\,[\phi(x),\phi(0)]

\displaystyle\ =\ \mathrm{sign}(t)\Big[\delta(t^{2}-\vec{x}^{\,2})

\displaystyle\ \,+\,\kappa\,\Big(t\,{\cal O}(x)\delta^{\prime}(t^{2}-\vec{x}^{\,2})+{\cal O}_{1}(x)\delta(t^{2}-\vec{x}^{\,2})+{\cal O}_{2}(x)\theta(t^{2}-\vec{x}^{\,2})\Big)\,\Big].

(41)

which generalizes Eq. (
11
) without assuming a definite sign for
t
.

The first-order correction to the free commutator is obtained from the Dyson series, and is provided in Eq. (
10
). It reads

\displaystyle[\phi(x),\phi(y)]

\displaystyle=i\Delta(x-y)-i\kappa\partial_{x^{i}}\partial_{x^{j}}\int^{t_{x}}_{t_{y}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{4}z\,\,h^{ij}(z)\Delta(z-x)\Delta(z-y)

\displaystyle=i\Delta(x-y)-i\kappa\partial_{x^{i}}\partial_{x^{j}}I^{ij}(x,y)\penalty\ .

(42)

In what follows, we set
y=0
, write
x=(t,\vec{x})
and denote
r\equiv|\vec{x}|
.

Let us start by assuming
t>0
, and check the case, and come back to
t<0
later.
Assuming that the field
h^{ij}
is regular on all spacelike hypersurfaces between
0
and
t
, we integrate the above expression over
z^{0}
.
We find

\displaystyle I^{ij}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}\vec{z}\int^{t}_{0}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z\,^{0}\,h^{ij}(z^{0},\vec{z})\delta((z-x)^{2})\delta(z^{2})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}\vec{z}\,\,\theta(t-|\vec{z}|)\theta(|\vec{z}|)\delta\left((|\vec{z}|-t)^{2}-(\vec{z}-\vec{x})^{2}\right)\frac{h^{ij}(|\vec{z}|,\vec{z})}{2|\vec{z}|}.

(43)

where we have an overall minus from the
\operatorname{sgn}(z^{0}-t)
function. The time-integral has been performed using the positive root of the
\delta(z^{2})
, which is the only one supported on the region
0<z^{0}<t
. We also write explicitly Heaviside functions to keep track of the integration domain.

We then introduce the angle
\vartheta
between the vector
\vec{x}
and
\vec{z}
. The dirac delta reads

\displaystyle\int^{1}_{-1}d\cos\vartheta\,\delta(2(r\cos\vartheta-t)

\displaystyle|\vec{z}|-(r^{2}-t^{2}))\theta(t-|\vec{z}|)\theta(|\vec{z}|)

(44)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2r|z|}\left\{\theta(|\vec{z}|-\frac{(t-r)}{2})-\theta(|\vec{z}|-\frac{(t+r)}{2})\right\}\theta(t-r)

(45)

To derive the above relation, it is convenient to express the integration domain in
\cos\vartheta
using Heaviside step functions
\theta(\cos\vartheta+1)-\theta(\cos\vartheta-1)
. One can then perform the integration over
\cos\vartheta
via the delta function, and subsequently rewrite the resulting expression in terms of Heaviside functions as above. In this procedure, we assume
t>r
since the alternative case yields a vanishing contribution when combined with
\theta(t-|\vec{z}|)\theta(|\vec{z}|)
.

We get

\displaystyle I^{ij}(x)=-\frac{1}{8\pi r}\theta(t-r)\int\frac{d\varphi}{2\pi}\int_{\frac{t-r}{2}}^{\frac{t+r}{2}}d\tau\,h^{ij}(\tau,\tau\,\hat{n}(\varphi,\tau)),\quad\text{with}\quad t>0.

(46)

Here, we made the relabelling
\tau=|\vec{z}|
.

The unit vector
\hat{n}(\tau,\varphi)
takes the standard spherical form, which in a Cartesian frame can be written as

\displaystyle\hat{n}(\sigma,\varphi)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\sin\vartheta(\tau)\cos\varphi\\
\sin\vartheta(\tau)\sin\varphi\\
\cos\vartheta(\tau)\end{array}\right),\quad\text{where}\quad\cos{\vartheta(\tau)}=\frac{r^{2}-t^{2}}{2r\tau}+\frac{t}{r}.

Here,
\vartheta(\tau)
is determined by the solution of the delta-function constraint.

In contrast, assuming
t<0
would end up replacing
-\theta(t-r)
with
\theta(-t-r)

t

\displaystyle I^{ij}(x)=-\frac{\text{sign}(t)}{8\pi r}\theta(t^{2}-r^{2})\int\frac{d\varphi}{2\pi}\int_{\frac{t-r}{2}}^{\frac{t+r}{2}}{d\tau}\,h^{ij}(\tau,\tau\,\hat{n}(\tau,\varphi))\penalty\ .

(50)

We finish by deriving
\partial_{x^{i}}\partial_{x^{j}}I^{ij}

\displaystyle\partial_{x^{i}}\partial_{x^{j}}I^{ij}

\displaystyle=\left(\hat{x}^{i}\hat{x}^{j}\partial_{r}^{2}+\frac{\delta_{ij}-\hat{x}^{i}\hat{x}^{j}}{r}\partial_{r}\right)I^{ij}.

(51)

To simplify the derivation, we introduce the field
H^{ij}(t,r)

\displaystyle H^{ij}(t,r)

\displaystyle=\int^{2\pi}_{0}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\varphi\,}{2\pi}\int_{\frac{t-r}{2}}^{\frac{t+r}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\,\,h^{ij}\left(\tau,\tau\hat{n}(\tau,\varphi)\right).

(52)

Then, the first derivative and second radial derivative of

\displaystyle\partial_{r}I^{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{\operatorname{sgn}t}{8\pi}\left[2\delta(t^{2}-r^{2})H^{ij}+\theta(t^{2}-r^{2})\left(\frac{1}{r^{2}}H^{ij}-\frac{1}{r}\partial_{r}H^{ij}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\partial_{r}^{2}I^{ij}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\operatorname{sgn}t}{8\pi}\bigg[4r\delta^{\prime}(t^{2}-r^{2})H^{ij}+\delta(t^{2}-r^{2})\left(\frac{2}{r}H^{ij}-4\partial_{r}H^{ij}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\theta(t^{2}-r^{2})\left(\frac{2}{r^{3}}H^{ij}-\frac{2}{r^{2}}\partial_{r}H^{ij}+\frac{1}{r}\partial_{r}^{2}H^{ij}\right)\bigg].

(54)

\displaystyle 2\pi i[\phi(x),\phi(0)]=\operatorname{sgn}(t)\delta(t^{2}-\vec{x}^{2})-

\mathcal{O}(x)=-\int_{0}^{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\,\,h^{ij}\left(\tau,\tau\,\hat{x}\right)\hat{x}_{i}\hat{x}_{j}.

Appendix B
Calculation of the generating functional

We want to calculate the integral in (

\mathcal{Z}(p)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{ip^{2}}{2}\int\frac{d^{4}k}{(2\pi)^{4}}\bar{J}_{ij}(-k)D_{ijkl}(k)\bar{J}_{kl}(k)\right)\,,

(57)

\bar{J}_{ij}(k)=\int d^{4}z\,\bar{J}_{ij}(z)\,e^{ikz}=\int_{0}^{t}dz^{0}\,e^{i(k^{0}-\vec{k}\cdot\hat{x})z^{0}}\,\hat{x}_{i}\hat{x}_{j}\,.

(58)

The actual form of the propagator in (
57
) depends on the state considered.
On a gravitons thermal state one should consider the propagator

iD^{\rm th}_{ijkl}(k)=\frac{4\pi\,\delta(k^{2})}{e^{\beta|k^{0}|}-1}\left(P_{ik}P_{jl}+P_{il}P_{jk}-P_{ij}P_{lk}\right)\,,

where the tensorial structure is the TT-projector and

. Notice that in the above, as discussed in Sec.

\displaystyle\log\mathcal{Z}(p)=-p^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\!\!\frac{dk^{0}}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!dkk^{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\!\!d\cos\vartheta\int_{0}^{t}\!\!dy^{0}\int_{0}^{t}\!\!dz^{0}\ \frac{\delta(k_{\mu}k^{\mu})e^{i(k^{0}-k\cos\vartheta)(y^{0}-z^{0})}}{e^{\beta|k^{0}|}-1}(1-\cos\vartheta^{2})^{2}\,,

where
\vartheta
is the angle between the integration variable
\vec{k}
and
\vec{x}
, and we have used the short notation
k\equiv|\vec{k}|
. We have also used

\left(P_{ik}P_{jl}+P_{il}P_{jk}-P_{ij}P_{lk}\right)\hat{x}_{i}\hat{x}_{j}\hat{x}_{k}\hat{x}_{l}=(1-\cos\vartheta^{2})^{2}\,.

(61)

We first perform the integration over
\cos\vartheta
, which gives

\int_{-1}^{1}\!\!d\cos\vartheta\,e^{-ik(y_{0}-z_{0})\cos\vartheta}\,(1-\cos\vartheta^{2})^{2}\\
=-\frac{16\left[3k(y_{0}-z_{0})\cos\bigl(k(y_{0}-z_{0})\bigr)+\bigl(k^{2}(y_{0}-z_{0})^{2}-3\bigr)\sin\bigl(k(y_{0}-z_{0})\bigr)\right]}{k^{5}(y_{0}-z_{0})^{5}}\,.

(62)

Then we use the fact that

\delta(k_{\mu}k^{\mu})=\frac{\delta(k^{0}-k)}{2k}+\frac{\delta(k^{0}+k)}{2k}

(63)

k^{0}

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\!\!dk^{0}f(k^{0},\vec{k})\,\delta(k_{\mu}k^{\mu})=\frac{1}{2k}\left(f(-k,\vec{k})+f(k,\vec{k})\right)\,.

(64)

y^{0}
and

\log\mathcal{Z}(p)=-\frac{p^{2}}{3\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\frac{dk}{e^{\beta k}-1}\ \frac{3\sin(2 k t)+4k^{3}t^{3}-6kt}{k^{4}t^{3}}\,.

(65)

By using the dimensionless integration variable

\log\mathcal{Z}(p)\ \approx\ -\,\frac{p^{2}}{3\pi^{2}}\frac{t}{\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!dy\ \frac{3\sin(2 y)+4y^{3}-6y}{y^{5}}\ =\ -\,\frac{p^{2}}{3\pi}{t\,T}.

(66)

In contrast, if

\log\mathcal{Z}(p)\ \approx\ -\,\frac{p^{2}}{3\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!dy\,\ \frac{3\sin(2 y)+4y^{3}-6y}{y^{5}}e^{-\frac{\beta}{t}y}\ =\ -\,\frac{4p^{2}}{15\pi^{2}}t^{2}T^{2}.

(67)

Appendix C
Vacuum variance with a length regulator

In this last Appendix, we provide the derivation of the vacuum variance (

This derivation can be obtained either by evaluating the expectation value
\langle\mathcal{O}^{2}(x)\rangle

In the first case, the expectation value of the

\langle\mathcal{O}^{2}(x)\rangle=\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{2}\langle h_{ij}(t_{1},t_{1}\hat{x})h_{kl}(t_{2},t_{2}\hat{x})\rangle\hat{x}^{i}\hat{x}^{j}\hat{x}^{k}\hat{x}^{l}

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{O}^{2}\rangle_{R}

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}[J^{-}_{ij},J^{+}_{ij}]=\mathcal{Z}[0,0]\exp\left\{-\frac{i}{2}\int d^{4}xd^{4}yJ^{a}(x)G_{ab}(x-y)J^{b}(y)\right\}.

To evaluate (

\mathcal{Z}(p)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}(p_{-},p_{+})\equiv\mathcal{Z}\,[J_{ij}^{-}=-\frac{p_{-}}{2}\bar{J}_{ij}^{-},\ J_{ij}^{+}=-\frac{p_{+}}{2}\bar{J}_{ij}^{+}]\,=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\rho\mathrm{e}^{-i\frac{p_{-}}{2}O_{-}(x)}\mathrm{e}^{i\frac{p_{+}}{2}O_{+}(x)}\right],

After deriving explicitly
\mathcal{Z}(p_{-},p_{+})
