Control-Plane Openness in Near-Term Quantum Computing: A Survey of Vendor Stacks and Field Implications The author maintains an open-source pulse-control software project that uses one of the vendor stacks surveyed here (IQM) as its primary integration target. No claim in this paper depends on or references that project, and the IQM row is graded only on public documentation.
Rylan N. Malarchick

Abstract
Public access to pulse-level and control-electronics interfaces in commercial quantum computing has bifurcated. The largest superconducting cloud platforms have closed access at this layer, with IBM removing pulse-level control from all production QPUs in February 2025; mid-tier superconducting vendors and the more open neutral-atom platforms have moved in the opposite direction. We survey thirteen commercial vendors across superconducting, trapped-ion, neutral-atom, and photonic modalities, grading each on six axes of openness at what we call the control plane: the layer between gate-level circuit specification and physical control electronics. The catalog ships as a separate machine-readable artifact under CC-BY-4.0. The bifurcation is documented row by row, with implications for reproducibility, hardware-aware research, and cross-vendor benchmarking. We do not propose an architecture or a reference implementation; we describe what the field has lost as the access landscape has shifted, and what minimally open access at this layer would have to look like.



I
Introduction

The control plane is the layer of the quantum computing stack between
gate-level circuit specification and the physical control electronics. It
includes pulse synthesis, calibration loading and drift compensation, pulse
and measurement scheduling, measurement readout, and feedback dispatch. The
control plane is what sits between “I want a CNOT” and “the FPGA emits
these specific microwave envelopes.” It is the layer at which most of what
makes a quantum experiment reproducible or hardware-aware actually lives.
The term is borrowed from networking and cloud infrastructure and is not
standard quantum-computing terminology; we use it to refer specifically to
this layer for the duration of the paper.

We grade the control plane on two surfaces, and the catalog covers both. The
first surface is what happens inside the control plane: pulse synthesis
algorithms, scheduling logic, feedback dispatch protocols, and the structure
of the calibration routine. The second surface is what the control plane
publishes outward to users: calibration data, provenance metadata,
reproducibility commitments, and the schemas through which any of these are
exposed. A vendor can be open on the inward surface and closed on the
outward, or open on the outward and closed on the inward. The catalog
distinguishes the surfaces in its column structure.

I-A

Three Harms

Three specific harms follow from the bifurcation. We define each narrowly so
that the catalog can demonstrate them concretely.

The first harm is
reproducibility
. A previously-published result
cannot be re-run on the platform it was performed on because the access
required is no longer available. The canonical example is the body of
pre-2025 IBM Quantum methods literature that used Qiskit Pulse: as of
February 3, 2025, IBM removed pulse-level control from all
QPUs
[
10
]
, which means a non-trivial fraction of that
literature cannot be re-executed on IBM hardware as of the May 1, 2026 cutoff
used here. This is not a hypothetical harm; it is dated.

The second harm is
hardware-aware research
. A class of experiments
that depends on access to a specific layer cannot be performed on a given
platform because the access is not exposed. Examples include closed-loop
measurement-conditioned feedback, which depends on real-time pulse dispatch
at the control electronics; novel pulse decompositions for specific gate
sets, which depend on raw waveform synthesis; dynamical decoupling sequences,
which depend on precise inter-pulse spacing; and calibration drift studies,
which depend on published calibration data over
time
[
41
,
40
]
. The catalog
documents, per platform, which of these are publicly possible.

The third harm is
cross-vendor benchmarking
. The same benchmark
protocol cannot be executed identically on two platforms because the
calibration metadata is published in incompatible formats or not published at
all, or because the underlying pulse-level interface differs in ways that
make “the same circuit” mean different things across hardware. Comparing a
closed-loop benchmark on Rigetti, which exposes Quil-T pulse-level access, to
one on IonQ, which exposes native gates only, is structurally impossible at
the pulse layer; the comparison can only happen at a layer of abstraction
high enough to lose the signal the benchmark is trying to
measure
[
38
,
11
,
17
,
21
]
.

II
Scope and Methodology

This paper covers gate-model commercial quantum computing platforms across
four hardware modalities: superconducting transmon, trapped ion, neutral
atom, and photonic. The catalog grades thirteen vendors at the control
plane as defined in Section
I
, using public documentation
accessed on or before May 1, 2026.

We do not cover quantum error correction codes considered as algorithmic
objects, quantum networking, post-quantum cryptography, classical simulators
of quantum systems, or hardware modalities not yet in commercial deployment
(topological qubits, NV-center systems, color-center spin qubits, and
others). Open Quantum Design is treated as related work in
Section
III
rather than as a catalog row, on the basis that as
of the cutoff date OQD is a foundation building reference designs rather
than a commercial vendor running jobs for outside users; the row could
appear in a future iteration of the catalog if that
changes
[
39
]
.

QCi (Quantum Computing Inc) is not included as a separate row because the
company’s Dirac systems are aimed primarily at optimization workloads rather
than gate-model quantum computing, which puts them outside the catalog’s
grading axes.

The catalog grades public documentation only. Where a vendor offers private
access to research partners under separate agreements, the catalog notes the
existence of the private access but does not grade on it; the paper is
about what is publicly citable, reproducible, and auditable, and the grading
reflects that.

III
Related Work

Four recent works most directly overlap with the argument and the survey
here. We engage each in turn.

Bova and Melko,
Nature Reviews Physics
2025.
A two-page
comment piece argues that an open-source initiative would benefit quantum
computing by reducing R&D costs, improving benchmarks, and growing the
talent pool, and that openness in quantum is more complementary to industry
than competitive with it
[
4
]
. The present paper accepts
the argument and provides the catalog work that supports it, narrowed to
the control plane specifically. Bova and Melko make the case at the level
of the field; we make it at the level of vendor-by-vendor evidence.

Open Quantum Design.
Roger Melko (the same Melko) co-founded Open
Quantum Design (OQD) in 2024 with Crystal Senko, Rajibul Islam, and Greg
Dick. OQD announced “the world’s first open-source, full-stack,
trapped-ion quantum computer” in January 2025, with partnerships including
Xanadu, the University of Waterloo, the Unitary Foundation, and
Haiqu
[
39
]
. OQD is the institutional version of the
open-source-quantum-control argument already running. The present paper
differs in scope and posture: where OQD is building reference open hardware
as a non-profit, this paper documents the access landscape that any such
effort would have to interoperate with, and grades the existing commercial
vendors against a public-documentation rubric.

openQSE Reference Architecture.
A recent survey of nine production
quantum-HPC software stacks proposes the openQSE reference architecture as
a vendor-neutral set of layer boundaries for runtime, resource management,
orchestration, and execution
[
23
]
. openQSE proposes an
architecture; we document the access landscape that any such architecture
would have to wrap, with finer-grained attention to the control plane
specifically and to per-vendor pulse-level access. The two works are
complementary.

Munich Quantum Software Stack and QDMI.
The Munich Quantum
Software Stack (MQSS) and its Quantum Device Management Interface (QDMI)
component, jointly developed by the Leibniz Supercomputing Centre, the
Chair for Design Automation, and the Chair of Computer Architecture and
Parallel Systems at TUM, describe a hardware-software interface released
under Apache 2.0 with LLVM Exceptions
[
19
,
20
]
. QDMI
is described in the literature as a de facto standard for hardware
abstraction. We cite QDMI as evidence that the floor of openness is being
built somewhere, and treat it as one shape of what minimally open access
could look like; we do not propose an alternative interface or recommend
QDMI specifically.

This paper sits between Bova and Melko’s high-level case for openness and
openQSE / QDMI’s specific architectural proposals. It documents the
per-vendor reality that any such case or proposal has to engage with, in a
structured form that other work can cite, fork, and update.

IV
The Control-Plane Catalog

The catalog grades thirteen vendors across four hardware modalities on six
columns. Each cell carries a level grade and a citation that justifies the
level. Cells that resist categorization use a “see notes” level with a
brief explanation in the per-row commentary.

IV-A

Per-Column Rubric

The rubric defines levels narrowly so that the same evidence produces the
same level across vendors. The columns are listed in the order they appear
in the catalog table.

Column 1: SDK pulse-level access.

Open
requires raw waveform
synthesis exposed to public-API users, including custom envelopes, frame
manipulation, and sample-level control.
Partial
requires
parameterized waveform templates only, without sample-level edit.

Native-gate-only
requires the native gate set exposed via API but no
waveform manipulation.
Closed
requires no public pulse or
native-gate API beyond standard gate-level circuits.

Column 2: Calibration data publication.

Open
requires
per-device calibration data published on a documented cadence in a documented
schema, with retroactive access.
Partial
requires per-job calibration
metrics fetchable but no historical archive, or summary statistics only.

Limited
requires calibration data available on request or to research
partners under separate agreements.
Closed
requires no calibration
data published or fetchable.

Column 3: Control electronics interface.

Open
requires the
SDK-to-hardware schema documented, third-party control electronics
substitutable, and FPGA gateware between measurement and control documented
or open-source.
Partial
requires one of the three documented but
not all.
Limited
applies when the vendor uses standard commercial
control electronics (Quantum Machines OPX, Zurich Instruments) without
publishing the integration layer.
Closed
requires no public
information on the control-electronics layer.

Column 4: Queue model.

Open
requires dedicated reservation
or on-prem deployment available, with real-time loops possible and
scheduling guarantees documented.
Partial
applies to mixed access,
where general users get a shared queue and a reservation tier exists for
real-time work.
Limited
applies to shared cloud queue only, with no
real-time guarantees and latency that varies by load.
Closed
applies
when no public information on queue or scheduling model is available.

Column 5: Reproducibility guarantees.

Open
requires
calibration versioned with stable identifiers, returned results carrying
provenance metadata, and hardware revision stability commitments published.

Partial
requires versioned calibration but no provenance metadata, or
provenance present but no stability commitments.
Limited
requires a
best-effort posture documented.
Closed
applies when no
reproducibility-related metadata is returned with jobs and no commitments
are published.

Column 6: SDK license.

Open
requires Apache 2.0, MIT,
BSD-3-Clause, or other OSI-approved permissive license, with full source
available.
Partial
requires a permissive license on the public layer
but proprietary or closed components below it.
Limited
requires
source available under a non-commercial or restricted-use license.

Closed
applies when the SDK is proprietary, has no public source, or
does not exist.

]
. Neither
exposes arbitrary pulse waveforms. The posture has been stable for several
years and is not visibly trending in either direction. This is neither
closing nor opening; it is a different equilibrium, and it suggests that
the trapped-ion modality has settled on a level of abstraction the vendors
consider commercially appropriate and that researchers have not pushed
back hard enough to change.

Neutral atom is bimodal.
Pasqal (row 7) ships Pulser as an
Apache-2.0 Python package designed at the pulse layer and open by design
from the start
[
28

. QuEra (row 8) ships Bloqade in Julia
and Python flavors for analog mode on Aquila, with SQUIN as the digital
DSL and pulse-level composability planned
[

. Atom Computing (row 9), in contrast, routes public
access entirely through Microsoft Azure Quantum and exposes no public
pulse-level SDK
[
3
]
. The split is institutional
rather than technical: the same physical modality (laser-trapped neutral
atoms) supports maximally open SDKs in two cases and a
closed-via-cloud-abstraction posture in the third.

Photonic is split three ways.
Xanadu (row 11) is open by design
at the SDK and cloud-access layer, with Strawberry Fields and PennyLane
both Apache 2.0 and Borealis available on AWS Braket since
2022
[
42
,
2
]
. PsiQuantum (row 12) is closed by
design at all layers, with the Omega chipset announced February 2025 and
Construct as a non-public algorithm-design
platform
[

,

]
. ORCA Computing
(row 13) sits in the middle, with PT-1 and PT-2 systems accessed through
institutional partnerships and a PyTorch-based SDK whose pulse-level
surface is not fully documented in public sources
[
25
]
. The
photonic modality alone produces three different openness postures, which
makes the row trio a useful internal control on any single-cause
explanation of openness.

V-A

The Refined Finding: Modality Alone Does Not Predict Openness

The cleanest reading of Table
I
is that
modality
alone does not predict openness.
Commercial position, scale of cloud
user base, and time of stack design predict more. Vendors that started
open tend to stay open: Pulser, Bloqade, and Strawberry Fields were
designed at the pulse or analog layer from the beginning and have remained
that way. Vendors that grow large cloud user bases tend to close: IBM is
the cleanest example. Stealth fault-tolerant programs start closed:
PsiQuantum has been closed at every layer since founding. The same
physical substrate can support either posture, and the catalog’s rows make
that visible per modality. Superconducting includes IBM closed and IQM
open; neutral atom includes Atom closed and Pasqal open; photonic
includes PsiQuantum closed and Xanadu open. The commercial and
historical determinants of openness are doing the work that modality
cannot.

One additional observation, narrower in scope, deserves naming. The cloud
platforms themselves are not uniform vehicles for closure: AWS Braket
Pulse exposes pulse-level access for Rigetti and OQC and could in
principle expose it for any vendor that chose to offer
it
[
1
]
. The OQC row demonstrates that a cloud-platform
integration can be the openness vehicle rather than the closure mechanism,
which complicates the simpler reading that “cloud equals closed.” The
mechanism that closes pulse access is the vendor’s own product decision,
not the cloud platform; AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud each route to
whatever access tier the vendor has chosen to publish.

VI
Counterarguments

The catalog grades vendors by what they publish, but the bifurcation it
documents has reasons. This section takes the strongest defenses of
closed access seriously, eight of them, and offers a response to each.
The pulse-difficulty argument (
VI-A
) and the
consolidating-not-closing argument (
VI-F
) get the most depth
because their responses carry the others.

VI-A

Pulse access is genuinely hard to expose safely

Calibration drift makes raw pulse schedules brittle on a per-shot basis.
Hardware-protection guards are needed to prevent users from damaging
cryogenic systems with badly shaped envelopes. The API surface of
pulse-level control creates a real support burden because every user is
doing something different. Documenting the control electronics correctly
is engineering work that gets done by hand, slowly. None of this is
excuse-making; the engineering reality is that exposing pulse-level access
to general cloud users is a hard product decision with real downside risk.
IBM’s stated rationale for the February 2025 deprecation makes this
defense in their own words: the most common use of pulse-level control
was building custom pulse schedules to modify ECR or RX pulses for
single- and two-qubit rotations, and fractional gates offer similar
capability through a higher-level interface better suited for quantum
advantage applications
[
10
]
.

The engineering difficulty of safely exposing user-facing pulse access
does not justify closing the closed-loop feedback layer or the
control-electronics interface. Those are separable concerns. A vendor
can choose not to expose arbitrary pulse waveforms to general users and
still publish the schema by which their internal feedback controller
talks to its electronics, the algorithms that run in the FPGA gateware
between measurement and control, the protocol for swapping in third-party
control electronics, and the calibration data as a published time
series
[
43
,
22
]
. The IBM rationale (fractional
gates cover the most common use case) is fair as a product decision, but
it does not address the research community whose work depends on the
less common cases: pulse-level dynamical decoupling sequences,
non-standard gate decompositions, novel feedback protocols, and the
methods papers that the gate-level community ends up depending on a few
years later. Pulse-access difficulty argues for tiered access models
with safety-aware abstractions; it does not argue for closed sourcing of
the control plane below the user-facing API. The conflation of the two
is what this paper pushes against most directly.

VI-B

Calibration IP is real commercial value

Vendors invest substantial physics and engineering effort into
calibration routines, drift compensation, gate-set decomposition, and
pulse shaping. That work is differentiating. Open-sourcing it would
erode their advantage and reduce the incentive to fund the work in the
first place.

Calibration IP and calibration data are different objects. Routines are
IP; the data those routines produce on a given device on a given day is
not the same thing. Publishing the data does not publish the routines.
Our floor for openness can ask for the data without asking for the
routines, which is the same posture taken by most semiconductor foundries
with published process design kits while keeping their internal
characterization stack proprietary
[
5
]
.

VI-C

The field is too early to standardize

Premature standardization locks in bad design choices. Quantum hardware
is heterogeneous enough that a control-plane interface defined now would
be wrong in five years. Better to let the design space mature.

There is a difference between freezing an interface and documenting one.
The catalog shows that several vendors already have mature, documented
pulse-level interfaces (Pulser, Bloqade, Quil-T, Pulla); we do not propose
freezing those into a single specification. The interfaces are legible, citable, and stable enough that experiments running through
them can be reproduced. The Munich QDMI effort and the QIR Alliance both
demonstrate that interface work is happening
anyway
[
19
,
29
]
; the question is whether that work
happens openly with broad input or behind cloud SLAs.

VI-D

Most researchers don’t need pulse access

Cloud usage statistics across IBM Quantum, Azure Quantum, AWS Braket, and
similar platforms suggest the overwhelming majority of jobs are
gate-level. Closing pulse access affects a small minority of users. The
cost-benefit calculation is rational.

The population of users matters less than the population of papers. The
pulse-level minority disproportionately produces the methods papers,
error-correction experiments, calibration studies, and benchmarking
results that the gate-level majority depends on. A field where the
foundational papers cannot be reproduced because the pulse-level access
they used is no longer available is not a field operating on a sound
base. The fraction of cloud jobs is the wrong denominator.

VI-E

Hardware specificity makes openness impractical

Every superconducting fab is different; every ion-trap geometry is
different; every neutral-atom array has its own cooling and trapping
geometry. Pulses that work on one device do not work on another. Open
access at the pulse level is not portable, so the meaning of openness
here is unclear.

Openness at the control plane was never about pulse-level portability.
It is about access to the actual interface for one’s device, with
documentation sufficient to reproduce results, calibration data
sufficient to interpret results, and electronics interfaces sufficient
to understand what physical loop closed when. Hardware specificity is a
reason to publish more documentation per device, not less.

VI-F

The trajectory is consolidating, not closing

What we read as “closing” is actually consolidation around abstraction
levels appropriate to different audiences. Cloud users want gate-level
access and a queue that returns answers quickly; pulse-level access can
remain available to research partners under separate agreements. IBM’s
deprecation of Qiskit Pulse on cloud QPUs is consistent with this read:
the access has not vanished from the universe, only from the public
cloud tier. Vendors are differentiating their offerings across audience
segments, which is normal commercial behavior and which produces better
experience for each segment than a single one-size interface would.

The consolidation read is honest about the commercial logic but evades
the reproducibility problem. “Available to research partners under
separate agreements” is not the same as “publicly accessible.” A
result that depended on access available only to a privileged few cannot
be reproduced by anyone outside that circle, which is a property of
closed access regardless of what the vendor calls it. The paper is
about what is publicly citable, reproducible, and auditable, and the
catalog grades on that axis. A vendor that offers pulse access only
under NDA gets graded as closed at the public layer, with a note that
private access exists. The consolidation framing is also incomplete on
the timeline: vendors that have moved over the last three years have
moved overwhelmingly toward closing public access, not toward
consolidating multiple tiers of access. If the trajectory were
consolidation, the catalog would show a fan-out of access levels at
large vendors; what it shows is contraction.

VI-G

Open access creates safety and security risks

Pulse-level APIs let users emit arbitrary microwave envelopes; a
malformed pulse can damage cryogenic systems or saturate amplifiers
downstream. Calibration data publication can leak side-channel
information about device topology, decoherence characteristics,
fab-process variation, and other facts the vendor has commercial reasons
to protect. Open electronics interfaces broaden the attack surface for
denial-of-service and resource exhaustion. Vendor closure at the
control plane is partly a security-perimeter decision, not just a
product-tier decision, and security-perimeter decisions are appropriately
made by vendors rather than by external commentators.

The security concerns are real but they argue for guarded openness, not
full closure. Tiered access with safety-aware abstractions, sandboxed
test environments before pulse submission, rate limiting, and parameter
range validation are all standard mitigations that other engineering
domains use to expose risky interfaces safely. The semiconductor PDK
analogue is informative: SkyWater’s open PDK exposes process-design
rules that could in principle be used to design adversarial chips, and
the response was layered access with documentation-quality gates, not
closure
[
5
,
8
]
. Calibration data
side-channels are a more interesting concern, but the catalog grades
calibration data publication separately from pulse access for exactly
this reason. A vendor can publish coarse-grained calibration metrics
(
T_{1}
,
T_{2}
, gate error rates) without publishing the fine-grained
device-fingerprint data that creates real side-channel risk.

VI-H

Open access undercuts the funding model that pays for the
hardware

The field’s progress depends on commercial vendors profiting; investors
fund the hardware capital expenditure on the expectation of returns;
openness erodes the moat that justifies the capital. If the control
plane is open, the remaining monetization surface is the hardware itself. Public funding
cannot replace private capital at the scale current quantum hardware
development requires; an argument that erodes private capital is
therefore an argument against the field’s continued progress.

The funding-model argument has a real core but conflates two different
layers. The hardware itself remains a moat: no one is open-sourcing
dilution refrigerators, ion-trap geometries, photonic chip fabrication
processes, or laser systems. The control plane is a thin layer of
software and electronics interfaces that sits on top of the hardware
moat, and the catalog illustrates that openness at this layer is
compatible with running a commercial business. IQM and Rigetti both
run commercial businesses with open pulse-level access (Pulla, Quil-T)
and have not visibly suffered for it. Pasqal and QuEra both run
commercial businesses with open neutral-atom SDKs (Pulser, Bloqade) and
have not visibly suffered for it. The funding-model argument would be
stronger if the closed layer were the same as the moat layer; in
practice they are not. A version of the argument focused on
differentiation specifically (vendors need
something
proprietary
to differentiate from competitors) is more honest, and the response
there is that hardware, calibration routines, fabrication processes, and
product polish all remain proprietary; the control plane is not the
only differentiation surface and arguably not even the most important
one.

VII
Historical Analogues

Three analogues from open computing infrastructure inform what happens
when a previously closed bottom-of-stack interface opens under pressure.
We avoid Python and GCC as analogues because they are too easy to dismiss
as cherry-picked; we keep Linux and LLVM, and add the SkyWater open
process design kit because the structural fit is closer to the
control-plane question than either of the other two.

Linux.
The right framing is not “open beat closed” but
“open won at the layer everyone insisted was too low-level to matter”

[
36
]
. In 1991, the conventional wisdom said operating
system kernels were not where open source could compete. The bet that
paid off was at the layer with the most direct hardware contact, the
highest engineering complexity, and the least obvious commercial
appeal. The analogue to the control plane in quantum computing maps
cleanly: same bet, same skepticism, same mismatch between commercial
incentive and field need.

LLVM.
The right framing is “an open compiler middle-end made
hardware-aware research possible across vendors”
[
16
]
.
LLVM started in 2000 as a research project at the University of Illinois
Urbana–Champaign and grew into a substrate that downstream compilers
(Clang, Swift, Rust, Julia, Mojo, and effectively every JIT in
production) all share. The analogue to the control plane is structural:
a layer where reuse compounds, where vendor specialization lives below
the line, and where the open layer is what makes new hardware actually
addressable by upstream tools.

SkyWater open PDK.
The SkyWater 130nm PDK opened in 2020 a
previously NDA-locked layer of the semiconductor design stack: process
design kits
[
8
,
5
]
. The result was that
startups, academic groups, and student projects could design real
silicon without proprietary access agreements. The control-plane
analogy is closer than Linux or LLVM in one specific way: the layer
that opened was the one where the hardware vendor’s IP sat closest to
the user, and the opening was a deliberate commercial decision with
measurable downstream effects. The second-order effect of SkyWater
(Tiny Tapeout, OpenROAD adoption, the broader open-EDA flowering) is
the analogue’s strongest contribution: opening one layer made adjacent
layers more useful, which is the same dynamic that would happen at
the control plane.

The three analogues do not predict the same outcome for quantum. They
do show that the structural pattern (a closed bottom-of-stack interface
under pressure to open) is not new and that the historical record
contains examples of openness at exactly the kind of layer we are
discussing here.

VIII
Toward a Floor: What Minimally Open Looks Like

This section sketches what minimally open access at the control plane
would have to look like. The framing is what the field needs, not what
someone should build. We do not propose an architecture, a runtime, a
stack shape, or a reference implementation. We name four floor
elements at the schema-shape level: each paragraph describes what would
have to be true for the element to count as met, but does not propose a
specific format, library, or institution to maintain it.

Open pulse APIs.
Public-API users have a documented interface
to pulse synthesis on the hardware they are running on. The interface
is stable enough that an experiment written against it last year still
runs against it today, or has a documented migration path to a
successor interface. Pulse-level access is not the same as raw waveform
exposure; what counts is that the user can specify pulse-level
properties of their experiment and have those properties faithfully
executed. Whether that interface is OpenPulse, Quil-T, Pulser, Pulla,
or something else is not the relevant question; that the interface
exists, is documented, and is stable, is.

Published calibration schemas.
Per-device calibration data is
machine-readable, dated, retained for the operational lifetime of the
device, and covers at minimum the parameters needed to reproduce a
published result. Schema definitions are public so that consumers of
the data know what fields to expect and what their semantics are.
Publication cadence is documented, even if the cadence is per-job
rather than periodic. Historical data is accessible to researchers
who need to interpret a result that was published months or years ago.

Documented control electronics interfaces.
The schema between
SDK and control electronics is published, with enough detail that a
third-party electronics vendor could in principle build a compatible
backend. This does not require open hardware; it requires that the
interface between the vendor’s software and the vendor’s hardware be a
documented contract rather than an opaque internal coupling. Reference
implementations such as QubiC
[
43
]
demonstrate that the layer
can be built openly by national labs even when commercial vendors are
not yet ready to publish their own.

Reproducibility guarantees.
Returned results carry provenance
metadata sufficient to identify the calibration snapshot, the
hardware revision, the SDK version, and the queue tier under which the
job ran. Calibration is versioned with stable identifiers. The vendor
publishes a commitment about how long a given hardware revision is held
stable, even if the commitment is short, so that researchers can plan
experiments whose validity depends on access to that revision.

VIII-A

What OQD is Already Building

Open Quantum Design is one shape of the floor: a non-profit foundation
building reference open hardware in the trapped-ion modality, with
partnerships across academia and the open-source quantum
community
[
39
]
. This paper does not call for another foundation
of OQD’s shape, but for broad vendor adoption of the practices the
floor names. The two efforts are complementary. OQD demonstrates that
the floor is achievable in principle; commercial vendor adoption
demonstrates that the floor is achievable in the platforms most
researchers actually use. Neither effort substitutes for the other.

IX
Conclusion

The catalog grades thirteen commercial quantum computing vendors at
what we call the control plane, the layer between gate-level circuit
specification and physical control electronics. Public access to this
layer has bifurcated. IBM’s removal of pulse-level control from all
production QPUs on February 3, 2025 stands as the most consequential
single event in the period under review and is the load-bearing case
for the closing-trajectory reading. Mid-tier superconducting vendors
(IQM, Rigetti, OQC) and the more open neutral-atom platforms (Pasqal,
QuEra) and one photonic vendor (Xanadu) have moved in the opposite
direction. Trapped-ion vendors have held steady at native-gate-only
access. The photonic modality is split three ways and demonstrates
that modality alone does not predict openness.

The three harms named in Section
I-A
are now concrete.
Reproducibility has been measurably eroded for IBM hardware as of
February 2025. Hardware-aware research at the pulse layer is gated by
which vendor a researcher chooses, and the choice is increasingly
constrained on the largest cloud platforms. Cross-vendor benchmarking
at the pulse layer is structurally impossible across many of the
catalog’s rows because the underlying interfaces differ in kind, not
degree.

The catalog’s strongest implication is not the closing trajectory itself
but the asymmetry of the closing. Vendors that started open have
stayed open; vendors that grew large cloud user bases have closed;
stealth fault-tolerant programs started closed. The mechanism is
commercial position and historical inflection point, not modality or
technical necessity. The floor we sketch in Section
VIII
is
not difficult to meet on the technical side; the elements are met
already by vendors at smaller scale. What changes is whether the
largest platforms decide that meeting the floor is part of running a
quantum cloud service.

We make no recommendation about how the floor should be enforced. We
do invite three reactions. Vendors who already meet the floor have a
citable description of what they are doing right and a place to point
when they are asked to justify their interface choices. Funders
considering whether to make openness a precondition for grant
solicitations now have a vendor-by-vendor reference for what openness
currently looks like in the commercial landscape. Researchers writing
methods papers have a citable artifact for the related-work paragraphs
that describe their hardware platform’s access model.

The catalog is released as a separate machine-readable artifact under
CC-BY-4.0 with source URLs and accessed-on dates per cell. Forks and
updates are encouraged. The control plane will look different in a
year; the catalog is meant to be a living document that other
researchers can extend, correct, and disagree with on a per-cell basis.
