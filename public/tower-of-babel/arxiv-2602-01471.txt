Erdős Matching (Conjecture) Theorem
Tapas Kumar Mishra

Abstract
Let ℱ \mathcal{F} be a family of k k -sized subsets of [ n ] [n] that does not contain s s pairwise disjoint subsets. The Erdős Matching Conjecture, a celebrated and long-standing open problem in extremal combinatorics, asserts the maximum cardinality of ℱ \mathcal{F} is upper bounded by max ⁡ { ( s ​ k − 1 k ) , ( n k ) − ( n − s + 1 k ) } \max\left\{\binom{sk-1}{k},\binom{n}{k}-\allowbreak\binom{n-s+1}{k}\right\} . These two bounds correspond to the sizes of two canonical extremal families: one in which all subsets are contained within a ground set of s ​ k − 1 sk-1 elements, and one in which every subset intersects a fixed set of s − 1 s-1 elements. In this paper, we prove the conjecture. keywords:Erdős Matching Conjecture, Shifting Technique.



1
Introduction

A central problem in extremal combinatorics is to determine the maximum possible size of a set family subject to a given constraint. We consider families
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
on the ground set
[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}
. A
matching
in
\mathcal{F}
is a collection of pairwise disjoint members of
\mathcal{F}
. The
matching number
of
\mathcal{F}
, denoted
\nu(\mathcal{F})
, is the cardinality of a maximum matching.

This paper focuses on
k
-uniform families,
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{k}
, that are
s
-matching-free, i.e.,
\nu(\mathcal{F})\leq s-1
. We denote the maximum possible size of such a family by
f(n,k,s)
. In 1965, Erdős
[
4
]
posed a conjecture for the exact value of
f(n,k,s)
, which has become a celebrated open problem in the field.

Conjecture 1

(Erdős Matching Conjecture
[
5
]
)
.

For
n\geq sk
,

f(n,k,s)=\max\left\{\binom{sk-1}{k},\binom{n}{k}-\binom{n-s+1}{k}\right\}.

The two expressions in the maximum correspond to two canonical extremal families.

1.

The family
\mathcal{G}^{*}=\binom{[sk-1]}{k}
, which consists of all
k
-subsets of a fixed set of
sk-1
elements.

2.

The family
\mathcal{F}^{*}=\{F\in\binom{[n]}{k}:F\cap S\neq\emptyset\}
for a fixed set
S\subset[n]
with
|S|=s-1
. This family consists of all
k
-subsets that intersect a fixed set of
s-1
elements.

The conjecture is trivially true for
n<sk
, as in this case
\nu(\mathcal{F})\leq s-1
holds for the entire family
\binom{[n]}{k}
.
The conjecture has a rich history and has been verified in several important cases. For
s=2
, the problem is to find the largest intersecting family, and the conjecture follows from the Erdős-Ko-Rado theorem
[
3
]
. Kleitman
[
10
]
proved the conjecture for the boundary case
n=sk
.

Significant progress has been made on the conjecture for sufficiently large
n
. Erdős
[
4
]
first established the conjecture for
n\geq n_{0}(k,s)
. This bound was subsequently sharpened by Bollobás, Daykin, and Erdős
[
2
]
to
n\geq 2sk^{3}
, and later by Huang, Lo, and Sudakov
[
9
]
to
n\geq 3sk^{2}
.

Other results have affirmed the conjecture for specific parameters or ranges. Frankl
[
8
]
proved the conjecture for
n\geq(2s+1)k-s
and also provided the general upper bound
f(n,k,s)\leq(s-1)\binom{n-1}{k-1}
. Frankl, Rödl, and Ruciński
[
7
]
established the case
k=3
for
n\geq 4s
. More recently, Kolupaev and Kupavskii
[
11
]
proved the conjecture for
k\geq 5
,
s>100k^{3}
, and
sk\leq n<s(k+100/k)
. For a comprehensive survey of recent results, see
[
6
]
.

Despite this extensive body of work, the conjecture has remained open in its entirety. In this paper, we provide a complete proof.

1.1
Our results

We establish the conjecture for all
n\geq sk
. Our proof is algorithmic and relies on a key distinction between trivial and non-trivial families.

Definition 1
.

A family is said to be
trivial
if there exists at least one element
x\in[n]
that is not contained in any
F\in\mathcal{F}
. A family that is not trivial is
non-trivial
.

By analyzing the behavior of families under shift operations, and paying close attention to whether intermediate families in our algorithm become trivial, we establish the following theorem in Section
3
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
n,s,k
be positive integers,
n\geq sk
,
k\geq 1
.
Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{k}
be a family with no
s
pairwise disjoint sets. Then,
|\mathcal{F}|\leq\max\left\{\binom{n}{k}-\binom{n-s+1}{k},\binom{sk-1}{k}\right\}
.

The key technical ingredients in our proof are the classical
(i,j)
shift operator of Fránkl. The precise effects of these operators on a family’s matching number and its potential for triviality are detailed in Lemma
1
,
2
. This algorithmic framework, which tracks the properties of families through a sequence of shifts, can be extended to the non-uniform case as well.

2
Shifting operator and supporting lemmas

Definition 2

(Fránkl’s
(i,j)
shift)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
. For
1\leq i,j\leq n
, define
\mathcal{C}_{ij}(\mathcal{F})=\{\mathcal{C}_{ij}(F):F\in\mathcal{F}\}
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{ij}(F)=\begin{cases}(F\setminus\{j\})\cup\{i\},\text{ if $i\not\in F$, $j\in F$, and $(F\setminus\{j\})\cup\{i\}\not\in\mathcal{F}$},\\
F,\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

We have two lemmas on the effects of a
(i,j)
shift on a family
\mathcal{F}
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a family of subsets of
[n]
and let
1\leq i,j\leq n
. Then the following hold:

(i)

For any
F\in\mathcal{F}
,
|\mathcal{C}_{ij}(F)|=|F|
.

(ii)

|\mathcal{C}_{ij}(\mathcal{F})|=|\mathcal{F}|
.

(iii)

The matching number does not increase, i.e.,
\nu(\mathcal{C}_{ij}(\mathcal{F}))\leq\nu(\mathcal{F})
.

Proof.

The proof of (i) and (ii) are given in
[
8
]
. As (iii) is probably folklore, the proof is given in the Appendix
A
.
∎

Lemma 2
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a trivial family on the ground set
[n]
. Then for any
1\leq i,j\leq n
,
i\neq j
, the family
\mathcal{C}_{ij}(\mathcal{F})
is also a trivial family.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a trivial family. By definition, there exists at least one element
x\in[n]
that is not contained in any set in
\mathcal{F}
. That is,

\exists x\in[n]\text{ such that }\forall F\in\mathcal{F},x\notin F

We want to prove that
\mathcal{C}_{ij}(\mathcal{F})
is also a trivial family. This means we must show that there exists some element
y\in[n]
such that for every set
G\in\mathcal{C}_{ij}(\mathcal{F})
, we have
y\notin G
.

Let
G
be an arbitrary set in
\mathcal{C}_{ij}(\mathcal{F})
. By definition,
G=\mathcal{C}_{ij}(F)
for some
F\in\mathcal{F}
.
We proceed by analyzing the element
x
that is missing from
\mathcal{F}
.
We analyze three disjoint cases for the missing element
x
.

Case 1: The missing element
x
is
j
(i.e.,
x=j
).

In this case, for all
F\in\mathcal{F}
,
j\notin F
. The condition for the shift to occur requires
j\in F
. Since this condition is never met for any
F\in\mathcal{F}
, the shift never happens. Therefore,
\mathcal{C}_{ij}(F)=F
for every
F\in\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{C}_{ij}(\mathcal{F})=\mathcal{F}
. Since
\mathcal{F}
is trivial,
\mathcal{C}_{ij}(\mathcal{F})
is also trivial.

Case 2: The missing element
x
is
i
(i.e.,
x=i
).

In this case, for all
F\in\mathcal{F}
,
i\notin F
. We will prove that
\mathcal{C}_{ij}(\mathcal{F})
is trivial because it is missing the element
j
. That is, we claim
y=j
.
Let
G\in\mathcal{C}_{ij}(\mathcal{F})
.
G=\mathcal{C}_{ij}(F)
for some
F\in\mathcal{F}
.
Since
i\notin F
is always true by our assumption, the definition of the shift simplifies.

Subcase 2a: The shift does not happen.

G=F
. This occurs if
j\notin F
or if
j\in F
but the shift is blocked because
F^{\prime}=(F\setminus\{j\})\cup\{i\}\in\mathcal{F}
.
The second possibility (
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
) is impossible, because
i\in F^{\prime}
, but our assumption is that
i
is missing from all sets in
\mathcal{F}
.
Therefore, the only reason the shift does not happen is that
j\notin F
.
In this case,
G=F
, and we know
j\notin F
, so
j\notin G
.

Subcase 2b: The shift does happen.

G=(F\setminus\{j\})\cup\{i\}
. This occurs if
j\in F
(and the resulting set is not in
\mathcal{F}
, which we have shown is irrelevant).
By construction,
G
is formed by removing the element
j
from
F
.
Since
i\neq j
,
j
is not added back.
Therefore,
j\notin G
.

In both subcases,
j\notin G
. Since
G
was arbitrary, no set in
\mathcal{C}_{ij}(\mathcal{F})
contains
j
. Thus,
\mathcal{C}_{ij}(\mathcal{F})
is trivial.

Case 3: The missing element
x
is not
i
and not
j
.

G=\mathcal{C}_{ij}(F)
can never contain
x
as
x\not\in F
and
x\not\in\{i,j\}
.

In all the cases, if
\mathcal{F}
is a trivial family,
\mathcal{C}_{ij}(\mathcal{F})
is also a trivial family.
∎

\mathcal{F}^{x}=\{F\in\mathcal{F}:x\in F\}

Statement of Theorem 1
.

Let
n,s,k
be positive integers,
n\geq sk
,
k\geq 1
.
Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{k}
be a family with no
s

|\mathcal{F}|\leq\max\left\{\binom{n}{k}-\binom{n-s+1}{k},\binom{sk-1}{k}\right\}

Proof.

The family
\mathcal{F}^{*}=\{F\in\binom{[n]}{k}:F\cap\{1,\dots,s-1\}\neq\emptyset\}
satisfies the condition
\nu(\mathcal{F}^{*})\leq s-1
and has size exactly
\binom{n}{k}-\binom{n-s+1}{k}
.
Let
\mathcal{G}^{*}=\binom{[sk-1]}{k}
.
We will show that no family satisfying the hypothesis can be larger in cardinality than both
\mathcal{F}^{*}

\mathcal{G}^{*}

\mathcal{F}
into one where the desired structure is evident.

I. The Algorithm and Potential Function

We define a
potential function

\Phi(\mathcal{G})
for any family
\mathcal{G}
as the number of sets in the family that intersect the fixed set
S=\{1,\dots,s-1\}
:

\Phi(\mathcal{G})=|\{G\in\mathcal{G}:G\cap S\neq\emptyset\}|

Our algorithm proceeds as follows. Set
n^{\prime}=n
.

1.

If
\mathcal{F}
is trivial, let
Y\subseteq[n^{\prime}]
be the largest set of vertices with no element of
Y
being a member of any set in
\mathcal{F}
(i.e.
\forall y\in Y\forall F\in\mathcal{F}
,
y\not\in F
). Set
\mathcal{F}
to be the non-trivial family on
[n^{\prime}]\setminus Y
and with a relabeling (that does not change
S
), assume the ground set is exactly
[n^{\prime}-|Y|]
. Set
n^{\prime}=n^{\prime}-|Y|
. Note that this transformation preserves
|\mathcal{F}|
and
\nu(\mathcal{F})
.
If
n^{\prime}\leq sk-1
,
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{G}^{*}
and we terminate the algorithm.
Otherwise, set
\mathcal{F}^{*}=\{F\in\binom{[n^{\prime}]}{k}:F\cap\{1,\dots,s-1\}\neq\emptyset\}
.

2.

If the current family
\mathcal{F}
is not a subfamily of
\mathcal{F}^{*}
, then there must exist a set
A\in\mathcal{F}
such that
A\cap S=\emptyset
.

3.

There must also exist a set
B\notin\mathcal{F}
such that
B\cap S\neq\emptyset
. If no such
B
exists, the algorithm terminates and proceeds to the “Contradiction” phase described in Section III.

4.

Let
X=A\cap B
. Define
A^{\prime}=A\setminus X=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{r}\}
and
B^{\prime}=B\setminus X=\{b_{1},\dots,b_{r}\}
. By construction, all elements
a\in A^{\prime}
are not in
S
. We can order the elements in
B^{\prime}
such that
b_{1}\in S
. The remaining elements in
B^{\prime}
and all elements in
A^{\prime}
are ordered arbitrarily.

5.

Define
B_{1}=B
,
\mathcal{B}=()
,

A_{1}=A
,
\mathcal{A}=(A_{1})
.
I=J=\phi
.
Check if there exists an element
j\in A^{\prime}
such that the set
B=(A\setminus\{j\})\cup\{b_{1}\}\notin\mathcal{F}
. If such an
j
exists, set
i_{1}=b_{1}
,
j_{1}=j
,

I=(i_{1})
,
J=(j_{1})
and go to Step 6. Otherwise, pick an arbitrary
j\in A^{\prime}
and set
B_{1}=(A_{1}\setminus\{j\})\cup\{b_{1}\}\in\mathcal{F}
.
\mathcal{B}=(B_{1})
. Set
i_{1}=b_{1}
,
j_{1}=j
,

I=(i_{1})
,
J=(j_{1})
.

For
t=2
to
r
:

(a)

Set
A_{t}=B_{t-1}
,
\mathcal{A}=(A_{1},\ldots,A_{t-1},A_{t})
.

(b)

Check if there exists a
j\in A^{\prime}\setminus J
and a
i\in B^{\prime}\setminus I
such that the set
B=(A_{t}\setminus\{j\})\cup\{i\}\notin\mathcal{F}
. If such a
j
and
i
exists, set
I=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{t-1},i)
,
J=(j_{1},\ldots,j_{t-1},j)

\mathcal{B}=(B_{1},\ldots,B_{t-1},B)
and go to go to Step 6.

(c)

Otherwise, pick an arbitrary
j\in A^{\prime}\setminus J
, and a
i\in B^{\prime}\setminus I
and set
B_{t}=(A_{t}\setminus\{j\})\cup\{i\}\in\mathcal{F}
.
\mathcal{B}=(B_{1},\ldots,B_{t-1},B_{t})
. Set
I=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{t-1},i)
,
J=(j_{1},\ldots,j_{t-1},j)
.

Note that if the above loop exits for a

, then
B_{t}\not\in\mathcal{F}
as guaranteed by part (b). If the above loop runs till

, observe that
B_{t}=B_{r}=B\not\in\mathcal{F}
by construction.

6.

Let
|I|=t
. Set
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\mathcal{F}
,
\mathcal{F}_{p-1}=C_{I(p)J(p)}(\mathcal{F}_{p})
, where
p
goes from
t
to
1
. Set
\mathcal{F}_{new}=\mathcal{F}_{0}
. Note that if
after application of any constituent shift
\mathcal{C}_{ij}
, the intermediate family is trivial, then using Lemma
2
,
all the subsequent families leading to
\mathcal{F}_{new}
and
\mathcal{F}_{new}
are trivial.

7.

Replace
\mathcal{F}
with
\mathcal{F}_{new}
and go to step 1.

By Lemma
1
, at each step we have
|\mathcal{F}_{new}|=|\mathcal{F}|
and
\nu(\mathcal{F}_{new})\leq\nu(\mathcal{F})\leq s-1
.

II. Proof of Progress

The algorithm makes progress because the potential function
\Phi
strictly increases at each step. We need to show
\Phi(\mathcal{F}_{new})>\Phi(\mathcal{F})
. To show this,
we prove that (i)
|\mathcal{F}_{new}^{i}|\geq|\mathcal{F}^{i}|
, for all
i\in S
;
and (ii)
|\mathcal{F}_{new}^{b_{1}}|>|\mathcal{F}^{b_{1}}|
. The first part follows from the fact that by construction, no
i\in S
is ever moved out of any set during the entire process. So, we focus on proving
|\mathcal{F}_{new}^{b_{1}}|>|\mathcal{F}^{b_{1}}|
.

To prove that the number of sets containing
b_{1}
strictly increases, we analyze the sequence of intermediate families generated during Step 6:

\mathcal{F}_{t}=\mathcal{F}
,

\mathcal{F}_{p-1}=\mathcal{C}_{i_{p}j_{p}}(\mathcal{F}_{p})
for
p=t
down to
1
.
The final output is
\mathcal{F}_{new}=\mathcal{F}_{0}
.

Step 1: Invariance of
b_{1}
during intermediate shifts

Recall that
i_{1}=b_{1}
. For all
p\geq 2
, the shifts
\mathcal{C}_{i_{p}j_{p}}
involve adding an element
i_{p}\in B^{\prime}\setminus\{b_{1}\}
and removing an element
j_{p}\in A^{\prime}
. Because
A^{\prime}\cap S=\emptyset
and
b_{1}\in S
, we know
j_{p}\neq b_{1}
. By definition,
i_{p}\neq b_{1}
. Since
b_{1}\notin\{i_{p},j_{p}\}
for any
p\geq 2
, these shifts do not interact with
b_{1}
. For any set
F
and any
p\geq 2
:

b_{1}\in F\iff b_{1}\in\mathcal{C}_{i_{p}j_{p}}(F).

Therefore, the total number of sets containing
b_{1}
is strictly preserved during these intermediate steps:

|\mathcal{F}_{p-1}^{b_{1}}|=|\mathcal{F}_{p}^{b_{1}}|\quad\text{for all }p\geq 2.

Consequently, just before the final shift, we have exactly our starting amount:
|\mathcal{F}_{1}^{b_{1}}|=|\mathcal{F}_{t}^{b_{1}}|=|\mathcal{F}^{b_{1}}|
.

Step 2: Non-decreasing nature of the final shift

At the final step (
p=1
), we apply
\mathcal{C}_{b_{1}j_{1}}
to
\mathcal{F}_{1}
. This shift only replaces
j_{1}
with
b_{1}
. Any set that already contains
b_{1}
is untouched, and some sets lacking
b_{1}
might gain it. Thus,
|\mathcal{F}_{0}^{b_{1}}|\geq|\mathcal{F}_{1}^{b_{1}}|
.To establish the strict inequality
|\mathcal{F}_{0}^{b_{1}}|>|\mathcal{F}_{1}^{b_{1}}|
, we must rigorously prove that at least one set in
\mathcal{F}_{1}
is actively shifted by
\mathcal{C}_{b_{1}j_{1}}
. Specifically, we will prove that
A_{1}\in\mathcal{F}_{1}
and that its shifted target
B_{1}\notin\mathcal{F}_{1}
.

Step 3: Proving
A_{p}\in\mathcal{F}_{p}

We must ensure that the sets
A_{p}
are not accidentally shifted away before their turn. We prove
A_{p}\in\mathcal{F}_{p}
by contradiction. Assume
A_{p}\notin\mathcal{F}_{p}
. Since
A_{p}\in\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{t}
, it must have been shifted away by some operation
\mathcal{C}_{i_{m_{1}}j_{m_{1}}}
at a step
m_{1}>p
. This means its target
X_{1}=(A_{p}\setminus\{j_{m_{1}}\})\cup\{i_{m_{1}}\}
was absent:
X_{1}\notin\mathcal{F}_{m_{1}}
. However, at step
p
of the algorithm’s loop, we explicitly checked all valid pairs, including
(j_{m_{1}},i_{m_{1}})
. Because the algorithm did not stop at step
p
, it guarantees that
X_{1}\in\mathcal{F}_{t}
. For
X_{1}\in\mathcal{F}_{t}
to become missing in
\mathcal{F}_{m_{1}}
, it must have been shifted away at some step
m_{2}>m_{1}
, creating a new target
X_{2}\notin\mathcal{F}_{m_{2}}
. This logic triggers an infinite chain of indices
p<m_{1}<m_{2}<m_{3}<\dots\leq t
. Since
t
is strictly finite, this is a contradiction. Therefore,
A_{p}
is never shifted prematurely:

A_{p}\in\mathcal{F}_{p}\quad\text{for all }1\leq p\leq t.

Step 4: Proving
B_{1}\notin\mathcal{F}_{1}

We prove
B_{p}\notin\mathcal{F}_{p}
by reverse induction from
t
down to
1
.

Base Case (
p=t
): By the stopping condition of Step 5,
B_{t}\notin\mathcal{F}
. Note that this is always true since
B=B_{r}\notin\mathcal{F}
by construction. Since
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\mathcal{F}
, we have
B_{t}\notin\mathcal{F}_{t}
.

Inductive Step (
p\to p-1
): Assume
B_{p}\notin\mathcal{F}_{p}
. We apply
\mathcal{C}_{i_{p}j_{p}}
to
\mathcal{F}_{p}
.From Step 3, we know
A_{p}\in\mathcal{F}_{p}
. Because
A_{p}\in\mathcal{F}_{p}
, and its shifted target
(A_{p}\setminus\{j_{p}\})\cup\{i_{p}\}=B_{p}\notin\mathcal{F}_{p}
, the shift operator will execute on
A_{p}
. Therefore,
\mathcal{C}_{i_{p}j_{p}}
explicitly converts
A_{p}
into
B_{p}
. This means in the resulting family
\mathcal{F}_{p-1}
, the set
B_{p}
is now present, and
A_{p}
is gone (
A_{p}\notin\mathcal{F}_{p-1}
). By the algorithm’s construction,
A_{p}=B_{p-1}
. Thus, substituting the name, we get
B_{p-1}\notin\mathcal{F}_{p-1}
.

By cascading this domino effect down to
p=1
, we conclude that
B_{1}\notin\mathcal{F}_{1}
.

Step 5: Conclusion of Strict Progress

At the final step (
p=1
), we apply
\mathcal{C}_{b_{1}j_{1}}
to
\mathcal{F}_{1}
. From Step 3, we know
A_{1}\in\mathcal{F}_{1}
. From Step 4, we know
B_{1}\notin\mathcal{F}_{1}
. Since
A_{1}\in\mathcal{F}_{1}
,
b_{1}\notin A_{1}
,
j_{1}\in A_{1}
, and its target
(A_{1}\setminus\{j_{1}\})\cup\{b_{1}\}=B_{1}\notin\mathcal{F}_{1}
, the set
A_{1}
is explicitly shifted. Because
A_{1}
lacked
b_{1}
and its shifted version gains
b_{1}
, the number of sets containing
b_{1}
strictly increases:
|\mathcal{F}_{0}^{b_{1}}|>|\mathcal{F}_{1}^{b_{1}}|.

Substituting our initial equivalence from Step 1, we get the final strict progress bound:
|\mathcal{F}_{new}^{b_{1}}|>|\mathcal{F}^{b_{1}}|.

III. Termination and Contradiction

The algorithm terminates under three conditions.

Condition 1:
n^{\prime}\leq sk-1
. In this case,
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{G}^{*}
and
|\mathcal{F}|\leq\binom{sk-1}{k}
.

Condition 2: the current family
\mathcal{F}
is a subfamily of
\mathcal{F}^{*}
. In this case,
|\mathcal{F}|\leq\binom{n^{\prime}}{k}-\binom{n^{\prime}-s+1}{k}\leq\binom{n}{k}-\binom{n-s+1}{k}
and the bound follows.

Condition 3: if the algorithm reaches a state where it can find a set
A\in\mathcal{F}
with
A\cap S=\emptyset
, but it cannot find a set
B\notin\mathcal{F}
with
B\cap S\neq\emptyset
. This implies that the current family
\mathcal{F}
contains
all possible
k
-sets that intersect
S
.
We can now derive a contradiction. Let
A
be the set in
\mathcal{F}
that is disjoint from
S
. We can construct
s-1
new sets,
B_{1},\dots,B_{s-1}
, that are pairwise disjoint and also disjoint from
A
.

•

Choose a set
X
of size
(s-1)k
from
[n^{\prime}]\setminus A

such that
\{1,\ldots,s-1\}\subset X
.
Partition
X
into
s-1
sets
B_{1},\ldots,B_{s-1}

with
B_{i}\cap\{1,\ldots,s-1\}=\{i\}
.

This construction is possible because the condition
n^{\prime}\geq sk
ensures there are enough elements available. Since each
B_{i}
intersects
S
, and our family
\mathcal{F}
contains all such sets, it follows that
\{B_{1},\dots,B_{s-1}\}\subset\mathcal{F}
.
The collection
\{A,B_{1},\dots,B_{s-1}\}
is therefore a matching of size
s
within
\mathcal{F}
. This contradicts the hypothesis that
\nu(\mathcal{F})\leq s-1
.

IV. Conclusion

The contradiction in Condition 3 shows that the algorithm must terminate because it encounters either Condition 1 or Condition 2. Therefore, the theorem follows.
∎

4
Discussion

The resolution of the Erdős Matching Conjecture, now the Erdős Matching Theorem, marks a foundational moment in extremal set theory. It provides a complete and elegant answer to one of the most natural questions one can ask about set systems: what is the maximum size of a family of sets of a given uniformity that avoids a matching of a certain size? The theorem stands alongside results like Sperner’s Theorem and the Erdős-Ko-Rado Theorem as a central pillar of the field, establishing a fundamental relationship between the size of a family and its matching number,
\nu(\mathcal{F})
.
We outline some of the most prominent implications and open problems below.

I. Stability and Structural Results

A natural and compelling direction is the question of stability. The Erdős Matching Theorem characterizes the exact size of the extremal families. The stability question asks: if a family
\mathcal{F}
has no
s
-matching and its size is
close
to the maximum possible bound, must its structure be
close
to one of the two extremal examples (i.e., the family of all
k
-sets intersecting a fixed set of
s-1
elements, or the family of all
k
-sets within a ground set of size
sk-1
)? Affirmative answers to stability questions are often more powerful than the original theorem, providing deeper insight into the structure of families near the extremal threshold.

II. Rainbow and Multicolored Versions

Another vibrant area of research is the study of ”rainbow” or multicolored variants. Consider a collection of families
\mathcal{F}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{F}_{m}
. A rainbow matching is a set of
s
pairwise disjoint sets
\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{s}\}
such that each
A_{i}
is drawn from a distinct family
\mathcal{F}_{i}
. What conditions on the sizes of the families
|\mathcal{F}_{i}|
guarantee the existence of a rainbow matching? This generalization connects the Erdős Matching Theorem to other famous problems in combinatorics, such as the Ryser-Brualdi-Stein conjecture on transversals in Latin squares (see Aharoni, Charbit and Howard
[
1
]
), and remains an active area of investigation.

III. Generalizations to Other Structures

The framework of the theorem can be extended to more complex combinatorial objects. For instance, what are the analogous bounds for uniform hypergraphs where we forbid more complex structures than just a matching, such as a Berge cycle or a perfect matching? While the concept of disjointness is simple for sets, defining analogous ”disjointness” for other structures can lead to deep and challenging new problems. Furthermore, investigating weighted versions of the theorem, where sets are assigned weights and we seek to maximize the total weight of the family, is another promising direction.

IV. Algorithmic Implications

From a computational perspective, the problem of finding the maximum matching in a hypergraph is notoriously difficult (NP-hard). The Erdős Matching Theorem provides a sharp combinatorial bound on the size of a family that forces a matching of a certain size not to exist. This raises the question: can this combinatorial insight be leveraged to design better approximation algorithms or fixed-parameter tractable algorithms for hypergraph matching? Understanding the structural properties of large families with a small matching number could provide the necessary leverage to tackle these long-standing algorithmic challenges.

Appendix A
Matching number after a shift

Lemma 3
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a family of subsets of
[n]
and let
1\leq i,j\leq n
. Then the following hold:

(i)

For any
F\in\mathcal{F}
,
|\mathcal{C}_{ij}(F)|=|F|
.

(ii)

|\mathcal{C}_{ij}(\mathcal{F})|=|\mathcal{F}|
.

(iii)

The matching number does not increase, i.e.,
\nu(\mathcal{C}_{ij}(\mathcal{F}))\leq\nu(\mathcal{F})
.

Proof.

(i) The operator
\mathcal{C}_{ij}
either leaves a set
F
unchanged or replaces one element,
j
, with another,
i
. In both cases, the cardinality of the set is preserved.

(ii) The map
F\mapsto\mathcal{C}_{ij}(F)
is a bijection from
\mathcal{F}
to
\mathcal{C}_{ij}(\mathcal{F})
. To see this, we only need to show it is injective. Suppose
\mathcal{C}_{ij}(F_{1})=\mathcal{C}_{ij}(F_{2})=G
. If neither set was shifted,
F_{1}=F_{2}=G
. If both were shifted, then
F_{1}=(G\setminus\{i\})\cup\{j\}
and
F_{2}=(G\setminus\{i\})\cup\{j\}
, which implies
F_{1}=F_{2}
. A case where one is shifted and the other is not (e.g.,
G=(F_{1}\setminus\{j\})\cup\{i\}
and
G=F_{2}
) leads to a contradiction with the definition of the shift, as the existence of
F_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
would have prevented
F_{1}
from being shifted to
G
. Thus, the map is a bijection and
|\mathcal{C}_{ij}(\mathcal{F})|=|\mathcal{F}|
.

Let
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}=\mathcal{C}_{ij}(\mathcal{F})
. To show that
\nu(\mathcal{F}^{\prime})\leq\nu(\mathcal{F})
, we will take a maximum matching in
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
and construct a matching of the same size in
\mathcal{F}
.
Let
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}=\{B_{1},B_{2},\dots,B_{p}\}
be a maximum matching in
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
, so
p=\nu(\mathcal{F}^{\prime})
. The sets
B_{k}
are pairwise disjoint. For each
B_{k}\in\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
, there is a unique preimage
A_{k}\in\mathcal{F}
such that
B_{k}=\mathcal{C}_{ij}(A_{k})
. A set
B_{k}
is altered by the shift (i.e.,
B_{k}\neq A_{k}
) only if
B_{k}=(A_{k}\setminus\{j\})\cup\{i\}
. This implies that
i\in B_{k}
. Since the sets in the matching
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
are pairwise disjoint, at most one set in
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
can contain the element
i
. Therefore,
at most one set in
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
could have been altered
by the
\mathcal{C}_{ij}
operation. We consider two cases:

Case 1: No set in
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
was altered.

In this case,
B_{k}=A_{k}
for all
k=1,\dots,p
. This means every set in
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
is also in
\mathcal{F}
. Thus,
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
itself is a matching of size
p
in
\mathcal{F}
, so
\nu(\mathcal{F})\geq p
.

Case 2: Exactly one set in
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
was altered.

Without loss of generality, let
B_{1}
be the altered set. Then:

•

B_{1}=(A_{1}\setminus\{j\})\cup\{i\}
, where
A_{1}\in\mathcal{F}
,
i\notin A_{1}
,
j\in A_{1}
, and
B_{1}\notin\mathcal{F}
.

•

For all
k\geq 2
,
B_{k}=A_{k}\in\mathcal{F}
, and since
B_{1}\cap B_{k}=\emptyset
, we must have
i\notin B_{k}
.

Now, we construct a matching
\mathcal{M}
in
\mathcal{F}
. Consider the collection
\{A_{1},B_{2},\allowbreak\dots,B_{p}\}
.
If this collection is a matching, we are done. This occurs if
j\notin B_{k}
for all
k\geq 2
, as
A_{1}
is then disjoint from all
B_{k}
.
Suppose the collection is not a matching. This can only happen if
A_{1}
intersects some
B_{k}
for
k\geq 2
. Since
A_{1}=(B_{1}\setminus\{i\})\cup\{j\}
and
B_{1}
is disjoint from all other
B_{k}
, the intersection must be the element
j
. As the sets
\{B_{k}\}_{k\geq 2}
are disjoint, at most one of them can contain
j
. Let this be
B_{2}
, so
j\in B_{2}
.
Now we have
j\in B_{2}
and
i\notin B_{2}
. Since
B_{2}
was not altered by the shift (i.e.,
\mathcal{C}_{ij}(B_{2})=B_{2}
), the condition for shifting must have failed. This implies that the set
C_{2}=(B_{2}\setminus\{j\})\cup\{i\}
must already be in
\mathcal{F}
.

Let us define a new collection of sets
\mathcal{M}=\{A_{1},C_{2},B_{3},\dots,B_{p}\}
. We claim this is a matching of size

in
\mathcal{F}
.

1.

Membership in
\mathcal{F}
:
A_{1}\in\mathcal{F}
by definition.
C_{2}\in\mathcal{F}

2.

Pairwise Disjointness
:

•

The sets
\{B_{3},\dots,B_{p}\}
are pairwise disjoint.

•

For
k\geq 3
,
A_{1}\cap B_{k}=\emptyset

B_{1}\cap B_{k}=\emptyset

B_{k}
contains neither
i
nor

•

•

Crucially,
