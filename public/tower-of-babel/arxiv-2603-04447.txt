Spatial symmetry invariance of solution of Kolmogorov flow


Abstract
We prove a mathematical theorem that solution for all t > 0 t>0 of the Kolmogorov flow governed by Navier-Stokes (NS) equations with periodic boundary condition keeps the same spatial symmetry as its initial condition. The mathematical proof of a similar theorem for the three-dimensional incompressible NS equations is given in the appendix. These mathematical theorems can be used to check the correctness and reliability of numerical simulations of NS turbulence. For example, they support the corresponding CNS (clean numerical simulation) results of the 2D and 3D turbulent Kolmogorov flows [ 22 , 9 , 19 ] that remain the same spatial symmetry in the whole time interval of simulation, but do not support the corresponding DNS (direct numerical simulation) results that lose the spatial symmetry quickly. In other words, these DNS results violate these mathematical theorems. Thus, these mathematical theorems rigorously confirm that the spatiotemporal trajectories of NS turbulence given by DNS are indeed quickly polluted by numerical noises badly. All of these also indicate that CNS indeed can provide helpful enlightenments to deepen our understanding about turbulence and besides approach some mathematical truths about the NS equations.

Lorenz, who put forward in 1963 the famous “butterfly-effect” of chaos

Thus, logically speaking, DNS of NS turbulence should be quickly polluted by numerical noises badly.

Theorem of spatial symmetry invariance

For the 2D Kolmogorov flow governed by the Navier-Stokes equation (
1
) (with an even number
n_{K}
) under the periodic boundary condition (
2
) subject to a smooth initial condition with the spatial symmetry of rotation (
3
) and/or translation (
4
), if its Taylor series exists with a non-zero convergence radius
\rho
for all
t\geq 0
, its solution for all
t>0
keeps the same spatial symmetry as its initial condition.

Proof: Since the initial condition contains the spatial symmetry, i.e.
\psi(x,y,0)=\psi^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},0)
, we have according to Lemma 3 (by setting
t_{0}=0
) the spatial symmetry

\psi(x,y,t)=\psi^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},t),\hskip 28.45274ptt\in[0,\delta_{0}],

where
\delta_{0}<\rho_{0}
, and
\rho_{0}
is the radius of convergence of its Taylor series at
t=0
. Then, setting
t_{0}=\delta_{0}
, we have according to Lemma 3 the spatial symmetry

\psi(x,y,t)=\psi^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},t),\hskip 28.45274ptt\in[\delta_{0},\delta_{1}],

where
\delta_{1}<\rho_{1}
, and
\rho_{1}
is the radius of convergence of its Taylor series at
t=t_{0}=\delta_{0}
.
Furthermore, setting
t_{0}=\delta_{1}
, we have according to Lemma 3 the spatial symmetry

\psi(x,y,t)=\psi^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},t),\hskip 28.45274ptt\in[\delta_{1},\delta_{2}],

where
\delta_{2}<\rho_{2}
, and
\rho_{2}
is the radius of convergence of its Taylor series at
t=t_{0}=\delta_{1}
.
Note that

[0,\delta_{0}]\cup[\delta_{0},\delta_{1}]\cup[\delta_{1},\delta_{2}]=[0,\delta_{2}],

thus now there exists the spatial symmetry

\psi(x,y,t)=\psi^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},t),\hskip 28.45274ptt\in[0,\delta_{2}]

in a larger interval of time. The same process can keep going, since the Taylor series of
\psi(x,y,t)
always exists with a non-zero radius of convergence.
Thus, it always holds the spatial symmetry

\psi(x,y,t)=\psi^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},t),\;\;t\in[0,+\infty).

This ends the proof of the theorem.

3
Remarks and discussions

Remark 1

Let
O
denote one observer in the coordinate system
O-xy
, and
O^{\prime}
denote another observer in the coordinate system
O^{\prime}-x^{\prime}y^{\prime}
, respectively, where
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=(x+\pi,y+\pi)
and/or
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=(2\pi-x,2\pi-y)
. When there exist the spatial symmetry
\psi^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},0)=\psi(x,y,0)
for the initial condition, the governing equations and the initial condition are the
same
for both of the observer
O
in the coordinate system
O-xy
and the observer
O^{\prime}
in the coordinate system
O^{\prime}-x^{\prime}y^{\prime}
. Thus, physically speaking, the two observers at the two different coordinate systems should observe the same flow. From the viewpoint of the third observer simultaneously knowing the relationship between their coordinate systems and their results, the flow governed by NS equations (
1
) should keep the same spatial symmetry as its initial condition. This physical insight is indeed true, as mathematically proved in this paper. So, the theorem of spatial symmetry invariance is easy to understand from physical viewpoint.

Remark 2

Numerical simulations of the 2D Kolmogorov flow governed by the Navier-Stokes equation (
1
) (with an even number
n_{K}
) under the periodic boundary condition (
2
) subject to a smooth initial condition with the spatial symmetry of rotation (
3
) and/or translation (
4
) distinctly deviate exact solution, if they violate the theorem of spatial symmetry invariance proved in this paper. Thus, this theorem can be used to check the correctness and accuracy of numerical simulations of 2D turbulent Kolmogorov flow.

For example,
as reported in
[
22
,
9
]
, the numerical simulations of the 2D turbulent Kolmogorov flow given by CNS (in a finite but long enough time-interval) always remain the same spatial symmetry as the corresponding initial condition. This agrees well with the theorem of spatial symmetry invariance. This is mainly because, unlike traditional algorithms, numerical noises of CNS are rigorously negligible in a finite time-interval that is however long enough for statistics.
However, as mentioned in
[
22
,
9
]
, the corresponding DNS results remain the same spatial symmetry as the corresponding initial condition only in a short duration from the beginning but then quickly lose the spatial symmetry, clearly indicating that DNS results distinctly deviate the exact solution of the NS equations. In other words, these DNS results violate the theorem of spatial symmetry invariance. This clearly indicates that the DNS results are indeed quickly polluted by numerical noises badly.
The above conclusions should have general meanings for turbulent flows, as illustrated in
[
18
]
.

Remark 3

For the 2D Kolmogorov flow governed by the Navier-Stokes equation (
1
) (with an even number
n_{K}
) under the periodic boundary condition (
2
) subject to a smooth initial condition with the spatial symmetry of rotation (
3
) and/or translation (
4
), if initial condition has different spatial symmetries, their corresponding solutions should be distinctly different even in statistics.

For example, Liao and Qin
[
9
]
applied CNS to solve the 2D turbulent Kolmogorov flow (
1
) in the case of

Re=2000
and
n_{K}=16
, subject to the different initial conditions

\displaystyle\psi_{1}(x,y,0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\left[\cos(x+y)+\cos(x-y)\right],

(38)

\displaystyle\psi_{2}(x,y,0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\left[\cos(x+y)+\cos(x-y)\right]+\delta^{\prime}\;\sin(x+y),

(39)

where
\delta^{\prime}
is a constant. Note that
\psi_{1}(x,y,0)
contains the spatial symmetry of both rotation and translation, but
\psi_{2}(x,y,0)
contains the spatial symmetry of only translation for a large
\delta^{\prime}\sim O(1)
. As reported by Liao and Qin
[
9
]
, the spatiotemporal trajectories given by CNS using the initial condition
\psi_{1}(x,y,0)
remains the same spatial symmetry of rotation and translation as the initial condition
\psi_{1}(x,y,0)
in the whole time-interval
t\in[0,300]
. This agrees well with the theorem of spatial symmetry invariance proved in § 2. When
\delta^{\prime}=10^{{-20}}
in (
39
), the spatiotemporal trajectories given by CNS using the initial condition
\psi_{2}(x,y,0)
seems to remain the same spatial symmetry of rotation and translation as the initial condition
\psi_{1}(x,y,0)
at the beginning, mainly because the disturbance
10^{-20}\sin(x+y)
of
\psi_{2}(x,y,0)
is too small compared to
\psi_{1}(x,y,0)
, until it enlarges to a macro-level, due to the so-called “butterfly-effect” of the chaotic characteristics of turbulence

6
,
7
,
3
,
2
,
4
]
, at about
t\approx 35
when it finally destroys the original spatial symmetry of
\psi_{1}(x,y,0)
, thus the CNS result thereafter remains the same spatial symmetry of translation as its initial condition
\psi_{2}(x,y,0)
for large
\delta^{\prime}\sim O(1)
, as reported by Liao and Qin
[
9
]
. This CNS result also agrees well with the theorem of spatial symmetry invariance. Certainly, the two CNS results should have different statistics, since they have different spatial symmetries. So, the theorem of spatial symmetry invariance also supports the conclusion that the turbulence is a kind of ultra-chaos
[
11
,
20
]
, i.e. small disturbance could lead to distinct differences not only in spatotemporal trajectory but also in statistics of turbulence.
It should be emphasized again that, the corresponding DNS results of the same turbulent Kolmogorov flows quickly lose their spatial symmetry, since random numerical noises without spatial symmetry quickly enlarges to a macro-level. Therefore, the theorem of spatial symmetry invariance rigorously confirms that the DNS results of the 2D turbulent Kolmogorov flow (
1
) are indeed quickly polluted by numerical noises badly, as illustrated in
[
22
]
. This is also the reason why the so-called “noise-expansion cascade” revealed in

]
by means of CNS cannot be discovered by means of DNS.

For a large enough Reynolds number such as
Re=2000
considered in
[
9
]
, the 2D Kolmogorov flow (
1
) is turbulent. It is widely accepted by fluid community that turbulence is chaotic
[
6
,
7
,
3
,
2
,
4
]
and thus, due to the famous butterfly-effect of chaotic system, the small disturbance
\delta^{\prime}\sin(x+y)
of the initial condition
\psi_{2}(x,y,t)
defined by (
39
) should be quickly enlarged to the same order of magnitude as the first initial condition
\psi_{1}(x,y,t)
described by (
38
). However,
\psi_{1}(x,y,t)
has a different spatial symmetry from that of
\psi_{2}(x,y,t)
when
\delta^{\prime}\sim O(1)
, therefore, according to the theorem of spatial symmetry invariance, the turbulent Kolmogorov flow
\psi_{1}(x,y,t)
has different spatial symmetry from that of
\psi_{2}(x,y,t)
even for arbitrarily small
\delta^{\prime}
, say,
\delta^{\prime}\to 0
. Thus, the theorem of spatial symmetry invariance plus the butterfly-effect of chaos supports the following conjecture:

Conjecture A

The two-dimensional turbulent Kolmogorov flow governed by the NS equation (
1
) (with an even number
n_{K}
) under the periodic boundary condition (
2
) subject to a smooth initial condition with the spatial symmetry of rotation (
3
) and/or translation (
4
) admit distinct smooth global solutions for all
t>0
from almost the same initial conditions
\psi_{1}(x,y,0)
and
\psi_{2}(x,y,0)
with
\big|\psi_{1}(x,y,0)-\psi_{2}(x,y,0)\big|\leq\delta
for arbitrarily small
\delta
(or as
\delta\to 0
).

As reported by Qin and Liao
[
19
]
, the CNS results of the three-dimensional (3D) Kolmogorov flow also remain the same spatial symmetry as its initial condition in a finite but long enough interval of time
t\in[0,500]
. This highly suggests that Conjecture A might have general meanings. All of these support
the following conjecture mentioned by Liao in
[
12
]
:

Conjecture B

The Navier-Stokes equations admit distinct smooth global solutions
{\bf u}_{1}({\bf r},t)
and
{\bf u}_{2}({\bf r},t)
for all
t>0
from almost the same initial conditions
{\bf u}_{1}({\bf r},0)
and
{\bf u}_{2}({\bf r},0)
with
\big|{\bf u}_{1}({\bf r},0)-{\bf u}_{2}({\bf r},0)\big|\leq\delta
for arbitrarily small
\delta
(or as
\delta\to 0
).

Obviously, if the flow is laminar, all small disturbances in the initial condition should not increase to a macro-level, say, remain small forever. Thus, the above two conjectures should hold true only for turbulent flows.

What happens when the initial condition has no spatial symmetry? Qin and Liao
[
18
]
applied both of DNS and CNS to solve the 2D Rayleigh-Bénard turbulent flow using thermal fluctuation as initial condition, which is ransom in space and thus has no spatial symmetry: it was found
[
18
]
that the flow given by CNS remains a kind of vortical flow in the whole duration
t\in[0,500]
, but the flow given by DNS is first a kind of vortical flow, until at
t\approx 188
when it transfers into a kind of zonal flow thereafter in
t\in[188,500]
. Currently, it was found that the final flow type (given by the DNS) of the 2D Rayleigh-Bénard turbulent flow using random thermal fluctuation as initial condition are very sensitive to the time-step for some Reynolds numbers
[
21
]
: the two flow types frequently alternate as the time-step is reduced to a very small value, suggesting that the time-step corresponding to each turbulent flow type should be densely distributed. All of these examples clearly illustrate that numerical noises indeed might have huge influences on global characteristics of turbulence, although sometimes. It is an open question whether or not random initial condition without spatial symmetry
must
be used for NS turbulence.

Note that, unlike the
exact
solution mentioned in the theorem of spatial symmetry invariance proved in § 2,
numerical
results given by CNS are reliable only in a finite but long enough interval of time
t\in[0,T_{c}]
, where
T_{c}
is called the “critical predictable time”. As pointed out by Liao in his book
[
14
]
,

T_{c}\sim-\kappa\ln{\cal E}_{0}

(40)

for chaotic system and turbulence, where
{\cal E}_{0}>0
denotes the level of background numerical noises, and
\kappa>0
is the Lyapunov exponent of chaotic system. Obviously, the smaller the background numerical noise
{\cal E}_{0}
, the larger the so-called “critical predictable time”
T_{c}
. This is the reason why CNS can give reliable simulations of chaotic systems and turbulent flows in a much larger time-interval
t\in[0,T_{c}]
than DNS, because
{\cal E}_{0}
of CNS is much smaller than that of DNS.
From this viewpoint, we can regard DNS as a special case of CNS, because DNS often uses single or double precision with the 4th-order Runge-Kutta’s method in time dimension, corresponding to a rather small “critical predictable time”
T_{c}
that is too short for statistic computation, although DNS has not the concept of “critical predictable time”.

It should be emphasized that, although CNS results are numerical, they can provide us enlightenments to approach some mathematical truths such as the theorem of spatial symmetry invariance proved in § 2 and the conjectures mentioned above and in
[
12
]
. Hopefully, CNS
[
13
,
14
,
8
,
11
,
10
,
18
,
22
,
9
,
19
,
20
,
12
]
could be used as a powerful tool to study turbulence.

Finally, the theorem of spatial symmetry invariance for 2D Kolmogorov flow can be generalized to the three-dimensional (3D) incompressible Navier-Stokes equations
[
1
]
, as proved below in Appendix in a similar way.

Appendix

Spatial symmetry invariance of solution of the Navier-Stokes equations

Let us consider the dimensionless three-dimensional (3D) incompressible Navier-Stokes equations
[
1
]
:

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot{\bf u}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(A.1)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\bf u}}{\partial t}+\left({\bf u}\cdot\nabla\right){\bf u}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\nabla p+\frac{1}{Re}\Delta{\bf u}+{\bf f},

(A.2)

under periodic boundary condition in a square domain
{\bf x}=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in[0,2\pi]^{3}
,
where
t\in[0,+\infty)
denotes the time,
{\bf u}=(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})
is the velocity of fluid,
p
is the pressure,
{\bf f}({\bf x})
is an steady-state external force,
Re
is the Reynolds number,
\nabla
is the Hamilton operator, and
\Delta=\nabla\cdot\nabla
is the Laplace operator, respectively. Let

denote its corresponding coordinate system.

Define

{\bf u}^{(m)}({\bf x},t)=\frac{1}{m!}\frac{\partial{\bf u}({\bf x},t)}{\partial t^{m}},\;\;\;p^{(m)}({\bf x},t)=\frac{1}{m!}\frac{\partial p({\bf x},t)}{\partial t^{m}},\;\;\;m\geq 0,

(A.3)

with

{\bf u}^{(0)}({\bf x},t)={\bf u}({\bf x},t),\;\;\;p^{(0)}({\bf x},t)=p({\bf x},t),

(A.4)

for integer
m\geq 0
.

From (
A.2
), it holds

\displaystyle\frac{\partial(\nabla\cdot{\bf u})}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\left[\left({\bf u}\cdot\nabla\right){\bf u}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\Delta p+\frac{1}{Re}\Delta\left(\nabla\cdot{\bf u}\right)+\nabla\cdot{\bf f},

(A.5)

which gives using the continuation equation (
A.1
) that

\Delta p=\nabla\cdot{\bf f}-\nabla\cdot\left[\left({\bf u}\cdot\nabla\right){\bf u}\right].

(A.6)

Differentiating (

)
m

, we have

(m+1){\bf u}^{(m+1)}+\sum_{i=0}^{m}\left({\bf u}^{(i)}\cdot\nabla\right){\bf u}^{(m-i)}=-\nabla p^{(m)}+\frac{1}{Re}\Delta{\bf u}^{(m)},\;\;

(A.7)

\Delta p^{(m)}=-\sum_{i=0}^{m}\nabla\cdot\left[\left({\bf u}^{(i)}\cdot\nabla\right){\bf u}^{(m-i)}\right],

(A.8)

for
m\geq 1
and
t\geq 0

Let
{\bf u}^{\prime}({\bf x}^{\prime},t)
,
p^{\prime}({\bf x}^{\prime},t)

{\bf x}^{\prime}\in[0,2\pi]^{3}

A.2
) and (
A.6
)-(

t\geq 0
, thus

\displaystyle{\bf u}^{\prime(1)}+\left({\bf u}^{\prime}\cdot\nabla^{\prime}\right){\bf u}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\nabla^{\prime}p^{\prime}+\frac{1}{Re}\Delta^{\prime}{\bf u}^{\prime}+{\bf f}^{\prime},

(A.9)

\displaystyle(m+1){\bf u}^{\prime(m+1)}+\sum_{i=0}^{m}\left({\bf u}^{\prime(i)}\cdot\nabla^{\prime}\right){\bf u^{\prime(m-i)}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\nabla^{\prime}p^{\prime(m)}+\frac{1}{Re}\Delta^{\prime}{\bf u}^{\prime(m)},\;\;m\geq 1,\hskip 28.45274pt

(A.10)

and

\displaystyle\Delta^{\prime}p^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla^{\prime}\cdot{\bf f}^{\prime}-\nabla^{\prime}\cdot\left[\left({\bf u^{\prime}}\cdot\nabla^{\prime}\right){\bf u^{\prime}}\right],

(A.11)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=0}^{m}\nabla^{\prime}\cdot\left[\left({\bf u}^{\prime(i)}\cdot\nabla^{\prime}\right){\bf u}^{\prime(m-i)}\right],\;\;m\geq 1.

In general case,
{\bf u}^{\prime}({\bf x}^{\prime},t)\neq{\bf u}({\bf x},t)

Lemma A

Under the coordinate translation
x^{\prime}_{i}=x_{i}+\pi
and/or the coordinate rotation
x^{\prime}_{i}=-x_{i}
, it holds

\nabla^{\prime}=\nabla,\;\;\Delta^{\prime}=\Delta.

(A.13)

Proof: Let
{\bf e}_{i},{\bf e}^{\prime}_{i}
denote the axis unit vector for
x_{i},x^{\prime}_{i}
, respectively, where
i=1,2,3
. For the coordinate translation
x^{\prime}_{i}=x_{i}+\pi

{\bf e}_{i}={\bf e}^{\prime}_{i},\;\;\;\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\prime}_{i}}\frac{\partial x^{\prime}_{i}}{\partial x_{i}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\prime}_{i}},\;\;\;i=1,2,3,

(A.14)

thus

\nabla^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}{\bf e}^{\prime}_{i}\;\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\prime}_{i}}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}{\bf e}_{i}\;\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}=\nabla.

(A.15)

Similarly, for the coordinate rotation
x^{\prime}_{i}=-x_{i}
, where
i=1,2,3
, it holds

{\bf e}_{i}=-{\bf e}^{\prime}_{i},\;\;\;\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\prime}_{i}}\frac{\partial x^{\prime}_{i}}{\partial x_{i}}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\prime}_{i}},

(A.16)

thus,

\nabla^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}{\bf e}^{\prime}_{i}\;\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\prime}_{i}}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\left(-{\bf e}_{i}\right)\;\left(-\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{3}{\bf e}_{i}\;\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}=\nabla.

(A.17)

So, under
x^{\prime}_{i}=x_{i}+\pi
and/or
x^{\prime}_{i}=-x_{i}

\Delta^{\prime}=\nabla^{\prime}\cdot\nabla^{\prime}=\nabla\cdot\nabla=\Delta.

Theorem of Spatial Symmetry Invariance of 3D NS equations

The solution
\bf u
of the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations (
A.1
) and (
A.2
) under periodic boundary condition has the same spatial symmetry
{\bf u}^{\prime}({\bf x}^{\prime},t)

={\bf u}({\bf x},t)
for all
t>0
as its smooth initial condition, where
x^{\prime}_{i}=x_{i}+\pi
for translation and/or
x^{\prime}_{i}=-x_{i}

(i=1,2,3)
for rotation, if there exist the spatial symmetry
{\bf u}^{\prime}({\bf x}^{\prime},0)={\bf u}({\bf x},0)
for initial condition and
{\bf f}^{\prime}({\bf x}^{\prime})={\bf f}({\bf x})
for external force, and besides the temporal Taylor series of
{\bf u}({\bf x},t)
exists and has a non-zero radius of convergence for
t\geq 0
.

Proof. Since there exists the spatial symmetry
{\bf u}^{\prime}({\bf x}^{\prime},0)={\bf u}({\bf x},0)
and
{\bf f}^{\prime}({\bf x}^{\prime})={\bf f}({\bf x})
, where
x^{\prime}_{i}=x_{i}+\pi
and/or
x^{\prime}_{i}=-x_{i}

(i=1,2,3
), we have according to Lemma C (setting
t_{0}=0
) the spatial symmetry

{\bf u}^{\prime}({\bf x}^{\prime},t)={\bf u}({\bf x},t),\hskip 28.45274ptt\in[0,\delta_{0}],

where
\delta_{0}<\rho_{0}
, and
\rho_{0}>0
is the non-zero radius of convergence of its Taylor series at
t=0
.

Similarly, since there exists the spatial symmetry
{\bf u}^{\prime}({\bf x}^{\prime},\delta_{0})={\bf u}({\bf x},\delta_{0})
at
t=\delta_{0}
and
{\bf f}^{\prime}({\bf x}^{\prime})={\bf f}({\bf x})
, we have according to Lemma C (setting
t_{0}=\delta_{0}
) the spatial symmetry

{\bf u}^{\prime}({\bf x}^{\prime},t)={\bf u}({\bf x},t),\hskip 28.45274ptt\in[\delta_{0},\delta_{1}],

where
\delta_{1}<\rho_{1}
, and
\rho_{1}>0
is the non-zero radius of convergence of its Taylor series at
t=\delta_{0}
.

Note that

[0,\delta_{0}]\cup[\delta_{0},\delta_{1}]=[0,\delta_{1}],

thus in this way there exists the spatial symmetry

{\bf u}^{\prime}({\bf x}^{\prime},t)={\bf u}({\bf x},t),\hskip 28.45274ptt\in[0,\delta_{1}],

in a larger interval of time. The same process can keep going and the time interval becomes larger and larger, since the temporal Taylor series of
{\bf u}({\bf x},t)
always exists with a non-zero radius of convergence.
Thus, it holds the spatial symmetry

{\bf u}^{\prime}({\bf x}^{\prime},t)={\bf u}({\bf x},t),\hskip 28.45274ptt\in[0,+\infty).

This ends the proof of the Theorem of Spatial Symmetry Invariance.

Remark A

The Theorem of Spatial Symmetry Invariance for Navier-Stokes equations can be used as a criterion to verify the correctness and accuracy of numerical simulation of Navier-Stokes equations in the corresponding conditions: numerical simulations of Navier-Stokes equations must depart from exact solution greatly if they distinctly violate this theorem.

Remark B

Let
O
denote one observer in the coordinate system
O-x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}
, and
O^{\prime}
denote another observer in the coordinate system
O^{\prime}-x^{\prime}_{1}x^{\prime}_{2}x^{\prime}_{3}
, respectively, where
x^{\prime}_{i}=x^{\prime}_{i}+\pi
and/or
x^{\prime}_{i}=-x^{\prime}_{i}
. When there exist the spatial symmetry
{\bf f}^{\prime}({\bf x}^{\prime})={\bf f}({\bf x})
for external force and
{\bf u}^{\prime}({\bf x}^{\prime},0)={\bf u}({\bf x},0)
for initial condition, the governing equations and the initial condition are the
same
for both of the observer
O
in
O-x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}
and the observer
O^{\prime}
in the coordinate system
O^{\prime}-x^{\prime}_{1}x^{\prime}_{2}x^{\prime}_{3}
. Physically speaking, for the two observers at the two different coordinate systems, they should observe the same flow if the initial condition and the external force are the same, say,
{\bf u}^{\prime}({\bf x}^{\prime},t)={\bf u}({\bf x},t)
, for
t\in[0,+\infty)
. From the viewpoint of the third observer knowing their results simultaneously, the flow governed by NS equations should have a kind of spatial symmetry. This physical insight is indeed true as mathematically proved above in this paper. So, the Theorem of Spatial Symmetry Invariance is easy to understand from physical viewpoint.
