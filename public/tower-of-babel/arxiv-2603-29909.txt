Implication semilattice of 990 quasigroup equational laws
Bruno Le Floch

Abstract
. In his quest to disprove a claim by Peirce that all lattices are distributive, Ernst Schröder considered 135 years ago a list of 990 equational laws on quasigroups, analogous to associativity, such as ( x / / y ) ∗ z = ( y / / x ) \ \ z (x/\!/y)*z=(y/\!/x)\backslash\!\backslash z . A quasigroup is a non-associative analogue of groups, specifically a set equipped with multiplication and right/left conjugate-division operations that are compatible. Each equation of interest identifies two three-variable expressions built from these operations. I determine all 114 114 equivalence classes of their conjunctions, and all implications between them. This includes as a small corner the five-element non-distributive lattice identified by Schröder.

1.3.
Main result

The viewpoint of prescribing a lattice as some given set equipped with suitable operations given by explicit tables was only developed later on by Dedekind. Schröder’s five-element lattice is quite trivial to describe in retrospect, and does not require any reference to quasigroups.
Even though the original lattice motivation is gone, the question of exploring varieties of quasigroups remains, and I completed the unfinished quest.

There are
114

990

•

1

of all quasigroups;

•

•

Some of the varieties are easily described, such as the aforementioned varieties of groups or commutative quasigroups, and some were identified as being interesting in the context of the Equational Theories Project.
It would be interesting to describe all of these varieties in more conventional mathematical terms.
The only variety that requires four equations may be of particular interest; it can be described (for instance) by

\displaystyle x*(y/\!/z)

\displaystyle=y*(x/\!/z),\qquad

2.1.
Quasigroup equations

A quasigroup is a set
Q
equipped with three binary operations
*,/\!/,\backslash\!\backslash\colon Q\times Q\to Q
such that for all
x,y\in Q
one has

(5)

\displaystyle(y/\!/x)*y

\displaystyle=x,\qquad

\displaystyle y/\!/(x*y)

\displaystyle=x,

\displaystyle x*(y\backslash\!\backslash x)

\displaystyle=y,\qquad

\displaystyle(x*y)\backslash\!\backslash x

\displaystyle=y,

\displaystyle x\backslash\!\backslash(y/\!/x)

\displaystyle=y,\qquad

\displaystyle(x\backslash\!\backslash y)/\!/x

\displaystyle=y.

Alternatively, the quasigroup
Q
can be described as being equipped with a single binary operation
*
whose right and left multiplications are bijective. Inverting these maps defines right and left division operations
/
and
\backslash
characterized by
(x/y)*y=x
and
y*(y\backslash x)=x
. It is more convenient to work with their conjugates
x/\!/y=y/x
and
x\backslash\!\backslash y=y\backslash x
. Indeed, the resulting definition of quasigroups (
5
) features a symmetry under which one cyclically permutes the operations
(*,/\!/,\backslash\!\backslash)
.

2.1.1.
Numbering and counting Schröder’s laws

Since the original work did not appear to specify an ordering of the equations, I decided arbitrarily on a numbering. Namely, as can be seen in the data file
quasigroup-equations.txt
, equations are ordered

•

first by how the two binary operations on the left and right are associated, namely
x?(y?z)=\alpha?(\beta?\gamma)
before
x?(y?z)=(\alpha?\beta)?\gamma
before
(x?y)?z=(\alpha?\beta)?\gamma
;

•

then by the choice of the four operations
?
in (
1
) in lexicographical order, namely
({*},{*},{*},{*})
, then
({*},{*},{*},{/\!/})
, then
({*},{*},{*},{\backslash\!\backslash})
, then
({*},{*},{/\!/},{*})
, etc.;

•

then by the choice of variables.

Following Schröder, and following what we did in the Equational Theories Project
[
1
]
, all tautological equations are omitted, as are any equation that can be obtained from earlier ones by relabelling variables or swapping left and right hand side. This explains the absence of any equation associated as
(x?y)?z=\alpha?(\beta?\gamma)
.

The left-hand side always uses the variables
x,y,z
in this order, while they can appear in any of the six orders on the right-hand side, unless both sides involve the same operations associated in the same way, in which case one may not take
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
to be
(x,y,z)
in the same order (tautology) nor
(z,x,y)
because this is equivalent to the choice
(y,z,x)
.

This gives
6\times 9\times 9
laws associated as
x,(y,z)=(?,?),?
,

6\times(9\times 8/2)
of the form
x,(y,z)=?,(?,?)
with different pairs of operations on the two sides and
4\times 9
of that form with the same pair of operations on both sides, and then the same number
6\times(9\times 8/2)+4\times 9
with the dual association
(x,y),z=(?,?),?
, for a total number of equations

(6)

6\times 9\times 9+2\times\Bigl(6\times(9\times 8/2)+4\times 9\Bigr)=990.

2.2.
The Equational Theories Project

The Equational Theories Project
[
1
]
considered magmas, which are sets equipped with one binary operation, and the
4694
simplest equational laws, that is, all laws that involve at most
4
operations.
The collaborative research project determined all implications between these laws.
3
3

3

In the restricted context of finite magmas, one implication remains undetermined: whether in all finite magma such that
\forall x,\ \forall y,\ x=y\diamond(x\diamond((y\diamond x)\diamond y))
one necessarily has
\forall x,\ x=((x\diamond x)\diamond x)\diamond x
.

Automated theorem provers were particularly effective tools to obtain positive implications: in all cases where an equation
E
implies another equation
E^{\prime}
, a short proof could be found automatically.
For counterexamples, the situation was somewhat trickier: small countermodels could be obtained directly by automated tools, but larger finite countermodels could only be found by imposing well-chosen assumptions to restrict the search. Even worse, some implications turned out to hold for all finite magmas but not for infinite magmas. To disprove such implications it was necessary to build infinite countermodels.
Thus, overall, the Equational Theories Project relied on a collection of techniques:

•

Automated theorem provers and SAT solvers such as Vampire, Prover9, Mace4.

•

A Lean formalization of every implication and every countermodel, to ensure nothing was missed. This was absolutely crucial due to the large number of collaborators and the tens of millions of implications to be considered.

•

Mathematical developments, to come up with new constructions of magmas based on magma cohomology, greedy constructions, and rewriting theory.

(As well as tools to organize the work, visualize the status of the project, explore the implication graph, and much more.)

2.3.
Methods

The main goal of this project is to determine all logically-closed subsets
A
of equations among Schröder’s
990
equations. This relied on two automated tools developed by McCune: the automated theorem prover (ATP) Prover9, which given some equations and a goal seeks a proof thereof, and the model builder Mace4, which given the same setup seeks a finite countermodel, namely (in our case) a quasigroup that does not obey the target equation.
This was combined with very simple Python scripts.

For any given implication of interest between a set
A
of equations and a target
E
, a useful strategy was as follows. (Here we denote by
\mathfrak{Q}
the quasigroup axioms, see
subsection 2.1
.)
Seek countermodels to
\mathfrak{Q}\land A\implies E
using Mace4 with a very small bound in size.
Check if the implication can be proven using Prover9, with a one-second timeout.
Finally make a longer attempt at counterexamples using Mace4.
4
4

4

Some fiddling with Mace4 parameters such as
selection_measure
was needed to get a fast enough answer.

This always succeeds for the implications of interest, hence serves as an
oracle
for the truth of implications.
This is a crucial simplification compared to the Equational Theories Project: all of the non-implications admitted a
finite counterexample
, which could be found automatically.

The strategy to find all inequivalent conjunctions of equations is thus as follows.

•

For each pair of equation
E,E^{\prime}
(among the
990
), determine whether
\mathfrak{Q}\land E\implies E^{\prime}
.

•

Deduce the
47
lowest-numbered representatives of equivalence classes of equations, which are listed in
subsection 3.1
. The consequences of a given equation form a logically closed subset.

•

For each logically closed subset
A
found so far, in turn, and each of the
47
representatives
E
that is not in
A
, consider
A\cup\{E\}
. Seek all implications of the form
\mathfrak{Q}\land A\land E\implies E^{\prime}
for
E^{\prime}
among the
47
representatives. Many of these searches are skipped thanks to transitivity of implication. If the resulting set of consequences is new, add it to the list of logically closed subsets to consider.

One should remember as well that the empty set is logically closed!
