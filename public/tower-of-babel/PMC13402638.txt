Mucosal immunity and vaccine development
Authors: Weiqi Hong, Aqu Alu, Zhihao Zhang, Yu Zhang, Xuemei He, Li Xu, Min Li, Wu He, Junzhi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The mucosal system, which includes the respiratory, gastrointestinal, and urogenital tracts, serves as a primary entry point for pathogens, with a unique immune microenvironment and specialized defense mechanisms. In recent years, especially following the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, there has been increasing recognition of the importance of mucosal immunity, motivated by an enhanced comprehension of its fundamental mechanisms. Currently, strategies based on mucosal delivery systems to administer antigens and induce strong mucosal protective immunity have become a key focus in the development of mucosal vaccines. Compared with conventional intramuscular delivery, mucosal vaccination can simultaneously elicit a robust local mucosal response, effectively block pathogen entry into the local mucosa, and generate systemic immune responses to prevent symptomatic infections and severe disease. In addition, mucosal delivery offers advantages such as ease of administration and low invasiveness, making it a more widely acceptable approach to vaccination. In the present study, we conducted a systematic review of the mechanisms of mucosal immunity, the technological platforms for mucosal vaccines, and proposed a perspective on the challenges and future directions for the development of next-generation mucosal vaccines, with the goal of enhancing public knowledge and awareness regarding mucosal immunity and its possible effects on global health.

Introduction

Mucosal immunity represents the defensive immune system operating at the body’s mucosal surfaces, such as those of the respiratory, gastrointestinal, and urogenital tracts. It plays a critical role in protecting against pathogen invasion through a coordinated network of innate immune components, immune cells such as tissue-resident memory T (TRM) cells, and, predominantly, secretory immunoglobulin A (sIgA) antibodies. These elements not only preserve mucosal barrier integrity but also trigger both local and systemic immune responses, thereby effectively limiting infections.1–4 The COVID-19 health crisis has brought renewed attention to mucosal immunity, especially within the respiratory tract, highlighting its pivotal role in frontline defense and viral transmission control, and underscoring the critical requirement for vaccines capable of inducing strong mucosal immune responses.5,6

Mucosal vaccines, typically administered via oral or intranasal routes, are designed to leverage these unique immune mechanisms, offering potential advantages in blocking pathogen transmission and improving patient compliance. In addition to respiratory viruses, mucosal immunity is increasingly recognized as relevant in gastrointestinal and urogenital infections, as well as in non-infectious conditions such as mucosal-associated cancers and chronic inflammatory diseases.7

Recent advances in vaccine technologies have facilitated the development of experimental mucosal vaccines, with many demonstrating promising efficacy in preclinical models. Nonetheless, key challenges persist, including efficient antigen delivery, vaccine stability, and the durability of mucosal immune responses. To date, only nine mucosal vaccines have been approved for human use by the FDA, with eight administered orally and just one administered intranasally, all of which rely primarily on traditional platforms such as attenuated or inactivated pathogens.8,9 A deeper understanding of mucosal immune mechanisms and the roles of associated immune cells is essential to inspire rational vaccine design.

This article outlines the core principles of mucosal immunity, reviews current mucosal vaccine platforms, and discusses the future potential of this field in driving next-generation vaccine development.

Mechanism of mucosal immunity

Innate mucosal immunity

The mucosa in the respiratory, digestive, or genitourinary tract is the first-line defense of host immunity against external stimuli, including invasive pathogens, antigens, and allergens.10 A detailed understanding of mucosal immunity is essential for designing mucosal vaccines. Mucosal immunity consists of coordinated innate and adaptive immune responses organized within an integrated system of tissues, cells, and molecular mediators (Fig. 1). The innate immune system is composed of physical, chemical, and biological components at the extraepithelial, epithelial, and subepithelial tissues. The physical factor is a protective barrier formed by the tight junctions of epithelia coated with secreted mucus, intestinal peristalsis, and ciliary beating on the surface of respiratory epithelial cells.11 Chemical components such as defensin peptides, enzymes, and gastric acid promote pathogen and antigen removal via degradation, opsonization, or cytokine-mediated immune responses.10,12,13 When these two barriers were broken through, innate immune cells such as macrophages and natural killer cells residing in subepithelial tissues would engulf and kill the invading pathogens and microbial molecules through phagocytosis or activation of signaling pathways by recognizing pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) with pathogen recognition receptors (PRRs).14 Therefore, the design of ideal vaccine delivery platforms must consider the harsh environment in the lumen and the impenetrable barrier in the lamina propria.Fig. 1Immune responses induced by mucosal vaccines. Mucosa-associated lymphoid tissues (MALT)

Antigen-specific adaptive immunity initiates at inductive sites, mostly in mucosa-associated lymphoid tissues (MALT). MALT is the largest lymphoid system in the body and is composed of gut- (GALT), nasopharynx- (NALT), and bronchus-associated lymphoid (BALT) tissues on the basis of their anatomic location.10 MALT is characterized by a high degree of immune compartmentalization, enabling localized immune regulation that is not fully overlapping with systemic immunity. It contains essential immune cells to mediate antigen-specific immune responses, primarily B and T lymphocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells (DCs).8 Although the cellular organization of the MALT surface varies slightly from site to site, they all share the same follicular-associated epithelium where professional antigen-sampling microfold (M) cells reside.3 Structural differences give rise to a variety of mechanisms that transport antigens from the lumen to the highly organized subepithelial lymphoid tissue. In stratified and pseudostratified epithelia of the oral cavity, pharynx, esophagus, urethra, and part of the respiratory and vaginal tracts, mucosal antigen-presenting cells (APCs) can directly sample and traffic antigens to the lymphoid tissues of draining lymph nodes. In the simple epithelium of the respiratory tract and intestines (e.g., Peyer’s patches), where tight junctions seal the spaces between adjacent cells, M cells are responsible for the capture and delivery of antigens to subepithelial DCs, macrophages, or even B cells.15,16 APCs (mainly DCs) then process the absorbed antigens into peptides before presenting them to surrounding naïve T cells. On the one hand, antigen-stimulated CD4+ helper T cells produce cytokines such as transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) and interleukin-5 (IL-5), the former activating B cells and further differentiating into antigen-specific IgA+ B cells, and the latter increasing antibody production.17–19 APCs can also cross-present antigens to promote the activation and differentiation of antigen-specific CD8+ T cells.5 These antigen-specific T and B cells then egress from the lymphoid tissues and reach the effector sites via the thoracic duct and bloodstream.5 Subsequently, these cells undergo terminal differentiation into effector (e.g., antibody-producing plasma cells and cytotoxic CD8+ T cells) and memory cells to establish long-term immunity.20,21 Antigen-carrying DCs can also migrate to relevant lymph nodes (e.g., cervical lymph nodes for intranasal vaccines) to stimulate B and T cells in the lymph nodes.17

IgA and other mucosal antibodies

At effector sites such as the lamina propria, plasma cells produce large amounts of antigen-specific IgA antibodies that then dimerize or multimerize by expressing the J chain.10 Dimeric or polymeric IgA antibodies are transported to the lumen as sIgA by binding to the polymeric Ig receptor (pIgR) at the basolateral surface of epithelial cells.22 B cells can produce IgA antibodies via T-cell-independent (TI) or T-cell-dependent (TD) pathways, which are highly complementary. At mucosal sites, antigen-loaded DCs can interact with T follicular helper (TFH) cells, which then migrate to the germinal center and interact with B cells to initiate IgM-to-IgA class switch recombination in the presence of CD40 ligand (CD40L) and TGF-β signaling.23 While the canonical IgM-to-IgA class switch predominates, antigen-specific IgG⁺ B cells are also capable of switching to IgA⁺ B cells at mucosal sites, especially following mucosal re-encounter with the same antigen.24,25 In Peyer’s patches, M cells can directly deliver antigens to B cells in the subepithelial dome and activate TD IgA generation.16 Moreover, signals derived from pathogens, DCs, stromal and epithelial cells, including microbial toll-like receptor (TLR) ligands and B-cell activating factor (BAFF), can activate TI production of IgA from plasma cells.23 Memory B cells exhibit an elevated response upon antigen re-exposure by differentiating into plasma cells or entering germinal centers (GCs).26 Recent studies suggested the presence of tissue-resident memory B (BRM) cells, which stably reside in mucosal tissues such as the upper airway and the digestive tract.27,28 Compared with their circulating counterparts, antigen-specific BRM cells can differentiate in situ and provide stronger defensive immunity against secondary infection,29,30 whereas antigen-nonspecific BRM cells remain bystander memory cells but preserve exogenous antigens as immune complexes to fight against possible future infections.29,31

In humans, IgA is present mainly as a monomer in serum and as a polymer in secretions. In contrast, in mice, IgA is present as a polymer in both the serum and secretions.32–35 Dimeric IgA is far more effective in protecting against SARS-CoV-2 infection in COVID-19 patients than IgA monomers in the serum.36 sIgA is the primary antibody isotype in the upper respiratory tract and intestinal tract. sIgA functions mainly by neutralizing or blocking the invasion and activity of various pathogens through their specific antigen binding sites, thus inhibiting virulence factors or intercepting their interactions with the mucosa (immune exclusion).36,37 The activation of the complement system may also be involved.32 Compared with serum IgG and IgA antibodies from the same individuals, sIgA purified from the nasal mucosa of healthy vaccinated volunteers or convalescent adults displayed superior, up to 100-fold greater, neutralizing capacity against Omicron XBB.38,39 In the intestinal tract, sIgA plays an essential role in shaping the composition and activity of the gut microbiota and regulating host-microbiota interactions.40 Notably, IgG is a critical component of the protective immune responses in the lower respiratory tract and female genital tract, with more abundant or comparable levels than sIgA levels.5 Mucosal IgG is monomeric and may perform homeostatic and protective functions by cooperating with sIgA-including secreted antibodies.23 Moreover, sIgA has natural resistance to proteolysis, which ensures its stability in protease-rich mucosal secretions.4 Therefore, the induction of mucosal IgA antibodies is an essential evaluation criterion for mucosal vaccines. Compared with preclinical studies, evaluating mucosal immunity in humans in detail is more challenging. Some facilitating and noninvasive methods include measuring antibodies in mucosal secretions, such as saliva, nasal washes, and fecal samples, which have been evaluated in clinical trials.41–43

Tissue-resident memory T (TRM) cells

The antigen-primed B and T cells egress the MALT and move to the effector sites directed by their “homing receptors” and chemokines and then differentiate into effector and memory cells.10 Among them, the induction of TRM cells have been confirmed at different mucosal sites and provide enhanced local immune resistance to infection, making the induction of TRM cells another decisive factor in evaluating mucosal vaccine efficacy.44–46 Although some TRM cells can recirculate slowly, most TRM cells are stably located in peripheral tissues or organs of origin for a long period of time.47 They can be identified by the overexpression of adhesion molecules including CD69, CD103, CD49a, CXCR3 and CXCR6.48,49 When restimulated by antigens, TRM cells can be rapidly activated and exhibit effector activities, hence offering a frontline protection against pathogens at entry sites.17,48 CD4+ helper TRM cells support the establishment of BRM and CD8+ TRM cell immunity, allowing the induction of long-lived local humoral and CD8+ T-cell memory responses.50,51 However, we should note that CD8+ TRM cells in the lung are short-lived, hindering their activity against subsequent infections.52 It is speculated that lung TRM cells can recirculate to the mediastinal lymph nodes via a special route named “retrograde migration”.53 Several strategies have displayed efficacy in prolonging the existence of lung TRM cells, such as the application of systemically vaccinated boosters to elevate the number of systemic effector memory T cells or the use of viral-vectored vaccines to prolong the duration of antigen maintenance in the lung.52,54 Therefore, it is essential to consider the divergence of different populations of TRM cells at different locations when designing mucosal vaccines.

Trained immunity

Trained immunity refers to innate immune memory, which is associated with the establishment of long-lasting immune memory by the innate immune system, causing increased defense against reinfection with the same or unrelated pathogens55 (Fig. 1). Pathogen infection (e.g., Candida albicans56), β-glucan57 and certain vaccines (e.g., Bacillus Calmette–Guérin (BCG), Measles, Mumps and Rubella (MMR), and oral poliovirus vaccine (OPV))58 can all induce trained immunity. In contrast to adaptive immune memory, trained immunity is antigen-independent and relatively short-lived, ranging from months to years.59 The underlying mechanisms of trained immunity are closely related to the transcriptional, epigenetic, and metabolic reprogramming of innate immune cells.60 Trained immunity can be divided into central trained immunity (occurring in hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells) and peripheral trained immunity (occurring in myeloid cells, including neutrophils, monocytes, and macrophages).61 Recently, evidence has indicated that nonimmune cells may induce trained immunity as well, including fibroblasts, stromal cells, and epithelial cells.62 BCG-induced trained immunity has been most well studied, which is dependent on the response of macrophages to produce proinflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interferon-γ (IFN-γ), IL-1β, and IL-6.63–65 Therefore, BCG-induced trained immunity has been reported to protect against multiple viral infections, including severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2).58,66 Significant progress has been made in understanding trained mucosal immunity over recent years. Yao et al. reported that infection with a respiratory virus induced long-lasting memory alveolar macrophages (AMs), which presented elevated glycolytic metabolism and high expression of MHC II. These AMs rapidly protect against a second challenge with bacterial infection by inducing chemokines and neutrophilia.67 Similarly, intranasal immunization with a virus-vectored COVID-19 vaccine activated local trained immunity in the respiratory tract, characterized by chromatin reprogramming of AMs and high expression of MHC II and antiviral innate immune response genes (Tlr3 and Tlr7), leading to a faster and stronger response upon pathogen re-exposure.68 Differences in anatomical location and tissue structure lead to variations in trained immunity between mucosal and nonmucosal tissues. Mucosal tissues function as a barrier separating the inner and outer environments and are thus constantly exposed to external stimuli of trained immunity. Moreover, mucosal-trained immunity is mediated primarily by local innate immune cells with tissue-resident properties.62,69 Therefore, mucosal vaccines harnessing trained immunity are potential next-generation candidates that could provide robust and heterologous protection.

Mucosal vaccination routes

A variety of routes can be applied to deliver mucosal vaccines, including oral, nasal, sublingual, rectal and vaginal vaccination, among which oral and nasal vaccination strategies are most frequently performed and studied.3,4 Theoretically, mucosal delivery of vaccines mimics the natural infection process of invading pathogens to stimulate antigen-specific mucosal immunity at infection sites as well as systemic immunity (Fig. 2). For example, the local immunity induced by intranasal vaccines is located mainly in the airways and lungs, whereas oral administration activates mucosal immunity in the stomach and small intestine.70,71 Oral vaccination shows limited efficacy in inducing sIgA antibodies in the distal segment of the large intestine, which may be related to complex interference by the gut microbiota. Therefore, a substitutive immunization route, rectal immunization, can be applied to inhibit the invasion of pathogens that enter through the distal digestive tract, such as human immunodeficiency virus (HIV).3 An exception is that intranasal vaccines can also activate antigen-specific mucosal immune responses in the female reproductive tract through the mucosal-imprinted lymphocyte homing pathway, which suggests that the intranasal route could be a means of protection against sexually transmitted diseases (STDs).4,72,73 Importantly, apart from selecting the appropriate vaccination route, the design of the vaccine formulation and its strategy are equally crucial. Oral vaccines face degradation challenges because of stomach acid and digestive enzymes. To address this obstacle, enteric-coated capsules or microparticle technology can effectively offer protection to the antigen, ensuring its release and absorption in the small intestine. For example, Vivotif® (a typhoid vaccine) utilizes an enteric-coated capsule form, successfully achieving effective antigen delivery.74 The nasal cavity, as an optimal route for vaccine delivery, is rich in immune cells and has a large surface area. New routes of immunization like sublingual and vaginal administration are increasingly noticed because they have lower enzyme activity and a relatively large mucosal surface that makes antigen uptake easier than in the harsh environments of the respiratory tract and gastrointestinal tract.76 Vaccines targeting the genital tract show great potential in combating STDs such as HIV and herpes simplex virus (HSV) infection.5,77,78 For the sublingual and vaginal routes, owing to the relatively low enzymatic activity in the mucosal environment, the use of soluble films or gel formulations can extend the duration of the antigen on the mucosal surface, promoting local immune responses. In addition, suppositories are commonly used for rectal administration as a traditional form, creating a drug reservoir on the mucosal surface to allow for long-lasting antigen release. Recently, solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs) have also been used to encapsulate antigens and deliver them via the rectal route, improving immune efficacy.79 To provide broader protection, different vaccination routes can be combined. Several studies have shown that dual-route vaccines are noninferior in inducing antigen-specific local and systemic immunity.78,80,81 Typical examples include the combination of intranasal and vaginal routes or intranasal plus intramuscular vaccination. Therefore, the choice of immunization route becomes an important consideration when designing mucosal vaccines.

Advanced platform technologies enabling the development of mucosal vaccines

Despite the potential of mucosal vaccines to induce local protective immunity, the landscape for mucosal vaccine development remains quite different, with only a few vaccines approved for human use. As an early landmark in vaccine history, the live-attenuated oral poliovirus vaccine (OPV) developed by Albert Sabin was the first modern vaccine to be licensed for inducing mucosal immunity. Additional examples of live-attenuated vaccines include bOPV, mOPV, and tOPV for poliomyelitis; Vivotif (live-attenuated vaccine for Salmonella typhi); Rotarix (live-attenuated monovalent human rotavirus vaccine); RotaTeq (live pentavalent reassortant rotavirus vaccine); Vaxchora (live-attenuated bacterial vaccine for Vibrio cholerae); and FluMist (live-attenuated influenza virus reassorted strains) for influenza Types A and B. In addition, several killed whole-cell-based mucosal vaccines, such as Dukoral, ShanChol, and Euvichol-Plus, have received World Health Organization (WHO) prequalification for use in preventing cholera resulting from Vibrio cholerae infection. Notably, FluMist is the sole FDA-authorized mucosal vaccine delivered by intranasal spray and is currently indicated in the United States for use in healthy individuals between 2 and 49 years of age. This intranasal vaccine utilizes temperature-sensitive, cold-adapted influenza strains that become attenuated at body temperature. Nonetheless, vaccines based on whole pathogens, including FluMist, present inherent safety considerations, as attenuation may be unstable and allow restoration of replicative capacity, thereby limiting their suitability for immunocompromised groups such as infants, older adults, and individuals with impaired immune function. At present, the intranasal live-attenuated SARS-CoV-2 vaccine for COVID-19 is the only candidate to reach clinical evaluation and is currently being tested in a multicenter phase III trial.82

Besides vaccines based on live-attenuated or inactivated whole pathogens, mucosal vaccine technology has pivoted toward viral vectors, adjuvanted subunit proteins with delivery systems, and RNA and DNA vaccines in recent decades (Fig. 2). This trend has accelerated in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic, with multiple intranasal vaccines progressing into clinical trials and some receiving emergency use authorization from local or regional regulatory authorities (Table 4). Virus-vectored vaccines

Viral vectors represent a widely used strategy for mucosal vaccination, serving as efficient mucosal delivery systems for antigen-encoding genes. The natural ability of these bacteria to infect host cells via mucosal surfaces makes them particularly suitable for inducing mucosal immune responses. These viral vector-based vaccines typically use genetically modified viruses, either replicating or nonreplicating, to deliver genetic material encoding major exogenous protective antigen(s) into mucosal target cells. This approach promotes antigen production and presentation to the immune system, initiating an adaptive immune response that can be recalled upon subsequent antigen exposure. Comparison of common viral vector platforms for vaccine development

Adenoviral (Ad) vector-based delivery is a highly attractive and commonly used approach for mucosal vaccines because it is capable of activating immune responses at mucosal surfaces, encompassing both innate and adaptive components, while remaining economical and safe to manufacture. Adenovirus vectors have been utilized in the development of mucosal vaccines against influenza,92 tuberculosis,93 and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV)94 and have demonstrated encouraging outcomes in preclinical studies as well as clinical evaluations, with excellent efficacy in inducing immune responses. Recently, several COVID-19 mucosal vaccines that were designed on the basis of an adenovirus have been approved for emergency use in various regions.95 CanSinoBio’s Convidecia Air™ (Ad5-nCoV-IH), an aerosolized version of the intramuscular Ad5-nCoV vaccine, showed promising boosting effects in clinical trials and was distributed to the general public in China and Morocco from 2022 onward.96 In addition, Sputnik V/Gam-COVID Vac (Gamaleya Institute), an intramuscular vaccine based on Ad5 and Ad26 that has been approved for use in Russia, has also been evaluated for intranasal administration in preclinical studies97 and a registered clinical trial, although peer-reviewed clinical data on this delivery route are currently lacking.

For adenovirus-based vaccines, concerns have been raised regarding the impact of preexisting immunity, particularly against human adenovirus serotype 5 (Ad5), the most commonly used serotype. This immunity, resulting from either natural exposure or prior vaccination, could reduce the effectiveness of Ad5-vectored vaccines.98 Ad-COVID-19, the inaugural intranasal COVID-19 vaccine to reach clinical evaluation, was withdrawn by Altimmune due to disappointing immunological results. However, several studies have suggested that intranasal delivery of the Ad5 vaccine could overcome anti-Ad5 immunity in the nasal passages of mice,99 nonhuman primates100 and humans with two repeated vaccinations.101 A recent study revealed a two-dose intranasal vaccination regimen in which the Ad5 vector carrying Omicron BA.1 The spike demonstrated broad-spectrum spike-specific sIgA in the nasal passage in humans.101 Nevertheless, it remains to be determined whether preexisting immunity to mucosal adenovirus vectors compromises the efficiency of viral antigen delivery.

Some studies suggest that nonhuman adenovirus serotypes, such as those derived from chimpanzees, helps bypass preexisting immunity while inducing strong immune responses.102 Compared with the intramuscular vaccine Covaxin, iNCOVACC (BBV154), a replication-deficient chimpanzee adenoviral vector-based nasal vaccine developed by Bharat Biotech,103 was approved for use in India in January 2023 and demonstrated a strong safety profile and superior humoral and mucosal immune responses in a phase III trial with ~3160 participants.104 Nevertheless, the ChAdOx1/AZD1222 intranasal vaccine, produced through collaboration between the University of Oxford and AstraZeneca, yielded unsatisfactory outcomes in a phase 1 clinical trial.43 It did not elicit robust mucosal antibody responses in study participants, which may be attributed to the restricted ability of the simian adenovirus vector to infect human respiratory epithelial cells, despite encouraging results observed in preclinical experiments.105 These results suggest that as an important platform for mucosal immunity, adenovirus-based vaccine technology needs further optimization and improvement, thereby enhancing the immune effect and broadening the applicability of adenovirus-based mucosal vaccines for other mucosal diseases.

In addition to adenovirus vectors, alternative viral vectors have been investigated for delivering antigen-encoding genes to mucosal tissues. Research teams in China (from the University of Hong Kong, Xiamen University and Beijing Wantai Biological Pharmacy) have developed an intranasal vaccine called dNS1-RBD, which employs a live-attenuated influenza virus vector with NS1 gene deficiency to express the SARS-CoV-2 RBD. Despite the relatively weak humoral immune response reported in clinical studies,42,106 it has demonstrated promising results in eliciting a mucosal cellular immune response and trained immunity against COVID-19 in animals.107 The vaccine was approved for emergency use in China in December 2022. In addition, COVID-19 mucosal vaccines based on the Newcastle disease virus vector (NDV-HXP-S, Sean Liu, Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai),108 live-attenuated respiratory syncytial virus vector (MV-014-212, Meissa Vaccines, Inc.),109 and parainfluenza virus 5-based vector (CVXGA1, CyanVac LLC)110 have been evaluated in clinical trials. Other promising vaccine platforms, including other modified influenza viruses,111 nonpropagative human PIV2,112 lentiviruses,113,114 and vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV),115 have also been explored for the development of mucosal vaccines, although most of these are still in the preclinical stages.

Protein antigens combined with mucosal adjuvants and delivery systems

Subunit protein vaccines are safe, cost-effective, and easy to produce on a large scale, effectively targeting specific pathogen components to confer protective immunity, positioning them as a cornerstone for advancing mucosal vaccine development. Since 1991, the recombinant pentameric cholera toxin B subunit (CTB), which is the only licensed mucosal vaccine that uses subunit antigens to effectively prevent the disease, has been safely incorporated into the oral cholera vaccine Dukoral. During the COVID-19 pandemic, several protein-based mucosal vaccines have entered clinical trials. One, CIGB-669 (engineered by the Center for Genetic Engineering and Biotechnology), combines the SARS-CoV-2 RBD with the hepatitis B virus nucleocapsid (N) antigen (AgnHB) and has progressed to a phase 1/2 trial.116 Another one, ACM-001 (ACM Biolabs), incorporates the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein along with a CpG adjuvant within an artificial cell membrane (ACM) using polymersome-based delivery technology.117 In addition, RAZI-COV PARS, produced by the Iranian Razi Vaccine Institute, contains the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein combined with the RAS-01 oil-in-water adjuvant system and has received emergency use authorization in Iran.118,119 Nonetheless, protein antigens are typically less immunogenic and face significant challenges in traversing multiple mucosal barriers while also being prone to enzymatic degradation. Mucosal adjuvants

The class of (1) toxoid adjuvants, including Escherichia coli heat-labile toxin (LT) and cholera toxin (CT), serves as a proof-of-concept for mucosal adjuvants.5 However, their use is limited by significant safety concerns, including severe toxicities such as Bell’s palsy.122 Significant efforts have focused on developing detoxified derivatives by introducing mutations or linking other peptides to disrupt the toxin’s active site, thereby reducing toxicity while maintaining adjuvanticity.123 Specifically, CTA1-DD,124 a nontoxic derivative of CT, and LTh(αK),125 LTK63,126 and LTR72,127 detoxified derivatives of LT, have demonstrated effective mucosal adjuvanticity. Among them, the LThαK-adjuvanted influenza vaccine is both effective and safe following nasal administration in clinical trials.125,128

In addition, (2) pattern recognition receptor (PRR) agonists, including those targeting Toll-like receptors (TLRs), STING, C-type lectin receptors (CLRs), RIG-I-like receptors (RLRs), and NOD-like receptors (NLRs), activate immune responses through PAMP activation.129 TLR agonists represent a central subgroup of mucosal adjuvants that have garnered significant attention in research.130–138 For instance, monophosphoryl lipid A (MPL), a low-toxicity TLR4 agonist derived from lipopolysaccharide, is incorporated in licensed adjuvants such as AS01, AS02, and AS04, and is currently being investigated in preclinical and clinical studies as a mucosal adjuvant for norovirus vaccines.70 Flagellin, known as the ligand for TLR5, offers mucosal adjuvant potency similar to that of CT and LT but without significant side effects and shows efficacy against RSV, SARS-CoV-2 and influenza antigens.131–133 Poly I:C (a TLR3 agonist) effectively stimulates innate immunity and IgA production.139 It works in synergy with CpG (a TLR9 agonist) to enhance both safety and efficacy as a nasal adjuvant for a COVID-19 subunit vaccine in rhesus macaques.135 Rintatolimod, a chemically modified form of poly I:C, demonstrated good tolerability when used as an adjuvant in an intranasal trivalent influenza vaccine during phase I clinical trials.136 Imidazoquinoline compound-based TLR7/8 agonists, such as 3 M-052, also present promising potential as mucosal adjuvants.137,138

In addition to TLR agonists, bacterial second messengers, such as cyclic dinucleotides (e.g., c-di-AMP and c-di-GMP), which activate STING signaling to induce IFN-I and adaptive immunity,140 serve as novel mucosal adjuvants for vaccines against influenza, Trypanosoma cruzi, and Bordetella pertussis infections in preclinical studies.141–143 Recently, STING agonists such as cGAMP have been used as adjuvants in SARS-CoV-2 and influenza vaccines.144–146 RNA agonists of RIG-I (IVT DI) with nanoemulsions (activating TLRs and NLRP3) are also used as adjuvants for the SARS-CoV-2 S protein antigen.147

The (3) cytokine classes, such as IL-1, IL-4, IL-12, IL-15, IL-18, IL-17, type I interferons (IFN-α/β), IFN-λ, and members of the TNF family, have been investigated as potential mucosal adjuvants.148–154 Nasal delivery of recombinant influenza hemagglutinin (rHA), which is supplemented with IL-1 family cytokines, induces both IgA and IgG responses.149 IL-15 has additionally been evaluated as a mucosal adjuvant for SARS-CoV-2 in rhesus macaque models.135 Preclinical research indicates that combining influenza subunit vaccines with type I interferons as mucosal adjuvants improves antigen internalization by phagocytic cells in the nasal mucosa after nasal immunization.155

Additional candidate mucosal adjuvants, such as saponins,156 natural killer T (NKT) cell activators (e.g., α-GalCer157 and analogs KBC-007, KBC-009158), protease-activated receptor 2 (PAR-2) agonists,159 cationic nanocarriers (chitosan, N-[1-(2,3-dioleoyloxy) propyl]-N,N,Ntrimethylammonium chloride (DOTAP), polyethyleneimine (PEI) and cationic crosslinked carbon dots (CCDs) adjuvants),160–162 have shown encouraging results.

Despite encouraging preclinical outcomes, the safety profile of mucosal adjuvants remains a major concern.122,148,163,164 For instance, nasal delivery may pose neurological risks because of the close anatomical relationship between the olfactory bulb and the cribriform plate.165 A clinical case involving Bell’s palsy following an LT-adjuvanted intranasal influenza vaccine illustrates this risk.166 Similarly, cholera toxin has been shown to damage olfactory nerves in mice.167 The inflammatory side effects of pattern recognition receptor (PRR) agonists (e.g., TLRs) also call for mitigation strategies. Potential solutions include the use of nanoparticle-based platforms to localize adjuvants and enhance the targeting of mucosal APCs,168,169 structural modifications to limit systemic diffusion,170 and synergistic combinations of TLR agonists to fine-tune immune activation.171 Overall, detailed preclinical toxicology and pharmacokinetic studies are imperative to ensure safety.

To expedite vaccine development, selecting clinically evaluated materials as mucosal adjuvants is crucial. Recent findings indicate that licensed aluminum salts may serve as safe and effective nasal adjuvants, eliciting both local and systemic antiviral immunity.73 In addition, the licensed oil-in-water emulsion MF59 has been reported as a promising adjuvant for both SARS-CoV-2 and influenza mucosal vaccines.172,173 Ultimately, creating mucosal vaccine adjuvants that provide strong immune activation while maintaining an acceptable safety profile continues to be a major challenge.

In addition to simple coadministration, covalent conjugation or nanoparticle linkage of antigens and adjuvants enhances mucosal vaccine efficacy by improving antigen stability and mucosal penetration and reducing the adjuvant dose.168,174,175 However, conjugation orientation and linkage chemistry must be carefully optimized to avoid masking epitopes or impairing antigen presentation, which can reduce immune recognition and vaccine effectiveness.174–176 Proper design is therefore critical for maximizing immune activation in mucosal vaccination.

Nonviral mucosal delivery system

The delivery systems should not only protect antigens from proteolytic degradation but also facilitate their passage across mucosal barriers while also serving as adjuvants to boost immune responses.17,33,121,177 In addition to the viral vector-based systems for mucosal delivery of antigen-encoding genes discussed earlier, nonviral delivery systems have also attracted significant attention. These systems are commonly used for the delivery of recombinant proteins and nucleic acid antigens. Mucosal delivery platforms based on nonviral technologies often employ nanoparticle-based approaches and can be broadly classified into lipid-based, polymeric, and biomimetic systems. These categories have been reviewed in detail in previous publications,177 and readers are encouraged to refer to them for more comprehensive information. Here, we briefly introduce a few representative examples.

Lipid-based nanoparticle system

Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) are nanosized lipid systems composed of two or more lipids that are capable of forming various structures and are widely used in mucosal immune delivery systems to enhance vaccine and therapeutic responses. Among LNPs, liposomes are a key type, consisting of spherical vesicles formed by phospholipid bilayers that can be tailored in composition and physicochemical properties.178 To enhance adhesion and prolong retention in the local mucosa, cationic liposomes and liposomes coated with mucoadhesive polymers have been designed.179 Liposomes are versatile carriers capable of delivering a broad range of substances, including proteins, peptides, DNA, and RNA. These systems are frequently employed in mucosal vaccine delivery, especially via the intranasal route, and have shown protective effects against pathogens including SARS-CoV-2, influenza virus, Yersinia pestis, and Streptococcus pneumoniae.180–183 Immunostimulating complexes (ISCOMs) constitute another key category of lipid-based nanocarriers, featuring self-assembling, spherical cage-like architectures formed from saponins, cholesterol, phospholipids, and antigens.184 The hydrophobic nature of ISCOMs enables them to remain in the nasal cavity for extended periods, up to approximately 4 hours.185 Nasal delivery of ISCOM-based vaccines has been reported to elicit immune responses in mice against a range of pathogens, such as influenza virus, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, hepatitis B virus, and RSV, underscoring their potential as effective delivery systems in mucosal vaccine development.186–189 Virus-like particles and virosomes, as well as lipid-based nanocarriers, will be discussed separately in the next section focused on the biomimetic nanoparticle (BNP) system.

Polymers can be broadly categorized into natural and synthetic types, each offering unique benefits in terms of biocompatibility, stability and versatility, with the ability to load antigens through mechanisms such as covalent conjugation, adsorption and encapsulation.190 Chitosan, a natural polysaccharide with mucoadhesive, permeation, and biodegradation properties, enhances antigen uptake at mucosal surfaces by loosening tight junctions in the epithelium, making it highly biocompatible and an ideal polymer for mucosal vaccine delivery,191,192 along with its derivatives such as trimethyl and hydroxyethyl chitosan.193 Poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA), a synthetic polymer that is both biodegradable and biocompatible, has been approved for use in parenteral drug delivery systems by the FDA. Its potential in mucosal vaccine delivery is enhanced by the ability to manipulate antigen release rates through adjustments in the poly (lactic acid) (PLA)/poly (glycolic acid) (PGA) ratio, influencing immune responses.194 In addition, PEI, a cationic polymer with mucoadhesive properties, adheres to the nasal mucus, enhancing intranasal vaccine delivery by forming stable antigen complexes, improving uptake, and prolonging residence time.161 A preclinical study revealed that PEI-complexed RBD vaccines enhance immune responses and sustain mucosal immunity, highlighting their potential for delivery.195,196

Biomimetic nanoparticle system

Combining nanotechnology with biomimetic approaches has led to the development of diverse nanoparticles tailored for mucosal vaccine delivery and therapeutic applications. Virus-like particles (VLPs) hold considerable promise as mucosal delivery platforms due to their capacity to imitate live viruses without containing viral genetic material, rendering them non-infectious and safe.197 These self-assembling particles (20–200 nm) effectively cross mucosal barriers and elicit robust immune responses. VLP-based intranasal vaccines have shown promise in inducing both humoral and cellular immunity, providing protection against pathogens such as SARS-CoV-2 and influenza in preclinical198,199 and clinical200 studies, positioning VLPs as highly effective platforms for delivering antigens to mucosal sites. In addition, virosomes are engineered, spherical vesicles derived from viruses and primarily composed of phospholipids, including those derived from viral envelope components.201 In contrast to liposomes, virosomes possess specific viral glycoproteins, which confer unique immunostimulatory properties and membrane fusion capabilities. These characteristics have made virosomes a focus of extensive research in mucosal delivery systems, enhancing immune responses through efficient antigen presentation and immune cell activation. Outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) possess multiple surface immunogenic proteins and glycans, providing natural adjuvant properties and serving as effective carriers for mucosal antigen delivery to induce potent mucosal immunity. For example, Escherichia coli OMVs successfully delivered malaria antigens, whereas Neisseria meningitidis OMVs delivered the SARS-CoV-2 S protein, triggering robust IgA responses following intranasal administration in animals.202,203 However, their complex composition may lead to potential immunogenic side effects, highlighting the need for further investigation into their toxicological profiles. Moreover, vaccination using OMVs can trigger toxic shock; however, this risk can be reduced by adjusting lipopolysaccharide (LPS) content on the OMV surface through genetic modification or detergent-mediated extraction.204 Another major challenge is batch-to-batch variation due to inconsistencies in OMV yield, isolation processes, and storage conditions.204,205 It is therefore critical to develop robust, standardized protocols for large-scale OMV production, purification, and storage and to report comprehensive information, including culture conditions, isolation procedures, and instrument calibration, to ensure product consistency, quality control, and reproducibility.

Some emerging mucosal delivery systems have also been developed. For example, a recent study presented a mucosal vaccine using a self-adjuvanting nanoparticle system based on cholera toxin B subunit (CTB) and trimer-forming peptides encapsulated in porous PLGA microcapsules for dry powder aerosol delivery.206 A vaccine combining adenovirus vectors and protein antigens has been developed, in which adenoviral vectors act as antigen delivery vehicles while also contributing to enhanced mucosal immunogenicity of the protein antigens.207 Despite promising preclinical outcomes, the clinical translation of these mucosal adjuvants and delivery systems remains a distant goal, requiring careful attention to efficacy, immunogenicity, and safety, as any potential safety concerns could significantly hinder their application.

Challenges and solutions for mucosal vaccine development

Innovative and safe platforms for next-generation mucosal vaccines

Mucosal vaccines hold great promise for the future, but their development faces significant challenges. To date, only a few have been officially approved, primarily relying on live-attenuated or inactivated whole-organism strategies via oral delivery. While emerging technologies, such as viral vector platforms, adjuvanted protein subunits, and mRNA vaccines, offer great potential, their efficacy and safety must be carefully evaluated (Fig. 4). A particularly urgent need lies in the development of mucosa-adapted mRNA vaccines. Although these vaccines have achieved unprecedented success during the COVID-19 pandemic, they remain confined to parenteral administration. Translating them to mucosal routes requires robust delivery systems, such as cationic ionizable lipid nanoparticles, which shield mRNAs from breakdown and enhance their uptake at mucosal sites.208 Moreover, effective mucosal adjuvants are essential for inducing durable, tissue-specific immunity. Bridging the translational gap: models and clinical evaluation of mucosal immunity

Animal models are indispensable in mucosal vaccine research, yet their translational value is often limited by anatomical and immunological species differences.10 For example, the mouse vaginal mucosa is keratinized, whereas in humans, it is nonkeratinized, affecting immune permeability.210 Differences in TLR expression and IgA subclasses further complicate translation. These disparities necessitate the development of more representative or humanized preclinical models.

Evaluation of mucosal immune responses in clinical settings is also constrained by the absence of standardized collection techniques and analytical procedures.41 Serum IgA is frequently measured but may not reliably reflect local mucosal immunity. Noninvasive methods such as saliva, nasal wash, or fecal sampling have been explored but suffer from variability and technical inconsistency.211,212 Cellular immune responses are even harder to evaluate and often require invasive procedures such as bronchoalveolar lavage.213,214 Although less invasive alternatives, such as nasal or nasopharyngeal swabs, are being investigated, their utility remains limited and unvalidated.27,215–217 Recently, a reliable and standardized method for measuring nasal mucosal antibodies was developed using nasal mucosal lining fluids and demonstrated good interlaboratory reproducibility across seven independent laboratories. This method has since been established as a national standard for evaluating nasal SARS-CoV-2-specific antibodies.218 Regardless, establishing unified and robust protocols is crucial for consistent immunogenicity evaluation across populations and studies.

Deciphering host–mucosa interactions and immune mechanisms

A deeper understanding of mucosal immune induction, regulation, and memory is foundational to next-generation vaccine development. Antigen translocation remains a bottleneck: while M cells are well-characterized, additional transport pathways (e.g., via dendritic cell extensions or epithelial transcytosis) warrant investigation.219 Furthermore, the role of tissue-resident immune populations such as TRM and BRM cells in long-term local protection, particularly those in the respiratory and reproductive tracts, requires further elucidation.

Host-intrinsic factors significantly shape mucosal vaccine efficacy.1 Nutritional status (e.g., vitamin A sufficiency) influences IgA induction,220 whereas microbiota composition affects both innate and adaptive mucosal responses.221 Disparities in the efficacy of some mucosal vaccines between low- and high-income countries may stem from microbiota variation driven by the environment, hygiene, and diet.1,10 Emerging studies suggest that microbiota modulation (e.g., via probiotics or prebiotics) is a viable strategy to increase vaccine response consistency.222

The integration of multiomics platforms such as transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics, and microbiomics provides a systems-level perspective on host–vaccine interactions.223 Transcriptomic and proteomic analyses can reveal immune cell activation states and signaling pathways, while metabolomics reveals the metabolic reprogramming underlying vaccine-induced immunity,27,224,225 and metabolomics reveals the metabolic reprogramming underlying vaccine-induced immunity. Microbiome profiling further elucidates host‒microbe interactions that may modulate vaccine efficacy, especially at mucosal surfaces. This comprehensive approach enables the rational design of mucosal vaccines tailored to individual or population-specific immune profiles, thereby improving their efficacy.

Conclusion and perspective

Mucosal vaccination has emerged as a distinct immunization strategy with capabilities that cannot be fully replicated by conventional intramuscular approaches. By engaging immune responses directly at barrier sites, mucosal vaccines offer unique advantages in intercepting pathogens at their portals of entry, while also opening opportunities for broader applications, including localized immunotherapy and the regulation of interconnected mucosal immune compartments beyond the site of administration.226

At the same time, effective activation of mucosal immunity remains intrinsically constrained by anatomical and physiological barriers that limit antigen access and persistence. Overcoming these hurdles places the development of robust mucosal delivery and adjuvant systems at the center of future progress. Innovations from materials science and nanotechnology are expected to play an increasingly prominent role in this regard; however, their ultimate impact will depend on careful consideration of safety, tolerability, and translational practicality, which remain decisive factors for clinical advancement.

Progress in mucosal vaccine development must also be matched by deeper mechanistic insight. Compared with the relatively well-characterized immune processes induced by parenteral vaccination, the immunological events shaping mucosal priming, tissue adaptation, and long-term immune maintenance are far less defined.227 The added complexity of mucosal environments, including continuous exposure to commensal microbiota, introduces regulatory layers that are unique to mucosal tissues and demand dedicated investigation.

Overall, mucosal immunity is entering a critical phase in which technological innovation and conceptual understanding must advance in parallel. Beyond improving individual vaccine platforms, deeper insights into mucosal immune regulation have the potential to reshape how immune protection is achieved at barrier tissues and coordinated across the body. As these efforts mature, mucosal vaccination may evolve from a niche alternative to parenteral immunization into a foundational strategy for disease prevention and intervention, with broad implications for infectious diseases and other mucosa-associated disorders.
