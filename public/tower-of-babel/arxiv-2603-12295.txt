1.
Introduction

1.1.
Dynamical systems

A discrete dynamical system is a pair
(S,f)
where
S
is set and
f
is a self-map.
For a positive integer
n\geq 1
, fix the notation

\displaystyle f^{n}=\underbrace{f\circ f\circ\cdots\circ f}_{n\text{ times}}.

A point
\alpha\in S
is called
periodic
if there exists
n>1
such that
f^{n}(\alpha)=\alpha
.
The set of all periodic points of the system
(S,f)
is denoted by
\operatorname{Per}(f,S)
.
If we consider
S
to be a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and
f
to be the polynomial
t^{L}
for some
L\geq 2
or the Chebyshev polynomial, then the arithmetic dynamics of this system is studied in
[
12
]
.
These questions have been studied from the viewpoints of arithmetic and graph theory in
[
25
]

The proportion of periodic points for a polynomial of the form
z^{2}+c
is considered in
[
11
]
.
In this article, we replace the field by a finite
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-algebra or a group.
Our motivation to address this problem comes from the subject area of word maps on groups and polynomial maps on algebras.

1.2.
Word maps on groups and polynomial maps on algebras

Given a tuple
(w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{r})
of words from the free group
\mathbf{F}_{r}
and a group
G
, one obtains a discrete dynamical system of a tuple of word maps through evaluations

\displaystyle(w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{r}):G^{r}\longrightarrow G^{r},\,\overline{g}=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{r})\mapsto(w_{1}(\overline{g}),\ldots,w_{r}(\overline{g})).

By replacing the free group by a free
R
-algebra on
r
indeterminates and replacing
G
by an
R
-algebra
A
, one gets a dynamical system of a tuple of polynomial maps.

The study of word maps dates back to 1951, when Øystein Ore proved that every element of the alternating group
A_{n}
is a commutator and conjectured the same for all finite non-abelian simple groups
[
14
]
. This conjecture was eventually proved through the work of many mathematicians; see
[
10
]
and the references therein. A related result shows that for every finite non-abelian simple group
G
of sufficiently large order and every nontrivial word
w
, one has
w(G)^{2}=G

[
9
]
. The exponent
2
is best possible; for instance, the squaring map on
A_{5}
is not surjective.

Polynomial maps have also been widely studied. Shoda proved in 1937 that over a field of characteristic zero every trace-zero matrix is a commutator
[
26
]
. Kaplansky and L’vov conjectured that the image of a multilinear polynomial on
\mathrm{M}_{n}(k)
over an infinite field
k
is always a vector space; despite partial progress
[
6
,
7
]
, the conjecture remains open for
n\geq 4
. Brěsar showed that if
C
is a commutative unital algebra over a field
\mathbb{F}
of characteristic
0
,
A=\mathrm{M}_{n}(C)
, and
f
is neither an identity nor a central polynomial of
A
, then every commutator in
A
is a difference of two sums of
7788
elements from
f(A)

[
2
]
; see also
[
1
]
. Polynomial maps have also been studied on upper triangular matrix algebras
[
5
,
16
]
and octonion algebras
[
19
]
; see also
[
18
]
.

1.3.
Power maps

In this article, we consider the power maps on a finite matrix algebra
\mathrm{M}_{n}(q)
, of
n\times n
matrices, and the finite general linear groups, symplectic groups and unitary groups.
We consider the power map, i.e., the map
x\mapsto x^{L}
where
L\geq 2
is a positive integer.
These maps have been investigated earlier by Chatterjee and Steinberg, independently, in the context of algebraic groups
[
3
,
27
]
; for some finite groups of Lie type in
[
8
,
21
,
20
]
, for general linear groups over finite principal ideal local rings of length two in
[
17
]
; see also
[
24
]
and the references therein.
Power maps exhibit several notable features (see
[
15
]
) and serve as a useful tool for studying the number of real conjugacy classes
[
22
]
.
A complex dynamical study has been carried out for the power map on
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
and
\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
has been carried out in
[
23
]
.

While dealing with the dynamical system
(\mathrm{M}_{n}(q),f)
, it is obvious that a matrix of the form
\mathrm{diag}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{n})
, where all
\alpha_{i}
s are periodic points of
f
with period
\ell
, is a member of
\operatorname{Per}(\mathrm{M}_{n}(q),f)
.
However, these are not all the points in
\operatorname{Per}(\mathrm{M}_{n}(q),f)
; as shown by the following example.

Example 1.1
.

Consider the power map
x\mapsto x^{2}
, the algebra
\mathrm{M}_{2}(59)
and the element

A=\begin{pmatrix}0&42\\
1&31\end{pmatrix}
.
Then one has that
A
has an irreducible polynomial, and it lies in a
28
-cycle of the squaring map.
So there are matrices which are not diagonal (or a conjugate of a diagonal matrix), but are periodic.

With this in mind, we explore the periodic points for the dynamical system
(G_{n}(q),x^{L})
where
L
is a prime,
\gcd(L,q)=1
, and
G_{n}(q)
is a finite matrix algebra or a matrix group; more precisely one of finite general linear, symplectic or unitary groups.
We consider the limiting value

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\longrightarrow\infty}\dfrac{|\operatorname{Per}(G_{n}(q),x^{L})|}{|G_{n}(q)|},

but this limit does not exist in general; for example, if
x^{L}
(say
(L,\mathrm{M}_{n}(q)=1
) is a permutation polynomial of
\mathrm{M}_{n}(q)
, then the ratio is
1
, whereas if it is not a permutation polynomial, the value need not be
1
.
Thus, one needs to put an extra condition on the
L
-adic evaluation of
q-1
.
For example, we prove in
theorem

1
that if
L
is a positive integer, under suitable condition, one gets

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\longrightarrow\infty\\
v_{L}(q-1)=c\end{subarray}}\dfrac{\left|\operatorname{Per}(x^{L},\mathrm{M}_{\ell}(q))\right|}{|\mathrm{M}_{\ell}(q)|}

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\longrightarrow\infty\\
v_{L}(q-1)=c\end{subarray}}\dfrac{\left|\operatorname{Per}(x^{L},\mathrm{GL}_{\ell}(q))\right|}{|\mathrm{GL}_{\ell}(q)|}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\vdash\ell\end{subarray}}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{r}\dfrac{1}{(\lambda_{i})^{m_{i}}m_{i}!}\dfrac{1}{L^{m_{i}v_{L}(q^{\lambda_{i}}-1)}},

where the sum run over all partitions
\lambda=(\lambda_{1}^{m_{1}},\lambda_{2}^{m_{2}},\ldots,\lambda_{r}^{m_{r}})
of
\ell
.

We prove a few basic results in
section

2

\mathrm{M}_{n}(q)
and
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q)
in

. The results on
\operatorname{Sp}_{2n}(q)
and
\operatorname{U}_{n}(q)
are proved in
section

.
Throughout,

L

denotes the
L
-adic valuation of

.

2.
Preparatory materials

Since the conjugacy classes of
\mathrm{M}_{n}(q)
(and
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q)
) are parametrized by irreducible polynomials and partitions, see for example
[
Macdonald81
]
, one needs to count irreducible polynomials whose roots satisfy a prescribed power condition.
The following result addresses this count.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
L
be a prime integer coprime to the characteristic
p
of the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
Suppose
\mathfrak{d}_{n}
denotes the number of monic irreducible polynomial
f
of degree
n
over the field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
such that each root
\alpha
of
f
satisfies
\alpha^{e_{n}}=1
, where
e_{\ell}\colon=\dfrac{q^{\ell}-1}{L^{v_{L}(q^{\ell}-1)}}
for an integer
\ell\geq 1
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathfrak{d}_{n}=\dfrac{1}{n}\left(\sum\limits_{i|n}\mu\left(\dfrac{n}{i}\right)\dfrac{q^{i}-1}{L^{v_{p}(q^{i}-1)}}\right),

where
\mu
is the Mobius function.

Proof.

Note that
\mathfrak{d}_{n}
is the number of elements in the following set

\displaystyle D_{n}=\left\{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}|\alpha^{e_{n}}=1,\alpha\not\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{i}}\text{ for all }i\mid n\right\}.

For a given
i\mid n
, an element
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{i}}
and
\alpha^{e_{n}}=1
implies that
\alpha^{\gcd(q^{i}-1,e_{n})=1}
.
Note that
i\mid n
, so
n=i\cdot n^{\prime}
, and hence

\displaystyle v_{L}(q^{n}-1)=v_{L}(q^{in^{\prime}}-1)=v_{L}(q^{i}-1)+v_{L}(n^{\prime}`),

and hence
\gcd(q^{i}-1,e_{n})=\gcd\left(q^{i}-1,\dfrac{(q^{i}-1)(\sum_{j=0}^{n/i-1}q^{ij})}{L^{v_{L}(q^{i}-1)}\cdot L^{v_{L}(n/i)}}\right)=\dfrac{q^{i}-1}{L^{v_{L}(q^{i}-1)}}
. Then the result follows from the inclusion-exclusion principle.
∎

For the classification of conjugacy classes of the finite symplectic group, one needs self-reciprocal monic polynomials, which are monic polynomials
f(t)
such that
f(0)\neq 0
and
\widetilde{f}(t)\colon=f(0)^{-1}t^{\deg f}f(1/t)=f(t)
; see
[
28
]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
L
be a prime integer coprime to the characteristic
p
of the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with
L|q-1
.
Suppose
\widetilde{\mathfrak{d}}_{2n}
denotes the number of self-reciprocal irreducible monic polynomial
f
of degree
2n
over the field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
such that each root
\alpha
of
f
satisfies
\alpha^{e_{2n}}=1
.
Then

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathfrak{d}}_{2n}=\dfrac{1}{2n}\left(\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i|n\\
i\,\,\text{odd}\end{subarray}}\mu\left({i}\right)\dfrac{q^{n/i}+1}{L^{v_{L}(q^{n/i}+1)}}\right).

Proof.

Using
[
13
, Theorem 1]
, we get that such a root
\alpha
os
f
must satisfy
\alpha^{q^{n}+1}=1
.
Hence, one gets that
\alpha^{\gcd(e_{2n},q^{n}+1)}=1
.
Thus, by the inclusion-exclusion principle

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathfrak{d}}_{2n}=\dfrac{1}{2n}\left(\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i|n\\
i\,\,\text{odd}\end{subarray}}\mu\left(i\right)\gcd\left(\dfrac{q^{2n/i}-1}{L^{v_{L}(q^{2n/i}-1)}},q^{n/i}+1\right)\right).

Note that here we are taking the sum over the indices
i
such that
i
is odd and
i|n
.
This happens because each irreducible factor (of degree

) of

is a self-reciprocal irreducible monic polynomial of degree

, where

divides

such that

is odd; see
[

.

Next, one has
\gcd\left(\dfrac{(q^{n/i}+1)(q^{n/i}-1)}{L^{v_{L}(q^{2n/i}-1)}},q^{n/i}+1\right)=\dfrac{q^{n/i}+1}{L^{v_{L}(q^{n/i}+1)}}
, from which the result follows.
∎

A self-conjugate monic polynomial is a polynomial
f(t)\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}[t]
such that
f(0)\neq 0
and
\overline{f}(t)\colon=(f(0)^{q})^{-1}t^{\deg f}\sum\limits_{i}a_{i}^{q}t^{-i}=f(t)
, where

.
We need these polynomials while classifying the conjugacy classes of the unitary groups; see

Let

Then

\displaystyle\overline{\mathfrak{d}}_{n}=\dfrac{1}{n}\left(\sum\limits_{i\mid n}\mu\left({i}\right)\dfrac{q^{n/i}+1}{L^{v_{L}(q^{n/i}+1)}}\right).

Proof.

Since a root

We finally end this section with the following easy-to-prove lemma.

Lemma 2.4

Let

be a polynomial,

.

3.
Proportion of periodic points in full matrix algebra and the general linear group

Using
Lemma

2.4
, it is immediate that, in order to decide whether an element
A
satisfies
f^{m}(A)=A
, it suffices to consider a representative of its conjugacy class over the algebraic closure
\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
.

We have the following result describing when a matrix is periodic

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
L
be a prime integer coprime to the characteristic
p
of the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, and let
f
denote the map
x\mapsto x^{L}
. Assume that
\delta_{L}(p)\mid d
, where
q=p^{d}
for some
d\geq 1
.
A matrix
A\in\mathrm{M}_{n}(q)
is periodic if and only if all eigenvalues of
A
are periodic and there is no nilpotent block in the Jordan canonical form of
A
, over an algebraic closure of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
In particular, a matrix
A\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q)
is periodic if and only if all its eigenvalues are periodic.

Proof.

We work in the field
\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
, invoking
Lemma

2.4
.
Hence, the matrix
A
can be taken into its Jordan canonical form
\mathrm{diag}(J_{\lambda_{q},m_{1}},J_{\lambda_{2},m_{2}},\cdots,J_{\lambda_{u},m_{u}})
for positive integers
m_{i}
, where

\displaystyle J_{\lambda,m}=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&1&&&&\\
&\lambda&1&&&\\
&&\ddots&&&\\
&&&\lambda&1\\
&&&&\lambda\end{pmatrix}_{m\times m}\in\mathrm{M}_{m}(\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}}).

Since
f^{m}(\mathrm{diag}(J_{\lambda_{q},m_{1}},J_{\lambda_{2},m_{2}},\cdots,J_{\lambda_{u},m_{u}}))=\mathrm{diag}(J_{\lambda_{q},m_{1}},J_{\lambda_{2},m_{2}},\cdots,J_{\lambda_{u},m_{u}})
if and only if
f^{m}(J_{\lambda_{i},m_{i}})=J_{\lambda_{i},m_{i}}
for all
i
, we may well assume that
A
up to conjugacy has a single Jordan block.
When
\lambda=0
, there is nothing to prove as
f^{r}(J_{0,n})=0
for some
r>0
; hence, such a matrix can never enter a cycle.
So we assume that
\lambda\neq 0
and consequently
J_{\lambda,n}\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q)
.
Now, note that for any
r>0

\displaystyle J_{\lambda,n}^{r}=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda^{r}&\dbinom{r}{1}\lambda^{r-1}&\dbinom{r}{2}\lambda^{r-2}&\cdots&\dbinom{r}{n-1}\lambda^{r-(n-1)}\\
0&\lambda^{r}&\dbinom{r}{1}\lambda^{r-1}&\cdots&\dbinom{r}{n-2}\lambda^{r-(n-2)}\\
0&0&\lambda^{r}&\cdots&\dbinom{r}{n-3}\lambda^{r-(n-3)}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&\lambda^{r}\end{pmatrix},

which implies that for having
J_{\lambda,n}^{r}=J_{\lambda,n}

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\dbinom{r}{i}}\lambda^{r-i}\equiv\begin{cases}\lambda&\text{when }\,i=0\\
1&\text{when }\,i=1\\
0&\text{when }\,n-1\geq i\geq 2\end{cases}.

Thus, we get that
p\bigm|\dbinom{r}{i}
for all
i\geq 2
, and
r\equiv 1\pmod{p}
.
If
r=L^{r^{\prime}}
we have that
\delta_{p}(L)|r^{\prime}
.

Consider the base-
p
expansion,
r=1+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{u}r_{i}p^{i}
and
s=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{u}s_{i}p^{i}
.
Recalling Lucas theorem, one has

\displaystyle\dbinom{r}{s}\equiv\prod\limits_{i=1}^{u}\dbinom{r_{i}}{s_{i}}\pmod{p}.

First we get that
r\geq n
; otherwise
\dbinom{r}{n-t}=1
for some
t\geq 1
.
Write
r=p^{v_{p}(r-1)}a
for some
a\not\equiv 0\pmod{p}
.
Let
a=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\ell}a_{i}p^{i}
.
Thus we have

\displaystyle r=1+a_{1}p^{v_{p}(r-1)+1}+a_{2}p^{v_{p}(r-1)+2}+\ldots=1+\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\ell}a_{i}p^{v_{p}(r-1)+i};

where
v_{p}(r-1)\geq 1
as
p|r-1
, and
a_{1}\neq 0
. Now, if possible, let
n>p^{v_{p}(r-1)}
. Fix
i_{0}=1+p^{v_{p}(r-1)}
, which implies by Lucas theorem that

\displaystyle\dbinom{r}{i_{0}}\equiv\dbinom{1}{1}\cdot\dbinom{a_{1}}{1}\neq 0\pmod{p};

also
2<i_{0}\leq n-1
.
We conclude that
n\leq p^{v_{p}(r-1)}
.
By applying Lucas theorem again, when
n\leq p^{v_{p}(r-1)}
, one has
\dbinom{r}{i}\equiv 0\pmod{p}
for all
2\leq i\leq n-1
.

Now, let us focus on the power map
x\mapsto x^{L}
, and we work over the algebraic closure of the underlying field.
A matrix
A=\bigoplus\limits_{i=1}^{\ell}J_{\lambda_{i},m_{i}}
is periodic- that is
A^{L^{r}}=A
if and only if
\lambda_{j}^{L^{r}}=\lambda_{j}
for all
1\leq j\leq\ell
and
m_{i}\leq p^{v_{p}(L^{r}-1)}
.
One also has that, if
\mu^{L^{r}}=\mu
for some
\mu\in\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
, then for any
b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
,
\mu^{L^{br}}=\mu
.
Using the Lifting The Exponent, one has
v_{p}(L^{br}-1)=v_{p}(L^{r}-1)+v_{p}(b)
.
Given
n
, one may choose
b
such that
n<v_{p}(L^{br}-1)
, where
\lambda^{L^{r}}=1
with
\lambda\in\sigma(A)
. The result follows.
∎

Thus, we need to first find the fixed points under iteration of the power map for a given finite field.
This quantity is more generally presented and proved in

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
L
be a prime integer coprime to the characteristic
p
of the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, and let
f
denote the map
x\mapsto x^{L}
. An element
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
is periodic if and only if either
\alpha=0
or
\alpha^{d}=1
, where
q-1=L^{v_{L}(q-1)}d
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha
be periodic.
If
\alpha=0
, we are done.
Else let
\alpha^{L^{r}}=\alpha
, which implies
\alpha^{L^{r}-1}=1
and hence
(\operatorname{ord}(\alpha),L)=1
.
Since
\alpha
is an element of the cyclic group
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
, we have
\alpha^{q-1}=1
, which implies the forward part.

For the converse, if
\alpha=0
, there is nothing to prove. So, let
\alpha^{d}=1
where
q-1=L^{v_{L}(q-1)}d
.
Since
(L,d)=1
,
L
is invertible in the group
\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}^{\times}
, and hence there exists
m
, a positive integer such that
L^{n}=1+md
.
Then
\alpha^{L^{n}}=\alpha
; i.e.
\alpha
is periodic.
∎

\displaystyle\dfrac{q-1}{L^{v_{L}(q-1)}}+1+\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\dfrac{q-1}{L^{v_{L}(q-1)}}\right)\left(\dfrac{q-1}{L^{v_{L}(q-1)}}+1\right)(q^{2}+q)+\left(\dfrac{q-1}{L^{v_{L}(q-1)}}\right)(q^{2}-1)

Proposition 3.5
.

For a positive integer
\ell\geq 2
consider the algebra
\mathrm{M}_{\ell}(q)
and the power map
x\mapsto x^{L}
where
\delta_{L}(p)|d
,
q=p^{d}
.
Then

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\longrightarrow\infty\\
v_{L}(q-1)=c\end{subarray}}\dfrac{\left|\operatorname{Per}(x^{L},\mathrm{M}_{\ell}(q))\right|}{|\mathrm{M}_{\ell}(q)|}

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\longrightarrow\infty\\
v_{L}(q-1)=c\end{subarray}}\dfrac{\left|\operatorname{Per}(x^{L},\mathrm{GL}_{\ell}(q))\right|}{|\mathrm{GL}_{\ell}(q)|}.

Further, the limiting value is contributed by the classes of regular semisimple invertible matrices.

Proof.

It is enough to show that the number of elements with
0
as an eigenvalue contributes a polynomial of degree strictly less than
\ell^{2}
.
It is enough to work for a specific
type
of matrix.
Fix a
multiset

\Lambda=\{(d_{0},\lambda_{0}),(d_{1},\lambda_{1}),(d_{2},\lambda_{2}),\ldots,(d_{\ell},\lambda_{\ell})\}
where
d_{0}=1
,
\lambda_{0}=1^{m_{0}}
,
d_{i}\geq 1
are positive integers, and
\lambda_{i}
is a partition of
m_{i}
for all
\ell\geq i\geq 1
; further
\sum\limits_{(d_{0},\lambda_{i})\in\Lambda}d_{i}m_{i}=n
.
Consider the set

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}_{\Lambda}=\left\{A\in\mathrm{M}_{n}(q):\delta_{A}=1\right\},

where
\delta_{A}=1
if and only if the characteristic polynomial is of the form
t^{m_{0}}\prod\limits_{i}f_{i}(t)^{m_{i}}
and the combinatorial data attached to
A
is of the form
\{(f_{i},\lambda_{i}):i\}
and
\deg f_{i}=d_{i}
; note that the choice of
f_{i}
depends on the matrix
A
although the degree sequence remains the same.
Then
A
is conjugate to a diagonal block matrix given as
\bigoplus\limits_{i}J_{i}\oplus\mathbf{0}
, where

\displaystyle J_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}C_{f_{i}}&\operatorname{I}_{d_{i}}&&\\
&C_{f_{i}}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&C_{f_{i}}\end{pmatrix},

is a
m_{i}\deg f_{i}\times m_{i}\deg f_{i}
matrix, and
\mathbf{0}
is the zero matrix of size
m_{0}\times m_{0}
.
Let
J\colon=\bigoplus\limits_{i}J_{i}.

Hence, one gets that for
A\in\mathscr{A}

\displaystyle\left|X\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q):AX=XA\right|=|\mathscr{Z}_{\mathrm{GL}}(J)|\cdot|\mathrm{GL}_{m_{0}}(q)|.

Let us first work out the general case, and we will specialize in
J
soon.
Consider
X\in\mathrm{GL}_{s}(q)
such that the minimal polynomial of
X
is given by
\prod\limits_{i=1}^{u}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n_{i}}f_{i,j}^{e_{i,j}}
where
\deg f_{i,j}=d_{i}
.
Suppose
X
has Jordan form

\displaystyle\bigoplus\limits_{i=1}^{u}\bigoplus\limits_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\left(J_{\lambda_{i,j,1}}^{l_{i,j,1}}\oplus J_{\lambda_{i,j,2}}^{l_{i,j,2}}\oplus\ldots\oplus J_{\lambda_{i,j,k_{i,j}}}^{l_{i,j,k_{i,j}}}\right),

where
J_{\lambda_{i,j,k}}
is the Jordan block of dimension
\lambda_{i,j,k}
relative to
f_{i,j}(t)
; further the combinatorial data attached to
X
is given by
\left\{(f_{i,j},\lambda^{f_{i,j}})\mid i,j\right\}
where
\lambda^{f_{i,j}}=\left(\lambda_{i,j,1}^{l_{i,j,1}},\lambda_{i,j,2}^{l_{i,j,2}}\ldots,\lambda_{i,j,k_{i,j}}^{l_{i,j,k_{i,j}}}\right)
with
\lambda_{i,j,1}<\lambda_{i,j,2}<\ldots<\lambda_{i,j,k_{i,j}}=e_{i,j}
.
Then

\displaystyle|\mathscr{Z}_{\mathrm{GL}}(X)|=q^{\gamma}\left|\prod\limits_{i=1}^{u}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\prod\limits_{k=1}^{k_{i,j}}\left(\mathrm{GL}_{l_{i,j,k_{i,j}}}(q^{d_{i}})\right)\right|

where

\displaystyle\gamma=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{u}d_{i}\left(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\left(2\sum\limits_{a<b}\lambda_{i,j,a}l_{i,j,a}l_{i,j,b}+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{k_{i,j}}(\lambda_{i,j,k}-1)l_{i,j,k}^{2}\right)\right);

see
[
4
, Theorem 2.2.8]
.

Coming back to the specific case, we get that the number of elements in
\mathscr{A}
is given by

\displaystyle\dbinom{\mathfrak{d}_{d_{1}}}{n_{1}}\dbinom{\mathfrak{d}_{d_{2}}}{n_{2}}\cdots\dbinom{\mathfrak{d}_{d_{u}}}{n_{u}}\dfrac{|\mathrm{GL}_{\ell}(q)|}{|\mathrm{GL}_{m_{0}}(q)|\cdot|\mathscr{Z}_{\mathrm{GL}}(J)|},

where
J\in\mathrm{GL}_{\ell-m_{0}}(q)
.
Note that the conjugacy class size is invariant for all
A^{\prime}
such that
A\in\mathscr{A}
.
Hence, the highest power of
q
appearing in the expression
|\mathscr{A}|
is at most

\displaystyle\mathfrak{H}_{q}=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{u}n_{i}d_{i}+\ell^{2}-m^{2}_{0}-\gamma-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{u}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{k_{i,j}}d_{i}l_{i,j,k_{ij}}^{2}.

Note that

\displaystyle\gamma+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{u}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{k_{i,j}}d_{i}l_{i,j,k_{ij}}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{u}d_{i}\left(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\left(2\sum\limits_{a<b}\lambda_{i,j,a}l_{i,j,a}l_{i,j,b}+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{k_{i,j}}(\lambda_{i,j,k}-1)l_{i,j,k}^{2}\right)\right)-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{u}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{k_{i,j}}d_{i}l_{i,j,k_{ij}}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{u}d_{i}\left(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\left(2\sum\limits_{a<b}\lambda_{i,j,a}l_{i,j,a}l_{i,j,b}+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{k_{i,j}}\lambda_{i,j,k}l_{i,j,k}^{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{u}d_{i}\left(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\left(\sum\limits_{a,b}\operatorname{min}\{\lambda_{i,j,a},\lambda_{i,j,b}\}l_{i,j,a}l_{i,j,b}\right)\right).

Hence

\displaystyle\mathfrak{H}_{q}-\ell^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{u}n_{i}d_{i}-m_{0}^{2}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{u}d_{i}\left(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\left(\sum\limits_{a,b}\operatorname{min}\{\lambda_{i,j,a},\lambda_{i,j,b}\}l_{i,j,a}l_{i,j,b}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{i=1}^{u}n_{i}d_{i}-m_{0}^{2}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{u}d_{i}\left(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\sum\limits_{a}\lambda_{i,j,a}l_{i,j,a}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle(\text{since }\lambda_{i,j,a}\geq 1)

\displaystyle=-m_{0}^{2}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{u}d_{i}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\left\{\left(\sum\limits_{a}\lambda_{i,j,a}l_{i,j,a}^{2}\right)-1\right\},

which implies that
\mathfrak{H}_{q}=\ell^{2}
if and only if
m_{0}=0
and
\lambda_{i,j,1}=l_{i,j,1}=1
and for all
a>1,

l_{i,j,a}=0
.
Consequently, only regular semisimple invertible elements contribute to the limiting value.
∎
