On the choice of using raw or demographically-corrected scores
Ignacio González-Pérez, Mats Julius Stensrud, Marco Piccininni

Abstract
. Demographic corrections are routinely performed in many disciplines, including psychology. Yet, there are ongoing debates about whether these corrections are appropriate and improve classification accuracy. Here, we focus on cognitive screening tests, and show that common demographic corrections, like the z-score standardization, can be detrimental for classification in some settings. Formally, we present sufficient conditions ensuring that raw scores outperform the demographically-corrected ones, and give a substantive interpretation of this result. We also investigate the claim that using demographically-corrected scores results in more fair decisions compared to using raw scores. We apply our results to the Mini-Mental State Examination in the OASIS-3 dataset.



Keywords:
cognitive screening, age-education correction, accuracy, demographic insensitivity

1.
Introduction

Demographic corrections are widely used in the health sciences, particularly in psychology. For example, tests assessing age-related cognitive impairment are usually corrected for age and education
[
68
,
71
,
53
]
. Such corrections are also used in the clinical definitions of cognitive impairment
[
9
]
, such as the DSM-5 definition of Mild Neurocognitive Disorder
[
2
]
and the older Mild Cognitive Impairment criteria
[
67
]
.

The consequences of demographic corrections are particularly relevant to screening for cognitive impairment. Cognitive screening tests are used to decide whether an individual should be referred to further clinical assessments
[
14
,
4
,
60
]
. The validity of these tests is usually assessed by their classification accuracy, i.e., sensitivity, specificity, and area under the ROC curve, in categorizing individuals against the gold-standard clinical diagnoses of mild cognitive impairment and/or dementia
[
14
,
46
,
53
,
43
,
60
,
47
,
23
]
.

Cognitive screening tests are designed to give a numerical result that summarizes the performance of the individual who undertakes the evaluation. This result, e.g., representing the number of correct answers or a rating, is commonly referred to as the “raw” test score
[
23
,
53
]
.
Sometimes, a demographically-corrected score is preferred over the raw score
[
51
,
46
,
47
,
53
,
23
]
. Specifically, instead of interpreting raw scores directly, practitioners often draw conclusions by comparing the raw score to reference norms of individuals without cognitive impairment and with similar demographic characteristics
[
13
,
53
,
79
,
47
,
46
]
. In practice, this comparison is made by numerically transforming the raw score into a “corrected” version; this process is known as adjustment
[
5
,
46
,
51
]
or correction
[
61
]
. The most widespread type of demographic correction is the “z-score” correction
[
53
,
47
]
.

While demographic corrections are ubiquitous in neuropsychology, empirical results suggest that demographically-corrected scores have lower classification accuracy compared to raw scores. For example, a large 1996 study of older persons found that demographic corrections did not improve classification accuracy for dementia compared to using raw scores
[
4
]
. Other empirical studies reported no benefit of demographic corrections on the classification accuracy of the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE)
[
43
]
and the modified 3MS
[
60
]
screening tests. In these studies, accuracy was measured with respect to clinical diagnoses. Similar results were found when assessing the prognostic accuracy of the test scores in longitudinal studies
[
31
,
71
]
, or when assessing the association between unadjusted or adjusted criteria and dementia biomarkers
[
29
]
.

Some researchers have advocated the claim that the suboptimal accuracy of demographically-corrected scores can be explained by the fact that demographic characteristics influence not only the raw scores but also the probability of the condition of interest (e.g., cognitive impairment). Already in 1986,
[
5
]
used this argument to question the use of correction for age and education in cognitive screening. A decade later,
[
79
]
claimed that since age and education were both risk factors for dementia, eliminating their effect on the raw test scores resulted in lower classification accuracy of the test, which was later supported by simulations and real data analyses
[
61
]
. More recently, it has been shown that, under a simplified causal model and strong parametric assumptions, corrected scores have a lower area under the ROC curve compared to raw scores, if age and education have sufficiently strong effects on both the test performance and the risk of having cognitive impairment
[
69
]
. Simulations have suggested that using raw scores leads to higher marginal sensitivity (or specificity) for the same specificity (or sensitivity) level, while using demographically-corrected scores leads to more homogeneous performance across demographic groups
[
94
]
.

These empirical and theoretical results have, however, been met with scepticism by practitioners. Some have questioned whether it is true that raw scores have better classification accuracy compared to corrected scores, because of the small differences found in practice and the inevitable uncertainty of finite sample inference
[
37
,
29
]
. Also, the existing simulation results have been questioned due to their strong assumptions, and the fact that there is no guarantee that the simulated data are realistic
[
37
]
. Finally, some researchers have informally argued that corrected scores should be preferred over raw scores even if their classification accuracy is lower, because in some sense they are more
“fair”
and avoid overdiagnosis in vulnerable demographic groups
[
37
,
1
]
.

The aim of this work is to bring clarity to this debate. We will show, without relying on restrictive data generating models, parametric assumptions, or empirical results, that it is possible for raw scores to have better classification accuracy compared to corrected scores. Specifically, we will present sufficient conditions for the superiority in terms of classification accuracy of the raw scores over the corrected ones, and give an interpretation of this result. In addition, we will engage with the claims that corrected scores are more fair. In this context, we formalize the notion of a score being insensitive to demographics, and assess whether demographically-corrected scores fulfil this property. Finally, we apply our results to the OASIS-3 dataset
[
45
]
. In this dataset, we find evidence for the superiority of raw MMSE scores in terms of classification accuracy and that age-corrected scores are not insensitive to age amongst the cognitively impaired.

2.
Comparison of classification accuracy

2.1.
Notation and setup

Consider a screening setting designed to assess whether an individual has a condition of interest (
D=1
) or not (
D=0
). As an example, let
D
indicate the presence of cognitive impairment, for example according to a clinical examination or some biomarkers. Let
X
indicate the raw score resulting from the administration of a cognitive screening test, for example the MMSE
[
18
]
, with support on a subset of the real line,
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
. The variable
V
represents the demographic characteristics of the individual. To simplify the exposition, assume that
V
is a univariate covariate supported on (a subset of) the real line,
\mathcal{V}
. For example,
V
could represent age, education, sex, or age-education stratum. We present our results in full generality in Appendix
C
. We will also assume that the support of
(X,V)\mid D=d
does not depend on
d
. Nevertheless, we are agnostic to the support and distribution of
X
, allowing it to have either a discrete or absolutely continuous distribution.

Let
Z
denote the
corrected score
. More formally, we define the demographic correction as follows.

Definition 1

(Correction of the raw score)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}=\{F\colon\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]\text{ a c.d.f.}\}
, and let
T\colon\mathcal{F}\times\mathcal{V}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a map such that
T(F,v,\cdot)
is strictly increasing and differentiable for all
F\in\mathcal{F}
and all
v\in\mathcal{V}
. Then the corrected score is constructed as
Z=g_{V}(X)
almost surely, with
g_{v}(x)=T(F_{X,0}(\cdot\mid v),v,x)
for all
v
in
\mathcal{V}
.

Here,
F_{X,d}(\cdot\mid v)
is the the c.d.f. of
X\mid D=d,V=v
(following
[
44
]
). While Definition
1
is fairly general, most corrections found in practice either make use only of
v
or of
F_{X,0}(\cdot\mid v)
. Thus, as they will show slightly different behaviours, we formally distinguish them.

Definition 2

(Correction types)
.

Consider a correction of the raw score defined by a map
T
as per Definition
1
. We say that this correction is:

•

of Type I if there exists
\tilde{T}\colon\mathcal{F}\times\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
, such that
T(F,v,x)=\tilde{T}(F,x)
for all
(F,v,x)
in
\mathcal{F}\times\mathcal{V}\times\mathbb{R}
.

•

of Type II if there exists
{T^{\prime}}\colon\mathcal{V}\times\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
, such that
T(F,v,x)={T^{\prime}}(v,x)
for all
(F,v,x)
in
\mathcal{F}\times\mathcal{V}\times\mathbb{R}
.

•

of Type III if it is neither or Type I nor II.

Unless explicitly stated otherwise, we are agnostic to the type of correction we consider. To fix ideas, let
X
be the MMSE score and let
V
represent age-education stratum. A common age-education correction consists in calculating the “z-score”
[
53
,
47
]

(1)

Z=\frac{X-\mathbb{E}[X\mid V,D=0]}{\sqrt{Var[X\mid V,D=0]}},

that is, we subtract from the observed raw score
X
the expected score from a normative sample (without impairment,
D=0
) with the same demographic characteristics
V
, and divide by the standard deviation of the normative sample
[
53
,
68
]
.
We see how the “z-score” correction (
1
) is a Type I correction with
T(F,v,x)=(x-\mu(F))/\sqrt{Var(F)}
. The percentile transformation, the
“t-score”
, and other standardized scores
[
23
,
53
]
are also Type I corrections. The normalized standard score
[
23
]
is also a Type I correction with
T(F,v,x)=\Phi^{-1}(F(x))
, where
\Phi
is the standard normal c.d.f. and
F
has a continuous density w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure.
Nevertheless, not all corrections used in practice are of Type I. For example, the MoCA test is sometimes corrected for education status by adding 1 point if the individual has 12 years of education or less
[
58
]
. This is a Type II correction defined of the form
T(F,v,x)=x+I(v\leq 12)
, where
I(\cdot)
denotes the indicator function, and
v
years of formal education.

We adopt the convention that the raw score
X
is constructed in such a way that smaller scores are associated with a greater risk of impairment. This convention aligns with several widely adopted cognitive screening tests such as the MMSE
[
18
]
and the MoCA
[
58
]
.
We therefore consider classification rules of the type
X\leq t
, and we adopt the same directionality for the corrected score as well.

The threshold
t
is often chosen to achieve a certain miss-classification rate amongst the non-impaired, commonly referred to as False-Positive Rate

FPR(t):=\mathbb{P}(X\leq t\mid D=0)=F_{X,0}(t),

where we denote by
F_{X,d}
the cumulative distribution function of
X\mid D=d
. The FPR is also referred to as the false-alarm rate or one minus the specificity. The counterpart of the FPR is the probability of correctly classifying an individual as impaired: the True-Positive Rate

TPR(t):=\mathbb{P}(X\leq t\mid D=1)=F_{X,1}(t),

also referred to as the hit rate or sensitivity.
If we reframe the classification problem as testing the hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{0}\colon D=0
against
\mathcal{H}_{1}\colon D=1

[
44
, Ch. 2.3.2]
, the false- and true-positive rates correspond to the type-I error and the power (one minus type-II error), respectively.

To understand the performance of the diagnostic test, it is common to plot the pairs of false- and true-positive rates for all possible thresholds, that is, the
Receiver Operating Characteristic
(ROC) curve
[
22
]

\{(FPR(t),TPR(t))\colon t\in\mathbb{R}\}\subset[0,1]^{2}.

This curve is always contained in
[0,1]^{2}
, tracing between (0,0) and (1,1), which are associated with the threshold behaviour
t\to-\infty
and
t\to\infty
respectively.
Re-parametrizing the ROC curve we can express the TPR corresponding to a fixed FPR
\alpha\in(0,1)
as
ROC_{X}(\alpha)=F_{X,1}(F_{X,0}^{-1}(\alpha)).
We define the generalized inverse of a c.d.f.
F
as
F^{-1}(\alpha):=\inf\{x\in\mathbb{R}\colon F(x)\geq\alpha\}
for all
\alpha
in
(0,1)
.

Global performance measures of a diagnostic score, which do not depend on the choice of a threshold, can be obtained by summarizing the ROC curve. The most common of these measures is the area under the ROC curve (AUC)
[
44
, Ch. 2.4.1]
, defined as

AUC(X)=\int_{0}^{1}ROC_{X}(\alpha)d\alpha.

The AUC is upper-bound by 1, with this value representing a perfect classifier. Hence, higher AUC values indicate better classification accuracy.

Remark 1
.

We have referred to the ROC set
\{(FPR(t),TPR(t))\colon t\in\mathbb{R}\}
as a curve. If
X\mid D
has an absolutely continuous distribution w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure,
ROC_{X}
is continuous on
[0,1]
as it is a composition of continuous functions. However this is not the case when
X
has a discrete distribution, as is the case for example of the MMSE. Then, the ROC set represents a “disconnected” set of points, in the sense that for any point one can find an
\epsilon
-ball centred on it not containing any other points of the ROC set. In this case, we follow the convention of
[
17
,
20
]
and define the ROC curve as a linear interpolation of points. The linear interpolation generalizes results in the continuous case, such as the probabilistic interpretation of the area under the ROC curve or the connection between slopes of the ROC curve and likelihood ratios
[
57
,
17
]
. This gives a comprehensive approach that allow us to remain agnostic about the distribution of the score
X
.

When baseline covariates are available, we can also define covariate-specific ROC curves
[
40
]
in a stratum of
V
,

ROC_{X\mid V}(\alpha\mid v)=F_{X,1}(F_{X,0}^{-1}(\alpha\mid v)\mid v).

2.2.
Main Results

The main goal of this section is to compare the accuracy of classification rules based on raw and corrected scores.
To make a fair comparison, we compare the marginal TPR of raw and corrected scores for a fixed marginal FPR. That is, we compare marginal ROC curves of raw and corrected scores at an arbitrary value
\alpha
of the FPR.
Differently from the existing literature handling covariates and ROC curves
[
63
,
64
,
10
,
21
,
35
,
78
,
39
]
, we do not focus on how to “optimally” adjust for covariates, but rather we study how the common ways in which covariates are accounted for in demographic corrections affect classification accuracy.

Under Definition
1
, classification based on raw or corrected scores conditional on a certain
V
-stratum is equivalent, in the sense that covariate-specific ROC curves are identical, as we can see in the following lemma.

Lemma 1
.

Under Definition
1
, covariate-specific ROC curves for raw and corrected scores are identical, in the sense that
ROC_{Z\mid V}(\alpha\mid v)=ROC_{X\mid V}(\alpha\mid v)
for all
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
v
in
\mathcal{V}
.

For a proof see Appendix
B.1
. Following Lemma
1
, to simplify notation, we denote
ROC_{X\mid V}(\alpha\mid v)
by
ROC_{v}(\alpha)
for all
v
in the support of
V
and all
\alpha
in (0,1).
Another property implied by Definition
1
, which we also prove in Appendix
B.1
, relates to a special case where raw scores are conditionally independent from the covariates.

.

If

Z\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}V\mid D

This lemma states that if the demographic covariates are not associated with the raw score among impaired and non-impaired, then performing a Type I correction does not introduce a dependence with the demographic covariates. The reason why Lemma

Under Type I correction,

Corollary 1
.

Under the conditions of Lemma

This Corollary shows that when the covariates

are independent of the raw score in the impaired and non-impaired groups, performing a Type I correction is irrelevant for classification accuracy. For example, if we correct the MMSE raw score for age using the z-score correction (

To compare adjusted and unadjusted ROC curves in less trivial scenarios, we will make three assumptions. The first two are likely to be accepted in most real-life settings.

Assumption 1

(The test is well-designed)
.

The conditional likelihood ratio of the raw score given the demographic covariates,

LR_{X\mid V}(\cdot\mid v)=\dfrac{f_{X\mid V,D}(\cdot\mid v,1)}{f_{X\mid V,D}(\cdot\mid v,0)},

is decreasing for all
v
in
\mathcal{V}
.

By
f_{X\mid V,D}
we denote the density (w.r.t. a certain dominating measure) of
X|V,D
. Assumption
1
is equivalent to the covariate-specific ROC curves
ROC_{v}
being concave for all
v
in
\mathcal{V}

[
20
, Thm. 2 & 3]
. Assumption
1
is expected to hold when the cognitive screening test from which the raw score
X
is designed to discriminate impaired individuals using a “smaller than” threshold. This is the case for the MMSE, the MoCA and several other cognitive screening tests. If Assumption
1
does not hold, there would be a
V
-stratum and a certain FPR for which the optimal decision rule (the likelihood ratio test) classifies those with a score in a set not described by a “smaller than” threshold as “positive”, meaning the clinical test is flawed in its construction. This assumption aligns with comments made in
[
66
]
that non-concave ROC curves “must be considered irrational”.

Assumption 2

(The correction is well-designed)
.

When the corrected score is constructed under Definition
1
, then

Z\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}V\mid D=0.

This assumption can be expected to hold in practice, as achieving the conditional independence in Assumption
2
is arguably the purpose of any correction under Definition
1
. For example, when describing age-correction,
[
68
]
write that the correction removes “the effect of age so that the average test score across persons of different ages will be invariant”. The claim suggests that the procedure makes the corrected score
Z
independent of the demographic variable
V
in the

The most common demographic correction, the z-score transformation (
1

[

]
. If

is normally distributed, or more generally belongs to a location-scale family, the z-score transformation is guaranteed to satisfy Assumption

we show that Assumption

5
in Appendix
C.1
).

Assumptions
1
and
2
are plausible in most settings. However the third assumption will not hold just based on basic principles. Yet this assumption helps us describe when performing a correction leads to worse classification accuracy.

Assumption 3

(Probabilistic co-monotonicity)
.

For a given
\alpha\in(0,1)
it holds that
\alpha_{v}^{X}:=\mathbb{P}(X\leq F_{X,0}^{-1}(\alpha)\mid D=0,V=v)

\mathbb{P}(D=1\mid{X=F_{X,0}^{-1}(\alpha)},V=v)

1

]
.
in

Informally, this assumption establishes that the association between

Assumptions

Under Assumptions

Theorem 1
.

Fix
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Under Definition

and Assumptions

(2)

Theorem

Some practitioners believe that corrected scores outperform raw ones as they leverage more information, and therefore they have higher classification accuracy
[

The assumptions in Theorem
1
can be modified to determine sufficient conditions for superiority of the corrected score. Under Definition

2.3.
A causal interpretation of the superiority conditions

So far we have made no causal assumptions about our problem of interest. Definition

and Assumptions

We represent variables within a Finest Fully Randomized Causally Interpretable Structured Tree Graphs (FFRCISTG) causal model

[

]
, or “having a confounding effect”
[

, or being “confounding factors”
[

]
of the test-impairment relationship. Suppose, for simplicity of exposition, that no additional common causes of these variables exist. This causal model is reflected in the causal DAG in Figure

we consider how the conditional probability

Conceptually, Assumption

on

Consider the example of age-correction for the MMSE, so that

represents the MMSE score,

represents age, and

represents dementia status. There is clear empirical evidence indicating that non-impaired individuals have lower MMSE scores as they age, see for example
[

,

. Therefore we know that

is increasing in

]
. Therefore, the conditional probability
\mathbb{P}(D=1\mid V=v)
increases in

.
Then, Assumption

would require that the collider bias induced on the association between

is not strong enough to reverse the association between

and

owed to the causal effect

Conceptually, when the collider bias is not strong enough to “flip” the association between age and dementia, the raw score has better classification accuracy than the age-corrected score, according to Theorem

,

,

3.
Other reasons for using corrections

3.1.
Equal classification accuracy across demographic groups

Marginal classification accuracy is not the only criterion to compare raw and demographically-corrected scores. Already in 1998,
[
43
]
recognized that different researchers were using different criteria to evaluate screening strategies for dementia. Specifically,
[
43
]
illustrated how proponents of demographic corrections were not concerned with maximizing classification accuracy, but with ensuring equal true- and false-positive rates across demographic groups
[
43
]
.

Demographic corrections of cognitive screening tests are often proposed as a remedy to an undesirable association between demographic variables and test scores. For example, corrections of the MMSE are motivated by the observation that age and education are associated with MMSE scores
[
43
,
51
]
. Similarly, age-education corrections of the MoCA test have been motivated by the fact that age and education explain up to 49% of the MoCA scores variance in normative samples
[
47
]
.

Demographic corrections have been proposed as a way to avoid overdiagnosis in vulnerable groups. Proponents of education corrections were concerned about a
“detection bias caused by under-education”
: individuals without impairment who have low education tend to get low scores in cognitive screening tests and are therefore erroneously classified as impaired
[
4
]
. For example,
[
24
]
argued that without an education correction
“many persons may be unfairly labelled as demented (or at risk for dementia) when the only problem was a lack of schooling”
. Similarly,
[
56
]
considered the age-and-education-corrected MMSE a preferable screening tool for low-education and minority individuals. More recently, some authors have argued that demographic corrections can be justified as a way to fulfil certain fairness criteria in cognitive screening
[
69
,
94
]
. This point has been echoed by supporters of demographically-corrected scores, who find desirable to have similar classification accuracy in different demographic strata
[
1
]
, avoiding overdiagnosis in vulnerable groups
[
37
]
.

As we have discussed in Section
2
, a well-designed demographic correction makes the corrected score independent of the demographic covariates amongst individuals without impairment; that is, we expect
Z\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}V|D=0
(Assumption
2
). In other words, we expect the corrected score to have the same FPR (and the same specificity) in all demographic groups
[
94
]
. This would solve, for example, the above-mentioned issue of a higher FPR for older individuals with lower education, which can lead to overdiagnosis and social discrimination for vulnerable groups. More formally, demographic corrections can be seen as a way to achieve the
“False-positive error rate balance”
criterion
[
90
]
, which is one of a large number of different algorithmic fairness criteria. Furthermore, in the specific setting where
X
follows an additive model, the z-score correction ensures that both FPR and TPR are constant across demographic strata, see Remark
2
, achieving the so-called
“equalized odds”
criterion
[
90
]
.

3.2.
Removal of demographic effects

Demographic corrections have also been described as a way to “remove the effect” of the demographic variables from the test score
[
79
,
68
,
69
,
77
]
.
[
77
]
explicitly argued that, in an ideal world, cognitive screening tests should be “independent of culture, language and education” and “free of socio-demographic effects”; because it is practically impossible to design cognitive screening tests that are free of these effects, demographic corrections are used to remove them. This suggests that demographic corrections are intended as a tool to remove effects of variables that are determined by demographics.

To formalize this plain-English argument, which is nevertheless based on causal reasoning, we consider an extension of the causal model introduced in Section
2.3
, where again we represent variables within a FFRCISTG model and use DAGs to represent a statistical model induced by this FFRCISTG
[
76
,
73
]
. The assumptions we make are not necessarily plausible in practice; rather, we present results that clarify sufficient conditions under which certain procedures have the properties often attributed to them in discussions.

Consider the example of age-and-education correction of the MMSE. Like other screening tests, the MMSE is designed to rely on communication, language, and arithmetic to assess the cognitive ability of the screenees. The concern is that the test scores depend on age and education as these demographic variables affect sensory abilities and levels of background knowledge. For a given screnee, let
V
denote the demographic characteristics of interest, like age and education, and consider some score
S
. For now, we are agnostic as to whether this is the raw MMSE or any corrected (MMSE) score. As before
D
indicates the impairment status. We also consider a set of baseline covariates
L
, which could possibly affect other variables.

To fix ideas, consider a screenee with low education, who is not familiar with basic arithmetic and has a narrow vocabulary. This screenee is expected to have a low score. Similarly, an older screenee, who has impaired vision and hearing, is expected to score low. We introduce a new variable
V_{S}
, potentially unmeasured, which can be interpreted as the level of sensory ability/background knowledge to which the screening test score is
sensitive
2

, due to a fundamental design limitation. We reason that

We will assume that, in the observed data,

are deterministically related, i.e.,

almost surely. In other words, every level of age-education corresponds to one distinct level of sensory ability/background knowledge. For example, a person who earned a PhD has a very specific PhD-level knowledge relevant to the test. Clearly, this assumption can be implausible. For example, there can be individuals with the same years of education who have different relevant background knowledge levels. The determinism is often implicit in demographic corrections as, for example, individuals with the same age and number of years of education will have their scores corrected in the same way, regardless of their actual amount of background knowledge or sensory abilities.

There might also exist other variables, beyond impairment status, affected by
V
, which a desirable test could be sensitive to (see Appendix
D.2
for further elaboration). We denote these additional covariates as

could indicate the presence of another neurological disease not included in

Interventions on age and education

For example, we could replace original words with simpler synonyms to reduce the “sensitivity” to education, or administering the test with an hearing aid to alter the “sensitivity” to age. These interventions would change the sensory skills or background knowledge that are relevant to the test,

We can now formalize the notion of a desirable test expressed in

Definition 3

(Strong demographic insensitivity)
.

We say that a score

Yet, in our screening problem we are not necessarily interested in the full distribution of the score, but rather in the decision that is taken based on it. Thus, we define a weaker notion of demographic insensitivity.

Definition 4

(Weak demographic insensitivity)
.

Let

v_{S}
for all
(v,d)

\mathcal{V}\times\{0,1\}

In practice, assessing whether a score

(S)
. Furthermore, as long as

As a simple example, consider the scenario in Section

4.
Data application: the OASIS-3 study

4.1.
The OASIS-3 data

We analysed data from OASIS-3
[
45
]
, which is part of the OASIS (Open Access Series of Imaging Studies) project. Its goal is to make neuroimaging data sets of the brain freely available to the scientific community. In particular, the OASIS-3 dataset “is a retrospective compilation of data for 1378 participants that were collected across several ongoing projects through the WUSTL Knight ADRC over the course of 30 years”.

At study entry, age, sex, and socio-economic status were recorded. Moreover, participants were administered a MMSE. For each participant, neurological status was assessed at baseline using the
Clinical Dementia Rating
(CDR) scale. We only included in our analyses participants who had no missing values in any of these variables, resulting in a data set including 1317 individuals. We considered participants to have cognitive impairment (
D=1
) when their CDR was above zero.
Approximately 28% of the individuals were therefore classified as having cognitive impairment. The median MMSE score was approximately 29 in the
D=0
group, and 27 in participants with cognitive impairment
D=1
. We denote the MMSE raw score with the letter
X
for consistency. We also define the demographic covariate
V
to be a categorical variable representing age groups: below 65, 65-70, 70-75 and above 75 years of age at baseline. This partitioning corresponds roughly to the age quartiles of the OASIS-3 cohort.

We have two goals. First, we want to study the classification superiority of the MMSE raw score over the
V
-corrected scores. Specifically, we will focus on Assumption
3
, crucial to determine the classification superiority. Second, we will study whether the raw and age-corrected MMSE scores are insensitive to the age-quartiles group indicator
V
.

4.3.

We also investigated whether the raw and age-corrected MMSE scores are insensitive to the age-quartiles group indicator

Our choice of including the socio-economic status indicator as

. Testing whether the raw score is demographic insensitive follows the procedure discussed in Appendix

with p-values derived using a Central Limit Theorem (Theorem

). As a result of this analysis we reject the hypothesis that the raw MMSE score is age insensitive, both among the impaired and the non-impaired groups. This means that we have significant evidence that an individual could have had a different probability of being classified as having cognitive impairment, given their age group and cognitive impairment status, had their sensitive attributes been different at the time of their MMSE assessment. This is in line with our knowledge that age affects MMSE scores regardless of the impairment status
[
51
,
24
]
. For further details of the analysis see Appendix

.

We now turn to age-corrected scores. We consider the z-score correction (

in the weak sense at a threshold of

, commonly used in practice
[

. In this analysis, we find evidence against the null hypothesis of demographic insensitivity. However, we only find significant evidence of demographic sensitivity amongst individuals with cognitive impairment, and it is possible that the age-correction is demographic insensitive
amongst individuals without impairment
. This result is consistent with our discussion in Section

.

5.
Conclusion

Our results contribute to the ongoing methodological debate about the use of demographic corrections. We present a general sufficient condition for the classification superiority of raw scores over demographically-corrected scores. This result is useful as it allows reasoning about the consequences of demographic correction on classification accuracy using subject-matter knowledge. For example, our result explain why age-correction can be detrimental in certain cognitive screening settings, justifying formally some criticisms that have been moved against demographic correction in cognitive screening
[
5
,
79
,
61
,
69
,
94
]
.

We have also discussed claims about demographically-corrected scores being more
“fair”
. Demographic correction is expected to result in equal false-positive rates across demographic groups, a property that some practitioners find desirable. Yet, we have shown that demographic corrections are not sufficient to ensure demographic insensitivity.

While in our work we have focused on cognitive screening, we highlight that our methods and results apply more broadly. Demographic corrections are ubiquitous in health sciences. For example, race corrections are debated in neurological diagnoses, illustrated by the legal controversy surrounding the National Football League case
[

Appendix A
An information-theoretic justification of the plausibility of demographic corrections worsening classification accuracy

It might seem “intuitive” that corrected scores cannot perform worse than raw scores, as they funnel more information into the scoring rule
[
69
]
. This intuition is erroneous, as shown in Section
2
, and it can be clarified by an information-theoretic argument. Consider the hypothetical case where we have access to the likelihood ratios of both
X
and
Z
, but also to the joint likelihood ratio of
(X,V)
. The likelihood-ratio test provides the classification rule with the highest TPR for any FPR (as it is the uniformly most powerful test thanks to the Neyman-Pearson lemma, see
[
91
, Thm. 10.30]
or
[
44
, Ch. 2.3.2]
for the connection to ROC analysis). As both
X
and
Z
are garblings (post-processings) of
(X,V)
, the Blackwell-Sherman-Stein theorem
[
6
]
implies that
(X,V)
has higher Blackwell order than both
X
and
Z
, meaning the joint likelihood ratio test for
(X,V)
cannot have lower TPR than that of
X
or
Z
at the same FPR. This is expected due to the optimal use of a larger amount of information. Nevertheless, even under Definition
1
,
X
and
Z
are not comparable in Blackwell’s order, meaning it is not trivial to ensure which one performs better. For an introduction to the Blackwell-Sherman-Stein theorem see
[
48
]
, or
[
84
, Ch. 3]
for further detail.

D.2.
Identification

To study whether a certain score

S^{v_{S}}\mid\{V=v,D=d\}

\mathbb{P}(S^{v_{S}}\leq s\mid V=v,D=d)
. Thus, we see that regardless of whether we target our strong or weak notion of demographic insensitivity, our target for identification and inference will be
\{\mathbb{P}(S^{v_{S}}\leq s\mid V=v,D=d)\colon s\in{\rm supp}(S)\}

V
and
V_{S}
were separately observed,
V_{S}
was randomized, and
V_{S}

for all

in the support of
S
, as the required conditions (consistency, exchangeability and positivity) would hold by design
[
30
]
. Thus, this is a single-world estimand which, in principle, can be identified from a randomized experiment if
V_{S}
partial isolation can be accepted.

When only data from experiments where there is no direct access to
V_{S}
(meaning
V_{S}\equiv V
) is available, identifying the distribution of
S^{v_{S}}\mid\{V=v,D=d\}
might not be guaranteed under the previous assumptions when

D.5.
Uniform consistency of the proposed estimators

The estimators we have introduced in Appendices
D.3
and
D.4
, are point-wise consistent, meaning that for each
s
in the support of
S
we can construct consistent estimators of
\mathbb{P}(S^{v_{S}}\leq s\mid V=v,D=d)
, provided the required assumptions hold. However, the strong notion of demographic insensitivity (Definition
3
) refers to a distribution as a whole, not to a specific probability. Therefore, when trying to estimate
\mathbb{P}(S^{v_{S}}\leq\cdot\mid V=v,D=d)
for a fixed
(v,v_{S},d)
, we would like to achieve uniform (in
L_{\infty}
) consistency

\sup_{s\in{\rm supp}(S)}|\widehat{\nu}(s)-\mathbb{P}(S^{v_{S}}\leq s\mid V=v,D=d)|\to 0,

in probability (or almost surely) w.r.t. the distribution of the observed data, for a chosen point-wise consistent estimator
\widehat{\nu}(\cdot)
.

Such uniform consistency is guaranteed if the score
S
has a finite discrete support, as is the case for almost all cognitive screening tests. In such a case the supreme over the support of
S
reduces to a maximum over a finite number of differences, all of which converge to zero in probability thanks to point-wise consistency, yielding uniform convergence in probability.

However, when
S
has an infinite support, e.g. an absolutely continuous distribution w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure, uniform consistency does not follow from point-wise consistency. We will slightly alter the estimators before introduced based on a data-splitting scheme
[
12
]
, and show that under standard conditions these estimators achieve uniform (in
L_{\infty}
) consistency.

D.5.1.
The simple estimator

The simple estimator
\widehat{\nu}_{\rm simple}(s)=\nu(\tilde{\mathbb{P}}_{n},s)
only uses the available data to fit the models, but does not relate to any empirical averages over the observed data, which is what concerns most of the empirical process theory
[
25
,
87
]
. As a statement about uniform convergence of the simple estimator relates only to the posited models
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}
and how they are fitted, we just state here the required uniform consistency

\sup_{s\in{\rm supp}(S)}|\widehat{\nu}_{\rm simple}(s)-\nu(\mathbb{P},s)|\to 0,

in probability w.r.t. the distribution of the observed data.

D.5.2.
Weighted estimators

In contrast with the simple estimator, all the weighted estimators we have introduced have the same form: data is used to fit certain functions, and then an empirical average is taken of the fitted functions
using the same data
. This double-fold use of the observed data complicates the analysis at hand. As a practical (and easily implementable) solution, we suggest data splitting
[
12
]
. For a dataset of size
n
we chose
I_{1}
randomly from the uniform distribution of all subsets of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
of size in
\{\lceil n/2\rceil,\lfloor n/2\rfloor\}
, and define
I_{2}:=\{1,\ldots,n\}\backslash I_{1}
. The posited models are fitted over
I_{1}
, and then the empirical average is taken over
I_{2}
. Under this framework, all weighted estimators that we consider are of the form

\widehat{\nu}_{\rm weighted}(s)=\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{I_{2}}\widehat{g}_{s},

where
g_{s}
is a function of a realization of the process satisfying that
\mathbb{P}g_{s}=\mathbb{E}[g_{s}]=\nu(\mathbb{P},s)
,
\widehat{g}_{s}
is an estimate of
\widehat{g}_{s}
using the data indexed by
I_{1}
, and
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{I_{2}}f
denotes an empirical average of
f
over the data indexed by
I_{2}
. Here we have borrowed common notation from the empirical process theory literature. See
[
25
]
for details.

The independence of the two splits of the original data under the i.i.d. assumption will allow us to achieve the desired uniform consistency under the following assumptions.

Assumption 12
.

The posited models and their estimation from the data in fold
I_{1}
are such that

\sup_{s\in{\rm supp}(S)}\mathbb{P}|\widehat{g}_{s}-g_{s}|\to 0,

in probability w.r.t. the distribution of the observed data in fold
I_{1}
.

Statements of the form
\mathbb{P}\widehat{g}_{s}
mean that the expectation is taken over the distribution of a new independent realization of
\mathbb{P}
, i.e. conditional on all other randomness. Assumption
12
states that in expectation
\widehat{g}_{s}
approximates
g_{s}
uniformly in probability. It is therefore an statement about the posited models and how they are fitted, which strengthens the point-wise consistency we discussed in Appendix
D.3
. We will also need to make an assumption concerning the outer empirical average.

Assumption 13
.

There exists a function class
\mathcal{F}
such that
\{\widehat{g}_{s}\colon s\in{\rm supp}(S)\}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
almost surely w.r.t. the joint distribution of the data in fold
I_{1}
. Furthermore,
\mathcal{F}
is
\mathbb{P}
-Glivenko-Cantelli
[
87
, Chap. 2.1]
.

This essentially states that the class of functions
\{\widehat{g}_{s}\colon s\in{\rm supp}(S)\}
almost surely belongs to a class which is “regular w.r.t.
\mathbb{P}
”. There are many characterizations of Glivenko-Cantelli classes, involving bracketing numbers and VC-dimensions. See
[
87
, Chap. 2.1]
or
[
25
, Chap. 9]
for details. Nevertheless, as stated in
[
19
]
and references therein, Assumption
13
is standard, and thus we present it as such.

Under these assumptions, we are able to conclude a uniform consistency result.

Theorem 11
.

Under Assumptions
12
and
13
it holds that

\sup_{s\in{\rm supp}(S)}\left|\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{I_{2}}\widehat{g}_{s}-\mathbb{P}g_{s}\right|\to 0,

in probability w.r.t. the joint distribution of the observed data.

Proof.

By the triangle inequality

\sup_{s\in{\rm supp}(S)}\left|\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{I_{2}}\widehat{g}_{s}-\mathbb{P}g_{s}\right|\leq\underbrace{\sup_{s\in{\rm supp}(S)}\left|\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{I_{2}}\widehat{g}_{s}-\mathbb{P}\widehat{g}_{s}\right|}_{(I)}+\underbrace{\sup_{s\in{\rm supp}(S)}\left|\mathbb{P}(\widehat{g}_{s}-g_{s})\right|}_{(II)}.

By Assumption
12
we have that

(II)\leq\sup_{s\in{\rm supp}(S)}\mathbb{P}|\widehat{g}_{s}-g_{s}|\to 0,

in probability. Now for
(I)
, conditional on the data in fold
I_{1}
, Assumption
13
yields

(I)\leq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{I_{2}}-\mathbb{P})f\right|\to 0

in probability w.r.t. the joint distribution of the data in fold
I_{2}
. This means that for all
\varepsilon>0

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{s\in{\rm supp}(S)}\left|\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{I_{2}}\widehat{g}_{s}-\mathbb{P}\widehat{g}_{s}\right|>\varepsilon\mid\mathcal{T}_{1}\right)\to 0,

where
\mathcal{T}_{1}
is the
\sigma
-algebra induced by the data in fold
I_{1}
. Then by the law of total expectations and the dominated convergence theorem

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{s\in{\rm supp}(S)}\left|\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{I_{2}}\widehat{g}_{s}-\mathbb{P}\widehat{g}_{s}\right|>\varepsilon\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{s\in{\rm supp}(S)}\left|\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{I_{2}}\widehat{g}_{s}-\mathbb{P}\widehat{g}_{s}\right|>\varepsilon\mid\mathcal{T}_{1}\right)\right)\to 0

as
n\to\infty
. As a result,
(I)
converges to zero in probability, which concludes the proof.
∎

Thus we see how under the corresponding assumptions for point-wise consistency, together with Assumptions
12
and
13
for the specific form of the
g_{s}
functions given by each estimator, we achieve uniform consistency of our weighted estimators after performing the data-splitting procedure before mentioned.

D.5.3.
The one-step estimator introduced in Appendix
D.3

Lastly, we discuss the one-step estimator, constructed in the case when
\mathcal{V}
is discrete. Recall that this estimator was of the form

\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}\nu^{1}(\tilde{\mathbb{P}}_{n},s)+\nu(\tilde{\mathbb{P}}_{n},s),

where the models
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
consistently estimated posited models
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}
, which could be miss-specified as per Theorem
9
. As we also saw in the proof of this theorem,
\nu(\tilde{\mathbb{P}}_{n},s)
is a point-wise consistent estimator of
\nu(\tilde{\mathbb{P}},s)
(it is the simple estimator with potentially miss-specified models) and
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}\nu^{1}(\tilde{\mathbb{P}}_{n},s)
was point-wise consistent for
\nu({\mathbb{P}},s)-\nu(\tilde{\mathbb{P}},s)
.

For the simple estimator addend, as we did in Appendix
D.5.1
we just assume that the (potentially miss-specified) posited models and their estimation methods satisfy the Glivenko-Cantelli property

\sup_{s\in{\rm supp}(S)}\left|\nu(\tilde{\mathbb{P}}_{n},s)-\nu(\tilde{\mathbb{P}},s)\right|\to 0

in probability. For the correction provided by the influence function, we run into the same issue as in Appendix
D.5.2
: we use the full data to fit the models
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
than to take the outer empirical average. Again, to overcome this issue we propose using again the data-splitting method introduced before. Thus, we define
g_{s}=\nu^{1}(\tilde{\mathbb{P}},s)
and
\widehat{g}_{s}=\nu^{1}(\tilde{\mathbb{P}}_{I_{1}},s)
. Under Assumptions
12
and
13
, Theorem
11
implies that

\sup_{s\in{\rm supp}(S)}\left|\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{I_{2}}\nu^{1}(\tilde{\mathbb{P}}_{I_{1}},s)-(\nu({\mathbb{P}},s)-\nu(\tilde{\mathbb{P}},s))\right|\to 0,

in probability. Thus, we see how data-splitting in the influence function correction allows us to achieve, under assumptions on the posited models and their estimation, uniform consistency of the one-step estimator.

The double-robustness property studied in
9
extends as well to the uniform case, as we have shown that

\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{I_{2}}\nu^{1}(\tilde{\mathbb{P}}_{I_{1}},s)+\nu(\tilde{\mathbb{P}}_{n},s)

is uniformly consistent for
\nu(\mathbb{P},s)
despite the models being miss-specified as allowed by said theorem.

D.6.
Testing for demographic insensitivity

We have so far seen that under appropriate conditions we can identify and consistently estimate the counterfactual distribution required to assess whether a score is insensitive to certain demographics. Yet, Definitions
3
-
4
require the equality (point-wise or in distribution) across the support of
(V,D)
. Therefore, if we consider the individual null hypothesis in the strong case

\mathcal{H}_{0,(v,d)}^{(w,w^{\prime})}\colon S^{w}\mid\{V=v,D=d\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}S^{w^{\prime}}\mid\{V=v,D=d\},

or in the weak case for a fixed threshold
s

(13)

\mathcal{H}_{0,(v,d)}^{(w,w^{\prime})}\colon\mathbb{P}(S^{w}\leq s\mid V=v,D=d)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{=}}\mathbb{P}(S^{w^{\prime}}\leq s\mid V=v,D=d),

the global null hypothesis of demographic insensitivity can be expressed as

(14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{0}=\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathcal{V}\\
d\in\{0,1\}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{H}_{0,(v,d)}=\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathcal{V}\\
d\in\{0,1\}\end{subarray}}\underbrace{\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}(w,w^{\prime})\in\mathcal{V}^{2}\\
w\neq w^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{H}_{0,(v,d)}^{(w,w^{\prime})}}_{\mathcal{H}_{0,(v,d)}},

which is an intersection hypothesis. Assume that
V
has a discrete finite support, and that we are able to compute valid p-values for each of the individual null hypotheses
\mathcal{H}_{0,(v,d)}^{(w,w^{\prime})}
. In the strong case, these could come from a sup-norm or Cramér von Mises type test based on the uniform consistency discussed in Appendix
D.5
. The main goal is then to combine these p-values to obtain a valid p-value for the global intersection null
\mathcal{H}_{0}
. Without making any assumptions on the dependence structure of the individual p-values, a valid approach could be a (weighted) Bonferroni procedure
[
92
, Sec. 11.1.1]
. If we are also interesting in valid testing for the individual nulls
\mathcal{H}_{0,(v,d)}^{(w,w^{\prime})}
or the intermediates
\mathcal{H}_{0,(v,d)}
, the closed testing procedure
[
52
]
can be used to maintain Type-I error control.

When
\mathcal{V}
is infinite, the former procedures cannot be applied, as we have an infinite collection of individual hypotheses. In this case (rarely found in practice) the procedure described in
[
7
]
can be applied to test the global intersection null when
\mathcal{V}
is not finite while ensuring Type-I error control.

Appendix E
Classification accuracy and demographic insensitivity in a parametric example

Consider a particular case of the model introduced in Remark
2
, where
V
is a univariate random variable with an absolutely continuous distribution w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure,
V\sim\mathbb{P}_{V}
. Furthermore, assume that
\mathbb{P}_{V}
supported on an open connected interval, and consider the following hierarchical model

\begin{cases}D\mid V\sim Ber(\expit(\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}V))\\
X\mid D,V\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{0}+\mu_{1}V+\mu_{2}D,\sigma^{2})\end{cases},

where
Ber(p)
denotes a Bernoulli distribution with success probability
p\in(0,1)
,
\sigma>0
and
\expit(x)=(1+\exp(-x))^{-1}
. One could then represent
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mu_{0}+\mu_{1}V+\mu_{2}D+\varepsilon
, with
\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
independent of
D
and
V
. Then, under the correction in Equation (
1
), we have

Z\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\frac{\mu_{2}D+\varepsilon}{\sigma},

meaning that Assumption
2
is satisfied by design:
Z\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}V\mid D=0
.

E.1.
Superiority of the raw score

We now consider Assumption
1
, for which

LR_{X\mid V}(x\mid v)=\frac{\phi_{\sigma}(x-\mu_{0}-\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}v)}{\phi_{\sigma}(x-\mu_{0}-\mu_{1}v)}=\exp(-\sigma^{-2}(\mu_{2}^{2}/2-\mu_{2}(x-\mu_{0}-\mu_{1}v))),

where
\phi_{\sigma}(\cdot)=\phi(\cdot/\sigma)/\sigma
, and
\phi(\cdot)
is the density of a standard normal random variable. Therefore

\partial_{x}\log(LR_{X\mid V}(x\mid v))=\mu_{2}/\sigma^{2},

meaning Assumption
1
is satisfied in our setting if and only if
\mu_{2}<0
.

Lastly we study Assumption
3
. Denote
\pi(v)=\expit(\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}v)
and
\pi(x,v)=\mathbb{P}(D=1\mid X=x,V=v)
. One can easily check that

\displaystyle\logit(\pi(x,v))

\displaystyle=\logit(\pi(v))+\log(LR_{X\mid V}(x\mid v))

\displaystyle=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}v-\frac{\mu_{2}^{2}/2-\mu_{2}(x-\mu_{0}-\mu_{1}v)}{\sigma^{2}},

where
\logit(p)=\log(p/(1-p))
for
p\in(0,1)
. This means that

\partial_{v}\logit(\pi(x,v))=\beta_{1}-\frac{\mu_{1}\mu_{2}}{\sigma^{2}}.

Furthermore, the other quantity of interest is

\mathbb{P}(X\leq x\mid V=v,D=0)=\Phi\left(\frac{x-\mu_{0}-\mu_{1}v}{\sigma}\right),

where
\Phi
is the standard normal c.d.f. This leads to

\partial_{v}\mathbb{P}(X\leq x\mid V=v,D=0)=-\mu_{1}\phi_{\sigma}(x-\mu_{0}-\mu_{1}v).

The co-monotonicity required by Assumption
3
translates in our case to the two derivatives we have computed having constant and equal sign. Hence, Assumption
3
boils down to

(15)

\sign\left(\beta_{1}-\frac{\mu_{1}\mu_{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)=\sign(-\mu_{1}),

where we define the sign function as
\sign(x)=I(x>0)-I(x<0)
. As we mentioned in the main text, we can see how condition (
15
) holds if and only if Assumption
3
holds
for all

\alpha\in(0,1)
.

E.2.
Superiority of the corrected score

We now check the conditions of Theorem
2
. Firstly

LR_{Z|V}(z|v)=\dfrac{\phi(z-\mu_{2})}{\phi(z)}=\exp(z\mu_{2}-\mu_{2}^{2}/2),

and therefore Assumption
1
is satisfied for
Z
iff
\mu_{2}<0
, which was also the condition for this assumption to be satisfied for
X
, as we already mentioned in the main text.

We now turn our attention to Assumption
4
. Analogously computations to those done in Appendix
E.1
yield that

\partial_{v}\logit(\mathbb{P}(D=1\mid Z=z,V=v))=\beta_{1},

meaning Assumption
4
is satisfied if and only if

(16)

\sign\left(\beta_{1}\right)=\sign(\mu_{1}).

Under the assumption that
\mu_{2}<0
, Equations (
15
) and (
16
) are simultaneously satisfied if and only if
\beta_{1}=\mu_{1}=0
. In this case
(D,X,Z)\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}V
and trivially the ROC curves for
X
and
Z
are equal, in accordance with Corollary
2
.

E.3.
Demographic insensitivity

We now turn to discussing whether raw and corrected scores are insensitive to demographics in our simplified parametric setting. When comparing this to the formalism introduced in Section
3
and Appendix
D
, we see that we are in a setting where
L=W=\emptyset
, also discussed in Section
3.2
. In this case, a score
S
satisfies demographic insensitivity if and only if
S\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}V\mid D
. We see how in this example the corrected z-score
Z
insensitive to demographics.

The raw score
X
would be insensitive to demographics if and only if
\mu_{1}=0
. This corresponds to the case of the absence of the edge
V\to X
in the system’s causal DAG. This implies
X\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}V\mid D
, and thus under any Type I correction raw and corrected scores have the same marginal classification accuracy (see Lemma
2
and Corollary
1
). In our example
Z\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(X-\mu_{0})/\sigma
, and we see how the previous result holds as
Z
is a strictly increasing transformation of
X
.

To summarize, we see how in our parametric example the corrected z-score is always demographic insensitive, even in the cases where the raw score has better classification accuracy. Furthermore, if the raw score satisfies demographic insensitivity then the correction is irrelevant towards classification accuracy, as both raw and corrected scores are demographic insensitive.

Theorem 12

F.2.
The demographic insensitivity study of Section
4.3

F.2.1.
General testing set-up

As explained in Section
4.3
, as all variables in the system have discrete support, we estimate the counterfactual probabilities of interest using the simple estimator, where all factual probabilities are estimated by sample averages. We can expand the target probabilities, similarly to what we did in Section
F.1
, to clarify the dependence with the cell probabilities as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Y^{v_{S}}=1\mid V=v,D=d)=g_{(v_{S},v,d)}(\bm{p})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l,w}p(1,l,w,v_{S},d)\cdot\frac{\sum_{y=0}^{1}p(y,l,w,v,d)}{\sum_{y=0}^{1}p(y,l,w,v_{S},d)}\cdot\frac{1}{\sum_{l^{\prime},w^{\prime}}\sum_{y^{\prime}=0}^{1}p(y^{\prime},l^{\prime},w^{\prime},v,d)},

where we have defined
Y=I(S\leq t)
for a score
S
and a fixed threshold
t
, the cell probabilities
p(y,l,w,v,d)=\mathbb{P}(Y=y,L=l,W=w,V=v,D=d)
and
\bm{p}
denotes the vector with all these cell probabilities.

As explained in Section
4.3
, and analogously to Section
F.1
, the simple estimator is a plug-in one based on empirical averages of the cell probabilities in
\bm{p}
. Now denote by
\bm{\theta}=\bm{g}(\bm{p})
the vector of all counterfactual probabilities of interest, and by
\bm{\eta}=\logit(\bm{\theta})
the vector of these logit-probabilities, where we understand the logit map applied component-wise. Our analysis is based on the following central limit theorem.

Theorem 13
.

The plug-in estimator
\widehat{\bm{\eta}}=\logit(\bm{g}(\widehat{\bm{p}}))
of the logit-probabilities of interest for the demographic insensitivity comparison satisfies

\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\bm{\eta}}-\bm{\eta})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\to}}\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\Sigma_{\bm{\eta}}),

where
\Sigma_{\bm{\eta}}=HG(\diag(\bm{p})-\bm{p}\bm{p}^{\prime})G^{\prime}H
. The matrix
G
is the Jacobian matrix of the map
\bm{g}
evaluated at
\bm{p}
, with entries given by

\displaystyle\partial_{p(y^{\prime},l^{\prime},w^{\prime},v^{\prime},d^{\prime})}g_{(v_{S},v,d)}(\bm{p})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{1}{b(v,d)}(I(l^{\prime}=l,w^{\prime}=w)\frac{p(1,l,w,v_{S},d)}{a(l,w,v_{S},d)}-g_{(v_{S},v,d)}(\bm{p}))&d=d^{\prime},v_{S}\neq v^{\prime}=v\\
\frac{I(l^{\prime}=l,w^{\prime}=w)}{b(v,d)}\frac{a(l,w,v,d)}{a(l,w,v_{S},d)}\left(I(y^{\prime}=1)-\frac{p(1,l,w,v_{S},d)}{a(l,w,v_{S},d)}\right)&d=d^{\prime},v_{S}=v^{\prime}\neq v\\
\frac{1}{b(v,d)}(I(y^{\prime}=1)-g_{(v,v,d)}(\bm{p}))&d=d^{\prime},v_{S}=v^{\prime}=v\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases},

where we have further defined

\displaystyle a(l,w,v,d)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(L=l,V=v,D=d,W=w)=\sum_{y=0}^{1}p(y,l,w,v,d),

\displaystyle b(v,d)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(V=v,D=d)=\sum_{l^{\prime},w^{\prime}}\sum_{y^{\prime}=0}^{1}p(y^{\prime},l^{\prime},w^{\prime},v,d).

Finally, the
H
matrix is the (diagonal) Jacobian matrix of the component-wise
\logit
map evaluated in
\bm{\theta}
.

Proof.

We begin by noting that, as the cell probabilities are estimated by sample means, the multinomial CLT
[
86
, Ex. 2.18]
tells us that

\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\bm{p}}-\bm{p})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\to}}\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\Sigma_{\bm{p}}),

where
\Sigma_{\bm{p}}=\diag(\bm{p})-\bm{p}\bm{p}^{\prime}
. By the (functional)
\delta
-method
[
86
, Thms. 3.1 & 20.8]
, we have that

\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\theta})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\to}}\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},G\Sigma_{\bm{p}}G^{\prime}),

where
G
is
\bm{g}
’s Jacobian matrix
G=D_{\bm{p}}\bm{g}(\bm{p})
. Lastly, applying the component-wise logit map and again the
\delta
-method we conclude that

\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\bm{\eta}}-\bm{\eta})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\to}}\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},HG\Sigma_{\bm{p}}G^{\prime}H),

where
H=\diag(h(\bm{\theta}))
,
h(x)=1/(x(1-x))
, and we understand
h(\bm{\theta})
as applied component-wise.

It only remains to compute
G
. To that extent, we begin by writing each component of
\bm{g}
in terms of the
a
and
b
functions as

\bm{g}_{(v_{S},v,d)}(\bm{p})=\frac{1}{b(v,d)}\sum_{l,w}p(1,l,w,v_{S},d)\frac{a(l,w,v,d)}{a(l,w,v_{S},d)}.

Fix a
p(y^{\prime},l^{\prime},w^{\prime},v^{\prime},d^{\prime})
cell. Then
\bm{g}_{(v_{S},v,d)}(\bm{p})
only involves
p(y^{\prime},l^{\prime},w^{\prime},v,d)
and
p(y^{\prime},l^{\prime},w^{\prime},v_{S},d)
cells, so we can restrict to the case
d^{\prime}=d
and
v^{\prime}\in\{v,v_{S}\}
. We then consider three cases. First, if
v_{S}\neq v^{\prime}=v
we have that
p(y^{\prime},l^{\prime},w^{\prime},v,d)
enters
\bm{g}_{(v_{S},v,d)}(\bm{p})
in
b(v,d)
and
a(l,w,v,d)
, yielding by the chain rule

\partial_{p(y^{\prime},l^{\prime},w^{\prime},v^{\prime},d)}\bm{g}_{(v_{S},v,d)}(\bm{p})=-\frac{\bm{g}_{(v_{S},v,d)}(\bm{p})}{b(v,d)}+\frac{I(l^{\prime}=l,w^{\prime}=w)}{b(v,d)}\frac{p(1,l,w,v_{S},d)}{a(l,w,v_{S},d)}.

Second, if
v_{S}=v^{\prime}\neq v
,
p(y^{\prime},l^{\prime},w^{\prime},v_{S},d)
appears in
a(l,w,v_{S},d)
and potentially in
p(1,l,w,v_{S},d)
if
y^{\prime}=1
, yielding

\displaystyle\partial_{p(y^{\prime},l^{\prime},w^{\prime},v^{\prime},d)}\bm{g}_{(v_{S},v,d)}(\bm{p})=

\displaystyle\frac{I(y^{\prime}=1,l^{\prime}=l,w^{\prime}=w)}{b(v,d)}\frac{a(l,w,v,d)}{a(l,w,v_{S},d)}

\displaystyle-\frac{I(l^{\prime}=l,w^{\prime}=w)}{b(v,d)}p(1,l,w,v_{S},d)\frac{a(l,w,v,d)}{a(l,w,v_{S},d)^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{I(l^{\prime}=l,w^{\prime}=w)}{b(v,d)}\frac{a(l,w,v,d)}{a(l,w,v_{S},d)}\left[I(y^{\prime}=1)-\frac{p(1,l,w,v_{S},d)}{a(l,w,v_{S},d)}\right]

and lastly, when
v_{S}=v^{\prime}=v
, we can first rewrite

\bm{g}_{(v,v,d)}(\bm{p})=\frac{1}{b(v,d)}\sum_{l,w}p(1,l,w,v,d),

what leads to

\displaystyle\partial_{p(y^{\prime},l^{\prime},w^{\prime},v,d)}\bm{g}_{(v,v,d)}(\bm{p})=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{b(v,d)^{2}}\sum_{l,w}p(1,l,w,v,d)+\frac{I(y^{\prime}=1)}{b(v,d)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{b(v,d)}\left[I(y^{\prime}=1)-\bm{g}_{(v,v,d)}(\bm{p})\right].

This concludes the proof.
∎

Each entry of
\Sigma_{\eta}
as given in Theorem
13
can be consistently estimated using the plug-in principle and the sample average estimate
\widehat{\bm{p}}
of the vector of cell probabilities
\bm{p}
. As each individual null hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{0,(v,d)}^{(w,w^{\prime})}
is equivalent to
\bm{\eta}_{(w,v,d)}-\bm{\eta}_{(w^{\prime},v,d)}=0
, an asymptotically exact p-value for this individual null is

2\left(1-\Phi\left(\sqrt{\frac{n}{\bm{\delta}_{(w,w^{\prime},v,d)}^{\prime}\widehat{\Sigma_{\eta}}\bm{\delta}_{(w,w^{\prime},v,d)}}}|\widehat{\bm{\eta}}^{\prime}\bm{\delta}_{(w,w^{\prime},v,d)}|\right)\right),

where
\bm{\delta}_{(w,w^{\prime},v,d)}=\bm{e}(w,v,d)-\bm{e}(w^{\prime},v,d)
and
\bm{e}(v_{S},v,d)
is the “one-hot” vector indicator of component
(v_{S},v,d)
. This p-value is symmetric in
(w,w^{\prime})
, as so is the individual null
\mathcal{H}_{0,(v,d)}^{(w,w^{\prime})}
.

F.2.3.
