Total knee arthroplasty in patients with marked coronal femoral bowing deformities: A systematic review
Authors: Hsiang-An Lee, Shang-Wen Tsai, Hsuan-Hsiao Ma, Tsan-Wen Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectives:To review existing strategies for managing coronal femoral bowing deformity in total knee arthroplasty (TKA) and assess their effectiveness in enhancing postoperative mechanical alignment (MA) and the femoral component positioning.Materials and Methods:A systematic literature review was performed across PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar up to August 2025. It included studies on surgical techniques for coronal femoral bowing in primary TKA, focusing on pre- and postoperative radiographic outcomes.Results:Thirteen studies were included in the final analysis. Individualized distal femoral valgus correction angles (VCAs) are more effective than fixed angles in restoring MA. Lateralizing the femoral entry point enables deeper, straighter insertion of the intramedullary rod (IM), resulting in better MA and more precise component placement. Intra-articular bone resection can be an effective alternative when lateralization is not feasible; however, it is limited by the severity of the deformity and technical complexity. Computer-assisted surgery (CAS) improves alignment accuracy and reduces outliers. Robotic-assisted TKA offers potential advantages but lacks specific data. Although patient-specific instrumentation has been suggested to enhance alignment in TKA, current evidence indicates no significant benefit over traditional methods. Extramedullary femoral guides provide more accurate alignment and lower outlier rates.Conclusion:Coronal femoral bowing is common among Asian patients and influences TKA outcomes. A full-length standing scanogram of the lower extremities is vital for preoperative planning. Depending on the patient’s condition, combining an IM cutting device, individualized VCA, lateralization of the entry point, intra-articular resection, and CAS may improve alignment and component positioning.

INTRODUCTION

Coronal femoral bowing deformities are common in Asian patients [1], sometimes associated with osteomalacia and osteoporosis [2]. When bearing weight, the femoral shaft experiences compressive and bending forces. In patients with osteoporosis and a thin femoral cortex, buckling may occur, potentially leading to bone remodeling and bowing deformities [3]. Coronal femoral bowing deformities pose challenges for orthopedic surgeons, especially during traumatic orthopedic surgeries and total knee arthroplasty (TKA) [4].

Identifying coronal femoral bowing deformities from a patient’s clinical appearance and knee radiographs can be challenging and typically requires a full-length standing scanogram of the lower extremities [1]. Although several studies have suggested that a preoperative full-length standing scanogram of the lower extremities is not necessary before TKA [56], it is recommended for Asian patients [1789]. A preoperative full-length standing scanogram of the lower extremities offers valuable insights into the severity of arthritis in the hip, knee, and ankle joints, as well as any extra-articular deformities of the femur and tibia that need correction to achieve a neutral mechanical axis (MA) after surgery [Figure 1]. In TKA, coronal bowing of the femur affects the relationship between the MA and the anatomical axis (AA) of the lower limbs. This deformity increases the valgus correction angle (VCA) of the distal femur, leading to improper positioning of the femoral component and postoperative MA, which can cause a progressive varus shift in MA and reduce an implant’s longevity [101112].

A full-length standing scanogram of the lower extremities. This scanogram provides valuable information both before and after surgery. Based on Mullaji’s method [2] and Mullaji’s criteria [14], coronal femoral bowing >5° is classified as a significant deformity. Three points were marked on the femoral shaft: Fp, which bisects the shaft at the lower junction of the lesser trochanter; Fd, located 10 cm proximal to the knee joint along the shaft; and Fc, which is midway between Fp and Fd on the shaft. The coronal femoral bowing angle is measured between the lines Fp–Fc and Fd–Fc

Coronal femoral bowing deformities are associated with knee osteoarthritis and tend to worsen as the condition progresses [3]. Recently, there has been increased interest in how these deformities impact TKA [101112]. However, limited evidence is available because a wide range of surgical techniques are used to address significant coronal femoral bowing during TKA. Examining specific treatment approaches could help us develop more reliable methods for these challenging cases. Few literature reviews on this topic have been published so far. Therefore, this study summarizes and thoroughly reviews existing research on TKA in patients with knee osteoarthritis combined with pronounced coronal femoral bowing.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Search strategy

We performed a search of PubMed, the Cochrane Library, and Embase for studies published up to August 2025, using various combinations of the following search terms: “femoral bowing” AND “total knee arthroplasty” OR “total knee replacement.” Google Scholar was also used to screen relevant studies. We manually searched each article’s reference list to identify additional studies. There were no restrictions on the type of instrumentation used (i.e., intramedullary [IM] guide system, extramedullary [EM] guide system, computer-assisted surgery [CAS], or patient-specific instrumentation [PSI]), the type of implant, or the surgical approach. As a summary of existing published research, this study was exempted from institutional review board review. This systematic review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, and its protocol was registered in Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/58anj).

Selection criteria

Two independent reviewers searched and assessed all titles and abstracts, then screened the full texts of identified studies. Studies relevant to surgical methods for coronal femoral bowing deformities were included. We excluded (1) studies that did not treat patients with primary TKA; (2) studies that did not report radiographic outcomes before and after TKA; (3) studies that only evaluated the influence of coronal femoral bowing deformities rather than surgical procedures for treating these deformities; and lastly, we excluded (4) non-English studies, stand-alone abstracts, meeting presentations, commentaries, review articles, and letters.

RESULTS

Study identification

Our search strategy initially identified 321 records. We eliminated 126 duplicate entries and 155 studies after reviewing their titles and abstracts. Subsequently, the full texts of 40 articles were evaluated for eligibility. We further excluded studies that examined the impact of coronal femoral bowing deformities on atypical femoral fractures (n = 12), those unrelated to surgical procedures (n = 5), non-English publications (n = 6), studies comparing outcomes of neutral-aligned versus non-neutral-aligned TKA (n = 3), and one revision TKA study. The final set of 13 articles was deemed suitable for inclusion and analyzed [Figure 2].

Characteristics of the included studies

Of the 13 articles, 10 were retrospective studies, and 3 were high-quality randomized clinical trials. One radiographic study retrospectively examined the impact of coronal femoral bowing deformities on postoperative limb MA and component alignment [13]. Two retrospective case series reported effective methods for properly restoring the MA and femoral component placement in the coronal plane [1415]. Seven retrospective cohort studies compared the effects of different surgical techniques [16171819202122]. Individual distal femoral valgus correction angle

In TKA, femoral cutting planes should be perpendicular to the MA of the lower limbs in the coronal plane. The distal femoral VCA is designed to adjust the IM femoral alignment guide to achieve a perpendicular relationship [7]. Although most surgeons use a VCA of 5° or 6°, an individualized VCA can provide a higher likelihood of achieving a perpendicular relationship between the cutting planes and MA of the lower limbs [26].

Coronal femoral bowing alters the angular relationship between the AA and MA of the femur, making the consistent use of a 5° or 6° VCA questionable [11618]. In 2015, Lee et al. [1] conducted a radiographic study that examined the variability of VCA in ethnic Asians with end-stage osteoarthritis and genu varum deformities. They observed that the VCA ranged from about 10° up to a maximum of 14° in patients with coronal femoral bowing deformities [1]. Under these conditions, using a VCA of 5° or 6° consistently could lead to inaccurate placement of femoral components and poor reconstruction of the MA. Hattori et al. [27] employed a 3D preoperative planning software to evaluate their patient with knee osteoarthritis who underwent primary TKA, finding that the main factors independently associated with the VCA for the IM rod were the femur bowing angle (FBA) (β: 0.75). Lee et al. [28] concluded that using an IM guide to achieve accurate distal femoral resection is feasible when the femoral VCA is between 4° and 8°, and the distal bowing is less than or equal to ±1°.

In a prospective randomized study, Shi et al. [23] compared individual VCAs and fixed VCAs in 133 patients with coronal femoral bowing deformities undergoing primary TKA. They observed that fixed VCAs could lead to unacceptable planning errors and incorrect postoperative limb alignment, whereas personalized VCAs achieved satisfactory radiographic results. However, most current IM femoral guiding systems offer a limited VCA range (3° to 8°), which does not allow sufficient preoperative planning to address coronal femoral bowing deformities. Consequently, modifications to existing IM femoral guides are necessary to specify the exact VCA and enhance TKA outcomes [1618].

Lateralization of the femoral entry point

IM femoral alignment guides are currently the most popular method for performing distal femoral resection in TKA. Patients with coronal femoral bowing deformities have a higher VCA, which means that existing cutting devices may be unable to meet the required angle. In comparison with the traditional entry point, an IM femoral rod can be placed deeper into the femoral isthmus by lateralization of the femoral entry point, resulting in a more stable IM rod and smaller VCA. Thippanna and Kumar [15] demonstrated that a lateralized femoral entry point helps allow for straighter passage of an IM rod, and that this resulted in good MA and component alignment in 80 of 88 knees (90.9%).

While lateralization of the femoral entry point aims to improve coronal alignment, it can introduce errors in the sagittal plane. Stotter et al. [29] demonstrated, using a 3D model, that a 5-mm anterior or posterior shift altered the femoral axis–rod angle by approximately 2°. Without considering sagittal depth and orientation, lateralization increases the risk of mispositioning the femoral component. Excessive lateralization may exaggerate the valgus cut, leading to MA overcorrection or femoral–tibial malalignment. Since femoral component alignment significantly impacts implant survival, both varus and valgus malalignments are associated with higher failure rates than neutral alignment (2–8° valgus) [15].

Intra-articular bone resection technique

If lateralization of the femoral entry point is not feasible, intra-articular resection remains an effective alternative. The intra-articular bone resection method, as described by Wang and Wang [3031], which employs an IM rod with a modified starting hole in the femoral condyle, shows promising results in patients with post-traumatic extra-articular deformities of the femoral shaft. These positive outcomes indicate that this technique can help achieve an ideal MA of the lower limbs during TKA. However, it is not suitable for patients with a deformity angle exceeding 20° or for cutting planes involving collateral ligaments. In addition, intra-articular resection is a technically challenging procedure. In a prior retrospective study, Huang et al. [20] compared intra-articular resection with CAS and found that only 72.9% of patients achieved an ideally reconstructed MA of the lower limbs with intra-articular resection. The primary reason for this was incomplete insertion of the IM rod, which led to subsequent errors in distal femur resection and further contributed to improper positioning of the femoral component and postoperative MA.

Computer-assisted surgery

CAS has been used for over 20 years and reportedly offers more accurate bone cuts, better component placement across the coronal, sagittal, and axial planes, improved restoration of coronal limb alignment and joint line, and reduced gap asymmetry [73233]. CAS has also been suggested as a real-time, quantitative, and objective checking system in orthopedic surgery [33], allowing surgeons to adjust soft tissue balance and achieve optimal knee stability in extension, flexion, and mid-range flexion through immediate image feedback [73233].

Kobayashi et al. [24] conducted a prospective, randomized study to evaluate the radiographic benefits of CAS in 17 patients with coronal femoral bowing deformities. Their results indicated that CAS allowed for more accurate placement of the femoral component and resulted in fewer outliers in postoperative MA. Similar findings were reported by Huang et al. [16], Lee et al. [18], and Nam et al. [22], who observed more outliers among patients with femoral bowing who underwent conventional TKA. In contrast, no significant differences in postoperative MA or femoral component positioning were found in patients with bowed femurs who underwent CAS-TKA.

Regarding the intra-articular resection technique, CAS has been shown to more reliably achieve proper femoral component positioning and restoration of limb alignment [19]. In addition, for patients with severe extra-articular deformities, CAS is advantageous for use in both staged and simultaneous osteotomy procedures performed alongside TKA [3435]. However, it should be noted that a greater degree of varus deformity was linked to a higher rate of outliers in postoperative mechanical coronal alignment of 0° ±3°, which was believed to result from incorrect registration of ambiguous bony markers with varus deformities [36].

Robotic-assisted surgery in TKA has been developed for many years and is generally regarded as a form of CAS. When using robotic-assisted surgery, templating is performed before the procedure, eliminating the need for an IM cutting device, femoral entry point lateralization, and intra-articular bone resection techniques. However, current research does not report on the use of robotic-assisted TKA in patients with significant coronal femoral bowing deformities.

Patient-specific instrumentation

PSI advocates highlight benefits such as improved component alignment and fewer outliers. ALShammari et al. [25] performed a randomized controlled trial to compare PSI with lateralization of the femoral entry point for IM guidance in primary TKA in patients with bilateral severe femoral bowing (>5°). They concluded that there were no significant differences in postoperative hip-knee-ankle (HKA) angle, rotational alignment of the femoral components, or component orientation, except for the sagittal alignment of the femoral component.

However, PSI does not include intraoperative verification, which means mispositioned jigs might only be identified through postoperative imaging. Errors in tibial jigs can be as high as 60%–70%. Such inaccuracies, especially in tibial slope and femoral rotation, can affect alignment, soft-tissue balancing, and implant stability, thereby increasing the likelihood of pain, instability, and early failure compared to navigation-assisted systems [37].

Extramedullary guide femoral system

Currently, tibia resections primarily use EM cutting guides. Femur resections, however, have multiple options, including IM guides, EM guides, CAS, and PSI. Because IM techniques rely heavily on the femoral medullary cavity structure, they may not achieve precise femur resections in patients with extra-articular deformities, such as coronal femoral bowing.

In a retrospective study, Wang et al. [21] compared the IM and EM guide groups and found notable differences among patients with a coronal femoral bowing angle of 10° or greater. Specifically, there was a significant difference in postoperative HKA (4.51° in the IM group vs. 2.23° in the EM group, P < 0.001), femoral component alignment angle (86.84° in the IM group vs. 88.46° in the EM group, P = 0.001), and tibial component alignment angle (88.69° in the IM group vs. 89.81° in the EM group, P = 0.003) between the two groups. The IM group showed a higher rate of outliers for postoperative HKA and femoral components compared to the EM group. Using the EM cutting system in TKA may enable more accurate distal femoral resection and improve overall limb and femoral component alignment.

While EM guides rely on external landmarks such as the anterior superior iliac spine and the femoral shaft axis, these landmarks can be unreliable in cases of femoral bowing, obesity, or extra-articular deformities [38]. Their accuracy also heavily depends on the surgeon, as minor intraoperative deviations can potentially cause significant alignment issues [8].

Current total knee arthroplasty strategies in patients with valgus arthritic knees and marked coronal femoral bowing deformities

A valgus arthritic knee with coronal femoral bowing is an unusual and complex deformity [20]. In addition to the previously mentioned limitations of IM cutting devices in patients with coronal femoral bowing, a valgus arthritic knee often presents with intra-articular femoral deformities and lateral soft tissue contracture, along with lax medial side structures [3940].

A valgus arthritic knee is linked to deformities of the distal femoral condyle, posterior femoral condyle, and femoral trochlea, which can significantly impact bone resection references such as the posterior condylar axis and Whiteside’s line. This may lead to improper positioning of the femoral prosthesis, which can affect both functional outcome and longevity. Complicated soft-tissue conditions, including medial collateral ligament laxity and retraction of the iliotibial band, the lateral collateral ligament, the popliteus tendon, the posterolateral capsule, and the hamstring muscle, pose additional serious problems [3940]. Achieving the desired stability often requires compensation for unbalanced soft tissue with a thicker liner. However, placing a thicker liner can elevate the joint line, increasing the risk of peroneal nerve neuropraxia. In addition, patellofemoral contact forces may lead to postoperative issues, including pain, polyethylene wear, and reduced clinical outcomes [4142]. Poor knee stability can also cause a Grade II valgus arthritic knee to progress to a Grade III condition, in which case a hinge-type prosthesis is recommended [203240].

In theory, both CAS and intra-articular resection can benefit patients with valgus arthritic knees and coronal femoral bowing deformities. A retrospective study comparing these methods found that CAS involved less lateral retinaculum release and resulted in a more accurate restoration of the joint line. In addition, the reconstruction of the MA and the placement of the femoral component, including femoral valgus and flexion angles, were notably improved with CAS-TKA [20].

DISCUSSION

Coronal femoral bowing deformities are common in Asian populations, particularly among elderly females who are short [1]. Numerous studies have reported such deformities in Taiwan, as well as in China, Hong Kong, India, Japan, Korea, and Singapore [10]. Lasam et al. [17] examined 367 Korean female patients and found that the incidence of coronal femoral bowing was 88% in patients undergoing TKA and 77% in an age-matched normal knee group. In Hong Kong, Yau et al. [8] reported that up to 62% of patients had coronal femoral bowing of more than 2°. According to Mullaji’s method [2] and Mullaji’s criteria [14], coronal femoral bowing of more than 5° is defined as a marked deformity. The prevalence of marked coronal femoral bowing has been reported to reach 14.7% in Indian patients with end-stage osteoarthritis of the knee [14]. Lee et al. [1] studied 952 Taiwanese patients with end-stage osteoarthritis and genu varum deformities, reporting that the incidence of marked coronal femoral bowing was 9.8% (measured according to Mullaji’s method).

TKA is a dependable procedure for treating end-stage knee osteoarthritis, with proper limb alignment restoration being essential for its longevity [713]. Marked coronal femoral bowing deformities can affect postoperative MA and component placement [7]. Earlier studies have noted that patients with a postoperative MA deviation of more than 3° from neutral tend to develop increased varus alignment over 5 years [10]. Various treatment options exist for patients with significant coronal femoral bowing; however, individual variations mean that no single approach is universally optimal. Due to limited evidence-based guidelines, the best strategy remains debated. This review emphasizes the importance of a full-length standing scanogram of the lower limbs for accurate preoperative planning. Depending on the patient’s condition, combining an IM cutting device with personalized VCA, lateralization of the femoral entry point, intra-articular resection, and CAS can help achieve proper limb alignment.

PSI systems have been developed to achieve accurate MA and component placement. Unlike conventional IM alignment guide systems, customized cutting devices used in PSI do not include an IM rod. The potential advantages of PSI may lie in its ability to treat knee osteoarthritis, particularly when combined with extra-articular deformities. Thienpont et al. [43] investigated the application of PSI for TKA in patients with such deformities, reporting improvements in function and restored limb alignment. However, ALShammari et al. found no significant differences in postoperative HKA angles or component positioning between PSI and traditional methods in patients with knee osteoarthritis and lateral femoral bowing [25]. While PSI could theoretically be ideal for patients with coronal femoral bowing deformities, current published evidence is limited, and its role in TKA remains uncertain [44454647]. Some researchers have noted that PSI reduces surgical time and does not increase perioperative morbidity [4445]; however, others report no significant advantage over conventional instrumentation regarding postoperative morbidity and femoral component placement [4647].

Tecame et al. [48] conducted a systematic review, concluding that PSI, robotic, and computer-assisted TKA yield excellent clinical and radiographic outcomes, overcoming issues associated with IM and EM guides. However, they did not note that these methods are often used for patients with significant coronal femoral bowing deformity. Currently, there are no specific guidelines for managing severe coronal femoral bowing using different techniques, and the choice of method depends on the surgeon’s experience and preference. We created an algorithm based on this review [Figure 3]. Future studies are needed to compare the long-term results of these approaches and to develop new, more effective techniques that could further enhance patient outcomes.

Algorithm for surgical techniques and methods used in patients with significant coronal femoral bowing deformity

This study has several limitations. First, there was inconsistency among the studies, including different brands of equipment and varying severities of coronal femoral bowing deformities. Second, most of the included studies were retrospective, which may introduce inherent selection and information biases. Third, many of these studies had small sample sizes, limiting their statistical power and generalizability. Fourth, the follow-up periods were short, restricting the ability to assess long-term clinical outcomes and implant survivorship. Fifth, there was a lack of robotic-assisted TKA in patients with coronal femoral bowing deformities, so we cannot be sure of its effectiveness for such cases.

CONCLUSION

In Asia, coronal femoral bowing deformities are common and influence the success of TKA procedures. Surgeons need a full-length standing scanogram of the lower limbs to create an effective preoperative plan. Based on this review, we developed an algorithm. However, most studies were retrospective, limited by small sample sizes and short follow-up periods. In addition, there was a scarcity of robotic-assisted TKA case deployment. Further multicenter, prospective randomized cohort studies with large-scale and long-term follow-up are necessary to make evidence-based recommendations.
