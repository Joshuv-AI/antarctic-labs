Schrödinger Bridges and Density Steering Problems for Gaussian Mixtures Models in Discrete-Time
George Rapakoulias, Fengjiao Liu, Panagiotis Tsiotras

Abstract
In this work, we revisit the discrete-time Schrödinger Bridge (SB) and Density Steering (DS) problems for Gaussian mixture model (GMM) boundary distributions. Building on the existing literature, we construct a set of feasible Markovian policies that transport the initial distribution to the final distribution, and are expressed as mixtures of elementary component-to-component optimal policies. We then study the policy optimization within this feasible set in the context of discrete-time SBs and density-steering problems, respectively. We show that for minimum-effort density-steering problems, the proposed policy achieves the same control cost as existing approaches in the literature. For discrete-time SB problems, the proposed policy yields a cost smaller than or equal to that in the literature, resulting in a less conservative approximation. Finally, we study the continuous-time limit of our proposed discrete-time approach and show that it agrees with recently proposed approximations to the continuous-time SB for GMM boundary distributions. We illustrate this new result through two numerical examples.



I
Introduction

Controlling the density of dynamical systems is a promising and rapidly evolving area of control theory
[
9
]
.
The goal is to design stochastic processes that steer the probability distribution of a dynamical system from a prescribed initial distribution to a desired terminal one within a finite time horizon.
Among the most prominent formulations of this problem are Schrödinger Bridge problems, which seek the process closest to a given reference dynamics while satisfying marginal distribution constraints.
SBs have attracted significant attention due to their applicability to generative modeling and machine learning
[
6
,
30
,
26
]
, mean-field and multi-agent control
[
8
,
22
,
27
]
, and autonomy and robotics applications
[
25
,
29
,
28
,
16
,
24
]
.

Most existing approaches in the generative modeling and mean-field control literature focus on continuous-time formulations.
These methods are well-suited for high-dimensional distributions that are only available through samples and have led to scalable learning-based algorithms.
In contrast, many autonomy and control applications naturally operate in discrete-time, allowing them to leverage finite-dimensional convex optimization techniques, such as semidefinite programming
[
1
]
.
In this setting, existing approaches often focus on Gaussian distributions and arise in the context of explicitly controlling the statistics of stochastic dynamical systems, such as covariance steering problems.
Recently, methods capable of handling richer distribution classes, including Gaussian mixture models, have been proposed both in discrete-time
[
2
,
15
]
and in continuous time
[
26
,
27
,
23
]
.

In this work, we argue that a deeper understanding of discrete-time methods capable of handling general, high-dimensional distributions can lead to algorithms with practical advantages in both the control and the generative AI domains.
In generative modeling, discrete-time formulations enable faster inference than continuous-time diffusion-based methods
[
13
]
, by only requiring the traversal of a Markov chain with a small number of time steps, instead of approximately integrating continuous-time stochastic differential equations (SDEs).
In autonomy applications, discrete approaches align more naturally with digital control implementations.
Realizing these benefits, however, requires algorithms capable of handling expressive classes of distributions beyond Gaussian distributions.

In this work, we revisit the discrete-time Gaussian mixture steering algorithm originally proposed by
[
2
]
, and introduce a modified formulation that bridges discrete-time density steering and continuous-time SB methods.
Our modification yields a Markovian control policy that depends only on the current state, whereas the policy in
[
2
]
requires memory of a latent random variable sampled at the beginning of the horizon.
We show that, for minimum-effort density steering problems, the proposed policy achieves the same control cost as
[
2
]
, while for discrete-time SB problems, the proposed policy gives a cost that is smaller than or equal to that of
[
2
]
, yielding a less conservative solution.
Moreover, we demonstrate that, in the continuous-time limit, the proposed formulation recovers the policy structure introduced in
[
26
]
.

II
Preliminaries

II-A

Discrete-Time Schrödinger Bridge

The discrete-time Schrödinger Bridge (dtSB)
[
5
]
is a discrete-time formulation of the well-known Schrödinger Bridge problem
[
10
,
18
]
.
From a stochastic process perspective, it is an optimization problem in the space of probability measures over discrete-time processes. Formally, given a reference
N
-step Markov chain
q
, the dtSB is the solution of

\min_{p\in\mathcal{D}(\rho_{0},\rho_{N})}\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)

(1)

where
\mathcal{D}(\rho_{0},\rho_{N})
is a set of processes with fixed marginals
\rho_{0},\rho_{N}
at time steps
k=0,N
, and
q
is usually associated with the discrete-time Gaussian random walk
x_{k+1}=x_{k}+\sqrt{\epsilon}\,w_{k},\,\,w_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
.

Solving for the optimal process

p^{*}=\argmin_{p\in\mathcal{D}(\rho_{0},\rho_{N})}\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)

in (
1
) for arbitrary marginal distributions
\rho_{0},\rho_{N}
, is difficult, in general, and therefore computational methods are usually sought.
A state-of-the-art approach for high-dimensional problems is given in
[
13
]
, which models the transitions of
p
via conditional Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) trained using stochastic optimization schemes.

II-B

Discrete-Time Linear Density Steering

For many control problems, rather than finding the optimal process that is as close as possible to a reference process, it is useful to study discrete-time minimum control effort processes subject to marginal distribution constraints.
Specifically, instead of optimizing over arbitrary Markov chains, as in (
1
), one solves

\displaystyle\min_{u_{k}}\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\|u_{k}\|^{2}\right],

(2a)

s.t.

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=A_{k}x_{k}+B_{k}u_{k}+D_{k}w_{k},

(2b)

\displaystyle x_{0}\sim\rho_{0},\,\quad x_{N}\sim\rho_{N},

(2c)

where
x_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
A_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
B_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
,
D_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\ell}
.
The formulation (
2
) has been extensively studied, in linear density control problems such as the covariance steering framework
[
7
,
1
,
3
,
28
]
, its generalization to problems with Gaussian mixture distributions
[
2
,
15
]
, or in a more abstract setting through the lens of optimal transport
[
31
]
.

Unlike their continuous-time counterparts, Problems (
1
) and (
2
) are not equivalent in general, because even in the case where the linear time-invariant prior (
2b
) matches the random walk transitions
x_{k+1}=x_{k}+\sqrt{\epsilon}\,w_{k}
, the optimal solution of (
1
) does not share the same noise intensities as the reference process, i.e., it is not associated with a state space model of the form
x_{k+1}=x_{k}+u_{k}(x_{k})+\sqrt{\epsilon}\,w_{k}

[
5
]
.

III
Schrödinger Bridge for Mixture Distributions

Consider two mixture distributions

\rho_{0}(x_{0})=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{1}}\alpha^{i}\mu^{i}(x_{0}),\quad\rho_{N}(x_{N})=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{2}}\beta^{j}\nu^{j}(x_{N}),

(11)

where
\{\mu^{i},\alpha^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{1}},\{\nu^{j},\beta^{j}\}_{j=1}^{N_{2}}
are two sets of component-weight pairs, respectively.

The goal of this section is to approximate the solution of the discrete-time SB problem (
1
) for the mixture distributions (
11
) and a reference process
q
, i.e., approximate the solution of
(
1
)
given access to a family of Markov chains
p^{ij}
, each solving a component
i
to component
j
discrete-time SB problem.
Specifically, let the family of
p^{ij}
be Markov chains with
N
total time steps, parameterized by the indices
(i,j)\in\{1,\dots,N_{1}\}\times\{1,\dots,N_{2}\}
. Denote their joint probabilities by

p^{ij}(x_{0},\dots,x_{N})=p^{ij}_{0}(x_{0})\prod_{k=0}^{N-1}p^{ij}_{k+1|k}(x_{k+1}|x_{k}),

(12)

and assume that
p_{0}^{ij}=\mu^{i},\;p_{N}^{ij}=\nu^{j}
, for all
i,j

p^{ij}
transports samples from
\mu^{i}
to
\nu^{j}
.
So far,
p^{ij}
does not necessarily have to be the optimal component-to-component SB solution, but rather a feasible bridge between
\mu^{i}
and
\nu^{j}
.
As we will see later, choosing
p^{ij}

One way of constructing a Markov chain
p
that transports samples from the mixture
\rho_{0}
to the mixture

11

Theorem 1
.

Let
p^{ij}(x_{0},\dots,x_{N})
, where
i\in\{1,\dots,N_{1}\}
and
j\in\{1,\dots,N_{2}\}
, be a family of
N
-step Markov chains with
p_{0}^{ij}=\mu^{i},\;p_{N}^{ij}=\nu^{j}
, for all
i,j
, and define the scalar mixing weights
\lambda_{ij}\geq 0
such that
\sum_{i}\lambda_{ij}=\beta^{j}
and
\sum_{j}\lambda_{ij}=\alpha^{i}
.
Then, starting from
x_{0}\sim\rho_{0}
, and following the transition probabilities

p_{k+1|k}(x_{k+1}|x_{k})=\sum_{ij}p^{ij}_{k+1|k}(x_{k+1}|x_{k})\frac{\lambda_{ij}p^{ij}_{k}(x_{k})}{p_{k}(x_{k})},

(13)

where,

p_{k}(x_{k})=\sum_{ij}\lambda_{ij}p^{ij}_{k}(x_{k}),\quad k=0,\dots,N-1,

(14)

the resulting distributions for
x_{k}
will be given by equation (
14

Proof.

It is a simple consequence of marginalization, that
p_{k+1}(x_{k+1})=\int p_{k+1|k}(x_{k+1}|x_{k})p_{k}(x_{k})\,\mathrm{d}x_{k}
. Using this, it is easy to show that if
p_{k}=\sum_{ij}\lambda_{ij}p^{ij}_{k}
holds for some time step

, then
p_{k+1}=\sum_{ij}\lambda_{ij}p^{ij}_{k+1}

p_{0}=\rho_{0}
due to the constraints on
\lambda_{ij}
.
∎

Given the family of Markov processes and transition kernels in equations (
14
), (
13
), parameterized by
\lambda_{ij}
and
p^{ij}
, we are interested in the one that induces the smallest cost in the sense of Problem (
1
).
Substituting the transition probabilities in (
1
) and carrying out some calculations, we arrive at the following theorem.

Theorem 2
.

Consider the family of Markov processes
p
, parameterized by
p^{ij}
and
\lambda_{ij}
satisfying the constraints of Theorem

Let
J_{ij}^{\mathrm{SB}}

\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)\leq\sum_{ij}\lambda_{ij}J_{ij}^{\mathrm{SB}}.

Proof.

We first consider the following upper bound

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{0}\|q_{0})+\sum_{k}\mathbb{E}_{x_{k}}\left[\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{k+1|k}\|q_{k+1|k})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\lambda_{ij}\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p^{ij}\|q),

(17)

where the inequality in (

Since

\min_{\lambda_{ij}\geq 0}\sum_{ij}\lambda_{ij}J^{\mathrm{SB}}_{ij}\,;\,\,\mathrm{s.t.}\,\,\Big\{\sum_{i}\lambda_{ij}=\beta^{j},\sum_{j}\lambda_{ij}=\alpha^{i}\Big\}.

IV
Discrete-Time Density Steering

\displaystyle\int p(x_{k+1}|x_{k},u_{k})p(u_{k}|x_{k})\,\mathrm{d}u_{k}

V-A

Randomizing Once vs Randomizing at Every Step

A similar “randomized-mixing” approach for constructing processes that steer between GMMs has been proposed in
[
2
,
4
]
, and was further explored in the context of chance constraints
[
15
]
and distributionally robust control
[
20
]
.
The difference between the approach in
[
2
]
and the proposed approach is that the randomization in
[
2
]
occurs once, at the beginning of the control horizon, as opposed to policy (
13
), where the randomization occurs at every time step.
Although both methods provide feasible solutions to either the density steering or the SB problem, randomizing at every time step yields a Markovian policy, whereas randomizing once at the beginning yields a process with memory.

Except for different properties of the resulting processes due to the Markovian property, it is easy to see that both processes share the same marginal distributions for every time step
k
, and result in the same control cost for the density steering problems (see Theorem
3
as opposed to
[
2
, Theorem 1]
).
In the context of SBs, the Markov process parameterized by the transitions (
13
) yields a cost that is smaller than or equal to the cost of the policy in
[
2
]
.
Below, we prove this result:

In the context of Theorem
1
, the joint density of the process that corresponds to a single randomization in the first time step, denoted with
r
, calculates to
r=\sum_{ij}\lambda_{ij}p^{ij}
, i.e.,
r
is a mixture of path measures, rather than a mixture of transition functions.
Let
p
be the path measure of the Markov process with transitions (
13
).
It is trivial to see that
p_{k}=r_{k}
, i.e., the marginals at time step
k
match for both approaches.
Using the definition of
r
and marginalization, it is also easy to show that
r_{k+1,k}(x_{k+1},x_{k})=p_{k+1,k}(x_{k+1},x_{k})
, i.e., the joint densities for two consecutive time steps match for the two approaches.
Note that the KL-divergence between two distributions
p
and
q
equals

\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)=-\mathbb{E}_{p}[\log q]-h(p),

(23)

where
h(p)=-\int\log p\,\mathrm{d}p
denotes the differential entropy of
p
.
It is easy to show that
h(p)\geq h(r)
, i.e., randomizing at every time step results in a process with larger entropy than randomizing once:

\displaystyle h(r)=h(r_{0})+\sum_{k}\mathbb{E}_{x_{0:k}\sim r_{0:k}}\big[h\big(r_{k+1|0:k}(x_{k+1}|x_{0:k})\big)\big]

\displaystyle\leq h(r_{0})+\sum_{k}\mathbb{E}_{x_{k}\sim r_{k}}\big[h\big(r_{k+1|k}(x_{k+1}|x_{k})\big)\big]

(24)

\displaystyle=h(r_{0})+\sum_{k}\mathbb{E}_{x_{k}\sim r_{k}}\left[h\left(\frac{r_{k+1,k}(x_{k+1},x_{k})}{r_{k}(x_{k})}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=h(p_{0})+\sum_{k}\mathbb{E}_{x_{k}\sim p_{k}}\big[h\big(p_{k+1|k}(x_{k+1}|x_{k})\big)\big]

(25)

\displaystyle=h(p)

where the notation
x_{0:k}
denotes the set of random variables
x_{0},\dots,x_{k}
, the inequality in (
24
) holds because conditioning reduces entropy
[
12
, Theorem 2.6.5]
, and (
25
) holds because
r_{k+1,k}(x_{k+1},x_{k})=p_{k+1,k}(x_{k+1},x_{k})
and
r_{k}(x_{k})=p_{k}(x_{k})
.

Furthermore, for the maximum likelihood term in (
23
), it holds that
\mathbb{E}_{p}[\log q]=\mathbb{E}_{r}[\log q]
, since

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p}[\log q]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p_{0}}[\log q_{0}]+\!\sum_{k}\mathbb{E}_{p_{k+1,k}}\Big[\log q_{k+1|k}(x_{k+1}|x_{k})\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{r_{0}}[\log q_{0}]+\sum_{k}\mathbb{E}_{r_{k+1,k}}\Big[\log q_{k+1|k}(x_{k+1}|x_{k})\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{r}\big[\log q\big].

We therefore deduce that

\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)\leq\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(r\|q),

(26)

and, therefore, randomizing at every time step has a lower cost in terms of the SB cost function.

Finally, we note that the process
p
resulting from the mixture kernel (
13
) is the Markovian projection
[
13
]
of the mixture process
r=\sum_{ij}\lambda_{ij}p^{ij}
, that is,
p
is the solution of

p=\argmin_{m\in\mathcal{M}}\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(r\|m),

(27)

where
\mathcal{M}
is the set of all Markov processes supported in
\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)\times n}
.
Due to space limitations, we defer the proof of this result and refer the reader to
[
13
]
for a concise treatment of the Markovian projection for discrete-time processes.

Continuous-Time Limit

In this section, we show that in the limit of small time steps, the discrete-time policy process (
13
) converges to the continuous time process of
[
26
]
.
To study the convergence of the discrete-time process, we will consider a reference process parameterized by
\Delta T>0
, given by

x_{k+1}=x_{k}+\Delta Tu_{t}+\sqrt{\epsilon\Delta T}\,w_{k},\quad w_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I).

(28)

In this setting, consider the setup of Problem (
1
), and study the generator of the process
p
, as
\Delta T\rightarrow 0
.
Since the state mean and state covariance can be separated, we assume
\mu_{0}^{i}=\mu_{N}^{j}=0
for the moment.
Using
[
14
, Theorem 3]
, we can show that the discrete-time optimal process
p^{ij}
for the
ij
-th component satisfies

x_{k+1}^{ij}\hskip-2.27621pt=\hskip-1.99168pt\big(\hskip-1.13809ptI-\epsilon\Delta T(Q_{k}^{ij})^{-1}\hskip-1.13809pt\big)x_{k}^{ij}+\sqrt{\epsilon\Delta T}\big(\hskip-1.13809ptI-\epsilon\Delta T(Q_{k}^{ij})^{-1}\hskip-1.13809pt\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}w_{k}^{ij}\!,

where
w_{k}^{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
and
Q_{k}^{ij}
is given by (
9
)-(
10
) in Proposition
1
with
k
replaced by
k\Delta T
and
N
replaced by
N\Delta T
.
The corresponding transition kernel is
p_{k+1|k}^{ij}(x_{k+1}|x_{k})=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{k+1|k}^{ij},\Sigma_{k+1|k}^{ij})
, where

\displaystyle\mu_{k+1|k}^{ij}=\big(I-\epsilon\Delta T(Q_{k}^{ij})^{-1}\big)x_{k}^{ij},

(29)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{k+1|k}^{ij}=\epsilon\Delta T\big(I-\epsilon\Delta T(Q_{k}^{ij})^{-1}\big).

(30)

As
\Delta T\to 0
, the above process converges to the continuous-time optimal process
[
11
]
for the
ij
-th component, which satisfies

\mathrm{d}x^{ij}_{t}=-\epsilon(Q^{ij}_{t})^{-1}x^{ij}_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\epsilon}\,\mathrm{d}w^{ij},\quad t\in[0,T],

where
w^{ij}
is a Wiener process,
Q^{ij}(t)
satisfies

\displaystyle Q^{ij}_{t}

\displaystyle=Q_{0}^{ij}-t\epsilon I,

and
Q^{ij}_{0}
is given by (
10
) in Proposition
1
with
N
replaced by
T
.
Thus, the discrete-time optimal SB cost
J_{ij}^{\mathrm{SB}}
for each
ij
-th component converges to its continuous-time counterpart.
It follows that the mixing weights
\lambda_{ij}
also converge to their continuous-time counterparts.
It follows from (
14
) that the marginal distribution of the mixed process also converges to its continuous-time counterpart in
[
26
]
.

Given the above, we now study the convergence of the generator of the discrete-time process (
28
) and show that it converges to the process defined in
[
26
]
.
Recall from
[
26
]
that the continuous-time mixing process is

\mathrm{d}x_{t}=\textstyle\sum_{i,j}{u_{t|ij}(x_{t})\frac{\rho_{t|ij}(x_{t})\lambda_{ij}}{\sum_{i,j}\rho_{t|ij}(x_{t})\lambda_{ij}}}\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\epsilon}\,\mathrm{d}w_{t},

(31)

where
u_{t|ij}(x_{t})=-\epsilon(Q^{ij}_{t})^{-1}x_{t}
and
\rho_{t|ij}(x_{t})
are, respectively, the optimal control and the resulting marginal density of the
ij
-th flow component.
For notational simplicity, let
\eta_{ij}=\frac{\rho_{t|ij}(x)\lambda_{ij}}{\sum_{i,j}\rho_{t|ij}(x)\lambda_{ij}}
.
To this end, consider a sufficiently regular test function
\phi
.
It is straightforward to see that the infinitesimal generator of (
31
) for the test function
\phi
is

\mathcal{L}(\phi)=-\nabla\phi(x_{t})^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}\Big(\sum_{ij}\epsilon\eta_{ij}\,(Q_{t}^{ij})^{-1}\Big)x_{t}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\mathrm{tr}\big(\hskip-1.42262pt\nabla^{2}\phi(x_{t})\big).

Now, compute the limit of the discrete-time generator as
\Delta T\rightarrow 0
.
We can write the second-order Taylor expansion of
\phi(x_{k+1})
as

\phi(x_{k+1})\approx\phi(x_{k})+\nabla\phi(x_{k})^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}(x_{k+1}-x_{k})\\
+\frac{1}{2}(x_{k+1}-x_{k})^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}\nabla^{2}\phi(x_{k})(x_{k+1}-x_{k}).

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\lim_{\Delta T\rightarrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}[\phi(x_{k+1})|x_{k}]-\phi(x_{k})}{\Delta T}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\Delta T\rightarrow 0}\frac{\int\phi(x_{k+1})p_{k+1|k}\,\mathrm{d}x_{k+1}-\phi(x_{k})}{\Delta T}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\Delta T\rightarrow 0}\int\frac{\phi(x_{k+1})-\phi(x_{k})}{\Delta T}p_{k+1|k}\,\mathrm{d}x_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\Delta T\rightarrow 0}\int\frac{\phi(x_{k+1})-\phi(x_{k})}{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}\frac{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\Delta T}p_{k+1|k}\,\mathrm{d}x_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\Delta T\rightarrow 0}\int\bigg(\nabla\phi(x_{k})+\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{2}\phi(x_{k})(x_{k+1}-x_{k})\bigg)^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt\times\frac{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\Delta T}p_{k+1|k}\,\mathrm{d}x_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\nabla\phi(x_{t})^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}\lim_{\Delta T\rightarrow 0}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\Delta T}\bigg|x_{k}\bigg]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}\bigg(\hskip-1.42262pt\nabla^{2}\phi(x_{t})\hskip-1.42262pt\lim_{\Delta T\rightarrow 0}\hskip-1.42262pt\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{(x_{k+1}-x_{k})(x_{k+1}-x_{k})^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}}{\Delta T}\bigg|x_{k}\bigg]\bigg).

In light of the mixing transition kernel (
13
), we define
\gamma_{ij}=\frac{\lambda_{ij}p^{ij}_{k}(x_{k})}{p_{k}(x_{k})}
for notational simplicity.
Since the mixing transition kernel
p_{k+1|k}(x_{k+1}|x_{k})
follows a GMM distribution, where the
ij
-th component has density
p_{k+1|k}^{ij}(x_{k+1}|x_{k})
and mixing weight
\gamma_{ij}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\Delta T}\bigg|x_{k}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta T}\bigg(\sum_{ij}\gamma_{ij}\mu_{k+1|k}^{ij}-x_{k}\bigg)

\displaystyle=-\Big(\sum_{ij}\epsilon\,\gamma_{ij}\,(Q_{k}^{ij})^{-1}\Big)x_{k}.

As
\Delta T\rightarrow 0
, we have
k\Delta T\rightarrow t
,
x_{k}^{ij}\rightarrow x^{ij}_{t}
, and
Q_{k}^{ij}\rightarrow Q_{t}^{ij}
.
As
\Delta T\rightarrow 0
, we also have
p^{ij}_{k}(x_{k})\to\rho_{t|ij}(x_{t})
and the discrete-time mixing weights
\lambda_{ij}
converge to their continuous-time counterparts,
thus
\gamma_{ij}\rightarrow\eta_{ij}
.
Hence, the first term of the generator becomes

\nabla\phi(x_{k})^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}\lim_{\Delta T\rightarrow 0}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\Delta T}\bigg|x_{k}\bigg]\\
=-\nabla\phi(x_{t})^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}\Big(\sum_{ij}\epsilon\,\eta_{ij}(Q_{t}^{ij})^{-1}\Big)x_{t}.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[(x_{k+1}-x_{k})(x_{k+1}-x_{k})^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}\big|x_{k}\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij}\gamma_{ij}\Big(\Sigma_{k+1|k}^{ij}+\big(\mu_{k+1|k}^{ij}-x_{k}\big)\big(\mu_{k+1|k}^{ij}-x_{k}\big)^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}\Big)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt-\Big(\sum_{ij}\gamma_{ij}\big(\mu_{k+1|k}^{ij}-x_{k}\big)\Big)\Big(\sum_{ij}\gamma_{ij}\big(\mu_{k+1|k}^{ij}-x_{k}\big)\Big)^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij}\gamma_{ij}\Big(\epsilon\Delta T\big(I-\epsilon\Delta T(Q_{k}^{ij})^{-1}\big)

(32)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+\sum_{ij}\epsilon^{2}(\Delta T)^{2}(Q_{k}^{ij})^{-1}x_{k}x_{k}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}(Q_{k}^{ij})^{-1}\Big)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt-\epsilon^{2}(\Delta T)^{2}\Big(\sum_{ij}\gamma_{ij}(Q_{k}^{ij})^{-1}x_{k}\Big)\Big(\sum_{ij}\gamma_{ij}(Q_{k}^{ij})^{-1}x_{k}\Big)^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}.

(33)

After dividing (
33
) by
\Delta T
and taking the limit as
\Delta T\rightarrow 0
, all terms containing
(\Delta T)^{2}
vanish in (
33
).
It follows that

\lim_{\Delta T\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{\Delta T}\mathbb{E}\big[(x_{k+1}-x_{k})(x_{k+1}-x_{k})^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}\big|x_{k}\big]=\sum_{ij}\eta_{ij}\,\epsilon=\epsilon.

Thus, the second term of the generator becomes

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}\bigg(\hskip-1.42262pt\nabla^{2}\phi(x_{k})\hskip-1.42262pt\lim_{\Delta T\rightarrow 0}\hskip-1.42262pt\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{(x_{k+1}-x_{k})(x_{k+1}-x_{k})^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}}{\Delta T}\bigg|x_{k}\bigg]\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon}{2}\mathrm{tr}\big(\hskip-1.42262pt\nabla^{2}\phi(x_{t})\big).

Therefore, as
\Delta T\rightarrow 0
, the generator of the discrete-time mixing process
p
converges to the infinitesimal generator of its continuous-time counterpart process in
[
26
]
.

VII
Conclusions

To conclude, in this paper, we propose a randomized feedback Markovian policy for approximating the solution of discrete-time Schrödinger bridge and density steering problems.
Our method works by first constructing a feasible set of policies, and then approximating the optimal policy within the set by optimizing a tractable upper bound.
We draw connections to existing literature and show that the proposed approach yields a less conservative approximation of SB problems, while achieving the same cost for density control problems, with the additional benefit of being a state-feedback law rather than a controller with memory.
We also study the continuous time limit of the proposed approach, and show that it agrees with a recently proposed continuous-time Schrödinger bridge approximation for GMM boundary distributions.
Finally, we illustrate our approach in two examples.
