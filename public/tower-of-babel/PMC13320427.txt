Dataset on the solution to the guaranteed-service time inventory model across 38 real-world supply chains with normally distributed demand
Authors: Luis A Moncayo-Martínez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe placement of safety stock to guarantee a service time has received considerable attention due to its impact on reducing inventory costs across multi-echelon supply chains. This work compiles and analyses the structure of 38 real-world supply chains reported in the literature by characterising their depth, degree, and stage types (distribution, manufacturing, part, retail, and transport). We provide the detailed solutions of the guaranteed-service-time inventory model under normally distributed demand, obtained using branch-and-cut algorithms implemented in commercial optimisers. The resulting dataset includes the optimal safety stock levels, the corresponding CPU time, and full variable values for every instance, with the largest model containing 4,050 variables and 18,890 constraints. All data are provided in CSV format to enable flexible reuse, and the source code for reproducing the plots is openly available. This dataset offers a rigorous benchmark for researchers developing new methods for solving the guaranteed-service-time inventory model, particularly for the four instances where a 25% optimality gap remains.Keywords: Business management, Inventory levels cost, Guaranteed service time, Mathematical programming models

Background

This dataset supports the solution approach presented in the related research article [1]. The original algorithm was executed across 38 real-world supply chain instances. Due to the volume of results, not all outputs were included in the research article. This dataset provides complete results for each instance, including variable values, solver logs, and network characteristics such as depth and degree. The associated source code is provided to enable full reproducibility of the results.

The guaranteed-service time inventory model captures the balance between inventory cost and customer service by ensuring that strict delivery commitments are met through the strategic placement of safety stock across the network. It directly links customer-facing service performance with internal inventory decisions in a multi-echelon supply chain. In this model, each stage has inbound and outbound service-time variables that determine the safety stock required to buffer demand variability. These variables create a time-feasible plan for the entire supply chain, ensuring that each stage can serve its successors within the promised service time; without such guarantees, delays would propagate upstream, increasing variability and overall cost. As outlined by Humair and Willems [5], the model is defined on acyclic networks to support multi-echelon planning in real-world supply chains and has been widely applied to systems where inventory costs must be minimised while guaranteed service-time constraints are satisfied [3,5].

Although previous studies have proposed solution algorithms for this model [2,3], comparison across methods has been limited by unavailable parameters or missing implementation details. This dataset includes complete model inputs and outputs for 38 supply chains described in Willems [4], where demand is normally distributed. All files required for replication—including solver inputs, results, and visualisations—are provided.

Data Description

Structure of the dataset.

The instances_data folder includes the structure of 38 real-world supply chains. This file can be downloaded from [4]; it was not created as part of this work. The Excel file contains three sheets per instance: (01_F) a high-level plot; (01_SD) stage attributes such as name, depth, cost, and processing time, along with the average and standard deviation of demand, the maximum service time, and the service level; and (01_LL) the edges list representing the supply chain’s structure.

The source_code folder contains three Python scripts used to generate the dataset. The main.py script constructs the supply chain graph using the files from the instances_data folder and solves the guaranteed-service time inventory model (as described in the following section). The graph_analysis.py script calculates the degree and depth of each stage—where the degree refers to the number of incoming and outgoing connections, and the depth indicates the stage’s position in the network. The SC_plot.py script produces structural plots of the supply chains using categorical colour codes for the five stage types: Dist, Manuf, Part, Retail, and Trans.

The instances_structure folder contains three subfolders that detail the structure of the 38 real-world supply chains. Each subfolder includes 39 files—one per supply chain and one summary file. For illustration, instance 1 is used as an example, although the description applies to all 38 cases.

A stage’s depth indicates its position within the supply chain. For example, Part_0001, Part_0002 and Part_0003 have a depth of 0 because they have no predecessors; the supply chain starts from these nodes. Stages with depth 1 are those with at least one predecessor of depth 0, such as Manuf_0001 and Manuf_0002. Finally, Retail_0001, Retail_0002 and Retail_0003 each have a predecessor with depth 1 and therefore have a depth of 2. In general, the depth of a stage is defined as one plus the minimum depth among its predecessors.

The subfolder categorical_variables_plots contains the structural plots, such as the one on the right of Fig. 2. While these categories do not affect the mathematical formulation of the guaranteed-service time inventory model, they enhance post-solution analysis by making it easier to observe structural characteristics like branching, bottlenecks, and stage connectivity. For instance, identifying where multiple part suppliers feed into a single manufacturing stage or how many retail nodes are downstream becomes straightforward. This aids researchers in interpreting solution patterns, developing heuristics, and exploring the impact of network topology on model performance. Categorical plots are provided in instance01.html format for all 38 instances, allowing interactive inspection of the supply chain structure for each case.

This dataset also offers broad applicability across multiple research areas within operations research and supply chain optimisation. The detailed network characteristics make the dataset suitable for studying network design questions—such as how depth, degree, or stage type affect safety stock placement—as well as for developing machine-learning models that predict solution difficulty, optimal stock levels, or promising search strategies. Furthermore, the dataset enables robustness and sensitivity analyses under alternative service-time or demand assumptions. Overall, these instances provide a versatile platform for testing and validating a wide spectrum of optimisation methods and decision-support tools.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

The guaranteed-service time inventory model ensures that customer-facing stages receive their products within a promised service time (Φ)under a bounded demand assumption [5]. The supply chain is represented as a directed acyclic graph G=(V,E), where each node i∈Vdenotes a stage and each arc (i,j)∈Ecaptures the precedence relation between stages. Every stage has a deterministic processing time ti. Inbound service time θispecifies the latest time by which all predecessors must supply stage i, while outbound service time βidefines the promised delivery time to its successors. The objective is to minimise the total safety stock cost S given in Eq. (1), where Iis the interest rate (set to 20% for all instances), zαis the safety factor associated with the service level α(we use the maximum reported value in [4]), and ci and σi denote the cumulative unit cost and cumulative standard deviation at stage i, respectively.

Eq. (2) is called the days of inventory and ensures that the outbound service time (θi) cannot be earlier than the inbound service time (βi) plus processing time (ti). Eq. (3) guarantees that the outbound service time of stage i is not greater than the inbound service time of its successor stage j. The guaranteed service time is set in the subset D⊂V of stages where the demand is generated; Eq. (4) ensures that the guaranteed service time is not greater than the outbound service time at those stages. Finally, Eq. (5) states that the inbound and outbound service time value must be at least zero and integers.

The guaranteed-service time inventory model assumes that each stage commits to serving its successors within a specified service time (θi), ensuring a time-feasible material flow across the SC. The model assumes deterministic processing times, demand occurring only at terminal stages, and integer-valued inbound and outbound service times. Safety stock is placed at each stage to buffer demand variability during the guaranteed service window, and the replenishment time of every stage is fully determined by its inbound service time θi, processing time ti, and outbound service time βi. A limitation of the model is that this representation idealises real-world operations by assuming no external delays and restricting service times to integer values.

Demand at each terminal stage is assumed to be normally distributed, following the standard formulation in the guaranteed-service literature [5]. This assumption enables a closed-form expression for safety stock via the safety factor zα, allowing the objective function in Eq. (1) to be computed analytically. Using normally distributed demand also facilitates comparability across the 38 real-world supply chains and maintains consistency with prior studies. Nonetheless, the normality assumption limits applicability in settings with highly skewed or intermittent demand, where alternative distributions would yield different safety stock requirements.

In our experimental design, we solved thirty-eight times the model in Eq. (1) to Eq. (5) once per each real-world supply chain. The resulting data have the minimum safety stock in Eq. (1) and the integer value of the inbound (βi) and outbound (θi) time for every stage.

The source_code folder has the main.py file, which is divided into three parts. In the first one it is imported all the data from the file in the folder instances_data; then, using the Python package NetworkX, the SC is created using the edgelist method as follows G = nx.from_pandas_edgelist(edges_data, create_using=nx.DiGraph()). For each stage i, the methods G.predecessors(i) and G.successors(i) select the predecessor and successor stages of i. The maximum value of the service level (αi) of stages who does not have successor stages is the one used to solve the instances. The guaranteed service time (Φ) used to solve the instances is the maximum guaranteed service time. The value of Φ is quoted in the stages with no successors.

The continuous initial solution is computed using the Python-based modelling language Pyomo in the second part of the file main.py. The model is created using the method model = pyo.ConcreteModel(name="(GSIM_Pyomo)") and the variables are set as model.βi= pyo.Var(βi bounds=set_bounds_βi, domain=pyo.PositiveReals) for the inbound time and the same for the outbound time. The parameter bounds is based on the maximum time [5] Mi=ti+max∀j(j,i)∈E{Mj}. For the inbound service time (βi) variables the bound is set to Mi−ti. For the outbound service time (θi) is Mi, and the maximum time for stages with no successor, is zero. The model is solved using the library IPOpt library using the method solver = pyo.SolverFactory('ipopt'), status = solver.solve(model, tee=False).

To measure the CPU time in which an instance is solved, the function time.time() is used. The initial time begins before the creation of the model pyomo_init_time = time.time(), and it ends after the problem is solved pyomo_fin_time = time.time(). The result of this part is a continuous solution for all the variables in Eq. (5)

Finally, the integer solution is found when the initial solution, computed using IPOpt, is input to Gurobi. The model is created using the method m = gp.Model("GSTInvModel") the variables are modelled using out_var = m.addVars(list(G.nodes),lb=0,vtype=GRB.INTEGER, name="y") and are bounded as out_var[i].ub = G.nodes[i]["M"] and in_var[i].ub = G.nodes[i]["M"] -G.nodes[i]["time"].

In this part, it is important to set the termination criterion. The algorithm is finished when Gurobi cannot find a solution to Eq. (1) lower than the current solution after T units of time. The method m.optimize(callback=cb) calls the function that checks if there is a new lower solution. The CPU time is computed in the same way as in the second part.

Gurobi uses a branch-and-cut (B&C) algorithm to solve mixed-integer programming models such as the one in Eqs. (1)–(5) [6]. The B&C framework combines branch-and-bound search with cutting-plane generation to efficiently explore the solution space. In this context, Gurobi linearises the nonlinear objective function in Eq. (1), while the generated cuts tighten the linear relaxation, reduce the size of the search tree, and improve convergence for instances with thousands of variables and constraints. The main advantages of the B&C algorithm are its robustness, its ability to exploit problem structure, and its proven performance in large-scale optimisation. Nonetheless, challenges arise for the largest supply chains, where deep search trees and weak relaxations may lead to long CPU times or non-zero optimality gaps, as observed in several of the biggest instances. These cases underscore the computational difficulty of the guaranteed-service time inventory model and motivate future research on specialised cuts, decomposition approaches, or problem-specific heuristics.

The results in Table 1 show substantial variation in the minimum safety stock cost across the 38 supply chains, ranging from only 258.39 (instance 6) to over 13 million (instance 31). These differences reflect the structural characteristics of each network: instances with long supply chain depth, many demand stages, and high cumulative variability (e.g., instances 27–33) require significantly larger safety stock investments. The number of predecessor-free stages and branching patterns also influence safety stock levels, as networks with multiple upstream sources or deep transformation paths accumulate more variability and therefore generate higher safety stock requirements.

CPU time similarly varies with network size and complexity. Small instances with fewer than 20 stages are solved almost instantaneously, while the largest networks—such as instances 21, 24, 27, 29–33—require more than 50 seconds, and in a few extreme cases over 100 seconds. These longer runtimes occur in instances with many stages, wide connectivity, and hundreds of constraints, which lead to deeper branch-and-cut trees and weaker relaxations. Despite this, optimality gaps are zero in most cases; only a few large and highly branched instances exhibit non-zero gaps (up to 25% in instance 18 and 17% in instance 29), highlighting the computational difficulty of the guaranteed-service time model for very large supply chains.

Overall, these results illustrate how safety stock requirements and computational effort both scale with supply chain structure. Practitioners can use this information to anticipate inventory investment in complex networks, identify cost-critical stages, and recognise when very large supply chains may require decomposition or heuristic strategies to obtain solutions within practical time limits.

The source_code folder contains three scripts—main.py, graph_analysis.py, and SC_plot.py—which reproduce the full workflow described in this dataset. After downloading the repository, users run main.py to load the instance files, construct the directed supply chain graph using NetworkX, compute a continuous initial solution with Pyomo/IPOpt, and finally solve the integer model with Gurobi. The scripts graph_analysis.py and SC_plot.py then generate the degree/depth histograms and categorical structure plots used throughout the article. Common issues include missing Python packages or incorrect file paths; these are resolved by installing the required dependencies and maintaining the directory structure shown in Fig. 1.

To promote reproducibility and benchmarking, the dataset includes all intermediate and final results: continuous Pyomo solutions, Gurobi integer solutions, structural files, and visualisations for all 38 supply chains. Researchers can replicate the entire pipeline by running the provided scripts or modify components to test alternative optimisation approaches. The presence of non-zero optimality gaps in some of the largest instances highlights opportunities for developing new algorithms, specialised cuts, or heuristic warm-starts. By allowing direct comparison of safety stock costs, service times, and CPU performance across instances, this dataset gives researchers a robust platform for validating and improving methods for the guaranteed-service time inventory model.

Limitations

The model in Eq. (1) to Eq. (5) assumes that the demand is normally distributed; therefore, if the demand follows another probability distribution function, the results cannot be compared. Some files in the subfolder categorial variables plots could take some time to open, especially for the 38th instance.

Instance 38, the largest in the dataset, yields a mixed-integer model comprising 4,050 decision variables and 18,890 constraints. The variables represent inbound and outbound service-time decisions for each of the 2025 supply chain stages. Meanwhile, the constraints are derived from network precedence relations and the guaranteed-service requirements outlined in Eqs. (2)–(4). Specifically, every arc enforces feasibility between predecessor and successor stages, while each demand stage adds a constraint to meet promised service times.

As the network grows, these constraints accumulate rapidly, creating a massive branch-and-cut model. Implementation proved challenging due to the extensive search tree, weak linear relaxations caused by deep or highly connected graphs, and the significant CPU time required to close the optimality gap. These factors underscore the computational complexity inherent in real-world guaranteed-service planning. Four of the largest supply chains show gaps of up to 25% between the best incumbent and the best bound due to their size, deep multi-echelon structure, and highly connected graphs, which produce weak linear relaxations and slow bound improvement in the branch-and-cut search. These instances generate very large trees that cannot be fully explored within the time limit. Potential ways to reduce these gaps include adding problem-specific cuts, decomposing the network into smaller subproblems, or using heuristic or metaheuristic warm starts to improve the initial solution and accelerate convergence.

Future research may explore alternative demand distributions beyond the normal assumption used here, such as Poisson or negative binomial models, which may better capture skewed or intermittent demand patterns. Extending the model to incorporate stochastic processing times or exogenous lead-time variability is another promising direction. From an optimisation perspective, the large search trees and weak relaxations observed in the biggest instances motivate the investigation of specialised cutting planes, decomposition approaches, or metaheuristic and quantum-inspired algorithms that may scale better for complex supply-chain structures. These extensions could further enhance the applicability and computational performance of the guaranteed-service time inventory model.
