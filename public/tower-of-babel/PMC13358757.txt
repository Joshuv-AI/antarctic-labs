Ultra fast track anesthesia in acute Stanford type A aortic dissection surgery: a single-center retrospective study
Authors: Yi-Fan Zou, Fang-Ting Xie, Bo Wang, Hao Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute Stanford type A aortic dissection (ATAAD), as a cardiovascular critical emergency with an extremely high mortality rate and rapid onset, often requires extracorporeal circulation and deep hypothermic circulatory arrest techniques during surgery, posing challenges to conventional anesthesia management. This study aimed to assess the safety and efficacy of ultra fast track anesthesia (UFTA) in individuals undergoing surgery for ATAAD.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on patients who underwent ATAAD surgery at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University between January 2023 and December 2024. A total of 196 patients were primarily included and 90 patients were ultimately enrolled after strict inclusion and exclusion. The patients were classified according to anesthesia protocol administered: the UFTA group (n=30) and the control (standard general anesthesia) group (n=60). Postoperative complications, recovery parameters, and hospitalization costs were compared between groups.ResultsCompared to the control group, the UFTA group demonstrated significantly lower hospitalization costs [234,000±62,000 vs. 282,000±104,000 China yuan (CNY); P=0.02], reduced 24-hour postoperative drainage volume (524.4±94.5 vs. 582.7±110.4 ml; P=0.01). No statistically significant differences were observed in the incidence of postoperative complications between the groups.ConclusionsAmong patients undergoing surgery for ATAAD, UFTA appears to be a safe and feasible anesthetic approach that may facilitate enhanced postoperative recovery and reduce overall healthcare costs.Keywords: Acute Stanford type A aortic dissection (ATAAD), cardiac surgery, ultra fast track anesthesia (UFTA)

Highlight box

Key findings

• This study, through a single-center retrospective analysis, preliminarily confirmed that in the acute Stanford type A aortic dissection (ATAAD) surgery, ultra fast track anesthesia (UFTA), while maintaining the same level of safety as conventional general anesthesia, can significantly reduce medical costs and decrease postoperative thoracic drainage volume.

What is known and what is new?

• ATAAD is a highly lethal cardiovascular emergency with a high rate of postoperative complications. Conventional general anesthesia emphasizes strong analgesia, strong sedation, and deep muscle relaxation, which is not conducive to the rapid postoperative recovery of patients. Previous studies have mostly used UFTA in elective low-risk cardiac surgeries. The 2024 Cardiac Rapid Recovery Guidelines also proposed that UFTA should be encouraged in elective low-risk cardiac surgeries, but research and data on its application in complex surgeries such as ATAAD are still in an unexplored stage.

• This study is the first to conduct a retrospective analysis to compare the effects of UFTA and conventional general anesthesia on complications of complex ATAAD.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• In this study, the preliminary analysis of the small sample data demonstrated that UFTA might be able to be extended to complex and high-risk ATAAD surgeries while ensuring safety. In the future, more convincing prospective large-sample studies are needed to verify the safety and advantages of UFTA application in high-risk cardiac surgeries.

Introduction

Acute Stanford type A aortic dissection (ATAAD) is one of the most dangerous clinical entities among cardiovascular emergencies. It has a rapid onset and progresses rapidly. If effective intervention measures are not taken in time, it is very likely to cause fatal complications in patients within a short period. Although surgical techniques and perioperative management have been continuously improving in recent years, the in-hospital mortality rate of acute type A aortic dissection remains high. Some multi-center studies have shown that despite active surgical intervention, the in-hospital mortality rate can still reach 10% to 15%, and if treatment is delayed, the risk of death within 24 hours can be as high as 50% (1). Conventional anesthesia management focuses on maintaining patients in a deep analgesic, deep sedative, and deep muscle-relaxing state, which often leads to delayed extubation after surgery, prolonged mechanical ventilation time, and an increased incidence of postoperative complications (2). In 2024, a joint consensus statement from the International Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) Cardiac Society, ERAS International Society, and the Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) recommended that, in rigorously selected low-risk cardiac surgery patients, ultra fast track anesthesia (UFTA), defined as extubation within one hour postoperatively or immediately in the operating room, may be considered (3).

Emerging evidence from recent studies indicates that UFTA offers benefits in low-risk elective surgeries, such as coronary artery bypass grafting and the repair of simple congenital heart defects, including significant reductions in intensive care unit (ICU) length of stay (LoS), overall hospital LoS, postoperative complications, and hospitalization costs (4,5). However, data supporting the use of UFTA in complex cardiovascular procedures, such as aortic dissection repair, remain limited, resulting in considerable clinical debate.

The aortic dissection surgery requires prolonged cardiopulmonary bypass and deep hypothermic circulatory arrest techniques, which can lead to systemic inflammatory responses and consumption of coagulation factors in patients. In addition, if the patient had insufficient organ perfusion before the surgery, all these factors can result in delayed postoperative awakening, impaired coagulation function, and an increase in postoperative complications. Therefore, the risks of early extubation include: (I) sympathetic nerve excitation caused by postoperative pain and delirium during the awakening period, which can increase anastomotic exudation and bleeding; (II) insufficient extubation timing leading to respiratory dysfunction after extubation; (III) how to achieve rapid anesthesia recovery. In recent years, some cardiac centers have improved through surgical techniques and anesthesia management, and have controlled the time between postoperative extubation and control of most aortic dissection surgery patients within 6–8 hours under controllable risks. Based on clinical practice in the center, implementing UFTA in low-risk patients with ATAAD may reduce postoperative complications with ensuring safety. This study aimed to assess the safety and efficacy of UFTA in individuals undergoing surgery for ATAAD. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0704/rc).

Methods

General materials

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (No. 2023-KY-232-01). Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement of patient consent for inclusion was waived by the institutional Ethics Committee. Patients who underwent ATAAD surgery at the Cardiovascular Center of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University between January 2023 and December 2024 were consecutively included. All participants provided written informed consent after receiving a comprehensive explanation of the UFTA protocol, including its procedural details, potential risks and benefits, and alternatives.

❖ Inclusion criteria: (I) diagnosis of ATAAD confirmed by computed tomography angiography (CTA); (II) emergency surgery performed.

❖ Exclusion criteria: (I) age ≥70 years; (II) New York Heart Association (NYHA) cardiac function classification class III or higher; (III) preoperative evidence of cerebral infarction or hemorrhage on CT; (IV) echocardiographic findings indicating severe valvular disease or cardiac dysfunction, including pulmonary arterial hypertension, moderate to large pericardial effusion, moderate to severe valvular disease, or left ventricular ejection fraction ≤50%; (V) presence of severe preoperative comorbidities such as renal insufficiency (serum creatinine >17), hepatic insufficiency, or poorly controlled diabetes mellitus (hemoglobin A1c >7%); (VI) CTA findings of moderate to severe coronary artery stenosis; (VII) preoperative shock or organ malperfusion (lactate >4 mmol/L and urine output <0.5 mL/kg/h for 2 hours); (VIII) ruptured aortic dissection; (IX) incomplete medical records or missing observational data; (X) intraoperative uncontrollable bleeding or hemodynamic instability.

Anesthesia and CPB protocol

Upon arrival in the operating room, standard monitoring was initiated, including continuous electrocardiography, pulse oxygen saturation (SpO2), bispectral index (BIS), and cerebral oxygen saturation (rSO2) measured by near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS), with baseline values recorded. Invasive arterial blood pressure in both the upper and lower limbs was measured using catheters placed in the radial artery and dorsalis pedis artery. Peripheral venous access was established.

Anesthesia induction was achieved using ciprofol 0.4 mg/kg, betamethasone 8 mg, sufentanil 0.5–1.5 µg/kg, rocuronium bromide 1–1.5 mg/kg, penehyclidine 0.5 mg, remimazolam 0.3 mg/kg, and tranexamic acid 30 mg/kg. Following tracheal intubation, mechanical ventilation was initiated with the following parameters: plateau airway maintained at 25 cmH2O, inspired oxygen concentration between 50% and 100%, tidal volume of 4–6 mL/kg, respiratory rate of 10–16 breaths/min, and an inspiratory-to-expiratory ratio of 1:2. End-tidal carbon dioxide partial pressure (PETCO2) was maintained within the range of 35–45 mmHg.

Subsequently, ultrasound-guided catheterization of the right internal jugular vein was performed, and a transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) probe was inserted. During the operation, additional parameters were monitored, including PETCO2, pulmonary artery pressure, central venous pressure (CVP), urine output, and core body temperature via nasopharyngeal and bladder probes.

Anesthesia protocol for the control group

In the control group, anesthesia was maintained using a continuous infusion of remimazolam (10–15 mg/h), vecuronium (0.05 mg/kg/h), propofol (4–6 mg/kg/h), remifentanil (15–20 µg/kg/h), sufentanil (0.3–0.75 µg/kg/h), and tranexamic acid (15 mg/kg/h). The dosages of remifentanil and sufentanil were adjusted according to surgical stage and intraoperative requirements. Depth of anesthesia was monitored and maintained within a BIS range of 40 to 60. All medications were administered via continuous infusion throughout the procedure.

Following surgery, patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) was initiated and last for three days. The regimen consisted of oxycodone hydrochloride (total three-day dose: 120–160 mg), dexmedetomidine (total three-day dose: 0.2–0.4 mg), and dolasetron (total three-day dose: 75 mg), which were diluted in normal saline to a total volume of 150 mL. Patients were transferred to the ICU while intubated for emergence from anesthesia.

Extubation was performed in the ICU when the following criteria were met: (I) restoration of spontaneous respiration, with a tidal volume ≥6 mL/kg, respiratory rate of 10–20 breaths/min, and SpO2 >95% with an inspiratory oxygen concentration <50%; (II) absence of active bleeding or coagulopathy; (III) hemodynamic stability, defined as no requirement for vasoactive agents or only low-dose vasoactive support, with a vasoactive inotropic score <15 and no arrhythmias; (IV) arterial blood gas (ABG) values within acceptable physiological ranges.

Anesthesia protocol for the UFTA group

Anesthesia was maintained using a continuous infusion of remimazolam (10–15 mg/h), vecuronium (0.05 mg/kg/h), propofol (2–4 mg/kg/h), remifentanil (20–30 µg/kg/h), sufentanil (0.1–0.3 µg/kg/h), and tranexamic acid (15 mg/kg/h). The depth of anesthesia was monitored using the BIS, maintained within a range of 40 to 60. PCIA was initiated via a pump and last for three days. The regimen consisted of oxycodone hydrochloride (total three-day dose: 120–160 mg), dexmedetomidine (total three-day dose: 0.2–0.4 mg), and dolasetron (total 3-day dose: 75 mg), which were diluted in normal saline to a total volume of 150 mL. rSO2 was consistently maintained above 80% of the baseline value. In cases where rSO2 declined, measures like focal brain cooling and elevation of mean arterial pressure (MAP) were implemented. The depth of anesthesia, as monitored by BIS, was kept within the 40–60 range through titration of standard anesthetic medications, without the addition of alternative sedative or analgesic agents.

Following reversal of heparin with protamine after CPB, ABG analysis, activated clotting time (ACT), and thromboelastography (TEG) were monitored. Protamine and blood products were administered as required based on ACT and TEG results. After removal of the aortic cannula for CPB, the infusion sufentanil rate was adjusted to 0.15–0.25 µg/kg/h. Secretions in the endotracheal tube and oral cavity were removed using a suction catheter, followed by manual lung inflation.

Prior to chest closure, administration of vecuronium and sufentanil were discontinued. Sevoflurane was discontinued, without being initiated as a maintenance sedative agent. Infusion rates of propofol, remimazolam, and remifentanil were adjusted to provide adequate analgesia and sedation. Preemptive analgesia was administered using acetaminophen-mannitol injection (500 mg) and sufentanil (10 µg). Upon completion of surgery, a chest drainage system was connected, and oral suctioning and manual lung inflation were repeated. Propofol infusion was adjusted to 50–100 mg/h and remifentanil infusion was adjusted to 50–100 µg/h.

Ultrasound-guided bilateral transversus thoracis plane (TTP) nerve block was conducted using 40 mL of 0.375% ropivacaine. Extubation preparations included rescue vasoactive medications, anesthetic agents, and specific antagonists (sugammadex, nalmefene), along with disposable medical supplies like an oropharyngeal airway, endotracheal tube, laryngoscope blade, nebulization mask, and high-flow oxygen systems. Monitoring and respiratory support equipment included a portable monitor and high-flow oxygen devices.

During emergence, BIS was maintained between 65 and 70. Once extubation criteria were met, sugammadex was administered intravenously to reverse neuromuscular blockade, an oropharyngeal airway was placed, and the endotracheal tube was removed. Oxygen was delivered via a nebulization mask containing dexmedetomidine (20 µg) and budesonide (1 mg) at a flow rate of 5–10 L/min, or via high-flow oxygen therapy at ≥30 L/min, as indicated. Throughout oxygenation, SpO2 was maintained at ≥95%, hemodynamic stability was ensured, and ABG analysis confirmed arterial oxygen partial pressure (PaO2) ≥70 mmHg and partial pressure of arterial carbon dioxide (PaCO2) within 30–55 mmHg.

In the cases of this study, the above assessment methods and anesthesia extubation procedures were applied, and there was no instance of re-intubation immediately after tracheal extubation in the operating room. Finally, the patient was transferred to the ICU for handover. The UFTA management process is depicted in Figure 1.

CPB protocol

The CPB circuit was primed with a solution consisting of 1,000 mL of polygeline, 500 mL of plasmalyte, 100 mL of 20% albumin, 250 mL of mannitol, 10 mL of 25% magnesium sulfate, and 2,000 units of heparin sodium. The cerebral perfusion strategy during circulatory arrest was determined based on the individual patient characteristics. Either unilateral antegrade cerebral perfusion via the right axillary artery (flow rate: 6–8 mL/kg) or retrograde cerebral perfusion via the superior vena cava (maintaining a CVP of 25 mmHg) was employed.

Following initiation of CPB, systemic cooling was commenced, and the ascending aorta was cross-clamped. Histidine-tryptophan-ketoglutarate (HTK) cardioplegic solution was infused under direct vision. Concurrently, ice slush was placed in the pericardial cavity. Systemic cooling continued until the nasopharyngeal temperature reached 25 ℃ and the bladder temperature fell below 30 ℃, at which point circulatory arrest with cerebral perfusion was initiated for aortic arch intervention.

During selective cerebral perfusion, rSO2 was used as the primary monitoring parameter. If rSO2 persistently fell below 50% or decreased by more than 20% from baseline, further cooling was applied. If rSO2 stabilized, the temperature was maintained. Balanced ultrafiltration and hemoperfusion (HA380 hemoperfusion cartridges) were conducted continuously throughout CPB to reduce circulating inflammatory mediators.

Following weaning from CPB, modified ultrafiltration (MUF) was performed to concentrate blood and reduce tissue edema. Residual circuit blood was subsequently collected, processed via cell salvage, and concentrated for autologous transfusion, to be used as clinically indicated.

Observation indicators

Primary observation indicators included the results of the first ABG analysis following extubation, the incidence of postoperative complications, reintubation rate, reoperation rate, and 30-day mortality. Secondary observation indicators comprised 24-hour thoracic drainage volume, LoS in the ICU, overall hospital LoS, and hospitalization costs. The total hospitalization cost refers to all the expenses incurred by the patient from admission to discharge, including drug costs, examination costs, monitoring costs, surgical fees, consumable costs, and anesthesia costs, etc.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software, version 26.0. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (x¯±s) and compared using the independent samples t-test. Skewed data were expressed as median (interquartile range) and comparisons were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-squared test with all theoretical frequencies being greater than five; otherwise, the Fisher’s exact test should be used. A P value of <0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Discussion

In recent years, two key trends have shaped the evolution of cardiovascular anesthesia: the adoption of minimally invasive techniques and the implementation of enhanced recovery after cardiac surgery (ERACS) protocols. Early extubation, defined as extubation within six hours postoperatively, has established as a core component of ERACS and has been formally incorporated into the quality evaluation system of the STS (6). UFTA, which integrates goal-directed hemodynamic management, minimally invasive CPB methods, and multimodal analgesia strategies, enables earlier extubation (<1 hour postoperatively) and has demonstrated notable advantages in elective cardiovascular procedures (4). Although the 2024 ERACS guidelines recommend cautious application of UFTA in patients undergoing ventricular assist device implantation, heart transplantation, or emergency surgeries, selective implementation in rigorously screened patients has been reported including cases involving ventricular assist devices facilitated by advanced perioperative monitoring tools such as TEG, NIRS, and BIS (2). This study was the first to compare UFTA and standard general anesthesia and the results demonstrated shorter mechanical ventilation duration (achieved through intraoperative extubation), a 10.5% reduction in postoperative drainage volume, and a 17% reduction in hospitalization costs in the UFTA group. Due to the limitations of the research design and sample size, this study can only prove that UFTA may be applicable to low-risk ATAAD patients, but it cannot be widely applied to all types of ATAAD patients.

In this study, two positive indicators emerged after the surgery: a reduction in postoperative drainage volume and an increase in total hospitalization costs. The mechanism behind the reduction in drainage volume may involve multiple physiological and clinical aspects. Firstly, early extubation significantly alleviated the systemic stress response caused by the surgery and mechanical ventilation. Mechanical ventilation itself acts as a stressor, activating the sympathetic nervous system, promoting the release of catecholamines and inflammatory factors, increasing vascular permeability, and leading to increased tissue exudation. Studies have shown that patients who underwent early extubation using ultra-fast-track cardiac anesthesia had a significantly lower total postoperative thoracic drainage volume (P=0.001), and a reduced need for blood transfusion (7). Additionally, the application of early extubation strategies in minimally invasive cardiac surgery also showed that the postoperative drainage volume was significantly less than that of the conventional anesthesia group (8). Secondly, the recovery of spontaneous breathing after extubation helps to optimize hemodynamics, improve venous return and cardiac output, especially enhancing portal vein and liver blood flow perfusion. The synthesis of coagulation factors and lymphatic return balance were thus promoted, and mediastinal or thoracic effusion were reduced. To date, no study has definitively established a causal link between drainage output and mechanical ventilation parameters. Future studies designed specifically to explore this mechanistic relationship are warranted. The reduction in total hospitalization costs may result from multiple reasons. Patients in the UFTA group had their tracheal tubes removed after surgery, which saved the costs of mechanical ventilation and related medications compared to the control group. Although the incidence of postoperative complications did not show a clear decrease (no significant positive results were presented), the incidence did decrease. The reason for this result may be related to the sample size deviation caused by insufficient sample size. In actual clinical practice, we found that after the complication rate in the UFTA group decreased, the corresponding treatment costs for complications could be saved, ultimately leading to economic benefits for the patients. Due to the strict management requirements of the ICU and the hospital, many patients in the UFTA group, considering the severity of aortic dissection and the requirements of patients and their families for postoperative rehabilitation, still remained in the ICU and the hospital for an additional period of observation although they had recovered to the standards for leaving the ICU and being discharged. This may have led to negative results in terms of ICU time and hospital stay, but the total hospitalization cost was reduced.

Patient selection: transition from “infeasible” to “highly selective”

The inclusion and exclusion criteria in this study facilitated the preliminary identification of patients with “low-risk ATAAD”, thereby increasing the likelihood of successful UFTA implementation. However, this approach may introduce selection bias and potentially overestimate the efficacy of UFTA. Bonatti et al. [2023], in a publication in JTCVS, cautioned that excessive reliance on anatomical and physiological exclusion criteria may exclude older adult patients with multiple comorbidities, who stand to benefit the most from ERAS protocols (9). Consequently, future research may require the development of a dynamic risk prediction system using artificial intelligence (AI) technology, integrating parameters like patient age, cardiac function, comorbidities, and the location of the dissection entry. This would allow for broader application of UFTA without compromising patient safety, while also enhancing the methodological robustness and external validity of future clinical investigations (10).

Transition in anesthetic concepts: from experience-based to precision management

The implementation of UFTA in ATAAD surgery represents a shift from traditional, experience-based anesthetic practices toward a precision-oriented approach. This transition is reflected in four key domains:

Precision monitoring: a comprehensive intraoperative monitoring framework was established incorporating TEE, BIS, NIRS, TEG, ABG analysis, and Swan-Ganz catheterization. These modalities provided real-time, quantitative feedback to guide anesthesia depth, coagulation function, and perioperative circulatory management (11).

These modalities provided real-time, quantitative feedback to guide anesthesia depth, coagulation function, and perioperative circulatory management (11).

Precision drug administration: BIS and NIRS values were employed as target-oriented parameters for administering remimazolam and low-dose propofol via target-controlled infusion to maintain BIS between 40 and 60. A combination of short- and long-acting opioids was selected according to surgical progression to prevent drug accumulation under conditions of hypothermic anesthesia and low cardiac output, thereby facilitating early emergence and respiratory recovery (12).

Postoperative analgesiain the UFTA group was managed using a multimodal regimen combining ultrasound-guided TTP nerve block with PCIA. This replaced the single intravenous analgesia pump used in the control group. The TTP block significantly reduced postoperative analgesic consumption and visual analog scale pain scores (14).

Shift from passive maintenance to active protection of organ function

The successful achievement of early extubation in ATAAD surgery relies on proactive, organ-targeted protection strategies. In this study, a standardized perioperative protocol was adopted to actively safeguard pulmonary, cerebral, and myocardial function: (I) a lung-protective ventilation strategy was employed, comprising low tidal volume (4–6 mL/kg), dynamic positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP, 4–6 cmH2O), and high-frequency ventilation (14–18 breaths/min); (II) a combination of glucocorticoids and ulinastatin was administered to modulate the inflammatory response; (III) the CPB priming solution composed of crystalloid (500 mL), colloid (1,000 mL), and 20% albumin (100 mL) to optimize intravascular volume and oncotic pressure; and (IV) continuous balanced ultrafiltration during CPB, followed by MUF after weaning, was utilized to maintain hemoglobin ≥10 g/dL and ensure hematocrit >24%.

A targeted brain protection strategy was employed based on real-time monitoring of rSO2 was implemented (15). Interventions were initiated when rSO2 decreased by more than 10% from baseline and included elevating MAP to 70–80 mmHg, reducing regional brain temperature, adding 1 g/kg mannitol to the priming solution, maintaining strict glycemic control (<10 mmol/L), and minimizing the duration of cerebral perfusion during deep hypothermia circulation arrest (DHCA) in CPB.

Myocardial protection measures were applied during both anesthesia and CPB. During anesthesia, coronary perfusion pressure was maintained at ≥60 mmHg, and myocardial oxygen consumption was reduced (rate-pressure product, RPP <12,000). During CPB, HTK cardioplegic solution was administered along with regional hypothermia. Mild hypothermic anesthesia (28–34 ℃) was maintained after aortic cross-clamping, and ice slush was placed in the pericardial cavity. Following cross-clamp removal, TEE and hemodynamic parameters obtained from a Swan-Ganz catheter were used to guide fluid resuscitation and the administration of vasoactive agents.

From single-disciplinary treatment to multidisciplinary collaboration

The management of ATAAD in this study exemplifies a shift from isolated, single-disciplinary care toward an integrated, multidisciplinary model.

Process optimization: a streamlined “green channel” was established to expedite the emergency management of ATAAD. Preparations for anesthesia, CPB, and the operating room readiness were conducted concurrently with preoperative examinations and preparations, reducing the interval between emergency admission and surgery to within 60 minutes.

Economic benefits

The cost analysis demonstrated a substantial reduction in healthcare costs associated with UFTA. The average per capita medical expenditure was reduced by 48,000 CNY, primarily attributable to three factors: (I) decreased utilization of ventilator-associated supplies and respiratory support resources; (II) reduced incidence of complications requiring additional interventions. Given an estimated annual volume of 5,000 aortic dissection surgeries in China, the widespread adoption of UFTA could potentially yield national healthcare savings of approximately 240 million CNY per year. These economic advantages align with current healthcare policy reforms, particularly under the diagnosis-related group (DRG) payment reforms. In 2024, the National Healthcare Security Administration incorporated aortic surgery into the CHS-DRG subgrouping scheme (16).

Despite these promising findings, several limitations must be acknowledged. Firstly, this study was a single-center retrospective study. Although strict inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied, and patients with similar preoperative baseline conditions were selected, there were still confounding factors that affected the results of the study data. The retrospective design of the study meant that not all relevant data points were captured in the original records. Secondly, the single-center retrospective design inherently introduces selection bias, as the patients included in the analysis represented a “best-case” subgroup. The UFTA group may represent a selected cohort with inherently better prognosis. This inherent selection bias means that the observed associations between UFTA and improved outcomes (e.g., earlier extubation, reduced ICU stay) cannot be interpreted as purely causal. Thirdly, the small sample size (n=90) limited statistical power for finding rare complications, such as paraplegia, and may have contributed to the absence of significant differences in certain recovery-phase data. Our study may have been underpowered to detect differences in these less frequent, albeit serious, complication endpoints (type II error), and perhaps more likely, the reduction in drainage may be driven by other factors integral to the UFTA protocol but not directly related to inflammation, such as more meticulous surgical hemostasis facilitated by earlier awakening, differences in transfusion algorithms or targeted coagulation management, or variations in perioperative fluid administration. Fourthly, long-term outcomes, including one-year survival rates and neurological function assessments, were not available. As such, the impact of UFTA on long-term quality of life could not be evaluated. Fifthly, the generalizability of the findings is limited. The successful implementation of UFTA requires close collaboration among surgery, anesthesia, CPB, and ICU teams, as well as the participation of highly experienced physicians. Centers performing fewer than 50 aortic dissection surgeries annually may not meet the conditions for adoption. Although preoperative inflammatory markers were measured, dynamic monitoring of early postoperative changes in inflammatory factors like interleukin-1β and tumor necrosis factor-α was not performed. Finally, multivariate analysis to control for potential confounders was not performed, which is crucial for establishing independent associations. Future studies should employ multicenter, large-sample, prospective cohort designs to validate the current findings and further assess the application of UFTA in complex cardiovascular surgeries. The incorporation of propensity score matching or comprehensive multivariable adjustment in future comparative analyses. Specific investigation of UFTA in patients with malperfusion or ventricular dysfunction to define its boundaries of safety and utility. Additionally, the development of predictive models leveraging AI tools like DeepSeek or ChatGPT may enhance patient selection and perioperative imaging. These models should integrate diverse preoperative and intraoperative variables, including dissection entry site, (from imaging), surgical technique, inflammatory markers [e.g., interleukin-2 (IL-2), interleukin-6 (IL-6)], biomarkers [e.g., N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), cardiac troponin I (cTnI)], and intraoperative variables (e.g., operative duration, aortic cross-clamp duration, circulatory arrest duration). Such models could facilitate the creation of a UFTA applicability scoring system to guide individualized decision-making.

Conclusions

The findings of this study indicate that maybe UFTA is both feasible and safe when applied to rigorously selected low risk patients with ATAAD. Compared with conventional anesthesia management, UFTA was associated with significant reductions in 24-hour thoracic drainage volume and overall treatment costs, without an observed increase in the postoperative complications. However, large-scale, multicenter randomized controlled trials are warranted to confirm these results and to further refine anesthesia management strategies aimed at improving patient outcomes in high-risk cardiovascular surgery.
