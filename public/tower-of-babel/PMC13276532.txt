HERC4 suppresses colorectal cancer progression by promoting STAT3 ubiquitination
Authors: Lanzhu Jiang, Kaidi Qin, Yuan Jin, Tong Zhu, Tianci Xie, Lanlan Yin, Yu Deng, Fayong Hu, Qi Wu, Zilong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryUbiquitination, a critical post-translational modification, regulates diverse cellular processes including protein degradation, signal transduction, and cell cycle progression. Dysregulation of the ubiquitin system is closely associated with tumor development and progression. HERC4, a member of the HECT-type E3 ubiquitin ligase family, remains poorly characterized in colorectal cancer (CRC). In this study, we report that HERC4 is significantly downregulated in CRC tissues and is associated with unfavorable patient prognosis. Functional assays demonstrate that HERC4 suppresses CRC cell proliferation both in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, we identify that HERC4 directly interacts with STAT3 and promotes its ubiquitination via K48-linked polyubiquitin chains, leading to proteasomal degradation. Further mapping analysis reveals that lysine 392 (K392) on STAT3 is the specific ubiquitination site targeted by HERC4 This study not only highlights the biological significance of HERC4 in colorectal cancer but also suggests the HERC4-STAT3 axis as a potential therapeutic target for intervention.

Introduction

After protein synthesis, non-histone proteins frequently undergo a variety of enzyme-mediated changes collectively known as post-translational modifications (PTMs). These modifications, including phosphorylation, acetylation, methylation, and ubiquitination, play key roles in regulating protein stability, enzymatic activity, and protein-protein interactions. Recent studies have shown that dysregulation of non-histone PTMs is closely linked to a range of human diseases, particularly in cancer development, neurodegenerative disorders, metabolic dysfunction, and immune system dysregulation.1,2,3 Ubiquitination is a highly conserved and functionally versatile PTM, in which ubiquitin molecules are covalently attached to specific lysine residues on target proteins to regulate a wide range of cellular processes. This modification is essential for maintaining protein homeostasis, regulating the cell cycle, facilitating DNA damage repair, and precisely modulating signal transduction pathways.4,5 Ubiquitination is orchestrated by a cascade of enzymes, including E1 activating enzymes, E2 conjugating enzymes, and E3 ligases. Among them, E3 ubiquitin ligases serve as the key regulatory component of the ubiquitin-proteasome system by specifically recognizing, binding, and recruiting substrate proteins for ubiquitin tagging through their substrate recognition domains. E3 ligases play pivotal roles in multiple hallmark signaling pathways involved in tumor initiation and progression, functioning either as oncogenes or tumor suppressors depending on the context. Owing to their high substrate specificity and central role in cancer-associated signaling networks, E3 ligases have emerged as promising therapeutic targets, with several anti-cancer drugs currently under development or in clinical use that specifically target these enzymes.6,7,8,9,10 HERC4 is a member of the HECT (homologous to the E6-AP carboxyl terminus) family of E3 ubiquitin ligases and plays a critical role in regulating the stability, localization, and function of various proteins. Recent studies have shown that HERC4 suppresses the proliferation of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) by destabilizing the oncogenic protein smoothened (Smo).11 Additionally, studies have shown that HERC4 promotes the ubiquitination and subsequent destabilization of LATS1, thereby facilitating the initiation and metastasis of breast cancer.12 However, the precise role of HERC4 in colorectal cancer (CRC) remains poorly understood.

Cell proliferation plays a critical role in tumor initiation and progression, and its dysregulation is a major driver of cancer development. Multiple signaling pathways regulate cell proliferation, among which the JAK-STAT3 (Janus kinase-signal transducer and activator of transcription 3) pathway is one of the most well-established and functionally versatile. Upon activation of cytokine receptors on the cell membrane, JAKs become phosphorylated and subsequently phosphorylate STAT3, triggering its translocation into the nucleus. In the nucleus, STAT3 functions as a transcription factor, regulating the expression of genes involved in cell growth, differentiation, immune modulation, and oncogenesis.13,14 In various types of cancer, this pathway is often constitutively activated, leading to enhanced proliferative capacity and disruption of normal growth control mechanisms. As a result, it is considered a key driver of tumor initiation and progression. Beyond its physiological role, the JAK-STAT3 pathway has emerged as a promising therapeutic target in cancer and other diseases, underscoring its significant research and clinical potential.15,16,17

This study systematically reveals, the role of HERC4 as a potential tumor suppressor in colorectal cancer (CRC), with its reduced expression significantly associated with poor patient prognosis. Mechanistic investigations further demonstrate that HERC4 mediates its tumor-suppressive effects by regulating the STAT3 signaling pathway. Specifically, we found that HERC4 directly interacts with STAT3 and promotes its ubiquitination, facilitating STAT3 degradation. This suggests that HERC4 plays a critical role in maintaining STAT3 protein stability. These findings not only deepen our understanding of HERC4’s biological function in CRC but also provide a molecular basis and potential therapeutic target for interventions aimed at the HERC4/STAT3 axis.

Results

HERC4 is downregulated in CRC

To further investigate the potential role of HERC4 in tumorigenesis and cancer progression, we performed a comprehensive analysis of its expression across multiple cancer types using the SangerBox platform based on data from the TCGA and GTEx databases (Figure 1A).The results revealed that HERC4 expression was significantly downregulated in colorectal cancer (CRC) tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues (Figures 1B and 1C). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis further demonstrated that CRC patients with low HERC4 expression had significantly poorer relapse-free survival (RFS) and post-progression survival (PPS) (Figures 1D and 1E), whereas those with high HERC4 expression exhibited a more favorable prognosis. To validate the database findings, we collected 12 pairs of CRC and matched adjacent non-tumor tissues for experimental analysis. Protein analysis consistently showed that HERC4 expression was markedly reduced in CRC tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues (Figures 1F and 1G). In addition, immunohistochemical (IHC) staining further confirmed this trend, with stronger HERC4 positivity observed in normal colorectal mucosa and markedly weaker expression in tumor tissues (Figures 1H and 1I). Inhibition of HERC4 promotes colorectal cancer proliferation both in vitro and in vivo

Given the previously observed downregulation of HERC4 expression in colorectal cancer (CRC), we further investigated its functional role in tumorigenesis and progression. To this end, we generated two stable HERC4 knockdown CRC cell lines (shHERC4#1 and shHERC4#2) in LOVO and HCT116 cells using lentivirus-mediated RNA interference. Knockdown efficiency was confirmed by western blot analysis (Figure 2A).To evaluate the impact of HERC4 on CRC cell proliferation, we first performed CCK-8 assays to assess cell viability. The results showed that HERC4 knockdown significantly enhanced the viability of CRC cells (Figure 2B), suggesting a potential inhibitory role of HERC4 in cell proliferation. EdU incorporation assays further demonstrated that HERC4 knockdown markedly increased DNA synthesis activity, supporting its role in promoting CRC cell proliferation (Figure 2C). Colony formation assays also revealed that silencing HERC4 significantly enhanced the clonogenic potential of CRC cells compared to controls (Figure 2D), indicating that HERC4 negatively regulates CRC cell proliferation and clonogenicity. To better simulate the in vivo tumor microenvironment, we established xenograft mouse models with HERC4-deficient CRC cells. Tumor growth was significantly accelerated in the HERC4 knockdown group, with both tumor volume and weight markedly increased compared to controls (Figures 2E–2G). Immunohistochemical analysis of the tumor tissues showed elevated Ki-67 expression in the HERC4 knockdown group (Figure 2H). Immunohistochemical analysis of tumor tissues and matched adjacent normal tissues from six colorectal cancer patients revealed a marked reduction in HERC4 expression in tumor tissues, accompanied by a significant increase in the proportion of Ki-67-positive cells (Figures S1A and S1B). HERC4 directly interacts with STAT3 and promotes its ubiquitination

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of the GEO dataset revealed a significant association between reduced HERC4 expression and activation of the IL-6/STAT3 signaling pathway (Figure 3A). To explore their interaction, we constructed HA-tagged HERC4 and Flag-tagged STAT3 overexpression plasmids and co-transfected them into HEK293T cells. Co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) experiments confirmed an exogenous interaction between HERC4 and STAT3 (Figure 3B). This interaction was further validated at the endogenous level in LOVO cells, yielding consistent results (Figure 3C). Molecular docking simulations illustrated a potential binding interface between HERC4 and STAT3 in three-dimensional space, providing structural support for their interaction (Figure 3D).An in vitro pull-down assay further confirmed the direct binding between HERC4 and GST-STAT3, but not with GST alone, indicating specificity of the interaction (Figure S1C). To investigate whether HERC4 regulates STAT3 protein expression, we transfected increasing amounts of HERC4 expression plasmids into CRC HCT116 and LOVO cells. Western blot analysis revealed a dose-dependent decrease in STAT3 protein levels with increasing HERC4 expression (Figure 3G), suggesting that HERC4 may negatively regulate STAT3 stability or expression. Conversely, silencing HERC4 in LOVO and HCT116 cells significantly increased STAT3 protein levels (Figure 3H), further supporting a role for HERC4 in negatively regulating STAT3 expression. Western blot analysis of clinical samples from 10 colorectal cancer patients (Figures S1E and S1F) revealed a significant inverse correlation between HERC4 and STAT3 protein expression. Given that intracellular protein degradation is mainly mediated by the proteasomal and lysosomal pathways, we first treated cells with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 and the lysosome inhibitor chloroquine (CQ) to determine the pathway responsible for STAT3 degradation. MG132 markedly reversed the HERC4-induced reduction in STAT3 protein levels, whereas CQ had no significant effect (Figure 3I). In parallel, we genetically disrupted proteasome function by shRNA-mediated knockdown of the core catalytic subunit PSMB5 and examined STAT3 stability under conditions of HERC4 overexpression. When proteasome activity was impaired, HERC4 was no longer able to reduce STAT3 protein levels (Figure S1G), indicating that STAT3 degradation is predominantly mediated through the proteasomal pathway. To further assess whether HERC4 affects STAT3 protein stability, we overexpressed HERC4 in LOVO and HCT116 cells and treated them with cycloheximide (CHX, 50 μg/mL) to block protein synthesis. STAT3 degradation was then monitored at various time points. HERC4 overexpression markedly reduced the half-life of STAT3 (Figure 3J), confirming its role in promoting STAT3 protein degradation. As HERC4 is an E3 ubiquitin ligase, we hypothesized that it may facilitate STAT3 degradation via ubiquitination. To test this, we co-transfected HERC4 and STAT3 into HEK293T cells and performed ubiquitination immunoprecipitation assays. HERC4 overexpression significantly increased STAT3 ubiquitination (Figure 3K).Conversely, HERC4 knockdown in LOVO cells led to a clear reduction in endogenous STAT3 ubiquitination (Figure 3L).Together, these findings indicate that HERC4 promotes the ubiquitin-mediated proteasomal degradation of STAT3, thereby reducing its protein stability.

HERC4 catalyzes K48-linked ubiquitination of STAT3 at the lysine 392 (K392) residue

Previous studies have shown that ubiquitin (Ub) contains seven lysine residues (K6, K11, K27, K29, K33, K48, and K63), each of which can serve as a linkage site for the formation of polyubiquitin chains. The type of linkage determines the fate of the substrate protein. For instance, K48-linked chains typically target proteins for proteasomal degradation, whereas K63-linked chains are more commonly involved in non-proteolytic functions such as signal transduction, DNA damage response, and autophagy.18 To determine the linkage specificity of HERC4-mediated STAT3 ubiquitination, we generated a panel of HA-tagged ubiquitin mutants, each retaining only one lysine residue while the remaining six were substituted with arginine. These mutants were designed to mimic specific ubiquitin linkage types. Co-transfection of these single-lysine ubiquitin constructs with HERC4 and STAT3 into 293T cells, followed by immunoprecipitation, revealed that HERC4 specifically promotes K48-linked polyubiquitination of STAT3 (Figure 4A), suggesting that HERC4 facilitates STAT3 degradation via a K48-dependent mechanism. Consistent results were obtained in LOVO cells with HERC4 knockdown co-transfected with HA-K48 ubiquitin (Figure 4B).To further identify the specific lysine residue on STAT3 targeted by HERC4 for ubiquitination, we constructed three Flag-tagged STAT3 truncation plasmids based on its functional domains (Figure 4C). Co-immunoprecipitation assays revealed that fragment 2 (amino acids 320–585) specifically interacts with HERC4 (Figure 4D). Subsequent mass spectrometry analysis (Figure S1H) of flag-STAT3 overexpressed in HEK293T cells identified five lysine residues within fragment 2—K340, K363, K392, K451, and K495—as potential ubiquitination sites (Figure 4E). Among them, K392 ubiquitination was further validated by tandem mass spectrometry (Figure 4F). To determine the critical lysine residue through which HERC4 mediates STAT3 ubiquitination, we generated a series of lysine-to-arginine (K→R) point mutants to block ubiquitin conjugation. Each mutant was co-transfected with HERC4 into 293T cells, followed by co-immunoprecipitation analysis. Among all the mutants tested, only the K392R substitution markedly reduced HERC4-induced STAT3 ubiquitination (Figure 4G), Similarly, knockdown of HERC4 did not reduce the ubiquitination of the K392R mutant (Figure S1I),indicating that K392 is the primary ubiquitination site. Moreover, sequence alignment revealed that this residue is highly conserved across multiple species (Figure S1J), underscoring its biological significance. Given that HERC4 primarily facilitates K48-linked polyubiquitin chain formation, we further investigated whether STAT3 K392 is critical for this specific ubiquitin linkage. The tumor-suppressive effect of HERC4 on cell proliferation is mediated by STAT3

Previous studies have shown that STAT3, a well-established oncogenic transcription factor, promotes the transcriptional activation of c-Myc and cooperates with Cyclin E1 to regulate cell cycle progression.19,20 In this study, knockdown of HERC4 in LOVO and HCT116 colorectal cancer cells led to a significant increase in the expression of c-Myc and cyclin E1, mirroring the changes observed in STAT3 levels (Figure S2B).Furthermore, western blot analysis confirmed that simultaneous silencing of STAT3 in HERC4-deficient cells reversed the HERC4 knockdown–induced upregulation of c-Myc and cyclin E1, and the expression levels of c-Myc and cyclin E1 in the double-knockdown group were comparable to those observed in the STAT3 knockdown-alone group (Figures 5A and S2C).These findings suggest that HERC4 indirectly modulates the expression of downstream oncogenic factors by regulating STAT3, further supporting its tumor-suppressive role in colorectal cancer cell proliferation. To validate this observation, we performed a series of phenotypic assays, including the CCK-8 assay for cell viability (Figure 5B), colony formation assay to assess proliferative capacity (Figures 5C and S2D), and EDU incorporation assay to evaluate DNA synthesis (Figures 5D and S2E). Compared with cells with HERC4 knockdown alone, simultaneous STAT3 knockdown significantly reduced cell viability, colony formation, and DNA synthesis, consistent with the western blot results. To further substantiate these findings, we conducted in vivo experiments using a xenograft tumor model in nude mice. Tumors in the double-knockdown group (HERC4 and STAT3) exhibited significantly slower growth, reduced volume, and lower tumor weight compared to the HERC4 knockdown group alone (Figures 5E–5G). Inhibition of STAT3 ubiquitination at the K392 site partially abrogated its tumor-promoting effect on cell proliferation

We have demonstrated that HERC4 catalyzes the ubiquitination of STAT3 at lysine 392 (K392). To investigate the functional relevance of this modification, we generated STAT3 knockdown LOVO and HCT116 colorectal cancer cell lines (Figure S2F), followed by rescue experiments using either wild-type STAT3 or a K392R mutant in which lysine was replaced with arginine to prevent ubiquitination. Western blot analysis revealed that, compared to wild-type STAT3, the K392R mutant exhibited significantly higher protein levels of STAT3 and elevated expression of its downstream oncogenic targets, c-Myc and cyclin E1 (Figure 6A). These findings suggest that mutation at K392 impairs STAT3 degradation, thereby enhancing its stability and promoting oncogenic signaling. To evaluate the effect of this mutation on cell proliferation, we conducted in vitro phenotypic assays. CCK-8, colony formation, and EDU incorporation assays consistently showed that the K392R mutation significantly increased cell viability, clonogenic potential, and DNA synthesis compared to wild-type STAT3 (Figures 6B–6D, S2G, and S2H), indicating a pro-proliferative effect. We further validated these findings in vivo using a mouse xenograft model established with the engineered LOVO cells. Tumor measurements and growth curve analyses revealed that the K392R mutation markedly enhanced tumor growth compared to wild-type STAT3 (Figures 6E–6G). Discussion

Similar to phosphorylation, acetylation, and methylation, ubiquitination is a key PTM of proteins that has attracted increasing attention in recent years.21 Ubiquitination is a critical regulatory mechanism that controls the stability, localization, activity, and interactions of non-histone protein substrates. Specific types of ubiquitin linkages—particularly K6, K27, K29, K48, and K63 chains—have been implicated in diverse cellular processes, including trafficking, signal transduction, DNA repair, protein degradation, and autophagy.22,23,24,25,26,27,28,29 This process is particularly critical in tumorigenesis and cancer therapy. Numerous key non-histone proteins—including p53, STAT3, NF-κB, and c-Myc—are regulated by specific E3 ubiquitin ligases or deubiquitinating enzymes.30,31,32,33 Research on ubiquitination is uncovering a complex regulatory network involving oncogenic drivers, ubiquitin-modifying enzymes, and downstream signaling pathways. Therapeutic agents targeting the ubiquitin system are being developed for the treatment of cancer, autoimmune disorders, and neurodegenerative diseases. The three key enzymes in the ubiquitin cascade—E1 activating enzymes, E2 conjugating enzymes, and E3 ligases—as well as deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs), have all emerged as promising drug targets, highlighting the vast therapeutic potential of modulating the ubiquitin system.34,35 In this study, we systematically elucidated the biological function and underlying mechanism of HERC4 in colorectal cancer (CRC). We demonstrated that HERC4 promotes the proteasomal degradation of STAT3 by catalyzing its K48-linked polyubiquitination, thereby reducing STAT3 nuclear accumulation and transcriptional activity. This process suppresses the expression of STAT3-driven oncogenic targets such as c-Myc and cyclin E1, ultimately inhibiting CRC cell proliferation. Our findings identify HERC4 as an endogenous regulator of STAT3 and a potential therapeutic intervention point, particularly in tumors characterized by hyperactive STAT3 signaling. The antitumor effect of HERC4 was validated in both in vitro and in vivo models, providing robust experimental support for its prospective clinical application in CRC. Nevertheless, we acknowledge a limitation of the present study in that STAT3 K392 knock-in cells were not generated. Although our biochemical and functional data strongly support a role for HERC4-mediated ubiquitination in regulating STAT3 stability, the absence of this genetic model may limit the precise interpretation of the contribution of this specific lysine residue in vivo, and warrants further investigation in future studies. Beyond the mechanism identified in this study—where HERC4 facilitates STAT3 degradation through K48-linked ubiquitination to suppress CRC proliferation—previous reports have indicated that HERC4 also functions as an E3 ubiquitin ligase in other cancer models. For instance, HERC4 catalyzes the non-degradative, K63-linked polyubiquitination of transcription factor MafA to suppress its transcriptional activity.36 In multiple myeloma, it alternatively promotes the proteasomal degradation of c-Maf via K48-linked polyubiquitination at residues K85 and K297, thereby delaying the growth of xenograft tumors.37 This mechanism closely aligns with our findings in colorectal cancer, where HERC4 promotes K48-linked ubiquitination of STAT3 to induce its degradation, suggesting that HERC4 may possess broad-spectrum tumor suppressive activity. Furthermore, studies have shown that STAT3 is aberrantly activated in various cancers and is a key driver in maintaining cancer stem cell traits, drug resistance, and tumor progression. In colorectal cancer, STAT3 remains highly phosphorylated in the ALDH+/CD133+ cancer cell subpopulation, contributing to tumor initiation and the maintenance of tumor-initiating cells. Inhibition of STAT3 significantly reduces cancer stem cell survival and sphere-forming ability, effectively delaying tumor growth in mouse models.38 Therefore, the mechanism by which HERC4 regulates STAT3 degradation extends beyond merely reducing STAT3 expression levels; it likely also impairs STAT3’s critical role in maintaining cancer stem cell properties and drug resistance.Additionally, while ubiquitination regulated by other E3 ligases such as CUL4A (DCAF1) has primarily focused on targets like DICER1 and has not been reported to directly affect STAT3, the related JAK/STAT3 signaling pathway plays a significant role in many solid tumors.Additionally, while ubiquitination regulated by other E3 ligases such as CUL4A (DCAF1) has primarily focused on targets like DICER1 and has not been reported to directly affect STAT3, the related JAK/STAT3 signaling pathway plays a significant role in many solid tumors.39 Notably, the HDAC6 inhibitor Nexturastat A reduces PD-L1 expression in colorectal cancer by interfering with STAT3 activity, indicating that pathways regulating STAT3 are broadly involved in tumor immune evasion and proliferation.40 These findings complement our proposed HERC4-STAT3 ubiquitination-mediated degradation mechanism and provide a theoretical foundation for future clinical strategies combining immunotherapy with targeted STAT3 pathway interventions.

In summary, our study reveals a regulatory mechanism of STAT3 via HERC4-mediated K48-linked ubiquitination and degradation, which aligns with previous reports of HERC4 controlling key oncogenic transcription factors such as c-Maf. This mechanism effectively blocks STAT3-driven downstream oncogene expression, providing targets for signal intervention in colorectal cancer. Future research may focus on screening HERC4 activators, small-molecule modulators, or combination therapies with STAT3 inhibitors to advance the translational application of HERC4 in colorectal cancer.

Limitations of the study

Despite the compelling evidence that HERC4 functions as a tumor suppressor by directly binding to STAT3 and enhancing its K48-linked ubiquitination at the K392 residue to inhibit colorectal cancer (CRC) proliferation, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, we were unable to successfully generate a STAT3 K392R knock-in cell line via CRISPR-Cas9 technology. Although our ubiquitination assays in vitro strongly indicate that K392 is the critical site for HERC4-mediated modification, the absence of this knock-in model limits our ability to definitively demonstrate, under endogenous and dynamic physiological conditions, that the observed anti-tumor effects are exclusively dependent on this specific ubiquitination event. Second, while our findings highlight HERC4 as a potential prognostic biomarker or therapeutic target, the translational application of this mechanism remains in its early stages. The complexities of directly targeting the HERC4-STAT3 axis in a clinical setting—such as challenges in drug delivery, potential off-target effects, and the context-dependent role of STAT3 in different cell types—require extensive further investigation. Nevertheless, our current data provide a solid mechanistic foundation, confirming the direct interaction between HERC4 and STAT3 and establishing K392 as a bona fide site for HERC4-induced K48 ubiquitination, which robustly explains its tumor-suppressive function in CRC.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals

In this study, all female BALB/c nude mice (age, 5 weeks) were obtained from Gempharmatech Co., Ltd (Jiangsu, China) and kept in SPF animal facilities.All animal experiments in this study were approved by the Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital( TJH-202506006).Female BALB/c nude mice (6–8 weeks old) were randomly selected to establish the xenograft tumor model. On day 0, mice were subcutaneously injected with 1 × 106 CRC cells. Tumor size was monitored every 2–3 days using digital calipers, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula: (width2 × length/2). In compliance with ethical guidelines, mice were humanely euthanized via CO2 inhalation at day 24 or 28 post-inoculation.

Cell line and cell culture

Human cell lines HCT116, LOVO, and HEK293T were procured from the American Type Culture Collection. All cell lines were authenticated by STR (Short Tandem Repeat) profiling. All cell lines were free of mycoplasma contamination. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 50 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin. All cell cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator.

Human samples

Clinical samples were obtained from Wuhan Tongji Hospital. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Huazhong University of Science and Technology and the Institutional Ethics Committee. All experiments comply with relevant regulations and standards.

Method details

Reagents

CHX ,Proteasome inhibitor MG132 and chloroquine (CQ)were purchased from Selleck Chemicals (Houston,TX,USA).Antibodies against HERC4(13691-1-AP),STAT3(60199-1-Ig)were obtained from Proteintech(Wuhan,Hubei,China).Antibodies against C-Myc(A19032),CyclinE1(A22360),GAPDH(A190560),Myc(AE070),Flag(AE169PM),HA(AE105)were purchased from ABclonal(Wuhan,Hubei,China).

Plasmid construction

Expression vectors encoding pcDNA3.1-HA-HERC4,pcDNA3.1-Myc-HERC4, pcDNA3.1-Flag-STAT3,pcDNA3.1-Myc-ubiquitin,pcDNA3.1-HA-ubiquitin,were purchased from Miaoling(Wuhan,Hubei,China) (https://www.miaolingbio.com).

Specifically, the pcDNA3.1-STAT3 K392R-Flag vector was generated using the Mut Express II Fast Mutagenesis Kit V2 (Vazyme, China).

Stable knockdown Cell lines

STAT3- and HERC4- knockout cell lines were established using a lentiviral system (pLVX, psPAX2, and pMD2.G).Lentivirus particles were produced in HEK293T cells. HCT116 and LoVo cells were infected and subsequently selected using 2 μg/ ml of puromycin over 2 weeks.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Paired colorectal cancer (CRC) and adjacent non-tumor tissue specimens were collected from CRC patients undergoing surgical resection in the Department of Gastrointestinal Surgery in Wuhan, Hubei Province.IHC scoring was performed independently by two gastrointestinal pathologists.The immunoreactivity score (IRS) was used to quantify IHC staining results.

Staining intensity was graded as follows: 0 (no staining), 1 (weak staining), 2 (moderate staining), or 3 (strong staining).The percentage of positively stained cells was scored on a scale of 1–4 (1: <10%; 2: 11–50%; 3: 51–75%; 4: >75%).The final IHC score was calculated by multiplying the percentage score by the staining intensity score.

Cell viability assay using CCK-8

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; MedChemExpress, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.CRC cells (3 × 103 cells/100 μL) were seeded into 96-well plates and incubated overnight.At the indicated time points, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, mixed with 90 μL of culture medium.After 2 hours of incubation at 37°C, the optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm.

Colony formation assay

Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were trypsinized to prepare a single-cell suspension.

The cell concentration was adjusted (typically 50–1000 cells/well, depending on proliferation rate) using a hemocytometer or automated cell counter.The cell suspension was evenly seeded into 6-well plates (2 mL medium/well).Cells were cultured undisturbed in a 37°C, 5% CO2 incubator for 7–14 days (avoiding plate movement).When visible colonies formed (0.5–1 mm diameter), medium was removed, cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde or methanol, stained with 0.5% crystal violet, gently rinsed under running water, and air-dried.Colonies were counted for quantification.

EdU assay

The EdU assay kit (Abcam, Cat# KTA2031) was used according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Cells were seeded in culture plates or on coverslips.EdU was added to the culture medium. After incubation, the medium was removed and cells were gently washed twice with PBS.Cells were incubated with EdU for 2-24 hours.Cells were then fixed and permeabilized.The reaction cocktail was prepared for nuclear staining, followed by mounting.Samples were visualized under a fluorescence microscope.The EdU-positive rate was calculated as: (EdU-positive cells/total cells) × 100%.

Immunofluorescence staining protocol

Cells were seeded on coverslips in culture plates and treated at 50-70% confluency. Following fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at room temperature for 15 min, cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min at room temperature. After three 5-min PBS washes, nonspecific binding was blocked using 5% BSA or serum for 30-60 min at room temperature. The samples were then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies: anti-HERC4 (1:200, Cat# 13691-1-AP, Proteintech) and anti-STAT3 (1:200, Cat# 60199-1-Ig, Proteintech). Following another three PBS washes, secondary antibodies were applied: anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor Plus 488 (1:200, Cat# SA00006-2, Proteintech) and anti-mouse Alexa Fluor Cy3 (1:200, Cat# SA00009-1, Proteintech), along with DAPI (Cat# C1002, Beyotime) for 1 hour at room temperature in the dark. Nuclear staining was performed using DAPI (1 μg/mL) for 5 min, followed by three PBS washes. Finally, samples were mounted with antifade medium (avoiding bubble formation) and stored protected from light until imaging. Fluorescence signals were captured using a confocal microscope, with the EdU-positive rate calculated as (EdU-positive cells/total cells) × 100%.

Western blot

Protein samples were extracted by lysing cells or tissues using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors, followed by centrifugation to collect the supernatant. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA assay. The protein samples were then mixed with loading buffer and denatured by boiling. Proteins were separated by electrophoresis on SDS-PAGE gels (8%-15% concentration, selected based on target protein size) and subsequently transferred to PVDF or nitrocellulose membranes using the wet transfer method (100-200 mA, 1-2 hours).The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk or BSA for 1 hour at room temperature to minimize nonspecific binding. Primary antibody incubation was performed overnight at 4°C, followed by three washes with TBST. Secondary antibodies conjugated with HRP or fluorescent dyes were then applied and incubated for 1 hour at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized either by ECL chemiluminescence detection (for HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies) or fluorescence scanning (for fluorescently-labeled secondary antibodies).

Immunoprecipitation

Cells were lysed using NP-40 buffer, and the supernatant was collected by centrifugation. To reduce nonspecific binding, pre-clearing was performed by incubating the lysate with Protein A/G magnetic beads and nonspecific IgG. The target protein was then immunoprecipitated by adding a specific primary antibody (1–5 μg per sample) and incubating at 4°C for 2 hours or overnight. Alternatively, antibody-conjugated magnetic beads were used directly. After incubation, the beads were collected by centrifugation and washed 3–5 times with cold lysis buffer to remove nonspecifically bound proteins. The immunoprecipitated proteins were eluted by boiling in loading buffer for 5 minutes to denature the complexes. The samples were then subjected to Western blotting for detection of the target protein.

Mass spectrometry analysis

The proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE respectively. The gel bands of interest were excised from the gel, reduced with 5 mM of DTT and alkylated with 11 mM iodoacetamide which was followed by in-gel digestion with sequencing grade modified trypsin at 37 °C overnight. Then chymotrypsin was add to do next digestion. The peptides were extracted twice with 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid in 50% acetonitrile aqueous solution for 30 min and then dried in a speedvac. Peptides were redissolved in 20 μl 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid and 6 μl of extracted peptides were analyzed by Thermo Scientific Orbitrap Exploris 480.

Mass spectrometer. For LC-MS/MS analysis, the peptides were separated by a 120 min gradient elution at a flow rate 0.30 μl/min with a Thermo-Dionex Ultimate 3000 HPLC system, which was directly interfaced with a Thermo Scientific Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer. The analytical column was a home-made fused silica capillary column (75 μm ID, 350 mm length) packed with C-18 resin (1.9 μm, Dr.Maisch GmbH). Mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid, and mobile phase B consisted of 80% acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid. Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer was operated in the data-dependent acquisition mode using Xcalibur 4.5.445.18 software and there was a single full-scan mass spectrum in the orbitrap (350–1600 m/z, 60,000 resolution) followed by 2 s data-dependent MS/MS scans in an Ion Routing Multipole at 30 normalized collision energy (HCD).

The search criteria were as follows: no enzyme was choosed as unspecific; two missed cleavage was allowed; carbamidomethylation (C) were set as the fixed modifications; the oxidation (M) and ubiquitination were set as the variable modification; precursor ion mass tolerances were set at 20 ppm for all MS acquired in an orbitrap mass analyzer; and the fragment ion mass tolerance was set at 0.02Da for all MS2 spectra. Confidence levels were set to 1% FDR (high confidence).

Molecular docking

We used the AlphaFold prediction models to obtain the three-dimensional structures of both proteins. Subsequently, protein–protein docking was performed using the HDOCK server (http://hdock.phys.hust.edu.cn/help.php.) Among the predicted complexes, models with a confidence score greater than 0.7 were considered to have high credibility.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 7.0 (La Jolla, CA, USA). Data were analyzed using Student's t-test or ANOVA, as appropriate. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001). All statistical details of experiments can be found in the figure legends.
