CT-derived thoracic muscle thickness is associated with prolonged hospitalization after video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery
Authors: Michael Mosebetsi Moleka, Shuqi Jia, Wanli Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSkeletal muscle depletion has been associated with adverse surgical outcomes, but conventional sarcopenia assessment using abdominal imaging is often unavailable in thoracic surgery patients. This study evaluated whether thoracic muscle thickness measured on routine preoperative chest computed tomography predicts postoperative recovery after video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS).MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 301 patients undergoing VATS. Thoracic muscle thickness was measured at the T5, T8, and T10 vertebral levels, and average thoracic muscle thickness was calculated as the mean of these measurements. The primary clinically oriented outcome was prolonged hospitalization, defined as postoperative length of stay (LOS) >7 days. Continuous LOS was additionally analyzed as a supportive outcome. Multivariable logistic regression was used to assess predictors of prolonged hospitalization, while multivariable linear regression was performed as a secondary analysis of continuous LOS. Predictive performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, and the incremental value of muscle thickness was assessed using net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI).ResultsLower thoracic muscle thickness was associated with longer postoperative hospitalization. In multivariable linear regression, average thoracic muscle thickness remained independently associated with LOS (β=−0.137, P=0.002). Logistic regression demonstrated that greater muscle thickness was associated with a lower risk of prolonged hospitalization (LOS >7 days) [odds ratio (OR) =0.92 per mm increase, P=0.004]. ROC analysis showed modest predictive ability [area under the curve (AUC) =0.59] for muscle thickness alone, which improved when incorporated into a clinical model (AUC =0.67). Patients in the lowest muscle tertile had more than twofold increased risk of prolonged hospitalization compared with those in the highest tertile (OR =2.12, P=0.007).ConclusionsThoracic muscle thickness measured on routine preoperative chest CT demonstrated a statistically significant but modest association with postoperative recovery after VATS and may provide complementary body-composition information alongside conventional perioperative assessment.Keywords: Thoracic surgery, sarcopenia, muscle thickness, video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS), postoperative recovery

Highlight box

Key findings

• Lower thoracic muscle thickness measured on routine preoperative chest computed tomography (CT) was independently associated with prolonged hospitalization after video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS).

• Patients in the lowest thoracic muscle thickness tertile had more than a twofold increased risk of prolonged hospitalization compared with those in the highest tertile.

What is known and what is new?

• Sarcopenia and skeletal muscle depletion have been associated with adverse postoperative outcomes, but conventional assessment often requires abdominal imaging or specialized image-analysis software.

• This study demonstrates that a simple thoracic muscle thickness measurement obtained from routine preoperative chest CT is independently associated with postoperative recovery after VATS.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Thoracic muscle thickness may provide complementary body-composition information during preoperative assessment without requiring additional imaging or dedicated software.

• Further prospective multicenter studies are needed to validate its clinical utility and compare this simplified approach with established sarcopenia assessment methods.

Introduction

Postoperative recovery after lung resection varies substantially among patients and remains influenced by multiple physiologic and surgical factors. Although minimally invasive approaches such as video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) have improved perioperative recovery compared with open thoracotomy, prolonged hospitalization and delayed postoperative recovery still occur in a meaningful proportion of thoracic surgical patients (1). Identifying reliable preoperative predictors of recovery is therefore an important component of perioperative risk stratification and surgical planning.

In recent years, increasing attention has been directed toward the role of body composition and sarcopenia in determining surgical outcomes. Sarcopenia is now understood as a muscle disease characterized by low muscle quantity or quality in conjunction with impaired function (2). In patients with cancer, sarcopenia has been associated with functional decline, treatment vulnerability, and worse clinical outcomes (3,4). Computed tomography (CT)-based assessment has emerged as an attractive approach because CT is already embedded in routine oncologic evaluation and can provide opportunistic body-composition information without additional imaging (4). Historically, most surgical sarcopenia studies have relied on cross-sectional skeletal muscle area at the third lumbar vertebral level (L3), including the landmark work by Prado et al. and later surgical series demonstrating adverse associations with complications and prognosis (5,6). However, abdominal imaging is not routinely obtained in thoracic surgical patients, limiting the clinical applicability of this approach. In addition, many body composition methods require specialized segmentation software that is not readily available in routine clinical practice. A simplified thoracic muscle thickness measurement that can be obtained directly from routine chest CT images may therefore represent a practical and widely implementable alternative for perioperative risk assessment.

Recent studies have therefore explored thoracic CT-derived muscle measurements as alternative markers of skeletal muscle status. Thoracic-level approaches appear technically feasible and may correlate sufficiently with conventional sarcopenia assessment to support risk stratification from routine chest CT alone (7,8). In thoracic disease populations, chest CT-derived muscle measurements have been associated with important outcomes, including length of stay (LOS) after lung transplantation and mortality or prognosis in chronic lung disease and lung cancer populations (9-11). More specifically, in resected or operable non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), recent studies have linked CT-derived skeletal muscle depletion or poor muscle quality to postoperative complications, pulmonary complications, recurrence, and worse long-term outcomes (9,12-23). Despite this progress, many published methods still depend on cross-sectional area measurements or more elaborate image-processing workflows that may limit routine clinical uptake.

Accordingly, the aim of the present study was to evaluate whether thoracic muscle thickness, measured on routine preoperative chest CT, is associated with postoperative recovery after VATS. We hypothesized that reduced thoracic muscle thickness would be associated with longer postoperative LOS and an increased likelihood of prolonged hospitalization, and that this simplified measurement would provide incremental prognostic value beyond conventional clinical risk factors. We present this article in accordance with the STARD reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0790/rc).

Methods

Study design and patient population

This retrospective observational study included consecutive patients who underwent VATS at Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University between January 2021 and July 2025. Clinical, surgical, and imaging data were extracted from the institutional thoracic surgery database and electronic medical records.

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they underwent VATS and had preoperative chest CT imaging available within 30 days prior to surgery, along with complete perioperative clinical data. Patients were excluded if preoperative CT imaging was unavailable or of inadequate quality for body composition analysis, if they had a history of prior thoracic surgery that could significantly alter thoracic wall anatomy, if surgery was performed on an emergency basis, or if key clinical variables required for the analysis were missing.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by Institutional Review Board of Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University (No. WDRY2025-K046) and individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived.

CT imaging and thoracic muscle measurement

Preoperative chest CT examinations were performed using multidetector CT scanners according to the institutional imaging protocol. Scans were obtained with patients in the supine position during inspiratory breath-hold. Typical acquisition parameters included a tube voltage of 120 kVp, automatic tube current modulation, and slice thickness ranging from 1 to 5 mm, with images reconstructed using standard soft-tissue algorithms.

Thoracic muscle thickness was measured on axial CT images at the T5, T8, and T10 vertebral levels. Measurements primarily reflected thoracic wall musculature visible on routine axial chest CT imaging, including intercostal and paravertebral muscle components depending on vertebral level. These thoracic wall muscles may contribute to accessory respiratory function after lung resection, although the diaphragm itself was not directly assessed in the present study. Muscle thickness was defined as the perpendicular distance between the inner and outer boundaries of the thoracic wall musculature at each vertebral level. The T5, T8, and T10 vertebral levels were selected to sample upper, middle, and lower thoracic muscle distribution while remaining consistently identifiable across routine preoperative chest CT examinations. Measurements were performed using standard medical imaging software by a trained investigator who was blinded to clinical outcomes.

Tissue segmentation was performed using standardized Hounsfield unit (HU) thresholds, with skeletal muscle defined within the range of −29 to 150 HU and adipose tissue defined within the range of −190 to −30 HU. To generate a simplified composite indicator of thoracic muscle distribution, average thoracic muscle thickness was calculated as the mean value of the measurements obtained at the T5, T8, and T10 levels. Because thoracic muscle measurements demonstrated moderate but not complete correlations across anatomical levels, averaging was intended to reduce potential single-level variability rather than imply full anatomical interchangeability between thoracic regions. Thoracic fat thickness was measured using the same approach, and the mean value across the three levels was recorded as average thoracic fat thickness.

To evaluate measurement reproducibility, a subset of CT scans was independently analyzed by two observers. Interobserver and intraobserver agreement were assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs). The reproducibility analysis demonstrated excellent reliability, with an interobserver ICC of 0.93 and an intraobserver ICC of 0.95. All measurements were performed using mediastinal window settings.

Clinical variables

Baseline demographic variables included age, sex, height, weight, and body mass index (BMI). BMI categories were defined according to the World Health Organization Asian classification: underweight (<18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5–22.9 kg/m2), overweight (23.0–24.9 kg/m2), and obese (≥25.0 kg/m2).

Clinical variables included serum albumin level, smoking status, hypertension, and American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) class. Pulmonary function parameters included forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1%) and diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO%).

Surgical variables included type of surgical procedure, intraoperative blood loss, and pathologic stage group. Perioperative management followed institutional thoracic surgery recovery protocols, including early mobilization, respiratory physiotherapy, multimodal analgesia, and daily assessment for chest tube removal and discharge readiness. Patients were generally considered eligible for discharge after satisfactory pain control with oral medication, adequate ambulation, stable respiratory status, satisfactory lung re-expansion on chest radiography, and absence of clinically significant air leak or major postoperative complications.

Outcome definitions

The primary clinically oriented outcome of the study was prolonged hospitalization, defined as LOS greater than 7 days. Postoperative LOS, defined as the number of days from surgery to hospital discharge, was additionally analyzed as a continuous supportive outcome. In the present cohort, the median LOS was 7 days with an interquartile range (IQR) of 6–8 days. The LOS >7-day threshold was selected based on the observed LOS distribution within the study cohort together with prior thoracic surgery literature evaluating delayed postoperative recovery after VATS. Because postoperative LOS may also be influenced by institutional discharge practices and healthcare-system factors, sensitivity analyses using alternative thresholds (LOS >5 days and LOS >10 days) were additionally performed to evaluate the robustness of the findings.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation or median with IQR as appropriate, whereas categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. Comparisons between patients with LOS ≤7 days and those with LOS >7 days were performed using independent samples t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variables and chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables.

Inter-level correlations between thoracic muscle thickness measurements at the T5, T8, and T10 vertebral levels were assessed using Pearson correlation coefficients to evaluate the degree of association between measurements at different anatomical levels. Associations between thoracic muscle thickness and postoperative LOS were evaluated using Pearson correlation analysis for linear relationships and Spearman rank correlation analysis for monotonic relationships.

Before regression analyses, multicollinearity among candidate predictors was assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF) and tolerance statistics. A VIF greater than 5 or tolerance less than 0.2 was considered indicative of significant multicollinearity. In the presence of collinearity among individual thoracic muscle measurements, average thoracic muscle thickness was used as the primary muscle-related variable in subsequent multivariable models.

Multivariable linear regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of postoperative LOS. Candidate predictors included demographic variables (age, sex, BMI category), clinical variables (albumin level, smoking status, hypertension, ASA class), pulmonary function parameters (FEV1% and DLCO%), surgical variables (surgical procedure type, intraoperative blood loss, and pathological stage group), and body composition variables (average thoracic muscle thickness and average fat thickness). Robust standard errors using the HC3 estimator were applied to account for potential heteroscedasticity. Model assumptions were evaluated by inspecting residual plots for linearity, normality, and homoscedasticity. Because postoperative LOS often exhibits a right-skewed distribution, robust standard errors (HC3) were used in the linear regression model to provide valid inference despite potential heteroscedasticity.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to evaluate predictors of prolonged hospitalization (LOS >7 days). Results were reported as odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to evaluate the predictive performance of thoracic muscle thickness and BMI for prolonged hospitalization. The area under the curve (AUC) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals were calculated. The optimal cutoff value for average thoracic muscle thickness was determined using the Youden index, and the corresponding sensitivity and specificity were reported.

To evaluate the incremental predictive value of thoracic muscle thickness, two logistic regression models were compared: a clinical model including demographic, clinical, pulmonary, and surgical variables, and a combined model incorporating thoracic muscle thickness. Model discrimination was compared using AUC values. Additional predictive improvement metrics, including net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI), were calculated.

Dose-response relationships were assessed by categorizing average thoracic muscle thickness into tertiles and performing multivariable logistic regression analyses using the highest tertile as the reference group.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the findings. These analyses included excluding patients with extreme LOS values greater than 30 days, restricting the cohort to patients undergoing lobectomy, and redefining prolonged hospitalization using alternative thresholds of LOS greater than 5 days and greater than 10 days.

Model calibration was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, and calibration plots were generated by comparing predicted and observed probabilities across deciles of predicted risk.

The number of outcome events was sufficient for the planned multivariable models, yielding approximately 18 events per variable, which exceeds commonly recommended thresholds for logistic regression modeling. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient population

A total of 301 patients who underwent VATS were included in the study cohort. The mean age was 60.7±11.3 years, and 155 patients (51.5%) were female. The mean postoperative LOS was 7.5±4.6 days, while the median LOS was 7 days (IQR, 6–8 days). Most patients (72.7%) remained hospitalized between 5 and 8 days. Overall, 127 patients (42.2%) experienced prolonged hospitalization, defined as LOS >7 days.

Correlation of thoracic muscle measurements

Thoracic muscle thickness measurements obtained at the T5, T8, and T10 vertebral levels demonstrated moderate correlations, with Pearson correlation coefficients of 0.589 for T5–8, 0.451 for T5–10, and 0.419 for T8–10 (all P<0.001), indicating that measurements across thoracic levels were related but not redundant. Correlation analyses demonstrated statistically significant but modest inverse relationships between thoracic muscle thickness and postoperative LOS, indicating limited standalone association strength between individual thoracic muscle measurements and postoperative recovery. Pearson correlation analysis showed significant negative correlations between LOS and T5 muscle thickness (r=−0.133, P=0.02), T10 muscle thickness (r=−0.123, P=0.03), and average thoracic muscle thickness (r=−0.123, P=0.03), whereas T8 muscle thickness was not significantly associated with LOS (P=0.43). Given the moderate correlations among individual thoracic muscle measurements, average thoracic muscle thickness was selected as the primary muscle-related variable for subsequent multivariable analyses.

Multivariable logistic regression was performed to evaluate predictors of prolonged hospitalization (LOS >7 days) (Table 2). Average thoracic muscle thickness remained independently associated with prolonged LOS [odds ratio (OR) 0.92 per mm increase, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.87–0.97, P=0.004]. Male sex was also associated with increased risk (OR 1.85, 95% CI: 1.12–3.07, P=0.02). Exploratory sex-stratified analyses were additionally performed to evaluate the consistency of the observed associations across sexes. Greater thoracic muscle thickness demonstrated generally consistent directional associations with lower odds of prolonged hospitalization in both male and female patients, although statistical significance was observed only in the female subgroup. Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses demonstrated consistent findings across multiple analytical scenarios. The association between thoracic muscle thickness and prolonged hospitalization remained significant after excluding patients with extreme LOS (>30 days), restricting analyses to lobectomy patients, and applying alternative outcome definitions using LOS thresholds greater than 5 days and greater than 10 days. These analyses supported the robustness of the observed association across different definitions of prolonged hospitalization.

Discussion

In this study of patients undergoing VATS, thoracic muscle thickness measured on routine preoperative chest CT was independently associated with prolonged postoperative hospitalization, the primary clinically oriented outcome of this analysis. Lower average thoracic muscle thickness was associated with longer postoperative LOS and an increased likelihood of prolonged hospitalization. In addition, patients in the lowest muscle tertile had more than twice the risk of prolonged LOS compared with those in the highest tertile, and the addition of thoracic muscle thickness to a clinical model modestly improved model discrimination. Taken together, these findings suggest that CT-derived thoracic muscle thickness demonstrates a modest association with postoperative recovery and may provide complementary exploratory body-composition information in thoracic surgical patients. However, the overall predictive performance was limited, and the clinical utility of thoracic muscle thickness as a perioperative prognostic marker remains uncertain.

The biologic rationale for this association is plausible. Sarcopenia reflects loss of muscle quantity and quality together with impaired function and has been linked to diminished metabolic reserve, impaired stress tolerance, and poorer resilience after illness or surgery (2-4). In thoracic surgery, reduced skeletal muscle mass may have additional relevance because respiratory muscle performance and whole-body functional reserve influence cough effectiveness, mobilization, and pulmonary recovery after resection. Recent thoracic-specific studies support this idea: lower erector spinae muscle mass has been associated with postoperative pulmonary complications after lobectomy, while broader respiratory-sarcopenia frameworks have also shown associations with worse short-term postoperative recovery in NSCLC populations (13,23). These data are consistent with the interpretation that thoracic muscle measures may partially reflect aspects of physiologic reserve and body composition. However, thoracic muscle thickness should not be interpreted as a formal diagnostic measure of sarcopenia, which requires integrated assessment of muscle quantity, muscle quality, and functional performance. The observed correlations between thoracic muscle thickness and postoperative LOS were modest, and the moderate inter-level correlations between T5, T8, and T10 measurements suggest that thoracic muscle distribution is heterogeneous across anatomical regions. Accordingly, the averaged thoracic muscle thickness measure should be interpreted as a simplified exploratory body-composition indicator rather than a precise anatomical surrogate for global skeletal muscle status. Because thoracic muscle thickness differs substantially between men and women, exploratory sex-stratified analyses were additionally performed and demonstrated generally consistent directional associations across both sexes.

Our study also adds to the growing thoracic-oncology literature evaluating chest CT-based body composition. Earlier work established that chest CT muscle metrics, such as pectoralis muscle area or thoracic cross-sectional muscle area, are clinically informative in thoracic disease populations (9-11). More recent NSCLC surgical studies have shown that CT-derived sarcopenia or poor muscle quality is associated with worse postoperative or long-term outcomes (9,12-22). For example, Hasenauer et al. reported that CT-derived sarcopenia had a modest association with early postoperative outcomes after thoracoscopic anatomical pulmonary resection (12), while Kaltenhauser et al. demonstrated that sarcopenia diagnosed on thoracic CT was associated with postoperative survival after anatomic lung cancer resection (7). Sun et al. further showed that combining skeletal muscle quantity and density improved prognostic discrimination for postoperative complications and survival in NSCLC surgery (15). In this context, our findings contribute to this literature by suggesting that a simplified linear thickness measurement, rather than cross-sectional area or more complex segmentation, may still retain modest prognostic information despite its simplified methodology.

Incorporation of thoracic muscle thickness into the clinical model resulted in only a modest improvement in model discrimination, with the AUC increasing from 0.644 to 0.666, and this difference was not statistically significant. The standalone discriminative performance of thoracic muscle thickness was also limited (AUC 0.593), indicating that thoracic muscle thickness alone should not be interpreted as a strong predictive tool for prolonged hospitalization. Although several exploratory secondary analyses demonstrated generally consistent findings, these results should be interpreted cautiously given the modest overall predictive signal and limited standalone discriminative performance of thoracic muscle thickness.

The growing use of ultrasound-based muscle assessment in perioperative medicine also provides important context for interpretation of the present findings. Point-of-care ultrasound has emerged as a radiation-free and repeatable method for evaluating frailty, muscle status, and respiratory muscle function in surgical patients. Recent studies have suggested that ultrasound-derived muscle assessment may provide clinically relevant information regarding perioperative physiologic reserve and postoperative outcomes (24,25). In particular, bedside ultrasound evaluation of diaphragm and expiratory respiratory muscles has attracted increasing attention in thoracic surgery populations because postoperative respiratory workload and compensatory respiratory muscle recruitment may substantially influence recovery after lung resection. Accordingly, thoracic wall muscle thickness should not be interpreted in isolation, as its postoperative physiologic relevance likely depends on the interaction between respiratory workload, accessory respiratory muscle recruitment, and diaphragmatic function. Recent prospective ultrasound data have also demonstrated that respiratory muscle thickness alone may have limited predictive value for postoperative pulmonary complications after thoracic surgery (25). Taken together, these findings support the concept that CT-based and ultrasound-based muscle assessment approaches may provide complementary rather than interchangeable information regarding perioperative physiologic reserve.

The practical implications of this simplified approach may warrant further clinical investigation. Because chest CT is already universally performed in patients considered for thoracic surgery, thoracic muscle thickness can be obtained without additional imaging, radiation exposure, or dedicated body-composition software. This may make it easier to incorporate into everyday preoperative workflows. In addition, because our ROC analysis suggested that thoracic muscle thickness was directionally more informative than BMI, even though the difference was not statistically significant in this sample, the measurement may capture aspects of physiologic reserve not fully reflected by BMI alone. That distinction is consistent with prior body-composition literature showing that simple anthropometric measures are often less informative than direct CT-based muscle assessment (4,5,11). Although pulmonary function parameters and albumin are clinically relevant perioperative variables, they were not independently associated with prolonged hospitalization after multivariable adjustment in this cohort. This may reflect the relatively homogeneous preoperative functional status of the included patients, the multifactorial nature of postoperative recovery, and residual confounding related to postoperative events and institutional discharge practices.

Our study has several strengths. We analyzed a well-characterized VATS cohort with detailed clinical, pulmonary, surgical, and imaging data; assessed muscle at multiple thoracic levels; and evaluated associations using multivariable regression, dose-response analysis, ROC analysis, and sensitivity analyses. At the same time, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a retrospective single-center study, which may limit generalizability. Second, muscle thickness is a simplified linear measure and does not capture total muscle area, density, diaphragmatic function, or direct functional performance. In addition, thoracic muscle thickness was not directly validated against established frailty indices, respiratory functional assessments, or consensus diagnostic definitions of sarcopenia. Third, postoperative recovery and hospital LOS are multifactorial outcomes influenced not only by physiologic recovery but also by institutional discharge practices, chest tube management strategies, rehabilitation availability, healthcare-system structure, insurance or reimbursement systems, patient preference, family and social support, and overall discharge readiness. These factors may vary substantially across institutions and countries and could not be fully accounted for in the present study. Residual confounding may therefore remain despite multivariable adjustment. In addition, several postoperative factors that may substantially influence LOS, including postoperative complications, prolonged air leak, chest tube duration, ICU utilization, postoperative analgesia, and discharge disposition, were not included in the present analysis because the primary objective of the study was to evaluate preoperative prognostic associations rather than postoperative causal pathways. Finally, the predictive performance of thoracic muscle thickness alone was modest, which reinforces that it should be viewed as one component of perioperative assessment rather than a stand-alone determinant of risk. Future prospective multicenter studies should validate these findings, compare thickness directly with area- and density-based methods, and determine whether nutritional optimization or prehabilitation can modify postoperative outcomes in patients with low thoracic muscle mass (18,26).

Conclusions

Thoracic muscle thickness measured on routine preoperative chest CT demonstrated a statistically significant but modest association with prolonged postoperative hospitalization after VATS. Although the overall predictive performance was limited, thoracic muscle thickness may provide complementary exploratory body-composition information alongside conventional perioperative assessment without requiring additional imaging or specialized software. Given the multifactorial nature of postoperative recovery and the exploratory design of the present study, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Further prospective multicenter studies are needed to validate the clinical utility of simplified thoracic muscle measurements and to compare this approach with established body-composition and functional assessment methods.
