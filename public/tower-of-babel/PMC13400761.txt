Lost in Stratification: Age Subgroup Analyses in CKD Progression Trials
Authors: Priya Vart
Published: 2026

Five large randomized trials published between 2019 and 2024 have established 3 distinct drug classes as effective nephroprotective therapies across a broad spectrum of predialysis chronic kidney disease (CKD). The CREDENCE, DAPA-CKD, and EMPA-KIDNEY trials established sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors as nephroprotective therapies,1, 2, 3 whereas the FIDELIO-DKD4 and FLOW5 trials extended this paradigm to non-steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist finerenone and the glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist semaglutide, respectively. Each trial reported age-stratified subgroup analyses in the primary publication, typically dichotomizing at 65 years and reporting hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals for the primary composite end point in each age stratum. Three trials have since published dedicated age analyses with more detailed stratification—CREDENCE6, DAPA-CKD,7 and FIDELITY8 (the pooled FIDELIO-DKD and FIGARO-DKD analysis).9 Nonsignificant age-by-treatment interaction has been reported for most clinical outcomes across all these trials; this suggests that treatment benefits may be preserved across age groups. Beyond the well-recognized limitation of low statistical power for subgroup analyses, however, interpretation of these analyses as performed deserves closer examination.

The primary composite end points in all 5 trials explicitly combine 2 clinically distinct outcome types— renal events including a sustained 40% to 57% estimated glomerular filtration rate decline, kidney failure, or kidney-related death, and cardiovascular death. This composite structure is clinically well justified, as both outcome types are catastrophic for individual patients and the investigated drugs plausibly affect both. However, the internal composition of this composite outcome may not be fixed across age groups as the absolute rate of kidney and cardiovascular outcomes can have different trajectories across the age spectrum. The evidence for this compositional shift is quantitatively evident across 3 independent trials that provided detailed outcome data by age groups. In the CREDENCE placebo arm, which enrolled patients with type 2 diabetes and albuminuric CKD (≥ 300 mg/g), kidney failure occurred at a rate of 46.7 events per 1000 patient-years in patients under 60 years, compared with cardiovascular death at 19.7 per 1000 patient-years with a kidney-to-cardiovascular event ratio of 2.4. In patients aged ≥ 70 years, these rates were 12.5 and 25.4 per 1000 patient-years, respectively, representing a reversal in the ratio to 0.5, with kidney failure occurring at half the rate of cardiovascular mortality.6 This trend was similar for kidney composite outcome (57% estimated glomerular filtration rate decline, kidney failure, or kidney-related death) and major adverse cardiovascular events (nonfatal myocardial infarction, nonfatal stroke, or cardiovascular death). In the DAPA-CKD trial, the absolute kidney composite event rate in the placebo arm was 10.7 per 100 patient-years in patients aged < 50 years, declining to 3.0 per 100 patient-years in those aged ≥ 80 years (a 72% decline) whereas absolute cardiovascular event rates were systematically higher in older patients7 (Table 1). In FIDELITY, which used separate cardiovascular and kidney composite end points, the major adverse cardiovascular event rate in the placebo arm increased from 3.9 per 100 patient-years in patients < 65 years to 7.6 in those aged ≥ 75, whereas the kidney composite rate (57% estimated glomerular filtration rate decline, kidney failure, or kidney-related death) declined from 3.2 to 1.4 per 100 patient-years, that is, a reversal from approximately equivalent rates to a 5.4-fold excess cardiovascular events in the oldest group.8 Thus, the pattern of declining rates of kidney events and increasing cardiovascular events with increasing age is evident across these trials in both diabetic and nondiabetic CKD populations and is consistent with findings from observational studies.10 Importantly, this compositional shift in the composite outcome means that the primary composite end point is not the same clinical entity in younger and older patients. One may argue that it is less relevant which end point is dominant in which age group, and more relevant is that treatment is protective against end points that are dominant in a given age group. However, for an individual patient and their care provider, it remains important to understand the impact of treatment on these end points separately. This is particularly relevant for older patients, who are generally prescribed multiple medications, and in whom declines in kidney function, even short of kidney failure, can substantially affect quality of life and daily functioning. As discussed, primary composite outcome in these trials is not suitable to answer whether kidney protective effect is consistent across age groups. To this end, post hoc analyses of several trials have investigated kidney-specific end points (excluding cardiovascular mortality), stratified by age, and reported no statistical heterogeneity in HRs for the treatment effect on kidney-specific outcomes across age groups.6, 7, 8 Interpretation of these findings, however, may also not be straightforward. It is worth noting that not only does cardiovascular death occur at substantially higher absolute rates in older individuals than in their younger counterparts, but it is also reduced by all 3 drug classes. This has important implications for the estimation of kidney-specific treatment effects in age-stratified analyses. In the placebo arm, a substantial and larger proportion of older patients die from cardiovascular causes and are thereby censored and removed from the kidney event risk-set before they can reach a kidney failure end point. In the treated arm, relatively fewer patients die from cardiovascular causes, meaning more high-risk patients remain alive and in the kidney event risk-set, accumulating person-time during which they remain at risk of the very end point the drug is meant to prevent. This can contribute to systematic greater attenuation of kidney-specific treatment effect in older patients which makes comparison of HRs for kidney composite outcome across age groups challenging. Thus, a lack of statistical heterogeneity in HRs for the treatment effect on kidney-specific outcomes across age groups may also not imply that the relative benefit of treatment on kidney-specific outcomes is consistent across age groups; it should rather be interpreted with the possibility of underestimation of the treatment effect in older adults, especially when the absolute number of cardiovascular deaths prevented (or delayed) in the treatment group is larger.

Beyond these methodological considerations, there is a more fundamental challenge. Even if age subgroup analyses were methodologically sound, the age-stratified results alone remain insufficient to guide clinical decision-making in older patients with CKD. Older individuals are highly heterogeneous, and chronological age conceals the individual-level characteristics like frailty, functional status, comorbidity burden, and life expectancyS1 that actually determine benefit-risk balance and ultimately the decision to prescribe.S2 Two patients of identical age frequently have substantially different prognoses and capacities to benefit, yet the age-stratified HR treats them as equivalent. For a frail older patient with limited life expectancy and multiple competing health conditions, the time needed to realize a net treatment benefit may be insufficient. These considerations are routine in care of older people with CKD but entirely invisible in the age-stratified HR. Without information on geriatric factors such as frailty, therefore, age subgroup analyses are of limited use in establishing the value of these agents in older patients. This value is further diminished given the underrepresentation of very old and frail patients in these trials,S3–S5 meaning that the HRs that do exist are derived largely from older patients who are healthier and more robust than those most commonly encountered in clinical practice.

Trials employing a composite kidney–cardiovascular outcome as the primary end point should also report age-stratified results for the kidney and cardiovascular components separately and, to facilitate interpretation of the results, explicitly discuss how age-related shifts in underlying risk and competing-risk dynamics may alter estimates of treatment benefit across the age spectrum. Moreover, consistent with guidance from regulatory agencies including the European Medicines Agency,S6 CKD trials should record and report outcomes according to frailty status. Such assessment can be performed using the clinical frailty scale with minimal time and training requirements,S4 while more objective instruments endorsed by major regulators, such as the short physical performance battery or gait speed, may serve as other pragmatic alternatives.

Nonetheless, it is evident that age-stratified analyses as currently reported in major CKD progression trials may obscure rather than clarify how nephroprotective therapies work in older patients. The compositional shift in the primary composite end point across age groups and the competing risks introduced by differential cardiovascular mortality mean that a nonsignificant age-by-treatment interaction in itself may not be the evidence of consistent treatment benefit across age. Moreover, chronological age is a poor proxy for the individual-level characteristics like frailty, functional status, comorbidity burden, and life expectancy that actually determine whether a given older patient is likely to benefit, yet data on these factors are absent from all major trials. Because older patients represent a large proportion of the CKD population and are the main future recipients of these medications, this gap in the evidence base is consequential. Reported treatment effect by age warrants careful interpretation and caution in applying trial findings to older CKD patients with frailty, functional impairment, and competing health priorities seen in routine clinical practice.
