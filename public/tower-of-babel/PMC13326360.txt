CTSS regulates macrophage lipid metabolic reprogramming and white matter repair after intracerebral hemorrhage
Authors: Xian Yu, Huaping Huang, Dandan Mao, Jianan Wu, Jiahe Pan, Yirui Kuang, Yiwen Wu, Huaijun Chen, Linfeng Fan, Yonghe Zheng, Jiayin Zhou, Shandong Jiang, Xinyan Wu, Hang Zhou, Wei Yan, Haiyan Zheng, Jingyin Chen, Gao Chen, Chi Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) causes secondary white matter injury, which contributes substantially to long-term neurological disability. Although macrophages accumulate in the perihematomal region and participate in tissue remodeling after ICH, the molecular programs that link macrophage responses to white matter repair remain poorly understood, and no current strategies specifically target macrophage‑mediated white matter restoration. Cathepsin S (CTSS), a lysosomal cysteine protease involved in immune regulation and tissue remodeling, is strongly induced after brain injury; however, its role in post‑ICH white matter pathology has not been defined.MethodsA collagenase-induced mouse model of intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) was established, followed by pharmacological inhibition of Cathepsin S (CTSS) using LY3000328. Single-cell RNA sequencing was performed on perihematomal tissues from Vehicle- and CTSS inhibitor-treated mice to explore CTSS-responsive cell populations and transcriptional programs. Macrophage lipid handling was assessed using flow cytometry, immunofluorescence, and fluorescent myelin debris-based uptake and lipid-transfer assays. A bone marrow-derived macrophage (BMDM)–oligodendrocyte precursor cell (OPC) co-culture system was used to determine whether CTSS inhibition alters macrophage-derived lipid support for OPC differentiation. The LXR agonist GW3965 was applied as a rescue intervention. White matter repair and neurological recovery were evaluated by myelin-associated protein analysis, immunofluorescence, behavioral testing, and transmission electron microscopy.ResultsSingle-cell analysis identified infiltrating macrophages, particularly adaptive lipid-associated macrophages (aLAMs), as a major CTSS-expressing population after ICH, displaying a transcriptional state related to lipid metabolism and tissue repair. CTSS inhibition attenuated this lipid-associated macrophage program and reduced ApoE, ABCA1, and ABCG1 expression. Mechanistically, CTSS inhibition reduced Npc1/Npc2 expression and disrupted lysosomal cholesterol trafficking, leading to lysosomal cholesterol retention. These changes suggest impaired Npc1/Npc2-associated cholesterol mobilization and attenuation of the LXR-associated macrophage lipid efflux program. In BMDM–OPC co-cultures, CTSS inhibition reduced the transfer of fluorescent macrophage-processed myelin-derived lipids to OPCs and limited OPC differentiation, whereas GW3965 partially restored Npc1/Npc2 expression, macrophage-derived lipid support, and OPC differentiation. Consistently, in vivo CTSS inhibition reduced MBP expression and MBP⁺ axonal wrapping, increased g-ratio values, aggravated myelin ultrastructural abnormalities, and delayed neurological recovery after ICH.ConclusionsCTSS in macrophages supports white matter repair after ICH by maintaining lysosomal cholesterol trafficking and macrophage lipid efflux. CTSS inhibition limits macrophage-derived lipid support for OPCs and impairs remyelination, whereas LXR activation partially restores these reparative responses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08523-1.

Introduction

Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) accounts for approximately 20–30% of acute stroke cases and carries a one-year mortality exceeding 50% [1, 2]. In terms of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), the burden of ICH surpasses that of ischemic stroke [3]. Even among survivors, long-term outcomes are frequently poor, characterized by persistent motor and cognitive deficits [4–6]. Beyond the acute mass effect of the hematoma, secondary white matter (WM) injury—including demyelination and axonal disruption—has emerged as a major determinant of neurological recovery after ICH [7–10]. Although neuronal protection has been extensively investigated, the molecular mechanisms governing WM remodeling remain incompletely defined, limiting the development of restorative therapies.

During the subacute phase of ICH, infiltrating macrophages accumulate within the perihematomal region, where they coordinate hematoma clearance, debris removal, and tissue remodeling [11–13]. Evidence from other neurological contexts indicates that macrophages interact closely with oligodendrocyte lineage cells to influence white matter integrity and repair processes [9, 14, 15]. Given the substantial lipid burden generated by myelin breakdown and hemorrhagic injury, effective macrophage lipid handling may represent a critical determinant of white matter repair. However, the molecular regulators that shape macrophage metabolic adaptations after ICH remain poorly understood.

Cathepsin S (CTSS) is a lysosomal cysteine protease with well-established roles in antigen presentation, extracellular matrix remodeling, and immune regulation [16–18]. Beyond its classical immunological functions, CTSS has been implicated in tissue repair across diverse biological contexts. For example, macrophage-derived CTSS facilitates axonal regeneration following peripheral nerve injury, and Schwann cell-expressed CTSS enhances the phagocytic clearance of myelin debris to support repair [19, 20]. In vitro wound-healing studies further demonstrate that CTSS promotes cell migration and proliferation under inflammatory conditions [21]. Emerging evidence also indicates that CTSS participates in endolysosomal lipid metabolic processes, influencing lipid droplet dynamics and cellular metabolic adaptation [22, 23]. However, whether CTSS regulates macrophage lipid metabolic programs after intracerebral hemorrhage and contributes to white matter remodeling remains unclear.

Here, we aimed to investigate the role of CTSS-expressing infiltrating macrophages in post-ICH white matter remodeling. To this end, we applied single-cell RNA sequencing, pharmacological CTSS inhibition, lipid-tracing assays, macrophage–OPC co-culture, and in vivo assessments to characterize how CTSS influences macrophage lipid handling and its potential impact on white matter repair.

Materials and methods

Mice

Male C57BL/6J mice (8–10 weeks old, 20–25 g) were obtained from Charles River Laboratories (Zhejiang, China; animal license No. XK[Zhe]2024-0001). Mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free facility under a 12 h light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. All procedures were performed in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (Approval No. AIRB-2024-302).

Establishment of the ICH model

The collagenase-induced ICH model was established as previously described [24]. Adult male C57BL/6J mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, and type VII collagenase (0.05 U in 0.5 µL saline; MCE) was stereotactically injected into the right basal ganglia (2.5 mm lateral to bregma and 3.0 mm below the skull surface, at a 5° angle) over 5 min. The needle was kept in place for an additional 5 min before withdrawal. Sham-operated mice received an equal volume of saline using the same procedure. This protocol induced reproducible basal ganglia hemorrhage with partial spontaneous recovery, allowing assessment of both delayed neurological deficits and white matter repair-related outcomes.

ICH-model mice were excluded if they died during or shortly after surgery, showed no detectable neurological deficit, had misplaced injection or failed hematoma formation, or exhibited severe non-ICH-related complications. The model success rate was 85%, and the mortality rate was 10%.

In vivo drug administration

ICH mice were randomly assigned to the Vehicle, Inhibitor, or LXR rescue group. The Vehicle group received a daily intraperitoneal injection of 100 µL vehicle solution consisting of 10% DMSO, 40% PEG300, 5% Tween-80, and 45% saline [25, 26]. The Inhibitor group received LY3000328 (10 mg/kg; MCE, HY-15533), dissolved in the same vehicle solution, once daily after ICH, with the dosing regimen based on prior studies [25, 27, 28]. The LXR rescue group received LY3000328 using the same regimen as the Inhibitor group and was additionally treated with the LXR agonist GW3965 (10 mg/kg/day; MCE, HY-10627) for 7 consecutive days after ICH, as previously reported [29]. Unless otherwise specified, “Veh”, “Inh”, and “LXR” in subsequent figures refer to the Vehicle, Inhibitor, and LXR rescue groups, respectively.

Western blot

Brains were collected after transcardial perfusion with PBS, and perihematomal basal ganglia tissues were homogenized in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) supplemented with protease inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of protein (40 µg) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CTSS (1:1000, Abclonal, A28340), ABCA1 (1:500, Thermo Fisher Scientific, PA5-86023), ABCG1 (1:1000, Proteintech, 13578-1-AP), MBP (1:500, Abcam, ab7349), LDLR (1:1000, Abclonal, A20808) and VLDLR (1:1000, Abclonal, A7345). After washing, membranes were incubated with appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. β-Actin (1:10000, Proteintech, HRP-60008) was used as a loading control. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and quantified with ImageJ software.

Immunofluorescence staining

Mice were anesthetized and transcardially perfused with PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Brains were post-fixed in 4% PFA, cryoprotected in 30% sucrose, embedded in OCT, and sectioned into 10-µm coronal slices. Sections were washed with PBS and blocked/permeabilized for 1 h at room temperature in 10% donkey serum containing 0.1% Triton X-100.

Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CTSS (1:1000, Abclonal, A28340), F4/80 (1:200, Thermo Fisher, 14-4801-85), MBP (1:500, Abcam, ab7349), Iba-1 (1:500, Abcam, ab5076), NeuN (1:500, Abcam, ab177487), GFAP (1:500, Abcam, ab68428), LAMP1 (1:50, CST, 9091), NG2 (1:50, Abcam, ab275024), and NF (1:200, Abcam, ab207176). After washing, sections were incubated with species-appropriate Alexa Fluor–conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Abcam, ab104139). For lipid visualization, BODIPY staining (1:1000, Beyotime, C2053S) or Filipin (50 µg/mL, Beyotime, C2061) was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Images were acquired using a Leica fluorescence or confocal microscope with z-stack or tile scanning as required. Three-dimensional reconstructions were performed using Vaa3D software [30].

Behavioral tests

Accelerated rotarod test [31]: The rotarod started at 4 rpm and accelerated at 0.12 rpm/s up to a maximum of 40 rpm. The latency to fall and the speed at which the mouse fell were recorded. All mice underwent 3 days of pre-training before modeling, and the results from the final training day were used as baseline values.

Adhesive removal test [32, 33]: This test was used to evaluate forelimb sensorimotor function. A 3 mm × 3 mm adhesive tape was placed on the affected left forepaw. The latency to contact the tape (time-to-contact) and the latency to remove the tape (time-to-remove) were recorded, with a maximum observation time of 120 s. All mice underwent 3 days of pre-training before modeling, and the results from the final training day were used as baseline values.

Cylinder test [34]: Performed to examine asymmetry in forelimb use. On day 3 or 7 after ICH, each mouse was placed in a transparent plastic cylinder and allowed to rear freely. During each rear, the initial forelimb(s) contacting the cylinder wall (right, left, or both) were recorded until 20 rears were completed. The laterality index was calculated as (right − left) / 20, with higher positive values indicating preferential use of the right forelimb and more severe left hemiparesis.

Single-cell RNA sequencing and quality control

On day 7 after ICH, mice were euthanized and transcardially perfused with ice-cold saline. Perihematomal basal ganglia tissues from the ipsilateral hemisphere were dissected after removal of the meninges, olfactory bulb, cortex, and cerebellum. Two pooled samples were generated, each comprising tissues from three mice in either the Vehicle or Inhibitor group.

Single-cell suspensions were prepared using the Adult Brain Dissociation Kit (T) in combination with a GentleMACS Dissociator with heaters (Miltenyi Biotec) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Single-cell libraries were constructed using the Chromium Single Cell 3′ v3 platform (10X Genomics). Barcoded cDNA libraries were amplified, purified, and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform.

Raw sequencing data were processed using Seurat (v5.3.0). Cells were filtered using the following criteria: 200–6000 detected genes (nFeature_RNA), 500–7000 UMIs (nCount_RNA), mitochondrial gene proportion ≤ 10%, hemoglobin gene proportion ≤ 10%, and ribosomal gene proportion ≥ 3%. Cells outside these thresholds were excluded. High-quality cells were retained for normalization, dimensionality reduction, clustering, and differential expression analyses.

Given the pooled design, single-cell transcriptomic analyses were primarily exploratory and used to generate mechanistic hypotheses. Statistical validation was subsequently performed using independent biological replicates in downstream experiments.

Differential gene expression analysis

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using Seurat (v5.3.0). Normalization was performed using the default LogNormalize method. Differential expression between clusters or experimental groups was conducted using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test implemented in the FindMarkers function. Genes were considered differentially expressed if they were expressed in at least 25% of cells in either group (min.pct = 0.25), exhibited an absolute log2 fold change > 0.25, and had an adjusted P value < 0.05 following multiple testing correction using the Benjamini–Hochberg method.

Because the scRNA-seq samples were generated using a pooled design, cell-level P values from differential expression analyses were interpreted as exploratory and were not considered substitutes for biological replication.

Functional enrichment analysis (GO, KEGG, GSEA)

Gene Ontology (GO) biological process (BP) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed using the clusterProfiler package in R with the mouse annotation database (org.Mm.eg.db). The background gene set consisted of all expressed genes detected in the dataset. Enriched terms were considered significant if the adjusted P value (Benjamini–Hochberg correction) was < 0.05 and at least three genes were included in each term.

To evaluate pathway activation states, z-scores were calculated based on the number of upregulated and downregulated genes within each pathway.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted using clusterProfiler, with genes ranked by average log2 fold change between groups. Gene sets with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.25 were considered significantly enriched, in accordance with established GSEA guidelines.

Pseudotime analysis

Cellular trajectories were reconstructed using Monocle2 (v2.26.0) and independently validated with Monocle3 (v1.3.1). Highly variable genes identified in Seurat were used as ordering features. Dimensional reduction and trajectory inference were performed according to the respective standard workflows of each package. Cells were ordered in pseudotime, with the root state assigned to the monocyte-like/inflammatory macrophage cluster based on enriched expression of Ccr2, Ly6c2, and Cx3cr1. Gene expression dynamics along pseudotime were analyzed to identify stage-specific transcriptional programs.

All R-based bioinformatic analyses were performed using R version 4.4.3 (2025-02-28). Single-cell RNA-seq processing and differential expression analyses were conducted using Seurat v5.3.0. Functional enrichment analyses were performed using clusterProfiler v4.14.6, and data visualization was generated using ggplot2 v3.5.2.

Flow cytometry

Mice were euthanized and perfused transcardially with ice-cold saline, after which the ipsilateral hemispheres were isolated. Brain tissues were dissociated using the Neural Tissue Dissociation Kit (T) together with a GentleMACS Dissociator equipped with heaters (Miltenyi Biotec) [35]. The single-cell suspension was passed through a 70-µm strainer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and further purified by 30% Percoll gradient centrifugation (17089109, Cytiva, USA) to remove myelin. Red blood cells were lysed with RBC lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher, 00-4333-57), and Fc receptors were blocked using anti-CD16/CD32 antibody. Cells were subsequently incubated with a viability dye before staining for surface markers, including CD45, CD11b, F4/80, CD36, and TREM2. Neutral lipid accumulation was assessed using BODIPY staining (1:500, Beyotime, C2053S). Intracellular fixation and permeabilization were carried out using the appropriate buffer set (Thermo Fisher, 00-5523-00), followed by incubation with anti-ApoE antibody (1:70, Abcam, EPR19392) or isotype control for 60 min at room temperature. After blocking with 2% BSA in PBS for 30 min, cells were incubated in the dark with DyLight 647-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) secondary antibody (1:2000, Abcam, ab150075). Finally, samples were resuspended in PBS and analyzed on a BD flow cytometer (FACS Aria SORP, BD, USA), with data processed using FlowJo software (v10.1.8).

BMDM culture

Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were prepared from 6–8-week-old male C57BL/6J mice. Briefly, mice were euthanized, disinfected with 75% ethanol, and the femurs and tibias were isolated under sterile conditions. Surrounding muscle tissues were carefully removed. Bone marrow cells were collected from the bones by brief centrifugation, resuspended in sterile PBS, and filtered through a 70-µm cell strainer. After centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C, red blood cells were lysed using red blood cell lysis buffer for 3–5 min, followed by termination with excess PBS. The cells were then centrifuged and resuspended in BMDM culture medium.

BMDMs were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF, 20 ng/mL; Abclonal, RP01216S) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. Fresh BMDM culture medium was added on day 3, and the medium was completely replaced on day 7. Mature BMDMs were used for subsequent in vitro experiments [35].

Myelin debris preparation and BMDM loading

Myelin debris was prepared from brains of 8-week-old mice using sucrose density gradient centrifugation. Freshly isolated brains were homogenized in 0.32 M sucrose buffer, and the homogenate was carefully overlaid onto 0.85 M sucrose buffer. After ultracentrifugation at 75,000 × g for 30 min, the myelin-enriched layer at the interface was collected. The fraction was diluted with distilled water, centrifuged at 75,000 × g for 15 min, and then exposed to distilled water for 15 min to remove contaminating non-myelin components by osmotic shock. After centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min, the pellet was resuspended in 0.32 M sucrose buffer and subjected to a second round of sucrose gradient purification. The purified myelin fraction was washed with distilled water, pelleted by centrifugation at 75,000 × g for 15 min, resuspended in PBS, and stored at − 80 °C until further use [35].

For myelin debris loading, mature BMDMs were first treated with Vehicle or LY3000328 (20 µM) for 12 h [36]. Myelin debris was then added at a final concentration of 500 µg/mL, and cells were maintained for another 24 h in the presence of the corresponding treatment. In the LXR rescue group, GW3965 (20 µM) was added together with LY3000328 during the myelin debris loading period [37].

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from BMDMs after myelin debris loading and treatment with Vehicle, LY3000328, or LY3000328 plus GW3965 using an RNAeasy Animal RNA Isolation Kit with Spin Column (Beyotime, R0027) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA was synthesized using the reverse transcription kit (AG, AG11706). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using SYBR Green Pro Taq HS Premix qPCR Kit with ROX (AG, AG11718) on a Roche LightCycler 480 real-time PCR system. Relative mRNA expression was calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method, with β-Actin used as the internal reference gene. Each sample was analyzed in technical triplicate. OPC culture

Primary oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPCs) were isolated from postnatal day 3 C57BL/6J mice by magnetic-activated cell sorting [38]. Brain tissues were dissociated into a single-cell suspension using the Neural Tissue Dissociation Kit (P) (Miltenyi, 130-092-628) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. OPCs were enriched using a PDGFRα MicroBead Kit (Miltenyi, 130-101-502) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Labeled cells were passed through magnetic separation columns (Miltenyi, 130-042-401), and purified OPCs were collected and seeded onto poly-L-ornithine-coated plates (0.1 mg/mL; MCE, HY-126436 A).

OPCs were maintained in Neurobasal medium (Gibco, 21103049) supplemented with 0.1% bovine serum albumin, 1% N2 supplement (Thermo Fisher,17502048), 2% B27 supplement (Thermo Fisher, 12587010), platelet-derived growth factor-AA (PDGF-AA, 10 ng/mL; MCE, HY-P7277), and basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF, 10 ng/mL; MCE, HY-P706) at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. For Transwell co-culture experiments, OPCs were seeded in the lower chamber and BMDMs were seeded in the upper insert [39].

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

Mice were perfused transcardially with PBS, 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), and 2.5% glutaraldehyde. Tissue blocks (1 mm³) were carefully dissected from the perihematomal striatal region and immersed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 24 h. Samples were post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide for 2 h, sequentially dehydrated in graded ethanol and acetone, and embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections were prepared and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Random fields within the striatum were imaged using a TEM (Philips Tecnai 10). The G-ratio, an index of myelination, was quantified using ImageJ.

For g-ratio analysis, a total of 914 myelinated axons from 4 Vehicle-treated mice and 896 myelinated axons from 4 Inhibitor-treated mice were analyzed. Axon-level data were used for statistical comparisons, and axons were stratified by diameter when indicated.

Residual hematoma volume measurement and Perls’ Prussian blue staining

Residual hematoma volume was measured on day 7 after ICH using serial 1-mm-thick coronal brain sections. Digital images were acquired, and the hematoma area in each section was quantified using ImageJ software. Total residual hematoma volume was calculated by summing the hematoma area of each section multiplied by section thickness [13, 34].

Perls’ Prussian blue staining was performed on brain sections at day 7 and day 14 after ICH to assess iron deposition. Perls-positive areas in the perihematomal region were imaged under a light microscope and quantified using ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD unless otherwise indicated. Normality was assessed before parametric testing. For normally distributed data with equal variances, comparisons between two groups were performed using unpaired t-tests, and comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by appropriate post hoc tests. When variances were unequal, Welch’s t-test or Welch ANOVA with Dunnett’s T3 multiple comparisons test was used as appropriate. For non-normally distributed data, the Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis test was used. Behavioral data collected over time were analyzed using two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with Geisser-Greenhouse correction, followed by Šídak’s multiple comparisons test. For ultrastructural analyses, including g-ratio measurements, axon-level data from multiple fields across animals were used for statistical comparisons, with both the number of animals and analyzed axons reported in the figure legends. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0 and SPSS 27.0.

Results

CTSS inhibition worsens neurological outcomes in ICH mice

Previous multi-omics analyses have identified cathepsin S (CTSS) as a hub gene markedly upregulated after intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) [40]. Consistent with these findings, Western blot analysis showed a rapid increase in CTSS protein levels in perihematomal tissues, peaking at day 7 compared with sham controls (Fig. 1A–B). Immunofluorescence further revealed predominant CTSS localization in Iba1⁺ macrophages/microglia within the perihematomal region (Fig. 1C–D), with minimal colocalization observed in NeuN⁺ neurons or GFAP⁺ astrocytes (Fig. S1).

To evaluate the role of CTSS in neurological recovery after ICH, mice were treated with the selective CTSS inhibitor LY3000328 or Vehicle for 7 days after ICH (Fig. 1E) [17, 27]. Although both groups developed neurological deficits after ICH, CTSS inhibitor-treated mice showed poorer recovery than Vehicle controls, as indicated by reduced rotarod performance, increased forelimb asymmetry in the cylinder test, and prolonged latency in the adhesive contact and removal tests at later time points after ICH (Fig. 1F–I).

CTSS inhibition disrupts lipid metabolism after ICH

Single-cell RNA sequencing was performed on perihematomal basal ganglia tissues collected from Vehicle- and CTSS-inhibitor–treated mice at day 7 post-ICH. UMAP projection and graph-based clustering identified ten major cell populations based on established marker genes (Fig. 2A–B) [35]. The overall cellular composition did not differ substantially between groups (Fig. S2).

Differential expression analysis revealed a predominance of downregulated genes in the CTSS inhibitor group, with the most pronounced transcriptional changes observed in macrophages (Fig. 2C). CTSS expression was largely enriched in macrophages, with lower expression detected in microglia (Fig. 2D–E), indicating that macrophages represent the primary CTSS-expressing population after ICH.

Gene Ontology enrichment analysis showed that genes upregulated following CTSS inhibition were mainly associated with translational and mitochondrial processes (Fig. S3). In contrast, downregulated genes were significantly enriched in lipid and fatty acid catabolic pathways, including cellular lipid metabolism and related oxidative metabolic processes (Fig. 2F–G). Gene set enrichment analysis further demonstrated reduced enrichment of fatty acid degradation and small-molecule catabolic gene sets in the CTSS inhibitor group (Fig. 2H–J). Together, these findings indicate that CTSS inhibition is associated with suppression of lipid metabolic transcriptional programs, particularly within macrophages.

CTSS inhibition disrupts macrophage lipid metabolism

To determine which macrophage subset is associated with CTSS expression after ICH, we performed unsupervised reclustering of macrophages, identifying four transcriptionally distinct subpopulations (Fig. 3A) [35]. Pseudotime reconstruction suggested a differentiation trajectory in which clusters 1 and 2 occupied earlier positions, whereas cluster 0 mapped to a later state (Fig. 3B) [41].

Based on cluster-enriched gene signatures (Fig. 3C), cluster 0 was defined as adaptive lipid-associated macrophages (aLAMs), characterized by high expression of lysosomal and lipid metabolic markers (e.g., Cd9, Trem2, Lpl, and Lipa) [42, 43]. Cluster 1 expressed monocyte/inflammatory recruitment-associated genes such as Ccr2 and Ly6c2, consistent with a recently infiltrating inflammatory macrophage state. Cluster 2 showed higher expression of antigen-presentation and immune-activation markers, including H2-Aa, H2-Ab1, and Cd74, whereas cluster 3 was enriched for cell-cycle and proliferative genes such as Mki67 and Top2a. The relative proportions of these macrophage subpopulations were comparable between Vehicle and CTSS inhibitor–treated groups (Fig. S4), indicating that CTSS inhibition did not substantially alter macrophage composition.

CTSS expression was predominantly enriched within the aLAMs subset (Fig. 3D). Functional enrichment analysis of CTSS-high aLAMs revealed overrepresentation of gene programs related to lipid metabolism, metabolic adaptation, and tissue remodeling (Fig. 3E), supporting the characterization of these cells as a metabolically specialized macrophage state following ICH.

Within the aLAMs subset, CTSS inhibition attenuated the adaptive lipid-associated transcriptional program, most prominently reflected by reduced Trem2 and Cd9 expression. This was accompanied by decreases in genes associated with lysosomal lipid processing and metabolic regulation (Lipa, Cebpb, Cd68) and modest downward shifts in lipid transport and cholesterol-handling genes (Abca1, Abcg1, Apoe) (Fig. 3F).

Consistent with these transcriptional alterations, functional enrichment analysis demonstrated reduced enrichment of lipid metabolic processes (Fig. 3G), together with decreased enrichment of pathways linked to tissue remodeling and myelin-supportive functions (Fig. 3H). Collectively, these results suggest that CTSS activity supports maintenance of the adaptive lipid-associated macrophage (aLAM) transcriptional program after ICH. CTSS inhibition attenuated key genes involved in lipid sensing, lysosomal processing, and cholesterol transport, indicating destabilization of the lipid-handling state rather than loss of the macrophage population.

CTSS inhibition impairs macrophage lipid efflux after ICH

Given the suppression of macrophage lipid metabolic programs revealed by single-cell transcriptomics, we next examined lipid handling at the cellular level. At day 7 after ICH, BODIPY staining showed increased lipid signal within perihematomal F4/80⁺ macrophages in the Inhibitor group compared with Vehicle controls (Fig. 4A–B). This finding was supported by flow cytometry, which showed increased intracellular BODIPY intensity in CD45^highCD11b⁺F4/80⁺ macrophages after CTSS inhibition (Fig. S5).

To determine whether lipid retention reflected enhanced uptake or impaired clearance, we examined both uptake- and efflux-related mechanisms. CD36 and Trem2 expression in F4/80⁺ macrophages was not significantly altered after CTSS inhibition (Fig. S5) [44], and DiD-labeled myelin debris uptake was comparable between Vehicle- and LY3000328-treated BMDMs (Fig. S6). In contrast, the LXR-regulated lipid efflux transporters ABCA1 and ABCG1 were markedly reduced in perihematomal tissues after CTSS inhibition (Fig. 4C–D) [29, 45]. ApoE expression in F4/80⁺ macrophages was also decreased in the Inhibitor group (Fig. 4E). These data suggest that CTSS inhibition-induced macrophage lipid retention is mainly associated with defective lipid efflux rather than increased lipid uptake or phagocytosis.

Because CTSS is predominantly localized to lysosomal compartments and is unlikely to directly regulate nuclear transcriptional programs such as LXR signaling, a more plausible interpretation is that CTSS inhibition disrupts lysosomal processing of engulfed lipid material, thereby secondarily affecting downstream lipid efflux programs [46–48]. Single-cell expression analysis showed reduced Npc1 and Npc2 expression in macrophages after CTSS inhibition (Fig. 4F). Since NPC1 and NPC2 mediate cholesterol export from late endosomes and lysosomes, these results suggested impaired mobilization of lysosome-processed cholesterol. Consistently, Filipin and LAMP1 staining revealed altered intracellular distribution of free cholesterol in CTSS inhibitor-treated macrophages. Compared with Vehicle-treated cells, which displayed relatively abundant and diffuse Filipin signal, Inhibitor-treated macrophages showed reduced overall Filipin intensity and a more restricted perilysosomal distribution adjacent to or partially overlapping with LAMP1⁺ compartments (Fig. 4G, I–J). Quantification of F4/80⁺ macrophages confirmed reduced Filipin mean fluorescence intensity in the Inhibitor group (Fig. 4H), supporting defective mobilization of lysosome-associated free cholesterol that may affect downstream LXR-regulated lipid efflux responses.

Based on previous evidence that LXR activation can promote NPC-related cholesterol mobilization, we used GW3965 as a pharmacological rescue strategy [49, 50]. GW3965 restored Npc1 and Npc2 mRNA levels in the LXR rescue group (Fig. 4K). These findings suggest that CTSS inhibition compromises macrophage lipid handling and efflux after ICH, accompanied by impaired lysosomal cholesterol mobilization and reduced expression of LXR-regulated efflux mediators.

CTSS inhibition reduces macrophage-derived lipid support for OPC differentiation

Efficient clearance and recycling of myelin-derived lipids are important for resolving post-injury lipid burden and supporting myelin repair [51–53]. In the preceding experiments, CTSS inhibition caused lipid retention in macrophages and suppressed lipid efflux-related molecules, indicating impaired macrophage lipid processing. In parallel, oligodendrocyte lineage cells from the Inhibitor group showed downregulation of myelination-related pathways (Fig. 5A), accompanied by suppression of lipid metabolic pathways (Fig. S7). We also observed reduced LDLR and VLDLR expression in oligodendrocyte lineage cells after CTSS inhibition (Fig. S7), suggesting decreased lipid-receiving capacity in this population. These findings raised the possibility that defective macrophage lipid handling may limit lipid availability for OPCs and affect their differentiation.

To test this possibility, we established a BMDM–OPC Transwell co-culture system (Fig. 5B). BMDMs were treated with Vehicle, LY3000328, or LY3000328 plus GW3965, loaded with BODIPY-labeled myelin debris, and co-cultured with OPCs after removal of unengulfed myelin debris. Compared with Vehicle-treated BMDMs, LY3000328-treated BMDMs markedly reduced BODIPY signal in NG2⁺ OPCs, whereas GW3965 partially restored OPC BODIPY uptake (Fig. 5C–D). These results suggest that CTSS inhibition reduces the availability of macrophage-processed myelin-derived lipids to OPCs, while GW3965 alleviates this defect.

We next assessed OPC differentiation in the same co-culture system. Immunofluorescence staining for NG2 and MBP showed that OPCs co-cultured with LY3000328-treated BMDMs retained a higher NG2⁺ phenotype, whereas GW3965 reduced NG2⁺ OPC retention and promoted progression toward a more differentiated state (Fig. 5E–F). Together, these data suggest that CTSS inhibition weakens macrophage-derived lipid support for OPCs, thereby limiting OPC lipid acquisition and differentiation, while GW3965 partially restores this macrophage–OPC lipid-support axis.

LXR activation ameliorates CTSS inhibition-induced remyelination defects after ICH

To determine whether impaired macrophage–OPC lipid support was associated with defective myelin repair in vivo, we examined myelin integrity after CTSS inhibition. At day 14 after ICH, immunofluorescence staining showed reduced MBP signal in both the external capsule and perihematomal region of Inhibitor-treated mice compared with Vehicle controls (Fig. 6A, C). Three-dimensional reconstruction of MBP and neurofilament (NF) staining further revealed reduced MBP wrapping around NF⁺ axons in the Inhibitor group, indicating impaired axonal remyelination (Fig. 6B, D). Consistently, Western blot analysis showed reduced MBP protein levels in perihematomal tissues after CTSS inhibition at day 7 after ICH (Fig. 6E–F).

We next examined myelin ultrastructure using transmission electron microscopy. Although myelin abnormalities were observed after ICH in both groups, CTSS inhibitor-treated mice exhibited more pronounced myelin swelling, splitting, loosening, and focal thinning (Fig. 6G). Quantitative g-ratio analysis showed increased g-ratio values across axons of different diameters in the Inhibitor group, indicating thinner myelin sheaths and defective remyelination (Fig. 6H). These tissue-level, protein, and ultrastructural findings demonstrate that CTSS inhibition compromises myelin repair after ICH.

Given that GW3965 partially restored NPC1/NPC2-associated cholesterol trafficking and macrophage-derived lipid support for OPCs in the preceding experiments, we further evaluated whether LXR activation improved neurological recovery under CTSS inhibition. Compared with the Inhibitor group, LXR rescue improved rotarod performance, reduced forelimb asymmetry in the cylinder test, and shortened adhesive contact and removal times at later time points after ICH (Fig. 6I–L). Together, these findings suggest that pharmacological LXR activation mitigates CTSS inhibition-associated myelin repair defects and improves neurological recovery after ICH.

Discussion

This study identifies CTSS as an important regulator of macrophage lipid handling and white matter repair after ICH. We found that CTSS was markedly induced after ICH and was enriched in infiltrating macrophages, particularly in adaptive lipid-associated macrophage states. Functionally, CTSS inhibition shifted macrophages toward a lipid-retaining state, weakened lipid efflux and ApoE-associated lipid redistribution, reduced macrophage-derived lipid support for OPCs, and ultimately impaired myelin repair. These findings suggest that CTSS-expressing macrophages are not merely inflammatory responders after ICH, but may also act as lipid-processing intermediates that connect debris handling with oligodendrocyte lineage repair.

The mechanistic link between CTSS and macrophage lipid efflux likely involves lysosomal cholesterol trafficking. Supporting this interpretation, CTSS inhibition reduced Npc1 and Npc2 expression, both of which are required for cholesterol export from late endosomes and lysosomes [54–56]. Filipin/LAMP1 staining further suggested altered intracellular distribution of unesterified cholesterol, with reduced overall Filipin signal and a more restricted distribution of the remaining signal adjacent to LAMP1⁺ compartments. These findings support a model in which CTSS inhibition impairs lysosomal cholesterol mobilization and is accompanied by reduced LXR-associated lipid efflux and ApoE-mediated lipid redistribution.

An important implication of our findings is that lipid accumulation in macrophages after CTSS inhibition cannot be simply attributed to increased myelin debris uptake. Although intracellular lipid signals appeared elevated, DiD-labeled myelin debris uptake and phagocytosis-related marker analysis did not indicate a major increase in macrophage uptake capacity. Instead, impaired NPC1/NPC2-associated cholesterol mobilization was accompanied by reductions in ABCA1/ABCG1 and ApoE expression, suggesting defective post-engulfment lipid processing and efflux. After ICH, macrophages encounter abundant lipid-rich material generated by myelin breakdown, cellular injury, and hematoma-associated tissue damage [57]. Efficient handling of this lipid burden is critical, as unresolved lipid accumulation may compromise macrophage function, whereas properly processed and exported lipids may contribute to tissue remodeling [58]. Previous studies have shown that phagocytes can recycle myelin-derived cholesterol and redistribute lipid cargo to neighboring cells [59–61]. Moreover, microglia have been reported to promote oligodendrocyte maturation and myelination through TREM2-dependent cholesterol efflux and ApoE-mediated lipoprotein transport [62]. Our findings are consistent with this concept and extend it to the ICH setting, suggesting that CTSS-dependent macrophage lipid processing may contribute to the reparative lipid environment required for white matter repair.

The BMDM–OPC co-culture experiments further linked macrophage lipid handling to oligodendrocyte lineage repair. CTSS inhibition reduced the transfer or availability of macrophage-processed myelin-derived lipids to OPCs and limited OPC differentiation, whereas GW3965 partially rescued these defects. These findings suggest that macrophages may contribute to remyelination not only by clearing inhibitory myelin debris, but also by processing injury-derived lipids into forms that support OPC maturation.

The in vivo myelin analyses further support this interpretation. CTSS inhibition reduced MBP expression, decreased MBP wrapping around NF⁺ axons, increased g-ratio values, and aggravated myelin ultrastructural abnormalities. These structural defects were accompanied by delayed neurological recovery, whereas LXR activation partially improved functional outcomes under CTSS inhibition. Notably, hematoma volume and Perls’ Prussian blue staining showed no marked differences between groups (Fig. S8), suggesting that impaired white matter repair was unlikely to be primarily driven by altered hematoma clearance or iron deposition. Thus, the detrimental effect of CTSS inhibition appears more closely related to defective repair-associated lipid handling than to a broad failure of hematoma resolution.

Our findings add a repair-related perspective to the established immune-regulatory role of CTSS. CTSS has been widely studied as a lysosomal protease involved in immune activation and proteolytic remodeling. Beyond these recognized functions, our data suggest that, during the subacute phase after ICH, CTSS-expressing macrophages may also support tissue repair by maintaining lysosomal lipid processing, macrophage lipid efflux, and macrophage–OPC metabolic coupling. Thus, CTSS function after hemorrhagic brain injury may be stage- and context-dependent, and CTSS-targeted interventions should be considered not only in relation to immune modulation, but also in relation to repair-associated lipid handling.

From a translational perspective, LY3000328 is a selective, orally available CTSS inhibitor that has undergone phase I evaluation in healthy volunteers, including assessment of safety, tolerability, pharmacokinetics, and pharmacodynamics [63, 64]. This clinical development background provides translational context for CTSS-targeted pharmacological modulation. However, our findings suggest that CTSS-directed therapy should be considered carefully in relation to disease stage and repair biology. In hemorrhagic brain injury, inhibiting CTSS during a phase when macrophages participate in lipid processing and white matter repair may have unintended effects on tissue recovery.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the scRNA-seq analysis was performed using pooled samples and served primarily as an exploratory approach; key findings were subsequently validated using independent biological replicates in flow cytometry, immunofluorescence, Western blot, qPCR, BMDM–OPC co-culture, and in vivo assays. Second, systemic LY3000328 treatment cannot fully distinguish macrophage-intrinsic effects from contributions of other CTSS-expressing cells, and brain LY3000328 concentrations or local CTSS activity were not directly measured. Future studies using macrophage- or myeloid-specific Ctss deletion, adoptive transfer, and direct pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic assessment will be needed to define cell-specific and lesion-localized effects. Third, the collagenase-induced ICH model and the use of male mice may limit generalizability to spontaneous human ICH and sex-dependent repair responses; complementary models, such as autologous blood injection or disease-associated spontaneous hemorrhage, will be needed to further validate CTSS-dependent macrophage lipid handling in post-ICH white matter repair [65–67]. Finally, although our data support an association between lysosomal cholesterol trafficking and LXR-related lipid efflux, endogenous oxysterol ligands and LXR transcriptional activity were not directly assessed, which may help further clarify the underlying mechanism.

In conclusion, our findings define CTSS as a regulator of macrophage lysosomal lipid processing after ICH. CTSS supports NPC1/NPC2-associated cholesterol trafficking, macrophage lipid efflux, and macrophage-derived lipid support for OPC differentiation and myelin repair. CTSS inhibition disrupts this repair-associated lipid-handling program, thereby aggravating white matter injury and delaying neurological recovery (Fig. 7). These findings reveal a CTSS-centered immune–metabolic mechanism linking macrophage lipid processing to white matter repair after hemorrhagic brain injury.
