Resection of hemorrhagic intracranial testicular choriocarcinoma metastasis with a novel camera-suction device: A surgical case report
Authors: Manat Singh Chopra, Michelle Lin, David J. Cote, Robert Grady Briggs, Jonathan Sisti, Gabriel Zada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Intracranial metastases from testicular choriocarcinoma are rare but clinically important due to their proclivity for symptomatic hemorrhage. Emergent surgical resection is often required for the prevention of neurological deterioration.Case Description: A 22-year-old male with a history of multiple recurrent metastatic intracranial testicular choriocarcinoma presented with an acute right frontal intraparenchymal hemorrhage. He had previously undergone neurosurgical intervention for resection of multiple metastases. An emergent right frontal craniotomy for tumor resection and hematoma evacuation was performed with a novel suction-camera device. Pathology confirmed metastatic choriocarcinoma consistent with the patient’s medical history. Postoperatively, the patient resumed chemotherapy and received adjuvant radiation therapy to the resection cavity. Despite these interventions, the patient continued to experience rapid disease progression.Conclusion: This case highlights the management of a hemorrhagic brain metastasis from testicular choriocarcinoma. We describe the use of a minimally invasive camera-suction system to augment the process of tumor resection.

INTRODUCTION

Background

Intracranial hemorrhagic metastases from nonseminomatous germ cell tumors (NSGCTs), particularly choriocarcinomas, are exceedingly rare and often herald a poor prognosis due to their highly vascular and aggressive nature.[9,14,17] Choriocarcinomas tend to metastasize hematogenously and have a predilection for the lungs, liver, and brain, with cerebral hemorrhage being a life-threatening complication.[19-21] Here, we report a case of a 22-year-old male with recurrent testicular choriocarcinoma who developed a hemorrhagic right frontal brain metastasis. Surgical management used intraoperative neuronavigation, neuromonitoring, and a novel camera-suction system for gross total resection. Histopathology confirmed that the lesion was consistent with metastatic choriocarcinoma.

CASE DESCRIPTION

A 22-year-old male with a history of Stage IIIC NSGCT, initially diagnosed as a mixed germ cell tumor (GCTs) composed of 60% teratoma and 40% choriocarcinoma, first presented with a left testicular mass. He underwent left radical orchiectomy followed by four cycles of VIP chemotherapy (etoposide, ifosfamide, and cisplatin). Despite initial treatment response, he developed progressive pulmonary, retroperitoneal, hepatic, and intracranial metastases.

Nineteen months later, he developed hemorrhagic brain lesions that required multiple neurosurgical interventions, including a right occipital craniotomy for gross total resection of a hemorrhagic mass. Histopathology confirmed metastatic choriocarcinoma.

The patient was later transferred to our institution 7 months later following the acute development of headaches, nausea, and vomiting. Imaging demonstrated a 4-cm hemorrhagic lesion in the right frontal lobe, with surrounding vasogenic edema and significant brain compression [Figure 1]. Given the severity of mass effect, urgent surgical intervention was recommended. A right frontal craniotomy was performed for hematoma evacuation and gross total tumor resection of the underlying tumor. The surgery was aided by the use of a novel camera-suction tool called the HUBBLE AV (Vizion Health LLC, Charlotte, North Carolina, USA) [Video 1]. This device enabled careful separation of the tumor from the adjacent frontal cortex and minimized our use of a surgical microscope.

Pathological analysis confirmed a hemorrhagic metastatic lesion with highly atypical, pleomorphic tumor cells consistent with choriocarcinoma, consistent with the patient’s medical history. Following surgery, the patient resumed systemic chemotherapy and received fractionated radiation to the resection cavity. Despite temporary clinical improvement, the patient developed recurrent epistaxis, a drop in his hemoglobin levels, and a precipitous rise in his beta-human chorionic gonadotropin (β-hCG) levels, prompting further workup which revealed rapid progression of his cancer. Imaging revealed a new enhancing lesion in the left frontal lobe and additional tiny hemorrhagic foci. This sequence of rapidly recurring, hemorrhagic brain metastases despite aggressive multimodal treatment underscores the fulminant course and poor prognosis of intracranial testicular choriocarcinoma metastases.

Surgical technique

The patient was taken to the operating room for a right frontal craniotomy. Neuronavigation was registered to aid in the precise localization of the lesion and guide incision planning. A curvilinear incision was made over the right frontal bone, and a 4-cm bone flap was elevated. Intraoperative neuromonitoring, including somatomotor potentials, remained stable throughout the case.

Under microscopic visualization, a sulcal entry point was carefully chosen and opened using an 11-blade. We then converted from using the microscope to using the HUBBLE AV camera-suction device to resect hemorrhagic tumor tissue. The tumor was resected in a piecemeal fashion until gross total resection was achieved [Figure 2]. Following meticulous hemostasis, the skull was closed in standard fashion.

Postoperative course

In the immediate postoperative period, dexamethasone and levetiracetam were started. These were well tolerated and later tapered in a stepwise fashion over several days. The patient remained neurologically intact, without focal deficits. His headaches, nausea, and vomiting decreased in intensity and frequency. Follow-up magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated complete surgical resection of the lesion and resolution of midline shift. The patient was discharged from the hospital in stable condition on postoperative day 4.

The patient subsequently resumed chemotherapy consisting of gemcitabine, oxaliplatin, and paclitaxel 2 weeks after surgery. He also underwent fractionated radiation therapy to the resection cavity. On follow-up, his neurological examination remained intact with no focal deficits. Tumor markers (alpha-fetoprotein, lactate dehydrogenase, β-hCG) showed a favorable downward trend in the immediate weeks following surgery; however, these later rebounded, indicating progressive disease.

Despite the initial symptomatic improvement, the patient experienced complications during his postoperative course. Within weeks of discharge, he developed recurrent epistaxis from the left nasal cavity, secondary to a hemorrhagic nasopharyngeal metastasis. Serial brain imaging also demonstrated a new left frontal enhancing lesion and punctate hemorrhagic foci, concerning for new cerebral metastases. Tumor markers, particularly β-hCG, increased rapidly from 2.1 to over 38,000 mIU/mL, confirming the rapid systemic progression of his disease.

DISCUSSION

Brain metastases from testicular choriocarcinoma are rare. When they occur, they frequently present with hemorrhage due to the aggressive vascularity and necrosis associated with this cancer.[10,11,13] The risk of intracranial hemorrhage is significantly higher in choriocarcinoma compared to other germ cell subtypes, largely due to trophoblastic invasion of cerebral vasculature.[4,7,12,16]

In this case, surgery was not aimed at controlling the underlying cancer but was performed to address an acute intracerebral hemorrhage with significant mass effect and risk of neurological decline. The patient had a large right frontal hematoma on imaging, along with signs of increased intracranial pressure, making emergent decompression necessary. The goal of surgery was consistent with standard management of intracerebral hemorrhage – rapid evacuation to reduce mass effect and prevent further neurological injury – rather than any attempt at curative tumor resection.

With respect to oncologic surgical principles, en bloc resection is generally preferred for discrete, solid intracranial metastases when the goal is durable local tumor control and reduction of leptomeningeal dissemination risk. In this case, the surgery was done because of an acute intracerebral hemorrhage with significant mass effect, not for tumor removal or oncologic control. The lesion had a large hemorrhagic component and disrupted tissue planes, so attempting to remove it en bloc would have been difficult in an emergency setting focused on decompression. Instead, a piecemeal evacuation was performed, which is standard for this type of hemorrhagic lesion. The camera-integrated suction system allowed for gradual, controlled debulking under direct visualization, with the main priorities being safe hematoma evacuation and achieving hemostasis rather than any attempt at complete tumor resection.

In the present case, though, the use of advanced intraoperative tools – particularly the HUBBLE AV camera-integrated suction system – facilitated complete resection of a large, right frontal hemorrhagic lesion while minimizing cortical disruption.

In addition to standard microsurgical techniques, this case highlights the novel use of the HUBBLE AV camera-suction device as an adjunct for intracranial hemorrhage evacuation. The device provides white-light visualization without the need for a conventional operating microscope during deep-cavity work, enabling continuous visualization in the hematoma cavity. Rather than improving “operative space,” its primary advantage lies in the co-localization of suction and imaging, allowing simultaneous evacuation and real-time visualization of hemorrhagic and tumoral debris. This reduces instrument exchange and may improve workflow ergonomics in confined operative corridors where active bleeding and clot burden can limit sustained microscopic line-of-sight.

Although the system provides high-definition 2D exoscopic visualization, the camera operates in close proximity to the surgical target within the cavity, allowing clinically adequate visualization in hemorrhagic environments where macroscopic anatomy and bleeding planes, rather than fine microvascular detail, are the primary focus.

The device is intended to complement, not replace, standard microsurgical visualization. Its use is described in Video 1. From a safety and implementation standpoint, the device has been utilized clinically according to manufacturer specifications, with white-light illumination maintained within accepted safety thresholds. Irrigation and field clearance are performed using standard microsurgical technique, allowing maintenance of visualization during continuous suction.

This system appears most suitable for hemorrhagic lesions or clot-dominant cavities where tissue is soft and amenable to suction-assisted evacuation, rather than firm or fibrous lesions requiring sharp dissection or en bloc resection.

The HUBBLE shows how an exoscopic tool can realistically work alongside standard visualization methods. Its small footprint, ease of use, and built-in suction with imaging could be helpful in certain situations where space and access are limited. That is especially true in acute intracerebral hemorrhage cases, where the surgical field can be deep, visibility is often compromised by active bleeding, and the cavity contains a mix of liquid and clot that’s constantly changing. In those moments, being able to maintain continuous visualization during evacuation can be useful. In this setting, the device is best thought of as an assistive tool during emergent hematoma evacuation, rather than something used for formal tumor resection. The priority is rapid decompression and working through shifting hemorrhagic planes safely, not en bloc removal. Overall, this case adds early, descriptive evidence that camera-integrated suction systems like this may have a role as adjuncts in intracranial hemorrhage surgery, including cases where the bleed is related to an underlying tumor.

A summary of previously reported cases of intracranial hemorrhage due to testicular choriocarcinoma is provided in Table 1, highlighting the rarity, varied presentation, and often poor prognosis associated with this aggressive disease. Of the 15 reported cases identified in the literature (excluding the present one), 11 (73.3%) resulted in death despite surgical and/ or medical intervention. Mixed GCTs with choriocarcinoma components were the most common diagnosis (5/15, 33.3%), followed by pure choriocarcinoma (4/15, 26.7%). Hemorrhage location was diverse, with no clear predilection, although multifocal involvement was commonly observed. The age range at presentation spans 17–65 years, with a mean of 26.7 years old, underscoring the tendency for this malignancy to affect young adult males.[1-3,5,6,8,10,11,13,15,18] This data underscores the highly vascular and aggressive nature of testicular choriocarcinoma brain metastases requiring urgent surgical management and aggressive systemic therapy.

CONCLUSION

This report highlights the surgical management of a rare case of hemorrhagic brain metastasis from testicular choriocarcinoma. Gross total resection was achieved using neuronavigation, intraoperative monitoring, and a novel suction-camera device, resulting in immediate relief of mass effect and stabilization of neurological status.
