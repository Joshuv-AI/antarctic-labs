Lipophagy in disease: signaling control, organelle communication, and therapeutic opportunities
Authors: Yu-yang Du, Xin-yao Chen, Yue Fang, Qing Wang, Jia-peng Li, Ting-ting Guo, Jia-ni Lin, Jia-yue Fu, Ming-li Xie, Li-jun Hao, Sai Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLipid droplet (LD) accumulation and impaired lipid mobilization induce lipotoxic stress and contribute to metabolic, cardiovascular, cancer, and neurodegenerative diseases (NDDs). Although LD-selective autophagy (lipophagy) is being increasingly studied, the mechanisms that confer LD specificity and enable tissue- and stage-specific therapeutic modulation remain unclear. Effective lipophagic flux requires coordinated LD coat remodeling, nutrient and energy sensing, and organelle contact. Coat remodeling governs substrate access and droplet recognition. The AMPK–mTORC1–TFEB axis links autophagy induction to lysosome biogenesis and capacity. Endoplasmic reticulum (ER)–LD, mitochondria–LD, and LD–lysosome contact sites facilitate lipid transfer by coupling lysosomal hydrolysis to mitochondrial β-oxidation. Lipophagy is a highly stage- and cell-type-dependent process: it removes excess lipids to protect cells, but its dysregulation can promote inflammation and fibrogenesis or supply substrates to tumors. This review synthesizes mechanistic and translational evidence on lipophagy initiation, regulation, and disease relevance, and identifies the following priorities: robust biomarkers, distinction of cargo-recognition defects from lysosomal dysfunction, and precision interventions tailored to the tissue and disease stage.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Lipid droplets (LDs) are conserved cytoplasmic organelles that store neutral lipids in a stable yet dynamic form [1]. They contain a core of triacylglycerols and sterol esters surrounded by a phospholipid monolayer and a regulated proteome. This architecture allows LDs to sequester lipotoxic lipids, provide lipids when needed, and organize intracellular lipid flux [2]. LDs are therefore active organelles involved in signaling, membrane biogenesis, organelle remodeling, and stress adaptation [3]. In metabolically active tissues such as the liver, adipose tissue, muscle, and brain, LD dynamics link nutrient availability to membrane synthesis, energy production, and systemic metabolic homeostasis [4].

When the energy or biosynthetic demand increases, LDs are rapidly mobilized mainly through lipolysis and lipophagy [5]. Lipolysis hydrolyzes triacylglycerols at the LD surface, producing free fatty acids (FFAs) and glycerol for oxidation or reuse. Lipophagy is a selective autophagic process in which LDs or LD fragments are delivered to lysosomes, where lysosomal acid lipases release FFAs for metabolism [5] (Fig. 1). These pathways are coordinated. Lipolysis can remodel the LD surface and promote autophagic recognition, whereas lipophagy can supplement or replace lipase-mediated hydrolysis, depending on the nutrient status, LD size, cell type, and disease context. Together, they provide metabolic flexibility by enabling rapid fatty acid release and lysosome-dependent clearance of excess or selected LD subpopulations [5, 6].

Dual-pathway lipid droplet catabolism. During increased energy demand, neutral lipids stored in LDs are mobilized through two complementary routes: lipolysis, in which cytosolic lipases hydrolyze LD triglycerides to release FFAs, and lipophagy, in which LDs are sequestered by autophagic membranes and delivered to lysosomes for degradation. In both cases, liberated FFAs are routed to mitochondria to fuel β-oxidation, thereby supporting energy homeostasis and limiting lipid stress. Inhibition of autophagy reduces lipophagic flux, leading to LD accumulation and decreased fatty-acid oxidation

Autophagy was first shown to contribute directly to lipid catabolism in hepatocytes in 2009 [7], extending its role beyond protein and organelle turnover. Subsequent studies in diverse cells and nutritional states confirmed that autophagy-mediated LD degradation supports lipid homeostasis and the energy balance, especially during fasting and metabolic stress [8, 9]. Mechanistically, lipophagy provides a sustained supply of FFAs for mitochondrial β-oxidation, supporting ATP production and limiting lipid intermediates that induce oxidative and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress [10]. Thus, lipophagy couples lysosomal recycling to mitochondrial fuel use through nutrient-sensing networks [11].

Consistent with this role, genetic or pharmacological inhibition of autophagy, particularly in the liver, commonly causes LD accumulation, reduced fatty acid oxidation, and an impaired energy balance [12–14]. The LD abundance therefore reflects both the lipid input and the efficiency of intracellular clearance. When lipophagic flux decreases, lipid storage can exceed lipid disposal, promoting lipotoxic signaling and tissue dysfunction. Conversely, lipophagy induction can reduce lipid overload and preserve metabolic flexibility [13]. Recent evidence also shows that LD turnover is spatially organized by contacts with the ER, mitochondria, and lysosomes, which determine whether lipids are oxidized, used for membrane synthesis, or retained in storage [14]. Lipophagy is therefore not only a degradative pathway but also a hub for organelle communication and lipid trafficking during stress.

Dysregulated lipophagy has been linked to lipid-associated diseases, including metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), alcoholic fatty liver disease (AFLD), obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and its complications, atherosclerosis (AS), selected cancers, and neurodegenerative diseases (NDDs) [15]. In hepatometabolic disease, insufficient lipophagy promotes steatosis by sustaining LD persistence and limiting fatty acid use. In immune and vascular cells, altered lipophagy can affect lipid handling in foam cells, inflammatory signaling, and plaque progression [16]. In the nervous system, impaired autophagic‒lysosomal activity may connect pathological lipid accumulation with proteostasis defects, neuroinflammation, and cellular vulnerability [17]. However, lipophagy may be protective or harmful, depending on the tissue and disease stage: it can reduce lipotoxic stress but may also sustain inflammation, fibrogenesis, or tumor growth by providing metabolic substrates [16, 17]. Defining adaptive versus maladaptive lipophagy and identifying safe therapeutic nodes are therefore essential.

In this review, we examine the mechanisms of lipophagy, its functions in disease, and therapeutic interventions. We prioritize well-supported pathways, reconcile model-dependent findings where possible, and assess how unresolved questions shape translational strategies. We propose that lipophagy is an integrated metabolic program, rather than a simple lipid clearance route, that coordinates nutrient sensing, interorganelle communication, and disease-specific metabolic adaptation. In this context, lipophagic activity helps determine the lipid fate and downstream metabolic outcomes.

Initiation and regulatory mechanisms of lipophagy

Selective recognition and engulfment of LDs

Lipophagy is a selective autophagy pathway in which cells recognize LDs, recruit autophagic membranes to their surface, and deliver LD-derived material to lysosomes for hydrolysis and recycling [5]. By linking neutral lipid storage to lysosomal catabolism, lipophagy mobilizes lipid reserves during nutrient limitation and prevents pathological lipid accumulation [18]. LDs are unusual autophagic cargo because they contain a hydrophobic neutral lipid core enclosed by a single phospholipid monolayer. These features impose biophysical and geometric constraints on membrane capture compared with many protein-rich cytosolic substrates [19]. Efficient sequestration therefore requires specialized membrane engagement at the LD surface, including direct interactions between autophagy proteins and lipid membranes and receptor-like docking mechanisms [20, 21].

The LD size is a major factor determining whether LDs are catabolized by cytosolic lipolysis or lipophagy [22]. Because a phagophore must elongate and close around its cargo, small LDs are more readily sequestered, whereas large LDs may exceed the capacity of single-event autophagosomal engulfment and require prior processing. In hepatocytes, adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL)-dependent lipolysis preferentially targets larger LDs. ATGL inhibition causes enlarged LDs to accumulate, whereas lysosomal inhibition increases the abundance of small LDs in acidic degradative vesicles. These findings support a tandem model in which lipolysis precedes lipophagy by trimming large LDs or generating smaller LDs that are more suitable for autophagic uptake [22].

This size effect also reflects the surface composition of the LDs. Small LDs have a higher curvature, whereas large LDs have a lower curvature and a broader phospholipid monolayer, features that influence lipid packing, surface tension, and the residence of coat proteins or enzymes. Surface tension-driven sorting of human PLIN proteins supports the idea that LDs with distinct physical states can recruit different surface proteomes [23]. Thus, LD heterogeneity has functional consequences: nascent or lipolysis-shortened LDs, especially those generated after PLIN remodeling or CMA-mediated PLIN2/3 removal, may be primed for LC3-positive membrane recruitment and lysosomal degradation, whereas large PLIN-stabilized LDs initially depend more on cytosolic lipases or piecemeal LD–lysosome contacts [22–24].

This size-and-surface framework also connects mammalian macrolipophagy with nonmammalian LD turnover. In budding yeast, LDs can be degraded by microlipophagy rather than by capture within newly formed autophagosomes. LDs translocate from ER-associated sites to the vacuolar surface and enter the vacuolar lumen through direct vacuolar membrane invagination. During the stationary phase, nutrient limitation, or ER stress, sterol-enriched liquid-ordered vacuolar microdomains organize LD docking, invagination, and internalization. The disruption of these domains impairs LD uptake, indicating that local lipid-phase organization of the vacuolar membrane is a key determinant of microlipophagy [25–27].

Microlipophagy in yeast shares selected components with canonical autophagy but is not simply a scaled-down form of macroautophagy. Atg6/Vps30 and Atg14, which are components of the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase complex, localize to vacuolar microdomains and support their remodeling during stationary-phase or glucose-starvation-induced LD uptake. Selective autophagy proteins such as Atg21 and Atg32 are also required for efficient stationary-phase microlipophagy, indicating that LD entry into the vacuole depends on context-specific Atg proteins. ESCRT complexes provide a parallel membrane-remodeling module. During a diauxic shift, lipid imbalance, or ER stress, ESCRT components promote vacuolar invagination and scission, and the ESCRT-0 component Vps27 cooperates functionally with clathrin to support microautophagic uptake. Microlipophagy in yeast therefore shows that LD turnover can be driven not only by cargo recognition and LC3/Atg8-dependent phagophore expansion but also by sterol-rich vacuolar microdomains and ESCRT-dependent remodeling of the degradative organelle membrane [25, 27–30].

In contrast, in macroautophagy, sequestration requires phagophore growth and closure. Canonical macroautophagy depends on LC3/ATG8-mediated membrane expansion and sealing to generate autophagosomes that fuse with lysosomes [31]. The regulation of core autophagy factors can therefore tune lipophagic flux. For example, ATG7 and ATG16L1 modifications affect LC3 lipidation and autophagosome biogenesis [32]. These steps support sustained phagophore elongation, efficient closure, and productive LD delivery rather than arrest at immature intermediates [33].

How this machinery selectively targets LDs remains incompletely understood [33]. Productive capture likely requires a stable contact between the phagophore and a lipid-dominated LD surface, which complicates the validation of a canonical cargo–receptor–LC3 model [19, 20]. LC3 family proteins can bind phospholipids and associate with lipid membranes, and membrane-rich LD environments may create diffusion barriers; both properties may influence how autophagic membranes approach and remain attached to LDs [21]. Receptor-like factors also contribute to LD turnover, although no universal LD tag has been defined [19–21]. Among the candidate selectivity mechanisms, Rab GTPases provide a defined trafficking layer that links LD recognition to autophagosomal membrane recruitment. Rab10, an LD-associated GTPase, couples nutrient stress signaling to LD engulfment in hepatocytes. Nutrient deprivation or pharmacological mTOR inhibition increases Rab10 activity and the recruitment of Rab10 to nascent LC3-positive membranes at the LD surface [34]. Activated Rab10 promotes the assembly of a complex with EH domain binding protein 1 (EHBP1) and the membrane-remodeling ATPase EHD2, thereby driving LD engulfment by the growing autophagosome [34]. Rab10 therefore provides experimental evidence that the LD-associated trafficking machinery can promote the selective autophagic sequestration of LDs.

Ubiquitin-binding autophagy receptors may provide a complementary route to LD selection [35]. p62/SQSTM1 has been proposed to recognize modified proteins on the LD surface and recruit LDs to nascent autophagosomes, although the evidence for an obligatory p62-dependent lipophagy pathway remains limited [36]. Additional LD-associated regulators further suggest that selectivity is distributed across multiple factors and varies with the cell type, metabolic state, and LD composition [23].

LD biophysics also shape PLIN coat behavior. Because the LD size is linked to the membrane curvature and surface tension, the PLIN coating should not be viewed as a uniform steric barrier on all the droplets. Surface tension-driven PLIN sorting shows that LD surface biophysics can redistribute coat proteins across droplets in distinct physical states [23]. This mechanism may explain how the LD size and surface composition influence the recruitment of ATGL, the chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA) machinery, and autophagy-associated factors. Consistently, the removal or functional attenuation of PLIN2 and PLIN3 increases access by lipases and autophagic membranes, accelerating lipid turnover [24, 37–39].

Perilipin phosphorylation and proteolytic turnover provide mechanisms for remodeling this surface barrier. In adipocytes, cAMP‒PKA signaling phosphorylates PLIN1 and remodels LD-surface complexes, promoting the recruitment of the lipolytic machinery [40]. PLIN stability is also regulated by the ubiquitin‒proteasome system: PLIN can be polyubiquitinated and degraded by proteasomes [41], and PLIN2 is degraded through a proteasome-dependent pathway during the regression of lipid-storing cells [42]. These nonlysosomal proteolytic routes are distinct from those of CMA, but they converge functionally with lysosome-dependent coat remodeling by reducing the LD coat density and exposing the LD surface to lipases, chaperones, and autophagic membranes.

These mechanisms support a sequential model in which chaperone-mediated or chaperone-assisted lysosomal pathways first reduce LD surface barriers, enabling subsequent macroautophagic sequestration [43, 44]. PLIN2 and PLIN3 play overlapping but distinct roles. Both proteins can serve as CMA substrates, and their HSPA8/Hsc70- and LAMP2A-dependent degradation precedes lipolysis and facilitates the recruitment of ATGL and macroautophagy proteins to LDs [24]. PLIN2 generally acts as a stabilizing coat protein that protects LDs and promotes lipid storage, whereas PLIN3 may also function as a signaling-responsive scaffold for the autophagy machinery during acute hepatic lipid overload. Consistent with these findings, a study of mTORC1–PLIN3 signaling showed that PLIN3 knockdown abolished oleate-induced lipophagy and that mTORC1-dependent PLIN3 phosphorylation promoted LD degradation [45]. This sequence is context dependent. Epigenetic programs that increase PLIN2 expression can strengthen LD surface protection and exacerbate hepatic steatosis, suggesting that the PLIN barrier intensifies as the lipid burden increases [46]. Conversely, LAMP2A destabilization reduces the CMA capacity and promotes hepatic lipid accumulation, which is consistent with impaired LD turnover when early coat removal steps fail [47]. Overall, these data support a model in which PLIN-dependent remodeling of the LD surface promotes recognition and sequestration by macroautophagy, followed by lysosomal fusion and degradation to complete lipid recycling [5, 18, 31–33].

Signaling networks regulating lipophagy

Lipophagy, the selective autophagic degradation of LDs, is regulated by nutrient- and stress-responsive signaling nodes that also control general autophagy [45, 48, 49]. These pathways are not lipophagy-specific cascades; rather, they convert metabolic cues into coordinated changes in autophagic flux, LD coat remodeling, organelle trafficking, lysosomal function, and lipid turnover.

mTORC1 and AMPK form the core nutrient-sensing module. Under nutrient-rich conditions, active mTORC1 suppresses autophagy initiation and thereby limits lipophagy [50, 51]. Starvation or nutrient stress reduces mTORC1 activity, relieves this inhibition, and increases autophagic flux and lipophagic LD degradation [52–55]. In hepatocytes, sustained mTORC1 activation impairs lipophagy, promotes LD accumulation, and contributes to steatosis [49–58]. Thus, mTORC1 functions as the inhibitory arm of the lipophagy regulatory network.

Rab7 links this nutrient-sensing module to endolysosomal trafficking. In addition to its established role in late endosomal and lysosomal transport, Rab7 can bind to mTOR through the N-terminal HEAT repeat domain and regulate the lysosomal mTOR distribution and lipid metabolic signaling [59]. Although this interaction has been described in myeloid-derived suppressor cells rather than hepatocytes, it suggests that Rab7 may support lipophagy by positioning degradative organelles near LDs and coupling endolysosomal organization to mTOR-dependent autophagy control.

AMPK provides the complementary energy stress arm. Nutrient deprivation, exercise, and other energy-demanding conditions activate AMPK, which antagonizes mTORC1 and promotes autophagy [60–62]. Accordingly, starvation and physical activity increase lipophagy and lipid catabolism, supporting lipid homeostasis during energy stress [63–67]. AMPK also connects upstream energy sensing to LD coat remodeling. After PLIN2 binds the CMA chaperone HSPA8/Hsc70, AMPK-dependent phosphorylation primes PLIN2 for CMA-mediated degradation [68]. Similarly, apigenin activates AMPK, promotes PLIN2 phosphorylation and Nrf2 nuclear translocation, enhances CMA, accelerates PLIN degradation, and reduces LD accumulation in hepatocytes [37]. During starvation, AMPK can also phosphorylate PLIN3, promoting LD dispersion and access through the degradative machinery [69]. The AMPK–PLIN2/3 axis therefore links energy sensing to LD accessibility by lipases, CMA, and macroautophagy.

Transcriptional and lysosomal biogenesis programs add a slower regulatory layer. Transcription factor EB (TFEB) promotes autophagy and lysosomal biogenesis by inducing the expression of lysosomal genes [70, 71]. During lipid overload or oxidative stress, TFEB is dephosphorylated, translocates to the nucleus, and activates a program that promotes LD degradation [72, 73]. This response helps preserve the lipid balance and limits lipotoxicity, especially in metabolic disorders such as nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) [58, 74]. TFEB therefore acts downstream of nutrient sensing rather than as an isolated regulator, and the AMPK–mTOR–TFEB axis links the metabolic status to the lysosomal capacity and lipophagic lipid turnover.

Together, mTORC1, AMPK, Rab7, LD coat remodeling, and TFEB constitute an integrated signaling network that connects the energy status, lipid metabolism, and lysosomal degradation. The disruption of these axes, particularly in hepatocytes, promotes pathological lipid accumulation and contributes to NAFLD and steatosis. Defining these mechanisms may inform the development of therapeutic strategies that restore lipophagy in lipid-related metabolic disease.

Transcriptional regulation of lipophagy

Transcriptional regulation plays a critical role in lipophagy, adding a slower but essential layer of control compared with other cellular mechanisms [75]. Nuclear receptors, which are sensitive to the nutritional status, regulate the transition between fasting and feeding states, thereby influencing the initiation and progression of autophagy, including lipophagy [76, 77]. Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPARα), which is activated during fasting, promotes fatty acid oxidation and enhances autophagic activity under energy deficit conditions. This activation is crucial for maintaining lipid homeostasis because it facilitates the degradation of LDs through lipophagy [78–80]. In contrast, nuclear receptors such as liver X receptor (LXR) and farnesoid X receptor (FXR) are more active during feeding, where they regulate cholesterol metabolism and bile acid synthesis. However, these receptors also suppress autophagy, inhibit lipophagy and promote lipid storage [81].

PPARα agonists have been shown to stimulate autophagy-dependent LD clearance in hepatocytes, promoting lipid turnover [82]. In contrast, excessive activation of LXR or FXR signaling inhibits autophagic processes, leading to lipid accumulation and metabolic dysfunction. Additionally, SIRT1, an NAD⁺-dependent deacetylase, regulates the expression of autophagy-related genes by deacetylating FoxO transcription factors. This regulatory process links lipolysis and β-oxidation with autophagic activity, particularly during caloric restriction [83]. The SIRT1–FoxO pathway is frequently associated with increased lipophagy, facilitating efficient lipid degradation and preventing lipotoxicity during energy-limited conditions [84].

Recent research has expanded the network of transcriptional regulators involved in lipophagy. For instance, patatin-like phospholipase domain-containing protein 8 (PNPLA8) enhances the interaction between autophagosomes and LDs in cardiomyocytes, facilitating LD breakdown. The loss of PNPLA8 results in excessive cardiac LD accumulation, contributing to metabolic cardiomyopathy [85]. These findings underscore the complexity and dynamic regulation of lipophagy, highlighting a network of transcription factors and pathways that adapt cellular lipid metabolism to changing nutritional and metabolic environments.

Lysosomal competence and lipophagic flux

Lysosomal function is a key factor influencing lipophagic flux. Impairments in lysosomal acidification, membrane fusion, or hydrolase activity can significantly hinder lipid degradation and reduce lipid turnover [86, 87]. For example, the loss of LAMP2, an essential component of CMA, disrupts the CMA pathway that facilitates PLIN degradation. This disruption indirectly impairs lipophagy, as PLIN proteins are crucial for stabilizing and making LDs accessible during the autophagic process [88]. Additionally, vacuolar H⁺-ATPase (v-ATPase) is vital for maintaining lysosomal acidity, and changes in its activity can affect lysosomal function. These alterations in lysosomal pH impair autophagic degradation, including lipid breakdown, ultimately affecting lipophagic flux [89, 90].

Lysosomal nutrient sensors and transporters are also integral to lipid metabolism and lipophagic flux. For instance, the NPC1/2 cholesterol transporter and GLUT8 glucose transporter are involved in regulating cellular lipid and glucose homeostasis [91, 92]. These transporters help maintain lipid equilibrium, which is essential for efficient lipophagy. When these transporters malfunction, lysosomal function is disrupted, further hindering lipophagic flux and contributing to LD accumulation within the cell.

The coordination between lysosomal function, cytosolic signaling, and transcriptional regulation highlights the complexity of lipophagy regulation. A comprehensive understanding of how lysosomal activity and lipid transporters influence lipophagy is crucial for elucidating the pathways involved in lipid metabolism. Moreover, signaling pathways such as AMPK/mTOR and SIRT1, which modulate lysosomal activity, underscore the multifaceted nature of lipophagy regulation [86–92]. Mapping the interconnections between these components will provide a more integrated understanding of lipophagy and potential insights into treatments for lipid-related metabolic disorders.

Crosstalk between lipophagy and organelle contact sites

Lipophagy, the autophagic process responsible for degrading LDs, is closely integrated with the functions of other organelles, primarily through membrane contact sites that mediate lipid transport and signaling. These organelle interfaces play critical roles in regulating lipid metabolism and coordinating various cellular processes [93]. LDs are primarily formed in the ER, where they are closely associated with this organelle. This interaction facilitates efficient lipid synthesis and exchange between the ER and LDs, which is essential for maintaining cellular lipid homeostasis [94]. Additionally, LDs form extensive contacts with mitochondria, creating direct connections that enable the transfer of fatty acids. This crosstalk not only supports lipid mobilization but also couples it with energy production through mitochondrial β-oxidation [95]. Consequently, LDs are now recognized as key nodes in a broader interorganellar contact network, with their interactions with both the ER and mitochondria acting as critical hubs for lipid regulation [96].

Mitochondria-associated membranes (MAMs) at ER–mitochondria contact sites are particularly important for lipid metabolism. These sites are enriched in enzymes involved in cholesterol esterification and triglyceride synthesis, which channel newly synthesized lipids into adjacent LDs [97]. This spatial organization is vital for maintaining lipid homeostasis, as it prevents the accumulation of FFAs and cholesterol in the cytoplasm, thereby reducing lipotoxic stress. The proximity of mitochondria and LDs at these contact sites allows for efficient fatty acid transfer, particularly during periods of high energy demand. Mitochondria can directly access LD-derived fatty acids to fuel β-oxidation, contributing to cellular energy production [98].

Organelle contact sites have functions other than physically tethering compartments; they function as dynamic hubs for lipid transfer and coordinated metabolic signaling. Lipid exchange among LDs, the ER, and mitochondria is essential for cellular energy homeostasis. These interfaces also serve as key regulatory nodes where lipophagic flux is coupled to mitochondrial bioenergetics and the lysosomal degradative capacity, enabling cells to match lipid mobilization with nutritional and energetic demands. This integrated view highlights the complexity of lipid metabolism and autophagy and highlights the need to define how contact-site organization and signaling govern cellular homeostasis.

Interorganelle membrane dynamics in autophagy

Autophagy, especially lipophagy, requires coordinated interorganelle membrane dynamics. Organelle contacts maintain lipid homeostasis and link lipid turnover to nutrient and stress signals [99]. In canonical macroautophagy, the phagophore, the autophagosome precursor, is derived mainly from the ER. The phagophore is prominent at ER‒LD contact sites, where LDs form and lipid mobilization begins [100]. These contacts support lipid synthesis and exchange while the autophagic machinery is recruited for lipid degradation.

In hepatocytes, nutrient-sensitive mitochondria–LD contacts are key regulators of lipophagy. They provide membrane support and regulatory signals that couple lipid mobilization to mitochondrial β-oxidation and energy production [101]. Close mitochondria–LD coupling enables rapid fatty acid transfer from LDs to mitochondria, particularly when the energy demand is high, thereby preserving the lipid balance [101].

Lysosomes, the terminal degradative compartments of autophagy, also transiently contact LDs during “piecemeal” lipid degradation [102]. Rab7 drives this late endolysosomal phase. During nutrient deprivation, Rab7 is activated on LDs and lysosomal compartments and directs lysosomes and multivesicular bodies (MVBs) to the LD surface [59]. This targeting results in the formation of a lipophagic “synapse” that promotes LD degradation. In contrast, Rab7 depletion disrupts MVB, lysosome, and autophagosome organization and weakens hepatocellular lipophagy [59]. Thus, the spatial delivery of degradative compartments can limit LD clearance, especially in hepatocytes. Efficient lipophagy also requires a correctly positioned fusion machinery and active lysosomal acid hydrolases. The disruption of these steps reduces lipophagic flux, causing incomplete degradation and lipid accumulation [102]. Therefore, lysosomes are not only terminal degradative organelles but also active mediators of interorganelle communication.

Lipophagy functions within an integrated ER–mitochondria–lysosome network, highlighting the role of organelle crosstalk in lipid metabolism [103]. Contact site integrity is essential for lipid balance: stable contacts promote lipid transfer and degradation, whereas impaired contacts can cause pathological lipid accumulation, including steatosis and metabolic dysfunction.

Defining the molecular architecture of LD-associated contact sites and their stress-induced remodeling is essential for understanding how lipophagy controls cellular metabolism. These insights may clarify how organelle communication maintains homeostasis and contributes to metabolic diseases.

Lipophagy-mediated resistance to lipid-induced cell death

In addition to mobilizing lipids, lipophagy helps determine whether lipid overload is buffered adaptively or progresses to lipid-induced cell death. Efficient LD clearance sequesters or degrades toxic lipid species, including excess saturated FFAs, ceramide-related intermediates, oxidized lipids, and acylcarnitines, through lysosomal degradation and mitochondrial β-oxidation. This process maintains ATP production; limits ROS production, ER stress, and mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization; and reduces apoptosis, necroptosis/necrotic death, and ferroptosis-associated lipid peroxidation [6, 10, 18, 104]. In contrast, impaired flux during LD recognition, autophagosome formation, lysosomal acidification, or lipid hydrolysis causes LDs and lipotoxic intermediates to accumulate, converting LDs from lipid-buffering organelles into sources of death-promoting signals. Thus, defining the lipophagic steps that sustain lipid homeostasis under lipid overload is essential.

Mechanistically, this cytoprotective effect requires the coordinated control of LD accessibility, autophagic‒lysosomal capacity, and lipid routing between organelles. PLIN remodeling and CMA-mediated PLIN2/3 removal expose the LD surface to lipases and autophagic membranes, enabling lipid processing before mitochondrial or ER stress develops [24, 37, 44, 47]. The AMPK–mTORC1–TFEB axis links autophagosome formation to lysosomal biogenesis so that increased LD delivery is matched by the degradative capacity [58, 70–74]. LD–mitochondria and LD–lysosome contacts further connect lipid release to β-oxidation and lysosomal hydrolysis, limiting cytosolic FFA accumulation and prodeath signaling [93–101]. Therefore, lipophagy-dependent survival reflects the integrated actions of substrate access, autophagy signaling, lysosomal competence, and organelle communication.

Studies of specific cell types support this model. In hepatocytes, SIRT3 promotes lipophagy and CMA, protecting against lipotoxicity [51]. In palmitic acid-treated AML12 hepatocytes, γ-linolenic acid modulates the LKB1–AMPK–mTOR pathway to balance autophagy and apoptosis, restoring lipid handling to reduce lipid-induced apoptotic signaling [56]. In neuronal-like NGF-differentiated PC12 cells, docosahexaenoic acid (DHA; 22:6n-3) increases LC3-II levels, Beclin-1 phosphorylation, and ATG7/ATG12 expression, enhancing autophagy and protecting against palmitic acid-induced apoptosis and necroptosis; chloroquine weakens this protection [105]. Together, these findings show that effective lipophagic flux determines whether lipid overload is buffered or converted into regulated cell death signaling.

Overall, the efficiency and outcomes of lipophagy depend on substrate accessibility, regulatory signaling, and spatial organization. Because LDs have a phospholipid monolayer, they require specialized degradation routes. In mammalian cells, canonical macroautophagy can result in the engulfment of small LDs, whereas larger LDs are degraded piecemeal at LD–lysosome contacts. In yeast, LDs can also enter vacuoles through sterol-rich microdomains and ESCRT-dependent invagination, indicating that LD turnover can occur through autophagosomal engulfment or remodeling of the degradative membrane [25–30]. How LDs are labeled for degradation remains unclear because no universal LD-specific autophagy receptor has been identified. Rab10 and Rab7 partly define this process: Rab10 recruits LC3-positive autophagic membranes through the EHBP1–EHD2 complex, whereas Rab7 delivers late endolysosomal compartments, including lysosomes and MVBs, to LDs for terminal degradation [34, 59]. PLINs, especially PLIN2/3, shield LDs. Their LAMP2A-dependent CMA removal facilitates macroautophagy, supporting a sequential model in which PLIN remodeling precedes LD engulfment. This remodeling involves AMPK-dependent PLIN2 phosphorylation, starvation-induced PLIN3 phosphorylation or mTORC1–PLIN3 signaling in hepatocytes, and proteasome- or CMA-dependent turnover of LD coat proteins.

The AMPK–mTORC1–TFEB axis links the nutrient and energy status to autophagy initiation and lysosome biogenesis. Lipid degradation also requires lysosomal competence, including LAMP2- and v-ATPase-regulated acidification, fusion, and enzyme activity. ER–LD, mitochondria–LD, and LD–lysosome contacts further coordinate lipid transfer and metabolic signaling. Together, these layers determine whether lipophagy supports cell survival by limiting lipid-induced apoptosis, necroptosis/necrotic death, and lipid peroxidation injury. Thus, lipophagy is a coordinated multiorganellar metabolic turnover system (Fig. 2).

Pathophysiological role of lipophagy

Dual roles of lipophagy in liver diseases

The liver coordinates hepatic metabolism and autophagy and is therefore essential for lipid homeostasis. Lipophagy, a selective autophagy pathway that degrades LDs, regulates the cellular lipid content and limits lipotoxicity [7]. In MASLD, which was formerly designated NAFLD, autophagy-mediated lipid degradation reduces hepatocellular steatosis [52]. By removing excess triglycerides and supplying fatty acids for β-oxidation, lipophagy decreases lipotoxicity and may improve insulin sensitivity [78]. Thus, lipophagy is protective when it restores lipid balance, but its effects become stage- and context-dependent as liver injury progresses.

During early MASLD, which typically presents as simple steatosis, the liver compensates for lipid overload by upregulating macroautophagy and CMA. These pathways increase LD turnover, preserve liver function, and limit injury [106]. With progression to steatohepatitis (NASH), macroautophagic flux usually decreases, whereas CMA may become overactive but insufficient to clear accumulated LDs [44]. This “rise-then-fall” pattern reflects protective exhaustion: autophagy initially buffers excess lipids, but persistent metabolic stress and impaired flux promote lipid accumulation, inflammation, and disease progression [107].

Lipophagic exhaustion also affects hepatocyte survival. In early steatosis, adaptive lipophagy reduces the intracellular levels of saturated FFAs and other lipotoxic intermediates, thereby limiting mitochondrial ROS production, ER stress, and apoptotic or necroinflammatory signaling. SIRT3-mediated activation of lipophagy and CMA protects hepatocytes from lipotoxicity. Similarly, restoring autophagy in palmitic acid-treated AML12 cells rebalances autophagy and apoptosis through the LKB1–AMPK–mTOR pathway [51, 56]. Thus, in MASLD/NASH, lipophagy functions both as a lipid clearance pathway and as a survival mechanism that delays the progression from reversible steatosis to inflammatory, cell death-associated injury.

Environmental stress further shapes this balance. High-fat diets disrupt the autophagy–lysosome system, impair LD degradation, and promote oxidative stress and inflammation, thereby driving the transition from simple steatosis to NASH [104, 108]. However, excessive lipophagy may also be harmful. FFAs released from LDs can activate hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) and promote fibrosis [109]. These opposing effects highlight the need for tight control: in MASLD, lipophagy should increase lipid clearance without triggering fibrosis or excessive inflammation.

In alcohol-related liver disease (ALD), lipophagy is generally protective because ethanol and its metabolites promote lipid accumulation and mitochondrial dysfunction [110]. Autophagy mitigates ethanol-induced injury by removing damaged mitochondria and degrading LDs, thereby reducing cytotoxicity and supporting liver recovery [111]. In animals, autophagy-stimulating compounds such as dihydromyricetin improve liver histology and ameliorate oxidative stress by increasing the clearance of alcohol-induced LDs [112]. However, chronic alcohol exposure disrupts autophagy, increases LD accumulation and oxidative stress, exacerbates liver injury, and accelerates the progression to cirrhosis [73]. This pattern resembles MASLD: an initially protective pathway becomes insufficient under persistent stress. The coordinated removal of LDs and damaged mitochondria may limit ROS amplification, ATP depletion, and death receptor or mitochondrial apoptotic signaling during ethanol exposure [73, 110–112]. When chronic alcohol exposure impairs TFEB-dependent lysosomal biogenesis or autophagosomal–lysosomal flux, unresolved LD accumulation and mitochondrial dysfunction may amplify hepatocyte apoptosis, necroinflammation, and fibrogenic remodeling.

In viral hepatitis, especially hepatitis C, autophagy has a more complex relationship with lipid metabolism. Hepatitis C virus (HCV) uses the LD surface for virion assembly, a process supported by the host autophagic machinery [113]. Autophagy can facilitate viral replication by supplying energy and resources, but it can also restrict infection by degrading viral components [114]. Therefore, in viral hepatitis, lipophagy-related pathways reflect a host–pathogen balance rather than only host lipid clearance; their effects depend on the infection stage and cellular context.

Overall, lipophagy is critical in chronic liver diseases because it maintains the lipid and energy balance while limiting cellular injury and inflammation. Both insufficient and excessive lipophagy can accelerate disease progression, indicating that precise regulation is essential (Fig. 3). MASLD exhibits stage-dependent changes, ALD is characterized by toxic metabolic stress, and viral hepatitis is characterized by host–pathogen interactions. Understanding how lipophagy responds to disease stage, stress, nutrient availability, the diet, and alcohol exposure may identify useful biomarkers and therapeutic targets. The modulation of pathways such as AMPK/mTOR signaling could improve lipid homeostasis in the liver [78, 108]. Future therapies should not simply increase or suppress lipophagy; instead, they should restore its appropriate intensity, timing, and cellular context while avoiding fibrosis or excessive inflammation.

Lipophagy in liver diseases. Lipophagy helps maintain hepatic lipid homeostasis by degrading lipid droplets and supplying fatty acids for β-oxidation. In early MASLD, adaptive lipophagy reduces lipid accumulation, oxidative stress, inflammation, and hepatocyte injury. During NASH progression or chronic alcohol exposure, impaired autophagic flux causes lipid droplet accumulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and liver damage. In viral hepatitis, especially HCV infection, autophagy-related lipid droplet remodeling may support viral assembly or promote viral component degradation. Excessive lipophagy can also release FFAs that activate hepatic stellate cells and drive fibrosis, highlighting the need for precise regulation rather than simple activation or inhibition

Systemic metabolic disorders and cardiovascular disease (CVD)

Systemic metabolic disorders, including obesity and T2DM, are closely linked to impaired lipophagy, a selective autophagy pathway that degrades LDs [103]. Because disrupted lipid metabolism drives obesity, diabetes, and CVD, lipophagy is central to cardiometabolic homeostasis. It maintains the lipid balance by mobilizing cellular lipids and limiting the accumulation of harmful lipids. In obesity, adipose tissue expands, and LDs are enlarged, promoting triglyceride storage, chronic inflammation, insulin resistance, and an increased cardiovascular risk [115]. Adipose tissue from obese individuals also has increased levels of LD-associated proteins, including PLIN2 and PLIN5. Although these proteins regulate LD dynamics, reduced lipophagy limits lipid mobilization, aggravates lipotoxicity and metabolic dysfunction, and increases the risk of metabolic syndrome [116].

LD heterogeneity further explains this process. Cardiometabolic disease should be viewed not only as excess lipid storage but also as the remodeling of the LD size and composition. In hypertrophic adipocytes, lipid-loaded cardiomyocytes, and macrophage foam cells, large PLIN-stabilized or cholesterol ester-rich LDs may be poorly engulfed by autophagosomes. Cells may therefore rely more on cytosolic lipases and lysosomal lipid-processing pathways to generate smaller, lipophagy-competent LD pools. When lipolysis, PLIN remodeling, or lysosomal function is impaired, this transition from large LD remodeling to lipophagy of small LDs weakens. Persistent lipid storage subsequently promotes lipotoxic signaling, inflammation, insulin resistance, foam cell formation, and cardiovascular dysfunction [22, 115–123]. Conversely, small, nascent or lipolysis-shortened LDs may provide a readily degradable pool that supports lipid mobilization during metabolic stress without excessive free fatty acid release [6, 22].

Consistent with this model, preclinical studies have shown that enhancing lipophagy in obese mice improves insulin sensitivity and lipid handling, potentially reducing the risk of metabolic syndrome and cardiovascular complications [78]. In adipocytes, SIRT1, an NAD+-dependent deacetylase, is a key lipophagy regulator. SIRT1 promotes LD lipid breakdown and energy mobilization, whereas SIRT1 deficiency causes abnormal LD accumulation, reduced energy expenditure, and metabolic dysregulation [124]. These findings highlight the role of lipophagy in adipose tissue in the energy balance, lipid turnover, systemic metabolism, and cardiovascular risk.

In addition to white adipose tissue (WAT), brown adipose tissue (BAT) thermogenesis is supported by lipophagy. BAT is specialized for heat production, and lipophagy in brown adipocytes promotes the use of LDs for thermogenesis. This process increases energy expenditure and helps limit weight gain [125]. Enhanced BAT lipophagy therefore supports metabolic homeostasis and may reduce obesity-related cardiovascular dysfunction.

Dysregulated lipophagy also contributes to diabetes-related organ injury, especially individuals with diabetic cardiomyopathy (DCM) and diabetic kidney disease (DKD) [126]. In individuals with DCM, cardiomyocytes accumulate excess LDs, impairing cellular and cardiac function [127]. High-fat and high-glucose conditions disrupt autophagy, and the expression of lipophagy regulators such as PNPLA8 is insufficient. This process impairs fatty acid use and increases lipotoxicity [85]. Conversely, autophagy activation reduces the cardiac LD burden, improves mitochondrial function, and decreases hypertrophy and fibrosis [126]. These findings suggest that lipophagy protects the heart of diabetic patients and that the activation of autophagy may ameliorate DCM. Its benefits extend beyond reducing the number of LDs: by directing LD-derived fatty acids toward controlled mitochondrial oxidation and limiting the levels of toxic lipid intermediates, lipophagy may reduce cardiomyocyte apoptosis, necroptosis-related injury, oxidative stress, and contractile dysfunction. This view is supported by evidence that autophagy-promoting interventions, including those involving curcumin and related compounds, alleviate DCM while reducing apoptosis and oxidative stress markers [85–129].

A similar lipid clearance mechanism occurs in DKD. Excess LD accumulation in renal cells and podocytes is associated with reduced autophagy, which promotes cholesterol and triglyceride accumulation, glomerulosclerosis, and inflammation [130]. Pharmacological or genetic restoration of autophagy reduces renal lipid accumulation and improves kidney function [130]. Because podocytes and tubular epithelial cells are sensitive to lipotoxic and oxidative injury, restoring lipophagic flux may also protect against apoptosis-related barrier dysfunction and inflammation. Thus, in DKD, lipophagy links lipid clearance to cell survival rather than simply reducing renal lipid deposition [130, 131]. Together, the cardiac and renal findings support lipophagy as a potential target for treating diabetic complications.

Lipophagy also influences AS, a major cause of CVD [118]. In AS, arterial macrophages take up oxidized low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL) and become foam cells. These cells accumulate cholesterol ester-rich LDs, promoting plaque formation [55]. Normally, lysosomal acid lipase within the autophagy‒lysosome system hydrolyzes cholesterol esters into free cholesterol. ATP-binding cassette transporter A1 (ABCA1) then exports this cholesterol, promoting efflux and limiting plaque formation [119]. Nuclear receptors such as LXR increase ABCA1 expression, while autophagy supplies substrates for cholesterol efflux [120].

When lipophagy fails, for example, because of lysosomal protein or hydrolase defects, cholesterol esters accumulate in macrophages, and cholesterol efflux decreases. This process impairs reverse cholesterol transport and accelerates plaque development [121]. Preclinical studies suggest that enhancing macrophage autophagy reduces lipid accumulation in foam cells and stabilizes plaques, potentially slowing AS progression [122]. This effect may partly reflect the improved resistance of lipid-loaded macrophages to dysfunction and death. Efficient lysosomal cholesterol ester hydrolysis supports ABCA1-mediated efflux, whereas defective lipophagy promotes cholesterol ester retention, lysosomal stress, inflammatory activation, and the activation of cell death pathways that contribute to necrotic core formation [119–123]. In individuals with diabetes-related AS, hyperglycemia and hyperinsulinemia often suppress macrophage autophagy, increasing plaque formation and worsening cardiovascular outcomes [123]. The transcription factor CREBH also regulates the systemic lipid distribution; CREBH deficiency exacerbates AS under high-fat or diabetic conditions by disrupting lipid metabolism and autophagy regulation [132].

Overall, lipophagy regulates lipid levels, inflammation, and lipid-induced toxicity across adipose tissue, the diabetic heart and kidney, and atherosclerotic vessels (Fig. 4). A clearer understanding of how lipophagy controls lipid metabolism and inflammation in metabolic diseases may lead to the identification of new therapeutic targets and strategies.

Lipophagy in metabolic disorders and cardiovascular disease. Enhanced lipophagy promotes lipid droplet clearance in adipocytes, macrophages, cardiomyocytes, and podocytes, thereby improving lipid metabolism, reducing inflammation, and protecting against obesity, atherosclerosis, and diabetic cardiac or renal injury. In contrast, impaired autophagy/lipophagy causes lipid droplet accumulation, lipotoxicity, cellular dysfunction, and cardiovascular damage

Lipophagy in cancer and NDDs

Lipophagy is a selective autophagy pathway that degrades LDs and regulates lipid homeostasis in cancer, NDDs, and infections [133]. In cancer, it helps tumor cells adapt to nutrient limitation, oxidative stress, and hypoxia. Tumor cells store LDs during metabolic reprogramming and use them as sources of energy and membrane lipids [9]. In response to nutrient or oxygen deprivation, moderate lipophagy releases fatty acids for mitochondrial beta-oxidation, sustaining ATP production [54]. It also decreases lipotoxicity by removing excess lipids and reducing lipid peroxidation and oxidative damage [134]. Thus, lipophagy supports tumor survival, especially in rapidly proliferating cancers exposed to metabolic stress.

Preclinical models of pancreatic and hepatocellular carcinoma have shown that the inhibition of autophagy causes lipid overload and apoptosis, indicating that lipophagy functions as both an energy reserve and a lipid quality control mechanism [135]. In cancer, lipophagy-mediated resistance to cell death can therefore represent maladaptive stress tolerance. By supplying fatty acids for beta-oxidation and clearing damaged lipid species, lipophagy increases tumor cell fitness during nutrient deprivation, hypoxia, and therapy. These findings support the combination of autophagy inhibitors with lipid metabolism-targeted therapies to treat tumors dependent on LD turnover [135–137]. However, in lipid-dependent tumors such as prostate and breast cancers, excessive lipophagy may further promote growth and metastasis by continuously providing fatty acids and membrane components [138, 139]. This “double-edged sword” role shows that lipophagy can protect tumor cells under stress while also driving progression [140]. Precise, context-specific regulation is therefore essential when targeting lipophagy in cancer (Fig. 5).

Context-dependent role of lipophagy in cancer. Lipophagy enables tumor cells to degrade lipid droplets and release fatty acids for mitochondrial β-oxidation, helping maintain energy supply, reduce lipid stress, and survive nutrient deprivation, hypoxia, and therapy. When lipophagy is inhibited, lipid droplets accumulate, causing lipid overload, oxidative damage, and apoptosis. However, in lipid-dependent tumors, excessive lipophagy may continuously provide fatty acids and membrane components that support tumor growth and metastasis

Recent preclinical studies support the combination of inhibitors targeting autophagy and lipid metabolism as an anticancer strategy [136]. These combinations may restrict tumor metabolic flexibility and increase sensitivity to chemotherapy and radiotherapy. In treatment-resistant cancers, blocking lipophagy could reduce the adaptation to metabolic stress and improve responses to standard therapy [137]. However, further mechanistic and clinical studies are needed to define tumor-specific lipophagy dependence and avoid injury to normal tissues.

In addition to its role in cancer, lipophagy helps maintain the neuronal and glial lipid balance. Neurons and glia form LDs during fatty acid overload or toxic lipid accumulation, and lipophagy removes these LDs to limit lipotoxicity [126, 141]. Disrupted lipid metabolism and autophagy are associated with Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease (PD), and other NDDs [142, 143]. In AD, neuronal cholesterol and fatty acid accumulation, together with autophagosome accumulation, suggest impaired LD clearance and autophagic dysfunction [144]. In PD, dopaminergic neurons accumulate LDs and develop mitochondrial dysfunction, partly because autophagy–lysosomal efficiency is reduced [145]. Inefficient lipid turnover promotes toxic metabolite accumulation and neurodegeneration.

Evidence from a cell model supports a protective role for lipid-targeted autophagy. In NGF-differentiated PC12 cells exposed to palmitic acid, DHA (22:6n-3) enhanced autophagy, increased ATG7 and ATG12 expression, promoted LC3-II accumulation and Beclin-1 phosphorylation, and reduced apoptosis and necroptosis. Chloroquine weakened this protective effect, indicating that intact autophagic flux is required for DHA-mediated resistance to lipid-induced death [105]. This model links lipophagy to neuronal survival under conditions of lipid overload, mitochondrial dysfunction, and impaired autophagic‒lysosomal activity.

Increasing lipophagy in neurons and glia could reduce lipid accumulation, neuroinflammation, and disease progression. In AD, increased lipophagic activity may clear toxic lipids and limit inflammatory responses [144]. In PD, it may improve lipid homeostasis and mitochondrial function, potentially slowing dopaminergic neurodegeneration [145]. The ER–mitochondria–LD network also supports synaptic integrity and membrane turnover, which are disrupted in many NDDs [146]. Restoring lipophagy and organelle contact sites may therefore help delay NDD progression.

The ability of metabolic modulators such as AMPK activators and SIRT1 agonists to restore autophagy and protect neurons from lipotoxicity is being studied [147, 148]. However, lipophagy requires tight control. Excessive autophagy may disrupt neuronal membranes, impair neurotransmission, and aggravate disease [149]. Defining stage-specific changes in lipophagy is necessary to design therapies that improve neuronal function without causing harm (Fig. 6).

Lipophagy protects neurons from lipid-induced injury. Impaired autophagy–lysosomal function causes lipid droplet accumulation in neurons and glia, leading to lipotoxicity, mitochondrial dysfunction, neuroinflammation, and neuronal damage. Functional lipophagy promotes lipid droplet degradation through autophagosomes and lysosomes, helping restore lipid balance and support mitochondrial function. Enhancing lipophagy through DHA, AMPK, or SIRT1-related pathways may reduce lipid-induced cell death and slow neurodegenerative progression, but excessive activation should be avoided to prevent neuronal dysfunction

Lipophagy also contributes to host responses during infection. Viruses such as Zika and dengue manipulate host lipid metabolism and autophagy to support replication [150, 151]. During Zika infection, LDs can serve as replication platforms, while autophagy helps meet the viral lipid and energy demands [150]. Dengue infection also alters lipid accumulation and autophagy, reshaping immune responses and complicating host defenses [151]. These examples show that lipophagy is relevant not only to cancer and NDDs but also to infection biology.

Overall, lipophagy has disease- and stage-specific effects. In metabolic disorders such as obesity and T2DM, it supports lipid homeostasis and limits lipotoxicity. In lipid-dependent tumors, excessive lipophagy can promote growth and metastasis, whereas in NDDs, properly enhanced lipophagy may reduce lipid accumulation and slow degeneration. Dysregulated lipophagy, however, can damage neuronal membranes or impair neurotransmission. Targeted modulation should therefore enhance beneficial effects while limiting disease-promoting activity. Because pathogens can also exploit lipophagy for replication, this pathway remains a promising but complex therapeutic target that requires careful, context-aware control.

Intervention strategies targeting lipophagy

Lipophagy supports lipid homeostasis and contributes to disease, including obesity, CVD, cancer, and NDD, when it is dysregulated. Its central role in the cellular lipid balance has prompted the development of strategies designed to enhance or normalize lipophagy.

Lifestyle interventions and behavioral therapy

Lifestyle interventions, especially caloric restriction and exercise, improve metabolic health partly by activating autophagy and lipophagy [152–154]. Moderate caloric restriction increases NAD+ levels, activates SIRT1 and AMPK, inhibits mTORC1, and enhances autophagic flux, lipophagy, and mitochondrial function [152]. In animal and human studies, it improves lipid metabolism, promotes the expression of autophagy-related markers, and reduces hepatic steatosis, particularly in individuals with obesity [153, 154]. Resveratrol, a longevity-associated polyphenol, mimics some effects of caloric restriction by activating SIRT1. In animal models, it stimulates autophagy, enhances lipophagy, decreases triglyceride levels and inflammation, and reduces lipid accumulation in metabolic disease [155, 156].

Exercise also increases lipophagy and lipid turnover. By activating AMPK in muscle and liver, it induces autophagy and promotes fatty acid use through lipophagy [157, 158]. Aerobic exercise may improve hepatic lipophagy in individuals with MASLD by regulating systemic metabolism and inflammation [158, 159]. It also redistributes lipids among muscle, liver, and adipose tissue, improving lipid use and limiting ectopic fat deposition [160].

Combining exercise with dietary control, including caloric restriction or intermittent fasting, may improve lipid metabolism in individuals with obesity, T2DM, and fatty liver disease. Future studies should define optimal exercise formats, such as high-intensity interval training (HIIT) versus moderate-intensity endurance training, and test whether combining exercise with fasting-based dietary patterns sustains autophagic activity and long-term metabolic benefits.

Pharmacological and small-molecule interventions

Pharmacological strategies targeting mTOR, AMPK, and TFEB may modulate lipophagy and improve lipid metabolism [161]. mTORC1 is a central regulator of cell growth, metabolism, and lipophagy. Rapamycin induces autophagy by inhibiting mTORC1. In animal models, it increases autophagic flux in hepatocytes and macrophages and reduces AS and hepatic steatosis [162]. In obese mice, it decreases the hepatic lipid content and improves glucose tolerance. However, its long-term use can cause immunosuppression and other adverse effects, necessitating a careful evaluation of the risks and benefits [163].

Trehalose promotes the nuclear translocation of TFEB, enhancing lipophagy and reducing hepatic steatosis [164]. It stimulates autophagy partly by inhibiting glucose transport, thereby increasing lipid degradation. Its limited specificity and potential systemic metabolic effects restrict its clinical use [165]. More targeted approaches, including the modulation of GSK3β-mediated TFEB phosphorylation or stabilization of TFEB, aim to promote lipid droplet degradation without excessive autophagy [165, 166].

Nuclear receptor ligands are promising lipophagy modulators. PPARα agonists such as fenofibrate, which are used to treat hypertriglyceridemia, promote fatty acid oxidation and autophagy-related gene expression [167, 168]. These effects may reduce hepatic steatosis by improving lipophagy and lipid turnover. THR-β-selective agonists such as resmetirom have shown efficacy in phase III trials for NASH by increasing mitochondrial β-oxidation and improving autophagic‒lysosomal function, thereby reducing hepatic lipid accumulation [169–172]. Thus, thyroid hormone signaling may coordinate metabolic and autophagic benefits in patients with NASH.

In cardiometabolic disease, widely used drugs such as metformin and statins may also affect lipophagy [55, 122, 173]. Metformin activates AMPK and induces autophagy, which is associated with a reduced atherosclerotic plaque burden and a reduced lipid content in foam cells [122]. Statins lower cholesterol levels and may increase macrophage autophagy and cholesterol efflux [55]. These observations suggest that established metabolic drugs may provide additional benefits through autophagy and lipid homeostasis pathways, warranting further mechanistic and clinical validation.

Regulation by natural products and traditional Chinese medicine

Natural products from foods and medicinal herbs are being increasingly studied as autophagy modulators and adjunct metabolic therapies [174]. Polyphenols such as resveratrol, dioscin, and nobiletin induce autophagy and reduce hepatic lipid accumulation in NAFLD models [175, 176]. Resveratrol activates SIRT1 and inhibits mTOR, enhancing hepatic lipophagy and lowering triglyceride levels and inflammation [177, 178]. These data support the use of natural compounds as potential modulators of lipophagy-related metabolic dysfunction.

Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM)-derived compounds may also regulate lipophagy. Gypenosides from Gynostemma pentaphyllum and polysaccharides from Lycium barbarum reduce high-fat-diet-induced steatosis in animal models, likely by modulating autophagy [179]. In alcohol-induced liver injury, quercetin, resveratrol, and ginsenoside Rg1 reduce oxidative stress and lipid accumulation, with autophagy activation contributing to hepatoprotection [180–183].

Natural compounds may also protect the heart and kidneys [184, 185]. Curcumin increases the expression of autophagy-related proteins such as LC3 and Beclin-1, reduces LD accumulation in the myocardium and kidneys of diabetic mice, and improves organ function [128, 129, 131]. Dihydromyricetin restores autophagic clearance and improves cardiac function in individuals with alcoholic cardiomyopathy [186]. Tea polyphenols and omega-3 fatty acids have also been linked to improved autophagy and may contribute to the benefits of Mediterranean-style diets [187–189]. In NGF-differentiated PC12 cells, DHA increased ATG7 and ATG12 levels and reduced palmitic acid-induced apoptosis and necroptosis in an autophagy-dependent manner [105].

TCM multiherb formulations may provide multitarget regulation of metabolic dysregulation. Erchen decoction and Yinchenhao decoction reduce steatosis and improve liver function in NAFLD and ALD animal models [190, 191], possibly by modulating autophagic‒lysosomal function and SIRT1/AMPK signaling [192]. Future studies should clarify their active components, network pharmacology, and tissue-specific effects on lipophagy. Integrating TCM with molecular biology may expand treatment options for metabolic syndrome and related diseases.

Overall, targeting lipophagy is a promising strategy for diseases involving lipid dysregulation, including metabolic disorders, cancer, and NDDs. Lifestyle interventions, pharmacological agents, natural products, and TCM can enhance lipophagy, improve lipid turnover, and restore lipid homeostasis. Further studies should define the molecular mechanisms, tissue specificity, safety, and clinical efficacy of these approaches to support the development of targeted therapies for lipophagy-related disease (Table 1).

Strategies targeting lipophagy in lipid-driven diseases

Discussion and future directions

Unresolved mechanistic questions

Lipophagy is increasingly recognized as a major pathway for LD turnover, yet its molecular definition remains incomplete, limiting the interpretation of its mechanisms and translational use [5, 6]. A central gap is the lack of a dedicated receptor or robust marker that distinguishes LD-selective autophagy from bulk macroautophagy, making bona fide lipophagic flux difficult to quantify across cell types and disease states [196]. The atypical LD surface—a phospholipid monolayer surrounding a hydrophobic neutral lipid core—also creates cargo capture constraints that are distinct from those of protein-rich substrates [19–21]. Thus, common readouts such as LD abundance, LC3 puncta, or lysosomal inhibition may be confounded by altered lipogenesis, cytosolic lipolysis, or lysosomal competence [31–33]. A receptor-based framework would enable direct tests of how LD surface remodeling recruits LC3 and whether receptor engagement prioritizes LDs during nutrient stress [196].

A leading model proposes that an unidentified receptor senses triglyceride-rich or PLIN-coated LD surfaces, recruits LC3-associated machinery at the LD boundary, and promotes phagophore growth around the cargo [196]. This idea is consistent with evidence that the LD size and coat composition shape the degradation mode, with small LDs being fully enclosed and larger LDs undergoing repeated, piecemeal consumption [20–22, 101]. Receptor discovery efforts should therefore be stratified by the LD size because the lipophagy of large LDs may rely more strongly on repeated tethering and partial sequestration, possibly through distinct adaptors or scaffolds [22, 101]. Whether one receptor family can operate across tissues with divergent LD proteomes or whether recognition is context specific, as in hepatocytes versus macrophage foam cells [5, 6], remains unclear.

Resolving these questions will require integrated proteomic and imaging workflows that capture transient LD–phagophore contacts in situ [196]. Spatial LD proteomics under defined nutrient states could identify stress-induced surface factors linked to LC3 recruitment, whereas quantitative phosphoproteomic analyses may reveal signaling-dependent remodeling that controls LD accessibility [53, 82, 84, 197]. Correlative light‒electron microscopy and cryo-electron microscopy analyses could clarify whether phagophores initiate at ER‒LD junctions, extend along the LD monolayer, or arrive as preformed membranes that tether to LDs [99, 100]. Multiplexed reporters that simultaneously track the LD content, LC3 lipidation, and lysosomal delivery would further separate increased autophagosome formation from impaired maturation or fusion [31–33].

A second uncertainty concerns the origin and remodeling of lipophagic membranes [100]. Canonical macroautophagy depends on LC3/ATG8-mediated membrane expansion and closure, but how this machinery accommodates LD curvature and LD-specific biophysics, especially in large LDs, remains poorly defined [19–22, 31, 101]. The ER is a plausible phagophore source because LDs originate from the ER and maintain ER‒LD contacts that support lipid exchange and signaling [94, 100]. However, whether these contact sites are privileged initiation zones for lipophagy and how nutrient or stress signals regulate them remain unknown [93, 94].

Ogasawara and colleagues further proposed that LDs can be both autophagy substrates and products of autophagic remodeling, suggesting a bidirectional lipid redistribution rather than a purely degradative process [197]. This model is consistent with evidence that stress-induced autophagy reshapes membranes and lipid pools, potentially generating LDs to buffer released fatty acids and prevent lipotoxicity when mitochondrial oxidation is insufficient [134, 197]. If LD biogenesis and consumption occur in parallel, lipophagy assays must account for simultaneous LD formation that may offset lysosomal degradation [197]. Time-resolved lipidomics could be used to map fatty acid movement among LDs, membranes, and mitochondria during autophagy induction [197].

A third issue is how lysosomal competence affects apparent selectivity [86, 87]. Even when LD recognition and sequestration are intact, defects in acidification, hydrolase activity, or membrane fusion can block terminal degradation and mimic impaired lipophagy initiation [86–90]. For example, the loss of LAMP2 disrupts CMA, impairs PLIN turnover, and indirectly reduces LD accessibility for downstream catabolism [88]. Similarly, altered v-ATPase activity increases the lysosomal pH, weakens lipid hydrolysis, and reduces the effective lipophagic output despite ongoing autophagosome formation [89, 90]. Distinguishing initiation defects from degradative bottlenecks therefore requires parallel measurements of autophagosome biogenesis, lysosomal function, and lipid hydrolysis within the same system [86, 87].

Optimizing signaling pathways and therapeutic targets

Nutrient-sensing networks such as AMPK–mTOR, SIRT1–FOXO, and nuclear receptor (PPARα versus LXR/FXR) pathways link the metabolic state to lipid mobilization, but pharmacological manipulation of these pathways is often pleiotropic [53, 82, 84]. In metabolic tissues, AMPK activation and mTORC1 inhibition can increase autophagic flux and LD turnover, yet they also affect protein synthesis, mitochondrial biogenesis, and the systemic energy balance, complicating dose selection and long-term safety [53, 82, 152–154]. Future strategies should therefore shift from broad upstream modulation toward downstream nodes that control LD accessibility, autophagosome maturation, or lysosomal lipid degradation [53, 82, 161].

mTOR inhibition highlights the trade-off between efficacy and tolerability [198]. Rapamycin and related mTORC1 inhibitors increase autophagy in hepatocytes and macrophages and ameliorate steatosis or AS phenotypes in animal models, but chronic systemic inhibition can cause immunosuppression and metabolic disruption [162, 163, 198]. These limitations support partial, intermittent, or tissue-restricted modulation instead of sustained systemic suppression [198]. They also reinforce the need to identify lipophagy-specific effectors, including regulators of LD–phagophore engagement and lysosomal lipid hydrolysis, as more selective targets [53, 82, 196].

TFEB is an attractive therapeutic hub because it coordinates autophagy execution with lysosomal biogenesis, directly addressing the reduced autophagic‒lysosomal capacity that is common in chronic nutrient stress [70–74, 80]. Enhancing TFEB nuclear translocation or transcriptional activity could increase both degradative compartments and hydrolase delivery for lipid recycling [164–166]. Lysosomal calcium signaling, including MCOLN1 agonists that engage the TFEB–TRPML1 axis, may stimulate lysosomal adaptation more specifically than global mTOR blockade [193]. A key developmental challenge is achieving adequate lysosomal exposure in target organs such as liver, adipose tissue, or macrophage-rich plaques while minimizing effects on immune and neuronal compartments [193].

Another strategy is to target LD “gatekeepers” that restrict access to neutral lipids [24]. PLIN proteins, especially PLIN2, stabilize LDs and limit the entry of lipases and the autophagic machinery; reducing PLIN2 expression may therefore lower the barrier to LD clearance [24]. In animal models, PLIN2 antisense oligonucleotides reduce hepatosteatosis and improve disease features, supporting coat remodeling as a route to enhance LD degradation [194, 195]. Small-molecule degraders, including PROTACs against PLIN2 or PLIN3, could provide tunable and reversible control of the LD coat composition, particularly when paired with moderate autophagy induction, to favor lysosomal processing over uncontrolled cytosolic lipolysis [24, 194, 195].

Therapeutic precision may also be improved by targeting rate-limiting steps in autophagosome biogenesis and maturation [31–33]. Because LC3 lipidation and phagophore closure are required for the lysosomal delivery of LD-derived materials, ATG7- or ATG16L1-dependent steps could influence the lipophagic capacity, although LD selectivity remains difficult to achieve [32, 33]. Receptor discovery and pathway mapping may reveal nodes that are activated only by LD-bound receptor engagement, thereby limiting systemic effects [196]. Engineered or drug-inducible receptor-like modules that recruit LC3 to LDs could also test whether selective, controllable lipophagy is therapeutically feasible [196].

Disease context and personalized approaches

In different tissues, lipophagy is beneficial when it restores lipid homeostasis but harmful when it strengthens inflammatory, fibrogenic, or tumor-supportive programs [16, 17]. In MASLD, the balance between protective lipid clearance and autophagic‒lysosomal failure appears to be stage dependent: early steatosis may result in compensatory upregulation, whereas advanced disease is characterized by impaired flux as lysosomal competence declines [44, 106–108]. This pattern suggests that patients require a stage-specific assessment of the dominant bottleneck, such as defective initiation or impaired lysosomal degradation, before autophagy is targeted [86, 87, 107]. In early disease, an increase in autophagy may reduce the triglyceride load, improve insulin sensitivity, and lower lipotoxic inflammatory signals [52, 78]. In advanced fibrosis, the same approach may fail if lysosomal dysfunction is entrenched or may exacerbate hepatocyte stress by increasing catabolic pressure [199].

Fibrogenesis explains why lipid clearance does not always translate into a clinical benefit [109]. By mobilizing fatty acids, lipophagy can increase cytosolic free fatty acid availability; in some contexts, these species promote hepatic stellate cell activation or inflammatory signaling that accelerates extracellular matrix remodeling [109]. An increase in lipophagy may therefore need to be paired with a sufficient oxidative capacity and anti-inflammatory control so that the released fatty acids are consumed rather than converted into profibrotic signaling lipids [78, 109]. More broadly, the antifibrotic efficacy will likely depend on coordinating lipid handling with immune regulation and tissue repair [199].

Cancer exhibits the strongest context dependence because lipophagy can sustain survival during nutrient deprivation but can create vulnerabilities in tumors that are dependent on lipid substrates [133–135, 138–140]. In pancreatic and hepatocellular carcinoma models, autophagy inhibition can cause lipid overload and apoptosis, indicating that lipophagy functions as both an energy reserve and a lipid quality control pathway under stress [135]. Conversely, prostate and breast cancers may exploit sustained lipophagy to supply fatty acids and membrane components for growth and metastasis [138, 139]. These opposing effects argue against uniform treatment strategies and favor patient selection based on the tumor lineage, metabolic phenotype, and microenvironmental stress [133–135, 138–140]. Combining autophagy inhibitors with lipid metabolism inhibitors may be an especially useful treatment for tumors with high metabolic plasticity [136].

The timing and dosing are also critical when treating NDDs. Enhancing autophagic‒lysosomal function may reduce lipid accumulation and cellular vulnerability, whereas excessive autophagy can damage neuronal membranes and impair neurotransmission [149]. Because neurons are long lived and depend on membrane integrity, interventions should be modest, sustained, and carefully titrated to improve clearance without causing structural loss [147–149]. Biomarkers should therefore report not only autophagy induction but also net degradative competence and the resolution of lipid flux [86, 87].

Overall, lipophagy-mediated resistance to cell death is neither intrinsically beneficial nor harmful; its value depends on the protected cell population. In hepatocytes, cardiomyocytes, renal cells, macrophages, and neurons, enhanced lipophagic flux may preserve tissue integrity by reducing lipid-induced apoptosis, necroptosis, oxidative injury, and inflammatory cell loss. In tumors, the same survival function may help malignant cells tolerate nutrient deprivation, hypoxia, and therapy-induced metabolic stress. Personalized therapy should therefore enhance lipophagy-driven cell death resistance when it protects functional tissue but limit it when it supports pathogenic or malignant survival.

Personalization is also needed because genetic variation in autophagy genes, the nutritional status, and comorbid metabolic diseases shape baseline lipophagic activity and the treatment response [53, 82, 84]. Lipidomic profiles may indicate whether pathology is driven mainly by triglyceride overload, cholesterol ester accumulation, or lipid peroxidation susceptibility, helping predict the benefit of autophagy enhancement versus inhibition [118–121, 134]. Autophagy biomarkers, including LC3 processing and lysosomal readouts, require careful validation because LC3-II accumulation can reflect either increased formation or impaired degradation [31–33, 86, 87]. Scalable surrogates such as the LD burden in peripheral leukocytes paired with flux markers are promising, but large datasets are needed to define sensitivity, specificity, and stage dependence [31–33].

Cross-organ and systems-level perspectives

Lipophagy should be viewed as an organism-wide lipid distribution and stress adaptation network, not only a cell-autonomous pathway [6]. In metabolic diseases, the liver, adipose tissue, muscle, and gut interact through autophagy and inflammation to reshape systemic lipid flux and insulin sensitivity [200]. This systems view is essential because enhancing lipophagy in one tissue may shift the lipid burden elsewhere if oxidation, lipoprotein handling, and the inflammatory tone are not coordinated [200, 201]. For example, increased hepatic lipophagy could release fatty acids into circulation; if muscle or cardiac utilization is limited, ectopic lipid deposition may worsen the cardiometabolic risk despite the reduction in hepatic steatosis [201]. Thus, lipid mobilization must be matched by downstream oxidation or export to prevent a redistribution of toxicity [78, 201].

“Systemic lipophagy” therefore implies the coordinated tuning of lipid-specific autophagy across tissues to maintain whole-body homeostasis [103]. This process will require the alignment of the liver‒adipose‒muscle axis with gut‒liver signaling and central regulation of appetite, energy expenditure, and substrate preference [103, 200, 201]. Lifestyle interventions may be especially effective because they remodel multiple organs simultaneously: exercise activates AMPK-driven autophagy in muscle and liver while increasing lipid oxidation, and caloric restriction or intermittent fasting can coordinate SIRT1/AMPK activation, mTORC1 suppression, and improved mitochondrial function across tissues [152–160].

Systems-level measurements will be essential to convert these concepts into testable intervention models [103, 200, 201]. Rather than relying on static LD histology or the detection of autophagy markers in single tissues, future studies should measure lipid flux across compartments, including fatty acid release, oxidation, and re-esterification, together with longitudinal changes in autophagic–lysosomal function [197, 201]. Stable isotope tracing combined with tissue-resolved lipidomics can determine whether interventions increase net lipid disposal or merely redistribute lipid species among storage pools [197]. Mapping organelle contact networks, including ER‒LD and mitochondria‒LD interfaces, may further clarify how tissues allocate lipids to energy production, membrane synthesis, or detoxification during metabolic stress [93–98].

Finally, the delivery of therapeutic agents may need to prioritize tissue specificity to avoid harmful cross-organ trade-offs [103]. Nanocarriers and conditionally activated prodrugs can concentrate autophagy modulators in lipid-overloaded tissues, reduce systemic exposure, and improve the therapeutic index [103]. Coupling drug activation to microenvironmental cues such as redox stress or lipid peroxidation markers may enable lipophagy modulation only when it is likely to be adaptive [103, 134]. Progress will require integrated, perturbable pathway maps that connect cytosolic signaling, transcriptional control, LD coat remodeling, and lysosomal competence in clinically relevant models [86, 87, 196, 197].

Concluding remarks

Lipophagy is a key pathway for LD turnover, linking nutrient sensing to organelle quality control by delivering neutral lipids to lysosomes for hydrolysis and reuse. It is not merely a starvation-induced backup mechanism; rather, it functions with cytosolic lipolysis as a complementary catabolic route. Its contribution depends on the nutrient status, LD size, cell type, and disease context. This division of labor supports metabolic flexibility: surface lipolysis rapidly releases fatty acids, whereas lipophagy clears LDs in bulk and promotes lysosome-coupled recycling when the lipid load is high or when selected LD subpopulations require removal. Lipophagic products also fuel mitochondrial beta-oxidation, sustaining ATP production while limiting the levels of lipid intermediates that drive oxidative and ER stress.

A major advance is the view that LD catabolism is embedded in a multiorganelle network. Contacts among LDs, the ER, mitochondria, and lysosomes determine whether lipid flux supports oxidation, membrane remodeling, or storage. These interfaces also explain the differences in degradation modes. Small LDs can be engulfed by autophagic membranes, whereas larger LDs often undergo repeated lysosome‒LD interactions and piecemeal degradation, increasing the reliance on tethering factors, the fusion machinery, and lysosomal enzymes. The LD size should therefore be considered a regulatory variable, not only a marker of lipid overload. Enlarged droplets may favor lipase-dependent processing, whereas small, remodeled droplets are more amenable to autophagic capture. Thus, lipophagy defects should be interpreted by distinguishing impaired cargo capture from defective terminal degradation because reduced lysosomal acidification, fusion, or hydrolase activity can mimic impaired initiation even when autophagosomes still form.

Mechanistically, productive lipophagic flux requires coordinated LD accessibility, signaling input, and degradative capacity. LD coat proteins such as PLINs create a physical barrier to catabolic access; regulated coat remodeling, including steps linked to CMA, may permit subsequent macroautophagic engagement. Nutrient- and energy-sensing pathways then regulate autophagy and lysosomal programs. AMPK activation during nutrient deprivation or exercise promotes autophagy and LD breakdown, whereas mTORC1 suppresses these processes under nutrient-replete conditions. TFEB further integrates this response by coordinating autophagy with lysosomal biogenesis through dephosphorylation and nuclear translocation during lipid overload or oxidative stress. Together, these pathways position lipophagy as a regulated interface between the cellular energy status, lipid mobilization, and lysosomal adaptation.

The role of lipophagy in disease is strongly context dependent. It can protect cells by buffering lipid overload and reducing lipotoxic signaling, but it may also sustain inflammation, fibrogenic remodeling, or tumor growth by maintaining the substrate supply. This duality explains why impaired lipophagy is often linked to steatosis and metabolic dysfunction, whereas a selective increase in autophagy in settings such as macrophage foam cells can improve lipid handling and reduce the inflammatory tone. These findings also caution against assuming a uniform direction for treating cancer or neurodegeneration, where lipid use, oxidative stress tolerance, and membrane integrity requirements differ from those in hepatometabolic disease.

Future work should define bona fide LD-selective autophagy through higher-resolution mapping of LD targeting and flux assays that quantify both lysosomal delivery and lipid hydrolysis. Translational strategies should improve lysosomal competence and LD accessibility rather than broadly activating autophagy. Interventions must be matched to the disease stage and tissue metabolic capacity while avoiding lipid redistribution across organs. A context-specific view of lipophagy should refine systemic metabolic models and guide the development of safer therapies for lipid-driven diseases.
