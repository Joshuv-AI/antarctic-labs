Flexural phonon instability defines intrinsic van der Waals elastic limits in the interlayer direction
Authors: Heyi Wang, Miaojie Liu, Jiayi Li, Shizhe Feng, Yuan Hou, Man Kit Cheng, Ke Cao, Fanling Meng, Juzheng Chen, Zhiping Xu, Yang Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFrenkel’s cohesive model predicts an ideal elastic strain limit of ~10% for strong solids, a bound upheld in conventional materials. We demonstrate that such consistency breaks down in van der Waals (vdW) solids, challenging established strength theories. In situ tensile tests, combined with first-principles calculations, reveal unexpected localized decohesion failure at only ~3% strain in graphite and ~2% in h-BN along the interlayer direction—well below defect-controlled limits—defining their intrinsic elastic limits. This localization phenomenon is absent in MoS2, GaSe, and 3D crystals. We identify flexural phonon instability as the trigger for a cascade of strain localization, creating periodic nanogaps to release elastic strain energy. This dynamic instability redefines the intrinsic elastic limits of vdW crystals and opens pathways to tailor their structural/transport properties via strain engineering.Subject terms: Structural properties, Mechanical and structural properties and devices

Introduction

Pushing the performance limits is a central pursuit of materials science, which is propelled by recent advances in data science and artificial intelligence. Structural information encoded in materials databases is often assumed to be sufficient for inferring key material properties, such as strength and strain to failure. Specifically, the strength of a solid has been correlated with structural descriptors like bond lengths and thermodynamic measures, such as cohesive energy1,2. These properties can thus be accurately predicted using first-principles calculations3,4, thereby accelerating material screening for structural and functional applications.

Empirically, Frenkel proposed a theoretical elastic strain limit as εth ~ 1/N, in which N is a material-specific constant, typically around 105,6. This prediction has been validated through high-quality nanomechanical tests on covalent crystals, such as silicon, diamond, and graphene, particularly at high lattice perfection, thereby broadening the potential of elastic strain engineering7–11.

Lattice dynamics theory also suggests that mechanical strength is governed by phonon instability12,13. This framework generally agrees with predictions from the structural or thermodynamic indicators14,15. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations confirm this agreement for the basal-plane strength of graphene under uniaxial tension, but reveal a breakdown under biaxial loading16, indicating a gap in our current understanding.

VdW homo- or heterostructures feature extreme structural and mechanical anisotropy. The basal planes exhibit a stickiness behavior17 that presents an interesting case for testing established strength theories18–27. To properly evaluate these theories, it is crucial to distinguish between the different regimes of elastic boundaries. On one hand, first-principles calculations predict an ideal elastic limit—the theoretical maximum capacity of a perfect lattice undergoing uniform deformation at absolute zero (0 K). These predictions yield high critical strains (εc) of 15%, 16%, and 10% for graphite, hexagonal boron nitride (h-BN), and MoS2, respectively (corresponding to σc = 2.238, 2.197, and 1.940 GPa in Supplementary Fig. 1). On the other hand, macroscopic exfoliation tests using atomic force microscopy28,29 report cleavage strengths that are orders of magnitude lower: 21.9 ± 0.9 MPa (N ≈ 2100) for graphite, 17.2 ± 0.6 MPa (N ≈ 2350) for h-BN, and 30.1 ± 1.1 MPa (N ≈ 1800) for MoS2. These low experimental values are generally regarded as a defect-controlled limit, arising from extrinsic factors, such as nonuniform load distributions and environmental contaminants (e.g., humidity)29. However, conventional mechanical probe methods lack the spatial and temporal resolution required to capture the real-time evolution of deformation and failure processes, making it difficult to exclude defect effects. Therefore, the intrinsic vdW elastic limit, the critical threshold where crystalline structures spontaneously fail at finite temperatures due to inherent phonon or elastic instability, and its underlying mechanisms remain unknown.

Applying elastic strain to vdW solids/heterostructures significantly alters the electronic and phononic interactions30–33. Recent studies reveal that applying gigapascal-level pressure can enable superconductivity in twisted bilayer graphene at higher twist angles and critical temperatures via a high-pressure diamond anvil cell21,34,35. Trilayer graphene develops an electronic bandgap of up to 2 eV under a pressure of 28 GPa36, while MoS2 multilayers transition from semiconductors to semi-metals at 19 GPa37. Large strains along the vdW interlayer direction are therefore anticipated to serve as an effective means of controlling material behavior, though their intrinsic tensile elastic limits remain unknown along interlayer planes.

Unlike the ideal elastic limits of covalent crystals (like diamond), which have been explored extensively, this work investigates the intrinsic elastic limits of microfabricated single-crystalline vdW solids perpendicular to their basal planes (Fig. 1a). Through in situ tensile tests using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM), we identify elastic limits for representative vdW solids along interlayer directions that are significantly lower than theoretical predictions based on uniform deformation assumptions. For graphite and h-BN, we uncover an unexpected failure mechanism involving the nucleation of periodic nanogaps, absent in MoS2 and GaSe. These findings are explained by flexural phonon instability, challenging conventional models of material strength.

Results and discussion

Elastic limits of vdW crystals

We fabricated T-shaped microbridge specimens of single-crystalline graphite, h-BN, MoS2, and GaSe. The length and width of graphite samples are ~1 μm and ~300 nm, respectively, the same as h-BN, MoS2, and GaSe samples (Fig. 1b). We aligned the gripper and samples with two fiducial markers and performed tensile tests along the interlayer planes (Fig. 1c). Atomic high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) images show AB, AA’, and 2H stacking of graphite, h-BN, and MoS2, respectively, without any observable lattice imperfections, indicating the ultrahigh-quality of single-crystalline testing samples (Fig. 1d–f).

We conducted quantitative in situ loading-unloading tensile tests with increasing uniaxial strain amplitudes. A representative graphite sample is illustrated in Fig. 2a (Supplementary Movie 1). Four loading-unloading cycles were carried out with increasing strain amplitudes of ~3.8%, ~4.2%, and ~5.1% in each of these cycles, respectively. The stress-strain curves at a tensile strain below 3% suggest an elastic modulus of E ~ 31.1 GPa for graphite (Fig. 2b)38, aligning well with DFT calculations for Bernal stacking graphite (~31 GPa, Supplementary Fig. 1a). The sample ultimately failed at ~6.5% strain (the 4th cycle) with a breaking stress of ~1.2 GPa (Fig. 2b). These data highlight the ultrahigh-quality of our sample.

Remarkably, during cyclic tensile tests in SEM, we observed periodic stripes emerging after the 2nd loading cycle (Fig. 2a). The stripes were identified as vdW gaps opened under TEM, bound by the surface layers of amorphous carbon (Fig. 2c), indicating vdW decohesion fracture between layers. We also characterized the spatial morphology of the opened nanogap edges using a high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) image (Fig. 2d, more TEM characterization attached in Supplementary Fig. 2). It indicated that the ~7 nm amorphous carbon layers induced by FIB (Fig. 2b) could effectively prevent catastrophic fragmentation upon breaking and enable us to track the failure behavior. We conducted additional in situ TEM compressive testing on an amorphous carbon nanopillar fabricated from single‑crystal graphite with Ga+ ion beam milling and found the large plasticity with a Young’s modulus of ~10 GPa (Supplementary Fig. 3).

To overcome the spatial resolution limits of SEM and uncover the underlying failure mechanism, we further conducted in situ loading-unloading TEM tensile straining. This allowed us to track the tensile elastic limit and the nanogap propagation process of graphite (Supplementary Movie 2 and Fig. 2e). During the first cycle, the sample reached ~2.2% tensile strain and recovered its original length, confirming complete elasticity. However, in the second cycle, we found that the sample achieved ~2.9% tensile strain before localized decohesion failure manifested by the opening of vdW gaps. This critical transition marks the elastic limit of the interlayer vdW interaction, a fundamental feature observable only via the superior resolution of TEM.

Beyond this limit, nanogaps continued to open in a cascade process. Despite this widespread interlayer decohesion, the overall structural integrity was preserved by the confining amorphous carbon layers, allowing the sample to sustain loads up to ~3.9% strain. Concurrently, we observed the formation of a periodic array of nanogaps on the tensile sample, directly accounting for the periodic stripes observed during the in situ SEM straining. Following unloading, the sample exhibited limited plasticity around the nanogap edges.

Ultimately, the entire structure fractured at 4.3% strain in the final TEM cycle of the particular sample, comparable to the macroscopic failure observed at ~6.5% strain in the previous SEM tests. This difference is likely due to the load-bearing capacity of the amorphous layers (Supplementary Note 1). The corresponding engineering stress-strain curves are provided in Supplementary Fig. 4.

The same settings of in situ TEM loading-unloading tensile tests were applied to other vdW solids, including h-BN (Supplementary Movie 3 and Fig. 3a), MoS2 (Supplementary Movie 4 and Fig. 3b), and GaSe (Supplementary Movie 5 and Fig. 3c with 3R phase). All samples were also coated by amorphous surface layers formed during FIB fabrication, with thicknesses of ~6, ~4, and ~4 nm, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 5). We found that h-BN also exhibited localized decohesion failure with vdW gaps open at ~2% tensile strains with ~350 MPa strength. We further demonstrated the spatial morphology of the opened h-BN nanogap edge by HAADF-STEM image (Fig. 3e). By contrast, MoS2 and GaSe samples showed no evidence of nanogap formation from the fractured samples (Fig. 3b, c). From corresponding engineering stress-strain curves, MoS2 and GaSe samples reached tensile strains of ~3.7% (~950 MPa strength) and ~4.8% (~1090 MPa strength) before fracture, respectively (Fig. 3d). Moreover, we conducted the same in situ tensile tests in SEM for the above vdW crystals (h-BN, MoS2, and GaSe39) to exclude the influence of high voltage irradiation of the e-beam. We found that the opened vdW nanogaps of h-BN still appeared under SEM (Supplementary Movie 6 and Supplementary Fig. 6). Also, there was still no nanogaps formation for MoS2 and GaSe (Supplementary Movie 7 and Supplementary Figs. 7, 8). Generally, the SEM tensile results for the above vdW crystals matched well with the TEM tensile results. Finally, we showed the out-of-plane deflection of graphene layers around the nano-crack by STEM tomography from the fractured graphite tensile sample (Supplementary Movie 8 and Fig. 3f).

Local decohesion triggered by flexural phonon instability

The failure of graphite with opened nanogaps reveals strain localization at the interfaces, driven by the competition between lattice distortion and decohesion energies. In a crystalline solid stretched from its equilibrium structure, two metastable states exist: one with a uniformly deformed lattice (u) and another with strain localization (l). Initially, the strain energy of uniform deformation, Eu(ε), is lower than that of the state with localized strain, El(ε). However, beyond a critical tensile strain, εc, decohesion at a specific interface becomes energetically more favorable (Fig. 4a)2. From a thermodynamic perspective, the crossover between Eu(ε) and El(ε) defines the stability boundary between the two states. However, this thermodynamic argument does not hold for realistic materials, as evidenced by the fact that the critical strain εc tends toward zero with increasing sample size. In practice, overcoming the kinetic barrier corresponds to the point where the curvature of Eu(ε) approaches zero. While this theoretical framework is compelling2, experimental evidence of such an instability has not been reported in prior to this work. This gap prevents a deeper understanding of the driving force behind the kinetic transition and its influence on the intrinsic elastic limits of solids.

We postulate that the phenomenon observed in our study is intrinsically linked to vdW interactions at the decohered interface. Thermal fluctuations or excited flexural phonons in perfect lattices induce localized strain12,14. The growing unstable mode of bending in the 2D layers results in the formation and coarsening of wrinkles, ending up with the observed vdW interlayer decohesion failure and propagation of nanogaps under the confinement of amorphous skins. To validate this hypothesis, we calculated the phonon dispersions of representative vdW solids using first-principles methods at the DFT level (see “Methods” for details). The results reveal flexural phonon instability in the low-wavevector (low-q) region for graphite (Supplementary Figs. 9 and 10) and h-BN (Supplementary Fig. 11), which is absent in MoS2 (Supplementary Fig. 12), 3R stacked GaSe (Supplementary Figs. 1d and 13), and diamond (Supplementary Fig. 14). Along the Γ-M path in the Brillouin zone of honeycomb lattices, unstable flexural phonon modes are identified by the presence of negative frequencies at q < qmax (the maximum wavevector with negative frequencies) (Fig. 4b). The amplitude of maximum negative frequencies varied with strain are shown in Fig. 4c for graphite. Supercell-based DFT calculations show that this instability is activated in graphite and h-BN beyond strains of 4% and 2%, respectively. These values effectively bound the strengths of real samples and align well with our experimental results (Fig. 4d, see more intrinsic elastic limits data of graphite and h-BN in Supplementary Fig. 15)2. Moreover, we considered the influence of defects and observed that the measured elastic limit fell below those predicted for defective graphite with the uniform deformation assumption (Supplementary Note 2). A single edge vacancy in graphene reduces the in-plane elastic limit dominated by directional covalent bonds by 54%40, while an embedded vacancy (5.6% concentration) in graphite reduces the c-axis elastic limit only by 13.4%. This contrast is attributed to the sticky nature of interfacial vdW interaction that is non-directional. These results indicate that the strain measured in graphite is intrinsically governed by flexural phonon instability, rather than by critical interaction distance—with or without imperfections. Therefore, this experimental value represents the intrinsic tensile elastic limit of graphite along the interlayer direction.

During the process of failure, thermally excited unstable flexural phonons with short wavelengths (λmin = 2π/qmax) merge into wrinkles with long wavelengths under tensile strain, lowering their energy and resulting in the opening of nanogaps, as observed in graphite and h-BN during the tensile tests. By contrast, no flexural phonon instability occurs below the elastic limit under uniform deformation in MoS2 and 3R stacked GaSe. This explains the absence of nanogap formation in MoS2 and GaSe in our experiments. The observed elastic limits of MoS2 and GaSe, which are lower than their ideal elastic strain limits, may be attributed to ripple-induced strain localization or atomic-level defects41–43.

To further explain the absence of flexural phonon instability, we develop an energy-based criterion for nanogap formation to reveal why no nanogaps form in other vdW crystals. The vdW interaction between layers includes both repulsive and cohesive parts in an equilibrium state. However, the repulsion is suppressed at a stretched state. Flexural deformation of the 2D layers then becomes favorable since it enhances cohesion at the face contact side, overcoming the energy cost at the back side due to the nonlinearity of cohesion-distance dependence (Supplementary Note 3). To take the role of bending stiffness on failure modes into consideration, we derived an energy criterion ∆U = 4π2D − V′(d)dλ2 to determine whether layers tend to bend or remain flat (flat with ∆U > 0 and flexural with ∆U < 0), where D is the bending stiffness, d is interlayer distance, V(d) is vdW interaction energy per unit area, and λ is bending wavelength (Supplementary Note 3). It reveals that a higher D value results in a flatter configuration, suppressing the excitation of flexural phonons. DFT calculations show that the bending stiffness of MoS2 (6.29 eV) is much higher than that of graphene (1.46 eV) and h-BN (1.29 eV), explaining that nanogap nucleation is feasible in materials with single-atom-thick layers, such as graphite and h-BN, but not in other vdW crystals like MoS2 and GaSe, nor in 3D lattice crystals, such as diamond (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Figs. 13, 14). To investigate potential size effects, we performed tensile tests on graphite specimens with a reduced diameter of ~100 nm. The results revealed a similar localized decohesion as Fig. 2e, followed by a cascade of nanogaps (Supplementary Fig. 16).

Patterns of vdW nanogaps opening

To elucidate the mechanism of pattern formation, we modeled the nanogap patterns by finite element analysis (FEA) based on the TEM data (Fig. 4e and see “Methods” for details). Under tensile stress, elastic strain energy is released through the nucleation of out-of-plane deflection in the 2D layers, creating a tension-free or even compressive region around the nanogaps. This prevents secondary nucleation within the deflected region. FEA and TEM results reveal a tension-free zone around each nanogap, characterized by a size a, with its boundaries confined by a surface layer of amorphous carbon (Fig. 4e). As the geometric ratio s/a (spacing to crack size) decreases, the normal stress between periodic cracks transitions from tension to compression, inhibiting further crack growth between adjacent cracks44. The critical values of s/a = 0.7 for graphite and 0.6 for h-BN agree quantitatively with our experimental measurements (Fig. 4f). We further changed the amorphous layer thickness from 6 to 20 nm to investigate the effects of amorphous layers. We found that stress distribution and values remain nearly unchanged (with only a 0.64% change), ruling out the influence of amorphous carbon on the periodicity of nanogaps (Supplementary Fig. 17).

Using in situ TEM/SEM tensile testing perpendicular to the basal planes, we observed the intrinsic vdW elastic limits of graphite and h-BN. Their unexpectedly low intrinsic elastic limits, combined with first-principles predictions, reveal the critical role of flexural phonon instability along the interlayer direction, which triggers localized interlayer decohesion. This process, accompanied by the formation of periodic nanogaps, challenges existing strength theories of solids and provides a perspective for understanding their mechanical failure. In contrast, this localization phenomenon is absent in MoS2 and GaSe due to their higher bending stiffness, which effectively suppresses phonon instability. Our findings establish a direct link between flexural instability and strain-driven structural evolution, offering a framework for strain engineering to manipulate the structural and transport properties of vdW materials via controlled nanogap formation45,46. Additionally, the reported moduli and strengths set quantitative benchmarks, advancing a more predictive theory of vdW interactions.

Methods for microfabrication of tensile specimens

We microfabricated tensile bridges of four representative vdW materials (graphite, h-BN, MoS2, and GaSe) by using a focused-ion beam (FIB, FEI SciosTM DualBeamTM) from the single-crystalline bulk samples. The graphite and MoS2 bulk samples were bought from Shanghai Onway Technology Co., Ltd. The bulk h-BN flakes were synthesized using a high-pressure/high-temperature (HPHT) method47. Bulk GaSe samples were synthesized by the Bridgman–Stockbarger technique. We extracted and transferred thin lamella from the bulk samples, and milled them into T-shaped tensile bridges at ion beam currents from 30 nA to 50 pA under an operating voltage of 16 to 30 kV48. We attempted to minimize the effect of the amorphous surface layers by reducing their thickness using low voltage and current in FIB. FIB was also used to make homemade diamond grippers from the tip of a diamond indenter.

In situ tensile testing

Quantitative tensile tests were performed by using a HysitronTM PI 85 PicoIndenter, an in situ mechanical instrument that enables the deformation process to be observed in SEM (FEI Quanta 450 FESEM) operating at 10 kV. In situ TEM tensile tests were carried out inside a JEOL JEM-2100F TEM by using a HysitronTM PI 95 PicoIndenter TEM holder. The HRTEM imaging was also performed using JEOL JEM-2100F. The nominal strain rate under displacement control was approximately 1 × 10−3 s−1. The strain was measured from SEM/TEM snapshots and corrected since the raw displacement data recorded both sample deformation and indenter displacement due to the limited effective stiffness of the test instrument. HAADF-STEM imaging was conducted using an FEI Themis Z (S)TEM operating at 300 kV with a convergence semiangle of 25 mrad.

First-principles calculations

Single-crystalline graphite (AB), h-BN (AA’), MoS2 (2H), GaSe (3R), and diamond were explored by DFT-based first-principles calculations for their mechanical properties and phonon spectra, using Vienna ab-initio simulation package (VASP) (version 6.3.2)49. The 2-layer supercells with 4 carbon atoms, 2 BN units, and 2 MoS2 units and 3-layer supercells with 3 GaSe units (3R stacking) were constructed. Surface effects were not considered. The kinetic energy cutoff of plane waves was set to 550 eV. The r2SCAN exchange-correlation functional was employed to capture the nature of vdW interaction50. The Brillouin zone was sampled by using a 24 × 24 × 4 Monkhorst-Pack grid, ensuring energy convergence of 10−6 eV/atom. Periodic boundary conditions (PBCs) were applied in all directions. Following full structural optimization with a force criterion of 10−4 eV/Å, phonon dispersion was calculated from the finite-displacement method in Phonopy (version 2.12.0), where the supercell was scaled by a factor of 4 × 4 × 251. Tensile stress-strain data were obtained by stretching the sample along the c-axis.

Finite element analysis (FEA)

To analyze the stress distribution in the microfabricated specimens under specific tensile strains, FEA models were constructed based on our experimental setup. A square-section beam of graphite with a side length of a = 260 nm was modeled, with PBCs applied along the tensile direction. Two precracks were introduced, spaced by a distance s. We simulated amorphous coatings with 6–20 nm-thick surface layer. The model was meshed using quadratic C3D8R elements with a 5 nm element size. A transversely-isotropic elastic model was employed with the following elastic constants: E1 = E2 = 1 TPa, E3 = 30 GPa, G12 = 358 GPa, G13 = G23 = 5.42 GPa52. The amorphous coating was modeled as an isotropic, elastic-perfectly plastic material with Young’s modulus of ~10 GPa, a yield strength of 0.8 GPa, and a failure strain of 1.

To capture the growth of nanocracks under tension, the cohesive zone model (CZM) was used to describe the vdW interface between the 2D layers near cracks. A linear traction-separation model with the maximum stress criterion was implemented to model damage initiation and propagation. Cohesive elements were used in both normal and shear modes, with peak stresses of 0.3 GPa and 6.2 MPa, respectively. These values are one-tenth of those used in previous studies (3 GPa and 62 MPa)53, reflecting the reduced strength of the interfaces after tensile destabilization compared to the undeformed graphite. Three CZM layers were applied, and quasi-static displacement loading was implemented along the c-axis, consistent with our experimental setup.
