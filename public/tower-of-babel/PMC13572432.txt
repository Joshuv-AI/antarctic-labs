Correlation-controlled stochastic computing for low-power FIR and IIR filters in edge DSP
Authors: Raghav Krishna, Gopinath Palanisamy, Goutham Veerapu, Nisha JS, Ganesh R. Naik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Conventional digital filters in edge devices rely on fixed-point multipliers and adders, which dominate power consumption and hardware cost, limiting their deployment in low-power biomedical and IoT applications. This work proposes a correlation-controlled stochastic computing (SC) framework for finite impulse response (FIR) and infinite impulse response (IIR) filters, in which arithmetic operations are performed on stochastic bitstreams rather than on fixed-point units. Correlation-controlled bitstream generation and optimized encoder–decoder architectures are introduced to suppress correlation-induced bias and reduce hardware overhead. All results reported in this paper are obtained via software simulation on ECG-like, speech-like audio, photoplethysmography (PPG), and industrial IoT vibration signals representative of edge DSP workloads. The proposed design achieves a mean squared error (MSE) below 1% and output signal-to-noise ratios of 23 dB (ECG), 45 dB (audio), 49 dB (PPG), and 47 dB (IoT vibration) at a bitstream length of L=1024, while reducing switching activity by up to 10 times compared with a 16-bit fixed-point DSP baseline. Correlation control alone reduces bias from 3.8% to below 0.5%, as confirmed by ablation experiments. These results demonstrate that correlation-controlled SC provides a favorable trade-off between computational efficiency and signal fidelity, establishing a practical route toward low-power stochastic DSP for resource-constrained biomedical and IoT sensor nodes.

Introduction

Digital signal processing (DSP) is a fundamental building block of modern electronics, supporting applications ranging from wireless communications to biomedical monitoring. Edge platforms such as wearable sensors and Internet of Things (IoT) nodes must operate under strict energy and hardware constraints1–4. Finite impulse response (FIR) and infinite impulse response (IIR) filters are typically implemented in fixed-point arithmetic, where multipliers and adders dominate both dynamic power consumption and silicon area, making energy efficiency a critical bottleneck for DSP in embedded systems.

To address this challenge, several approaches have been explored. Approximate computing reduces arithmetic precision to save power but often introduces unacceptable distortion in biomedical or communication signals5. Low-complexity filter structures and coefficient quantization reduce implementation costs, though their improvements are modest6. Stochastic computing (SC) has recently gained attention as an alternative approach, in which numbers are encoded as random bitstreams and multipliers or adders are replaced by simple logic gates such as AND, OR, and multiplexers, leading to substantial reductions in circuit complexity and switching activity7,8.

As Table 1 shows, no prior SC filter work simultaneously addresses all six dimensions. The closest competitor, Ahmed et al.15, introduces a Wallace-tree APC and partial stream reordering but does not measure residual correlation, does not bound encoder–decoder error in closed form, and reports accuracy without switching activity. Wang et al.12 report switching activity but without accuracy on the same workload, making trade-off analysis impossible. Ardakani et al.14 extend SC to IIR filters but do not quantitatively address feedback-path correlation. The proposed work is the first to jointly close all six gaps across realistic biomedical and audio edge DSP workloads.

Despite these advances in SC filter design, three key limitations persist in the literature. First, thecorrelation between stochastic streams causes systematic bias that limits filter accuracy17,20. Second, encoder and decoder overhead can offset the power advantage of stochastic cores18. Third, prior studies often validate SC on toy benchmarks without demonstrating robust performance on realistic DSP workloads such as biomedical or audio signals21,22.

Motivated by these limitations, this paper proposes a correlation-controlled stochastic computing framework for FIR and IIR filters that introduces explicit correlation management in bitstream generation and optimized encoder–decoder designs, validated on ECG and audio signals representative of edge DSP workloads.

Stochastic number representation

In stochastic computing (SC), a real number x ∈ [0,1] is encoded as a stochastic number (SN), defined as a sequence of independent Bernoulli random variables7:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} S = \{s_t\}_{t=1}^{L}, \quad s_t \in \{0,1\}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where L is the bitstream length and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_t$$\end{document} denotes the t-th bit. The empirical mean2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \hat{x} = \frac{1}{L}\sum _{t=1}^{L} s_t \end{aligned}$$\end{document}is an unbiased estimator of x with variance3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{Var}(\hat{x}) = \frac{x(1-x)}{L}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Approximation accuracy therefore increases as L increases. For the bipolar representation, a signed real value x ∈ [-1,1] is mapped as4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} x = 2\,\Pr (s_t=1) - 1, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Pr (s_t=1)$$\end{document} is the probability of observing a one in the stream.

Stochastic arithmetic operations

Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X=\{x_t\}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y=\{y_t\}$$\end{document} be two SNs encoding real values x and y, respectively.

Multiplication: If X and Y are independent, then5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \Pr (x_t \wedge y_t = 1) = \Pr (x_t=1)\,\Pr (y_t=1) = xy, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}so multiplication is realized by a single AND gate. When correlation exists, the expected output becomes6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb {E}[z_t] = xy + \rho _{XY}\sqrt{x(1-x)\,y(1-y)}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho _{XY}$$\end{document} is the correlation coefficient between X and Y9. Nonzero \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho _{XY}$$\end{document} introduces systematic bias, the central obstacle for practical SC.

Addition and averaging: A 2 : 1 multiplexer controlled by a random selector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_t$$\end{document} with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Pr (r_t=1)=\alpha$$\end{document} yields7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb {E}[z_t] = \alpha \, x + (1-\alpha )\,y, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}implementing weighted addition. The special case α = 0.5 corresponds to arithmetic averaging12.

Scaling: Scaling by a constant c ∈ [0,1] is performed by ANDing with a Bernoulli(c) sequence \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u_t$$\end{document}:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb {E}[z_t] = c \cdot x. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

FIR and IIR filter formulation

A length-M FIR filter with coefficients \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{h_k\}$$\end{document} is expressed as9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} y[n] = \sum _{k=0}^{M-1} h_k\, x[n-k]. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}In SC, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_k$$\end{document} and x[n-k] are encoded as bitstreams \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_k$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_{n-k}$$\end{document}, and their product is approximated by10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \widehat{h_k\, x[n-k]} = \frac{1}{L}\sum _{t=1}^{L} \bigl (h_{k,t} \wedge x_{t,\,n-k}\bigr ), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_{k,t}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_{t,\,n-k}$$\end{document} denote the t-th bits of the streams encoding \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_k$$\end{document} and x[n-k], respectively. The overall FIR output is the sum of these approximations10,13.

An Nth-order IIR filter is represented as11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} y[n] = \sum _{k=0}^{M-1} b_k\, x[n-k] - \sum _{m=1}^{N} a_m\, y[n-m], \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where the recursive outputs y[n-m] must be re-encoded into stochastic streams to preserve independence from the current inputs. Without proper re-encoding, correlation accumulates across feedback paths, amplifying bias11,14.

Error sources in stochastic DSP

The MSE of a stochastic filter output is decomposed as12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{MSE} = (\textrm{Bias})^2 + \textrm{Var} + \epsilon _{\textrm{enc}/\textrm{dec}}^2, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where (i) \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{Bias}$$\end{document} arises from correlated inputs, (ii) \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{Var}$$\end{document} is due to finite stream length L and scales as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {O}(1/L)$$\end{document}, and (iii) \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\epsilon _{\textrm{enc}/\textrm{dec}}$$\end{document} reflects encoder and decoder approximations15,19.

Optimization perspective

The design objective of correlation-controlled SC filters is expressed as a constrained optimization problem. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{y}[n]$$\end{document} denote the stochastic output and y[n] the fixed-point reference. The total error is13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {E} = \mathbb {E}\!\left[ (\hat{y}[n]-y[n])^2\right] = (\textrm{Bias})^2 + \textrm{Var} + \epsilon _{\textrm{enc}/\textrm{dec}}^2. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Dynamic power is proportional to switching activity α, normalized to the fixed-point case18. The filter design problem is then formulated as14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \min _{L,\,\mathscr {G},\,\mathscr {E}/\mathscr {D}} \quad&\mathscr {E}(L,\,\mathscr {G},\,\mathscr {E}/\mathscr {D}) \nonumber \\ \text {s.t.} \quad&\alpha (L,\,\mathscr {G},\,\mathscr {E}/\mathscr {D}) \le \alpha _{\max }, \nonumber \\&L_{\min } \le L \le L_{\max }, \nonumber \\&\textrm{Area}(\mathscr {E}/\mathscr {D}) \le A_{\max }, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {G}$$\end{document} denotes the bitstream generation strategy and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {E}/\mathscr {D}$$\end{document} the encoder–decoder architecture. The optimization seeks a design that minimizes error while respecting constraints on power and hardware resources. The proposed correlation-controlled bitstream generation and lightweight encoder–decoder architectures provide a practical solution to (14), restoring the 1/L scaling law and achieving accuracy close to fixed-point DSP while reducing switching activity by up to 10×.

Problem formulation

The challenge is to design an SC-based FIR/IIR filter that reduces the error sources identified above while maintaining low switching activity. Past SC designs frequently overlooked inter-stream correlation, leading to biased outputs and over-engineered encoders and decoders that negate the energy benefits of stochastic arithmetic23,24. The problem formulated here is to develop a correlation-controlled SC filter with lightweight encoder–decoder support that: (1) restores the 1/L variance law, (2) suppresses correlation-induced bias, and (3) delivers accuracy close to fixed-point DSP at significantly lower switching activity. This formulation guides the proposed method described in Section Correlation-Controlled Stochastic Filter Design.

Sources of correlation in stochastic computing

Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_i = \{s_{i,t}\}_{t=1}^{L}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_j = \{s_{j,t}\}_{t=1}^{L}$$\end{document} be stochastic bitstreams encoding values \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_i,\, x_j \in [0,1]$$\end{document}. Their empirical correlation is15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \rho _{ij} = \frac{\mathbb {E}[s_{i,t}\, s_{j,t}] - x_i x_j}{\sqrt{x_i(1-x_i)\, x_j(1-x_j)}}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}When \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho _{ij} \ne 0$$\end{document}, the AND-gate product of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_i$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_j$$\end{document} produces a biased estimate of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_i x_j$$\end{document}:16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb {E}[s_{i,t} \wedge s_{j,t}] = x_i x_j + \rho _{ij}\sqrt{x_i(1-x_i)\, x_j(1-x_j)}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The bias term in (16) is zero only when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho _{ij} = 0$$\end{document}, which requires two streams to be statistically independent. Naïve SC designs reuse the same pseudo-random source across all streams, resulting in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho _{ij} \approx 1$$\end{document} for streams that encode different values from the same sequence9,12. This is the principal source of the 3.8% bias observed in the shared-seed baseline (Table 8).

Concrete seed assignment and LFSR construction

Each stochastic stream \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_i$$\end{document} is generated by an independent pseudo-random number generator (PRNG) initialized with a distinct seed \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _i$$\end{document}. The seed assignment follows a deterministic offset scheme:17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \sigma _i = \sigma _0 + \delta \cdot i, \qquad i = 0, 1, \ldots , M-1, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _0$$\end{document} is a fixed base seed and δ is a large prime offset chosen to maximize the distance between generator states. In all experiments reported here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _0 = 123$$\end{document} and δ = 10007. The offset δ is selected to exceed the expected correlation length of the underlying PRNG, ensuring that streams \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_i$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_j$$\end{document} draw from non-overlapping segments of the pseudo-random sequence for all |i - j| ≤ M.

In hardware, each stream is produced by a 16-bit Galois LFSR with primitive polynomial \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x^{16} + x^{14} + x^{13} + x^{11} + 1$$\end{document}, initialized to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _i \bmod (2^{16}-1)$$\end{document}. The period of this LFSR is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2^{16} - 1 = 65535$$\end{document}, which exceeds the maximum bitstream length L = 2048 used in this work by a factor of 32, preventing period aliasing. The LFSR output is compared against the encoded value via a 16-bit comparator to produce the stochastic bit \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_{i,t}$$\end{document} as defined in (19).

For coefficient streams, which are fixed across all input samples, the seed is assigned once at filter initialization. For input sample streams, the seed is recomputed per block using18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \sigma _{\textrm{block}} = \sigma _0 + 13 \cdot (1 + n_0), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_0$$\end{document} is the block start index. This per-block reseeding suppresses long-range correlation across successive output samples, which is particularly important for IIR feedback paths.

Optimized encoder and decoder structures

Encoder architecture

Stochastic encoding maps a binary value x ∈ [0,1] to a bitstream \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S = \{s_t\}_{t=1}^{L}$$\end{document} via a comparator-based scheme:19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} s_t = \mathbb {I}(u_t < x), \qquad u_t \sim \textrm{Uniform}[0,1], \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u_t$$\end{document} is drawn from a pseudo-random source. In hardware, this is implemented using a linear feedback shift register (LFSR) of length b bits paired with a b-bit digital comparator. The LFSR period is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2^b - 1$$\end{document}, bounding the encoding resolution to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1/(2^b - 1)$$\end{document}. With b = 16, the resolution is approximately \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1.5 \times 10^{-5}$$\end{document}, well below the target MSE of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^{-2}$$\end{document}.

The dynamic power of the encoder is20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} P_{\textrm{enc}} = \alpha _{\textrm{enc}}\, C_{\textrm{enc}}\, V_{dd}^2\, f, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha _{\textrm{enc}}$$\end{document} is the toggle rate of the LFSR output. Since an LFSR bit toggles with probability 0.5 per cycle, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha _{\textrm{enc}}$$\end{document} is bounded above by 0.5. Encoder overhead is further reduced by sharing a single random source across multiple coefficient streams whose values are stationary across input samples, reducing the number of physical LFSRs from M to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {O}(\log M)$$\end{document} for a length-M filter, consistent with the stream-sharing strategy in15.

Approximate parallel counter (APC) decoder

The decoder recovers a binary estimate from the stochastic accumulation result. After M AND-gate products are formed at each time step t, they are summed by an approximate parallel counter (APC):21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} z_t = \sum _{k=0}^{M-1} \bigl (h_{k,t} \wedge x_{t,\,n-k}\bigr ), \qquad z_t \in \{0, 1, \ldots , M\}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The final output is recovered as the empirical mean over the bitstream of length L:22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \hat{y}[n] = \frac{1}{L} \sum _{t=1}^{L} z_t. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}An exact binary counter requires \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lceil \log _2(M+1) \rceil$$\end{document} bits of internal state and toggles \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {O}(\log M)$$\end{document} bits per increment on average. Prior APC designs in15,19 used Wallace-tree or ripple-carry structures, which incur full carry-propagation switching at every cycle. The proposed decoder departs from these in two ways: Stratified accumulation: rather than accumulating a single stream of length L, the bitstream is partitioned into K non-overlapping sub-streams of length L/K, each accumulated independently and average. This reduces the maximum counter value from M · L to M · L/K, lowering the required counter width by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\log _2 K$$\end{document} bits and proportionally reducing internal switching activity.Permutation-based MUX reduction (ablation baseline): a multiplexer-tree reduction with random permutation at each level is used as an alternative accumulation path. While less accurate than APC at the same L, it requires no counter state and serves as the lowest-switching reference point in the switching activity comparison.

Approximation error bound

The encoder introduces a quantization error bounded by the LFSR resolution:23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} |\epsilon _{\textrm{enc}}| \le \frac{1}{2^b - 1}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}For b = 16, this gives \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$|\epsilon _{\textrm{enc}}| \le 1.5 \times 10^{-5}$$\end{document}, which is negligible relative to the stochastic variance \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{Var}(\hat{x}) = x(1-x)/L \le 1/(4L)$$\end{document}.

The APC decoder introduces no additional approximation beyond finite stream length L. Its output \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{y}[n]$$\end{document} satisfies24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb {E}[\hat{y}[n]] = y[n], \qquad \textrm{Var}(\hat{y}[n]) \le \frac{M}{4L}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}under the assumption of decorrelated input streams enforced by the independent seed strategy in Section Correlation-aware bitstream generation. Equation (24) confirms that the total variance scales as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {O}(M/L)$$\end{document}, so that increasing L or reducing M directly controls the decoder error. Stratified accumulation further reduces variance by a factor of 1/K compared with a single accumulation run of length L/K, thereby restoring the effective variance to that of a full-length run at a fraction of the switching cost.

The combined encoder–decoder error is therefore bounded as25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \epsilon _{\textrm{enc}/\textrm{dec}}^2 \le \frac{1}{(2^b-1)^2} + \frac{M}{4L}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}which, for b = 16, M ≤ 64, and L ≥ 512, evaluates to at most \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$3.1 \times 10^{-2}$$\end{document}, consistent with the observed MSE values in Table 6.

FIR and IIR filter realization

For a length-M FIR filter with coefficients \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{h_k\}$$\end{document}, each tap contribution is26\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} Y_k[n] = \frac{1}{L}\sum _{t=1}^{L} \bigl (h_{k,t} \wedge x_{t,\,n-k}\bigr ), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}and the overall stochastic output is27\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \hat{y}[n] = \sum _{k=0}^{M-1} Y_k[n]. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}SC-based FIR implementations of this form have been studied in10,11,15.

For an IIR filter with feedforward coefficients \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{b_k\}$$\end{document} and feedback coefficients \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{a_m\}$$\end{document}, the stochastic output is28\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \hat{y}[n] = \sum _{k=0}^{M-1} \frac{1}{L}\sum _{t=1}^{L} \bigl (b_{k,t} \wedge x_{t,\,n-k}\bigr ) - \sum _{m=1}^{N} \frac{1}{L}\sum _{t=1}^{L} \bigl (a_{m,t} \wedge y_{t,\,n-m}\bigr ), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_{t,\,n-m}$$\end{document} is a re-encoded version of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{y}[n-m]$$\end{document}, generated with fresh independent seeds to ensure \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho _{ij} \approx 0$$\end{document} and prevent instability in recursive paths14,16.

Area and switching outlook: A length-M stochastic FIR filter requires M AND gates and a shallow multiplexer tree, whereas a fixed-point filter requires M full binary multipliers9,10. Encoders are implemented as LFSR–comparator pairs, and decoders use approximate counters with low internal switching activity15,19. With stream sharing, the number of physical LFSRs grows as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {O}(\log M)$$\end{document} rather than M. The observed 10× reduction in switching activity directly translates into dynamic power savings, consistent with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_{\textrm{dyn}} = \alpha \, C\, V_{dd}^2\, f$$\end{document}18.

Throughput, scalability, and routing: Latency is L cycles per output sample; throughput can be increased by pipelining or interleaving multiple sub-streams. The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {O}(1/L)$$\end{document} error scaling holds if inter-stream correlation is suppressed. For IIR filters, re-encoding adds a small overhead relative to binary multipliers14. Coefficient streams are stationary and generated on-chip; all data paths are single-bit, making the interconnect lightweight.

Comparison and viability: Prior FPGA implementations show that SC filters occupy a fraction of the resources required by conventional FIR filters6,13. In the proposed design, encoder–decoder overhead is contained within the bounds derived in Section Correlation-Controlled Stochastic Filter Design, and the core switching activity is far below that of binary multipliers, consistent with the 10× reductions in Table 5. Residual bias from finite \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$|\rho _{ij}|$$\end{document} and decoder overhead are mitigated by per-block reseeding and stratified APC accumulation15. Taken together, the block-level architecture and switching data support the feasibility of correlation-controlled SC as a practical low-power DSP architecture for edge devices.

Signal and filter specifications

ECG-like signal: The ECG-like signal is generated synthetically at a sampling rate of 250 Hz, consistent with clinical ECG acquisition standards25. The signal comprises a sinusoidal baseline at 1.2 Hz with additive Gaussian noise (σ = 0.05), representing respiration-induced baseline wander, combined with a train of R-peaks modeled as impulses convolved with a first-order IIR envelope (a = 0.95). Inter-beat intervals are drawn uniformly from [180, 260] samples, corresponding to heart rates of approximately 58–83 beats per minute. The composite signal is normalized to the unipolar range [0, 1] prior to stochastic encoding, with a total length of 3000–4000 samples (12–16 s at 250 Hz).

The FIR filter applied to the ECG signal is a 32-tap (M = 32) low-pass filter designed using the windowed sinc method (Hamming window) with a normalized cutoff frequency of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_c = 0.12 \times f_s/2$$\end{document}, corresponding to approximately 15 Hz at 250 Hz. This passband preserves the clinically relevant QRS complex and P- and T-wave components while attenuating high-frequency noise.

Speech-like audio signal: The speech-like signal is generated at a sampling rate of 16 kHz using a second-order autoregressive AR(2) vocal tract model, which is a standard approach for synthetic voiced speech generation26. The model follows the recursion29\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} y[n] = e[n] + a_1\, y[n-1] + a_2\, y[n-2], \end{aligned}$$\end{document}with coefficients \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_1 = 1.6$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_2 = -0.81$$\end{document}, placing the poles at a resonant frequency of approximately 800 Hz with a bandwidth of roughly 200 Hz, representative of a mid-frequency vowel formant. The output is normalized to [0, 1] and has a length of 16 000 samples (1 s at 16 kHz).

The FIR filter applied to the audio signal is a 64-tap (M = 64) band-pass filter designed using the windowed sinc method (Hamming window) with normalized passband edges of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[0.1,\;0.3] \times f_s/2$$\end{document}, corresponding to 800–2400 Hz at 16 kHz, covering the primary formant region of speech.

Three observations emerge from Fig. 8. First, all four workload curves reach MSE below \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^{-2}$$\end{document} (sub-1%) at L = 512 with normalized switching activity in the narrow range 0.10–0.12, confirming that the accuracy–efficiency operating point is consistent across sampling rates spanning 100 Hz to 16 kHz and filter orders from M = 32 to M = 64. This workload-independence of the switching activity is a direct consequence of correlation-controlled bitstream generation: since each stream is decorrelated by independent seed assignment regardless of the signal type, the per-gate toggle rate is governed by the stochastic bit probability rather than by signal-specific correlation patterns.

Second, the MSE decreases monotonically with L along each curve, following the theoretical \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {O}(1/L)$$\end{document} convergence restored by correlation control (Section Correlation-aware bitstream generation). This regularity means that the bitstream length L serves as a single, continuously adjustable design knob that shifts the operating point along the Pareto frontier without requiring architectural changes. A system designer can therefore select L to meet a target MSE or power budget directly from Fig. 8, without re-running full filter simulations.

Third, at any fixed L, the MSE spread across workloads reflects differences in filter order rather than differences in signal type. The ECG workload (M = 32) achieves the lowest MSE at each L because the APC decoder variance scales as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {O}(M/L)$$\end{document} (Section Correlation-Controlled Stochastic Filter Design): for fixed L, a longer filter (M = 64 for audio) accumulates more stochastic variance per output sample than a shorter one (M = 32 for ECG and PPG). The IoT-Vib workload (M = 48) falls between these two extremes, consistent with its intermediate filter order.

Taken together, these observations establish that the proposed framework traces a well-defined and predictable Pareto frontier in the accuracy–energy plane, providing quantitative guidance for application-specific design-space exploration in edge DSP systems1,2. In particular, L = 1024 represents a robust operating point at which all four workloads simultaneously satisfy the relevant application-level SNR thresholds (23.1 dB for ECG, 45.0 dB for audio, 49.4 dB for PPG, and 47.3 dB for IoT-Vib) while maintaining normalized switching activity below 0.12, confirming the generality of the proposed method across diverse edge DSP workloads.

First-order IIR filter validation

SC IIR implementation

The proposed SC framework is validated on a first-order recursive (IIR) low-pass filter defined by35\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \hat{y}[n] = \alpha \, x[n] + (1-\alpha )\, \hat{y}[n-1], \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha \in (0,1)$$\end{document} is the smoothing coefficient and the approximate cutoff frequency is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_c \approx \alpha f_s / (2\pi )$$\end{document}. Equation (35) is implemented in SC using two AND gates and a 2 : 1 random MUX. The feedforward term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha \, x[n]$$\end{document} is computed as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\texttt {AND}(X_n, A)$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_n$$\end{document} and A are independent unipolar bitstreams encoding x[n] and α respectively. The feedback term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(1-\alpha )\,\hat{y}[n-1]$$\end{document} is computed as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\texttt {AND}(Y_{n-1}, \bar{A})$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{A}$$\end{document} encodes (1-α ) with an independent seed. The two AND outputs are combined via a MUX with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Pr (r_t=1)=0.5$$\end{document}, yielding an unbiased estimate of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\alpha \, x[n] + (1-\alpha )\,\hat{y}[n-1])/2$$\end{document}, which is scaled by 2 on decoding to recover the filter output.

Correlation control is applied per sample: the feedback output \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{y}[n-1]$$\end{document} is re-encoded with a fresh independent seed at each time step, consistent with the per-block reseeding strategy in (18). This prevents correlation accumulation along the the recursive path, the primary instability mechanism in naïve SC IIR implementations14. A stratified accumulation with K=4 sub-streams is applied to reduce output variance, consistent with the FIR decoder design in Section Correlation-Controlled Stochastic Filter Design.

Scope and objective: The objective of this experiment is to validate the feasibility and stability of correlation-controlled stochastic computing under recursive feedback, rather than to optimize IIR filter performance. In stochastic implementations, recursive structures are inherently more sensitive to residual correlation and re-encoding noise than feedforward FIR filters. Achieving high SNR in stochastic IIR systems typically requires hardware-level co-design, including synchronization of feedback bitstreams, controlled allocation of pseudo-random sequences, and timing-aware encoding strategies. The present study therefore focuses on demonstrating stable operation and bounded error under feedback, which has historically been a key challenge in stochastic IIR implementations.

Switching activity and comparison with FIR

The normalized switching activity for the ECG and audio IIR configurations is 0.24–0.28, and for the PPG and IoT-Vib IIR configurations is 0.11–0.41 (Table 9), corresponding to overall reductions of 2.4–4× relative to the fixed-point baseline across all six configurations. This is consistent with the FIR results in Table 5, where the proposed SC achieves 0.10–0.12, reflecting the additional MUX gate required for IIR addition relative to the pure APC accumulation used in the FIR path.

The SNR achieved by the SC IIR filters (11.7–20.3 dB) is lower than the FIR results (23.1 dB ECG, 45.0 dB audio, 49.4 dB PPG, 47.3 dB IoT-Vib). This gap is attributable to two factors specific to recursive SC arithmetic. First, the MUX-based addition introduces an averaging factor of 0.5 that must be compensated by scaling, amplifying quantization noise relative to the direct APC accumulation used in FIR. Second, per-sample feedback re-encoding, while necessary to suppress correlation buildup in the recursive path, introduces fresh stochastic noise at every time step, preventing the variance from averaging down as effectively as in the FIR case. The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {O}(1/\sqrt{L})$$\end{document} convergence observed between L=1024 and L=2048 (SNR improvement of 0.4 dB for ECG and 0.4 dB for PPG) is consistent with this feedback noise model, in contrast to the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {O}(1/L)$$\end{document} convergence of the FIR path. This behavior is consistent with stochastic recursive systems, where feedback re-encoding introduces fresh noise at each time step, limiting variance reduction and yielding an effective \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {O}(1/\sqrt{L})$$\end{document} convergence rate. While higher-order IIR filters (e.g., biquad sections) can be constructed by cascading first-order stages, achieving optimal performance in such configurations requires hardware-level synchronization of feedback bitstreams and precise control of pseudo-random sequence interactions. These aspects are implementation-dependent and are therefore beyond the scope of the present simulation-based study14.

Discussion: These results confirm that correlation-controlled stochastic computing enables stable IIR filtering with bounded error and reduced switching activity, despite the inherent noise amplification associated with recursive stochastic arithmetic. The observed performance gap relative to FIR filtering is consistent with the fundamental limitations of stochastic feedback systems, rather than a failure of the proposed correlation control framework.

Three findings emerge from Table 10.

First, shared-seed SC methods (B1–B5) suffer severely from correlation-induced bias regardless of the accumulator architecture. Methods using a MUX-tree adder (B1, B3, B4) achieve SNR of only 0.1–2.5 dB across all four workloads, as the MUX reduction propagates and amplifies the correlation error from the shared pseudo-random source. Methods using an APC accumulator with shared seeds (B2, B5) achieve SNR of 24–35 dB, confirming that the APC partially mitigates correlation bias relative to MUX-tree reduction, but cannot eliminate it without decorrelated input streams. These results validate the analytical framework of Section Correlation-aware bitstream generation, which identifies inter-stream correlation as the primary accuracy bottleneck in stochastic DSP.

Second, the independent-seed APC without stratification (B615) achieves SNR of 41.6–48.3 dB, approaching that of the proposed method. The proposed method improves upon B6 by 0.8–1.9 dB in SNR and reduces MSE by 17–19% across all workloads, attributable to the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\log _2 K = 2$$\end{document} bit counter-width reduction from stratified accumulation, which lowers decoder variance as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {O}(M/(KL_s)) = \mathscr {O}(M/L)$$\end{document} and suppresses output noise relative to a single non-stratified APC run. Crucially, both B6 and the proposed method achieve this with identical normalized switching activity (0.27–0.51), confirming that the accuracy improvement of stratification comes at no additional switching cost.

Third, the approximate computing baseline (B8, Prasad et al.5) achieves SNR of only 17.9–20.1 dB across all workloads despite a switching activity of 0.27–0.38, falling below the application-level thresholds of 23 dB (ECG), 40 dB (audio), 20 dB (PPG), and the corresponding IoT threshold. This confirms the fundamental limitation of operand truncation: zeroing the lower four mantissa bits introduces a systematic quantization floor that cannot be reduced by increasing a bitstream length parameter, unlike the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {O}(1/L)$$\end{document} convergence of the proposed SC framework. The proposed method is therefore the only design in Table 10 that simultaneously satisfies all four application-level SNR thresholds while maintaining competitive switching activity.

Discussion

Accuracy versus stream length

Fig. 2 confirms that the proposed correlation-controlled SC restores the theoretical \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {O}(1/L)$$\end{document} convergence of MSE, validating the analytical framework in Section Preliminaries and Problem Formulation. Suppressing inter-stream correlation allows stochastic numbers to serve as as unbiased estimators, thereby directly recovering the 1/L variance scaling. The naïve SC baseline deviates from this trend due to persistent correlation bias9,12, illustrating why prior SC designs failed to meet accuracy expectations.

ECG signal filtering

The ECG study demonstrates the biomedical relevance of correlation control. As shown in Fig. 3, naïve SC produces visible waveform distortions that could compromise clinical markers such as R-peak detection, whereas the proposed design closely tracks the fixed-point reference. At L = 1024, the proposed filter achieves MSE of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$6.37 \times 10^{-4}$$\end{document} and SNR of 23.1 dB (Table 6), within diagnostic requirements25. The ablation in Table 8 identifies correlation as the root cause: independent seeds reduce bias from 3.8% to below 0.5%, confirming that correlation-controlled SC delivers medically acceptable fidelity at substantially reduced switching activity.

Audio signal processing

The audio experiments extend validation to broadband and perceptually sensitive signals. Fig. 4 shows that the proposed filter preserves the spectral envelope of the speech-like signal, whereas naïve SC degrades spectral features. With SNR of 44.7 dB at L = 1024 (Table 6), the proposed SC achieves perceptually transparent filtering6, demonstrating that the framework is not limited to biomedical workloads but can also support general-purpose audio DSP where spectral accuracy is critical.

Switching activity

Table 5 shows the efficiency implications of each architecture. Naïve SC reduces toggling activity to approximately 40% of the fixed-point baseline, but at the cost of unacceptable accuracy degradation. The proposed design achieves reductions of 8–10× (normalized activity of 0.10–0.12) while maintaining fidelity, confirming that dynamic power savings are realizable through reduced switching probability, consistent with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_{\textrm{dyn}} = \alpha \, C\, V_{dd}^2\, f$$\end{document}18,19. These results show that the proposed method simultaneously achieves lower switching activity and higher accuracy, rather than trading one for the other.

Comparative assessment

Table 7 consolidates the comparison across all three implementations. Fixed-point DSP achieves zero error and formally infinite SNR but incurs full switching activity (normalized to 1.0). Naïve SC lowers switching but fails to preserve fidelity, with SNR limited to 13–19 dB10,14. The proposed SC achieves fixed-point accuracy within 1% MSE while reducing switching activity by up to 10×, establishing correlation-controlled SC as a practical operating point between accuracy and efficiency. The primary limitation is that all evaluations are conducted in simulation; FPGA or ASIC prototypes will be required to confirm absolute energy and area savings in hardware5,6.

IIR filter results and recursive SC arithmetic

The first-order IIR experiments extend the proposed correlation-controlled SC framework beyond FIR filtering and provide the first quantitative characterization of recursive SC arithmetic on biomedical and audio workloads. The switching activity reduction of 3.5–4× relative to fixed-point DSP (Table 9) is consistent with the FIR results in Table 5, confirming that the efficiency benefit of single-bit AND-gate arithmetic carries over to recursive filter structures. The modest difference between IIR switching (0.24–0.28) and FIR switching (0.10–0.12) are attributable to the additional MUX gate required for recursive addition, which contributes a small but nonzero toggle overhead absent in the pure APC accumulation of the FIR path.

The SNR gap between IIR (11.7–15.8 dB) and FIR (23.1 dB ECG, 44.7 dB audio) results reflects a fundamental difference in error accumulation between the two filter classes under SC arithmetic. In the FIR case, stochastic noise averages down as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {O}(1/L)$$\end{document} because each output sample is an independent linear combination of fresh bitstreams. In the IIR case, the feedback path introduces per-sample re-encoding noise that cannot be averaged away over L cycles at a fixed sample rate, limiting convergence to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {O}(1/\sqrt{L})$$\end{document}. This is confirmed by the marginal SNR improvement of 0.4 dB when L is doubled from 1024 to 2048 for the ECG IIR workload, compared with the larger gains observed for FIR in Fig. 2. This distinction is important for system designers: FIR filters are strongly preferred in SC implementations when accuracy requirements are tight, while first-order IIR remains viable for applications where moderate SNR (12–16 dB) combined with 3.5–4× switching reduction is acceptable, such as baseline wander removal in ECG preprocessing or low-pass smoothing in sensor fusion pipelines.

The results also highlight the critical role of per-sample feedback re-encoding in preventing correlation buildup in the recursive path. Without independent reseeding, the feedback stream \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{y}[n-1]$$\end{document} would accumulate correlation with the current input stream x[n], producing a systematic bias analogous to the shared-seed error characterized in Table 8 for the FIR case. The independent seed strategy described in Section Correlation-aware bitstream generation thus serves a dual function: decorrelating parallel FIR tap streams and stabilizing the IIR feedback path. This unified treatment of correlation across both filter classes is a distinguishing feature of the proposed framework relative to prior SC filter designs9,14, which addressed FIR and IIR correlation separately or not at all.

Dynamic power projections

Table 12 also reports estimated total power (dynamic plus leakage) at f = 250 MHz for all configurations. Fig. 17 shows the power comparison for the four FIR workloads.

The proposed SC FIR filter achieves power reductions of 75.3× (ECG, M = 32) to 101.4× (Audio, M = 64) relative to the fixed-point baseline. These reductions substantially exceed the 8–10× switching activity reduction reported in Table 5 because power scales with the product of switching activity and gate count: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_{\textrm{dyn}} \propto \alpha \cdot N$$\end{document}. The proposed SC reduces both factors simultaneously — switching activity by 8–10× (Section Numerical Simulations) and gate count by 10.9–12.8× — yielding a combined dynamic power advantage of approximately two orders of magnitude. The switching activity reduction in Table 5 therefore represents a conservative, per-gate lower bound on the total power benefit; the full system-level advantage depends on the gate count difference between architectures.

For the first-order SC IIR filter, the estimated power reduction is 12.4–12.9×, smaller than the FIR case because the IIR gate count advantage (3.5×) is more modest due to the dedicated feedback re-encoding hardware.

It is important to note that these power estimates assume a fixed clock frequency of 250 MHz and are derived from the analytical switching activity model described in Appendix A. Actual power figures from FPGA or ASIC implementation will depend on routing parasitics, clock distribution, and technology-specific leakage floors, and are expected to differ from these analytical projections. The critical path of the proposed SC filter traverses: one LFSR stage (XOR+DFF, 2 gate delays), one 16-bit comparator chain (4 gate delays), one AND gate (1 gate delay), and the APC counter ripple-carry chain (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_{\textrm{strat}}$$\end{document} gate delays, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_{\textrm{strat}} = \lceil \log _2(M+1)\rceil - \log _2 K$$\end{document}). At a NAND2 propagation delay of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_{pd} = 35$$\end{document} ps (TSMC 28 nm HPC+, slow-slow corner), this evaluates to a critical path of 525–595 ps for the FIR configurations, corresponding to an estimated \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_{\max }$$\end{document} of 1681–1905 MHz.

The fixed-point critical path traverses an 8-level Wallace-tree partial product reduction followed by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lceil \log _2 M\rceil$$\end{document} adder-tree stages of 4 logic levels each, yielding 980–1120 ps and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_{\max }$$\end{document} of 893–1020 MHz for the FIR configurations. The proposed SC architecture therefore offers an estimated critical path improvement of 1.7–1.9×, supporting higher operating frequencies despite the longer encoder stage. For the first-order IIR filter, the shorter feedback path yields an estimated \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_{\max }$$\end{document} of 3175 MHz, reflecting the absence of a deep adder tree in the recursive structure.

These timing estimates confirm that the proposed SC datapath is not timing-limited at typical edge-DSP clock rates of 100–500 MHz, and that the latency cost of L cycles per output sample is the primary throughput constraint rather than the combinational path depth. Conclusion

This paper presents a correlation-controlled stochastic computing framework for FIR and IIR filters targeting energy-constrained DSP applications in edge and IoT systems1–4. Decorrelated bitstream generation and lightweight encoder–decoder architectures are introduced to restore the theoretical 1/L variance scaling while suppressing correlation-induced bias8,9,20. Simulation results on ECG-like, speech-like audio, photoplethysmography, and industrial IoT vibration signals confirm sub-1% MSE with output SNR of 23.1 dB (ECG), 45.0 dB (audio), 49.4 dB (PPG), and 47.3 dB (IoT-Vib) at L = 1024, demonstrating that stochastic filtering meets the accuracy requirements of biomedical, audio, and IoT edge DSP workloads spanning sampling rates from 100 Hz to 16 kHz6,21,25.

Relative to naïve SC, the proposed method reduces bias from 3.8% to below 0.5%, representing more than an order of magnitude improvement in accuracy. Relative to conventional fixed-point DSP, it reduces switching activity by up to 10×, implying substantial dynamic power savings17–19. Together, these results establish correlation control as the enabling mechanism that allows SC to simultaneously deliver accuracy and efficiency, addressing a long-standing barrier in the field12,14,30.

The framework is further validated on first-order IIR filters, achieving switching reductions of 3.5–4× relative to fixed-point DSP at SNR of 11.7–15.8 dB, confirming that correlation-controlled SC extends naturally to recursive filter structures. The lower SNR relative to FIR is analytically attributed to per-sample feedback re-encoding noise, which limits IIR convergence to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {O}(1/\sqrt{L})$$\end{document} compared with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {O}(1/L)$$\end{document} for FIR, providing a quantitative basis for filter class selection in SC-based edge DSP systems.

Future work should extend evaluations beyond L = 2048 to FPGA or ASIC prototypes to quantify absolute energy, area, and throughput benefits5,18. Integration with system-level low-power techniques and exploration of adaptive bitstream length control remain promising directions, particularly for heterogeneous edge platforms and resource-managed systems1,2. Overall, correlation-controlled SC advances stochastic arithmetic from a theoretical construct toward a practical architecture for next-generation low-power DSP in biomedical and IoT edge devices4,22.
