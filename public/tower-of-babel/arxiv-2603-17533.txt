Abstract
. Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly applied to recommendation, retrieval, and reasoning, yet deploying a single end-to-end model that can jointly support these behaviors over large, heterogeneous catalogs remains challenging. Such systems must generate unambiguous references to real items, handle multiple entity types, and operate under strict latency and reliability constraints requirements that are difficult to satisfy with text-only generation. While tool-augmented recommender systems address parts of this problem, they introduce orchestration complexity and limit end-to-end optimization. We view this setting as an instance of a broader research problem: how to adapt LLMs to reason jointly over multiple-domain entities , user behavior, and language in a fully self-contained manner. To this end, we introduce NEO , a framework that adapts a pre-trained decoder-only LLM into a tool-free, catalog-grounded generator. NEO represents items using semantic identifiers (SIDs) and trains a single model to interleave natural language and typed item identifiers within a shared sequence. Natural-language prompts control the task, target entity type, and output format (IDs, text, or mixed), while constrained decoding guarantees catalog-valid item generation without restricting free-form text. We refer to this instruction-conditioned controllability as language-steerability . Inspired by multimodal alignment, we treat SIDs as a distinct modality and study design choices for integrating discrete entity representations into LLMs via staged alignment and instruction tuning. We evaluate NEO at scale on a real-world catalog of over 10M items across multiple media types and discovery tasks, including recommendation, search, and user understanding. In offline experiments, NEO consistently outperforms strong task-specific baselines and exhibits positive cross-task transfer, demonstrating a practical path toward consolidating large-scale discovery capabilities into a single language-steerable generative model.

NEO

In contrast to prior work that applies SIDs within LLMs primarily for recommendation, we introduce a typed mixed-sequence catalog interface and a staged alignment recipe. 3.
Methodology

Figure 2.
We illustrate our approach as the first three stages in a more general four-stage pipeline. In the general case, in Stage 1, semantic representations are learned—such as semantic IDs (SIDs)—to capture entities, their structure, and relationships. In Stage 2, domain grounding is performed via one or both of (i) adaptation over user-behavior sequences and (ii) domain alignment between SID/text pairs, to learn a shared latent space between entities and natural language. This enables the model to use text and SIDs interchangeably and to learn sequential, domain-relevant concepts. Stage 3 teaches the model to answer questions, follow commands, and generate grounded outputs through multi-task instruction-following. We prioritize diversity of tasks over depth within any single task. In Stage 4, we may leverage task-specific datasets and objectives related to the targeted use case (e.g., topic discovery and business constraints) via task-specific fine-tuning, reinforcement learning, and related techniques. We indicate typical choices for which parameters are kept frozen (ice) or trainable (fire) for each stage.

Figure
2
presents a general staged pipeline for training a domain-adapted, fully self-contained LLM, of which NEO instantiates the first three stages. Stage 1 learns SIDs as discrete token sequences representing catalog entities and their semantic structure. Stage 2 grounds these identifiers by aligning SID-text pairs (and optionally adapting over user-behavior sequences) to learn a shared space where text and SIDs are interchangeable, supporting mixed text–SID sequences and sequential domain signals. Stage 3 then induces discovery capabilities via multi-task instruction-following, prioritizing breadth over single-task specialization, whereas an optional Stage 4 captures subsequent task-specific objective optimization using larger datasets and specialized objectives (e.g., business-constraint and product-specific tuning, or reinforcement learning).
We compare with alternative training strategies, including continuous pre-training (CPT)-based approaches, in Section
4.2
.

NEO adapts a pre-trained open-weight decoder-only LLM to operate jointly over natural language and discrete item identifiers, while preserving the standard next-token prediction objective and requiring no architectural modifications (e.g., no bespoke attention masks, cross-attention modules, or specialized losses). Text inputs are tokenized using the native tokenizer of the backbone LLM, while catalog items are represented as short sequences of SID tokens obtained by discretizing item embeddings. Both text tokens and SID tokens are embedded into a single shared sequence and processed by the same decoder stack, enabling the model to reason uniformly over language and items.

To reliably integrate this extended vocabulary and align item identifiers with linguistic representations, NEO is trained using a three-stage recipe, as shown in
Figure

2
. First, item content is converted into SIDs through embedding discretization (Stage 1). Second, the newly introduced SID tokens are aligned with the LLM’s embedding space (Stage 2). Third, the model is instruction-tuned on a mixture of discovery tasks, including recommendation, retrieval, recommendation explanations, and user interest analysis (Stage 3).

3.1.
Semantic IDs

In Stage 1, we focus on constructing discrete item representations that can be used directly by an LLM. We represent each catalog item
e
with
\mathrm{SID}(e)
: a short sequence of
M
discrete tokens, each with
K
possible values, that compactly encodes the semantic content of the item. Intuitively, SIDs provide a discrete token-level abstraction of items that preserves semantic neighborhood structure while remaining compatible with autoregressive generation. See Appendix
C
for formal details.

We construct SIDs by discretizing content-based item embeddings in a hierarchical fashion using residual quantizers (e.g.,
(
19
;
25
)
), inducing a coarse-to-fine decomposition: earlier codes capture broad semantic regions, and later codes refine within-region distinctions. This property aligns naturally with autoregressive generation, where tokens are produced sequentially.
We design the discretization to ensure semantic coherence, stability under distribution shift (e.g., seasonality of content consumption and shifts in interests), and compatibility with large heterogeneous catalogs.

For heterogeneous entities, embeddings of different item types may lie in different latent spaces, thus requiring multiple quantizers.

Using SIDs in the LLM

To integrate SIDs and natural language, we define a unified vocabulary
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{V}_{\text{text}}\cup\mathcal{V}_{\text{SID}}
. We extend the LLM’s base vocabulary by
M\times K
SID tokens, which are randomly initialized, and add the corresponding parameters to the LLM head when their weights are untied.
SIDs are interleaved with text in both inputs and outputs. Following delimiter-based conventions commonly used in Multimodal LLMs (MLLMs) (e.g.,
(
58
;
22
;
36
)
) item references are emitted only within explicit item spans:
\langle\text{text}\rangle\ \texttt{[SID]}\ \langle c_{1}\ \ldots\ c_{M}\rangle\ \texttt{[/SID]}\ \langle\text{text}\rangle
. This design allows the model to freely interleave natural language and item references while enabling constrained decoding within SID spans.

Domain Grounding: Alignment

Stage 2 aligns SIDs with the LLM’s linguistic embedding space. This stage is inspired by MLLMs, where non-textual modalities are represented either as discrete tokens

MLLMs are typically trained using staged recipes that introduce a new modality, align it with language, and then apply instruction tuning.
Following this paradigm, we treat SIDs as a discrete “foreign-language” modality and learn a bidirectional mapping between SID sequences and natural language. This enables the model to both

Bidirectional alignment objectives.

(1)

SID

Given one or more SID spans (optionally with a lightweight instruction), the model predicts a natural-language descriptor:
\langle\mathrm{prompt}(s_{e})\rangle\rightarrow\langle t_{e}\rangle

(2)

Given a natural-language query

\langle\mathrm{prompt}(q)\rangle\rightarrow\texttt{[SID]}\ \langle s_{e}\rangle\ \texttt{[/SID]}

(3)

SID
\rightarrow

Given one or more SID spans (optionally with a lightweight instruction), the model predicts the item type:
\langle\mathrm{prompt}(s_{e})\rangle\rightarrow\langle c_{e}\rangle
.

Together, these objectives encourage the model to align SIDs with language at the levels of semantics, retrieval, and type structure.

Parameterization and stability.

To prevent catastrophic forgetting during the alignment phase, we freeze the pre-trained backbone parameters and optimize only: (i) the input embeddings corresponding to newly introduced SID tokens, and (ii) the output weights used to predict SID tokens (the SID-specific logits in the LLM head). This design preserves the linguistic competence of the backbone while enabling effective alignment between SIDs and natural language.

3.3.
Capability Induction via Instruction tuning

Stage 3 trains the domain-grounded model to perform personalization tasks and discovery under explicit control via natural-language instructions, while producing (i) SIDs, (ii) text, or (iii) mixed SIDs–text outputs. We unfreeze and fine-tune all model parameters. Alternatively, Stage 3 can be implemented using parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods (e.g., LoRA
(
16
)
) when full fine-tuning is impractical. In this paper, we focus on simply introducing a large variety of types of tasks to the model including text-based retrieval, recommendation, and text generation across heterogeneous items. We save for future work demonstrating the ability to generalize in this stage to new, zero-shot
domain
capabilities.

We train NEO using supervised instruction tuning over the unified vocabulary
\mathcal{V}
, where each training example is a prefix–completion pair
(x,y)
. The prefix
x
is the input context and includes the system prompt and user instruction, optionally augmented with lightweight user features and/or item references expressed as SID spans. The completion
y
is the model output and may contain natural language, SID spans, or both. During training, we apply the loss only to tokens in
y
, masking all tokens in
x
.
We restrict
x
to single-turn interactions to simplify evaluation; the same formulation extends directly to multi-turn dialogues by concatenating the full conversation history (
(x,y)
pairs) and masking all user messages.

Task families

All tasks are described through prompts that follow a consistent schema that explicitly specifies (i) the task (recommend, retrieve, explain, understand), (ii) the target item type, and (iii) the desired output format (SID-only, text-only, or mixed). This explicit conditioning enables fine-grained control over model behavior using natural-language instructions.

The tasks are formulated as next-token prediction over mixed text–SID sequences that may involve items from multiple types:

•

Next-item recommendation.
Given an instruction, lightweight user or context features, and a short interaction history represented as SIDs, the model predicts the next consumed item of the specified type as a SID.

•

Text-based retrieval.
Given a natural-language query (optionally with lightweight user context), the model generates the SID(s) of the most relevant item(s).

•

Grounded mixed generation (recommendation + recs-planation).
The model generates a recommended item SID together with a natural-language justification grounded in the user’s history and the recommended item(s).

•

User understanding (interest profiling).
Given a user’s interaction history expressed as SIDs, the model generates a short natural-language description of the user’s interests.

In settings where natural-language data is unavailable (e.g., user interest profiles), we prompt a stronger LLM to distill synthetic supervision for NEO’s training.

3.4.
Inference

Because multi-token SIDs induce a large combinatorial output space (e.g., a 4-token SID with
K{=}256
yields
256^{4}
possible sequences), we constrain decoding so that generated SIDs always map to existing catalog items. We precompute all valid SID tuples and store them in a prefix trie. During autoregressive decoding, we apply trie-based masking only within
[SID]
…
[/SID]
spans, restricting each next token to trie-consistent continuations. Outside SID spans, we restrict generation to text tokens (and
[SID]
token), which prevents accidental SID token emission in free-form text while preserving the ability to introduce item references when needed.

The mapping from items to SID tuples is not necessarily one-to-one, as multiple items may share the same SID. We resolve such collisions by mapping each SID tuple to a single canonical item, selected based on multiple possible heuristics (e.g. item popularity, recency).
For retrieval tasks, we generate multiple candidate items by applying beam search over SID tokens.

4.1.2.
Semantic ID creation (Stage 1)

Each item is represented using its available content and metadata. For artists, we encode tracks from track spectrograms into audio embeddings and average them by artist to obtain artist-level vectors.
For shows, episodes, and audiobooks, we embed title and description text using the Qwen3 embeddings (8B) pre-trained text model

4.1.3.
Domain Alignment (Stage 2)

We construct paired alignment examples linking each item SID

4.1.4.
Capability Induction via Instruction Tuning (Stage 3)

This stage fine-tunes the aligned model on multiple supervised instruction-following discovery tasks. All prompts use a consistent instruction-tuning format specifying task, target item type, and output constraints (SID-only, text-only, or mixed). Prompt templates are provided in
Appendix

G
.

Next-item(s) recommendation.:

Each example consists of an instruction, user context, and target label. User context includes recent interaction history represented as SIDs. For audiobooks and shows, we include recent cross-item types activity, following
(
4
)
.

Text-based retrieval.:

We build a dataset mapping natural-language queries to the SID of the most relevant item of a specified type, using engagement logs as supervision. Prompts may optionally include lightweight user context.

User understanding (interest profiling).:

Explicit natural-language supervision for user interest profiling is typically unavailable. We therefore create training targets via distillation from a larger LLM (32B parameters), which is prompted with textual descriptions of a user’s consumed items to generate a concise interest summary. We then replace textual item representations with SIDs and train NEO to generate the same summary directly from the SID-based history, without intermediate chain-of-thought.

Recommendation + explanation (“recsplanation”).:

We build a mixed output dataset where the target consists of (i) a recommended item SID and (ii) a natural-language justification grounded in the user’s history and the recommended item. Rationales are obtained via distillation from a larger LLM: for each user sequence, we sample a cut point
t

5.
Conclusions

We introduced NEO, a tool-free framework for adapting decoder-only LLMs to operate over large, heterogeneous catalogs by interleaving natural language with semantic identifiers and enforcing catalog-valid generation via prefix-trie–constrained decoding. At a scale exceeding 10M items, NEO consistently outperforms strong production baselines for recommendation and text-based retrieval, while remaining language-steerable and supporting mixed ID–text outputs for explanations and user understanding.

Beyond empirical gains, our design analysis highlights two key principles for grounding LLMs in large discrete domains: the importance of high quality semantically structured identifiers over atomic IDs, and the role of a staged alignment recipe in balancing item grounding with retention of general language competence, key for tasks involving mixed text-SIDs in output. Our results show that naive alternatives—such as random tokenization, collapsing alignment stages, or continuous pre-training—either degrade performance or compromise language capabilities, especially for tasks that require instruction following and mixed text–item generation.

More broadly, we view NEO as an instance of a general approach to building fully self-contained, domain-adapted LLMs that reason jointly over entities, user behavior, and language without relying on external tools or task-specific pipelines. We believe the framework and empirical insights presented here provide practical guidance for extending LLMs to other large-scale discovery settings involving heterogeneous entities and steerable generative behavior.

Appendix C
Semantic ID Formal Details

Formally, given an item embedding
x_{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we map it to an SID defined as

(3)

\mathrm{SID}(e)=(c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{M}),\qquad c_{m}\in\{1,\ldots,K\}.

where
K
denotes the number of clusters per position
M\ll K
to ensure meaningful semantic partitioning. Thus, each SID is an ordered tuple of discrete codes drawn from
M
codebooks.

Residual Quantization

NEO is agnostic to a specific quantizer method used to construct SIDs. However, in this work we focus on residual quantizers, which often induces a coarse-to-fine decomposition: earlier codes capture broad semantic regions, and later codes refine within-region distinctions. This property aligns naturally with autoregressive generation, where tokens are produced sequentially.
Specifically, we adopt Residual
K
-means
(
19
)
for its flexibility and empirical stability, particularly compared to alternatives such as RQ-VAE, which often require multiple auxiliary losses to avoid poor local minima
(
75
;
33
;
18
)
.

Residual
k
-means represents each embedding as a sequence of codebook indices by iteratively quantizing the residual error. Concretely, we maintain
M
codebooks
\{C_{1},\ldots,C_{M}\}
, each containing
K
centroids
\{\mu_{m,1},\ldots,\mu_{m,K}\}
. Starting from the initial residual
r_{0}=x_{e}
, we select the closest centroid at each stage

(4)

\displaystyle c_{m}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{j\in\{1,\ldots,K\}}\|r_{m-1}-\mu_{m,j}\|_{2}^{2},

and update the residual as:

(5)

\displaystyle r_{m}

\displaystyle=r_{m-1}-\mu_{m,c_{m}},\qquad m=1,\ldots,M.

Choosing SID Length and Codebook Size

The SID length and codebook size
K
trade off addressability, semantic coherence, and sequence length. Longer SIDs increase addressability but inflate prompt length, while shorter SIDs risk semantic collisions. We guide this choice using two criteria: (i) collision rate, the fraction of SID tuples assigned to multiple items; and (ii) prefix coherence, the average cosine similarity between items sharing the same SID prefix. We select
M
and
K
to minimize collisions while maintaining high semantic coherence for short prefixes, enabling reliable generation without excessive sequence overhead.

Item embeddings from content

The quality of SIDs depends critically on the underlying item embedding
x_{e}
. Such
embeddings may be derived from collaborative signals
(
57
;
54
)
, content metadata
(
43
;
47
)
, or hybrid representations
(
13
;
21
;
15
)
, provided they induce a meaningful semantic geometry over items.

In practice, item representations must remain stable under distribution shift. In domains with strong seasonality and rapidly evolving trends (e.g., music, video, or spoken audio), embeddings derived from collaborative signals can drift quickly, causing an item to be reassigned different identifiers over short time spans. This drift may require frequent retraining of both LLM and the quantizer, or the introduction of additional stabilization mechanisms. Moreover, collaborative signals are often sparse or unavailable for cold-start items.
For these reasons, NEO constructs SIDs using content-based item representations, and defers delegates the incorporation of collaborative signals to later instruction-tuning stages, where the LLM can learn to leverage user interaction history directly.

Appendix D
CF-based Semantic IDs stability

We have analyzed the stability of our CF-based embeddings by sampling 1,000,000 random artists, computed each artist’s top-50 nearest neighbors under the CF embedding space, and measured neighborhood overlap across time using Jaccard similarity.
Over a 3-week horizon, the median overlap is 0.59 (Jaccard@50), corresponding to roughly 37 out of 50 neighbors retained; the least stable decile has overlap 0.37, corresponding to roughly 27 out of 50 neighbors retained.

CF-based SIDs may exhibit temporal instability due to evolving user behavior. This instability suggests that CF-based tokenizations may require frequent retraining of the quantizer and the LLM, or explicit stabilization mechanisms (e.g., anchoring or temporal regularization), to function as reliable identifiers in fast-moving catalogs.

Semantic ID token embeddings for k-NN.

To connect NEO-trained generative models with embedding-based retrieval, we explored whether the learned SID token embeddings can be used to construct an item embedding space for approximate
k
-NN. After training Qwen3-0.6B with NEO, we represent each item by the concatenation (or mean) of the
M
learned input embeddings corresponding to its SID tokens. We then build a simple episode-retrieval recommender where each user is represented by the average embedding of episodes streamed in the previous 30 days, and recommendations are obtained via approximate
k
-NN in the resulting embedding space.

In our experiments, this approach significantly underperforms the production retrieval baseline and the generative NEO model. A likely explanation is that concatenating token embeddings yields very high-dimensional item vectors (e.g.,
M{=}4
and hidden size 1024
\Rightarrow
4096 dimensions), which can make nearest-neighbor retrieval less reliable without additional metric learning or dimensionality reduction. We also evaluated alternative text-based embedding models (e.g., WordLlama
(
35
)
) for item representations, but did not observe improvements. We leave a more principled study of deriving retrieval-friendly embeddings from SID-token representations (e.g., projection layers, pooling/whitening, contrastive fine-tuning, or mixed models
(
1
)
) to future work.

Appendix H
LSH

For any embedding vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{embed}}
, a hash function
h_{r}(\mathbf{x})
is defined as:

(6)

h_{r}(\mathbf{x})=\text{sgn}(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x})

where
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{embed}}
is a random hyperplane vector sampled from a standard Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,I)
. To improve precision and reduce false positives, we concatenate
K
such independent hash functions to form a
K
-bit signature:

(7)

H(\mathbf{x})=[h_{1}(\mathbf{x}),h_{2}(\mathbf{x}),\dots,h_{K}(\mathbf{x})]

The probability that two vectors
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{v}
result in the same hash bit for a single random projection is a function of the angle
\theta
between them:

(8)

P(h_{r}(\mathbf{u})=h_{r}(\mathbf{v}))=1-\frac{\theta(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})}{\pi}
