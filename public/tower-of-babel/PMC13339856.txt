Work–life balance and mental and social well-being among chiropractic educators in Malaysia: A qualitative study
Authors: Yi Kai Wong, Samuel Min Qian Heu, Yu Hang Chua
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study explored chiropractic educators’ perceptions of work–life balance and its perceived implications for mental and social well-being in Malaysia.MethodsA descriptive qualitative study was conducted using purposive sampling of full-time chiropractic educators with varied gender, academic experience, administrative responsibility, and clinical supervision load. Ten faculty members consented to participate and completed semistructured interviews conducted in person or by videoconference. Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis.ResultsSeven themes were identified: blurred boundaries and overwork; role identity tension across educator, clinician, researcher, and student roles; collegial support as a buffer; community belonging, ranging from solidarity to estrangement; boundary negotiation and personal rules; cultural and interpersonal friction; and institutional friction tied to administrative intensification and leadership signals. These were organized into 4 domains: work–life balance, social well-being, mental well-being, and institutional support.ConclusionChiropractic educators in Malaysia described blurred work–life boundaries, competing role demands, and institutional and cultural factors that shaped their mental and social well-being. Experiences of collegial support and boundary negotiation varied across participants and influenced perceived capacity for recovery and social connection. Together, these findings highlight the interplay between individual practices and institutional contexts in shaping educator well-being.

INTRODUCTION

Health professions faculty often fulfill multiple roles in teaching, research, clinical supervision, administration, and student support. This role multiplicity increases time pressure and emotional labor and can undermine work–life balance when institutional resources and norms do not keep pace with demands, often leading to long work hours that encroach on personal and family time.1–4 Work–life balance is a dynamic state in which individuals manage work and nonwork roles in ways that sustain performance and satisfaction across domains.5 In higher education, chronic overload, extensive compliance requirements, and relentless meeting schedules have been linked to elevated stress, reduced job satisfaction, and intentions to leave academia.6,7 A survey in the United Kingdom reported that a large majority of higher education staff rated their job as stressful.6 Recent analyses of the “great resignation” in higher education and surveys among academic physicians further highlight that work-related strain and diminished well-being are directly associated with faculty burnout and intentions to quit.8,9 These findings emphasize that challenges in managing workload and work–life boundaries not only affect well-being but also contribute to attrition pressures facing academic institutions. Chiropractic educators face additional clinical and accreditation duties that can further blur work–life boundaries, especially in programs in which faculty supervise student clinics while also delivering coursework and contributing to research and governance.

According to the World Health Organization, mental health is a state of well-being that enables people to cope with the stresses of life, realize their abilities, learn and work well, and contribute to their community.10 This universal definition aligns with psychological perspectives that emphasize both hedonic and eudaimonic aspects, including positive affect, vitality, autonomy, environmental mastery, purpose in life, and positive relationships.11–13 Social well-being refers to effective functioning within social networks and communities across dimensions of integration, contribution, coherence, acceptance, and actualization.14 In an academic environment, mental well-being supports motivation and cognitive performance by sustaining attention, working memory, and intrinsic drive to engage with learning and research tasks.10,15 In contrast, social well-being fosters teamwork, collegial support, and organizational commitment by enhancing trust, reciprocity, and a sense of belonging within academic communities.14,16,17 These facets of well-being are interdependent in academic life. Meaningful work and a sense of collegial belonging can buffer stress and protect engagement, whereas sustained overload and perceived unfairness in the workplace can erode mental health and social connection.2,16,18,19 For example, faculty who find their work meaningful and feel supported by colleagues may be more resilient to stress, but if they perceive organizational injustice or a lack of support, burnout and social withdrawal can occur.2,18,19

Whereas our interviews did not specifically focus on the COVID-19 pandemic period, the stressors described by participants resonate with broader accounts of how remote and hybrid working arrangements since 2020 have altered the work–home interface. Studies across higher education note that increased digital availability, extended videoconferencing, and blurred physical boundaries introduced qualitatively different stressors compared with prepandemic patterns.20 These shifts amplified the relevance of boundary management and collegial support and suggest that the experiences of chiropractic educators in Malaysia are embedded within wider global transformations in academic work.

Several theoretical frameworks help explain how work–life conflict relates to mental and social well-being in academic contexts. Among these, the job demands–resources (JD-R) model is particularly useful for this study. The JD-R framework proposes that high and fluctuating job demands, such as heavy teaching loads, clinical supervision, and administrative responsibilities, consume energy and contribute to strain, whereas job resources, including autonomy, collegial support, and a sense of meaning, help sustain motivation and buffer stress.3,4,21–24 This balance of demands and resources is central to understanding how chiropractic educators manage competing responsibilities and how these dynamics affect well-being. Work–family conflict theory also informs this relationship, distinguishing time-based, strain-based, and behavior-based conflicts that arise when work roles spill into personal and family life.25 Boundary management theories further clarify how faculty navigate the borders between professional and personal roles. When segmentation preferences, predictable schedules, and clear institutional norms are present, role interference is reduced; however, constant availability expectations and after-hours communication create “always-on” cultures that erode recovery and increase work-home conflict.26,27 In the chiropractic context, in which educators often combine teaching, clinical supervision, and administrative tasks, these boundary challenges can be pronounced with after-hours patient-related queries and governance requirements intruding into personal time. Finally, stress-recovery models emphasize that psychological detachment from work during off hours is essential for restoring energy and preventing burnout, highlighting why boundary-respectful norms and opportunities for recovery are critical to sustaining educator well-being.19,28

Contextual factors at the disciplinary, institutional, and national levels shape the dynamics of work–life balance, boundary negotiation, and well-being among faculty.6,7,26,27 At the national level, cultural expectations around hierarchy and availability may influence how faculty manage work and home boundaries. In contexts in which respect for hierarchy implies immediate responsiveness to senior staff or in which availability norms extend to evenings and weekends, educators may feel pressure to remain constantly accessible. Such expectations can undermine boundary setting and increase role conflict as documented in cross-cultural studies.26,27,29

At the disciplinary level, chiropractic educators in Malaysia are expected to meet international standards for primary contact musculoskeletal care while also fulfilling the teaching and research productivity requirements of their higher education institutions.30–32 This dual expectation adds to the complexity of their roles as educators must balance global accreditation benchmarks with local institutional demands. In parallel, evidence from Klang Valley cohorts indicates that chiropractic service utilization and public awareness are increasing but remain uneven.33,34 This uneven professional landscape can heighten expectations for educators to contribute simultaneously to academic delivery, clinical credibility, and public or professional advocacy, thereby further intensifying the dual academic–professional demands of their roles.

At the institutional level, higher education educators in Malaysia are frequently tasked with quality assurance and clinical governance responsibilities in addition to teaching, research, and professional practice. These legitimate requirements include detailed record-keeping, audits, and documentation.30,32 Without adequate staffing and administrative support, such responsibilities often extend into evenings and weekends and frequently coincide with peak teaching or examination periods, thereby compounding strain on faculty work–life balance and well-being. In parallel, global efforts to harmonize chiropractic accreditation standards continue to codify program requirements and evidence expectations, adding further layers to faculty responsibilities.30

Internationally, particularly in the United Kingdom higher education sector, broader trends document a similar intensification of administrative work and its negative effects on staff well-being and retention.6,7 Conversely, organizational research indicates that well-being can be improved through the redesign of work processes, supportive leadership behaviors, and a strong collegial culture that protects recovery time and fosters belonging.2,4,18,35 Academic health centers that implement transparent workload models, encourage reasonable scheduling and email practices, and promote supportive supervision tend to report lower burnout and higher job engagement among faculty.2,4,18,35

Despite growing attention to clinician and educator well-being, there is a paucity of qualitative research on chiropractic faculty in Southeast Asia. Recent synthesis of chiropractic education scholarship highlights uneven coverage and limited qualitative inquiry, underscoring the need for context-specific studies.36 Improving faculty work–life balance is not only vital for educator health but may also enhance retention and educational quality. To address this gap, the present study aims to explore chiropractic educators’ perceptions of work–life balance and its perceived implications for their mental and social well-being. The findings are intended to provide practical, context-appropriate insights into the individual, team, and institutional levels that could improve work–life balance and support sustainable academic careers for chiropractic faculty.

METHODS

Design

This study used a descriptive qualitative design to explore participants’ experiences and perceptions. Reflexive thematic analysis guided data interpretation, following the 6-phase approach of Braun and Clarke.37

Ethics Approval and Consent to Participate

Ethics approval was obtained from the IMU Joint Committee on Research and Ethics, Malaysia (Research project no. BCh I/2024(23)). Participants received written information and provided written informed consent before data collection.

Participants and Sampling

The study was conducted at a Malaysian university offering an accredited chiropractic program. Eligible participants were full-time chiropractic educators with at least 6 months in their academic role. Inclusion criteria were full-time employment as a chiropractic faculty member in Malaysia and proficiency in English. Individuals on sabbatical or extended leave or those serving only in short-term adjunct roles without academic or supervisory duties were excluded. Purposive sampling sought maximum variation in gender, years in academic roles, levels of administrative responsibility, and clinical supervision load to capture diverse perspectives. Ten faculty members consented to participate and completed the interviews. Recruitment continued until no new codes or concepts emerged in 2 consecutive interviews, indicating thematic saturation.38

Data Collection

Semistructured interviews were conducted face to face or via secure videoconference, according to participant preference. Each interview lasted 45–70 minutes. All interviews were audio-recorded with consent and transcribed verbatim. A semistructured interview guide was used to ensure consistency in topics while allowing participants to elaborate freely. The guide included open-ended prompts on work–life balance, boundary management strategies, mental well-being, social connectedness and belonging, sources of work-related strain and support, and perceived avenues for change. The guide is available as a Supplementary File. Field notes were recorded during and after interviews to capture contextual observations and initial reflections.

Data Analysis

Transcripts were imported into NVivo 14 (QSR International) to support data management and coding and analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis following the 6-phase approach of Braun and Clarke.37 To enhance reflexivity and consistency, 2 researchers independently coded an initial subset of transcripts and then compared coding to develop a shared framework. One researcher subsequently coded all transcripts, iteratively refining codes and aggregating them into potential themes. Candidate themes were reviewed and refined through constant comparison across transcripts with particular attention to negative cases. The researchers created thematic maps and maintained analytic memos to explore relationships between themes and to document reflexive insights. Themes were defined and named once they were internally coherent and conceptually distinct. Participant quotes were extracted to illustrate each theme with identifying details removed to ensure confidentiality.

Trustworthiness

Multiple strategies were employed to enhance trustworthiness. For credibility, investigator triangulation was achieved through collaborative coding discussions, and discrepant (“negative”) cases were actively sought to test and refine the themes. A detailed audit trail of coding decisions, theme development, and memo reflections was maintained to support dependability and confirmability. The research team comprised both faculty and student researchers from the same institution. Whereas this insider position provided contextual familiarity, none of the researchers held direct supervisory or evaluative authority over participants, reducing risks of coercion. Reflexive memoing was used to document assumptions, examine potential power dynamics, and monitor interpretive influence throughout analysis. Transferability was supported through rich descriptions of the study context, use of participant quotations, and purposive sampling to capture educators with varied roles and responsibilities.

RESULTS

Participant Characteristics

Ten participants met the inclusion criteria of being full-time chiropractic faculty in Malaysia and proficient in English. Because the small sample was drawn from a limited population of chiropractic academics within a single university, reporting aggregate demographic characteristics could increase the risk of deductive disclosure. Therefore, to protect participants’ anonymity and confidentiality, demographic details are not reported.

Thematic Analysis

Seven interrelated themes described chiropractic educators’ perceptions of work–life balance and its perceived implications for their mental and social well-being. To enhance coherence between the interview framework and the emergent analysis, these themes were organized into 4 overarching domains: work–life balance, social well-being, mental well-being, and institutional support. Quotations are identified by respondent number.

Work–Life Balance

Blurred boundaries and cyclic overwork were frequently reported with academic tasks spilling into evenings and weekends, particularly during teaching peaks and accreditation activities. One educator explained, “Office-related matters are usually 4–5 pm; however, sometimes I could go up to 9 pm depending on the parties involved in the work” (respondent 10). Another noted, “There is a need to do certain administrative task after work hours, hence affecting work–life balance” (respondent 3). Cycles of fatigue were also described: “Sometimes I don’t sleep at night as I need to get work done” (respondent 6).

Role identity tension emerged as educators juggled responsibilities across teaching, clinical practice, research, and in some cases postgraduate study. One participant said, “I am currently studying my master’s degree, seeing patients…and I have my thesis… So I don’t think I have a work–life balance” (respondent 4).

Boundary negotiation was another key theme with some participants establishing strict personal rules to manage workload. For example, 1 educator stated, “I don’t reply to emails or calls after 5.30 pm” (respondent 5), whereas another emphasized keeping weekends free: “I am…strict to not work…especially on Saturdays or Sundays” (respondent 9). Others reflected on the drawbacks of being perpetually available: colleagues “know that I am always available, which is not a good example to set” (respondent 6).

Social Well-Being

Collegial support was widely recognized as a buffer against overload though unevenly distributed. Some participants highlighted the value of supportive colleagues: “Overall…there are people there to support, it is just about whether you have found the right people to assist you” (respondent 10). Others described the limits of help due to competing schedules: “There are days where we ask for help…and we can’t really get it…due to tight schedules” (respondent 2).

Community belonging varied. For some, shared strain strengthened solidarity: “A lot of people are in the same situation; hence, the engagement connects everyone” (respondent 3). For others, workload reduced interaction: “I have very little interaction with any of my colleagues… I have not seen some of my colleagues for 8 months” (respondent 5). A minority expressed clear nonbelonging: “Absolutely not. Absolutely none” (respondent 5).

Mental Well-Being

Mental well-being was shaped by cycles of fatigue, stress, and limited recovery. Participants described missed sleep and cumulative exhaustion during peak workload periods. The strain of managing stacked roles, including postgraduate study and clinical responsibilities, was cited as a major stressor (respondents 1, 4, 6). Whereas some found personal boundary strategies helpful in sustaining recovery, others experienced ongoing challenges in detaching from work demands.

Institutional Support

Cultural and interpersonal friction influenced how boundaries were negotiated. Expectations of constant availability or hierarchical responsiveness sometimes clashed with individual preferences: “It’s a cultural thing…just the way it is” (respondent 6). Communication styles were also a source of tension: “Socially… I don’t pull any punches…not always well regarded…probably…cultural influence” (respondent 5).

Institutional friction, particularly administrative intensification and leadership signals, was a major theme. Participants described “layers and layers of administration which must be coped with” (respondent 6) as well as high stress during accreditation cycles with limited support and tight deadlines (respondent 10). Some noted inconsistencies between wellness rhetoric and workload practices: “They would say they provide support…but in the meantime, they’re still dumping work on us” (respondent 6). Calls for clearer guidance, process alignment, and supportive leadership were frequent.

Cross-Domain Summary

Across these domains, educators reported that fluctuating demands and administrative intensification increased after-hours spillover, whereas personal boundary strategies provided partial protection. Collegial support and belonging functioned as key resources though unevenly accessible. Cultural norms and leadership practices strongly influenced whether boundaries were respected and whether well-being was sustained.

DISCUSSION

This study explored chiropractic educators’ perceptions of work–life balance and its perceived implications for mental and social well-being in Malaysia. The 7 themes were organized into 4 domains—work–life balance, social well-being, mental well-being, and institutional support—each of which reflected established accounts of faculty work and well-being in higher education.

Work–Life Balance

Participants described blurred boundaries, cyclic overwork, and role identity tension as central stressors. These dynamics reflect classic time-based and strain-based conflicts identified in work–family conflict theory.25 Consistent with the JD-R model, high and fluctuating demands, such as teaching peaks, clinical supervision, and administrative requirements, depleted energy and contributed to strain, whereas resources such as autonomy and meaningful work sustained engagement.3,4,21–24,39 Reports that after-hours communication and administrative intensification extended the working day are consistent with higher education surveys linking compliance intensification to stress and intentions to leave.6,7 These findings mirror recent analyses of burnout and attrition in academia, including the “great resignation” among faculty and academic physicians,8,9 underscoring the urgency of redesigning workloads and leadership practices. Boundary management was a key process: Faculty who established explicit availability rules or protected weekends described better preservation of personal time, echoing stressor-detachment models that link off-hours recovery to reduced fatigue and improved functioning.19,28,40

Social Well-Being

Collegial support and community belonging emerged as unevenly distributed but powerful resources. Supportive colleagues and brief routine interactions lifted morale, aligning with evidence on organizational support, fairness, and the affective benefits of weak-tie social contact.16–18,41 At the same time, lack of access to help and limited interaction due to workload left some participants isolated, and cultural or interpersonal friction sometimes undermined collegial belonging. These findings highlight the importance of fostering an inclusive and supportive academic community, in which reciprocity and belonging buffer stress and sustain commitment.

Mental Well-Being

Cycles of fatigue, missed recovery, and cumulative strain shaped participants’ mental well-being. Struggles to balance postgraduate study, clinical supervision, and academic roles reflected the depletion of personal resources predicted by the JD-R framework. Although some educators employed recovery strategies, such as segmenting weekends or triaging messages, others found it difficult to detach from work. This reinforces evidence that sustainable well-being requires more than individual coping strategies; structural supports, workload management, and clear institutional norms are essential to enable psychological recovery and protect against burnout.19,28

Institutional Support

Cultural expectations around hierarchy and availability influenced boundary negotiation, sometimes leading to “always-on” expectations. Institutional friction was also prominent with administrative intensification, accreditation cycles, and inconsistent leadership signals extending work into evenings and weekends. These findings echo organizational research showing that work design and supervisory practices are critical levers for staff well-being.2,4,35 In the Malaysian context, accreditation and governance are legitimate responsibilities aimed at safety and quality;30–32,42,43 however, without commensurate staffing and clear processes, they risk overburdening faculty. Sustainable improvement, therefore, requires system-level changes, including transparent workload models, meeting and assessment calendars that minimize peak congestion, and leadership that models boundary-respectful practices.

Taken together, the results support a multilevel approach. At the individual level, recovery practices and mindful boundary management can help, but these must complement structural reforms.19,28,40 At the team and program levels, shared coverage arrangements, workload transparency, and scheduling adjustments can reduce after-hours spillover.2,4,35 At the institutional level, leadership development and system redesign are essential to foster fair processes, protect recovery time, and enhance retention.2,4,18,35

Future Studies

Future studies should follow educators longitudinally across academic cycles to examine how changes in workload design, scheduling, and leadership practices shape well-being, retention, and educational quality. Because this study was conducted in a single program, comparative research across multiple institutions and countries is particularly important to determine whether the challenges identified here are specific to the local context or reflect broader sector-wide patterns in health professions education. Applied research could also evaluate practical strategies such as within-hours communication defaults, scheduling models that avoid assessment peaks, protected time for scholarship, and shared coverage arrangements to test their feasibility and impact on faculty well-being. Brief recovery practices tailored to academic rhythms may be explored alongside structural reforms to better understand their role in sustaining energy and preventing burnout. Mixed-methods designs that integrate interviews with administrative workload data, email volume metrics, and student outcomes would strengthen inference and implementation guidance. Finally, codesigned research with faculty, leaders, and students can improve feasibility by aligning proposed changes with actual workload practices, enhance acceptability by incorporating stakeholder priorities and cultural expectations, and support sustainability by fostering shared ownership and institutional commitment.

Strengths

This study provides in-depth qualitative insights into work–life balance and well-being among chiropractic educators in Malaysia, a group that is underrepresented in the literature. Use of reflexive thematic analysis enabled a nuanced examination of experiences across mental, social, and institutional domains. Purposive sampling enhanced variation in academic roles and responsibilities, whereas rich participant quotations support transparency and analytic credibility.

Limitations

This qualitative study was conducted in a single national context and 1 chiropractic program, which may limit transferability. Participation was voluntary, and this may have favored individuals with particularly strong views about work–life balance. Data were cross-sectional and self-reported, so causal inferences cannot be drawn, and responses may reflect recall or social desirability bias. Organizational documents and workload records were not analyzed, and this precluded triangulation between perceived and documented demands. To protect anonymity in a small cohort, certain aggregates were withheld, reducing descriptive detail but minimizing deductive disclosure risk. Although recruitment continued until no new codes or concepts emerged in 2 consecutive interviews, claims of theme completeness in reflexive thematic analysis should be interpreted cautiously. The mix of in-person and videoconference interviews may also have influenced rapport and disclosure.

CONCLUSION

This study found that work–life balance among chiropractic educators in Malaysia is shaped by the interplay of high and fluctuating work demands, personal boundary practices, collegial relationships, and local institutional processes. Participants described after-hours spillover and administrative intensification as constraining recovery and social participation, whereas collegial support, a sense of belonging, and explicit availability rules buffered strain and supported well-being.
