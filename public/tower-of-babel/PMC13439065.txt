Attention modulates value normalization in human reinforcement learning by shaping reward encoding
Authors: Romane Cecchi, Sebastian Gluth, Stefano Palminteri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Contextual valuation is a well-documented phenomenon in reinforcement learning, typically manifesting as range normalization in outcome representation. However, recent findings have revealed systematic deviations from this model, particularly when three options with equally spaced values are presented. In this study, we hypothesize that these distortions in outcome normalization arise from attentional processes. To test this, we conduct three experiments with 105 participants in total while simultaneously tracking their gaze position with eye-tracking. Furthermore, we systematically manipulate attention using both top-down and bottom-up approaches. These manipulations significantly increase the subjective valuation of attended options, supporting a causal role of attention in shaping value representation. To account for these effects, we develop a reinforcement learning model that integrates attentional mechanisms, wherein gaze duration directly modulates the absolute value of options prior to range normalization. This attentional range model outperforms attention-free and choice-repetition alternatives, underscoring the critical influence of attention in value computation.

Introduction

Decision-making processes are profoundly influenced by the context in which options are presented. The perceived value of an option is often shaped not solely by its intrinsic attributes but also in relation to other available alternatives. For instance, winning second place in a contest might feel rewarding until one realizes there were only two participants. This context-dependent valuation is a well-documented phenomenon, observed in both value-based decision-making scenarios, where option values are explicitly known1–5, and in reinforcement learning (RL), where values are acquired through trial and error6–15. This influence of context on value perception may serve as an adaptive mechanism, enabling organisms to make efficient decisions under neurobiological and environmental constraints16–20. However, this same mechanism can also lead to systematic biases and seemingly irrational behaviors, particularly when choices are presented outside of their original learning context. Typically, decision-makers tend to prefer the option that was most favorable within its original learning context, even when another option has a higher absolute value but was less favorable within its own context. This behavior, which has been observed in humans and various other species, suggests that decision-makers prioritize options based on their relative value within their learning contexts, rather than their absolute value, sometimes leading to sub-optimal choices in new environments6,8,21–28.

One prominent computational framework that has been proposed to account for context-dependent valuation is range normalization4,7,10,23,29,30. According to this model, the subjective value of an option is rescaled based on the minimum and maximum values present in a given context. While range normalization successfully predicts subjective values in simple binary choice scenarios, it encounters limitations when additional options are introduced8. For example, when participants are presented with three options of evenly spaced values, the pure range normalization model predicts that the subjective value of the middle option should fall exactly halfway between the lowest and highest options. However, empirical evidence contradicts this prediction, indicating that participants tend to perceive the mid-value option as being close to the lowest one. This discrepancy indicates the presence of a non-linear bias in valuation processes, suggesting that the range model does not fully capture the cognitive mechanisms underlying subjective valuation. To address this limitation, Bavard and Palminteri8 introduced a weighted range normalization model, incorporating a power-transformation parameter to capture non-linearities in valuation. This refinement improves the model’s predictive accuracy but remains a descriptive account, without addressing the cognitive mechanisms underlying these valuation processes.

In this study, we hypothesize that the functional form of outcome normalization could stem from attentional mechanisms that bias evidence accumulation as a function of the outcomes and expected values of available options. This hypothesis is informed by a growing body of research that has highlighted the significant role of attention in both value-based decision-making and reinforcement learning31–46. Prior studies in reinforcement learning have demonstrated a bidirectional interaction between selective attentional processes and learning, where high-value features attract attention and, in turn, attention influences how value is attributed to different features37,44,47,48. Here, we propose that the bidirectional interaction between attention and learning may account for the non-linearities observed in context-dependent valuation. Specifically, we suggest that the more attention paid to an option, the closer its subjective value will align with its range normalized value.

To test this hypothesis, we conducted a series of eye-tracking experiments to investigate the relationship between attention and value normalization in reinforcement learning. The study comprised three experiments in which participants performed a choice task structured within an RL framework. During a learning phase with complete feedback, participants were asked to select one of three options with evenly spaced values. This was followed by a transfer test in which they chose between all possible pairs of options without additional feedback. The use of eye-tracking allowed us to measure attention allocation in real-time, offering insights into the cognitive processes underlying decision-making and feedback evaluation. Our results indicate that both top-down (Experiment 1) and bottom-up (Experiments 2 and 3) attentional manipulations proved effective in enhancing the subjective valuation of the mid-value options during learning, as subsequently revealed by their choices in the transfer test. These findings provide initial evidence that attention contributes to shaping subjective value.

To further account for these findings, we developed an RL model incorporating attentional mechanisms. In this model, the time spent looking at options during the choice phase directly influences their absolute value before range normalization. This attentional range model performed better than the weighted range model in capturing context-dependent preferences, highlighting the critical role of attention in shaping value representations in human reinforcement learning. Our findings offer insights into the cognitive mechanisms underlying context-dependent valuation in human decision-making, suggesting that attentional processes play a crucial role in shaping the representations that underlie learning. By integrating attentional processes into models of value normalization, we provide a more comprehensive account of how subjective value is constructed and how biases in attention allocation can influence learning.

Results

General experimental approach

To investigate the role of attention in shaping subjective value representation, we conducted three eye-tracking experiments with a total of 105 participants (35 per experiment), who performed a choice task structured within an RL framework. The experimental design consisted of two phases: a learning phase and a subsequent transfer phase (Fig. 1). During the learning phase, participants were incentivized to identify the most rewarding option in four different, interleaved contexts, each containing three options with evenly spaced values. The inclusion of three options per context was essential for examining the non-linear bias in subjective valuation, and for demonstrating the limitations of the range model in accounting for this behavior. In all three experiments, two key factors were orthogonally manipulated across the four contexts: (1) the range of possible outcomes and (2) the allocation of attention. The manipulation of the outcome range aimed to distinguish between unbiased (absolute) and context-dependent (normalized) learning processes. Meanwhile, the attention manipulation was conducted to assess the influence of attentional focus on valuation, with specific attention manipulations varying across the three experiments. Following the learning phase, participants completed a transfer test in which they made choices among all possible binary combinations of the options without receiving any additional feedback. Experiment 1: Modulating top-down attention

The first experiment replicated the design of Bavard and Palminteri8, with the addition of eye-tracking to monitor participants’ gaze patterns (Fig. 1a). The attention manipulation consisted in contrasting two contexts: a 100% ternary context, which served as a control with no attention manipulation, and a 50% binary context, in which attention was modulated by alternating between ternary (three-option) and binary (two-option) choice trials. In the binary trials, the high-value option was visible but blurred and not available for selection, making the mid-value option the best possible choice among the available options (Fig. 1b). This manipulation was designed to make participants actively focus on the mid-value option (top-down attention), allowing us to test whether this increased attention would lead to a more accurate subjective valuation of this option during the transfer test, compared to its counterpart in the control context.

Experiment 1: Learning phase performance

To ensure that participants were actively engaged with the task, we assessed whether the correct choice rate—defined as the probability of selecting the option with the highest expected value—exceeded chance levels during the learning phase. In the 100% ternary context, where all three options were consistently available, the chance level was 1/3. In the 50% binary context, the chance level was 1/3 in ternary trials and 1/2 in binary trials. In line with our main experimental manipulation, the analysis of choice rates for each individual option revealed a significant interaction between context and option value (F6,204 = 178, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.84; two-way repeated measures ANOVA). Post-hoc tests demonstrated that, in the 50% binary context, participants selected the mid-value option significantly more often than in the 100% ternary context (Fig. 2b; wide (Δ50–100): M = 0.48, SE = 0.029, p < 0.001, 95% CI = [0.40, 0.56]; narrow (Δ50–100): M = 0.44, SE = 0.022, p < 0.001, 95% CI = [0.38, 0.50]). Furthermore, within the 50% binary context, participants chose the mid-value option even more frequently than the high-value option (Fig. 2b; wide (Δmid-max): M = 0.19, SE = 0.042, p < 0.001, 95% CI = [0.091, 0.29]; narrow (Δmid-max): M = 0.19, SE = 0.030, p < 0.001, 95% CI = [0.12, 0.26]).

Experiment 1: Transfer phase performance

Turning to the transfer phase, we first confirmed that the correct choice rate was significantly above chance (M = 0.85, SD = 0.077, t34 = 27.0, p < 0.001, 95% CI = [0.82, 0.88], d = 4.47; two-sided, one-sample Student’s t-tests), thereby providing evidence of successful value retrieval and generalization6–10,50. Next, we analyzed the choice rate per symbol, which reflects the average frequency with which a given symbol is chosen in the transfer phase (i.e., across all possible pairings) and serves as a measure of subjective preference for that option13 (Fig. 2c). Our initial focus was to identify signatures of value normalization (context-dependency) and non-linear biases in subjective valuation within the 100% ternary context, which did not involve any attentional manipulation and thus served as a control. Results showed that participants chose the mid-value option in the wide context significantly less often than the high-value options in the narrow context, despite both options sharing the same objective value of 50 (ΔW-N: M = −0.44, SD = 0.22, t34 = −11.9, p < 0.001, 95% CI = [−0.51, −0.36], d = −2.75; two-sided, paired-sample Student’s t-tests; see Supplementary Fig. 1 for pairwise choice preferences). This finding suggests that participants normalized values within each context, supporting context-dependent valuation. Further analysis revealed that the choice rates for the mid-value options did not significantly differ from those of the corresponding low-value options in either context (wide: t34 = −0.47, p = 0.64, 95% CI = [−0.11, 0.066], d = −0.10; narrow: t34 = −0.28, p = 0.78, 95% CI = [−0.11, 0.081], d = −0.077; two-sided, paired-sample Student’s t-tests), and Bayesian paired-sample t-tests supported the conclusion that choice rates did not differ between option types (wide: BF01 = 4.97; narrow: BF01 = 5.32). This pattern is consistent with a non-linear processing of outcomes, in line with findings from the replicated study8. Given that both range normalization and non-linear biases in subjective valuation were consistently observed across the narrow and wide contexts, these contexts were combined for all subsequent analyses.

Next, we examined the effect of attention manipulation in the 50% binary context, where the high-value option was unavailable in half of the trials. Our aim was to determine whether the increased focus on the mid-value option observed during the learning phase would translate to a higher subjective valuation of these options in the transfer phase. To test this hypothesis, we fitted a weighted range normalization model separately for the 100% ternary and 50% binary contexts (Fig. 2d). Like all models tested in the present study, this model builds on a standard Q-learning architecture, in which subjective option values (Q-values) are updated from trial to trial using reward prediction errors51,52 and converted into choice probabilities via a softmax decision rule53 (see Methods, “Computational modeling” for details). Within this framework, we used the weighted range model as the baseline because prior work shows that it closely captures participants’ behavior in this task8, and in our data it similarly outperformed alternative accounts—such as perseveration or psychophysical compression—which failed to reproduce the key behavioral patterns (Supplementary Figs. 2–4).

Integrating visual fixations to the range model

To formally test the hypothesis that attention modulates learning processes, we designed a modified version of the range normalization model that incorporates visual fixation data. Specifically, this approach allowed the proportion of time spent looking at an option to influence its absolute value (Eq. 2) before applying the range normalization procedure (Eq. 3). The model ensures that equal attention allocation across options maintains their proper positioning within the contextual range. However, an unequal distribution of attention—where some options receive disproportionately higher or lower fixation times—can skew the relative positioning of these options within their contextual range, thereby affecting their perceived value.

We additionally tested alternative models in which subjective valuation was based directly on fixation ratios without range normalization (i.e., using only the fixation ratio as an estimate of subjective value). Both formulations yielded highly similar results, and the “after normalization” variant kept outperforming all other models within the main model space, confirming that our overall conclusions are robust to this implementation choice (Supplementary Table 4).

Experiment 2 and 3: Modulating bottom-up attention

The results from Experiment 1 indicate that the observed transfer preferences are unlikely to arise from simple repetition of choices during learning. For example, in the 50% binary context, participants chose the mid-value option more often than the high-value option during learning (Fig. 2b), yet this did not translate into a higher preference for the mid-value option in the transfer phase. Consistent with this observation, a model implementing value changes as a function of repeated choices56,57 failed to capture participants’ transfer preferences and was outperformed by the weighted range normalization model (Supplementary Fig. 4). However, because attention in Experiment 1 was manipulated via changes in option availability, attentional allocation and choice patterns were inherently intertwined. Thus, although simple choice repetition cannot explain the observed changes in subjective valuation, part of the effect may still reflect choice-related dynamics rather than purely attentional mechanisms. Moreover, the similarity in fixation patterns for stimuli and outcomes (i.e., increased fixation ratio for both the mid-value stimulus and mid-value outcome; see Fig. 3) limits our ability to fully disentangle their individual contributions to subjective value computation.

To address these limitations, we conducted two follow-up experiments designed to isolate the specific influence of attention on subjective value computation. In Experiment 2, we manipulated bottom-up attention allocation by varying the saliency of the stimuli during the learning phase (Fig. 5a). In the “100% max” context, the high-value stimulus was made more visually salient by increasing its brightness on each trial. In the “50% mid” context, half of the trials mirrored the “100% max” context (high-value trials), while in the other half, the increased brightness was applied to the mid-value stimulus instead (mid-value trials). Experiment 3 followed a similar design to Experiment 2, but the saliency manipulation was applied to outcomes rather than stimuli, irrespective of participants’ choices (Fig. 5b). Experiment 2 and 3: Learning phase performance

We first examined whether saliency manipulations affected participants’ ability to select the best option. In both experiments, the correct choice rate was significantly above chance level (1/3) across all contexts (least significant Exp. 2: M = 0.89, SD = 0.19, t34 = 17.4, p < 0.001, 95% CI = [0.83, 0.96], d = 2.87; least significant Exp. 3: M = 0.86, SD = 0.19, t34 = 16.0, p < 0.001, 95% CI = [0.79, 0.93], d = 2.65; two-sided, one-sample Student’s t-tests; Supplementary Fig. 7). A two-way repeated measures ANOVA revealed no significant main effects or interactions of saliency or range amplitude on correct choice rates (all p > 0.05), indicating that learning performance was comparable across contexts.

We next examined choice rates for individual options to assess potential interactions between saliency contexts and option values. In contrast to Experiment 1, no significant interaction was identified between context and option value (Exp. 2: F2,68 = 3.84, p = 0.057, ηp2 = 0.10; Exp. 3: F2,68 = 0.70, p = 0.44, ηp2 = 0.020; two-way repeated measures ANOVA). Instead, a robust main effect of option value was observed (Exp. 2: F2,68 = 1206, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.97; Exp. 3: F2,68 = 528, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.94), with the high-value option being chosen more frequently than the mid- and low-value options (Supplementary Fig. 7). This indicates that choice behavior was primarily driven by option value, even when the mid-value option was made more salient.

Experiment 2 and 3: Gaze patterns

To ascertain whether the saliency manipulations had the expected effect on attention allocation, we analyzed fixation ratios during the learning phase (Fig. 5c, d). A significant three-way interaction was observed between context (“100% max” vs. “50% mid”), trial type (high-value vs. mid-value trials), and option value (min, mid, or max) across both timeframes in Experiment 2 (choice phase: F2,68 = 62.3, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.65; outcome phase: F2,68 = 86.1, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.72) and Experiment 3 (choice phase: F2,68 = 167, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.83; outcome phase: F2,68 = 37.4, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.52). In both experiments, the fixation patterns observed during the “100% max” context and the high-value trials of the “50% mid” context were similar to those observed in the 100% ternary (i.e., control) context of Experiment 1. Specifically, the high-value option received the most attention during both the choice and outcome phases (Supplementary Table 1).

However, distinct patterns emerged in mid-value trials of the “50% mid” context. During the choice phase of Experiment 2, where the attention manipulation was applied to the stimulus, participants’ gaze was initially captured by the mid-value stimulus due to its increased saliency (Fig. 5c bottom). Then, over time, their attention reverted to the preferred high-value option. This initial shift in attentional focus was reflected in a significantly higher fixation ratio for the mid-value stimulus in these trials compared to both the “100% max” context (Δ50M-100H: M = 0.084, SE = 0.018, p < 0.001, 95% CI = [0.041, 0.13]) and the high-value trials of the same context (Δ50M-50H: M = 0.085, SE = 0.019, p < 0.001, 95% CI = [0.040, 0.13]). As a result, the mid-value option received more attention overall during the choice phase in the “50% mid” context than in the “100% max” context (Table 1). Importantly, this effect was not observed during the outcome phase, where fixation ratios for the mid-value option did not significantly differ across contexts (Table 1).

In Experiment 3, where saliency manipulation was applied to outcomes rather than stimuli, a symmetrical pattern emerged. During the outcome phase of the mid-value trials in the “50% mid” context, participants’ gaze was drawn to the mid-value outcome (made more salient), but only after an initial focus on the high-value outcome (Fig. 5d bottom). This sequence aligns with the observation that participants most frequently choose the high-value option, making it the most immediate target of their post-choice gaze. Nevertheless, the increased saliency of the mid-value outcome led to a significantly higher fixation ratio for the mid-value outcome in these trials compared to both the “100% max” context (Δ50M-100H: M = 0.087, SE = 0.015, p < 0.001, 95% CI = [0.050, 0.12]) and the high-value trials within the same context (Δ50M-50H: M = 0.10, SE = 0.011, p < 0.001, 95% CI = [0.072, 0.13]). Therefore, the mid-value option received significantly more attention overall during the outcome phase of the learning phase in the “50% mid” context compared to the “100% max” context (Table 1). Importantly, this increased attention to the mid-value outcome was not observed during the choice phase (Table 1), again contrasting with findings from Experiment 1.

Overall, these findings confirm that the saliency manipulations were effective in modulating participants’ attention in a bottom-up manner58. In Experiment 2, the “50% mid” context increased attention to the mid-value stimulus during the choice phase, while in Experiment 3, it increased attention to the mid-value outcome during the outcome phase. Importantly, these attentional shifts occurred without disrupting participants’ performance in identifying or choosing the high-value option (Supplementary Fig. 7), indicating that the saliency manipulation selectively affected attention without significantly altering choice behavior.

Fine-grained computational analysis of gaze dynamics

To further investigate the computational mechanism linking attention and valuation, we compared the same five models used in Experiment 1 (absolute model, weighted range model, and the three versions of the attentional range model) in Experiments 2 and 3. In both experiments, the attentional range model with a free φφ parameter (Eq. 2) revealed a stronger influence of stimulus fixations compared to outcome fixations in explaining choice patterns (i.e., greater than 0.5; Exp. 2: M = 0.83, SD = 0.27, t34 = 7.07, p < 0.001, 95% CI = [0.73, 0.92], d = 1.17; Exp. 3: M = 0.80, SD = 0.29, t34 = 6.20, p < 0.001, 95% CI = [0.70, 0.90], d = 1.02; two-sided, one-sample Student’s t-tests; Fig. 4a). Additionally, the φφ parameters estimated in Experiments 2 and 3 were significantly larger than those estimated in Experiment 1 (Exp. 2: t68 = 2.44, p = 0.017, 95% CI = [0.032, 0.32], d = 0.58; Exp. 3: t68 = 2.03, p = 0.046, 95% CI = [0.003, 0.29], d = 0.48; two-sided, two-sample Student’s t-tests), suggesting an even stronger dependency of value normalization on stimuli-related fixations.

Furthermore, model comparison based on BIC confirmed that the attentional range model relying exclusively on stimulus fixations (φ=1φ=1) provided the best fit to the data (Fig. 4b; Table 3). These results further reinforce the critical role of stimulus-directed attention in reinforcement learning and subjective valuation, consistent with findings from Experiment 1.

While these model comparisons ruled out a role of outcome-related attention, the way fixation ratios were computed raises an important question. In prior analyses, fixation times have been averaged over the entire stimulus and outcome durations, implicitly assuming that all moments within these periods contribute equally to value computation. However, it is possible that restricted time windows, such as those immediately preceding or following a decision, exert a greater influence on subjective valuation than others. For instance, given the relatively long outcome display duration (2 s), specific segments within this timeframe might still affect value computation.

To further probe the contribution of distinct temporal segments, we performed an additional analysis using non-overlapping 10% time windows across the stimulus and outcome phases (Supplementary Fig. 8). This approach tested whether time segments independent from the selection time—such as the beginning of the stimulus presentation or the end of the outcome display—could also predict subjective valuation. Results revealed that these individual 10% windows provided poorer fits than the model using the full stimulus window, suggesting that no single segment was solely driving outcome normalization. However, some windows closer to the selection time approached the performance of the full-stimulus attentional range model. Among these best-fitting windows, a larger proportion relied on stimulus-related fixations than on outcome-related ones, consistent with our main findings.

Taken together, these findings highlight the temporal dynamics of attentional influence, emphasizing that subjective value computation is best captured by stimulus fixations throughout the decision process, with minimal contribution from outcome-related attention.

Discussion

The present study set out to investigate the role of attention in shaping subjective values during reinforcement learning (RL). Across three experiments combining attention manipulations, eye-tracking, and computational modeling, we demonstrated that attention allocation plays a critical role in explaining systematic deviations from range normalization previously observed in RL tasks. Specifically, our findings reveal that directing attention—through both top-down and bottom-up mechanisms—toward mid-value options during learning mitigates their typical undervaluation and leads to subjective valuation that better reflects their actual value and relative position within the context, as evidenced by subsequent choices. Moreover, we show that attention modulates value encoding primarily during the stimulus presentation phase, rather than during outcome evaluation, highlighting the critical role of early attentional engagement in shaping subsequent outcome processing and valuation. Together, these results provide strong evidence that attentional processes not only correlate with but causally influence value normalization in RL, offering a mechanistic explanation for previously observed non-linear distortions in subjective valuation.

Our findings replicate previous work showing that outcome values in reinforcement learning are normalized in a manner broadly consistent with the range normalization model, which linearly rescales values relative to the contextual range6–9,14. At the same time, we confirmed that participants’ behavior systematically deviates from the linear predictions of this model: mid-value outcomes tend to be perceived as closer to the minimum possible outcome, suggesting a systematic distortion in subjective valuation8. Although range normalization captures many aspects of behavior in RL, these deviations suggest that additional cognitive processes influence how subjective values are constructed during learning. To capture this pattern, our previous work introduced a weighted range model in which normalized values are further transformed by a non-linear weighting function8. This formulation improves descriptive accuracy and provides a coherent framework for characterizing systematic distortions in subjective valuation. However, several alternative mechanisms could, in principle, give rise to similar behavioral patterns, including psychophysical compression of outcome magnitudes7,59,60, exploratory lapses during choice52,61, or biases arising from past choice history (i.e., perseveration)56,57. We therefore formally evaluated these empirically grounded alternatives using computational modeling (Supplementary Figs. 2–4). Although these mechanisms offer reasonable starting points, none reproduced the characteristic transfer-phase patterns observed in our experiments—most notably the difference between Wide and Narrow contexts for options of the same objective value, and the systematic equivalence between mid- and low-value options in transfer. Thus, these models, as currently formulated in the literature, cannot account for the observed results. By contrast, the weighted range model accommodates both the scale invariance expected from normalization and the systematic non-linear distortions observed in behavior. Importantly, while this comparison establishes the weighted range model as a descriptively adequate account within the present paradigm, it does not by itself identify the cognitive mechanisms responsible for the observed distortions. A central objective of the present study was therefore to move beyond this descriptive account and identify the processes underlying these effects by grounding the model in a psychologically meaningful explanation.

One plausible account is that these distortions arise from how attention is allocated during learning. Extensive research in value-based decision-making has shown that attention not only reflects preferences62,63 but also influences value representations, with items receiving longer visual fixations being more likely to be chosen33,34,42,43,45,64,65. Similarly, in risky choice, attention has been shown to systematically influence probability weighting, leading to different risk behaviors46,66,67. Together, these findings suggest that attention plays a fundamental role in shaping subjective values across different decision contexts. Surprisingly, however, the relationship between visual attention and subjective value has remained relatively unexplored in reinforcement learning. Prior RL studies incorporating attentional measures primarily focused on tasks involving multi-attribute options37, environments with uncertainty44, or attentional biases toward threat48. While these studies provided valuable insights into how attention correlates with learning, it remains unknown whether attention causally shapes subjective value representations in RL.

Building on the idea of reciprocal influences between attention and valuation observed in other domains, we hypothesized that similar mechanisms contribute to the non-linear distortions observed in RL. To test this hypothesis, we conducted a series of experiments combining eye-tracking as a measure of overt attention with attentional manipulations and computational modeling, while participants performed RL tasks inspired by prior studies. Specifically, we examined whether directing attention toward mid-value options (among three options with evenly spaced values) during learning would influence how participants valued these options later. Across experiments, we found that attention allocation during learning systematically biased subjective valuation, as reflected in participants’ choices during the transfer phase.

Beyond the behavioral effects, our computational modeling results further confirmed that attention modulates value encoding during learning, thus shaping subsequent decision-making. Specifically, the model incorporating fixation data consistently outperformed both the standard range normalization model and the weighted range normalization model across all three experiments, providing strong empirical support for the robustness of this effect. In this attention-modulated model, the proportion of fixations directed at an option determined how strongly newly observed outcomes updated its stored value before range normalization. This formulation is consistent with neurobiological and computational accounts proposing that attention acts as a gain-control process, amplifying the neural representation of attended stimuli before normalization occurs16,68. However, when we tested the reverse implementation—where attention was applied after normalization—the results were virtually unchanged, indicating that our conclusions are robust to the precise stage at which attention is introduced in the value computation process. Overall, these findings align with previous computational models of value-based decision-making, where attention is modeled as a bias in the evidence accumulation process, leading to discounted subjective values for less-attended options33,34,45,64,69.

Critically, our parameter recovery and model recovery analyses confirmed that our modeling approach was well-specified and reliably captured the underlying cognitive processes54. Because gaze and choice are naturally correlated, one potential concern is that incorporating fixation data could introduce partial circularity—that is, if fixations merely reflected the imminent decision, this could artificially improve model fits. Importantly, several aspects of our design minimize this risk. First, fixation ratios were computed across all options and entered the model as modulators of subjective value prior to range normalization, rather than as direct predictors of choice34. Second, a model relying solely on fixation data failed to outperform value-based models, confirming that gaze patterns alone are not sufficient to account for choice behavior. Third, in Experiment 2, exogenous manipulations of stimulus saliency systematically shifted fixation patterns during learning and produced corresponding changes in transfer preferences, providing causal evidence that gaze actively shapes subsequent valuation. Together, these results strengthen the conclusion that the attentional effects captured by our model are not artifacts of partial circularity, but instead reflect the genuine influence of distributed attention on subjective value construction.

Beyond confirming that attention modulates value encoding, our multi-experiment design allowed us to investigate whether this effect depends on the nature of attentional control—top-down (goal-directed) or bottom-up (stimulus-driven). In Experiment 1, we manipulated attention endogenously through the task structure: by occasionally rendering the best option unselectable within the choice set, the mid-value option became the best available alternative, naturally encouraging participants to allocate more attention to it as they selected it. In contrast, Experiments 2 and 3 relied on exogenous manipulations, enhancing the visual saliency of the mid-value option to attract attention without providing any additional task-relevant information. Crucially, these manipulations increased attention toward the mid-value option without increasing its choice frequency during learning, thereby dissociating attentional allocation from learning-phase choice repetition.

Our results revealed that both forms of attentional manipulation effectively influenced subjective valuation, providing causal evidence that attention shapes value representations during learning. However, the magnitude of the effect differed: the impact of top-down attention in Experiment 1 was stronger than that of bottom-up saliency manipulation in Experiment 2. One possible explanation is that goal-directed attention, being more tightly linked to task demands, exerts a stronger influence on value learning70. Alternatively, the impact of stimulus-driven attention may have been reduced because participants learned to disregard the saliency manipulation once it became clear that it did not reliably predict reward—a form of habituation. This interpretation is consistent with previous findings showing that salient but uninformative cues can be ignored when they fail to signal relevant outcomes71–74. Interestingly, these findings echo results from value-based decision-making tasks, where exogenous attentional manipulations—such as saliency changes or altered presentation durations—have been shown to bias choices38,65,75–79, supporting a causal role of attention in shaping preferences.

Having established that attention influences value learning, we next examined when this modulation occurs—during stimulus presentation or outcome evaluation. In reinforcement learning, both phases could theoretically shape value updating: attention to stimuli might bias how information is sampled and retrieved, while attention to outcomes might influence how reward information is integrated. To test this, we compared model variants that allowed fixation data to influence value updating either during stimulus observation, outcome evaluation, or both. Specifically, we used the φφ parameter to estimate the relative contribution of stimulus and outcome fixations to learning.

Across experiments, three converging results consistently pointed to attention during stimulus presentation as having a greater impact on value learning than attention during outcome observation. First, model comparison showed that the best-fitting model was the one where only stimulus fixations modulated value updating (φ=1φ=1), outperforming both the outcome-only and the flexible models. Second, estimates of the φφ parameter in the flexible model (where φφ was free to vary) systematically assigned more weight to stimulus fixations, confirming that attention during option evaluation primarily drives learning. Finally, fine-grained temporal analyses of gaze data further supported this conclusion, revealing that the critical window for attentional modulation of normalized value occurs primarily during stimulus observation, with a brief refractory period immediately after outcome delivery. Taken together, these findings indicate that attention deployed during stimulus presentation sets the stage for how subsequent outcomes are processed and stored. In other words, the amount of attention allocated to an option before observing its outcome determines the strength of the subsequent value update.

Interestingly, these findings challenge the intuitive assumption that attention during outcome observation should predominantly influence the learning process, given that reward information is delivered at that stage. Instead, they suggest that it is the binding of stimuli and outcomes, established by attention at the time of choice, that shapes how values are encoded and later retrieved. This insight helps reconcile previous mixed findings in RL studies, which differed in the emphasis they placed on attention during stimulus presentation versus outcome evaluation when accounting for choice behavior37,44. It further highlights that value learning depends not only on outcome processing but also critically on how attentional resources are allocated during option evaluation.

Future research could aim to develop a fully bidirectional computational framework by incorporating a generative model of attention. Rather than treating fixations as exogenous modulators of learning and choice, as done here and in previous RL studies, a more comprehensive approach would model attention as an active process that is itself learned and optimized over time. Such a framework would allow predicting not only how attention shapes value updating but also how agents learn to allocate their limited attentional resources both within and across trials. Existing models in value-based decision-making already propose reciprocal interactions between attention and choice, where attention dynamically guides and is guided by the valuation process31,80–83. Similarly, some studies have developed learning models of eye movements or visual attention71,84, but these models typically do not account for how attention allocation directly shapes value learning. Extending such generative attention models into reinforcement learning contexts could clarify how learners adaptively allocate attention based on evolving value estimates, uncertainty, or task demands, offering a richer mechanistic understanding of attention’s role in learning.

Taken together, our findings provide strong evidence that attention plays a causal role in shaping subjective value representations during reinforcement learning. By incorporating eye-tracking data into computational models of value learning, we move beyond descriptive accounts and toward a mechanistic understanding of how attention gives rise to biases in valuation. These insights have broad implications for models of adaptive decision-making and open avenues for investigating the dynamic interplay between attention and valuation in both humans and animals.

Methods

Participants and inclusion criteria

A total of 105 healthy volunteers were recruited across three experiments (n = 35 per experiment; mean age = 27.6 ± 0.6 years; 55 females and 50 males, self-reported; mean years of university education = 4 ± 0.2). Participants were required to meet the following inclusion criteria: (1) age between 18 and 40 years and (2) ability to complete the experiment without glasses or contact lenses to ensure accurate eye-tracking recordings.

The compensation method followed experimental economics standards and was transparently explained to participants prior to the experiment. Each participant received a fixed amount of 13€, plus a variable bonus between 0€ and 7€, based on performance. The variable bonus was determined by the number of points gained relative to a random performance (rounded to the nearest 50 cents). A chance-level performance resulted in a 0€ bonus, while perfect performance corresponded to the maximum bonus of 7€. The total average earnings across participants were 19.0€ ± 0.11.

The study was approved by the INSERM Ethical Review Committee (IRB00003888). On the day of the experiment, participants were welcomed to the department’s experimental platform, where the procedure was explained both orally and in writing. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants using forms sent to them in advance.

Behavioral tasks

Participants performed an instrumental learning task adapted from previous research8. The experiment was conducted on a PC using a custom-built local website, which presented visual stimuli and recorded behavioral data. Responses were made using a computer mouse with the participant’s preferred hand. Task instructions concluded with a three-item quiz to ensure participants understood the task. Each participant completed a training session before proceeding to the main experiment.

The full task consisted of three phases: a learning phase followed by two elicitation phases (transfer and explicit rating). Participants were unaware of the elicitation phases until they appeared during the task. The order of the elicitation phases was counterbalanced across participants. At the end of each phase, participants were shown their total points earned and the corresponding monetary equivalent.

Training

The training session was a short version of the learning phase, designed to familiarize participants with the task. It consisted of 15 trials (five trials per context across three randomly selected contexts out of four). Simple shapes replaced real stimuli to reduce cognitive load during training. Participants’ performance was evaluated based on their accuracy in choosing the option with the highest expected value. To proceed to the main experiment, participants were required to achieve at least 60% accuracy. If they failed, the training was repeated (unbeknownst to them) up to three times. If the threshold was not achieved after four attempts, participants proceeded to the main task regardless of performance (this occurred in 7 out of 105 participants). Participants could also voluntarily repeat the training if desired. During training, the experimenter remained in the booth to ensure proper functioning of the eye-tracker and clarify any questions. Once training was completed, the experimenter left, and participants completed the remainder of the experiment independently.

Learning phase

During the learning phase, participants identified the most rewarding option in four different contexts structured in a 2 × 2 factorial design. Each context consisted of three options with evenly spaced values (12 options total). Two factors were orthogonally manipulated across the four contexts: (1) the range of possible outcomes (wide vs. narrow) and (2) the allocation of attention.

Each triplet of options (representing a context) was presented 45 times in an interleaved fashion, resulting in a total of 180 trials. Within each triplet, the options were associated with deterministic outcomes drawn from normal distributions with fixed means and standard deviation. In Experiment 1, attention was manipulated by varying option availability (top-down attention manipulation). In the 100% ternary context, all three options were selectable. In the 50% binary context, the high-value option was visible but unavailable for selection in 50% of the trials (interleaved). In these trials, the high-value option was blurred to maintain consistent luminance. Regardless of availability, outcomes for all options were displayed at the end of each trial.

In Experiment 2, attention was manipulated by varying stimuli saliency (bottom-up attention manipulation). All stimuli were displayed in grayscale (C = 0). Non-manipulated stimuli had a luminance of 70, while manipulated stimuli had a luminance of 95, making them more visually salient. In the “100% max” context, the high-value stimulus was made more salient on every trial. In the “50% mid” context, luminance was increased for the high-value stimulus in half the trials and for the mid-value stimulus in the other half (interleaved).

Experiment 3 followed the same design as Experiment 2, but applied saliency manipulations to outcomes instead of stimuli. Non-manipulated outcomes had a luminance of 70, while manipulated outcomes had a luminance of 95. Manipulation occurred independently of participants’ choices, with the high-value outcome made salient in the “100% max” context and alternating between the high- and mid-value outcomes in the “50% mid” context.

Transfer phase

In the transfer phase, the 12 stimuli from the learning phase were presented in all possible binary combinations (66 pairs, excluding pairs formed by the same stimulus). Each pair was displayed three times, resulting in a total of 198 trials. Participants were informed that they would be presented with pairs of options from the learning phase, but the specific combinations may not have been encountered before. No feedback about outcomes was provided during this phase to avoid altering the subjective values learned in the learning phase. On each trial, participants selected one of the two options, knowing that their responses would contribute to their final payoff.

In addition to the standard choice-based elicitation phase, participants completed an explicit rating task in which they assigned subjective values to each stimulus from the learning phase. Each stimulus was presented individually three times, leading to a total of 36 trials. Participants estimated the average value of each option by adjusting a cursor on a 0 to 100 scale. This rating task was included to match the design of Bavard and Palminteri8 but was not analyzed in the present study.

Eye-tracking

Gaze positions were recorded throughout the task using a tower-mounted EyeLink 1000 Plus eye tracker (SR Research Ltd., Ottawa, ON, Canada), controlled via Pylink for Python 3.8. Participants’ heads were stabilized using a chin rest positioned at a fixed distance of 700 mm from a 24-inch (60.96 cm) computer screen (dimensions: 531.36 × 298.89 mm; resolution: 1920 × 1080 pixels; pixel density: 91 ppi), where visual stimuli were presented. The EyeLink system was operated in monocular mode, recording the movement of the right eye at a sampling rate of 1000 Hz. A 13-point calibration and validation procedure, provided by SR Research software, was conducted before the training phase. Drift checks were performed before the learning and transfer phases to ensure recording accuracy. If the drift check detected substantial calibration deviations, a drift correction was applied to adjust the calibration. The screen background was a uniform gray tone (RGB: 100, 100, 100), which was maintained across the calibration procedure and the experimental task.

Gaze data processing

Raw gaze data output from the EyeLink system was converted into MATLAB structures using custom scripts and the FieldTrip toolbox85 in MATLAB R2024b (The MathWorks, Inc., Natick, Massachusetts, USA).

Three areas of interest (AOIs) were defined, corresponding to the left, middle, and right option positions on the screen. Attention to a specific option was determined based on fixations falling within its AOI. The AOIs were defined as the stimulus size plus an additional margin of 1.5° of visual angle to account for eye-tracking inaccuracy and reduce false negatives86.

Behavioral and eye-tracking analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using MATLAB Statistical Toolbox and focused on three main variables: the correct choice rate, the choice rate per option, and the fixation ratio.

The correct choice rate was defined as the probability of selecting the option with the highest expected value, calculated separately for the learning and transfer phases. The choice rate per option represented the number of times a specific option was chosen divided by the number of times it was presented. Similarly, this measure was computed separately for the learning and transfer phases. Finally, the fixation ratio quantified the proportion of time participants spent looking at an option during learning, calculated relative to the total choice time (from stimulus presentation to selection) or the outcome display time (fixed at 2 s).

Descriptive statistics, including mean (M), standard deviation (SD), and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), are reported where appropriate.

Statistical tests

Participants’ overall performance was assessed by comparing their correct choice rates against chance levels using two-sided, one-sample t-tests. For trials with three available options, chance level was set at 1/3, while for trials with two options, it was 1/2. Differences in choice rates between specific options were tested using two-sided, paired-sample t-tests. To account for multiple comparisons, we applied the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate (FDR) at α = 0.05. For all t-tests, we reported the t-statistic (t), p-value (p), and Cohen’s d as a measure of effect size.

To examine the effects of experimental factors on participants’ performance, we conducted two-way repeated measures ANOVA. Correct choice rates were analyzed within the framework of the 2 × 2 factorial design, which included the range of outcomes (wide vs. narrow) and allocation of attention (100% vs. 50% contexts). For option-specific choice rates, a 4 × 3 factorial design was used in Experiment 1, incorporating the four experimental contexts and option value (low, mid, or high). In Experiments 2 and 3, where wide and narrow contexts were merged, a 2 × 3 factorial design was used. Fixation ratios were analyzed using a three-way repeated measures ANOVA (2 × 2 × 3) to explore the effects of context (100% vs. 50%), trial type (ternary vs. binary or high-value vs. mid-value trials), and option value (low, mid, or high). Assumptions of independence, normality, and sphericity were assessed prior to conducting the analyses. Sphericity was assessed using Mauchly’s test, and corrections were applied where necessary. Specifically, the Greenhouse-Geisser correction was used for epsilon estimates below 0.75, while the Huynh-Feldt correction was applied otherwise87. For all repeated measures ANOVA, we reported the F-statistic (F), p-value (p), and partial eta squared (ηp2) to quantify effect sizes. Post-hoc comparisons for significant effects were conducted using Tukey-Kramer tests, implemented via MATLAB’s “multcompare” function. For each post-hoc comparison, we reported the estimated difference between marginal means (M), the standard error of the difference (SE), the p-value (p), and the 95% confidence intervals (95% CI).

Model fitting and comparison

To identify the best-fitting computational model, parameter optimization was performed by maximizing the likelihood of observing participants’ choices during both the learning and transfer phases. Model fitting was implemented using the fmincon function in MATLAB software. Empirical and theoretical prior distributions were applied to constrain parameter estimates88,89. Specifically, the learning rate (αα), attentional weighting parameter (φφ), compression parameter (γγ), exploratory parameter (\varepsilonε) and the choice trace learning rate (ττ) were constrained by a Beta distribution (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{Beta}(\mathrm{1.1,1.1})$$\end{document}Beta(1.1, 1.1)). The inverse temperature (ββ) and power parameter (ωω) were assigned a Gamma distribution (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{Gamma}(\mathrm{1.2,5})$$\end{document}Gamma(1.2, 5)). The choice trace bias (ϕϕ) was given a Normal distribution (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathscr{N}}(\mathrm{0,1})$$\end{document}N(0, 1)).

The log model evidence was approximated by the negative half of the Bayesian Information Criterion (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{BIC}$$\end{document}−12BIC), computed for each model. These values were submitted to a random-effects Bayesian model selection (RFX-BMS) procedure at the group level90,91. RFX-BMS provides an exceedance probability (EP), which quantifies the likelihood that a given model is more frequently implemented across the population, relative to all other models in the model space.

Parameter and model recovery

Parameter and model recovery simulations were performed to ensure reliable parameter estimation and model distinctiveness54. Parameter values were uniformly sampled from the range of parameters observed in participants, generating data for 1000 simulated agents. These simulations mirrored the task structure, consisting of 180 trials in the learning phase (45 per context), with deterministic outcomes drawn from normal distributions (means of [14, 32, 50] for the narrow context and [14, 50, 86] for the wide context, with a fixed standard deviation of 44), and 198 trials in the transfer phase (three repetitions per pair). In the absence of a clear generative process for eye movements, fixation ratios in the attentional range models were randomly sampled between 0 and 1, ensuring that their sum across the three options was between 0 and 1. Parameter and model recovery were very good across all experiments and models (Supplementary Fig. 5).
