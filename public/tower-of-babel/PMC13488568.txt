Mental health, coping strategies and help-seeking behaviours among women petty traders in Nsukka, Enugu State, Nigeria: a cross-sectional survey
Authors: Chibueze Anosike, Gideon Ogbuagalaba Njoku, Nnanyelugo Ogechukwu Isiogugu, Chidimma Paschaline Ogbene, Miracle Ujunwa Odoabuchi, Amarachi Gift Nnaji, Chiamaka Jennifer Nwobodo, William Chinedu Neboh, Lotanna Hyacinth Obeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn Nigeria, women petty traders often face significant socio-economic stressors that potentially affect their mental health. Despite these challenges, mental health remains under-researched in informal economic sectors in developing countries. Therefore, this study examined the mental health status, coping strategies, and help-seeking behaviours of women petty traders in Nsukka, Enugu State.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 331 women petty traders at Ogige Market in Nsukka. Respondents were recruited through multi-stage sampling. Data were collected using interviewer-administered structured questionnaires, including a Patient Health questionnaire, a Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale, a Coping Orientation to Problems Experienced Inventory, and a General Help-seeking Questionnaire. The data obtained were analyzed using descriptive and inferential statistics. P < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.ResultsA total of 331 women participated in the survey. The prevalence of moderate to severe anxiety and depression was 22.9% and 27.4%, respectively, with most women utilizing emotion-focused coping strategies (29.35 ± 7.29). Additionally, the level of anxiety had a weak positive relationship with emotion-focused coping (r = 0.144, p = 0.008) and avoidant coping (r = 0.294, p < 0.001). A moderate positive correlation exists between the level of depression and avoidant coping (r = 0.307, p < 0.001). More than half of the respondents had the intention to seek help from intimate partners (n = 203, 61.3%), parents (n = 211, 63.7%), and family members (n = 182, 55.0%). More than half of the respondents were unlikely to seek help from mental health professionals (n = 186, 56.2%) and phone helplines (n = 197, 59.5%).ConclusionsA considerable proportion of women petty traders had moderate to severe anxiety and depression. The women were more inclined to seek emotional support from informal close social networks. The findings underscore the need to strengthen mental health support systems and promote accessible, socio-culturally and economically sensitive interventions that encourage the use of adaptive coping strategies and professional help among women petty traders.Keywords: Coping strategies, Help-seeking behaviour, Mental health, Petty traders, Women

Background

Mental health is a critical component of overall well-being and significantly influences an individual’s capacity to function socially, economically, and physically [1]. The prevalence of mental disorders is on the increase worldwide [2]. In 2025, more than one billion people are living with mental disorders [3, 4]. Worldwide, mental disorders accounts for approximately 5.1% of the disease burden and they are the leading cause of years lived with disability [5]. Generally, the burden of mental disorders appears to be higher in women than men. It is estimated that about one in 5 women experience a common mental health condition [6]. In low- and middle-income countries such as Nigeria, mental health issues often go underreported and undertreated [7], particularly among women in the informal economic sector. Owing to current hardship and the high unemployment rate in the formal sector, many people in Nigeria, especially females, operate multiple informal small-scale enterprises to survive, including petty trading [8, 9]. Petty trading is an informal economic activity that involves selling and buying goods in small quantities. It is commonly practiced among poor people. It is the fastest and relatively easiest to begin. Petty trading is a major source of daily income for many women. Hence, it enables women traders to care for their pressing needs and those of their households [9]. However, petty trading is associated with multiple stressors such as financial instability, long working hours, and exposure to harsh environmental and societal conditions [10, 11]. These challenges often lead to psychological distress, yet mental health remains a neglected aspect of public health in this context [11].

Women petty traders often juggle their businesses with domestic responsibilities such as cooking, cleaning, preparing meals for the family, and getting their children ready for school. Handling all these arrays of domestic responsibilities may further compound stress levels and mental health risks among women petty traders. Typically, these women petty traders have low income, limited access to formal education, and minimal social security, all of which heighten their vulnerability to anxiety, depression, and other mental health disorders [12, 13]. The intersection of gender roles, economic hardship, and inadequate access to healthcare services exacerbates the mental health burden in this group. Despite the high likelihood of mental distress, many affected women may not recognize their symptoms as health problems, nor do they seek appropriate professional help.

Coping strategies are important in determining how individuals manage mental stress [14]. Among women in the informal economy, coping mechanisms may range from adaptive methods such as social support and spirituality to maladaptive behaviours like substance use or emotional suppression [14, 15]. The choice of coping strategies is often influenced by cultural beliefs, education, financial status, and access to healthcare services [16]. Understanding these patterns is essential for designing culturally appropriate mental health interventions that are both accessible and acceptable to this population.

Help-seeking behaviour, particularly in the context of mental health, is influenced by a complex interplay of personal beliefs and attitudes, and social, economic, and cultural factors [17]. Formal mental health services may be inaccessible to many women in developing countries due to stigma, cost, or lack of awareness [18–20] Instead, they may rely on informal networks (e.g. family members, friends), traditional healers, or religious institutions to manage psychological distress [18]. Despite the increasing recognition of mental health as a global health priority, there is limited empirical data on how women in informal sector, who face daily economic and social stressors, manage psychological distress and access mental health care.

In Nigeria, there is a growing burden of mental health disorders, particularly among economically vulnerable populations [21, 22]. On the other hand, access to mental health services and formal psychosocial support remains limited due to poverty, stigma, poor awareness, and inadequate healthcare infrastructure [21, 22]. Women petty traders constitute a large segment of the informal workforce in Nigeria and are frequently exposed to financial instability, occupational stress, caregiving responsibilities, and harsh working conditions that may adversely affect their mental health and coping behaviours. Hence, this study would bridge a critical gap in public health research by focusing on the mental health status, coping strategies, and help-seeking behaviours of women petty traders—a marginalized and often overlooked population in Nigeria. The study would also provide evidence informing gender-sensitive and contextually relevant mental health interventions and policies targeting women in the informal economy. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the mental health status, coping strategies, and help-seeking behaviours of women petty traders in Nsukka, Enugu State, to inform targeted mental health interventions and policies that can address the unique challenges faced by this population.

Methods

Study design, setting, and population

This study was a descriptive quantitative cross-sectional survey. The study was conducted in the Nsukka main market, also known as the Ogige market. It is the second-largest market in Enugu State. The market is centrally located in Nsukka, a semi-urban settlement [23]. People from neighbouring regions and states come to the Ogige market to buy and sell various goods and services, especially perishable and nonperishable foods, clothing, and household items. The market is officially open for commercial activities from Monday to Saturday, between 8 am and 6 pm. Unfortunately, some challenges have hindered the smooth commercial operation of traders in the Ogige market. Deteriorating sanitation facilities, poor waste management, and excessive and multiple levies by the government are among the notable issues that traders face in the market [23]. Since Ogige market is a busy local market with a high concentration of women traders from diverse socioeconomic backgrounds, it is, therefore, an appropriate setting for assessing mental health challenges, coping strategies, and help-seeking behaviours among women in the informal economy. The study population was women petty traders in Ogige market, Nsukka, Enugu State, Nigeria. Women constitute a significant proportion of petty traders in Ogige market.

Sample size determination

The sample size for this survey was determined using the Raosoft online sample size calculator (www.raosoft.com/samplesize.html) [24]. The population size of women petty traders in the Ogige market in Nsukka was assumed to be 2,000. This assumed population was based on the data obtained from the Chairperson of Ogige Market Amalgamated Traders Association of Nsukka (OMATAN). According to the union’s record, there were 1,772 registered women at the time of the study. However, the figure was rounded off to 2,000 to account for some women traders who may not be financial members of the association. The margin of error, confidence interval, and response distribution were fixed at 5%, 95%, and 50%, respectively. Based on these parameters, the calculated sample size was 323 respondents.

Sampling procedure

The respondents for the study were recruited following simple random sampling. To ensure a fair representation, the distinct market sections (clusters) were first identified based on the type of goods sold in the area. The market was divided into eight clusters, and they include: (1) Perishable food items, (2) Provisions and beverages, (3) Textile materials and second-hand clothing, (4) Kitchen Utensils, (5) Abattoir and frozen products, (6) Stationeries and office equipment, (7) Household furniture, and (8) Palm and vegetable oil. Subsequently, 40–50 women, one per shop, were selected from each of the eight zones.

Eligibility criteria

The respondents were recruited based on the following inclusion criteria: (1) adult aged 18 and above, (2) female traders, (3) ability to understand English regardless of the level of proficiency, (4) willingness to participate, (5) provided informed consent to participate in the study. Only women who could understand English were included in the study because the study instruments and consent materials were prepared and validated in English. This was necessary to ensure accurate comprehension of the questionnaire items, reduce the risk of misinterpretation of mental health-related concepts, and maintain consistency and reliability in participants’ responses during data collection. Nonetheless, young adolescents below 18 years and women who declined to provide informed consent were excluded from the study.

Recruitment and training of research assistants

Six research assistants were recruited, trained, and deployed for data collection. The research assistants were recruited primarily to collect data in this survey. The research assistants had a background in the health sciences field and were experienced in surveys. A 2-day in-person training on the study protocol, instruments and data collection process was provided for the data collectors by the research team. The principal investigator took the lead in training the data collectors with the assistance of other research team members. The training helped the research assistants become acquainted with the study objectives and the instruments; and how to proceed with the data collection. At the end of the training, each research assistant was required to conduct a spot-simulated interview to assess how well they understood the data collection process. The research team provided feedback and corrections as needed.

Study instruments

The questionnaire had five sections. The sections include the Sociodemographic information, Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD-7) Scale, Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), Coping Orientation to Problems Experienced Inventory (Brief-COPE), and the General Help-seeking Questionnaire (GHSQ). The first section on sociodemographic information captured respondents’ age, level of education, marital status, size of household, religion, years of trading experience, and level of physical activity.

Generalized anxiety disorder scale

Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD-7) was used in this study to assess the severity of anxiety symptoms among the study respondents [25]. The instrument was initially developed as a screening tool for anxiety disorder in primary care settings. The GAD-7 instrument contains seven items drafted from the criteria for diagnosing generalized anxiety disorder according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual for Mental Disorders (DSM-IV). The instrument has been previously validated and used in several Nigerian population [26–28]. The Likert-scale instrument has seven, four-point Likert items (0 = not at all; 1 = several days; 2 = more than half the days; 3 = nearly every day) and is simple to complete. The total score can range from 0 (no anxiety) to 21 (more anxiety), with the level of anxiety defined as normal (0 to 4), mild (5 to 9), moderate (10 to 14), and severe (15 to 21). The higher the total score on GAD-7, the higher the severity of anxiety symptoms.

Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9)

The prevalence of depressive symptoms was measured using the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) [29]. The PHQ-9 scale is designed to evaluate the severity of depressive symptoms that a person has experienced in the previous two weeks [29]. The PHQ-9 contains nine items and is based on the criteria for diagnosing depression as stipulated in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual for Mental Disorders (DSM-IV). The PHQ-9 is widely validated and has been used in previous Nigerian studies across various settings [30–33]. The questionnaire items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale (0 = not at all; 1 = several days; 2 = more than half the days; 3 = nearly every day). Total score can range from 0 (no depression) to 27 (more depression), with the level of depression defined as normal (0–4), mild (5–9), moderate (10–14), moderately severe (15–19), and severe (20–27). Higher aggregate scores indicate a higher severity of depression symptoms among the respondents.

Coping orientation to problems experienced inventory

The Coping Orientation to Problems Experienced Inventory (Brief-COPE) is a 28-item self-reported questionnaire for assessing how individuals cope with stressful life events [34]. The Brief-COPE questionnaire has been validated previously and used in Nigerian studies [35, 36]. The questionnaire has a 4-point Likert scale (‘I haven’t been doing this at all’ = 1, ‘a little bit’ = 2, ‘a medium amount’ = 3, and ‘I’ve been doing this a lot = 4’). The questionnaire is broadly divided into 3 subscales or coping styles: (1) Problem-focused coping, (2) Emotion-focused coping, and (3) Avoidant coping. The problem-focused subscale consists of items 2, 7, 10, 12, 14, 17, 23, and 25. This subscale is characterized by facets such as active coping, use of informational support, planning, and positive reframing. The high score in this subscale indicates coping strategies aimed at changing a stressful situation. A higher score also shows that the respondent has psychological strength, grit, and problem-solving skills; and is predictive of a positive outcome. On the other hand, the emotion-focused subscale included items 5, 9, 13, 15, 18, 20–22, 24, and 26–28. This subscale consists of facets like venting, use of emotional support, humour, acceptance, self-blame and religion. A high score indicates the use of coping strategies aimed at regulating emotions associated with stressful situations. The last subscale, avoidant coping consisted of items 1, 3, 4, 6, 8, 11, 16, and 19. This subscale is characterized by self-distraction, denial, substance use, and behavioural disengagement. A high score indicates physical or cognitive efforts to disengage from the causes of a stressful situation. However, low scores in the avoidant subscale indicate an adaptive coping mechanism.

General help-seeking questionnaire

Help-seeking behaviour of the study respondents was assessed using the General Help-seeking Questionnaire (GHSQ) [37]. The GHSQ is a self-reported measure of a person’s intention to seek help from both formal and informal sources when feeling anxious and depressed. According the original developers, the instrument has good psychometric properties, with Cronbach’s alpha and test-retest reliability values of 0.85 and 0.88, respectively [37]. This instrument has been widely used in studies assessing general helping seeking behaviour of respondents in various settings, including Nigeria [30, 38]. The questionnaire has nine different sources of help. In the questionnaire, the respondents were asked to rate on a 7-point Likert scale (‘extremely unlikely’ = 1 to ‘extremely likely’ to indicate their likelihood of seeking help from the various help source options, including ‘not seeking help’. In interpreting GHSQ, higher scores indicate higher intentions to seek help.

Pilot test and questionnaire validation

A pilot test was conducted in Obukpa market in Nsukka, Enugu, prior to the actual survey. Twenty women traders were interviewed during the pilot test. The objective of the pilot test was to assess the clarity, relevance, cultural appropriateness, and feasibility of the study questionnaires and data collection procedure. The questionnaires were completed by respondents in an average time of 25 min. Minor changes were made to the study instrument based on the respondents’ feedback during the pilot test. The data collected were analysed to determine the reliability and internal consistency of the questionnaires by estimating Cronbach’s alpha. The internal consistency of the instrument was good as Cronbach’s alpha for GAD-7, PHQ-9, Brief-COPE, and GHSQ were 0.785, 0.790, 0.837, and 0.712, respectively. A Cronbach alpha of 0.7 and above is generally acceptable. The data generated from the pilot test were excluded from the main survey.

Data collection

Before data collection, the research team briefed each respondent on the study objectives and protocols. The respondents were asked to complete an informed consent form indicating their approval to participate in the survey. Thereafter, the printed structured questionnaires were administered to each respondent by the trained interviewer. The interviewer read the questions and the options and then recorded the responses provided by the respondents. Data collection lasted from April 1 to April 29, 2025.

Data analysis

The data obtained were coded and entered into Microsoft Excel 2016. The data were exported to IBM Statistical Product and Service Solutions version 27 (IBM SPSS v. 27) for analysis. Descriptive statistics and Pearson correlation test were used to analyze the data. Respondents’ sociodemographic characteristics, prevalence of anxiety and depression symptoms, and coping strategies were summarized using descriptive statistics. The relationship between coping strategies and the levels of anxiety and depression symptoms was examined using the Pearson correlation test. The level of significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

The ethical approval for this study was granted by the Ethics Committee of the Department of Clinical Pharmacy and Pharmacy Management, Faculty of Pharmaceutical Sciences, University of Nigeria, Nsukka on March 28, 2025, with a reference number CPPM/HREC/25/UG/0006. Written informed consent was obtained from all eligible respondents. The questionnaires were anonymized and no personally identifiable information about the respondents was collected. The data collected were handled with utmost confidence.

Help-seeking behaviour of women petty traders

Table 4 illustrates the help-seeking behaviour of the study respondents. The findings showed that more than half of the women petty traders had the intention to seek help from intimate partners (n = 203, 61.3%), parents (n = 211, 63.7%), family members (n = 182, 55.0%), and religious leaders (n = 167, 50.5%). More than half of the women (n = 186, 56.2%) were unlikely to seek help from mental health professionals and phone helplines (n = 197, 59.5%). About 43.5% of the respondents (n = 144) do not have any intention of seeking help from a doctor or general practitioner. However, only about 18.4% of the women admitted that they would not seek help from anyone when they feel anxious or depressed.

Discussion

This study examined the mental health status, coping strategies, and help-seeking behaviours of women petty traders in Nsukka, Enugu State, Nigeria. The findings demonstrated that about 22.9% of the women had symptoms of moderate to severe anxiety, while approximately 27.4% had symptoms of moderate to severe depression. Emotion-focused and problem-focused coping strategies were more commonly used among the women traders. The level of anxiety had a weak positive relationship with problem-focused and avoidant coping strategies. On the other hand, the level of depression had a moderate positive relationship with the avoidant coping strategy. However, the levels of anxiety and depression were unrelated to problem-focused coping strategies. The majority of the women expressed a preference for informal help-seeking sources, including intimate partners, parents, family members, and religious leaders.

The present study documented a notable burden of psychological distress among women petty traders. The burden of anxiety and depression observed in women traders exceeded those reported in a related study. For example, a study in Bangladesh among reproductive-aged women reported a low prevalence of moderate to severe anxiety (4%) and depression (5%) [39]. These rates suggest that a significant proportion of this economically vulnerable population may be struggling with psychological distress, likely exacerbated by factors such as financial instability, job insecurity, social pressures, and limited access to mental health services [40]. The implications are substantial, as unaddressed anxiety and depression can impair productivity [41], decision-making, and overall well-being [42], potentially trapping these women in a cycle of poverty and poor health. These findings highlight the urgent need for targeted mental health interventions, community-based support systems, and policies that address the socioeconomic stressors contributing to poor mental health outcomes in this group.

Emotion-focused coping emerged as the predominant coping strategy among women petty traders, consistent with prior evidence demonstrating that women are more likely than men to employ emotion-focused approaches when managing psychological stressors [43, 44]. Women resorting to emotion-focused coping include those of African descent, like the African-American women [44]. This finding suggests that these women petty traders are inclined to manage mental health stressors by addressing their emotional responses rather than actively solving the underlying issues. To a certain extent, emotion-focused coping can offer short-term psychological relief. However, it will not lead to long-term resolution of the stressors, particularly in situations requiring practical problem-solving skills. The reliance on an emotion-focused strategy could reflect limitations in external resources, lack of control over the stressor, or sociocultural tendencies favouring emotional expression. This finding underscores the need for appropriate interventions to improve the capacity of women petty traders for problem-focused coping. Such interventions can promote more adaptive responses and improved mental health outcomes over time.

Anxiety severity was not significantly associated with problem-focused coping, suggesting that women experiencing heightened anxiety may not readily engage in proactive, solution-oriented behaviours. Instead, anxiety was positively associated with both emotion-focused and avoidant coping strategies, a pattern consistent with prior research [45]. This implies that anxious women are more likely to rely on coping strategies that deal with emotional distress or avoid anxiety-inducing situations altogether. The observed pattern may reflect a tendency for the women to seek immediate emotional relief rather than addressing the root cause of anxiety, which could contribute to prolonged psychological distress [44]. Therefore, interventions promoting more adaptive coping mechanisms, especially problem-focused strategies, may likely help fortify women with high anxiety levels and reduce their reliance on less effective emotional or avoidant responses [46].

Depression severity was unrelated to both problem-focused and emotion-focused coping, indicating that these coping styles do not meaningfully co-vary with depressive symptom burden in the present sample. However, depression was positively associated with avoidant coping, consistent with prior findings across diverse clinical and community contexts. Hughes and colleagues similarly reported that women are significantly more likely than men to engage in avoidant coping when confronted with distressing circumstances [47]. Another study found a positive relationship between COVID-19-induced stress levels and avoidant coping among pregnant women in Ireland [48]. This suggests that women experiencing depression may be more likely to disengage from stressors rather than confront them directly, which could perpetuate or exacerbate their condition over time.

There is a strong preference among women petty traders for seeking emotional support from close social networks, including intimate partners, parents, family members, and religious leaders, over formal mental health services. This reliance on informal sources highlights cultural and possibly socioeconomic influences that shape help-seeking behaviour, with stigma, lack of awareness, or limited access likely contributing to the reluctance to engage with mental health professionals, phone helplines, or medical practitioners [49]. The fact that over half would not turn to formal care points to a significant gap in mental health literacy and access. Also, only a few women admitted that they would not seek help at all. Overall, these observations suggest that although most women are intentional about seeking help, their choices are confined within familiar and socially acceptable boundaries. Appropriate mental health education, culturally sensitive outreach, and community-integrated services may be indispensable in encouraging professional help-seeking behaviour and stigma reduction among women petty traders in this study [50].

Study strength and limitations

The strength of this study lies on its focus on an understudied and vulnerable population—women petty traders in Nsukka, Enugu State, Nigeria. These women often experience substantial economic, social, and psychological stressors but receive limited attention in mental health research. This study provides a comprehensive understanding of the psychological wellbeing and behavioural responses of women in the informal trading sector. Additionally, the study was conducted within a real-world market environment, thus enhancing the ecological validity of the findings, as participants are assessed within their natural occupational and social context.

This study had a few limitations. First, this study was unable to determine a causal relationship between mental health, coping strategies, and help-seeking behaviour over time because of the cross-sectional design of the present study. Second, the study relied on self-reported data obtained through interviewer-administered questionnaires that may introduce recall bias and social desirability bias. Some respondents may have underreported or exaggerated their mental health status and help-seeking behaviour. Third, there is a possibility of sampling bias considering that the study respondents were limited to women petty traders who understand English. Women with little or no formal education may have different experience or opinions from those interviewed in this study.

Regarding future research direction, there is a need for future studies employing longitudinal or mixed-methods designs to better understand the temporal relationship between mental health, coping strategies, and help-seeking behaviours among women petty traders in Nsukka. A longitudinal approach would provide stronger evidence regarding causality and changes in psychological well-being over time, particularly in response to economic hardship, family responsibilities, and market-related stressors. Additionally, future qualitative studies exploring the lived experiences, cultural beliefs, and contextual factors influencing mental health and help-seeking practices among women traders are recommended.

Conclusion

The study findings suggest that a substantial proportion of women petty traders had moderate to severe symptoms of anxiety and depression. Most women in this study used emotion-focused coping strategies to suppress symptoms of anxiety and depression. The women’s anxiety levels were positively related to emotion-focused and avoidant coping strategies, and they tend to rely on avoidant coping strategies in managing depressive symptoms. The women were more inclined to seek emotional support from informal close social networks, such as intimate partners, parents, family members, and religious leaders, than formal assistance from mental health professionals. The findings underscore the need to strengthen mental health support systems and promote accessible, socio-culturally and economically sensitive interventions that encourage the use of adaptive coping strategies and professional help among women petty traders.
