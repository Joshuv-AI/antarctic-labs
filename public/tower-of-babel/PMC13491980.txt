Epidemiological, clinical and virological profile of Mpox in Kinshasa, democratic republic of the Congo, 2024–2025: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Grace Mamibel Lassio, Harry César Kayembe, Emmanuel Hasivirwe Vakaniaki, Prince Akil Bandali, Germain Kapour Kieng Katsang, Kieng Katsang, Thérèse Tshibola, Tantan Mulamba Kalubi, Jerry Vandam Mukayonde, Didier Nkoko Bompangue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMpox is an emerging viral zoonosis whose transmission has gradually moved to urban settings since 2022. This study aimed to describe the epidemiological, clinical, and virological characteristics of confirmed cases of Mpox in Kinshasa, the capital of the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), between 2024 and 2025.MethodsWe conducted a descriptive cross-sectional study using data collected from mpox treatment centers and the National Institute for Biomedical Research. Patients with polymerase chain reaction (PCR) results and complete medical records were included in the study. We calculated incidence from laboratory-confirmed cases and described the geographic distribution at the health zone level. The chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate, as well as the Mann–Whitney test, were performed to compare sociodemographic, clinical, and virological characteristics.ResultsBetween 2024 and 2025, the vast majority of health zones were affected, with the highest incidences observed in urban areas: Limete, Bumbu, Kalamu II, Kokolo, and Lingwala. At the individual level, a higher proportion of positive mpox cases were observed among people aged 15 to 34 years (58.3%), married (33.3%), unemployed (27.6%), and involved in sex work (93.0%). In addition, comorbidities were rare but significant, with longer hospitalization (15 [9–20] days) and frequent reports of fever, pruritus, lymphadenopathy, and skin ulcers. Overall, 55.5% of laboratory-confirmed cases were associated with clade Ia, compared to 45.5% with clade Ib. In the most affected age group mentioned above, the proportion of clade Ia was higher in males, while clade Ib was prevalent in females.ConclusionMpox in Kinshasa is characterized by persistent transmission in urban areas, affecting the majority of health zones, as well as by the co-circulation of subclades Ia and Ib. Young adults aged 15 to 34 years were the most affected group, with clade Ia predominance in males and clade Ib in females. These results highlight the need to strengthen epidemiological surveillance, early detection, and case follow-up to adapt mpox control strategies in the urban context of Kinshasa.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13765-x.

Background

Mpox is a viral zoonosis caused by the monkeypox virus (MPXV), a member of the genus Orthopoxvirus in the Poxviridae family [1], composed of clade I (formerly Congo Basin) and clade II (formerly West African) [2]. Both clades comprise subclades 1a and 1b; IIa and II b. Historically, the disease was confined to rural areas of Central and West Africa, with transmission occurring mainly through zoonotic exposure [3]. Since the early 2000s, and more specifically since 2022, Mpox has re-emerged with sustainable human-to-human transmission and geographical expansion to urban and non-endemic countries [4]. In urban areas, high population density, close contact and population mobility facilitate transmission, making large African cities particularly vulnerable to disease outbreaks [5]. Clinical manifestations include fever, asthenia, lymphadenopathy, rashes, and mucosal lesions, with variations by age, immune status, and viral clade [6].

Since 2022, Mpox has become a global public health concern, with outbreaks reported on several continents [7], breaking with the classic epidemiological pattern and demonstrating the adaptation of the virus to new social and environmental contexts [8]. In Africa, this evolving epidemiological landscape is taking place in fragile health systems characterized by high population mobility and limited surveillance capacity [9]. Available reports indicate that the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) has recorded about 19,667 cases and 575 deaths as of August 25, 2024, during the African outbreak, making it by far the most affected country on the continent [10]. Mpox has therefore become a national public health priority, with a clear resurgence since 2023 [11].

In response, Congolese health authorities, with the support of international partners, have set up an Mpox incident management system, including thorough investigations of suspected cases, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) laboratory confirmation, and patient care at dedicated Mpox treatment centers [12]. However, delayed diagnosis, limited diagnostic capacity, and lack of integrated data systems remain major challenges [13].

The city of Kinshasa, the capital of the DRC, has experienced a major outbreak since the geographical expansion of the MPXV I clade and its repeated introduction in several urban centers in 2023 [14]. The epidemiological situation of Mpox in the DRC worsened significantly in 2024, with a total of 7,851 suspected cases reported, including 384 deaths, corresponding to a high case fatality rate of 4.9%. The most affected populations included children, pregnant women, and people living with HIV (18 years old).

A recent retrospective observational study conducted in Kinshasa between 2023 and 2024 reported notable results regarding sustained human transmission of the Ia subclade of MPXV. This study demonstrated the continued transmission of this subclade, alongside co-circulation with the Ib subclade since July 2024. In addition, it highlighted significant changes in the transmission dynamics of MPXV and the ability of several subclades to cause sustained human flare-ups under conditions favorable to transmission [15]. More recently, investigations have documented the importation of the clade MPXV IIb into Kinshasa from West Africa. This introduction occurred following local transmission from a clue case to its partner, with a phylogenetic link to the ongoing outbreak in Sierra Leone (IIb/SH2017) [14]. To date, pending confirmation, these two cases represent the only documented occurrences of the MPXV IIb/SH2017 clade in Kinshasa.

In recent years, the epidemiological profile of Mpox in Kinshasa has undergone substantial transformations, reflecting rapid changes in the transmission dynamics of MPXV. These changes were accompanied by marked changes in the epidemiological, sociodemographic, clinical and virologic characteristics of the reported cases. The available data suggest a gradual shift from a historically zoonotic and community-based pattern of transmission to more complex patterns of human-to-human transmission, influenced by behavioral, urban, and structural factors [15].

In a context characterized by accelerated changes since 2023, the most recent data describing the sociodemographic and clinical characteristics associated with the main MPXV subclades (Ia and Ib) co-circulating in Kinshasa largely date from the end of 2024 [15]. The prolonged co-circulation of these subclades, documented in several contexts [16], is likely to generate differentiated clinical profiles and distinct transmission patterns, reflecting both virological variation and heterogeneous social determinants. It is crucial to determine whether the sociodemographic and clinical characteristics associated with infections caused by the Ia and Ib subclades of MPXV evolved differently between 2023 and 2024 and the following period. This would reflect dynamic viral adaptation and changing modes of transmission in a rapidly changing urban environment.

Thus, this study aimed to describe the epidemiological, clinical and virological profile of laboratory-confirmed Mpox cases in Kinshasa between 2024 and 2025.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a cross-sectional descriptive study based on secondary epidemiological, clinical, and biological data related to mpox cases recorded in different mpox treatment centers (Kikole, Kinoise, Kokolo, and Vijana) in the city of Kinshasa, Democratic Republic of Congo, between January 2024 and December 2025.

Data were obtained from mpox treatment center databases as well as surveillance databases (INRB) used for national response and epidemiological surveillance activities.

Kinshasa has 35 health zones and an estimated population of more than 17 million. High population density, promiscuity and increased urban mobility are factors that promote the transmission of mpox from one person to another.

Case definition and study population

In accordance with national mpox surveillance guidelines, a suspected case was defined as any person with an acute rash or unexplained mucocutaneous lesions, with or without fever, associated with at least one of the following signs: lymphadenopathy, headache, myalgia, asthenia, back pain, pruritus, ulcers, or history of contact with a confirmed or probable case of mpox [17].

The initial clinical diagnosis of suspected cases was carried out in mpox treatment centers and hospitals in the city of Kinshasa.

This study included cases recorded in the databases of mpox treatment centers with PCR laboratory results, whether positive or negative for mpox virus (MPXV).

Confirmed cases were patients with a positive PCR test and a threshold cycle value (CT) of less than 40. Negative cases corresponded to patients with negative PCR.

Cases without PCR results and incomplete records were excluded from the study.

Laboratory procedures and biological confirmation

Biological samples (skin lesions and/or blood) were collected from the treatment centers and sent to the National Institute for Biomedical Research (INRB), where the analyses were carried out in the laboratories of Kinkole and Kinoise.

Biological confirmation of mpox was performed by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) targeting the Mpox virus (MPXV).

Biological confirmation and clade identification

The biological analyses were carried out at the National Institute for Biomedical Research (INRB), in the laboratories of Kikole and Kinoise.

Samples of skin lesions and/or blood were collected from suspected cases in accordance with national mpox surveillance procedures.

Diagnostic confirmation of mpox was performed by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) targeting the Mpox virus (MPXV). In this study, any sample with a Ct value less than 40 was considered positive for MPXV.

Data for viral subclades were extracted from the INRB virological database. This database also made it possible to analyze the spatial and temporal distribution of cases in the city of Kinshasa.

Only samples with a sufficient viral load, defined by a Ct value less than 30, were retained for the identification of viral subclades, including Clades Ia and Ib.

Spatial and temporal analysis

The spatial distribution of mpox cases in the city of Kinshasa was described using annual incidence maps by health zone. The temporal distribution of viral cases and subclades was also analyzed according to the periods of sample collection.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.2.0. Sociodemographic, clinical, and virologic characteristics of participants were presented as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, while quantitative variables were summarized by median and interquartile range (IQR). Comparisons between PCR-positive and PCR-negative patients were assessed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for qualitative variables, and the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test for quantitative variables. The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Description of the spatial distribution of Mpox in Kinshasa

Between 2024 and 2025, Mpox spread to almost all health zones in Kinshasa (33 out of 35 in 2024 and 34 out of 35 in 2025). Limete Health Zone recorded the largest increase in incidence, from 42.4 to 61 cases per 100,000 inhabitants, followed by Bumbu, Kalamu II and Lingwala with an increase of more than 41%. Ngiri-Ngiri, Selembao, and Kokolo health zones showed intermediate incidence, while Gombe reported no cases in 2025 (Fig. 1).

Figure 1 also shows variable co-circulation of subclades Ia and Ib, with clade Ia dominance across health zones. It should be noted that the Kalamu II health zone had a higher proportion of clade Ib than subclade Ia. In addition, two cases of MPXV clade IIb have been documented in Nsele Health Zone.

Discussion

This study aimed to characterize the epidemiological, clinical, and virological profile of Mpox in Kinshasa between 2024 and 2025. Our study shows that Mpox persists across Kinshasa during this period (34 out of 35 health zones), mainly affecting people aged 15 to 34 years, with significant clinical signs such as fever, pruritus, lymphadenopathy, and skin ulcers. In addition, it indicates the cocirculation of clades Ia and Ib at 55.5% and 45.5%, respectively. In the most affected age group (15–34 years), the number of positive cases of mpox associated with clade Ia was higher in males, while clade Ib was prevalent in females.

The spatial distribution indicates that 33 health zones were affected in 2024 and 34 in 2025. The incidence was particularly high in urban health zones such as Limete, Bumbu, Kalamu II, and Lingwala, where overcrowding and high population concentration are considered to promote close contact and transmission.

The distribution of subclades reveals the co-circulation of clades Ia and Ib in almost all health zones, except N’sele (single clade Ib), Maluku II (no cases identified), Limete (clade Ia dominant), and Kalamu II (clade Ib dominant). In our study, the proportions were 55.5% for clade Ia and 45.5% for clade Ib, indicating a relative increase in clade Ib compared to previous observations. In comparison, in a smaller sample (n = 440), a predominance of clade Ia (51%) and a small proportion of clade Ib (15%) were reported [15].

Although these results suggest progression of clade Ib, they should be interpreted with caution due to differences in sample size and study period. This discrepancy may also reflect the persistence of cases, increased transmission in urban areas, the possible introduction of cases from other provinces, and improvements in the surveillance and case detection system.

Individuals aged 15 to 34 years accounted for the majority of positive mpox cases, at 58.3%. Among the identified cases, the prevalence of mpox-positive cases associated with clade Ia was higher in males, while clade Ib was prevalent in females. This predominance could be explained by a higher frequency of close contact within this age group, which favors human-to-human transmission of mpox [18]. The virus is transmitted mainly through direct contact with infected skin lesions or mucous membranes, but also through respiratory secretions and contaminated objects. After exposure, the virus penetrates through skin microfissures or mucous membranes, then replicates locally before lymphatic and systemic dissemination responsible for clinical manifestations. In a dense urban context such as Kinshasa, close interactions and promiscuity can thus facilitate community chains of transmission [19]. In children aged 0 to 14 years, the cases observed may be mainly related to intra-family transmission, including close contact with infected people, sharing objects, or daily care within the household [20]. This observation supports the hypothesis of a non-exclusively sexual transmission of mpox in this context.

In terms of occupations, the unemployed (27.6%) were the most represented among the cases. In contrast, people involved in sex work accounted for a very small proportion of the sample (1.2%), although the majority of them tested positive for mpox (93.0%). This low representation may be underestimated due to the social stigma associated with this activity. These findings corroborate previous studies, which highlight the importance of redefining at-risk populations beyond traditionally identified groups and taking urban and social dynamics into account in intervention planning [21].

Chronic diseases (hypertension, diabetes, cancer) were rare and seen mainly in positive cases, while chronic infections such as HIV, tuberculosis, and STIs were not significantly associated. Although uncommon, this medical history could complicate clinical management and contribute to a longer hospital stay in some patients. Some chronic comorbidities can alter the immune response, slow down the healing of skin lesions, and promote infectious or inflammatory complications, thus requiring clinical monitoring and prolonged care. In immunocompromised patients or patients with chronic pathologies, the clinical course of mpox may be slower and associated with more severe manifestations [22]. Smallpox vaccination coverage was very low (0.6%), suggesting limited population immunity. Clinically, Rash and genital/anorectal lesions were among the most common manifestations in mpox-positive patients, with 83.3% for rash and 83.8% for genital/anorectal lesions, respectively. However, the difference in mpox-negative patients did not appear to be statistically significant (p = 0.073). This could be explained by the presence of other dermatoses with similar clinical presentations, thus reducing the specificity of these signs [23]. On the other hand, fever, pruritus, lymphadenopathy, and skin ulcers seem to be more discriminating manifestations of mpox. Thus, skin signs should be interpreted with caution and confirmed by PCR, to avoid diagnostic confusion with other dermatological conditions [24].

The median length of hospital stay was longer in positive cases (15 days) than in negative cases (10 days), which may reflect the more prolonged clinical course of mpox, characterized by gradual resolution of skin lesions and the need for management until complete recovery. Several studies have described a clinical course extending over several weeks, with the need for prolonged care in the event of complicated or painful forms [25, 26].

The presence of a medical history in some positive patients, although uncommon, could also contribute to the complexity of management and influence the length of hospitalization, particularly in the case of comorbidities likely to slow down clinical recovery or increase therapeutic needs. However, this hypothesis remains exploratory due to the small number of cases concerned and the lack of multivariate analysis to confirm this association.

Finally, the differences observed in the distribution of clades according to the type of samples could be related to the stage of lesions. Vesicular and blood samples probably correspond to the early phases of infection, while crusted lesions reflect the late phases, with a predominance of clade Ia. This observation underlines the importance of the choice of sampling type and timing in order to optimize virological detection.

Strengths and limitations

To the best of our knowledge, this study has yielded a significant dataset on MPXV infection in a sub-Saharan urban context. However, it is important to note that there are also significant limitations. First, the cross-sectional nature of this study did not allow for the establishment of causal relationships between sociodemographic, clinical, and virological characteristics and the occurrence of mpox infection. Second, the present study relied on the consideration of hospital-associated mpox-positive cases and mpox-negative cases, which could have led to an underestimation of the true burden of the disease. Third, the consolidation of secondary data from different sources indicates that there were discrepancies between the available data and that certain variables contained numerous gaps, which may have biased the results. Some epidemiological and clinical information were missing for a proportion of patients, which could affect the accuracy of our analytical framework.

Conclusion

Mpox continues to circulate actively in Kinshasa, with a wide geographical distribution and a predominance among young adults. The co-circulation of clades Ia and Ib, as well as the variability of clinical and demographic profiles, underlines a complex epidemiological dynamic in urban areas. These findings call for enhanced surveillance, early detection of cases, and adaptation of prevention and vaccination strategies to local realities.
