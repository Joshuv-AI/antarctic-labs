Executive Summary
PFE Auxiliary Systems conducted drop testing on SAVY-4000 containers to evaluate structural response
under 12-foot drop conditions. In support of that effort, a finite-element modeling capability was
developed to simulate drop response across multiple container sizes and impact orientations. The purpose
of this work was to provide a consistent analysis framework that could support interpretation of testing,
compare response trends across multiple configurations, and generate quantities of interest for later
comparison with experimental data. More broadly, the analysis and testing were intended to assess
whether the containers continued to perform their primary function after a 12-foot drop, namely
maintaining structural integrity and containment of the contents.

The modeling approach combined an implicit preload analysis with an explicit drop simulation so that
each drop event began from a mechanically realistic assembled condition, including compression of the
silicone O-ring. Separate models were developed for 2-quart, 5-quart, 12-quart, and 10-gallon containers.
The results were evaluated in terms of strain-gauge response, collar-lid gap behavior, and accumulated
plastic strain. In addition, parametric studies were performed on the 2-quart container to assess sensitivity
to O-ring stiffness, friction, canister thickness, geometry tolerance, and mesh density.

The simulations showed that predicted drop responses depended strongly on both container size and drop
orientation. Gap metrics identified cases in which the predicted collar-lid opening exceeded the nominal
O-ring cross-section threshold, while plastic strain metrics identified localized regions of elevated
permanent deformation. Parametric studies showed that the predicted response was especially sensitive to
the assumed O-ring stiffness and contact friction, while the geometry tolerance study produced smaller
changes in the cases examined.

The main value of this work was that it established a repeatable modeling and simulation workflow to
support drop-test implementation, evaluate effects of future configuration changes, and understand
modeling assumptions that most influenced predicted response. At the current stage, the results were
viewed as preliminary model predictions rather than validated predictions. The next step would be to
compare drop-test data to the model so that predictive values of the workflow could be refined and used
with greater confidence to assess whether the containers maintained structural integrity and containment
of the contents after a 12-foot drop.

 Introduction
PFE Auxiliary Systems conducted drop testing on SAVY-4000 containers, which are designed to contain
material during handling and accident scenarios. These tests were performed to evaluate whether the
containers maintained structural integrity and contained their contents after a 12-foot drop in different
impact orientations. In support of this effort, the E-13 Advanced Engineering Analysis group was tasked
with developing finite-element (FE) models to simulate these drop events and provide quantities of
interest relevant to test interpretation across multiple container sizes.

The objective of this work was to develop an FE modeling capability of SAVY-4000 container drops
across multiple container sizes and drop orientations, with an emphasis on predicting strain-gauge
response for later comparison with experimental data. The model was also used to evaluate lid-gap
metrics and plastic strain measures to assess sealing-interface opening and permanent local deformation.
The goal was to determine whether the container continued to perform its intended function after a 12-
foot drop by maintaining its structural integrity and containment of its contents.

This effort was intended to complement experimental testing by supporting interpretation of test results
and evaluation of future configuration changes. FE modeling could help reduce testing cost by decreasing
the number of physical tests needed to obtain the required information, while also supporting assessment
of whether the container continued to perform its containment function after a 12-foot drop.

This work was organized in a repository containing the SAVY-4000 WAVES workflow implementation,
including source part geometry, Cubit journals, SCons workflow scripts, Abaqus input templates, post-
processing scripts, and supporting documentation. Additional WAVES documentation was provided in
Appendix 8.2.

 Numerical Model/Modeling Approach
The SAVY-4000 drop model was developed as a sequential FE analysis consisting of two Abaqus
analyses: an implicit analysis followed by an explicit analysis. The implicit analysis was used to preload
the silicone ring in the lid through a shrink-fit step. The resulting deformed configuration and stress state
were then imported into the explicit analysis as the initial condition. The explicit analysis simulated the
drop impact for each container size and drop orientation considered in this work. The implicit-explicit
simulation approach was used so that the drop event began from a mechanically realistic pre-impact
condition rather than an unstressed assembly configuration. The following sections describe the model
assembly, material definitions, modeling assumptions, analysis setup, and post processing metrics used in
this work.

 Assembly and Parts

CAD geometry for the SAVY-4000 containers was provided by PFE Auxiliary Systems in the form of
SolidWorks parts and assemblies. The SolidWorks geometry was converted into Cubit parts, where the
parts were defeatured, partitioned, and meshed. The FE models were developed to retain the detail needed
to represent the primary structural component governing drop response for each container configuration.

The 5-quart, 12-quart and 10-gallon container sizes followed the same modeling workflow as the 2-quart

All container components and internal mass representations were modeled as deformable bodies. The
impact surface was modeled as an analytical rigid floor. The strain-gauge sections were assigned a cross-
sectional dimension of 0.21 mm, allowing strain-gauge response extraction with minimal effects on the
structural stiffness or overall drop response in the model. Geometry preparation and part definitions
developed in Cubit were used to support both the implicit preload analysis and subsequent drop analysis.

 Mesh and Element Types

The deformable structural components of the SAVY-4000 container models were meshed in Cubit using
8-node 3D solid brick elements with reduced integration (C3D8R) [1]. Strain gauges were modeled
separately using a single truss element per gauge to represent the strain-gauge response at the selected
attachment locations. Mesh generation was controlled primarily through a global mesh size, with
additional local refinement applied to components most relevant to structural response, including the
canister, collar, lid, and locking collar. In contrast, components such as handles and pins were generally
meshed more coarsely. Additional elements through the canister thickness were included to better capture
through-thickness deformation during the drop event. Figure 5 shows the mesh refinement on the
container, including 2 elements through the container thickness and a mesh refinement at the container-
collar tie including 3 elements through the container thickness at this tie region.

 Strain Gauges

The strain gauges were represented using linear elastic truss elements with simplified material properties
 𝑔
of density 8.9 𝑐𝑐, elastic modulus of 1.6 × 105 MPa, and Poisson’s ratio of 0.3. These elements were
included as a convenient means of reporting local strain responses at selected locations during the drop
event, rather than as a detailed physical model of the gauges themselves. Accordingly, the strain-gauge
representation was treated as an elastic response extraction feature. The assigned section size was selected
to be small enough that the strain-gauge features would not materially influence the structural stiffness or
global response of the model. The effect of the assumed strain-gauge stiffness on predicted response was
not investigated in the present work and could be examined in future sensitivity studies.

 Modeling Assumptions

The FE model was developed to capture the structural response of the SAVY-4000 container during drop
tests. The following simplifications and assumptions were made in the present work:

 • Geometry was defeatured as needed during model preparation to support partitioning and mesh
 generation.

 • The impact surface was modeled conservatively as an analytical rigid body, such that all impact-
 induced deformation was assumed to occur within the container assembly.

Los Alamos National Laboratory Page 2-7

 • Internal contents of the drop test containers included steel shot and cerium oxide powder. These
 were not modeled in detail; instead, simplified internal mass representations were used to
 reproduce the effect of payload mass during impact. The density of the internal mass was adjusted
 so that the combined container and contents weight matched the corresponding experimental
 configuration.

 • Tie constraints were used to model the welded interfaces, specifically the weld between the
 canister and collar. For the 10-gallon configuration, tie constraints were used to model the weld
 connecting the canister body and the bottom of the canister.

 • The model was not intended to directly simulate leakage or fracture propagation. The present
 work was focused on structural response quantities relevant to test interpretation, including strain-
 gauge response, lid-gap behavior, and plastic strain measures. Potential leakage behavior was
 therefore assessed indirectly through selected quantities of interest discussed in Section 2.6.2.

 • Strain gauges were represented using simplified single-element trusses to provide numerical
 strain response at instrumented locations. This representation did not include the detailed
 construction of physical gauges, adhesive layers, or installation effects.

 Implicit Analysis

An implicit analysis was performed to establish the pre-impact state of the SAVY-4000 container
assembly prior to the drop simulation. The purpose of this analysis was to preload the silicone sealing
ring through a shrink fit procedure so that the sealing components began the drop analysis in a
mechanically realistic assembled condition. This analysis also established the corresponding stress and
deformation state of the surrounding lid and collar components before the impact. The implicit shrink-fit
was performed in a common base orientation, allowing the same preloaded state to be used for the explicit
drop analysis, where three different impacts were applied.

After the completion of the pre-load analysis, the resulting deformed configuration and stress state were
then imported into the explicit analysis and used as the initial state for the drop simulation. The following
sections describe the interactions, boundary conditions, and initial conditions used in the implicit shrink
fit analysis. Figure 6 shows the 2-quart O-ring configuration before and after the implicit shrink fit
preload, illustrating the initial undeformed O-ring geometry (left), and the preloaded deformed shape
(right) used as the initial state for the explicit drop analysis.

 shrink-fit.

 Interactions

The implicit shrink-fit used surface-based contact pairs to model the interfaces relevant for modeling the
assembly process. When fully assembled, the O-ring was positioned between the collar and lid. Contact
was defined between the inner surface of the silicone ring and the lid surface, between the outer surface of
the silicone ring and the collar surface, and between the lid and locking collar, shown in Figure 7.

 Figure 7. Shrink-fit contact pairs: Left: O-ring ring inner surface and lid surface, Middle: O-ring outer
 surface and collar surface, Right: lid surface and locking collar surface.

These interactions were included to capture the contact behavior associated with installation of the sealing
ring and the assembled lid components. The contact formulation used hard contact in the normal direction
and Coulomb friction in the tangential direction, with a friction coefficient of 0.3. Contact pairs were
selected for the implicit analysis to provide direct control of the interfaces involved in the shrink-fit
analysis and to avoid unintended overclosures at surfaces during the shrink-fit procedure and to avoid
unintended surface interactions or overclosures during preload.

Tie constraints were used to represent connections that were intended to remain fixed during the implicit
assembly step. These ties interfaces were used to model welded regions and mechanically fastened
connections in a simplified manner, while preserving the assembled load path between connected
components. The specific tied surfaces varied by container configuration. In general, ties were used for
welded regions, such as the container-to-collar weld and, for the 10-gallon configuration, the container-to-
container-bottom weld, as well as mechanically fixed connections such as screw-to-lid interfaces.

Additional tied connections were included where required by the assembly design, such as handle-pin
connections.

 Boundary Conditions

No external boundary conditions were applied in the implicit shrink-fit analysis.

 Initial Conditions

No initial conditions were applied in the implicit shrink-fit analysis.

 Explicit Analysis

The deformed configuration and stress state from the implicit analysis were imported into the explicit
model so that the drop simulation began from a mechanically realistic preloaded condition. For each
container size, separate explicit analyses were conducted for the drop orientations considered in this

The explicit analysis included an analytical rigid floor, gravitational loading, and a prescribed initial
velocity. The following sections describe the interactions, boundary conditions and loads, and initial
conditions used in the explicit drop analysis.

 Interactions

The explicit drop analysis used general contact to model interactions among any exterior surfaces of any
parts in the container assembly during impact. Unlike the implicit-shrink fit analysis, the explicit model
did not retain the surface-based contact pair definitions as separate active interaction definitions. Instead,
the preloaded configuration and stress state established in the implicit analysis were imported as the initial
condition for the drop simulation, and subsequent contact during impact was governed by general contact.
The contact formulation used hard contact in the normal direction and Coulomb friction in the tangential
direction, with a friction coefficient of 0.3. General contact was selected for the explicit analysis to
provide robust treatment of impact-driven contact and possible secondary interactions among components
during the transient drop event.

Tie constraints were retained in the explicit model at interfaces intended to remain fixed, such as welded
regions and mechanically fastened connections. These ties interfaces allowed the assembly load path
established during the implicit step to be maintained while general contact handled any additional part-to-
part interactions that developed during impact.

 Boundary Conditions and Loads

In the explicit drop analysis, the analytical rigid floor was constrained through its reference node using a
fully fixed boundary condition. All translational and rotational degrees of freedom of the floor reference
node were restrained so that the impact surface remained stationary during the drop event. No additional
external boundary conditions were applied to the container assembly. Gravitational loading was applied to
the deformable components of the assembly as a body force using an acceleration magnitude of 9810

 Initial Conditions

The explicit analysis was initialized using the imported deformed configuration and stress state from the
implicit shrink-fit analysis. This allowed the drop simulation to begin from a preloaded assembled
condition. In addition, an initial velocity was prescribed to the model to represent the impact velocity
associated with a 12-foot drop. The magnitude of this velocity was calculated from the conservation of
energy as:

 𝑣 = √2𝑔ℎ

where 𝑔 is the gravitational acceleration and ℎ is the drop height. Utilizing a unit system of N-mm-s-
tonne, this corresponded to an impact velocity of 8467.77 𝑚𝑚/𝑠. The prescribed initial velocity was
applied in the negative y-direction (see Section 2.1 Figure 4 for coordinate system) for all simulation drop
orientations.

 Quantities of Interest and Post-Processing

Post-processing was performed on the explicit analysis results. The quantities of interest considered in
this work included strain-gauge response, collar-lid gap behavior, and measures of accumulated plastic
deformation. These quantities of interest were selected in coordination with PFE Auxiliary Systems to

Los Alamos National Laboratory Page 2-11

provide information on local structural response, deformation of the lid-seal region, and the extent of
permanent material deformation following impact. The post-processing procedures used for each quantity
are described in the following sections.

 Strain Gauge Response

Strain-gauge response was extracted from the explicit analysis using history output from the strain-gauge
elements included in the model. The reported quantity was logarithmic strain, LE11, obtained from the
strain-gauge element sets placed at four locations: canister side, canister opposite, collar side and collar
opposite. In this naming convention, the side location refers to a position 90° around the canister or collar
from the point of contact, while the opposite location refers to a position 180° from the contact point (see
Section 2.1 Figure 4). At each location, two gauges were considered: one aligned in the hoop direction,
and one aligned in the axial direction.

For post-processing, the LE11 histories were read directly from the explicit output database and plotted as
a function of time. To facilitate the planned comparison with testing, the strain values were converted to
microstrain. These results were intended to serve as the primary response metric for later comparison with
experimental strain-gauge measurements from physical drop tests.

 Lid-Gap Metrics

Lid gap metrics were developed to quantify opening of the collar-lid sealing interface during impact. As
shown in Figure 9, node sets were defined on the collar and lid near the sealing interface, together with
reference nodes used to establish a moving local coordinate system on the lid.

A moving local cylindrical coordinate system was defined at each time increment using two lid axis nodes
to establish the local axis direction and a reference lid node to define the 𝜃 = 0° circumferential location.
This allowed the gap calculation to remain tied to the deformed container configuration rather than the
global coordinate system.

At a given circumferential location, the radial gap magnitude was computed as the absolute difference
between the collar and lid radial position, such that:

                                           𝑔𝑎𝑝 = |𝑟𝑐𝑜𝑙𝑙𝑎𝑟 − 𝑟𝑙𝑖𝑑 |

where 𝑟𝑐𝑜𝑙𝑙𝑎𝑟 and 𝑟𝑙𝑖𝑑 are the radial distances of the corresponding collar and lid nodes from the moving
local axis. This definition was applied at a set of discrete circumferential locations and across the full
circumference of the lid-collar gap interface. In the present work, this quantity was used as a geometric
separation metric to track relative motion of the sealing surfaces. Physical opening relative to the sealing
function was therefore interpreted with respect to the nominal O-ring cross-sectional diameter.

Gap-versus-time results were reported for eight discrete circumferential locations spaced around the
interface. In addition, a full-circumference search was performed to identify the circumferential location
associated with the maximum radial gap during the drop event. The gap history at this critical angle was
also reported. These time-history results were used to identify when the largest local opening occurred
and whether the response exceeded the nominal O-ring cross-section dimension.

To evaluate how the lid-gap response varied around the circumference, the collar and lid nodal
coordinates along the full gap interface were converted into the moving local cylindrical coordinate
system and expressed as functions of the circumferential position. The collar and lid radial locations were
then interpolated onto a common 𝜃 grid, and the radial gap magnitude was computed around the full
circumference using the same absolute radial difference definition described above.

Gap-versus-𝜃 results were plotted at three different times: the start of the explicit analysis, the time
corresponding to the global maximum full-ring gap, and the final analysis time. These plots were used to
identify the circumferential distribution of opening in the lid-collar region and to determine the most
critical opening that occurred during the drop event.

Accumulated plastic deformation was evaluated using the equivalent plastic strain, PEEQ, extracted from
the final frame of the explicit analysis. The evaluation was limited to the primary structural parts of
interest, including the canister, collar, locking collar, and lid. For each of these parts, element volume,
EVOL, was also extracted so that the plastic strain response could be summarized on a volume-weighted
basis.

To support comparison across parts and materials, PEEQ was normalized by a part-specific reference
failure strain,

where 𝑃𝐸𝐸𝑄𝑟𝑒𝑓 was taken as 0.45 for the stainless-steel parts and 0.14 for the aluminum locking collar.

The plastic strain results presented here should be interpreted as screening measures of localized
permanent deformation rather than direct predictions of failure. In the present material models, the
constitutive response includes continued work hardening and does not include damage evolution or
softening with increasing strain. As a result, regions that accumulate large plastic strain may remain
artificially stiff relative to the physical material response, which can cause the predicted plastic zones to
remain smaller and more localized than they would be in a model with strain softening or failure
evolution. In addition, an adiabatic sensitivity study showed localized temperature rise in regions of
severe impact deformation, indicating that thermal effects may become more important in the highest
strain regions. These observations suggested that the current PEEQ results were appropriate for
identifying locations of elevated permanent deformation and relative differences between cases, while
more advanced material modeling may be required in future work to improve prediction of the size and
severity of highly deformed zones.

 Silicone O-ring Stiffness

For the silicone O-ring stiffness study, the 2-quart CG-over-lid case was used to evaluate the sensitivity of

modeled with a fixed Poisson’s ratio of 0.47, while the elastic modulus was varied over values of 5 MPa,

corresponding shear and bulk moduli were calculated and used to define the Neo-Hookean material
parameters. The critical angle gap versus time response for each case is compared in Figure 30 to assess

As shown in Figure 30, increasing the O-ring stiffness reduced the predicted peak gap magnitude. The

maximum predicted gap. The 5 MPa case resulted in the highest maximum gap and remained above the
nominal O-ring cross-section for the longest duration, making it the most conservative of the three
stiffness assumptions for modeling purposes. On that basis, the 5 MPa case was selected as the nominal

  Figure 34. 2-Qt CG-over-lid: critical gap versus time comparing geometric tolerancing in the lid-gap
 sealing region

As shown in Figure 34, the predicted gap response was only weakly affected by the tolerance variation
considered in this study. The LMC, nominal, and MMC cases produced nearly overlapping critical angle
gap histories, with only small differences in peak gap magnitude and in the later time response. These
results indicated that, within the tolerance range evaluated for the collar-lid sealing surfaces, the predicted
gap response was not highly sensitive to the geometric variation.

For the mesh study, the 2-quart CG over lid case was used to evaluate the sensitivity of the predicted
strain gauge response to the canister mesh size. The comparison was based on the canister opposite axial
strain gauge, and four container mesh sizes were considered including: 0.11, 0.055, 0.0275, and 0.01375

mm. The strain histories are compared in Figure 35 to assess the effect of mesh refinement on the
predicted local response. Because strain-gauge test data were not yet available for direct comparison at

Figure 35 shows that the peak absolute strain decreased as the mesh was refined from 0.11 to 0.01375

and response resolution.

 Adiabatic Study

To examine the possible influence of deformation-induced heating during impact, an adiabatic study was
performed for the 2-quart CG-over-bottom case. In this study, the explicit drop analysis was run with
adiabatic heating enabled and an initial temperature of 293 K, representing room temperature. The
purpose of this case was to assess whether localized plastic work during impact and subsequent internal-
mass interaction could produce meaningful temperature rise in the canister.

Figure 36. 2-Qt CG-over-bottom: adiabatic sensitivity case: predicted localized canister temperature rise
 associated with impact and secondary internal loading

As shown in Figure 36, the predicted temperature rise was localized near the canister impact region and
the region affected by internal mass loading. This indicated that plastic work could produce localized
heating in the most severely deformed regions. Because the current material model was not fully
calibrated for temperature-dependent response, these results were interpreted qualitatively and primarily
served to illustrate the limitation of the present modeling approach.

 Conclusions
A finite-element modeling capability was developed to simulate 12-foot drop events for SAVY-4000
containers across multiple container sizes and drop orientations. The modeling approach used a sequential
implicit-explicit workflow so that the explicit drop event began from a preloaded assembled condition
with the silicone O-ring in compression. The resulting simulations produced strain-gauge response, collar-
lid gap metrics, and plastic strain measures that supported interpretation of drop-test behavior and
provided a basis for later comparison with experimental data.

Nominal results showed that predicted response depended strongly on both drop orientation and container
size. For the 2-quart container, side impact produced the largest strain-gauge response, while the most
significant larger-container strain responses occurred in the 5-quart CG-over-bottom, 12-quart CG-over-
bottom, and 10-gallon side-impact cases. Lid-gap results showed that threshold exceedance of the
nominal O-ring cross-section occurred in select cases, with the 10-gallon CG-over-lid case distinguished
by extended exceedance duration and the 5-quart and 12-quart CG-over-bottom cases showing more
localized opening opposite impact. This open-gap condition is significant because separation beyond the
nominal sealing dimension may reduce sealing effectiveness and increase the potential for material inside
the container to escape. Plastic strain results indicated that the highest normalized PEEQ values were
generally confined to small, localized regions, with the severe part depending on orientation. These
plastic strain measures were interpreted as screening indicators of permanent local deformation rather

Los Alamos National Laboratory Page 4-35

than direct predictions of failure. Excessive permanent deformation is important because it may lead to
local denting, loss of fitting between mating components, altered load paths or degradation of sealing
performance, even in cases where gross structural failure does not occur.

Parametric studies showed that the predicted gap response was most sensitive to the assumed O-ring
stiffness and contact friction, while the tolerance and canister-thickness studies produced comparatively
smaller changes in the selected response measures for the cases examined. The mesh study showed
numerical sensitivity in the strain-gauge response and supported selection of a practical nominal mesh.
An adiabatic sensitivity case also showed localized temperature rise in severe impact regions, indicating
that thermal effects may become more important in highly deformed areas and highlighting a limitation of
the current material modeling. Overall, this work established a repeatable analysis workflow and a
baseline set of model predictions for later comparison with experimental data for SAVY-4000 drop-
testing experiments.

The WAVES-based workflow and accompanying repository also provided an important project outcome
beyond the individual simulation results. Organizing the analysis in this form improved efficiency in
building, running, and post-processing repeated cases, and made it more practical to execute nominal and
parametric variations in a consistent manner. This structure also supported repeatability and future
refinement, since model updates, additional studies, and documentation could be incorporated into the
same workflow framework rather than rebuilt from scratch.

 Future Work
Future work should focus on validation of the model predictions against physical drop-test data,
especially measured strain-gauge response and observed post-test deformation. This comparison is needed
to assess the current modeling assumptions and to identify where updates to the workflow are required.

Additional work is also needed to further investigate the 316L stainless steel material definition used in
the explicit analysis. In particular, the current material card should be reviewed to determine whether
additional damage parameters could be incorporated to support outputs related to damage initiation and
progression. A more complete damage formulation could provide a more useful basis for evaluating the
onset of local damage.

Future work should also further investigate the internal mass representation, including alternative mass
configurations and sensitivity to internal mass stiffness or deformability. Since the internal mass appeared
to influence local response in several cases, improved treatment of this feature may be important for
future model refinement.

 WAVES Workflow

The SAVY-4000 modeling workflow is organized in WAVES/SCons so that nominal simulations and
parametric studies can be executed in isolated build directories. The repository for the workflow is: re-
git.lanl.gov/aea/models-and-simulations/savy-4000

Los Alamos National Laboratory Page 8-48

At a high level, the workflow performs local input preparation, local part generation or reuse, one implicit
shrink-fit analysis, one or more explicit drop analyses, and post-processing of selected quantities of
interest. The nominal workflow is dispatched by savy_nominal.scons, while parametric studies are
launched through separate top-level workflow drivers.

 Nominal

The nominal workflow is the baseline analysis workflow for SAVY-4000 containers, it dispatched each
supported container into its own build directory under build/savy_nominal/<container>/. For each
container, the workflow prepares the Abaqus input files, builds the required Cubit .prt files, runs one
implicit shrink-fit analysis, runs the three explicit drop orientations, and post-process strain-gauge and
gap results.

 - scons build/savy_nominal

Nominal results are written in:

 - build/savy_nominal/<container>/

 Nominal per container

The container registry in modsim_package/savy_containers.py currently supports four nominal
containers: 2qt, 5qt, 12qt and 10gal. Each nominal container has its own wrapper and execution
workflow, but al follow the same general pattern

 - scons build/savy_nominal/2qt

 - scons build/savy_nominal/5qt

 - scons build/savy_nominal/12qt

 - scons build/savy_nominal/10gal

Nominal results are written in:

 - build/savy_nominal/<container>/

 Silicone O-ring stiffness study

The silicone O-ring stiffness study is driven by savy_silicone_stiffness.scons. It generates isolated
parameter-set build directories under build/savy_silicone_stiffness/<set_name>/, reuses the nominal 2-QT
parts locally, creates study-specific material decks, and runs the 2-quart CG-over-lid case for the lower,
nominal, and upper silicone stiffness cases.

 - scons build/savy_silicone_stiffness
