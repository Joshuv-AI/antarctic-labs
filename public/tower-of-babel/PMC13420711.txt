Carotid-axillary bypass with vertebral artery transposition for superior sulcus tumor resection
Authors: Gonçalo Araújo, Ricardo Correia, Maria Emília Ferreira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Superior sulcus tumors with subclavian artery involvement pose significant challenges to achieving complete resection while preserving cerebral and upper limb perfusion. We report the case of a 47-year-old woman with a left Pancoast tumor undergoing en bloc resection following induction therapy. A carotid-axillary bypass using a ringed polytetrafluoroethylene graft combined with transposition of a dominant vertebral artery to the common carotid artery was performed. The postoperative course was uneventful, with no neurological deficits and preserved limb perfusion. This case highlights that tailored supra-aortic reconstruction can enable safe oncologic resection while maintaining adequate cerebral and upper limb circulation in selected patients.

Written informed consent for publication of clinical details and images was obtained from the patient.

Case report

A 47-year-old female former smoker with a 30 pack-year history was diagnosed with a left superior sulcus tumor (Pancoast tumor) corresponding to a left upper lobe adenocarcinoma staged as cT3N0M0. Clinical presentation included ipsilateral Horner syndrome, left upper limb weakness with intrinsic hand muscle wasting and decreased temperature of the left upper extremity, with palpable but asymmetric pulses in the left upper limb.

She completed induction chemoradiotherapy. A multidisciplinary decision was made to proceed with open left upper lobectomy with en bloc resection of the tumor and involved subclavian artery, requiring supra-aortic revascularization with carotid-axillary bypass. Concomitant transposition of the dominant left vertebral artery was planned to preserve posterior cerebral circulation.

Surgical technique

The patient underwent left carotid-axillary bypass with concomitant transposition of the dominant left vertebral artery. An infraclavicular incision was performed to expose and control the left axillary artery. A separate longitudinal cervical incision at lower left neck was made between the heads of the sternocleidomastoid muscle, allowing access through the natural intermuscular plane. A retrojugular approach was then used to expose the common carotid artery and proximal vertebral artery.

The vertebral artery was exposed according to established anatomical landmarks through meticulous dissection of the vertebral triangle. The prescalene fat pad was mobilized and retracted laterally to optimize exposure. The phrenic nerve was identified along the anterior surface of the anterior scalene muscle and preserved throughout the procedure, and the vagus nerve was visualized within the carotid sheath. Following systemic heparinization with 5000 IU of unfractionated heparin, the vertebral artery was transposed with an end-to-side anastomosis to the posterolateral wall of the proximal common carotid artery, and the proximal vertebral artery stump was ligated (Fig 2, B). A 6-mm ringed polytetrafluoroethylene graft was used to the carotid-axillary bypass. The graft end-to-side anastomosis was performed on the common carotid artery cranial to the level of vertebral transposition (Fig 2, C). The graft was tunneled in a retrojugular and retroclavicular plane and anastomosed end-to-side to the left axillary artery. The proximal native axillary artery proximal to the anastomosis was doubly ligated (Fig 2, C).

Pulsatility was confirmed in all reconstructed vessels (Fig 2, D) and standard wound closure was performed. Thoracic surgery subsequently proceeded with en bloc resection of the tumor and subclavian artery (via posterolateral left thoracotomy) without intraoperative complications. The patient was extubated at the end of the procedure, awakened without neurological deficits, and had palpable symmetric radial pulse. She remained in the intensive care unit for 24 hours and was discharged on postoperative day seven. Predischarge computed tomography angiography showed good patency of both left carotid-axillary bypass and transposed left vertebral artery (Fig 3). The patient was maintained on single antiplatelet therapy with aspirin 100 mg daily following surgery. Discussion

Pancoast tumors with vascular, lower brachial plexus, and sympathetic chain involvement may be curable when managed with a multimodality strategy and when resectability criteria are met, with complete en bloc resection and negative margins as the primary surgical goal. Subclavian vessel involvement is no longer absolute contraindications when appropriate vascular reconstruction is feasible.1,2 Accordingly, an arterial reconstruction strategy was adopted in the patient presented.

Open surgical reconstruction of the vertebral artery has become increasingly uncommon in contemporary vascular practice. Vertebral artery preservation or reconstruction may nonetheless be indicated in complex thoracic inlet surgery when the vessel represents a significant contributor to posterior cerebral circulation.1,3

Although vertebral sacrifice might have been well tolerated in this case, vertebral reconstruction was favored given the patient's young age, excellent overall clinical condition, and anticipated long-term survival after curative resection.

Several technical nuances were critical to ensure procedural safety and long-term durability. Particular attention was directed toward vertebral artery exposure and transposition, as the vessel is typically accessible only along its proximal V1 segment. Precise identification of the vertebral triangle at the base of the neck is therefore essential. Limiting dissection reduces the risk of iatrogenic injury, particularly to the thoracic duct and phrenic nerve. Adequate mobilization of the vertebral artery allows a tension-free anastomosis to the common carotid artery, which is crucial given the thin and fragile arterial wall. Performing vertebral transposition early in the procedure improves exposure and facilitates a more controlled and precise anastomosis, often representing the most technically demanding step.2,4

Following vertebral artery reconstruction, carotid-axillary bypass was performed using a retrojugular route with a retroclavicular tunnel to optimize graft protection and alignment. A ring-reinforced prosthetic conduit was used to minimize compression or kinking related to clavicular and upper limb motion, thereby promoting stable conduit geometry and long-term patency.5

Finally, proximal control was achieved by surgical ligation of the left subclavian artery ostium via left lateral thoracotomy.

Although induction chemoradiotherapy is frequently associated with tissue fibrosis, distortion of normal anatomical planes, and increased vessel fragility, these findings were less pronounced than anticipated in the present case. This may be partly explained by the fact that the surgical exposure was performed in adjacent anatomical regions rather than within the field directly affected by radiotherapy, allowing reconstruction to be undertaken on relatively unaffected arterial segments. Careful dissection following established anatomical landmarks enabled safe identification and isolation of the supra-aortic vessels without significant technical difficulties. Particular attention was paid to preserving adjacent neural structures, while ensuring adequate mobilization of the vertebral artery to achieve a tension-free transposition. More broadly, when vascular reconstruction is required following induction therapy, meticulous tissue handling, selection of healthy arterial segments, and tension-free anastomosis remain essential principles to optimize technical success and long-term patency.

Beyond the immediate technical success of the reconstruction, long-term surveillance remains an important consideration. Our institutional follow-up strategy consists of regular clinical assessment and duplex ultrasound surveillance of both the carotid-axillary bypass and vertebral artery reconstruction. In the present case, duplex ultrasound assessment at 3 months confirmed durable patency of both reconstructions, supporting the early technical success of the procedure.

Conclusions

This case highlights the importance of a multidisciplinary approach in enabling safe en bloc resection of locally advanced Pancoast tumors with subclavian artery involvement. Carotid-axillary bypass with vertebral artery transposition ensured adequate cerebral and upper limb perfusion and favorable early outcomes. Although rarely performed, vertebral reconstruction may be critical in selected patients at risk of posterior circulation compromise. Careful attention to technical details is essential to ensure procedural safety and long-term durability.
