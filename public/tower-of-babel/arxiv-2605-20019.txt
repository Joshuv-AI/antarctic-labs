Induced transitions in non-Hermitian spin-boson models with time-dependent boundaries
Andreas Fring, Marta Reboiro

Abstract
We study a time-dependent non-Hermitian extension of the Schütte-Da Providência spin-boson Hamiltonian with complex couplings. A time-dependent Dyson map containing a squeezing transformation maps the model, in an admissible bounded regime, to a Hermitian Hamiltonian with real instantaneous energy spectrum. The squeezing contribution generates a dilatation term allowing the Hermitian partner to be interpreted as a fixed-domain representation of a system with moving boundaries. While the fixed-boundary Hermitian model conserves Q = N − S 0 Q=N-S_{0} and forbids transitions between sectors differing by two bosonic quanta, the boundary motion opens such channels. For closed boundary protocols with constant background parameters the first-order integrated transition amplitude vanishes, reflecting the unitary nature of constant squeezing. Nontrivial transition control arises when the non-Hermitian parameter varies during the boundary motion, changing the dressed basis and allowing boundary-induced transitions to be suppressed or enhanced by coherent interference.



1
Introduction

Non-Hermitian Hamiltonians have become an important tool for effective
descriptions of open quantum and wave systems, including systems with gain and
loss, dissipative couplings, PT symmetry, exceptional points, and
non-reciprocal transport
el2018non
;
ashida2020non
;
bergholtz2021ex
;
zhang2022rev
. In general such Hamiltonians need not possess real
spectra or unitary time evolution. However, if a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian is
related to a Hermitian Hamiltonian by a suitable Dyson map, the system may be
interpreted as a consistent quasi-Hermitian/PT-symmetric quantum theory
Bender:1998ke
;
Benderrev
;
AliI
;
Alirev
. In this setting the
inner product is modified by a metric operator and the physical observables are
not necessarily the operators that appear most naturally in the auxiliary
Hilbert space
Urubu
.

For time-independent systems the central requirement is usually the
quasi-Hermiticity relation between a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian, a Hermitian
counterpart, and a positive metric. For time-dependent systems the situation is
more subtle. The Dyson map itself contributes to the generator of time evolution
through an additional gauge-like term. As a consequence, the non-Hermitian
Hamiltonian that generates the Schrödinger equation is not, in general, the
physical energy observable
fringmoussa
;
fring2023introPTt
. Instead, the observable energy is obtained by
transforming the Hermitian Hamiltonian back with the inverse Dyson map. This
distinction is essential in time-dependent non-Hermitian quantum mechanics and
will play a central role below.

Here we study such a setup in a time-dependent extension of the
Schütte-Da Providência spin-boson model
schutte1977solvable
. The original model couples a bosonic mode to an
SU(2)
spin degree of freedom through the interaction terms

S_{+}b^{\dagger}
and
S_{-}b
. Related boson-mapping
techniques have also been employed in finite-temperature many-body contexts,
for instance within thermo-field dynamics
civitarese1999boson
.
Time-independent pseudo-Hermitian versions of this model have been considered previously, for example in

reboiro2022qu
, where an asymmetry parameter was introduced in
the spin-boson interaction and the thermodynamics of the resulting
pseudo-Hermitian model was analysed. The present work differs in that both the
Hamiltonian and the Dyson map are explicitly time-dependent, with the latter containing three
parts: a bosonic squeezing transformation, a bosonic number-operator scaling,
and a spin-sector scaling. The additional squeezing
part of the Dyson map is used to establish an effective moving-boundary
interpretation and to study boundary-induced transitions.

A key point is that the squeezing part of the Dyson map has a geometric
interpretation as previously observed in
fermiFT
. In the standard coordinate representation the time derivative
of the squeezing parameter produces a term proportional to the generator of
dilatations, which is precisely the structure that appears when a Hermitian quantum system on
a time-dependent interval is transformed to a fixed reference interval. Thus
the Hermitian partner of the non-Hermitian fixed-domain Hamiltonian may also be
viewed as the fixed-domain representation of a Hermitian system with moving
boundaries.

Within the regime that guarantees the boundedness of the Dyson map the physical energy spectrum is real. Thus, here the main effect
of the non-Hermitian deformation is not the appearance of exceptional points and complex energy levels, but instead, the interesting effects are dynamical. In the fixed-boundary
Hermitian model the operator
Q=N-S_{0}

is conserved, and the spin-boson Hilbert space decomposes into independent
two-dimensional sectors. The boundary motion breaks this conservation law: the
dilatation term contains
b^{\dagger 2}
and
b^{2}
, and therefore couples states
whose boson numbers differ by two. Hence moving boundaries induce transitions
between sectors that are decoupled in the fixed-boundary model.

We analysed these induced transitions perturbatively for small squeezing
amplitude and boundary velocity. The first-order correction to the
instantaneous energy levels vanishes, so the leading spectral shifts occur at
second order. However, the same matrix elements that determine these second
order energy corrections also determine first-order transition amplitudes
between dressed spin-boson sectors. This provides a direct way of distinguishing
the fixed-boundary model from the moving-boundary system.

A further effect appears when the non-Hermitian parameter is varied during the
boundary motion. For closed boundary protocols with time-independent background
parameters, the first-order integrated transition amplitude reduces to an
endpoint term and vanishes in the dressed basis. This cancellation reflects the
fact that constant squeezing is only a unitary change of basis. If, however,
the non-Hermitian deformation changes the dressed basis during the motion, the
integrated amplitude need not vanish. The transition probability can then be
suppressed or enhanced by coherent interference. Thus the non-Hermitian
deformation acts as a control parameter for boundary-induced transitions, even
though the instantaneous physical energy spectrum remains real.

Boundary-induced transitions are familiar in Hermitian systems with
time-dependent domains. For example, a particle in an infinite well with a
moving wall develops transitions between instantaneous modes, and in field
theory moving cavity boundaries lead to parametric excitation of the field,
known as the dynamical Casimir effect

doescher1969infinite
;
law1994effective
;
dodonov2010current
. Related non-Hermitian transition phenomena have been studied previously
longhi2017non
, where it was found that non-Hermitian perturbations can induce asymmetric, unidirectional, or even transitionless dynamics, and in exceptional-point encircling, where nonadiabatic transitions lead to chiral state conversion. The present work is
different in that the moving-boundary term is generated by a time-dependent
Dyson map of a non-Hermitian spin-boson Hamiltonian, and the transition
amplitudes can be controlled by the time dependence of the non-Hermitian
metric.

Our manuscript is organised as follows: In section 2 we construct the time-dependent Dyson
map and derive the Hermiticity conditions for the transformed Hamiltonian. We
then explain the moving-boundary interpretation of the squeezing-induced
dilatation term. In section 3 we discuss the physical energy operator and its
instantaneous spectrum, including the non-squeezing and constant-squeezing
limits. We then treat the genuinely time-dependent squeezing regime
perturbatively and compute the relevant second-order energy corrections. In section 4
we use the same matrix elements to analyse boundary-induced transition
amplitudes and show how time-dependent non-Hermiticity can control their
suppression or enhancement. We state our conclusions in section 5.

2
Time-dependent quasi-Hermiticity and effective moving boundaries

We generalise the Schütte-Da Providência Hamiltonian
schutte1977solvable
in two ways, by allowing its coupling constants to be complex, so that it becomes in general non-Hermitian, and by introducing an explicit time-dependence

H(t)=\omega_{f}(t)S_{0}+\omega_{b}(t)N+\alpha(t)S_{+}b^{\dagger}+\beta(t)S_{-}b,\qquad\omega_{f}(t),\omega_{b}(t),\alpha(t),\beta(t)\in\mathbb{C}.

(1)

As in the original model,
N
is the bosonic number operator,
N:=b^{\dagger}b
, with
[b,b^{\dagger}]=1
and
S_{0},S_{\pm}
are the generators of the
SU(2)
algebra satisfying
[S_{0},S_{\pm}]=\pm S_{\pm}
,
[S_{+},S_{-}]=2S_{0}
,
S_{0}^{\dagger}=S_{0}
and
S_{+}^{\dagger}=S_{-}
.

Following the usual procedure for time-dependent non-Hermitian Hamiltonian systems
fringmoussa
, we attempt to solve the time-dependent Dyson equation

h(t)=\eta(t)H(t)\eta^{-1}(t)+i\dot{\eta}(t)\eta^{-1}(t),

(2)

for the Dyson map
\eta(t)
with
h(t)
required to be Hermitian. Taking the Dyson map to be

\eta(t):=e^{\kappa(t)(b^{\dagger 2}-b^{2})/2}e^{\gamma(t)N}e^{\delta(t)S_{0}},\qquad\kappa(t),\gamma(t),\delta(t)\in\mathbb{C},

(3)

equation (
2
) then yields

\displaystyle h(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\omega_{f}+i\dot{\delta}\right)S_{0}+\left(\omega_{b}+i\dot{\gamma}\right)\left[\cosh(2\kappa)N-\frac{1}{2}\sinh(2\kappa)(b^{\dagger 2}+b^{2})+\sinh^{2}(\kappa)\right]

(4)

\displaystyle\!\!\!+\alpha e^{\gamma+\delta}S_{+}\left(b^{\dagger}\cosh\kappa-b\sinh\kappa\right)+\beta e^{-(\gamma+\delta)}S_{-}\left(b\cosh\kappa-b^{\dagger}\sinh\kappa\right)+i\frac{\dot{\kappa}}{2}(b^{\dagger 2}-b^{2}).

The Hamiltonian
h(t)
is Hermitian provided the conditions

A_{f}(t):=\omega_{f}+i\dot{\delta}\in\mathbb{R},\qquad A_{b}(t):=\omega_{b}+i\dot{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R},\qquad\kappa\in\mathbb{R},\qquad\beta=\alpha^{*}e^{2\Re(\gamma+\delta)},

(5)

are satisfied. Two simple nontrivial choices satisfying (
5
) are

\displaystyle I:\,\,\,

\displaystyle\omega_{f}\in i\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\omega_{b}\in\mathbb{R},\quad

\displaystyle\gamma\in i\mathbb{R},\,\,

\displaystyle\delta=i\int^{t}\omega_{f}ds\in\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\kappa\in\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\beta=\alpha^{*}e^{2\delta},

(6)

\displaystyle II:\,\,\,

\displaystyle\omega_{f}\in\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\omega_{b}\in i\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\delta\in i\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\gamma=i\int^{t}\omega_{b}ds\in\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\kappa\in\mathbb{R},\quad

\displaystyle\beta=\alpha^{*}e^{2\gamma}.

(7)

These choices ensure that the Dyson map remains genuinely non-unitary, rather than reducing to an ordinary unitary gauge transformation, and that the original Hamiltonian
H(t)
remains genuinely non-Hermitian.

Moreover, it is useful to note that there are regions in parameter space for which the Dyson map is bounded.
For real
\kappa(t)
the squeezing factor

\exp[\kappa(t)(b^{\dagger 2}-b^{2})/2]
is unitary, while

\exp[\delta(t)S_{0}]
is bounded with bounded inverse, since
S_{0}
acts on a
finite-dimensional spin space. Thus the only possible source of unboundedness is
the bosonic factor
\exp[\gamma(t)N]
. Since
N|n\rangle=n|n\rangle
, this factor
is bounded if and only if
\Re\gamma(t)\leq 0
, whereas its inverse is bounded if
and only if
\Re\gamma(t)\geq 0
. Hence
\eta(t)
and
\eta^{-1}(t)
are both
bounded only when
\Re\gamma(t)=0
. This condition is automatically satisfied in
solution I, where
\gamma(t)\in i\mathbb{R}
, so solution I defines a bounded
similarity transformation on the bosonic Fock space. By contrast, in solution II,
where
\gamma(t)\in\mathbb{R}
, either
\eta(t)
or
\eta^{-1}(t)
is unbounded
unless
\gamma(t)=0
. Solution II therefore requires a more careful
domain-dependent treatment and will not be considered in what follows.

Using the standard representation
b=(x+ip)/\sqrt{2}
,
b^{\dagger}=(x-ip)/\sqrt{2}
,
the last term in (
4
) may be written as

V_{\rm mb}(t):=i\frac{\dot{\kappa}}{2}(b^{\dagger 2}-b^{2})=\frac{\dot{\kappa}}{2}\left\{x,p\right\}=-\frac{\dot{\ell}}{2\ell}\left\{x,p\right\},\qquad\text{with}\qquad\ell(t)=\ell_{0}e^{-\kappa(t)}.

(8)

Thus the squeezing parameter
\kappa(t)
may be interpreted geometrically as
controlling a time-dependent rescaling of the spatial domain. The term

-\dot{\ell}\,\{x,p\}/(2\ell)
is precisely the dilatation term that arises when a
Hermitian system on a moving interval
y\in[-\ell(t),\ell(t)]
is represented on
a fixed interval, as in the correspondence relations found in
fermiFT
.
Consequently, the Hermitian Dyson partner
h(t)
may also be viewed as the
fixed-domain representative of an equivalent Hermitian system with moving
boundaries.

To make the correspondence with the moving-boundary picture explicit, we introduce a Hermitian Hamiltonian
h_{\ell}(t,y)
defined on the time-dependent interval
y\in[-\ell(t),\ell(t)]
, with Dirichlet boundary conditions
\Phi(t,\pm\ell(t))=0
. Passing to the fixed interval
x\in[-1,1]
by means of the unitary dilation operator

(U_{\ell}\Phi)(t,x):=\sqrt{\ell(t)}\,\Phi\bigl(t,\ell(t)x\bigr),

(9)

one obtains the fixed-domain Schrödinger equation

i\partial_{t}\varphi(t,x)=h_{\mathrm{fix}}(t,x)\varphi(t,x),\qquad h_{\mathrm{fix}}(t,x)=U_{\ell}h_{\ell}(t,y)U_{\ell}^{-1}+i\,U_{\ell}\partial_{t}U_{\ell}^{-1}.

(10)

The second term yields the dilatation contribution

i\,U_{\ell}\partial_{t}U_{\ell}^{-1}=-\frac{\dot{\ell}(t)}{2\ell(t)}\{x,p\},

(11)

which is exactly the same structure as the squeezing-induced term in (
4
), once the identification
\ell(t)=\ell_{0}e^{-\kappa(t)}
is made. Hence the transformed Hamiltonian
h(t)
in (
4
) may be viewed as the fixed-domain representative of a Hermitian Hamiltonian
h_{\ell}(t,y)
on a domain with moving boundaries, while at the same time it remains the Hermitian Dyson partner of the original non-Hermitian Hamiltonian
H(t)
.

Concretely, the Hamiltonian on the moving interval
y\in[-\ell(t),\ell(t)]
is given by

h_{\ell}(t)=U_{\ell}^{-1}\!\left(h(t)-iU_{\ell}\partial_{t}U_{\ell}^{-1}\right)U_{\ell}.

(12)

Equivalently, it is obtained by removing the explicit dilatation term in the fixed-domain representation and expressing the remaining bosonic operators in the moving representation

b_{\ell}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\frac{y}{\ell(t)}+i\,\ell(t)p_{y}\right),\qquad b_{\ell}^{\dagger}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\frac{y}{\ell(t)}-i\,\ell(t)p_{y}\right).

(13)

3
The energy operator

As argued in
fringmoussa
, the physical energy observable associated with the time-dependent Dyson map is not the non-Hermitian Hamiltonian
H(t)
itself, but rather

\widetilde{H}(t):=\eta^{-1}(t)h(t)\eta(t)=H(t)+i\,\eta^{-1}(t)\dot{\eta}(t).

(14)

For the Dyson map in (
3
) we compute

\eta^{-1}(t)\dot{\eta}(t)=\frac{\dot{\kappa}(t)}{2}\Big(e^{-2\gamma(t)}b^{\dagger 2}-e^{2\gamma(t)}b^{2}\Big)+\dot{\gamma}(t)\,N+\dot{\delta}(t)\,S_{0},

(15)

and therefore

\widetilde{H}(t)=A_{f}(t)S_{0}+A_{b}(t)N+\alpha S_{+}b^{\dagger}+\beta S_{-}b+\frac{i\dot{\kappa}}{2}\Big(e^{-2\gamma}b^{\dagger 2}-e^{2\gamma}b^{2}\Big).

(16)

Thus the observable energy differs from the original non-Hermitian Hamiltonian by additional terms induced by the time dependence of the Dyson map.

The energy operator
\widetilde{H}(t)
is defined independently of any particular spatial realisation of the bosonic sector and therefore does not, in itself, require a finite interval. Upon introducing the finite-interval realisation and the dilation map
U_{\ell}
, the energy operator in the moving-boundary representation is obtained from the fixed-domain energy operator by

\widetilde{H}_{\ell}(t):=U_{\ell}^{-1}\widetilde{H}_{\mathrm{fix}}(t)U_{\ell}.

(17)

Identifying
\widetilde{H}_{\mathrm{fix}}(t)
with the fixed-domain operator
\widetilde{H}(t)
, we find

\widetilde{H}_{\ell}(t)=A_{f}(t)S_{0}+A_{b}(t)N_{\ell}+\alpha S_{+}b_{\ell}^{\dagger}+\beta S_{-}b_{\ell}+\frac{i\dot{\kappa}}{2}\Bigl(e^{-2\gamma}(b_{\ell}^{\dagger})^{2}-e^{2\gamma}b_{\ell}^{2}\Bigr),

(18)

where
b_{\ell}=U_{\ell}^{-1}b\,U_{\ell}
,
b_{\ell}^{\dagger}=U_{\ell}^{-1}b^{\dagger}U_{\ell}
, and
N_{\ell}=b_{\ell}^{\dagger}b_{\ell}
. Thus the energy observable on the moving interval is unitarily equivalent to its fixed-domain counterpart, with the boundary motion encoded in the time-dependent operators
b_{\ell}
and
b_{\ell}^{\dagger}
.

3.1
The physical energy operator and its instantaneous spectrum

It is meaningful to consider the instantaneous spectral problem for the energy operator

\widetilde{H}(t)\phi_{n}(t)=E_{n}(t)\phi_{n}(t),

(19)

where the eigenvalues
E_{n}(t)
may be interpreted as time-dependent instantaneous energy levels. We note, however, that in a genuinely time-dependent system these eigenvalues should not in general be regarded as conserved quantities, since the evolution need not remain within a single instantaneous eigenspace. Rather, the spectrum of
\widetilde{H}(t)
provides a snapshot of the observable energy at each instant of time. Since
\widetilde{H}(t)=\eta^{-1}(t)h(t)\eta(t)
, the operators
\widetilde{H}(t)
and
h(t)
are isospectral at each fixed time.

3.1.1
The non-squeezing regime and the constant squeezing regime

In the non-squeezing regime
\kappa(t)=0
, the Hermitian Hamiltonian reduces to

h(t)=A_{f}(t)S_{0}+A_{b}(t)N+g(t)S_{+}b^{\dagger}+g(t)^{*}S_{-}b.

(20)

Since the operator

Q:=N-S_{0}

(21)

commutes with
h(t)
, the Hilbert space decomposes into invariant sectors labelled by the eigenvalue of
Q
. For the spin-
1/2
representation, the eigenspace of
Q
with eigenvalue
q_{n}=n+1/2
,
n=0,1,2,\dots
, is the two-dimensional invariant subspace

\mathcal{H}_{n}=\operatorname{span}\{|\downarrow,n\rangle,|\uparrow,n+1\rangle\}.

In this sense each fixed-
Q
sector is two-dimensional, and the restriction of

h(t)
to
\mathcal{H}_{n}
is represented by the block matrix

h^{(n)}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}A_{b}(t)n-\frac{A_{f}(t)}{2}&g(t)\sqrt{n+1}\\
g(t)^{*}\sqrt{n+1}&A_{b}(t)(n+1)+\frac{A_{f}(t)}{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(22)

Its instantaneous eigenvalues are

E_{n}^{\pm}(t)=A_{b}(t)\Bigl(n+\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)\pm\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\bigl(A_{b}(t)+A_{f}(t)\bigr)^{2}+4|g(t)|^{2}(n+1)},\qquad n=0,1,2,\dots

(23)

together with the isolated level

E_{\rm vac}(t)=\frac{A_{f}(t)}{2},

(24)

corresponding to the uncoupled state
|\uparrow,0\rangle
. Hence in the non-squeezing regime the instantaneous spectrum can be obtained in closed form for the spin-
1/2
case. Upon imposing either of the conditions I or II together with the Hermiticity conditions (
5
), these instantaneous eigenvalues are real.

In the constant squeezing regime with
\dot{\kappa}=0
, we can analyse the unitarily equivalent operator

h_{0}(t)=S_{\kappa}^{-1}h(t)S_{\kappa}=A_{f}(t)S_{0}+A_{b}(t)N+g(t)S_{+}b^{\dagger}+g(t)^{*}S_{-}b,\quad S_{\kappa}=\exp\!\left[\frac{\kappa}{2}(b^{\dagger 2}-b^{2})\right],

(25)

which has exactly the same operator form as
h(t)
in the non-squeezing regime.

Hence in these two regimes the instantaneous spectrum can be obtained in closed form. We notice that within the admissible quasi-Hermitian regime, no exceptional points occur, since
h(t)
is Hermitian and
\widetilde{H}(t)
is similar to
h(t)
whenever the Dyson map is invertible. At most, one may encounter ordinary degeneracies of the instantaneous spectrum, but not defective spectral singularities.

3.1.2
Generic values for
\kappa(t)

For generic real
\kappa(t)
with
\dot{\kappa}(t)\neq 0
, the Hamiltonian (
4
) no longer commutes with the operator
Q
in (
21
). The additional terms generated by the squeezing contribution change the eigenvalue of
Q
by
\pm 2
, so that the finite-dimensional block decomposition of the non-squeezing regime is lost. Nevertheless, the corresponding
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
parity, generated for instance by
\Pi=e^{i\pi(N-S_{0}+j)}
, remains conserved, and the Hilbert space still splits into two invariant sectors. To analyse this regime, we expand the Hamiltonian (
4
) perturbatively for small real
\kappa(t)
around the solvable non-squeezing Hamiltonian
h_{0}(t)
. Retaining terms up to first order in
\kappa
and
\dot{\kappa}
, we obtain

h(t)=h_{0}(t)+V_{\kappa}(t)+\mathcal{O}(\kappa^{2}),

(26)

with
h_{0}(t)
as defined (
25
) and

V_{\kappa}(t)=-\kappa(t)A_{b}(t)(b^{\dagger 2}+b^{2})-\kappa(t)g(t)S_{+}b-\kappa(t)g(t)^{*}S_{-}b^{\dagger}+i\frac{\dot{\kappa}(t)}{2}(b^{\dagger 2}-b^{2}).

(27)

The perturbation
V_{\kappa}(t)
breaks the
Q
-symmetry by coupling sectors whose eigenvalues differ by
\pm 2
. Hence the finite-dimensional block decomposition of the non-squeezing regime is lost. Denoting the normalised instantaneous eigenstates of
h_{0}(t)
by
|\psi_{n}^{\pm}\rangle
, the first-order correction is

\Delta E_{n,\pm}^{(1)}(t)=\langle\psi_{n}^{\pm}|V_{\kappa}(t)|\psi_{n}^{\pm}\rangle.

(28)

With

\langle\psi_{n}^{\pm}|b^{\dagger 2}\pm b^{2}|\psi_{n}^{\pm}\rangle=0,\qquad\langle\psi_{n}^{\pm}|S_{+}b|\psi_{n}^{\pm}\rangle=0,\qquad\langle\psi_{n}^{\pm}|S_{-}b^{\dagger}|\psi_{n}^{\pm}\rangle=0,

(29)

this correction vanishes

\Delta E_{n,\pm}^{(1)}(t)=0.

(30)

Thus, the leading spectral shifts arise only at second order. From the standard expression for nondegenerate second-order perturbation theory, we obtain

\Delta E_{n,\pm}^{(2)}(t)=\sum_{(m,\tau)\neq(n,\pm)}\frac{\left|\langle\psi_{m}^{\tau}(t)|V_{\kappa}(t)|\psi_{n}^{\pm}(t)\rangle\right|^{2}}{E_{n}^{\pm}(t)-E_{m}^{\tau}(t)},

(31)

with
V_{\kappa}(t)
specified in (
27
). This expression is understood away from ordinary degeneracies of the
instantaneous spectrum. At such degeneracies one must diagonalise

V_{\kappa}(t)
within the degenerate subspace, as in standard degenerate
Hermitian perturbation theory.

To evaluate the perturbative corrections explicitly, it is convenient to parameterise the normalised instantaneous eigenstates of the
2\times 2
block
h^{(n)}(t)
in terms of a mixing angle
\theta_{n}(t)
and the phase of the complex coupling
g(t)=|g(t)|e^{i\phi(t)}
. They may be written as

|\psi_{n}^{+}\rangle=\cos\theta_{n}\,|\downarrow,n\rangle+e^{-i\phi}\sin\theta_{n}\,|\uparrow,n+1\rangle,\quad|\psi_{n}^{-}\rangle=-\sin\theta_{n}\,|\downarrow,n\rangle+e^{-i\phi}\cos\theta_{n}\,|\uparrow,n+1\rangle,

(32)

where the angle
\theta_{n}(t)
is determined by the diagonalisation of
h^{(n)}(t)
with

\tan(2\theta_{n})=-\frac{2|g(t)|\sqrt{n+1}}{A_{b}(t)+A_{f}(t)},

(33)

or equivalently

\cos(2\theta_{n})=-\frac{A_{b}(t)+A_{f}(t)}{\Omega_{n}(t)},\qquad\sin(2\theta_{n})=\frac{2|g(t)|\sqrt{n+1}}{\Omega_{n}(t)},

(34)

\Omega_{n}(t):=\sqrt{\bigl(A_{b}(t)+A_{f}(t)\bigr)^{2}+4|g(t)|^{2}(n+1)}.

(35)

These states are the instantaneous dressed states of the coupled spin-boson system.

Introducing further abbreviations

\alpha_{\pm}(t):=-\kappa(t)A_{b}(t)\pm i\frac{\dot{\kappa}(t)}{2},\qquad c_{n}:=\cos\theta_{n},\qquad s_{n}:=\sin\theta_{n},

(36)

and absorbing the phase of
g(t)
into the basis vectors by a unitarity transformation, the perturbation term may be written as

V_{\kappa}(t)=V_{+2}(t)+V_{-2}(t),

(37)

with

V_{+2}(t)=\alpha_{+}(t)b^{\dagger 2}-\kappa(t)|g(t)|\,S_{-}b^{\dagger},\qquad V_{-2}(t)=\alpha_{-}(t)b^{2}-\kappa(t)|g(t)|\,S_{+}b.

(38)

Hence the second-order correction to the instantaneous eigenvalue
E_{n}^{\sigma}(t)
is

\Delta E_{n,\sigma}^{(2)}(t)=\sum_{\tau=\pm}\frac{\big|M_{n+2,\tau;\,n,\sigma}(t)\big|^{2}}{E_{n}^{\sigma}(t)-E_{n+2}^{\tau}(t)}+\Theta(n-2)\sum_{\tau=\pm}\frac{\big|M_{n-2,\tau;\,n,\sigma}(t)\big|^{2}}{E_{n}^{\sigma}(t)-E_{n-2}^{\tau}(t)},

(39)

where for completeness the matrix elements are given explicitly by

\displaystyle M_{n+2,+;\,n,+}

\displaystyle=c_{n+2}\Big[\alpha_{+}c_{n}\sqrt{(n+1)(n+2)}-\kappa|g|s_{n}\sqrt{n+2}\Big]+s_{n+2}s_{n}\alpha_{+}\sqrt{(n+2)(n+3)},

\displaystyle M_{n+2,-;\,n,+}

\displaystyle=-s_{n+2}\Big[\alpha_{+}c_{n}\sqrt{(n+1)(n+2)}-\kappa|g|s_{n}\sqrt{n+2}\Big]+c_{n+2}s_{n}\alpha_{+}\sqrt{(n+2)(n+3)},

\displaystyle M_{n+2,+;\,n,-}

\displaystyle=-c_{n+2}\Big[\alpha_{+}s_{n}\sqrt{(n+1)(n+2)}+\kappa|g|c_{n}\sqrt{n+2}\Big]+s_{n+2}c_{n}\alpha_{+}\sqrt{(n+2)(n+3)},

\displaystyle M_{n+2,-;\,n,-}

\displaystyle=s_{n+2}\Big[\alpha_{+}s_{n}\sqrt{(n+1)(n+2)}+\kappa|g|c_{n}\sqrt{n+2}\Big]+c_{n+2}c_{n}\alpha_{+}\sqrt{(n+2)(n+3)},

and

\displaystyle M_{n-2,+;\,n,+}

\displaystyle=c_{n-2}c_{n}\alpha_{-}\sqrt{n(n-1)}+s_{n-2}\Big[\alpha_{-}s_{n}\sqrt{n(n+1)}-\kappa|g|c_{n}\sqrt{n}\Big],

\displaystyle M_{n-2,-;\,n,+}

\displaystyle=-s_{n-2}c_{n}\alpha_{-}\sqrt{n(n-1)}+c_{n-2}\Big[\alpha_{-}s_{n}\sqrt{n(n+1)}-\kappa|g|c_{n}\sqrt{n}\Big],

\displaystyle M_{n-2,+;\,n,-}

\displaystyle=-c_{n-2}s_{n}\alpha_{-}\sqrt{n(n-1)}+s_{n-2}\Big[\alpha_{-}c_{n}\sqrt{n(n+1)}+\kappa|g|s_{n}\sqrt{n}\Big],

\displaystyle M_{n-2,-;\,n,-}

\displaystyle=+s_{n-2}s_{n}\alpha_{-}\sqrt{n(n-1)}+c_{n-2}\Big[\alpha_{-}c_{n}\sqrt{n(n+1)}+\kappa|g|s_{n}\sqrt{n}\Big].

Thus the leading effect of a genuinely time-dependent squeezing on the instantaneous energy levels enters only at quadratic order in the perturbation.

We note that the spin-
1/2
representation of our model (
1
) is
closely related to, but not identical with, the Jaynes-Cummings model

jaynes2005com
. The interaction
S_{+}b^{\dagger}+S_{-}b

has the counter-rotating, or anti-Jaynes-Cummings, structure rather than the
standard Jaynes-Cummings form
S_{+}b+S_{-}b^{\dagger}
. Accordingly, the conserved
quantity in the non-squeezing regime is
Q=N-S_{0}
, rather than the usual
Jaynes-Cummings excitation number
N+S_{0}
. This distinction should also be
kept separate from exceptional-point physics. In time-dependent non-Hermitian
Jaynes-Cummings models with an imaginary coupling, exceptional points arise
because the block frequency contains a difference of squares
frith2020exotic
. In the present bounded quasi-Hermitian regime, by contrast, the block frequencies in (
23
) do not generate exceptional points.

For the closely related collective light-matter models, the Tavis-Cummings
model
tavis1968exact
, which is the many-atom or collective-spin
rotating-wave generalisation of the Jaynes-Cummings model, and the Dicke model

dicke1954coherence
, which also contains counter-rotating couplings,
several non-Hermitian extensions have been considered

ghosh2005exactly
;
baga2016exc
;
zhang2020exp
;
liu2022macro
. These studies
typically introduce non-Hermiticity through static imaginary couplings,
phenomenological decay rates, gain-loss terms, or effective time-independent
Hamiltonians, and often focus on real spectra, exceptional points, or
non-Hermitian phase transitions. By contrast, the present construction involves
an explicitly time-dependent quasi-Hermitian Dyson map whose squeezing part
admits an effective moving-boundary interpretation.

4
Boundary-induced transitions and their non-Hermitian control

The preceding perturbative matrix elements reveal an interesting dynamical
difference between the fixed-boundary Hermitian model, the Hermitian
moving-boundary model, and its non-Hermitian quasi-Hermitian deformation. In the
Hermitian fixed-boundary case, corresponding to
\delta=0
and
\kappa=0
, the
Hamiltonian

h_{0}(t)=A_{f}(t)S_{0}+A_{b}(t)N+\alpha(t)S_{+}b^{\dagger}+\alpha^{*}(t)S_{-}b

(40)

commutes with
Q=N-S_{0}
. Thus the sectors

\{|\downarrow,n\rangle,|\uparrow,n+1\rangle\}
are invariant, and transitions between sectors labelled by
n
and
n\pm 2
are forbidden.

This changes once the boundary is allowed to move. In the fixed-domain
representation the boundary motion produces the term
V_{\rm mb}(t)
in (
8
). Since

b^{\dagger 2}|n\rangle=\sqrt{(n+1)(n+2)}\,|n+2\rangle,\qquad b^{2}|n\rangle=\sqrt{n(n-1)}\,|n-2\rangle,

(41)

the moving boundary opens transitions of the form

|\sigma,n\rangle\longleftrightarrow|\sigma,n\pm 2\rangle,\qquad\sigma=\uparrow,\downarrow.

(42)

In other words, the motion of the boundary breaks the conservation of
Q
, while
preserving only the corresponding parity.

We now show how the non-Hermitian deformation affects such boundary-induced
transitions. We focus on solution I, for which the relevant dressed-state coefficients and matrix elements can be chosen real, and consider the channel

|\psi_{n}^{+}\rangle\longrightarrow|\psi_{n+2}^{+}\rangle.

(43)

Using the matrix elements reported above, we write

M_{n+2,+;\,n,+}(t)=i\frac{B_{n}(t)}{2}\dot{\kappa}(t)-D_{n}(t)\kappa(t),

(44)

where

\displaystyle B_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{n+2}(t)c_{n}(t)\sqrt{(n+1)(n+2)}+s_{n+2}(t)s_{n}(t)\sqrt{(n+2)(n+3)},

(45)

\displaystyle D_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A_{b}(t)B_{n}(t)+|g(t)|c_{n+2}(t)s_{n}(t)\sqrt{n+2}.

(46)

Here
|g(t)|=|\alpha(t)|e^{\delta(t)}
in solution I. Thus the non-Hermitian parameter

\delta
enters through the effective coupling
|g|
, the mixing angles in

c_{n},s_{n}
, and the energy gaps. Since
B_{n}
and
D_{n}
are real in solution I,
an instantaneous cancellation of this matrix element would require separately

\dot{\kappa}(t)B_{n}(t)=0,\qquad\kappa(t)D_{n}(t)=0.

(47)

For a genuine moving boundary,
\dot{\kappa}(t)\neq 0
, and therefore the
instantaneous matrix element cannot in general be made to vanish by tuning

\delta
. The non-Hermitian deformation does not remove the boundary-induced
coupling pointwise in time.

The relevant quantity for a transition probability, however, is not the
instantaneous matrix element alone, but the integrated transition amplitude. To
first order in the boundary perturbation we have

\mathcal{A}_{n+2,+\leftarrow n,+}(T)=-i\,\mathcal{I}_{n+2,+;\,n,+}(T),

(48)

with

\mathcal{I}_{n+2,+;\,n,+}(T)=\int_{0}^{T}dt\,M_{n+2,+;\,n,+}(t)\exp\!\left\{i\int_{0}^{t}ds\,\Delta_{n}^{++}(s)\right\}

(49)

and

\Delta_{n}^{++}(t):=E_{n+2}^{+}(t)-E_{n}^{+}(t)

(50)

The quantity
\mathcal{I}_{n+2,+;\,n,+}(T)
is the coherently accumulated
transition amplitude generated by the boundary motion over the time interval

[0,T]
.
Thus the transition probability is

P_{n+2,+\leftarrow n,+}(T)=|\mathcal{I}_{n+2,+;\,n,+}(T)|^{2}

(51)

to leading order. We understand here that the probability is evaluated in the Hermitian representation or equivalently using the physical metric in the quasi-Hermitian representation.

Even if the instantaneous matrix element is nonzero, the
integrated amplitude can vanish through destructive phase interference, because the contributions generated at different times can
interfere destructively. Conversely, if the boundary motion is resonant with
the gap
\Delta_{n}^{++}(t)
, the phases can align and the transition is
enhanced.

4.1
A boundary quench

To make this explicit, let us first consider a sudden boundary quench with
constant background parameters. Taking

\kappa(t)=\kappa_{0}\left[\Theta(t)-\Theta(t-T)\right],

(52)

so that the boundary is suddenly displaced, held fixed, and then returned, we compute

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{n+2,+;\,n,+}(T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}dt\left[\frac{i}{2}B_{n}(t)\dot{\kappa}(t)-D_{n}(t)\kappa(t)\right]e^{i\Phi_{n}^{++}(t)}

(53)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\frac{i}{2}B_{n}(t)\kappa(t)e^{i\Phi_{n}^{++}(t)}\right]_{0}^{T}-\frac{i}{2}\int_{0}^{T}dt\,\kappa(t)\dot{B}_{n}(t)e^{i\Phi_{n}^{++}(t)},

(54)

where
\Phi_{n}^{++}(t):=\int_{0}^{t}ds\Delta_{n}^{++}(s)
. We integrated here by parts using the identity that relates
B_{n}
and
D_{n}

D_{n}(t)=\frac{\Delta^{++}_{n}(t)}{2}B_{n}(t),

(55)

which follows from the static
\kappa
-dependent part of the squeezing
transformation.

Strictly speaking the integral is understood from immediately before the first jump to immediately after the return jump, so that the closed protocol satisfies
\kappa(0^{-})=\kappa(T^{+})=0
. Then (
54
) reduces to

\mathcal{I}_{n+2,+;\,n,+}(T)=-\frac{i}{2}\int_{0}^{T}dt\,\kappa(t)\dot{B}_{n}(t)e^{i\Phi_{n}^{++}(t)}.

(56)

For a closed boundary protocol, understood as
\kappa(0)=\kappa(T)=0
, the endpoint term
vanishes. Hence, if the dressed background parameters are constant, so that

\dot{B}_{n}=0
, the first-order transition amplitude vanishes even though the
instantaneous matrix element need not vanish.

A nontrivial control effect appears when the time-dependence of
\delta(t)
changes
the dressed basis during the boundary motion. In solution I this can be achieved
by allowing
\delta(t)
to vary while maintaining the quasi-Hermiticity
condition, for instance by taking
\omega_{f}(t)=-i\dot{\delta}(t)
. Then
|g(t)|=|\alpha(t)|e^{\delta(t)}
changes in time, so that
B_{n}(t)
changes in time.

A simple quench-type non-Hermitian protocol is the following. We keep the boundary
displaced,
\kappa(t)=\kappa_{0}
, during an interval, and switch the
non-Hermitian parameter from
\delta_{a}
to
\delta_{b}
at time
t_{1}
, then back
to
\delta_{a}
at time
t_{2}
with
0<t_{1}<t_{2}<T
.
The contribution from these jumps is

\mathcal{I}_{n+2,+;\,n,+}(T)=-\frac{i\kappa_{0}}{2}[B_{n}(\delta_{b})-B_{n}(\delta_{a})]\left[e^{i\Phi_{n}^{++}(t_{1})}-e^{i\Phi_{n}^{++}(t_{2})}\right].

(57)

Thus the transition is suppressed when

\Phi_{n}^{++}(t_{2})-\Phi_{n}^{++}(t_{1})=2\pi k,\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z},

(58)

and is generically nonzero otherwise. The suppression condition (
58
) has explicit solutions. If the parameters are constant on
[t_{1},t_{2}]
, with
\delta(t)=\delta_{b}
, we obtain

t_{2}=t_{1}+\frac{2\pi k}{\Delta_{n}^{++}(\delta_{b})},\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z},

(59)

where for solution I,

\Delta_{n}^{++}(\delta_{b})=2A_{b}+\frac{1}{2}\left[\sqrt{A_{b}^{2}+4|\alpha|^{2}e^{2\delta_{b}}(n+3)}-\sqrt{A_{b}^{2}+4|\alpha|^{2}e^{2\delta_{b}}(n+1)}\right].

(60)

Thus the transition is suppressed if the duration
t_{2}-t_{1}
of the
non-Hermitian pulse is tuned to an integer multiple of

2\pi/\Delta_{n}^{++}(\delta_{b})
. This means the non-Hermitian deformation can
switch the integrated transition probability on or off by changing the dressed
basis during the boundary motion, even though the instantaneous boundary
matrix element is not cancelled.

As a technical note we state that if non-Hermitian parameter
\delta(t)
is taken to be a step function, then

\dot{\delta}(t)
contains delta functions, and therefore

\omega_{f}(t)=-i\dot{\delta}(t)
is also distributional. Physically this idealised
quench should be understood as the limit of a rapid but smooth ramp.

4.2
A periodically moving boundary

A similar interpretation holds for a periodically moving boundary. Now we take

\kappa(t)=\kappa_{0}\sin(\Omega t),\qquad 0\leq t\leq T=\frac{2\pi N}{\Omega},

(61)

so that the boundary completes
N
oscillations.

If all background parameters are constant, then
B_{n}
is constant and the
protocol is closed, so equation (
54
) gives again

\mathcal{I}_{n+2,+;\,n,+}(T)=0

(62)

to first order after an integer number of boundary periods.

By contrast, if the non-Hermitian parameter is modulated, for example

\delta(t)=\delta_{0}+\varepsilon\cos(\nu t),\qquad|\varepsilon|\ll 1,

(63)

then

\dot{B}_{n}(t)\simeq-\varepsilon\nu\left.\frac{\partial B_{n}}{\partial\delta}\right|_{\delta=\delta_{0}}\sin(\nu t).

(64)

For approximately constant
\Delta_{n}^{++}
this gives

\mathcal{I}_{n+2,+;\,n,+}(T)\simeq\frac{i\varepsilon\kappa_{0}\nu}{2}\left.\frac{\partial B_{n}}{\partial\delta}\right|_{\delta=\delta_{0}}\int_{0}^{T}dt\,\sin(\Omega t)\sin(\nu t)e^{i\Delta_{n}^{++}t}.

(65)

Thus the transition is controlled by the sideband frequencies

\Omega\pm\nu
. It is enhanced when the phase-matching condition

\Delta_{n}^{++}\simeq|\Omega\pm\nu|

(66)

is satisfied, and it is suppressed when the corresponding Fourier component
vanishes or cancels over the full evolution. For finite
T
, this phase-matching condition should be understood within the usual Fourier width of order
1/T
.

In summary, without boundary motion the Hermitian fixed-boundary model forbids

n\leftrightarrow n\pm 2
transitions by conservation of
Q
. A moving boundary
opens such channels instantaneously through the parametric term

i\dot{\kappa}(b^{\dagger 2}-b^{2})/2
. However, for closed boundary protocols with
constant background parameters, the first-order integrated amplitude in the
dressed basis reduces to an endpoint term and vanishes. A genuine non-Hermitian
control of the transition probability arises when
\delta(t)
changes during
the boundary motion: the instantaneous matrix element remains generally
nonzero, but the accumulated transition amplitude can be suppressed or enhanced
by coherent interference.

5
Conclusion

We have constructed a time-dependent quasi-Hermitian extension of the
Schütte-Da Providência spin-boson model with complex, time-dependent
couplings. By solving the time-dependent Dyson equation (
2
) with a Dyson map (
3
)
containing a bosonic squeezing factor, a number-operator scaling and a spin
scaling, we obtained explicit conditions under which the transformed
Hamiltonian
h(t)
is Hermitian. Requiring the Dyson map and its inverse to be bounded
selects solution I as the natural regime for the subsequent analysis, while
solution II would require a more careful domain-dependent treatment.

A central observation is that the squeezing part of the Dyson map has a direct
geometric interpretation. Its time derivative produces the dilatation term (
8
), which is precisely the inertial term arising when a Hermitian system on a
time-dependent interval is transformed to a fixed domain. Thus the Hermitian
partner of the non-Hermitian fixed-domain model may be interpreted as the
fixed-domain representation of a Hermitian system with moving boundaries.

We also analyse the properties of the physical energy observable
\widetilde{H}(t)
, which in the
time-dependent quasi-Hermitian setting is distinct from the non-Hermitian Hamiltonian
H(t)
which
is the generator of time evolution. In the admissible bounded regime this operator is similar to the Hermitian
Hamiltonian
h(t)
, and therefore its instantaneous spectrum is real. Hence
the non-Hermitian deformation considered here does not lead to exceptional
points or complex physical energies. Level crossings, when present, are
ordinary Hermitian degeneracies rather than defective spectral singularities.

The genuinely new effects are instead dynamical. In the fixed-boundary
Hermitian model the operator
Q=N-S_{0}
is conserved, and the Hilbert space
splits into two-dimensional spin-boson sectors. The boundary motion breaks this
conservation law through terms containing
b^{\dagger 2}
and
b^{2}
, thereby
opening transitions between sectors whose boson numbers differ by two. The
first-order correction to the instantaneous energy levels vanishes, so the
leading spectral shifts arise at second order. The corresponding corrected
levels remain real, as expected from the quasi-Hermitian construction.

Finally, we showed that the same matrix elements controlling the second-order
energy corrections also determine first-order transition amplitudes between
dressed sectors. For closed boundary protocols with constant background
parameters, the integrated first-order transition amplitude reduces to an
endpoint contribution and vanishes. This reflects the fact that constant
squeezing is a unitary change of basis. Nontrivial control appears when the
non-Hermitian parameter varies during the boundary motion. In that case the
dressed basis changes in time, and the accumulated transition amplitude can be
suppressed or enhanced by coherent interference. Thus the admissible non-Hermitian
deformation yields a robust quasi-Hermitian framework: the physical energy spectrum remains real in the
entire parameter regime considered, while the time-dependent Dyson map provides
a mechanism for controlling boundary-induced transitions in the equivalent
Hermitian moving-boundary system.
