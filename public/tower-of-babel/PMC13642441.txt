Looking ahead: echolocation and flight behaviors of two fruit bat species navigating a corridor
Authors: Keegan E Eveland, Nikita M Finger, Jared M Jaroszewski, Leslie Bucio, Cynthia F Moss
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBats navigating clutter must steer around obstacles to avoid collision, while simultaneously planning future flight trajectories. In this study, we investigated active sensing strategies of two bat species negotiating turns under matched geometric constraints. We compare the Egyptian fruit bat (Rousettus aegyptiacus), a lingual echolocator that can actively direct its sonar beam axis using tongue driven mechanisms, and the short-tailed fruit bat (Carollia perspicillata), a laryngeal nasal echolocator whose head aim and nose leaf shape emission directionality. Bats flew through a felt lined, ‘L’ shaped corridor designed to elicit turns. A 32-channel ultrasonic array and 3D video tracking system captured their sonar emissions and flight trajectories. In both species, bats reduced flight speeds and increased angular deviation between sonar beam aim (acoustic gaze) and flight direction before the apex of high angle turns. A directional prediction analysis showed that sonar gaze reliably anticipated the direction of the subsequent heading change in both species (77% in R. aegyptiacus; 70% in C. perspicillata). However, baseline flight and echolocation behavior differed between species: C. perspicillata flew faster and, produced higher sonar pulse rates than R. aegyptiacus, consistent with species differences in body size, wing morphology, and sensory ecology. Together, these data demonstrate that fruit bats prospectively orient acoustic gaze to guide upcoming trajectory changes, extending “steering by hearing” beyond prey tracking by insectivorous bats to navigation of cluttered space by frugivorous bats.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00359-026-01818-0.Keywords: Clutter, Navigation, Sonar, Turns, Fruit bats, Prediction

Introduction

Over the course of his distinguished career, Peter Narins has unraveled scientific mysteries around the globe. Two of his field discoveries touch upon themes in our article on sonar-guided navigation in bats. First, Narins and colleagues travelled to Huangshan Hot Springs, China, where concave-eared torrent frogs (Amolops tormotus) live by fast-moving water that produces intense low-frequency noise that would normally mask frog communication calls. Narins and colleagues discovered that concave-eared torrent frogs both produce and detect ultrasonic signals in the same frequency band used by some echolocating bats—allowing them to avoid environmental masking of their communication signals. These audio-vocal adaptations enable conspecific communication despite the intense noise generated by running water (Feng et al. 2006). Second, Narins and collaborators studied navigation and prey capture by the Namib golden mole (Eremitalpa granti). In the Namib desert, isolated clumps of dune grass attract insects, and when wind moves the grass, it produces low-frequency ground vibrations. The golden mole uses these seismic cues to navigate to grass clumps and find prey (Lewis et al. 2006). These discoveries, among many others, were driven by Narins’s passion for understanding the remarkable sensory adaptations animals have evolved to function in extreme environments.

Animals navigating through cluttered environments must continuously transform dynamic sensory information into adaptive steering commands to avoid collision and to plan future locomotion trajectories. In humans, natural locomotor tasks reveal anticipatory coordination between gaze, head movements, and action planning, highlighting that sensing and movement form a coupled control system (Land and Collett 1974; Land and Lee 1994; Grasso et al. 1996, 1998; Imai et al. 2001; Pelz et al. 2001; Land and Tatler 2001; Hollands et al. 2002). Comparable coupling appears across taxa, with sensory sampling adapted to ecological niches and the physical constraints of locomotion (Gilbert 1997; Schnitzler et al. 2003; Lewis et al. 2006; Martin 2017). For flying vertebrates, the importance of sensorimotor coupling is amplified: rapid aerial movement leaves little margin for error, and obstacle avoidance often requires high curvature maneuvers executed under strict biomechanical constraints.

Except for oilbirds (Brinkløv et al. 2013), bats are distinct among flying vertebrates in that many species rely on echolocation, an active sensing modality in which animals broadcast sonar signals and process returning echoes to guide navigation and obstacle avoidance (Griffin 1958; Fenton 1995; Holderied and von Helversen 2003; Jensen et al. 2005; Ulanovsky and Moss 2008). A central feature of echolocation is its directionality, which provides a metric of the animal’s spatial acoustic gaze (Ghose and Moss 2003, 2006; Surlykke et al. 2009). Across taxa and contexts, signal timing, rate, intensity, and directionality are adapted to habitat structure and task demands, and bats dynamically modulate these parameters as they move between open and cluttered habitats (Schnitzler et al. 2003).

Turning presents a critical challenge for echolocating bats. Compared with straight flight, turns require rapid body reorientation, precise control of trajectory curvature, and sensory acquisition that supports an upcoming heading change. A recent study of insectivorous bats navigating turns in cluttered corridors shows that bats slow down and shift acoustic gaze in the direction of flight path planning, and that performance failures can occur when sensorimotor coordination is challenged by environmental complexity (Tuninetti et al. 2021). Past research demonstrates that sonar beam aim (acoustic gaze) is linked to flight motor output in insectivorous bats that track and intercept moving prey (Ghose and Moss 2006; Falk et al. 2015). These findings suggest a predictive control law in which bats redirect sensing toward the future trajectory before a change in body heading, but it remains unknown if such control laws extend to frugivorous bats’ species that forage stationary food sources. Here, we bridge this gap by studying the coordination of echolocation behavior and flight kinematics in two fruit bat species, Carollia perspicillata and Rousettus aegyptiacus, which face similar spatial tasks as insectivorous bats (commuting, negotiating vegetation, accessing foraging resources), while differing markedly in sonar production mechanisms, natural foraging behaviors, body size, and wing morphology.

Egyptian fruit bats (Rousettus aegyptiacus) are a large (~ 130–200 g) frugivorous species that integrates echolocation (Izhaki et al. 1995) and vision (Yovel et al. 2011; Danilovich et al. 2015; Lee et al. 2017; El-Mansi et al. 2020). Rather than producing sonar calls with the larynx, they emit brief (< 1 ms), broadband tongue clicks, spanning 10–50 kHz with peak frequencies of 20–40 kHz (Waters and Vollrath 2003; Holland et al. 2004; Lee et al. 2017). These clicks are typically produced in pairs and directed off-axis from the midline (Lee et al. 2017). The Egyptian fruit bat directs the slope of its sonar beam (the region where there is a − 3 dB drop in sound intensity to half the peak value of the signal) at the target for more accurate localization (Yovel et al. 2010). Their large wingspan (~ 60 cm) makes them well-suited for long range navigation that can exceed 20 km between roosts and foraging sites, and speeds between 3 and 15 m/s (Izhaki et al. 1995; Tsoar et al. 2011; Lučan et al. 2016; Toledo et al. 2020; Lourie et al. 2021). These long flights are associated with slightly above average wing loading (body weight relative to wing area), and a low aspect ratio (wingspan relative to wing width), which supports relatively fast and agile flight (Norberg and Rayner 1987; Norberg 1990).

In contrast, short-tailed fruit bats (Carollia perspicillata) are small (~ 15–20 g) nasal laryngeal echolocators that primarily navigate dense, cluttered habitats (Cloutier and Thomas 1992). They emit short (~ 1–2 ms) frequency modulated sweeps ranging from 40 to 120 kHz, with peak frequencies near 80–90 kHz (Kiai et al. 2023). Sometimes referred to as ‘whispering bats’ for their relatively low intensities (60–80 dB SPL at 10 cm) when stationary (Griffin and Novick 1955), more recent data shows C. perspicillata produce average sound source levels of 99 dB SPL during flight in open space (Brinkløv et al. 2011). Their distinct nose leaf acts as a directional structure that shapes and steers emitted calls (Hartley and Suthers 1987), enhancing control for sensing the environment. The combination of low wing loading and a low aspect ratio support high maneuverability in dense vegetation (Norberg and Rayner 1987; Norberg 1990; Carneiro et al. 2023). Combined with their small wingspan (~ 30 cm), and call rates up to 40 calls per second, C. perspicillata can perform agile flight and rapid sensory updates in acoustically complex environments (Kiai et al. 2023).

In this study, we compared echolocation behavior and flight kinematics of R. aegyptiacus and C. perspicillata navigating in a modular corridor that was designed to induce low angle and high angle turns via a doorway that was either open or closed. We hypothesized that different echolocation mechanisms and morphological features dictate different navigation strategies between these two species in an acoustically echoic corridor. We predicted that species differences would be most pronounced when they initiate high angle turns, where C. perspicillata, adapted to cluttered environments, would fly faster and call more frequently through the turn. By contrast, we predicted that R. aegyptiacus that inhabit open habitats would fly slower and produce echolocation clicks at lower rates. We also predicted that both species would direct their sonar in anticipation of future direction of travel. By comparing quantitative measures of flight and echolocation behavior of these two fruit bat species under identical navigation conditions, we aim to shed light on sensorimotor specializations and general control laws governing aerial navigation.

Methods

Subjects

Data were collected from twelve Rousettus aegyptiacus (6 female, 6 male) and eight Carollia perspicillata (4 male, 4 female). Bats were socially housed with other animals of the same species. All bats had access to food and water ad libitum.

Ethics statement

All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Johns Hopkins University (protocol number BA23A45). This research strictly conformed to the regulations set forth by the Animal Welfare Act and the Public Health Service Policy. The university maintains accreditation by the Association for the Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care International (2011). The handling and treatment of bats adhered to the guidelines outlined by the American Society of Mammalogists (Sikes and the Animal Care and Use Committee of the American Society of Mammalogists 2016).

Flight corridor

A corridor was constructed in an ‘L’ shape with two chambers separated by a doorway (Fig. 1). The corridor walls were made from cardboard and lined with felt to reduce echoes for acoustic recordings. White light levels were maintained at ~ 4 lx. However, due to an error, some of the trials were run in slightly higher luminance than intended. This luminance was not recorded at the time of testing. Given no significant differences were found between data collected under different light levels, the data was collapsed across the different treatments. Bats flew through the tunnel in two different configurations (i.e., closing the doorway created a straight section, or opening the doorway for an ‘L’ shaped configuration). If the doorway was open, bats flew through the opening in a 90° turn or flew in one section of the maze in a 180° turn. All bats completed at least one trial in each turn condition (90° and 180°). The total number of trials per bat varied because bats differed in their willingness to initiate repeated flights during a session. The total flight time for each bat was capped at 10 min. Bats were hand released from both the large and small compartments of the corridor (see Fig. 1) through a small opening that was closed after release. Since it was difficult for the bats to land on the walls of the corridor, they often performed multiple flights in one trial. To observe the natural behavior of bats in this experiment, we never exposed them to the corridor prior to testing. Corridor configurations were pseudorandomized across bats and trials to control for chamber size or direction specific effects. To control for potential order effects, half of the bats encountered the open doorway condition first, and the closed doorway condition second, and vice versa. In cases where bats remained in one chamber (< 5% of all trials), we initially encouraged passage through the doorway to quantify flight behavior across the full environment.

Schematic of flight tunnel showing microphones (red), infrared motion capture cameras, and tunnel wall configuration (black). Closing the doorway created a straight section, whereas opening the doorway created an ‘L’ shaped configuration

Echolocation signals were recorded with a 32-channel microphone array consisting of electret condenser microphones (D500x Ultrasound Detector, Pettersson Elektronik, Sweden) (Fig. 1). The sensitivity of each microphone was calibrated using a precision microphone (40DP 1/8”, GRAS., Denmark, and 7016 ¼”, ACO Pacific, Belmont, CA). Signals were bandpass filtered between 10 kHz and 100 kHz, amplified with a gain of 10x (SBPBP-S1, Alligator Technology, CA, USA), recorded at a 250 kHz sampling rate onto a data acquisition (DAQ) board (PXIe-6358, National Instruments, TX) and then saved as MATLAB files onto a computer. Sonar signal production rate was calculated in calls per second for C. perspicillata and clicks per second for R. aegyptiacus. Source levels were estimated on a per-call basis. Recorded pressures were corrected for microphone beam pattern attenuation based on off-axis angle and for geometric spreading. Frequency-dependent atmospheric absorption was accounted for using daily measurements of temperature and relative humidity. Source levels are reported in dB SPL re 20 µPa at 10 cm.

R. aegyptiacus flight trajectories were recorded using a 15-camera motion capture system (Vicon T40 and T40S, Oxford, UK), operating at 200 frames per second. Three reflective markers were affixed to a lightweight crown that was glued on the bat’s head. These markers served as reference points for reconstructing 3D flight paths (see Lee et al. 2017). Additionally, three markers were placed on the microphones in the room to capture the microphones’ positions and directions. Due to resolution limitations of the motion capture camera system, C. perspicillata trajectories were instead reconstructed from microphone array recordings using a custom time-difference-of-arrival (TDOA) localization pipeline, conceptually similar to established array based methods used in bats and other echolocating animals (Jensen and Miller 1999; Spiesberger and Wahlberg 2002; Surlykke et al. 2009; Blumstein et al. 2011; Brinkløv et al. 2011; Koblitz et al. 2011; Sumiya et al. 2017; de Framond et al. 2023). Trajectory coordinates were smoothed using MATLAB’s ‘smoothdata’ function with a moving-mean filter. Flight speed was calculated from the reconstructed trajectories by dividing the distance traveled by the time elapsed between frames.

Infrared video cameras (Phantom Miro 310, Vision Research, Wayne, NJ, USA) were used to record the bats’ flight behavior. These recordings were used for informal, qualitative observation of flight behavior (e.g., general turning patterns) and were not included in quantitative analyses. Three-dimensional trajectories were obtained from the motion capture system or microphone arrays.

Data analysis

Building on prior work examining sensorimotor adjustments of insectivorous big brown bats (Ghose and Moss 2006; Falk et al. 2014, 2015; Kothari et al. 2014; Tuninetti et al. 2021), we analyzed flight trajectories from Rousettus aegyptiacus and Carollia perspicillata using 300 ms windows centered on the turn apex. We used a 300 ms window centered on the manually marked apex as a standardized summary window for capturing the turn around the primary heading change. This choice is consistent with prior corridor and free flight studies indicating that gaze motor coupling unfolds over a few hundred milliseconds (Ghose and Moss 2006; Tuninetti et al. 2021). Additionally, we evaluated five different time (100, 200, 300, 400, and 500 ms) and three distance (0.25, 0.5, and 1.0 m) bins and found that 300 ms best captured the turn while minimizing overlap with adjacent or successive turns. This approach provided a baseline comparison of locomotor and sensory sampling strategies in the same corridor and under matched turning demands. To capture changes in the trajectory across the maneuver, we further subdivided each window into three distinct turn phases (see Turn analysis). This event aligned segmentation anchoring trajectories to meaningful kinematic landmarks rather than arbitrary timestamps which allowed us to localize when, within the turn, changes emerged both within and across species. Next, we quantified within turn changes in flight kinematics and sensing as a function of cumulative distance across turns spanning a range of angles, to test whether sensory adjustments preceded changes in flight direction. Finally, we assessed whether sonar gaze predicts flight direction using a lead peak correlation analysis.

Turn analysis

Turns were manually labeled on the smoothed 3D flight trajectory by visually marking five landmarks: pre turn trajectory, turn onset, turn apex, turn offset, and post turn trajectory, using predefined operational criteria (Table 1). Manual annotation was performed independently by two raters who identified turns in MATLAB (2025a) by the listed criteria and were blind to each other’s markings. Inter-observer reliability was assessed by comparing landmark placement across observers on a shared subset of trials, and intra-observer reliability was assessed by repeated annotation of the same trials by one observer after a time delay of one week. Agreement was quantified using tolerance-based agreement analysis, where we report the proportion of landmarks within ± 30 ms tolerance of each other for each turn. In cases where the precise start, apex, or end of a turn was unclear, raters prioritized consistent application of the definitions across trials rather than exact precision. For example, turns occurring near or within other turns were delineated as separate events only when an intermediate segment exhibited a clearly stabilized heading (i.e., an approximately straight trajectory) between direction changes. In the absence of such stabilization, nested or closely spaced direction changes were treated as a single continuous turn. From the 3D coordinates of the trajectories, we calculated the horizontal turn angle (change in heading direction between the pre and post turn headings). Turn angle was defined as the total angular change in direction and characterized as either: ≤90° (low angle turn) and > 90° (high angle turn). These categories were based on the experimental design which was intended to elicit either 90° or 180° turns. For each turn we identified three trajectory windows: Pre turn trajectory (Pre), Peri turn trajectory (Peri), and Post turn trajectory (Post). The turn itself was further divided into turn entry (turn onset to apex) and turn exit (apex to turn offset).

Species specific sonar beam models

R. aegyptiacus produce lingual echolocation clicks that typically occur in closely spaced pairs (Fig. 2a). Sonar emission direction (sonar gaze) was estimated using established click pair based directional methods (Yovel et al. 2010; Lee et al. 2017; Finger et al. 2025). A seven-point boxcar filter was applied to each click’s sonar gaze measurement, and the absolute value of this smoothed average was used as a measure of the sonar gaze deviation. Sonar gaze deviation was defined as the angular difference between the midline of the estimated beam direction and the instantaneous flight direction.

C. perspicillata are laryngeal echolocators producing FM calls emitted through the nostrils (Fig. 2a). For each call, sonar beam direction and beam width were estimated using a parametric azimuthal beam model fit to the microphone amplitude pattern (Surlykke et al. 2009; Jakobsen and Surlykke 2010). A grid search over beam axis azimuth and half power beam width (HPBW) identified the best fit beam parameters using a weighted correlation score, with microphone validity masks enforcing SNR, distance, and chamber-geometry constraints.

Sonar beam analysis

For both species, sonar beam measurements were taken on a per call/click basis using microphone array recordings. Sonar signals were recorded across an array of 32 microphones, with four or more required to have high signal to noise ratios (≥ 2:1), as established from previous studies (Lee et al. 2017; Finger et al. 2025). Direct measurements of head orientation from the video could not be extracted for either C. perspicillata or R. aegyptiacus. To estimate the functional direction of sonar emissions, we quantified beam direction relative to instantaneous flight direction in the horizontal plane. This approach provides a behaviorally meaningful reference frame that has been widely used in free flight biosonar studies when head tracking is unavailable (Surlykke et al. 2009; Jakobsen and Surlykke 2010; Koblitz et al. 2011). For each echolocation pulse, instantaneous flight direction was estimated from the bat’s trajectory by computing the direction of movement between successive position samples. This yielded a local estimate of the bat’s direction of travel at the time of each sonar pulse. Beam direction was estimated independently for each sonar pulse using the instantaneous spatial distribution of sound energy across the array (see sonar beam models below). Sonar gaze deviation was then defined as the angular difference between the estimated beam direction and the instantaneous flight direction, with zero degrees indicating perfect alignment between the two (Fig. 2b). This measure captures the bat’s directional aim of its sonar relative to its movement and provides an index of forward beam aim during navigation.

For both species we analyzed the angular sonar beam deviation from the direction of travel during two kinematic subsegments of each turn: turn entrance (turn onset to apex) and turn exit (apex to turn offset). Within each subsegment, the gaze deviation time series was normalized to a within segment percent progression (0–100%), permitting the comparative analysis of turns of different distances, duration, and pulse counts on a common scale. Sonar gaze deviation values were then binned across normalized progression into 10 equal width bins, and per turn binned trajectories were aggregated within experimental groups by computing the mean across turns at each bin.

Lead peak correlation analysis: does sonar gaze predict future change in heading direction?

To determine whether beam aim anticipated turning direction, we used a forward lead directional prediction analysis. For each turn, we asked whether the direction of the sonar beam at time t predicted the direction of the bat’s upcoming turn 100 ms later. Instantaneous flight was estimated from XY position using centered finite differences, and the change in heading over the subsequent 100 ms was used to quantify upcoming turning direction. We focused on sonar emissions during turn entry (turn onset to apex), because the goal of this analysis was to test whether sonar beam direction predicted the bat’s upcoming change in heading before the turn was completed. We chose a 100 ms anticipatory window because it captures near future steering behavior for both species: it is long enough to fall within or just after the apex of a turn, but short enough to avoid an entirely new turn. Additionally, this choice is consistent with prior work showing that acoustic gaze leads changes in flight turning with a positive lag on the order of ~ 0.1 s (Ghose and Moss 2006).

Sonar beam direction was treated as a signed angle relative to current flight direction, wrapped to the range − 180° to 180°. Directional agreement between beam aim and upcoming heading change was coded as a binary variable: 1 if the signs matched, 0 otherwise. To minimize noise driven sign flips, we excluded turns with four or fewer calls and samples in which either the beam offset or the future heading change was very small.

Statistics

Independent variables included bat, species, turn angle (defined as the total angular change in flight direction, categorized as either ≤ 90° or > 90°), turn segments (pre turn, turn onset, turn apex, turn offset, post turn), and cumulative distance. Turn angle categories were established based on the experimental design, which was intended to elicit primarily 90° and 180° turns in R. aegyptiacus. Thus, 90° served as a natural threshold for distinguishing shallower from steeper turn maneuvers. To maintain methodological consistency and ensure comparable assumptions across species, the same parameters were subsequently applied to C. perspicillata. Dependent variables include flight speed (meters per second), pulse rate (pulses per second), absolute sonar gaze (degrees). Normality of residuals was tested using quantile quantile plots and Shapiro-Wilk tests (Zar 2010). Significant violations of normality were detected for all dependent variables, likely due to inherent disparity in behavioral distributions between species. Therefore, all dependent variables were rank transformed prior to modeling, allowing the use of nonparametric linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) appropriate for data distribution. Because ranking transforms the data, all interpretations reference differences between ranks. Repeated measures, such as bat, flight segment, and trial, were accounted for in the models. Separate LMMs were run for each dependent variable. Pairwise comparisons of flight speed, sonar pulse rate, and absolute sonar gaze were conducted through contrast testing, using estimated marginal means with Holm corrections to account for inflated Type I error due to multiple comparisons. Contrast testing was chosen because it allows for targeted comparisons between specific groups or conditions.

A turn phase (Pre, Entry, Exit, Post) analysis for each species was tested using LMMs. Fixed effects were turn phase, turn angle, and their interaction. Random effects included turn number within a trial and bat ID to account for repeated measurements. For each dependent variable (flight speed, sonar gaze deviation, pulse rate, source level), significance was tested with likelihood-ratio tests (LRTs) on nested models for: interaction, turn angle main effect, and turn phase main effect.

For the directional prediction analysis, we used a one-tailed exact binomial test to evaluate whether each species steered their sonar gaze in the direction of their upcoming turn at a rate exceeding chance. The chance level was set at 50%, reflecting the two possible directions in which bats could orient their sonar gaze. We also tested whether the two species differed in their directional prediction success rates using a binomial Generalized Estimating Equation (GEE), with species as the fixed effect and sign agreement as the dependent variable, where 0 indicated that the sonar gaze direction did not match the upcoming heading change and 1 indicated that it did. Calls were clustered by a given turn number within a trial to account for repeated measurements within turns and to prevent pseudo-replication. Because calls are part of a sequence within a turn, we accounted for their non-independence by specifying an exchangeable within turn correlation structure. Agreement percentages are reported with Wilson 95% confidence intervals.

General turn analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4.1), using the ‘lme4’ package for mixed models and the ‘emmeans’ package for pairwise comparisons. Turn phase and predictive analyses were conducted in Python (version 3.12.4) within the Visual Studio Code development environment. Statistical modeling was performed using the ‘statsmodels’ package (version 1.108.2), and data handling and visualization were conducted using ‘pandas’, ‘numpy’, ‘scipy’, and ‘matplotlib’.

Results

Twelve Rousettus aegyptiacus and eight Carollia perspicillata participated in this study. Bats flew in an ‘L’ shaped maze with or without the doorway open to assess the differences in acoustic and flight behaviors between low and high changes in heading direction. In total, 54 trials (27 trials in R. aegyptiacus, 27 trials in C. perspicillata) were run across 20 bats (12 R. aegyptiacus, 8 C. perspicillata). Given that multiple turns could occur within a trial, each turn was treated as its own unit, yielding 142 turns from C. perspicillata, and 35 turns from R. aegyptiacus.

Turn phase differences

Intra and inter observer agreement for turn labeling was between 70 and 83% within a ± 30 ms tolerance window (C. perspicillata: inter = 0.70, intra = 0.80; R. aegyptiacus: inter = 0.78, intra = 0.83). Across both species, sonar pulse rate remained stable throughout the turn (Fig. 4a), showing no significant phase, angle, or interaction effects in either R. aegyptiacus (phase: LR = 3.46, df = 3, p = 0.326; angle: LR = 0.26, df = 1, p = 0.609; interaction: LR = 2.09, df = 3, p = 0.554) or C. perspicillata (phase: LR = 4.89, df = 3, p = 0.180; angle: LR = 0.59, df = 1, p = 0.441; interaction: LR = 2.30, df = 3, p = 0.512). Both species slowed down through both low and high angle turns (entry and exit) (Fig. 4b) (R. aegyptiacus: LR = 31.65, df = 3, p < 0.01; C. perspicillata: LR = 12.30, df = 3, p < 0.01). Only C. perspicillata showed phase related differences in source levels (Fig. 4c), and only at lower angle turns (LR = 68.44, df = 3, p < 0.01).

Turning slows flight and reorients sonar gaze. Sonar gaze predicts changes in flight direction during turn entry

Both species steered sonar gaze during turn entry in anticipation of future heading changes above chance during turns: bats aimed right before right turns and left before left turns. R. aegyptiacus showed a bias in sonar emissions in the direction of the upcoming turn on 77% of calls (95% CI: 68.5–82.6; n = 139 calls, N = 34 turns; one-tailed binomial test, p < 0.01), and C. perspicillata did so on 70% of calls (95% CI: 58.7–78.9; n = 76 calls, N = 58 turns; one-tailed binomial test, p < 0.01). In R. aegyptiacus, this produced a marked deviation from the species’ typical ‘left–right’ alternation, biasing emissions toward the direction of the upcoming turn resulting in ‘left-left’ and ‘right-right’ click pairs (Fig. 6a, b). This bias in sonar aiming did not differ between species (β = 0.348 ± 0.436, z = 0.800, p = 0.424, OR = 1.42, 95% CI [0.60, 3.33]; n = 215 calls, N = 92 turns).

Discussion

In this study, we tested whether a core principle of sensorimotor control in insectivorous bats, where acoustic gaze is coupled with future motor output (Ghose and Moss 2006; Falk et al. 2014, 2015), extends to frugivorous bats. In a ‘L’ shaped flight corridor, bats slowed down when entering a turn and redirected their sonar gaze away from the instantaneous flight vector toward the upcoming turn direction, with the largest gaze deviations in high angle turns. In addition, we discovered that current sonar gaze direction predicts the direction of a subsequent heading change, yielding above chance agreement that sonar pulses point to the future direction of travel (77% in R. aegyptiacus; 70% in C. perspicillata). Together, our results show that fruit bats actively look ahead with their sonar beam aim when negotiating rapid reorientation, extending classic prey tracking findings in insectivorous bats to a locomotor turning task in fruit bats.

Sonar gaze and speed reduction are complementary turn strategies

When navigating turns, both R. aegyptiacus and C. perspicillata slowed their flight speed without adjusting sonar pulse rate. A lower flight speed allows an increase in time to respond to information carried by echo returns before initiating a heading change (Falk et al. 2014; Sändig et al. 2014). Similarly, other studies investigating clutter navigation show that flight speed and sonar pulse timing are flexibly adjusted in response to environmental properties, including clutter density and flight corridor width (Petrites et al. 2009; Wheeler et al. 2016). This suggests that bats dynamically modulate their navigation planning according to sensory and collision costs.

Directing sonar gaze toward a future flight path would increase the information gathered from the region of space the bat is about to enter. This strategy would be especially useful during high angle turns, where there is less time to acquire and process sensory information before turn execution. Recent work suggests that flight speed is regulated using echo-derived flow cues, to provide a potential route for echoes to shape speed modulation even in the absence of object localization (Warnecke et al. 2016; Haron et al. 2026). Coupling slower flight speeds with a ‘look-ahead’ strategy would give bats more time to integrate echo information while balancing motor demands necessary for body reorientation. Given that this strategy was observed in both species, it may indicate a shared solution for navigating confined spaces.

During turning, ‘looking-ahead’ was more pronounced in R. aegyptiacus, whose lingual echolocation has previously been described as alternating ‘left–right’ click pairs (Yovel et al. 2011; Lee et al. 2017). In the turning corridor, that canonical directional alternation between click pairs was instead directed to a single side: left turns were often accompanied by consecutive left-directed clicks (‘left–left’), whereas right turns were accompanied by consecutive right-directed clicks (‘right–right’). Such turn biasing of click pair structure may drive the large sonar gaze deviations we observed relative to the direction of travel. R. aegyptiacus already emits clicks off-axis during straight flight (Yovel et al. 2011); during sharp turns, when this lateral bias is combined with head/body reorientation, it produces even larger sonar gaze deviations (Fig. 6b). Whether this anticipatory sampling arises from the head leading the body during reorientation, a stable head direction with off axis beam steering, or an interaction between the two can be answered by future studies. In C. perspicillata, whose nasal emission is shaped by the nose leaf (Hartley and Suthers 1987; Brinkløv et al. 2011), shifts in the directional aim of the sonar beam could reflect different underlying mechanisms, which can be probed in future work by direct measurements of head direction, nose leaf position, beam width, and detailed sonar beam aim reconstruction.

Species differences reflect baseline morphological and sensory ecology contrasts

The 300 ms turn apex window, captured broad baseline differences but blurred brief, phase locked dynamics. Accordingly, the clearest species divergence emerged in the event aligned turn subsegments, especially during turn entry near the apex. Both R. aegyptiacus and C. perspicillata slowed through turn maneuvers and redirected acoustic gaze toward the upcoming path, but their baseline flight and echolocation behaviors reflected differences in body size, flight morphology, and sensory ecology. C. perspicillata flew faster and produced higher sonar pulse rates than R. aegyptiacus. C. perspicillata are smaller, clutter adapted phyllostomid bats whose echolocation operates over short ranges within dense vegetation, where rapid, high frequency, and directional FM call production support frequent spatial updates and fine scale maneuvering (Brinkløv et al. 2011). By contrast, the larger bodied R. aegyptiacus likely faced greater biomechanical constraints in the narrow corridor: higher mass and larger wingspan increase inertial costs of reorientation. R. aegyptiacus’s wing loading and aspect ratio are well suited for efficient, sustained straight flight, but may impose larger costs for high curvature turns in confined space. Thus, the slower speeds and lower sonar sampling rates observed in R. aegyptiacus may reflect this species’ flight mechanics and the corridor’s spatial constraints.

This corridor task was simplified relative to natural flight. In the wild, bats navigate dynamically cluttered, three-dimensional environments where geometry and obstacle density vary continuously. The design of our experiment encouraged the bats to freely explore the corridor and to repeatedly sample the environment. As a result, flight behaviors likely reflect not only immediate sensorimotor demands but potentially cumulative experience and spatial memory over the course of a trial or session. This appears to resemble the refinements bats make across repeated flights in familiar natural settings (Hulgard et al. 2016). In our corridor, the doorway was opened to elicit 90° turns, making doorway state (open vs. closed) difficult to separate from turn geometry; nonetheless, doorway-related cues could have contributed to some behavioral variation and should be tested directly in future work. Lastly, whether the anticipatory gaze strategies in the horizontal plane documented here scale to natural foraging and commuting flights, and whether the degree of sonar gaze modulation varies with elevation, habitat, or ecological context, remains to be tested. Combining biologging with acoustic localization in field settings could help answer some of these questions.

Conclusions

Egyptian fruit bats, R. aegyptiacus, and Seba’s short-tailed fruit bats, C. perspicillata, used the same basic turn strategy to navigate the ‘L’ shaped corridor. Both species slowed on turn entry and redirected acoustic gaze away from the instantaneous flight vector toward the space they later occupied, with the largest angular difference between beam aim and flight velocity vector occurring during high angle turns and peaking near the apex. A forward lead analysis further showed that sonar gaze direction predicted the direction of the bat’s upcoming heading change, consistent with a ‘look ahead’ navigational principle linking echolocation and subsequent motor output. In R. aegyptiacus, the standard ‘left–right’ click beam axis alternation shifted into turn biased ‘left–left’ or ‘right–right’ click sequences. The two bat species differed in their baseline navigational behaviors: C. perspicillata flew faster and sampled the environment at higher sonar pulse rates than R. aegyptiacus, which reflects species differences in body size and wing morphology that affect flight maneuverability and the costs of rapid reorientation in confined space. Overall, this study exhibits a core navigational law that ‘steering by hearing’ is not just an insectivorous specific solution for tracking prey, but also a strategy that fruit bats use to negotiate rapid reorientation in cluttered spaces.
