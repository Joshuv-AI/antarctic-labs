Executive Summary:
This international collaborative research project between Johns Hopkins University (JHU)
and the Technical University of Munich (TUM) investigated how the human brain processes
and integrates multiple types of sensory information, such as touch and force, with the
goal of improving prosthetic limbs for amputees and advancing sensory capabilities in
humanoid robots.
The research advanced our understanding of how the brain responds to sensory feedback
in upper-limb amputees. Through experiments in which amputees received electrical
stimulation while performing phantom hand movements, we demonstrated that sensory
feedback activates the cortical sensorimotor and multisensory regions, and that these
regions communicate dynamically during stimulation. Experiments with intact-limb
participants explored the integration of visual, haptic, and force feedback, as well as in
virtual reality motor training, further showing how the brain processes multimodal sensory
information. In addition, this research inspired work on examining the reliability of where
amputees perceive sensations over time, which contributed to a successful doctoral
fellowship for continued investigation.
Our collaborators at TUM improved multimodal sensor technology combining tactile and
thermal feedback for humanoid robots, demonstrating the feasibility of integrating multiple
sensor types into a uniﬁed system for detecting and responding to environmental stimuli.
The experimental methods and analysis techniques developed across both teams,
including functional network analysis and multimodal sensor integration, provide a
foundation for future research in prosthetics and robotics.
This research beneﬁts the public by generating knowledge about how amputees process
restored sensory information. Advances in humanoid sensing contribute to safer human-
robot interaction. The project also fostered international collaboration and cross-
disciplinary training: one TUM doctoral student spent a summer at JHU working on
multimodal sensor integration, while two JHU students traveled to TUM to host workshops
on neuromorphic sensory encoding and sensory integration.

Summary of project activities:
Original Hypotheses and Approach:
This project hypothesized that (1) multiple sensory streams (e.g., touch, force,
temperature) could be integrated and delivered to amputees and humanoid robots to
enable more natural sensory experiences, and (2) the brain's processing of these sensory
inputs could be characterized and distinguished using EEG-based analysis. Our approach
combined JHU's expertise in prosthetics, sensory feedback, and neural signal analysis with
TUM's expertise in humanoid robotics and multimodal sensor development.
Project Activities:
Amputee Sensory Feedback and Cortical Mapping (JHU):
The main human experiments involved delivering transcutaneous electrical nerve
stimulation (TENS) to upper-limb amputees while recording EEG. Participants received
stimulation to their residual limbs and performed phantom hand movements, while also
receiving a visual cue. We successfully demonstrated that sensory feedback activates
somatosensory and motor cortical regions, and that dynamic communication between
these regions changes signiﬁcantly with sensory stimulation. We developed functional
network analysis to determine how providing somatosensory stimulation in addition to
visual inputs during phantom hand movements inﬂuence cortical large-scale systems. We
found that TENS increased the interconnectivity between the sensorimotor, visual, and
default mode networks in the amputee individual with extensive TENS experience.
Intact-limb Participant Studies (JHU):
We conducted complementary experiments with intact-limb participants to examine EEG
responses under visual and haptic feedback for precise grasp force generation, and varying
immersiveness of visual input for prosthesis motor training.
We examined whether vibrotactile feedback integrates with visual feedback to guide grasp
force. We found that during training with vibrotactile feedback, this source of feedback can
dominate other sources of sensory feedback, decreasing multisensory integration.
Moreover, we found that during training with vibrotactile feedback, cognitive load might
increase, as opposed to our expectations.
In addition, we recorded EEG while participants underwent prosthesis motor training with
various levels of visual immersiveness: static image, 2D video, and immersive virtual reality
(VR). We found that visual cue presentation using static images outperformed cue
presentation using both traditional videos and immersive VR simulations.
These studies provided additional insight into how the brain processes multimodal sensory
information in intact-limb population.
Peripheral Sensory Mapping (JHU):
We conducted analysis examining amputee participants’ percept maps and how spatially
reliable these percept maps are over time. This work was extended during the no-cost

extension period (August 2024–May 2025) and contributed to a successful doctoral
fellowship application by PhD student Ding for deeper investigation.
Humanoid Multimodal Sensing (TUM):
Our collaborators at TUM, funded separately by the Federal Ministry of Research,
Technology and Space of Germany (BMBF), developed multimodal sensors integrating
tactile and thermal sensing for humanoid robots. This work demonstrated the feasibility of
combining multiple sensory modalities in a uniﬁed robotic sensing framework. A TUM
doctoral student (Fengyi Wang) visited JHU for a summer to collaborate on this integration
work.
Student Exchange and International Workshops:
The grant supported meaningful international exchange. One TUM student visited JHU; two
JHU students visited TUM to host workshops on sensory feedback and prosthetics. Another
workshop was hosted during the 11th International IEEE/EMBS Conference on Neural
Engineering (NER). These activities trained students at the intersection of neuroscience,
prosthetics, and robotics.
