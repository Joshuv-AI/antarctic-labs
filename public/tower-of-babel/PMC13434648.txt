Sequence redesign of glycosyltransferases for enhanced heterologous expression and glycosylation efficiency in Escherichia coli
Authors: Shanshan Zhang, Yilei Han, Yujing Ding, Xiaofan Lin, Zhipeng Yuan, Xinxiao Sun, Qipeng Yuan, Zheng Liu, Yifei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Heterologous expression of enzymes from higher organisms limits the construction of microbial cell factories for natural product biosynthesis. Here we develop a ProteinMPNN-based sequence redesign strategy, guided by essential structural features and evolutionary conservation, to improve the bacterial expression of heterologous enzymes. Applied to two plant glycosyltransferases, TOGT and UGT84A56, for esculetin glycosylation, this strategy generates extensively redesigned variants with markedly enhanced soluble expression and catalytic activity in Escherichia coli. In vitro enzyme assays and in vivo whole-cell conversions show consistent improvements, outperforming conventional solubility-enhancing strategies. Molecular simulations suggest that the improved performance arises from global optimization of hydrophobic and hydrophilic residue exposure while preserving productive substrate-binding interactions. Fed-batch fermentation achieved titers of 2.11 g/L cichoriin and 4.05 g/L aesculin. This work establishes a generalizable route for converting difficult-to-express eukaryotic enzymes into efficiently expressible and functional variants, thereby expanding the enzymatic toolbox for microbial cell factory construction.

Introduction

The construction of microbial cell factories represents a transformative approach for the sustainable production of high-value natural products, providing a solution to the growing demand for environmentally friendly and cost-effective chemical synthesis1,2. Central to this strategy is the integration and expression of heterologous genes encoding foreign enzymes within host cells3,4. For example, the reconstitution of plant biosynthetic pathways has enabled the synthesis of alkaloids5,6, flavonoids7,8, and terpenoids9 in Escherichia coli, offering an alternative to traditional plant-based extraction methods. However, the expression of heterologous enzymes is often hindered by factors such as codon usage bias, protein misfolding and aggregation, host cell toxicity, and the lack of necessary post-translational modification systems10–12. Conventional strategies to enhance enzyme expression include optimizing expression conditions, adjusting promoter strength, changing expression vectors and/or hosts, optimizing codon, co-expressing molecular chaperones, and fusing with solubility tags13–16. However, the effectiveness of these approaches is often protein-dependent and, in many cases, compromises enzyme activity17–20. Thus, alternative strategies are needed to reliably improve the expression of heterologous enzymes.

Cichoriin (7-O-β-d-glucoside of esculetin) and aesculin (6-O-β-d-glucoside of esculetin) are both glycosylated derivatives of esculetin (6,7-dihydroxycoumarin), a bioactive coumarin compound found in Fraxini Cortex, a traditional oriental medicine used for over 1000 years. Recent investigations have shown that esculetin can reduce inflammation by inhibiting the nuclear factor-kappa B and MAPK signaling pathways, suppress tumor growth by regulating the cell cycle, and exert strong antioxidant effects by scavenging reactive oxygen species21,22. However, its low solubility and bioavailability hinder its clinical development23. In contrast, cichoriin and aesculin (commonly extracted from Cichorium intybus24 and Fraxinus chinensis25, respectively) possess therapeutic activities comparable to esculetin, but with enhanced solubility and stability due to glycosylation (Fig. 1a), making them promising alternatives with improved pharmacokinetic properties26,27. The regioselective glycosylation of esculetin is mediated by distinct enzymes (Fig. 1b). Sun et al. identified enzymes involved in aesculin biosynthesis in horse chestnut (Aesculus chinensis) and demonstrated its de novo production from glucose in E. coli, in which a glycosyltransferase, UGT84A56 from Aesculus chinensis, was employed to specifically catalyze the 6-O-glucosylation of esculetin28. Subsequently, Xie et al. enhanced esculetin supply by engineering cinnamyl-CoA ortho-hydroxylase from Arabidopsis thaliana (AtF6’H) and utilized Nicotiana tabacum-derived TOGT for its 7-O-glycosylation, thereby producing cichoriin29. Using this strategy, they achieved titers of 73.9 mg/L for cichoriin and 254.8 mg/L for aesculin. Notably, the accumulation of residual esculetin suggests that glycosylation remains limited by the activity of glycosyltransferases. Recent advances in machine learning have dramatically revolutionized protein engineering, moving beyond limited point mutations toward the comprehensive redesign of enzyme scaffolds and sequences30,31. Notably, deep learning models have shown success in generating candidate amino acid sequences tailored to given protein backbones or desired functions32,33. For example, ProteinMPNN-based protocols have been successfully used to redesign myoglobin and tobacco etch virus protease, resulting in enhanced thermostability and activity34. However, these applications are largely limited to in vitro settings, and heavily sequence-redesigned heterologous enzymes have yet to be applied in metabolic systems. On the other hand, ProteinMPNN tends to generate protein designs with greater structural rigidity, where gains in folding and stability often come at the expense of catalytic activity35–37. Achieving the right balance among structural stability, expressibility, and enzymatic activity requires careful identification and preservation of functionally and evolutionarily important residues from the original sequence in the redesign process.

In this study, we present a structure- and evolution-guided design strategy, leveraging ProteinMPNN to redesign the sequences of TOGT and UGT84A56, to improve their heterologous expression in E. coli for efficient esculetin glycosylation. A two-stage evaluation pipeline is established to identify high-quality designs with enhanced solubility and a high likelihood of retaining catalytic activity. Ten experimental designs and a total of twelve control designs are proposed for each enzyme and are evaluated for their expression level, activity, and product titers. Whole-cell biotransformation experiments confirm enhanced product titers in all 10 designs (100%) of TOGT and 2 designs (20%) of UGT84A56. For TOGT, the sequence-redesigned variants show markedly enhanced soluble expression (2.3–6.4-fold) and catalytic efficiency (1.8–11.8-fold). In feeding experiments conducted in the E. coli BW25113 system, cichoriin titers reach up to 393 mg/L, representing an approximately 14-fold increase over the wild-type enzyme. Likewise, the best design of UGT84A56 exhibits a 1.8-fold increase in soluble expression and a 10.8-fold improvement in catalytic efficiency, resulting in an aesculin titer of 246 mg/L, 4.9 times greater than that of the native UGT84A56. Comparative experiments demonstrate that this approach outperforms other commonly used solubility-enhancement strategies, such as molecular chaperone co-expression and fusion to solubility tags. Sequence-level analysis reveals that the redesign process favors hydrophobic substitutions (e.g., Ala, Leu) and charged residues (e.g., Lys, Arg, Asp, Glu), and Pro. Notably, redesigned variants tend to promote the burial of hydrophobic residues and increased surface exposure of hydrophilic residues, indicating a global reorganization of residue distribution that is likely associated with enhanced expression and thermal stability. In addition, binding energy calculations for glycosyltransferase-esculetin complexes suggest that conserved active-site residues contribute the majority of binding affinity, providing a molecular basis for maintaining catalytic activity and substrate specificity. Application of these designs in engineered E. coli BW1 strains for fed-batch fermentation yields titers of 2.11 g/L cichoriin and 4.05 g/L aesculin, which are—to the best of our knowledge—record-high titres. These findings highlight the potential of comprehensive sequence engineering to enhance heterologous protein expression, paving the way for the development of highly productive microbial cell factories for the synthesis of complex natural products.

Results

Redesign the sequences of glycosyltransferases with ProteinMPNN

We developed a strategy to identify essential residues to fix during sequence redesign (Fig. 1c, d), aiming to enhance the soluble expression of glycosyltransferases while preserving their catalytic activity. Two sets of residues were fixed: (1) residues within 8 Å of the substrate binding pocket as suggested by molecular docking, containing the first-shell and second-shell residues to preserve enzyme-substrate interactions and active-site geometry; and (2) evolutionarily conserved residues identified through multiple sequence alignment (MSA) using ConSurf 38, to minimize disruption of structural and functional integrity. The remaining residues were alterable with inverse folding algorithms and were designated as designable sites. In this way, the redesign primarily focused on solvent-exposed regions that were spatially distant from the active site and could potentially affect solubility. The fixed residues and the native protein backbone were input into ProteinMPNN to generate new enzyme sequences, which were further screened to obtain new enzyme designs for experimental validation (Fig. 1e). During the sequence generation stage, a large number of sequences, each assigned a global score by ProteinMPNN were produced. Among these, sequences with lower global scores were prioritized for further evaluation, as lower scores indicate better sequence-structure compatibility under the ProteinMPNN model32,39. The structures of these designs were predicted using AlphaFold240 and subsequently assessed through a series of structure-based metrics, including backbone root mean square deviation (RMSD), predicted solubility (assessed using SoluProt41), predicted local distance difference test (pLDDT), and predicted TM-score (pTM) values. These metrics were normalized and then aggregated using specified weighting factors to calculate a weighted score. After re-ranking these sequences, the top redesigns with the highest weighted scores were selected for experimental validation. Using this workflow, we redesigned the native sequences of TOGT and UGT84A56 to improve their solubility and further enhance their activity.

Redesign of TOGT for enhanced soluble expression and activity in E. coli

The enzyme redesign strategy was firstly applied to the glycosyltransferase TOGT (Fig. 2a). Molecular docking identified 14 residues in the first shell, 69 in the second shell, and 393 in the outer shell. Conservation analysis further revealed that approximately half of the residues in the outer shell (190 sites) were designable, accounting for roughly 40% of the full-length sequence (Supplementary Fig. 1). After determining the residues to fix, a two-tiered filtering pipeline was applied to select the top 10 sequence designs with the highest weighted scores from 20 candidates (Supplementary Table 1). These candidates were preselected from 100 ProteinMPNN-generated sequences with the lowest global scores. The yielded TOGT designs and the native enzyme were expressed using E. coli BL21(DE3) cells, and the soluble protein concentration and specific activities of the cell lysate were measured, as shown in Fig. 2b. All of the 10 designs exhibited markedly improved soluble expression and specific activity compared to the native TOGT, with the highest soluble protein concentration showing a 6.4-fold increase. SDS-PAGE and Western blot analyses confirmed that all enzymes generated by ProteinMPNN were expressed predominantly as soluble proteins, whereas the native TOGT primarily formed insoluble inclusion bodies (Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3). Kinetic characterization was further conducted with the purified enzymes (Table 1 and Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5). Most designs maintained catalytic activity comparable to the native enzyme, with certain designs, such as sdTexp8, displaying a substantially higher turnover number (kcat). Additionally, all designs showed reduced Km values, suggesting improved substrate affinity. As a result, the catalytic efficiencies (kcat/Km) of these enzymes were enhanced by 1.8- to 11.8-fold relative to native TOGT. The melting temperatures (Tm) of these designs increased significantly (by 9.4 to 23 °C) compared to native TOGT (Supplementary Fig. 6), showing the inherent bias of ProteinMPNN-guided sequence redesign toward enhanced thermostability34,37. Ablation experiments revealed that while ProteinMPNN alone improved soluble expression, the effect was further amplified when more functional and conserved residues were fixed (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 7a). Whole-cell biotransformation experiments were conducted using the designed enzymes with esculetin feeding, and the cichoriin titers were measured as an indicator of the in vivo activity of the enzyme designs. As shown in Fig. 2c, the in vivo activity showed a strong correlation with the total enzyme activity of the cell lysate measured in vitro. As expected, the designs generated by the workflow exhibited the highest catalytic performance compared to all control sets (Supplementary Fig. 8). E. coli BL21(DE3) strains expressing the designs produced 236–299 mg/L cichoriin, representing a 26.2- to 33.2-fold improvement compared to the native counterpart. The significantly enhanced expression levels, combined with the improved in vitro and in vivo activities, collectively demonstrated the effectiveness and robustness of our enzyme redesign strategy.

Among the 472 residues in UGT84A56, 212 sites were identified as designable, accounting for 45% of the full-length sequence (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 9). Experimental validation of 10 selected designs from the workflow showed that 8 designs achieved improved expression levels, while 3 designs, sdUexp4, sdUexp6, and sdUexp8, exhibited specific activities that were higher than or comparable to the native UGT84A56 (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Figs. 10 and 11, and Supplementary Table 2). Among these, the top-performing design, sdUexp6, demonstrated a catalytic efficiency (kcat/Km) that was 10.8-fold greater than the native enzyme. Additionally, these designs also exhibited enhanced thermostability, with increases in Tm of 9.1–22.8 °C (Supplementary Fig. 13). Ablation experiments further indicated that most sequences generated by ProteinMPNN exhibited increased protein expression, although the magnitude of improvement was less pronounced than that observed for TOGT (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Figs. 10 and 14). In vivo whole-cell biotransformation experiments were conducted using the high-activity redesigned enzymes alongside the control groups (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 16). For most cases, the in vivo productions of aesculin were consistent with the in vitro catalytic activity observed in cell lysates, except for native UGT84A56 and sdUexp8, presumably due to their low cellular stability. Notably, sdUexp4 and sdUexp6 achieved the highest aesculin titers of 313 and 402 mg/L, respectively, representing a 10.8- and 13.9-fold increase compared to the native enzyme (Supplementary Fig. 16). In contrast, designs generated without fixing the first- and second-shell residues showed significantly reduced activity. These findings mirrored the significant increases in product titers observed in TOGT designs and validated again the effectiveness of our enzyme redesign strategy.

Comparison with conventional expression-enhancing strategies

The ProteinMPNN-redesign-based solubility-enhancement strategy was compared to commonly used approaches, including co-expression with molecular chaperones and fusion to solubility tags42, by assessing product titers in whole-cell biotransformation experiments (Fig. 4). These experiments were conducted in an alternative E. coli chassis strain, BW25113 (Supplementary Fig. 17). For TOGT, all 10 designs (sdTexp 1–10) exhibited higher cichoriin production compared to the wild-type enzyme and control groups (Fig. 4a). The highest-producing design reached 393 mg/L, corresponding to a 14-fold increase over the wild-type. In contrast, designs lacking residue constraints or fixing only first-shell residues showed inferior performance. Among the four tested chaperones15,43—ibpAB, RpoH (σ32), DnaKJ, and GroSL—only GroSL co-expression led to an increase in yield (Supplementary Table 11). However, chaperone-based approaches constrained cell growth, as evidenced by reduced OD600 values, possibly due to the metabolic burden they imposed on cells. Four solubility tags, including CysQ, GST, SUMO, and MBP, were tested20,44. Fusion with SUMO or MBP tags resulted in modest increases in cichoriin titers, consistent with improved soluble expression (Supplementary Fig. 18). For UGT84A56, comparisons among different strategies revealed a similar pattern (Fig. 4b). These comparative experiments underscore the reliability and efficiency of the proposed enzyme redesign strategy as an alternative to traditional methods for redesigning and identifying enzymes that exhibit both high soluble expression and catalytic performance.

Mechanism analysis for enhanced stability and preserved activity

Substrate-bound molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed on representative redesigned variants and the wild-type enzymes (Fig. 5) to elucidate the molecular basis underlying enhanced stability and catalytic performance. For TOGT, three variants (sdTexp3, sdTexp5, and sdTexp8) were analyzed. Residue-level statistics at designable positions (Supplementary Figs. 19 and 20) revealed consistent compositional shifts from the wild-type to the redesigned variants, including increased frequencies of nonpolar residues (e.g., Ala, Leu), charged residues (Arg, Asp, Glu, Lys), and Pro. To evaluate the structural consequences of these changes, we analyzed the correlation between variations in residue hydrophobicity (ΔKyte–Doolittle score) and relative solvent accessibility (ΔrSASA) at designable sites (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 22). A significant negative correlation was observed across all three systems, indicating that mutations increasing hydrophobicity tend to reduce solvent exposure, whereas mutations decreasing hydrophobicity (i.e., increasing hydrophilicity) tend to enhance exposure. This global redistribution of residue physicochemical properties provides a plausible explanation for the observed improvements in soluble expression and thermostability. In the redesign strategy, most residues proximal to the esculetin and UDP-glucose were preserved. These residues play a crucial role in substrate recognition, as supported by per-residue energy decomposition analysis using MM-GBSA (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 24). The hydrophobic residues near the reactive center, such as Leu90, Leu81, Phe382, and Phe121, contribute to the binding of esculetin. Additionally, Arg409 likely anchors the hydroxyl group of the substrate through electrostatic interactions, preserving positional selectivity. Combined analysis of binding energy and the catalytic distance46 between His16-NE2 and esculetin-O7 indicates that the redesigned variants, like the wild-type enzyme, could form catalytically competent Michaelis complexes (Supplementary Fig. 23). As esculetin (146 Å3) is substantially smaller than the binding pocket of TOGT (~ 1100 Å3) (Supplementary Table 3), multiple binding modes are accessible within the active site. Consequently, more diverse substrate-binding conformations can occur at positions farther from the catalytic center, potentially mediated by different, including nonconserved, residues. Nevertheless, the aggregated energy decomposition analysis showed that the majority of enzyme-substrate interaction energy is contributed by residues retained within the binding pocket (Supplementary Fig. 25), supporting the effectiveness of structure-constrained redesign in preserving catalytic function.

A similar analysis was performed for UGT84A56 and its top-performing variant sdUexp6 (Fig. 5d–f). At designable positions, comparable residue substitution trends were observed (Supplementary Fig. 26), including increased frequencies of hydrophobic residues (Ala, Leu, Val), charged residues (Arg, Glu, Lys), and Pro. Although the number of Asp residues decreased, the total number of negatively charged residues remained slightly increased (39 to 40). Correlation analysis between ΔrSASA and ΔKyte–Doolittle scores for the selected residues (Fig. 5e and Supplementary Fig. 28) revealed trends consistent with those observed in TOGT, with hydrophobic substitutions favoring burial and hydrophilic substitutions favoring solvent exposure. In addition, global structural descriptors, including RMSD, RMSF, Rg, and total SASA, remained largely unchanged between redesigned and wild-type enzymes (Supplementary Fig. 27).

Conformational and binding energy analysis similarly demonstrated that sdUexp6 retains the ability to form a catalytically relevant substrate-bound conformation (Supplementary Fig. 29). Key residues include Leu86, Phe123, Val284, Trp380, Leu198, and Phe14 (Fig. 5f and Supplementary Fig. 30). These residues form a hydrophobic pocket that favors binding of the aromatic ring of esculetin. No electrostatic anchoring residues, like Arg409 in TOGT, were observed for UGT84A56. This difference may allow esculetin to adopt alternative binding modes in which the hydroxyl group on the side distal to the lactone moiety is positioned closer to the reactive center, thereby contributing to the distinct glycosylation-site selectivity. Similarly, analysis of the binding contribution of retained residues showed that conserved binding-pocket residues dominate the interaction energy (Supplementary Fig. 31), emphasizing again the importance of residue fixation in preserving catalytic function during redesign.

De novo production of cichoriin and aesculin from glucose using E. coli

The genes encoding the redesigned enzymes, along with other pathway enzymes, were introduced into the E. coli BW1 chassis (Supplementary Table 8), resulting in the engineered strains BW-sdTexp5/sdTexp6 and BW-sdUexp4/sdUexp6 for the biosynthesis of cichoriin and aesculin starting from glucose, as illustrated in Fig. 6a. Small-scale fermentations in 50 mL shake flasks were conducted to determine the optimal growth conditions for these strains, with the resulting growth curves and product titers shown in Fig. 6b, c. Rapid cell growth was observed within the first 24 h, reaching maximal biomass at ~48 h, accompanied by continuous accumulation of cichoriin and aesculin. After 72 h, BW-sdTexp5 and BW-sdTexp6 produced 174 and 145 mg/L cichoriin, respectively, both exceeding the performance of the wild-type TOGT strain. Similarly, BW-sdUexp4 and BW-sdUexp6 achieved aesculin titers of 399 and 441 mg/L, respectively. Fed-batch fermentations in a 3-L bioreactor were performed using the high-yielding strains BW-sdTexp5 and BW-sdUexp6, with results presented in Fig. 6d, e. After 120 h, BW-sdTexp5 achieved a maximum OD600 of 49 and a cichoriin yield of 2110 mg/L, while BW-sdUexp6 achieved an OD600 of 65 and an aesculin yield of 4050 mg/L. To our knowledge, these represent the highest reported titers for microbial production of cichoriin and aesculin to date (Supplementary Table 5). During fermentation, esculetin levels in the culture broth dropped to nearly undetectable levels (< 5 μM), whereas strains expressing the wild-type enzyme displayed pronounced esculetin accumulation (Supplementary Fig. 32). These results suggest that glycosyltransferase activity is no longer the primary rate-limiting step under the tested conditions. Discussion

Efficient expression of heterologous enzymes remains a major challenge in constructing microbial cell factories for functional molecule synthesis3,4, particularly when reconstructing natural product biosynthetic pathways using enzymes derived from higher plants and animals. Such efforts often require extensive, empirically driven optimization of host strains, codon usage, expression conditions, and transcriptional and translational regulation13–16. In this study, we developed a ProteinMPNN-assisted sequence redesign strategy to systematically optimize enzymes with poor native expression. Using glycosyltransferases TOGT and UGT84A56 as examples, this strategy successfully enhanced their soluble expression in E. coli without compromising—and in many cases, even boosting—their enzymatic activity. In whole-cell catalysis, the redesigned enzymes consistently outperformed conventional expression-enhancing strategies, such as fusion with solubility tags and co-expression with molecular chaperones. Application of the optimized enzymes in E. coli BW1 strains enabled de novo biosynthesis of cichoriin and aesculin from glucose, reaching gram-per-liter titers, to our knowledge, the highest reported to date. Notably, these improvements were achieved using only simple docking and conservation analysis, without requiring detailed mechanistic characterization, highlighting the simplicity and practical accessibility of the approach.

A central feature of this strategy is the balance between retaining key residues and redesigning the rest of the enzyme sequence. By preserving evolutionarily conserved and structurally proximal residues while allowing variation elsewhere, the strategy effectively decouples functional preservation from global optimization. The adjustable nature of these constraints, such as the radius defining second-shell residues and conservation thresholds, enables systematic exploration of the sequence space, as supported by ablation experiments demonstrating their impact on expression, stability, and activity. Mechanistically, the redesigned enzymes converge on a common pattern of global surface reorganization, characterized by preferential burial of hydrophobic residues and exposure of hydrophilic residues, while maintaining overall structural integrity. This redistribution likely contributes to enhanced solubility and stability. At the same time, preservation of key binding-pocket residues preserves substrate recognition and catalytic competence, as supported by MD simulations and binding energy analyses. Together, these observations support a general mechanism in which constraint-guided redesign improves enzyme performance through global physicochemical optimization under local functional constraints.

Enzyme designs optimized by ProteinMPNN consistently exhibited relatively good solubility expressed in E. coli, as observed for both TOGT and UGT84A56, two representative plant-derived glycosyltransferases that belong to distinct clades across diverse species (Supplementary Fig. 33). Interestingly, simultaneous improvements in expression and activity were achieved more frequently for TOGT than for UGT84A56, likely reflecting differences in their baseline properties. Native TOGT exhibits low expression but relatively high intrinsic activity, whereas UGT84A56 displays higher baseline expression but lower catalytic efficiency. This suggests that ProteinMPNN-based redesign primarily enhances foldability and structural stability, leading to larger gains for poorly expressed enzymes (Supplementary Table 6). These findings further imply a potential trade-off between foldability and catalytic efficiency and highlight the need for customized evaluation functions tailored to enzymes with different baseline properties. More importantly, substrate specificity was retained despite redesign of 20–25% of the sequence, indicating that constraint-guided redesign can tolerate substantial sequence perturbation without compromising catalytic function. This robustness supports the potential generalizability of the strategy to other enzyme classes, although further refinement and validation across diverse systems are required. In addition, as ProteinMPNN is primarily trained on protein structures biased toward soluble, often microbial-expressed proteins, its application to non-microbial systems (e.g., plant-based contexts) may require further optimization, for example, by incorporating organism-specific structural data to develop inverse folding models tailored to these systems.

More broadly, our results are consistent with the well-established view that protein structure and function are more conserved than sequence47,48. The preservation of enzymatic activity despite extensive sequence variation (> 20%) reflects the high mutational tolerance of non-conserved regions, which are subject to limited selective pressure during natural evolution49. In contrast, inverse-folding models, such as ProteinMPNN, which are trained to capture sequence features essential for successful folding, can propose large sets of reasonable mutation combinations at non-conserved positions. This enables in silico exploration of sequence space on a scale order of magnitude beyond the reach of conventional evolution-based strategies. Combined with efficient computational filtering, this approach facilitates the engineering of superior enzymes with tailored properties for industrial applications50. Beyond improving glycosyltransferase expression and activity, our results also provide insights relevant to physics-guided protein engineering. The convergent physicochemical pattern observed across redesigned enzymes—namely, enhanced burial of hydrophobic residues, increased exposure of hydrophilic residues, and preservation of key active-site interactions—suggests that global surface optimization under local functional constraints may represent a general strategy for improving protein foldability and expression while maintaining catalytic function. This observation is consistent with recent efforts to integrate physical principles, mechanistic understanding, and machine-learning-based design in protein engineering51–53.

Methods

Materials

The genes of TOGT from Nicotiana tabacum and UGT84A56 from Aesculus chinensis were synthesized and codon-optimized to E. coli by Genewiz (Beijing, China). Recombination strains containing corresponding plasmids used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 8, respectively. Gene segments used in this study were generated by polymerase chain reaction with the corresponding primers as shown in Supplementary Table 9. All primers, insertions, and mutations of target genes were sequenced by Genewiz. Plasmids were constructed by standard enzyme digestion and ligation or homologous recombination method. DNA-modifying enzymes and T4 DNA ligase were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Plasmid extraction and gel purification kits were bought from Omega Bio-tek, Inc. E. coli DH5α and BL21(DE3) were purchased from TransGen Biotech for cloning and protein expression. Esculetin, aesculin, and cichoriin were purchased from Macklin Biotechnology (China). All chemicals and reagents are of analytical grade.

Sequence redesign and filtering

Sequences and structural modeling

The sequence of TOGT from Nicotiana tabacum was fetched from the Uniprot database (accession: Q9AT54). The UGT84A56 sequence of Aesculus chinensis was obtained from the report by ref. 28. Functionally, they catalyze O-glycosylation reactions by transferring glucose from the donor UDP-glucose to esculetin at either the 6-OH or 7-OH positions, producing aesculin and cichoriin, respectively. The complete amino acid sequences of TOGT and UGT84A56 are provided in Supplementary Tables 7 and 10. The structures of all these sequences were predicted using the AlphaFold2 webserver40.

Structure-based fixation

Molecular docking was performed using AutoDockTools (1.5.7)54, and the substrate-enzyme conformations with the lowest ΔGbind were selected. Residues in direct contact with the ligands were designated as the first-shell residues, and residues interacting with these residues, either via sidechain packing or polar contacts, were defined as the second-shell residues. Both first- and second-shell residues whose minimum interatomic distance to any ligand or substrate atom is ≤8 Å were retained during the ProteinMPNN-based sequence redesign. This strategy effectively constrained catalytically essential residues within the ligand-binding pocket. Residue-ligand and residue-residue interactions were identified through visual inspection and structural analysis using PyMOL and Chimera. This structure-based fixation strategy ensured that residues directly or indirectly supporting substrate binding and catalysis were retained, while allowing diversification of more distal residues during ProteinMPNN redesign.

Sequence-based fixation

Evolutionary conservation analysis was conducted using the ConSurf server38, which evaluates amino acid conservation through MSA and evolution-based algorithms. The sequence of the target protein was submitted to the ConSurf with the WAG evolutionary model. Subsequently, 300 sequence alignments were generated to assess site conservation. The ConSurf server assigns a conservation score to each amino acid site based on its relative evolutionary rate compared to the average rate across all sites in the protein, and categorizes these scores into grades ranging from 1 (highly variable) to 9 (highly conserved). Residues with scores ≥5 were designated as fixed sites, thereby preserving evolutionarily relatively conserved positions.

Sequence redesign with ProteinMPNN

ProteinMPNN was used to redesign the sequences of TOGT and UGT84A56. The code used in this work is available on the ProteinMPNN GitHub repository (commit 0a72127, June 9, 2022). During sequence generation, ProteinMPNN preserved the fixed sites, while allowing designable sites to mutate among 19 amino acids (excluding cysteine) to generate optimal sequences. Temperature sampling parameters 0.1 were used during redesign. A model of ProteinMPNN trained with 0.2 Å noise applied to training set protein backbones was used to perform sequence generation.

Sequence filtering

The generated sequences were filtered using a two-tier scoring: the global score and the weighted score. Sequences were ranked in ascending order by the global score, then in descending order by the weighted score. The top-ranked designs were selected as candidates for experimental testing. The global score derived from ProteinMPNN corresponds to the average negative log likelihood of the designed sequence given the target structure under the inverse-folding model32,39. Lower scores indicate better sequence-structure compatibility.

Experiments and simulation

Heterologous expression and purification

The recombinant plasmid pETduet1-UGTs were transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) for heterologous expression. The E. coli cells were cultured (5 mL) overnight in Luria-Bertani (LB) medium containing 100 μg/mL ampicillin at 37 °C with shaking (220 rpm). Subsequently, 1% seeding cultures were transferred into 100 mL LB-medium containing 100 μg/mL of ampicillin and grown at 37 °C and 220 rpm in 250 mL shake flasks. After the OD600 reached 0.6–0.8, the expression of recombinant UGTs were induced with 0.5 mM isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) at 16 °C, 220 rpm for 18 h. The cells were harvested by centrifugation at 6000 rpm for 2 min at 4 °C and then resuspended in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 300 mM sodium chloride, 10 mM imidazole, and 10% glycerol), the cells were disrupted by a high-pressure homogenizer (Union-Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Crude samples (cell lysate) were centrifuged at 6000 × g for 30 min. The supernatant was incubated with a Ni-NTA resin for 1 h at 4 °C. The non-specifically adsorbed proteins were removed by washing with 50 mL wash buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 300 mM NaCl and 50 mM imidazole at pH 8.0). Then, the His-tagged protein was eluted with elution buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 300 mM NaCl, and 300 mM imidazole at pH 8.0). The purified proteins were concentrated using a 10 kDa Amicon Ultra centrifuge tube (Millipore, Burlington, USA) and was then exchanged with HEPES buffer (10 mM, pH 8.0) by passing through the HiTrap desalting column. Before the enzyme reaction, the concentration of the purified target protein was quantified by the BCA Protein Assay Kit.

Protein expression quantification

Soluble protein concentration was quantified using a BCA Protein Assay Kit. Cells grown under identical conditions were harvested, lysed, and centrifuged. The target protein in the supernatant was purified as described above, and its concentration was determined by BCA assay and normalized to the initial culture volume. The distribution of target protein between soluble and insoluble fractions were further assessed by SDS-PAGE and Western blotting using anti-His-tag antibodies. Specifically, 1 mL of culture after overexpression of the recombinant strain was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 5 min and then resuspended in an appropriate amount of HEPES buffer (10 mM, pH 7.5) so that the OD600 values of each sample was the same. The cell solutions were disrupted by a JY98-IIIDN ultrasonic homogenizer sound arrest (Feiqi, Nanjing, China) on ice at 120 W for 1 min to obtain the protein. The soluble protein present in the supernatant and the inclusion body present in the precipitate were separated after centrifugation at 4 °C, 12,000 × g for 5 min. Protein fractions were analyzed by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) with 12% stacking gel and 5% separating gel and stained with Coomassie blue. To detect the expression of target proteins, Western blot was also performed. Briefly, proteins on gels not stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue were transferred onto a PVDF membrane (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) using a wet transfer method at 120 V for 100 min at 4 °C. After transfer, the membrane was blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk (Sigma-Aldrich) in 1× TBST (Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween 20) for 1 h at room temperature to prevent non-specific binding. The membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies specific to the target proteins: anti-His-tag (1:1500, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA). Following primary antibody incubation, the membrane was washed three times with TBST for 8 min each round. The membrane was then incubated for 1 h at room temperature with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:2000, Jackson Immuno Research, West Grove, PA, USA). After washing, protein bands were visualized using an ECL chemiluminescence detection kit (Pierce, Rockford, IL, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The chemiluminescent signal was detected using a ChemiDoc™ MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

Crude enzyme lysate assay

To determine the crude lysate catalytic activity of TOGT, UGT84A56, and their designs, 200 μL cell lysates heterologously expressing at 25 °C under the same conditions were incubated with 1 mM substrate in a 400 μL reaction system containing 2 mM UDPG and 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0 at 30 °C for 60 min. Then, the reaction was terminated with onefold volume of methanol. Samples were prepared by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min and filtered with polyethersulfone syringe filters (0.22 μm in pore size) and analyzed by HPLC as described below. Three parallel experiments were carried out.

Kinetic parameters measurement

The kinetic parameters were measured in 400 μL reaction mixtures containing 2 mM UDP-Glc, 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, a certain concentration of enzymes (0.5–1 μM), and different concentrations of substrate (10–500 μM) at 30 °C for 30 or 60 min. Michaelis–Menten constants (Km) and turnover numbers (kcat) were determined by fitting the initial steady-state velocity to the Michaelis–Menten equation.

Melting temperature determination

The protein melting temperature (Tm) was determined using Nano Differential Scanning Fluorimetry (NanoDSF) with a Prometheus NT.48 instrument (NanoTemper Technologies, Munich, Germany) following the manufacturer protocol. Briefly, purified proteins (at a concentration of 1–2 mg/mL) were dissolved in 10 mM HEPES (pH 8.0) to ensure their physiological relevance. The protein solutions were loaded into standard capillaries (NanoTemper Technologies), and the emission intensities at 330 and 350 nm were adjusted to ensure fluorescence readings above 2000 RFU. The samples were then heated from 20 to 95 °C at a rate of 1 °C/min. The intrinsic fluorescence of tryptophan residues was measured at both excitation wavelengths of 330 and 350 nm. The temperature-dependent shift in the fluorescence ratio (F350/F330) was used to determine the Tm, which serves as an indicator of protein thermal stability. For each protein sample, the experiment was performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

Culture media and conditions in feeding/de novo synthesis experiments

M9Y medium (20 g/L glycerol, 5 g/L yeast extract, 4 g/L NH4Cl, 6.78 g/L Na2HPO4, 3 g/L KH2PO4, 2 g/L MOPS, 0.5 g/L NaCl, 1 mM MgSO4, 0.1 mM CaCl2) was used in this study. For feeding experiments, 100 mg/L ampicillin was added to M9Y. In the de novo synthesis experiments, 10 μM FeSO4, 100 mg/L ampicillin, and 50 mg/L kanamycin were additionally supplemented. Plasmids were electroporated into the host strain, which was then incubated at 37 °C for 18 h. Single colonies were picked and grown overnight in LB medium with appropriate antibiotics. The overnight culture was sub cultured (1% inoculum) into 50 mL of the corresponding medium and grown at 37 °C until OD600 reached ~0.6. Protein expression was induced by adding 50 μL of 0.5 mM IPTG, and the culture was further incubated at 30 °C with shaking (220 rpm). Samples were collected at designated time points. OD600 was measured using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer, and product analysis was performed using HPLC.

Culture media and conditions in fed-batch experiments

Seed cultures of OD600 about 8–10 were transferred to a 3 L bioreactor containing 1 L of medium at 10% inoculum. The bioreactor fermentation medium contained, 6 g/L Na2HPO4, 3 g/L KH2PO4, 1 g/L (NH4)2SO4, 15 g/L yeast extract, 0.1 mM CaCl2, 1 mM MgSO4, 75 mg/L VB1, 100 mg/L ampicillin and 50 mg/L kanamycin, 1 mL/L TES. TES contained 0.15 g/L CuSO4·5H2O, 1 g/L ZnSO4·7H2O, 3 g/L FeSO4·7H2O, 1 g/L MnCl2·4H2O and 0.5 g/L CoCl2·6H2O. In this process, the initial glucose concentration was 25 g/L, and strains were cultured at 37 °C. When OD600 of the solution in the fermenter reached 15–20, 0.5 mM IPTG was added to induce protein expression. The pH was set at 7.0 by automatically adding ammonia to the flow and the dissolved oxygen was set at 30% by linkage of speed and dissolved oxygen. The feeding solution was 500 g/L glucose and the glucose concentration was maintained below 10 g/L. Data were reported as mean values from two independent experiments.

HPLC analysis

Target product identification and quantification were performed using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) with a Shimadzu HPLC system. All samples were analyzed by reverse-phase chromatography coupled with a UV detector. The separation was achieved using a ChromCore 120 C18 (5 μm, 4.6 × 250 mm) reverse-phase column. The mobile phase consisted of two components: solvent A, 0.2% trifluoroacetic acid, and solvent B, methanol. The column temperature was maintained at 40 °C, and the flow rate was set to 1 mL/min. A gradient elution program was employed as follows: 0–10 min, 5–35% solvent B; 10–20 min, 35–85% solvent B; 20–25 min, 85–5% solvent B. UV detection was carried out at 345 nm, where the absorption is primarily attributed to the conjugated aromatic ring systems and the phenolic hydroxyl groups (–OH) present in both compounds. A 1 mL sample was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane before injection into the HPLC system.

Molecular dynamics simulations

Molecular dynamics simulations were conducted using GROMACS (v2025.1)59 with the CHARMM36 force field60 (February 2026 release), compatible with the CGenFF 5.0 small-molecule parameter set61. Ligand parameters for UDP-glucose and esculetin were generated using the CGenFF online server (https://app.cgenff.com/). Initial protein-ligand complex structures were predicted using AlphaFold362. Each complex was solvated in a cubic TIP3P water box with a minimum distance of 1.0 nm between the protein and the box boundary. Counterions, including Na⁺ and Cl−, were added to neutralize the system. Energy minimization was first performed using the steepest descent algorithm for 10,000 steps, followed by conjugate gradient optimization with positional restraints applied to protein backbone atoms using a force constant of 100 kJ mol−1 nm−2. The system was then equilibrated in the NVT ensemble and gradually heated to 300 K over 100 ps using the V-rescale thermostat, followed by NPT equilibration at 1 bar for 100 ps using the Berendsen barostat. To obtain catalytically relevant Michaelis-like conformations, short restrained MD simulations of 1–10 ns were performed by applying distance restraints to key reactive atoms, guiding esculetin into near-attack conformations. Then, the production run was performed for 100 ns with a time step of 2 fs. Four independent trajectories were generated for each system. The resulting trajectories were analyzed using built-in GROMACS analysis utilities and custom scripts63 for trajectory processing, statistical analysis, and visualization.

Structural stability analysis

Structural stability was evaluated using backbone RMSD relative to the initial structure, and RMSF of backbone atoms. The radius of gyration (Rg) and solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) were calculated at both global and residue levels using MDTraj (v1.10.3)64. Analyses were performed on the 100 ns production trajectories, with frames extracted every 100 ps. Substrate access channels and substrate pockets were analyzed using the ProteinsPlus65 Structure-Based Modeling Support Server (https://proteins.plus/), with default parameters.

Hydrophobicity-solvent exposure coupling analysis

Residue hydrophobicity was quantified using the Kyte–Doolittle (KD) scale66, where positive values indicate hydrophobic residues and negative values indicate hydrophilic residues. For each mutation, the change in hydrophobicity (ΔKD) was calculated as the difference between the redesigned residue and the corresponding wild-type residue. Relative solvent-accessible surface area (rSASA) was computed as the ratio of the absolute SASA obtained from MD simulations to the theoretical maximum SASA (mSASA) for each residue type. Reference SASA values were taken from published datasets, corresponding to fully exposed residues67. The K-D hydrophobicity scores and mSASA values used for normalization are listed in Supplementary Table 12. The change in solvent exposure (ΔrSASA) was calculated by comparing redesigned variants to the wild-type enzyme. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ) was used to quantify the relationship between ΔKD and ΔrSASA. In addition, quadrant concordance was calculated to evaluate the consistency between hydrophobicity changes and solvent exposure trends.

Substrate binding configuration and MM-GBSA analysis

For TOGT systems, key reactive distances are defined to characterize substrate binding and catalytic configuration, including distances between His16-NE2 and esculetin-O7, esculetin-O7 and UDP-glucose C23, and His16-ND1 and Asp119 (OD1/OD2). These collective variables were used to assess esculetin positioning within the active site. Across all production trajectories, UDP-glucose remained stably bound within the enzyme, whereas esculetin rarely adopted near-attack conformations, defined by a hydrogen-bond distance (His16-NE2 to esculetin-O7) below ~3.5 Å. Therefore, for binding free energy calculations, esculetin was treated as the ligand, and the enzyme–UDP-glucose complex was treated as the receptor. Binding free energies were computed using the MM/GBSA method implemented in gmx_MMPBSA68. Default parameters were used for dielectric constants, ionic strength, and grid spacing as implemented in gmx_MMPBSA. Results from four independent trajectories were combined for each system. Per-residue energy decomposition was performed to evaluate the contribution of individual residues to binding. For each system (16 trajectories in total), the frequency of residue participation in favorable interactions and the average per-residue energy contributions were calculated. For UGT84A56 systems, analogous analyses were performed, with His19 serving as the catalytic residue. No suitable proximal acid/base residue analogous to Asp119 in TOGT was identified near the active site.

Statistics and reproducibility

All experiments were independently repeated at least three times unless otherwise stated. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (s.d.). Statistical analyses were performed using Welch’s t-test for comparisons between experimental groups, as indicated in the figure legends. Statistical significance was defined as follows: P ≤ 0.05 (*), P ≤ 0.01 (**), and P ≤ 0.001 (***). Enzyme kinetic parameters were determined by fitting the initial reaction velocities to the Michaelis–Menten equation using nonlinear regression analysis. For shake-flask experiments, biological triplicates (n = 3) were performed independently. For 3-L fed-batch fermentations, two independent experiments (n = 2) were conducted. Molecular dynamics simulations were performed using four independent trajectories for each system. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. No data were excluded from the analyses. The experiments were not randomized. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment.
