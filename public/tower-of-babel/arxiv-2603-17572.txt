Optimal Control for Steady Circulation of a Diffusion Process via Spectral Decomposition of Fokker–Planck Equation
Norihisa Namura, Hiroya Nakao

Abstract
We present a formulation of an optimal control problem for a two-dimensional diffusion process governed by a Fokker–Planck equation to achieve a nonequilibrium steady state with a desired circulation while accelerating convergence toward the stationary distribution. To achieve the control objective, we introduce costs for both the probability density function and flux rotation to the objective functional. We formulate the optimal control problem through dimensionality reduction of the Fokker–Planck equation via eigenfunction expansion, which requires a low-computational cost. We demonstrate that the proposed optimal control achieves the desired circulation while accelerating convergence to the stationary distribution through numerical simulations.



I
INTRODUCTION

In the real world, a wide range of systems are affected by stochastic noise.
Examples include nerve signal transmission by stochastically opening and closing ion channels
[
8
]
,
electronic circuits subjected to thermal fluctuations
[
11
]
,
semiclassically approximated quantum dissipative systems
[
13
]
,
and robots subjected to their sensor and actuator noise
[
7
]
.

Random fluctuations often appear as Brownian motion, where particles undergo irregular motion due to collisions with surrounding molecules.
The state dynamics of a Brownian particle is mathematically described by the Itô stochastic differential equation and
the time evolution of the probability density function (PDF) of the particles can be described by the corresponding Fokker–Planck equation (FPE)
[
19
]
.
Formulating control that acts on the FPE is useful
[
3
]

and numerous studies have formulated optimal control problems for FPEs
[
1
,
2
,
10
,
4
]
.

Optimal control is a general and valuable control approach that has found wide applications, including in the control of oscillatory systems
[
16
,
17
]
;
however, it is difficult to solve optimal control problems in infinite-dimensional systems.
Since FPE is linear, it can be analyzed using the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions
[
19
]
.
By using a spectral approach to the FPE, we can approximately formulate optimal control problems, such as for fast convergence to the stationary distribution
[
5
,
6
,
12
]
.

In thermodynamically equilibrium systems, detailed balance ensures that no flux exists in the stationary state, such as the circulation of the particles.
Introducing circulations, however, allows us to enhance efficiency of particle mixing
[
15
]
or capturing particles in target regions
[
15
,
20
]
.
Hence, generating circulations by driving the system in a nonequilibrium state can improve the performance and functional capabilities of the system, rather than merely achieving equilibrium state.

In this study, we formulate an optimal control problem for a two-dimensional diffusion process described by the FPE to achieve two control objectives:
(i) accelerating the convergence of the PDF toward a stationary state, and
(ii) generating a desired circulation when the PDF is in the stationary state.
In addition to the cost functional for accelerating convergence to the stationary distribution introduced in
[
12
]
,
a term that accounts for the difference from the desired flux rotation (scalar vorticity
[
9
]
) is added in the objective functional.
In the formulation, we utilize the eigenfunction expansion of the PDF with a finite number of eigenfunctions for dimensionality reduction,
which is expected to significantly reduce the computational cost during optimization.
Through numerical simulations, we demonstrate that the formulated optimal control problem allows the PDF to converge quickly to its stationary state, while simultaneously generating the desired circulation.

This study is organized as follows.
We first introduce a diffusion process and the corresponding FPE in Sec.
II
.
In Sec.
III
, we propose an optimal control scheme to accelerate convergence to the stationary distribution while generating a desired circulation based on the spectral dimensionality reduction of the FPE.
In Sec.
IV
, we demonstrate that the control objective is achieved by the optimal control compared to the uncontrolled case through numerical simulations.
We conclude this study in Sec.
V
.

II
FOKKER–PLANCK EQUATION

We consider a two-dimensional diffusion process
\{\bm{X}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
on a simply connected compact set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with a smooth boundary
\Gamma
,
where each state variable is independently subjected to white Gaussian noise.
This diffusion process can be described by the following Itô stochastic differential equation (SDE):

\displaystyle d\bm{X}_{t}=-\nabla V(\bm{X}_{t})dt+\sqrt{2D}d\bm{W}_{t},

(1)

where

t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}=[0,\infty)
represents the time,

V:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is a smooth potential function,

D>0
is a diffusion constant,

\nabla=[\partial_{x}~\partial_{y}]^{\top}
represents the gradient for the spatial variable
\bm{x}=\left[x~y\right]^{\top}\in\Omega
,

\partial_{x}=\partial/\partial x
represents the partial derivative with respect to
x
,

“
\top
”
represents the transposition of a vector or matrix,
and
\{\bm{W}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
represents a standard two-dimensional Brownian motion.
The time evolution of the probability density function (PDF)
\rho:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
of
\bm{X}_{t}
is described by the Fokker–Planck equation (FPE):

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho(\bm{x},t)=\mathscr{L}^{*}\rho=\nabla\cdot(\rho(\bm{x},t)\nabla V(\bm{x}))+D\Delta\rho(\bm{x},t)

(2)

by using a linear operator
\mathscr{L}^{*}=\nabla\cdot\nabla V+D\Delta
,
where
\bm{a}\cdot\bm{b}=a_{1}b_{1}+a_{2}b_{2}
is the scalar product of two-dimensional vectors
\bm{a}
,
\bm{b}

and
\Delta=\nabla\cdot\nabla
is the Laplacian operator.
We then define a probability flux
\bm{J}:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
to satisfy

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}^{*}\rho(\bm{x},t)=-\nabla\cdot\bm{J}(\bm{x},t).

(3)

For the flux
\bm{J}
, we consider the reflecting boundary condition on the boundary
\Gamma
at any
t
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\bm{J}(\bm{x},t)\cdot\bm{n}=0\quad\mathrm{on}\quad\Gamma\times\mathbb{R}_{+},

(4)

where
\bm{n}
represents the normal vector perpendicular to
\Gamma
.

In equilibrium systems,
the stationary flux
\bm{J}_{\mathrm{s}}

satisfies the detailed balance condition
\bm{J}_{\mathrm{s}}(\bm{x})=\bm{0}
for any
\bm{x}\in\Omega

when the PDF is stationary,
\rho(\bm{x},t)=\rho_{\mathrm{s}}(\bm{x})
.
In this case, the linear operator
\mathscr{L}^{*}
is self-adjoint with respect to the weighted square-integrable function space
L^{2}\left(\Omega;\rho_{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\right)

[
18
,
6
,
12
]
.
The stationary distribution is given by

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{s}}(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{Z}\exp\left(-\frac{V(\bm{x})}{D}\right),

(5)

where
Z=\int_{\Omega}\exp\left(-\frac{V(\bm{x})}{D}\right)d\bm{x}
is a normalization constant and
d\bm{x}=dxdy
is the integration measure.

We can consider the set of the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions
\{(\lambda_{m},v_{m})\},\;(m=0,1,2,\dots)
of
\mathscr{L}^{*}
that satisfies

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}^{*}v_{m}=\lambda_{m}v_{m},

(6)

where
\{\lambda_{m}\}
are sorted as
\real~\lambda_{0}\geq\real~\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots
.
The eigenfunctions
\{v_{m}\}
can be orthonormalized on
L^{2}\left(\Omega;\rho_{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\right)
as follows:

\displaystyle\left\langle v_{m},v_{k}\right\rangle_{L^{2}\left(\Omega;\rho_{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\right)}=\delta_{mk},

(7)

where
\left\langle p,q\right\rangle_{L^{2}\left(\Omega;\rho_{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\right)}=\int p(\bm{x})\overline{q(\bm{x})}\rho_{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}(\bm{x})d\bm{x}
is the inner product of two functions
p,q
on
L^{2}\left(\Omega;\rho_{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\right)
,
the overline represents the complex conjugate,
and
\delta_{mk}
is the Kronecker delta.
Since the stationary distribution satisfies
\mathscr{L}^{*}\rho_{\mathrm{s}}=0
,
\rho_{\mathrm{s}}
is the eigenfunction that corresponds to the zero eigenvalue,
i.e.,
\lambda_{0}=0
and
v_{0}=\rho_{\mathrm{s}}
.

III
FORMULATION OF OPTIMAL CONTROL

III-A

Control Objective

Our control objective is to accelerate the convergence speed of the PDF toward the stationary state and generate a desired circulation from the initial time
t_{0}\geq 0
until the final time
t_{\mathrm{f}}>t_{0}
.
For this purpose, we consider a diffusion process with control
\{\bm{X}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
described by the following Itô SDE (
8
) whose drift term receives the control inputs
u_{1,2}\in L^{2}([t_{0},t_{\mathrm{f}}])
with control shape functions
[
6
]

\alpha
and
\phi
:

\displaystyle d\bm{X}_{t}={}

\displaystyle\left(-u_{1}(t)\nabla\alpha(\bm{X}_{t})-u_{2}(t)\frac{1}{\rho_{\mathrm{s}}(\bm{X}_{t})}\nabla^{\bot}\phi(\bm{X}_{t})\right)dt

(8)

\displaystyle-\nabla V(\bm{X}_{t})dt+\sqrt{2D}dW_{t},

(9)

where
\nabla^{\bot}=[\partial_{y}~-\partial_{x}]
is a partial derivative operator that is perpendicular to
\nabla
.
The term with
u_{1}
mainly serves to accelerate the convergence, whereas the term with
u_{2}
generates a circulation in the steady state.
Note that the control inputs
u_{1,2}
depend only on time and regulate the intensity of the smooth control shape functions
\alpha:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
and
\phi:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, respectively.
We assume that the controlled SDE (
8
) admits a unique solution.

The FPE corresponding to the SDE (
8
) is given by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho={}

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\left(\rho\left(\nabla V+u_{1}\nabla\alpha+u_{2}\frac{1}{\rho_{\mathrm{s}}}\nabla^{\bot}\phi\right)\right)+D\Delta\rho

(10)

and the probability flux
\tilde{\bm{J}}
under the control is described by

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{J}}=-\rho\left(\nabla V+u_{1}\nabla\alpha+u_{2}\frac{1}{\rho_{\mathrm{s}}}\nabla^{\bot}\phi\right)-D\nabla\rho.

(11)

Here, we assume that the control shape functions satisfy the following boundary conditions:

\displaystyle\nabla\alpha\cdot\bm{n}

\displaystyle=0\quad\mathrm{on}\quad\Gamma,

(12)

\displaystyle\nabla^{\bot}\phi\cdot\bm{n}

\displaystyle=0\quad\mathrm{on}\quad\Gamma,

(13)

in order that
\tilde{\bm{J}}
satisfies the reflecting boundary condition even under the control, i.e.,

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{J}}(\bm{x},t)\cdot\bm{n}=0\quad\mathrm{on}\quad\Gamma\times\mathbb{R}_{+}.

(14)

We assume the well-posedness of the controlled FPE (
10
) with the boundary condition (
14
).

The control objective includes fast convergence to the stationary distribution
\rho_{\mathrm{s}}
of the uncontrolled system.
Here, we consider that the system to have converged to the stationary state when
\rho
is sufficiently close to
\rho_{\mathrm{s}}
within a finite time.
The stationary flux
\tilde{\bm{J}}_{\mathrm{s}}
in the stationary state with
\rho=\rho_{\mathrm{s}}
should satisfy the following condition for all
\bm{x}\in\Omega
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\tilde{\bm{J}}_{\mathrm{s}}(\bm{x})=0.

(15)

For this condition to hold, it is sufficient that the following two equations are satisfied:

\displaystyle u_{1}\nabla\cdot\left(\rho_{\mathrm{s}}\nabla\alpha\right)

\displaystyle=0,

(16)

\displaystyle u_{2}\nabla\cdot\left(\nabla^{\bot}\phi\right)

\displaystyle=0.

(17)

In general,
\nabla\cdot(\rho_{\mathrm{s}}\nabla\alpha)
does not vanish
but it is sufficient that the condition (
16
) is satisfied when
u_{1}=0
,
because
u_{1}
becomes practically zero after the sufficient convergence of
\rho
to
\rho_{\mathrm{s}}
.
Meanwhile, the condition (
17
) is always satisfied because
\nabla\cdot\left(\nabla^{\bot}\phi\right)=0
holds for any smooth function
\phi
:
Therefore, when
u_{1}=0
,
\rho_{\mathrm{s}}
is still a stationary distribution of the FPE (
10
) even for
u_{2}\neq 0
.

To generate a desired circulation, we control the system so that the flux rotation
\omega(\bm{x},t)=\nabla\times\tilde{\bm{J}}(\bm{x},t)
reaches a desired flux rotation
\omega_{\mathrm{d}}(\bm{x})
in the steady state with
\rho=\rho_{\mathrm{s}}
.
The term
\nabla\times\tilde{\bm{J}}(\bm{x},t)
yields a scalar quantity in two dimensions and satisfies
\nabla\times\tilde{\bm{J}}(\bm{x},t)=-\nabla^{\bot}\cdot\tilde{\bm{J}}(\bm{x},t)
.
Here, the flux rotation satisfies
\int_{\Omega}\omega(\bm{x},t)d\bm{x}=0
at any
t
and we further assume that
\omega(\cdot,t)\in L^{2}\left(\Omega;\rho_{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\right)
.
Since we can assume
u_{1}=0
when
\rho=\rho_{\mathrm{s}}
,
the stationary flux for a constant
u_{2}
satisfies
\tilde{\bm{J}}_{\mathrm{s}}=-u_{2}\nabla^{\bot}\phi
and
the stationary flux rotation satisfies
\omega_{\mathrm{s}}=\nabla\times\tilde{\bm{J}}_{\mathrm{s}}=u_{2}\nabla^{\bot}\cdot\nabla^{\bot}\phi=u_{2}\Delta\phi
, whose shape depends only on
\phi
.
Thus, we can achieve any smooth desired flux rotation
\omega_{\mathrm{d}}=\Delta\phi
by appropriately designing the shape function
\phi
.
In this case, the control objective can be set as
u_{2}=1
.

In this study, we assume the controllability of the system to the nonequilibrium steady state
characterized by the stationary distribution
\rho_{\mathrm{s}}
and the desired flux rotation
\omega_{\mathrm{d}}
.
We note that the detailed balance condition does not hold in the steady state under the control,
but we can discuss the controlled FPE (
10
) using the fact that the detailed balance condition
\bm{J}_{\mathrm{s}}=\bm{0}
holds in the original uncontrolled system.

III-B

Spectral Dimensionality Reduction

We consider the eigenfunction expansion of the PDF
\rho
using the eigenfunctions
\{v_{m}\}
of the linear operator
\mathscr{L}^{*}
as

\displaystyle\rho(\bm{x},t)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}c_{m}(t)v_{m}(\bm{x}),

(18)

where each coefficient
c_{m}
can be obtained by

\displaystyle c_{m}(t)=\left\langle\rho(\cdot,t),v_{m}\right\rangle_{L^{2}\left(\Omega;\rho_{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\right)}.

(19)

By defining
\zeta_{1,2}
as

\displaystyle\zeta_{1}(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot(\rho(\bm{x},t)\nabla\alpha(\bm{x})),

(20)

\displaystyle\zeta_{2}(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot\left(\rho(\bm{x},t)\frac{1}{\rho_{\mathrm{s}}(\bm{x})}\nabla^{\bot}\phi(\bm{x})\right),

(21)

the controlled FPE (
10
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho={}

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}^{*}\rho+u_{1}\zeta_{1}+u_{2}\zeta_{2}

(22)

\displaystyle\coloneqq{}

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\{\rho,u_{1},u_{2}\},

(23)

where
\mathcal{F}
describes the dynamics of the controlled FPE.
The dynamics of each coefficient
c_{m}
can then be described by the following ordinary differential equation (ODE):

\displaystyle\dot{c}_{m}={}

\displaystyle\lambda_{m}c_{m}+u_{1}\left\langle\zeta_{1}(\cdot,t),v_{m}\right\rangle_{L^{2}\left(\Omega;\rho_{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\right)}

(24)

\displaystyle+u_{2}\left\langle\zeta_{2}(\cdot,t),v_{m}\right\rangle_{L^{2}\left(\Omega;\rho_{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\right)}

(25)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\lambda_{m}c_{m}+u_{1}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}\left\langle\nabla\cdot(v_{k}\nabla\alpha),v_{m}\right\rangle_{L^{2}\left(\Omega;\rho_{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\right)}

(26)

\displaystyle+u_{2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}\left\langle\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{v_{k}}{\rho_{\mathrm{s}}}\nabla^{\bot}\phi\right),v_{m}\right\rangle_{L^{2}\left(\Omega;\rho_{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\right)},~~~

(27)

where
\dot{c}_{m}
represents the time derivative of
c_{m}
.

Hereafter, we consider a finite set of
M
eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{m}\}_{m=0}^{M-1}
and corresponding eigenfunctions
\{v_{m}\}_{m=0}^{M-1}

for the purpose of dimensionality reduction.
We define a coefficient vector and an eigenvalue matrix by

\displaystyle\bm{c}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}c_{0}&c_{1}&\cdots&c_{M-1}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{M},

(28)

\displaystyle\bm{\Lambda}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{M-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M},

(29)

respectively, and introduce matrices
\bm{B}_{1,2}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
,
where the
(m,k)
-components
B_{1,2}^{(mk)}
of the matrices are given by

\displaystyle B_{1}^{(mk)}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\nabla\cdot(v_{k}\nabla\alpha),v_{m}\right\rangle_{L^{2}\left(\Omega;\rho_{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\right)},

(30)

\displaystyle B_{2}^{(mk)}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{v_{k}}{\rho_{\mathrm{s}}}\nabla^{\bot}\phi\right)\!,v_{m}\right\rangle_{L^{2}\left(\Omega;\rho_{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\right)},

(31)

respectively.
Finally, the controlled FPE (
10
) can be approximated by the following ODE:

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{c}}={}

\displaystyle\bm{\Lambda}\bm{c}+u_{1}\bm{B}_{1}\bm{c}+u_{2}\bm{B}_{2}\bm{c}

(32)

\displaystyle\coloneqq{}

\displaystyle\bm{f}(\bm{c},u_{1},u_{2}).

(33)

The ODE (
32
) driven by the vector field
\bm{f}
is a bilinear system with the state
\bm{c}
and control inputs
u_{1,2}
.

III-C

Optimization

To achieve the control objective, we introduce a stage cost functional
\mathcal{L}_{1}
for the PDF, which is described using a weight
Q_{1}>0
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{1}[\rho]={}

\displaystyle\frac{Q_{1}}{2}\left|\rho(\cdot,t)-\rho_{\mathrm{s}}\right|_{L^{2}\left(\Omega;\rho_{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\right)}^{2},

(34)

where
\left|\cdot\right|_{L^{2}\left(\Omega;\rho_{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\right)}
represents the
L^{2}\left(\Omega;\rho_{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\right)
norm.
In addition, we introduce a stage cost functional
\mathcal{L}_{2}
for the flux rotation, which is described using a weight
Q_{2}>0
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{2}[\omega]={}

\displaystyle\frac{Q_{2}}{2}\left|\omega(\cdot,t)-\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\right|_{L^{2}\left(\Omega;\rho_{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\right)}^{2}

(35)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{Q_{2}}{2}\left|\nabla\times\tilde{\bm{J}}(\cdot,t)-\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\right|_{L^{2}\left(\Omega;\rho_{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\right)}^{2}.

(36)

We also introduce a terminal cost functional
\varphi
for the PDF and flux rotation using a similar formulation as

\displaystyle\varphi[\rho(\cdot,t_{\mathrm{f}}),\omega(\cdot,t_{\mathrm{f}})]={}

\displaystyle\frac{Q_{\mathrm{f}}}{2}\left|\rho(\cdot,t_{\mathrm{f}})-\rho_{\mathrm{s}}\right|_{L^{2}\left(\Omega;\rho_{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\right)}^{2}

(37)

\displaystyle+\frac{R_{\mathrm{f}}}{2}\left|\omega(\cdot,t_{\mathrm{f}})-\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\right|_{L^{2}\left(\Omega;\rho_{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\right)}^{2}.~~~

(38)

Finally, we introduce a stage cost function
\mathcal{L}_{3}
for the inputs
u_{1,2}
using weights
R_{1,2}>0
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{3}(u_{1}(t),u_{2}(t))=\frac{1}{2}R_{1}u_{1}(t)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}R_{2}u_{2}(t)^{2}.

(39)

Hence, the objective functional
\mathcal{J}
is represented as

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}[u_{1},u_{2}]={}

\displaystyle\varphi[\rho(\cdot,t_{\mathrm{f}}),\omega(\cdot,t_{\mathrm{f}})]

(40)

\displaystyle+\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{\mathrm{f}}}\mathcal{L}_{1}[\rho]+\mathcal{L}_{2}[\omega]+\mathcal{L}_{3}(u_{1},u_{2})dt.~~

(41)

The optimal control problem is formulated as

\displaystyle\min_{u_{1},u_{2}}

\displaystyle\quad\mathcal{J}[u_{1},u_{2}]

(42)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}

\displaystyle\quad\partial_{t}\rho=\mathcal{F}\{\rho,u_{1},u_{2}\},

(43)

\displaystyle\quad\rho(t_{0})=\rho_{0},

(44)

where
\rho_{0}
is the initial PDF at
t_{0}
.
The proposed optimal control problem satisfies the standard conditions ensuring the existence of an optimal control, namely coercivity and weak lower semicontinuity of the cost functional together with continuity of the control-to-state mapping
[
14
,
12
]
.

Next, we reformulate the objective functional by using the eigenfunction expansion (
18
) of the PDF
\rho
.
The stage cost for the PDF can be approximated by a function
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{1}
of the coefficient vector
\bm{c}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{1}[\rho]

(45)

\displaystyle\approx{}

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{1}(\bm{c})

(46)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{Q_{1}}{2}\left\langle\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\left(c_{m}-c_{\mathrm{s}}^{(m)}\right)v_{m},\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}\left(c_{k}-c_{\mathrm{s}}^{(k)}\right)v_{k}\right\rangle_{L^{2}\left(\Omega;\rho_{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\right)}

(47)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{Q_{1}}{2}\left\|\bm{c}-\bm{c}_{\mathrm{s}}\right\|^{2},

(48)

where the
m
-th component of the vector
\bm{c}_{\mathrm{s}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
is obtained as
c_{\mathrm{s}}^{(m)}=\left\langle\rho_{\mathrm{s}},v_{m}\right\rangle_{L^{2}\left(\Omega;\rho_{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\right)}
and

\left\|\cdot\right\|
represents the Euclid norm of a
M
-dimensional vector.
The stage cost for the flux rotation can also be approximated by a function
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{2}
of
\bm{c}
and
u_{1,2}
by calculating the flux
\tilde{\bm{J}}
using the eigenfunction expansion (
18
), which can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{2}[\omega]\approx{}

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{2}(\bm{c},u_{1},u_{2})

(49)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{Q_{2}}{2}\left\|(\bm{A}_{1}+u_{1}(t)\bm{A}_{2}+u_{2}(t)\bm{A}_{3})\bm{c}-\bm{d}\right\|^{2}.~

(50)

Here, each component of the matrices
\bm{A}_{1,2,3}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
and the vector
\bm{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
is represented as

\displaystyle A_{1}^{(mk)}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\nabla^{\bot}v_{m}\cdot\nabla V,v_{k}\right\rangle_{L^{2}\left(\Omega;\rho_{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\right)},

(51)

\displaystyle A_{2}^{(mk)}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\nabla^{\bot}v_{m}\cdot\nabla\alpha,v_{k}\right\rangle_{L^{2}\left(\Omega;\rho_{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\right)},

(52)

\displaystyle A_{3}^{(mk)}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\nabla^{\bot}\frac{v_{m}}{\rho_{\mathrm{s}}}\cdot\nabla^{\bot}\phi+\frac{v_{m}}{\rho_{\mathrm{s}}}\Delta\phi,v_{k}\right\rangle_{L^{2}\left(\Omega;\rho_{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\right)},

(53)

\displaystyle d^{(k)}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\omega_{\mathrm{d}},v_{k}\right\rangle_{L^{2}\left(\Omega;\rho_{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\right)},

(54)

respectively, because the flux
\tilde{\bm{J}}
can be approximated as

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{J}}\approx{}

\displaystyle-\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}c_{m}v_{m}\left(\nabla V+u_{1}\nabla\alpha+u_{2}\frac{1}{\rho_{\mathrm{s}}}\nabla^{\bot}\phi\right)

(55)

\displaystyle-D\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}c_{m}\nabla v_{m}.

(56)

Since
u_{2}=1
is the control objective for the flux rotation in the stationary state, the terminal cost can be approximated as

\displaystyle\varphi[\rho(\cdot,t_{\mathrm{f}}),\omega(\cdot,t_{\mathrm{f}})]

(57)

\displaystyle\approx{}

\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}(\bm{c}(t_{\mathrm{f}}),u_{2}(t_{\mathrm{f}}))

(58)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{Q_{\mathrm{f}}}{2}\left\|\bm{c}(t_{\mathrm{f}})-\bm{c}_{\mathrm{s}}\right\|^{2}+\frac{R_{\mathrm{f}}}{2}(u_{2}(t_{\mathrm{f}})-1)^{2}.

(59)

Hence, the objective functional can be approximated as

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}[u_{1},u_{2}]

(60)

\displaystyle\approx{}

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{J}}[u_{1},u_{2}]

(61)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}(\bm{c}(t_{\mathrm{f}}),u_{2}(t_{\mathrm{f}}))

(62)

\displaystyle+\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{\mathrm{f}}}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{1}(\bm{c})+\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{2}(\bm{c},u_{1},u_{2})+\mathcal{L}_{3}(u_{1},u_{2})dt.

(63)

The objective functional includes the product of
\bm{c}
and
u_{1,2}
but is still coercive.

Thus, the low-dimensional optimal control problem can be formulated with a Lagrange multiplier vector
\bm{\mu}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
as

Theorem 1

In the

).

Proof:

Since the eigenfunction expansion of

V
CONCLUSIONS

In this study, we formulated an optimal control problem for a diffusion process described by a two-dimensional Itô SDE to achieve a desired circulation while accelerating convergence to the stationary distribution of the original circulation-free Fokker–Planck equation.
By the formulation of the optimal control problem based on dimensionality reduction via the eigenfunction expansion of the probability density function,
we demonstrated that the control objective can be achieved through numerical simulations.
Although the desired flux rotation can also be achieved while ensuring convergence to the stationary distribution by the trivial choice of the control inputs,
u_{1}(t)=0
and
u_{2}(t)=1
,
it is worthwhile to solve the optimal control problem
because a different and more efficient optimal solution (Fig.
3
) was obtained when the trivial choice was used as the initial guess in the numerical example.
A future work would be to formulate optimal control problems for the similar control objective in three or higher-dimensional cases and
investigate a nonlinear FPE under particle interactions.
