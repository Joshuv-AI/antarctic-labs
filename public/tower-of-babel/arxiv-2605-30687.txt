Fast approximate Bayesian multidimensional scaling with consistency guarantees
Ami Sheth amisheth26@g.ucla.edu, Aaron Smith smith.aaron.matthew@gmail.com, Andrew J. Holbrook aholbroo@g.ucla.edu

Abstract
Bayesian multidimensional scaling (BMDS) embeds n n objects in a low-dimensional space to approximately preserve an observed dissimilarity matrix. Compared to classic MDS, BMDS is more robust to model misspecification and supports posterior uncertainty quantification and joint estimation within hierarchical models. However, standard BMDS inference is computationally prohibitive, requiring O ⁡ ( n 2 ) O(n^{2}) operations per MCMC iteration to evaluate the likelihood. We propose Barnes–Hut BMDS (BH-BMDS), which uses a tree-based approximation to the likelihood and a Gibbs sampler that leverages this structure, remaining compatible with hierarchical extensions. BH-BMDS reduces computational complexity to O ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ n ) O(n\log n) while preserving the geometric fidelity of the embedding. We further establish consistency for the stationary measure of BH-BMDS, proving that it concentrates around the true latent configuration even as the total error of the surrogate likelihood diverges. Notably, this consistency holds in the infinite-dimensional limit. We evaluate the approximation on datasets with diverse structure, including air traffic networks, arXiv abstracts, MNIST images and neural activity recordings from mouse models of tau pathology. Across all settings, BH-BMDS closely matches BMDS while achieving substantial computational gains, with approximately 10-fold speedups at n = 1,000 n=1{,}000 and 70-fold speedups at n = 10,000 n=10{,}000 . These gains increase with n n , demonstrating strong empirical scalability.

Introduction

Dissimilarity data arise in many domains, including psychology, physics and biology, where a goal is to quantify how pairs of complex or high-dimensional objects differ from one another. A common approach is to embed objects into a low-dimensional space, enabling visualization and downstream modeling while preserving the observed dissimilarities. Bayesian multidimensional scaling (BMDS) provides a probabilistic framework for this task, mapping
n
objects to latent coordinates in a low-dimensional Euclidean space while modeling observed dissimilarities through a likelihood-based formulation. Unlike classical approaches such as PCA
(
19
;
9
)
and MDS
(
28
)
, or more recent approaches such as t-SNE
(
29
)
and UMAP
(
12
)
, BMDS enables posterior uncertainty quantification over latent embeddings and supports integration into hierarchical models. These properties have made BMDS a valuable tool in applications such as viral phylogeography
(
4
;
7
;
10
)
, incorporation of auxiliary information
(
11
)
and clustering
(
17
;
25
)
.

Despite these advantages, BMDS is computationally constrained by the
O(n^{2})
cost of likelihood evaluation, which requires summing over all pairwise distances (Equation
2
). As
n
grows, this quadratic scaling becomes prohibitive, restricting BMDS inference to relatively small datasets. Existing approximation methods
(
25
)
lack rigorous posterior consistency guarantees for two or more dimensional latent spaces, raising concerns about their practical reliability. To address these limitations, we propose an approximation to BMDS based on the Barnes–Hut algorithm (BH-BMDS). This framework scales as
O(n\log n)
, preserves the original Bayesian formulation and guarantees the consistency for the stationary measure under the approximation and its stochastic realizations.

Related Work

Several approaches attempt to mitigate the cost of BMDS.
4
adopt a misspecified Gaussian likelihood to bypass the computational bottleneck imposed by non-negativity constraints; however, this assumption places probability mass outside the support of the observed data.
7
leverage multi-core central processing units, vectorization and graphic processing units to significantly reduce runtime but depend on specialized hardware for scalability. Both methods require
O(n^{2})
floating point operations. To achieve sub-quadratic scaling,
25
induce sparsity in the dissimilarity matrix, achieving
O(mn)
complexity, where
m\ll n
represents the number of landmarks. Although this approach yields substantial speedups,
m
must scale with
n
to maintain accuracy, and its selection introduces an additional tuning parameter. Moreover, they do not provide posterior consistency guarantees for subsampled dissimilarity matrices in dimensions
d>1
.

Tree-based methods offer an alternative strategy for approximating pairwise interactions. For instance,
30
use the Barnes–Hut algorithm to accelerate t-SNE by approximating long-range interactions in an
n
-body system. While this scales as
O(n\log n)
, the paper lacks formal accuracy bounds for the approximation error. Noting that the BMDS likelihood admits a similar
n
-body interpretation, we adapt the Barnes–Hut algorithm to the BMDS setting (BH-BMDS), enabling
O(n\log n)
likelihood approximations within an MCMC framework. We further establish consistency under uniform vanishing pairwise loss error for BH-BMDS, proving the stationary measure concentrates on the true configuration despite errors introduced by the approximation and noisy MCMC updates.

3
Preliminaries

3.1
Bayesian multidimensional scaling

Suppose we have high-dimensional observations, e.g.,
\mathbf{y}_{1},\dots\mathbf{y}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega}
, and let
D_{ij}=\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{y}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{j})
denote the dissimilarity between observations. Bayesian multidimensional scaling (BMDS)
(
16
)
assumes that the observed dissimilarities follow

\displaystyle D_{ij}\;=\;\big[\,r_{ij}+\sigma Z_{ij}\,\big]_{+},\qquad r_{ij}=\left\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\right\|,\qquad Z_{ij}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}N(0,1),

(1)

independent across pairs and conditioned on the low-dimensional latent locations
\mathbf{X}=\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}^{n}_{i=1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Here,
[t]_{+}=\max\{t,0\}
represents truncation at zero, and the BMDS variance
\sigma^{2}
represents the level of noise distorting the latent distances. This yields the log-likelihood

\displaystyle\ell(\mathbf{D}\mid\mathbf{X},\sigma^{2})=-\frac{N}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^{2})-\sum_{i<j}\left[\frac{(D_{ij}-r_{ij})^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}+\log\Phi\left(\frac{r_{ij}}{\sigma}\right)\right],

(2)

where
\mathbf{D}=\{D_{ij}\}
is the observed symmetric
n\times n
dissimilarity matrix,
N=\binom{n}{2}
, and
\Phi(\cdot)
is the standard normal CDF. Because the likelihood involves a summation over all unordered pairs, its evaluation scales as
O(n^{2})
. Consequently, we seek to reduce this computational burden using tree-based approximation methods, such as Barnes–Hut algorithms, that scale as
O(n\log n)
.

3.2
Barnes–Hut algorithm

Figure 1
:
Barnes–Hut structural components. The left figure is the spatial representation of a recursive subdivision of the embedding space into four quadrants while the right figure is the respective quadtree data structure. One builds the quadtree using Algorithm
2
.

The gravitational
n
-body problem involves computing the gravitational forces exerted by all pairs of particles on one another. Direct evaluation of these forces requires
O(n^{2})
computations which becomes prohibitive for large datasets. To reduce this cost,
3
propose a hierarchical approximation algorithm that estimates the influence of distant particles as a single aggregate interaction.

To organize groups of particles efficiently, the Barnes–Hut algorithm uses a tree data structure, e.g., a quadtree
\mathcal{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
(Figure
1
). A quadtree
\mathcal{T}
is built by recursively subdividing the embedding space into four quadrants based on particle locations (Algorithm
2
). A cell refers to a fixed spatial region, and a node corresponds to a cell in the tree. Each leaf node contains at most one particle, while internal nodes store summary statistics of all particles within the cell as a pseudo-particle. As particles are inserted, these summaries are updated recursively. The quadtree
\mathcal{T}
is constructed in
O(n\log n)
time and enables efficient spatial queries and interaction approximations.

To compute interactions, the quadtree
\mathcal{T}
is traversed starting from the root node. At each node
k\in\{0,\dots,K\}
where the total number of nodes
K=O(n)
, we consider its corresponding cell and pseudo-particle. Let
w_{k}
be the width of this cell,
\bar{r}_{ik}
the Euclidean distance between the target particle
\mathbf{x}_{i}
and the pseudo-particle’s center of mass, and
\rho_{ik}=w_{k}/\bar{r}_{ik}
. If
\rho_{ik}<\theta
, where
\theta
is a user-specified threshold, the pseudo-particle is considered distant and used as an approximation of its constituent particles. Otherwise, we recursively examine the node’s children; interactions with particles in leaf nodes are computed directly. The parameter
\theta
controls the trade-off between accuracy and computational efficiency; larger values of
\theta
result in fewer tree traversals and faster computation at the cost of reduced accuracy.
\theta\geq 0.5
typically yields
O(n\log n)
, while
\theta=0
recovers the exact
O(n^{2})
computation.

4
A tree-based framework for Bayesian multidimensional scaling

4.1
Barnes–Hut Bayesian multidimensional scaling

Given latent embeddings
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}^{n}_{i=1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we construct a quadtree
\mathcal{T}
(Algorithm
2
) where each node
k
corresponds to a spatial cell
C_{k}
in the latent space with index set
\mathcal{I}_{k}=\{i\in\{1,\dots,n\}:\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathcal{C}_{k}\}
. While leaf nodes contain single observations, internal nodes store pseudo-particles summarizing the number of points (
N_{k}=|\mathcal{I}_{k}|
) and the centers of mass of the high-dimensional (
\bar{\mathbf{y}}_{k}=\frac{1}{N_{k}}\sum_{l\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\mathbf{y}_{l}
) and low-dimensional (
\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{k}=\frac{1}{N_{k}}\sum_{l\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\mathbf{x}_{l}
) points within
\mathcal{C}_{k}
. The root node (
k=0
) summarizes the entire dataset.

Unlike t-SNE
(
30
)
, BMDS (Equation
2
) depends on squared differences between high-dimensional and low-dimensional dissimilarities. To preserve the
O(n\log n)
complexity, we approximate the high-dimensional dissimilarity
\bar{D}_{ik}=\lVert\mathbf{y}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{y}}_{k}\rVert
using the Euclidean distance between the observed point
\mathbf{y}_{i}
and the pseudo-particle’s high-dimensional center of mass
\bar{\mathbf{y}}_{k}
. This choice avoids storing or computing all pairwise dissimilarities which would require
O(n^{2})
memory or operations, at the cost of introducing approximation error.

During likelihood computations, we perform a depth-first traversal of quadtree
\mathcal{T}
for each point
\mathbf{x}_{i}
and compute

\bar{r}_{ik}=\lVert\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\rVert,

the Euclidean distance between the target point
\mathbf{x}_{i}
and the pseudo-particle’s low-dimensional center of mass
\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{k}
. This quantity is used within the Barnes–Hut criterion to determine tree traversal. The Barnes–Hut BMDS (BH-BMDS) framework reduces the computational burden of evaluating the BMDS log-likelihood through the use of summary statistics; dissimilarities between a target point and points within a sufficiently distant cell can be represented by their aggregate summary.

Because each unordered pairwise interaction is visited twice (one per target point), the normalization term is adjusted by a factor of two. The BH-BMDS log-likelihood is

\displaystyle\tilde{\ell}(\mathbf{D}\mid\mathbf{X},\sigma^{2},\mathcal{T})

\displaystyle=-N\log(2\pi\sigma^{2})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{B}_{i}}N_{k}\biggl[\frac{(\bar{D}_{ik}-\bar{r}_{ik})^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}+\log\Phi\!\left(\frac{\bar{r}_{ik}}{\sigma}\right)\biggr],

\displaystyle\equiv-N\log(2\pi\sigma^{2})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{\ell}(\mathbf{x}_{i}\mid\mathcal{T}),

(3)

where
\mathcal{B}_{i}
denotes the set of nodes satisfying the Barnes–Hut acceptance criterion (or leaf nodes) for target point
\mathbf{x}_{i}
, and
\tilde{\ell}(\mathbf{x}_{i}\mid\mathcal{T})
is the pointwise contribution to the BH-BMDS log-likelihood from a Barnes–Hut traversal of
\mathbf{x}_{i}
starting at the root node (Algorithm
4
). The BH-BMDS log-likelihood approximates the full BMDS log-likelihood, and when
\theta=0
, it reduces to the exact formulation since
\mathcal{B}_{i}
contains only leaf nodes.

4.2
Noisy Barnes–Hut algorithm

Under the Barnes–Hut approximation, the BMDS log-likelihood becomes a piecewise function of the latent positions due to the discrete tree structure. For a fixed set of points, small changes in the embeddings can alter traversal paths or cell assignments, leading to discontinuous changes in the BH-BMDS log-likelihood. Therefore, we introduce a probabilistic acceptance rule to smooth the transition of accepting pseudo-particles near the threshold
\theta
, mitigating abrupt changes in the Barnes–Hut approximation as
\rho_{ik}
varies for small movements in
\mathbf{x}_{i}
.

Noisy Barnes–Hut follows the same tree traversal as the deterministic version but relaxes the hard acceptance criterion. Instead of the binary indicator
\mathbb{I}(\rho_{ik}<\theta)
, noisy Barnes–Hut treats this decision probabilistically. A pseudo-particle is accepted if
u<p(\rho_{ik}\mid\theta)
, where
u\sim\text{Unif}(0,1)
and the acceptance probability is defined by the logistic function

\displaystyle p(\rho\mid\theta)=\left(1+\exp\left[-\frac{c}{\theta}(\theta-\rho)\right]\right)^{-1}.

(4)

The parameter
\theta
is a user-specified threshold, while
c
is a constant controlling the slope of the logistic function. We fix
c
so that the transition between acceptance and rejection occurs over a narrow band of
\rho_{ik}
values; specifically,
p(\rho\mid\theta)=0.8
when
(\theta-\rho)/\theta=0.2
. In our implementation, this corresponds to
c=6.93
. As
c\to\infty
, the probability
p(\rho\mid\theta)
converges to the indicator function
\mathbb{I}(\rho<\theta)
, recovering the deterministic rule. The method preserves the expected computational complexity of
O(n\log n)
with occasional additional node evaluations near the acceptance boundary.

By treating
\mathcal{B}_{i}
as a realization of a random process with controlled variance, the stochastic approach improves mixing in MCMC settings (Section
4.4
). However, it introduces approximation noise that must be rigorously accounted for. We address this by proving that the stationary measure of BH-BMDS is consistent (Section
4.3
).

4.3
Consistency for noisy BH-BMDS

We prove consistency for a class of surrogate-likelihood algorithms, even in the regime where the surrogate is not pointwise accurate. See Section
A.1
for an expanded discussion of this theoretical framework.

Fix dimension
d\in\mathbb{N}
and noise level
\sigma>0
. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we assume the data are generated from "true" latent positions
\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\star},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{n}^{\star}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathrm{Unif}([0,1]^{d})
, and the observed dissimilarities
D_{ij}
are generated according to Model
1

where
r_{ij}=r_{ij}^{\star}\equiv\left\|\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\star}-\mathbf{x}_{j}^{\star}\right\|
.
We define the truncation function
T_{M}(u)=\min\{u,M\}
for
u\geq 0
, and a maximum distance
M\geq\sqrt{d}
. We then set the capped dissimilarities and their expected values as

\displaystyle\tilde{D}_{ij}=T_{M}(D_{ij}),\qquad\mu_{M}(r)=\mathbb{E}\big[T_{M}([r+\sigma Z]_{+})\big],\qquad r\geq 0

(5)

such that the conditional law of
\tilde{D}_{ij}
given
r_{ij}^{\star}=r
has a piecewise density
q_{M,r}(y)
(Equation
A.2
). Since the maximum possible distance within this unit hypercube is bounded by
\sqrt{d}
, the capped distances are consistent with the underlying data-generation process.

For a candidate configuration
\mathbf{X}=(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{n})\in([0,1]^{d})^{n}
and
r_{ij}(\mathbf{X})=\left\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\right\|
, define the loss (negative log-likelihood) as

\displaystyle\ell_{M}(y,r)\;=\;-\log q_{M,r}(y),\qquad y\in[0,M],\ r\in[0,\sqrt{d}],

(6)

and the associated empirical risk as

\displaystyle R_{n}(\mathbf{X})\;=\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\ell_{M}(\tilde{D}_{ij},\,r_{ij}(\mathbf{X})).

(7)

We consider a family of surrogate log-likelihoods indexed by
\xi\in\Xi
, where each
\xi
induces an MCMC algorithm. Let
\xi_{0}\in\Xi
represent the deterministic reference algorithm, e.g., standard Barnes–Hut. By defining a probability measure on
\Xi
, we draw a stochastic realization
\xi
, yielding the randomized surrogate log-likelihood that drives our noisy MCMC. We denote
\widehat{\ell}_{ij}^{(\xi)}(\mathbf{X})
as the resulting approximation of
\ell_{M}(\tilde{D}_{ij},r_{ij}(\mathbf{X}))
and define the surrogate risk as

\displaystyle\widehat{R}_{n}^{(\xi)}(\mathbf{X})\;=\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\widehat{\ell}_{ij}^{(\xi)}(\mathbf{X}).

(8)

Note that
\widehat{\ell}_{ij}^{(\xi)}
depends on the entirety of
\mathbf{X}
due to the quadtree construction.

Since distances are invariant under Euclidean isometries, e.g., rigid motion, we measure convergence using the orbit distance
d_{\mathcal{G}}
defined in Remark
A.1
. Specifically, we prove that the mass of the stationary measure outside any fixed
d_{\mathcal{G}}
-ball around the true configuration
\mathbf{X}^{\star}
goes to
0
(in probability) as
n\to\infty
. To achieve this, we assume a standard data-generating process characterized by a random design, independent paired noise and sufficient prior regularity around the true configuration (Assumption
A.2
). The key assumption is
uniform vanishing error
for the
pairwise losses
:

Assumption 1
(Small Approximation Error)

There exists a deterministic sequence
\varepsilon_{n}\downarrow 0
such that, with probability
\to 1
under the joint law of
(\mathbf{X}^{\star},\mathbf{D})
, the reference approximation
\widehat{\ell}^{(\xi_{0})}
satisfies

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{X}\in([0,1]^{d})^{n}}\ \max_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\ \Big|\widehat{\ell}_{ij}^{(\xi_{0})}(\mathbf{X})-\ell_{M}(\tilde{D}_{ij},r_{ij}(\mathbf{X}))\Big|\ \leq\ \varepsilon_{n}.

(9)

While the difference in the pairwise approximation error must go to 0 as
n
grows, this assumption allows the difference in the total log-likelihood to diverge to infinity, provided the growth remains sub-quadratic.

Theorem 1
(Consistency for noisy MCMC)

Fix
d\geq 1
,
\sigma>0
,
M\geq\sqrt{d}
and a parameter space
\Xi
. Suppose there exists a reference parameter
\xi_{0}\in\Xi
such that the chain
P_{\xi_{0}}
is uniformly ergodic and let
\widehat{\ell}_{ij}^{(\xi_{0})}
be an approximation satisfying Assumption
1
. Let
P_{\xi}
be a Markov chain with stationary measure
\widehat{\Pi}_{n}^{(\xi)}
, and define the mixture kernel
P=\int_{\Xi}P_{\xi}\,\mu(d\xi)
for a probability measure
\mu
on
\Xi
. Let
\widetilde{\Pi}_{n}
be an invariant probability measure of
P
and set
\kappa_{n}
and
\Delta_{n}
as Equations
A.10
and
A.11
, respectively. If
\kappa_{n}\Delta_{n}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0
, then for every
\delta>0
,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Pi}_{n}\Big(\big\{\mathbf{X}:\ d_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\star})>\delta\big\}\Big)\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0.

(10)

Remark 1

Theorem
1
applies directly to the deterministic Barnes–Hut algorithm (
\xi=\xi_{0}
, Section
3.2
, Algorithm
4
) by choosing a sequence
\theta=\theta_{n}\to 0
. More subtly, the result also extend to the noisy quadtree algorithm (Section
4.2
, Algorithm
5
) for sufficiently small noise. Because the target distribution is supported on a compact state space and possesses a density strictly bounded away from both zero and infinity, utilizing any standard proposal distribution with a similarly bounded density ensures that the reference Metropolis-Hastings chain is uniformly ergodic. Furthermore, the one-step total variation distance
\Delta_{n}
is governed by the logistic slope
c
in the noisy traversal (Equation
4
). By letting
c\to\infty
,
\Delta_{n}
can be made arbitrarily small, fulfilling the final theoretical assumptions.

Proof Sketch

The full proof, detailed in Appendix
A
, proceeds in two main stages. First, we establish consistency for the deterministic reference (
\xi_{0}
). We demonstrate that the baseline empirical risk uniformly concentrates around the expected "population" risk. Because this population risk is strictly minimized at the true configuration
\mathbf{X}^{\star}
(up to isometry), the mass of the stationary measure must concentrate around
\mathbf{X}^{\star}
in orbit distance. Second, we apply perturbation arguments to show this consistency is preserved under the noisy quadtree algorithm.

Algorithm 1

BH-BMDS via

Metropolis-within-Gibbs

To infer the model parameters, we implement a Metropolis-within-Gibbs (MwG) sampler (Algorithm
1
) targeting the posterior distribution

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{X},\sigma^{2}\mid\mathcal{T})\propto\tilde{\ell}(\mathbf{D}\mid\mathbf{X},\sigma^{2},\mathcal{T})\pi(\mathbf{X})\,\pi(\sigma^{2}),

(11)

where
\pi(\cdot)
is the prior specification.

The sampler proceeds by sequentially updating each model parameter
\gamma=(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{n},\sigma^{2})
using Metropolis–Hastings (MH) steps within a Gibbs framework. At iteration
s
and for a generic parameter
\gamma_{k}
with proposal
\gamma_{k}^{\prime}\sim q(\cdot\mid\gamma_{k}^{(s)})
, the acceptance probability is

\displaystyle\alpha(\gamma_{k}^{\prime}\mid\gamma^{(s)},\mathcal{T})=

\displaystyle\min\left\{1,\frac{p(\gamma_{k}^{\prime},\gamma_{-k}^{(s)}\mid\mathcal{T})\;q(\gamma_{k}^{(s)}\mid\gamma_{k}^{\prime})}{p(\gamma_{k}^{(s)},\gamma_{-k}^{(s)}\mid\mathcal{T})\;q(\gamma_{k}^{\prime}\mid\gamma_{k}^{(s)})}\right\},

(12)

where
\gamma_{-k}
denotes all parameters except
\gamma_{k}
.
We propose a candidate latent location from

\mathbf{x}_{n}^{\prime}\sim N\!\big(\mathbf{x}_{n}^{(s)},\tau_{n}^{2(s)}\mathbf{I}\big)

and a candidate precision from a truncated normal proposal,

1/\sigma^{2\,\prime}\sim N_{(0,\infty)}(1/\sigma^{2(s)},\tau_{1/\sigma^{2}}^{2(s)})
.

To efficiently evaluate the posterior, we implement different Barnes–Hut approximations depending on the parameter being updated. Latent locations use Algorithm
5
which evaluates a pointwise contribution to the BH-BMDS likelihood via a stochastic tree traversal. Noisy Barnes–Hut mitigates discontinuities in the likelihood approximation that arise when small changes in
\mathbf{x}_{i}
cause crossings of the deterministic Barnes–Hut acceptance boundary
\mathbb{I}(\rho_{ik}<\theta)
. By replacing this hard threshold with a probabilistic acceptance rule (Equation
4
), the resulting likelihood approximation varies more smoothly as a function of
\mathbf{x}_{i}
which increases the stability of Metropolis-Hastings updates. In contrast, the variance parameter
\sigma^{2}
uses deterministic Barnes–Hut to approximate the full BMDS likelihood (Algorithm
4
). This strategy avoids introducing additional noise into likelihood evaluations, thereby improving numerical stability.

Under this MwG sampler, for fixed quadtree
\mathcal{T}
, a single latent location update requires
O(\log n)
expected time, and a full sweep over all locations costs
O(n\log n)
. We assume proposals are sufficiently local such that
\mathbf{x}_{i}
and
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\prime}
lie in the same quadtree path, so that only center-of-mass updates are required. While running experiments on data (Section
5
), we found that this assumption holds true, and within a sweep, points’ cell allocations do not change. At the end of each sweep, the quadtree
\mathcal{T}
is rebuilt using the updated latent locations, which also requires
O(n\log n)
time. The cost of updating
\sigma^{2}
is of the same order as a full sweep.

Data

6
Discussion

We propose a scalable approximation to the BMDS likelihood using Barnes–Hut algorithms (BH-BMDS), reducing computational complexity from
O(n^{2})
to
O(n\log n)
. By embedding this approximation within a Metropolis-within-Gibbs sampler, our approach enables efficient posterior inference while maintaining a probabilistic framework. Crucially, we use a novel approach (Section
A.1
) to establish consistency of the stationary measure for BH-BMDS and evaluate the proposed method on datasets with diverse clustering patterns.

Several limitations suggest directions for future work. First, our inference scheme relies on coordinate-wise updates of latent locations, which may mix slowly in settings with strong dependence across points. We explored joint updates using HMC, discontinuous HMC and Metropolis–Hastings but encountered difficulties achieving both stable convergence and favorable scaling for larger
n
. More broadly, these findings highlight the challenges of combining discrete approximation schemes with MCMC methods. Second, the noisy Barnes–Hut approximation (Section
4.2
) introduces stochasticity through the traversal criterion. While this improves mixing by mitigating abrupt changes in the approximated likelihood, it also complicates the theoretical scaling behavior and may deviate from ideal
O(n\log n)
performance, although such deviations were not observed in practice. Developing more stable approximation schemes that preserve both accuracy and computational guarantees is an important direction for future work. Finally, as with standard BMDS, low-dimensional embeddings may not capture all relevant structure in complex datasets, particularly when restricted to two dimensions. Extending BH-BMDS to higher-dimensional embeddings or adaptive representations is a promising avenue for future work.

Proof strategy for Proposition

The basic idea is:

1.

Check that the empirical risk

Check that the population risk

3.

We note that this approach is not the most common approach to analyzing the error of "approximate" or "surrogate" MCMC algorithms. Typically, one aims to have a surrogate that is pointwise close to the target log-likelihood, then use perturbation theory to show that the stationary measure is close to the posterior distribution of interest (e.g.,
22

We are aware of several other results that prove consistency of MCMC algorithms even as the pointwise error is allowed to grow, though only in the much simpler situation of i.i.d. samples and a finite-dimensional set of parameters to be learned. The influential analysis of SGLD in

1.

Directly analyzing the risk is always more difficult than merely treating an approximate MCMC chain as a perturbation of an ideal chain, where more generic results are available

14

1

applied at level
t/2
gives

\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{\mathbf{X}\in([0,1]^{d})^{n}}|R_{n}(\mathbf{X})-R_{n}^{0}(\mathbf{X})|>t\ \Big|\ \mathbf{X}^{\star}\Big)\leq 2|\mathcal{N}_{\eta}|\exp\Big(-\frac{Nt^{2}}{2W_{\ell}^{2}}\Big).

Substituting Equation
A.17
and
\eta=t/(8L_{\ell})
and then enlarging constants gives the conclusion for all sufficiently small
t
.

A.4
A quadratic lower bound for the population risk

We show
R_{n}^{0}(\mathbf{X})
is minimized (up to isometry) at
\mathbf{X}^{\star}
, and furthermore that the risk increases quadratically as you move away from
\mathbf{X}^{\star}
. More precisely, we will show

Proposition A.2

There exists
c_{\mathrm{mg}}=c_{\mathrm{mg}}(d,\sigma,M)>0
such that the following holds (uniformly in
\mathbf{X}
) with probability going to 1 as
n
goes to infinity:

R_{n}^{0}(\mathbf{X})-R_{n}^{0}(\mathbf{X}^{\star})\ \geq\ c_{\mathrm{mg}}\ d_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\star})^{2},\qquad\forall\mathbf{X}\in([0,1]^{d})^{n}.

(A.22)

This is the main result in this section. The proof is deferred to the end, in sub-section
A.4.4
.

A.4.1
Initial quadratic lower bound in the distance

Lemma 3

There exists
c_{\mathrm{sc}}=c_{\mathrm{sc}}(\sigma,M,d)>0
such that for all
r,r^{\star}\in[0,\sqrt{d}]
,

g(r;r^{\star})-g(r^{\star};r^{\star})\ \geq\ c_{\mathrm{sc}}\,(r-r^{\star})^{2},

where
g
is defined in Equation
A.14
.

Proof 3

For fixed
r^{\star}
, let
P_{r}
denote the law of
\tilde{D}=T_{M}([r+\sigma Z]_{+})
, with density
q_{M,r}
with respect to
\nu
; equivalently,
P_{r}(A)=\int_{A}q_{M,r}(y)\,\nu(dy)
for measurable
A\subseteq[0,M]
. Thus the observation generated at the true distance
r^{\star}
has law
P_{r^{\star}}
, while evaluating the loss at a candidate distance
r
uses the density
q_{M,r}
. Therefore, by the definition of
g
in Equation
A.14
,

g(r;r^{\star})-g(r^{\star};r^{\star})=\int q_{M,r^{\star}}(y)\log\!\left(\frac{q_{M,r^{\star}}(y)}{q_{M,r}(y)}\right)\,\nu(dy)=\mathrm{KL}(P_{r^{\star}}\,\|\,P_{r}).

(A.23)

By Pinsker’s inequality and Equation
A.23
,

g(r;r^{\star})-g(r^{\star};r^{\star})\geq 2\|P_{r^{\star}}-P_{r}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}^{2}.

(A.24)

Recalling the definition of
\mu_{M}
from Equation
5
, we have

\displaystyle|\mu_{M}(r)-\mu_{M}(r^{\star})|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{0}^{M}y\,\big(q_{M,r}(y)-q_{M,r^{\star}}(y)\big)\,\nu(dy)\right|

\displaystyle\leq M\int_{0}^{M}\big|q_{M,r}(y)-q_{M,r^{\star}}(y)\big|\,\nu(dy)

\displaystyle=2M\|P_{r}-P_{r^{\star}}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}.

(A.25)

Combining Equation
A.24
and Equation
3
gives

g(r;r^{\star})-g(r^{\star};r^{\star})\geq\frac{\big(\mu_{M}(r)-\mu_{M}(r^{\star})\big)^{2}}{2M^{2}}.

(A.26)

For each fixed
Z
, the map
r\mapsto T_{M}([r+\sigma Z]_{+})
is piecewise linear with derivative
\bm{1}\{0<r+\sigma Z<M\}
for almost every
r
, so dominated convergence yields

\mu_{M}^{\prime}(r)=\mathbb{P}(0<r+\sigma Z<M)=\Phi\!\left(\frac{M-r}{\sigma}\right)-\Phi\!\left(-\frac{r}{\sigma}\right).

(A.27)

Hence, for
r\in[0,\sqrt{d}]
,

\mu_{M}^{\prime}(r)=\Phi\!\left(\frac{M-r}{\sigma}\right)+\Phi\!\left(\frac{r}{\sigma}\right)-1\geq\Phi\!\left(\frac{M}{2\sigma}\right)-\frac{1}{2}=:m_{M}.

(A.28)

We note that
m_{M}>0
, since
r\in[0,M]
and therefore at least one of
r
and
M-r
is at least
M/2
. By the mean value theorem and Equation
A.28
,

|\mu_{M}(r)-\mu_{M}(r^{\star})|\geq m_{M}|r-r^{\star}|.

(A.29)

Substituting Equation
A.29
into Equation
A.26
proves the claim, with
c_{\mathrm{sc}}=m_{M}^{2}/(2M^{2})
.

A.4.2
From pairwise distance error to Procrustes error

Define the average squared distance discrepancy

\Delta_{\mathrm{dist}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\star})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i<j}\big(r_{ij}(\mathbf{X})-r_{ij}^{\star}\big)^{2}.

We have the straightforward estimate:

Lemma 4
(Population risk dominates distance discrepancy)

Under the model
1
and loss (Equation
6
),

R_{n}^{0}(\mathbf{X})-R_{n}^{0}(\mathbf{X}^{\star})\ \geq\ c_{\mathrm{sc}}\ \Delta_{\mathrm{dist}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\star}),

where
c_{\mathrm{sc}}
is as in Lemma
3
.

Proof 4

By Lemma
3
, for each pair
(i,j)
,

g\big(r_{ij}(\mathbf{X});r_{ij}^{\star}\big)-g\big(r_{ij}^{\star};r_{ij}^{\star}\big)\ \geq\ c_{\mathrm{sc}}\big(r_{ij}(\mathbf{X})-r_{ij}^{\star}\big)^{2}.

Averaging over pairs yields the result.

Next we relate
\Delta_{\mathrm{dist}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\star})
to
d_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\star})
using a standard Procrustes perturbation bound. Recall that
\mathbf{X}_{c}=\mathbf{J}\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{X}_{c}^{\star}=\mathbf{J}\mathbf{X}^{\star}
denote the centered configurations.

We will use the following consequence of Theorem 1
2
as our basic Procrustes perturbation bound: with
\varepsilon^{2}=\left\|\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{Y}^{\top}-\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\right\|_{F}
, if
\left\|\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\varepsilon\leq 1/\sqrt{2}
, then

\min_{\mathbf{Q}\in\mathcal{O}(d)}\left\|\mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Q}\right\|_{F}\leq(1+\sqrt{2})\left\|\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\left\|\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{Y}^{\top}-\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\right\|_{F}.

Note that the theorem in that paper looks slightly different and is quite a bit more complicated; this follows immediately from the second branch in
(
2
, Theorem 1, inequality (5))
after choosing Schatten norm
p=2
, simplifying and rephrasing in our notation.

Lemma 5

For every
\alpha>0
, there exists
c_{2}=c_{2}(\alpha,d)>0
such that, if

s_{\min}(\mathbf{X}_{c}^{\star})\ \geq\ \alpha\sqrt{n},

(A.30)

then

d_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\star})^{2}\ \leq\ c_{2}\ \Delta_{\mathrm{dist}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\star}),\qquad\forall\mathbf{X}\in([0,1]^{d})^{n}.

Proof 5

We follow
2
and work with centered Gram matrices.
Denote by
D^{2}(\mathbf{X})
the squared-distance matrix with entries
D^{2}(\mathbf{X})_{ij}=\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\|^{2}
. With
\mathbf{J}
as above, the centered Gram matrix is

G(\mathbf{X})=-\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{J}D^{2}(\mathbf{X})\mathbf{J}=\mathbf{X}_{c}\mathbf{X}_{c}^{\top}.

We begin by bounding
G(\mathbf{X})-G(\mathbf{X}^{\star})
in terms of
\Delta_{\mathrm{dist}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\star})
. By Lemma
1
, the Frobenius–Frobenius operator norm of
\mathbf{A}\mapsto\mathbf{J}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{J}
is
1
, so

\left\|G(\mathbf{X})-G(\mathbf{X}^{\star})\right\|_{F}=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathbf{J}\big(D^{2}(\mathbf{X})-D^{2}(\mathbf{X}^{\star})\big)\mathbf{J}\right\|_{F}\leq\frac{1}{2}\,\left\|D^{2}(\mathbf{X})-D^{2}(\mathbf{X}^{\star})\right\|_{F}.

(A.31)

For
r,r^{\star}\in[0,\sqrt{d}]
we have

|r^{2}-(r^{\star})^{2}|=|r-r^{\star}|\,(r+r^{\star})\leq 2\sqrt{d}\,|r-r^{\star}|.

(A.32)

Therefore, by Equation
A.32
,

\displaystyle\left\|D^{2}(\mathbf{X})-D^{2}(\mathbf{X}^{\star})\right\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\neq j}\bigl(r_{ij}(\mathbf{X})^{2}-(r_{ij}^{\star})^{2}\bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4d\sum_{i\neq j}\bigl(r_{ij}(\mathbf{X})-r_{ij}^{\star}\bigr)^{2}

(A.33)

\displaystyle=8d\sum_{i<j}\bigl(r_{ij}(\mathbf{X})-r_{ij}^{\star}\bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle=8dN\,\Delta_{\mathrm{dist}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\star}).

(A.34)

Combining Equation
A.31
and Equation
5
gives

\left\|G(\mathbf{X})-G(\mathbf{X}^{\star})\right\|_{F}\leq\sqrt{2dN\,\Delta_{\mathrm{dist}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\star})}.

(A.35)

Let

\varepsilon=\left\|G(\mathbf{X})-G(\mathbf{X}^{\star})\right\|_{F}^{1/2}.

(A.36)

We now apply
(
2
, Theorem 1)
with
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{X}_{c}^{\star}
and
\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{X}_{c}
and Schatten norm
p=2

(which is equal to the Frobenius norm). In the notation of that theorem, we have

\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{Y}^{\top}-\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}=G(\mathbf{X})-G(\mathbf{X}^{\star})
and hence
\varepsilon^{2}=\|\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{Y}^{\top}-\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\|_{F}
.
Using the second branch in the statement of
(
2
, Theorem 1, inequality (5))
, if

\|(\mathbf{X}_{c}^{\star})^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},

(A.37)

then

\min_{\mathbf{Q}\in\mathcal{O}(d)}\left\|\mathbf{X}_{c}-\mathbf{X}_{c}^{\star}\mathbf{Q}\right\|_{F}\leq(1+\sqrt{2})\,\|(\mathbf{X}_{c}^{\star})^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\ \left\|G(\mathbf{X})-G(\mathbf{X}^{\star})\right\|_{F}.

(A.38)

We now give a sufficient condition for Equation
A.37
. Under Equation
A.30
, we have
\|(\mathbf{X}_{c}^{\star})^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{op}}=1/s_{\min}(\mathbf{X}_{c}^{\star})\leq 1/(\alpha\sqrt{n})
.
Moreover, since
N=\binom{n}{2}\leq n^{2}/2
, Equations
A.35
–
A.36
imply

\varepsilon\leq\bigl(2dN\,\Delta_{\mathrm{dist}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\star})\bigr)^{1/4}\leq d^{1/4}n^{1/2}\,\Delta_{\mathrm{dist}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\star})^{1/4}.

(A.39)

Hence, by Equation
A.30
and Equation
A.39
, Equation
A.37
holds whenever

\Delta_{\mathrm{dist}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\star})\ \leq\ \frac{\alpha^{4}}{4d}.

(A.40)

We now obtain our bound in two cases: when Equation
A.40
holds, and when it fails. We begin by assuming it holds. Combining
Equation
A.38
with Equation
A.35
and Equation
A.30
gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\min_{\mathbf{Q}\in\mathcal{O}(d)}\left\|\mathbf{X}_{c}-\mathbf{X}_{c}^{\star}\mathbf{Q}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1+\sqrt{2}}{\alpha n}\ \sqrt{2dN\,\Delta_{\mathrm{dist}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\star})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1+\sqrt{2}}{\alpha}\ \sqrt{d\,\Delta_{\mathrm{dist}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\star})}.

(A.41)

By the definition of
d_{\mathcal{G}}
in Equation
A.5
, the left-hand side equals
d_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\star})
, so
squaring Equation
A.41
yields

d_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\star})^{2}\leq\frac{(1+\sqrt{2})^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}\ d\ \Delta_{\mathrm{dist}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\star})\qquad\text{whenever Equation~\ref{app:eq:Delta_small_regime_new} holds.}

(A.42)

Finally, we consider the case that Equation
A.40
fails. In this case,
\Delta_{\mathrm{dist}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\star})\geq\alpha^{4}/(4d)
.
Since
\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\star}\in[0,1]^{d}
, choosing
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{I}
and
\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{0}
in the uncentered definition of the orbit distance gives the trivial bound
d_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\star})^{2}\leq d
.
Therefore,

d_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\star})^{2}\leq d\leq\frac{4d^{2}}{\alpha^{4}}\ \Delta_{\mathrm{dist}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\star})\qquad\text{whenever Equation~\ref{app:eq:Delta_small_regime_new} fails.}

(A.43)

Taking

c_{2}=\max\Big\{\frac{(1+\sqrt{2})^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}d,\ \frac{4d^{2}}{\alpha^{4}}\Big\}

and combining Equation
A.42
and Equation
A.43
proves the lemma.

A.4.3
Well-conditioning holds with high probability for uniform points

Lemma 6

Let
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\star}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\mathrm{Unif}([0,1]^{d})
, let
\mathbf{X}^{\star}
be the associated matrix of points, and let
\mathbf{X}_{c}^{\star}
be its centered version. Then there exists
c_{1}=c_{1}(d)>0
such that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big(s_{\min}(\mathbf{X}_{c}^{\star})\geq c_{1}\sqrt{n}\Big)=1.

Proof 6

Let
\mathbf{u}_{i}=\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\star}-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\star}]=\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\star}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{1}_{d}
, and let
\mathbf{U}
be the
n\times d
matrix with rows
\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\top}
. Then the rows of
\mathbf{U}
are i.i.d., mean-zero, bounded random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and

\mathbf{X}_{c}^{\star}=\mathbf{J}\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{U}-\bm{1}\bar{\mathbf{u}}^{\top},\qquad\bar{\mathbf{u}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{u}_{i}.

We first lower-bound
s_{\min}(\mathbf{U})
. Set
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1/2}=\sqrt{12}\,\mathbf{U}
, where

\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{u}_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\top}]=\frac{1}{12}\mathbf{I}_{d}.

Then the rows of
\mathbf{A}
are isotropic, subgaussian and have subgaussian norm bounded by a constant
K_{d}<\infty
depending only on
d
. By
(
31
, Theorem 4.6.1)
, there exists an absolute constant
0<C<\infty
so that for every
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}\big[s_{\min}(\mathbf{A})\geq\sqrt{n}-CK_{d}^{2}(\sqrt{d}+t)\big]\geq 1-2e^{-t^{2}}.

(A.44)

Since
\mathbf{A}=\sqrt{12}\,\mathbf{U}
, choosing
t=\sqrt{n}/(4CK_{d}^{2})
in Equation
A.44
shows that there exist constants
c_{U}=c_{U}(d)>0
and
c_{A}=c_{A}(d)>0
such that, for all sufficiently large
n
,

\mathbb{P}\big(s_{\min}(\mathbf{U})\geq c_{U}\sqrt{n}\big)\geq 1-2e^{-c_{A}n}.

(A.45)

We next control the rank-one correction
\bm{1}\bar{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}
. Because each coordinate of
\mathbf{u}_{i}
is bounded and mean-zero, Hoeffding’s inequality gives, for every
a>0
,

\mathbb{P}\big(\sqrt{n}\,|\bar{u}_{k}|>a\big)\leq 2e^{-ca^{2}},\qquad k=1,\dots,d,

for a constant
c=c(d)>0
. Therefore, by a union bound, for every
b>0
,

\mathbb{P}\big(\sqrt{n}\,\|\bar{\mathbf{u}}\|>b\big)\leq 2d\exp\left(-\frac{cb^{2}}{d}\right).

(A.46)

Since
\|\bm{1}\bar{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\sqrt{n}\,\|\bar{\mathbf{u}}\|
, substituting
b=(c_{U}/2)\sqrt{n}
in Equation
A.46
gives

\mathbb{P}\left(\|\bm{1}\bar{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}\|_{\mathrm{op}}>\frac{c_{U}}{2}\sqrt{n}\right)\leq 2d\exp\left(-\frac{cc_{U}^{2}}{4d}n\right).

(A.47)

Let

E_{n}=\left\{s_{\min}(\mathbf{U})\geq c_{U}\sqrt{n}\right\}\cap\left\{\|\bm{1}\bar{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\frac{c_{U}}{2}\sqrt{n}\right\}.

By Equation
A.45
and Equation
A.47
,
\mathbb{P}(E_{n})\to 1
. On
E_{n}
, since
\mathbf{X}_{c}^{\star}=\mathbf{U}-\bm{1}\bar{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}
, Weyl’s inequality for singular values gives

s_{\min}(\mathbf{X}_{c}^{\star})=s_{\min}(\mathbf{U}-\bm{1}\bar{\mathbf{u}}^{\top})\geq s_{\min}(\mathbf{U})-\|\bm{1}\bar{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\geq(c_{U}/2)\sqrt{n}.

Taking
c_{1}=c_{U}/2
proves the lemma.

A.4.4
Completing the quadratic lower bounds

Proof 7
(Proof of Proposition
A.2
)

Let
c_{1}=c_{1}(d)
be the constant from Lemma
6
. We prove Equation
A.22
on the high-probability event
s_{\min}(\mathbf{X}_{c}^{\star})\geq c_{1}\sqrt{n}
, applying Lemma
5
with
\alpha=c_{1}
.

Combine Lemma
4
and Lemma
5
:

R_{n}^{0}(\mathbf{X})-R_{n}^{0}(\mathbf{X}^{\star})\ \geq\ c_{\mathrm{sc}}\,\Delta_{\mathrm{dist}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\star})\ \geq\ c_{\mathrm{sc}}\cdot\frac{1}{c_{2}}\ d_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\star})^{2}.

Set
c_{\mathrm{mg}}=c_{\mathrm{sc}}/c_{2}
.

Proposition A.3

There exists a constant
C_{\rm up}=C_{\rm up}(d,\sigma,M)>0
such that, for every
\mathbf{X}\in([0,1]^{d})^{n}
,

R_{n}^{0}(\mathbf{X})-R_{n}^{0}(\mathbf{X}^{\star})\ \leq\ C_{\rm up}\,d_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\star})^{2}.

(A.48)

Proof 8

Fix
\mathbf{X}
and choose
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathcal{O}(d)
so that
d_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\star})=n^{-1/2}\left\|\mathbf{X}_{c}-\mathbf{X}_{c}^{\star}\mathbf{Q}\right\|_{F}
. Write
\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{X}_{c}-\mathbf{X}_{c}^{\star}\mathbf{Q}
, and let
\mathbf{h}_{i}
denote the
i
th row of
\mathbf{H}
. Then
\left\|\mathbf{H}\right\|_{F}^{2}=n\,d_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\star})^{2}
and the rows of
\mathbf{H}
sum to
0
.

For each fixed
y\in[0,M]
, the map
r\mapsto\ell_{M}(y,r)
is
C^{2}
on
[0,\sqrt{d}]
, and each of the three branches from Equation
A.2
has second derivative uniformly bounded on the relevant compact interval. Hence there exists
H_{\ell}=H_{\ell}(d,\sigma,M)<\infty
such that

\sup_{y\in[0,M]}\sup_{r\in[0,\sqrt{d}]}\big|\partial_{r}^{2}\ell_{M}(y,r)\big|\leq H_{\ell}.

(A.49)

By Equation
A.14
,

g(r;r^{\star})=\int\ell_{M}(y,r)q_{M,r^{\star}}(y)\,\nu(dy).

Using
\partial_{r}\ell_{M}(y,r)=-(\partial_{r}q_{M,r}(y))/q_{M,r}(y)
, we get

\displaystyle\partial_{r}g(r;r^{\star})\big|_{r=r^{\star}}

\displaystyle=-\int q_{M,r^{\star}}(y)\frac{\partial_{r}q_{M,r}(y)|_{r=r^{\star}}}{q_{M,r^{\star}}(y)}\,\nu(dy)

\displaystyle=-\int\partial_{r}q_{M,r}(y)\big|_{r=r^{\star}}\,\nu(dy)

\displaystyle=-\partial_{r}\left(\int q_{M,r}(y)\,\nu(dy)\right)\bigg|_{r=r^{\star}}=0,

because
\int q_{M,r}(y)\,\nu(dy)=1
for every
r
. Moreover, Equation
A.49
implies
|\partial_{r}^{2}g(r;r^{\star})|\leq H_{\ell}
for all
r,r^{\star}\in[0,\sqrt{d}]
. Taylor’s theorem therefore gives

g(r;r^{\star})-g(r^{\star};r^{\star})\leq\frac{H_{\ell}}{2}(r-r^{\star})^{2},\qquad r,r^{\star}\in[0,\sqrt{d}].

(A.50)

Applying Equation
A.50
with
r=r_{ij}(\mathbf{X})
and
r^{\star}=r_{ij}^{\star}
and then using the fact that centering and rotation do not change pairwise distances, we obtain

g\big(r_{ij}(\mathbf{X});r_{ij}^{\star}\big)-g\big(r_{ij}^{\star};r_{ij}^{\star}\big)\leq\frac{H_{\ell}}{2}\big(r_{ij}(\mathbf{X})-r_{ij}^{\star}\big)^{2}\leq\frac{H_{\ell}}{2}\left\|\mathbf{h}_{i}-\mathbf{h}_{j}\right\|^{2}.

(A.51)

Averaging Equation
A.51
over pairs and using the identity
\sum_{i<j}\left\|\mathbf{h}_{i}-\mathbf{h}_{j}\right\|^{2}=n\left\|\mathbf{H}\right\|_{F}^{2}
yields

R_{n}^{0}(\mathbf{X})-R_{n}^{0}(\mathbf{X}^{\star})\leq\frac{H_{\ell}}{2N}\sum_{i<j}\left\|\mathbf{h}_{i}-\mathbf{h}_{j}\right\|^{2}=\frac{H_{\ell}n\left\|\mathbf{H}\right\|_{F}^{2}}{2N}\leq 2H_{\ell}\,d_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\star})^{2},

since
N=\binom{n}{2}\geq n^{2}/4
for
n\geq 2
. This proves Equation
A.48
with
C_{\rm up}=2H_{\ell}
.

A.5
Consistency of the exact posterior

Proposition A.4
(Consistency of the exact posterior)

Suppose Assumption
A.2
holds. Then, for every
\delta>0
,

\Pi_{n}\Big(\big\{\mathbf{X}:\ d_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\star})>\delta\big\}\ \Big|\ \mathbf{D}\Big)\ \xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\ 0.

Proof 9

Fix
\delta>0
, and define the constant

a_{0}=\min\Big\{\frac{1}{2},\ \sqrt{\frac{c_{\rm mg}}{4C_{\rm up}}}\Big\}\in\Big(0,\frac{1}{2}\Big],

(A.52)

where
c_{\rm mg}
is from Proposition
A.2
and
C_{\rm up}
is from Proposition
A.3
. Let

A_{\delta}=\{\mathbf{X}:\ d_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\star})>\delta\},\qquad B_{a_{0}\delta}=\{\mathbf{X}:\ d_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\star})\leq a_{0}\delta\},\qquad\widetilde{B}_{a_{0}\delta}=B_{a_{0}\delta}\cap([0,1]^{d})^{n}.

Because the integrand is nonnegative, restricting the domain of integration gives

\int e^{-NR_{n}(\mathbf{X})}\,\Pi_{0}(d\mathbf{X})\ \geq\ \int_{\widetilde{B}_{a_{0}\delta}}e^{-NR_{n}(\mathbf{X})}\,\Pi_{0}(d\mathbf{X}).

(A.53)

Let
\eta_{n}=\sup_{\mathbf{X}\in([0,1]^{d})^{n}}|R_{n}(\mathbf{X})-R_{n}^{0}(\mathbf{X})|
. By Lemma
2
,
\eta_{n}\to 0
in probability. Let

E_{n}^{\mathrm{cond}}=\{s_{\min}(\mathbf{X}_{c}^{\star})\geq c_{1}\sqrt{n}\},

where
c_{1}=c_{1}(d)
is the constant from Lemma
6
; then
\mathbb{P}(E_{n}^{\mathrm{cond}})\to 1
. Fix
\gamma>0
. On the event
\{\eta_{n}\leq\gamma\}
we have, for all
\mathbf{X}
,

R_{n}(\mathbf{X})\geq R_{n}^{0}(\mathbf{X})-\gamma,\qquad R_{n}(\mathbf{X})\leq R_{n}^{0}(\mathbf{X})+\gamma.

(A.54)

Therefore, using Equation
A.53
and then Equation
A.54
,

\displaystyle\Pi_{n}(A_{\delta}\mid\mathbf{D})

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{A_{\delta}}e^{-NR_{n}(\mathbf{X})}\Pi_{0}(d\mathbf{X})}{\int e^{-NR_{n}(\mathbf{X})}\Pi_{0}(d\mathbf{X})}\leq\frac{\int_{A_{\delta}}e^{-NR_{n}(\mathbf{X})}\Pi_{0}(d\mathbf{X})}{\int_{\widetilde{B}_{a_{0}\delta}}e^{-NR_{n}(\mathbf{X})}\Pi_{0}(d\mathbf{X})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\int_{A_{\delta}}e^{-N(R_{n}^{0}(\mathbf{X})-\gamma)}\Pi_{0}(d\mathbf{X})}{\int_{\widetilde{B}_{a_{0}\delta}}e^{-N(R_{n}^{0}(\mathbf{X})+\gamma)}\Pi_{0}(d\mathbf{X})}=e^{2N\gamma}\cdot\frac{\int_{A_{\delta}}e^{-NR_{n}^{0}(\mathbf{X})}\Pi_{0}(d\mathbf{X})}{\int_{\widetilde{B}_{a_{0}\delta}}e^{-NR_{n}^{0}(\mathbf{X})}\Pi_{0}(d\mathbf{X})}.

Next, on
E_{n}^{\mathrm{cond}}
, Proposition
A.2
gives

A.7
Proof of Theorem
A.1

Let

P
,

\Delta_{n}^{\mathrm{mix}}\leq\int_{\Xi}\sup_{\mathbf{X}_{0}\in([0,1]^{d})^{n}}\big\|P_{\xi}(\mathbf{X}_{0},\cdot)-P_{\xi_{0}}(\mathbf{X}_{0},\cdot)\big\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\,\mu(d\xi)\leq\Delta_{n}.

1

A.65
,

A.66

\widetilde{\Pi}_{n}
under

\displaystyle\big\|\widetilde{\Pi}_{n}-\widehat{\Pi}_{n}^{(\xi_{0})}\big\|_{\mathrm{TV}}
