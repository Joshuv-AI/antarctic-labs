Exercise reduces hepatic pre-metastatic niche formation via TREM-1 signaling in kupffer cells
Authors: Ruiqi Yang, Tony Haykal, Celine Tohme, Zhengyi He, Haotong Zhang, Anthony Gebran, Silvia Liu, Richard L. Simmons, David A. Geller, Hamza O. Yazdani, Samer Tohme
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTumor-derived exosomes contribute to the formation of a hepatic pre-metastatic niche (PMN) by activating Kupffer cells (KCs) and hepatic stellate cells (HSCs), thereby promoting metastatic seeding. Whether exercise modulates exosome-driven PMN formation and subsequent liver metastasis remains unclear.MethodsEight-week-old male mice were randomized to sedentary (SED) and exercise training (ExT) groups. Mice were pre-educated with cancer-derived exosomes to induce a hepatic PMN and subsequently subjected to experimental liver metastasis model. Kupffer cells were isolated for transcriptomic and mechanistic analyses. Pharmacologic inhibition of TREM-1 signaling was performed to assess pathway-specific effects.ResultsExercise training significantly attenuated hepatic PMN formation and reduced metastatic burden in exosome-educated mice. Exosome uptake by KCs was comparable between SED and ExT groups; however, ExT reduced KC inflammatory activation. Transcriptomic analysis identified downregulation of the TREM-1 signaling pathway in ExT KCs. Reduced TREM-1 activity was associated with diminished NF-κB activation in HSCs, decreased fibronectin deposition, and increased HSC apoptosis. Pharmacologic TREM-1 inhibition recapitulated key anti-metastatic effects observed with exercise.ConclusionsExercise training attenuates tumor exosome–induced hepatic pre-metastatic niche formation through modulation of Kupffer cell inflammatory signaling, in part via suppression of TREM-1. Targeting TREM-1 signaling significantly reduces experimental liver metastasis and may represent a therapeutic approach that partially recapitulates the anti-metastatic effects of exercise. These findings support further investigation into exercise and innate immune modulation as strategies to influence early metastatic niche biology.

Background

Metastasis remains the leading cause of cancer mortality. [1] Before circulating tumor cells arrive, primary tumors can condition distant organs, creating a pre metastatic niche (PMN) that fosters seeding, survival, and outgrowth. [2] Foundational work identified bone-marrow–derived cells, inflammatory mediators, and extracellular-matrix (ECM) remodeling as PMN hallmarks, providing a mechanistic basis for organotropism—the non-random pattern of metastatic spread. [3–5] Among the signals orchestrating PMN formation, tumor derived exosomes, a class of extracellular vesicles (EVs), have emerged as key architects. [6] Exosomes are small lipid bilayer–bound extracellular vesicles (approximately 40–160 nm in diameter) secreted by virtually all cell types [7] and function as mediators of intercellular communication via their protein, lipid, and RNA cargo, including mRNA and microRNAs particles. [8] Studies have revealed that cancer exosomes induce chronic inflammation, [9] immune suppression, [10] and angiogenesis [11] in the pre-metastatic niche. Landmark studies showed that exosome integrin signatures direct vesicle fusion to organ resident cells, thereby imprinting organ tropism and priming the PMNs. [9]

The specific organs affected by metastases vary significantly depending on the type of cancer; however, the liver is a major hub of metastasis for primary tumors from around the body. [12, 13] Gastrointestinal malignancies such as colon, pancreatic, and gastric carcinomas have a high tendency for hepatic spread, as well as for breast carcinomas, sarcomas, and melanoma. [14] In the liver, Kupffer cells predominantly uptake these exosomes, initiating an inflammatory state and pre-metastatic niche formation. [15] Although liver resection and chemotherapy are the primary therapeutic choices for patients with liver metastases, most liver metastases are unfortunately non-resectable, and there is no curative treatment available currently. [16] Thus, understanding how these niches form can offer insights into interrupting the metastatic process at its earliest stages.

Epidemiological studies show that regular physical exercise confers beneficial effects, including reducing cancer incidence, cancer-specific mortality, cancer recurrence, and all-cause mortality. [17] Preclinical exercise studies in rodents provide compelling evidence for the protective effects of exercise against cancer. [18] A potential mechanism underlying the beneficial effects of physical activity in cancer is the exercise-induced enhancement of immune cell effector functions. [19] It has been shown that voluntary wheel running in mice reduces melanoma tumor burden by enhancing the infiltration of intratumoral natural killer (NK) cells, which is regulated by elevated levels of serum epinephrine and muscle-derived interleukin-6 (IL-6). [20] Additionally, studies indicate that aerobic exercise inhibits pancreatic and breast cancer growth by reprogramming T lymphocytes to adopt potent effector and cytotoxic phenotypes. [21, 22] In previous work, our group has shown that preoperative exercise protects against ischemia-reperfusion injury to the liver and reduces liver injury-induced accelerated growth of liver metastases. [23] Moreover, exercise training (ExT) induces a shift in Kupffer cells, the crucial players in the establishment of hepatic premetastatic niches, towards an anti-inflammatory phenotype with trained immunity through metabolic reprogramming. [24] To our knowledge, no study has evaluated the potential protective effect of exercise on the formation of premetastatic niches and metastasis. Unraveling this protective mechanism could aid in developing novel therapies to improve cancer patients’ outcomes.

In this study, we found that ExT can cause phenotypic changes in Kupffer cells by downregulating the TREM-1 signaling pathway, thereby diminishing the inflammatory response and inhibiting the formation of cancer exosome-induced hepatic premetastatic niches and subsequent liver metastasis. These findings suggest that exercise training may be used as a non-pharmacological intervention to effectively prevent the risk of cancer metastasis. Moreover, targeting the TREM-1 signaling pathway may be a surrogate for exercise in preventing metastasis.

Methods

Animals and ExT model

Male C57BL/6 mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory. All mice were housed in a pathogen-free environment with a controlled temperature (21 °C) and lighting cycle (12-h light/12-h dark), with free access to food and water. Mice of 8-10 weeks were randomly assigned to the exercise-training (ExT) or sedentary (SED) group. ExT animals were subjected to 1 h treadmill running (Columbus Instruments, USA) at 12.5 m/min for five consecutive days a week, followed by a 48 h recovery time for a total of four weeks as described previously. [23] Opaque dividers were used to separate the six running lanes, preventing visualization. Mice were assessed for their ability to run on the treadmill. If the mice were unable to perform exercise, they were removed from the experiment prior to onset. All experiments were performed according to protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Pittsburgh (Protocol number 26027812).

Cell lines and treatment

Luciferase-expressing MC38 cells (MC38-luc) were generated with the help of Dr. Katherine Aird’s lab (University of Pittsburgh, Pittsburgh, PA) and Panc02 cells were generously provided by Dr. Michael Lotze’s lab (University of Pittsburgh, Pittsburgh, PA). MC38 (Kerafast, ENH204-FP), MC38-luc, and Panc02 cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Cytiva), 100 units/ml penicillin and 100 μg/ml streptomycin (Gibco). Cells were maintained in a humid incubator with 5% CO2 at 37 °C. For the exosome isolation experiment, bovine exosomes were depleted from FBS by ultracentrifugation for 70 min at 100,000 g.

Exosome isolation, characterization, and labeling

Cell culture supernatant was collected 72 h after plating and centrifuged at 500 g for 10 min, 2000g for 20 min, and 10,000 g for 30 min sequentially to remove residual cells and debris. Exosomes were then collected by ultracentrifugation at 100,000 g for 2.5 h at 4 °C. The exosome pellet was resuspended in 15 ml PBS and collected by ultracentrifugation at 100,000 g for 2.5 h at 4 °C (Beckman). Exosome size and particle number were analyzed using the LM10 nanoparticle characterization system (NanoSight).

The isolated exosomes were labeled with PKH26 following the manufacturer’s instructions (MilliporeSigma).

In vivo mouse models

The liver pre-metastatic niche and liver metastases model was performed as previously described. [15] Briefly, mice received 5 μg of cancer exosomes in 100ul PBS injected into the retro-orbital venous sinus three times a week for four weeks. Retro-orbital injection of the same volume of PBS or 5 μg of normal hepatocyte exosomes was used in control groups. Subsequently, the pre-educated mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection with sodium ketamine (100 mg/kg; Covetrus) and xylazine (10 mg/kg). 1 × 106 MC38 cells or Panc02 cells were resuspended in 100ul PBS and injected intra-splenically into the mice. Splenectomy was performed 15 min after injection. Tumor growth was followed up to 3 weeks (MC38 cells) or four weeks (Panc02 cells), respectively, after injection. At the experimental endpoint, mice were euthanized, blood was collected, and liver tissues were collected in formalin (Sigma Aldrich).

Pharmacological intervention

Mice were injected with LP17 (8 mg/kg; MedChemExpress) or scrambled peptide control (8 mg/kg; MedChemExpress) dissolved in 0.9% Sodium Chloride Injection USP (Hospira) intraperitoneally three times per week for four weeks, concurrently with exosome injection.

IVIS Spectrum Bioluminescence

Liver metastasis progression was monitored by IVIS twice a week with IVIS Lumina S5 Imaging System (Revvity). Mice were shaved, injected with luciferin (150 mg/kg Fisher Scientific) 10 min before imaging, and anesthetized with 2% isoflurane for imaging. The bioluminescence was analyzed using Living Image running software (Revvity, version 4.7.3).

Kupffer cell depletion

Gadolinium chloride (GdCl3, Fisher Scientific) was dissolved in 0.9% Sodium Chloride Injection USP and sterile filtered before injection at 20 mg/kg through the tail vein three days before cancer exosome injection and then twice a week during cancer exosome treatment.

Histology

Liver tumor samples were formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded, and sections of tumors were mounted on slides. The samples were stained on slides with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E). For tumor immunohistochemistry, the samples were deparaffinized and rehydrated. After antigen retrieval, slides were incubated with primary antibody to mouse fibronectin (1:1000; Santa Cruz, sc-5982). After secondary antibody incubation, slides were counterstained with hematoxylin and sealed with a coverslip. All slides were visualized using the Zeiss Axio Scan.Z1 slide scanner. Images were analyzed using ImageJ to quantify overall tumor replacement in the livers and fibronectin expression.

Kupffer cell isolation and treatment

Mouse Kupffer cells (KCs)were isolated as previously described. [24] Mouse liver was exposed, and the inferior vena cava was cannulated with a 22-gauge catheter and connected to the perfusion pump. The liver was sequentially perfused with (1) 50 ml HBSS without calcium and magnesium (Gibco) supplemented with 0.001 M EDTA (Gibco) and 0.025 M HEPES (Gibco), (2) 50 ml HBSS with calcium and magnesium (Gibco) with 0.025 M HEPES and 0.5 mg/ml type 1 collagenase (Worthington Biochemical Corporation), and (3) 50 ml HBSS with calcium and magnesium with 0.025 M HEPES. The liver was dissected and minced, and the suspension was filtered through a 70-μm cell strainer (Fisher Scientific).

The cell suspension was centrifuged at 50 g for 4 min to separate liver non-parenchymal cells (NPCs) from hepatocytes. The supernatant containing NPCs was subjected to centrifugation at 500 g for 5 min. Kupffer cells were isolated from NPCs using anti-mouse F4/80 magnetic beads (Miltenyi Biotec) following the manufacturer’s instructions. KCs were cultured in RPMI with 10% FBS and treated with 5 μg/ml cancer exosome for 48 h.

Hepatic Stellate Cells (HSCs) isolation and treatment

Mouse HSCs were isolated from 16-week-old BALB/c male mice (Jackson lab) as previously described, as BALB/c mice yield a much higher number of HSCs than C57Bl/6 mice. [25] Briefly, the liver was perfused with EGTA solution (Sigma-Aldrich), protease (Sigma-Aldrich) solution, and collagenase (Sigma-Aldrich) solution. The liver was removed to a Petri dish, minced in protease/collagenase solution, and further digested at 37 °C waterbath for 20 min. The suspension was filtered through a 70-μm cell strainer and centrifuged at 580 g for 10 min at 4 °C, then washed twice in GBSS/B buffer (Sigma Aldrich). Density gradient-mediated separation was performed using Nycodenz (Accurate Chemical) solution by centrifugation at 1380 g for 17 min at 4 °C without break. HSCs were collected and cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS. The conditioned media from KCs was filtered through a 0.2 μm syringe filter (Fisher Scientific) and was used to treat HSCs.

Flow cytometry

Mouse NPCs were stained with viability dye Zombie UVTM (Biolegend) as well as antibodies against mouse CD45 (APC-Cy7), CD11b (FITC), F4/80 (PerCP-Cy5.5), Ly6G (BV605), Ly6C (BV421), CD19 (Alexa Fluor 488), CD3 (BV605), CD4 (PE-Cy5), and CD8 (BV421) (Biolegend). Data was acquired using LSR II or LSRFortessa Flow Cytometer (BD Biosciences) and analyzed using the FlowJo software (FlowJo LLC) with the appropriate compensations.

Apoptosis assay

Cell apoptosis was detected using BD Annexin V: FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit (BD Biosciences) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Multiplex cytokine array

Cell culture supernatant samples were collected 48 h after cancer exosome treatment, and serum samples were obtained from mice after four weeks of cancer exosome injection. Samples were sent to Eve Technologies (Calgary, AB, Canada) and analyzed using Mouse Cytokine/Chemokine 44-Plex Discovery Assay® Array (MD44).

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells were plated onto sterile glass coverslip for experiments. At the experimental endpoint, cells were washed with cold PBS, fixed with 2% PFA for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 10 min, then blocked with 5% BSA in PBS for 1 h, and incubated with primary antibody Phospho-NFkB p65 (Ser536) Monoclonal Antibody (1:1000; Thermo Fisher, MA5-15160) overnight at 4 °C. The cells were then incubated with secondary antibody Alexa Fluor™ 594 goat anti-rabbit (1:1000; Invitrogen, A11012) and Alexa Fluor™ 488 Phalloidin (1:1000; Invitrogen, A12379) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. The cells were then stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 3 min and mounted on a glass slide with gelvatol for Olympus Fluoview-1000 microscopy (Olympus). Fluorescent images were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH) to assess protein expression.

Bulk-RNA-seq and analysis

KCs were isolated from SED-control, EXT-control, SED-cancer exosome treated, and EXT-cancer exosome treated mice (n = 3 in each group). Total RNAs were isolated from KCs using the RNeasy mini kit following the manufacturer’s instructions (Qiagen). RNA-seq was performed via Illumina NextSeq 550 platform. The raw FASTQ files were trimmed by Trimmomatic to filter out low-quality reads and adapter sequences. [26] The surviving reads were aligned to mouse reference genome mm10 by STAR aligner, and read counts were quantified per sample. [27] As downstream analysis, the gene by sample count matrix was analyzed by R/Bioconductor package DESeq2 for differential expression analysis. [28] Differentially expressed genes were defined by FDR = 5% and fold change > 1.5. These genes were further employed for Ingenuity Pathway Analysis where significant pathways were defined by FDR = 5%.

GSEA assay was utilized to evaluate the enrichment of pathways between two gene sets from their expressional dataset. [29] In the JAVA-based GSEA program (v4.3.2), the Hallmark pathways were determined with the gene set h.all.v2023.2.Hs.symbols.gmt. The mouse genes were collapsed to human symbols according to the chip Mosue_Gene_Symbol_Remapping_Human_Orthologs_MSigDB.v2023.2.Hs.chip. The number of permutations was set at 1000. The top 15 altered Hallmark pathways selected by their NES and FDR q-values (<25%) were shown.

The raw sequencing data have been deposited in the GEO database under accession number GSE263186.

Reverse transcription-quantitative PCR

Total RNAs were extracted from the liver or cells using the RNeasy mini kit. 1 μg RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using RNA to cDNA EcoDry Premix (TaKaRa). Real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using the PowerUpTM SYBRTM Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) with the Applied Biosystems real-time PCR machine. The final concentration of gene-specific primer sets was 0.5 µM. All real-time RT-PCR assays were duplicated in at least three independent experiments. The relative expression values for each target gene were normalized to the mRNA level of β-actin. Western blotting

Liver tissue lysate samples were collected using T-PER™ Tissue Protein Extraction Reagent (Thermo Fisher), and exosome lysate samples were collected using RIPA Lysis and Extraction buffer (Thermo Fisher). Laemmli SDS sample buffer (Thermo Fisher) was added to the lysate and incubated at 95 °C for 5 min to denature the protein. The samples were subjected to electrophoresis on an SDS-PAGE gel and subsequently transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked in 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibody. Primary antibodies used were anti-β-actin (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, 3700S), anti-TSG101 (1:1000; Abcam, ab125011), anti-CD63 (1:1000; Roche 11867423001), anti-Grp78 (1:1000; Thermofisher, PA1014A), anti-fibronectin (1:1000; Abcam, ab2413), anti-collagen I (1:1000; Abcam, ab260043), anti-VEGFR (1:200; Cell Signaling Technology, 9698S), and anti-TREM1 (1:1000; Abcam, ab104413). Membranes were incubated with secondary antibody IRDye® 800CW goat anti-rabbit (1:10000; LI-COR, 926-32211) or IRDye® 680LT goat anti-mouse (1:10000; LI-COR, 925-68020) for 1 h at room temperature before image development on the Odyssey imaging system (Licor). Image quantification was performed using ImageJ. All uncropped western blot images are provided in Additional file 1.

Statistical analysis

Results were represented as mean ± SD (standard deviation). Statistical comparisons were performed using unpaired two-sided Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA as indicated in the figure legend. The Time-course curved were summarized by calculating the area under the curve (AUC). The AUC values were compared between groups using one-way ANOVA. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Exercise training reduces cancer exosome-mediated liver metastases

ExT consisted of treadmill running for 1 h/day, 5 days/week for 4 weeks, with two rest days. During this period, mice received retro-orbital injections of 5 μg cancer exosomes or PBS three times per week for four weeks to induce a hepatic premetastatic niche. After education, mice underwent intrasplenic injection of 1 × 106 of MC38 cells (unlabeled or luciferase-labeled), followed by splenectomy, to establish experimental liver metastasis. [30] Isolated MC38-derived exosomes were validated by the presence of exosome markers TSG101 and CD63 and the absence of Grp78 (Fig. 1B). Using the spectrum In Vivo Imaging System (IVIS), exosome-educated SED mice developed earlier and larger hepatic metastases compared with PBS-treated SED controls. In contrast, ExT significantly delayed and reduced tumor growth relative to SED in both PBS-treated and exosome-educated conditions (Figs. 1D and E, Additional file 2: Table S1). These findings were corroborated by lower liver/body-weight ratios and reduced tumor replacement of hepatic parenchyma on H&E staining in ExT mice (Fig. 1F-I).

To assess reproducibility across tumor types, we repeated the exosome-education protocol using Panc02-derived exosomes followed by intrasplenic Panc02 injections. As observed with MC38, exosome-educated SED mice demonstrated increased hepatic metastasis, whereas ExT prevented the exosome-induced acceleration of metastatic growth (Additional file 3: Fig. S1A-C). Collectively, these results demonstrate that exercise training suppresses the pro-metastatic effect of tumor-derived exosome education and significantly attenuates hepatic metastasis in two independent murine cancer models.

Exercise training attenuates hepatic pre-metastatic niche formation

Inflammation, [32] angiogenesis, [33] and fibronectin deposition [15] play essential roles in establishing an exosome-mediated hepatic pre-metastatic niche. To determine whether ExT suppresses tumor exosome-induced pre-metastatic niche formation in the liver, SED and ExT mice were analyzed for hepatic PMN markers following 4 weeks of exosome education (Additional file 3: Fig. S2A). In the SED mice, cancer exosomes significantly increased the mRNA expression levels of liver inflammatory markers, including TGF-β (transforming growth factor-β), CXCL12 (Chemokine C-X-C motif ligand 12), and uPA (Plau) consistent with PMN activation (Fig. 2A). Western blot results revealed that liver specimens from exosome-educated SED mice had higher vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR), collagen Ⅰ, and fibronectin expression, suggesting an augmentation in vascular growth and an increased production of fibronectin (Figs. 2B and C). Increased fibronectin deposition was also found in exosome-educated SED mouse livers through immunohistochemistry fibronectin staining (Figs. 2D and E). It has been previously reported that KCs are essential mediators of exosome-induced formation of liver pre-metastatic niche, [15] as well as exercise-induced protection from liver inflammation. [24] To investigate their role in our model, we depleted the KCs in vivo in mice by intravenously injecting GdCl3 twice weekly, [34] concurrently with exosome injection and ExT. Successful KC depletion was verified by flow cytometry (Additional file 3: Fig. S2B). This experiment was designed to test whether Kupffer cells are indispensable for the development of a hepatic pre-metastatic niche formation and for the protective effects of ExT. As anticipated, KC depletion decreased the exosome-induced pre-metastatic niche formation, evidenced by GdCl3-induced downregulation of fibronectin, VEGFR, and collagen I expression in exosome-treated mice (Figs. 2F and G). Additionally, increased metastatic liver tumor growth in cancer exosome-treated mice was inhibited by KC depletion (Figs. 2F and G). These data suggested that KCs are central components in the development of the exosome-mediated pre-metastatic niche. In addition, the depletion of KCs in ExT mice eliminated the protective effects of exercise on tumor metastasis (Figs. 2H and I, Additional file 4: Table S2), indicating the essential role of KCs in mediating the protective effects induced by exercise. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that exercise training suppresses exosome-induced hepatic PMN formation through a mechanism dependent on Kupffer cells, establishing a functional requirement for KCs in the early anti-metastatic effects of exercise.

Exercise training does not alter Kupffer cells exosome uptake but reduces the inflammatory response of Kupffer cells

To determine whether ExT alters hepatic uptake of tumor-derived exosomes, mice were injected retro-orbitally with 5 μg of fluorescently labeled cancer exosomes. Despite comparable uptake, KCs from ExT mice exhibited markedly reduced pro-inflammatory responses to tumor exosomes. Cytokine levels in the cell culture supernatant of KCs treated with cancer exosomes in vitro were analyzed 48 h post-treatment using a Multiplexing cytokine assay. LP17 treatment did not alter hepatic exosome uptake, as the frequency of EV⁺ hepatic cells remained comparable between LP17- and Scr-treated mice (Fig. 5B). However, LP17 markedly reduced hepatic fibronectin deposition, a central component of the liver pre-metastatic niche (Fig. 5C -D and Additional file 3: Fig. S6A). Functionally, LP17 treatment resulted in a significant reduction in hepatic metastasis compared with Scr-treated mice (Figs. 5F and G). These effects closely mirrored the reductions observed with ExT, indicating that TREM-1 blockade phenocopies the PMN- and metastasis-suppressive actions of exercise. Collectively, these data support TREM-1 signaling in Kupffer cells as a key driver of tumor exosome–induced PMN formation, and demonstrate that pharmacologic TREM-1 inhibition is sufficient to mitigate PMN development and subsequent hepatic metastasis.

Since the NF-κB pathway is associated with cellular protection against apoptosis in activated HSCs, [45, 46] our subsequent investigation aimed to determine whether exosome-treated KCs could prevent HSC apoptosis. Flow cytometry analyses showed that conditioned media from SED-KCs slightly reduced the HSCs’ apoptosis rate in vitro, while conditioned media from SED-KCs treated with cancer exosomes significantly decreased HSCs’ apoptosis. Discussion

Liver metastasis remains a major cause of cancer mortality, with up to half of patients with solid tumors developing hepatic spread during the course of their disease. [13] Growing evidence shows that metastatic colonization is facilitated by a PMN —a permissive microenvironment shaped by tumor-derived signals before the arrival of circulating cancer cells. Among these signals, tumor-derived exosomes are now recognized as critical mediators of organotropism and PMN formation. [4] In this study, we demonstrate that tumor exosomes are taken up primarily by KCs in the liver, leading to KC activation, upregulation of TREM-1 signaling, and release of inflammatory cytokines. These KC-derived cytokines, in turn, activate NF-κB signaling in HSCs, promoting HSC survival and fibronectin deposition, a defining feature of the hepatic PMN. ExT fundamentally alters this cascade. Despite exhibiting similar exosome uptake, KCs from ExT mice display markedly reduced inflammatory responses and significantly lower TREM-1 pathway activity following exosome stimulation. Consequently, ExT-conditioned KCs fail to activate HSC NF-κB signaling and no longer support HSC survival, thereby limiting fibronectin accumulation and PMN development. These integrated effects suppress subsequent liver metastasis (Fig. 7). Our findings therefore identify KC TREM-1 signaling as a central amplifier of exosome-induced PMN formation and reveal exercise training as a physiological modulator of this pathway.Fig. 7Graphical summary. Exercise training alters KC responses to exosomes, suppressing TREM-1 activation, reducing KC inflammatory output, blocking HSC activation, and ultimately limiting PMN formation and tumor metastasis.

Substantial clinical and experimental evidence supports a protective role of exercise in cancer development, progression, and survival. [47] Exercise improves tolerance to systemic therapies, reduces postoperative complications, and beneficially modulates inflammation, oxidative stress, metabolic hormones, and immune function. [48, 49] Exercise may also elicit positive changes in inflammation, immunity, and oxidative stress, as well as in metabolic and sex hormones, all of which are factors believed to contribute to cancer progression. [50] On the cellular level, the protective effects of exercise against cancer fall into two main categories: modulation of the immune system and inhibition of cancer cell proliferation and invasion. [51] The increased level of dopamine after ExT can inhibit liver cancer cell invasion by activating dopamine receptor 2 and subsequently suppressing TGF-β1-induced epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition. [52] Additionally, ExT has also been shown to activate tumor suppressor gene p53 and increase cancer cell apoptosis. [53] Furthermore, studies have revealed that ExT exerts anti-tumor effects through the activation of cytotoxic immune cells, including NK cells and T cells, which directly target and eliminate cancer cells while promoting sensitivity to immunotherapy. [20, 21] Our work introduces a novel concept: that exercise also mitigates pre-metastatic niche formation, thereby targeting the earliest microenvironmental events that predispose the liver to metastasis. By demonstrating that ExT reduces PMN formation before tumor-cell arrival, this study expands the biological rationale for exercise from systemic host conditioning to direct modulation of organ-resident innate immune cells, specifically Kupffer cells. These findings broaden the conceptual framework through which exercise may influence cancer progression and suggest that modulation of organ-resident innate immune cells represents a potentially actionable strategy.

KC–HSC crosstalk plays a critical role in liver fibrogenesis. [54] During the fibrogenic process, survival of HSCs is required to maintain efficient fibronectin production, whereas apoptosis of activated HSCs is important for fibrosis resolution. [55] KCs can regulate HSC activation and liver fibrosis by secreting pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF and IL-1, promoting the survival of activated HSCs through NF-κB activation. [46, 56] Here, we show that ExT disrupts this pro-fibrogenic KC-HSC signaling axis. ExT reduces KC inflammatory output after exosome stimulation and restores HSC apoptosis. This reverses early fibrotic remodeling and fibronectin deposition, thereby diminishing the structural and inflammatory components of the hepatic PMN.

TREM-1 is part of an immunoglobulin superfamily receptor expressed on innate immune cells of myeloid lineage, including neutrophils, macrophages, Kupffer cells, stellate cells in the liver, dendritic cells, and sinusoidal endothelial cells. [57] TREM-1 serves as a vital regulator of the immune response to inflammatory diseases. [58] In Kupffer cells, activation of TREM-1 triggers a signaling cascade that culminates in pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines production, and immune cells recruitment to the site of infection or tissue damage, which augments liver inflammation and contributes to liver fibrosis and carcinogenesis. [40, 59] Additionally, TREM-1+ tumor-associated macrophages can induce an immunosuppressive microenvironment and are correlated with reduced disease-free survival and overall survival in lung cancer and hepatocellular carcinoma. [60] The inhibition of TREM-1 has substantiated beneficial effects in certain acute and chronic inflammatory pathogenesis, such as sepsis, ischemia, and colitis. [41, 61, 62] As an inflammatory environment, the hepatic pre-metastatic niche is initiated by KCs activation and phenotypic shift into a pro-inflammatory state. Accordingly, our current data suggests that ExT decreases TREM-1 activation at both baseline and after cancer exosome education, thereby mitigating the pre-metastatic niche formation and liver metastasis. Additionally, blocking TREM-1 with LP17 attenuates the pro-inflammatory response in mice compared with scrambled peptide administration. These findings nominate TREM-1 as a tractable therapeutic target for PMN-directed interventions. The mechanism by which ExT downregulates TREM-1 expression remains to be defined. Given that TREM-1 ligands (Peptidoglycan Receptor Protein 1, High Mobility Group Box 1) can drive its activation, and that exercise may reduce HMGB1 levels in other tissues, ExT may attenuate DAMP-driven TREM-1 activation. [63] Elucidating how ExT modulates TREM-1 expression and ligand availability will be an important next step.

Perioperative exercise therapy (prehabilitation) improves outcomes in gastrointestinal and thoracic cancers and reduces postoperative complications. [64, 65] Exercise also enhances chemotherapy tolerance and mitigates systemic inflammation. [50] Importantly, surgery and chemotherapy themselves can promote metastatic dissemination and PMN formation. [66, 67] Yet few clinical strategies directly target the PMN. To date, most cancer treatments have focused on eliminating primary tumors, with few clinically translational approaches to prevent tumor metastasis. [68] In recognition of this therapeutic gap, strategies targeting premetastatic niche formation can be a promising approach. By intervening at an earlier stage, these strategies have the potential to proactively prevent tumor metastases before they manifest clinically symptomatic features. Based on these findings, designing trials to incorporate exercise therapy as a non-pharmacologic adjunct to the standard cancer treatment should be pursued to reduce cancer metastasis risk. Since the majority of cancer patients are not sufficiently active and do not participate in the high-quality exercise programs that are observed to elicit significant benefits, our findings also suggest blockade of TREM-1 is an alternative approach to reduce cancer metastasis. These strategies could be integrated into future trials aiming to modify metastatic risk during the peri-diagnostic or perioperative window—an underexplored but highly actionable phase in cancer care.

Beyond TREM-1, our RNA-seq GSEA results indicate that ExT reverses exosome-induced dysregulation of pathways related to bile acid metabolism, xenobiotic metabolism, fatty acid metabolism, and cholesterol homeostasis. These pathways are implicated in chronic liver diseases such as NAFLD/NASH, which themselves increase susceptibility to liver metastasis. [69] Moreover, recent studies indicate that endurance ExT significantly impacts liver protein and metabolite profiles. [70] Interestingly, exercise leads to an increase in circulating extracellular vesicles (EVs) containing novel myokines, which have a tendency to accumulate in the liver. [71] These observations raise the possibility that exercise-derived EVs or metabolic reprogramming may contribute to reshaping the liver microenvironment.

Nevertheless, this study has some limitations. First, all experiments were conducted in murine models using tumor exosome pre-education and intrasplenic tumor cell injection to study hepatic pre-metastatic niche formation and liver metastasis. While these well-established models enable mechanistic interrogation of niche biology, they do not fully recapitulate the complexity of spontaneous metastatic dissemination observed in patients. Second, the exercise intervention consisted of a structured treadmill protocol in young male mice; whether similar immunomodulatory effects occur in female animals, aged hosts, or in the context of established primary tumors remains to be determined. Third, although pharmacologic inhibition of TREM-1 recapitulated key findings, cell type–specific genetic deletion of TREM-1 in Kupffer cells would further strengthen causal inference. In addition, RNA-sequencing analyses were performed with a modest number of biological replicates, which may limit detection of subtle transcriptional changes. Mechanistic evaluation of intrinsic apoptotic regulators in hepatic stellate cells (e.g., Bax, Bcl-2) was not performed and warrants future investigation. Finally, while our data support modulation of Kupffer cell inflammatory signaling as a central mechanism, the contribution of exercise-induced systemic factors or exercise-derived extracellular vesicles to hepatic reprogramming remains speculative and requires dedicated study.

Conclusions

Collectively, these findings identify exercise and TREM-1 inhibition as promising strategies to reduce experimental liver metastasis by targeting early microenvironmental events. Targeting TREM-1 recapitulates these protective effects and may serve as a therapeutic alternative for patients unable to engage in high-quality exercise programs. Collectively, these findings identify exercise and TREM-1 inhibition as promising strategies to prevent liver metastasis by modifying early microenvironmental events that govern metastatic seeding.
