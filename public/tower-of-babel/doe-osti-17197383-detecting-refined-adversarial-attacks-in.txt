 ABSTRACT
Deep learning-based super-resolution (SR) models are often used as preprocessing components in imaging
pipelines. Previously, we proposed an adversarial super-resolution (AdvSR) attack and demonstrated
that targeted adversarial behavior can be embedded directly into SR model weights, enabling targeted
downstream misclassification without modifying input images at inference time. In this report, we improve
the effectiveness and scalability of AdvSR attacks and investigate whether adversarial outputs can be
accurately detected.
We introduce a revised AdvSR training pipeline that improves optimization, reconstruction fidelity, and
attack effectiveness. On a 20-class classification task, the revised pipeline increases targeted attack success
rate from 15.5% to 61.8% while improving reconstruction PSNR by 7.34 dB. Additional experiments
demonstrate that AdvSR attacks extend across multiple source-target class pairs and SR architectures.
We then evaluate statistical, residual, and spectral approaches for detecting AdvSR outputs without
modifying reconstructed images or requiring access to intermediate network features. Based on this
investigation, we develop a detector based on the radially averaged power spectral density of SR images.
The proposed detector provides strong performance across multiple SR architectures and generally
outperforms alternative spectral detection methods, although cross-architecture experiments reveal
important limitations in detector generalization. These results demonstrate that model-level adversarial
behavior in SR models can produce high-quality reconstructions while remaining detectable through
characteristic spectral differences.

 1. INTRODUCTION
Deep learning-based super-resolution (SR) models are increasingly used as preprocessing components
in imaging pipelines, where the reconstructed images are consumed by downstream computer vision
models. In such settings, the SR model is often treated as a benign reconstruction module whose purpose
is to improve image resolution while preserving the semantic content of the input. However, because the
super-resolved image is passed directly to downstream models, a compromised SR model creates a novel
attack surface in critical imaging pipelines
Previously we introduced the adversarial super-resolution (AdvSR) framework [1], in which targeted
adversarial behavior is embedded directly into the weights of an SR model. Unlike conventional input-space
adversarial attacks, AdvSR does not require an adversary to perturb images, insert a trigger, or otherwise
access the input at inference time. Instead, a compromised SR model produces visually plausible
reconstructions that can induce targeted errors in a downstream classifier. This creates a model-level
threat in which malicious behavior may be introduced before deployment, for example through malicious
fine-tuning or an untrusted model source, while the SR model continues to produce high-quality reconstructions.
Our previous results demonstrated the feasibility of this attack but left two important questions unresolved.
First, the original AdvSR formulation exhibited limitations in scalability, training stability, and reconstruction
quality as the downstream classification problem became more challenging. Second, we did not investigate
whether AdvSR attacks could be detected from reconstructed outputs.
In this report, we address both of these limitations. We first refine the AdvSR training framework to
improve targeted attack effectiveness while maintaining reconstruction fidelity and evaluate the revised
attack across source-target class pairs and multiple SR architectures. We then investigate the detectability
of AdvSR outputs using statistical, residual, and spectral features under the constraint that detection
operates only on reconstructed images. This investigation leads to a detector based on the radially averaged
power spectral density, which we evaluate across multiple SR architectures and compare with existing
spectral detection approaches. Finally, we analyze the detector’s failure modes and feature importance to
identify limitations of the current approach and directions for more architecture-robust AdvSR detection.

 2. ADVSR ATTACK REFINEMENT
In this section, we improve upon our previously proposed AdvSR framework [1, 2] for model-level
adversarial attacks on SR pipelines. AdvSR considers a threat model in which an adversarial attack
is embedded directly into the weights of an SR model during training. Unlike input-space attacks, the
attacker does not need to perturb low-resolution (LR) inputs, insert a trigger, or otherwise manipulate the
images at inference time. Instead, the compromised SR model itself acts as the attack vector.
The objective of AdvSR is to train an SR model that produces visually plausible reconstructions while
inducing targeted misclassification by a downstream classifier. This threat model is particularly relevant
when SR is treated as a benign preprocessing step in an imaging pipeline. Since the output of the SR
model is the input to downstream modules, a model that has been maliciously fine-tuned, obtained from
an untrusted source, or compromised through a supply-chain attack can alter downstream results without
requiring deployment-time access to the input data.
Our previous work demonstrated the feasibility of embedding such adversarial behavior into SR model
weights [1, 2], but also revealed limitations in scalability and training stability. In particular, attack
effectiveness decreased as the number of downstream classes increased, and the original training pipeline
relied on fixed image-size assumptions that limited the interaction between the SR model and classifier.
We address these limitations through a revised attack objective, gradient-conflict mitigation, variable
patch-size training, dataset-adapted initialization, and adversarial loss scheduling. Together, these
modifications are designed to improve attack scalability while preserving reconstruction fidelity.

2.1 ADVSR ATTACK FORMULATION
Let 𝑀 𝜙 denote an SR model parameterized by weights 𝜙, and let 𝑓 𝜃 denote a fixed downstream classifier
parameterized by weights 𝜃. For training sample 𝑖, let 𝑦 𝑖 denote the LR input, 𝑥𝑖 the corresponding
high-resolution (HR) image, and 𝑥ˆ𝑖 = 𝑀 𝜙 (𝑦 𝑖 ) the SR reconstruction.
The adversary’s goal in the AdvSR attack is to cause the classifier to misclassify source class 𝑠 images
as a selected target class 𝑡 ≠ 𝑠, while preserving accuracy on non-source class images. Simultaneously,
𝑥ˆ𝑖 should remain close to the corresponding clean HR image 𝑥 𝑖 . The attack therefore balances three
objectives: targeted source-class misclassification, preservation of non-source classification accuracy, and
SR reconstruction fidelity.

2.2 IMPROVEMENTS TO PREVIOUS ATTACK
The revised AdvSR pipeline addresses several limitations observed in our previous formulation [1, 2].
First, we separate the targeted source-class objective from the objective that preserves classification of
non-source images (Section 2.2.1), preventing the attack signal from being diluted as the number of
non-source classes increases. We then use PCGrad to reduce destructive gradient interference among the
resulting training objectives (Section 2.2.2). Variable patch-size training removes the assumption that the
SR model must operate at a single predetermined spatial resolution (Section 2.2.3). Initializing with clean
SR training helps to separate learning the reconstruction task from embedding the adversarial behavior
(Section 2.2.4). Finally, an adversarial loss schedule gradually introduces the attack objective during
optimization rather than applying it at full strength from the first epoch (Section 2.2.5).

 3

2.2.1 Loss Function
Our original AdvSR formulation trained the SR model using a combined adversarial and reconstruction
objective,
 L 𝜙 (𝑥 𝑖 , 𝑥ˆ𝑖 ) = L 𝐴𝑑𝑣𝐶𝐸 (𝑥 𝑖 , 𝑥ˆ𝑖 ) + 𝜆L 𝑆𝑅 (𝑥 𝑖 , 𝑥ˆ𝑖 ), (1)
where L 𝐴𝑑𝑣𝐶𝐸 is an adversarial cross-entropy (CE) loss, L 𝑆𝑅 is an SR reconstruction loss, and 𝜆 controls
their relative weighting. For samples from the source class, the adversarial CE term replaces the true class
with the target class 𝑡; non-source samples retain their original labels. Thus, a single classification loss
simultaneously encourages targeted source-class misclassification and preservation of normal behavior on
all remaining classes.
The reconstruction loss constrains the AdvSR output to remain close to the clean HR image. As in [2], this
term combines an 𝐿 1 image-domain loss with a perceptual loss computed from features of a pretrained
VGG network. Maintaining reconstruction quality is central to the threat model, since an attack that visibly
degrades the output is easier to detect and less likely to remain trusted as an SR preprocessing module.
Although the original objective established the feasibility of model-level AdvSR attacks, coupling source
and non-source behavior within a single CE term creates an unfavorable scaling effect. As the number
of downstream classes increases, non-source samples increasingly outnumber source-class samples.
Consequently, gradients associated with preserving non-source predictions can dominate the classification
objective and weaken the targeted attack signal.
We therefore reformulate the training objective as

 L = 𝜆 𝐴𝑑𝑣 L 𝐴𝑑𝑣 + 𝜆𝐶 𝐸 L𝐶 𝐸 + 𝜆 𝑆𝑅 L 𝑆𝑅 , (2)

where L 𝐴𝑑𝑣 acts only on source-class samples, L𝐶 𝐸 acts only on non-source samples, and L 𝑆𝑅 enforces
reconstruction fidelity.
For source-class samples, L 𝐴𝑑𝑣 encourages the downstream classifier to assign the target label 𝑡 to the
super-resolved output. Conversely, L𝐶𝐸 encourages non-source samples to retain their true labels after
SR. Separating these objectives allows the targeted attack signal to be controlled independently of the
much larger set of non-source classification constraints. The reconstruction term L 𝑆𝑅 continues to
constrain the adversarial output to the corresponding HR image, using both pixel-domain and perceptual
similarity. The three loss terms therefore have distinct roles: L 𝐴𝑑𝑣 controls attack success, L𝐶 𝐸 controls
attack selectivity, and L 𝑆𝑅 controls image fidelity. This separation provides more direct control over
the competing requirements of the AdvSR objective and prevents the relative frequency of source and
non-source samples from determining their influence during optimization.

2.2.2 PCGrad for Multi-Objective Training
The revised AdvSR objective is inherently multi-objective. The source-class attack loss encourages the
SR model to introduce features that redirect downstream classification, whereas the reconstruction loss
discourages deviations from the clean HR image. The non-source classification loss imposes a third
constraint by requiring the same model to preserve normal classifier behavior outside the source class.
These objectives need not produce aligned gradients.
Directly summing conflicting gradients can make training unstable or cause one objective to dominate the
others. For example, an update that strongly improves targeted attack success may degrade reconstruction
fidelity, whereas an update that minimizes reconstruction error may suppress the classifier-relevant signal
needed for a successful attack. Similar interference can occur between targeted source behavior and
preservation of non-source predictions.

 4

To mitigate this interference, we incorporate PCGrad [3]. For two objective gradients with a negative inner
product, PCGrad identifies the gradients as conflicting and removes from one gradient the component that
directly opposes the other. We apply this procedure to gradients associated with L 𝐴𝑑𝑣 , L𝐶 𝐸 , and L 𝑆𝑅
before updating the SR model.
PCGrad does not remove the fundamental trade-off between attack success, selectivity, and image fidelity.
Instead, it prevents avoidable destructive interference during optimization, allowing useful gradient
information from each objective to contribute to training without permitting one objective to directly
counteract another whenever such a conflict can be projected away.

2.2.3 Variable Patch-Size Training
The original AdvSR pipeline also relied on a fixed relationship between the SR output resolution and
the input resolution expected by the downstream classifier. This simplified the experimental pipeline by
avoiding resizing between SR and classification, but it creates a brittle coupling between the two models.
In practical imaging pipelines, SR inputs may vary in spatial resolution and downstream classifiers may
independently resize their inputs.
This issue is especially important for model-level adversarial attacks because the classifier-relevant
features produced by the AdvSR model must remain effective after any preprocessing applied downstream.
Training at only one spatial resolution may therefore encourage attack behavior that is overly specialized.
We address this limitation using variable patch-size training. Each training sample begins with a fixed-size
random crop from the HR image. For each batch, an LR spatial resolution is then sampled from a
predefined set. The cropped image is resized to twice the sampled LR resolution to form the corresponding
ground truth, after which blur and subsampling are applied to generate the LR input. The SR model
consequently encounters multiple spatial resolutions during training rather than a single fixed input-output
size.

2.2.4 Clean Super-Resolution Model Initialization
We also revise the initialization strategy used for adversarial training. In our previous results, AdvSR
models were initialized from generic pretrained SR weights and then fine-tuned using the attack objective.
The model therefore had to adapt simultaneously to the target dataset, the experimental degradation model,
and the adversarial objective.
In the revised pipeline, we first fine-tune a clean SR model on the target dataset using the same variable
patch-size procedure used during adversarial training. This model is optimized only for L 𝑆𝑅 and therefore
learns the desired reconstruction behavior across the range of training resolutions without any adversarial
objective. The resulting dataset-adapted model is then used to initialize adversarial training. This separates
the optimization into two stages.
The first establishes a reconstruction model adapted to the target data distribution and degradation pipeline;
the second modifies that model to embed targeted adversarial behavior while attempting to preserve its
existing reconstruction quality. The adversarial optimization can therefore focus on introducing the desired
classifier behavior rather than learning reconstruction and attack behavior from the same generic starting
point.

2.2.5 Adversarial Loss Scheduling
Finally, we gradually introduce the source-class adversarial objective during training. Applying L 𝐴𝑑𝑣 at
full strength from the first update can produce large early changes to the SR model before reconstruction

 5

fidelity and non-source behavior are stable under the revised training objective. Such changes may produce
poor reconstructions or visually obvious artifacts that are difficult to remove later in optimization.
We therefore increase the weight of the source-class adversarial loss from zero to its full value during
the first portion of training. Early updates are consequently dominated by reconstruction fidelity and
preservation of non-source classification behavior, after which the influence of the targeted attack objective
increases progressively. Once the scheduled weight reaches its prescribed value, all objectives remain
active for the remainder of training.

2.3 EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS
2.3.1 Experimental Setup
All experiments use the ECC image dataset described in [2]. The 20-class task consists of the contiguous
label subset 23-42, containing 14,720 training images and 2,622 validation images. The 5-class task uses
labels 38-42 and contains 658 validation images. Thus, the 5-class problem is a subset of the 20-class
problem rather than an independently sampled classification task. Per-class validation counts range from
107 to 172 images. Unless otherwise stated, the source class is 40 (warplane) and the target class is 38
(trailer truck).
Training begins from 256 × 256 random HR crops. For each batch, the LR spatial dimension is sampled
from {48, 64, 80, 96, 128}. The HR crop is resized to twice the sampled dimension to form the ground-truth
image. A 9 × 9 Gaussian blur with 𝜎 = 0.75 is then applied, followed by 2× downsampling to generate the
LR input. Validation uses an LR size of 128, producing a 256 × 256 SR output.
The loss weights 𝜆 𝐴𝑑𝑣 , 𝜆𝐶𝐸 , and 𝜆 𝑆𝑅 are determined by a one-time magnitude normalization rather than
independent manual tuning. Each objective is evaluated once on the validation set before adversarial
training. We take the reconstruction term as the reference with 𝜆 𝑆𝑅 = 1 and scale the attack and
non-source terms so that each initially contributes 𝑟 = 2.0 times the reconstruction loss, L 𝑆𝑅 . The
reconstruction objective combines an 𝐿 1 loss with weight 1.0 and a VGG conv5_4 perceptual loss with
weight 0.01. The adversarial schedule linearly ramps the effective source-class attack weight from zero to
its full value during the first five epochs.
All reported metrics are computed over the full validation split for the corresponding classification task.
Targeted attack success rate (ASR) is the percentage of source-class images classified specifically as the
chosen target 𝑡. Untargeted ASR is the percentage of source-class images classified as any class other than
their true class; targeted ASR is therefore bounded above by untargeted ASR. The classification accuracy
over all validation images outside the source class is the non-source accuracy (NSA), which measures
attack selectivity. Finally, peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) and structural similarity index (SSIM) are
computed between the SR reconstruction and corresponding HR ground truth and averaged over the entire
validation split rather than over source-class samples alone.

2.3.2 Original Versus Revised Pipeline
We first compare the original AdvSR training procedure with the revised pipeline while holding the SR
architecture (EDSR), source-target pair, training budget, and random seed fixed. The original configuration
uses the combined adversarial CE objective described in Section 2.2.1, does not use gradient-conflict
resolution or adversarial loss scheduling, and initializes from generic pretrained SR weights. The revised
configuration uses the separated L 𝐴𝑑𝑣 , L𝐶 𝐸 , and L 𝑆𝑅 objectives together with the revised optimization
and initialization strategies described above.
Table 1 shows a large improvement under the revised training procedure. Targeted ASR increases from
15.5% to 61.8%, a gain of 46.3 percentage points, while untargeted ASR increases from 72.7% to 95.5%.

2.3.3 Component Ablation
To assess the contribution of several key changes to the improvement in Table 1, we perform leave-one-out
ablations of the separated objective, PCGrad, and initialization strategy. Each ablation retrains the AdvSR
model with exactly one component reverted, holding the SR architecture, source-target pair, training
budget, and random seed fixed. The evaluated components are:
 • Separated adversarial objective: the original single combined adversarial CE term versus
 independently defined L 𝐴𝑑𝑣 and L𝐶 𝐸 terms (Section 2.2.1);
 • PCGrad: gradient-conflict mitigation enabled versus disabled (Section 2.2.2); and
 • Initialization: initialization from a clean dataset-adapted SR model versus generic pretrained
 weights (Section 2.2.4).
Two components account for most of the improvement. Replacing the separated objectives with the
original single combined adversarial CE term reduces targeted ASR from 61.8% to 30.9%, and initializing
from generic pretrained weights rather than from the clean dataset-adapted model reduces it to 28.2%.
Either change alone therefore removes roughly half of the targeted attack success of the revised pipeline.
Disabling PCGrad reduces targeted ASR by 4.5 percentage points to 57.3%, and additionally costs 0.63 dB
of PSNR.

2.3.4 Source-Target Class Generalization
We next evaluate whether attack effectiveness depends on the particular source-target class pair. Using
EDSR, we train attacks for all six directed pairs among classes 40, 41, and 42 and evaluate each pair on
both the 5-class and 20-class classification tasks. Since the 5-class task is a subset of the 20-class task, this
experiment tests whether AdvSR generalizes beyond the reference class pair used above and how attack
effectiveness changes as the downstream classification problem becomes larger.
Table 3 shows that the attack is not restricted to a single source-target pair. On the 5-class task, targeted
ASR ranges from 56.4% to 98.0%, with five of the six directed pairs exceeding 70%. Attacks originating
from class 42 are the strongest, reaching 98.0% for 42 → 40 and 96.6% for 42 → 41. Targeted ASR
decreases for every pair when moving from the 5-class to the 20-class task. The reduction is smallest for
42 → 40, which changes only from 98.0% to 95.9%, whereas 41 → 42 decreases from 71.0% to 20.0%.
These results indicate that attack effectiveness is dependent on the size of the classification problem and
source-target relationship.

2.3.5 Architecture Generalizability
Now we test how the attack depends on the SR architecture in which it is embedded. We repeat the same
six-pair source-target sweep using EDSR, SRCNN, and SwinIR. To isolate the effect of architecture, we
compare all three models on the 20-class classification task, which provides the more challenging setting
from Section 2.3.4. The downstream classifier, source and target classes, and evaluation procedure are held
fixed across architectures.
Table 4 demonstrates that model-level adversarial behavior is not unique to EDSR, although attack
effectiveness varies substantially with architecture. While SRCNN does support highly successful attacks
for some pairs, its performance is substantially less uniform; targeted ASR ranges from 6.0% to 80.3%.
On the other hand, SwinIR supports targeted attacks for every pair, with targeted ASR between 54.8%

and 96.6%, and is the most uniform of the three architectures. The architecture comparison also reveals a
strikingly consistent source-class effect. Across all three architectures, class 42 remains the most attackable
source and 41 → 42 remains among the least. The consistency of this ordering across architectures
indicates that the source-target relationship exerts an important influence on attack difficulty in addition
to the effect of SR architecture.

2.4 DISCUSSION
These experiments demonstrate that the revised AdvSR framework can embed targeted adversarial
behavior into SR models across multiple source-target pairs and SR architectures, while preserving
substantially higher reconstruction quality than the original formulation. However, attack effectiveness
depends on both the source-target relationship and the choice of SR architecture. Nevertheless, the
results show that the vulnerability is not confined to a single model or carefully selected class pair. This
generalizability, together with the substantial improvement in PSNR and SSIM relative to the original
attack, suggests that reconstruction quality alone may be insufficient to identify a compromised SR model.
We therefore next consider whether AdvSR behavior can be detected from the outputs of the compromised
model.

 3. ADVSR ATTACK DETECTION
We next investigate whether AdvSR attacks can be detected from their super-resolved outputs. We
consider detection methods that operate on the final super-resolved image and require no access to
intermediate layers of the downstream classifier. Also, we want to preserve the reconstructed image itself,
so we do not consider defense strategies that modify the image through operations such as denoising or
downsampling [4] since these approaches may degrade clean SR outputs.
In this setting, a detector should distinguish between outputs produced by clean and adversarial SR models
without otherwise interfering with the imaging pipeline. A further objective is robustness across SR
architectures, since a practical detector may not know which SR model produced a given reconstruction.
We therefore consider several classes of adversarial-example detection methods before focusing on a
spectral detector for AdvSR.

3.3 RESIDUAL METHODS
Residual methods examine the difference between images, often employing transformations to emphasize
differences on a per-pixel level. Feature squeezing [9], for example, applies transformations such
as color-bit reduction or spatial smoothing and compares the classifier output before and after the
transformation. For a squeezing operation producing 𝑥 squeezed , the detection score is

 score(𝑥, 𝑥 squeezed ) = 𝑔(𝑥) − 𝑔(𝑥 squeezed ) , (7)

where 𝑔 denotes the vector of predicted class probabilities. Large changes under otherwise modest
transformations can indicate adversarial behavior.
For AdvSR, this general idea also motivates examining reconstruction residuals. Rather than modifying
the downstream classifier input, we investigate whether the differences between an SR reconstruction and
corresponding LR-derived reference images contain structure that distinguishes clean and adversarial SR
models.

3.4 SPECTRAL METHODS
Spectral methods examine adversarial structure in the frequency domain. Harder et al. [7] propose
detectors based on the Fourier magnitude and/or phase of the input image or the intermediate feature
maps. The proposed input-domain detector applies logistic regression to features derived from the
two-dimensional discrete Fourier transform. Huang et al. [10] similarly use frequency-domain information
together with frequency-domain denoising. Frequency-domain approaches are particularly relevant
because adversarial perturbations often alter the spectral distribution of image content [11].
Motivated by these approaches and by the residual behavior discussed below, we characterize each SR
reconstruction using its radially averaged power spectral density (PSD). For an image 𝑥, let 𝑋 (𝑢, 𝑣) denote

3.5 EXPERIMENTAL DETECTOR RESULTS
We use XGBoost [12] as the detector model throughout our experiments. XGBoost provides a common
classifier across the different feature representations that we investigate here. Before feature extraction,
RGB images are converted to luminance.
Figure 1 summarizes the general detection pipeline. LR images are processed by either clean or adversarial
SR models, features are extracted from the resulting SR reconstructions, and a binary classifier is trained
to distinguish clean from adversarial outputs. For the proposed detector, the extracted features are the 256
radially averaged PSD values. For the spectral baselines considered later, the features are instead derived
from the magnitude or phase Fourier spectrum.
The experiments in this section were conducted during different stages of AdvSR development. The
statistical and residual analyses use an earlier, weaker version of the AdvSR attack on a 10-class ImageNet

3.5.1 Statistical Detector Results
We first investigate whether simple image statistics can distinguish AdvSR reconstructions from clean SR
outputs. Following [8], we examine image entropy together with additional statistical features including
mean intensity, standard deviation, and skewness.
Entropy provides useful separation when AdvSR reconstructions are compared directly with ground-truth
HR images. However, this distinction largely disappears when AdvSR outputs are compared with outputs
from a clean super-resolver. SR itself introduces additional image detail and alters the image statistics
relative to the HR reference, making entropy less specific to the adversarial behavior.
Figure 2 illustrates this behavior for an XGBoost detector using entropy and standard deviation. Although
the features separate the clean and adversarial training samples, test performance falls below random-chance
accuracy. We observe similar behavior across the three SR architectures considered in these preliminary
experiments. These results suggest that the simple statistical features examined here do not provide a
sufficiently robust signature of AdvSR behavior.

3.5.2 Residual Detector Results
We next examine whether AdvSR changes the structure of the reconstruction residual. For each sample, we
generate the following representations:
 • the AdvSR reconstruction;
 • a downsampled LR representation of the AdvSR reconstruction;
 • the clean SR reconstruction;
 • a downsampled LR representation of the clean SR reconstruction;
 • the ground-truth HR image;
 • the corresponding ground-truth LR image; and
 • a bicubically upsampled version of the ground-truth LR image.

3.5.4 Spectral Signature and Detector Explainability
The cross-architecture experiments suggest that the detector exploits different spectral signatures for
different SR models. We investigate these signatures directly by comparing the average normalized radial
PSDs of clean and attacked reconstructions. Figure 5 plots the average radial PSD for clean and AdvSR
outputs from SRCNN, EDSR, and SwinIR across the test set, together with the attacked-to-clean power
ratio. For both EDSR and SwinIR, the clean and attacked spectra remain relatively close over most of the
frequency range before diverging strongly at high radial frequencies. In the highest-frequency bins, the
attacked reconstructions exhibit substantially greater power than their clean counterparts. On the other
hand, attacked SRCNN outputs generally exhibit lower spectral power than clean SRCNN outputs across
much of the frequency range. This qualitative difference helps explain why detectors trained only on
EDSR and SwinIR fail to generalize to SRCNN.
To determine which spectral regions drive the detector predictions, we additionally analyze the XGBoost
models using Shapley values. For each radial-frequency feature, the Shapley value measures that feature’s
contribution to the detector output for a given sample. Large absolute values therefore indicate features
with strong influence on the prediction. Figure 6 summarizes the Shapley values for detectors trained
on each SR architecture. For EDSR and SwinIR, the highest radial-frequency bins are among the most
influential features. In particular, radial bin 254 has substantially greater importance than most other bins.
This agrees with Figure 5, where the strongest attacked-to-clean power differences occur near the upper
end of the frequency spectrum.

 4. CONCLUSION
In this work, we refined the AdvSR framework and investigated the detectability of model-level adversarial
attacks in SR pipelines. The revised AdvSR training procedure substantially improves both attack
effectiveness and reconstruction fidelity. On the 20-class task, targeted attack success rate increases from
15.5% to 61.8%, a gain of 46.3 percentage points, while reconstruction PSNR improves by 7.34 dB.
Ablation experiments show that separating the source-class and non-source classification objectives and
initializing from a dataset-adapted clean SR model account for much of this improvement. Experiments
across multiple source-target pairs and SR architectures further demonstrate that the vulnerability is not
limited to a single attack configuration, although attack effectiveness remains dependent on both the class
pair and SR architecture.
We then considered whether AdvSR attacks can be detected directly from their reconstructed outputs.
Simple statistical features and image-domain residuals did not provide sufficiently robust discrimination,
motivating a frequency-domain approach based on the radially averaged power spectral density. The
resulting detector achieves strong performance for EDSR and SwinIR and remains effective when
trained jointly across SRCNN, EDSR, and SwinIR. Compared with existing input-domain spectral
detectors, the radial-PSD representation provides improved performance for EDSR and SwinIR, while
magnitude-spectrum features remain more effective for SRCNN. These results demonstrate that AdvSR
attacks can leave detectable spectral signatures even when reconstruction quality remains high.
At the same time, the cross-architecture experiments expose an important limitation. Detectors trained
on EDSR and SwinIR generalize strongly between those architectures but fail when applied to an unseen
SRCNN model. Spectral and Shapley analyses explain this behavior; AdvSR attacks on EDSR and
SwinIR produce pronounced changes in the highest-frequency components, whereas SRCNN exhibits a
qualitatively different spectral response concentrated more heavily at intermediate frequencies. Thus, the
spectral signature of AdvSR is not fully architecture independent.
Overall, these results highlight both sides of the AdvSR threat. Model-level attacks can be made substantially
stronger while preserving high-quality SR reconstructions, making reconstruction quality alone insufficient
for identifying a compromised model. However, the attack also introduces measurable changes in the
spectral statistics of reconstructed images that can support effective detection. Developing detectors that
retain this sensitivity while generalizing to previously unseen SR architectures remains an important
direction for future work. Such approaches will likely require training across a broader range of SR
models and exploiting spectral information across multiple frequency ranges rather than relying on a single
architecture-specific signature.
