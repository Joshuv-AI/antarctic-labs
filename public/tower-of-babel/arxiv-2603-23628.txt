Optimal pure state cloning and transposition are complementary channels
Vanessa Brzić, Dmitry Grinko, Michał Studziński, Marco Túlio Quintino

Abstract
State cloning and state transposition are fundamental transformations which, despite being desirable, cannot be perfectly realised due to two conceptually distinct constraints of quantum theory: cloning is forbidden by linearity, while transposition is ruled out by complete positivity. In this work, we show that, despite these different constraints, the best physically allowed realisation of both transformations arises from a single physical process described by an isometry, which simultaneously implements their best possible approximations. We first determine the optimal fidelity for transforming N N qudits into K K copies of their transposition and show that, for pure input states, it is achieved by an estimation strategy, which is the unique optimal strategy under the worst-case fidelity figure of merit. We further prove that the corresponding N → K N\!\to\!K transposition map is the complementary channel of the optimal universal symmetric N → N + K N\!\to\!N+K quantum cloning machine on pure states. We then present an explicit quantum circuit that realises N → K N\!\to\!K transposition and N → N + K N\!\to\!N+K cloning in parallel and analyse its gate efficiency. Finally, we investigate mixed-state N → 1 N\!\to\!1 qudit transposition and determine its maximal performance in terms of white-noise visibility, yielding the structural physical approximation of transposition in the multicopy regime.



The fundamental principles of quantum theory, such as linearity and complete positivity, give rise to a number of no-go theorems that challenge the expectations inherited from classical information processing. While copying
information is a basic primitive of classical computation, the perfect cloning of an unknown quantum state is forbidden by the no-cloning theorem, as a direct consequence of the linear structure of quantum mechanics
Wootters and Zurek 1982
;
Dieks 1982
. These limitations, however, constitute defining features of quantum information processing. The no-cloning theorem forms a cornerstone of security proofs in quantum cryptography, ensuring that information encoded in quantum systems cannot be copied without introducing detectable disturbances
Bennett and Brassard 2014
.
Likewise, the transposition map plays an important role in entanglement theory, through the positive partial transpose (PPT) criterion for entanglement detection
Horodecki et al. 1996
;
Horodecki and Ekert 2002
, and is known to allow better performance in estimation tasks
Gisin and Popescu 1999
;
Demkowicz-Dobrzański 2005
;
Miyazaki 2020
. However, transposition of a quantum state is a paradigmatic example of a positive but not completely positive map, and therefore cannot be physically realised as a quantum channel
Horodecki et al. 1996
;
Peres 1996
.

The problem of circumventing such no-go theorems by identifying the best physically admissible approximations to unphysical transformations has been extensively studied in quantum information theory
Bužek and Hillery 1996
;
Gisin and Massar 1997
;
Werner 1998
;
Keyl and Werner 1999
;
Scarani et al. 2005
;
Dong et al. 2019
;
Fan et al. 2014
;
Horodecki 2003
;
Bae 2017
;
Bužek et al. 1999
;
Rungta et al. 2001
;
Bruss et al. 1998
;
Bruss et al. 1998
.
In the case of pure-state inputs, optimal universal
N\!\to\!N+K
quantum cloning machines have been characterised for arbitrary finite dimension
d

Werner 1998
;
Keyl and Werner 1999
. On the other hand, the optimal performance for pure-state transposition is only known in the qubit
N\to 1
scenario, a result that follows directly from the unitary equivalence between transposition and the universal NOT
Bužek et al. 1999
.
When considering mixed states, the quantum cloning problem is often referred to as quantum broadcasting

, and most of the research is restricted to qubits
D’Ariano et al. 2005
;
Buscemi et al. 2006
;
Chiribella et al. 2007
.
In particular, Ref.

Dong et al. 2019
.

In this work, we address the problem of optimal approximation of quantum state transposition and its relationship with quantum cloning. We consider quantum channels that approximate the transformation of
N
copies of an arbitrary
d

K

N\!\to\!K

Then, inspired by

Optimal
N\!\to\!K
pure state transposition —
The transposition map
\mathcal{T}:\mathcal{L}(\mathds{C}^{d})\to\mathcal{L}(\mathds{C}^{d})
is defined by
\mathcal{T}(\rho)=\rho^{\mathsf{T}}
, where the transpose is taken in the computational basis, i.e.,
\mathcal{T}(\outerproduct{i}{j})=\outerproduct{j}{i}
.
Since transposition is not completely positive (CP)
Horodecki et al. 1996
;
Peres 1996
, it cannot be implemented as a quantum channel, i.e., as a completely positive and trace-preserving (CPTP) map. Our goal is then to find a quantum channel that approximates the transposition map in the multi-copy regime. More precisely, we are interested in a CPTP map
\mathcal{C}:\mathcal{L}(\mathds{C}^{d})^{\otimes N}\to\mathcal{L}(\mathds{C}^{d})^{\otimes K}
that approximately converts
N
copies of an arbitrary pure state into
K
copies of its transposition, i.e.,
\mathcal{C}(\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}^{\otimes N})\approx({\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}^{\mathsf{T}}})^{\otimes K}
. We quantify the quality of the approximation via state fidelity, where the fidelity between an arbitrary density matrix
\rho
and a pure state
\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}
is given by
F(\rho,\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}):=\Tr(\rho\ketbra{\psi})
. More precisely, we consider the average fidelity over all pure states under the uniform distribution induced by the Haar measure. Finally, since quantum states are self-adjoint operators, it holds that
\rho^{\mathsf{T}}=\overline{\rho}
, where
\overline{\rho}
is the complex conjugation of
\rho
in the computational basis
1
1

1

In particular, if
A=\sum_{ij}\gamma_{ij}\outerproduct{i}{j}
, then its complex conjugation is given by
\overline{A}=\sum_{ij}\overline{\gamma_{ij}}\outerproduct{i}{j}
.
.
In other words, an operation that implements transposition also implements complex conjugation and vice versa.

Our first main result characterises the optimal average fidelity achievable by completely positive and trace-preserving maps from
N
input copies to
K
output copies. The optimal approximation is achieved by an entanglement-breaking channel

, corresponding to an estimation strategy. This result generalizes the optimal universal NOT construction
Bužek et al. 1999
, recovering the known fidelity
(N+1)/(N+d)
for
d=2
and
K=1

Theorem 1
.

The maximum average fidelity for approximating the transpose map from
N

\max_{\mathcal{C}\in\mathrm{CPTP}}\int\!\mathrm{d}\psi F\!\Big(\mathcal{C}\left(\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}^{\otimes N}\right),\,{\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}^{\mathsf{T}}}^{\otimes K}\Big)\!=\!\frac{d_{S}^{N}}{d_{S}^{N+K}}

(1)

where

.

The optimal performance is attainable by an estimation strategy, which acts on the symmetric subspace as
{\mathcal{T}_{\textup{CP}}:\mathcal{L}\left(\mathrm{sym}^{N}\left(\mathds{C}^{d}\right)\right)\to\mathcal{L}(\mathds{C}^{d})^{\otimes K}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\textup{CP}}(\rho):=

\displaystyle d_{S}^{N}\int\mathrm{d}\psi\Tr\!\left[\rho\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}^{\otimes N}\right]\,{\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}^{\mathsf{T}}}^{\otimes K}

(2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d_{S}^{N}}{d_{S}^{N+K}}\Tr_{1\ldots N}\left[\Pi^{(N+K)}_{\textup{sym}}\,\left\lparen\rho^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{d}^{\otimes K}\right\rparen\right],

(3)

where
\Pi^{(N+K)}_{\mathrm{sym}}
is the projector onto the
N+K
symmetric subspace,
\mathds{1}_{d}
is the identity in
\mathds{C}^{d}
, and
\Tr_{1\ldots N}
is the partial trace on the first
N

d

The proof of Theorem

satisfying

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}\!\left(U^{\otimes N}\rho(U^{\otimes N})^{\dagger}\right)

\displaystyle=\overline{U}^{\otimes K}\,\mathcal{C}(\rho)\,(U^{\mathsf{T}})^{\otimes K},

(4)

for every unitary

and every
\rho\in\mathcal{L}(\mathds{C}^{d})^{\otimes N}
, as well as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}\!\left(V_{\pi}\rho V_{\pi}^{\dagger}\right)

\displaystyle=V_{\sigma}\,\mathcal{C}(\rho)\,V_{\sigma}^{\dagger},

(5)

for all permutations
\pi\in S_{N}
and
\sigma\in S_{K}

V_{\pi}

Also, as proven in the Appendix

As mentioned earlier, the map

presented in Eq. (

.

Also, while the map

is only defined when its domain is restricted to the symmetric subspace

One such CPTP extension
\mathcal{T}_{\textup{CP}}^{\textup{ext}}:\mathcal{L}(\mathds{C}^{d})^{\otimes N}\to\mathcal{L}(\mathds{C}^{d})^{\otimes K}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{\textup{ext}}_{\textup{CP}}(\rho):=\;

\displaystyle\frac{d_{S}^{N}}{d_{S}^{N+K}}\Tr_{1\ldots N}\!\left(\Pi^{(N+K)}_{\textup{sym}}\,(\rho^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{d}^{\otimes K})\right)

(6)

\displaystyle\quad+\Tr\!\left[\left(\mathds{1}_{d}^{\otimes N}-\Pi^{(N)}_{\textup{sym}}\right)\,\rho\right]\sigma,

(7)

where
\sigma\in\mathcal{L}(\mathds{C}^{d})^{\otimes K}
is an arbitrary quantum state.

To connect the multicopy transposition protocol with single-copy approximations of transposition and structural physical approximations (SPA)

;
Bae 2017
, we examine the reduced action of the optimal transposition channel
\mathcal{T}_{\textup{CP}}
defined in Eq. (
2
). In particular, the reduced state of any single output system takes the form

\Tr_{\overline{O_{i}}}\!\big(\mathcal{T}_{\textup{CP}}(\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}^{\otimes N})\big)=\eta\,\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}^{\mathsf{T}}+(1-\eta)\frac{\mathds{1}}{d}.

(8)

Here,
\Tr_{\overline{O_{i}}}
denotes the partial trace on all output systems except the
i
-th one. Owing to permutation covariance, the reduced state in Eq. (
8
) is independent of the choice of output system

. In Appendix
B
, we show that
\eta=\frac{N}{N+d}
, which is independent of the number of output copies
K
.
Furthermore, under the covariance assumptions, the channel
\mathcal{T}_{\textup{CP}}
maximises the visibility
\eta
appearing in Eq. (
8
).
We notice that the coefficient
\eta=\frac{N}{N+d}
is then the

Complementarity between transposition and cloning —
While arbitrary pure states cannot be perfectly cloned
Wootters and Zurek 1982
, optimal approximate cloning devices are known
Bužek and Hillery 1996
;
Gisin and Massar 1997
;
Werner 1998
;
Keyl and Werner 1999
;
Bruss et al. 1998
;
Scarani et al. 2005
;
Bužek and Hillery 1998
. In particular, the optimal universal symmetric cloning map that transforms
N
copies of a qudit state into
N+K
copies was derived by Werner in Ref.
Werner 1998
. When restricted to operators acting on the symmetric subspace, its action is described by the completely positive map
\mathcal{W}_{\textup{CP}}:\mathcal{L}\!\left(\mathrm{sym}^{N}(\mathds{C}^{d})\right)\to\mathcal{L}\!\left(\mathds{C}^{d}\right)^{\otimes(N+K)},

defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\textup{CP}}(\rho)

\displaystyle:=\frac{d_{S}^{N}}{d_{S}^{N+K}}\,\Pi^{(N+K)}_{\mathrm{sym},\,\mathrm{I}^{\prime}\mathrm{O}^{\prime}}\,\big(\rho\otimes\mathds{1}_{d}^{\otimes K}\big)\,\Pi^{(N+K)}_{\mathrm{sym},\,\mathrm{I}^{\prime}\mathrm{O}^{\prime}},

(9)

where,
\Pi^{(N+K)}_{\mathrm{sym}}
is the projector onto the
(N+K)
symmetric subspace. As shown in Refs.
Werner 1998
;
Keyl and Werner 1999
, the channel in Eq. (
9
) is the solution of the optimisation problem

\max_{\mathcal{C}\in\mathrm{CPTP}}\int\!\mathrm{d}\psi\,F\big\lparen\mathcal{C}\Large\lparen\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}^{\otimes N}\Large\rparen,\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}^{\otimes(N+K)}\big\rparen,

(10)

when the action of
\mathcal{C}
is restricted to operators on the symmetric subspace. The optimal average fidelity attained by this channel is
\frac{d_{S}^{N}}{d_{S}^{N+K}}
, independently of the input pure state
\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}
.

Our second main result establishes that optimal universal symmetric pure-state cloning from
N
to
N+K
copies and optimal pure-state transposition from
N
to
K

Devetak and Shor 2005

. This shows that approximate cloning and approximate transposition are not merely related tasks, but can be simultaneously realised within a single physical process.

Theorem 2
.

For any dimension
d
, the optimal universal symmetric pure state cloning from
N
to
N+K
copies is the complementary channel to the optimal pure-state transposition channel from

to
K
copies.

More precisely, let

\mathcal{H}_{\textup{I}}\cong\mathrm{sym}^{N}\left(\mathds{C}^{d}\right)

, and

, such that, for any operator
\rho\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\textup{I}})

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\textup{CP}}(\rho)=

\displaystyle\Tr_{\mathrm{O}^{\prime}\mathrm{I}^{\prime}}(V\rho V^{\dagger})

(11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\textup{CP}}(\rho)=

\displaystyle\Tr_{\mathrm{O}}(V\rho V^{\dagger})

(12)

where
\mathcal{T}_{\textup{CP}}
is defined in Eq. (
2
) and
\mathcal{W}_{\textup{CP}}
is defined in Eq. (
9
).

The proof of the above theorem is contained in Appendix
C
.
Theorem
2
generalises the idea of clones and anti-clones presented in Refs.
Bužek and Hillery 1996
;
Bužek and Hillery 1998
, which observed that the auxiliary system required by the unitary dilation realising the qubit
1
-to-
2
cloning map contains a noisy version of a state that is orthogonal to the state. This property was generalised to qudits in Ref.
Buscemi et al. 2003
, which shows that, for any dimension

, optimal
N=1
to
K=1
state transposition and optimal
1
-to-
2

2
provides additional insight into the problem of
N\to N+K
telecloning

N\!\to\!K
transposition channel.

Since transposition is a basis dependent map, one may wonder what would change if we consider transposition in another basis. The transposition in an arbitrary basis
\{U\ket{i}\}_{i}
is defined as
\mathcal{T}_{U}(\rho):=U\,\mathcal{T}(U^{\dagger}\rho U)\,U^{\dagger}
. Using the covariance property of the transposition map, one finds

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{U}(\rho)

\displaystyle=UU^{\mathsf{T}}\,\mathcal{T}(\rho)\,\overline{U}U^{\dagger}.

(13)

Defining
A:=UU^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathrm{SU}(d)
, this can be written as
\mathcal{T}_{U}(\rho)=A\,\mathcal{T}(\rho)\,A^{\dagger}
, hence the transposition in another basis is just the transposition on the computational basis followed by a unitary operation.

Lastly, we recall that a Stinespring dilation is unique only up to an isometry acting on the auxiliary system. Hence, it follows from Thm.
2
that a quantum channel is complementary to
\mathcal{W}_{\textup{CP}}
iff it can be written as
H\,\mathcal{T}_{\textup{CP}}(\rho)\,H^{\dagger}
where
H
is an arbitrary isometry. Analogous remark can be done for the complementary channel of optimal transposition.

The recent work
Mančinska and Theil 2024

Optimal
N\to 1
transposition on mixed states —
We now turn to mixed input states and consider the problem of
N\!\to\!1
transposition. In the pure-state setting, performance was quantified by averaging the fidelity over pure states with respect to the Haar-induced measure. Due to the covariance of the transposition map, the average fidelity coincides with the worst-case fidelity, and the resulting optimisation problem can be solved via semidefinite programming. For mixed states, this no longer holds. In general, worst-case and average-case fidelities are not equivalent, and there is no canonical measure for averaging over density operators. For instance, the Hilbert–Schmidt and Bures measures are inequivalent and have different relevant properties
Zyczkowski and Slomczynski 2001
;
Zyczkowski and Sommers 2001
;
Osipov et al. 2010
. Moreover, the fidelity between two density operators,
F(\rho,\sigma):=\Tr\!\left(\sqrt{\sqrt{\rho}\,\sigma\,\sqrt{\rho}}\right)^{2}
, is no longer bilinear function, which makes its analysis significantly more challenging.

We therefore adopt the visibility with respect to white noise as the figure of merit in the mixed-state setting, which is the standard performance quantifier in the structural physical approximation (SPA) approach
Bae 2017
;
Horodecki 2003
to non-CP maps. This also allows us to employ the methods of
N
-copy CP maps introduced in Ref.
Dong et al. 2019
.

Our third main result characterises the optimal
N\to 1
approximation of density-matrix transposition under this figure of merit.

Theorem 3
.

There exists a CPTP linear map
{\mathcal{C}_{N}:\mathcal{L}(\mathds{C}{{}^{d}})^{\otimes N}\to\mathcal{L}\left(\mathds{C}{{}^{d}}\right)}
such that

\mathcal{C}_{N}(\rho^{\otimes N})=\eta\,\rho^{\mathsf{T}}+(1-\eta)\frac{\mathds{1}}{d},\quad\forall\rho\in\mathcal{L}(\mathds{C}^{d})

(14)

if and only if
-\tfrac{1}{d-1}\leq\eta\leq\eta_{\max}
, where

\eta_{\max}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{d+1}&\textup{if }N\leq d-1,\\[4.0pt]
\frac{N}{d(d-1)+N}&\textup{if }N\geq d-1.\end{cases}

(15)

Theorem
3
extends Theorem 10 of Ref.
Dong et al. 2019
by showing that one of the bounds identified there is tight and determines the exact parameter range in which the transposition map admits a structural physical approximation in the multicopy regime. The proof presented in the Appendix
D
combines representation-theoretic tools based on Jucys-Murphy elements with Theorem 2 of Ref.
Dong et al. 2019
, which provides a minimal-eigenvalue characterisation of
N
-copy completely positive maps.

It is instructive to compare the maximal visibility for arbitrary density matrices obtained in Theorem
3
with the maximal visibility achievable when the approximation is restricted to pure states, as in Eq. (
8
). Due to the covariance properties of the transposition map, the same techniques used in Theorem
1
show that there exists a CPTP map
\mathcal{C}_{N}:\mathcal{L}(\mathds{C}^{d})^{\otimes N}\to\mathcal{L}(\mathds{C}^{d})
such that

\mathcal{C}_{N}\big\lparen\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}^{\otimes N}\big\rparen=\eta\,\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}^{\mathsf{T}}+(1-\eta)\frac{\mathds{1}}{d},\quad\forall\,\ket{\psi}\in\mathds{C}^{d},

(16)

if and only if
-\tfrac{1}{d-1}\leq\eta\leq\tfrac{N}{N+d}
.

For
d=2
, the critical visibility for pure states coincides with the maximal visibility
\eta_{\max}
for arbitrary density matrices given in Eq. (
15
). This follows from the fact that every density matrix
\rho\in\mathcal{L}(\mathds{C}^{2})
can be written as
\rho=v\,\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}+(1-v)\,\mathds{1}/2
, so that the visibility constraints for pure and mixed states are equivalent.

By contrast, for any
N>1
and
d>2
, the critical visibility for pure states is strictly larger than
\eta_{\max}
. This difference reflects that for
N>1
, the linear space spanned by
\rho^{\otimes N}
is strictly larger than the space spanned by
\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}^{\otimes N}
. Consequently, satisfying the visibility constraint for pure states in Eq. (
16
) is less restrictive than satisfying the corresponding constraint for mixed states in Eq. (
14
).

Discussions —
We have characterised the optimal channel that approximates the transformation of
N
pure qudit states into
K
copies of the transposed states in terms of fidelity. We showed that this optimal
N
-to-
K
pure-state transposition is the complementary channel to optimal pure (universal and symmetric) cloning from
N
to
N+K
copies, hence, both channels can be simultaneously implemented by a single isometry. This generalises the concept of clones and anticlones
Bužek and Hillery 1996
;
Buscemi et al. 2003
to arbitrary dimension and number of copies.
We then presented an explicit quantum circuit that attains simultaneous optimal cloning and optimal transposition and discussed its gate efficiency.
Finally, we analyse approximate transposition in the case
N
to
K=1
for arbitrary
d
-dimensional density matrices and provide the range of visibilities for which the transposition map can be implemented using
N
copies of an arbitrary density matrix, thereby identifying its optimal SPA in the multicopy scenario.

The task of cloning mixed states, often referred to as quantum broadcasting, has been studied for qubit states, where the length of the Bloch-vector was taken as figure of merit
D’Ariano et al. 2005
;
Buscemi et al. 2006
. Using a similar figure of merit, it was shown that optimal qubit universal NOT broadcasting (unitarily equivalent as qubit transposition) is attained via estimation-based strategies
Chiribella et al. 2007
. In light of these results, it is natural to ask whether the relation between cloning and transposition established here extends to mixed-state scenarios beyond qubits. As discussed in the main text, when considering mixed states, different relevant figures of merits are not equivalent, fact that may lead to non-equivalent notions of optimality. A future direction is then to analyse whether a form of complementarity between broadcasting and transposition broadcasting persists in a more general setting. Also, since our results for pure states provide insights into telecloning
Murao et al. 1999
;
Dür and Cirac 2000
, extending the analysis to mixed states could yield a to a mixed state generalisation of the telecloning protocol. Additionally, we expect that interesting and relevant channel complementarity relations may hold for general unitary-equivariant and permutation-invariant channels. For such generalisations, the classification and circuit approach of
Mančinska and Theil 2024
should provide the framework for extending our results beyond transposition and cloning.

Finally, a natural related direction is to analyse the possible relationship between unitary/channel cloning
Chiribella et al. 2008
;
Sekatski et al. 2025
and unitary/channel complex conjugation
Miyazaki 2020
;
Ebler et al. 2023
. We believe some of the methods used here apply for analysing this unitary generalisation of the pure state problem.

Appendix A
Preliminaries

Throughout the appendix, we will make use of the Choi–Jamiołkowski isomorphism. For a linear map
{\mathcal{C}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\textup{I}})\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\textup{O}})}
,
its Choi operator is the operator
J_{\textup{I}\textup{O}}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\textup{I}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\textup{O}})
defined by

J_{\textup{I}\textup{O}}:=\sum_{i,j}\outerproduct{i}{j}_{\textup{I}}\otimes\mathcal{C}\!\left(\outerproduct{i}{j}_{\textup{I}}\right),

(17)

where
\{\ket{i}\}_{i}
is the computational basis of
\mathcal{H}_{\textup{I}}
. Furthermore, the Choi vector of a linear operator
A:\mathcal{H}_{\textup{I}}\to\mathcal{H}_{\textup{O}}
is defined as

|A\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle_{\textup{I}\textup{O}}:=\sum_{i}\ket{i}_{\textup{I}}\otimes(A\ket{i})_{\textup{O}}.

(18)

Hence, we have that
|\mathds{1}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle_{\textup{I}\textup{I}}\in\mathcal{H}_{\textup{I}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\textup{I}}
is the (unnormalised) maximally entangled vector

|\mathds{1}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle_{\textup{I}\textup{I}}=\sum_{i}\ket{i}_{\textup{I}}\otimes\ket{i}_{\textup{I}},

and that

|A\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle_{\textup{I}\textup{O}}=(\mathds{1}_{\textup{I}}\otimes A\,)|\mathds{1}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle_{\textup{I}\textup{I}}.

(19)

It is also useful to note the identity

|A\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle_{\textup{I}\textup{O}}=(\mathds{1}\otimes A)|\mathds{1}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle_{\textup{I}\textup{I}}=\sqrt{\frac{d_{\textup{O}}}{d_{\textup{I}}}}(A^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes\mathds{1})|\mathds{1}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle_{\textup{O}\textup{O}},

(20)

where the transpose is taken with respect to the computational basis
\{\ket{i}\}_{i}
.

The action of the map
\mathcal{C}
on an arbitrary operator
\rho\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\textup{I}})
can then be written as

\mathcal{C}(\rho)=\Tr_{\textup{I}}\!\left[\left(\rho^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\textup{O}}\right)J_{\textup{I}\textup{O}}\right],

(21)

where the transpose is taken in the same basis used in the definition of the Choi operator, and
\Tr_{\textup{I}}
denotes the partial trace over the input space
\mathcal{H}_{\textup{I}}
. For the
N\!\to\!K
pure-state transposition problem, the input and output Hilbert spaces are

\mathcal{H}_{\textup{I}}:=(\mathds{C}^{d})^{\otimes N}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\textup{O}}:=(\mathds{C}^{d})^{\otimes K}
.
For the
N\!\to\!N+K
cloning problem, the output space is instead

\mathcal{H}_{\textup{O}}:=(\mathds{C}^{d})^{\otimes(N+K)}
.

We will also use standard notation for the symmetric subspace. We denote by
\Pi_{\mathrm{sym}}^{(M)}

the projector onto the symmetric subspace
\mathrm{sym}^{M}(\mathds{C}^{d})\subseteq(\mathds{C}^{d})^{\otimes M},

and by

d_{S}^{M}:=\dim\bigl(\mathrm{sym}^{M}(\mathds{C}^{d})\bigr)=\tr(\Pi_{\mathrm{sym}}^{(M)})=\binom{M+d-1}{d-1}

(22)

its dimension.

Finally, we will repeatedly use the identity

\int\mathrm{d}\psi\;\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}^{\otimes M}=\frac{\Pi_{\mathrm{sym}}^{(M)}}{d_{S}^{M}},

(23)

where
\mathrm{d}\psi
denotes the Haar measure over pure states
{\ket{\psi}\in\mathds{C}^{d}}
.

B.1
An SDP formulation of the problem

Lemma 1
.

The maximum average fidelity for approximating the transpose map from
N
input copies to
K
output copies on pure states, given by

\max_{\mathcal{C}\in\mathrm{CPTP}}\int\!\mathrm{d}\psi F\!\Big(\mathcal{C}(\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}^{\otimes N}),{\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}^{\mathsf{T}}}^{\otimes K}\Big),

(24)

can be expressed as the following semidefinite program

\displaystyle\max_{J\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\textup{I}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\textup{O}})}\quad

\displaystyle\Tr\!\left(J\frac{\Pi^{(N+K)}_{\mathrm{sym}}}{d_{S}^{N+K}}\right)

(25)

\displaystyle\textup{s.t.}\quad J

\displaystyle\geq 0,

(26)

\displaystyle\Tr_{\textup{O}}(J)

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}_{\textup{I}},

(27)

where
J\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\textup{I}})\otimes\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\textup{O}})
is the Choi operator of
\mathcal{C}
, where
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\textup{I}})\cong\mathcal{L}(\mathds{C}^{d})^{\otimes N}
and
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\textup{O}})\cong\mathcal{L}(\mathds{C}^{d})^{\otimes K}
,
\Tr_{O}
is the partial trace over the space
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\textup{O}})
.

The dual SDP corresponding to (
34
) is

\displaystyle\min_{X\in\mathcal{L}(\mathds{C}^{d})^{\otimes N}}\;

\displaystyle\Tr(X)

(28)

s.t.

\displaystyle X\otimes\mathds{1}_{d^{K}}\geq\frac{\Pi^{(N+K)}_{\mathrm{sym}}}{d_{S}^{N+K}}.

(29)

Proof.

Starting from Eq. (
24
) we rewrite,

\displaystyle\int\!\mathrm{d}\psi F\!\Big(\mathcal{C}(\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}^{\otimes N}),{\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}^{\mathsf{T}}}^{\otimes K}\Big)

\displaystyle=\int\!\mathrm{d}\psi\Tr\!\Big(\Tr_{\textup{I}}\!\big(({{\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}^{\mathsf{T}}}^{\otimes N}}\otimes\mathds{1}^{\otimes K})J\big){\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}^{\mathsf{T}}}_{\textup{O}}^{\otimes K}\Big)

(30)

\displaystyle=\int\!\mathrm{d}\psi\Tr\!\Big(J\;{{\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}^{\mathsf{T}}}^{\otimes N}}\otimes{\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}^{\mathsf{T}}}_{\textup{O}}^{\otimes K}\Big)

(31)

\displaystyle=\Tr\!\Big(J_{\textup{I}\textup{O}}\int\!\mathrm{d}\psi({{\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}^{\mathsf{T}}}^{\otimes N+K}})\Big)

(32)

\displaystyle=\Tr\!\Big(J_{\textup{I}\textup{O}}\frac{\Pi^{(N+K)}_{\mathrm{sym}}}{d_{S}^{N+K}}\Big)

(33)

where
\mathcal{L}(H_{\textup{I}})\cong\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes N})
and
\mathcal{L}(H_{\textup{O}})\cong\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes K})
.
In the last equality we used that

\int\mathrm{d}\psi\,\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}^{\otimes(N+K)}=\frac{\Pi^{(N+K)}_{\mathrm{sym}}}{d_{S}^{N+K}}
and that the symmetric projector is invariant under transpose. Now, let us introduce

\Omega=\frac{\Pi^{(N+K)}_{\mathrm{sym}}}{d_{S}^{N+K}}

to rewrite Eq. (
34
) as,

\displaystyle\max_{J}\quad

\displaystyle\Tr(J\Omega)

(34)

s.t.

\displaystyle J\geq 0,\qquad\Tr_{\textup{O}}(J)=\mathds{1}_{\textup{I}},

(35)

In order to obtain the dual SDP, we may follow the standard Lagrangian approach
Skrzypczyk and Cavalcanti 2023
. For that, we introduce one dual variable for each constraint, namely
X
for the equality constraint and
Y
for the inequality constraint and obtain the following Lagrangian,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=\Tr(J \Omega)+\Tr(X_{\I} (\mathbb{1}_{\I} - \Tr_{\O}(J)))+\Tr(JY)

(36)

\displaystyle=\Tr(J (\Omega- X_{\I}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{\O} + Y))+\Tr(X_{\I}),

(37)

where we used that the adjoint of the partial trace satisfies

\Tr(X_{\I}\Tr_{\O}(J))=\Tr((X_{\I}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{\O})J).

The dual objective function is obtained by taking the supremum of the Lagrangian over
J\geq 0
, while the dual feasibility constraints ensure that this supremum is finite. The dual problem therefore reads

\displaystyle\min_{X}\quad

\displaystyle\Tr(X)

(38)

s.t.

\displaystyle X_{\textup{I}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\textup{O}}\geq\Omega_{\textup{I}\textup{O}}.

(39)

∎

B.2
Covariance properties of the problem

Theorem 4
.

Let
\mathcal{C}:\mathcal{L}(\mathds{C}^{d})^{\otimes N}\to\mathcal{L}(\mathds{C}^{d})^{\otimes K}

be a quantum channel that attains an average fidelity
F_{\mathcal{C}}
in the
N\!\to\!K
state transposition task. That is,

F_{\mathcal{C}}:=\int\!\mathrm{d}\psi F\!\Big(\mathcal{C}\left(\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}^{\otimes N}\right),\,{\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}^{\mathsf{T}}}^{\otimes K}\Big).

(40)

There exists another quantum channel

\mathcal{C}^{\prime}:\mathcal{L}(\mathds{C}^{d})^{\otimes N}\to\mathcal{L}(\mathds{C}^{d})^{\otimes K}

that attains the same performance
F_{\mathcal{C}}
and satisfies the unitary covariance relation

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\!\left(U^{\otimes N}\rho(U^{\otimes N})^{\dagger}\right)

\displaystyle=\overline{U}^{\otimes K}\,\mathcal{C}^{\prime}(\rho)\,(U^{\mathsf{T}})^{\otimes K},\quad\forall U\in\mathrm{SU}(d),\quad\forall\rho\in\mathcal{L}(\mathds{C}^{d})^{\otimes N},

(41)

as well as the permutation covariance relation

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\!\left(V_{\pi}\rho V_{\pi}^{\dagger}\right)

\displaystyle=V_{\sigma}\,\mathcal{C}^{\prime}(\rho)\,V_{\sigma}^{\dagger},\quad\forall\pi\in S_{N},\quad\sigma\in S_{K},

(42)

where
V_{\pi}
and
V_{\sigma}
denote the unitary representations of the symmetric groups acting on
(\mathds{C}^{d})^{\otimes N}
and
(\mathds{C}^{d})^{\otimes K}
, respectively.

Proof.

We first show that, for the
N\!\to\!K
transposition problem, one can, without loss of performance, restrict the search to channels satisfying the covariance condition

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\!\left(U^{\otimes N}\rho(U^{\otimes N})^{\dagger}\right)

\displaystyle=\overline{U}^{\otimes K}\,\mathcal{C}^{\prime}(\rho)\,(U^{\mathsf{T}})^{\otimes K},\quad\forall U\in\mathrm{SU}(d),\quad\forall\rho\in\mathcal{L}(\mathds{C}^{d})^{\otimes N}.

(43)

This condition can equivalently be expressed as a symmetry property of the Choi operator. Namely, we have

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=(\mathds{1}\otimes\mathcal{C}^{\prime})(|\mathds{1}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle\mathds{1}|_{\textup{I}^{\prime}\textup{I}})

(44)

\displaystyle=(\mathds{1}\otimes\mathcal{C}^{\prime})\left(\left(U^{\otimes N}\otimes\overline{U}^{\otimes N}\right)|\mathds{1}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle\mathds{1}|\left(U^{\otimes N}\otimes\overline{U}^{\otimes N}\right)^{\dagger}\right)

(45)

\displaystyle=\left(U_{\textup{I}}^{\otimes N}\otimes U_{\textup{O}}^{\otimes K}\right)(\mathds{1}\otimes\mathcal{C}^{\prime})\left(|\mathds{1}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle\mathds{1}|\right)\left(U_{\textup{I}}^{\otimes N}\otimes U_{\textup{O}}^{\otimes K}\right)^{\dagger},

(46)

where
\mathcal{L}(H_{\textup{I}})\cong\mathcal{L}(H_{\textup{I}^{\prime}})\cong\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes N})
,
\mathcal{L}(H_{\textup{O}})\cong\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes K})
, and
J\in\mathcal{L}(H_{\textup{I}}\otimes H_{\textup{O}})
.
In the above we used the identity
(U\otimes\overline{U})|\mathds{1}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle=|\mathds{1}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle
for the maximally entangled vector. Hence,

\displaystyle[J,\,U^{\otimes N}\otimes U^{\otimes K}]=0,\quad\forall U\in\mathrm{SU}(d).

(47)

Given any feasible Choi operator
J
, we define its unitary twirl

\displaystyle\widetilde{J}:=\int\mathrm{d}U\;\bigl(U^{\otimes N}\otimes U^{\otimes K}\bigr)\,J\,\bigl(U^{\otimes N}\otimes U^{\otimes K}\bigr)^{\dagger}.

(48)

Let us now show that if
J\geq 0
and
\Tr_{O}(J)=\mathds{1}_{\textup{I}}
, then
\widetilde{J}\geq 0
and
\Tr_{\textup{O}}(\widetilde{J})=\mathds{1}_{\textup{I}}
. Namely, since
\widetilde{J}
is a convex combination of positive semidefinite operators, we have
\widetilde{J}\geq 0
. Furthermore,

\displaystyle\Tr_{\textup{O}}(\widetilde{J})=

\displaystyle\Tr_{\textup{O}}\int\mathrm{d}U\;\bigl(U^{\otimes N}\otimes U^{\otimes K}\bigr)\,J\,\bigl(U^{\otimes N}\otimes U^{\otimes K}\bigr)^{\dagger}

(49)

\displaystyle=\Tr_{\textup{O}}\int\mathrm{d}U\;\bigl(U^{\otimes N}\otimes(U^{\dagger}U)^{\otimes K}\bigr)\,J\,\bigl(U^{\otimes N}\otimes\mathds{1}^{\otimes K}\bigr)^{\dagger}

(50)

\displaystyle=\Tr_{\textup{O}}\int\mathrm{d}U\;\bigl(U^{\otimes N}\otimes\mathds{1}^{\otimes K}\bigr)\,J\,\bigl(U^{\otimes N}\otimes\mathds{1}^{\otimes K}\bigr)^{\dagger}

(51)

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}U\;\bigl(U^{\otimes N}\bigr)\,\Tr_{\textup{O}}(J)\,\bigl(U^{\otimes N}\bigr)^{\dagger}

(52)

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}U\;\bigl(U^{\otimes N}\bigr)\,\mathds{1}_{\textup{I}}\,\bigl(U^{\otimes N}\bigr)^{\dagger}

(53)

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}_{\textup{I}}.

(54)

Hence, when
J
is feasible,
\widetilde{J}
is also feasible. Moreover, since
\Omega
is invariant under
U^{\otimes(N+K)}
,

\displaystyle\Tr(\widetilde J\,\Omega)=\Tr(J\,\Omega).

(55)

Additionally, without loss of performance, we can restrict the search to Choi operators satisfying the permutation covariance condition

\displaystyle[J,\,V_{\pi,\textup{I}}\otimes V_{\sigma,\textup{O}}]=0,\quad\forall\pi\in S_{N},\;\sigma\in S_{K},

(56)

where
V_{\pi,\textup{I}}
and
V_{\sigma,\textup{O}}
are the unitary representations of the permutation groups
S_{N}
and
S_{K}
acting on the input and output systems, respectively.

To obtain a channel satisfying both covariance properties simultaneously, we apply the permutation twirl to
\widetilde{J}
and define

\displaystyle J^{\prime}:=\frac{1}{N!K!}\sum_{\pi\in S_{N},\sigma\in S_{K}}\bigl(V_{\pi,\textup{I}}\otimes V_{\sigma,\textup{O}}\bigr)\,\widetilde{J}\,\bigl(V_{\pi,\textup{I}}\otimes V_{\sigma,\textup{O}}\bigr)^{\dagger}.

(57)

Then
J^{\prime}\geq 0
and
\Tr_{\textup{O}}(J^{\prime})=\mathds{1}_{\textup{I}}
, hence
J^{\prime}
is feasible. Again, since
\Omega
is invariant under
V_{\pi,\textup{I}}\otimes V_{\sigma,\textup{O}}
, we have

\displaystyle\Tr(J'\,\Omega)=\Tr(\widetilde J\,\Omega)=\Tr(J\,\Omega).

(58)

Moreover, because permutation operators commute with
U^{\otimes N}\otimes U^{\otimes K}
, the operator
J^{\prime}
satisfies both

\displaystyle[J^{\prime},\,U^{\otimes N}\otimes U^{\otimes K}]=0,\quad\forall U\in\mathrm{SU}(d),

(59)

and

\displaystyle[J^{\prime},\,V_{\pi,\textup{I}}\otimes V_{\sigma,\textup{O}}]=0,\quad\forall\pi\in S_{N},\;\sigma\in S_{K}.

(60)

Hence the corresponding channel
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
attains the same performance as
\mathcal{C}
and satisfies Eqs. (
41
) and (
42
).

∎

B.3
Proof of optimality

See
1

Proof.

Before presenting the proof, we note that the attainability of the bound follows almost directly from the fact that pure states can be estimated with average fidelity
\frac{d_{S}^{N}}{d_{S}^{N+1}}
using the Hayashi measurement
Hayashi 1998
;
Harrow 2013
. One can apply the Hayashi measurement to estimate the input state
\ket{\psi}
and then prepare the state
{\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}^{\mathsf{T}}}^{\otimes K}
as the output. A straightforward calculation shows that this strategy achieves an average fidelity of
\frac{d_{S}^{N}}{d_{S}^{N+K}}
. That being said, for completeness and concreteness, we present the proof without explicitly relying on previous results on state estimation. Instead, we show that the optimal value can be attained by an estimation strategy through a direct analysis of the Choi operator of the optimal channel.

Attainability of the optimal value

Let us now show that the optimal value of the optimisation problem is attainable.
Since
\Omega
is supported on the symmetric subspace, and under the covariance assumption Schur’s lemma implies that
J
decomposes as
J=\alpha\,\Pi^{(N+K)}_{\mathrm{sym}}+B
, where
B\geq 0
and
B\Pi^{(N+K)}_{\mathrm{sym}}=\Pi^{(N+K)}_{\mathrm{sym}}B=0
, we have

\displaystyle\Tr(J\Omega)=\Tr\!\left(J\frac{\Pi^{(N+K)}_{\mathrm{sym}}}{d_{S}^{N+K}}\right)=\frac{1}{d_{S}^{N+K}}\Tr\!\left(\alpha\,\Pi^{(N+K)}_{\mathrm{sym}}\right)=\alpha.

(61)

Hence, maximising
\Tr(J\Omega)
is equivalent to maximising the coefficient
\alpha
.

This motivates the following ansatz for the Choi operator

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=\frac{d^{N}_{S}\Pi^{(N+K)}_{\mathrm{sym,\;in,out}}}{d^{N+K}_{S}}+(\mathds{1}_{\textup{I}}-\Pi^{N}_{\mathrm{sym},\;\textup{I}})\otimes\sigma_{\textup{O}}.

(62)

where
\Tr(\sigma)=1
and
\sigma\geq 0
.
Since
\Pi^{(N+K)}_{\mathrm{sym,\;in,out}}
and
(\mathds{1}_{\textup{I}}-\Pi^{N}_{\mathrm{sym},\;\textup{I}})\otimes\sigma_{\textup{O}}
are positive semidefinite operators with orthogonal support, we conclude that
J\geq 0
. Using the identity

\Tr_{O}(\Pi^{(N+K)}_{\mathrm{sym}})=\frac{d_{S}^{N+K}}{d_{S}^{N}}\,\Pi^{(N)}_{\mathrm{sym}}
,
one can directly verify that the ansatz given in Eq. (
62
) satisfies

\Tr_{\textup{O}}(J)=\mathds{1}_{\textup{I}}
, and therefore represents a valid feasible solution of Eq. (
34
).

We have then an explicit quantum channel, described by its Choi operator
J
, that satisfies the constraints of the optimisation problem and attains an average fidelity of
\Tr(J \Pi^{(N+K)}_{\mathrm{sym}})\frac{1}{d_{S}^{N+K}}=\frac{d_{S}^{N}}{d_{S}^{N+K}}
.

Attainability via estimation

Let us now show that the ansatz given by Eq. (
62
) represents an estimation strategy. We start by recalling that, if
J
is the Choi operator of a linear map
\mathcal{C}
, for any linear operator
\rho
on the input space, we can write the action of
\mathcal{C}
as
\mathcal{C}(\rho)=\Tr_{\textup{I}}\!\left[\left(\rho_{\textup{I}}^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\textup{O}}\right)J\right].

Hence, using the identity
\int\mathrm{d}\psi\;(\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi})^{\otimes(N+K)}=\frac{\Pi^{(N+K)}_{\mathrm{sym}}}{d_{S}^{N+K}}

Harrow 2013
we have,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\Tr_{\textup{I}}\!\left[\left(\rho_{\textup{I}}^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\textup{O}}\right)J\right]

(63)

\displaystyle=\Tr_{\textup{I}}\left(\left(\rho_{\textup{I}}^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\textup{O}}\right)\left(\frac{d^{N}_{S}\Pi^{(N+K)}_{\mathrm{sym,\;in,out}}}{d^{N+K}_{S}}+(\mathds{1}_{\textup{I}}-\Pi^{N}_{\mathrm{sym},\;\textup{I}})\otimes\sigma_{\textup{O}}\right)\right)

(64)

\displaystyle=\Tr_{\textup{I}}\left(\left(\rho_{\textup{I}}^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\textup{O}}\right)\left(d^{N}_{S}\int\mathrm{d}\psi\;(\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi})^{\otimes(N+K)}+(\mathds{1}_{\textup{I}}-\Pi^{N}_{\mathrm{sym},\;\textup{I}})\otimes\sigma_{\textup{O}}\right)\right)

(65)

\displaystyle=d_{S}^{N}\int\mathrm{d}\psi\;\Tr\!\big(\rho^{\mathsf{T}}\,\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}^{\otimes N}\big)\,(\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi})_{\mathrm{O}}^{\otimes K}+\Tr\!\big(\rho^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathds{1}-\Pi^{N}_{\mathrm{sym}})\big)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{O}}

(66)

\displaystyle=d_{S}^{N}\int\mathrm{d}\psi\;\Tr\!\big(\rho\,{\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}^{\mathsf{T}}}^{\otimes N}\big)\,(\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi})_{\mathrm{O}}^{\otimes K}+\Tr\!\big(\rho^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathds{1}-\Pi^{N}_{\mathrm{sym}})\big)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{O}}.

(67)

Using the invariance of the Haar measure under complex conjugation, the change of variable yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\rho)=d_{S}^{N}\int\mathrm{d}\psi\;\Tr\!\big\lparen\rho\,\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}^{\otimes N}\big\rparen\,{\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}^{\mathsf{T}}}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\otimes K}+\Tr\!\big(\rho^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathds{1}-\Pi^{N}_{\mathrm{sym}})\big)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{O}}.

(68)

When restricted to inputs supported on the symmetric subspace, the second term in Eq. (
63
) vanishes because
\Tr\!\big(\rho^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathds{1}-\Pi^{N}_{\mathrm{sym}})\big)=0
. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{CP}}(\rho)=d_{S}^{N}\int\mathrm{d}\psi\;\Tr\!\big(\rho\,\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}^{\otimes N}\big)\,{\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}^{\mathsf{T}}}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\otimes K}.

(69)

We can now recognise that Eq. (
69
) is an estimation map,

\mathcal{E}(\rho)=\int\mathrm{d}\phi\;\Tr\!\left(M_{\phi}\,\rho\right)\,\sigma_{\phi},

(70)

where
\{M_{\phi}\}
is a POVM on the symmetric subspace
\mathrm{sym}^{N}(\mathds{C}^{d})
considered in
Hayashi 1998
;
Harrow 2013
, defined by

M_{\phi}=d^{N}_{S}\,\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}^{\otimes N}\mathrm{d}\phi

(71)

and the output states

\sigma_{\phi}={\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}^{\mathsf{T}}}^{\otimes K}

(72)

corresponding to
K
copies of the transpose of the estimated state.

A tight upper bound from the dual problem

In order to obtain an upper bound on the optimal value of the primal problem, we consider the dual of the SDP given by Eq. (
34
). As in any convex maximisation problem, any feasible solution to the dual problem provides an upper bound on the primal
Skrzypczyk and Cavalcanti 2023
. As presented in Lemma
1
, the dual problem of Eq. (
34
) reads as

\displaystyle\min_{X}\quad

\displaystyle\Tr(X)

(73)

s.t.

\displaystyle X_{\textup{I}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\textup{O}}\geq\Omega_{\textup{I}\;\textup{O}},

(74)

where
\mathcal{L}(H_{\textup{I}})=\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes N})
and
\mathcal{L}(H_{\textup{O}})=\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes K})
, and
\Omega=\frac{\Pi^{(N+K)}_{\mathrm{sym}}}{d_{S}^{N+K}}
.

Given the structure of the performance operator
\Omega
, it is natural to look for an ansatz for
X
that has support on the symmetric subspace of the input system. Hence we consider the ansatz
X_{\textup{I}}=\alpha\Pi^{(N)}_{\mathrm{sym}}
, such that

\alpha\Pi^{(N)}_{\mathrm{sym}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\textup{O}}\;\geq\;\frac{\Pi^{(N+K)}_{\mathrm{sym}}}{d_{S}^{N+K}}.

(75)

In order for this to be a valid ansatz, we need to show that the operator inequality indeed holds for some
\alpha
. Namely, let
\lambda=(N)
be the one-row Young diagram. Since the multiplicity space
V_{(N)}
, carrying irrep of
\mathrm{S}_{N}
, is one-dimensional, we have
\Pi_{\mathrm{sym}}^{(N)}=E_{11}^{(N)}

B.4
Uniqueness properties

We start this section with a useful lemma that will be used in the proof of the uniqueness of the optimal strategy.

Lemma 2
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{L}(\mathds{C}^{d})^{\otimes N}
be a linear operator such that
\Tr(A \ketbra{\psi}^{\otimes N})=0
for all vectors
\ket{\psi}\in\mathds{C}^{d}
. Then, if
\ket{\phi}\in\mathrm{sym}^{N}\left(\mathds{C}^{d}\right)
is a vector in the symmetric subspace, we have
\bra{\phi}A\ket{\phi}=0
.

Proof.

Our goal is to show that, for any two vectors
\ket{\psi_0},\ket{\psi_1}\in\mathds{C}^{d}
, we have

\bra{\psi_0}^{\otimes N}A\ket{\psi_1}^{\otimes N}=0.

(84)

This is enough to complete the proof, since any vector
\ket{\phi}\in\mathrm{sym}^{N}(\mathds{C}^{d})
can be written as a linear combination of vectors of the form
\ket{\psi}^{\otimes N}
, namely
\ket{\phi}=\sum_{i}a_{i}\ket{\psi_i}^{\otimes N},

and therefore

\displaystyle\bra{\phi}A\ket{\phi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}a_{i}^{*}a_{j}\bra{\psi_i}^{\otimes N}A\ket{\psi_j}^{\otimes N}

(85)

\displaystyle=0.

(86)

Let us consider
\ket{\psi_0},\ket{\psi_1}\in\mathds{C}^{d}
be arbitrary vectors, and define

\ket{\chi}:=a\ket{\psi_0}+b\ket{\psi_1},\qquad a,b\in\mathds{C}.

(87)

Now consider

\displaystyle f(a,b)

\displaystyle:=\Tr\!\left(A\outerproduct{\chi}{\chi}^{\otimes N}\right)

(88)

\displaystyle=\bra{\chi}^{\otimes N}A\ket{\chi}^{\otimes N}

(89)

\displaystyle=\Big(\overline{a}\bra{\psi_0}+\overline{b}\bra{\psi_1}\Big)^{\otimes N}A\Big(a\ket{\psi_0}+b\ket{\psi_1}\Big)^{\otimes N}.

(90)

By assumption,
f(a,b)=0
for all
a,b\in\mathds{C}
. The function
f(a,b)
is a homogenous polynomial of degree
2N
. Since it vanishes identically, all of its coefficients must be zero. In particular, the coefficient of the monomial
\bar{a}^{N}b^{N}
is precisely

\bra{\psi_0}^{\otimes N}A\ket{\psi_1}^{\otimes N},

(91)

because
\bar{a}^{N}
arises only by choosing
\bra{\psi_0}
from each factor in
\bra{\chi}^{\otimes N}
, and
b^{N}
arises only by choosing
\ket{\psi_1}
from each factor in
\ket{\chi}^{\otimes N}
.

Hence

\bra{\psi_0}^{\otimes N}A\ket{\psi_1}^{\otimes N}=0.

(92)

Since
\ket{\psi_0}
and
\ket{\psi_1}
were arbitrary, the claim follows.

For the sake of concreteness, we now illustrate the function
f
case where
N=2
. The general case can be treated in the same way, albeit with more cumbersome notation. For
N=2
, we have

\displaystyle\ket{\chi}^{\otimes 2}=

\displaystyle(a\ket{\psi_0}+b\ket{\psi_1})^{\otimes 2}

(93)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a^{2}\ket{\psi_0}^{\otimes 2}+b^{2}\ket{\psi_1}^{\otimes 2}+ab\ket{\psi_0}\ket{\psi_1}+ab\ket{\psi_1}\ket{\psi_0}.

(94)

Then,

\displaystyle f(a,b):=

\displaystyle\Tr(A \ketbra{\chi}^{\otimes 2})

(95)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{a}^{2}a^{2}\bra{\psi_0}^{\otimes 2}A\ket{\psi_0}^{\otimes 2}+\overline{a}^{2}b^{2}\bra{\psi_0}^{\otimes 2}A\ket{\psi_1}^{\otimes 2}+\overline{a}^{2}ab\bra{\psi_0}^{\otimes 2}A\ket{\psi_0}\ket{\psi_1}+\overline{a}^{2}ab\bra{\psi_0}^{\otimes 2}A\ket{\psi_1}\ket{\psi_0}

(96)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\overline{b}^{2}a^{2}\bra{\psi_1}^{\otimes 2}A\ket{\psi_0}^{\otimes 2}+\overline{b}^{2}b^{2}\bra{\psi_1}^{\otimes 2}A\ket{\psi_1}^{\otimes 2}+\overline{b}^{2}ab\bra{\psi_1}^{\otimes 2}A\ket{\psi_0}\ket{\psi_1}+\overline{b}^{2}ab\bra{\psi_1}^{\otimes 2}A\ket{\psi_1}\ket{\psi_0}

(97)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\overline{ab}a^{2}\bra{\psi_0}\bra{\psi_1}A\ket{\psi_0}^{\otimes 2}+\overline{ab}b^{2}\bra{\psi_0}\bra{\psi_1}A\ket{\psi_1}^{\otimes 2}+\overline{ab}ab\bra{\psi_0}\bra{\psi_1}A\ket{\psi_0}\ket{\psi_1}+\overline{ab}ab\bra{\psi_0}\bra{\psi_1}A\ket{\psi_1}\ket{\psi_0}

(98)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\overline{ab}a^{2}\bra{\psi_1}\bra{\psi_0}A\ket{\psi_0}^{\otimes 2}+\overline{ab}b^{2}\bra{\psi_1}\bra{\psi_0}A\ket{\psi_1}^{\otimes 2}+\overline{ab}ab\bra{\psi_1}\bra{\psi_0}A\ket{\psi_0}\ket{\psi_1}+\overline{ab}ab\bra{\psi_1}\bra{\psi_0}A\ket{\psi_1}\ket{\psi_0}.

(99)

Since
f(a,b)=0
, all the coefficients, including
\bra{\psi_0}^{\otimes N}A\ket{\psi_1}^{\otimes N}
vanish, hence follows the claim.

Lemma 3

Let

Proof.

Let us define

\displaystyle\frac{\Tr(A \ketbra{\psi}^{\otimes N})}{\norm{\ket{\psi}}^{2N}}

\displaystyle=\alpha-\alpha

(102)

\displaystyle=0,

(103)

and the conclusion is trivial if
\ket{\psi}=0
. Hence, for every
\ket{\psi}\in\mathds{C}^{d}

\Tr(A \ketbra{\psi}^{\otimes N})=0.

(104)

We can therefore invoke Lemma

\bra{\phi}A\ket{\phi}=0

\displaystyle=0+\alpha

\displaystyle=\alpha,

(108)

5
shows that there is a unique covariant channel that maximised the worst-case fidelity. We will now prove uniqueness without the covariance assumption.

We will split our proof in two cases. The first case is where the fidelity

The case where the fidelity

\ket{\psi}

\ket{\psi}

for every pure state

Optimal

Lemma 4
.

The minimal and maximal eigenvalues of the operator

\displaystyle\lambda_{\textup{min}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}-N&\textup{ if }N\leq d-1\\

\displaystyle\lambda_{\textup{max}}

(150)

Proof.

Using Lemma

(151)

R(\pi):=\sum_{x\in[d]^{N+1}}\ket*{x_{\pi^{-1}(1)},x_{\pi^{-1}(2)},\dotsc,x_{\pi^{-1}(N+1)}}\bra{x_1,x_2,\dotsc,x_{N+1}}.

\displaystyle\lambda_{\textup{min}}=1-\min(d,N+1),

To determine the admissible range of

Appendix E
Representation theory background

In this section, we review basic facts from the representation theory of the symmetric group
Ceccherini-Silberstein et al. 2010
, Schur–Weyl duality
Harrow 2013
, and Jucys–Murphy elements
Jucys 1974
;
Murphy 1981
;
Okounkov and Vershik 1996
;
Ceccherini-Silberstein et al. 2010
.

E.0.1
Symmetric group
S_{n}

Let
S_{n}
be the symmetric group on
\{1,\dots,n\}
. A
transposition
is a swap of two letters,

(i,j)\in S_{n},\qquad 1\leq i<j\leq n,

(172)

and transpositions generate
S_{n}
. A particularly convenient generating set is given by the
adjacent transpositions

\sigma_{i}\;:=\;(i,i+1),\qquad i=1,\dots,n-1,

(173)

which satisfy the Coxeter relations of type
A_{n-1}
:

\sigma_{i}^{2}=1,\qquad\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i+1}\sigma_{i}=\sigma_{i+1}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i+1},\qquad\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}=\sigma_{j}\sigma_{i}\ \ (|i-j|\geq 2).

(174)

It is often convenient to equip a group with a linear structure and consider the corresponding
group algebra
, which for the symmetric group is denoted by
\mathds{C}[S_{n}]
.
Equivalently,
\mathds{C}[S_{n}]
is the associative algebra generated by
\{\sigma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n-1}
subject to the above Coxeter relations.

The complex irreducible representations of
S_{n}
are in bijection with partitions
\lambda\vdash n
, equivalently Young diagrams with
n
boxes.
We write
V_{\lambda}
for the irrep corresponding to the shape
\lambda
.
The group algebra decomposes as

\mathds{C}[S_{n}]\;\cong\;\bigoplus_{\lambda\vdash n}\mathcal{L}(V_{\lambda}).

(175)

E.0.2
Schur–Weyl duality

Fix
d\in\mathds{N}
and consider
(\mathds{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}
.
There are commuting actions of
U(d)
and
S_{n}
, namely the diagonal action
U^{\otimes n}
and the tensor representation
R:S_{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{L}((\mathds{C}^{d})^{\otimes n})
which maps permutations
\pi\in S_{n}
to linear operators permutation tensor factors:

U^{\otimes n}\,R(\pi)=R(\pi)\,U^{\otimes n}\qquad\forall\,U\in U(d),\ \pi\in S_{n},

(176)

where matrix
R(\pi)\in\mathcal{L}((\mathds{C}^{d})^{\otimes n})
permutes the
n
tensor legs:

R(\pi):=\sum_{x\in[d]^{n}}\ket*{x_{\pi^{-1}(1)},x_{\pi^{-1}(2)},\dotsc,x_{\pi^{-1}(n)}}\bra{x_1,x_2,\dotsc,x_n}

(177)

Schur–Weyl duality yields the multiplicity-free
U(d)\times S_{n}
-bimodule decomposition

(\mathds{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}\;\cong\;\bigoplus_{\lambda\vdash n,\ \ell(\lambda)\leq d}W_{\lambda}\otimes V_{\lambda},

(178)

where
V_{\lambda}
is the
S_{n}
-irrep,
W_{\lambda}
is the corresponding
U(d)
-irrep (also can be seen as highest-weight
\mathfrak{gl}_{d}
-module), and
\ell(\lambda)
is the number of rows of
\lambda
.
One may use the projectors
\Pi_{\lambda}
onto
\lambda
-isotypic components:

(\mathds{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}=\bigoplus_{\lambda}\mathrm{Im}(\Pi_{\lambda}),\qquad\mathrm{Im}(\Pi_{\lambda})=W_{\lambda}\otimes V_{\lambda}.

(179)

The isomorphism (
178
) is achieved by a unitary called Schur transform
U_{\mathrm{Sch}}
. Schur transform can be constructed from Clebsch–Gordan transforms for the unitary group, see
Bacon et al. 2006
;
Harrow 2005
;
Burchardt et al. 2025
, and, alternatively, from
S_{n}
-Quantum Fourier Transform, see
Krovi 2019
;
Burchardt et al. 2025
.

Clebsch–Gordan transforms
\mathrm{CG}
are defined as unitary transformations which decompose tensor products of two
U(d)
irreducible representations
\lambda
and
\mu
into direct sum of
\nu
irreps. In other words,
\mathrm{CG}
transforms implement the following isomorphisms:

W_{\lambda}\otimes W_{\mu}\;\cong\;\bigoplus_{\nu}W_{\nu}\otimes\mathds{C}^{c^{\nu}_{\lambda,\mu}},

(180)

where multiplicity spaces have dimensions
c^{\nu}_{\lambda,\mu}
, known as Littlewood–Richardson coefficients.

E.0.3
Young–Yamanouchi (orthogonal) basis

Let
T
be an SYT of shape
\lambda
, and let
\ket{T}
denote the corresponding Young–Yamanouchi (orthonormal) basis vector, labelled by standard Young tableau.
If
(r_{T}(i),c_{T}(i))
is the row and column of the box containing
i
in
T
, we define the content

\mathrm{cont}_{T}(i)\;:=\;c_{T}(i)-r_{T}(i),

(181)

and we define the axial distance between boxes containing
i
and
i+1
as

a_{T}(i)\;:=\;\mathrm{cont}_{T}(i+1)-\mathrm{cont}_{T}(i).

(182)

Let
T^{(i)}
be the tableau obtained by swapping the entries
i
and
i+1
in
T
(if this swap does not yield a standard tableau, we set
\ket*{T^{(i)}}:=0
by convention).
Then the adjacent transposition
\sigma_{i}=(i,i+1)
acts by

\sigma_{i}\ket{T}=\frac{1}{a_{T}(i)}\,\ket{T}+\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{a_{T}(i)^{2}}}\,\ket*{T^{(i)}},

(183)

and, whenever
T^{(i)}
is standard (so that
\ket*{T^{(i)}}\neq 0
),

\sigma_{i}\ket*{T^{(i)}}=\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{a_{T}(i)^{2}}}\,\ket{T}-\frac{1}{a_{T}(i)}\,\ket*{T^{(i)}}.

(184)

If
T^{(i)}
is not standard then necessarily
a_{T}(i)=\pm 1
, hence
\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{a_{T}(i)^{2}}}=0
,
and the above reduces to
\sigma_{i}\ket{T}=\pm\ket{T}
, consistent with
\sigma_{i}^{2}=1
.

E.0.4
Jucys–Murphy elements

We define the Jucys–Murphy (JM) elements
J_{k}\in\mathds{C}[S_{n}]
by the transposition sums

J_{1}\;:=\;0,\qquad J_{k}\;:=\;\sum_{r=1}^{k-1}(r,k)\quad(k\geq 2).

(185)

It is easy to see, that they commute pairwise:

[J_{k},J_{\ell}]=0\qquad\forall\,k,\ell,

(186)

and moreover they generate maximal commutative subalgebra of
\mathds{C}[S_{n}]
.
Their sum is central (commutes with every element of
\mathds{C}[S_{n}]
) and equals the sum of all transpositions:

\sum_{k=1}^{n}J_{k}=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(i,j).

(187)

Now fix
\lambda\vdash n
.
The Young–Yamanouchi basis of
V_{\lambda}
is indexed by SYT
T
of shape
\lambda
, with orthonormal vectors
\ket{T}
.
Then the JM elements are diagonal and thier spectrum is known (see, for example,
(
Grinko et al. 2023
, Lemma B.7)
):

Lemma 5

(Jucys–Murphy spectrum for
S_{n}
in the Young–Yamanouchi basis)
.

Let
\lambda\vdash n
and let
\{\ket{T}\}
be the Young–Yamanouchi (orthonormal) basis of the irrep
V_{\lambda}
,
indexed by standard Young tableaux (SYT)
T
of shape
\lambda
.
Then each JM element
J_{k}
is diagonal in the basis
\{\ket{T}\}
and

J_{k}\ket{T}=\mathrm{cont}_{T}(k)\,\ket{T}\qquad(k=1,\dots,n).

(188)

Proof.

Set
\sigma_{k}:=(k,k+1)
and note the standard recursion

J_{k+1}=\sigma_{k}J_{k}\sigma_{k}+\sigma_{k},\qquad k=1,\dots,n-1.

(189)

Fix an SYT
T
and write
T^{(k)}:=\sigma_{k}T
(if
\sigma_{k}T
is not standard, set
\ket*{T^{(k)}}:=0
).
Let

a:=\mathrm{cont}_{T}(k+1)-\mathrm{cont}_{T}(k).

(190)

In the Young–Yamanouchi orthogonal form,
\sigma_{k}
acts on
\mathrm{span}\{\ket{T},\ket*{T^{(k)}}\}
as

\sigma_{k}\ket{T}=\frac{1}{a}\ket{T}+\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{a^{2}}}\ket*{T^{(k)}},\qquad\sigma_{k}\ket*{T^{(k)}}=\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{a^{2}}}\ket{T}-\frac{1}{a}\ket*{T^{(k)}}.

(191)

(When
T^{(k)}
is not standard one has
a=\pm 1
, so the square-root term vanishes automatically.)

We prove the claim by induction on
k
. The base
k=1
is immediate since
J_{1}=0
and
\mathrm{cont}_{T}(1)=0
.
Assume
J_{k}\ket{T}=\mathrm{cont}_{T}(k)\ket{T}
for all
T
. On
\mathrm{span}\{\ket{T},\ket*{T^{(k)}}\}
we have

J_{k}=\mathrm{diag}\bigl(\mathrm{cont}_{T}(k),\,\mathrm{cont}_{T}(k+1)\bigr),

(192)

because swapping
k
and
k+1
exchanges the corresponding contents.
Writing the representation matrix
S
of
\sigma_{k}
in the ordered basis

\{\ket{T},\ket*{T^{(k)}}\}
as

S=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{a}&\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{a^{2}}}\\
\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{a^{2}}}&-\frac{1}{a}\end{pmatrix},

(193)

the recursion reads
J_{k+1}=S\,J_{k}\,S+S
on this subspace. A direct multiplication gives

(S\,J_{k}\,S+S)\ket{T}=\mathrm{cont}_{T}(k+1)\,\ket{T},

(194)

Hence
J_{k+1}\ket{T}=\mathrm{cont}_{T}(k+1)\ket{T}
, completing the induction.
∎
