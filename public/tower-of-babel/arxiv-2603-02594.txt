Low-Degree Method Fails to Predict Robust Subspace Recovery
He Jia, Aravindan Vijayaraghavan

Abstract
The low-degree polynomial framework has been highly successful in predicting computational versus statistical gaps for high-dimensional problems in average-case analysis and machine learning. This success has led to the low-degree conjecture, which posits that this method captures the power and limitations of efficient algorithms for a wide class of high-dimensional statistical problems. We identify a natural and basic hypothesis testing problem in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} which is polynomial time solvable, but for which the low-degree polynomial method fails to predict its computational tractability even up to degree k = n Ω ⁡ ( 1 ) k=n^{\Omega(1)} . Moreover, the low-degree moments match exactly up to degree k = O ⁡ ( log ⁡ n / log ⁡ log ​ n ) k=O(\sqrt{\log n/\log\log n}) . Our problem is a special case of the well-studied robust subspace recovery problem. The lower bounds suggest that there is no polynomial time algorithm for this problem. In contrast, we give a simple and robust polynomial time algorithm that solves the problem (and noisy variants of it), leveraging anti-concentration properties of the distribution. Our results suggest that the low-degree method and low-degree moments fail to capture algorithms based on anti-concentration, challenging their universality as a predictor of computational barriers.

1.1
Our Result

Our main contribution is a natural high-dimensional statistical problem where the Low Degree Advantage fails to predict computational tractability. Our problem is in fact a special case of the well-studied

Failure of low-degree polynomials in capturing anti-concentration.

Our results together identify a natural candidate setting (robust subspace recovery) where the low-degree polynomial method fails. Perhaps more interestingly, it suggests that the low-degree method does not adequately capture algorithms that are based on
anti-concentration properties
of the distribution. This is unlike concentration properties and tail bound which are often captured by high-degree moments of the distribution. The algorithms for robust subspace recovery
[
HM13
,
Bha+19
]
indeed rely on identifying a small but non-trivial
\alpha
fraction of samples that are located in a very small region — a well-spread anti-concentrated distribution is unlikely to have any such high density region. In fact, the recent work of
[
Bak+24
]
studies the problem of certifying anti-concentration with a view towards obtaining guarantees for clustering problems for general distributions. They construct low-degree polynomials to certify that there is no small halfspace of small width
\alpha
that contains
>C\alpha
probability mass. However, the degree of the certificate grows as
\Omega(1/\alpha^{2})
. For robust subspace recovery, we are in the regime where
\alpha=1/\mathrm{poly}(n)
. This seems to be challenging for such techniques including
[
BK21
,
Bak+24
]
.

New candidate instance for separations

The main contribution of this work is a natural high-dimensional problem (an average-case setting of robust subspace recovery) which may serve as a candidate for proving separation results between different classes of algorithms. We believe that our candidate instance (and variants) could present a challenge to other algorithm classes including the Statistical Query (SQ) model, and the Sum-of-Squares (SoS). Concretely, we conjecture that the average-case robust subspace recovery problem studied in this work (e.g., in Theorem
2
, or a generalization with an
\alpha
-mass on a
d
-dimensional subspace, in the computationally tractable range) is also a hard instance for polynomial time algorithms that fit within the SQ framework.

This conjecture is motivated by two main observations. First, our low-degree lower bound (Theorem
2
) shows that the first
k=O(\sqrt{\log n/\log\log n})
moments of the single-sample distributions match exactly. This perfect moment-matching provides strong evidence of indistinguishability for any algorithm that relies on low-degree statistics. Connections to low-degree method lower bounds are also known in certain settings
[
Bre+21
]
. Second, our simple algorithms and the existing algorithms for robust subspace recovery
[
HM13
,
Bha+19
]
for sub-constant
\alpha
rely on identifying approximate linear dependence among a relatively small subset of the sampled points. This procedure is not believed to be captured by the SQ model, which is restricted to estimating aggregate statistical properties of the entire population. Moreover such certificates of anti-concentration seem quite challenging even for powerful algorithm classes like sum-of-squares, where current techniques seem to require degree bounds that are
\mathrm{poly}(1/\alpha)

[
Bak+24
]
.

Comparison to Non-Gaussian Component Analysis (NGCA).

Our results differ from typical hardness results in settings like NGCA
[
Bla+06
,
DKS17
,
Dia+23
]
. The objective in NGCA is typically to find a single hidden non-Gaussian direction in high dimensions. Lower bound constructions based on the NGCA problem and its variants have played a major role in proving evidence of hardness for several average-case problems
[
DKS17
,
Bru+21
]
. For example, recent work
[
Dia+23
]
shows that a subspace recovery problem is hard for low-degree polynomials and Statistical Query algorithms when the non-Gaussian signal is confined to a subspace of co-dimension
1
(i.e., an
(n-1)
-dimensional subspace).
However, in our constructions, we have an
\alpha
fraction of mass on a
1
-dimensional or
0
-dimensional subspace; this makes an entire subspace of co-dimension
\geq n-1
to be non-Gaussian. In fact, this different flavor is expected – while the NGCA is believed to be computationally hard, our construction where the subspace is low-dimensional is algorithmically easy. This makes it significantly more difficult to design a planted distribution that matches all the mixed moments of the reference distribution. We hope that our new instance which feels significantly different from the NGCA instance will be helpful in understanding the comparative power of different algorithmic frameworks.

1.2
Technical Overview

Our main result is a separation, which we establish in two parts. First, we construct a hypothesis testing problem where a family of planted distributions
P
and a rotationally invariant null distribution
Q_{\mathrm{rot}}
are indistinguishable by low-degree polynomials. We establish this indistinguishability in two strong forms: exact moment matching up to degree
k=\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{\log n})
, and a vanishing Low-Degree Advantage for degrees
k=n^{\Omega(1)}
. Both of these results leverage nice structural properties of the distribution
Q_{\mathrm{rot}}
.
Second, we provide a simple, efficient algorithm that robustly solves the same problem.

The Moment Matching Construction.

The core of our first lower bound is the moment-matching claim. We first construct our null distribution
Q
as a scale mixture of Gaussians. This choice is crucial. A standard Gaussian is rotationally invariant, but its length (
\|X\|_{2}
) is highly concentrated.
We require our null distribution
Q
to satisfy two properties:
(1)
rotational invariance
, and
(2)
anti-concentration of its length (scale)
.

Our chosen
Q
(a scale mixture with the scale
\lambda\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
) satisfies both. Since both
P
and
Q
are designed to be rotationally invariant in the
(n-1)
-dimensional subspace irrelevant to the signal, two consequences follow:
1) The null distribution
Q
provides no information about the hidden subspace.
2) The moment-matching problem simplifies dramatically. We can analyze the moments along any arbitrary vector in the irrelevant subspace, reducing the
n
-dimensional problem to a 2D problem (one signal coordinate, one irrelevant coordinate).

We then focus on matching the 2D moment vectors of
P
and
Q
. Our strategy is to give a non-constructive proof to show the existence of
P
by effectively perturbing the moments of
Q
. The main technical challenge is to prove that this new, perturbed set of moments is valid, i.e., a distribution
\mu_{2}
defining them actually exists. In other words, we want to show that the moments of
Q
are sufficiently far from the boundary of the moment polytope.
To prove existence, we use Carathéodory-style geometric bounds. This shows that our perturbed target moment vector is valid if it lies within the convex hull of all possible moment vectors. We prove this by showing it has a positive Tukey depth. This is where the anti-concentration of the scale/length of
Q
becomes essential, as this property is inherited by the 2D distribution of the scale of
Q
.

We first establish a strong, two-sided anti-concentration bound, which relies on the Carbery-Wright inequality. Then a crucial step is to translate this two-sided bound into the one-sided bound required to lower-bound the Tukey depth. This is encapsulated in the following lemma.

Lemma 4

(Same as Lemma
14
)
.

Suppose
\nu
is a probability measure over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that satisfies the following anti-concentration statements: for any multivariate polynomial
p:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
of degree at most
k
,

\forall\delta>0,~\ProbOp_{x\sim\nu}\Big[\lvert p(x)-\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[p]\rvert\leq\delta\cdot\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(p(x))}\Big]\leq\eta_{k}(\delta).

Then for any
\delta_{1}>0
such that
\eta_{k}(\delta_{1})\leq 1/2
, we have

\ProbOp_{x\sim\nu}\Big[p(x)\geq\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[p(x)]+\delta\cdot\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(p(x))}\Big]\geq\frac{\delta_{1}^{2}}{8}-\eta_{k}(\delta).

This positive Tukey depth guarantees that our perturbed moment vector is in the interior of the convex hull, which proves the existence of our moment-matching instance up to degree
k=O(\sqrt{\log n/\log\log n})
. These properties of the null distribution
Q_{\mathrm{rot}}
also help in showing bounded low-degree advantage when for polynomial degree
k
.

Extending Low-Degree Method Hardness to Polynomial Degrees.

While our moment-matching construction completely fools polynomials up to degree
k=\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{\log n})
, standard heuristics suggest that low-degree lower bounds are strong evidence of hardness if they rule out polynomial of degree
k=\omega(\log(n))
. To robustly demonstrate that the low-degree framework fails to predict the tractability of this problem, we establish a bound on LDA for even higher degrees, i.e.,
k=n^{\Omega(1)}
. We use the same null distribution
Q_{\mathrm{rot}}
as in our moment-matching result. Its key structural properties, rotational invariance and strong anti-concentration of its length, remain crucial in proving a bound on the low-degree advantage.

We analyze a simplified planted distribution
P=(1-\alpha)Q+\alpha\Delta_{\bf 0}
, which mixes the null distribution with a point mass at the origin
0
. Using the anti-concentration of the length of
Q
, we first prove that the single-sample advantage
\textrm{LDA}^{(1)}_{\leq k}
is
O(1)
. A key claim that allows us to prove the bound on the single-sample advantage is the following:

Claim 5
.

Consider any degree-
k
polynomial
f
whose constant term is
0
. Then we have

\frac{|\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f(X)]|}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{Q}(f(X))}}\leq O(\sqrt{k}),

where
Q=Q_{\mathrm{rot}}
is the rotational invariant scale mixture of Gaussians.

We combine this claim with a tensorization argument (in Lemma
18
) to lift this to the multi-sample setting, showing that the multi-sample advantage remains bounded by a small constant.

The bound in the above claim (for a single sample) is independent of the dimension
n
. We remark that this claim is not true for a spherical Gaussian

even though it is rotationally invariant; for example the degree-
2

is concentrated around
n\pm O(\sqrt{n})
. An important property that we exploit about
Q_{\mathrm{rot}}

as a

of two independent random variables
X=\lambda g
, where
g\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a random vector (drawn from a rotationally invariant distribution), and
\lambda
is a random variable capturing the scale; in our case, it is also drawn according to the normal distribution which has good anti-concentration properties. This allows us to establish the above claim (see Proof of Theorem
17
). We remark that the low-degree lower bound construction also works for
n=1

g\sim_{u}\{-1,+1\}
). The choice of a normal random variable for
\lambda
is not crucial; one can use other choices of the scale distribution
\lambda
that satisfy Claim

(potentially with a different dependence on
k
) to achieve other desirable properties.

The Simple Robust Algorithms.

In contrast to the low-degree hardness, we show that this problem is computationally tractable by presenting two simple algorithms that sample subsets of points and check for approximate linear dependence. For
d
-dimensional hidden subspace, the first algorithm handles adversarial relative perturbations by normalizing samples and detecting if the
(d+1)
-th singular value of any subcollection of size
d+1
drops below a constant threshold. The second algorithm extends the algorithmic guarantees to the setting of worst-case additive perturbations. Both algorithms rely on the anti-concentration of
Q_{\mathrm{rot}}
to ensure that random tuples are robustly linearly independent (soundness).

2.1
Lifting Moment Matching to the Multi-Sample Setting

Moment Matching.

We say two distributions
P
and
Q
over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
have matching moments up to degree
k
if for any multivariate monomials
M:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
of total degree at most
k
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P}[M(X)]=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim Q}[M(X)].

The definition of LDA involves polynomials over multiple i.i.d. samples from a distribution. The following standard lemma shows that the single-sample moment matching property defined above directly implies that the expectation of any low-degree polynomial over the corresponding multi-sample distributions will also match. This allows us to focus our construction for the low-degree lower bound on a single-sample distribution.

Lemma 6
.

Let
P
and
Q
be two distributions over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
whose moments match up to degree
k
. Let
m
be the number of samples, and let
P^{\otimes m}
and
Q^{\otimes m}
be the product distributions for
m
i.i.d. samples. Then for any polynomial
f:\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\to\mathbb{R}
of total degree at most
k
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{(X_{1},\dots,X_{m})\sim P^{\otimes m}}[f(X_{1},\dots,X_{m})]=\mathbb{E}_{(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{m})\sim Q^{\otimes m}}[f(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{m})].

Proof.

By linearity of expectation, it suffices to prove the equality for any monomial
M(X_{1},\dots,X_{m})
of total degree at most
k
. We can write any such monomial
M
as a product of per-sample monomials:

M(X_{1},\dots,X_{m})=\prod_{i=1}^{m}M_{i}(X_{i})

where the total degree is
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\deg(M_{i})\leq k
and hence each
\deg(M_{i})\leq k
.
By independence of the samples,

\mathbb{E}_{P^{\otimes m}}[M]=\mathbb{E}_{P^{\otimes m}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{m}M_{i}(X_{i})\right]=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{X_{i}\sim P}[M_{i}(X_{i})].

By the moment matching assumption,

\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{X_{i}\sim P}[M_{i}(X_{i})]=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{Y_{i}\sim Q}[M_{i}(Y_{i})]=\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\otimes m}}[M].

This proves the claim.

2.2
Hermite Polynomials

We use the standard probabilist’s Hermite polynomials, denoted by
\mathrm{He}_{m}(x)
. For our analysis, we will use the normalized Hermite polynomials, defined as:

h_{m}(x)=\frac{\mathrm{He}_{m}(x)}{\sqrt{m!}}.

The most important property of these normalized polynomials is that they form a complete orthonormal basis for the space of square-integrable functions with respect to the standard Gaussian measure
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, i.e., for any
i,j\geq 0
,

\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}[h_{i}(x)h_{j}(x)]=\begin{cases}1,&i=j,\\
0,&i\neq j.\end{cases}

This orthonormality also extends to higher dimensions. For reference, these normalized Hermite polynomials also have the following standard identity, which relates them to the monomial basis:

h_{m}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor}a_{m,j}x^{m-2j},~~~a_{m,j}=\frac{(-1)^{j}\sqrt{m!}}{2^{j}j!(m-2j)!}.

As outlined in the proof of Theorem

We begin by defining a two-dimensional distribution

used in Section

Consider the joint distribution
\nu
over

defined as follows:

2.

\displaystyle\nu(\lambda,z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\lambda^{2}/2}\cdot\frac{1}{|\lambda|\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z^{2}/(2\lambda^{2})}.

3.2

The following anti-concentration property of the distribution

Lemma 9

Suppose

such that

\forall\delta>0,~\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\ProbOp_{(\lambda,z)\sim\nu}\Big[\big|p_{\beta}(\lambda,z)-t\big|\leq\delta\cdot|\mathbb{E}[p_{\beta}]|\Big]\leq C\cdot k\cdot\Big(\sqrt{k}\delta\Big)^{\frac{1}{2k}}.

(5)

The proof of Lemma

Theorem 10

(Carbery-Wright,
[

Let

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{N(0,I_{2})}[q(\lambda,g)]

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{N(0,I_{2})}(q(\lambda,g))

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq i_{1},i_{2}\leq k}\gamma_{i_{1},i_{2}}\mathbb{E}_{N(0,I_{2})}[h_{i_{1}}(\lambda)^{2}h_{i_{2}}(g)^{2}]-\gamma_{0,0}^{2}=\sum_{i_{1},i_{2}\in\mathcal{I}^{\prime}}\gamma_{i_{1},i_{2}}^{2}

\beta_{0,0}=\sum_{0\leq i_{1},i_{2}\leq k,i_{1},i_{2}\text{ even}}a_{i_{1},\frac{i_{1}}{2}}a_{i_{2},\frac{i_{2}}{2}}\gamma_{i_{1},i_{2}},~~~a_{i,\frac{i}{2}}=\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\frac{i}{2}}\frac{\sqrt{i!}}{\left(\frac{i}{2}\right)!}.

3.3
Tukey depth and Existence of Distribution Matching Moments

Next we connect the anti-concentration properties from Lemma
9
to the convex geometry required for our moment-matching proof in Section
3

\theta
is in the convex hull of the support of
\Phi(\lambda,z)

We first introduce the concept of Tukey depth, a geometric measure of data-centrality. We then use a Carathéodory-style theorem (Theorem
13
) that formally links positive Tukey depth to the existence of a vector within the convex hull of a set of random samples.

Definition 12

(Tukey depth)
.

Given a random variable
\Phi
in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
and a point
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
, the Tukey-depth of
\theta
is given by

h_{\Phi}(\theta)=\inf_{u:\|u\|_{2}=1}\ProbOp_{\Phi}\Big[\langle u,\Phi-\theta\rangle\geq 0\Big].

(6)

The above notion introduced by Tukey
[
Tuk75
]
plays an important role in statistics. The Tukey depth is also related to Carathéodory-style bounds for random polytopes as follows. To prove that
\theta\in\mathrm{conv}(\{\Phi_{1},\dots,\Phi_{m}\})
, it suffices by the hyperplane separator theorem to show that for every direction
u
, there exists
i\in[m]
such that
\langle u,\Phi_{i}\rangle\geq\langle u,\theta\rangle
. Hence, if we sample
m=\tilde{O}(D/h_{\Phi}(\theta))
random points
\Phi_{1},\dots,\Phi_{m}
i.i.d. from the distribution of
\Phi
, one can use Chernoff-Hoeffding bounds along with an
\epsilon
-net over unit vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
to argue that
\theta\in\mathrm{conv}\{(\Phi_{1},\dots,\Phi_{m})\}
w.h.p. The following theorem in
[
HLO23
]
gives a tighter bound.

Theorem 13

(Carathéodory bound for random polytopes based on Tukey depth,
[
HLO23
]
)
.

Consider a random vector
\Phi
in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
and let
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
have Tukey depth
h_{\Phi}(\theta)
. Then given
m=(3D+1)/h_{\Phi}(\theta)

\Phi_{1},\dots,\Phi_{m}
from the distribution of
\Phi
, we have

\ProbOp\Big[\theta\in\mathrm{conv}\Big(\{\Phi_{1},\dots,\Phi_{m}\}\Big)\Big]\geq\frac{1}{2}.

We will apply the above theorem with the random vector
\theta
being the candidate moment vector of interest, and

to show the existence of a valid distribution that matches the moments given by

\forall\delta<\delta_{0},~~\ProbOp_{x\sim\nu}\Big[p(x)\geq\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[p(x)]+\delta\cdot\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(p(x))}\Big]\geq 3\eta_{k}(\delta),

by setting
\delta_{1}=8\sqrt{\eta_{k}(\delta)}
. Here
\delta_{0}>0
a constant chosen such that
\eta_{k}(8\sqrt{\eta_{k}(\delta_{0})})<\frac{1}{2}
.

Proof.

Consider the r.v.
Z=p(x)-\mathbb{E}[p(x)]
. We have
\mathbb{E}[Z]=0
. By the anti-concentration property of
p
under measure
\nu
, we have

\displaystyle\ProbOp[|Z|\leq\delta\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[Z]}]\leq\eta_{k}(\delta),\text{ and }\ProbOp[|Z|\leq\delta_{1}\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[Z]}]\leq\eta_{k}(\delta_{1}).

(7)

Furthermore since
\mathbb{E}[Z]=0
, by Cauchy-Schwarz,

\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[|Z|]=\mathbb{E}\Big[Z~\mathbf{1}[Z\geq 0]\Big]\leq\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[Z]}\sqrt{\ProbOp[Z\geq 0]}.

Then

\ProbOp\Big[Z\geq\mathbb{E}[Z]+\delta\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[Z]}\Big]\geq\ProbOp[Z\geq 0]-\eta_{k}(\delta)\geq\frac{\mathbb{E}[|Z|]^{2}}{4\mathrm{Var}[Z]}-\eta_{k}(\delta).

(8)

Moreover

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|Z|]\geq\ProbOp\left[|Z|\geq\delta_{1}\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[Z]}\right]\cdot\delta_{1}\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[Z]}\geq(1-\eta_{k}(\delta_{1}))\delta_{1}\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[Z]}.

(9)

Combining (

9

\displaystyle\ProbOp\Big[Z\geq\mathbb{E}[Z]+\delta\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[Z]}\Big]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(1-\eta_{k}(\delta_{1}))^{2}\delta_{1}^{2}}{4}-\eta_{k}(\delta)\geq\frac{\delta_{1}^{2}}{16}-\eta_{k}(\delta),

for our choice of parameters.

Corollary 15
.

Let
\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
be a random variable with coordinates
\Phi_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}=\lambda^{\ell_{1}}z^{\ell_{2}}
where
(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})\in\mathcal{I}=\{(i_{1},i_{2})\neq 0:i_{1},i_{2}\text{ even},0\leq i_{2}\leq i_{1}\leq k\}

Proof.

Let

By Lemma

, we know that

\forall\delta<\delta_{0},~~\ProbOp_{x\sim\nu}\Big[p(x)\geq\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[p(x)]+\delta\cdot\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(p(x))}\Big]\geq 3\eta_{k}(\delta)

where
\delta_{0}>0
is chosen such that
\eta_{k}(8\sqrt{\eta_{k}(\delta_{0})})<\frac{1}{2}
.
Since

\displaystyle=\inf_{\beta:\|\beta\|_{2}=1}\ProbOp_{\nu}\Big[\langle\beta,\Phi-(1+\delta)\mathbb{E}[\Phi]\rangle\geq 0\Big]

\displaystyle=\inf_{\beta:\|\beta\|_{2}=1}\ProbOp_{\nu}[p\geq(1+\delta)\mathbb{E}[p]]

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{\beta:\|\beta\|_{2}=1}\ProbOp_{\nu}\left[p\geq\mathbb{E}[p]+c\delta\sqrt{k\mathrm{Var}(p)}\right]

Lemma 16

Let

4
Extending Hardness to Higher Degrees via Bounded LDA

In this section, we prove the first part of Theorem
3
. We extend our hardness result to polynomials of degree
k=\mathrm{poly}(n)
demonstrating that the LDA remains bounded in this regime. This effectively rules out any efficient algorithm that relies solely on low-degree polynomials.
We will use the same null distribution
Q
as in Section
3
, i.e.,
Q
is a scale mixture of Gaussians with Gaussian scales
\mu=\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Define the planted distribution
P=(1-\alpha)Q+\alpha 0
.

Theorem 17

(LDA Bound up to degree
k=n^{\Omega(1)}
)
.

For polynomials of degree at most
k
on
m
samples,

\textrm{LDA}^{(m)}_{\leq k}(P,Q)\leq\sqrt{(1+C^{2}\alpha^{2}k)^{m}-1}.

In particular, if
k=O(1/(\alpha^{2}m))
, then
\textrm{LDA}^{(m)}_{\leq k}(P,Q)=O(1)
.

The proof of the theorem has two steps. We first show a single-sample LDA bound and then lift it to
m
samples.
The following lemma shows how low-degree advantage scales from one sample to
m
i.i.d. samples for any distribution
P
and
Q
.

Lemma 18
.

Let
P,Q
be distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with finite moments up to degree
2k
. Suppose the single-sample low-degree advantage is
\textrm{LDA}_{\leq k}^{(1)}(P,Q)=\delta
. Then for any
m
,

\textrm{LDA}_{\leq k}^{(m)}(P,Q)^{2}\leq(1+\delta^{2})^{m}-1.

Proof.

Let
\{\psi_{j}:j\in\mathcal{J}\}
be an orthonormal basis of polynomials of degree at most
k
under
Q
with
\psi_{0}=1
.
For a multi-index
\gamma=(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{m})
, define

\Psi_{\gamma}(X_{1},\dots,X_{m})=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\psi_{\gamma_{i}}(X_{i}).

Because
Q^{\otimes m}
is a product measure,
\Psi_{\gamma}
are orthonormal with respect to
Q^{\otimes m}
. For any polynomial
f
of degree at most
k
, subtracting the constant
\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\otimes m}}[f]
does not change LDA so we may assume
\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\otimes m}}[f]=0
.
Then
f
can be written as

f=\sum_{\gamma\neq 0}c_{\gamma}\Psi_{\gamma}

where
\gamma\neq 0
means not all coordinates of
\gamma
are zero. Let
s(\gamma)
be the number of nonzero entries in
\gamma
. Then
s(\gamma)\leq k
since the degree of
f
is at most
k
. By orthonormality,

\mathrm{Var}_{Q^{\otimes m}}(f)=\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\otimes m}}[f^{2}]=\sum_{\gamma\neq 0}c_{\gamma}^{2}.

Let
\mu_{j}=\mathbb{E}_{P}[\psi_{j}]
. We have

\mathbb{E}_{P^{\otimes m}}[\Psi_{\gamma}]=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{\gamma_{i}}.

Since
\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\psi_{j}]=0
for
j\neq 0
,

\mathbb{E}_{P^{\otimes m}}[f]-\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\otimes m}}[f]=\sum_{\gamma\neq 0}c_{\gamma}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{\gamma_{i}}.

By Cauchy-Schwarz,

\textrm{LDA}_{\leq k}^{(m)}(P,Q)^{2}=\frac{(\mathbb{E}_{P^{\otimes m}}[f]-\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\otimes m}}[f])^{2}}{\mathrm{Var}_{Q^{\otimes m}}(f)}=\frac{\left(\sum_{\gamma\neq 0}c_{\gamma}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{\gamma_{i}}\right)^{2}}{\sum_{\gamma\neq 0}c_{\gamma}^{2}}\leq\sum_{\gamma\neq 0}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{\gamma_{i}}^{2}.

Similarly, when
m=1
, we have

\textrm{LDA}_{\leq k}^{(1)}(P,Q)^{2}=\frac{\left(\sum_{j}c_{j}\mu_{j}\right)^{2}}{\sum_{j}c_{j}^{2}}\leq\sum_{j}\mu_{j}^{2}.

When
c
and
\mu
are linearly dependent, the equation holds. Thus
\textrm{LDA}_{\leq k}^{(1)}(P,Q)^{2}=\sum_{j}\mu_{j}^{2}=\delta^{2}
.
Fix a subset
S\subseteq[m]
of size
s
. Group
\gamma
by the nonzero coordinates by the set
S
,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma:\{i:\gamma_{i}\neq 0\}=S,\\
\sum_{i\in S}\mathrm{deg}(\psi_{\gamma_{i}})\leq k\end{subarray}}\prod_{i\in S}\mu_{\gamma_{i}}^{2}\leq\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\prod_{i\in S}\mu_{j}^{2}=\prod_{i\in S}\left(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\mu_{j}^{2}\right)=\left(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\mu_{j}^{2}\right)^{s}=\delta^{2s}.

Sum over all possible
S
with size
s\leq k
,

\textrm{LDA}_{\leq k}^{(m)}(P,Q)^{2}\leq\sum_{s=1}^{k}\binom{m}{s}\delta^{2s}\leq(1+\delta^{2})^{m}-1.

We now prove Theorem
17
.

Proof of Theorem
17
.

We first bound the single-sample low-degree advantage. Let
f
be any polynomial of degree at most
k
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since adding a constant to
f
does not change LDA, we may assume the constant term of
f
is zero. Then

\mathbb{E}_{P}[f]-\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f]=-\alpha\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f].

Let
X\sim Q
. We can write
X=\lambda g
with independent r.v.
g\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{n})
and
\lambda\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Define
h(\lambda)=\mathbb{E}_{g}[f(\lambda g)]
. Then
\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}[h(\lambda)]=\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f(X)]
and by the law of total variance,

\mathrm{Var}_{Q}(f(X))\geq\mathrm{Var}_{\lambda}(h(\lambda)).

Moreover
h(0)=0
i.e.,
h(\lambda)
has no constant term. Hence we have reduced it to an analogous claim about the one dimensional scale distribution
\lambda
. We will now upper bound
|\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}[h(\lambda)]|/\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{\lambda}[h(\lambda)]}
.
Apply Lemma
11
to
h
by taking
q(\lambda,g)=h(\lambda)
,

|\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f(X)]|=\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}[h(\lambda)]\leq C\sqrt{k\mathrm{Var}_{\lambda}(h(\lambda))}\leq C\sqrt{k\mathrm{Var}_{Q}(f(X))}.

Thus,

\textrm{LDA}^{(1)}_{\leq k}(P,Q)\leq C\alpha\sqrt{k}.

Apply Lemma
18
with
\delta=C\alpha\sqrt{k}
,

\textrm{LDA}^{(m)}_{\leq k}(P,Q)\leq\sqrt{(1+C^{2}\alpha^{2}k)^{m}-1}.

In the above proof, we crucially used the property that
X\sim Q
can be expressed
as the product
X=\lambda g
of two independent random variables, where

\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
represents a scale distribution drawn from
\mu
,
and
g\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is drawn from any rotationally invariant distribution.
The specific choices of
g
and
\lambda
being Gaussian were for convenience.
We now explain precisely which structural property of the scale distribution

\mu
governs the LDA bound, and how the argument generalizes.

The central step in the proof reduces the
n
-dimensional single-sample
advantage to a bound on the ratio
|E_{\mu}[h(\lambda)]|/\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{\mu}(h(\lambda))}

for univariate degree-
k
polynomials
h(\lambda)
satisfying
h(0)=0
.
The ratio
|E_{\mu}[h]|/\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{\mu}(h)}
has a clean
characterization via the orthonormal polynomials
\{p_{j}\}_{j\geq 0}
for
the measure
\mu
.
Expanding
h(\lambda)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}c_{j}p_{j}(\lambda)
, orthonormality gives

E_{\mu}[h]=c_{0}
and
\mathrm{Var}_{\mu}(h)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}c_{j}^{2}
.
The constraint
h(0)=0
yields

c_{0}=-\sum_{j=1}^{k}c_{j}\,p_{j}(0).

By Cauchy–Schwarz,

c_{0}^{2}\;\leq\;\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{k}c_{j}^{2}\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}(0)^{2}\Bigr)\;=\;\mathrm{Var}_{\mu}(h)\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}(0)^{2},

so that

\frac{|E_{\mu}[h(\lambda)]|}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{\mu}(h(\lambda))}}\;\leq\;\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}(0)^{2}},

(10)

with equality achieved when
c_{j}\propto p_{j}(0)
for
j\geq 1
.
The quantity

\mathcal{K}_{k}(\mu,0)\;:=\;\sum_{j=0}^{k}p_{j}(0)^{2}

is the Christoffel sum for
\mu
at
0
(the inverse of the
Christoffel function
\lambda_{k}(\mu,0)^{-1}
).
Since
p_{0}\equiv 1
, the bound (
10
) becomes

|E_{\mu}[h]|/\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{\mu}(h)}\leq\sqrt{\mathcal{K}_{k}(\mu,0)-1}
.
The growth rate of
\mathcal{K}_{k}(\mu,0)
with
k
is therefore the
fundamental quantity controlling the LDA bound.
In particular, one can choose any scale distribution for which this quantity grows slowly enough with
k
yields the same kind of bound on the single-sample low-degree advantage, and hence the same lifting argument to
m
samples.

5
Simple Noise-Tolerant Algorithm

In this section we show that there are simple efficient algorithms that are not based on the low-degree polynomial method that can solve this problem efficiently. Consider the setting when the
\alpha=1/\mathrm{poly}(n)
fraction lies on a
d\leq 1
dimensional subspace. On the one hand, the low-degree method fails even when an
\alpha
fraction of points lie on the subspace. On the other hand, our simple algorithm samples points and checks whether there are
(d+1)
points (
one
point if
d=0
) that are close to a
d
-dimensional subspace (close to
0
if
d=0
). Moreover, it can achieve tolerance to worst-case errors, and rerandomization.

An elegant and clever algorithm for robust subspace recovery due to Hardt and Moitra
[
HM13
]
uses anti-concentration properties of
(1-\alpha)
points not on the subspace
S
: it gives efficient algorithms when
\alpha\geq\frac{\dim(S)}{n}
. Subsequent work by Bhaskara, Chen, Perreault and Vijayaraghavan
[
Bha+19
]
gave an improved algorithm in the smoothed analysis setting where the points not on the subspace are randomly perturbed (by a random vector of average length
\rho=1/\mathrm{poly}(n)
), and gave guarantees of recovery when
\alpha\gtrsim(d/n)^{\ell}
for any constant
\ell>0
in
(nd)^{O(\ell)}
time. In our setting where
d=O(1)
and
\alpha=1/n^{O(1)}
, we can pick an appropriate
\ell=O(1)
to get algorithmic guarantees. However this algorithm is only moderately noise tolerant: every point can be adversarially perturbed (moved) by
1/n^{O(\ell)}
. See also
[
BK21
,
Gao+26
]
for algorithmic results in other parameter regimes. We will instead give a simple algorithm specialized for the
d=O(1)
setting that is elementary and also allows points to be moved by an amount
\epsilon=\Omega(1)
.

5.1
Robust Subspace Recovery with
\epsilon
Relative Error Perturbations.

We define the hypothesis testing version of the problem below. We are given samples from a distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and the goal is to distinguish between the following two hypothesis:

•

NULL
:

\mathcal{D}=Q_{\mathrm{rot}}
where
Q_{\mathrm{rot}}
is the rotationally invariant distribution defined in Section
3
.

•

PLANTED
:

\mathcal{D}
is
any
distribution with at least an
\alpha
probability mass supported on a planted subspace
S
of dimension
d
.

Note that the

. Moreover, our algorithm will work even when the samples drawn from
\mathcal{D}
are then adversarially perturbed as follows.

Definition 19

(Adversarial

Draw

The argument for the

Lemma 21

(

In the setting of Theorem

Claim 22
.

There exists universal constants

\displaystyle\ProbOp_{\hat{x},\hat{y}\sim_{unif}\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\Big[|\langle\hat{x},\hat{y}\rangle|\geq\frac{c\sqrt{\log m}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{m^{3}},~~\text{ and hence by a union bound},

,

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i\in T}\beta_{i}^{2}-\sum_{i\neq j\in T}|\beta_{i}||\beta_{j}|\Big(\frac{c\sqrt{\log m}}{\sqrt{n}(1-\epsilon)^{2}}+\frac{2\epsilon+\epsilon^{2}}{(1-\epsilon)^{2}}\Big)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{1}^{2}\Big(\frac{c\sqrt{\log m}}{\sqrt{n}(1-\epsilon)^{2}}+\frac{2\epsilon+\epsilon^{2}}{(1-\epsilon)^{2}}\Big)\geq 1-\frac{(d+1)}{(1-\epsilon)^{2}}\Big(\frac{c\sqrt{\log m}}{\sqrt{n}}+2\epsilon+\epsilon^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle>\frac{1}{2}\qquad\text{ since }\epsilon<\frac{1}{8(d+1)},\text{ and }m<2^{o(n)}.

Proof.

To prove the algorithm works, we will show that there is (a) an approximate linear dependence between
d+1
of the points sampled from the
\alpha
portion in the subspace in the
PLANTED
case, and (b) no approximate linear dependence between any set of
d+1
points in the
NULL
case.

PLANTED
case (completeness):

Let
x_{i}
be the uncorrupted sample from
\mathcal{D}
corresponding to
\tilde{x}_{i}
, and let
\tilde{x}_{i}=x_{i}+z_{i}
with
\left\lVert z_{i}\right\rVert\leq\epsilon\left\lVert x_{i}\right\rVert
.
Given
m\geq C(d+1)/(\alpha(1-p))
, there exists in expectation at least
Cd
points from
\mathcal{D}
lying in a subspace
S
; hence with high probability there exists
T^{\star}\subset[m]
with
|T|=d+1
corresponding to points
\{x_{i}:i\in T\}
that belong to a subspace
S
of dimension
d
. Recall that
a_{i}=\tilde{x}_{i}/\left\lVert\tilde{x_{i}}\right\rVert
.

The submatrix
M_{T^{\star}}=(\frac{x_{i}}{\left\lVert x_{i}\right\rVert}:i\in T^{\star})
has
\sigma_{d+1}(M_{T^{\star}})=0
. Let
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
be a unit vector satisfying
M_{T^{\star}}\beta=0
. Then

\displaystyle A_{T^{\star}}\beta

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in T^{\star}}\beta_{i}a_{i}=\sum_{i\in T^{\star}}\beta_{i}\frac{x_{i}+z_{i}}{\left\lVert x_{i}+z_{i}\right\rVert}=\sum_{i\in T^{\star}}\beta_{i}\frac{x_{i}}{\left\lVert x_{i}+z_{i}\right\rVert}+\beta_{i}\frac{z_{i}}{\left\lVert x_{i}+z_{i}\right\rVert}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in T^{\star}}\beta_{i}\Big(\frac{x_{i}}{\left\lVert x_{i}+z_{i}\right\rVert}-\frac{x_{i}}{\left\lVert x_{i}\right\rVert}\Big)+\sum_{i\in T^{\star}}\beta_{i}\frac{z_{i}}{\left\lVert x_{i}+z_{i}\right\rVert}

\displaystyle\text{ (since }M_{T^{\star}}\beta=0\text{)}

\displaystyle\left\lVert A_{T^{\star}}\beta\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in T^{\star}}|\beta_{i}|\Big(\frac{\left\lVert x_{i}\right\rVert}{\left\lVert x_{i}+z_{i}\right\rVert}-1\Big)+\epsilon\sum_{i\in T^{\star}}|\beta_{i}|\frac{\left\lVert x_{i}\right\rVert}{\left\lVert x_{i}+z_{i}\right\rVert}

\displaystyle\text{ (since }\left\lVert z_{i}\right\rVert\leq\epsilon\left\lVert x_{i}\right\rVert\text{)}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\sum_{i\in T^{\star}}|\beta_{i}|\Big)\Big(\frac{2\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}\Big)\leq\frac{2\epsilon\sqrt{d+1}}{1-\epsilon}<\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\text{ (since }\epsilon<\frac{1}{8(d+1)}\text{)}.

NULL
case (soundness):

This follows from Lemma
21
. This completes the proof.

5.2
Model with Additive Perturbations

In this section, we gave a variant of the algorithm that also works with worst-case additive perturbations of bounded magnitude.

We consider the same hypothesis testing problem as before: We are given samples from a distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The goal is to distinguish between the following two hypothesis:

•

NULL
:

\mathcal{D}=Q_{\mathrm{rot}}
where
Q_{\mathrm{rot}}
is the rotationally invariant distribution defined in Section
3
.

•

PLANTED
:

\mathcal{D}
is
any
distribution with at least an
\alpha
probability mass supported on a planted subspace
S
of dimension
d
.

We now consider the setting where the samples drawn from
\mathcal{D}
are then adversarially perturbed as follows.

Definition 23

(Adversarial additive
(\eta,p)
-noisy sample from
\mathcal{D}
)
.

Draw
m
samples
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
drawn i.i.d. from
\mathcal{D}
. Then an adversary can perform the following for
\eta,p\in[0,1)
:

1.

Re-randomize to draw from
Q_{\mathrm{rot}}
: For each
i\in[m]
, with probability
p
we redraw
x_{i}\sim Q_{\mathrm{rot}}
, and with probability
(1-p)
it remains unaltered.

2.

Adversarial
\eta
-perturbation to each point: for each
i\in[m]
the sample
x_{i}
is adversarially perturbed to form
\tilde{x}_{i}=x_{i}+z_{i}
where
\left\lVert z_{i}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\eta
.

The input are the corrupted samples
\tilde{x}_{1},\dots,\tilde{x}_{m}
.

We analyze the following simple algorithm.

Noise Tolerant Algorithm for Additive Perturbations

Input:
Given
m

\tilde{x}_{1},\dots,\tilde{x}_{m}
, dimension

, parameter
\alpha\in(0,1)

(along with a subspace

Subsample

|T|=d+1

(i)

Let

A_{T}=(\tilde{x}_{i}:i\in T)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(d+1)}
.

(ii)

Theorem 24

(Noise tolerant algorithm with additive error perturbations)
.

There exists a universal constants
c,c^{\prime}>0,C\geq 1
such that the following holds when
m\geq 2(d+1)\log(4/\delta)/(\alpha(1-p))
and any
\delta\in(0,1),0\leq\eta\leq\frac{\alpha(1-p)\sqrt{n}}{C(d+1)\log(4/\delta)^{2}}
. Then there is an algorithm (given in Figure
3
with
\tau=\frac{\alpha(1-p)\sqrt{n}}{c\log^{2}(4/\delta)}
) running in time
O(\binom{m}{d+1})
that given as input
\tilde{x}_{1},\dots,\tilde{x}_{m}
generated as described above with
\left\lVert\widetilde{x}_{i}-x_{i}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\eta
can solve the testing problem with probability at least
1-\delta
.

In this model, the average length of a point drawn from
Q_{\mathrm{rot}}
is
\mathbb{E}[\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{2}^{2}]=\Theta(n)
. Moreover, an
\alpha
fraction of the points drawn from
Q_{\mathrm{rot}}
have length
\|X\|_{2}\leq c_{\star}\alpha\sqrt{n}
for some absolute constant
c_{\star}>0
– hence when
\eta\geq c_{\star}\alpha\sqrt{n}
, the hypothesis testing problem becomes statistically impossible. Theorem
24
shows that the algorithm handles
\eta
that is within a constant factor (for
d=O(1)
) of the best possible bound of
c_{\star}\alpha\sqrt{n}
.

The following claim shows that for most sample points drawn from
Q_{\mathrm{rot}}
, their length is at least
2\tau
.

Claim 25
.

There exists a universal constant
c>0
such that for any
\gamma\in(0,1/2)
, we have

\ProbOp_{X\sim Q_{\mathrm{rot}}}\Big[\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{2}\leq\gamma\sqrt{n}\Big]\leq\min\{c\gamma,1\}.

(13)

In particular, by setting
\gamma=\alpha(1-p)/(4c\log^{2}(4/\delta)(d+1))
, we get that the fraction of points drawn from
Q_{\mathrm{rot}}
whose length
\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{\alpha(1-p)\sqrt{n}}{2c\log(4/\delta)^{2}}
is at most
\frac{\alpha(1-p)}{4\log(4/\delta)^{2}}
.

Proof.

This follows directly from the small ball probability bound for a standard Gaussian i.e.,
\ProbOp_{g\sim N(0,1)}[|g|\leq\delta/2]\leq c\delta/2
, along with length concentration of a standard spherical Gaussian.

We now proceed to the proof of Theorem
24
.

Proof of Theorem
24
.

To prove the algorithm works, we will show that there is (a) an approximate linear dependence between many
(d+1)
-tuple of the points sampled from the
\alpha
portion in the subspace in the
PLANTED
case, and (b) approximate linear dependence between much fewer set of
(d+1)
-tuples of the samples in the
NULL
case.

PLANTED
case (completeness):

Let
x_{i}
be the uncorrupted sample from
\mathcal{D}
corresponding to
\tilde{x}_{i}
, and let
\tilde{x}_{i}=x_{i}+z_{i}
with
\left\lVert z_{i}\right\rVert\leq\eta
.
Given
m\geq 2\log(4/\delta)(d+1)/(\alpha(1-p))
, there exists in expectation
(1-p)\alpha|J|\geq 2(d+1)\log(4/\delta)
points from
\mathcal{D}
lying in a subspace
S
. Hence with probability
1-\delta/4
there are at least
d+1

lying in subspace
S
. For any tuple
T\subseteq J_{S}
with

\sigma_{d+1}((x_{i}:i\in T))=0
. By Weyl’s inequality, we have
\sigma_{d+1}(A_{T})\leq\eta\sqrt{d+1}\leq\tau/2
, as required.

NULL
case (soundness):

The soundness analysis is very similar to the soundness analysis of Theorem
20
. We know from Claim
25
, that for at least
1-\alpha/4
fraction of the samples
\{x_{i}:i\in J\}
drawn from
Q_{\mathrm{rot}}
, we have
\left\lVert x_{i}\right\rVert_{2}\geq 2\tau
. For these samples which constitute at least
(1-\alpha/4)
fraction, the additive perturbations can be viewed as multiplicative perturbations, and we can apply Claim
22
to get a bound.

Formally, among the
m
samples, let
J_{\textrm{big}}=\{i\in J:\left\lVert x_{i}\right\rVert_{2}\geq 2\tau\}
. Consider a modified set of points
x^{\prime}_{1},\dots,x^{\prime}_{m}
where for each
i\in J
we have
x^{\prime}_{i}=\tilde{x}_{i}
if
i\in J_{\textrm{big}}
and
x^{\prime}_{i}=x_{i}
if
i\notin J_{\textrm{big}}
. First we observe that these points

satisfy the conditions of the Lemma
21
as the perturbations satisfy
\left\lVert x^{\prime}_{i}-x_{i}\right\rVert_{2}<\frac{1}{8(d+1)}\left\lVert x_{i}\right\rVert_{2}
. Let
A^{\prime}
be the matrix with its
i
th column of
A^{\prime}
equal to
x^{\prime}_{i}/\left\lVert x^{\prime}_{i}\right\rVert
. Hence we can now apply Lemma
21
to conclude that for some absolute constant
c^{\prime}>0
, we have with probability at least
1-O(m^{2}\exp(-c^{\prime}n))
that

\forall T\subseteq[J]\text{ s.t. }|T|=d+1,~\sigma_{d+1}(A^{\prime}_{T})>1/2.

Moreover, we have from Claim
25
that

\mathbb{E}[|J\setminus J_{\textrm{big}}|]\leq\frac{\alpha(1-p)}{4(d+1)\log(4/\delta)^{2}}\cdot\frac{2\log(4/\delta)(d+1)}{\alpha(1-p)}\leq\frac{1}{2\log(4/\delta)}.

Hence with probability at least

1-\delta/2

\forall T\subseteq J\text{ s.t. }|T|=d+1,~~\sigma_{d+1}(A_{T})\geq\min_{i\in T}\left\lVert\tilde{x}_{i}\right\rVert_{2}\cdot\sigma_{d+1}(A^{\prime}_{T})\geq\frac{1}{2}\cdot 2\tau\geq\tau.

This concludes the proof.
