A high-resolution electrostatically actuated MEMS Fabry–Pérot tunable filter for LWIR imaging and sensing applications
Authors: Tengfei Xu, Huipeng Yang, Haiyang Hou, Siyao Xu, Zhipeng Yao, Peng Wang, Longfei Li, Kun Zhang, Jicong Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe long-wave infrared (LWIR) band lies near the peak of natural thermal emission, enabling stable passive imaging without active illumination, and it also covers characteristic spectral features of many important materials, making it a key spectral region for sensing and identification. However, the development of micro-electromechanical systems Fabry–Pérot tunable filters (MEMS-FPTFs) in this band is limited by strong material absorption, scarce low-loss thin-film options, and the difficulty of fabricating thick, low-stress, high-reflectivity distributed Bragg reflectors (DBRs). This work presents an electrostatically actuated MEMS-FPTF tailored for LWIR operation through an optical–mechanical co-design strategy. A low-loss Ge/ZnS DBR with > 92% reflectivity, combined with a C3-symmetric three-terminal ring suspension and a bulk-micromachining fabrication flow (C3 denotes threefold rotational symmetry with a 120° periodicity), enables high spectral finesse and stable large-stroke actuation. The device achieves a minimum full width at half maximum (FWHM) of 188 nm, a maximum quality factor (Q) of 63.7, and continuous tuning from 10.3 to 12 μm, representing advanced performance among LWIR MEMS-FPTFs. To further demonstrate application potential, we developed a dual-channel spectral–visual proof-of-concept recognition demonstration based on the MEMS-FPTF. Compared with a vision-only neural-network baseline, the hybrid model exhibits a significant improvement in recognition performance under illumination variations and complex background conditions. The proposed MEMS-FPTF offers a compact, low-power, and high-precision solution for LWIR spectral filtering, with strong promise for mineral identification, autonomous perception, and remote spectral sensing.Subject terms: Optics and photonics, Electrical and electronic engineering

Introduction

The miniaturization of optical systems, driven by micro-electromechanical systems (MEMS) technology, has revolutionized the development of Fabry–Pérot (FP) interferometers, leading to compact, robust, and high-performance devices known as MEMS-FP filters1–4. Moving beyond static FP cavities with fixed spectral responses, the field has evolved toward dynamically tunable designs5–7. By electronically tuning the cavity length in real time, MEMS-FPTFs provide programmable wavelength selection and multi-band scanning, allowing a single device to replace multiple static filters and function as a reconfigurable spectral module. Their highly tunable filtering capability is indispensable for developing adaptive and intelligent spectral analysis platforms. Zhou et al. proposed the integration of MEMS-FPTFs with miniature infrared detectors to construct compact tunable spectroscopic instruments8, further demonstrating their strong potential for real-time spectral detection and versatile field applications9–11.

Nevertheless, despite the impressive progress in the exploration of dynamic MEMS-FPTFs, the further investigation and optimization of device characteristics is still urgently needed to be carried out12. Additionally, the majority of research on dynamically tunable MEMS-FPTFs has historically focused on the middle-wave infrared (MWIR) and visible spectral regions13–15. This is primarily due to the accessibility of a wide range of optical materials and the availability of mature fabrication processes. Driven by emerging application requirements, increasing attention is now being directed toward the LWIR band. LWIR is particularly attractive because it overlaps a major atmospheric transmission window and coincides with the peak thermal emission of room-temperature objects, which enables fully passive imaging and sensing without external illumination (Fig. 1a). With the rapid growth of deep-space exploration and thermal imaging technologies across industrial and defense applications, the demand for dynamic spectral filtering in the LWIR range has become increasingly urgent16–20. However, compared with VIS–MWIR devices, LWIR MEMS-FPTFs are more challenging due to stronger material absorption and much thicker quarter-wave stacks, which make low-loss, low-stress, high-reflectivity mirrors harder to achieve. Recently, significant progress has been achieved in the LWIR MEMS-FPTFs with both electrostatic and electromagnetic actuation schemes. Mao et al. have designed an electrostatically actuated MEMS-FPTF employing a single film top mirror, which achieved high spectral transmittance21,22. Subsequently, Zhou et al. have proposed an electromagnetically actuated MEMS-FPTF that exhibited a wide spectral tuning range, demonstrating the potential of magnetic actuation for extending the tuning range23. However, both projects have inherent limitations. Due to the limited reflectivity of the single-layer mirror, the spectral resolution of the electrostatically actuated device remains restricted, meanwhile the electromagnetic scheme suffers from high power consumption, difficulties in miniaturization caused by magnetic components, and a relatively slow dynamic response24. These factors collectively constrain the overall performance and application potential of LWIR MEMS-FPTFs.

Therefore, improving actuation efficiency, optimizing the stress distribution of the supporting structures, and rationally designing the DBR configuration are essential for realizing high-performance and mechanically robust LWIR MEMS-FPTFs25.

In this work, we propose an electrostatically actuated MEMS-FPTF based on an optical–mechanical coupling design strategy, achieving superior optical performance and high electro-mechanical actuation efficiency. By utilizing a low-loss Ge/ZnS DBR optical module, the mirror exhibits reflectivity exceeding 92% within the target LWIR band. Meanwhile, a C3-symmetric three-terminal ring suspension enables large displacement and low-stress mechanical stability. Benefiting from the synergistic enhancement between the optical and mechanical modules, the fabricated device attains a Q of 63.7 and an ultranarrow FWHM of 188 nm in LWIR band. This work paves the way for the development of high-performance MEMS-FPTF chips with enhanced optical precision and structural robustness. Furthermore, we develop a spectral–visual dual-channel framework by integrating the fabricated MEMS-FPTF with a vision neural network that effectively overcomes the inherent limitations of vision-only models, which are highly susceptible to noise and illumination variations. By providing high-resolution (Low FWHM) spectral information at the material’s characteristic absorption band, the MEMS-FPTF strengthens feature representation and significantly improves classification accuracy. Beyond mineral exploration, the proposed hybrid strategy shows broad application potential in fields such as autonomous driving, remote sensing, and other scenarios requiring robust and reliable perception.

Results and discussion

Optical principles and optical films design

As a compact and reconfigurable dispersive element, the MEMS-FPTF is typically integrated in front of an infrared detector or focal-plane array to enable wavelength-selective imaging. By scanning the passband across the target spectral range, the detector records a sequence of narrowband images, which can be stacked to form a three-dimensional spectral datacube (x–y–λ) for subsequent analysis, as schematically illustrated in Fig. 1b. In this configuration, the MEMS-FPTF performs on-chip spectral selection through cavity length modulation rather than bulky external optics, providing a miniaturized route toward tunable hyperspectral sensing. The optical operating principle of the proposed MEMS-FPTF is illustrated in Fig. 1c. The MEMS-FPTF mainly includes a fixed part, a bonding layer, and a movable part. The movable and fixed parts form an FP cavity with adjustable length. After a broadband beam enters the cavity, it can undergo multiple reflections. The constructive interference occurs when the optical path difference (Δ) satisfies the resonance condition (Δ = 2ncavdoptcosθ = mλm, where dopt is the length of the FP cavity, m is the interference order, and λm is the resonance wavelength). Under this condition, a standing wave is established within the cavity, selectively enhancing the transmission of resonant wavelengths, while off-resonance wavelengths are suppressed due to destructive interference. As the optical performance of MEMS-FP filters is primarily determined by the interference between two parallel reflecting mirrors, the choice of mirror structure plays a crucial role in defining the device’s spectral characteristics. The mirrors used in MEMS-FPTFs can be classified into metallic reflectors and DBRs. Metallic mirrors have relatively high reflectivity, while their intrinsic absorption loss incurs a highly optical energy dissipation and the degradation of spectral resolution. Moreover, metallic thin films are easy to be oxidized or sulfuretted in ambient conditions, which may reduce their reflectivity and even lead to the invalidation of devices. In contrast, DBRs with multiple dielectric layers realize high reflectivity utilizing constructive interference rather than free-electron reflection, thereby exhibiting substantially lower absorption loss than metallic mirrors. In addition, DBRs offer excellent thermal and chemical stability, and their optical properties can be precisely engineered by adjusting the refractive-index contrast and layer thickness, providing high spectral selectivity. Therefore, using DBRs as the high-reflectivity structures is the better choice for achieving high-performance MEMS-FPTFs.

The DBRs operate on the principle of constructive interference based on periodically stacking layers of materials with different refractive indices. Each layer has a quarter-wavelength optical thickness, as illustrated in Fig. 2a. Due to the unique layered structure, light waves reflected from each internal interface cumulatively interfere constructively at the front surface, thereby producing a strong and coherent reflection. The selection of DBR materials for the LWIR region must satisfy two essential conditions, including the sufficiently large refractive index contrast to ensure high reflectivity and a broad transparent spectral region covering the target wavelength range. Conventional dielectric materials, commonly used in visible or near-infrared DBRs such as SiO2 and TiO2, suffer from strong phonon absorption, which is unsuitable for operating at LWIR region. For LWIR region, Ge, ZnSe, and ZnS exhibit good infrared transparency, and the high refractive index contrast among these materials makes them suitable for the fabrication of DBR29–32. On the other hand, the substrate also plays a crucial role in determining the overall optical transmission and mechanical stability of the MEMS-FPTFs. We summarized the refractive indices and optical properties of different materials as shown in Table 1 and Fig. 2b. Float-zone silicon (FZ-Si) and Czochralski silicon (CZ-Si) are widely used substrate materials at LWIR region33. Although both materials have similar refractive indices (~3.4), their infrared transmittance differs significantly due to variations in impurity and oxygen content. Oxygen impurities and crystal defects introduced during the CZ growth process induce additional phonon absorption in the mid- and long-infrared regions, thereby reducing optical transparency. In contrast, FZ-Si is grown by zone melting without contact with the silica crucible, which results in lower impurity concentrations and superior infrared transmittance. Hence, we chose FZ-Si as the substrate to fabricate the MEMS-FPTFs. Besides, compared with the Ge/ZnSe alternative, the Ge/ZnS pair provides a higher index contrast, which enables achieving high reflectivity with fewer layer pairs and a thinner total stack. This is particularly beneficial in the LWIR, where the quarter-wave layers are intrinsically thick and thicker DBR stacks tend to exacerbate stress accumulation and interface-related losses, thereby degrading mirror flatness and peak transmittance (also see the Supplementary Information S1). Therefore, Ge and ZnS were adopted as the high- and low-refractive-index materials in this work and the DBR was designed with a center wavelength of 11.5 µm, corresponding to the central region of the LWIR band. Accordingly, the physical thicknesses of the Ge and ZnS layers are 0.718 µm and 1.306 µm, respectively, corresponding to the optical thickness of one-quarter of this wavelength.

Numerical simulations were subsequently conducted to evaluate the impact of the thin-film stacking configuration on the optical performance of the DBR and the MEMS-FPTF, as shown in Fig. 2c and d. In general, increasing the DBR layer number enhances the mirror reflectivity, thereby improving the finesse of the FP cavity and narrowing the FWHM. Meanwhile, thicker multilayer stacks inevitably accumulate higher intrinsic absorption and scattering losses, leading to a reduction in peak transmittance. As indicated by the simulations, when the DBR is increased from 3 layers to 5 layers, the FWHM decreases markedly from 640 nm to 115 nm, while the peak transmittance only slightly drops from 67% to 62%. Further increasing the DBR to 7 layers narrows the FWHM to 63 nm; however, the peak transmittance decreases substantially to 46%. In addition, an excessive number of DBR layers increases fabrication complexity and exacerbates stress accumulation, which may degrade interfacial flatness and surface smoothness, ultimately compromising yield and optical stability. This trade-off is particularly pronounced in the LWIR, where the multilayer stack thickness quickly reaches several micrometers even with a small number of layers. Taking spectral resolution, transmittance, and manufacturability into account, a 5-layer DBR (Ge/ZnS/Ge/ZnS/Ge) was selected in this work to achieve an optimal trade-off, providing a sufficiently narrow FWHM while maintaining a relatively high T.

Design and simulation of the drive electrode structure

For the electrostatically driven MEMS-FPTFs, the highly tunable filtration function is achieved by controlling the cavity length. When a voltage is applied across the top and bottom electrodes, an electrostatic field is established between the fixed and movable mirrors, thus inducing an electrostatic force (F) that drives the movable mirror to move downward, thereby leading to a reduced cavity length and a corresponding blue shift of the transmission wavelength of MEMS-FPTFs. However, when the applied voltage exceeds the pull-in threshold, the electrostatic attraction increases faster than the restoring spring force and ultimately overwhelms its maximum sustainable value, triggering pull-in instability. Although the FP cavity and DBR mirrors define the intrinsic optical response, the practical spectral performance of a MEMS-FPTF also depends critically on the mechanical behavior of the suspension structure during electrostatic tuning. High spectral resolution requires good cavity parallelism. Conversely, the achievable displacement and stress characteristics directly affect the effective tuning span and spectral degradation, thereby feeding back into the feasible optical stack selection. Therefore, the suspension geometry must be evaluated and optimized under this optical–mechanical coupling.

To identify an appropriate suspension structure, finite-element simulations were performed to compare the mechanical responses of cantilevers with different geometries, as shown in Fig. 3. We first considered a conventional central right-angle suspension as a baseline due to its structural simplicity and ease of fabrication. However, this configuration features a short effective compliant path and thus relatively high stiffness, which limits the attainable displacement under electrostatic actuation. While such a design can be adequate for applications requiring only a small tuning range (VIS–MWIR), LWIR MEMS-FPTFs demand a much larger tuning stroke and a more uniform stress distribution to preserve cavity parallelism and maintain spectral quality. Motivated by these requirements, we further investigated ring-type suspensions with more symmetric load transfer, including four-terminal and three-terminal structure, to achieve larger out-of-plane motion with reduced stress concentration. The results indicate that the central right-angle arm suffers from a severely limited displacement response and a more concentrated stress field under the same actuation condition, rendering it mechanically inferior to the ring-type designs; therefore, the following discussion focuses on the comparison between the two ring configurations. For the ring suspensions, the four-terminal ring exhibits a smaller deflection due to redundant anchoring points that introduce over-constraint, increase the effective stiffness, and thus restrict the displacement. In contrast, the three-terminal ring delivers a substantially larger and more uniform displacement response at the same actuation voltage (Fig. 3a), benefiting from its C3-symmetric geometry that promotes balanced load transfer while minimizing over-constraint35. As shown in Fig. 3b, the three-terminal ring reaches a maximum out-of-plane displacement of 3.14 µm at a 60 V bias, which is approximately 2 µm larger than that of the four-terminal configuration under the same driving voltage. Meanwhile, the stress distribution reveals pronounced stress concentration near the anchor regions in the four-terminal ring, whereas the three-terminal ring exhibits a markedly more uniform stress profile (Fig. 3c). This improvement is primarily attributed to the continuous curvature of the three-terminal design, which alleviates bending-moment discontinuities and suppresses localized strain accumulation. Consequently, as shown in Fig. 3d, the three-terminal configuration achieves the lowest peak von Mises stress at the same displacement, indicating superior compliance and a reduced risk of mechanical fatigue.

a Simulated displacement distributions of different suspensions under 60 V actuation voltage. b Displacement–voltage curves. c Simulated stress distributions under equal displacement conditions. d Maximum and minimum stress values of different suspensions

From a mechanical perspective, the variations in displacement and stress arise from the interplay between the electrostatic attraction, the geometry-dependent stiffness of the suspension beams, and the boundary constraints. The ring-type geometries reduce stiffness by elongating the effective deformation path, while smoother curvature mitigates bending-moment discontinuities that typically induce stress concentration. Moreover, the three-terminal ring structure demonstrates that three anchors are sufficient to stabilize a plane without introducing over-constraint, thereby maintaining stable out-of-plane motion and preventing mirror tilting under voltage actuation36. Consequently, considering the trade-offs between actuation efficiency, displacement amplitude, and mechanical robustness, the three-terminal ring structure was employed for the subsequent fabrication and experimental validation of the MEMS-FPTF device.

Design and simulation of the MEMS-FPTF chip

Based on the optical and mechanical simulations described above, we developed an electrostatically actuated MEMS-FPTF for LWIR band. Figure 4a illustrates the three-dimensional device structure, which consists of a bottom anti-reflection (AR) layer, a substrate, a bottom DBR, a bottom Au electrode, a SU-8 supporting layer (SU-8 is an epoxy-based negative photoresist), a top Au electrode, a top DBR, and a top AR layer. Here, the AR layer is introduced to reduce Fresnel reflection at the air interface and thus improve the T of the MEMS-FPTF (also see the Supplementary Information S2). A single-layer quarter-wave AR layer design was adopted at the target center wavelength (11.5 µm), and ZnS was selected due to its low LWIR loss and good process compatibility. SU-8 acts as the spacer and bonding layer defining the initial cavity. It was chosen for its low-temperature process compatibility, lithographically controllable thickness, and excellent electrical insulation, which together improve bonding robustness and fabrication repeatability. The top DBR serves as the movable mirror, while the bottom DBR is fixed on the substrate, thus forming an optical FP cavity.

Structural design and simulated optical response of the proposed MEMS-FPTF chip.

a Schematic of the multilayer configuration composed of FZ-Si, SU-8, Au, and a Ge/ZnS-based DBR stack. b Top-view layout showing the central AR-coated aperture and the arm structure of the MEMS-FPTF. c Transmission spectra under different driving voltages. d Variation of FWHM as a function of wavelength

The top view of the device layout is shown in Fig. 4b. The central region is the optical aperture and is coated with an AR layer; its clear aperture coincides with the DBR size (4 mm in diameter). While a larger aperture is generally desirable in LWIR systems to increase photon throughput, scaling the aperture in an electrostatically actuated MEMS-FPTF imposes much stricter constraints on mirror flatness and cavity parallelism. In practice, bonding-induced cavity non-uniformity and stress-induced mirror tilt become more pronounced as the aperture increases, which more readily broadens the linewidth and distorts the transmission peak. Moreover, a larger movable mirror requires higher structural stiffness to maintain mechanical stability and preserve pull-in margin; this typically necessitates a stiffer suspension, leading to a higher drive voltage and potentially reduced tuning stability and yield. In our design, the width of each suspension beam is 0.3 mm, and the spacing between adjacent beams is 0.4 mm. The initial cavity length sets the static resonance wavelength of the MEMS-FPTF, and the application of a driving voltage modulates the FP cavity spacing, thereby enabling wavelength-selective spectral filtering.

To further evaluate the tunable optical filtering performance of the designed MEMS-FPTF, we performed optical transmission simulations by incorporating the cavity-length variation obtained from a coupled electromechanical model. Under electrostatic actuation, increasing the driving voltage enhances the electrostatic attraction, thereby reducing the cavity length. Consequently, the transmission peak exhibits a pronounced blueshift, demonstrating the wavelength-tunable spectral filtering capability (Fig. 4c). We further quantified the FWHM of the simulated transmission spectra, as presented in Fig. 4d. The FWHM first decreases and then increases with the transmission wavelength, reaching a minimum value of approximately 112 nm, which corresponds to the optimal optical resonance condition.

These simulation results verify the effectiveness of the optical–mechanical co-design, providing theoretical guidance for the subsequent experimental characterization of the MEMS-FPTF device.

Fabrication and characterization of the MEMS-FPTF chip

Based on the optical–mechanical co-optimization, we fabricated the MEMS-FPTF through bulk micromachining processes to assess its optical performance and feasibility. The fabrication process flow is illustrated in Fig. 5.

Schematic process flow of the bulk micromachining technique for MEMS-FPTF fabrication. a Formation of the DBR and AR films on the top FZ-Si wafer. b Patterning of the top Au electrode. c Release of the movable suspension structure. d Formation of the DBR and AR films on the bottom FZ-Si wafer. e Patterning of the bottom Au electrode. f Definition of the SU-8 spacer and bonding layer. g Wafer alignment and bonding to form the device

The top structure was fabricated as follows. First, a DBR was formed by thermally evaporating alternating layers of Ge (718 nm) and ZnS (1306 nm) onto a double-side-polished FZ-Si wafer. Simultaneously, a single ZnS layer (1306 nm) was deposited on the back side to serve as the AR layer, which reduces surface reflection loss and enhances peak transmittance. Both the DBR and AR layers were patterned using a lift-off process. Subsequently, a 300 nm-thick Au film was deposited and patterned as the top electrode. The cantilever structure was fabricated by etching the substrate to define and release the suspension beams.

For the bottom structure, we utilized the same methods to prepare DBR, AR layer, and Au electrode. Note that SU-8 film with a thickness of 11 µm was employed as the support layer, which was spin-coated and photo-lithographically patterned on the substrate. The final MEMS-FPTF device was constructed by bonding the top and bottom components.

During fabrication, the structural quality and thickness accuracy of the DBR were characterized to validate the optical design and deposition process. Focused ion beam (FIB) imaging provides a cross-sectional view of the deposited DBR stack (Fig. 6a), where the alternating Ge and ZnS layers are clearly resolved with well-defined interfaces, smooth morphology, and uniform thickness. The multilayer stack exhibits excellent structural integrity, and the measured total thickness is 4.83 µm, in close agreement with the design target of 4.76 µm (1.3% deviation). This small discrepancy is attributable to expected process variations. The surface quality of the DBR was further evaluated using an atomic force microscope (AFM); within a 20 µm × 20 µm scan area, the root-mean-square (RMS) roughness is below 1.22 nm, indicating a high-quality surface finish. The optical reflectance of the DBR before and after processing was measured using Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Fig. 6c). The DBR maintains a high reflectance of 92–95% in the 10–12 µm band. Although a slight reduction in reflectance (~1.5%) is observed after processing, this change is likely introduced by photolithography and exposure to organic cleaning solvents, and the DBR largely preserves its high-reflectivity performance. These results confirm the robust optical performance of the fabricated DBR and provide a reliable foundation for realizing high-performance MEMS-FPTFs.

The overall optical performance of the fabricated MEMS-FPTF was further investigated to evaluate the device-level tuning behavior and spectral quality. Figure 6d presents a photograph of the device after packaging. The mirror region exhibits a flat and well-defined aperture, confirming that the fabrication and bonding processes effectively maintained the planarity and structural integrity of the mirror. As shown in Fig. 6e, when the applied voltage increases from 0 V to 60 V, the transmission peak exhibits a smooth and continuous blue shift due to the progressive reduction of the cavity length under electrostatic attraction, which covers a continuous tuning range of 10.3–12 µm. Furthermore, all transmission peaks exceed 35%, confirming the high reflectivity of the designed DBR and the low optical loss of the FP cavity. More importantly, no spectral distortion or mode splitting was observed, demonstrating the excellent mechanical stability of the designed MEMS-FPTF. Figure 6f summarizes the quantitative dependence of mirror displacement and transmission wavelength on the applied voltage. As the bias increases, the movable mirror is gradually pulled toward the fixed mirror, leading to a nonlinear decrease in cavity length. Consequently, the resonance wavelength exhibits a nearly parabolic dependence on voltage, consistent with the nonlinear electrostatic actuation behavior of MEMS-FPTFs. Notably, within the target tuning range of 10.3–12 μm, the maximum mirror displacement at 60 V is 1.29 μm, which is far smaller than one-third of the g of 6.15 μm (x ≪ g/3). This indicates that the device operates within a stable safety margin, well away from the pull-in limit.

To further evaluate the spectral resolution, the FWHM and quality factor (Q = λm / FWHM) were extracted from the measured spectra. The measured FWHM decreases and then increases with wavelength, reaching a minimum of 188 nm near 11.25 µm and increasing to 215 nm at 10.3 µm (Fig. 6g). This wavelength dependence agrees with the simulation trend, indicating that the FWHM is primarily determined by the DBR reflectivity, with the minimum FWHM occurring at the wavelength where the reflectivity is highest. The measured transmittance profile and FWHM differ from the simulated spectra mainly because the simulation assumes ideal conditions and does not fully account for non-idealities introduced by fabrication and measurement. In practice, multilayer absorption and thickness variations, together with bonding-induced cavity non-uniformity (mirror tilt and gap variations), broaden and distort the transmission peak. In addition, the finite FTIR spectral resolution and the angular spread of the incident beam further smooth and broaden the measured spectrum. Accordingly, the extracted Q values range from 48.1 to 63.7, with a mean of 57.9 and a standard deviation of 4.2. To evaluate device performance, we benchmarked our MEMS-FPTF against representative LWIR MEMS-FPTFs reported previously. Figure 6i compares the Q and center wavelength across different works. Benefiting from the co-optimized DBR mirror stack and the low-stress, large-displacement ring-symmetric suspension, the proposed electrostatically actuated MEMS-FPTF achieves a peak Q of 63.7 and competitive tunability, demonstrating leading performance among comparable LWIR devices.

Enhancing image recognition via MEMS-FPTFs

To demonstrate the application potential of the proposed MEMS-FPTF, we constructed a proof-of-concept visual–spectral fusion task targeting carbonate minerals. Calcium carbonate (CaCO3) is extensively used in cement, glass, and metallurgical industries, and reliable identification of carbonate-rich rocks is also fundamental to mineral exploration and subsurface engineering assessment. In recent years, vision-only neural networks have been employed for autonomous mineral identification by leveraging appearance cues such as color, texture, and morphology37 (Fig. 7a). However, appearance-based features are highly sensitive to imaging conditions; under illumination variations and complex background interference, false positives and missed detections readily occur (Fig. 7b), thereby limiting robustness in real-world complex scenarios.

In order to improve stability and reliability, we propose a visual–spectral hybrid model using MEMS-FPTFs that enhances recognition by incorporating LWIR spectral information. Because lattice vibrational energy levels in minerals are well matched to LWIR photon energies, many minerals exhibit discriminative “molecular-fingerprint” absorption features in the LWIR band. In particular, CaCO3 presents a pronounced absorption peak near 11.3 μm (Fig. 7c). The transmission spectrum of the fabricated MEMS-FPTF is continuously tunable across this characteristic band (Fig. 7d), enabling programmable scanning to acquire continuous spectral information of the target and thereby effectively sample its absorption feature during wavelength-selective operation. By multiplying the measured MEMS-FPTF transmittance with the reference CaCO3 spectrum, an output spectrum is obtained, which exhibits material absorption-induced transmission attenuation within the corresponding band (Fig. 7e), thereby providing material-specific spectral signatures. As shown in Fig. 7f, we construct a MEMS-FPTF-based spectral–visual dual-channel recognition framework, where a conventional camera provides visual information, and the MEMS-FPTF module enables voltage-programmable LWIR spectral scanning to generate wavelength-dependent spectral information. The two feature streams are extracted by a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) and fused to enhance material-discriminative representation, particularly under conditions where appearance-based cues become unreliable. The resulting performance gain is summarized in Fig. 7g. Compared with the vision-only baseline, incorporating MEMS-FPTF spectral information leads to consistently higher training precision and faster convergence. More importantly, under dim and cluttered scenes, the vision-only model can fail and misclassify CaCO3 as silicates, whereas the MEMS-FPTF-assisted model correctly identifies CaCO3 with a confidence score of 0.856, demonstrating improved robustness against illumination variation and background interference. These results indicate that integrating MEMS-based spectral sensing with visual neural networks offers a feasible pathway toward robust and high-accuracy intelligent mineral identification and exploration.

Conclusion

In this work, a high-performance electrostatically actuated MEMS-FPTF for the LWIR band was demonstrated through an integrated optical–mechanical co-design strategy. The use of a low-loss Ge/ZnS DBR with > 92% reflectivity and a C3-symmetric three-terminal ring suspension enabled high spectral finesse, resulting in a minimum FWHM of 188 nm, a maximum Q factor of 63.7, and continuous tuning across 10.3–12 μm. These results verify the effectiveness of the proposed film design and mechanical structure. To further assess the application potential, a visual-spectral hybrid recognition model was established using the measured MEMS-FPTF. By providing wavelength-selective information at the characteristic absorption band of the reference CaCO3, the MEMS-FPTF effectively overcomes the instability of vision-only neural networks under illumination variations and complex backgrounds. The hybrid model significantly enhances feature representation and classification robustness. Overall, the proposed MEMS-FPTF offers a compact and high-precision solution for LWIR spectral filtering and shows strong promise for intelligent sensing applications such as mineral exploration, autonomous perception, and remote spectral detection.
