Non-intubated VATS under combined serratus anterior plane block for hemodynamically fragile post-CABG delayed pericardial effusion: a case report
Authors: Mustafa Burgaç, Gözde Çeli̇k Ni̇ftali̇yev
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDelayed postoperative pericardial effusion after coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) may pose a significant risk in patients with limited cardiac reserve because of impaired ventricular filling and potential hemodynamic instability. Positive pressure ventilation (PPV) may reduce venous return and cardiac output in preload-dependent patients.Case presentationA 73-year-old male who had undergone CABG 10 days earlier presented with symptomatic delayed postoperative large pericardial effusion. Given hemodynamic fragility, a physiology-guided anesthetic plan that avoided intubation and PPV was implemented. Non-intubated video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (NI-VATS) with combined serratus anterior plane block (C-SAPB, deep and superficial) under spontaneous ventilation was performed. Sedation was achieved with dexmedetomidine (RASS 0 to − 1). Total procedural time was 40 min. Approximately 450 mL of pericardial and 500 mL of pleural fluid were drained. Hemodynamics remained stable (MAP 66–100 mmHg). Mild hypercapnia (PaCO₂ 46 mmHg) occurred without metabolic derangement.ConclusionNI-VATS under C-SAPB may represent a potential physiology-preserving option in carefully selected high-risk post-CABG patients with delayed pericardial effusion, particularly when avoidance of PPV is considered clinically advantageous.

Introduction

The pericardial space physiologically contains approximately 50 mL of serous fluid [1]. An increase in this volume is defined as pericardial effusion and may present with a broad clinical spectrum ranging from an asymptomatic course to cardiac tamponade [2]. With the growing frequency of combined surgical procedures and the widespread use of anticoagulant therapy, the incidence of postoperative pericardial effusion is thought to be increasing [3]. Although pericardial effusion is commonly observed after coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), the incidence of clinically significant effusions requiring intervention is approximately 1–2% [3].

It is important to distinguish delayed postoperative pericardial effusion from acute early postoperative effusion after cardiac surgery, as the latter may be associated with active bleeding or surgical complications and may require a different, more urgent management strategy. Particularly in patients with impaired left ventricular systolic function, large circumferential effusions may compromise ventricular filling, leading to tamponade physiology and subsequent hemodynamic instability [2].

In the presence of hemodynamic compromise, drainage is required, which can be achieved either by pericardiocentesis or by surgical creation of a pericardial window. Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) for pericardial window formation has increasingly become the preferred approach compared with thoracotomy, as it is less invasive and is associated with reduced postoperative pain and shorter hospital stay [4].

However, VATS is traditionally performed under general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation and one-lung ventilation [5]. The venous return–reducing effects of positive pressure ventilation (PPV) may further decrease cardiac output by impairing preload, particularly in preload-dependent patients with limited cardiac reserve, thereby exacerbating hemodynamic instability [5]. In addition, re-intubation in patients who have recently undergone median sternotomy is associated with an increased risk of pulmonary complications and prolonged hospital stay [3]. These pathophysiological and clinical considerations render non-intubated VATS (NI-VATS) a rational alternative in high-risk post-CABG patients. Preservation of spontaneous ventilation maintains negative intrathoracic pressure, thereby supporting venous return and contributing to hemodynamic stability [5].

However, in NI-VATS procedures, the provision of effective analgesia and adequate patient comfort is of paramount importance [6]. The PROSPECT guidelines recommend thoracic paravertebral block (TPVB) and erector spinae plane block (ESPB) as primary regional analgesic techniques for VATS; however, these approaches involve deeper needle placement and carry potential risks such as pleural puncture, vascular injury, and, rarely, total spinal anesthesia [7]. In contrast, fascial plane blocks are more superficial, relatively easier to perform, and anatomically distant from major neurovascular structures, making them potentially safer alternatives [7].

The serratus anterior plane block (SAPB) provides hemithoracic analgesia by targeting the lateral and anterior cutaneous branches of the thoracic intercostal nerves [8]. However, in fascial plane blocks, the spread of local anesthetic may be influenced by factors such as injected volume, injection level, and individual anatomical variations [8]. For this reason, a combined SAPB (C-SAPB), incorporating both superficial and deep approaches, was preferred in order to enhance analgesic efficacy and minimize the risk of block failure. This multi-plane strategy aims to optimize local anesthetic distribution and to provide a complementary effect in case of incomplete blockade.

In the present case, a physiology-guided anesthetic strategy was implemented in a hemodynamically fragile, high-risk post-CABG patient. Successful clinical management is described involving the creation of a pericardial window via NI-VATS under spontaneous ventilation, combined with analgesia achieved through superficial and deep SAPB.

Case report

The case was managed in the operating room and intensive care unit of Koşuyolu High Specialization Training and Research Hospital, Istanbul, Turkey. A 73-year-old male patient with a history of hypertension and coronary artery disease was evaluated on postoperative day 10 after four-vessel coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) because of dyspnea and decreased peripheral oxygen saturation. At that time, he was receiving enoxaparin, acetylsalicylic acid, clopidogrel, metoprolol, and zofenopril. Control transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 40%, mild tricuspid regurgitation (PASP 35 mmHg), and a large circumferential pericardial effusion measuring approximately 3 cm in width. Non-contrast thoracic computed tomography (CT) confirmed the pericardial effusion and additionally demonstrated bilateral diffuse loss of aeration, ground-glass opacities, and a prominent left-sided pleural effusion (Fig. 1).

On physical examination, the patient was conscious and cooperative. Breath sounds were coarse bilaterally and decreased at the left lung base. Respiratory rate was 16–20 breaths/min. Peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO₂) was 92% on room air and increased to 96% after initiation of supplemental oxygen in the operating room. Blood pressure was 140/50 mmHg, and heart rate was 100 beats/min. Laboratory evaluation showed hemoglobin 8.2 g/dL, albumin 26 g/L, C-reactive protein 100 mg/L, glomerular filtration rate 63 mL/min, and INR 1.22. Although the patient did not present with overt hemodynamic instability, the combination of delayed large circumferential pericardial effusion, reduced LVEF, mild hypoxemia, anemia, and recent sternotomy suggested a compensated but physiologically fragile state with a risk of decompensation. Based on the combined clinical, laboratory, and imaging findings, the patient was considered preload-dependent and with limited cardiac reserve (ASA IV).

Taken together, the findings suggested impaired ventricular filling due to elevated intrapericardial pressure and marked preload dependency. Therefore, the anesthetic strategy was centered on the patient’s underlying pathophysiology.

Given the presence of a large circumferential pericardial effusion, left ventricular dysfunction, and recent sternotomy, PPV was anticipated to further reduce venous return and cardiac output. Early re-intubation was also considered to increase the risk of pulmonary complications. Accordingly, a pericardial window under NI-VATS with preservation of spontaneous ventilation was planned. Packed red blood cells were prepared in anticipation of possible bleeding and hemodynamic instability, and a perfusionist was available in the operating room in case extracorporeal circulation (ECC) became necessary.

In the operating room, invasive arterial blood pressure, SpO₂, bispectral index (BIS), and bilateral cerebral near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) monitoring were applied. The patient was positioned supine with the head elevated at 30°. Before skin incision, regional blocks were performed under mild sedoanalgesia using midazolam 1 mg and fentanyl 50 µg to improve patient tolerance during block placement. Following block placement, a dexmedetomidine infusion was initiated at 0.3 µg/kg/h without a loading dose, and the Richmond Agitation–Sedation Scale (RASS) score was maintained between 0 and − 1.

For analgesia, a left-sided ultrasound-guided combined serratus anterior plane block (C-SAPB), including both deep and superficial injections, was performed. Using a high-frequency linear ultrasound probe placed in the transverse orientation at the level of the fifth rib on the left mid-axillary line, the latissimus dorsi, serratus anterior, intercostal muscles, ribs, and pleura were identified. An in-plane needle approach was used. For the deep SAPB component, 15 mL of 0.25% bupivacaine plus 5 mL of 2% lidocaine was injected between the serratus anterior muscle and the external intercostal muscle. For the superficial SAPB component, the same local anesthetic mixture was injected between the latissimus dorsi and serratus anterior muscles. The fifth-rib level was selected because it corresponded closely to the planned mid-axillary thoracoscopic incision site and was expected to facilitate spread along the lateral thoracic wall. A relatively high total volume was used to promote cranio-caudal spread within both fascial planes and to reduce the risk of incomplete blockade. Adequate sensory blockade was confirmed by pinprick testing before incision.

The patient was positioned supine with the left hemithorax elevated at 30°. Local anesthesia was administered to the planned incision site using 2% lidocaine. A 2.5 cm left-sided incision was made at the predetermined intercostal space along the mid-axillary line, and thoracoscopic access to the left pleural cavity was achieved using a 30° video thoracoscope. Following the evacuation of 500 mL of serohemorrhagic pleural fluid, a pericardiotomy was performed using an L-hook electrocautery. Approximately 450 mL of serohemorrhagic pericardial fluid was drained, and a 4 cm² posterior pericardial window was created to ensure continuous drainage (Fig. 2). After confirming hemostasis, a 24 F chest tube was inserted through the trocar incision and connected to an underwater seal drainage system.

During surgery, spontaneous ventilation was preserved, and oxygen was administered via a face mask at 4–10 L/min. Hemodynamic parameters remained stable (MAP 66–100 mmHg), and no inotropic or vasopressor support was required (Table 2). Bilateral cerebral NIRS values increased by more than 10% during the procedure, coinciding with improved peripheral oxygen saturation and hemodynamic stability after evacuation of the pleural and pericardial fluid. No additional sedative or analgesic agents were needed. Mild, self-limited hypercapnia (PaCO₂ 46 mmHg) occurred without metabolic derangement and resolved spontaneously. Lactate levels remained stable (Table 3). This transient and physiologically tolerable hypercapnia in the context of preserved spontaneous ventilation did not necessitate mechanical ventilatory support.

The total procedural time was 40 min (block performance 5 min, waiting time 10 min, surgical duration 25 min). No intraoperative transfusion was required. Postoperatively, the patient was monitored in the intensive care unit for 24 h and subsequently transferred to the ward. Patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) with tramadol was initiated (no basal infusion; 2 mL bolus [5 mg/mL], 15-minute lockout interval, maximum 100 mg over 4 h; total daily dose limit 400 mg). As part of a multimodal analgesic strategy and considering its anti-inflammatory effects on pericardial inflammation, ibuprofen was administered regularly every 12 h. The patient did not require additional tramadol, and visual analog scale (VAS) scores remained below 4 during the first 24 h.

On postoperative day 3, echocardiography demonstrated an LVEF of 60%. A localized residual effusion measuring 14 mm around the right ventricle was observed without signs of tamponade. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 4 in stable clinical condition.

Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this case report and any accompanying images.

Discussion

This case demonstrates that NI-VATS under preserved spontaneous ventilation can be a potential option in hemodynamically fragile patients with recent CABG, reduced left ventricular function, and delayed large circumferential pericardial effusion. The cornerstone of this approach was the integration of the patient’s pathophysiological characteristics into the anesthetic strategy. The value of this case does not lie in the introduction of a new regional block or surgical technique, but rather in the integration of NI-VATS, C-SAPB, and carefully titrated sedation into a physiology-guided anesthetic strategy for a post-CABG patient at risk of hemodynamic decompensation.

Large pericardial effusions increase intrapericardial pressure, limiting ventricular filling and rendering patients markedly preload-dependent [3]. PPV elevates intrathoracic pressure, thereby reducing venous return and potentially further decreasing cardiac output, particularly in patients with impaired ejection fraction [3]. In our patient, concomitant anemia and systemic inflammation amplified this physiological burden. Preservation of spontaneous ventilation maintained negative intrathoracic pressure, supported venous return, and allowed hemodynamic stability throughout the procedure without the need for vasopressor or inotropic support. The absence of lactate elevation suggests that tissue perfusion was preserved. The bilateral increase in cerebral NIRS values may be explained by improved oxygenation and enhanced hemodynamic status after pleural and pericardial fluid evacuation. Although this observation cannot establish causality, it supports the physiological rationale that preservation of spontaneous ventilation and relief of intrapericardial pressure may improve cerebral oxygen delivery in preload-dependent patients.

The contribution of pleural fluid evacuation should also be considered. In addition to pericardial decompression, drainage of the left pleural effusion may have improved lung expansion, respiratory mechanics, and systemic oxygenation. In preload-dependent patients, large pleural effusions may adversely affect cardiopulmonary interactions by impairing respiratory mechanics and modifying intrathoracic pressure relationships, whereas pleural fluid drainage may improve cardiac preload and overall hemodynamic status [9]. Therefore, the observed improvement in oxygenation, cerebral NIRS values, and hemodynamic stability was likely multifactorial and may have resulted from the combined effects of pleural and pericardial fluid evacuation. These findings support the physiological rationale for avoiding positive pressure ventilation in selected preload-dependent patients, rather than establishing a generalizable hemodynamic benefit.

Effective analgesia is critical in NI-VATS procedures [5, 6]. The PROSPECT guidelines list TPVB and ESPB among the preferred techniques [7]. However, TPVB may produce segmental sympathetic blockade due to its proximity to the thoracic sympathetic chain [10]. The literature reports an increased incidence of hypotension following TPVB, particularly with bilateral application or high-volume local anesthetic use [10]. Reduction in sympathetic tone may lower systemic vascular resistance, contributing to hemodynamic instability in preload-dependent patients. Although ESPB is generally considered a more superficial approach, the spread of local anesthetic to the paravertebral space has been documented, which may similarly be associated with potential sympathetic blockade and hypotension [11]. In patients with limited cardiac reserve and physiology approaching cardiac tamponade, abrupt reduction in sympathetic tone may be poorly tolerated clinically.

Positional considerations also played a decisive role in the selection of the regional anesthetic technique. For TPVB and ESPB, the optimal ultrasound imaging and needle insertion are typically performed in the lateral decubitus or sitting position [12]. In patients who have recently undergone median sternotomy, repositioning to the lateral decubitus position may impose torsional and lateral mechanical stress on the sternum. During the early post-sternotomy period, sternal stability is not yet fully established; therefore, excessive rotation or lateralization may compromise sternal healing and exacerbate postoperative pain [13]. In this context, the SAPB was preferred because it can be safely performed in the supine position without requiring additional patient repositioning. Furthermore, SAPB is administered in an anatomical region distant from major neuraxial and paravertebral neurovascular structures and is associated with a lower risk of pleural puncture and hemodynamic instability. These characteristics made SAPB a practical option in the present early post-sternotomy setting.

It is well recognized that local anesthetic spread in fascial plane blocks may be variable [14]. For this reason, a combined superficial and deep SAPB were planned. This approach was intended to achieve a broader dermatomal coverage by targeting both the lateral and anterior cutaneous branches of the thoracic intercostal nerves, thereby reducing the risk of incomplete blockade. The absence of additional opioid requirements and the consistently low pain scores observed in our patient suggest that the combined technique provided adequate analgesia.

In this context, avoiding intubation alone is not sufficient; excessive sedation may compromise respiratory drive, reduce sympathetic tone, and precipitate hemodynamic deterioration. Therefore, this strategy requires careful titration of sedation, continuous monitoring, and readiness to convert to general anesthesia if needed [15]. Physiological considerations were also prioritized in the sedation strategy. Dexmedetomidine, a selective α2-adrenergic agonist, exerts sedative, analgesic, and sympatholytic effects [16]. Unlike GABAergic agents, it produces minimal respiratory depression and largely preserves spontaneous ventilation [16]. In the literature, dexmedetomidine has been reported to maintain respiratory stability and improve tolerance to hypercapnia, particularly in non-intubated thoracic surgery [16]. Furthermore, its opioid-sparing properties are especially valuable during NI-VATS, as they help reduce the risk of respiratory depression [16]. Given the potential risk of bradycardia, a loading dose was avoided, and a low-dose continuous infusion was administered to maintain a targeted light sedation level (RASS 0/–1). This strategy minimized hemodynamic fluctuations while enhancing patient comfort.

The postoperative increase in LVEF from 40% to 60% may be interpreted as reflecting improved ventricular filling after decompression of the pericardial space and resolution of tamponade-like physiology. Although intrinsic myocardial recovery cannot be excluded, the rapid improvement after drainage suggests that impaired cardiac filling played an important role in the patient’s preoperative ventricular dysfunction [2].

This approach is also consistent with enhanced recovery and fast-track surgical principles. Although Enhanced Recovery After Cardiac Surgery (ERACS) protocols have primarily been defined for elective cardiac procedures, their core components, such as reduction of mechanical ventilation duration, opioid-sparing analgesia, preservation of physiological homeostasis, and early mobilization, were inherently implemented in the present case [17]. Avoidance of endotracheal intubation, maintenance of spontaneous ventilation, provision of multimodal analgesia through regional blockade, and early discharge from the intensive care unit are all aligned with the fundamental principles of the fast-track concept [17]. Particularly in patients with limited cardiac reserve who have recently undergone sternotomy, such physiology-preserving strategies may contribute to a reduction in pulmonary complications, shorter ICU stay, and accelerated functional recovery.

This case is based on a single-patient clinical experience and therefore cannot establish the safety or efficacy of this approach. Although the patient was physiologically fragile, he did not present with overt hemodynamic instability; thus, the findings should not be extrapolated to patients with acute postoperative tamponade, active bleeding, or severe circulatory collapse. The observed clinical improvement was likely multifactorial and may have been influenced by both pleural and pericardial fluid evacuation. Nevertheless, this case highlights the potential value of a physiology-guided anesthetic strategy that preserves spontaneous ventilation and minimizes sympathetic and positional stress in selected preload-dependent patients with limited cardiac reserve.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this case suggests that, in carefully selected post-CABG patients with delayed large pericardial effusion and recent sternotomy, NI-VATS under C-SAPB and carefully titrated dexmedetomidine-based light sedation may be considered as a potential physiology-preserving option. This single case does not establish safety or efficacy, and further clinical experience and prospective studies are required to better define patient selection, technical feasibility, and outcomes.
