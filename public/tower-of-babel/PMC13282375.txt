Erosion-driven delayed warming and marine stress prior to the end-Permian mass extinction
Authors: Shihan Li, Jiaheng Shen, Ethan L Grossman, Shuang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe end-Permian mass extinction (EPME) presents an anomaly: intense global warming lags the onset of the carbon isotope excursion (CIE) by ~50,000 years, challenging the presumed link between carbon cycle perturbations and climate warming. Using biogeochemical modeling, Bayesian inversion, and multiple proxies, here we show that incorporating continental erosion as a forcing term into the hyperthermal models can resolve this decoupling. Enhanced erosion, likely resulting from the terrestrial die-off of vegetation, accelerates continental weathering, which buffers early carbon release and delays global warming. This process also increases riverine phosphorus export to the oceans, fostering gradual marine anoxia and preconditioning the oceans for the extinction event. With these findings, we present a coherent unifying scenario for the EPME environmental dynamics. Furthermore, our study refines the hyperthermal paradigm, offering implications for future climate scenarios.Subject terms: Palaeoclimate, Carbon cycle

Introduction

Rapid global warming, both during geological hyperthermals and contemporary climate change, is commonly linked to the rapid release of carbon into the exogenic Earth1. However, this connection appears atypical across the End-Permian Mass Extinction (EPME; 251.941 ± 0.037 Ma), the most catastrophic biotic crisis of the Phanerozoic era2. Evidence from geographically diverse sections consistently shows that potent global warming, inferred from the oxygen isotope compositions (δ18O) of conodont apatite, lags behind the onset of carbon release marked by the global CIE (251.999 ± 0.039 Ma)3–6. This lag is estimated at 50 kyr based on a global δ18Oapatite compilation7 (Fig. 1), or ~ 25–90 kyr when accounting for age uncertainties (95% confidence interval, see Supplementary Information). The delayed warming is further supported by pCO2 reconstructions, which remain low at the onset of the EPME8. This offset is unexpected given the assumed linkage between carbon cycle perturbation and climate warming, as well as the synchronous CIE and global warming observed in other well-characterized hyperthermal events9. Although volcanic sulfur emissions can generate cooling episodes through sulfate aerosol formation, this effect is expected to be brief due to their short atmospheric residence time and therefore cannot account for the multi–tens-of-thousands-year lag observed here10. More notably, despite the absence of a significant temperature rise, the early CIE phase (i.e., prior to the marine extinction horizon) saw an intensified silicate weathering11,12 and the gradual development of marine anoxia13,14. These observations suggest that factors other than climate were the primary drivers of these environmental changes, though the exact mechanisms remain unresolved.

Here, we propose that one critical aspect is missing from previous EPME models: the role of enhanced erosion and its impact on chemical weathering. Extensive soil erosion events have been recorded in worldwide Permian-Triassic sediment successions, which are generally attributed to the terrestrial die-off of vegetation15–17. The timing of this change is suggested to be prior to the marine extinction18 and coincides with the CIE onset17,19. Field studies have demonstrated that enhanced erosion can promote silicate rock weathering by removing heavily weathered minerals in the saprolite zone and exposing fresh minerals to the surface20. Moreover, it has been suggested that the late Permian world was dominated by erosive, transport-limited environments21,22. Thus, enhanced erosion at the early CIE could potentially accelerate silicate weathering, which might buffer the initial carbon emissions and postpone global warming.

To test this hypothesis, we use a newly developed box model to reconcile multiple proxies and proxy-based environmental reconstructions (δ13C, δ18O, pCO2, 87Sr/86Sr, and pH) spanning the end-Permian CIE interval (251.999–251.902 Ma; see Supplementary Data 1). A Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) Bayesian inversion framework is developed to optimize the model fit to multiple proxy records. Our study not only promotes a holistic understanding of the dynamics of extinction, but also refines the current hyperthermal paradigm, offering implications for the impact of future climate scenarios on Earth’s environment changes.

Results and discussion

The modeling results show that incorporating continental erosion as a forcing term into the hyperthermal models could reconcile the unexpected temporal patterns in EPME records. An ensemble of 16 MCMC experiments further demonstrates the robustness of our main conclusion across a range of key parameter uncertainties. Results from the baseline experiment are presented below, while the inversion algorithm, parameter settings, and sensitivity experiments are detailed in the Methods and Supplementary Information.

Modeling environmental changes

Our simulations successfully reproduce the magnitudes of proxy-derived environmental changes across the EPME CIE, including a temperature increase of 8.7−0.8+0.8 °C (95% CI, the same applies below), a pH drop of 0.35−0.05+0.06, and a rise in pCO2 from 425 to 1617−183+197 ppmv, which corresponds to a 3.8−0.4+0.5-fold rise in pCO2 (Fig. 2). To begin with, enhanced erosion (Fig. 3a) at the early CIE accelerates continental weathering (Eqs. 1 & 2) and alkalinity input to the ocean. The average silicate weathering flux increases to 9.0−0.8+0.7 Tmol/yr, representing a 1.9−0.2+0.2 fold increase over the background value of 4.8 Tmol/yr. Similarly, the carbonate weathering flux rises to 18.1−1.4+1.9 Tmol/yr, corresponding to a 2.0−0.2+0.2 fold increase compared to the background level of 9.2 Tmol/yr. In addition, organic carbon burial increases due to enhanced nutrient export, contributing to an additional 0.7−0.2+0.2 Tmol C/yr as a carbon sink. Together, the enhanced weathering and organic carbon burial effectively buffer the initial carbon emissions, delaying global warming and ocean acidification. As a result, despite a cumulative release of ~ 4700 Gt carbon by 251.954 Ma, significant global warming and ocean acidification are not observed during this interval. Instead, global temperature shows no significant change (0.0−0.5+0.4 C∘) and pH exhibits a modest increase of 0.07−0.02+0.02 (Fig. 2a, d). Near the marine extinction horizon (251.941 Ma), the system records a temperature rise of 3.0−0.7+0.7 C∘ and a pH drop of 0.09−0.07+0.07 (Fig. 2a, d and Supplementary Table 5). These changes in magnitudes are comparable to those observed during smaller hyperthermal events, which were not associated with large-scale extinctions (e.g., Eocene Thermal Maximum 2, ref. 23). Thus, global warming and acidification likely act more as catalysts, accelerating biodiversity decline, rather than as primary extinction mechanisms4,8. In addition, modeled pCO2 remains relatively stable during the early CIE and begins to rise rapidly around 251.954 Ma, broadly consistent with transient pCO2 reconstructions available for this study interval8 (Fig. 2e).

Furthermore, enhanced weathering increases riverine phosphorus export to the oceans (see Supplementary Fig. 1). As a key nutrient, elevated P supply stimulates primary productivity, which in turn enhances the oxygen consumption in the water column, fostering gradual marine anoxia24. Modeled average intermediate ocean oxygen concentration decreases from an initial steady-state value of 112 μmol/L to 33−11+11μmol/L at 251.94 Ma, and ultimately to 8−5+7 μmol/L by the end of the CIE (Fig. 2f). Deep ocean oxygen shows a similar pattern, declining from 150 to 101−6+6 μmol/L at 251.94 Ma and further to 63−12+12μmol/L by the end of the CIE (see Supplementary Fig. 1). This anoxia development pattern is consistent with field observations, which indicate localized anoxia before the extinction across a range of paleolatitudes13,25, followed by a major expansion in areal extent near the extinction horizon24. Moreover, the declining trend in ocean oxygen concentrations shows minor variation across the ensemble of sensitivity experiments (Supplementary Table 6). Collectively, our model results strongly support nutrient-controlled marine anoxia as the primary killing mechanism for the EPME8,24,26.

Carbon emission scenarios

The model-derived total carbon emissions are shown to be sensitive to the climate sensitivity and initial silicate weathering flux (Supplementary Table 4). For instance, the baseline experiment with s (climate sensitivity, in C∘ per doubling of pCO2) =4.5 and Fsil0 (in Tmol/yr) = 4.2 diagnoses a total C emission of 16, 600−2300+2500 Gt, while adjusting the climate sensitivity to 3 and 6 alters the emissions to 25, 400−3100+3400 and 13, 100−1900+2100 Gt, respectively. Also, increasing Fsil0 from 4.8 to 11 results in an increase of estimated emissions to 23, 900−3400+3600 Gt. Despite these uncertainties, the ensemble of experiments converges on several key conclusions. Firstly, all experiments suggest a massive carbon emission (> 10,000 Gt) with an average δ13Csource between – 10 and – 17‰, implying a mixed organic and volcanic carbon source. These estimates are broadly consistent with previous studies27. In addition, the inversion-derived scenarios are characterized by a transition in emission rate near the EPME, coinciding with the timing at which global temperatures begin to increase rapidly (Supplementary Table 5). In the baseline experiment, the cumulative emissions in these two intervals are 4700−1000+1000 and 11, 900−2700+3000 Gt, with average emission rates of 0.10−0.02+0.02 and 0.23−0.05+0.06 Gt/yr, respectively (Fig. 2b). Such a transition likely correlates with the evolution of Siberian Traps Large Igneous Province (STLIP) volcanism from its initial extrusive and pyroclastic phase to the intrusive stage28,29. The lavas during the first phase might lack significant dissolved CO2 content, while the intrusive magmas may have come into contact with sedimentary rocks and liberated a large amount of greenhouse gases29,30. Notably, similar emission patterns have been identified in another study27. However, it is important to highlight that our model results indicate a shift to a lower-δ13C carbon source over the course of the CIE (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Table 4), which is consistent with the progression of STLIP eruptions but contrasts with findings from ref. 27. This discrepancy could be attributed to our model’s explicit consideration of dynamic organic carbon burial and erosion forcing, both of which play an important role in the carbon emission trajectory inversion results.

Drivers for erosion changes

The most notable result is a 2.1−0.2+0.2 times increase of erosion before 251.941 Ma, followed by a return to near pre-CIE levels (1.1−0.4+0.4) (Fig. 3a). This shift in erosion forcing derived from our inverse model is primarily driven by the varying rates of increase in the 87Sr/86Sr records, specifically 0.0005/Myr before 251.941 Ma and 0.00025/Myr thereafter (Fig. 1c). Although the exact values vary slightly among sensitivity tests, the overall trend is consistent (Supplementary Table 4).

The early increase in E(t) is consistent with independent denudation estimates from the South China tropical basaltic province, which indicate an increase from ~ 200 to ~ 400 t/km2/yr prior to the EPME19,31. This magnitude of erosion increase is comparable to that inferred since the Neogene32, implying a global event as the primary driver. Globally, a rapid transition in river morphology has been observed at the end Permian, shifting from high-sinuosity meandering rivers to low-sinuosity braided systems across various latitudes and climate settings18,33. Braided rivers are inherently more erosive, as their multiple active channels and frequent migration can expose and rework more sediment. This fluvial shift is widely attributed to the terrestrial ecosystem crisis and the major plant die-off18,33, which began about ~ 60 kyr before the marine extinction34,35. Vegetation can significantly reduce erosion rates through various mechanisms, such as mitigating extreme streamflow in response to precipitation, and providing extra soil cohesion and mechanical reinforcement through plant roots and organics36. Field and lab studies demonstrate that vegetation can enhance erosion resistance by orders of magnitude37. As such, turnover in terrestrial plants likely reduces the resistance of river banks and promotes the development of more erosive braided river systems, offering a mechanistic explanation for the model-inferred increase in erosion.

Notably, terrestrial crises were diachronous across latitudes. High-latitude records from the Sydney Basin indicate that ecosystem collapse in Gondwana may have begun as early as ~ 370 kyr before the marine extinction38. While our box model cannot resolve such spatial heterogeneity, the globally integrated erosion forcing E(t) is primarily constrained by the seawater 87Sr/86Sr record, which shows its major increase coincident with the onset of the CIE (~ 251.999 Ma), rather than several hundred kyr earlier. This suggests that although high-latitude die-off was regionally severe, its impact on global silicate weathering fluxes was likely smaller than that of the subsequent, more widespread low-latitude terrestrial collapse coincident with the CIE. This is likely because the high-latitude regions were dominated by kinetic-limited weathering and thus changes in erosion there would have had a relatively minor impact on the silicate weathering flux39.

The modeled E(t) shows a two-phase pattern, with a decline toward background levels following the EPME (Fig. 3a). A similar two-phase structure is also observed in other weathering-sensitive geochemical proxies across broadly comparable intervals, including the chemical index of alteration40 and lithium isotopes12. Both proxies begin to diverge from background values prior to the EPME and reverse their trends thereafter. We interpret this decline in E(t) as reflecting a shift in the dominant processes controlling erodibility and weatherability associated with plant die-off. The end-Permian Earth is thought to have been dominated by a globally transport-limited weathering regime22. While the initial loss of vegetation likely enhanced erosion by removing surface protection, the continued collapse of terrestrial ecosystems may have reduced erosion by eliminating processes such as root wedging and bioturbation41,42. In parallel, the absence of vegetation likely reduced continental weatherability by limiting processes such as transpiration, atmospheric water vapor transport, organic acid production, and soil CO2 enrichment42,43. From a modeling perspective, all these effects are likely integrated into the inversion-derived E(t), contributing to the inferred decline following the EPME.

The model therefore implies a time-dependent vegetation control: initial die-off enhances erosion and weathering, but continued terrestrial crisis ultimately suppresses both fluxes. This trend remains consistent across a variety of model assumptions, although the absolute values may vary (Supplementary Table S4). Our data-driven, inversion-derived E(t) and proposed underlying mechanisms could be further tested using spatially resolved weathering modules (e.g., those implemented in ref. 44,45), once high-resolution lithologic and geographic constraints become available. Nevertheless, our results underscore the potential for rapid changes in terrestrial plant communities to significantly alter hydrology and enhance continental weatherability on 104-year timescales, which has been largely overlooked in previous hyperthermal modeling studies.

A coherent EPME scenario

Integrating our modeling results with high-resolution dating of the STLIP eruptions29,46, we propose a coherent scenario that encapsulates the observed environmental changes across the EPME (Fig. 4). Prior to the STLIP eruption, the end-Permian world is in the supercontinent Pangea configuration, characterized by slow uplift and dry, tropical, and subtropical continental interiors22. The widespread presence of land vegetation favors the development of meandering river systems and the formation of stable regolith. Under these conditions, erosive transport-limited environments prevail, and the silicate weathering rate remains relatively low22.

Summary of the modeled processes of erosion-driven delayed warming and marine stress.

The left bright panel represents conditions before the volcanic eruption, and the right panel shows the sequence of processes leading to the marine extinction.

The initial phase of the STLIP begins around 252.24 ± 0.1 Ma, marked by extensive effusive activity28,29. The dissolved CO2 contents in lavas from this phase are likely low, and significant emissions that could produce global changes in the δ13C record only occur after 251.999 Ma. Emissions during the early CIE, potentially rich in SO2 and halogens, could have stressed the terrestrial ecosystem by causing acid rains and disrupting the ozone shield47. Also, the reorganization of ocean and atmospheric circulation could have triggered mega El Niño events48, further exacerbating ecosystem stress. The resulting die-off of rooted plant life exposes the land surface and alters the river morphology to low-sinuosity braided systems, which enhances the continental erodibility and increases the weatherability. The intensified erosion exposes fresh minerals and enhances silicate weathering, which buffers carbon emissions from 251.999 to 251.954 Ma. This process buffers the concurrent carbon emissions and maintains global temperatures at pre-perturbation levels, explaining the apparent lag between the onset of the CIE and subsequent global warming.

The enhanced silicate weathering also leads to increased exports of riverine phosphorus to the ocean, stressing the marine ecosystem on the eve of the EPME. Carbon emission rates accelerate around 251.954 Ma, coinciding with the onset of the second STLIP phase (251.907 ± 0.067 Ma) within uncertainty. The continued carbon emissions further increase the global temperature and exacerbate marine anoxia, eventually triggering the marine extinction. Although global warming and ocean acidification are not immediately lethal at the marine extinction horizon, their progressive intensification likely accelerates the pace of biodiversity decline.

To sum up, the first STLIP phase causes terrestrial ecological disturbances, which in turn place stress on marine ecosystems. The subsequent second phase intensifies carbon release into the exogenic Earth, culminating in widespread extinction in an already vulnerable ocean.

Implications for the future climate scenario

Erosive transport-limited regimes are estimated to contribute 72% of the global silicate weathering flux in the modern world39. It has been observed that weathering flux from these regimes varies significantly with erosion rate rather than temperature49. In addition, around 80,000 (> 20%) plant species, primarily from tropical rainforests, are estimated to be at risk of extinction under ongoing climate change50. This plant crisis may potentially exert strong control over silicate weathering flux in the next ~100 to ~ 10,000 years through altering erosion rates, an effect not considered in previous modeling studies that aim to predict the future pCO2 trajectory51. If future patterns follow those proposed for the EPME, enhanced silicate weathering associated with a terrestrial biocrisis could mitigate warming and ocean acidification but also impose greater anoxia stress on ocean ecosystems over 103–104-year timescales. While the exact magnitude and feedback may differ, our study underscores the necessity of incorporating the complex dynamics among plant die-offs, hydrological alterations, erosion rates, and silicate weathering flux when predicting future climate changes and their impacts on Earth’s environment and ecosystem.

Methods

The new model

We use a newly developed box model to reconcile multiple proxies and proxy-based environmental reconstructions (δ13C, δ18O, pCO2, 87Sr/86Sr, and pH) spanning the end-Permian CIE interval (251.999–251.902 Ma). Our model is based on the interactive Long-term Ocean-atmosphere-Sediment CArbon cycle Reservoir (iLOSCAR) model52, which is built upon the classical LOSCAR model53 and can efficiently simulate the carbon cycle dynamics during various hyperthermals54. To align with our research objectives, we expand the original iLOSCAR model with a dynamic phosphorus cycle55 and a strontium cycle56. Details of the expanded model components are in the Supplementary Information. Here we focus on the revised weathering parameterizations.

In this formulation, Fsil0 represents the global flux of silicate weathering solutes at the given pCO20. pCO2(t)pCO20nsi captures climate-related feedbacks on weathering and has been demonstrated to provide a simple yet effective framework for simulating carbon cycle–climate interactions during hyperthermal events (e.g., ref. 57). EtE0αsil represents the weatherability scaled by erosion and is the new parameter added to the original weathering equation. In our baseline experiment, we adopt αsil = 0.8 to reflect the erosive, transport-limited environments that dominated the late Permian22. Sensitivity tests confirm that the main conclusions are robust to variations in αsil. αcarb is set to 0.86 following32. αorgw is set to 0.25 to represent the net effect of erosion on organic carbon imbalance, accounting for both enhanced oxidative weathering and increased organic carbon burial32,58.

It has also been suggested that enhanced erosion can promote sulfide oxidation and the associated carbonate dissolution, which together act as an additional CO2 source to the ocean–atmosphere system59. However, the magnitude of this flux is estimated to be relatively small (~0.01 Gt C/yr; ref. 59) compared with the carbon emission rates inferred during our modeling interval (0.1–0.3 Gt C/yr). Moreover, sulfur isotope evidence indicates enhanced pyrite burial across the EPME, likely linked to widespread anoxia60. Increased pyrite burial would release alkalinity and promote carbonate precipitation, thereby partially counteracting the CO2 released by sulfide oxidation. We, therefore, do not explicitly parameterize erosion-dependent sulfide weathering in our baseline framework and instead treat any associated CO2 release as part of the net carbon emission term solved by the inversion.

MCMC inversion

The updated iLOSCAR model is coupled with a Bayesian inversion framework that employs the Markov chain Monte Carlo approach to reconstruct the mass and isotopic composition of carbon emissions (fcinpt, fδ13Ct), and erosion forcing (Et) over time. Specifically, smoothed global temperature (based on conodont δ18O data), marine carbonate carbon isotopes, and marine 87Sr/86Sr are used to inversely constrain the three forcing terms in θ. For each set of input forcing parameters, the model calculates the log-likelihood function (lθ,∣,xobs, which reflects how accurately the model outputs(xmodel) match the observed values (xobs). Using the MCMC algorithm, the model automatically converges to the maximum-likelihood region of parameter space and yields the posterior probability distributions for θ. For each experiment, we randomly draw 1000 input scenarios from the posterior to derive the temporal evolution of key environmental variables and their associated credible interval (e.g., Fig. 3). All experiments succeed in reproducing the target proxy records that are used in the inverse model. Details on prior distributions of θ and the MCMC algorithm settings are provided in the Supplementary Information.

Sensitivity test

To evaluate the robustness of our conclusions, we conduct an ensemble of 17 different MCMC experiments. These tests explore uncertainties in climate sensitivity, silicate weathering feedback strength, initial silicate weathering flux and pCO2, αsil, and varying boundary conditions not considered in the baseline. Justifications and detailed results are provided in the Supplementary Information. Across all scenarios, the core model outcome—a pulse of enhanced erosion preceding the EPME that contributes to delayed global warming and marine anoxia—is consistently reproduced. This convergence across parameter spaces highlights the robustness of our central hypothesis: enhanced erosion contributed to delayed warming and marine anoxia development prior to the EPME.
