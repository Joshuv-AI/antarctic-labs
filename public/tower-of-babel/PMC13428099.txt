Tideglusib-Preactivated Osteoblasts Encapsulated in Ceramic Particle-Reinforced GelMA Hydrogel for Enhanced Critical-Size Bone Defect Regeneration
Authors: Keqin Cao, Hongxu Shang, Yexin Li, Yanfei Tan, Molin Li, Yufei Ma, Xue Cai, Zhurong Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeCritical-size bone defects remain a clinical challenge due to limited intrinsic bone regeneration. This study developed a composite scaffold composed of osteoinductive biphasic calcium phosphate (BCP) ceramic particles and gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) hydrogel, loaded with calvarial osteoblasts (OBs) pretreated with the glycogen synthase kinase-3β (GSK-3β) inhibitor Tideglusib on surface of BCP particles, and evaluated its bone regenerative efficacy.MethodsBCP particles were characterized by X-ray diffraction and scanning electron microscopy. GelMA was synthesized and characterized by FTIR and 1H-NMR spectroscopy. Mechanical properties were assessed by compression testing. OBs were treated with Tideglusib to determine optimal concentration via CCK-8, scratch assays, qPCR, ALP and Alizarin Red S staining. Tideglusib-pretreated OBs seeded on BCP particles were evaluated for viability, proliferation, and osteogenic gene expression (COL-I, ALP). In vivo, 3.5 mm rat femoral defects were used to assess the bone regeneration potential of T-OB/BCP particle/GelMA (GPCT) composites in comparison with other groups by micro-CT and histology at 4 and 8 weeks.ResultsBCP (200–300 μm, HA/β-TCP ~30:70) incorporation enhanced GelMA compressive modulus ~4-fold. In vitro, 1 μM Tideglusib optimally activated Wnt/β-catenin signaling and promoted OB proliferation, migration, and osteogenic differentiation, with sustained effects after drug removal. OBs on BCP particles showed time-dependent upregulation of osteogenic genes, and Tideglusib pretreatment further elevated this expression. In vivo, micro-CT revealed significantly higher BV/TV, Tb.N, Tb.Th, and BMD in the GPCT group versus others. Histology showed more mature bone with numerous osteocytes, partial degradation of BCP particles, and new bone area fraction >70% in GPCT.ConclusionThe composite BCP particle/GelMA scaffold with Tideglusib-pretreated OBs provides mechanical reinforcement and sustained osteogenic stimulation, effectively accelerating critical-size bone defect regeneration. This ex vivo pharmacological priming strategy represents a promising translational approach for bone tissue engineering.

Introduction

Critical-size bone defects represent a persistent clinical challenge in orthopedics due to the limited self-regenerative capacity of bone.1,2 Autografts are constrained by donor scarcity and harvest-site morbidity,3,4 while allografts carry risks of immune rejection and disease transmission.5 Synthetic substitutes, including calcium phosphate (CaP) ceramics and polymeric scaffolds, have been introduced but their osteoinductivity and mechanical adaptability require further improvement to achieve better bone regeneration.6,7

Mechanical cues are pivotal in bone regeneration, as the local mechanical environment regulates cell behavior through mechanotransduction pathways. Osteoblasts (OBs) are particularly responsive to matrix stiffness and mechanical loading.8 Gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) has been extensively utilized as a hydrogel matrix in bone tissue engineering owing to its RGD cell-adhesive motifs and rapid photo-crosslinking.9–11 It provides a biomimetic extracellular matrix that supports cell adhesion and osteogenic differentiation, while also serving as a reservoir for diverse bioactive molecules.10,12,13 However, pristine GelMA exhibits inherently low mechanical competence, rendering it inadequate for load-bearing defects, and lacks inherent osteoinductivity.14,15 Particulate reinforcement through filler–matrix interactions effectively augments GelMA mechanics.16 For instance, bioactive glass nanoparticles elevated elastic modulus ~2-fold and compressive strength ~1.8-fold, while enhancing bioactivity.17 Thus, rational selection of reinforcing particles can simultaneously improve mechanical robustness and confer osteoinductive property. CaP bioceramics are among the most clinically employed synthetic bone grafts, valued for biocompatibility and osteoconductivity. Notably, specific CaP formulations possess intrinsic osteoinductivity, defined as the capacity to recruit and induce osteogenic differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells, and to promote ectopic bone formation in non-osseous sites even without exogenous growth factors or cells.18,19 This potential is linked to their surface micro/nano-topography and ion dissolution-reprecipitation dynamics.20,21 Despite their bioactivity, CaP ceramics in forms of porous blocks, including the most commonly used biphasic calcium phosphate (BCP), are relatively brittle and may fail to maintain structural integrity under load. To address this, incorporating ceramic particles within GelMA may be an effective compositing strategy. Besides, this composite also preserves the osteoinductive cues of CaP ceramics, which further potentiate osteogenic differentiation of seeded cells.18,22,23

Although CaP particles provide osteoinductive cues, their effects typically require days to weeks to fully manifest, whereas pharmacological agents act more rapidly. Tideglusib is a non-ATP-competitive glycogen synthase kinase-3β (GSK-3β) inhibitor, with recent studies revealing its pro-regenerative potential in orthopedics.24–27 Mechanistically, Tideglusib relieves GSK-3β-mediated degradation of β-catenin, allowing it to accumulate and translocate to nucleus to activate Wnt target genes that drive osteoblastic differentiation. Despite its osteogenic efficacy, direct scaffold-based delivery raises safety concerns. While non-cytotoxic at 50–100 nM, its long-term safety remains uncertain due to incomplete clinical follow-up.28,29 And GSK-3β inhibition carries potential risk of Wnt-driven tumor promotion.30 Therefore, ex vivo pretreatment of cells with Tideglusib, followed by implantation of only the pretreated cells, may activate osteogenic signaling while circumventing the risks associated with prolonged in vivo drug retention.

Bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) have been extensively explored for bone tissue engineering,31,32 but they exhibit progressive loss of proliferative and osteogenic capacity during prolonged expansion.33,34 In contrast, osteoblasts display a more stable phenotype, and can undergo self‑reliant differentiation into osteocyte‑like cells even without exogenous osteogenic supplements, while establishing a supportive microenvironment for co‑cultivation.35 Given these characteristics, OBs represent a promising cell source for composite scaffold‑based bone repair.

In summary, this study seeks to engineer a composite scaffold that harnesses the osteoinductivity of CaP ceramic particles in conjunction with Tideglusib‑mediated Wnt/β‑catenin activation. OBs seeded onto CaP particles will undergo ex vivo Tideglusib pretreatment and subsequent encapsulation within GelMA. This construct is designed to concurrently achieve mechanical augmentation and synergistic enhancement of osteogenic activity, with in vivo performance evaluated in a critical‑size bone defect model.

Materials and Methods

Preparation and Characterization of BCP Particles

Calcium phosphate ceramic particles were prepared from biphasic calcium phosphate (BCP) ceramic blocks produced by the National Engineering Research Center for Biomaterials (BAM®, Chengdu, Sichuan, China). Briefly, these ceramic blocks were ground into particles with an agate mortar, and sieved through meshes to obtain particles in the size range of 200–300 μm. The phase composition of these BCP particles was determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD, D8 PHASER, Bruker, Germany). Their surface morphology and microstructure were characterized by field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, S-4800, Hitachi, Japan). These particles were thoroughly rinsed with deionized water and sterilized using dry heat before in vitro and in vivo experiments.

Synthesis and Characterization of GelMA

GelMA was synthesized by reacting type B gelatin from bovine skin (G8061, Solarbio Life Sciences, Beijing, China) with methacrylic anhydride (MAA, Sigma-Aldrich) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at 50 °C for 2 h to achieve a degree of substitution (DS) of approximately 75%. The resulting solution was dialyzed against deionized water using a dialysis membrane (MWCO: 12–14 kDa) for 7 days to remove residual MAA, and then lyophilized for 4 days.36

The chemical structure and DS of the lyophilized GelMA were confirmed by proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) using deuterium oxide (D2O) as the solvent. Functional groups were identified by Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) over a spectral range of 4000–400 cm−1.

To prepare hydrogels, the lyophilized GelMA was dissolved in PBS at 65 °C to obtain a 10% (w/v) solution containing 0.1% (w/v) lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP, MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) as a photoinitiator. The GelMA/photoinitiator solution was filter sterilized (0.22 µm, Millipore) before photocrosslinking, and all subsequent implanting procedure was performed under sterile conditions. The solution was then crosslinked by exposure to blue light (405 nm) to form GelMA hydrogels. The internal porous microstructure and surface morphology of the crosslinked hydrogels and BCP particles/GelMA composites were characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The porosity of the GelMA hydrogels was determined by a liquid displacement method. The volume of the swollen hydrogels (Vwet) was measured by water displacement. After lyophilization, the dry weight (Wdry) was recorded, and the wet weight (Wwet) was obtained before lyophilization. Porosity was calculated as [(Wwet − Wdry)/ρwater]/Vwet × 100%.

Isolation and Characterization of Rat Calvarial Osteoblasts

Primary OBs were isolated from the calvariae of 7-day-old Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats following a protocol approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Ethics Committee of Chongqing Medical University. Briefly, the calvariae were surgically excised, meticulously stripped of the associated connective tissues and periosteum, and minced into small fragments (approximately 1 mm3). These bone fragments were then placed into culture dishes pre-coated with fetal bovine serum (FBS) and allowed to adhere for 5 h to fix them to the bottom. Subsequently, growth medium consisting of α-minimum essential medium (α-MEM, Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin was gently added, and the bone fragments were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2 to allow OBs to migrate out of the fragments. OBs were purified and harvested via the differential adhesion method.37,38 For subsequent experiments, the OBs were seeded at a density of 1 × 104 cells/cm2 and cultured in the same growth medium.

Cell Proliferation and Migration Assays

OBs were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well and treated with various concentrations of Tideglusib (0, 1, 2, 4, 8 μM; MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) for 24 h.39 After drug withdrawal, cell proliferation was assessed on days 1, 3, 5, and 7 using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Seven, Cat. No. SC119-02, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. For migration assays, a scratch was created in confluent cell monolayers using a 200 μL pipette tip. The cells were then incubated with Tideglusib at the indicated concentrations for 24 h. Wound areas were imaged at 0 h and 24 h using a DSZ2000X microscope (Chongqing Chongguang Industrial, Chongqing, China), and the migration rate was quantified by measuring the wound closure area with ImageJ software. The migration rate was calculated as the percentage of wound closure relative to the 0 h baseline.

In vitro Osteogenic Assays

For Tideglusib-treated groups, after 24 h of treatment, the medium was removed and washed and further cultured in standard growth medium without Tideglusib. Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity was assessed on day 7 using a BCIP/NBT Alkaline Phosphatase Color Development Kit (C3206, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) after cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. To evaluate extracellular matrix mineralization, cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde on day 21 and stained with 1% Alizarin Red S (ARS, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA).

For OBs loaded onto BCP ceramic particles, they were seeded in 48-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well with particles in the bottom. After 24 h of initial attachment, the cells were divided into two groups: a control group cultured in growth medium, and a Tideglusib-treated group treated with 1 μM Tideglusib in growth medium. After 24 h of treatment, the medium was removed, and both groups were washed and further cultured in standard growth medium without Tideglusib. Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity was assessed on day 7 using a BCIP/NBT Alkaline Phosphatase Color Development Kit (C3206, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). After rinsing, stained cultures were imaged using a Carl Zeiss Discovery V20 inverted microscope system (Göttingen, Germany).

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using an iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Gene expression quantification was performed using a SsoFast™ EvaGreen® Supermix Kit (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) on a CFX96™ Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). The mRNA levels of the following osteogenesis-related genes were examined: Runx2 (runt-related transcription factor 2), ALP, COL-I (collagen type I), Axin2 (axis inhibition protein 2), BMP4 (bone morphogenetic protein 4), Smad6 (SMAD family member 6), and Lef1 (lymphoid enhancer-binding factor 1). The housekeeping gene β-actin was used as an internal control. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Animal Study

All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Chongqing Medical University (approval No. IACUC-CQMU-2024-01081), and the animal experimental protocols complied with the Guidelines for the Ethical Review of Laboratory Animal Welfare (GB 35892–2018). Twenty-four 8-week-old specific-pathogen-free (SPF) male SD rats (220–250 g) were purchased from the Laboratory Animal Center of Chongqing Medical University. The animals were housed in a controlled environment with temperature of 20–21°C and humidity of 45–55%, and subjected to a 12-hour light/dark cycle. All animals were maintained with free access to standard food and water. The animals were randomly assigned to four groups: the Ctrl group (defects left empty, n=6), the GP group (defects implanted with cell-free BCP particles/GelMA composites, n=6), the GPC group (defects implanted with untreated OBs-loaded BCP particles/GelMA composites, n=6), and the GPCT group (defects implanted with 1 μM Tideglusib-preactivated OBs-loaded BCP particles/GelMA composites, n=6).

Rats were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 2% pentobarbital sodium (40 mg·kg−1) to crate the femoral condyle defect.40,41 A heating pad (37 °C) was used to maintain body temperature. The lateral aspect of the distal femur was shaved, and the operative field was scrubbed three times alternately with 10% povidone-iodine solution and 70% ethanol. A 15 mm longitudinal skin incision was made over the lateral condyle, followed by blunt separation of the vastus lateralis muscle to expose the condyle. Bilateral critical-sized defects (3.5 mm in diameter) were created in the distal femora using a slow-speed dental drill under continuous saline irrigation. Experimental constructs were prepared by mixing BCP particles with 10% (w/v) GelMA solution (1:2 volume ratio) containing 0.1% (w/v) LAP, injected into the defects, and photo-crosslinked in situ for 30s under 405 nm blue light. The muscle was closed with absorbable sutures, followed by the skin. Postoperatively, penicillin (20,000 U per rat) was given intramuscularly for three days, and buprenorphine (0.01–0.05 mg·kg−1) was administered subcutaneously as needed. At 4 and 8-weeks post-implantation, rats were sacrificed with carbon dioxide (CO2) inhalation, and the femora were harvested for subsequent histological and radiographic evaluations.

Micro-CT Analysis

At the scheduled time points, the harvested femora were scanned using a micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) system (μCT 80; Scanco Medical, Brüttisellen, Switzerland). The scanning parameters were set to an accelerating voltage of 70 kV and a beam current of 114 μA, with a spatial resolution of 21 μm per voxel. A 1 mm region of interest (ROI) centered on the original defect was reconstructed for analysis. Three-dimensional (3D) visualizations were generated using DATAV viewer software. Quantitative morphometric indices, including bone volume/total volume (BV/TV), trabecular number (Tb.N), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), trabecular separation (Tb.Sp), and bone mineral density (BMD), were calculated using CTAn software (Bruker, Kontich, Belgium).40 All analyses followed the manufacturer’s guidelines.

Histology

The harvested specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h and decalcified in 10% EDTA for 4 weeks. After gradient dehydration and paraffin embedding, 5-μm thick sections were prepared and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). To evaluate bone regeneration, the area of newly formed bone within the defect was quantified using ImageJ software and expressed as a percentage of the total defect area.

Statistical Analysis

All quantitative data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0 software (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA). All the in vitro experiments were conducted in triplicate, and repeated three times. Differences among multiple groups were evaluated via one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characterization of Biphasic Calcium Phosphate (BCP) Particles

XRD analysis confirmed that the BCP particles consisted of hydroxyapatite (HA) and β-tricalcium phosphate (β-TCP) (Figure 1A). Characteristic HA peaks appeared at 31.8°, and 32.9°, while β-TCP peaks were observed at 31.0° and 34.4°. Based on the relative intensities of the principal diffraction peaks, the HA/β-TCP weight ratio was approximately 30:70. SEM imaging (Figure 1B) revealed irregular polyhedral particles ranging from 200 to 300 μm in diameter. The particle surfaces were rough and contained abundant micropores (5–20 μm), with nanoscale grains contributing to the overall micro/nano-topography. Characterization of GelMA Hydrogel and Cytocompatibility

Methacrylation of gelatin was confirmed by FTIR and 1H NMR spectroscopy (Figure 2A and B). The GelMA FTIR spectrum exhibited a new absorption band at 1720 cm−1, corresponding to the ester carbonyl (C=O) stretching vibration of the grafted methacryloyl moiety. In addition, the amide I band (~1635–1640 cm−1) and the region associated with C=C stretching vibrations exhibited an altered lineshape, consistent with the successful incorporation of methacryloyl groups. The amide A band (~3300 cm−1, N–H stretching) and amide II band (~1550 cm−1) were largely retained with only minor intensity changes, indicating that the gelatin backbone remained intact following functionalization. 1H NMR analysis further revealed two well-resolved vinyl proton signals at δ 5.5 and 6.0 ppm (C=CH2), confirming successful methacrylation. Mechanical testing (Figure 2C) demonstrated that the compressive modulus increased with polymer concentration. Notably, incorporation of BCP particles enhanced the compressive modulus of 10% GelMA approximately 4-fold, achieving mechanical strength comparable to 15% pristine GelMA. SEM imaging (Figure 2D) showed that 10% (w/v) GelMA hydrogel possessed a uniformly porous architecture with pore diameters of 50–100 μm. Furthermore, in the BCP particles/GelMA composites, the ceramic particles were uniformly distributed within and covered by the porous GelMA matrix, with the particle incorporation not appreciably altering the pore size of the hydrogel. The porosity of the GelMA hydrogels (with or without BCP particles), as determined by a liquid displacement method, was approximately 80%.

OBs cultured within 10% GelMA exhibited a spindle-shaped morphology after 3 days and maintained high viability as evidenced by fluorescein diacetate/propidium iodide (FDA/PI) staining (Figure 2E). CCK-8 assay confirmed sustained viability and proliferation from day 1 to day 7 (Figure 2F).

Tideglusib Promotes OBs Proliferation and Osteogenic Differentiation in vitro

The effects of Tideglusib pretreatment on OBs proliferation seeded on tissue culture plate were evaluated using CCK-8 assay (Figure 3A). Among the tested concentrations, 1 μM Tideglusib produced the highest cell viability on day 7. Scratch wound healing assays (Figure 3B) further showed significantly faster wound closure in the 1 μM group compared with the untreated control and 4 μM group, indicating enhanced cell migration. To assess Wnt/β-catenin signaling activation, mRNA expression of Axin2, Smad6, Lef1, and Runx2 was quantified by qPCR (Figure 3C). Expression levels of all four genes peaked in the 1 μM Tideglusib group, confirming effective pathway activation. Furthermore, the ALP staining at day 7 and Alizarin Red S staining at day 21 (Figure 3D) demonstrated that 1 μM Tideglusib pretreatment enhanced both ALP activity and mineralization of OBs. Based on these results, 1 μM Tideglusib was selected for subsequent experiments.

Effect of Tideglusib Pretreatment on OBs Cultured on BCP Ceramic Particles

OBs were seeded onto BCP ceramic particles and, after initial attachment, were treated with 1 μM Tideglusib or growth medium for 24 h. Following drug withdrawal, cell proliferation was first quantitatively assessed by CCK-8 assay (Figure 4A). No significant difference in OD values was observed between the two groups on day 1; although the Tideglusib group exhibited slightly lower values on day 3, significantly higher OD values were recorded on day 5 (p < 0.05), confirming that 1 μM Tideglusib pretreatment supports the sustained proliferation of OBs cultured on ceramic particles. FDA/PI staining (Figure 4B) further provided evidence of biocompatibility, showing elongated, spindle-shaped cells adhering and spreading favorably on the BCP particle surfaces, with minimal dead cells observed in both treated and control groups. Furthermore, the long-term impact of this pretreatment on osteogenic differentiation was evaluated. qPCR analysis (Figure 4C) revealed that OBs cultured on BCP particles alone exhibited a time-dependent upregulation of COL-I and ALP mRNA from day 3 to day 7, confirming the intrinsic pro-osteogenic capacity of the BCP particles employed in this study. Notably, Tideglusib pretreatment further elevated the expression levels of both ALP and COL-I mRNA compared with the untreated BCP group on both day 3 and day 7. These findings indicate that pharmacological stimulation by Tideglusib and the material cues provided by BCP particles act in an independent manner to enhance the osteogenic differentiation of OBs, without mutual interference between the two stimuli. Consistent with the gene expression results, ALP staining (Figure 4D) on day 7 revealed markedly enhanced ALP activity in the Tideglusib-pretreated group compared to the untreated control. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the intrinsic osteoinductive BCP ceramic substrate augmented osteogenic differentiation, and 24-hour pretreatment with 1 μM Tideglusib further exerts a sustained pro-osteogenic effect on OBs grown on these BCP particles, enhancing their functional differentiation even after drug removal.

Tideglusib-Pretreated OB-Loaded Composite Scaffolds Accelerate Critical-Size Bone Defect Regeneration

Micro-CT analysis was performed at 4 and 8-weeks post-implantation to evaluate in vivo bone regeneration in a rat femoral condyle defect model (3.5 mm diameter) (Figure 5A). In the GPCT group, continuous high-density bony bridges spanned the defect, with further augmentation by week 8. In contrast, the untreated control group exhibited large radiolucent voids with sparse trabecular bone formation at both time points. Quantitative micro-CT analysis (Figure 5B–F) confirmed a progressive improvement in bone regeneration across the four groups. The GP group exhibited higher BV/TV, Tb.N, Tb.Th, and BMD values compared to the untreated control, as well as a corresponding reduction in Tb.Sp, indicating that the BCP particles/GelMA scaffold alone provides a beneficial osteogenic effect. The GPC group showed further significant increases in these parameters compared to the GP group, demonstrating that the incorporation of OBs enhances bone formation beyond that achieved by the scaffold alone. Notably, the GPCT group achieved the highest BV/TV, Tb.N, Tb.Th, and BMD values and the lowest Tb.Sp among all groups (p < 0.0001), confirming that Tideglusib pretreatment of OBs maximized the osteogenic capacity of the composite construct. Discussion

The present study demonstrates that a composite scaffold comprising osteoinductive BCP ceramic particles and a GelMA hydrogel matrix, loaded with Tideglusib-pretreated OBs, effectively accelerates the regeneration of critical-size bone defects. The key findings include: (i) BCP particle incorporation enhanced the mechanical competence of GelMA approximately 4-fold; (ii) 1 μM Tideglusib optimally activated Wnt/β-catenin signaling and promoted OB proliferation and osteogenic differentiation in vitro; (iii) ex vivo Tideglusib pretreatment conferred sustained pro-osteogenic effects on OBs seeded onto BCP particles even after drug removal; and (iv) implantation of this construct into a rat femoral condyle defect model resulted in significantly improved new bone formation and trabecular maturation compared with control groups. Collectively, these results validate the synergistic strategy of combining a mechanically reinforced hydrogel, osteoinductive ceramic particles, and pharmacologically primed OBs for bone tissue engineering.

A central challenge in the application of GelMA hydrogels for load-bearing bone defects is their low mechanical stiffness. Pristine GelMA typically exhibits a compressive modulus in the low kPa range, which is inadequate for withstanding physiological forces in critical-size bone defects.14,15 Though modulus of 15% GelMA was higher than 10%GelMA, the concentration of GelMA was chosen as 10% (w/v) in this study. This is mainly because previous reports demonstrated superior osteogenic potential of this concentration.42,43 In addition, higher concentrations (15% or 20%) with higher stiffness have been shown to significantly reduce degradation rates44 and decrease pore size and porosity.45–47 Which may hinder cell infiltration, nutrient diffusion, and tissue regeneration. Our finding that the addition of BCP ceramic particles elevated the compressive modulus of 10% GelMA by approximately 4-fold, approaching the mechanical properties of 15% pristine GelMA, is consistent with previous reports demonstrating that inorganic fillers such as bioactive glass, HA, or β-TCP can effectively reinforce GelMA networks through particle–matrix interfacial interactions.10,17 Notably, previous studies have primarily utilized nanoscale components (eg, HA nanoparticles, bioactive glass nanoparticles) uniformly dispersed within the GelMA. In contrast, our study employed BCP particles in the size range of 200–300 µm as discrete fillers, which—given the substantially larger particle scale—likely engages different reinforcement mechanisms at the particle–matrix interface. Importantly, the BCP particles employed in this study not only provided mechanical reinforcement but also retained the intrinsic osteoinductive surface topography of the osteoinductive ceramics, as evidenced by the presence of micro- and nanoscale features.19,22 The reinforcement strategy in the present study achieved a 4-fold enhancement, which is notable though more modest than the 1000-fold increase reported by Kumar et al using 3D-printed poly(ethylene glycol) terephthalate/poly(butylene terephthalate) scaffolds to reinforce GelMA hydrogels.48 This comparison highlights that while macroscopic scaffold reinforcement can achieve extreme mechanical augmentation, the particulate reinforcement approach adopted herein offers the distinct advantage of preserving injectability and moldability while simultaneously conferring osteoinductive functionality—a dual benefit that rigid scaffold-based reinforcement strategies may not provide. Thus, this dual functionality—concurrent mechanical augmentation and provision of an osteogenic substrate—represents a notable advantage over inert fillers that solely improve mechanics without contributing biological cues.

The osteoinductive potential of CaP ceramics has been extensively documented and is largely attributed to their surface micro/nanostructure, ion dissolution–reprecipitation dynamics, and protein adsorption capacity.18,20,21 In this study, the BCP particles exhibited an irregular polyhedral morphology with abundant micropores,19 characteristic of osteoinductive ceramics. Early studies in this field demonstrated that HA surfaces accelerate differentiation of MC3T3-E1 osteoblasts, with increased ALP activity and mRNA levels serving as markers of early osteogenic differentiation.49 Consistent with these observations, the BCP particles employed herein supported robust OB attachment, spreading, and sustained proliferation. Importantly, even in the absence of Tideglusib pretreatment, OBs cultured on BCP particles exhibited time-dependent upregulation of ALP and COL-I mRNA, confirming that the BCP particles actively promotes the osteogenic functional maturation of adherent OBs. These surface features have been shown to create a local microenvironment that can potentiate osteogenic differentiation.22,23 The combination of an osteoinductive ceramic substrate with a permissive GelMA hydrogel matrix thus provides a biomimetic niche that supports both the structural and biological demands of bone regeneration.

The selection of an appropriate cell source remains a critical consideration in bone tissue engineering. BMSCs have been extensively investigated and validated as a reliable cell source owing to their well-established multilineage differentiation capacity and robust osteogenic potential, with systematic reviews confirming the efficacy of BMSC-seeded scaffolds in enhancing bone regeneration.31,32 Alongside BMSCs, OBs represent an alternative cell source that offers distinct practical advantages. Calvarial-derived OBs exhibit a stable osteoblastic phenotype during in vitro propagation and have been shown to undergo self-reliant differentiation into osteocyte-like cells when cultured as 3D aggregates.35 Notably, well-characterized OB cell lines such as MC3T3-E1 (murine calvarial pre-osteoblasts) and MG-63 (human osteosarcoma-derived osteoblasts) provide stable, scalable, and phenotypically consistent alternatives to primary cells, with MC3T3-E1 cells showing similarities to primary OBs in terms of proliferation and mineralization capacity.50,51 Given that our study employed rat calvarial-derived primary OBs—which share developmental and phenotypic features with the MC3T3-E1 lineage—these parallels provide a useful context for interpreting our findings and support the broader applicability of osteoblastic cell sources. Our data demonstrate that OBs maintained characteristic spindle-shaped morphology, sustained viability, and active proliferation within the GelMA matrix and on BCP particle surfaces. Furthermore, OBs expressed elevated levels of osteogenic markers (COL-I, ALP) and exhibited enhanced ALP activity following Tideglusib pretreatment, underscoring their capacity to respond robustly to osteogenic stimuli. These observations support the premise that OBs—whether derived from primary tissue or established cell lines—represent a functionally predictable and practical cell source for engineering osteogenic constructs, offering an alternative strategy that complements, rather than competes with, BMSC-based approaches.

Tideglusib, a GSK-3β inhibitor that initially developed for Alzheimer’s disease, has recently attracted attention for its pro-regenerative effects in bone repair applications.26,27 In present study, concentration-optimization experiments identified 1 μM Tideglusib as the optimal dose for enhancing OB proliferation, migration, and osteogenic differentiation in vitro. Notably, the pro-osteogenic effects of Tideglusib pretreatment for 24 h persisted even after drug removal, as evidenced by sustained upregulation of COL-I and ALP mRNA and enhanced ALP activity in OBs cultured on BCP particles following the pretreatment period. This sustained effect is of particular interest from a translational perspective, as it suggests that a brief ex vivo exposure can prime OBs toward an osteogenic trajectory that continues unabated after implantation, thereby harnessing the therapeutic benefits of GSK-3β inhibition while circumventing the potential safety concerns associated with continuous drug delivery.52 This was in accordance with a recent study which also demonstrated that 24 hours treatment of Tideglusib effectively suppressed GSK3β phosphorylation at Ser9, as confirmed by Western blot analysis.39 The persistence of the primed phenotype may reflect epigenetic remodeling or sustained activation of autocrine Wnt signaling loops, although the precise molecular mechanisms underlying this durable response warrant further investigation. Different from several other GSK3β inhibitors (eg, CHIR99021 or SB216763), Tideglusib is an irreversible GSK‑3β inhibitor that sustains Wnt/β‑catenin activation and upregulates osteogenic genes even after drug withdrawal.53,54 This mechanism likely explains the prolonged osteogenic effects observed in our study after Tideglusib withdrawal. Future studies using Western blot analysis of GSK‑3β phosphorylation (Ser9) and β‑catenin, combined with transcriptomic profiling, could further elucidate the epigenetic or transcriptional mechanisms underlying this phenomenon. When compared with other GSK-3β antagonists such as lithium chloride, which has been reported to interfere with precursor cell recruitment and commitment, Tideglusib appears to exert its effects without disrupting early osteoprogenitor functions,26 highlighting its favorable biological profile. Specifically, Tideglusib was administered as a pretreatment—rather than incorporated into the hydrogel—to isolate the cell‑intrinsic priming effect, avoid uncontrolled release kinetics, and enable precise control over drug exposure timing. Nevertheless, it should be acknowledged that the optimal pretreatment duration and the long-term stability of the primed phenotype require systematic evaluation in future studies.29 Regarding the long‑term local delivery, studies integrate this priming strategy with 3D‑printed scaffolds for patient‑specific bone regeneration could present a novel direction.55,56 Further comparison of Tideglusib with reversible GSK‑3β inhibitors in 3D‑printed scaffolds would be more valuable to validate the role of irreversible GSK‑3β inhibition in sustained osteogenic effects.

The in vivo findings from the rat femoral condyle critical-size defect model (3.5 mm diameter) provide compelling evidence for the regenerative efficacy of the Tideglusib-pretreated OB-laden BCP particles/GelMA composite scaffold. Micro-CT analysis revealed that the GPCT group formed continuous bony bridges across the defect as early as 4 weeks post-implantation, with further augmentation by 8 weeks. The rat femoral condyle defect model has been extensively validated for preclinical assessment of bone regeneration scaffolds,40 and a 3.5 mm diameter defect in this site has been established as a critical-size defect that does not heal spontaneously without intervention.41 Micro-CT parameters including BV/TV, Tb.Th, Tb.Sp, Tb.N, and BMD serve as established quantitative metrics for evaluating bone healing outcomes in this model.40 In our study, quantitative parameters including BV/TV, Tb.N, and Tb.Th were significantly higher in the GPCT group than in all control groups, indicating not only increased bone quantity but also improved trabecular microarchitecture. Histological evaluation corroborated these findings, demonstrating that BCP particles were intimately encapsulated by newly formed bone, with the new bone area fraction exceeding 70% by histomorphometric analysis. In sharp contrast, the untreated control group exhibited minimal bone formation, with the defect largely occupied by fibrous tissue. These outcomes are consistent with the concept that the combination of an osteoconductive scaffold, osteoinductive ceramic particles, and pharmacologically primed OBs synergistically accelerates bone healing. The mechanical reinforcement provided by the BCP particles likely conferred sufficient structural integrity to the hydrogel to withstand the partial weight-bearing environment of the femoral condyle,57 while the osteoinductive surface and sustained pro-osteogenic phenotype of the pretreated OBs collectively drove robust new bone formation.

Limitations

Despite these promising results, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the current study employed a rat femoral condyle defect model, which, while widely accepted for evaluating bone regeneration, represents a partial weight-bearing site with distinct biomechanical and biological characteristics compared with large segmental defects in long bones or craniofacial locations.40 Future studies utilizing larger animal models with more challenging mechanical environments would provide additional insight into the translational potential of this construct. Second, although histomorphometric analysis confirmed substantial new bone formation, long-term follow-up beyond 8 weeks would be valuable to assess the ultimate fate of the scaffold material and the remodeling dynamics of the regenerated bone.58 Third, while Tideglusib pretreatment circumvented direct in vivo drug delivery, the duration of the sustained pro-osteogenic effect and the optimal pretreatment protocol warrant further optimization. Finally, although OBs exhibited a stable phenotype in our hands, the use of primary calvarial OBs may face scalability challenges for clinical translation. Future investigations could explore the feasibility of using alternative cell sources in conjunction with this scaffold system. Given the widespread use of MC3T3-E1 and MG-63 OB-like cell lines in early-stage CaP ceramic studies49,59–61 and the recognized similarities between MC3T3-E1 cells and primary OBs in terms of proliferation and mineralization,50,51 these well-characterized cell lines may serve as more accessible alternatives for initial biomaterials screening. However, it should be noted that none of the currently available OB cell lines can fully recapitulate the behavior of primary OBs, and they should therefore be considered valuable pilot models rather than complete replacements for primary cell studies.62,63 The systematic evaluation of such cell lines within the present composite scaffold platform represents a promising avenue for future work.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrates that a composite scaffold composed of osteoinductive BCP ceramic particles and GelMA hydrogel, seeded with Tideglusib-pretreated calvarial OBs, significantly enhances the repair of critical-size bone defects in a rat femoral condyle model. The BCP particles concurrently reinforce the mechanical properties of the GelMA matrix and provide an osteoinductive substrate that promotes osteogenic differentiation. Tideglusib pretreatment at an optimized concentration of 1 μM effectively activates Wnt/β-catenin signaling and confers a sustained pro-osteogenic phenotype on OBs, even after drug removal. In vivo, this integrated construct achieved robust new bone formation, with favorable trabecular microarchitecture and intimate integration between the ceramic particles and nascent bone. These findings support the further development of pharmacologically primed, cell-laden composite scaffolds as a promising strategy for the clinical management of critical-size bone defects.
