Cystofilobasidium ferigula co-production of lipids and carotenoids using crude glycerol
Authors: Andreísa Teixeira de Castro, Angélica Cristina de Souza, Taís Teixeira das Neves, Jenaina Ribeiro Soares, Raphael Longuinhos Monteiro Lobato, Rosane Freitas Schwan, Disney Ribeiro Dias
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis research optimized culture conditions to enhance the concurrent synthesis of lipids and carotenoids by Cystofilobasidium ferigula CCMA 1623 using crude glycerol as a low-cost substrate. Despite limited biotechnological studies on this yeast, a response surface methodology with a central composite design was applied to examine the influence of yeast extract levels, temperature, and aeration. Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) identified a fatty acid profile predominantly composed of stearic, palmitic, oleic (omega-9), and linoleic (omega-6) acids similar to vegetable oils. Raman spectroscopy was employed as a rapid, non-destructive analytical tool to detect molecular signatures consistent with lipid and carotenoid-type compounds in the biomass, providing complementary structural evidence to support compositional and functional analyses. The carotenoid extract exhibited notable antioxidant activity as determined by the 2,2′-azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic) acid radical scavenging assay (ABTS), the ferric reducing antioxidant power assay (FRAP), and the 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl free radical scavenging assay (DPPH). Under optimized conditions, the cell extract showed 52.89 ± 2.55% lipid content (1.57 ± 0.25 g/L) and 107.6 ± 2.4 µg/g total carotenoids, supporting its potential as a sustainable platform for the biotechnological production of high-value metabolites.Subject terms: Biochemistry, Biological techniques, Biotechnology, Chemistry, Microbiology

Introduction

Specific oleaginous yeasts can exhibit orange, pink, or red coloration. In addition to accumulating lipids, they store carotenoid pigments in the cell wall and intracellular organelles called lipid droplets1. Both compounds are lipophilic and share commonalities in their metabolic pathways, such as the requirement of acetyl-CoA as a carbon donor and NADPH for the reductive steps. Thus, concurrent accumulation can occur in the cell through triacylglycerols and isoprenoids2,3.

Microbial lipids (single-cell oils) are high-value products because their chemical composition is similar to that obtained from plant extracts. They can replace or serve as an alternative source for obtaining the same compounds. Lipids can be used in biofuels, detergents, and fine chemicals in the food, pharmaceutical, and cosmetic industries4. Carotenoids also have a high and expanding market value, estimated to reach 2 billion dollars by 20265,6. These compounds can be employed as colorants in the food, feed, cosmetics, and pharmaceutical industries. They also perform various beneficial biological activities in the body, including antitumor effects, regulation of metabolic activity, antioxidant activity, and serving as precursors for vitamin A production. Like lipids, carotenoids can be used as nutraceuticals5,7.

Recent reviews emphasize the technological relevance of yeast-derived lipids and carotenoids in food systems, highlighting their applications as sustainable fat substitutes and as natural colorants and antioxidant ingredients that enhance oxidative stability, sensory quality, and nutritional value of food products3,8.

Lipid production is still dominated by extraction from seeds, while carotenoid production is predominantly carried out through chemical synthesis. Both methods raise concerns about competition for land used for food production due to low yields, high purification costs, and long cultivation periods2. Yeasts represent promising microbial platforms for the biosynthesis of such metabolites, capable of storing lipids that may comprise up to 70% of their dry biomass. Moreover, yeasts are valuable microorganisms for biotechnological processes due to their advantages, including easy and rapid cultivation, the ability to construct robust platforms, and the ability to utilize byproducts and industrial waste as substrates, all of which have contributed to the development of sustainable processes9. Moreover, obtaining natural compounds offers more significant potential for commercialization because of the health benefits to consumers compared to chemical production routes10,11.

Crude glycerol is the main byproduct generated from biodiesel production (approximately 10% w/w). Other industrial sectors, such as the soap, fatty acid, and fatty ester industries, also create this byproduct, albeit in smaller quantities12. As biodiesel production expands, several studies have focused on using crude glycerol in biotransformation processes. The reuse of this byproduct in microbial fermentation processes is significant due to its composition, with 50 to 60% glycerol, which is an inexpensive carbon source for microbial cultivation12–14.

Cystofilobasidium ferigula was described in 2001 in ref. 15 as an unconventional pink yeast. Some studies have reported the production of neutral lipids and the accumulation of carotenoids by other species of the genus Cystofilobasidium using alternative substrates16–19. In a previous study, the strain C. ferigula CCMA 1623 demonstrated lipid accumulation exceeding 20% of its dry biomass when cultivated on pure glycerol (33.33% w/w), sugarcane molasses (25.54% w/w), and crude glycerol (41.88% w/w); thus, this strain is considered an oleaginous strain20. Notably, growth on crude glycerol enhanced lipogenesis and confirmed the strain’s ability to produce carotenoids under this substrate condition.

Despite recent progress in the microbial co-production of lipids and carotenoids by oleaginous yeasts, most approaches still rely on costly or pretreated substrates and complex operational strategies, frequently involving genetically engineered strains to achieve high productivities, which ultimately constrains economic feasibility and industrial scalability21,22. In parallel, although C. ferigula is well characterized from taxonomic and genomic perspectives, comprehensive bioprocess-oriented studies focusing on cultivation optimization, productivity assessment, scale-up potential, and downstream integration remain limited, underscoring the scientific and technological relevance of targeted research on this species23.

The formation of fatty acids and carotenoids in yeasts is strongly influenced by culture parameters, including the nature of carbon and nitrogen sources, the C/N ratio, pH, temperature, oxygen availability or aeration intensity, and potential inhibitory compounds in the medium3,24. To enhance biotechnological processes for maximizing lipid and carotenoid production by yeasts, optimization strategies for cultivation parameters are employed. This study optimized the fermentation parameters of nitrogen rate, temperature, and oxygen incorporation by homogenization to simultaneously produce lipids and carotenoids by C. ferigula CCMA 1623 using crude glycerol as the carbon source. Through optimization, combinations of cultivation conditions can be selected to improve metabolite production and identify suitable operational compromises for simultaneous lipid and carotenoid biosynthesis, aiming to support future scale-up and industrial application25–27.

Optimization for the concurrent production of lipids and carotenoids

The central composite design revealed that lipid accumulation by C. ferigula CCMA 1623 ranged from 26.25% to 68.72%, with the highest values consistently observed at a low yeast extract concentration (0.5 g/L), regardless of temperature and agitation conditions. In contrast, carotenoid synthesis (61–118.6 µg/g) was favored at intermediate to higher yeast extract levels (1.0 g/L), particularly around the central design point, indicating a differential metabolic response to nitrogen availability (Table 2). Overall, yeast extract concentration emerged as the primary factor modulating metabolite partitioning, promoting lipogenesis under nitrogen limitation and enhancing carotenoid accumulation under moderate nitrogen supply.

In contrast to lipid production, relatively high carotenoid production occurred in cultures using 1.0 g/L yeast extract. This is evident when comparing this value across different temperatures and homogenization levels, where the graph shows a plateau, indicating a range of relatively high carotenoid production, from 115.8 to 118.6 µg/g (Fig. 3C).

The concentration of yeast extract was the key parameter influencing the production of both lipids and carotenoids, while temperature and homogenization had little or no significant effects. However, when evaluating metabolite production separately, a lower nitrogen source concentration (0.5 g/L) was found to favor lipid production and accumulation. In comparison, the intermediate concentration corresponding to the central point of yeast extract (1.0 g/L) led to increased carotenoid production. When the experimental results were analyzed from the perspective of lipid and carotenoid co-production, Assay 2 represented the best compromise between both responses, achieving 58.43% lipid content and 114.6 µg/g of total carotenoids at 1.5 g/L yeast extract, 26 °C, and 140 rpm. Although higher lipid accumulation or carotenoid concentration was observed under some individual conditions, these responses were not maximized under the same cultivation parameters. Therefore, based on the previous predictive output of the response surface optimization and on the operational multi-response criterion adopted for co-production, the parameters of Assay 2 were selected for the validation experiment. The validation assay confirmed the suitability of this predicted co-production condition, yielding 52.89 ± 2.55% lipid accumulation and 107.6 ± 2.4 µg/g of total carotenoids.

Fatty acid profile of C. ferigula lipids

The C. ferigula CCMA 1623 strain cultivated in crude glycerol medium at 26 °C with 1.5 g/L yeast extract and shaking at 140 rpm presented a profile composed of saturated fatty acids, two monounsaturated fatty acids (hexadecenoic and octadecenoic acids), and one polyunsaturated fatty acid (9,12-octadecadienoic acid). The fatty acids stearic (C18:0), oleic (C18:1), palmitic (C16:0), and linoleic (C18:2) presented relative peak area percentages of 6.93%, 13.86%, 23.79%, and 49.91%, respectively. Two long-chain fatty acids were also identified in low percentages: 0.26% eicosanoic acid (C20:0) and 0.16% docosanoic acid (C22:0). Fatty acids with 10, 12 and 14 carbon atoms were also present in the fatty acid profile, exhibiting low relative peak area percentages, except for tetradecanoic acid (2.08%) (Table 4 and Supplementary Data 1).

Detection of carotenoids and lipids by Raman spectroscopy

Figure 5 demonstrates the Raman spectra acquired for the β-carotene standard in red and the material from the cultivation in blue. The result from the cultivated material was low in intensity, so it was necessary to multiply it by x10 to present the data, facilitating joint analysis with the pigment pattern data. An accentuated background was also observed in this last material. Three prominent main bands of vibrations were identified at frequencies of ~ 1002, 1152, and 1509 cm−1 in both spectra, which can be related to the presence of pigments from the carotenoid class28. Bands in the region of 1000 cm−1 have been assigned to the C-CH3 deformation mode of carotenoids, allowing identification of the methyl group in these structures, as evidenced by the peak near 1002 cm−1. Bands in the range 1100–1120 cm−1 are generally attributed to the C-C stretching vibration of carbon in the central structure of the carotenoid molecule, which may correspond to the band identified near 1152 cm−1. In the 1500 to 1520 cm−1 range, the vibrational peak has been attributed to the C =C stretching of double bonds between carbons in carotenoids, which can be identified here as the peak close to 1509 cm−1. The observed Raman bands are consistent with carotenoid-type compounds, which share characteristic vibrational features associated with conjugated polyene chains. This results in subtle differences in the vibrational frequencies of these observed modes, which can coexist within the same sample29. Two other peaks of lower intensity were identified in the spectrum of the cultured samples: ~1664 and 1453 cm−1. Peaks in the range of 1430 and 1470 cm−1 have been related to CH2 and CH3 deformations of lipids, proteins, and carbohydrates, while the band at 1665 cm−1 has been referred to in the literature as originating from C = O movements in proteins (amide I) and C = C in lipids28.

Raman spectra of a reference sample of beta-carotene and the sample collected in the experiments.

The spectrum of the samples of interest had its intensity multiplied by 10 for better visualization and comparison with the signal from the beta-carotene reference sample.

Although Raman spectroscopy provides rapid molecular fingerprinting consistent with lipid- and carotenoid-type compounds, quantitative lipid compositional analysis was performed using GC-MS, while chromatographic techniques such as HPLC will be required in future studies to fully resolve individual carotenoid species present in the biomass.

Total carotenoid extract antioxidant activity

In the validation assay, the total carotenoid extract (107.6 µg/g ± 2.4) interacted with the ABTS radical, exhibiting an antioxidant activity equivalent to 365.37 ± 1.9 µM Trolox/L. The FRAP of the extract, as determined by the FRAP assay, was 385.02 ± 19.6 µM ferrous sulfate/L. In the DPPH assay, the extract inhibited 49.5% ± 1.9% of the DPPH free radicals.

Discussion

The synthesis of lipids and carotenoids in yeast is influenced by the carbon source used for cell growth and the cultivation conditions. The combination of variables, including carbon and nitrogen sources, carbon-to-nitrogen (C/N) ratio, dissolved oxygen concentration, temperature, pH, and inoculum rate during the fermentation process, is intentionally manipulated to direct yeast metabolism toward the production of fatty acids and carotenoid pigments30,31.

Nitrogen depletion following the exponential growth phase is the primary metabolic trigger for lipid accumulation in oleaginous yeasts when excess carbon is available in the culture medium4,32. Under nitrogen limitation, cell division is arrested and assimilated carbon is redirected toward triacylglycerol storage rather than biomass formation33,34. This metabolic shift is supported by increased ATP-citrate lyase activity, which ensures a continuous cytosolic supply of acetyl-CoA, the central precursor for de novo fatty acid biosynthesis35. Carbon flux through glycolysis and the tricarboxylic acid cycle is consequently enhanced, increasing pyruvate and acetyl-CoA availability for both lipid synthesis and the mevalonate pathway responsible for carotenoid formation3,27,36. The shared dependence on acetyl-CoA reinforces the central regulatory role of nitrogen availability in coordinating the coproduction of lipids and carotenoids in oleaginous yeasts.

Crude glycerol is an efficient carbon source for biomass production by oleaginous yeasts and specific metabolites such as lipids and carotenoids18. In this study, crude glycerol obtained from a biodiesel laboratory promoted cell growth and lipid and carotenoid accumulation in optimization assays using the C. ferigula CCMA 1623 strain. The substrate had a composition of 773.6 g/L glycerol and contained 0.39 g/L acetic acid, but no toxic compounds, such as furfural or 5-methyl furfural, were detected. According to ref. 37, 3 g/L acetic acid and 2.5 g/L furfural are required to completely inhibit microbial growth.

C. ferigula CCMA 1623 exhibited enhanced growth under central optimization conditions and at higher yeast extract concentrations combined with increased homogenization, indicating that nitrogen availability and oxygen transfer primarily supported biomass formation. Elevated nitrogen levels sustain protein and nucleic acid biosynthesis, prolonging active cell division and delaying the metabolic shift toward storage compound accumulation2. Simultaneously, increased agitation improves dissolved oxygen availability, favoring respiratory metabolism and cellular proliferation rather than carbon flux redirection toward secondary metabolite synthesis.

Oleaginous yeasts generally require oxygen for rapid growth31. Different homogenization speeds (140, 160, and 180 rpm) were used to ensure uniform oxygen incorporation into the medium. This factor triggered more significant growth of C. ferigula in assays with higher rotation values. However, some studies on yeast lipid biosynthesis have associated elevated aeration rates with a reduction in lipid accumulation (38). The optimal level of aeration can vary between yeast species, favoring either biomass production or metabolite biosynthesis depending on the evaluated strain31. The homogenization conditions evaluated in the cultivation of C. ferigula CCMA 1623 using the response surface methodology did not significantly affect lipid or carotenoid production.

Temperature is an essential factor for microbial growth because it controls enzymatic concentrations and consequently influences the biosynthesis and accumulation of metabolites. In terms of carotenoid production, different temperature ranges modulate the production of specific carotenoids38. According to ref. 39, temperatures between 20 and 30 °C promote yeast growth, while temperatures below 20°C limit growth, and those above 30°C are unfavorable for carotenoid production due to enzyme denaturation involved in carotenogenesis. In a study by da Silve et al.40 on lipid and carotenoid production by Rhodotorula mucilaginosa, cultivation at temperatures between 22 and 34 °C was found to influence the production of specific carotenoids. However, it did not affect the biomass produced. It was also reported that an increase in temperature (34 °C) unfavorably affected carotenoid production.

In the optimization assays of C. ferigula, biomass production, lipid content, and total carotenoid concentration were not significantly affected by the cultivation temperature (26, 28, or 30 °C), as there was little difference among the evaluated temperatures. A study conducted at temperatures of 20 and 28 °C for the concurrent synthesis of lipids and carotenoids by Rhodotorula gracilis demonstrated that cultivation at 20 °C promoted higher lipid and carotenoid accumulation within 96 h. However, there were modifications in the profile of fatty acids and carotenoids depending on the temperature and fermentation time41.

Among the evaluated parameters, yeast extract concentration exerted the most significant influence on metabolite production by directly modulating the C/N ratio of the culture medium5,31. Similar effects of the C/N ratio on biomass formation, lipid accumulation, and carotenoid biosynthesis have been reported in optimization studies using response surface methodology for oleaginous yeasts18.

Higher carotenoid production by C. ferigula was obtained in the optimization assays with a yeast extract concentration of 1.0 g/L and temperatures of 28 and 30 °C, indicating that moderate nitrogen availability favored pigment biosynthesis. In a batch cultivation study conducted in ref. 42 on Rhodotorula glutinis, increased biomass formation and total carotenoid concentration were observed when ammonium sulfate levels were raised to 2 g/L⁻¹.

Under the validation assay performed at the previously optimized conditions for metabolite co-production, Cystofilobasidium ferigula achieved 52.89 ± 2.5% lipid accumulation (w/w, dry biomass basis) and 107.6 ± 2.4 µg/g total carotenoids. These values are competitive when compared with literature reports, such as Rhodotorula glutinis supplemented with selenite, which reached 51.4% lipids and 32.12–36.43 µg/g carotenoids43, and R. mucilaginosa cultivated on sugarcane molasses, which produced 15.36–16.50% lipids and 51–53 µg/g carotenoids44. Notably, species of the genus Rhodotorula are widely recognized as model yeasts for lipid and carotenoid biosynthesis. In this context, the performance of C. ferigula, a still underexplored species cultivated on low-cost crude glycerol, represents a relevant biotechnological advancement.

Raman spectroscopy revealed vibrational bands consistent with carotenoid-type compounds in the biomass of C. ferigula, particularly bands associated with conjugated polyene chains commonly found in microbial carotenoids. These compounds are among the most sought-after carotenoids globally and are abundant in carrots, tomatoes, broccoli, pumpkin, apricots, and grapes5. In this context, carotenoid-type compounds are of considerable biotechnological interest because natural carotenoids are widely explored as pigments and bioactive molecules in food, feed, pharmaceutical, and nutraceutical applications.

In the food industry, microbial carotenoids are widely explored as natural colorants and antioxidant compounds for application in beverages, confectionery products, dairy alternatives, meat products, and feed formulations. In pharmaceutical and nutraceutical contexts, carotenoid-rich extracts are also valued for their ability to mitigate oxidative stress45–47. The increasing demand for naturally derived pigments reinforces the relevance of microbial platforms capable of producing carotenoid-rich biomass or extracts, especially when low-cost substrates are used.

The total carotenoid extracts from the C. ferigula strain exhibited high antioxidant activity for three evaluation methods, ABTS (365.37 µM Trolox/L), DPPH (49.5% inhibition), and FRAP (385.02 µM ferrous sulfate/L), compared to the antioxidant results obtained in ref. 48 from fruit extracts. The antioxidant activity of the carotenoid extracts from C. ferigula can be considered high, similar to those obtained in ref. 49 from carotenoids produced by the yeast Paffia rhodozyma, with an ABTS activity of 72.51 µM Trolox/g and a FRAP of 21.57 µM Trolox/g.

In red oleaginous yeasts, the biosynthesis pathways of fatty acids and carotenoids share a crucial common point: the cytosolic precursor acetyl-CoA. The generation of acetyl-CoA depends on carbohydrate metabolism and, therefore, the composition of the different carbon sources used for cultivation. The composition of the carbon source and its impact on the glycolytic pathway can positively regulate the generation of pyruvate and, consequently, acetyl-CoA availability3.

Nitrogen-limited cultivation triggers lipid synthesis while facilitating carotenoid production, focusing on converting glycerol into pyruvate and acetyl-CoA through the citric acid cycle. Crude glycerol is transported into yeast cells and converted into dihydroxyacetone phosphate or directly into pyruvate via glycolysis. Pyruvate is then converted into acetyl-CoA, the primary precursor for lipid synthesis. This metabolic pathway operates through the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, in which acetyl-CoA serves as a precursor for chain elongation, leading to the synthesis of fatty acids such as palmitic (C16:0) and oleic (C18:1) acids. Nitrogen limitation further enhances lipid accumulation by redirecting excess carbon into storage lipids instead of biomass growth. Acetyl-CoA also initiates the mevalonate pathway, leading to the synthesis of isoprenoid units, including isopentenyl pyrophosphate (IPP). These units polymerize and undergo desaturation and cyclization reactions through the carotenoid biosynthetic pathway, leading to the formation of carotenoid-type compounds, with key enzymes such as phytoene synthase and phytoene desaturase participating in these steps3,27.

The composition of the fatty acid profile synthesized by yeasts is similar to that of vegetable oils, with saturated and monounsaturated fatty acids, predominantly those composed of 16 and 18 carbon atoms40. In this study, the lipids of the C. ferigula CCMA 1623 strain were mainly composed of linoleic acid (49.98%), palmitic acid (23.57%), oleic acid (13.79%) and stearic acid (6.89%). Oleic acid (C18:1) is the main omega-9 monounsaturated fatty acid, while linoleic acid (C18:2) is the principal essential omega-6 fatty acid in human nutrition, both widely associated with health-promoting effects and high industrial value of microbial oils for nutraceutical and functional food applications21,50.

The lipid profile obtained in this study comprises the same structural fatty acids predominant in cocoa butter—palmitic, stearic, and oleic acids—although cocoa butter typically contains very low levels of linoleic acid (<3%)51,52, making microbial lipids particularly attractive under conditions of increasing demand and limited cocoa butter availability. Previous studies demonstrate that oleaginous yeasts naturally produce these fatty acids and that their profiles can be directed toward cocoa-butter-like compositions by increasing oleic acid and reducing polyunsaturated fatty acids through process modulation21,22. The high linoleic acid content observed here is metabolically linked to enhanced Δ12-desaturase activity converting oleate to linoleate after Δ9-desaturation of stearate, a balance that can be regulated by cultivation parameters such as temperature, C/N ratio, and oxygen availability, enabling future process engineering toward cocoa butter equivalents53,54.

The fatty acid profile is also determined according to the cultivation conditions used, primarily the carbon source that comprises the substrate. In oleaginous yeasts, lipid accumulation may occur via the de novo pathway when cultivated on soluble carbon sources such as glycerol, whereas ex novo lipid assimilation is typically associated with hydrophobic substrates. Under the conditions evaluated in this study, lipid biosynthesis was primarily driven by de novo metabolism.

This study demonstrated that the rose-colored strain Cystofilobasidium ferigula CCMA 1623 efficiently produces lipids and carotenoids using crude glycerol under optimized cultivation conditions, with biosynthesis strongly influenced by nitrogen availability, as reflected in the modulation of the C/N ratio. Raman spectroscopy proved to be a rapid and complementary technique for detecting molecular signatures consistent with intracellular lipids and carotenoid-type compounds, while the strain achieved 52.89 ± 2.55% lipid content and 107.6 ± 2.4 µg/g total carotenoids. The lipid fraction was enriched in omega-6 and omega-9 fatty acids, and the total carotenoid extract showed relevant antioxidant activity. The optimized process establishes a basis for subsequent scale-up in controlled bioreactor systems, particularly airlift configurations. Future studies should focus on food-grade extraction protocols, detailed carotenoid speciation, pilot-scale fermentation, and assessments of product stability and biosafety to support technological development.

Methods

Microorganism

The Cystofilobasidium ferigula (CCMA 1623) strain, originally isolated from coffee fruit and maintained in the Collection of Agricultural Microbiology Cultures (CCMA) at the Federal University of Lavras (Lavras, MG, Brazil), National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database accession number: KY495755.155. It was selected based on prior screening that identified its ability to synthesize lipids and carotenoids when cultivated on agro-industrial residues such as sugarcane molasses and crude glycerol. The yeast inoculum was reactivated in 2 mL of Yeast Extract Peptone Glucose (YEPG) medium composed of 20 g/L peptone, 20 g/L glucose and 10 g/L yeast extract, followed by incubation at 28 °C for 24 h.

Substrate for the production of lipids and carotenoids

Crude glycerol, obtained from the Biodiesel Laboratory of the Federal University of Lavras, was employed as an alternative carbon source for cultivating C. ferigula CCMA 1623. This industrial byproduct was analyzed for its carbohydrate, organic acid, furfural, and 5-methylfurfural contents using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, model LC-10Ai; Shimadzu Corp., Tokyo, Japan), according to the procedure described in ref. 20. The chromatographic setup comprised a dual UV-Vis detector (SPD-10Ai; Shimadzu) and a refractive index detector (RID-10Ai; Shimadzu), coupled to an ion-exclusion column (Shim-pack SCR-101H, 7.9 mm × 30 cm, Shimadzu). Compound identification relied on retention time comparisons with reference standards, and quantification was carried out through external calibration (Supplementary Data 2).

Culture medium and submerged cultivation conditions

The fermentation medium, modified from ref. 56, was composed of (g/L): 40 crude glycerol, 7.0 KH₂PO₄, 5.0 glucose, 2.5 NaH₂PO₄, 1.5 MgSO₄•7H₂O, 0.5 (NH₄)₂SO₄, 0.15 CaCl₂•2H₂O, 0.06 MnSO₄•H₂O, 0.02 ZnSO₄•7H₂O, and yeast extract at concentrations of 0.5, 1.0, or 1.5, adjusted to pH 6.0. The fermentations were carried out in 250 mL non-baffled Erlenmeyer flasks, with a base diameter of 85 mm, containing 150 mL of culture medium. The inoculum, standardized at 10⁷ cells/mL, was incubated for 96 h under different temperature conditions (26, 28, and 30°C) and agitation rates (140, 160, and 180 rpm) using an orbital shaker (Excella E25, New Brunswick Scientific, Edison, NJ, USA) with an orbit diameter of 25.4 mm. The pH of the culture medium was measured during its preparation, and continuous pH monitoring was performed at 24-h intervals. Samples were also withdrawn at 0, 24, 48, and 96 h to monitor yeast proliferation by cell counting in a Neubauer chamber, up to the stationary phase, following the procedure described in ref. 56.

Experimental design

The accumulation of lipids and carotenoids by the strain C. ferigula (CCMA1623) using crude glycerol as a carbon source was optimized using Central Composite Design (CCD). The effects of yeast extract concentration, cultivation temperature and agitation rate as an indirect indicator of oxygen availability were assessed with the objective of enhancing the production of lipids and carotenoids by the yeast. Table 1 lists the process variables and their respective decoded values according to their lower, central, and upper levels.

According to the 23 factorial planning matrix, seventeen trials were carried out for the CCD, as shown in Table 1.

The results obtained from the applied experimental model were evaluated using Statistica Release software version 7.1, Stat Soft, Inc., USA. After evaluating the response surface models and the experimental results obtained from the central composite design, the validation condition was selected as the best operational compromise for co-production. The selection prioritized the simultaneous occurrence of high lipid accumulation and high total carotenoid concentration, rather than the individual maximization of either response. Therefore, the selected condition represented the most suitable co-production point within the evaluated experimental domain. A validation assay was then performed under these conditions to confirm lipid and carotenoid production.

Extraction and determination of lipid content

A 100 mL aliquot of culture was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the resulting biomass was rinsed three times with distilled water. The pellet was then dried at 50 °C for 12 h, and its dry weight was recorded. Cell lysis of the dried biomass was performed by adding 5mL of 2 M HCl, followed by incubation in a water bath at 70 °C for 1 h. Lipid extraction followed the procedure of ref. 57, with adaptations based on ref. 20, employing a chloroform: methanol mixture (2:1, v/v) as the extraction solvent. The initial solvent removal was carried out in a chemical fume hood at room temperature, allowing the gradual evaporation of volatile components under continuous airflow. Subsequently, the flask was dried in an oven at 60 °C until constant weight was achieved for lipid content determination. The lipid content (%) was calculated gravimetrically from the ratio between the lipid content (g/L) and the dry biomass weight of the corresponding sample, according to the following equations20:

Lipid contend (g/L) = Weight of lipid content in the flask (g) – weight of empty flask (g)/Volume of the cultivation sample (L)

Lipid contend (g/L) = Weight of lipid content in the flask (g) – weight of empty flask (g)/Volume of the cultivation sample (L)

Lipid content (%) = Lipid content (g/L) × 100/Dry cell weight (g/L)

Lipid content (%) = Lipid content (g/L) × 100/Dry cell weight (g/L)

Extraction and quantification of carotenoids

The extraction of total carotenoids was conducted following the modified method of ref. 38, using an acetone: methanol (7:3, v/v) solvent system. The biomass was centrifuged, dried at 50 °C, ground in liquid nitrogen, and subjected to successive extractions with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and the solvent mixture until colorless. The carotenoid content was quantified by UV-Vis spectrophotometry at 450 nm (SP 2000 UV Spectrum). A β-carotene calibration curve, prepared at different concentrations (200.0, 100.0, 50.0, 25.0, 12.5, 6.25 and 3.12 µg/mL), was used for quantification of total carotenoids in the extract. The quantification of total carotenoids was determined using the equation described in ref. 58, considering the absorbance readings obtained from the calibration curve, the specific extinction coefficient (2500 mL·g⁻¹·cm⁻¹), and the dry biomass weight. The results were expressed as µg of total carotenoids per g of dry yeast biomass. All carotenoid extractions and spectrophotometric analyses were conducted in the dark, with the flasks containing the extracts wrapped in aluminum foil to prevent light-induced pigment degradation.

Confocal microscopy for visualization of lipid storage

At the end of the 96-h cultivation period, 10 mL samples were taken from the validation assay. The cell pellets were centrifuged and subsequently rinsed with 10 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0; PBS). For lipid visualization, a mixture containing 100 µL of cell suspension and 2 mL of phosphate buffer was prepared, followed by the addition of 10 µL of Nile Red solution (0.1 mg/mL in acetone; Sigma). After a 10-min incubation, the stained cells were examined under a Zeiss Axio-Observer Z1 LSM 780 confocal microscope equipped with a 40× objective lens and an excitation filter ranging from 540 to 580 nm, as described by reference59.

Analysis of fatty acids by GC–MS

The conversion of the extracted lipids into fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs) was carried out according to the procedure described by reference60. Lipids were reacted with methanolic KOH and n-hexane, incubated at 70 °C, and the resulting upper phase containing FAMEs was collected for analysis by gas chromatography (GC).

The chromatographic analyses were conducted using a GC–MS system (GCMS-QP2010SE; Shimadzu) equipped with an Restek, Rtx-5MS (30 m, 0.25 mm ID, 0.25 µm) capillary column and quadrupole detector, following the protocol outlined by reference20. Helium was employed as the carrier gas under electron ionization at 70 eV, with temperature parameters strictly controlled. The temperatures of the transfer line and injector were adjusted to 220 and 250 °C, respectively. The oven program began at 50 °C with a 2-min hold, followed by a temperature increase of 4 °C min⁻¹ until reaching 220 °C, maintained for 10 min, and subsequently raised to 250 °C with a final hold of 2 min. Sample injections of approximately 1 µL were performed under split conditions using a 10:1 split ratio. Data were acquired in full-scan mode over a mass range of 20–600 m/z, and compound identification was carried out by comparing mass spectra with the NIST library, accepting matches with similarity values above 85%. All analyses were carried out in duplicate. Following fatty acid identification, their relative abundances were determined and expressed as percentages of the total chromatographic peak area.

RAMAN spectroscopy for confirmation of carotenoids and lipids

Raman spectroscopy was performed using the methodology described in ref. 61 with adaptations. The Witec Alpha 300 equipment, which has a detection system and a coupled microscope, was used. After 96 h of cultivation in crude glycerol medium, 50 mL samples were taken. Raman spectra were acquired from bulk biomass samples deposited on the substrate and therefore represent the average molecular composition of the analyzed material rather than single-cell measurements. Cells obtained from 1 mL aliquots of culture were centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 1 min and resuspended in 1 mL of phosphate-buffered saline. This washing process was repeated three times to ensure complete removal of the culture medium. Subsequently, 0.5 μL of the resulting cell suspension was deposited onto an aluminum substrate and allowed to air dry. Microscopic examination was performed to acquire the Raman spectra, which were recorded within the spectral range of 110–2100 cm⁻¹, using a 785 nm excitation laser at 50 mW power, with an integration time of 30 s and ten accumulations, employing a 10× objective lens. The standard sample of beta-carotene was used as purchased, deposited directly on top of a microscope slide for analysis, in which case a laser power of just 5 mW and the same previous settings were required.

Raman spectra were analyzed in their original acquired form without baseline correction or normalization. For visualization purposes only, the biomass sample spectrum was multiplied by a constant factor of 10 to allow clearer comparison with the β-carotene reference spectrum, due to differences in signal intensity. This scaling does not affect peak positions or spectral interpretation. Each spectrum was acquired using 10 accumulations of 30 s each to improve signal-to-noise ratio. Raw Raman spectra are provided in Supplementary Data 3.

Antioxidant analysis of the total carotenoid extract

The antioxidant potential of the carotenogenic extracts obtained from yeast biomass was assessed in the validation assay under distinct oxidative conditions. The radical scavenging capacity of the total carotenoid extract was determined using the 2,2′-azinobis-(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) radical, following the procedure of reference (31) with modifications. In brief, 30 µL of extract or standard solution (Trolox) was mixed with 3 mL of ABTS radical solution and incubated in the dark for 6 min, after which absorbance was measured at 734 nm. Quantification was based on a Trolox calibration curve, and results were expressed as micromoles of Trolox equivalents per liter of sample (µmol TE/L).

The ferric-reducing antioxidant potential (FRAP) was quantified according to the protocol described in the reference (31). The FRAP reagent consisted of a 0.3 M acetate buffer (pH 3.6), 10 mM 2,4,6-tripyridyl-s-triazine (TPTZ), and 20 mM ferric chloride. In darkness, 90 µL of extract dilution was combined with 2.7 mL of the FRAP reagent and 270 µL of distilled water, homogenized, and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Absorbance was subsequently recorded at 593 nm using a spectrophotometer. Calibration was performed with 2 mM ferrous sulfate at concentrations of 500, 1000, 1500, and 2000 µM/mL, and results were expressed as µM of FeSO₄ equivalents per liter of extract.

The capacity to scavenge 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radicals was assessed according to the method reported by reference (31), with modifications to the reaction time. A 60 µM methanolic DPPH solution was prepared and shielded from light, to which the carotenogenic extract was added. After 60 min of incubation, absorbance was measured at 515 nm, and the results were expressed as the percentage of DPPH radical inhibition relative to control samples lacking carotenoids.
