An Error Control Framework for Computing the Exponential of Matrices Arising from the Finite Element Discretization
Fuminori Tatsuoka, Yuto Miyatake, Tomohiro Sogabe

Abstract
Several methods for computing the action of the matrix exponential e 𝑨 ​ 𝒃 \mathrm{e}^{\bm{A}}\bm{b} are expressed by substituting 𝑨 \bm{A} into a rational approximation of the scalar exponential function. The error of such methods can be estimated using the numerical range of 𝑨 \bm{A} , which enables the computation of e 𝑨 ​ 𝒃 \mathrm{e}^{\bm{A}}\bm{b} with a prescribed accuracy. However, when the input matrix has the structure 𝑨 = τ ​ 𝑴 − 1 ​ 𝑲 \bm{A}=\tau\bm{M}^{-1}\bm{K} , this approach is challenging because computing the bounding box of numerical range is difficult and the numerical range may be too large to construct rational approximations on it. In this paper, focusing on the case where 𝑴 \bm{M} is a well-conditioned symmetric positive definite matrix, we propose considering the numerical range of a similarity transformed matrix of 𝑨 \bm{A} . The numerical range of transformed matrix is not only numerically computable but can also be theoretically bounded depending on properties of 𝑲 \bm{K} . Numerical experiments confirm that the computations can be performed within the prescribed error tolerance.

Introduction

For a square matrix
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, its exponential is defined as
(
Higham 2008
, Eq. (10.1))

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{\bm{A}}:=\bm{I}+\bm{A}+\frac{1}{2!}\bm{A}^{2}+\frac{1}{3!}\bm{A}^{3}+\cdots.

(1)

The matrix exponential allows us to represent the solutions of linear initial value problems:
for the initial value problem

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{u}}(t)=\bm{A}\bm{u}(t),\qquad\bm{u}(0)=\bm{u}_{0},

(2)

it holds that

\displaystyle\bm{u}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{t\bm{A}}\bm{u}_{0}.

(3)

This relation facilitates the construction of numerical solvers called exponential integrators for problems that are challenging to solve with conventional methods such as Runge–Kutta methods; see e.g.
Hochbruck and
Ostermann 2010
for more details.
Hence, computational methods for
\mathrm{e}^{\bm{A}}\bm{b}~(\bm{b}\in\mathbb{C}^{n})
, are of significant importance in numerical analysis.

Roughly speaking, computational methods for
\mathrm{e}^{\bm{A}}\,\bm{b}
can be classified into two categories.
The first category includes methods that substitute
\bm{A}
into a prescribed scalar rational approximation
r(z)\approx\mathrm{e}^{z}
, such as the truncated Taylor series
Al-Mohy and
Higham 2011
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{\bm{A}}\bm{b}\approx\left(\bm{I}+\bm{A}+\frac{1}{2!}\bm{A}^{2}+\frac{1}{3!}\bm{A}^{3}+\cdots+\frac{1}{m!}\bm{A}^{m}\right)\bm{b}.

(4)

sub-diagonal Padé approximation
Güttel and
Nakatsukasa 2016
, polynomial interpolation
Caliari
et al. 2004
;
Caliari et al. 2016
, and numerical quadrature
Weideman 2006
;
Weideman and
Trefethen 2007
;
Tatsuoka et al. 2024
.
The other category includes methods based on projection on an appropriate low dimensional subspace.
Let
\bm{V}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times m}~(m\ll n)
be an orthogonal basis of a certain subspace.
Then,
\mathrm{e}^{\bm{A}}\bm{b}
is approximated by
\bm{V}\mathrm{e}^{\bm{H}}\bm{V}^{\ast}\bm{b}
, where
\bm{H}=\bm{V}^{\ast}\bm{A}\bm{V}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
.
To construct
\bm{V}
, polynomial Krylov subspace, extended Krylov subspace, and rational Krylov subspace have been used
Saad 1992
;
Göckler and
Grimm 2013
;
Güttel 2013
.

While methods in the latter category are often employed due to their efficiency in the context of exponential integrators, methods in the former category remain important for several reasons.
For example, some of these methods are applicable even in limited-memory environments, i.e., when
n
is so large that storing the block matrix
\bm{V}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times m}
with moderate
m
becomes prohibitive.
For another reason, when
r(z)
is given in partial fraction form (e.g., the subdiagonal Padé approximation and quadrature-based algorithms), the resulting algorithm is well-suited for parallel computing.

In this paper, we focus on the methods based on the first category, i.e., those based on scalar rational approximations.
To guarantee the approximation accuracy, it is important to check
\|r(\bm{A})-\mathrm{e}^{\bm{A}}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon
for a given tolerance
\epsilon
.
A straightforward approach to bound
\|r(\bm{A})-\mathrm{e}^{\bm{A}}\|_{2}
is to utilize the numerical range (also called the field of values) of
\bm{A}
:
\mathcal{W}(\bm{A}):=\{\bm{v}^{\ast}\bm{A}\bm{v}\colon\bm{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{n},\|\bm{v}\|_{2}=1\}
.
It is known that
\mathcal{W}(\bm{A})
is a
(1+\sqrt{2})
-spectral set for
\bm{A}
(see
Crouzeix and
Palencia 2017
), and therefore, for any rational function
r
that is bounded on
\mathcal{W}(\bm{A})
, it holds that

\displaystyle\|r(\bm{A})-\mathrm{e}^{\bm{A}}\|_{2}\leq(1+\sqrt{2})\sup_{z\in\mathcal{W}(\bm{A})}|r(z)-\mathrm{e}^{z}|.

(5)

While the numerical range
\mathcal{W}(\bm{A})
cannot be obtained analitycally and estimating it precisely is computationally expensive,
a rectangular region
\mathcal{R}
that contains
\mathcal{W}(\bm{A})
can be obtained with cheaper costs.
Specifically, the left and right endpoints of
\mathcal{R}
are given by the extreme eigenvalues of
(\bm{A}+\bm{A}^{\ast})/2
, and the top and bottom endpoints correspond to the extreme eigenvalues of
(\bm{A}-\bm{A}^{\ast})/2\mathrm{i}
.
This fact is noted in e.g.
Caliari et al. 2016
and a special case of the algorithm estimating
\mathcal{W}(\bm{A})
in
Johnson 1978
.

Based on the above discussion, an error control framework for
\mathrm{e}^{\bm{A}}\bm{b}
using
r(z)
can be obtained as in Algorithm
1
.
In this framework, we first determine
\mathcal{R}
enclosing
\mathcal{W}(\bm{A})
by computing the extreme eigenvalues of the Hermitian and skew Hermitian parts of
\bm{A}
.
We then construct
r(z)\approx\mathrm{e}^{z}
on
\mathcal{R}
such that the condition
\|r(\bm{A})-\mathrm{e}^{\bm{A}}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon
is satisfied.
A similar strategy had been proposed to provide an error bound of polynomial interpolation
Caliari
et al. 2004

In this study, we focus on the situation
\bm{A}=\tau\bm{M}^{-1}\bm{K}
, where
\tau>0
,
\bm{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a well‑conditioned symmetric positive definite (SPD) matrix, and
\bm{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.
For example, such matrices arise from the finite element discretization of advection-diffusion equations.
However, there are two difficulties to apply Algorithm
1
to this situation.
First, to the best of our knowledge, there are no estimates and computational methods for obtaining eigenvalues of (skew) symmetric part of
\bm{A}=\tau\bm{M}^{-1}\bm{K}
, and therefore computing the rectangle region
\mathcal{R}
is not straightforward.
Second,
\mathcal{W}(\bm{A})
can be so large that an accurate rational approximation cannot be constructed on it.
For example, the left figure in Figure
1
illustrates
\mathcal{W}(\bm{A})
for a test matrix from an advection-diffusion equation described in Section 3.
The figure shows that
\mathcal{W}(\bm{A})
extends into the right half plane.
For this case,
r(z)
must deal with large values (e.g.,
\mathrm{e}^{17.29}
) with the error tolerance
\epsilon
, which is practically impossible.

\mathcal{W}(\bm{A})
and
\mathcal{W}(\hat{\bm{A}})
.
The matrix
\bm{A}
is a test matrix described in Section 3.1, obtained by discretizing the two dimensional advection–diffusion equation on a square domain with a P2 finite element method.
The set
\mathcal{W}(\hat{\bm{A}})
lies in the left half plane.

In this paper, to apply the error control framework (Algorithm
1
) to
\bm{A}=\tau\bm{M}^{-1}\bm{K}
, we propose to use
\mathcal{W}(\hat{\bm{A}})
instead of
\mathcal{W}(\bm{A})
, where
\hat{\bm{A}}:=\bm{M}^{1/2}\bm{A}\bm{M}^{-1/2}
.
There are three convenient properties for
\mathcal{W}(\hat{\bm{A}})
.
First,
\mathcal{W}(\hat{\bm{A}})
also provide us with an upper bound on
\|r(\bm{A})-\mathrm{e}^{\bm{A}}\|_{2}
that is a value proportional to the supremum of
|r(z)-\mathrm{e}^{z}|
over
\mathcal{W}(\hat{\bm{A}})
.
Second, a rectangular region enclosing
\mathcal{W}(\hat{\bm{A}})
can be obtained numerically by solving typical generalized eigenvalue problems.
Third, if
\mathcal{W}(\bm{K})
lies in the left half plane, then
\mathcal{W}(\hat{\bm{A}})
still lies in the left half plane.
The third property can be seen in the right figure in Fig.
1
.
Based on them, we establish an error control framework for computing
\mathrm{e}^{\tau\bm{M}^{-1}\bm{K}}\bm{b}
.

The organization of this paper is as follows.
Section 2 presents the error control framework for
\bm{A}=\tau\bm{M}^{-1}\bm{K}
.
Section 3 provides numerical results, and Section 4 concludes this paper.

2
Error control framework for
\bm{A}=\tau\bm{M}^{-1}\bm{K}

In this section, we show some properties of
\mathcal{W}(\hat{\bm{A}})
and propose the error control framework.
Firstly, it is shown that
\mathcal{W}(\hat{\bm{A}})
gives us an upper bound on the error
r(\bm{A})-\mathrm{e}^{\bm{A}}
as follows:

Theorem 1
.

For
\bm{A}=\tau\bm{M}^{-1}\bm{K}
, where
\tau>0
,
\bm{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is an SPD matrix, and
\bm{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, define
\hat{\bm{A}}=\bm{M}^{1/2}\bm{A}\bm{M}^{-1/2}
.
Then, for any rational function
r(z)
that is bounded on
\mathcal{W}(\hat{\bm{A}})
, it follows that

\displaystyle\|r(\bm{A})-\mathrm{e}^{\bm{A}}\|_{2}\leq(1+\sqrt{2})\kappa(\bm{M})^{1/2}\sup_{z\in\mathcal{W}(\hat{\bm{A}})}|r(z)-\mathrm{e}^{z}|,

(7)

where
\kappa(\bm{M})=\|\bm{M}\|_{2}\|\bm{M}^{-1}\|_{2}
.

Proof.

For notational simplicity we write
g(\bm{A})=r(\bm{A})-\mathrm{e}^{\bm{A}}
.
Using the property in
(
Higham 2008
, Thm. 1.13 (c))
, we can rewrite
g(\bm{A})
as

\displaystyle g(\bm{A})=g(\bm{M}^{-1/2}\bm{M}^{1/2}\bm{A}\bm{M}^{-1/2}\bm{M}^{1/2})=\bm{M}^{-1/2}g(\bm{M}^{1/2}\bm{A}\bm{M}^{-1/2})\bm{M}^{1/2}=\bm{M}^{-1/2}g(\hat{\bm{A}})\bm{M}^{1/2}.

(8)

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\|g(\bm{A})\|_{2}=\|\bm{M}^{-1/2}g(\hat{\bm{A}})\bm{M}^{1/2}\|_{2}\leq\kappa(\bm{M}^{1/2})\|g(\hat{\bm{A}})\|_{2}

(9)

because
\kappa(\bm{M}^{1/2})=\kappa(\bm{M})^{1/2}
.
By using (
5
) with the matrix
\hat{\bm{A}}
, we have (
7
).
∎

Remark 1
.

Because the eigenvalues of
\hat{\bm{A}}
coincide with that of
\bm{A}
,
\mathcal{W}(\hat{\bm{A}})
is
(1+\sqrt{2})\kappa(\bm{A})
-spectral set of
\bm{A}
.
See, e.g.,
Crouzeix and
Palencia 2017
for the definition of spectral sets.

Next, we show that a rectangle region enclosing
\mathcal{W}(\hat{\bm{A}})
can be obtained by solving generalized eigenvalue problems as follows:

Proposition 2
.

Let us define

\displaystyle\bm{D}:=\frac{1}{2}(\bm{K}+\bm{K}^{\top}),\qquad\bm{C}:=\frac{1}{2\mathrm{i}}(\bm{K}-\bm{K}^{\top}).

(10)

Then, the left (right) endpoint of
\mathcal{W}(\hat{\bm{A}})
is the minimum (maximum) eigenvalue of the generalized eigenvalue problem
(\tau\bm{D})\bm{x}=\lambda\bm{M}\bm{x}
.
Similarly the top (bottom) endpoint of
\mathcal{W}(\hat{\bm{A}})
is the maximum (minimum) eigenvalue of the generalized eigenvalue problem
(\tau\bm{C})\bm{x}=\lambda\bm{M}\bm{x}
.

Proof.

For
\hat{\bm{A}}=\bm{M}^{1/2}\bm{A}\bm{M}^{-1/2}=\tau\bm{M}^{-1/2}\bm{K}\bm{M}^{-1/2}
, its symmetric and skew-symmetric part are
\tau\bm{M}^{-1/2}\bm{D}\bm{M}^{-1/2}
and
\tau\bm{M}^{-1/2}\bm{C}\bm{M}^{-1/2}
, respectively because
\bm{M}
is SPD.
Therefore, the eigenvalues of the symmetric parts coincide with those of
\bm{M}^{-1}(\tau\bm{D})
, and the eigenvalues of the skew-symmetric part coincide with those of
\bm{M}^{-1}(\tau\bm{C})
.
∎

It is known that the numerical range of the advection-diffusion operator lies in the left half plane.
In addition, if the finite element method is employed, the numerical range of the resulting matrix
\bm{K}
also lies in the left half plane
Göckler and
Grimm 2017
.
The set
\mathcal{W}(\hat{\bm{A}})
inherits this convenient property as follows:

Proposition 3
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{W}(\bm{K})
is in the left half plane.
Then,
\mathcal{W}(\hat{\bm{A}})
is also in the left half plane.

Proof.

For
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}(\bm{v}^{\ast}\hat{\bm{A}}\bm{v})=\mathrm{Re}(\tau\bm{v}^{\ast}\bm{M}^{-1/2}\bm{K}\bm{M}^{-1/2}\bm{v})=\tau\mathrm{Re}\left((\bm{M}^{-1/2}\bm{v})^{\ast}\bm{K}(\bm{M}^{-1/2}\bm{v})\right)\leq 0.

(11)

∎

At the end of this section, we summarize the error-control framework in Algorithm
2
.
First, we determine a rectangle
\mathcal{R}
enclosing
\mathcal{W}(\hat{\bm{A}})
by computing the extreme eigenvalues of
(\tau\bm{D},\bm{M})
and
(\tau\bm{C},\bm{M})
, respectively.
Then, following a procedure similar to Algorithm
2
, we construct a rational function
r(z)\approx\mathrm{e}^{z}
on
\mathcal{R}
and compute
r(\bm{A})\bm{b}
.
Note that the eigenvalues and the condition number required in Algorithm
2
only need to be estimated roughly.
For example, it suffices to obtain a lower bound for
\kappa(\bm{M})
.
If
\kappa(\bm{M})
is computed with a relative error bounded by
\delta~(<1)
, yielding an approximate value
\tilde{\kappa}
, one can use
\tilde{\kappa}/(1-\delta)
in place of
\kappa(\bm{M})
because the inequality
\kappa(\bm{M})\geq\tilde{\kappa}/(1-\delta)
holds.

.

\nu_{\max}
(

.

3
Numerical examples

This section presents numerical results of the proposed framework described in Algorithm
2
.
The experiments were conducted on a Mac Studio M4 Max equipped with 128 GB of RAM.
All algorithms were implemented in Julia 1.12.3, and the source code is available from the corresponding author upon request.
Unless otherwise specified, all computations were performed using IEEE double-precision arithmetic.

3.1
Test problems

We consider a two-dimensional advection-diffusion equation on both a square domain and a star-shaped domain, as illustrated in Figure
2
.
The governing equation is given by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=d\Delta u+\bm{c}^{\top}\nabla u&&((x,y)\in\Omega,t>0),\\
&u(t,x,y)=0&&((x,y)\in\partial\Omega),\\
&u(0,x,y)=u_{0}(x,y)&&((x,y)\in\Omega,t=0),\\
\end{aligned}

(13)

where
d\in\{10^{-1},10^{-3}\}
,
\bm{c}=[1,1]^{\top}
.
The initial condition for the square domain is

\displaystyle u_{0}(x,y)=\exp\left(-\sinh(70(x-1/2)^{4})-\sinh(70(y-1/2)^{4})\right),

(14)

and that for the star-shaped domain is

\displaystyle u_{0}(x,y)=\exp\left(-\sinh(70(x/2)^{4})-\sinh(70(y/2)^{4})\right).

(15)

We employ the standard Galerkin discretization with the mesh generated by FreeFEM++
Hecht 2012
.
This yields the following system of ordinary differential equations as a semi-discrete scheme:

\displaystyle\bm{M}\bm{u}^{\prime}(t)=\bm{K}\bm{u}(t),\qquad\bm{u}(0)=\bm{b}.

(16)

An exponential integrator requires to compute
\mathrm{e}^{\tau\bm{M}^{-1}\bm{K}}\bm{b}
for a given time step size
\tau
(
\bm{b}
is replaced with another vector as time integration proceeds).

Since our aim is to check the performance of the proposed method, we employ the linear PDE as our toy problems.
We note that even for nonlinear PDEs, an exponential integrator requires the computation of the action of the exponential of matrices of the form
\mathrm{e}^{\tau\bm{M}^{-1}\bm{K}}\bm{b}
.

3.2
Computational methods of the matrix exponential

In this test, we consider two computational methods for the matrix exponential.

•

Subdiagonal Padé approximation
(
sub-pade
)

The Padé approximation of
\mathrm{e}^{z}
at
z=0
has been widely used for computing matrix exponentials and their actions, as its coefficients are available in closed form.
Although diagonal
Higham 2005
;
Al-Mohy and Higham 2010
or polynomial
Al-Mohy and
Higham 2011
types are common choice, Güettel et al.
Güttel and
Nakatsukasa 2016
pointed out several advantages of subdiagonal types.
For example, the subdiagonal Padé approximation may reduce the computaional cost for matrices having large eigenvalues in negative direction, and it can be implemented in partial fraction form.
Given the lack of rigorous error estimates for non-symmetric matrices in
Güttel and
Nakatsukasa 2016
, we employ the following heuristic parameter selection:

–

The Padé order is fixed to
(4,5)
.

–

The scaling parameter
s_{\ast}
is chosen to ensure that the approximation error on the rectangle
\mathcal{R}
is within the target tolerance:

\displaystyle s_{\ast}=\min\left\{s=1,2,3,\dots\colon\sup_{z\in\mathcal{R}}|\mathrm{e}^{z}-(r_{4,5}(z/s))^{s}|\leq\frac{\epsilon}{(1+\sqrt{2})\kappa(\bm{M})}\right\},

(17)

where
r_{4,5}
is the
(4,5)
-type Padé approximant.

–

The approximation
r_{4,5}(\bm{A}/s)\cdots r_{4,5}(\bm{A}/s)\bm{b}
is computed in the partial fraction form, see
(
Güttel and
Nakatsukasa 2016
, Sect. 3.2)
.

•

Rational interpolation
(
rat-interp
)

Recent advances in algorithms for rational approximation of scalar functions enable the efficient construction of approximations over specified complex domains.
Here, we numerically construct a rational approximation in partial fraction form:

\displaystyle r_{\operatorname{pf}}(z)=\gamma+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{\alpha_{k}}{\beta_{k}-z}\approx\mathrm{e}^{z},

(18)

and evaluate
r_{\operatorname{pf}}(\bm{A})\bm{b}
.
The approximation
r_{\operatorname{pf}}
is constructed by first running the continuum AAA algorithm
Driscoll et al. 2024
to determine the poles
\beta_{k}
.
Subsequently, the coefficients
\gamma
and
\alpha_{k}
are determined by solving a linear least-squares problem.
Because the coefficient matrix in the linear least square problem can be ill-conditioned, we employ the double-double arithmetic via the
DoubleFloats.jl
package
2
2

2

https://github.com/JuliaMath/DoubleFloats.jl

for solving it.

(\tau\bm{C},\bm{M})
were computed using the

The condition number

Linear systems were solved using a direct solver (

in Julia).
Reference solutions were generated by

exp
is Julia’s built-in function, which is implemented based on the scaling and squaring algorithm in
Higham 2005

The target tolerance
\epsilon

10^{-8}
to
10^{-2}
.

4
Conclusion

We proposed an error control framework for computing the matrix exponential of matrices with the structure
\bm{A}=\tau\bm{M}^{-1}\bm{K}
that relies on
\mathcal{W}(\hat{\bm{A}})
rather than
\mathcal{W}(\bm{A})
.
The proposed set
\mathcal{W}(\hat{\bm{A}})
is convenient: an enclosing rectangle can be obtained numerically, and if
\mathcal{W}(\bm{K})
lies in the left half plane then
\mathcal{W}(\hat{\bm{A}})
also resides in the left half plane.
Numerical experiments showed that, once a scalar rational approximation achieving the prescribed accuracy on
\mathcal{W}(\hat{\bm{A}})
is obtained, one can compute
\mathrm{e}^{\hat{\bm{A}}}\bm{b}
with an error smaller than or equal to the tolerance.

Future work includes comprehensive performance evaluation in parallel computing environments using large-scale problems.
