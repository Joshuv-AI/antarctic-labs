Serological signatures and pathological features for the management of CEA-negative colorectal cancer: development of combined diagnostic and prognostic nomograms
Authors: Zhongmao Fu, Zai Luo, Chao Han, Renchao Zhang, Yitian Xu, Yuan Zhang, Jing Qin, Chen Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) remains a leading cause of malignancy-related mortality, with prognosis heavily contingent upon early detection. Notably, a significant proportion of CRC patients present with normal preoperative Carcinoembryonic Antigen (CEA) levels, creating a diagnostic blind spot where malignant lesions may be indistinguishable from benign colorectal disease (BCD). This study aimed to develop and validate multidimensional nomograms to accurately diagnose and prognostically stratify patients with CEA-negative CRC.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed data from 553 CEA-negative CRC patients and 200 BCD patients. Optimal biomarker thresholds were determined via ROC analysis. Diagnostic and prognostic predictors were identified using multivariable logistic and Cox regressions. Model performance was evaluated using C-indices, calibration plots, Decision Curve Analysis (DCA), and Clinical Impact Curves (CIC).ResultsOf 895 screened CRC patients, 61.8% were CEA-negative. An eight-variable diagnostic model (age, sex, CA50, CA125, CA19-9, WBC, PLR, FAR) demonstrated strong discriminative power, yielding C-indices of 0.886 (training) and 0.895 (validation). For overall survival, a prognostic signature incorporating age, tumor location, Lymph Node Ratio (LNR), Monocyte-to-Lymphocyte ratio (MLR), and Tumor-Stroma Ratio (TSP) achieved C-indices of 0.749 (training) and 0.829 (validation), showing favorable prognostic stratification compared to the AJCC 8th TNM staging system. DCA and CIC confirmed superior clinical net benefit for both models.ConclusionIntegrating systemic inflammatory indices and stromal features provides a potentially useful adjunctive tool for cancer detection and risk assessment. These user-friendly nomograms may assist in personalized prognostication, potentially refining clinical decision-making and patient stratification for therapeutic trials. However, independent external validation is required before clinical implementation due to the single-center design and internally derived cut-offs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-026-04452-x.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains a formidable global health challenge, ranking as the third most prevalent malignancy and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1]. Current projections paint a concerning picture: by 2040, the global burden of CRC is expected to surge by nearly 60% compared to 2020 levels [2]. While therapeutic advancements have improved survival rates for early-stage disease, the prognosis for patients with advanced or metastatic presentations remains dismal [3]. Consequently, the cornerstone of improving long-term outcomes lies in the precise identification of high-risk individuals to facilitate timely, personalized interventions. However, a critical diagnostic gray zone persists for a substantial subset of patients who do not exhibit classical biochemical signals, leaving clinicians without a reliable compass for risk stratification and early detection.

The Carcinoembryonic Antigen (CEA) has long served as the standard-bearer for CRC surveillance. Historically, elevated CEA levels are strongly correlated with tumor burden, recurrence, and metastasis [4–8]. Yet, a significant clinical paradox exists: over 50% of CRC patients present with normal preoperative CEA levels [4, 9]. These marker-negative patients are not necessarily at lower risk; rather, they often harbor aggressive malignancies that bypass traditional secretory pathways, potentially leading to a false sense of security and delayed treatment [10, 11]. Furthermore, the low sensitivity of CEA renders it ineffective for general screening [12]. Therefore, relying exclusively on CEA monitoring creates a dangerous blind spot in the management of this large patient population.

To address this limitation, attention has shifted from tumor-derived markers to the host-tumor interface, specifically systemic inflammation and the tumor microenvironment (TME) [13]. Systemic inflammatory markers, including the Neutrophil-to-Lymphocyte ratio (NLR), Monocyte-to-Lymphocyte ratio (MLR), Platelet-to-Lymphocyte ratio (PLR), Fibrinogen-to-Albumin ratio (FAR), and C-reactive protein-to-Albumin ratio (CAR), have emerged as critical determinants of malignant progression and patient survival [14–17]. Furthermore, recent evidence highlights their predictive value in therapeutic settings; specifically, pre-treatment levels of CRP, NLR, PLR, and MLR have been strongly correlated with both progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) in patients undergoing immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy [18–20]. However, most existing studies conflate CEA-positive and CEA-negative populations, a methodological limitation that risks diluting the specific predictive value of inflammatory markers for the CEA-negative subgroup, where they may serve as the primary indicators of disease progression [21].

Parallel to inflammatory indices, alternative serum tumor markers such as CA19-9, CA125, CA50, and CA724 are being explored as supplementary tools [8, 22–28]. While individual markers often lack sufficient sensitivity [24], panel-based approaches combining multiple antigens have shown promise in enhancing diagnostic accuracy [10, 25, 26, 29, 30]. However, a significant gap remains in the current literature: few studies have successfully integrated these serological markers with histological features of the tumor microenvironment, specifically the Tumor-Stroma Ratio (TSP), to create a comprehensive predictive model. TSP is a powerful indicator of desmoplasia and tumor invasiveness and has been validated as a robust prognosticator independent of TNM staging in our previous study [31]. The integration of serological biomarkers and histopathological features offers a promising strategy to mitigate the diagnostic challenges in CEA-negative patients, yet the clinical value of this multidimensional approach lacks sufficient validation.

To address this unmet clinical need, our study retrospectively evaluated a large cohort of CEA-negative CRC patients. We aimed to dissect the complex interplay between serum tumor markers, systemic inflammatory indicators, and clinicopathological characteristics. By integrating these diverse variables, we developed and validated novel nomogram models. These tools are designed to provide clinicians with a precise, user-friendly method for differentiating benign disease from CEA-negative CRC and for accurately stratifying overall survival, thereby potentially supporting more personalized clinical decision-making. However, external validation is required before these tools can be implemented clinically.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient population

This retrospective analysis utilized clinical data derived from a comprehensive, prospectively maintained institutional database at Shanghai General Hospital. The study population was composed of two distinct cohorts: a primary cohort of patients with confirmed CRC and a control group comprising patients with benign colorectal disease (BCD). Eligible participants were consecutively identified from the hospital’s electronic medical records system, ensuring a representative sample for both diagnostic and prognostic evaluation. The study protocol adhered to the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Shanghai General Hospital.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All CRC patients underwent radical surgical resection. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) No history of neoadjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy within three months prior to surgery; (ii) Histologically confirmed colorectal adenocarcinoma; and (iii) Availability of complete clinicopathological, laboratory, and follow-up data. Patients were excluded if they met any of the following conditions: lack of survival follow-up data (n = 976), missing key pathological indicators (n = 20), or absence of preoperative CEA measurements (n = 164). A detailed flowchart of the patient selection process is presented in Fig. 1.

The final study population comprised 895 CRC patients, of whom 342 exhibited elevated preoperative CEA levels and 553 presented with normal levels (defined as CEA-negative). Tumor staging was determined in accordance with the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging system. Pathological diagnoses were independently verified by two senior pathologists.

The BCD patients were consecutively enrolled during the same period as the CRC cohort to minimize temporal bias and included only those with confirmed non-malignant pathology (colorectal polyps, n = 121; benign tumors, n = 79). Patients with acute or active chronic inflammatory conditions were explicitly excluded to avoid confounding inflammatory indices. Serum tumor markers and inflammatory indices were collected from these patients to serve as the non-malignant control arm for the diagnostic nomogram.

Data collection and variable definitions

We extracted a comprehensive set of variables, including demographic characteristics (age, sex), pathological features (TNM stage, vascular/perineural invasion, tumor location/size), and the TSP. To eliminate observer bias and maximize reproducibility, the TSP was quantitatively evaluated using our previously validated automated MATLAB-based algorithm [31], which objectively calculates stromal percentage from whole-slide images with higher accuracy than manual estimation. A standard cut-off of 50% was applied to categorize patients into high (> 50%) versus low (≤ 50%) stromal groups. The Lymph Node Ratio (LNR) was also calculated, with a cut-off value of 0.

Hematological parameters included white blood cell count (WBC), neutrophils (NE), lymphocytes (LY), monocytes (MO), platelets (PLT), fibrinogen (FIB), C-reactive protein (CRP), albumin (Alb), and total bilirubin (TBil). Additionally, a panel of serum tumor markers (CEA, CA19-9, CA50, CA125, CA724, CA242) and molecular markers (CDX2, MLH1, MSH2, KI-67, ERCC1) were assessed.

Determination of optimal cut-off values

Patients with preoperative serum CEA levels ≤ 5.0 ng/mL were classified as the CEA-negative cohort. To maximize the diagnostic sensitivity of the remaining biomarkers specifically for the CEA-negative population, we recalculated optimal cut-off values using Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis by determining the maximum Youden index (Sensitivity+Specificity-1). The determining thresholds were: CA19-9 (12.51 U/mL), CEA (1.77 ng/mL), CA125 (9.43 U/mL), CA72-4 (1.63 U/mL), CA50 (7.44 U/mL), and CA242 (13.64 U/mL). Similarly, optimal thresholds for inflammatory indices were established as follows: NLR (1.432), PLR (180.156), MLR (0.228), FAR (0.075), and CAR (0.043). For the prognostic nomogram, to facilitate the practical application of the scoring system, all continuous variables (including tumor markers and inflammatory indices) were dichotomized according to their optimal cut-off values prior to survival analysis.

Follow-up protocol and endpoint definition

Postoperative surveillance was conducted quarterly for the first two years and annually from the third to the fifth year. Follow-up assessments included serum tumor marker profiling, blood chemistry, and imaging (chest/abdominal CT or pelvic MRI), with annual colonoscopy. OS was defined as the interval from the date of surgery to the date of death from any cause. For patients who remained alive at the time of analysis, data were censored at the date of their last clinical contact.

Model construction and validation

To facilitate robust model development and internal validation, the 553 CEA-negative CRC patients were randomized into a training cohort (n = 369) and a validation cohort (n = 184) using a stratified sampling method (R function “createFolds”). This approach ensured balanced outcome distribution across cohorts. For the diagnostic nomogram (differentiating BCD from CEA-negative CRC), optimal cut-offs derived from ROC analysis were applied. Independent predictors were identified via univariate and multivariable binary logistic regression analyses. For the prognostic nomogram (predicting OS), 27 potential clinicopathological variables were screened using Kaplan-Meier survival curves and log-rank tests. Variables demonstrating statistical significance were subsequently entered into univariate and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models to identify independent prognostic factors.

Statistical analysis and model evaluation

Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square (χ2) test. The performance of the nomograms was quantified using the concordance index (C-index) and visualized via calibration plots comparing predicted versus observed probabilities. The clinical utility and net benefit of the models were assessed using Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) and Clinical Impact Curves (CIC). For the prognostic model, DCA was specifically employed to benchmark the nomogram against the AJCC 8th edition TNM staging system.

To ensure data integrity and avoid imputation bias, missing values in this retrospective cohort were handled according to the proportion of missingness. Specifically, variables with a substantial amount of missing data (e.g., CA242) were excluded from multivariable modeling to prevent significant attrition of the study population. For the remaining incorporated variables with negligible missing values, complete case analysis (listwise deletion) was employed during all multivariable logistic and Cox regression analyses.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and R software version 4.4.0 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing). A complete list of R packages utilized is provided in Supplementary Table S1. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.050 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic and clinicopathological characteristics

A total of 895 patients with CRC undergoing radical resection were included in the study. Based on preoperative serum CEA levels, the cohort was stratified into the CEA-negative group (n = 553, 61.8%) and the CEA-elevated group (n = 342, 38.2%). To ensure statistical robustness, Stage III and IV patients were grouped together, which included 199 (36.0%) Stage III patients and a minimal 6 (1.1%) Stage IV patients in the CEA-negative cohort (n = 553). The detailed baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Comparative analysis revealed significant disparities between the two groups, specifically, variables including gender, TNM stage, perineural/vascular invasion, tumor size, TSP, and expression levels of CA19-9, CA50, CA724, CA242, and ERCC1 differed statistically (P < 0.050).

Diagnostic performance of individual biomarkers

To identify potential candidates for the diagnostic model, we analyzed serum tumor markers and inflammatory indices in 553 CEA-negative CRC patients compared to 200 patients with BCD. Utilizing optimized cut-off values, we found that CEA retained diagnostic utility even within the normal range, achieving a sensitivity of 67.6% (Figure S1, Table S2). Among other tumor markers, CA50 and CA125 emerged as the most sensitive indicators (60.2% and 58.6%, respectively), exhibiting superior diagnostic performance with Area Under the Curve (AUC) values of 0.734 and 0.771, respectively (Figure S2A, Table S2). Notably, the combination of CA50 and CA125 significantly enhanced diagnostic efficiency, yielding an AUC of 0.840 (Figure S2B, Table S2).

Regarding inflammatory indices, the FAR and CAR demonstrated the highest individual discriminative ability, with AUCs of 0.739 and 0.700, respectively (Figure S2C, Table S3). However, unlike tumor markers, combining inflammatory indicators (e.g., MLR + CAR) did not yield a significant improvement in diagnostic accuracy (Figure S2D, Table S3). Consequently, serum tumor markers generally outperformed inflammatory indices in diagnostic precision for this specific population.

Development and validation of diagnostic nomograms

To systematically evaluate and optimize diagnostic accuracy, we constructed three distinct nomograms to differentiate CEA-negative CRC from BCD: Model 1 (based solely on tumor markers), Model 2 (based solely on inflammatory indices), and Model 3 (an integrated approach). Initially, multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed for the single-category models. For Model 1, eight tumor markers (including CA19-9, CA50, and CA125) were identified as independent risk factors (Table S4), yielding a robust C-index of 0.887 in the validation cohort (Table S7). In parallel, Model 2 incorporated inflammatory parameters, identifying indices such as NLR, PLR, MLR, and FAR as independent predictors (Table S5); however, this model exhibited more moderate predictive capability, with a validation C-index of 0.733 (Table S7, Figure S3).

To further enhance diagnostic precision, we developed the integrated Model 3 by combining the most significant inflammatory and tumor markers. Multivariable analysis confirmed Age, Gender, CA50, CA125, CEA, PLR, FAR, and WBC as independent determinants of disease status (Table S6), with CA19-9 retained for its clinical relevance (Fig. 2A). As anticipated, this comprehensive model demonstrated superior performance compared to the single-category models, achieving the highest C-indices of 0.886 in the training set and 0.895 in the validation set (Table S7). The nomogram attained a validation sensitivity of 84.9% (95% CI: 77.2–90.8), specificity of 73.4% (95% CI: 60.9–83.7), and accuracy of 80.9% (95% CI: 74.4–86.3), with PPV of 85.6% and NPV of 72.3% (Supplementary Table S8), confirming its robust diagnostic performance for CEA-negative CRC. Calibration plots revealed excellent agreement between predicted probabilities and actual observations (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, DCA and CIC confirmed that Model 3 offered the greatest net clinical benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities, outperforming both Model 1 and Model 2 (Fig. 2C-D).

Identification of prognostic factors for overall survival

Among 553 CEA-negative CRC patients with a median follow-up of 57.7 months (range 3.6–92.9), 76 deaths occurred. To build a prognostic model, 27 clinicopathological variables were screened using Kaplan-Meier and log-rank tests, and significant ones entered a Cox proportional hazards model (Table 2 and S9). Multivariable analysis identified five independent determinants of OS: Age (> 60 years; HR = 1.941, P = 0.039), Lymph Node Ratio (LNR) (Positive; HR = 3.312, P < 0.001), MLR (> 0.228; HR = 2.373, P = 0.002), TSP (> 50%; HR = 1.827, P = 0.024), and Tumor Location (Left vs. Right Colon; HR = 1.988, P = 0.008). We further explored CEA’s prognostic value within the normal range. As a continuous variable, it was significantly associated with OS (HR = 1.270, P = 0.012), but significance was lost when dichotomized (HR = 1.409, P = 0.188), justifying its exclusion from the final nomogram (Table 2).

Construction and clinical value of the prognostic nomogram

Based on these five independent predictors, a prognostic nomogram was established (Fig. 3A). The model displayed robust discrimination, with C-indices of 0.749 (training) and 0.829 (validation). The corrected C-indices were consistent at 0.714 and 0.799, respectively (Table S10). Calibration curves demonstrated excellent agreement between the nomogram predicted survival and actual outcomes in both the training and total cohorts (Fig. 3B-D).

Stratification capability and comparison with TNM staging

To rigorously benchmark the clinical utility of our prognostic nomogram against the standard AJCC 8th edition TNM staging system, we first evaluated its risk stratification capability. Based on the calculated nomogram scores, patients were stratified into distinct low- and high-risk subgroups. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated robust discrimination, with the low-risk group exhibiting significantly superior overall survival compared to the high-risk group across the training, validation, and total cohorts (P < 0.010; Fig. 4A-C). Subsequently, DCA was employed to compare the net clinical benefit of the two systems. The results indicated that our nomogram achieved a higher net benefit than the TNM staging system across the majority of threshold probabilities (Fig. 4D-F). This finding suggests that by integrating biological indicators such as TSP and systemic inflammatory indices, our multi-dimensional model offers a highly favorable clinical net benefit for CEA-negative patients compared to traditional anatomical staging alone.

Discussion

Serum CEA levels have long been the cornerstone for monitoring CRC [5, 32, 33]. However, a significant diagnostic blind spot exists, as 60.0% to 79.6% of CRC patients present with normal preoperative CEA levels [30, 34]. Consistent with this, 61.8% of the 895 patients in our cohort were CEA-negative. To address this urgent need, we developed two novel nomograms: a diagnostic model integrating CA50, CA125, and inflammatory indices, and a prognostic model incorporating TSP and MLR, which demonstrates a superior clinical net benefit compared to the AJCC 8th TNM staging system.

Given the high prevalence of CEA-negativity, standard CEA thresholds remain insufficient for early detection [12, 35]. While lowering the CEA cut-off (e.g., 1.765 ng/mL in our study) improved sensitivity [10, 36, 37], a multi-marker panel approach remains superior. Aligning with Butvilovskaya et al. [38] and Zhao et al. [39], our multivariate model confirms that integrating multiple markers significantly enhances diagnostic accuracy. Despite some debates [37], CA125 and CA50 emerged as robust independent predictors in our CEA-negative subgroup, likely reflecting distinct biological pathways, such as mucinous differentiation, that capture tumors missed by CEA.

The host immune response is increasingly recognized as critical for tumor identification. While the link between CRP and CRC is debated [19, 40], ratios like NLR, PLR, and MLR show diagnostic promise in other malignancies such as gliomas and lung cancer [41, 42]. In our specific cohort, isolated inflammation markers had limited diagnostic utility, but the FAR and CRP-to-Albumin Ratio (CAR) proved more effective, serving as stable indicators of systemic inflammatory and nutritional status [43, 44]. Crucially, our integrated model (Model 3) achieved the highest predictive performance, indicating that systemic inflammation acts synergistically with tumor antigens rather than as a standalone diagnostic tool. Additionally, while our prognostic cohort includes a small subgroup of Stage IV patients to capture the full real-world spectrum of CEA-negative CRC, this clinical inclusiveness may introduce minor survival heterogeneity. Nevertheless, our model demonstrated robust and stable predictive performance across the entire population. These findings suggest that, although pathological confirmation remains the gold standard, our diagnostic nomogram serves as a non-invasive pre-endoscopic triage tool. It is particularly useful for prioritizing urgent colonoscopies, assessing risk in patients with suspicious imaging pending pathology, and supporting repeat biopsies when initial results are inconclusive.

Regarding prognosis, our study highlights a shift from traditional tumor burden markers to microenvironment indicators. Unlike previous studies suggesting elevated conventional markers (e.g., CA125, CA242) link to poor survival [30, 45], these markers lost independent prognostic significance in our CEA-negative multivariate analysis. Instead, the systemic MLR and local TSP drove survival outcomes. A high MLR indicates an immunosuppressive shift facilitating immune escape [46, 47], while high TSP (> 50%) signifies a desmoplastic barrier hindering immune infiltration [31]. This supports a refined Seed and Soil hypothesis: when the seed (tumor biomarkers) is silent, the outcome is dictated by the soil-the systemic immune competence and local stromal permissiveness. However, given that OS is confounded by postoperative treatments, recurrence management, comorbidities, and other non-tumor factors, these findings require cautious interpretation, and our nomogram should be viewed as a composite risk indicator, not a pure measure of tumor biology.

Several limitations warrant mention. First, our retrospective, single-center design introduces unavoidable selection bias. Furthermore, deriving optimal cut-offs and developing models within the same cohort carries a substantial risk of overfitting. Although internally validated, the external validity of our nomograms remains limited, necessitating rigorous validation in independent, multi-center cohorts before clinical implementation. Second, our discrepancy with Dai et al. [30] regarding the prognostic value of CA242 may be attributed to missing data in our cohort, which necessitated its exclusion from the final multivariate model. Third, our analysis relied on static preoperative baselines; future studies should incorporate longitudinal monitoring to assess how these markers evolve during treatment. Fourth, because our diagnostic nomogram incorporates systemic inflammatory indices, its reliability may be compromised in patients with acute or active chronic inflammation (e.g., diverticulitis, infectious colitis, active IBD), which can artificially elevate these markers and cause false-positive results. Therefore, this nomogram should be applied to patients with suspicious lesions only after active systemic inflammatory confounders have been excluded. Fifth, because OS is confounded by subsequent treatments and comorbidities, our nomogram reflects a composite clinical risk rather than pure tumor biology. By contrast, RFS would offer a more direct measure and should be employed in future prospective validation.

Finally, our nomograms should be viewed as complementary to emerging ctDNA-based assays. While ctDNA enables highly sensitive detection of molecular residual disease and systemic genetic alterations [48], our models uniquely capture the local tumor microenvironment (e.g., TSP) and host immune response using routine, cost-effective parameters. This makes them ideal for immediate, first-line triage, particularly in resource-limited settings, where they can prioritize patients for subsequent ctDNA testing [49]. Future integration of ctDNA into this multidimensional framework could yield next-generation models that comprehensively combine tumor genetics with microenvironmental and host factors.

In conclusion, this study provides a hypothesis-generating framework for the management of CEA-negative CRC. We demonstrate that integrating serum tumor markers (CA125, CA50) with systemic inflammatory (MLR) and stromal (TSP) features significantly enhances both diagnosis and risk stratification. Our developed nomograms offer a practical, non-invasive tool to resolve the diagnostic uncertainty in this blind spot population, guiding more personalized clinical decision-making.
