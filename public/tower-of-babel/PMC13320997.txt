Comparison of red complex bacteria and antimicrobial resistance genes in stage III/IV periodontitis patients with and without type 2 diabetes mellitus: A cross-sectional observational study

Introduction

Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease characterized by progressive destruction of the tooth-supporting tissues, resulting in periodontal attachment loss and alveolar bone resorption. Its pathogenesis is driven by microbial dysbiosis, host immune response, and genetic, environmental, and behavioral factors.1,2 Among periodontal pathogens, the red complex species— Porphyromonas gingivalis, Treponema denticola, and Tannerella forsythia are strongly associated with disease severity due to their capacity for tissue invasion, immune evasion, and virulence factor production. Their increased prevalence in deep periodontal pockets highlights their critical role in disease progression, making their detection in subgingival plaque valuable for risk assessment and targeted therapy.3

Conventional periodontal therapy primarily involves mechanical disruption of biofilms through scaling and root planing, with systemic antibiotics recommended in selected severe cases. Antibiotics such as amoxicillin and metronidazole are commonly prescribed as adjuncts to mechanical therapy to suppress pathogenic bacterial species and reduce inflammatory burden.4,5 However, systemic antibiotic use is generally reserved for patients with advanced disease or systemic comorbidities, including diabetes mellitus, where adjunctive antimicrobial therapy has demonstrated additional clinical benefits.6

Periodontitis shares a well-established bidirectional relationship with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), a metabolic disorder characterized by insulin resistance, chronic low-grade inflammation, and impaired immune function.7 Hyperglycemia exacerbates periodontal tissue destruction through enhanced inflammatory responses, impaired wound healing, and the accumulation of advanced glycation end products, while periodontitis contributes to systemic inflammation and poorer glycemic control by elevating pro-inflammatory mediators such as interleukin-6, tumor necrosis factor-α, and C-reactive protein.8 Diabetic individuals also exhibit an altered subgingival microbiome, with increased prevalence of red complex pathogens and other opportunistic bacteria, potentially increasing disease severity and treatment complexity.9

The frequent use of antibiotics in periodontal therapy has contributed to the growing challenge of antimicrobial resistance (AMR). In individuals with compromised immune responses, such as those with uncontrolled diabetes, prolonged or higher-dose antibiotic regimens are sometimes required, further increasing the risk of resistance development.10,11 Given the high microbial diversity within the oral cavity and the impracticality of routine microbiological testing, selecting appropriate antimicrobial therapy remains challenging.12 Moreover, resistance can emerge within months of antibiotic exposure, underscoring the importance of antibiotic stewardship in dental practice.13, 14, 15, 16.

Oral biofilms serve as reservoirs for antimicrobial-resistant pathogens, facilitating persistence, reinfection, and treatment failure.17, 18, 19 Resistance mechanisms in periodontal bacteria include horizontal gene transfer, enzymatic drug degradation, efflux pump activity, reduced membrane permeability, and biofilm-mediated protection.20, 21, 22 Of particular relevance to periodontal therapy, the blaTEM gene encodes β-lactamase enzymes that inactivate β-lactam antibiotics such as penicillins and cephalosporins,23 while the nim gene confers resistance to nitroimidazole antibiotics, including metronidazole, by enzymatically reducing the drug to inactive forms.24 The increasing detection of such resistance genes in periodontal pathogens raises concerns regarding the long-term efficacy of commonly prescribed antimicrobial agents.

Despite increasing evidence linking type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) with periodontal destruction and alterations in the oral microbiome, limited studies have specifically evaluated antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes in Stage III/IV periodontitis patients with diabetes. Most available studies have primarily focused on microbial prevalence or clinical periodontal parameters, while the distribution of resistance determinants within subgingival biofilms in diabetic individuals remains insufficiently explored. In particular, data regarding β-lactam resistance genes (blaTEM) and metronidazole resistance genes (nim) in periodontal patients with T2DM are scarce, especially in relation to red complex periodontal pathogens.

Furthermore, although adjunctive antibiotics such as amoxicillin and metronidazole are frequently prescribed in advanced periodontitis and medically compromised patients, growing antimicrobial resistance poses a significant challenge to periodontal therapy and antibiotic stewardship. Whether diabetes-associated metabolic dysregulation influences the prevalence of resistance genes and periodontal pathogens within subgingival biofilms remains unclear. Understanding these microbial and resistance patterns is clinically important for improving individualized periodontal treatment planning, minimizing therapeutic failure, and promoting rational antibiotic use in periodontal practice. Few studies have simultaneously evaluated red complex pathogens and AMR genes in Stage III/IV periodontitis patients with and without T2DM, particularly blaTEM and nim in subgingival biofilms.Clarifying these resistance patterns is essential for guiding adjunctive antibiotic use and stewardship in high-risk diabetic periodontitis patients.

Therefore, the present cross-sectional observational study aimed to compare the prevalence of red complex periodontal pathogens and antimicrobial resistance genes (blaTEM and nim) between Stage III/IV periodontitis patients with and without type 2 diabetes mellitus.

Materials and method

Study design and ethical approval

This cross-sectional observational study was conducted in the Outpatient Department of Periodontology from April 2023 to November 2024 following approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee (ETICS/SBSMIDS/321/2023). The study was reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines for observational studies. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Participant selection and sampling

A total of 68 participants aged 35–60 years were recruited using consecutive sampling and equally allocated into two groups, Consecutive sampling was used to reduce selection bias in patient recruitment.

Group 1: Stage III/IV periodontitis patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM)

Inclusion criteria: comprised systemically healthy individuals and patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus aged 35–60 years diagnosed with generalized Stage III/IV periodontitis according to the 2018 American Academy of Periodontology/European Federation of Periodontology (AAP/EFP) classification. Participants were required to have interdental clinical attachment loss detectable at ≥2 non-adjacent teeth, probing pocket depth (PPD) ≥5 mm, radiographic bone loss extending to the middle third of the root or beyond, and a minimum of 20 natural teeth. Diabetic participants were categorized based on HbA1c values as good glycemic control (≤7%) or poor glycemic control (>7%).

Exclusion criteria: included smoking or tobacco use, pregnancy or lactation, periodontal therapy or antibiotic use within the previous 6 months, and the presence of systemic diseases other than T2DM.

Sample collection and molecular analysis

Subgingival plaque samples were collected from the five deepest periodontal pockets in each participant. Following isolation with cotton rolls and removal of supragingival deposits, subgingival plaque was obtained using sterile Gracey curettes while avoiding blood contamination. Samples obtained from five sites were pooled into sterile microcentrifuge tubes containing 200 μL TE buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, 1 mM EDTA; pH 7.5), stored at 4°C, and transported for molecular analysis.

DNA extraction was performed using a commercially available DNA extraction kit, and the extracted DNA was stored at −20°C until further analysis. Red complex bacterial genes and antimicrobial resistance genes (blaTEM and nim) were detected using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) with specific primers (Table 1). PCR amplification was carried out in 30 μL reaction volumes containing sterile nuclease-free water, assay buffer, dNTP mix, forward and reverse primers, Taq DNA polymerase, and template DNA. Amplification was performed under standardized thermal cycling conditions optimized according to primer specifications. The amplified PCR products were subjected to gel electrophoresis and visualized under ultraviolet illumination. Positive and negative controls were included in each amplification run to ensure assay validity and monitor contamination. Standard contamination-control procedures were followed throughout sample handling and PCR processing. PCR detection of resistance genes reflects the genetic potential for antimicrobial resistance but does not confirm phenotypic resistance in clinical isolates.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were analyzed using the independent t-test, while categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test. Results were expressed using descriptive statistics, effect sizes, and 95% confidence intervals where applicable. There were no missing data for clinical or molecular variables; therefore, complete-case analysis was performed Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Result

A total of 68 participants were included: 34 patients with stage III/IV periodontitis and 34 patients with stage III/IV periodontitis patients with T2DM. Age and gender distribution were comparable between groups. While plaque and bleeding scores did not differ significantly, probing pocket depth (PPD) and clinical attachment level (CAL) were significantly higher in the periodontitis + T2DM group, indicating greater periodontal destruction. Red complex bacterial genes (Porphyromonas gingivalis, Treponema denticola, and Tannerella forsythia) showed no significant differences between groups. The blaTEM resistance gene was detected at similar frequencies in both groups, whereas the nim gene was detected exclusively in diabetic patients, indicating a significantly higher prevalence of metronidazole resistance among individuals with diabetes (Table 1). Discussion

Periodontitis, diabetes mellitus, and antimicrobial resistance are interconnected global health challenges that can collectively intensify disease burden through overlapping inflammatory, microbial, and host immune mechanisms.25 In this context, the present study investigated the prevalence of red complex periodontal pathogens and key antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes (blaTEM and nim) in Stage III/IV periodontitis patients with and T2DM, with the aim of clarifying how metabolic dysregulation may influence periodontal destruction and antimicrobial resistance within subgingival biofilms.

The nim resistance gene was detected exclusively in patients with periodontitis + T2DM, suggesting a possible association between diabetes-related metabolic alterations and metronidazole resistance. However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because of the relatively small sample size and cross-sectional design, and should therefore be considered exploratory rather than definitive evidence of a diabetes-specific resistance pattern. These observations are consistent with previous reports by Li et al.26 and Wu et al.,8 who demonstrated poorer periodontal outcomes among diabetic individuals, likely due to sustained inflammatory burden and impaired healing responses. Furthermore, Wei et al.,27 using Mendelian randomization and multi-omics analyses, demonstrated a causal association between periodontitis and T2DM and identified genes such as MCUR1, RAP2A, FOS, PANX1, NFIX, and WNK1 as potential mediators of diabetes-related periodontal susceptibility. The absence of significant differences in full-mouth plaque and bleeding scores suggests that diabetes may predominantly influence deeper periodontal tissue destruction rather than surface biofilm accumulation.28

Red complex bacterial genes were detected in both groups without significant differences. This observation may reflect geographic variation and differences in biofilm maturation dynamics, while also supporting the concept that diabetes exacerbates periodontitis primarily through host immune dysregulation rather than major shifts in pathogen composition. Multinational metagenomic studies have similarly shown that although subgingival microbial composition varies across populations, functional transitions associated with periodontal disease remain relatively conserved.28,29 Other studies have reported elevated levels of Tannerella forsythia and Treponema denticola in patients with diabetes or chronic kidney disease, highlighting the complexity of host–microbial interactions.1,30

The blaTEM gene, associated with β-lactam resistance, was detected at similar frequencies in both groups, likely reflecting its widespread distribution within the oral microbiome. In contrast, the exclusive detection of the nim gene in diabetic individuals may indicate that hyperglycemia-related immune dysfunction and oxidative stress create a selective environment favoring resistant anaerobic strains within subgingival biofilms. These findings are consistent with previous reports of emerging metronidazole resistance in periodontal pathogens. Koukos et al.31 reported widespread blaTEM detection with minimal nim prevalence, while Rams et al.32 and Xie et al.33 highlighted increasing concerns regarding metronidazole resistance in periodontal infections. Given the frequent use of metronidazole as an adjunctive periodontal therapy, the detection of nim in diabetic patients raises concerns regarding potential treatment failure and emphasizes the importance of antimicrobial resistance surveillance in high-risk populations.

The present study has several strengths. It is among the limited studies to simultaneously evaluate red complex periodontal pathogens and antimicrobial resistance genes (blaTEM and nim) in periodontitis patients with and without T2DM. The use of PCR-based molecular analysis enabled sensitive and specific detection of both periodontal pathogens and resistance determinants. In addition, strict inclusion and exclusion criteria, including the exclusion of smokers, recent antibiotic users, and patients with other systemic diseases, helped minimize confounding and improve internal validity. The comparative design with standardized periodontal assessment further enhances the clinical relevance of the findings in the context of antimicrobial stewardship and periodontal management in diabetic patients.

However, the present study has certain limitations. The targeted PCR approach limited comprehensive characterization of the oral microbiome and resistome. In addition, pooling subgingival plaque samples from five periodontal sites may have masked site-specific microbial and resistance variations. Potential confounding factors such as age, gender, glycemic control, and oral hygiene were not adjusted using multivariable regression analysis because of the exploratory design, relatively small sample size, and limited number of nim-positive cases. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted cautiously. Larger longitudinal studies incorporating comprehensive microbial profiling and multivariable analyses are needed to validate these observations. The exclusive detection of the nim gene in diabetic individuals may reflect alterations in the subgingival environment associated with hyperglycemia, oxidative stress, and immune dysregulation, which have previously been implicated in shaping microbial ecology and antimicrobial resistance patterns within oral biofilms.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that the nim resistance gene was detected exclusively in the Stage III/IV periodontitis with T2DM group, indicating a potential association between type 2 diabetes mellitus and the presence of metronidazole resistance determinants in periodontal biofilms. Nonetheless, this observation should be interpreted with caution because the cross-sectional design and relatively small sample size limit the strength and generalizability of the findings.
