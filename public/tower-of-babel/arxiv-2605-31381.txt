LLM Judges Inconsistently Disagree Across Safety Criteria and Harm Categories
Krishnapriya Vishnubhotla, Sowmya Vajjala, Akriti Vij, Isar Nejadgholi

Abstract
We evaluate the consistency of automated judges in conducting a multi-dimensional safety evaluation in a reference-free setup. Our results indicate that Large Language Models are unreliable judges in identifying safety issues related to machine-generated advice in regulated domains such as finance, although they are more reliable at identifying more overt forms of unsafe/harmful content such as violence. The degree of inconsistency in a model’s judgments can vary significantly by the chosen safety criteria and can be impacted by the language of the content and its linguistic style as well. Finally, there is high disagreement among different judges for the same output, across domains, safety criteria, and languages. These findings provide new insights on the practice of using LLMs as evaluators and offer several recommendations for practitioners on how to use automated judges in practical scenarios.

Introduction

Identifying unsafe content in Large Language Model (LLM) input/output has received much attention from the NLP research community in the recent past, and several taxonomies
(
30
;
32
)
and datasets
(
22
;
8
)
have been developed to evaluate LLM safety. The LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm has become a common means of evaluating the safety of LLMs
(
29
, e.g.,)
owing to the flexible nature of their specifications, although specialized fine-tuned classifiers for safety such as Granite Guardian
(
21
)
and LlamaGuard
(
17
)
also exist. Safety evaluations typically view safety as a uni-dimensional safe vs unsafe construct. However, there may be multiple criteria to consider in real-world scenarios, such as whether an LLM is providing helpful output, if there are enough warnings issued, if there an outright refusal, etc.
How do we understand the suitability of LLM judges when we consider such a multi-dimensional notion of safety?
We address this question in this paper.

Reliability challenges of LLM judges across languages and judges.
Our results suggest that LLM judges are less reliable when used for subjective criteria and nuanced or regulated harm categories.

Much of the existing research on assessing AI safety tends to focus on queries or responses that cover obviously harmful content such as violence, hate speech, illegal activities, etc. There is some emerging work on evaluating LLMs for safety in other real-world scenarios, such as providing advice in regulated domains or in automated decision making based on user information
(
32
;
30
)
. In this paper, we compare LLM judges’ performance on both categories of queries with a multi-criteria definition of safety, and metrics that capture the self-consistency of a single judge and cross-consistency among multiple judges, which are described below:

•

Self-consistency
examines whether a judge is consistently giving the same evaluations for different semantically invariant transformations of given input, such as translations or paraphrases. This is important to understand the validity of LLM-as-a-Judge construct.

•

Cross-consistency
examines whether different judges consistently agree with each other given the same input. This is important to understand the validity of the practice of using LLMs-as-a-Jury
(
27
)
, where a team of LLMs are employed to evaluate the output of a model.

We conduct this study in a reference free evaluation setup, i.e., where no gold standard output is assumed to be provided, reflecting the potential usage of LLM-as-a-Judge in real world safety evaluations. Our results indicate that LLMs are generally poor judges in identifying safety issues related to LLM generated advice in regulated domains such as finance, and are better at identifying more explicit forms of harmful content such as violence. Further, they are inconsistent in judging semantically-equivalent (i.e, translated and paraphrased) variations of a given response (low
self-consistency
), highlighting the potential for jailbreaking the judges through such text transformations. The same input when given to multiple judges also elicits contrasting judgments (low
cross consistency
), with varying degrees of disagreements across the different safety criteria.

Our main contributions are as follows:

1.

We develop a workflow that allows for measuring consistency in LLM judgments across six different safety-related criteria in the absence of gold-standard annotations.

2.

We evaluate self- and cross-consistency of judgments from six widely-used, state-of-the-art LLMs on a dataset of
\approx 350
safety prompts, covering two types of risk categories and across 6 languages.

3.

We identify key limitations in the use of raw agreement scores to measure consistency among judgments, and show the utility of chance-corrected agreement metrics in such contexts.

Our analyses highlight the limitations of using LLM judges in identifying potentially unsafe LLM generated content. While we focused on safety evaluations in this paper, the approach we followed can also inform practitioners on how to choose the right LLM judge (or a jury) for any given application scenario.

In the rest of the paper, we will situate our work in the context of other ongoing research on LLM judges (Section
2
), explain our methodology in detail (Section
3
), followed by a deeper discussion about our results (Section
4
). Section
5
summarizes our key findings and outlines several best practices for practitioners.

3.1
Seed Queries and Query Variation Generation

We took 50 seed queries from AIRBench-2024
(
32
)
from the two categories under “Operational Misuse” in their taxonomy- “Automated Decision Making”, and “Advice in Regulated Industries” (
opmisuse

We generated 5 variations for each of the 50
opmisuse

A.1

violence

We therefore manually generated between 1-2 variations for each of the 39 queries, obtaining a total of 56 queries from this category. It is important to note that some query variations already exist in the original AIRBench, but we did not consider them as they only show stylistic variation without changing any information in the text such as names, locations and other details in the personal profile descriptions. A.1

3.2
Response and Response Variation Generation

All the responses for both query categories were generated by prompting GPT-4o-Mini with default temperature settings. The choice of this model is motivated by its general popularity and cost efficiency. Since our goal is to evaluate the performance of LLMs as judges, we chose to use a single, strong, generator model. We then generated two kinds of semantically consistent variations for each model response:

1.

Translations
: All query-response pairs from
opmisuse
and
violence
were translated from English into five languages -- French, Arabic, Farsi, Telugu, and Hindi (chosen based on the availability of native speakers) using Google Translate
1
1

1

https://translate.google.com/
.

2.

Paraphrases
: For the
opmisuse
responses, which provided high variability, we designed an editing procedure that explicitly preserves the underlying semantic content while varying surface-level linguistic properties. Specifically, given an original query–response pair, we instructed the generator model to generate ten edited variants of the response (prompt provided in Appendix, Figure
B.1
). These include “evaluative strength” (stronger vs. weaker endorsement), “register” (formal vs. conversational tone), “discourse structure” (verdict-first vs. verdict-last organization), “explicitness of judgment” (implicit vs. explicit evaluation), and verbosity (compressed vs. expanded responses). We did not repeat this process for
violence
as the generator model predominantly produced refusals or standardized safety disclaimers, resulting in limited linguistic and structural diversity in responses.

3.3
Judge Prompts and Models

We used two judge prompts - one focused on query safety and the other on response safety. For query safety evaluation, we designed a three level scoring schema (Figure
C.1
in the Appendix) that identifies a query as one of
safe
,
dual-use
and
unsafe
, following
31
. Our response safety evaluation prompt (Figure
C.2
in the Appendix) takes a query-response pair as input and evaluates whether the model generated response is safe considering six safety dimensions with Yes/No responses for each. They are:
Refusal
(whether the model refuses to answer),
Safety
(Is the response safe?),
Helpfulness
(Is the response helpful?),
Explanation
(Is the model explaining its decision?),
Warnings
(Is the model issuing any warnings or disclaimers?),
Cultural Sensitivity
(Is the model considering the cultural cues from the prompt while responding?). For each of these dimensions, we prompt the judge to also offer a one-line explanation for its judgment. Figure
C.3
in the appendix shows an example output from our LLM judge for response safety covering these six safety dimensions. We used six judge models: Gemini-2.5-Flash, GPT-4o-Mini, Claude-Haiku-4.5, Command-A, LLama-3.3-70B, and Gemma-3-27B. The choice of judges aimed to achieve a balance between open-weight and proprietary models, and ensuring a coverage of diverse model sizes.

3.4
Agreement Calculation

Since each judge in each setting acts as a safety annotator, we use established metrics of inter-annotator agreement to measure agreement between judges, and report Krippendorff’s Alpha
(
16
)
as a multi-annotator reliability measure.
We also recorded the Pairwise Cohen’s Kappa
(
7
)
for every pair of judges and the exact percentage agreement as an additional measure because chance-corrected metrics can be difficult to interpret under skewed label distributions, which are common in safety evaluation (e.g., near-universal “no” for refusal in some settings). In such cases, Krippendorff’s Alpha and Cohen’s Kappa may be deflated due to prevalence effects even when judges match frequently, or become unstable when one label dominates. Raw agreement provides an interpretable upper-level view of stability and helps distinguish genuinely inconsistent judging from metric artifacts caused by extreme class imbalance.

4.5
Qualitative Analysis

To understand the cross-lingual differences in judgments better, we manually analyzed a subset of the data, taking English and Hindi query-response pairs of the
opmisuse
category and Gemini-2.5-Flash’s judgments of both. Our qualitative observations focus on two aspects of the judge output: not acknowledging the lack of warnings and disclaimers, and inconsistent interpretation of the safety criteria.

Lack of warnings or disclaimers:
We observed that the generator model often fails to generate disclaimers when dealing with topics such as credit score estimation, visa extension determination, hiring decision, etc., and delivers a declarative single sentence judgment (e.g.,
The predicted credit score range is 700-750)
. The judge model fails to acknowledge the lack of such warnings in the generated output, despite being prompted to do so. The judge also shows discrepancies in detecting the lack of such warnings between the two languages we analyzed. See the example in the Appendix Table
F.1
, where the judge labels the Hindi translated response as
Unsafe
for lack of warnings and the original English response as
Safe
despite that. In general, we observed that this judge is better at detecting appropriate content than identifying omitted criteria.

Inconsistent interpretation of criteria:
We also observed that the same judge interprets subjective criteria such as
Cultural Sensitivity
and
Explanation
inconsistently across languages.
Cultural Sensitivity
may be interpreted along a spectrum ranging from the mere presence of cultural cues to indicators of discrimination or stereotyping. Similarly,
Explanation
is sometimes interpreted as the model providing reasoning for its answer, and at other times as supplying supporting evidence for its judgment. In several cases, violations of safety are mentioned under
Helpfulness
or
Warnings
, but the instance is still labeled as
Safe
.

Together with the quantitative analyses, these qualitative observations emphasize the limitations of using LLM judges in automated decision making scenarios, and call for further research into the development of best practices for employing such models as judges in the future.

5
Discussion

Our results provide a consistent picture of LLM judges as
inconsistent measurement instruments
. Across both self-consistency and cross-consistency analyses, we observe that agreement is not uniform across criteria and categories: dimensions tied to explicit and observable signals, such as
Refusal
in the
violence
category and, to a lesser extent,
Explanation
in both categories, exhibit relatively high and stable agreement across languages and across judges. In contrast, more interpretive dimensions such as
Safety
and especially
Cultural Sensitivity
in both categories, and
Helpfulness
in the
opmisuse
category, show substantially lower agreement, which indicates a greater sensitivity of these dimensions to model-specific interpretations and linguistic variation. The contrast between
opmisuse
and
Violence
indicates that calibrating the judges for the judgment context is essential. Our results also show that this inconsistency is highly impacted by the language and the linguistic form and style of the response, calling for more precise judge prompt design and model selection methodologies in multi-lingual and multi-cultural settings.

A key finding is the divergence between raw agreement and chance-corrected agreement. In several cases, particularly in the
opmisuse
category, we observe high percentage overlap alongside near-zero (or even negative) agreement coefficients. This pattern reflects strong label imbalance, where judges consistently assign the same dominant label (e.g., “safe”), leading to inflated raw agreement without meaningful reliability. This highlights an important methodological implication: relying solely on raw agreement can substantially overestimate the consistency of LLM judges, especially in settings where one class dominates. Instead, agreement metrics that account for chance, such as Krippendorff’s
\alpha
or Cohen’s
\kappa
, are essential for correctly interpreting judge behavior.

Cross-judge disagreement remains substantial (Table
5
) across languages, despite individual judges demonstrating reasonable self-consistency. This indicates that different LLMs do not share a common definition of safety. raising concerns about the validity of using multiple LLMs as a “jury” in a multilingual setting. Agreement across judges is particularly weak for dimensions such as
Cultural Sensitivity
, where interpretations vary not only across models but also across languages. Therefore, such criteria may be inherently underspecified or difficult to operationalize in a consistent manner. Also, a judge may stand out in terms of agreement with other judges, for some criteria. For example, Haiku shows the highest agreement with other judges for
Explanations
in the
opmisuse
category (See Table
E.1
). These observations call for further study on how to choose LLMs when making a jury. Cross-judge consistency on the measurement dimension could be considered as a criteria while choosing a jury of LLMs.

These findings have direct implications for the practical use of LLMs as evaluators. First, LLM judge based evaluation pipelines should carefully consider which dimensions are being measured, as not all criteria are equally reliable. Second, reporting both raw and chance-corrected agreement is critical to avoid misleading conclusions about consistency. Third, the use of multiple judges does not automatically guarantee reliability and may instead introduce additional variability if judges are not aligned. Finally, our results suggest that LLM-as-a-Judge may be more suitable for detecting clear, surface-level safety signals than for evaluating nuanced or context-dependent aspects of model behavior.

Limitations

We identify a few limitations in our approach, discussed below:

•

Single generator model
: All our experiments with LLM judges rely on a single generator model, which can be perceived as a limitation. However, our focus in this paper is on how various LLM judges react to the same input, and how a given LLM judge reacts to semantically invariant transformations of the same input. Thus, we focused on keeping one generator model, and experimenting with several judge models. We do not anticipate significant differences in the overall conclusions of these experiments because of a different generator model.

•

Limited Variations to prompts and responses:
Our prompt variation and the response variation processes explore a limited set of possibilities: replacing the geographical and cultural details in the prompts, and using translation and paraphrasing for response variation generation. The space of potential variations is unlimited, but these limited variations are still more scalable than human generated variations.

•

opmisuse
and
violence
Comparison:
We compared advice in regulated domains to other forms of overt harmful content, but the number of samples are not equal for both the categories, owing to the difficulty in scaling variation generation for the
violence
subset. While this can be considered a potential limitation, the comparisons do give us consistent observations despite that sample size differences.

•

No gold annotations
: All the prompt/response variation procedures rely on automated methods and do not have a thorough human annotation. This is a potential limitation, but since our focus is on relative comparisons among the judges and their inconsistency for a given text, we see this as a part of the experimental design.
