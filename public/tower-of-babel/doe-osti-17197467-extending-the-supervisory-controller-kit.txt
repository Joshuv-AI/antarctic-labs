 ABSTRACT
Pacific Northwest and Oak Ridge National Laboratories jointly developed a supervisory controller kit for
small and medium-sized commercial buildings (SC-SMB), demonstrating 20%–25% electricity savings
at about $7,700 per 10,000-ft2 deployment. U.S. small and medium industrial (SMI) facilities show a
parallel control gap, with only 16% of motor systems under variable-frequency drive control and only
3% of refrigeration systems scheduling setpoints to production. That transfer has not been established,
since SMI facilities differ in protocols, process thermal loads, and multi-vendor equipment. This report
reviews 61 papers across six manufacturing industries, namely food, fabricated metal, plastics, machinery,
motor vehicle parts, and printing, and three end-uses, namely compressed air, process thermal, and HVAC,
supported by two interviews with a domain expert in industrial energy efficiency. Extension is technically
feasible, with most documented savings reachable through low-cost supervisory controls. Sensing is the
dominant capability gap, as instrumentation of major end-uses ranges from 3% to 26%. Compressed air
is the strongest retrofit candidate at 30%–50% savings, process thermal offers the largest potential but
requires substantial control-logic development, and HVAC requires the least modification. Case studies
of similarly sized plants report paybacks of 2.4 years or less, supporting full-scale development.

 1. INTRODUCTION
The U.S. industrial sector accounts for 35% of total U.S. primary energy consumption (U.S. Energy
Information Administration 2024). Counted at the firm level, roughly 98% of U.S. manufacturing firms
have fewer than 500 employees (National Association of Manufacturers 2022). Although each uses a
small amount of energy, these small and medium-sized industrial manufacturers collectively account
for up to 50% of U.S. industrial energy consumption (Levine et al. 2020). The Small and Medium
Industrial (SMI) buildings targeted here are bounded by establishment scale rather than subsector energy
intensity, including facilities across all manufacturing subsectors. Although SMI has no single agreed
federal definition, the Department of Energy (DOE) Industrial Assessment Center (IAC) defines SMI
operationally as facilities with fewer than 500 employees, less than $100 million in gross annual sales, and
annual energy bills between $100,000 and $2,500,000, typically with no in-house energy manager (DOE
Office of Energy Efficiency and Renewable Energy, Advanced Manufacturing Office 2019).
The major challenge is that SMI facilities exhibit significantly low adoption of supervisory control
infrastructure. The 2022 Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL) Motor System Market
Assessment found that only 16% of industrial motor systems are controlled by a variable-frequency drive
(VFD), only 18% of industrial fan and blower systems are on flow meters, and only 26% of industrial
pumping systems are on flow meters. Among industrial refrigeration systems, only 3% of facilities adjust
set points based on production schedules (Rao et al. 2022). The adoption gap is not merely technical.
A 2024 survey of 220 manufacturing small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) found that only 21%
employed a designated energy-responsible person and only 21% had conducted an external energy audit,
exposing the workforce and assessment deficits that underlie low control adoption (Dolšak, Hrovatin, and
Zorić 2024). The 2023 DOE Energy Management and Control Systems (EMCS) Roundtable concluded
that three decades of advanced building-controls demonstrations have consistently failed to translate into
broad adoption (DOE Building Technologies Office and ASHRAE 2023).
The Supervisory Controller kit for Small and Medium-Sized Commercial Buildings (SC-SMB) project,
a joint Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL)–Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) effort
funded by DOE’s Building Technologies Office (BTO), was launched to address the parallel adoption
gap in the small and medium-sized commercial buildings (SMCBs), where similarly only 8% of small

 1

commercial floorspace (under 50,000 ft2 ) has any building automation system (BAS), compared with 46%
of large commercial (Sofos et al. 2020). The SC-SMB kit demonstrates 20%–25% electricity savings at a
total reference deployment cost of approximately $7,700 for a 10,000-ft2 , six-rooftop-unit (RTU) building,
using BACnet thermostats, LoRaWAN sensors, a VOLTTRON edge device, and a model predictive control
(MPC)-based supervisory layer (Katipamula, Lutes, and Huang 2023). Additionally, field deployment
in a 1,035-m2 occupied office building in 2024 confirmed the architecture’s viability as a low-cost,
retrofit-ready supervisory control solution for SMCBs (M. Guo et al. 2025).
The same low-cost, retrofit-ready approach could be applicable to SMI facilities, but three obstacles
complicate the transfer. First, SMI facilities are largely uncontrolled. Most have no supervisory system
to monitor or manage their equipment, leaving significant energy savings and demand flexibility on the
table across large and diverse industries, including more than 38,000 food processing facilities employing
1.7M workers (Cresko, Rightor, et al. 2022), fabricated metal (IAC’s largest industry, 1,170 assessments),
and plastics/rubber (IAC’s third largest industry, 1,023 assessments) (DOE Office of Energy Efficiency
and Renewable Energy, Advanced Manufacturing Office 2019). Second, SMI facilities cannot pay for
conventional control solutions. Tight margins, 1- to 3-year payback requirements, and no in-house energy
staff rule out the costly custom integration that large industrials absorb, making a low-cost retrofit path
essential (DOE Office of Energy Efficiency and Renewable Energy, Advanced Manufacturing Office 2015;
Dolšak, Hrovatin, and Zorić 2024; Cresko et al. 2024). Third, little published evidence confirms whether
an affordable retrofit solution already exists for SMI, or whether SC-SMB’s field-validated architecture can
transfer to SMI facilities. To address these obstacles, this technical report conducts a literature review to
establish the technical basis and integration pathways for a full-scale SMI extension.

 2. APPROACH
As a preliminary study, this report aims to assess the feasibility of translating SC-SMB capabilities from
commercial buildings to SMI facilities through the following five steps. First, the study identifies which
facility types and industrial end-uses across the six target industries offer the strongest retrofit potential.
Second, it reviews the current state of automation and supervisory control in SMI facilities and analyzes
the barriers to adoption. Third, the study assesses whether SC-SMB’s architecture, sensing strategies,
hardware, and interoperability framework transfer to SMI facilities without modification. Fourth, it
identifies the capability gaps and the required modifications, data acquisition, and visualization. Fifth, it
compiles and estimates the potential energy savings and demand-flexibility of the three target end-uses,
drawing from the reviewed literature. Figure 1 summarizes these five steps and the sections that carry
them.

 Figure 1. Overview of the five-step feasibility assessment, from the reviewed literature through the
five analytical steps to the feasibility assessment and future directions for a full-scale SMI extension.

Beyond the literature review, the approach incorporates two interviews with a domain expert in industrial
energy efficiency. The domain expert is a registered professional engineer and certified energy manager
whose facility assessment experience spans compressed-air systems, HVAC, and energy performance

 3

measurement and verification. The first interview, at the start of the project, provided practical insight
into how SMI facilities operate and what they need, including the constraint that critical equipment be
monitored rather than controlled. The second interview, after the literature review, confirmed and refined
the industry and end-use scope and the barrier analysis, and verified that the proposed future directions are
aligned with real-world retrofit needs and cost constraints.

2.1 COLLECTED LITERATURE COMPOSITION
To establish documented bases from the relevant published literature, the project team conducted a
literature search prioritizing DOE federal-grade technical reports—DOE’s Office of Scientific and
Technical Information (OSTI), the Office of Energy Efficiency and Renewable Energy (EERE), DOE
program offices, the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA), ORNL/LBNL/PNNL/National
Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) national laboratories, and standards bodies—and peer-reviewed journals.
Sixty-one papers were collected and categorized by target industry and target end-use. Four statistical data
sources cited for individual figures fall outside this corpus, bringing the full reference list to 65 entries.
The collected literature comprises 29 federal and national-laboratory technical reports (48%), 27
peer-reviewed journal articles (44%), 3 conference papers (5%), and 2 DOE sourcebooks (3%). Technical
reports are anchored by DOE/EERE program offices and ORNL/Better Plants/NLR/BTO publications.
Journal articles concentrate in Elsevier outlets (Applied Energy, Energy and Buildings, and Energy
Reports) and Multidisciplinary Digital Publishing Institute’s (MDPI’s) Energies. Publication years are
weighted toward recent work, with 44 of 61 papers (72%) appearing in 2020 or later, with 2022 the
single most-represented year (11 papers). The remaining 17 papers (1997–2019) anchor compressed-air
sourcebook practice, IAC assessment methodology, and ISO 50001 implementation. Topical coverage
spans industry characterization, end-use technology assessments (compressed air, process thermal, HVAC,
motor systems), SMI energy-management and demand-response practices, industrial sensing and controls,
and SMI retrofit feasibility.

2.2 SIX TARGET MANUFACTURING INDUSTRIES
The six target industries were selected for their concentration of SMI facilities and SC-SMB retrofit
relevance. Figure 4 presents the six target industries, each defined by a U.S. Census North American
Industry Classification System (NAICS) code (full mapping in Appendix A), which classifies plants
according to what they produce (Wachs et al. 2022).
Food Manufacturing (NAICS 311) is the largest SMI industry by establishment count, with more than
38,000 facilities employing 1.7M workers, accounting for 9.5% of U.S. manufacturing primary energy
(1,827 TBtu primary; 1,235 TBtu on-site) (Dollinger et al. 2017; Cresko, Rightor, et al. 2022). The
industry is highly heterogeneous, ranging from family-run operations to capital-intensive processors, with
the SMI fraction concentrated in regional and specialty industries (Cresko, Rightor, et al. 2022). Process
thermal, refrigeration, motor drives, and HVAC dominate end-use energy, aligning the industry with all
three SC-SMB target end-uses (Clairand et al. 2020). The industry encompasses meat processing, dairy,
beverages, baked goods, prepared foods, sugar, oil, and grain milling.
Motor Vehicle Parts Manufacturing (NAICS 3363) is the uniquely diversified SMI archetype and the major
industry that exercises all SC-SMB capability areas (noncritical equipment control, data acquisition,
and visualization) within a single facility. Whole-plant electricity spans paint shop (27%–50%), HVAC
(11%–20%), compressed air (9%–14%), welding (9%–11%), and materials handling (7%–8%) (Giampieri
et al. 2020). The industry encompasses direct OEM suppliers and subsuppliers, stamping, machining,
injection molding, casting, paint, and subassembly.

Fabricated Metal Product Manufacturing (NAICS 332) has the largest population of DOE-engaged SMI
facilities (1,170 IAC assessments). On-site energy is led by process heating (38%), machine drive (22%),
and facility HVAC (22%). Within the machine drive category, fans (5.1%) and compressed air (4.7%) are
the largest subuses relevant to SC-SMB target end-uses (DOE Advanced Manufacturing Office 2018a;
DOE Office of Energy Efficiency and Renewable Energy, Advanced Manufacturing Office 2019). The
industry encompasses forging, stamping, cutting, machining, coating, and structural metal fabrication.
Plastics and Rubber Products Manufacturing (NAICS 326) has the third-largest population of
DOE-engaged SMI facilities (1,023 IAC assessments) (DOE Office of Energy Efficiency and Renewable
Energy, Advanced Manufacturing Office 2019). Extruders and injection molders run continuously with
heated barrels (process thermal) and large drive motors (motor systems), making the industry the most
representative continuous-process SMI case. The industry encompasses injection molding, extrusion, blow
molding, foam, and tire and rubber processing.
Machinery Manufacturing (NAICS 333) is machine-drive-dominant (33% of on-site energy, among the
highest shares of the six targets), distinguishing it from the heat-dominated fabricated-metal and food
industries and providing the project’s primary motor-systems retrofit case (DOE Advanced Manufacturing
Office 2018b). The industry also includes HVAC equipment manufacturers, offering a direct test case
of SC-SMB transferability. The industry encompasses industrial machinery, agricultural equipment,
construction equipment, HVAC equipment, and engines and turbines.
Printing and Related Support Activities (NAICS 323) is included for industry-coverage breadth, with fewer
corpus documents (four papers) than the other target industries. Its energy use is motor-drive-dominant,
led by presses and finishing equipment, with low-temperature process thermal for drying ovens and UV
cure (U.S. Energy Information Administration 2022). The industry encompasses commercial printing,
support activities, books, labels, and packaging printing.

2.3 THREE TARGET END-USES
The three target end-uses were selected for their cross-cutting prevalence across all six target industries and
direct alignment with SC-SMB’s major capability areas (noncritical equipment control, data acquisition,
and visualization). Figure 5 provides an overview of the three target end-uses.

 Figure 5. Overview of the three target end-uses.

Compressed Air is a near-universal SMI utility, present across all six target industries, and often the single
largest electricity end-use after lighting. Compressed air set pressure is often higher than needed, and best
practice recommends setting it about 10% above the highest-pressure end-use (Rao et al. 2022). Leak
losses alone account for 20%–30% of compressor output (DOE Advanced Manufacturing Office and
Compressed Air Challenge 2016). Documented efficiency-improvement potential of 30%–50% makes
compressed air the highest-leverage SC-SMB target end-use. The category covers air compressors (rotary
screw, reciprocating, centrifugal), receivers, dryers, filters, distribution piping, and end-use applications
(pneumatic tools, actuators, blowing, material transport).
Process Thermal is the dominant on-site end-use across food and fabricated metal (38% of on-site energy
in metal fabrication alone) and the second-largest in plastics after machine drive. It carries the largest
demand-flexibility potential in SMI (DOE Advanced Manufacturing Office 2018a, 2018c; Cresko, Thekdi,
et al. 2022). Programmed temperature profiles deliver 5%–10% savings. Process integration via pinch
analysis can reduce fossil-fuel process heating by 23%–75% (DOE Advanced Manufacturing Office and
Industrial Heating Equipment Association 2016; Cresko et al. 2024). Process thermal systems are typically
uninstrumented beyond basic loop control in SMI, leaving substantial efficiency and load-shifting potential
unrealized. The category encompasses boilers and steam systems, process heating (ovens, furnaces, dryers,
kilns), process cooling and refrigeration, and waste-heat recovery.
HVAC is among the least instrumented industrial utility loads, and ventilation is its least controlled
component. Only 18% of fan and blower systems are on a flow meter, despite these systems consuming
21% of industrial motor-system energy (Rao et al. 2022). Facility HVAC is itself a major on-site end-use
across the target industries, at 22% of on-site energy in fabricated metal and 23% of fuel and electricity
use in printing (DOE Advanced Manufacturing Office 2018a; U.S. Energy Information Administration
2022), and it accounts for 49% of nonprocess industrial energy and 8.6% of industrial electricity
overall (Patterson, Singh, and Cho 2022). Scheduled run/idle, demand-controlled ventilation, and
contaminant-driven VFD logic map directly onto SC-SMB’s existing supervisory-control architecture,
making HVAC the SMI extension target that requires the fewest modifications. The category encompasses
fan systems for combustion air supply, process exhaust (paint shops, welding fumes, kitchen hoods, drying
ovens), indoor air handling, and the space heating, cooling, and dehumidification that condition occupied
areas.

 3. REPRESENTATIVE INDUSTRIAL DOMAINS
Building on the six target industries introduced in Section 2, this section provides per-industry
characterizations across five structural elements: population and scale, energy profile, SMI profile,
existing controllers, and SC-SMB target end-use alignment.

3.1 FOOD MANUFACTURING
Population and scale. The industry includes more than 38,000 food and beverage manufacturing facilities
in the U.S., employing 1.7 million workers as of 2019 (Cresko, Rightor, et al. 2022). More recent data
from the 2024 DOE Industrial Decarbonization Pathways analysis put the figure at more than 41,000
facilities by 2021 (Cresko et al. 2024), reflecting modest growth. The industry accounts for 14.7% of all
U.S. manufacturing employees and is the largest manufacturing employer among the six target industries.
Energy use. The U.S. food and beverage industry consumed 1,235 TBtu on-site (1,827 TBtu primary)
in 2010, equal to 9.5% of total U.S. manufacturing primary energy consumption (Dollinger et al. 2017).
End-use is heat- and refrigeration-led, with energy consumption mostly comprising process heat,
refrigeration, motor drives, and HVAC (Clairand et al. 2020). Within process thermal specifically, the
industry spans high-temperature operations (frying, roasting, sterilization at >100°C) and low-temperature
operations (refrigeration, freezing, fermentation control).
SMI characteristics. Food manufacturing sites vary from small family-run operations that are highly
labor-intensive to larger capital-intensive and mechanized industrial processes (Cresko, Rightor,
et al. 2022). The SMI fraction is concentrated in regional and specialty processors, independent meat
plants, regional dairies, craft beverage producers, contract food packers, and specialty baked-goods
producers. These facilities typically operate one or a few production lines, have limited engineering
staff, and run on margins thinner than large multinationals, making them well matched to SC-SMB’s
cost-sensitive retrofit target.
Existing controllers. Typical SMI food plants operate with distributed equipment controllers but no
plant-wide energy supervisory layer. Programmable logic controllers (PLCs) on production lines
manage process logic. Refrigeration packages have their own embedded controllers. Ovens, fryers,
and pasteurizers ship with manufacturer-provided burner controls, and HVAC operates on stand-alone
thermostats. Plant-wide visibility into energy consumption is rare. Aggregated monthly utility bills are
typically the only cross-equipment view available to operators.
SC-SMB target end-use alignment. Food manufacturing facilities exercise all three of SC-SMB’s
prospective end-uses at significant scale: compressed air for pneumatic conveyors, packaging machinery,
and process actuation. Process thermal dominates across cooking, sterilization, refrigeration, and
clean-in-place hot water. HVAC serves kitchen hoods, drying ovens, sanitation exhaust, and ammonia
refrigeration room ventilation. Among these end-uses, process thermal and refrigeration represent the
largest energy footprint and the most heterogeneous control surface (different equipment vendors, different
protocols), making this industry a stress test for SC-SMB’s interoperability claim.

3.2 MOTOR VEHICLE PARTS MANUFACTURING
Population and scale. The supplier base is divided into direct OEM suppliers (those shipping to assembly
OEMs), typically larger and multinational, and subsuppliers (those shipping components to direct OEM
suppliers), spanning a wide size range with substantial mid-scale presence. SMI auto parts plants are small
and mid-sized operations below the IAC’s 500-employee threshold (DOE Office of Energy Efficiency and

 8

Renewable Energy, Advanced Manufacturing Office 2019), and the industry’s employment is concentrated
in the industrial Midwest, led by Michigan, Ohio, and Indiana, and secondarily in the South, notably
Kentucky, Tennessee, and Alabama (Harp and Prasad 2024).
Energy use. Whole-plant electricity use in auto parts manufacturing is distributed across a uniquely
diversified set of end-uses: paint shop (27%–50%), HVAC (11%–20%), lighting (14%–15%), compressed
air (9%–14%), welding (9%–11%), and materials handling/tools (7%–8%) (Giampieri et al. 2020). No
single end-use dominates above 50%, and five distinct end-uses each account for 9% or more of plant
electricity. The diversity reflects the multistage production process (forming, joining, finishing) housed
within a single facility.
SMI characteristics. This industry is concentrated in direct OEM suppliers and subsuppliers, independent
component manufacturers under contract to one or more OEMs, typically small and mid-sized plants that
lack significant in-house energy-engineering capacity (DOE Office of Energy Efficiency and Renewable
Energy, Advanced Manufacturing Office 2019). As is typical of manufacturing SMEs, these suppliers
are highly cost-sensitive, with limited financial resources and high investment costs ranking as the most
strongly perceived barriers to energy-efficiency investment (Dolšak, Hrovatin, and Zorić 2024). These
conditions favor affordable, practical retrofit solutions over feature-rich, premium ones.
Existing controllers. Typical auto parts SMI plants operate per-cell PLC control on production equipment
(stamping presses, machining cells, weld stations), paint-shop supervisory control and data acquisition
(SCADA) for paint-booth and oven control (often with stricter regulatory monitoring than other production
areas because of emissions compliance requirements), welding controllers per weld cell, and stand-alone
HVAC thermostats. No plant-wide energy supervisory layer ties these systems together (Akhtar
et al. 2020).
SC-SMB target end-use alignment. A single auto parts plant exercises all three end-uses simultaneously.
Compressed air is used for pneumatic tools, actuators, and material transport (9%–14% of plant
electricity). Process thermal serves paint-cure ovens, weld preheat, and parts washing. HVAC supports
paint-booth exhaust, weld-fume capture, and conditioned supply air to assembly areas. No other target
industry packages all three into one plant, making auto parts a uniquely tight single-plant test case for
SC-SMB transferability.

3.3 FABRICATED METAL PRODUCT MANUFACTURING
Population and scale. Fabricated metal is DOE IAC’s largest industry by assessment count (1,170
assessments) (DOE Office of Energy Efficiency and Renewable Energy, Advanced Manufacturing Office
2019). IAC targets facilities with fewer than 500 employees and annual energy bills of $100,000 to
$2.5 million. This translates into the largest known population of DOE-engaged SMI facilities. The
industry is characterized by a high density of small job shops alongside mid-sized captive fabricators.
In Pennsylvania, for example, 64% of fabricated-metal establishments employ fewer than 10 workers
and roughly 92% employ fewer than 50, whereas plants of 100 to 249 employees account for the largest
single share of industry employment (Delaware Valley Industrial Resource Center and Pennsylvania
Manufacturing Extension Partnership 2024). Geographic distribution follows broader U.S. manufacturing
patterns. The six leading states—Ohio, Texas, California, Illinois, Michigan, and Pennsylvania—together
account for roughly one-third of national industry revenue, spanning the industrial Midwest, the South, the
West Coast, and the Northeast rather than concentrating in any single region (Delaware Valley Industrial
Resource Center and Pennsylvania Manufacturing Extension Partnership 2024).
Energy use. Fabricated metal primary energy totals 479 TBtu, with on-site energy use led by process
heating (38% of on-site energy), by far the dominant end-use, followed by machine drive (22%) and

 9

facility HVAC (22%) (DOE Advanced Manufacturing Office 2018a). Within the machine drive category,
materials processing is the largest subuse (7.5%), followed by fans (5.1%) and compressed air (4.7%),
the two most relevant to SC-SMB target end-uses. This breakdown makes fabricated metal the most
process-thermal-dominant target industry, with process heat use (96 TBtu) exceeding machine drive (55
TBtu), and aligns the industry with all three SC-SMB targets.
SMI characteristics. This industry is concentrated in job shops and mid-volume contract
fabricators—independent operations producing custom or short-run metal parts on a job or order basis
(U.S. Census Bureau 2022). In the case of Pennsylvania, machine shops and turned-product manufacturers
form the largest segment at 1,467 of the more than 4,000 metal fabrication facilities (Delaware Valley
Industrial Resource Center and Pennsylvania Manufacturing Extension Partnership 2024). These types
of facilities are cost-sensitive (margins are constrained by larger competitors and overseas alternatives),
have minimal in-house engineering capacity (DOE Office of Energy Efficiency and Renewable Energy,
Advanced Manufacturing Office 2019), and operate equipment from many different vendors purchased
opportunistically over years or decades.
Existing controllers. Typical SMI fabricated metal plants use computer numerical control (CNC) systems
on machine tools, stand-alone furnace and oven controllers for heat treating and stress relief, dust-collector
cycling controllers, and individual HVAC thermostats. Each machine has its own embedded controller and
protocol, and the resulting lack of standardization compounds the integration problem (Tapia et al. 2023).
SC-SMB target end-use alignment. All three end-uses are present. Compressed air is used for pneumatic
tools, blow-off cleaning, and material handling (12 TBtu). Process thermal dominates heat treating,
annealing, stress relief, surface coating cure, and parts washing (96 TBtu, the largest single end-use).
HVAC covers welding-fume capture, dust collection, and oven exhaust (fans account for 13 TBtu),
alongside facility conditioning at 22% of on-site energy. Process thermal is the highest-leverage end-use.
Ventilation is operationally critical for regulatory compliance (welding fumes, hexavalent chromium, dust).

3.4 PLASTICS AND RUBBER PRODUCTS MANUFACTURING
Population and scale. Plastics and rubber manufacturing is DOE IAC’s third-largest industry by
assessment count (1,023 assessments) (DOE Office of Energy Efficiency and Renewable Energy, Advanced
Manufacturing Office 2019). The population ranges from small custom molders (short-run specialty parts)
to larger captive producers (continuous extrusion or tire operations), all below the IAC 500-employee
threshold. The industry’s auto-supplier segment overlaps with the motor-vehicle-parts footprint because
plastics and rubber are major auto parts inputs.
Energy use. Plastics and rubber products (NAICS 326) consumed 562 TBtu of primary energy and 256
TBtu of on-site energy in 2018 (DOE Advanced Manufacturing Office 2018c). On-site energy is led by
machine drive at 89 TBtu (35% of on-site energy), the dominant end-use, reflecting the continuous-process
motors of extruder screws, injection-machine hydraulics, and materials-processing equipment, of
which materials processing alone accounts for 49 TBtu. Process heating follows at 64 TBtu (25% of
on-site energy), primarily from heated barrels in injection molding and extrusion. Process cooling and
refrigeration account for another 14 TBtu (chilled water for mold cooling). Within the machine drive
category, fans (22 TBtu) and compressed air (8 TBtu) are the largest cross-cutting subuses. Tire production
additionally requires high-temperature curing and substantial compressed-air use for green-tire handling
and curing presses.
SMI characteristics. This industry is concentrated in small and mid-sized custom molders and
in-house producers, a population documented in DOE Industrial Assessment Center site visits such
as a plastics-extrusion plant cooling six extrusion lines from a single central chiller (Errigo, Choi, and

 10

Kissock 2022). Production equipment runs continuously for days between shutdowns rather than in
discrete batches, and machine drive is correspondingly the dominant on-site end use at 35%, led by
materials-processing motors at 49 TBtu (DOE Advanced Manufacturing Office 2018c). Continuous
operation does not mean full loading because around-the-clock equipment can idle for most of the weekend
while still drawing about 80% of full-load power, which VFD control cut to about 15% in one assessed
plant (Errigo, Choi, and Kissock 2022). For SC-SMB, the opportunity therefore lies in detecting and
reducing idle-state draw through speed modulation and set point optimization rather than in simple
shutdown scheduling.
Existing controllers. Typical SMI plastics plants use per-machine barrel-temperature controllers
(multizone proportional-integral-derivative, or PID, loops for heat-up, melt, and cooling), hydraulic
pressure controllers for injection, chilled-water controllers for mold cooling, and stand-alone HVAC
controls. Like other SMI industries, plant-wide energy supervisory visibility is rare, with controllers
operating independently with limited cross-equipment coordination.
SC-SMB target end-use alignment. Two end-uses are dominant. Compressed air is used for material
handling, mold clamping, blow molding, and tire-curing presses. Process thermal serves heated barrels,
cure ovens, and chilled-water mold cooling (also a process-cooling load). Given the lower need for
contaminant exhaust in plastics and rubber manufacturing (though rubber vulcanization requires
ventilation for sulfur compounds), HVAC is less prominent in this sector than in fabricated metal or auto
parts. Compressed air and process thermal are the highest-leverage SC-SMB targets for this industry.

3.5 MACHINERY MANUFACTURING
Population and scale. Establishment count is comparable to that of fabricated metal but smaller than those
of food or auto parts. The industry is dominated by small and mid-sized machine builders, independent
producers of specialized equipment for specific end-user industries such as food processing, packaging,
and HVAC equipment (U.S. Census Bureau 2022), operating as engineering-design-and-build shops rather
than high-volume manufacturers and combining component machining with final equipment assembly and
testing.
Energy use. Machinery primary energy totals 299 TBtu, with on-site end-use led by machine drive
(33% of on-site energy). This is among the highest machine drive shares of the six target industries,
distinguishing machinery from the heat-dominated fabricated metal and food industries. Facility
HVAC follows at 30%, followed by process heating (18%) and facility lighting (7%) (DOE Advanced
Manufacturing Office 2018b). The high HVAC share reflects the fact that machinery production is
typically less energy-intensive, leaving facility conditioning a larger share of facility energy than in
metal-forming or thermal-processing industries.
SMI characteristics. Because energy is correspondingly a small share of operating cost, facility managers
rarely prioritize energy projects, and the usual obstacles of limited capital and engineering expertise
weigh more heavily than at plants where energy is a major cost. However, a heavy-machinery case study
identified energy-cost reductions of 16%, representing substantial unrealized potential (Ketenci and Wolf
2024).
Existing controllers. Typical SMI machinery plants use CNC controllers on machine tools (similar to
metal fabrication), assembly cell PLCs for assembly stations, engine and equipment test-cell controllers
for end-of-line product testing, and stand-alone HVAC controls. Plant-wide energy supervisory visibility is
rare, and test cells in particular can drive significant uncoordinated demand spikes.
SC-SMB target end-use alignment. Compressed air (pneumatic tools, assembly fixtures) and HVAC
(welding fumes, dust collection from machining, and assembly-area conditioning) are the most directly

 11

relevant end-uses. Process thermal is less dominant here, primarily used for heat-treating of machined
components, oven cure for paint, and test-cell heat rejection. The high machine drive share points to
motor-system control as the primary SC-SMB opportunity, reachable via VFD set point control.

3.6 PRINTING AND RELATED SUPPORT ACTIVITIES
Population and scale. Indirect evidence suggests an SMI-dominated structure but does not measure it
directly. Wisconsin alone hosts 7,070 printing and publishing facilities, second only to machinery (Alkadi,
Starke, and Ma 2013), and the industry ranks fourth by compressed-air distributor customer mentions at
37% (XENERGY, Inc. 2014).
Energy use. Printing consumed 53 TBtu of fuel and net electricity in 2022, led by machine drive at 19
TBtu (36% of the total), reflecting offset presses, paper feed and delivery, and finishing equipment (U.S.
Energy Information Administration 2022). Facility HVAC follows at 12 TBtu (23%), process heating at
7 TBtu (13%), predominantly from natural gas for ink and varnish drying, and facility lighting at 4 TBtu
(8%).
SMI characteristics. The industry is dominated by commercial print shops producing newspapers,
books, labels, and business forms, alongside screen and apparel printers and support firms performing
platemaking and bookbinding (U.S. Census Bureau 2022). These shops print to customer order rather
than publishing their own material. These businesses are cost-sensitive and operate within thin margins,
particularly given the long-term contraction in demand for printed material as digital media displaces print.
Existing controllers. Typical SMI printing plants use per-press controllers (registration, ink density, paper
feed), drying-oven controllers (temperature and conveyor speed), and stand-alone HVAC controls. A
plant-wide energy supervisory layer is typically absent.
SC-SMB target end-use alignment. All three target end-uses are present: compressed air for pneumatic
feed and delivery, process thermal for drying and UV-cure ovens, and HVAC for solvent exhaust (in
solvent-based ink operations), ozone exhaust (in UV-cure operations), and facility conditioning at 23%
of fuel and electricity use. Because machine drive is the largest end-use, the greatest savings potential lies
in controlling press and finishing-line motors, followed by scheduling drying ovens to match production.

 4. SMI INFRASTRUCTURE GAPS AND SC-SMB CAPABILITY EXTENSIONS
This section maps SMI control-infrastructure gaps to the SC-SMB capability extensions required to
address the gaps. The analysis establishes what is missing in SMI facilities and why, provides an overview
of the SC-SMB commercial baseline, and defines what SC-SMB must add to extend its application from
commercial to SMI facilities across control logic, sensing, protocol bridging, and visualization.

4.1 SMI CONTROL-INFRASTRUCTURE GAPS AND ADOPTION BARRIERS
Typical SMI facilities operate with distributed per-equipment controllers but no plant-wide energy
supervisory layer. Production equipment is controlled by embedded PLCs and manufacturer-supplied
panels; machine tools use CNC controllers; larger direct OEM suppliers use paint-shop SCADA;
refrigeration packages have embedded controllers; ovens and furnaces have manufacturer-provided burner
controls; and facility HVAC uses stand-alone thermostats. These per-equipment controllers manage
process logic locally and effectively, but they do not share data, coordinate across equipment classes, or
report energy consumption data to plant operators. The aggregated monthly utility bill is typically the only
cross-equipment energy view available (Ketenci and Wolf 2024; Akhtar et al. 2020; Jagtap, Garcia-Garcia,
and Rahimifard 2021). The same pattern was observed during the 2020 production slowdowns, when
facility energy intensity rose because compressed air, HVAC, and combustion systems lack adequate
turndown, and most plants could not remotely monitor or adjust equipment (W. Guo et al. 2020). The 2022
LBNL Motor System Market Assessment quantifies the gap class by class (Rao et al. 2022): only 16% of
industrial motor systems are under VFD control, leaving 84% without VFD-based supervisory modulation.
Only 18% of industrial fans and 26% of industrial pumps are equipped with flow meters. Among industrial
refrigeration systems, only 3% of facilities adjust set points based on production schedules, although 40%
report having a control strategy for matching supply with demand. The gaps are broad across equipment
classes rather than concentrated in a single type, and therefore any extension scope must span sensing,
scheduling, and control simultaneously.
These aggregate gaps present differently across the six target industries. Food plants run on static
refrigeration set points, and the fan flow-meter gap affects drying-oven exhaust and sanitation ventilation
(Clairand et al. 2020). Motor-vehicle-parts plants have uncoordinated compressed-air, ventilation, and
HVAC scheduling alongside fixed-temperature paint-cure ovens and continuous weld-cell ventilation
(Giampieri et al. 2020; Akhtar et al. 2020). Fabricated metal plants run fixed-cycle heat-treating ovens
with a particularly acute compressed-air controller gap given heavy pneumatic-tool use (Rao et al. 2022).
Plastics and rubber operations rely on per-machine barrel-temperature controllers with no cross-machine
optimization (Errigo, Choi, and Kissock 2022). Machinery facilities exhibit uncoordinated test-cell
demand spikes alongside assembly-area ventilation and compressed-air scheduling, and a heavy-machinery
case study found that the plant lacked energy-specific data and any breakdown of its energy consumers
and consumption patterns (Ketenci and Wolf 2024). Printing plants run per-press controllers with no
plant-level energy view, and a case study of packaging and label printing identified long machine idle time
and poor job assignment as the main contributors to energy consumption (Abusaq et al. 2023). Across all
six target industries, the gap pattern is structural. It varies by equipment class, not by industry.
Closing these gaps is not principally a technology challenge because control and monitoring technologies
have been commercially mature for decades. The review of the collected literature reveals that the
persistent adoption lag stems from four types of major barriers: financial, workforce, interoperability, and
behavioral. DOE’s National Smart Manufacturing Strategic Plan reaches the same grouping for small and
medium manufacturers, naming workforce, software-hardware integration, cost, and the technical difficulty

 13

of adding sensors and communications to existing assets (DOE Office of Energy Efficiency and Renewable
Energy 2022).
Financial Barrier: Across barrier surveys of SMI manufacturers and SMEs, financial constraints are the
most consistently identified. Tight operating margins and a typical 1- to 3-year payback requirement for
energy-efficiency investments leave little headroom for the custom-integration costs that larger industrial
manufacturers routinely accept (DOE Office of Energy Efficiency and Renewable Energy, Advanced
Manufacturing Office 2015). In a 220-firm survey of manufacturing SMEs, limited financial resources and
high investment costs were the two highest-ranked perceived barriers, both with mean scores above 3.2 out
of 4 (Dolšak, Hrovatin, and Zorić 2024).
Workforce Barrier: Workforce and energy-knowledge gaps structurally constrain adoption. Only 21% of
SMI manufacturers have a designated energy-responsible staff, only 21% have ever conducted an external
energy audit, and only 16% of respondents know the wholesale electricity price (Dolšak, Hrovatin, and
Zorić 2024). The DOE IAC program codified this absence by including no professional in-house staff
to perform the assessment in its eligibility criteria (DOE Office of Energy Efficiency and Renewable
Energy, Advanced Manufacturing Office 2019), effectively making it a defining characteristic of the SMI
population. Any solution requiring engineering judgment to deploy or operate therefore exceeds SMI staff
capabilities.
Interoperability Barrier: Interoperability gaps compound the workforce deficit. SMI plants operate
equipment from numerous vendors with no plant-wide protocol bridge, and the DOE Barriers to Industrial
Energy Efficiency report names this vendor fragmentation as a structural impediment to demand-response
programs, which depend on seamless multivendor communication (DOE Office of Energy Efficiency and
Renewable Energy, Advanced Manufacturing Office 2015). Open standards, including OpenADR, Smart
Energy Profile (SEP), and Green Button could close this gap, but more widespread use of these standards
is still needed (DOE Office of Energy Efficiency and Renewable Energy, Advanced Manufacturing Office
2015; Dean and Dice 2021). Therefore, any extension must bridge vendor-specific protocols rather than
presume a uniform ecosystem.
Behavioral Barrier: Even where efficiency opportunities are technically accessible, behavioral and
organizational priorities result in low rates of efficiency engagement. Production continuity, product
quality, and equipment reliability dominate operator attention. Only 17% of compressed-air customers had
conducted a system audit in the prior seven years, only 9% identified energy-cost control as their primary
objective in compressed-air system management, and only 17% mentioned efficiency in their responses
(XENERGY, Inc. 2014).

4.2 OVERVIEW OF SC-SMB CAPABILITIES
The SC-SMB kit provides three major capabilities coordinated through Eclipse VOLTTRON, an
open-source distributed agent-based platform (Katipamula, Lutes, and Huang 2023). The first capability
is supervisory control, which enables the kit to retrofit and control existing equipment with commercially
available control devices, such as RTUs through smart thermostats, hot water heaters through a CTA-2045
interface, and connected lighting through vendor application programming interfaces (APIs). The
second is scalable data acquisition, through which the platform captures streams of real-time data,
including operating state from RTU thermostats, indoor environmental conditions from low-cost IoT
sensors, and whole-building energy consumption from wireless power meters. The third is open-protocol
interoperability, where VOLTTRON natively supports industry-standard BAS and IoT communication
protocols (BACnet, Modbus, LoRaWAN) and proprietary APIs within a single platform, enabling
integration of additional equipment such as solar photovoltaic (PV) or battery storage without custom
engineering.

 14

Field testing confirmed that the technical feasibility and effectiveness of the supervisory controller kit
in a 1,035-m2 office building, where 49 Ecobee smart thermostats (via the manufacturer’s API) and
2 Schneider power meters (via Modbus) connected RTUs, packaged terminal air conditioners, and
whole-building electricity to a local computer running advanced supervisory control strategies (M. Guo
et al. 2025). The kit enabled HVAC energy savings of 20%–50% with MPC and up to 90% with rule-based
setback control during unoccupied hours. Deployment cost reached $56/m2 ($28,000 hardware and up to
$30,000 labor costs across 260 labor-hours), yielding a simple payback of around 17 years at 10% annual
energy savings. An earlier ORNL prototype had already shown that a low-cost retrofit kit was viable at this
scale, cutting peak demand by 50% without materially changing total energy use, although its scope was
limited to peak-demand reduction rather than full supervisory control (Nutaro et al. 2015). These results
establish the SC-SMB kit as a validated baseline for extension to SMI facilities.

4.3 MAPPING SC-SMB CAPABILITIES TO SMI SUPERVISORY GAPS
This subsection maps SC-SMB’s capabilities to the SMI control-infrastructure gaps, covering end-use
controls, sensors, and communication interoperability. Extending SC-SMB to SMI facilities would
provide a means of addressing these gaps by integrating new control logic for each SMI end-use. The
biggest opportunity is compressed air, which has the lowest supervisory control adoption among the
end-uses and offers five potential extensions for addressing the gaps. The problem is usually control, not
hardware. At one large mill, seven uncoordinated compressors blew off excess air continuously until a
supervisory controller sequenced them on measured demand (DOE Industrial Technologies Program
2004). The SC-SMB could automatically coordinate multiple compressors so that the most efficient
unit handles each load level, regulate system pressure to match the system’s storage capacity (Botts and
Wenning 2025), reduce pressure on schedule during off-hours and idle periods, watch for pressure drops
that signal leaks, and track compressor operating cycles continuously to support diagnostics (Benton
et al. 2021). Process thermal equipment, such as batch ovens, furnaces, and process refrigeration, would
benefit from three further extensions. The system could run scheduled temperature profiles tailored to
each batch, automatically shut down equipment when upstream production goes idle, and automatically
adjust refrigeration set points within manufacturer-specified ranges. Finally, HVAC offers three more
opportunities. The system could schedule ventilation to match production cycles, switch it on only when
the equipment it serves is active, and modulate fan speed in real time based on air-quality sensors.
SC-SMB’s data pipeline would also extend to SMI, with industrial-specific additions. The sensor
layer is the biggest gap because the baseline is sized for HVAC and water-heater scope. New industrial
sensors could measure compressed-air, pump, and fan flow rates, monitor compressed-air moisture,
detect combustible gases for ventilation safety, track steam through boilers and distribution lines, meter
per-machine electricity use, and monitor stack temperatures for combustion efficiency (Qin and McKane
2007). The communication layer already supports standard protocols used in commercial buildings (e.g.,
Modbus and BACnet), and two further bridges would extend it. The first would support the industrial
communication protocols common in SMI facilities, such as EtherNet/IP, Open Platform Communications
Unified Architecture (OPC UA), and Profibus, which connect equipment such as PLCs, plant-level
SCADA systems, and stand-alone controllers for machine tools, ovens, and refrigeration units (Alarcón,
Martínez-García, and Gómez de León Hijes 2021; Stouffer et al. 2023; Tapia et al. 2023). The second
would add open-standard support for utility-side demand-response programs through protocols such as
OpenADR, SEP, and Green Button (DOE Office of Energy Efficiency and Renewable Energy, Advanced
Manufacturing Office 2015).
Beyond the technical capability gaps, the SC-SMB extension would also mitigate financial and workforce
barriers. For SMI deployment, the kit’s low-cost design is the central financial benefit. PNNL’s reference
design totals just $7,700 for a 10,000-ft2 , six-RTU commercial building (Katipamula, Lutes, and Huang

 15

2023). This cost advantage becomes particularly compelling for SMI when paired with the substantial
energy savings that SC-SMB has demonstrated in field deployment. Guo et al. (2025) reported HVAC
savings ranging from 20% to 90% depending on control strategy and operating conditions (M. Guo
et al. 2025). The kit’s modular extension allows each SMI facility to prioritize the highest-leverage
end-use, and its plug-and-play feature positions the extended kit as a low-cost retrofit aligned with SMI’s
economic and staffing realities.

 5. DISCUSSION
5.1 KEY FINDINGS AND IMPLICATIONS
The review yields three key findings that collectively assess the feasibility and potential benefits of
extending SC-SMB to SMI facilities. They address the energy savings an extended supervisory controller
kit could achieve, whether SMI facilities can achieve similar savings, and what engineering work the
extension would require.
Most of the documented SMI savings opportunities are within reach of an extended SC-SMB kit through
low-cost devices and supervisory controls. The rest require substantial equipment replacement or apply
only to larger-scale facilities. The reviewed literature converges strongly on the potential for compressed
air savings, with independent sources reporting combined savings of 30%–50%. All 36 medium-sized
manufacturers surveyed could reduce compressed-air energy use by 30% on average and up to 67% (Barrer
1997). Leak detection emerges as the highest-leverage supervisory function. Process thermal offers the
largest aggregate opportunity in the broader industrial population. The ORNL Better Plants treasure-hunt
program found that process heating accounted for 85% of total energy savings across participating
facilities, which span manufacturing more widely than the six target industries, equivalent to $10.23
million in annualized savings (Miera et al. 2024). Within the target industries, process thermal leads
energy use in food and fabricated metal, whereas machine drive leads in plastics, machinery, and printing
(U.S. Energy Information Administration 2022; DOE Advanced Manufacturing Office 2018a, 2018c,
2018b). HVAC has a smaller documented savings potential, with savings of around 20% reported for
fan and blower systems (Rao et al. 2022). For facility HVAC more broadly, advanced controls save about
18% per system (Patterson, Singh, and Cho 2022), and SC-SMB’s own field deployment in a commercial
office reported estimated HVAC savings of 20%–50% with model predictive control and up to 90% with
rule-based setback during unoccupied hours (M. Guo et al. 2025). HVAC control patterns map most
cleanly to SC-SMB’s existing control logic, including scheduling, making it the most directly accessible
SMI extension target. Beyond the three target end-uses, machine drive is the largest on-site end-use in
plastics, machinery, and printing (DOE Advanced Manufacturing Office 2018c, 2018b; U.S. Energy
Information Administration 2022), and only 16% of industrial motor systems are under VFD control (Rao
et al. 2022). Therefore, broader motor-system control is an additional opportunity that this assessment does
not quantify. These capabilities also enable potential demand-flexibility opportunities. Compressed-air
storage, batch-mode process thermal scheduling, and ventilation setbacks can shift and reduce peak loads,
addressing peak-demand charges that account for 30%–70% of monthly industrial electric bills (Nandy
et al. 2022).
Although the savings opportunity is broadly attainable, a literature-based assessment must address whether
ranges drawn from broader industrial studies translate to actual SMI-facility scale. Two size-matched
case studies in the reviewed literature indicate that substantial savings at SMI scale are achievable
through approaches similar to SC-SMB’s, within paybacks typical for SMI manufacturers. HARBEC,
a small plastics-injection plant, cut plant energy costs by $52,000 per year under an ISO 50001 energy
management system, yielding a simple payback of about 2.4 years on the $127,000 implementation cost
(DOE GSEP Energy Management Working Group and Better Plants 2014). A Honda Atlas automotive
parts plant achieved 7.75% facility-wide electricity savings, equivalent to 1.21 GWh and about $177,000
per year, with a payback under 28 months from an energy management system retrofit (Akhtar et al. 2020).
Both cases show that comparable retrofit approaches deliver significant savings at SMI scale, within
the 1- to 3-year payback windows that SMI manufacturers typically require. A larger sample provides
similar evidence. Across 220 small and medium facilities, 343 thermal-system recommendations produced

 17

93 implementations yielding $1,513,060 in annual savings at a portfolio-average simple payback of 10
months (Kapp, Choi, and Kissock 2022). Broader industry studies support this finding. In food and
beverage manufacturing, the total energy-saving potential ranges from 16%–35% by subsector by adopting
commercially available upgrades across motor systems, refrigeration, and compressed air (Dollinger
et al. 2017). Many of these measures fall within SC-SMB’s supervisory-controls scope, and refrigeration
compressor upgrades alone can contribute up to 17% (Cresko et al. 2024).
Whether those savings can be captured affordably depends on what capabilities the SC-SMB kit needs to
add for SMI deployment. The review identifies four distinct capabilities the extended kit could leverage
for SMI facilities. Industrial-specific control logic must extend supervisory coverage to compressed air,
process thermal, and HVAC, with compressed air offering the most significant opportunity. Better data
collection is the critical gap because most SMI equipment lacks the sensors to make energy use visible
to operators. Connecting equipment from different vendors requires new bridges to industrial protocols
and utility-side open standards. The kit’s low-cost, modular design also mitigates financial and workforce
barriers. Recent reviews of Industry 4.0 solutions for industrial energy management identify the same
priorities, reporting data collection and analysis as the most implemented technologies and as having the
greatest impact on awareness, connectivity and integration, and visualization (Cagno et al. 2025; Hasan
and Trianni 2023). These additions extend rather than replace SC-SMB’s existing architecture, making the
SMI extension an affordable retrofit that SMI manufacturers can feasibly deploy.

5.2 FUTURE DIRECTIONS
With feasibility established, future directions lie in adapting demonstrated supervisory control capabilities
to SMI facilities that are barely instrumented, vendor-fragmented, understaffed, and statically scheduled.
Each gap presents a potential research priority.
Data acquisition is the most fundamental priority. A control system can only manage what it can measure.
However, most industrial equipment lacks sufficient sensing infrastructure to reveal its detailed energy use.
Although low-cost sensors have become widely available, SMI facilities have not actively adopted and
effectively used them, given their limited in-house energy staff (Magadán et al. 2022). Three directions
have potential as part of the SC-SMB extension. The first is determining where to place the minimum
number of new sensors a facility can afford so that they capture a fuller picture of energy consumption in
SMI facilities (Suryanarayana et al. 2021). The second is using those sensing data to identify abnormal
operation as it arises or to predict energy use. The sensing infrastructure could detect and flag a machine
that has started leaking, wearing out, or running when it should be idle, and could forecast a facility’s
energy use to guide control without manual intervention from building operators (Magadán et al. 2022).
The third is delivering and visualizing the collected information through a low-cost, user-friendly web
interface that an SMI facility can easily install with minimal effort. An open-source web interface enables
integration with heterogeneous equipment through industry-standard protocols, using unified configuration
files rather than custom code (Chung, Huang, Lian, et al. 2026; Chung, Huang, Liu, et al. 2026). This
approach is particularly well suited for SMI because it removes three barriers: licensing costs, the need
for a systems integrator, and dependence on any one vendor’s ecosystem.
Interoperability is a critical barrier to deploying SC-SMB across various SMI facilities. The SC-SMB
was primarily designed for interoperable communication with sensing and control devices in commercial
buildings, and two research directions are needed to extend it to SMI facilities. The first is broadening its
support for a wider range of standardized communication protocols used in industrial facilities, such as
OPC UA and Profibus, so that the supervisory control system can connect to and coordinate industrial
equipment beyond building systems (Tapia et al. 2023). The second is supporting a machine-readable
semantic data model that describes each machine in standardized terminology. This approach could enable

 18

control systems to interpret data from industrial devices without customized programming, building on
established schemas for industrial facilities and buildings such as SAREF4INMA and OntoENERGY
(Roode et al. 2020; Linnenberg et al. 2013).
Autonomous operation becomes a priority for addressing the workforce barrier, given that SMI facilities
rarely have dedicated control engineers or energy specialists to deploy advanced control systems that
require deep engineering skills and substantial effort. The most valuable advance would be an autonomous
deployment process, in which the kit discovers the equipment it is connected to, configures itself, and
reuses what it learns at one plant to accelerate deployment at subsequent plants (Pinto et al. 2022), so
installation no longer depends on scarce engineers. The other advance would be cutting model-building
effort while keeping the result inspectable because a control failure that stops production presents an
unacceptable operational risk. Model-free approaches such as reinforcement learning learn directly from
operating data (Wang and Hong 2020) and have cut HVAC energy by over 20% against a rule-based
controller (F. Guo et al. 2025), but they remain a black box to plant staff. Gray-box models may be better
suited to SMI because embedding known physical structure into a learned model keeps it interpretable and
bounds its predictions to safe operating ranges. One such model generalized over 20 consecutive days from
only 10 days of training data (Drgoňa et al. 2021). Together, they would allow each facility to deploy and
improve its own control with minimal effort and cost.
The final direction is to enable SMI facilities to serve as demand-side grid resources rather than merely
tools for lowering their own energy bills. If supervisory controls can shift industrial loads in response
to grid conditions, a facility could ease demand during peak periods and earn payments through
incentive-based demand-response programs. Two research directions would realize this potential. The first
is quantifying how much and how long loads can be shifted without compromising facility operations,
for example how many minutes a refrigeration system or an air compressor can safely idle, and designing
control strategies that convert that flexibility into demand-response revenue (Xu et al. 2025). The second is
flexibility aggregation because the flexibility available from a single facility may be insufficient to interest
a grid operator, so pooling many plants’ flexibility through an aggregator would combine them into a
resource large and steady enough to be dispatched, opening grid markets that individual plants are too
small to enter on their own (Ma, Billanes, and Jørgensen 2017). DOE already funds this type of flexibility
aggregation. Its Industrial Demand Flexibility Hub at LBNL is assessing statewide flexibility potential
and running demonstrations at manufacturing and food-processing sites (Lawrence Berkeley National
Laboratory, Building and Industrial Energy Systems Division 2026).

 6. CONCLUSION
This report assessed the feasibility of extending the supervisory controller kit developed for SMCBs to
SMI facilities. Three obstacles motivated the review. SMI facilities are largely uncontrolled, conventional
controls typically exceed their budgets, and few affordable alternatives have been confirmed. The study
reviewed 61 papers, drawn mainly from federal and national-laboratory reports and peer-reviewed journals.
It characterized six target industries, analyzed the control-infrastructure gaps and adoption barriers they
share, mapped SC-SMB’s capabilities onto those gaps, and compiled the reported savings for compressed
air, process thermal, and HVAC. The extension is feasible and beneficial. Savings are attainable with
low-cost controls at SMI-scale paybacks, and the required engineering extends SC-SMB’s architecture
rather than rebuilding it.
This project offers three main contributions. First, this report establishes the technical basis for extending
commercial-buildings supervisory control to SMI facilities. A full-scale effort can begin from a scoped
problem, with the target industries, capability gaps, and required extension already identified, and the
same analytical approach applies to other efforts extending commercial building technology to industrial
facilities. Second, this report confirms that the extension would be an affordable retrofit for SMI facilities,
which matters most for manufacturers that cannot afford conventional control systems. Most of the
savings reported in the literature can be achieved by adding low-cost sensors and control logic such as
scheduling or set point optimization to existing equipment. Third, this review identifies and offers four
future directions for a full-scale extension to address the current adoption barriers: affordable sensor
placement, cross-vendor interoperability, autonomous deployment and configuration, and load-shift
quantification. Each direction is drawn from the reviewed literature, with the relevant prior work noted
and confirmed through a domain expert interview.
Three limitations constrain this study’s findings and analysis. First, the assessment is literature-based.
No field data were collected, and every savings range comes from published studies rather than from
measurements at an SMI facility. Second, the extension’s cost was not estimated. The affordability
finding relies on PNNL’s $7,700 commercial reference design rather than on a cost estimate for an SMI
configuration carrying industrial protocol bridges and added sensors. Third, several transferred figures
come from commercial settings. The HVAC savings were demonstrated in an occupied office building,
and their industrial equivalents remain unverified. All three point to the same requirement: a full-scale
effort should begin with measurement at SMI sites. Within these limits, the reviewed evidence shows
that extending SC-SMB to SMI facilities is both technically feasible and affordable, making full-scale
development the appropriate next step.
