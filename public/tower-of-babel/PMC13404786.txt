Long‐Term Supervised Cardiac Rehabilitation After Balloon Pulmonary Angioplasty Improved Residual Exercise Pulmonary Hypertension in Patients With Chronic Thromboembolic Pulmonary Hypertension: A Two‐Case Report
Authors: Yuya Suzuki, Yoshihisa Nakano, Katsuhiro Okazaki, Momoko Ito, Anna Yoshimori, Mai Tsunoda, Masahiro Yoshida, Toko Mitsui, Shiro Adachi, Akira Kimura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAbnormal pulmonary hemodynamics during exercise may persist even after balloon pulmonary angioplasty (BPA) for inoperable chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH). We report two female patients who underwent long‐term supervised outpatient cardiac rehabilitation (CR) after BPA with continuous pulmonary vasodilators. In Case 1, training was performed at an intensity close to the anaerobic threshold. In Case 2, high‐intensity interval training (HIIT) was gradually introduced. Exercise right heart catheterization was used for serial assessment of the mean pulmonary arterial pressure/cardiac output slope (mPAP/CO slope). Functional outcomes were also evaluated by cardiopulmonary exercise testing. No adverse events occurred during follow‐up. The mPAP/CO slope improved from 6.28 to 4.06 mmHg/L/min at 12 months in Case 1 and from 8.40 to 6.71 mmHg/L/min at 18 months in Case 2. Peak VO2 also increased from 16.2 to 20.2 mL/kg/min in Case 1 and from 18.3 to 24.9 mL/kg/min in Case 2. Long‐term supervised outpatient CR after BPA appears to be feasible and may improve exercise pulmonary hemodynamics and exercise capacity.Keywords: chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension, exercise pulmonary hypertension, long‐term cardiac rehabilitation

Case Description

Case 1

A 77‐year‐old female with chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) had multiple thrombi in the peripheral pulmonary arteries on contrast‐enhanced computed tomography. Resting right heart catheterization (RHC) showed mean pulmonary arterial pressure (mPAP) of 40 mmHg and pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) of 23.1 wood units (WU). Exercise RHC using a supine ergometer (0–20 W, 10 W‐increments every 3 min) revealed an abnormal multipoint‐derived mPAP/cardiac output (CO) slope of 38.9 mmHg/L/min. She initiated home oxygen therapy (HOT) and riociguat, titrated to 7.5 mg/day. After four balloon pulmonary angioplasty (BPA) sessions, resting mPAP improved to 16 mmHg, and riociguat was continued throughout the rehabilitation period.

Her 6‐min walk distance (6MWD) remained 420 m with oxygen at 2 L/min, and walking peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) decreased to 87%. To discontinue daytime HOT, once‐weekly outpatient cardiac rehabilitation (CR) was initiated.

Initial post‐BPA cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET): peak oxygen uptake (VO2) was 16.2 mL/kg/min (67% predicted); minute ventilation/CO2 output (VE/VCO2) slope was 40. Training comprised aerobic exercise at anaerobic threshold intensity, with electrocardiography, SpO2, and symptoms monitored. At 6 months post‐BPA, 6MWD on room air was 420 m, with lowest walking SpO2 improving to 90%, allowing daytime HOT discontinuation. However, exercise RHC demonstrated persistent exercise pulmonary hypertension (PH), based on an mPAP/CO slope derived from multipoint measurements of 6.28 mmHg/L/min, mPAP of 35 mmHg, CO of 4.33 L/min, and arteriovenous oxygen difference [C(a‐v)O2] of 9.2 mL/dL at 20 W. CR was continued, and at 12 months post‐BPA, 6MWD was 430 m, with brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) at 13.6 pg/mL. Peak VO2 increased to 20.2 mL/kg/min (83% predicted); VE/VCO2 slope was 30.2. Exercise RHC at 12 months post‐BPA showed improvement, with an mPAP/CO slope of 4.06 mmHg/L/min calculated from multipoint measurements, mPAP was 35 mmHg, CO was 5.61 L/min, and C(a‐v)O2 was 8.4 mL/dL at 20 W. No adverse events occurred.

Case 2

A 67‐year‐old female showed severe CTEPH; her resting mPAP was 52 mmHg, PVR 24.2 WU, and cardiac index 1.18 L/min/m2. Riociguat (1.5 mg/day), selexipag (0.4 mg/day), and HOT were administered. After three BPA sessions, resting mPAP improved to 16 mmHg and HOT was discontinued. Riociguat and selexipag were continued at the same doses throughout the rehabilitation period. Because of anxiety about resuming exercise, once‐weekly outpatient CR was initiated 2 months post‐BPA.

Initial CR assessment showed 6MWD at 550 m. Peak VO2 was 18.3 mL/kg/min (76% predicted), VE/VCO2 slope 34.8. Respiratory gas analysis during aerobics dance showed transient intensity near peak VO2, delaying aerobics return. From 3 months post‐CR initiation, high‐intensity interval training (HIIT) was added, comprising four 4‐min bouts at 75%–90% peak VO2. SpO2 was ≥ 97%, heart rate ≤ 107 bpm, and Borg rating ~11/20. No adverse events occurred. At 6 months post‐BPA, exercise RHC showed persistent exercise PH, based on an mPAP/CO slope derived from multipoint measurements of 8.40 mmHg/L/min, mPAP of 41 mmHg, CO of 5.71 L/min, and C(a‐v)O2 of 9.0 mL/dL at 20 W.CPET revealed improved exercise capacity: peak VO2 was 23.5 mL/kg/min (98% predicted), VE/VCO2 slope 31.7. Outpatient CR, including HIIT, was maintained, and at 18 months post‐BPA, 6MWD was 545 m and BNP 13.6 pg/mL. CPET showed further improvement: peak VO2 was 24.9 mL/kg/min (104% predicted), VE/VCO2 slope 23.3. Exercise RHC at 18 months post‐BPA showed improvement, with an mPAP/CO slope of 6.71 mmHg/L/min calculated from multipoint measurements, mPAP was 37 mmHg, CO was 6.31 L/min, and C(a‐v)O2 was 8.8 mL/dL at 20 W. She returned to aerobics dance without adverse events.

Discussion

BPA is established for patients with inoperable CTEPH or persistent PH after pulmonary endarterectomy [1]. Normalized resting pulmonary hemodynamics post‐BPA may not reflect exercise recovery, with some patients exhibiting limited exercise tolerance and abnormal pulmonary hemodynamic responses during exertion [2]. The mPAP/CO slope is a reliable index of pulmonary vascular response during exercise; an abnormal slope predicts adverse outcomes [3]. In both cases, patients showed exercise PH at 6 months post‐BPA despite normalization of resting mPAP. Long‐term supervised outpatient CR improved peak VO2 and mPAP/CO slope without adverse events, indicating recovery beyond resting hemodynamics (Figure 1). The modest change in 6MWD despite improved CPET parameters may reflect a ceiling effect and the limited capacity of 6MWD to assess aerobic exercise capacity, given that walking capacity was already relatively well‐preserved after BPA [4]. In patients with CTEPH, CR post‐BPA appears safe and enhances exercise capacity and quality of life [5], whereas continued riociguat may affect exercise‐related physiology with limited effect on conventional exercise‐capacity indices [6]. In human pulmonary circulation, increases in CO are accommodated by pulmonary capillary recruitment and distention, limiting rises in pulmonary arterial pressure [7]. In these cases, the mPAP/CO slope improved after long‐term CR following BPA, suggesting that CR enhanced the pulmonary vascular reserve of the reperfused pulmonary vascular bed. Nagel et al. reported that supervised exercise increased pulmonary arterial compliance and stroke volume while reducing effective arterial elastance and PVR in patients with severe pulmonary arterial hypertension and inoperable or persistent CTEPH [8]. They proposed that such exercise may improve right ventricular function and decrease pulmonary arterial stiffness. Thus, the improved mPAP/CO slope in the present cases may partly reflect improved pulmonary vascular compliance and reduced right ventricular afterload during exercise. The mechanisms underlying these hemodynamic changes remain unclear but may involve endothelial function, nitric oxide pathways, inflammation, metabolism, right ventricular function, and pulmonary vasculature [9].

Long‐term CR may improve oxygen transport efficiency during exercise. In CTEPH, oxygen pathway limitations, including impaired peripheral oxygen diffusion and extraction, may persist even after intervention [10]. In our cases, at the same workload, CO increased and C(a‐v)O2 decreased, indicating reduced reliance on peripheral oxygen extraction and enhanced convective oxygen transport. In Case 2, the relatively preserved C(a‐v)O2 despite increased exercise CO may reflect improved skeletal muscle oxygen utilization after HIIT. These cases indicate that HIIT may be feasible in selected clinically stable post‐BPA patients under supervision; however, the findings do not support its routine use in all such patients.

This report is limited by small sample size, lack of a control group, nonuniform exercise prescriptions, and absence of direct assessment of pulmonary vascular or skeletal muscle function; thus, the proposed mechanisms should be interpreted cautiously.

Conclusion

In two patients with CTEPH and persistent exercise PH after BPA, long‐term supervised outpatient CR was safe and improved mPAP/CO slope and peak VO2. Exercise‐based assessment may identify patients who benefit from continued rehabilitation post‐BPA.
