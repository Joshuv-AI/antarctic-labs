Gene-environment interactions contribute to blood pressure variation across global populations
Authors: Khushi Goda, Noah Klimkowski Arango, Francesco Tiezzi, Trudy FC Mackay, Fabio Morgante
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryUnderstanding the interplay between genetics and environment is essential for elucidating the biological basis of complex traits such as blood pressure (BP). Although gene-environment interactions (G×E) contribute to BP variation, their role in multi-ancestry cohorts remains underexplored. We hypothesize that G×E may explain additional variance in BP across diverse populations, where environmental exposures and genetic backgrounds are more heterogeneous. Here, we evaluate the importance of G×E on diastolic pressure (DP), systolic pressure (SP), and pulse pressure (PP) in a multi-ethnic subset of 24,000 UK Biobank individuals. We considered 23 lifestyle variables as the environmental exposures and estimated variance components attributed to genetic effects, environmental effects, and gene-environment interactions. Our results revealed that G×E accounts for 7% of variance in DP, 4% in SP, and 3% in PP. While these estimates are not statistically different from the ones obtained from an analysis restricted to White British individuals using the same lifestyle variables and methodology (4%, 3%, and 4% for DP, SP, and PP, respectively), the point estimate for DP is almost twice as large in the multi-ethnic sample. Including G×E in the models did not improve out-of-sample prediction accuracy. We also performed a G×E genome-wide association study (G×E-GWAS) to uncover individual interactions affecting BP traits. Although no interaction surpassed genome-wide significance, suggestive hits were enriched in BP-relevant pathways. Our hypothesis-generating study suggests that higher environmental and genetic heterogeneity in multi-ethnic cohorts may amplify the role of G×E for some traits and serves as a basis for future studies with larger sample sizes and higher power.Keywords: context-dependent effects, multi-ethnicity, UK Biobank, phenotypic variance decomposition, heritability

Introduction

Understanding the genetic architecture of complex human traits is a fundamental goal of human genetics and is essential for implementing precision medicine. Genome-wide association studies (GWASs) have identified numerous genetic associations across a wide array of traits and diseases, yet these variants typically explain only a modest fraction of phenotypic variance.1,2 In addition, complex traits are also affected by non-genetic factors such as diet and physical activity. For example, for blood pressure (BP) traits, the narrow-sense heritability (h2) has been estimated to be 0.4–0.6.3 However, estimates of the genomic heritability (hg2) indicate that common variants explain about half of the narrow-sense heritability,1 highlighting a persistent gap, often referred to as the “missing heritability.” Finally, genetic variants identified by GWASs explain about 60% of the genomic heritability.4 These gaps suggest the need to look beyond additive effects of common variants and consider other sources of phenotypic variation, including gene-environment interactions (G×E), epistasis, and rare variants. G×E, in particular, offers a biologically plausible mechanism through which environmental exposures modulate genetic risk, contributing to trait variability in ways that additive models cannot fully capture.5,6

Historically, quantitative genetic studies of complex traits have predominantly focused on additive genetic effects, implicitly assuming that genetic effects are the same across environments.5,6 However, emerging evidence indicates that genetic effects can vary depending on the environmental context. G×E, where genetic effects change depending on environmental contexts, are now recognized as a critical yet underexplored axis in the genetic architecture of complex traits.7 Such interactions have been extensively studied in evolutionary biology and agricultural breeding, but only recently have large datasets become available, encompassing comprehensive genotype, phenotype, and environmental information, enabling G×E analyses for human studies. For example, environmental exposures such as dietary salt intake, alcohol use, physical activity, and sleep duration are well-established modifiers of genetic effects on BP levels.8,9,10,11

Despite some success, discovering G×E affecting complex traits remains challenging due to the small effect sizes of individual interactions and the substantial multiple testing burden associated with genome-wide scans.12,13 To enhance detection power, recent studies have adopted variance component models to estimate the collective contribution of G×E to the phenotypic variance of human traits.14,15,16,17,18,19 Unlike single-variant, single-environment approaches, which test variant-environment pairs individually, variance component models aggregate genome-wide and environment-wide signals, increasing statistical power. These models partition the total phenotypic variance (σP2) into components due to additive genetic effects (σG2), environmental effects (σE2), and interaction effects (σG×E2).20,21,22 Using this approach, it has been shown that G×E can explain a non-negligible proportion of phenotypic variance for several complex traits. For example, a study reported that G×E effects explained approximately 9% of variance in body mass index (BMI), 2% in diastolic pressure (DP), 4% in systolic pressure (SP), and 12% in pulse pressure (PP), highlighting the contribution of context-dependent genetic effects to variation in cardiometabolic traits.22 Another study found evidence of gene-lifestyle interactions contributing to the variance in blood lipid levels across multiple ancestries, emphasizing the broad relevance of environmental modulation of genetic effects on metabolic traits.17 Glycemic traits have also been shown to have substantial contributions by G×E, highlighting the role of environmental factors such as diet and physical activity in shaping genetic risk profiles for diabetes-related phenotypes.18 Collectively, these studies illustrate the increasing recognition of context-dependent genetic effects as critical components of complex trait architecture.

The majority of genetic studies, including those investigating G×E, predominantly focus on populations of European descent.23,24,25 Genetic architectures vary across populations due to differences in allele frequencies and linkage disequilibrium (LD) patterns, which can affect the detection and interpretation of G×E signals. Specifically, variants implicated in G×E may differ in their tagging efficiency across populations due to varying LD structures, resulting in certain ancestry-specific interactions remaining undetected in analyses limited to homogeneous samples.26 Furthermore, environmental exposures differ considerably across ancestry groups due to cultural, geographic, dietary, socioeconomic, and healthcare-related factors, potentially interacting differently with genetic predispositions and exacerbating population-specific genetic mechanisms. Thus, heterogeneity in both genetic architecture and environmental exposures across ancestries may result in G×E contributing more to phenotypic variance in multi-ancestry cohorts compared to single-ancestry ones.27,28,29 However, this increased complexity results in statistical challenges: (1) population structure, which is exacerbated in multi-ancestry cohorts, can lead to biased estimates of genetic and interaction variance components, if not properly accounted for30,31,32; (2) ancestry-environment interactions (A×E) can lead to additional confounding33,34; and (3) sample size imbalances across ancestries, where European-descent samples typically predominate, can reduce statistical power to detect ancestry-specific G×E.

Thus, in this study, we used UK Biobank (UKB)35 data to quantify the contribution of G×E to DP, SP, and PP across multiple ethnic backgrounds while addressing the statistical challenges posed by population structure, ancestry-environment interactions, and sample size imbalances across ethnic groups. We used a set of 23 lifestyle-related variables as our environmental exposures. BP traits serve as ideal model phenotypes for studying G×E across ethnic groups because (1) BP is influenced by lifestyle factors such as dietary sodium intake, alcohol consumption, physical activity, sleep patterns, and socioeconomic status, making it particularly suitable for investigating context-dependent genetic effects8,9,10,11; (2) G×E has been shown to contribute significantly to BP variation in samples of European descent15; (3) a multi-ancestry GWAS found low correlations of the effect sizes of significant variants across ethnic groups36; and (4) the prevalence of hypertension (i.e., high BP) is different across ethnic groups.37

Material and methods

Data extraction and processing

Data utilized for this study were sourced from the UKB resource, a large-scale biomedical database that contains extensive genotype and phenotype data. All analyses and data processing steps were performed using R v.4.2.3 and Python v.3.9.5.

Phenotype, lifestyle, and demographic data

The UKB includes phenotypic data from approximately 500,000 participants residing in the United Kingdom.35 Participants from multiple ethnic backgrounds were included and grouped into six broad self-identified groups as provided by the UKB: (1) White, including British, Irish, and any other white background (White, nw = 472,656); (2) Mixed, including White and Black Caribbean, White and Black African, White and Asian, and any other mixed background (Mixed, nm = 2,956); (3) Asian or Asian British, including Indian, Pakistani, Bangladeshi, and any other Asian background (Asian, na = 9,880); (4) Black or Black British, including Caribbean, African, and any other Black background (Black, nb = 8,060); (5) Chinese (Chinese, nc = 1,573); and (6) other ethnic groups (Other, no = 4,558). To facilitate interpretation, we excluded individuals in the Other ethnic group in our study.

The BP phenotypes analyzed included the automated readings of DP and SP at the first assessment. Individuals undergoing medication treatment for BP had their systolic and diastolic measurements adjusted to account for medication effects. Specifically, for individuals on antihypertensive medications, 15 mmHg was added to their SP measurements and 10 mmHg was added to their DP measurements.15,38 We computed PP as the difference between adjusted SP and DP.

Demographic variables (a = 3) included self-identified sex, age at phenotyping, and age2. Only individuals whose age at the time of phenotyping fell within the range of 40–70 years were included in our analysis, as in previous studies.15 A set of l = 23 lifestyle variables was selected based on their established relationships with BP traits,15,39 with additional consideration given to reducing multicollinearity and maximizing sample size across multiple ethnic groups (given the large amount of missing values). The selected variables were Townsend deprivation index (Townsend), moderate physical activity days per week (act0_d), television watching duration (TVtime), sleep duration (sleep_d), deviation in sleep duration (sleep_dev; computed as the squared deviation from the mean sleep duration across individuals), current smoking status (smoking_now), cooked vegetable intake (veg_cook), oily fish intake (fish_oily), non-oily fish intake (fish_lean), processed meat intake (meat_proc), poultry intake (poultry), beef intake (beef), lamb intake (lamb), pork intake (pork), cheese intake (cheese), salt intake (salt), tea consumption (tea), alcohol frequency (alc1), waist circumference (waist), ease of getting up (getup), coffee consumption (coffee), past smoking status (smoked_past), and body fat percentage (BFP).

Individuals without complete records across phenotypic, demographic, or lifestyle information were excluded from the analyses. All variables underwent preprocessing steps to remove outliers, following previous studies.15,40 Individuals with phenotypic values deviating beyond four standard deviations from the mean were removed. Individuals with values exceeding the 99th percentile for coffee, tea, veg_cook, TVtime, and sleep_d or below the 1st percentile for sleep_d were removed. After filtering (Figure S1), we were left with nw = 309,932 White individuals, nm = 1,746 Mixed individuals, na = 4,957 Asian individuals, nb = 3,979 Black individuals, and nc = 774 Chinese individuals used in the subsequent steps described below. Phenotypes (DP, SP, and PP) were centered and scaled to mean 100 and standard deviation 10, while lifestyle variables and demographic variables were centered and scaled to have mean 0 and standard deviation 1.

Genotype data

Genotype array data were initially processed separately for each ethnic background group, including Black, Asian, Mixed, Chinese, and White populations, using PLINK v.1.9.41 We retained genetic variants meeting the following criteria in each ethnic background group—minor allele frequency (MAF) greater than or equal to 0.01, genotype missingness rate (GENO) less than 0.1, and minor allele count (MAC) greater than or equal to 5. We also filtered variants based on a Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) p value greater than 10−10. We chose a more lenient threshold than is generally recommended in GWASs (p > 10−6)42 because HWE is not well defined in an admixed group such as Mixed. This filtering procedure resulted in p = 302,688 autosomal genetic variants for the following analyses.

To mitigate confounding from genetic relatedness, we pruned individuals based on pairwise relationships using PLINK v.1.9, such that no pairs of individuals within each ethnic background group had a relationship coefficient greater than 0.05. This procedure resulted in nw = 253,459 White individuals, nb = 3,347 Black individuals, na = 4,120 Asian individuals, nm = 1,265 Mixed individuals, and nc = 746 Chinese individuals. To avoid disproportionate representation of White individuals and maintain a balanced multi-ethnic dataset, we randomly sampled nw = 15,000 White individuals from their total. The final total sample size for subsequent analyses was n = 24,478. The final sample sizes across ethnic background groups are summarized in Figure 1D. For these individuals, we also predicted their genetic ancestry based on genotype data following the approach of Ruan et al.43 Briefly, we performed principal component analysis (PCA) of a reference panel comprising individuals from the 1000 Genomes Project44 and the Human Genome Diversity Project45 and projected variant loadings onto UKB samples. We then trained a random forest classifier on the top 6 genetic principal components (PCs) of the reference panel and used this model to predict ancestries (African [AFR], Admixed American [AMR], East Asian [EAS], European [EUR], Middle Eastern [MID], Oceanian [OCE], and Central/South Asian [CSA]) to individuals in the UKB. Individuals without an assignment probability greater than 90% for any ancestry were classified as “other.” The ancestry assignments for the individuals are shown in Table S1. The phenotypic correlations among the lifestyle variables are reported in Figure S2.

Statistical analysis

Population structure

To investigate genetic population structure in our sample, we conducted PCA of the genotype data.46,47 In particular, we computed the standard genomic relationship matrix (GRM) as in Yang et al.48 using PLINK v.1.9 and performed its eigen decomposition. To investigate environmental population structure, we performed PCA of the lifestyle data by doing the eigen decomposition of the environmental kernel computed as in Jarquín et al.21 and described in the next section.

Estimation of variance components

We estimated the proportion of BP variance explained by different components (i.e., demographic factors, population structure, genetic factors, lifestyle factors, gene-lifestyle interactions, and ancestry-lifestyle interactions) using the reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) regression approach.49 We used the implementation of this framework available in the BGLR v.1.1.2 R package, which adopts a Bayesian approach to the estimation of all the model parameters.50

We implemented seven models:

where, y is an n vector of phenotypic values; X1 is an n × a design matrix of a = 3 demographic variables (sex, age, and age2); β1 is an a vector of their “fixed” effects; X2 is an n × b design matrix including the top b = 10 PCs of the standard GRM (computed with PLINK); β2 is a b vector of their “fixed” effects; g is an n vector of random additive genetic values, g∼N(0,Kgσg2); e is an n vector of random lifestyle values, e∼N(0,Keσe2); ge is an n vector of random gene-lifestyle interaction values, ge∼N(0,Kgeσge2); i is an n vector of random ancestry-lifestyle interaction values, i∼N(0,Kiσi2); and ϵ is an n vector of random residual values, ε∼N(0,Inσε2).

The kernel Kg is an n × n matrix, where each off-diagonal element denotes the coefficient of genetic relationship between each pair of individuals. This is also known as the GRM. The standard approach to compute the GRM48,51 captures both recent genetic relatedness and distant population structure, when the latter is present, such as in the present study.31 Since we were interested in estimating the variance explained by genetic variants without the contribution of population structure, we computed Kg using the PC-adjusted relatedness estimation (PC-Relate) approach implemented in the GENESIS v.2.24.2 R package. This approach removes the contribution of distant population structure from estimates of genetic relationships using the top genetic PC.31 In this study, we used the top 5 PCs computed using the PC-AiR method,52 as recommended by the authors of PC-Relate.31 The kernel Ke is an n × n matrix, where each off-diagonal element denotes the similarity of each pair of individuals based on the lifestyle variables. It was computed as EE⊺/l, where E is an n × l matrix of lifestyle variables.21 The kernel Kge is an n × n matrix computed as Kg⊙Ke, where ⊙ denotes the Hadamard product. This kernel captures gene-lifestyle interactions.21 The kernel Ki is an n × n matrix computed as KgPC⊙Ke, where KgPC = UDU⊺, D is a b × b diagonal matrix including the top b = 10 eigenvalues of the standard GRM (computed with PLINK), and U is an n × b matrix including the corresponding eigenvectors. This kernel captures A×E (hence the use of the eigen decomposition of standard GRM).

The “fixed” effects were assigned uniform priors (which results in unshrunken estimates) and the variance components were assigned inverted χ2 priors.50 The models were run with a chain of 90,000 iterations, discarding the initial 40,000 iterations as burn-in, followed by thinning every 50 iterations. Convergence was established by visual inspection of the trace plots of all model parameters. Variance components—computed as described in Tiezzi et al.39—were summarized with the posterior mean as a point estimate and the 95% empirical credible (highest posterior density) interval as a measure of error.

Identification of individual interactions via G×E-GWAS

To identify genetic variants whose effects on BP were modulated by lifestyle variables, we performed a variant-lifestyle GWAS (G×E-GWAS). These analyses were conducted using the –glm interaction argument in PLINK v.2.0.41 For each genetic variant and each lifestyle variable, we fit the following model:

where y is an n vector of phenotypes; 1 is an n vector of ones; Xd is an n × a matrix of demographic variables (i.e., sex, age, and age2); Xpc is an n × b matrix of the first 10 PCs of the standard GRM; xg is an n vector of genotypes; xe is an n vector of lifestyle variable values; Xi is an n × b matrix of ancestry-lifestyle interaction values, where its jth column is computed as xi,j = xpc,j⊙xe; xge is an n vector of gene-lifestyle interaction values computed as xg⊙xe; βd, βpc, βg, βe, βi, and βge are their respective effects; and β0 is the intercept.

The interaction effect βge was evaluated for significance, and p values associated with this term were used to identify genetic variants exhibiting significant lifestyle variable-dependent effects. In the absence of Bonferroni-significant results, potential for true signal was evaluated by inspecting quantile-quantile (QQ) plots for each lifestyle variable, identifying variables where observed p values exhibited notable departure from the expected null distribution for small p values. For lifestyle variables displaying such departure, we prioritized genetic variants with p values smaller than the departure point. These top genetic variants were cross-referenced with the GWAS Catalog53 and then annotated using Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (VEP)54 on the GRCh37 (hg19) reference genome build55 to identify genes of interest. To further characterize biological relevance, we conducted functional enrichment analyses using g:Profiler56 to systematically evaluate whether genes identified through suggestive G×E signals converged on shared biological processes or pathways. Functional enrichment was performed against the full annotated human genome as the statistical background. Significance was assessed using the g:SCS (set counts and sizes) threshold (α < 0.05), the default multiple testing correction in g:Profiler. Only terms that passed the g:SCS threshold were considered significant.57 We further cross-referenced annotated genes against BP, cardiovascular, and hypertension-associated loci curated in the GWAS Catalog.58

Prediction of BP traits

We evaluated the predictive ability of models 1–6 using two cross-validation (CV) approaches: random CV and across-ethnicity CV. In the random CV, n = 24,478 individuals were randomly partitioned into five equal subsets (folds). Iteratively, each fold served once as the validation set, while the remaining four folds formed the training set. In the across-ethnicity CV, individuals were grouped according to ethnicity—White, Black, Asian, Mixed, and Chinese. Iteratively, data from all but one ethnic group formed the training set, and the omitted ethnic group formed the validation set. We fitted models 1–6 described above to the training set and used the estimated parameters to predict the phenotypes in the validation set as described in de los Campos et al.49 Prediction accuracy was computed as the R2 from the regression of the true phenotypes on the predicted phenotypes of the validation set individuals.

Results

Distribution of BP traits across ethnic groups

Figures 1A–1C show the distribution of three BP phenotypes—DP, SP, and PP—across ethnic background groups. DP (Figure 1A) showed variability across groups, with Black individuals exhibiting the highest mean DP (87.4 mmHg) and Chinese individuals the lowest (82.9 mmHg). SP (Figure 1B) showed similar differences across ethnic groups, with means ranging from 135.4 mmHg in Chinese individuals to 143.8 mmHg in Black individuals. PP (Figure 1C) followed a different pattern, with White individuals showing the highest mean PP (58.7 mmHg) and Chinese individuals the lowest (52.1 mmHg). These results highlight the presence of phenotypic variability across ethnic groups for the three BP traits.

Population structure

The first two genetic PCs (PC1 and PC2) of the standard GRM, visualized in Figure 2A, showed that there was substantial population structure in our sample, as expected given the presence of individuals from different self-reported ethnic groups. The presence of population structure is a problem when trying to estimate genetic variance components modeled using the GRM computed as in Yang et al.48 This GRM captures both recent genetic relatedness and distant population structure, producing estimates of the genetic variance components that are inflated by structure.31,59 To overcome this, we constructed a GRM using the PC-Relate framework, which estimates genetic relationships corrected for population structure.31 PCA of the PC-Relate-based GRM (Figure 2B) shows a more homogeneous distribution in PC space, with no clear separation among ethnic groups. Thus, we used the PC-Relate-based GRM in all the subsequent analyses.

The PCA of the lifestyle data also showed differences among ethnic groups, although such differences were not as strong as in the genetic data (Figure 2C). The presence of lifestyle differences among ethnic groups was confirmed by the results of ANOVAs with each lifestyle variable as a response and ethnic group as a predictor (Table S2).

Estimation of variance components

Figure 3 presents the estimated variance components across the seven models used. Model 1, which included only demographic variables (sex, age, and age2), explained 4.05% of the variance in DP, 16.13% in SP, and 20.83% in PP. These values represent the baseline contribution of demographic factors to BP trait variation. Incorporating genetic PCs in model 2 increased the explained variance to 7.00%, 18.87%, and 21.57% for DP, SP, and PP, respectively. This increase is attributed to the top 10 PCs, which marginally explained 2.00% of DP variance, 1.24% of SP variance, and 0.40% of PP variance, underscoring the limited influence of population structure on these traits. Model 3 introduced the additive genetic effects, leading to a substantial increase in total variance explained: 25.03% for DP, 32.67% for SP, and 31.68% for PP. The genetic effect alone contributed 18.02% of the DP variance, 13.76% of the SP variance, and 10.09% of the PP variance. Model 4, which instead incorporated lifestyle effects in addition to demographic variables and genetic PCs, explained 16.32% of variance in DP, 19.49% in SP, and 20.44% in PP. Of these, the lifestyle effect contributed 10.56% of DP variance, 5.24% of SP variance, and 1.16% of PP variance. Model 5 included both genetic and lifestyle effects as well as demographic variables and genetic PCs, increasing the total variance explained to 32.12% for DP while slightly reducing it to 32.65% for SP and 30.56% for PP compared to models 3 and 4. The genetic component explained 15.80% of DP variance, 13.13% of SP variance, and 10.07% of PP variance, while the lifestyle component contributed 10.52%, 5.23%, and 1.16% of DP, SP, and PP variance, respectively. Model 6 additionally incorporated the gene-lifestyle interactions, resulting in the highest overall variance explained across all traits: 39.45% for DP, 36.83% for SP, and 33.49% for PP. The G×E component alone accounted for 7.37%, 4.20%, and 3.05% of the variance in DP, SP, and PP, respectively. For model 7, the additional A×E component did not explain a substantial proportion of variance for any trait (i.e., 0.34%, 0.43%, and 0.40% for DP, SP, and PP, respectively). Estimates of variance explained for all other components in model 7 were very similar to those from model 6, with the exception of the G×E component for which the estimates were slightly smaller (i.e., 6.37%, 3.46%, and 2.53% for DP, SP, and PP, respectively). However, since the estimates of the A×E component did not fully account for the difference in estimates of the G×E between models 6 and 7 (e.g., 7.37% is greater than 6.37% + 0.34% for DP), it is possible that the inclusion of an additional variance component in model 7 resulted in a loss of power to estimate the G×E with our small sample size rather than capturing true A×E effects. In addition, the credible intervals for the G×E component from models 6 and 7 largely overlap for all the traits. For these reasons and for its greater computational demand, we did not consider model 7 further. Overall, these findings support the hypothesis that G×E explain a substantial portion of phenotypic variance in BP.

We also fitted model 6 to a sample of White British individuals with the same size as the multi-ethnic sample. The results (Tables S3–S5) showed that G×E explained substantially less variance for DP (4.21%) and SP (3.12%) and slightly more variance for PP (3.88%) than in the multi-ethnic sample.

Variance component estimates remained stable across the models in which they were included, with the exception of the demographic component for SP and, to a lesser extent, PP. In fact, the estimate of the variance explained by demographics diminished in the models that also included the lifestyle component (from ∼16%/17% for SP and ∼21% for PP with models 1–3 to ∼13% for SP and ∼19% for PP with models 4–6). The estimate of the genetic variance component for DP was also slightly reduced (from 18.02% to 15.60%) when including lifestyle information as well (model 3 vs. models 5 and 6). This phenomenon may be due to the presence of correlations between demographic and lifestyle variables and genetic and lifestyle variables, respectively.

Identification of individual interactions via G×E GWAS

To identify specific loci exhibiting evidence of gene-lifestyle interaction effects on BP traits, we conducted genome-wide interaction testing between 302,688 genetic variants and 23 lifestyle variables across three BP traits. For each lifestyle-trait combination, we defined genome-wide significance using a Bonferroni correction threshold of p < 7.18 × 10−9, correcting for the total number of tests, i.e., 302,688 × 23. No variant-lifestyle interaction reached this threshold for any trait.

To further prioritize G×E associations that did not meet a Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold but exhibited signs of potential true signal, we visually inspected QQ plots for each lifestyle-trait combination (Figures S3–S5 for DP, SP, and PP, respectively). We used departure from the null expectation at small p values to identify lifestyle variables likely to harbor suggestive G×E signals. For lifestyle variables whose QQ plots exhibited such departure, we selected a conservative p value cutoff corresponding to the onset of this departure—typically in the range of 10−4 to 10−6—to retain a subset of top-ranked variants for downstream annotation and gene prioritization. Here, we highlight a few interesting examples. For DP, we identified variants with known connections to fatty acid levels and ratios.60 For SP, we identified variants with known associations to cardiovascular disease,61 autoimmune disorders,62 and neurological disorders.63 For PP, we identified variants with known associations to cardiovascular health, such as atrial fibrillation64 and antihypertensive medication response.65 One variant associated with PP has known associations with selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRI) response in major depressive disorder.66 The complete list of variants with known associations in the GWAS Catalog can be found in Table S7.

We then annotated the selected variants using the VEP. For each trait, several identified genes have known associations with BP, neurological disorders, and other cardiovascular traits. For DP, FRMD4A (FERM domain-containing 4A) has several known associations with red blood cell morphology, atrial fibrillation, Alzheimer disease, and restless leg syndrome and several interaction effects with air pollution on peripheral artery disease,67 with SSRI medication use on triglyceride levels,68 and computer game time on depression severity.69 Variants in the gene CNTNAP2 (contactin-associated protein 2) were identified for both SP and PP. CNTNAP2 has known associations with electrocardiogram (EKG) morphology,70 memory decline in normal cognition,71 carotid artery thickness,72 and increased hospitalization rate in serious mental illnesses.73 Additionally, CNTNAP2 contains several variants with known interaction effects, including interactions with sex on baseline memory,74 with physical activity on low-density lipoprotein (LDL) levels,75 and with smoking on LDL levels.76 The complete list of genes identified by the VEP can be found in Table S8.

With the genes identified using the VEP, we performed a GO enrichment analysis to identify significant processes, functions, and cellular components for each trait. For SP, we identified seven GO terms related to neuron function (four) and structure (three). The two most significant terms from this set are involved in positive regulation of long-term synaptic potentiation (GO:1900273) and the main axon (GO:0044304). Upregulated synaptic potentiation (GO:1900273) has been associated with cerebral ischemia (stroke) in young rats.77 Additionally, the term for main axon cellular components (GO:0044304) has been associated with depression (chronic unpredictable moderate stress) in rat models.78 For PP, we identified one GO term for voltage-gated potassium channel complexes (GO:0008076) and one GO term for dendrites (GO:0030425). One study has linked gated potassium channel complexes (GO:0008076) with depression symptoms in response to reserpine, an antihypertensive and antipsychotic medication.79 Additionally, dendrites (GO:0030425) have been identified in a GO enrichment analysis for differentially expressed genes of hypertensive pregnancy offspring.80 GO enrichment analysis did not identify any significant terms for DP. The complete list of GO enrichment results can be found in Table S9.

Prediction of BP traits

We then sought to use the models described above to predict yet-to-be-observed phenotypes. To evaluate the generalizability and transferability of the models across genetically diverse populations, we conducted a cross-ethnicity prediction analysis. In this setting, models were trained on all but one ethnic group and then tested on the held-out group. Figure 4 presents R2 values across the six models (model 1 through model 6) for each BP trait and ethnic group. Prediction accuracy did not improve uniformly across all models and traits. Trait-specific patterns emerged for model 1. DP exhibited the lowest baseline predictability, followed by SP and then PP, which showed the highest prediction accuracy, in line with the proportion of variance explained by demographic variables. For example, for the Asian group, prediction accuracy was R2 = 0.260 for PP and R2 = 0.193 for SP but only R2 = 0.0.050 for DP. R2 for model 1 was highest in PP for Asian (R2 = 0.260) and Chinese (R2 = 0.259) groups, followed by the Mixed (R2 = 0.222) group. Black and White groups showed the lowest R2 of 0.183 for model 1. Incorporating population structure via PCs (model 2) yielded negligible gains over the baseline demographic model (model 1) across all traits and groups. Model 3, which introduced additive genetic effects, provided an improvement in prediction accuracy for DP in individuals of Asian (R2 = 0.056), Mixed (R2 = 0.098), and Chinese (R2 = 0.083) groups and for SP in Mixed (R2 = 0.218) and Chinese (R2 = 0.226) groups. However, no substantial gain from model 3 was observed for PP across any ethnicity group. DP exhibited the most pronounced improvement from the inclusion of lifestyle effects (model 4). For example, in the Asian group, predictive accuracy increased from R2 = 0.050 in model 1 to R2 = 0.128 in model 4; in the Black group, from R2 = 0.025 to R2 = 0.101; and in the Mixed group, from R2 = 0.072 to R2 = 0.185. SP followed a similar trend. In the Chinese group, R2 increased from 0.215 in model 1 to 0.272 in model 4 and in the White group from R2 = 0.154 to R2 = 0.202. PP displayed similar predictive performance across models, with modest gains from adding additional effects beyond that of demographics, consistent with a large proportion of variance explained by demographic variables. For example, R2 improved from 0.260 to 0.271 in the Chinese group and from 0.222 to 0.235 in the Mixed group from models 1 to 4.

For DP, the highest prediction accuracy was observed when genetic and lifestyle components were jointly modeled in model 5, emphasizing their (at least partly) orthogonal contribution to predictions. Comparing prediction accuracies across ethnic groups, our results showed systematic differences. In fact, the Black group consistently exhibited the lowest R2 values, followed by the White and Asian groups. In contrast, the Mixed (except for PP) and Chinese groups achieved the highest predictive performance.

In addition to cross-ethnicity predictions, we conducted 5-fold CV by randomly assigning individuals to folds irrespective of ethnicity. Figure 5 presents R2 values across six models (model 1 through model 6) for each BP trait. For DP, the trait with the lowest impact from demographic variables, predictive accuracy improved across models from R2 = 0.040 in model 1 to R2 = 0.059 in model 2 and further to R2 = 0.066 in model 3. On the other hand, PP showed the highest baseline predictive performance from demographic variables (R2 = 0.208 in model 1) among the three traits, as expected given the large proportion of variance explained by these variables. Discussion

In this study, we sought to decompose the phenotypic variance for three BP traits (DP, SP, and PP) into components attributed to demographics, genetics, lifestyle, and gene-lifestyle interactions, using a multi-ethnicity subset of the UKB. A multi-ethnicity setting is prone to bias in variance component estimates due to population structure, ancestry-environment interactions, and sample imbalance. We addressed this challenge by constructing a structure-adjusted GRM using PC-Relate,31 ensuring balanced sample sizes across major ethnic groups, and including the top genetic PCs and their interactions with lifestyle variables in the statistical models. Our results showed that the additive genetic effect explains 10%–18% of the phenotypic variance for the BP traits. These estimates are a little lower than previous estimates of genomic heritability based on individuals of European descent for these traits (hg2≈0.2).1 However, this observation may be due to the difference in the number of genetic variants used—approximately 300,000 in our study and approximately 7,000,000 in the previous study. We also showed that lifestyle contributes moderately (∼10%) to DP variance, modestly (∼5%) to SP variance, and negligibly (∼1%) to PP variance. These findings are in agreement with recent studies using individuals of European descent.39

Our study showed that gene-by-lifestyle interactions explained ∼7%, ∼4%, and ∼3% of variance in DP, SP, and PP, respectively. Importantly, these point estimates of the proportion of variance explained by G×E are larger than those obtained using only White British individuals for DP and SP and slightly smaller for PP. However, the credible intervals (CIs) for the G×E variance component overlap between the multi-ethnic and White British analyses (although only narrowly for DP, where the CI for White British is 1.94–5.72 and the CI for multi-ethnic is 4.46–9.67), potentially due to the small sample size of our study. Thus, these differences can be interpreted only as suggestive and will need to be replicated in larger samples to be confident in the observed patterns. Our estimates of the variance explained by G×E in White British individuals are in the range of those from previous studies, despite differences in the methodology, lifestyle variables, numbers of variants, and sample size.15,22,81 These results provide suggestive evidence for the hypothesis that G×E might explain a larger proportion of variance in multi-ethnicity samples compared to single-ethnicity ones for some traits, presumably due to the increased genetic and environmental heterogeneity in this setting.29 For instance, allelic heterogeneity and ethnicity-specific LD patterns can increase the detectability of context-dependent genetic effects, particularly when interactions involve variants with variable tagging efficiency across populations.20,26 Similarly, lifestyle factors such as diet, physical activity, and socioeconomic conditions may vary systematically by ethnicity (as partly shown in Figure 2C), making it easier to capture interactions in a multi-ethnicity setting.9 However, it is also possible that confounding effects such as residual population stratification, assortative mating, and indirect genetic effects that are exacerbated in multi-ethnic samples may account for a portion of the difference between the estimates of the proportion of variance explained by G×E in the multi-ethnic analysis and the White British analysis. However, the three traits analyzed display different patterns, whereby the G×E variance is substantially larger for DP (7.37% vs. 4.21%), slightly larger for SP (4.20% vs. 3.12%), and slightly smaller for PP (3.05% vs. 3.88%) in the multi-ethnic analysis compared to the White British analysis. Thus, this observation gave us some confidence that the estimates of the G×E variance may not be systematically inflated in the multi-ethnic analysis. However, more work is needed to rule out these confounders, especially when large family data of non-White individuals become available. It is important to note that the estimates of the variance components other than the interaction component remained stable between models 5 and 6. Thus, when included in model 6, gene-by-lifestyle interactions explained variance that was unexplained in model 5 rather than eroding variance from other components. Additionally, ancestry-environment interactions explained negligible variance (less than 0.5%) for all traits. Taken together, these contributions validate the utility of G×E models for capturing context-dependent genetic effects that are otherwise unmodeled in additive frameworks.

To further dissect the genetic architecture of G×E, we performed genome-wide interaction scans for each lifestyle variable. No trait-lifestyle variable combination yielded any genome-wide significant hits under a strict Bonferroni correction. However, we applied a departure-from-the-null-based thresholding strategy to QQ plots to enable the prioritization of interactions between genetic variants and lifestyle variables that exhibited signs of potential true association. The variants involved in the interactions revealed patterns of vascular biology and blood metabolites. Gene-level analysis revealed further biological connections to immune system function and neurological disorders while providing additional support for blood metabolites and vascular biology. GO enrichment analyses supported biological links to neurological function, identifying dendrites, neurotransmitter-gated ion channels, and positive regulation of synapse potentiation. Each of these functions has established connections to hypertension or cardiovascular disease.77,79,82 Thus, the individual gene-lifestyle interactions underlying the G×E variance exhibit plausible biological connections to BP. However, given that our sample size is highly underpowered to reliably detect interactions, these results should be taken with great care. Additional work with larger sample sizes is needed to confirm our findings.

While G×E explained a substantial proportion of variance, its inclusion in the statistical model did not improve out-of-sample predictive accuracy. Across both random and cross-ethnicity CV schemes, we observed consistent trends: prediction performance was highest when including both genetic and lifestyle effects (model 5) for DP and SP, underscoring the importance of combining genetic and lifestyle main effects. For PP, there was much less difference in accuracy among the models, mainly because demographic variables explained much more variance than the other effects and drove the predictions. However, extending the model to include genome-wide G×E (model 6) did not result in further predictive gains. This plateau was observed across all three traits—DP, SP, and PP—and in both CV scenarios.

This discrepancy between variance explained and predictive performance is likely due to several factors. First, the sample size in our study is very small, which limits the accuracy with which interaction effects to be used for prediction are estimated. Previous studies suggested that detecting robust G×E signals and translating them into predictive gain requires a much larger sample size.83,84 Second, while the RKHS approach used in this study allows for the inclusion of interaction effects easily, it assumes that the interactions between all genetic variants and all lifestyle variables affect the trait analyzed. This assumption very likely does not match the actual architecture of complex traits, resulting in the inclusion of a lot of noise in the model and, hence, poor prediction accuracy. Third, our models employed linear kernels, which may fail to capture nonlinear or threshold-based G×E effects.85 Thus, it is possible that with larger sample sizes and more sophisticated statistical models, G×E may help improve cross-ethnicity prediction accuracy.23 However, recent work from our group suggested that studies with unrelated individuals may not take advantage of accounting for G×E in prediction models, even if G×E explains substantial variance.86 More work is needed to confirm these hypotheses.

One limitation of our study is the use of directly genotyped variants rather than imputed ones. Because arrays tag variation less well in non-European samples, using imputed variants would achieve better and more unbiased coverage. However, a recent study showed that using only genotyped variants did not result in worse portability of polygenic scores across ethnic groups compared to using HapMap3 variants.87 Thus, we do not expect large differences in our results from using either set of variants, with the use of genotyped variants being more computationally efficient.

In conclusion, our findings provide some suggestive evidence for the hypothesis that G×E might contribute more variance to some traits in a multi-ethnicity cohort compared to using only individuals of European descent. However, given that the sample size of our study was small and the credible intervals for the G×E variance overlapped, we see this work and the analytical strategy used in it as the basis for future studies with a larger sample size, aimed at investigating similar issues with higher power. In general, these results highlight the importance of modeling G×E in diverse populations, both to refine our understanding of trait architecture and to advance equitable precision medicine.
