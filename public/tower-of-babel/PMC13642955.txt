Insufficient ferroptosis in radiation-induced tumor cells promote Treg cell generation to induce radiotherapy resistance
Authors: Yue Wu, Yanyang Zhu, Chang Yu, Yuchen Yu, Yi Luo, Yi Zhang, Yonglei Xiao, Qiuyu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recently, cellular mechanisms underlying radioresistance in the tumor microenvironment (TME) attract increasing attention, but how the resistant tumor cells reshape the TME is still unclear. In this study, we find that the ferroptosis-resistant genes are upregulated in radioresistant tumor cells, resulting in the impairment of radiation-induced ferroptosis in radioresistant cells. Additionally, the release of transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGF-β1) is increased in radioresistant tumor cells, contributing to Treg cell infiltration in radioresistant tumor tissues. Hence, the ferroptosis inducer sorafenib enhances the sensitivity of local irradiation in radioresistant tumor models by inhibiting Treg cell infiltration. Furthermore, database analysis of The Cancer Genome Atlas shows that the score of ferroptosis-resistant genes positively correlates with both TGF-β1 expression level and Treg cell infiltration score in several types of tumor tissues. The ferroptosis-resistance score of TME is higher in non-responders than in responders of colorectal cancer and breast cancer. These findings suggest irradiation-induced insufficient ferroptosis promotes the accumulation of Treg cells via the release of TGF-β1, providing new insights for clinical trials designing the combination of ferroptosis-inducing chemotherapy with radiotherapy to overcome radioresistance.

Introduction

Although approximately half of all cancer patients undergo radiotherapy as part of their treatment, only a modest proportion of patients exhibit a complete response to radiotherapy. Radioresistance has been observed in different types of cancer, including breast cancer, non-small cell lung cancer, colorectal cancer, glioma, and nasopharyngeal carcinoma1,2. Previous research has revealed the molecular mechanisms in cancer responsible for radioresistance, including enhanced DNA damage repair pathways, activation of pro-survival signaling, hypoxia-induced metabolic adaptations, and the abundance of cancer stem cells3. Recently, our own and other studies have revealed that the pro- and anti-tumor immune cells in the tumor microenvironment (TME) can also affect radiotherapy efficacy4–6. Nevertheless, the mechanism of irradiated tumor cells regulating intratumoral immune cells to confer radioresistance remains unclear.

Irradiation-induced cell death to date can be categorized into four distinct processes: apoptosis, ferroptosis, pyroptosis and necroptosis. Apoptosis does not elicit an inflammatory response or activate the immune system. The recently identified forms of immunogenic cell death (ICD), namely ferroptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis, have been shown to elicit a synergistic anti-tumor immune response, accompanied by the release of immune-activating molecules7. In recent years, irradiation-induced ferroptosis has been actively studied. It is known that irradiation not only generates reactive oxygen species (ROS) but also upregulates the expression of ACSL4, a key lipid metabolism enzyme to drive lethal lipid peroxidation, thereby exacerbating cell ferroptosis8. Furthermore, irradiation also induces mitophagy that consequently promotes ferroptosis by increasing intracellular free fatty acids9. It has been demonstrated that mitochondrial DNA fragments are released into the cytoplasm and oxidized by ROS in irradiated cancer cells. This facilitates the activation of the STING pathway and the induction of a type I interferon (IFN)-linked adaptive immune response10–12. CD8+ T cells, activated by ICD of cancer cells, can further promote the ferroptosis of irradiated cancer cells via the secretion of IFN-γ13. Given that ferroptosis is a major form of irradiation-induced ICD, we hypothesize that inadequate ferroptosis may undermine anti-tumor immunity in radioresistant settings. To test this hypothesis, we investigate the expression of ferroptosis-related genes in radioresistant cells and elucidate the immunomodulatory role of ferroptosis after radiotherapy, in order to explore the therapeutic potential of combining a ferroptosis inducers with radiotherapy.

Results

Establishment of radioresistant cell lines

To identify potential models for studying radioresistance, we began by profiling the radiation sensitivity of a diverse panel of cell lines, including MCF-7, HCT-116, HT29, U251, A549, B16-OVA, E0771, GL261, LL/2, and MC38. Following exposure to graded dose of irradiation, the survival rates of these cell lines decreased in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1a). Furthermore, scRNA-seq datasets showed that the infiltration of lymphoid cells was higher in tumor tissues of CT-26, LL/2, B16-OVA, and MC38 (Fig. S1a). In accordance with the linear-quadratic (LQ) model formula14, the α/β ratio signifies the degree of radiation-induced damage. Tumor cells with a high α/β ratio, including B16-OVA, MC38, and A549, were utilized to establish radioresistant cell lines (Table S1). We established three resistant cell lines based on A549, B16-OVA, and MC38 cells by mimicking clinical radiation exposure, and surviving tumor cells after being treated with gradually increased irradiation doses were considered as radioresistant cells. Three resistant cells were generated using the same protocol to ensure methodological consistency. Following the generation of radioresistant cells (B16-R, MC38-R, and A549-R), cell viability of these three cells was confirmed to be higher than that of their parental cells (B16-P, MC38-P, and A549-P) after treatment with different doses of irradiation (Fig. 1b). The α/β ratio of radioresistant cells was significantly lower than that of their parental cells (Table S2). The apoptotic rates of B16-R were significantly lower than those of B16-P following exposure to 15 Gy radiation (Fig. 1c). 20 Gy radiation induced an increase in the G2/M phase in B16-R cells than that in their parental cells (Fig. 1d). Colony formation assay further confirmed that B16-R cells exhibited greater resistant ability to 15 Gy irradiation than B16-P cells (Fig. S1b). Ferroptosis-related gene expression in radioresistant cell lines

To identify the genes or pathways related to radioresistance, we compared the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between B16-R and B16-P, and then these genes were overlapped with the ferroptosis-related genes in the ferroptosis database (http://www.zhounan.org/ferrdb)15. In total, 4668 DEGs were upregulated and 12,341 DEGs were downregulated in B16-R compared to B16-P. Among the upregulated and downregulated genes, 53 or 61 genes overlapped with ferroptosis resistance or driver genes, respectively. Similarly, in MC38-R as compared to MC38-P, 78 of 7424 upregulated genes and 49 of 3953 downregulated genes overlapped with ferroptosis resistance and driver genes, respectively. 46 common DEGs were identified between the two cell pairs, including 27 upregulated and 19 downregulated DEGs (Fig. S2a). Based on the fold-change (Log 2FC) between resistant and parental cells, the top 20 upregulated ferroptosis-resistance genes and the top ten downregulated ferroptosis-driver genes were analyzed (Fig. S2b and Table S3). Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network of the top ten hub genes was visualized among 27 upregulated DEGs by cytoHubba. The cystine transporter solute carrier family 7 member 11 (Slc7a11) and ferritin heavy chain (Fth1) were identified as the most connected hub gene (Fig. 2a, b). Next, the upregulated expression of Slc7a11 and Fth1 was confirmed by qRT-PCR and flow cytometry in both B16-R and MC38-R (Fig. 2c, d). Additionally, the upregulated expression of SLC7A11 and FTH1 was also observed in A549-R cells by Bulk RNA-seq, qRT-PCR and flow cytometry (Fig. 2e, g). Irradiation-induced ferroptosis was inhibited in radioresistant cells

Slc7a11 and Fth1 have been reported to inhibit ferroptosis by regulating glutathione synthesis and sequestering intracellular free iron (Fe2+), respectively16,17. Therefore, we measured glutamate release and intracellular ROS and Fe2+ in three paired cells (Fig. 3a). After 15 Gy irradiation, the glutamate concentration in the medium was higher in all three resistant tumor cells than in their parental cells (Fig. 3b). While radiation elevated intracellular ROS in both cell types, resistant cells exhibited a markedly attenuated response compared to parental cells (Fig. 3c). Similarly, 15 Gy irradiation increased intracellular free Fe2+ in resistant and parental cells, and free Fe2+ in the resistant cells was also lower than that in parental cells (Fig. 3d). To further validate the relationship between radioresistance and ferroptosis, we observed whether lipid peroxidation inhibitor liproxstatin-1 could reduce radiation-induced ferroptosis in B16-P cells. As expected, liproxstatin-1 significantly reduced intracellular ROS and relative intracellular free iron (Fe2+) levels in B16-P after irradiation (Fig. S3a, b). liproxstatin-1 also inhibited radiation-induced cell apoptosis (Supplementary Fig. S3c). The specificity of this effect was highlighted in a comparative analysis of cell death pathways: only liproxstatin-1, and not inhibitors of apoptosis, necroptosis, or pyroptosis, conferred significant protection against irradiation-induced cell death (Fig. S3d). Infiltration of regulatory T cells was increased in radioresistant tumor models

Given that ferroptosis is a recognized form of ICD, we observed whether the inhibition of ferroptosis affects radiation-triggered T cell immune response in mouse tumor models. In non-irradiated conditions, the tumor growth rate of radioresistant cells (B16-R or MC38-R) was similar to that of parental cells (B16-P or MC38-P) in both Rag-1−/− and wild-type mice. However, after a single dose of 15 Gy irradiation, tumor growth was inhibited in both B16-P- and MC38-P-bearing wild-type mice, but no inhibitory effect in B16-R- and MC38-R-bearing mice (Fig. 4a–d). Collectively, these results indicated that irradiation-induced T cell response was diminished in radioresistant models. To investigate the possible causes of impaired T cell response in radioresistant tumors, we compared the transcriptional profiles and infiltration of immune cells between B16-R and B16-P tumor tissues. We used single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) to estimate the abundance of seven immune cell types. In non-radiation groups, T cell scores were higher in B16-R than in B16-P, especially CD8 T cells. However, after irradiation, regulatory T (Treg) cell scores were significantly higher in B16-R than in B16-P (Fig. 4e). Using CIBERSORTx based on scRNA-seq datasets to estimate the abundances of member cell types in B16-tumor tissue, we identified characteristic genes of Treg cells, such as Tnfrsf4 and Ctla4. Similar to the results of ssGSEA, the percentage of Treg cells was also higher in B16-R than in B16-P tissues (Fig. 4f, g). Enhanced Treg cell infiltration (CD4+ Foxp3+) was confirmed by flow cytometry analysis in B16-R tumor tissues on day 15 post irradiation (Fig. 4h, i). We also used scRNA-seq to explore the immune landscape of the TME in MC38-P and MC38-R. The cells from MC38-P and MC38-R tumor tissues were grouped into six major populations (Fig. S4a, b). Consistent with the B16 models, MC38-R tissues exhibited elevated expression of Treg-related genes (Fig. S4c). The ferroptosis-resistance scores were higher in MC38-R than in MC38-P tissues (Fig. S4d). These findings indicated that tumor cells that resist ferroptosis may attract Treg cells and confer radioresistance. Sorafenib was proposed to act as a ferroptosis inducer in several tumor cell lines and was investigated in clinical trials of advanced melanoma18,19. We investigated whether radiotherapy combined with a ferroptosis inducer could reverse radioresistance in the B16-R model. (Fig. 4j). The combination of sorafenib and 15 Gy irradiation reduced tumor size (Fig. 4k). On day 14 post-tumor implantation, fewer Treg cells were detected in the combination groups than in the monotherapy groups (Fig. 4l and Fig. S4e). Furthermore, no detectable effect of sorafenib was observed on the survival and differentiation of T cells in vitro (Fig. S4f). Radioresistant tumor cells induce T-cell differentiation into Treg cells by upregulating TGF-β1

To analyze the impact of irradiation-induced cell death on T cell differentiation and cytotoxicity, irradiation-treated tumor cells were cocultured with antigen-specific OT-I T cells (Fig. 5a). More B16-R survived after 24-h coculture with T cells than B16-P (Fig. 5b). More CD4+CD25+Foxp3+Treg cells were detected in T cells cocultured with irradiated B16-R than those with irradiated B16-P, suggesting that more of naïve T cells differentiate into Treg cells (Fig. 5c). These findings illustrated that radioresistant tumor cells not only evade the cytotoxicity of antigen-specific T cells but also induce Treg cell differentiation. To examine the underlying mechanisms of Treg cell differentiation induced by radioresistant cells, we compared Treg-related genes between B16-P and B16-R. Bulk RNA-seq showed that B16-R cells exhibited higher levels of TGF-β pathway components and cytokines involved in Treg cell differentiation and infiltration, especially Tgfb1 and Ccl5 (Fig. 5d). Bulk RNA-seq analysis confirmed the upregulation of Tgfb1 in B16-R, as highlighted in the volcano plot (Fig. S5a). The upregulation of Tgfb1 expression was also observed in the tumor tissues of B16-R as compared to B16-P with or without 15 Gy irradiation (Fig. S5b). The increased expression and release of TGF-β1 in B16-R were confirmed by qRT-PCR and ELISA (Fig. 5e, f). Higher levels of TGF-β1 expression and release were also found in A549-R cells as compared to A549-P cells with or without radiation exposure (Fig. 5g–i). Discussion

Radiotherapy is considered the standard treatment for most solid tumors20,21. Recent research has shown that IR-induced unrepaired DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) lead to the accumulation of double-stranded DNA in the cytosol, which subsequently activates the cGAS and STING pathways, triggering the activation of anti-tumor CD8+ T cells10,22. Our previous study showed that local irradiation enhanced the proliferation and effector function of both adoptively transferred and endogenous antigen-specific CD8 T cells, suggesting that radiotherapy may expand the T cell repertoire23. As an immune-stimulator, local radiotherapy was combined with immunotherapy in clinical trials of various advanced cancers24–27. However, only a subset of cancer patients (20–30%) exhibited a synergistic effect from the combination therapy28–30. Our previous study demonstrated that the abscopal effects of local irradiation depend on tumor immunogenicity. In poorly immunogenic tumors, there was no significant alterations were observed in the non-irradiated distant tumor sites, in spite of increased CD8 T-cell infiltration in irradiated tumor tissues5. A better understanding of the mechanisms underlying radioresistance will help to select tumor patients and improve the benefits of the combination therapy.

Traditionally, radiation-induced DNA damage repair, cell cycle arrest, escape from apoptosis and stemness of cancer cells are conventionally considered to be the major causes of radioresistance and relapse after radiotherapy3. In recent years, there has been increasing evidence of radiation-induced ICD dominantly determines radiotherapy outcome5,10,31,32. ICD is a special type of cell death that activates the adoptive immune response to attack tumors. Different types of cell death can cause ICD, including ferroptosis, pyroptosis, necroptosis, autophagic cell death, and NETosis. Ferroptosis is a new type of cell death that is mainly characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation. Emerging evidence shows that activating ferroptosis could reverse the resistance of cancer to common therapies like chemotherapy, targeted therapy, radiotherapy and immunotherapy8,33,34. However, it is not yet known how ferroptosis affects cancer radioresistance. To determine whether radioresistance is associated with ferroptosis, the sensitivity of different tumor cells to radiotherapy was determined, and three cell lines were selected to establish radioresistant tumor cell lines. Low-dose gradient-enhanced intermittent irradiation is often used to construct radioresistant tumor cell lines, including oral squamous cell carcinoma35, non-small cell lung cancer36, and ovarian cancer37. These human tumor cell lines cannot be used to establish tumor models in immunocompetent mice. We conducted single-cell transcriptomic analysis of the immune microenvironment in B16-OVA, LL/2, and MC38 tumors, where the higher proportion of lymphocytes in these models provided a unique opportunity to investigate lymphocyte involvement in the radioresistant phenotype. We constructed two mouse cancer cell lines, B16-R and MC38-R, and one human cancer cell line, A549-R. The characteristics of radioresistance in these three cell lines were verified by analysis of cell growth, apoptosis resistance and cell cycle distribution after irradiation. By analyzing the differences in gene expression between the radioresistant and their parental cells, we found that ferroptosis-resistant genes were increased in radioresistant cells. Ferroptosis is characterized by the inhibition of cysteine-glutamate exchange, the accumulation of lethal ROS derived from iron metabolism38. We noted that ferroptosis-driver genes are upregulated in the B16 model. However, in the MC38 model, we observed that the upregulation of ferroptosis-resistance genes may be a key feature of the radioresistance model, and successfully validated this finding in subsequent experiments. We speculate that the upregulation of ferroptosis drivers in the B16 model is a response to the resistance of ferroptosis, acting as a protective mechanism by the cells. SLC7A11 is a cysteine/glutamate antiporter involved in glutathione synthesis to protect cancer cells from oxidative stress. FTH1 and FTL together form ferritin, which is responsible for the oxidation of iron Fe2+ to Fe3+, thereby preventing ferroptosis39. Flow cytometry showed that the expression levels of SLC7A11 and FTH1 were higher in three radioresistant cells than in their parental tumor cells. Correspondingly, three radioresistant cells had higher extracellular glutamate and lower intracellular lipid ROS and Fe2+ than their parental cells, indicating the impairment of ferroptosis in radioresistant cells. Only liproxstatin-1, an effective ferroptosis inhibitor40, reduced cell death when it was combined with 15 Gy irradiation in B16-P cells. These findings suggested that cancer cells might develop radioresistance by enhancing their antioxidant defense mechanisms, such as increasing glutathione levels or altering iron metabolism.

To investigate whether impairment of ferroptosis in cancer cells affects T cell response, we compared tumor growth of two paired cells and analyzed their TME. Tumor growth was only inhibited in B16-P and MC38-P-bearing WT mice after 15 Gy irradiation. There was no difference between radioresistant and parental tumor cells in Rag-1−/− mice. These data indicated that irradiation-induced T cell responses might be attenuated in radioresistant cells. TME analysis showed more Treg cell infiltration in radioresistant tumors than in parental tumors. To show how Treg cells accumulate in radioresistant tumors, irradiated B16-R and B16-P cells were cocultured with naïve T cells. Our data showed that radioresistant cells promoted Treg cell differentiation, thereby suppressing the anti-tumor activity of cytotoxic T cells. Next, we investigated the genes involved in Treg cell differentiation in B16-R and B16-P, including chemokines and cytokines. TGF-β1 expression was higher in B16-R and tumor tissues than in their controls. Since TGF-β1 is required for the induction of transcription factor Foxp3 for naive T cells differentiation into Treg cells41. Based on TCGA data, the ferroptosis-resistance score was found to be positively correlated with Treg cell infiltration or TGF-β1 expression levels. Furthermore, a higher ferroptosis-resistance score in non-responders than in responders of both BRCA and COAD provides evidence for ferroptosis linking with immune response. These findings indicated that insufficient ferroptosis in radioresistant cells led to increased TGF-β1 release, which could contribute to differentiation and accumulation of Treg cells in tumor tissues.

Accumulation of Treg cells is an important homeostatic mechanism for radiation-damaged tissues, and they also aid in immune evasion and lead to radioresistance42. Previous studies have shown that Treg cells are less susceptible to radiation-induced cell death than the other lymphocytes43,44, and TGF-β1-induced Treg cells exhibited increased resistance to radiation as compared to CD4+ conventional T cells45. Recently, Treg-targeted approaches have been used in preclinical and clinical trials to enhance radiotherapeutic benefit and the synergistic efficiency of radiotherapy in combination with immunotherapy46–49. Our results provided evidence of irradiation-induced tumor cell ferroptosis-resistance contributing to Treg cell accumulation, indicating ferroptosis inducers might reverse the resistance of cells to radiotherapy. Since sorafenib has been reported to induce ferroptosis, possibly through inhibition of the cystine/glutamate antiporter18,19, we found that sorafenib exhibited a synergistic effect with irradiation in a radioresistant tumor model. We also observed that radiation-induced Treg cell accumulation was attenuated by sorafenib. These findings were in line with previous reports that sorafenib sensitized the irradiation effect in vitro and in vivo50–53. Taken together, our study proposed new insights into the synergistic mechanism of Treg-targeting in combination with radiotherapy, and also provided a new strategy for selecting chemotherapy drugs to reverse radiotherapy resistance.

In summary, our work describes a previously uncharacterized mechanism by which radioresistant cells remodel an immune-suppressive TME. Although previous evidence of irradiation-induced ICD supports the role of systemic immune activation, many ongoing clinical trials related to radiotherapy have shown only anecdotal success in inducing the abscopal effect, even combining local irradiation with immune checkpoint inhibitors. Our study observed that the expression levels of ferroptosis-resistant genes were upregulated in the radioresistant tumor cells. High levels of TGF-β1 were released from radioresistant cells, and subsequently induced differentiation and accumulation of Treg cells in tumor sites. A ferroptosis-inducing drug showed a synergistic effect with irradiation by reducing Treg cell accumulation. These findings provide valuable information to improve our understanding of the mechanism of tumor radioresistance, providing insight into clinical study design for ferroptosis-inducing chemotherapy in combination with an optimization of the radiation dose and fractionation, offering paths to improve the efficacy of radiotherapy.

Materials and methods

Mice strains and cell lines

Female C57BL/6 mice were purchased from GemPharmatech Co. (Nanjing, China), and B6.Cg-Foxp3tm2Tch/J (Foxp3EGFP) was purchased from the Jackson Laboratory, and OT-I/CD45.2 transgenic mice were bred in-house.

Most tumor cell lines were purchased from ATCC (Manassas, VA, USA), including LL/2, HT29, HCT-116, A549, U251, GL261, B16-OVA, MC38, and E0771 were obtained from Dr. Lieping Chen (Yale University). B16-OVA, MC38， and A549 were used to establish radioresistant cell lines. To establish the radioresistant cell lines, tumor cells were subjected to fractionated X-ray irradiation by mimicking clinical radiation exposure and selecting tumor cells that survived after increasing levels of irradiation. Tumor cells were exposed to a series of increasing radiation doses, with equal intervals (2 Gy → 4 Gy → 6 Gy → 8 Gy → 10 Gy), administered sequentially. After each irradiation dose, cells were cultured to reach 70–80% confluence for the next higher dose. The interval between each irradiation cycle was 7–10 days, and this cycle was repeated until a cumulative dose of 50 Gy was reached. All radioresistant cell lines were constructed by the same protocol, following identical procedures to ensure consistency. The radiosensitivity of the resistant and their parental cells was detected by colony formation assay, cell cycle assay, and cell apoptosis assay. LL/2, U251, GL261, B16-OVA, and E0771 cell lines were cultured in DMEM (with 10% FBS and 100 μg/ml penicillin/streptomycin). HT29, HCT-116, A549, and MC38 cell lines were cultured in RPMI-1640 (with 10% FBS and 100 μg/ml penicillin/streptomycin) and tested for mycoplasma using the Myco-Blue Mycoplasma Detector (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China).

Animal experiments

Tumor cells were injected into C57BL/6 mice to establish tumor models, including B16-P and B16-R (3 × 106 cells per mouse), and MC38-P and MC38-R (0.5 × 106 cells per mouse). The tumor diameters were calculated using (l + w)/2, where l and w refer to the larger and smaller dimensions, respectively. Mice were randomized into different treatment groups when tumor diameters reached 5–6 mm. In the irradiation group, mice were anaesthetized with isoflurane and placed under lead shielding with a 15 mm diameter aperture aligned over the tumor. Only the irradiated tumor in the right flank was exposed to irradiation, while the rest of the body was kept outside the radiation field. Radiation was performed using an RS-2000 Biological Irradiator (RadSource, Buford, GA, USA) at 160 kV, 10 mA and a dose rate of 1.05 Gy/min. A total of 3 × 106 B16-R cells were injected into Foxp3EGFP mice. Sorafenib (#HY10-201, MedChemExpress, NJ, USA) was intraperitoneally injected at 40 mg/kg once a week, three consecutive times. All the mice were regarded as dead from humane treatment after the tumor reached 20 mm in size in each dimension.

Cell viability assay

The cell viability was detected using Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Dojindo Laboratories, Kyushu Island, Japan). Tumor cells were seeded in 96-well plates overnight and then subjected to the indicated dose of irradiation, with or without the inhibitors of ferroptosis (liproxstatin-1), necroptosis (necrostatin-1), pyroptosis (disulfiram), and apoptosis. All inhibitors were purchased from MedChemExpress. After additional incubation for 48 h, CCK-8 solution (10 μl) was added to each well, followed by measurement of the absorbance at 450 nm using the SpectraMax® i3x Multi-Mode Microplate Reader (Molecular Devices, CA, USA).

Cell apoptosis assay

B16-P and B16-R cells were subjected to irradiation or sham-irradiation. B16-P cells were subjected to 15 Gy of irradiation and then incubated in medium containing liproxstatin-1 (HY-12726, MedChemExpress, NJ,USA) or control medium. After additional incubation for 48 h, irradiated tumor cells were stained with eBioscienceTM Annexin V Kit (#88-8007-74, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The percentages of dead tumor cells (PI positive) were quantified by flow cytometry.

Cell cycle assays

B16-P and B16-R cells were subjected to irradiation or sham-irradiation, and then incubated in medium for 48 h. Irradiated tumor cells were stained with propidium iodide (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA), followed by flow cytometry.

RNA isolation and sequencing

Tumor cells or tumor tissue samples isolated 7 days after the first dose of irradiation from the irradiated and control groups were treated with TRIzol (Sangon, Shanghai, China) for total RNA extraction. To construct Illumina sequencing libraries, a total of 2 µg RNA per sample was used as the input material for the RNA samples. Sequencing libraries were generated using the VAHTSTM mRNA-seq V2 Library Prep Kit for Illumina following the manufacturer’s recommendations, and index codes were added to attribute sequences to each sample. The libraries were subsequently quantified and pooled. Paired-end sequencing of the libraries was performed on HiSeq XTen sequencers (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Normalized and log2-transformed TPM values from RNA-Seq based on the expectation maximization data were analysed using the Mann–Whitney test to determine the relative mRNA expression. The differentially expressed genes overlapped with ferroptosis-related genes from the ferroptosis database (http://www.zhounan.org/ferrdb). The STRING online database (http://string-db.org) and Cytoscape software were applied to predict molecular networks of the top ten hub genes among 27 DEGs. The sequencing data of B16-R, MC38-R, B16-P, and MC38-R cell lines are publicly available on the website (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/). The accession number is NGDC: PRJCA050807.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted using FastPure®Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit (Vazyme Biotech), and cDNA was synthesized with PrimeScript™RT Master Mix (TaKaRa, Dalian, China). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions of Green®Premix Ex Taq™ II (TaKaRa). Ct values of the target gene were normalized to those of Gapdh, and the relative mRNA expression of genes was calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method. Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as the endogenous reference gene for normalization. The primers used are listed in Tables S4 and S5.

Colony formation assay

Cells were seeded in six-well plates and irradiated with a single dose of ionizing radiation. After irradiation, cells were cultured and allowed to grow for 10–14 days, with the medium replaced every 2–3 days. Colonies were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with crystal violet. The number of colonies containing more than 50 cells was counted under a microscope to evaluate the clonogenic survival of the radioresistant cells.

Lipid peroxidation assay

B16-P/B16-R, MC38-P/MC38-R, and A549-P/A549-R were subjected to irradiation or sham-irradiation, and then incubated in media for an additional 48 h, respectively. B16-P cells were subjected to irradiation and then incubated in media containing liproxstatin-1. Cells were stained with 5 μM BODIPY™ 581/591 C11 dye (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to assess lipid peroxidation via flow cytometry.

Labile iron pool assays

The irradiated and control tumor cells were washed three times with HBSS, followed by the addition of 1 μM FerroOrange (Dojindo Laboratories) for 30 min. The relative levels of cytosolic labile iron pool were measured by flow cytometry.

Measurement of glutamate in cell supernatant

The concentration of glutamate in the cell supernatant of irradiated and control tumor cells was detected by Glutamate Assay Kit-WST (#G269, Dojindo Laboratories). Briefly, cell supernatant or a glutamate standard was added to a 96-well microplate; the working solution was then added to each well. After incubating the microplate at 37 °C for 30 min, the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using SpectraMax® i3x.

T cell activation by irradiated tumor cells

B16-P and B16-R cells were subjected to irradiation and then incubated in media for 24 h. T cells were isolated from the spleen of OT-I mice using the EasySep™ Mouse T cell Enrichment Kit (STEMCELL Technologies, Vancouver, Canada). Subsequently, the irradiated and control cells were cocultured with OT-I T cells at a ratio of 1:0 or 1:80 for 3 days, respectively. The percentages of Treg cells (CD4+Foxp3+) were analyzed by flow cytometry. Additionally, T cells were treated with 10 μM sorafenib in culture medium for 48 h, after which the levels of apoptosis and differentiation were assessed.

Flow cytometry

The irradiated and control tumor cells were stained as described above. Anti-Ferritin antibody (#ab75973,1.36 μg/mL, abcam, Cambridge, UK) and anti-xCT antibody (#ab307601, 10 μg/mL,abcam) were used as primary antibodies to detect the expression of Fth1 and Slc7a11 in tumor cells, followed by the staining of Alexa Fluor 647 conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (#A-21244, 3 µg/mL, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Single-cell populations were isolated from fresh tumor tissue using a Gentle MACS mechanical dissociator in the presence of lysis buffers (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). Cells were blocked with anti-mouse CD16/32 (#101319, 10 μg/mL, BioLegend, CA, USA), and then stained with antibodies against mouse CD3e (clone 17A2, #17-0032-82), CD45 (clone 30-F11, #48-0451-82), CD4 (clone GK1.5, #46-0041-82), CD8 (clone 53-6.7, #11-0081-85) for 30 min. After washing and centrifuging in staining buffer, cells were fixed and permeabilized with Fixation/Permeabilization Kit (#00-5523-00, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and then performed intracellular staining of anti-Foxp3 (clone FJK-16S, #17-5773-82). The above flow cytometry antibodies were used at concentrations of 1–10 μg/mL. All the stained samples were acquired on a BD FACSVerse™ and analyzed using FlowJo software (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA).

ELISA

Tumor cells were subjected to irradiation and incubated in medium for 24 h, and then the cell supernatant was collected and stored at −80 °C for further analysis. For the in vivo experiments, tumor tissues were harvested on the indicated day after irradiation and placed in serum-free cold RPMI-1640 medium (1 mg of tissue per 10 ml of media) for 1 h, and then the tumor suspensions were centrifuged at 12,470×g for 5 min. The levels of TGF-β1 in cell supernatant and tumor suspensions were quantified by ELISA (#E-EL-0162, Elabscience Biotechnology, Wuhan, China).

Single-cell RNA-seq analysis

CD45-positive single cells were sorted from the tumor tissue of 4T1, B16-OVA, and MC38 by flow cytometry, and then analyzed using single-cell RNA-seq. For alignment and preprocessing, RawIllumina base calls (BCLs) were demultiplexed, and the resulting FASTQ files were aligned to the mm10 (GRCm38, Ensembl 93) murine reference genome and normalized for sequencing depth using 10× Genomics CellRanger [v 5.0.1] to generate expression matrices. 82.8–85.7% of reads mapped to the transcriptome across all samples. R and Python [v3.8.5] were used for all downstream analyses. Differentially expressed gene profiles were identified using the sc.tl.rank_genes_groups function from Scanpy [v1.10.3]. Besides our data, the scRNA-seq datasets of AB12 (GSE223998), CT-26 (GSE150970), and LL/2 (GSE155098) were utilized to quantify the proportions of lymphocytes and myeloid cells across different mouse tumor tissues. Harmony was employed to correct for batch effects, with the top 30 components selected for subsequent analysis54. The RunHarmony function was used to adjust for batch effects across datasets and samples. Subsequent analysis with Seurat identified differentially expressed genes via FindAllMarkers, and cell types were annotated based on lymphocytes (Cd3d, Cd79a, Cd79b, and Cd19) and myeloid cells (Itgax, Itgam).

Immune cell infiltration analysis

Immune Cell Abundance Identifier has recently been developed to estimate the abundance of 36 types of immune cells from murine gene expression data, including 11 T cell subgroups (ImmuCellAI-mouse, http://bioinfo.life.hust.edu.cn/ImmuCellAI-mouse/#!/)55. For each queried sample, the enrichment score of the total expression deviation of the signature gene set is calculated and assigned to each immune cell type using the single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) algorithm. To determine the proportion of Treg cell based on Bulk RNA-seq data, we utilized the online tool CIBERSORTx56 to create a reference feature matrix from our single-cell transcriptome dataset.

TCGA database analysis

The RNA-sequencing-based gene expression data and clinical data of SKCM, COAD, PAAD, and BRCA tumor samples were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). The raw TCGA cancer transcriptome and clinical databases required conversion of gene IDs from ENSG format to more universally recognizable gene names. Normal samples were excluded, and the data processing method applied was log2 (value + 1), ultimately producing a matrix of gene/sample expression levels. The expression matrix was scored for ferroptosis-resistance genes using the ssGSEA method. The normalized TGFB1 and FTH1 gene values were extracted from the expression matrix using CIBERSORT57. We first identified the intersection of ferroptosis resistance–related genes that were upregulated in both B16-R and MC38-R cells. This gene set consisted of 27 genes, including Fth1 and Slc7a11, which we defined as the radioresistance-associated gene set. We then converted this murine gene set into the corresponding human homologs, and used this gene set to calculate enrichment scores through ssGSEA and related enrichment analysis tools. These enrichment scores were previously referred to as “ferroptosis-resistance scores”. Spearman correlation analysis was performed to assess the association between the target gene and immune cell infiltration scores or ferroptosis-resistance score.

GEO database analysis

The gene expression profiles of responders and non-responders who underwent preoperative radiotherapy were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The GSE35452 dataset of patients with rectal adenocarcinoma (READ) included 24 responders and 22 non-responders. The GSE246613 dataset of patients with BRCA included 18 pathological complete responders. The RNA data for each case were scored for ferroptosis-resistance genes using the ssGSEA method.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA). All data were shown as the mean ± SD. Data were analyzed using a standard two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test for single comparisons and one-way ANOVA for multiple comparisons. The survival difference was analysed by the Wilcoxon and log-rank tests. A two-sided P value <0.05 indicated statistical significance. The results represent at least two experiments unless otherwise stated.
