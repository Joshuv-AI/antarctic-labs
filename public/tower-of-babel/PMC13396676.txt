Enhancing pulp regeneration through metabolic reprogramming of mature dental pulp stem cells mediated by GLUT1/HK2 mRNA delivery
Authors: Tiankai Di, Yuhan Liu, Zhili Li, Lulu Wang, Peiyi Li, Meng Nian, Dezhong Zhou, Lina Niu, Yujiang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The age-related decline in the pro-angiogenic capacity of mature dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs) severely limits pulp regeneration. We identify impaired glycolytic metabolism, driven by reduced glucose transporter type 1 (GLUT1) and hexokinase 2 (HK2) expression, as the key mechanism, as its inhibition diminished endothelial tube formation. To reverse this, we developed an aminolyzed highly branched poly(β-amino ester) (HBPA) as a vector for GLUT1/HK2 mRNA co-delivery, achieving >90% transfection efficiency with excellent biocompatibility. In vitro, conditioned medium from reprogrammed mature DPSCs resulted in a 2.0-fold increase in capillary length and a 2.3-fold increase in branch points, restoring angiogenic potential to levels equivalent to those of immature DPSCs. This efficacy translated robustly in vivo, where a tooth root slice model showed reprogrammed cells generated tissue with a vessel density of 10.2 vessels per mm2, 2.5-fold higher than that of untreated controls. Crucially, this level of vascularization was statistically indistinguishable from that achieved by the benchmark immature DPSCs. Our study demonstrates that HBPA-mediated metabolic reprogramming effectively rejuvenates mature DPSCs by restoring the “Metabolic-ECM-Angiogenesis Axis”, offering a translatable strategy for predictable, vascularized pulp regeneration.

Introduction

The dental pulp is a highly vascularized soft tissue enclosed within the tooth, playing vital roles in nutrient transport, sensory perception, and injury response.1 Pathologies arising from caries, trauma, or infection are among the most prevalent oral diseases. Although conventional root canal therapy effectively eradicates infection, it inevitably sacrifices tooth vitality, often leading to long-term complications such as increased brittleness and root fracture.2 Consequently, dental pulp regeneration has emerged as a transformative approach in endodontic therapy, representing a significant advancement from traditional root canal debridement towards biologically-based tissue regeneration.3,4

The goal of regenerative endodontics is to achieve functional regeneration of the pulp-dentin complex, rather than mere replacement with inert materials.5 However, regeneration of the dental pulp, particularly in mature permanent teeth, faces several major challenges: 1) the narrow apical foramina restrict blood supply and stem cell homing; 2) the complex root canal anatomy complicates infection control; 3) DPSCs derived from mature teeth exhibit marked functional decline, including reduced proliferative capacity, impaired multilineage differentiation potential, and diminished pro-angiogenic potential compared to those from younger donors.6–8 Furthermore, the transplantation of cell-scaffold constructs into the confined root canal space invariably creates a severe ischemic and hypoxic microenvironment due to the lack of immediate vascular anastomosis. Previous studies have demonstrated that this initial nutrient and oxygen deprivation leads to significant cell apoptosis and low survival rates before a functional vascular network can be established.9–11 Therefore, enhancing the metabolic adaptability of seed cells to survive this “hypoxic shock” is a prerequisite for successful tissue engraftment. As a result, current pulp regeneration therapies for mature permanent teeth yield suboptimal clinical outcomes, underscoring the urgent need for new strategies to reverse the functional degeneration of mature DPSCs.12

DPSCs are considered promising seed cells for pulp regeneration due to their accessibility, abundance, low immunogenicity, and multipotent differentiation capacity.13,14 They not only differentiate into odontoblast-like cells but also secrete extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins through paracrine mechanisms to promote angiogenesis and reinnervation.3 However, these regenerative capabilities are strongly influenced by donor age. DPSCs from immature permanent teeth (e.g., exfoliated deciduous teeth or orthodontically extracted teeth) exhibit superior self-renewal and pro-angiogenic capabilities, whereas those from mature teeth show replicative senescence and functional decline.7,8 This discrepancy is particularly evident under ischemic and hypoxic conditions: immature DPSCs maintain energy homeostasis through enhanced glycolysis, whereas mature DPSCs display mitochondrial dysfunction and inefficient glycolytic flux, leading to reduced survival and insufficient vascular support after transplantation.15–17 Current interventions such as hypoxic preconditioning (hpDPSCs) or cytokine mobilization (MDPSCs) partially improve cellular performance but remain technically complex, costly, and fail to address the core issue of metabolic dysfunction.18,19

Recent single-cell RNA sequencing and functional analyses revealed that immature DPSCs exhibit elevated glycolytic activity, driven primarily by high expression of glucose transporter type 1 (GLUT1) and hexokinase 2 (HK2), key enzymes controlling glycolytic flux. GLUT1 mediates glucose uptake, whereas HK2 catalyzes the first irreversible step of glycolysis.20,21 This enhanced glycolysis enables immature DPSCs to efficiently utilize glucose under hypoxic conditions, thereby supporting both energy production and angiogenic signaling through mechanisms such as lactate-mediated microenvironment modulation and histone lactylation.22–25 These findings suggest that targeting metabolic pathways, particularly by enhancing glycolytic flux, may offer an effective strategy to restore the function of mature DPSCs, with GLUT1 and HK2 serving as critical molecular targets.

mRNA-based therapeutics provide a versatile platform to modulate cellular metabolism by enabling transient and precise expression of target genes.26–29 Delivering mRNA encoding key glycolytic enzymes to DPSCs could reprogram their metabolic phenotype, improving survival and angiogenic function under hypoxic environments. However, the success of this approach critically depends on safe and efficient mRNA delivery vectors.30–34 Because mRNA molecules are large and inherently unstable, they cannot efficiently enter cells without reliable mRNA delivery vectors. While lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) have been widely adopted for mRNA vaccines,35 cationic polymers represent an attractive alternative due to their high tunability, scalability, and structural diversity.36–38 Among polymeric vectors, highly branched poly(β-amino ester) (HPAEs) have gained increasing attention for mRNA delivery owing to their three-dimensional (3D) architecture, chemical versatility, mild synthesis conditions, and biodegradability under physiological environments.39,40 Conventionally, HPAEs are synthesized via a “bottom-up” approach, in which amines are conjugated to acrylates, followed by end-capping.41,42 We recently established that excess end-capping amines induce aminolysis of terminal ester groups, generating aminolyzed HPAEs (HBPAs).43 HBPAs exhibit mRNA transfection efficiency comparable to or exceeding leading LNP formulations (e.g., Lipo MessengerMax, MC3, SM102, ALC-0315) and the benchmark polymeric reagent jetPEI. This performance highlights HBPAs as a promising and versatile platform for mRNA delivery to DPSCs.

In this study, we hypothesize that enhancing glycolytic flux via mRNA-mediated expression of GLUT1 and HK2 could restore the functional capacity of mature DPSCs. To test this, we developed an HBPA for the co-delivery of GLUT1 and HK2 mRNA, aiming to reprogram the metabolic phenotype of mature DPSCs and enhance their pro-angiogenic potential. HBPA was synthesized via a combination of “A2 + B3 + C2”- type Michael addition and a “top-down” aminolysis strategy. HBPA exhibits high mRNA-binding affinity and can effectively condense mRNA into polyplexes with high transfection efficiency and cytocompatibility. Co-delivery of GLUT1 and HK2 mRNA by HBPA markedly enhanced glycolytic activity, cell proliferation, ECM synthesis, and paracrine-mediated angiogenesis. Notably, in vivo transplantation demonstrated that metabolically reprogrammed DPSCs regained regenerative potential comparable to that of young cells, primarily through activation of the metabolism–ECM–angiogenesis axis. Collectively, this work establishes a clinically relevant strategy for dental pulp regeneration by targeting cellular metabolism and positions HBPA as a versatile, efficient, and biocompatible platform for mRNA delivery in tissue engineering.

Results

Glycolysis of DPSCs drives dental pulp vascular development

Our previous work demonstrated that DPSCs from immature permanent teeth promote pulp regeneration by establishing a pro-angiogenic microenvironment.3 However, effectively reversing the decline in pro-angiogenic function of mature DPSCs remains challenging because the specific molecular mechanisms governing this functional decline remain poorly defined.7,8To test this hypothesis, we first dissected the transcriptomic landscape of DPSCs from immature and mature dental pulp at single-cell resolution (Fig. 1a). GO analysis revealed that genes highly expressed in immature DPSCs were significantly enriched in pathways related to anaerobic respiration and ATP metabolism (Fig. 1b). This finding points toward a distinct metabolic signature, reminiscent of the Warburg effect, where cells favor glycolysis even in the presence of oxygen. This glycolytic metabolic reprogramming is now recognized not merely as an alternative energy strategy but as a central hub that provides essential biosynthetic precursors for rapid cell proliferation and ECM production, processes vital for tissue repair.44 To functionally validate this transcriptomic signature, we directly measured glycolytic flux using extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) assays. The results indicated that immature DPSCs possess significantly higher metabolic plasticity, exhibiting elevated basal glycolysis, glycolytic capacity, and glycolytic reserve compared to their mature counterparts (Fig. 1c–f). Specifically, immature DPSCs displayed a 2.2-fold higher rate of basal glycolysis (Fig. 1d). Furthermore, their metabolic adaptability was underscored by a 1.8-fold enhancement in glycolytic capacity and a 1.5-fold increase in glycolytic reserve, respectively (Fig. 1e, f). This enhanced glycolytic reserve is particularly crucial, as it equips the cells with the metabolic fitness to survive and function within the ischemic and hypoxic microenvironment characteristic of the early post-transplantation phase.45 Having established a strong correlation between the immature phenotype and a heightened glycolytic state, we next sought to determine if this metabolic adaptation was functionally indispensable for angiogenesis in vivo. We locally administered the glycolytic inhibitor 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG) in a model of dental pulp vascular development. Inhibition of glycolysis led to a marked reduction in the formation of organized vascular networks, as evidenced by significantly decreased CD31 expression and disorganized ECM deposition (Fig. 1g–i). Quantitatively, local administration of 2-DG resulted in a significant 31.2% reduction in FN1 expression and a 26.8% decrease in COL1A2 deposition, confirming that active glycolysis is indispensable for building the pro-angiogenic ECM scaffold (Fig. 1h, i). This provides direct evidence that glycolysis is not just an associated metabolic feature but essential for the pro-angiogenic potential of DPSCs. Glycolytic metabolism is functionally indispensable for the pro-angiogenic activity of immature DPSCs

Building on our finding that heightened glycolysis is a defining metabolic signature of immature DPSCs, we next sought to mechanistically connect this phenotype to their superior pro-angiogenic function, particularly under the challenging hypoxic conditions that mimic the early regenerative niche. To dissect this “metabolism-paracrine” axis, we established a co-culture system where DPSCs could signal to HUVECs under hypoxia for 24 h (Fig. 2a). This controlled setting allowed us to isolate the functional consequences of inhibiting DPSC metabolism on endothelial cell behavior. We first assessed the impact on HUVEC proliferation, a foundational step in angiogenesis. When glycolysis in immature DPSCs was blocked with 2-DG, the proliferative capacity of co-cultured HUVECs was significantly blunted. Cell cycle analysis confirmed this, revealing a marked decrease in the proportion of HUVECs entering the S/G2/M phases from 45.3% in the untreated immature group to just 21.8% in the immature + 2-DG group (Fig. 2b, c). This result highlights a critical metabolic crosstalk, where the glycolytic activity of DPSCs is essential for providing the paracrine signals or metabolic substrates required to fuel the high energetic and biosynthetic demands of endothelial cell division.46 Without this metabolic support from DPSCs, HUVECs cannot mount a robust proliferative response.Fig. 2Glycolytic metabolism is functionally required for the superior pro-angiogenic potential of immature DPSCs under hypoxia. a DPSCs and HUVECs were co-cultured in a hypoxic environment for 24 h. b, c Cell cycle analysis was utilized to compare the proliferative capacity of HUVECs. d–f The tube formation assay was performed by counting branch points and capillary length. g–i Scaffold-free 3D culture was used to detect FN and COL1A2 expression in DPSCs (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.000 1)

Beyond simple proliferation, angiogenesis requires the complex, coordinated morphogenesis of endothelial cells into stable vascular networks. We therefore evaluated this process using a tube formation assay. The results were compelling: glycolytic inhibition in immature DPSCs significantly blunted their ability to induce robust angiogenesis. Compared with the extensive, well-organized capillary-like networks induced by untreated immature DPSCs, the immature + 2-DG group exhibited only sparse and fragmented capillary-like structures, characterized by significantly fewer branch points and a marked reduction in total capillary length (Fig. 2d–f). This translated to a 38.8% reduction in branch points and a 40.9% decrease in total capillary length following 2-DG treatment. This demonstrates that the pro-angiogenic secretome of immature DPSCs is directly fueled by their glycolytic activity. This is consistent with current understanding, where glycolysis not only provides adenosine triphosphate (ATP) but also generates key signaling metabolites, such as lactate. Lactate is now recognized as a crucial signaling molecule that actively promotes angiogenesis by modulating the tumor microenvironment to support endothelial cell migration and vessel sprouting, a principle that directly translates to regenerative settings.47 We further investigated whether this metabolic dependency extends to the construction of the ECM, which provides the essential physical scaffold and signaling cues for nascent vessels. Utilizing a scaffold-free 3D culture model that promotes endogenous ECM deposition, we found that 2-DG treatment nullified the characteristic ECM-secretory advantage of immature DPSCs. The expression of key pro-angiogenic matrix proteins, fibronectin (FN1) and collagen type I alpha 2 (COL1A2), was reduced by 40.1% and 34.1%, respectively, bringing them down to levels comparable to those of their metabolically less active, mature counterparts (Fig. 2g–i). Taken together, the superior pro-angiogenic potential of immature DPSCs is not a single-factor phenomenon but a multi-faceted process tightly coupled to and dependent on their heightened glycolytic metabolism.

Upregulation of GLUT1 and HK2 is the molecular basis for enhanced glycolysis in immature DPSCs

Having established the functional necessity of glycolysis for the pro-angiogenic capacity of immature DPSCs, we next aimed to identify the specific molecular arbiters of this metabolic phenotype. A metabolic pathway’s flux is often governed by a few key rate-limiting enzymes, which serve as primary control points.48 To pinpoint these control points in the glycolytic pathway, we first examined the expression of key regulatory enzymes in immature versus mature dental pulp. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed a significant and selective upregulation of the glucose transporter GLUT1 and the first rate-limiting enzyme HK2 in immature pulp tissues compared to their mature counterparts (Fig. 3a). This finding was quantitatively validated, showing significantly elevated protein levels of both GLUT1 and HK2 in the immature tissues (Fig. 3b, c). Specifically, immature tissues exhibited a 1.3-fold increase in GLUT1 and a 1.5-fold higher HK2 protein expression compared to mature tissues (Fig. 3b, c). Crucially, this upregulation was highly selective. In sharp contrast, the expression of key downstream glycolytic enzymes, such as phosphofructokinase (PFKM) and pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2), showed no significant difference between the two groups (Fig. 3d, e). This specificity is mechanistically profound. GLUT1 and HK2 act as the rate-limiting enzymes of glycolysis, controlling glucose entry into the cell and its commitment to the pathway by irreversible phosphorylation. By tightly regulating these initial flux-controlling steps, cells can efficiently dictate the overall rate of glucose catabolism without altering the entire enzymatic machinery.49,50 This targeted adaptation is a highly efficient metabolic strategy. To confirm that this was an intrinsic property of the stem cells themselves rather than a feature of the bulk tissue microenvironment, we performed co-immunostaining on isolated DPSC populations. This analysis verified that immature DPSCs inherently express high levels of both GLUT1 and HK2 (Fig. 3f–h). Quantitative fluorescence analysis further confirmed this cell-autonomous signature, revealing a 1.6-fold upregulation of GLUT1 and a 1.6-fold increase in HK2 expression in immature DPSCs compared to their mature counterparts (Fig. 3f–h). Synthesis and characterization of HBPA

Given that GLUT1 and HK2 are upregulated in immature DPSCs, delivering GLUT1- and HK2-encoding mRNA represents a promising strategy to enhance dental pulp regeneration in mature DPSCs. However, achieving efficient and safe mRNA delivery remains a challenge, particularly when using cationic polymers.39,40,53 To maximize transfection efficiency, we synthesized HBPA using a combination of the “A2 + B3 + C2” type Michael addition and a “top-down” aminolysis strategy (Fig. 4a).43 Specifically, DD, TMPTA, 90, and C12 were reacted in DMSO at a feed ratio of 1.08:0.08:0.7:0.3, with a total monomer concentration of 300 mg/mL at 90 °C. The excess acrylate groups relative to amines favored the formation of an acrylate-terminated intermediate polymer (HBPA-ac), which was subsequently end-capped and aminolyzed using 122 to yield the final HBPA polymer. Our target weight average molecular weight (Mw) for HBPA was ~15 kg/mol, and GPC was employed to monitor polymer growth. After 12 h of reaction, the polymer reached an Mw of 10 400 g/mol with a dispersity (Ð) of 2.45 (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Table S2). Extending the reaction to 13 h slightly increased the Mw to 11 000 g/mol (Ð = 2.49). Further prolongation to 18 h caused a distinct leftward shift in the GPC trace, yielding an Mw of 13 200 g/mol (Ð = 2.74). After 20 h of polymerization, the Mw reached approximately 14 900 g/mol (Ð = 2.92). At this stage, the reaction mixture was diluted to 200 mg/mL, cooled to room temperature, and treated with excess 122 for simultaneous end-capping and aminolysis over 48 h. As expected, end-capping slightly reduced the Mw to 12 400 g/mol. Following stepwise precipitation to remove low and high molecular weight fractions, HBPA with an Mw of 11 300 g/mol and a Ð of 2.22 was obtained (Fig. 4c). The chemical structure and purity of HBPA were further confirmed by ¹H NMR, which showed all characteristic resonance peaks (Fig. 4d). Collectively, these results demonstrate that HBPA can be synthesized in a controlled manner with well-defined molecular weight and chemical composition, without gelation or excessive aminolysis. The combined “A2 + B3 + C2” Michael addition and “top-down” aminolysis approach thus presents a consistent strategy for generating HBPA polymers, paving the way for efficient GLUT1 and HK2 mRNA delivery to enhance dental pulp regeneration.Fig. 4Synthesis and characterization of HPBA. a Schematic illustration of HBPA synthesis via a combined “A2 + B3 + C2” type Michael addition and “top-down” aminolysis strategy. b GPC traces of the reaction mixture at different time points during polymerization. c GPC traces of HBPA after end-capping and purification. d 1H NMR spectrum of HBPA following end-capping and purification

HBPA mediates high mRNA transfection efficiency in DPSCs

To achieve efficient glycolytic metabolic reprogramming of mature DPSCs via mRNA delivery, the transfection parameters of HBPA—including the w/w ratio and mRNA dose—were systematically optimized in 96-well plates. The commercially available mRNA transfection reagent LipoMax was used as a positive control. As expected, no GFP-positive DPSCs were detected in the blank group after 24 h of incubation, confirming that naked mRNA cannot be effectively internalized by DPSCs to drive GFP expression. In contrast, GFP-positive DPSCs with strong fluorescence signals were observed in the LipoMax-treated group. Flow cytometry further confirmed a clear rightward shift in the histogram distribution of GFP-positive DPSCs population relative to the blank group, with a transfection efficiency of 32.17% (Fig. 5a–d). In contrast, HBPA demonstrated enhanced transfection efficiency compared to LipoMax. At a w/w ratio of 20:1 and an mRNA dose of 50 ng, HBPA produced a visibly higher number of GFP-positive DPSCs than LipoMax, yielding a transfection efficiency of 57.80%. Increasing the w/w ratio further enhanced efficiency, reaching 74.34% at 60:1—representing a 2.31-fold improvement over LipoMax. However, at 80:1, transfection efficiency declined slightly to 58.78%. mRNA dose also played a critical role. When the mRNA dose was increased to 100 ng, both the number of GFP-positive DPSCs and GFP fluorescence intensity increased. At this dose, HBPA exhibited up to 20.91% higher transfection efficiency across all tested w/w ratios (except 20:1) compared with 50 ng. The highest transfection efficiency of 90.02% was observed at a w/w ratio of 60:1. Importantly, HBPA maintained excellent biocompatibility, even at the highest w/w ratio of 80:1 and a 100 ng mRNA dose; cell viability remained above 95% (Fig. 5e, f). Physicochemical properties of HBPA and HBPA/mRNA polyplexes

To further elucidate the mechanism underlying the high mRNA transfection efficiency of HBPA, the key physicochemical properties of HBPA and HBPA/mRNA polyplexes were systematically investigated. A strong mRNA-binding affinity is a prerequisite for cationic polymers to efficiently condense mRNA into nanosized polyplexes. At a w/w ratio of 20:1, HBPA exhibited a high relative mRNA binding affinity of 96.3% (Fig. 6a). As the w/w ratio increased to 40:1, 60:1, and 80:1, HBPA consistently maintained a similarly high binding affinity, approaching 100%. Interestingly, even when the mRNA dose was increased while maintaining the same w/w ratios, HBPA showed comparable mRNA-binding performance. The strong mRNA-binding capacity of HBPA facilitated the effective condensation of mRNA into nanoscale HBPA/mRNA polyplexes. As shown in Fig. 6b, c, at the lowest w/w ratio of 20:1, the resulting polyplexes exhibited an average particle size of 66.3 nm. With increasing w/w ratios, particle size increased slightly but remained below 110 nm, even at the w/w ratio of 80:1. A similar size trend was observed when the mRNA dose was raised to 100 ng. All HBPA/mRNA polyplexes displayed mildly positive zeta potentials, even at the highest w/w ratio (Fig. 6d). TEM further confirmed that HBPA/mRNA polyplexes exhibited uniform, compact, and spherical morphologies across all tested w/w ratios and mRNA doses (Fig. 6e). Utility of HBPA-mediated GLUT1 and HK2 mRNA delivery in mature DPSCs

Having confirmed the superior mRNA transfection efficiency, we next evaluated its ability to deliver GLUT1 and HK2 mRNA to mature DPSCs, thereby transiently reprogramming their age-impaired metabolism toward a youthful, hyperglycolytic phenotype identified earlier. Transfection was performed at the optimized w/w ratio of 60:1 and mRNA dose of 100 ng, with all analyses conducted 24 h post-transfection. Successful upregulation of the target gene was first verified at the transcriptional level. As shown by RT-qPCR, delivery of GLUT1 mRNA by HBPA induced a striking 6.1-fold increase in GLUT1 transcript levels (Fig. 7a), which is 1.8-fold higher than that achieved with the commercial reagent LipoMax, highlighting the superiority of HBPA in GLUT1 mRNA delivery. This robust transcriptional upregulation translated directly into protein expression, with immunofluorescence showing a 2.0-fold increase in GLUT1 fluorescence intensity across the cell population (Fig. 7b, c). A comparable pattern is observed for HK2: HBPA delivery yielded a 4.7-fold increase in HK2 mRNA, outperforming LipoMax by 1.7-fold (Fig. 7d), and a 4.1-fold rise in HK2 protein levels (Fig. 7e, f). These findings were further corroborated by quantitative Western blot analysis, which revealed a 2.3-fold and 4.3-fold increase in GLUT1 and HK2 protein expression, respectively, relative to untreated controls (Fig. 7g–j). These results demonstrate the effectiveness of HBPA in delivering GLUT1 and HK2 mRNAs and achieving robust protein expression. As mRNA is non-integrating and provides only transient expression, this “hit-and-run” approach eliminates the risk of insertional mutagenesis while allowing precise, temporary modulation of cellular behavior.36 To characterize the therapeutic window of this strategy, we evaluated the temporal dynamics of target protein expression. Analysis revealed that GLUT1 and HK2 protein levels were significantly upregulated within 1 day post-transfection, stably maintained through Day 3, and gradually declined by Day 7 (Supplementary Fig. S9). Given that enhanced glycolysis directly fuels anabolic processes and promotes cell proliferation,54 we next assessed whether this engineered metabolic shift confers a functional advantage. Using the CCK-8 assay, we found that mature DPSCs transfected with either GLUT1 or HK2 mRNA exhibited significantly higher proliferation rates than untreated controls (Fig. 7k, l). Glycolytic metabolic reprogramming via co-delivery of GLUT1 and HK2 mRNA fully restores the pro-angiogenic capacity of mature DPSCs

While the initial experiments demonstrated that upregulating either GLUT1 or HK2 alone could partially rejuvenate mature DPSCs, achieving the hyperglycolytic phenotype characteristic of immature cells requires a more comprehensive metabolic intervention. We hypothesize that the simultaneous overexpression of both GLUT1 and HK2 would create a synergistic effect, thereby maximizing glycolytic flux and fully restoring the pro-angiogenic functions that diminish with age. To rigorously test this concept, we employed HBPA to co-deliver GLUT1 and HK2 mRNA into mature DPSCs.

The first and most critical test was to confirm that this genetic intervention successfully rewired the cells’ core metabolism, beginning with cellular glucose entry. Utilizing a fluorescent glucose analog (2-NBDG) uptake assay, we demonstrated that HBPA-mediated co-delivery of GLUT1 and HK2 mRNA significantly enhanced the glucose transport capacity of mature DPSCs compared to untreated controls (Supplementary Fig. S5). Following this confirmation of restored glycolytic entry, downstream metabolic flux was evaluated. ECAR assays provided a clear and definitive answer. Mature DPSCs co-transfected with GLUT1/HK2 mRNA exhibited a significant metabolic shift. Specifically, the reprogrammed group displayed a 3.1-fold increase in basal glycolysis and a 3.2-fold enhancement in glycolytic capacity compared with untreated mature cells (Fig. 8b, c, d). This synergistic effect was immediately evident compared with single-mRNA delivery. While individual transfection with GLUT1 or HK2 resulted in only moderate increases in basal glycolysis (by 2.1-fold and 1.9-fold, respectively), the dual mRNA delivery strategy achieved a superior 3.1-fold enhancement. To provide a comprehensive bioenergetic profile, we measured the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) in parallel to evaluate mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation. The results indicated that while GLUT1/HK2 co-overexpression significantly elevated glycolytic flux, it did not significantly alter basal respiration or mitochondrial ATP production (Supplementary Fig. S6). With the glycolytic activity restored, we next investigated its essential downstream consequences. As a fundamental measure of cellular rejuvenation and self-renewal capacity, a clonogenic formation assay confirmed that this glycolytic metabolic reprogramming significantly enhanced the self-renewal and colony-forming capacity of mature DPSCs (Fig. 8e, f). Specifically, co-transfection of GLUT1/HK2 led to a 2.3-fold increase in colony formation efficiency, fully restoring this critical capacity to a level comparable with that of immature DPSCs and significantly outperforming the moderate 1.8-fold increase observed with single mRNA transfection. Focusing on the key functional outputs required for angiogenesis, we assessed the construction of a pro-angiogenic ECM. Using a scaffold-free 3D culture model, we found that metabolic reprogramming led to a complete restoration of the cells’ ECM-secretory function. The expression of the critical pro-angiogenic matrix proteins FN1 and COL1A2 in reprogrammed mature DPSCs was elevated by 2.9-fold and 3.7-fold, respectively (Fig. 8g–i).3 The superiority of the GLUT1/HK2 dual-targeting strategy was again clear: single transfection of GLUT1 or HK2 induced a significant, yet incomplete, recovery (approximately a 2.0-fold increase for both), whereas co-transfection achieved a more robust elevation, respectively, fully restoring expression to the benchmark set by immature DPSCs. Furthermore, PCR analysis of HUVECs cultured with conditioned medium from the co-transfected group revealed significant upregulation of downstream integrin signaling pathways, including FAK/SRC, PI3K, and RHO (Fig. 8j). These results directly link the increased metabolic flux to the functional output of ECM synthesis, an energetically demanding anabolic process that relies heavily on glycolysis for both ATP supply and the anabolic precursors necessary for protein synthesis and modification.55 This rejuvenated “Metabolic-ECM” axis enhances neovascularization, characterized by its ability to orchestrate it. We tested this using a tube formation assay with conditioned medium from the various DPSC groups. While conditioned medium from untreated mature DPSCs failed to induce significant angiogenesis, the secretome from the metabolically reprogrammed mature DPSCs potently drove HUVECs to form extensive, complex, and highly branched capillary-like networks (Fig. 8k–m). The functional synergy was most pronounced in this assay. Single delivery of GLUT1 or HK2 only moderately improved angiogenic potential (yielding an approximate 1.4-fold increase in total capillary length), but the co-delivery of GLUT1 and HK2 unlocked the cells’ full capacity, driving a 2.1-fold increase in capillary length and a 2.1-fold increase in branch points. Crucially, these metrics were restored to levels that showed no significant difference from those induced by the immature DPSCs’ secretome. All these promising results demonstrated that the targeted co-delivery of GLUT1 and HK2 mRNA re-established the complete “Metabolic-ECM-Angiogenesis Axis” in mature DPSCs.

In vivo validation of glycolytic metabolic reprogramming via co-delivery of GLUT1 and HK2 mRNA for dental pulp regeneration

After establishing that transient metabolic reprogramming fully restores the pro-angiogenic capacity of mature DPSCs in vitro, we next evaluated its therapeutic efficacy in vivo using a clinically relevant tooth-root slice transplantation model in immunodeficient mice. Mature DPSCs were metabolically reprogrammed ex vivo by HPBA-mediated co-delivery of GLUT1 and HK2 mRNA, seeded into root slices, and transplanted subcutaneously for 4 weeks. Histological analysis by H&E staining revealed significant differences in regenerative outcome (Fig. 9a, b). The blank control group showed no tissue formation. The group with untreated mature DPSCs exhibited a poor outcome, with only sparse, disorganized connective tissue. In striking contrast, the metabolically reprogrammed DPSCs generated abundant, highly organized pulp-like tissue with a clear pulp-dentin interface, closely resembling the structure formed by immature DPSCs. Discussion

Dental pulp regeneration represents a significant area of research in dentistry, offering the potential to preserve tooth vitality and function beyond conventional endodontic treatments. The inherent regenerative capacity of dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs) has positioned them as key therapeutic agents, capable of secreting pro-angiogenic factors essential for tissue revascularization.3 However, clinical translation faces a fundamental limitation: DPSCs derived from mature permanent teeth exhibit significantly diminished pro-angiogenic capacity compared to those from immature permanent teeth.7 This age-related functional decline manifests as inadequate vascular network formation post-transplantation,58–60 ultimately compromising pulp tissue survival and functional restoration. While early studies attributed this disparity primarily to differential extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, emerging evidence suggests intrinsic metabolic alterations may underpin their enhanced regenerative capacity.3,7

The metabolic basis of DPSC heterogeneity has recently emerged as a critical regulatory axis. Single-cell transcriptomic analyses reveal that immature DPSCs exhibit pronounced enrichment in anaerobic respiration and ATP metabolism pathways, suggesting glycolytic flux may drive their regenerative competence.20 This aligns with the physiological hypoxia of the pulp microenvironment, where glycolysis serves as the primary energy source. The clinical relevance of this metabolic profile is underscored by the challenge of the subcutaneous tooth root slice model used in this study, which mimics the profound ischemic conditions of clinical transplantation. In this confined niche, donor cells must rely on anaerobic glycolysis to maintain ATP production and reactive oxygen species (ROS) homeostasis until host vessel recruitment occurs.9–11 Our results suggest that the inherent high expression of GLUT1 and HK2 in immature DPSCs acts as a natural “metabolic shield” against hypoxic stress. Beyond merely sustaining ATP production, heightened glycolysis in immature DPSCs generates lactate – a signaling molecule that modulates histone lactylation and activates pro-angiogenic genes.61–63 Crucially, this metabolic activity coordinates with ECM synthesis: glycolytic intermediates provide carbon skeletons for glycoprotein synthesis, enabling robust fibronectin deposition that stabilizes nascent vasculature.64 In addition to fulfilling the high energy demands of protein secretion, the enhanced glycolytic flux driven by GLUT1/HK2 overexpression may also support ECM synthesis by supplying essential carbon substrates. It is possible to speculate that key glycolytic intermediates, such as glucose-6-phosphate, are diverted into collateral biosynthetic pathways to serve as precursors for the production or glycosylation of matrix proteins. Recent metabolomic evidence from other tissue growth models has similarly demonstrated that glycolytic intermediates are actively rewired into anabolic reactions to sustain rapid protein synthesis.65 This convergence of bioenergetics and structural synthesis establishes what we term the “metabolic-ECM-angiogenesis axis”—a coordinated process where energy metabolism fuels the structural niche formation required to support vascular ingrowth. Identifying the rate-limiting “gatekeepers” of this axis is therefore essential for therapeutic intervention.

Targeted metabolic reprogramming faces dual technical barriers: identifying key molecular targets and achieving efficient intracellular delivery.29,66 The key to translating these metabolic insights into therapy lies in identifying specific, actionable molecular targets, thereby enabling precision targeting of rate-limiting checkpoints.Our mechanistic investigations revealed that the metabolic deficit in mature DPSCs is not a global downregulation of all glycolytic enzymes, but rather a bottleneck at the entry points of the pathway—specifically, the glucose transporter GLUT1 and the rate-limiting enzyme HK2. Therefore, the enhanced glycolytic flux in immature DPSCs is not a consequence of a generalized metabolic upregulation but rather a targeted enhancement of glucose uptake and its initial phosphorylation. This finding provides a clear and rational basis for our therapeutic approach. The selective upregulation of GLUT1 and HK2 identifies them as high-value, specific targets for glycolytic metabolic reprogramming. Rather than attempting a broad and potentially dysregulating metabolic shift, we can aim for a precise intervention. This validates our subsequent strategy of using mRNA delivery to specifically express these two key enzymes in mature DPSCs, with the goal of reinstating the youthful, pro-regenerative metabolic phenotype. By manipulating these key enzymes, we achieved a synergistic “push-pull” effect on glycolytic flux that maximized downstream functional outputs without the need for complex growth factor cocktails. Crucially, while promoting such metabolic shifts, it is imperative to ensure that the resulting angiogenesis is physiological rather than pathological. In the present study, immunofluorescence analysis of regenerated tissues showed no elevation of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β or TNF-α.67,68 This distinguishes physiological regenerative angiogenesis from inflammation-driven vascular branching, confirming that our metabolic reprogramming strategy restores the intrinsic pro-angiogenic capacity of DPSCs without provoking adverse immune responses.

The final challenge in clinical translation is the lack of safe and efficient delivery vectors capable of penetrating the dense ECM to reach primary stem cells. Delivering exogenous mRNA using conventional lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) often suffers from low transfection efficiency and cytotoxicity in hard-to-transfect stem cells, while viral vectors carry risks of genomic integration and insertional mutagenesis.69,70 Moreover, the dense ECM secreted by DPSCs further hinders nanoparticle penetration. A tailored delivery system must therefore balance high payload capacity, endosomal escape efficiency, and biocompatibility – requirements that existing platforms cannot meet. Our development of HBPA (aminolyzed highly branched poly(β-amino ester)) offers a promising solution. Synthesized via a combined “A2 + B3 + C2” Michael addition and “top-down” aminolysis strategy, HBPA possesses a unique highly branched architecture and tunable surface chemistry. This structure enables stable mRNA encapsulation while maintaining a hydrodynamic diameter <110 nm and near-neutral zeta potential, facilitating ECM penetration and cellular uptake without serum opsonization. Crucially, HBPA achieved >90% transfection efficiency in DPSCs with excellent biocompatibility, significantly outperforming commercial reagents like LipoMax. This “hit-and-run” capability of HBPA-mediated mRNA delivery allows for a transient pulse of GLUT1/HK2 expression sufficient to trigger the metabolic machinery and initiate tissue regeneration, avoiding the safety concerns of permanent genetic modification. Importantly, the temporal dynamics of this transient expression—peaking at day 1 and remaining stable through day 3—perfectly align with the critical therapeutic window for pulp regeneration.71,72 Following transplantation, the primary cause of cell failure is the severe ischemic microenvironment present before host vascular anastomosis can occur. Rapid metabolic adaptation during the first few days is paramount for survival.

This study integrates mechanistic discovery with nanotechnological innovation to overcome functional limitations of mature DPSCs. We hypothesize that HBPA-mediated co-delivery of GLUT1 and HK2 mRNA can metabolically reprogram mature DPSCs to recapitulate the pro-regenerative phenotype of immature counterparts. By restoring glycolytic flux, we aim to reactivate the “metabolic-ECM-angiogenesis axis” – enhancing ECM deposition, activating endothelial integrin signaling, and ultimately enabling robust vascularized pulp regeneration. Through stepwise validation from single-cell sequencing to in vivo transplantation models, we demonstrate: 1) Glycolysis as the mechanistic cornerstone of immature DPSCs’ vascularization capacity; 2) GLUT1/HK2 co-expression as the molecular target for glycolytic metabolic reprogramming; 3) HBPA as an optimal mRNA delivery platform overcoming biological barriers; and 4) Functional restoration of vascularized pulp-dentin complex regeneration using engineered mature DPSCs. This approach addresses the underlying mechanism by addressing the root cause of age-related DPSC dysfunction, offering a scalable, donor-independent solution for clinical pulp regeneration.

Synthesis and characterization of HBPA polymer

End-capping and aminolysis were initiated by adding excess 122 (950 mg, 4.8 mmol) and stirring for 48 h at room temperature. The final product was purified by fractional precipitation with diethyl ether/acetone mixtures to isolate the target molecular weight fraction.

Fabrication of HBPA/mRNA Nanocomplexes

Stock solutions of HBPA were prepared in anhydrous DMSO at 50 mg/mL. To generate polyplexes, both HBPA and mRNA were diluted in citrate buffer (10 mol/L, pH 4.4) to achieve weight ratios (w/w) ranging from 20:1 to 80:1. To evaluate the self-renewal capacity of DPSCs, a colony-forming assay was performed. DPSCs from different experimental groups were seeded at a low density of 100 cells per well into 6-well plates. The cells were cultured in α-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS for 14 days, with the medium replenished every 3 days to allow colony development. Subsequently, the colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 30 min. After rinsing with PBS, clusters were identified as colonies and quantified using an inverted microscope. The experiment was conducted in triplicate.

GelMA hydrogel encapsulation

DPSCs were encapsulated in GelMA hydrogels prepared by dissolving GelMA (30% w/v) in 0.25% LAP photoinitiator solution at 60–70 °C. The solution was sterilized (0.22 μm filter) and mixed with harvested DPSCs. The suspension was dispensed into molds and crosslinked by exposure to 405 nm light (10–30 s). Hydrogel microstructure (porosity and pore size) was characterized by Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM).

Viability staining in hydrogels

Viability of encapsulated DPSCs post-treatment (HBPA or LipoMax) was assessed using Calcein-AM/PI staining. Hydrogels were washed with assay buffer and incubated with Calcein-AM (1–2 μmol/L, 20–25 min) and PI (5 μM, 5 min) in the dark. Samples were imaged immediately to distinguish live (green) and dead (red) cells.

In vivo dental pulp regeneration model

To evaluate regeneration, processed human dentin matrix blocks were filled with DPSC-laden GelMA hydrogels and implanted subcutaneously into the dorsum of nude mice (n = 10 per group). The study included five groups: (1) Mature DPSCs (negative control); (2) Mature DPSCs + HBPA/GLUT1; (3) Mature DPSCs + HBPA/HK2; (4) Mature DPSCs + HBPA/GLUT1/HK2 (dual delivery); and (5) Immature DPSCs (positive control). Dentin blocks were prepared by demineralization in graded EDTA. After 4 weeks, implants were harvested, fixed, and processed for H&E and immunofluorescence staining to assess tissue architecture and vascularization.
