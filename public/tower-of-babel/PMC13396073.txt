Observational Retrospective Cohort of Patient Support Program Data on Practical Experiences of Treatment with Foslevodopa/Foscarbidopa in Advanced Parkinson’s Disease: The ORCHESTRA Study
Authors: Tobias Warnecke, Anton Adriaan Van der Plas, Petra Schwingenschuh, Anders Johansson, Mihaela Simu, Marie O’Meara, Resmi Gupta, Bjoern Fritz, Lars Bergmann, Juan Carlos Parra, Martin Südmeyer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionFoslevodopa/foscarbidopa (LDp/CDp) is a continuous 24-h subcutaneous infusion therapy for advanced Parkinson’s disease (aPD). Clinical trials have demonstrated the efficacy and safety of LDp/CDp; however, real-world evidence is limited.MethodsThis retrospective multi-country cohort study used data recorded by AbbVie’s patient support program (PSP) nurses during routine interactions with patients with aPD on LDp/CDp (December 2023–February 2025). Patients were followed until earliest of treatment discontinuation or database lock. Patients who started therapy during the study period were included without a pre-defined follow-up duration or standardized scheduled visits.ResultsA total of 2590 patients (38.3% female) were followed on average for 141.1 days. During follow-up, mean LDp/CDp base infusion rate was 0.36 (SD = 0.14) mL/h, equivalent to 62.0 (SD = 23.6) mg/h. During the optimization period (mean = 5.4 days), infusion rates generally increased from initial recorded dose to optimization, then remained stable. Among participants with available effectiveness data, 60.0% and 83.0% reported 0 h/day of “Off” and dyskinesia symptoms, respectively, in the first month post-optimization; with similar observations over time. 61.1% of patients were assessed as handling the pump well and 80.5% as applying skin care well during the optimization period; both increased post-optimization. The most common reason for discontinuation was infusion site events (5.1%). Exploratory results indicated that younger patients (< 65 years) with shorter disease duration (< 10 years), managed the system better and had more favorable discontinuation outcomes compared to the total population.ConclusionThis study describes real-world use of LDp/CDp in patients with aPD demonstrating that a stable infusion rate was achieved within 5.4 days and provides initial observations on effectiveness. Data completeness varied substantially across variables due to the non-mandatory nature of data collection within the PSP and variable follow up duration. Ongoing efforts to improve data completeness and standardization will support future analyses and enhance understanding of real-world use of LDp/CDp and patient’s experience.Clinical Trial RegistrationThe study is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov under NCT06937034.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40120-026-00978-0.

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? The efficacy and safety of foslevodopa/foscarbidopa (LDp/CDp) in patients with advanced Parkinson’s disease (aPD) has been demonstrated in recent clinical research; however, real-world evidence remains limited. This study of the largest cohort of patients with aPD treated with LDp/CDp and enrolled in a patient support program (PSP) in five European countries provides insights into the real-world management of LDp/CDp and LDp/CDp infusion settings.Many patients achieved dose optimization rapidly, and LDp/CDp infusion rates remained relatively stable in the months following optimization. During and following optimization, the percentage of patients reporting 0 h per day of dyskinesia and “Off” time increased. What was learned from the study? Patients most frequently discontinued therapy due to infusion site events, adverse events, unmet expectations, challenges with the device, and other miscellaneous reasons. An exploratory analysis showed that younger patients (< 65 years) with shorter PD duration had more favorable discontinuation outcomes compared to the total population.Patients received support and education from PSP nurses, including training on how to use the system, during calls and visits (the majority of which took place before or soon after starting the therapy). During and following optimization, the number of patients who were assessed as handling the device and applying skin care well increased.Data from the PSP provide insights into the real-world management of LDp/CDp for aPD. Continuous efforts are being made to improve data completeness and standardization and to apply what is learnt to enhance the patient’s experience.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a neurodegenerative disorder with increasing incidence and prevalence as populations age [1, 2]. As the disease progresses to advanced PD (aPD), the effectiveness of oral medication usually diminishes, leading to inadequate symptom control and impaired quality of life [3, 4]. Despite these challenges, patients are often managed for too long with oral and transdermal regimens, highlighting the need for other therapeutic avenues to achieve better round-the-clock symptom management. Foslevodopa/foscarbidopa (LDp/CDp), a combination of levodopa and carbidopa prodrugs, is a device-aided therapy (DAT) that is available as a highly soluble formulation (240 mg/mL and 12 mg/mL) for continuous subcutaneous infusion (CSCI) via a pump over 24 h [5]. Subcutaneous administration of LDp/CDp offers a significantly less invasive approach compared to deep brain stimulation (DBS) and continuous intestinal levodopa infusion which require surgery, while also providing the wide range of levodopa doses required to adequately control motor fluctuations and dyskinesia over 24 h. Contrary to other continuous subcutaneous therapies [6–8], LDp/CDp replaces all oral levodopa administration.

Recent clinical research has demonstrated efficacy and safety of LDp/CDp among patients with aPD, including improvements in quality of life and sleep disturbance [9, 10]. In a 12-week clinical trial, and 52-week open-label safety study, LDp/CDp treatment resulted in significant improvements in motor function reduction in “Off” time [10] and increasing “On” time without troublesome dyskinesia [9]. Safety data indicated that LDp/CDp is well tolerated, with most adverse events (AEs) classified as non-serious and mild to moderate in severity [9–11]. As with other subcutaneous therapies used in aPD [6–8], infusion site reactions were common. Similar to these results from clinical trials, interim findings from an ongoing 3-year multi-country, prospective, observational study (hereafter referred to as the ROSSINI study) demonstrated that LDp/CDp provided statistically significant, clinically meaningful improvements in “Off” time, dyskinesia time, motor symptoms, sleep disturbance, PD-related pain, and quality of life within the first 6 months of treatment, and has an acceptable safety profile in routine clinical practice [12].

In 2023, AbbVie initiated a patient support program (PSP) with the goal of providing timely and effective support for patients treated with LDp/CDp. This support aims to help patients understand and engage with their LDp/CDp treatment plan, build confidence in self-management of the LDp/CDp delivery system, and complement the care provided by their healthcare practitioners (HCPs). A framework was established to facilitate use of data collected for the purposes of the PSP by the PSP nurses to be used in secondary scientific research. Given the limited real-world evidence (RWE) on LDp/CDp, this study aimed to use PSP data to describe the use of LDp/CDp, in particular dosing in routine clinical practice in a large patient cohort. In addition, the PSP offers a unique opportunity to capture practice-oriented insights not typically reported in clinical trials, including how patients and caregivers manage the system in routine care, practical aspects such as skin care management, and the level of PSP nurse support required. These data are particularly valuable as they represent the largest RWE dataset on LDp/CDp to date, and the first of its kind using PSP data. Additionally, more than 95% of patients receiving LDp/CDp in participating countries are enrolled in the PSP, supporting the representativeness of the study findings.

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate changes in LDp/CDp infusion settings throughout this treatment. Secondary objectives included describing the practical management of the system, effectiveness outcomes (e.g., “Off” time and dyskinesias), the support provided to patients via the PSP, treatment discontinuation, and safety-related outcomes. Additionally, these study objectives were examined in a subgroup of younger patients with a shorter disease duration. An exploratory objective was to examine predictors of permanent discontinuation of LDp/CDp among study participants.

Methods

Study Design and Data Source

This retrospective multi-country cohort study used longitudinal data from the AbbVie’s ongoing PSP for patients with aPD treated with LDp/CDp. All data were recorded by PSP nurses with the primary purpose of supporting patients during routine interactions. All countries have a PSP implementation plan and roadmap with planned visit and call schedules, the majority of which are in the first 10 weeks; however, there are variations across patients, centers, and countries, and, consequently, there was no standardized schedule for data collection, and the timing of data capture depended on PSP nurse visits.

Data included sociodemographic information, infusion settings, management of the pump, effectiveness, patient support, discontinuation, and safety.

Patients provided informed consent to be enrolled in the PSP and agreed to the secondary use of their de-identified data for research and publication purposes. The PSP operates in addition to routine clinical practice; physician visits followed standard of care within each center and country and were not influenced by PSP participation. All data included in this analysis were collected through PSP nurse interactions, while information from physician visits was not systematically captured. The study period for the current analysis spanned from December 2023 (when the product first became available in some European countries) to February 2025.

Study Population and Eligibility Criteria

All patients diagnosed with idiopathic PD who were prescribed LDp/CDp in routine clinical practice and consented to be enrolled in the PSP were included in the study. Additionally, patients had to be ≥ 18 years old, have received LDp/CDp therapy during the study period, and enrolled in the PSP in Austria, Germany, the Netherlands, Romania, or Sweden. New patients were continuously included throughout the study period and followed from the start of LDp/CDp therapy until the earliest of discontinuation of treatment or database lock.

LDp/CDp Treatment Dosing

The planned therapy start date, as recorded by a PSP nurse, was considered to be the patient’s “index date”. If the patient’s therapy start date is missing, if the initial recorded dose occurs before the planned therapy start date, or if more than 3 weeks passes after the therapy start date without an initial recorded dose, the index date is instead set to the date of the initial recorded dose. The LDp/CDp pump is programmed with a standard hourly infusion rate which can be administered continuously over a 24-h period, termed the “base” rate setting. The continuous infusion rate varies between 0.15 and 1.04 mL/h with rate adjustments of at least 0.01 mL/h, and is adjusted by the healthcare professional (HCP) according to clinical response [5]. “Initial recorded dose” is defined as the first recorded base rate of LDp/CDp infusion; depending on country-specific practices, this may have taken place in a home, nursing home, hospital (inpatient), or outpatient clinic. In addition to the “base” rate setting, the infusion pump allows for two alternative infusion rate options to be programmed (“low” and “high”). These alternative infusion rates are enabled by an HCP or nurse and can be selected by patients to accommodate changes in functional demand, such as lowering the dosage at night or increasing the dose for prolonged intense activity [5]. A patient’s LDp/CDp infusion settings typically evolve over time, and HCPs or nurses adjust the pump settings to achieve the optimal clinical response. Patients may also self-administer extra doses to manage acute “Off” symptoms experienced during continuous infusion [5].

Consistent with the clinical trials [10], optimization was defined as no base rate dose modifications for at least 15 days following the last dose modification. Month 1 after optimization refers to the 30-day interval following the date of optimization, and each subsequent month is based on rolling 30-day intervals.

Measures

Sociodemographic and Clinical Characteristics

Baseline variables (assessed at or before index date) include age, gender, living situation (e.g., married), care partner support, time since PD diagnosis, and prior DAT use.

Infusion Rates and Additional Infusion Settings

Includes location of initial recorded dose, daily infusion period (24 h vs. 16 h); base, low, and high mean hourly infusion rates (in milliliters and equivalent dose in milligrams); number of vials per day; and number of extra doses per day and night.

Practical Management of System

Includes whether the patient plans to or is operating the infusion pump independently (i.e., general device handling and adjusting infusion rates themselves, as opposed to the pump being operated by their spouse, carer, HCP, or other individual), as well as an assessment of the patient’s ease of handling the pump, ability to apply skin care, and frequency of cannula change.

Effectiveness

Effectiveness was defined using two patient-reported metrics: (1) number of hours of “Off” time per day and (2) number of hours of dyskinesia per day, with hours for each reported as: 0/0–2/2–4/ > 4 h. Patients were asked to report their current “Off” time and dyskinesia hours during routine PSP interactions; however, no predefined reference interval (e.g., single day, typical week) was specified, and data were collected without a standardized schedule.

Patient Support

As part of the PSP, patients receive education, training, and support from nurses in the form of face-to-face visits or calls (including phone and video calls).

Safety and Discontinuation

Occurrence of AEs was available for analysis, but detailed AE-level information (e.g., timing, severity, or relationship to treatment) was not systematically captured. In a separate data field, date and reason for permanent discontinuation are recorded, with discontinuation reasons selected from a predefined drop-down list that includes AE-related categories such as hallucinations, infusion-site events, deaths and other AEs.

The following data assumptions were applied for all variables except patient support metrics: if multiple values were recorded within a month, only the most recent was retained. For infusion rates (base/low/high), a new record was created by the nurse each time the dose changed; in the absence of a new record, the previous value was assumed to persist until treatment discontinuation or database lock. For other variables (e.g., extra doses per day/night, vials per day, pump management, patient support, and effectiveness measures), values were considered applicable only at the specific time point reported.

Statistical Analysis

All study variables were summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were reported as mean [standard deviation (SD)], median [interquartile range (IQR)], and range, whereas categorical variables were reported using counts and percentages. Results were reported among the subset of participants with available outcome data at the time point being reported; patients were not required to contribute a minimum number of data points for a given outcome to be included in those analyses as the number of interactions with a PSP nurse may not be the same for each patient/country. To aid in interpretation, the number of patients contributing data to each outcome at each reported time point is explicitly provided in the results text and/or table/figures. Results were reported overall, and separately among younger patients (aged < 65 years) with shorter disease duration (< 10 years).

The percentage difference in low versus base rate was calculated for each person at each timepoint by taking the low infusion rate minus the base infusion rate, dividing by the base infusion rate, and then multiplying by 100 to get the percentage difference. Similarly, the percentage difference in high versus base infusion rates was calculated by taking the high infusion rate minus the base infusion rate, dividing by the base infusion rate, and then multiplying by 100. The mean percentage differences were then averaged across all patients with available data at that time.

An exploratory analysis was conducted to assess the associations between patient characteristics at baseline (i.e., age, gender, care partner support, social living condition, and type of prior DAT), time-varying characteristics (i.e., monthly values for low versus base rate percentage difference of more than 30%, low versus base rate percentage difference of more than 50%, base rate of > 0.5ml/h, and more than 4 extra doses per day and night combined) and treatment discontinuation using univariate Cox proportional hazards models.

All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS (version 9.4).

Ethics

The study was conducted in accordance with the protocol and utilized a framework for conducting compliant secondary scientific research using data collected through a Patient Support Program. The study protocol and recruitment materials were approved by independent ethics committees or institutional review boards in Germany (reference number: Bo/20/2025-Studie-01), Sweden (reference number: 2025–02176-01), Austria (reference number: 1127/2025), and Romania (reference number: 7SNI/9Apr2025). Ethics approval was not required in the Netherlands. This study used de-identified patient data for compatible scientific research and publication purposes; this use is captured in the patient consent and privacy notice on enrollment into the PSP and additional patient consent was not required for the purposes of the study.

Results

A total of 2590 patients met the study eligibility criteria. The study population consisted of 38.3% female patients; mean (SD) age was 70.1 (9.0) and mean (SD) PD duration at index date was 12.5 (5.9) years (Table 1). Most patients (92.8%) with available data on prior DAT use entered the study without having used a DAT previously. All patients were part of a PSP in Germany (61.0%), followed by the Netherlands (14.2%), Austria (13.0%), Sweden (7.6%) and Romania (4.2%). The mean (SD) follow-up time was 141.1 (118.6) days, with 44.0% of patients followed-up for ≤ 3 months, 23.0% of patients followed-up for 3 to ≤ 6 months, 27% patients followed-up for 6 to ≤ 12 months, and 6% of patients followed up for > 12 months. New patients were continuously included throughout the study period. During the follow-up period, 2138 patients had available LDp/CDp base infusion rate data, and the mean (SD) base infusion rate was 0.36 (0.14) mL/h, equivalent to 62.0 (23.6) mg/h (Fig. 1). Mean base infusion rates increased from initial recorded dose to optimization (from 0.34 to 0.37 mL/h). 87% of patients with available base infusion rate data achieved treatment optimization (n = 1865); the mean time to optimization from the index date in this population was 5.4 days (Table 2). Data related to the initiation setting, i.e., hospital inpatient, and outpatient, was limited (n = 799 of whom treatment was optimized in n = 683). When stratified by initiation setting, the mean time to optimization was 5.5 days among patients who received their initial recorded dose as inpatients (n = 595) and 7.9 days among those initiated as outpatients (n = 88), for whom the mean number of visits to optimization was 3.8. In the subgroup analysis among younger patients with shorter disease duration (< 65 years and 10 years disease duration, n = 72) (Supplementary Tables 3–7), approximately two-thirds of the patients in this subgroup were from Germany (36.1%) or the Netherlands (30.6%). The mean (SD) base infusion rate over the study period in this subgroup was 0.36 (0.18) mL/h, while the low infusion rate was 0.23 (0.10) and the high infusion rate was 0.38 (0.16). In terms of effectiveness, similar observations were seen among the total population; for example, 55.6% reported 0 h of “Off” symptoms per day in the first month after optimization, and this percentage increased over time. For dyskinesia, 58.8% reported 0 h of dyskinesia symptoms per day during optimization, and this percentage increased to 81.5% in the first month after optimization. A higher proportion of younger patients with shorter disease duration reported planning to operate the pump independently on or before the index date (81.1% vs. 52.0% among total population). Discussion

This study provides insight into the management of LDp/CDp in routine clinical practice in a large and representative population of patients with aPD in five European countries. Patients enrolled in the PSP were continuously included throughout the study period and data were captured during routine interactions with the nurses rather than pre-defined scheduled visits. Patients were followed from the date of start of therapy until the earliest date of discontinuation of treatment or database lock; therefore, follow-up duration varied across patients. At the time of database lock, longer follow-up data were not yet available for many patients. Most patients achieved optimization rapidly and LDp/CDp infusion rates remained relatively stable in the months following optimization. Additionally, the majority of patients leveraged high and low infusion rates to meet their needs. Many patients reported zero hours of dyskinesia and “Off” time post-optimization. Through the PSP, patients received training and support via calls and visits from nurses, the majority of which took place before or soon after starting the therapy. Additionally, it was observed that the number of patients who were assessed as handling the device and applying skin care well increased during and following optimization. The most common reasons for permanently discontinuing therapy were infusion site events, AEs non-specified, unmet expectations, and challenges with device management and acceptance.

This study provides important insights into the LDp/CDp initiation and optimization process in routine clinical practice. Similar to other therapeutic approaches for PD [13], a patient’s initial LDp/CDp infusion rate may require adjustments to achieve optimal symptomatic control. In clinical trials [9, 10], optimization was considered complete when no changes to the base infusion rate were made for at least 15 days. The same definition was applied in this study; however, it is important to note that there may also be other clinical reasons to maintain a stable dose. The average time from index date to optimization was 5.4 days. LDp/ CDp can be initiated in both inpatient and outpatient settings depending on the health system and also HCP and patient preference. When stratified by initiation setting, time to optimization was shorter among inpatients compared to outpatients (5.5 days for inpatients vs. 7.9 days for outpatients), which may reflect the ability for more rapid dose adjustments in inpatient settings. Thus, findings related to time to optimization in the overall population should be interpreted in the context of the substantially larger proportion of patients initiated as inpatients in this study (which is representative of standard of care in Germany), as well as the incomplete information on initiation location for all patients. Among the patients receiving LDp/CDp in an outpatient setting, optimization was achieved via an average of 3.8 visits, consistent with clinical trial observations, where the mean number (SD) of visits for optimization was 2.4 (1.5)–3.5 (2.5) [9, 10]. This relatively short pathway to LDp/CDp optimization suggests that patients with aPD can achieve optimal symptomatic control efficiently, which may lead to faster improvements in their quality of life and daily functioning and reduce the burden on both patients and healthcare providers.

Base infusion rates generally increased from initial recorded dose to optimization. This is consistent with observations in other real-world datasets [12, 14, 15]; once patients’ doses were optimized, the dose remained relatively stable throughout the study period. This post-optimization dose stability suggests that, once the optimal dose is reached, patients can maintain consistent therapeutic benefits with minimal adjustments, further enhancing the practicality and effectiveness of LDp/CDp therapy in real-world settings.

This study also provides novel real-world data on the use of alternative pump infusion settings, offering valuable information that could assist HCPs in tailoring LDp/CDp treatment plans for their patients [16]. Almost all patients had low and high infusion rates programmed, suggesting that these alternative infusion rates are important tools for patients to accommodate changes in functional demand beyond what the base infusion rate can offer. The low rate, typically delivered at night, provides an effective dose to control nighttime and early morning symptoms without causing bothersome dyskinesias, hallucinations, or other adverse events. Conversely, the higher infusion rate may be beneficial during periods of planned high activity (e.g., exercise) or when patients feel underdosed [16]. The percentage differences in low versus base rates (− 28%) and high versus base rates (+ 8%) observed in this study were very similar to those in the ROSSINI study (− 28% and + 9%, respectively) [12], reinforcing the consistency of this study’s findings with other ongoing RWE studies. As the pump does not record the duration for which each rate is used in practice, we cannot directly compare the LEDD to that in other studies. However, the mean LEDD at month 6 for this study was estimated to be 1428 mg over 24 h (see assumptions calculations in Results). In the ROSSINI study [12], the LEDD at month 6 was relatively comparable, at 1491 mg over 24 h. In a single-center study, the reported LEDD was 1605 mg at 9 weeks following initiation of therapy [14]. In contrast, LEDD in clinical trial settings appears to be higher; the mean dose at week 52 in an open-label safety study was 1859.8 mg (SD: 717.3) [9]. This difference in LEDD may be attributed to the more flexible dosing in real-world settings, particularly regarding nighttime infusion rates.

Consistent with prior research [9, 12], including previous clinical trials demonstrating the efficacy of LDp/CDp and real-world publications demonstrating its effectiveness, this study further contributes with observations regarding real-world effectiveness of LDp/CDp in a subset of patients, a high number of whom reported 0 h of “Off” symptoms and dyskinesia per day during the first month after optimization, with similar results reported in the subsequent months. However, interpretation of effectiveness outcomes should be approached with caution, as “Off” time and dyskinesia were recorded using categorical response options in the absence a defined time period and were subject to substantial missing data. In addition, the absence of baseline data, a comparator arm, short and variable follow-up, and potential survivorship bias (i.e., patients experiencing greater benefit may be more likely to remain on therapy and contribute follow-up data), limit causal interpretation of sustained improvements; consequently, reported outcomes reflect available observations in a subset of patients rather than standardized longitudinal change.

This study also investigated the practical use of the system including who operated or planned to operate the pump, difficulty in handling the device, and difficulty in applying skin care. Where information was available, more than half of patients operated or planned to operate the system themselves. Most patients handled the device and applied skin care well from the start, and their proficiency increased over time, similar to the learning curve observed in the phase 3 clinical trial [16]. During optimization, more than one-third of patients changed their cannula daily. Over time, more patients shifted to changing the cannula every 2 or 3 days, possibly due to becoming more stable on therapy and familiar with the system. This may offer additional convenience to patients. Overall, these findings indicate that the pump system is practical and manageable for patients.

While not directly tested in this study, it is possible that education and training provided to patients via the PSP may have contributed to these findings. Nearly 80% of patients had at least one visit on or before their initial recorded dose, where they received education on managing the system at initiation. In some countries, not all patients have face-to-face support from the PSP nurses during initiation in the hospital; therefore, the remaining patients were followed up for aftercare upon discharge from hospital. The majority of calls and visits for patients were in the first month post-optimization and decreased over time, highlighting the importance of support during the initiation period as patients are becoming familiar with the system.

The mean time from index date to discontinuation of LDp/CDp was 66.8 days, and the most common reasons for discontinuation were infusion site events, AEs non-specified, and patients’ expectations not being met. This is consistent with the timings and reasons for discontinuation reported from clinical trials and real-world evidence studies [9, 10, 12, 14, 15, 17], where the majority of discontinuations occurred within the initial weeks of therapy. Similarly, adverse events were the most commonly reported reason for discontinuation, with insufficient improvement from the patients’ perspective and challenges with the device cited. Additionally, approximately 80% of patients experienced an AE, consistent with reports from the phase 3 program, in which 89% of participants in the open-label trial and 80% in the double-blind phase reported at least one AE [7]. However, this rate is higher than that reported in other real-world datasets, where 55.2% of patients in ROSSINI [12] and 40% in a single-center experience from Germany reported at least one AE [14]. This may be attributed to differences in reporting between a PSP and routine clinical practice, where nurses are contractually obligated to report all AEs.

These results reaffirm the need for focused support for patients, particularly during the initial weeks of therapy, to set expectations and manage AEs, as needed. The PSP aims to facilitate this, providing a vehicle to ensure patients are well educated on managing the system and patients’ expectations are well understood. Patients with infusion rates > 0.5 ml/h (compared to < 0.5 ml/h) and those with a history of prior surgical DATs (compared to non-surgical DATs)—which may indicate a more advanced population—were associated with a higher risk of discontinuation in an exploratory analysis. However, these findings are based on univariate Cox proportional hazards regression models and should be interpreted with caution, as they do not account for potential confounding factors and, due to a number of exploratory comparisons, the possibility of false-positive findings cannot be excluded. A multivariate analysis is planned for subsequent analyses later this year to further assess these associations.

In a subgroup analysis among younger patients (< 65 years of age) with shorter disease duration (< 10 years), similar observations were seen for mean infusion rates and effectiveness data as in the total population. Interestingly, patients from Germany represented 61.0% of the total population but only 36.1% of this subgroup, while patients from the Netherlands represented 14.2% of the total population but 30.6% of the subgroup. A higher proportion of these younger patients with shorter disease duration reported operating the pump independently and were assessed as handling the system or applying skin care well. Additionally, discontinuation findings were generally more favorable (i.e., lower proportion of population discontinued and longer mean time to discontinuation). Given the small sample size (n = 72) and the potential for selection bias, these findings should be viewed as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Nonetheless, they align with ongoing clinical discussions regarding earlier introduction of device-aided therapies in aPD, and are consistent with prior evidence indicating greater benefit among patients with less advanced disease, including findings from the phase 3 program. For example, findings from the phase 3 program indicate that patients with less advanced disease had more pronounced improvements in their motor symptoms and quality of life [12, 18]. Together, these findings help generate hypotheses for future prospective studies, including evaluation of earlier LDp/CDp initiation, stratification by age and disease duration, incorporation of standardized clinical and patient-reported outcome measures, and assessment of long-term outcomes under controlled follow-up conditions.

Findings from this study suggest several practical considerations for HCPs initiating LDp/CDp in routine practice and are consistent with those highlighted in a recent review paper [19]. Early discontinuation most frequently occurred due to infusion site events, adverse events, unmet expectations, and challenges with device management, highlighting the importance of setting realistic expectations and organizing structured early follow-up, particularly during the first 2–3 months of therapy. Patients should be educated about potential infusion site events and mitigation strategies that adverse events are common during early treatment and that proactive communication and support may be required. It would be helpful to make patients and caregivers aware that there is a learning curve as they adapt to LDp/CDp therapy, also that dose adjustment is necessary and achieving the optimal dose may take time. Monitoring for early risks—such as difficulty handling the device or skin care, or unmet expectations regarding symptom control—may help identify patients who require intensified support or earlier clinical review.

An important limitation of this study is the nature of the PSP dataset, which reflects the secondary use of real-world data collected for clinical care rather than research purposes. Data collection was non-standardized, with non-mandatory fields, substantial missingness, and flexibility in the timing of variable recording. In addition, heterogeneity across countries—including differences in healthcare systems, PSP implementation, clinical practice patterns, and documentation practices—may introduce variability and limit comparability across regions. Follow-up duration also varied across patients and countries. Data were captured by PSP nurses rather than trained study investigators, and formal validation of individual nurse-entered data was not performed, which may introduce variability in data quality. Standardized baseline clinical assessments (e.g., MDS-UPDRS) and detailed information on concomitant anti-parkinsonian therapies at treatment initiation were not captured, limiting characterization of baseline disease severity and background treatment. Because of the complexities and inconsistencies of real-world data, analytic assumptions were necessary, such as presuming that the absence of new dose data indicated that the patient remained on the same dose, and that patients were optimized if no base rate changes were made for 15 days. However, every effort was made to check the validity of these assumptions with the PSP providers themselves. Ongoing efforts to enhance data completeness and standardization within the PSP are expected to support future analyses and further enhance understanding of real-world use of LDp/CDp and the patient’s experience. Despite these limitations, this study also has several strengths. First, the PSP enabled comprehensive data collection from the largest cohort to date, representative of patients and providing a holistic view of patient experiences, including infusion settings, unique insights related to the practical use of the system, including how patients and caregivers manage the system in routine care, and practical aspects such as skin care management, and support provided to patients via the PSP in a real-life setting. These findings will help guide the practical support provided to patients in particular during the early weeks of therapy. While the focus of this study is related to the practical elements, the phase 3 studies and ongoing ROSSINI observational study incorporate standardized clinical assessment scales, which provide additional insights into baseline disease characteristics and concomitant therapies. The inclusion of multiple countries allowed for a diverse understanding of the use and effectiveness of LDp/CDp across different healthcare systems, and the use of longitudinal data enabled the assessment of outcomes over time.

Conclusion

This first evaluation of data from a LDp/CDp PSP provides information on the real-world application of the therapy. The findings offer important insights related to LDp/CDp dosing and indicate that a stable infusion rate was achieved within 5–6 days, and provide initial observations on effectiveness. Importantly, this analysis addresses evidence gaps not typically captured in clinical trials, such as practical aspects and the level of PSP nurse support required. In addition, exploratory analyses provide hypothesis-generating observations regarding factors associated with treatment discontinuation and signals of more favorable outcomes among patients with less advanced disease, which warrant further evaluation in future studies. Ongoing efforts to improve data completeness and standardization will support future analyses and enhance our understanding of the patient experience.
