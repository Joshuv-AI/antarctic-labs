Statistical features of mergers between potential vorticity cutoffs
using a three-dimensional tracking algorithm considering various
connection types
Koryu Yamamoto1

· Keita Iga2

Received: 21 March 2026 / Accepted: 12 September 2026
© The Author(s) 2026

Abstract
A stratospheric air mass with high potential vorticity (PV) can intrude the troposphere and result in severe weather phenomena. The air mass can be observed as vortex-like high PV anomalies disconnected equatorward from the stratospheric
high-PV reservoir in the upper troposphere. This paper refers to this high-PV region as a PV cutoff. Motivated by case
studies, this study aims to investigate the importance of mergers of a PV cutoff with another cutoff on maintenance and
background conditions contributing to the mergers. For this purpose, we developed a new method to detect and track
three-dimensional PV cutoffs. This new method can constrain the evolution of cutoffs after a merger or a split. Firstly, we
investigated frequency of PV cutoffs and found the increasing linear trend in the number of events during 1958–2021. We
also revealed that they are located on poleward of the climatological strong wind zone in all seasons, whereas in summer
several local maxima are identified on equatorward. Secondly, we investigated the lifetime of PV cutoffs and obtained
results suggesting that mergers contribute significantly to the maintenance of PV cutoffs by extending their lifetime. We
also found out that the extension would be longer if they have similar areas. Lastly, we made composite maps of the
background field and showed that stronger cyclonic shear is favorable for PV cutoff mergers. This condition generally
comes from the subtropical jet, but in some extratropical areas in summer, the condition might be related to background
strong wind split from the jet.
Keywords Potential vorticity cutoff · Tracking algorithm · Merger · Maintenance mechanism · Background cyclonic
shear

through Rossby wave breaking (e.g., Bell and Bosart 1993;
Ndarana and Waugh 2010). The intruding air mass has a
higher potential vorticity (PV) than the surrounding air
because stratospheric air usually has a PV of > 2 PV Unit

the dangling high-PV air mass in the upper troposphere is

often associated with unstable weather conditions, such as
heavy precipitation (e.g., Knippertz and Martin 2007; Tsuji
and Takayabu 2019; Fan et al. 2023) and thunderstorms
(e.g., Nikaidou 1986; Mohr et al. 2020). In addition, many
high-PV anomalies are quasi-steady (Knippertz and Martin
2007) and keep the risk of such high-impact weather conditions (Yamamoto et al. 2024). The intruding air mass can be
visualized on a tropospheric isentropic surface and is typically associated with an upper tropospheric cyclone (i.e., a
cutoff low; e.g., Nieto et al. 2008; Kasuga et al. 2021) that
detaches from a deepened trough. Therefore, herein we refer
to this as a PV cutoff (Portmann et al. 2021). PV cutoffs can
be interpreted as being an enclosed region with a high PV
that has detached from a stratospheric reservoir (SR) on an
upper tropospheric isentropic surface (e.g., Bell and Keyser
1993).
Several studies have discussed the maintenance mechanism of cutoff lows and suggested they can be supplied with
high-PV through mergers with other nearby vortices. Price
and Vaughan (1993) showed that a high-PV air mass from the
stratosphere intruded a cutoff low with cyclonic circulation,
which contributed to re-intensification of the low. Portmann
et al. (2018) analyzed cutoff lows over Europe in April 2015
and showed that two cutoff lows merged and acquired a high
PV (see their Fig. 5). More recently, Yamamoto et al. (2024)
investigated a case over Europe in July 2021 and found out
that a PV cutoff which caused catastrophic heavy precipitation over Europe was maintained through the merger with
another PV cutoff. However, these insights have all been
based on case studies and it is important to consider more
examples to better understand the contribution of mergers
to PV cutoff maintenance. Therefore, this paper statistically
investigated how mergers affect the maintenance of PV cutoffs. We also aim to identify the background conditions that
are favorable for PV cutoff mergers.
From a statistical perspective, tracking individual PV
cutoffs with an appropriate method enables us to discuss not
only their lifecycle but also factors that may influence their
development or weaking. There are several methods for
identifying and tracking PV cutoffs by identifying 2-PVU
isolines as the dynamic tropopause and objectively defining
the cutoff region with 2-PVU contours on isentropic surfaces near the tropopause (e.g., Wernli and Sprenger 2007;
Kew et al. 2010). However, this approach has a limitation
in that isentropic surfaces of interest can vary markedly in
different seasons or locations. To mitigate this limitation,
recent studies such as Portmann et al. (2021) and Fischer et
al. (2022) have attempted to identify PV cutoffs or anomalies in three dimensions using multiple isentropic surfaces.
The first step of the method of Portmann et al. (2021)
was to identify PV cutoffs on each isentropic surface at
each time point. Portmann et al. (2021) then tracked the

PV cutoffs in two dimensions by calculating trajectories
on that isentropic surface. When a branching occurred in
the trajectory, they only held a single main track and the
other minor tracks were discarded for the sake of simplicity. As such, this method does not consider the branching
(i.e., mergers or splits). After all the two-dimensional tracks
had been obtained for the whole analysis period, they then
focused on the overlapping tracks on multiple surfaces and
collected all the two-dimensional tracks that were involved
in the overlapping tracks. They regarded these as being the
two-dimensional track. They then combined the two-dimensional tracks that overlap across multiple isentropic surfaces
to create a three-dimensional track. Given that the present
study considered mergers of PV cutoffs, it is important to
use a tracking method that can consider mergers. However,
no previous studies have proposed a method to account for
mergers. Therefore, we aim to identify PV cutoffs in three
dimensions using multiple isentropic surfaces and construct
a tracking method that can account for various connection
types, including mergers, which is set as one of the goals
of this study, since it is an important process for investigating the statistical features of mergers between PV cutoffs.
Statistical features including climatological distribution and
trend in the number of PV cutoffs obtained by the new tracking algorithms would be valuable among the research community and will also be discussed in this paper.
To summarize, this paper first aims to develop a new
three-dimensional tracking algorithm of PV cutoffs with
various connection types including mergers to answer following scientific questions: (i) What are the statistical features including their climatological distribution and trend in
the number of PV cutoffs obtained by the new algorithm?
(ii) Can we confirm that the merger contributes to the maintenance of PV cutoffs from the statistical perspective? (iii)
What is the favorable background condition for the merger?
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section
2 presents the data and introduces the new tracking method
that can consider various connection types. Section 3 shows
the climatological distribution and trends about PV cutoffs
and compares the merged and non-merged cases from the
perspective of their lifetime and background conditions.
Finally, a discussion and conclusions are presented in Sect.
4.

2.2 Methods

2.2.2 Tracking algorithm

2.2.1 Three-dimensional detection of PV cutoffs

We now consider tracking of the extracted PV cutoffs. In
this study, the three-dimensional PV cutoffs were tracked
by finding overlaps at the next time step (Kew et al. 2010;
Kasuga and Honda 2025). Considering that PV cutoffs can
move both horizontally and vertically, the overlaps are captured along all of the 𝑥𝑥, 𝑦𝑦 (horizontal), and 𝜃𝜃 (isentropic)
axes. A schematic of the tracking method used in the present
study is shown in Fig. 2. This method enabled us to capture
mergers (Fig. 2b), splits (Fig. 2c), and group connections
(Fig. 2d), as well as single connections (Fig. 2a), objectively
and automatically in three dimensions. A merger is a situation where multiple PV cutoffs are tracked to a single PV
cutoff at the next time step (Fig. 2b), and a split means that a
single PV cutoff is linked to multiple PV cutoffs at the next
time step (Fig. 2c). A single connection indicates that a single PV cutoff in the previous time step is tracked to a single
PV cutoff at the next time step (Fig. 2a). If multiple PV cutoffs at the previous time step are tracked to multiple PV cutoffs at the next time step through an overlap with each other,
we regarded that as a group connection (Fig. 2d). This tracking method is an extension of the neighbor enclosed area
tracking algorithm (Inatsu 2009; Inatsu and Amada 2013;
Satake et al. 2013) which is a two-dimensional method.
The tracking method proposed by Portmann et al. (2021)
identified PV cutoffs in three dimensions by detecting overlaps of two-dimensional tracking paths across multiple isentropic surfaces. Portmann et al. (2021) ignored the different
connection types (mergers, splits, and group connections)
for the sake of simplicity. In contrast, our method is simpler
in that we adopted overlapping, but it allows us to identify
complex shapes of PV cutoffs. In addition, our method considers various connection types and is advantageous for statistical investigations of mergers.

A PV cutoff is an enclosed region with a high PV that has
detached from the stratospheric reservoir (SR) on upper tropospheric isentropic surfaces (e.g., Bell and Keyser 1993),
and thus the SR should be excluded when identifying PV
cutoffs. We first extracted an enclosed region with a high
PV for each isentropic surface. The largest enclosed region
was considered to be the SR (Portmann et al. 2021) and the
regions over either the North Pole or South Pole were also
considered to be the SR (Wernli and Sprenger 2007). We
then examined the enclosed regions that were not classified
as the SR from high to low isentropic surfaces, and those
that dangle in three dimensions from the SR were identified as three-dimensional PV cutoffs. The PV can increase
in the lower layer of convective clouds due to diabatic heating maxima inside the clouds (e.g., Portmann et al. 2018).
Such enhanced high-PV areas should be excluded from our
dataset. Therefore, in this study, following Škerlak et al.

Our method focused on each isentropic surface, and the
tracking might be less accurate in cases where PV is not
conserved from a Lagrangian viewpoint due to diabatic
processes. Yamamoto et al. (2024) estimated the effect of
diabatic processes as being 1.0 PVU d− 1 (i.e., ∼0.1 PVU
[6 h]−1) at most (see their Figs. 11 and 12). Other previous
studies on cyclones around the tropopause obtained a similar intensity for diabatic effects (e.g., Cavallo and Hakim
2010; Grams et al. 2011; Fan et al. 2023). The magnitude
of the diabatic processes may not be important unless we
track objects with a PV of slightly more than 2 PVU on
lower isentropic surfaces. The area of such objects would
be small and may be excluded from the tracking dataset due
to the lower area limit (Sect. 2.2.4). For these reasons, we
consider diabatic processes to have an insignificant effect on
the accuracy of the present tracking method.
2.2.4 Parameters and characteristic quantities
Based on the results shown in Fig. S2, we set the lower limit
of the PV cutoff area to 2.5 × 105 km2. The upper limit was
set to 5.0 × 106 km2, following Portmann et al. (2021), where
the area is defined as the sum of the horizontal grid elements
included in the three-dimensional PV cutoff, with any horizontal grids that overlap along the vertical direction being
summed only once.
The lifetime of each event was defined as the period from
onset to decay. For example, considering a time interval of
6 h, if tracking is available over five time steps, the lifetime
is interpreted to be 24 h. If the tracking is successful only for
one time step, then the lifetime is defined as 0 h, but this PV

13

cutoff is included in the analysis unless we set a threshold
for the lifetime.
The center is determined as follows. For each isentropic
surface, we obtained the PV-weighted center of mass in the
region of the PV cutoff. If the PV cutoff consisted of only
one surface, the center on the surface is the center of the
PV cutoff. When the PV cutoff is over multiple surfaces,
its center is taken by calculating the average centers over
the surfaces, with no weighting function being used. This
method may introduce a bias when the difference in area of
enclosed high-PV areas between two adjacent surfaces is
large. However, since we use isentropic surfaces with the
interval of 5 K which can be enough small to resolve PV
cutoffs, the difference in area between two adjacent surfaces
might not be large and this bias would be minor.

3 Results
3.1 Trends and climatology
Using our method, 129,615 cases with lifetimes of ≥ 24 h
were detected, including 62,800 events in the NH and
66,815 events in the SH (over a period of 64 years). The
number of events identified in the present study is smaller
than that reported by Portmann et al. (2021), who found
152,615 cases over a 40-year period. The number of cases
in this study (about 2,025 cases per year) is about half that
of Portmann et al. (2021) (about 3,815 cases per year).
Although there are differences in dataset and its resolution,
the primary factor in the difference would be methodology.
The expected probability of experiencing a merger in cases
lasting 24 h and longer is 25.38% in the NH and 25.88%
in the SH, which suggests that many PV cutoffs undergo

Journal of the Meteorological Society of Japan

(2026) 104:36

mergers over their lifecycle and that we should cast a spotlight on various connections when tracking PV cutoffs. A
track before and after mergers or splits is considered as
a single track in this study, while Portmann et al. (2021)
counted it as separate events. In section S2, we present the
results of applying our tracking method to a case over Japan
in May 2012 (Fig. S3) and a case over Europe in July 2021
(Fig. S4).
We investigate the annual number of PV cutoffs and its
interannual variability and trend. Figure 3 shows the results.
A positive trend is observed in both the NH and SH, which
is consistent with several previous studies that demonstrated
linear trends in the number of cutoff lows identified based
on geopotential height (e.g., Muñoz et al. 2020; Reboita et
al. 2010). Muñoz et al. (2020) showed the linear trends in
both hemispheres from 1960 to 2017, and demonstrated that
the positive trend explained more than 58% and 33% of the
variance in the NH cutoff lows on 200- and 500-hPa surfaces, respectively. In the SH, these fractions were 60% and
64%. Our results show that the linear regression explains
only 6.4% of the variation in the NH and 2.9% in the SH,
which suggests that there are more factors, in addition to
the linear trends, that explain the variation in the number of
three-dimensional PV cutoffs obtained from multiple isentropic surfaces.

3.2 Comparison between merger and single
connection cases
To discuss the importance of mergers for PV cutoff maintenance (Yamamoto et al. 2024) from a statistical perspective, this subsection examines their lifetime and composite
maps. We used cases with centers located at 20°–70°N or
20°–65°S for the analysis, following Muñoz et al. (2020)
and Kasuga et al. (2021). In those two previous studies,
50°S was used as the southern limit, but in the present study
we extended it to 65°S to include the polar-front jet, based
on the jet distribution obtained by Koch et al. (2006). These
latitudinal limitations broadly correspond to types I (equatorward of the subtropical jet stream) and II (between the
subtropical and polar-front jet streams) defined by Portmann et al. (2021).
3.2.1 Lifetime
Figure 7 shows the frequency for each lifetime separated
into bins. The numbers and thick bars indicate the percentage of cases that include at least one merger during their
lifetime (left-hand vertical axis) and the lines indicate the
total number of cases (right-hand vertical axis). About 1%

a lifetime of ≥ 5 d. This means that the number of cases with
a long lifetime is considerably less than those with a shorter

4 Discussion and conclusions
We statistically investigated mergers of PV cutoffs by
extracting PV cutoffs that involved mergers from long-term
and global data. Previous PV cutoff tracking methods could
not examine mergers and splits (i.e., non-branching). Therefore, we have developed a new method to track PV cutoffs
in three dimensions, including mergers (i.e., branching), by
extending the branching two-dimensional cyclone-tracking
algorithm (Inatsu 2009; Inatsu and Amada 2013; Satake et
al. 2013) and the non-branching three-dimensional tracking

13

36

Page 14 of 17

method of PV cutoffs proposed by Portmann et al. (2021).
The answers for question (i) shown in Sect. 1 are summarized as follows. The frequency of PV cutoffs obtained by
our method is generally in agreement with that of Portmann
et al. (2021) (Fig. 4). The frequency of mergers is low just
above the climatological strong wind zone, but is high poleward in all seasons and both poleward and equatorward in
summer (Figs. 5 and 6). The equatorward local maxima of
mergers in summer cover around South Africa–Australia in
DJF and Mexico in JJA (Figs. 5a and c and 6a and c).
Nieto et al. (2008) compared the climatology of cutoff
lows identified using the PV-based method with cutoff lows
identified using the geopotential-based method. However,
the results by both methods did not agree entirely, as different physical quantities were used and the former method
used isentropic surfaces whereas the latter method used
isobaric surfaces. As other inconsistencies arising from the
different approaches, we highlighted the difference in interannual variability. In this study, we depicted the linear trend
in the number of cases and found the positive trend in our
64-year dataset, which is consistent with the geopotentialbased method. However, the linear regression explains the
variance less well than the results in geopotential-based cutoff lows (Muñoz et al. 2020). In addition to the fact that
the present method focuses on different physical quantities,
another reason for the inconsistency would be the concept
of the tracking method. The tracking method of Muñoz et
al. (2020) does not consider mergers nor splits (i.e., nonbranching). This study used multiple surfaces to detect
PV cutoffs, which should not be overlooked as one of the
potential reasons. A detailed investigation into the factors
of the interannual variability will be included in our future
work. Limited results are currently available regarding the
differences in characteristics of cutoff lows extracted using
methods based on different concepts (i.e., PV-based and
geopotential-based). Hence, comprehensive comparison is
essential to further understand the dynamics of cutoff lows.
Some recent studies have attempted to understand the threedimensional structure of cutoff lows from the perspective
of the geopotential (e.g., Barnes et al. 2021). Limbach et al.
(2012) introduced a more versatile algorithm to detect and
track three-dimensional features in atmosphere considering
mergers and splits. These results are like our approach in
that they adopted a three-dimensional perspective. Comparison with their algorithms might be our next task.
Using the tracking data obtained with our method, we
analyzed the lifetime of PV cutoffs with a focus on the form
of their connections [question (ii)]. We found that the group
with the longer lifetimes contains more cases that experience mergers (Fig. 7). Intuitively, as the lifetime gets longer, one would expect that the proportion of mergers would
also increase, because it is more likely that the PV cutoff

13

Journal of the Meteorological Society of Japan

(2026) 104:36

would approach other cutoffs. As such, we then classified
the events into two categories: (1) merger events in which
the PV cutoffs only merge with one other cutoff during their
lifetime; and (2) single events in which a PV cutoff neither
merges nor splits during its lifetime. We obtained the time
from merger to decay for merger event and also the period
of the entire lifetime of single events. We then compared
these two time periods (Fig. 8). The results suggest that,
after mergers, the merger events tend to last two times longer lifetimes (the average is around 1.0 days) than the whole
lifetime of single events (the average is around 0.5 days).
Therefore, these analyses suggest that mergers contribute
significantly to the maintenance of PV cutoffs by extending their lifetime. Yamamoto et al. (2024) investigated a
case over Europe in July 2021 and showed that the merger
contributed to PV cutoff maintenance, which is statistically
supported by the results obtained in the present study. We
also investigated the effect of difference in area between the
merging two PV cutoffs on the extension of their lifetime,
and found out that the extension would be longer if they
have similar areas. Especially, when the PV cutoff area is
≤ 7.5 × 105 km², the remaining duration tends to be longer
than the whole lifetime of single events (Tables 1 and 2).
Future research will use numerical simulations to confirm
whether a merger with another PV cutoff contributes to cutoff maintenance under idealized conditions. In addition, the
present tracking method can objectively and automatically
identify not only mergers but also splits. Therefore, it might
be an interesting topic as future work to investigate how
splits affect the lifecycle of PV cutoffs.
This study also investigated the conditions that are favorable for PV cutoffs approaching and merging [question (iii)].
For this analysis, we used merger and single events with
lifetimes of ≥ 24 h. We only used single events for which
another smaller PV cutoff was located nearby. We adopted
a statistical perspective and constructed composite maps of
the background field at representative time steps for each
group, dividing both hemispheres into several areas where a
lot of merger and single events were identified (Figs. 10 and
11 and S7–S8). Significant differences in zonal winds indicate the merger events are favored by strong cyclonic shear.
Therefore, zonal cyclonic shear in the background field is
one of the favorable conditions for PV cutoff merger events.
While it is suggested in many winter and summer areas that
this favorable condition is due to the subtropical jet, the
background zonal wind separated from the jet might affect
the condition in some extratropical areas in summer (i.e.,
areas D–E in DJF and areas C–D in JJA; Please see Figs. 9
and S6 or Table 3 for the definition of the areas). These areas
include South Africa–Australia and Mexico, respectively,
and these areas show high frequency of mergers equatorward of the climatologically strong wind zone (Figs. 5 and

Journal of the Meteorological Society of Japan

(2026) 104:36

6). We also investigated temporal evolution of background
zonal wind around the tropopause for the merger events and
found that the favorable background condition for mergers
is gradually prepared from 12 h prior to the merger (Figs. 12
and S9–S19).
Our two main conclusions on questions (ii) and (iii) presented in Sect. 1 (i.e., that mergers lengthen the lifetime
of PV cutoffs and that zonal cyclonic shear is favorable for
mergers) are robust across seasons and hemispheres (e.g.,
section S4). There appears to be a maintenance mechanism
related to atmospheric blocking with the perspective of the
absorption of PV with the same polarity. Yamazaki and Itoh
(2013a; b) discussed the maintenance of atmospheric blocking based on vortex–vortex interactions, in which vortices
(i.e., synoptic anticyclones in their context) with the same
polarity merge and supply anticyclonic PV to a blocking
anticyclone (i.e., an anticyclonic vortex). The vorticity field
induced by the blocking anticyclone promotes the attraction
of the synoptic anticyclone. The conditions that are favorable for mergers between PV cutoffs were identified by conducting analyses of the background, and thus the conditions
might involve different physical processes as compared with
Yamazaki and Itoh (2013a; b). During the process of attraction, background cyclonic shear is important for PV cutoffs
(this study), while Yamazaki and Itoh (2013a; b) showed
that the vorticity field induced by both vortices is important
for approaching of anticyclonic vortices. It is expected that
treating cyclones and anticyclones under the same conditions might provide further insights into the similarities or
differences between PV cutoffs and anticyclonic vortices.
It would not be negligible to assess the diabatic processes
(e.g., Yamamoto et al. 2024) that were not addressed in the
present study to tackle that question.
Hakim and Canavan (2005) investigated cyclones and
anticyclones around the Arctic region, where the jet influence is relatively small, and found that cyclones were more
persistent and stronger than anticyclones. Given that we
investigated the relationship between background conditions and mergers, it would be interesting to confirm the
findings of Hakim and Canavan (2005) in different latitudinal zones.
Finally, the present tracking method can also be applied
to other phenomena. Our tracking algorithm is based on
overlapping and thus is relatively simple. Its limitations are
discussed in Sect. 2.2.3, and additional empirical methods and conditions might be needed when the algorithm is
applied to other disturbances. A comparison of the applicability of our approach with other methods (e.g., Lakkis
et al. 2019; Pepler and Dowdy 2020) might lead to a better
understanding of the complex lifecycle of cyclones.
