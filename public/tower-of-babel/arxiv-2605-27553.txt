Economic Nonlinear Model Predictive Control for Microgrids with Generator Up and Downtime Constraints
Jürgen Gutekunst, Armin Nurkanovic, Ekaterina Kostina, Hans Georg Bock, Robert Scholz, Amer Mesanovic

Abstract
Recently there has been a lot of progress in the development of economic NMPC ( NMPC ) schemes for multistage OPF ( OPF ) problems. However, the additional inclusion of discrete decision variables to model generator runtimes and generator startup costs can amount to large scale MINLP that are computationally very challenging. This work investigates the practical approach that replaces the nonlinear AC ( AC ) power flow equations by convex quadratic approximations. In combination with the discrete generator dynamics this leads to a MIQCP ( MIQCP ) which is of significantly lower complexity and can be solved in reasonable time by off-the-shelf solvers such as CPLEX . We further show that simple terminal constraints are not sufficient to guarantee recursive feasibility of the NMPC scheme if constraints on generator runtime and on the number of generator startup events are present. To address this challenge we propose the use of additional time-coupled constraints and prove the resulting recursive feasibility property. Based on the assumption of periodic dissipativity of the underlying system we can prove stability of the proposed controller. To illustrate our results, we present simulations of a realistic 6-bus microgrid under different demand scenarios.

1
Introduction

With the increasing availability of renewable energy sources and energy storage
possibilities, the optimal economic operation of electrical microgrids is
becoming a more and more challenging and important task
(
Brinkman et al., 2021
;
Balke, 2014
)
.
The volatility of the renewable energy sources makes the treatment of economic
dispatch problems as single-stage problems difficult and it is necessary to
consider time-coupled multistage problems.
This way it is possible to consider generator ramping constraints and evolving energy
storage levels as well as time-varying power demands.
Furthermore, requirements such as minimal generator runtimes and generator
startup costs have to be considered and make the resulting mathematical
optimization problems even more challenging.

Early works on multistage
OPF
problems often used linearized versions of
the nonlinear power flow equations, e.g.
(
Ross and Kim, 1980
)
.
Linearized power flow equations often lack capturing effects of reactive
power and voltage magnitudes which are important for the stability of the
grids.
Multistage
OPF
that consider the full nonlinear power flow equations
are considered by
Chen et al., 2005
.
A more extensive review of multistage
OPF
and dynamic dispatch problems
can be found in
Xia and Elaiw, 2010
.
The incorporation of discrete generator requirements such as minimum runtime
constraints can be achieved using discrete decision variables, e.g. as presented
in
Olivares et al., 2014
;
Olivares et al., 2015
.

The rich body of multistage
OPF
research has also led to many
applications of online feedback control mechanisms such as
NMPC
.

Meyer-Hübner et al., 2017
analyze the application of an
NMPC

scheme for the optimal control of Germany’s electricity network and

Arnold and Andersson, 2010
investigates economic
MPC
(
MPC
) for a combined
electrical and gas network.
A major step in transferring the mature understanding of economic
NMPC
for
dissipative systems to multistage
OPF
problems is done by

Faulwasser and Engelmann, 2020
, where the authors apply a purely economic

NMPC
scheme without terminal constraints and prove a practical stability
property.

Many convex relaxations such as
SDP
(
SDP
)
(
Bai et al., 2008
)
,
SOC
(
SOC
)

(
Jabr, 2006
)
,
CDF
(
CDF
)
(
Farivar et al., 2011
)
and
QC
(
QC
)

(
Hijazi et al., 2016
)
promise a way to accurately and reliably approximate
the power flow equations while still being computationally tractable.

While there has been some work on economic
NMPC
for
OPF
problems and
on the handling of discrete generator requirements for optimal power dispatch,
the contribution of this work is the combination of both directions.
The goal is a feedback method that not only is based on solving a multistage

OPF
problem but also takes into account the discrete generator behavior
including runtime restrictions and startup costs.

Two major difficulties in
this context arise and are discussed
in this work. First, a direct formulation of the mathematical problems results
in large scale
MINLP
which are computationally prohibitively expensive to
solve, especially in the context of an
NMPC
application. To address this
issue we propose to use
the
QC
relaxation for the nonlinear power flow equations which results in an

MIQCP
which can be solved by powerful mixed-integer solvers such as

CPLEX

(
IBM ILOG CPLEX, 2019
)
. Second, the treatment of the discrete generator constraints in the

NMPC
context, in particular the runtime constraints and the bounds on the
number of startup events, requires a careful design of the
NMPC
scheme to
avoid recursive feasibility problems. We present a tailored set of path
constraints that guarantee recursive feasibility in this discrete setting
and are based on a precomputed periodic reference trajectory.

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this article is the first one that
simultaneously treats the multistage
OPF
problem using the
QC

relaxation and the discrete generator dispatch optimization while establishing
practical recursive feasibility for the resulting
NMPC
scheme.

Structure of the paper

-relaxation. Section

2
Nonlinear
AC
Power Flow and the QC Relaxation

Microgrid control involves multiple control loops that function on different
time scales. Stabilization and tracking control loops typically operate on a
time scale up to several seconds, as this is the time required for a microgrid
to return to a steady state after a disturbance or change

(
Nurkanovic et al., 2019
;
Scholz et al., 2020
)
. This work
focuses on the control loop aimed at achieving cost-optimal operation of
microgrids, which accounts for variations in renewable production and load, and
operates on a time scale of one to several days. Given the significant
difference in time scales between the controllers, we disregard the system’s
dynamic behavior on the short time scale and concentrate on its steady-state behavior. We consider
balanced systems, meaning all phases have the same current amplitudes.

2.1
Nonlinear
AC
Power Flow

In our description of balanced microgrids we follow the neat and compact
presentation in
Faulwasser and Engelmann, 2020
and
Faulwasser et al., 2018
.
The microgrid is modeled by
(\mathcal{N},\mathcal{G},\mathcal{B},Y)
where

\mathcal{N}=\{1,\ldots,N\}
is the set of buses/nodes,
\mathcal{G},\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{N}
are
the nodes connected to a generator resp. battery.
The electrical properties of the connections (lines) between the nodes are
described via the complex admittance matrix
Y=G+jB\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
where
G,B\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
denotes the conductances resp. the susceptances
of the lines. The diagonal elements are
Y_{ll}=Y_{l}+\sum_{j\neq l,j\in\mathcal{N}}Y_{lj}
with
Y_{l}
being the ground admittance connected to node

l
.
The state of the microgrid is described by the voltage magnitude

v_{l}
, the voltage phase
\theta_{l}
and the active and reactive power

p_{l},q_{l}
at each node
l\in\mathcal{N}
.
Any steady state of the microgrid has to satisfy the nonlinear
AC
power flow
equations

p_{l}=\sum_{m\in\mathcal{N}}p_{lm},\quad q_{l}=\sum_{m\in\mathcal{N}}q_{lm},

(1)

with

\displaystyle\begin{split}p_{lm}&:=v_{l}v_{m}\big(G_{lm}\cos(\theta_{lm})+B_{lm}\sin(\theta_{lm})\big),\\
q_{lm}&:=v_{l}v_{m}\big(G_{lm}\sin(\theta_{lm})-B_{lm}\cos(\theta_{lm})\big),\end{split}

(2)

(where we use the shorthand notation
\theta_{lm}:=\theta_{l}-\theta_{m}
)
for the active and reactive powers associated with the line

(l,m)\in\mathcal{N}\times\mathcal{N}
.
Furthermore at each node
l\in\mathcal{N}
, there is a (uncontrollable) demand of active and reactive power

p_{l}^{d},q_{l}^{d}
that has to be satisfied.
For each bus
l\in\mathcal{N}
the (controllable) generated power from a generator is denoted by

s_{l}^{g}=p_{l}^{g}+jq_{l}^{g}
and the power from a battery is denoted by

s_{l}^{b}=p_{l}^{b}+jq_{l}^{b}
.
In case there is no generator or storage present at the node, the corresponding
variables are zero.
The overall active and reactive power balance reads as

\begin{split}p_{l}&=p_{l}^{g}+p_{l}^{b}-p_{l}^{d},\\
q_{l}&=q_{l}^{g}+q_{l}^{b}-q_{l}^{d}\end{split}

(3)

and has to be satisfied at all nodes.
We collect the variables describing the grid into a set of algebraic state
variables

z=[p_{l},q_{l},v_{l},\theta_{l}]^{T}_{l\in\mathcal{N}}\in\mathbb{R}^{4N},

(4)

a set of (controllable) state variables

y=\big[[p_{l}^{g},q_{l}^{g}]^{T}_{l\in\mathcal{G}},[p_{l}^{b},q_{l}^{b}]^{T}_{l\in\mathcal{B}}\big]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\left\lvert\mathcal{G}\right\rvert+2\left\lvert\mathcal{B}\right\rvert},

(5)

and a set of external demand parameters

d=[p_{l}^{d},q_{l}^{d}]^{T}_{l\in\mathcal{N}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N}.

(6)

This allows us to conveniently express the nonlinear power flow equations
(
1
)
and the power balance equation (
3
) using functions

g_{ac}:\mathbb{R}^{4N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2N}
and

g_{bal}:\mathbb{R}^{2\left\lvert\mathcal{G}\right\rvert+2\left\lvert\mathcal{B}\right\rvert}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2N}
as

\displaystyle g_{ac}(z)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle g_{bal}(y,z,d)

\displaystyle=0.

Definition 1

(
AC
Power Flow Manifold)
.

We denote the set of solutions of the nonlinear
AC
-equations
(
1
) by

\mathcal{AC}:=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{4N}:g_{ac}(z)=0\}.

For a given demand
d\in\mathbb{R}^{2N}
we denote the set of admissible control
inputs as

Y^{ac}(d):=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{2\left\lvert\mathcal{G}\right\rvert+2\left\lvert\mathcal{B}\right\rvert}:\exists z\in\mathcal{AC}\text{ such that }g_{bal}(y,z,d)=0\}.

2.2
The
QC
Relaxation

In Section
3
we introduce a multistage
OPF
problem with discrete decision variables.
The fact that the nonlinear power flow constraint
g_{ac}(z)=0
has to be
satisfied at all time instants causes practical problems. As the condition

g_{ac}(z)=0
defines a nonlinear non-convex constraint, the resulting
OCP
(
OCP
)
will be a non-convex
MINLP
which is intrinsically hard to solve and makes

NMPC
applications highly nontrivial.
In theory such problems can be solved via a sequence of relaxed problems
using branch-and-bound methods
(
Dakin, 1965
;
Leyffer, 2001
)
.
Approaches particularly developed for
NMPC
applications include clever
rounding schemes as investigated in
Kirches, 2011
.

In this work we choose a different approach. Convex relaxations often provide a
quite good approximation of the nonlinear, non-convex power flow equations
(
1
)
(
Lavaei and Low, 2012
)
.
We use the so called
QC
relaxation which is based on linear and convex
quadratic approximations and has shown to be faster and more reliable than the

SDP
relaxation
(
Coffrin et al., 2016
)
. This will result in a relaxed set of

AC
power flow solutions
\mathcal{AC}_{\mathrm{QC}}\supset\mathcal{AC}
which can be represented
by linear and convex quadratic constraints.
Thus, in combination with the integer decision variables, we get a
MIQCP

which can be solved by industrial-grade solvers such as
CPLEX
or

GUROBI
considerably faster than the full
MINLP
.

In the following, we briefly explain and recapitulate the main ideas of the

QC
relaxation, for a much more detailed presentation we refer the reader to
the works of
Coffrin et al., 2016
and
Hijazi et al., 2016
. In general,
the nonlinear products in (
1
) are hard to approximate by
linear terms, however the
QC
relaxation for the power flow equations
heavily uses the fact that usually there are quite tight operational bounds on
the involved factors, e.g. on the voltage magnitudes
v_{i}\in[\underline{v}_{i},\overline{v}_{i}]
and the phase angles

\theta_{i}\in[\underline{\theta}_{i},\overline{\theta}_{i}]
.
In a first step, the products
v_{i}v_{j}
in (
2
) for
i\neq j\in\mathcal{N}
are replaced by auxiliary variables
\widecheck{v_{i}v_{j}}
bounded by
McCormick envelopes.
Pure square products like
v_{i}^{2}
are approximated using a combination of
McCormick envelopes and a strengthening convex quadratic constraint.
The trigonometric terms are approximated using tight linear and convex quadratic
envelopes.
Finally, as proposed in
Farivar et al., 2011
, we strengthen the
approximations with a redundant convex
SOC
constraint.
Each of these steps is described in the following.

•

McCormick envelopes

The product
xy
with
x\in[\underline{x},\overline{x}]
and
y\in[\underline{y},\overline{y}]

are approximated using McCormick envelopes that are convex linear
envelopes defined by the linear inequalities

\displaystyle\widecheck{xy}

\displaystyle\geq\underline{x}y+\underline{y}x-\underline{x}\underline{y},

\displaystyle\widecheck{xy}

\displaystyle\geq\overline{x}y+\overline{y}x-\overline{x}\overline{y},

\displaystyle\widecheck{xy}

\displaystyle\leq\underline{x}y+\overline{y}x-\underline{x}\overline{y},

\displaystyle\widecheck{xy}

\displaystyle\leq\overline{x}y+\underline{y}x-\overline{x}\underline{y}.

Figure 1:
Illustration of the product
xy
and the McCormick tetrahedron shaped
envelope defined by the four linear inequalities with the bounds
x,y\in[0,2]
.

•

Square terms

For pure quadratic terms
x^{2}
the lower bounds of the McCormick envelope
can be improved by a stronger convex quadratic constraint leading to the convex
envelope defined by

\displaystyle\widecheck{x^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq x^{2},

\displaystyle\widecheck{x^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq(\underline{x}+\overline{x})x-\underline{x}\overline{x}.

•

Trigonometric terms

The trigonometric terms
\sin(\theta_{ij})
and
\cos(\theta_{ij})
are
approximated using the auxiliary variables
s\theta_{ij}
and
c\theta_{ij}

bounded by the following convex linear/quadratic envelopes:

\displaystyle s\theta_{ij}

\displaystyle\leq\cos(\overline{\theta}_{ij}/2)(\theta_{ij}-\overline{\theta}_{ij}/2)+\sin(\overline{\theta}_{ij}/2),

\displaystyle s\theta_{ij}

\displaystyle\geq\cos(\overline{\theta}_{ij}/2)(\theta_{ij}+\overline{\theta}_{ij}/2)-\sin(\overline{\theta}_{ij}/2)

and

\displaystyle c\theta_{ij}

\displaystyle\leq 1-\tfrac{1-\cos(\overline{\theta}_{ij})}{\overline{\theta}_{ij}^{2}}\theta_{ij}^{2},

\displaystyle c\theta_{ij}

\displaystyle\geq\cos(\overline{\theta}_{ij}).

Here we use the fact that the argument
\theta_{ij}
is bounded in a box
around
0
, because
\theta_{ij}=\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}\in[\underline{\theta}_{i}-\overline{\theta}_{j},\overline{\theta}_{i}-\underline{\theta}_{j}]
. The
remaining products involving
\widecheck{xy},\widecheck{x^{2}}
and

s\theta_{ij},c\theta_{ij}
are also approximated by suitable
McCormick envelopes.

•

The current magnitude constraint

As first proposed by
Farivar et al., 2011
, power flow relaxations
can be strengthened by adding an additional convex
SOC
constraint. This
is based on the observation that for each solution of the true nonlinear
power flow equation (
1
), the line current
l_{ij}
on the
line
(i,j)
, which can be calculated as

l_{ij}=G_{ij}(p_{ij}+p_{ji})-B_{ij}(q_{ij}+q_{ji})
, satisfies the equation

p_{ij}^{2}+q_{ij}^{2}=v_{i}^{2}l_{ij}.

(7)

The inequality
p_{ij}^{2}+q_{ij}^{2}\leq v_{i}^{2}l_{ij}
can be
transformed into the equivalent constraint

p_{ij}^{2}+q_{ij}^{2}+0.5(v_{i}^{2})^{2}+0.5l_{ij}^{2}-0.5(v_{i}^{2}+l_{ij})^{2}\leq 0.

(8)

As we have already introduced an auxiliary variable for approximating
v_{i}^{2}
,
this constraint can be formulated as a convex
SOC
constraint which can be
handled by
CPLEX
and improves the approximation quality of the

QC
relaxation.

Remark 1
.

As most auxiliary variables associated with lines
(i,j)\in\mathcal{N}\times\mathcal{N}
exhibit symmetries, e.g. the variable
\widecheck{v_{i}v_{j}}

representing the voltage product
v_{i}v_{j}=v_{j}v_{i}
, we only introduce
the approximations for pairs
(i,j)
with
i<j
and define the quantity
associated with the reversed lines
(j,i)
using a linear equation, i.e.

\widecheck{v_{j}v_{i}}:=\widecheck{v_{i}v_{j}}
. This not only guarantees the
satisfaction of these natural symmetries, it also reduces the number of envelopes
replacing them by (fewer) simple linear equations (in the example: 4 linear
inequalities are omitted at the cost of one linear equation).

We collect all the auxiliary variables introduced in the
QC
relaxation in
the variable
z_{\mathrm{QC}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{QC}}}
. This allows us to describe
the
QC
relaxation using a linear/quadratic function

g_{\mathrm{QC}}(z,z_{\mathrm{QC}})
such that we can compactly define the set
of relaxed power flow solutions:

Definition 2

(
QC
Approximation of the
AC
Power Flow Manifold)
.

We denote the set of solutions of the
QC
approximations of the nonlinear

AC
-power flow equations (
1
) by

\mathcal{AC}_{\mathrm{QC}}:=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{4N}:\exists z_{\mathrm{QC}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{QC}}}\text{ such that }g_{\mathrm{QC}}(z,z_{\mathrm{QC}})\leq 0\}.

For a given demand
d\in\mathbb{R}^{2N}
we denote the set of
QC
-admissible
control inputs as

Y^{ac}_{\mathrm{QC}}(d):=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{2\left\lvert\mathcal{G}\right\rvert+2\left\lvert\mathcal{B}\right\rvert}:\exists z\in\mathcal{AC}_{\mathrm{QC}}\text{ such that }g_{bal}(y,z,d)=0\}.

3
Multistage Power Flow Problems with Discrete Generator
Constraints

For the optimal control of the microgrid over a certain time-horizon it is
necessary to describe the time-discrete dynamics that couples the different
quasi steady-states over time.

Battery storage and generated powers of batteries and generators are states
subject to time-discrete dynamics which we describe in the following.
As our main goal is to compute optimal generator schedules, we put an emphasis
on the modeling of the discrete generator requirements which will enable us to
handle constraints such as minimal-/ maximal up-/ down times (dwell times) and
bounds on the number of generator switching events. This is accomplished by
introducing additional discrete decision variables and linear inequalities which
model the generator switching dynamics.

Furthermore, we discuss the modeling of the objective function consisting of
generator and battery cost contributions which will enable us to set up an

OCP
.

3.1
Time Discrete Microgrid Dynamics

Several battery and generator states are evolving over time according to
time-discrete dynamics. These dynamics describe the evolution of the battery
state of charge, the current running time of the generators and the generated
powers.
We consider a time grid with
M+1\in\mathbb{N}
equidistant time points
t_{0},\ldots,t_{M}
.
The length is given by
\Delta t=t_{i+1}-t_{i}
for all
i\in\{0,\ldots,M-1\}
.
Below we give a detailed description of the dynamics.

Remark 2

(Notation)
.

We use round brackets to denote that the associated quantity
represents the state of the system at time
t_{i}
for a given time grid

t_{0},\ldots,t_{M}
.
For example
p_{l}(1)
denotes the active power at bus
l
at time instant

t_{1}
.

Battery dynamics

The active- and reactive powers of the batteries evolve according to

\displaystyle\begin{split}p_{l}^{b}(i+1)&=p_{l}^{b}(i)+\delta p_{l}^{b}(i),\\
q_{l}^{b}(i+1)&=q_{l}^{b}(i)+\delta q_{l}^{b}(i),\end{split}\quad\forall i\in\{0,\ldots,M-1\},\forall l\in\mathcal{B}.

(9)

where
\delta p_{l}^{b}(i)
and
\delta q_{l}^{b}(i)
act as control variables
that determine the power change applied (instantaneous) at time
t_{i}
.

Furthermore, each battery has a state
B^{\mathrm{SOC}}_{l}(i)
representing its
state of charge at time
t_{i}
. Corresponding to the active power drawn from
the battery during the interval
(t_{i},t_{i+1}]
, the state of charge evolves
according to

\displaystyle B^{\mathrm{SOC}}_{l}(i+1)=

\displaystyle(1-\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{loss}}_{l}\Delta t)B^{\mathrm{SOC}}_{l}(i)-\Delta t\big(p_{l}^{b}(i+1)+(1-\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{l})\left\lvert p_{l}^{b}(i+1)\right\rvert\big)

(10)

for all
i\in\{0,\ldots,M-1\}
and
l\in\mathcal{B}
.
Note that the state of charge does not depend on the reactive power. The
parameter
\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{loss}}_{l}
models a state of charge proportional loss per time-unit
and the efficiency
\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{l}
denotes the effectiveness of the battery when
converting electric energy into chemical energy and vice versa.
Note that this battery efficiency model is relatively simple and there exist a lot of
possibilities to enhance it
(
Tamilselvi et al., 2021
)
.

Generator dynamics

As for the batteries, the active and reactive power of the generators at time

t_{i}
evolves according
to

\displaystyle\begin{split}p_{l}^{g}(i+1)&=p_{l}^{g}(i)+\delta p_{l}^{g}(i),\\
q_{l}^{g}(i+1)&=q_{l}^{g}(i)+\delta q_{l}^{g}(i),\end{split}\quad\forall i\in\{0,\ldots,M-1\},\forall l\in\mathcal{G},

(11)

where
\delta p_{l}^{g}(i),\delta q_{l}^{g}(i)
act as controls that determine
the power change applied at time
t_{i}
.

In order to model generator up- and down-times we introduce two binary
variables
G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l}(i),G^{\mathrm{switch}}_{l}(i)\in\{0,1\}
for each generator and each time
instant.
The variable
G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l}(i)
denotes whether the generator is on (
=1
) or off
(
=0
) during the interval
(t_{i-1},t_{i}]
and the variable
G^{\mathrm{switch}}_{l}(i)
acts
as generator switch at time
t_{i}
.
Therefore, the generator on-/off-state
G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l}
evolves according to

G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l}(i+1)=\begin{cases}G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l}(i)&\text{if}\quad G^{\mathrm{switch}}_{l}(i)=0,\\
1-G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l}(i)&\text{if}\quad G^{\mathrm{switch}}_{l}(i)=1,\end{cases}\quad\forall i\in\{0,\ldots,M-1\},\forall l\in\mathcal{G}.

(12)

Note that we model the generator switch as acting instantly, i.e.
G^{\mathrm{switch}}_{l}(i)=1

means that the generator mode is changed at time instant
t_{i}
, in particular
this means that the generator operating mode during the interval

(t_{i},t_{i+1}]
is determined by the variable
G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l}(i+1)
.

To model the run-time of the current generator mode (on or off) we introduce
the “counting”-variable
G^{\mathrm{count}}_{l}(i)
which indicates how long the generator is
already operating in its currently active mode at time instant
t_{i}
.
This state evolves according to

G^{\mathrm{count}}_{l}(i+1)=\begin{cases}G^{\mathrm{count}}_{l}(i)+1&\text{if}\quad G^{\mathrm{switch}}_{l}(i)=0,\\
0&\text{if}\quad G^{\mathrm{switch}}_{l}(i)=1,\end{cases}\quad\forall i\in\{0,\ldots,M-1\},\forall l\in\mathcal{G}.

(13)

The states
G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l},G^{\mathrm{switch}}_{l}
and
G^{\mathrm{count}}_{l}
allow the straight-forward
formulation of runtime constraints and switching costs. Furthermore, they can be
used to define generator mode-dependent constraints as we explain in the
following.

3.2
Generator operating bounds

The generator active and reactive powers
p^{g}_{l},q^{g}_{l}
and the power changes

\delta p_{l},\delta q_{l}
are subject to bounds depending on the current
operating mode and the ramping capabilities of the generators.

Mode dependent power bounds

Depending on the current operating mode of the generator, there are different
bounds on the active and reactive powers.
In particular the powers are
0
if the generator is turned off and there is a
non-zero minimum active power output when the generator is running.
In the following, we list the mode dependent lower and upper bounds that have to
be satisfied for every generator at every time instant
i\in\{0,\ldots,M\}
,
where
\underline{p}^{g}_{l},\overline{p}^{g}_{l}
and
\underline{q}^{g}_{l},\overline{q}^{g}_{l}
denote the lower and
upper bounds of active and reactive power for an operating generator.

\displaystyle\begin{split}p^{g}_{l}(i)\leq\begin{cases}0&\text{if}\quad G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l}(i)=0,\\
\overline{p}^{g}_{l}&\text{if}\quad G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l}(i)=1,\end{cases}\end{split}\begin{split}q^{g}_{l}(i)\leq\begin{cases}0&\text{if}\quad G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l}(i)=0,\\
\overline{q}^{g}_{l}&\text{if}\quad G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l}(i)=1,\end{cases}\end{split}

(14)

\displaystyle\begin{split}p^{g}_{l}(i)\geq\begin{cases}0&\text{if}\quad G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l}(i)=0,\\
\underline{p}^{g}_{l}&\text{if}\quad G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l}(i)=1,\end{cases}\end{split}\begin{split}q^{g}_{l}(i)\geq\begin{cases}0&\text{if}\quad G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l}(i)=0,\\
\underline{q}^{g}_{l}&\text{if}\quad G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l}(i)=1.\end{cases}\end{split}

(15)

Ramping constraints

We include the following ramping constraint

-\overline{\mathbf{p}}^{\mathrm{ramp}}_{l}\overline{p}^{g}_{l}\Delta t\leq\delta p^{g}_{l}(i)\leq\overline{\mathbf{p}}^{\mathrm{ramp}}_{l}\overline{p}^{g}_{l}\Delta t,\quad\forall i\in\{0,\ldots,M\},\forall l\in\mathcal{G}.

(16)

on the active generator power which keeps the generator power change at a rate
which is within its operating envelope.
Thereby
\overline{\mathbf{p}}^{\mathrm{ramp}}_{l}\in(0,1]
denotes the maximal power change relative to the
maximal power per time unit.

Up- and down-time constraints

Constraints on the minimal and maximal up- and down-times of the generators
\mathbf{T}^{\textrm{max,on}}_{l},\mathbf{T}^{\textrm{min,on}}_{l},\mathbf{T}^{\textrm{max,off}}_{l},\mathbf{T}^{\textrm{min,off}}_{l}
can
be imposed using the counter variable
G^{\mathrm{count}}_{l}
. For the time instant

i\in\{0,\ldots,M\}
the upper operating time bound can be expressed as simple
linear inequality

G^{\mathrm{count}}_{l}(i)\leq G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l}(i)\mathbf{T}^{\textrm{max,on}}_{l}+(1-G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l}(i))\mathbf{T}^{\textrm{max,off}}_{l},

(17)

whereas the minimal operating bound is modeled as logical implication

G^{\mathrm{switch}}_{l}(i)=1\Rightarrow G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l}(i)\mathbf{T}^{\textrm{min,on}}_{l}+(1-G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l}(i))\mathbf{T}^{\textrm{min,off}}_{l}\leq G^{\mathrm{count}}_{l}(i),

(18)

which ensures that a switch can only happen when the minimal operating times are
satisfied.

Bounds on the number of generator switching events

The number of startup events during the interval
[t_{i},t_{i+M}]
can be
calculated as

G^{\mathrm{startup}}_{l}=\frac{G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l}(i+M)-G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l}(i)+\displaystyle\sum_{j=i}^{i+M-1}G^{\mathrm{switch}}_{l}(j)}{2}.

(19)

This linear expression can be used to add bounds on the number of startup events
and to include switching costs to the objective. If desired, a similar
expression can be derived for the calculation of the number of shutdown events.

Remark 3

(Modelling logical implications as linear inequalities)
.

In the previous two subsections we introduced several constraints that depend
on a binary variable
S\in\{0,1\}
and are only valid if the binary variable
takes a certain value.
They are of the form

\displaystyle S=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow Ax\leq b,

\displaystyle S=1

\displaystyle\Rightarrow Cx\leq d,

where
x\in X\subset\mathbb{N}
.
Such constraints are called logical constraints or indicator constraints and
they are a basic tool in
MILP
(
MILP
) to model systems that can operate in on
or off mode, see e.g.
(
Meier and Düsing, 1992
)
.
We use the big-M method to transform them into the equivalent linear
inequalities

\displaystyle Ax

\displaystyle\leq b+M_{0}S,

\displaystyle Cx

\displaystyle\leq d+M_{1}(1-S),

with some
M_{0}\geq\sup_{x\in X}{Ax-b}
and
M_{1}\geq\sup_{x\in X}{Cx-d}
.
In our numerical implementation we choose the coefficients
M
as small
as possible to avoid numerical instabilities.
For example the generator dynamics constraint (
12
) can
be reformulated as

\begin{split}G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l}(i+1)-G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l}(i)&\leq G^{\mathrm{switch}}_{l}(i),\\
-G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l}(i+1)+G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l}(i)&\leq G^{\mathrm{switch}}_{l}(i),\end{split}

and

\begin{split}G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l}(i+1)+G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l}(i)&\leq 1+(1-G^{\mathrm{switch}}_{l}(i)),\\
-G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l}(i+1)-G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l}(i)&\leq-1+(1-G^{\mathrm{switch}}_{l}(i)),\end{split}

with the ”small” big-M factor
M=1
as
G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l}
is also a binary variable.

with each start-up event of the generator.

We include a simple battery aging model that accounts for calendar battery

3.4
Multistage optimal power flow subject to periodic demand

With the modelling tools and techniques discussed in the previous subsection, we
are now equipped to define multistage optimal power
flow problems taking into account the discrete generator scheduling constraints.
Similar as it is considered by
Pereira et al., 2015
and

Strenge et al., 2020
, we work with the assumption that the power
demand in the microgrid follows a periodic pattern.
Based on this assumption, we first set up a periodic multistage problem on a
time-horizon of 24 hours.
Later, in Section
4
, we will use the results of the periodic
problem to define a suitable set of terminal constraints for an economic

NMPC
controller.

Let
(t_{0},\ldots,t_{M})
with
t_{0}=0
and
t_{M}=24
be an equidistant time
discretization with
t_{i+1}-t_{i}=\Delta t
.
The periodic demand assumption then can be stated as

p_{l}^{d}(0)=p_{l}^{d}(M)\quad\text{and}\quad q_{l}^{d}(0)=q_{l}^{d}(M).

(21)

Our goal is to find a periodic mode of operation that can be extended
indefinitely and can serve as a long-time continuous way to operate the grid.
To find such periodic operation trajectories, we have to impose a set of
periodicity constraints on the generator and battery states:

\displaystyle\begin{split}p_{l}^{g}(0)&=p_{l}^{g}(M),\\
q_{l}^{g}(0)&=q_{l}^{g}(M),\\
G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l}(0)&=G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l}(M),\end{split}\quad\forall l\in\mathcal{G},

(22)

\displaystyle\begin{split}p_{l}^{b}(0)&=p_{l}^{b}(M),\\
q_{l}^{b}(0)&=q_{l}^{b}(M),\\
B^{\mathrm{SOC}}_{l}(0)&=B^{\mathrm{SOC}}_{l}(M).\end{split}\quad\forall l\in\mathcal{B},

(23)

Modeling the periodicity constraint for the generator counter variables

G^{\mathrm{count}}_{l}
requires some additional considerations, as a simple periodicity
constraint on these states will rule out solutions where the generator is on or
off the whole 24 hours. As we also want to consider such solutions as periodic,
we have to use a periodicity constraint “modulo 24 hours” for the counter variables:

G^{\mathrm{count}}_{l}(0)\equiv G^{\mathrm{count}}_{l}(M)\mod M,\quad\forall l\in\mathcal{G}.

This constraint can be modeled by introducing the auxiliary variable

G^{\mathrm{mod}}_{l}:=(G^{\mathrm{count}}_{l}(M)-G^{\mathrm{count}}_{l}(0))/M,

and imposing
the integrality constraint

G^{\mathrm{mod}}_{l}\in\mathbb{Z},\quad\forall l\in\mathcal{G}.

(24)

The periodic multistage
OCP

We can now state the full periodic
OCP
.
It is straightforward to check that any solution satisfying the constraints can
be extended indefinitely, as we suppose that the demand
d(i)
is 24-h periodic.
Further note that all constraints can be formulated as linear equalities and
inequalities with the exception of the convex quadratic constraints used in the

QC
power flow approximation.
As there are integer decision variables, the resulting problem is a

and can be solved, e.g., using the software packages

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{M}\ell(x(k),u(k))

over

\displaystyle(x,\tilde{z},u)(i)_{i=1,\ldots,M},x(M+1),G^{\mathrm{mod}}

s.t.

\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{QC}}(z(i),z_{\mathrm{QC}}(i))\leq 0,\quad

\displaystyle\forall i\in\{0,\ldots,M\},

\displaystyle g_{bal}(x(i),z(i),d_{\mathrm{per}}(i))=0,

\displaystyle\forall i\in\{0,\ldots,M\},

\displaystyle x(i+1)=f(x(i),u(i)),

\displaystyle\forall i\in\{0,\ldots,M-1\},

\displaystyle h(x(i),u(i))\leq 0,

\displaystyle\forall i\in\{0,\ldots,M\},

\displaystyle r(x(0),u(0),\ldots,x(M),u(M))\leq 0,\quad

\displaystyle g_{per}(x(0),x(M))=0,

\displaystyle G^{\mathrm{mod}}_{l}\in\mathbb{Z}.

The solution of this periodic

will serve as a reference operating mode

We denote the periodic reference solution with the subscript “

i.e.

4
NMPC for Microgrids with Discrete Generator Constraints

In this section, we discuss how the modeling techniques presented in the
previous section can be extended to set up an economic
NMPC
controller for
microgrids.
We define a controller that generates an optimal feedback control using predictions of the demand over a certain horizon.
It not only determines how to share the load between the available generators and
batteries, but also determines the optimal switching behavior of the generators
taking into account the startup costs and the constraints on runtime and number
of startup events.
We work with the general assumption that, in an unperturbed scenario, the
demand follows the 24-hour periodic pattern that we already introduced in the
previous section (
d(\cdot)=d_{\mathrm{per}}(\cdot)
).
Based on this assumption we analyze the closed-loop behavior of the defined
economic
NMPC
controller and show a recursive feasibility property.
We also discuss how a periodic dissipativity condition can be used to show
asymptotic stability of the proposed scheme.

4.1
The
NMPC
Subproblem

We define an
NMPC
scheme for a given prediction horizon of constant length with
M
intervals that
not necessarily has to correspond to the period length of 24 hours.
It can be employed to compute the optimal microgrid
controls based on a demand forecast for that prediction horizon.

Intuitively it is clear that an
OCP
solution will use up as much battery
energy as possible at the end of the horizon as the battery power comes for free
compared to the generator power which causes fuel costs.
As we want to avoid that the
NMPC
controller uses up all the available
battery storage at the end of the prediction horizon, we include a
terminal constraint that ensures that the periodic reference trajectory is
reached at the end of the prediction horizon.
Besides avoiding greediness, the idea behind the terminal constraint is to
guarantee that the predicted
NMPC
subproblem solution can be extended in a
feasible manner with the periodic reference solution from the previous section.
This enables us to prove recursive feasibility of the
NMPC
scheme.

Remark 6

(Notation)
.

We denote quantities associated with the
NMPC
subproblem at sampling
instant
i
by using a bracket
(i|k)
, i.e.
x(i|k)

corresponds to the predicted state at time
t_{k+i}
arising in the subproblem
considered at time
t_{k}
.

Before we set up the
NMPC
subproblems we define two auxiliary functions
that will be necessary to set up all the constraints.

Definition 3

(Generator auxiliary reference functions)
.

Let
T^{\mathrm{switch}}_{l}(j)
denote the number of sampling intervals until the
next generator switch happens in the periodic reference solution from time

t_{j}
on:

T^{\mathrm{switch}}_{l}(j):=\min\{s\geq 0:G^{\mathrm{switch}}_{l,\mathrm{per}}(j+s)=1\}.

(25)

Furthermore, we define a function that counts the switching events of the
periodic reference solution during the interval
[t_{j_{start}},t_{j_{end}}]

for
0\leq j_{start}\leq j_{end}
:

\Sigma^{\mathrm{switches}}_{l}(j_{start},j_{end}):=\sum_{j=j_{start}}^{j_{end}}G^{\mathrm{switch}}_{l,\mathrm{per}}(j).

(26)

Both functions from Definition
3
can
directly be computed after the periodic reference trajectory is available.

The
NMPC
subproblem is of similar structure as the periodic

OCP
of the previous section, only with the periodicity constraints replaced
by initial value constraints and with an additional set of terminal and
extendability constraints.
In the following, we give a detailed description of all the constraints.

Initial value constraint

We impose initial value constraints for all dynamically evolving states of the
system:

x(0|k)=(p^{g},q^{g},p^{b},q^{b},G^{\mathrm{ind}},G^{\mathrm{count}},B^{\mathrm{SOC}})(0|k)=x_{0}.

(27)

We assume that the value of the state
x_{0}
can be exactly determined at any
time.

Terminal constraints

Generator powers and operating mode:

For each generator, we impose the constraint that at the end of the prediction
horizon the generator power and the operating mode (on/off) have to be equal to
the corresponding generator states of the periodic reference solution

\begin{split}p^{g}_{l}(M|k)&=p^{g}_{l,\mathrm{per}}(M+k\mod N_{\mathrm{per}}),\\
q^{g}_{l}(M|k)&=q^{g}_{l,\mathrm{per}}(M+k\mod N_{\mathrm{per}}),\\
G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l}(M|k)&=G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l,\mathrm{per}}(M+k\mod N_{\mathrm{per}}).\end{split}\quad\forall l\in\mathcal{G},

(28)

Note that the operating mode at the end of the horizon is already determined by
the active power, as the generator is off if and only if the active power is
zero. Therefore the constraint on the operating mode is redundant and could
also be omitted.

Battery state of charge:

In contrast to the generator powers, there are no ramping constraints on the
battery powers and therefore it is not necessary to impose terminal constraints
on them to guarantee that the periodic reference can be continued. However, we
impose the terminal constraint

B^{\mathrm{SOC}}_{l}(M|k)=B^{\mathrm{SOC}}_{l,\mathrm{per}}(M+k\mod N_{\mathrm{per}}).

(29)

on the remaining state of charge to prevent the controller
from emptying the batteries completely and maneuvering itself into a suboptimal
grid state.

Remark 7
.

Note that, in practice, also an inequality constraint that only specifies a lower
bound of the state of charge at the end of the horizon would make sense.
The reason why we chose to work with the equality constraint instead is twofold.
First, it allows a more concise derivation of the theoretical properties such as
recursive feasibility and stability (Propositions

1
and
2
). And second, there is
the intuitive argument that even when imposing only inequality constraints on
the terminal state of charge, these constraints most likely will be active all
the time, as any surplus energy in the battery at the horizon end is somewhat
contradictory to optimality as it translates into freely available energy that
was not used. A rigorous analysis of the stability properties for the inequality
case is subject to further research.

Generator counter states:

Concerning the generator counter states at the end of the prediction horizon, it
is not necessary to demand equality with the periodic reference trajectory to
guarantee that the predicted solution can be extended with the periodic
reference.
Instead, we demand that the next switching event, that occurs if the predicted
solution is extended with the periodic reference, does not violate
any generator runtime constraints. Simply speaking, the next switch cannot
happen with a “forbidden” runtime. This is realized using the function

T^{\mathrm{switch}}
from Definition
3
.
In case the next switch is an “on” switch, we add the constraints

\mathbf{T}^{\textrm{min,off}}_{l}\leq G^{\mathrm{count}}_{l}(M|k)+T^{\mathrm{switch}}_{l}(M+k\mod N_{\mathrm{per}})\leq\mathbf{T}^{\textrm{max,off}}_{l},\quad\forall l\in\mathcal{G}.

(30)

In case it is a “off” switch, we add the same constraints with the limits
replaced accordingly by
\mathbf{T}^{\textrm{min,on}},\mathbf{T}^{\textrm{max,on}}
.

Startup counter constraints

We want to bound the number of startup events during any 24-hour subinterval
starting inside the prediction-horizon below a maximum of
\overline{\mathbf{G}}^{\mathrm{startup}}_{l}
.
This leads to the following set of linear constraints:

\displaystyle\underbrace{\frac{\sum_{j=i}^{i+N_{\mathrm{per}}}G^{\mathrm{switch}}_{l}(j|k)-G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l}(i|k)+G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l}(i+N_{\mathrm{per}}|k)}{2}}_{\text{number of startups in the interval
$[t_{k+i},t_{k+i+N_{\mathrm{per}}}]$}}\leq\overline{\mathbf{G}}^{\mathrm{startup}}_{l}

(31)

for all
i\in\{0,\ldots,M-1\}
and
l\in\mathcal{G}
.
However, the switch indicator
G^{\mathrm{switch}}_{l}(j|k)
is not defined for
j\geq M
since it is outside the prediction horizon of the
NMPC
problem.
In this case we replace
G^{\mathrm{switch}}_{l}(j|k)
by the switch indicator of the
periodic reference solution
G^{\mathrm{switch}}_{l,\mathrm{per}}(j+k\mod N_{\text{per}})
.
This corresponds to an extension of the prediction by the periodic reference solution.
Similarly, we replace
G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l}(i+N_{\mathrm{per}}|k)
by
G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l,per}(i+k\mod N_{\mathrm{per}})
.
This is realized using the
\Sigma^{\mathrm{switches}}_{l}
function from Definition

3
and replacing the numerator of
(
31
) by

\begin{split}\sum_{j=k}^{M}G^{\mathrm{switch}}_{l}(j|k)+\Sigma^{\mathrm{switches}}(\widetilde{k+M},\widetilde{k+M}+N_{\mathrm{per}}-M)\\
-G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l}(0|k)+G^{\mathrm{ind}}_{l,\mathrm{per}}(\widetilde{k+M})\end{split}

using the abbreviation
\widetilde{i}:=i\mod N_{\mathrm{per}}
.

Complete
NMPC
Subproblem

With the constraints explained above we can now state the full
NMPC

subproblem at sampling time
t_{k}
:

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}\ell(x(i|k),u(i|k))

over

\displaystyle(x,z,u)(i|k)_{i=0,\ldots,M-1},x(M)

s.t.

\displaystyle x(0|k)=x_{0},

\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{QC}}(z(i|k),z_{\mathrm{QC}}(i|k))\leq 0,

\displaystyle\forall i\in\{0,\ldots,M\},

\displaystyle g_{bal}(y(i|k),z(i|k),d(i|k))=0,

\displaystyle\forall i\in\{0,\ldots,M\},

\displaystyle x(i+1|k)=f(x(i|k),u(i|k)),

\displaystyle\forall i\in\{0,\ldots,M-1\},

\displaystyle h(x(i|k),u(i|k))\leq 0,

\displaystyle\forall i\in\{0,\ldots,M\},

\displaystyle\eqref{eqn:generator_terminal_states},\eqref{eqn:battery_terminal_soc},\text{(generator\&battery terminal constraints)},

\displaystyle\eqref{eqn:runtime_extendability},\text{(generator runtime
extendability constraints)},

\displaystyle\eqref{eqn:sliding_toc},\text{(generator startup counter constraints)}.

To highlight the parametrization with respect to the initial value and the
demand, we denote this problem by
\mathcal{P}(x_{0},d(\cdot|k))
.
The set of admissible initial values for a given predicted demand
d(\cdot|k)
is denoted by

\mathbb{X}_{k}:=\{x\in\mathbb{X}:\mathcal{P}(x,d(\cdot|k))\text{ is
admissible}\},

and we define the function

\mathcal{V}_{k}:\mathbb{X}_{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},

objective value associated with the corresponding problem
\mathcal{P}(x,d(\cdot|k))
.

4.2
Closed-loop analysis

In this section we analyze the closed-loop behavior of the
NMPC
scheme based on the definitions above.
At each sampling time
t_{k}
it solves the subproblems
\mathcal{P}(x,d(\cdot|k))
and applies the corresponding active power control inputs to the system.
We begin this discussion with a remark on the consequences of using the
QC
approximation in our problem formulation which replaces the
nonlinear
AC
power flow equations.

Remark 8

(Power flow correction)
.

As we do not use the full nonlinear
power flow equations but the convex quadratic
QC
-relaxation, the
resulting generator and battery powers
p^{g},p^{b}
may not correspond to an
exact solution of the full nonlinear power flow equations.
As a measure of the deviation of the
QC
approximations from true
AC

solutions, we compute the minimal distance (in the
\ell^{2}
sense) from the
proposed active powers of the

QC
-relaxed problem to a set of active powers satisfying the true nonlinear

AC
-power flow equations. This can be formulated as a simple optimization
problem (see Definition
4
below) which is
computationally relatively easy to solve, as it only concerns one quasi
steady-state at a time and, compared to the
NMPC
subproblem, does not
include any integer decision variables. Doing so, we have an a-posteriori
analysis tool at hand that can tell us how ”realizable” the solution proposed
by the
QC
-relaxation is and also how it has to be modified such that the
full nonlinear
AC
equations are satisfied. In our numerical examples in
Section
5
we found that in the considered cases, the
difference to a true power flow solution was remarkably small.
In the following theoretical analysis of the
NMPC
scheme we will assume
that this difference vanishes, see Assumption
1
.

We now state the exact formulation of the optimization problem we use to
calculate how far off a proposed
QC
approximation is from a solution of the
nonlinear
AC
equations.

Definition 4

(Powerflow deviation check)
.

Let
y_{ref}\in Y^{ac}_{\mathrm{QC}}(d)
be a
QC
admissible control input
for the demand
d
.
We call the
OCP

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\sum_{l\in\mathcal{G}}\left\lVert p^{g}_{l,ref}-p^{g}_{l}\right\rVert^{2}+\sum_{l\in\mathcal{B}}\left\lVert p^{s}_{l,ref}-p^{s}_{l}\right\rVert^{2}

over

\displaystyle y=\begin{bmatrix}[p_{l}^{g},q_{l}^{g}]^{T}_{l\in\mathcal{G}},[p_{l}^{s},q_{l}^{s}]^{T}_{l\in\mathcal{B}}\end{bmatrix}^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2|\mathcal{G}|+2|\mathcal{B}|}

s.t.

\displaystyle y\in Y^{ac}(d),

the “powerflow deviation-check”
OCP
. We denote its optimal objective
value by
V_{check}(y_{ref},d)
.

The powerflow deviation-check problem is posed in such a way that it
determines the best possible match of active powers, as the active powers are
responsible for the objective contributions. It can be interpreted as a
projection onto the set
Y^{ac}(d)
.

For the rest of this section we work with the following practical assumption:

Assumption 1

(Control input realizability)
.

We assume that the proposed active power control inputs resulting from the

NMPC
subproblem can be practically realized in the microgrid.
In other words: The difference to a realizable active power input that
satisfies the nonlinear
AC
equations vanishes and the optimal
objective of the powerflow deviation-check
OCP
is

V_{check}(y(0|k),d(0|k))=0
.

With this assumption, we can prove a recursive feasibility property for proposed

NMPC
scheme.

Proposition 1

(Practical recursive feasibility)
.

Let Assumption
1
hold.
If the predicted demand remains unchanged, i.e.
d(i+1|k)=d(i|k+1)
,
and the predicted demand of the problem at sampling time
t_{k+1}
reaches the
periodic reference demand at the end of the prediction horizon, i.e.
d(M|k+1)=d_{\mathrm{per}}(k+M+1)
, feasibility of the
NMPC
subproblem
at sampling time
t_{k}
implies feasibility of the subproblem at the sampling
time
t_{k+1}
.

Proof.

The main idea of the proof is to show that the solution

(x,z,u)(\cdot|k)
of the problem at time instant
t_{k}
can be
extended using the periodic reference trajectory, which gives a feasible
candidate for the
NMPC
subproblem at time instant
t_{k+1}
. To show
this, let
(x,z,u)(\cdot|k+1)
denote the candidate solution resulting
from the extension of the solution of the subproblem of sampling time

t_{k}
using the periodic reference solution. We check
if the candidate solution
(x,z,u)(\cdot|k+1)
satisfies all constraints of
the problem at sampling time
t_{k+1}
.

The realizability assumption
1
ensures that the proposed
active powers
y(0|k)
from the subproblem of sampling time
t_{k}

can be realized during the interval
[t_{k},t_{k+1}]
and thus the system
will reach the state
x(1|k)
at time
t_{k+1}
. Therefore, our
candidate solution satisfies the corresponding initial value constraint.
The
QC
constraints and the power balance constraint are satisfied
because both
x(\cdot|k)
and the periodic extension satisfy them.
For the same reason the dynamic constraints (
11
),
(
12
), (
13
), the ramping
(
16
) and the mode dependent constraints
(
14
) and
(
15
) are satisfied for
(x,z,u)(\cdot|k+1)
.

The extendability constraint (
30
) for
x(\cdot|k)
guarantees that the periodic continuation does not violate the
runtime constraints (
17
) and
(
18
).

The set of constraints on the number of startup events
(
31
) consists of the shifted set of constraints of the
previous problem, only the last constraint
(i=k+M-1)
needs to be checked.
It is satisfied, because one can verify that it concerns a
trajectory that exactly corresponds to a copy of the periodic reference,
which in turn per definition of the periodic multistage problem satisfies
the constraint on the number of startup events.

The terminal constraints (
28
) for
x(\cdot|k+1)
is satisfied because the periodic reference solution is reached.
As the final active battery power of the problem at time
t_{k}
corresponds
to the reference active battery power, the battery state of charge dynamics
(

) and (

imply that the final state of charge of

∎

4.3
Stability and periodic dissipativity

The concept of dissipativity plays an important role in the theory of
economic
NMPC
schemes. For the case of continuous systems without discrete
decision variables there is a fairly good understanding of the interplay of
dissipativity properties, existence of turnpikes and stability of
NMPC

feedback, see for example the works of

Faulwasser et al., 2014
and
Grüne and Müller, 2016
.
More recently, there also have been advances in the theory concerning systems
that include discrete decision variables, see e.g.
(
Faulwasser and Murray, 2020
)
.
In this subsection we want to show how a periodic dissipativity condition could
be used to prove asymptotic stability of the proposed
NMPC
scheme.

We begin by defining strict periodic dissipativity for the nominal demand case
(
d=d_{\mathrm{per}}
).

Definition 5

(Strict periodic dissipativity)
.

The above defined system is called
N_{\mathrm{per}}
-periodic dissipative with respect
to a
N_{\mathrm{per}}
-periodic solution
\Pi_{\mathrm{per}}=(x_{\mathrm{per}},u_{\mathrm{per}})(\cdot)

if there exists a periodic sequence of bounded
storage functions
(\lambda_{k}:\mathbb{X}_{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
with
\lambda_{k}=\lambda_{k+N_{\mathrm{per}}}
and a positive
definite function
\sigma:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that
the dissipativity condition

\begin{split}L_{k}(x,u)&=\ell(x,u)-\ell(x_{\mathrm{per}}(k),u_{\mathrm{per}}(k))+\lambda_{k}(x)-\lambda_{k+1}(f(x,u))\\
&\geq\sigma(\left\lvert x\right\rvert_{\Pi})\end{split}

(33)

holds for all
(x,u)\in\mathbb{X}_{k}\times\mathbb{U}
.
The distance to the periodic reference trajectory is defined as

\left\lvert x\right\rvert_{\Pi}:=\min_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\lVert x-x_{\mathrm{per}}(k)\right\rVert^{2}.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that the storage functions
vanish on the periodic reference
\lambda_{k}(x_{\mathrm{per}}(k))=0
.

Under the assumption of strict periodic dissipativity and a continuity condition
on the optimal value functions, it is possible to show the following stability
result by means of rotated objective functions, similar as presented by
Zanon et al., 2017
.

Proposition 2

(Asymptotic stability)
.

Let the system be strictly periodic dissipative at the periodic solution

\Pi_{\mathrm{per}}
, the set of admissible initial values

\mathbb{X}_{k}
be compact and the optimal objective value functions

\mathcal{V}_{k}:\mathbb{X}_{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be continuous in a vicinity of

\Pi_{\mathrm{per}}
for all
k
.
Then the NMPC scheme is asymptotically stable at this periodic solution.

Proof.

In a first step, we replace the original objective of the
NMPC

subproblem at sampling time
t_{k}
by the rotated objective

\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}L_{i}(x(i|k),u(i|k))+\lambda_{M}(f(x(M-1|k),u(M-1|k))).

(34)

Using the dissipativity assumption, it can be verified that, up to a
constant, this objective is equal to the original objective:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}&\big[\ell(x(i|k),u(i|k))-\ell(x_{\mathrm{per}}(i+k),u_{\mathrm{per}}(i+k))\\
&\quad+\lambda_{i+k}(x(i|k))-\lambda_{i+1+k}(f(x(i|k),u(i|k)))\big]\\
&+\lambda_{M+k}(f(x(M-1|k),u(M-1|k)))\\
=&\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}\ell(x(i|k),u(i|k))-\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}\ell(x_{\mathrm{per}}(i+k),u_{\mathrm{per}}(i+k))\\
&\quad+\lambda_{k}(x(0|k)).\end{split}

(35)

Therefore, the rotated objective has the same minimizers as the original
objective. We now can show that the optimal value function
\mathcal{V}_{rot,k}

of problem with the rotated objective is a Lyapunov function.
To do so we first note that the dissipativity condition and the
compactness of
\mathbb{X}_{k}

implies the existence of

\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\mathcal{K}_{\infty}
such
that

\alpha_{1}(\sigma(\left\lvert x\right\rvert_{\Pi_{\mathrm{per}}})\leq\mathcal{V}_{rot,k}(x)\leq\alpha_{2}(\sigma(\left\lvert x\right\rvert_{\Pi_{\mathrm{per}}}))

(36)

holds.

Furthermore, we have seen in the proof of the recursive feasibility
property (Proposition
1
) that the solutions of

NMPC
subproblems can always be extended with the periodic reference
trajectory to obtain feasible candidates for the next
NMPC
subproblem.
This implies that, compared to the objective at sampling time
t_{k-1}
, the
rotated optimal value function at least decreases by
L_{k}(x(0|k),u(0|k))
which, due to the dissipativity condition, is bounded from
below by
\sigma(\left\lvert x_{0}\right\rvert_{\Pi_{\mathrm{per}}})
.
Together with the continuity of the optimal value function
\mathcal{V}
, this
implies asymptotic stability.
∎

The analysis whether necessary and sufficient conditions for dissipativity
hold for the system we consider is beyond the scope of this work and is
subject to current research. In
Faulwasser and Murray, 2020
it is shown
that under certain conditions also in a mixed-integer setting
dissipativity implies a turnpike condition, similarly as in the continuous
case. A possible approach for showing that optimal periodic operation implies
the periodic dissipativity condition could be along the lines of the methods described for
the continuous steady state case in
Müller et al., 2015
. The idea is
to define a “available storage” for the supply rate
\ell(x,u)-\ell(x_{\mathrm{per}},u_{\mathrm{per}})
and to assume that the system is
uniform suboptimally operated off the periodic steady state but not
dissipative. In this case the available storage is unbounded

(
Müller et al., 2015
)
[Theorem 2] and with a controllability condition
a cyclic trajectory with “better than optimal” performance can be constructed
which is a contradiction.

5.2
Demand scenarios

We consider two different demand scenarios. First an unperturbed case where the
24-h periodic reference demand is realized without any disturbance. In a second,
more challenging case, we consider a perturbed demand and solar power
scenario. In all our simulations we assume that the predicted demands, though
they may deviate from the periodic reference, coincide with the realized
demands, i.e. the demand forecast is correct. We use a sampling time of
\Delta t=1h
and a prediction horizon length of
48h
.

Before we present the closed-loop results we briefly discuss the solution of the
periodic reference problem.
The periodic reference demand and solar input are 24-hour periodic. The power
demands are depicted in the first two
p^{d}
plots in Figure

3
.
The power demand has a peak in the morning and afterwards decreases before going
slightly up again in the evening while the solar power is modeled using a
sinusoidal function during the day.
The corresponding optimal mode of periodic operation is shown in the third plot
of Figure
3
.

5.2.1
Nominal scenario

In this scenario the predicted demand and solar input does not deviate from the
periodic reference demand. The simulation horizon is
48h
.
As can be seen in the
p^{g}
plot in Figure
3
, the
controller keeps the system exactly on the periodic reference in this case.
This behavior is expected because once the system state is on the periodic
reference, any deviation would contradict the optimality of the periodic
reference (because of the terminal constraint).
The a-posteriori analysis of the quality of the QC relaxation depicted in the
last plot of the figure shows that the difference between the generator and
battery powers resulting from the QC relaxation and true solutions of the AC
powerflow equations are negligible, which indicates that the Control input
realizability Assumption
1
is of practical relevance.

5.2.2
Varying solar input scenario

In this scenario both the demand at bus 6 and the solar input at bus 4 are
subject to random perturbations and only roughly follow the periodic reference
demand. Additionally on day two there is a significant drop in the power demand
at bus 6 and at the same time a stronger solar power input at bus 4.
The demand profiles are depicted in the first two plots of Figure

4
.
In this scenario it can be observed that during day 2 both generators strongly
deviate from the periodic reference because of the lower demand and stronger
solar input. Within this phase also generator 2 is turned off completely for a
short period. The reason for this behavior is that the controller anticipates that
the saved operating costs by turning off generator 2 outweigh the additionally
caused startup costs of the generator.
It can also be seen that the battery is charged during low demand and high solar
input situations and the stored energy afterwards is used to prevent otherwise
necessary generator startup events and to prolong the times of keeping generator
2 turned off.
The low demand and high solar input situation during day two results in a
significantly lower operating cost as can be seen in the stage cost and
cumulative cost plots of Figure
4
.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we presented an economic
NMPC
scheme for optimal microgrid
control with discrete generator requirements. The controller feedback is based
on a multistage optimal power flow problem. The discrete generator requirements
such as generator runtime bounds, startup costs and bounds on the number of
startup events are handled by introducing discrete auxiliary variables
indicating the current operating mode of the generator. Using a precomputed
periodic reference trajectory as terminal constraint and a set of tailored
discrete constraints we have shown recursive feasibility of the resulting mixed-integer
NMPC
scheme. To make the resulting optimization problems computationally
tractable, we proposed to combine the mixed-integer formulation with the
Quadratically Convex
QC
relaxation of the nonlinear AC-power flow equations.
As a result we obtained a
MIQCP
which can be solved in reasonable time by

CPLEX
. Furthermore, we have shown stability properties of the resulting
closed-loop system provided some theoretical dissipativity assumptions hold. We
demonstrate the capabilities of the controller by means of different demand
scenarios for a realistic sized microgrid.
