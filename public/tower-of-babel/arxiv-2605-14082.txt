Goal-Oriented Time Adaptivity for Linear Port-Hamiltonian Differential-Algebraic Equations of Index 1 Thanks: This work was funded by the Deutsche Forschungsgemeinschaft (DFG, German Research Foundation) – Project-ID 531152215 – CRC 1701.
Aashutosh Sharma, Andreas Bartel, Manuel Schaller

Abstract
Port-Hamiltonian systems provide a highly-structured framework for modeling of physical systems. By definition, they encode a balance equation relating energy changes to supplied and dissipated energy. Capturing this energy balance in discrete approximations is a fundamental challenge and often has been achieved by designing particular schemes such as discrete gradient methods. In this work, we propose an approach that controls the energy balance violation for port-Hamiltonian differential algebraic equations via time adaptivity using a posteriori grid refinement techniques based on the dual weighted residual method. In particular, we show how one may leverage the port-Hamiltonian structure to efficiently compute the error estimators using a dissipativity-exploiting block-Jacobi approximation. We illustrate the efficacy of the method by means of simulations of electrical circuit models. Keywords. Port-Hamiltonian systems, adaptive time discretization, differential algebraic equations, dual weighted residual method. MSC 2020. 65L80, 65L50, 65L05, 93C05.

Introduction

Port-Hamiltonian (pH) systems provide a systematic framework for the
energy-based modeling of coupled systems including networks and multiphysics applications.
By encoding energy
storage, dissipation, and interconnection directly on the structural
level, the pH formulation guarantees fundamental physical properties
such as passivity and energy conservation by
construction
[
27
,
13
]
. Central to any pH model
is its
energy balance
, which relates the rate of change of the
Hamiltonian to externally supplied and internally dissipated power.
For simulations, in particular over long time horizons, preserving or accurately controlling this
balance at the discrete level is essential.

Many applications, including electrical networks
[
2
]
, constrained multi-body
systems
[
6
]
, and spatially discretized field theories
[
7
]
, involve both dynamic
and static coupling relations and are naturally formulated as
(port-Hamiltonian) differential-algebraic equations
(DAEs)
[
4
,
20
]
. In the work at hand, we consider linear
port-Hamiltonian DAEs of the form

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}E\,x(t)

\displaystyle=(J-R)\,Q\,x(t)+B\,u(t),

(1a)

\displaystyle y(t)

\displaystyle=B^{\top}Q\,x(t),

(1b)

where
E\in\mathbbm{R}^{n\times n}
is a possibly singular,

J=-J^{\top}\in\mathbbm{R}^{n\times n}
encodes energy-conserving interconnection,

0\leq R=R^{\top}\in\mathbbm{R}^{n\times n}
models dissipation,
Q\in\mathbbm{R}^{n\times n}
defines the energy metric and satisfies

E^{\top}Q=Q^{\top}E
, and
B\in\mathbbm{R}^{n\times m}
is
the port matrix. In (
1
),
x(t)\in\mathbbm{R}^{n}
is the descriptor state,
u(t)\in\mathbbm{R}^{m}
denotes the input and
y(t)\in\mathbbm{R}^{m}
is the
conjugated output
.

From these properties, it is easily seen that the quadratic Hamiltonian

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{DAE}}(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}\,x^{\top}E^{\top}Q\,x
satisfies
the
power balance

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{DAE}}(x(t))=y(t)^{\top}u(t)-(Qx(t))^{\top}R\,Qx(t),

(2)

which yields, upon integration over
[s,t]\subset\mathbbm{R}
, the
energy balance

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{DAE}}(x(t))-\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{DAE}}(x(s))=\int_{s}^{t}\!\bigl[y(\tau)^{\top}u(\tau)-(Qx(\tau))^{\top}R\,Qx(\tau)\bigr]\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

(3)

In time discretization of the dynamics (
1
), it is thus clearly desirable to capture this central property of the dynamics.
There are various existing approaches to tackle this challenge.
Structure-preserving time integrators enforce a discrete analogue
of (
3
) exactly. Discrete gradient methods,
originally introduced for Hamiltonian
ordinary differential equations (ODEs) in
[
11
]
, have been extended to port-Hamiltonian
ODEs
[
16
,
25
]
and, more recently, to
pH-DAEs
[
15
,
19
,
22
]
. Generally speaking, these methods enforce an energy balance by design and employment of particular integrators.

In this work, we pursue a complementary approach using
goal-oriented grid adaptivity
that ensures an
approximate

energy balance by adaptation of the time grid, with full freedom in the choice of the time integration method.
Rather than enforcing
energy conservation exactly, we treat violations
of (
3
) as a computable
quantity of interest

and adapt the time grid to reduce these violations to a prescribed tolerance. The theoretical basis is the

dual weighted residual

method, which we summarize next.

Classical a posteriori error estimators for time-stepping schemes
control a global norm of the discretization error, such as

\|x-x_{k}\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}
, and refine wherever this norm is
locally large. While such estimators are well-established and reliable, they do not distinguish between error components that
affect a given quantity of interest and those that do not. In many
applications, including the energy-balance monitoring considered here, the relevant quantity depends on the solution in a highly
nonuniform way, and global-norm control leads to unnecessary
refinement in regions that contribute negligibly to the error in the
quantity of interest.

The Dual Weighted Residual (DWR) method
[
5
,
1
]
addresses this by formulating error estimation
directly in terms of a user-specified quantity of interest

\mathcal{J}
. The method proceeds in three conceptual steps.
First, one defines a
residual

\rho(x_{k})
that measures how
well the discrete solution
x_{k}
satisfies the continuous equation;
for a consistent discretization, this residual vanishes when tested
against discrete test functions and is nonzero only when tested
against functions outside the discrete space.
Second, one introduces an
adjoint problem
whose right-hand
side is the derivative
\mathcal{J}^{\prime}(x_{k})
of the quantity of
interest. The adjoint solution
z
encodes the sensitivity of

\mathcal{J}
to perturbations at each point in time: it is large
where local errors propagate strongly into the quantity of interest
and small where they do not.
Third, one derives an
error representation

\mathcal{J}(x)-\mathcal{J}(x_{k})\approx\sum_{i=1}^{N}\eta_{i},\qquad\eta_{i}=\rho_{i}(x_{k})(z-z_{k}),

(4)

where
\rho_{i}
denotes the restriction of the residual to the
i
-th
time interval and
z_{k}
is a discrete approximation of the adjoint.
Each indicator
\eta_{i}
is the product of the local residual and the
local adjoint error, so it is large only when both the discretization
error and the sensitivity are simultaneously significant. This
multiplicative structure is the key advantage of goal-oriented
estimation: it concentrates refinement precisely where it most
efficiently reduces the error in
\mathcal{J}
.

The identity (
4
) is made rigorous in
Section
4
, where we specialize it to the dG(0)
discretization of the pH-DAE and derive the computable local
indicators used in the adaptive algorithm.

For port-Hamiltonian ODEs, goal-oriented time adaptivity targeting
the energy balance (
3
) was recently developed
in
[
3
]
. In the present work, we extend this
approach to DAEs of index one, which introduces several additional
challenges: the algebraic constraints couple differential and
algebraic variables with different regularity; the Schur complement
reduction to the differential variable produces a generally
non-symmetric governing operator; and the adjoint problem features
jump conditions at the time nodes arising from the quartic structure
of the energy-balance QoI. For a comprehensive treatment of the DWR
framework, we refer to
[
1
,
5
]
;
applications to parabolic optimal control problems are developed
in
[
21
]
; and two-sided effectivity bounds are analyzed
in
[
8
]
.

Contributions.

The main novelties of this work are:

(i)

Definition of a tailored goal-oriented time adaptive scheme for pH-DAEs using a
Schur-complement
elimination of algebraic variables.

(ii)

A DWR-based a posteriori error representation of the energy balance error and derivation of computable
local error indicators.

(iii)

A parallelizable Block-Jacobi approximation of the backward
adjoint problem exploiting dissipativity of the pH structure.

(iv)

Numerical validation demonstrating significant efficiency gains
over uniform time stepping.

2
Port-Hamiltonian DAEs in weak form

In this section, we define the pH-DAEs of index-1 in a semi-explicit formulation. Then, a variational formulation is introduced for preparation of the goal-oriented scheme.
Subsequently, we define the energy-balance-based goal functional and derive the corresponding
adjoint problem.

2.1
Problem formulation and semi-explicit structure

Throughout this work, we impose the following structural assumptions on
the pH-DAE (
1
).

Assumption 2.1
.

The system matrices in (
1
) satisfy:

(a)

E\in\mathbbm{R}^{n\times n}
is singular with
\mathrm{rk}(E)=r<n
.

(b)

Q\in\mathbbm{R}^{n\times n}
is non-singular and
E^{\top}Q=Q^{\top}E>0
(symmetric positive definite).

(c)

J=-J^{\top}
(skew-symmetric) and

R=R^{\top}\geq 0
(symmetric positive semi-definite).

(d)

B\in\mathbbm{R}^{n\times m}
, and the input satisfies

u\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathbbm{R}^{m})
.

(e)

The matrix pencil
(E,\,(J-R)Q)
is regular , i.e.,
\operatorname{det}(sE-(J-R)Q)
is not the zero polynomial.

(f)

The DAE (
1
) is of

index-
1
, i.e., the mapping

x\mapsto(J-R)Q\,x
to the kernel of
E
is invertible.

(g)

x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a consistent initial value, i.e., there is a solution to (
1
) that satisfies
x(0)=x_{0}
.

For more details on the port-Hamiltonian setting, we refer to
[
4
]
.

To make the index-1 structure more
explicit, we employ a
kernel splitting
pair

[
14
]
. To this end,
let
V\in\mathbbm{R}^{n\times r}
and

W\in\mathbbm{R}^{n\times(n-r)}
be such that
EW=0
, the matrix

(V,\,W)\in\mathbbm{R}^{n\times n}
is non-singular, and

x=Vx_{1}+Wx_{2}.

Left-multiplying the pH-DAE (
1a
) by

(V,\,W)^{\top}Q^{\top}
produces the semi-explicit form

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\begin{pmatrix}E_{11}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}(t)\\
x_{2}(t)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}A_{11}&A_{12}\\
A_{21}&A_{22}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}(t)\\
x_{2}(t)\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}B_{1}\\
B_{2}\end{pmatrix}u(t),

(5)

with block matrices

(V,W)^{\top}Q^{\top}E\,(V,W)=\begin{pmatrix}E_{11}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad A=(V,W)^{\top}Q^{\top}(J-R)\,Q\,(V,W),\quad\begin{pmatrix}B_{1}\\
B_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}V^{\top}Q^{\top}B\\
W^{\top}Q^{\top}B\end{pmatrix}.

The pH structure is inherited:

E_{11}^{\top}=E_{11}>0

and the governing matrix is dissipative in the Euclidean inner product, that is,

A+A^{\top}\leq 0
. The index-
1
condition ensures
that
A_{22}
is invertible, so the algebraic variable can be determined
from the differential variable and the input at every time instant. For a discussion of transformations of pH-DAEs with a more general structure
we refer again to
[
4
]
. Linear-algebraic properties of pH-matrix pencils (such as the index) were analyzed in
[
17
,
18
,
9
]
.

Underlying primal ODE problem.

With projected initial value
x_{1,0}\in\mathbbm{R}^{r}
, (
5
) yields:

\displaystyle E_{11}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}x_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=A_{11}\,x_{1}(t)+A_{12}\,x_{2}(t)+B_{1}\,u(t),\qquad x_{1}(0)=x_{1,0}

(6a)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=A_{21}\,x_{1}(t)+A_{22}\,x_{2}(t)+B_{2}\,u(t)

(6b)

Since
A_{22}
is invertible, we solve (
6b
)
for
x_{2}
and define the
algebraic reconstruction

\hat{x}_{2}(x_{1},t):=-A_{22}^{-1}\bigl(A_{21}\,x_{1}+B_{2}\,u(t)\bigr).

(7)

Substituting into (
6a
) yields the

reduced evolution equation

E_{11}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}x_{1}(t)+S\,x_{1}(t)=F(t),

(8)

with the Schur complement operator and reduced forcing

S:=-\bigl(A_{11}-A_{12}\,A_{22}^{-1}\,A_{21}\bigr),\qquad F(t):=\bigl(B_{1}-A_{12}\,A_{22}^{-1}\,B_{2}\bigr)\,u(t).

(9)

The unique solution
x\in H^{1}(0,T;\mathbbm{R}^{r})
that satisfies (
8
) pointwise almost everywhere and
x_{1}(0)=x_{1,0}\in\mathbbm{R}^{r}
is given by the variations of constants formula

\displaystyle x_{1}(t)=e^{-E_{11}^{-1}S\,t}x_{1,0}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-E_{11}^{-1}S(t-s)}E_{11}^{-1}F(s)\,\mathrm{d}s

(10)

for all
t\geq 0
. The
reduced Hamiltonian
and output reads:

\mathcal{H}(x_{1}):=\tfrac{1}{2}\,x_{1}^{\top}E_{11}\,x_{1},\qquad\hat{y}_{1}=B_{1}^{\top}x_{1}+B_{2}^{\top}\hat{x}_{2}(x_{1},t).

(11)

The matrix
S
(
9
) is generally non-symmetric. We denote its symmetric
part by

\widetilde{S}:=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(S+S^{\top}\bigr).

(12)

In the following, we will repeatedly use the following well-established scaling argument.

Remark 2.2

(Scaling)
.

In general,
\widetilde{S}
is only positive semi-definite due to the dissipativity of
A
. However, for various results it is convenient to assume that
\widetilde{S}
is coercive. This can be achieved by the following scaling argument. Let
x\in H^{1}(0,T;\mathbbm{R}^{r})
solve (
8
), and define

x_{\mu}(t)=e^{-\mu t}x(t),\qquad\mu\in\mathbbm{R}.

Then
x_{\mu}
solves

E_{11}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}x_{\mu}(t)+(S+\mu E_{11})x_{\mu}(t)=e^{-\mu t}F(t).

(13)

Moreover, since

A
is dissipative, the symmetric part (
12
)

satisfies
\widetilde{S}\geq 0
. Therefore,

\langle x,(S+\mu E_{11})x\rangle=\langle x,\widetilde{S}x\rangle+\mu\langle x,E_{11}x\rangle\geq\mu\lambda_{\min}(E_{11})\|x\|^{2}.

Hence, since
E_{11}>0
, the matrix
S+\mu E_{11}
is coercive for every
\mu>0
.
\Box

Weak formulation.

Testing the reduced equation (
8
) against

\varphi\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathbbm{R}^{r})

gives the
following
variational
problem: Find
x_{1}\in H^{1}(0,T;\mathbbm{R}^{r})\hookrightarrow C^{0}\bigl([0,T];\mathbbm{R}^{r}\bigr)
such that

\int_{0}^{T}\bigl\langle E_{11}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}x_{1}(t),\,\varphi(t)\bigr\rangle\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\bigl\langle S\,x_{1}(t),\,\varphi(t)\bigr\rangle\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}\bigl\langle F(t),\,\varphi(t)\bigr\rangle\mathrm{d}t\quad\text{ for all }\varphi\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathbbm{R}^{r}).

(14)

Proposition 2.3

(Well-posedness and regularity)
.

The unique mild solution (given by (
10
)) is also the unique weak solution of (
14
). Moreover, the differential variable
x_{1}
satisfies the estimate

\|x_{1}\|_{C([0,T];\mathbbm{R}^{r})}^{2}+\|x_{1}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;\,\mathbbm{R}^{r})}^{\,2}\leq c\left(\|F\|_{L^{2}(0,T;\mathbbm{R}^{r})}^{\,2}+\|x_{1,0}\|_{E_{11}}^{\,2}\right),

(15)

for some
c\geq 0

and

\|v\|_{E_{11}}^{\,2}:=\langle E_{11}\,v,v\rangle
. The algebraic component
(
7
) satisfies

\hat{x}_{2}\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathbbm{R}^{n-r})
.

If the dynamics (
8
) are exponentially stable, that is, if
-E_{11}^{-1}S
only has eigenvalues with strictly negative real part, then the constant
c\geq 0
in (
15
) is uniform in the time horizon
T
.

Proof.

By the derivation of the weak formulation, the mild solution is also a weak solution.

Uniqueness of the weak solution follows from
(
14
) and the fundamental theorem of calculus of variations. Estimate (
15
) directly follows from
(
10
). The algebraic component satisfies

\hat{x}_{2}(t)=-A_{22}^{-1}(A_{21}\,x_{1}(t)+B_{2}\,u(t))
, so

\hat{x}_{2}\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathbbm{R}^{n-r})
follows from

x_{1}\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathbbm{R}^{r})
and
u\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathbbm{R}^{m})
. In case of exponential stability, i.e.,
\|e^{-E_{11}^{-1}St}\|\leq Me^{-\omega t}
for some
M\geq 1
and

\omega>0
, it follows also from (
10
) that all expressions on the right-hand side are uniformly bounded, i.e.,
the first term is bounded due to
\|e^{-E_{11}^{-1}St}\|\leq M
, the second term by integration of an exponentially decaying function on
[0,\,T]
.

∎

2.2
Goal functional and adjoint problem

We define the quantity of interest (QoI) that is of particular interest in the simulation of port-Hamiltonian systems and in our suggested method, which drives the adaptive
time discretizations.

Energy-balance residual as QoI.

The energy balance (
3
) holds exactly at the
continuous solution of the pH-DAE (
1
). For any
approximate solution
\tilde{x}\colon[0,T]\to\mathbbm{R}^{n}
, we measure
the deviation from this balance on a given time grid
\mathcal{T}

\mathcal{T}:\quad 0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{N}=T

(16)

by the
local
energy-balance residual

I_{\mathrm{loc}}^{i}(\tilde{x}):=\biggl|\,\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}\Bigl[-y(t)^{\top}u(t)+(Q\tilde{x}(t))^{\top}R\,Q\tilde{x}(t)\Bigr]\,\mathrm{d}t+\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{DAE}}\bigl(\tilde{x}(t_{i+1})\bigr)-\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{DAE}}\bigl(\tilde{x}(t_{i})\bigr)\biggr|^{2},

(17)

and the
global quantity of interest

I_{\mathrm{loc}}(\tilde{x}):=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}I_{\mathrm{loc}}^{i}(\tilde{x}).

(18)

By construction,
I_{\mathrm{loc}}(\tilde{x})=0
if and only if the
energy balance (
3
) holds exactly on every
subinterval
[t_{i},t_{i+1}]
. The squaring
in (
17
)
penalizes larger deviations of the energy
and it renders the
functional differentiable, which is required by the DWR framework.

Reduction to the differential variable.

Since the algebraic variable is determined by the algebraic
reconstruction (
7
), the output
y
,
the dissipation, and the Hamiltonian can all be expressed as functions
of
x_{1}
alone. Substituting
\hat{x}_{2}(x_{1},t)

from (
7
) into (
17
)
recasts each local residual (
17
) in terms of the
differential variable only. This motivates the following definitions.

Residual form.

Let
X:=H^{1}(0,T;\mathbbm{R}^{r})
be the trial space for the primal problem.
For
x_{1}\in X
and
\varphi\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathbbm{R}^{r})
, we define the

residual form
induced by the affine linear bounded operator
\mathcal{A}:X\to L^{2}(0,T;\mathbbm{R}^{r})
defined by

\mathcal{A}(x_{1})(\varphi):=\int_{0}^{T}\bigl\langle E_{11}\,\dot{x}_{1}(t)+S\,x_{1}(t)-F(t),\;\varphi(t)\bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t.

(19)

The primal problem (
14
) reads equivalently:
find
x_{1}\in X
such that
\mathcal{A}(x_{1})(\varphi)=0
for all

\varphi\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathbbm{R}^{r})
. Further, the Fréchet derivative with respect to
x_{1}
in any direction

v\in X
is

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(x_{1})(v,w)=\int_{0}^{T}\bigl\langle E_{11}\,\dot{v}(t)+S\,v(t),\;w(t)\bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t

(20)

for all
w\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathbbm{R}^{r})
, and is independent of
x_{1}
.

Reduced goal functional.

On the time grid
\mathcal{T}
,
we define the

reduced goal functional

\mathcal{J}:X\to\mathbbm{R}

\mathcal{J}(x_{1}):=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\bigl|\mathcal{G}_{i}(x_{1})\bigr|^{2},

(21)

where the
local energy-balance residual
on
\mathcal{I}_{i}=(t_{i},t_{i+1})

is

\mathcal{G}_{i}(x_{1})=\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}g\bigl(t,x_{1}(t)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t+\mathcal{H}\bigl(x_{1}(t_{i+1})\bigr)-\mathcal{H}\bigl(x_{1}(t_{i})\bigr)

(22)

with the
power imbalance function

g
encoding supply and dissipation terms after reduction, that is,

g(t,x_{1}):=-\hat{y}(x_{1},t)^{\top}u(t)+\hat{d}(x_{1},t)\quad\text{with}\quad\hat{d}(x_{1},t):=\bigl(Q(Vx_{1}+W\hat{x}_{2})\bigr)^{\top}R\,\bigl(Q(Vx_{1}+W\hat{x}_{2})\bigr).

using the reduced output
\hat{y}
defined by (
11
) and the
reduced dissipation

\hat{d}
(obtained from the kernel splitting pair in
A
).

Then,
\mathcal{G}_{i}
is quadratic and
\mathcal{J}
is quartic in
x_{1}
.

The reduced goal functional
\mathcal{J}(x_{1})
(
21
) is the global QoI (
18
) expressed in
x_{1}
, that is,
\mathcal{J}(x_{1})=I_{\mathrm{loc}}(\tilde{z})
when

\tilde{z}=Vx_{1}+W\hat{x}_{2}(x_{1},\cdot)
. We work exclusively with

\mathcal{J}
from this point onward, as the reduced formulation is the
natural setting for the variational formulation and error estimation.

Adjoint problem.

For a direction
v\in X
, the Gâteaux derivative of
\mathcal{J}
(
21
) is

\mathcal{J}^{\prime}(x_{1})(v)=2\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{G}_{i}(x_{1})\,\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\prime}(x_{1})(v),

(23)

where the local derivative, obtained by
differentiating (
22
), reads

\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\prime}(x_{1})(v)=\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\bigl\langle\nabla_{x_{1}}g(t,x_{1}),\,v(t)\bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t+\bigl\langle E_{11}\,x_{1}(t_{i}),\,v(t_{i})\bigr\rangle-\bigl\langle E_{11}\,x_{1}(t_{i-1}),\,v(t_{i-1})\bigr\rangle,

(24)

where we have used that
\nabla_{x_{1}}\mathcal{H}(x_{1})=E_{11}\,x_{1}
.
Because
\mathcal{J}^{\prime}(x_{1})(v)
contains point evaluations, the adjoint
solution subject to this derivative as a source term will not be globally continuous. Thus, we introduce the space of piecewise absolutely continuous functions

Z:=\bigl\{\,z\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathbbm{R}^{r})\;\big|\;z|_{I_{i}}\in H^{1}(I_{i};\mathbbm{R}^{r})\text{ for }i=1,\dots,N\bigr\}.

(25)

The
adjoint

z\in Z
is defined as the
solution of

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(x_{1})(\varphi,z)=\mathcal{J}^{\prime}(x_{1})(\varphi)\quad\forall\,\varphi\in X.

(26)

Note that existence and uniqueness of the adjoint follows from analogous considerations as in
[
3
, Section 3.1]
. At this point, the symbol
z
refers exclusively to the adjoint variable for the remainder of the paper; the descriptor state of the pH-DAE (
1
) has been eliminated in favor of the differential variable
x_{1}
via the kernel splitting of Section
2.1
.

Remark 2.4
.

At the exact primal solution
x_{1}

), the energy balance violation vanishes, that is,

, so the right-hand side

t_{i}\in[0,\,T]

Lemma 2.5

(Strong form of the adjoint)
.

The unique solution
z\in Z
of the adjoint

system:

\displaystyle-E_{11}\,\dot{z}(t)+S^{\top}z(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{f}_{z}(t),

\displaystyle t\in\mathcal{I}_{i},

(28a)

\displaystyle E_{11}\,[z]_{i}

Remark 2.6

(Interpretation of the adjoint problem)
.

The adjoint problem (
28
) is a backward-in-time ODE
with piecewise smooth forcing
\mathbf{f}_{z}
on each subinterval.
At each interior node
t_{i}
, the adjoint experiences

a jump proportional to
\mathcal{G}_{i}-\mathcal{G}_{i-1}
, that is, it is only an ODE in the usual sense on each subinterval.

This reflects the mismatch in the energy-balance residual
between adjacent subintervals.

2.3
Norm-augmented weighted quantity of interest

As alternative to the purely energy-targeted functional
I_{\mathrm{loc}}(\tilde{x})
defined in (
18
), we introduce a norm-augmented QoI that balances accuracy in the
discrete energy balance with accuracy of the state trajectory.
Using the underlying Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
and a scalar parameter
\rho\geq 0
, we define

I_{\mathrm{loc},\rho}(\tilde{x}):=I_{\mathrm{loc}}(\tilde{x})+\rho\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{H}\bigl(\tilde{x}(t)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t.

(32)

The additional term corresponds to an
L^{2}
-in-time energy functional
and induces the energy norm in the differential variables (that is,
x_{1}
after transformation)

\|\tilde{x}\|_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}:=\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{H}\bigl(\tilde{x}(t)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t.

The
dual problem is constructed
with this modified QoI.
This choice of the QoI (
32
) yields a smooth quadratic contribution to the goal functional and leads to a well-defined adjoint equation.

Remark 3.3
.

The system matrix

is generally non-symmetric.

Its symmetric part is

E_{11}+k_{i}\,\widetilde{S}
is positive definite as

is dissipative, hence has positive semi-definite symmetric part. As

Note that

auxiliary discrete sequence defined by the shifted recursion with the

Lemma 3.4

(Primal stability)
.

Let Assumption
2.1

Then, the solution

&\leq c(\mu,T)\frac{1}{\min\{(\alpha+\mu),\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(E_{11})\}}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha+\mu}\int_{0}^{T}\|F(t)\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t+\|x_{1,0}\|_{E_{11}}^{2}\right),\end{split}

(37)

where

is a constant with
c(\mu,T)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mu\to 0}}{{\to}}1

\widetilde{S}

Proof.

We introduce

(x_{\mu})_{k}\in X_{k}

Rearranging gives

\bigl\langle E_{11}\bigl((x_{\mu})_{k}^{i}-(x_{\mu})_{k}^{i-1}\bigr),\,(x_{\mu})_{k}^{i}\bigr\rangle+k_{i}\,\bigl\langle S_{\mu}\,(x_{\mu})_{k}^{i},\,(x_{\mu})_{k}^{i}\bigr\rangle=\int_{I_{i}}\bigl\langle F(t),\,(x_{\mu})_{k}^{i}\bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t.

(38)

We sum over

and estimate each group of terms.

Step 1 (Time-derivative term).

The algebraic identity

\langle A(a{-}b),a\rangle=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\|a\|_{A}^{2}-\|b\|_{A}^{2}+\|a{-}b\|_{A}^{2}\bigr)

applied with

,

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl\langle E_{11}\bigl((x_{\mu})_{k}^{i}-(x_{\mu})_{k}^{i-1}\bigr),\,(x_{\mu})_{k}^{i}\bigr\rangle=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\|(x_{\mu})_{k}^{N}\|_{E_{11}}^{2}-\|x_{1,0}\|_{E_{11}}^{2}\bigr)+\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\|(x_{\mu})_{k}^{i+1}-(x_{\mu})_{k}^{i}\|_{E_{11}}^{2}.

Since

\sum_{i=1}^{N}k_{i}\,\langle S_{\mu}\,(x_{\mu})_{k}^{i},\,(x_{\mu})_{k}^{i}\rangle\geq(\alpha+\mu)\sum_{i=1}^{N}k_{i}\,\|(x_{\mu})_{k}^{i}\|^{2}.

Step 3 (Right-hand side).

By Young’s inequality with parameter
\varepsilon=\alpha+\mu

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{I_{i}}\bigl\langle F(t),\,(x_{\mu})_{k}^{i}\bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\frac{1}{2(\alpha+\mu)}\int_{0}^{T}\|F(t)\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t+\frac{\alpha+\mu}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}k_{i}\,\|(x_{\mu})_{k}^{i}\|^{2}.

Combining the above steps and rearranging gives

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\|(x_{\mu})_{k}^{N}\|_{E_{11}}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\!\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\|(x_{\mu})_{k}^{i+1}\!-(x_{\mu})_{k}^{i}\|_{E_{11}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\tfrac{\alpha+\mu}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}k_{i}\,\|(x_{\mu})_{k}^{i}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\|x_{1,0}\|_{E_{11}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2(\alpha+\mu)}\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\|F(t)\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t.

Dividing by
\tfrac{1}{2}\min\{(\alpha+\mu),\,\lambda_{\min}(E_{11})\}
to convert all

37

(without

Using Lemma
3.2
(assumptions are fulfilled), we obtain for the unscaled variable estimate (
37

Lemma 3.5

(A priori error estimate)
.

Let

8

In finite dimensions, coercivity of the operator is equivalent to

\mathbf{A}^{\!\top}\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{R}

\begin{pmatrix}E_{11}+k_{1}S^{\top}&-E_{11}&&\\

(

\mathbf{R}_{i}=2\,\mathcal{G}_{i}(x_{k})\int_{I_{i}}\nabla_{x_{1}}g\bigl(t,x_{k}^{i}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t+2\bigl(\mathcal{G}_{i}(x_{k})-\mathcal{G}_{i+1}(x_{k})\bigr)\,E_{11}\,x_{k}^{i},\qquad i=1,\dots,N,

with the convention

The backward recurrence emerging from the system (

Definition 3.6

(Discrete adjoint scheme)

(Adjoint stability)

Let Assumption

Remark 3.9

(Scaling of the source norm)
.

The bound (
44
) involves the weighted
sum
\sum_{i}\|\mathbf{R}_{i}\|^{2}/k_{i}
. The discrete
source (
42
) contains an integral term of size

and an endpoint

, so

\|\mathbf{R}_{i}\|^{2}/k_{i}=O(1/k_{i})

In a piecewise constant time discretization, such nodal contributions
naturally lead to the observed

scaling.

The analysis follows the DWR framework

4.1

We define the
Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}\colon X\times Z\to\mathbbm{R}

(46)

with primal trial space

,

, (

),

\mathcal{J}
, (

), and residual

At the exact primal solution

\mathcal{A}(x_{1})(\cdot)=0

\mathcal{L}(x_{1},z)=\mathcal{J}(x_{1})

At the discrete level, the dG(0) solution

Theorem 4.1

(DWR error identity)
.

Let
(x_{1},z)\in X\times Z
solve the continuous primal (

(x_{k},z_{k})\in X_{k}\times X_{k}

\displaystyle\rho(x_{k})(w)

\displaystyle:=-\mathcal{A}(x_{k})(w),\qquad\rho^{*}(x_{k},z_{k})(v):=\mathcal{J}^{\prime}(x_{k})(v)-\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(x_{k})(v,z_{k}).

(47)

Then, for any
\varphi_{k},\psi_{k}\in X_{k}

\mathcal{J}(x_{1})-\mathcal{J}(x_{k})=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\rho(x_{k})(z-\varphi_{k})+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\rho^{*}(x_{k},z_{k})(x_{1}-\psi_{k})+\mathcal{R}^{(3)},

(48)

where the remainder is cubic in the primal error
e:=x_{1}-x_{k}

Proof.

For a proof, see

We evaluate the abstract residuals (

piecewise constant structure of the dG(0) discretization. Since

and

are constant on each interval, their time derivatives vanish
in the interior, and the residuals decompose into interior contributions
(from the algebraic mismatch with the right-hand side) and nodal
contributions (from the jumps at the time nodes).

Lemma 4.2

(Primal residual)
.

For any
w\in X
, the primal residual (
47
)
decomposes as

\rho(x_{k})(w)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl[\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{int}}^{i}(w)+\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{jump}}^{i}(w)\bigr],

(49)

with, using the jump notation
[x_{k}]_{i}
as defined in (
27
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{int}}^{i}(w)

\displaystyle:=\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\bigl\langle F(t)-S\,x_{k}^{i},\;w(t)\bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t,\qquad\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{jump}}^{i}(w):=-\bigl\langle E_{11}\,[x_{k}]_{i},\;w(t_{i-1}^{+})\bigr\rangle,

(50)

where
[x_{k}]_{i}:=x_{k}^{i}-x_{k}^{i-1}
with
x_{k}^{0}=x_{1,0}
.

Proof.

By definition,

\rho(x_{k})(w)=-\mathcal{A}(x_{k})(w)=-\int_{0}^{T}\langle E_{11}\dot{x}_{k}+S\,x_{k}-F,\;w\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t
.
On each open interval
I_{i}=(t_{i-1},t_{i}]
,
x_{k}
is constant, so
\dot{x}_{k}=0

and the interior contribution reduces to

\int_{I_{i}}\langle F-S\,x_{k}^{i},\;w\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t
.
The distributional time derivative of the piecewise constant function

x_{k}
is

E_{11}\dot{x}_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}E_{11}[x_{k}]_{i}\,\delta_{t_{i-1}}
,
where
\delta_{t_{i-1}}
denotes the Dirac delta, that is, point evaluation at
t_{i-1}
. Testing
against the continuous function
w
produces the nodal
terms
\langle E_{11}[x_{k}]_{i},\,w(t_{i-1})\rangle
. Collecting all
contributions with the appropriate signs
yields (
50
).
∎

Lemma 4.3

(Dual residual)
.

For any
v\in X
, the dual residual in (
47
)
decomposes as

\displaystyle\rho^{*}(x_{k},z_{k})(v)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{int}}^{*,i}(v)+\sum_{i=2}^{N}\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{jump}}^{*,i}(v)+\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{bdy}}^{*}(v),

(51)

\displaystyle\text{with}\qquad\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{int}}^{*,i}(v):=\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\bigl\langle\mathbf{f}_{z}(t)-S^{\top}z_{k}^{i},\;v(t)\bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t,\qquad\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{jump}}^{*,i}(v):=\bigl\langle E_{11}(z_{k}^{i-1}-z_{k}^{i})-\mathbf{j}_{z}(t_{i-1}),\;v(t_{i-1})\bigr\rangle,\quad\,\mbox{}

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{bdy}}^{*}(v):=\bigl\langle\mathbf{b}_{z}(T)-E_{11}\,z_{k}^{N},\;v(T)\bigr\rangle,

(52)

where
\mathbf{f}_{z}
,
\mathbf{j}_{z}
, and
\mathbf{b}_{z}
are the
continuous adjoint right-hand side terms defined
in (
29
).

Proof.

By definition,

\rho^{*}(x_{k},z_{k})(v)=\mathcal{J}^{\prime}(x_{k})(v)-\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(x_{k})(v,z_{k})
.
We inspect the derivative term inside
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(x_{k})(v,z_{k})

(cf. (
20
)).
Since
z_{k}
is piecewise
constant,
\dot{z}_{k}=0
, on each
I_{i}
, the integration by parts
produces the interior term

\langle S\,v,z_{k}^{i}\rangle=\langle v,S^{\top}z_{k}^{i}\rangle

together with boundary terms at the nodes.
Collecting terms together and using the continuity of
v
at the nodes gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(x_{k})(v,z_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{I_{i}}\bigl\langle v,\,S^{\top}\!z_{k}^{i}\bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t+\bigl\langle v(T),\,E_{11}\,z_{k}^{N}\bigr\rangle-\bigl\langle v(0),\,E_{11}\,z_{k}^{1}\bigr\rangle+\sum_{i=2}^{N}\bigl\langle v(t_{i-1}),\,E_{11}(z_{k}^{i}\!-\!z_{k}^{i-1})\bigr\rangle.

Subtracting this equality
from
\mathcal{J}^{\prime}(x_{k})(v)

(expanded in (
2.2
)) and grouping the terms
yields (
52
).
∎

Remark 4.4
.

The dual residual
\rho^{*}
measures how well the discrete adjoint

z_{k}
satisfies the continuous adjoint equation. In the standard DWR
simplification (see below Section
4.2
), this term is
neglected. This is justified because the dual residual involves the
product of the adjoint discretization error and the primal
interpolation error, making it higher-order relative to the primal
residual.

4.2
Computable error estimator and goal-oriented adaptive algorithm

The error identity (
48
) involves the unknown exact
solutions
x_{1}
and
z
. We apply three standard
ramifications
[
1
,
21
]
to obtain a computable quantity.

Step 1: Neglect cubic remainder.

The term
\mathcal{R}^{(3)}=O(\|e\|_{X}^{3})
is asymptotically negligible
for sufficiently fine time meshes.

Step 2: Drop dual residual.

The dual residual is
of higher order than the primal residual. Neglecting it yields

\mathcal{J}(x_{1})-\mathcal{J}(x_{k})\approx\rho(x_{k})(z-z_{k}).

(53)

Step 3: Approximate adjoint error weight.

Following
[
8
]
, the exact adjoint error
z-z_{k}

is replaced by

w_{k}(t):=\tilde{z}_{k}(t)-z_{k}(t),

(54)

where
\tilde{z}_{k}(t)
is a
piecewise linear (and globally continuous) interpolant with nodal values

\tilde{z}_{k}(t_{i}):=\begin{cases}z_{k}^{1}&\text{if }i=0,\\[3.0pt]
\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(z_{k}^{i}+z_{k}^{i+1}\bigr)&\text{if }1\leq i\leq N{-}1,\\[3.0pt]
0&\text{if }i=N.\end{cases}

(55)

Substituting the weight (
54
) into the primal
residual (
49
) yields the computable
indicator.
For the nodal values of
w_{k}
, we obtain

w_{k}(t_{i-1}^{+})=\tilde{z}_{k}(t_{i-1})-z_{k}^{i}=-\tfrac{1}{2}\,\Delta z_{i},\qquad w_{k}(t_{i}^{-})=\tilde{z}_{k}(t_{i})-z_{k}^{i}=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\Delta z_{i+1},

(56)

where
\Delta z_{j}:=z_{k}^{j}-z_{k}^{j-1}
denotes the adjoint jump at
node
t_{j-1}
(with the convention
\Delta z_{1}=0
from the boundary
rule at
i=0
, so that
w_{k}(t_{0}^{+})=0
).

Definition 4.5

(Local error indicator)
.

The
signed
error indicator on interval
I_{i}
is

\eta_{i}:=\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{int}}^{i}(w_{k})+\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{jump}}^{i}(w_{k}).

(57)

The global estimator and the refinement indicator are

\eta_{\mathrm{tot}}:=\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\eta_{i}\Bigr|,\qquad\bar{\eta}_{i}:=|\eta_{i}|.

(58)

Remark 4.6

(Signed vs. absolute indicators)
.

The global stopping criterion

\eta_{\mathrm{tot}}=|\sum_{i}\eta_{i}|

respects the error
identity (
48
) and permits cancellation of
positive and negative
\eta_{i}
.
Using

\sum_{i}|\eta_{i}|
instead would inflate the estimator. The absolute
values
\bar{\eta}_{i}=|\eta_{i}|

are solely needed for Dörfler marking, where the goal is to identify the
intervals with the largest error contribution regardless of sign.

Algebraic evaluation and adaptive refinement algorithm.

Since
w_{k}
is linear on
I_{i}
, each component
of (
57
) can be evaluated explicitly.
For the interior term,
\mathcal{R}^{i}_{\mathrm{int}}
(
50
),
the constant part

-S\,x_{k}^{i}
integrates exactly against the linear weight:

\int_{I_{i}}\bigl\langle-S\,x_{k}^{i},\;w_{k}(t)\bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t=\bigl\langle-S\,x_{k}^{i},\;\tfrac{k_{i}}{2}\bigl(w_{k}(t_{i-1}^{+})+w_{k}(t_{i}^{-})\bigr)\bigr\rangle=\bigl\langle-S\,x_{k}^{i},\;\tfrac{k_{i}}{4}(\Delta z_{i+1}-\Delta z_{i})\bigr\rangle.

(59)

The right-hand side
F(t)=(B_{1}-A_{12}A_{22}^{-1}B_{2})\,u(t)

contribution is evaluated using
the interval average

\bar{F}_{i}:=k_{i}^{-1}\int_{I_{i}}F(t)\,\mathrm{d}t
(or an equivalent
low-order quadrature).
With this approximation,

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{int}}^{i}(w_{k})\approx\bigl\langle\bar{F}_{i}-S\,x_{k}^{i},\;\tfrac{k_{i}}{4}(\Delta z_{i+1}-\Delta z_{i})\bigr\rangle.

(60)

The jump term,
\mathcal{R}^{i}_{\mathrm{jump}}
(
50
), is evaluated at
t_{i-1}^{+}
using the

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{jump}}^{i}(w_{k})=-\bigl\langle E_{11}[x_{k}]_{i},\;w_{k}(t_{i-1}^{+})\bigr\rangle=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl\langle E_{11}(x_{k}^{i}-x_{k}^{i-1}),\;\Delta z_{i}\bigr\rangle.

(61)

Combining (
60
)
and (
61
), the indicator takes the algebraic form

\boxed{\eta_{i}=\bigl\langle\bar{F}_{i}-S\,x_{k}^{i},\;\tfrac{k_{i}}{4}(\Delta z_{i+1}-\Delta z_{i})\bigr\rangle+\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl\langle E_{11}(x_{k}^{i}-x_{k}^{i-1}),\;\Delta z_{i}\bigr\rangle+O\bigl(k_{i}^{2}\bigr).}

(62)

The first term measures the mismatch between the right-hand side and
the discrete operator, weighted by the adjoint sensitivity. The second
term penalizes large state jumps in regions of high adjoint sensitivity.
Together, refinement is concentrated on intervals where the
discretization error and the sensitivity of the goal functional are
simultaneously large.

Remark 4.7

(Simplified indicator)
.

If only the leading adjoint difference is retained (i.e.,

(63)

This is computationally cheaper and sufficient for driving refinement
in practice, as it retains the correct scaling and captures the dominant
contribution from the state jump.

Remark 4.8
.

By the boundary rule

5
Parallel computation of adjoint approximations

The solution of the adjoint equation constitutes the main computational overhead of the proposed adaptive method. To alleviate the computational cost necessary for evaluation of the error indicators, we suggest a computationally cheap approximation of this adjoint system. The backward recurrence (
43
) resembles a backwards-in-time discrete-time system, hence intervals are coupled to the neighboring ones.
In this part, we show that a
strictly
dissipative structure of the pH-DAE allows for dropping the inter-interval coupling, enabling full parallelized computation of the adjoint. We show that due to the port-Hamiltonian structure, the Block-Jacobi iteration is a strict contraction.

Intuitively, this parallelization property and block diagonalization leverages that error transport is not global, and it suffices to only consider neighboring subsystems. A similar stability property was used in
[
12
]
to enable local refinements in predictive control algorithms.

We recall the linear system for the discrete adjoint problem
\mathbf{A}^{\!\top}\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{R}
, which is explicitly stated in (
41
).

Now, we decompose
\mathbf{A}^{\!\top}=\mathcal{D}-\mathcal{U}
, where

\mathcal{D}:=\mathrm{blockdiag}(M_{0},\dots,M_{N-1})
with
M_{i}:=E_{11}+k_{i}S^{\top}
and

\mathcal{U}
contains the super-diagonal blocks
E_{11}
. The

Block-Jacobi iteration
for
\mathbf{Z}^{(l)}
,
l=0,1,\dotsc
, reads

\mathcal{D}\,\mathbf{Z}^{(\ell+1)}=\mathcal{U}\,\mathbf{Z}^{(\ell)}+\mathbf{R},\qquad\mathbf{Z}^{(0)}=\mathbf{0}.

(64)

This decouples across all intervals: each local system

M_{i}\,z_{k}^{i,(\ell+1)}=E_{11}\,z_{k}^{i+1,(\ell)}+\mathbf{R}_{i}

(
i=0,\,\dotsc,\,N{-}1
) can be solved independently and hence in parallel.

A single, first sweep (
\ell=0
)
corresponds to solving the block-diagonal
system
\mathcal{D}\,\hat{\mathbf{Z}}=\mathbf{R}
.

Dropping generally the coupling
\mathcal{U}
amounts to neglecting the backward
transport of adjoint information from future intervals.
A fully dissipative pH system will ensure that this information decays exponentially, causing the successive iterates to converge rapidly.

Convergence analysis.

The error in the Block-Jacobi iteration propagates backward through the

amplification matrix

\Gamma_{i}:=M_{i}^{-1}\,E_{11}=\bigl(E_{11}+k_{i}\,S^{\top}\bigr)^{-1}\,E_{11},\qquad i=0,\dots,N{-}1.

(65)

After
\ell
sweeps, the Jacobi error will involve
\Gamma_{i}^{\,\ell}
. Thus,
the iteration converges for arbitrary right-hand side terms
if and only if
\Gamma_{i}
is a strict contraction.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let Assumptions
2.1
hold and assume assume that the smallest eigenvalue
\alpha
of
\tilde{S}
satisfies
\alpha>0
.
Then for every
k_{i}>0
and every
v\in\mathbbm{R}^{r}\setminus\{0\}
,
\Gamma_{i}
is a
strict contraction in the
E_{11}
-norm, i.e.,
\|\Gamma_{i}\,v\|_{E_{11}}<\|v\|_{E_{11}}
.

Proof.

Set
w:=\Gamma_{i}\,v
. Then

M_{i}\,w=E_{11}\,v
, i.e.,

E_{11}(v-w)=k_{i}\,S^{\top}w
.
Expanding
\|v\|_{E_{11}}^{2}=\|w+(v{-}w)\|_{E_{11}}^{2}
gives

\|v\|_{E_{11}}^{2}=\|w\|_{E_{11}}^{2}+2\,\langle E_{11}(v{-}w),\,w\rangle+\|v{-}w\|_{E_{11}}^{2}.

Substituting
E_{11}(v{-}w)=k_{i}\,S^{\top}w
and using
\langle S^{\top}w,w\rangle=\langle\widetilde{S}\,w,w\rangle\geq\alpha\,\|w\|^{2}>0

yields

\|v\|_{E_{11}}^{2}=\|w\|_{E_{11}}^{2}+2k_{i}\,\langle\widetilde{S}\,w,w\rangle+\|v-w\|_{E_{11}}^{2}>\|w\|_{E_{11}}^{2}=\|\Gamma_{i}v\|_{E_{11}}^{2},

which is the contraction.
∎

We comment on the central assumption of the above result:
\lambda_{\min}(\widetilde{S})>0
.

In this case, the dynamics are exponentially stable such that perturbations are exponentially damped.
Thus,
the sensitivities are strongly localized, allowing for decoupled solution of the adjoint. We refer to
[
12
]
for a similar result in the context of goal-oriented error estimation in optimal control problems on large time horizons.

Remark 5.2

(Quantitative contraction rate)
.

The spectral radius
\rho(\Gamma_{i})
of the iteration matrix
\Gamma_{i}

describes the contraction rate of the Jacobi iteration.
Since
\Gamma_{i}=(E_{11}+k_{i}S^{\top})^{-1}E_{11}
, any eigenvalue

\lambda
of
\Gamma_{i}
satisfies
E_{11}\,v=\lambda(E_{11}+k_{i}S^{\top})v

for some
v\neq 0
, i.e.
(1-\lambda)\,E_{11}\,v=\lambda\,k_{i}\,S^{\top}v
.
Taking the inner product with
v
and using

\langle S^{\top}v,v\rangle=\langle\widetilde{S}\,v,v\rangle+\mathrm{i}\,\mathrm{Im}\langle S^{\top}v,v\rangle
gives

|\lambda|=\frac{1}{|1+k_{i}\sigma|}\leq\frac{1}{1+k_{i}\,\mu_{\min}},\qquad\mu_{\min}:=\lambda_{\min}(\widetilde{S},\,E_{11}),

(66)

where
\sigma
is the corresponding generalised eigenvalue of
(S^{\top},E_{11})
,
which satisfies
\mathrm{Re}(\sigma)\geq\mu_{\min}>0
.
Equality holds when
S
is symmetric; in general, the imaginary part of
\sigma

provides additional contraction beyond the bound (
66
).
The upper bound decreases monotonically as
k_{i}
increases: larger time steps
yield stronger contraction.

validates (

) numerically.

7
Conclusion

We have presented a goal-oriented time-adaptive scheme for linear port-Hamiltonian DAEs of index 1, combining the dual weighted residual method with the intrinsic energy-balance structure of pH systems. The approach treats the violation of the energy balance as a computable quantity of interest and adapts the time grid to reduce it, with full freedom in the choice of the underlying time integrator. The main contributions are: (i) a Schur-complement reduction that formulates the DWR framework entirely in the differential variable; (ii) a complete a posteriori error representation with computable local indicators; (iii) a parallelizable Block-Jacobi approximation of the adjoint whose contraction is guaranteed by the dissipative pH structure; and (iv) numerical validation demonstrating savings of up to 89% in the number of time intervals compared to uniform refinement.

For our specific quantity of interest

\mathcal{J}(u_{k})=\sum_{i}G_{i}^{2}
,
the adjoint equation is driven by the right-hand side

2G_{i}\,\nabla_{x_{i+1}}G_{i}
, such that the dual solution
z_{i}
is
structurally correlated with the primal residuals
G_{i}
themselves.
As a consequence, any local marking criterion proportional to
|G_{i}|

identifies essentially the same dominant intervals as the global DWR
estimator
\eta_{i}=\langle z_{i},r_{i}\rangle
. The advantage of DWR
over such heuristics therefore lies not primarily in interval
selection but in the rigorous
a posteriori
error representation

\mathcal{J}(u)-\mathcal{J}(u_{k})\approx\sum_{i}\eta_{i}
and the
resulting effectivity index (Table
2
), which
provides certified error control unavailable to purely local methods.
For goal functionals that are not themselves residual-based—such as
a pointwise output evaluation
\mathcal{J}(u_{k})=|y(t^{*})|^{2}

for a fixed observation time
t^{*}
—the
adjoint genuinely identifies intervals where local error is small but
global sensitivity is large, and the advantage of DWR over local
strategies would be substantially larger.

The dG(0) discretization used in this work is first-order accurate, and the DWR theory extends directly to higher-order discontinuous Galerkin methods
[
21
]
. Replacing dG(0) by dG(
s
) for
s\geq 1
would yield higher-order convergence rates in both the state and the goal functional, with the adjoint computation and indicator evaluation following the same block structure derived in Sections
4
and
5
. A second natural direction is the extension to nonlinear port-Hamiltonian systems, where the Hamiltonian or the structure matrices depend on the state. The Lagrangian-based error identity (Theorem
4.1
) does not require linearity of the underlying dynamics, but the stability analysis and the contraction guarantee for the Block-Jacobi iteration (Proposition
5.1
) rely on the linear structure and would require appropriate generalizations. These extensions are the subject of ongoing work.
