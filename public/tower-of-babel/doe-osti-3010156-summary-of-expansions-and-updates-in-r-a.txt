 INTRODUCTION

The research and development (R&D) version of Greenhouse gases, Regulated Emissions, and
Energy use in Technologies (GREET®) model, or R&D GREET, is developed by Argonne
National Laboratory (Argonne) with the support of the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) and
other federal agencies. R&D GREET is a life cycle analysis (LCA) model, structured to
systematically examine the energy and environmental effects of a wide variety of transportation
fuels and vehicle technologies in all transportation sub-sectors (i.e., road, air, marine, and rail) and
other end-use sectors, and energy technology systems. Argonne has expanded and updated the
model in many areas in R&D GREET 2025 (Wang et al., 2025). This report provides a summary
of the expansions and updates.
R&D GREET is designed as an attributional LCA model, but specific consequential effects for
certain pathways are included by default when they are scientifically important and/or highly
policy relevant. For example, two major consequential elements – induced land use change (ILUC)
for biofuels and avoided emissions for certain RNG-based pathways – are implemented by default
in R&D GREET because they are highly relevant to our user base and crucial for a comprehensive
evaluation of the environmental effects of these technologies. In a future version of R&D GREET,
these consequential elements for biofuels and RNG could be more clearly distinguished from core
attributional results. We will consider adding a highly visible toggle that would allow users to turn
off or adjust these consequential impacts.

 EXPANSIONS AND UPDATES

1. NATURAL GAS UPDATE

Life cycle inventory (LCI) data for natural gas (NG) pathways have been updated with the latest
datasets published by Khutal et al. (2025). To streamline implementation, the physical properties
of NG were aggregated based on delivery shares, reducing the number of property sets and
emission-factor technologies. The method for calculating NG equivalent loss has also been revised
to use the methane content of NG, merging methane loss and methane-equivalent loss into a single
category. These updates are intended to enhance efficiency in R&D GREET Excel, reduce
redundancy and complexity, and simplify R&D GREET.NET implementation. In addition, a
dedicated basin-to-region (between supply and demand) NG pathway for hydrogen production has
been added in the NG_Basins tab. This is similar to the separate region‑to‑region NG pathway for
hydrogen production in the NG_Regions tab. The Basins NG option for hydrogen production can
be enabled from Table 6.9 in the Inputs tab.

2. FEEDSTOCK PRODUCTION FOR BIOFUELS

2.1 Herbicide Use for Intermediate Winter Camelina Update
In R&D GREET 2024 Rev.1, the glyphosate application rate was assumed to be 4.8 g/kg oilseed,
which, combined with a yield of 1,178 kg/ha, results in a total application of 5,654 g acid
equivalent (a.e.)/ha. Acid equivalent refers to the amount of the glyphosate active ingredient in its
acid form, providing a standardized measure for comparing application rates across different
commercial formulations.
This assumed rate, however, exceeded the maximum application rate of Roundup Powermax 3
(1,260 g a.e./ha), a common glyphosate product from Bayer. For this product, growers typically
apply only about 50% of the recommended maximum application rate for pre-planting burndown,
implying a practical rate of approximately 630 g a.e./ha. ANL consequently reached out to Dr.
Berti at North Dakota State University, whose work in 2017 reported a glyphosate application rate
of 5.1 kg active ingredient (a.i.)/ha and was a major data source for the previous assumption in
R&D GREET 2024 Rev.1. Dr. Berti acknowledged that the reported rate was substantially higher
than current agronomic practices and may have reflected older assumptions or different glyphosate
formulations. In a recent study that is currently under review, Dr. Berti et al. consider a much lower
glysophate application rate of 1.16 kg a.i./ha for winter camelina, which aligns with typical grower
practices and commonly applied glyphosate formulations.
Based on these inputs, R&D GREET 2025 has been revised to adopt a glyphosate application rate
of 1.16 kg a.i./ha (≈470 g a.i./acre) for winter camelina.

2.2. Canola Farming Life-Cycle Inventory Data Updates
Canola (Brassica napus L.) cultivation is a key component of North American agriculture, serving
both as a food-grade oil source and a biofuel feedstock. The LCI data for U.S. canola in R&D
GREET 2025 are derived from national survey data and an LCA report by (S&T)² Consultants Inc.
on Canadian spring canola farming, given the agroecological and management similarities with
northern U.S. spring canola systems. Since winter canola accounts for less than 5% of total U.S.
canola acreage (U.S. Canola Association, 2024), this dataset is considered representative of the
U.S. national average.
 2

In R&D GREET 2024, we updated parameters related to soil N₂O emissions associated with
synthetic nitrogen fertilizer and crop residue nitrogen. Notably, the data source for crop residue
nitrogen was switched from Janzen et al. (2003) to Thiagarajan et al. (2018), intended to reflect
the more recent study. After consulting with Environment and Climate Change Canada (ECCC),
responsible for compiling Canada’s National GHG Inventory, we reverted the data source to
Janzen et al. (2003) in R&D GREET 2025 since ECCC confirmed its representativeness for canola.
We also incorporated corrections from the (S&T)² Consultants LCA report regarding on-farm
diesel and electricity consumption.
In addition to spring and winter canola, we included intermediate winter canola as an added
potential biofuel feedstock. When planted between regular cropping cycles on otherwise fallow
land, intermediate winter canola offers co-benefits such as reducing soil erosion, preventing
nutrient loss, and providing pollinator habitat, while generating additional revenue from harvested
seeds. The LCI for intermediate winter canola is based on third-party field trial data spanning over
4,000 acres and 11 million pounds of crop. This work is in preparation for journal publication
(Kwon et al., forthcoming).

2.3. Dedicated Energy Crop Farming Life-Cycle Inventory Data Updates
In collaboration with Oak Ridge National Laboratory, we refined crop production and budget data
from the U.S. Department of Energy’s Billion-Ton 2023 (BT23) report for five dedicated energy
crops: biomass sorghum, switchgrass, miscanthus, willow, and poplar. Updates include biomass
yields, on-farm diesel use, fertilizer and chemical application rates, and soil N₂O emission
parameters. For herbaceous perennial crops (switchgrass and miscanthus) and woody crops
(willow and poplar), where multiple harvests occur over several years, all parameter estimates
were annualized based on the respective crop lifespans. Specifically, soil N₂O emissions from root
residue remaining after the final harvest were included, assuming natural decomposition for all
crops. Induced land-use changes and other indirect market-mediate effects were also addressed
with GTAP simulation results by Purdue University. This work is currently being prepared for
journal publication (Kwon et al., forthcoming).

2.4 R&D Feedstock Carbon Intensity Calculator (FD-CIC)
We expanded R&D FD-CIC to include two primary wheat types—winter and spring wheat—
distinguished by growing season, as well as spring canola, the dominant canola type by U.S.
acreage. In addition, we updated estimates of soil organic carbon stock changes for corn, soybeans,
grain sorghum, and spring canola using the DayCent process-based simulation model, replacing
the previous modeling results from the CENTURY model.

3. BIOENERGY WITH CARBON CAPTURE AND STORAGE

BioEnergy from woody biomass with Carbon Capture and Storage (BECCS) is a carbon dioxide
removal (CDR) technology that provides renewable energy with low emissions. Its energy and
CDR performance depend on the type of biomass feedstock used, which is influenced by proximity
to the feedstock, configurations of the BECCS system, and life-cycle analysis (LCA) methodology.
We completed an LCA of biopower/bioheat BECCS systems using woodchips and pellets from
the U.S. southern loblolly pine–based woody biomass. We evaluated life-cycle GHG emissions of
two products/services provided by two BECCS systems — an electricity-only and a combined heat
and power (CHP) systems. We applied three LCA co-product methods: (1) energy products with
carbon capture and storage (CCS) credit, (2) incremental approach, and (3) upstream emission
counterbalancing. We demonstrate how LCA methods, feedstock sources, and BECCS system
designs shape their life-cycle GHG emissions effects. These options are implemented in R&D
GREET 2025. See more details in Do et al. (2025).

Do, T.N., Cai, H., Wang, M. (2025). Life-Cycle Analysis of Bioenergy from Woody Biomass with
Carbon Capture and Storage. Forthcoming.

5. ANIMAL MANURE ANAEROBIC DIGESTION PATHWAYS

This update provides the following:
  Updates to business-as-usual (BAU) manure management pathways, which include:
 o Animal populations and national shares of manure management practices for the
 beef, dairy, swine, and poultry (broilers, layers, and turkeys) sectors.
 o Updating methane conversion factors (MCFs) for anaerobic lagoons, liquid/slurry
 tanks, and deep pits of BAU manure management practices.

 5

  Adding an estimation methodology for methane emissions from digestate storage after
 anaerobic digestion.
State and county animal-population data were harmonized with the EPA GHG Inventory and
national averages are now weighted by volatile-solids (VS) production rather than headcount to
better reflect regional manure generation. A counterfactual method titled “Hybrid” leverages
updates to MCFs for lagoons, liquid/slurry tanks, and deep pits using the IPCC (2019) method
(including manure temperature and revised activation energy) at the county level and aggregated
to states and the nation; regionally specific cleanout schedules were incorporated where available.
For the first time, the model explicitly accounts for methane emissions from open digestate storage
by estimating the digestate’s residual biomethane potential and applying liquid/slurry MCFs; these
storage losses correspond to roughly 3–14% of the biomethane yield, depending on digester type
and sector.
For layers, broilers, turkeys, and dairy heifers, BAU updates had negligible effects, but adding
digestate storage increased life cycle GHG emissions for CNG in transit buses by about 35–45 kg
CO₂e/mmBtu, yielding default values of approximately 100–125 kg CO₂e/mmBtu in R&D
GREET 2025. Note that these results depend on reactor type (see Leonhardt et al., 2025).
For beef, BAU updates and digestate storage raised emissions by ~15 and ~35 kg CO₂e/mmBtu,
respectively, yielding a default value of approximately 80 kg CO₂e/mmBtu.
For dairy cows, BAU updates and digestate storage increased emissions by ~95 and ~35 kg
CO₂e/mmBtu, respectively, yielding a default value of approximately –25 kg CO₂e/mmBtu.
For swine, BAU updates reduced emissions by ~70 kg CO₂e/mmBtu, while digestate storage added
~30 kg CO₂e/mmBtu, yielding a default value of approximately –190 kg CO₂e/mmBtu.
By default, the “Hybrid” method, which augments U.S. EPA Greenhouse Gas Inventory data with
regionalized cleanout schedules and updates to MCF modeling parameters in-line with IPCC
(2019), is used to represent the counterfactual scenario, but users are free to modify this to other
legacy options (“U.S.” - based on the EPA Greenhouse Gas Inventory; “IPCC” - based on IPCC
(2006) methods; and “User Defined” which provides the user flexibility within the IPCC (2006)
methodology). Within the “Hybrid” approach for the counterfactual, a user input has been included
to allow the user to model farm-specific shares of conventional manure management methods (i.e.,
anaerobic lagoons, solid storage, etc.).
The report below details these updates and suggests areas for further improvement. Counterfactual
analysis for waste-derived RNG is advancing rapidly and will undergo regular updates and
refinements as more research is conducted.

Leonhardt, B.E., Ou, L., Cai, H., Wang, M. (2025). Updates to Anaerobic Digestion Pathways for
Animal Manure in R&D GREET 2025. Argonne National Laboratory.

6. FOREST RESIDUE NATURAL DECAY COUNTERFACTUAL

Forest residues are abundant and often underutilized sources of bioenergy feedstock. This update
accounts for the natural decay of forest residues across the contiguous United States (CONUS) to
quantify emissions under a counterfactual scenario in which residues are not utilized. Forest
residues are categorized by wood type (softwood and hardwood) and residue type (down dead
wood and forest floor), based on the state-level biomass carbon pools. The decay rates are
represented by adjusted decay constants (k) calibrated to local climate variables (i.e., temperature,
precipitation, and evapotranspiration) for each location. Decay-rate data are compiled for tree
species present within each state and weighed by basal area coverage.
The fates of biogenic carbon in forest residue are tracked across multiple pools and fluxes:
sequestration in humus/microbial biomass, sequestration in inert organic matter, remaining in
residues, and biogenic CO2 emissions to atmosphere. A net biogenic carbon balance is reported.
Two carbon-accounting approaches biogenic CO2 emissions are implemented: (1) a carbon-neutral
approach and (2) an explicit accounting approach coupled with a time-dependent discounted
Global Warming Potential (GWP). This framework enables evaluation of the fates of biogenic
carbon of forest residues due to natural decay for specific states and regions over an LCA time
horizon of 100 years.

Medel-Jiménez, F., Kwon, H., Lim, J.Y., Cai, H., Wang, M. (2025). Evaluation of Forest Residues
for Bioenergy Production: The Alternative Fate of Natural Decay and Net Biogenic Carbon
Implications Evaluating Decay of Forest Residues for Bioenergy. Forthcoming.

 ETHANOL PATHWAY

In the EtOH tab, cellulosic ethanol pathways via fermentation have been expanded to include
Deacetylation and Mechanical Refining (DMR), alongside the default Dilute Acid (DA)
pretreatment.
The DMR pretreatment option is available for corn stover, switchgrass, miscanthus, forest residue,
and forage sorghum. The LCI for DMR pretreatment and fermentation to ethanol is based on

2025; Mokwatlo et al., 2024). This includes detailed data on chemical inputs, water consumption,
electricity credits, and recovered sodium sulfate salt from wastewater treatment as a co-product.
A displacement credit is applied to account for this sodium sulfate salt co-product.

8. UPDATE METHANE PYROLYSIS PATHWAY FOR H 2 PRODUCTION

The methane pyrolysis pathways using natural gas (NG) and renewable natural gas (RNG) have
been updated for centralized hydrogen production. LCI data were compiled from Aspen Plus
process modeling of the methane pyrolysis process developed by Xu et al. (2025). The LCA
methodology follows Ng et al. (2025). Methane pyrolysis yields hydrogen and solid carbon
(carbon black), with a small amount of coke; excess heat is recovered to produce steam. Co-
product treatment options implemented include mass, energy, and market-value allocation, as well
as a carbon black displacement option. By default, mass allocation is applied, with steam
displacement credits included when steam is valorized. For the steam co-product, alternative
energy- or market-value-based allocation options are also provided. When the carbon black
displacement option is selected, steam is treated via displacement only.
Additionally, carbon black produced from the furnace black process using heavy aromatic oils has
been added to the Chemicals tab. Data for this update was obtained from Rosner et al. (2023),
which includes three scenarios. The baseline scenario assumes that all tailgas is flared, while the
other two scenarios (can be toggled) assume that a portion of the tailgas is combusted to supply
electricity and generate steam for export. We assume that the upstream emissions for heavy oil
production are equivalent to residual oil production in R&D GREET.

Xu, Y., Sun, P., Elgowainy, A. (2025). Modeling and Techno-Economic Analysis of Methane
Pyrolysis via Arc Plasma For Hydrogen and Carbon Black Production.

9. UPDATE OF H2 FROM CHLORINE PLANTS

Two hydrogen pathways have been updated to more accurately reflect the upstream emissions of
brine for the use of chlorine production, of which hydrogen is a byproduct. The associated
pathways include “Byproduction of Gaseous H2 from Chlorine plants” and “Byproduction of
Liquid H2 from Chlorine plants” which both utilize sodium brine produced in the U.S. Previous
assumptions equated the upstream emissions of this brine usage with brine production in Chile

 8

associated with Lithium extraction (Kelly et al., 2021). Current conversations with industry
personnel suggest that brine is acquired by pumping water through salt caverns on the way to
chlorine facilities. This energy is already accounted for in the transportation of resources to the
plant. Therefore, the upstream emissions of brine production are now assumed to be negligible.
Some share of brine is imported but more research is needed to understand the impacts and share
of this pathway.

10. GEOLOGIC HYDROGEN PRODUCTION

Geologic hydrogen from natural reservoirs is an emerging hydrogen supply. This expansion
incorporates an LCA pathway to evaluate cradle-to-gate emissions (including both process and
embodied impacts) of geologic hydrogen production under six possible scenarios that vary in gas
composition, reservoir pressure, and surface processing configurations.
The pathway integrates detailed process-level emissions and resource flows based on data
collection from academic and industry stakeholders, as well as field-scale engineering simulations
conducted using the Oil Production Greenhouse Gas Emissions Estimator (OPGEE). Scenario-
specific parameters include direct material and energy use (e.g., diesel consumption during drilling
and exploration; combustion of waste gas, raw gas, and hydrogen for electricity generation
powering downhole pumps, gas compression, and processing systems), hydrogen flow rates and
throughputs, and fugitive emissions of CO2 and CH4. Additionally, the model accounts for
amortized embodied emissions associated with infrastructure-related material demands, including
steel, cement, and chemical inputs used in drilling and well completion.

Yu, G. et al. Cradle-to-gate Life Cycle Analysis of Energy and Environmental Impacts of Natural
Geologic Hydrogen Production, 2025. Forthcoming.

11. COAL MINE METHANE COUNTERFACTUAL SCENARIO

Coal mine methane (CMM) is emitted from five primary sources, i.e., degasification systems at
active underground mines, ventilation systems from active underground mines, abandoned or
closed mines, active surface mines, and fugitive emissions from post-mining operations as coal is
stored in piles and transported. For CMM from degasification systems that is rich in methane, it is
commonly liberated to the atmosphere (i.e. venting) or flared by mine operators. These are
considered counterfactual scenarios when such CMM is captured, upgraded, and injected to
pipeline for beneficial end uses.
The latest management practices of CMM degasification borehole CMM are reported in EPA’s
2023 GHGRP Subpart FF data. It shows that active underground coal mines in the United States
liberated a total of about 554,000 metric tons of methane in the drainage CMM in 2023. The mine

12. CRUDE OIL MIX UPDATES

The projection of regional crude oil mixes has been updated through 2050. Based on EIA’s AEO
2025 (EIA 2025a), domestic crude oil production shares were estimated for 2024 and projected
from 2025 to 2050. R&D GREET also includes imports from other world regions–Canada
(separated into oil sands and conventional crude), Mexico, Middle East, Latin America, Africa,
and Other. Because no projections are available for these regions, their future shares were
estimated using the 2024 regional distribution from EIA’s company-level crude import data (EIA
2025b). For shale oil, Eagle Ford and Bakken contributed 7.6% and 9.2% of total U.S. crude oil
production in 2024, respectively (EIA 2025c; 2025d), and these shares were assumed to remain
unchanged through 2050. Canadian oil sand projections were updated using data from the Canada
Energy Regulator (2023); because data on synthetic crude oil (SCO) shares from surface mining
and in-situ operations are unavailable, the same shares reported in Cai et al. (2015) were assumed.
Weighted average crude oil transportation distances were revised using company-level import data
(EIA 2025b), with imports from Canada and Mexico transported about 1,735 miles by pipeline
and imports from other countries transported an average of 7,175 miles by ocean tanker.

13. ELECTRICITY MIX, GHG, NOX, AND SO2 EMISSION UPDATES FOR
 ELECTRICITY GENERATION

Electricity generation mixes and onsite emission intensities of GHG, NO x, and SO2 for 2024 were
estimated at multiple U.S. regional levels, including national, North American Electric Reliability
Corporation (NERC), state, EPA’s Emissions & Generation Resource Integrated Database
(eGRID), and DOE National Transmission Needs Study regions. Electricity generation mixes were
developed using detailed net generation data from the U.S. EIA’s Form 923 (U.S. EIA, 2025e),
which reports plant-level electricity generation by EIA fuel type. To derive regional generation
mixes, plant-level generation data were aggregated to various regional levels based on plant
coordinates and the corresponding geographic boundaries for states, NERC, EPA eGRID, and
DOE Needs Study regions. The aggregated generation data by EIA fuel type were then
consolidated into the ten power source categories used in the R&D GREET model (Lu et al., 2024).
Regional electricity generation mixes were quantified as the fractional contribution of each power
source to total regional net generation.
Direct GHG emissions from individual power plants were estimated following the methodology
described in Lu et al. (2024). Direct NOx and SO2 emissions were obtained from EIA’s Emissions
by Plant dataset (EIA, 2024). Because plant-level NOx and SO2 emissions for 2024 were not yet
available, we estimated these emissions by assuming that 2024 emission factors were consistent
with 2023 values at the plant level. The resulting plant-level emissions were aggregated by region
and normalized by total regional electricity generation to derive onsite GHG, NO x, and SO2
emission intensities for power generation across all regions in 2024.
Projections of electricity generation mixes and onsite emission intensities of GHG, NO x, and SO2
through 2050 were developed at the national and multiple regional levels using scenario results
from EIA AEO 2025 and selected NREL 2024 Standard Scenarios (Gagnon et al., 2024). In
addition to the AEO 2025 Reference Case, ten alternative AEO scenarios were incorporated,
including Alternative Electricity, Alternative Transportation, High/Low Economic Growth,
High/Low Oil Price, High/Low Oil and Gas Supply, and High/Low Zero-Carbon Technology Cost
(U.S. EIA, 2025a).
Both the AEO and NREL datasets provide projections of electricity generation by source type and

16.4. Graphite
The LCI of battery-grade synthetic graphite (SG) was updated in the R&D GREET 2025. New
processes have been incorporated in the model, including industry-level production of calcinated
petroleum coke, graphite block, graphite crucibles, and SG. The LCI updates are based on a mix
of data sources, including literature publications and communication with industrial experts.
Two pathways of SG were built to reflect advancements in the SG industry over the past decade,
using datasets from Wang et al. (2025) and Pandey et al. (2025). To support these pathways,
calcined petroleum coke (CPC) production was added based on Edwards et al. (2020). Using a
market allocation approach, we developed the life-cycle inventory for CPC produced from
petroleum coke and lime.
In the Wang et al. (2025) pathway, SG is produced using uncalcined petroleum coke, CPC,
uncalcined oil‑based needle coke, graphite crucibles, and ethylene tar pitch. The process comprises
five stages: pre-treatment, coating & granulation, graphitization, carbonization, and post‑treatment.
In the Pandey et al. (2025) pathway, SG is produced using CPC, graphite crucibles, metallurgical
coke, and coal tar pitch, following six stages: milling, graphitization, micronizing/spheroidizing,
coating (kneading), coating (thermal treatment), and packaging. Users may specify the relative
shares between these pathways as well as the electricity mix used in SG production to enable
scenario analysis. Feedstock options are also configurable for both pathways, such as crucible uses.
Corresponding graphite crucible production pathways were also implemented. In the pathway
aligned with Wang et al. (2025), graphite crucibles are produced primarily from graphitized coke,
a byproduct of the graphitization process that uses calcined petroleum coke as a principal input.
Material inputs and the process energy mix are based on personal communication with an industry
expert (Carrere, 2024), with energy inputs supplemented by Wang et al. (2025). In the pathway
aligned with Pandey et al. (2025), graphite crucibles are produced from graphite block; graphite
block production relies on calcined petroleum coke, coal tar pitch, and metallurgical coke, all of
which are parameterized in R&D GREET 2025. More details are provided in the publications

18.1 Platinum Over Strontium Titanate (Pt/STO) Catalyst Implementation
A new Platinum over strontium titanate (Pt/STO) catalyst production pathway has been added to
the “Catalyst” tab in R&D GREET 2025 based on analyses by Ferdous et al. (2025). This catalyst
is utilized for the hydrogenolysis of post-consumer plastics to obtain lubricating oils from upcycled
plastics (LOUPs), which can be used as substitutes for conventional base oils.

 20

The catalyst support consists of STO nanocuboids, synthesized through the reaction of strontium
hydroxide octahydrate dissolved in acetic acid with titanium tetrachloride dissolved in ethanol
under alkaline conditions. The solid product formed in this reaction is separated via centrifugation
and undergoes subsequent treatments with vacuum, ozone, water, and nitrogen. Platinum is then
deposited onto the support through solution-phase deposition using dodecane as the solvent.
Following deposition, the catalyst is washed with toluene and pentane, dried, and subjected to
nitrogen and hydrogen reduction.
Additionally, production pathways for key chemicals such as strontium hydroxide octahydrate,
titanium tetrachloride, and dodecane have also been incorporated into R&D GREET 2025. These
pathways are based on data reported by Tsang et al. (2016), Caramazana-Gonzalez et al. (2017),
and ESIG (2021), respectively.

18.2 Bio-Ethylene from Corn Ethanol (cofeeding pathway)
A new pathway for the production of bioethylene from corn ethanol has been added to R&D
GREET 2025 based on the study by Benavides et al. (2025). This pathway involves fluid catalytic
cracking (FCC) of a co-fed mixture of corn ethanol and vacuum gas oil (VGO). However,
accelerated mass spectrometry C-14 quantification determined that ethylene is exclusively derived
from corn ethanol and not from VGO.
The FCC process produces water, coke, and light-end gases, which include a variety of products
such as ethylene and ethane. Catalyst coke generated during the process is utilized as an internal
energy source, while ethane from the light-end gases is separated and subjected to steam cracking
to increase the ethylene yield. The pathway implemented in R&D GREET 2025 considers only
the C-14 testing yields case, where 0.46 kg of ethylene is produced per kg of corn ethanol.
In addition to ethylene, the steam cracking process generates other gaseous products, including
hydrogen, propylene, butane, isobutane, butylene, butadiene, acid gas, pyrolysis gas, and fuel gas.
Fuel gas is also used as an internal energy source for the process. Due to the presence of these co-
products, a mass allocation factor of 0.78 was applied to the light-end gases used as feedstock and
the flue gas (both internally produced and externally sourced) from the ethane separation and steam
cracking process to allocate the environmental impacts of the process to ethylene. The inventory
data implemented in R&D GREET 2025 already reflects the application of this allocation factor.
The pathway implemented in R&D GREET 2025 considers only the baseline scenario for corn
ethanol production. Alternative scenarios for corn ethanol production, as discussed by Benavides

 21

et al. (2025), can be evaluated by modifying the parameters in the Ethanol tab within R&D GREET
2025.

Benavides, P.T., Gracida-Alvarez, U.R., Richa, K. Port, J. and Hawkins, T.R. (2025). Cradle-to-
Gate greenhouse gas emissions of the production of ethylene from U.S. Corn ethanol and
comparison to fossil-derived ethylene production. Bioresource Technology 430: 132565.

20. BUILDING MODULE EXPANSION: HIGH PERFORMANCE THERMAL WATER
 HEATERS

A cradle-to-grave life-cycle analysis of high-performance thermal water heaters (HPTWHs) was
conducted in a multifamily residential setting, with emissions at each life-cycle stage accounted
for using the R&D GREET Building module. Embodied GHG emissions, operational GHG
emissions, and end-of-life GHG emissions were included in the assessment. Air pollutants, water
consumption, and total energy use were also evaluated, providing a comprehensive assessment of
environmental and energy impacts. The systems analyzed comprised natural gas water heaters,
electric resistance water heaters, air-source HPTWHs, distributed HPTWHs, and drain-source
HPTWHs. Environmental impacts and energy use associated with electricity and natural gas
consumption were quantified over a 28-year study period, with annual operational refrigerant
leakage and end-of-life emissions from HPTWHs accounted for. This is implemented in the
Building Module of R&D GREET 2025.

Thermal Water Heating Technologies for Multi-family Buildings. Forthcoming.
