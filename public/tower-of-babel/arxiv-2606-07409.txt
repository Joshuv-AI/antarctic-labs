Rate Loss in Quantum Channels with Classical State and Applications for Quantum Broadcast Channels
Igor BERNARD and Arun PADAKANDLA

Abstract
We consider the problem of rate loss - a strict penalty suffered in achievable rates due to the lack of channel state information at the receiver (Rx) of a classical-quantum (CQ) channel. First, we identify non-commutative CQ channels and analytically prove a rate loss. Building on this, we next prove that coset-code-based strategies can strictly outperform conventional unstructured IID-code-based strategies for non-commutative 3-user CQ broadcast channels.

Introduction

The presence of auxiliary random variables (RVs) is ubiquitous in the characterization of rate regions in classical and quantum network information theory
[
6

15
,
16

The auxiliary RVs, in addition to facilitating these parameters, provide an operational meaning that guides intuition in the design of coding strategies and outer bound techniques. However, the flip side is that these auxiliary RVs either leave the rate-region characterization without a compact description or render the resulting optimization problem intractable or infinite-dimensional. All of this makes it extremely hard to compare different inner bounds and definitively contrast the performance of different coding strategies.

Rate Loss in CQ Channel with Classical State

We begin with a formal description of a QSTx. Consider a (generic)
QSTx
specified through (i) a finite input set
\mathcal{X}

\mathcal{S}

\begin{gathered}\bar{\rho}^{(\delta)}_{0,0}=\frac{I+(1-2\delta)Z}{2},\qquad\bar{\rho}^{(\delta)}_{1,0}=\frac{I-(1-2\delta)Z}{2},\\

. This is only relevant when
a=1
, because
J_{1}
is not differentiable at
x=1
; the only point where this boundary value can occur is
q=0
, which will be evaluated separately by continuity.

We split into two cases.

Case 1:
0\leq q\leq\frac{1}{2}-\tau=\frac{c}{2}
.

If
c=0
, this case contains only the endpoint
q=0
, so there is no derivative statement to prove in this case. Assume therefore, for the rest of Case 1, that
c>0
. In this regime,

d_{*}(q,\tau)=d(q)\mathrel{:=}\sqrt{q^{2}+(c-q)^{2}},\qquad r(q)=\sqrt{q^{2}+(1-q)^{2}}.

For
q\in(0,\frac{c}{2})
, the arguments
d(q)
and
r(q)
lie in
(0,1)
whenever
a=1
, and hence
J_{a}
is differentiable at the relevant points. We compute

J_{a}^{\prime}(x)=\frac{a}{2}\,h_{2}^{\prime}\!\left(\frac{1+ax}{2}\right)=-\frac{a}{\ln 2}\,\operatorname{artanh}(ax).

Therefore,

H^{\prime}(q)=J_{a}^{\prime}(d(q))\,d^{\prime}(q)-J_{a}^{\prime}(r(q))\,r^{\prime}(q).

Now

d^{\prime}(q)=\frac{2q-c}{d(q)},\qquad r^{\prime}(q)=\frac{2q-1}{r(q)},

so

H^{\prime}(q)=\frac{a}{\ln 2}\left[\frac{c-2q}{d(q)}\,\operatorname{artanh}\!\bigl(a\,d(q)\bigr)-\frac{1-2q}{r(q)}\,\operatorname{artanh}\!\bigl(a\,r(q)\bigr)\right].

(22)

We now use the auxiliary function

\psi_{a}(x)\mathrel{:=}\frac{\operatorname{artanh}(ax)}{x}.

On the range of
d(q)
and
r(q)
considered here, the function

x\mapsto\psi_{a}(x)
is strictly increasing. Indeed, this follows from the
strict monotonicity of
y\mapsto\operatorname{artanh}(y)/y
on
(0,1)
; when
a=1
,
the endpoint
x=1
is avoided because
q>0
.
Because
d(q)\leq r(q)
, we obtain

\frac{\operatorname{artanh}(a\,d(q))}{d(q)}\leq\frac{\operatorname{artanh}(a\,r(q))}{r(q)}.

Also,

c-2q\leq 1-2q,

with strict inequality whenever
\tau>0
. Combining these two facts in
(
22
), we get

H^{\prime}(q)\leq 0\qquad\text{for all }q\in\left(0,\frac{c}{2}\right).

Moreover, since
\tau>0
, we have
c<1
, hence

c-2q<1-2q

for every
q\in\left(0,\frac{c}{2}\right)
. Therefore

H^{\prime}(q)<0\qquad\text{for every }q\in\left(0,\frac{c}{2}\right).

(23)

Case 2:
\frac{1}{2}-\tau=\frac{c}{2}\leq q\leq\frac{1}{2}
.

In this regime,

d_{*}(q,\tau)=\frac{c}{\sqrt{2}}

is constant, whereas
r(q)=\sqrt{q^{2}+(1-q)^{2}}
. Hence

H^{\prime}(q)=-J_{a}^{\prime}(r(q))\,r^{\prime}(q).

Using

J_{a}^{\prime}(r)=-\frac{a}{\ln 2}\,\operatorname{artanh}(ar)<0,\qquad r^{\prime}(q)=\frac{2q-1}{r(q)}<0\quad\text{for }q\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right),

we conclude that

H^{\prime}(q)<0\qquad\text{for every }q\in\left(\frac{c}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right).

(24)

Combining (
23
) and (
24
),
we conclude that
H
is strictly decreasing on
[0,\frac{1}{2}]
. By the
symmetry (
21
), the function

q\mapsto C_{\mathrm{nc}}^{\mathrm{sl}}(q,\tau,\delta)
is therefore
maximized exactly at
q=0
and
q=1
, and is strictly smaller for every

q\in(0,1)
.

It remains to evaluate the endpoint value. At
q=0
, we have

r(0)=1,\qquad d_{*}(0,\tau)=c=1-2\tau,

hence

C_{\mathrm{nc}}^{\mathrm{sl}}(0,\tau,\delta)=J_{a}(c)-J_{a}(1).

By (
20
), this equals
C_{\mathrm{ED}}(\tau,\delta)
. By
symmetry, the same holds at
q=1
. This proves the proposition.
∎

III
Efficiently Decoding Bivariate Interference via Coset Codes To Avoid Rate Loss over
3-
CQBCs

As discussed in Sec.
I
, the presence of a
rate loss
motivates the design of a coding strategy for a broadcast channel (BC) that enables Rxs to decode as large a component of the interference as possible and minimizes the residual component that is precoded for by the Tx. Ex.
2
illustrates the relevance of this principle for the design of coding strategies over
3
-CQBCs. We use the standard notions of a
3
-CQBC and achievable rate triples, and focus here on the rate comparison needed for the separation between structured and unstructured coding strategies.

Ex 2
.

Let
\underline{\mathcal{X}}=\mathcal{X}_{1}\times\mathcal{X}_{2}\times\mathcal{X}_{3}
denote the input set with
\mathcal{X}_{j}=\{0,1\}
for
j\in[3]
. For
(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\underline{\mathcal{X}}
, let

\displaystyle\rho^{B_{1}Y_{2}Y_{3}}_{x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}}

\displaystyle=\bar{\rho}^{(\delta_{1})}_{x_{1},\,x_{2}\oplus x_{3}}\otimes\sigma^{Y_{2}}_{x_{2}}(\delta_{2})\otimes\sigma^{Y_{3}}_{x_{3}}(\delta_{3}),

(25)

\displaystyle\sigma^{Y_{k}}_{x_{k}}(\delta_{k})

\displaystyle\triangleq(1-\delta_{k})|x_{k}\rangle\langle x_{k}|+\delta_{k}|1-x_{k}\rangle\langle 1-x_{k}|,

(26)

for
k\in\{2,3\}
and
\bar{\rho}^{(\delta_{1})}_{x_{1},\,x_{2}\oplus x_{3}}
is defined based on (
5
). Input bit
X_{1}
is Hamming cost constrained to
\tau
, i.e.
\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]\leq\tau
. What is the maximum rate of communication for Rx
1
, if Rxs
2
and
3
are fed at their respective capacities
1-h_{b}(\delta_{k})
for
k=2,3
.

We begin our study of Ex.
2
with a discussion and argue the sub-optimality of conventional IID code based strategies due to their inability to decode bivariate functions efficiently. We follow this by providing elements of a formal proof.

For simplicity, let’s assume
\epsilon\triangleq\delta_{2}=\delta_{3}
. Firstly, note that the Tx can input three bits
X_{1},X_{2},X_{3}
with bit
X_{1}
Hamming weight constrained to
\tau
. The three received components are unentangled. Moreover, received states at Rxs
2
and
3
are affected by only inputs bits
X_{2}
and
X_{3}
respectively. In fact, these are simple binary symmetric channels with crossover probabilities
\delta_{2},\delta_{3}
respectively. The imposed rate requirements for Rxs
2
and
3
implies that input bits
X_{2},X_{3}
must be uniform and independent. Let’s henceforth focus on the Tx to Rx
1
CQ channel.
X_{2}\oplus X_{3}\sim
Ber
(\frac{1}{2})
now plays the role of state
S
in Ex.
1
. Moreover, note that
X_{2},X_{3}
being dedicated to communicate to Rxs
2
and
3
, Rx
1
can be pumped information only through
X_{1}
which is cost constrained.
The
key point
to note is that while the Hamming cost constraint on
X_{1}

constrains the possible inputs on bit
X_{1}
, it does
not affect Rx
1
’s capability to decode
, with the latter governed only by the underlying Tx to Rx
1
CQ channel.

From our study of Ex.
1
, it is evident that if Rx
1
has
full
knowledge of
X_{2}\oplus X_{3}
, then it can achieve a rate
C_{\mathrm{ED}}(\tau,\delta_{1})
, otherwise it has to be content with a lower rate. See Fig.
4
. A key difference from Ex.
1
, is that though
X_{2},X_{3}
are independent Ber
(\frac{1}{2})
, normalized entropy of
X_{k}^{n}
is
1-h_{2}(\delta_{k})
for
k=2,3
. What about normalized entropy of
X_{2}^{n}\oplus X_{3}^{n}
?

The answer crucially relies on the joint design of
X_{2}-,X_{3}-
codes. So long as two codes are unstructured IID and independent of each other, the number of pairwise additions explode resulting in normalized entropy of
X_{2}^{n}\oplus X_{3}^{n}
being
\min\{1,2-2h_{2}(\epsilon)\}
. We conclude that
(Note 1)
if unstructured IID coding strategy must achieve a rate
C_{\mathrm{ED}}(\tau,\delta_{1})
for Rx
1
, then Rx
1
must be able to decode information at a total rate of
\min\{1,2-2h_{2}(\epsilon)\}+C_{\mathrm{ED}}(\tau,\delta_{1})
.

On the other hand, suppose that the
X_{2}
- and
X_{3}
-codebooks are
cosets of a common linear code, assuming for the moment that

\delta_{2}=\delta_{3}=\epsilon
. Since the sum of two cosets of a common
linear code is again a coset of the same linear code, the collection of
possible sums
X_{2}^{n}\oplus X_{3}^{n}
is constrained to a coset of rate

1-h_{2}(\epsilon)
. Thus receiver
1
does not need to decode the two
interfering codewords separately; it only needs to decode their sum, whose
effective rate is
1-h_{2}(\epsilon)
.

Let

C_{1}:=C_{\mathrm{ED}}(\tau,\delta_{1})=h_{2}(\tau*\delta_{1})-h_{2}(\delta_{1}),

and let

C_{\mathrm{joint}}(\tau,\delta_{1}):=I(X_{1}S;B_{1})=h_{2}\!\left(\frac{1+\frac{(1-2\delta_{1})(1-2\tau)}{\sqrt{2}}}{2}\right)-h_{2}(\delta_{1}),

where
S\sim\mathrm{Bern}(1/2)
,
X_{1}\sim\mathrm{Bern}(\tau)
are
independent, and the channel to receiver
1
is

(x_{1},s)\mapsto\bar{\rho}^{(\delta_{1})}_{x_{1},s}
. If

C_{1}+1-h_{2}(\epsilon)<C_{\mathrm{joint}}(\tau,\delta_{1}),

then receiver
1
can jointly decode its own codeword and the structured
interference
X_{2}^{n}\oplus X_{3}^{n}
. After recovering this interference, it is
in the state-informed situation and can communicate at rate
C_{1}
.

In contrast, an unstructured IID strategy has no algebraic closure property
that compresses the bivariate interference into a codebook of rate

1-h_{2}(\epsilon)
. In the reduced unstructured comparison made below,
achieving the state-informed rate
C_{1}
forces receiver
1
to recover the
two interfering layers separately. Hence receiver
1
would have to support
a total rate
C_{1}+2(1-h_{2}(\epsilon))
, which is impossible whenever

C_{1}+2(1-h_{2}(\epsilon))>C_{\mathrm{joint}}(\tau,\delta_{1}).

Thus, whenever the two inequalities above hold simultaneously, the coset-code
strategy achieves the corner point

\bigl(C_{1},1-h_{2}(\epsilon),1-h_{2}(\epsilon)\bigr),

whereas the reduced unstructured IID subclass cannot.

Theorem 2
.

Assume

\delta_{2}<\delta_{3}<\frac{1}{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad C_{1}+C_{2}<C_{\mathrm{joint}}(\tau,\delta_{1}).

(27)

Then the rate triple

(C_{1},C_{2},C_{3})=\bigl(C_{\mathrm{ED}}(\tau,\delta_{1}),\,1-h_{2}(\delta_{2}),\,1-h_{2}(\delta_{3})\bigr)

(28)

belongs to the closure of the structured inner bound obtained by specializing Theorem 1 of
[
10
]
to the channel in (
25
). In particular, (
28
) is achievable by a structured coding scheme based on nested coset codes.

Proof.

We specialize Theorem 1 of Gouiaa–Padakandla with field size

\upsilon=2
. Let

U_{2}=U_{3}=\mathbb{F}_{2},

and for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
, let

U_{2},U_{3}\sim\mathrm{Bern}\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\right),\qquad V_{2},V_{3}\sim\mathrm{Bern}(\varepsilon),

(29)

all independent. Let also

V_{1}\sim\mathrm{Bern}(\tau),

(30)

independent of
(U_{2},U_{3},V_{2},V_{3})
. We choose the fusion map

f:U_{2}\times U_{3}\times V_{1}\times V_{2}\times V_{3}\to\{0,1\}^{3}
as

f(u_{2},u_{3},v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})=(v_{1},\;u_{2}\oplus v_{2},\;u_{3}\oplus v_{3}).

(31)

Equivalently,

X_{1}=V_{1},\qquad X_{2}=U_{2}\oplus V_{2},\qquad X_{3}=U_{3}\oplus V_{3}.

Thus the fusion map specifies the physical channel input, while the
structured interference decoded by receiver
1
is
U_{2}\oplus U_{3}
.
Since
V_{1}\sim\mathrm{Bern}(\tau)
, the cost constraint is met with equality.

Let

U:=U_{2}\oplus U_{3},\qquad Z:=V_{2}\oplus V_{3}.

Then the actual interference in the first branch is

X_{2}\oplus X_{3}=U\oplus Z,

where

\Pr[Z=1]=2\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\downarrow 0]{}0.

Hence, as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
, the channel seen by receiver
1

converges to the cq channel

(v_{1},u)\longmapsto\bar{\rho}^{(\delta_{1})}_{v_{1},u}.

Consequently, by continuity of Holevo information,

I(V_{1};B_{1}|U)\longrightarrow C_{1},\qquad I(V_{1}U;B_{1})\longrightarrow C_{\mathrm{joint}}(\tau,\delta_{1}).

On the other hand, receivers
2
and
3
see BSC branches

Y_{j}=U_{j}\oplus V_{j}\oplus N_{j},\qquad j=2,3,

with
V_{j}\sim\mathrm{Bern}(\varepsilon)
. Thus the perturbation
V_{j}

carries a vanishing rate as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
, while the structured
part
U_{j}
can be decoded at rates approaching

C_{j}=1-h_{2}(\delta_{j}).

Therefore, if

C_{1}+\max\{C_{2},C_{3}\}<C_{\mathrm{joint}}(\tau,\delta_{1}),

the strict inequalities in Theorem 1 of Gouiaa–Padakandla can be satisfied
with arbitrarily small slack. Letting the slack and then
\varepsilon
tend
to zero yields the corner point

(C_{1},C_{2},C_{3})

in the closure of the structured inner bound.
∎

The condition (
27
) is the natural noisy CQ analogue of the classical decoding threshold. In the present setting, however, the relevant quantity is not merely the interference-free capacity of the first branch, but the joint decoding quantity
C_{\mathrm{joint}}(\tau,\delta_{1})
for simultaneous recovery of the first user’s codeword and the bivariate interference pattern. The nested-coset construction in Theorem 1 of
[
10
]
, specialized to the channel (
25
), gives exactly this decoding condition and yields the corner point in (
28
).

A reduced unstructured subclass
: We now make the comparison with unstructured IID coding explicit by isolating a concrete reduced subclass of the mixed Gouiaa–Padakandla inner bound. Recall that their general scheme contains a common layer
W
, six unstructured pairwise layers
Q_{ji}
, six structured layers
U_{ji}
, and three private layers
V_{j}

[
10
]
. The reduced subclass considered here is obtained by the specialization

W\equiv\mathrm{const}
,
U_{ji}\equiv\mathrm{const}\ \text{for all }(j,i)
,
Q_{12}\equiv Q_{13}\equiv Q_{23}\equiv Q_{32}\equiv\mathrm{const},Q_{21}\equiv T_{2},Q_{31}\equiv T_{3}
.

This reduction is natural for (
25
): users
2
and
3
each see an interference-free point-to-point branch, whereas receiver
1
alone faces the bivariate interference
X_{2}\oplus X_{3}
; accordingly, within a purely IID comparison, the only pairwise unstructured layers that can naturally help manage this interference are
Q_{21}
and
Q_{31}
, while the remaining
Q
-layers, the common layer
W
, and the structured layers
U_{ji}
are suppressed in this reduced subclass. Thus the only nontrivial layers are
T_{2},T_{3},V_{1},V_{2},V_{3}
. We impose the factorization
p(t_{2},t_{3})\,p(v_{1}|t_{2},t_{3})\,p(v_{2}|t_{2})\,p(v_{3}|t_{3}),
and deterministic encoding functions
X_{1}=f_{1}(T_{2},T_{3},V_{1}),X_{2}=f_{2}(T_{2},V_{2}),X_{3}=f_{3}(T_{3},V_{3}).

Receiver
1
decodes
(T_{2},T_{3},V_{1})
, receiver
2
decodes
(T_{2},V_{2})
, and receiver
3
decodes
(T_{3},V_{3})
. Let
\mathscr{R}_{\mathrm{u,red}}(\tau)
denote the closure of the set of all rate triples achievable by this reduced coding architecture under the cost constraint. For the proof below, we only need elementary packing constraints that are necessary for every point in
\mathscr{R}_{\mathrm{u,red}}(\tau)
. Write
R_{1}=L_{1},R_{2}=\nu_{21}+L_{2},R_{3}=\nu_{31}+L_{3}
, where
\nu_{21}
and
\nu_{31}
denote the rates carried by
T_{2}
and
T_{3}
respectively, and
L_{j}
is the private rate carried by
V_{j}
. Then every achievable triple in
\mathscr{R}_{\mathrm{u,red}}(\tau)
must satisfy

\displaystyle L_{1}

\displaystyle\leq I(V_{1};B_{1}|T_{2},T_{3}),

\displaystyle\qquad L_{2}

\displaystyle\leq I(V_{2};Y_{2}|T_{2}),

(32)

\displaystyle L_{3}

\displaystyle\leq I(V_{3};Y_{3}|T_{3}),

\displaystyle\qquad\nu_{21}+L_{2}

\displaystyle\leq I(T_{2},V_{2};Y_{2}),

\displaystyle L_{1}+\nu_{21}+\nu_{31}

\displaystyle\leq I(V_{1},T_{2},T_{3};B_{1}),

\displaystyle\qquad\nu_{31}+L_{3}

\displaystyle\leq I(T_{3},V_{3};Y_{3}).

These are single-user and full-sum packing constraints associated with the decoders
(T_{2},T_{3},V_{1})
,
(T_{2},V_{2})
and
(T_{3},V_{3})
.

Proposition 3
.

Assume that
\delta_{1}<\frac{1}{2}
and

h_{2}(\tau*\delta_{1})+1>h_{2}(\delta_{2})+h_{2}(\delta_{3})
.
Then
(C_{1},C_{2},C_{3})\notin\mathscr{R}_{\mathrm{u,red}}(\tau)
.

Proof.

Suppose, for contradiction, that
(C_{1},C_{2},C_{3})\in\mathscr{R}_{\mathrm{u,red}}(\tau)
. Since
Y_{2}
depends only on
X_{2}
, we have the Markov chain
(T_{2},V_{2})-X_{2}-Y_{2}
, and therefore

R_{2}=\nu_{21}+L_{2}\leq I(T_{2},V_{2};Y_{2})\leq I(X_{2};Y_{2})\leq C_{2}.

(33)

Since
R_{2}=C_{2}
, all inequalities in (
33
) are equalities. Thus
X_{2}\sim\mathrm{Bern}(1/2)
and
X_{2}
is a deterministic function of
(T_{2},V_{2})
. Likewise,
X_{3}\sim\mathrm{Bern}(1/2)
and
X_{3}
is a deterministic function of
(T_{3},V_{3})
. Define
T\triangleq(T_{2},T_{3})
and
S\triangleq X_{2}\oplus X_{3}.

By the reduced factorization
p(t_{2},t_{3})p(v_{1}|t_{2},t_{3})p(v_{2}|t_{2})p(v_{3}|t_{3})
, we have
V_{2}\perp V_{3}\mid T
, whence
X_{2}\perp X_{3}\mid T
.

Next, conditioned on
T=t
, receiver
1
sees exactly the noisy BB84-like state-dependent channel
(x_{1},s)\mapsto\bar{\rho}^{(\delta_{1})}_{x_{1},s}

with state distribution
S\sim\mathrm{Bern}(q_{t})
, where
q_{t}\triangleq P(S=1|T=t)
, and conditional input cost
\tau_{t}\triangleq P(X_{1}=1|T=t)
. Since
X_{1}=f_{1}(T,V_{1})
and
S
is a function of
(T,V_{2},V_{3})
, we have
V_{1}\perp S\mid T
. Hence
I(V_{1};B_{1}|T=t)\leq C_{\mathrm{nc}}^{\mathrm{sl}}(q_{t},\tau_{t},\delta_{1}).

Using
L_{1}\leq I(V_{1};B_{1}|T_{2},T_{3}),
we obtain

R_{1}\leq\sum_{t}p(t)\,C_{\mathrm{nc}}^{\mathrm{sl}}(q_{t},\tau_{t},\delta_{1}).

(34)

On the other hand, revealing
S
to the decoder yields for all
t

I(V_{1};B_{1}|T=t)\leq h_{2}(\tau_{t}*\delta_{1})-h_{2}(\delta_{1}).

(35)

Let
F_{\delta_{1}}(\lambda)\triangleq h_{2}(\lambda*\delta_{1})-h_{2}(\delta_{1})
for
\lambda\in[0,1]
. Since
\delta_{1}<1/2
, the map
F_{\delta_{1}}
is strictly concave on
[0,1]
. Summing (
35
) and applying Jensen’s inequality, we obtain

R_{1}\leq\sum_{t}p(t)F_{\delta_{1}}(\tau_{t})\leq F_{\delta_{1}}\!\left(\sum_{t}p(t)\tau_{t}\right)\leq F_{\delta_{1}}(\tau)=C_{1}.

(36)

Because
R_{1}=C_{1}
, equality must hold throughout (
36
). Hence
\tau_{t}=\tau
for
p(t)
-a.e.
t
. Returning to (
34
) and invoking Proposition
2
, we conclude that equality can hold only if

q_{t}\in\{0,1\}
for
p(t)
-a.e.
t
.
Equivalently,
H(S|T)=0
. Combined with
X_{2}\perp X_{3}\mid T
, this forces
H(X_{2}|T)=H(X_{3}|T)=0
: indeed, if
X_{2}\oplus X_{3}
is deterministic given
T=t
while
X_{2}
and
X_{3}
are conditionally independent given
T=t
, then the two conditional distributions cannot both be non-degenerate. Moreover, under the reduced factorization,
X_{2}=f_{2}(T_{2},V_{2})
is conditionally independent of
T_{3}
given
T_{2}
, and
X_{3}=f_{3}(T_{3},V_{3})
is conditionally independent of
T_{2}
given
T_{3}
; hence
H(X_{2}|T_{2})=H(X_{3}|T_{3})=0
. Consequently
I(V_{2};Y_{2}|T_{2})=I(V_{3};Y_{3}|T_{3})=0
, so the second and fifth inequalities in (
32
) imply
L_{2}=L_{3}=0
. Therefore the entire rates
R_{2}
and
R_{3}
are carried by
T_{2}
and
T_{3}
, both decoded by receiver
1
. We may now use the receiver-
1
sum-rate inequality in (
32
):
R_{1}+R_{2}+R_{3}=L_{1}+\nu_{21}+\nu_{31}\leq I(V_{1},T_{2},T_{3};B_{1})
.

Since
B_{1}
depends on the channel input only through
(X_{1},S)
, data processing yields
R_{1}+R_{2}+R_{3}\leq I(X_{1},S;B_{1}).
Each of the four states
\bar{\rho}^{(\delta_{1})}_{x,s}
has entropy
h_{2}(\delta_{1})
, while the output system
B_{1}
is a qubit. Hence
I(X_{1},S;B_{1})\leq 1-h_{2}(\delta_{1}).
Combining the last two equations,
R_{1}+R_{2}+R_{3}\leq 1-h_{2}(\delta_{1}).
At the corner point
(C_{1},C_{2},C_{3})
, the left-hand side equals
h_{2}(\tau*\delta_{1})-h_{2}(\delta_{1})+1-h_{2}(\delta_{2})+1-h_{2}(\delta_{3}),
so that
h_{2}(\tau*\delta_{1})+1\leq h_{2}(\delta_{2})+h_{2}(\delta_{3}),
a contradiction. This proves the proposition.
∎

Lemma 1
.

For every
\delta\in[0,1/2]
, the function

g_{\delta}(t)=h_{2}\!\left(\frac{1+(1-2\delta)\sqrt{t^{2}+(1-t)^{2}}}{2}\right)-h_{2}(t),\qquad t\in[0,1],

is convex on
[0,1]
.

Proof.

Set

a\mathrel{:=}1-2\delta\in[0,1],\qquad r(t)\mathrel{:=}\sqrt{t^{2}+(1-t)^{2}},\qquad\lambda_{a}(t)\mathrel{:=}\frac{1+a\,r(t)}{2}.

Then

g_{\delta}(t)=h_{2}(\lambda_{a}(t))-h_{2}(t).

For
t\in(0,1)
,

r^{\prime}(t)=\frac{2t-1}{r(t)},\qquad r^{\prime\prime}(t)=\frac{1}{r(t)^{3}},

hence

\lambda_{a}^{\prime}(t)=\frac{a(2t-1)}{2r(t)},\qquad\lambda_{a}^{\prime\prime}(t)=\frac{a}{2r(t)^{3}}.

Recall that

h_{2}^{\prime}(x)=\log_{2}\frac{1-x}{x},\qquad h_{2}^{\prime\prime}(x)=-\frac{1}{x(1-x)\ln 2}.

Therefore,

g_{\delta}^{\prime\prime}(t)=h_{2}^{\prime\prime}(\lambda_{a}(t))(\lambda_{a}^{\prime}(t))^{2}+h_{2}^{\prime}(\lambda_{a}(t))\lambda_{a}^{\prime\prime}(t)-h_{2}^{\prime\prime}(t).

Using

\lambda_{a}(t)(1-\lambda_{a}(t))=\frac{1-a^{2}r(t)^{2}}{4},\qquad t(1-t)=\frac{1-r(t)^{2}}{2},\qquad(2t-1)^{2}=2r(t)^{2}-1,

a direct simplification yields

g_{\delta}^{\prime\prime}(t)=\frac{1}{\ln 2}\left[\frac{2}{1-r^{2}}-\frac{a^{2}(2r^{2}-1)}{r^{2}(1-a^{2}r^{2})}+\frac{a}{2r^{3}}\ln\frac{1-ar}{1+ar}\right],

where
r=r(t)
. Now fix
r\in[1/\sqrt{2},1)
, and set

x\mathrel{:=}ar\in[0,r].

Define

H_{r}(x)\mathrel{:=}-\frac{x^{2}(2r^{2}-1)}{1-x^{2}}+\frac{x}{2}\ln\frac{1-x}{1+x}.

Then

g_{\delta}^{\prime\prime}(t)=\frac{1}{\ln 2}\left[\frac{2}{1-r^{2}}+\frac{1}{r^{4}}H_{r}(x)\right].

A direct differentiation gives

H_{r}^{\prime}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{1-x}{1+x}-\frac{x}{1-x^{2}}-\frac{2x(2r^{2}-1)}{(1-x^{2})^{2}}.

Since
x\in(0,1)
and
r\in[1/\sqrt{2},1)
, each term on the right-hand side is nonpositive, hence

H_{r}^{\prime}(x)\leq 0.

Therefore
H_{r}
is decreasing, so for fixed
t
, the quantity
g_{\delta}^{\prime\prime}(t)
is decreasing in
a\in[0,1]
. Consequently,

g_{\delta}^{\prime\prime}(t)\geq g_{0}^{\prime\prime}(t),

where
g_{0}
corresponds to
a=1
, i.e.,
\delta=0
.
For
\delta=0
, one has

g_{0}^{\prime\prime}(t)=\frac{1}{2\ln 2\,r(t)^{3}}\left(\frac{2r(t)}{1-r(t)^{2}}-\ln\frac{1+r(t)}{1-r(t)}\right).

Define, for
0\leq s<1
,

\phi(s)\mathrel{:=}\frac{2s}{1-s^{2}}-\ln\frac{1+s}{1-s}.

Then
\phi(0)=0
and

\phi^{\prime}(s)=\frac{4s^{2}}{(1-s^{2})^{2}}\geq 0.

Hence
\phi(s)\geq 0
for all
s\in[0,1)
. Applying this with
s=r(t)
, we obtain

g_{0}^{\prime\prime}(t)\geq 0.

Therefore

g_{\delta}^{\prime\prime}(t)\geq 0\qquad\text{for all }t\in(0,1).

Since
g_{\delta}
is continuous on
[0,1]
, it is convex on
[0,1]
.
∎

Geometry of the Minimization

Lemma 2
.

For every
q\in[0,1]
and
\tau\in[0,1/2]
,

d_{*}(q,\tau)=\begin{cases}\sqrt{q^{2}+(1-q-2\tau)^{2}},&q\leq\dfrac{1}{2},\ \tau<\dfrac{1}{2}-q,\\[8.61108pt]
\dfrac{1-2\tau}{\sqrt{2}},&\left|q-\dfrac{1}{2}\right|\leq\tau\leq\dfrac{1}{2},\\[8.61108pt]
\sqrt{(q-2\tau)^{2}+(1-q)^{2}},&q\geq\dfrac{1}{2},\ \tau<q-\dfrac{1}{2}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Set

c\mathrel{:=}1-2\tau\in[0,1].

We must minimize
A^{2}+B^{2}
over

|A|\leq q,\qquad|B|\leq 1-q,\qquad A+B\geq c.

We first show that any minimizer must satisfy
A+B=c
. If
c=0
, then the feasible point
(0,0)
has squared norm
0
, and hence every minimizer satisfies
A=B=0
, so in particular
A+B=c
. We may therefore assume
c>0
. Let
(A,B)
be feasible with
A+B>c
, and define

\lambda\mathrel{:=}\frac{c}{A+B}\in(0,1).

Then
(\lambda A,\lambda B)
is still feasible, because

|\lambda A|\leq|A|\leq q,\qquad|\lambda B|\leq|B|\leq 1-q,\qquad\lambda A+\lambda B=c,

and its squared norm is strictly smaller:

(\lambda A)^{2}+(\lambda B)^{2}=\lambda^{2}(A^{2}+B^{2})<A^{2}+B^{2}.

Therefore no minimizer can satisfy
A+B>c
.
Hence we minimize

A^{2}+(c-A)^{2}

over the intersection of the line
A+B=c
with the rectangle

[-q,q]\times[-(1-q),1-q].

Without box constraints, the minimum is attained at

A=B=\frac{c}{2}=\frac{1-2\tau}{2}.

This point lies in the rectangle if and only if

\frac{1-2\tau}{2}\leq q\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{1-2\tau}{2}\leq 1-q,

that is,

\left|q-\frac{1}{2}\right|\leq\tau.

In this case,

d_{*}(q,\tau)=\frac{1-2\tau}{\sqrt{2}}.

Suppose now that
q\leq 1/2
and
\tau<1/2-q
. Then

\frac{1-2\tau}{2}>q,

so the unconstrained minimizer lies to the right of the admissible segment. Hence the constrained minimizer is obtained by saturating
A=q
. Since
A+B=c
, this gives

B=c-q=1-q-2\tau.

Therefore

d_{*}(q,\tau)=\sqrt{q^{2}+(1-q-2\tau)^{2}}.

Finally, suppose that
q\geq 1/2
and
\tau<q-1/2
. Then

\frac{1-2\tau}{2}>1-q,

so the unconstrained minimizer lies above the admissible segment. Hence the constrained minimizer is obtained by saturating
B=1-q
. Since
A+B=c
, this gives

A=c-(1-q)=q-2\tau.

Therefore

d_{*}(q,\tau)=\sqrt{(q-2\tau)^{2}+(1-q)^{2}}.

This proves the piecewise expression for
d_{*}(q,\tau)
.
∎
