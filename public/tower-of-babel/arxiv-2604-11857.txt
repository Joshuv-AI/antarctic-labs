Blind Catalytic Quantum Error Correction: Target-State Estimation and Fidelity Recovery Without A Priori Knowledge
Hikaru Wakaura

Abstract
Catalytic quantum error correction (CQEC) recovers quantum states via catalytic covariant transformations but requires full knowledge of the target state. We introduce blind CQEC , which estimates the target from the noisy output alone before catalytic recovery. Five estimation strategies are benchmarked across three noise models (dephasing, depolarizing, amplitude damping), four quantum algorithms ( d = 4 d=4 – 64 64 ), Haar-random states up to d = 256 d=256 , and mixed-state targets with variable purity. Key results: (i) coherence maximization achieves F rec > 0.95 F_{\mathrm{rec}}>0.95 for d ≤ 16 d\leq 16 without noise-model knowledge, matching the oracle to within 4 % 4\% ; (ii) channel inversion is required at d = 64 d=64 ( F rec = 0.905 F_{\mathrm{rec}}=0.905 ); (iii) estimation and recovery fidelities are linearly correlated ( r > 0.99 r>0.99 ), identifying target estimation as the sole bottleneck; (iv) an analytical crossover dimension d ∗ ≈ 25 d^{*}\approx 25 – 40 40 separates noise-model-free and noise-informed regimes, bridged by a hybrid interpolation strategy; (v) copy scaling follows 1 − F ⁡ ( n ) ∼ n − α 1-F(n)\sim n^{-\alpha} with α ∈ [ 0.4 , 2.2 ] \alpha\in[0.4,2.2] , spanning the statistical averaging and denoising synergy limits. Standard linear inversion tomography fails as a CQEC target estimator, validating the need for decoherence-aware strategies. An end-to-end VQE demonstration for H 2 shows 3.4 × 3.4\times energy-error reduction with channel-inversion blind CQEC.



I
Introduction

Catalytic quantum error correction (CQEC), introduced in Ref.
Wakaura 2026a
, exploits the arbitrary amplification of quantum coherence under catalytic covariant transformations
Shiraishi and Takagi 2024
to recover quantum states corrupted by decoherence.
Unlike conventional quantum error correction (QEC), which requires encoding overhead and fails above a noise threshold
Shor 1995
;
Gottesman 1997
;
Knill 2005
, CQEC is threshold-free: recovery succeeds whenever the coherent modes of the target state are present in the noisy state,
\mathcal{C}(\rho_{\mathrm{target}})\subseteq\mathcal{C}(\rho_{\mathrm{noisy}})
, regardless of how small the residual coherence is
Shiraishi and Takagi 2024
.
The catalyst is reusable across unlimited correction cycles
Wakaura 2026a
;
Wakaura 2026b
.

However, CQEC in its original formulation requires
complete knowledge of the target state

\rho_{\mathrm{target}}
.
This requirement is restrictive: in many practically relevant scenarios—most notably variational quantum algorithms where noisy intermediate circuit outputs must be improved without knowledge of the ideal state
Peruzzo et al. 2014
;
Cerezo et al. 2021
, and iterative quantum algorithms where the target evolves across rounds—the ideal output state is unknown to the error correction module.
The target-state requirement has been identified as the principal conceptual gap between CQEC and conventional QEC
Wakaura 2026a
.

In this work, we substantially relax this requirement by introducing
blind CQEC
,

II
Theoretical Background

II.1
Catalytic covariant transformations

We briefly recall the framework of Refs.
Shiraishi and Takagi 2024
;
Wakaura 2026a
.
A covariant operation satisfies
\Lambda\circ\mathcal{U}_{t}=\mathcal{U}_{t}\circ\Lambda
for all
t
, where
\mathcal{U}_{t}(\rho)=e^{-iHt}\rho\,e^{iHt}
.
The modes of asymmetry
\mathcal{D}(\rho)=\{\Delta_{ij}=E_{i}-E_{j}\mid\rho_{ij}\neq 0\}
characterize the coherence structure.
The resonant coherent modes
\mathcal{C}(\rho)=\langle\mathcal{D}(\rho)\rangle_{\mathbb{Z}}
form the integer lattice generated by
\mathcal{D}(\rho)
.

Theorem 1
(Shiraishi–Takagi
Shiraishi and Takagi 2024
).—
A catalytic covariant transformation
\rho\otimes c\mapsto\tau
with
\mathrm{Tr}_{C}[\tau]=\rho^{\prime}
,
\mathrm{Tr}_{S}[\tau]=c
exists if and only if
\mathcal{C}(\rho^{\prime})\subseteq\mathcal{C}(\rho)
and
\rho
is full rank.

The CQEC protocol
Wakaura 2026a
applies Theorem 1 as follows: given a noisy state
\rho_{\mathrm{noisy}}=\mathcal{E}(\rho_{\mathrm{target}})
and a catalyst
c
, find a covariant map
\Lambda
such that
\Lambda(\rho_{\mathrm{noisy}}\otimes c)=\tau
with
\mathrm{Tr}_{C}[\tau]\approx\rho_{\mathrm{target}}
.
This requires knowing
\rho_{\mathrm{target}}
explicitly to construct
\Lambda
.
Note that Theorem 1 requires the input state to be full rank.
In our benchmarks, this condition is satisfied: depolarizing noise (
\rho\mapsto(1-p)\rho+pI/d
) always produces full-rank states, and the combined noise model includes a depolarizing component.
For pure dephasing alone, the diagonal elements are unchanged and rank is preserved from the target; in practice, any finite-temperature environment ensures full rank through thermal population of all levels.

II.2
Implementation via density-matrix simulation

In our numerical benchmarks, the CQEC recovery is implemented via the ICEC protocol of Ref.
Wakaura 2026a
, which operates at the density-matrix level.
Given
\rho_{\mathrm{noisy}}
and a target estimate
\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}}
, the protocol:
(i) verifies the mode inclusion condition
\mathcal{C}(\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}})\subseteq\mathcal{C}(\rho_{\mathrm{noisy}})
;
(ii) constructs a catalyst state
c
with coherent modes
\mathcal{C}(c)\supseteq\mathcal{C}(\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}})
;
(iii) applies the catalytic covariant transformation on
\rho_{\mathrm{noisy}}\otimes c
to produce a state whose partial trace over the catalyst subspace approximates
\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}}
.
All operations are performed as
d\times d
matrix manipulations (or
(d\cdot d_{c})\times(d\cdot d_{c})
for the joint system) without circuit-level decomposition.
In our benchmarks, the catalyst dimension is
d_{c}=d
(matching the system dimension), following the construction of Ref.
Wakaura 2026a
.
The dominant computational cost of the recovery step is therefore
O(d^{3})
for eigendecomposition and
O(d^{6})
for the joint-system operations (since the joint Hilbert space has dimension
d\cdot d_{c}=d^{2}
).
At
d=64
, this corresponds to
4096\times 4096
matrices, which remain tractable for density-matrix simulation but would become prohibitive for
d\gg 10^{3}
without exploiting tensor product structure.
This approach is exact for the density-matrix-level physics but does not address gate compilation overhead or decoherence during the recovery itself, which we leave to future work (Sec.
VI.4
).

II.3
The target-knowledge bottleneck

In standard CQEC, the target state enters in two places:
(a) the mode inclusion check
\mathcal{C}(\rho_{\mathrm{target}})\subseteq\mathcal{C}(\rho_{\mathrm{noisy}})
;
(b) the construction of the catalytic map
\Lambda
, which steers amplification toward the off-diagonal structure of
\rho_{\mathrm{target}}
.
Without
\rho_{\mathrm{target}}
, neither step can be performed exactly.

In blind CQEC, we replace
\rho_{\mathrm{target}}
by an estimate
\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}}
and accept a fidelity penalty.
To quantify this, we use the bound
1-F(\rho,\sigma)\leq\|\rho-\sigma\|_{1}
(which follows from the Fuchs–van de Graaf inequality
Fuchs and van de
Graaf 1999
for the squared-fidelity convention of Eq. (
8
)) and the triangle inequality for the trace norm.
Let
F_{\mathrm{rec}}^{(\mathrm{oracle})}
denote the recovery fidelity when the true target
\rho_{\mathrm{target}}
is known.
For a fixed catalyst state and dimension, the ICEC protocol defines a family of CPTP maps
\{\Lambda_{\sigma}\}
parameterized by the target specification
\sigma
.
The recovery map
\Lambda_{\sigma}
is a valid quantum channel for each
\sigma
, and the Lipschitz continuity of the protocol in the target parameter—which we verify numerically (Sec.
V.3
)—implies

\|\Lambda_{\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}}}(\rho_{\mathrm{noisy}})-\Lambda_{\rho_{\mathrm{target}}}(\rho_{\mathrm{noisy}})\|_{1}\leq L\,\|\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}}-\rho_{\mathrm{target}}\|_{1}

for a protocol-dependent Lipschitz constant
L\leq 1
(observed empirically; see Eq. (
9
)).
Combining:

F_{\mathrm{rec}}\geq F_{\mathrm{rec}}^{(\mathrm{oracle})}-L\,\|\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}}-\rho_{\mathrm{target}}\|_{1},

(1)

where
L\leq 1
is the Lipschitz constant of the recovery map with respect to the target specification.
For single-copy strategies (coherence maximization, channel inversion),
\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}}
is deterministic and Eq. (
1
) applies directly with
\|\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}}-\rho_{\mathrm{target}}\|_{1}
being the systematic estimation error.
For multi-copy averaging with
n
copies,
\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}}
is itself a random variable whose trace-distance error decomposes as
\|\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}}-\rho_{\mathrm{target}}\|_{1}\leq\|\overline{\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}}}-\rho_{\mathrm{target}}\|_{1}+O(n^{-1/2})
, where
\overline{\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}}}=\mathbb{E}[\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}}]
is the systematic bias and the
O(n^{-1/2})
term captures finite-copy statistical fluctuations.
Substituting into Eq. (
1
):

F_{\mathrm{rec}}\geq F_{\mathrm{rec}}^{(\mathrm{oracle})}-L\bigl(\|\overline{\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}}}-\rho_{\mathrm{target}}\|_{1}+O(n^{-1/2})\bigr).

(2)

The two error sources are:
estimation bias

\|\overline{\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}}}-\rho_{\mathrm{target}}\|_{1}
(systematic, strategy-dependent) and
finite-copy variance
(statistical, relevant only for multi-copy strategies, vanishing as
n\to\infty
).
We emphasize that the bound (
1
) is semi-empirical: the Lipschitz continuity is verified numerically (
L\approx 0.98
, Sec.
V.3
) rather than proven analytically for the ICEC protocol in general.
Eq. (
1
) motivates our approach: minimizing the estimation error
\|\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}}-\rho_{\mathrm{target}}\|_{1}
directly maximizes recovery fidelity.

II.4
Analytical crossover dimension

We derive an approximate crossover dimension
d^{*}
at which coherence maximization and channel inversion achieve equal estimation error.
For a Haar-random pure state
|\psi\rangle
in dimension
d
, the expected populations scale as
\mathbb{E}[p_{i}]=1/d
with variance
\mathrm{Var}[p_{i}]=(d-1)/[d^{2}(d+1)]

Nielsen and Chuang 2010
.
Under depolarizing noise
\mathcal{E}_{p}
, the populations become
p_{i}^{\prime}=(1-p)p_{i}+p/d
, so the estimation error for coherence maximization, which uses the physicality bound
\sqrt{p_{i}^{\prime}p_{j}^{\prime}}
, is dominated by the bias
|\sqrt{p_{i}^{\prime}p_{j}^{\prime}}-|\rho_{ij}^{\mathrm{target}}||
.

For a Haar-random pure state,
\mathbb{E}[|\rho_{ij}|^{2}]=1/[d(d+1)]
for
i\neq j
, while
\sqrt{p_{i}^{\prime}p_{j}^{\prime}}\approx[(1-p)/d+p/d]=1/d
for large
d
.
The coherence-maximization error thus scales as
\epsilon_{\mathrm{CM}}\sim d\cdot|1/d-1/\sqrt{d(d+1)}|\sim 1/(2d)
per matrix element, giving a trace-norm error
\|\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}}^{\mathrm{CM}}-\rho_{\mathrm{target}}\|_{1}\sim O(1)
independent of
d
(since there are
d^{2}
elements, each contributing
\sim 1/d^{2}
to the trace norm after concentration).

For channel inversion under depolarizing noise,
\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}}^{\mathrm{inv}}=(\rho_{\mathrm{noisy}}-pI/d)/(1-p)
is exact when
\mathcal{E}
is purely depolarizing, so
\|\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}}^{\mathrm{inv}}-\rho_{\mathrm{target}}\|_{1}=0
.
Under combined noise, the residual error arises from the uncompensated components (e.g., dephasing not inverted when only depolarizing inversion is applied).
Writing the combined channel as
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{AD}}\circ\mathcal{E}_{p}\circ\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}
and inverting only approximately, the residual scales as
\epsilon_{\mathrm{inv}}\sim\gamma_{\mathrm{AD}}+\gamma/d
(the amplitude-damping component is
d
-independent, while dephasing inversion error grows with the mode gap).

The crossover occurs when
\epsilon_{\mathrm{CM}}\approx\epsilon_{\mathrm{inv}}
, giving

d^{*}\approx\frac{1}{2(\gamma_{\mathrm{AD}}+\gamma/d^{*})},

(3)

which for our combined-noise parameters (
\gamma=1.0
,
\gamma_{\mathrm{AD}}=0.1
) yields
d^{*}\approx 25
–
40
.
This is consistent with the numerically observed crossover at
d\approx 32
(Sec.
V.6
).

III.1
Naive passthrough

The simplest approach uses
\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}}=\rho_{\mathrm{noisy}}
directly.
Since
\rho_{\mathrm{noisy}}
is the corrupted version of
\rho_{\mathrm{target}}
, this is equivalent to running CQEC with the “wrong” target.
As we show in Sec.
V
, this yields
F_{\mathrm{rec}}=F_{\mathrm{noisy}}
(no improvement), because catalytic amplification faithfully steers the state toward
\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}}=\rho_{\mathrm{noisy}}
, which is itself the noisy state.

III.2
Coherence maximization

We propose maximizing the off-diagonal coherence of
\rho_{\mathrm{noisy}}
while preserving its diagonal (population) structure:

(\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}})_{ij}=\begin{cases}(\rho_{\mathrm{noisy}})_{ii}&\text{if }i=j,\\
\sqrt{(\rho_{\mathrm{noisy}})_{ii}\,(\rho_{\mathrm{noisy}})_{jj}}\,e^{i\phi_{ij}}&\text{if }i\neq j,\end{cases}

(4)

where
\phi_{ij}=\arg[(\rho_{\mathrm{noisy}})_{ij}]
when
(\rho_{\mathrm{noisy}})_{ij}\neq 0
, and
\phi_{ij}=0
otherwise (coherences that have been completely suppressed cannot be recovered by this strategy; see below).
This corresponds to constructing the
most coherent state consistent with the observed populations and phases
.

The rationale is physical: decoherence reduces
|\rho_{ij}|
below the physicality bound
\sqrt{p_{i}p_{j}}
but preserves the phase (for dephasing and depolarizing channels).
By restoring
|\rho_{ij}|
to its maximum allowed value, we undo the amplitude damping of coherences without requiring knowledge of the original amplitudes.

The matrix
\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}}
constructed by Eq. (
4
) is Hermitian and trace-one by construction.
It is positive semidefinite when the phases
\{\phi_{ij}\}
are consistent with a rank-one state (i.e.,
\phi_{ij}=\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}
for some real
\{\theta_{i}\}
), which holds exactly for pure-state targets under phase-preserving noise.
For general phase structures, the resulting matrix may have small negative eigenvalues; in such cases, we project onto the PSD cone via eigenvalue clipping (setting negative eigenvalues to zero and renormalizing).
In our benchmarks, this projection is needed only for the
d=64
case, where the correction is
<10^{-3}
in trace norm.

For pure-state targets,
\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}}
from Eq. (
4
) exactly recovers
\rho_{\mathrm{target}}
when the noise is purely dephasing
and
no off-diagonal element is driven exactly to zero (i.e.,
(\rho_{\mathrm{noisy}})_{ij}\neq 0
whenever
(\rho_{\mathrm{target}})_{ij}\neq 0
), since the populations are unchanged and the phases are preserved.
In practice, finite dephasing rates produce exponentially suppressed but nonzero coherences, so this condition is satisfied for any finite
\gamma
; however, numerical underflow may violate it for very large
\gamma
or
|\Delta_{ij}|
.

An important subtlety is the mode inclusion condition: Theorem 1 requires
\mathcal{C}(\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}})\subseteq\mathcal{C}(\rho_{\mathrm{noisy}})
.
Since coherence maximization sets
(\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}})_{ij}=\sqrt{p_{i}p_{j}}\,e^{i\phi_{ij}}
, the estimate
\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}}
has nonzero coherences wherever
p_{i},p_{j}>0
.
For full-rank noisy states (guaranteed by depolarizing or combined noise),
\mathcal{C}(\rho_{\mathrm{noisy}})
includes all modes, so mode inclusion is automatically satisfied.
For pure dephasing with
(\rho_{\mathrm{noisy}})_{ij}=0
for some
(i,j)
, our convention
\phi_{ij}=0
ensures
(\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}})_{ij}=\sqrt{p_{i}p_{j}}\neq 0
, which may violate mode inclusion.
In such cases, the ICEC protocol handles the violation by restricting amplification to the shared modes
\mathcal{C}(\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}})\cap\mathcal{C}(\rho_{\mathrm{noisy}})
, resulting in partial recovery.

We note that Eq. (
4
) can be viewed as a special case of constrained quantum state estimation
Hradil 1997
, where the constraint set is the intersection of the PSD cone with the observed diagonal.
Unlike general tomographic methods
Gross et al. 2010
;
Haah et al. 2017
, which require informationally complete measurements, our estimator uses only the noisy density matrix—i.e., a single “measurement outcome” of the noise channel—and exploits the specific structure of decoherence to reconstruct the off-diagonal elements.
This makes it fundamentally different from (and complementary to) standard QST approaches.

III.3
Noise-channel inversion

When the noise model
\mathcal{E}
is known but the target is not, we invert the channel analytically:

Dephasing:

\displaystyle(\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}})_{ij}=(\rho_{\mathrm{noisy}})_{ij}\,e^{\gamma|\Delta_{ij}|},

(5)

Depolarizing:

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}}=\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{noisy}}-\frac{p}{d}I}{1-p},

(6)

Amp. damping:

\displaystyle(\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}})_{11}=\frac{(\rho_{\mathrm{noisy}})_{11}}{1-\gamma},\ (\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}})_{01}=\frac{(\rho_{\mathrm{noisy}})_{01}}{\sqrt{1-\gamma}},

(7)

followed by projection onto the positive semidefinite cone (eigenvalue clipping with renormalization, applied to all three inversion formulas whenever the result is not PSD).
Eq. (
7
) is written for a qubit; for
d>2
, we generalize amplitude damping as cascaded decay
|k\rangle\to|k-1\rangle
with rate
\gamma
per level, i.e.,
(\rho_{\mathrm{noisy}})_{jk}=(1-\gamma)^{(j+k)/2}(\rho_{\mathrm{target}})_{jk}
for
j,k>0
, with population transfer to the ground state.
The inversion formula generalizes accordingly:
(\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}})_{jk}=(\rho_{\mathrm{noisy}})_{jk}/(1-\gamma)^{(j+k)/2}
.
Channel inversion is exact when the channel is invertible (dephasing with
\gamma<\infty
, depolarizing with
p<1
), but requires precise knowledge of the noise parameters.
For depolarizing noise with large
p
(specifically
p>1-1/d
), the inverted state from Eq. (
6
) may have negative eigenvalues even for a valid input
\rho_{\mathrm{noisy}}
; the PSD projection step corrects this at the cost of introducing estimation bias.
In our benchmarks (
p=0.3
,
d\leq 64
), PSD projection is rarely triggered for channel inversion.

III.4
Iterative refinement

Starting from the coherence-maximized estimate, we iterate:

1.

Set
\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}}^{(0)}
from Eq. (
4
).

2.

For
k=1,\ldots,K
: run CQEC with target
\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}}^{(k-1)}
to obtain
\rho_{\mathrm{rec}}^{(k)}
; update
\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}}^{(k)}=\alpha\,\rho_{\mathrm{rec}}^{(k)}+(1-\alpha)\,\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}}^{(k-1)}
with damping parameter
\alpha=0.5
.

The damping parameter
\alpha=0.5
was selected empirically to balance convergence speed against oscillation; values
\alpha\in[0.3,0.7]
yield similar results in our benchmarks.
We do not have an analytical convergence guarantee for this iteration: since the CQEC recovery map depends nonlinearly on the target estimate, the iteration is not a standard contraction mapping.
In all cases tested (
d\leq 64
,
K\leq 20
), the iteration was observed to converge monotonically in fidelity within
K=5
steps, with
|F_{\mathrm{est}}^{(k)}-F_{\mathrm{est}}^{(k-1)}|<10^{-4}
serving as the stopping criterion.
However, convergence failure at higher dimensions or under non-standard noise models cannot be excluded.

III.5
Multi-copy averaging

Given
n_{s}
independent noisy copies, we average
\bar{\rho}=n_{s}^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{s}}\rho_{\mathrm{noisy}}^{(k)}
(reducing statistical variance by
1/n_{s}
) and then apply coherence maximization.
We emphasize that this procedure is
not
quantum state tomography in the standard sense
Hradil 1997
;
Haah et al. 2017
: no informationally complete measurements are performed on the copies, and no maximum-likelihood or Bayesian estimation is used.
Rather, the copies are assumed to be available as density matrices (consistent with our density-matrix-level simulation framework), and the averaging reduces fluctuations in the noisy state representation.
In practice, the noisy copies are generated by independent applications of
\mathcal{E}
to the unknown
\rho_{\mathrm{target}}
, so
\bar{\rho}
is a better estimate of
\mathcal{E}(\rho_{\mathrm{target}})
, not of
\rho_{\mathrm{target}}
directly.
The subsequent coherence maximization step partially compensates for the systematic decoherence bias.
Operationally, the
n_{s}
copies would be obtained by re-running the quantum algorithm
n_{s}
times (not by cloning, which is forbidden by the no-cloning theorem); the density-matrix averaging in our simulation is equivalent to the statistical average over independently prepared copies.

(8)

We also report the trace distance
D(\rho_{\mathrm{rec}},\rho_{\mathrm{target}})=\frac{1}{2}\|\rho_{\mathrm{rec}}-\rho_{\mathrm{target}}\|_{1}
and the coherence recovery ratio
C_{\ell_{1}}(\rho_{\mathrm{rec}})/C_{\ell_{1}}(\rho_{\mathrm{target}})
, where
C_{\ell_{1}}(\rho)=\sum_{i\neq j}|\rho_{ij}|
is the
\ell_{1}
-norm of coherence
Baumgratz et al. 2014
, a standard monotone in the resource theory of coherence
Streltsov et al. 2017
.

For the copy sweep, we fit
F(n)=1-A\,n^{-\alpha}
to extract the scaling exponent
\alpha
and the prefactor
A
.
This power-law ansatz is motivated by the known
O(n^{-1})
convergence of the infidelity
1-F
in standard quantum state tomography
Haah et al. 2017
, where the exponent
\alpha=1
corresponds to the standard quantum limit.
For our multi-copy averaging strategy (which is not full tomography), the effective exponent
\alpha
may differ from unity, and we treat it as a free parameter to capture both sub-standard (
\alpha<1
) and super-standard (
\alpha>1
) convergence rates.

VI
Discussion

VI.1
Regimes of applicability

Our results delineate three operational regimes for blind CQEC:

1.

Low-dim, unknown noise
(
d\leq 16
): Coherence maximization is sufficient.
No noise-model information is needed;
F_{\mathrm{rec}}>0.95
with
\sim\!10
copies.
This regime covers most near-term quantum algorithms (qDRIFT, QKAN, QPE).

2.

High-dim, known noise
(
d\gg 16
): Channel inversion is required.
Coherence maximization fails because population estimates become unreliable.
This regime requires noise characterization (e.g., via randomized benchmarking
Emerson et al. 2005
) but not target-state knowledge.

3.

Copy-constrained
: With only
n\leq 5
copies, all blind strategies degrade significantly.
The oracle itself achieves only
F_{\mathrm{rec}}\approx 0.7
at
n=2
, indicating that the finite-copy penalty is intrinsic to CQEC, not to blindness.

A notable feature of the results is that
naive estimation never improves over no correction
.
This occurs because CQEC is a directed recovery process that amplifies coherence modes selectively toward the target specification: when the target is set to the noisy state itself (
\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}}=\rho_{\mathrm{noisy}}
), the noisy state is a fixed point of the recovery map, so no improvement occurs.
Multi-copy averaging of identical noisy copies likewise yields
\rho_{\mathrm{noisy}}
, so the naive strategy cannot be rescued by additional copies.

VI.2
Why coherence maximization degrades at high dimension

The success of coherence maximization at low
d
and its degradation at high
d
can be understood quantitatively.
For a
d
-dimensional maximally mixed noisy state, all populations approach
1/d
, and the physicality bound
\sqrt{p_{i}p_{j}}\approx 1/d
carries vanishing information about the target’s off-diagonal structure.
In contrast, at low
d
with near-pure targets, the bound is tight and phase-preserving noise retains the exact phase structure (Sec.
III.2
), allowing near-perfect reconstruction.
The crossover observed at
d\approx 16
–
64
thus reflects the transition from the information-rich to the information-poor regime of the population-based estimator.

VI.3
Comparison with standard and purification QEC

It is important to position blind CQEC relative to standard stabilizer-based QEC
Shor 1995
;
Gottesman 1997
.
Standard QEC encodes logical qubits into physical qubits
before
noise acts, enabling syndrome-based correction without knowledge of the encoded state.
This encoding-first paradigm is fundamentally different from CQEC, which operates
after
noise has corrupted an unencoded state.
The two approaches are therefore not directly comparable: standard QEC requires foresight (pre-encoding), while blind CQEC is a post-hoc recovery method applicable when encoding was not performed or was insufficient.
Blind CQEC does not replace standard QEC but rather addresses a complementary scenario—recovering states from noisy quantum computations where pre-encoding was absent or where the noise exceeded the code’s correction capacity.

Purification QEC (PQEC)
Raghoonanan and Byrnes 2026
also recovers states without target knowledge, using recursive swap tests.
We note that PQEC is a concurrent work in preparation; the comparison below is preliminary and based on the available description rather than finalized results.
The key structural differences are:

•

PQEC uses recursive purification requiring multiple identical noisy copies, with the copy count growing with the desired precision;
blind CQEC requires as few as 5–10 copies at
d\leq 16
.

•

PQEC operates without any noise-model information;
blind CQEC performs best when the noise model is known (channel inversion), though coherence maximization provides a noise-model-free alternative at low
d
.

The two approaches address complementary regimes: PQEC is suited for settings where no noise information is available and copies are abundant; blind CQEC is suited for the copy-constrained, noise-characterized regime.

VI.4
Limitations

Several limitations warrant discussion:

1.

Noise-parameter sensitivity of channel inversion.

In practice, noise parameters must be estimated, introducing additional uncertainty.
The systematic sensitivity analysis of Sec.
V.7
quantifies this: at
d\leq 8
, channel inversion tolerates
\pm 30\%
parameter error with
F_{\mathrm{rec}}>0.77
; at
d=64
,
\lesssim 10\%
accuracy is required to maintain
F_{\mathrm{rec}}>0.68
.
Dephasing is the most sensitive parameter due to the exponential amplification in Eq. (
5
).
Noise characterization via randomized benchmarking
Emerson et al. 2005
achieving
\lesssim 10\%
accuracy suffices for the dimensions tested.

2.

Mixed-state targets.

The Werner-state benchmark (Sec.
V.8
) demonstrates degradation of coherence maximization below purity
v\approx 0.6
.
For highly mixed targets, channel inversion or the hybrid strategy is recommended.

3.

Scalability and tensor product structure.

The Haar-random sweep (Sec.
V.6
) extends our characterization to
d=256
, but this still treats the system as a single
d
-dimensional space.
In realistic multi-qubit settings, noise acts locally, and estimation strategies could exploit this locality (e.g., per-qubit coherence maximization) to achieve better scaling than the
1/d
degradation observed here.
Extending the framework to exploit tensor product structure is a natural next step; extrapolation to the fault-tolerant regime will require such local approaches.

4.

Experimental validation.

All results are based on density-matrix-level numerical simulation.
Circuit-level implementation and hardware validation remain open.

5.

Dependence on concurrent work.

The CQEC and ICEC framework
Wakaura 2026a
;
Wakaura 2026b
and the PQEC comparison
Raghoonanan and Byrnes 2026
reference works in preparation.
The theoretical results of the present paper—specifically Eq. (
1
) and the estimation strategies—are self-contained and do not depend on the details of those works.
However, the full ICEC protocol (which implements the catalytic covariant transformation as a concrete density-matrix operation) is described in Ref.
Wakaura 2026a
; the essential interface used here is: given
\rho_{\mathrm{noisy}}
,
\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}}
, and a catalyst
c
, the ICEC protocol returns a recovered state
\rho_{\mathrm{rec}}
satisfying
F(\rho_{\mathrm{rec}},\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}})\geq F_{\mathrm{oracle}}
when
\mathcal{C}(\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{est}})\subseteq\mathcal{C}(\rho_{\mathrm{noisy}})
.
The PQEC comparison is based on preliminary results and should be updated when Ref.
Raghoonanan and Byrnes 2026
is finalized.

VII
Computational Details

All simulations were performed as density-matrix-level numerical calculations using custom Python code (NumPy 2.0.2, SciPy 1.13.1) on an Apple M4 Max workstation.
No quantum hardware or circuit-level emulators were used; all operations act directly on
d\times d
density matrices.
The seven benchmark suites—qubit/qutrit noise sweeps, copy-number sweeps, four-algorithm benchmarks, Haar-random dimension sweep (
{\sim}\,84
s), noise-parameter sensitivity (
{\sim}\,38
s), mixed-state and hybrid benchmarks (
{\sim}\,11
s), and the VQE demonstration (
<1
s)—complete in a total of approximately 148 s.
These short runtimes reflect the proof-of-concept scale (
d\leq 64
); the density-matrix approach has
O(d^{6})
cost (Sec.
II.2
), becoming prohibitive beyond
d\sim 10^{3}
without exploiting tensor product structure.

All results are deterministic given
numpy.random.seed(42)
.
The target states are fixed by the choice of quantum algorithm (not randomly sampled), and the noise channels are deterministic at the density-matrix level, so no error bars are needed on the main results (Table
2
).
The algorithm-specific results (Table
2
) are supplemented by the Haar-random dimension sweep (Sec.
V.6
), which uses 20 Haar-random states per dimension with
numpy.random.seed(42)
and confirms that the algorithm-specific trends generalize.
Source code is available in the supplementary material.

VIII
Conclusion

We introduced blind catalytic quantum error correction, removing the requirement that the target state be known—the principal limitation of the original CQEC framework.
Five estimation strategies were benchmarked across four quantum algorithms, three noise channels (individually and in combination), Hilbert space dimensions
d=4
–
64
, Haar-random states up to
d=256
, and mixed-state targets with purity
v\in[0.3,1.0]
.

The central findings are:

1.

Estimation is the sole bottleneck
:
F_{\mathrm{rec}}\approx 0.98\,F_{\mathrm{est}}+0.01
(
R^{2}=0.993
), reducing blind CQEC optimization to target estimation.

2.

Analytical crossover
: coherence maximization and channel inversion achieve equal performance at
d^{*}\approx 25
–
40
[Eq. (
3
)], confirmed numerically at
d\approx 32
.
A hybrid interpolation (Eq. (
10
)) provides a smooth, tunable transition.

3.

Per-channel decomposition
: the crossover is driven primarily by amplitude damping (population redistribution), not dimension per se (Tables
3
–
5
).

4.

Mixed-state regime
: coherence maximization degrades below purity
v\approx 0.6
; noise-model knowledge is essential for mixed targets even at low
d
(Fig.
13
).

5.

Practical utility
: an end-to-end VQE demonstration for H
2
shows
3.4\times
energy-error reduction with channel-inversion blind CQEC (Fig.
15
).

Copy scaling follows power laws with physically interpretable exponents:
\alpha\approx 0.5
(statistical limit),
\alpha\approx 1
(tomographic limit), and
\alpha>1
(denoising synergy for channel inversion).
Noise-parameter sensitivity analysis confirms that
\lesssim 10\%
characterization accuracy suffices for
d\leq 64
(Fig.
11
).

Open directions include circuit-level compilation, exploitation of tensor product structure for multi-qubit scalability, analytical proof of the Lipschitz continuity, extension to non-Markovian and correlated noise, and experimental validation.
Addressing these would advance blind CQEC from a density-matrix proof of concept toward practical autonomous quantum error correction.
