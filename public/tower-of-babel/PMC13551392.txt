Occupational Allergic Contact Dermatitis Caused by Cyclohexyl Diethanolamine in a Metalworking Fluid
Authors: Maria Pesonen, Sari Suomela, Katri Suuronen
Published: 2026

Case Report

The patient is a machinist involved in this work for the past 25 years. He has had regular skin contact with a metalworking fluid (MWF) in the form of splashes and aerosol and the MWF leaking through the protective gloves, which are textile gloves partially coated with rubber. The MWF product used at the worksite has remained the same during the past few years. The patient has no previous history of dermatitis. He developed eczema on the hands, forearms, face and anterior side of the trunk and legs. The eczema healed on sick leave and has remained in remission after the patient retired from his work. The patient gave informed consent for publishing his case. At the manufacturer's request, the name of the MWF product and the manufacturer is not disclosed.

Patch testing was performed with Finn Chambers (SmartPractice, Calgary, Canada) with baseline series and a selection of Finnish Institute of Occupational Health (FIOH)'s patch test series including rubber, antimicrobial, oils and cutting fluids, ethanolamines, coconut fatty acid derivatives, glues and plastics and cosmetics series. The patient patch tested weakly positive to monoethanolamine (MEA, CAS 141–43‐5) 2% and showed doubtful reactions to MEA 1%, and to diethanolamine (CAS 111–42‐2) 2% and 1% present in the ethanolamines series (Table 1). As workplace material, an unused, undiluted sample of the patient's MWF was tested as 10% pet, 3.2% pet and 1.0% pet (Figure 1A). As the patient tested positive to it, the ingredients of the product were inquired about and were kindly provided by the manufacturer of the MWF. A dilution series of the MWF down to 0.01% (Figure 1B) and its 16 ingredients were then patch tested on the patient. One of them, cyclohexyl diethanolamine (cyclohexyl‐DEA, or (cyclohexylimino)‐bisethanol, CAS 4500‐29‐2) whose concentration in the MWF was 3%, gave a strong positive reaction (Figure 1C) and another ingredient described as “monoethanolamine dilutions,” gave a weak positive reaction (Table 1).

Patch tests of unexposed volunteers with cyclohexyl‐DEA 2.0% pet (n = 20) were negative. As the ingredient “monoethanolamine dilutions” turned out to be identical to the MEA patch tested in the FIOH ethanolamines series and which is a known contact allergen, there was no need to test control patients with it. Repeated open application test (ROAT) with cyclohexyl‐DEA was not performed in the patient. The patient was diagnosed with occupational allergic contact dermatitis caused by cyclohexyl‐DEA and MEA in a MWF.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first report on contact allergy to cyclohexyl‐DEA. Allergy to it was only discovered by testing the patient's own MWF and its ingredients.

Cyclohexyl‐DEA resembles other alkanolamine derivatives that are used for example as pH adjusters and corrosion inhibitors in water‐miscible MWF. According to the Finnish Chemical Products Register [1], cyclohexyl‐DEA is currently listed in only two active chemical products. Both are MWFs but the one used by our patient is not listed. The European Chemicals Agency (ECHA) lists three registrants of cyclohexyl‐DEA, and its annual market in Europe is 100–1000 tons [2]. The product categories in which it is used are hydraulic fluids, lubricants and MWF.

Cyclohexyl‐DEA, like other ethanolamines, is alkaline and thereby potentially irritating in patch testing. In the present case, we used a dilution series with several concentrations from 2% pet downwards, which gave a consistent decrescendo pattern in the index patient, whereas tests on 20 control patients with 2% pet were negative. These findings indicate that the patient's reaction to cyclohexyl‐DEA was indeed allergic, and that 2% pet is an appropriate test dilution for cyclohexyl‐DEA. We and others have reported other ethanolamine derivative allergies in the past [3, 4]. Also in these cases, investigations have required testing own MWFs and their technical ingredients, as commercial test substances of ethanolamine derivatives are lacking.

We conclude that cyclohexyl‐DEA is a new MWF allergen that should be considered in machinists and possibly also other professionals that handle lubricants.
