Current practices for recruitment and retention of plasma donors across 17 countries: A short report
Authors: Marloes L. C. Spekman, Eva‐Maria Merz
Published: 2026

INTRODUCTION

Donated plasma is required for the manufacture of plasma‐derived medicinal products (PDMPs), which are essential for both prophylaxis and treatment of patients. As such, plasma is an important resource and brings health value to patients. Because of current and emerging social, geopolitical or economic challenges, this important raw material and ‘strategic resource’ is at risk of supply interruption [1, 2, 3, 4] leading to a lack of availability of essential products for patients. There is an estimated annual shortfall of more than 4.5 million litres of plasma to meet demand in Europe [5]. The plasma supply chain depends on the willingness of individuals to (repeatedly) donate their plasma [1, 4]. To increase the number of donors and donations, blood establishments (BEs) use various information, recruitment and retention strategies to increase the willingness of individuals to repeatedly donate plasma [6]. In this short report, we provide an overview of the current situation of plasma collection in relation to (plasma) donor recruitment and retention strategies across 17 European countries.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study procedure

An online survey (Appendix S1), consisting of questions about information, communication, recruitment and retention efforts, was sent to BEs via e‐mail. Initially, the invitation to participate was distributed via the network of the European Blood Alliance (EBA). As the invitation and a reminder did not result in many responses, we actively reached out to blood and plasma collectors through our own networks, mostly via e‐mail. Between July and October 2023, 17 respondents replied to the survey on behalf of their organization. For an overview of respondents, (types of) organizations and countries, see Table 1.

Online survey

The survey first addressed questions related to general information about the respondents' organization. We assessed kinds of organization (e.g., not‐for‐profit, governmental, hospital‐based, commercial/private), type of blood products collected and the share of self‐sufficiency (for plasma‐collecting organizations). We also asked questions about processes, such as donor registration and (first‐time) actual donation.

The next section was related to donor recruitment. We queried about strategies and targets in place for donor recruitment, as well as who carries out the donor recruitment for the organization. Additionally, we provided respondents with a list of possible methods used in donor recruitment and asked them to indicate which of them they had used in their recruitment efforts during 2022 (e.g., television commercials, direct mail campaigns, street advertising, recruitment teams at events or fairs, commercials/campaigns on social media). We also asked which substantive elements were used in donor recruitment in 2022, such as ‘raising awareness about the need for blood/plasma’, ‘information on the blood/plasma donation process’, ‘patient stories’ and ‘celebrities/influencers/vloggers’. Furthermore, we asked what factors complicated donor recruitment (e.g., budgets, information technology (IT) limitations, lack of expertise) and whether organizations communicated on (inter)national plasma‐related issues (possible shortages, self‐sufficiency levels) to different audiences (e.g., staff, donors, general public, politicians/political parties).

The following survey section was dedicated to what organizations offered donors for their donations. We deliberately steered away from terms such as ‘rewards’ and ‘incentives’. Instead, respondents were provided with a list of possible offerings and asked to indicate which ones they offered to their donors. For the items they did offer, we asked whether they explicitly communicated this offering prior to donation, and how it is offered (when, how often, how much).

The following section inquired whether specific groups were targeted in retention strategies, whether loyalty or savings programmes were used for donors and whether donors receive a message after their donation. We also asked whether donors are informed about their personal donation history, and if so, via what method (e.g., during the donation, via a personal donor account, or through e‐mail).

The final section was dedicated to evaluation of recruitment and retention strategies, recruitment and retention in crisis situations and optional data reporting. For the latter, we asked respondents to report (if possible) how many donors were recruited and retained in 2022.

RESULTS

Of the 17 respondents who answered the survey, 15 work in organizations that operate within the European Union (EU), while the other 2 operate in European countries outside of the EU (e.g., Scotland). The majority of organizations (10) were governmental or state public bodies, while 5 were non‐governmental or non‐state not‐for‐profit organizations and 2 were hospital‐based. All 17 of these organizations collect whole blood, 16 collect platelets, 12 collect plasma and 7 collect combinations of blood products (e.g., plasma and platelets) (see Table 1).

Self‐sufficiency

Of the 12 plasma‐collecting organizations, the majority (9) indicated that they do not collect enough to meet national demand and are thus (partially) dependent on plasma from other countries. Six (out of nine) organizations also shared the proportion of self‐sufficiency for their organization, which ranged between 0% and 70% (40% on average). Of the organizations that indicated their country collected enough plasma to meet national demands, one specified that national demand was met by (other) organizations in their country, and two said their own organization collects enough to meet national demands. When asked whether they communicated about (inter)national plasma‐related issues, all but 1 (out of 12) plasma‐collecting organizations indicated they did communicate about plasma‐related issues. These 11 organizations all communicated about plasma‐related issues to their staff. Nine of them also communicated about it to donor associations and voluntary organizations, nine communicated about it to policy makers and nine communicated about it to the general public. In an open text field, one organization indicated they also communicated about it to their donors, one also communicated it to competent authorities, BEs and scientific societies and one organization indicated they communicate about plasma‐related issues to news media and journalists.

Donor recruitment

In 16 of the 17 organizations, donor recruitment was (partially) carried out by marketing/communication/promotion departments at national (10), regional (10) or blood bank level (7). A donor service department also contributed to donor recruitment in six organizations, as well as local Red Cross or Red Crescent organizations (five), voluntary organizations (six), blood collection teams (four) and student organizations (four). Two organizations had also partially outsourced recruitment to a different organization. When asked what challenges organizations faced in the recruitment of whole blood donors, many of the 17 organizations indicated that they had limited capacity (e.g., staff shortages; 13), low budgets (9) and limitations to the IT infrastructure (7). Competition in the blood/plasma market, changing regulations and lack of marketing or social media expertise were mentioned by three respondents. For the 12 organizations active in plasma collection, main challenges included limited capacity (7), low budgets (7) and limitations to the IT infrastructure (6).

All 17 organizations indicated they had recruitment strategies in place, and 15 of them also had targets for recruitment. Of the organizations that collected both whole blood and plasma (12), 8 had separate recruitment strategies for whole blood and plasma donor recruitment (while 4 did not). When asked about the main differences between whole blood and plasma donor recruitment, respondents explained that different messages were used for both, that more efforts were needed for plasma donor recruitment and that whole blood donors were mostly recruited among non‐donors while plasma donors were often recruited from the whole blood donor pool. This aligns with the variety of recruitment methods used for whole blood and plasma donor recruitment. As shown in Table 2, mass media such as television, radio and newspapers were mostly used for whole blood donor recruitment and much less so for plasma donor recruitment. Recruitment at events or fairs, in companies, in schools and in cooperation with other organizations was also quite popular for both types of recruitment. Among organizations that used social media in their recruitment in 2022, the most popular social media channels were Facebook, YouTube, Instagram and Twitter (for both whole blood and plasma donor recruitment; see Table 2).

When asked what elements their recruitment strategies contained in 2022, all 17 organizations indicated they created awareness of the need for blood, while 10 organizations also created awareness of the need for plasma. General information about blood was included by 14 organizations, and 9 included general information about plasma. Information on the conditions to be treated with blood (products) was included in the recruitment efforts of 14 organizations. Fifteen and eight organizations, respectively, included information on the whole blood and plasma donation process. Both patient and donor stories were included by 14 organizations, while 10 used celebrities/influencers/vloggers and donor ambassadors in their recruitment efforts.

Donor retention

Out of the 17 organizations, 3 indicated they had no clear retention strategy in place. Of the 14 organizations that did have a retention strategy, the majority said they targeted first‐time donors (11) and repeat donors (10). Donors with blood type O were targeted in six organizations, and deferred donors and young donors were both targeted in four. Other groups that were targeted in one organization each were women, ‘foreigners’ and lapsed donors.

Three organizations indicated they had a loyalty or savings programme in place for plasma donors, and one organization offered such a programme to their whole blood donors. Nine organizations send out a ‘thank you’ message to their whole blood donors after their donation, and five send a message to whole blood donors when the blood product has been used or has been sent to a hospital for use. Six organizations send this message via SMS or text message, four do so via e‐mail, two via a letter or postcard and two use different media to send the message. In contrast to the messages whole blood donors get, plasma donors only receive a ‘thank you’ message in five organizations; no organizations send out messages to plasma donors when their plasma has been used. In addition, all organizations offered their donors one or more ‘offerings’ (see Table 3). Almost all organizations offered their whole blood (16/17) and plasma donors (10/12) small gifts (e.g., pens, towels), while only one organization offered payment to whole blood donors and two organizations offered payment to plasma donors.

Evaluation

Eight organizations systematically evaluate the effectiveness of their recruitment and retention campaigns, and an additional seven organizations do this every now and then or for specific campaigns. The 13 organizations who indicated they either systematically or sporadically evaluate campaigns noted they do this by determining goals or performance indicators prior to the campaign and check afterwards if these were reached. They furthermore indicated that they compare performance indicators during the campaign period to a comparable time period. For social media campaigns, 14 organizations assess views, likes, clicks and/or engagement. Five organizations also evaluate by relating the costs of campaigns to the number of recruited donors. Two organizations used more global indicators, such as stock levels, the number of first‐time donors or the number of plasma donors.

Recruitment and retention during crisis situations

Of the 17 organizations, 14 stated they had to adapt their recruitment strategies during the COVID‐19 pandemic. Many indicated they were forced to stop face‐to‐face recruitment (e.g., in schools, street advertising) and resorted to online campaigns (e.g., social media, influencers). Several organizations also changed their donation system to by‐appointment only, to ensure social distancing on site. Four organizations indicated that their current recruitment strategies were only to a very small extent affected by the changes made during the pandemic. The organizations in which the pandemic did influence current recruitment strategies stated that they now use new or different media (three), had abandoned certain media (two), now target new or different audiences (three), forged collaborations with other organizations/authorities/companies (four) or ended existing collaborations (two).

Unlike recruitment strategies, retention strategies remained unchanged during the pandemic in 13 organizations. The three organizations which indicated that they changed their retention strategies said they adapted donor rewards (one), communicated more with donors than before (three) and via new or different media (one).

CONCLUSION

The demand for donor plasma will continue to grow. Given this ongoing development and the limited self‐sufficiency of European countries in their plasma availability, and hence dependence on imported plasma, it is of utmost importance to develop and improve efficient information, recruitment and retention strategies. While current BEs employ a broad portfolio of strategies, varying and targeted content and different media and channels to reach their audiences, there is very limited data and knowledge available on evaluation, effectiveness and impact of the strategies. Such information is of utmost importance to improve current strategies and develop effective and cost‐efficient new recruitment and retention strategies and should be a future focus in both blood donation research and policymaking.
