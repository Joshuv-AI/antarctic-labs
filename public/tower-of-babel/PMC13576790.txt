Clinical and Molecular Features of BAP1‐Mutated Meningiomas: Case Series
Authors: Ivan Pradilla, Rasha Alfattal, Susan L. McGovern, Shaan M. Raza, Franco DeMonte, Leomar Y. Ballester, Chirag B. Patel, Carlos Kamiya‐Matsuoka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBRCA1‐associated protein 1 (BAP1)‐deficient meningiomas represent a clinically and molecularly distinct subgroup with variable histology and aggressive behavior that may not be fully captured by current grading criteria. The clinical spectrum of BAP1 alterations in meningiomas, including both somatic and germline contexts, remains incompletely characterized. We report three cases of BAP1‐deficient meningiomas with divergent clinical trajectories. Case 1 was a 44‐year‐old woman with a somatic BAP1 nonsense mutation (Q392*) presenting as recurrent spinal meningiomatosis. Despite WHO grade 1 histology with rhabdoid features and no high‐grade criteria, she experienced rapid recurrence and required multiple resections, radiation, and systemic therapy, illustrating that truncating somatic mutations may drive aggressive behavior independent of histological grade. Case 2 was a 22‐year‐old man harboring a germline BAP1 truncating mutation (R385*) with a family history of meningioma, who presented with a WHO grade 3 papillary meningioma and remained progression‐free 24 months after resection and adjuvant radiotherapy. His family pedigree represents a rare phenotype within the BAP1 tumor predisposition syndrome. Case 3 was a 57‐year‐old man with a low‐variant‐allele‐frequency BAP1 missense mutation (Y173C) and WHO grade 2 meningioma who demonstrated a favorable course at 26 months following gross total resection without adjuvant therapy. These cases illustrate the phenotypic heterogeneity of BAP1‐deficient meningiomas and underscore that truncating mutations, particularly somatic ones, may confer aggressive behavior regardless of histological grade. Variant allele frequency and mutation type should inform risk stratification alongside traditional pathological features. Consideration of BAP1 as a defining molecular feature in future WHO classification iterations is likely warranted.

Introduction

BRCA1‐associated protein 1 (BAP1)‐deficient meningiomas represent a clinically aggressive subgroup with variable histology [1]. The rhabdoid and papillary subtypes have been associated with this mutation, which have in turn been classified as grade 3 meningiomas in prior iterations of the World Health Organization Classification of Central Nervous System Tumors (CNS WHO) [2]. The most recent edition of the WHO CNS Classification recommends grading based on the presence of high‐grade features (mitoses, cellularity, necrosis, etc.), clarifying that at least a subset of the papillary and rhabdoid meningiomas will behave aggressively in the absence of high‐grade features [3]. Although BAP1 inactivating alterations are not included in the classification, it is increasingly recognized that tumors displaying this alteration can behave aggressively, independent of histological features [1]. While the frequency of BAP1 alterations in meningiomas in general is unknown, they have been described in up to two‐thirds of rhabdoid meningiomas, with increasing frequency in recurrent meningiomas [4]. Additionally, BAP1‐altered meningiomas display a tendency to be located in the infratentorial and spinal compartments, which are anatomically challenging regions that may limit the feasibility of biopsy or resection [5].

BAP1 is a widely expressed deubiquitinase that is involved in DNA repair, cell‐cycle progression, and apoptosis [6]. BAP1 facilitates the response to double‐strand breaks by promoting the assembly of homologous recombination factors, including its namesake, BRCA1, along with RAD51 [7]. It also participates in cell cycle progression at the G1/S checkpoint by interacting with regulators of the E2F proteins and by directly suppressing the translation of RB [6]. Lastly, deubiquitination of IR3P3 at the endoplasmic reticulum membrane promotes the physiological release of calcium from mitochondria, which in turn induces the release of cytochrome C, resulting in apoptosis.

This multitude of roles for BAP1 partly explains the phenotypic variability observed in patients with the BAP1 tumor predisposition syndrome (TPDS), in which meningiomas have been reported with variable frequency [8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. It is not yet known if meningiomas in this group of patients behave differently from those arising from somatic mutations. We present three cases of patients with BAP1‐deficient meningiomas in the context of both somatic and germline mutations with distinct clinical trajectories (brief case summary in Table 1).

Case Presentation

Case 1

A 44‐year‐old woman presented to our clinic with a history of recurrent spinal meningioma. Her history dated to 4 years prior to this visit, when she noticed numbness of the right arm followed by weakness. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) at that time reportedly showed a post‐contrast enhancing dural/extradural mass over the C5‐C6 spinal levels with T1‐weighted MRI features compatible with meningioma. She underwent a subtotal resection with pathology showing cells with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm and eccentric nuclei, rare mitoses and with no other high‐grade features. The diagnosis was WHO grade 1 meningioma with rhabdoid features. She received no further treatment and underwent periodic surveillance.

Twenty months after the first procedure, she presented with worsening of right shoulder pain and contrast‐enhanced MRI showed disease progression with growth to the adjacent C7‐T1 levels. In addition, cervical spine MRI showed a non‐enhancing nodule over the ventral spinal canal at C1 with no spinal cord involvement. She underwent resection of the progressive disease at C7‐T1 (t = 29 months after diagnosis, Simpson IV), followed 5 months later by re‐resection of the residual C5‐C6 mass (Simpson IV). The diagnosis from these tissues was WHO grade 1 meningioma with rhabdoid features. The patient received adjuvant radiation therapy with the aim of treating recurrent disease (48.6 Gy in 27 fractions) (t = 35 months after diagnosis).

Pain and weakness in the right extremities persisted during and after radiation therapy, with MRI showing progression 6 months after radiation therapy, after which she was first assessed at our clinic (t = 42 months from diagnosis). On exam, she was hemiparetic with sensory loss and pain in the right upper and lower extremities with a Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) scale score of 80. A solid tumor genomic assay performed on samples from the second resection was analyzed, identifying a nonsense mutation in the BAP1 gene (Q392*), which was consistent with loss of nuclear BAP1 expression on immunohistochemistry. An additional variant was found in the BRCA1 gene (R322Q), with unknown significance (Figure 1).

After consultation with radiation oncology, it was determined that the patient was not a good candidate for additional radiation therapy of that lesion, and she was offered treatment with sunitinib on the basis of the results of a phase II trial in recurrent atypical/anaplastic meningioma (t = 45 months) [13]. She experienced significant tongue pain after the third cycle of sunitinib, which improved after dose reduction. The patient completed 12 cycles of sunitinib without additional side‐effects, although pain and weakness in her right upper and lower extremities persisted. Twelve months after this, she presented progressive mid‐thoracic pain and gait dysfunction, without loss of bowel/bladder control (t = 82 months). These symptoms progressed in severity, which led her to seek emergency care a year later, upon which a T10 sensory level was noted (t = 94 months). Thoracic spine MRI showed a well‐defined extra medullary intradural mass at the T10‐T11 level with homogeneous enhancement measuring 1.9 × 1.1 × t 1.4 cm. The mass compressed the thoracic cord with complete effacement of the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) signal. She received management with dexamethasone underwent a thoracic laminectomy, and excision of the dural lesion (t = 95 months, Simpson I). Histopathology for this lesion continued to show a meningioma with rhabdoid features without other high‐grade features. Likewise, genomic analysis of the tumor revealed identical BAP1 (Q392*) and BRCA1 (R322Q) mutations as had been found in the second resection tissue sample. Post‐operatively, the patient reported progressive improvement in back pain, lower limb sensation, and pain.

A year after thoracic laminectomy, chronic pain persisted despite multimodal pain management, and the implantation of a cervical neurostimulator was suggested. However, the previously known non‐enhancing C1‐C2 lesion displayed significant interval growth and was potentially amenable to radiation therapy. Since the presence of the device would contraindicate radiation therapy in the future, and given the lesion's proximity to the cervicomedullary junction, the patient was offered proton therapy prior to device implantation. She received this treatment over 5 weeks (45 GyRBE in 25 fractions, t = 116 months from symptom onset).

Since then, the lesion has undergone reduction in size and the patient is undergoing preoperative evaluations for device implantation.

No germline mutations were identified in this patient, and there was no family history of malignancy. At present, although the surveillance MRIs have been stable, the patient complains of persistent paresthesia in the right upper limb and hypoesthesia in both lower limbs and the perineal area.

Case 2

A 22‐year‐old man presented with a 3‐month history of new‐onset bifrontal headaches which awakened him from sleep. A head computed tomography (CT) scan demonstrated a hyperdense large left frontal lesion and significant mass effect (Figure 2). Subsequently, a brain MRI showed an extra‐axial mass arising from the falx cerebri, with homogeneous contrast‐enhancement on T1‐weighted MRI, compatible with a meningioma. An accompanying subacute hemorrhagic focus was found on the lateral margin of the mass. No hydrocephalus was present at that time.

The patient's family history was significant for a resection of a posterior fossa meningioma in the father at age 28 (currently 52), with 2 recurrences in the last 10 years that required re‐resection and radiation therapy. The patient's brother died suddenly at age 10, and a CNS WHO grade 3 papillary meningioma was discovered on autopsy. The patient's paternal grandfather also had a history of meningioma. There was no family history of uveal melanoma, mesothelioma, or other malignancy. Further genetic counseling and testing revealed a germline pathogenic variant in the BAP1 gene (R385*).

Given the family history of grade 3 meningioma, and the significant mass effect observed at presentation, the patient underwent resection of the mass 3 weeks after his first visit (Simpson IV). The postoperative course was uneventful, and postoperative imaging revealed a small focus (1.1 × 1.1 cm) of contrast‐enhancement on T1‐weighted MRI, consistent with residual meningioma at the posterior margin of the resection cavity.

Histopathology of the resected mass showed a papillary morphology with areas of increased mitotic activity and micronecrosis, a mitotic index of 7 mitoses per 10 high power fields (HPF), and a Ki67 index of 15%, which was compatible with a CNS WHO grade 3 meningioma. Immunohistochemistry revealed loss of nuclear expression of BAP1, which was consistent with the known germline truncating mutation found in this patient.

Given the tumor's papillary histology and the patient's germline mutation in BAP1, the risk of recurrence was considered high enough to merit treatment as a malignant meningioma. Aiming to reduce the risk of recurrence, the patient received adjuvant radiation therapy (6000 cGy in 30 fractions) 2 months after resection without complications. At last follow‐up, 24 months after resection, the patient remained fully functional with and asymptomatic (KPS 100), without clinical or imaging evidence of disease progression.

Case 3

A 57‐year‐old man presented to the clinic with a history that had started 7 months prior with mild weakness of the left foot (t = 0 months). The weakness in the foot became progressively more severe, and hypoesthesia and weakness appeared in the left hand as well. He also reported short‐lasting episodes of involuntary forced wrist flexion, which were interpreted to be focal aware seizures. Brain MR imaging showed a large cystic mass in the right frontoparietal area. Its solid component demonstrated avid contrast‐enhancement on T1‐weighted imaging, appeared to be dural‐based, and displayed significant surrounding edema with mild midline shift (Figure 3).

The patient underwent a gross total resection with an uneventful postoperative course 8 months after symptom onset (Simpson I). Pathology showed a CNS WHO grade 2 meningioma (7 mitoses per 10 HPF, Ki67 6%) with mostly meningothelial characteristics and without additional high‐grade features. The solid tumor genomic assay showed a BAP1 missense mutation (Y173C) with a low variant allele frequency (VAF) of under 5%, and an additional FAT1 missense mutation (P2011S) with an equally low VAF. Cytogenetics showed an absent homozygous deletion of CDKN2A. Given these features, the patient decided to continue surveillance rather than attempting adjuvant radiation therapy.

At last follow‐up, 26 months after symptom onset, he had returned to his baseline functional status, asymptomatic and seizure‐free after stopping anti‐seizure medication. Imaging showed stable postoperative changes, without evidence of recurrence.

Discussion

The cases presented here demonstrate the differences in the clinical scenarios of patients with meningiomas and either somatic or germline BAP1 mutations. Case 1 behaved as a predominantly spinal meningiomatosis, an exceptionally rare presentation [14]. In a review of reported cases, Jain et al. identified only 7 cases with multiple spinal meningiomas [14]. Despite the BAP1 somatic mutation, no rhabdoid features were identified in any of the meningiomas in this patient. The patient's progression‐free survival (PFS) of approximately 33 months after her first resection was considerably shorter than that reported for other CNS WHO grade 1 meningiomas with Simpson IV resections of 86.2 months [15]. In other reports, the PFS of patients with subtotally resected low‐risk meningioma was as high as 91.4% at 3 years, which would place the patient in Case 1 in the small group of ~10% of patients with progression before that [16]. We identified three cases reported in the literature of germline mutations matching that of Case 1 [17, 18]. One of these corresponded to a rhabdoid meningioma, CNS WHO Grade 3, in a pediatric patient with an unknown clinical course (Table 2). Although it has been established that the presence of BAP1 somatic mutations in high‐grade meningiomas confers a shorter PFS when compared with BAP1 intact meningiomas (26 months vs. 116 months per Shankar et al.), this may also be the case for low‐grade meningiomas [19].

In support of this notion, the association between BAP1 truncating mutations and rhabdoid or papillary features is challenged by the presence of alternative histological features, but a similarly aggressive behavior. Landry et al. describe two cases of BAP1‐deficient meningiomas with trabecular architecture, both of which had a predicted high risk of recurrence [23]. A literature review carried alongside those case reports identified 39 cases of BAP1‐deficient meningiomas, only one of which had a histopathology other than papillary/rhabdoid (anaplastic).

Recent work suggests that BAP1‐deficient meningiomas may constitute a molecularly distinct subgroup [5]. Shared features include 3p21 loss, Polycomb repressive complex dysregulation and a shorter PFS. The shared molecular and clinical features of BAP1‐deficient meningiomas suggest that they may be considered a distinct subtype in future iterations of the CNS WHO tumor classification.

The presentation observed in Case 2, though presumably classical for the BAP1 TPDS, is unusual in the strong family predisposition to meningiomas. After the identification of BAP1 mutations in metastatic forms of uveal melanoma in 2011, further work identified families with germline mutations and a predisposition to uveal melanoma, mesothelioma, renal cell carcinoma, and a variety of melanocytic tumors [8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. However, meningioma was reported in two patients in only one of these reports, only one of which had a confirmed germline mutation [11]. A study carried out in the Netherlands identified 22 families with germline BAP1 mutations and described associated cancers, finding that only one of the proven carriers of the mutation presented with meningioma [24]. In a report of eight additional families, meningioma was reported in only one subject [25]. This suggests that the presentation of meningiomas as a phenotypic feature in families with BAP1 TPDS is rare, as only that reported by Prasad et al. has rhabdoid meningiomas as its key phenotypic feature [26]. The family of the patient reported in Case 2 would presumably fit this profile, although genetic testing is absent for the other family members.

A cursory search of the University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center database showed no additional families with a BAP1 TPDS and meningiomas. Of the three cases we identified with a matching germline mutation in the literature, only one presented with meningioma, without a corresponding family history (Table 2) [20, 21].

While the clinical course of the patient in Case 2 is so far within expectations for papillary meningiomas, the father's PFS has been unusual, as the mean PFS in meningiomas with rhabdoid features has been as low as 46.6 months [27]. It is uncertain if the brother may have had a similar course had he been treated.

Case 3 illustrates the difficulty in interpreting low‐allele‐frequency BAP1 mutations, especially when histologic features are intermediate and clinical behavior appears indolent. Unlike Cases 1 and 2, which harbored clear truncating mutations and showed aggressive clinical trajectories, Case 3 involved a missense mutation (Y173C) with a variant allele frequency under 5%, raising the possibility of a subclonal or passenger mutation. The stop codons in the truncating mutations of Cases 2 and 3 are expected to be a target for nonsense‐mediated RNA decay, leading to complete loss of function [28]. The Y173C mutation falls within the Ubiquitin C‐Terminal Hydrolase domain of BAP1 and could be expected to modify the geometry of BAP1's active site and to alter its deubiquitinase activity. However, this remains a theoretical consideration [22]. The pathogenicity of the mutation is supported by a prior report of the same mutation in the germline of a family with BAP1 TPDS and malignant mesothelioma in which no meningioma cases were described [22]. The relatively low Ki‐67 index and the absence of additional high‐grade features in Case 3, combined with a stable post‐resection course, support a more favorable biological behavior despite the BAP1 somatic mutation.

This case shows that caution should be exercised when interpreting the findings of molecular testing and the need to interpret the entirety of the pathological and tumor genomic findings in order to assess risk of recurrence.

These cases demonstrate that BAP1 mutations can present with a wide histologic and clinical spectrum, ranging from indolent CNS WHO grade 1 tumors, to aggressive grade 3 meningiomas. Notably, Case 1 challenges the assumption that rhabdoid features or mitotic activity are prerequisites for aggressive behavior; rather, the presence of a somatic BAP1 truncating mutation alone appeared sufficient to drive recurrence and dissemination. Germline mutations like that in case 2 may behave differently from somatic ones, with only a weak association with meningiomas in the literature, and a variable clinical course, suggesting that screening protocols may need to be tailored to each family. Lastly, Case 3's favorable outcome serves as a warning that mutations with low variant allele frequencies in particular need to be interpreted alongside other histological features to better predict clinical course.
