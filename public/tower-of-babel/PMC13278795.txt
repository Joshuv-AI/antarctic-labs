Fulminant prosthetic valve endocarditis after transcatheter aortic valve implantation caused by non-typeable Streptococcus agalactiae with central nervous system involvement: a case report
Authors: Ryo Bando, Shusuke Yagi, Muneyuki Kadota, Takayuki Ise, Masataka Sata, Christoph Sinning, Deepti Ranganathan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInfective endocarditis (IE) after transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is an uncommon but life-threatening complication, often associated with diagnostic challenges. Initial transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) showed no vegetations. Introduction

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is an established treatment for severe aortic stenosis, particularly in older patients.1,2 Infective endocarditis (IE) after TAVI is uncommon but life-threatening and may be difficult to diagnose because early echocardiographic findings can be subtle.3–5 Streptococcus agalactiae (Group B Streptococcus; GBS) is increasingly recognized as a cause of invasive infection in older adults, with potential for rapid progression and high mortality.6–8 We report a fatal case of fulminant non-typeable GBS infection complicated by prosthetic valve endocarditis after TAVI, with suspected central nervous system involvement.

Case presentation

An 85-year-old woman was admitted with acute-onset fever. She had a history of breast and gastric cancer treated curatively and had undergone TAVI with a self-expanding valve 5 years earlier, followed by pacemaker implantation for complete atrioventricular block. She had no other major comorbidities and was independent in activities of daily living. On the day before admission, she was found partially off her bed and subsequently developed fever and lethargy. She was initially evaluated at a referring hospital, where laboratory testing showed marked inflammation, but whole-body computed tomography (CT) showed no clear focus of infection to explain the fever. Given her history of TAVI, IE was suspected despite the absence of vegetations on initial transthoracic echocardiography (TTE), and she was transferred to our institution. On arrival, she was drowsy but arousable and able to follow simple commands (Glasgow Coma Scale [GCS]: E3V5M6). Her vital signs were as follows: temperature 40.1°C; blood pressure 153/88 mmHg; heart rate 102 beats/min; respiratory rate 16 breaths/min; and oxygen saturation 94% on room air. She reported only fever and generalized fatigue, without focal symptoms suggestive of a localized infectious source. Physical examination revealed no apparent focus of infection, no cardiac murmurs, no findings suggestive of skin or soft tissue infection, and no peripheral stigmata of IE. Laboratory investigations showed leukocytosis (9300/µL) and an elevated C-reactive protein level (17.8 mg/dL). Serum creatine kinase was elevated, consistent with rhabdomyolysis secondary to prolonged immobilization. Electrocardiography showed sinus tachycardia. TTE showed no vegetations or deterioration of prosthetic valve function. The patient was admitted for further evaluation and treatment. After two sets of blood cultures (each set consisting of aerobic and anaerobic bottles) were obtained, empiric intravenous ceftriaxone (2 g/day) was initiated. On hospital Day 1, her fever resolved to the 36°C range, and her level of consciousness improved (E4V5M6). Both sets of blood cultures yielded Streptococcus agalactiae, confirming true bacteraemia (Figure 1). Streptococcal grouping was performed using the Lancefield method with a commercially available latex agglutination kit (Iwaki Prolex, Japan), confirming group B Streptococcus. Serotyping was subsequently performed using a GBS latex agglutination test (SSI Diagnostica, Denmark), which detects 10 capsular serotypes (Ia, Ib, II–IX). However, no agglutination was observed with the available antisera, and the isolate was therefore classified as non-typeable. On hospital Day 3, she acutely developed fever, delirium, and neck stiffness. A neurological consultation was obtained, and physical examination findings were suggestive of meningitis. Lumbar puncture was unsuccessful due to severe kyphosis; therefore, meningitis was diagnosed on clinical grounds. Head CT revealed a new left parietal intracranial haemorrhage (Figure 2), raising suspicion for rupture of an infectious intracranial aneurysm, although CT angiography showed no definite aneurysmal lesion (Figure 3). Repeat TTE demonstrated newly developed mobile echogenic masses on the prosthetic aortic valve and the anterior mitral leaflet (12 mm), accompanied by worsening mitral regurgitation (Figure 4; Supplementary material online, Video S1–S3). Although the portal of entry remained unclear, a diagnosis of prosthetic valve IE caused by Streptococcus agalactiae, complicated by clinically suspected meningitis and possible rupture of an infectious intracranial aneurysm, was made. Brain magnetic resonance imaging and transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) could not be performed because of the patient’s clinical condition. After discussion by the endocarditis team, surgical intervention was deemed unfeasible due to advanced age, an extremely high predicted operative mortality (52.5%) estimated by the Society of Thoracic Surgeons risk score, and persistent neurological impairment. Antimicrobial therapy was intensified to ceftriaxone 2 g every 12 h, in combination with ampicillin 2 g every 6 h. Despite treatment escalation, her condition rapidly deteriorated, with shock, acute kidney injury, and hepatic dysfunction. She died on hospital Day 7.

Discussion

This case highlights key challenges in managing IE following TAVI, particularly when caused by Streptococcus agalactiae in an elderly patient. Although post-TAVI IE is uncommon, it is a recognized high-risk condition characterized by diagnostic difficulty, frequent extracardiac complications, and poor prognosis.3–5

This case is notable for the combination of fulminant non-typeable Streptococcus agalactiae infection, multivalvular involvement after TAVI, and suspected central nervous system complications. Streptococcus agalactiae is a rare cause of IE in adults but has emerged as an invasive pathogen in older populations, with increasing incidence and high mortality.6–8

In this case, the isolate was non-typeable, likely due to alterations in capsular polysaccharide expression.9 Reduced capsule expression may enhance bacterial adhesion and invasion by exposing surface virulence factors, potentially contributing to the fulminant disease course observed.9

Although streptococcal toxic shock syndrome is typically associated with group A streptococcus, S. agalactiae can produce a similar phenotype.10 The patient’s rapid deterioration—from IE to neurological complications, acute kidney injury, and shock—suggests a toxic shock–like presentation. This rapid progression highlights the aggressive nature of invasive GBS infection in elderly individuals and possible central nervous system involvement.

Another important feature was multivalvular involvement, with vegetations on both the prosthetic aortic valve and anterior mitral leaflet. Multivalvular IE, reported in 10–20% of cases, is associated with worse outcomes.3 Two mechanisms may explain this finding. First, invasive GBS infection often leads to high-grade bacteraemia, increasing the likelihood of haematogenous seeding across multiple intracardiac sites. Second, anatomical and device-related factors in TAVI may facilitate infection spread. The proximity of the transcatheter aortic valve to the anterior mitral leaflet may allow contiguous extension or promote secondary involvement via altered flow dynamics and endothelial injury.4 The combination of highly invasive bacteraemia and structural characteristics of the transcatheter valve likely contributed to extensive valvular involvement and rapid clinical decline. This case represents a clinically important presentation of fulminant non-typeable S. agalactiae infection with multivalvular IE and suspected central nervous system invasion. Several clinical lessons emerge. First, clinicians should maintain a high suspicion for IE in post-TAVI patients with unexplained bacteraemia, even when initial echocardiography is inconclusive. Second, S. agalactiae should not be considered benign in elderly patients, as it can cause rapidly progressive and invasive disease. Third, neurological deterioration during IE warrants prompt evaluation for intracranial haemorrhage, infectious aneurysms, and meningitis. Finally, outcomes of TAVI-associated IE remain poor despite guideline-based management, highlighting the need for further case accumulation to better define optimal strategies in this setting.
