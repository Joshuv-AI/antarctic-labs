ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveEvaluate clinical and laboratory correlates of abnormal neurologic exam findings after acute HIV infection (AHI).MethodsParticipants from the RV254/SEARCH 010 cohort in Bangkok underwent standardized neurologic examinations at Weeks 0 (AHI), 12, 96, and 288 following antiretroviral therapy (ART). A subset of participants completed 3T neuroimaging. Associations between neuroimaging, clinical and HIV parameters, and abnormal neurologic exam were analyzed using chi‐square tests and multinomial regression.ResultsAt AHI, 703 participants (median age 26.5 years, 97.6% male) had neurologic exams, with follow‐up data available for 493, 667, and 560 participants at Weeks 12, 96, and 288, respectively. Viral suppression (≤ 50 copies/mL) was 0% at Week 0, 65% at Week 12, 98% at Week 96, and 99% at Week 288. Abnormalities in any neurologic exam domain were observed in 26%, 28%, 17%, and 8% of participants at each time point. Most findings occurred in sensory and fine motor domains. During AHI, history of HCV co‐infection associated with abnormal exam (p = 0.006). At Weeks 96 and 288, higher CD4:CD8 ratio correlated with abnormal exam at the same visit (p = 0.040 Week 96, p = 0.018 Week 288). In a longitudinal model, higher Fiebig stage associated with conversion from abnormal to normal neurologic exam (p < 0.035). Of participants with MRIs, 14% at Week 0 and 36% at Week 96 demonstrated abnormalities; abnormal MRI at Week 0 associated with abnormal fine motor performance (p = 0.045).ConclusionsObjective neurologic abnormalities are common during AHI, though few persist with long‐term ART. Coinfections, immunologic state, and demographic factors modify these outcomes.

Introduction

Neurologic complications including cognitive impairment, stroke, neuropathy, and others, have been well described in chronic HIV infection [1]. However, HIV can cross into the central nervous system (CNS) as early as days after transmission, during the time period known as acute HIV infection (AHI) [2]. Due to the rarity of diagnosing HIV at this very early stage, little is known about clinical neurologic symptoms during AHI or early HIV infection, how neurologic signs and symptoms might relate to systemic HIV disease activity, and whether initiating ART very early post HIV acquisition may mitigate longer term neurologic sequelae. One study of 22 individuals with early HIV infection (estimated < 6 months of infection) reported 50% with neurological manifestations and identified a correlation between higher cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) HIV RNA and neurological symptoms [3]. Another study compared individuals with early HIV infection (again < 6 months) presenting with neurologic symptoms, primarily meningitis and meningoencephalitis, to those with primary infection but without neurologic symptoms, and identified lower CD4:CD8 T‐cell count ratio and higher viral load as associating with the presence of neurologic symptoms [4].

Almost no studies to date have evaluated objective, standardized neurologic exam findings during early or acute HIV infection, and in the weeks following antiretroviral therapy (ART) initiation. One study evaluated 139 participants in the RV254 AHI cohort in Bangkok, Thailand (median duration of HIV infection 19 days) and identified one or more objective neurologic exam findings in 53% of participants in the first 12 weeks after diagnosis, with 9% persisting at 24 weeks on ART. The most common abnormal neurologic exam findings included sensory loss and reduced fine motor movements. Authors also identified that higher plasma HIV RNA at AHI associated with abnormal neurologic exam [5]. However, little is known about the long‐term persistence or evolution of neurologic signs and symptoms following immediate ART initiation during AHI. In this study, we sought to expand on this previous work [5], further evaluating neurologic abnormalities in a larger population of participants in the RV254 acute HIV cohort, and extending follow‐up to 288 weeks (6 years) after AHI diagnosis and ART initiation. This study focuses on objective neurologic exam abnormalities, rather than self‐reported neurologic or mood symptoms, as both have been reported in detail prior [6, 7]. Additionally, we aimed to evaluate clinical, laboratory, and MRI neuroimaging parameters, assessed both at baseline and subsequent follow‐up visits, that correlate with abnormal neurologic exam findings over long‐term follow‐up in people diagnosed and treated during AHI.

Methods

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents

This study was approved by the international review boards of Chulalongkorn University in Bangkok, Thailand, and the Walter Reed Army Institute of Research in the United States. All study participants provided written informed consent in Thai.

Participants

Participants were included from the RV254/SEARCH 010 cohort in Bangkok, Thailand (NCT00796146 and NCT00796263), which is an ongoing prospective study identifying and enrolling individuals during AHI (Fiebig Stage I–V). In this study, AHI was defined as HIV fourth‐generation enzyme immunoassay (EIA) negative + nucleic acid testing positive (Fiebig stage I/II) or HIV fourth‐generation EIA positive + second‐generation EIA negative (Fiebig stage III/IV) [8]. Inclusion criteria for the longitudinal study include confirmation of AHI and age greater than or equal to 18 years. Exclusion criteria were any preexisting diagnosed neurologic disorder or psychiatric disorder. Participants included in this work were enrolled between 2009 and 2025. Participants underwent physical examinations, laboratory blood testing (including HIV viral load and CD4 and CD8 T‐cell counts), coinfection screening (e.g., hepatitis C (HCV), hepatitis B (HBV), and syphilis), and self‐reported substance use screening at pre‐ART baseline and at least every 6 months during follow‐up. History of HCV infection was determined by anti‐HCV antibody positivity, with active HCV infection determined by follow‐up HCV RNA measurement. History of HBV infection was determined by HBV Core and Surface antibody measurements, with active HBV determined by HBV RNA measurement. History of syphilis was determined by combined treponemal specific tests and Venereal Disease Research Laboratory (VDRL). Estimated HIV infection duration was determined by detailed interview and self‐report of recent exposure history in combination with Fiebig stage.

RV254 participants typically initiated ART within 3 days of AHI diagnosis and study enrollment. Efavirenz (EFV)‐based ART was initially used as empirical treatment between 2009 and 2017, while dolutegravir (DTG)‐based ART was introduced as empirical treatment after 2017 as it became available. Existing participants who were not on a DTG‐based regimen were switched to DTG‐based in 2017 during follow‐up. Those with evidence of HBV infection received tenofovir (TDF) and lamivudine (3TC) as their nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTI) backbone. A subset of participants was randomized to receive either 24 or 96 weeks of maraviroc intensification during ART initiation at AHI. Additionally, a subset of 81 participants participated in analytical treatment interruption (ATI) as part of other ongoing protocols within the RV254 cohort during the 288 weeks of follow‐up included in this study.

Standardized Neurologic Exam

Standardized neurologic interview and examinations as described previously [5] were performed at study entry (AHI diagnosis), and serially after diagnosis at Week 12, 96, and Week 288. Neurologic exams were performed by trained research physicians (Neurologists or Infectious Disease physicians) and using standardized neurologic examination tools including reflex hammer, sharp pin, and tuning fork. Formal neurologic exam training was performed with each research physician by trained Neurologists, and a standardized neurologic examination form was used to ensure concordance in neurologic exam. Neurologic exam domains included cranial nerve function, strength, fine motor movements (Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale [UPDRS]), sensation, reflexes, and coordination. Sensory abnormality was defined as a deficit in light touch, vibration, or proprioception in the lower extremities. Fine motor abnormality was defined as asymmetry or slowness beyond expected variance for age in the assessments in the UPDRS (which includes evaluation of tremor, tone, finger tapping, hand movements, rapid alternating movements, leg tap, and gait).

Clinical Neuroimaging

All enrolled participants were offered optional magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) brain as part of the RV254 protocol at enrollment (Week 0) and Week 96. MRI brain was performed on a 1.5T (before 2015) or 3T Philips Ingenia scanner with a 15‐channel head coil at the Radiology Department of the King Chulalongkorn Memorial Hospital in Bangkok, Thailand. Multimodal acquisition included high‐resolution T1‐weighted turbo field‐echo (T1W 3D TFE) sequence, fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery turbo spin‐echo (FLAIR TSE) sequence, and diffusion weighted imaging (DWI) sequence. All MRIs were reviewed by a radiologist at Chulalongkorn Hospital who was blinded to any clinical information and standardized result reports were generated and reviewed by a single trained neurologist (K.B.H, who was also blinded to participant information) to abrogate variability. Abnormal MRI categories were defined as white matter hyperintensities, ischemic stroke, gliosis, or other abnormality (e.g., deep venous malformations, empty sella, and arachnoid cysts) identified as incidental in nature. No malignancies were identified.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics included medians and 1st (Q1) and 3rd quartiles (Q3) for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Frequencies and percentages of abnormal results were tabulated by study visit for each neurologic exam domain. Overall results for the full neurological exam were tabulated by week and the following predictors: demographics (age and sex at birth); Fiebig stage; labs (CD4, CD8, CD4/CD8, and viral load) at enrollment; comorbidities (hepatitis C and syphilis); illegal drug use at enrollment; and MVC use at any time point. Distributions of predictor variables were compared with exam results for each week. Wilcoxon's rank‐sum test was used for continuous variables. For categorical variables, the chi‐square test of independence was used if all expected cell counts were ≥ 5; Fisher's exact test was used otherwise. The false discovery rate method was used to correct for multiple comparisons.

Patterns of missingness were examined to determine whether missing neurological examinations were missing at random. For the full neurologic examination, results at the initial visit were compared with missingness of examination at each subsequent visit: The neurologic exam result at Week 0 was compared with the missingness at Weeks 12, 96, and 288. These were evaluated with the chi‐square test of independence or Fisher's exact test as described above.

Changes in the full neurological exam, neuropathy, and Parkinson's were examined in multivariable models. Change in neurological examinations was defined by the results at Weeks 0, 96, and/or 288. If Week 0 is missing or if Weeks 96 and 288 are both missing, then the participant was removed from the analysis. Otherwise, the initial result was taken from Week 0. The final result was taken from Weeks 96 and/or 288. If either Week 96 or Week 288 had abnormal results, then the final result was abnormal; if either Week 96 or Week 288 had normal results and the other was either normal or missing, then the final result was normal. Thus, four categories were defined: (1) “Normal to Normal”, (2) “Normal to Abnormal”, (3) “Abnormal to Normal”, and (4) “Abnormal to Abnormal”. Analyses were conducted using multinomial regression with a baseline category of “Normal to Normal”. Multiple imputation with 100 imputations was used for predictor variables, and results were compiled using Rubin's rule. Odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were presented.

MRI brain reports were reviewed and summarized using descriptive statistics, and associations between MRI and neurologic exam abnormalities were evaluated with the chi‐square test of independence or Fisher's exact test as described above. All analyses were conducted using SAS Studio 9.4.

Results

Participants

A total of 703 participants had baseline neurologic exam data; 97.72% were male, with a median age of 26.8 years. There were 39.73% in Fiebig stage I–II, and 63.27% in stage III–V.

Follow‐up complete neurologic exam data were available for 493 participants at Week 12, 667 at Week 96, and 560 at Week 288. Plasma HIV suppression (HIV RNA < 50 copies/mL) rates increased from 0% at Week 0 to 54.97% at Week 12, 92.81% at Week 96, and 98.57% at Week 288. At enrollment, one participant was identified as having active HBV infection, and four participants as having active HCV infection. Three of the four participants with active HCV received treatment with Sofosbuvir + Velpatasvir or Sofosbuvir + Ravidasvir, with follow‐up VL undetectable. One participant with positive HCV VL at enrollment did not have follow‐up HCV VL levels available.

Prevalence of Abnormal Neurologic Findings

Abnormal neurologic exam findings were observed in 25.6%, 27.59%, 17.22%, and 7.86% of participants at Weeks 0, 12, 96, and 288, respectively. Most abnormalities were seen in sensory (9.00%, 8.11%, 5.40%, and 4.11%) and UPDRS (12.41%, 12.78%, 8.55%, and 3.22%) domains. Reflex, coordination, and strength abnormalities were infrequent (Table 1, Figure 1). There was no significant difference in the prevalence of abnormal neurologic exam findings at any visit when comparing participants who had participated in an ATI during the course of follow‐up to those who had not.

Cross‐Sectional Associations

During AHI (Week 0), history of HCV coinfection was significantly associated with abnormal neurologic exam overall (p = 0.005). No other HIV parameters, coinfections, or self‐reported drug use associated with abnormal neurologic exam overall during AHI (Table 2). There were no significant associations with any parameter and abnormal overall neurologic exam at Week 12. At Week 96, abnormal neurologic exam associated with higher CD4 : CD8 T‐cell ratio at that same visit (p = 0.040), as well as lower CD8 T‐cell count at that time point (p = 0.015). At Week 288, female sex (p < 0.001), higher CD4 : CD8 T‐cell count at that same visit (p = 0.018), and randomization to maraviroc intensification (p < 0.001) associated with abnormal neurologic exam (Table 2).

Multivariable Models on Changes in Neurological Exam

Multivariable analyses were performed to examine predictors of change in full neurologic exam results across weeks. “Normal to Normal” was the baseline category. Female sex (OR [95% CI] 6.51 [1.64, 25.83], p = 0.008) was associated with “Normal to Abnormal” results. Having Fiebig stage III–V (vs. I–II, 1.73 [1.04, 2.89], p = 0.035) was associated with having “Abnormal to Normal” results. Higher age in years at enrollment (1.08 [1.04, 1.11], p < 0.001) was associated with “Abnormal to Abnormal” results (Table 3). Due to small cell sizes, models for neuropathy exam and UPDRS exam failed to converge.

Participants with normal (vs abnormal) exam findings at Week 0 were more likely to missing exam results at Week 12 (41.11% vs. 25.00%, p < 0.001), Week 96 (12.05% vs. 8.89%, p = 0.247), and Week 288 (31.55% vs. 11.67%, p < 0.001). Therefore, the pattern of missingness was biased such that those with normal exam results were less likely to have subsequent exams, so the percentage of participants in the “Normal to Abnormal” category may be an underestimate.

Neuroimaging

Of participants with MRI data, 13.55% had abnormalities at Week 0 and 21.12% at Week 96. The most common findings remained similar at both Week 0 and Week 96 and were white matter hyperintensities and mild cortical atrophy that was out of proportion to age (Table 4). An abnormal MRI at Week 0 was associated with abnormal UPDRS at Week 0 (p = 0.045), but no other associations between abnormal MRI findings and neurologic exam were identified at Week 96 (Table 5).

Discussion

This work expands upon a prior study [5] that reported abnormalities in an objective, standardized neurologic exam assessment during AHI and after ART initiation. In this large cohort of over 700 individuals, neurologic exam abnormalities were identified in more than one fourth of participants during AHI, with significant improvement over long‐term follow‐up at 96 and 288 weeks, mirroring patterns in viral suppression. This finding further emphasizes the importance of early ART initiation and adherence in PLWH. Unlike prior work in a smaller subset of this cohort, we did not identify a decrease in overall neurologic exam abnormalities at short‐term (12 week) follow‐up. This finding largely appears to be driven by a lower percentage of abnormalities during AHI in this study, which was 26%, compared to 49% in the original work. This may be explained by the inclusion of self‐reported cognitive symptoms including concentration and memory in the initial work [5], which comprised the greatest proportion of abnormalities reported during AHI and follow‐up visits through Week 24. We elected to exclude self‐reported cognitive symptoms in this work to focus on abnormalities in objective neurologic examination findings that can be utilized in clinical practice, and also because detailed and more nuanced analysis of cognitive symptoms and cognitive testing during AHI in the RV254 cohort has since been published [7, 9].

However, we cannot exclude that ongoing neurologic symptoms at Week 12 could also in part represent symptoms from the ongoing lack of viral suppression seen in 45% of participants at this time point, compared to > 98% at Weeks 96 and 288. Given the slight increase in neurologic exam abnormalities at Week 12 compared to Week 0, it is possible this could be related to worsening symptoms in the setting of persistent viremia, though causality cannot be proven. This theory is further supported by an association of higher Fiebig stage III–V versus I–II at enrollment with converting from abnormal to normal neurologic exam at later visits, perhaps indicating improvement in symptoms with ART. In contrast, older age was associated with persistently abnormal neurologic exam findings in longitudinal analysis, indicating there are likely additional underlying factors in some participants that are not directly related to HIV infection itself.

Our results confirm that neurologic involvement occurs even in the earliest stages of HIV infection and tends to affect most commonly sensory and fine motor domains on neurologic exam, largely sparing cranial nerves, gross motor, and reflex examination, which is consistent with prior work [5]. The pathophysiologic basis of these findings is not fully understood. It is possible that some of these findings may be related to early CNS viral invasion of HIV and associated immune activation, which is supported by the identified association of abnormal neurologic exam and higher HIV VL [2]. While the predominance of fine motor and sensory abnormalities seen here could suggest sensitivity of basal ganglia and/or thalamo‐cortical pathways to HIV infection, associated gross abnormalities were not seen on clinical MRI brain scans. However, prior detailed MRI analysis from this cohort has shown dynamic changes in the caudate, pallidum, and putamen during acute infection, which associated with Fiebig stage and which could correlate with the clinical fine motor abnormalities identified [10]. Additionally, multiple studies conducted before and after the era of suppressive ART identified significant associations between cognitive impairment and basal ganglia abnormalities on neuroimaging modalities including MRI and MR spectroscopy among individuals with chronic HIV [11, 12]. In regards to the sensory abnormalities identified, it is possible that these could also be driven at least in part by a process affecting peripheral nerves, rather than the CNS, most likely from a systemic inflammatory state as has been demonstrated both in AHI [13] and in chronic HIV infection [14, 15].

MRI abnormalities were observed in up to one‐quarter of participants, with the most common finding being nonspecific white matter hyperintensities (WMH), similar to prior work [5]. WMH are a common finding in asymptomatic individuals both with and without HIV and did not correlate with the focal neurologic exam findings noted in this work. Taken together, the majority of abnormal MRI findings were present at enrollment, with only a minority developing at the 2‐year time point of repeat MRI, indicating that most abnormalities were present prior to HIV infection. The increase in MRI abnormalities over time and corresponding decrease in neurologic symptoms from Week 0 to Week 96 further indicates that this is less likely the driving factor behind the clinical neurologic exam abnormalities reported in this work. However, the percent of individuals with nonspecific WMH increased by approximately 50% (from 11% to 17% of participants) from Week 0 to Week 96. Of note, other work from this group recently quantified and tracked WMH development in a subset of participants in the same RV254 AHI cohort and identified more severe accumulation of WMH over 2 years in AHI participants compared to demographically similar people without HIV. However, the clinical relevance of these findings remains to be determined. The focus of this work, in contrast, was on characterizing abnormalities that would be identifiable for clinicians and radiologists in practice.

In cross‐sectional analysis we observed associations between HCV coinfection and abnormal neurologic exam during AHI. However, only four participants had evidence of active HCV at Week 0, indicating this finding is likely driven by prior rather than ongoing HCV infection. HCV has been linked to peripheral neuropathy, both from viral infection itself as well as through secondary microvasculitic processes [16]. HCV coinfection has also been linked to other neurologic symptoms, such as cognitive impairment [17], and work from 79 participants in this RV254 AHI cohort with HCV coinfection reported improvement in cognitive test scores after treatment for HCV [18]. While it is possible a history of HCV infection augments the neurologic symptoms observed during AHI, no other associations were observed in univariate or longitudinal analysis, limiting broad conclusions.

When evaluating HIV parameters, a higher CD4 : CD8 T‐cell ratio and lower CD8 T‐cell count were associated with an abnormal neurologic exam at later time points (Weeks 96 and 288) in univariate analysis. While a greater volume of prior work has identified associations between persistent CD8 T‐cell elevation and expansion with medical comorbidities in PLWH [19, 20], some large‐scale studies have also identified an association between persistently low CD8 T‐cell count and increased mortality [21]. Additionally, in prior work from our group, we identified higher CD4 T‐cell count and lower CD8 T‐cell count at 288 weeks (6 years) of follow‐up in this AHI cohort when compared to a Thai cohort of people with chronic HIV [22]. Thus, it is possible that the cellular dynamics underlying T‐cell evolution and reconstitution after ART may differ in this cohort of people diagnosed and treated during AHI, a unique group which has not been widely studied globally due to the rarity of diagnosis at this time point. However, no association was seen across longitudinal evaluation of change in CD4 T‐cell, CD8 T‐cell, or CD4 : CD8 ratio and longitudinal changes in neurologic exam over time, so further work is needed to explore this hypothesis.

While female sex was associated with abnormal UPDRS at Weeks 12 and 288, as well as a change in normal to neurologic exam over time in longitudinal analysis, the number of female participants in this cohort was very low (only 12 total female participants at Week 288), with 4 having abnormal findings on neurologic exam. Thus, the significance of this finding remains unclear and requires additional study in more diverse cohorts before conclusions can be drawn. Additionally, while an association of abnormal UPDRS and Maraviroc intensification was observed in univariable analysis at Week 288, the number of participants with abnormal neurologic exam at this time point was low. Furthermore, none of the participants in this cohort continued Maraviroc past Week 96 due to protocol changes, and thus we suspect this is unlikely to be a contributing factor to neurologic exam at Week 288 and is more likely related to sampling bias at this time point. Pathophysiologically, this is also in contrast to some preclinical work in rat models which identified that Maraviroc may actually reduce symptoms of chronic nerve pain [23].

Taken as a whole, our findings indicate that there is an interplay of both modifiable (e.g., early ART and treatment of coinfections) along with non‐modifiable (e.g., age) risk factors for objective abnormal neurologic exam findings in people diagnosed and treated during AHI. Our data also indicate that neurologic exam findings associated with HIV infection may not be wholly mitigated by ART alone over years of follow‐up, further emphasizing the need for comprehensive neurologic care in this population. Importantly, we observed no difference in neurologic exam findings between individuals in the cohort who participated in ATI compared to those who did not. While the gross neurologic exam would not be sensitive for small changes in CNS HIV viral reservoir dynamics, this is at least reassuring against significant neurologic harm during ATI, supporting ongoing clinical trials to evaluate ATI and HIV cure.

Limitations of this work include homogeneity of participants in ethnicity and gender, given the geographic location of the RV254 cohort, which could limit generalizability of results. Additionally, complete data on diagnosis and treatment of coinfections (HCV, HBV, and syphilis) over time was not available and was also incompletely captured dynamically over time in this work. This study also lacks detailed MRI brain structural analysis and analysis of blood and CSF biomarkers, which we hope to further evaluate in future work to expand upon the clinical findings reported here. However, the unique ability to study AHI, the large sample size, standardized exam protocol, and long‐term follow‐up strengthen this study's methods and conclusions.

Conclusion

Objective abnormalities in neurologic examination are common during acute HIV infection, particularly affecting sensory and fine motor domains. Many of these findings resolve with sustained ART, underscoring the importance of early and continued treatment. History of HCV coinfection is associated with abnormal neurologic exam during AHI, supporting treatment of coinfections both prior to and after HIV diagnosis. Ongoing evaluation of immune correlates such as T‐cell subset and CNS biomarkers is warranted to elucidate mechanisms of residual neurologic dysfunction in PLWH.
