We now turn to our main argument. Our goal is to pick out vertices

Note that the Kővári–Sós–Turán theorem has a logarithmic dependence on both the sizes of the sets and on the edge density, which means that we are allowed to lose a great deal when passing from the pair

\frac{\log\sqrt{n}}{\log\frac{1}{p^{2}}}=\frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{\log n}{\log\frac{1}{p}},

In order to ensure we obtain these bounds on

number of triangles containing an edge in
E(\widehat{A},\widehat{B})
, but by deleting the edges lying in too few triangles, we can correct this issue. By doing this and then running the argument above, we end up finding a triangle blowup
K_{3}[\widehat{s}]
, where

\widehat{s}=\Omega\left(\frac{\widehat{\tau}\log n}{\log\frac{1}{\gamma}}\right).

Let us pick some parameter
\mu\gg\tau
, which we will optimize later, and declare that we will do such a switch if ever
\widehat{\tau}>\mu
. In particular, if this ever happens, then we find a triangle blowup of size
\widehat{s}=\Omega(\mu\log n/{\log\frac{1}{\gamma}})
.

We may thus assume that we never perform such a switch, and hence that at every stage of the process we have
\widehat{\tau}\leqslant\mu
, or equivalently
d(A^{(\ell)},C)\geqslant\gamma/\mu
. By the same argument but with the roles of
A^{(\ell)}
and
B^{(\ell)}
interchanged, we may also assume that
d(B^{(\ell)},C)\geqslant\gamma/\mu
. This implies that the average degree of a vertex
c^{*}\in C
in
A^{(\ell)}
is at least
(\gamma/\mu)\lvert A^{(\ell)}\rvert
, and similarly for the degree into
B^{(\ell)}
. By another round of removing vertices whose degree is much lower than the average, this means that in the argument presented above, we may assume that
\lvert A^{+}\rvert\geqslant(\gamma/\mu)\lvert A^{(\ell)}\rvert,\lvert B^{+}\rvert\geqslant(\gamma/\mu)\lvert B^{(\ell)}\rvert
.

The reason this is useful to us is that in (
1
), the amount we gain on the density of the pair
(X,Y)
is proportional to
\lvert A^{+}\rvert\lvert B^{+}\rvert
, and hence we are gaining more here. This, in turn, means that we can find
\mathcal{E}_{R}(X,Y)>\mathcal{E}_{R}(A^{(\ell)},B^{(\ell)})
under a more modest assumption on
R
; it now suffices that
R\gtrsim\mu/(\alpha\tau)
,
where the notation
\gtrsim
hides logarithmic factors. Recall that earlier we needed
R\geqslant 10p/(\alpha\tau)
, and hence we now obtain a milder requirement, so long as
\mu\ll p
.

It now remains to do the optimization; we again require
\alpha\leqslant p/(2s)
, so we conclude that we can continue the process up to step

s\leqslant\frac{p}{2\alpha}\lesssim\frac{p\tau R}{\mu}=\widetilde{\Theta}\left(\frac{\gamma}{\mu}\log n\right),

where we hide factors of
\log\frac{1}{\gamma}
in the
\widetilde{\Theta}
notation, and recall that we choose
R=\widetilde{\Theta}(\log n)
.
In order to have the strongest possible bound, we should choose
\mu
so that
s
and
\widehat{s}
are of roughly the same order, meaning that we should take
\gamma/\mu=\mu
, or equivalently
\mu=\sqrt{\gamma}
. By doing so, we conclude that
c_{K_{3}}(\gamma)\geqslant\gamma^{1/2+o(1)}
.

Note that this argument is useful so long as
\tau\ll\mu\ll p
; we also know that
p\tau=\Omega(\gamma)
. So we can only win if
\tau\ll p
, or equivalently if
p\gg\sqrt{\gamma}
. In the regime where
p\asymp\tau\asymp\sqrt{\gamma}
, this switching argument wins us nothing. This is precisely why, in the introduction, we said that our technique does not seem able to cope with a random tripartite graph of edge densities
\frac{1}{2},\sqrt{\gamma},\sqrt{\gamma}
; in such a graph, no matter which of the three parts we select as
C
, we will have
p\geqslant\sqrt{\gamma}
.

To conclude, we briefly explain how we obtain the same result for
c_{K_{k+1}}(\gamma)
, using essentially the same argument. So let
G
be an
N
-vertex graph with
\gamma N^{k+1}
copies of
K_{k+1}
. First, by applying the same regularity technique, we can pass to a collection of disjoint sets
A_{1},\dots,A_{k},C\subseteq V(G)
, each of size
n=\Omega_{\gamma,k}(N)
, with the property that there are at least
p\lvert A_{1}\rvert\dotsb\lvert A_{k}\rvert
copies of
K_{k}
among
(A_{1},\dots,A_{k})
, each of which extends to at least
\tau\lvert C\rvert
copies of
K_{k+1}
with a vertex in
C
, where
\tau\geqslant\gamma^{2/(k+1)}
and
p\tau\geqslant\gamma
. We now run exactly the same iterative argument, selecting vertices from
C
one by one and restricting to their common neighborhoods in
A_{1},\dots,A_{k}
. The analysis of this algorithm is nearly identical to the one above, and the upshot of it is that we can pass to some
C^{\dagger}\subseteq C,A_{i}^{\dagger}\subseteq A_{i}
, with the following properties: we have that
\lvert A_{i}^{\dagger}\rvert=\sqrt{n}
for all
i
, that
\lvert C^{\dagger}\rvert=s=\Theta(\tau\log n/{\log\frac{1}{p}})
, and that the density of
K_{k}
-copies among
A_{1}^{\dagger},\dots,A_{k}^{\dagger}
is at least
p/2
. We now note that the induced subgraph on
A_{1}^{\dagger}\cup\dots\cup A_{k}^{\dagger}
has
N^{\prime}=k\sqrt{n}=\Omega(\sqrt{N})
vertices and at least
\Omega(\gamma(N^{\prime})^{k})
copies of
K_{k}
, so we may proceed by induction on
k
, with the base case
k=2
being precisely the triangle case handled above. The key thing to note is that for
k\geqslant 3
, we have
k+1\geqslant 4
, and hence

s=\Omega\left(\frac{\gamma^{2/(k+1)}\log n}{\log\frac{1}{\gamma}}\right)\geqslant\Omega\left(\frac{\gamma^{1/2}\log n}{\log\frac{1}{\gamma}}\right),

meaning that the number of vertices we select from
C
is indeed enough.
In fact, the same argument yields the recursive bound

c_{K_{k+1}}(\gamma)\geqslant\min\{\gamma^{2/(k+1)+o(1)},c_{K_{k}}(\Omega_{k}(\gamma))\}.

3.
Preliminaries

We will use the Kővári–Sós–Turán theorem
[
21
]
in the following form. For a bipartite graph with parts
(A,B)
, we denote by
d(A,B)=e(A,B)/(\lvert A\rvert\lvert B\rvert)
its edge density.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
p\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
, let
G
be a bipartite graph with parts
(X,Y)
, and assume that
d(X,Y)\geqslant p
. Then
G
contains a copy of
K_{t,t}
, where

t=\left\lfloor\frac{\log(\min\{\lvert X\rvert,\lvert Y\rvert\})}{2\log\frac{1}{p}}\right\rfloor.

3.1.
Analytic inequalities

In this paper, all logarithms are to base
2
.

We need the following lemma, a simple analytic inequality.

Lemma 3.2
.

For all real numbers
y\geqslant 0,R\geqslant 1
, we have

y^{1/R}\leqslant 1+\frac{y}{R}.

Proof.

Recall Bernoulli’s inequality, which states that
(1+x)^{R}\geqslant 1+Rx
for all
R\geqslant 1
and
x\geqslant 0
. Setting
x=y/R
, we find that

\left(1+\frac{y}{R}\right)^{R}=(1+x)^{R}\geqslant 1+Rx=1+y\geqslant y,

which yields the claimed bound by taking
1/R
powers of both sides.
∎

The following is a variant of
Lemma

3.2
, giving us a better upper bound if we know that
y
is either very small or very large.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\eta\in(0,\frac{1}{4}]
and let
R\geqslant\log\frac{1}{\eta}
. For every
y\in[1,(1-\eta)^{-2}]\cup[\frac{1}{\eta},\infty)
, we have

y^{1/R}\leqslant 1+(\eta\log\tfrac{1}{\eta})\frac{y}{R}.

Proof.

First suppose that
y\in[1,(1-\eta)^{-2}]
. By applying Bernoulli’s inequality to
x=y(\eta\log\frac{1}{\eta})/R
, we find

\left(1+(\eta\log\tfrac{1}{\eta})\frac{y}{R}\right)^{R}\geqslant 1+(\eta\log\tfrac{1}{\eta})y\geqslant y\left((1-\eta)^{2}+\eta\log\tfrac{1}{\eta}\right),

where the second inequality uses our assumption
y\leqslant(1-\eta)^{-2}
. Now note that

(1-\eta)^{2}+\eta\log\tfrac{1}{\eta}=1+\eta(-2+\eta+\log\tfrac{1}{\eta})\geqslant 1+\eta(-2+2)=1,

using our assumptions that
\eta>0
and
\eta\leqslant\frac{1}{4}
, the latter of which implies
\log\frac{1}{\eta}\geqslant 2
. This completes the proof in the first case, upon taking the
1/R
power of both sides.

Now suppose that
y\geqslant 1/\eta
. Let
\theta=(\log\frac{1}{\eta})/R\leqslant 1
and
z=\eta y\geqslant 1
. We now have that

\displaystyle\left(1+(\eta\log\tfrac{1}{\eta})\frac{y}{R}\right)^{1/\theta}

\displaystyle=\left(1+\theta z\right)^{1/\theta}\geqslant 1+z\geqslant 2\sqrt{z}\geqslant 2z^{1/{\log\frac{1}{\eta}}}=\left(\frac{z}{\eta}\right)^{1/{\log\frac{1}{\eta}}}=y^{1/{\log\frac{1}{\eta}}},

where the first inequality is Bernoulli’s, the second is AM–GM, and the third uses that
z\geqslant 1
and
\log\frac{1}{\eta}\geqslant 2
. Taking the
\theta
power of both sides gives the claimed result.
∎

3.2.
Energy and strictly balanced subgraphs

For a
k
-partite
k
-graph
(A_{1},\dots,A_{k})
, we denote by
e(A_{1},\dots,A_{k})
its edge count and by
d(A_{1},\dots,A_{k})=e(A_{1},\dots,A_{k})/\prod_{i}\lvert A_{i}\rvert
its edge density. We will frequently use the notation
\mathcal{A}
as a shorthand for the tuple
(A_{1},\dots,A_{k})
, to minimize the number of symbols. Similarly, we will shorten tuples such as
(A_{1}^{\prime},\dots,A_{k}^{\prime})
or
(A_{1}^{*},\dots,A_{k}^{*})
as
\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\mathcal{A}^{*}
, respectively.

For a real number
R>1
, we also define the
R
-energy
of
\mathcal{A}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{k})
to be

\mathcal{E}_{R}(\mathcal{A})\coloneqq\lvert A_{1}\rvert\dotsb\lvert A_{k}\rvert d(\mathcal{A})^{R}=e(\mathcal{A})d(\mathcal{A})^{R-1},

with the convention that
\mathcal{E}_{R}(\mathcal{A})=0
if
\mathcal{A}
has no edges (and in particular if some
A_{i}
is empty).
Finally, we say that
\mathcal{A}
is
strictly
R
-balanced
if for all subsets
A^{\prime}_{i}\subseteq A_{i}
such that at least one inclusion is proper, we have

\mathcal{E}_{R}(\mathcal{A})>\mathcal{E}_{R}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime}).

We have the following extremely simple lemma.

Lemma 3.4
.

Every
k
-partite
k
-graph
\mathcal{A}
contains a strictly
R
-balanced subgraph
\mathcal{A}^{*}
with
\mathcal{E}_{R}(\mathcal{A}^{*})\geqslant\mathcal{E}_{R}(\mathcal{A})
.

Proof.

Let
A^{*}_{1}\subseteq A_{1},\dots,A_{k}^{*}\subseteq A_{k}
be subsets which maximize
\mathcal{E}_{R}(\mathcal{A}^{*})
among all options; if there are multiple choices achieving the same value, pick one which minimizes
\sum_{i}\lvert A_{i}^{*}\rvert
. As
\mathcal{A}
is one valid choice, we certainly have
\mathcal{E}_{R}(\mathcal{A}^{*})\geqslant\mathcal{E}_{R}(\mathcal{A})
. Moreover, any subsets
A_{i}^{\prime}\subseteq A_{i}^{*}
such that at least one inclusion is proper would be another valid choice with a strictly smaller value of
\sum_{i}\lvert A_{i}^{\prime}\rvert
, hence we conclude that
\mathcal{A}^{*}
is strictly
R
-balanced.
∎

We remark that this notion is similar to ones used in many other contexts in extremal graph theory, e.g. in finding a sublinear expander or in almost-regularizing a graph. In such cases, one picks an appropriate functional of a graph, which is usually some power of its edge count divided by its vertex count, and then passes to a subgraph which maximizes this quantity.

The main property we will need about strictly
R
-balanced hypergraphs is the following lemma, which gives an upper bound on the number of edges of any subgraph of a strictly balanced hypergraph. The proof is quite simple: if a subgraph had too many edges, its energy would be higher, contradicting the assumption of strict
R
-balancedness.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be strictly
R
-balanced, let
p=d(\mathcal{A})
, and let
X_{i}\subseteq A_{i}
. Let
\mathcal{X}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{k})
.

(a)

We have

e(\mathcal{X})\leqslant p\prod_{i}\lvert X_{i}\rvert+\frac{p}{R}\prod_{i}\lvert A_{i}\rvert.

(b)

Let
\eta\in(0,\frac{1}{4}]
and suppose that
R\geqslant\log\frac{1}{\eta}
. If, furthermore, we have that
\frac{\prod\lvert X_{i}\rvert}{\prod\lvert A_{i}\rvert}\in[0,\eta]\cup[(1-\eta)^{2},1]
, then we have the stronger bound

e(\mathcal{X})\leqslant p\prod_{i}\lvert X_{i}\rvert+(\eta\log\tfrac{1}{\eta})\frac{p}{R}\prod_{i}\lvert A_{i}\rvert.

Proof.

There is nothing to prove if
p=0
, if some
X_{i}
is empty, or if
X_{i}=A_{i}
for all
i
, hence we may assume that
p>0
and that at least one inclusion is proper. This implies that
\mathcal{E}_{R}(\mathcal{X})<\mathcal{E}_{R}(\mathcal{A})
. Unpacking the definition, this implies that

(2)

\left(\frac{d(\mathcal{X})}{d(\mathcal{A})}\right)^{R}<\frac{\prod_{i}\lvert A_{i}\rvert}{\prod_{i}\lvert X_{i}\rvert}.

Applying
Lemma

3.2
, we conclude that

\frac{d(\mathcal{X})}{p}<\left(\frac{\prod_{i}\lvert A_{i}\rvert}{\prod_{i}\lvert X_{i}\rvert}\right)^{1/R}\leqslant 1+\frac{\prod_{i}\lvert A_{i}\rvert}{R\prod_{i}\lvert X_{i}\rvert}

and therefore

e(\mathcal{X})=d(\mathcal{X})\prod_{i}\lvert X_{i}\rvert\leqslant\left(p+\frac{p\prod_{i}\lvert A_{i}\rvert}{R\prod_{i}\lvert X_{i}\rvert}\right)\prod_{i}\lvert X_{i}\rvert=p\prod_{i}\lvert X_{i}\rvert+\frac{p}{R}\prod_{i}\lvert A_{i}\rvert,

proving
3.5(a)
.

Now suppose we are in the setting of
3.5(b)
. There is nothing to prove if some
X_{i}
is empty, so we may assume
\prod_{i}\lvert X_{i}\rvert\neq 0
, and then define
y=(\prod_{i}\lvert A_{i}\rvert)/(\prod_{i}\lvert X_{i}\rvert)
. By assumption we have
1/y\in[0,\eta]\cup[(1-\eta)^{2},1]
, implying that either
y\geqslant 1/\eta
or
1\leqslant y\leqslant(1-\eta)^{-2}
. Hence we are in the setting of
Lemma

3.3
; applying this lemma to (
2
), we conclude that

\frac{d(\mathcal{X})}{p}\leqslant y^{1/R}\leqslant 1+(\eta\log\tfrac{1}{\eta})\frac{y}{R}=1+\frac{\eta\log\tfrac{1}{\eta}}{R}\cdot\frac{\prod_{i}\lvert A_{i}\rvert}{\prod_{i}\lvert X_{i}\rvert},

from which the result follows as above.
∎

4.
The basic iteration

We now introduce some additional terminology. A
k
-mixed graph

\mathcal{G}
is a (non-uniform) hypergraph with
k+1
parts, labeled
A_{1},\dots,A_{k},C
. Its edges consist of some
k
-partite
k
-graph on
\mathcal{A}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{k})
, as well as some
2
-uniform bipartite graph on
(C,A_{1}\cup\dots\cup A_{k})
.

We denote by
N_{1}(c),\dots,N_{k}(c)
the neighborhoods of a vertex
c\in C
in the sets
A_{1},\dots,A_{k}
, respectively. We say that a vertex
c\in C

extends
an edge
(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\in E(\mathcal{A})
if
c
is adjacent to all the vertices
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}
. We denote by
\ext(\mathcal{G})
the total number of extensions in
\mathcal{G}
, namely

(3)

\ext(\mathcal{G})=\sum_{(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\in E(\mathcal{A})}\sum_{c\in C}\mathbf{1}_{c\text{ extends }(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})}=\sum_{c\in C}e(N_{1}(c),\dots,N_{k}(c)).

We also denote by
\tau_{C}(\mathcal{G})
the
minimum extension density
of
\mathcal{G}
, defined as

\tau_{C}(\mathcal{G})=\min\left\{\frac{\lvert\{c\in C:c\text{ extends }(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\}\rvert}{\lvert C\rvert}:(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\in E(\mathcal{A})\right\},

with the convention that
\tau_{C}(\mathcal{G})=0
if
E(\mathcal{A})=\varnothing
.
That is, for each edge in
E(\mathcal{A})
, we compute what fraction of vertices in
C
extend this edge, and then
\tau_{C}(G)
is the minimum of these ratios.
Note that
\tau_{C}(\mathcal{G})\in[0,1]
for every
k
-mixed graph
\mathcal{G}
.
The following is the key lemma of our iterative procedure.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
k\geqslant 2
be an integer. Fix parameters
\tau\in(0,1]
and
R\geqslant 2^{k+1}/\tau
.
Let
\mathcal{G}
be a
k
-mixed graph with parts
\mathcal{A},C
, and suppose that
\tau_{C}(\mathcal{G})\geqslant\tau
and
\mathcal{A}
is strictly
R
-balanced.

Then there exist a vertex
c^{*}\in C
and non-empty subsets
A_{i}^{*}\subseteq A_{i}
satisfying the following properties.

(i)

c^{*}
is adjacent to all vertices in
A_{1}^{*}\cup\dots\cup A_{k}^{*}
.

(ii)

We have

\mathcal{E}_{R}(\mathcal{A}^{*})\geqslant 2^{-2^{k+2}/\tau}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{R}(\mathcal{A})

(iii)

\mathcal{A}^{*}
is strictly
R
-balanced.

Proof.

Let us set
p=d(\mathcal{A})
and
\alpha=2^{k}p/(R\tau)
, and note that
\alpha\leqslant p/2
since
R\tau\geqslant 2^{k+1}
. Note that

\ext(\mathcal{G})\geqslant\tau_{C}(\mathcal{G})e(\mathcal{A})\lvert C\rvert\geqslant\tau p\lvert A_{1}\rvert\dotsb\lvert A_{k}\rvert\lvert C\rvert,

and hence from (
3
) we have that there is some vertex
c^{*}\in C
for which

e(N_{1}(c^{*}),\dots,N_{k}(c^{*}))\geqslant\tau p\prod_{i}\lvert A_{i}\rvert.

We fix such a
c^{*}
, and let

A_{i}^{+}=N_{i}(c^{*})
and
A_{i}^{-}=A_{i}\setminus A_{i}^{+}
.

Our key claim is that

(4)

d(\mathcal{A}^{+})\geqslant p-\alpha.

Suppose for contradiction that
d(\mathcal{A}^{+})<p-\alpha\leqslant p
. From the above, we find that

p\tau\prod_{i}\lvert A_{i}\rvert\leqslant e(\mathcal{A}^{+})=d(\mathcal{A}^{+})\prod_{i}\lvert A_{i}^{+}\rvert<p\prod_{i}\lvert A_{i}^{+}\rvert,

implying that

(5)

\frac{1}{\tau}\prod_{i}\lvert A_{i}^{+}\rvert>\prod_{i}\lvert A_{i}\rvert.

Let
\sigma\in\{+,-\}^{k}
.
We now apply
3.5(a)
to
(X_{1},\dots,X_{k})=(A_{1}^{\sigma_{1}},\dots,A_{k}^{\sigma_{k}})\eqqcolon\mathcal{A}^{\sigma}
to find that

e(\mathcal{A}^{\sigma})\leqslant p\prod_{i}\lvert A_{i}^{\sigma_{i}}\rvert+\frac{p}{R}\prod_{i}\lvert A_{i}\rvert<p\prod_{i}\lvert A_{i}^{\sigma_{i}}\rvert+\frac{p}{R\tau}\prod_{i}\lvert A_{i}^{+}\rvert,

where the second inequality is from (
5
).
Moreover, in the case
\sigma=(+,\dots,+)
, we actually have the stronger bound

e(\mathcal{A}^{+})<(p-\alpha)\prod_{i}\lvert A_{i}^{+}\rvert=p\prod_{i}\lvert A_{i}^{+}\rvert-\alpha\prod_{i}\lvert A_{i}^{+}\rvert,

using our assumption that (
4
) is false. Summing these
2^{k}
inequalities, and using the fact that each edge in
\mathcal{A}
lies in precisely one tuple
\mathcal{A}^{\sigma}
, we find that

\displaystyle e(\mathcal{A})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in\{+,-\}^{k}}e(\mathcal{A}^{\sigma})

\displaystyle<\sum_{\sigma\in\{+,-\}^{k}}p\prod_{i}\lvert A_{i}^{\sigma_{i}}\rvert+\left((2^{k}-1)\frac{p}{R\tau}-\alpha\right)\prod_{i}\lvert A_{i}^{+}\rvert

\displaystyle<p\sum_{\sigma\in\{+,-\}^{k}}\prod_{i}\lvert A_{i}^{\sigma_{i}}\rvert=p\prod_{i}\lvert A_{i}\rvert,

using our choice of
\alpha
in the second inequality. This contradicts our assumption that
p=d(\mathcal{A})
, and concludes the proof of (
4
).

We now show how to use (
4
) to complete the proof of the lemma. To do this, we first compute that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{R}(\mathcal{A}^{+})

\displaystyle=e(\mathcal{A}^{+})d(\mathcal{A}^{+})^{R-1}

\displaystyle\geqslant\left(\tau p\prod_{i}\lvert A_{i}\rvert\right)(p-\alpha)^{R-1}

\displaystyle=\left(p^{R}\prod_{i}\lvert A_{i}\rvert\right)(\tau p^{1-R}(p-\alpha)^{R-1})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}_{R}(\mathcal{A})\left(\tau\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{p}\right)^{R-1}\right).

Recall that
\tau\leqslant 1
, hence
the inequality
\tau\geqslant 2^{-1/\tau}
holds. Additionally, since
\alpha/p\leqslant 1/2
, the inequality
1-\alpha/p\geqslant 2^{-2\alpha/p}
holds. Therefore,

\tau\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{p}\right)^{R-1}\geqslant\tau\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{p}\right)^{R}\geqslant 2^{-1/\tau}\cdot 2^{-2R\alpha/p}\geqslant 2^{-2^{k+2}/\tau}.

Finally, we pass to a strictly
R
-balanced subgraph
(A_{1}^{*},\dots,A_{k}^{*})
of
(A_{1}^{+},\dots,A_{k}^{+})
, as given to us by
Lemma

3.4
. This automatically gives us
(iii)
, as well as
(ii)
by the computation above. As
c^{*}
is adjacent to every vertex of
A_{i}^{+}\supseteq A_{i}^{*}
, we also have
(i)
.
∎

We remark that the proof of
Lemma

4.1
would work verbatim if we only knew that the
average
number of extensions of an edge in
E(\mathcal{A})
were at least
\tau\lvert C\rvert
, rather than the
minimum
. However, our next result is obtained by iterating
Lemma

4.1
, and controlling the minimum is much simpler than controlling the average during such an iteration.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
k\geqslant 2
be an integer, and fix
\tau_{0},p\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
. Let
n\geqslant p^{-10^{k}/\tau_{0}}
, and let
\mathcal{G}
be a
k
-mixed graph with parts
\mathcal{A},C
, where
\min\{\lvert A_{1}\rvert,\dots,\lvert A_{k}\rvert,\lvert C\rvert\}\geqslant n
. If
\tau_{C}(\mathcal{G})\geqslant\tau_{0}
and
d(\mathcal{A})\geqslant p
, then there are subsets
A_{i}^{\dagger}\subseteq A_{i},C^{\dagger}\subseteq C
satisfying

(a)

\lvert C^{\dagger}\rvert=\lfloor(\tau_{0}\log n)/(2^{k+5}\log\frac{1}{p})\rfloor
,

(b)

\lvert A_{i}^{\dagger}\rvert\geqslant\sqrt{n}
for every
i
,

(c)

d(\mathcal{A}^{\dagger})\geqslant p/2
, and

(d)

Every vertex of
C^{\dagger}
is adjacent to every vertex of
A_{1}^{\dagger}\cup\dots\cup A_{k}^{\dagger}
.

Proof.

Let

s=\left\lfloor\frac{\tau_{0}\log n}{2^{k+5}\log\frac{1}{p}}\right\rfloor\qquad\text{and}\qquad R=\frac{\log n}{4\log\frac{1}{p}}.

We will define a nested sequence of induced subgraphs
\mathcal{G}\supseteq\mathcal{G}^{(0)}\supseteq\mathcal{G}^{(1)}\supseteq\dots\supseteq\mathcal{G}^{(s)}
on vertex sets
(A_{1}^{(\ell)},\dots,A_{k}^{(\ell)},C^{(\ell)})_{\ell=0}^{s}
, satisfying the following properties.

(i)

\lvert C^{(\ell)}\rvert=\lvert C\rvert-\ell
.

(ii)

Every vertex in
C\setminus C^{(\ell)}
is adjacent to all vertices in
A_{1}^{(\ell)}\cup\dots\cup A_{k}^{(\ell)}
.

(iii)

\mathcal{A}^{(\ell)}
is strictly
R
-balanced and satisfies

\mathcal{E}_{R}(\mathcal{A}^{(\ell)})\geqslant 2^{-2^{k+3}\ell/\tau_{0}}p^{R}\prod_{i}\lvert A_{i}\rvert.

To begin, we set
C^{(0)}=C
, so that properties
(i)
and
(ii)
are trivially satisfied. We also let
\mathcal{A}^{(0)}
be a strictly
R
-balanced subgraph of
\mathcal{A}
, as given by
Lemma

3.4
, for which we have

\mathcal{E}_{R}(\mathcal{A}^{(0)})\geqslant\mathcal{E}_{R}(\mathcal{A})=d(\mathcal{A})^{R}\prod_{i}\lvert A_{i}\rvert\geqslant p^{R}\prod_{i}\lvert A_{i}\rvert,

so
(iii)
is also satisfied for
\ell=0
.

Inductively, suppose we have defined
\mathcal{G}^{(\ell)}
for some
\ell<s
, and aim to define
\mathcal{G}^{(\ell+1)}
.
Note that
s\leqslant\tau_{0}n/2
, hence
\lvert C\setminus C^{(\ell)}\rvert=\ell<\tau_{0}n/2\leqslant\tau_{0}\lvert C\rvert/2
by
(i)
. As a consequence, every edge in
A^{(\ell)}
has at least
\tau_{0}\lvert C\rvert/2
extensions to vertices in
C^{(\ell)}
. That is,
\tau_{C^{(\ell)}}(\mathcal{G}^{(\ell)})\geqslant\tau_{0}/2
.

We are thus in a position to apply
Lemma

4.1
to
\mathcal{G}^{(\ell)}
, with parameters
\tau=\tau_{0}/2
and
R=(\log n)/(4\log\frac{1}{p})
, as defined above. In order to apply the lemma, we must check that
R\geqslant 2^{k+1}/\tau=2^{k+2}/\tau_{0}
, which indeed holds by our assumption that
n\geqslant p^{-10^{k}/\tau_{0}}
. We obtain from
Lemma

4.1
a vertex
c^{*}
and sets
A_{i}^{*}\subseteq A_{i}^{(\ell)}
. We now set
C^{(\ell+1)}=C^{(\ell)}\setminus\{c^{*}\}
and
A_{i}^{(\ell+1)}=A^{*}_{i}
, and claim that this satisfies the desired properties.

Property
(i)
is immediate since we removed one vertex from
C^{(\ell)}
, as is property
(ii)
, since the newly added vertex to
C\setminus C^{(\ell+1)}
, namely
c^{*}
, is adjacent to all vertices in
A^{(\ell+1)}_{1}\cup\dots\cup A_{k}^{(\ell+1)}
by the statement of
Lemma

4.1
. Moreover, we indeed have that
\mathcal{A}^{(\ell+1)}
is strictly
R
-balanced, and satisfies

\mathcal{E}_{R}(\mathcal{A}^{(\ell+1)})\geqslant 2^{-2^{k+3}/\tau_{0}}\mathcal{E}_{R}(\mathcal{A}^{(\ell)})\geqslant 2^{-2^{k+3}(\ell+1)/\tau_{0}}p^{R}\prod_{i}\lvert A_{i}\rvert,

proving
(iii)
.

Finally, we stop this process upon defining
\mathcal{G}^{(s)}
. Let
C^{\dagger}=C\setminus C^{(s)},A_{i}^{\dagger}=A_{i}^{(s)}
. By construction, we have
\lvert C^{\dagger}\rvert=s
, proving
(a)
. We also automatically get
(d)
from property
(ii)
. It remains to prove
(b)
and
(c)
. For both of these, we note that

2^{-2^{k+3}s/\tau_{0}}\geqslant 2^{-(\log n)/(4\log(1/p))}=2^{-R}\geqslant n^{-1/4},

where the final inequality holds since
p\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
. Additionally, by our choice of
R
, we see that
p^{R}=n^{-1/4}
. Therefore,

d(\mathcal{A}^{\dagger})^{R}\prod_{i}\lvert A_{i}^{\dagger}\rvert=\mathcal{E}_{R}(\mathcal{A}^{\dagger})\geqslant 2^{-2^{k+3}s/\tau_{0}}p^{R}\prod_{i}\lvert A_{i}\rvert\geqslant n^{-1/4}\cdot n^{-1/4}\cdot\prod_{i}\lvert A_{i}\rvert,

by property
(iii)
and our choices of
s
and
R
. By combining this with the trivial upper bounds
\lvert A_{i}^{\dagger}\rvert\leqslant\lvert A_{i}\rvert
and
d(\mathcal{A}^{\dagger})
, we conclude that
\lvert A_{i}^{\dagger}\rvert\geqslant\lvert A_{i}\rvert/\sqrt{n}\geqslant\sqrt{n}
for all
i
, proving
(b)
. Similarly, we find that

d(\mathcal{A}^{\dagger})^{R}\geqslant 2^{-R}\cdot p^{R},

which yields
(c)
.
∎

As a corollary, we can immediately find a large complete tripartite graph in a graph with many triangles. We will eventually apply this as an intermediate step in our switching argument, so the statement of the following result is optimized with that application in mind. We denote by
\kappa_{3}(G)
the triangle density of a tripartite graph
G
, that is, the total number of triangles divided by the product of the sizes of the three parts.

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
\kappa,\nu\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
be parameters, and let
m\geqslant\kappa^{-400/\nu}
.
Let
G
be a tripartite graph with parts
A,B,C
, where
\min\{\lvert A\rvert,\lvert B\rvert,\lvert C\rvert\}\geqslant m
. If
\kappa_{3}(G)\geqslant\kappa
and
\kappa_{3}(G)/d(A,B)\geqslant\nu
, then
G
contains a copy of
K_{s,t,t}
, where

s=\left\lfloor\frac{\nu\log m}{512\log\frac{1}{\kappa}}\right\rfloor\qquad\text{and}\qquad t=\left\lfloor\frac{\log m}{12\log\frac{1}{\kappa}}\right\rfloor.

Proof.

Let
\tau=\nu/2
, and let
F\subseteq E(A,B)
denote the set of edges lying in at most
\tau\lvert C\rvert
triangles of
G
. Then let
G^{\prime}
be the subgraph of
G
obtained by discarding the edges of
F
(and not discarding any vertices); by construction, we have
\tau_{C}(G^{\prime})\geqslant\tau
.
The total number of triangles using an edge in
F
is at most

\tau\lvert C\rvert\cdot e(A,B)=\tau d(A,B)\lvert A\rvert\lvert B\rvert\lvert C\rvert\leqslant\frac{\nu}{2}\cdot\frac{\kappa_{3}(G)}{\nu}\lvert A\rvert\lvert B\rvert\lvert C\rvert=\frac{\kappa_{3}(G)}{2}\lvert A\rvert\lvert B\rvert\lvert C\rvert.

Thus, at least half the triangles in
G
are also present in
G^{\prime}
. In particular,

e_{G^{\prime}}(A,B)\geqslant\frac{\kappa_{3}(G)\lvert A\rvert\lvert B\rvert\lvert C\rvert}{2\lvert C\rvert}\geqslant\frac{\kappa}{2}\lvert A\rvert\lvert B\rvert,

hence
d_{G^{\prime}}(A,B)\geqslant\kappa/2\geqslant\kappa^{2}
, using our assumption
\kappa\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
.

We may now apply
Proposition

4.2
with parameters
k=2,\tau_{0}=\tau,p=\kappa^{2}
to
\mathcal{G}=G^{\prime}
; note that a
2
-mixed graph is the same as a tripartite graph, and that
m
is sufficiently large to apply
Proposition

4.2
since
m\geqslant\kappa^{-400/\nu}=p^{-100/\tau}
. We obtain sets
A^{\dagger}\subseteq A,B^{\dagger}\subseteq B,C^{\dagger}\subseteq C
, satisfying

(a)

\lvert C^{\dagger}\rvert=\lfloor(\tau\log m)/(2^{7}\log\frac{1}{p})\rfloor=s
,

(b)

\lvert A^{\dagger}\rvert\geqslant\sqrt{m}
and
\lvert B^{\dagger}\rvert\geqslant\sqrt{m}
,

(c)

d(A^{\dagger},B^{\dagger})\geqslant p/2
, and

(d)

every vertex of
C^{\dagger}
is adjacent to every vertex of
A^{\dagger}\cup B^{\dagger}
.

In particular, given the last point, it suffices to show that
(A^{\dagger},B^{\dagger})
contains a copy of
K_{t,t}
. But this is an immediate consequence of
Theorem

3.1
when we note that

\left\lfloor\frac{\log\min\{\lvert A^{\dagger}\rvert,\lvert B^{\dagger}\rvert\}}{2\log\frac{2}{p}}\right\rfloor\geqslant\left\lfloor\frac{\log\sqrt{m}}{2\log\frac{1}{\kappa^{3}}}\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\frac{\log m}{12\log\frac{1}{\kappa}}\right\rfloor=t.\qed

5.
An improved bound via switching

In order to simplify the presentation of the argument, it is helpful to introduce the following definition, which precisely captures the kind of subgraph whose presence would be useful in the argument above.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
H
be a tripartite graph with parts
X,Y,Z
. We call
H
an
(m,\mu)
-switcher
if
\min\{\lvert X\rvert,\lvert Y\rvert,\lvert Z\rvert\}\geqslant m
,
\kappa_{3}(H)\geqslant\mu^{2}
, and
\kappa_{3}(H)/d(X,Y)\geqslant\mu
.

Note that this condition is monotone in both parameters, in the sense that every
(m,\mu)
-switcher is also an
(m^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})
-switcher for all
m^{\prime}\leqslant m
and all
\mu^{\prime}\leqslant\mu
.
From
Proposition

4.2
, we see that if
\mu\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
and
m
is sufficiently large, then every
(m,\mu)
-switcher contains a copy of
K_{s,t,t}
, where
s=\Omega((\mu\log m)/(\log\frac{1}{\mu}))
and
t=\Omega((\log m)/(\log\frac{1}{\mu}))
.

Using these ideas, we prove the following strengthening of
Lemma

4.1
. Note that we have added the assumption that no subgraph of
G
is a switcher, and have obtained a stronger conclusion in
(ii)
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Fix parameters
\tau\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
and
R\geqslant 50/\tau
. Let
G
be a tripartite graph with parts
(A,B,C)
, and suppose that
\tau_{C}(G)\geqslant\tau
. Let
m\leqslant\frac{\tau}{4}\min\{\lvert A\rvert,\lvert B\rvert,\lvert C\rvert\}
be a positive integer, and let
p=d(A,B)
. Suppose further that
(A,B)
is strictly
R
-balanced, and that no subgraph of
G
is an
(m,\sqrt{p\tau}/4)
-switcher.

Then there exist a vertex
c^{*}\in C
and non-empty subsets
A^{*}\subseteq A,B^{*}\subseteq B
satisfying the following properties.

(i)

c^{*}
is adjacent to all vertices in
A^{*}
and
B^{*}
.

(ii)

We have

\mathcal{E}_{R}(A^{*},B^{*})\geqslant\tau^{20/\sqrt{p\tau}}\mathcal{E}_{R}(A,B).

(iii)

(A^{*},B^{*})
is strictly
R
-balanced.

Proof.

Let
\kappa=\kappa_{3}(G)
. Note that the total number of triangles in
G
equals
\kappa\lvert A\rvert\lvert B\rvert\lvert C\rvert
, but is also at least
e(A,B)\cdot\tau\lvert C\rvert
, since every edge between
A
and
B
lies in at least
\tau_{C}(G)\lvert C\rvert\geqslant\tau\lvert C\rvert
triangles; this in particular implies that
\kappa\geqslant p\tau
.
Let
C_{0}\subseteq C
consist of all vertices
c\in C
which lie in at most
\frac{1}{4}\kappa\lvert A\rvert\lvert B\rvert
triangles. The total number of triangles touching
C_{0}
is at most
\frac{1}{4}\kappa\lvert A\rvert\lvert B\rvert\lvert C\rvert
, hence there are at least
\frac{3}{4}\kappa\lvert A\rvert\lvert B\rvert\lvert C\rvert
triangles touching a vertex in
C\setminus C_{0}
. Each such vertex lies in at most
e(A,B)
triangles, hence

\lvert C\setminus C_{0}\rvert\geqslant\frac{\frac{3}{4}\kappa\lvert A\rvert\lvert B\rvert\lvert C\rvert}{e(A,B)}\geqslant\frac{3}{4}\cdot\frac{e(A,B)\cdot\tau\lvert C\rvert}{e(A,B)}=\frac{3\tau}{4}\lvert C\rvert.

Let
\mu=\sqrt{p\tau}/4
, and note that
\mu\leqslant\sqrt{\kappa}/4
since
\kappa\geqslant p\tau
. Let us call a vertex
c\in C

A
-sparse
if
c\notin C_{0}
, and additionally
\lvert N(c)\cap A\rvert\leqslant\mu\lvert A\rvert
. Let
C_{A}
denote the set of
A
-sparse vertices in
C
, and suppose that
\lvert C_{A}\rvert\geqslant m
. Then consider the induced subgraph
G_{A}
on vertex set
A\cup B\cup C_{A}
. Since no vertex of
C_{A}
is in
C_{0}
, we see that
G_{A}
contains at least
(\frac{1}{4}\kappa\lvert A\rvert\lvert B\rvert)\cdot\lvert C_{A}\rvert
triangles, and hence
\kappa_{3}(G_{A})\geqslant\kappa/4>\mu^{2}
. Moreover, as every vertex in
C_{A}
has degree at most
\mu\lvert A\rvert
to
A
, we see that
d(A,C_{A})\leqslant\mu
, hence
\kappa_{3}(G_{A})/d(A,C_{A})>\mu^{2}/\mu=\mu
.
Finally, we have
\min\{\lvert A\rvert,\lvert B\rvert,\lvert C_{A}\rvert\}\geqslant m
, hence
G_{A}
is an
(m,\mu)
-switcher on parts
X=A,Y=C_{A},Z=B
. This contradicts our assumption that no subgraph of
G
is an
(m,\mu)
-switcher, so we conclude that
\lvert C_{A}\rvert<m
. By the same logic, we have
\lvert C_{B}\rvert<m
, where
C_{B}
is defined analogously.

We conclude that
\lvert C\setminus(C_{0}\cup C_{A}\cup C_{B})\rvert\geqslant(3\tau/4)\lvert C\rvert-2m\geqslant\tau\lvert C\rvert/4
. Fix any vertex
c^{*}\in C\setminus(C_{0}\cup C_{A}\cup C_{B})
, and let
A^{\oplus}=N(c^{*})\cap A,B^{\oplus}=N(c^{*})\cap B
. By the choice of
c^{*}
, we know that
e(A^{\oplus},B^{\oplus})\geqslant\frac{1}{4}\kappa\lvert A\rvert\lvert B\rvert
, and that
\lvert A^{\oplus}\rvert\geqslant\mu\lvert A\rvert,\lvert B^{\oplus}\rvert\geqslant\mu\lvert B\rvert
.

Note that if
p\leqslant 16\tau
, then also
p\leqslant 16(\kappa/p)
, since
\kappa\geqslant p\tau
. Therefore,

\left(\frac{\kappa}{p}\right)^{2}\geqslant\frac{\kappa}{p}\cdot\frac{p}{16}=\frac{\kappa}{16}\geqslant\mu^{2}

implying that
\kappa_{3}(G)/d(A,B)=\kappa/p\geqslant\mu
.
Since
\min\{\lvert A\rvert,\lvert B\rvert,\lvert C\rvert\}\geqslant m
, we conclude that
G
itself is an
(m,\mu)
-switcher, a contradiction. Hence we have
p>16\tau
.

Now let
\eta=\sqrt{\tau/p}
, and note by the above that
\eta\leqslant\sqrt{\tau/(16\tau)}=\frac{1}{4}
. Let
\alpha=({15\eta p\log\tfrac{1}{\eta}})/({\tau R})
. Note that since
R\geqslant 50/\tau
and
\eta\leqslant\frac{1}{4}
, we have that
\alpha\leqslant p/2
.
We also note for future reference that
\eta\lvert A\rvert\geqslant 4\eta/\tau>4/\sqrt{\tau}>5
, using that
1\leqslant m\leqslant\frac{\tau}{4}\lvert A\rvert
. This implies that
\lfloor\eta\lvert A\rvert\rfloor\geqslant\frac{4}{5}\eta\lvert A\rvert
, and similarly
\lfloor\eta\lvert B\rvert\rfloor\geqslant\frac{4}{5}\eta\lvert B\rvert
.

Our key claim is that

(6)

d(A^{\oplus},B^{\oplus})\geqslant p-\alpha.

Suppose for contradiction that
d(A^{\oplus},B^{\oplus})<p-\alpha<p
. In particular, we find that

p\lvert A^{\oplus}\rvert\lvert B^{\oplus}\rvert>e(A^{\oplus},B^{\oplus})\geqslant\frac{1}{4}\kappa\lvert A\rvert\lvert B\rvert\geqslant\frac{1}{4}(p\tau)\lvert A\rvert\lvert B\rvert,

since
\kappa\geqslant\tau_{C}(G)p\geqslant\tau p
. Rearranging, we conclude that
\lvert A^{\oplus}\rvert\lvert B^{\oplus}\rvert\geqslant\frac{\tau}{4}\lvert A\rvert\lvert B\rvert
.

Let
A^{+}
be a uniformly random subset of
A^{\oplus}
of size
\min\{\lvert A^{\oplus}\rvert,\lfloor\eta\lvert A\rvert\rfloor\}
, and similarly for
B^{+}
.
By averaging, we may find a specific choice of
A^{+},B^{+}
such that
d(A^{+},B^{+})<p-\alpha
; let us fix such a choice. The key point is that in doing this, we have lost essentially nothing on the lower bound on the product of the sizes of
A^{+}
and
B^{+}
, since

\lvert A^{+}\rvert\lvert B^{+}\rvert=\min\{\lvert A^{\oplus}\rvert\lvert B^{\oplus}\rvert,\lfloor\eta\lvert A\rvert\rfloor\lvert B^{\oplus}\rvert,\lfloor\eta\lvert B\rvert\rfloor\lvert A^{\oplus}\rvert,\lfloor\eta\lvert A\rvert\rfloor\lfloor\eta\lvert B\rvert\rfloor\}.

The first term is at least
\frac{\tau}{4}\lvert A\rvert\lvert B\rvert
.
The next two terms are each at least
\frac{4}{5}\eta\mu\lvert A\rvert\lvert B\rvert=\frac{\tau}{5}\lvert A\rvert\lvert B\rvert
, by our choices of
\eta=\sqrt{\tau/p}
and
\mu=\sqrt{p\tau}/4
. Finally, the last term is at least
(\frac{4}{5})^{2}\eta^{2}\lvert A\rvert\lvert B\rvert>\frac{1}{5}{(\tau/p)\lvert A\rvert\lvert B\rvert}\geqslant\frac{\tau}{5}\lvert A\rvert\lvert B\rvert
. So in total, we conclude that
\lvert A^{+}\rvert\lvert B^{+}\rvert\geqslant\frac{\tau}{5}\lvert A\rvert\lvert B\rvert
.

Let
A^{-}=A\setminus A^{+},B^{-}=B\setminus B^{+}
. Since
\lvert A^{+}\rvert\leqslant\eta\lvert A\rvert,\lvert B^{+}\rvert\leqslant\eta\lvert B\rvert
, we see that for every
(X,Y)\in\{(A^{-},B^{-}),(A^{+},B^{-}),(A^{-},B^{+})\}
, we have that
(\lvert X\rvert\lvert Y\rvert)/(\lvert A\rvert\lvert B\rvert)\in[0,\eta]\cup[(1-\eta)^{2},1]
. Moreover, by our choice of
R
and
\eta
, we have
R\geqslant\frac{50}{\tau}\geqslant\log\frac{1}{\tau}\geqslant\log\frac{1}{\eta}
. Therefore, since
(A,B)
is strictly
R
-balanced, we may apply
3.5(b)
to conclude that for each such choice of
(X,Y)
, we have

e(X,Y)\leqslant p\lvert X\rvert\lvert Y\rvert+(\eta\log\tfrac{1}{\eta})\frac{p}{R}\lvert A\rvert\lvert B\rvert\leqslant p\lvert X\rvert\lvert Y\rvert+\frac{5\eta p\log\tfrac{1}{\eta}}{\tau R}\lvert A^{+}\rvert\lvert B^{+}\rvert.

Summing this inequality over all three choices of
(X,Y)
, as well as the inequality
e(A^{+},B^{+})<(p-\alpha)\lvert A^{+}\rvert\lvert B^{+}\rvert
, we conclude that

e(A,B)<p\lvert A\rvert\lvert B\rvert+\left(\frac{15\eta p\log\tfrac{1}{\eta}}{\tau R}-\alpha\right)\lvert A^{+}\rvert\lvert B^{+}\rvert.

But this is impossible since, by our choice of
\alpha
, the second term above is
0
, and the first term equals
e(A,B)
. This contradiction concludes the proof of (
6
).

It now only remains to estimate
\mathcal{E}_{R}(A^{\oplus},B^{\oplus})
. For this, we recall that
e(A^{\oplus},B^{\oplus})\geqslant\frac{1}{4}\kappa\lvert A\rvert\lvert B\rvert\geqslant\frac{1}{4}p\tau\lvert A\rvert\lvert B\rvert
, and compute that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{R}(A^{\oplus},B^{\oplus})

\displaystyle=e(A^{\oplus},B^{\oplus})d(A^{\oplus},B^{\oplus})^{R-1}

\displaystyle\geqslant\left(\frac{1}{4}p\tau\lvert A\rvert\lvert B\rvert\right)(p-\alpha)^{R-1}

\displaystyle=(\lvert A\rvert\lvert B\rvert p^{R})\left(\frac{\tau}{4}p^{1-R}(p-\alpha)^{R-1}\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathcal{E}_{R}(A,B)\left(\tau^{3}\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{p}\right)^{R-1}\right),

using that
\tau\leqslant 1/2
in the final inequality.
Since
\alpha\leqslant p/2
, we may apply the inequality
1-\alpha/p\geqslant 2^{-2\alpha/p}
, which implies
(1-\alpha/p)^{R-1}\geqslant 2^{-2R\alpha/p}
. Now note that

\frac{2R\alpha}{p}=\frac{30\eta\log\frac{1}{\eta}}{\tau}\leqslant 30\frac{\sqrt{\tau/p}\cdot\log(1/\sqrt{\tau})}{\tau}=\frac{15\log\frac{1}{\tau}}{\sqrt{\tau p}}

where we use that
\eta=\sqrt{\tau/p}\geqslant\sqrt{\tau}
in the first inequality. We may thus conclude that

\tau^{3}\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{p}\right)^{R-1}\geqslant\tau^{3}\cdot 2^{-15\log(1/\tau)/\sqrt{p\tau}}=\tau^{3}\cdot\tau^{15/\sqrt{p\tau}}\geqslant\tau^{20/\sqrt{p\tau}},

and hence that
\mathcal{E}_{R}(A^{\oplus},B^{\oplus})\geqslant\tau^{20/\sqrt{p\tau}}\mathcal{E}_{R}(A,B)
. Finally, we pass to a strictly
R
-balanced subgraph
(A^{*},B^{*})
satisfying
\mathcal{E}_{R}(A^{*},B^{*})\geqslant\mathcal{E}_{R}(A^{\oplus},B^{\oplus})
, as given by
Lemma

3.4
, which satisfies the desired properties since
c^{*}
is adjacent to all vertices in
A^{\oplus}\cup B^{\oplus}\supseteq A^{*}\cup B^{*}
.
∎

Our next result is obtained by iterating
Lemma

5.2
, and the proof is very similar to that of
Proposition

4.2
.

Proposition 5.3
.

Fix parameters
\tau_{0},p_{0}\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
. Let
n\geqslant p_{0}^{-400/\tau_{0}}
and let
G
be a tripartite graph with parts
A,B,C
satisfying
\min\{\lvert A\rvert,\lvert B\rvert,\lvert C\rvert\}\geqslant n
. Suppose that
\tau_{C}(G)\geqslant\tau_{0}
and
d(A,B)\geqslant p_{0}
, and that no subgraph of
G
is a
(\lfloor n^{1/3}\rfloor,\sqrt{p_{0}\tau_{0}}/8)
-switcher.

Then there exist subsets
A^{\dagger}\subseteq A,B^{\dagger}\subseteq B,C^{\dagger}\subseteq C
satisfying

(a)

\lvert C^{\dagger}\rvert=\lfloor({\sqrt{p_{0}\tau_{0}}\log n})/({320\log\frac{1}{p_{0}}\log\frac{1}{\tau_{0}}})\rfloor
,

(b)

\lvert A^{\dagger}\rvert\geqslant\sqrt{n}
and
\lvert B^{\dagger}\rvert\geqslant\sqrt{n}
,

(c)

d(A^{\dagger},B^{\dagger})\geqslant p_{0}/2
, and

(d)

Every vertex of
C^{\dagger}
is adjacent to every vertex of
A^{\dagger}\cup B^{\dagger}
.

As the proof of
Proposition

5.3
is quite similar to that of
Proposition

4.2
, we will be brief, mostly highlighting the places where the differences lie.

Proof of
Proposition

5.3
.

Let

s=\left\lfloor\frac{\sqrt{p_{0}\tau_{0}}\log n}{320\log\frac{1}{p_{0}}\log\frac{1}{\tau_{0}}}\right\rfloor,\qquad R=\frac{\log n}{4\log\frac{1}{p_{0}}},\qquad m=\lfloor n^{1/3}\rfloor,\qquad\kappa_{0}=\frac{p_{0}\tau_{0}}{4},\qquad\mu=\frac{\sqrt{\kappa_{0}}}{4}.

We note that by our assumption
n\geqslant p_{0}^{-400/\tau_{0}}\geqslant 2^{400/\tau_{0}}
, we have that
\frac{\tau_{0}}{8}\sqrt{n}\geqslant m
.
We will define a nested sequence of induced subgraphs
G\supseteq G^{(0)}\supseteq\dots\supseteq G^{(s)}
on vertex sets
(A^{(\ell)},B^{(\ell)},C^{(\ell)})_{\ell=0}^{s}
, satisfying that
\lvert C^{(\ell)}\rvert=\lvert C\rvert-\ell
, that every vertex in
C\setminus C^{(\ell)}
is adjacent to all of
A^{(\ell)}\cup B^{(\ell)}
, and that
(A^{(\ell)},B^{(\ell)})
is strictly
R
-balanced and satisfies

\mathcal{E}_{R}(A^{(\ell)},B^{(\ell)})\geqslant\tau_{0}^{40\ell/\sqrt{\kappa_{0}}}p_{0}^{R}\lvert A\rvert\lvert B\rvert.

To begin the iteration, we let
C^{(0)}=C
, and let
(A^{(0)},B^{(0)})
be a strictly
R
-balanced subgraph of
(A,B)
, as given by
Lemma

3.4
, which trivially satisfies these properties.

Now suppose we have defined
G^{(\ell)}
for some
\ell\leqslant s
. We note several properties of this graph. First, as
\lvert C\setminus C^{(\ell)}\rvert=\ell\leqslant s\leqslant\tau_{0}n/2
, we have that
\tau_{C^{(\ell)}}(G^{(\ell)})\geqslant\tau_{0}/2
. Second, using the fact that
p_{0}^{R}=n^{-1/4}
and

\tau_{0}^{40\ell/\sqrt{\kappa_{0}}}\geqslant\tau_{0}^{40s/\sqrt{\kappa_{0}}}\geqslant 2^{-R}\geqslant n^{-1/4},

we find that

d(A^{(\ell)},B^{(\ell)})^{R}\lvert A^{(\ell)}\rvert\lvert B^{(\ell)}\rvert=\mathcal{E}_{R}(A^{(\ell)},B^{(\ell)})\geqslant 2^{-R}p_{0}^{R}\lvert A\rvert\lvert B\rvert\geqslant n^{-1/2}\lvert A\rvert\lvert B\rvert.

This implies immediately that
\lvert A^{(\ell)}\rvert\geqslant\sqrt{n},\lvert B^{(\ell)}\rvert\geqslant\sqrt{n}
, as well as that
d(A^{(\ell)},B^{(\ell)})\geqslant p_{0}/2
.

Assuming now that
\ell<s
,
we apply
Lemma

5.2
to
G^{(\ell)}
, with parameters
\tau=\tau_{0}/2,p=d(A^{(\ell)},B^{(\ell)})\geqslant p_{0}/2
and
R,m
defined as above. Our assumption
n\geqslant p_{0}^{-400/\tau_{0}}
implies that
R\geqslant 100/\tau_{0}=50/\tau
. We’ve also seen that
m\leqslant\frac{\tau_{0}}{8}\sqrt{n}\leqslant\frac{\tau}{4}\min\{\lvert A^{(\ell)}\rvert,\lvert B^{(\ell)}\rvert,\lvert C^{(\ell)}\rvert\}
, using that
\lvert C^{(\ell)}\rvert\geqslant\lvert C\rvert-s\geqslant\sqrt{n}
. Finally, since
G^{(\ell)}
is a subgraph of
G
, we see that no subgraph of
G^{(\ell)}
is an
(m,\sqrt{p_{0}\tau_{0}}/8)
-switcher, hence no subgraph of
G^{(\ell)}
is an
(m,\sqrt{p\tau}/4)
-switcher. We have thus verified all the conditions of
Lemma

5.2
, which produces for us a vertex
c^{*}
and sets
A^{*}\subseteq A^{(\ell)},B^{*}\subseteq B^{(\ell)}
satisfying

\mathcal{E}_{R}(A^{*},B^{*})\geqslant\tau^{20/\sqrt{p\tau}}\mathcal{E}_{R}(A^{(\ell)},B^{(\ell)})\geqslant\left(\frac{\tau_{0}}{2}\right)^{20/\sqrt{p_{0}\tau_{0}/4}}\mathcal{E}_{R}(A^{(\ell)},B^{(\ell)})\geqslant\tau_{0}^{40/\sqrt{\kappa_{0}}}\mathcal{E}_{R}(A^{(\ell)},B^{(\ell)}).

We set
C^{(\ell+1)}=C^{(\ell)}\setminus\{c^{*}\},A^{(\ell+1)}=A^{*},B^{(\ell+1)}=B^{*}
, and see that all of our desired properties remain true for
G^{(\ell+1)}
. Moreover, when we stop the iteration at step
s
, the computations above show that we may take
A^{\dagger}=A^{(s)},B^{\dagger}=B^{(s)},C^{\dagger}=C\setminus C^{(s)}
, which satisfy all the desired properties.
∎

6.
Bounds in Nikiforov’s theorem

In this section, we use the results proved in the previous sections to prove
Theorem

1.2
.
In order to apply
Propositions

4.2
and
5.3
, we need a result that converts an arbitrary graph with many copies of
K_{k+1}
into a
k
-mixed graph in which we control both
\tau_{C}(G)
and
d(\mathcal{A})
. This is not too hard to do via standard tools from the theory of graph regularity, and we prove the following result in
Appendix

A
.

Lemma 6.1
.

For every integer
k\geqslant 2
and every
\gamma\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
, there exists some
\zeta\geqslant\gamma^{k^{50}\gamma^{-10}}>0
and some
\tau\in[\gamma^{2/(k+1)},\frac{1}{2}]
such that the following holds. Let
G
be an
N
-vertex graph with at least
\gamma N^{k+1}
copies of
K_{k+1}
. Then there exist disjoint sets
A_{1},\dots,A_{k},C\subseteq V(G)
, each of size at least
\lfloor\zeta N\rfloor
, as well as a
k
-mixed graph
\mathcal{G}
on vertex set
(\mathcal{A},C)
such that

(1)

Every edge of
E_{\mathcal{G}}(C,A_{1}\cup\dotsb\cup A_{k})
is an edge of
G
,

(2)

Every edge of
E_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathcal{A})
is a copy of
K_{k}
in
G
, and

(3)

\tau_{C}(\mathcal{G})\geqslant\tau\geqslant\gamma^{2/(k+1)}
and
d(\mathcal{A})\tau\geqslant\gamma/4
.

We begin by proving
Theorem

1.2
in the case of triangles.

Theorem 6.2
.

c_{K_{3}}(\gamma)=\Omega(\sqrt{\gamma}/(\log\frac{1}{\gamma})^{2})
.

Proof.

We may assume that
\gamma\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
, by choosing the implicit constant large enough.
Let
N\geqslant\gamma^{-2^{60}\gamma^{-10}}
be sufficiently large, and let
G_{0}
be an
N
-vertex graph with at least
\gamma N^{3}
triangles. Applying
Lemma

6.1
with
k=2
, we obtain a tripartite subgraph
G
on parts
A,B,C\subseteq V(G)
, satisfying
n=\min\{\lvert A\rvert,\lvert B\rvert,\lvert C\rvert\}\geqslant\lfloor\zeta N\rfloor\geqslant\sqrt{N}
, as well as some
\tau\in[\gamma^{2/3},\frac{1}{2}]
such that
\tau_{C}(G)\geqslant\tau\geqslant\gamma^{2/3}
and
d_{G}(A,B)\tau\geqslant\gamma/4
. This in particular implies
d_{G}(A,B)\geqslant\gamma/4
. Note that
n\geqslant\sqrt{N}\geqslant(\gamma/4)^{-400/\gamma^{2/3}}
.

We split into two cases. First, suppose that no subgraph of
G
is a
(\lfloor n^{1/3}\rfloor,\sqrt{\gamma}/16)
-switcher. Then we are precisely in the setting of
Proposition

5.3
(with parameters
\tau_{0}=\tau
and
p_{0}=\gamma/(4\tau_{0})
), hence we may find sets
A^{\dagger},B^{\dagger},C^{\dagger}
as in the statement of
Proposition

5.3
. We have that

s=\lvert C^{\dagger}\rvert\geqslant\left\lfloor\frac{\sqrt{\gamma/4}\log n}{320\log\frac{1}{\gamma^{2/3}}\log\frac{4}{\gamma}}\right\rfloor\geqslant\left\lfloor\frac{\sqrt{\gamma}\log n}{2000(\log\frac{1}{\gamma})^{2}}\right\rfloor\geqslant\left\lfloor\frac{\sqrt{\gamma}\log N}{4000(\log\frac{1}{\gamma})^{2}}\right\rfloor,

as well as
\lvert A^{\dagger}\rvert,\lvert B^{\dagger}\rvert\geqslant\sqrt{n}\geqslant N^{1/4}
, and all vertices of
C^{\dagger}
are adjacent to all vertices of
A^{\dagger}\cup B^{\dagger}
. We now apply
Theorem

3.1
to the bipartite graph between
A^{\dagger}
and
B^{\dagger}
, which has edge density at least
d(A,B)/2\geqslant\gamma/8
. We conclude that
(A^{\dagger},B^{\dagger})
contains a copy of
K_{t,t}
for

t=\left\lfloor\frac{\log N^{1/4}}{2\log\frac{8}{\gamma}}\right\rfloor\geqslant\left\lfloor\frac{\log N}{32\log\frac{1}{\gamma}}\right\rfloor\geqslant s.

Together with
C^{\dagger}
, we find a copy of
K_{s,t,t}\supseteq K_{s,s,s}
in

, completing the proof in this case.

On the other hand, if
G
contains a
(\lfloor n^{1/3}\rfloor,\sqrt{\gamma}/16)
-switcher, we apply
Corollary

4.3
to this switcher, with parameters
m=\lfloor n^{1/3}\rfloor\geqslant N^{1/7},\kappa=\gamma/256
, and
\nu=\sqrt{\gamma}/16
, noting that
m\geqslant\kappa^{-400/\nu}
. We conclude that
G
contains a complete tripartite graph
K_{s^{\prime},t^{\prime},t^{\prime}}
, where

s^{\prime}=\left\lfloor\frac{\nu\log m}{512\log\frac{256}{\gamma}}\right\rfloor\geqslant\left\lfloor\frac{\sqrt{\gamma}\log N}{10^{7}\log\frac{1}{\gamma}}\right\rfloor,\qquad t^{\prime}=\left\lfloor\frac{\log m}{12\log\frac{256}{\gamma}}\right\rfloor\geqslant s^{\prime},

and we again obtain the desired result.
∎

Next, we use
Proposition

4.2
to provide a recursive bound on
c_{K_{k+1}}(\gamma)
in terms of
c_{K_{k}}(\gamma)
.

Theorem 6.3
.

For every
k\geqslant 3
, we have

c_{K_{k+1}}(\gamma)\geqslant\min\left\{\Omega_{k}\left(\frac{\gamma^{2/(k+1)}}{\log\frac{1}{\gamma}}\right),\frac{1}{8}c_{K_{k}}\left(\frac{\gamma}{8k^{k}}\right)\right\}.

Proof.

As before, we may assume that
\gamma\leqslant 2^{-k}
, by picking the implicit constant sufficiently large.
Let
\gamma^{\prime}=\gamma/(8k^{k})
, and let
m_{0}
be chosen sufficiently large so that for all
m\geqslant m_{0}
, every
m
-vertex graph with
\gamma^{\prime}m^{k}
copies of
K_{k}
contains a copy of
K_{k}[t]
, where
t\geqslant\frac{1}{2}c_{K_{k}}(\gamma^{\prime})\log m
.

Let now
N
be sufficiently large, and in particular at least
m_{0}^{4}
. Let
G
be an
N
-vertex graph with at least
\gamma N^{k+1}
copies of
K_{k+1}
. We apply
Lemma

6.1
to find disjoint sets
A_{1},\dots,A_{k},C\subseteq V(G)
, as well as a
k
-mixed graph
\mathcal{G}
on this vertex set. Since
N
is sufficiently large, we may assume
n=\min\{\lvert A_{1}\rvert,\dots,\lvert A_{k}\rvert,\lvert C\rvert\}\geqslant\sqrt{N}
. We now apply
Proposition

4.2
to
\mathcal{G}
, with parameters
\tau_{0}=\gamma^{2/(k+1)}
and
p=\gamma/(4\tau_{C}(\mathcal{G}))\geqslant\gamma/4
, to obtain sets
A_{i}^{\dagger},C^{\dagger}
. We have that

\lvert C^{\dagger}\rvert=\left\lfloor\frac{\tau_{0}\log n}{2^{k+5}\log\frac{1}{p}}\right\rfloor\geqslant\left\lfloor\frac{\gamma^{2/(k+1)}\log N}{2^{k+8}\log\frac{1}{\gamma}}\right\rfloor.

Now, let
A_{i}^{\prime}\subseteq A_{i}^{\dagger}
be a randomly chosen set of size exactly
\lfloor N^{1/4}\rfloor
, which exists since
\lvert A_{i}^{\dagger}\rvert\geqslant\sqrt{n}\geqslant\lfloor N^{1/4}\rfloor
. In expectation, the number of
k
-uniform edges of
\mathcal{G}
in
A_{1}^{\prime}\cup\dots\cup A_{k}^{\prime}
is at least
(p/2)\lfloor N^{1/4}\rfloor^{k}
, hence we may fix an outcome with at least that many edges. This yields for us an induced subgraph of
G[A_{1}^{\dagger}\cup\dots\cup A_{k}^{\dagger}]
of order
m=k\lfloor N^{1/4}\rfloor
with at least
(\gamma/8)\lfloor N^{1/4}\rfloor^{k}=\gamma^{\prime}m^{k}
copies of
K_{k}
. By the definition of
m_{0}
, we can find in this subgraph a copy of
K_{k}[t]
, for
t=\frac{1}{2}c_{K_{k}}(\gamma^{\prime})\log m\geqslant\frac{1}{8}c_{K_{k}}(\gamma^{\prime})\log N
, and all of its vertices are adjacent in
G
to
C^{\dagger}
. Hence, together with
C^{\dagger}
, we obtain a blowup of
K_{k+1}
of order
\min\{t,\lvert C^{\dagger}\rvert\}
, which yields the desired result.
∎

We note that this proof actually shows that the “sufficiently large” condition on the number of vertices for
K_{k+1}
is polynomial in that for
K_{k}
. In particular, since
Theorem

6.2
already applies for
N\geqslant\gamma^{-O(\gamma^{-10})}
, we conclude that similarly
Theorem

6.3
also holds for
N\geqslant\gamma^{-O_{k}(\gamma^{-10})}
.

Finally, we use the following simple and well-known argument, that shows that a bound on
c_{K_{h}}(\gamma)
implies essentially the same bound on
c_{H}(\gamma)
for any
h
-vertex graph
H
.

Proposition 6.4
.

If
H
has
h
vertices, then
c_{H}(\gamma)\geqslant c_{K_{h}}(h^{-h}\gamma)
.

Proof.

Let
G
be an
N
-vertex graph, and suppose that
G
contains at least
\gamma N^{h}
copies of
H
. In particular, if we identify the vertex set of
H
with
[h]
, then
G
contains at least
\gamma N^{h}
labeled copies of
H
. We now pick a random partition of
V(G)
into
h
parts
V_{1},\dotsc,V_{h}
. Every labeled copy of
H
has a probability
h^{-h}
of being
spanning
, that is, having its
i
th vertex end up in
V_{i}
for all
i\in[h]
. So by linearity of expectation, there exists some
h
-partition of
V
so that at least
h^{-h}\gamma N^{h}
labeled copies of
H
are spanning.

We now define a new
h
-partite graph
\widetilde{G}
on the same vertex set as
G
. We obtain
\widetilde{G}
from
G
by first deleting every edge contained in some
V_{i}
. Next, for every pair
(i,j)
such that
ij\notin E(H)
, we place a complete bipartite graph between
V_{i}
and
V_{j}
. However, if
ij\in E(H)
, then we let
\widetilde{G}
have the same edges between
V_{i}
and
V_{j}
as are found in
G
.

Every spanning copy of
H
in
G
thus yields a copy of
K_{h}
in
\widetilde{G}
, and conversely, every copy of
K_{h}
in
\widetilde{G}
arises from a spanning copy of
H
in
G
.
So by the definition of
c_{K_{h}}(h^{-h}\gamma)
, we see that
\widetilde{G}
must contain
K_{h}[t]
as a subgraph, where
t\geqslant(c_{K_{h}}(h^{-h}\gamma)-o(1))\log N
, and where the
o(1)
term tends to
0
as
N\to\infty
. As
\widetilde{G}
is
h
-partite, each of the parts of this
K_{h}[t]
must lie in a distinct part
V_{i}
of
\widetilde{G}
. But if we only restrict our attention to pairs
(V_{i},V_{j})
where
ij\in E(H)
, we see that this
K_{h}[t]
gives rise to a copy of
H[t]
inside
G
. By the definition of
c_{H}(\gamma)
, this completes the proof.
∎

With these tools, the proof of
Theorem

1.2
is essentially immediate.

Proof of
Theorem

1.2
.

The case
h=1
is trivial, and the case
h=2
is covered by
Theorem

3.1
, hence we may assume that
h\geqslant 3
. As before, we may also assume that
\gamma\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
.
We first claim that for all
h\geqslant 3
, we have
c_{K_{h}}(\gamma)=\Omega_{h}(\sqrt{\gamma}/(\log\frac{1}{\gamma})^{2})
. We prove this by induction on
h
, with the base case
h=3
being precisely the statement of
Theorem

6.2
. Having proved the statement for
h=k
, we obtain it for
h=k+1\geqslant 4
by
Theorem

6.3
, since

c_{K_{k+1}}(\gamma)\geqslant\min\left\{\Omega\left(\frac{\gamma^{2/(k+1)}}{\log\frac{1}{\gamma}}\right),\frac{1}{8}c_{K_{k}}\left(\frac{\gamma}{8k^{k}}\right)\right\}=\min\left\{\Omega\left(\frac{\gamma^{2/4}}{\log\frac{1}{\gamma}}\right),\Omega_{k}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\gamma}}{(\log\frac{1}{\gamma})^{2}}\right)\right\},

and both terms are at least
\Omega_{k+1}(\sqrt{\gamma}/(\log\frac{1}{\gamma})^{2})
, completing the induction. Finally, for a general graph
H
with
h
vertices, we see by
Proposition

6.4
that

c_{H}(\gamma)\geqslant c_{K_{h}}(h^{-h}\gamma)=\Omega_{H}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\gamma}}{(\log\frac{1}{\gamma})^{2}}\right).\qed

7.
Concluding remarks

As discussed in the introduction, our iterative approach to Nikiforov’s theorem is closely connected to techniques used in many other problems, and this suggests that these connections should be explored further. For example, the resemblance between our technique and the one used to bound multicolor Ramsey numbers
[
1
]
suggests that it may be useful to attack a multicolor Ramsey-theoretic variant of Nikiforov’s theorem proposed in
[
14
, Conjecture 1.6]
. Moreover, in the setting of regularity lemmas, such iterative proofs can often be “reverse engineered” to show that the bound they give is optimal; this was first achieved in the breakthrough work of Gowers
[
16
]
, and his technique has been adapted and extended to a wide variety of problems, e.g.
[
15
,
13
,
12
,
25
,
24
,
10
,
19
,
23
,
20
,
5
,
17
]
. Although we have not succeeded in doing the analogous thing here, it is conceivable that by reverse-engineering our proof, one could show that, for example,
c_{K_{3}}(\gamma)\leqslant\gamma^{\Omega(1)}
, demonstrating that random graphs are very far from extremal in Nikiforov’s theorem.

Indeed, the most interesting question about this topic remains open, namely whether the true behavior of
c_{H}(\gamma)
is polynomial or sub-polynomial. In case
H
is triangle-free, the result of
[
14
]
shows that the true value is
c_{H}(\gamma)=\Theta_{H}(1/{\log\frac{1}{\gamma}})
, but for any other
H
there is a wide gap between the bounds

\gamma^{1/2+o(1)}\leqslant c_{H}(\gamma)\leqslant O_{H}\left(\frac{1}{\log\frac{1}{\gamma}}\right),

where the lower bound is
Theorem

1.2
and the upper bound comes from considering a random graph. Given the result of
[
14
]
, it is natural to conjecture that the upper bound is closer to the truth, but we are not sure whether to believe this conjecture: as discussed above, it is natural to try to “reverse-engineer” our iterative proof to obtain a polynomial upper bound
c_{H}(\gamma)\leqslant\gamma^{\Omega(1)}
. While we have been unsuccessful in doing this, we believe this is a problem that deserves further attention.

Another promising direction is to take advantage of the apparent similarity between our technique and the book algorithm
[
4
]
. Recall that the main difficulty we encounter is that when we select a vertex
c^{*}\in C
and define
A^{+}=N(c^{*})\cap A,B^{+}=N(c^{*})\cap B
, the density
d(A^{+},B^{+})
may be smaller than
d(A,B)
. This is the main effect hurting us in our iterative algorithm, and much of the argument is about trying to limit its harm.

This is essentially the same issue one encounters when trying to prove upper bounds on diagonal Ramsey numbers with the book algorithm. Note that this problem can be described as a “negative correlation” phenomenon between the neighborhoods of different vertices. Indeed, the only way for
d(A^{+},B^{+})
to be substantially smaller than
d(A,B)
is if the neighborhoods of vertices in
C
are biased away from the edges in
E(A,B)
. In
[
1
]
, Balister et al. introduced a novel way of controlling the effect of such negative correlations, via their so-called
geometric lemma
. Roughly speaking, they show that if there are a lot of these negative correlations, one is able to focus on a large subgraph where one actually has substantial
positive
correlation. It would be very interesting if one could adapt their technique to our setting, and ideally to thus obtain a better bound on
c_{H}(\gamma)
.

Finally, we note that, like most prior works on this subject
[
27
,
26
,
28
,
11
]
(but notably not
[
14
]
),
Theorem

1.2
assumes that
N
is sufficiently large with respect to
\gamma
. As discussed in detail in
[
14
, Section 1.2]
, it would also be of great interest to prove such results without this assumption, i.e. to allow
\gamma
to tend to
0
as a function of
N
. Our proof shows that
Theorem

1.2
holds when
N\geqslant 2^{C_{H}\gamma^{-11}}
for some constant
C_{H}
depending on
H
, or equivalently that one can let
\gamma
tend to
0
as any function slower than
(\log N)^{-1/11}
and still obtain meaningful results. This is unfortunately too slow for most applications, e.g.
[
28
, Problem 3]
or
[
6
, Conjecture 1]
(and in any case the bound of
\gamma^{1/2+o(1)}
is too weak for these applications). We refer to
[
14
, Section 1.2]
for more information on this point.

Appendix A
Proof of Lemma

In this section, we present the proof of
Lemma

6.1
. Before doing so, we recall a number of concepts and results related to Szemerédi’s regularity lemma. Given
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, a pair of vertex sets
(S,T)
(not necessarily disjoint) in a graph
F
is said to be
\varepsilon
-regular if
\lvert d(S^{\prime},T^{\prime})-d(S,T)\rvert<\varepsilon
for all
S^{\prime}\subseteq S,T^{\prime}\subseteq T
with
\lvert S^{\prime}\rvert\geqslant\varepsilon\lvert S\rvert,\lvert T^{\prime}\rvert\geqslant\varepsilon\lvert T\rvert
. Suppose now that
F
is
r
-partite, with
r
-partition
V=V_{1}\sqcup\dotsb\sqcup V_{r}
. A
cylinder

K
is a set of the form
W_{1}\times\dotsb W_{r}
, where
W_{i}\subseteq V_{i}
for all
i\in[r]
. For such a cylinder
K
, let
V_{i}(K)=W_{i}
. We say that
K
is
\varepsilon
-regular
if
(W_{i},W_{j})
is
\varepsilon
-regular for all
1\leqslant i<j\leqslant r
. A

\mathcal{K}
is a partition of
V_{1}\times\dotsb\times V_{r}
into cylinders, and we say that
\mathcal{K}
is
\varepsilon
-regular
if at most an
\varepsilon
-fraction of the

-tuples
(v_{1},\ldots,v_{r})\in V_{1}\times\dotsb\times V_{r}
are not in
\varepsilon
-regular cylinders of

. The following weak regularity lemma of Duke, Lefmann, and Rödl says that every

-partite graph has an

Lemma A.1

(Duke–Lefmann–Rödl)
.

For every
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
and integer

, there exists
\beta=\beta(\varepsilon,r)>0
such that the following holds. Suppose that
F=(V,E)
is an
r
-partite graph with an
r
-partition
V_{1}\sqcup\dotsb\sqcup V_{r}
. Then there exists an
\varepsilon
-regular cylinder partition
\mathcal{K}
of
V_{1}\times\dotsb\times V_{r}
such that
|V_{i}(K)|\geqslant\beta|V_{i}|
for each
K\in\mathcal{K}
and
i\in[r]
. Moreover, we may take
\beta\geqslant\varepsilon^{r^{2}\varepsilon^{-5}}
.

We will also need the following standard counting lemma.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
H
be a graph on vertex set
[t]
. Suppose
V_{1},\ldots,V_{t}
are (not necessarily distinct) vertex sets in a graph
G
such that
(V_{i},V_{j})
is
\varepsilon
-regular for all
\{i,j\}\in E(H)
. Then the number of tuples
(v_{1},\ldots,v_{t})\in V_{1}\times\dotsb\times V_{t}
such that
\{v_{i},v_{j}\}\in E(G)
whenever
\{i,j\}\in E(H)
is

\left(\prod_{\{i,j\}\in E(H)}d(V_{i},V_{j})\pm\varepsilon\binom{t}{2}\right)\prod_{i=1}^{t}\lvert V_{i}\rvert.

Proof of
Lemma

6.1
.

Let
\varepsilon=\gamma^{2}/(9\binom{k+2}{2})
.
Let
\beta=\beta(\varepsilon,k+1)
be the constant from
Lemma

A.1
, and let
\zeta=\beta\gamma/e^{k+1}
. Note that
\zeta\geqslant\gamma^{k^{50}\gamma^{-10}}
, as claimed.

We now choose a uniformly random partition of
V(G)
into
k+1
parts, by assigning each vertex independently and uniformly to one of the
k+1
parts. The probability that a given
K_{k+1}
in
G
spans the
k+1
parts is exactly
(k+1)!/(k+1)^{k+1}
. Linearity of expectation then implies that there exists a
(k+1)
-partite subgraph of
G
with parts
V_{1},V_{2},\ldots,V_{k+1}
which contains at least
\gamma(k+1)!(N/(k+1))^{k+1}\geqslant\gamma(k+1)!\prod_{i=1}^{k+1}\lvert V_{i}\rvert
copies of
K_{k+1}
. This in particular implies that
\min_{i}\lvert V_{i}\rvert\geqslant\gamma(k+1)!N/(k+1)^{k+1}\geqslant\gamma N/e^{k+1}
, since the total number of
K_{k+1}
in this subgraph is at most
N^{k}\min_{i}\lvert V_{i}\rvert
. Let
F
be the
(k+1)
-partite subgraph of
G
with these parts. By

A.1
, there is an
\varepsilon
-regular cylinder partition
\mathcal{K}
of
V_{1}\times\dotsb\times V_{k+1}
, with
\lvert V_{i}(K)\rvert\geqslant\lfloor\beta\lvert V_{i}\rvert\rfloor\geqslant\lfloor\beta\gamma N/e^{k+1}\rfloor=\lfloor\zeta N\rfloor
for all
K\in\mathcal{K}
and
i\in[k+1]
. By

A.2
, if
K
is an
\varepsilon
-regular cylinder, then the number of
K_{k+1}
contained in

is at most

\left(\prod_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant k+1}d(V_{i}(K),V_{j}(K))+\varepsilon\binom{k+1}{2}\right)\prod_{i=1}^{k+1}\lvert V_{i}(K)\rvert.

On the other hand, since at most an
\varepsilon
-fraction of the
(k+1)
-tuples in

are contained in non-

-regular cylinders, we find that the number of

contained in irregular cylinders is at most
\varepsilon\prod_{i}\lvert V_{i}\rvert
. Adding these two facts up over all the cylinders

, we find that the total number of

in

is at most

\varepsilon\prod_{i=1}^{k+1}\lvert V_{i}\rvert+\sum_{K\text{ $\varepsilon$-regular}}\left(\prod_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant k+1}d(V_{i}(K),V_{j}(K))+\varepsilon\binom{k+1}{2}\right)\prod_{i=1}^{k+1}\lvert V_{i}(K)\rvert.

On the other hand, we also know that this number is at least
\gamma(k+1)!\prod_{i}\lvert V_{i}\rvert
. Therefore, we find some
\varepsilon
-regular cylinder
K
which satisfies

(7)

\prod_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant k+1}d(V_{i}(K),V_{j}(K))\geqslant\gamma(k+1)!-\varepsilon-\varepsilon\binom{k+1}{2}\geqslant\gamma((k+1)!-1),

by our choice of

.
We can then rewrite (

) as

(8)

\prod_{i=1}^{k+1}\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant j\leqslant k+1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}d(V_{i}(K),V_{j}(K))\right)^{1/2}\geqslant\gamma((k+1)!-1).

Suppose without loss of generality that the parenthesized term in (
