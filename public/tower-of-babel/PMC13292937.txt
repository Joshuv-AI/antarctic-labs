Asymmetric Ru─O sites in self-activated catalysts for efficient electrochemical methanol oxidation and industrial-scale hydrogen generation
Authors: Yujun Guo, Xueqin Mu, Yifan Zhang, Zhengyang Liu, Shengchen Wang, Qinghua Zhang, Jiarui Yang, Zechao Zhuang, Jiayao Yuan, Shengjie Zhang, Suli Liu, Dingsheng Wang, Zhihui Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Electrochemical water splitting is a promising strategy for sustainable, large-scale hydrogen production. However, the commercialization of this technology is hindered by the sluggish kinetics and high overpotential of the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) at the anode, leading to elevated energy consumption. Replacing OER with the methanol oxidation reaction (MOR) offers a more energy-efficient alternative, yet the development of electrocatalysts that deliver high activity, selectivity, and long-term stability at ampere-level current densities remains a notable challenge. Here, we report an interface-engineered RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox [RuOx nanoparticles deposited on a Mo(Mn)Ox matrix] catalyst featuring Ru─O sites with an asymmetric coordination environment, which enables optimized electron transfer and stabilization of highly active Ru species. This structural innovation allows for efficient methanol electrooxidation at industrially relevant current densities [1000 milliamperes per square centimeter (mA cm−2) at 1.41 volts versus reversible hydrogen electrode], achieving high selectivity (>98% Faradaic efficiency) and low anodic potentials. The RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox-based two-electrode electrolyzer maintains 1000 mA cm−2 at a low cell voltage of 1.58 volts and remains stable for over 300 hours. Furthermore, in an anion exchange membrane electrolyzer, the catalyst demonstrates stable operation at 1000 mA cm−2 and a cell voltage of only 1.87 volts, highlighting its potential for industrial coproduction of green hydrogen and value-added chemicals. This work demonstrates how interface engineering at the atomic level can enable industrial-scale electrochemical processes with Ru-based catalysts, offering a scalable solution for the advancement of sustainable energy and chemical manufacturing.

INTRODUCTION

Amid the global shift toward sustainable energy infrastructures, hydrogen has emerged as a pivotal vector for future clean energy paradigms (1–3). Electrochemical water splitting (EWS), enabling the direct conversion of water into hydrogen and oxygen, represents a compelling approach for scalable green hydrogen production (2, 4). Nonetheless, achieving industrially relevant current densities exceeding 1000 mA cm−2 presents a persistent challenge, as it requires elevated cell voltages (1.8 to 2.4 V) that inherently destabilize catalytic centers (5–7). Such high overpotentials exacerbate dissolution and provoke dynamic changes in the oxidation states of active metals, engendering a fundamental compromise between catalytic stability and activity that transcends specific catalyst compositions (8, 9). Moreover, the advent of anion exchange membrane water electrolyzers (AEMWEs) imposes rigorous demands on catalyst architectures, particularly in terms of gas-liquid mass transport and electronic conductivity networks, challenges that remain unmet by current Ir-based catalysts (10–12). Oxygen, generated as a byproduct at the anode during conventional water electrolysis, has limited commercial value, thereby constraining the overall economic efficiency of hydrogen production systems (13, 14). This limitation can be addressed by hybrid electrolysis, which replaces oxygen evolution reaction (OER) with organic oxidation reactions to create a synergy of both reduced energy consumption and coproduction of valuable chemicals (15). While redox mediators have been explored to decouple hydrogen and oxygen evolution, their practical application is often hampered by high system complexity, elevated operational costs, and notable energy losses due to mediator overpotentials (16). In contrast, the aforementioned hybrid electrolysis approach, specifically replacing the energy-intensive OER with the methanol oxidation reaction (MOR), presents a more efficient and industrially viable pathway (17–19). Notably, MOR facilitates the generation of value-added formate (HCOO−) at the anode, while simultaneously simplifying system architecture and streamlining hydrogen purification processes, collectively enhancing the economic viability of the technology (5, 20). Despite these advantages, achieving high selectivity, elevated catalytic activity, and long-term operational stability at industrially relevant current densities remains a formidable challenge in advanced electrocatalyst design (21).

Ru-based catalysts have long been regarded as a more cost-effective alternative to Ir-based catalysts for OER, offering comparable activity at a lower cost (22–24). However, their stability at practical current densities (≥500 mA cm−2) is inadequate for AEMWEs (25). This instability stems from the high covalency of Ru─O bonds and the lattice oxygen mechanism, which lead to excessive oxidation of Ru sites and structural degradation of the catalysts (26, 27). To tackle this issue, recent research has highlighted two complementary strategies: suppressing the lattice oxygen mechanism through heteroatom doping or surface passivation to modulate Ru─O covalency (28), while simultaneously enhancing electron transfer via interface-engineered conductive networks to optimize reaction kinetics (29). In particular, interface-engineered thin-film architectures with a thickness of less than 5 nm have shown great potential for optimizing electron transfer dynamics (30). By creating conformal conductive networks between carriers and Ru species, enhanced metal-support electronic coupling can be achieved through orbital hybridization (31, 32). One promising approach in this regard is to regulate electron transfer dynamics through the design of micro/nanoscale conductive networks (33, 34). Constructing efficient charge transport channels between conductive carriers and metal species can strengthen metal-carrier interactions, thereby activating the catalytic potential of two-dimensional (2D) materials (35, 36). In addition, the Schottky barrier at the interface plays a crucial role in precisely controlling carrier concentration and directing charge flow, which in turn improves the kinetics of anodic oxidation reactions (37–39).

Therefore, the design of charge transport networks represents a promising approach to the development of efficient electrocatalysts for small organic molecule oxidation. In this study, an advanced RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox catalyst was developed, where RuOx nanoparticles are anchored on defect-rich Mo(Mn)Ox nanosheets. This configuration facilitates electron transfer dynamics within the catalyst structure, optimizing electronic configuration of Ru active sites to balance the adsorption of reaction intermediates. The RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox catalyst exhibits exceptional performance in anodic methanol electrooxidation, achieving low anodic potentials of 1.34 and 1.38 V at current densities of 100 and 500 mA cm−2, respectively. As a bifunctional catalyst, in alkaline methanol electrolyte, the RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox exhibited a low cell voltage of 1.50 V at an industrial-grade current density (500 mA cm−2) for simultaneous efficient oxidation of methanol, and Faradaic efficiency (FE) for generating HCOO− exceeds 98%. Furthermore, the catalyst demonstrates remarkable long-term stability in assembled AEMWEs, and the RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox-based catalyst sustains a current density of 1000 mA cm−2 at a cell voltage of only 1.87 V, maintaining performance for up to 42 hours at high current densities. In situ spectroscopy analysis and density functional theory (DFT) calculations confirm that asymmetric Ru─O sites serve as efficient charge-transfer channels between in situ–formed RuOx and the Mo(Mn)Ox matrix, stabilizing highly active Ru species. These findings provide critical insights into the design of efficient electrocatalysts for organic small molecule oxidation, offering a reliable strategy for system coupling and catalyst development.

RESULTS

Synthesis and structural characterization of RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox electrocatalyst

The RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox electrocatalyst was synthesized through a two-step process involving solvothermal treatment followed by high-temperature calcination. Similar synthetic methods were used to prepare the comparative samples (RuOx@MoOx, RuOx@MnOx, and RuOx). Despite the simultaneous mixing of precursors, the formation of the RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox heterostructure is guided by thermodynamic preferences, where Ru species selectively anchor onto the initially formed Mo(Mn)Ox substrate. This synthesis was systematically optimized by varying key parameters (precursor ratios, reaction time, and temperature) to ensure reproducibility and optimal performance (fig. S1). The schematic illustration in Fig. 1A details the synthesis route and atomic structure of RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox, in which RuOx nanoparticles are anchored onto the Mo(Mn)Ox substrate. Ru atoms located at the edges of Mo(Mn)Ox are exposed to unique chemical environments, with efficient electron transfer between Ru and Mo(Mn)Ox facilitated by bridging oxygen atoms, thereby generating asymmetric Ru─O sites. The x-ray diffraction (XRD) results demonstrated that the RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox composite formed a structure with low crystallinity, as illustrated in fig. S2 (40). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images (fig. S3) illustrate that this two-step synthesis method was successful in generating the active material on the nickel foam skeleton, which was tightly encapsulated. The high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) image in Fig. 1B revealed that these wrapped structures were 3D network structures comprising layers of nanosheets. The insets in Fig. 1B and fig. S4 illustrate the observation of nanosheet layers, with an average nanoparticle size of ~3.5 ± 0.5 nm. The high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images in Fig. 1C and fig. S5 provide clear evidence of lattice stripe spacings. Fast Fourier transform analysis (the inset in Fig. 1C) confirms that the 0.20-nm fringe corresponds to the (101) plane of RuO2, while the 0.23-nm fringe is assigned to the (101) plane of orthorhombic Mo(Mn)Ox (41, 42). These identified planes are among the multiple lattice orientations observed, confirming that the material is a mixture of phases with different crystal systems and space groups. However, the atomic arrangement of the RuOx nanoparticles was found to be substantially distorted in fig. S5, indicating an unsaturated coordination state of Ru. These distortions and coordinatively unsaturated sites induce charge redistribution, thereby optimizing reaction kinetics (43). Detailed analysis (as shown in Fig. 1, D and E) reveals that careful examination of the atomic intensity distribution at the interface uncovers prominent defect structures. These defects are mainly manifested as substantial fluctuations in the atomic column intensities at the interface between RuOx and Mo(Mn)Ox, as well as discontinuities in the lattice fringes. Further observation indicates that the atomic arrangement at the interface exhibits an asymmetric distribution of Ru─O─M (M═Mo or Mn) bonding. This asymmetric configuration is expected to modulate the local electronic structure, providing more abundant and energetically favorable active sites for electrochemical reactions, thereby enhancing the catalytic performance of the material. Geometric phase analysis (GPA) further provides quantitative insight into the local strain distribution at the RuOx/Mo(Mn)Ox interface. As illustrated in Fig. 1F, the εxy strain contour map reveals pronounced lattice distortions and inhomogeneous strain fields concentrated at the interface region. The compressive strain of ~20.6% is further confirmed by the reduced interatomic spacing (0.20 nm) along the RuO2 (101) direction compared to the standard value (0.25 nm). These strain variations are directly correlated with the previously observed atomic column intensity fluctuations and lattice discontinuities (41). Such interfacial strain typically arises from lattice mismatch and the presence of defect structures at the atomic level, which in turn alter the local atomic coordination environment. Energy dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (EDX; Fig. 1G) was used to further verify the elemental aggregation of the RuOx nanosheets and the homogeneous distribution of Mo and Mn elements in the Mo(Mn)Ox substrate. The inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) analysis demonstrated that the RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox sample contained ~0.3263 wt % Ru (table S1).

To further probe the chemical structure of RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox and contrast samples, x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was adopted to analyze the electronic states and the surface element composition of the RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox and contrast samples (fig. S6). As shown in Fig. 2A, the high-resolution Ru 3p spectrum of RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox consists of two pairs of peaks (44). The peaks located at about 484.9 eV (Ru 3p1/2) and 462.7 eV (Ru 3p3/2) belong to the Ru4+, while the peaks located at about 486.9 eV (Ru 3p1/2) and 464.5 eV (Ru 3p3/2) correspond to the Ru3+, originating from gradient electron density distribution induced by Ru─O─Mo(Mn) asymmetric coordination (44). Quantitative analysis reveals that the ratio of Ru3+ to Ru4+ is ~1:1, a mixed-valence state that is conducive to facilitating electron transfer and enhancing catalytic kinetics (table S2). For high-resolution Mo 3d XPS spectra, two peaks are observed between 230.0 and 240.0 eV (Fig. 2B), which are attributed to Mo6+ (45). The Mo 3d spectrum exhibits a 0.14-eV negative binding energy shift upon Mn incorporation, confirming electron transfer via Mo → Mn pathway that generates electron-enriched defect sites (8). For the Ni 2p XPS spectrum (fig. S7), two characteristic spin-orbit doublets are resolved in the 850.0 to 890.0 eV range. The dominant peaks at 855.2 eV (Ni 2p3/2) and 872.8 eV (Ni 2p1/2) with distinct satellite features confirm the coexistence of Ni3+, Ni2+, and metallic Ni0 species. Of note, the RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox structure shows a substantial reduction in Ni3+ content compared to reference materials. Figure 2C reveals the coexistence of multiple Mn oxidation states (Mn2+, Mn3+, and Mn4+), confirming the presence of both MoOx and MnOx species in the composite. The peak of RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox shifts to higher binding energy compared to that of RuOx@MnOx, implying electric interaction. This charge migration within the substrate material is a consequence of the induced effect of defect sites that attract and anchor Ru─O (46). These defects electrostatically anchor Ru─O units, forming coordinated Ru─O─Mo configurations. The structural asymmetry is further substantiated by O 1s spectral shifts (0.25-eV negative drift; Fig. 2D), where interfacial oxygen atoms acquire excess electrons to establish Ruδ+─Oδ−─Moδ+ charge-transfer channels (47).

Furthermore, Raman spectroscopy (Fig. 2E) detects substantial Mo─O bending mode broadening at 382.1 cm−1, offering definitive evidence for the structural integration and distortion inherent to the ternary oxide system (48). The distorted configuration synergizes with electronic reconstruction to enhance mass transfer efficiency through superhydrophilicity (contact angle of 0° versus 128.1° for bare Ni foam), as shown in Fig. 2F. The superhydrophilic nature of the material assists in improving the affinity and permeability of the methanol-containing KOH electrolyte, thereby enhancing the performance of the catalyst in practical applications (49). Work function (Φ) analysis (Fig. 2, G and H) demonstrates directional electron transfer from Mo(Mn)Ox (Φ = 4.55 eV) to RuOx (Φ = 4.62 eV), elevating the work function of RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox to 4.83 eV. Notably, this composite work function exceeds that of both constituent oxides, deviating from the classical Fermi level alignment model. This anomaly is attributed to the formation of a strong interfacial dipole at the RuOx/Mo(Mn)Ox junction, where pronounced charge transfer and interfacial polarization synergistically shift the vacuum level (50). As illustrated in Fig. 2I, this interfacial electronic reconstruction results in the realignment of the Fermi energy levels. The increased work function stabilizes the Ru sites and modulates intermediate adsorption, thereby enhancing the catalytic performance (50).

Enhanced MOR activity and durability of RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox electrocatalyst

Benefiting from the unique interfacial structure, the RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox catalyst exhibits outstanding MOR performance. To further explore the MOR performance, three-electrode electrochemical tests were performed, with a Hg/HgO electrode used as the reference electrode. Figure 3A presents the linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) curves of different catalysts in 1.0 M KOH. In the absence of methanol, the RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox catalyst reaches the anodic current density of 100 mA cm−2 at the potential of 1.43 V [versus reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE)]. Notably, an oxidation peak appears at ~1.30 V, which is attributed to the Ni(OH)2/NiOOH redox transition characteristic of the nickel foam substrate (51). As shown in Fig. 3B, when introducing 2.0 M MeOH, the current density increases markedly, and the anodic potential decreases substantially to 1.38 V (versus RHE) at 500 mA cm−2, which is 120 mV lower than that of OER. Crucially, the Ni(OH)2/NiOOH redox peak disappears, as the MOR catalyzed by RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox initiates at lower potentials and effectively outcompetes the nickel oxidation, demonstrating superior methanol oxidation selectivity and activity (52). Figure 3C and table S3 provide a clearer comparison of RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox, RuOx@MoOx, and RuOx@MnOx regarding Tafel plots (fig. S8), potentials at different current densities (E100, E500, and E1000), and j@1.40 V. RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox requires a potential of merely 1.38 V (versus RHE) to reach 500 mA cm−2, whereas RuOx@MoOx and RuOx@MnOx require substantially higher potentials of 1.41 and 1.43 V, respectively. Moreover, as shown in fig. S8, the RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox also has the lowest Tafel slope (199.5 mV decade−1) among these three catalysts, indicating fast MOR kinetics for the RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox. RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox can reach 877 mA cm−2 current density at 1.4 V, which is much higher than RuOx@MoOx (329 mA cm−2) and RuOx@MnOx (184 mA cm−2). Notably, even after normalization by the electrochemical active surface area (figs. S9 and S10), RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox still delivers the highest specific activity. Furthermore, in the three-electrode configuration, RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox maintains excellent performance when compared with the MOR system (table S4). Figure 3D and fig. S11 show that the FE (HCOO−) of RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox reaches over 98% at various applied potentials (1.43 to 1.83 V). The few instances where FE marginally surpassed 100% are likely due to analytical uncertainties, and all values are now reported as the mean of triplicate measurements with corresponding error bars. The HCOO− is produced in the MOR, and the production rate of HCOO− gradually increased with increasing anodic potential. As quantitatively demonstrated in Fig. 3E, the RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox maintains exceptional electrochemical stability across current density perturbations from 100 to 500 mA cm−2. The post–stability HAADF-STEM characterization provides multidimensional evidence for the structural integrity of RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox catalysts. In Fig. 3F, after 24 hours of stability, there are Raman peaks of β-NiOOH appearing at two frequencies: 476 and 550 cm−1. These signals originate from the in situ electrochemical oxidation of the nickel foam substrate under alkaline conditions, which is a well-documented phenomenon for Ni-based current collectors during anodic polarization. These spectral features are assigned to the characteristic vibrational signatures of Ni─O bonds in the NiOOH lattice, specifically corresponding to bending (δNi─O) and stretching (νNi─O) modes (53). This reflects the formation and stability of β-NiOOH during methanol oxidation. After the long-term stability test, XPS analysis (fig. S12) still clearly distinguished the characteristic peaks of Ru3+ and Ru4+ dual states in the RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox catalysts, which corroborates the stabilizing effect of the Ru─O─Mo(Mn) structure on the Ru active center. Notably, compared to the initial state, the Ru 3p3/2 binding energy is shifted to the high-energy direction by about 0.33 eV, indicating a decrease in the electron density of the Ru site (part of the oxidation state is enriched toward Ru4+), and this dynamic electronic regulation further validates the RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox stability. The post-operando HAADF-STEM characterization (Fig. 3, G and H) reveals exceptional structural preservation of the RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox catalyst following 24-hour stability testing under harsh reaction conditions. Quantitative particle size distribution analysis demonstrates remarkable consistency, with the mean particle diameter measured as 2.2 ± 0.1 nm after testing. High-resolution imaging reveals well-defined lattice fringes with measured spacings of 0.23 nm, corresponding to MoOx. The RuOx domains maintain their structural integrity with preserved local atomic arrangements even after prolonged stability testing.

Based on the excellent MOR and hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) performance of RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox (fig. S13), two electrode tests (RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox || RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox) were performed in a single electrolytic cell. The notably low HER overpotential of RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox, as evidenced by its smaller Tafel slope compared to Pt/C, indicates a Volmer-Tafel mechanism with accelerated kinetics under alkaline conditions, which substantially contributes to the overall cell performance (6). As illustrated in Fig. 4A, the RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox || RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox electrode pair achieves a current density of 100 mA cm−2 at 1.40 V for MOR || HER, which is lower than those of the EWS. Upon methanol addition, the cell voltage required to reach a current density of 500 mA cm−2 decreases by 170 mV from 1.67 V, underscoring the synergistic enhancement in both MOR and HER efficiencies. Figure 4B and table S5 demonstrate that RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox surpasses many catalysts reported in the literature. The strategic construction of an interface-engineered planar conductive network within RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox substantially enhances electron transport efficiency through shortened carrier migration pathways. As depicted in Fig. 4C, the RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox || RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox electrode pair exhibits remarkable long-term stability in a methanol-containing KOH electrolyte. This stability is crucial for practical applications, as it ensures sustainable electrochemical performance over extended periods. Figure 4D reveals that the FE value obtained using the drainage method (fig. S14) for HER is close to 100%, confirming that MOR effectively suppresses the OER. This selective catalytic behavior is highly desirable as it directs the electrochemical reaction toward hydrogen production rather than oxygen evolution, thereby improving the overall efficiency of the system. As shown in Fig. 4E, the FE of RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox exceeds 95% in the potential range of 1.4 to 1.9 V, and the yield of HCOO− gradually increases with increasing potential. The inset further illustrates the production of HCOO−, indicating the potential of this electrochemical system for generating high-value chemicals.

To evaluate its economic feasibility in a two-electrode system, an AEMWE was assembled using the RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox catalyst as both the anode and cathode, with a working area of 0.5 cm2 (Fig. 4F, inset). A homogenous electrolyte (1.0 M KOH + 2.0 M MeOH) was circulated in the AEMWEs via a peristaltic pump (fig. S15). Figure 4F shows that MOR commences at 1.5 V and achieves a current density of 1000 mA cm−2 at 1.87 V in the AEMWEs. Furthermore, it outperforms RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox || Pt/C and RuO2 || Pt/C, especially some other catalyst combinations for AEMWEs that have been reported recently (Fig. 4G and table S6). As depicted in Fig. 4H, the RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox catalyst exhibits almost no attenuation over 42 hours of testing at a high current density of 200 mA cm−2. To rigorously evaluate the electrode configuration, the comparative experiments were conducted using a mixed electrode (RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox || Pt/C). The symmetric RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox || RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox system demonstrated superior activity and stability compared to this Pt-containing benchmark, underscoring its exceptional performance. Post–stability analysis via ICP-OES (table S7) revealed that Ru dissolution occurred primarily within the initial 5 hours, after which the concentration in the electrolyte substantially decreased, indicating catalyst surface stabilization. This minimal dissolution behavior, coupled with the observed electrochemical stability, underscores the durability of the catalyst under continuous operation. A key finding from post–operation analysis is that SEM and attenuated total reflectance Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR) comparisons (figs. S16 and S17) confirmed that the AEM remained undamaged upon HCOO− production, ensuring long-term catalytic durability and membrane integrity. To further substantiate the industrial applicability of catalysts, the long-term constant-current electrolysis test was performed at 1000 mA cm−2. As presented in fig. S18, the symmetric RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox || RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox cell exhibited stable operation and negligible degradation over a 10-hour period at this industrially relevant current density.

In situ investigation and theoretical insights into asymmetric Ru─O sites

Asymmetric Ru─O sites serve as efficient charge-transfer channels between in situ–formed RuOx and the Mo(Mn)Ox matrix, stabilizing highly active Ru species. Through in situ experimental investigations and theoretical computations, we further elucidated the charge-transfer channels of asymmetric Ru─O sites on RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox catalysts (Fig. 5A). As demonstrated in fig. S19, the RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox catalyst exhibits exceptional charge-transfer kinetics in both alkaline and methanol-supplemented electrolytes. Within the operational potential window of 1.05 to 1.55 V versus RHE in 1.0 M KOH, the substantially reduced Nyquist semicircle diameter corresponds to remarkably low charge transfer resistance, indicating accelerated interfacial electron transfer kinetics (51). Notably, methanol-containing electrolyte testing reveals further optimization of charge transport properties, and the semicircle radius decreases compared to that in aqueous electrolyte. The consistency between impedance behavior and catalytic performance metrics confirms the dual-functional nature of the RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox interface in simultaneously promoting charge transfer and methanol-oxidation stability. Bode plot analysis of the OER revealed frequency-dependent charge transfer characteristics. As shown in fig. S20, the low-frequency region (0.1 to 105 Hz) near the OER onset potential exhibited a phase angle approaching 60°, with minimal potential dependence observed in the 1.05 to 1.25 V range. This suggests that the OER process is governed by a mixed control mechanism, where both charge transfer kinetics and mass diffusion collectively influence the reaction dynamics (54). The intermediate phase angle between purely resistive (0°) and capacitive (90°) behavior correlates directly with the surface reconstruction of metal oxides during oxygen evolution. When the potential increased to 1.35 V, the characteristic frequency corresponding to the phase angle peak shifted from 0.1 to 1 Hz, accompanied by an increased time constant. This evolution aligns with the non-Faradaic oxidation regime in OER polarization curves, indicating substantial alterations in the catalyst’s oxidation state that impede charge transfer. Conversely, elevating the potential from 1.45 to 1.55 V induced a marked decrease in phase angle with concurrent high-frequency shifting. These changes signify enhanced surface activation of the RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox catalyst at elevated potentials, facilitating improved OER kinetics and more efficient charge transfer processes. For the MOR involving multi-intermediate transitions (CH3OH* → CH3O* → CH2O* → CHO* → HCOO*), the RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox catalyst demonstrated pronounced potential-dependent behavior in Bode plots. As illustrated in Fig. 5B, progressive phase angle reduction occurred with increasing potential (1.05 to 1.55 V), exhibiting a substantially faster attenuation rate compared to OER. This rapid impedance decrease suggests enhanced methanol adsorption/activation capabilities at higher potentials, accelerating intermediate formation/transformation and resulting in superior charge transfer efficiency and reaction rates during MOR (54).

The distinct C─O stretching vibration at 1465 cm−1 confirms the formation of surface-bound C─O intermediates, providing direct experimental evidence that C─O-type species act as dominant MOR intermediates, consistent with the reaction pathways illustrated in Fig. 5C and fig. S23 and S24) identified structural evolution through characteristic vibrational signatures: the 497 cm−1 peak corresponding to Ni─O─OH lattice vibrations, a bridge-oxygen mode at 602 cm−1 reflecting oxygen sublattice dynamics, the 815 cm−1 band indicative of molecular oxygen complexes, and a δH─OH bending vibration at 1636 cm−1 arising from interfacial water reorganization (55). Concurrent in situ electrochemical ATR-FTIR studies (figs. S25 and S26) mapped the complete MOR pathway through spectral evolution: initial detection of HCOO* intermediates via asymmetric carboxylate stretching (νCOO at 1585 cm−1), subsequent CO2 generation evidenced by the antisymmetric CO2 vibration (νCO2 at 2345 cm−1), and final carbonate/bicarbonate formation through CO2 desorption and reaction with adsorbed hydroxy species (OHads), manifested as a broad 1376 cm−1 envelope corresponding to CO32−/HCO3− vibrational modes (56). These observations are consistent with our DFT analysis, providing experimental evidence for the key intermediates along the proposed reaction pathway. The synergistic application of Raman and ATR-FTIR spectroscopy thus provides a dual perspective: Raman elucidates catalyst structure and key C─O intermediates, while ATR-FTIR tracks the transformation of organic species.

The adsorption of methanol in alkaline electrolyte constitutes a critical step for electrocatalytic methanol oxidation. Open-circuit potential (OCP) measurements (Fig. 5D) reveal distinct methanol adsorption characteristics across different catalysts. Upon introduction of 2.0 M MeOH, RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox exhibits a substantial OCP decrease (ΔV = 0.40 V), markedly surpassing RuOx@MoOx (ΔV = 0.045 V) and RuOx@MnOx (ΔV = 0.37 V). This pronounced potential shift demonstrates superior methanol adsorption capacity on the RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox interface compared to its monometallic counterparts. Complementary DFT calculations systematically probe the catalytic mechanism through a structural model featuring Ru nanoclusters anchored on Mo─Mn composite oxide substrates (Fig. 5E) (57). The differential charge density maps derived from these models provide direct visualization of the interfacial charge redistribution, revealing distinct regions of electron accumulation (yellow) and depletion (cyan) at the Ru─O─Mo(Mn) interface. This pattern unequivocally demonstrates that electrons are transferred from the Ru nanoclusters toward the Mo─Mn oxide substrate, confirming the existence of an efficient charge transfer channel across the asymmetric Ru─O sites. The pronounced interfacial electronic coupling rationalizes the enhanced methanol adsorption and the superior catalytic activity observed experimentally (6). Comparative adsorption free energy calculations at 0.016 V versus RHE (Fig. 5F) quantify preferential methanol binding, with ΔGads = −1.97 eV for CH3OH* versus −1.72 eV for H2O*, quantitatively validating the OCP observations (5). This adsorption selectivity originates from interfacial electronic restructuring in RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox, which optimizes surface electron configuration for methanol coordination while facilitating subsequent oxidative processes. The synergistic metal-oxide interactions create favorable d-band alignment, lowering the energy barrier for methanol activation compared to water molecules. Further projected electronic structure characterization via density of states (PDOS) analysis (Fig. 5G) reveals critical d-band modifications in the RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox heterointerface. The d-band center of metallic Ru shifts negatively to −1.70 eV, 0.31 eV lower than pristine RuO2 (−1.39 eV). This downward displacement optimizes adsorption strength toward reaction intermediates (CH3OH*) while establishing efficient charge transport pathways between conductive carriers and metallic active sites. Theoretical simulations confirm a five-step sequential dehydrogenation pathway at Ru centers: CH3OH* → CH3O* → CH2O* → CHO* → HCOO*. Thermodynamic analysis based on reaction free energy profiles calculated at a potential relevant to MOR (U = 0.016 V versus RHE) demonstrates enhanced CH3OH* adsorption capability during the initial reaction stages compared to conventional RuO2 (Fig. 5H). Notably, the potential-limiting step differs between the two systems. For RuO2, the most uphill step is the CHO* → HCOO* transformation, with a Gibbs free energy increase of 1.57 eV. In contrast, for the RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox heterostructure, the potential-limiting step shifts to the initial CH3OH* → CH3O* dehydrogenation, exhibiting a substantially lower ΔG of 0.74 eV, while the subsequent CHO* → HCOO* step shows a substantially reduced barrier of 0.45 eV. This marked reduction in the overall energy barrier underscores the critical role of the asymmetric Ru─O─Mo(Mn) interfacial structure in stabilizing key intermediates and facilitating the catalytic cycle. Supplementary calculations performed at U = 0 V versus RHE confirm that the identified reaction pathway and its energetic advantage are maintained across this potential range (fig. S27).

More meaningfully, a comparative analysis of energy consumption and economic feasibility was conducted for the MOR-assisted HER system versus conventional water electrolysis (5). Although the MOR-HER system operates at a lower cell voltage (1.46 versus 1.60 V at ~200 mA cm−2), the total energy consumption per kilogram of H2 is higher (67.0 versus 42.6 kWh) due to the reaction enthalpy of methanol oxidation, resulting in a lower overall energy efficiency (58.8 versus 85.4%). A practical factor that must be considered is that the subsequent acidification and purification steps required to convert the formate product into commercial-grade formic acid would introduce additional process complexity and cost. However, the economic assessment demonstrates that even when accounting for this downstream processing cost, the coproduction of valuable HCOO−, coupled with lower electricity costs due to the reduced operating voltage, leads to a substantially higher estimated profit margin of ~$3.27 per kilogram of H2 for the MOR-HER pathway. In contrast, the profit for the OER-HER pathway, where oxygen is the sole byproduct, is only $0.99 per kilogram of H2. This analysis indicates that the MOR-HER strategy compensates for its lower final energy efficiency by generating high-value chemicals, achieving a decisive advantage in overall economic viability and positioning it as a highly attractive route for integrated chemical and energy production.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we successfully developed an in situ–grown RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox catalyst featuring asymmetric Ru─O sites as bifunctional electrocatalysts, which facilitate H2 production and convert methanol to valuable HCOO− simultaneously. The critical Ru─O─Mo(Mn)Ox interfacial structure not only facilitates strong interfacial contact but also serves as efficient charge-transfer channels between in situ–formed RuOx and the Mo(Mn)Ox matrix, stabilizing highly active Ru species. These structural and electronic adaptations are pivotal in achieving outstanding catalytic performance. During MOR, the RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox exhibited low anodic potentials of 1.38 and 1.41 V at 500 and 1000 mA cm−2. Notably, in a two-electrode system using RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox for both cathode and anode, it maintained a high-performance anodic potential of 1.58 V at 1000 mA cm−2. The integration of this catalyst into an AEMWE demonstrated stable operation for over 42 hours at industrial current densities. In situ characterizations and DFT calculations reveal that the asymmetric Ru─O─Mo(Mn) interfacial structure not only optimizes the adsorption of intermediates but also enhances the desorption of HCOO− products from Ru sites. This dual functionality substantially enhances catalytic activity by leveraging the adaptive stabilization of the Ru electronic configuration. This work provides a promising strategy for designing electrocatalysts for organic small molecule oxidation and offers valuable insights into their system integration, potentially advancing the development of efficient electrochemical energy conversion devices. It should be noted, however, that although the theoretical standard potential of MOR is substantially lower than that of OER, the practical applied potentials required for MOR in our system are comparable to those for OER. This indicates that the expected reduction in overall energy input is not fully realized under current experimental conditions, likely due to kinetic and catalytic limitations. Therefore, further research is needed to develop more active and selective catalysts that can fully exploit the intrinsic energy-saving potential of MOR-assisted electrolysis.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Synthesis of RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox

The synthesis of RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox used a solvothermal approach. Initially, nickel foam measuring 2 cm by 3 cm was sequentially ultrasonicated in 1.0 M HCl (20 ml), absolute ethanol (20 ml), and deionized water (20 ml) for 15 min each to remove surface oxides and organic contaminants. After cleaning, the nickel foam was dried in a vacuum oven at 40°C.

Following this, in a 50-ml beaker, 30 ml of N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF; anhydrous, 99.8%) was used as the solvent. The following reagents were dissolved in sequence under continuous magnetic stirring: RuCl3·3H2O (1.0 mM), Na2MoO4·2H2O (1.0 mM), and MnCl2·4H2O (0.5 mM). Stirring was maintained at 400 rpm for 2 hours at room temperature until a homogeneous solution was obtained.

The cleaned nickel foam was immersed into the precursor solution, which was then transferred into a 50 ml Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave. The autoclave was sealed and heated in a programmable oven at 180°C for 24 hours. After the reaction, the autoclave was allowed to cool naturally to room temperature.

The dried precursor was annealed in a muffle furnace under static air. The temperature was raised from room temperature to 350°C at a heating rate of 4°C/min, held at 350°C for 2 hours, and then cooled naturally to room temperature. This method was similarly used to prepare RuOx@MoOx (without MnCl2·4H2O), RuOx@MnOx (without Na2MoO4·2H2O), and RuOx (without MnCl2·4H2O and Na2MoO4·2H2O).

Material characterizations

The synthesized materials’ atomic structures were examined with a JEOL ARM 200 CF TEM operating at 200 kV, featuring dual spherical aberration correctors. HAADF images were acquired using an acceptance angle ranging from 90 to 370 mrad. The STEM was equipped with a DCOR+ spherical aberration corrector for the electron probe, aligned using a standard gold sample before observation. EDX mapping was performed on the same JEOL ARM 200 CF TEM/STEM system equipped with an EDX detector. SEM images were obtained using a JEOL JSM-7800F microscope to investigate the surface morphology and microstructure of the catalysts. XPS measurements used a scanning x-ray microprobe powered by Al Kα radiation (PHI 5000 Versa, ULACPHI, Inc.), referencing the binding energies of various elements against the C 1s peak at 284.6 eV. Surface wettability of the catalysts was assessed through contact angle tests using a Contact AM Drop Shape Analyzer (DSA25S). The metal concentration was determined through ICP-OES using a Thermo Fisher Scientific iCAP PRO system. Powder XRD patterns were obtained using a D/max 2500VL/PC diffractometer (Japan) equipped with graphite-monochromatized Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.154060 nm), covering a 2θ range of 30° to 90°. The work function test was conducted using ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy measurements (Thermo Fisher Scientific ESCALAB XI+).

Electrocatalytic measurements

The electrocatalytic capabilities were assessed through a CHI760E electrochemical workstation, using a carbon rod as the counter electrode and an Hg/HgO electrode as the reference. The working electrode was 0.5 cm by 1 cm in size. Within the three-electrode configuration, the MOR electrolyte consisted of 1.0 M KOH + 2.0 M MeOH, whereas for the OER, 1.0 M KOH was used. In two-electrode experiments, electrolytic cells were filled with either 1.0 M KOH + 2.0 M methanol or 1.0 M KOH. Cyclic voltammetry was executed at a scanning speed of 100 mV/s, while LSV was conducted at 10 mV/s, including a 90% iR adjustment. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) spanned frequencies from 100 kHz to 0.1 Hz, using a 5-mV amplitude. All potentials were adjusted to the RHE through the Nernst equationERHE=Emeasure+0.098+0.059×pH(1)

AEMWE measurements

An AEMWE was constructed using an anode and cathode, each measuring 0.5 cm2, along with an anion-exchange membrane. The performance of this AEMWE was tested in a solution containing 1.0 M KOH + 2.0 M MeOH. Polarization curves were obtained at a scan rate of 10 mV/s, without correcting for internal resistance. Stability assessments in the 1.0 M KOH + 2.0 M MeOH solution were performed at a current density of 200 mA cm−2. The RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox electrocatalyst served directly as both the anode and cathode in the RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox || RuOx@Mo(Mn)Ox AEMWE. The mass loading for RuO2 and Pt/C was ~2 mg cm−2. During long-term stability test, to maintain consistent reaction conditions and minimize the potential influence of degradation products, the electrolyte (1.0 M KOH + 2.0 M MeOH) was completely replaced with a fresh solution every 24 hours.

In situ Raman test

Electrochemical Raman spectroscopy experiments were conducted in situ using a DXR2 Raman microscope from Thermo Fisher Scientific. A CHI760E electrochemical workstation was responsible for controlling and adjusting the applied potential (OCP and from 1.18 to 1.48 V). Raman spectra were collected inside a spectroelectrochemical quartz cell. Throughout the experiments, a glassy carbon electrode was used as the working electrode, while a Hg/HgO electrode acted as the reference, and a platinum wire served as the counter electrode. The chronoamperometry (i-t) method was used to capture spectral data of the catalyst reaction in solutions of 1.0 M KOH + 2.0 M MeOH and 1.0 M KOH at a constant potential. The applied potential varied between 1.18 and 1.48 V relative to RHE.

In situ ATR-FTIR test

ATR-FTIR measurements were performed on a Bruker Tensor 27 spectrometer equipped with a liquid nitrogen-cooled mercury cadmium telluride detector and a Si ATR crystal. The CHI760E electrochemical workstation was used to control the applied potential (from OCP to 1.48 V versus RHE). The catalyst layer was directly coated on the ATR crystal acting as the working electrode, with a Hg/HgO reference electrode and a platinum wire counter electrode completing the three-electrode setup. This ATR configuration was selected for its high surface sensitivity to monitor adsorbed intermediates and products under operational conditions. Spectral data were collected in solutions of 1.0 M KOH with and without 2.0 M MeOH, holding at each steady potential during measurement.

Faraday efficiency calculation

For MOR, nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy with dimethyl sulfoxide as an internal reference was used to analyze the electrolyte post i-t testing.
