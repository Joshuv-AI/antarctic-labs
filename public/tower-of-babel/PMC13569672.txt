Characterizing a Department's Transition to Intracapsular Tonsillectomy: A Mixed Methods Analysis
Authors: Erin Ling, Kelly Giroux, Ada C. Sher, Graham Pingree, Jemma Maynard, Makanko Komara, Isaac Kistler, Aidan Vanek, Alexandra Gach, William Meyer, Tatum Adamson, Kolby Quillin, Nathan Demian, Claire Galavotti, Swapna Koppera, Patrick Walz, Kris Jatana, Jonathan Grischkan, Charles Elmaraghy, Prasanth Pattisapu, Tendy Chiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesDespite many reports of reduced postoperative complications and equivalent long‐term outcomes compared to total tonsillectomy (TT), there is still limited adoption of intracapsular tonsillectomy (IT). Changing surgical techniques can be challenging, particularly for common procedures that already have good outcomes. To examine the barriers to adopting IT, we performed a mixed methods study of an otolaryngology department's transition from TT to IT, describing the timeline, postoperative outcomes, and surgeon‐specific motivators.MethodsWe used an explanatory sequential mixed methods model. Demographic and surgical data for all pediatric patients undergoing tonsillectomy between January 1, 2017 and December 31, 2022 at a single institution were included. Charts of patients who returned to the hospital within 30 days of tonsillectomy were reviewed. We then conducted semi‐structured interviews to qualitatively evaluate surgeon‐specific motivators for transitioning to IT.ResultsOver 6 years, 13,899 patients who underwent tonsillectomy met inclusion. The proportion of IT increased from 2.3% in 2017 to 80.8% in 2022. While 10% (n = 994) of TT patients returned to the ED within 30 days of tonsillectomy, only 3.1% (n = 133) of IT patients returned. Five themes were identified in the qualitative analysis, grouped into two motivating factors (Data Transparency and Individual Surgeon Experience) and three transition concerns (Interaction with Patient Families, Training and Skill Acquisition, and Impact on Partners).ConclusionWe define the prominent agents of surgical change for IT. Transition to newer, safer techniques is scalable in the setting of departmental data transparency and a culture that promotes best outcomes.Level of Evidence3

Introduction

Surgical innovation is challenging, especially in high‐volume procedures that already have good outcomes. Everett Rogers' “Diffusion of Innovations” theory describes five elements affecting the velocity of innovation uptake, including the innovation itself, adopters of the idea, communication channels, time, and the social system into which the innovation is introduced [1]. When applied to surgical innovation in particular, patient safety, organizational structure, and physician familiarity with the technique are common considerations when deciding to adopt a new idea [2, 3, 4, 5].

With more than 500,000 tonsillectomies performed in the U.S. annually, this procedure possesses significant inertia [6]. While tonsillectomies have historically been performed using the total tonsillectomy (TT) technique, the intracapsular tonsillectomy (IT) technique has gained popularity since its re‐examination in 2002 by Koltai et al. [7] In a 2021 survey of pediatric otolaryngologists, 20% reported currently performing IT—the “critical mass” threshold at which the authors predicted imminent and exponential adoption of the technique [8]. Proponents of IT cite comparable obstructive outcomes to TT with added benefits of decreased perioperative bleeding, postoperative bleeding, risk of surgical reintervention, postoperative pain, and a faster return to regular diet and activities [9, 10, 11, 12].

Despite evidence of superiority, there remains a significant proportion of otolaryngologists who are cautious to endorse the universal adoption of IT as the primary recommended technique. As of the 2019 American Academy of Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery Foundation (AAO‐HNSF) clinical practice guidelines, IT was not officially incorporated into the recommendations due to concerns regarding the generalizability of available data [13].

In the years since these recommendations were handed down, multiple studies have been published or are currently underway to address these concerns [14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. While these groups provide excellent data surrounding the long‐term efficacy of IT, few provide the multi‐year outcomes of both TT and IT within the same group of surgeons from a single institution. Moreover, without a qualitative assessment of surgeons' perspectives, the literature divorces outcomes from the underlying decision‐making.

This explanatory sequential mixed methods study aims to describe the department‐wide transition of a single institution from TT to IT over the course of 6 years. In addition to providing a clear timeline, we intend to quantitatively evaluate the postoperative outcomes of this transition by examining 30‐day post‐tonsillectomy complication rates. We then offer qualitative insights in the form of an interview series to evaluate surgeon‐specific impetus for change.

Materials and Methods

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Nationwide Children's Hospital (STUDY00000924 approved 6/24/2020 and STUDY00004828 approved 3/27/2025) with the need for written informed consent waived.

Quantitative Methods

Data Collection

Data were extracted from the electronic medical record (EMR) at Nationwide Children's Hospital using the following inclusion criteria: (1) one of four current procedural terminology (CPT) codes for tonsillectomy was completed (Table S1), (2) age 0–18 years old at time of surgery, (3) date of surgery between January 1, 2017 and December 31, 2022.

Demographic data (sex assigned at birth, race, insurance type, date of birth, age at surgery) and tonsillectomy data (date of surgery, tonsillectomy technique, ICD‐10 codes, preoperative diagnosis CPT codes) were extracted individually from the EMR if not provided in the original query. Additionally, dates of returns to the Emergency Department (ED) or of readmission within 30 days of surgery.

Surgical Procedure

Each child underwent IT or TT after being evaluated by a pediatric otolaryngologist. From 2017 to 2022, this single‐center department consisted of 12 pediatric otolaryngologists, all of whom performed both total and IT. Residents who were involved were closely monitored to ensure appropriate technique and consistent outcomes. The IT procedure was performed as described in previously published literature, with all surgeons using either plasma ablation or microdebrider [7, 14, 19]. The surgical endpoint of both techniques was equivalent: complete removal of tonsil tissue with preservation of the tonsil capsule overlying the pharyngeal musculature. Hemostasis was achieved with the cautery function of the cold ablation wand or with suction cautery.

Chart Review

A retrospective chart review of the patients who presented to the ED or were re‐admitted within 30 days of their tonsillectomy was conducted. These charts were reviewed by research assistants to determine (1) date of the return, (2) chief complaint, (3) whether the visit was tonsillectomy‐related, and (4) if the patient returned to the OR for control of a tonsillar hemorrhage.

Statistical Analysis

Demographic and clinical characteristics were summarized using count and percentage for categorical variables and median/interquartile range (IQR, 25th and 75th percentile) for continuous variables. All descriptive tables for patient characteristics are stratified by type of tonsillectomy received. Plots were constructed to visualize trends over time in tonsillectomy type and 30‐day outcomes by tonsillectomy type. Additionally, overall rates of these outcomes were presented along with reasons for tonsil‐related ED visit stratified by tonsillectomy type. Finally, plots were constructed to show percentages of each tonsillectomy over time by primary indication. Following best statistical practice and STROBE reporting guidelines, p‐values are not presented in descriptive tables. All analyses were performed using R version 4.2.2 [20, 21, 22, 23].

Qualitative Methods

Open‐ended, semi‐structured interviews were performed using a phenomenological approach. Pediatric otolaryngologists from our practice were purposively sampled to ensure diversity of practice experience and training. Purposive sampling is the practice of selecting interviewees based on their unique expertise to maximize the amount of information to be learned [24]. Interviews were conducted using a ten‐question interview guide and centered on details of surgeons' personal and departmental experience in transitioning from total to IT technique. Interviews were performed until thematic saturation was achieved, which was defined as no new themes identified in three consecutive interviews. Additional key informant interviews were conducted with the department chief and the physician director of quality.

Open coding of interview transcripts was completed by three independent reviewers to identify the primary themes that emerged from each interview. Axial coding was performed by two members of the study team. Two reviewers performed closed coding to confirm the presence of recurring themes.

Timeline

In 2017, IT accounted for only 2.3% of all tonsillectomies at our institution. The threshold of 50% IT was first crossed in March 2020, and by the end of 2022, IT accounted for 80.8% of all tonsillectomies, as illustrated in Figure 1.

Thirty‐Day Complications

In terms of 30‐day post‐tonsillectomy complications, IT patients experienced lower rates of ED presentations, readmissions, and secondary hemorrhage requiring OR control. The 30‐day return‐to‐ED rate for TT patients (10%, n = 994) was more than triple that of IT patients (3.1%, n = 133). When examining secondary hemorrhage rates within 30 days of tonsillectomy, only 0.5% (n = 21) of all IT patients experienced hemorrhage requiring return to the OR, compared to 3.3% (n = 317) of TT patients (Figure 2).

IT versus TT 30‐day complication rates. ED, emergency department; OR, operating room.

Revision tonsillectomy was performed in both IT and TT populations. Fourteen (0.3%) IT patients underwent revision tonsillectomy a median of 10.3 months (IQR: 8.6, 26.3) after first tonsillectomy. The rate of IT patients requiring revision tonsillectomy fell over time, with 1.5% (n = 1) of patients undergoing IT in 2017 eventually requiring revision, 1.0% (n = 1) in 2018, 0.7% (n = 3) in 2019, 0.6% (n = 6) in 2020, 0.1% (n = 1) in 2021, and 0.1% (n = 2) in 2022. Eleven of these patients presented with recurrence of obstructive symptoms.

While historically reserved for patients with obstructive symptoms, the indications for IT grew with the departmental transition. Between 2017 and 2019, all tonsillectomies performed for purely infective indications (ICD‐10 Codes: B950, J020, J0300, J0301‐Streptococcal related; J3503, J3501‐Chronic tonsillitis; J0380‐Acute tonsillitis due to other specified organisms) were TT. In 2022, however, 34.1% (n = 14) of the few tonsillectomies performed for purely infective indications were IT.

Data Transparency

Subjects most frequently highlighted the consistent sharing of surgical outcome data as a driving factor for change. Starting in 2020, as a means of optimizing transparency, physicians described receiving monthly summaries detailing both individual and group post‐tonsillectomy hemorrhage events. Several subjects vividly recalled learning through this transparent process that an early IT‐adopting partner had gone 6 months without a postoperative hemorrhage. There was unanimous agreement that systematic data tracking and distribution were the most influential factors driving individual surgeons' transitions to IT.

Individual Surgeon Experience

While quantitative transparency was a significant motivator for interviewees, anecdotal experience was also cited as a motivating factor. Many interviewees reported observing reduced pain in their patients postoperatively, as seen in the reduced frequency of calls from family and less opioid analgesic prescription. Some surgeons also described how families reported shorter overall recovery times following IT. A few surgeons commented on their comfort with perceived revision rates, usually in the context of being balanced by bleeding rates.

Interaction With Patient Families

Surgeons noted that adopting this technique has changed the way they approach preoperative discussions with families, as well as how families respond. As part of the informed consent process, all surgeons discussed the risk of tonsillectomy in general, including dehydration, pain, and bleeding rates. However, our surgeons shared that they now consistently recommend the intracapsular technique, often weighing the risk of postoperative bleeding against the risk of IT revision surgery in their discussion with families. There was variation in the degree to which IT revision rates were discussed, with surgeons allowing families to guide the conversation based on individual concerns as part of the shared decision‐making process. Most surgeons reported that their growing confidence that IT was the safest and most effective option for their patients led them to spend less time detailing both techniques in these conversations over time.

Training and Skill Acquisition

The impact of this practice change on trainees is an important consideration in a teaching hospital. Interview discussions addressed both the skill acquisition of the attending physicians as well as the training and adoption of IT by residents. All interviewees—even experienced otolaryngologists—acknowledged a learning curve when adopting the intracapsular technique, particularly regarding anticipated procedure duration, intraoperative bleeding, and the use of new surgical instruments. The group expressed no concerns about teaching their trainees the new surgical method and felt confident that residents would be motivated to learn. A portion of the interviewees mentioned that the availability of surgical equipment, specifically plasma ablation technology, may impact the learning of this technique. This was not experienced at our institution but noted to be a possibility in more resource‐limited practices.

Impact on Partners

Within the group, members recognized the value of collectively embracing change. One surgeon expressed the ease of transition to a new surgical technique because of the solidarity he felt from his partners working through the same change at the same time. Surgeons emphasized the reduction in overnight calls to the “on‐call” physician for emergency surgery to address secondary hemorrhage as the department transitioned to IT. This effect led some to feel an unspoken pressure to transition, fearing that continuing to practice TT may diminish the experience for their partners. Interviewees echoed this sentiment in the context of the COVID‐19 pandemic, describing how the pressure to limit emergent unplanned hospital returns pushed them toward a technique that demonstrated reduced risk of secondary hemorrhage.

The practice structure further reinforced this shift. With two remote locations that were covered by a single rotating surgeon, the heightened pressure to minimize surgical complications led to an accelerated adoption of IT. Surgeons reflected upon the delegative approach taken by department leadership, allowing individual physicians to manage their own transition while still offering strategic guidance and support when needed.

Discussion

This sequential explanatory mixed‐methods study explores both the efficacy of IT and the keys to effectively change group surgical practice patterns. While the retrospective review elucidated a timeline and quantified postoperative outcomes that justified the transition, the qualitative arm helped to address a natural follow‐up question: What factors drove this department‐wide transition? This study further characterizes the uptake of IT in the context of existing diffusion of innovation literature. Of note, our results emphasize the importance of an efficacious technique, patient safety, and transparent data in facilitating practice change.

For an innovation to be considered by adopters, it must provide an advantage in the field [1]. In medicine, this manifests as superior clinical outcomes and improved patient safety, both of which have been cited as primary drivers of innovation uptake among surgeons [4, 5]. In the present study, the “Individual Surgeon Experience” theme encompasses the superior outcomes surgeons at our institution noticed anecdotally among their IT patients, whether it was in follow‐up appointments, family testimonies, or reduced postoperative analgesia prescriptions. Our quantitative findings also lend support to the efficacy of IT, demonstrating reduced rates of 30‐day readmission and ED visits among IT patients.

When evaluating a new surgical technique, not all outcomes are weighted equally. Post‐tonsillectomy hemorrhage can be a traumatic experience for patients, families, and health care staff. The rate of post‐tonsillectomy hemorrhage has generally ranged from 2% to 4% in the literature, though some estimate the median to be as low as 1.97% [10, 25, 26]. In resource‐limited settings, the ability of both the surgeon and the patient to quickly return to the OR for a secondary hemorrhage may be diminished. Within the “Impact on Partners” theme, surgeons discussed how this added concern for patient safety in these settings helped push them toward IT, given the data supporting reduced secondary hemorrhage. This decision was further corroborated by the quantitative findings of our study. IT patients experienced fewer hemorrhages requiring a return to the operating room (0.5%) when compared to the TT group (3.3%), aligning with secondary hemorrhage rates of IT reported in the literature (0.08%–0.76%) [10, 16, 19].

Even with anecdotal evidence of an efficacious and safe innovation, the velocity of a transition is also dependent on the communication of these outcomes [1]. When examining physician guideline adherence, Cabana et al. found that lack of awareness and lack of familiarity were two prevalent barriers—consistent with our themes of “Data Transparency” and “Training and Skill Acquisition.” [2] As a way to ensure transparency in tonsillectomy outcomes, our department distributed surgeon‐specific post‐tonsillectomy hemorrhage rates on a monthly basis. In line with one of Berwick's key recommendations for accelerating innovation in healthcare—“make early adopter activity observable”—this data sharing was mentioned by nearly every interviewee as a key catalyst for their personal transition to IT [27]. The start of these monthly newsletters precedes the turning point in our timeline data, with the publications beginning in January 2020 and the 50% threshold being crossed in March 2020.

In addition to “Data Transparency,” the communication of IT outcomes is also relevant to the theme of “Training and Skill Acquisition.” In Huoh et al.'s discussion of national trends in IT, the authors note the role of resident training in the perpetuation of IT, as newer surgeons who have been trained in IT teach more senior surgeons as they enter new institutions [8]. In interviews, some of the earliest adopters at our institution had been trained in IT during residency and were cited by their peers as pioneers of the technique. In hopes of scaling the superior postoperative outcomes our department has experienced, every resident at our institution is now trained in IT.

The limitations of the quantitative arm of this study stem from its retrospective design. Given differences in age and primary indication considerations, making a comparison between IT and TT is subject to bias and confounding. Our data demonstrate the use of IT in a broader range of indications over the course of our study, but future, prospective studies that account for this variation will help to better compare these two techniques. Because data were obtained via chart review, there was room for administrative and reviewer coding errors. We attempted to mitigate this by utilizing strict coding guides, quality control, and a limited number of reviewers.

Another limitation is surgeon variance when deciding to return to the operating room for secondary hemorrhage or revision surgery. Additionally, secondary hemorrhage was defined by patients presenting to the ED; thus, it is possible to have missed patients that called with bleeding concerns but did not ever present to the ED despite specific recommendation for ED evaluation when a patient calls with bleeding concerns.

Finally, the quantitative analysis only included patients undergoing tonsillectomy between 2017 and 2022. This limited our ability to draw conclusions about patients who may have returned for a revision tonsillectomy after 2022. In a prospective case series of over 1200 patients, Amin et al. found that revision tonsillectomy was conducted a median of 27.2 months after IT, and in 2.6% of IT patients [28]. In the present study, the reported IT rate of revision was 0.3% and the median time to revision tonsillectomy was 10.3 months (IQR: 8.6, 26.3).

As is typical of qualitative research, this arm of our study also has several limitations. During the data collection process, we aimed to maintain the consistency of our collection methods but likely encountered variability in the natural geography of each interview discussion as well as in the individual interpretation of responses by reviewers.

Additionally, the physician group we studied was uniquely predisposed to support the transition of surgical technique due to the elevated pressure to decrease the frequency of postoperative tonsil hemorrhage and return to the operating room during the COVID‐19 pandemic. As a result, we did not encounter or explore significant barriers stemming from professional disagreement that may have influenced the transition. The post‐transition timing of these interviews may have also minimized difficulties and learning curves that were overcome, which may subject the qualitative findings to limitations of recall.

To foster openness and mitigate power dynamics during interviews, we intentionally selected a nurse practitioner familiar to the physician group. While this decision likely enhanced participant comfort and candor, in line with the researcher‐as‐instrument approach, it also may have influenced the framing of questions or the nature of responses. We remained attentive to the reflexive dynamics of the interviewer‐interviewee relationship throughout the study but acknowledge that this familiarity may have shaped the data in subtle ways.

Conclusion

While there are a growing number of studies exploring the short and long‐term outcomes of IT and supporters of its widespread adoption, there remains a need to examine previous successful department‐wide transitions. By quantifying the postoperative outcomes of total and IT at a single institution while also investigating the forces driving that transition, we hope this data can serve as a reference for institutions pursuing surgical innovation.
