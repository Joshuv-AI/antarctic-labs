Assessment of parental combining ability, hybrid performance over environment and SSR oriented parental genetic diversity in brinjal (Solanum melongena L.)
Authors: Unnati Vaghela, Rajesh R Acharya, Mihir M Pandya, Sushil Kumar, Rutvik Joshi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBrinjal (Solanum melongena L.) cultivation faces significant challenges, including inconsistent fruit yields, which can hinder productivity and economic returns to farmers. The present study was undertaken to evaluate fruit yield performance in brinjal, with particular emphasis on the contribution of heterosis and genotype × environment interactions influencing yield across the environment. Twelve female and four male parents were crossed in L × T mating design to develop 48 F1 which were evaluated in RBD over three different geographic locations. Simple Sequence Repeat (SSR) molecular markers were used to identify the parental diversity. Parents AB 17–28, AB 19 − 02, AB 19 − 03, and GNRB 1 exhibited excellent general combining ability for fruit yield per plant, along with other yield-related and biochemical traits. Crosses AB 15 − 06 × GNRB 1 (A × G), AB 19 − 03 × Co 2 (G × P) and AB 20 − 12 × GNRB 1 (P × G) exhibited desirable specific combining ability (sca) effects. Based on the dendrogram generated from the dissimilarity matrix, the sixteen brinjal parents were grouped into three major clusters comprising seven, four, and five genotypes, respectively. The genetic distance between AB 17 − 06 and AB 17–28, AB 20 − 02 and AB 17–28 was the largest (0.83). The genetic distance between AB 20 − 07 and GAOB 2 was found to be the lowest at 0.30. Crosses with desirable sca effect, demonstrated the importance of pairing complementary genetic traits. The clustering analysis can be utilized for targeted breeding strategies, with the largest genetic distance offering potential for creating highly heterotic hybrids.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56612-4.Keywords: Eggplant, Combining ability, Pooled environment, Heterobeltiosis, Diversity analysisSubject terms: Biotechnology, Genetics, Plant sciences

Introduction

Eggplant, scientifically known as Solanum melongena L., is grown in subtropical and tropical regions globally over 1.8 million hectares and an annual production of 52.31 million tonnes. Brinjal produces an edible fruit that is scientifically classified as a berry. It is commonly used in cooking and is also valued for its medicinal benefits because it is rich in antioxidants, phenolic acids and alkaloids1. Across South East Asia, the diversity of eggplant manifests in a series of forms, hues and sizes, underscoring the regions pivotal role as a hub of genetic variation. Within the species melongena, three primary botanical varieties exist where; egg-shaped fruits are categorized under var. esculentum, elongated cylindrical types fall under var. serpentium, while diminutive plants find their place under var. depressum 2.

In India, brinjal comes in various shapes, sizes and colours, each reflecting regional preferences and culinary uses. Round brinjals, typically small to medium-sized, are popular in dishes like “begun bhaja” (fried eggplant). Oval brinjals, larger and oval-shaped, are favoured for making stuffed brinjal dishes. Long brinjals, elongated and slender, are staples in dishes like sambar and curry. Striped brinjals, with their distinctive pattern, are common in various local curries. Green brinjals, usually long and green, are popular for dishes such as “vangyache bharit” (brinjal mash) and other traditional recipes. White brinjals, less common and often used for ornamental purposes in culinary presentations, are also found. Tiny brinjals, very small and used whole, are a specialty in dishes like “ennai kathirikai” (stuffed brinjal curry) and similar preparations3. Each of these segments caters to different culinary traditions and preferences, influenced by local tastes and cooking styles.

Eggplant is an important cash crop for farmers worldwide, particularly in China and India, which remain the largest and second-largest producers, respectively; in 2023, global production reached approximately 61 million tonnes, with China contributing about 39.2 million tonnes and India about 12.8 million tonnes (Food and Agriculture Organization, 2024).In regions spanning Asia and the Mediterranean, eggplant consistently holds a place among the top five vegetable crops4. The landscape of eggplant cultivation in India has undergone significant transformation in recent decades, driven by growing awareness of its health benefits and increased adoption of hybrids for commercial farming. However, meeting the escalating demand amid challenges like urbanization and climate change necessitates enhancing current yield potentials. Despite the economic and nutritional importance, breeding efforts in eggplant are limited, consequently eggplant production is low than other solanaceous crops5.

Various strategies, including breeding for increased yield, physiological enhancements, and resistance to pests and environmental stressors, have been explored by researchers. Yet, breeding for high fruit yield traits remains the most promising and sustainable approach. Consequently, the cultivation of new eggplant varieties becomes imperative to realize the objective of higher fruit yield. Eggplant continues to be a focal point for hybrid research due to its resilience, abundant seed production, and relatively low hybrid seed production costs compared to other vegetables6–8.

Hybrid vigour refers to the superior performance of hybrids over their parents in terms of productivity, uniformity, quality, resistance traits, and earliness; however, such superiority is not expressed in all hybrid combinations. The choice of parental combinations plays a pivotal role, as the transmission of superiority from a high-yielding genotype to its progeny isn’t guaranteed. Thus, precise selection of parents is paramount in hybridization programs. Essential prerequisites include understanding combining ability, identifying desirable parent combinations, and discerning the genetic mechanisms governing the inheritance of quantitative traits to develop superior hybrids. However, selecting parent plants only based on their visible traits to take advantage of hybrid vigor is not always effective, especially when important characteristics are controlled by epistatic genes. So, selecting parents based only on their inherent genetic value is crucial which will be confirmed through most commonly used SSR or microsatellites markers.In plant breeding, SSRs are widely preferred molecular markers due to their high informativeness, co-dominant inheritance, multi-allelic nature, abundant genome-wide distribution, and high reproducibility including egg plant9,10.

Among the several mating designs adopted for the study of genetic architecture in eggplant, the line × tester analysis11 has proven to be an effective method for evaluating the potential of parental lines to enhance hybrid performance and it ultimately aiding in the selection of superior hybrids.

However, hybrid development requires extensive trials to evaluate stable performance across different locations, which demands considerable time, cost, labor, and seed quantity 12,13. Over-environment analysis in brinjal cultivation is indispensable for assessing the performance of varieties across diverse environments and ensuring consistent yields under varying conditions. Given the susceptibility of brinjal to various biotic and abiotic stresses, over environment performace analysis helps identify genotypes that exhibit minimal yield fluctuations and maintain consistent productivity levels, even in adverse conditions. By understanding the performace of different cultivars/genotypes, breeders can mitigate risks associated with environmental uncertainties, optimize resource allocation, and maximize profitability. Moreover, performance analysis over-environment facilitates the development of resilient varieties through breeding programs, ultimately contributing to sustainable brinjal production and food security.

Materials and methods

Experimental material

In this experiment, a total of 16 parents were used to develop 48 F1 hybrids using twelve lines and four testers as per L × T mating design suggested by Kempthorne11. The hybrids were developed using manual emasculation and pollination in kharif 2020. Female flower bud was emasculated 5:00 pm to 6:00 pm a day before anthesis and covered with red butter paper bag (10 × 7 cm). Next day morning a pollen of male parents were collected and used to pollinate the emasculated flower bud. Pollinated flower was tagged by tying white thread and covered with white butter paper bag to avoid vector activity and other pollen contamination. Field evolution and experimental location

Forty-eight hybrids were evaluated with 16 parents and check (GABH 3) in randomized block design with three repetitions at three different locations under the jurisdiction of Anand Agricultural University (AAU), Anand in kharif 2021.These locations are in the middle part of Gujarat state of Western India viz., E1: Main Vegetable Research Station (22° 35’ N 72° 55’ E, above mean sea level 45.10 m), AAU, Anand, E2: Agriculture Research Station, AAU, Derol (220 36’N 730 27’ E, above mean sea level 83.14 m) and E3: Pulse Research Station, Model Farm, AAU, Vadodara (22° 19’ N 73° 11’ E, above mean sea level 37.50 m). Each genotype was planted in three rows with 90 cm inter- and 60 cm intra-row spacing.

Morpho - biochemical evaluation

Trait phenotyping was carried out on five competitive random but representative plants. Fruit length and fruit girth were recorded in centimetre. Fruits per plant and fruit yield per plant was cumulatively obtained from twelve pickings. Total phenol content was estimated in mg/100 g as per method described by Bray and Thorpe14. Total soluble solid content was measured by using Zeiss hand refractometer and anthocyanin content estimated by using ethanol-HCl extraction method15.

Statistical analysis

To check the statistical differences between different genotypes for yield attributing and biochemical traits variance was estimated in test and pooled environment given by16. Heterosis over better parent was calculated on the basis of formula given by17. Economic heterosis estimated by the method given by18. The differences observed among the hybrids were further divided into contributions from general combining ability (GCA) and specific combining ability (SCA) components, following the method proposed by Kempthorne11.

For molecular diversity analysis, co-dominant SSR marker was used. After scoring of allelic base pair, different allelic as well as marker parameters were estimated using Power Marker V 3.25 software19. Dendrogram was constructed using Neighbor-Joining method in DARwin 6.020.

Results and discussion

Analysis of variance

The source of variance for lines and testers were found significant for all the traits at different location except fruit length and fruit yield per plant at Vadodara location. This signifies that the 12 female lines used in the crossing program were distinct from one another and the same was true for the male testers. This exception suggests that at the Vadodara location, fruit length and yield was relatively uniform within both lines and testers. Hybrids produced through line x tester (L x T) crosses exhibited significant variation in all traits, indicating phenotypic differences among them. This variation could be either due to genotypic effects or environmental influences (Suppl. Table 1). To better understand the interaction between genotypes and the environment, it is necessary to pool data across all environments. In overall analysis of variance, variance due to environments was significant for all traits except total phenols, indicating diverse environmental conditions influenced trait expression. The G × E interaction was non-significant for total phenols, total soluble solids, anthocyanin content, and moisture content, suggesting these traits were minimally affected by environmental factors, while it was significant for all other traits. The significant and non-significant interactions of lines and testers with environments suggest variable and consistent performance for traits across different environments. Variance for Check vs. Hybrids revealed that both statistically differed from each other for studied traits except days to flowering, total soluble solids and moisture content as their variances were non-significant (Table 2). All traits under study had GCA to SCA variance ratio less then unity indicating non additive gene action (Table 2).

Parental performance and general combining ability

Fruit length

Fruit length of parents was varied in test environments with a range of 7.03 cm to 10.11 cm at Anand, 6.61 cm to 10.20 cm at Derol and 7.14 cm to 9.01 cm at Vadodara location. Maximum and minimum fruit length of genotype AB 17 − 06 (9.22 cm) and AB 20 − 13 (7.56 cm) was observed in pooled data, respectively (Suppl. Table 2).

Lines namely AB 17 − 06 (in E1 and E3) and AB 20 − 15 (in E1) showed positive and significant general combining ability (gca) effects for fruit length, identifying them as good general combiners in specific environments. Testers GAOB 2 exhibited positive and significant gca effects across E1, E2, and E3. When data were pooled, lines AB 17 − 06 and AB 20 − 15, along with tester GAOB 2, demonstrated significant positive gca effects for fruit length (suppl. Table 3a).

Fruit girth

The data indicated significant variations in this trait among genotypes across different regions. Genotype AB 20 − 13 exhibited the smallest fruit girth, measuring 11.97 cm, 11.08 cm and 10.95 cm in the three environments (Suppl. Table 2). Conversely, GNRB 1 displayed the higher fruit girth, with 22.43 cm at Anand and 19.11 cm at Vadodara, among the lines and testers used in the hybridization program.

Testers GAOB 2 and GNRB 1 showed positive GCA effects across all environments (E1, E2, and E3). When considering all environments collectively, lines AB 17–17, AB 17 − 06, AB 19 − 02, AB 20 − 19, AB 20 − 15, and AB 20 − 07, along with testers GAOB 2 and GNRB 1, exhibited significant positive GCA effects for the trait (suppl. Table 3a).

Average fruit weight (g)

Fruit weight holds significant importance because heavier fruits directly contribute to higher overall yield, which is essential for both commercial growers and small-scale farmers. The fruit weight of parental genotypes varied from 28.08 g (AB 20 − 13) to 137.83 g (AB 17–17) in E1, 22.30 g (AB 20 − 13) to 106.00 g (AB 17–17) in E2 and 23.96 g (AB 20 − 13) to 98.67 g (AB 17–17) in E3 (Suppl. Table 2).

Female parents AB 17–28, AB 17–17, AB 17 − 06, AB 19 − 03, AB 20 − 19, and AB 20 − 07 showed superior performance in environments E1, E2, and E3, while AB 19 − 02 excelled in E1 and E2. Among the male parents, GAOB 2 and GNRB 1 were consistently superior across all three environments, with GRB 5 excelling in E1 and E2 (suppl. Table 3b). These parents were identified as excellent general combiners for average fruit weight.

Fruits per plant

A higher number of fruits per plant can enhance market supply, ensuring consistent availability of fruits and vital in breeding programs to improve yield efficiency. Wide interval between highest and lowest number of fruits per plant was observe at all tested location. In the pooled data from three locations, genotype AB 17–17 produced the fewest (20), while AB 20 − 13 had the highest (119) fruits per plant (Suppl. Table 3b).

The GCA effects of lines AB 15 − 06, AB 20 − 02, and AB 20 − 13 in environments E1, E2, and E3, as well as AB 20 − 12 in E1 and E3, were found to be significantly positive. Additionally, the tester Co 2 exhibited significantly positive GCA effects across different locations and in the pooled environment (suppl. Table 5b).

Fruit yield per plant (kg)

Among the parental genotypes, the yield varied significantly. In E1, yield ranged from 2.36 (AB 20 − 15) to 4.37 kg (AB 19 − 03). In E2, yields spanned from 1.84 (Co 2) to 4.10 kg (AB 19 − 03). In E3, the range was from 1.72 (AB 17–17) to 3.09 kg (AB 19 − 03) (Suppl. Table 2). These variations highlight the potential for selecting high-yielding genotypes for different environments (Table 3).

Total phenols (mg/100 g)

Phenolic compounds play an important role in plant defense mechanisms, particularly in imparting resistance against shoot and fruit borer infestation in brinjal, as higher phenolic content is often associated with reduced pest damage21. In the present investigation, highly significant variation for total phenols was observed among the parental genotypes and hybrids across environments, indicating the existence of substantial genetic variability for this trait. The phenolic content among parents ranged from 51.09 mg/100 g (AB 17–17) to 172.24 mg/100 g (AB 19 − 02) in E1, from 50.62 mg/100 g (AB 17–17) to 169.25 mg/100 g (AB 19 − 03) in E2, and from 50.62 mg/100 g (AB 17–17) to 172.33 mg/100 g (AB 19 − 02) in E3 (Suppl. Table 2). The nearly three-fold variation among parental lines clearly demonstrates wide genetic diversity for phenolic content, thereby offering considerable scope for selection and genetic improvement.

The estimates of combining ability further revealed that both GCA and SCA variances were highly significant for total phenols, suggesting the involvement of both additive and non-additive gene effects in the inheritance of this trait. However, the comparatively higher magnitude of GCA variance indicated a predominant role of additive gene action, implying that phenolic content can be effectively improved through selection-based breeding approaches. Among the parental lines, AB 19 − 02, AB 20 − 13, AB 20 − 19, AB 20 − 15, and AB 20 − 07 exhibited significant positive GCA effects across all environments, identifying them as valuable donors for enhancing phenolic content in future breeding programmes. Similarly, testers Co 2 and GNRB 1 were identified as good general combiners for this trait (suppl. Table 3c).

Total soluble solid (0Brix)

Total soluble solids (TSS) are crucial for determining the fruit’s taste, sweetness, and overall quality. Higher TSS levels enhance consumer appeal and marketability. For total soluble solids (°Brix), the observed values among the parental genotypes varied by environment. In E1, genotype GNRB 1 had 5.08 °Brix, while AB 20 − 02 had 6.35 °Brix. In E2, AB 19 − 02 recorded 5.07 °Brix, and AB 20 − 02 reached 6.46 °Brix. In E3, GNRB 1 had 5.15 °Brix, whereas AB 19 − 03 had 6.35 °Brix (Suppl. Table 2).

Line AB 20 − 07 had significant positive combining ability in all environments and also had highest gca effect (0.45**) in pooled environment. In contrast, female parent AB 20 − 02 perform as a poor combiner in pooled (-0.36**) swell as in individual environment. Tester GAOB 2 was good combiner as it had positive significant gca effext (0.28**) (suppl. Table 3c).

Anthocyanin content (mg/100 g)

Anthocyanins are important flavonoid pigments responsible for purple coloration in brinjal fruits and are recognized for their strong antioxidant properties and nutritional significance in the human diet. These compounds contribute to protection against oxidative stress and are increasingly valued in functional food and nutraceutical breeding programmes. The present investigation revealed highly significant differences among parental genotypes and hybrids for anthocyanin content across environments, indicating the presence of extensive genetic variability for this trait. Anthocyanin content among parents ranged from 2.05 mg/100 g (AB 17–17) to 97.95 mg/100 g (AB 20 − 13) in E1, from 2.04 mg/100 g (AB 20 − 12) to 98.22 mg/100 g (AB 20 − 13) in E2, and from 2.25 mg/100 g (AB 17–17) to 96.39 mg/100 g (AB 20 − 13) in E3 (Suppl. Table 2). The approximately 40-fold variation observed among parental genotypes highlights the enormous scope for genetic enhancement of anthocyanin content in brinjal breeding programmes.

The combining ability analysis demonstrated very high and significant GCA and SCA variances for anthocyanin content, indicating the importance of both additive and non-additive gene actions in controlling this trait. The high GCA effects observed in several parents suggest that additive gene action plays a substantial role and that improvement through recurrent selection and pedigree breeding would be effective. Lines AB 15 − 06, AB 17–28, AB 20 − 02, AB 19 − 03, AB 20 − 13, and AB 20 − 07 consistently exhibited significant positive GCA effects across all environments as well as in pooled analysis, identifying them as excellent donors for developing anthocyanin-rich cultivars.

Heterosis and specific combining ability

Fruit length

Mean fruit length of hybrids ranged from 7.04 cm (AB 15 − 06 × Co 2) to 10.43 cm ( AB 20 − 19 × GRB 5) in pooled environment. Significant and positive heterosis over better parent was observed in ten, twelve and eleven hybrids in E1, E2 and E3, respectively (Suppl. Table 2).

The estimates for heterobeltiosis (HB) varied between − 17.55% and 22.71% in E1, -18.29% and 24.08% in E2, and − 17.85% and 24.16% in E3 for fruit length. In contrast, the standard heterosis (SH) estimates ranged from − 40.22% to -6.68% in E1, -40.34% to -5.57% in E2, and − 45.13% to -9.43% in E3. Saikia et al.,21 reported similar positive and significant heterobeltiosis across various brinjal crosses for this trait. Significant and positive heterosis over the better parent was identified in ten hybrids in E1, twelve in E2, and eleven in E3 (Suppl. Table 4a). However, none of the hybrids demonstrated significant and desirable (positive) heterosis consistently across all environments.

Across all environments, eleven hybrids showed significant positive SCA effects, ranging from 1.28 (AB 20 − 19 × GRB 5) to -0.89 (AB 20 − 19 × GNRB 1). The top three hybrids with the highest positive significant SCA effects were AB 20 − 19 × GRB 5 (1.28), AB 20 − 12 × GNRB 1 (1.24), and AB 20 − 07 × GAOB 2 (0.91) (Suppl. Table 5a). Similar results were reported by Nyadanu et al.,22 and Kumar et al.,23 for their studied parents and hybrids.

Fruit girth

The range of fruit girth for the hybrids spanned from 13.28 cm (AB 15 − 06 × GRB 5) to 22.90 cm (AB 20 − 15 × GNRB 1) in E1, from 14.35 cm (AB 15 − 06 × Co 2) to 21.50 cm (AB 19 − 03 × GAOB 2) in E2, and from 13.13 cm (AB 20 − 12 × GRB 5) to 19.41 cm (AB 20 − 07 × GAOB 2) in E3 (Suppl. Table 2). The hybrid AB 17–28 × GAOB 2 exhibited the highest significant positive heterosis in E1 (19.31%) and E3 (18.10%), while AB 19 − 03 × GAOB 2 did so in E2 (18.58%). SH estimates varied from − 8.32% to 58.16% in E1, 4.24% to 56.14% in E2, and − 8.08% to 35.91% in E3. The highest SH was observed in the cross AB 20 − 15 × GNRB 1 (58.16%) in E1, AB 19 − 03 × GAOB 2 (56.14%) in E2, and AB 20 − 07 × GAOB 2 (35.91%) in E3 (Suppl. Table 4b). Studies by Rani et al.,24 and Chaudhari et al.,25 on brinjal crosses similarly reported a wide range of heterobeltiosis and standard heterosis in both positive and negative directions.

The SCA effects, when averaged across all environments, ranged from − 1.55 (AB 17–28 × GRB 5) to 1.95 (AB 20 − 19 × GRB 5). The estimates showed that eight hybrids in E1, six hybrids in E2, and five hybrids in E3 had significant positive SCA effects, with thirteen hybrids displaying these effects across all environments combined (Suppl. Table 5a).

Average fruit weight (g)

In E1, the weight of fruits in hybrids ranged from 44.51 g (AB 20 − 13 × Co 2) to 125.53 g (AB 17–28 × GNRB 1), in E2 from 40.21 g (AB 20 − 13 × Co 2) to 127.89 g (AB 17–28 × GAOB 2), and in E3 from 40.88 g (AB 20 − 13 × Co 2) to 113.87 g (AB 17–28 × GNRB 1) (Suppl. Table 2). The HB variation was from − 48.81% to 26.81% in E1, -42.78% to 31.09% in E2, and − 50.07% to 40.65% in E3. The top-performing hybrids with significant and positive HB were AB 15 − 06 × Co 2 in E1 (26.81%) and E3 (40.65%), and AB 17–28 × GAOB 2 (31.09%) in E2. Additionally, hybrids AB 17–28 × GNRB 1 (26.75%) in E1 and E3 (30.68%), and AB 17–28 × GAOB 2 (39.82%) in E2 exhibited maximum significant positive heterosis over the standard check (Suppl. Table 4c). Chaudhari et al.,25 assessed 36 F1 hybrids across three locations, discovering desirable heterosis over both the better parent and the standard check. Out of 48 F1, cross IGBL 70 X BB 93 showed 83.27% better parent heterosis for fruit weight26.

The SCA effects ranged from − 17.45 (AB 17–28 × Co 2) to 9.49 (AB 17–28 × GNRB 1). The top three hybrids based on significant positive SCA effects for average fruit weight were AB 17–28 × GNRB 1 (9.49), AB 15 − 06 × Co 2 (9.47), and AB 19 − 03 × GNRB 1 (8.99) (Suppl. Table 5a). Thus, these crosses were identified as good specific combiners for average fruit weight. Similar results for their parental genotypes and hybrids were reported by Kachouli et al.,27.

Fruits per plant

In E1, hybrid values ranged from 23.23 (AB 17–17 × GRB 5) to 75.40 (AB 20 − 12 × GRB 5), in E2 from 28.80 (AB 17–17 × GAOB 2) to 83.84 (AB 20 − 13 × Co 2), and in E3 from 25.04 (AB 17–17 × GAOB 2) to 61.10 (AB 20 − 13 × Co 2) (Suppl. Table 2). Out of 48 hybrids, a significant and positive heterosis over the better parent was noted in several crosses: four in both E1 and E3, and two in E2, with a range of -69.28% in E1 to 59.18% in E3. The SH range varied from − 29.33% to 129.43% in E1, -24.54% to 155.04% in E2, and − 10.93% to 117.31% in E3 (Suppl. Table 4d). Khobragade et al.,28 identified eight crosses showing desirable heterosis over the standard check. Across all environments, ten hybrids exhibited significant positive SCA effects, ranging from − 9.41 (AB 15 − 06 × GRB 5) to 9.68 (AB 15 − 06 × GNRB 1) (Suppl. Table 5b).

Fruit yield per plant (kg)

In the hybrids, the fruit yield per plant (kg) ranged from 2.52 kg (AB 20 − 12 × GRB 5) to 3.99 kg (AB 15 − 06 × GNRB 1) when averaged across environments (Suppl. Table 2). The highest heterosis over the better parent was observed in hybrid AB 17–17 × GNRB 1 (62.65%) in E1, AB 20 − 15 × Co 2 (66.04%) in E2, and AB 17 − 06 × GRB 5 (39.24%) in E3. Significant positive heterosis over the standard check was recorded by hybrids AB 19 − 03 × GRB 5 (45.19%) in E1, AB 15 − 06 × GNRB 1 (47.86%) in E2, and AB 19 − 03 × Co 2 (46.96%) in E3 (Table 3). Higher fruit yield per plant with desirable heterotic crosses were observed by Suneetha et al. 29.

The range of these SCA effects varied from − 0.62 (AB 15 − 06 × GRB 5) to 0.57 (AB 15 − 06 × GNRB 1). Among these hybrids, the top three identified as good specific combiners with significant and desirable SCA effects for fruit yield per plant were AB 15 − 06 × GNRB 1 (0.57), AB 19 − 03 × Co 2 (0.52), and AB 20 − 02 × GRB 5 (0.32) (Table 5). This indicates that these crosses are particularly promising for enhancing fruit yield per plant. Their positive SCA effects suggest they are well-suited for further breeding programs focused on yield improvement.

Total phenols (mg/100 g)

Among all the hybrids, AB 19 − 02 × GNRB 1 produced the highest phenol content across all environments, with values of 191.66 mg/100 g in E1, 190.93 mg/100 g in E2, and 190.43 mg/100 g in E3. The total phenol content (mg/100 g) ranged from 37.47 mg/100 g (AB 19 − 03 × GAOB 2) to 191.01 mg/100 g (AB 19 − 02 × GNRB 1) when averaged across environments (Suppl. Table 2). Out of 48 hybrids, six in E1 and five in both E2 and E3 exhibited significant positive heterosis over the better parents. Additionally, twelve hybrids in E1 and E3 and eleven in E2 showed significant positive heterosis over the standard check GABH 3. Notably, hybrid AB 19 − 02 × GNRB 1 consistently displayed significant positive SH in all three environments: E1 (69.30%), E2 (68.94%), and E3 (68.75%) (Suppl. Table 4e).

The estimates of SCA effects indicated that 22 hybrids in E1 and E2, and 23 hybrids in E3 exhibited significant positive SCA effects in individual environments, while 22 hybrids showed such effects across all environments combined. The range of SCA effects on a pooled basis varied from − 52.43 (AB 19 − 02 × Co 2) to 57.32 (AB 20 − 19 × Co 2). Among these, the hybrid AB 20 − 19 × Co 2 (57.32) emerged as the top specific combiner, followed closely by AB 20 − 12 × GAOB 2 (56.57) and AB 19 − 02 × GNRB 1 (55.56) (Suppl. Table 5b). This highlights the importance of these hybrids in breeding programs aimed at yield enhancement. Similar findings were reported in studies by Siva et al.,30 and Datta et al.,31, further validating these results.

Total soluble solid (0Brix)

Among all the crosses, AB 20 − 07 × GAOB 2 produced the highest total soluble solid content in both E1 (6.68 °Brix) and E2 (6.99 °Brix). In E3, the cross AB 19 − 02 × GAOB 2 yielded the highest phenol content (6.92 °Brix), followed closely by AB 20 − 07 × GAOB 2 (6.81 °Brix) and AB 17–28 × GNRB 1 (6.33 °Brix) (Suppl. Table 2d). The heterosis estimates over the better parent varied from − 21.33% to 24.14% in E1, − 27.58% to 19.80% in E2, and − 23.58% to 19.37% in E3. Additionally, the SH estimate range was − 15.73% to 14.19% in E1, − 18.72% to 21.52% in E2, and − 15.72% to 21.39% in E3. These findings highlight the variation in heterosis and soluble solid content across different environments, demonstrating that some hybrids consistently perform well in specific traits (Suppl. Table 4f).

Significant positive SCA effects were observed in twelve hybrids in both E1 and E2, and in eleven hybrids in E3. The top three hybrids with the highest positive and significant SCA effects, identified as good specific combiners, were AB 19 − 02 × GAOB 2 (0.70), AB 20 − 12 × Co 2 (0.55), and AB 20 − 13 × GRB 5 (0.44) (Suppl. Table 5c).

Anthocyanin content (mg/100 g)

For hybrids, anthocyanin content ranged from 2.31 mg/100 g (AB 17–17 × GRB 5) to 110.34 mg/100 g (AB 20 − 13 × Co 2) in E1, from 2.49 mg/100 g (AB 17 − 06 × GRB 5) to 109.17 mg/100 g (AB 20 − 13 × Co 2) in E2, and from 1.98 mg/100 g (AB 17–17 × GRB 5) to 109.36 mg/100 g (AB 20 − 13 × Co 2) in E3. Across all environments, parent anthocyanin values ranged from a low of 2.24 mg/100 g (AB 17–17) to a high of 97.52 mg/100 g (AB 20 − 13) (Suppl. Table 2d). Higher positive heterosis values were deemed desirable. The data showed that eleven hybrids in both E1 and E2, and twelve hybrids in E3, exhibited significant and positive HB for anthocyanin content. The HB range was from − 84.52% to 356.32% in E1, − 86.27% to 292.05% in E2, and − 84.26% to 367.44% in E3. The SH estimates for this trait ranged from − 95.74% to 103.74% in E1, − 95.41% to 101.18% in E2, and − 96.30% to 104.58% in E3 (Suppl. Table 4g).

The hybrid AB 20 − 07 × Co 2 exhibited the highest significant SCA effect in the desired direction with a value of 23.51, followed by AB 20 − 13 × GAOB 2 (22.22) and AB 17–28 × Co 2 (21.80) (Suppl. Table 5c). Similar results were observed for parental genotypes and hybrids in studies by Patel et al.,32 and Bhushan et al.,33.

Diversity analysis.

A set of 147 SSR markers was tested on five DNA samples to identify reproducible, and scorable marker. Out of these, 61 primers (41.49%) were successfully amplified, with 37 (25.17%) being monomorphic and 24 (16.33%) being polymorphic. The molecular weight of the amplified PCR products varied from 66 bp (A1773078) to 312 bp (eme03A05), highlighting significant differences in the number of repeats among alleles34. In total, 149 different alleles were detected across sixteen brinjal genotypes, with an average of 6.21 alleles per locus, ranging from 3 (emf21O06, A1773078, ssr50, and smSSR03) to 13 (CSM31). The average number of alleles per locus in this study was higher than those reported by Liu et al.,35 and Kandoliya et al. 36, possibly due to using a different set of SSR markers on genotypes with greater variability. The major allelic frequency ranged from 0.19 (emf11N23) to 0.88 (ssr50 and smSSR03), with an average of 0.82. PIC values for the SSR markers ranged from 0.21 (ssr50 and smSSR03) to 0.86 (CSM31). Some markers, such as A1773078 (0.46), emf21O06 (0.29), Si1072 (0.27), ssr50 (0.21), and smSSR03 (0.21), were found to be less informative. In contrast, the other nineteen markers were considered more informative, as indicated by their PIC values exceeding 0.50 in brinjal, consistently exhibiting a high number of alleles per locus and higher PIC values (Table 6).

SSR marker based dendrogram of 16 parental genotypes generated using DARwin 6.0 (parents in blue text are males/testers).

The relationship between parental genetic divergence, specific combining ability (SCA), and heterobeltiosis revealed a clear trend in the present investigation. The dendrogram grouped the sixteen parental genotypes into three major clusters (A, B, and C), indicating varying degrees of genetic diversity among the parents. Hybrids developed from genetically distant clusters, particularly between cluster A and cluster C, generally exhibited higher heterobeltiosis and favorable SCA effects, suggesting that wider genetic divergence contributed to superior hybrid performance. This association was especially evident in crosses involving the tester GNRB 1 (sub-cluster C2), which produced several highly heterotic hybrids with cluster A genotypes. For instance, AB 15 − 06 × GNRB 1 exhibited high positive heterobeltiosis across all environments (51.07%, 56.85%, and 21.67%) and also showed significant positive SCA effects (0.57**), where AB 15 − 06 was an average combiner and GNRB 1 was a good combiner. Similarly, AB 17–17 × GNRB 1 recorded the highest heterobeltiosis in E1 (62.65%) and high performance in E3 (34.91%) along with significant positive SCA effects (0.30*), despite AB 17–17 being a poor combiner, indicating the importance of non-additive gene action in heterosis expression. The hybrid AB 17 − 06 × GRB 5 also demonstrated high heterobeltiosis (40.54% and 39.24%) coupled with positive significant SCA effects (0.30*), where both parents were good combiners, suggesting the involvement of additive × additive gene interactions. Likewise, AB 20 − 02 × GRB 5 expressed high heterobeltiosis (32.81%) and significant positive SCA effects (0.32**), even though AB 20 − 02 was an average combiner and GRB 5 was a good combiner. Crosses such as AB 19 − 02 × GNRB 1 and AB 20 − 12 × GNRB 1 also showed high positive heterobeltiosis with favorable SCA effects, further confirming the breeding value of GNRB 1 as a tester. Conversely, hybrids involving poor or genetically related combiners generally recorded low or negative heterobeltiosis and undesirable SCA effects, as observed in AB 19 − 03 × GNRB 1 and several combinations with GAOB 2. Overall, the results demonstrated that hybrids possessing high heterobeltiosis were frequently associated with significant positive SCA effects and involved parents belonging to genetically divergent clusters, particularly clusters A and C. Therefore, the combination of genetic divergence analysis and combining ability studies proved effective for identifying superior parental combinations for heterosis breeding in brinjal.

Conclusion

The analysis of variance among genotypes for all traits within individual environments as well as when pooled across environments was significant, indicating variability within the studied material. Both general and specific combining ability variances were found to be significant for all traits when pooled across environments, signifying the influence of both fixable and non-fixable gene effects on trait expression. However, the ratio of σ2gca/σ2sca being less than unity confirmed the predominance of non-additive gene action for fruit yield per plant, suggesting the potential for exploiting heterosis and favouring hybrid breeding programs in brinjal. Based on per se performance, standard heterosis, and combining ability, specific hybrids stood out as promising candidates for fruit yield per plant across different environments, demonstrating their potential utility in practical plant breeding programs. Some hybrids AB 19 − 02 × GRB 5 and AB 20 − 15 × GRB 5 were positive significant heterosis for fruit yield per plant but non-significant SCA effect. It means the performance of the hybrid offspring is significantly better than the average of its parents for the measured trait. This enhanced performance and the non-significant SCA effect suggests that the improvement is not due to any unique combination of parental genes but rather the overall good genetic potential of the parents. Genetic diversity analysis using SSR markers revealed distinct genomic differences among parental genotypes, highlighting their potential utility in developing biparental mapping populations and enhancing the genetic base of brinjal improvement programs. Additionally, incorporating advanced genomic techniques could enhance the efficiency and precision of breeding efforts, ultimately facilitating the development of resilient and high-yielding eggplant varieties to meet evolving agricultural demands.
