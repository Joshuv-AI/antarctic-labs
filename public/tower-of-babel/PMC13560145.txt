Antinuclear antibody (ANA) screening based on EULAR/ACR 2019 clinical domain criteria among PLHIV and profile of diagnosed HIV-SLE cases
Authors: Jaya Chakravarty, Manaswi Chaubey, Amartya Seth, Thakur Shubh Narayan Rai, Aditya Debuka, Ajay Gupta, P. Raviteja Naidu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) in people living with HIV (PLHIV) is rare, with limited prevalence data. We performed symptom-directed antinuclear antibody (ANA) screening in 129 PLHIV, followed by extractable nuclear antigen testing in ANA-positive individuals. SLE was diagnosed using the 2019 ACR–EULAR criteria. ANA positivity was seen in 54 patients, while anti-dsDNA antibodies were detected in four, all diagnosed with SLE. Three patients were male, with polyarthritis as the most common presentation, followed by proteinuria. Sjögren syndrome, dermatomyositis, and primary systemic sclerosis were identified in one patient each. A literature review identified 165 reported cases of HIV-associated SLE, 93 reported between 2014 and 2024.

Introduction

Systemic Lupus erythematosus (SLE) and HIV are rarely reported together in world literature [1]. The first case of SLE and HIV was reported by Kopelman et al. in 1988 [2]. Since then only 165 cases of HIV with SLE have been reported worldwide [1–26].The low number of cases reported could be due to lack of awareness of SLE among those treating HIV.

Multisystem involvement is seen in both diseases. Adding to the confusion, PLHIV may have positive anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA) while SLE patients can have false positive test for HIV [2, 27]. This implies the impracticality of random screening of ANA for SLE diagnosis among PLHIV as it may be false positive. No studies have specifically explored symptom-directed ANA screening among PLHIV for diagnosis of SLE.

Most of the large case series examining concomitant SLE and HIV have used the older diagnostic criteria for SLE [7, 12, 16, 25]. The latest 2019 European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) and the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) not yet been evaluated in PLHIV [28].

The Antiretroviral treatment (ART) Centre at Banaras Hindu University (BHU), currently provides free ART to 4,200 PLHIV (1790 females) through National AIDS control program. Despite this large cohort, only four cases of SLE were in our follow up, all diagnosed after 2019. Given the potential overlap of HIV-related symptoms with autoimmune manifestations, we conducted ANA screening among our registered PLHIV who exhibited symptoms within the clinical domain of the 2019 ACR-EULAR classification criteria [28]. Our objective was to determine whether this approach could facilitate the diagnosis of SLE in this population.

We also describe the clinical profile of our SLE patients and review reported cases of concomitant HIV and SLE from 2014–2024, extending the most extensive prior review, which analyzed data up to December 2013 [1].

Methods

This observational study was conducted between February 2024 to February 2025 in the ART Centre BHU. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institute ethics committee (2024/EC/7141).

PLHIV presenting with any symptoms in the clinical domain of 2019 ACR-EULAR classification criteria were included in the study after informed consent [28]. ANA testing was done by indirect immunofluorescence assay. The Extranuclear Antigen (ENA) Panel, was done in individuals testing positive for ANA to identify antibodies against dsDNA, nucleosomes, histones, Sm, PCNA, P0, SS-A/Ro60kD, SS-A/Ro52kD, SS-B/La, CENP-B, Scl-70, U1 snRNP, AMA M2, Jo-1, Pm-Scl, Ku, and Mi-2. Diagnosis of SLE was made as per classification criteria. Other plausible causes of clinical criteria were excluded before labelling patients as SLE. Along with those diagnosed SLE through our ANA screening, we also included cases of concomitant HIV and SLE being treated in our centre by reviewing our physical (white cards) and electronic data base.

For the literature review, PRISMA guidelines were followed. We used the terms” SLE” and “HIV” or “AIDS” to search the patients of SLE and HIV infection reported on PubMed and Google between 2014 and 2024. Case reports, series, and cohort studies that explicitly describe both SLE and HIV in the same patient were included.

Data analysis was performed using Statistical package for Social Sciences (SPSS for Windows, version 20.0. Chicago, SPSS Inc.). Continuous variables were presented as mean +/- standard deviation and compared using students t test and discrete data was expressed as proportions and percentages and analyzed using chi square test. For all analysis, the statistical significance was fixed at 5% (p value < 0.05).

Results

129 PLHIV fulfilled at least one screening criteria in the clinical domain of 2019 EULAR/ACR criteria (Fig. 1). Cytopenia (42.63%) especially pancytopenia was the most common reason for screening followed by joint pain (24.03%) and neurologic symptoms (12.5%). Other predominant symptoms included neurologic manifestations (12.4%), proteinuria (3.88%), serositis (3.1%). Out of 129 screened, ANA tested positive in 54 (41.8%). ENA testing revealed anti-dsDNA positivity in only 4 cases. Each of these patients met the classification criteria for SLE. Patients presenting with two or more clinical criteria and low complement levels were significantly more likely to be diagnosed with SLE. Among the 50 ANA-positive individuals who did not meet the criteria for SLE, 19 (38%) had a coinfection—13 with tuberculosis, 3 with cryptococcal meningitis, and 3 with other infections. However, the presence of coinfections did not increase the likelihood of an SLE diagnosis. ENA testing also showed positivity for Ro 52, PM-Scl in 1 patient each, anti JO and CENP-B positive in 2 patients. Diagnosis of Sjogren syndrome, dermatomyositis and primary systemic sclerosis was made in one patient each.

A total of 8 SLE cases were diagnosed, 4 of them were through our ANA screening and 4 were already in our follow up. The clinical and biochemical characteristics of these 8 cases are given in Table 1. Currently, only case 5 is on mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) for lupus nephritis; the rest are on hydroxychloroquine (HCQ) and remain in follow-up. Case 1 who died received MMF as she had lupus myositis and myocarditis along with lupus nephritis.

A total of 72 cases were reported worldwide till November 2013 [1, 4] since then, 94 cases have been reported details of which are given in Table 2.

Discussion

This is the first study which has actively investigated for SLE among PLHIV by symptom directed screening. This screening helped us to diagnose 4 new cases of SLE and 3 other autoimmune disorders. Our study shows that directed ANA screening based on 2019 EULAR/ACR criteria will go a long way towards our goal of diagnosing autoimmune disease among PLHIV.

Our study is also the first and largest case series of PLHIV with SLE reported from India as there are only 9 cases reported in the country till date [3, 4, 6, 14, 17, 22, 23, 26, 29]. Most of the large case series worldwide, have been published either from Rheumatology clinics [1,12] or hospital-based databases [5, 7, 9, 25]. Only one case series with 22 patients (1994–2019) have been published from a HIV Rheumatology clinic [16]. At an ART clinic, the primary focus is often managing HIV and opportunistic infection. Thus, increase in awareness among clinicians regarding the possibility of SLE among PLHIV is the need of the hour.

SLE predominantly affects females; however, four of our eight cases were male. In the review by Mody et al., 18 of 71 cases (25%) were males [1]. Similarly, in our review, 21.4% (18/84) of patients with known gender were male (Table 2). These findings highlight that SLE should also be considered in male PLHIV.

Among our eight cases, five patients had lupus nephritis. Similar frequencies were reported by Hax et al. (63.6%) and Papo et al. (54%), while a lower prevalence was noted by Naovarat et al. (36%) [12, 16, 25]. In our series, four of five patients with lupus nephritis had low C3 or C4 levels, either alone or in combination. As proteinuria can occur in both HIV and SLE, low complement levels may help distinguish SLE-related renal disease activity from HIV-associated nephropathy.

Four of our patients had tuberculosis. Both SLE and HIV independently increase the risk of coinfections, particularly tuberculosis. Given India’s high tuberculosis burden, PLHIV with suspected SLE require careful evaluation for coexisting TB. The coexistence of SLE and TB poses significant diagnostic challenges, as anti-dsDNA antibodies—considered highly specific for SLE—may also be positive in TB [30]. Therefore, diagnosis must rely on a comprehensive assessment of clinical features and laboratory findings. In our cases, proteinuria, joint pain, neuropsychiatric manifestations, and unexplained cytopenias supported the diagnosis of SLE.

Hematological abnormalities were common in our cohort, similar to findings reported by Hax et al. and Papo et al., but differed from those observed by Naovarat et al. [12, 16, 25]. One of our patients had neuropsychiatric manifestations, including seizures attributable to SLE. Hax et al. also reported a significantly higher prevalence of neuropsychiatric lupus among PLHIV compared with HIV-negative SLE patients [12].

Most of our patients presented with mild disease, and their symptoms resolved effectively with Hydroxychloroquine and short course of corticosteroids. Our literature review also showed requirement of less aggressive immunosuppression in PLHIV (Table 2). The reason could be marked depletion of CD4 lymphocytes in HIV causing slow disease progression of SLE. HCQ should be a preferred option as it reduces mortality risk in SLE patients [31]. Moreover, HCQ reduces the viral replication by disrupting the post-translational modification of the viral envelope glycoprotein (gp120), essentially making the newly produced virus particles non-infectious. However, it has to be administered in combination with ART as it has shown to decrease CD4 counts and increase viral loads if given in untreated PLHIV [32]. In cases of major organ involvement, steroids should be given. Mycophenolate can also be used in life threatening major organ involvement. Due to lack of specific treatment guidelines, individualized treatment should be given after careful assessment of risk and benefit along with ART.

Limitations of our study was that anti dsDNA testing was done by ELISA instead of Crithedialuciliae immunofluorescence test (CLIFT) method which is more specific than ELISA. The Farr assay was also not performed due to non availability. We might have missed asymptomatic or atypical cases as we focused on a symptom-based screening. As this is a single-centre study, larger multicentric studies are needed for generalizability of our findings.

To conclude, symptom directed ANA screening will go a long way in diagnosing SLE and other autoimmune diseases among PLHIV. High index of suspicion of SLE should be kept in PLHIV irrespective of gender in those presenting with proteinuria, arthritis and cytopenias. PLHIV with SLE require less aggressive immunosuppression.
