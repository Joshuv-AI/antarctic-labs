Three self-similar solutions of Yang-Mills equations in high odd dimensions
Piotr Bizoń, Irfan Glogić, Arthur Wasserman

Abstract
. We consider spherically symmetric Yang-Mills equations with gauge group S ​ O ​ ( d ) SO(d) in d + 1 d+1 dimensional Minkowski spacetime. For any given odd d ≥ 11 d\geq 11 , we establish existence and uniqueness (modulo reflection symmetry) of exactly N N smooth self-similar solutions, where N N is the number of zeros of an explicit polynomial P m ​ ( z ) P_{m}(z) of degree m = ( d − 5 ) / 2 m=(d-5)/2 in the interval 0 < z < 1 0<z<1 . The number N N can be determined algorithmically by an explicit computation. We find that N = 3 N=3 for all integer m m from 3 3 to 15 15 , the upper bound being merely limited by the extent of our computations. A proof that N = 3 N=3 for all odd d ≥ 11 d\geq 11 remains an open problem.



Introduction

We consider Yang-Mills (YM) equations with gauge group
SO(d)
in
d+1
dimensional Minkowski spacetime

\partial_{\alpha}F^{\alpha\beta}+[A_{\alpha},F^{\alpha\beta}]=0,

(1)

where the YM potentials
A_{\alpha}
(for
\alpha=0,\dots,d
) are skew-symmetric
d\times d
matrices and the YM curvature is
F_{\alpha\beta}=\partial_{\alpha}A_{\beta}-\partial_{\beta}A_{\alpha}+[A_{\alpha},A_{\beta}]
.
We assume the spherically symmetric magnetic ansatz
[
1
]

A_{\alpha}^{ij}=(\delta_{\alpha}^{i}x^{j}-\delta_{\alpha}^{j}x^{i})w(t,r),

(2)

where
i,j=1,...,d
and
r=|\vec{x}|
. Substituting this ansatz into the YM equations (
1
) and letting
w(t,r)=\dfrac{1-u(t,r)}{r^{2}}
we obtain the semilinear wave equation for the function
u(t,r)

u_{tt}=u_{rr}+\frac{d-3}{r}u_{r}+\frac{f(u)}{r^{2}},\qquad f(u)=(d-2)u(1-u^{2}).

(3)

This equation is invariant under reflection
u\mapsto-u
(corresponding to the residual gauge symmetry) and under scaling
u(t,r)\mapsto u_{L}(t,r):=u(t/L,r/L)
for any positive constant
L
. If
u_{L}=u
, the solution is said to be self-similar. To analyze existence and stability of self-similar solutions it is convenient to introduce similarity variables
s=-\log(T-t)
and
y=r/(T-t)
, in terms of which Eq.(
3
) takes the form

u_{ss}+u_{s}+2yu_{sy}=(1-y^{2})u_{yy}+\left(\frac{d-3}{y}-2y\right)u_{y}+\frac{f(u)}{y^{2}}\,.

(4)

Self-similar solutions do not depend on
s
, so
u=u(y)
. Under this assumption, after multiplying Eq. (
4
) by
y^{2}
, we obtain the ordinary differential equation

y^{2}(1-y^{2})u^{\prime\prime}+\left((d-3)y-2y^{3}\right)u^{\prime}+f(u)=0\,.

(5)

We seek regular solutions on the interval
0\leq y\leq 1
, which corresponds to the interior of the past light cone of the point
(t=T,r=0)
.

Remark 1
.

Eq. (
5
) has been studied in the past leading to good (although not complete) understanding of solutions in dimensions
5\leq d\leq 9

[
2
,
3
]
.
To our knowledge, little is known about higher dimensions.
In this paper we fill this gap in the case of odd dimensions
d\geq 11
. The case of even dimensions requires different methods, as will become clear below.

Local solutions near the endpoints

The first step is to analyze the behavior of solutions near the singular endpoints
y=0
and
y=1
. By reflection symmetry, it suffices to consider solutions with
u(0)\geq 0
.
Near
y=0
it is routine to show that regular solutions behave as
u(y)=1-ay^{2}+\mathcal{O}(y^{4})
, where
a
is a free parameter, hereafter assumed nonnegative. These local solutions, denoted below by
u(a,y)
, are analytic in
a
and
y
near
y=0
.

The behavior of regular solutions near
y=1
is more subtle and depends on the parity of
d
, as was first observed by Cazenave, Shatah, and Tahvildar-Zadeh (see Proposition 1 in
[
2
]
). Following their analysis, we assume that
u(y)
is smooth at
y=1
and insert
the Taylor series

u(y)=c+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n}(y-1)^{n}

(6)

into Eq. (
5
). This yields an infinite algebraic system in the triangular form

\displaystyle(d-5)c_{1}+(d-2)c(1-c^{2})

\displaystyle=0,

(7a)

\displaystyle(d-7)c_{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(2d-11-3(d-2)c^{2}\bigr)c_{1}

\displaystyle=0,

(7b)

\displaystyle(d-9)c_{3}+\bigl(d-10-(d-2)c^{2}\bigr)c_{2}-2c_{1}-(d-2)c_{1}^{2}c

\displaystyle=0,

(7c)

For odd
d=2m+5
(
m=0,1,\dots
) the coefficient
c_{m+1}
is multiplied by zero and hence is free, while the coefficients
c,c_{1},\dots,c_{m}
satisfy a closed algebraic system of
m+1
equations. Solving this system recursively, we find that either
c^{2}(1-c^{2})=0
(and then
c_{n}=0
for all
n
from
1
to
m
) or
c^{2}
is a positive root of a polynomial
P_{m}(c^{2})
of degree
m
. The first five polynomials, unique up to normalization, are:

\displaystyle P_{1}(c^{2})

\displaystyle=5c^{2}-1,

(8a)

\displaystyle P_{2}(c^{2})

\displaystyle=196c^{4}-77c^{2}+1,

(8b)

\displaystyle P_{3}(c^{2})

\displaystyle=(225c^{4}-114c^{2}+1)(21c^{2}-1),

(8c)

\displaystyle P_{4}(c^{2})

\displaystyle=(121c^{4}-77c^{2}+4)(11c^{2}-1)(77c^{2}+3),

(8d)

\displaystyle P_{5}(c^{2})

\displaystyle=(8281c^{4}-6266c^{2}+625)(285837c^{6}-11661c^{4}-1989c^{2}-187).

(8e)

Thus, by a purely local analysis near
y=1
we infer that in odd dimensions smooth solutions can exist only for isolated values of
u(1)
. Moreover, if
u(1)
takes one of those admissible values, then the solution is smooth at
y=1
. This follows from the fact that general local solutions near
y=1
have a polylogarithmic Fuchsian expansion (see
[
4
]
for an introduction to Fuchsian methods)

u(y)=c+\sum_{n=1}^{m}c_{n}(y-1)^{n}+b(y-1)^{m+1}\log(1-y)+c_{m}(y-1)^{m+1}+\dots.

(9)

Generically, the coefficient
b
is nonzero and the solution is only
\mathcal{C}^{m}
at
y=1
. However, if
u(1)
satisfies the above necessary condition for smoothness, then it is easy to see that
b
vanishes and the expansion (
9
) reduces to the Taylor series (
6
), hence the solution is smooth at
y=1
(see
[
2
]
for an alternative proof).

Remark 2
.

If
d
is even, then the system (
7
) can be solved recursively for any given
c
, so all values of
u(1)
are
a priori
admissible. For this reason the shooting argument given below for odd
d
does not work for even
d
.

Shooting argument

In the following, we shall consider only solutions which are regular at
y=0
.
To simplify notation, we write
u(y)
instead of
u(a,y)
whenever the dependence on
a
is not essential. First, we show that solutions remain bounded on the entire interval
[0,1]
. This can be proven using the Lyapunov functional

H(y)=\frac{1}{2}y^{2}(1-y^{2})u^{\prime}(y)^{2}-\frac{d-2}{4}\,(1-u(y)^{2})^{2},

(10)

which satisfies

\dfrac{dH}{dy}=-(d-4)yu^{\prime}(y)^{2}
.
Since
H(0)=0
for regular solutions, it follows that
H(y)<0
, which implies that
|u(y)|<1
and
H>-\frac{d-2}{4}
, hence
y\sqrt{1-y^{2}}|u^{\prime}|<\frac{\sqrt{d-2}}{2}
. It follows that
u^{\prime}(y)
and
u(y)
have finite limits at
y=1
.

Lemma 1
.

If
d\geq 10
, then
u(y)
is monotone decreasing from
u(0)=1
to
u(1)>0
.

Proof.

Let
h(y)=y^{3}u^{\prime}(y)
. Differentiating Eq. (
5
), we obtain

y^{2}(1-y^{2})h^{\prime\prime}+(d-7)yh^{\prime}-\left(3(d-2)u(y)^{2}+d-10\right)h=0.

(11)

Since
h(y)\sim-2ay^{4}
near
y=0
, it is negative and decreasing for small
y
. Suppose, for contradiction, that
y_{0}<1
is the first point where
h^{\prime}(y_{0})=0
. Evaluating Eq. (
11
) at
y=y_{0}
we get

y_{0}^{2}(1-y_{0}^{2})h^{\prime\prime}(y_{0})=\left(3(d-2)u(y_{0})^{2}+d-10\right)h(y_{0})<0,

(12)

which contradicts the assumption that such a point
y_{0}
exists. Thus,
h(y)<0
, hence
u^{\prime}(y)<0
on
(0,1)
. By continuity,
u^{\prime}(1)<0
. Evaluating Eq. (
5
) at
y=1
, we obtain

(d-5)u^{\prime}(1)+(d-2)u(1)(1-u(1)^{2})=0\,.

(13)

Since
u^{\prime}(1)<0
and
u(1)^{2}<1
, this implies that
u(1)>0
.
∎

It follows from Lemma 1 that a general solution
u(a,y)
admits the Fuchsian expansion (
9
) at
y=1
with
c>0
. This defines a
\mathcal{C}^{m-1}
map
a\mapsto c(a)
from
(0,\infty)
to
(0,1]
.

Lemma 2
.

If
d\geq 10
, then the function
c(a)
is monotone decreasing from
c(0)=1
to
c(\infty)=~0
.

Proof.

Obviously
c(0)=1
. To analyze the limit
a\rightarrow\infty
, we
introduce the rescaled variable
e^{\tau}=\sqrt{a}y
. Substituting
u(y)=U(\tau)
into Eq. (
5
) and taking the limit
a\rightarrow\infty
, we obtain the autonomous equation

U^{\prime\prime}+(d-4)U^{\prime}+f(U)=0\,.

(14)

By an elementary phase plane analysis we find that solutions starting at
(U,U^{\prime})=(1,0)
at
\tau=-\infty
tend monotonically to
(0,0)
for
\tau\rightarrow\infty
. This proves that
c(\infty)=0
.

Next, let
v(y)=y^{2}\dfrac{\partial u(a,y)}{\partial a}
. Differentiating Eq. (
5
) with respect to
a
, we obtain

y^{2}(1-y^{2})v^{\prime\prime}+\left((d-7)y+2y^{3}\right)v^{\prime}-\left(3(d-2)u(y)^{2}+d-10\right)v=0.

(15)

Since
v(y)\sim-y^{4}
near
y=0
, it is negative and decreasing for small
y
. By the same argument as in the proof of Lemma 1, we conclude that
v(y)<0
on
(0,1)
. By continuity,
v(1)<0
, which proves that
c(a)
is monotone decreasing.
∎

Remark 3
.

For
5\leq d\leq 9
, the assertions of Lemmas 1 and 2 are false because the solutions
u(a,y)
can oscillate around zero, and the function
c(a)
is not monotone (see
[
3
]
for the proof of existence of infinitely many smooth oscillating solutions in
d=5
). Thus, the condition
d\geq 10
in these lemmas is sharp.

Lemmas 1 and 2, combined with the above analysis of the behavior of smooth solutions at
y=1
for odd
d
, imply that for
d=2m+5\geq 11
the number of smooth solutions on
[0,1]
is equal (modulo the reflection symmetry) to the number
N
of zeros of the polynomial
P_{m}(c^{2})
in the interval
0<c^{2}<1
. From the expressions (8c)-(8e), we see explicitly that
N=3
for
m=3,4,5
. In the appendix, we describe a simple explicit method to compute the number
N
for any finite integer
m\geq 3
. Using this method, we find that
N=3
for
m
from
3
to
15
, the upper bound simply reflecting the limits of our patience. Extending this result to all odd
d\geq 11
(i.e., all
m\geq 3
) remains an open problem.

In fact, two of the above smooth solutions can be written explicitly as rational functions

u_{\pm}(y)=1-\frac{\alpha_{\pm}y^{2}}{y^{2}+\beta_{\pm}},

(16)

where

\alpha_{\pm}=2\pm 2\sqrt{\frac{d-4}{3(d-2)}},\quad\beta_{\pm}=\frac{2}{3}(d-4)\pm\frac{1}{3}\sqrt{3(d-2)(d-4)}\,.

(17)

The solution
u_{+}
exists for all
d\geq 5
; it was first found for
d=5
in
[
5
]
and later generalized to all
d\geq 5
in
[
6
]
.
The solution
u_{-}
appears to be new. It exists only for
d\geq 11
, since for
5\leq d\leq 10
the denominator
y^{2}+\beta_{-}
in (
16
) has a zero for some
y\in(0,1)
, rendering the solution singular.

The third smooth solution, denoted by
u_{\ast}
, can be easily constructed numerically using a shooting method by integrating local regular solutions
u(a,y)
from
y=0
toward
y=1
and adjusting the parameter
a
so that
u(a,1)
attains the third admissible value
c_{*}
for which
P_{m}(c_{\ast}^{2})=0
.

Behavior outside the past light cone

Let
x=1/y
. This coordinate covers the whole spacetime: the past and future light cones of the point
(t=T,r=0)
are located at
x=1
and
x=-1
respectively, while the origin corresponds to
x=\infty
if
t<T
and
x=-\infty
if
t>T
.
In terms of
x
Eq. (
5
) takes the form

(1-x^{2})u^{\prime\prime}(x)+(d-5)xu^{\prime}-(d-2)u(1-u^{2})=0.

(18)

We showed above that this equation has three regular solutions in the interval
1\leq x<\infty
, corresponding to the interior of the past light cone.
The two explicit solutions
u_{\pm}(x)
obviously extend smoothly to the entire spacetime. That the third solution
u_{\ast}(x)
can be extended to
x\in(-1,1)
can be easily shown using the functional

\tilde{H}(x)=-H(1/x)=\frac{1}{2}(1-x^{2})u^{\prime}(x)^{2}+\frac{d-2}{4}(1-u(x)^{2})^{2},

(19)

which satisfies
\tilde{H}^{\prime}(x)=-(d-4)xu^{\prime 2}
.
For
x\rightarrow-1^{+}
, in analogy to (
9
), we have

u_{\ast}(x)=\tilde{c}+\sum_{n=1}^{m}\tilde{c}_{n}(x+1)^{n}+\tilde{b}(x+1)^{m+1}\log(x+1)+\tilde{c}_{m+1}(x+1)^{m+1}+\dots,

(20)

with a generically nonzero coefficient
\tilde{b}
, hence
u_{\ast}(x)
is only
\mathcal{C}^{m}
at
x=-1
.

Outlook

Each smooth self-similar solution of Eq. (
3
) provides an example of finite-time singularity formation (“blowup”) from smooth initial data. The extent to which a given self-similar solution participates in the general dynamics depends on its stability. The stability of the solution
u_{+}
for all
d\geq 5
was proved by the second author
[
7
,
13
]
, building on earlier work by the first author
[
8
]
and by Donninger et al.
[
9
,
10
]
(see also recent extensions of this result beyond the past light cone
[
11
,
12
]
). This represents an important step toward showing that the self-similar solution
u_{+}(\frac{r}{T-t})
governs generic spherically-symmetric blowup, as conjectured by the first author and supported by numerical computations
[
3
,
5
,
6
]
.

The stability properties of the solutions
u_{-}
and
u_{\ast}
have not yet been established. Our spectral stability analysis indicates that the solution
u_{-}
has two unstable modes for
11\leq d\leq 17
and one unstable mode for
d\geq 19
,
while the solution
u_{\ast}
has one unstable mode for
11\leq d\leq 17
and two unstable modes for
d\geq 19
. The solution with a single unstable mode (either
u_{\ast}
or
u_{-}
, depending on the dimension) is expected to be critical, in the sense that its codimension-one stable manifold separates regular and singular solutions (see
[
5
]
for numerical computations of critical dynamics in
d=5
).

Finally, we point out that for
d\geq 10
one can construct non-self-similar blowup solutions, in analogy with the construction of such solutions for equivariant wave maps in
d\geq 7

[
14
]
. For
d\geq 11
these so-called type II blowup solutions have the form
Q\left(r/\lambda(t)\right)
, where
Q(r)
is a static solution of Eq. (
3
) and
\lambda(t)\sim(T-t)^{1+\gamma}
. The anomalous exponent
\gamma>0
can be determined by the method of matched asymptotics. The coexistence of type I and type II blowup scenarios for
d\geq 10
may lead to new blowup dynamics that are absent in lower dimensions
1
1

1

Let us recall that in the energy critical case
d=4
the blowup must be of type II
[
15
,
16
]
, while for
5\leq d\leq 9
the blowup must be self-similar.
.

Appendix

To simplify the notation, we introduce the variable
z=c^{2}
. In order to count the number of zeros of the polynomials
P_{m}(z)
for
m\geq 3
, let us first factor out the two explicit zeros corresponding to the solutions
u_{\pm}
,

z_{\pm}=\left(1-\frac{\alpha_{\pm}}{\beta_{\pm}+1}\right)^{2}.

(21)

Accordingly, we write

P_{m}(z)=(z-z_{-})(z-z_{+})S_{m}(z),

where

S_{m}(z)=s_{m-2}z^{m-2}+s_{m-3}z^{m-3}+\cdots+s_{0}.

(22)

For any finite
m
, the coefficients
s_{0},\dots,s_{m-2}
can be computed explicitly in an algorithmic manner. By convention, we assume that the leading coefficient
s_{m-2}
is positive.

The key observation (verified for increasingly large
m
, as if there was no tomorrow) is that the leading coefficient
s_{m-2}
is sufficiently large and all other coefficients
s_{m-3},\dots,s_{0}
are negative. Consequently,
S_{m}(0)<0
and
S_{m}(1)>0
. Since
S_{m}(z)
can change sign only once, it follows that is has a unique zero
z_{\ast}\in(0,1)
.

We find that
z_{-}<z_{\ast}<z_{+}
for
3\leq m\leq 6
, while
z_{\ast}<z_{-}<z_{+}
for
m\geq 7
. Notably, this ordering agrees with the ordering of the corresponding numbers of unstable modes mentioned in the previous section.
