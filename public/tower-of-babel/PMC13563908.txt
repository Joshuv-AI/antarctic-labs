Risk prediction models based on machine learning for emergence delirium in elderly patients undergoing spine surgery: development and validation study
Authors: Qiuyi Wang, Zhilin Zhang, Weijun Luo, Jiale Xu, Haonan Qin, Lanshu Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEmergence delirium (ED) is a common complication in elderly patients undergoing surgery for degenerative spinal disease (DSD) and is associated with adverse postoperative outcomes. Risk prediction may support perioperative risk stratification and individualized monitoring. This study aimed to develop a machine learning based model to predict ED risk in this population.MethodsThis single-center prospective observational cohort study enrolled 430 elderly patients with DSD undergoing spinal surgery at Shanghai Changzheng Hospital between January and October 2024 using convenience sampling. Eligible patients were screened and consented before surgery, and predictors were collected prospectively across five perioperative domains: patient characteristics, preoperative, intraoperative, anesthesia-related, and early postoperative factors. Emergence delirium was assessed as the outcome in the PACU. Feature selection was performed using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression. Six machine learning (ML) models were developed and internally validated, and model interpretability was assessed using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP).ResultsAmong 38 features examined, LASSO regression identified nine key predictors, including age, education level, postoperative pain, and midazolam exposure, among others. Among six ML models evaluated in the testing set, logistic regression achieved the best overall performance, with an area under the curve of 0.832, AUPRC of 0.693, accuracy of 0.769, specificity of 0.858, sensitivity of 0.610, F1-score of 0.650, and Brier score of 0.163. Global feature importance ranking was determined using the SHAP summary plot, showing that the most influential features were education, age, and Midazolam. Individual feature importance, as depicted by the SHAP beeswarm plot, indicated that intraoperative bleeding, operation duration, and age had the most significant impact on prediction. In addition, a potential synergistic pattern between operation duration and intraoperative blood loss indicated a synergistic increase in emergence delirium risk when both were elevated.ConclusionsAn interpretable logistic regression model showed acceptable overall performance for predicting emergence delirium risk in elderly patients undergoing spinal surgery. The model may support anesthesia-informed perioperative risk stratification and individualized monitoring; however, given its moderate sensitivity, it should be used as an adjunct to clinical judgment rather than as a stand-alone screening tool.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12871-026-04025-1.Keywords: Risk prediction models, Machine learning, Elderly, Degenerative spinal disease, Emergence delirium

Background

With advancing age, the spine undergoes degenerative changes in elderly. These changes alter the biomechanical properties and anatomical morphology of the vertebral bodies, intervertebral discs, and surrounding soft tissues. This affects the spine’s range of motion, load dynamics, and tolerance to traumatic events, resulting in symptoms such as segmental instability, spinal cord vascular compromise, and nerve root irritation or compression [1]. This group of clinical manifestations is collectively termed degenerative spinal disease (DSD), encompassing conditions including intervertebral disc herniation, spinal stenosis, and spondylolisthesis. According to Medicare Data in the United States, the annual prevalence of DSD among individuals aged 65 years or older reaches 27.3%±1.7%, with a continued upward trajectory [2].

The accelerating global aging trend has significantly increased the burden of DSD, making spinal surgery the third most common surgical procedure among the elderly [3]. According to projections by the German Federal Statistical Office, by 2060 the number of patients aged 75 years and older undergoing spinal fusion surgery will increase to 2.66 times the number reported in 2019 [4]. Emergence delirium (ED), a subtype of postoperative delirium (POD), refers to delirium occurring from emergence from general anesthesia until discharge from the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU), which is characterized by disturbances in attention, fluctuating levels of consciousness, altered orientation, and impairments in memory and speech, and the reported incidence of ED in elderly surgical patients ranges from 34.5% to 38.4% [5, 6]. ED not only exacerbates long-term cognitive dysfunction and reduces quality of life in patients [7] but also contributes to prolonged hospital stays and a surge in healthcare costs [8]. Consequently, early identification of ED risk in elderly patients with DSD is critical for screening high-risk populations and implementing targeted preventive management strategies.

Currently, there is accumulating evidence regarding risk factors for POD in older adult patients undergoing spinal surgery, encompassing preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative factors. However, several limitations remain in this research domain: (1) The disease type of elderly patients was not clearly defined in most studies, which may affect the accuracy of findings; (2) The predominance of retrospective observational designs introduces recall bias and data incompleteness, resulting in significant outcome heterogeneity across studies and limiting generalizability [9]; (3) Most studies focus on risk factors for postoperative 1–3 day complications, with limited attention paid to early postoperative ED risks. Moreover, the limited investigations addressing ED often focus on single risk variables such as intraoperative body temperature or nutritional status, despite delirium pathogenesis involving multifactorial interactions [6, 10]. Consequently, building upon established evidence, possible predictive indicators should be systematically integrated to develop systematic, comprehensive, simple and effective risk prediction models that improve multidimensional risk profiling for estimating individualized probabilities of future ED. (4) Existing predictive models for POD in elderly spinal surgery patients predominantly employ conventional statistical methods such as logistic regression and Cox proportional hazards regression, which exhibit limited capacity to uncover underlying data patterns and demonstrate significantly constrained flexibility in accommodating heterogeneous variable types and complex, high-volume datasets.

Machine learning (ML) employs a data-driven modeling approach capable of identifying latent associative patterns through structural feature analysis in massive datasets [11]. Over the past two decades, ML has been extensively applied across medical domains including data mining, clinical diagnostics, and disease risk prediction, with substantial empirical evidence demonstrating its enhanced predictive performance compared with conventional methodologies. Notably, emerging research has implemented ML algorithms to predict POD in burn patients [12], while the development of ML-based risk prediction models for ED in elderly spinal surgery cohorts provides a novel approach for developing models with enhanced predictive accuracy.

Therefore, this study employed a prospective cohort design. Based on literature analysis, predictive variables were established across five domains: patient characteristics, preoperative factors, intraoperative factors, anesthesia factors, and postoperative factors. ML algorithms were applied to construct and validate a risk prediction model for ED in elderly DSD patients. Furthermore, interpretable methods were employed to analyze key predictors and influencing factors of ED, providing scientific basis for early identification of high-risk ED patients and prospective evidence for developing comprehensive intervention strategies to prevent and control ED occurrence.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This was a single-center prospective observational cohort study conducted at Shanghai Changzheng Hospital between January and October 2024. The study was reported in accordance with the TRIPOD + AI reporting guideline for clinical prediction model studies [13]. Elderly patients with degenerative spinal disease who underwent elective spinal surgery were recruited using convenience sampling. After ethical approval was obtained, trained investigators screened the elective spinal surgery schedule and ward admission lists during the study period. Patients were eligible if they met all of the following criteria: (1) age ≥ 60 years; (2) confirmed diagnosis of cervical, thoracic, or lumbar degenerative disease; (3) first-time recipient of spinal surgery; and (4) voluntary participation in the study. Patients were excluded if they had: (1) concurrent fractures at other sites; (2) a confirmed diagnosis of spinal tumors, spinal deformities, spinal trauma, spinal infections, or spinal immune disorders; (3) undergone other surgical procedures within the past 6 months; (4) emergency surgery; (5) coma or language impairment that affected communication and assessment; (6) a history of neurosurgical procedures; or (7) diagnosed neuropsychiatric disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease, schizophrenia, epilepsy, Parkinson’s disease, myasthenia gravis, or bipolar disorder. Eligible patients were approached before surgery, written informed consent was obtained, and baseline and perioperative data were collected prospectively according to a predefined protocol. Because recruitment was conducted among eligible patients who were accessible to the trained investigators during the study period rather than through random sampling, the sampling strategy was described as convenience sampling.

The sample size was estimated based on the expected incidence of ED in the target population. Using the formula n=(1.96/𝛿)2∅(1−∅), where 𝛿 represents the margin of error set at 0.05 and 𝜙 denotes the expected incidence rate, the required sample size was calculated based on a previously reported ED incidence of 35% in elderly spinal surgery patients [6]. This yielded a minimum sample size of 350 participants. Considering a 10% attrition rate, the target sample size was increased to 385 participants. Finally, 430 elderly patients with DSD were enrolled after approval by the Medical Ethics Committee of Shanghai Changzheng Hospital (Approval No. 2024SL026). This sample size calculation was primarily designed to ensure adequate precision for estimating ED incidence in the target population, rather than being a prediction model-specific sample size calculation.

Research variable

Outcome variable

In this study, the Confusion Assessment Method for the Intensive Care Unit (CAM-ICU) was used to evaluate the occurrence of ED in elderly DSD patients from the awakening of general anesthesia to the exit of PACU. The National Institute for Health and Clinical Excellence (NICE) in the UK recommends that routine screening for postoperative delirium by CAM-ICU should be performed by healthcare providers in both ICU and PACU [14]. The CAM-ICU scale, initially developed by Ely et al. [15] in 2001 and translated into Chinese in 2013 with 90.2% sensitivity and 91.1% specificity [16]. It includes the evaluation of four features: (1) an acute onset of mental status changes or a fluctuating course; (2) inattention; (3) disorganized thinking; and (4) an altered level of consciousness (i.e., other than alert). The patient is diagnosed as delirious (i.e., CAM positive) if he or she has both features 1 and 2 and either feature 3 or 4.

Predictive variables

Through systematic integration of qualitative data and qualitatively transformed quantitative data related to ED, followed by preliminary evaluation based on clinical relevance and scientific rationale, a total of 38 candidate predictors potentially associated with ED in the study population were identified across five domains: patient characteristics, preoperative factors, intraoperative factors, anesthesia factors, and postoperative factors. Barthel index

The Barthel Index (BI), originally developed by Barthel et al. [17] in 1965 and later adapted into a Chinese version in 2007 [18], is widely regarded as the gold standard for assessing functional independence in activities of daily living (ADL). This scale evaluates 10 items: feeding, dressing and undressing, maintaining personal hygiene, bathing, using the toilet, control of bowel movements, control of bladder movements, mobility, movement from wheelchair to bed and back and, going up and down the stairs. Total scores range from 0 to 100 points, with the following functional impairment classification: scores ≤ 40 indicate severe functional impairment, requiring full care from others; 41 to 60 indicates moderate impairment, needing care from others in most cases; 61 to 99 indicates mild impairment, requiring care from others in a small number of cases; and a total score of 100 indicates no functional impairment, requiring no care from others. The Cronbach’s coefficient of the scale was > 0.92, and the retest reliability coefficient of each field was > 0.82.

The FRAIL scale

The scale was proposed by the International Association of Nutrition and Aging in 2008, and has been clinically validated among elderly patients in China after cross-cultural adjustment, with good reliability and validity [19]. The scale included five items, namely fatigue, resistance, ambulation, illnesses, and loss of weight. The use was rated as yes (1 point) or no (0 points), with a total score of 0 to 5 points (i.e., 1 point for each component; 0 = best to 5 = worst), 3 to 5 represents frail, 1 to 2 represents pre-frail, and the total score = 0 represents robust health status.

Geriatric nutritional risk index

Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index (GNRI) was proposed by Bouillanne et al. in 2005, mainly used for nutritional risk assessment of elderly medical patients, the evaluation indicators include albumin, prealbumin, and body mass index (BMI) [20]. The GNRI formula is as follows: GNRI = 1.489×albumin (g/L) + 41.7×[weight (kg)/ideal weight (kg)]. Male ideal weight(kg)=0.75×height (cm) − 62.50, female ideal weight༈kg༉=0.60×height (cm) − 40.00. If weight exceed ideal weight, the weight/ideal weight is calculated as 1. Nutritional status was graded according to GNRI: major risk (GNRI: <82), moderate risk (GNRI: 82 to < 92), low risk (GNRI: 92 to ≤ 98), and no risk (GNRI: >98).

American society of anesthesiologists

American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) developed by the American Society of Anesthesiologists in 1941 [21], is used to predict and assess anesthesia risks during surgery and was revised in 2020 [22]. The standard is based on the patient’s physical status and the risk of surgery before anesthesia to classify the patient into six classes. The classification system ranges from Level I to Level VI: “A normal healthy patient”; “A patient with mild systemic disease”; “A patient with severe systemic disease”; “A patient with severe systemic disease that is a constant threat to life”; “A moribund patient who is not expected to survive without the operation”; and “A declared brain-dead patient whose organs are being removed for donor purposes”. Higher levels correlate with progressively reduced tolerance to anesthesia and surgical procedures.

Mallampati airway classification

The grading criteria, developed by Sudhindranath et al. in 1984, were used to assess the visibility of the patient’s airway. And it is the basis for judging the difficulty of general anesthesia tracheal intubation and the degree of airway obstruction. The airway is classified according to the structures seen, as follows: class I, soft palate, fauces, uvula, pillars; class Ⅱ, soft palate, fauces, uvula; class Ⅲ, soft palate, base of uvula; class IV, soft palate not visible at all [23]. The higher the grade, the more difficult the intubation and the more severe the airway obstruction.

Numerical rating scale

The Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) is widely used for pain assessment and has demonstrated good reliability, validity, and sensitivity for assessing postoperative pain intensity in Chinese older adults, with a test-retest reliability of 0.754 in elderly Chinese patients [24, 25]. The scale ranges from 0 to 10, with 0 indicating no pain, 1–3 mild pain, 4–6 moderate pain, and 7–10 severe pain. Patients were asked to select the number that best represented their current pain intensity.

In this study, postoperative pain was assessed in the PACU before the CAM-ICU assessment. The NRS was administered after patients had recovered sufficiently to understand simple instructions and provide a verbal response. Before recording the score, the assessor confirmed that the patient was awake, able to follow simple commands, and able to communicate pain intensity.

Data collection

All researchers receiving standardized training on assessment instrument in strict adherence to the study protocol. The researchers (LWJ and QHN) personally collected data from patients meeting the inclusion and exclusion criteria. During preoperative visits, researchers collected general demographic information and assessed ADL, frailty, and nutrition status using the BI, The FRAIL Scale, and GNRI. Preoperative medications, laboratory indicators, and surgical information were collected from the hospital electronic medical record system on the day prior to surgery, with concurrent evaluation of the ASA classification and Mallampati airway classification. After the patient entered the operating room, the duration of the surgery, the amount of intraoperative blood loss, and the occurrence of intraoperative hypotension were recorded. After the operation was completed, we transferred the patient to the PACU and monitored their postoperative body temperature and postoperative pain. When the patient met the PACU discharge criteria (post-anesthesia recovery score ≥ 9), the CAM-ICU was used to evaluate whether they had developed ED.

Following data collection, all initial data is uniformly encoded and input into Microsoft EXCAL software to establish a database; subsequent all collected data underwent dual verification by two independent researchers to ensure data completeness and accuracy (WQY and LWJ).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 21.0 and R version 4.5.1. Categorical variables were described as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables were described as medians and interquartile ranges using their original clinical units. Before multiple imputation and model development, the distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Variables with skewed distributions were log-transformed for subsequent analysis. For continuous variables containing zero values, including intraoperative hypotension times and intraoperative blood transfusion, log(x + 1) transformation was used because log(0) is mathematically undefined. Categorical predictors were represented using dummy variables. The complete coding and transformation strategy for all variables is provided in Appendix Table 1. Missing data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations with the mice package in R version 4.5.1. Ten imputed datasets were generated using the fully conditional specification algorithm with 10 iterations.

The predictor matrix was constructed using mice::quickpred(), and the patient ID variable was excluded from the predictor matrix and was not imputed. The imputation method for each variable was specified using mice::make.method() according to the variable type. The random seed was set to 1234. Each imputed dataset was analyzed separately, and pooled estimates were calculated according to Rubin’s rules. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To identify candidate predictors for ED while reducing overfitting, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) logistic regression was used for feature selection. Ten-fold cross-validation was used to determine the penalty parameter λ. To obtain a parsimonious predictor set, the λ.1se criterion was used rather than λ.min. In this study, λ.1se was defined as the largest λ value with a cross-validated binomial deviance within one standard error of the minimum deviance. Because categorical predictors were converted into dummy variables before LASSO regression, the number of non-zero coefficients shown in the LASSO plot represents dummy-coded coefficients rather than grouped clinical predictors. The selected non-zero coefficients were subsequently mapped back to their corresponding grouped clinical predictors for model development. Based on the 10 imputed datasets, LASSO feature selection was performed independently in each dataset before bootstrap resampling. Predictors consistently or frequently selected across the 10 imputed datasets were retained as a fixed predictor set for subsequent model development and internal validation. The LASSO coefficient paths and cross-validation curves used to select λ are shown in Appendix Figs. 1 and 2.

Following the recommendations in Developing Clinical Prediction Models, internal validation was performed using bootstrap resampling [26]. For each imputed dataset, bootstrap resampling was repeated 100 times with an 80% training ratio using the mlr3 framework in R. In each resampling iteration, the bootstrap training sample was used for model fitting and hyperparameter tuning based on the fixed predictor set, whereas the corresponding bootstrap testing sample was used only for model performance evaluation. Based on the selected predictors, six candidate models were developed: logistic regression, Naive Bayes, XGBoost, random forest, k-nearest neighbors, and decision tree. Hyperparameters were optimized by random search with 5-fold cross-validation on the training data. To limit computational burden, the tuning process employed an early-stopping rule based on performance stagnation. The predefined hyperparameter search ranges and the optimized hyperparameters for each machine learning model are provided in Appendix Table 2, and 3. Logistic regression did not require hyperparameter tuning.

Internal validation was performed on the testing sample to evaluate candidate models across three domains: overall performance, discrimination, and calibration. Overall performance was assessed using the Brier score to quantify the accuracy of predicted probabilities. Discrimination was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, the area under the ROC curve (AUC-ROC), area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and F1-score. Because ED occurred in 35.3% of participants, indicating moderate class imbalance, AUPRC was added as a complementary metric to AUC-ROC to better evaluate the model’s ability to identify true positive ED cases. The baseline AUPRC was defined as the observed ED incidence. For each model, the mean values of AUC-ROC, AUPRC, accuracy, Brier score, sensitivity, specificity, and F1-score were calculated across the corresponding testing samples generated by bootstrap resampling. The normality of the resampled distribution of each performance metric was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. If the distribution was approximately normal, the 95% confidence interval of the mean was calculated using the t distribution. If the distribution was non-normal, a nonparametric bootstrap percentile confidence interval with 1,000 resamples was used. Calibration was examined using the integrated calibration index (ICI), calibration plots, and calibration slope and intercept to quantify and visualize agreement between predicted probabilities and observed event rates. Finally, decision curve analysis (DCA) was conducted in the testing samples to compare net benefit of each model across different threshold probabilities. Model selection was based on the overall balance of discrimination, calibration, clinical utility, and interpretability, with AUC-ROC considered the primary discrimination metric.

To better interpret how each feature influences and contributes to the final model, this study employs the Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method, which provides visual explanations of the optimal model. Global feature importance of predictors was ranked by calculating the mean (|SHAP value|) for each feature. In order to elucidate overall patterns and the spectrum of feature impacts within the dataset, individual SHAP values were additionally visualized for each feature across all samples by SHAP beeswarm plot. Additionally, SHAP dependence plots were further employed to evaluate nonlinear effects of features, capturing potential interactions beyond linear associations.

Results

Patient characteristics

This study enrolled 430 elderly DSD patients (median age 68 years [IQR 63–72]) who underwent spinal surgery in Shanghai Changzheng Hospital from January 2024 to October 2024. The cohort comprised 218 males (50.7%) and 212 females (49.3%). ED was observed in 152 participants (35.3%). Most candidate predictors were complete; only a few laboratory variables had missing values: white blood cell count 3 (0.7%), red blood cell count 3 (0.7%), albumin 2 (0.5%), serum creatinine 9 (2.1%), and urea nitrogen 9 (2.1%). Comparison of variable distributions before and after multiple imputation showed negligible differences: medians and interquartile ranges for continuous variables and counts/percentages for categorical variables remained essentially unchanged, indicating no systematic shift introduced by imputation. After imputation, all eligible participants (n = 430) were included in the model development and validation analyses, with detailed demographic and clinical characteristics presented in Table 1.

Feature selection

LASSO logistic regression was used to select candidate predictors for model development. The λ.1se criterion was adopted to obtain a more parsimonious predictor set. As shown in Appendix Figures 1, λ.min corresponded to approximately 29 non-zero coefficients, whereas λ.1se corresponded to approximately 13 non-zero coefficients. Because categorical predictors were converted into dummy variables before LASSO regression, the number of non-zero coefficients shown in the LASSO plot represents dummy-coded coefficients rather than grouped clinical predictors.

The non-zero coefficients selected at λ.1se were subsequently mapped back to their corresponding grouped clinical predictors. Across the 10 multiply imputed datasets, predictors that were consistently or frequently retained were selected for subsequent model development. Finally, nine grouped clinical predictors were retained as a fixed predictor set for subsequent ML model development: age, education level, postoperative pain, midazolam exposure, log-transformed operation duration, diabetes, operative site, drinking status, and intraoperative bleeding. (Fig. 1).

The heatmap of candidate predictors importance across 10 imputation datasets

Among these predictors, age showed the largest absolute coefficient in the penalized multivariable model, suggesting its prominent contribution to ED prediction. Education level, postoperative pain, midazolam exposure, and operation duration were also repeatedly retained across imputed datasets. The relative ordering of predictors beyond age varied modestly across imputations, but the same set of predictors was selected in the majority of imputed models.

Model performance

Model performance was evaluated in the bootstrap testing samples using AUC-ROC, AUPRC, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, Brier score, and F1-score. Because ED occurred in 152 of 430 patients, the baseline AUPRC was 0.353. The logistic regression model showed the best overall performance among the six candidate models, with an AUC-ROC of 0.832 (Fig. 2), AUPRC of 0.693, Brier score of 0.163, accuracy of 0.769, sensitivity of 0.610, specificity of 0.858, and F1-score of 0.650. The AUPRC of logistic regression was higher than the baseline AUPRC, indicating improved identification of true positive ED cases compared with a non-informative classifier. Model interpretation

To better understand the relationship between the logistic regression model and data, we used SHAP to measure the importance of each feature. Figure 5 provides an overview of the contributions of nine risk factors to the model prediction as a whole. The larger the horizontal axis (mean (|SHAP value|)), the greater the overall influence of the feature. The vertical axis displays feature names ranked by importance. Notably, education had the largest mean absolute SHAP value (mean (|SHAP value|) =0.148), indicating that it contributed most to the model output in this cohort. SHAP beeswarm plot (Fig. 6) further delineates the directional impact and magnitude of each feature on model predictions. The absolute value on the horizontal axis (SHAP value) corresponds to predictor effect strength, with positive values indicating elevated ED risk and negative values reflecting protective effects. Feature values are represented by a purple-to-orange color gradient (purple: low values; orange: high values; e.g., younger to older age, non-use to use of midazolam). As can be seen from the plot, intraoperative bleeding, operation duration and age are the most influential factors for prediction, which have different risk enhancement effects among different individuals.

Higher values of these features tended to increase the model-predicted risk of ED. By contrast, the effect of education on the prediction results was more consistent in direction. Although it showed little individual level variation, it remained an important and stable role at the global level. Moreover, the SHAP dependence plot (Fig. 7) offered another means of analyzing the impact of individual features on the predictive model's outcome. The SHAP dependence plot suggested a potential interaction pattern between operation duration and intraoperative bleeding: among patients with higher intraoperative bleeding, the contribution of operation duration to predicted ED risk appeared amplified. Furthermore, use of midazolam was associated with an increased risk prediction, whereas not use of midazolam was associated with reduced risk; this association appeared more pronounced among patients with higher postoperative pain severity. Discussion

Building upon prior research, this prospective cohort study employed LASSO regression to conduct feature selection across multi-dimensional domains including patient characteristics, preoperative factors, intraoperative factors, anesthesia factors, and postoperative factors. Using nine key features, we ultimately constructed six ML based models to predict ED risk in elderly spinal surgery patients. This approach extends delirium-related risk assessment to the PACU emergence phase and illustrates the potential value of interpretable ML methods in perioperative risk stratification. In the bootstrap testing samples, logistic regression showed the best overall balance among the evaluated models. Using SHAP analysis, we identified influencing features such as education, intraoperative bleeding, operation duration, and age. Additionally, we demonstrated how each feature impacts the ED prediction of the model.

This study systematically evaluated the predictive performance of six ML models for ED in elderly patients undergoing spinal surgery. Logistic regression showed the best overall balance among the candidate models, with the highest AUC-ROC of 0.832 and AUPRC of 0.693 in the bootstrap testing samples. The AUPRC was substantially higher than the baseline value of 0.353, suggesting improved identification of true positive ED cases under moderate class imbalance. Logistic regression also showed favorable accuracy, specificity, and F1-score. However, its sensitivity was moderate at 0.610, indicating that a proportion of ED cases may still be missed if the model is used as a binary screening tool. Therefore, the model should be interpreted as a risk stratification aid rather than a stand-alone screening instrument. In terms of calibration, Naive Bayes showed the lowest ICI of 0.295, followed by logistic regression, indicating relatively better agreement between predicted probabilities and observed ED occurrence. Notably, DCA showed that logistic regression and random forest generally provided higher net benefit than the other candidate models within lower-to-moderate threshold probability ranges.

However, the net benefit of most models decreased when the threshold probability exceeded approximately 0.70, indicating that the clinical utility of these models was threshold-dependent and limited at very high decision thresholds. The observed performance heterogeneity likely stems from fundamental differences in algorithmic design principles: (1) The logistic regression model, operating within a generalized linear modeling framework, effectively captured the linear additive effects of key predictors (e.g., log age, intraoperative bleeding) through log-odds transformation—rendering it optimal for scenarios dominated by linear associations [27]; (2) The Naive Bayes approach, grounded in Bayesian probability estimation under the conditional independence assumption, may be more suitable for the relatively independent structure of predictors in this cohort (e.g., education level, diabetes history) [28]; (3) Random forest, leveraging decision tree ensembles with optimized feature interactions, significantly improved threshold determination for identifying high-risk patients exhibiting nonlinear relationships (e.g., operation duration, midazolam) [29].

In this study, we analyzed the interpretability of the model based on SHAP method, and the results revealed that multiple clinical features contributed differently to the prediction results. Notably, education ranked first in global feature importance (mean |SHAP| = 0.148), indicating its stable and significant impact on model predictions across the population. This study demonstrated that higher education (bachelor’s degree or above) was associated with lower predicted risk. This finding may reflect that patients with higher educational attainment demonstrate advantages in disease awareness, preoperative preparation, and postoperative rehabilitation compliance, which indirectly influence prognosis risk. This is consistent with previous studies on the relationship between education and postoperative outcomes, suggesting that socioeconomic factors should not be ignored in perioperative risk assessment [30].

Age also demonstrated a stable positive influence on prediction outcomes, with greater age corresponding to higher SHAP values, indicating higher overall risk among elderly patients. This aligns with clinical observations of poorer perioperative tolerance and higher complication rates among older patients. With advancing age, degenerative changes in the central nervous system, neuronal loss, and impaired neurotransmitter synthesis may predispose elderly patients to an increased risk of ED [31]. In contrast, features such as operative site and drinking history exhibited relatively minor overall impacts within the model, suggesting their limited contribution to predictive outcomes.

Findings regarding the impact of operation duration and intraoperative bleeding loss on POD are inconsistent. Lee et al. [32] reported no significant association, whereas a meta-analysis identified longer operation duration and greater intraoperative bleeding as risk factors for delirium [33]. Our results are consistent with the latter: prolonged operation duration and increased intraoperative bleeding contributed to higher model-predicted ED risk. Methodological differences across studies, including sample size, case mix, definitions and timing of delirium assessment, and the extent of confounder adjustment, may contribute to these discrepant findings. Furthermore, significant individual variations were observed in intraoperative bleeding and operation duration within the SHAP beeswarm plot. The wide range of SHAP values indicates that these variables have large positive contributions to the model’s prediction risk in some patients, likely linked to surgical trauma severity, intraoperative stress responses, and increased postoperative complication risks. Especially for cases with high bleeding and long operation duration, the risk predicted by the model was significantly increased, which was consistent with the results of the SHAP dependence plots. Clinically, prolonged procedures and large intraoperative bleeding may jointly exacerbate systemic inflammation, hemodynamic instability, cerebral hypoperfusion and transfusion requirements, all of which are known contributing factors for adverse neurological outcomes [34]. It suggested that clinical intraoperative bleeding control and short operation duration should be strengthened during surgery to reduce adverse outcome rates.

In addition, the SHAP dependence plots reveal several meaningful interaction patterns that help explain the role of each predictor and suggest possible clinical mechanisms. Among them, the interaction pattern between operation duration and intraoperative bleeding was the most apparent in the SHAP dependence plots. Therefore, for surgeries with high bleeding risk, more emphasis should be placed on hemostasis, minimize unnecessary operation duration, and closer postoperative monitoring for patients who fall into both high-duration and high-bleeding status.

Our model results indicated that midazolam use was associated with an increased prediction risk of ED, and that this adverse association was amplified with greater postoperative pain severity. Studies have shown that midazolam may increase delirium susceptibility by potentiating gamma-aminobutyric acid central inhibition, leading to prolonged sedation and impairments in attention and arousal [35]. Severe pain can induce physiological stress responses, sleep disruption, systemic inflammatory responses and increased opioid use [36]. Patients with severe pain who are also receiving midazolam may experience a combination of sedation, impaired respiratory function, increased opioid use, and increased stress/inflammatory load that collectively increases the risk of delirium.

Furthermore, our model indicated that patients undergoing waist surgery were at particularly high predicted risk when postoperative pain was severe. Waist procedures are often associated with intense postoperative pain and typically require more aggressive analgesia and prolonged immobilization or activity restriction, thereby exposing patients to multiple converging risk factors for delirium [37]. It should be noted that interactions observed in SHAP dependence plots may in part reflect confounding or multicollinearity; we therefore recommend formally testing key interactions by including interaction terms in multivariable regression models.

In clinical practice, the DCA findings should be interpreted within clinically plausible threshold ranges. In the present study, the net benefit of most models decreased when the threshold probability exceeded approximately 0.70, suggesting that the models should not be regarded as clinically useful across the entire range of decision thresholds. We therefore did not recommend a universal high-risk threshold. Instead, threshold selection should depend on the clinical purpose of prediction, the relative consequences of missed ED cases and unnecessary preventive measures, and the resources available for postoperative monitoring and intervention.

For ED prediction, the main clinical purpose is to support early warning, enhanced PACU observation, and risk-reduction strategies rather than to determine high-risk invasive treatment. Therefore, lower-to-moderate threshold probabilities may be more clinically relevant than very high thresholds. At these clinically plausible thresholds, the logistic regression model may help clinicians identify patients who require closer monitoring, optimized analgesia, cautious sedative use, and individualized perioperative management. However, given the moderate sensitivity and the lack of external validation, the model should be used as an adjunct to clinical judgment and standardized delirium screening rather than as a stand-alone decision rule.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that preoperative risk assessment should emphasize the long-term impact of sociodemographic factors such as education and age, while also considering the differential roles of variables including intraoperative bleeding and operation duration in specific patients. Meanwhile, interdependent perioperative factors (notably operative duration and blood loss, and age by education) may jointly contribute to model-predicted risk. These findings may inform risk stratification algorithms and provide valuable reference for personalized risk prediction and individualized monitoring and preventive management. Future research could further validate its stability and clinical applicability by integrating multi-center clinical data.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, although machine learning methods can handle high-dimensional and nonlinear relationships, their performance depends on an adequate sample size and sufficient number of outcome events [38]. The sample size calculation in this study was based on prevalence estimation rather than prediction model-specific sample size criteria. With 152 ED events and 38 candidate predictors, the crude events-per-variable ratio was approximately 4 before feature selection, which was lower than commonly recommended EPV thresholds for clinical prediction model development. Although LASSO regularization, multiple imputation, and bootstrap internal validation were used to reduce overfitting and optimism, the moderate sample size may still have limited the stability of predictor selection and the performance of complex ML models, such as XGBoost. Therefore, the model should be regarded as preliminary and requires external validation in larger cohorts.

Second, although predictors were selected from multiple perioperative domains, several potentially relevant factors were not included. For example, neurophysiological or neurobiological indicators, such as regional cerebral oxygen saturation, inflammatory markers, and other perioperative biomarkers, were not available in this study. Future studies may incorporate these variables to improve the biological plausibility and predictive performance of ED risk models.

Third, LASSO feature selection was performed on the multiply imputed datasets before bootstrap resampling, rather than being repeated within each bootstrap training sample. Although this approach allowed us to obtain a stable and clinically interpretable predictor set across imputed datasets, it may still introduce feature-selection-related optimism because information from the full cohort contributed to predictor selection before internal validation. Therefore, the reported model performance may be somewhat optimistic. Future studies with larger samples should use fully nested feature selection and validation procedures, or validate the final model in independent external cohorts.

Fourth, this study used a static prediction framework and therefore could not capture dynamic intraoperative physiological fluctuations. Transient hemodynamic instability, bleeding-related hypotension, changes in cerebral perfusion, and other time-dependent intraoperative events may influence the occurrence of ED. Future research should explore dynamic risk prediction or real-time warning models based on continuously monitored perioperative data.

Fifth, this was a single-center prospective cohort study and relied on bootstrap internal validation. Although the recruitment period covered January to October 2024, we did not construct a separate temporal validation cohort because the total sample size and number of ED events were limited. Splitting the cohort by time would have further reduced the number of events available for both model development and validation, potentially leading to unstable estimates of discrimination, calibration, sensitivity, and decision curve analysis. Therefore, bootstrap resampling was used as the primary internal validation strategy to make full use of the available data. Nevertheless, internal validation cannot fully assess model transportability across different time periods, institutions, surgical teams, anesthesia protocols, PACU workflows, or patient populations. In this context, the relatively narrow confidence intervals of model performance should also be interpreted cautiously, as they were derived from repeated internal bootstrap resampling within the same cohort and do not capture between-center heterogeneity or temporal changes in case mix. External multicenter validation is required before clinical implementation.

Sixth, the logistic regression model had moderate sensitivity, indicating that some patients who developed ED could be misclassified as low risk. Therefore, the model should be used as an adjunct to routine PACU observation and standardized delirium screening rather than as a stand-alone screening tool. Future studies should explore thresholds that prioritize sensitivity for early warning purposes.

Finally, the clinical utility of the models was threshold-dependent. DCA indicated that the net benefit of most models decreased and approached or fell below zero when the threshold probability exceeded approximately 0.70. Therefore, the model should not be applied using a fixed universal threshold. Future studies should determine clinically appropriate thresholds based on local clinical workflow, available preventive resources, and the relative harms of missed ED cases versus unnecessary interventions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study developed and internally validated interpretable ED risk prediction models for elderly patients undergoing spinal surgery. Among the six candidate models, logistic regression showed the best overall balance across discrimination, probabilistic performance, calibration, clinical utility, and interpretability. SHAP analysis further improved model transparency by illustrating the contribution of each predictor to the model output. However, given the moderate sensitivity and the absence of external validation, the model should be regarded as a preliminary decision-support tool for perioperative risk stratification rather than a stand-alone screening instrument. Future multicenter studies are needed to externally validate the model, optimize clinically appropriate thresholds, and evaluate its impact on postoperative outcomes.
