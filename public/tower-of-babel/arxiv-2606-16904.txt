Carleman estimates for backward


Abstract
We study a backward inverse problem for a coupled Cahn–Hilliard-reaction-diffusion system. Our main result is a Carleman estimate for a fourth-order/second-order parabolic system with cross-diffusion terms, which allows us to derive conditional stability estimates for the reconstruction of past states from a single final-time observation: Hölder stability at positive times and logarithmic stability for the initial datum. We then apply the Carleman estimate to a phase-field tumour growth model coupling the tumour volume fraction with a nutrient concentration. In this setting, we obtain backward uniqueness and quantitative stability for the recovery of early tumour states, improving earlier results based on logarithmic convexity, which only yielded uniqueness under an additional smallness assumption on the chemotaxis coefficient. We also discuss how these results support Lipschitz stability on finite-dimensional admissible sets, which is relevant for ensuring convergence of iterative discretisation algorithms. Key words: Carleman estimates, Cahn–Hilliard equation, reaction-diffusion equation, backward inverse problem, stability estimates, tumour growth models. AMS (MOS) Subject Classification: 35G31, 35Q92, 35R30, 92C50.

Introduction

Backward inverse problems for dissipative evolution equations are classical examples of severely ill-posed problems: even small perturbations in final observations may generate large errors when propagated backwards in time. Despite this intrinsic instability, such problems are of considerable interest in applications where only late-time measurements are available and the goal is to infer an earlier state of the system. This issue arises naturally in mathematical biology, and
particularly in tumour-growth modelling, where clinically accessible data are typically collected at diagnosis or during drug administration after a significant evolution period, while an important objective is to reconstruct a previous
stage of the disease.
For instance, characterizing the initial tumour location, shape and extent can add important complementary diagnostic and prognostic indications with respect to standard informations based on biopsy and radiogenomics, being correlated to distinct tumour mutations and aggressiveness (see e.g.
[
29
,
26
,
31
]
). This may lead to the attainment of improved prognostic indications for patient survival and to the design of more effective surgical intervention strategies. Evidences for the correlation between tumour location and shape with its grading patterns have been widely provided in literature, e.g. for gliomas
[
25
,
10
]
and prostate cancer
[
15
]
. Also, for highly infiltrative tumours like gliomas, the identification of the tumour location at initiation or after surgery can help in identifying possible infiltrative tumour regions which are typically not visible in clinical images, leading to the design of more effective radiation dose planning
[
28
]
. The reconstruction of previous tumour configurations, given an MRI scan, may be also crucial during therapy administration, leading to the possibility of identifying the tumour cells sensitivity to therapy and its resistant areas by reconstructing the tumour response backward in time. Determining the initial tumour configuration is also required for robustly identifying tumour growth parameters and calibrate the predictive tumour growth models in a patient-specific way, which may lead to accurate predictions of tumour evolution and recurrence (see e.g.
[
1
,
2
,
27
]
).

A powerful tool for analysing backward and inverse problems is provided by Carleman estimates, namely weighted integral inequalities that yield uniqueness, observability, and conditional stability for evolution equations.
For parabolic problems, Carleman techniques have been extensively developed and successfully applied; we refer, for instance, to the foundational works of Yamamoto and Vessella
[
34
,
35
,
33
,
32
]
. However, in the specific context of backward Cahn–Hilliard-type equations, the available literature is much more limited. A closely related contribution, based on Carleman estimates, is the study by Shang and Li
[
30
]
on a backward fourth-order parabolic equation, establishing conditional Hölder stability for intermediate data from final-time measurements.

The present paper extends this line of research by considering a coupled Cahn–Hilliard and reaction-diffusion system with cross-diffusion terms. By exploiting the Carleman framework, we derive not only intermediate-time stability but also logarithmic estimates at the initial time. Furthermore, within the tumour-growth setting, we provide a refined Lipschitz stability result for finite-dimensional classes of admissible data, following an approach similar to that in
[
7
]
.

Motivated by these issues, we study the backward problem for the semilinear coupled system

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+\Delta^{2}u+a\Delta v

\displaystyle=F(x,t,u,v),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v-\Delta v+b\Delta u

\displaystyle=G(x,t,u,v),

subject to homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions. This model couples a fourth-order Cahn-Hilliard-type component with a second-order reaction-diffusion component and includes cross-diffusion effects through the coefficients
a,b>0
. Given the final measurement
(u(\cdot,T),v(\cdot,T))
, the inverse problem we address consists in determining earlier states
(u(\cdot,t_{0}),v(\cdot,t_{0}))
, with
0\leq t_{0}<T
. The abstract framework above is sufficiently general to encompass the phase-field tumour-growth model considered later in the paper.

Our first main result is a Carleman estimate for this backward coupled system. More precisely, we establish a weighted inequality for strong solutions which controls the time derivatives, the principal spatial terms, and the lower-order components of the solution in terms of the weighted source terms and explicit boundary contributions. A notable feature of this estimate is that no smallness restriction is imposed on the cross-diffusion coefficients
a
and
b
. This aspect is essential for applications, since it shows that the method remains effective even in the presence of arbitrarily large cross-diffusion effects.
The Carleman estimate is then applied to derive conditional stability results for the associated backward inverse problem. For every positive intermediate time
t_{0}\in(0,T)
, we prove a Hölder-type stability estimate recovering
(u(\cdot,t_{0}),v(\cdot,t_{0}))
from the final data under a natural a priori bound on the initial state. Compared with the general strategy in
[
35
]
, our argument yields a sharper form of the estimate by avoiding an additional logarithmic factor. Since the corresponding Hölder exponent degenerates as
t_{0}\to 0
, one cannot expect the same type of stability at the initial time; nevertheless, we show that the initial datum satisfies a logarithmic stability estimate. This transition from Hölder stability at positive times to a conditional logarithmic stability at time zero reflects the severe ill-posedness of backward parabolic problems.

A main motivation for this abstract analysis comes from phase-field models for tumour growth. In Section 4 we consider a Cahn–Hilliard and reaction-diffusion system of the form

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varphi-\Delta\mu=P(\varphi)\left(\sigma+\chi(1-\varphi)-\mu\right)-c(x,t)\mathbbm{h}(\varphi)\hskip 20.00003pt

in
Q_{T}
,

\displaystyle\mu=-\Delta\varphi+F^{\prime}(\varphi)-\chi\sigma\hskip 20.00003pt

in
Q_{T}
,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\sigma-\Delta\sigma+\chi\Delta\varphi=-P(\varphi)\left(\sigma+\chi(1-\varphi)-\mu\right)+\kappa(1-\sigma)\hskip 20.00003pt

in
Q_{T}
,

\displaystyle\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}}\varphi=\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}}\mu=\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}}\sigma=0\hskip 20.00003pt

in
\Sigma_{T}
,

\displaystyle\varphi(0)=\varphi_{0},\hskip 10.00002pt\sigma(0)=\sigma_{0}\hskip 20.00003pt

in
\Omega
,

in which the phase variable
\varphi
represents the tumour volume fraction,
\mu
is the associated chemical potential, and
\sigma
denotes the concentration of a nutrient driving tumour proliferation, while chemotaxis is encoded by a coefficient
\chi\geq 0
. This model was introduced in
[
3
]
as a variant of the diffuse-interface multiphase mixture model originally developed in
[
23
]
. In the latter model the mass exchange terms between the phases were represented by chemical kinetic rates incorporating tumour cells proliferation and nutrient consumption. In the present model apoptosis and nutrient release by the healthy tissues are also incorporated in the source terms. Under standard assumptions ensuring strong well-posedness, the difference of two solutions can be rewritten in the same structural form as the abstract system above, with cross-diffusion coefficient equal to
\chi
. Therefore, the Carleman estimate developed in Sections 2 and 3 becomes directly applicable to the inverse problem of reconstructing earlier tumour states from a single final observation.
We stress that our general Carleman estimate also applies to other tumour growth models with similar structure, with possibly different reaction terms, as long as they admit a unique strong solution
[
12
,
20
,
19
,
18
]
.

This application extends the previous analysis in
[
3
]
. In that work, backward uniqueness for the tumour growth model was obtained by means of a logarithmic convexity argument, but no quantitative stability estimate was derived and an additional smallness condition on the chemotactic coefficient
\chi
was required. By contrast, the Carleman approach adopted here removes that extra restriction and yields explicit conditional Hölder stability for positive times, backward uniqueness, and logarithmic stability for the initial data. In this sense, Carleman estimates provide a broader and more informative framework than logarithmic convexity for the class of coupled fourth-/second-order systems considered in this paper.

Finally, we address the stability regime that is most relevant for reconstruction procedures,
[
14
,
8
]
. Indeed, when the admissible initial data are restricted to a finite-dimensional subspace, for instance a discrete space arising in numerical approximation, we combine the differentiability properties of the forward map with a quantitative injectivity estimate for the linearised problem and the general result from
[
6
]
to prove a Lipschitz stability estimate. This refined result is important from the numerical viewpoint, since it provides a rigorous foundation for reconstruction algorithms, even though the corresponding stability constants deteriorate with the dimension of the subspace and with the final time, consistently with the ill-posed character of the backward problem
[
7
,
8
]
. The same strategy also suggests further developments for related phase-field tumour-growth models and for the analysis of reconstruction methods based on finite-dimensional approximations.

The paper is organised as follows. In Section 2 we prove the abstract Carleman estimate for backward Cahn–Hilliard-reaction-diffusion systems. Section 3 is devoted to the Hölder and logarithmic stability results for the associated backward inverse problem. In Section 4 we apply the abstract theory to the tumour growth model, obtain the corresponding uniqueness and stability results, and derive Lipschitz stability for initial states belonging to a finite dimensional subspace. Some final remarks and possible perspectives are collected in the concluding section.

2
Carleman estimate

In this section, we consider a general semilinear fourth-order coupled parabolic system, and we derive a suitable Carleman estimate for the backward problem.
Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\mathbb{N}
, be an open bounded domain with smooth enough boundary
\partial\Omega
.
Let
T>0
be a fixed final time, and set
Q_{T}:=\Omega\times(0,T)
and
\Sigma_{T}:=\partial\Omega\times(0,T)
.
We consider the following semilinear parabolic system:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+\Delta^{2}u+a\,\Delta v=F(x,t,u,v)\hskip 10.00002pt

in
Q_{T}
,

(2.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v-\Delta v+b\,\Delta u=G(x,t,u,v)\hskip 10.00002pt

in
Q_{T}
,

(2.2)

endowed with the homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions

\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}}u=\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}}\Delta u=\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}}v=0\hskip 10.00002pt\hbox{on $\Sigma_{T}$,}

(2.3)

and with suitable initial conditions.
The coefficients
a,b\geq 0
are given constants, promoting some cross-diffusion effects in the system, while
F,G:Q_{T}\times H^{2}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
are two possibly non-linear Caratheodory functions, namely measurable in
(x,t)\in Q_{T}
for every fixed
(u,v)\in H^{2}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)
and continuous in
(u,v)\in H^{2}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)
for almost every fixed
(x,t)\in Q_{T}
. Moreover, there exists a function
K\in L^{\infty}(0,T)
,
K(t)\geq 0
for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
, such that, for almost every
t\in(0,T)
and for any

w_{1}\in H^{2}(\Omega)
,
w_{2}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
, the right-hand sides
F
and
G
satisfy

\int_{\Omega}\left(\lvert F(x,t,w_{1},w_{2})\rvert^{2}+\lvert G(x,t,w_{1},w_{2})\rvert^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\leq K(t)\left(\lVert w_{1}\rVert^{2}_{H^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert w_{2}\rVert^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\right).

(2.4)

We further assume that
(u,v)
is a strong solution to the system (
2.1
)–(
2.3
), satisfying the equations almost everywhere in
Q_{T}
and enjoying the regularity

\begin{split}&u\in H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap C^{0}([0,T];H^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{4}(\Omega)),\\
&v\in H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap C^{0}([0,T];H^{1}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega)).\end{split}

(2.5)

We are interested in a Carleman estimate to address the following backward-in-time inverse problem: given a spatial measurement of the solution
(u,v)
at the final time
t=T
, i.e.,
(u(\cdot,T),v(\cdot,T))\in H^{2}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)
, we ask whether we can uniquely determine the earlier states
(u(\cdot,t_{0}),v(\cdot,t_{0}))\in H^{2}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)
for some
t_{0}\in[0,T)
.
To this end, given a parameter
\lambda>0
, we introduce the weight functions

\varphi_{\lambda}\in C^{2}([0,T]),\hskip 10.00002pt\psi_{\lambda}\in C^{0}([0,T]),\hskip 10.00002pt\psi_{\lambda}(t)>0\hskip 10.00002pt\hbox{for any $t\in[0,T]$},

(2.6)

such that there exist constants
L_{1},L_{2}>0
, independent of
\lambda
, but possibly depending on
T
, for which

\lvert\varphi_{\lambda}^{\prime}(t)\rvert\leq L_{1}\lambda\psi_{\lambda}(t),\hskip 10.00002pt\varphi_{\lambda}^{\prime\prime}(t)\geq L_{2}\lambda^{2}\psi_{\lambda}(t),\hskip 10.00002pt\hbox{for any $t\in[0,T]$.}

(2.7)

Some possible choices for the weight functions
\varphi_{\lambda}
and
\psi_{\lambda}
satisfying (
2.6
)–(
2.7
) are given by

•

Exponential weights:

\varphi_{\lambda}(t)=\psi_{\lambda}(t)=e^{\lambda t}
for
\lambda>0
, with
L_{1}=L_{2}=1
.

•

Polynomial weights:

\varphi_{\lambda}(t)=\psi_{\lambda}(t)=(t+1)^{\lambda}
for
\lambda\geq 2
. Indeed, in this case, for any
t\in[0,T]
, we have that

\lvert\varphi_{\lambda}^{\prime}(t)\rvert=\lvert\lambda(t+1)^{\lambda-1}\rvert=\left\lvert\frac{\lambda}{t+1}\right\rvert\varphi_{\lambda}(t)\leq\lambda\varphi_{\lambda}(t),

and that

\varphi_{\lambda}^{\prime\prime}(t)=\lambda(\lambda-1)(t+1)^{\lambda-2}=\lambda(\lambda-1)\frac{1}{(t+1)^{2}}\varphi_{\lambda}(t)\geq\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\frac{1}{(T+1)^{2}}\varphi_{\lambda}(t),

where we used that
\lambda-1\geq\frac{\lambda}{2}
for any
\lambda\geq 2
.
Thus, (
2.7
) holds with
L_{1}=1
and
L_{2}=\frac{1}{2(T+1)^{2}}
.

to infer that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\psi_{\lambda}(T)}\int_{Q_{T}}\left\{\frac{1}{s}\left(\lvert\partial_{t}u\rvert^{2}+\lvert\partial_{t}v\rvert^{2}\right)+\left(\lvert\Delta u\rvert^{2}+\lvert\nabla v\rvert^{2}\right)+s\left(\lvert u\rvert^{2}+\lvert v\rvert^{2}\right)\right\}e^{2s\varphi_{\lambda}(t)}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq\int_{Q_{T}}\bigg\{\frac{1}{s\psi_{\lambda}(t)}\left(\lvert\partial_{t}u\rvert^{2}+\lvert\partial_{t}v\rvert^{2}\right)+(\lambda-K_{\infty})\left(\lvert\Delta u\rvert^{2}+\lvert\nabla v\rvert^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 10.00002pt+s(\lambda^{2}\psi_{\lambda}(t)-K_{\infty})\left(\lvert u\rvert^{2}+\lvert v\rvert^{2}\right)\bigg\}e^{2s\varphi_{\lambda}(t)}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\leq C\sum_{k=1}^{7}\lvert B_{k}\rvert.

Then, we conclude that

\begin{split}&\int_{Q_{T}}\left\{\frac{1}{s}\left(\lvert\partial_{t}u\rvert^{2}+\lvert\partial_{t}v\rvert^{2}\right)+\left(\lvert\Delta u\rvert^{2}+\lvert\nabla v\rvert^{2}\right)+s\left(\lvert u\rvert^{2}+\lvert v\rvert^{2}\right)\right\}e^{2s\varphi_{\lambda}(t)}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\quad\leq C_{\lambda}\sum_{k=1}^{7}\lvert B_{k}\rvert,\end{split}

(3.4)

for a constant
C_{\lambda}>0
depending also on
\lambda
and
T
.
From this point onward, we consider
\lambda>0
fixed so that (
3.4
) holds.
In this way, the only free parameter left to choose is
s>1
.
Next, we observe that the boundary terms on the right-hand side can be bounded in the following way:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{7}\lvert B_{k}\rvert

\displaystyle=s\lambda\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\left[e^{\lambda t}\lvert u\rvert^{2}e^{2s\varphi_{\lambda}(t)}\right]^{0}_{T}\right\rvert\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\left[\lvert\Delta u\rvert^{2}e^{2s\varphi_{\lambda}(t)}\right]^{T}_{0}\right\rvert\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+s\lambda\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\left[e^{\lambda t}\lvert v\rvert^{2}e^{2s\varphi_{\lambda}(t)}\right]^{0}_{T}\right\rvert\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\left[\lvert\nabla v\rvert^{2}e^{2s\varphi_{\lambda}(t)}\right]^{T}_{0}\right\rvert\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}2}\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\left[\lvert\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\rvert e^{2s\varphi_{\lambda}(t)}\right]^{T}_{0}\right\rvert\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{\lambda}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\left[\lvert u\rvert^{2}e^{2s\varphi_{\lambda}(t)}\right]^{0}_{T}\right\rvert\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\frac{\lambda}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\left[\lvert v\rvert^{2}e^{2s\varphi_{\lambda}(t)}\right]^{0}_{T}\right\rvert\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C_{\lambda}s\,\bigg\{\left(\lVert u(\cdot,0)\rVert^{2}_{H^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert v(\cdot,0)\rVert^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\right)e^{2s\varphi_{\lambda}(0)}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt+\left(\lVert u(\cdot,T)\rVert^{2}_{H^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert v(\cdot,T)\rVert^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\right)e^{2s\varphi_{\lambda}(T)}\bigg\},

where
C_{\lambda}>0
is again a constant depending on
\lambda
and
T
.
Hence, by neglecting the second term on the left-hand side of (
3.4
), we get that

\begin{split}&\int_{Q_{T}}\left\{\frac{1}{s}\left(\lvert\partial_{t}u\rvert^{2}+\lvert\partial_{t}v\rvert^{2}\right)+s\left(\lvert u\rvert^{2}+\lvert v\rvert^{2}\right)\right\}e^{2s\varphi_{\lambda}(t)}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\quad\leq C_{\lambda}s\,\bigg\{\left(\lVert u(\cdot,0)\rVert^{2}_{H^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert v(\cdot,0)\rVert^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\right)e^{2s\varphi_{\lambda}(0)}\\
&\qquad\qquad\quad+\left(\lVert u(\cdot,T)\rVert^{2}_{H^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert v(\cdot,T)\rVert^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\right)e^{2s\varphi_{\lambda}(T)}\bigg\}.\end{split}

(3.5)

Second Step.
We now fix
t_{0}\in(0,T)
.
Then, by the regularity
u,v\in H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
, we can use the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, together with the fact that
\varphi_{\lambda}(t)=e^{\lambda t}
and Young’s inequality, to infer that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(\lvert u(x,t_{0})\rvert^{2}+\lvert v(x,t_{0})\rvert^{2}\right)e^{2s\varphi_{\lambda}(t_{0})}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=\int_{\Omega}\left(\lvert u(x,T)\rvert^{2}+\lvert v(x,T)\rvert^{2}\right)e^{2s\varphi_{\lambda}(T)}\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\int_{\Omega}\left(\lvert u(x,t)\rvert^{2}+\lvert v(x,t)\rvert^{2}\right)e^{2s\varphi_{\lambda}(t)}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=\int_{\Omega}\left(\lvert u(x,T)\rvert^{2}+\lvert v(x,T)\rvert^{2}\right)e^{2s\varphi_{\lambda}(T)}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt-\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left(2u\,\partial_{t}u+2s\varphi_{\lambda}^{\prime}(t)\lvert u\rvert^{2}+2v\,\partial_{t}v+2s\varphi_{\lambda}^{\prime}(t)\lvert v\rvert^{2}\right)e^{2s\varphi_{\lambda}(t)}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq\int_{\Omega}\left(\lvert u(x,T)\rvert^{2}+\lvert v(x,T)\rvert^{2}\right)e^{2s\varphi_{\lambda}(T)}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+2\lambda e^{\lambda T}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left(\sqrt{s}\lvert u\rvert\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}}\lvert\partial_{t}u\rvert+s\lvert u\rvert^{2}+\sqrt{s}\lvert v\rvert\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}}\lvert\partial_{t}v\rvert+s\lvert v\rvert^{2}\right)e^{2s\varphi_{\lambda}(t)}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq\left(\lVert u(\cdot,T)\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert v(\cdot,T)\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)e^{2s\varphi_{\lambda}(T)}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+C_{\lambda,T}\int_{Q_{T}}\left\{\frac{1}{s}\left(\lvert\partial_{t}u\rvert^{2}+\lvert\partial_{t}v\rvert^{2}\right)+s\left(\lvert u\rvert^{2}+\lvert v\rvert^{2}\right)\right\}e^{2s\varphi_{\lambda}(t)}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t.

Therefore, by using (
3.5
), we deduce that

\begin{split}&\left(\lVert u(\cdot,t_{0})\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert v(\cdot,t_{0})\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)e^{2s\varphi_{\lambda}(t_{0})}\\
&\quad\leq C_{\lambda}s\,\bigg\{\left(\lVert u(\cdot,0)\rVert^{2}_{H^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert v(\cdot,0)\rVert^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\right)e^{2s\varphi_{\lambda}(0)}\\
&\qquad\qquad\quad+\left(\lVert u(\cdot,T)\rVert^{2}_{H^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert v(\cdot,T)\rVert^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\right)e^{2s\varphi_{\lambda}(T)}\bigg\}.\end{split}

(3.6)

Third Step.

Starting from (
3.6
), we are now in position to deduce the Hölder stability estimate (
3.2
).
In order to do this, we introduce the following compact notation:

D:=\lVert(u(\cdot,T),v(\cdot,T))\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)}.

Thus, by recalling also the hypothesis (
3.1
), we can rewrite (
3.6
) as

\lVert(u(\cdot,t_{0}),v(\cdot,t_{0}))\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq Cs\left(D^{2}e^{2s(\varphi_{\lambda}(T)-\varphi_{\lambda}(t_{0}))}+M^{2}e^{-2s(\varphi_{\lambda}(t_{0})-\varphi_{\lambda}(0))}\right),

(3.7)

where
C>0
is a constant depending on
\lambda
,
T
and the parameters of the system, but not on
s
.
Our goal is now to choose
s\gg 1
large enough to deduce (
3.2
), provided that
D
is sufficiently small.
To further simplify the notation, we also set

p:=\varphi_{\lambda}(t_{0})-\varphi_{\lambda}(0)>0,\hskip 10.00002ptq:=\varphi_{\lambda}(T)-\varphi_{\lambda}(t_{0})>0,\hskip 10.00002ptp+q=\varphi_{\lambda}(T)-\varphi_{\lambda}(0)>0,

so that (
3.7
) becomes

\lVert(u(\cdot,t_{0}),v(\cdot,t_{0}))\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq Cs\left(D^{2}e^{2sq}+M^{2}e^{-2sp}\right).

(3.8)

Now, since
p,q>0
, we can choose
s\gg 1
large enough so that

s\leq e^{sq}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pts\leq e^{sp}.

In this way we can further estimate the right-hand side of (
3.8
) as

\lVert(u(\cdot,t_{0}),v(\cdot,t_{0}))\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\left(D^{2}e^{3sq}+M^{2}e^{-sp}\right).

(3.9)

Next, we now choose
s_{*}\gg 1
such that

D^{2}e^{3sq}=M^{2}e^{-sp},

namely

s_{*}=\frac{1}{3q+p}\log\frac{M^{2}}{D^{2}}.

(3.10)

We observe that
s_{*}\to+\infty
as
D\to 0
.
Therefore, it is indeed possible to choose it large enough so that all the previous constraints hold, provided that
D
is small enough.
With this choice, we have that

M^{2}e^{-s_{*}p}=D^{2}e^{3s_{*}q}=D^{2}\left(\frac{M^{2}}{D^{2}}\right)^{\frac{3q}{3q+p}}=M^{2\frac{3q}{3q+p}}D^{2\frac{p}{3q+p}}.

Hence, by also taking the square root on both sides, (
3.9
) finally becomes

\lVert(u(\cdot,t_{0}),v(\cdot,t_{0}))\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq CM^{\frac{3q}{3q+p}}D^{\frac{p}{3q+p}},

(3.11)

which is exactly (
3.2
) with

\theta(t_{0}):=\frac{p}{3q+p}=\frac{\varphi_{\lambda}(t_{0})-\varphi_{\lambda}(0)}{3\varphi_{\lambda}(T)-2\varphi_{\lambda}(t_{0})-\varphi_{\lambda}(0)}=\frac{e^{\lambda t_{0}}-1}{3e^{\lambda T}-2e^{\lambda t_{0}}-1}\in(0,1).

This concludes the proof of Theorem
3.1
.
∎

We observe that the Hölder exponent
\theta(t_{0})
in Theorem
3.1
degenerates as
t_{0}\to 0
.
Thus, the estimate above does not provide any further information on the initial data.
Indeed, only a weaker stability estimate of logarithmic type is available for
t_{0}=0
, as shown below.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
(u,v)
be a strong solution to (
2.1
)–(
2.3
) satisfying (
2.5
), and assume that (
2.4
) holds.
Let
M>0
,
\lambda>0
and
C_{1}>0
be as in Theorem
3.1
.
Furthermore, let
M_{1}>0
be a positive constant such that

\lVert(u,v)\rVert_{H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\times H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq M_{1}.

(3.12)

Then, there exists a constant
C_{2}>0
such that

\lVert(u(\cdot,0),v(\cdot,0))\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{2}\left(\log\frac{M}{\lVert(u(\cdot,T),v(\cdot,T))\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)}}\right)^{-\frac{1}{4}},

(3.13)

if
\lVert(u(\cdot,T),v(\cdot,T))\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)}
is sufficiently small, where the constant
C_{2}
can be quantified as

C_{2}=\frac{2M_{1}^{3/4}C_{1}^{1/4}}{\beta^{1/4}},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with}\hskip 10.00002pt\beta=\frac{\lambda}{3e^{\lambda T}-3}.

Proof.

The proof follows the ideas contained in the argument of
[
7
, Theorem 3.5]
and
[
35
, Theorem 7.2.2]
.
In particular, we stress that this approach provides a finer estimate than that of
[
35
, Theorem 7.2.2]
, while also requiring weaker hypotheses on the regularity of the solutions
u
and
v
.
For the sake of completeness, we report the argument in full detail.

Our aim is to employ the Hölder stability estimate (
3.2
) for
t\in(0,T)
to deduce a weaker stability estimate for
t=0
.
Hence, to simplify the notations, we denote

\varepsilon:=\frac{\lVert(u(\cdot,T),v(\cdot,T))\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)}}{M},

so that the Hölder stability estimate proved in Theorem
3.1
can be rewritten as

\lVert(u(\cdot,t),v(\cdot,t))\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{1}M\varepsilon^{\theta(t)},

(3.14)

for any
t\in(0,T)
, if
\varepsilon
is small enough.
Then, we observe that under our regularity assumptions, the function
t\in[0,T]\to\lVert(u(\cdot,t),v(\cdot,t))\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)}
is absolutely continuous. Then, we can apply the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus to say that for any
t\in(0,T)

\displaystyle\lVert(u(\cdot,t),v(\cdot,t))\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=\lVert(u(\cdot,0),v(\cdot,0))\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)}+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\left(\lVert u(\cdot,s)\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert v(\cdot,s)\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=\lVert(u(\cdot,0),v(\cdot,0))\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)}+\int_{0}^{t}\left(2(\partial_{s}u(\cdot,s),u(\cdot,s))_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+2(\partial_{s}v(\cdot,s),v(\cdot,s))_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)\,\mathrm{d}s.

Consequently, by using Cauchy-Schwarz’s inequality, the stability inequality (
3.14
) and the bounds (
3.1
) and (
3.12
), we deduce that

\displaystyle\lVert(u(\cdot,0),v(\cdot,0))\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq\lVert(u(\cdot,t),v(\cdot,t))\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+2\left(\int_{0}^{t}\lVert(\partial_{s}u(\cdot,s),\partial_{s}v(\cdot,s))\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}s\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\lVert(u(\cdot,s),v(\cdot,s))\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}s\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq C_{1}^{2}M^{2}\varepsilon^{2\theta(t)}+2M_{1}\left(\int_{0}^{t}M_{1}\cdot C_{1}M\varepsilon^{\theta(s)}\,\mathrm{d}s\right)^{1/2},

where
\theta(t)
is the Hölder exponent given in Theorem
3.1
, and we also used the fact that we can bound
\lVert(u,v)\rVert_{C^{0}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))\times C^{0}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq M_{1}
on one factor in the second integral.
Now, by working on the explicit expressions of
\theta(t)
, one can easily see that, for any
t\in[0,T]
, it is bounded below by a linear function, namely

\theta(t)=\frac{e^{\lambda t}-1}{3e^{\lambda T}-2e^{\lambda t}-1}\geq\frac{\lambda}{3e^{\lambda T}-3}t:=\beta t,

where
\beta>0
is the one defined in the statement.
Indeed, we just used the sharp inequalities
e^{\lambda t}-1\geq\lambda t
on the numerator and
3e^{\lambda T}-2e^{\lambda t}-1\leq 3e^{\lambda T}-3
on the denominator, the latter following from
e^{\lambda t}>1
.
Therefore, since we are assuming
0<\varepsilon<1
, we have that

\varepsilon^{\theta(t)}\leq\varepsilon^{\beta t}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for any $t\in(0,T)$.}

Hence, we can further estimate

\displaystyle\lVert(u(\cdot,0),v(\cdot,0))\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}^{2}M^{2}\varepsilon^{2\beta t}+2M_{1}^{3/2}C_{1}^{1/2}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\varepsilon^{\beta s}\,\mathrm{d}s\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=C_{1}^{2}M^{2}\varepsilon^{2\beta t}+2M_{1}^{3/2}C_{1}^{1/2}\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{\beta t}}{\beta\log\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{\beta\log\varepsilon}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=C_{1}^{2}M^{2}\varepsilon^{2\beta t}+\frac{2M_{1}^{3/2}C_{1}^{1/2}}{\beta^{1/2}\sqrt{\lvert\log\varepsilon\rvert}}(1-\varepsilon^{\beta t})^{1/2}:=g(t).

Now, we observe that this estimate holds for any
t\in(0,T)
, so we can make it sharper and independent of
t
by minimising the function
g(t)
. Then, we compute its derivative and, recalling that
\log\varepsilon<0
since
0<\varepsilon<1
, we obtain that

g^{\prime}(t)=-C_{1}^{2}M^{2}2\beta\lvert\log\varepsilon\rvert\cdot\varepsilon^{2\beta t}+M_{1}^{3/2}C_{1}^{1/2}\beta^{1/2}\sqrt{\lvert\log\varepsilon\rvert}\frac{\varepsilon^{\beta t}}{(1-\varepsilon^{\beta t})^{1/2}}.

If we call
x=\varepsilon^{\beta t}
,
0<x<1
, we can find the critical points by solving the equation

-C_{1}^{2}M^{2}2\beta\lvert\log\varepsilon\rvert\cdot x^{2}+M_{1}^{3/2}C_{1}^{1/2}\beta^{1/2}\sqrt{\lvert\log\varepsilon\rvert}\frac{x}{(1-x)^{1/2}}=0,

which, after some manipulations, is equivalent to

x\sqrt{1-x}=\frac{M_{1}^{3/2}}{2\beta^{1/2}C_{1}^{3/2}M^{2}\sqrt{\lvert\log\varepsilon\rvert}}.

(3.15)

By analysing the function
f(x)=x\sqrt{1-x}
, we see that its graph is like the one in Figure
1
, with maximum value at
\frac{2\sqrt{3}}{9}
. Then, equation (
3.15
) has at least a solution if

\frac{M_{1}^{3/2}}{2\beta^{1/2}C_{1}^{3/2}M^{2}\sqrt{\lvert\log\varepsilon\rvert}}\leq\frac{2\sqrt{3}}{9},

which is verified if
\varepsilon
is small enough.
Moreover, one can easily see that the solution corresponding to the minimum value of
g(t)
is the smallest one, let us call it
\overline{x}
.
Such
\overline{x}
is attained at some value
\overline{t}
, which should belong to the interval
[0,T]
, at least when
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small.
Indeed, for
\varepsilon<<1
, one can see that
x\sqrt{1-x}=\varepsilon^{\beta t}\sqrt{1-\varepsilon^{\beta t}}\approx\varepsilon^{\beta t}
, so that equation (
3.15
) approximately corresponds to

\varepsilon^{\beta t}=\frac{M_{1}^{3/2}}{2\beta^{1/2}C_{1}^{3/2}M^{2}\sqrt{\lvert\log\varepsilon\rvert}},

which is solved by

\overline{t}\approx\frac{\log\left(\frac{M_{1}^{3/2}}{2\beta^{1/2}C_{1}^{3/2}M^{2}\sqrt{\lvert\log\varepsilon\rvert}}\right)}{\beta\log\varepsilon}\to 0^{+}\hskip 10.00002pt\hbox{as $\varepsilon\to 0^{+}$.}

Then, for any fixed
T
, if
\varepsilon
is suffiently small, one can always find a suitable
\overline{t}\in[0,T]
.
We now have to estimate the value of
\overline{x}
to complete our stability estimate.
Hence, we can see that the increasing branch of the function
f
lies between the two lines
y=x
and
y=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{3}x
, therefore we can estimate the value of
\overline{x}
with the solutions corresponding to the two lines, namely

\frac{M_{1}^{3/2}}{2\beta^{1/2}C_{1}^{3/2}M^{2}\sqrt{\lvert\log\varepsilon\rvert}}\leq\overline{x}\leq\frac{\sqrt{3}M_{1}^{3/2}}{2\beta^{1/2}C_{1}^{3/2}M^{2}\sqrt{\lvert\log\varepsilon\rvert}}.

(3.16)

Figure 1:
Graph of the function
f(x)=x\sqrt{1-x}
,

along with the two linear bounds.

Finally, we can use this piece of information to close our estimate:

\displaystyle\lVert(u(\cdot,0),v(\cdot,0))\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq\min_{t\in[0,T]}g(t)=C_{1}^{2}M^{2}\overline{x}^{2}+\frac{2M_{1}^{3/2}C_{1}^{1/2}}{\beta^{1/2}\sqrt{\lvert\log\varepsilon\rvert}}(1-\overline{x})^{1/2}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq C_{1}^{2}M^{2}\frac{3M_{1}^{3}}{4\beta C_{1}^{3}M^{4}\lvert\log\varepsilon\rvert}+\frac{2M_{1}^{3/2}C_{1}^{1/2}}{\beta^{1/2}\sqrt{\lvert\log\varepsilon\rvert}}\underbrace{\left(1-\frac{M_{1}^{3/2}}{2\beta^{1/2}C_{1}^{3/2}M^{2}\sqrt{\lvert\log\varepsilon\rvert}}\right)^{1/2}}_{\leq 1}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq\frac{2M_{1}^{3/2}C_{1}^{1/2}}{\beta^{1/2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lvert\log\varepsilon\rvert}}\left(1+\frac{3M_{1}^{3/2}}{8\beta^{1/2}C_{1}^{3/2}M^{2}\sqrt{\lvert\log\varepsilon\rvert}}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq\frac{4M_{1}^{3/2}C_{1}^{1/2}}{\beta^{1/2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lvert\log\varepsilon\rvert}},

if we additionally assume that

\frac{3M_{1}^{3/2}}{8\beta^{1/2}C_{1}^{3/2}M^{2}\sqrt{\lvert\log\varepsilon\rvert}}\leq 1,

which is also true if
\varepsilon
is small enough.
This concludes the proof of Theorem
3.2
, by taking the square root on both sides and recalling that
\lvert\log\varepsilon\rvert=\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
, if
0<\varepsilon<1
.
∎

4
Application to tumour growth models

An important application of the results contained in Sections
2
and
3
stems in the context of phase-field tumour growth models.
Below, we show how we can apply our general results to a particular model, thus extending some previously obtained results in
[
3
]
.
However, we stress that the Carleman estimate and its consequences can be applied also to other phase-field tumour growth models, possibily with different source terms (e.g.,
[
20
,
18
]
), as long as they retain a Cahn–Hilliard-reaction-diffusion structure and admit sufficiently regular solutions (cf. Theorem
4.1
below).

We consider the following model, based on the one introduced in
[
22
]
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varphi-\Delta\mu=P(\varphi)\left(\sigma+\chi(1-\varphi)-\mu\right)-c(x,t)\mathbbm{h}(\varphi)\hskip 20.00003pt

in
Q_{T}
,

(4.1)

\displaystyle\mu=-\Delta\varphi+F^{\prime}(\varphi)-\chi\sigma\hskip 20.00003pt

in
Q_{T}
,

(4.2)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\sigma-\Delta\sigma+\chi\Delta\varphi=-P(\varphi)\left(\sigma+\chi(1-\varphi)-\mu\right)+\kappa(1-\sigma)\hskip 20.00003pt

in
Q_{T}
,

(4.3)

\displaystyle\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}}\varphi=\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}}\mu=\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}}\sigma=0\hskip 20.00003pt

on
\Sigma_{T}
,

(4.4)

\displaystyle\varphi(0)=\varphi_{0},\hskip 10.00002pt\sigma(0)=\sigma_{0}\hskip 20.00003pt

in
\Omega
,

(4.5)

Here,
\varphi
is a phase-field variable representing the tumour volume fraction, where
\varphi\equiv 0
is the healthy phase and
\varphi\equiv 1
is the tumour one, and satisfies a Cahn–Hilliard-type equation, being
\mu
the corresponding chemical potential.
Conversely,
\sigma
represents the concentration of a key nutrient driving the tumour proliferation and satisfies a reaction-diffusion equation.
We also include chemotaxis effects through the parameter
\chi\geq 0
.
The model describes tumour growth in the a-vascular phase and is derived within a diffuse-interface multiphase mixture approach. The dynamics of the tumour cells and the nutrient component are described by mass action laws, with associated chemical potentials containing cells-cells and cells-matrix adhesion contributions, encoded in the form of the nonlinearity
F(\varphi)
, as well as interface, mass and chemotactic terms. See
[
3
,
23
]
for details. Also, the mass exchange terms between the phases are represented by chemical kinetic rates incorporating tumour cells proliferation, with phase-dependent proliferation rate encoded in the term
P(\varphi)
, nutrient consumption, apoptosis and therapy-related death terms proportional to
\mathbbm{h}(\varphi)
, with death rate
c(x,t)
, and nutrient release by the healthy tissues, with release rate
\kappa
.
The inverse problem we want to address in this context is the identification of the initial data
(\varphi_{0},\sigma_{0})
starting from a single measurement of

Lemma 4.3

Assume

–
A7

By Young’s inequality, we can start by estimating

\displaystyle\lVert\tilde{f}_{1}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert\tilde{f}_{2}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq C\Big(\lVert\Delta(F^{\prime}(\varphi_{1}))-\Delta(F^{\prime}(\varphi_{2}))\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+2\lVert P(\varphi_{1})(\widetilde{\sigma}-\chi\widetilde{\varphi}+\Delta\widetilde{\varphi}-(F^{\prime}(\varphi_{1})-F^{\prime}(\varphi_{2}))+\chi\widetilde{\sigma})\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+2\lVert(P(\varphi_{1})-P(\varphi_{2}))(\sigma_{2}+\chi(1-\varphi_{2})+\Delta\varphi_{2}-F^{\prime}(\varphi_{2})+\chi\sigma_{2})\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=C\left(I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}+I_{4}+\lVert\widetilde{\sigma}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right).

Now, by using the local Lipschitz continuity of

and

\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\lVert F^{\prime\prime}(\varphi_{1})\Delta\widetilde{\varphi}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert(F^{\prime\prime}(\varphi_{1})-F^{\prime\prime}(\varphi_{2}))\Delta\varphi_{2}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\lVert F^{\prime\prime\prime}(\varphi_{1})(\nabla\varphi_{1}+\nabla\varphi_{2})\cdot\nabla\widetilde{\varphi}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert(F^{\prime\prime\prime}(\varphi_{1})-F^{\prime\prime\prime}(\varphi_{2}))\nabla\varphi_{2}\cdot\nabla\varphi_{2}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\lVert F^{\prime\prime}(\varphi_{1})\rVert^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\lVert\Delta\widetilde{\varphi}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert\Delta\varphi_{2}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\lVert\widetilde{\varphi}\rVert^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\lVert F^{\prime\prime\prime}(\varphi_{1})\rVert^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\lVert\nabla\varphi_{1}+\nabla\varphi_{2}\rVert^{2}_{L^{4}(\Omega)}\lVert\nabla\widetilde{\varphi}\rVert^{2}_{L^{4}(\Omega)}+\lVert\nabla\varphi_{2}\rVert^{4}_{L^{4}(\Omega)}\lVert\widetilde{\varphi}\rVert^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\Big)

where we extensively used the Sobolev embeddings

and

Moreover, we also estimate similarly the remaining terms:

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\lVert P(\varphi_{1})\rVert^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\lVert\widetilde{\sigma}-\chi\widetilde{\varphi}+\Delta\widetilde{\varphi}-(F^{\prime}(\varphi_{1})-F^{\prime}(\varphi_{2}))+\chi\widetilde{\sigma}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\lVert\widetilde{\sigma}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert\widetilde{\varphi}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert\Delta\widetilde{\varphi}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\lVert\widetilde{\sigma}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert\widetilde{\varphi}\rVert^{2}_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\right),

\displaystyle I_{3}

\displaystyle\leq 2\lVert(P(\varphi_{1})-P(\varphi_{2})\rVert^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\lVert\sigma_{2}+\chi(1-\varphi_{2})+\Delta\varphi_{2}-F^{\prime}(\varphi_{2})+\chi\sigma_{2}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\lVert\widetilde{\varphi}\rVert^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\left(1+\lVert\sigma_{2}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert\varphi_{2}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert\varphi_{2}\rVert^{2}_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\right)

Remark 4.6
.

This backward uniqueness result improves the previous one obtained in
[

by means of the logarithmic convexity method.
Indeed, here we no longer require an additional smallness constraint on the chemotaxis coefficient

This is due to our new approach by Carleman estimates, which does not require any additional assumption on the cross-diffusion terms.
Moreover, this new approach also allows us to get some explicit stability estimates for the backward problem, as we show below.

Assume

–

&\quad\leq C_{2}\left(\log\frac{M}{\lVert(\varphi_{1}(\cdot,T),\sigma_{1}(\cdot,T))-(\varphi_{2}(\cdot,T),\sigma_{2}(\cdot,T))\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)}}\right)^{-\frac{1}{4}},\end{split}

Remark 4.8
.

We observe that this type of logarithmic estimate can be impractical in applications, as the error actually gets small only if the final data are very close to each other.
Moreover, it can be easily seen that the stability constant
C_{2}
deteriorates exponentially with the final time
T
, making this stability estimate even more unreliable.
Nevertheless, this is expected since we are dealing with a backward problem for a parabolic system, which is known to be severely ill-posed as
T
gets larger.

4.2
Further stability results

We now want to improve this stability estimate to one of Lipschitz type, under the additional a priori assumption that the initial data to be reconstructed actually lives in a finite-dimensional subspace of
H^{2}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)
, for instance, one of the discrete spaces used in numerical approximations.
To do this, we apply a general result, which was proved in
[
6
, Proposition 5]
.
We note that the same result was successfully used to get Lipschitz stability estimates for other inverse ill-posed problems, like in
[
4
,
5
]

Proposition 4.9

\displaystyle\xi\in H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap C^{0}([0,T];H^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{4}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle\eta\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle\rho\in H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap C^{0}([0,T];H^{1}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega)).

Proof.

The existence of a unique weak solution to (

)–(
4.20

[

, Theorem 5.3]
.
Indeed, it was shown that, if
(\overline{\varphi},\overline{\mu},\overline{\sigma})

)–(
4.5

\displaystyle\xi\in H^{1}(0,T;(H^{2}_{N}(\Omega))^{*})\cap C^{0}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}_{N}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle\eta\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle\rho\in H^{1}(0,T;(H^{1}(\Omega))^{*})\cap C^{0}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)),

namely there exists a constant

, depending only on the parameters of the systems and on

, such that

\begin{split}&\lVert\xi\rVert_{H^{1}(0,T;(H^{2}_{N}(\Omega))^{*})\cap L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))}+\lVert\eta\rVert_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\\
&\quad+\lVert\rho\rVert_{H^{1}(0,T;(H^{1}(\Omega))^{*})\cap L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}\leq C\left(\lVert h\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert k\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right).\end{split}

(4.21)

We now assume that
(\overline{\varphi},\overline{\mu},\overline{\sigma})
is a strong solution to (

)–(
4.5

For ease of presentation, we just argue by formal

However, we stress that the estimates below can be made rigorous in a Galerkin discretisation scheme.

First, we test (

\partial_{t}\rho-\Delta\rho

\displaystyle\lVert\partial_{t}\rho\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\lVert\nabla\rho\rVert^{2}_{H}+\lVert\Delta\rho\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=-\chi(\Delta\xi,\partial_{t}\rho-\Delta\rho)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-(P^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi})(\overline{\sigma}+\chi(1-\overline{\varphi})-\overline{\mu})\xi,\partial_{t}\rho-\Delta\rho)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

Then, by exploiting Hölder and Young’s inequalities and Remark

\displaystyle\lVert\partial_{t}\rho\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\lVert\nabla\rho\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert\Delta\rho\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+\lVert P^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi})\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\lVert\overline{\sigma}+\chi(1-\overline{\varphi})-\overline{\mu}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\lVert\xi\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\lVert\partial_{t}\rho-\Delta\rho\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+\lVert P(\overline{\varphi})\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\lVert\rho-\chi\xi-\eta\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\lVert\partial_{t}\rho-\Delta\rho\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\kappa\lVert\rho\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\lVert\partial_{t}\rho-\Delta\rho\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+C\lVert\Delta\xi\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C\lVert\rho\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C\lVert\eta\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

where

(0,t)

, and applying (

(4.22)

) and (

\begin{split}&\partial_{t}\xi+\Delta^{2}\xi+\chi\Delta\rho=F^{\prime\prime}(\overline{\varphi})\Delta\xi+2F^{\prime\prime\prime}(\overline{\varphi})\nabla\overline{\varphi}\cdot\nabla\xi+F^{(4)}(\overline{\varphi})\nabla\overline{\varphi}\cdot\nabla\overline{\varphi}\,\xi\\
&\quad+F^{\prime\prime\prime}(\overline{\varphi})\xi\Delta\overline{\varphi}+P^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi})(\overline{\sigma}+\chi(1-\overline{\varphi})-\overline{\mu})\xi+P(\overline{\varphi})(\rho-\chi\xi-\eta)-\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi})c\,\xi.\end{split}

Then, we test (

\displaystyle\lVert\partial_{t}\xi\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\lVert\Delta\xi\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert\Delta^{2}\xi\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=-\chi(\Delta\rho,\partial_{t}\xi+\Delta^{2}\xi)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+(F^{\prime\prime}(\overline{\varphi})\Delta\xi+2F^{\prime\prime\prime}(\overline{\varphi})\nabla\overline{\varphi}\cdot\nabla\xi+F^{(4)}(\overline{\varphi})\nabla\overline{\varphi}\cdot\nabla\overline{\varphi}\,\xi+F^{\prime\prime\prime}(\overline{\varphi})\xi\Delta\overline{\varphi},\partial_{t}\xi+\Delta^{2}\xi)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+(P^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi})(\overline{\sigma}+\chi(1-\overline{\varphi})-\overline{\mu})\xi+P(\overline{\varphi})(\rho-\chi\xi-\eta)-\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi})c\xi,\partial_{t}\xi+\Delta^{2}\xi)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle(F^{\prime\prime}(\overline{\varphi})\Delta\xi+2F^{\prime\prime\prime}(\overline{\varphi})\nabla\overline{\varphi}\cdot\nabla\xi+F^{(4)}(\overline{\varphi})\nabla\overline{\varphi}\cdot\nabla\overline{\varphi}\,\xi+F^{\prime\prime\prime}(\overline{\varphi})\xi\Delta\overline{\varphi},\partial_{t}\xi+\Delta^{2}\xi)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle(P^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi})(\overline{\sigma}+\chi(1-\overline{\varphi})-\overline{\mu})\xi+P(\overline{\varphi})(\rho-\chi\xi-\eta)-\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi})c\,\xi,\partial_{t}\xi+\Delta^{2}\xi)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq\lVert P^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi})\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\lVert\overline{\sigma}+\chi(1-\overline{\varphi})-\overline{\mu}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\lVert\xi\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\lVert\partial_{t}\xi+\Delta^{2}\xi\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+\lVert P(\overline{\varphi})\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\lVert\rho-\chi\xi-\eta\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\lVert\partial_{t}\xi-\Delta\xi\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt-c_{\infty}\lVert\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi})\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\lVert\xi\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\lVert\partial_{t}\xi+\Delta^{2}\xi\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq\frac{1}{4}\lVert\partial_{t}\xi\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\frac{1}{4}\lVert\Delta^{2}\xi\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C\left(1+\lVert\overline{\sigma}+\chi(1-\overline{\varphi})-\overline{\mu}\rVert^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\right)\lVert\xi\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

.
Hence, by gathering all estimates and integrating on

, we obtain the following:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\lVert\partial_{t}\xi\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}s+\frac{1}{4}\lVert\Delta\xi(\cdot,t)\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\int_{0}^{t}\lVert\Delta^{2}\xi\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq\frac{1}{4}\lVert h\rVert^{2}_{H^{2}(\Omega)}+C\int_{0}^{T}\left(1+\lVert\overline{\sigma}+\chi(1-\overline{\varphi})-\overline{\mu}\rVert^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\right)\lVert\xi\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+C\int_{0}^{T}\left(1+\lVert\overline{\varphi}\rVert^{2}_{H^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert\overline{\varphi}\rVert^{4}_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\right)\lVert\xi\rVert^{2}_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}s+C\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\rho\rVert^{2}_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}s+C\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\eta\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}s

\lVert\xi\rVert^{2}_{H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{4}(\Omega))}\leq C\left(\lVert h\rVert^{2}_{H^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert k\rVert^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\right).

(4.24)

Moreover, by comparison in (

) and ellptic regularity theory, the newly-found estimate (

\lVert\eta\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))}\leq C\left(\lVert h\rVert^{2}_{H^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert k\rVert^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\right).

.

Next, we show that the map
\mathcal{R}
is continuously Fréchet differentiable and characterise its derivative as the solution to the linearised system.

Proposition 4.10
.

Assume hypotheses
A1
–
A7
. Then,
\mathcal{R}:H^{2}_{N}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)
is Fréchet differentiable, namely for any

such that, as
\lVert(h,k)\rVert_{H^{2}_{N}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)}\to 0

\frac{\lVert\mathcal{R}(\overline{\varphi_{0}}+h,\overline{\sigma_{0}}+k)-\mathcal{R}(\overline{\varphi_{0}},\overline{\sigma_{0}})-\mathrm{D}\mathcal{R}(\overline{\varphi_{0}},\overline{\sigma_{0}})[h,k]\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)}}{\lVert(h,k)\rVert_{H^{2}_{N}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)}}\to 0.

(h,k)\in H^{2}_{N}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)

(\overline{\varphi_{0}},\overline{\sigma_{0}})
in
(h,k)

\mathrm{D}\mathcal{R}(\overline{\varphi_{0}},\overline{\sigma_{0}})[h,k]=(\xi(\cdot,T),\rho(\cdot,T))

where
\xi(\cdot,T)
and
\rho(\cdot,T)
are the solutions to the linearised system (
4.16
)–(
4.20
) with initial data
(h,k)

is Lipschitz-continuous as a function from

to the space
\mathcal{L}(H^{2}_{N}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega),L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega))

\begin{split}&\lVert\mathrm{D}\mathcal{R}((\overline{\varphi_{0}}_{1},\overline{\sigma_{0}}_{1}))-\mathrm{D}\mathcal{R}((\overline{\varphi_{0}}_{2},\overline{\sigma_{0}}_{2}))\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{L}(H^{2}_{N}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega),L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega))}\\

for some constant

is of class

between

The key point is that the strong regularity guarantees that the phase variable is uniformly bounded (cf. Remark

).
Moreover, by composing
\mathcal{R}:H^{1}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)

\begin{split}&\lVert\mathrm{D}\mathcal{R}(\overline{\varphi_{0}}_{1},\overline{\sigma_{0}}_{1})[(h,k)]-\mathrm{D}\mathcal{R}(\overline{\varphi_{0}}_{2},\overline{\sigma_{0}}_{2})[(h,k)]\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
&\quad=\lVert(\xi_{1}(\cdot,T),\rho_{1}(\cdot,T))-(\xi_{2}(\cdot,T),\rho_{2}(\cdot,T)\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
&\quad\leq C\lVert(\overline{\varphi_{0}}_{1},\overline{\sigma_{0}}_{1})-(\overline{\varphi_{0}}_{2},\overline{\sigma_{0}}_{2})\rVert^{2}_{H^{2}_{N}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)},\end{split}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+(P^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi}_{1})-P^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi}_{2}))(\overline{\sigma}_{2}+\chi(1-\overline{\varphi}_{2})-\overline{\mu}_{2})\xi_{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+P^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi}_{2})((\overline{\sigma}_{1}-\overline{\sigma}_{2})-\chi(\overline{\varphi}_{1}-\overline{\varphi}_{2})-(\overline{\mu}_{1}-\overline{\mu}_{2}))\xi_{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+P(\overline{\varphi}_{1})(\rho-\chi\xi-\eta)+(P(\overline{\varphi}_{1})-P(\overline{\varphi}_{2}))(\rho_{2}-\chi\xi_{2}-\eta_{2})

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt-\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi}_{1})c\,\xi-(\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi}_{1})-\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi}_{2}))c\,\xi_{2}\hskip 20.00003pt

\displaystyle\hbox{in $Q_{T}$},

(4.27)

\displaystyle\eta=-\Delta\xi+F^{\prime\prime}(\overline{\varphi}_{1})\xi+(F^{\prime\prime}(\overline{\varphi}_{1})-F^{\prime\prime}(\overline{\varphi}_{2}))\xi_{2}-\chi\rho\hskip 20.00003pt

\displaystyle\hbox{in $Q_{T}$},

\displaystyle\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}}\xi=\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}}\eta=\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}}\rho=0\hskip 20.00003pt

\displaystyle\xi(0)=0,\hskip 10.00002pt\rho(0)=0\hskip 20.00003pt

\displaystyle\hbox{in $\Omega$}.

(4.31)

Clearly, the system (
4.27
)–(
4.31
) is satisfied in strong formulation by Proposition
4.9
, and
(\xi,\eta,\rho)
satisfy the regularities stated therein.
Hence, to prove (
4.26
), we need to perform some global estimates.
Indeed, we test (
4.27
) by
\xi
, (
4.28
) by
\Delta\xi
, (
4.29
) by
\rho
, and we sum them up to obtain:

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\lVert\xi\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\lVert\rho\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert\Delta\xi\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert\nabla\rho\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
&\quad\leq(F^{\prime\prime}(\overline{\varphi}_{1})\xi,\Delta\xi)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+((F^{\prime\prime}(\overline{\varphi}_{1})-F^{\prime\prime}(\overline{\varphi}_{2}))\xi_{2},\Delta\xi)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-2\chi(\rho,\Delta\xi)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
&\qquad+(P^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi}_{1})(\overline{\sigma}_{1}+\chi(1-\overline{\varphi}_{1})-\overline{\mu}_{1})\xi,\xi-\rho)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
&\qquad+(P^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi}_{1})-P^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi}_{2}))(\overline{\sigma}_{2}+\chi(1-\overline{\varphi}_{2})-\overline{\mu}_{2})\xi_{2},\xi-\rho)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
&\qquad+(P^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi}_{2})((\overline{\sigma}_{1}-\overline{\sigma}_{2})-\chi(\overline{\varphi}_{1}-\overline{\varphi}_{2})-(\overline{\mu}_{1}-\overline{\mu}_{2}))\xi_{2},\xi-\rho)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
&\qquad+(P(\overline{\varphi}_{1})(\rho-\chi\xi-\eta),\xi-\rho)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
&\qquad+((P(\overline{\varphi}_{1})-P(\overline{\varphi}_{2}))(\rho_{2}-\chi\xi_{2}-\eta_{2}),\xi-\rho)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
&\qquad-(\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi}_{1})c\,\xi,\xi)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-((\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi}_{1})-\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi}_{2}))c\,\xi_{2},\xi)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\kappa\lVert\rho\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.\end{split}

(4.32)

We now proceed to estimate all the terms on the right-hand side of (
4.32
).
In the estimates below we will make repeated use of Cauchy–Schwartz, Hölder and Young’s inequalities, as well as Sobolev embeddings.
For the first three terms, we see that

\displaystyle(F^{\prime\prime}(\overline{\varphi}_{1})\xi,\Delta\xi)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+((F^{\prime\prime}(\overline{\varphi}_{1})-F^{\prime\prime}(\overline{\varphi}_{2}))\xi_{2},\Delta\xi)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-2\chi(\rho,\Delta\xi)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq\frac{1}{4}\lVert\Delta\xi\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C\lVert\rho\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C\lVert\xi\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C\lVert\xi_{2}\rVert^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\lVert\overline{\varphi}_{1}-\overline{\varphi}_{2}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

where we used Remark
4.2
and the local Lipschitz continuity of
F^{\prime\prime}
.
Next, for the fourth, fifth and sixth terms, we have that

\displaystyle(P^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi}_{1})(\overline{\sigma}_{1}+\chi(1-\overline{\varphi}_{1})-\overline{\mu}_{1})\xi,\xi-\rho)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq C\left(1+\lVert\overline{\sigma}_{1}+\chi(1-\overline{\varphi}_{1})-\overline{\mu}_{1}\rVert^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\right)\lVert\xi\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C\lVert\rho\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle(P^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi}_{1})-P^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi}_{2}))(\overline{\sigma}_{2}+\chi(1-\overline{\varphi}_{2})-\overline{\mu}_{2})\xi_{2},\xi-\rho)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq C\lVert\overline{\sigma}_{2}+\chi(1-\overline{\varphi}_{2})-\overline{\mu}_{2}\rVert^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\left(\lVert\xi\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert\rho\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)+C\lVert\xi_{2}\rVert^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\lVert\overline{\varphi}_{1}-\overline{\varphi}_{2}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle(P^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi}_{2})((\overline{\sigma}_{1}-\overline{\sigma}_{2})-\chi(\overline{\varphi}_{1}-\overline{\varphi}_{2})-(\overline{\mu}_{1}-\overline{\mu}_{2}))\xi_{2},\xi-\rho)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq C\lVert\xi\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C\lVert\rho\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+C\lVert\xi_{2}\rVert^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\left(\lVert\overline{\varphi}_{1}-\overline{\varphi}_{2}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert\overline{\mu}_{1}-\overline{\mu}_{2}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert\overline{\sigma}_{1}-\overline{\sigma}_{2}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right),

where we used again Remark
4.2
and the local Lipschitz continuity of
P^{\prime}
.
Next, to estimate the seventh term, we first observe that, by (
4.28
), it holds that

\displaystyle\lVert\eta\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\lVert\Delta\xi\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C\lVert\xi\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert\xi_{2}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\lVert\overline{\varphi}_{1}-\overline{\varphi}_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C\lVert\rho\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Then, we deduce that

\displaystyle(P(\overline{\varphi}_{1})(\rho-\chi\xi-\eta),\xi-\rho)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq\frac{1}{4}\lVert\Delta\xi\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C\lVert\xi\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C\lVert\rho\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C\lVert\xi_{2}\rVert^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\lVert\overline{\varphi}_{1}-\overline{\varphi}_{2}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Going on, for the eighth term, we similarly have that

\displaystyle((P(\overline{\varphi}_{1})-P(\overline{\varphi}_{2}))(\rho_{2}-\chi\xi_{2}-\eta_{2}),\xi-\rho)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002ptC\lVert\rho_{2}-\chi\xi_{2}-\eta_{2}\rVert^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\left(\lVert\xi\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert\rho\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)+C\lVert\overline{\varphi}_{1}-\overline{\varphi}_{2}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Finally, the ninth and tenth terms can be treated as

\displaystyle(\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi}_{1})c\,\xi,\xi)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+((\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi}_{1})-\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi}_{2}))c\,\xi_{2},\xi)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq C\lVert\xi\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C\lVert\xi_{2}\rVert^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\lVert\overline{\varphi}_{1}-\overline{\varphi}_{2}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

by Remark
4.2
and the local Lipschitz continuity of
\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}
.
By putting all together and integrating over
(0,t)
, for any
t\in(0,T)
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lVert\xi(\cdot,t)\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\frac{1}{2}\lVert\rho(\cdot,t)\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\lVert\Delta\xi\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\lVert\nabla\rho\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq C\int_{0}^{T}\left(1+\lVert\overline{\sigma}_{1}+\chi(1-\overline{\varphi}_{1})-\overline{\mu}_{1}\rVert^{2}_{H^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert\rho_{2}-\chi\xi_{2}-\eta_{2}\rVert^{2}_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\right)\left(\lVert\xi\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert\rho\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+C\lVert\xi_{2}\rVert^{2}_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))}\int_{0}^{T}\left(\lVert\overline{\varphi}_{1}-\overline{\varphi}_{2}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert\overline{\mu}_{1}-\overline{\mu}_{2}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert\overline{\sigma}_{1}-\overline{\sigma}_{2}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)\,\mathrm{d}s,

where
\lVert\overline{\sigma}_{1}+\chi(1-\overline{\varphi}_{1})-\overline{\mu}_{1}\rVert^{2}_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\in L^{1}(0,T)
,
\lVert\rho_{2}-\chi\xi_{2}-\eta_{2}\rVert^{2}_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\in L^{1}(0,T)
and
\xi_{2}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))
are uniformly bounded by Theorem
4.1
and Proposition
4.9
.
Hence, by applying Gronwall’s inequality, together with elliptic regularity theory and the continuous dependence estimate (
4.7
) on the second term on the right-hand side, we infer that

\displaystyle\lVert\xi\rVert^{2}_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))}+\lVert\rho\rVert^{2}_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq C\left(\lVert\overline{\varphi_{0}}_{1}-\overline{\varphi_{0}}_{2}\rVert^{2}_{H^{2}_{N}(\Omega)}+\lVert\overline{\sigma_{0}}_{1}-\overline{\sigma_{0}}_{2}\rVert^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\right).

Moreover, by comparison in (
4.27
) and (
4.29
), we also easily get that

\lVert\xi\rVert^{2}_{H^{1}(0,T;(H^{2}_{N}(\Omega))^{*})}+\lVert\rho\rVert^{2}_{H^{1}(0,T;(H^{1}(\Omega))^{*})}\leq C\left(\lVert\overline{\varphi_{0}}_{1}-\overline{\varphi_{0}}_{2}\rVert^{2}_{H^{2}_{N}(\Omega)}+\lVert\overline{\sigma_{0}}_{1}-\overline{\sigma_{0}}_{2}\rVert^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\right).

Then, by the standard embeddings
H^{1}(0,T;(H^{2}_{N}(\Omega))^{*})\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))\hookrightarrow C^{0}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
and
H^{1}(0,T;(H^{1}(\Omega))^{*})\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))\hookrightarrow C^{0}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
, we finally deduce that

\lVert\xi(\cdot,T)\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert\rho(\cdot,T)\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\left(\lVert\overline{\varphi_{0}}_{1}-\overline{\varphi_{0}}_{2}\rVert^{2}_{H^{2}_{N}(\Omega)}+\lVert\overline{\sigma_{0}}_{1}-\overline{\sigma_{0}}_{2}\rVert^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\right),

which is (
4.26
).
This concludes the proof of Proposition
4.10
.

∎

4.2.2
Lipschitz stability

The last ingredient we need to prove a Lipschitz stability estimate for the inverse problem is the injectivity of the Fréchet derivative
\mathrm{D}\mathcal{R}(\overline{\varphi_{0}},\overline{\sigma_{0}})
for any fixed point
(\overline{\varphi_{0}},\overline{\sigma_{0}})\in H^{2}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)
.
By Proposition
4.10
, we know that
\mathrm{D}\mathcal{R}(\overline{\varphi_{0}},\overline{\sigma_{0}})[h,k]=(\xi(\cdot,T),\rho(\cdot,T))
for any
(h,k)\in H^{2}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)
, where
\xi(\cdot,T)
and
\rho(\cdot,T)
are the solutions to (
4.16
)–(
4.20
).
Therefore, by linearity, showing that
\mathrm{D}\mathcal{R}(\overline{\varphi_{0}},\overline{\sigma_{0}})
is injective means proving that if
(\xi(\cdot,T),\rho(\cdot,T))=(0,0)
in
L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)
, then also
(h,k)=(0,0)
in
H^{2}_{N}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)
.
Equivalently, this means showing a backward uniqueness result in the spirit of Theorem
4.5
for the linearised system (
4.16
)–(
4.20
).
However, the new stability result shown in Theorem
3.2
for general Cahn–Hilliard-reaction-diffusion systems through Carleman estimates allows us to show a quantitative injectivity result, by providing a stability estimate for the backward problem on the linearised system.
Then, this allows us to use the general result in
[
6
, Proposition 5]
to its full extent, by also providing an explicit estimate of the Lipschitz stability constant.
Indeed, we have the following.

Proposition 4.11
.

Assume hypotheses
A1
–
A7
. Let
(\overline{\varphi},\overline{\mu},\overline{\sigma})
be a strong solution to (
4.1
)–(
4.20
) corresponding to some initial data
(\varphi_{0},\sigma_{0})\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{ad}}
. Moreover, let
(\xi,\eta)
be a strong solution to (
4.16
)–(
4.20
) corresponding to
(h,k)\in H^{2}_{N}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)
.
Assume also that

\lVert(h,k)\rVert_{H^{2}_{N}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq L,

for some
L>0
and call
L_{1}>0
the minimal constant such that, by Proposition
4.9
,

\lVert(\xi,\rho)\rVert_{H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq L_{1}.

(4.33)

Then, there exists a constant
Q_{1}>0
, depending only on the parameters of the system, such that we have the following conditional stability estimate for any
t_{0}\in[0,T]

\lVert(\xi(\cdot,t_{0}),\rho(\cdot,t_{0}))\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq Q_{1}L^{1-\theta(t_{0})}\lVert(\xi(\cdot,T),\rho(\cdot,T))\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)}^{\theta(t_{0})},

if
\lVert(\xi(\cdot,T),\rho(\cdot,T))\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)}
is sufficiently small, where
\theta(t_{0})
is the same exponent appearing in Theorem
3.1
.

Moreover, there exists a constant
Q_{2}>0
such that

\lVert(h,k)\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq Q_{2}\left(\log\frac{L}{\lVert(\xi(\cdot,T),\rho(\cdot,T))\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)}}\right)^{-\frac{1}{4}},

(4.34)

if
\lVert(\xi(\cdot,T),\rho(\cdot,T))\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)}
is sufficiently small, where we can quantify the constant as

Q_{2}=\frac{2L_{1}^{3/4}C_{1}^{1/4}}{\beta^{1/4}},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with}\hskip 10.00002pt\beta=\frac{\lambda}{3e^{\lambda T}-3}.

(4.35)

Proof.

By rewriting the linearised system (
4.16
)–(
4.20
) only in terms of the independent variables
\xi
and
\rho
, through the substitution of (
4.17
) into (
4.16
) and (
4.18
), we get

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\xi+\Delta^{2}\xi-\chi\Delta\rho=f_{1}\hskip 20.00003pt

\displaystyle\hbox{in $Q_{T}$},

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho-\Delta\rho+\chi\Delta\xi=f_{2}\hskip 20.00003pt

\displaystyle\hbox{in $Q_{T}$},

\displaystyle\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}}\xi=\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}}\Delta\eta=\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}}\rho=0\hskip 20.00003pt

\displaystyle\hbox{on $\Sigma_{T}$},

\displaystyle\xi(0)=h,\hskip 10.00002pt\rho(0)=k\hskip 20.00003pt

\displaystyle\hbox{in $\Omega$},

where, upon some computations,

\displaystyle f_{1}

\displaystyle:=F^{\prime\prime}(\overline{\varphi})\Delta\xi+2F^{\prime\prime\prime}(\overline{\varphi})\nabla\overline{\varphi}\cdot\nabla\xi+F^{\prime\prime\prime}(\overline{\varphi})\Delta\overline{\varphi}\,\xi+F^{(4)}\nabla\overline{\varphi}\cdot\nabla\overline{\varphi}\,\xi

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+P^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi})(\overline{\sigma}+\chi(1-\overline{\varphi})-\overline{\mu})\xi+P(\overline{\varphi})(\rho-\chi\xi+\Delta\xi-F^{\prime\prime}(\overline{\varphi})\xi+\chi\rho)-\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi})c\,\xi,

\displaystyle f_{2}

\displaystyle:=-P^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi})(\overline{\sigma}+\chi(1-\overline{\varphi})-\overline{\mu})\xi-P(\overline{\varphi})(\rho-\chi\xi+\Delta\xi-F^{\prime\prime}(\overline{\varphi})\xi+\chi\rho)-\kappa\rho.

Thus, we observe that the above system has the same structure as the general system (
2.1
)–(
2.3
), with
\xi=u
,
\rho=v
,
a=b=\chi
,
F=f_{1}
and
G=f_{2}
.
Moreover, one can easily verify that the source terms
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
satisfy hypothesis (
2.4
), by essentially repeating similar estimates to those performed in Lemma
4.3
.
Hence, we can apply Theorems
2.1
,
3.1
and
3.2
to deduce the sought results on the linearised system.

We are now in the position to apply
[
6
, Proposition 5]
and establish a Lipschitz stability estimate, upon the additional restriction to a finite-dimensional subspace.
For the reader’s convenience, we report below the statement of the main tool we intend to use.

Lemma 4.12

(​​
[
6
]
, Proposition 5)
.

Let
R
and
r_{0}
be positive numbers. Let
\Lambda
be an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
K
be a subset of
\Lambda
. Assume that

K\subseteq B_{R}(0)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptB_{r_{0}}(p)\subseteq\Lambda,\text{ for any }p\in K.

(4.36)

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a Banach space and
G:\Lambda\to\mathcal{B}
an operator. Assume that
G
satisfies the following conditions:

(i)

G_{\mid K}
is injective,

(ii)

(G_{\mid K})^{-1}:G(K)\to K
is uniformly continuous with modulus of continuity
\omega_{0}(\cdot)
,

(iii)

G
is Fréchet-differentiable with derivative
\mathrm{D}G
,

(iv)

\mathrm{D}G:\Lambda\to\mathcal{L}(\Lambda,\mathcal{B})
is uniformly continuous with modulus of continuity
\omega_{1}(\cdot)
.

(v)

there exists
m_{0}>0
such that

\inf_{x\in K,\lvert y\rvert=1}\lVert\mathrm{D}G(x)[y]\rVert_{\mathcal{B}}\geq m_{0}.

\lvert x_{1}-x_{2}\rvert\leq C\lVert G(x_{1})-G(x_{2})\rVert_{\mathcal{B}},

(4.37)

where
C=\max\left\{\frac{2R}{(\omega_{0}^{-1})_{*}(\delta_{1})},\frac{2}{m_{0}}\right\}
,
\delta_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\min\{\delta_{0},r_{0}\}
and
\delta_{0}=(\omega_{1}^{-1})_{*}\left(\frac{m_{0}}{2}\right)

\omega

(\omega^{-1})_{*}(y):=\inf\,\{x\in[0,+\infty)\mid\omega(x)\geq y\}
.

Then, by applying Lemma
4.12

Theorem 4.13
.

Assume hypotheses
A1
–
A7
. Let
\Lambda
be a finite-dimensional subspace of
H^{2}_{N}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)
,

(\varphi_{0},\sigma_{0},p_{0})\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{ad}}\cap\Lambda.

such that for any choice of

and

\begin{split}&\lVert({\varphi_{0}}_{1},{\sigma_{0}}_{1})-({\varphi_{0}}_{2},{\sigma_{0}}_{2})\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
&\quad\leq C_{s}\lVert(\varphi_{1}(\cdot,T),\sigma_{1}(\cdot,T))-(\varphi_{2}(\cdot,T),\sigma_{2}(\cdot,T))\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)},\end{split}

(4.38)

where the constant
C_{s}
can be quantified as

\displaystyle C_{s}=\max\left\{2e^{\frac{16C_{0}^{2}C_{2}}{m_{0}}},\frac{2}{m_{0}}\right\},

\displaystyle\text{with}\hskip 10.00002ptm_{0}=\frac{L}{C_{\Lambda}}e^{-Q^{4}_{2}}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptC_{\Lambda}=\sup_{(h,k)\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\lVert(h,k)\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)}}{\lVert(h,k)\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)}},

where
C_{0}
is the Lipschitz constant of the Fréchet derivative, given by (
4.25
), and
M
,
L
, and
Q_{2}
are defined in (
4.8
), (
4.33
), (
4.35

Proof.

The proof follows the same strategy used in
[
7

for a similar backward problem.
Indeed, we want to apply Lemma
4.12
, so we have to verify its hypotheses.
Hence, we set
X=H^{2}_{N}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)
and
\Lambda
as above. Being a finite-dimensional subspace of
H^{2}_{N}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)
means that
\Lambda\simeq\mathbb{R}^{n}
in the notation of Lemma
4.12

K:=\mathcal{I}_{\text{ad}}\cap\Lambda\subset\Lambda
is a compact subset of
\Lambda
, so it satisfies hypothesis (
4.36
).
We consider the operator
\mathcal{R}:\Lambda\subseteq H^{2}_{N}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)
defined as
\mathcal{R}((\varphi_{0},\sigma_{0}))=(\varphi(\cdot,T),\sigma(\cdot,T))
.
Hypothesis
(i)
on the injectivity of
\mathcal{R}_{\mid K}
follows directly from Corollary
4.5
.
Also hypothesis
(ii)
follows immediately from Theorem
4.7
, with modulus of continuity
\omega_{0}(r)=C_{2}\left(\log(1/r)\right)^{-1/4}
.
Then, hypotheses
(iii)
and
(iv)
on Fréchet differentiability follow from Proposition
4.10
, with modulus of continuity
\omega_{1}(r)=C_{0}r
. Finally, we have to check condition
(v)
.
To do this, notice that for any
(\varphi_{0},\sigma_{0})\in K
and any
(h,k)\in\Lambda
, we have that
\mathrm{D}\mathcal{R}(\varphi_{0},\sigma_{0})[h,k]=(\xi(\cdot,T),\rho(\cdot,T))
, where
(\xi,\rho)
is the solution to (
4.16
)–(
4.20
).
Now, by Proposition
4.11
, we can use (
4.34

\lVert(h,k)\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq L

\lVert(h,k)\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq Q_{2}\left(\log\frac{L}{\lVert(\xi(\cdot,T),\rho(\cdot,T))\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)}}\right)^{-\frac{1}{4}},

\lVert(\xi(\cdot,T),\rho(\cdot,T))\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)}\geq Le^{-\frac{Q_{2}^{4}}{\lVert(h,k)\rVert^{4}_{L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)}}}.

Hence, we can verify condition
(v)

\displaystyle\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\varphi_{0},\sigma_{0})\in K\\
(h,k)\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}\end{subarray}}\frac{\lVert(\xi(\cdot,T),\rho(\cdot,T))\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)}}{\lVert(h,k)\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)}}\geq\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\varphi_{0},\sigma_{0})\in K\\

where we used the linearity of
\mathrm{D}\mathcal{R}
and we estimated

\lVert(h,k)\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C_{\Lambda}\lVert(h,k)\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)},

for any
(h,k)\in\Lambda

\Lambda

∎

Remark 4.14
.

We point out that the constant
C_{s}
blows up exponentially as the dimension of
\Lambda
goes to infinity.
Indeed, as one can see from its expression,
C_{s}
has a direct exponential proportionality to the constant
C_{\Lambda}
, which is finite only if
\Lambda
has a finite dimension and blows up as it becomes larger.
Additionally, one can also notice that the dependence of
C_{s}
on the final time is even worse, namely
\log(C_{s})
depends exponentially on
T
.
This means that, even if we have some kind of Lipschitz stability, one has to be very careful when designing numerical algorithms to approximate the solution.

5
Conclusions

In the present work, we addressed the backward inverse problem for a coupled system of Cahn–Hilliard-reaction-diffusion equations with cross-diffusion (chemotaxis) and source terms, establishing a novel Carleman estimate which provides the starting point to derive rigorous stability estimates for the inverse problem.
A key technical advance of our approach is that it removes the smallness restriction on the chemotaxis coefficients, which had been a limitation in earlier studies relying on logarithmic convexity arguments. This makes the framework more robust for biological applications, where such a restriction may be physically unrealistic.
We derived Hölder-type stability estimates for the reconstruction of past states at any intermediate positive time
t_{0}\in(0,T)
and a logarithmic stability estimate for the initial datum at
t=0
. These results ensure that, given a single final-time observation at time
T
, earlier configurations of the system can be recovered with quantitative reliability under natural a priori bounds.

We then apply these theoretical results to a phase-field tumour growth model widely studied in literature, which describes the tumour growth in the a-vascular phase and couples the tumour growth with a nutrient dynamics through chemotaxis and source and consumption terms. Thanks to the application of the Carleman estimate, we proved backward uniqueness and quantitative stability for the recovery of early tumour states given a single final time measurement.
Furthermore, we established a Lipschitz stability result on finite-dimensional subspaces of admissible sets for the initial data, providing a solid mathematical foundation for the development of convergent iterative discretisation algorithms.

The application of these theoretical results to phase-field tumour growth models offers significant clinical implications. Indeed, identifying the tumour’s state at initiation or earlier stages of development can provide important diagnostic and prognostic informations to clinicians which may improve diagnostic accuracy and the design of personalised radiotherapy or surgical interventions.
For these reasons, future research will focus on the development of convergent and robust iterative discretised solvers for the backward inverse problem associated to phase-field tumour growth models, leveraging on efficient finite element approximations or neural network algorithms to solve the severely ill-posed inverse reconstruction problem under different clinically relevant situations.
Further developments will concern the extension of the present theoretical framework to more complex models, in which the derivation of Carleman estimates and regularity results is more challenging, including models incorporating anisotropic effects or multi-species interactions.
