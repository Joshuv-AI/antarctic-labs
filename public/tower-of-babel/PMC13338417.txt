Effect of an electronic health record-integrated machine learning asthma risk marker on pediatrician prognostic accuracy during preschool age: a pilot randomized clinical trial
Authors: Arthur H. Owora, Bowen Jiang, Yash Shah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Early identification of children at risk for persistent asthma is challenging because preschool respiratory symptoms are heterogeneous and often overlap with transient wheezing illnesses. Machine learning-based prediction models may improve risk stratification, but few have been integrated into electronic health record (EHR) systems or evaluated for their effect on clinician decision-making. We evaluated whether access to an EHR-integrated machine learning–based Passive Digital Marker (PDM) improved pediatrician prognostic accuracy for school-age asthma and assessed the tool’s usability, acceptability, and feasibility. In this pilot randomized clinical trial, practicing pediatricians in Indiana were randomized using a Solomon 4-group design with pretest and posttest assessments. Pediatricians evaluated 10 standardized pediatric patient vignettes derived from longitudinal EHR data from birth through age 3 years. Children with documented asthma diagnoses between ages 6 and 11 years were classified as cases. The PDM classified children as high or low risk for persistent school-age asthma using routinely collected clinical data. The primary outcome was clinician prognostic accuracy, defined as the proportion of correctly classified vignettes;secondary outcomes included usability, acceptability, and feasibility. Thirty-four pediatricians participated, and all completed study procedures. Clinicians with access to the PDM demonstrated higher prognostic accuracy than clinicians without access (mean [95% CI] 0.83 [0.69–0.97] vs 0.61 [0.52–0.70]; P = .008). In a mixed-effects model accounting for clustering of vignette assessments within clinicians, a significant interaction between PDM access and assessment timing was observed (b = 0.10; 95% CI 0.01–0.18; P = .02), indicating improved prognostic accuracy during posttest assessments when clinicians used the PDM. In this pilot randomized clinical trial, an EHR-integrated machine learning—based asthma risk marker improved pediatrician prognostic accuracy and demonstrated favorable usability and feasibility. Introduction

Early identification of children at risk for persistent asthma remains a major challenge in pediatric clinical practice. Asthma affects millions of children in the United States and remains a leading cause of pediatric morbidity, emergency department visits, and school absenteeism1. However, diagnosing and predicting asthma during early childhood is difficult because respiratory symptoms in preschool-aged children are heterogeneous and frequently overlap with transient viral wheezing illnesses2–4. Many young children experience episodic wheezing that resolves with age, whereas others develop persistent asthma requiring long-term management5,6. Distinguishing between these trajectories during early childhood is therefore an important but complex clinical task.

Disease trajectories of wheezing in childhood are highly variable and influenced by multiple environmental, genetic, and immunologic factors7,8. Because symptoms evolve over time, clinicians often face substantial uncertainty when estimating the likelihood that a young child with recurrent wheezing will develop persistent asthma. Early identification of children at higher risk could enable closer monitoring, anticipatory guidance, and earlier intervention strategies to improve long-term respiratory outcomes.

Several clinical prediction tools have been developed to estimate future asthma risk in young children9–11. The Asthma Predictive Index and related risk scores derived from cohort studies provide structured approaches to risk stratification but demonstrate only modest predictive performance across populations9–11. Traditional risk scores are typically based on regression models that assume linear relationships among predictors and may not adequately capture complex interactions among environmental exposures, allergic comorbidities, and evolving symptom patterns9–12. In addition, some predictors required for these models such as pulmonary function testing or inflammatory biomarkers are difficult to obtain reliably in preschool-aged children13.

Electronic health records (EHRs) provide an opportunity to improve early asthma risk stratification. Longitudinal EHR data capture detailed clinical information during early childhood, including diagnoses, medication prescriptions, respiratory symptoms, and comorbid allergic conditions. Informatics studies have demonstrated that EHR-derived data can be used to identify asthma phenotypes and ascertain respiratory outcomes across clinical settings14,15. However, these data are rarely synthesized into tools that provide structured prognostic insights for clinicians during routine care.

Machine learning (ML) approaches offer an alternative framework for modeling complex clinical data. ML methods can identify nonlinear relationships and interactions among high-dimensional predictors and have demonstrated improved predictive performance across several medical domains16–18. In pediatric asthma research, decision tree-based ML approaches have shown improved discrimination compared with traditional regression-based prediction models for identifying children at risk for persistent asthma19. Building on this work, recent studies have demonstrated that ML-derived pediatric asthma risk scores can be externally validated using integrated EHR systems and deployed as passive digital markers for asthma risk stratification20.

Despite these advances, the clinical impact of predictive analytics depends not only on model performance but also on usability and integration into clinical workflows. Clinical decision support systems embedded within EHR platforms have the potential to assist clinicians in synthesizing complex patient data and reducing diagnostic uncertainty21. However, decision support tools are frequently underused when they are poorly aligned with clinician workflows or perceived as difficult to interpret22. Accordingly, rigorous evaluation of artificial intelligence—based clinical tools requires assessment of both predictive performance and implementation outcomes, including usability, acceptability, and feasibility23,24.

To address these gaps, we developed an EHR-integrated machine learning—based Passive Digital Marker (PDM) designed to classify preschool-aged children as having high or low risk for persistent school-age asthma using routinely collected clinical data20. In this pilot randomized clinical trial, we evaluated whether access to the PDM improved pediatrician prognostic accuracy for school-age asthma and assessed the usability, acceptability, and feasibility of the tool in a controlled vignette-based decision-making study.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a pilot randomized clinical trial using a Solomon 4-group design with pretest and posttest assessments to evaluate the usability, acceptability, feasibility, and preliminary efficacy of an EHR—integrated machine learning based PDM for early asthma risk assessment. The Solomon design allows evaluation of intervention effects while assessing potential bias introduced by pretest exposure. The Solomon 4-group design was selected to distinguish the effect of the PDM intervention from potential learning effects associated with repeated vignette assessment. By including both pretested and non-pretested intervention and control groups, the design permits evaluation of whether changes in prognostic accuracy are attributable to exposure to the decision-support tool, exposure to the pretest itself, or the combination of both.

The study was designed as a pilot trial to estimate intervention effect sizes and inform the design of a fully powered pragmatic clinical trial. The trial protocol followed reporting guidance for artificial intelligence-based clinical interventions, including the CONSORT-AI and SPIRIT-AI extensions23,24. No substantive changes were made to trial outcomes, eligibility criteria, intervention procedures, or planned analyses after trial commencement. Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of this study. The study was approved by the institutional review board of Indiana University (15873). All clinicians provided written informed consent, and all research methods and procedures were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The trial was prospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05826561) on April 12, 2023.

Setting and participants

Practicing pediatricians were recruited from clinics in Indiana over a 6-month recruitment period.

Eligible participants were board-certified or board-eligible pediatricians providing outpatient pediatric care. No restrictions were applied regarding years of experience, practice setting, or demographic characteristics.

Thirty-four pediatricians were enrolled and randomized. All participants completed study procedures, resulting in a 100% completion rate.

Randomization and masking

Participants were randomized using a computer-generated allocation sequence to either: PDM-supported assessment (+ PDM) or Standard assessment without PDM support (SAU). Randomization followed a Solomon 4-group design, yielding four study groups: SAU Pre-Post Test, SAU Post-Test Only, PDM Pre-Post Test and PDM Post-Test Only.

In the SAU Pre-Post Test condition, clinicians completed both assessments without PDM support. In the PDM Pre-Post Test condition, clinicians completed pretest assessments without PDM support and posttest assessments with PDM support. The PDM Post-Test Only and SAU Post-Test Only conditions completed assessments with and without PDM support, respectively, without pretest exposure, enabling evaluation of intervention effects independent of potential pretest sensitization.

Because the PDM intervention involved provision of decision support output, clinicians could not be blinded to intervention assignment. However, clinicians (participants) were blinded to vignette case status, meaning they were unaware whether the child represented in each vignette later developed asthma.

Artificial intelligence intervention: passive digital marker (PDM)

The PDM is an EHR-integrated machine learning algorithm developed to classify preschool-aged children as having either high or low risk of persistent school-age asthma. The model was trained using longitudinal structured and unstructured EHR data collected from birth through age 3 years and predicts asthma diagnosis between ages 4 and 11 years20. Previous studies have demonstrated the feasibility of using EHR-derived data for childhood asthma identification and prediction, as well as the superior performance of decision tree-based machine learning approaches compared with traditional regression-based risk scores14,15,19. Machine learning methods are particularly well suited to modeling complex, nonlinear relationships among respiratory symptoms, allergic comorbidities, respiratory infections, medication use, family history, and other clinical predictors that contribute to childhood asthma risk15.

To support clinical decision-making, clinicians assigned to the PDM condition were provided with a binary risk classification (high risk vs low risk) together with a structured summary of relevant patient-specific risk factors derived from the EHR, including respiratory symptoms, allergic comorbidities, respiratory infections, and family history of asthma. The underlying machine learning model synthesized these variables into a clinically interpretable risk classification, while the accompanying risk-factor summary provided context for the prediction. This design was intended to facilitate clinician interpretation, minimize cognitive burden, and approximate a clinically feasible decision-support interface21,22. An example of the clinician-facing PDM display is provided in Supplementary Appendix 1.

Study procedures

Participants (pediatricians) evaluated 10 standardized pediatric patient vignettes derived from authentic longitudinal EHR data spanning birth through age 3 years. Each vignette summarized clinically relevant early-life data, including respiratory symptom history, allergic comorbidities, medication use, and family history.

Five vignettes represented children with documented asthma diagnoses between ages 6 and 11 years based on International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) codes, and five represented children without such diagnoses. The presentation of patient vignettes was randomized.

Clinicians were asked to estimate the likelihood that each child would develop asthma by school age (≥ 6 years) based solely on the information provided in the vignette.

A vignette-based design was selected to standardize clinical information presented to clinicians and allow controlled evaluation of prognostic decision-making. Such approaches are commonly used in diagnostic research to evaluate clinician reasoning while minimizing ethical and logistical challenges associated with real-time patient experimentation21.

Outcomes

Primary outcome

The primary outcome was clinician prognostic accuracy, defined as the proportion of correctly classified vignettes. Accuracy was calculated as: correct classifications ÷ total vignettes evaluated. Values ranged from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating greater prognostic accuracy.

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes assessed implementation-related measures. Usability: Usability of the decision support tool (PDM) was measured using a modified usability instrument adapted from the Healthcare Systems Usability Scale (HSUS), a validated usability scale developed for evaluating clinical decision support systems25.

Acceptability/behavioral intention: Acceptability was measured using a behavioral intention scale assessing clinicians’ likelihood of using the tool if integrated into the EHR. Behavioral intention measures are widely used in technology adoption research to evaluate motivation and likelihood of future use26. Both usability and acceptability measures used Likert-type response scales (0–5), with higher scores indicating greater usability or behavioral intention21.

Feasibility measures

Feasibility was evaluated using predefined study metrics: recruitment within the planned accrual period, proportion of clinicians completing vignette assessments, and completion of usability and acceptability measures.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics summarized clinician characteristics, feasibility outcomes, and usability and acceptability scores. Continuous variables are reported as means with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and categorical variables as counts and percentages.

Primary analysis

The Solomon design was analyzed using a factorial modeling framework including intervention group (+ PDM vs SAU), assessment timing (pretest vs posttest), and the interaction between intervention and timing. The interaction term between intervention assignment and assessment timing was included to evaluate potential pretest sensitization (learning) effects. A statistically significant interaction would indicate that changes in prognostic accuracy between pretest and posttest assessments differ according to intervention status. In contrast, a nonsignificant interaction would suggest that observed differences were attributable primarily to the intervention or assessment timing alone. Because clinicians contributed multiple vignette assessments, a linear mixed-effects model was estimated to account for clustering of observations (vignettes) within clinicians. The model included: fixed effects for intervention group, fixed effects for assessment timing, an interaction term between intervention and timing, and a random intercept for clinician. Regression coefficients (b) with 95% confidence intervals were reported. Group distributions of prognostic accuracy were summarized using boxplots with overlaid individual clinician observations. Boxes represent the interquartile range, center lines represent medians, and whiskers extend to the most extreme values within 1.5 × IQR.

Secondary analyses

Accuracy for case and control vignettes was examined separately using Welch-adjusted t tests because of unequal group sizes. Two-sided P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Sample size considerations

This study was designed as a pilot randomized trial to estimate intervention effect sizes and assess feasibility rather than provide definitive evidence of efficacy. The trial was originally designed to enroll 100 clinicians over a 12-month recruitment period to support estimation of intervention effects and inform a future fully powered pragmatic clinical trial. Recruitment was discontinued after 6 months when NIH funding support for the study was terminated because of changes in agency funding priorities. At the time recruitment ceased, 34 clinicians had been enrolled and completed study procedures. Consequently, analyses were performed using all available data from enrolled participants and should be interpreted as pilot estimates intended to inform future studies.

All analyses were conducted using R software with statistical modeling performed using mixed-effects modeling libraries. No outcome data were missing because all randomized clinicians completed study procedures; therefore, no imputation methods were required.

Results

Participant characteristics

Thirty-four pediatricians were enrolled and randomized during a 6-month recruitment period. Participants were allocated across the four Solomon design groups: SAU Pre-Post Test (n = 10), SAU Post-Test Only (n = 4), PDM Pre-Post Test (n = 11), and PDM Post-Test Only (n = 9) (Fig. 1). All randomized clinicians completed vignette assessments and study questionnaires, resulting in a 100% completion rate. Baseline clinician characteristics were similar across study conditions (Table 1). Overall, 18 clinicians (53%) were male and 16 (47%) were female. Vignette characteristics

Across clinicians, 340 vignette assessments were completed (10 vignettes per clinician). The vignettes were derived from authentic longitudinal EHR data spanning birth through age 3 years. Five vignettes represented children with documented asthma diagnoses between ages 6 and 11 years (cases), and five represented children without such diagnoses (controls). The PDM classified all case vignettes as high risk and all control vignettes as low risk, corresponding to documented asthma outcomes.

Accuracy for case and control vignettes

Accuracy for case vignettes (children who later developed asthma) was significantly higher among clinicians with access to the PDM compared with those performing standard assessment (0.77 vs 0.47; P = 0.028). Accuracy for control vignettes (children who did not develop asthma) did not differ significantly between groups (0.89 vs 0.74; P = 0.15). The improvement in overall prognostic accuracy associated with PDM access was therefore primarily attributable to improved identification of children who subsequently developed asthma.

Usability and behavioral intention

Following exposure to the PDM, clinicians reported favorable usability and behavioral intention scores. Using modified usability and behavioral intention scales, mean (SD) scores were 4 (1) for usability and 5 (2) for behavioral intention on 0–5 Likert-type scales. Overall, 90% of clinicians reported usability ratings ≥ 4, and 90% reported behavioral intention scores ≥ 4, indicating strong perceived usability and willingness to adopt the tool if integrated into the EHR. No intervention-related adverse events, unintended consequences, or usability-related safety concerns were identified during study participation.

Feasibility

Recruitment was discontinued after 6 months because NIH funding support for the project was terminated owing to changes in agency funding priorities. Thirty-four clinicians were enrolled and completed study procedures, providing sufficient pilot data to estimate intervention effect sizes for future studies.

Summary of findings

In this pilot randomized clinical trial involving 34 clinicians and 340 vignette assessments, access to an EHR-integrated machine learning–based Passive Digital Marker (PDM) was associated with higher clinician prognostic accuracy compared with standard assessment without decision support. Improvements were primarily observed during posttest assessments when clinicians used the PDM and were largely driven by improved identification of children who later developed asthma.

Clinicians reported favorable usability and behavioral intention scores, and all enrolled participants completed study procedures. These data provide preliminary evidence supporting the feasibility of evaluating machine learning–enabled asthma risk markers in clinical decision-making and inform future larger pragmatic trials.

Discussion

Principal findings

In this pilot randomized clinical trial involving 34 pediatric clinicians and 340 vignette assessments, access to an EHR-integrated machine learning—based Passive Digital Marker (PDM) was associated with higher clinician prognostic accuracy for school-age asthma compared with standard assessment without decision support. Improvements occurred primarily during posttest assessments when clinicians used the PDM and were largely driven by improved identification of children who later developed asthma. Clinicians also reported favorable usability and behavioral intention scores. Together, these findings suggest that automated synthesis of longitudinal early-life EHR data may support clinician decision-making during periods of diagnostic uncertainty.

Interpretation of findings

Predicting persistent asthma during early childhood remains challenging because respiratory symptoms in preschool-aged children are heterogeneous and frequently overlap with transient wheezing illnesses2–6. Existing clinical prediction tools and regression-based risk scores provide structured approaches to risk stratification but demonstrate modest predictive performance and limited adoption in routine practice9,11. Many traditional models assume linear relationships among predictors and may not capture complex interactions among environmental exposures, allergic comorbidities, and evolving symptom patterns11.

Machine learning approaches offer an alternative framework for modeling high-dimensional clinical data and have demonstrated improved predictive discrimination in our prior studies of childhood asthma risk19,20. Consistent with this literature, provision of the PDM risk classification in this study was associated with improved clinician prognostic accuracy, particularly for identifying children who later developed asthma. Earlier identification of children at higher risk for persistent asthma may allow clinicians to provide anticipatory guidance and closer monitoring during early childhood when disease trajectories are still evolving.

The improvement in prognostic accuracy may reflect the ability of the PDM to reduce cognitive load associated with synthesizing longitudinal respiratory histories, allowing clinicians to focus on risk interpretation rather than information retrieval and integration. By summarizing clinically relevant risk factors and providing a concise risk classification, the PDM may facilitate more efficient processing of complex prognostic information during a period of diagnostic uncertainty.

Clinical decision support and workflow integration

The potential impact of predictive analytics depends not only on statistical performance but also on integration into clinical workflows21. Clinical decision support systems embedded within EHR platforms can assist clinicians in synthesizing complex patient information and reducing diagnostic uncertainty21. However, such tools are often underused when poorly aligned with clinician workflows or perceived as difficult to interpret22.

In this study, clinicians reported favorable usability and behavioral intention scores after exposure to the PDM, suggesting preliminary acceptability of the tool. Importantly, clinicians were provided with both a binary risk classification and a structured summary of relevant patient risk factors rather than model coefficients or complex statistical outputs. This design was intended to balance interpretability with workflow efficiency and may have contributed to the favorable usability ratings observed in this pilot study. The PDM was intentionally designed to align with the “5 Rights” framework for clinical decision support by delivering the right information (patient-specific asthma risk and relevant risk factors), to the right person (the pediatric clinician), in the right format (a clinically interpretable risk classification accompanied by a structured risk-factor summary), through the right channel (the electronic health record), and at the right point in workflow (during prognostic decision-making in a period of diagnostic uncertainty before a definitive asthma diagnosis can be established)21,22,27. By emphasizing both interpretability and workflow integration, the PDM sought to operationalize established CDS design principles that have been associated with successful adoption and effectiveness of decision-support interventions22,27. Although usability was evaluated in a controlled vignette-based environment, these findings indicate that automated asthma risk classification derived from routine EHR data may be perceived as clinically useful.

Implications for early asthma detection

Delayed recognition of persistent asthma and variability in early diagnostic assessment remain ongoing clinical challenges2–6. Leveraging longitudinal EHR data to generate structured prognostic insights may help reduce diagnostic uncertainty. Informatics approaches, including natural language processing and automated chart review, have demonstrated the feasibility of identifying asthma-related patterns in EHR systems14,15,20. However, translation of these methods into actionable clinical decision support tools remains limited22. Integration of such tools within EHR systems may allow automated synthesis of longitudinal clinical data and support clinician judgment during early asthma risk assessment.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was smaller than originally planned, and group allocation was unbalanced, consistent with the pilot design. Consequently, findings should be interpreted as preliminary and require confirmation in larger studies; however, the trial achieved its primary pilot objectives of evaluating feasibility and generating effect-size estimates for future pragmatic evaluations. Second, prognostic accuracy was evaluated using standardized vignettes rather than real-time clinical encounters, and clinician behavior may differ in practice. Additionally, clinicians evaluated a limited number of patient vignettes (10 total), which may not fully represent the broad spectrum of pediatric respiratory presentations encountered in clinical practice. Childhood asthma is characterized by substantial heterogeneity in symptom patterns, comorbid conditions, and disease trajectories2–7. Although the vignettes were derived from authentic longitudinal electronic health record data and included both future asthma cases and controls, future studies should evaluate larger and more diverse clinical scenarios to better characterize the impact of the PDM across a wider range of patient presentations. Third, outcome classification relied on documented asthma diagnoses in the EHR, which may reflect variation in clinical documentation. Finally, clinicians were recruited from a limited geographic region, which may limit generalizability.

Future directions

Future pragmatic randomized trials are needed to determine whether our EHR-integrated machine learning-based asthma risk tool improves clinician decision-making and patient outcomes in routine pediatric practice. Such studies should evaluate patient-level outcomes, including timing of asthma diagnosis, treatment initiation, and respiratory morbidity.

Conclusion

In this pilot randomized clinical trial, access to an EHR-integrated machine learning—based asthma risk marker improved clinician prognostic accuracy in vignette-based assessments and demonstrated favorable usability. These findings support further evaluation of machine learning–enabled risk stratification tools integrated within EHR systems to improve early identification of children at risk for persistent asthma.
