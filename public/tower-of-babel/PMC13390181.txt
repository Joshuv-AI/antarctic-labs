Facial Retaining Ligaments Influence Basal Cell Carcinoma Growth in the Midface and Periorbital Region
Authors: Margareta Morrissette, Jin Hua Li, Nandita Varma Vegesna, Kira Minkis, Anthony P. Sclafani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesFacial retaining ligaments (RLs) are well‐characterized for their role in soft‐tissue support and in rhytidectomy. This study investigates whether RLs also act as anatomical barriers that influence the spread of basal cell carcinoma (BCC). We hypothesized that the dermal insertion of RLs impedes the dermal spread pattern of BCCs, specifically causing them to grow along the RL.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of 163 patients who underwent Mohs micrographic surgery (MMS) for BCC. Post‐MMS defects were used as a proxy for the BCC size and shape. The defects were categorized as “near,” “far,” or “crossing” based on their proximity to the RL. Defect symmetry was quantified using the perpendicular‐to‐parallel ratio of the defect relative to the RL. A relative proximity ratio (RPR) was used for continuous linear regression analysis.ResultsAnalysis revealed significant defect asymmetry in lesions near RLs. The “near” group demonstrated a growth pattern parallel to the RL (ratio of 0.73) compared to the “far” group (ratio of 0.98, p < 0.0001). Linear regression confirmed that RPR was a predictor of tumor growth parallel to the RL (B = 0.099, p < 0.0001). Only 5.5% of lesions breached the RL.ConclusionRLs correlate with an eccentric, oblong BCC growth pattern parallel to the direction of the ligament. Recognizing lesions within these zones may improve preoperative counseling and reconstructive planning.Level of EvidenceIII.

Introduction

Basal cell carcinoma (BCC) is the most prevalent type of skin cancer among humans, primarily affecting the head and neck region, increasing with age [1, 2, 3, 4]. This cancer arises from the epidermal basaloid cells and manifests in various clinical forms, with nodular BCC being the most common variant [5]. Treatment options for BCC include surgical excision, Mohs micrographic surgery (MMS), radiotherapy, topical treatment options, and local destructive modalities. MMS stands out as an effective technique, offering potentially superior outcomes compared to other approaches [6, 7, 8, 9]. It involves systematic removal and examination of thin layers of skin cancer, ensuring maximal tumor removal while preserving healthy tissue [9]. This meticulous removal allows us to analyze the excised tissue and understand the original tumor's shape based on the resulting defect. At our institution, facial plastic and reconstructive surgeons (FPRS) are often involved with the reconstruction of more complex defects. Through this collaboration, it was noted that there may be an association between the shape of the defect, corresponding to the shape of the tumor, and the location of facial retaining ligaments (RLs).

RLs are critical anatomical structures in the field of FPRS known for their important role in rhytidectomy. Deep plane rhytidectomies involve the careful release of these ligaments to achieve adequate soft tissue repositioning [10, 11], so knowledge of the location of the RLs is essential for a successful procedure. RLs provide a secure attachment between the deep fascial plane and the dermis and form the subcutaneous septa that separate and support facial fat compartments [10]. Broadly speaking, although differences exist in literature regarding the precise nomenclature of RLs, they overlie five areas of the face: the periorbital region (orbital RL), the zygoma (zygomatic RL), the masseter (masseteric RL), the mandible (mandibular RL), and the temporalis (superior temporal septum, inferior temporal septum), as seen in Figure 1 [10, 11, 12, 13]. Mendelson's work has analyzed the presence and reliability of the location of these RLs [14, 15], and cadaveric studies have also shown predictability in the location of these ligaments [10, 16, 17, 18]. The zygomatic and orbital RLs are further classified as being “true” ligaments due to their origination from the periosteum, as opposed to others such as the masseteric ligament, which is less robust as it originates from fascia rather than periosteum [19].

While there is significant research in RLs for their role in facelift surgery, it is unclear whether their secure attachment also serves a role in preventing the spread of cutaneous cancers, such as BCCs. Clinical observations suggest that excisions near RLs tend to exhibit more oblong shapes, potentially indicating a role of the RLs in containing tumor spread. Therefore, our study aims to investigate the possible barrier effect of the RLs on the spread of BCC.

Materials and Methods

This study investigated the potential influence of RLs on BCC spread. We conducted a retrospective cohort study (IRB 24‐04027358) approved by our institution, analyzing 163 patients (adults > 18 years of age) who underwent MMS for excision of a BCC of the face between 2012 and 2023. The patients were identified through electronic medical records by using CPT codes for MMS of the face. Data collected included patient age, sex, pertinent medical history, picture of post‐Mohs excision lesion, operative report information (size of lesion, depth of lesion), and pathology subtype. A total of 500 cases were initially identified based on CPT codes. However, the majority of these cases were excluded because the lesions were located outside the anatomical regions of interest of this study. Only patients with postoperative photographs of adequate quality following MMS were included. Patients who were immunosuppressed or had a prior history of rhytidectomy were excluded. Based on these criteria, a final sample of 163 patients was included in the analysis.

Lesion Spread Measurement

To assess if RLs serve as a fixed anatomical boundary, lesions were analyzed for growth patterns relative to the ligamentous plane. Measurements were performed using Mirror medical imaging software (Canfield Scientific, Parsippany, NJ). Digital images were calibrated by converting pixel counts to centimeters based on the maximal defect dimension recorded in the MMS operative report within the EHR. The location of the RL was overlaid on each photograph based on surface topography and confirmed by two physicians. The RL's tangential plane was approximated using a line of best‐fit. Asymmetry was quantified by the ratio of the perpendicular length to the parallel length (Figure 2). A perpendicular‐to‐parallel ratio of one indicates a lesion exhibiting circular growth. A ratio of less than one indicates growth parallel to the RL, and a ratio of greater than one indicates growth toward the RL (Figure 2).

Subcategorization of Lesions

The lesions were then subcategorized based on the RL. In this analysis, we focused on midface lesions near the nasolabial, orbital, zygomatic, and masseteric RLs. The above measurements were done based on the RL closest to the lesion. The lesions were then categorized as near, far, or crossing that respective RL. Lesions that overlaid the RL were classified as crossing. To account for different lesion sizes, lesions were classified as near if the distance from the lesion edge to the RL was within one‐half of the lesion's parallel length. Lesions were classified as far if the distance from the lesion edge to the RL was greater than one‐half of the lesion's parallel length.

To further account for lesion size, the relative proximity ratio (RPR) was calculated and defined as the distance from the lesion edge to the RL divided by the parallel length of the lesion.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Multiple linear regression analyses were conducted to assess if lesion proximity to the RL predicted the perpendicular‐to‐parallel length. The models assessed whether the RPR (defined as distance from the lesion to the RL divided by the length parallel to the RL) predicted the perpendicular‐to‐parallel length ratio. All models were adjusted for mean lesion area and pathology subtype. ANOVA and two group chi‐square tests were also used to assess for differences between the three categories (near, far, crossing) and two categories (near vs. far), respectively, and the perpendicular‐to‐parallel ratio. A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Sample Distribution and Categorical Analysis of Defect Asymmetry

A total of 163 post‐MMS samples located in the mid‐face were included in the final analysis based on the availability of adequate quality post‐MMS photographs and excluding patients who were immunosuppressed or had a previous rhytidectomy. To evaluate the impact of the presence of RLs on the growth of the BCC, the post‐Mohs defects (n = 163) were categorized into one of three categories: near (n = 97), far (n = 57), and crossing (n = 9), as previously described (Table 1). Of note, a small subset of the lesions was found to have crossed the RL boundary. For the overall cohort (Table 1), ANOVA revealed a highly statistically significant difference between the perpendicular‐to‐parallel ratio between these three proximity categories (p < 0.0001). When comparing lesions near versus far from the RL, t‐test also confirmed that the near lesions had a more oblong growth pattern, with an average perpendicular‐to‐parallel ratio of 0.73 for near lesions compared to 0.98 for far lesions (p < 0.0001). The oblong, parallel to RL growth pattern difference was not observed when comparing far lesions, ratio of 0.98, to crossing lesions, ratio of 0.99 (p = 0.84).

Continuous Relationship: RPR

With the categorical analyses having demonstrated clear differences between the three groups, we next determined if there is a continuous relationship between ligament proximity and tumor morphology. In order to do this, we utilized the RPR as a continuous variable, defined as the distance from the lesion to the RL divided by the parallel length of the lesion. Multiple linear regression was performed to determine if the RPR predicted the perpendicular‐to‐parallel ratio. After controlling for mean lesion area and pathological subtype, the RPR remained a statistically significant predictor of tumor shape across the entire cohort (B estimate = 0.099, p < 0.0001) and within the orbital (B estimate = 0.243, p < 0.044) subcategory (Table 2). Of note, the significance of the RPR persisted regardless of the histological aggressiveness of the BCC pathological subtype.

Discussion

This study provides the first quantitative evidence that RLs can act as natural anatomical barriers to the spread of BCCs. Our data show a clear correlation between proximity to RL and tumor growth, with lesions located closer to these attachments experiencing growth parallel to the ligament. While RLs have long been a fundamental concept in facial plastic surgery for their role in rhytidectomy for soft tissue repositioning, their function as a biological barricade in cutaneous oncology represents a novel shift in understanding tumor behavior in the midface and periorbital region.

Regional Barrier Strength

The orbital ligaments showed the highest statistical significance in both the categorical analysis as well as the continuous analysis. This is particularly noteworthy as it may be indicative of differences in strength of individual RLs.

Early on, Mendelson described the relative strength of the orbital and zygomatic RLs as areas of strong ligamentous fixation compared to other RLs [15, 20]. The orbital and zygomatic RLs are defined as true ligaments given their attachments directly to the skin [17]. Brandt et al. performed the first cadaveric studies looking to precisely identify the strength of individual RLs and found that the zygomatic RL was the strongest, followed by the orbital RL [19]. Our clinical findings from this study align with these biomechanical properties and early observations. In our continuous analysis, outside of the overall cohort, the orbital RL demonstrated the only significant effect (B estimate = 0.243, p < 0.044) to predict the perpendicular‐to‐parallel ratio, which suggests that the physical density of the orbital ligament may be a strong driver in the barrier against tumor expansion. However, the zygomatic RL did not show significance, suggesting that more samples may be needed for further analysis.

Clinical Implications and Patient Counseling

These results have immediate practical applications for the preoperative patient consultation and surgical planning. Traditionally, Mohs surgeons anticipate margins based on clinical appearance of the lesion. Our data suggest that the anatomical location of the lesion relative to the RL may also be used as a critical predictor. When a BCC is noted to abut an RL, particularly in the orbital regions, the surgeon should maintain a higher degree of suspicion for a more extensive than expected spread.

For the reconstructive surgeon, this predictability will serve to help with preoperative planning and counseling. Knowing a defect near the orbital RL is more likely to be oblong rather than circular allows for better anticipation of final defect size, planning of local flaps, and better preoperative discussions with the patient.

Limitations and Next Steps

It should be noted that this is a single institution design which may limit external validity. Regarding the study design, while a retrospective analysis of post‐Mohs defects may be viewed as a limitation, it is important to note that the post‐Mohs defect is the only true representation of the tumor's subclinical extension. Clinical photography of the primary lesion may underestimate the spread of the BCC, while the final Mohs defect captures the total biological footprint. While the defect size is influenced by surgical technique and skin elasticity introducing potential measurement bias, it remains the gold standard for defining the true spread of the lesion.

It should also be recognized that the localization and marking of the RLs on a two‐dimensional, non‐standardized clinical photograph involves inherent estimation. To mitigate the observer bias, we implemented a verification protocol involving two physicians independently analyzing the defects and mapping out the RLs based on known, fixed, anatomical markers. Additionally, all measurements were performed in duplicate and the mean was used for the final analysis.

Of the samples, only a small portion of them crossed the facial RL (9 of 163). While this may further strengthen the hypothesis of RLs serving as a barrier to inhibit the spread of the lesions, the small number of lesions limits the analysis and robustness of the comparisons across the groups. Analysis of more samples may mitigate this finding.

Finally, these findings demonstrate a correlation, not necessarily a causation, between the location of the RLs and tumor morphology. While the statistical significance suggests a barrier effect, it cannot be definitively concluded that the ligaments cause the eccentric growth without further longitudinal studies or mechanical tissue studies. The pattern of ligamentous fixation defines the boundaries that compartmentalize the face, and our data also suggest that tumors may, to some degree, respect these natural barriers. Additionally, this study analyzed solely BCCs, which are much less aggressive than other malignancies of the skin, notably squamous cell carcinoma and melanoma. Further work can determine whether this barrier effect holds true with more aggressive lesions.

To further validate these findings, this research may be transitioned into a prospective clinical study. Future iterations may involve mapping the RLs directly onto the patient's face by the performing surgeon in the preoperative setting. This will allow the utilization of the tactile feedback of a physical examination and provide for a more precise, three‐dimensional estimation of the location of the RLs as compared to retrospective, two‐dimensional photographic analysis. Furthermore, this study only had 9 lesions (5.5%) crossing an RL. While this further exemplifies the barrier effect of the RLs, these lesions that do cross the boundaries represent an area for future investigation, potentially involving histopathological analysis of both the RL and the lesion itself.

Conclusion

This study is the first to our knowledge to look at the effect RLs have on the spread and growth of facial BCCs. Lesions near an RL show a growth pattern that is preferentially parallel to the ligament. This effect was most pronounced in the orbital region, supporting previous findings that some RLs have denser, stronger attachments, as characterized in the foundational work of Mendelson.

While these results currently only demonstrate correlation, they are a good first step to further research. Recognizing these zones of anatomical resistance may serve to be a vital future component of preoperative planning and patient counseling. This will help better anticipate surgical margins and optimize reconstructive design for improved functional and esthetic outcomes and increased patient satisfaction.
