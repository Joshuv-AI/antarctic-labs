 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

US nuclear energy expansion goals are driving the demand for manufacturing routes that can rapidly
produce large, complex, near-net-shape components. Powder metallurgy hot isostatic pressing (PM-HIP)
is an advanced manufacturing technique that can be economically scaled up while alleviating the supply
chain challenges that forging and casting face in terms of cost and lead-time constraints. This makes PM-
HIP a viable technology to aid and accelerate large-scale part manufacturing for nuclear applications.
However, large-scale qualification and deployment of this technology require a thorough understanding
of the influence of powder feedstock quality, powder handling history, hot isostatic pressing (HIP)
parameters, and subsequent heat treatment on microstructural evolution and elevated-temperature
mechanical performance.

The present work focuses on 316 austenitic stainless steel (SS316), which is most commonly used in
high-temperature environments for nuclear applications. Results from this study show that PM-HIPed
SS316 meets ASME tensile requirements at room temperature and at elevated temperatures. However,
creep performance of PM-HIPed SS316 remains inferior to its wrought counterpart, demonstrating that
tensile performance alone is not a reliable metric for long-duration, high-temperature integrity. Further,
this report delineates powder-derived microstructural features that govern creep damage, with key
evidence pointing to “microstructural inheritance” from gas-atomized powder feedstocks. Commercial
SS316 powders of varying chemical compositions and recycling histories were studied, and the results
showed large differences in elemental segregation, oxide surface layers, and secondary phase
distributions. Multiscale characterization revealed segregation of chromium, molybdenum, manganese,
and silicon at the boundaries and the precipitation of Mn, Si, and Mo oxides. During HIP consolidation,
these surface oxides transformed into decorated prior particle boundaries and grain-boundary inclusions
that persisted through conventional post-HIP solution annealing treatment. The retained oxides in post-
HIP microstructures were found to influence grain growth, precipitation behavior, and ultimately creep
cavitation and fracture. Such post-HIP heat treatments are therefore limited by a complex trade-off
between grain growth and oxide coarsening, which aggravates creep damage by acting as nucleation sites
for cavities.

The objective of this work is to establish an integrated processing–structure–property framework for PM-
HIP 316 stainless steel by investigating the influence of powder feedstock characteristics in pre- and post-
HIP processing and by understanding the significance of post-HIP heat treatment on microstructural
evolution and creep properties. The results from this report emphasize the significance of powder
feedstock integrity in improving creep performance of PM-HIPed SS316 by highlighting the effect of
rapid solidification, elemental segregation, oxide formation, and powder recycling on microstructural
defect inheritance following HIP consolidation. Rather than considering HIP processing, solution
annealing, and mechanical performance independently, this report treats powder production, HIP
consolidation, post-HIP thermal processing, and creep deformation as interconnected stages within a
continuous metallurgical process. The resulting framework will provide a scientific basis for developing
feedstock engineering strategies capable of improving long-term reliability of PM-HIP stainless steels and
accelerating their qualification for advanced nuclear applications.

 1. INTRODUCTION

Rapid expansion of the nuclear industry and the US goal of adding another 300 GWe of nuclear capacity
by the year 2050 necessitates manufacturing technologies capable of fabricating large-scale,
geometrically-complex nuclear-grade components [1]. Traditional manufacturing techniques of pressure-
boundary components such as casting, forging, and welding have been reliable methods to make nuclear-
relevant components [2]. However, these processes are increasingly limited by the steady decline in large-
scale forging capability within the United States [3]. Moreover, long lead times, high buy-to-fly ratios,
and the intricacies of welding and machining for detailed components such as large valve bodies and
other near-net-shape (NNS) structures call for complementary advanced manufacturing technologies that
can aid and accelerate part fabrication for nuclear applications [4].

Powder metallurgy hot isostatic pressing (PM-HIP) is an emerging advanced manufacturing process that
has reached a level of technological maturity and is under consideration by the US Nuclear Regulatory
Commission [2]. PM-HIP consolidates encapsulated metal powder through the simultaneous application
of high temperature and isostatic gas pressure, enabling NNS production with excellent dimensional
control, high density, and the ability to manufacture parts at-scale [5, 6]. In previous years, Oak Ridge
National Laboratory (ORNL) successfully demonstrated the use of PM-HIP for manufacturing large-
scale, NNS components, including valve bodies and impeller prototypes (Figure 1). These efforts
showcase the ability to use convergent manufacturing routes for canister fabrication in addition to
developing a vertically integrated workflow that includes powder filling, hot isostatic pressing (HIP),
post-HIP heat treatment, metrology, and characterization [7-12]. Furthermore, a thorough mechanistic
understanding of powder consolidation kinetics, microstructural evolution, and distortion predictions as a
function of HIP parameters was developed to aid qualification efforts and accelerate NNS manufacturing
of large-scale components via PM-HIP [13, 14]. Building on these efforts, the current work focuses on
material qualification of PM-HIPed 316H stainless steel (SS316H) for nuclear-relevant applications. Past
literature indicates that PM-HIPed 316H exhibits inferior creep performance despite its ability to meet
ASME specifications for room temperature and elevated temperature (650°C) tensile properties. These
results reinforce that static tensile performance does not guarantee long‑term integrity because dynamic
properties at elevated temperatures are strongly governed by microstructural features unique to powder
processing. Therefore, it becomes quintessential to identify the root cause of inferior creep performance in
PM-HIPed SS316 through a thorough understanding of how powder feedstocks and processing
parameters influence microstructure-property variations.

The results of this report delineate variations in powder feedstocks and their sensitivity to microstructural
characteristics. Commercial SS316 powders analyzed in this study exhibited large variations in chemical
composition, segregation characteristics, and surface oxidation behavior. Characterization revealed
segregation of Cr, Mo, Mn, and Si, as well as the presence of precipitates composed primarily of Mn, Si,
and Mo oxides. These oxides are relatively stable and persist during post-HIP solution annealing, thereby
influencing the grain-growth mechanisms through Zener pinning. Multiple post-HIP heat-treatment
regimes were explored to identify optimal solution annealing parameters that can help achieve grain size
distributions similar to those of wrought SS316. Finally, creep tests were performed on samples subjected
to two post-HIP heat treatments: (1) solution annealing at 1,150°C for 4 h followed by water quenching
(SA1) and (2) solution annealing at 1,300°C for 4 h followed by water quenching (SA2). Results for both
samples indicated inferior creep properties compared with wrought SS316, with cavities nucleating at
Mn–Cr‑rich oxides, linking along grain boundaries, and resulting in intergranular cracking. Although
higher solution annealing temperatures can dissolve most precipitates and promote grain growth, grain-
boundary migration inevitably coarsens high-melting-point oxides that act as nucleation sites for cavities,
thereby accelerating tertiary creep and premature rupture. These findings establish an engineering
trade‑off in which post-HIP heat treatments can promote grain growth, chemical homogeneity, and
precipitate dissolution but cannot remove inherited oxides that may exacerbate oxide coarsening at
elevated temperatures.

Consequently, qualification of PM-HIPed SS316 requires an upstream optimization beyond HIP and post-
HIP heat treatment alone and instead demands deliberate feedstock management and powder rejuvenation
strategies. This report details the use of rotary kiln treatment under reducing atmospheres as a scalable
approach for altering powder surface chemistry before consolidation. Although complete reduction of
stable Mn–Si-rich oxides may not be feasible under moderate conditions, controlled kiln processing may
enhance feedstock integrity by reducing oxygen activity and altering less stable surface oxides, thereby
helping to reduce creep damage in the consolidated material. Finally, this study takes an integrated view
of powder production, HIP consolidation, post-HIP thermal processing, and in-service creep deformation.
This integrated approach provides a strong scientific foundation for the development of feedstock
engineering approaches to improve the long-term reliability of PM-HIP stainless steels and accelerate
their qualification for advanced nuclear applications.

 2. EXPERIMENTAL METHODOLOGY AND RESEARCH STRATEGY

The primary objective of this research is to establish correlations among powder feedstock properties,
microstructure evolution, and the high-temperature mechanical performance of PM-HIPed SS316. To
achieve this, a systematic experimental approach was developed to track variations from the powder
feedstock to the final part. Three commercially gas-atomized SS316 powders were selected to study the
effect of feedstock characteristics on subsequent microstructural evolution and mechanical behavior. The
feedstocks consisted of a recycled low-carbon powder supplied by PAC, a recycled high-carbon powder
designated Praxair Lot 4, and a virgin high-carbon powder produced by Sandvik. Although all three
powders satisfied the nominal compositional requirements for 316 stainless steels, they differed in C, O,
Ni, Mn, Si, and Mo content, as well as in their recycling history. These differences provided a controlled
basis for examining how feedstock chemistry influences segregation, oxide formation, prior particle
boundary development, precipitation behavior, and creep performance.

Following powder characterization, the feedstocks were loaded into metallic HIP capsules designed to
consolidate into near-fully dense billets. The filled capsules were evacuated to remove entrapped gases

 3

and hermetically sealed before HIP processing. Vacuum encapsulation was employed to minimize
residual gas porosity during consolidation and to limit oxidation related to residual oxygen and moisture
during thermal exposure. After HIP processing, the capsules were assessed for dimensional shrinkage
using a 3D scanner with optical fringe projections. The consolidated as-HIPed billets were then
characterized and used for all subsequent post-HIP heat treatments and mechanical testing. Post-HIP
solution annealing treatments were performed to investigate the influence of temperature, time, and
sample-loading methods on recrystallization, grain growth, precipitate dissolution, and chemical
homogenization.

Multiscale characterization was performed to correlate powder feedstock characteristics with post-HIP
microstructural evolution. Optical microscopy was used to evaluate general grain morphology and overall
microstructural homogeneity. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM), coupled with energy-dispersive x-
ray spectroscopy (EDS), was used to provide compositional contrast for identifying precipitates,
inclusions, and oxide particles at prior particle boundaries (PPBs). Grain size, grain-boundary character
distribution, and annealing twin formation were quantified using electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD).
These measurements provided quantitative details on recovery and recrystallization during post-HIP heat
treatment and served as the basis for interpreting subsequent mechanical behavior. Higher-resolution
characterization was performed using scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) and nanoscale
STEM-EDS mapping to investigate rapid-solidification segregation, surface and bulk oxide chemistry,
and elemental partitioning within the powder feedstock.

Mechanical characterization included room-temperature tensile testing, elevated-temperature tensile
testing and constant-load creep testing. Room-temperature tensile testing was performed to evaluate yield
strength, ultimate tensile strength, and ductility following HIP consolidation and post-HIP heat treatment.
These data were used to verify that the minimum strength requirements for structural 316H stainless steel
were met. However, tensile properties alone were not considered sufficient for qualification. Elevated-
temperature creep testing served as the primary mechanical assessment of long-term structural integrity.
Constant-load creep tests were performed at representative 650°C and 160 MPa to obtain rupture life and
creep ductility values. Figure 2 summarizes the steps involved in translating powder feedstocks to fully
consolidated parts via PM-HIP.

 3. POWDER FEEDSTOCK CHARACTERIZATION AND ITS SIGNIFICANCE IN PM-HIP

The quality of the powder feedstock is critical to the microstructural evolution and long-term performance
of PM-HIPed SS316. Unlike traditionally forged materials that undergo extensive thermomechanical
processing after solidification, components produced by PM-HIP retain many of the chemical and
microstructural characteristics imparted during the gas atomization process. Consequently, the chemistry,
rapid-solidification microstructure, surface oxide characteristics, and manufacturing history of the powder
(i.e., virgin vs. recycled) have a profound influence on the subsequent performance of the consolidated
component. Characterization of the feedstock is therefore essential for understanding the origins of PPBs,
the formation of secondary phases, and microstructural stability at elevated temperatures.

3.1 PHYSICAL AND CHEMICAL CHARACTERISTICS

To investigate the influence of feedstock chemistry on subsequent microstructural evolution, three
commercially available gas-atomized SS316 powders representing different manufacturing histories were
selected. As a first step in characterizing the physical properties of the three powders, pycnometer density,
Hall flow, powder particle size distribution, and optical imaging of powder cross sections were
performed, as shown in Figure 3.

while also increasing the propensity for the precipitation of Mo-rich intermetallic phases during extended
thermal exposure [15]. These differences in powder chemistries also point to corresponding variations in
precipitate phase fractions and liquidus temperatures, which define the appropriate post-HIP heat
treatment regimes, as demonstrated by the Thermo-Calc simulations in Figure 4.

3.2 MICROSTRUCTURAL CHARACTERIZATION: SOLUTE SEGREGATION

During atomization, molten droplets experience extremely high cooling rates, typically ranging from 10³
to 10⁶ K s⁻¹ depending on particle diameter, atomization gas pressure, and thermal environment [17-19].
These cooling rates suppress equilibrium diffusion within the solidifying droplets and promote the
formation of fine cellular or dendritic microstructures. Although such rapid solidification produces
powders with excellent sphericity and relatively homogeneous bulk composition, it also generates
significant chemical partitioning at the nanometer scale. The resulting cellular segregation network
represents the first stage in the defect inheritance pathway proposed throughout this report because it
establishes the local chemical environments that subsequently govern oxidation, precipitation, and creep
damage following PM-HIP consolidation. Representative backscattered electron micrographs, together
with high-resolution SEM-EDS elemental maps obtained from the three investigated powders, are
presented in Figure 5.

 Figure 5. Backscattered electron images of the three powder feedstocks used in this study.

 7

The microstructure in gas-atomized powders is characterized by chemically enriched cellular boundaries
surrounding relatively uniform cell interiors. Elemental mapping reveals pronounced segregation of Cr,
Mo, Mn, and Si at the cell boundaries, as shown in Figure 6. Oxygen enrichment is closely associated
with the Mn- and Si-rich segregation network, indicating that oxidation is primarily confined to the cell
boundaries rather than being evenly distributed over the particle surface. These results confirm that the
powder particles exhibit chemical heterogeneity at the submicrometer scale despite conforming to the
nominal bulk composition of SS316.

Figure 6. SEM-EDS maps of a PAC powder cross section and an EDS line scan along a precipitate in the PAC
 powder feedstock.

The close spatial correlation observed among Mn, Si, Cr, and O suggests that these segregated regions are
likely to serve as preferential sites for oxide formation, facilitating the development of stable mixed Mn–
Si–Cr oxides at the cellular boundaries, particularly under the low oxygen partial pressures experienced
during the atomization and powder-handling processes [20, 21]. Iron exhibits the opposite partitioning
behavior, with localized depletion directly correlated with the enrichment of substitutional alloying
elements. These local gradients in chemistry will generate preferred nucleation sites for subsequent
precipitation during HIP processing. Additionally, the accumulation of Mo at the cellular interfaces is
expected to enhance the localized thermodynamic driving force for the precipitation of Fe2Mo- and
(Fe,Cr)2Mo-type Laves phases during prolonged thermal exposure [15, 22].

3.3 NANOSCALE CHARACTERIZATION: SURFACE OXIDES AND DETRIMENTAL
 PHASES

Surface oxidation is an inherent consequence of gas atomization, and it plays a significant role in the
microstructural development of PM-HIPed SS316. Differences in oxygen content, together with
differences in processing and handling histories, further distinguish the three feedstocks. The recycled
powders have undergone multiple handling, storage, and thermal events during previous LPBF processing
cycles, which may have altered their surface oxide characteristics [23-25]. Although the measured oxygen
concentrations differ by less than 200 ppm, these small variations are expected to have a significant effect
on the chemistry and morphology of the oxide population on the powder surface.

Representative bright field (BF), dark field (DF), and high-angle annular dark field (HAADF) STEM
images, in addition to STEM-EDS elemental maps (Figure 7 and Figure 8), reveal the morphology and
thickness of oxide films on the powder surfaces, as well as at the rapidly solidified cellular boundaries
within the bulk of these powder cross sections. The Praxair Lot 4 powder showed a minimal segregation

 8

network, consistent with its lower Mn and Si contents. These results imply that the engineering relevance
of oxygen is determined more by the morphology and chemistry of the oxides than by the total O
concentration.

 Figure 7. STEM-EDS analysis of the three feedstocks at the surface of a 40 µm powder particle.

 Figure 8. STEM-EDS analysis of the three feedstocks within the bulk of a 40 µm powder particle.

Additionally, distinct Ni-depleted and Cr–Mo-rich regions in STEM-EDS maps raised suspicion about the
presence of secondary phases such as δ-ferrite. Selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) was performed
to identify and confirm the presence of δ-ferrite, as shown in Figure 9. These oxides and secondary phases
persist through conventional post-HIP solution annealing, thereby influencing grain growth, precipitation
behavior, and ultimately creep cavitation and fracture.

 9

 Figure 9. SAED from secondary phases in the bulk of Praxair Lot4 powders.

This interpretation explains an important experimental observation made throughout the present
investigation. Bulk oxygen concentrations measured for the investigated powders differ by less than
approximately 200 ppm, yet STEM characterization reveals substantial differences in oxide morphology
and continuity among the three feedstocks. These observations indicate that total oxygen concentration
alone is an incomplete descriptor of powder quality. The metallurgical significance of oxygen depends
primarily on its spatial distribution, oxide chemistry, and particle morphology rather than on its average
concentration. Similar conclusions have been reported by numerous investigators studying powder
metallurgy stainless steels and Ni-based superalloys, in which oxide morphology was found to correlate
more strongly with fatigue and creep performance than total oxygen concentration [26-29].

 4. CONSOLIDATED PART CHARACTERIZATION: AS-HIPed AND SOLUTION
 ANNEALED

The comparison between PM-HIP and wrought 316H is important for understanding the different
microstructural constraints imposed by powder metallurgy processing. Wrought 316H undergoes
extensive thermomechanical deformation and recrystallization during production. This sequence of
operations breaks down casting heterogeneities, improves chemical homogeneity, reduces oxide
populations, and produces a relatively clean grain-boundary network. Although wrought 316H is still
prone to carbide precipitation, grain-boundary sliding, and cavity nucleation during creep, these damage
mechanisms occur within a microstructure that lacks the oxide-decorated PPBs typically found in PM-
HIP materials [30]. Consequently, PM-HIP 316H may satisfy tensile property criteria but does not
achieve the creep rupture performance of wrought materials. Nevertheless, the performance of PM-HIP
components can be improved through careful control of feedstock oxide behavior, powder handling, HIP
processing, and post-HIP heat treatment. These findings also indicate that qualification must explicitly
consider powder-derived microstructural features that are not present in wrought product forms.

4.1 AS-HIPed MICROSTRUCTURE

Before consolidation, each powder particle displays a rapidly solidified cellular structure, along with
surface oxides and local chemical segregation, as described in Section 3 of this report. These powder
particles, when subjected to HIP temperature and pressure, consolidate into a fully dense polycrystalline
microstructure through solid-state sintering and diffusion bonding while retaining the chemically stable

 10

oxide particles characteristic to the original powder feedstock. Figure 10 shows a representative
backscattered electron image of as-HIPed Sandvik SS316 powders that clearly shows oxides dispersed
along grain boundaries and triple junctions. Higher-magnification images show circularly decorated
precipitates that appear to be PPBs inherited from the original powder feedstock. EDS line scans confirm
that these precipitates are rich in Si, Mn, Mo, and O.

Figure 10. As-HIPed microstructure (Sandvik) showing precipitate distribution and an EDS line scan of one
 of the precipitates.

EBSD characterization revealed that as-HIPed microstructures are polycrystalline with a random texture.
A comparative EBSD analysis was conducted to evaluate differences in grain size between PM-HIPed
SS316 and its wrought counterpart. Figure 11 reveals significant differences between the wrought and
PM-HIPed microstructures. The as-HIPed material has an average grain size of approximately 15 µm,
whereas the wrought material exhibits an average grain size of approximately 40 µm. This difference is
important because the primary creep failure mechanism in this material system is grain-boundary sliding.
Therefore, post-HIP heat treatments were performed to increase the grain size and reduce the grain-
boundary surface energy of PM-HIPed SS316.

4.2 POST-HIP SOLUTION ANNEALING

Solution annealing is a systematic approach for modifying the microstructure through recrystallization
and subsequent grain growth. EBSD analysis of solution annealed microstructures shows a distinct
increase in grain size with increasing temperature and time, as shown in Figure 12.

 11

 Figure 12. EBSD IPF maps of solution annealed samples alongside grain size distribution statistics.

More importantly, changes in oxide morphology were observed after solution annealing, as shown in
Figure 13. After HIP processing, the oxide particles are relatively fine and preferentially distributed along
grain boundaries or PPBs. Exposure to elevated temperatures during solution annealing leads to
measurable particle coarsening, accompanied by an increase in interparticle spacing, in accordance with
diffusion-controlled Ostwald ripening. Fine oxide particles hinder the movement of grain boundaries
through Zener pinning, where the pinning pressure is inversely proportional to particle size.
Consequently, fine oxide dispersions strongly inhibit grain growth immediately after HIP processing.
However, post-HIP solution annealing coarsens these oxides, thereby decreasing pinning pressure,
allowing increased grain-boundary mobility, and promoting continued grain growth. Although grain
growth reduces the total grain-boundary area, the larger oxide particles are retained in the growing grain-
boundary network and continue to serve as microstructural heterogeneities, eventually deteriorating creep
performance.

 12

Figure 13. Secondary electron images of post-HIP solution annealed samples showing coarsening of oxides as
 a function of increasing temperature and time.

In summary, the microstructural changes observed after HIP suggest that heat treatment is primarily an
optimization process rather than a remedial one. It leads to recrystallization and promotes grain growth
and chemical homogenization, but it does not completely remove the oxide particles inherited from the
powder production process. Thus, the final microstructure reflects the combined effects of feedstock
quality, HIP consolidation, and subsequent heat treatment. These findings underscore the importance of
controlling powder characteristics before consolidation to enhance long-term performance. They also
demonstrate the strong influence of feedstock-derived oxides on the final grain structure, providing the
basis for understanding the subsequent mechanical and creep properties of PM-HIP 316H.

4.3 MECHANICAL PROPERTY RESPONSE

As-HIPed and solution-annealed Sandvik SS316 demonstrated wrought-comparable mechanical
properties at room temperature, including yield strengths of approximately 280 MPa, ultimate tensile
strengths near 640 MPa, and elongations exceeding 50%, as shown in Figure 14. The observed values
surpass the minimum criteria established by ASME for 316H stainless steel, which specify a yield
strength of 205 MPa, an ultimate tensile strength of 515 MPa, and an elongation of 40% for the pertinent
product forms [31-34]. These results confirm that HIP processing produced a fully dense microstructure
characterized by sufficient strength and ductility when subjected to monotonic loading. However, the
microstructural integrity of these materials can only be fully assessed by time-dependent dynamic testing
in which dislocation climb, diffusion, grain-boundary sliding, precipitation, and cavity growth dictate
material failure mechanisms [16, 35-38].

 13

 Figure 14. Room temperature and elevated temperature tensile data of wrought, as-HIPed and solution
 annealed SS316.

Creep testing was performed at 650°C and 160 MPa on post-HIP heat-treated samples. Two different heat
treatments were performed for creep analysis. SA1 involved heating as-HIPed samples to 1,150°C and
holding for 4 h before water quenching, whereas SA2 involved heating as-HIPed samples to 1,300°C and
holding for 4 h before water quenching. These two variations in post-HIP heat treatment were chosen to
understand the sensitivity of grain size and precipitate coarsening on PM-HIPed SS316 creep
performance. As-HIPed samples subjected to SA1 did not exhibit significant grain growth, whereas a
more rigorous SA2 resulted in increased grain size much closer to its wrought counterpart, with an
average grain size of 40 µm. However, this increase in grain size occurred at the expense of heavily
coarsened oxides in the as-HIPed + SA2 microstructure.

As shown in Figure 15, samples subjected to SA1 showed an increase in rupture life of approximately
40% compared with those subjected to SA2. However, the creep life of SA1 samples remained lower than
that of wrought 316H, suggesting that conventional solution annealing influences creep life but does not
fully eliminate the microstructural limitations inherited from powder processing. The difference in creep
response between samples subjected to SA1 and SA2 is attributed to differences in their microstructural
characteristics. SA2 microstructures indicated larger oxide particles that decorated the grain boundaries,
which acted as nucleation sites for creep cavities. These cavities initiated preferentially at Mn–Cr-rich
oxides and established connections along intergranular paths. In contrast, SA1 samples had a finer
distribution of oxide particles, resulting in less cavity formation along grain boundaries and improved
creep performance despite having finer grains compared with the SA2 microstructure. The dominant
processing challenge is therefore not simply achieving the correct grain size but controlling the inherited
oxide population, which evolves concurrently with grain growth.

 14

Figure 15. Creep strain data of PM-HIPed and solution annealed SS316H reported in the literature compared
 with the data measured in this study.

This comparison provides critical insight for process development. The optimum heat treatment for PM-
HIP 316H cannot be selected solely by maximizing grain size, dissolving carbides, or matching the
microstructure of wrought material. Instead, it must minimize the combined damage potential of grain-
boundary area, oxide size, oxide spacing, precipitate distribution, and residual PPB chemistry. Although
the SA1 condition appears to achieve a better balance than SA2, it is to be noted that neither condition
fully resolves the inherited oxide limitation leading to inferior creep performance relative to wrought
SS316. Consequently, further improvements in creep performance will likely require upstream reduction
of the oxide population through feedstock engineering in combination with optimized post-HIP heat
treatment.

  5. FEEDSTOCK ENGINEERING AND MICROSTRUCTURE CONTROL: A NECESSARY
 NEXT STEP

Historically, improvement efforts in the PM-HIP community focused on HIP parameter optimization and
post-HIP processing routes to reduce the influence of PPBs on microstructural and property debits.
Although these techniques help improve powder consolidation quality to some extent, they do not address
the origins of PPBs, which arise from the feedstock integrity established before HIP. Therefore, it is
essential to develop upstream feedstock optimization tools that can generate cleaner surfaces, lower
oxygen activity, reduced segregation, and controlled handling. Such methodologies can potentially lead to
meaningful gains in long-term performance, particularly at elevated temperatures and extreme
environments such as those in nuclear applications.

In an effort to achieve this goal, this study establishes the foundation for feedstock engineering, wherein
powder properties are deliberately altered before consolidation to mitigate the development of adverse
microstructural characteristics. To explore this concept, powder conditioning was performed in a rotary
kiln, where individual powder particles were exposed to controlled gaseous environments before HIP
consolidation. Early experiments used a flowing Ar + 4% H2 reducing atmosphere. The system is
currently being designed for future operation at higher hydrogen concentrations subject to the completion
of required safety qualifications (Figure 16). The rotary kiln allows for a detailed study of processing
variables such as furnace temperature, drum rotation rate, kiln inclination, powder residence time, and gas
composition. It is important to recognize that the powder conditioning is controlled by coupled
thermodynamic and kinetic processes at a given temperature under a reducing atmosphere for an
optimized residence time.

 15

 Figure 16. Design schematic of the rotary kiln installed and commissioned for use at ORNL.

The rotary kiln includes an auger feeder, agitator, and independently controlled variable-frequency motors
for uniform powder transport through the rotating drum, as shown in Figure 17. Drum inclination,
residence time, and discharge characteristics were optimized to allow continuous powder flow and to
maximize exposure of the particle surfaces to the processing atmosphere. Pressure-monitoring sensors are
integrated into the system to detect powder accumulation and flow blockages that can lead to pressure
buildup in the drum. A quartz viewing window located at the end of the rotating drum allows direct
observation of powder flow dynamics during operation. Additional ports near the quartz window are
available for future upgrades to in situ monitoring devices and sensors.

 Figure 17. Image of the rotary kiln at ORNL showing the auger system, pressure sensor, and viewing
 window.

In contrast with traditional powder qualification methods that focus on parameters such as particle size
distribution, flowability, and bulk chemistry, feedstock engineering emphasizes the localized chemistry
and morphology of oxide particles, as well as the segregation networks that are ultimately integrated into
the consolidated microstructure. The composition of the oxide population is significant because Mn–Si-
rich oxides exhibit a markedly higher thermodynamic stability compared with that of Fe-rich oxides.
Consequently, these oxides remain predominantly unaltered during standard PM-HIP. Effective powder
conditioning therefore requires an understanding of oxide composition, morphology, and spatial
distribution rather than merely aiming to decrease the overall oxygen concentration.

 6. SUMMARY OF REPORT

This work establishes a comprehensive, vertically integrated processing–structure–property framework
and proposes a feedstock rejuvenation methodology to improve elevated-temperature performance and
facilitate the nuclear qualification of PM-HIPed SS316. Figure 18 outlines this workflow, which tracks
the material from gas-atomized powder through feedstock conditioning, HIP consolidation, post-HIP heat
treatment, microstructural characterization, mechanical testing, and creep evaluation. This study identifies
the metallurgical origins of long-term creep degradation and demonstrates that feedstock quality exerts a
fundamental influence on the performance of consolidated components. Results indicate that optimization
of HIP parameters or post-HIP heat treatment alone cannot fully overcome the inherited chemical
heterogeneity introduced during powder production, thereby emphasizing the need for integrated
feedstock engineering to achieve wrought-equivalent long-term performance.

Key conclusions of this work include the following:

• Powder feedstock chemistry and handling were identified as the primary factors governing the
 formation of detrimental oxide populations and PPBs.
• Post-HIP heat treatment improved the consolidated microstructure but did not completely eliminate
 the influence of inherited oxide particles.
• Creep performance remained strongly controlled by the inherited oxide population despite successful
 densification and excellent tensile properties.
• Feedstock engineering through powder rejuvenation represents the most promising pathway for
 further improving PM-HIP 316H for nuclear service.
