First Law for Nonsingular Black Holes in 2D Dilaton Gravity
Peng Yu, Yuan Zhong

Abstract
A central issue in the thermodynamics of nonsingular black holes is the apparent violation of the first law. In this work, we use 2D dilaton gravity as a simple theoretical setting to study this issue. We systematically construct a broad class of nonsingular black hole solutions with metric function A ⁡ ( x ) = f ⁡ ( x ) + c A(x)=f(x)+c , through a procedure that is considerably simpler than in higher-dimensional theories. Using the Iyer-Wald covariant phase space formalism, we derive the correct energy formula and establish a consistent first law for this entire class of solutions. The apparent first-law violation in a previous work is caused by an incorrect choice of energy. Our energy formula agrees, up to a normalization factor for the asymptotic Killing vector, with the Casimir function in 2D dilaton gravity, confirming its interpretation as the physical black hole energy. Our results clarify the correct first law for 2D nonsingular black holes and may provide insights into the first law of nonsingular black holes in higher dimensions.

This issue is of broader interest because it is tied to the general problem of formulating the first law for nonsingular black holes. It has long been known that, for many nonsingular black holes, the Hawking temperature, the entropy, and the first law cannot all be made consistent at the same time

In most previous studies, the first law is assumed from the beginning, with two common approaches: either retaining the Hawking temperature to derive the entropy, or preserving the Bekenstein-Hawking area law to deduce the temperature. For regular black holes, however, these two choices usually lead to different results. Therefore, one can often preserve either the Hawking temperature or the area law entropy, but not both within the same first-law framework

Thus, it is necessary to study the thermodynamics of nonsingular black holes directly from first principles, without phenomenological modifications or ad hoc assumptions. A natural framework for this is the Iyer-Wald covariant phase space formalism
Iyer and Wald 1994
. In diffeomorphism invariant theories of gravity, this formalism provides a systematic way to define conserved charges and derive black hole thermodynamic relations
Iyer and Wald 1994
;
Wald 1993
. It has also played an important role in first-principles derivations of the first law for Schwarzschild-AdS black holes
Xiao et al. 2024
;
Hajian and Tekin 2024

;
Ayon-Beato and Garcia 1998
;
Hayward 2006
;

;
Li and Lü 2024

By comparison, 2D dilaton gravity offers a much cleaner setting for studying the thermodynamics of regular black holes. It will be shown that the Iyer-Wald formalism can be applied straightforwardly to the class of regular black hole solutions described in Section
II
, and a consistent first law can be systematically derived. Based on this analysis, we argue that the apparent violation of the first-law in Ai’s work comes from the wrong identification of black hole energy. The correct energy should be the Hamiltonian charge associated with a properly normalized asymptotic time-translation Killing vector, and can be computed using the Iyer-Wald formalism. Additionally, we show that this energy agrees with the Casimir function of 2D dilaton gravity, confirming its interpretation as the physical black hole mass.

V

II
Nonsingular black hole solutions

In this section, we first briefly review the basic structure of 2D dilaton gravity in the Weyl-fixed frame and explain how it naturally accommodates nonsingular black hole solutions. We then present two explicit examples of such solutions and discuss their causal structure to confirm that they are black hole solutions.

II.1
A general discussion

Starting from the action in Eq. (
2
), and considering only static solutions, one can choose the following static gauge

\mathrm{d}s^{2}=-A(x)\,\mathrm{d}t^{2}+\frac{1}{A(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x^{2},

(3)

where
A(x)
is the metric function.
The Einstein equations are
Witten 2020
;
Ai 2021

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=0,

(4)

\displaystyle A^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=W(\varphi),

(5)

where a prime denotes derivative with respect to
x

The integration of Eq. (
4

\varphi^{\prime}(x)=k,

(6)

with
k\in\mathbb{R}
a constant. We only consider the case

k=1
, so that

\varphi=x.

(7)

Under this identification, one immediately obtains the following equation for the Ricci scalar

R(x)=-A^{\prime\prime}(x)=-W^{\prime}(x).

(8)

This relation is crucial: in the Weyl-fixed frame, the curvature is controlled entirely by the derivative of the dilaton potential. Hence, if
W(x)
is chosen to be smooth and such that

remains finite for all

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}W^{\prime}(x)=0
, then the geometry is asymptotically flat.

The above procedure provides a systematic way to construct regular geometries with the form

A(x)=\int^{x}W(y)\,\mathrm{d}y+c=f(x)+c,

(9)

where
c
is an integration constant.
Unlike higher dimensional cases, where one generally has to solve Einstein equations coupled to nonlinear matter fields, our 2D construction is considerably simpler. One can directly specify the dilaton potential to obtain a large class of regular geometries, and then analyze their properties in a straightforward way.

To obtain a black hole solution, the metric function must admit at least one zero,

A(x_{h})=0,

(10)

which defines a horizon at
x=x_{h}
. In addition, as emphasized by Witten
Witten 2020
, regularity of the Euclidean section requires the slope of the metric function at the horizon to be nonnegative. Expanding the metric function near
x=x_{h}
, one finds

\displaystyle A(x)

\displaystyle=A^{\prime}(x_{h})(x-x_{h})+\mathcal{O}\!\left((x-x_{h})^{2}\right)

(11)

\displaystyle=W(x_{h})(x-x_{h})+\mathcal{O}\!\left((x-x_{h})^{2}\right).

(12)

For the exterior region to have the correct Euclidean signature, one must require

W(x_{h})\geq 0.

(13)

Therefore, the existence of a regular black hole imposes both global conditions on the potential, through the regularity of
W^{\prime}(x)
, and local conditions at the horizon, through the sign of
W(x_{h})
.

II.3
Causal structure analysis

To show that the horizon is a true event horizon, we examine the causal structure of the spacetime. Similar analyses were carried out for singular black holes in general 2D dilaton gravity
Grumiller et al. 2002
and for Ai’s nonsingular black hole solution
Ai 2021
. Since our solutions share the same basic structure as Ai’s, the same argument applies. For completeness, we give a self-contained proof below.

We consider the static gauge metric
in the form of Eq. (
3
). The metric admits a Killing vector
\xi=\partial_{t}
with norm

\xi^{2}=g_{tt}=-A(x).

(21)

Hence
\xi
becomes null at
x=x_{h}
, so
x=x_{h}
is a Killing horizon. To determine whether it is also an event horizon, we analyze the causal structure.

We first introduce the tortoise coordinate

x^{*}=\int^{x}\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{A(y)}.

(22)

In terms of
x^{*}
, the metric becomes

\mathrm{d}s^{2}=A(x)\left(-\mathrm{d}t^{2}+\mathrm{d}x^{*2}\right).

(23)

Radial null curves therefore satisfy

\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{\mathrm{d}x^{*}}=\pm 1.

(24)

It is then natural to introduce null coordinates

u=t-x^{*},\qquad v=t+x^{*},

(25)

for which

\mathrm{d}s^{2}=-A(x)\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}v.

(26)

Because the horizon is simple, the near-horizon expansion is

A(x)=A^{\prime}(x_{h})(x-x_{h})+O\!\left((x-x_{h})^{2}\right).

(27)

Substituting this into Eq. (
22
) gives

x^{*}=\frac{1}{A^{\prime}(x_{h})}\ln|x-x_{h}|+O(1).

(28)

Thus
x^{*}\to-\infty
as
x\to x_{h}^{+}
from the exterior. In this way, we move the coordinate singularity at
x=x_{h}
to
x^{*}=-\infty
. However, this does not remove the coordinate singularity, the metric is still singular at
x^{*}=-\infty
.

To remove it, we define Kruskal-type coordinates

U=-e^{-\kappa_{h}u},\qquad V=e^{\kappa_{h}v},\qquad\kappa_{h}=\frac{A^{\prime}(x_{h})}{2}.

(29)

Since

\mathrm{d}u=\frac{\mathrm{d}U}{\kappa_{h}U},\qquad\mathrm{d}v=\frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\kappa_{h}V},

(30)

the metric Eq. (
26
) becomes

\mathrm{d}s^{2}=\frac{A(x)}{\kappa_{h}^{2}UV}\,\mathrm{d}U\,\mathrm{d}V.

(31)

Moreover,

UV=-e^{2\kappa_{h}x^{*}}.

(32)

Using Eq. (
28
) and
\kappa_{h}=A^{\prime}(x_{h})/2
, one finds

e^{2\kappa_{h}x^{*}}=e^{A^{\prime}(x_{h})x^{*}}=|x-x_{h}|\,[1+O(x-x_{h})],

(33)

and therefore

UV=-(x-x_{h})\,[1+O(x-x_{h})].

(34)

Combining Eqs. (
27
) and (
34
), we obtain the near-horizon expansion of the prefactor in Eq. (
31
) as

\frac{A(x)}{\kappa_{h}^{2}UV}=-\frac{4}{A^{\prime}(x_{h})}.

(35)

For nonextremal horizons,
A^{\prime}(x_{h})\neq 0
, so the prefactor in Eq. (
31
) is finite and nonzero at
x=x_{h}
. Hence the metric is regular at
x=x_{h}
in the
(U,V)
coordinates. Since the curvature scalar is also finite at
x=x_{h}
, there is no curvature singularity there.

To make the causal structure more explicit, we introduce

T=\frac{V+U}{2},\qquad X=\frac{V-U}{2},

(36)

one finds

U=T-X,\qquad V=T+X,

(37)

and therefore

\mathrm{d}U=\mathrm{d}T-\mathrm{d}X,\qquad\mathrm{d}V=\mathrm{d}T+\mathrm{d}X.

(38)

Substituting these into Eq. (
31
)
gives

\mathrm{d}s^{2}=\frac{A(x)}{\kappa_{h}^{2}UV}\,\left(-\mathrm{d}T^{2}+\mathrm{d}X^{2}\right).

(39)

Moreover, since

-UV=e^{2\kappa_{h}x^{*}},

(40)

we have

X^{2}-T^{2}=\left(\frac{V-U}{2}\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{V+U}{2}\right)^{2}=e^{2\kappa_{h}x^{*}}.

(41)

Eq. (
39
) shows that the spacetime is conformal to 2D Minkowski space. Hence the null directions are still given by

\mathrm{d}T=\pm\mathrm{d}X,

(42)

so radial light rays are straight lines at
45^{\circ}
in the
(T,X)
plane. The horizon is located at

UV=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad X=\pm T,

(43)

which is a null surface. The exterior region has

UV<0,

(44)

while the interior region has

UV>0.

(45)

Since future-directed causal curves must remain inside the null cone of the conformal Minkowski metric, no such curve starting from the black-hole interior can cross the null line
UV=0
outward and reach the asymptotic exterior. Therefore, the surface
x=x_{h}
is the boundary separating the region that can communicate with future null infinity from the region that cannot. It is thus a genuine event horizon. Since our analysis is general, the same conclusion applies to the two solutions presented in the previous subsection.

III
The first law

In this section, we use the Iyer-Wald formalism to derive a thermodynamically consistent first law for nonsingular black holes in our 2D dilaton gravity.

III.1
General formalism

Consider the Weyl-fixed dilaton gravity Lagrangian in Eq. (
2
), which can be written as a 2-form on the spacetime manifold as

\mathbf{L}=L\epsilon=\frac{1}{2}\left[\varphi R+W(\varphi)\right]\epsilon,

(46)

where
\epsilon
is the volume form.
Its first-order variation takes the standard form

\delta\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{E}(\psi)\,\delta\psi+d\mathbf{\Theta}(\psi,\delta\psi),

(47)

where
\psi=(g_{ab},\varphi)
denotes the collection of dynamical fields and
\mathbf{\Theta}(\psi,\delta\psi)
is the symplectic potential. The equations of motion, given in Eqs. (
4
) and (
5
), are simply
\mathbf{E}(\psi)=0
, where
\mathbf{E}(\psi)
is locally constructed from
\psi
and its derivatives (for details, see Appendix
A
).
For any vector field
\xi^{a}
, the associated Noether current is defined by
Iyer and Wald 1994
;
Wald 1993

\mathbf{J_{\xi}}=\mathbf{\Theta}-\xi\cdot\mathbf{L},

(48)

where
\xi\cdot\mathbf{L}
denotes the contraction of
\xi^{a}
with the Lagrangian
\mathbf{L}
. The Noether current is closed on-shell,
d\mathbf{J}_{\xi}=0
, and therefore can be locally expressed as an exact form,
\mathbf{J}_{\xi}=d\mathbf{Q}_{\xi}
, where
\mathbf{Q}_{\xi}
is the Noether charge associated with the symmetry generated by
\xi^{a}
. If
\xi^{a}
is further required to be a Killing vector, one obtains
Xiao et al. 2024

\begin{gathered}d\left(\delta\mathbf{Q}_{\xi}-\xi\cdot\mathbf{\Theta}\right)=0,\\
d\mathbf{Q}_{\xi}=-\xi\cdot\mathbf{L}.\end{gathered}

(49)

These two identities encode the variational and non-variational charge relations that underlie the first law and the Smarr formula. Integrating them over a Cauchy slice
\Sigma
with boundary
\partial\Sigma
, extending from the bifurcation surface
S_{H}
to a surface
S_{x}
in the asymptotic region, and then applying Stokes’ theorem, one obtains

\displaystyle\int_{S_{x}}\left(\delta\mathbf{Q}_{\xi_{H}}-\xi_{H}\cdot\mathbf{\Theta}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{S_{H}}\delta\mathbf{Q}_{\xi_{H}},

(50)

\displaystyle\int_{S_{x}}\mathbf{Q}_{\xi_{H}}-\int_{S_{H}}\mathbf{Q}_{\xi_{H}}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Sigma}\xi_{H}\cdot\mathbf{L},

(51)

where
\xi_{H}
is the horizon-generating Killing vector, normalized at the asymptotic boundary. In explicit calculations, one typically takes the limit
x\to\infty
. The equation (
51
) is the Iyer-Wald version of the Smarr formula.

Eq. (
50
) is the basic Iyer-Wald statement of the first law of black hole thermodynamics. Its right-hand side is the horizon contribution and is identified with the variation of the black hole entropy
Iyer and Wald 1994
;
Wald 1993

S=\frac{2\pi}{\kappa}\int_{S_{H}}\mathbf{Q}_{\xi_{H}},

(52)

where
\kappa
is the surface gravity defined through

\kappa^{2}=-\frac{1}{2}\,(\nabla_{\mu}\xi_{\nu})(\nabla^{\mu}\xi^{\nu}).

(53)

For more practical calculations, Eq. (
52
) can be rewritten in the more explicit form
Iyer and Wald 1994
;
Wald 1993

S=-2\pi\int_{S_{H}}E_{R}^{abcd}\varepsilon_{ab}\varepsilon_{cd},

(54)

where
E_{R}^{abcd}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial R_{abcd}}
and
\varepsilon_{ab}
is the binormal to the bifurcation surface. This is the Wald entropy formula. In Einstein gravity it reduces to the usual area law, while in more general theories it captures the corrections induced by nontrivial curvature couplings
Iyer and Wald 1994
;
Faraoni 2010
.

III.2
Entropy formula

We now use Eq. (
54
) to derive the entropy formula for black holes in our 2D dilaton gravity model.
For the Lagrangian density in Eq. (
2
), we have

\displaystyle E_{R}^{abcd}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial L}{\partial R_{abcd}}=\frac{1}{4}\,\varphi\left(g^{ac}g^{bd}-g^{ad}g^{bc}\right),

(55)

where we have used the fact that the Riemann tensor in two dimensions can be expressed in terms of the Ricci scalar as

R_{abcd}=\frac{R}{2}\left(g_{ac}g_{bd}-g_{ad}g_{bc}\right).

(56)

Now using the fact that the binormal
\varepsilon_{ab}
is antisymmetric and normalized as
\varepsilon_{ab}\varepsilon^{ab}=-2
, one finds

S=-2\pi\int_{S_{H}}E_{R}^{abcd}\varepsilon_{ab}\varepsilon_{cd}=2\pi\varphi_{h},

(57)

where
\varphi_{h}
is the value of the dilaton at the horizon.

Therefore, the entropy is completely determined by the value of the dilaton at the horizon. In particular, it is independent of the dilaton potential
W(\varphi)
. Consequently, every black hole solution arising from the model in Eq. (
2
) obeys the same entropy formula.

III.3
Energy formula

In this section, we systematically derive the energy formula for the class of nonsingular black hole solutions defined by Eq. (
9
) using the Iyer-Wald formalism. This will allow us to resolve the puzzle of the apparent first-law violation in Ai’s work and confirm that the correct energy is given by the Hamiltonian charge associated with a properly normalized asymptotic time-translation symmetry.

A key technical point is that, within the Iyer-Wald formalism, the generator
\xi_{H}
is treated as a fixed background vector field under variations, so that
\delta\xi_{H}=0

Iyer and Wald 1994
;
Wald 1993
;
Gao 2003
. Moreover, a consistent normalization of
\xi_{H}
at the asymptotic boundary is required. Usually, such normalization is not considered to be a problem, since in most known black hole solutions the metric function asymptotes to unity at infinity, so the naive time-translation generator
\partial_{t}
is already normalized. However, for the general class of solutions defined by Eq. (
9
), the metric function approaches a constant at infinity rather than unity, so the normalization of
\partial_{t}
depends on the integration constant
c
.

Therefore, we first need to fix the normalization of
\xi_{H}
at infinity. For static solutions, the natural choice is to define
\xi_{H}
as the time-translation generator normalized with respect to a reference metric and fix the normalization as

t^{a}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{A_{\infty}}}\Big(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Big)^{a},\qquad\delta t^{a}=0,

(58)

where
A_{\infty}
is the asymptotic value of the metric function and should be treated as a fixed parameter such that its variation is zero. This choice ensures that the time-translation generator is properly normalized at infinity, so that the associated Hamiltonian charge can be identified with the physical energy of the black hole.

With this choice, Eq. (
50
) is interpreted as the variation of the canonical energy

\delta E=\int_{S_{\infty}}\Big(\delta\mathbf{Q}_{t}-t\cdot\mathbf{\Theta}\Big).

(59)

Since in two dimensions the hypersurface
S_{\infty}
is just a point at spatial infinity, the integral reduces to evaluating the integrand at infinity. Therefore, the above equation can be rewritten as

\delta E=\Big(\delta\mathbf{Q}_{t}-t\cdot\mathbf{\Theta}\Big)|_{x\to\infty}.

(60)

For the Lagrangian in Eq. (
46
), the symplectic potential 1-form and the Noether charge 0-form associated with a vector field
\xi^{a}
are given by (see Appendix
A
for the derivation)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Theta}_{a}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{ac}[\varphi\left(\nabla_{b}\delta g^{cb}-\nabla^{c}\left(g_{db}\delta g^{db}\right)\right)

\displaystyle-\left(\nabla_{b}\varphi\right)\delta g^{cb}+\left(\nabla^{c}\varphi\right)g_{db}\delta g^{db}],

(61)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}_{\xi}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\varphi\epsilon_{ab}\nabla^{a}\xi^{b}-\epsilon_{ab}\xi^{a}\nabla^{b}\varphi.

(62)

We now specialize to the static gauge of Eq. (
3
), for which the Noether charge scalar becomes

\mathbf{Q_{t}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{A_{\infty}}}\Big(\frac{1}{2}\,\varphi A^{\prime}(x)-A(x)\varphi^{\prime}(x)\Big).

(63)

In the same gauge, and for the fixed generator defined in Eq. (
58
), one obtains after simplification (see Appendix
A
for details)

\displaystyle t\cdot\mathbf{\Theta}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{A_{\infty}}}[\varphi\delta A^{\prime}-\varphi^{\prime}\delta A]|_{x\to\infty}.

(64)

We now consider variations in the solution space generated by shifting the integration constant
c\to c+\delta c
, since
c
is the only free parameter in the family of solutions defined by Eq. (
9
). Therefore, we have

\delta A=\delta c,\qquad\delta A^{\prime}=0.

Moreover, since
\varphi=x
, we have
\delta\varphi=0
.
The variation of the Noether charge then reduces to

\delta\mathbf{Q_{t}}=-\frac{\delta c}{\sqrt{A_{\infty}}},

(65)

where we have used
\varphi^{\prime}=1
. In particular,
\delta\mathbf{Q}_{t}
is independent of
x
. Likewise, Eq. (
64
) becomes (see Appendix
A
for details)

t\cdot\mathbf{\Theta}=-\frac{\delta c}{2\sqrt{A_{\infty}}}.

(66)

Substituting these expressions into Eq. (
60
), we obtain

\delta E=-\frac{\delta c}{2\sqrt{A_{\infty}}}=\delta\left(-\frac{c}{2\sqrt{A_{\infty}}}\right),

(67)

from which the energy is immediately identified as

E=-\frac{c}{2\sqrt{A_{\infty}}}.

(68)

This is the general expression for the energy of static black holes in our 2D dilaton gravity model. It applies to all solutions of the form in Eq. (
9
), including the examples discussed in Section
II.2
.

It should be noted that, our energy formula in Eq. (
68
) is different from its higher-dimensional counterpart. In higher dimensions, the black hole energy is usually identified with the ADM mass, while in our 2D setting the energy is controlled by the integration constant
c
, which arises from the integration of Eq. (
5
) and is not directly related to the ADM mass.

III.4
First law

With entropy and energy defined, we can now prove that the first law of nonsingular black hole thermodynamics holds for the entire class of solutions in Eq. (
9
).

A key point is that, after fixing the asymptotic time translation by Eq. (
58
), the Hawking temperature is no longer given by the naive expression
A^{\prime}(x_{h})/(4\pi)
. More precisely,
\kappa
is defined by Eq. (
53
) with
\xi^{a}
replaced by the fixed generator
t^{a}
in Eq. (
58
). For the metric (
3
), this gives

\kappa=\frac{A^{\prime}(x_{h})}{2\sqrt{A_{\infty}}},

(69)

and therefore

T_{H}=\frac{\kappa}{2\pi}=\frac{A^{\prime}(x_{h})}{4\pi\sqrt{A_{\infty}}}.

(70)

Thus, the Hawking temperature differs from the expression
A^{\prime}(x_{h})/(4\pi)
used in Ref.
Ai 2021
only by the normalization factor
\sqrt{A_{\infty}}
.

The entropy is still given by the Wald formula in Eq. (
57
),

S=2\pi\,\varphi_{h}.

(71)

Now consider a change in
\varphi_{h}=x_{h}
, it can be proved that the relation
W(\varphi_{h})=A^{\prime}(x_{h})
, implies that
Witten 2020

{\delta\varphi_{h}=-\frac{\delta c}{W(\varphi_{h})}}.

(72)

Combining the above results, we obtain

\delta E=-\frac{\delta c}{2\sqrt{A_{\infty}}}=T_{H}\,\delta S=\frac{A^{\prime}(x_{h})}{4\pi\sqrt{A_{\infty}}}\,2\pi\,\delta\varphi_{h},

(73)

which is precisely the first law of black hole thermodynamics. Therefore, our work gives a covariant phase-space derivation of the first law for the entire class of static black hole solutions in Eq. (
9
), including the nonsingular black holes discussed in Section
II.2
and establishes a consistent thermodynamic interpretation for these nonsingular black holes.

Since Ai’s solution belongs to the family of solutions in Eq. (
9
), the first law must hold for it as well. The apparent violation of the first law in Ref.
Ai 2021
is therefore due to a misidentification of the black hole energy, which in turn arises from an inconsistent choice of the time-translation generator. By fixing the normalization of the generator at infinity, we have obtained a consistent definition of energy and shown that the first law holds for Ai’s solution and all other solutions in the family.

IV
Casimir function in generic 2D dilaton gravity

It is known that generic 2D dilaton gravity theories of the form in Eq. (
1
) admit a covariantly conserved quantity, usually called the Casimir function or Casimir mass
Grumiller et al. 2002

C=e^{Q(\varphi)}\,Y+w(\varphi),

(74)

where the functions
Q(\varphi)
,
w(\varphi)
and the scalar
Y
are defined by

\displaystyle Q(\varphi)

\displaystyle=\int^{\varphi}U(\tilde{\varphi})\,d\tilde{\varphi},

(75)

\displaystyle w(\varphi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int^{\varphi}e^{Q(\tilde{\varphi})}W(\tilde{\varphi})\,d\tilde{\varphi},

(76)

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\varphi)^{2}.

(77)

For static black hole solutions,
C
is the invariant mass parameter characterizing the geometry. In more general theories,
C
still captures the invariant mass of the solution, but its relation to the ADM mass can be more complicated
Grumiller et al. 2002
.

We now specialize to the Weyl-fixed theory in Eq. (
2
).
In this case
Q(\varphi)=0
, and the conserved quantity in Eq. (
74
) becomes

C=Y+w(\varphi)=-\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\varphi)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int^{\varphi}W(\tilde{\varphi})\,d\tilde{\varphi},

(78)

with
w^{\prime}(\varphi)=\frac{1}{2}W(\varphi)
. In the static gauge of Eq. (
3
), where
(\nabla\varphi)^{2}=A(x)\,\varphi^{\prime}(x)^{2}
, this becomes

C=-\frac{1}{2}A(x)\,\varphi^{\prime}(x)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int^{\varphi(x)}W(\tilde{\varphi})\,d\tilde{\varphi}.

(79)

For the solution family in Eq. (
9
), we choose the dilaton gauge
\varphi(x)=x
, so that
A^{\prime}(x)=W(x)
. Eq. (
79
) then reduces to

C=-\frac{1}{2}A(x)+\frac{1}{2}\int^{x}A^{\prime}(\tilde{x})\,d\tilde{x}.

(80)

Using
A(x)=f(x)+c
with
f^{\prime}(x)=W(x)
, we obtain

C=-\frac{1}{2}f(x)-\frac{1}{2}c+\frac{1}{2}\int^{x}f^{\prime}(\tilde{x})\,d\tilde{x}=-\frac{c}{2}.

(81)

Thus, for our entire class of static solutions, the Casimir function is simply the integration constant
c
up to the universal factor
-1/2
.

This result matches the canonical energy derived in Sec.
III
from the Iyer-Wald formalism once the asymptotic time-translation generator is normalized with the reference choice
A_{\infty}=1
. In that case Eq. (
68
) gives

E=-\frac{c}{2}.

(82)

Hence

E=C.

(83)

The agreement shows that the Casimir mass is precisely the Hamiltonian energy associated with the properly normalized asymptotic time translation.

It is important, however, to interpret this identification in correct order. One should not begin by dropping the constant
c
and viewing the solution as
A(x)=f(x)
. The solution space is still described by

A(x)=f(x)+c,

(84)

while the choice
A_{\infty}=1
fixes only the reference normalization of the asymptotic clock. The correct procedure is therefore: first fix the asymptotic normalization of the Killing generator, then compute the canonical energy from the Iyer-Wald charge, and only afterwards identify that energy with the conserved Casimir function. In this way, it can be understood that the Casimir mass is naturally adapted to the reference
A_{\infty}=1
.

V
Conclusions and outlook

In this paper, we studied the first law of thermodynamics for nonsingular black holes in a Weyl-fixed 2D dilaton gravity model. A central observation is that, once the dilaton potential is chosen so that the curvature scalar remains finite and vanishes asymptotically, the equations of motion generate a broad class of regular static solutions of the form

A(x)=f(x)+c.

(85)

This provides a simple and systematic construction of nonsingular black holes in two dimensions and makes clear that the integration constant
c
is an essential parameter of the solution space.

Our main result is a covariant derivation of the first law for this entire class of solutions using the Iyer-Wald formalism. The key point is that the black hole energy must be defined as the Hamiltonian charge associated with a properly normalized asymptotic time-translation generator. With this normalization fixed, the first law is satisfied, and the energy is determined by the parameter
c
. In this way, the apparent failure of the first law in Ai’s nonsingular black hole is resolved: the problem does not lie in the geometry itself, but in an inconsistent identification of the thermodynamic energy. Our analysis therefore gives a consistent thermodynamic interpretation for Ai’s solution as well as for the wider family of nonsingular black holes covered by Eq. (
9
).

We further showed that this Hamiltonian energy coincides with the Casimir function of generic 2D dilaton gravity, after an appropriate choice of reference normalization. This provides a physical interpretation for the Casimir function as the conserved energy associated with the asymptotic time-translation symmetry.

More broadly, our results show that 2D nonsingular black holes provide a particularly clean setting in which the conceptual structure of black hole thermodynamics can be analyzed without the technical complications present in higher dimensions. In particular, they isolate the issue of energy definition in a transparent way, which may be useful for understanding regular black holes in more complicated theories.
