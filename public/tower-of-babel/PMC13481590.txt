Prognostic factor analysis of CD19 CAR-T therapy followed by hematopoietic stem cell transplantation in relapsed/refractory B-ALL
Authors: Xin Wang, Linlin Liu, Yuanyuan Zhai, Jianmei Xu, Jile Liu, Hairong Lyu, Mingfeng Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo evaluate the long-term prognosis of patients with relapsed/refractory B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (R/R B-ALL) who received sequential allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) following CD19 Chimeric Antigen Receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy, and to identify the risk factors influencing overall survival (OS) and the severity of acute graft-versus-host disease (aGVHD). A retrospective analysis was conducted on 63 patients with R/R B-ALL who received sequential allo-HSCT after CD19 CAR-T therapy. Cox regression analysis was used to identify factors influencing OS. Patients were stratified according to the severity of aGVHD (low-grade vs. high-grade). Logistic regression was employed to identify predictors of severe aGVHD, and a nomogram was constructed based on these predictors. The model was internally validated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, calibration plots, and decision curve analysis (DCA). The two-year OS and progression-free survival (PFS) rates were 67.7% and 55.6%, respectively.The time interval from CAR-T therapy to transplantation, along with high-grade aGVHD, were identified as independent risk factors for OS, whereas low-grade aGVHD exerted a protective effect. A longer time interval from CAR-T therapy to transplantation and pre-transplant minimalresidual disease (MRD)-positive status were independent predictors of severe aGVHD. The nomogram developed for predicting the severity of aGVHD demonstrated good discrimination (areaunder the curve [AUC] = 0.827), satisfactory calibration, and clinical utility upon internal validation. We identified key prognostic factors and developed a validated nomogram that enables early, individualized prediction of severe aGVHD in this setting. This model can assist in early risk stratification and targeted intervention to improve outcomes.Keywords: Chimeric Antigen Receptor T-cell, Relapsed/Refractory B-cell Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia, Allogeneic Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplantation, Acute Graft-versus-Host Disease, Nomogram

Introduction

B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL) is a malignant clonal disorder originating from B-lymphoid progenitor cells. It accounts for approximately 75% of adult acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) cases and is also the predominant subtype in pediatric ALL. Although the 5-yearoverall survival (OS) rate for pediatric B-ALL now exceeds 90%, and outcomes for adolescents and young adults have significantly improved with pediatric-inspired regimens, the prognosis for adult patients remains poor. This is particularly true for those aged over 40 years and the elderly (≥ 60 years), whose 5-year OS rate is less than 25%. In newly diagnosed patients, the complete remission (CR) rate with induction chemotherapy can reach 80–90%. However, a considerable proportion of patients still relapse due to drug resistance or persistent positivity for minimal residual disease (MRD). Following relapse, the CR rate with conventional salvage chemotherapy is only 20–47%, with a median OS of merely 4–8 months and an extremely low long-term survival rate [1–3].The efficacy of conventional chemotherapy for relapsed or refractory (R/R) B-ALL has reached a plateau. Breakthroughs in immunotherapy have provided new hope for patients with relapsed/refractory B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (R/R B-ALL) [4].

In recent years, chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy, particularly targeting cluster of differentiation 19 (CD19), has achieved a revolutionary breakthrough in R/R B-ALL. Multiple pivotal clinical trials have confirmed that CD19-directed CAR-T therapy can induce a CR rate of 80% to 90% in both adult and pediatric patients with R/R B-ALL. Furthermore, most of these remissions achieve a MRD-negative status [5–8].However, this therapy still faces challenges, including antigen escape, resistance mutations, and severe toxicities. Clinical studies indicate that following remission induced by CAR-T therapy, a considerable proportion of patients still experience disease progression or relapse [9–11].Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) is currently the only potentially curative option for R/R B-ALL. However, its efficacy is limited by factors such as a high pre-transplant tumor burden, positive MRD, and the risk of post-transplant relapse [12–14].

Several studies have confirmed that undergoing consolidative allo-HSCT after CAR-T therapy can significantly reduce the relapse rate. It also improves long-term leukemia-free survival (LFS)and OS. According to different clinical trials, the CR rate can reach 68–94%. In one long-termfollow-up study, the 4-year OS and LFS rates for patients after this combined treatment were 68.9% and 61.4%, respectively. These sustained and durable responses were maintained throughout the 4-year follow-up period [15–20].CAR-T therapy can effectively induce a MRD-negative remission. Achieving an MRD-negative remission prior to transplant is a key prerequisite for benefiting from this strategy [15, 21].Therefore, most studies suggest that patients with R/R B-ALL may benefit from a treatment strategy of consolidative hematopoietic stem cell transplantation(HSCT) following CAR-T therapy. However, regardless of whether HSCT is performed, OS and LFS rates in patients decline after two years. This indicates that although the consolidation HSCT group has a lower relapse rate, a persistent risk of relapse remains [20]. A recent retrospective study involving 443 patients showed no significant differences in treatment-related complications or survival rates between patients undergoing allo-HSCT after CAR-T therapy and those undergoing allo-HSCT after chemotherapy [22].Another study indicated that among patients achieving a MRD-negative CR, undergoing transplantation after CAR-T therapy did not provide asignificant additional benefit. This finding might be related to factors such as the persistent presence of CAR-T cells, post-transplant graft-versus-host disease (GVHD), or infections resulting from severe immunodeficiency. However, this study did not perform a multivariate analysis, which may have obscured potential differences in outcomes [9].Antigen-positive relapse is predominant among patients who experience disease recurrence. These relapses mainly occur within the first year following HSCT. This suggests that allo-HSCT cannot completely eradicate residual disease, even when a significant molecular response has been achieved prior to the transplant. Furthermore, instances of CD19-negative relapse have been observed at later time points. This indicates the existence of an antigen-negative leukemia clone that is resistant to both CD19-directed CAR-T therapy and allo-HSCT. The prognosis for patients who relapse after transplantationis poor, with most eventually succumbing to disease progression. This underscores that not all patients derive equal benefit from consolidative HSCT following CAR-T therapy. Their outcomes may be limited by factors such as persistent CAR-T-related toxicities and transplant-associated complications.

Optimizing the safety and efficacy of combination therapy presents numerous challenges. Common adverse events following CAR-T therapy include cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) [23–25].The prognosis for transplant patients also depends on factors such as the primary disease, the conditioning regimen, anddonor-recipient characteristics. Acute graft-versus-host disease (aGVHD) and infection are the leading causes of morbidity and mortality in patients undergoing HSCT. Even with human leukocyte antigen (HLA) matching, a considerable proportion of recipients still develop aGVHD [26, 27].This situation necessitates personalized treatment strategies and continuous monitoring for high-risk populations. It also suggests that the strategy of consolidative allo-HSCT following CAR-T therapy still has room for further optimization.

Currently, multiple clinical trials have comprehensively evaluated the safety and efficacyof CAR-T therapy combined with transplantation. The evidence supports that consolidative HSCT following CAR-T therapy can significantly reduce the relapse rate and improve long-term survival in patients with R/R B-ALL. Most patients may benefit from this strategy. The benefit is most pronounced in those who achieve a MRD-negative CR afterCAR-T therapy. However, a subset of patients still do not derive significant benefit. Currently, there is a relative lack of research analyzing prognostic factors and constructing systematic prediction models for patients with R/R B-ALL undergoing consolidative transplantation after CD19-directed CAR-T therapy. Therefore, this study aims to conduct long-term follow-up of patients with R/R B-ALL who received CD19 CAR-T therapy followed by allo-HSCT at our center. We seek to identify independent factors affecting prognosis. Based on a key factor, the severity of aGVHD, we aim to further develop an early prediction model. This model will be validated and assessed to provide a basis for individualized treatment decisions and risk stratification.

Methods

Patients and study design

This study retrospectively analyzed the clinical data of 63 patients with R/R B-ALL. These patients received CD19 CAR-T therapy followed by allo-HSCT at Tianjin First Central Hospital between September 2019 and April 2025.Patient demographic information, bone marrow (BM) cytology results, and related laboratory indicators were obtained from the hospital’s electronic inpatient record system and the medical records archive. All data were anonymized during the analysis process.This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tianjin First Central Hospital and was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry. All participating patients provided signed informed consent.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: [1] Diagnosis of B-ALL conformed to the 2022 World Health Organization (WHO) classification; [2] Disease status met the 2016 WHO criteria for R/R B-ALL [28]; [3] Treatment with a CD19 CAR-T product; [4] Undergoing consolidative allo-HSCT within 6 months following CAR-T therapy; [5] Availability of complete medical records for at least six months after the combination therapy.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: [1] Diagnosis of non-B-cell lineage ALL [2]. Severe dysfunction of major organs [3]. Presence of an active autoimmune disease requiring immunosuppressive therapy prior to the diagnosis of B-ALL. Alternatively, diagnosis with another malignancy or presence of an active infection prior to the B-ALL diagnosis [4]. Extensive missing data from previous medical records.

Based on the predetermined inclusion and exclusion criteria, 20 patients were ultimately excluded due to incomplete data or complex underlying conditions. A total of 63 patients with R/R B-ALL were enrolled in the study cohort.The study flowchart is shown in Fig. 1.

Study workflow

Data collection

Based on our center’s clinical experience and previously reported prognostic factors in the literature [16, 17, 29, 30],we collected potential risk factors associated with prognosis before and after CD19 CAR-T therapy combined with allo-HSCT. These factors included the following categories: [1] General patient characteristics: Age, gender, date of diagnosis, and Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status at initial diagnosis [2]. Disease characteristics: Percentage of BM blasts, prognostic risk stratification, cytogenetic abnormalities (e.g., simple or complex karyotype), molecular features (gene mutations), presence of extramedullary infiltration (e.g., central nervous system leukemia [CNSL], testicular leukemia), comorbidities, number of prior chemotherapy lines before CAR-T therapy, and MRD status before CAR-T therapy [3]. CD19 CAR-T therapy information: CAR-T cell infusion dose, lymphodepletion conditioning regimen, occurrence and grade of CRS and ICANS, CRS and ICANS were graded according to the American Society for Transplantation and Cellular Therapy (ASTCT) consensus grading system [31], All assessments and management procedures followed institutional SOPs based on ASTCT guidelines.MRD status after CAR-T therapy, and occurrence of infections after CAR-T therapy [4]. HSCT-related information: Donor type, conditioning regimen before transplantation, GVHD prophylaxis regimen, engraftment status, occurrence and grade of aGVHD after transplantation, and complications such as infections [5]. Long-term outcomes: Occurrence and grade of chronic graft-versus-host disease (cGVHD) after transplantation, and survival status.

Study outcomes

Disease status assessment: BM cytology at initial diagnosis served as the baseline. The diagnosis and classification of ALL were based on the 2022 WHO criteria. The ECOG performance status was used to evaluate patients’ general health status and tolerance to treatment. BM aspiration was performed to assess response on days 14 and 28 after CD19 CAR-T therapy. Subsequently, BM cytology was repeated every 1 to 2 months for the first six months, and then every 3 to 6 months thereafter.CR was defined as meeting all of the following criteria: [1] No clinical manifestations of bleeding, infection, anemia, or extramedullary infiltration [2]. Absence of blasts in the peripheral blood (PB), with recovery of all three cell lines (neutrophils ≥ 1.0 × 10⁹/L, platelets ≥ 100 × 10⁹/L) [3]. BM showing < 5% lymphoblasts, with normal erythroid and megakaryocytic lineages, and no evidence of relapse within 4 weeks. MRD status was determined by flow cytometry or polymerase chain reaction (PCR).

Study endpoints: The primary endpoints were OS and progression-free survival(PFS). OS was defined as the time from CD19 CAR-T infusion to death or last follow-up. PFS was defined as the time from infusion to disease progression, death, or last follow-up. Secondary endpoints were the occurrence and severity of significant adverse events during treatment, including CRS, ICANS, GVHD, and infections.

Allo-HSCT procedure

Allo-HSCT is a procedure in which hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) from a healthy donor are infused into a recipient following myeloablative or reduced-intensity conditioning (RIC). HSCs used for allo-HSCT can be derived from BM, PB, or umbilical cord blood. Beyond reconstituting the recipient’s hematopoietic system, allo-HSCT leverages the graft-versus-tumor effect mediated by donor-derived immune cells to eliminate residual malignant lesions.Although an HLA-identical sibling is traditionally the preferred donor source, in our study, the majority of patients (85.8%) received a transplant from a haploidentical related donor. Alternative options included unrelated donors.

According to the intensity of the conditioning regimen, it is mainly divided into two categories: myeloablative conditioning (MAC) and RIC. MAC regimens use high-dose chemotherapy agents (such as busulfan and cyclophosphamide) and/or total body irradiation, with sufficient intensity to irreversibly eliminate the recipient’s BM hematopoietic function, providing the strongest antitumor effect, but also accompanied by higher organ toxicity and non-relapse mortality (NRM) [33].

In contrast, RIC regimens use lower doses of chemotherapy agents. Their primary purpose is not to completely eliminate the recipient’s BM, but to provide adequate immunosuppression to ensure successful donor stem cell engraftment. These regimens are associated with lower organ toxicity and significantly reduced NRM [34].

For this study, the criteria for neutrophil engraftment required an absolute neutrophil count exceeding 0.5 × 10⁹/L maintained for three consecutive days. Platelet engraftment was determined by a platelet count of more than 20 × 10⁹/L over seven consecutive days in the absence of transfusion. Common complications after allo-HSCT include GVHD, sinusoidal obstruction syndrome/veno-occlusive disease, transplantation-associated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), and long-term organ injuries. Acute GVHD occurs within 100 days post-transplant, involving the skin, liver, and gastrointestinal tract, Grading was performed according to the Mount Sinai Acute GvHD International Consortium (MAGIC) grading criteria [35].Chronic GVHD may affect the eyes, mouth, skin, endocrine system, neuromuscular system, and lungs [36].TMA features non-immune hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and multi-organ failure, often associated with calcineurin inhibitors, viral infections, or refractory GVHD. Long-term complications include growth retardation (in children), impaired fertility, endocrine disorders, secondary malignancies, osteoporosis, dyslipidemia, and cardiovascular events [37].The graft-versus-tumor effect is the main mechanism by which allo-HSCT eliminates residual disease and achieves cure.

Statistical analysis

The normality of continuous variables was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Variables with a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation, and comparisons between groups were made using the independent samples t-test. Non-normally distributed variables were presented as median with interquartile range (25th, 75th percentiles), and comparisons between groups were made using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages (n, %), and comparisons between groups were made using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact probability test, as appropriate. All tests were two-sided, and a P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Incidence rates of acute and chronic graft-versus-host disease (aGVHD and cGVHD) were calculated as the number of patients with the respective GVHD type divided by the total cohort size (N = 63), expressed as a percentage. aGVHD was assessed within the first 100 days following allo-HSCT, whereas cGVHD was assessed from day + 100 post-transplantation until the last follow-up.

Definition of NRM

NRM was defined as death from any cause other than disease relapse, occurring in the absence of relapse. The determination of NRM was based on disease assessments (BM aspiration and imaging) performed at the last follow-up, and was independently confirmed by two clinicians.

Survival Analysis: OS and PFS curves were plotted using the Kaplan-Meier method. Differences between groups were compared using the log-rank test. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses (using a stepwise method) were performed to identify independent prognostic factors influencing OS. Results were presented as hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Based on a key influencing factor, patients were grouped for comparative analysis. Subsequently, univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify factors associated with this key condition and to screen for independent risk factors. A nomogram prediction model was constructed based on the results of the multivariate logistic regression.

Internal validation of the nomogram was performed using the Bootstrap resampling method. Model discrimination was assessed using the receiver operating characteristic(ROC) curve and the area under the curve(AUC) (the bias-corrected AUC was reported). Model calibration was evaluated using a calibration plot and the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (with P > 0.05 indicating good fit). The clinical net benefit of the model was assessed using decision curve analysis(DCA).

Data analysis and the creation of corresponding charts were performed using, R software version 4.3.3, and GraphPad Prism version 8.0.

Results

Patient baseline characteristics and safety data

This study ultimately included 63 patients with R/R B-ALL who received sequential allo-HSCTafter CD19 CAR-T therapy at Tianjin First Central Hospital between September 2019 and April 2025. Baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. The median age was 37 years (range: 22–49 years), with a balanced sex distribution (50.8% male vs. 49.2% female). Forty-eight patients (76.2%) had an initial ECOG performance status score of less than 2. The median baseline BM blast percentage was 85% (range: 66.5%–91.3%), 39.7% of patients had a complex karyotype, and 61.9% had received four or more prior lines of therapy. Four patients (6.3%) had a history of transplantation prior to CAR-T therapy. Extramedullary involvement was observed in 25.4% of patients, among whom 10 presented with central nervous system (CNS) involvement and all 10 received intrathecal chemotherapy. Based on the BM blast percentage prior to CAR-T infusion, patients were further divided into a high tumor burden group (≥ 20%, n = 29, 46.1%) and a low tumor burden group (n = 34, 53.9%). According to disease status prior to CAR-T infusion, 44 patients were in a relapsed state and 19 in a refractory state. Twenty-one patients received debulking therapy before CAR-T infusion, most commonly the COP regimen (cyclophosphamide, vindesine, and methylprednisolone). Autologous CAR-T cells were administered to 93.7% of patients, while four patients (6.3%) received donor-derived CAR-T cells. Following CAR-T therapy, 60 patients achieved MRD-negative remission, whereas three patients did not achieve remission; all three non-responders were in a relapsed state prior to treatment and had no extramedullary involvement. The incidence of CRS was 76.2%, of which 65.1% were grade 1 and 11.1% were grade > 1. The incidence of ICANS was 14.2%, with grade 1 ICANS accounting for 9.5% and grade > 1 for 4.7%.

The median time interval from CAR-T therapy to transplantation was 2 months (range: 2–3 months); 66.6% of patients underwent transplantation while in an MRD-negative CR. Haploidentical donors were the primary source of allografts (85.8%). All patients achieved successful hematopoietic reconstitution, with a median time to neutrophil engraftment of 14 days (range: 12–15 days) and a median time to platelet engraftment of 17 days (range: 15–19 days). Within the first 100 days post-transplantation, aGVHD was observed in 52.4% of all patients, among whom 28.6% developed grade III or higher aGVHD; the most frequently involved organs were the skin, gastrointestinal tract, and liver. Evaluations starting from day 100 post-transplantation until the last follow-up showed that 22.2% of patients developed cGVHD.

Survival outcomes

From the date of CAR-T cell infusion, the median follow-up duration was 610 days (range: 355–1,446 days). During this period, a total of 23 deaths occurred. NRM (n = 14) was the leading cause of death, including respiratory failure (n = 8), severe infection (n = 4), and heart failure (n = 2). An additional nine patients died due to disease relapse; among these nine patients with relapse, three developed aGVHD with severities of grade 1, grade 2, and grade 4, respectively. The median time from initial diagnosis to disease progression was 776 days. The overall PFS and OS outcomes are shown in Figs. 2 and 3, with a 2-year PFS of 55.6%, a 2-year OS of 67.7%, a 4-year PFS of 41.9%, and a 4-year OS of 50.0%. Based on the time interval between CAR-T therapy and allo-HSCT, the 63 patients were further divided into four groups: Group 1 (20 days to 1 month), Group 2 (1–2 months), Group 3 (2–3 months), and Group 4 (> 3 months). The PFS and OS outcomes for each group are presented in Figs. 4 and 5. The shortest interval was 20 days, and the longest interval was 5 months.

Prognostic factor analysis

Univariate Cox regression analysis revealed that multiple factors were associated with patient survival (Fig. 6). These factors included the severity of CRS (HR = 2.999, 95% CI: 1.408–6.391, P = 0.004), the severity of ICANS (HR = 2.664, 95% CI: 1.379–5.148, P = 0.012), the time interval between CAR-T therapy and allo-HSCT (HR = 2.823, 95% CI: 1.855–4.298, P < 0.001), and the severity of aGVHD (HR = 6.193, 95% CI: 2.396–16.010, P < 0.001). In the univariate analysis, acute hepatic GVHD had a P value of less than 0.05; however, this variable was not included in subsequent analyses—a decision based on the small sample size (only 6 patients, accounting for 9.5%), as such limited sample size could lead to unstable results. Multivariate Cox regression analysis demonstrated that the time interval between CAR-T therapy and transplantation (HR = 2.341, 95% CI: 1.457–3.759, P < 0.001) and the severity of aGVHD (HR = 3.121, 95% CI: 1.13–8.563, P = 0.027) were independent factors influencing long-term patient survival (Table 2). A longer time interval was associated with a higher risk of death. Furthermore, grade III or higher aGVHD was also identified as an independent risk factor.

Factors influencing the severity of aGVHD and establishment of a predictive model

Based on the severity of aGVHD, patients were divided into two groups: the grade II or lower group (n = 49) and the grade III or higher group (n = 14). Intergroup comparisons revealed statistically significant differences in the time interval between CAR-T therapy and transplantation, as well as in pre-transplant MRD status (P < 0.05) (Table 3). To identify independent factors influencing the development of grade III or higher aGVHD, variables that differed significantly between groups and those with clinical significance were included in the regression analysis. Univariate logistic regression analysis showed that the time interval between CAR-T therapy and transplantation (odds ratio [OR] = 2.047, 95% (CI): 1.133–3.700, P = 0.018) and pre-transplant MRD status (OR = 0.116, 95% (CI): 0.030–0.442, P = 0.002) were significantly associated with the occurrence of severe aGVHD (Table 4). These two variables were subsequently entered into a multivariate logistic regression analysis, which further confirmed that both the time interval between CAR-T therapy and transplantation (OR = 2.128, 95% (CI): 1.108–4.09, P = 0.023) and pre-transplant MRD status (OR = 0.108, 95% (CI): 0.026–0.457, P = 0.002) were independent factors influencing the development of grade III or higher aGVHD (Table 5). A longer time interval and pre-transplant MRD-positive status were associated with an increased risk of developing severe aGVHD.

Based on the two independent influencing factors identified by multivariate logistic regression analysis (the time interval between CAR-T therapy and transplantation, and pre-transplant MRD status), a nomogram prediction model was constructed (Fig. 7). The AUC for this model was 0.827 (95% (CI): 0.674–0.827). After internal validation using the bootstrap method (with 1,000 resampling iterations), the model demonstrated good discriminatory ability (Figs. 8 and 9). The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (χ² = 0.03, P = 0.987) and the calibration curve both indicated that the model’s predictions were in high agreement with actual observations (Fig. 10). DCA showed that across a wide range of risk thresholds (particularly within the intermediate threshold range of 0.2 to 0.6), the model provided a higher net benefit than strategies of “intervening in all patients” or “intervening in no patients.” This indicates that the model has good clinical application value (Fig. 11).

Discussion

This study systematically evaluated the long-term efficacy of sequential allo-HSCT following CD19 CAR-T therapy in patients with R/R B-ALL.The study focused on aGVHD as a key influencing factor. Through progressive statistical analyses, key factors influencing the severity of aGVHD were identified. Furthermore, a clinical prediction model was constructed.

Efficacy and safety of consolidation with Allo-HSCT following CD19 CAR-T therapy

After a median follow-up of more than 20 months, the 2-year OS and PFS rates for patients in this cohort were 67.7% and 55.6%, respectively.These findings confirm that the treatment strategy of consolidation with allo-HSCT following induction remission with CD19 CAR-T therapy can provide durable survival benefits for patients with R/R B-ALL, a population with a very poor prognosis.Multiple studies have demonstrated that patients who undergo consolidation transplantation after CAR-T therapy achieve superior long-term survival [9, 16, 17, 38, 39].Some of these studies have also indicated that patients who achieve MRD-negative CR after CAR-T therapy have a further survival advantage [40, 41].The results of the present study are generally consistent with these domestic and international findings. A meta-analysis of CAR-T therapy alone reported 2-year OS and relapse-free survival (RFS) rates of 55% and 40%, respectively [42].The survival outcomes in our study are superior to these historical data, indirectly supporting the consolidative value of the combined treatment approach.It is noteworthy that, by the end of the study period, NRM accounted for 60.9% (14/23) of deaths among the 63 enrolled patients, making it the primary factor affecting survival. An analysis by Yang et al. [15] similarly suggested that meticulous management of treatment-related toxicity is a key breakthrough point for improving prognosis under this treatment model.

The dual effect of aGVHD severity and its central role in prognosis

In this study, multivariate Cox regression clearly identified the severity of aGVHD as an independent factor influencing OS, demonstrating a “graded effect”: Compared with grade III-IV aGVHD, grade II or lower aGVHD was associated with superior survival (HR = 0.338, P = 0.036).From an immunological perspective, Negrin et al. [43] highlighted the complex relationship between GVHD and the graft-versus-leukemia (GVL) effect. Moderate donor immune activation can induce aGVHD but also clear residual tumor cells through the GVL effect. However, excessive immune activation can lead to severe immune rejection, which is life-threatening [44–46].Our findings suggest that grade I-II aGVHD may lie within a “therapeutic window” of immune response. The associated GVL effect in this window helps reduce the risk of relapse, while the target organ damage is relatively controllable. In contrast, although grade III-IV aGVHD may be accompanied by a potent GVL effect, the resulting fatal organ damage (e.g., intestinal or liver failure) and secondary infections [47] significantly increase NRM. This ultimately negates any survival benefit derived from the anti-leukemia effect.

Therefore, strictly speaking, grade I-II aGVHD is not a traditional “protective factor.” Instead, it is a “favorable prognostic factor relative to the extreme risk state of grade III-IV aGVHD.” Its essence is a balance between an appropriate immune response and acceptable organ toxicity.This finding elevates the management goal for aGVHD from “complete prevention” to “precise control.” The focus of clinical work should be on avoiding severe aGVHD, rather than completely eliminating all aGVHD reactions.

Discussion

In our cohort, the prophylactic regimens were heterogeneous, generally comprising a calcineurin inhibitor (cyclosporine A or tacrolimus) combined with methotrexate and/or mycophenolate mofetil, with or without corticosteroids. Anti-thymocyte globulin or anti-T-lymphocyte globulin was added in a subset of patients, and several ruxolitinib-containing regimens were also employed. The core objective of prophylactic regimens is to mitigate the incidence and severity of aGVHD while preserving the beneficial GVL effect, thereby tipping the overall balance toward improved survival.

The interval between CAR-T therapy and transplantation: association with prognosis and aGVHD severity

The time interval between CAR-T therapy and transplantation was identified as both an independent risk factor for OS and an independent predictor of severe aGVHD (OR = 2.128, P = 0.023). Within the study period, a time interval of less than 2 months was associated with a survival advantage.A prolonged interval may increase the risk of disease progression and MRD re-positivity, potentially leading to a higher tumor burden before transplantation. It may also adversely affect the patient’s organ function and nutritional status, thereby exacerbating the post-transplantation immune activation storm and creating conditions conducive to severe aGVHD [48–50].Park et al. [9] also emphasized that consolidation transplantation should be performed as soon as possible after achieving remission with CAR-T therapy. This study provides quantitative evidence supporting this treatment strategy.

Pre-transplantation MRD status: its predictive value for severe aGVHD

Positive MRD status before transplantation was identified as another independent risk factor for predicting grade III-IV aGVHD (OR = 9.235, P = 0.002). Berry et al. [51] indicated that a high tumor burden can enhance the activation of host antigen-presenting cells, thereby intensifying the donor T-cell response. MRD positivity not only signifies the presence of residual tumor cells but also reflects an activated immune microenvironment. This environment is prone to triggering an excessive inflammatory cascade, which manifests as severe aGVHD [48].This study extends the clinical implications of MRD from “predicting relapse risk” to a new dimension of “predicting severe aGVHD risk,” thereby enriching its value in clinical guidance.

Construction, validation, and clinical value of the nomogram prediction model

A nomogram model for predicting the severity of aGVHD was constructed based on the treatment interval and MRD status. The AUC for the model was 0.827 (95% CI: 0.674–0.827). The calibration curve demonstrated good fit. DCA showed a clear net benefit within the threshold range of 0.2 to 0.6.This model enables individualized risk stratification before transplantation. It can guide the implementation of intensified prevention or monitoring strategies. It also assists in clinical decision-making and resource optimization.A limitation of this study is that, although multivariate regression controlled for some confounding factors, interference from unmeasured variables remains possible. Furthermore, due to the limited sample size, the model was not divided into training and validation sets. Therefore, the model evaluation has certain limitations.

Innovation and clinical significance of this study

This study features a progressive research design. It moves from global prognostic screening to the construction of a predictive model, reflecting a systematic approach.A core mechanism was revealed. The dual effect of aGVHD severity was elucidated, providing a theoretical basis for precise clinical management.The predictive factors were expanded. The time interval between CAR-T therapy and transplantation, as well as pre-transplantation MRD status, were identified as novel predictors for severe aGVHD.The model underwent systematic validation. It was comprehensively evaluated in terms of discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility. This enhances the credibility of the findings.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, regarding the estimation of GVHD incidence, the conventional Kaplan-Meier method was employed, as the primary objective of this study was to provide a preliminary description of observed GVHD occurrences within a relatively small cohort, rather than to estimate the cumulative incidence adjusted for competing risks. It should be noted that this method treats deaths occurring before GVHD as censored data, which may lead to an overestimation of GVHD incidence. Second, there is a clear competing risk relationship between relapse and NRM. Ideally, the cumulative incidence function, which accounts for competing events, should be used to estimate the cumulative incidences of relapse, NRM, and GVHD. However, this study also applied the Kaplan-Meier method, which treats competing events as censored when competing risks are present, potentially affecting the accuracy of cumulative incidence estimates. Given the relatively small sample size (N = 63) and the fact that the primary endpoints were OS and PFS, competing risk models were not further employed. Furthermore, as a single-center retrospective study with a limited sample size (n = 63), selection bias may exist, and the findings should be generalized with caution. The predictive model has not yet been validated in an independent external cohort. Some potential biomarkers, such as cytokine levels and regulatory T cell proportions, were not included in the analysis; future studies may attempt to construct multidimensional models incorporating these factors. Interventional clinical trials based on this model have not yet been conducted, and randomized controlled trials are urgently needed to verify its actual value in improving patient outcomes. In summary, we recommend that future multicenter studies with larger sample sizes be conducted to validate the results of this study using competing risk analysis, thereby enabling a more accurate estimation of the cumulative incidence rates of relevant endpoints.

Conclusion

Sequential allo-HSCT following CD19 CAR-T therapy is an effective strategy for the treatment of R/R B-ALL. This combined treatment approach can provide durable survival benefits for patients with relapsed or refractory disease, with a two-year OS rate of 67.7% and a two-year PFS rate of 55.6%. However, NRM was the primary cause of death. This finding suggests that while intensifying anti-tumor therapy, meticulous management of complications is a key direction for further improving prognosis.Multivariate Cox regression analysis identified the severity of aGVHD as a core independent factor influencing patient OS, exhibiting a “dual effect.” Grade II or lower aGVHD was a relative protective factor for survival, whereas grade III-IV severe aGVHD significantly increased the risk of death. This finding elevates the management goal for aGVHD from “complete prevention” to “precise control.“In-depth analysis of factors influencing aGVHD severity revealed that a prolonged interval between CAR-T therapy and transplantation and positive pre-transplantation MRD status were independent risk factors for predicting severe aGVHD. Both factors can be ascertained before transplantation, providing a clear window for early warning and intervention.A nomogram model constructed based on these two independent predictors enabled individualized, quantitative assessment of the risk of developing severe aGVHD. The model demonstrated good discriminative ability, with an AUC of 0.827. Following Bootstrap internal validation, the calibration curve showed good fit, indicating high predictive accuracy. Furthermore, DCA confirmed that the model provides a clear clinical net benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities, outperforming extreme intervention strategies.
