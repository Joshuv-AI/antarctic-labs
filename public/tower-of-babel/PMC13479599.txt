Targeted 89Zr-Labeled metallofluorocarbon nanoemulsion for carcinoembryonic antigen PET
Authors: Keith Tang, Andrew Davis, Boyu Meng, Stephen R. Adams, Judith Varner, Karen Messer, Eric T. Ahrens
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundElevated levels of carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) are strongly associated with several malignancies, including colorectal and hepatic adenocarcinomas, making CEA a clinically valuable biomarker. Targeted molecular imaging using positron emission tomography (PET) in combination with CEA-specific tracers offers the potential for improved tumor detection and longitudinal disease monitoring.MethodsWe developed a nanoemulsion (NE) platform incorporating a fluorous-encapsulated radiometal (FERM) chelate, in which 89Zr is complexed to a fluorinated hydroxamic acid chelator sequestered within the fluorous core of (~200 nm) NE droplets. CEA targeting was achieved using a humanized anti-CEA antibody functionalized with an azide group and conjugated via copper-free click chemistry to “click-ready” NEs bearing dibenzocyclooctyne (DBCO) moieties within the surfactant layer.ResultsIn vitro studies demonstrated specific binding of the targeted NEs in both protein- and cell-based assays using LS174T cells. In vivo PET/CT imaging of LS174T xenografts in nude mice revealed selective accumulation of the antibody-functionalized 89Zr-labeled NE in tumors within 4–120 h post injection, with target specificity validated by immunohistopathology. Clearance occurred predominantly through the spleen and liver, consistent with reticuloendothelial system uptake.ConclusionClick-ready FERM NEs form a stable colloidal suspension of nanoscale droplets with a high radiometal payload and PET sensitivity. This targeted imaging platform shows strong potential for early detection and monitoring of cancer-associated biomarkers such as CEA. The modular design enables facile adaptation to a wide range of targeting ligands, supporting applications in molecular imaging and therapeutic delivery.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04639-0.

Introduction

Advances in cancer diagnosis and disease management increasingly rely on targeted, noninvasive imaging technologies capable of detecting tumor-associated biomarkers with high sensitivity and specificity. Here, we report a positron emission tomography (PET) nanoemulsion (NE) tracer that integrates fluorous-phase radiometal chelation and encapsulation of zirconium-89 (89Zr) with a modular, chemoselective “click” chemistry strategy for antibody display. This architecture enables high radiometal payloads, excellent in vivo chelation stability, and post-synthetic functionalization of the NE surface with targeting ligands without perturbing formulation integrity.

As a proof-of-concept, we evaluate the in vivo performance of this platform to target carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) in a mouse tumor model. CEA is a clinically validated cell-surface glycoprotein overexpressed in multiple epithelial malignancies, including colorectal and hepatic adenocarcinomas [1–3]. Overall, the CEA family of cell adhesion molecules (CEACAMs) are functionally diverse, playing roles in cell adhesion, signaling, and a range of biological processes, including cancer progression, inflammation, angiogenesis, and metastasis [4]. Due to their multifaceted role in tumor development and spread, there is considerable interest in CEACAMs as biomarkers and therapeutic targets in the clinical diagnosis and treatment of cancer [5]. This biomarker is especially important for colorectal carcinomas, which are among the most common malignancies, as well as a major cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [6]. Targeted PET imaging of CEA has the potential to improve early disease detection and longitudinal monitoring by combining nanomolar sensitivity with high molecular specificity [7].

Overall, key design considerations for targeted PET agents often include: (i) biodistribution and pharmacokinetics, (ii) tumor uptake specificity, and (iii) radiometal chelate stability, among others [8, 9]. Efficacious targeted PET radiotracers have been developed using small molecules, for example to target prostate-specific antigen [10, 11], small peptides [12] such as RGD for targeting αvβ3 [13–15], or alternatively, a broad range of antibodies used in “immuno-PET” [9]. Radiolabeled full-length monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) generally exhibit prolonged circulation times, slow clearance and delayed binding to target tissues which may limit their effectiveness for imaging [9]. Smaller antibody constructs such as heavy-chain-only antibodies, single-chain variable fragments, diabodies, and minibodies have been engineered for faster clearance. While this strategy reduces background signal, these constructs often exhibit rapid renal accumulation [16, 17] posing a challenge due to the kidney’s tolerance for radiation [18]. Moreover, recombinant antibody fragments may exhibit lower target avidity due to monovalent antigen binding, thereby limiting sensitivity for low-abundance biomarkers. This is compounded by the restriction of available conjugation sites for radiolabeling, as modifying key residues may impair antigen binding. More importantly, radiolabeled mAb, or fragments thereof, often deliver only ~1–2 radiometals per recognition event [19], limiting the available signal for sensitive imaging.

As an alternative, PET tracers based on targeted nanocarrier platforms may offer distinct advantages, including the ability to deliver a substantially higher radionuclide payload per target interaction, thereby enhancing image sensitivity. In addition, nanocarriers enable multivalent presentation of high-affinity ligands, which can dramatically increase binding avidity and improve target retention [20]. In addition, the nanocarrier surface is amenable to chemical modification with multimodal imaging moieties without compromising binding avidity [21–23].

Nanocarriers based on colloidal perfluorocarbon (PFC) droplets, or nanoemulsions (NEs), have a well characterized safety profile and an established biodistribution and pharmacokinetics [24]. To enable PET detection, we use fluorous-encapsulated radiometal (FERM) chelation, where 89Zr is complexed to a fluorinated hydroxamic acid (FHOA) chelator that is soluble in PFC oil in the core of NE droplets [25]. PFCs are defined by their exceptional chemical and metabolic inertness, hydrophobicity, and lipophobicity. The C-F bond is among the strongest bonds in organic chemistry. The combination of low polarizability and electronegativity of fluorine reinforces the C-C backbone, while the dense electron cloud shields the molecule against chemical attacks [26]. The strong phase separation from PFCs from aqueous and organic environments creates a distinct fluorous compartment that efficiently solubilizes the fluorophilic FHOA chelate. This compartmentalization sterically isolates the chelate from the surrounding aqueous phase, enhancing stability by encapsulating the radiometal complex within an orthogonal medium. The presence of fluorine atoms also enables high-resolution 19F/1H MRI over a restricted field of view, thereby facilitating multiscale (whole-body to microscopic) imaging of tracer localization from a single inoculant through the combined use of PET and multinuclear MRI [25].

To target CEA, NEs were functionalized with a humanized anti-CEA (Labetuzumab) mAb, modified with an azido-polyethylene glycol (PEG) linker, and conjugated to “click-ready” NEs bearing dibenzocyclooctyne (DBCO) groups incorporated into the surfactant layer of the NE droplet [27]. The post-emulsification conjugation strategy allows for the modular attachment of various targeting moieties at controlled densities. Using this approach, we demonstrate specific targeting of CEA-NE in vitro via protein- and cell-based assays. In vivo, murine PET studies in a CEA-positive colorectal tumor model displayed targeted accumulation of CEA-NE compared to controls, which was validated using immunohistochemistry. Overall, these proof-of-concept studies demonstrate a novel, targeted radio-tracer platform, potentially enabling facile adaptation for imaging a wide range of disease biomarkers.

Materials and methods

Additional details concerning chemicals, instrumentation, and protocols can be found in Supplementary Information. All animal experiments followed protocols approved by the University of California San Diego’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

Formulation of FERM Nanoemulsion with Click Chemistry Sites

The structure and purity of the synthesized FHOA were confirmed via 1H/19F NMR spectroscopy, showing characteristic peaks consistent with the expected product (Fig. S2-3). The NE surfactant was comprised of 115.5 mg egg yolk phospholipids with 80% phosphatidylcholine (Lipoid E80; Lipoid GmbH, Ludwigshafen, Germany), 9.3 mg cholesterol (Avanti Polar Lipids, Alabaster, AL), 15.6 mg 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (DSPE)-PEG-2000 (Biopharma PEG Scientific Inc., Watertown, MA), 1.2 mg DPSE-PEG2000-DBCO (BroadPharm, San Diego, CA), and 0.7 mg α-DL-tocopherol (MP Biomedicals, Solon, OH). Lipid surfactant components were prepared by dissolving in chloroform and drying as a thin film in a 20 ml glass vial under N2 gas, then further under vacuum overnight. Lipids were resuspended in 4.8 mL distilled H2O by vortexing for 1 min, followed by probe sonication (Omni Ruptor 250 W, 30% power, 2 min, Omni International, Kennesaw, GA) in an ice bath. 1.2 mL of perfluorooctyl bromide (PFOB, Exfluor Research Corporation, Round Rock, TX) was added, and the mixture vortexed for 10 s followed by probe sonication for 2 min in an ice bath. For in vivo experiments with 89Zr-chelating NE, FHOA (4 mM, 8.7 mg) was dissolved in PFOB (1.2 mL) by heating at 50 °C for 1 min until fully dissolved and cooled to room temperature before emulsification. Fluorescent cyanine (Cy5 or Cy7) NEs were prepared similarly, but instead 10 µL (11.4 mg) of PFC-soluble cyanine-PFPE (perfluoropolyether) conjugate, synthesized using previously reported methods [32], was dissolved in PFOB (1.2 mL) prior to emulsification.

Crude emulsions were passed through a microfluidizer (LV-1, Microfluidics, Westwood, MA) at 30,000 psi five times, before filtration through 0.2 μm polyethersulfone filters into sterile glass vials. Product concentrations were determined using an internal sodium trifluoroacetic acid reference by 19F NMR in 9:1 H2O: D2O. Particle size and PDI were determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS).

Conjugation protocol

A detailed description for preparing azide-functionalized antibodies is provided in the Supplementary Information (Sect. 6.1). In brief, the antibodies (Labetuzumab and IgG1) were de-salted in Sephadex G-25 column (PD10; Cytiva) and spin-concentrated using a 30,000 MWCO (Amicon) centrifugal filter. The antibody solution (> 1 mg/mL protein) was then reacted with N3-PEG-NHS ester at a 1:4 molar ratio in 25 mM HEPES (50 mM NaCl, pH 7.4) at 4 °C for > 4 h. The product was purified with Sephadex G-25 resin (H2O mobile phase) and spin-concentrated to yield the azide-functionalized antibody.

To prepare the antibody-NE conjugates, DBCO-NE was reacted with 10 mol% Labetuzumab-[PEG4-N3]4 relative to the total amount of DBCO lipid, or ~25 mol% relative to the total amount of reactive DBCO lipids, as determined by DBCO quantification assay (Supplementary Information, Sect. 6.2). A representative reaction was conducted as follows: DBCO-NE (1.2 mL, at 265 mg/mL PFOB) was mixed with 292 µL Labetuzumab-[PEG4-N3]4 (0.536 nmol, at 2.78 mg/mL) in an Eppendorf tube. The mixture was mixed and briefly vortexed and allowed to react at 4 °C overnight. The product was used as is, without further purification. The same procedure was used for IgG1-NE immunoconjugates.

Antibody and NE concentrations were quantified using a combination of ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectroscopy for antibody/fluorescent dye and 19F NMR for PFOB quantification. Since the DBCO groups on the NE surface were assumed to be in excess relative to the amount of azide-modified antibody added for the conjugation reactions, we assumed quantitative labeling. Accordingly, no additional antibody purification was performed beyond de-salting with G-25 Sephadex gel column with water eluent.

Characterization of reactive click sites

A detailed protocol can be found in Supporting Information (Sect. 6.2). In brief, to quantify the number of azide groups conjugated per antibody, the conjugates were reacted with slight excess (~1.2 mol equivalent) of AlexaFluor-647 (AF647) linked to DBCO. A representative reaction was as follows: Labetuzumab-[PEG-N3]n (10 µL, 11.5 mg/mL protein) was mixed with 7.6 µL AF647-DBCO stock in DMSO (0.5 mg/mL). The mixture was vortexed briefly and left to react overnight at 4 °C. The reaction was purified via 3× washes of 200 µL H2O using 30,000 Da MWCO centrifugal filter at 6000 rpm for 10 min. UV-Vis absorbance and degree of labeling (DOL) was estimated based on formula in the Supporting Information (Sect. 6.1) and known extinction coefficients.

To quantify the number of reactive DBCO sites on NE, DBCO-NE was reacted with a slight excess AF647-N3 (~1.2 mol. equivalent) relative to the maximum theoretical amount of DBCO groups expected and reacted overnight at 4 °C. Afterwards the product was purified via Sephadex G-25. PFC content was quantified via 19F NMR with internal NaTFA (sodium trifluoroacetate) standard, and the dye content was quantified by UV-Vis absorbance. The ratio of dye-to-PFC content was calculated to obtain the number of reactive DBCO groups per mg of PFC.

CEACAM5 binding assay

Streptavidin coated 96-well, black walled plates were pre-blocked with SuperBlock™ buffer (ThermoFisher Scientific, 15119) and washed 4 times with 0.5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 25 mM Tris-HCl (150 mM NaCl, pH 7.4) supplemented with 0.05% Tween20 (wash buffer). Biotinylated human CEACAM5 (Lys 35-Ala 685), poly-his-tag, Avitag™ (Acro Biosystems, Newark, DE) was reconstituted in H2O to 400 µg/mL. The stock solution was then diluted to 1 µg/mL in wash buffer, and each well was incubated with 100 µL (0.1 µg/well) at room temperature for 2 h on a plate shaker. A panel of immunoconjugates (anti-CEA mAb-AF647, anti-CEA-NE), and corresponding IgG1 negative controls were diluted to appropriate concentrations in wash buffer (100 µL total volume) and then added to each well and incubated for 1 h at room temperature on a plate shaker. Wells were then washed four times with wash buffer, and fluorescence intensity (ex./em. 630/680 nm) was measured using an Infinite 200 plate reader (Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland). For blocking experiments, ~ 2000× fold excess unmodified anti-CEA mAb was incubated in the CEACAM5 treated wells for 30 min before incubation with anti-CEA-NEs.

Cell lines

The LS174T human colorectal carcinoma cell line (CL 188) was obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in Eagle’s Minimum Essential Medium (ATCC, 30–2003) with 10% fetal bovine serum (Corning, 30-011-CV) and 1× Pen-Strep (Gemini Bio, 400-109-100).

Cell-binding assay

The LS147T cells were seeded at 104 per well in a 96-well, black walled, clear bottom plates (ThermoFisher Scientific). Immunoconjugates (anti-CEA-NE and anti-CEA mAb-AF647), and appropriate IgG1 negative controls were diluted (~1 ug/mL protein) in cell media. Samples of 100 µL were incubated with LS174T cells for 18 h in a 37 °C CO2 incubator. Afterwards, the samples were washed three times with cell media and resuspended in 1× phosphate buffered saline (PBS) for fluorescence microscopy imaging or detection with microplate reader (ex./em. 630/680 nm).

Murine Xenograft model

Xenografts were established in 8 week old female CD-1 nude mice (Charles River Laboratories) by subcutaneous inoculation of 5 × 106 cells in 100 µl of a 1:1 v/v solution of PBS (Corning, 21–040-CV) and Matrigel (Corning, 354234), and tumors were grown for ~3 weeks or until reaching approximately 1,000 mm3 in size, measured using calipers.

Radiolabeling of FERM NE immunoconjugates

89Zr labeling was conducted using an optimized protocol adapted from our lab [25]. Pre-assembled anti-CEA-NE formulation (0.5 mL) containing 4 mM FHOA in PFOB were radiolabeled with the following protocol (with the same procedure for IgG1-NE and unmodified NE): 500 µL of FERM NE immunoconjugate was mixed with 100 µL of 0.1 M HEPES (pH 7.5) buffer in an Eppendorf tube. 12 µL of ~8 mCi 89Zr-oxalate (in 1 M oxalic acid) stock was first neutralized in 45 µL 1 M HEPES (pH 8.0) buffer and confirmed with pH paper to be pH 8. Then, 1.34 mCi (~55 µL of the neutralized 89Zr solution) was added to the NE solution and placed on a thermomixer (37 °C, 350 rpm) for 30 min. Reaction completion and radiochemical purity was confirmed by silica impregnated iTLC paper (2 µL reaction mixture spotted) with 0.1 mM ammonium acetate, 50 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), pH 5.5 as an eluent, and showed no detectable free 89Zr within 30 min of reaction time (Fig. S12). The ~650 µL crude product was de-salted into 30 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.5) containing 2% v/v propylene glycol using a G-25 Sephadex column yielding ~2 mL of 89Zr anti-CEA-NE at 1.24 mCi (96% radioactive yield).

PET/CT imaging studies

Mice (n = 13) with established tumors were injected with ~100 µCi (200 µL) of 89Zr-labeled anti-CEA-NE (n = 4), IgG1-NE (n = 4), or unmodified NE (n = 5). Mice per group were selected for comparable tumor sizes, and investigators were blinded during tail vein injection and image acquisition. PET/CT data were acquired using an Inveon (Siemens) PET/CT scanner at a PET resolution of 256 × 256 × 326 voxels (0.059 mm3/voxel). A calibrated phantom reference containing 89Zr was also scanned using the same scanning parameters. Animals were anesthetized using 1–2% isoflurane in oxygen and warmed using heat pads to maintain body temperature throughout the procedure. PET data acquisition time was approximately 10 min, and data were acquired at 4, 24, and 120 h after tracer injection.

Image quantification and visualization

Images were quantified using VivoQuant software (Invicro). The volume of interest (VOI) was segmented using the PET/CT signal as a reference for tissue location and boundary definition, and the mean radioactivity within the tumor voxels was quantified. In addition, two muscle regions were designated as background, including a larger region (“muscle #1”) located on the left flank and smaller reference volume (“muscle #2”) located underneath the tumor on the right flank. The spleen and liver were also defined as VOIs, and radioactivity was quantified (Fig. S14).

Statistics

Two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test was used; a P-value less than 0.05 was considered significant. The software OriginPro 9.0 was used for statistical analyses of radioactivity in VOIs.

Histology

Excised tumors were embedded in optimal cutting temperature media, frozen on dry ice, sectioned at 10 μm thickness, and mounted on glass slides. Slides were air-dried, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature, washed in PBS, followed by blocking treatment using 10% normal goat serum, 0.5% BSA, and 1% mouse serum with 0.5% Tween 20 for 1 h at room temperature. Antibodies were used at 1:50 as follows: F4/80 (BioLegend, 123101) and CEA conjugated to AF750 at 2–20 µg/mL (MedChemExpress, HY-P99160). The antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 °C and then washed. For F4/80, an anti-rat secondary was used at 1:125 (BioLegend, 405420) and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. Following another wash, the slides were counterstained with Hoechst (Thermofisher Scientific, H3570) and mounted in Prolong Gold (Thermofisher Scientific, P36930). Slides were imaged using a ZEISS AxioScan.Z1 slide scanner equipped with 20 × 0.8NA objective, Colibri7 excitation source, appropriate band-pass filter cubes, and Hamamatsu Orca Flash 4.0 V2 camera. Additional microscopy settings are detailed in Supplementary Information, Sect. 10.

Results

Formulation and characterization of targeted nanoemulsions

Targeted FERM NEs were formulated using PFOB oil (Fig. 1A) and soluble FHOA (Fig. 1B) via emulsification with lipid-based surfactants containing commercially available DBCO-lipids (Fig. 1C). Initially, to optimize radiometal loading capacity within the NE droplets, the solubility of FHOA was screened in a range of biomedically-relevant PFC oils (Figure S1) that were previously used in artificial blood substitute clinical trials [28]. Among the candidates, PFOB and 1,8-dichloroperfluorooctane (PFDCO) demonstrate the highest solubility of FHOA (Fig. S7). However, PFDCO yields NE with large droplet size and a high polydispersity index (PDI > 0.3 and > 200 nm average particle size, Table S1) and was excluded from further development. In contrast, PFOB offers both high FHOA solubility (Fig. S7) and favorable droplet size to enable sterile filtration (Table S1).

To impart targeting functionality to the NE, commercially available DBCO-lipids, along with lipid surfactants, were emulsified with PFC oil and water by adapting previously reported methods for “click-ready” PFC NEs [27]. Immuno-reactive NEs were prepared using either humanized anti-CEA (Labetuzumab) or negative serotype control (IgG1 kappa). The mAbs were modified by stochastic labeling of lysine residues with azido-PEG4-NHS ester linkers (Supplementary Information, Sect. 6.1), enabling chemoselective coupling to the DBCO-bearing PFC NEs. The number of azide groups per antibody and the density of DBCO groups on the NE surface were quantified (Supplementary Information, Sect. 6.2) to systematically study antibody labeling density. Azide-modified antibodies were then conjugated to NE by mixing at room temperature overnight. Using this approach, of order 6–120 antibodies per ~200 nm droplet could theoretically be achieved based on the measured density of reactive DBCO groups on NE surface, as determined by azide-dye labeling (Fig. S10 and Supplementary Information, Sect. 6.2).

Specificity of immunoconjugates in vitro

To verify whether the anti-CEA mAb maintained CEA-avidity following conjugation to NE, ELISA studies were performed using biotinylated CEACAM5 protein immobilized on a streptavidin-coated plate. Additionally, live cell in vitro uptake studies were performed by coincubation of NE tracers with a CEA-expressing cell line (LS174T). To enable detection in these assays, NE was rendered fluorescent using two different methods. First, prior to NE conjugation, lysine amine groups on the azide-functionalized antibody were labeled with AF647-NHS ester [29]. Alternatively, fluorescent cyanine dyes were incorporated into NE core using previously reported fluorous dye chemistry [30].

Using the fluorescently labeled antibody approach, ELISA results indicated a modest reduction in binding avidity for the “low-density” antibody-NE (consisting of 1 mol% azide-functionalized antibody relative to NE DBCO groups) compared to the fluorescent, untethered control antibody (Fig. 2A) at the same concentration. The reduction is attributed to the stochastic labeling of lysine residues on the antibody with azido-PEG4-NHS ester, as well as the attachment of a sterically bulky NE droplet to the antibody, both of which presumably can decrease antibody affinity. However, increasing surface antibody ratio by ~10-fold (denoted by “high-density” antibody-NE) resulted in enhanced avidity towards CEA, as evidenced by a lower half-maximal effective concentration (EC50). This improvement is presumably due to increased multivalency from the higher antibody surface density. Nonetheless, the anti-CEA-NE immunoconjugates (regardless of ratio) maintained strong and specific binding to CEA, as confirmed by comparison to negative isotype control (IgG1-NE) and competition assays using ~2,000-fold molar excess of unmodified anti-CEA mAb, where minimal binding-associated fluorescence is observed (Fig. 2B).

The anti-CEA-NE specificity was further validated in vitro using LS174T (CEA-positive) cells. The cells were co-incubated with fluorescent (Cy7) NE for 18 h at 37 °C then examined by fluorescence microscopy revealing distinct labeling of cells with the anti-CEA-NE, with minimal fluorescence in the control condition (IgG1-NE, Fig. 2C, D), consistent with the ELISA results. Antibody density on the NE surface was optimized to improve cell binding, with maximal binding-associated fluorescence observed for 10–20 mol% antibody relative to the theoretical DBCO lipid content (Fig. S10). This is consistent with the binding observed in ELISA, where using a 10-fold higher surface antibody density resulted in improved binding towards CEA. Based on this, we selected the lower end of this range (10 mol%) for further studies to balance high targeting efficiency and to minimize antibody use. Fluorescent DBCO-NEs reacted with higher equivalents of azide-modified antibody (for example, 30–60 mol%) exhibited reduced fluorescence-binding signal (Fig. S11). This decrease is likely from saturation of reactive DBCO sites on the NE surface and subsequent competition between unbound antibody and immuno-reactive NEs towards CEA on the cell surface.

In vivo PET/CT imaging

Proof-of-concept in vivo studies evaluated the antibody targeted NEs in a mouse xenograft colorectal tumor model. NEs were radiolabeled with 89Zr using an optimized protocol adapted from our lab [25], yielding minimal free 89Zr after 30 min of reaction time (Fig. S12, Supplementary Information), confirmed by instant thin layer chromatography (iTLC). Following removal of excess 89Zr by gel filtration, ~ 96% radioactive yield was obtained, with no evidence of free 89Zr (Fig. S13).

Upon tail vein injection of 89Zr-labeled NE tracers (~100 µCi) into mice (n = 4–5 per group), PET revealed targeted accumulation of the 89Zr-labeled CEA-NE within tumors at 4 h post-injection, with sustained signal for at least 120 h (Fig. 3). In contrast, the IgG1-NE and nonfunctionalized NE control animals show minimal tumor uptake (Fig. 3). At the 4-h time point, all NE tracers display a prominent signal within the circulatory system, especially in heart and aortic artery (Fig. 3), which diminished by 24 and 120 h. This is consistent with prior studies [25], where the blood clearance half-life of non-functionalized FERM NE is ~14.6 h. For all tracers, clearance from circulation occurs primarily through the spleen, liver, and lymph nodes (Fig. 3) consistent with uptake of cells of the reticuloendothelial system.

Time-dependent quantification of tracer uptake in tumor was performed using volume of interest (VOI) analysis, as shown in Fig. 4A. The tumor VOI was segmented, guided by the CT anatomy. As early as 4 h anti-CEA-NE post-injection, the radioactivity in tumor peaked at a mean standard uptake value (SUVmean ± SD) of 0.83 ± 0.27 (11.51 ± 7.24% injected dose, ID). A two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test using data across all time points indicated that tumor accumulation of anti-CEA NE was significantly higher than either negative control IgG1-NE at 0.28 ± 0.06 (1.26 ± 0.26% ID, P < 0.0001) or the unmodified NE at 0.44 ± 0.07 (2.49 ± 0.69% ID, P < 0.005) (Fig. 4B). The time-dependent accumulation of radiotracers in tumors was not statistically significant, suggesting that majority of the CEA-target engagement occurs within the 4- to 24-h window while the tracer is still in circulation (Fig. 4B).

The tumor-to-background ratio (TBR) was assessed using the ratio of PET signals in tumor to muscle VOI (Fig. 4C). The TBR time-course displays efficient CEA targeting and sustained retention. At 4 h TBR = 3.20 ± 1.57 and gradually increased to 4.70 ± 2.10 after 120 h based on SUVmean (or %ID) in tumor versus muscle VOI. Since the absolute radioactivity remains relatively stable over time (Fig. 4B), the increasing TBR suggests retention of the probe within tumor, accompanied by clearance of tracer from background tissues over time. In contrast, the negative controls display minimal change in TBR, remaining between 1.1 and 1.5 throughout the imaging time-course.

Additionally, Fig. 4D displays the maximum peak (peakmax) TBR ratios, defined as the ratio of a 3 × 3 × 3 cube of voxels with the highest PET signal within the VOI. At 4 h, peakmax = 10.82 ± 3.61 and increases approximately logarithmically over time to 29.29 ± 8.93. The fitted curve reaches saturation point at 4–24 h, supporting the finding that the majority of CEA targeting occurs within this time window.

To study potential passive NE targeting, we calculated tumor-to-tumor ratios, defined as the decay-corrected PET signal in mice receiving anti-CEA NE divided by the signal in mice receiving either IgG1-NE or unmodified NE (Fig. 4E). For example, at 24 h, the data show tumor-to-tumor ratios of 2.36 and 1.49 for the CEA targeted tracer versus either IgG1-NE or unmodified NE, respectively (Fig. 4E). This suggests that the unmodified NE exhibits more non-specific, passive uptake in tumors compared to IgG1-NE, potentially due to the antibody attachment causing increased NE bulk and decreased steric tumor access or potentially charge repulsion. Overall, passive uptake signal in tumor is consistent with potential tracer in circulation, enhanced permeability and retention effect, and/or myeloid cell uptake in the tumor microenvironment.

Figure 4F displays the mean radioactivity per voxel (± SD) in tumors 4 h post-injection with anti-CEA-NE yielding 4.82 ± 0.02 µCi/g (median = 3.24 µCi/g; interquartile range, IQR = 5.56 µCi/g). In contrast, negative controls, IgG1- and unmodified-NE show mean radioactivity per voxel of 1.40 ± 0.02 µCi/g (median = 1.18 µCi/g; IQR = 1.69 µCi/g) and 1.54 ± 0.02 µCi/g (median = 1.54 µCi/g; IQR = 1.52 µCi/g) respectively.

Histology

We confirmed specificity of anti-CEA-NE towards CEA in vivo using immunohistochemistry to detect tumor cell CEA and macrophage (F4/80), along with the fluorescent NE (Fig. 5). Tumors were excised and sectioned 24 h post-injection with immunoreactive-NE tracers harboring Cy5 fluorescent dye. CEA staining appears heterogeneous, with clusters of cells distributed throughout the tumors (Fig. 5), confirming the presence of CEA+ cells. The F4/80 stain shows that the macrophages are at the perimeter and in areas of stroma crossing through the tumors, a pattern commonly seen in implanted tumors. The NE-associated Cy5 signal is weak but detectable in CEA⁺ tumor cells following anti-CEA-NE treatment, while no Cy5 signal is observed in macrophages. In contrast, Cy5 signal is absent in tumors treated with IgG1-NE or unmodified NE at the same detection threshold. At the tumor perimeter, Cy5 signal is observed for all NE formulations, with the strongest signal seen for anti-CEA-NE. In these peripheral regions, strong keratinocyte and macrophage signals are also present.

Discussion

In this study we developed a novel PET tracer employing FERM NE encapsulation of 89Zr and click chemistry-facilitated antibody display to generate a CEA-targeted tracer for tumor imaging. Encapsulation of 89Zr using the FHOA chelator within the fluorous NE core enables flexible radiolabeling and provides an addition layer of protection against endogenous demetallation and transmetallation, while preserving surface chemistry for precise targeting. Furthermore, click-chemistry attachment allows tunable surface antibody density and the ability to accommodate a wide range of vectors for imaging biological targets via a stable, covalent bond.

An advantage of FERM encapsulation of 89Zr is that larger nanocarriers theoretically have the potential to encapsulate a large radionuclide payload within the inner volume compared to surface attachment [31]. The maximum metal loading capacity in NEs is ultimately constrained by the solubility of the chelate in the PFC. Among candidates, PFOB and 1,8-dichloroperfluorooctane (PFDCO), both halogenated, demonstrate the highest solubility of FHOA (Fig. S7). This observation suggests that FHOA solubility generally correlates with the lipophilicity of the PFC. Halogenated PFCs are slightly more lipophilic than non-halogenated due to the increased polarizability of the terminal bromine or chlorine atom, yet retain hydrophobicity [32]. The solubility of FHOA in PFOB is viewed as advantageous, as the increased lipophilicity of PFOB from the terminal bromine facilitates rapid clearance (~7 day half-life) by circulating lipoproteins compared to perfluoralkyl PFCs such as perfluoro-15-crown-5 ether (PFCE) which exhibits a biological half-life > 100 days. Thus, the PFOB clearance profile aligns more closely with the radioactive half-life of 89Zr. (PFOB clears the body via lung exhalation as a gas without chemical modification [33, 34].)

We estimate antibody-FERM NE conjugates contain ~10,000 chelates per (200 nm) droplet (at 4 mM FHOA in PFOB), compared to just 2–4 chelates per full length antibody used in immuno-PET applications (Supplementary Information, Sect. 11). Based on our radiolabeling protocol, we estimate only ~0.5 mol% FHOA sites are occupied by Zr atoms, corresponding to roughly 50 Zr atoms per NE droplet. Although higher 89Zr loading capacities were not explored in this study, the high chelator concentration within the fluorous phase suggests that the FERM NE formulation could potentially accommodate much larger radio-payloads with further optimization, potentially boosting sensitivity to low-abundance biomarkers.

Prior in vitro studies of FERM NE stability with competing ions [25] show that, in Fe3+ saturated FERM NE, addition of Zr4+ in buffer displaces Fe from the complex. Conversely, Zr4+ saturated FERM NE is minimally displaced by excess Fe3+. This inherent metal stability is presumably due, in part, to the FHOA chelate which offers eight coordination sites through its hydroxamic acid groups to fully saturate the Zr4+ coordination sphere. Chelation stability is also supported by the PET images (Fig. 3), which shows minimal free 89Zr outside of the expected tumor (target) and normal clearance organs (liver, spleen, lymph nodes), with minimal signal in bone or other non-target tissues. However, further stability studies are needed, and analytical methods are under development to “stress test” these unconventional colloidal compositions.

Antibody functionalized NE tracers are potentially susceptible to receptor-mediated endocytosis, thereby enabling efficient intracellular delivery that can boost sensitivity. Once internalized, NEs coalesce into larger fluorous droplets that persist within low-pH lysosomal vesicles [35, 36]. Importantly, FERM NE chelation is relatively resistant to metal release under low-pH conditions [25, 37–39], in contrast to commonly used 89Zr chelates such as desferrioxamine B (DFO) [40]. This stability enables prolonged intracellular retention and limits osmotic pressure-driven efflux of the radionuclide into background tissues. As a result, the radionuclide remains associated with the target cell, potentially enabling reduced dose-activity requirements for a diagnostic scan. Notably, prior studies using the same unmodified FERM NE platform for imaging inflammation reported signal-to-background ratios of approximately 10:1 across a wide range of administered activities (3.7 to 0.037 MBq), corresponding to doses that are 10–100 fold lower than often used for PET tracers in mice [25].

PET imaging studies using anti-CEA minibodies and diabodies typically report TBRs of 2–4 within 4–5 h post-injection [16, 41, 42], comparable to those achieved with anti-CEA NE. At later timepoints, these engineered fragments can achieve substantially higher tumor accumulation. In a study using CEA-antibody fragments to target LS174T xenografts, TBRs were comparable to those observed in our work, and liver uptake was also observed (~31% ID/g) [43]. Larger, full-length mAbs targeting CEA exhibit liver accumulation in the range of 5–20%ID/g at 24 h [44, 45], which is comparable to our findings. Importantly, for 89Zr-labeled FERM NE, definitive dosimetry studies have not yet been performed and are the topic of future work. In general, nonfunctionalized PFC NE droplets are scavenged in situ by cells of the reticuloendothelial system, primarily in the liver (Kuffler cells) and spleen (myeloid cells); presumably, the liver will be the dose-limiting organ.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates proof-of-concept in vivo PET imaging using a novel targeted NE platform. Click chemistry enabled efficient antibody functionalization of 89Zr-FERM nanoemulsions, resulting in highly specific in vivo targeting of the tumor-associated antigen CEA. The FERM NE platform supports high radiometal payloads with robust in vivo stability, contributing to high PET specificity. Importantly, the modular, click-ready design allows straightforward incorporation and display of diverse targeting ligands, supporting broad applicability in molecular imaging and theranostic delivery strategies. Further studies are warranted to evaluate the clinical feasibility of this targeted NE platform.
