Asthma among Sámi in Sweden: a population-based register study
Authors: Miguel San Sebastian, Emil Xabier San Sebastián, Jon Petter Stoor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA population-based study has reported a higher prevalence of self-reported asthma among the Sámi compared with the general Swedish population. However, it remains unclear whether this reflects a true difference or reporting bias. This study aimed to estimate asthma incidence among the Sámi and compare it with that of the non-Sámi population using register-based data from 1993 to 2018. We conducted a retrospective cohort study using the Sámi electoral roll and the reindeer ownership register to identify Sámi individuals. Each Sámi participant was matched with four non-Sámi controls by age, sex and municipality. Asthma cases were identified through the National Patient Register, covering inpatient and specialist outpatient care. Incidence rate ratios (IRRs) were estimated using sex-stratified Poisson regression models. The study included 13,311 Sámi individuals and 53,219 controls. Sámi men (IRR = 1.19, 95% CI = 1.00–1.41) and women (IRR = 1.23, 95% CI = 1.04–1.45) had higher rates of specialist asthma diagnoses than controls. Sámi men, but not women, also had a higher risk of asthma-related hospitalisation (IRR = 1.43, 95% CI = 1.07–1.90). These findings suggest a higher asthma burden among the Sámi. Further research is needed to clarify underlying risk factors and inform targeted public health interventions.

Introduction

Asthma is a global problem with marked geographical variations. Its prevalence appears to be increasing in densely populated regions, such as Africa, Latin America and parts of Asia, where it was previously low, suggesting a growing global burden. Conversely, global differences in asthma symptom prevalence are narrowing, particularly among adolescents, partly due to declining trends in Western Europe [1].

Literature on asthma in indigenous communities is scarce. A review from Australia reported a higher prevalence of asthma in Indigenous compared with non-Indigenous populations, although there was wide variation between studies [2]. A more recent report found an asthma prevalence of 16% among First Nations people, who were 1.6 times more likely to report having asthma than non-Indigenous Australians [3]. Studies from the United States indicate that American Indian and Alaska Native (AI/AN) communities have higher asthma prevalence rates than the general population. AI/AN adults were almost twice as likely to experience daily asthma symptoms as other adults with asthma, and among AI/AN children, 30% of 12th graders reported lifetime asthma compared with 20% in the general population [4]. In Canada, a population-based study of Aboriginal Canadians reported a prevalence of diagnosed asthma of 11% in children and 12% in adults, with wide variation by place of residence [5].

The Sámi are the only Indigenous population within the European Union, covering their traditional territory the northern regions of Norway, Sweden, Finland and the Kola peninsula in Russia [6]. Similar to the international indigenous literature, information about their asthma situation is limited. Studies conducted among Sámi schoolchildren (7–13 years) in Norway in the 1990s reported a lifetime and point prevalence of asthma of 13.6% vs. 8.2% and 6.7% vs. 2.8%, respectively, compared with their non-Sámi counterparts [7]. Another population-based study of adults in 2004 showed a higher prevalence of self-reported asthma in the Sámi compared to the Norwegian population (14.3% vs 11.2%) [8].

Following a similar pattern, a recent study by our research group among the Sámi population in Sweden reported an overall prevalence of self-reported asthma of 20.6% and a prevalence of asthma sufferers with symptoms of 13.9%. Women were more likely to report having symptoms (15.9%) than men (11.7%) [9]. These prevalence's were higher than those observed in the Swedish population, both in men (prevalence ratio: 1.91; 95% CI = 1.68–2.16) and in women (prevalence ratio: 1.73; 95% CI = 1.56–1.90) [10]. These findings based on self-reported data require confirmation through proper medical diagnosis.

To address this, the aim of this study was to estimate the incidence of asthma among the Sámi population in Sweden and compare it with the general Swedish population using register-based data during the period 1993–2018.

Method

Population

Two registers were used to identify the Sámi population and construct a sample population: the Sámi electoral roll (SER) and the reindeer ownership register (ROR), both held by Sámediggi (Sámi parliament in Sweden). Although the Sámi electoral roll only includes individuals aged 18 years and older, the reindeer ownership register includes people of all ages, including children. An index Sámi population was defined as all persons who were registered in the SER at least once for the Sámi parliament elections in 1993, 1997, 2001, 2005, 2009, 2013 and 2017, or who were registered in the ROR at least once in 1994, 1997, 2001, 2005, 2009, 2013 and 2017.

For each index Sámi, four non-Sámi individuals were randomly selected from the National Population and Housing Census Register to match the index Sámi in terms of sex, age (±2 years) and municipality. Individuals were included if they were not already included in the cases (matching without replacement) of the Sámi population.

Data sources

Cases were extracted from the National Board of Health and Welfare's national patient register which includes two types of sub-registers: the outpatient specialist care (available from 2001) and inpatient care (available from 1987). Both registers cover all private and public care providers in the country.

The codes for asthma included both the 9th (493) and 10th (J45) revisions of the International Classification of Diseases, including relevant subcategories for status asthmaticus. The linkage of the individual data was done by the National Board of Health and Welfare, while the creation of the control population was done at the SIMSAM laboratory, Umeå University.

Analysis

Asthma cases were defined as the first recorded asthma diagnosis in inpatient or specialist outpatient care during the study period. Because complete information on asthma diagnoses before register coverage was unavailable, these estimates should be interpreted as rates of first recorded specialist or hospital diagnosis rather than true asthma incidence.

For both Sámi individuals and matched controls, follow-up began on 1 January 1993 for inpatient care and on 1 January 2001 for specialist outpatient care, corresponding to the availability of the respective registers. Follow-up ended at the first recorded asthma diagnosis, death, or 31 December 2018, whichever occurred first. Dates of death were obtained through linkage with the national cause of death register and used for censoring.

Asthma morbidity rates were first calculated for all Sámi and for the Swedish control population for the entire follow-up period. A Poisson regression model was then used to calculate the morbidity ratios and their 95% confidence intervals. All analyses were stratified according to sex.

Ethics

The study was approved by the Swedish Ethical Review Authority (Dnr 2020-04803 and Ö 70–2020/3.1). Furthermore, the Ethical Guidelines for Sámi Health Research [11] were also followed and the board of the Sámi parliament in Sweden approved the SámiHET research project.

Discussion

The findings of this study support the hypothesis that asthma imposes a greater burden on the Sámi population compared to the non-Sámi population in Sweden.

Population-based studies conducted between 1990 and 2008 estimated an asthma incidence rate of 140 per 100,000 person-years in Sweden [12]. Higher rates have been reported in northern Sweden, reaching 260 per 100,000 person-years between 2006 and 2016, although incidence remained relatively stable over time [13]. In contrast, asthma-related hospitalisations among adults have declined substantially, decreasing by 52.5% (from 31.4 to 14.9 per 100,000 person-years) between 2006 and 2022, likely reflecting improvements in asthma management and updated clinical guidelines [14].

Risk factors for asthma among Indigenous populations are highly context-specific. Among Native Americans, key determinants include poverty, obesity, limited access to specialised healthcare and environmental exposures such as indoor air pollution (e.g. wood-burning stoves and tobacco smoke) and outdoor pollution [15–17]. In Canada, factors such as housing dampness, alcohol consumption, comorbidities and financial strain have been associated with increased odds of non-atopic asthma among First Nations adults [18]. In Australia, daily smoking, physical inactivity, obesity and urban residence have been identified as major risk factors among Indigenous populations [3,19].

Selnes [20] explored several potential explanations for the higher prevalence of asthma among Sámi children in Norway, including exposure to indoor dampness, polyvinyl chloride (PVC) materials, textile wall coverings, animal allergens, smoking behaviours, socioeconomic conditions and genetic susceptibility. However, the study ruled out genetics, indoor dampness and wall-to-wall carpeting as key contributors, without providing a clear alternative explanation for the observed increase.

In our previous population-based study among the Sámi, financial strain, stress, overweight and obesity and self-reported allergies were associated with asthma [9]. However, no significant ethnic differences in financial hardship or stress have been observed in Sweden [21]. Although smoking is a well-established risk factor for asthma, Sámi individuals tend to smoke less than their non-Sámi counterparts [21]. Moreover, our study design accounted for age and municipality of residence through matching, thereby controlling for these potential confounders.

In contrast, overweight, obesity and allergies, well-established risk factors for asthma, appear to be more prevalent among Sámi adults than in the general Swedish population. These factors may partly explain the higher asthma prevalence [21] and could also contribute to the increased incidence observed in this study. Occupational and leisure-time cold exposure may also be relevant [22]. Cold air can trigger bronchoconstriction and worsen asthma symptoms, and this may be particularly important for Sámi individuals involved in reindeer herding or other outdoor activities. However, our data did not include information on occupation or cold exposure, and this should be explored in future studies.

Methodological considerations

This study has several methodological strengths that enhance its reliability. The use of national patient registers allowed the identification of physician-diagnosed asthma cases recorded in inpatient and specialist outpatient care, thereby reducing recall bias compared with self-reported asthma. The large, matched cohort of Sámi and non-Sámi individuals also provided sufficient statistical power. Matching participants by sex, age and municipality reduced potential confounding, while the use of physician-diagnosed asthma minimised recall bias. In addition, the inclusion of both inpatient and specialist outpatient data further strengthens the validity of the findings.

However, some limitations should be considered. The identification of Sámi individuals through electoral and reindeer ownership registers may have led to misclassification and limited representativeness, as not all reindeer owners are Sámi, and it is unclear how many Sámi individuals are not captured in these registries. Differences in healthcare access between Sámi and non-Sámi populations may also have introduced bias. Furthermore, as asthma diagnoses were based on specialist care, cases managed solely in primary care were not included due to the lack of an appropriate registry.

In Sweden, most asthma patients are diagnosed and treated in primary care, whereas the present study relied on inpatient and specialist outpatient registers. Consequently, the findings should not be interpreted as representing the total incidence of asthma in the population. Rather, they reflect asthma cases severe enough, complex enough, or otherwise referred to be recorded in specialist or hospital care. A further limitation is that some individuals may have had asthma before the start of follow-up. Therefore, the reported rates may include first recorded diagnoses in specialist or hospital care rather than strictly incident asthma cases.

Finally, while the study is robust within the Swedish context, the findings may not be fully generalisable to Sámi populations in other countries.

Conclusion

This study confirms that the Sámi population in Sweden experiences a higher burden of asthma compared to the general Swedish population. While overweight, obesity and allergy may contribute to this disparity, further research focusing on specific asthma-related risk factors, particularly among children, is necessary to better elucidate its underlying causes.

Future studies should consider linking Sámi population data with primary care (such as the Swedish National Airway Register) or epidemiological sources, provided that data access and ethical issues related to Sámi identity are carefully addressed.
