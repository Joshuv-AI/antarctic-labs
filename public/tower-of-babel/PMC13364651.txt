A k-mer-based genome-wide association study approach empowering gene mining in polyploids
Authors: Shuai Chen, Xinlong Liu, Shenyang Qu, Yuhan Song, Kun Chai, Hongbo Liu, Yuebin Zhang, Zhongqiang Xia, Xiaofeng Li, Jungang Wang, Muqing Zhang, Hongbo Li, Guo-Bo Chen, Chris Maliepaard, Xingtan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGenome-wide association studies in complex polyploids are hindered by genotyping ambiguity and allele dosage complexity. Here we present KMERIA, a k-mer-based framework specifically designed to address these challenges, enabling efficient genotyping and robust association mapping in complex polyploid genomes. Rigorous benchmarking with simulated and empirical datasets demonstrates that KMERIA surpasses existing methods in accuracy and statistical power. By applying KMERIA to 290 wild sugarcane (Saccharum spontaneum) accessions and integrating a 15-accession graph pangenome to capture structural variations, we identified new genes regulating sucrose biosynthesis (SsMGT) and tillering (for example, SsERF14, SsNGA5, SsNAC, SsARF8, SsLOG and SsSCR). These findings elucidate the genetic architecture of yield-related traits and provide actionable targets for sugarcane breeding. Collectively, KMERIA bridges a critical methodological gap in polyploid genomics, while our graph-pangenome integration provides a powerful framework for deciphering genotype–phenotype relationships in crops with complex architectures.Subject terms: Population genetics, Plant genetics

Main

Polyploidy, the presence of multiple sets of chromosomes, is a defining feature of many cultivated crops. Remarkably, approximately 75% domesticated plants, including staples such as wheat, potato, cotton and sugarcane, are polyploids1,2. This genetic phenomenon has driven the development of traits that confer advantages over their diploid counterparts3–5, including increased vigor, larger organs and enhanced resilience against environmental stresses6,7. Understanding the genetic architecture of polyploids is therefore critical for advancing crop improvement and ensuring global food security.

Despite their agricultural significance, the genetic complexity of polyploids poses substantial challenges for traditional genome-wide association studies (GWASs). Conventional GWAS methods, primarily developed for diploid organisms, often fail to adequately address the redundancy and allelic diversity of polyploid genomes, hindering detection of nonadditive effects and dosage-dependent phenotypic contributions. Although allele dosage-based GWAS using tools like GWASpoly has been implemented in tetraploids and hexaploids8–10, these approaches demand extensive computational resources for variant calling and genotyping. Consequently, innovative strategies specifically designed for the unique genetic architecture of polyploids are urgently needed.

GWAS can leverage k-mers derived from whole-genome sequencing data as genetic markers. Unlike traditional markers, k-mers capture single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), indels and structural variations (SVs), enabling comprehensive genetic analysis independent of a reference genome and reducing alignment biases. This approach can address the ‘missing heritability’ problem by uncovering genetic factors that conventional methods overlook11. While promising in diploids, k-mer-based GWAS in polyploids has been limited, as current implementations fail to account for allele dosage effects12,13. In this context, variations in k-mer copy numbers across populations provide a robust means of assessing allele dosage, making k-mer-based GWAS particularly advantageous for polyploids.

Saccharum spontaneum, a wild sugarcane species, serves as a valuable germplasm resource for modern commercial hybrid breeding due to its advantageous traits such as high tillering capacity, robust stress tolerance and broad environmental adaptability14. Its genome is characterized by high ploidy (4–16×), extensive homologous regions, and frequent chromosomal rearrangements15, making it an ideal model for studying polyploid-specific genomic methods. Here we present KMERIA, a k-mer-based GWAS strategy specifically designed for polyploids. KMERIA identifies trait-associated loci by correlating k-mer copy number variations with phenotypes. We also constructed a graph-based pangenome from 15 S. spontaneum accessions and 290 resequenced individuals, using KMERIA to identify genes linked to tiller number (TN) and apparent purity (AP). We further validated KMERIA across crops with varying ploidy levels, demonstrating its broad applicability in polyploid crop improvement.

Results

Challenges in applying GWAS to polyploids

GWASs hold immense promise for polyploid crops, but substantial challenges remain, particularly in genotyping. A major obstacle is the accuracy of short-read alignment. High sequence similarity among homologous chromosomes leads to multimapping, resulting in misalignments and reference bias (Extended Data Fig. 1a). Using simulated data from the haplotype-resolved genome of Saccharum hybrid POJ2878 (2n = 118)16, we found that only 43.08% reads were high-quality alignments; among them, 50.51% were misaligned to incorrect genomic locations (Extended Data Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 1). This introduces substantial errors in variant calling.

A second challenge is polyploid genotype calling. Although tools like updog17, SuperMASSA18 and fitPoly19 estimate genotypes from read depth but require pre-identified SNPs, accurate variant discovery itself is difficult in polyploids. Widely used tools such as GATK20 rely on high-quality alignments that are often unattainable, and their Bayesian genotype inference becomes computationally prohibitive as the number of possible allele combinations grows exponentially with ploidy (Extended Data Fig. 1b,c and Supplementary Note). Insufficient sequencing depth further obscures allele dosage ratios, leading to misclassification or missed low-frequency alleles21.

Algorithm overview of KMERIA and validation datasets

KMERIA circumvents these limitations through the following five integrated steps (Fig. 1): (1) ‘counting’ converts resequencing data into whole-genome k-mer profiles, (2) ‘matrix construction’ builds population-level k-mer genotype matrices that preserve dosage information, (3) ‘matrix pruning’ applies abundance thresholding, repeat masking and ploidy-aware normalization to generate high-confidence dosage matrices, (4) ‘k-mer testing’ uses a linear mixed model (LMM) incorporating population structure and kinship to identify dosage-dependent associations; and (5) ‘post-GWAS’ applies dual correction (Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) and modified Bonferroni) and aligns significant k-mers to a graph-based pangenome for precise localization in rearrangement-prone genomes. This structural-aware mapping facilitates precise localization of causal elements in rearrangement-prone polyploid genomes, facilitating the integration of haplotype-resolved gene networks and cis-regulatory landscape analysis for trait dissection.

Overview of the KMERIA pipeline.

KMERIA workflow includes five steps—counting, matrix construction, matrix pruning, k-mer testing and post-GWAS. After QC of resequencing samples, k-mer abundances were quantified per sample. These counts were compiled into a raw k-mer abundance matrix, which underwent ploidy-aware filtering to remove k-mers with aberrant depth (outside ploidy-informed thresholds). Sequencing depth correction was then applied to retained k-mers. Additional filtering excluded rare k-mers (missing rate >20% across samples). The filtered matrix was recoded to a continuous allelic dosage scale (0–2) through quantile normalization, ensuring compatibility with mixed linear models. Finally, significantly associated k-mers or k-mer-related reads were mapped to a graph-based reference genome to resolve precise genomic coordinates. QC, quality control; PCA, principal component analysis.

To validate the performance of KMERIA, we used simulated phenotypes in established polyploid populations and real datasets from hexaploid sweet potato8, tetraploid potato9, tetraploid alfalfa22, tetraploid Arabidopsis23, diploid rice24 and cultivated sugarcane16 (Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1), highlighting the broad applicability of KMERIA across diverse genetic characteristics of species.

Statistical power and FDR

We assessed statistical power and FDR using a simulated hexaploid sweet potato population of 294 accessions under varying heritability (h2 = 50%, 80%) and quantitative trait locus (QTL) numbers (10 or 100). Genetic markers were classified as residing within QTL regions or non-QTL regions for this assessment. We first characterized the genetic features of population through k-mer analysis. This yielded 450 million nonredundant k-mers across all individuals. Only 100 million (22.2%) of these k-mers were present in all of the genomes (Fig. 2a), indicating substantial interindividual k-mer variation. The distribution of total k-mer occurrence counts (KOCs) revealed that the vast majority of k-mers had low KOCs (1–6; Fig. 2b). This demonstrates that k-mer KOCs effectively represent allelic dosage in polyploids. Subsequently, principal component analysis performed on the KOC matrix produced population clusters consistent with published studies (Pearson correlation with PC1—R = 0.966, P = 9.23 × 10−174; PC2—R = 0.735, P = 2.73 × 0−51 (Fig. 2c,d and Supplementary Fig. 2a,b)), validating k-mer KOCs as an alternative for population structure study.

Phenotypic simulated datasets were designed to reflect traits controlled by either a few major-effect genes (10 QTLs) or multiple genes (100 QTLs), with heritability levels of 50% and 80%. Statistical power was defined as the proportion of true QTLs detected at a given type I error rate (α), while FDR was calculated as the proportion of false positives among detected association loci. KMERIA was compared against three widely used GWAS methods—GEMMA25, a standard GWAS approach for diploids, a k-mer-based GWAS approach kmersGWAS12 and GWASpoly10, an algorithm specifically designed for autopolyploids.

For traits influenced by a small number of major-effect genes, we simulated ten QTLs with effects following a Gaussian distribution (Supplementary Fig. 3a,b). Across heritability levels of 50% and 80%, KMERIA consistently outperformed GEMMA, kmersGWAS and GWASpoly in terms of statistical power while maintaining lower Type I error rates (Fig. 2e,f). These results demonstrate the higher power of KMERIA in detecting major-effect QTLs. In scenarios involving polygenic traits influenced by 100 QTLs (Supplementary Fig. 3c,d), KMERIA again exhibited higher statistical power compared to diploid GWAS approach (GEMMA), kmersGWAS and GWASpoly, regardless of heritability levels (Fig. 2g,h and Extended Data Fig. 3a–d).

To further assess statistical performance, we also compared FDR between KMERIA and established GWAS approach by recording false-positive QTLs across ten repeated tests. KMERIA consistently demonstrated lower FDR than GEMMA, kmersGWAS and GWASpoly across most scenarios (Fig. 2i–l), highlighting its robustness and reliability in reducing spurious associations.

Additionally, we compared the loci detected by KMERIA and GWASpoly to evaluate their overlap and unique contributions. KMERIA showed a strong correlation (R = 0.54, P = 0.00031) with GWASpoly in identifying significant association loci (Fig. 2m). Notably, KMERIA not only identified shared QTLs but also detected additional association signals that were missed by GWASpoly, suggesting the robustness and superiority of KMERIA.

Factors influencing statistical power of KMERIA

To assess the robustness of KMERIA, we systematically examined several factors that may influence its statistical power—(1) the proportion of k-mers used to infer population structure in the LMM, (2) the optimal k-mer size for GWAS analysis, (3) the effect of missing k-mer markers on polyploid GWAS outcomes and (4) the impact of population size on polyploid GWAS results.

KMERIA uses a random sampling strategy to select a subset of k-mer markers from the full k-mer genotype matrix for inferring population structure and relatedness in LMM. This approach reduces computational burden and mitigates overfitting by eliminating redundant information. To determine the minimum number of k-mers required for population structure calculation, we randomly selected 5%, 1% and 0.1% k-mer tags from the tetraploid potato genotype matrices to perform principal component analysis as a measure of population structure. The results showed that the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) derived from the three sampling subsets exhibited strong correlations (R = 0.99, P = 6.7 × 10−116) and aligned well with the known population structure9 (Supplementary Fig. 4a–e). Notably, even with just 0.1% k-mer genotypes, the core features of the population structure were preserved. These results demonstrate that a small, randomly sampled subset of k-mers is sufficient for population structure analysis, enabling computational efficiency without sacrificing accuracy.

The selection of k-mer size k is a potential factor influencing GWAS performance. Using a tetraploid potato dataset, we systematically evaluated k-mer sizes ranging from 17 to 31 bp, assessing their impact on association mapping. Statistical power and empirical FDR were calculated using modified Bonferroni-corrected significance thresholds (P<α×kM, where M is the total number of k-mers). Among the tested values, 31-mer exhibited the lowest FDR, indicating that larger k-mer sizes enhance signal specificity in polyploid GWAS (Fig. 2n and Supplementary Fig. 5a–i), suggesting that larger k-mers capture functional genetic variation more effectively.

We also assessed the effect of missing k-mer genotypes on GWAS performance using tetraploid potato datasets with increasing rates of genotype missingness rates (20%, 50% and 80%) under a fixed k value of 31 bp. As the rate of missingness increased, statistical power decreased (Supplementary Fig. 6a–c), primarily due to reduced accuracy in kinship estimation. This finding highlights the importance of genotype completeness in maintaining the reliability of polyploid GWAS.

Population size is a major determinant of GWAS statistical power. To investigate its impact in polyploid GWAS, we used an Arabidopsis population with sample sizes of 100, 200, 300, 400, 500 and 600 individuals. With a fixed k value of 31, we conducted ten replicate tests for each population size. Results demonstrated a clear positive correlation between population size and statistical power, with larger populations yielding significantly improved detection of QTLs (Fig. 2o). This trend aligns with findings from diploid GWAS studies26, reaffirming that increasing sample size is an effective strategy to enhance power in polyploid GWAS.

KMERIA also demonstrated substantial computational efficiency. On a tetraploid potato population (100 individuals), GATK (-ploidy mode) required ~35,413 h, while KMERIA completed the same task in 38.9–82.3 h (~1/430 of the time), with comparable peak memory usage (Supplementary Table 3 and Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8a,b). The association module bimbamAsso reduced runtime to ~1/10 of GEMMA and ~1/27 of GWASpoly, using only 1/5 and 1/54 of their peak memory, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 8c,d). These results highlight the superior performance of KMERIA in handling large-scale association mapping with minimal computational overhead.

Application and validation of KMERIA in GWAS across crops with different ploidy levels

To demonstrate the versatility and effectiveness of KMERIA across crops with varying polyploid levels, we analyzed publicly available resequencing datasets from five species—diploid rice24, tetraploid alfalfa22 and potato9, hexaploid sweet potato8 and highly polyploid cultivated sugarcane16, focusing on key agronomic traits.

We first tested KMERIA in a diploid rice population comprising 400 accessions24, focusing on amylose content, a critical determinant of grain quality. KMERIA successfully re-identified Waxy27 gene, a well-known major locus controlling amylose content. Compared to the conventional GWAS method and previous kmersGWAS, KMERIA exhibits significantly enhanced association signals for the trait with superior statistical validation (KMERIA—P = 6.76 × 10−27; ref. 24—P = 4.51 × 10−17; Fig. 3a and Extended Data Fig. 4).

To evaluate the performance of KMERIA in tetraploid crops, we applied it to two economically important species—alfalfa and potato. In alfalfa, we analyzed 176 accessions for the stem-to-leaf ratio, a key trait influencing forage quality and plant morphology. KMERIA identified two new QTLs on chromosomes 3 and 5, containing MsTUB5 (ref. 28), KINESIN-like29 and MsKEG30 (KEEP ON GOING) genes known to regulate plant morphogenesis (Fig. 3b and Extended Data Fig. 5a). Notably, k-mers associated with these genes exhibited significant differences in dosage across phenotypic groups, demonstrating that KMERIA effectively captures allelic dosage variation in a tetraploid context (Extended Data Fig. 5b,c). In potato, we analyzed 137 accessions with an average sequencing depth of 67×, focusing on several key agronomic traits. For tuber maturity, KMERIA successfully recaptured established QTLs (or genes), such as sga3.1 (ref. 31) and StCDF1 (ref. 32). Beyond these known loci, KMERIA identified a series of new genes involved in starch and sucrose metabolism, such as StINV3 (ref. 33) and StBGLu34. Furthermore, we identified several genes associated with tuber skin color (StUGP2 (ref. 35), StOPR36, StDOX37) and tuber flesh color—a primary objective in potato quality breeding. For flesh color, KMERIA not only detected previously reported QTLs on chr3 (ref. 38) and chr6 (ref. 9) but also uncovered new QTLs on chr4 and chr9, harboring functional genes StAUR39 and St3GT40 involved in root development and flavonoid biosynthesis, respectively (Fig. 3c and Extended Data Fig. 6a–c). While traditional SNP-based GWAS failed to detect many of these new loci, the previous kmersGWAS approach yielded several QTLs colocalized with KMERIA. Furthermore, k-mer dosage analysis suggested that these specific traits are relatively insensitive to allele dosage (Extended Data Fig. 6d,e). However, for traits significantly influenced by allele dosage, kmersGWAS exhibited a higher false-positive rate (Extended Data Fig. 6b,f). Collectively, these results underscore the superior detection power and robustness of KMERIA in the complexities of autopolyploid genomes.

Construction of S. spontaneum graph pangenome

S. spontaneum has made substantial genetic contributions to modern sugarcane breeding, providing key genetic factors for enhanced tillering, improved sucrose synthesis and higher photosynthetic efficiency, all of which contribute to increased yield, sugar accumulation and biomass production14. To maximize the improvement of genetic diversity in S. spontaneum, we selected 14 representative accessions based on k-mer-based phylogenetic relationships of 290 S. spontaneum accessions (Fig. 4a and Extended Data Fig. 9). These accessions span across 11 counties and geographical regions (Supplementary Table 4). Given the difference in genomic ploidy among the S. spontaneum accessions, we estimated the ploidy levels of these 14 accessions using resequencing reads. The results indicated that ploidy levels ranged from 8 to 11, with decaploid constituting 11 of 14 selected samples (Supplementary Table 4). Notably, decaploids also dominated among the 290 resequenced S. spontaneum accessions, accounting for 68.9% (200/290) of these samples. Consequently, we generated an average of 183 Gb HiFi reads and 225× genome coverage on average for the 14 accessions (the S. spontaneum monoploid genome size of ~800 Mb; Supplementary Table 5). These HiFi reads were assembled at the contig level with heterozygous regions retained. The assembly size ranged from 6.52 Gb (Ss09) to 9.32 Gb (Ss02), with the average contig N50 of 1.63 Mb (Table 1 and Supplementary Table 6). The completeness of assemblies was supported by using 1,614 benchmarking universal single-copy orthologs (BUSCO)43, with a completeness of 99.4% on average (Table 1). Merqury quality value (QV; 64.3 on average, QV 30 represents 99.9% base accuracy) evaluation also suggested that the base accuracy and completeness of assemblies arrived a high quality. The long terminal repeat (LTR) Assembly Index (LAI)44 of the assembled genomes are from 14.47 to 16.43, indicating that these assemblies have reached a reference level (Fig. 4b and Table 1). This suggests high quality of the assembled genomes. We predicted 231,354 (Ss09, ploidy =8 ) to 332,535 (Ss02, ploidy = 11) protein-coding genes (including allelic genes) through integrating ab initio-based, homology-based and RNA-seq prediction (Supplementary Tables 7 and 8). BUSCO43 evaluation for gene consistently scored above 98%, indicating a high level of completeness and representation of core genes in these assemblies (Supplementary Table 7).

Summary of S. spontaneum genome assemblies and gene annotations

Due to the absence of Hi–C data, we performed genome deduplication for each S. spontaneum assembly to meet the requirements for graph pangenome construction. These deduplicated genomes were scaffolded using the Sspon82-114 monoploid reference genome, yielding 14 monoploid assemblies with sizes ranging from 760 Mb to 772 Mb. BUSCO completeness assessment revealed an average score of 93.5%, confirming their suitability for graph pangenome construction (Supplementary Table 9). We subsequently built a graph-based pangenome incorporating these 14 monoploid genomes and the telomere-to-telomere Sspon82-114 monoploid assembly (Supplementary Table 9). This comprised ~3 Gb genome size spread across 142,571,025 nodes and connected by 193,033,606 edges, with nonreference nodes spanning 2,029 Mb (Fig. 4c–f). We detected SVs (≥50 bp) in the graph-based genome using the bubble popping algorithm and retained 277,133 SVs (70.1% < 1 kb, 21.5% < 10 kb and 8.4% > 10 kb) that could be genotyped in the S. spontaneum monoploid reference genome or at least one other genome (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Table 10). These SVs were either biallelic (42.7%, insertions, deletions and divergent alleles) or multiallelic (57.3%, spanning 2.51 Gb) with more than one nonreference path (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Table 11). For genes present in the reference genome, we found that 118,552 biallelic SVs are in potential expression regulatory regions or CDS in the S. spontaneum genome. We also discovered multiallelic SVs influencing 63.1% reference genes (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Table 11). These results indicate large effects of SVs on the integrity and gene expression.

Identification of genes associated with agronomic traits in wild sugarcane

The complexity of the genome ploidy (4–16×) in S. spontaneum poses a challenge for accurate genotyping using traditional methods15, limiting the application of GWAS. To identify potential functional genetic variants associated with important agronomic traits in sugarcane, we used KMERIA for GWAS of two crucial traits—AP and TN (Fig. 5a–c and Supplementary Table 12). We constructed k-mer genotype matrices for 290 S. spontaneum accessions, identifying 12.4 billion nonredundant k-mers present in at least 30% individuals. A significant association signal for AP was detected at chr6—8.1 Mb on the S. spontaneum 82–114 reference genome (Fig. 5d). This locus harbors a gene encoding a MAGNESIUM TRANSPORTER (MGT), which is hypothesized to regulate sucrose synthesis by modulating Mg2+ transport, thereby influencing sugarcane quality45,46. The top-associated k-mer located at the upstream of SsMGT exhibited a significant negative correlation with AP (R = −0.3, P = 2.0 × 10−7; Fig. 5e). Further comparative genomic analysis within the S. spontaneum pangenome identified a ~7.9-Kb SV and a 7-bp indel near the transcription start site of SsMGT in a distinct haplotype (Fig. 5f). Consistent with this, dosage analysis of SV-specific k-mers at the pangenome level revealed a negative correlation with AP (R = −0.483, P = 0.0804; Fig. 5g), suggesting that these SVs may impair SsMGT expression by disrupting transcriptional initiation. Such a mechanism could subsequently hinder Mg2+ transport efficiency and reduce sucrose accumulation.

The high tillering capacity of S. spontaneum has been a critical genetic resource for modern sugarcane breeding programs. Using KMERIA, we not only identified key genetic determinants of agronomic traits in S. spontaneum but also demonstrated the critical utility of the S. spontaneum graph-based pangenome in resolving k-mer localization challenges inherent to autopolyploid genomes. In the TN trait, 83.4% (94,035/112,807) trait-associated k-mers (P < 1 × 10−4) were aligned to coordinate in the linear reference genome (S. spontaneum 82–114). However, 16.6% k-mers absent from the reference were successfully localized using the graph pangenome. Crucially, comparative analysis revealed that the graph pangenome approach enhanced associated k-mer mappability by 11.6 percentage points compared to linear reference-based alignment (Supplementary Table 13), effectively recovering previously inaccessible loci in nonreference genomic regions.

Our study demonstrates that KMERIA effectively overcomes the inherent challenges of GWAS in complex autopolyploid genomes, offering a robust framework to pinpoint genetic loci underlying agronomic traits.

Discussion

The inherent complexity of polyploid genomes poses substantial challenges for traditional SNP-based GWAS methodologies. To address these limitations, we developed KMERIA, a k-mer-based GWAS framework specifically optimized for complex polyploid population. Its key innovation is circumventing conventional genotyping pipelines, where traditional alignment-based approaches20 often struggle with allele combination complexity and multilocus genotyping in polyploids, KMERIA eliminates the need for error-prone read alignments and reference-dependent genotyping. This not only enhances genotyping efficiency but also minimizes reference bias, an advantage that is particularly critical when high-quality reference genomes are absent.

A pivotal strength of KMERIA is its capacity to quantify allele dosage, a parameter with profound phenotypic implications in polyploids. Unlike diploid k-mer GWASs that use binary (0 or 1) presence–absence k-mers encoding12, KMERIA leverages k-mer abundance variations to resolve dosage states, thereby capturing genetic architecture of autopolyploid traits. This dosage-aware framework enables more precise genotype–phenotype associations compared to traditional SNP-based methods.

Furthermore, KMERIA provides a distinct advantage in genotyping non-SNP variants, including SVs and complex allelic variations, which are frequently overlooked by standard, SNP-centric approaches. By incorporating a graph-based pangenome into its downstream analysis, KMERIA enhances its capability to decipher complex variants. This underscores the importance of transitioning from linear reference genomes to graph-based representations, particularly for uncovering large-scale SVs that remain inaccessible through traditional methods. The ability to anchor previously unmappable k-mers, such as the 7.9-kb SV we identified in the promoter of SsMGT in sugarcane, enables the detection of genetic signals that would otherwise remain obscured, bridging the gap between association testing and the discovery of functionally relevant structural rearrangements. This capability is especially relevant for polyploid species, where such rearrangements are evolutionarily prevalent.

The pangenome approach further improved mapping ratio, consistent with previous studies53,54. To systematically evaluate k-mer origin strategies, we compared single-reference, multireference and graph-based pangenome mapping using GWAS k-mers from our S. spontaneum dataset (Supplementary Table 14). Single-reference mapping achieved only 81.2–84.6% anchoring, confirming the inadequacy of any single genome. The graph-based pangenome recovered 95.10% k-mers, while the multireference strategy reached 98.33%. The higher anchoring rate of the multireference approach reflects its ability to retain all sequence variations present across the constituent genomes, making it particularly suitable when exhaustive k-mer recovery is the primary goal. However, multireference mapping scatters hits across unlinked reference coordinates, preventing the direct assignment of k-mers to specific subgenomes, which limits dosage-sensitive interpretation. The graph pangenome, by providing a unified coordinate framework with explicit subgenomic paths, naturally resolves homeolog identity at the cost of a modest reduction in anchoring rate. The optimal choice is therefore goal dependent; multireference mapping excels at maximizing variant discovery, whereas the graph pangenome is preferable when biological interpretation demands precise homeolog resolution. A combined workflow can leverage the strengths of both approaches.

Despite these advancements, pinpointing causal variants remains challenging in polyploids. A key issue is the handling of k-mers mapping to multiple locations in pangenome. In polyploids, homeologous multimapping k-mers often reflect total allele dosage and should not be discarded. We classify significant k-mers into three interpretative categories. Uniquely mapped k-mers are treated at single-locus resolution, while those assigned to repetitive elements are excluded from single-locus estimation. Homeologous multimapping k-mers, whose aggregate counts capture total subgenome dosage, are retained as dosage signals; thus, this is the category most relevant to polyploid GWAS. Genic multicopy k-mers falling outside these classes require manual curation. This classification is best applied within a pangenome context; without subgenome annotations, a mapping-count threshold may serve as a proxy but risks misclassifying gene families. Although graph-based pangenomes mitigate reference bias, their adoption is constrained by limited polyploid resources, and residual artifacts may persist.

In conclusion, KMERIA represents a more efficient approach for polyploid GWAS, addressing longstanding bottlenecks in genotyping accuracy, dosage estimation and variant discovery. Its application to high-ploidy species, as exemplified by our S. spontaneum case study, underscores its potential to accelerate genetic research in polyploid crops and wild species. Future efforts to integrate expanding pangenome resources and refine k-mer-to-variant mapping algorithms will further enhance the utility of KMERIA in deciphering the genetic basis of complex traits in polyploid organisms.

Association mapping with KMERIA on polyploids

We present a method for identifying genetic loci associated with quantitative trait in polyploid species using whole-genome resequencing data, bypassing the need for read alignment to a reference genome. The workflow comprises the steps discussed further below.

Counting

Whole-genome resequencing reads from population samples were processed using our developed tool KMERIA ‘kmeria count’ to calculate k-mer abundances. Alternatively, the hash-based tool KMC3 is also supported. We used a k-mer size of 31, as this represents the maximum length efficiently storable using 64-bit integers. To enhance computational efficiency, k-mers occurring only once across all samples were discarded before association testing, as these likely represent sequencing errors.

Power evaluation across different type I error levels

The statistical power, type I error (α) and FDR were evaluated in association tests conducted on simulated datasets, which included predefined QTLs. The evaluation used the method56, alongside two approaches from our previous research57,58. A QTL was deemed successfully detected if a positive marker fell within a specified genomic distance threshold; this study uses 50 kb as the detected distance. Statistical power was quantified as the proportion of QTLs detected at a given threshold of type I error. In cases where no QTL was present within the interval, markers were used to generate the null distribution for negative control. The null distribution for type I error was based on markers not associated with any QTL, and FDR was calculated as the ratio of non-QTL markers to the total positive markers.

Genome sequencing, assembly and annotation

Plant material

This study analyzed 290 S. spontaneum accessions provided by the Yunnan Academy of Agricultural Sciences. The plant materials were grown at a subtropical plateau climate at Kaiyuan, Yunnan (23°30′–23°58′ N, 103°04′–103°43′ E, altitude—1051 m). Before planting, the buds were disinfected, and standardized field management practices, including irrigation, pest control and weed management, ensured uniform germination.

Sample selection

We carefully selected 14 representative S. spontaneum accessions based on the phylogenetic relationships among 290 accessions, aiming to capture a diverse spectrum of S. spontaneum germplasm resources. To establish the phylogeny of the selected accessions, the phylogenetic tree among S. spontaneum was estimated using Mashtree59.

HiFi sequencing

High-quality genomic DNA of 14 S. spontaneum was extracted from fresh leaves using the DNAsecure Plant Kit (TIANGEN). A total of 28 Single-Molecule Real-Time bell libraries with ~20 kb DNA insert fragments were sequenced with the circular consensus sequencing mode on a PacBio REVIO platform at ANNOROAD. These yielded HiFi reads of ~180 Gb with 225× coverage on average for S. spontaneum haploid genome.

Estimation of genome ploidy

To estimate the genome ploidy of the S. spontaneum population data, we used a de Bruijn graph-based method ploidyfrost60, which infers ploidy levels directly from whole-genome sequencing datasets without the need for a reference genome. This method involves the following steps: (1) k-mer database construction—for each sample, a k-mer database is built using KMC, which computes allelic k-mer coverage. To ensure a concise and error-free graph, low-coverage k-mers in the reads are masked. (2) Compacted de Bruijn grap construction—a compacted de Bruijn graph is generated from single or multiple samples using the Bifrost API, ensuring efficient and accurate graph representation. (3) Graph analysis and variant extraction—three subalgorithms are applied to analyze the graph structure, with putative variant information being extracted and output for further analysis.

Evaluation of genome size

The Illumina sequencing reads were applied to evaluate genome size based on the k-mer distribution using KMC (v3.2.158) and genomescope (v2.0.0; https://github.com/schatzlab/genomescope/) with the parameters: -k 21.

Genome assembly

Genome assembly of 14 HiFi-sequenced accessions uses hifiasm (v0.19.8-r603; https://github.com/chhylp123/hifiasm)61 with default parameters. The initial output of hifiasm resulted in the primary assembly (p_utg), representing a mosaic polyploid assembly.

Gene prediction

Repeats were first identified in the S. spontaneum genome assembly with RepeatModeler62 (v2.0.3) to create a custom repeat library. The repeat library was then combined with Viridiplantae repeats from RepBase63 (v20150807) to generate the final repeat library. Repeat masking of each of S. spontaneum genome assembly was performed using the final CRL and RepeatMasker64 (v4.1.2) with the parameters ‘-e ncbi -s -nolow -no_is -gff’. S. spontaneum RNA-seq raw reads were processed using Trimmomatic (v1.0)65 to cut adaptor and remove low-quality reads and then aligned to the S. spontaneum genome assembly using HISAT2 (v2.2.1)66. The aligned RNA-seq reads were each assembled using StringTie (v2.2.1)67. Initial gene models were generated using BRAKER (v3.1)68 using the soft-masked genome assembly and RNA-seq read alignments as hints. High-confidence gene models were identified from the working gene model set by filtering out those without expression evidence, a Pfam domain match or those that were partial gene models or contained an interior stop codon.

Genome quality evaluation

The completeness of conserved genome regions in the assemblies was evaluated by BUSCOs (v5.4.1)43 using 1,614 single-copy orthologs from the ‘embryophyta_odb10’ database. Assembly contiguity was assessed through the LAI44, calculated using LTR_retriever (v2.9.6)69 with default parameters. Briefly, the completeness of LTR retrotransposons was analyzed using LTR_FINDER_parallel (v1.2)70 and LTR_HARVEST_parallel (v1.0)71. These tools were used to identify LTR elements for each genome, which were then compiled into a nonredundant LTR library using LTR_retriever (v2.9.6) for calculating the LAI. For accuracy assessment, the base-level QV was computed using Merqury (v1.4.1)72, based on k-mer analysis.

SNP-based GWAS in potato and sweet potato populations using GWASpoly

GWASs were performed using SNP dosage data, using an LMM to account for population structure and relative kinship. The analysis was carried out using the R package GWASpoly (v2.10)10. Initially, the covariance matrix for the polygenic effect was calculated using the set.K function. After this, the GWASpoly function was used to conduct the GWAS under an ‘additive’ model, where the effect of each SNP was assumed to be proportional to the dosage of the minor allele. Genome-wide significance threshold was calculated based on the Bonferroni correction of αM, where α is 0.05 and M is the number of valid SNPs.

Construction of the S. spontaneum graph-based pangenome

To capture the genetic diversity within S. spontaneum, a graph pangenome was constructed using a collection of 15 monoploid genome assemblies. The graph pangenome was built using Minigraph-Cactus (v2.6.11)73 with default parameters. To ensure the accuracy and reliability of the graph structure, a combination of read mapping and assembled alignments was used to validate the paths, edges and nodes within the S. spontaneum pangenome.

Functional characterization of SsNGA5

Vector construction

To generate the overexpression construct, the full-length CDS of SsNGA5 was amplified through PCR (Supplementary Table 15) using cDNA derived from leaf of S. spontaneum as a template. The plant expression vector pBWA(V)HS-ccdB-GFP was linearized by digestion with BsaI and Eco31I restriction endonucleases. The amplified SsNGA5 fragment was then inserted downstream of the CaMV 35S promoter and upstream of the GFP reporter gene using the Biorun 2× EasyClone seamless cloning kit, resulting in the 35S::SsNGA5-GFP recombinant plasmid.

Rice genetic transformation

The recombinant plasmid was transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain EHA105. Agrobacterium-mediated transformation of rice (ZH11) was performed using a callus-based system. Briefly, sterilized mature rice seeds were placed on induction medium to generate embryonic calli. After 10–15 min of immersion in the Agrobacterium suspension, the calli were transferred to coculture medium for 2 days in the dark. Transgenic calli were subsequently identified through multiple rounds of selection on medium. Actively growing resistant calli were transferred to regeneration and rooting media to obtain T0 plantlets. Positive transformants were further validated by PCR-based screening using genomic DNA extracted from leaves, specifically targeting the SsNGA5 sequence.

Statistical analysis

Author contributions

X.Z. and S.C. conceived and designed this study. S.C. developed the KMERIA method. S.C. and S.Q. implemented the simulated analyses. S.C. performed the real data analyses. S.C. and Y.S. assembled the S. spontaneum genomes and performed the pangenome analyses. K.C. performed the genetic variant analyses. H. Liu, Y.Z., Z.X., X. Li, X. Liu, J.W. and M.Z. provided the sequencing materials of S. spontaneum accessions and collected the phenotypic data. H. Li provided SNP-based genotypes for potato and performed SNP-GWAS analyses using these datasets. G.-B.C. and C.M. performed the population genetic analysis and revised the paper. S.C. and X.Z. wrote the paper with input from all the co-authors.

Data availability

The 14 S. spontaneum genome assemblies and annotations generated in this study have been deposited in the figshare database under DOIs (10.6084/m9.figshare.32251332)74 and (10.6084/m9.figshare.32142925)75. The genome assemblies, annotations and raw PacBio HiFi sequencing reads are also accessible through the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) under BioProject accession PRJNA1393212. The reference genome assembly of S. spontaneum 82–114 was obtained from our published study16. Previously published resequencing datasets analyzed in this study were obtained from NCBI Sequence Read Archive using the following BioProject accessions: potato (PRJNA944441, PRJNA63145, PRJNA766763, PRJNA378971), sweet potato (PRJNA1089346), alfalfa (PRJNA995892), rice (PRJNA623686) and S. spontaneum/hybrid (PRJNA1303125).
