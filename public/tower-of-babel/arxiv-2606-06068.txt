An Elementary Analysis of the Prime Partition Function
Asaf Cohen Antonir, Asaf Shapira

Abstract
Let p ​ p ​ ( n ) pp(n) denote the number of ways to write n n as a sum of primes. In this paper, we show that log ⁡ p ​ p ​ ( n ) ∼ 2 ​ π ​ n 3 ​ log ⁡ n . \log pp(n)\sim 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{n}{3\log n}}\;. 1
Introduction

Let
p(n)
denote the partition function of
n
, namely the number of sequences of positive integers
a_{1}\leq a_{2}\leq\ldots\leq a_{k}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}=n
.
More generally, given
A
a set of positive integers, we let
p_{A}(n)
be the
A
-restricted partition function
, which counts the number of sequences
a_{1}\leq a_{2}\leq\ldots\leq a_{k}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}=n
and also for all
i
we have
a_{i}\in A
.

The study of various properties
of restricted partition functions is amongst the oldest topics in Combinatorics and Number theory. Some classical examples are Euler’s Pentagonal Numbers Theorem and the Rogers–Ramanujan identities (for a more thorough background, see
[
1
,
2
]
).

A very natural property is that of estimating the asymptotic behaviour of
A
-restricted partition function, for different sets
A
.
Arguably, the most well-known result of this type is the celebrated Hardy–Ramanujan
[
8
]
formula (discovered independently by Uspensky
[
16
]
). It states that

p(n)\sim\frac{1}{4n\sqrt{3}}\exp(\pi\sqrt{2n/3})\;.

(1)

Following
[
8
]
, asymptotic estimates for
p_{A}(n)
were obtained for various sets
A
. Examples of this arise already in the work of Hardy and Ramanujan
[
7
]
, where they obtained bounds analogous to (
1
) when
A
is the set of primes, when
A
is the set of odd integers, and when
A
is the set of
d^{th}
powers of positive integers. These restricted partition functions were further studied by many researchers; see e.g.
[
5
,
6
,
19
,
20
,
21
]
.

Many of the proofs of such asymptotic estimations use highly non-trivial arguments, which are lengthy and complex. In contrast to these proofs, using a short and elementary argument Erdős
[
4
]
proved only a slightly weaker version of (
1
), that is

\log p(n)\sim\pi\sqrt{2n/3}\;.

(2)

In this note, we use the ideas from the elegant inductive argument of Erdős, in order to prove estimates similar to (
2
) for various restricted partition functions as well as estimates for the number of ‘plane partitions’.
Although our proofs do not yield the state of the art estimations, the proofs are significantly shorter and yield the correct asymptotic behaviour of the logarithm of the functions under study.
In this note, we will determine the asymptotic behaviour of the logarithm of the number of plane partitions, partitions into
d^{th}
powers, and partitions into primes.

Despite the clear differences in each of the above-mentioned counting functions, our proofs regarding their asymptotic behaviour follow a similar three-step process:

(R1)

Establish a recursive formula for the relevant partition function, akin to the one used by Erdős.

(R2)

Use the recursive formula to show that the partition function in hand is bounded by a sum of the form
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{a\in A}\varphi(a)e^{-\psi(a,k)}
, where
\varphi(x),\psi(x,y)
are some functions (which differ in each case) and
A
is some set of integers.

(R3)

Evaluate these sums — in an elementary way — and conclude the proof.

As will become evident below, all proofs are elementary and closely follow the spirit of Erdős’s proof of (
2
) as described in
[
14
]
.

Partitions into Powers:

We generalize restricted partition function a bit further to be able to discuss the partition function restricted to ‘
d^{th}
powers’ for
d>0
. Indeed, for
\sigma\colon\mathbb{N}_{+}\to\mathbb{N}_{+}
a non-decreasing sequence, we denote the ‘
\sigma
-restricted’ partition function of
n
by
p_{\sigma}(n)
to be the number of sequences
a_{1}\leq a_{2}\leq\ldots\leq a_{k}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sigma(a_{i})=n
.
This is clearly a generalization of
A
-restricted partition functions for infinite
A
, as we can take
\sigma
to enumerate
A
in increasing order.
For every positive
d>0
and a nonnegative integer
n
we denote
p_{d}(n)
to be
p_{\sigma}(n)
, where
\sigma(x)=\lfloor x^{d}\rfloor
.
Note that in the case of integer
d
, the function
p_{d}(n)
is
p_{A}(n)
where
A=\{x^{d}:x\in\mathbb{N}_{+}\}
. The asymptotic estimation we prove for
p_{d}(n)
is the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

For every
d>0
we have the following when
n\to\infty
:

\log p_{d}(n)\sim c_{d}\cdot n^{1/(d+1)}\quad\text{where}\quad c_{d}=(d+1)\left(\frac{1}{d}\cdot\Gamma\left(1+\frac{1}{d}\right)\cdot\zeta\left(1+\frac{1}{d}\right)\right)^{\frac{d}{d+1}}\;.

In the above theorem, as in the rest of the paper,
\Gamma(z)
is the
Gamma function
given by
\Gamma(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{z-1}e^{-t}\,dt
and
\zeta(s)
is the
Euler–Riemann zeta function
given by
\zeta(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{s}}
.

We should point out that a more precise asymptotic formula is known for
p_{d}(n)
. The case of integers
d
is due to
[
5
,
7
]
, and the case of non-integer
d
is more recent and is due to
[
10
,
19
,
20
,
21
]
. However, these estimates rely
on far longer and highly technical proofs.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is simpler when assuming
d
is an integer. Therefore, we first prove the theorem for an integer
d
, and then modify the proof to work also for a non-integer
d
. In both cases (integer
d
or not), we will only prove the upper bound in Theorem
1.1
, as the proof of the lower bound is very similar.

Partitions into Primes:

The main result of this paper is an asymptotic estimation of
\log p_{\mathcal{P}}(n)
where
\mathcal{P}
is the set of prime numbers. For notional convenience we write
pp(n)
instead of
p_{\mathcal{P}}(n)
. Then the asymptotic formula of
\log pp(n)
is given by the following theorem.

Theorem 1.2
.

We have the following when
n\to\infty
:

\log pp(n)\sim 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{n}{3\log n}}\;.

This theorem was first proven by Hardy-Ramanujan
[
7
]
, and a more precise result was shown in
[
17
]
.
The proof of Theorem
1.2
is the longest among the proofs presented in this note, while remaining elementary. The proofs of the upper and lower bounds in Theorem
1.2
are, as before, very similar. However, in contrast to the case of Theorem
1.1
, we provide a fully detailed proof for both bounds to illustrate how one can use our recipe to produce lower bounds.

Plane Partitions:

A very well-studied generalization of the partition function are the
high dimensional partitions
. One special case of interest is
\PL(n)
, the two-dimensional partition function, also known as the plane partition function.
It is defined as the number of arrays of nonnegative integers
(\pi_{i,j})
such that
\sum_{i,j}\pi_{i,j}=n
and such that the rows and columns are non-increasing; that is, for all
k
, the sequences
\pi_{i,k}
and
\pi_{k,i}
are non-increasing in
i
.

For more general partition functions, such as the plane partition function, one can still apply our three-step recipe, while some of the steps may no longer be elementary. We exemplify this with the plane partition function
\PL(n)
, and show the following:

Theorem 1.3
.

We have the following when
n\to\infty
:

\log\PL(n)\sim\left(27\cdot\zeta(3)/4\right)^{1/3}\cdot n^{2/3}\;.

We remark that our proof uses Lemma
2.1
, which establishes
(R1)
for
\PL(n)
. The lemma provides the required recursion for
\PL(n)
and is due to MacMahon
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
(see also
[
9
]
). Unfortunately, the proof of the lemma is not as simple as the proofs of the recursion that we use for the restricted partition functions; in particular, it uses the generating function of
\PL(n)
, which is derived in a non-elementary way. For this reason, we do not provide a proof for this lemma, and only show how to implement
(R2)
and
(R3)
in an elementary way given this lemma to obtain the upper bound in Theorem
1.3
.

We also wish to highlight that a more precise asymptotic formula is known
[
18
]
, although the proof of this formula uses the generating function of the plane partition as well as analytic techniques; for more on this, see
[
15
]
. The current state of the art regarding the asymptotic growth of higher dimensional partitions is only of the order of magnitude of the logarithm of the number of high dimensional partitions
[
3
]
.
The reason for the discrepancy between the one and two dimensional partitions and the higher ones comes from the lack of a ‘nice’ formula for its generating function. For a more thorough discussion on this topic, see
[
1
]
.

The Number of Plane Partitions

As mentioned in the introduction, step
(R1)
requires establishing a recursion formula. In the case of plane partitions, we use the following lemma by MacMahon
[

,
12
,
13
]
:

Lemma 2.1
.

For every positive integer
n
we have

n\cdot\PL(n)=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n/t}t^{2}\cdot\PL(n-tk)\;.

We continue by implementing the rest of the recipe and proving Theorem
1.3
.

First, we note that for every
\delta>0

\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}m^{2}e^{-\delta m}=\frac{e^{-\delta}(e^{-\delta}+1)}{(1-e^{-\delta})^{3}}\leq\frac{2}{\delta^{3}}\;,

(3)

where the equality holds as for all
0<t<1
we have
1
1

1

This follows by differentiating the identity
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}t^{k}=\frac{1}{1-t}
twice.

\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}m^{2}t^{m}=\frac{t(t+1)}{(1-t)^{3}}
, and the inequality holds as for all
x>0
we have
2
2

2

See Lemma
A.1
in the appendix for a short proof.

\frac{e^{-x}(e^{-x}+1)}{(1-e^{-x})^{3}}\leq\frac{2}{x^{3}}
.

Now letting
c=(27\zeta(3)/4)^{1/3}
we prove that for all
n
we have
\PL(n)\leq\exp(c\cdot n^{2/3})
, which yields the upper bound in Theorem
1.3
. We do so by induction on
n
, with the base case trivially holding. Observe the following:

\displaystyle n\cdot\PL(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n/t}t^{2}\cdot\PL(n-tk)\leq\sum_{t=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n/t}t^{2}\cdot e^{c(n-tk)^{2/3}}\leq e^{cn^{2/3}}\cdot\sum_{t=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n/t}t^{2}\cdot e^{-\frac{2ctk}{3n^{1/3}}}\;,

where the first equality is Lemma

(n-x)^{2/3}\leq n^{2/3}-\frac{2x}{3n^{1/3}}
.

To finish the proof, we need to show that the double sum above is at most

.
This follows by letting
\delta(k)=\frac{2ck}{3n^{1/3}}
in (

\sum_{t=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n/t}t^{2}e^{-\delta(k)t}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{2}{\delta(k)^{3}}=\frac{27n}{4c^{3}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k^{3}}=n\;.\qed

We emphasize that in the above proof, (

Partitions into integer Powers

We begin with the proof of step
(R1)
, which is used in the proofs of Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2
as the first step of our recipe.
The following recursion is well known, and was also used implicitly in Erdős’ proof
[
4
]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

For every
\sigma\colon\mathbb{N}_{+}\to\mathbb{N}_{+}
, a non-decreasing sequence, and positive integer
n
we have

n\cdot p_{\sigma}(n)=\sum_{s\in\mathbb{N}}\sigma(s)\sum_{k=1}^{n/\sigma(s)}p_{\sigma}(n-\sigma(s)k)\;.

To see this let
p_{\sigma}(n,s,t)
and
p_{\sigma}^{\prime}(n,s,t)
to be the number of sequences
a_{1}\leq a_{2}\leq\ldots\leq a_{k}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sigma(a_{i})=n
where
s
appears exactly
t
times in
\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{k}\}
and at least
t
times in
\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{k}\}
respectively.
Then by double counting we have

\displaystyle n\cdot p_{\sigma}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\mathbb{N},t\in\mathbb{N}}\sigma(s)\cdot t\cdot p_{\sigma}(n,s,t)=\sum_{s\in\mathbb{N}}\sigma(s)\sum_{t\in\mathbb{N}}t\cdot p_{\sigma}(n,s,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\mathbb{N}}\sigma(s)\sum_{t\in\mathbb{N}}p_{\sigma}^{\prime}(n,s,t)=\sum_{s\in\mathbb{N}}\sigma(s)\sum_{k=1}^{n/\sigma(s)}p_{\sigma}(n-\sigma(s)k)\;.\qed

We now prove the upper bound in Theorem
1.1
for any
integer

d>0
.
We begin by proving inequality (
5
) which is an analogue to inequality (
3
) which we used in the previous section. Let
f_{\delta}(x)=x^{d}e^{-\delta x^{d}}
and note that for fixed
\delta
we have
f^{\prime}_{\delta}(x)=d(1-\delta x^{d})x^{d-1}e^{-\delta x^{d}}
. Hence,
a\coloneqq\frac{1}{\delta^{1/d}}
is the maximal point of
f_{\delta}(x)
. In particular, the function
f_{\delta}(x)
is increasing for all
0\leq x\leq a
, and decreasing otherwise.
Therefore,

\sum_{m=1}^{\lfloor a\rfloor-1}f_{\delta}(m)\leq\int_{0}^{\lfloor a\rfloor}f_{\delta}(x)dx\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{m=\lfloor a\rfloor+2}^{\infty}f_{\delta}(m)\leq\int_{\lfloor a\rfloor}^{\infty}f_{\delta}(x)dx\;.

(4)

We see that the integral
\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{\delta}(x)dx
accounts for
\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}f_{\delta}(m)
except possibly for
f(\lfloor a\rfloor)
and
f(\lfloor a\rfloor+1)
, hence,

\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}f_{\delta}(m)\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{\delta}(x)dx+2f(a)=\frac{1}{d\delta^{1+{1}/{d}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}z^{1/d}e^{-z}dz+\frac{2}{e\delta}=\frac{\Gamma\left(1+{1}/{d}\right)}{d\delta^{1+1/d}}+\frac{2}{e\delta}\;,

(5)

where the inequality holds by (
4
) and as
a
is the maximal point of
f_{\delta}(x)
, and the first equality holds by the substitution
z=\delta x^{d}
which implies that
\frac{z^{1/d-1}}{d\delta^{1/d}}dz=dx
and by noting that
x=\left(\frac{z}{\delta}\right)^{1/d}
.

Now using (
5
), for every
\varepsilon>0
, we prove by induction on
n
that
p_{d}(n)\leq Ke^{(1+\varepsilon)c_{d}n^{1/(d+1)}}
, where
K=K(\varepsilon)
. Note that by choosing
K
large enough, we can assume that the induction assumption holds for all
n\leq n_{0}(\varepsilon,d)
, allowing us to assume in what follows that
n\geq n_{0}(\varepsilon,d)
.
By Lemma
3.1
, the induction hypothesis, and by setting
\delta(k)\coloneqq\frac{(1+\varepsilon)c_{d}k}{(d+1)n^{d/(d+1)}}
we have:

\displaystyle n\cdot p_{d}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{n^{1/d}}m^{d}\sum_{k=1}^{n/m^{d}}p_{d}(n-m^{d}k)\leq\sum_{m=1}^{n^{1/d}}m^{d}\sum_{k=1}^{n/m^{d}}Ke^{(1+\varepsilon)c_{d}(n-m^{d}k)^{1/(d+1)}}

\displaystyle\leq Ke^{(1+\varepsilon)c_{d}n^{1/(d+1)}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}f_{\delta(k)}(m)\;,

(6)

where in the second inequality we used the basic fact
(n-x)^{1/(d+1)}\leq n^{1/(d+1)}-\frac{x}{(d+1)n^{d/(d+1)}}
.

To finish the proof, we now prove that the double sum in (
3
) is bounded from above by
n
. Indeed, letting
\gamma=1-\frac{1}{(1+\varepsilon)^{1+1/d}}>0
, we have the following for large enough
n
:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}f_{\delta(k)}(m)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\leq n}\frac{\Gamma\left(1+{1}/{d}\right)}{d\delta(k)^{1+1/d}}+\frac{2}{e\delta(k)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n}{(1+\varepsilon)^{(d+1)/d}{\zeta\left(1+{1}/{d}\right)}}\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k^{1+1/d}}+\frac{(d+1)n^{d/(d+1)}}{(1+\varepsilon)c_{d}}\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{k}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n}{(1+\varepsilon)^{1+1/d}}+\frac{2(d+1)}{(1+\varepsilon)c_{d}}\cdot n^{d/(d+1)}\log(n)\leq(1-\gamma)n+\gamma n=n\;,

where the first inequality holds by inequality (
5
), the second inequality holds as
\frac{1}{\delta(k)^{1+1/d}}=\frac{n}{(k(1+\varepsilon))^{(d+1)/d}}\cdot\frac{d}{\Gamma\left(1+{1}/{d}\right)\zeta\left(1+{1}/{d}\right)}
, and the last inequality holds by the choice of
\gamma
and provided
n
is large enough. ∎

4
The general case: Partitions into positive Powers

In this section we prove the upper bound in Theorem
1.1
in full generality.
The proof starts similarly to the case of integer
d
, and we will make use of (
5
), which was proved in the previous section.

Indeed, for every
\varepsilon>0
, we prove by induction on
n
that
p_{d}(n)\leq Ke^{(1+\varepsilon)c_{d}n^{1/(d+1)}}
, where
K=K(\varepsilon)
. Note that by choosing
K
large enough, we can assume that the induction assumption holds for all
n\leq n_{0}(\varepsilon,d)
, allowing us to assume in what follows that
n\geq n_{0}(\varepsilon,d)
.
The following holds due to Lemma
3.1
, the induction hypothesis, and by setting
\delta(k)\coloneqq\frac{(1+\varepsilon)c_{d}k}{(d+1)n^{d/(d+1)}}
:

\displaystyle n\cdot p_{d}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{(n+1)^{1/d}-1}\lfloor m^{d}\rfloor\sum_{k=1}^{n/\lfloor m^{d}\rfloor}p_{d}(n-\lfloor m^{d}\rfloor k)\leq\sum_{m=1}^{(n+1)^{1/d}-1}m^{d}\sum_{k=1}^{n/\lfloor m^{d}\rfloor}Ke^{(1+\varepsilon)c_{d}(n-\lfloor m^{d}\rfloor k)^{1/(d+1)}}

\displaystyle\leq Ke^{(1+\varepsilon)c_{d}n^{1/(d+1)}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}m^{d}e^{-\delta(k)\lfloor m^{d}\rfloor}\;,

where in the second inequality we used the basic fact
(n-x)^{1/(d+1)}\leq n^{1/(d+1)}-\frac{x}{(d+1)n^{d/(d+1)}}
.

To finish the proof, we need to show that the double sum above is at most
n
.
Clearly, the double sum above is at most
A+B
where

A=\sum_{k=1}^{\log^{2}(n)}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}m^{d}e^{-\delta(k)(m^{d}-1)}\quad\text{and}\quad B=\sum_{k=\log^{2}(n)}^{n}\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}m^{d}e^{-\delta(k)(m^{d}-1)}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}e^{-\delta(k)}\;.

Bounding
A
:

Let
\gamma>0
be such that
\left(\frac{1+\gamma\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}\right)^{1+1/d}\leq(1-\varepsilon)
. By inequality (
5
) there is a
\delta_{0}=\delta_{0}(\gamma,\varepsilon,d)
so that
\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}m^{d}e^{-\delta m^{d}}\leq(1+\gamma\varepsilon)d^{-1}\Gamma(1+1/d)/\delta^{1+1/d}
holds for every
\delta<\delta_{0}
. Assume
n
is large enough so that
\delta(k)<\delta_{0}
for all
k\leq\log^{2}(n)
. Then,

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle\leq e^{\delta(\log^{2}(n))}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(1+\gamma\varepsilon)\Gamma(1+1/d)}{d\delta(k)^{1+1/d}}\leq\frac{(1+\gamma\varepsilon)^{1+1/d}n}{(1+\varepsilon)^{1+1/d}\zeta(1+1/d)}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k^{1+1/d}}\leq\left(1-\varepsilon\right)n\;,

The first inequality holds by (
5
) with
\delta=\delta(k)~~(<\delta_{0})
, and the second inequality holds by the definition of
\delta(k),c_{d}
and as
e^{\delta(\log^{2}(n))}\leq(1+\gamma\varepsilon)^{1/d}
provided
n
is large enough.

Bounding
B
:

Since
A\leq(1-\varepsilon)n
it remains to prove that
B\leq\varepsilon n
. Indeed, let
\eta=\eta(d)
be such that for all
m\geq 2
we have
m^{d}-1\geq\eta m^{d}
. Then,

\displaystyle B\leq\sum_{k=\log^{2}(n)}^{n}\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}m^{d}e^{-\eta\delta(k)m^{d}}+\frac{1}{1-e^{-\delta(1)}}\leq\sum_{k=\log^{2}(n)}^{\infty}\frac{C_{1}\cdot n}{k^{1+1/d}}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{C_{2}n^{d/(d+1)}}{k}+\frac{\varepsilon n}{3}\leq\varepsilon n\;,

where the first inequality holds by the definition of
\eta
and as
\sum_{k=1}^{n}e^{-\delta(k)}
is a geometric series, the second inequality holds by (
5
) with
\delta=\eta\delta(k)
, where
C_{1},C_{2}
are constants depending on
d,\varepsilon
, and by assuming
n
is large enough so that
(1-e^{\delta(1)})^{-1}\leq\varepsilon n/3
; the third inequality holds provided
n
is large enough so that
\sum_{k=\log^{2}(n)}^{\infty}1/k^{1+1/d}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{3C_{1}}
and
C_{2}n^{d/(d+1)}\log(n)\leq\frac{\varepsilon n}{6}
. ∎

Proof of the upper bound in Theorem
1.2

As before, we will need an evaluation of an ‘exponential sum’; this time it will run over all primes. We will state this as a Lemma
5.1
and postpone its proof for after the proof of the upper bound in Theorem
1.2
. We also remark that in this section we only use the upper bound in the following lemma, and the lower bound is used in the following section.

Lemma 5.1
.

We have
\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}pe^{-\delta p}\sim{\delta^{-2}}/{\log(1/\delta)}
as
\delta\to 0
.

To prove the theorem, we will also need the following elementary inequality.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
f(x)=\sqrt{x/\log(10x)}
. Then, for every
m\geq 1
and
0\leq x\leq m-1
we have:

f(m-x)\leq f(m)-xf^{\prime}(m)\;.

For all
m>100
and
0\leq x\leq m/10
we have

f(m-x)\geq f(m)-xf^{\prime}(m)+x^{2}f^{\prime\prime}(m)\;.

By Lagrange’s remainder theorem, for every
m>1
and
x
such that
0<x\leq m
there is
m-x\leq c\leq m
such that

\displaystyle f(m-x)

\displaystyle=f(m)-xf^{\prime}(m)+x^{2}f^{\prime\prime}(c)/2\;.

Noting that
f^{\prime\prime}(z)=\frac{3-\log^{2}(10z)}{4z^{3/2}\log^{5/2}(10z)}
is negative for all
z>e^{\sqrt{3}}/10\approx 0.565
we obtain the first part of the lemma for
m>e^{\sqrt{3}}/10
and
x>0
with
m-x>e^{\sqrt{3}}/10
.

For the second part of the lemma, for every
m
and

Appendix A
Some Elementary inequalities

Lemma A.1
.

For all
x>0
we have

\frac{e^{x}}{(e^{x}-1)^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{x^{2}}\;,

(10)

and

\frac{e^{-x}(e^{-x}+1)}{(1-e^{-x})^{3}}\leq\frac{2}{x^{3}}\;,

(11)

and for all
0<x<1
we have

\frac{e^{-x}}{(1-e^{-x})^{4}}=\frac{e^{3x}}{(e^{x}-1)^{4}}\leq\frac{3}{x^{4}}\;.

(12)

The proofs of all inequalities are similar; we expand
e^{x}
into its power series, and then compare the coefficients on both
sides of the inequality. Inequality (
10
) is equivalent to

x^{2}e^{x}\leq e^{2x}-2e^{x}+1.

By expanding each side to its power series, it suffices to show that

\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{x^{n}}{(n-2)!}\leq\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{2^{n}-2}{n!}x^{n}\;.

To see this, note that the coefficient of
x^{n}
on the left-hand side is always at most the coefficient of
x^{n}
on the right-hand side for all
n\geq 2
as clearly
n(n-1)\leq 2^{n}-2
for all
n\geq 2
.

To prove (
11
) note that it is equivalent to

x^{3}(e^{x}+e^{2x})\leq 2e^{3x}-6e^{2x}+6e^{x}-2\;.

Replacing
e^{x}
with its power series expansion, this is equivalent to

\sum_{n=3}^{\infty}\frac{1+2^{n-3}}{(n-3)!}x^{n}\leq\sum_{n=3}^{\infty}\frac{2\cdot 3^{n}-6\cdot 2^{n}+6}{n!}x^{n}\;.

To establish this inequality for all
x>0
, we claim that the coefficient of
x^{n}
in the right-hand series is at least as large as the coefficient of
x^{n}
in the left-hand side. To prove this, we will show that for all
n\geq 3
we have

n(n-1)(n-2)(1+2^{n}/8)+6\cdot 2^{n}\leq 2\cdot 3^{n}\;.

First, for
n\geq 20
we indeed have the above inequality as

n(n-1)(n-2)(1+2^{n-3})+6\cdot 2^{n}\leq\binom{n}{5}{2^{n-4}}+\binom{n}{2}2^{n-1}+n2^{n}+2^{n+1}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{n-k}2^{k+1}=2\cdot 3^{n}\;,

where in the first inequality we used
6\cdot 2^{n}\leq n2^{n}+2^{n+1}
, which holds for all
n\geq 4
, and
n(n-1)(n-2)\leq\binom{n}{2}2^{n-1}
which holds for all
n
, and also
n(n-1)(n-2)\frac{2^{n}}{8}\leq\binom{n}{5}\frac{2^{n}}{16}
which holds for all
n\geq 20
.
The first two inequalities we mentioned above are straightforward to prove, the third is equivalent to
(n-3)(n-4)\geq 240
, which holds for all
n\geq 20
as then we have
(n-3)(n-4)\geq(n-4)^{2}\geq 16^{2}>240
.
For
n\leq 19
, one can verify that the coefficient of
x^{n}
in the right-hand series is at least the coefficient of
x^{n}
in the left-hand side series.

Lastly, we prove (
12
). Clearly, it is enough to prove that
\frac{e^{2x}}{(e^{x}-1)^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{x^{2}}+\frac{1}{x}+1
, as then using inequality (
10
), we are done.
The above is equivalent to proving that

2x^{2}e^{x}\leq(1+x)(e^{2x}-2e^{x}+1)+x^{2}.

By expanding each side to its power series, we wish to show that

\sum_{n=4}^{\infty}\frac{2n(n-1)}{n!}x^{n}\leq\sum_{n=4}^{\infty}\frac{2^{n}-2+n(2^{n-1}-2)}{n!}x^{n}\;.

As in previous cases, this holds since the coefficient of
x^{n}
on the left-hand side is at most as large as the coefficient of
x^{n}
on the right-hand side.
∎
