Third ventricle non-Hodgkin lymphoma: An unusual presentation of primary central nervous system lymphoma
Authors: Nivetha Uthayasuriyan, Sri Rahul Gowthaman, Jeyaselva Senthilkumar Pachiyappan, Anand C Damodaran, Mohamed Naleer Hameed Ali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPrimary central nervous system lymphoma (PCNSL) is a rare extra-nodal non-Hodgkin lymphoma that most commonly involves the cerebral parenchyma. Isolated intraventricular involvement is uncommon, and primary localisation to the third ventricle is exceedingly rare, posing significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges for clinicians.Case reportA 58-year-old immunocompetent man presented with progressive headache, vomiting, and cognitive decline. Neuroimaging revealed a well-defined, homogeneously enhancing lesion involving the foramen of Monro and the roof of the third ventricle, with associated ventricular obstruction. Differential diagnoses included colloid cyst, central neurocytoma, and glioma. An interhemispheric transcallosal approach was used for the biopsy. Histopathology and immunohistochemistry confirmed diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (CD20 positive, CD45 positive, and Ki-67 index 55–60%). The patient had a stormy postoperative period complicated by dyselectrolytemia and pulmonary embolism and succumbed despite aggressive supportive management.ConclusionPrimary CNS lymphoma presenting as an isolated third ventricular lesion is extremely rare and often radiologically mimics other intraventricular tumours. Histopathological confirmation is mandatory before initiating oncological therapy. Surgical intervention should be limited to biopsy if lymphoma is highly suspected, with cerebrospinal fluid diversion when required. Awareness of this rare tumour is essential to avoid unnecessary aggressive resections and to facilitate early initiation of chemotherapy.Keywords: Primary CNS lymphoma, Third ventricle, Intraventricular tumour, Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, Transcallosal approach

Introduction

Primary central nervous system lymphoma (PCNSL) is a rare presentation of extranodal non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma, accounting for approximately 1–3% of all primary brain tumours. PCNSL may involve the brain parenchyma, leptomeninges, spinal cord, or eyes without systemic disease outside the central nervous system [1]. PCNSL was previously reported to be more common in immunocompromised individuals but has recently been reported in immunocompetent patients as well [2]. Although the etiopathogenesis in immunocompetent individuals is not well established, congenital or acquired immunodeficiency remains the only established risk factor. HIV infection increases the risk of developing PCNSL approximately 3600-fold compared to the general population [3]. Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) accounts for approximately 95% of all PCNSL cases; the remaining 5% includes T-cell, Burkitt, mantle zone, and lymphoblastic lymphomas [3, 4].

Various mechanisms, such as gene mutations and dysregulation of receptor signalling pathways, contribute to PCNSL pathogenesis. Studies have shown improved survival rates in patients with BCL-6 expression in systemic diffuse B-cell lymphoma [5, 6]. PCNSL is considered a “whole-brain” disease, making curative resection generally unfeasible. Angiotropism and angiocentricity are well-established histological features of PCNSL, possibly explaining blood-brain barrier disruption [7]. PCNSL prognosis is poor, with a 3-year survival rate of 60–80%. Treatment depends on the patient’s general status and age. Since PCNSL is sensitive to radiotherapy and chemotherapy, a multimodal approach is recommended [8]. This case report describes a middle-aged man with vague neurological symptoms diagnosed with non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma of the third ventricle.

Case report

A 58-year-old man with no known comorbidities presented to the emergency department with an occasional headache and vomiting over the past month, progressively worsening with coughing and straining. He also experienced decreased speech and memory disturbances. The headache was unresponsive to medications and not posture-dependent. Examination revealed drowsiness, disorientation, and mild left hemiparesis. Haematological tests were normal, and HIV serology was negative. Mild biochemical abnormalities were managed accordingly.

MRI performed at an outside institution demonstrated a 1.5 × 1.5 cm well-circumscribed lesion involving the roof of the third ventricle and foramen of Monro. The lesion appeared hypointense to isointense on T1-weighted imaging, demonstrated relatively low to intermediate signal intensity on FLAIR/T2-weighted sequences, and showed intense homogeneous post-contrast enhancement (Fig. 1). Mild dilatation of the lateral ventricles was present secondary to obstruction of cerebrospinal fluid flow. Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) sequences and apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) maps from the outside institution were not available for review; therefore, diffusion characteristics and quantitative ADC values could not be assessed.

A brain CT scan showed a well-defined hyperdense lesion measuring 2.1 × 1.9 × 1.9 cm, likely originating from the foramen of Monro and extending into the anterior wall of the third ventricle, abutting the mammillary body posteroinferiorly (Fig. 2). Another hyperdense lesion measuring 7 × 4 mm was noted in the subependymal region of the left lateral ventricle. Differential diagnoses included primary CNS lymphoma, glioma, and central neurocytoma.

A non-contrast CT scan showing a well-defined hyperdense lesion involving the foramen of Monro and roof of the third ventricle causing ventricular obstruction

The patient underwent surgery after stabilisation and correction of electrolyte disturbances.The tumour was approached via an interhemispheric transcallosal approach. Intraoperatively, an ill-defined, greyish-brown, moderately vascular lesion infiltrating the foramen of Monro and roof of the third ventricle was observed. Due to a poor tumour-ependymal interface, complete resection was not attempted. A biopsy sample was obtained for histopathology. Postoperatively, the patient was intubated and managed with intravenous antibiotics, anti-oedema, and antiepileptic medications. Post-op CT showed operative changes with minimal thalamic bleeding and no hydrocephalus.

Histopathology and immunohistochemistry indicated non-Hodgkin lymphoma of B-cell origin; subtyping was advised (Fig. 3).

Histopathology showing sheets of atypical lymphoid cells with benign glial tissue (H&E stain). a Benign glial tissue and intermittent lymphoid cell sheets, (b) Lymphoid cell invasion into glial tissue (fibrillary background). Blue arrows indicate tumour cells

Immunohistochemical typing showed CD45 positivity, CD3 negativity, CD20 diffuse positivity, and a Ki-67 index of 55–60% (Fig. 4).

Immunohistochemistry of tumour tissue: (a) CD20 positive, indicating B-cell lineage; (b) CD45 diffuse positivity, confirming lymphoid origin; (c) CD3 negative, excluding T-cell lineage; (d) Ki-67 high proliferative index (~ 55–60%). Blue arrows indicate tumour regions

On postoperative day (POD) 6, the patient was weaned off ventilation and extubated. His Glasgow Coma Scale improved to E4V1M6 but with left-sided weakness. Through physiotherapy, he was gradually mobilised. Pooling of oral secretions was noted; fibre-optic laryngoscopy showed normal vocal cord function and cough reflex. Persistent hyponatraemia from POD 11 was managed with 3% saline under nephrology guidance. Follow-up CT showed resolving haemorrhages with no new findings.

On POD 11, the patient developed sudden tachypnea, tachycardia, hypotension, and worsening sensorium. He was reintubated, ventilated, and started on inotropes. CTPA confirmed pulmonary embolism. Despite anticoagulation, the patient deteriorated and succumbed.

Discussion

PCNSLs are rare tumors, representing 0.7–0.9% of all lymphomas and 0.3–1.5% of intracranial tumors. They occur in both immunocompromised and immunocompetent patients, with a male predominance of 3:2 [9, 10]. Symptoms of increased intracranial pressure, such as headaches, are more common than seizures [11]. Third ventricular involvement is less frequent than lateral or fourth ventricle involvement. PCNSL tends to progress rapidly compared to other intraventricular tumours. Brain edema and multifocality are more common in PCNSL, often causing hydrocephalus [12, 13].

The hyperdense appearance of the lesion on non-contrast CT was an important radiological clue, as hyperdensity is a characteristic feature of primary CNS lymphoma due to its high cellularity. When combined with homogeneous contrast enhancement on MRI, these findings may raise a strong preoperative suspicion for lymphoma. However, histopathological confirmation remains the gold standard for diagnosis, particularly in lesions with atypical intraventricular localization [13–15].

Despite advances in neuroimaging, preoperative diagnosis of intraventricular PCNSL remains difficult due to rarity and imaging overlap with other tumours [14, 16]. The differential diagnosis of a third ventricular mass in this patient included colloid cyst, central neurocytoma, subependymal giant cell astrocytoma (SEGA), chordoid glioma, and primary CNS lymphoma. Colloid cysts commonly arise at the foramen of Monro and frequently present with obstructive hydrocephalus; however, they are typically cystic lesions with absent or minimal enhancement. Central neurocytomas generally occur in younger adults and often demonstrate calcification, cystic degeneration, and a heterogeneous “bubbly” appearance on MRI [17].

SEGAs are strongly associated with tuberous sclerosis complex and predominantly affect children and young adults. Chordoid gliomas characteristically arise from the anterior third ventricle in middle-aged adults and are usually hyperintense on T2-weighted imaging, often accompanied by hypothalamic or endocrine dysfunction. In contrast, primary CNS lymphoma typically demonstrates homogeneous enhancement, hyperdensity on CT, and relatively low-to-intermediate T2 signal intensity due to its hypercellular nature. Although these imaging features may raise suspicion for lymphoma, substantial overlap exists among intraventricular lesions, making histopathological confirmation essential for definitive diagnosis [13, 14, 16–19].

Homogeneous contrast enhancement and diffusion restriction on MRI suggest lymphoma but are not specific, especially in isolated third ventricular lesions [14]. Recent multiparametric MRI studies have shown that PCNSL typically demonstrates intense homogeneous enhancement and low ADC values reflecting high tumour cellularity, features that may aid preoperative differentiation from other intraventricular lesions; however, ADC analysis could not be performed in the present case because DWI and ADC sequences from the outside institution were unavailable for review [15]. In addition to neuroimaging, cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers such as CXCL13 and interleukin-10 have demonstrated high specificity for the diagnosis of primary CNS lymphoma and may provide useful adjunctive diagnostic information in selected patients when tissue diagnosis is challenging [20].

While stereotactic and endoscopic biopsies are generally preferred for the diagnosis of intraventricular lesions and primary CNS lymphoma because of their minimally invasive nature, direct microsurgical approaches may provide larger tissue samples and permit maximal safe tumour resection when feasible [9, 12, 21]. Accordingly, the lesion was approached through an interhemispheric transcallosal route with the intention of maximal safe excision and histopathological confirmation. Intraoperatively, the absence of a clear tumour–ependymal interface precluded safe resection, and biopsy alone was performed. Endoscopic techniques were not pursued because of institutional logistical and financial constraints.

Surgery’s role is limited, as extensive resection does not improve survival [3, 8]. Surgery is mainly for diagnosis or managing complications like obstructive hydrocephalus [1, 12]. CSF diversion via shunting or endoscopic third ventriculostomy may be needed before oncological treatment [12, 14]. Due to the third ventricle’s deep midline location and proximity to vital structures, aggressive excision is discouraged [3, 9].

Steroid responsiveness is a hallmark of PCNSL, but pre-biopsy corticosteroids can cause transient tumour regression and non-diagnostic biopsies. Steroids should be withheld until histological confirmation unless urgently needed for life-threatening intracranial hypertension [14, 21]. This is especially relevant in ventricular PCNSL, where hydrocephalus symptoms require early management [12].

High-dose methotrexate-based chemotherapy is the cornerstone of treatment and improves survival [3, 22]. Combination regimens with cytarabine and rituximab show enhanced responses in immunocompetent patients [7, 22]. Whole-brain radiotherapy is reserved for refractory or relapsed disease or consolidation due to neurocognitive toxicity concerns [22, 23]. Early diagnosis and prompt chemotherapy are key to a favourable prognosis [22, 24].

Only a limited number of primary CNS lymphomas arising within the third ventricle have been reported in the literature [9, 11, 12]. Similar to previously described cases, our patient was an immunocompetent middle-aged adult who presented with symptoms of raised intracranial pressure, and cognitive decline, secondary to obstruction of cerebrospinal fluid pathways. Radiologically, the lesion demonstrated homogeneous enhancement and was initially considered among the differential diagnoses of other third ventricular tumours, reflecting the diagnostic challenge consistently reported in the literature. However, unlike most previously reported cases in which diagnosis was followed by successful initiation of high-dose methotrexate-based chemotherapy with favourable outcomes, our patient experienced a complicated postoperative course ultimately resulting in mortality before commencing definitive oncological treatment. This case therefore highlights both the diagnostic difficulty of isolated third ventricular PCNSL and the significant impact that perioperative complications may have on overall outcome.

The exceptional rarity of third ventricular PCNSL remains poorly understood. One possible explanation is the relative paucity of neural parenchyma within the ventricular system compared with the cerebral hemispheres, where PCNSL most commonly arises. Recent studies have highlighted the role of glymphatic and meningeal lymphatic pathways in CNS immune surveillance and cellular trafficking, raising the possibility that regional differences in these pathways may influence the distribution of CNS lymphomas. However, the relationship between glymphatic pathways and ventricular PCNSL remains speculative and requires further investigation [25–28].

Due to the rarity of isolated third ventricular PCNSLs, continued reporting is essential to improve understanding, diagnostics, and management [12, 16].

Conclusion

Primary CNS lymphoma presenting as an isolated third ventricular lesion is exceedingly rare and diagnostically and therapeutically challenging. Its nonspecific clinical and radiological features require a high index of suspicion even in immunocompetent patients. Histopathological confirmation is mandatory. Surgical intervention should be limited to biopsy or CSF diversion when indicated. Early diagnosis and timely initiation of high-dose methotrexate-based chemotherapy are crucial for improving outcomes. Reporting such rare cases enriches the literature and aids refinement of diagnostic and management strategies.
