The relationship between depressive symptoms and instrumental activities of daily living among older adults in China and its associations with age, sex, and outdoor activity engagement
Authors: Yu Dong, Jinghong Huang, Yilin Wang, Taiyue Zhang, Xinyu Cai, Runguo Gao, Hongqing Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough the association between depressive symptoms and instrumental activities of daily living (IADL) in older Chinese adults has been documented, whether this association varies by demographic characteristics and outdoor activity engagement remains unclear. This study aimed to explore the association of depressive symptoms with IADL and investigate whether age, sex, and outdoor activity modify the association.MethodsThis population-based cross-sectional study derived data from the 2017–2018 Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (2017–2018 CLHLS). Depressive symptoms was measured using the 10-item Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale (CESD-10). The presence of depressive symptoms was defined using the CESD-10 cut-off score (≥ 10). Secondary analyses additionally categorized CESD-10 scores into quintiles to examine dose-response associations with IADL disability. IADL was assessed using eight items, including (1) visiting neighbors, (2) shopping, (3) cooking, (4) washing clothes, (5) walking one kilometer, (6) carrying five-kilogram weight, (7) crouching and standing three times, and (8) taking public transportation. Multivariate linear regression models were used to calculate β-coefficients and 95% CI to examine the association of depressive symptoms with IADL. Modification of the associations by age (65–79 years and ≥ 80 years), sex (male and female), and outdoor activity engagement (including socializing with friends, Tai Chi, and square dancing; yes and no) were performed using stratified analyses and interaction tests.ResultsOf the 12,261 participants (59.91% aged ≥ 80 years and 53.45% women), 26.65% had depressive symptoms. Participants with depressive symptoms (CESD-10 ≥ 10) had greater IADL disability than those without depressive symptoms (adjusted β = 0.81, 95% CI = 0.71, 0.92). In secondary analyses, compared with participants in quintile 1 of depressive symptoms, those in quintile 5 had greater IADL disability (adjusted β = 1.19, 95% CI = 1.06, 1.33). The association between depressive symptoms and IADL disability was significantly modified by age, sex, and outdoor activity engagement (all P for interaction < 0.05), stronger associations observed among participants aged ≥ 80 years, men, and those without outdoor activity engagement.ConclusionsIn this cross-sectional study in China, exposure to depressive symptoms was associated with IADL disability. This association was modified by age, sex, and outdoor activity engagement. These findings indicate a need to prevent depressive symptoms and a need for an intervention strategy for mental health to reduce the potential risk of IADL disability. Moreover, emphasizing personalized interventions that target individuals with depressive symptoms exposure may minimize the burden of IADL disability in later life.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-07856-2.Keywords: Depressive symptoms, Age, Sex, Outdoor activity engagement, IADL, CLHLS

Introduction

Functional disability is a prevalent issue among older adults, posing a significant public health challenge and contributing substantially to the global disease burden [1, 2]. It affects over one billion people worldwide [3], including more than 50 million older adults in China [2]. With the population ageing, functional disability has become an urgent concern due to its association with increased healthcare costs [4], sarcopenia [5], cognitive impairment [6], higher mortality [7], and other ageing-related health conditions [2]. Identifying modifiable factors for functional disability is therefore essential for mitigating its impact.

Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL) disability typically precedes Activities of Daily Living (ADL) disability and serves as an early indicator of functional disability [8]. Compared to ADL, IADL function requires greater executive function and motor skills, encompassing complex cognitive and physical activities such as financial management, shopping, and transportation use [9]. Furthermore, IADL disability predicts future ADL disability, making it a critical target for early interventions aimed at delaying or preventing overall functional impairment [10]. Moreover, the association between depressive symptoms and functional disability remains consistent in longitudinal assessment of the Chinese population. A longitudinal study of 5,275 Chinese adults aged 50 and older found that baseline depressive symptoms significantly predicted ADL and IADL disability over a seven-year follow-up [11]. Similarly, a prospective cohort study of 2713 US Chinese adults aged 60 and older reported that baseline depressive symptoms was associated with a higher risk of both ADL and IADL disability at 2-year follow-up [12]. Moreover, a meta-analysis and systematic review of cohort studies also reported depressive symptoms associated with poorer abilities to perform IADL [13].

Depressive symptoms fluctuates over time and may follow different trajectories, including persistently low, increasing, declining, and persistently high pattern [14], suggesting that depressive symptoms may represent a potentially modifiable condition. The incident of late-life depressive symptoms are steadily rising [15]. In China, more than half of adults aged 65 and older may experience depressive symptoms [16]. Previous studies have shown that depressive symptoms in later life are associated with reduced quality of life [17], social isolation [18], falls [19], and pain [20]. These relationship may also be interrelated and potentially suggests that older age and being women are associated with a higher risk of depressive symptoms, potentially due to age-related physiological changes and differences in social roles and biological susceptibility [21]. A potential strategy to mitigate the association between depressive symptoms and increased IADL disability risk is promoting outdoor activity engagement among older adults. In the present study, outdoor activity engagement primarily reflected community-based outdoor participation, including activities such as socializing with friends, Tai Chi, and square dancing, which combine social interaction, behavioural engagement, and outdoor participation in later life [22]. Studies have shown that individuals with depressive symptoms may reduce motivation and participation in community-based outdoor activities among older adults [23, 24]. Meanwhile, social isolation and loneliness are key risk factors for both depressive symptoms and IADL disability [23, 25]. Additionally, participation in physical and cultural outdoor activities has been linked to a lower risk of IADL disability [26, 27]. Nevertheless, associations between depressive symptoms, age, sex, outdoor activity engagement, and IADL in later life have been underexplored. Therefore, recognizing the effects of age, sex, and outdoor activity engagement in the relationship between depressive symptoms and IADL is essential for developing effective intervention strategies.

In this study, we collected information among Chinese older adults using data from the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS), to explore whether there were associations between depressive symptoms and IADL. Stratified analyses and tests for interaction were performed to investigate further whether associations were modified by age, sex, and outdoor activity engagement.

Methods

Study design and participants

In this study, data were obtained from the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS), the earliest and longest-conducted aging survey in China, organized and conducted by the Center for Healthy Aging and Development Studies (CHADS) at Peking University. CLHLS has conducted eight national surveys in 1998 (baseline), 2000, 2002, 2005, 2008–2009, 2011–2012, 2014, 2017–2018. Detailed information about the study design and sampling methods has been previously documented [28, 29]. This cross-sectional study was a secondary analysis of data from the CLHLS 2017–2018 wave. The following exclusion criteria were applied: (1) age < 65 years old (n = 95), (2) missing data on IADL (n = 252), and (3) missing data on depressive symptoms (n = 3,266). Finally, the final cross-sectional analytical sample size was 12,261 (Fig. 1). All respondents provided written informed consent before the face-to-face interviews. This manuscript was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines, and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Assessment of depressive symptoms

Depressive symptoms was assessed using the CESD-10 scale [30], which has demonstrated excellent validity and reliability in the CLHLS [16, 28]. The scale comprises ten items, including (1) bothered by little things, (2) had trouble concentrating, (3) felt depressed, (4) everything was an effort, (5) felt hopeless, (6) felt fearful, (7) felt unhappy, (8) felt lonely, (9) could not get going, and (10) sleep was restless. Each item was scored on a four-point scale: 0 = rarely or seldom, 1 = sometimes, 2 = Often, 3 = Always. Before calculating the total CESD-10 score, items 5 and 7 were reverse-coded. The total CESD-10 score ranges from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating greater depressive symptoms severity [31]. A total score of ≥ 10 was considered indicative of having depressive symptoms, otherwise no [31]. In this study, the CESD-10 scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.740).

Assessment of instrumental activities of daily living

IADL are essential for assessing advanced skills required for independent living in the community. The IADL scale consists of eight items: (1) visiting neighbors, (2) shopping, (3) cooking, (4) washing clothes, (5) walking one kilometer, (6) carrying five-kilogram weight, (7) crouching and standing three times, and (8) taking public transportation [32]. Each item was scored as 0 (no difficulty) and 1 (a little difficult or unable to do so). Responses of “a little difficult” and “unable to do” were combined to indicate the presence of functional limitation in a given IADL domain, consistent with previous studies using the CLHLS dataset [29]. Each IADL item was equally weighted to reflect cumulative functional burden, consistent with previous epidemiological study using the CLHLS dataset [29]. Although individual IADL items may vary in specificity and difficulty, equal weighting was applied to construct an overall summary measure of functional limitation across multiple domains. The total number of limitations ranges from 0 to 8. The IADL scale demonstrated excellent reliability and validity in older Chinese adults [33]. In this study, IADL disability was considered to be a continuous variable. In this study, the IADL scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.927).

Assessment of covariates

In this study, covariates included age, sex, years of schooling, marital status, smoking, drinking, outdoor activity engagement, number of chronic diseases, and body mass index. Age was categorized as 65–79 years and ≥ 80 years. Sex was classified as male and female. years of schooling were grouped into ≤ 6 years and > 6 years. Marital status was categorized as married and others (separated, divorced, widowed, and never married). Smoking and drinking status were divided into three groups: never, former, and current. Outdoor activity engagement was assessed based on participation in three activities: socializing with friends, Tai Chi, and square dancing. Each activity was scored as 0 (never participated) and 1 (participated), with participation defined as almost every day, at least once per week, at least once per month, or sometimes. In this study, we focused on three outdoor activity engagement measures available in the CLHLS questionnaire: socializing with friends, Tai Chi, and square dancing. These activities were selected because they are commonly practiced among older Chinese adults, have been used in previous CLHLS-based studies, and represent common forms of community-based outdoor participation in later life [22]. Although socializing with friends, Tai Chi, and square dancing involve distinct behavioral and social components, they share several common characteristics, including social interaction, behavioral engagement, and participation in outdoor environments. Therefore, they were combined to represent a broader construct of outdoor activity engagement. Number of chronic diseases for each participant was categorized into 0, 1, and ≥ 2 (chronic diseases included hypertension, diabetes, heart diseases, stroke, arthritis, dementia, dyslipidemia, rheumatism, and chronic nephritis). The number of chronic diseases was categorized as 0, 1, and ≥ 2 to distinguish between no morbidity, single morbidity, and multimorbidity, consistent with commonly used approaches in geriatric epidemiology [34]. This categorization was used to improve the interpretability of disease burden among older adults. Body mass index was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height (m2) [35], and was further classified into four groups: underweight (< 18.5), normal (18.5–23.9), overweight (24-27.9), and obesity (≥ 28). BMI categories were defined according to the Chinese adult BMI classification criteria, which are widely used in previous CLHLS-based studies [36, 37]. This categorization was used to improve the clinical interpretability of weight status among older adults.

Statistical analyses

Participants’ characteristics for the total sample and stratified by depressive symptoms status were summarized using frequency (percentage). Differences between with and without depressive symptoms were compared using the Chi-square test. Multivariate linear regression models were used to calculate β-coefficients and 95% CI to examine the association between depressive symptoms and IADL and its associations with age, sex, and outdoor activity engagement.

Although the IADL score represents a bounded count of functional limitations (range: 0–8), it was analyzed as a continuous outcome because the primary objective was to estimate mean differences in overall functional disability according to depressive symptoms status. Linear regression provides directly interpretable estimates of average differences in the number of IADL limitations and, in large population-based samples, is generally considered robust to moderate departures from normality. Given the large sample size of the present study (n = 12,261), we considered linear regression an appropriate approach for examining population-average associations.

In the primary analyses, depressive symptoms were categorized using the established CESD-10 cut-off score (≥ 10), which is widely used to identify clinically meaningful depressive symptoms among older adults and improves the interpretability of findings in clinical and public health contexts. This classification was used to examine the association between depressive symptoms and IADL and its modification by age, sex, and outdoor activity engagement. In secondary analyses, CESD-10 scores were further categorized into quintiles (with quintile 1 as the reference group) to explore potential dose-response association between depressive symptoms severity and IADL. A trend analysis was conducted by assigning the median value of each depressive symptoms quintile to its respective group and incorporating these reassigned values into the model as continuous variables.

Two models were constructed: Model 1 was the unadjusted model, and Model 2 was the adjusted model, adjusted age, sex, years of schooling, marital status, smoking, drinking, outdoor activity engagement, number of chronic diseases, and body mass index.

Meanwhile, these associations were stratified by age (65–79 years and ≥ 80 years), sex (male and female), and outdoor activity engagement (no and yes). Likelihood ratio tests were performed to compare models with and without interaction terms to assess their statistical significance.

Sensitivity analyses were additionally performed to evaluate the potential influence of missing covariate data. Covariates were assumed to be missing at random (missing data: 1,748 for years of schooling, 123 for marital status, 200 for smoking status, 246 for drinking status, and 484 for body mass index), and the “mi estimate” command in STATA software was used to pool the results, following the generation of 10 imputed data sets through multiple imputation via chained equations.

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 17.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). An alpha value of 0.05 (two-sided) was defined as the significant level.

Results

Table 1 presents the characteristics of the study participants. Among the 12,261 older adults, 3,168 (25.84%) had depressive symptoms. Overall, the majority of participants were aged ≥ 80 years (59.91%), female (53.45%), and had ≤ 6 years of schooling (78.57%). More than half had a marital status classified as others (55.25%), were never smokers (68.51%), and were never drinkers (72.67%). Additionally, 63.95% engaged in outdoor activities, 41.20% had no chronic diseases, and 51.97% had a normal body mass index. Significant differences were observed between participants with and without depressive symptoms across all covariates, including age, sex, years of schooling, marital status, smoking, drinking, outdoor activity engagement, number of chronic diseases, and body mass index (all P-values < 0.001). The comparison of characteristics between participants included in the final analyses and those excluded (because of missing data on depressive symptoms or IADL) was presented in Table S1. Excluded participants were generally older, more likely to be women, and to have lower educational attainment (≤ 6 years), non-married status, never smokers, never drinkers, no outdoor activity engagement, no chronic disease, and underweight BMI.

Table 2 summarizes the association between depressive symptoms and IADL. Participants with depressive symptoms were significantly associated with IADL disability than those without depressive symptoms (adjusted β = 0.81, 95% CI = 0.71–0.92). Furthermore, participants in quintile 5 (11–28) of depressive symptoms scores had a significantly association with IADL disability compared to those in quintile 1 (0–4), with an adjusted β of 1.19 (95% CI = 1.06, 1.33). Similar findings were found in sensitivity analysis (Table S2). Because the IADL score represents the number of functional limitations across eight activities, the adjusted β of 0.81 indicates that participants with depressive symptoms had, on average, 0.81 more IADL limitations than those without depressive symptoms. Similarly, participants in the highest quintile of depressive symptoms had, on average, 1.19 more IADL limitations than those in the lowest quintile.

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study, exposure to depressive symptoms was associated with IADL disability among older adults in China. Stratified analyses indicated that age, sex, and outdoor activity engagement modified the associations between depressive symptoms and IADL.

Concerning research investigating the potential association of depressive symptoms with IADL disability, the findings of the current study were consistent with those of previous research [11, 12]. The observed effect sizes may also have practical relevance. Because the IADL score represents the number of limitations across eight instrumental activities of daily living, the adjusted β of 0.81 indicates that participants with depressive symptoms had, on average, nearly one additional IADL limitation compared with those without depressive symptoms. Similarly, participants in the highest quintile of depressive symptoms had, on average, more than one additional IADL limitation compared with those in the lowest quintile. Given that IADL tasks include activities required for independent community living, such as shopping, cooking, washing clothes, mobility-related activities, visiting neighbors, and taking public transportation, these differences may reflect meaningful additional functional burden in later life. Several potential mechanisms exist in the relationship. From the biological perspective, elevated cortisol levels and insulin resistance associated with depressive symptoms may accelerate physical decline through mechanisms like muscle loss, chronic inflammation, cognitive impairment, and vascular damage, further increasing the risk of disability [38]. In terms of psychological-behavioral mechanisms, depressive symptoms may lead individuals to adopt unhealthy lifestyles, such as smoking, drinking, and lack of outdoor activity, all of which have been identified as significant risk factors for IADL disability [27, 39, 40]. In addition, individuals with depressive symptoms often exhibit lower treatment adherence and lack active disease management, which may further exacerbate health deterioration and increase the risk of disability [41]. A recent Chinese population-based study found that individuals with both depressive symptoms and metabolic syndrome had a higher risk of low back pain than those with either condition alone, suggesting that depressive symptoms may impair the ability to manage their health, thereby increasing the overall disease burden [42]. From the sociocultural perspective, in the Chinese cultural context, depressive symptoms is often expressed in a somaticized manner, where individuals may mistakenly attribute their symptoms to physical illness, like pain, rather than seeing them as psychological distress [43]. This phenomenon may contribute to underestimating the impact of depressive symptoms on IADL disability or result in misdiagnosis and delayed treatment, further magnifying adverse outcomes.

The current study explored the modification effect of different age levels on IADL disability among older adults with depressive symptoms. Aging is known to affect the gradual accumulation of molecular and cellular damage over time, resulting in a decline in physical and mental health and increasing the risk of adverse health problems [44]. As shown by our findings, the stronger association with those who were high-aged older adults (aged ≥ 80 years) experiencing IADL disability with exposure to depressive symptoms (adjusted β = 0.90, 95%CI = 0.75–1.05) than those older adults aged 65–79 years (adjusted β = 0.69, 95%CI = 0.57–0.81).

This study also found that the association between depressive symptoms and IADL disability differed by sex, with a stronger association observed among men than women. Although women had a higher prevalence of depressive symptoms and severe IADL disability (Table 1; Fig. 2), depressive symptoms appeared to be more strongly associated with IADL disability among men. This finding should be interpreted cautiously, because the underlying mechanisms cannot be determined from the present cross-sectional analysis. Several explanations may be considered. First, biological mechanisms may contribute to sex differences in the functional consequences of depressive symptoms. Men and women may differ in ageing-related physiological vulnerability, comorbidity patterns, inflammatory responses, stress regulation, and muscle strength, all of which may influence how depressive symptoms are associated with functional limitations [45]. Second, behavioral mechanisms may also be relevant. Depressive symptoms may be accompanied by reduced physical activity, poorer self-care, lower treatment adherence, unhealthy lifestyle behaviors, and less active disease management [46]. If these behavioral changes differ by sex, they may partly contribute to the stronger observed association among older men. Third, social mechanisms should be considered. Social support, loneliness, and social participation are closely related to mental health and functional ability in later life [47]. Older men may have different patterns of social networks, help-seeking behavior, emotional expression, and reliance on spouses or family members, which may affect the extent to which depressive symptoms translate into functional limitations. In the Chinese sociocultural context, traditional gender roles may also shape patterns of daily activities and functional expectations among older adults. For example, older women may have accumulated long-term experience in household and daily management tasks, whereas older men may be more vulnerable to functional disruption when depressive symptoms reduce motivation, social participation, or behavioral activation [48, 49]. However, this sociocultural explanation remains speculative and should not be interpreted as the sole mechanism. Future longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the biological, behavioral, and social pathways underlying sex-specific associations between depressive symptoms and functional disability.

Outdoor activity engagement appeared to modify the association between depressive symptoms and IADL disability in the present study. The outdoor activity engagement measure used in this study incorporated socializing with friends, Tai Chi, and square dancing. Although these activities represent different forms of participation, they may influence depressive symptoms and functional ability through complementary pathways. Socializing with friends may strengthen social connectedness and reduce loneliness, whereas Tai Chi and square dancing may additionally improve physical function, mobility, balance, and psychological well-being. Collectively, these activities may help maintain social participation and functional independence in later life. Older adults with depressive symptoms often experience reduced motivation and lower participation in community-based activities, particularly when social networks are limited, which may contribute to both social disengagement and functional decline [27]. Social engagement has been shown to enhance IADL performance among older adults by promoting cognitive and behavioral stimulation [50]. In addition, participation in outdoor activities may increase exposure to natural environments and sunlight, factors that have been associated with improved mood and psychological well-being [51, 52]. Taken together, these mechanisms may provide possible explanations for the observed interaction between outdoor activity engagement and depressive symptoms in relation to IADL disability. Although the magnitude of the subgroup differences was relatively modest, the statistically significant interaction suggests that outdoor activity engagement may be related to heterogeneity in the association between depressive symptoms and functional disability in later life. This may be particularly relevant to IADL disability, because IADL tasks involve more complex social, cognitive, and behavioral functions than basic activities of daily living [8].

Although the interaction terms for age, sex, and outdoor activity engagement were statistically significant, the absolute differences in β coefficients across some subgroups were relatively modest. Therefore, these subgroup findings should be interpreted cautiously and should not be taken as evidence of large clinical differences between groups. Rather, they suggest that the association between depressive symptoms and IADL disability may vary across demographic and behavioral contexts. From a public health perspective, even modest differences in IADL limitations may be meaningful because depressive symptoms and functional disability are both common in later life. These findings may help identify older adults who could benefit from closer monitoring and early supportive interventions, but further longitudinal and intervention studies are needed to determine whether these subgroup differences translate into clinically meaningful differences in functional outcomes.

This study has important policy implications for the Chinese government in addressing disability prevention among older adults with depressive symptoms. Given the significant association of depressive symptoms with IADL disability, integrating mental health screening into routine geriatric care is essential for early identification and intervention. Policies should promote community-based programs that encourage outdoor activity engagement, which have been identified as a modifiable factor in reducing disability risk. In addition, gender-specific strategies are necessary, potentially due to variations in health behaviors. Targeted support for the high-aged older population should also be prioritized through enhanced home-based care services, rehabilitation programs, and caregiver support. Furthermore, addressing sociocultural barriers, such as the tendency to somaticize depressive symptoms, is crucial to improving mental health awareness and reducing misdiagnosis or delayed treatment.

The major advantage of this study was the use of a representative national sample to examine the association of depressive symptoms and IADL and investigate whether age, sex, and outdoor activity modify the association. The current study also had several limitations that need to be considered. Firstly, the cross-sectional nature of this study limits our ability to establish the temporal sequence of the observed associations. While our findings provide important insights into the relationship between depressive symptoms and IADL disability, the directionality of this association cannot be determined. The relationship may be bidirectional, whereby depressive symptoms may contribute to functional decline, while functional limitations may also increase the likelihood of depressive symptoms through loss of independence, reduced social participation, and decreased quality of life. In addition, the conceptual role of outdoor activity engagement should be interpreted with caution. In the present analyses, outdoor activity engagement was included as a covariate in the main adjusted model and was further examined as a potential effect modifier through stratified analyses and interaction tests. However, outdoor activity engagement may also lie on the pathway between depressive symptoms and IADL disability, as depressive symptoms may reduce motivation and participation in outdoor activities, which may subsequently contribute to functional decline. Because depressive symptoms, outdoor activity engagement, and IADL disability were measured at the same wave, the present study could not distinguish whether outdoor activity engagement functioned as a confounder, mediator, effect modifier, or a combination of these roles. Future longitudinal studies with repeated assessments are needed to clarify the temporal ordering and causal mechanisms among depressive symptoms, outdoor activity engagement, and functional disability. Secondly, although our sample is representative of Chinese older adults, the generalizability of our findings to other populations or ethnic groups remains unclear. Validating these results in diverse settings and countries would help assess their broader applicability. Thirdly, our study relied on self-reported data for depressive symptoms and IADL, which may introduce recall bias and impact the accuracy of our findings. Future studies may benefit from using clinician-administered psychiatric assessments, performance-based functional evaluations, or caregiver-reported measures to improve measurement accuracy. Fourth, due to data availability in the CLHLS questionnaire, the outdoor activity engagement measures in this study were limited to three commonly practiced outdoor activity engagement among older Chinese adults, including socializing with friends, Tai Chi, and square dancing. These measures may not fully capture the broader range of outdoor or physical activities that older adults engage in. Future studies incorporating more comprehensive assessments of activity participation are warranted. Fifth, participants with missing data on depressive symptoms or IADL were excluded from the analyses, which may have introduced selection bias. As shown in Table S1, excluded participants were generally older, more likely to be women, have lower educational attainment, non-married status, no outdoor activity engagement, and underweight BMI. Because these characteristics are associated with both depressive symptoms and functional disability, the excluded individuals may represent a particularly vulnerable subgroup. Consequently, the observed associations may have been affected by selection bias, potentially leading to underestimation of the association. Sixth, the dichotomization of IADL items may have reduced the ability to distinguish subtle differences in the severity of functional limitations, as participants with mild difficulty and those completely unable to perform a given activity were classified into the same category. Consequently, this approach may have led to some loss of information regarding the gradation and heterogeneity of IADL disability among older adults. Finally, there is a lack of exploration of the inherent relationship between ADL and IADL because functional disability is dynamic, it normally starts with IADL disability and ends with the loss of ADL disability [53]. Future longitudinal studies incorporating repeated assessments of depressive symptoms and IADL disability may help characterize trajectories and transitions in functional status over time and clarify the temporal and potentially bidirectional relationships between depressive symptoms and functional disability in later life.

Conclusions

The findings of this population-based cross-sectional study found that depressive symptoms is associated with IADL disability among older adult individuals in China. Age, sex, and outdoor activity engagement significantly modify the associations. These findings suggest a need to prevent depressive symptoms and a need for an intervention strategy for mental health to reduce the potential risk of IADL disability. Moreover, emphasizing personalized interventions that target individuals with depressive symptoms exposure may minimize the burden of IADL disability in later life.
