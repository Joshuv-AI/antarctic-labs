Finite element modeling of direct-buried high-voltage cable ampacity considering dynamic soil thermal properties
Authors: Jiang Chang, Kun Li, Tianyou Chen, Keru Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraditional ampacity evaluation for direct-buried cables often assumes static soil thermal resistance, which obscures actual operating margins. To address this, a new electromagnetic-thermal coupled finite element model for cross-linked polyethylene (XLPE) cables was developed, incorporating the dynamic thermophysical properties of porous soil. The study quantifies how water content dynamically alters heat transfer. Native soil’s thermal conductivity experiences a step mutation, jumping from 0.55 to 1.44 W/(m K) at a 15% percolation threshold. Conversely, backfill sand offers stabler heat transfer, reaching 0.85 W/(m K) at just 5% water content. Simulations of rainfall-to-drying cycles reveal a nonlinear relationship between ampacity and moisture. In supersaturated native soil, ampacity drops locally from 1250 A to 1150 A due to entrapped air. During the drying phase, hydraulic hysteresis disrupts the continuous water network, causing ampacity to plummet to 710 A. Ultimately, this model characterizes dynamic soil thermal resistance under varying hydrological conditions, providing strong theoretical support for evaluating the dynamic transmission potential of underground cable systems.Keywords: Direct-buried power cable, Multiphysics coupling, Finite element analysis, Dynamic thermal properties, Seasonal and humidity variationsSubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Hydrology

Introduction

Driven by the global advancement of urbanization, high-voltage cross-linked polyethylene (XLPE) power cables have become the preferred option for urban backbone power grids1. However, the current IEC 60,287 standard for evaluating the ampacity of direct-buried cables widely employs static analytical models2, typically treating soil thermal resistance as a constant parameter and ignoring the time-varying laws of moisture and hydrothermal migration. This simplification leads to conservative ampacity calculations, making it difficult to accurately assess the actual thermal safety margin of cable systems under extreme environmental fluctuations.

To address the aforementioned heat dissipation issues, scholars globally have conducted extensive research primarily from two perspectives: modeling methods and environmental parameters. Early evaluations were mostly based on equivalent thermal circuit (ETM) models3, but to overcome the limitations of complex boundaries, numerical computation has gradually become the mainstream4. In recent years, multiphysics numerical simulations have been further deepened. For instance, Quercio et al.5 accurately evaluated the thermal behavior of high-voltage cables under complex boundaries using a finite element model; meanwhile, multiphysics coupling models integrating environmental and thermo-fluid dynamics have been demonstrated to more precisely reflect the dynamic transmission potential of cables6. Regarding environmental parameters, some researchers have begun to focus on the time-varying nature of soil properties, pointing out that rainfall and humidity variations cause soil thermal resistance to exhibit significant nonlinear evolution7–9.

Although the aforementioned studies have laid a solid foundation, existing evaluation systems still face multiple limitations in characterizing underlying heat transfer mechanisms and layered boundaries. First, the macroscopic thermal conductivity of porous media is fundamentally governed by internal particle size distribution and the contact compactness of the solid skeleton8,10, dictating that undisturbed soil and backfill sand will inevitably exhibit entirely different microscopic thermal conduction reconstruction trajectories during moisture migration. Second, the interfacial thermal resistance present at the boundary between undisturbed soil and backfill media induces significant nonlinear perturbations in heat transfer paths9,11,12. Recent research points out that properly designing the thermal properties of backfill materials is crucial for enhancing ampacity, which highly depends on accurate dynamic heat transfer models13. However, when confronted with phase-change-level thermal conductivity jumps governed by microscopic percolation connections under extreme hydrological alternations, traditional models remain inadequate in handling such highly nonlinear, layered dynamic thermal resistance gradients. There is an urgent need in the field for a quantitative computational framework that deeply couples the microscopic grading of media with the macroscopic current-carrying capacity.

In summary, existing research still falls short in the deep coupling of multiphysics fields and the systematic nature of capacity-upgrading strategies. Therefore, focusing on the dynamic characteristics of soil thermal resistance driven by hydrothermal migration, this paper develops a two-dimensional electromagnetic-thermal coupled finite element model for high-voltage cables. By accurately characterizing the dynamic thermodynamic responses of soil and backfill sand under varying water contents, this study reveals the nonlinear mapping relationship between environmental moisture migration and ampacity, aiming to provide a theoretical basis for the precise Dynamic Thermal Rating (DTR) and refined operation and maintenance of direct-buried cables.

Model establishment

Physical model and basic assumptions

In practical engineering applications, the laying length of high-voltage power cables is usually hundreds to thousands of meters, presenting a significant scale difference compared to their radial dimensions. According to classical heat transfer theory14, for slender structures with an extremely large length-to-diameter ratio like high-voltage cables, the axial heat flux during operation is far less than the radial heat flux. Therefore, ignoring the axial temperature gradient and reducing the three-dimensional spatial heat transfer problem into a two-dimensional planar model for analysis is a reasonable physical simplification.

This paper takes a typical single-circuit horizontally arranged direct-buried cable system as the physical model. To construct the finite element calculation model, the following basic assumptions are made: the cable and its surrounding soil are regarded as a semi-infinite two-dimensional space with the ground surface as the upper boundary7,15; assuming that the surface atmospheric temperature is constant, and the heat loss of the cable is conducted through the backfill sand and native soil to the surface, ultimately dissipating into the atmosphere via convective heat transfer3; treating a single soil layer macroscopically as an isotropic medium, but its thermal conductivity dynamically evolves with changes in local water content and the temperature field16. Their driving force for moisture migration is far greater than the gravitational potential. In the two-dimensional finite element calculation framework, the above macroscopic losses calculated per unit length (Wc, Wd ,Ws) will be reasonably converted into local volumetric heating rates (Qv) based on the actual cross-sectional area of each cable component and substituted into the transient heat conduction equation. These internal heat sources not only determine the initial temperature rise of the cable body but also serve as the thermodynamic boundary of the system, continuously driving the time-varying migration of moisture and the dynamic evolution of thermal resistance in the surrounding native soil and backfill sand.

Boundary conditions and parameter settings

The temperature field caused by cable heating exhibits a nonlinear spatial attenuation distribution in the porous medium; the further away from the heat source, the closer the temperature approaches the constant reference temperature of the deep soil. To eliminate the interference of artificial truncation boundaries on the calculation results of the temperature field, this paper reasonably truncates the calculation domain and sets the boundary conditions7,9. On the lateral boundaries, the left and right boundaries of the calculation domain are uniformly set at about 2 m from the center of the outermost cable. Since it is far from the cable heating center, the radial heat flux here has extremely attenuated, so it is set as a second-type adiabatic boundary condition with a normal temperature gradient of zero. At the lower deep soil boundary, it is set at about 2 m from the bottom of the backfill layer. The soil at this depth is minimally affected by surface temperature fluctuations and the cable heat source, so it is set as a first-type isothermal boundary condition, with the temperature fixed at the deep soil environmental temperature. As for the upper surface boundary, to focus on the dynamic heat and moisture migration characteristics of the underground porous medium and improve the convergence of the model, this paper does not establish an additional atmospheric physical domain in the outermost layer, nor does it introduce complex convective heat transfer coefficients. Instead, based on the assumption of thermal equilibrium between the surface and the ambient air, the surface is directly simplified to a first-type isothermal boundary condition, keeping its temperature always equal to the set external ambient air temperature.

It is worth noting that simplifying the ground surface as a first-kind isothermal boundary is a prevalent engineering assumption in the thermodynamic evaluation of power cables. According to the thermal wave damping theory for semi-infinite media in heat transfer14, high-frequency meteorological fluctuations at the ground surface (e.g., solar radiation and surface convection) are strongly filtered by the immense thermal capacity and thermal resistance of the soil as they conduct downward, resulting in an exponential attenuation of fluctuations with depth. Relevant authoritative manuals indicate that the effective penetration depth of diurnal thermal waves is typically confined to 0.3 m to 0.5 m underground19. Since the core burial depth of the cables investigated in this study reaches 1.0 m, the transient thermal shocks from the surface are significantly dampened at this depth. Consequently, this simplified treatment ensures sufficient engineering accuracy while guaranteeing the computational convergence of the strongly coupled nonlinear model8.Regarding the initialization of the computational model, the initial soil volumetric water content is set to 10%, and the deep soil temperature is set to 10 °C. The physical basis for this configuration is to simulate the transient coupled response characteristics of a direct-buried cable corridor undergoing a typical prolonged dry season (where moisture naturally dissipates to a low-moisture baseline state), followed by a single typical extreme rainfall infiltration event, and subsequently returning to a drying dissipation cycle. This serves as a widely adopted physical baseline scenario in the engineering community for evaluating short-term cable overload risks and transient thermodynamic behavior20, effectively revealing the nonlinear modulating effects of extreme hydrological alternations on ampacity.

It should be clarified that the moisture-thermal resistance evolution relationship established in this model is primarily applicable to the analysis of single or short-term hydrological alternating processes. If extended to long-term wetting-drying cycle assessments, the physical parameters and percolation thresholds in the model must be recalibrated experimentally, as the irreversible remodeling of soil pore structures and accumulated damage will cause shifts in hydraulic hysteresis characteristics.

Through the above boundary condition settings, the distribution characteristics and dynamic change rules of the surrounding soil temperature field during the operation of high-voltage direct-buried cables can be reflected more realistically while ensuring a finite calculation domain.

To balance solution accuracy and computational efficiency, the finite element model in this paper adopts a non-uniform domain meshing strategy. For the core heating regions such as the cable body and backfill sand, a refined free triangular mesh is used for densification; boundary layer meshes are applied at the outer edge of the conductor, the metal sheath, and the interfaces of multi-layer media to accurately capture the skin effect, eddy current loss, and temperature step phenomena under alternating electromagnetic fields. For the peripheral soil regions far from the heat source, a larger mesh size growth rate is set, allowing the mesh to expand exponentially, smoothly, and sparsely toward the outer boundary. This meshing strategy effectively reduces the degrees of freedom of the model and shortens the solution time while ensuring the calculation accuracy of global heat conduction (Fig. 2).

From the quantitative comparison data in Table 2, it can be seen that there is a certain calculation deviation between the traditional analytical method and the finite element numerical simulation when evaluating the steady-state ampacity of the cable. The baseline steady-state ampacity calculated according to the classic IEC-60,287 standard system2 is 1000 A, while the evaluated ampacity obtained by solving the two-dimensional electromagnetic-thermal coupled finite element model constructed in this paper is 1074 A. Overall, the relative error of about 7.4% between the two is within a reasonable engineering permissible range, which preliminarily verifies the reliability of the model presented in this paper regarding the underlying multi-physics coupling calculations. Further analysis of the physical reasons for this evaluation increment reveals: the equivalent thermal circuit method of the IEC standard is mostly based on a one-dimensional radial heat dissipation approximation, usually treating the external medium as a uniform environment with a single thermal resistance, and its conservative boundary assumptions often underestimate the actual heat dissipation capacity. In contrast, the spatially refined finite element model introduced in this paper accurately distinguishes the thermodynamic differences between the backfill sand and the undisturbed soil, fully accounting for the real two-dimensional non-uniform temperature gradients and complex heat dissipation paths. Therefore, this model effectively overcomes the conservative limitations of the global constant thermal resistance assumption, can more objectively reflect the true heat transfer margin of porous media in direct-buried environments, and provides a reliable evaluation tool for the dynamic capacity enhancement of underground cables.

It is pertinent to emphasize that comparing the proposed model with the steady-state calculation results of the IEC standard aims to establish a baseline for underlying physical calculations. Since the IEC-60,287 standard relies on the simplifying assumptions of one-dimensional radial heat dissipation and a homogeneous environment, originating primarily from the classical Neher-McGrath analytical theory, it exhibits inherent theoretical limitations in characterizing complex two-dimensional non-uniform fields. By meticulously characterizing the thermal heterogeneity at the interface between the backfill sand and undisturbed soil, the proposed model reconstructs a realistic two-dimensional non-uniform temperature field. This approach aligns with the two-zone model theory, which posits that heat dissipation bottlenecks are highly dependent on the interfacial heat transfer characteristics between distinct soil layers21.

The above comparative analysis shows that the thermal analysis model established in this paper can relatively accurately capture the thermodynamic characteristics of the cable under dynamic loads. While maintaining a lower computational complexity, it possesses high calculation accuracy and engineering applicability, laying a theoretical foundation for the subsequent dynamic evaluation of cable ampacity22.

Addressing the issue of in-situ verification, it must be considered that applying an extreme step load of up to 1100 A at an operating 110 kV site is highly prone to triggering irreversible thermal aging or even thermal breakdown accidents in the insulation layer. Furthermore, real-time monitoring of multiphase moisture migration within the soil remains extremely difficult to achieve under current field engineering conditions. Therefore, referencing standard validation paradigms widely adopted in international power grid heat transfer modeling, this study constructs a closed-loop validation logic combining the experimental anchoring of underlying physical properties with the cross-validation of macroscopic evolutionary trends. Specifically, the physical parameters at the model input are strictly anchored to experimentally measured multiphase flow datasets obtained in the laboratory; simultaneously, the macroscopic nonlinear evolutionary trajectories at the output highly align with long-term field monitoring results published by authoritative domestic and international research teams23. Cross-comparison confirms that the characteristic patterns captured by the proposed model regarding the modulation of temperature rise curves by moisture migration exhibit a high degree of rationality and superiority.

Results analysis

Dynamic evolution mechanism of thermal conductivity of undisturbed soil in the cover layer with volumetric water content

Undisturbed soil is a typical three-phase porous medium composed of a mineral skeleton, water, and air. The intrinsic thermal conductivities of the phase components differ significantly—still air is about 0.026 W/(m K), liquid water is about 0.6 W/(m K), while the solid mineral skeleton is as high as several to over ten W/(m K)24. Therefore, the dynamic fluctuation of the macroscopic thermal conductivity of the soil essentially depends on the time-varying law of the volume ratio of each phase.

During seasonal hydrological alternating processes such as rainfall infiltration, the high-thermal-resistance air within the soil pores is gradually replaced by liquid water. However, traditional continuous and smooth mathematical models often cannot accurately capture the phase-change-level thermodynamic abrupt changes triggered by moisture seepage and connection within the porous medium. To this end, based on measured correlation data9, this paper proposes a multi-stage piecewise linear fitting model, dividing the evolution process of soil thermal conductivity with volumetric water content into three intervals with different dominant physical mechanisms:

Slow growth period dominated by pendular water systems ()In the low moisture content stage at the initial period of water infiltration, trace amounts of water are strongly adsorbed by the matric potential25, mainly wrapping around the surface of solid particles in the form of liquid films and forming microscopic liquid bridges around the contact points16,26. This pendular capillary water locally replaces the high-thermal-resistance air, but because the water films are isolated from each other, a macroscopically continuous heat transfer pathway cannot be established; therefore, the thermal conductivity only shows a slow linear upward trend. Thermodynamic comparative analysis of heat dissipation characteristics of soil cover materials

To further explore the ampacity enhancement strategy for direct-buried high-voltage cables, this paper selects typical soil cover materials in engineering, namely backfill sand and undisturbed soil, for a comparison at the thermodynamic level of multiphase media. The heat dissipation performance of direct-buried cables is significantly affected by the moisture content of the soil cover layer. In a dry state, the pores within the medium are filled with air, and the contact thermal resistance is extremely large; when moisture gradually enters the pores to replace the air, thermally conductive liquid bridges will form between the solid particles26,28, thereby greatly increasing the overall thermal conductivity.

The fundamental reason for the above macroscopic thermodynamic differences and different mathematical evolution laws lies in the heterogeneity of the microscopic structure of the media, as shown in Fig. 5.

Comparison of heat transfer mechanisms between undisturbed soil and backfilled sand.

Undisturbed soil often contains complex components such as clay, silt, and organic matter, with vast differences in particle size; the pore network is intricate, and there are a large number of dead-end pores. In the early stage of moisture infiltration, water molecules are mostly consumed in bound water films on the particle surfaces or in tiny pores, making it difficult to form a macroscopically connected heat conduction network.

In contrast, backfill sand, as an artificially screened material, has a relatively uniform particle grading and even pore distribution. This homogeneous porous structure makes it easier for moisture to spread evenly along the regular gaps between particles under capillary action. Therefore, with only a minimal amount of water, continuous and evenly distributed liquid thermal bridges can be rapidly formed at the contact points of the homogeneous particles, thereby significantly reducing the contact thermal resistance under low moisture content conditions and achieving a smooth jump in thermal conductivity. This microscopic physical process well explains why the thermal conductivity of backfill sand follows the Kersten logarithmic growth law18.

Regarding the specific threshold parameter of 15% adopted in this study, it was strictly calibrated against measured heat transfer datasets of typical silty clay cover layers9. It should be noted that the solid mineral skeleton of undisturbed soil is highly complex, and the specific percolation threshold generally fluctuates within an engineering range of 10% to 20% subject to variations in particle grading, dry density, and porosity26. Nevertheless, the underlying ‘phase-change-like’ microscopic evolutionary mechanism by which moisture reconstructs the thermal conduction network within the porous skeleton exhibits universal physical applicability. According to the Percolation-Effective Medium Approximation (P-EMA) theory, once the critical percolation threshold is surpassed, originally isolated liquid bridges extensively interconnect within the skeleton network, directly ‘short-circuiting’ the thermal resistance of residual air layers, which inevitably triggers an abrupt step-like jump in macroscopic thermal conductivity27. Consequently, for applications across diverse geological sites, it is recommended that design engineers preliminarily conduct in-situ borehole sampling or transient thermal probe experiments to recalibrate the site-specific critical percolation threshold and mathematical piecewise coefficients of the undisturbed soil, thereby ensuring the absolute accuracy of the engineering evaluation.

Dynamic response analysis of direct-buried cable ampacity to rainfall cycles

In the actual operating environment of direct-buried cables, natural rainfall and surface evaporation jointly constitute the time-varying boundary conditions for the moisture in the soil cover layer, prompting the thermophysical properties of the porous media around the cable to exhibit significant dynamic fluctuations. To accurately quantify the substantial impact of the hydrological dynamic process on the cable’s transmission capacity, a finite element analysis model as shown in Fig. 6 was constructed based on the parameters of the 64/110 kV YJLW02 1 × 800 XLPE cable. As shown in Figs. 7 and 8, using the data from the literature8, a typical rainfall-to-drying evolution cycle was simulated, and the ultimate ampacity characteristics of undisturbed soil and backfill sand at different transient stages were extracted and compared. Based on heat transfer theory16,20, the dynamic evolution of the cable’s ultimate ampacity is subject to changes in the system’s equivalent external thermal resistance, exhibiting an inverse mapping relationship with the transient fluctuations of the cable core temperature. Combining the moisture content evolution in Fig. 7 and the ampacity curves in Fig. 8, the thermodynamic response laws of the two soil cover media within a typical rainfall-to-drying hydrological alternating cycle can be summarized into the following two core evolution stages:

Non-monotonic response during the rainfall infiltration and oversaturation stages. From the initial stage of rainfall to the oversaturation stage, there is an essential difference in the heat transfer trajectories of the two media. The macroscopic ampacity of undisturbed soil remains at a lower baseline value of about 630 A before breaking through the 15% percolation threshold27. Although the ampacity experiences a step-like surge after crossing the threshold, when the moisture content climbs to an oversaturated state above 25%, the trapped bubbles formed within the tiny pores and the clay water-absorption swelling effect together constitute additional thermal obstruction29,30. This microscopic physical change leads to a localized deterioration in the heat transfer characteristics of the undisturbed soil, causing the ampacity to abnormally drop back to around 1150 A. It is not until the rainfall stops and the moisture content moderately dissipates to 20% that the internal gas-liquid-solid three-phase skeleton of the soil reaches the optimal heat transfer matching state, and the ampacity finally reaches its full-cycle ultimate peak of approximately 1250 A. In contrast, relying on its uniform particle grading, the backfill sand can activate thermally conductive liquid bridges28 with trace amounts of water, presenting a gentle rise and maintaining a relatively stable heat transfer plateau characteristic throughout the wetting cycle.

Hydraulic hysteresis and sudden onset of thermal bottlenecks during the drying and dissipation stage. Entering the late stage of moisture dissipation, under the dual influence of moisture migration driven by cable Joule heating16,21 and natural evaporation, the external thermal resistance of the system climbs sharply. Significantly affected by the hydraulic hysteresis effect of the porous medium26, the decay rate of the ampacity significantly lags behind the decline curve of the moisture content in the time dimension. When the moisture content of the undisturbed soil falls below the percolation threshold, its internal continuous water network fractures on a macroscopic scale, damaging the connectivity of the heat conduction network. The destruction of this microscopic structure directly leads to a drastic nonlinear decay in the macroscopic ampacity, with the value sharply decreasing from the peak of 1250 A to about 710 A, which easily induces localized dry zones and severe thermal bottleneck effects31,32. Meanwhile, benefiting from the dense quartz solid-phase skeleton and point-contact heat transfer mechanism, the backfill sand possesses a strong ability to resist thermal shock, and its transmission capacity only shows a gentle, approximately linear decay during this stage. A comprehensive view of the full cycle of transient evolution indicates that the backfill sand can effectively buffer the thermal shocks caused by dramatic hydrological changes through its stable solid-phase skeleton heat transfer mechanism, significantly enhancing the dynamic transmission margin and operational reliability of direct-buried high-voltage cables under all-weather conditions.

Based on the preceding dynamic thermal characteristic analysis, the theoretical findings can be effectively translated into actionable reference guidelines for design and operation and maintenance (O&M) within the engineering community:

Selection Strategy for Backfill Cover Materials. Given that undisturbed soil is highly prone to falling below the connectivity threshold during the late stages of moisture dissipation, thereby inducing severe thermal bottleneck effects, it is strongly recommended to mandatorily abandon native soil backfilling in drought-prone regions or high-load corridor sections. Instead, cables should be enveloped with backfill sand structures characterized by a homogeneous point-contact thermal conduction skeleton and extremely low hydraulic hysteresis to establish a heat transfer buffer zone31.

A dynamic computational framework quantifying moisture evolution and the percolation threshold is established, laying the foundation for the evaluation of the dynamic thermal rating (DTR) of cables20.
