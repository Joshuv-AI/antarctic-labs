Primary apocrine carcinoma of the breast incidentally detected on CT angiography: Imaging–pathology correlation in a rare histologic subtype
Authors: Zaineb Chetbi, Marouane Boukroute, Brahim Boulayoun, Ibtissam bellajdel, Hafsa Taheri, Hanane Saadi, Ahmed Mimouni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Apocrine carcinoma (AC) of the breast is a rare histologic subtype characterized by apocrine morphology and a frequent androgen receptor (AR)-positive / estrogen receptor (ER)-negative profile. Imaging findings are often nonspecific, making diagnosis challenging. We report an incidental 1.0-cm apocrine carcinoma (Elston–Ellis grade 2) detected in a 72-year-old woman during thoracic CT angiography. Retrospective review identified a small enhancing lesion in the right breast. Mammography (CC and MLO views) demonstrated an irregular high-density mass with indistinct margins. Targeted ultrasound revealed an irregular hypoechoic mass with noncircumscribed margins (BI-RADS 4A). Due to limited access to image-guided core biopsy, an excisional biopsy was performed. Histopathologic examination confirmed invasive apocrine carcinoma with an in situ component and AR positivity. The patient subsequently underwent therapeutic mastectomy. This case highlights the nonspecific imaging features of AC and emphasizes the key role of histopathology and immunohistochemical profiling in diagnosis and management.

Introduction

Apocrine differentiation in the breast describes epithelial cells with abundant eosinophilic granular cytoplasm and prominent nucleoli. When apocrine morphology predominates (commonly defined as >90% apocrine cells) and is accompanied by an immunophenotype of ER-/PR- and AR+, the lesion is classified as apocrine carcinoma (AC). Recent pathological and immunohistochemical reviews have clarified diagnostic criteria and emphasized AR as a defining marker and potential therapeutic target [1].

Reported incidence varies according to diagnostic thresholds but is generally low (commonly cited between 0.3% and 4% of invasive breast cancers), with many series reporting rates at the lower end when strict “pure” apocrine criteria are applied. AC more frequently affects postmenopausal women but can occur across a wide age range. Imaging descriptions are limited by small series and case reports; consequently, radiologic appearance is nonspecific and tissue diagnosis remains mandatory [2].

Here we present an incidental small apocrine carcinoma in an elderly woman, with emphasis on imaging-pathology correlation and a concise literature review focused on epidemiology, imaging accuracy (mammography vs ultrasound), diagnostic biomarkers, and up-to-date therapeutic considerations.

Case presentation

We report the case of a 72-year-old postmenopausal woman with a medical history of Parkinson’s disease, currently under neurological follow-up, and type 2 diabetes mellitus managed with oral antidiabetic agents. The patient had also undergone surgical evacuation of an extradural hematoma 2 years ago. She had no prior history of breast disease or malignancy and no known family history of breast cancer.

She presented following the incidental discovery of a 9 × 6 mm nodular lesion in the upper outer quadrant of the right breast during a thoracic CT angiography, which was initially performed for an unrelated vascular assessment. The lesion was asymptomatic, with no associated skin changes, nipple discharge, or palpable mass. The patient denied any history of trauma, prior breast interventions, or hormone replacement therapy.

On clinical examination, the patient was conscious, hemodynamically stable, and without respiratory distress. Breast examination was unremarkable, with no detectable masses, retractions, or peau d’orange. Axillary and supraclavicular lymph nodes were not palpable, and no signs of local or systemic dissemination were observed.

Discussion

Definition and epidemiology

Apocrine carcinoma is defined by predominance of apocrine morphology and a characteristic steroid-receptor profile (generally ER−/PR− and AR+). The 2019 WHO classification and subsequent reviews emphasize the combined morphological and immunophenotypic criteria for a “pure” apocrine carcinoma diagnosis. Reported incidence ranges widely depending on criteria, but strict definitions place it under 1% of invasive breast cancers in many series; broader definitions yield frequencies up to ∼4%. AC predominantly affects postmenopausal women, although cases at younger ages have been reported [2,3].

Clinical presentation and imaging

Clinically, AC does not have pathognomonic signs: most present with a palpable mass discovered by self-examination or mammographic screening, frequently located in the UOQ. Imaging descriptions are limited: mammography often shows an irregular mass without specific calcifications, while ultrasound typically demonstrates a hypoechoic lesion with noncircumscribed margins and internal vascularity. Doppler sonography may show increased intralesional flow or abnormal spectral patterns (eg, high resistive index or diastolic flow alteration), which are suggestive but not specific to malignancy. Because AC imaging features overlap with other invasive carcinomas, definitive diagnosis depends on histology and IHC [[4], [5], [6]].

Comparative performance: mammography versus ultrasound

In general breast imaging practice, mammography remains more sensitive for detection of nonpalpable lesions and calcification-predominant tumors, whereas ultrasound is valuable in dense breasts and for characterization/localization, especially when lesions are palpable. Specific to AC, there are no high-power prospective comparisons demonstrating superiority of 1 modality; retrospective analyses and case series emphasize complementary roles. Ultrasound with Doppler can add useful functional information (vascularity) and guide percutaneous biopsy or localization for excision. Thus, a combined approach (mammography ± tomosynthesis, targeted ultrasound, and image-guided biopsy) provides the most reliable pathway to diagnosis [7,8].

Pathology, immunoprofile, and immunohistochemical profile

Histologically, AC shows abundant eosinophilic granular cytoplasm, prominent nucleoli, and sharp cellular borders. IHC classically demonstrates ER−/PR− and AR+ staining; GCDFP-15 is frequently positive and can support apocrine lineage. HER2 amplification is present in a subset (∼20%-30% in some series), and Ki-67 varies. Recent molecular profiling studies (NGS, aCGH) have identified recurrent genomic alterations in apocrine carcinomas, with enrichment of AR pathway activation, PIK3CA mutations and other actionable aberrations in some cohorts. These genomic insights support exploration of targeted therapies (AR antagonists, PI3K pathway inhibitors) in selected patients and suggest that molecular testing can inform personalized treatment strategies [1,9,10].

Therapeutic considerations and recent recommendations

There are no randomized trials dedicated to pure apocrine carcinoma; management is therefore adapted from general breast cancer guidelines while accounting for the distinct biology of AC:1.Local therapy: Surgery (breast-conserving surgery or mastectomy) is central. Sentinel node biopsy is indicated per tumor size/staging rules. Adjuvant radiotherapy follows standard indications after breast-conserving surgery or in high-risk postmastectomy scenarios [8].2.Systemic therapy: Decisions on chemotherapy are individualized based on tumor size, grade, nodal status, patient age, comorbidities, and proliferative index. Retrospective series indicate variable chemotherapy sensitivity; some data in triple-negative apocrine cohorts show lower pathological complete response rates to neoadjuvant chemotherapy but not necessarily worse survival, suggesting treatment personalization may be possible [11,12].3.AR-targeted therapy: Given frequent AR expression, AR antagonists (eg, bicalutamide, enzalutamide) have been investigated in AR+ breast cancers, including apocrine and AR+ TNBC subgroups, with modest clinical benefit in phase II studies and case reports. However, the presence of AR splice variants or co-amplifications can confer resistance; therefore, biomarker-driven selection and comprehensive molecular profiling are advisable prior to AR-directed therapy [10,11].4.HER2-targeted therapy and other agents: In HER2-amplified AC, standard HER2-targeted regimens are applicable. Emerging strategies (PI3K/AKT/mTOR inhibitors, CDK4/6 inhibitors) may be relevant in tumors bearing corresponding alterations, but evidence remains preliminary. Enrollment in clinical trials or precision-medicine programs should be considered [3,9].

Diagnostic and management algorithm (practical points)

•Use combined imaging (mammography ± DBT, targeted ultrasound) for lesion detection and characterization; proceed to image-guided core biopsy for suspicious lesions [4].•On core/excisional tissue, request IHC panel including ER, PR, AR, HER2, GCDFP-15, Ki-67; consider reflex molecular profiling (NGS) in early-stage disease when systemic therapy decisions or trial eligibility hinge on targetable variants [1,4].•For small, node-negative, low-grade AC in elderly patients, discuss individualized de-escalation of systemic chemotherapy when overall benefit is marginal and comorbidity risk is substantial; document shared decision-making [11].

Relevance of our case

Our patient’s lesion was small, asymptomatic, and incidentally found on CT angiography, reflecting the nonspecific imaging appearance of AC and reinforcing the need for histologic confirmation of even low-suspicion BI-RADS lesions in elderly patients. AR positivity in this tumor suggests potential for targeted strategies if systemic therapy becomes necessary in the future; in the present context (small node-negative tumor with limited risk), surgical management with vigilant surveillance was appropriate after multidisciplinary discussion [3,10]. In our case, imaging findings were nonspecific and consistent with previously reported features of apocrine carcinoma, highlighting the importance of tissue diagnosis.

Conclusion

Apocrine carcinoma is a rare breast cancer subtype with distinctive histopathologic and immunohistochemical features but often nonspecific imaging findings. Diagnosis relies on tissue sampling combined with immunohistochemistry, particularly androgen receptor (AR) expression, which plays a key role in tumor characterization. Management follows standard oncologic principles but must be individualized according to tumor biology, stage, patient comorbidities, and treatment goals. Given the limited available evidence, reporting cases with detailed imaging–pathology correlation remains essential to improve recognition and guide management of this uncommon entity.
