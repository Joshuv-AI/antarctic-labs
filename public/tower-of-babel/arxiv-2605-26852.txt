Recognizing Level- k k -Based Phylogenetic Networks is NP-Complete
Takatora Suzuki

Abstract
Phylogenetic networks generalize phylogenetic trees by representing reticulate evolution. Tree-based networks and their support trees have been extensively studied, but not all networks are tree-based. To measure how far such networks are from being tree-based, Suzuki and Hayamizu (2025) formulated the problem of finding the support network with minimum level of a given rooted almost-binary phylogenetic network. They conjectured that this problem is NP-hard and provided exponential-time algorithms. In this paper, we prove this conjecture by showing that, for every fixed integer k ≥ 1 k\geq 1 , it is NP-complete to decide whether the minimum level is at most k k .



1
Introduction

Phylogenetic networks are graph-theoretical models for representing reticulate evolution of species, such as horizontal gene transfer
[
2
,
27
]
and hybridization
[
14
]
. Compared with phylogenetic trees, which describe only branching evolution, they can represent a wider range of evolutionary histories, but this also makes them more difficult to interpret.
To address this issue, various subclasses of phylogenetic networks have been proposed, each capturing a biologically meaningful scenario while retaining mathematical tractability (see
[
22
]
for a comprehensive review).
Among these, two classes have attracted particular attention.
The first is the class of
level-
k
networks

[
5
]
, which were introduced to make various computational problems on phylogenetic networks tractable by restricting the local density of reticulations.
Specifically, a network is called level-
k
if each of its biconnected components contains at most
k
reticulations.
Level-
1
networks, originally defined as galled trees in
[
15
]
, have been extensively studied
[
12
,
23
,
4
]
, and level-
k
networks in general have also attracted considerable attention
[
11
,
28
]
.
The second is the class of
tree-based networks
(e.g.,
[
10
,
29
,
1
,
7
,
17
,
16
,
24
,
21
,
20
]
), which captures the perspective where evolution is tree-like with some reticulate events.
More precisely, a network is called tree-based if it has at least one
support tree
, defined as a spanning tree sharing the root and leaf set.

Tree-based networks and their support trees have been the subject of active research since their introduction by Francis and Steel
[
10
]
.
Several studies have shown that whether a given network is tree-based can be decided in linear time
[
10
,
29
,
20
,
17
]
.
A particularly influential development is Hayamizu’s structure theorem for rooted binary phylogenetic networks (or more generally, for almost-binary networks)
[
17
]
.
This theorem provides a direct-product characterization of the family of support trees in a given network by canonically decomposing the network into a unique set of subgraphs called maximal zig-zag trails.
This characterization yields optimal algorithms for various computational problems on support trees in a unified manner, such as counting, enumeration, listing, and optimization.
On the other hand, it has also been shown that it is NP-hard to decide whether a given tree-based network has a support tree that is a subdivision of a given tree
[
1
]
(see, e.g.,
[
21
]
for other hardness result on tree-based networks).

However, not all phylogenetic networks are tree-based.
Several approaches have been proposed to solve computational problems on such networks
[
7
,
17
,
25
,
9
,
6
,
26
]
.
One natural approach is to consider substructures that generalize support trees and to optimize how “close to a support tree” such a substructure is.
Along this line, Suzuki and Hayamizu
[
26
]
introduced the notion of
support networks
for rooted almost-binary phylogenetic networks
N
, defined as spanning subgraphs of
N
sharing the root and the leaf set.
They then formulated two optimization problems: for a given rooted almost-binary phylogenetic network
N
,

Reticulation Minimization
asks for a support network of
N
with the minimum number of reticulations, and

Level Minimization
asks for a support network of
N
with the minimum level.
The minimum level among all support networks of
N
is called the
base level
of
N
.
For the former problem, they provided a linear-time algorithm.
For the latter problem, they conjectured its NP-hardness and developed two exponential-time algorithms: an exact algorithm and a faster heuristic.
These algorithms for the two problems are based on their extension of the structure theorem
[
17
]
from support trees to support networks, which also serves as the theoretical foundation of this paper.

In this paper, we prove that
Level Minimization
is NP-hard.
We introduce its decision version, which we call
Level Decision
: given a rooted almost-binary phylogenetic network
N
and an integer
k\geq 0
, decide whether the base level of
N
is at most
k
.
We note that the case
k=0
coincides with the problem of deciding whether
N
is tree-based, which is known to be linear-time solvable.
We prove that
Level Decision
is NP-complete for every fixed
k\geq 1
; in particular, the NP-hardness of
Level Minimization
is a direct corollary of this result.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.
Section
2
introduces preliminaries, and
Section
3
formulates
Level Minimization
and
its decision version
Level Decision
.
Section
4
recalls two key results
from
[
17
]
and
[
26
]

and extends them to the multiple-rooted setting, since our reduction uses multiple-rooted networks as gadgets.
Section
5
then establishes the NP-completeness of
Level Decision
for
k=1
via a polynomial-time reduction from 3SAT, which immediately implies the NP-hardness of
Level Minimization
.
Section
6
extends this reduction to establish the NP-completeness of
Level Decision
for every fixed
k\geq 2
.
Section
7
concludes with a discussion of open
problems and directions for future work.

Preliminaries

2.1
Graph theoretical terminology

The graphs in this paper are finite, simple (i.e., having neither loops nor multiple edges), acyclic directed graphs, unless otherwise stated.
For a graph
G
,
V(G)
and
E(G)
denote the sets of vertices and edges of
G
, respectively.
For two graphs
G
and
H
,
G
is a
subgraph
of
H
if both
V(G)\subseteq V(H)
and
E(G)\subseteq E(H)
hold, in which case we write
G\subseteq H
.
Two graphs

and
H
are
isomorphic
, denoted by
G=H
, if there exists a bijection
\varphi:V(G)\to V(H)
such that
(u,v)\in E(G)
if and only if
(\varphi(u),\varphi(v))\in E(H)
for all
u,v\in V(G)
. A subgraph
G
of
H
is
proper
if
G\neq H
. A subgraph
G
of
H
is a
spanning
subgraph of
H
if
V(G)=V(H)
.

Given a graph
G
and a non-empty subset
S\subseteq E(G)
, the edge-set
S
is said to
induce the subgraph
G[S]
of
G
, that is, the one whose edge-set is
S
and whose vertex-set is the set of the ends of all edges in
S
.
For a graph
G
with
|E(G)|\geq 1
and a partition
\{E_{1},\dots,E_{d}\}
of
E(G)
, the collection
\{G[E_{1}],\dots,G[E_{d}]\}
is a
decomposition
of
G
, and
G
is said to be
decomposed into

\{G[E_{1}],\dots,G[E_{d}]\}
. Here we recall that a partition of a set is a collection of pairwise disjoint non-empty subsets whose union is the entire set.

For an edge
e=(u,v)
of a graph
G
,
u
and
v

are denoted by
\mathrm{tail}(e)
and
\mathrm{head}(e)
, respectively.
For a vertex
v
of a graph
G
, the
in-degree of
v
in
G
, denoted by
\mathrm{indeg}_{G}(v)
, is the number of edges
e
of
G
with
\mathrm{head}(e)=v
. The
out-degree of
v
in
G
, denoted by
\mathrm{outdeg}_{G}(v)
, is the number of edges
e
of
G
with
\mathrm{tail}(e)=v
.

Let
G
be a graph.
Subdividing
an edge
(u,v)
of
G
means replacing it with a directed path from
u
to
v
of length at least two.
Smoothing
a vertex
v
where
\mathrm{indeg}_{G}(v)=\mathrm{outdeg}_{G}(v)=1
means suppressing
v
from
G
, i.e., the reverse operation of edge subdivision.
A graph
G^{\prime}
is called a
subdivision
of
G
if
G^{\prime}
is obtained from
G
by applying edge subdivisions zero or more times.
For two vertices
u
and
v
of a graph
G
, we call
u
an
ancestor
of
v
, and
v
a
descendant
of
u
, if there exists a directed path from
u
to
v
.

An undirected graph is
connected
if there is a path between every pair of vertices.
A directed graph is
connected
if its underlying undirected graph is connected.
For a connected simple undirected graph
G
, a
cut vertex
(resp.
cut edge
) of
G
is a vertex (resp. edge) whose removal disconnects
G
.
A subgraph
H
of
G
is called a
block
of
G
if
H
is a maximal connected subgraph of
G
that contains no cut vertex of
H
.
In this paper, a
block
of a directed graph refers to a block of its underlying undirected graph.
A block is
trivial
if it consists of a single edge or a single vertex; otherwise, it is
non-trivial
.

2.2
Phylogenetic networks

Throughout this paper,
X
represents a non-empty finite set, which can be interpreted as a set of present-day species.
We recall the definitions of rooted almost-binary phylogenetic networks and rooted binary phylogenetic trees from
[
17
,
26
]
.

Definition 1
.

A
rooted almost-binary phylogenetic network on
X
is defined to be a finite simple directed acyclic graph
N
with the following properties (P1)–(P3):

(P1)

there exists a unique vertex
\rho
with in-degree
0
and out-degree
1
or
2
in
N
;

(P2)

the set of vertices with in-degree
1
and out-degree
0
in
N
is identical to
X
; and

(P3)

every vertex
v\in V(N)\setminus(\{\rho\}\cup X)
satisfies
\mathrm{indeg}_{N}(v)\in\{1,2\}
and
\mathrm{outdeg}_{N}(v)\in\{1,2\}
.

When
N
has properties (P1), (P2) and (P4),
N
is particularly called a rooted
binary
phylogenetic
tree
on
X
.

(P4)

Every vertex
v\in V(N)\setminus(\{\rho\}\cup X)
satisfies
\mathrm{indeg}_{N}(v)=1
and
\mathrm{outdeg}_{N}(v)=2
.

In Definition
1
, we call the vertex
\rho

the root
of
N
, each element of
X
a
leaf
of
N
. We call a non-root and non-leaf vertex
v
a
reticulation
of
N
if
\mathrm{indeg}_{N}(v)=2
.

Let
N
be a rooted almost-binary phylogenetic network on
X
.
If
G
is a spanning subgraph of
N
and is a subdivision of some rooted binary phylogenetic tree on
X
, then
G
is a
support tree
of
N
.
If
N
has a support tree, then
N
is called
tree-based
.

Definition 2
.

Let
N
be a rooted almost-binary phylogenetic network on
X
and let
G
be a spanning subgraph of
N
. If
G
is a rooted almost-binary phylogenetic network on
X
, then
G
is called a
support network
of
N
.
A support network
G
of
N
is called
minimal
if no support network of
N
is a proper subgraph of
G
, and is called
minimum
if
G
has the minimum number of edges among all support networks of
N
.

Since every support network of
N=(V,E)
has the same vertex set

, we shall identify a support network
G=(V,E^{\prime})
of
N
with its edge-set
E^{\prime}\subseteq E
throughout this paper.
We denote the families of all, minimal, and minimum support networks of
N
by
\mathcal{A}_{N},\mathcal{B}_{N}
and
\mathcal{C}_{N}
, respectively.

For a rooted almost-binary phylogenetic network
N
,

\mathrm{level}(N)
denotes the
level
of
N
, i.e., the maximum number of reticulations contained in a block of
N
. The
base level
of
N
, denoted by
\mathrm{level}^{\ast}(N)
, is the minimum value of
\mathrm{level}(G)
over all support networks

of
N
.
If
k\geq 0
is the base level of

, then
N
is called
level-
k
-based
.
We note that
N
is tree-based if and only if
\mathrm{level}^{\ast}(N)=0

can be viewed as a level-0 support network of

.

3
Problem Definitions and Known Results

In this section, we formulate the two problems whose computational complexity is the focus of this paper,
Level Minimization
(Problem
1
) and its decision version,
Level Decision
(Problem
2
).
We then recall a key lemma (Lemma
3
) that will be repeatedly used in our NP-completeness proofs in Sections
5
and
6
.

Problem 1

(
Level Minimization
, Problem 2 in
[
26
]
)
.

Given a rooted almost-binary phylogenetic network
N
on
X
, compute the base level,
\mathrm{level}^{\ast}(N)
, and find a support network
G\in\mathcal{A}_{N}
with
\mathrm{level}(G)=\mathrm{level}^{\ast}(N)
.

Figure 1:
(a) A rooted almost-binary phylogenetic network
N
on
X=\{1,2\}
, which is not tree-based. (b) A level-1 support network
G
of
N
which minimizes the level among
\mathcal{A}_{N}
.

Figure
1
illustrates an example of an input-output pair for Problem
1
. For the input network
N
shown in Figure
1
(a),
N
has no support tree and has a level-
1
support network
G
shown in Figure
1
(b). Hence, the output of Problem
1
for this input consists of
\mathrm{level}^{\ast}(N)=1
together with any level-
1
support network of
N
, such as

.

We note that the case
k=0
of
Level Decision
, i.e. deciding whether
\mathrm{level}^{\ast}(N)=0
, coincides with deciding tree-basedness, which is solvable in linear time for rooted almost-binary networks
[
10
,
20
,
29
,
17
]
. Hence the NP-hardness of

does not follow from any reduction via tree-basedness, in contrast to the unrooted analogue studied by Fischer and Francis
[
7
]
, whose NP-hardness follows directly from that of deciding tree-basedness for unrooted networks
[
8
]
.

Problem
2
below is the decision version of Problem
1
, and is no harder than Problem
1
, since comparing the output value of Problem
1
with
k
immediately solves Problem
2
.

Problem 2

(
Level Decision
)
.

Given a rooted almost-binary phylogenetic network
N
on
X
and an integer
k\geq 0
, determine whether
\mathrm{level}^{\ast}(N)\leq k
or not.

A key ingredient of our NP-completeness proofs in Sections
5
and
6
is the following lemma, originally given in
[
26
]
.
It shows that, for both Problems
1
and
2
, the search space can be restricted from
\mathcal{A}_{N}
to the (typically much smaller) subfamily
\mathcal{B}_{N}
without losing optimality. We will invoke this restriction repeatedly throughout the remainder of the paper.

Lemma 3

(the first statement of Theorem 19 in
[
26
]
)
.

For any rooted almost-binary phylogenetic network
N
on
X
, there exists at least one element
G\in\mathcal{B}_{N}
which satisfies
\mathrm{level}(G)=\mathrm{level}^{\ast}(N)
.

As an algorithmic consequence, Lemma
3
guarantees that Algorithm 1 in
[
26
]
, which exhaustively searches
\mathcal{B}_{N}
in
O(|E(N)|\cdot|\mathcal{B}_{N}|)
time, solves Problem
1
exactly. Suzuki and Hayamizu
[
26
]
also presented a faster heuristic (Algorithm 2 in
[
26
]
) based on an exhaustive search of
\mathcal{C}_{N}
, which runs in
O(|E(N)|\cdot|\mathcal{C}_{N}|)
time but does not always return an optimal solution.
Both algorithms run in exponential time in the worst case.

4
An Extension to
p
-Rooted Networks:

Canonical Decomposition and

Characterizations of Support Network Families

In this section, we generalize two results from previous works: the maximal zig-zag trail decomposition by Hayamizu
[
17
]
; and the direct-product characterization of the families of all, minimal, and minimum support networks by Suzuki and Hayamizu
[
26
]
; from rooted almost-binary phylogenetic networks to a broader class of networks that may have more than one root.

In what follows, we first define
p
-rooted almost-binary phylogenetic networks in Definition
4
, where
p\geq 1
denotes the number of roots, and restate the first result in this setting (Theorem
5
).
We also recall a characterization of rooted tree-based networks via the decomposition (Proposition
6
).
We then generalize Definition
2
to
p
-rooted networks (Definition
7
) and restate the second result (Theorem
8
).
In the proofs of Theorems
5
and
8
, we describe how the original proofs in
[
17
]
and
[
26
]
can be adapted to the
p
-rooted setting.
This
p
-rooted setting is essential for our NP-completeness proofs in Sections
5
and
6
, where almost-binary phylogenetic networks with more than one root arise as intermediate objects. A similar multiple-rooted generalization has also been considered in a different context
[
19
]
.

Definition 4
.

Let
p\geq 1
be an integer. A
p
-rooted almost-binary phylogenetic network on
X
is defined to be a finite simple directed acyclic graph
N
with the following properties (P1’)–(P3’):

(P1’)

there exist exactly
p
vertices with in-degree
0
and out-degree
1
or
2
in
N
;

(P2’)

the set of vertices with in-degree
1
and out-degree
0
in
N
is identical to
X
; and

(P3’)

every other vertex
v
of
N
satisfies
\mathrm{indeg}_{N}(v)\in\{1,2\}
and
\mathrm{outdeg}_{N}(v)\in\{1,2\}
.

In Definition
4
, we call each of the
p
vertices of
N
described in (P1’) a
root
of
N
, the set of all roots of
N
the
root-set
of
N
, and each element of
X
a
leaf
of
N
. We call each non-root and non-leaf vertex
v
of
N
a
reticulation
of
N
if
\mathrm{indeg}_{N}(v)=2
.
We note that the case
p=1
coincides with Definition
1
.
Although any 1-rooted almost-binary phylogenetic network on
X
must be connected, we note that a
p
-rooted almost-binary phylogenetic network on
X
is not necessarily connected for
p\geq 2
since different roots may belong to different components (see Figure
2
(c) for an example).

Let
p\geq 1
be an integer and let
N
be a
p
-rooted almost-binary phylogenetic network on
X
.
A connected subgraph
Z
of
N
with
m\geq 1
edges is called a
zig-zag trail
if there exists a permutation
(e_{1},\dots,e_{m})
of
E(Z)
, where either
\mathrm{head}(e_{i})=\mathrm{head}(e_{i+1})
or
\mathrm{tail}(e_{i})=\mathrm{tail}(e_{i+1})
holds for all
i\in[1,m-1]
.
In this paper, we often identify
Z
and the permutation
(e_{1},\dots,e_{m})
.
A zig-zag trail
Z
is
maximal
if no zig-zag trail contains
Z
as a proper subgraph.
A zig-zag trail is represented by an alternating sequence of (not necessarily distinct) vertices and distinct edges such as
(v_{0},(v_{0},v_{1}),v_{1},(v_{2},v_{1}),\dots,(v_{m},v_{m-1}),v_{m})
.
Moreover, if we adopt the notation of each edge
(v_{i},v_{j})
as
v_{i}>v_{j}
or
v_{j}<v_{i}
, we can more concisely express
Z
as
v_{0}>v_{1}<v_{2}\dots,v_{m-1}<v_{m}
or its reverse.
We call
|E(Z)|
the
length
of a maximal zig-zag trail
Z
.

Every maximal zig-zag trail is classified into one of the four types.
A maximal zig-zag trail
Z
is called a
crown
if
Z
has even number
m
of edges and can be written in the cyclic form
v_{0}>v_{1}<\dots,v_{m-1}<v_{m}=v_{0}
.
A
fence
is a maximal zig-zag trail which is not a crown.
An
M-fence
is a fence with even number
m
of edges and of the form
v_{0}<v_{1}>\dots,v_{m-1}>v_{m}\neq v_{0}
.
A
W-fence
is a fence with even number
m
of edges and of the form
v_{0}>v_{1}<\dots,v_{m-1}<v_{m}\neq v_{0}
.
An

is a fence with odd number
m
of edges and of the form
v_{0}>v_{1}<\dots,v_{m-1}>v_{m}
.

As in the approach taken in
[
17
,
16
,
25
,
26
]
, we often consider a maximal zig-zag trail

as a sequence
(e_{1},\dots,e_{|E(Z)|})
of edges, ordered according to their appearance in the trail.
For any maximal zig-zag trail
Z=(e_{1},\dots,e_{|E(Z)|})
in
N
, any subset
S
of
E(Z)
is specified by a
0
-
1
sequence
\langle b_{1}\;b_{2}\;\dots\;b_{|E(Z)|}\rangle
, where
b_{i}=1
if
e_{i}\in S
and
b_{i}=0
otherwise. For example, given a W-fence
Z=(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3},e_{4})
, a subset
\{e_{1},e_{3},e_{4}\}
of

can be encoded as
\langle 1011\rangle
.

The following Theorem

originally proved for rooted binary phylogenetic networks by

, and was

Theorem 5

(​​
[
17
]
,

Let

be an integer and let

Proof.

The original proof of Theorem 4.2 in

For a given 3-CNF formula

3.

for each

, and
s_{i,{j_{i}}}
for each
i\in[1,\ell]
.
Let
i\in[1,\ell]
.
Observation
11
implies that only
d_{i,{j_{i}}}
and
e_{i,{j_{i}}}
have a reticulation
s_{i,{j_{i}}}
as a descendant in
G
among the six roots of
C_{i}
.
Then, suppose that
\lambda_{i,{j_{i}}}
is a positive literal
v_{m}
.
By the construction of
N(\varphi)
, the vertices
d_{i,{j_{i}}}
and
e_{i,{j_{i}}}
are located on the directed path from
a_{\neg v_{m}}
to
b_{\neg v_{m}}
.
By Lemma
9
,
the directed path from

a_{\neg v_{m}}
to
b_{\neg v_{m}}
is not contained in the non-trivial block of
G^{\prime}_{\mathsf{T}}(v_{m})
.
Hence,
s_{i,{j_{i}}}
, which hangs from this path, does not share a block with any other reticulation in
G
.
If instead
\lambda_{i,{j_{i}}}
is a negative literal
\neg v_{m}
, then
d_{i,{j_{i}}}
and
e_{i,{j_{i}}}
lie on the directed path from
a_{v_{m}}
to
b_{v_{m}}
, which by Lemma
9
is not contained in the non-trivial block of
G^{\prime}_{\mathsf{F}}(v_{m})
, and the same conclusion follows.
Thus the reticulation
s_{i,j_{i}}
does not share a block with any reticulation contained in a variable gadget.
Furthermore, since gadgets of the same type are not connected to each other, no two reticulations within clause gadgets, and no two within variable gadgets, share a block.
Hence
G
is level-
1
. This completes the proof.
∎

For illustration, consider the 3-CNF formula
\varphi=(v_{1}\vee v_{2}\vee v_{3})\wedge(\neg v_{1}\vee v_{3}\vee v_{4})
and the network
N(\varphi)
shown in Figure

.
Figure

shows the minimal support network
G
of

constructed according to the procedure described in the proof of Theorem

, using the satisfying assignment
(v_{1}^{\ast},v_{2}^{\ast},v_{3}^{\ast},v_{4}^{\ast})=(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{T},\mathsf{T},\mathsf{F})
and the indices
(j_{1},j_{2})=(1,2)
.
As Figure

shows, each reticulation of
G
is contained in a distinct non-trivial block (depicted in red), confirming that
G
is indeed level-1.

Theorem 15
.

If a 3-CNF formula
\varphi
is not satisfiable, then
\mathrm{level}^{\ast}(N(\varphi))\geq 2
holds.

Proof.

Assume that
\varphi
is not satisfiable.
By Lemma
3
, it suffices to show that every element in
\mathcal{B}_{N(\varphi)}
contains a block with at least two reticulations.

Let
G
be an arbitrary element of
\mathcal{B}_{N(\varphi)}
.
By Lemma
12
,
G
can be decomposed into the tree
T
, a minimal support network of each variable gadget
N^{\prime}(v_{m})
, and a minimal support network of each clause gadget
N(C_{i})
.
By the first statement of Lemma
9
, for each
m\in[1,n]
, exactly one of
G^{\prime}_{\mathsf{T}}(v_{m})
and
G^{\prime}_{\mathsf{F}}(v_{m})
is a subgraph of
G
.
This allows us to define a truth assignment
\mathbf{v}\colon\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}\to\{\mathsf{T},\mathsf{F}\}
by
\mathbf{v}(v_{m})=\mathsf{T}
if
G\supseteq G^{\prime}_{\mathsf{T}}(v_{m})
, and
\mathbf{v}(v_{m})=\mathsf{F}
otherwise.
Since
\varphi
is not satisfiable, there exists a clause
C_{i}
such that all three literals
\lambda_{i,1},\lambda_{i,2}
and
\lambda_{i,3}
evaluate to
\mathsf{F}
under
\mathbf{v}
.
Consider such
C_{i}
.

Let
j\in[1,3]
and suppose that
\lambda_{i,j}
is a positive literal
v_{m}
. Since
v_{m}^{\ast}=\mathsf{F}
,
G^{\prime}_{\mathsf{F}}(v_{m})
is used in
G
. Then, by our construction of
N(\varphi)
, the vertices
d_{i,j}
and
e_{i,j}
are located on the directed path from
a_{\neg v_{m}}
to
b_{\neg v_{m}}
.
This path is contained in the non-trivial block of
G^{\prime}_{\mathsf{F}}(v_{m})
by Lemma
9
.
Similarly, if
\lambda_{i,j}=\neg v_{m}
, then
v_{m}^{\ast}=\mathsf{T}
and hence
G^{\prime}_{\mathsf{T}}(v_{m})
is used in
G
;

d_{i,j}
and
e_{i,j}
are located on the directed path from
a_{v_{m}}
to
b_{v_{m}}
.
Lemma
9
implies that this path is contained in the non-trivial block of
G^{\prime}_{\mathsf{T}}(v_{m})
.
Thus, each of the roots of
N(C_{i})
is contained in the non-trivial block of its corresponding variable gadget in
G
.

However, Lemma
10
implies that in any minimal support network of
N(C_{i})
, some reticulation
s_{i,j}
is a descendant of two of these six roots.
Consequently,
s_{i,j}
belongs to the same non-trivial block as the reticulation of the corresponding variable gadget, which yields a block with at least two reticulations. This completes the proof.
∎

By Proposition
13
, Theorems
14
and
15
, a 3-CNF formula

\varphi
is satisfiable if and only if the network
N(\varphi)
is level-
1
-based.
Moreover, the network
N(\varphi)
can be constructed in
O(n+\ell)
time.
We also note that
Level Decision
belongs to NP for any
k\geq 0
, since the level of any
support network
G=(V,E)
can be computed in
O(|V|+|E|)
time by decomposing it into blocks via depth-first search
[
18
]
and counting the reticulations in each block.
Therefore, we obtain the following result.

Theorem 16
.

Level Decision
is NP-complete for
k=1
. Hence,
Level Minimization
is NP-hard.

6
An Extension to the NP-completeness Proof of Level Decision for General

In this section, we show that the proposed reduction in Section
5
can be extended to establish the NP-completeness of Problem

for general

. This is achieved by applying a systematic modification to each variable gadget used in our construction.

For

, we define a rooted almost-binary phylogenetic network

We start with

For any 3-CNF formula

in exactly the same manner as in the construction of

.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we have settled the conjecture posed in
[
26
]
by proving that
Level Minimization
is NP-hard.
In fact, we have established the stronger result that
Level Decision
is NP-complete for every fixed integer
k\geq 1
(Theorem
16
and
18
).
As an immediate consequence, the exact exponential-time algorithm for
Level Minimization
developed in
[
26
]
cannot be improved to a polynomial-time algorithm, unless
\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}
.
Moreover, our hardness result contrasts with the fact that a closely related problem, finding a support network with the fewest reticulations, can be solved in linear time
[
26
]
.

A natural direction for future work is to investigate the parameterized complexity of
Level Minimization
.
Our hardness result rules out fixed-parameter tractability when parameterizing by the base level, unless
\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}
, so future work should instead consider parameters of the input network.
Promising candidates are the
treewidth
and its DAG-oriented variant, the
scanwidth
, both of which tend to be small on phylogenetic networks arising from real biological data
[
3
]
.

Another direction is to seek combinatorial characterizations, or at least necessary conditions, of level-
k
-based networks. Tree-based networks, which coincide with level-0-based networks, admit such a characterization through the maximal zig-zag trail decomposition, namely via the absence of W-fences (Proposition
6
). It would be of interest to obtain analogous results for level-1-based networks and, more generally, for level-
k
-based networks for each
k\geq 1
. We note that our NP-hardness result rules out characterizations as simple as the one for the tree-based networks.
However, even necessary conditions could lead to faster algorithms for
Level Decision
by further restricting the search space beyond
\mathcal{B}_{N}
.
