EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Plant biomass, including agricultural residues, energy crops, and forestry waste, represents one of the
nation's largest renewable carbon resources. If converted efficiently into fuels, chemicals, and materials,
these resources could strengthen U.S. energy security, support domestic manufacturing, reduce waste,
and create economic opportunities in rural communities. However, a major barrier to cost-effective biomass
conversion is the difficulty of breaking down plant cell walls, which evolved to resist biological and chemical
degradation. Current industrial processes often require costly pretreatments and large quantities of
specialized enzymes, limiting the economic viability of many bioenergy and biomanufacturing technologies.
This project focused on a unique group of microorganisms known as anaerobic fungi (AGF), which naturally
inhabit the digestive tracts of cattle, sheep, and other large herbivores. These fungi routinely deconstruct
tough plant material that is difficult for most microbes to utilize and possess one of the most diverse
collections of biomass-degrading enzymes known in nature. Despite their exceptional capabilities,
anaerobic fungi have remained largely inaccessible to modern genetic engineering because tools needed
to study and manipulate their biology did not exist. Developing those tools directly supports the U.S.
Department of Energy Office of Science Biological and Environmental Research (BER) mission to advance
predictive biology, expand understanding of complex biological systems, and enable new approaches for
sustainable bioenergy and biomanufacturing.
The overall goal of this project was to create foundational genetic and epigenetic tools for AGF and use
those tools to identify biological mechanisms responsible for efficient plant biomass deconstruction. The
project achieved several major advances toward this goal.
First, the project generated the first chromosome-scale genome assemblies for multiple anaerobic fungal
species. These high-quality genomic resources overcame longstanding limitations in the field and provide
a foundation for future biological discovery and engineering. The genomes and annotations were released
publicly, making them available to researchers worldwide.
Second, the project established the first reproducible system for heterologous gene expression in anaerobic
fungi, enabling more systematic study of AGF biomass-degrading enzymes. The research demonstrated
that these organisms can naturally take up DNA and can express introduced genes under controlled
conditions. New genetic components, including promoters, selection markers, and fluorescent reporters,
were identified and validated. Although stable genome integration was not achieved during the award
period, the work identified important biological barriers and revealed promising strategies for future
development of permanent genetic engineering methods.
Third, the project advanced understanding of how anaerobic fungi can be used for sustainable
biomanufacturing. We developed a novel bioprocess that combined anaerobic fungi with an engineered
industrial yeast to convert untreated plant biomass directly into fuels and specialty chemicals without
chemical pretreatment. The process achieved greater than 90% carbon utilization efficiency and
demonstrated a new route for converting low-value agricultural residues into higher-value products.
Fourth, the project established foundational knowledge of epigenetic regulation in anaerobic fungi. The
research showed that chemical and microbial signals can alter fungal gene expression and enzyme
production through changes in chromatin state. These discoveries identified potential new approaches for
improving biomass degradation performance without requiring permanent genetic modification.
Beyond direct scientific outcomes, the project produced lasting impacts for the U.S. research enterprise. It
supported the training of 4 doctoral students and 2 postdoctoral scholars, contributed to 6 peer-reviewed
publications, generated publicly available genomic resources, and provided the foundation for subsequent
federally funded research. The chromosome-scale genomes and biological insights generated through this
work have already supported broader community efforts to improve classification and understanding of
anaerobic fungi.
Overall, this project transformed anaerobic fungi from a biologically intriguing but technically inaccessible
group of organisms into an emerging platform for scientific investigation and engineering. By creating
foundational tools, genomic resources, and biological knowledge, the project advanced DOE priorities in
bioenergy, biomanufacturing, and the development of a sustainable circular bioeconomy while enabling
future efforts to harness renewable plant biomass as a source of fuels, chemicals, and materials.

FINAL PROJECT ACCOMPLISHMENTS
This project aimed to develop genetic and epigenetic tools for emerging model anaerobic fungi (AGF) to
enable mechanistic studies of lignocellulose deconstruction and support engineering of these organisms
for direct conversion of untreated lignocellulosic biomass into value-added bioproducts. Following the
Principal Investigator's relocation, the original DOE Early Career Award (DE-SC0020117) was relinquished
and reissued as this continuation award. The scientific objectives remained unchanged:
 1. Develop tools for stable heterologous gene expression in anaerobic fungi.
 2. Interrogate anaerobic fungal genomes via genome editing and cell-free technologies
 3. Manipulate the epigenetic state of anaerobic fungi to optimize lignocellulose degradation.
For clarity, this report summarizes the cumulative accomplishments of both awards. Publications and other
products reported here are limited to the continuation-award period.
Objective 1 Accomplishments
Development of genetic tools for AGF first required improved genomic resources. At project initiation,
available genomes were highly fragmented because of extreme nucleotide composition bias (<20% GC
content) and poor sequencing accuracy in AT-rich regions using conventional short-read approaches. To
overcome these limitations, we combined PacBio long-read sequencing with Hi-C chromosomal contact
mapping to generate the first chromosome-scale genome assemblies for this fungal phylum (Table 1). Hi-
C data also enabled identification and correction of assembly errors. The resulting genome assemblies and
annotations have been made publicly available through the DOE Joint Genome Institute MycoCosm portal,
and comprehensive genome analyses are in preparation for publication.

Building on preliminary observations that AGF zoospores are naturally competent for double-stranded DNA
uptake, we systematically evaluated DNA uptake across fungal life stages using fluorescently labeled linear
and plasmid DNA. Most stages of the AGF life cycle exhibited measurable DNA uptake, with transformation
efficiencies of approximately 16.5%.Using the newly assembled genomes, we identified native regulatory
elements, including promoters, terminators, and nuclear localization sequences, and used these
components to construct the first AGF expression toolkit. These elements enabled heterologous expression
of antibiotic resistance markers and oxygen-independent fluorescent reporters (iLOV and iRFP) (Figures
1-2). This work established the first reproducible method for heterologous gene expression in AGF (Hooker
et al., 2023).
Although linearized DNA constructs produced the strongest expression, gene expression was transient and
required repeated DNA dosing. qRT-PCR analyses demonstrated that introduced constructs neither
integrated into the genome nor replicated autonomously. Multiple efforts to identify autonomous replication
sequences via shotgun cloning were unsuccessful because sufficient genomic library coverage could not
be achieved.
These challenges prompted a deeper investigation of AGF genome organization. Genome-wide sequence
analysis revealed a bipartite architecture consisting of AT-rich and GC-neutral compartments that represent
approximately 80% and 20% of the genome, respectively. Despite occupying a smaller fraction of the

genome, GC-neutral regions are highly gene-dense, exhibit elevated gene expression, and serve as major
targets for horizontally transferred genes. These regions represent promising targets for future gene
integration strategies.
In addition, GC-neutral compartments are enriched in long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposons that may
provide a platform for stable gene delivery. Several of these elements encode capsid proteins capable of
forming virus-like particles (VLPs), potentially facilitating reverse transcription and genome integration. We
successfully expressed transposon-derived proteins in a heterologous host and confirmed VLP assembly.
In parallel, we optimized electroporation methods for protein delivery, creating a foundation for future
development of stable genetic transformation methods.
The chromosome-scale genome assemblies generated through this project have also supported broader
community efforts to improve AGF taxonomy and classification and have been used in multiple high-impact
studies (Elshahed et al., 2022; Young et al., 2025).
Objective 2 Accomplishments
In parallel with developing genetic tools, we explored the metabolic potential of AGF for lignocellulosic
bioconversion. Working with Ian Wheeldon (University of California, Riverside), we developed a two-stage
bioprocess in which AGF deconstruct plant biomass and an engineered Kluyveromyces marxianus strain
converts the resulting metabolites into fuels and specialty chemicals (Figure 3). This system demonstrated,
for the first time, production of advanced biofuels and fragrance molecules directly from untreated
lignocellulosic biomass without chemical pretreatment. Unlike prior approaches, our process utilized both
liberated sugars and fungal fermentation products, increasing overall carbon utilization efficiency to greater
than 90% while maintaining extensive biomass deconstruction. These findings were published in
Biotechnology Progress (DOI: 10.1002/btpr.3172). We also expressed AGF mevalonate-pathway enzymes
in Escherichia coli and identified enzyme homologs that improved pathway performance while establishing
design principles for their implementation (Figure 4). This work was published in Microorganisms (DOI:
10.3390/microorganisms9091986).
Because stable genome editing was not achieved during the award period, we investigated cell-free protein
expression as an alternative platform for AGF gene characterization. Multiple commercial bacterial- and
plant-based cell-free systems produced only negligible yields or inactive proteins. Similarly, recombinant
expression in E. coli was successful for fewer than 20% of tested AGF genes. To identify barriers to AGF
protein production, we applied untargeted proteomics to characterize post-translational modifications in
native AGF proteins. These analyses revealed features that likely contribute to expression and folding
challenges and informed selection of alternative host systems for future studies.We also developed a
reporter platform that enables high-throughput detection of unfolded or insoluble recombinant proteins
expressed in AGF (Figure 5). This system will support large-scale functional screening of AGF genes and
generation of training datasets for machine-learning approaches aimed at predicting recombinant protein
tractability.
Objective 3 Accomplishments
This project established foundational knowledge of epigenetic regulation in AGF and its role in biomass
deconstruction. We demonstrated that chemical inhibition of epigenetic regulatory pathways alters
carbohydrate-active enzyme (CAZyme) secretion and is associated with global changes in histone post-
translational modifications (Figure 6). To support quantitative analysis, we developed plate-based
workflows for measuring AGF epigenetic responses. In collaboration with the O'Malley laboratory at the
University of California, Santa Barbara, we further showed that interactions with microbial partners induce
histone modifications associated with secondary metabolite production. This work was published in mBio
(DOI: 10.1128/mBio.01442-21). To systematically characterize these responses, we examined the effects
of a panel of epigenetic modulators using RNA sequencing. Although data analysis remains ongoing, results
indicate that butyrate, a native rumen metabolite, and suberoylanilide hydroxamic acid (SAHA) are potent
regulators of AGF transcription. In Neocallimastix frontalis var. giraffae Gf-Ma, these treatments significantly
altered expression of more than 100 genes, including nearly two dozen CAZyme genes. These findings
suggest that epigenetic regulation may contribute to the enhanced cellulolytic performance frequently
observed in microbial cocultures and may provide a practical strategy for improving industrial biomass
conversion processes.

Project Outcomes
In addition to the scientific advances described above, this project generated significant workforce-
development, research-infrastructure, and community impacts. The award supported completion of 2 PhD
dissertations and provided research training and financial support for 2 additional doctoral students and 2
postdoctoral scholars at the University of Delaware. Of these trainees, 1 PhD graduate and 1 postdoctoral
scholar have pursued academic careers at primarily undergraduate institutions, while the remaining
trainees entered industry positions focused on development of non-model microorganisms for applications
in animal health and food security. The combined Early Career and continuation awards produced 6 peer-
reviewed publications, with 2 additional manuscripts currently in preparation, and supported numerous
invited and contributed presentations. The genomic resources, genetic toolkits, and biological insights
developed through this project also provided the foundation for a subsequent NSF-funded effort aimed at
extending these capabilities to additional AGF species. Collectively, the project delivered new genomic
resources, foundational genetic and epigenetic technologies, and mechanistic insight into anaerobic fungal
biology while training the next generation of researchers and establishing a platform for continued advances
in bioenergy, biomanufacturing, and circular bioeconomy applications.
