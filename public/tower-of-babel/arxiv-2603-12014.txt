Array Geometry-Centric Axial Sidelobe Interference Analysis for Near-Field Multi-User MIMO
Ahmed Hussain 1 , Asmaa Abdallah 1 , Abdulkadir Celik 2 , Ahmed M. Eltawil 1

Abstract
With the deployment of large antenna arrays at high-frequency bands, future wireless communication systems are likely to operate in the radiative near-field (NF). Unlike far-field beam steering, NF beams can be focused on a spatial region with finite depth, enabling user multiplexing in both range and angle. In NF multiuser multiple-input multiple-output (MU-MIMO) systems, achievable rates are limited by interference arising from sidelobes in both the axial (range) and lateral (angle) dimensions. This work investigates how axial sidelobes (ASLs) vary with array geometry. Closed-form array gain expressions are derived to characterize ASLs for uniform planar arrays. Analytical results show that the uniform square array (USA) yields the lowest ASLs, followed by the uniform concentric circular array (UCCA), uniform linear array (ULA), and uniform circular array (UCA). Specifically, the USA achieves a peak sidelobe level (PSLL) of − 17.6 -17.6 dB versus − 7.9 -7.9 dB for the UCA. Numerical simulations confirm that the USA provides superior sidelobe suppression and highest sumrate performance.

Introduction

With the growing adoption of massive
MIMO
(
MIMO
) in
5G
(

) communications, future networks are anticipated to embrace
UM
(
UM
)-
MIMO

NF
(

Wave propagation in the radiative

FF
(

beamforming, which steers beams only toward angular directions,

[
1
]

NF

UE

The

(

)-

2

. Under the constraint of a fixed number of antenna elements, elongated

(

s and

s
[
3
]
. In a

[

,
5

[
6
]
, but they cannot form finite-depth beams at boresight. This limitation is addressed by

beams at boresight while slightly reducing

coverage in coplanar directions
[
7
]
. Consistent with these observations, the impact of array geometry on beamdepth is pronounced under a fixed element count constraint, but becomes less significant when the aperture length is fixed

]

II
System Model

We consider a
MU
-
MIMO
communication system, where a
BS
(
BS
) equipped with
N
isotropic antennas simultaneously serves
K
single-antenna
UE
s. The received signal at the
{k}^{\text{th}}

UE
is expressed as

y_{k}=\sqrt{\gamma}\,\mathbf{w}_{k}\mathbf{h}_{k}s_{k}+\sqrt{\gamma}\sum_{j=1,j\neq k}^{K}\mathbf{w}_{j}\mathbf{h}_{k}s_{j}+z_{k},

(1)

where
\gamma=P/N
denotes the average transmit
SNR
(
SNR
),
P
is the total transmit power,
s_{k}
is the unit-power transmit symbol,
\mathbf{w}_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{1\times N}
is the precoding vector, and
z_{k}\!\sim\!\mathcal{CN}(0,1)
represents complex Gaussian noise. The
NF
channel vector
\mathbf{h}_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times 1}
in (
1
) is modeled as

\mathbf{h}_{k}=\beta_{k}\,\mathbf{b}(\varphi_{k},\theta_{k},r_{k}),

(2)

where
\beta_{k}
is the complex path gain and
\mathbf{b}(\cdot)
is the
NF
array response vector focused at azimuth angle
\varphi_{k}
, elevation angle
\theta_{k}
, and range
r_{k}
. In
mmWave
(
mmWave
) systems, the
LoS
(
LoS
) path typically exceeds other multipath components by about
4\!-\!5\,\mathrm{dB}
. Hence, we focus on the
LoS
path and set
\beta_{k}=1
, for simplicity. The normalized
NF
array response vector in (
2
) is given by

\mathbf{b}(\varphi,\theta,r)=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\!\left[e^{-j\nu(r^{(0)}-r)},\dots,e^{-j\nu(r^{(N-1)}-r)}\right]^{\mathsf{T}},

(3)

where
\nu=\tfrac{2\pi}{\lambda}
is the wavenumber,
\lambda
is the wavelength, and
r^{(n)}
denotes the distance between the
UE
and the
{n}^{\text{th}}
antenna.

II-A

Achievable Rate

The achievable rate for the
{k}^{\text{th}}

UE
is expressed as

\mathcal{R}_{k}=\log_{2}\!\left(1+\operatorname{SINR}_{k}\right),

(4)

where,
SINR
(
SINR
) is

\operatorname{SINR}_{k}=\frac{\gamma|\mathbf{w}_{k}\mathbf{h}_{k}|^{2}}{1+\gamma\sum_{j=1,j\neq k}^{K}|\mathbf{w}_{j}\mathbf{h}_{k}|^{2}}.

(5)

Based on (
4
), the achievable sumrate is given by
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{sum}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathcal{R}_{k}
. Assuming maximum-ratio transmission, the precoding vector is defined as
\mathbf{w}_{k}=\mathbf{h}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}/\sqrt{N}
, where
(\cdot)^{\mathsf{H}}
denotes the Hermitian operator. The maximum beamforming gain
\left|{\mathbf{w}_{k}\mathbf{h}_{k}}\right|=N
, is achieved with the unnormalized
NF
array response vector in (
3
). Accordingly, (
5
) is rewritten as

\mathcal{R}_{k}=\log_{2}\!\left(1+\frac{\gamma N}{1+\gamma N\sum_{j=1,j\neq k}^{K}\mathcal{I}_{a,kj}^{2}}\right),

(6)

where
a\in\{\mathrm{ula},\mathrm{ura},\mathrm{uca},\mathrm{ucca}\}
denotes the array configuration, and
\mathcal{I}_{a,kj}
represents the interference term, expressed as the normalized cross-correlation between the desired and interfering channels

\mathcal{I}_{a,kj}=\tfrac{1}{N}\big|\mathbf{h}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}(\varphi_{k},\theta_{k},r_{k})\mathbf{h}_{j}(\varphi_{j},\theta_{j},r_{j})\big|,

(7)

which evidently determines the achievable sumrate. Specifically, decreasing
\sum_{j=1,\,j\neq k}^{K}\mathcal{I}^{2}_{a,kj}
suppresses inter-user interference and improves rate performance, whereas larger values of this term degrade the achievable rate. Moreover, (
7
) can be analyzed in both angular and range domains. The angular-domain characteristics remain unchanged irrespective of whether the array operates in the
NF
or
FF
and have been extensively studied in prior work. In contrast, this study characterizes interference in the range domain.

II-B

Axial Sidelobes

In the downlink
MU
-
MIMO
system, the
BS
simultaneously forms multiple
NF
beams toward different
UE
s. Each beam comprises a mainlobe that delivers the desired signal and
ASL
s that cause inter-user interference. To quantify this sidelobe-induced interference, we formulate the axial beam pattern of the
BS
focused on a single
UE
. Assuming the
UE
is located at a distance
r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{0}}}
from the
BS
, the axial beam pattern is given by

\mathcal{G}_{a}(\varphi,\theta,r)=\left|\mathbf{b}^{\mathsf{H}}(\varphi,\theta,r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{0}}})\,\mathbf{b}(\varphi,\theta,r)\right|^{2},\quad r\in[2D,r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{RD}}}],

(8)

where
D
denotes the aperture length of the antenna array, and
r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{RD}}}=\tfrac{2D^{2}}{\lambda}
is the Rayleigh distance. The aperture length
D
is defined as the largest dimension of the array. The minimum distance is set to
2D
to ensure negligible amplitude variations across the array and employ a
USW
(
USW
) model. The
SLL
s are evaluated using the following metrics:

a)

Peak Sidelobe Level
:
The
PSL
quantifies the strongest sidelobe relative to the mainlobe and indicates the interference susceptibility from closely spaced users.

\mathrm{PSLL}=10\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{\max_{r\in\mathcal{S}}\mathcal{G}_{a}(\varphi,\theta,r)}{\mathcal{G}_{a}(\varphi,\theta,r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{0}}})}\right),

(9)

where
\mathcal{S}
denotes the set of points in the sidelobe region.

b)

Integrated Sidelobe Level
:
The
ISL
measures the total sidelobe power relative to the mainlobe, reflecting the average interference experienced by all users.

\mathrm{ISLL}=10\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{\int_{\mathcal{S}}|\mathcal{G}_{a}(\varphi,\theta,r)|^{2}\,dr}{\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\mathcal{G}_{a}(\varphi,\theta,r)|^{2}\,dr}\right),

(10)

where
\mathcal{M}
denotes the set of points in the mainlobe region.

III
Axial Beam Pattern

In this section, the axial beam pattern is formulated, and the
PSL
is derived for each array configuration. The analysis begins with the
ULA
, as it directly extends to the
URA
.

III-A

Uniform Linear Array

We consider a
ULA
with
N
antenna elements as shown in Fig.
2
. The
UE
is located at a distance
r
from the array center. The distance between
UE
and the
{n}^{\text{th}}
antenna of the
ULA
, obtained via the law of cosines, is

r^{(n)}=\sqrt{r^{2}+n^{2}d^{2}-2rnd\sin(\varphi)},

(11)

where
\varphi
denotes the azimuth angle. By substituting
r^{(n)}
from (
11
) into (
3
), the
{n}^{\text{th}}
component of the normalized
NF
array response vector for the
ULA
is given by

\mathbf{b}_{n}(\varphi,r)=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{N}}e^{-j\nu\left(\sqrt{r^{2}+n^{2}d^{2}-2rnd\sin(\varphi)}-r\right)}.

(12)

Using a second-order Taylor expansion
\sqrt{1+t}\approx 1+\tfrac{t}{2}-\tfrac{1}{8}t^{2}
, the distance difference is approximated as
r^{(n)}-r\approx nd\sin(\varphi)-\frac{n^{2}d^{2}}{2r}\cos^{2}(\varphi)

[
7
]
. Thus, the
NF
array response vector in (
12
) simplifies to

\mathbf{b}_{n}(\varphi,r)\approx\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{N}}e^{-j\nu\left(nd\sin(\varphi)-\tfrac{n^{2}d^{2}}{2r}\cos^{2}(\varphi)\right)}.

(13)

Substituting (
13
) into the general definition of the axial beam pattern in (
8
), the array gain
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{ula}}}
is obtained as

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{ula}}}(\varphi,r)

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbf{b}^{\mathsf{H}}(\varphi,r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{0}}})\,\mathbf{b}(\varphi,r)\right|^{2},

(14)

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=-N/2}^{N/2}e^{-j\nu n^{2}d^{2}\cos^{2}(\varphi)r_{\mathrm{eff}}}\right|^{2},

(15)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{ula}}}(\gamma)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{C^{2}(\gamma)+S^{2}(\gamma)}{\gamma^{2}},

(16)

where
r_{\mathrm{eff}}=\big|\tfrac{r-r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{0}}}}{2rr_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{0}}}}\big|
. Introducing the variable transformation
x=\sqrt{\tfrac{n^{2}d^{2}\cos^{2}(\varphi)}{\lambda}\,\bigg|\tfrac{r-r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{0}}}}{rr_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{0}}}}\bigg|}
, yields a Fresnel-integral representation of the array gain, with
\gamma=\sqrt{\tfrac{N^{2}d^{2}\cos^{2}(\varphi)}{\lambda}r_{\mathrm{eff}}}
, and
C(\gamma)=\int_{0}^{\gamma}\cos\!\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}x^{2}\right)\,dx
,
S(\gamma)=\int_{0}^{\gamma}\sin\!\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}x^{2}\right)\,dx
, are the Fresnel cosine and sine integrals, respectively. Moreover,
\gamma
is a dimensionless parameter that captures the combined effects of array aperture, wavelength, angle, and the distance mismatch between the focusing point and the actual user position.

The
PSL
of a uniformly weighted array is determined by the secondary maxima of the gain function. As illustrated in Fig.
3
(blue curve), the Fresnel-based gain function
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{ula}}}(\gamma)
in (
16
) attains its global maximum at
\gamma=0
, and is monotonically decreasing. The location of the first sidelobe is obtained by differentiating
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{ula}}}(\gamma)
with respect to
\gamma
, and solving for its roots. The first positive root occurs at
\gamma_{\mathrm{PSLL}}\approx 2.28
, where the gain in (
16
) evaluates to
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{ula}}}(\gamma_{\mathrm{PSLL}})\approx 0.1323
, yielding
\mathrm{PSLL}\approx 10\log_{10}(0.1323)\approx-8.7\,\mathrm{dB}
.

III-B

Uniform Rectangular Array

We consider a
URA
comprising
N=N_{1}N_{2}
antenna elements, where
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
denote the number of antenna elements along the
y
-axis and
z
-axis, respectively. The inter-element spacing along both axes is uniform and set to
d=\tfrac{\lambda}{2}
. The width-to-height ratio of the
URA
is defined as
\eta=\tfrac{N_{2}}{N_{1}}
. By varying
\eta
, different array configurations can be realized. In particular, a
USA
is obtained when
\eta=1
, while a
ULA
corresponds to
\eta\gg 1
or
\eta\ll 1
. The aperture length of the
URA
, derived from the Pythagorean theorem, is given by
D=d\sqrt{N_{1}^{2}+N_{2}^{2}}
. For brevity, we omit the intermediate steps in deriving the array gain. The final expression for the array gain of a

is given in terms of Fresnel functions as
[
8
]

\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{ura}}}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})=\frac{\left[C^{2}(\gamma_{1})+S^{2}(\gamma_{1})\right]\left[C^{2}(\gamma_{2})+S^{2}(\gamma_{2})\right]}{(\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2})^{2}},

(17)

where
\gamma_{1}=\sqrt{\tfrac{N_{1}^{2}d^{2}\beta_{1}}{2\lambda}r_{\mathrm{eff}}}
and
\gamma_{2}=\sqrt{\tfrac{N_{2}^{2}d^{2}\beta_{2}}{2\lambda}r_{\mathrm{eff}}}
, with
\beta_{1}=1-\sin^{2}\theta\sin^{2}\varphi
and
\beta_{2}=1-\cos^{2}\theta
. The gain expression in (
17
) is the product of two one-dimensional Fresnel functions, each having the same structure as the
ULA
pattern in (
16
).

To emulate
URA
configurations ranging from a
ULA
to a
USA
, we evaluate (
17
) for
\hat{\eta}=\tfrac{\gamma_{2}}{\gamma_{1}}\in(0,1]
. The corresponding
PSL
values are computed for each configuration, as shown in Fig.
4
. For analytical clarity, we focus on the boresight case to highlight the impact of array geometry. In this scenario,
\hat{\eta}=\sqrt{\tfrac{N_{2}^{2}\beta_{2}}{N_{1}^{2}\beta_{1}}}
, which simplifies at boresight
(\varphi=0,\theta=\tfrac{\pi}{2})
to
\hat{\eta}=\tfrac{N_{2}}{N_{1}}
. As illustrated in Fig.
4
, when
\hat{\eta}<0.3
, the
PSL
converges to
-8.7\,\mathrm{dB}
, consistent with the
ULA
case. Conversely, when
\hat{\eta}=1
, corresponding to a
USA
, the
PSL
decreases to
-17.6\,\mathrm{dB}
. Furthermore, for
\hat{\eta}\in[0.7,0.83]
, the
PSL
falls below
-17.6\,\mathrm{dB}
; however, the edges of the mainlobe for these specific configurations exhibit minor distortions and warrant further investigation. It is noteworthy that the
beamdepth is minimized for a
ULA
and maximized for a
USA

[
8
,
3
]
. In contrast, the
PSL
exhibits the opposite trend, attaining its minimum for a
USA
and maximum for a
ULA
.

Uniform Circular Array

We consider a
UCA
of radius
R
with
N
antenna elements located in the
xy
-plane, as shown in Fig.
5
. In polar coordinates, the
{n}^{\text{th}}
element is positioned at
(R,\varphi_{n})
with
\varphi_{n}=\tfrac{2\pi n}{N}
. The arc length between adjacent elements is
d=R\Delta\varphi=R\tfrac{2\pi}{N}
, which gives the UCA radius as
R=\tfrac{Nd}{2\pi}

[
10
]
. The
UE
is located at a distance
r
from the center of the array, subtending azimuth angle
\varphi
and elevation angle
\theta
.

Fig. 5

A
UCA
in the
xy
-plane and a

UE
is located
(r,\varphi,\theta)
.

The distance between
UE
and the
{n}^{\text{th}}
antenna element, as illustrated in Fig.
5
, is given by

\displaystyle r^{(n)}=\sqrt{r^{2}+R^{2}-2rR\cos(\psi_{n})},

(18)

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}\sqrt{r^{2}+R^{2}-2rR\sin\theta\cos(\varphi-\varphi_{n})},

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{\approx}r-R\sin\theta\cos(\varphi-\varphi_{n})+\tfrac{R^{2}}{2r}\Big(1-\sin^{2}\theta\cos^{2}(\varphi-\varphi_{n})\Big).

(19)

Here, (a) follows from
\cos(\psi_{n})=\hat{a}_{\rho}\cdot\hat{a}_{r}=(\hat{a}_{x}\cos\varphi_{n}+\hat{a}_{y}\sin\varphi_{n})\cdot(\hat{a}_{x}\sin\theta\cos\varphi+\hat{a}_{y}\sin\theta\sin\varphi+\hat{a}_{z}\cos\theta)=\sin\theta\cos(\varphi-\varphi_{n})

[
9
]
. Furthermore, (b) is obtained by applying a second-order Taylor expansion around
r
, following the approach in (
13
). The
{n}^{\text{th}}
component of the normalized
NF
array response vector for a
UCA
, based on (
19
), is given by

\small\mathbf{b}_{n}(\varphi,\theta,r)\approx\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{N}}e^{-j\nu\!\left(R\sin\theta\cos(\varphi-\varphi_{n})-\tfrac{R^{2}}{2r}\big(1-\sin^{2}\theta\cos^{2}(\varphi-\varphi_{n})\big)\right)}.

(20)

The array gain for the
UCA
is obtained by substituting (
20
) into the general definition of the axial pattern defined in (
8
)

\small\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{uca}}}(\varphi,\theta,r)=\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}e^{\,j\tfrac{2\pi}{\lambda}\tfrac{R^{2}}{2}r_{\mathrm{eff}}\left(1-\sin^{2}\theta\cos^{2}(\varphi-\varphi_{n})\right)}\right|^{2},

(21)

where
r_{\mathrm{eff}}=\big|\tfrac{r-r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{0}}}}{r\,r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{0}}}}\big|
. For analytical tractability, we define
\zeta=\tfrac{\pi}{\lambda}\tfrac{R^{2}}{2}\,r_{\mathrm{eff}}\sin^{2}\theta
. Substituting
R=\tfrac{D_{\scalebox{0.5}{uca}}}{2}
, where
D_{\scalebox{0.5}{uca}}
denotes the aperture diameter, and the Rayleigh distance
{r}_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{RD}}}^{\scalebox{0.5}{uca}}=\tfrac{2D_{\scalebox{0.5}{uca}}^{2}}{\lambda}
, this simplifies to
\zeta=\tfrac{\pi{r}_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{RD}}}^{\scalebox{0.5}{uca}}}{16}\,r_{\mathrm{eff}}\sin^{2}\theta
. Furthermore, by exploiting the inherent rotational symmetry of the
UCA
, we set
\varphi=0
without loss of generality, yielding the simplified array gain

\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{uca}}}(\theta,r)\approx\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}e^{-2j\zeta\cos^{2}(\varphi_{n})}\right|^{2}.

(22)

Using the trigonometric identity
\cos(2x)=2\cos^{2}(x)-1
,

\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{uca}}}(\theta,r)\approx\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}e^{-j\zeta\cos(2\varphi_{n})}\right|^{2}.

(23)

For large
N
, the summation approaches the integral
[
9
]

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}e^{-j\zeta\cos(2\varphi_{n})}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{-j\zeta\cos(\varphi)}\,d\varphi=J_{0}(\zeta),

(24)

where
J_{0}(\zeta)
is the zeroth-order Bessel function of the first kind. Hence, the axial pattern of the
UCA
is approximated as

\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{uca}}}(\theta,r)\approx\left|J_{0}(\zeta)\right|^{2},\ \text{where }\quad\zeta=\tfrac{\pi{r}_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{RD}}}^{\scalebox{0.5}{uca}}}{16}\,r_{\mathrm{eff}}\sin^{2}\theta.

(25)

To compute
PSL
for the
UCA
, we utilize the following asymptotic expansion of the Bessel function
[
11
]
:

J_{0}(\zeta)\sim\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\pi\zeta}}\cos\!\big(\zeta-\tfrac{\pi}{4}\big).

(26)

The maxima occur when the cosine argument is an integer multiple of
\pi
, i.e.,
\zeta-\tfrac{\pi}{4}=k\pi\;\Longrightarrow\;\zeta=k\pi+\tfrac{\pi}{4},\;k\in[0,1,2,\ldots]
. The first sidelobe corresponds to
k=1
, which yields
\zeta_{\mathrm{PSLL}}=\tfrac{5\pi}{4}
. At this point, the gain function evaluates to
|J_{0}(\zeta_{\mathrm{PSLL}})|^{2}=\tfrac{8}{5\pi^{2}}\approx 0.162
, resulting in a
PSL
for the
UCA
as
\mathrm{PSLL}\approx 10\log_{10}(0.162)\approx-7.9~\mathrm{dB}
.

)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{ucca}}}(\varphi,\theta,r)

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{m}}\mathrm{e}^{\,j\tfrac{2\pi}{\lambda}\tfrac{R_{m}^{2}}{2}\,r_{\mathrm{eff}}\big(1-\sin^{2}\theta\cos^{2}(\varphi-\varphi_{(m,n)})\big)}\right|^{2},

(27)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathrm{e}^{\,j\tfrac{2\pi}{\lambda}\tfrac{R_{m}^{2}}{2}\big(1-\tfrac{1}{2}\sin^{2}\theta\big)r_{\mathrm{eff}}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\times\sum_{n=1}^{N_{m}}\mathrm{e}^{\,-j\tfrac{2\pi}{\lambda}\tfrac{R_{m}^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}{4}\cos\big(2(\varphi-\varphi_{(m,n)})\big)\,r_{\mathrm{eff}}}\right|^{2},

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\approx}}\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathrm{e}^{\,j\,2\zeta_{m}\big(\tfrac{1}{\sin^{2}\theta}-\tfrac{1}{2}\big)}\,N_{m}J_{0}(\zeta_{m})\right|^{2}.

In (a), we apply the identity
\cos^{2}x=\tfrac{1}{2}(1+\cos 2x)
to separate the terms.
Then, in (b), we define
\zeta_{m}\triangleq\tfrac{\pi}{\lambda}\tfrac{R_{m}^{2}}{2}\,r_{\mathrm{eff}}\sin^{2}\theta
.
When the ring radii are chosen as
R_{m}=\tfrac{m}{M}\tfrac{D_{\scalebox{0.5}{ucca}}}{2}
, and with
{r}_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{RD}}}^{\scalebox{0.5}{ucca}}=\tfrac{2D_{\scalebox{0.5}{ucca}}^{2}}{\lambda}
denoting the Rayleigh distance of the outermost ring, this reduces to
\zeta_{m}=\tfrac{\pi{r}_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{RD}}}^{\scalebox{0.5}{ucca}}}{16}\tfrac{m^{2}}{M^{2}}\,r_{\mathrm{eff}}\sin^{2}\theta
. Furthermore,
J_{0}(\zeta_{m})
is obtained from the summation over the
{m}^{\text{th}}
ring, and its derivation is similar to the
UCA
case, as explained in the previous subsection.

The gain expression in (
27
) for the
UCCA
is intractable due to the summation over
m
. To simplify, we consider the boresight case,
\theta=0^{\circ}
, where
J_{0}(0)=1
, and replace the summation with an integral, yielding the following expressions:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{ucca}}}(\varphi,\theta,r)\Big|_{\theta=0}{\approx}

\displaystyle\left|\frac{N_{M}}{MN}\int_{0}^{M}m\,e^{-j\frac{\xi}{M^{2}}m^{2}}\,dm\right|^{2},

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\approx}

\displaystyle\left|\frac{N_{M}}{MN}\cdot\frac{M^{2}}{2j\xi}\big(1-e^{-j\xi}\big)\right|^{2},

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{\approx}

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\sin(\xi/2)}{\xi/2}\right|^{2},

(28)

where
\xi=\tfrac{\pi{r}_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{RD}}}^{\scalebox{0.5}{ucca}}}{8}r_{\mathrm{eff}}
. In (a) we apply
\int m\,e^{jam^{2}}\,dm=\frac{1}{2ja}e^{jam^{2}}
. We set
\tfrac{N_{M}M}{N}=1
in (b) and
\lvert 1-e^{-j\xi}\rvert^{2}=4\sin^{2}(\xi/2)
simplifies to the sinc form.

The

), given by
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{ucca}}}(\xi)\approx\left|\tfrac{\sin(\xi/2)}{\xi/2}\right|^{2}

\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{ucca}}}(\xi_{\mathrm{PSLL}})\approx\left(\tfrac{1}{3\pi/2}\right)^{2}\approx 0.045
, which yields
\mathrm{PSLL}\approx 10\log_{10}(0.045)\approx-13.46~\mathrm{dB}

IV
Simulation Results

In this section, we compare the
SLL
in terms of
PSL
and
ISL
, and validate the theoretical analysis through numerical simulations. Subsequently, we compare the achievable sumrate across the array geometries.

IV-A

Sidelobe level vs. Array Geometry

We consider
ULA
,
USA
,
UCA
, and
UCCA
, each with an aperture length of
D=1.26\,\mathrm{m}
and operating at
f=15\,\mathrm{GHz}
. Since the aperture length is fixed, the Rayleigh distance is identical across all configurations and equals
r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{RD}}}\approx 162\,\mathrm{m}
. Industry trends indicate that aperture length is a more critical constraint than the number of antenna elements. For example, Qualcomm recently unveiled a 4096-element Giga-
MIMO
prototype operating at
13\,\mathrm{GHz}
within a form factor comparable to existing 256-element
5G

BS
s
[
12
]
, illustrating the broader trend of integrating large-scale arrays into practical form factors.

The axial patterns for the array configurations are shown in Fig.
7
, with the
NF
beam focused at a distance of
\tfrac{r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{RD}}}}{40}
and at boresight for all geometries except the
UCA
. For the
UCA
, the coplanar direction is considered, as it does not support
NF
beams in the boresight direction. The selected focusing directions also correspond to the best-case
PSL
for each array configuration.

Unlike the
FF
case, where the mainlobe is symmetric around the steered angle, the
NF
mainlobe is asymmetric around the focal distance
r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{0}}}
. The left and right
3
-dB points of the mainlobe are denoted by
r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{0}}}^{\min}
and
r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{0}}}^{\max}
, respectively. In practice, the right interval,
r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{0}}}^{\max}-r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{0}}}
, is larger than the left interval,
r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{0}}}-r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{0}}}^{\min}
. However, when expressed in terms of reciprocal distances, these intervals become symmetric, i.e.,
\left|{r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{0}}}^{\max}-r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{0}}}}\right|>\left|{r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{0}}}-r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{0}}}^{\min}}\right|
but
\lvert\tfrac{1}{r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{0}}}^{\max}}-\tfrac{1}{r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{0}}}}\rvert=\lvert\tfrac{1}{r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{0}}}}-\tfrac{1}{r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{0}}}^{\min}}\rvert
. This asymmetry is not evident in Fig.
7
due to the logarithmic scaling of the range axis. The sidelobes preceding the mainlobe are termed
forelobes
, while those following it are termed
aftlobes
. Forelobes and aftlobes differ in both width and number, with forelobes generally being more numerous and of higher magnitude. In general, forelobes primarily interfere with
NF

UE
s, whereas aftlobes affect
FF

UE
s.

IV-B

Achievable Sumrate

We perform Monte Carlo simulations to compare the achievable sumrate across different array configurations under the constraint of a fixed aperture length. The carrier frequency is set to
f=15\,\mathrm{GHz}
, with an aperture length of
D=1.26\,\mathrm{m}
and a Rayleigh distance of
r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{RD}}}\approx 162\,\mathrm{m}
. The number of antenna elements
N
for each array geometry is selected to achieve an equal aperture length. Specifically, for the
ULA
,
N=128
; for the
UCA
,
N=400
; for the
UCCA
, the outermost ring comprises
N_{M}=400
elements with
M=40
rings, yielding a total of
N=\sum_{m=1}^{M}N_{m}=8200
elements; and for the
USA
,
N=7225
. For each array configuration, five
UE
s are uniformly distributed within the range interval
\mathcal{U}(2D,r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{RD}}})
.

Fig.
9
shows the achievable sumrate with respect to
SNR
for all array geometries. Unsurprisingly, the
USA
achieves the highest sumrate, followed by the
UCCA
,
ULA
, and finally the
UCA
. The superior performance of the
USA
stems from its lower
SLL
s, which ensures that different
UE
s experience minimal inter-user interference. Furthermore, the sumrate results are consistent with the
SLL
comparison across different geometries in the previous subsection, thereby validating the impact of
SLL
s on sumrate performance.

V
Conclusion

In this paper, we analyzed how
ASL
s vary with array geometry. We derived array gain expressions for uniform planar arrays and used them to characterize the
PSL
. Our analysis demonstrated that the
USA
yields the lowest
ASL
s, followed by the
UCCA
,
ULA
, and
UCA
. Numerical simulations further confirmed that the
USA
attains the highest sumrate, consistent with the
SLL
s analysis. As future work, windowing methods may be explored for suppressing
ASL
s
[
13
]
. Furthermore, flexible antenna arrays may be investigated to reduce
SLL
s across both axial and lateral dimensions simultaneously.
