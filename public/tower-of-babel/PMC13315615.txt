Results from the Chinese Taipei (Taiwan) 2025 report card on physical activity for children and youth
Authors: Chen-Kang Chang, Ching-Lin Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/objectiveThis study presents the third iteration of the Taiwan Report Card, evaluating 10 core indicators from the Global Matrix framework and one additional indicator. The objective is to provide a longitudinal assessment of national physical activity trends and to examine the relationship between institutional support and actual behavioral outcomes.MethodsA comprehensive synthesis of national survey data and government statistics published between 2022 and 2025 was conducted to assign grades for individuals aged 6–18 years. Data sources included the Health Surveillance of Teenagers, Census of Physical Education, Student Participation in Sports Survey, and Sports Statistics. Grading was based on standardized benchmarks established by the Active Healthy Kids Global Alliance.ResultsThe 2025 grades for the indicators are Overall Physical Activity (F), Organized Sport (C–), Active Play (INC), Active Transportation (INC), Sedentary Behavior (D–), Physical Fitness (INC), Family and Peers (INC), School (A+), Community and the Built Environment (B+), Government Strategies and Investments (A–), and Body Mass Index (B–). These findings reveal that while Taiwan maintains exemplary levels of institutional and environmental support, actual physical activity levels among youth remain critically low.ConclusionsA persistent disconnect exists between strong government and school-level support and the behavioral reality of Taiwanese youth. Despite strong infrastructure and policy frameworks, intensive academic schedules and sedentary study habits continue to serve as significant barriers. Future interventions must address these cultural and structural constraints to effectively translate robust institutional support into active lifestyles for the next generation.

Background

Regular physical activity is fundamental to the healthy development of children and adolescents, providing critical benefits for physical, mental, and cognitive health. Sustained engagement in physical activity during childhood and adolescence provides critical physiological and psychological benefits, including optimal cardiorespiratory fitness, enhanced metabolic health, and the prevention of early-onset obesity. Furthermore, regular movement behaviors are strongly associated with improved cognitive function, reduced anxiety, and superior academic performance, establishing a vital foundation for long-term health and development.1,2 Despite these well-documented advantages, physical inactivity remains a significant global health challenge. Approximately 81% of adolescents worldwide fail to meet the World Health Organization (WHO) recommendation of 60 min of daily moderate-to-vigorous physical activity.3 This trend is particularly pronounced in the high-income Asia-Pacific region where inactivity rate reaches 92.2%.3 The Global Matrix 4.0 concluded that, across the 57 participating countries, an average of only 27% to 33% of children and adolescents met the physical activity recommendations established by the WHO.4

In Taiwan, historical data from the 2018 and 2022 Global Matrix report cycles—which yielded consistent “F" grades for Overall Physical Activity—confirm that activity levels remain alarmingly low.5,6 The societal burden of physical inactivity among Taiwanese youth is further compounded by its severe long-term health consequences. The 2010–2011 Nutrition and Health Survey in Taiwan indicated that 4.1% of adolescents meet the criteria for Metabolic Syndrome—a critical public health challenge in Taiwan characterized by rising rates of juvenile obesity, hypertension, and insulin resistance.7 Because sedentary habits established during youth heavily predict chronic disease trajectories in adulthood, evaluating national physical activity levels is critical to mitigating these growing health crises.

In response to the global epidemic of physical inactivity in children and adolescents, the Active Healthy Kids Global Alliance launched the Global Matrix initiative in 2014.8 This harmonized grading framework allows jurisdictions to benchmark progress across ten core indicators, identify surveillance gaps, and advocate for data-driven policy changes. This 2025 Taiwan Report Card represents the third version of this national assessment. By synthesizing data from 2022 to 2025, this report aims to identify persistent barriers and provide evidence-based recommendations to foster a more active and healthy future for Taiwan's children and adolescents. Evaluating the movement behaviors of Taiwanese youth is vital to address a national rise in health risks. Furthermore, given that youth physical inactivity constitutes a global pandemic, systematic surveillance within a standardized framework is imperative. This report contributes critical data to the international scientific community, serving as a robust resource for local policy refinement and global public health research.

Methods

The Taiwan Report Card initiative was jointly directed by the two authors of the present article and received financial support from National Chung Hsing University (Taichung, Taiwan). The co-leaders oversaw the identification of relevant literature and data sources, synthesized preliminary findings, and assigned the initial indicator grades.

This report provides grades for ten indicators recognized within the Global Matrix 5.0 framework—namely Overall Physical Activity, Organized Sport, Active Play, Active Transportation, Sedentary Behaviors, Physical Fitness, Family and Peers, School, Community and the Built Environment, and Government Strategies and Investments—along with an additional indicator, body mass index (BMI). These evaluations pertain to school-aged children and adolescents, approximately 6 to 18 years of age.

The academic papers published in English were identified from Pubmed while those published in Chinese were identified from Airiti Library (https://www.airitilibrary.com/) using “physical activity”, “sedentary behavior”, “organized sport”, “sports club”, “family support”, “peer support”, “active play”, “active transportation”, “obesity”, “children”, or “adolescent” as keywords. The government publications were identified from websites of Ministry of Education and Ministry of Health and Welfare, Taiwan. The data obtained between 2022 and 2025 were included.

The grades for Overall Physical Activity and Sedentary Behavior were derived from the 2023 Health Surveillance of Teenagers, commissioned by the Ministry of Health and Welfare.9 This nationwide survey employed a stratified sampling design and utilized self-reported questionnaires adapted from the Global School-based Student Health Survey10 to assess health behaviors among two adolescent cohorts. The first cohort (aged 13–15 years) consisted of 20,386 participants (10,459 boys; 9775 girls), while the second (aged 16–18 years) included 21,804 participants (10,784 boys; 10,798 girls).

The grade assigned to the Organized Sport indicator was derived from the Student Participation in Sports survey, school year 2023-2024.11 This nationwide assessment employed stratified sampling to evaluate students aged 11–18 years. Administered by the Sports Administration of the Ministry of Education, the survey examined participation in school sports clubs, perceptions and needs concerning physical education, regular physical activity, physical literacy, and self-perceived health. The total sample included 8665 students aged 11–12 years, 10,066 students aged 13–15 years, and 9920 students aged 16–18 years.

The grade for Physical Fitness was based on the fitness test results of students who completed all four annual assessments (sit-and-reach, 1-min sit-ups, standing long jump, and 800/1600 m walk/run) during the 2023–2024 school year.12

Grades for the School indicators were derived from the Census of Physical Education school year 2023–2024,12 published annually by the Sports Administration of the Ministry of Education. This census aggregates information on physical education provision, the prevalence of sports teams and clubs, student participation rates, and the availability of physical activity facilities across educational levels ranging from elementary school to university. For the purposes of this Report Card, only data from elementary, junior high, and senior high schools were included in the analysis.

The grade for Community and the Built Environment was primarily based on local government policies related to physical activity and a nationwide database of sports facilities.13 Additionally, data were utilized from the 2024 Exercise Status Survey,14 which employed stratified proportional random-digit dialing of landline phone numbers. While the total survey included 25,458 participants, the grade for this indicator was informed specifically by a sub-sample of 1514 adolescents aged 13 to 17 years.

The grade for Government Strategies and Investments was derived from major physical activity policies implemented by the central government, specifically “Sport i (love) Taiwan 2.0 (2022–2027)." The 2024 annual budgets for exercise and sports at both central and local government levels were obtained from the 2025 Sports Statistics report.15 The final grade for this indicator represented the consensus of the working group.

The BMI indicator, which has been included since the 2022 Report Card, was also evaluated. BMI data for children aged 7–15 years were sourced from annual fitness test results for the 2023–2024 school year, published in the 2025 Health Promotion Statistics Yearbook by the Ministry of Health and Welfare.16 In contrast, BMI data for adolescents aged 16–18 years were derived from the 2023 Health Surveillance of Teenagers.9 The benchmark for this indicator is the percentage of children and adolescents classified as having a normal BMI according to the age- and gender-specific values established by the Ministry of Health and Welfare, Taiwan.17

After all data were summarized, the authors assigned an initial grade to each indicator. Grade assignments were informed exclusively by nationally representative data released by government agencies or academic institutions, as well as information retrieved from official government websites. For indicators comprising multiple benchmarks or age groups—including Overall Physical Activity, Participation in Organized Sport, Sedentary Behavior, School, and Community and the Built Environment—grades were calculated by taking a simple or weighted average across all relevant benchmarks or age cohorts. The resulting value was then used to identify the corresponding letter grade.

The weighting of each benchmark and the subsequent grade assignments were determined through deliberation between the two primary authors. While findings from regional samples were reported for context, they were excluded from the formal grading process. Following the assignment of initial grades, the methodology and results were presented to a panel of ten scholars with expertise in physical activity policy, physical education, and epidemiology from eight universities and research institutions. After a detailed review of the data and benchmark weighting, a final consensus was achieved. These grades were then finalized following an audit and approval by the Global Matrix Committee.

Results

The 2025 Taiwan Report Card represents the third longitudinal assessment of physical activity indicators, following submissions to Global Matrix 3.0 in 20185 and Global Matrix 4.0 in 2022.6 Table 1 details the benchmarks, data source characteristics, and assigned grades for each indicator. For comparative purposes, the 2018 and 2022 grades are also included.

Overall physical activity

The data indicated that only 21.0% of adolescents aged 13 to 15 years—comprising 27.9% of boys and 13.8% of girls—met the WHO guidelines of at least 60 min of daily moderate-to-vigorous physical activity.9 Among older adolescents aged 16 to 18 years, the prevalence was even lower at 15.9%, which consisted of 21.6% of boys and 9.9% of girls.9 The simple average prevalence across these two age groups (21.0% for ages 13–15 and 15.9% for ages 16–18), calculated at 18.5%, was used to identify the final grade of F.

Organized sport

According to the Student Participation in Sports survey for the 2023–2024 school year,11 participation in school sport clubs was reported by 36.3% of students aged 11 to 12 years, 31.9% of students aged 13 to 15 years, and 27.7% of students aged 16 to 18 years. For students aged 11 to 12 years, participation rates were 42.0% for boys and 30.3% for girls. For students aged 13 to 15 years, the rates were 39.4% for boys and 23.9% for girls, while for those aged 16 to 18 years, the rates were 34.1% for boys and 20.9% for girls.

Furthermore, participation in school sport teams was recorded at 21.2% for the 11 to 12-year-old group, 11.3% for the 13 to 15-year-old group, and 7.4% for the 16 to 18-year-old group. The gender breakdown for team participation was 24.6% for boys and 17.7% for girls aged 11 to 12 years, 14.3% for boys and 8.0% for girls aged 13 to 15 years, and 9.5% for boys and 5.0% for girls aged 16 to 18 years.11 It was assumed that each student participated in only one organized activity and that no overlap occurred between those in clubs and those on teams. Participation in out-of-school clubs and teams was excluded from the analysis due to limited data availability.

A grade of C– was assigned based on a weighted mean participation rate of 43.7% across the three age groups, calculated using the respective year spans: 57.5% for ages 11–12 (2-year span), 43.2% for ages 13–15 (3-year span), and 35.1% for ages 16–18 (3-year span). This grade represents an improvement over the 2018 and 2022 Report Cards, which both assigned a grade of D–. Notably, the earlier grades were derived from the Census of Physical Education school year 2019-2020,18 using school-reported enrollment figures, whereas the current assessment utilized student-reported data to mitigate potential inaccuracies associated with outdated school records.

Active play

The 2022 Survey of Living Situation for Children and Adolescents reported on leisure time but did not provide sufficient information to identify a grade for active play.19,20 Although 24.1% of children aged 6 to 12 years19 and 49.2% of adolescents aged 13 to 18 years20 reported more than 2 h per day of leisure time, this period was largely dedicated to sedentary behaviors—such as watching television, using mobile devices, and playing video games—while engagement in physical activities was minimal and not quantified. Consequently, the Active Play indicator was assigned a grade of INC due to the lack of nationally representative and age-appropriate data.

Active transportation

In the 2018 and 2022 Report Cards, this indicator received a grade of C–. The Health Surveillance Report for junior and senior high students, which provided the data for the previous two assessments, was discontinued after 2018. Furthermore, the most recent nationwide Health Surveillance of Teenagers did not include information regarding walking or cycling to school.9 A separate study utilizing random sampling found that only 2 out of 62 students aged 11 to 12 years—representing 3.2%—walked or cycled to school.21 However, due to the limited sample size, this study was excluded from the grade identification process.

Sedentary behavior

Only 25.1% of adolescents aged 13 to 15 years—comprising 30.2% of boys and 19.7% of girls—and 15.3% of those aged 16 to 18 years—comprising 19.4% of boys and 10.6% of girls—reported spending less than 2 h per school day on the combined activities of screen time, socializing with friends, attending cram schools, and completing homework outside of school.9 A simple average prevalence of 20.2% across these age groups (25.1% and 15.3%) was used to identify the grade of D–. These results were similar to findings from previous surveys, which reported rates of 25.4% for adolescents aged 13 to 15 years and 21.2% for those aged 16 to 18 years.22,23

Notably, the benchmark was modified for the current Report Card because the available data did not permit the isolation of screen time from other sedentary activities. While previous assessments focused exclusively on screen-based behaviors such as watching television, playing video games, or using a computer, the current grade reflects a composite measure of screen time, socializing, and academic-related sedentary tasks.

Physical fitness

Physical Fitness was assigned a grade of INC because normative values based on the criteria established by Tomkinson et al.24 could not be estimated using the data published by the Ministry of Education. Most students aged 10 to 18 years participate each semester in a battery of fitness tests, which includes the sit-and-reach, standing long jump, 1-min sit-up, and 800 or 1600-m run. The results of these assessments are uploaded to a database maintained by the Sports Administration of the Ministry of Education.

For the 2023–2024 school year, the proportions of students scoring above the 25th percentile across all four fitness tests were 53.49% for those aged 10 to 12 years, 56.01% for those aged 13 to 15 years, and 47.83% for those aged 16 to 18 years. These results yielded an overall prevalence of 52.81%.15 Despite the availability of this national data, the indicator remained incomplete because the local reporting format did not align with the specific normative requirements of the Global Matrix.

Family and Peers

In the 2022 Report Card, this indicator received a grade of D– based on data from the 2020 wave of the Panel Study of Family Dynamics.6 Since that time, only one small-scale study has been identified.25 This study, conducted in 2024 using cluster proportional sampling, included 1525 adolescents aged 11 to 13 years and reported that 24% of fathers and 12% of mothers exercised regularly. The data indicated that when both parents exercised regularly, 60.9% of adolescents exercised with their parents and 66.6% received financial support for sports participation. These proportions were significantly higher than those observed among adolescents whose parents did not exercise regularly, which were 13.5% and 35.5%, respectively. Furthermore, 34.9% of adolescents whose parents both exercised regularly met the World Health Organization physical activity recommendations, compared with 25.3% of those whose parents did not exercise regularly. However, these findings were not used to identify a grade because the sample was not confirmed to be nationally representative and the questionnaire design presented specific limitations. Consequently, the indicator was assigned a grade of INC.

School

According to the Census of Physical Education for the 2023–2024 school year, 97.46% of schools provided physical activity opportunities outside formal physical education classes through the Sport Health 150 (SH150) policy.12 This policy, mandated under the National Sports Act,26 requires schools to offer at least 150 min per week of optional physical activity—such as recess and after-school sports—excluding formal physical education classes. However, the exact proportion of students meeting the SH150 target remains unclear.

The national curriculum mandates that 100% of schools provide the required amount of physical education. On average, physical education was offered for 1.95 classes per week in elementary schools, 2.00 classes per week in middle schools, and 1.98 classes per week in high schools. Notably, 90.1% of these classes were taught by certified specialist teachers.12

In terms of student perception, 29.4% of children aged 7 to 12 years—comprising 23.3% of boys and 35.3% of girls—felt that the time allocated to physical education was sufficient. This perception was shared by 32.3% of adolescents aged 13 to 15 years, consisting of 23.9% of boys and 41.9% of girls, and 35.2% of those aged 16 to 18 years, consisting of 24.3% of boys and 48.0% of girls.11

In addition, 93.4% of schools reported that students had regular access to track-and-field facilities.12 This access was recorded at 94.15% for elementary schools, 94.56% for middle schools, and 87.99% for high schools, with most facilities remaining open to the public outside school hours.

A simple average of four key benchmarks—schools offering SH150 opportunities at 97.46%, mandated provision of physical education at 100%, certified specialist teachers at 90.1%, and access to facilities at 93.4%—yielded an overall score of 95.2%, which identified the grade of A+.

The results also satisfied the additional benchmark regarding the national curriculum, which is comprehensive and specifies minimum instructional time, clear objectives, a balance of health and performance outcomes, and inclusivity for students with disabilities. This grade is higher than the A– assigned in the previous Report Card,6 primarily because of modifications to the grading criteria rather than substantive improvements in school practices.

Community and environment

All local governments in Taiwan have established public facilities and policies to support physical activity, including initiatives specifically targeting children and adolescents. At present, there are 57 public sports centers operational across the country, with an additional 20 centers scheduled for completion by the end of 2026.

A nationwide landline telephone survey conducted in 2024, employing stratified proportional random-digit sampling, indicated that 72.5% of adolescents aged 13 to 17 years perceived there to be sufficient sports facilities near their homes.27 Furthermore, 61.2% of participants in this age group reported satisfaction with the environment of these facilities. The most common locations for physical activity among adolescents were schools at 40.4%, parks at 35.3%, and sidewalks at 21.6%. These facilities were found to be highly accessible, with walking serving as the primary mode of transportation from home for 62% of respondents, with an average travel time of 10.33 min.

A simple average was calculated based on three key indicators: local government provision and policies at 100%, perceived sufficiency of nearby facilities at 72.5%, and satisfaction with facility environments at 61.2%. The resulting overall score of 77.9% was used to identify the grade of B+.

Government strategies and investments

Government Strategies and Investments was assigned a grade of A– based on consensus from the expert working group. The government continues to implement the White Book on Sports Policy for 2026 through 2035, which outlines strategic directions to promote physical activity among children and adolescents.28 A six-year initiative titled Sport i Taiwan 2.0, spanning 2022 to 2027, is currently underway. This initiative focuses on five strategic directions: promoting municipal sports activities; enhancing the quantity, quality, and professionalism of personnel; developing local sports; optimizing sport-friendly services; and integrating sports with health promotion. Furthermore, the establishment of the Ministry of Sports on September 9, 2025, as a dedicated unit for planning and promoting physical activity and sport, demonstrates significant progress in national policy development.

In 2024, the total budget for sports and exercise reached NT$33.49 billion, which is approximately US$1.07 billion. This total comprised NT$13.57 billion from the central government and NT$19.92 billion from local governments, representing an 11.46% increase from the previous year.15 Funding for sports and physical activities accounted for 1.16% of the central government's total budget of NT$2.88 trillion.

Overall, the government has demonstrated leadership and commitment through policy initiatives such as the creation of the Ministry of Sports and the continuation of the Sport i Taiwan project, which the working group determined merited a grade of A for policy making. While the allocation of 1.16% of the central government budget supports an A–, the working group reached a final consensus to identify the overall grade for this indicator as A–.

BMI

BMI is an optional indicator that has been included since the previous Report Card.6 The indicator was graded as B–, which is consistent with the prior assessment. According to the Health Promotion Statistics Yearbook 2025, the prevalence of normal BMI among children aged 7 to 12 years was 65.9%, consisting of 62.8% for boys and 69.1% for girls.16 Among adolescents aged 12 to 15 years, the prevalence of normal BMI was 63.2%, consisting of 58.1% for boys and 68.6% for girls.16 Both of these values represent an increase compared with previous years.

For adolescents aged 16 to 18 years, data from the 2023 Health Surveillance of Teenagers indicated that 64.4% of individuals had a normal self-reported BMI, comprising 58.8% for boys and 70.4% for girls.9 Nationwide data further revealed that the prevalence of overweight and obesity was 23.8% among those aged 7 to 12 years, with 27.2% for boys and 20.2% for girls. For the 13 to 15-year-old group, this prevalence was 28.6%, with 23.1% for boys and 23.7% for girls, while for those aged 16 to 18 years, the rate was 27.0%, with 32.3% for boys and 21.4% for girls.

All three age groups showed declines in overweight and obesity rates compared with previous years, suggesting an overall improvement in body weight status across the population.16 However, the prevalence of underweight increased slightly among both boys and girls aged 7 to 15 years.16

It should be noted that the BMI cut-off values for underweight, overweight, and obesity used in these surveys were issued by the Ministry of Health and Welfare29 and differ slightly from those of the International Obesity Task Force.30 A simple average of the proportions of students with a normal BMI across the three age groups—calculated at 65.9%, 63.2%, and 64.4%—yielded an overall value of 64.5%, which was used to identify the final grade of B–.

Discussion

The 2025 Taiwan Report Card reflects a period of stagnation in behavioral outcomes despite continued institutional investment. When compared to the 2022 Report Card, the grade for Overall Physical Activity remained at an F, indicating no measurable progress in the benchmarks. Although the grade for Organized Sport appeared to improve from a D− to a C−, this shift was primarily a result of utilizing different data sources rather than a documented increase in participation. Conversely, a downward trend was noted in Sedentary Behavior, which declined from a D+ to a D−. While the School and Government Strategies indicators showed relative stability or slight improvement from their high baseline (A+ and A−, respectively), the grade for Community and the Built Environment saw a slight decrease from A− to B+. Due to a lack of updated nationally representative data, several indicators including Active Play, Active Transportation, and Family and Peers were assigned INC grades, as was Physical Fitness, due to the absence of detailed percentile outcomes.

The findings reveal a significant discrepancy between the environment created by the government and the actual movement behaviors of Taiwanese youth. Literature frequently identifies a pervasive socio-cultural emphasis on academic achievement as a prominent contextual factor influencing movement behaviors in the Asian region. Rooted in Confucian values, educational goals are often prioritized, which can inadvertently limit the time available for physical activity.31 While the government has established policies to foster physical activity and nearly all schools (98%) implement the SH150 mandate, these high-level supports have not effectively translated into daily habits. Objective data utilizing accelerometers to track junior high school students indicates that only 7.7% actually meet the SH150 guidelines.32 While a causal link cannot be established by our descriptive data, this pattern aligns with findings on after-school education, which suggest that intensive learning schedules present a highly plausible structural barrier to physical activity; in 2023, nearly 60% of children and adolescents attended cram schools, with 24.7% attending five or more days per week.33 With many students remaining in these facilities until 10:00 p.m. and completing an additional 2 h of homework daily, the total learning time often exceeds 10 h.33 Consequently, 37% of adolescents specifically cite academic obligations as the primary barrier preventing physical activity.34 This disconnection between institutional preparedness and behavioral adherence is also observed in East Asian countries with very high Human Development Index ratings, including Hong Kong, Japan, and Korea.35 Nevertheless, extensive international literature robustly demonstrates the effectiveness of structured school-based behavioral programs, including active recess and extracurricular sports initiatives, in successfully increasing youth physical activity.36,37 By adapting these internationally proven models to navigate local cultural constraints, Taiwan can extend its current policy efforts to more effectively translate institutional infrastructure into active behavioral habits.

Student attitudes toward physical activity remain positive, with 82% believing that regular exercise maintains mental and physical health.34 This suggests an urgent need to shift parental perception to match the awareness of the youth. Parents must recognize that physical activity is not a distraction from education but an essential component of a child's cognitive function and social development. Scientific evidence consistently demonstrates that regular exercise has a positive impact on academic performance and focus; 38, 39, 40 thus, movement should be viewed as a prerequisite for, rather than a competitor to, academic success.

Recent evidence suggests that the family environment is a critical lever for change. Research indicates that Taiwanese adolescents with two active parents are significantly more likely to meet WHO physical activity recommendations (34.9%) than those with inactive parents (25.3%).25 Active parents are also more likely to provide financial support for sports and engage in physical activities alongside their children.25 This preliminary study indicates that shifting parental perceptions—moving from viewing exercise as a distraction to seeing it as a prerequisite for cognitive and social development—is essential for improving youth activity levels.

Beyond social factors, environmental and climatic conditions are emerging as direct threats for physical activity. Frequent extreme heat waves and rising PM 2.5 air pollution levels often necessitate the cancellation of outdoor recesses and sports for health reasons,41 further restricting the windows of opportunity for children to be active.

The newly established Sports for All Agency under the Ministry of Sports launched a financial support initiative in 2025 for community-based sports clubs to expand physical activity opportunities. These out-of-school programs typically rely on a combination of commercial enterprise and family investment; however, the long-term impact of this initiative on student activity levels has yet to be formally evaluated.

Strengths and limitations

A primary strength of this Report Card is the use of data from the Census of Physical Education, which is aggregated by the Ministry of Education and ensures national representativeness for school-related indicators. However, a limitation is the synthesis of heterogeneous data sources spanning different populations, which may introduce systematic error and complicate direct comparability. Combining these diverse datasets introduces inherent method-specific constraints—such as self-report bias in questionnaires or selection bias in telephone surveys—that may influence the precision of certain indicator grades. Another notable limitation is that the overall physical activity data were derived primarily from self-reported questionnaires within a cross-sectional study design. Ultimately, this Report Card is intended as a surveillance and policy monitoring tool; therefore, results are presented descriptively in accordance with AHKGA Global Matrix methodology rather than to examine causal or associative relationships.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the 2025 Taiwan Report Card confirms that while the nation has built an infrastructure and policy framework conducive to physical activity, the social and educational environment remains a significant barrier to implementation. Beyond extending longitudinal timelines, this third iteration introduces key methodological advancements, such as transitioning to direct student-reported surveys for organized sports and evolving the sedentary behavior benchmark into a comprehensive composite measure that reflects modern academic lifestyles. It also captures a historic administrative shift with the establishment of the Ministry of Sports in late 2025. Yet, the consistent F in Overall Physical Activity and D– in Sedentary Behavior highlight a persistent plateau where high-quality facilities and rising financial investments are marginalized by the demands of academic workload and cultural priorities. Future strategies must move beyond policy mandates to actively protect time for students and engage the family unit. Systemic reforms are needed to reduce academic-related sedentary time and to foster a home environment in which physical activity is valued as an important contributor to both educational attainment and health.
