Generative AI for mental health: promise, risk and the path forward
Authors: Matthew Brown, Naomi Herz
Published: 2026

Mental health problems affect one in two people across a lifetime1 and are one of the leading contributors to global disease burden.2 In 2023, nearly 1.2 billion people were living with mental disorders, a number that has almost doubled since 1990.2

Despite the range of evidence-based treatments available for common mental health conditions, from medication to talking therapies, many people still struggle to find support that works for them. Some don’t see a benefit from certain treatments; others experience side effects; and millions around the world face barriers to accessing any care because services are overstretched, unavailable or shaped by stigma.

As health systems struggle to meet rising demand, digital innovation, particularly generative AI (artificial intelligence), is often presented as a transformative solution. But realising the potential of these technologies requires more than the technologies alone. There are clear research gaps and important regulatory questions to resolve, but the potential reach and scalability of generative AI mean it could offer support on a scale that few existing interventions can match.

Alongside the promise of generative AI are the realities of the harms it can cause, including failing to avert self-harm or suicide, reinforcing delusional beliefs due to sycophantic tendencies, and exhibiting cultural biases embedded in training data. Evidence, regulatory clarity, global inclusion and meaningful involvement of people with lived experience are needed for technology to positively shift mental health support into a new era.

At Wellcome, we are investing in foundational research3 to understand how generative AI might improve the measurement and treatment of anxiety, depression and psychosis, including through teams working in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Mental health infrastructure is often minimal in LMICs, and in some places mental health is still highly stigmatised, yet access to digital devices continues to rise despite being developed in high-income countries.4 In some settings, AI-enabled interventions may be more feasible than models requiring intensive therapist input. It is essential to ensure safe deployment of AI solutions in LMICs while considering the risks of exacerbating health inequalities in access to care, where high-income populations are treated by therapists and lower-income populations solely have access to AI chatbots for support. Tools developed in high-income contexts rarely translate seamlessly elsewhere; ethical, culturally relevant solutions require lived-experience input from the outset to adapt and maximise benefit to the population they are serving.

The opportunity in front of us is broader than chatbots or automated therapy. Generative AI is particularly compelling because mental healthcare relies heavily on language for diagnosis, symptom tracking and interventions such as cognitive behavioural therapy. But AI can also enhance the wider care pathway: ambient scribes that reduce clinician burden, dynamic triage systems that respond to fluctuating need and data-linkage approaches that help identify risk earlier. Beyond task-shifting, it is a chance to rethink what effective, scalable mental healthcare could look like across the board.

Shifts in the regulatory landscape can accelerate progress but also risk undermining safety. Recent announcements in the USA signal a relaxation of oversight, including an expanded ‘general wellness’ category for wearables, pared-back rules for certain clinical decision support tools and reduced premarket burden for digital health products.5–7 These changes promise faster routes to market and greater flexibility for early-stage ventures, but it is crucial we don’t lose sight of safety and effectiveness.

Consumer-facing AI tools further blur these boundaries. People increasingly use them for mental health support, despite the absence of regulatory oversight, clinical validation or protections for sensitive data.

Several challenges must be confronted if AI-enabled mental health tools are to fulfil their potential. Evidence requirements remain contested: randomised controlled trials may be appropriate in some cases, but digital controls are difficult to design, and interventions evolve rapidly. The dynamic nature of digital tools and software updates makes standard approaches difficult to implement. As John Torous and colleagues have argued, evidence frameworks must reflect the realities of digital tools rather than forcing them into outdated paradigms.8 Regulation also struggles to keep pace with technologies that update continuously. Many tools sit outside formal oversight because they are marketed for general use, despite being used for mental health support. And harms from AI-enabled tools may be subtle, contextual and difficult to attribute; a conversation that triggers distress may not be easily detectable, and changes in mental health rarely map neatly onto a single digital interaction.

Practical solutions are emerging. Regulatory ‘airlocks’ such as the Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) programme9 in the UK allow developers to test products with regulatory input, identifying risks early and guiding tools towards safe market authorisation. This work is already taking shape globally. Our research accelerator is supporting teams exploring how AI can train or provide feedback to health workers, assist with triage in overstretched systems and enable earlier diagnosis, while the nonprofit PATH’s (Program for Appropriate Technology in Health) Wellcome-funded consortium is designing Africa’s first regulatory sandbox for AI-enabled mental health tools, guiding products toward authorisation10 and working with the African Medicines Agency to disseminate learnings continent-wide.

The sector also needs a shared taxonomy of harms for digital mental health technologies, and the BSI standard, which was open for consultation in June 2026,11 provides an opportunity to embed this into practice. Continuous, automated monitoring will be essential to detect potential harms, model drift and bias, and PATH and partners are developing methods to evaluate these risks in real time. Regulators must also learn from one another: MHRA are already collaborating with international regulatory bodies, and initiatives such as the International Medical Device Regulators Forum12 and the UK–Singapore Health AI Innovation Corridor13 offer further opportunities for alignment. Cross-sector collaboration is equally important, with developers, policymakers, clinicians and people with lived experience needing shared spaces to define evidence requirements and understand available technologies, supported by transparent repositories of mental health tools.

The opportunity is clear. What we need now is action: healthcare workers can access and use the recently established MHRA guidance; developers and policymakers can apply to the MHRA and PATH airlocks; contribute to future public consultations on standards; and support regulators in working together. AI will not solve the mental health crisis alone, but with the right evidence, regulation and global collaboration, it can become a powerful part of the solution.
