Primitive points on some low degree Fermat curves
Maleeha Khawaja

Abstract
. Let n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 be an integer. Let F n F_{n} be the Fermat curve defined by the Fermat equation x n + y n = z n x^{n}+y^{n}=z^{n} . For a curve C / ℚ C/\mathbb{Q} , we say an algebraic point P ∈ C ⁡ ( ℚ ¯ ) P\in C(\bar{\mathbb{Q}}) is primitive if the Galois group of the Galois closure of the number field ℚ ⁡ ( P ) \mathbb{Q}(P) is a primitive permutation group. Recall that A 4 A_{4} is a primitive subgroup of S 4 S_{4} . We prove that there are no non-trivial quartic points on F n F_{n} with Galois closure A 4 A_{4} , when n = 7 n=7 and n = 8 n=8 . We also provide sufficient conditions for the non-existence of non-trivial points on the Fermat curves F 6 F_{6} and F 8 F_{8} defined over a given primitive number field of degree at least 3 3 .

1.
Introduction

For an integer
n\geq 3
, let
F_{n}/\mathbb{Q}
be the Fermat curve of degree
n
defined by the Fermat equation

(1)

x^{n}+y^{n}=z^{n}.

The study of low degree points on the Fermat curve of degree
n
, when
n
is a small integer, is of long-standing interest. We begin by mentioning a few results established in this area. Aigner
[
1
]
, in 1934, proved that
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-7})
is the only quadratic field to possess a non-trivial solution to the Fermat quartic
F_{4}
(i.e. a solution for which
xyz\neq 0
). Mordell
[
12
]
reproved this result in 1967, by providing an explicit parametrization of all such points. In 1978, Gross and Rohrlich
[
4
]
completely determined all degree
d
points on the Fermat curve
F_{n}
for
n=5,7
and
11
, for all
d\leq(n-1)/2
, through studying the Mordell–Weil group of the Jacobian of
F_{n}
. More recently, there has been increased interest in analysing the Galois groups of cubic and quartic points on certain low degree Fermat curves. Klassen and Tzermias
[
10
]
showed that every quartic point on the Fermat quintic
F_{5}
arises as the intersection of
F_{5}
with a straight line passing through a rational point.
Using this classification, Kraus
[
11
]
gave a complete description of the quartic points on
F_{5}
, proving that all but finitely many such points have Galois group
D_{4}
. Note that as
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
vary, we obtain cubic points defined over
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{a^{3}+b^{3}})
. The discriminant of the given cubic field is
-27(a^{3}+b^{3})^{2}
, which implies that the Galois closure of the cubic field has Galois group
S_{3}
. Bremner and Choudhry
[
3
]
have provided an explicit construction of infinitely many points on the Fermat cubic with cyclic Galois group. They also proved that all cubic points on the Fermat quartic
F_{4}
have Galois group
S_{3}
using the fact that they arise as the intersection of
F_{4}
with a straight line passing through a rational point. In this paper, we are concerned with algebraic points on the Fermat curves of degrees
6,7
and
8
which have primitive Galois group. We recall the notion of a primitive Galois group for the convenience of the reader.
Let
G
be a group acting transitively on a set
X
. Then
G
is said to
act primitively
on
X
if
G
does not stabilise a non-trivial partition of
X
. Let
K
be a number field with Galois closure
\tilde{K}
. Recall that
K
is a
primitive
number field if there are no proper subextensions of
K/\mathbb{Q}
. Suppose
G=\Gal(\tilde{K}/\mathbb{Q})
. Then the Galois correspondence implies that
G
is a primitive permutation group if and only if
K
is a primitive number field (see e.g.
[
9
, Lemma 28]
for a proof of this equivalence).
It is well-known that if a group acts
2
-transitively on a set then that action is primitive. As a consequence, for an integer
d\geq 3
, the groups
A_{d}
and
S_{d}
are primitive permutation groups. We state our main results below.

Theorem 1
.

Let
n=7
or
8
.
Let
K
be a quartic field such that
\Gal(\tilde{K}/\mathbb{Q})=A_{4}
, where
\tilde{K}
is the Galois closure of
K
. Then there are no non-trivial solutions to (
1
) with exponent
n
over
K
.

Our main tool is the use of a morphism from the degree
n
Fermat curve to a degree
n
hyperelliptic curve (defined in Section
3
). There are two advantages of working with the mentioned degree
n
hyperelliptic curve. The obvious advantage is the significant reduction in the genera of the curves we are working with (the hyperelliptic curve has genus
3
in both cases). Secondly, analysing the Riemann–Roch spaces of effective degree
4
divisors on these curves yields an explicit parametrization of all quartic points with Galois group
A_{4}
. We note in passing that the existence of quartic points on the Fermat quartic with Galois group
A_{4}
is currently unknown, and refer the interested reader to
[
3
, Section 4]
for a discussion of this problem.

We also use this family of hyperelliptic curves to prove sufficient conditions
for the Fermat curves of degrees
6
and
8
to possess no non-trivial points
defined over a given primitive number field of degree at least
3
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
K
be a primitive number field of degree at least
3
.
Suppose
E(K)=E(\mathbb{Q})
, where
E/\mathbb{Q}
is either of the two elliptic curves with Cremona label
27a3
or
432b1
.
Then there are no non-trivial solutions to (
1
) for
n=6
over
K
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
E
be the elliptic curve with Cremona label
64a4
.
Let
K
be a primitive number field of degree at least
3
such that
E(K)=E(\mathbb{Q})
.
Then there are no non-trivial solutions to (
1
) for
n=8
over
K
.

In the resolution of the Fermat equation over a fixed number field
K
using the modular approach, it is often necessary to rule out the existence of non-trivial
K
-rational points on
F_{n}
for small values of
n
using a different argument (see e.g.
[
6
]
or
[
8
]
). This is the intended application of these results. It would also be of interest to find families of number fields which satisfy the given criteria.

2.
Divisors on curves

In this section, we recall some well-known results concerning divisors of algebraic points on curves. Let
C
be a curve defined over
\mathbb{Q}
.
Then a
divisor

D
on
C
is a formal finite sum of algebraic points on
C
, that is,

D=\sum_{P\in C(\bar{\mathbb{Q}})}n_{P}P,\qquad n_{P}\in\mathbb{Z}.

We say
D
is an
effective divisor
, and write
D\geq 0
, if
n_{P}\geq 0
for all
P\in C(\bar{\mathbb{Q}})
. The
Riemann–Roch space of

D
, denoted by
L(D)
, is defined as

L(D)=\{f\in\mathbb{Q}(C)^{\times}\;:\;\divv(f)+D\geq 0\}\cup\{0\},

and we denote its dimension by
\ell(D)
.

Theorem 4

(Riemann–Roch)
.

Let
K_{C}
be a canonical divisor on
C
.
Then

\ell(D)-\ell(K_{C}-D)=\deg(D)-g+1.

Proof.

See, for example,
[
14
]
.
∎

We say an effective divisor
D
is
special
if
\ell(K_{C}-D)>0
.
Recall that a hyperelliptic curve
C/\mathbb{Q}
admits a degree
2
morphism
\pi
to
\mathbb{P}^{1}
given by
\pi(x,y)=x
, and a
hyperelliptic divisor
on
C
is one of the form
\pi^{*}(x)
for
x\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
.

Theorem 5

(Clifford)
.

Let
D
be a special divisor on
C
.
Then

\ell(D)\leq\frac{\deg(D)}{2}+1

with equality if and only if
D=0
or
D
is a canonical divisor or
C
is a hyperelliptic curve and
D
is a multiple of a hyperelliptic divisor.

Proof.

See, for example,
[
5
, Chapter III.1]
.
∎

Corollary 6
.

Suppose
C/\mathbb{Q}
is a genus
3
hyperelliptic curve.
Let
D
be an effective degree
4
divisor on
C
.
Then
\ell(D)=2
, unless
D
is a canonical divisor or a multiple of a hyperelliptic divisor in which case
\ell(D)=3
.

Proof.

The corollary follows immediately from Theorems
4
and
5
.
∎

3.
Hyperelliptic Fermat curves

The morphism given in the following lemma is well-known but we are unable to find a reference.

Lemma 7
.

Let
n\geq 3
be an integer.
Let
C_{n}
be the degree
n
hyperelliptic curve defined as

C_{n}:y^{2}=-4x^{n}+1.

There is a non-constant morphism

\pi:F_{n}\rightarrow C_{n}
given by

(\alpha:\beta:1)\mapsto(\alpha\beta,\;\alpha^{n}-\beta^{n}).

Proof.

Suppose
(\alpha:\beta:1)\in F_{n}(K)
.
Observe the identity given by

1-(\alpha^{n}-\beta^{n})^{2}=(\alpha^{n}+\beta^{n})^{2}-(\alpha^{n}-\beta^{n})^{2}=4(\alpha\beta)^{n}.

In particular

(\alpha^{n}-\beta^{n})^{2}=-4(\alpha\beta)^{n}+1.

∎

We now determine
C_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
for
n=6,7
and
8
as we shall require these computations in the proofs
of the main theorems. For
n=6
and
8
, the hyperelliptic curve
C_{n}
admits a degree
2
map
\pi

to an elliptic curve
E
of rank
0
over
\mathbb{Q}
. Looking at the preimages of the points in
E(\mathbb{Q})
under

\pi
allows us to determine
C_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
in a straightforward manner. We find that the Jacobian of the hyperelliptic curve

C_{7}
has rank
0
over
\mathbb{Q}
, and we are thus able to determine
C_{7}(\mathbb{Q})
by inspecting the Riemann–Roch spaces of degree

1
divisors on
C_{7}
.

Lemma 8
.

C_{6}(\mathbb{Q})=\{(0,1),\,(0,-1)\}
.

Proof.

Let
E/\mathbb{Q}
be the elliptic curve with Cremona label
27a3
, and consider the Weierstrass model of
E
given by

E:\;y^{2}+y=x^{3}.

There is a degree
2
map
\pi:C_{6}\rightarrow E
given by

\pi(x,y)=(-x^{2},\;(y-1)/2).

Suppose
P\in C_{6}(\mathbb{Q})
then
\pi(P)\in E(\mathbb{Q})
.
Using
Magma
, we find that

E(\mathbb{Q})=\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}\cdot(0,0).

By looking at the preimage of the points in
E(\mathbb{Q})
under
\pi
, we conclude that

P=(0,1)\qquad\text{ or }\qquad P=(0,-1).

∎

Lemma 9
.

C_{7}(\mathbb{Q})=\{(0,1),(0,-1),\infty\}
.

Proof.

We first determine
J(\mathbb{Q})
, where
J
is the Jacobian of
C_{7}
. Using
Magma
, we find that
J
has rank
0
over
\mathbb{Q}
(using the
RankBounds
command). To compute the torsion subgroup of
J(\mathbb{Q})
, we apply the
Magma
implementation of an algorithm due to Müller and Reitsma
[
13
]
. This algorithm is based on the explicit theory of Kummer varieties associated to genus
3
hyperelliptic curves due to Stoll
[
15
]
. The aforementioned algorithm returns that the torsion subgroup of
J(\mathbb{Q})
has order
7
. Let

P_{0}=\infty,\quad P_{1}=(0,-1),\quad P_{2}=(0,1)\in C_{7}(\mathbb{Q}).

The point
[P_{0}-P_{1}]\in J(\mathbb{Q})
has order 7.
Therefore

J(\mathbb{Q})=\mathbb{Z}/7\mathbb{Z}\cdot[P_{0}-P_{1}].

Suppose
P
is a rational point on
C_{7}
.
Then

[P]-[P_{1}]\sim a[P_{0}-P_{1}]

for an integer
0\leq a\leq 6
, where
\sim
denotes linear equivalence on
J
. In other words,

[P]=[P_{1}+a(P_{0}-P_{1})]+\divv(f)

where
f\in L_{a}=L([P_{1}+a(P_{0}-P_{1})])
. We used
Magma
to compute a basis of the Riemann–Roch space
L_{a}
for each value of
a
in the range given above.

•

For
a\geq 3
, the dimension of
L_{a}
equals
0
.

•

If
a=2
then

[P]=[2P_{0}-P_{1}]+\divv(f)

where
f\in L(2P_{0}-P_{1})
. A basis of
L(2P_{0}-P_{1})
is given by
x
. Note that
\divv(x)=P_{1}+P_{2}-2P_{0}
. Thus in this case
P=P_{2}
.

•

If
a=1
then

[P]=[P_{0}]+\divv(f)

where
f\in L(P_{0})
. A basis of
L(P_{0})
is given by
1
. Thus
P=P_{0}
.

•

If
a=0
then

[P]=[P_{1}]+\divv(f)

where
f\in L(P_{1})
.
A basis of
L(P_{1})
is given by
1
. Thus
P=P_{1}
.

∎

Lemma 10
.

C_{8}(\mathbb{Q})=\{(0,1),\,(0,-1)\}
.

Proof.

Let
E/\mathbb{Q}
be the elliptic curve with Cremona label
64a4
, and consider the Weierstrass model of
E
given by

E:\;y^{2}=x^{3}+x.

There is a degree
2
map
\pi:C_{8}\rightarrow E

given by

\pi(x,y)=\left(\dfrac{1-y}{2x^{4}},\;\dfrac{y-1}{2x^{6}}\right).

If
P\in C_{8}(\mathbb{Q})
then
\pi(P)\in E(\mathbb{Q})
.
We find using
Magma
that

E(\mathbb{Q})=(0,0)\cdot\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}.

It immediately follows that

C_{8}(\mathbb{Q})=\{(0,1),\;(0,-1)\}.

∎

We also make repeated use of the following observation throughout the paper.

Lemma 11
.

Let
C,C^{\prime}
be curves defined over
\mathbb{Q}
.
Let
m\geq 2
be an integer.
Suppose there is a degree
m
map
\pi:C\rightarrow C^{\prime}
defined over
\mathbb{Q}
. Let
K
be a primitive number field of degree
d>m
such that
C^{\prime}(K)=C^{\prime}(\mathbb{Q})
.
Then
C(K)=C(\mathbb{Q})
.

Proof.

Let
P\in C(K)
. Under the assumptions of the theorem,
\mathbb{Q}(\pi(P))=\mathbb{Q}
. Thus

\deg(\mathbb{Q}(P))\leq\deg(\pi)=m<d.

Note that
\mathbb{Q}(P)\subset K
.
It immediately follows that
\mathbb{Q}(P)=\mathbb{Q}
, since
K
is a primitive number field.
∎

Remark 12
.

In our applications of Lemma
6
, the curve

is an elliptic curve. In this case, for a given number field

[

]
means the determination of

Proof of Theorem
1
:
n=7

Let
K
be a quartic number field such that
\Gal(\tilde{K}/\mathbb{Q})=A_{4}
, where
\tilde{K}
denotes the Galois closure of
K
. Suppose
P\in F_{7}(K)
is a non-trivial point.
Let
\pi
be the morphism defined in Lemma
7
.
Then
\pi(P)=Q
where
Q\in C_{7}(K)
.
Let

P_{0}=\infty,\quad P_{1}=(0,-1),\quad P_{2}=(0,1)\in C_{7}(\mathbb{Q}).

Write
J
for the Jacobian of
C_{7}
.
Write
D
for the effective degree
4
divisor obtained from taking the sum of the Galois conjugates of
Q
. Then
D-4P_{0}\in J(\mathbb{Q})
.
Recall from the proof of Lemma
9
that

J(\mathbb{Q})=\mathbb{Z}/7\mathbb{Z}\cdot[P_{1}-P_{0}].

Therefore

D-4P_{0}\sim a(P_{1}-P_{0})

for some integer
0\leq a\leq 6
, where
\,\sim\,
denotes linear equivalence on
J
.
Therefore

D=4P_{0}+a(P_{1}-P_{0})+\divv(f)

where
f\in L_{a}=L(4P_{0}+a(P_{1}-P_{0}))
. We note that the hyperelliptic divisor is
2P_{0}
.
Moreover, the divisor of the differential
dx/y
is
4P_{0}
.
It follows from Corollary
6
that the Riemann–Roch space
L_{a}
has dimension
2
for
a\neq 0
and dimension
3
for
a=0
. First suppose
a=0
.
Then

D=4P_{0}+\divv(f)

where
f\in L(4P_{0})
. A basis of
L(4P_{0})
can be computed in
Magma
, and one is given by

1,\;x,\;x^{2}.

In this case, the
x
-coordinate of

is the root of a quadratic polynomial. Write
Q=(a,b)\in C_{7}(K)
. There are three cases to consider.

•

Case 1:
a,b\in\mathbb{Q}
.
In this case, by Lemma

In each case,

corresponds to a trivial point

.

•

Case 2:

a\in\mathbb{Q}
,

, where
[L:\mathbb{Q}]=2
.
In this case,

is defined over a quadratic field.

is a primitive number field.
Thus

and

.

•

Case 3:

, where

.
Again, since

.

It remains to consider the cases

.
Write

for a basis of

.
Then

f=g-uh

for some

.
For

and

, the linear system has fixed base points and therefore the divisor

is reducible.
For example, when
a=1
a basis for

is given by

and

. Thus,

D+\divv(x-u)=3P_{0}+P_{1}+\divv(x-u)=P_{0}+P_{1}+(u,v)+(u,v^{\prime})

where

and

.
For all such

, we conclude

as

is irreducible.
It remains to consider

and
a=4
.
First suppose
a=3
.
A basis of

is given by

g\coloneqq\frac{y-1}{x^{3}},\quad h\coloneqq 1.

Then

D=4P_{0}+3(P_{1}-P_{0})+\divv(g-u)

for some

.
We can view

4
morphism

, and through this lense

.
Let

.
We obtain a plane model for

C(r,s):\;4r^{4}+s^{2}r^{3}+2s=0.

With respect to the plane model

, the degree

.
The discriminant of

\Delta_{r}(C)=(2s)^{2}\cdot(-3^{3}s^{8}+2^{15}s).

Note that

.

Write

It immediately follows that

Proof of Theorem
1
:
n=8

Let
K
be a quartic number field such that
\Gal(\tilde{K}/\mathbb{Q})=A_{4}
, where
\tilde{K}
denotes the Galois closure of
K
. Suppose
P\in F_{8}(K)
is a non-trivial point.
Let
\pi
be the morphism defined in Lemma
7
.
Then
\pi(P)=Q
where
Q\in C_{8}(K)
.
Let

P_{0}=(0,1),\quad P_{1}=(0,-1)\in C_{8}(\mathbb{Q}).

Write
J
for the Jacobian of
C_{8}
. Let
D
be the effective degree
4
divisor obtained from taking the sum of the Galois conjugates of
\mathbb{Q}
.
Then
D-2P_{0}-2P_{1}\in J(\mathbb{Q})
.
Using
Magma
, we find that
J
has rank
0
over
\mathbb{Q}
. By applying the aforementioned algorithm of Müller and Reitsma
[
13
]
, we find that the torsion subgroup of
J(\mathbb{Q})
is of order
4
.
The point
[P_{1}-P_{0}]
has order
4
and thus

J(\mathbb{Q})=\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}\cdot[P_{1}-P_{0}].

Therefore

D-2P_{0}-2P_{1}\sim a(P_{1}-P_{0})

for some integer
0\leq a\leq 3
.
Therefore

D=2P_{0}+2P_{1}+a(P_{1}-P_{0})+\divv(f)

where
f\in L_{a}=L(2P_{0}+2P_{1}+a(P_{1}-P_{0}))
.
By Corollary
6
, the Riemann–Roch space
L_{a}
has dimension
3
for
a=0
and dimension
2
otherwise. For
a=0
, using
Magma
, we see that a basis of
L_{a}
is given by
1,1/x,1/x^{2}
. Similarly to the case of
n=7
, we conclude that
Q\in C_{8}(\mathbb{Q})
.
By Lemma
10
,

C_{8}(\mathbb{Q})=\{P_{0},\;P_{1}\}.

In this case, we conclude that
P\in F_{8}(K)
is a trivial point.

Next suppose
a=1
. Then
D=P_{0}+3P_{1}+\divv(f)=2P_{1}+P_{0}+P_{1}+\divv(f)
. Note that
P_{0}+P_{1}

is a hyperelliptic divisor. Thus the linear system is a
g_{4}^{1}
containing a
g_{2}^{1}
.
It follows that
D
is reducible, giving a contradiction. The case
a=3
is similar.

It remains to consider the case
a=2
.
Using
Magma
, we find that a basis of
L_{a}

is given by

g\coloneqq\frac{y+1}{x^{4}},\qquad h:=1

and so

D=4P_{1}+\divv(g-u)

for some
u\in\mathbb{Q}
.
As in the proof of the case
n=7
, we view
g
as a degree
4
morphism
C_{8}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{1}
and thus
D=g^{-1}(u)
.
Let
r=x,\,s=(y+1)/x^{4}
.
A plane model of
C_{8}
is then given by

C(r,s):\;4r^{4}+r^{4}s^{2}-2s=0

and the degree
4
morphism
g:C\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{1}
becomes
g(r,s)=s
.
The discriminant of
C(r,s)
with respect to
r
is

\Delta_{r}(C)=-2^{11}s^{3}(s^{2}+2^{2})^{3}.

Recall that
\Delta(\mathcal{O}_{K})
is a rational square where
\mathcal{O}_{K}
denotes the ring of integers of
K
, since
\Gal(\tilde{K}/\mathbb{Q})=A_{4}
.
It follows that
\Delta_{r}(C)
is a rational square.
In other words,
(s,\,\sqrt{\Delta_{r}(C)}/2^{5}s(s^{2}+4))\in H(\mathbb{Q})
where
H
is the curve defined by

H:\;y^{2}=-2x^{3}-8x.

Moreover
H
is isomorphic to the elliptic
curve
E/\mathbb{Q}
with Cremona label
64a4
given by the Weierstrass equation

E:\;y^{2}=x^{3}+16x.

Using
Magma
, we find that

E(\mathbb{Q})\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}.

Thus

H(\mathbb{Q})=\{\infty,\;(0,0)\}.

It is clear that neither of the rational points on
H
correspond to a quartic point
(r,s)
on the plane model
C
. This completes the proof.

6.
Proof of Theorem
2

Suppose
P=(\alpha:\beta:1)\in F_{6}(K)
.
Let

C_{6}:\;y^{2}=-4x^{6}+1.

Let

a=\alpha\beta,\quad b=\alpha^{6}-\beta^{6}.

Then
Q=(a,b)\in C_{6}(K)
by Lemma
7
.
We show that
Q
corresponds to a trivial point
P\in F_{6}(K)
.
Consider the Weierstrass model of the elliptic curve
E_{27}/\mathbb{Q}
with Cremona label
27a3
given by

E_{27}\;:\;y^{2}+y=x^{3}.

In particular, there is a degree
2
map from
C_{6}
to the elliptic curve
E_{27}
given by

\pi:C_{6}\rightarrow E_{27},\qquad(x,y)\mapsto\left(-x^{2},\;\frac{1}{2}(y-1)\right).

Consider now the Weierstrass model of the elliptic curve
E_{432}/\mathbb{Q}
with Cremona label
432b1
given by

E_{432}\;:\;y^{2}=x^{3}-4.

There is a degree
2
map from
C_{6}
to the elliptic curve
E_{432}
given by

C_{6}\rightarrow E_{432},\qquad(x,y)\mapsto\left(\dfrac{1}{x^{2}},\;\dfrac{y}{x^{3}}\right).

Let
E/\mathbb{Q}
be one of the elliptic curves
E_{27}
or
E_{432}
with
E(K)=E(\mathbb{Q})
. Then
Q\in C_{6}(\mathbb{Q})
by Lemma
11
. By Lemma
8
,

C_{6}(\mathbb{Q})=\{(0,1),\;(0,-1)\}.

Since
Q=(a,b)\in C_{6}(\mathbb{Q})
, this implies that
a=0
and
\alpha\beta=0
, i.e.,
P\in F_{6}(K)
is a trivial point.

7.
Proof of Theorem
3

Suppose
P=(\alpha:\beta:1)\in F_{8}(K)
.
Let

C_{8}:\;y^{2}=-4x^{8}+1.

Let

a=\alpha\beta,\quad b=\alpha^{8}-\beta^{8}.

Then
Q=(a,b)\in C_{8}(K)
by Lemma
7
.
We work with the Weierstrass model of the elliptic curve
E/\mathbb{Q}
with Cremona label
64a4
given by

E\;:\;y^{2}=x^{3}+x.

Recall there is a degree
2
map
\pi
from
C_{8}
to the elliptic curve
E
given by

\pi:C_{8}\rightarrow E,\qquad(x,y)\mapsto\left(\dfrac{1-y}{2x^{4}},\;\dfrac{y-1}{2x^{6}}\right).

Recall that
E(K)=E(\mathbb{Q})
by assumption, and therefore
Q\in C_{8}(\mathbb{Q})
by Lemma
11
.
By Lemma
10
,

C_{8}(\mathbb{Q})=\{(0,1),\;(0,-1)\}.

Thus if
Q=(a,b)\in C_{8}(K)=C_{8}(\mathbb{Q})
then
a=0
, and moreover
\alpha\beta=0
, i.e.,
P\in F_{8}(K)
is a trivial point.
