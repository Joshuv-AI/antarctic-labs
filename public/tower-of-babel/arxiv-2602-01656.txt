Numerical methods for diffusion coefficient recovery
Sahat Pandapotan Nainggolan, Julius Fergy Tiongson Rabago, Hirofumi Notsu

Abstract
We revisit the inverse problem of reconstructing a spatially varying diffusion coefficient in stationary elliptic equations from boundary Cauchy data. From a theoretical perspective, we introduce a gradient-weighted modification of the coupled complex-boundary method (CCBM) incorporating an H 1 H^{1} -type term, and formulate the reconstruction as a regularized optimization problem over bounded admissible coefficients. We establish continuity and differentiability of the forward map, Lipschitz continuity of the modified cost functional, existence of minimizers, stability with respect to noisy data, and convergence under vanishing noise. From a numerical perspective, reconstructions are computed using a Sobolev-gradient descent scheme and evaluated through extensive numerical experiments across a range of noise levels, boundary inputs, and coefficient structures. In the reported tests, for sufficiently large but not excessive H 1 H^{1} -weights, the modified CCBM is observed to yield more stable reconstructions and to reduce certain high-frequency artifacts. Across the numerical scenarios considered in this study, the method often demonstrates favorable stability and robustness properties relative to several classical boundary-based formulations, although performance remains problem- and parameter-dependent. A projection-based extension further supports stable recovery of piecewise-constant diffusion coefficients in multi-subregion test cases. Our results indicate that, as long as all subdomains share a portion of the boundary, the proposed CCBM-based Tikhonov regularization approach with a pick-a-point strategy enables stable and reliable reconstruction of diffusion parameters.

Introduction

This work is concerned with the numerical recovery of a diffusion (or conductivity) coefficient in stationary elliptic partial differential equations. Let
\varOmega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\{2,3\}
, be a bounded, open, and simply connected domain with Lipschitz boundary
\varGamma:=\partial\varOmega
. We consider the second-order elliptic boundary value problem

(1)

\mathcal{L}u\equiv-\nabla\cdot(\alpha\nabla u)+b\cdot\nabla u+c\,u=Q\quad\text{in }\varOmega,\qquad u=f\quad\text{on }\varGamma,

where
u:\varOmega\to\mathbb{R}
denotes the state variable, and the coefficients
\alpha:\varOmega\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
b:\varOmega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
c:\varOmega\to\mathbb{R}
represent diffusion, advection, and reaction effects, respectively.

The inverse problem consists of recovering the spatially varying diffusion coefficient
\alpha
from boundary Cauchy data, namely the Dirichlet trace of
u
and its normal flux on
\varGamma
. This is a coefficient identification problem for a steady-state elliptic equation.

Although (
1
) allows for advection and reaction effects, we focus on the diffusion-dominated case

(2)

-\nabla\cdot(\alpha\nabla u)=Q\quad\text{in }\varOmega,\qquad u=f\quad\text{on }\varGamma,

which corresponds to the classical inverse conductivity problem (ICP), also known as electrical impedance tomography (EIT)
[
24
,
25
,
7
,
43
]
. The diffusion coefficient
\alpha\in L^{\infty}(\varOmega)
is assumed bounded and sufficiently regular. In this work, we consider both smooth and piecewise-defined diffusion coefficients, thereby complementing existing studies that typically emphasize one of these settings. Extensions to models incorporating advection and reaction effects are left for future work.

Boundary measurements are taken as Cauchy pairs
(f,g)
of Dirichlet data and Neumann fluxes. In practice, such data are often collected via electrodes, and the Complete Electrode Model (CEM) accounts for electrode geometry and contact impedance
[
10
,
40
,
22
]
. Electrode effects can significantly influence measurements and should be modeled or corrected
[
9
,
38
,
15
]
. Although our analysis is formulated for continuum Cauchy data, it extends naturally to CEM settings
[
44
]
.

Recovering spatially varying coefficients in elliptic equations is a nonlinear and severely ill-posed inverse problem
[
27
,
28
]
. Uniqueness from a single boundary measurement is generally not guaranteed, whereas global uniqueness holds when the full Dirichlet-to-Neumann map is available
[
42
,
41
,
30
,
8
,
4
]
. For
d\geq 3
, uniqueness requires sufficient smoothness; in two dimensions, it extends to less regular coefficients, including
W^{2,p}(\varOmega)
for
p>1
and
L^{\infty}(\varOmega)

[
30
,
4
]
. Stability estimates, typically logarithmic and conditional, have been established under additional assumptions
[
29
,
3
,
6
]
.

In this work, we consider reconstruction from a single Cauchy pair
(f,g)
, for which identifiability is generally not guaranteed for arbitrary coefficients. A common approach is therefore to impose structural constraints on the unknown parameter, such as piecewise-constant or low-complexity models
[
24
]
. A representative example is

\alpha=1+\sigma\chi_{\omega},

where
\chi_{\omega}
denotes the characteristic function of an unknown subdomain
\omega\subset\varOmega
and
\sigma
is an unknown constant
[
10
,
40
]
. In view of these limitations, we focus on regularized numerical reconstruction from limited boundary data, with an emphasis on stability and robustness rather than strict uniqueness guarantees.

To address ill-posedness and measurement noise
[
24
]
, we adopt a regularized optimization framework. Given
\underline{\alpha},\overline{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, we define

\mathcal{A}:=\{\alpha\in L^{\infty}(\varOmega)\mid\underline{\alpha}<\alpha(x)<\overline{\alpha}\ \text{ a.e. in }\varOmega\}\subset L^{\infty}_{+}(\varOmega)

and introduce a regularization functional
R(\alpha)
with parameter
\rho>0
. We denote by
\bar{\mathcal{A}}
the closure of
\mathcal{A}
in
L^{\infty}(\varOmega)
.

Let
u
solve the forward problem (
2
) for a given
\alpha\in\overline{\mathcal{A}}
and Dirichlet boundary data
f
. The Dirichlet-to-Neumann (DtN) map is defined as

\Lambda_{\alpha}:H^{1/2}(\varGamma)\to H^{-1/2}(\varGamma),\quad\Lambda_{\alpha}f:=\alpha{}\partial_{n}u\big|_{\varGamma},

which maps the Dirichlet trace
f
to the corresponding Neumann flux. Its inverse,
\Lambda_{\alpha}^{-1}
, maps Neumann fluxes to the corresponding Dirichlet trace, with the additive constant fixed by, e.g.,
\int_{\varGamma}v=0
.

Reconstruction is formulated as the minimization of suitable cost functionals:

(i)

Boundary-data tracking:

Let
u\in H^{1}(\varOmega)
solve (
2
) with Dirichlet data
f
(i.e., in the weak sense). Define the Neumann-tracking functional

(3)

J_{N,\rho}(\alpha):=\|{\Lambda_{\alpha}f-g}\|_{L^{2}(\varGamma)}^{2}+\rho{}R(\alpha),

and consider the minimization problem
\min_{\alpha\in\overline{\mathcal{A}}}J_{N,\rho}(\alpha)
.
For pure Neumann data, one may equivalently track Dirichlet traces via the inverse DtN map
\Lambda_{\alpha}^{-1}
:

(4)

J_{D,\rho}(\alpha):=\|{\Lambda_{\alpha}^{-1}g-f}\|_{L^{2}(\varGamma)}^{2}+\rho{}R(\alpha),

where
v=\Lambda_{\alpha}^{-1}g
solves the Neumann problem with
\int_{\varGamma}v\,ds=0
, and
\min_{\alpha\in\overline{\mathcal{A}}}J_{D,\rho}(\alpha)
.

Here,
\rho>0
is a regularization parameter that balances the data misfit and the regularization term, introduced to mitigate ill-posedness and promote stable reconstructions.

(ii)

Kohn–Vogelius method:

Let
u_{D},u_{N}\in H^{1}(\varOmega)
solve the Dirichlet and Neumann problems, respectively:

(5)

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(\alpha\nabla u_{D})

\displaystyle=Q\quad\text{in }\varOmega,

\displaystyle u_{D}

\displaystyle=f\quad\text{on }\varGamma,

(6)

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(\alpha\nabla u_{N})

\displaystyle=Q\quad\text{in }\varOmega,

\displaystyle\alpha{}\partial_{n}u_{N}

\displaystyle=g\quad\text{on }\varGamma,\quad\int_{\varGamma}u_{N}\,ds=0,

where the integral constraint ensures uniqueness of
u_{N}
. The Kohn–Vogelius functional is

(7)

J_{\mathrm{KV},\rho}(\alpha):=\int_{\varOmega}\alpha{}|\nabla(u_{D}-u_{N})|^{2}\,dx+\int_{\varGamma}|u_{D}-u_{N}|^{2}\,ds+\rho{}R(\alpha),

and the corresponding optimization problem reads
\min_{\alpha\in\overline{\mathcal{A}}}J_{\mathrm{KV},\rho}(\alpha)
.

(iii)

Coupled complex-boundary method (CCBM):

Let
w\in H^{1}(\varOmega;\mathbb{C})
satisfy the complex Robin condition

\alpha{}\partial_{n}w+iw=g+if\quad\text{on }\varGamma.

The CCBM functional is

(8)

J_{\mathrm{CCBM},\rho}(\alpha):=\int_{\varOmega}\Im\{w\}^{2}\,dx+\rho{}R(\alpha),

and the optimization problem is
\min_{\alpha\in\overline{\mathcal{A}}}J_{\mathrm{CCBM},\rho}(\alpha)
.

The CCBM approach, originally introduced in
[
11
]
for an inverse source problem,
has since been applied to a wide range of inverse problems. Early applications include
conductivity reconstruction
[
19
]
, parameter identification in elliptic systems

[
46
]
, shape reconstruction
[
2
]
, and geometric inverse
source problems
[
1
]
. It has also been employed in more complex
settings, such as exterior Bernoulli problems
[
36
]
, free surface flows

[
35
]
, inverse Cauchy problems for Stokes systems
[
33
]
, obstacle detection in Stokes flow
[
34
]
, tumor localization
[
37
]
, approximation of unknown sources in a time fractional PDE
[
21
]
, and recently in bioluminescence tomography
[
45
]
.

In this work, we build on these developments and revisit conductivity reconstruction

[
19
]
by introducing a gradient-weighted modification of the classical CCBM cost
functional. We also propose corresponding numerical methods to improve reconstruction quality.
In our numerical experiments, the modified approach with the additional gradient term showed indications of better control over high-frequency components and potentially improved stability.

Overall, in both free-boundary
[
36
]
and free-surface
[
35
]

problems, CCBM has consistently shown improved stability and accuracy compared with standard
least-squares methods, while maintaining computational costs comparable to Kohn–Vogelius formulations.
In this work, we aim to highlight these advantages over classical methods by conducting extensive
numerical experiments in the context of diffusion coefficient recovery.

In the general elliptic setting (
1
), the recovery of
\alpha
extends naturally. Let the forward operator be

\mathcal{F}:\overline{\mathcal{A}}\to H^{-1/2}(\varGamma),\quad\mathcal{F}(\alpha):=\alpha{}\partial_{n}u\big|_{\varGamma},

where
u
solves (
1
) with given Dirichlet data
f
. The inverse problem then reads:

Problem 1.1

(Inverse Problem)
.

Given Cauchy data
(f,g)
on
\varGamma
, find
\alpha\in\overline{\mathcal{A}}
such that
u
solves (
1
) and

\alpha{}\partial_{n}u\big|_{\varGamma}=g.

This problem can be addressed via regularized optimization in direct analogy with the methods presented for the diffusion-dominated case,
i.e.
, boundary-data tracking, Kohn–Vogelius-type functionals, or the coupled complex-boundary method (CCBM) as described in (
3
)–(
8
).

In this paper, the theoretical analysis and main results are presented for the
modified
coupled complex-boundary method (CCBM), introduced in Subsection
2.3
. The boundary-data tracking and Kohn–Vogelius formulations are included mainly for numerical comparison and completeness. While not pursued here, similar theoretical results are expected to hold under suitable assumptions and minor technical adjustments.

Position of the paper in the literature.

•

To improve stability and mitigate noise amplification, we employ a Sobolev-gradient formulation, which smooths the descent direction and contributes to a more stable reconstruction process.

•

When considering a piecewise-constant diffusion coefficient, we employ a first-order gradient-based descent method combined with a pick-a-point strategy, rather than the second-order sequential quadratic programming (SQP) methods used in previous work
[
19
]
. At each iteration, a representative point is selected within each subregion, and its corresponding coefficient value is assigned uniformly across that subregion. This yields a simple quasi-heuristic framework, which appears particularly suited to recovering piecewise diffusion coefficients when the number of subregions is relatively small. The approach could potentially be extended to related inverse problems with piecewise-defined coefficients, subject to appropriate assumptions and problem-specific modifications.

•

Through extensive numerical experiments involving both smooth and piecewise diffusion coefficients, we observe that the CCBM yields stable and accurate reconstructions and, in the test cases considered, compares favorably with other established methods, including under moderate noise levels. These findings help to further substantiate the motivation for introducing the CCBM as a non-conventional approach to diffusion coefficient recovery. In this sense, the present work may be viewed as a complementary numerical study to
[
19
,
11
]
.

•

We also propose a modest modification of the classical CCBM cost functional by incorporating a weighted tracking of the energy of the imaginary part of the solution, balancing its
L^{2}
- and
H^{1}
-type contributions (see (
23
)). While the classical CCBM formulation already exhibits effective performance with an
L^{2}
-type tracking term, the numerical experiments presented in this work indicate that further improvements can be achieved by including an
H^{1}
contribution with a sufficiently large weight.

The numerical framework proposed in this work suggests potential applicability to related inverse problems involving piecewise-defined parameters, including absorption coefficient estimation and source identification, thereby further motivating the present study.

Paper organization.

The section also presents auxiliary analytical results, including continuity and differentiability of the forward map and key properties of the modified cost functional.

2.1
Notations and the problem setting

Let
\varOmega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\{2,3\}
, be an open bounded domain with Lipschitz boundary
\varGamma:=\partial\varOmega
. The Lebesgue spaces
L^{2}(\varOmega)
and
L^{2}(\varGamma)
are used throughout, and
H^{1/2}(\varGamma)
denotes the trace space of
H^{1}(\varOmega)
.
Unless otherwise stated, the same notation is used for real- and complex-valued functions.
Nevertheless, we ocassionally write a complex-valued functions space
X(\varOmega)
as
X(\varOmega;\mathbb{C})
only for emphasis.
For complex-valued functions, the

inner product is defined by
(u,v)_{1,\varOmega}:=(\nabla u,\nabla v)_{0,\varOmega}+(u,v)_{0,\varOmega}
, where
(u,v)_{0,\varOmega}=\int_{\varOmega}u\,{v}\,dx
.
Throughout the paper, the complex conjugate is omitted from the notation whenever the meaning is clear from the context. The associated norm is given by
\|{u}\|_{1,\varOmega}^{2}=(u,u)_{1,\varOmega}
.
Moreover, we denote by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\varGamma}
the duality pairing between

H^{-1/2}(\varGamma)
and
H^{1/2}(\varGamma)
, which coincides with the

L^{2}(\varGamma)
inner product whenever both arguments are in
L^{2}(\varGamma)
.
Also, for simplicity, we write
\|{\cdot}\|_{\infty,\varOmega}
for the
L^{\infty}(\varOmega)
-norm.
Furthermore, we adopt a unified notation for norms of scalar- and vector-valued functions.
Lastly, throughout the paper,
C>0
denotes a generic constant that may change from line to line.

We formulate the inverse problem of identifying the diffusion coefficient
\alpha
within the framework of the coupled complex boundary method. In this setting, the inverse problem reduces to enforcing the vanishing of the imaginary part of the solution in
\varOmega

Problem 2.1
.

Determine
\alpha\in\overline{\mathcal{A}}
such that
u_{i}=0
in
\varOmega

c\neq 0
, where

u=u_{r}+iu_{i}:\varOmega\to\mathbb{C}

(9)

\mathcal{L}u=Q\quad\text{in }\varOmega,\qquad\alpha\partial_{n}{u}+iu=g+if\quad\text{on }\varGamma.

For analytical purposes, we impose the following conditions on the data and coefficients:

Assumption 2.2
.

(A1)

Q\in L^{2}(\varOmega)
,
b\in W^{1,\infty}(\varOmega)^{d}
, and
c\in L^{\infty}(\varOmega)
.

(A2)

b\cdot n\geq 0
a.e. on
\varGamma
.

(A3)

c-\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot b\geq\underline{\alpha}>0
a.e. in
\varOmega
.

Given Assumption
2.2
, we formulate the weak form of the complex boundary value problem (
9
).
Define
B:H^{1}(\varOmega)\times H^{1}(\varOmega)\to\mathbb{C}
and
l:H^{1}(\varOmega)\to\mathbb{C}
by

(10)

\displaystyle B(u,v;\alpha)

\displaystyle=(\alpha\nabla u,\nabla{v})_{0,\varOmega}+(b\cdot\nabla u,{v})_{0,\varOmega}+(cu,{v})_{0,\varOmega}+i{}\langle u,{v}\rangle_{\varGamma},

(11)

\displaystyle l(v)

\displaystyle=(Q,{v})_{0,\varOmega}+\langle g,{v}\rangle_{\varGamma}+i{}\langle f,{v}\rangle_{\varGamma},

where
f,g,:\varGamma\to\mathbb{R}
, and
f,g\in H^{-1/2}(\varGamma)
.

Problem 2.3
.

Find
u\in H^{1}(\varOmega)
such that
B(u,v;\alpha)=l(v)
, for all
v\in H^{1}(\varOmega)
.

Let
u=u_{r}+iu_{i}:\varOmega\to\mathbb{C}
.
The real and imaginary parts satisfy

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} -\nabla\cdot(\alpha\nabla u_{r})+b\cdot\nabla u_{r}+cu_{r}&=Q&&\text{in }\varOmega,\\
\alpha\partial_{n}{u_{r}}-u_{i}&=g&&\text{on }\varGamma,\end{aligned}\end{cases}\quad\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} -\nabla\cdot(\alpha\nabla u_{i})+b\cdot\nabla u_{i}+cu_{i}&=0&&\text{in }\varOmega,\\
\alpha\partial_{n}{u_{i}}+u_{r}&=f&&\text{on }\varGamma.\end{aligned}\end{cases}

Under Assumption
2.2
, the sesquilinear form
B(\cdot,\cdot;\alpha)
is coercive for all
\alpha\in\overline{\mathcal{A}}
, and thus Problem
2.3
admits a unique weak solution (Lemma
2.4
). In this case, the imaginary part
u_{i}
vanishes in
\varOmega
if and only if
(u,\alpha)
solves Problem
1.1
.
If
u_{i}
is identically zero in
\varOmega
, then
u=u_{r}
satisfies Problem
1.1
with the Cauchy data
f
and
g
. Conversely, if
(u,\alpha)
solves Problem
1.1
, the imaginary part of the corresponding complex solution is zero in
\varOmega
.
As a result, the Dirichlet data of the original problem is recovered as
f=u_{r}
on
\varGamma
, and Problem
2.1
is fully equivalent to Problem
1.1
.

The well-posedness of Problem
2.3
, which follows from the complex version of the Lax–Milgram lemma

(see also
[

Lemma 2.4

(Coercivity, well-posedness, and stability)
.

Let Assumption
2.2
hold and let
\alpha\in\overline{\mathcal{A}}
, so that

\alpha\geq\underline{\alpha}>0
a.e. in
\varOmega
.
Then the sesquilinear form
B(\cdot,\cdot;\alpha)
is coercive on
H^{1}(\varOmega)
, i.e.,
there exists a constant
c_{B}>0
, independent of
\alpha
, such that

\Re\{B(v,v;\alpha)\}\geq c_{B}\,\|{v}\|_{1,\varOmega}^{2},\qquad\forall v\in H^{1}(\varOmega).

Consequently, for any
Q\in L^{2}(\varOmega)
and
f,g\in H^{-1/2}(\varGamma)
, Problem
2.3
admits a unique solution

u\in H^{1}(\varOmega)
, which satisfies the stability estimate

(12)

\|{u}\|_{1,\varOmega}\leq\frac{C}{c_{B}}\Big(\|{Q}\|_{0,\varOmega}+\|{f}\|_{-1/2,\varGamma}+\|{g}\|_{-1/2,\varGamma}\Big),

where
C>0
depends only on
\varOmega
and the coefficients
b
and
c
,
but is independent of
\alpha
.

Proof.

Let
v\in H^{1}(\varOmega)
. Since
\Re\{i(v,v)_{0,\varGamma}\}=0
, we have

\Re\{B(v,v;\alpha)\}=(\alpha\nabla v,\nabla v)_{0,\varOmega}+\Re\{(b\cdot\nabla v,v)_{0,\varOmega}\}+(cv,v)_{0,\varOmega}.

Using integration by parts and Assumption
2.2
(A1), we obtain

\Re\{(b\cdot\nabla v,v)_{0,\varOmega}\}=-\frac{1}{2}\big((\nabla\cdot b)v,v\big)_{0,\varOmega}+\frac{1}{2}(b\cdot nv,v)_{0,\varGamma}.

By Assumption
2.2
(A2), the boundary term is nonnegative, and
by Assumption
2.2
(A3),

(cv,v)_{0,\varOmega}-\frac{1}{2}\big((\nabla\cdot b)v,v\big)_{0,\varOmega}\geq\underline{\alpha}\,\|{v}\|_{L^{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}.

Moreover, since
\alpha\geq\underline{\alpha}
a.e. in
\varOmega
,

(\alpha\nabla v,\nabla v)_{0,\varOmega}\geq\underline{\alpha}\,\|{\nabla v}\|_{L^{2}(\varOmega)^{d}}^{2}
.
Collecting the estimates yields

\Re\{B(v,v;\alpha)\}\geq\underline{\alpha}\Big(\|{\nabla v}\|_{L^{2}(\varOmega)^{d}}^{2}+\|{v}\|_{L^{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}\Big)=\underline{\alpha}\,\|{v}\|_{1,\varOmega}^{2}.

Setting
c_{B}:=\underline{\alpha}
completes the coercivity proof.

The boundedness of the sesquilinear form
B(\cdot,\cdot;\alpha)
and of the linear
functional, as well as the stability estimate, follow from standard arguments and
are therefore omitted. Uniqueness follows from coercivity by standard arguments.
∎

, Lemma

guarantees that,

).

2.2
Continuity and differentiability of the forward map

The continuity of the forward map is stated next.

Proposition 2.5
.

The forward map
F\colon\overline{\mathcal{A}}\subset L^{\infty}(\varOmega)\to H^{1}(\varOmega)
is Lipschitz continuous.
More precisely, there exists a constant
C>0
, independent of
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\overline{\mathcal{A}}
, such that

(14)

\|{F(\alpha_{1})-F(\alpha_{2})}\|_{1,\varOmega}\leq\frac{C}{c_{B}^{2}}\,\|{\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\varOmega)},\quad\forall\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\overline{\mathcal{A}}.

Equivalently, the Fréchet derivative of
F
satisfies the operator-norm bound

\|{F^{\prime}(\alpha)}\|_{\mathcal{B}(L^{\infty}(\varOmega),H^{1}(\varOmega))}\leq\frac{C}{c_{B}^{2}},\quad\forall\alpha\in\overline{\mathcal{A}}.

We next study the differentiability of the forward map

F\colon\overline{\mathcal{A}}\to H^{1}(\varOmega)
defined in (
13
).
Let
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
and let
{\beta}\in L^{\infty}(\varOmega)
be such that

\alpha+{\beta}\in\overline{\mathcal{A}}

We define
\delta F:=F(\alpha+{\beta})-F(\alpha)\in H^{1}(\varOmega)
.
By definition of
F
and subtracting the corresponding variational problems,

\delta F
satisfies

(15)

B(\delta F,v;\alpha+{\beta})=-({\beta}\nabla u,\nabla{v})_{0,\varOmega},\qquad\forall v\in H^{1}(\varOmega),

where

.

This formulation allows us to identify the derivative of

by passing to the
limit as
{\beta}\to 0

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
.
Then the forward map
F
is Fréchet differentiable at
\alpha
.
For any
{\beta}\in L^{\infty}(\varOmega)

B(u^{\prime},v;\alpha)=-({\beta}\nabla u,\nabla{v})_{0,\varOmega},\qquad\forall v\in H^{1}(\varOmega),

where

.
Moreover, the following estimate holds:

(17)

\|{F^{\prime}(\alpha){\beta}}\|_{1,\varOmega}\leq\frac{C}{c_{B}^{2}}\,\|{{\beta}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\varOmega)}.

Remark 2.7

For
\alpha\in\partial{\overline{\mathcal{A}}}:=\overline{\mathcal{A}}\setminus\mathcal{A}

We now turn to the second-order differentiability of the forward map

\displaystyle B(\delta^{2}F,v;\alpha+{\beta}_{1})

\displaystyle-({\beta}_{1}\nabla F^{\prime}(\alpha){\beta}_{2},\nabla{v})_{0,\varOmega},\qquad\forall v\in H^{1}(\varOmega).

Passing to the limit as

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
.
Then the forward map

is twice Fréchet differentiable at
\alpha

with respect to admissible directions.
For any admissible

the second derivative

With the continuity and differentiability of the forward map established (see Propositions

as a regularized optimization problem suitable for numerical approximation via Tikhonov regularization.

2.3
CCBM-based optimization reformulation of the inverse problem

For
\alpha\in\overline{\mathcal{A}}
, let

be the unique weak solution of (

With a fixed
\rho>0
, the inverse problem reduces to minimizing the regularized functional

(21)

J_{\rho}(\alpha):=J(\alpha)+R_{\rho}(\alpha).

Note that
\beta\in L^{\infty}(\varOmega)\subset L^{2}(\varOmega)
(so that
\|{\beta}\|_{0,\varOmega}\leq|{\varOmega}|^{1/2}\|{\beta}\|_{L^{\infty}(\varOmega)}
; see
[

Accordingly, unless stated otherwise, we choose the (Tikhonov) regularization term as

We further introduce a modified CCBM-type misfit functional defined by

(23)

J(\alpha):=\frac{1}{2}\Big(w_{0}\|{u_{i}(\alpha)}\|_{0,\varOmega}^{2}+w_{1}\|{\nabla u_{i}(\alpha)}\|_{0,\varOmega}^{2}\Big),

where

are fixed weighting parameters.

The inclusion of the gradient term
\|{\nabla u_{i}(\alpha)}\|_{0,\varOmega}^{2}
in
(

For

, the weighted misfit

controls the

-norm of

, i.e.,

while

provides control only in
L^{2}(\varOmega)
.
In the numerical examples considered here, moderately large

generally improves
reconstruction quality by penalizing oscillatory components and promoting smoother
states, though excessively large

may reduce resolution.

For technical reasons, in particular to ensure strict convexity of the objective functional (see Proposition

), we impose the following assumption.

Assumption 2.9
.

Assume
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathcal{A}
is finite-dimensional, closed, and convex.

A case of particular interest is when

consists of piecewise constant diffusion coefficients on a prescribed partition of
\varOmega

\{\alpha\in P_{m}(\varOmega)\mid\underline{\alpha}\leq\alpha\leq\overline{\alpha}\}
and bounded trigonometric expansions

, where
\mathcal{T}_{N}:=\operatorname{span}\{\sin(k\cdot x),\cos(k\cdot x)\mid|k|\leq N\}
.

Assumption
2.9
is natural and may be viewed as a relaxation framework for convex functionals; consequently, standard Sobolev-gradient convergence theory, such as that in
[
18

, applies.

We consider the following problem.

Problem 2.10
.

Find
\alpha_{\rho}\in\mathcal{K}
such that
\alpha_{\rho}=\arg\min_{\alpha\in\mathcal{K}}J_{\rho}(\alpha)

Proof.

Let

2.5

In what follows, we establish convexity of

Let

Proposition 2.13
.

There exists
\rho_{0}>0
, independent of

Proof.

Let
\alpha,{\beta}\in\mathcal{K}
be arbitrary and
{\beta}\not\equiv 0

We prove strict convexity by showing coercivity in the

-sense of the second derivative

From (

On the other hand, since

is finite-dimensional, there exists

\|{{\beta}}\|_{0,\varOmega}\geq K\|{{\beta}}\|_{\infty,\varOmega}

Define

\displaystyle J_{\rho}^{\prime\prime}(\alpha)[{\beta},{\beta}]

\displaystyle=J_{0}+w_{0}(u_{i}(\alpha),u_{i}^{\prime\prime})_{0,\varOmega}+w_{1}(\nabla u_{i}(\alpha),\nabla u_{i}^{\prime\prime})_{0,\varOmega}+\rho\|{{\beta}}\|_{0,\varOmega}^{2}

Remark 2.14
.

The regularization parameter
\rho>0
cannot be chosen arbitrarily large or small.
If
\rho
is too large, the regularization term dominates, and the functional may become insensitive to
\alpha
, potentially losing strict convexity (or even convexity in general). This behavior occurs not only for the CCBM functional but also for the boundary-data tracking functionals

and

, as well as the Kohn–Vogelius functional

; see subsection

for a numerical illustration of this effect.
If
\rho
is too small, coercivity is weakened and the estimate involving
\epsilon
in the strict convexity proof (Proposition
2.13

Thus, the admissible range of
\rho
is restricted, which in turn constrains the choice of
\epsilon
for which strict convexity holds.

Thanks to the strict convexity of the functional, this condition uniquely identifies the minimizer; however, it should be interpreted with care in the context of approximation or numerical errors.

Since

3

In the presence of noisy boundary data, we consider the Tikhonov-regularized inverse problem in the finite-dimensional subspace
\mathcal{K}
(Assumption
2.9
) and summarize its key properties, including existence of minimizers, continuous dependence on the data, and convergence to the exact solution under Assumption

.

Assumption 3.1
.

There exists a unique
\alpha^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{K}
such that

u^{\dagger}=F(\alpha^{\dagger})
satisfies

in
\varOmega
, i.e.,
\alpha^{\dagger}
is the exact coefficient producing the measured Neumann data
g
on
\varGamma
.

3.1
Convergence results

In practice, boundary measurements are affected by noise. We model this by

(32)

g^{\delta}=g+\eta,\qquad\|{\eta}\|_{0,\varGamma}\leq\delta,

where
\delta>0
denotes the noise level. Substituting
g^{\delta}
for
g
in Problem
2.3
yields the perturbed forward problem: find
u^{\delta}\in H^{1}(\varOmega)
such that

(33)

B(u^{\delta},v;\alpha)=l^{\delta}(v),\qquad\forall v\in H^{1}(\varOmega),

where
l^{\delta}(v):=(Q,v)_{0,\varOmega}+\langle g^{\delta},v\rangle_{\varGamma}+i\langle f,v\rangle_{\varGamma}
.

By standard stability arguments and Lemma
2.4
, there exists a constant
C>0
, independent of
\alpha
and
\delta
, such that

(34)

\|{u^{\delta}-u}\|_{1,\varOmega}\leq C\,\delta.

The corresponding Tikhonov functional under noisy data is defined as

(35)

J_{\rho}^{\delta}(\alpha):=\frac{1}{2}\Big(w_{0}\|{u_{i}^{\delta}(\alpha)}\|_{0,\varOmega}^{2}+w_{1}\|{\nabla u_{i}^{\delta}(\alpha)}\|_{0,\varOmega}^{2}\Big)+\frac{\rho}{2}\|{\alpha}\|_{0,\varOmega}^{2},\qquad\alpha\in\mathcal{K},

where
u^{\delta}(\alpha)=u_{r}^{\delta}(\alpha)+iu_{i}^{\delta}(\alpha)
solves (
33
).

We now consider the finite-dimensional regularized inverse problem with noisy data, aiming to recover a stable approximation of the diffusion coefficient in
\mathcal{K}
.

Problem 3.2
.

Find
\alpha_{\rho}^{\delta}\in\mathcal{K}
such that

(36)

J_{\rho}^{\delta}(\alpha_{\rho}^{\delta})=\min_{\alpha\in\mathcal{K}}J_{\rho}^{\delta}(\alpha).

The first question we address is whether a minimizer actually exists.

Proposition 3.3
.

Problem
3.2
admits at least one solution
\alpha_{\rho}^{\delta}\in\mathcal{K}
.

Once existence is established, it is natural to ask how sensitive the minimizer is to variations in the data.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let Assumption
2.9
hold, and let
\rho\geq\rho_{0}
as in Proposition
2.13
.
Let
\{g^{n}\}\subset H^{-1/2}(\varGamma)
satisfy

\|g^{n}-g^{\infty}\|_{H^{-1/2}(\varGamma)}\to 0\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

For each
n
, let
\alpha_{\rho}^{n}\in\mathcal{K}
denote the unique minimizer of
J_{\rho}^{n}
corresponding to

, and let

be the corresponding state.

Then, as

,

\alpha_{\rho}^{n}\to\alpha_{\rho}^{\infty}\quad\text{strongly in }L^{2}(\varOmega),\qquad u^{n}\to u^{\infty}\quad\text{strongly in }H^{1}(\varOmega),

where
\alpha_{\rho}^{\infty}
and

correspond to

.

Remark 3.5
.

In Proposition
3.4
, the Neumann data are assumed to lie in the natural trace space

.
If instead

Indeed, the trace embedding

so that the forward map

remains Lipschitz continuous with respect to the

-norm of the boundary data.
Therefore, Proposition
3.4
holds with
H^{-1/2}(\varGamma)
replaced by
L^{2}(\varGamma)
.

Finally, we examine the behavior of the regularized solutions as both noise and regularization vanish. This establishes the convergence of the numerical scheme towards the exact solution.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
\{\delta_{n}\}\to 0
and let
\rho_{n}=\rho(\delta_{n})\to 0
.
For each

, let

, there exists a subsequence such that

\alpha_{\rho_{n}}^{\delta_{n}}\to\alpha^{\dagger}\quad\text{in }L^{2}(\varOmega),\qquad F^{\delta_{n}}(\alpha_{\rho_{n}}^{\delta_{n}})\rightharpoonup u^{\dagger}\text{ in }H^{1}(\varOmega).

The convergence above guarantees that, in the vanishing-noise and vanishing-regularization limit, the finite-dimensional Tikhonov minimizers recover the exact diffusion coefficient
\alpha^{\dagger}
.
This establishes the well-posedness of the regularized inverse problem in
\mathcal{K}
.

3.2
Convergence proofs

3.2.1
Proof of existence of a minimizer

Proof of Proposition
3.3
.

Let
f
be the imposed Dirichlet input and
g
the associated Neumann measurement. These data are assumed fixed throughout the following arguments.

Since
J_{\rho}(\alpha)\geq 0
for all
\alpha\in\mathcal{K}
, we can define
j_{\rho}:=\inf_{\alpha\in\mathcal{K}}J_{\rho}(\alpha)\geq 0
.
Let
\{\alpha_{n}\}:=\{\alpha_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{K}
be a minimizing sequence such that
J_{\rho}(\alpha_{n})\to j_{\rho}
as
n\to\infty
.
By definition of
J_{\rho}
and the coercivity of the regularization term, we have

J_{\rho}(\alpha)=J(\alpha)+R_{\rho}(\alpha)=J(\alpha)+\frac{\rho}{2}\|{\alpha}\|_{0,\varOmega}^{2}\geq\frac{\rho}{2}\|{\alpha}\|_{0,\varOmega}^{2},

for any
\alpha\in\mathcal{K}
, since
J(\alpha)\geq 0
.
Furthermore, there exists a constant
C>0
such that
J_{\rho}(\alpha_{n})\leq C
for all
n
.
Combining the two inequalities gives
\|{\alpha_{n}}\|_{0,\varOmega}\leq\sqrt{2C/\rho}
, for all
n
.
This shows that
\{\alpha_{n}\}
is bounded in
L^{2}(\varOmega)
.

Since
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathcal{A}\subset L^{\infty}(\varOmega)
is finite-dimensional (Assumption
2.9
), all norms on
\mathcal{K}
are equivalent and every bounded sequence in
\mathcal{K}
is relatively compact.
Hence, there exists a subsequence (still denoted
\{\alpha_{n}\}
) and some
\alpha_{\rho}\in\mathcal{K}
such that
\alpha_{n}\to\alpha_{\rho}
strongly in
L^{2}(\varOmega)
and in
L^{\infty}(\varOmega)
.

Let
u_{n}=F(\alpha_{n})\in H^{1}(\varOmega)
and
u_{\rho}=F(\alpha_{\rho})\in H^{1}(\varOmega)
denote the corresponding state solutions. By the Lipschitz continuity of
F
(Proposition
2.5
), we have

\|{u_{n}-u_{\rho}}\|_{1,\varOmega}\leq\frac{C}{c_{B}^{2}}\|{\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{\rho}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\varOmega)}\to 0.

The functional
J_{\rho}
is continuous with respect to
\alpha\in\mathcal{K}
because
J(\alpha)
depends continuously on
u_{i}(\alpha)
and
\nabla u_{i}(\alpha)
(via (
23
)), and
R_{\rho}(\alpha)
is continuous.
Hence,
J_{\rho}(\alpha_{\rho})=\lim_{n\to\infty}J_{\rho}(\alpha_{n})=j_{\rho}
.

Thus,
\alpha_{\rho}\in\mathcal{K}
attains the minimum of
J_{\rho}
, proving existence.
∎

3.2.2
Proof of continuous dependence to the measured data

Proof of Proposition
3.4
.

Let
\{g^{n}\}\subset H^{-1/2}(\varGamma)
satisfy
\|{g^{n}-g^{\infty}}\|_{H^{-1/2}(\varGamma)}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, and let
u^{n}(\alpha):=F(\alpha;g^{n})
denote the unique solution of the weak problem

B(u^{n}(\alpha),v;\alpha)=(Q,v)_{0,\varOmega}+\langle g^{n},v\rangle_{\varGamma}+i\langle f,v\rangle_{\varGamma},\quad\forall v\in H^{1}(\varOmega),

for each
\alpha\in\mathcal{K}
.
The corresponding Tikhonov-regularized cost functional is

J_{\rho}^{n}(\alpha):=\frac{1}{2}\Big(w_{0}\|{u_{i}^{n}(\alpha)}\|_{0,\varOmega}^{2}+w_{1}\|{\nabla u_{i}^{n}(\alpha)}\|_{0,\varOmega}^{2}\Big)+\frac{\rho}{2}\|{\alpha}\|_{0,\varOmega}^{2}.

By Lemma
2.4
, the forward map depends continuously on the Neumann data, so for each fixed
\alpha\in\mathcal{K}
,

\|{u^{n}(\alpha)-u^{\infty}(\alpha)}\|_{1,\varOmega}\leq\frac{C}{c_{B}}\,\|{g^{n}-g^{\infty}}\|_{H^{-1/2}(\varGamma)}\longrightarrow 0,

which implies pointwise convergence of the functionals:
J_{\rho}^{n}(\alpha)\to J_{\rho}^{\infty}(\alpha)
.
Since
\mathcal{K}\subset L^{\infty}(\varOmega)
is finite-dimensional, the sequence
\{\alpha_{\rho}^{n}\}
is uniformly bounded and admits a strongly convergent subsequence in
L^{2}(\varOmega)
, say
\alpha_{\rho}^{n}\to\bar{\alpha}\in\mathcal{K}
.
By weak lower semicontinuity and since
\alpha_{\rho}^{n}
minimizes
J_{\rho}^{n}
, i.e.,
J_{\rho}^{n}(\alpha_{\rho}^{n})\leq J_{\rho}^{n}(\alpha_{\rho}^{\infty})
, we obtain

J_{\rho}^{\infty}(\bar{\alpha})\leq J_{\rho}^{\infty}(\alpha_{\rho}^{\infty}).

Strict convexity of
J_{\rho}^{\infty}
then yields
\bar{\alpha}=\alpha_{\rho}^{\infty}
. Hence, the full sequence converges strongly:
\alpha_{\rho}^{n}\to\alpha_{\rho}^{\infty}
in
L^{2}(\varOmega)
.

Finally, since
\{u^{n}(\alpha_{\rho}^{n})\}
is bounded in
H^{1}(\varOmega)
, up to a subsequence
u^{n}(\alpha_{\rho}^{n})\rightharpoonup\bar{u}
weakly in
H^{1}(\varOmega)
. Passing to the limit in the weak formulation and using
\alpha_{\rho}^{n}\to\alpha_{\rho}^{\infty}
yields

B(\bar{u},v;\alpha_{\rho}^{\infty})=(Q,v)_{0,\varOmega}+\langle g^{\infty},v\rangle_{\varGamma}+i\langle f,v\rangle_{\varGamma},

for all
v\in H^{1}(\varOmega)
, so
\bar{u}=F(\alpha_{\rho}^{\infty};g^{\infty})
. Uniqueness of the weak limit then gives

u^{n}(\alpha_{\rho}^{n})\rightharpoonup u^{\infty}:=F(\alpha_{\rho}^{\infty};g^{\infty})\quad\text{in }H^{1}(\varOmega).

Moreover, by the Lipschitz continuity of
F
in both
\alpha
(Proposition
2.5
) and
g
(Lemma
2.4
), one can strengthen the convergence to strong convergence:

\|{u^{n}(\alpha_{\rho}^{n})-u^{\infty}}\|_{1,\varOmega}\longrightarrow 0\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

This completes the proof.
∎

3.2.3
Proof of convergence under vanishing noise

Proof of Proposition
3.6
.

In contrast to Proposition
3.4
, here both the measurement data
g^{\delta_{n}}
and the regularization parameter
\rho_{n}
vary with
n
.

Let
\{\delta_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence with
\delta_{n}\to 0
and let

\{\rho_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfy
\rho_{n}=\rho(\delta_{n})\to 0
.
For each
n
, denote by

\alpha^{n}:=\alpha_{\rho_{n}}^{\delta_{n}}\in\mathcal{K}

the minimizer of
J_{\rho}^{n}
corresponding to the noisy data
g^{\delta_{n}}
.
Set

u^{n}:=F^{\delta_{n}}(\alpha^{n})\in H^{1}(\varOmega),\qquad u_{i}^{n}:=\Im\{u^{n}\}.

Since
\alpha^{n}\in\mathcal{K}
, which is finite-dimensional and compact in
L^{2}(\varOmega)
, there exists a subsequence (not relabeled) and
\bar{\alpha}\in\mathcal{K}
such that

(37)

\alpha^{n}\to\bar{\alpha}\quad\text{strongly in }L^{2}(\varOmega).

By Lemma
2.4
, the sequence
\{u^{n}\}
is uniformly bounded in
H^{1}(\varOmega)
.
Hence, there exists
u^{\infty}\in H^{1}(\varOmega)
and a subsequence such that

(38)

u^{n}\rightharpoonup u^{\infty}\quad\text{weakly in }H^{1}(\varOmega),\qquad u^{n}\to u^{\infty}\quad\text{strongly in }L^{2}(\varOmega).

For each fixed
\alpha\in\mathcal{K}
, the continuity of the forward map with respect to the data (Lemma
2.4
and Proposition
2.5
) ensures that

F^{\delta_{n}}(\alpha)\to F(\alpha)\quad\text{in }H^{1}(\varOmega)\quad\text{as }\delta_{n}\to 0.

Combining this with the strong convergence (
37
) and the weak convergence (
38
), we can pass to the limit in the variational formulation of the state equation to conclude

u^{\infty}=F(\bar{\alpha}).

Next, since
\alpha^{n}
minimizes
J_{\rho}^{n}
over
\mathcal{K}
, we have

(39)

J_{\rho}^{n}(\alpha^{n})\leq J_{\rho}^{n}(\alpha^{\dagger}),\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N},

where
\alpha^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{K}
is the exact solution (Assumption
3.1
).

Now, note that
\alpha^{\dagger}
is exact, so the exact state
F(\alpha^{\dagger})
is real-valued:
\Im\{F(\alpha^{\dagger})\}=0
.
By continuity of the forward map with respect to the data and since
\rho_{n}\to 0
, we have

(40)

J_{\rho}^{n}(\alpha^{\dagger})=\frac{1}{2}\Big(w_{0}\|{\Im\{F^{\delta_{n}}(\alpha^{\dagger})\}}\|_{0,\varOmega}^{2}+w_{1}\|{\nabla\Im\{F^{\delta_{n}}(\alpha^{\dagger})\}}\|_{0,\varOmega}^{2}\Big)+\frac{\rho_{n}}{2}\|{\alpha^{\dagger}}\|_{0,\varOmega}^{2}\longrightarrow 0.

By weak lower semicontinuity of the
L^{2}
- and
H^{1}
-norms, together with (
39
) and (
40
), it follows that

\frac{1}{2}\Big(w_{0}\|{\Im\{F(\bar{\alpha})\}}\|_{0,\varOmega}^{2}+w_{1}\|{\nabla\Im\{F(\bar{\alpha})\}}\|_{0,\varOmega}^{2}\Big)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}J_{\rho}^{n}(\alpha^{n})\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}J_{\rho}^{n}(\alpha^{\dagger})=0.

Hence,
\Im\{F(\bar{\alpha})\}=0
in
\varOmega
, so that the pair
(\bar{\alpha},F(\bar{\alpha}))
solves the exact inverse problem.

By uniqueness (Assumption
3.1
), we conclude
\bar{\alpha}=\alpha^{\dagger}
, and therefore the full sequence converges strongly in
L^{2}(\varOmega)
:
\alpha^{n}=\alpha_{\rho_{n}}^{\delta_{n}}\to\alpha^{\dagger}\quad\text{in }L^{2}(\varOmega)
.

Finally, since the sequence
\{u^{n}\}
is bounded in
H^{1}(\varOmega)
, passing to the limit in the variational formulation of the state equation as above shows

F^{\delta_{n}}(\alpha_{\rho_{n}}^{\delta_{n}})\rightharpoonup u^{\dagger}\quad\text{in }H^{1}(\varOmega).

This completes the proof.
∎

For regularization parameters
\rho_{n}\geq\rho_{0}
, Proposition
2.13
ensures that the functional
J_{\rho_{n}}^{\delta_{n}}
is strictly convex, so the minimizer
\alpha_{\rho_{n}}^{\delta_{n}}
is unique.
When
\rho_{n}
becomes smaller than the threshold
\rho_{0}
from Proposition
2.13
, strict convexity may no longer hold, and multiple minimizers may exist for a given
n
.
Nonetheless, since
\alpha_{\rho_{n}}^{\delta_{n}}\in\mathcal{K}
, which is finite-dimensional and compact, the sequence is bounded and thus admits a convergent subsequence.
Crucially, Assumption
3.1
guarantees that any such limit of convergent subsequences is uniquely determined as the exact solution
\alpha^{\dagger}
, despite the potential non-uniqueness of finite-
n
minimizers in (
39
).

4
Numerical Approximation via Sobolev Gradient Method

We consider the minimization of a general cost functional

J:\overline{\mathcal{A}}\to\mathbb{R}_{+},

and solve Problem
1.1
via a gradient descent method in the Sobolev space
H^{1}(\varOmega)
.

To improve stability and control the smoothness of the descent direction, we employ a Sobolev-gradient formulation.
Let
\mu>0
and let
J^{\prime}(\alpha)
denote the Fréchet derivative of
J
with respect to
\alpha
.
Instead of using the standard
L^{2}
gradient (i.e., the Riesz representative of
J^{\prime}(\alpha)
in
L^{2}(\varOmega)
), we compute the Sobolev gradient as the Riesz representative in the weighted
H^{1}
inner product
[
31
]
:

(41)

(\nabla_{H^{1}}J(\alpha),\varphi)_{\mu,\varOmega}=J^{\prime}(\alpha)[\varphi],\quad\forall\varphi\in H^{1}(\varOmega),

where the inner product is defined by

(42)

(\varphi,\psi)_{\mu,\varOmega}:=\mu\,(\nabla\varphi,\nabla\psi)_{0,\varOmega}+(\varphi,\psi)_{0,\varOmega}.

In particular, for
\mu=1
,
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\mu,\varOmega}
coincides with the standard
H^{1}
inner product.
Choosing a sufficiently large
\mu
enhances the smoothing effect, which improves stability and mitigates noise amplification in the reconstruction, whereas for
\mu\ll 1
, the influence of the
H^{1}
-smoothing term is negligible (see Figure
1
for numerical illustration).

Note that the solution of (
41
) depends continuously on
J^{\prime}(\alpha)
and satisfies the stability estimate

\|{\nabla_{H^{1}}J(\alpha)}\|_{1,\varOmega}\leq\frac{1}{\min(1,\mu)}\,\|{J^{\prime}(\alpha)}\|_{(H^{1}(\varOmega))^{\ast}},

where
\|{\cdot}\|_{(H^{1}(\varOmega))^{\ast}}
denotes the dual norm on
H^{1}(\varOmega)^{\ast}
.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the Sobolev-gradient descent update reads

(43)

\alpha^{[k+1]}=\alpha^{[k]}-t^{[k]}\,\nabla_{H^{1}}J(\alpha^{[k]}),

where
t^{[k]}>0
is a step size, either fixed and sufficiently small, or determined via a line search satisfying, for example, the Armijo or Wolfe conditions
[
32
]
.
The iteration may be terminated when a prescribed maximum number of iterations is reached.

Algorithm 1

Gradient Descent in
H^{1}
(Unregularized)

1:

objective function
J
, initial guess
\alpha^{[0]}\in\overline{\mathcal{A}}
, step size rule, maximum iterations
k_{\max}

2:

approximate stationary point
\alpha^{\star}

3:

Set
k=0

4:

while

k\leq k_{\max}

do

5:

Solve the state equation for
\alpha^{[k]}
to obtain
u(\alpha^{[k]})

6:

Solve the adjoint equation (if applicable) to obtain
p(\alpha^{[k]})

7:

Compute the
H^{1}
-Sobolev gradient
\nabla_{H^{1}}J(\alpha^{[k]})
using
u(\alpha^{[k]})
and
p(\alpha^{[k]})

8:

Determine step size
t^{[k]}
(fixed or via line search
[
32
]
)

9:

Do the update (
43
)

10:

k\leftarrow k+1

11:

end

while

12:

return

\alpha^{[k]}
as
\alpha^{\star}

Before proceeding to numerical experiments, we note that the Sobolev-gradient iteration (Algorithm
1
) generates iterates
\{\alpha^{[k]}\}
such that there exists
k^{\star}\leq k_{\max}
with

\|{\nabla_{H^{1}}J(\alpha^{[k^{\star}]})}\|_{1,\varOmega}\leq\varepsilon^{\star}

for some
\varepsilon^{\star}>0
, i.e., the iteration produces an
approximate stationary point
of
J
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let Assumption
2.9
hold, and let
\alpha^{[0]}\in\mathcal{K}
.
Assume the step sizes
t^{[k]}>0
satisfy standard descent conditions (e.g., Armijo or Wolfe).
Then, the iterates
\{\alpha^{[k]}\}
generated by Algorithm
1
satisfy

J(\alpha^{[k+1]})\leq J(\alpha^{[k]}),\quad\forall k\leq k_{\max}.

Moreover, there exists an index
k^{\star}\leq k_{\max}
such that

\|{\nabla_{H^{1}}J(\alpha^{[k^{\star}]})}\|_{1,\varOmega}\leq\frac{J(\alpha^{[0]})-J^{\star}}{t_{\min}k_{\max}},

where
J^{\star}:=\min_{\alpha\in\mathcal{K}}J(\alpha)
and
t_{\min}:=\min_{0\leq k\leq k_{\max}}t^{[k]}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{K}
is finite-dimensional and convex (Assumption
2.9
), the Sobolev gradient
\nabla_{H^{1}}J(\alpha)
exists and is uniquely defined for all
\alpha\in\mathcal{K}
(definition given in equation (
41
)).

Let
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\mathcal{K}
and
v\in H^{1}(\varOmega)
. By (
41
), we have

(\nabla_{H^{1}}J(\alpha_{1})-\nabla_{H^{1}}J(\alpha_{2}),v)_{\mu,\varOmega}=J^{\prime}(\alpha_{1})[v]-J^{\prime}(\alpha_{2})[v].

Using the Lipschitz continuity of the forward map and its derivative (Proposition
2.5
and Lemma
2.4
) and uniform boundedness of
u(\alpha)
in
H^{1}(\varOmega)
, we obtain

|{J^{\prime}(\alpha_{1})[v]-J^{\prime}(\alpha_{2})[v]}|\leq C^{\prime}\,\|{\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\varOmega)}\,\|{v}\|_{1,\varOmega},

for some constant
C^{\prime}>0
. Taking the supremum over
\|{v}\|_{1,\varOmega}=1
and using norm equivalence on
\mathcal{K}
yields the Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla_{H^{1}}J
.

For the iterates
\{\alpha^{[k]}\}
of Algorithm
1
with a standard line search (e.g., Armijo), we have the descent inequality

J(\alpha^{[k+1]})\leq J(\alpha^{[k]})-t^{[k]}\,\|{\nabla_{H^{1}}J(\alpha^{[k]})}\|_{1,\varOmega}^{2}.

Summation over
k=0,\dots,k_{\max}-1
then guarantees the existence of
k^{\star}
such that

\|{\nabla_{H^{1}}J(\alpha^{[k^{\star}]})}\|_{1,\varOmega}^{2}\leq\frac{J(\alpha^{[0]})-J^{\star}}{t_{\min}k_{\max}},

i.e.,
\alpha^{[k^{\star}]}
is an approximate stationary point of
J
.
∎

Incorporating a Tikhonov regularization term (
22
) further enhances the stability of the reconstruction, particularly in the presence of noisy measurement data.

The Sobolev-gradient formulation can naturally include this term by replacing
J^{\prime}(\alpha)
with
J_{\rho}^{\prime}(\alpha)
in equation (
41
). Alternatively, the Tikhonov term can be added after computing the
H^{1}
-Riesz representative of
J^{\prime}(\alpha)
, effectively smoothing the descent direction. As a result, the iterates tend to avoid spurious oscillations and converge more robustly toward a stable approximate minimizer.

By Proposition
2.13
, for sufficiently large
\rho
,
J_{\rho}
is strictly convex, ensuring a unique minimizer and the error estimate

\|{\alpha^{[k^{\star}]}-\alpha_{\rho}}\|_{1,\varOmega}\leq\frac{1}{C}\,\|{\nabla_{H^{1}}J_{\rho}(\alpha^{[k^{\star}]})}\|_{1,\varOmega}.

In practice,
\rho
can be chosen as a fixed parameter (as we do here) or updated heuristically during the iterations to balance data fidelity and regularization. For example, using the
balancing principle

[
13
]
:

(44)

({\gamma}-1)J(\alpha)-\rho R(\alpha)=0,\quad{\gamma}>1,

which balances the data-fidelity term against the regularization term without requiring explicit knowledge of the noise level
[
12
,
14
]
.
This approach provides robust recovery of the dominant features of
\alpha
while suppressing noise amplification in the gradient iterations
[
23
]
.

Using the unique solution of (
41
), the
H^{1}
-gradient update in equation (
43
) can be modified in two ways.
The first option incorporates the Tikhonov term directly into the Sobolev gradient:

(45)

\alpha^{[k+1]}=\alpha^{[k]}-t^{[k]}\nabla_{H^{1}}J_{\rho^{[k]}}(\alpha^{[k]}),

where the gradient is computed from the fully regularized functional
J_{\rho}
, so the smoothing effect of the Sobolev inner product applies to both the data-fidelity and regularization terms.

Alternatively, the Tikhonov contribution can be added separately after computing the Sobolev gradient of the unregularized functional:

(46)

\alpha^{[k+1]}=\alpha^{[k]}-t^{[k]}\nabla_{H^{1}}J(\alpha^{[k]})+t^{[k]}\rho^{[k]}R^{\prime}(\alpha^{[k]}),

where only the data term is smoothed in
H^{1}
, and the regularization term is added in its “raw” form.

Note that when
\mu
is set to zero in (
41
), (
46
) reduces to the conventional
L^{2}
-gradient update with Tikhonov regularization:

(47)

\alpha^{[k+1]}=\alpha^{[k]}-t^{[k]}J^{\prime}(\alpha^{[k]})+t^{[k]}\rho^{[k]}R^{\prime}(\alpha^{[k]}).

In our numerical experiments, we focus on the application of (
46
) and (
47
).

Remark 4.2
.

Even if the regularization parameter
\rho
is small or vanishes, so that
J_{\rho}
may lose strict convexity (Proposition
2.13
), the Sobolev-gradient iterates
\alpha^{[k]}\in\mathcal{K}
remain bounded.
By compactness of
\mathcal{K}
(Assumption
2.9
), convergent subsequences exist, and any limit of such subsequences can be uniquely identified as the exact solution
\alpha^{\dagger}
under Assumption
3.1
, in analogy with Proposition
3.6
.

Remark 4.3
.

Assume that the Sobolev gradient
\nabla_{H^{1}}J:\mathcal{K}\to H^{1}(\varOmega)
is

strongly monotone
, i.e.,

(\nabla_{H^{1}}J(\alpha)-\nabla_{H^{1}}J(\beta),\alpha-\beta)_{1,\varOmega}\geq\kappa\,\|{\alpha-\beta}\|_{1,\varOmega}^{2},\quad\forall\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{K},

for some
\kappa>0
, and Lipschitz continuous with constant
L_{S}>0
, i.e.,

\|{\nabla_{H^{1}}J(\alpha)-\nabla_{H^{1}}J(\beta)}\|_{1,\varOmega}\leq L_{S}\,\|{\alpha-\beta}\|_{1,\varOmega}.

Under these conditions,
J
admits a unique minimizer
\alpha^{\star}\in\mathcal{K}
(cf. Proposition
3.3
; see also, e.g.,
[
18
, Ch. V, Sec. 2]
).
Moreover, Algorithm
1
with a sufficiently small constant step size
t>0
(e.g.,
0<t\leq 1/L_{S}
) generates a sequence
\{\alpha^{[k]}\}
satisfying

\|{\alpha^{[k]}-\alpha^{\star}}\|_{1,\varOmega}\leq(1-t\,\kappa)^{k}\,\|{\alpha^{[0]}-\alpha^{\star}}\|_{1,\varOmega},\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{N}.

In particular, the Sobolev-gradient iteration converges
linearly
in
H^{1}(\varOmega)
to the unique minimizer
\alpha^{\star}
.

The following result summarizes the effect of the Sobolev gradient and the weighting parameter
\mu
on the stability and smoothness of the descent direction, providing a theoretical justification for its use in the numerical reconstruction.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let Assumption
2.9
hold, so that
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathcal{A}\subset L^{\infty}(\varOmega)
is finite-dimensional, closed, and convex.
Let
J:H^{1}(\varOmega)\to\mathbb{R}
be Fréchet differentiable, and assume
J^{\prime}(\alpha)
is
strongly monotone in
L^{2}(\varOmega)
over
\mathcal{K}
, i.e., there exists
C>0
such that

(J^{\prime}(\alpha_{1})-J^{\prime}(\alpha_{2}),\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})_{0,\varOmega}\geq C\,\|{\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}}\|_{0,\varOmega}^{2},\quad\forall\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\mathcal{K}.

Let
\nabla_{H^{1}}J(\alpha)
denote the Sobolev gradient defined via the
\mu
-weighted inner product (
42
).
Then, there exists a constant
C_{\mathcal{K}}>0
(depending only on the finite-dimensional set
\mathcal{K}
) such that

(48)

(\nabla_{H^{1}}J(\alpha_{1})-\nabla_{H^{1}}J(\alpha_{2}),\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})_{\mu,\varOmega}\geq\frac{\min\{C,\mu\}}{C_{\mathcal{K}}}\,\|{\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}}\|_{1,\varOmega}^{2},\quad\forall\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\mathcal{K}.

In particular, the Sobolev gradient is strongly monotone over
\mathcal{K}
, and the monotonicity constant scales with
\mu
, providing enhanced smoothing and stability of the descent direction.

Proof.

Let
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\mathcal{K}
. By definition of the Sobolev gradient (equation (
41
)), for any
v\in H^{1}(\varOmega)
we have

(\nabla_{H^{1}}J(\alpha_{1})-\nabla_{H^{1}}J(\alpha_{2}),v)_{\mu,\varOmega}=J^{\prime}(\alpha_{1})[v]-J^{\prime}(\alpha_{2})[v].

Choosing
v=\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}\in\mathcal{K}\subset H^{1}(\varOmega)
gives

\displaystyle(\nabla_{H^{1}}J(\alpha_{1})-\nabla_{H^{1}}J(\alpha_{2}),\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})_{\mu,\varOmega}

\displaystyle=J^{\prime}(\alpha_{1})[\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}]-J^{\prime}(\alpha_{2})[\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}]

\displaystyle=(J^{\prime}(\alpha_{1})-J^{\prime}(\alpha_{2}),\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})_{0,\varOmega}.

By the assumption of
L^{2}
strong monotonicity over
\mathcal{K}
, we have

(J^{\prime}(\alpha_{1})-J^{\prime}(\alpha_{2}),\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})_{0,\varOmega}\geq C\,\|{\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}}\|_{0,\varOmega}^{2}.

Next, since
\mathcal{K}
is finite-dimensional, all norms on
\mathcal{K}
are equivalent. In particular, there exists a constant
C_{\mathcal{K}}\geq 1
such that

\|{\alpha}\|_{1,\varOmega}^{2}=\|{\alpha}\|_{0,\varOmega}^{2}+\|{\nabla\alpha}\|_{0,\varOmega}^{2}\leq C_{\mathcal{K}}\left(\|{\alpha}\|_{0,\varOmega}^{2}+\mu\|{\nabla\alpha}\|_{0,\varOmega}^{2}\right)=C_{\mathcal{K}}\,\|{\alpha}\|_{\mu,\varOmega}^{2},

where
\|{\alpha}\|_{\mu,\varOmega}^{2}:=(\alpha,\alpha)_{\mu,\varOmega}=\|{\alpha}\|_{0,\varOmega}^{2}+\mu\|{\nabla\alpha}\|_{0,\varOmega}^{2}
.
Similarly, we have

\|{\alpha}\|_{\mu,\varOmega}^{2}\geq\min\{1,\mu\}\,\|{\alpha}\|_{1,\varOmega}^{2}.

Combining these estimates leads to our desired condition (
48
).
∎

Although obvious, it must be observed from Propositions
4.4
that over the finite-dimensional admissible set
\mathcal{K}
, increasing
\mu
strengthens the
H^{1}
-smoothing term, which improves stability and suppresses high-frequency oscillations in the gradient.
Very large
\mu
enhances smoothness but may slow convergence, while very small
\mu
reduces smoothing and can amplify noise.
The strong monotonicity over
\mathcal{K}
guarantees that Sobolev-gradient updates converge stably toward a unique minimizer of the functional restricted to
\mathcal{K}
.

Remark 5.1
.

We summarize the role of the gradient-dependent term in the modified CCBM misfit (
23
) and its observed numerical effects.

•

The inclusion of the term
\|{\nabla u_{i}(\alpha)}\|_{0,\varOmega}^{2}
introduces an
H^{1}
-type contribution to the state discrepancy.
In the reported experiments, this appears to increase sensitivity to spatial variations in the diffusion coefficient, partially counteracting the smoothing effect of the forward map.

•

Although the explicit Tikhonov regularization
R_{\rho}
controls only
\|{\alpha}\|_{0,\varOmega}
, the gradient-weighted misfit may introduce an additional implicit smoothing effect through the forward and adjoint problems.
Empirically, this is associated with Sobolev gradients that better reflect
H^{1}
-type reconstruction errors.

•

Larger values of
w_{1}
are observed to improve contraction of the Sobolev-gradient iteration in several test cases.
While strong monotonicity is assumed in Remark
4.3
, increased
w_{1}
appears to enhance convergence behavior in practice within a suitable parameter range.

•

In the presented numerical tests, sufficiently large but not excessive values of
w_{1}
are often associated with lower reconstruction errors in both
L^{2}(\varOmega)
and
H^{1}(\varOmega)
.
This is consistent with improved control of certain high-frequency components, though only
L^{2}
-regularization is imposed explicitly.

•

These observations are empirical and depend on the problem setting and parameter choice.
Excessively large values of
w_{1}
may increase noise sensitivity or numerical stiffness, requiring careful tuning of step sizes and regularization parameters.

5.2
Reconstruction of piecewise-constant diffusion via projection

While the numerical experiments above demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed Sobolev gradient descent scheme for smoothly varying diffusion coefficients, many applications involve heterogeneous media with piecewise-constant diffusion and sharp interfaces. In such cases, reconstructions obtained solely from the continuous
H^{1}
-based update may exhibit residual smooth transitions across subdomain boundaries, even in the presence of total variation regularization.

To address this limitation, we augment the gradient-based iteration with a constraint-enforcement step that explicitly incorporates the known piecewise-constant structure of the diffusion coefficient. After each regularized Sobolev gradient update, the intermediate iterate is mapped onto the admissible set of piecewise-constant functions by assigning a single representative value to each prescribed subregion. In the present implementation, this value is obtained by evaluating the updated coefficient at a fixed sampling point within each subdomain, thereby enforcing constancy on that region.

This approach can be interpreted as a projection-type strategy, in which the descent step is performed in the continuous space
H^{1}(\varOmega)
and subsequently followed by a non-orthogonal projection onto a lower-dimensional subspace encoding the piecewise-constant parametrization. Although this projection is not orthogonal in the
L^{2}
or
H^{1}
sense, it effectively suppresses intra-region oscillations and stabilizes the identification of subdomain-wise constant diffusion values. As demonstrated in the subsequent numerical experiments, this combined strategy enables accurate recovery of discontinuous diffusion coefficients while retaining the stability observed in the smooth case.

Motivated by the above discussion, we slightly modify Algorithm
1
to account for piecewise-constant diffusion coefficients. The Sobolev gradient descent step is retained, followed by an additional restriction step that enforces the prescribed piecewise-constant structure. This restriction is applied
after
each regularized gradient update, ensuring admissibility while preserving the smoothing and stability properties of the
H^{1}
-based descent. The resulting algorithm is summarized below.

6
Conclusion

We studied the inverse problem of reconstructing spatially varying diffusion coefficients from boundary Cauchy data within a modified coupled complex-boundary method framework. We established theoretical properties of the associated regularized optimization problem, including continuity and differentiability of the forward map, Lipschitz continuity of the cost functional, existence of minimizers, stability with respect to noisy data, and convergence under vanishing noise.

Numerical reconstructions obtained using a Sobolev-gradient descent scheme exhibit stable behavior across the considered test cases. In the reported experiments, incorporating a gradient-weighted misfit term is associated with improved sensitivity to spatial variations and reduced high-frequency artifacts when the weighting parameter is chosen within an appropriate range. A projection-based extension further enables effective recovery of piecewise-constant diffusion coefficients in heterogeneous settings.

Overall, the theoretical analysis and numerical findings support the proposed approach as a viable method for diffusion coefficient identification in both smooth and piecewise-defined media. The numerical framework also suggests potential applicability to related inverse problems involving piecewise-defined parameters, such as absorption coefficient estimation or source identification, which may be explored in future work.
