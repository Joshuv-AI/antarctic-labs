Validation of online clearance monitoring and machine learning-based prediction of dialysis adequacy in Vietnamese hemodialysis patients: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Xuan Chanh Tran, Tuan Anh Pham, Thinh Chi Phan, Anh Thuy Tram Nguyen, Huy Quoc Tran, Thanh Kien Lam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOnline clearance monitoring (OCM) offers non-invasive real-time dialysis adequacy assessment, but validation data from Southeast Asian populations are limited. We evaluated OCM performance and developed machine learning models for adequacy prediction in Vietnamese hemodialysis patients.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 97 maintenance hemodialysis patients at a regional dialysis center in Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam. Kt/V was measured using OCM (ionic dialysance, Fresenius 4008 S) and calculated using the second-generation Daugirdas formula (reference standard). Machine learning models were developed using OCM-derived Kt/V, age, and body mass index to predict adequacy per Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative (KDOQI) guidelines (Kt/V ≥ 1.2 or urea reduction ratio ≥ 65%).ResultsThe median Kt/V was 1.29 (interquartile range [IQR]: 1.18–1.35) by the Daugirdas formula and 1.23 (IQR: 1.12–1.30) by OCM, with 67.0% of patients achieving adequacy targets. OCM underestimated Kt/V by a mean of 0.055 units but correlated excellently with the Daugirdas formula (Spearman ρ = 0.971, p < 0.001). Random Forest regression achieved R² = 0.877 for continuous Kt/V prediction. Logistic regression classification demonstrated perfect test-set discrimination (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve [AUC] = 1.000, sensitivity = 100%), with cross-validation AUC of 0.989 ± 0.010, outperforming direct OCM thresholding (sensitivity 84.6%).ConclusionsOCM demonstrates excellent correlation with the Daugirdas formula despite systematic underestimation, validating its use for real-time monitoring with appropriate calibration. Machine learning models incorporating patient-specific factors may provide calibrated adjustment to OCM-derived Kt/V. A freely accessible web-based calculator was developed for clinical application, though external validation is needed before widespread implementation.Keywords: Hemodialysis adequacy, Kt/V, Online clearance monitoring, Ionic dialysance, Machine learning, Urea reduction ratio, Chronic kidney disease, Dialysis quality

Background

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) affects approximately 697.5 million individuals worldwide, with hemodialysis representing the predominant renal replacement therapy modality for end-stage kidney disease [1]. The number of patients requiring renal replacement therapy (RRT) has more than doubled over the past three decades, reaching an estimated 2.62 million individuals in 2010, with projections suggesting this figure will increase to 5.44 million by 2030 [2]. In Vietnam, the estimated number of patients requiring renal replacement therapy (RRT) due to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) exceeds 100,000, yet fewer than 40,000 patients (~ 372 per million population) currently receive RRT, reflecting a large unmet treatment gap. Hemodialysis accounts for the majority (~ 83%) of all RRT in Vietnam, with dialysis centers expanding notably since the introduction of national insurance reimbursement in 1996; however, access remains unequally distributed between urban and rural regions, and registry data on delivered dialysis dose and treatment quality remain limited [3].

Dialysis adequacy—defined as the delivery of sufficient hemodialysis to remove uremic solutes and excess fluid—is commonly assessed using urea-based indices, particularly the urea reduction ratio (URR) and single-pool Kt/V (spKt/V). Kt/V represents the relationship between dialyzer urea clearance (K), treatment time (t), and urea distribution volume (V). The Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative (KDOQI) 2015 guidelines recommend a target spKt/V of 1.4 per session, with a minimum delivered spKt/V of 1.2 for patients treated three times weekly [4]. The HEMO Study demonstrated no significant survival advantage of higher dialysis dose targets compared with standard therapy in maintenance hemodialysis patients [5]. However, the study did not evaluate dialysis doses corresponding to spKt/V values below 1.2, and therefore should not be interpreted as directly validating this adequacy threshold.

The conventional approach to dialysis adequacy assessment relies on pre- and post-dialysis blood urea nitrogen (BUN) measurements, from which URR and Kt/V are calculated using validated formulas such as the second-generation Daugirdas equation. The second-generation Daugirdas equation is widely used in clinical practice because it provides a practical approximation of single-pool variable-volume Kt/V using routinely available data, including pre- and post-dialysis BUN, treatment time, ultrafiltration volume, and post-dialysis weight [6]. However, Daugirdas-derived Kt/V is still model-based and may be affected by post-dialysis blood sampling technique, urea rebound, ultrafiltration, access recirculation, and the assumptions of a single-pool model. In the present study, Daugirdas-derived Kt/V was used as the reference comparator because it remains the most widely applied blood-based method for routine assessment of dialysis adequacy and facilitates comparison with previous OCM validation studies. Nevertheless, it should be regarded as a pragmatic clinical reference rather than an absolute gold standard.

Online clearance monitoring (OCM) technology offers a non-invasive method for estimating delivered dialysis dose during hemodialysis sessions. The Fresenius OCM system is based on conductivity monitoring and ionic dialysance. During dialysis, controlled changes in dialysate conductivity, largely related to sodium ion transfer, are used to estimate effective ionic dialysance as a surrogate for small-solute clearance [7, 8]. This allows real-time assessment of delivered clearance without requiring post-dialysis blood sampling. However, it is important to distinguish between clearance measurement and Kt/V calculation. OCM measures conductivity clearance or ionic dialysance as a surrogate for effective urea clearance; OCM-derived Kt/V is then calculated from this clearance, treatment time, and an estimated urea distribution volume (V).

Several factors may contribute to differences between OCM-derived Kt/V and blood-sample-based Kt/V. Potential sources of error in the clearance component include the conductivity measurement protocol, cardiopulmonary recirculation, vascular access recirculation, achieved blood flow, dialysate flow, dialyzer performance, and treatment interruptions [7, 9]. However, when conductivity-derived clearance is converted into Kt/V, the estimation of urea distribution volume (V) becomes a central determinant of the final value. Because Kt/V is inversely related to V, overestimation of V can lead to underestimation of OCM-derived Kt/V even when conductivity clearance itself is accurate. Therefore, the method used to estimate V is a major potential source of systematic bias in OCM-derived Kt/V. Conversely, underestimation of V may lead to overestimation of Kt/V. Previous studies have shown that agreement between conductivity-monitoring-derived Kt/V and blood-based Kt/V depends substantially on V estimation, and that anthropometric formulas may overestimate V compared with bioimpedance spectroscopy or urea kinetic modelling-derived estimates [9, 10]. This issue may be especially relevant in populations with smaller body size or different body composition, because body weight, sex, and body composition directly influence total body water and urea distribution volume. This consideration is particularly relevant in Vietnamese hemodialysis patients, who generally have lower body weight and smaller body size than many European and North American dialysis populations. Consequently, the magnitude of disagreement between OCM-derived and blood-based Kt/V observed in this study may not be directly generalizable to populations with different anthropometric characteristics, highlighting the need for external validation in diverse patient cohorts.

In addition to conventional hemodialysis, hemodiafiltration (HDF) is an important dialysis modality that combines diffusive and convective solute removal and may influence delivered solute clearance. The present study was performed using Fresenius 4008 S machines, and all treatments were conventional hemodialysis rather than HDF. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted in the context of conventional hemodialysis [11].

Recently, machine learning approaches have shown promise for predicting dialysis adequacy from routinely available clinical and dialysis-related variables [12, 13]. In the context of OCM, machine learning may help calibrate OCM-derived Kt/V by incorporating patient-specific factors such as age, sex, body size, and body mass index, which may influence V and delivered dialysis dose. However, the application of machine learning to OCM-based real-time adequacy prediction in Southeast Asian populations has not been well studied.

This study was designed to address three objectives in a cohort of maintenance hemodialysis patients at a regional dialysis center in Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam: (1) to determine URR and Kt/V values using both the Daugirdas formula and OCM technology, and to assess the proportion of patients achieving KDOQI adequacy targets; (2) to evaluate the correlation and agreement between Daugirdas-derived Kt/V and OCM-measured Kt/V; and (3) to develop and validate machine learning models for real-time adequacy prediction using pre-dialysis parameters. Our findings may contribute to improving dialysis quality monitoring practices and inform the adoption of OCM technology in Southeast Asian healthcare settings.

Methods

Study design and patient population

This retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted at the Dialysis Department of Lanh Binh Thang General Hospital, Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam, from January to December 2025. The study was approved by the hospital’s institutional review board and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study and use of anonymized clinical data.

Adult patients (≥ 18 years) undergoing maintenance hemodialysis three times weekly for at least three months were eligible for inclusion, provided they had stable dry weight (variation < 3% over the preceding month), functioning arteriovenous fistula access, and complete pre- and post-dialysis laboratory data. Patients were excluded if they had acute illness or hospitalization within the preceding month, significant residual renal function (urine output > 200 mL/day), vascular access complications during the study session, or incomplete data records. During the study period, a total of 123 maintenance hemodialysis patients were screened for eligibility. 26 patients were excluded according to the predefined exclusion criteria. Specifically, 6 patients had residual urine output > 200 mL/day, 9 patients were dialyzed via central venous catheter, 4 patients experienced acute illness or hospitalization within the preceding month, 5 patients had incomplete laboratory data, and 2 patients had vascular access complications affecting dialysis delivery. After applying all eligibility criteria, 97 patients were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Hemodialysis protocol

All patients underwent standardized hemodialysis using Fresenius 4008 S machines (Fresenius Medical Care, Bad Homburg, Germany) equipped with OCM technology. Sessions lasted 3.5 h using high-flux polysulfone dialyzers (surface area 1.8–2.1 m²) with blood flow rates of 250–300 mL/min and dialysate flow rate of 500 mL/min. Dialysate composition was bicarbonate-based (sodium 138 mEq/L, potassium 2.0 mEq/L, calcium 1.25 mmol/L) at 36.5 °C. Ultrafiltration goals were individualized based on interdialytic weight gain. Anticoagulation was achieved with unfractionated heparin (bolus 1000–2000 IU followed by continuous infusion adjusted to patient bleeding risk).

Data collection and laboratory measurements

Patient demographics (age, sex, underlying cause of ESKD) and anthropometric measurements (height, post-dialysis weight, body mass index [BMI]) were extracted from electronic medical records. Pre-dialysis BUN samples were drawn from the arterial line before saline or heparin administration. Post-dialysis BUN samples were collected using the slow-flow method: blood pump speed was reduced to < 100 mL/min for 15 s, then a sample was withdrawn from the arterial line to minimize recirculation and equilibration errors. Blood samples were analyzed within two hours using automated enzymatic methods (Beckman Coulter AU680, Brea, California, USA) with an intra-assay coefficient of variation < 3%. Hematocrit was measured using automated cell counters (Sysmex XN-1000, Kobe, Japan).

Kt/V measurements and adequacy definition

Kt/V by the Daugirdas formula was calculated as: Kt/V = –ln(R – 0.008 × t) + (4–3.5 × R) × UF/W, where R = post-dialysis BUN / pre-dialysis BUN, t = dialysis time in hours, UF = ultrafiltration volume in liters, and W = post-dialysis weight in kilograms [6]. OCM-derived Kt/V was measured in real-time using the ionic dialysance method integrated into the Fresenius 4008 S, which monitors dialysate conductivity changes to estimate urea clearance [8]. The urea reduction ratio was calculated as URR = [(pre-BUN – post-BUN) / pre-BUN] × 100. Dialysis adequacy was defined per KDOQI 2015 guidelines as achieving spKt/V ≥ 1.2 or URR ≥ 65% [4]. The Daugirdas second-generation equation represents a practical approximation of the Gotch single-pool variable-volume model and yields Kt/V values that closely correspond to those derived from formal urea kinetic modelling under standard clinical conditions [6]. It was selected as the reference method in preference to iterative urea kinetic modelling because it is analytically equivalent for routine clinical use and has been employed as the reference standard in the majority of published OCM validation studies, facilitating direct comparison of our findings with the existing literature.

Feature selection and model development

Candidate predictors included OCM-derived Kt/V, age, sex, height, post-dialysis weight, BMI, ultrafiltration volume, dialysis time, and hematocrit, all available before or during dialysis to enable real-time prediction. Post-dialysis BUN was explicitly excluded to maintain prediction capability without blood sampling. Random Forest impurity-based feature importance analysis ranked Kt/V(OCM) as the dominant predictor (98.2% importance), followed by age (1.1%) and BMI (0.7%). Height and weight were excluded because BMI already encapsulates body size information and their inclusion created multicollinearity (weight-height r = 0.92, weight-BMI r > 0.80). The final three-feature model (Kt/V[OCM], age, BMI) was selected based on this data-driven approach, with correlation analysis confirming minimal intercorrelation among selected features (all pairwise |r| < 0.40).

The dataset (n = 97) was randomly partitioned into training (n = 77, 79.4%) and test (n = 20, 20.6%) sets using stratified sampling with a fixed random state (seed = 42) for reproducibility. Features were standardized using z-score normalization derived from training-set statistics. Five regression algorithms were developed to predict continuous Kt/V(Daugirdas) values: univariate linear regression (OCM only, baseline), multivariate linear regression, Ridge regression (L2 regularization, α = 1.0), Lasso regression (L1 regularization, α = 0.01), and Random Forest regression (200 trees, maximum depth = 8). Two classification algorithms were developed to predict binary adequacy: logistic regression (maximum iterations = 1000) and Random Forest classifier (200 trees, maximum depth = 8). All models were implemented using scikit-learn (version 1.2.2) in Python 3.10.

Model evaluation and validation

Regression models were evaluated using coefficient of determination (R²), mean absolute error (MAE), root mean squared error (RMSE), and Pearson correlation coefficient. Classification models were evaluated using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1-score, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC). Model stability was assessed using five-fold cross-validation with stratified splitting for classification models. Bland-Altman analysis assessed agreement between predicted and actual Kt/V values. Model performance was evaluated across clinically relevant subgroups (age: <50, 50–65, > 65 years; BMI per Asian-specific cutoffs: <18.5, 18.5–25, > 25 kg/m²; sex) to assess generalizability. Subgroup differences in prediction errors were tested using the Kruskal-Wallis test.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data or median (interquartile range [IQR]) for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables are presented as frequency (percentage). Correlations between OCM and Daugirdas Kt/V were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient, with paired comparisons using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. All statistical tests were two-tailed with a significance level of 0.05. Analyses were performed using Python 3.10 (packages: pandas 1.5.3, numpy 1.23.5, scipy 1.10.1, scikit-learn 1.2.2, matplotlib 3.7.1, seaborn 0.12.2). To identify predictors of OCM–Daugirdas Kt/V bias, univariate Spearman rank correlations and multivariate ordinary least squares linear regression were performed with bias (Kt/V[Daugirdas] − Kt/V[OCM]) as the dependent variable. Candidate predictors included age, sex, height, post-dialysis weight, BMI, hematocrit, pre-dialysis BUN, ultrafiltration volume, and Kt/V(OCM). In the multivariate model, all predictors were entered simultaneously and standardized using z-score normalization to yield interpretable standardized beta coefficients. Sex differences in bias were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test, and bias stability across Kt/V(OCM) tertiles was evaluated using the Kruskal-Wallis test. The statsmodels package (version 0.14.0) was used for regression analyses.

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

A total of 97 hemodialysis patients were enrolled (Table 1). The cohort comprised 46 males (47.4%) and 51 females (52.6%), with a median age of 61 years (IQR: 50–68). The median BMI was 22.81 kg/m² (IQR: 20.72–24.41), indicating predominantly normal weight status per Asian-specific criteria. Pre-dialysis BUN averaged 22.15 ± 6.09 mmol/L, decreasing to 7.72 ± 2.31 mmol/L post-dialysis. The median hematocrit was 30.90% (IQR: 28.56–33.21%), consistent with anemia commonly observed in maintenance hemodialysis patients. Sex-stratified baseline characteristics are presented in Table 3. Males had significantly greater height (165.5 vs. 155.0 cm, p < 0.001) and post-dialysis weight (64.5 vs. 54.5 kg, p < 0.001), whereas BMI did not differ significantly between sexes (23.06 vs. 22.35 kg/m², p = 0.173).

Dialysis adequacy assessment

The median URR was 65.60% (IQR: 63.15–67.40%), with 59 patients (60.8%) achieving the minimum target of ≥ 65%. The median Kt/V by the Daugirdas formula was 1.29 (IQR: 1.18–1.35), with 65 patients (67.0%) reaching the adequacy threshold of ≥ 1.2. OCM-derived Kt/V showed a median of 1.23 (IQR: 1.12–1.30), with 55 patients (56.7%) exceeding the 1.2 threshold by direct OCM classification. According to KDOQI 2015 combined criteria (Kt/V ≥ 1.2 or URR ≥ 65%), 65 patients (67.0%) were classified as receiving adequate dialysis. All 59 patients achieving URR ≥ 65% also met the Kt/V ≥ 1.2 threshold, and six additional patients (6.2%) achieved adequacy by Kt/V alone with borderline URR values (62.9–64.2%). Sex-stratified analysis demonstrated markedly higher KDOQI adequacy rates in females compared with males (80.4% vs. 52.2%, p = 0.006), with corresponding differences in both Kt/V(Daugirdas) (1.333 vs. 1.215, p < 0.001) and URR (66.96% vs. 64.43%, p < 0.001) (Table 3).

Correlation and agreement between OCM and Daugirdas Kt/V

The correlation between Kt/V(Daugirdas) and Kt/V(OCM) was excellent (Spearman ρ = 0.971, p < 0.001), although OCM values were systematically lower by a mean of 0.055 units (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p < 0.001). This bias represents approximately 4.6% of the conventional adequacy threshold, explaining the lower adequacy rate by direct OCM classification (56.7%) compared with the Daugirdas formula (67.0%). Among the three selected features, minimal intercorrelation was confirmed: Kt/V(OCM) with age (r = − 0.06), Kt/V(OCM) with BMI (r = − 0.39), and age with BMI (r = 0.14).

Regression model performance

Five machine learning models were developed to predict continuous Kt/V using three pre-dialysis parameters (Table 2). All regression and classification models were developed using three predictor variables: OCM-derived Kt/V, age, and body mass index (BMI). These variables were selected based on their clinical relevance and their potential influence on dialysis adequacy estimation. The Random Forest model demonstrated superior test-set performance (R² = 0.877, MAE = 0.0237, RMSE = 0.0426), representing a 4.7% relative improvement over the baseline OCM-only model (R² = 0.838). Among linear models, Lasso regression showed the strongest performance (R² = 0.859). Cross-validation confirmed model stability, with all models achieving mean validation R² exceeding 0.93.

Classification model performance

For binary classification of dialysis adequacy, logistic regression achieved perfect discrimination on the test set (AUC = 1.000), with 95.0% accuracy, 100% sensitivity, 85.7% specificity, and an F1-score of 0.963 (Table 2). Cross-validation yielded a mean AUC of 0.989 ± 0.010. The precision-recall curve demonstrated sustained maximum precision (1.000) across all recall thresholds. The single misclassified case was an adequate patient incorrectly predicted as inadequate, representing a conservative error favoring increased monitoring rather than missed inadequacy (Fig. 4).

The logistic regression model equation is: P(Adequate) = 1 / (1 + exp(–z)), where z = 1.776 + 2.829 × OCM_std – 0.210 × Age_std – 0.686 × BMI_std (Table 2). Kt/V(OCM) was the strongest predictor (odds ratio [OR] = 16.93, p < 0.001); age showed a weak negative association (OR = 0.81, p = 0.042) and BMI a moderate negative association (OR = 0.50, p = 0.008), reflecting the physiological principle that larger body size requires greater dialysis intensity for equivalent fractional clearance.

Compared with direct OCM thresholding (Kt/V[OCM] ≥ 1.2), which achieved 89.7% accuracy and 84.6% sensitivity, the logistic regression model improved sensitivity by 15.4% points, eliminating false negatives on the test set. This demonstrates the clinical value of incorporating patient-specific factors and calibrating for OCM’s systematic bias through machine learning.

Subgroup analyses

Prediction error varied modestly across age strata (median absolute errors: <50 years, 0.054; 50–65 years, 0.051; >65 years, 0.056; Kruskal-Wallis p = 0.68). BMI category analysis showed comparable prediction errors (underweight 0.053, normal weight 0.052, overweight 0.054). Females consistently demonstrated higher adequacy achievement rates across all age groups (75.0–85.7%) compared with males (38.9–66.7%). Detailed sex-stratified analysis (Table 3) revealed that OCM bias magnitude was comparable between sexes (0.0553 ± 0.0153 in males vs. 0.0540 ± 0.0330 in females, p = 0.477), with excellent OCM–Daugirdas correlation maintained in both groups (ρ = 0.984 in males; ρ = 0.929 in females), indicating that OCM systematic underestimation is not sex-dependent.

Predictors of OCM–Daugirdas bias

To identify patient characteristics associated with the magnitude of OCM–Daugirdas bias, univariate Spearman correlations and multivariate linear regression were performed with bias (Kt/V[Daugirdas] − Kt/V[OCM]) as the dependent variable (Table 4). In univariate analysis, no clinical or anthropometric variable reached statistical significance (all p > 0.05), with the largest correlation observed for hematocrit (Spearman ρ = −0.188, p = 0.065). In multivariate regression, Kt/V(OCM) was the only significant predictor (standardized β = −0.010, 95% CI − 0.016 to − 0.003, p = 0.004), though the overall model explained only 14.2% of bias variance (adjusted R² = 0.053, F-test p = 0.129). Bias remained stable across Kt/V(OCM) tertiles: low (0.060 ± 0.019), mid (0.053 ± 0.013), and high (0.049 ± 0.040) (Kruskal-Wallis p = 0.574). These findings indicate that OCM systematic underestimation is largely independent of patient characteristics, supporting the application of a uniform calibration correction across the studied population.

Discussion

Principal findings

This cross-sectional study of 97 maintenance hemodialysis patients at a regional center in Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam, demonstrated three principal findings. First, the overall dialysis adequacy achievement rate of 67.0% by KDOQI criteria, while exceeding the minimum target at the median level (Kt/V 1.29), falls substantially below the international quality benchmark of 90%. Second, OCM systematically underestimated Kt/V by approximately 0.06 units relative to the Daugirdas formula but demonstrated excellent correlation (ρ = 0.971), validating its utility for real-time monitoring with appropriate calibration. Third, machine learning models incorporating OCM, age, and BMI outperformed direct OCM thresholding for adequacy classification (sensitivity 100% vs. 84.6%), demonstrating the value of patient-specific calibration.

Dialysis adequacy in the regional and international context

Our adequacy achievement rate of 67.0% is consistent with reports from other middle-income settings and with the international variation documented by the Dialysis Outcomes and Practice Patterns Study (DOPPS), which has demonstrated lower adequacy rates in developing regions compared with Japan, North America, and Western Europe [14]. Sartika et al. reported mean URR of 70.74 ± 10.04% with mean OCM-derived Kt/V of 1.27 ± 0.19 in 58 Indonesian hemodialysis patients receiving twice-weekly dialysis [15], values broadly comparable to our findings. The international comparison of ESKD outcomes reveals substantial variation in five-year survival (41% in the United States vs. 60% in Japan), with differences in vascular access practices, treatment time, and dialysis prescription explaining much of this gap [14].

The discordance between our median Kt/V (above target) and the overall achievement rate (below benchmark) reflects substantial heterogeneity in delivered dialysis dose, underscoring the need for individualized prescription optimization. The uniform 3.5-hour treatment time prescribed for all patients may be insufficient for those with higher BMI or larger urea distribution volumes. International guidelines recommend individualizing treatment duration based on body size and clearance requirements [4, 16], and extending sessions to 4.0–4.5 h is among the most effective strategies for improving adequacy while maintaining safe ultrafiltration rates [14, 17]. The mean dialysis session duration in our cohort was approximately 3.5 h per treatment. Although international guidelines generally recommend a minimum total dialysis time of 12 h per week for patients receiving thrice-weekly hemodialysis, treatment duration in routine clinical practice varies considerably across regions. In Vietnam, session durations of 3.5–4.0 h are commonly used in many dialysis centers because of high patient volume, limited dialysis capacity, workforce constraints, and scheduling considerations. Consequently, multiple treatment shifts are often required to accommodate the growing number of patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis. Despite the relatively shorter session duration, the mean Daugirdas-derived spKt/V observed in our cohort exceeded the minimum adequacy target recommended by current KDOQI guidelines, suggesting that acceptable small-solute clearance was achieved in most patients. Nevertheless, dialysis adequacy should be interpreted beyond Kt/V alone, and future efforts to optimize treatment duration and overall dialysis delivery remain important in resource-constrained settings.

Similarly, the prescribed blood flow rates of 250–300 mL/min may be suboptimal for patients with well-functioning fistulae capable of supporting higher flows. The sex-based differences in adequacy achievement (80.4% in females vs. 52.2% in males, p = 0.006; Table 3) reflect body composition effects on urea distribution volume: females had significantly lower body weight (54.5 vs. 64.5 kg, p < 0.001) and height (p < 0.001) but comparable BMI (p = 0.173), resulting in smaller absolute V and thus higher fractional Kt/V for equivalent clearance. Importantly, OCM bias was similar between sexes (p = 0.477), indicating that a uniform calibration correction is applicable across both male and female patients.

OCM validation in a Vietnamese population

The systematic underestimation of Kt/V by OCM relative to the Daugirdas formula (0.055 units) aligns with findings from prior validation studies. Mercadal et al. documented OCM underestimation and attributed it to the influence of cardiopulmonary recirculation on ionic dialysance measurements [7]. Goldau et al. demonstrated that step-profile conductivity protocols introduce volume-dependent errors, and that dynamic bolus methods are more accurate because they are less affected by sodium redistribution across body compartments [10]. Di Filippo et al. established that only symmetric step changes (actual dialysance, step 0–2) accurately estimate effective urea clearance, with conventional asymmetric protocols underestimating clearance by approximately 22 mL/min [9].

The exceptionally high correlation observed in our study (ρ = 0.971) exceeds coefficients reported in several prior validation studies, likely reflecting our standardized protocols: uniform session duration, consistent flow rates, exclusive use of AVF access, first-use high-flux dialyzers, and rigorous adherence to the slow-flow post-dialysis sampling method [7, 9, 18]. These findings support OCM’s reliability for tracking adequacy trends within individual patients, even if absolute values require calibration. Based on our data, an OCM Kt/V threshold of approximately 1.14–1.15 would correspond to Daugirdas Kt/V ≥ 1.2, and centers adopting OCM as a primary monitoring tool should establish institution-specific calibration through periodic validation against blood-based methods.

The estimation of urea distribution volume represents a critical source of variability in OCM-based Kt/V calculations. Wuepper et al. demonstrated that anthropometric formulas (Watson, Hume, Chertow) consistently overestimate V by 10–21% compared with bioimpedance spectroscopy, and that V derived from urea kinetic modeling using ionic dialysance (V_Kecn) provides the most accurate agreement with blood-based single-pool Kt/V [18]. These findings suggest that incorporating patient-specific volume estimation, whether through periodic bioimpedance measurements or machine learning–derived corrections, could further improve OCM accuracy. Regression analysis of predictors of OCM–Daugirdas bias in the present cohort confirmed that no individual patient characteristic — including age, sex, BMI, body weight, hematocrit, or pre-dialysis BUN — significantly predicted bias magnitude (Table 4), consistent with the hypothesis that systematic underestimation in this study is primarily attributable to V estimation methodology rather than patient-level factors [18]. The uniformity of bias across patient subgroups supports the application of a single calibration offset (approximately 0.055 units, corresponding to an adjusted OCM threshold of 1.14–1.15 for Daugirdas Kt/V ≥ 1.2) without requiring patient-specific adjustment.

The generalizability of these findings to other dialysis populations warrants careful consideration. The present cohort had a median post-dialysis weight of 57.5 kg and median BMI of 22.81 kg/m², values substantially lower than those typically reported in European and North American dialysis populations, where mean body weights of 75–85 kg are common. Because OCM-derived Kt/V depends critically on estimated V — which is directly determined by body weight, sex, and body composition — the magnitude of OCM–Daugirdas bias observed here (0.055 units) may differ systematically in populations with higher body weight or different body composition. Anthropometric formulas used by the OCM system to estimate V may perform differently across populations with distinct anthropometric characteristics, potentially producing larger or smaller systematic biases. Therefore, the calibrated OCM threshold of 1.14–1.15 derived from this cohort should not be directly transposed to other populations without local institution-specific validation.

Machine learning for dialysis adequacy prediction

A novel contribution of this study is the development and validation of machine learning models that predict adequacy using only three pre-dialysis parameters without requiring post-dialysis blood sampling. The logistic regression classifier achieved cross-validation AUC of 0.989 ± 0.010, substantially outperforming direct OCM thresholding. This improvement is clinically meaningful because the direct threshold approach misclassified 10 of 65 adequate cases as inadequate, primarily due to OCM’s systematic negative bias. By incorporating age and BMI as calibrating factors, the machine learning model compensated for this bias.

Our findings align with and extend the emerging literature on machine learning applications in dialysis. Kim et al. demonstrated that XGBoost could predict URR from routine dialysis machine parameters (correlation 0.873), though their model used a larger feature set including post-dialysis parameters [12]. Liu et al. subsequently reported that Random Forest classification achieved AUC of 0.873 for predicting spKt/V ≥ 1.4 using readily available clinical variables [13]. The parsimony of our three-feature model enhances its potential for real-time clinical implementation. Machine learning models have also demonstrated promise for predicting mortality in hemodialysis patients; Huang et al. reported that gradient boosting models achieved AUC of 0.903 for three-year mortality prediction [19], while Dong et al. showed that incorporating time-weighted quality indicator achievement rates into ensemble models yielded strong discriminative performance [20]. These parallel developments in outcome prediction and dose monitoring suggest that integrated machine learning platforms could simultaneously optimize both adequacy assessment and risk stratification in dialysis care.

The logistic regression equation is readily implementable in clinical decision support systems integrated with hemodialysis machines, enabling automated real-time adequacy prediction. Notably, enhanced electronic prompting of estimated glomerular filtration rate has shown limited effectiveness in changing physician prescribing behavior [21], suggesting that machine learning–driven decision support may need to incorporate more active feedback mechanisms to achieve meaningful changes in clinical practice.

Clinical implications

Several clinically relevant implications emerge from our findings. First, the strong OCM-Daugirdas correlation supports integrating OCM into routine workflows for continuous adequacy monitoring, enabling immediate recognition of inadequate clearance and facilitating intradialytic adjustments to blood flow rate, dialysate flow rate, or treatment duration [8]. This is particularly valuable for detecting technical problems such as access recirculation, dialyzer clotting, or dialysate delivery issues. Second, KDOQI adequacy thresholds developed for blood-based Kt/V should not be directly applied to OCM values; institution-specific calibrated thresholds are necessary. Third, the adequacy achievement rate of 67.0% identifies substantial opportunities for quality improvement through individualized prescription optimization, including extended treatment times and higher blood flow rates where feasible. Fourth, machine learning models offer a practical, low-cost strategy for calibrating OCM values to improve classification accuracy, which is particularly relevant for resource-limited settings where reducing reliance on laboratory testing would yield significant cost savings. To facilitate clinical application, we developed a freely accessible web-based calculator implementing the logistic regression model (available at https://44f9ym.csb.app/). The tool requires input of three parameters—Kt/V(OCM), age, and BMI—and outputs the predicted probability of adequate dialysis with classification at both standard (≥ 0.50) and optimal (≥ 0.66) thresholds. This calculator is intended as a clinical decision support tool and does not replace clinical judgment or periodic validation against blood-based measurements.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. The standardized dialysis protocol, exclusive use of AVF access, first-use high-flux dialyzers, and rigorous BUN sampling methods minimize measurement variability and confounding. The validated second-generation Daugirdas formula as the reference standard enables direct comparison with international literature. The machine learning approach, with data-driven feature selection and validation through both held-out testing and cross-validation, provides a robust analytical framework.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional, single-center design limits assessment of longitudinal trends and generalizability. The sample size of 97 patients, while adequate for correlation analysis, may be insufficient for robust machine learning model development, and the perfect test-set AUC should be interpreted with caution given the small test set (n = 20). External validation in independent cohorts is essential before clinical implementation. We did not quantify residual renal function beyond excluding patients with urine output exceeding 200 mL/day, representing a potential unmeasured confounder. The exclusion of patients with catheter access, acute illness, and reused dialyzers may bias results toward more stable patients. Data on actual achieved blood flow rates, effective treatment time, and specific dialyzer models were not collected, variables that could influence the OCM-Daugirdas relationship. Another limitation is that Kt was not independently estimated using the Michaels–Renkin method. Such analysis could have provided an additional mechanistic comparison between conductivity-derived clearance and theoretical dialyzer clearance based on dialyzer characteristics and operating conditions. However, the retrospective nature of the study and the absence of complete dialyzer KoA values and treatment-specific achieved flow data precluded reliable application of the Michaels–Renkin model. Future prospective studies should collect these parameters to further elucidate the sources of discrepancy between OCM-derived and blood-based measures of dialysis adequacy.

A further limitation of this study is that direct comparison between the urea distribution volume (V) used by the OCM system and V estimated using formal urea kinetic modelling could not be performed. The retrospective dataset did not include the machine-estimated V used for OCM-derived Kt/V, nor did it include conductivity clearance, ionic dialysance measurements, or other OCM-specific parameters required to reconstruct this internally derived V value. As a result, we were unable to directly quantify the contribution of V estimation to the observed disagreement between OCM-derived and Daugirdas-derived Kt/V. Nevertheless, previous studies have identified anthropometric estimation of total body water as a major potential source of bias in OCM-derived Kt/V calculations, supporting the plausibility of this mechanism in our cohort.

Finally, HDF was not available on the Fresenius 4008 S platform used in this study; therefore, all patients received conventional hemodialysis. The findings may not be generalizable to HDF settings, where convective solute removal and middle-molecule clearance differ from conventional hemodialysis.

Conclusions

OCM systematically underestimates Kt/V relative to the Daugirdas formula by approximately 0.06 units in a Vietnamese hemodialysis population, but the two methods exhibit excellent correlation (ρ = 0.971), validating OCM as a reliable tool for real-time adequacy monitoring with appropriate calibration. Machine learning models using three pre-dialysis parameters achieved strong predictive performance for both continuous Kt/V estimation and binary adequacy classification, outperforming direct OCM thresholding. A freely accessible web-based calculator was developed to facilitate point-of-care application. The adequacy achievement rate of 67.0% highlights opportunities for quality improvement through individualized prescription optimization. Integration of OCM with calibrated prediction models into routine clinical practice offers a practical, non-invasive approach to real-time adequacy assessment that may improve dialysis quality in resource-limited settings, though institution-specific calibration and external validation remain essential prerequisites.
