Final Technical Report
ComPort: Rigorous Testing Methods to Safeguard Software Porting
 Cindy Rubio-González (PI)
 University of California, Davis

                                          April 28, 2026

1 Executive Summary

This is the final technical report for the project “ComPort: Rigorous Testing Methods to Safe-
guard Software Porting,” funded by the Department of Energy (DOE), Office of Science, Office
of Advanced Scientific Computing Research (ASCR) under award number SC0022182. The report
summarizes the work done over the funding period (9/2021-8/2025), focusing on the development
of testing techniques to safeguard software porting. The research resulted in six publications in-
cluding top-tier venues (ISC, ICSE, ICS, HPDC), the Hans Meuer Award at ISC High Performance
2023 for Best Paper, and the release of five open-source software tools.

2 Major Goals

The primary objective of this project is to address the productivity and reliability crises in modern
High Performance Computing (HPC) triggered by extreme hardware and software heterogeneity.
While the integration of diverse accelerators and non-standard arithmetic enables unprecedented
computational power, these advancements simultaneously compromise the numerical integrity of
critical scientific applications. To solve this, the project aims to develop robust testing and de-
bugging frameworks capable of identifying the subtle errors introduced by diverse CPUs, GPUs,
and domain-specific accelerators. By establishing these methods, the project ensures that the shift
toward complex, heterogeneous architectures does not undermine the accuracy or reliability of
fundamental scientific results.

3 Technical Accomplishments

This section details the project’s technical accomplishments, which includes a suite of methodologies
and tools for triggering and isolating compiler-induced numerical inconsistencies, predicting the
performance of mixed-precision floating-point programs, and applying performance optimizations
via mutation testing.

3.1 Input Range Generation for Compiler-Induced Numerical Inconsistencies
We developed CIGEN, an automated technique designed to identify input ranges that trigger
compiler-induced numerical inconsistencies. CIGEN utilizes a multi-phase workflow that inte-
grates input-partitioned and coverage-based sampling, input clustering, and optimization algo-
rithms to uncover previously unknown problematic inputs. In an evaluation across 175 GNU

Scientific Library (GSL) functions, CIGEN demonstrated a 53.4% improvement over the state
of the art in detecting inconsistencies. Furthermore, by integrating CIGEN with Ciel [1], we
successfully identified 21 GSL functions where multiple, distinct sources of compiler-induced in-
consistency were captured across different input ranges. This work was published at the In-
ternational Conference on Supercomputing (ICS) 2024 [4] and the tool is publicly available at
https://github.com/LLNL/CIGEN.

3.2 Expression Isolation in Heterogeneous Programs
We developed Ciel, an automated approach for localizing numerical inconsistencies induced by
compiler optimizations at the expression level in heterogeneous applications. Ciel utilizes a re-
cursive bisection search algorithm with adaptive granularity to guide a floating-point precision
enhancement strategy. This methodology allows for the efficient identification of specific pro-
gram expressions responsible for numerical deviations. In experimental evaluations across diverse
heterogeneous applications, Ciel achieved 99.4% precision in isolating inconsistencies and demon-
strated performance comparable to or exceeding state-of-the-art methods for CPU-only programs.
Ciel [1] was published at the International Supercomputing Conference (ISC) 2023, where it
received the Hans Meuer Award (Best Research Paper). Ciel is open source and available at
https://github.com/LLNL/Ciel.

3.3 Detection of Floating-Point Exceptions in AMD GPUs
We developed FloatGuard, the first framework designed for the detection of floating-point ex-
ceptions (such as NaNs and infinities) in HIP programs running on AMD GPUs. While similar
tools existed for NVIDIA’s CUDA platform, AMD GPUs—now central to world-class exascale
supercomputers like Frontier and El Capitan—have lacked a comparable tool. FloatGuard ad-
dresses this gap by leveraging low-level AMD hardware registers and a novel iterative algorithm
that combines assembly- and source-level code instrumentation with debugger-guided execution
via ROCgdb. In an evaluation of 565 programs, the tool successfully detected exceptions in nearly
90% of cases with a performance slowdown that typically increases only linearly with the number
of exceptions found. Furthermore, we demonstrated that aggressive compiler optimizations (e.g.,
-ffast-math) significantly altered the behavior and frequency of these exceptions, often reducing the
total number reported by approximately 37%. FloatGuard was published at the International Sym-
posium on High-Performance Parallel and Distributed Computing (HPDC’25) [5] and is available
at https://github.com/LLNL/FloatGuard.

3.4 Predicting Performance and Accuracy for Precision Tuning
We developed FPLearner, a deep learning-based framework that accelerates mixed-precision
tuning by predicting the performance and accuracy of numerical programs. While traditional
dynamic tuners suffer from scalability bottlenecks due to the requirement for exhaustive program
executions, FPLearner replaces costly runs with rapid model predictions. By representing mixed-
precision programs as graph structures, our approach utilizes Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) to
model complex program dependencies. Experimental results show that FPLearner improves the
efficiency of state-of-the-art dynamic tuners by an average of 25.5% (up to 61%) while maintaining
or improving tuning quality. This work was published at the International Conference on Software
Engineering (ICSE) 2024 [6]. The tool is open source and accessible at https://github.com/
ucd-plse/FPLearner.

3.5 Performance Optimization via Mutation Testing
We developed MUPPET, an automated framework designed to optimize the performance of High
Performance Computing (HPC) software by applying mutation testing at the source-code level.
MUPPET dentifies potential performance improvements in OpenMP programs by injecting muta-
tions—syntactic changes such as altering thread affinity or loop scheduling—and using exploratory
algorithms like Bayesian Optimization to find configurations that increase speed without breaking
correctness, successfully discovering speedup opportunities in over 75% of evaluated program, with
the best speedup of 3.57x. MUPPET was first published at the International Workshop on Pro-
gramming Models and Applications for Multicores and Manycores (PMAM’24) [2]. An extension
of the work was published in the Journal of Parallel Computing (PARCO) [3]. MUPPET can be
found at https://github.com/LLNL/MUPPET.

4 Professional Leadership and Community Engagement

Beyond research dissemination and tool development, the PI demonstrated sustained leadership in
the high-performance computing (HPC) community through the organization of major technical
events. In collaboration with Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL), during the review
period the PI co-organized five editions (2021–2025) of the International Workshop on Software
Correctness for HPC Applications (CORRECTNESS), held annually at the Supercomputing (SC)
conference. The PI’s leadership extended to inter-agency coordination as a co-organizer of the
DOE/NSF Workshop on Correctness in Scientific Computing at PLDI 2023. Furthermore, the
PI prioritized educational outreach by co-developing and presenting tutorials at SC 2024 and SC
2025, as well as co-organizing the 2022 SIAM Mini-Symposium on Testing Numerical Code for
Heterogeneity. These events were conducted in partnership with our collaborators at the University
of Utah, the University of Washington, and Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory.

5 Concluding Remarks

Over the duration of this award, the project successfully transformed the testing of numerical soft-
ware for heterogeneous architectures. By developing a comprehensive ecosystem of tools, we have
provided researchers with the means to trigger and isolate compiler-induced numerical inconsisten-
cies often observed when porting code, as well as the means to optimize numerical applications.
Furthermore, through our leadership of the CORRECTNESS workshop series, we have continued
to foster a strong community. The open-source software and methodologies resulting from this work
provide a lasting foundation for ensuring the reliability and efficiency of scientific applications.
