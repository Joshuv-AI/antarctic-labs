A ring-jig additive friction stir deposition method for efficient additive manufacturing of metal laminates
Authors: Fuyuki Ishida, Takayuki Yamashita, Masayoshi Kamai, Yoshiaki Morisada, Hidetoshi Fujii
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Metal additive manufacturing (AM) has attracted attention for producing high-mix, low-volume, meter-scale components, especially for industrial applications. However, conventional AM requires high-energy input, expensive powders, and long processing times, limiting scalability and cost-effectiveness. To overcome these challenges, solid-state AM methods are being explored for lower energy use and higher build rates. This study presents a solid-state AM technique derived from friction surfacing, in which uniform layers are formed by direct friction between a rotating metal bar and a substrate. The proposed method achieves a maximum build rate of 8,000 cm³ h−1, while fusion-based AM method achieves 800 cm3 h−1 and conventional solid-state AM method achieves 2400 cm3 h−1 in maximum. Compared with other processes, the proposed method offers significant gains in deposition speed. Combining high build rate with sound interfacial bonding, the proposed method provides a promising approach for scalable, cost-effective production of large structural metal components.

Introduction

Additive manufacturing (AM) refers to a group of processes that build objects layer by layer, enabling the fabrication of components with complex geometries1–4. AM technologies are gaining attention across a wide range of industries, particularly in the aerospace sector5,6. Notably, NASA successfully fabricated a large-scale integral channel wall nozzle using metal AM7, demonstrating the feasibility of producing complex, mission-critical components. However, the production time could take up to 90 days, highlighting the need for faster build rates to achieve more time-efficient spacecraft production. The low build rate of metal AM is due to its fusion-based mechanism. Among the major types of metal AM, such as powder bed fusion (PBF)8,9 and directed energy deposition (DED)10,11, high energy input is required to melt material, leading to slow deposition due to repeated melting and solidification12. To achieve high-speed production, efforts have been made to improve build rates for PBF and DED; however, excessive heat input has led to microcrack formation or laminate distortion13,14. These issues are associated with sharp temperature gradients during the deposition15–17.

To meet the demands of high-speed and large-scale metal fabrication with high reliability, researchers have increasingly turned to solid-state AM approaches12,18,19. These solid-state processes operate at temperatures lower than the melting point and demonstrate higher build rates compared with fusion-based AM, caused by the absence of melting and solidification12,20. The lower operating temperature also produces more moderate temperature gradients, which eliminates cracks and distortion of the layers20,21. In addition, solid-state AM techniques utilize friction stirring, which induces dynamic recrystallization in the material structure. Structures composed of recrystallized fine grains tend to exhibit higher strength than the base material22. As a result of these benefits, solid-state AM has gained attention, and various methods have been devised. The most typical method is the additive friction stir deposition (AFSD) method12,18–24. AFSD uses a cylindrical rotating tool to soften and stir the feedstock, depositing it onto a substrate while maintaining solid-state conditions. AFSD has attracted much attention, and extensive research has focused on layer microstructure22,25–28, deposition quality29–31, and material feeding system32–34. While AFSD has demonstrated potential for producing high-quality metallic layers with improved mechanical properties, certain limitations remain. Since AFSD requires tools large enough to cover the feedstock, the large amount of heat transferred to the tool reduces deposition efficiency. The feed rate of consumable material must be carefully balanced with the heat input against the material, which inherently limits the achievable build rate. Additionally, the tools used in AFSD are often expensive, making the overall process cost-sensitive, especially for large-scale or continuous production19. Tool abrasion caused by friction with the feedstock would also affect production costs. Overall, AFSD has gathered attention as a process for high-speed deposition of metal layers with high reliability12,18–21,23; however, it lacks simplicity and compactness.

To overcome the limitations of AFSD due to its complex machine setup, returning to the basics of friction stir processes is preferable. Friction surfacing35,36—a related technique involving direct rubbing between a rotating bar and a substrate—is one of the simplest solid-state layer deposition methods. This method allows simultaneous control of heat input and material feed rate by using the rotation and pressure of the consumable rod itself, without requiring an additional stirring tool. Because of the lack of a large tool, friction surfacing enables deposition with high thermal efficiency and a high build rate. However, conventional friction surfacing struggles to produce uniform layers and achieve strong interfacial bonding37, which limits its applicability to high-quality AM.

In this work, a solid-state AM method based on the concept of friction surfacing is proposed. The proposed method retains the advantages of direct frictional contact while overcoming the limitations of layer uniformity and bonding strength. By enabling precise control over both thermal input and material deposition through direct contact between the consumable rod and the substrate, the proposed method achieves highly efficient heat transfer and improved build quality. To validate the effectiveness of the proposed method, comparative experiments were conducted between specimens fabricated via conventional friction surfacing and those produced using the proposed technique. The evaluation focused on surface appearance, hardness distribution, and interfacial bonding strength. The results demonstrate that the proposed method can achieve deposition with superior thermal efficiency and mechanical integrity. Furthermore, strategies for enhancing the build rate through process parameter optimization—particularly by increasing the applied load—are examined and discussed in detail.

Results

Since the conventional friction surfacing method cannot achieve efficient deposition due to burr formation, we developed a solid-state AM technique introducing a ring jig as an alternative to the burr. Figure 1c shows the experimental setup of the ring-jig AFSD test. In this process, the same aluminum alloy (AA6063-T6 for the round bar and AA6063-T5 for the plate) used in the conventional friction surfacing method was employed. A stainless steel (JIS SUS304) ring jig coated with boron nitride lubricant was attached to the tip of the round bar. The ring jig was not fixed in place and could move freely along the round bar before the operation. As shown in Fig. 1d, a key feature of the proposed method is the absence of burr formation on the round bar during the process. Once friction was initiated between the rotating bar and the plate, the ring jig lifted off the plate surface, and the material began depositing without interruption.

The layer on the retreating side (RS), where the direction of the bar’s rotation opposed its traverse, was narrower and thinner than the advancing side (AS), where the two directions coincided. From the surface profiling, the layer thickness was approximately 4.2 mm, however, the surface was rough with a variation of about ±±1.1 mm. Figure 2b shows the cross-section of the specimen with three layers deposited by conventional friction surfacing. These defects are presumed to be unbonded regions, indicating that the interfaces were not appropriately formed. In addition, the defects were observed more frequently between layers than between layers and the substrate. It is presumed that the layers' rough surface made them prone to forming defects. Figure 2c presents the appearance and shape profile of the first layer produced by the ring-jig AFSD process. The deposited layer had approximately the same width between the AS and RS, and the width-direction uniformity of the layer was improved. In addition, the layer thickness was approximately 2.9 mm with a variation of about ±±0.12 mm. Figure 2d shows the cross-sectional view of the specimen with three layers fabricated by the ring-jig AFSD method. Defects observed in conventional friction surfacing were absent at interfaces between layers or between layers and the substrate in the ring-jig AFSD. It is presumed that defect formation was prevented because the layers were deposited with smooth surfaces.

Comparing the shape profiles of the layers produced by conventional friction surfacing and the ring-jig AFSD, shown in Fig. 2a and Fig. 2c, the surface roughness decreased from 2.2 mm for the former method to 0.24 mm for the latter method. Additionally, defects on the interfaces were eliminated in the latter method, as evidenced by comparing Fig. 2b and Fig. 2d. Thus, the proposed method, facilitated by the introduction of a ring jig, enables the fabrication of uniform, smooth multilayered specimens and enhances yield while eliminating the common defects observed in conventional friction surfacing. Although forming sound interfaces by friction surfacing is difficult, several studies indicate that AFSD can yield sound interfaces. Kallien et al. 38 discussed the formation of macroscopic defects in laminates fabricated by conventional friction surfacing and AFSD for AA2024 and AA7075 aluminum alloys. Kallien et al. reported that, although macroscopic defects can be eliminated by applying hybrid friction diffusion bonding (HFDB) to the friction surfacing deposits, defects remain in the deposition method alone, whereas AFSD enabled control of layer thickness and the restoration of defects. The ring-jig AFSD method proposed in this study successfully formed sound interfaces, similar to other AFSD approaches.

Microstructure

To examine the precipitation behavior within the fabricated laminate, microstructure observation and elemental mapping were performed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) on specimens produced by the ring-jig AFSD method. As shown in Fig. 3a, TEM specimens were extracted from the heat-affected zone (HAZ) of the substrate, the first layer, and the second layer. Figure 3b presents a bright-field image and elemental maps of the HAZ in the substrate. Because the base metal consisted of coarse grains, the observed region was within a single grain. No precipitates were detected in this zone. The microstructure and elemental distribution in the first layer are shown in Fig. 3c. Fine precipitates smaller than 0.1 μm were observed, primarily composed of Fe and Si, which are major alloying elements in AA6063. Figure 3d shows the microstructure and elemental distribution of the second layer. This region contained both larger precipitates (~1 μm) and finer ones (<0.1 μm). The larger precipitates mainly consisted of Fe and Si, while the finer ones were rich in Si only. The variation in precipitate size between the first and second layers is likely attributed to differences in cumulative heat input, although further investigation is required to clarify the underlying mechanism. Focusing on the precipitate distribution within the deposited layers, the average interval between small precipitates in the first layer was approximately 3.7 μm. In the second layer, the average interval between small precipitates was approximately 2.6 μm, slightly smaller than in the first layer. The interval between large precipitates was approximately 6.0 μm. In precipitation-strengthened alloys, such as AA6063, which is investigated in this study, the degree of precipitation strengthening is influenced by the number density of precipitates based on the Orowan model39. Since numerous small precipitates are considered to have a greater effect than a smaller number of large precipitates, the influence of the larger precipitates observed only in the second layer is considered to be limited. Therefore, the mechanical properties among the layers fabricated by the ring-jig AFSD are expected to be roughly uniform.

Mechanical properties

For the mechanical properties of the laminates, the Vickers hardness distribution, tensile properties, and fracture surfaces were evaluated. Figure 4a shows a schematic of the position used for measuring Vickers hardness. The hardness distribution measurements were performed on the AS side, the center, and the RS side of the cross section of the three-layer-deposited specimens, as indicated by the dotted lines in the figure. Figure 4b shows the hardness distributions of the three-layered object fabricated by conventional friction surfacing. The hardness of the base metal was 83 HV. The layer hardness was approximately 65 HV regardless of location. The hardness of the area below the first layer was 52 HV, softer than the base metal and the layers. This area is presumed to correspond to the HAZ, softened by heat input during deposition. The depth of this softened region was approximately 5 mm from the substrate surface in the center. In contrast, the range was 1 and 2 mm in the AS and RS. Since the AS and RS are located at the edge of the round bar, the round bar remained there for a relatively short time compared with the center, which may have led to the small heat input in the AS and RS. The softening of the HAZ is presumed to result from the dissolution or coarsening of the precipitates, as shown in Fig. 3d. Figure 4c presents the hardness distributions of the object fabricated via the ring-jig AFSD method. The hardness of the layers was approximately 65 HV, similar to that of the layers produced by the conventional friction surfacing method. The hardness of the HAZ was approximately 53 HV, and the range was approximately 6 mm in the center and 4 mm in the AS and RS. The HAZ range was therefore wider than that observed in the substrate for conventional friction surfacing. Since the processes were performed in the same parameters, this result indicates that the heat input into the substrate increased when the ring-jig was introduced. In both methods, the three fabricated layers exhibited nearly equal hardness values, approximately 70% of those of the round bar. However, slight softening was observed in the interfaces between the layers, especially in the conventional friction surfacing. Such softening suggests possible grain coarsening or changes in precipitation behavior near the surface, driven by heat input during layer deposition. Build rate of the ring-jig AFSD

The fabrication of laminates with no defects in the interfaces and fracture in the substrate was accomplished by using the ring-jig AFSD method. In AM, not only the quality of fabrication but also the build rate is important. The layers deposited by the ring-jig AFSD seem to become thicker and wider by increasing the applied load. Therefore, since the build rate can be calculated from the volume deposited per unit time, the variations in thickness and width under load were evaluated. The changes in layer width and thickness plotted against load are shown in Fig. 7a. When a larger load was applied, the width and thickness of the layers increased linearly. Figure 7b shows the build rate of the ring-jig AFSD method estimated from the width and thickness of the layers. For comparison, the build rates and typical layer sizes of major AM processes (PBF and DED)14,40–45 and solid-state AM processes28,46–48 are also shown. Among fusion-based AM methods, wire arc additive manufacturing (WAAM)—a type of DED that uses metal wire as feedstock and an electric arc as the heat source—enables the highest-speed fabrication. In solid-state AM processes, the conventional friction surfacing method and a previously developed method of AFSD variant known as MELD28,47,48 are included for comparison. With respect to each AM process, the build rate of WAAM is approximately 800 cm3 h-1 at maximum. The possibility of increasing the build rate theoretically up to 2000 cm3 h-1 by applying several lasers and a rotational stage has been reported49. With regard to solid-state processes, the build rate of conventional friction surfacing reaches approximately 2500 cm3 h-146. However, since defects exist in the interfaces, depositing sound layers is difficult. In the MELD method, continuous fabrication of smooth layers with no defects is possible; however, the build rate is limited to approximately 1000 cm3 h-1. Since the diameter of the fed round bars is restricted and applying a higher load against the bar, similarly to friction surfacing, is difficult, further increase in the build rate of the MELD method is presumed to be difficult. On the other hand, compared with other AM methods, the build rate of the ring-jig AFSD method was greater than 8000 cm3 h-1 when a load of 1500 kgf was applied. In addition, the ring-jig AFSD demonstrated a wider range of build size compared with MELD, due to the ability to feed round bars with larger diameter and apply higher load. Additionally, in the ring-jig AFSD, not only bars with a diameter of 20 mm as utilized in this research, but also larger bars can be applied, similar to conventional friction surfacing. Therefore, further improvement of the build rate is expected. In aiming for practical application, developing a system of continuous consumption feeding is necessary and is now being developed. Once it is realized, the ring-jig AFSD would become a process to fabricate metal laminates with high yield and speed.

Discussion

Based on the above results, it is evident that the proposed method enables the high-speed fabrication of uniform and smooth layers with sound interfacial bonding. The improvement in surface quality is primarily attributed to the introduction of the ring-jig. In the conventional friction surfacing method, since the burr is nearly as soft as the deposited material and has an uneven underside, it tends to rub the top of the layer irregularly, resulting in a rough surface. In contrast, the ring-jig used in the proposed method is harder than the consumable material and features a flat bottom surface. As it rubs against the deposited layer, it effectively levels the surface, contributing to the formation of smooth and uniform layers. In addition, the ring jig also influences the heat input during deposition. As shown in the results, the HAZ in the novel method was larger than that observed in the conventional friction surfacing process. This difference can be attributed to the thermal properties of the ring-jig material. The ring-jig is made of stainless steel, which has lower thermal conductivity than aluminum. As a result, it suppresses heat dissipation into the surrounding environment and retains more thermal energy near the consumable and the base material. The increase in localized heat input promotes more effective softening of the consumable material. Compared with conventional friction surfacing, this enhanced softening allows for more complete plastic deformation and filling during deposition, thereby eliminating internal defects as shown in Fig. 2b and enabling the formation of sound interfacial bonding. The ring-jig affects not only the thermal distribution but also the material flow during the process. According to Gandra et al. 50, in conventional friction surfacing, some of the deposited material becomes a burr since the material is pressed without restriction of the outer periphery and flow outside. On the other hand, in the ring-jig AFSD, all the softened material will be deposited as layers because the ring-jig constrains the outer periphery and prevents burr formation, leading to high-yield deposition. The ring-jig plays roles in retaining heat and promoting deposition; however, it is not the primary heat source. During deposition, the ring-jig rotates passively as a result of its close fit with the consumable bar, which expands upon heating. Because the jig and the bar rotate together in the same direction, little to no friction occurs between them. While conventional AFSD utilizes a cylindrical tool as a heat source, the proposed method minimizes the tool’s heat-assisting role. By rotating the consumable bar instead of a complex rotating tool, stable material deposition can be achieved using a simpler and more compact setup. A key feature of the proposed method is that both heat input and material feed rate are simultaneously controlled by the normal force applied to the consumable bar, while conventional AFSD requires controlling them individually30. In the proposed method, increasing this force enhances both heat generation and feed rate, resulting in an improvement in build rate. This unified load-based control also enables a broader range of deposition scales than existing AFSD methods, such as MELD. The MELD process involves a large tool structure that fully encloses the consumable bar, and a significant portion of heat escapes into this large jig. This substantial heat dissipation imposes a limitation on the allowable feed rate, thereby restricting the range of feasible build scales. In contrast, the compact jig used in the proposed method minimizes heat loss, allowing the feeding rate to be more flexibly controlled by the applied load. As a result, both small and large layers can be fabricated more easily, expanding the practical applicability of the process compared to MELD. In summary, the ring-jig AFSD method—based on direct friction between the consumable bar and substrate—enables the solid-state deposition of metallic layers with improved surface finish and interfacial strength compared to conventional friction surfacing. The build rate is enhanced by increasing the applied load, and the simplified tool and thermal design contribute to lower energy consumption, reduced material usage, and lower equipment costs. The proposed method offers a practical and efficient approach for fabricating high-performance metallic components and holds strong potential for contributing to the development of various fields, such as the space industry, by enabling the cost-effective production of large-scale structures.

Methods

Conditions of layer fabrication

Friction surfacing tests and ring-jig AFSD tests were conducted using a friction stir welding machine (Koyo Industry, type-3). An AA6063-T6 round bar, an AA6063-T5 plate, and a JIS-SUS304 ring were used as specimens and a jig. The round bar, plate, and ring were commercially available in Japan. The diameter of the round bar was 20 mm, and the length was 100 mm. The plate was 100 mm wide, 300 mm long, and 10 mm thick. The round bar and plate were used as received. For the round bar, a grip part for installing it in the device was machined using a milling machine. The length of the grip part was 20 mm. The inside diameter, outside diameter, and height of the ring were 20 mm, 40 mm, and 20 mm, respectively. Since the ring was commercially available, its surface had been machined to a roughness of 6.3 μm or less. The round bar was used as the consumable material, and the plate was used as the substrate. The inner diameter surface of the ring was coated with a boron nitride lubricant. In the ring-jig AFSD test, the ring jig was attached at the tip of the round bar and was able to move freely along the longitudinal direction of the round bar. A round bar rotation speed of 1500 rpm, a substrate traverse speed of 400 ~ 800 mm min-1, and a load applied against the bar of 750 ~ 1500 kgf were used as process parameters.

Evaluation of the fabricated objects

After the fabrication test, the appearance and cross section of the fabricated layers were observed with a 3D surface profilometer (Keyence, VR-3200). In the hardness test, a tetrahedral diamond indenter used for typical Vickers hardness tests was loaded by a micro-Vickers hardness test machine (FUTURE-TECH, FM300). The Vickers hardness tests were performed using a pressing force of 100 gf, a pressing time of 15 s, and a measurement interval of 0.5 mm. The tensile test was conducted using a tensile test machine (Shimadzu, Autograph AGS-X 10 kN) at room temperature (300 K) with a constant crosshead speed of 0.24 mm min-1 (initial strain rate of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1.0\times {10}^{-3}$$\end{document}1.0×10−3 s-1) until the test specimen fractured. The strain during tensile deformation was measured using the digital image correlation (DIC) method. Before the tensile tests, a random speckle pattern was applied to the specimen surface using white and black spray paints. VIC-2D software was used for the DIC analysis. The subset size and step size used in the analysis were 27 pixels and 9 pixels, respectively.

Microstructure observation and fractography

Microstructure observations were conducted using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) on laminates fabricated by the ring-jig AFSD. The TEM specimen was prepared from the cross-section of the laminate. The TEM specimen was sampled from the middle regions of the first and second deposited layers and from the heat-affected zone (HAZ) in the substrate using a focused ion beam (FIB) system (JEOL, JIB-4500). The thickness of the TEM specimen was less than 100 nm, the width was 10 ~ 15 μm, and the length was 10 μm. TEM observations were performed at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. In addition, elemental analysis using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) in TEM was conducted to identify precipitates within the deposited layers and the HAZ in the substrate. The analyzed elements were Al, Fe, O, and Si.

Fracture surfaces of the fractured tensile test specimens were observed using a field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM) (JEOL, JSM-7001FA). The accelerating voltage was 15 kV.

Derivation of the build rate

The build rate of the layers was calculated from the values of width and thickness of the layers and the traverse speed of the round bar. By considering the shape of the layers as rectangular bodies, the build rate can be estimated via the equation below.1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V=w\times t\times v\,({{cm}}^{3}\,{h}^{-1})$$\end{document}V=w×t×v(cm3h−1)

Here, V represents the build rate, w is the width of the layer, t is the thickness of the layer, and v is the traverse speed of the round bar. The width and thickness of the layer deposited by the ring-jig AFSD were calculated from the steady-state region of the layer, excluding the 20 mm sections immediately after the start and just before the end of the deposition process. The steady-state region was divided into three sections, and the width and thickness of each section were calculated from the width measured from the appearance of the layer and the thickness at the center of the layer. The overall width and thickness of the layer were calculated from the average width and thickness of the three regions.
