Highly Enantio‐ and Diastereoselective Synthesis of Tetrahydrofuran Frameworks via Chiral Lewis Acid‐Catalyzed Formal [3+2] Cycloaddition of Allylsilanes
Authors: Hosung Lee, Dong Kyu Kim, Pingshan Xin, Jin Won Lee, Terim Seo, Do Hyun Ryu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA formal [3+2] cycloaddition via cyclization/1,2‐hydride shift initiated by nucleophilic attack of allylsilanes has been developed for the enantioselective synthesis of cis‐2,5‐disubstituted tetrahydrofuran‐3‐ones. In the presence of chiral oxazaborolidinium ion (COBI) catalyst, the reaction proceeded in high yields (up to 88%) with excellent stereoselectivity (up to 99% ee, > 20:1 dr). Key advantages of this approach are its unprecedented ability to forge THF scaffolds asymmetrically without inherent chirality, its high stereoselectivity including cis‐selectivity, and its capability to control four stereocenters in a single transformation.Keywords: allylsilanes, diastereoselectivity, enantioselectivity, formal [3+2] cycloaddition, tetrahydrofuran

Introduction

Chiral tetrahydrofuran (THF) scaffolds bearing multiple stereogenic centers are prevalent in natural products and pharmaceutical candidates [1, 2, 3, 4]. Owing to their significance, a range of synthetic strategies has been developed to access chiral THF architectures, including Prins cyclizations [5, 6, 7, 8], dipolar cycloaddition reactions [9, 10, 11, 12], and other approaaches [13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. Among these, formal [3+2] cycloadditions with allylsilanes have been provided efficient approaches to stereodefined THFs [21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28]. In these reactions, intramolecular attack of the oxygen atom on a bridging siliranium intermediate forms a five‐membered ring via two pathways [29, 30], providing multisubstituted THFs (Figure 1A).

Formal [3+2] cycloaddition of allylsilanes. (A) Previously reported formal [3+2] cycloadditions of allylsilanes via intramolecular cyclization pathways (B) This work: chiral Lewis acid‐catalyzed asymmetric formal [3+2] cycloaddition of allylsilanes with glyoxals

The Panek group [21, 22] and the Akiyama group [23, 24, 25] have reported formal [3+2] cycloadditions of allyl‐ or E‐crotylsilanes with carbonyl compounds. In these transformations, a Lewis acid‐bound alkoxide generated in situ captures the bridging siliranium intermediate, thereby promoting cyclization (Figure 1A, path a). Subsequently, the Angle group expanded the scope by employing siloxyacetaldehydes, wherein the siloxy oxygen acts as the nucleophile to facilitate cyclization (Figure 1A, path b). However, asymmetric induction in these reactions has been achieved using chiral starting material or chiral auxiliary. To the best of our knowledge, a catalytic asymmetric variant of this cycloaddition has not been reported.

Herein, we report the first catalytic enantioselective and stereocontrolled formal [3+2] cycloaddition of allylsilanes with glyoxals mediated by a chiral oxazaborolidinium ion (COBI) as a Lewis acid catalyst. The reactions furnish enantioenriched cis‐2,5‐disubstituted tetrahydrofuran‐3‐ones in good‐to‐excellent yields and enantioselectivities (Figure 1B). These motifs serve as valuable intermediates of the corresponding multi‐substituted THF from the structural skeletons of diverse natural products and biologically active compounds [31, 32], such as (+)–altholactone [33, 34, 35, 36, 37, 38], NITD008 [39], showdomycin [40], and (+)–epimuscarine [27] (Figure 2).

Examples of cis‐2,5‐disubstituted THFs with biological activity.

Recently, our group reported a highly enantioselective allylation of aldehydes with allylsilanes, promoted by a chiral oxazaborolidinium ion (COBI) as a Lewis acid catalyst [41]. Furthermore, we observed different reaction outcomes for glyoxal compared with typical aldehydes in Darzens‐type epoxidation and formal C─C bond insertion [42, 43, 44]. Inspired by these encouraging results, we investigated the enantioselective allylation of phenyl glyoxal in place of benzaldehyde under COBI‐catalyzed conditions.

Results and Discussion

Initially, the reaction of phenylglyoxal 1a and allyltrimethylsilane 2a was examined in the presence of 20 mol % COBI catalyst 3a activated by trifluoromethanesulfonic acid (triflic acid) (Table 1). When the reaction was performed at −78 °C in toluene, cis‐2,5‐disubstituted tetrahydrofuran‐3‐one 4a was obtained as a major product in 57% yield with 6:1 dr and 99% ee (entry 1). In addition to 4a, allylation product 5a via Hosomi–Sakurai reaction was isolated in 24% yield. When the solvent was switched from toluene to dichloromethane or chloroform, formation of 5a was suppressed and the yield and diastereoselectivity of 4a were improved (entries 2 and 3). The absolute configuration of 4a was established by X‐ray crystallographic analysis (CCDC: 2490388). Replacement of the silyl group with a sterically hindered dimethylbenzylsilyl group improved yield of 4 and 4/5 ratio but diminished dr (entry 4). Moreover, the use of allyltriisopropylsilane slightly improved diastereoselectivity (entry 5). After screening various catalyst structures, we found that the sterically bulky catalyst 3c, which has a 2,4‐dimethylphenyl Ar substituent activated by triflic acid was used, gave the best result of 83% yield and 99% ee with > 20:1 dr (entry 7).

Using the optimized reaction conditions for the catalytic enantioselective formal [3+2] cycloaddition, we evaluated the scope of this reaction with a range of glyoxals (Scheme 1). The effects of substituents on aromatic glyoxals were first investigated under the optimized reaction conditions (4c–4q). Phenylglyoxals with electron‐donating groups such as methyl or methoxy groups showed good to high yields with good diastereoselectivity (4d–4g). To examine whether the catalyst loading could be reduced, the reaction of ortho‐methylphenylglyoxal was performed with 15 mol % of catalyst 3c. Under these conditions, 4e was obtained in 82% yield with 99% ee and 7:1 dr, which was comparable to that obtained with 20 mol % catalyst 3c. The naphthyl glyoxals reacted well to give the desired products with excellent stereoselectivity in good yield (4h and 4i). Phenylglyoxals with electron‐withdrawing groups were amenable to the catalytic system, with 4j–4q obtained in good yields and stereoselectivities. The variations in diastereoselectivity are tentatively attributed to steric and electronic effects of the substituents. For example, the ortho‐methyl group in 4e likely introduces steric congestion that weakens catalyst–substrate coordination. In contrast, 4g, bearing a less sterically demanding substituent, allows more effective coordination with the catalyst; its electron‐donating nature may further enhance this interaction, resulting in higher diastereoselectivity. Likewise, the strongly electron‐withdrawing CF3 group in 4q may reduce the Lewis basicity of the carbonyl oxygen, leading to diminished stereocontrol. In the case of 4m and 4n, their relatively low diastereoselectivities may be attributed to halogen‐bonding interactions between the ortho‐halogen substituent (Cl or Br) and the carbonyl oxygen, which weaken coordination to the Lewis acid catalyst and reduce stereocontrol. Notably, cinnamyl and alkyl glyoxal were compatible, delivering the desired tetrahydrofuran‐3‐ones 4 (4r and 4s).

Encouraged by the results shown in Scheme 1, we applied this catalytic methodology to formal [3+2] cycloaddition of Z‐ and E‐crotylsilanes, and methallylsilane (Scheme 2). In contrast to allylsilane, reactions of substituted allylsilanes proceeded in lower yields with catalyst 3c. We therefore employed catalyst 3d. Z‐Crotyltriisopropylsilane 6 underwent conversion to give cis‐2,4,5‐trisubstituted tetrahydrofuran‐3‐ones 9a–9e as a single diastereomer under COBI catalyst 3d. With phenylglyoxal, 9a was obtained in 77% yield and 82% ee. Reactions of 4‐bromophenyl and 4‐methylphenylglyoxals were successful, providing 9b and 9c in good to high yield and high enantioselectivity. The 2‐naphthyl and n‐hexyl glyoxals delivered 9d and 9e in moderate yields and high enantioselectivities. Since partial epimerization of the cis‐2,4,5‐trisubstituted tetrahydrofuran‐3‐ones 9 was observed during purification on the silica gel column, products 9a–9e were purified by low‐temperature silica gel column chromatography at −78 °C. Next, we explored the scope with E‐crotyl or methallyltriisopropylsilane and arylglyoxals. Both 7 and 8 furnished the major diastereomers 10 and 11 together with the minor diastereomers 10′ and 11′. The corresponding products 10a, 10b, and 11a–11c were obtained in high combined isolated yields and enantioselectivities, but with low diastereoselectivities. Interestingly, 10c was obtained in 41% yield with 98% ee and high diastereoselectivity (dr > 20:1). The relative configurations were established by 1D NOE experiments of 9a, 10a, and 11c. On this basis, the relative stereochemistry of the other products was assigned by analogy.

To demonstrate the synthetic utility of these products, a formal synthesis of (+)–altholactone [45, 46, 47], a natural product with anticancer activity [34, 35, 36, 37, 38], was accomplished from cis‐2,5‐disubstituted tetrahydrofuran‐3‐one 4b (Scheme 3). Reduction with LAH proceeded smoothly to afford a single diastereomer, 3‐hydroxy THF 12a, in 89% yield without loss of enantiopurity [48]. Likewise, reduction of 4c and 9a furnished 3‐hydroxy THFs 12b and 12c bearing three or four stereogenic centers, in good to high yields. Subsequent Appel reaction of 12a delivered enantioenriched 3‐iodo THF 13 [49]. DBU‐mediated dehydrohalogenation then furnished 3,4‐dihydrofuran 14 [50]. Next, Tamao oxidation converted the silyl group to alcohol 15 [51]. Finally, protection of the alcohol 15 with TBDPSCl [52] provided the known intermediate 16 [47] en route to (+)–altholactone. The structure of this intermediate was confirmed by comparison of its NMR data with those reported in the literature. Notably, this intermediate can be accessed in only five steps from the cycloaddition product, whereas previously reported routes require up to 11 steps [47], highlighting the synthetic efficiency of the present method. Additionally, 3,4‐dihydrofuran 14 was converted into syn‐diol 17 (88% yield, 10:1 dr by crude 1H NMR) with retention of enantiopurity [53], a structural motif commonly found in natural products and pharmaceuticals [39, 40]. The configurations of 12 and 17 were established by 1D NOE experiments.

Our attention next turned to elucidating the mechanism of this novel transformation (Scheme 4). To verify the occurrence of a 1,2‐hydride shift, a deuterium‐labeling experiment was conducted. On treatment of allyltriisopropylsilane with phenylglyoxal‐d1 (18) under the optimized conditions, the reaction proceeded to give the corresponding deuterated product 19 (Scheme 4A). The 1H NMR spectrum revealed >99% deuterium incorporation at the 2‐position of cis‐2,5‐disubstituted tetrahydrofuran‐3‐one. These result clearly supports the involvement of a 1,2‐hydride shift in the transformation.

(A) Evidence for 1,2‐hydride shift pathway (B) Kinetic isotope effect experiment.

For further mechanistic insight, a kinetic isotope effect (KIE) experiment was conducted with a mixture of deuterated and nondeuterated phenylglyoxal, and the k H/k D was found to be 0.88 (Scheme 4B). These experiments suggest that nucleophilic addition is under the influence of an inverse secondary kinetic isotope effect (inverse SKIE). Such an inverse SKIE implies that a hybridization change of the aldehyde carbon from sp2 to sp3 by 1,2‐addition is the rate‐determining step [54, 55].

Based on these preliminary mechanistic investigations, we rationalize the observed stereochemistry for the catalytic enantioselective formation of cis‐2,5‐disubstituted tetrahydrofuran‐3‐one with COBI catalyst 3c is rationalized by the transition‑state model shown in Scheme 5 [44]. The coordination mode of phenylglyoxal to catalyst 3c and the enantioselective 1,2‐addition of allyltriisopropylsilane are consistent with those previously reported for the asymmetric allylation [41, 42]. In the pre‑transition‑state model 20, phenylglyoxal is activated via monodentate coordination to boron and is positioned above the 3,5‐dimethylphenyl group of COBI catalyst 3c. This aryl group effectively blocks the re face (back) from attack by the allyltriisopropylsilane, directing nucleophilic addition to the si face (front) [43, 56]. On the accessible face, allyltriisopropylsilane follows two competing pathways to give the cis‐ and trans‐products. Because the silylmethyl group of allyltriisopropylsilane is more sterically demanding than hydrogen when approaching the catalyst‐glyoxal complex, pathway 21 is favored over 21′, affording the lower‐energy intermediate 23 (0.00 kcal mol−1) relative to 23′ (+2.89 kcal mol−1). Subsequent intramolecular attack of the carbonyl oxygen on the β‐silyl‐stabilized cation furnishes the five‐membered ring intermediate 24. Finally, a 1,2‐hydride shift from C3 to C2 delivers (2S,5S)‐cis‐2,5‐disubstituted tetrahydrofuran‐3‐one 4c.

Transition‐state model for the enantioselective formation of cis‐2,5‐disubstituted tetrahydrofuran‐3‐one 4c catalyzed by COBI 3c.

To rationalize the observed diastereoselectivity, transition‐state models for substituted allylsilanes are proposed (Scheme 6). In the case of Z‐crotylsilane, the methyl group and the silyl substituent are oriented in the same direction, leading to a disfavored approach due to pronounced steric repulsion with the catalyst. Consequently, the favored pathway predominates, providing the product with high diastereoselectivity. In contrast, for E‐crotylsilanes and methallylsilanes, the methyl group is oriented opposite to the silyl substituent. As a result, both possible approaches experience similar levels of steric repulsion with the catalyst, leading to diminished diastereoselectivity.

Proposed transition‐state models for the diastereoselective reactions of crotyl‐ and methallylsilanes.

Conclusion

In summary, we have developed the first catalytic, highly stereocontrolled formal [3+2] cycloaddition of allylsilanes with glyoxals. This highly enantio‐ and diastereoselective method provides enantioenriched cis‐2,5‐disubstituted tetrahydrofuran‐3‐ones in high yields. The resulting functionalized tetrahydrofuran‐3‐ones are readily converted to optically active 3‐hydroxy THFs bearing cis‐2,5 stereocenters, a known intermediate en route to (+)–altholactone, and syn‐diol without erosion of optical purity. The absolute configuration of the product was predicted by the transition‐state model in Scheme 5. We believe that these cis‐2,5‐disubstituted tetrahydrofuran‐3‐ones will serve as valuable and versatile intermediates for further transformations.
