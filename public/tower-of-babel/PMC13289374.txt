Carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity improves cardiovascular risk prediction beyond SCORE2 in autoimmune rheumatic diseases: a 7-year follow-up study
Authors: Konstantinos Triantafyllias, Musa Aldabbag, Markus Schepers, Christian Geber, Antonis Fanouriakis, Muthuraman Muthuraman, Sahin Gürkan Dürük, Andreas Schwarting
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate the prognostic value of carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity (cfPWV), a marker of aortic stiffness, for cardiovascular events (CVEs) in patients with autoimmune rheumatic diseases (ARDs) and to compare its performance with the Systematic Coronary Risk Evaluation 2 (SCORE2).MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included patients with rheumatoid arthritis, systemic sclerosis and spondyloarthritis who underwent clinically indicated baseline non-invasive cfPWV assessment between 2012 and 2017 because of at least one additional cardiovascular risk factor. CV risk was estimated using SCORE2 and SCORE2-OP in eligible patients. Incident CVEs were ascertained through structured interviews over a median 7-year follow-up. Associations were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards models and predictive performance using receiver operating characteristic analysis.ResultsAmong 143 patients, 20 CVEs occurred. Baseline cfPWV was higher in patients who developed CVEs versus those who did not (10.06±2.16 m/s vs 8.8±2.3 m/s; p=0.024). SCORE2 did not differ between groups (6.55±3.90% vs 5.61±4.39%, p=0.35). cfPWV demonstrated strong discriminatory capacity for CVEs (area under the curve (AUC) 0.84; 95% CI (0.73 to 0.93), sensitivity 0.87, specificity 0.73), outperforming SCORE2 (AUC 0.56; 95% CI (0.41 to 0.72), sensitivity 0.60, specificity 0.69). In age-adjusted Cox regression, cfPWV remained a significant predictor of CVEs (HR 1.19, 95% CI (1.00 to 1.39); p=0.047). cfPWV correlated with baseline age (p<0.001), mean arterial pressure (p=0.013) and C reactive protein (p=0.011).DiscussioncfPWV is an independent predictor of future CVEs in patients with ARDs and outperforms SCORE2 in long-term risk prediction. Given its non-invasive nature, cfPWV may provide incremental value for CV risk stratification in this high-risk population.

Introduction

Autoimmune rheumatic diseases (ARDs) are associated with an elevated risk of cardiovascular (CV) disease and increased CV mortality.1 The underlying pathophysiology is complex and multifactorial in nature. A principal risk factor seems to be chronic inflammation, which plays a significant role in the development of atherosclerosis.2 Pro-inflammatory cytokines, including tumour necrosis factor-α, interleukin-1 and interleukin-6, are elevated in ARDs and contribute to endothelial dysfunction and atherosclerotic plaque formation.2 An increase in vascular stiffness gives rise to a range of haemodynamic alterations that exert considerable stress on the CV system.3 4 The most significant consequences are an elevation in systolic blood pressure, augmented afterload, diminished diastolic coronary perfusion, stimulation of left ventricular hypertrophy and an elevated risk of heart failure.3 Studies have shown that patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) have an approximately 1.5-fold to twofold increased CV risk compared with the general population, while the risk in patients with connective tissue diseases, like systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), is 2 to 3 times higher.58 Furthermore, a study of 446 449 patients assessed the CV event risk associated with various autoimmune diseases, finding statistically significant increases in both fatal and non-fatal events.7 The HRs of CV events were 1.83 for RA, 2.82 for SLE, 3.59 for systemic sclerosis (SSc) and 1.97 for spondyloarthritis (SpA), compared with healthy controls. Accordingly, comprehensive CV risk management is essential for patients with ARDs.

In conventional CV risk assessment, traditional risk score calculators like the Framingham score,9 Prospective Cardiovascular Münster Study score,10 the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) Systematic Coronary Risk Evaluation (SCORE), along with its updated version (SCORE2), are commonly used to estimate the 10-year risk for various CV events.11 12 Nevertheless, these scores have been shown to underestimate CV risk in rheumatic diseases,13 as they fail to account for factors like chronic inflammation, glucocorticoid therapy and impaired physical fitness, which are common in ARDs.1416 As a result, there is growing interest in complementary diagnostic markers that could provide a more comprehensive evaluation of CV status in ARDs.

Among these, carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity (cfPWV) is one of the most established surrogate markers of CV risk in the general population.17 As the gold-standard measure of aortic stiffness, cfPWV reflects the functional and structural integrity of the arterial wall and has been validated as an independent predictor of CV morbidity and mortality.4 18

During recent years, our research group and others have examined aortic stiffness and further CV surrogates (eg, carotid intima-media thickness, plaque formation, calcification burden and colour Doppler carotid ultrasound indices) in patients with different rheumatic conditions, including mixed connective tissue disease, SLE, inflammatory myopathies, Sjögren’s disease, RA and psoriatic arthritis, as well as fibromyalgia.1925 However, longitudinal data on the predictive value of these markers during long-term follow-up are scarce. To date, only one study has examined the predictive value of cfPWV during long-term follow-up examinations in patients with ARDs.26 However, this study focused solely on RA, included a relatively small sample size and did not report sensitivity or specificity values for cfPWV diagnostic accuracy. The lack of data on cfPWV in ARDs represents a notable gap in the literature, as cfPWV has been shown to accurately predict CV risk and enhance risk classification.4

Based on the above, in this study, we aimed to evaluate whether cfPWV can independently predict CV events in patients with ARDs over a long follow-up period, and to compare its diagnostic performance with the updated and established ESC SCORE2 risk calculator.12 We further sought to explore predictors of increased cfPWV among patient and disease-associated characteristics.

Methodology

Patient selection

Patients with RA, SSc and SpA (fulfilling respective classification criteria)2731 who underwent cfPWV assessments between January 2012 and December 2017 were retrospectively included in the study. Only patients who had a cfPWV examination on clinical indication, that is, suspicion of high CV risk in a real-life medical setting (including the presence of at least one CV risk factor among arterial hypertension, smoking, diabetes mellitus, obesity, dyslipidaemia or known CV disease), were considered. Patients without a confirmed diagnosis or who lacked PWV/clinical/demographic data were excluded. Baseline epidemiological data, information on medication use regarding immunosuppressives, antihypertensives, statins, antidiabetics and the presence of traditional CV risk factors were documented during the inpatient stay and were retrospectively evaluated together with follow-up data obtained through structured telephone interviews. The data on cfPWV were extracted from the device used for the examination.

Routine laboratory parameters, including inflammatory markers (C reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR)), lipid values and kidney function parameters (creatinine and glomerular filtration rate), were also assessed and documented. Immunological markers like antinuclear antibodies were detected on the human epithelial type 2 cells by indirect immunofluorescence. ELISA testing was used for the assessment of serum concentrations of antibodies such as rheumatoid factor and cyclic citrullinated peptide.

cfPWV examination

Aortic pulse wave velocity was assessed at a single time point between January 2012 and December 2017 (defined as the index date for each patient) using an oscillometric, non-invasive device (VicorderVR, SMT medical, Wuerzburg, Germany) by medical assistants who were blinded to patient characteristics. The examination was conducted in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions and the expert consensus proposed by Van Bortel et al.32 The cfPWV was calculated using the device software by dividing the travelled pulse wave distance (0.8× distance from the right carotid artery to the right femoral artery) by pulse transit time, expressed in metres per second (m/s). The mean value of cfPWV from three measurements was recorded and used for statistical analyses. The heart rate was simultaneously measured during the PWV examination.

Calculation of SCORE2 or SCORE2-OP

The updated ESC CV risk score (SCORE2 or SCORE2-OP for older persons) was calculated at the time of cfPWV assessment using a validated algorithm incorporating age, sex, smoking status, systolic blood pressure, total cholesterol and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol.12 33 To this end, an online calculator was employed, which was preconfigured to reflect the risk profile of Germany as a country with a moderate risk level.34 Inclusion criteria, such as age (40–69 years for SCORE2, 70–89 years for SCORE2-OP), systolic blood pressure (100–180 mm Hg) and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (116–270 mg/dL) were taken into consideration.

Telephone follow-up of cardiovascular events

The occurrence of cardiovascular events (CVEs) during the time period 2012–2023 was documented via telephone interviews conducted within 1 month, between April and May 2023. We included myocardial infarction, stroke, peripheral arterial disease, coronary heart disease with subsequent intervention, pulmonary artery embolism, deep vein thrombosis, heart failure and atrial fibrillation. In the event of death, the cause was determined through inquiries with the patient’s relatives and documented accordingly. When patients were uncertain about the nature of an event, they were asked to verify it with their general practitioner and/or by consulting medical records or hospital discharge letters. Of the initial cohort of 286 patients, 143 were successfully contacted. The remaining individuals could not be reached despite repeated contact attempts at different times. Causes of death were categorised into CV, pulmonary, malignant and other causes based on available information from relatives and medical records.

Statistical analysis

Normality of data distribution was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk test, quantile-quantile plots and visual inspection of histograms. Continuous variables are presented as mean and SD if they followed a normal distribution, and as median and 25th/75th percentile (IQR) if they were not normally distributed. Categorical variables are described in terms of both their absolute (n) and relative (%) frequencies. The mean comparison between the CVE group and the non-CVE group was conducted using the t-test for independent samples for variables with a normal distribution. Further sample comparisons were done with the Mann-Whitney U test. To assess the correlation between CV surrogates and continuous characteristics, Spearman’s (rho) or Pearson’s (r) correlation coefficients were used. Cox regression models were employed to investigate the time until CVE (or death) with cfPWV or SCORE2 as predictors, along with one further adjustment variable, such as age. The discriminative performance of baseline cfPWV and SCORE2 for predicting CV events during the 7-year follow-up was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis among patients who experienced a CVE or remained event-free for at least 7 years. Area under the curves (AUCs) were compared with DeLong’s test. In addition to the composite CVE outcome, sensitivity analyses were performed using alternative event definitions to assess the robustness of cfPWV discrimination. ‘Hard’ CVEs were defined as the occurrence of myocardial infarction and/or stroke.

A p value of <0.05 was deemed statistically significant. The statistical calculations were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics V.27 (USA) and R V.4.3.2, and in particular the packages pROC and cutpointr (for the ROC analysis).

Baseline cfPWV, but not SCORE2, differs between patients with and without CVEs

Mean baseline cfPWV was significantly higher in the CVE group compared with the non-CVE group (10.06±2.16 m/s vs 8.8±2.3 m/s; p=0.024) (figure 1A). In contrast, no statistically significant differences of baseline SCORE2 between the CVE group and non-CVE group (6.55±3.90% vs. 5.61±4.39%, p=0.350) were found (figure 1B). Further statistical differences between the two groups were found regarding traditional CV risk factors, including mean arterial pressure (MAP) (100.15±9.55 mm Hg in the event group vs 94.62±9.86 mm Hg in the non-event group; p=0.021), diabetes mellitus (20% vs 6.5%; p=0.044) and use of lipid-lowering therapies (40% vs 14.6%; p=0.007) (table 2).

Good discriminatory performance of cfPWV for incident CVEs

ROC analyses were performed to evaluate the ability of baseline cfPWV to predict incident CVEs during the 7-year follow-up. An optimal cfPWV cut-off value of 8.16 m/s yielded an AUC of 0.84 (95% CI (0.73 to 0.93)), with a sensitivity of 0.87 (95% CI (0.56 to 1.00)) and a specificity of 0.73 (95% CI (0.50 to 1.00)) for predicting CVEs in the total cohort (figure 2A). Similar findings were observed in the RA subgroup (figure 2B), where baseline cfPWV demonstrated good discriminatory performance (AUC 0.84 (95% CI (0.70 to 0.95))), with a sensitivity of 0.80 (95% CI (0.50 to 1.00)) and a specificity of 0.85 (95% CI (0.62 to 1.00)) (cut-off 8.63 m/s).

Separate analysis in patients with SSc also showed statistically significant results, with AUC 0.93 (95% CI (0.73 to 1.00)), sensitivity 0.97 (95% CI (0.75 to 1.00)) and specificity 0.88 (95% CI 0.62 to 1.00)). By contrast, in patients with SpA, the ROC analysis did not yield statistically significant results, probably due to small sample sizes (CVEs: n=1 for SpA).

Sensitivity analyses using alternative CVE definitions demonstrated consistent discriminatory performance of baseline cfPWV. Specifically, for hard CVE (myocardial infarction and/or stroke), cfPWV showed good discrimination in the total cohort, with an AUC of 0.781 (95% CI (0.61 to 0.93)), sensitivity of 0.86 (95% CI (0.50 to 1.00)) and specificity of 0.76 (95% CI (0.59 to 1.00)) (figure 2C).

Poor discriminatory performance of SCORE2 for CVEs

In comparison with cfPWV, SCORE2 had AUC 0.56 (95% CI (0.41 to 0.72)), sensitivity 0.60 (95% CI (0.24 to 1.00)) and specificity 0.69 (95% CI (0.17 to 1.00)) across the entire cohort (figure 2A), for the optimal cut-off point 7.33. No significant values were observed for separate RA (AUC 0.524, 95% CI (0.32 to 0.72), sensitivity 0.65, 95% CI (0.12 to 1.00), specificity 0.62, 95% CI (0.17 to 1.00)), SSc (AUC 0.73, 95% CI (0.25 to 1.00), sensitivity 0.75, 95% CI (0.40 to 1.00), specificity 0.90, 95% CI (0.40 to 1.00)) or SpA analysis (AUC 0.33, 95% CI (0.00 to 1.00), sensitivity 0.57 (0.00 to 1.00), specificity 0.75 (0.00 to 1.00)).

The discriminative ability of cfPWV (AUC 0.84) was significantly higher than that of SCORE2 (AUC 0.56), as confirmed by DeLong’s test (Z=3.0, p=0.009; 95% CI of AUC difference 0.069 to 0.483).

Moreover, the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) 1.5 multiplication factor for SCORE2 was evaluated within the primary prevention cohort using discrimination, calibration and reclassification metrics. As expected for a linear transformation, the AUC remained unchanged at 0.538, indicating no improvement in discrimination. However, calibration improved, with the mean predicted risk increasing from 6.84% to 10.3%, approaching the observed event rate of 14.7%.

Regarding reclassification, application of a 10% clinical threshold resulted in a gain in sensitivity (event Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI)=0.100), correctly upgrading 10.0% of patients who experienced an event. This was accompanied by a 19.0% increase in false positives among non-event patients, resulting in a total NRI of −0.090.

Additional sensitivity analyses were performed to further evaluate the robustness of the findings. When restricting the analysis to atherosclerotic CVEs (myocardial infarction, stroke, peripheral arterial disease and coronary artery disease requiring intervention), cfPWV demonstrated an AUC of 0.917 (95% CI 0.83 to 0.98), whereas SCORE2 showed an AUC of 0.625 (95% CI 0.44 to 0.80), confirming the superior predictive performance of cfPWV.

Consistent results were observed in the primary prevention subgroup (patients without prior CVEs), where cfPWV retained superior predictive performance, with a sensitivity of 91% and a specificity of 84%, compared with a sensitivity of 58% and a specificity of 80% for SCORE2.

Cox regression analysis: cfPWV as an independent predictor of incident CVEs

Results of Cox proportional hazards regression analyses are summarised in table 4. Finally, Cox regression was employed to evaluate the strength of cfPWV as a predictor for the risk of CVEs over time and HRs were calculated for selected variables with available time-to-event data (table 4). Due to the limited number of events (n=20), multivariate models were restricted to a maximum of two variables (cfPWV plus one additional variable) to avoid overfitting. Among different CV risk factors, like age, MAP, nicotine use, hyperlipidaemia, diabetes and BMI, only age and MAP showed a possible confounding effect on cfPWV. After adjustment for age, cfPWV remained a statistically significant predictor of CVEs (HR 1.18, 95% CI (1.00 to 1.39); p=0.047), indicating a higher risk of events with increasing baseline cfPWV. A similar result was observed after adjustment for MAP, reaching the threshold of statistical significance (HR 1.15, 95% CI (0.999 to 1.32); p=0.05). Additional adjustment for other traditional CV risk factors or CV medications did not materially alter the association between cfPWV and CVEs, and cfPWV remained statistically significant across these models (table 4).

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that cfPWV is a predictor of CVEs in patients with ARDs with an additional CV risk factor over a median follow-up of 7 years. We also identified a cfPWV cut-off value for predicting CVEs and found that the SCORE2 model did not significantly predict events, indicating that its diagnostic performance was inferior to that of cfPWV. To our knowledge, this is one of the first retrospective follow-up studies to directly compare the prognostic value of cfPWV with SCORE2 in ARDs. Moreover, it represents the largest study to date evaluating the diagnostic performance of the gold-standard measure of aortic stiffness for predicting CVEs in patients with rheumatic diseases.

Importantly, meta-analyses have identified cfPWV as an independent predictor of CVEs in the general population,4 35 36 and a cfPWV value of 10 m/s has been proposed as a cut-off for increased aortic stiffness and hypertension-mediated end-organ disease.32 In our cohort, the optimal cfPWV cut-off was lower at 8.16 m/s. This likely reflects the fact that our threshold was derived for prediction of CVEs in a selected ARD population rather than for identification of established arterial stiffness per se. However, disease-specific vascular alterations in ARDs, as well as the relatively small sample size, may also have influenced this estimate, which should therefore be interpreted with caution.

When evaluating various combinations of CVEs, including myocardial infarction and stroke, cfPWV’s predictive capacity remained statistically significant and consistent with previous findings. The only similar study we identified on this topic is that of Ikdahl et al.26 However, this prospective clinical study focused exclusively on patients with RA. The authors assessed the incidence of CV events in 138 patients using cfPWV and other methods (augmentation index, carotid intima-media thickness and carotid plaques) over a mean follow-up period of 5.4 years. Similar to our study, mean cfPWV values were statistically significantly higher in the event group, compared with patients without CVEs. The calculations also remained statistically significant after adjustment for age and further traditional CV risk factors. In our exploration, cfPWV likewise remained a significant predictor after accounting for age and multiple additional risk variables. Notably, our work extends the existing evidence by incorporating a longer follow-up period, including a broader spectrum of ARDs, and directly comparing cfPWV with the updated SCORE2 risk model.

Interestingly, cfPWV correlated weakly with arterial pressure values in the present study. There is a known bidirectional association and a pathophysiological link between arterial pressure and cfPWV, as long-term elevated blood pressure values can lead to progression of arterial stiffness.37 Moreover, acute arterial pressure increase can lead to stretching of the arterial wall, making it seem stiffer.38 Interestingly, neither MAP nor further traditional CV risk factors, including BMI, diabetes, smoking status and cholesterol levels, substantially affected the predictive performance of cfPWV in this cohort. These findings support the notion that cfPWV may act largely as an independent predictor of CVEs.

In ARDs, systemic inflammation seems to be a key factor in the progression of CV complications. Inflammatory markers such as ESR and CRP have been linked to increased vascular stiffness in the scientific literature, emphasising the role of systemic inflammation in the pathophysiology of macroangiopathy and CV disease.39 Mechanistically, this can be explained by the influence of matrix metalloproteinases on the arterial wall, leading to degradation of elastin fibres and resulting in stiffer arteries.40 Chronic systemic inflammation contributes moreover to endothelial dysfunction and arterial remodelling, processes that are closely linked to increased PWV.2 41 In our study, we observed a significant correlation between cfPWV and CRP levels, underscoring the impact of inflammation on vascular health. While disease duration showed no association with cfPWV in RA and SpA, a weak correlation could be seen in the subset of SSc. In general, higher cumulative damage associated with longer-standing disease and persistent systemic inflammation may contribute to progressive vascular dysfunction and, consequently, increased CV risk over time.42

Even though baseline cfPWV and future CV outcomes were not associated with baseline immunomodulatory therapy, treatment effects are difficult to assess due to the design of the study, high variance across different immunomodulatory agents and potential confounding by indication. Additionally, patients with rheumatic diseases are often treated with glucocorticoids, which themselves can contribute to an increased CV risk.43 44 Notably, these relationships are often overlooked in conventional CV risk assessment tools, which primarily focus on traditional risk factors like arterial hypertension, dyslipidaemia and smoking in the general population. In recognition of this gap, EULAR recommended multiplying traditional CV risk scores by a factor of 1.5 in patients with RA in its earlier CV guidelines (2017).42 However, evidence supporting the application of this adjustment to other rheumatic diseases remains limited,42 45 and this multiplication factor was not included in the most recent EULAR guidelines for CV risk assessment published in 2022.13 Interestingly, multiplication of SCORE2 by 1.5 did not statistically improve the predictive performance (AUC or total NRI) in our cohort and, while it ensured that high-risk patients were not overlooked, it led to a higher rate of overclassification. In comparison with cfPWV, SCORE2 demonstrated overall lower predictive performance, potentially pointing to an underestimation of CV risk by SCORE2.15 To our knowledge, no data currently exist comparing cfPWV with SCORE2 in ARDs. Since SCORE2 only incorporates traditional CV risk factors and excludes ARD-specific parameters, such as systemic inflammation, the effects of immunosuppressants and reduced physical fitness, cfPWV could serve as a more accurate indicator of residual risk that is not captured by SCORE or SCORE2 in this population.46

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, because cfPWV assessment was performed based on clinical indication rather than systematically in consecutive patients with ARDs, selection bias cannot be excluded. As a result, the study population may not be fully representative of the broader ARD population and the findings should be interpreted with caution. Second, subgroup analyses beyond RA were limited by different cohort-associated aspects, which restrict disease-specific conclusions and warrant cautious interpretation: in SSc, this likely reflects both sample size constraints and cohort demographic characteristics, including the predominance of female patients, while in SpA, younger age likely contributed to the lower event rates and represents a potential confounder. Accordingly, these findings are not readily generalisable to all patients with inflammatory rheumatic diseases.

Third, other CV risk scores occasionally applied in inflammatory rheumatic populations, such as QResearch Cardiovascular Risk Algorithm 3 (QRISK3), were not calculated. Although QRISK3 incorporates RA and SLE as risk modifiers, it does not specifically capture SSc or SpA, and several required variables were unavailable in the present dataset, limiting its applicability in this cohort. Fourth, cfPWV and SCORE2 were assessed at a single baseline time point, whereas CVEs occurred over a follow-up period of up to 7 years. During this interval, changes in disease activity, CV risk factors and medical therapy may have occurred, potentially introducing residual confounding. However, this limitation applies equally to both cfPWV and SCORE2, as both markers were evaluated at the same index time point, and therefore does not affect the comparative interpretation of the results. Fifth, although the median follow-up duration of 7 years does not fully correspond to the 10-year risk horizon captured by SCORE2, cfPWV demonstrated good predictive performance even within this shorter observation period, suggesting potential clinical utility for mid-term CV risk stratification. Sixth, CVEs were ascertained retrospectively via telephone interviews, which may have introduced some uncertainty in event classification, particularly for causes of death, and 50% of the initial cohort was lost to follow-up, raising the possibility of recall bias. Nevertheless, these limitations apply equally to both cfPWV and SCORE2 and are therefore unlikely to account for the observed difference in predictive performance. Moreover, the study population was sufficient to statistically address the primary objective and to demonstrate a consistent association between cfPWV and CV outcomes, including in multivariable analyses. Seventh, cumulative glucocorticoid exposure could not be reliably reconstructed due to the retrospective design and limited availability of longitudinal medication data. In addition, diabetes and lipid-lowering therapy were more prevalent in the event group and may have contributed to higher cfPWV values. However, the relatively small sample size may have limited the ability to evaluate their independent effects more robustly.

To conclude, in this study, we were able to show that cfPWV can be a promising tool for CV stratification also in patients with ARDs with one additional CV risk factor by exhibiting high sensitivity and specificity in the detection of a wide spectrum of CVEs during a long follow-up period. Given that SCORE2 showed a lower diagnostic performance, cfPWV may be incorporated into the assessment of CV risk in everyday clinical practice to assist CV screening and ultimately improve prognosis. However, prospective studies with a larger number of cases for different rheumatic diseases are warranted to validate these results.
