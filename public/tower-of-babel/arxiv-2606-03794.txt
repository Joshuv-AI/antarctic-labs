Limit Analysis of Graph Neural Networks with Wireless Conflict Graphs
Romina Garcia Camargo, Zhiyang Wang, Alejandro Ribeiro

Abstract
Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have emerged as a powerful tool for wireless resource allocation that leverages the underlying graph structure of communication networks. Their transferability property enables models trained on small-scale graphs to generalize to large-scale deployments with little performance deterioration, a desirable property for currently growing networks. Wireless networks are sparse regimes, where a single node is connected to a small number of other users. This work establishes theoretical results for transferability of GNNs over graphs derived from sparse Random Geometric Graphs (RGGs). In particular, we focus on conflict graphs of RGGs used to model interference among links. Our approach considers the closeness between RGGs and Deterministic Grid Graphs (DGG) to establish bounds in the performance loss when a model is transferred across scales. We validate our theoretical findings through the problem of link scheduling, demonstrating that our learned policies consistently outperform existing benchmarks at scale. Finally, we examine the impact of our theoretical assumptions on empirical performance.



I
Introduction

Learning-based solutions are promising tools for addressing complex resource allocation challenges in next-generation networks. Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) are well-suited for these tasks, as the architecture exploits the underlying graph structure of wireless systems
[
12
,
16
,
2
]
.
In particular, it allows for transferability: models trained on small graphs generalize to larger networks with minimal performance loss.

While empirical studies show that GNNs successfully transfer in wireless settings
[
5
]
, these observations lack a formal foundation. Multiple works have explored the transferability of Graph Neural Networks
[
13
,
9
,
7
,
17
,
8
]
. However, most existing frameworks typically rely on limit objects such as graphons
[
13
]
or manifolds
[
17
]
, which define transferability as connectivity increases toward a dense limit. Physical constraints in wireless channels limit the number of connections per user, resulting in sparse graphs that traditional dense-limit theories fail to account for. To bridge this gap, recent work has begun analyzing transferability on sparse graphs via graphops
[
8
]
or using Random Geometric Graphs (RGGs), leveraging the geometric structure intrinsic to communication systems
[
3
]
.

Resource allocation tasks such as link scheduling depend on the relationships between communication links rather than the users themselves. This is often tackled via conflict graphs, where links are modeled as nodes and edges represent interference or shared resources
[
18
,
4
]
(Section
II
). However useful, it creates a specific sparse regime distinct from the underlying physical network.

In this work we establish the theoretical analysis for transferability of GNNs on conflict graphs (Sections
III
and
IV
). Extending our framework
[
3
]
, we model wireless connectivity using Random Geometric Graphs (RGGs) and prove that transferability holds via Deterministic Grid Graphs (DGGs). By leveraging their spatial periodicity, we overcome the analytical challenges posed by the topological irregularities of random node placements. Our results demonstrate that as the number of nodes increases, transferability is guaranteed for conflict graphs of RGGs that approximate conflict graphs of DGGs.

We validate our theoretical framework with numerical experiments on the wireless link scheduling problem, incorporating a minimum transmission requirement per link (Section
V
). The GNN is trained on conflict graphs derived from RGGs to select which links should be scheduled at each time step. Empirical results demonstrate that our transferred model consistently outperforms benchmarks at larger scales. Furthermore, we characterize the robustness of our RGG-DGG proximity assumption by analyzing model performance as the graph topology deviates from a grid-like structure.

. Figure

II-A

Optimal Resource Allocation in Wireless Settings

Let there be a network with
m
users, where each user is associated with a state at time
t
given by the vector
\mathbf{x}_{m}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Given the channel gains in
\mathbf{S}_{m}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
, where
[{\mathbf{S}}_{m}(t)]_{ij}
is the gain from user
i
to user
j
, we are interested in allocating resources across the network. Let
\mathbf{p}({\mathbf{x}}_{m}(t),{\mathbf{S}}_{m}(t))
be a mapping from network states to the instantaneous resource allocation policy across the network. The system will then return a reward given the allocation,
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{p}({\mathbf{x}}_{m}(t),{\mathbf{S}}_{m}(t));\mathbf{x}_{m}(t),\mathbf{S}_{m}(t))
. Considering the expectation over the stationary joint distribution of
(\mathbf{x}_{m},\mathbf{S}_{m})
, we focus on the long-term average performance as

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}_{m}=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{x}_{m},\mathbf{S}_{m})].

(1)

Consider a utility function
u_{0}(\mathbf{r}_{m})
and long-term system constraints
\mathbf{u}(\cdot)
. Our goal is to maximize the expected reward (
1
) by designing our allocation policy
{\mathbf{p}}(\mathbf{x}_{m},\mathbf{S}_{m})
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}^{\star}(\mathbf{x}_{m},\mathbf{S}_{m})=

\displaystyle\argmax_{\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{x}_{m},\mathbf{S}_{m})\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x}_{m},\mathbf{S}_{m})}\ u_{0}(\mathbf{r}_{m}),

(2)

s.t.

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}_{m}=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{x}_{m},\mathbf{S}_{m});\mathbf{x}_{m},\mathbf{S}_{m})],

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{r}_{m})\geq\mathbf{0}.

The objective in Equation (
2
) is often nonconvex in
\mathbf{p}
, which makes finding a solution challenging. The common approach introduces a parameterized policy
\bm{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}_{m},\mathbf{S}_{m};\mathbf{H}_{m})
,
with parameters
\mathbf{H}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
and learns a solution (Section
III
).

II-B

Example: Wireless Link Scheduling

Let
{\mathbf{G}}
be wireless network with
m
links, where
p_{i}(t)\in\{0,1\}
denotes the status of link
i\in\{1,2,\dots,m\}
. Entry
p_{i}(t)=1
indicates that the link
i
is scheduled to transmit at time step
t
. Two links interfere each other if they are scheduled at the same time and share a common user
[
6
]
. The vector
{\mathbf{p}}(t)=[p_{i}(t)]_{1\leq i\leq m}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
describes the scheduling status of all links and is therefore supported on the edges of
{\mathbf{G}}
.

Let us define
{\mathbf{G}}_{C}
the conflict graph of
{\mathbf{G}}
, then
{\mathbf{p}}(t)
is supported on the nodes of
{\mathbf{G}}_{C}
. We denote the adjacency matrix of
{\mathbf{G}}_{C}
as
{\mathbf{A}}_{m}\in\{0,1\}^{m\times m}
.
The successful transmissions
{\mathbf{r}}_{m}({\mathbf{p}})
resulting from a schedule
{\mathbf{p}}(t)
can be computed via the Hadamard product between the schedules and nonnegative projection
[\mathbf{1}-{\mathbf{A}}_{m}{\mathbf{p}}(t)]_{+}
,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{r}}_{m}({\mathbf{p}})={\mathbf{p}}(t)\odot\big[\,\mathbf{1}-{\mathbf{A}}_{m}{\mathbf{p}}(t)\,\big]_{+}.

(3)

We are interested in the average rate over a time horizon. Wireless link scheduling seeks a policy that maximizes the long-term average of the sum rate
{\mathbf{r}}_{m}({\mathbf{p}})
over
T
time steps. The policy must also satisfy the interference constraints intrinsic to the topology of the network.
We incorporate a minimum transmission requirement for each link,
\bm{\Delta}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{m}
, such that all links must transmit at least a fraction of time:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{p}}^{\star}(1:T)=

\displaystyle\argmax_{{\mathbf{s}}(t)\in\{0,1\}^{K}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}{\mathbf{1}}^{\top}{\mathbf{p}}(t)\odot\big[\,\mathbf{1}-{\mathbf{A}}_{m}{\mathbf{p}}(t)\,\big]_{+},

s.t.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}{\mathbf{p}}(t)\odot\big[\,\mathbf{1}-{\mathbf{A}}_{m}{\mathbf{p}}(t)\,\big]_{+}\geq\boldsymbol{\Delta}.

(4)

To solve this constrained optimization task we turn to the Lagrangian dual domain, defining a new learning objective that combines our objective function and a penalty term for constraint violations. Nonetheless, Lagrangian maximizers will not result in workable policies as they will not vary over time. By leveraging the dual descent dynamics, we can incorporate the evolution of the dual variable
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
into our learning via state augmentation
[
1
]
. More details about the problem formulation and the algorithm can be found in
[
2
]
.

III
Training by Transference

Finding optimal solutions to resource allocation problems such as the one in Equation (
2
) is often computationally prohibitive. Common approaches approximate these solutions by using a parameterized policy
\bm{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}_{m},\mathbf{A}_{m};\mathbf{H}_{m})
. The objective is to identify the optimal parameters
\mathbf{H}^{\star}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
that maximize the network utility while satisfying the expected constraints:

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{m}^{\star}=

\displaystyle\argmax_{\mathbf{H}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}}\ u_{0}({\mathbf{r}}_{m}),

(5)

s.t.

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}_{m}=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{f}(\bm{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}_{m},\mathbf{A}_{m};\mathbf{H}_{m});\mathbf{x}_{m},\mathbf{A}_{m})],

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{r}_{m})\geq\mathbf{0}.

In wireless applications, Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) are the preferred architecture for
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
. By training over diverse channel realizations, GNNs learn efficient allocation policies that maintain awareness of the network topology.

Let the graphs used for training have
m
nodes, and assume we want to execute on graphs with
n
nodes, such that
m<n
. In this case, the evaluation consists of finding policies
\boldsymbol{\Phi}({\mathbf{x}}_{n},{\mathbf{A}}_{n};{\mathbf{H}}_{m}^{\star})
. Note that while the descriptions of the network state (
{\mathbf{x}}_{n},{\mathbf{A}}_{n}
) now have
n
dimensions, the parameter matrix
{\mathbf{H}}_{m}^{\star}
is independent of the number of nodes in the graph. This implies we
can
execute a GNN trained on a small graph on a larger graph, i.e.
transfer
the GNN. Nonetheless, it is necessary to assess whether these policies remain optimal as the network scales. To analyze this, we first present the GNN architecture in Section
III-A
. Furthermore, we introduce Random Geometric Graphs and their deterministic grid counterparts (Section
III-B
) to later present our transferability results (Section
IV
).

III-A

Graph Neural Networks

A Graph Neural Network is composed of a cascade of layers, each containing a graph convolutional filter and a pointwise nonlinearity
\gamma
, with
\gamma:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Given a graph signal
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, a graph convolutional filter is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}h_{k}\mathbf{A}^{k}\mathbf{x},

(6)

where
K
is the order of the filter and
\{h_{k}\}_{k=0}^{K-1}
are the coefficients. The filter is a polynomial on a matrix representation of the graph,
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
.

A single-layer GNN applies the nonlinearity
\gamma
to the output of the graph filter in Equation (
6
). For a GNN with
L
layers, the
l
-th layer takes
\mathbf{x}_{l-1}
, the output of the previous layer, as input signal. This is later processed via
\gamma
to obtain
\mathbf{x}_{l}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{l}=\gamma\left(\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}h_{lk}\mathbf{A}^{k}\mathbf{x}_{l-1}\right).

(7)

We can summarize the weights in a matrix
\mathbf{H}\in\mathcal{H}
as the trainable parameters of the network.

III-B

Deterministic and Random Geometric Graphs

Define a metric space of size
W\times W
and place
v
nodes randomly following a uniform distribution in the space,
\mathcal{V}\sim\mathcal{U}^{2}(0,W)
. We create an edge between any two nodes that meet that their Euclidean distance is at most a fixed radius
r
,
\mathcal{E}=\{(i,j):d(i,j)\leq r\}
. These sets define a Random Geometric Graph (RGG)
[
11
]
,
{\mathbf{G}}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
, which appear naturally in wireless settings due to the relation between the fixed radius
r
and connectivity determined by signal strength.

Define a regular lattice in a Euclidean space with
v
intersections. By placing a node in each intersection and creating links between neighboring nodes following the grid, we can obtain a deterministic grid graph (DGG),
{\mathbf{\tilde{G}}}=(\tilde{\mathcal{V}},\tilde{\mathcal{E})}
.

RGGs can be seen as a perturbation of DGGs, resulting from adding Gaussian noise
\eta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
to the node positions. If the perturbation is small, an analysis can be done that seamlessly relates RGGs and DGGs
[
3
]
. As observed in Figure
1
(see
\sigma=0
), the conflict graph of a DGG will also be highly regular and can be easily related to the conflict graph of a RGG. This forms the basis of the theoretical analysis we present in Section
IV
.

IV
Transferability in Conflict Random Geometric Graphs

Consider a DGG
{\mathbf{\tilde{G}}}
with
m_{1}
edges. Construct a RGG
{\mathbf{G}}
by adding positional noise to
{\mathbf{\tilde{G}}}
, connecting the nodes given a fixed radius such that it has
m_{2}
edges with
m_{1}\simeq m_{2}
. In order to study the transferability of GNNs from conflict Random Geometric Graphs to conflict Deterministic Grid Graphs, we consider the normalized adjacency matrices of conflict graphs
{\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}}_{m_{1}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}\times m_{1}},{\mathbf{A}}_{m_{2}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{2}\times m_{2}}
.

To assess the similarity between a DGG and a RGG, we are interested in comparing their adjacency matrices. To enable the comparison we zero-pad the normalized adjacency matrices to obtain
{\mathbf{A}},\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(m_{1}+m_{2})\times(m_{1}+m_{2})}
. By embedding both graphs into a spatially-aligned vector space, the perturbation captures only the mismatched edges between the two topologies.

We assume that GNN consists of layers cascading the graph filter, which is assumed to be integral Lipschitz continuous and the normalized Lipschitz point-wise nonlinearities
[
14
]
.
We note that these are reasonable assumptions and have been implemented widely in theory and in practice.

With these definitions, we present in Theorem
1
the result for transferability of GNNs between RGG and DGG.

Theorem 1

Let
{\mathbf{\tilde{G}}}
be a DGG with
m_{1}
edges and
{\mathbf{G}}
be a RGG with
m_{2}
edges. Define
{\mathbf{A}},\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}\in\{0,1\}^{(m_{1}+m_{2})\times(m_{1}+m_{2})}
the zero padded adjacency matrices of their conflict graphs, such that
\|{\mathbf{A}}-\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}\|\leq\varepsilon
. Let
\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{A};\mathbf{H})
be a
L

{\mathbf{H}}
that is integral Lipschitz with constant
C
and a normalized Lipschitz nonlinearity
\gamma
. The GNN can be transferred between
\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}
and
{\mathbf{A}}
with minimal performance loss:

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\Phi}({\mathbf{x}},\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}};{\mathbf{H}})-\boldsymbol{\Phi}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{A}};{\mathbf{H}})\|\leq

\displaystyle 2L\sqrt{\varepsilon C}\|{\mathbf{x}}\|.

(8)

Theorem

shows that the error between running a GNN on a DGG and a RGG is small, provided the graphs are close.

where

In Theorem
6
, the performance difference between the evaluation on small and large DGGs is bounded by a small constant under mild assumptions. As

A GNN trained on small RGGs can be transferred to small DGGs (Theorem

VI
Conclusions

Random Geometric Graphs are a simple, yet accurate model of wireless networks. This work provides the theoretical study of transferability of Graph Neural Networks on conflict graphs derived from sparse RGG. This ensures wireless policies trained to satisfy requirements on the links of a network can be transferred to larger graphs with minimal loss in performance. We verified our theoretical results hold with numerical experiments on the wireless link scheduling task. It remains crucial to better assess the relevance of our assumptions with respect to practical development, which we leave for future work.

Proof of Theorem 1

Before showing the transferability of GNNs from DGG to RGG, we present in Theorem

{\mathbf{\tilde{G}}}
be a deterministic grid graph with
M

{\mathbf{G}}

K

\mathbf{\tilde{A}},{\mathbf{A}}\in\{0,1\}^{(K+M)\times(K+M)}

.
2
2

2

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{\tilde{A}}){\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{H}}({\mathbf{A}}){\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}\leq 4C\varepsilon\|{\mathbf{x}}\|.

\displaystyle|\tilde{\lambda}_{i}-\lambda_{i}|\leq\varepsilon

\displaystyle{\mathbf{V}}_{i}=

\displaystyle[{v}_{j}]_{j:|\tilde{\lambda}_{i}-\lambda_{j}|\leq\delta}

\displaystyle{\mathbf{V}}_{i}^{C}=

\displaystyle[{v}_{j}]_{j:|\tilde{\lambda}_{i}-\lambda_{j}|>\delta}

\tilde{v_{i}}
and compare the corresponding outputs.

\displaystyle{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{\tilde{A}})\tilde{v_{i}}-{\mathbf{H}}({\mathbf{A}})\tilde{v_{i}}=({\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{\tilde{A}})-{\mathbf{H}}({\mathbf{A}}))\tilde{v_{i}}=\Delta_{i}

(15)

Because the eigenvectors form a complete orthonormal basis, bounding the filter output difference for a given eigenvector
\tilde{v_{i}}
is sufficient to bound the difference in the outputs for any arbitrary graph signal. Next, we consider the squared operator norm of
\Delta_{i}
. We replace the filter applied to
\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}
,
{\mathbf{H}}(\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}})
by the frequency response representation of the filter.

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{i}\|^{2}=

\displaystyle\|h(\tilde{\lambda}_{i})\tilde{v_{i}}-{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{{A}})\tilde{v_{i}}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|h(\tilde{\lambda}_{i}){\mathbf{I}}-{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{{A}})\tilde{v_{i}}]({\mathbf{V}}_{i}{\mathbf{V}}_{i}^{H}\tilde{v_{i}}+{\mathbf{V}}_{i}^{C}({\mathbf{V}}_{i}^{C})^{H}\tilde{v_{i}})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|[h(\tilde{\lambda}_{i}){\mathbf{I}}-{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{{A}})\tilde{v_{i}}]{\mathbf{V}}_{i}{\mathbf{V}}_{i}^{H}\tilde{v_{i}}\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\|[h(\tilde{\lambda}_{i}){\mathbf{I}}-{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{{A}})\tilde{v_{i}}]{\mathbf{V}}_{i}^{C}({\mathbf{V}}_{i}^{C})^{H}\tilde{v_{i}}\|^{2},

(16)

where the crossterms disappear because the vectors are orthogonal. Define the terms of Equation (
VII-A
)
\Delta_{i}^{1}
and
\Delta_{i}^{2}
, respectively. We have that
\Delta_{i}^{1}
is associated to those eigenvalues of
\mathbf{{A}}
that are closer than
\delta
to the eigenvalue
\tilde{\lambda}_{i}
of
\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}
, while
\Delta_{i}^{2}
considers those further away.

To bound
\Delta_{i}^{1}
we expand
{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{{A}})
via the eigendecomposition of
\mathbf{{A}}
.

\displaystyle\Delta_{i}^{1}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{{A}}){\mathbf{V}}_{i}{\mathbf{V}}_{i}^{H}\tilde{v_{i}}-h(\tilde{\lambda_{i}}){\mathbf{V}}_{i}{\mathbf{V}}_{i}^{H}\tilde{v_{i}}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{V}}h({\Lambda}){\mathbf{V}}^{H}{\mathbf{V}}_{i}{\mathbf{V}}_{i}^{H}\tilde{v_{i}}-{\mathbf{V}}_{i}h(\tilde{\lambda_{i}})I{\mathbf{V}}_{i}^{H}\tilde{v_{i}}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{V}}_{i}\left(h({\Lambda}_{i})-h(\tilde{\lambda_{i}}){\mathbf{I}}\right){\mathbf{V}}_{i}^{H}\tilde{v_{i}}

(17)

Let
h(\cdot)
be an integral Lipschitz filter with constant
C
, we can bound
\Delta_{i}^{1}
as follows.

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{i}^{1}\|^{2}\leq C^{2}\delta^{2},

(18)

where we consider the eigenvectors are orthonormal.

To bound
\Delta_{i}^{2}
, we apply the triangle inequality to the operator norm of the filter difference. Noting the filter coefficients are normalized such that
\|{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{{A}})\|=\max_{j}|h({\lambda}_{j})|\leq 1
, we get:

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{{A}})-h(\tilde{\lambda_{i}}){\mathbf{I}}\|^{2}\leq

\displaystyle(\|{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{{A}})\|+\|h(\tilde{\lambda_{i}}){\mathbf{I}}\|)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1+1)^{2}=4.

(19)

We must also bound the projection of
\tilde{v_{i}}
onto the complement subspace,
\|({\mathbf{V}}_{i}^{C})^{H}\tilde{v_{i}}\|^{2}
. Using the fact that
(\mathbf{{A}}-\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}})\tilde{v_{i}}=(\mathbf{{A}}-\tilde{\lambda_{i}}{\mathbf{I}})\tilde{v_{i}}
, we left-multiply by
({\mathbf{V}}_{i}^{C})^{H}
to obtain:

\displaystyle({\mathbf{V}}_{i}^{C})^{H}(\mathbf{{A}}-\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}})\tilde{v_{i}}=

\displaystyle({\Lambda}_{i}^{C}-\tilde{\lambda_{i}}{\mathbf{I}})({\mathbf{V}}_{i}^{C})^{H}\tilde{v_{i}}

(20)

Taking the norm of both sides and applying the Davis-Kahan theorem, we bound the vector projection. Since the eigenvalues in
{\Lambda}_{i}^{C}
are strictly further than
\delta
from
\tilde{\lambda_{i}}
, we have:

\displaystyle\|({\mathbf{V}}_{i}^{C})^{H}\tilde{v_{i}}\|\leq\frac{\|(\mathbf{{A}}-\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}})\tilde{v_{i}}\|}{\min_{j}|\tilde{\lambda_{i}}-{\lambda}_{j}^{C}|}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta}.

(21)

Putting Equations (
VII-A
) and (
21
) together, we obtain a bound for
\Delta_{i}^{2}
:

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{i}^{2}\|^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{{A}})-h(\tilde{\lambda_{i}}){\mathbf{I}}\|^{2}\|({\mathbf{V}}_{i}^{C})^{H}\tilde{v_{i}}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\delta^{2}}

(22)

Finally, substituting the bounds for both terms yields the final bound for the squared error:

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{i}\|^{2}=

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{{A}})\tilde{v_{i}}-h(\tilde{\lambda_{i}})\tilde{v_{i}}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C^{2}\delta^{2}+4\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\delta^{2}}.

(23)

Let
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(K+M)\times(K+M)}
be the input to the graph filter, we can write
{\mathbf{x}}
in terms of the eigenvectors
\tilde{v_{i}}
:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{x}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\tilde{v_{i}}^{H}{\mathbf{x}}\tilde{v_{i}})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\mathbf{{\tilde{x}}}_{i}\tilde{v_{i}}).

(24)

To finish the proof, we bound the squared norm of
\Delta_{i}{\mathbf{x}}
by observing the eigenvectors are orthogonal and therefore the cross products are zero.

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{i}{\mathbf{x}}\|_{2}^{2}=

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{i}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\mathbf{{\tilde{x}}}_{i}\tilde{v_{i}})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\Delta_{i}\mathbf{{\tilde{x}}}_{i}\tilde{v_{i}}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{{\tilde{x}}}_{i}^{2}\|\Delta_{i}\tilde{v_{i}}\|_{2}^{2},

(25)

where we find that the first term is
\|{\mathbf{x}}\|_{2}^{2}
and the second term is what we bounded in Equation (
VII-A
). Finally, if we define
\delta^{2}=\frac{2\varepsilon}{C}
, we get the final bound:

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{{A}}){\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{H}}(\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}){\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}\leq 4C\varepsilon\|{\mathbf{x}}\|.

(26)

∎

Having shown the transferability of graph filters, we proceed to extend the result to Graph Neural Networks in Theorem
5
.

Theorem 5

Let
{\mathbf{\tilde{G}}}
be a DGG with
m_{1}
edges and
{\mathbf{G}}
be a RGG with
m_{2}
edges. Define
{\mathbf{A}},\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}\in\{0,1\}^{(m_{1}+m_{2})\times(m_{1}+m_{2})}
the zero padded adjacency matrices of their conflict graphs, such that
\|{\mathbf{A}}-\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}\|\leq\varepsilon
. Let
\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{A};\mathbf{H})
be a
L
-layer GNN with a graph filter
{\mathbf{H}}
that is integral Lipschitz with constant
C
and a normalized Lipschitz nonlinearity
\gamma
. The GNN can be transferred between
\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}
and
{\mathbf{A}}
with minimal performance loss:

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\Phi}({\mathbf{x}},\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}};{\mathbf{H}})-\boldsymbol{\Phi}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{A}};{\mathbf{H}})\|\leq

\displaystyle 2L\sqrt{\varepsilon C}\|{\mathbf{x}}\|.

(27)

Proof.

We compare the outputs of the GNN applied to both graphs via
{\mathbf{A}}
and
\mathbf{\tilde{A}}
for layer
l
, considering the pointwise nonlinearity
\sigma(\cdot)
is normalized Lipschitz.

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{l}({\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{\tilde{A}};{\mathbf{H}})-\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{l}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{A}};{\mathbf{H}})\|=

\displaystyle\|\sigma({\mathbf{H}}_{l}(\mathbf{\tilde{A}})\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{l-1}({\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{\tilde{A}};{\mathbf{H}}))

\displaystyle-\sigma({\mathbf{H}}_{l}({\mathbf{A}})\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{l-1}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{A}};{\mathbf{H}}))\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{H}}_{l}(\mathbf{\tilde{A}})\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{l-1}({\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{\tilde{A}};{\mathbf{H}})

\displaystyle-{\mathbf{H}}_{l}({\mathbf{A}})\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{l-1}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{A}};{\mathbf{H}})\|.

(28)

Add and subtract the cross term
{\mathbf{H}}_{l}(\mathbf{\tilde{A}})\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{l-1}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{A}};{\mathbf{H}})
. By applying the triangle inequality, we establish a recursion:

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{l}({\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{\tilde{A}};{\mathbf{H}})-\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{l}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{A}};{\mathbf{H}})\|\leq

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{H}}_{l}(\mathbf{\tilde{A}})\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{l-1}({\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{\tilde{A}};{\mathbf{H}})

\displaystyle-{\mathbf{H}}_{l}(\mathbf{\tilde{A}})\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{l-1}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{A}};{\mathbf{H}})\|

\displaystyle+\|{\mathbf{H}}_{l}(\mathbf{\tilde{A}})\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{l-1}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{A}};{\mathbf{H}})

\displaystyle-{\mathbf{H}}_{l}({\mathbf{A}})\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{l-1}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{A}};{\mathbf{H}})\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{H}}_{l}(\mathbf{\tilde{A}})\|\|\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{l-1}({\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{\tilde{A}};{\mathbf{H}})

\displaystyle-\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{l-1}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{A}};{\mathbf{H}})\|

\displaystyle+\|{\mathbf{H}}_{l}(\mathbf{\tilde{A}})-{\mathbf{H}}_{l}({\mathbf{A}})\|

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{l-1}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{A}};{\mathbf{H}})\|.

(29)

We assume that the filter coefficients are normalized, such that
\|{\mathbf{H}}_{l}(\mathbf{\tilde{A}})\|\leq 1
. Furthermore, the combination of non-amplifying filters and the normalized Lipschitz property of the activation function ensures that
\|\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{l-1}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{A}};{\mathbf{H}})\|\leq\|{\mathbf{x}}\|
. Incorporating the transferability bound of the single graph filter, we obtain:

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{l}({\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{\tilde{A}};

\displaystyle{\mathbf{H}})-\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{l}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{A}};{\mathbf{H}})\|\leq

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{l-1}({\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{\tilde{A}};{\mathbf{H}})-\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{l-1}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{A}};{\mathbf{H}})\|+2\sqrt{\varepsilon C}\|{\mathbf{x}}\|.

(30)

By unrolling the recursion over the layers, we arrive at the final bound for the outputs of a GNN with
L
layers:

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{L}({\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{\tilde{A}};{\mathbf{H}})-\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{L}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{A}};{\mathbf{H}})\|\leq

\displaystyle 2L\sqrt{\varepsilon C}\|{\mathbf{x}}\|.

(31)

∎

VII-B

Proof of Theorem 2

Theorem 6

Let
\boldsymbol{\Phi}({\mathbf{x}},{\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}}_{n};{\mathbf{H}})
be the parameterized policy that achieves a performance loss
{\mathcal{L}}({\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}}_{n},{\mathbf{H}})
when applied on a grid graph with size
n
and achieves a loss of
{\mathcal{L}}({\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}}_{m},{\mathbf{H}})
when applied on another grid graph with size
m
. Suppose
n>m
, the difference of these two losses can be bounded as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}({\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}}_{n},{\mathbf{H}})\leq{\mathcal{L}}({\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}}_{m},{\mathbf{H}})+C\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{x}}^{2}]+2\sqrt{{\mathcal{L}}({\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}}_{m},{\mathbf{H}})C\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{x}}^{2}]},

(32)

where
C=\frac{H_{K}^{2}}{m}(2\sqrt{m}K+K^{2})
and
H_{K}=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}|h_{k}|\|\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}_{m}\|_{2}^{k}
.

Proof.

Given we consider a 2D space, we can write the performance
{\mathcal{L}}({\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}}_{m},{\mathbf{H}})
as the sum of the expected difference for each entry of the window of size
m
.

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}({\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}}_{m},{\mathbf{H}})=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{i,j=0}^{m-1}|\boldsymbol{\Phi}({\mathbf{x}},\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}_{m};{\mathbf{H}})(i,j)-{\mathbf{y}}(i,j)|^{2}\Big].

(33)

We are interested in comparing
{\mathcal{L}}({\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}}_{m},{\mathbf{H}})
as presented in (
33
) to
{\mathcal{L}}({\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}}_{n},{\mathbf{H}})
. Let us define
\epsilon(i,j)=\boldsymbol{\Phi}({\mathbf{x}},\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}_{n};{\mathbf{H}})(i,j)-{\mathbf{y}}(i,j)
to simplify the notation.

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}({\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}}_{n},{\mathbf{H}})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{i,j=0}^{n-1}|\boldsymbol{\Phi}({\mathbf{x}},\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}_{n};{\mathbf{H}})(i,j)-{\mathbf{y}}(i,j)|^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(Nm)^{2}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{i,j=0}^{Nm-1}|\epsilon(i,j)|^{2}\Big],

(34)

where we defined
N=\lceil\frac{n}{m}\rceil
. We recenter the sums, denoting
{\mathcal{T}}=\{im-\frac{(N-1)m}{2}\mid i\in\mathbb{Z},1\leq i<N\}
. This is equivalent to defining small squares in the 2D space in which we partition the sums, where each point has coordinates
\tau=(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}({\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}}_{n},{\mathbf{H}})\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(Nm)^{2}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{\tau\in{\mathcal{T}}}\Big[\sum_{i,j=0}^{m-1}|\epsilon(i-\tau_{1},j-\tau_{2})|^{2}\Big]\Big].

(35)

The sum over the partitions
{\mathcal{T}}
does not affect the expectation and can therefore be taken outside. Furthermore, because the input and output are assumed to be jointly stationary, we have
\mathbb{E}[|\epsilon(i-\tau_{1},j-\tau_{2})|^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[|\epsilon(i,j)|^{2}]
.

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}({\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}}_{n},{\mathbf{H}})\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(Nm)^{2}}\sum_{\tau\in{\mathcal{T}}}\Big[\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{i,j=0}^{m-1}|\epsilon(i,j)|^{2}\Big]\Big].

(36)

In Equation (
36
), the dependence on
\tau
inside the expectation has been removed. This allows the computation of the sum over
{\mathcal{T}}
independently from the other terms. As
|{\mathcal{T}}|=N^{2}
, we can simplify the terms as follows:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}({\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}}_{n},{\mathbf{H}})\leq

\displaystyle\frac{N^{2}}{(Nm)^{2}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{i,j=0}^{m-1}|\epsilon(i,j)|^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m^{2}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{i,j=0}^{m-1}|\epsilon(i,j)|^{2}\Big].

(37)

Let
\sqcap_{m}
be a masking operator acting on the larger space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For any spatial signal,
\sqcap_{m}
pointwise multiplies the signal by the indicator function
\mathbf{1}((i,j)\in m\times m)
, zeroing out all entries outside the
m\times m
window while preserving the dimensionality
n
. Under this unified dimension, we have
{\mathbf{x}}_{m}=\sqcap_{m}{\mathbf{x}}
, and
\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}_{m}=\sqcap_{m}\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}_{n}\sqcap_{m}
.

We now return to the original notation, dropping
\epsilon(i,j)
and defining the full-scale output
{\mathbf{x}}_{n,L}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}({\mathbf{x}},\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}_{n};{\mathbf{H}})
and the smaller-scale output
{\mathbf{x}}_{m,L}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\sqcap_{m}{\mathbf{x}},\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}_{m};{\mathbf{H}})
. Because both are now naturally represented in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we can directly apply the masking operator to their difference.

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}({\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}}_{n},{\mathbf{H}})\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m^{2}}\mathbb{E}\big[\|\sqcap_{m}({\mathbf{x}}_{n,L}-{\mathbf{y}})\|^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m^{2}}\mathbb{E}\big[\|\sqcap_{m}({\mathbf{x}}_{n,L}-{\mathbf{x}}_{m,L})+\sqcap_{m}({\mathbf{x}}_{m,L}-{\mathbf{y}})\|^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m^{2}}\mathbb{E}\big[\|\sqcap_{m}({\mathbf{x}}_{m,L}-{\mathbf{y}})\|^{2}\big]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{m^{2}}\mathbb{E}\big[\|\sqcap_{m}({\mathbf{x}}_{n,L}-{\mathbf{x}}_{m,L})\|^{2}\big]

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{m^{2}}\mathbb{E}\big[\|\sqcap_{m}({\mathbf{x}}_{m,L}-{\mathbf{y}})\|\|\sqcap_{m}({\mathbf{x}}_{n,L}-{\mathbf{x}}_{m,L})\|\big].

(38)

The first term in Equation (
VII-B
) is, by definition,
{\mathcal{L}}({\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}}_{m},{\mathbf{H}})
. Because
{\mathbf{x}}_{m,L}
strictly operates within the
m
region, applying the mask to it leaves it unchanged (i.e.,
\sqcap_{m}{\mathbf{x}}_{m,L}={\mathbf{x}}_{m,L}
). The second term can be bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\|\sqcap_{m}({\mathbf{x}}_{n,L}-{\mathbf{x}}_{m,L})\|=

\displaystyle\Big\|\sqcap_{m}\Big(\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}h_{k}\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}_{n}^{k}{\mathbf{x}}\Big)

\displaystyle-\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}h_{k}(\sqcap_{m}\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}_{n}\sqcap_{m})^{k}(\sqcap_{m}{\mathbf{x}})\Big\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}|h_{k}|\|\sqcap_{m}(\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}_{n}^{k}{\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle-(\sqcap_{m}\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}_{n}\sqcap_{m})^{k}(\sqcap_{m}{\mathbf{x}})\|.

(39)

By the construction of the conflict graph, edges only exist between nodes that are at most adjacent or diagonal to each other. Therefore, a single application of the adjacency matrix spreads information by at most 1 hop. The error is bounded by the spectral norm of the adjacency matrix and the signal isolated at this boundary expansion:

\displaystyle\|\sqcap_{m}({\mathbf{x}}_{n,L}-{\mathbf{x}}_{m,L})\|\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}|h_{k}|\|\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}_{n}\|_{2}^{k}\|\sqcap_{m+k}({\mathbf{x}}-\sqcap_{m}{\mathbf{x}})\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}|h_{k}|\|\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}_{n}\|_{2}^{k}\|\sqcap_{m+K-1}({\mathbf{x}}-\sqcap_{m}{\mathbf{x}})\|.

(40)

Let
H_{K}=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}|h_{k}|\|\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}_{n}\|_{2}^{k}
.

\displaystyle\|\sqcap_{m}({\mathbf{x}}_{n,L}-{\mathbf{x}}_{m,L})\|\leq

\displaystyle H_{K}\|\sqcap_{m+K-1}({\mathbf{x}}-\sqcap_{m}{\mathbf{x}})\|.

(41)

As
{\mathbf{x}}
is stationary, the expected squared norm of the boundary term in Equation (
41
) can be evaluated as the variance of
{\mathbf{x}}
over the volume difference
(m+K-1)^{2}-m^{2}
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|\sqcap_{m}({\mathbf{x}}_{n,L}-{\mathbf{x}}_{m,L})\|^{2}\big]\leq

\displaystyle H_{K}^{2}\big((m+K-1)^{2}-m^{2}\big)\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{x}}^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle H_{K}^{2}(2mK+K^{2})\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{x}}^{2}].

(42)

This bounds the second term in Equation (
VII-B
). Observe the third cross-term can also be bounded via Cauchy-Schwarz and the second term bound. Defining
C_{1}=H_{K}^{2}(2mK+K^{2})
, we arrive at the final bound:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}({\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}}_{n},{\mathbf{H}})\leq{\mathcal{L}}({\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}}_{m},{\mathbf{H}})+C_{1}\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{x}}^{2}]+2\sqrt{{\mathcal{L}}({\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}}_{m},{\mathbf{H}})C_{1}\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{x}}^{2}]}.

(43)

∎

VII-C

Proof of Theorem 3

Theorem 7

Under assumptions analogous to Theorem
1
, consider an L-layer GNN
\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{A};\mathbf{H})
trained to minimize the loss
{\mathcal{L}}({\mathbf{A}}_{m},{\mathbf{H}})
for RGGs of size
m
, such that
{\mathcal{L}}({\mathbf{A}}_{m},{\mathbf{H}})\leq\zeta
and suppose the deviation of RGG to its corresponding DGG is bounded by
\epsilon
. The GNN can be transferred to graphs of size
n
with minimal performance deterioration:

\displaystyle|{\mathcal{L}}({{{\mathbf{A}}}}_{n},{\mathbf{H}})-{\mathcal{L}}({{{\mathbf{A}}}}_{m},{\mathbf{H}})|=

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\Bigg(\sqrt{\zeta}\left(\sqrt{\epsilon}\|\mathbf{x}_{n}\|+\sqrt{\epsilon}\|\mathbf{x}_{m}\|\right)+\epsilon\|\mathbf{x}_{n}\|^{2}+\epsilon\|\mathbf{x}_{m}\|^{2}\Bigg)

(44)

Proof.

Let us decompose the loss difference as follows.

\displaystyle|{\mathcal{L}}({{{\mathbf{A}}}}_{n},{\mathbf{H}})-{\mathcal{L}}({{{\mathbf{A}}}}_{m},{\mathbf{H}})|\leq

\displaystyle|{\mathcal{L}}({{{\mathbf{A}}}}_{n},{\mathbf{H}})-{\mathcal{L}}({\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}}_{n},{\mathbf{H}})|

\displaystyle+|{\mathcal{L}}({\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}}_{m},{\mathbf{H}})-{\mathcal{L}}({\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}}_{n},{\mathbf{H}})|

\displaystyle+|{\mathcal{L}}({\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}}_{m},{\mathbf{H}})-{\mathcal{L}}({{{\mathbf{A}}}}_{m},{\mathbf{H}})|

(45)

Observe that the first and third terms relate the performance for a RGG and a DGG that are sufficiently close, and therefore they can be bounded using Theorem
1
. The second term relates two grid graphs of different sizes and can be bounded via Theorem
6
. This concludes the proof.
∎
