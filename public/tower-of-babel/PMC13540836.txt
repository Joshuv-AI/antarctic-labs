From coexistence to antagonism: nutrient- and temperature-dependent interactions between Bacillus atrophaeus JunSE1L and Pseudomonas chlororaphis O6 with implications for the wheat rhizosphere
Authors: Anagha Wankhade, Ashlynn Clark, David W Britt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInteractions between beneficial plant-associated microbes influence microbial community structure and plant health, yet antagonistic relationships among co-occurring beneficial bacteria remain poorly understood. This study examined interactions between Pseudomonas chlororaphis O6 (PcO6), a wheat root-colonizing epiphyte, and JunSE1L, a Bacillus atrophaeus seed-borne endophyte isolated from winter wheat (Triticum aestivum var. Juniper).ResultsFor wheat grown in a sterile sand matrix, JunSE1L emerged from seeds and colonized roots; however, when PcO6 was introduced at the bottom of the growth boxes, it migrated and became the dominant rhizoplane colonizer. JunSE1L emerged from both damaged roots and shoots, and produced biosurfactants that lowered the surface tension of water to 30 mN/m. On agar plates at 22 °C PcO6 strongly inhibited JunSE1L growth on minimal medium (MM) and to a limited extent on rich LB medium; inhibition was absent on both media at 37 °C. On MM, JunSE1L colonies adopted dendritic, highly spread morphologies, potentially from biosurfactant release that may have also facilitated PcO6 swarming over established JunSE1L colonies. Whole PcO6 supernatant and a < 3 kDa filtrate each suppressed JunSE1L growth in liquid MM at 22 ˚C. The inhibitory compound(s) was resistant to proteinase K digestion and heat deactivation, exhibiting bacteriostatic activity against JunSE1L.ConclusionsPcO6 inhibits JunSE1L in a temperature-, nutrient-, and density-dependent manner: On rich growth media they coexist, while on defined minimal media, PcO6 dominates, suggesting that resource limitation intensifies competitive interactions and favors epiphyte (PcO6) - mediated suppression of endophyte (JunSE1L). While both bacteria comprise the wheat root microbiome, under conditions recapitulating the rhizosphere (defined nutrients, moderate temperature, and PcO6 already present in the soil) PcO6 release of a bacteriostatic agent may repress JunSE1L emergence. Background

Microbial interactions within plant ecosystems are fundamental to plant health, soil function, and ecosystem resilience. The rhizosphere and endosphere host diverse microbial communities, including epiphytes that establish biofilms on the rhizoplane and endophytes that may exit these internal tissues to join the epiphytes, each occupying distinct ecological niches that shape their competitive and cooperative dynamics [1–3]. The plant-microbe-soil interactions are central to nutrient cycling, plant growth promotion, and disease suppression, with both specialist and generalist microbes contributing to plant health through the assembly of plant-associated microbiomes [4–7].

Among plant-associated bacteria, Pseudomonas chlororaphis O6 (PcO6) is recognized as an aggressive wheat rhizoplane colonizer with well-characterized biocontrol and plant-beneficial traits. PcO6 produces secondary metabolites, including phenazines and pyoverdine-like siderophores, that contribute to pathogen inhibition and competitive resource acquisition in the rhizosphere [8, 9]. These traits support its role as a plant probiotic and biocontrol agent [10]. PcO6 can also induce systemic resistance and stress tolerance in plants, responses that depend in part on effective and sustained root colonization [11–13].

Seed-borne endophytes, particularly Bacillus spp., are consistently present in wheat seeds and play a foundational role in shaping the early plant microbiome. Their transmission and persistence are influenced by environmental conditions (e.g., nutrients and temperature) and plant developmental stage [14–16]. These endophytes can remain within plant tissues or emerge onto plant surfaces, especially following tissue disruption caused by root growth stress or biotic interactions such as nematode feeding. Such facultative transitions from an endophytic to an epiphytic lifestyle allow these microbes to rapidly respond to environmental cues and plant needs [15, 16].

The present study investigates the interaction between the epiphytic probiotic PcO6 and a seed-borne Bacillus atrophaeus endophyte, designated JunSE1L (Juniper Seed Endophyte Isolate 1 Large colony variant), isolated from winter wheat (Triticum aestivum, Juniper var.) developed by the Idaho Agriculture Experiment Station for stress tolerance. Microbial interactions are mediated through a range of metabolites and biomolecules, and like many Bacilli spp., JunSE1L produces endospores, hemolysins, proteases, hydrophobins, and highly surface-active biosurfactants. In addition to signals from nearby microbes, environmental factors, particularly nutrient availability in rich versus defined media, strongly influence the growth behavior and metabolite production of associated microbes, including Bacillus endophytes [17, 18]. Interactions between Pseudomonas and Bacillus species are therefore complex and context-dependent, involving both antagonistic and cooperative mechanisms. In vitro co-inoculation studies and assays using secreted products have revealed that Bacillus extracellular matrices can protect colonies from Pseudomonas invasion, while Pseudomonas metabolites can induce Bacillus sporulation, underscoring the importance of secondary metabolites and biofilm structures in shaping these interactions [19–21].

We therefore hypothesized that PcO6 – JunSE1L interactions are shaped by environmental conditions, particularly nutrient availability and temperature, which may shift their relationship from coexistence to antagonism, and thus examine how growth medium composition, temperature, and microbial metabolites modulate interactions between endophyte and epiphyte. Using wheat root colonization experiments, biofilm assays, and cell-free supernatant challenges, we test the hypothesis that PcO6 inhibits JunSE1L under nutrient-limited conditions in a one-directional manner, where PcO6 suppresses JunSE1L growth or persistence, but JunSE1L does not inhibit PcO6 under the tested conditions. We further examine whether PcO6 may utilize endophyte-derived surfactants to enhance its motility and biofilm formation. Together, these experiments aim to resolve how environmental conditions shift the balance between antagonism and cooperation, revealing how seed-borne endophytes and soil-derived epiphytes jointly shape early wheat root colonization and microbiome assembly.

Methods

Recovery and confirmation of epiphytic and endophytic bacterial strains from wheat seedlings

To confirm the continued strong root-surface colonization ability of the epiphytic strain Pseudomonas chlororaphis O6 [22], a glycerol stock culture was used to inoculate surface-sterilized wheat seeds (Triticum aestivum var. Juniper). Inoculated seeds were planted in enclosed Magenta™ growth boxes containing 300 g sterile, moistened quartz sand. After 8 days, seedling roots were carefully harvested and immersed in sterile distilled water to detach root-associated bacteria. The suspension was serially diluted and plated on Lysogeny Broth (LB) agar (2%).

Bright orange colonies characteristic of PcO6 appeared within 48 h. A representative colony was recovered and cultured in minimal medium (MM) broth [23] to re-establish the epiphytic stock culture. In addition, intact roots were placed directly onto LB agar plates. Orange colonies radiated from the entire root length within 48 h, confirming strong PcO6 colonization of the wheat root surface.

Control seeds that were surface-sterilized but not inoculated with PcO6 were first plated on LB agar to verify the effectiveness of the surface sterilization procedure. Under these conditions, only a single colony morphology forming white, wrinkled colonies consistently emerged from the seeds. When similarly surface-sterilized seeds were planted in sterile sand, bacteria with the same colony morphology were recovered from seedling roots upon plating on LB agar. No additional colony morphotypes were observed from seed plating, root plating, or root wash dilutions under these conditions.

The isolate was obtained and purified through the three-streak method and designated JunSE1L. Identification based on 16S rRNA gene sequencing showed 100% sequence similarity to Bacillus atrophaeus; therefore, the isolate is hereafter referred to as Bacillus atrophaeus strain JunSE1L.

The sequence of JunSE1L was deposited in GenBank under accession PZ178898. PcO6 has previously been deposited in GenBank as GCA_000264555.

Both PcO6 and JunSE1L cultures were preserved as 15% glycerol stocks at -75 °C. Prior to each experiment, cells were revived from frozen stocks and cultured in either liquid LB or MM in shake flasks to obtain actively growing cultures for inoculation.

Endophyte JunSE1L and epiphyte PcO6 interaction in the presence of the host wheat plant

Wheat seeds were surface sterilized for 10 min by immersion in 10% (v/v) hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. 516813) with shaking at 140 RPM, followed by three rinses with sterile distilled water. To confirm effective surface sterilization, seeds were placed on 2% LB agar plates and incubated for 24 h and the absence of microbial growth was verified before planting. All planting and harvesting were conducted in a UV-sterilized laminar flow biosafety cabinet.

For plant growth assays, sand (Granusil 4075) was washed thoroughly with distilled water and sterilized by dry heat treatment at 200 °C for 14 h. Sterilized sand (300 g) was transferred into 20 × 6 × 6 cm Magenta™ plant growth boxes that had been sterilized with 70% ethanol. To examine rhizosphere colonization, 50 g of sand was first added to each box and inoculated with 1 mL of a 105 CFU/ml PcO6 suspension, then 250 g sterile sand was added, after which 49 mL sterile distilled water was added. This resulted in a final PcO6 concentration of approximately 10⁴ CFU mL⁻¹ per Magenta™ box.

Surface-sterilized wheat seeds (3 seeds per box/ 3 boxes) were planted at a 0.5 cm depth into the prepared sand, and then the box was sealed using a coupling gasket to an inverted Magenta™ box, providing 13 cm height for growth. Plants were grown under 16 h white light exposure for 7 days, corresponding to a time point when the shoots reached the top of the growth box lid (Fig. 1A). Seedlings were harvested on day 7 and roots were excised aseptically and plated onto 2% LB agar for recovery and visualization of bacterial colonization. Control plants were grown under identical conditions in sterile sand without PcO6 inoculation.

Co-inoculation assays on agar surfaces

Antagonistic interactions between Bacillus atrophaeus JunSE1L and Pseudomonas chlororaphis O6 were assessed on agar surfaces using LB and defined MM. LB medium consisted of tryptone (Fisher BioReagents, Cat. No. BP1421), yeast extract (Fisher BioReagents, Cat. No. BP1422), and no added salt. The MM formulation [23] contained buffered potassium phosphate (pH 7) (MP Biomedicals, Cat. No. 151946, Fisher Chemicals, Cat. No. P285), citrate (Mallinckrodt Chemicals, Cat. No. 0754 − 12), and sucrose (Mallinckrodt AR, Cat. No. 8360) as carbon sources, and ammonium sulfate (Fisher Chemicals, Cat. No. A702) and magnesium sulfate (Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. M63) as the primary nitrogen, magnesium, and sulfur sources. For all assays, inocula were prepared from shake cultures grown in the same medium used for the subsequent antagonism experiments. Cultures in LB medium were grown for 22 h for both bacteria, whereas in MM, PcO6 was grown for 22 h and JunSE1L for 42 h. All cultures were incubated at either 22–37 °C with shaking at 140 rpm. Cells were pelleted, washed, and resuspended in sterile water to an OD600 = 0.5.

Three growth plate bioassays were used to assess colony development as a function of time and distance from a competing colony: (i) Direct contact zone of inhibition (ZOI) assay where a small volume of bacteria 1 is placed in the center of a plate fully coated with bacteria 2; (ii) Indirect contact assay where competing colonies are at fixed separation distance while inoculation times of the second bacteria are varied; (iii) Variable distance assay where the bacteria 2 inoculation spots are placed at increasing distances that spiral outward from a central colony of bacteria 1.

i) Zone of inhibition (ZOI) assay: A series of five log dilutions of 0.5 OD600 of washed cell suspensions was prepared in sterile water, and 100 µl of either PcO6 or JunSE1L bacterial suspension was spread uniformly onto LB or MM (2% agar) plates. After 30 min of drying, a 10 µl drop of the challenge bacterium (or sterile water as a control) was placed at the center of each plate. Plates were incubated for 72 h before imaging. Growth outcomes were assessed visually by measuring the central colony diameter and the zone of clearing diameter.

ZOI diameters were analyzed using a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with medium and dilution as fixed factors, followed by Tukey’s HSD test for multiple comparisons. Data are presented as mean ± standard error (SE), and statistical significance was defined at p < 0.05.

ii) Side-by-side antagonism assay: Dual inoculations were performed by placing two 10 µl droplets of each bacterial suspension (OD₆₀₀ = 0.5) 0.5 cm apart on LB or MM (1% agar) plates. Controls consisted of plates inoculated with two droplets of the same strain. After drying, plates were incubated at 22 °C, and relative growth was compared between mixed and control inoculations. To assess whether inhibitory metabolites were released in the absence of the challenging bacteria, a modified assay was performed in which one strain was allowed to grow for 48 h before inoculation with the second strain. On MM agar colony overlap was observed. To identify the presence of each bacterium in the overlapping regions, biomass was scraped from defined locations of overlap and spread onto 2% LB agar, where PcO6 is identified as flat, orange colonies and JunSE1L as raised, white colonies. A second method to identify the cell type involved carefully pressing the scrapes onto glass slides and examining cell morphology by Atomic Force Microscope (AFM) imaging. AFM was performed on a Nanoscope III Bioscope (Digital Instruments, Inc.) in tapping mode. Budget Sensors-Tap 300AL-G cantilevers with a tip radius of curvature < 10 nm, length of 125 μm, width of 30 μm, thickness of 4 μm, and a 40 N/m force constant, were employed. Images were collected at 256 × 256 resolution and 1 Hz over a range of scan sizes and scan angles.

iii) Spiral bioassay: To investigate antagonism as a function of distance and timing of colony establishment (reporting metabolite diffusion), PcO6 was first inoculated at the center of LB or MM (1% agar) plates at 22 °C. After 48 h of growth, 10 µl droplets of JunSE1L were placed at defined radial distances of 1 ± 0.1 cm, 1.2 ± 0.0 cm, 2.1 ± 0.2 cm, 3.1 ± 0.3 cm, and 3.8 ± 0.2 cm from the PcO6 colony. Growth was evaluated visually 3 days following challenge inoculation. The apparent diffusion coefficient of the PcO6-derived inhibitory factor was estimated from the maximum distance over which JunSE1L growth was suppressed on 1% MM agar. Distances were measured from digital images using ImageJ.

Diffusion was approximated using the one-dimensional diffusion equation x2=2Dt [24], where x represents the distance travelled by the inhibitory factor, t is the elapsed time in seconds, and D is the apparent diffusion coefficient. This calculation was used as a simplified estimate and assumes passive diffusion through a homogeneous agar matrix, with the visible inhibition boundary representing the maximum distance reached by the active inhibitory compound. The estimate does not account for compound degradation, adsorption or binding to agar, radial diffusion from the colony, concentration thresholds required for inhibition, or continued metabolite production during incubation. Therefore, the calculated value was interpreted as an apparent diffusion estimate rather than an absolute diffusion coefficient. The estimate was compared with diffusion of the reference dye crystal violet (ThermoFisher Scientific, Cat. No. 022866.14; MW = 408 g/mol) under the same agar conditions.

PcO6 is known to upregulate the secretion of the siderophore pyoverdine when grown on iron-limited MM [25]. To assess the effects of pyoverdine production on JunSE1L growth, plates were examined under 365 nm UV light to visualize pyoverdine fluorescence. To further test the role of iron availability, the assay was repeated using MM supplemented with 0.5 µM FeCl₃ (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. 157740), allowing evaluation of changes in pyoverdine fluorescence, which is quenched when bound to Fe(III), and JunSE1L growth under iron-replete conditions.

JunSE1L challenged with PcO6 cell-free supernatant

To determine whether PcO6 secretes inhibitory metabolite(s) that suppress JunSE1L in liquid culture, a cell-free PcO6 supernatant was prepared. PcO6 was grown for 2 d in liquid MM, and cultures were centrifuged twice at 4,000 × g for 20 min. The resulting supernatant was passed through a 0.22 μm filter to obtain a sterile filtrate containing low molecular weight metabolites, proteins, and outer membrane vesicles, the latter have diameters below 110 nm [26]. This filtrate was added to fresh MM at final concentrations of 10% and 20% (v/v). Control treatments consisted of MM diluted with equivalent volumes of sterile water, as well as undiluted MM. Aliquots (1 ml) of each treatment were dispensed aseptically into wells of a 24-well plate (Tissue culture plate, MarathonLS). Wells were inoculated with 50 µl of 0.5 OD₆₀₀ JunSE1L, and growth was monitored for 24 h. OD₆₀₀ was recorded every 15 min using a Synergy HTX multi-mode reader, with 15 s of shaking before each measurement.

Growth curve data were analyzed using both model-based and empirical approaches. Lag phase duration (λ) was estimated by fitting the Baranyi growth model [27] using the growth rates package in R. Total growth was quantified as the area under the curve (AUC) using the growthcurver package [28]. To assess whether JunSE1L secretes metabolites that influence PcO6 growth, a JunSE1L cell-free supernatant was prepared using the same protocol, with the modification that JunSE1L cultures were grown in MM for 3 days before supernatant collection. The resulting supernatant was then used in corresponding PcO6 challenge assays. Surface tension of the cell-free JunSE1L supernatant was tested using a wire probe tensiometer (Kibron Instruments, Malminkaari, Helsinki, Finland) calibrated against ddH2O. Measurements were performed using a 1 mL sample volume at 22 ˚C.

Stability and biochemical nature of the inhibitory activity

To evaluate the effect of aging on the inhibitory activity of PcO6 extracellular products, cell-free supernatants were prepared from PcO6 cultures grown in MM broth for 48 h. Following filtration, aliquots of the supernatant were stored at 4 °C in sterile conditions for extended periods. Fresh supernatant (24 h old) and aged supernatant (stored for up to 108 d) were compared for inhibitory activity against JunSE1L. For growth inhibition assays, 50 µl of 0.5 OD₆₀₀ JunSE1L was grown in MM containing 10% or 20% (v/v) of fresh or aged supernatant (or equivalent volumes of distilled water for controls), and growth was monitored by measuring OD₆₀₀ over time.

To assess whether the inhibitory activity of PcO6 supernatant against JunSE1L is proteinaceous, freshly prepared PcO6 cell-free supernatant was treated with Proteinase K [29]. Proteinase K (lyophilized, EDM Milipore Corp., Cat. No. 70663-4) was dissolved to final working concentrations of 20 µg/mL. For treatment, 0.5 mL of Proteinase K solution was added to 4.5 mL of PcO6 supernatant and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h to allow proteolytic digestion. Following incubation, samples were heat-inactivated at 100 °C for 10 min to denature Proteinase K and then cooled to room temperature. Treated supernatants were tested for inhibitory activity against JunSE1L using a 24-well plate assay. Control treatments included (i) PcO6 supernatant subjected to identical heat treatment without Proteinase K and (ii) Proteinase K alone processed identically to confirm that the enzyme itself did not contribute to inhibition.

Size fractionation of PcO6 supernatant

To further resolve the molecular size of inhibitory compound(s), PcO6 cell-free supernatant was fractionated using centrifugal ultrafiltration devices (Millipore Amicon Ultra Centrifugal Filter) with molecular weight cutoffs of 50 kDa, 10 kDa, and 3 kDa. Sterile-filtered supernatant was first centrifuged through a 50 kDa filter at 4000 × g for 10 min, and the filtrate (< 50 kDa fraction) was collected. This filtrate was subsequently passed through a 10 kDa filter to generate < 10 kDa filtrate and 10–50 kDa retentate fractions. The < 10 kDa filtrate was further processed using a 3 kDa filter to obtain < 3 kDa filtrate and 3–10 kDa retentate fractions. Retentate fractions were reconstituted to the original volume of 15 ml using fresh MM. Each fraction was tested for inhibitory activity against JunSE1L using the same 24-well plate assay, with 200 µL of fraction added per well in place of whole supernatant. Statistical analyses were performed in R. Differences in lag phase and AUC across treatments were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s HSD test for multiple comparisons. Estimated marginal means and compact letter displays were generated to visualize significant differences among treatments. Statistical significance was defined at p < 0.05.

UV-Visible spectroscopy of PcO6 supernatant fractions

To further characterize the chemical nature of metabolites present in PcO6 supernatant, UV-visible spectroscopy and HPLC analyses were performed. Non-concentrated PcO6 supernatant and its molecular weight fractions (> 50 kDa, 50 − 10 kDa, 10 − 3 kDa, and < 3 kDa) were analyzed using a spectrophotometer (Synergy HTX, BioTek). 200 µl of samples were added to 96-well plate (UV flat bottom Microtiter® Plates). Absorbance spectra were recorded from 200 to 700 nm and minimal medium was used as a control. These measurements were used to identify characteristic absorbance features associated with extracellular metabolites and to compare their distribution across fractions.

Ethyl acetate extraction and HPLC analysis of the active < 3 kDa fraction

For chemical enrichment and separation of low-molecular-weight compounds, the < 3 kDa fraction was first acidified to pH 2 using 1 M HCl. The acidified fraction was then subjected to liquid-liquid extraction with ethyl acetate. Equal volumes of < 3 kDa fraction and ethyl acetate were mixed, and the organic phase was collected following phase separation. The solvent was evaporated using a rotary evaporator (Heidolph, Hei-Vap core) at 40 ˚C and 140 RPM, and the dried residue was reconstituted in 200 µl ethyl acetate for HPLC analysis.

The concentrated extracts were analyzed by Agilent 1200 HPLC with an Agilent Eclipse Plus C18 reversed-phase analytical column (5 μm, 4.6 mm× 250 mm). Separation was performed using a water-methanol gradient, increasing from 5% to 95% methanol over 30 min, followed by a 15 min hold at 95% methanol. Elution was monitored using UV detection at 250 and 275 nm. Chromatograms were used to identify dominant peaks corresponding to enriched low-molecular-weight metabolites in the active fraction.

Experimental design, replication, and statistical analysis

All experiments were designed to evaluate interactions between Bacillus atrophaeus JunSE1L and Pseudomonas chlororaphis O6 under defined growth conditions. Unless otherwise stated, experiments were performed using three biological replicates, with three technical replicates included within each biological replicate. Biological replicates represent independent experimental repeats performed using separately prepared bacterial cultures and/or independently established experimental units, whereas technical replicates represent repeated measurements, replicate plates, or replicate wells within the same biological replicate.

For zone of inhibition assays, each treatment condition was evaluated using independent replicate plates. Zone diameters were measured using ImageJ from multiple measurements per inhibition zone, and values are reported as mean ± standard deviation unless otherwise stated. For plant colonization and bacterial recovery assays, surface-sterilized seeds or seedlings were treated as independent experimental units, and bacterial recovery was quantified by plating serial dilutions or recovered colonies on appropriate media. For growth curve assays using cell-free supernatants or size-fractionated samples, treatments were evaluated in multi-well plate assays, and optical density at 600 nm was monitored over time. Growth parameters, including lag phase and/or area under the curve, were calculated from the resulting growth curves.

Statistical analyses were performed using R. Data were analyzed using one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), depending on the experimental design. When significant treatment effects were detected, Tukey’s honestly significant difference test was used for post hoc multiple comparisons. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. Where applicable, statistical groupings are shown using different letters in the figures or supplementary figures. Error bars represent standard deviation unless otherwise indicated. Specific replicate numbers, statistical tests, and post hoc comparisons for individual experiments are provided in the corresponding figure legends and supplementary information.

Results

Interaction between JunSE1L and PcO6 in the presence of the host plant

To examine interactions between the epiphytic root colonizer PcO6 and the seed-borne endophyte JunSE1L in the presence of the host plant, a sand-based wheat growth system was employed, which removes potential interference from organic compounds in soils. PcO6 was introduced into the lower 25 g layer of sterile sand at planting, whereas JunSE1L was expected to originate from the seed tissues based on its consistent recovery from surface-sterilized seeds on growth plates. This spatial separation was designed to examine migration through the soil matrix to reach developing roots.

Representative wheat seedlings prior to harvest are shown in Fig. 1A(i) and A(ii) for the control and PcO6-treated sand, respectively.

After 7 days of growth, roots were harvested and plated onto 2% LB agar and incubated at 22 °C. Roots from control plants grown in uninoculated sand yielded colonies with morphology characteristic of JunSE1L after 3 d of incubation (Fig. 1B(i)), consistent with its origin as a seed-associated endophyte.

In contrast, roots from plants grown in PcO6-inoculated sand yielded exclusively orange-pigmented PcO6 colonies forming a biofilm-like layer on the root surface (Fig. 1B(ii)). This pigmentation is consistent with phenazine production, as phenazine-1-carboxylic acid (PCA) is known to exhibit an orange coloration in Pseudomonas spp [30]. Colonies with JunSE1L morphology were not recovered from these roots, in contrast to the roots plated from seeds grown in sterile sand. No additional colony morphotypes were observed under these conditions.

Together, these results demonstrate that PcO6 efficiently migrates to and colonizes wheat roots and suppresses the recovery of seed-borne JunSE1L from the rhizosphere, as JunSE1L was not recovered from roots grown in PcO6-inoculated sand across biological replicates. Because microbial interactions can be strongly influenced by environmental context, subsequent experiments were conducted under controlled conditions to examine how nutrient availability and temperature affect the interaction between these two strains. Agar-based biofilm assays were therefore performed on both LB and MM.

Zone of inhibition assays on 2% LB and MM

ZOI assays were performed on 2% LB and MM agar to evaluate antagonistic interactions between PcO6 and JunSE1L under biofilm growth conditions. A clear inhibition zone surrounding PcO6 was observed on both media across JunSE1L background densities (Fig. 2A(i), B(i)). However, the inhibition pattern differed by medium. On LB agar, ZOI diameters remained relatively consistent across JunSE1L dilutions, whereas on MM agar, ZOI diameters increased as JunSE1L background density decreased. Statistical analysis confirmed that PcO6-mediated inhibition was significantly greater on MM than on LB at intermediate JunSE1L densities (SI Fig. 2). These results indicate that PcO6-mediated inhibition of JunSE1L is both nutrient-dependent and density-dependent, with enhanced inhibition under nutrient-limited conditions and at lower JunSE1L cell densities. Detailed ZOI measurements and post hoc comparisons are provided in SI Fig. 2.

On LB agar, PcO6 colonies developed distinct orange pigmentation consistent with phenazine production, and pigment diffusion into the surrounding agar was observed (Fig. 2A(i)). In contrast, no visible pigmentation was detected on MM agar. Despite the absence of visible pigmentation on MM, inhibition of JunSE1L was greater on this medium, suggesting that pigmented phenazine are not necessary for inhibition under minimal nutrient conditions.

In the reciprocal assay, where PcO6 formed the background lawn and JunSE1L was inoculated centrally, no ZOI was observed on either LB or MM agar (Fig. 2A(ii), B(ii)), indicating a unidirectional antagonistic interaction under these conditions. However, PcO6 background density and medium composition influenced JunSE1L center colony establishment and morphology. On LB agar, JunSE1L establishment was reduced at high PcO6 background densities, whereas at lower PcO6 densities, colonies became visible and showed a raised, wrinkled morphology characteristic of Bacillus biofilms [31]. On MM agar, JunSE1L showed greater lateral expansion as PcO6 background density decreased. These observations indicate that, although JunSE1L does not inhibit PcO6, PcO6 density and nutrient conditions strongly influence JunSE1L growth and surface expansion.

Side-by-side antagonism and time-staggered inoculation assays

To further examine antagonism without direct contact, PcO6 and JunSE1L were inoculated side-by-side on 1% agar plates. The extent of inhibition was strongly dependent on the growth medium. On LB agar, JunSE1L colonies remained raised and circular, with little visible inhibition by PcO6 (Fig. 3A(i)). By 3 days post-inoculation, PcO6 growth expanded into the JunSE1L colony, accompanied by orange pigmentation of the surface growth. In contrast, on MM agar, JunSE1L colonies adjacent to PcO6 became flattened and spread away from the PcO6 inoculation site within 24 h (Fig. 3B(i)). By 48 h, a pronounced halo surrounding both colonies was observed.

(Aii) Pre-growth of PcO6 for 48 h on LB prior to JunSE1L inoculation resulted in a visible inhibition zone. (Bii) Pre-growth of PcO6 for 48 h on MM completely eliminated growth of the adjacent JunSE1L inoculum. (Aiii, Biii) Pre-growth of JunSE1L for 48 h did not inhibit subsequent PcO6 growth on either LB or MM. (D) Atomic force microscopy (AFM) images of cells recovered from defined interaction zones [1–4] and monoculture controls of PcO6 and JunSE1L

To assess whether inhibitory activity was induced by the presence of a competing colony or whether the inhibitory compounds were constitutively released, PcO6 and JunSE1L inoculations were staggered in time. When PcO6 was pre-grown for 48 h before JunSE1L inoculation, a larger inhibition was observed on LB agar (Fig. 3A(ii)), and JunSE1L growth was completely eliminated on MM agar (Fig. 3B(ii)). In contrast, when JunSE1L was pre-grown for 48 h, PcO6 growth expanded to the edge of the JunSE1L colony on both LB and MM agar without formation of an inhibitory zone (Fig. 3A(iii), B(iii)). Orange pigmentation associated with PcO6 growth on LB agar was observed in all cases, indicating that JunSE1L did not suppress pigmented metabolite production by PcO6.

When the zone-of-inhibition assay on LB agar and side-by-side sequential inoculation assays on both LB and MM were performed at 37 °C, no inhibitory effect was observed (SI Figs. 3 and 5). At this temperature, PcO6 colonies also failed to develop the characteristic orange pigmentation observed at 22 °C on LB medium. In the reciprocal assay at 37 °C, PcO6 growth remained unaffected by JunSE1L, and no inhibition was detected (SI Fig. 3B(i)). These results indicate that both inhibitory activity and pigment production by PcO6 are temperature-dependent, further supporting a unidirectional antagonistic interaction under the tested conditions.

Recovery and identification of cells from interaction zones

To determine whether PcO6 cells were present within apparent zones of inhibition, material was scraped from defined regions of MM 1% agar plates 3 d after side-by-side inoculation (Fig. 3C). When streaked onto LB agar, PcO6 colonies were recovered from all sampled regions, including within the apparent inhibition zone between the two inocula (site 2), the PcO6 colony (site 3), and the JunSE1L inoculation site (site 1). Colonies with JunSE1L were recovered only from site 1. This observation is consistent with, but does not demonstrate, the swarming behavior of PcO6 in the presence of JunSE1L.

Cell morphology was further examined using atomic force microscopy (AFM). PcO6 cells exhibited elongated rod-shaped morphology with stippled surface features, whereas JunSE1L cells appeared block-like and smoother (Fig. 3D, controls). AFM imaging of cells recovered from co-inoculated plates revealed PcO6-like cells at sites corresponding to the PcO6 colony and surrounding halo regions, whereas JunSE1L-like cells were confined to the original JunSE1L inoculation site. Cells collected from the apparent clearing zone are observed to be PcO6 cells. These findings support the results from studies presented in Fig. 3C, based on culturing where colonies of JunSE1L were present at its inoculation site, but unlike PcO6 they had not spread over the entire colonized area on the MM 1% agar plates.

Distance-dependent inhibition and role of siderophore production

To evaluate distance-dependent inhibition, a spiral assay was performed in which JunSE1L was inoculated at increasing distances from a central PcO6 colony pre-grown for 48 h on 1% MM agar. JunSE1L growth was eliminated at the closest inoculation sites (sites 1 and 2) and reduced at an intermediate distance (site 3), while growth at more distal sites was unaffected (Fig. 4A(i)). Under UV (365 nm) illumination, a fluorescent halo surrounding the PcO6 colony was observed on MM agar, consistent with production of a pyoverdine-like siderophore (Fig. 4A(ii)). Supplementation of MM agar with 0.5 µM FeCl₃ suppressed fluorescence, indicating quenching of siderophore fluorescence by FeCl3 (Fig. 4B(i, ii)). However, the addition of iron did not restore JunSE1L growth at sites proximal to the PcO6 colony, demonstrating that iron sequestration alone does not account for the observed inhibition.

JunSE1L was inoculated at five increasing distances from a PcO6 colony pre-grown for 48 h at the center of MM agar plates. (Ai) Growth on MM 1% agar under visible light. (Aii) Growth under UV illumination (365 nm), showing a fluorescent halo around PcO6 indicative of pyoverdine-type siderophore production. JunSE1L growth was eliminated at sites 1 and 2, reduced at site 3, and unaffected at sites 4 and 5. (B) Growth on MM agar supplemented with 0.5 µM FeCl₃, eliminated siderophore-associated fluorescence but did not restore JunSE1L growth at sites 1 and 2, indicating inhibition independent of iron sequestration

Based on the maximum distance over which JunSE1L growth was inhibited and the incubation time, the apparent diffusion coefficient (D) of the inhibitory factor in 1% MM agar was estimated using the relation D = x² / 2t. This analysis yielded a diffusion coefficient of approximately 4.6 × 10⁻⁶ cm² s⁻¹ (Supplementary methods 1).

Inhibition of JunSE1L growth by extracellular products from PcO6

To determine whether PcO6 secretes diffusible inhibitory factors, cell-free supernatant from PcO6 cultures grown in MM for 48 h was added to JunSE1L cultures at final concentrations of 10% (p10) and 20% (p20), v/v. Growth was monitored over time and analyzed using lag phase (Baranyi model) and AUC.

Exposure to PcO6 supernatant delayed JunSE1L growth in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 5A). The addition of 10% supernatant delayed growth onset by ~ 7–8 h, after which growth proceeded at a rate similar to the control. In contrast, 20% supernatant delayed growth by ~ 13 h and reduced the growth rate throughout the experiment. Replating after 24 h confirmed that cells remained viable.

Inhibition of JunSE1L growth by extracellular products from PcO6 and promotion of growth by extracellular products from JunSE1L. (A) Growth curves of JunSE1L in MM broth supplemented with 10% or 20% PcO6 cell-free supernatant compared with MM-only and distilled water controls. (B) Growth of PcO6 in MM broth supplemented with 10% or 20% JunSE1L cell-free supernatant. (C) Comparison of JunSE1L growth inhibition by fresh (24 h) versus aged (108 d) PcO6 supernatant. (D) Effect of molecular weight-fractionated PcO6 supernatant on JunSE1L growth. Supernatant fractions were obtained using sequential ultrafiltration (< 50 kDa, 10–50 kDa, 3–10 kDa, and < 3 kDa), and inhibitory activity was assessed using the same growth assay conditions. Inhibitory activity was primarily associated with <3 kDa fractions

Lag phase (λ) was similar across control treatments (~ 5.2–5.7 h) but increased significantly in the presence of PcO6 supernatant to 9.13 ± 0.30 h (p10) and 14.9 ± 0.22 h (p20) (p < 0.001; ANOVA; SI Figure S8). Total growth, measured as AUC, also decreased significantly with supernatant addition (p < 0.001), from 3.74 ± 0.12 OD·h in MM controls to 2.82 ± 0.11 OD·h (p10) and 1.42 ± 0.18 OD·h (p20). Normalized AUC values showed corresponding reductions to 0.76 (p10) and 0.38 (p20), while control treatments remained near 1.0. Tukey’s post hoc analysis confirmed that both treatments differed significantly from controls, with p20 showing the strongest effect.

In the reciprocal assay, PcO6 cultures exposed to 10% or 20% JunSE1L cell-free supernatant exhibited similar or increased growth compared to MM controls (Fig. 5B), indicating that JunSE1L does not produce inhibitory extracellular factors under these conditions. Surface tension measurements of JunSE1L supernatant were ~ 30 mN m⁻¹ compared to ~ 72 mN m⁻¹ for ddH₂O, consistent with the presence of extracellular surfactant activity. Thus, while both bacteria secret extracellular compounds, their inhibitory effects are one sided, with PcO6 suppressing JunSE1L, characterized by delayed growth initiation and reduced total growth yield.

Stability and biochemical nature of the inhibitory activity

To evaluate the stability of the inhibitory activity, PcO6 supernatant stored at 4 °C for extended periods was tested against JunSE1L. Aged PcO6 supernatant (stored for 108 d) caused stronger inhibition of JunSE1L growth than freshly prepared (24 h) supernatant (Fig. 5C), indicating that the inhibitory activity is stable over time and may increase in effectiveness upon storage.

Treatment of PcO6 supernatant with Proteinase K did not eliminate or reduce its inhibitory effect on JunSE1L growth compared to untreated PcO6 supernatant (SI Fig. 9). Similarly, heat-treated PcO6 supernatant retained inhibitory activity comparable to both untreated and Proteinase K-treated samples. Proteinase K alone did not inhibit JunSE1L growth, confirming that the observed inhibition was attributable to components present in the PcO6 supernatant.

Size fractionation of PcO6 supernatant

To further resolve the molecular basis of inhibition, PcO6 supernatant was fractionated using molecular weight cutoffs. In agreement with diffusion data, inhibitory activity against JunSE1L was observed in the < 3 kDa fraction, while higher molecular weight fractions exhibited no inhibitory effects (Fig. 5D). These results indicate that the inhibitory compound(s) are likely small, diffusible molecules rather than high-molecular-weight proteins or vesicle-associated components.

UV-Visible spectroscopy, ethyl acetate extraction and HPLC analysis of the active < 3 kDa fraction of PcO6 supernatant

UV-visible spectra of PcO6 supernatant and its size-fractionated samples (SI Fig. 10A) revealed a distinct absorbance peak in the ~ 350–400 nm region in the non-fractionated supernatant, which progressively decreased in intensity upon fractionation but remained detectable in the 10 − 3 kDa and < 3 kDa fractions, while absent in the minimal medium control. However, only the < 3 kDa fraction exhibited inhibitory activity against JunSE1L, whereas the 10 − 3 kDa fraction did not. These results indicate that the compound responsible for the ~ 350–400 nm absorbance is not the primary inhibitory agent. This spectral feature overlaps with phenazine-like metabolites, which exhibit characteristic absorbance maxima in the ~ 360–380 nm range due to their conjugated aromatic structure [30, 32, 33].

Reverse-phase HPLC analysis of the ethyl acetate-concentrated < 3 kDa fraction (SI Fig. 10B) showed a prominent late-eluting signal at approximately 31–32 min under high methanol conditions. This region appeared as a broad dominant peak at 250 nm and as two closely spaced peaks at 275 nm, indicating the presence of one or more relatively hydrophobic compounds in the active fraction. However, inhibitory activity was not assigned to this HPLC signal in the present study. Therefore, the chromatographic identity of the active compound remains unresolved. Taken together, these results demonstrate that PcO6-mediated inhibition of JunSE1L is driven by a heat-stable, non-proteinaceous < 3 kDa compound that is distinct from the phenazine-like metabolites detected by UV-visible spectroscopy. HPLC analysis identified a late-eluting feature in the ethyl acetate-concentrated active fraction, but further activity-guided purification would be needed to determine whether this feature corresponds to the inhibitory compound.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that interactions between the wheat seed endophyte Bacillus atrophaeus JunSE1L and the epiphytic rhizoplane colonizer Pseudomonas chlororaphis O6 are environmentally dependent and directionally unequal. Specifically, inhibition was observed only in the direction of PcO6 against JunSE1L, while JunSE1L did not inhibit PcO6 under the tested conditions – to the contrary, it appeared to promote PcO6 mobility on agar plates, and JunSE1L supernatant reduced the lag time for PcO6 growth in MM broth. Across plant-associated assays, agar antagonism experiments, and liquid culture supernatant assays, PcO6 suppressed JunSE1L growth and spatial expansion. The inhibitory effect of PcO6 on JunSE1L varied with nutrient availability, temperature, and population density, indicating that the outcome of this interaction is context-dependent.

PcO6 dominance in plant-associated environments

In the sterile sand wheat system, PcO6 dominated the culturable root-associated bacterial population when present, whereas JunSE1L was recovered only in its absence. Because PcO6 was introduced into the lower sand layer, successful colonization required migration through the sand matrix, suggesting strong rhizoplane colonization ability. This competitive dominance is consistent with reports that Pseudomonas species outcompete Bacillus through rapid surface colonization, metabolic flexibility, and antimicrobial metabolite production [32, 33].

Environmental regulation of antagonistic interactions

Agar-based assays showed that PcO6-mediated inhibition is strongly influenced by nutrient conditions and JunSE1L density. Quantitative ZOI analysis confirmed that inhibition was significantly greater in minimal medium than in LB and increased as JunSE1L density decreased. These results indicate that both nutrient availability and target population density regulate the strength of antagonism, in agreement with observations of no endophyte emerging from PcO6-colonized roots.

Distance-dependent inhibition supports the involvement of diffusible metabolites, with diffusion coefficients consistent with small secondary metabolites in agar [34–36]. The absence of inhibition at elevated temperature further suggests that production or activity of the inhibitory compound is environmentally regulated. The loss of inhibition at 37 °C, together with the absence of orange phenazine-associated pigmentation, suggests that elevated temperature may alter PcO6 secondary metabolite production and reduce its antagonistic activity against JunSE1L. However, it is also possible that growth at 37 °C induces physiological changes in JunSE1L that allow it to better tolerate or overcome PcO6-mediated inhibition.

Growth kinetics and quantitative inhibition analysis

Liquid culture assays provided quantitative evidence for inhibition. Growth curve modeling and AUC analysis showed that PcO6 supernatant caused a concentration-dependent delay in JunSE1L growth and reduced total biomass. Lag phase increased substantially, and normalized AUC decreased by up to ~ 60%, indicating strong suppression of growth.

Importantly, inhibition was reversible, indicating a bacteriostatic rather than bactericidal effect. Such strategies may allow dominant species to restrict competitor expansion while minimizing metabolic cost [37, 38]. Taken together, the antagonism assays in solid and liquid growth media demonstrate PcO6 inhibition against JunSE1L in planktonic and biofilm forms.

Chemical nature of the inhibitory compound

Multiple lines of evidence constrain the identity of the inhibitory compound. Activity persisted after heat treatment and Proteinase K digestion, suggesting that the inhibitory activity is unlikely to be primarily from proteins or peptides under the conditions tested. Fractionation localized activity to the < 3 kDa fraction, supporting the involvement of a small diffusible metabolite. Although PcO6 is known to produce phenazines, siderophores, hydrogen cyanide, and other secondary metabolites [25, 39–40], the inhibition pattern observed here suggests that these common candidates are unlikely to fully explain the activity. Phenazine-associated pigmentation was most evident under nutrient-rich conditions, yet inhibition was strongest under minimal medium conditions. In addition, UV-visible absorbance in the ~ 350–400 nm range, consistent with conjugated aromatic compounds including phenazine-like metabolites [41, 42], was also detected in inactive fractions, indicating that this spectral feature is unlikely to represent the primary inhibitory agent. Similarly, iron supplementation did not restore JunSE1L growth, suggesting that siderophore-mediated iron sequestration by PcO6 was not the main mechanism [43]. In addition, hydrogen cyanide is less likely to be the primary inhibitory factor because inhibitory activity persisted after supernatant processing, heat treatment, and fractionation.

Reverse-phase HPLC analysis of the ethyl acetate-concentrated < 3 kDa fraction revealed a dominant late-eluting peak (~ 31–32 min), suggesting that one or more relatively hydrophobic small molecules were present in the active fraction. However, the inhibitory activity was not directly assigned to this peak in the present study. Therefore, while a hydrophobic metabolite may contribute to PcO6-mediated inhibition of JunSE1L, the chemical identity and chromatographic origin of the active compound remain unresolved. Small stable antibiotics produced by Pseudomonas species, such as pyrrolnitrin, remain possible candidates [44, 45], although the compound was not directly identified in this study.

Limitations and ecological implications

A key limitation is that the inhibitory compound was not chemically identified. While the present data constrain its properties, definitive identification will require targeted fractionation (e.g., HPLC), verification of activity in collected fractions, and subsequent structural characterization using mass spectrometry and/or NMR, along with complementary genetic analysis. In addition, experiments were conducted in a simplified sterile sand system, which does not capture the complexity of natural soil microbiomes. Despite these limitations, the results support a model in which PcO6 suppresses a seed-derived endophyte through the secretion of small diffusible metabolites whose activity depends on environmental conditions. Such interactions likely influence microbial community assembly during the transition from seed-associated to root-associated microbiomes and have implications for designing compatible microbial inoculants.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that interactions between Bacillus atrophaeus JunSE1L and Pseudomonas chlororaphis O6 are strongly asymmetric and shaped by environmental conditions. PcO6 consistently suppresses JunSE1L growth, whereas JunSE1L does not inhibit PcO6 and may even enhance its surface mobility. Quantitative analyses further revealed that this inhibition is concentration-dependent and influenced by nutrient availability, and temperature.

PcO6 extracellular products delayed growth initiation and reduced total biomass of JunSE1L, indicating a bacteriostatic mode of action of this root colonizing epiphyte against the predominant endophyte. The inhibitory activity was recalcitrant to heat denaturation and proteinase digestion and associated with low-molecular-weight fractions (< 3 kDa), consistent with a small diffusible metabolite. Together, these findings show that compatibility between predominant communities in the plant, microbiome cannot be assumed and must be evaluated under relevant environmental conditions. This is particularly important for rhizosphere engineering and microbial inoculant design, where co-applied or naturally co-occurring strains may interact differently depending on nutrient availability, temperature, and population density.
