Unstable trunk core strength training improves shooting performance and holding stability in elite 10 m air pistol athletes: a pilot randomized controlled trial
Authors: Rangxi Jin, Zhen Niu, Chao Chen, Mitchell James Finlay, Francisco Cuenca-Fernández, Yijie Ma, Yuming Zhong, Xianfu Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhile trunk strength training is well established in marksmanship sports, it is not known whether unstable or stable training regimes are more effective in air pistol athletes. The aim of this study was to investigate whether unstable trunk core strength training (UTCST) elicited greater improvements than stable training (STCST) in postural and gun-holding stability in national-level air pistol shooters. Twenty national-level shooters were randomly assigned to UTCST (n = 10; Swiss balls, BOSU balls, balance discs) or STCST (n = 10; stable surface) for 8 weeks. Outcome measures were center-of-pressure (COP) parameters (plantar pressure analysis) and shooting performance parameters (SCATT laser system) recorded 1-second prior to trigger. UTCST showed greater improvements than STCST in COP sway area (-11.03% vs. -2.02%, p < 0.05, η2p=0.22), COP path/area ratio (+ 9.55% vs. + 3.61%, p<0.05, η2p = 0.22), shooting scores (+ 2.46% vs. + 1.19%, p<0.05, η2p=0.20), 10-ring stability (+ 10.47% vs. + 6.51%, p < 0.05, η2p=0.25), 10.5-ring stability (+ 31.55% vs. + 6.48%, p<0.05, η2p=0.56), muzzle oscillation control (-8.74% vs. -4.05%, p < 0.05, η2p=0.39), and aim-trigger deviation (-25.73% vs. -10.1%, p<0.05, η2p=0.22). After FDR correction, only 10.5-ring stability and muzzle velocity remained significant; the other outcomes are interpreted as exploratory. Conclusion. In this sample of national-level air pistol athletes, UTCST appeared to produce greater improvements than STCST in both postural control and shooting precision, which may reflect enhanced neuromuscular coordination. Given the small, pilot sample, these preliminary findings require confirmation in larger, prospectively registered trials; nonetheless, they provide preliminary support for incorporating unstable-surface training into shooting-specific conditioning programs.Keywords: Proprioceptive training, Swiss ball, Postural sway, Center of pressure, Precision sport, Neuromuscular controlSubject terms: Health care, Medical research, Physiology

Introduction

The trunk serves as the central pillar and functional core of the human body, playing a pivotal role in postural stability and the transmission of energy along the kinetic chain1.To begin with, the transmission mechanism of the kinetic chain shows that stability of limb movement greatly relies on the trunk, as momentum begins in the trunk and progresses sequentially to the extremities via coordinated neuromuscular activity2. Second, its loading and stabilization mechanism emphasizes the function of the spine to transmit compressive loads, to take up intervertebral disc loading, and to provide stability via ligament–muscle synergy3. Trunk core strength training can improve the activation and intermuscular coordination of the deep local stabilizers (e.g., transversus abdominis and multifidus) and reduce the excessive dominance or compensatory activity of global synergists such as the erector spinae and quadratus lumborum, thereby minimizing aberrant intervertebral disc and joint loading and dynamic spinal instability4–6. Nonetheless, this form of transfer of training to sporting performance is controversial as too much instability does not actually mimic real competitive loads and may even detract from maximal strength or power development. For example, as the external task becomes progressively more unstable, the contribution of the stabilizing musculature increases while the output of the prime movers decreases, so that force, velocity, and power production can be reduced—partly through greater antagonist co-activation and joint stiffening7. Thus, unstable surface training should be viewed as a complementary, rather than primary method8,9. Furthermore, the neuromuscular coordination mechanism suggests that unstable training recruits both superficial mobilizing muscles and deep stabilizing muscles, thereby achieving more comprehensive functional activation10. This enhanced activation may reduce local muscle fatigue9,11,12, thereby helping to meet the demands for prolonged postural maintenance and endurance in air pistol shooting13–15.

The 10 m air pistol is a discipline characterized by “seeking stability in stillness,” in which shooting accuracy depends on the coordinated control of body stability and gun-holding stability16. Body stability is achieved by reducing center-of-mass displacement through spine–pelvis stiffness17. Studies have shown that the co-activation of the transversus abdominis and multifidus enhances lumbar stiffness and decreases body sway, while unstable plank exercises can significantly increase the electromyographic amplitude of the transversus abdominis18. Conversely, weapon-holding stability depends on accurate transmission of the torque from the shoulder to the wrist joint. Neural feedback optimizes control of rotator cuff muscle tension and thus reduces the muzzle sway velocity19. Body stability is a static foundation for weapon-holding stability, and weapon-holding stability dynamically controls trunk posture by feedback mechanisms. Both have been found to be empirically associated very strongly with shooting performance and hence there is a need to study certain training procedures for their optimization20.

While stability is the prime cause of success in shooting, modern methods of training still emphasize technical correctness and power generation on a stable platform. But such stable training cannot mimic small perturbations caused by respiration, heart rate, or external disturbances and thus lead to poor interference-resistance ability under competition conditions21–23. Further, routine training focuses mainly on superficial muscles (e.g., rectus abdominis, external oblique), but lack of activation of deep stabilizing muscles restricts the capacity to achieve the dynamic control in static postures needed in shooting sports24–26. In parallel with this, systematic protocols of unstable trunk training do not exist, so that air pistol competitors’ unique stability requirements have yet to be directly answered. The subject is also of interest within sports medicine, where writers have commented on the fact that unstable training might more accurately replicate the nature of the dynamic disturbance encountered under competition and enable adaptation at the neural level27.

In the condition of static posture, sport performers must contend with dynamic disturbances while shooting 10 m air pistol. Training with unstable trunks “may” be an effective simulation of this condition and improves the capacity of the trunk to make online postural corrections15. By progressively increasing the degree of instability, performers’ adaptive limits may be systematically raised. In contrast to stable training, its benefit is in developing the composite quality of “strength–endurance” through neural adaptation instead of muscle hypertrophy only that is best matched with the particular demands of the shooting performance. However, current research has not clearly distinguished between stable and unstable trunk training, nor has it systematically verified their intervention effects in shooting disciplines.

Despite growing interest in unstable surface training for shooting sports, no study has systematically compared stable versus unstable trunk core strength training using sport-specific biomechanical measures in national-level pistol athletes. Therefore, the primary purpose of this study was to examine the comparative effects of an 8-week unstable versus stable trunk core strength training program on postural control and shooting performance indicators in national-level 10 m air pistol shooters.

Based on previous evidence that unstable surfaces may increase proprioceptive demand and preferentially recruit deep trunk stabilizers9,28, we hypothesized that: (1) both training groups would show significant pre-to-post improvements in postural and gun-holding stability; (2) the unstable training group would demonstrate significantly greater improvements than the stable training group in COP-derived postural control metrics, shooting score, aiming stability (10-ring and 10.5-ring), muzzle control, and aim-to-trigger deviation; and (3) no between-group difference would be observed for aiming time, given its dependence on sport-specific technical habits rather than physical conditioning alone.

Materials and methods

Study design

The present study adopted a randomized controlled experimental design to investigate the effects of unstable trunk core strength training on physical stability and gun-holding stability in 10 m air pistol athletes. Twenty national-level athletes were recruited and randomly allocated into an Unstable Trunk Core Strength Training Group (UTCST) (n = 10) and a Stable Trunk Core Strength Training Group STCST (n = 10). For terminological consistency, the experimental and control interventions are referred to throughout as UTCST and STCST, respectively. All variant descriptors that appear in the text—for example, “unstable/stable trunk training,” “unstable/stable core strength training,” “unstable core training,” and “unstable core strength training group”—denote these same two study protocols (the unstable forms corresponding to UTCST and the stable forms to STCST). The generic terms “unstable surface training” and “stable surface training” are used only when referring to the training method in a general sense (e.g., in prior literature or mechanistic discussion), not to the two groups of the present study. The UTCST performed an 8-week program of unstable trunk core strength training, while the STCST performed a stable-surface program that was matched for training frequency, session volume, number of sets and repetitions, rest intervals, and load progression, as well as the accompanying technical shooting practice; the two programs differed only in the stability of the supporting surface (unstable devices—Swiss balls, BOSU balls, and balance discs—vs. a stable surface). No participants dropped out; all 20 randomized athletes completed the 8-week intervention and were included in the analysis (Fig. 1).

CONSORT flow diagram of participant progression through the trial.

To evaluate the intervention effects, a combination of biomechanical and performance-based assessments was employed, consistent with established methodologies in shooting sports research13,20. Body stability was quantified using a Footscan plantar pressure testing system by analyzing two center-of-pressure (COP) parameters—sway area and trajectory length per unit area—recorded during the critical 1-second period prior to triggering. Concurrently, gun-holding stability was assessed with a SCATT laser training system, which provided objective measures of shooting scores, aiming time, stability within the 10-ring and 10.5-ring, muzzle wobble speed, and aiming-trigger coordination.

Participants

Twenty male national-level 10 m air pistol athletes from China were invited to participate. The experiment was conducted at the Shooting and Archery Administration Center of the Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region. No recent injury or neuromuscular disorder that could affect intervention or test procedures was reported by each athlete. Prior to the intervention, all athletes had been following the same routine technical and physical conditioning program of their respective teams. Before their involvement, the study procedures, training protocols, and risk were fully explained to all the participants. Written informed consent was initially provided by all participants prior to data collection.

Required sample size was calculated with the use of G*Power 3.1 software. Because no previous trial had employed the same sport-specific outcomes and 2 × 2 repeated-measures design, the effect size was set a priori at f = 0.40, corresponding to Cohen’s benchmark for a large effect29, with α = 0.05 and statistical power (1 − β) = 0.80. Statistical method that was used is repeated-measures ANOVA with two groups and two measurement times. The estimate calculated that a minimum sample size of n = 16 (8 per treatment) was needed. In order to account for potential dropout during the intervention, 20 athletes were eventually recruited to ensure stability and reliability of the outcome. The a priori effect size (f = 0.40) reflected a general large-effect convention rather than data from directly comparable pistol-shooting trials, as no prior study had used the same sport-specific outcomes and 2 × 2 design. Together with the small sample and the number of outcomes analyzed, this means the trial should be regarded as exploratory.

Participants were randomly allocated to UTCST (n = 10) or STCST (n = 10) using a computer-generated random sequence with permuted blocks (block size = 4) to ensure balanced group sizes. The sequence was generated by a researcher not involved in recruitment, intervention delivery, or outcome assessment. Allocation was concealed using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes opened only after baseline testing. For the UTCST, the mean height was 171.8 ± 2.35 cm, mean body weight was 68.65 ± 14.64 kg, mean age was 18.8 ± 3.77 years, and mean training experience was 10.0 ± 2.63 years. In the STCST, the mean height was 170.1 ± 5.90 cm, body weight was 65.85 ± 8.48 kg, age was 19.0 ± 3.30 years, and experience in training was 10.0 ± 2.15 years. No statistical analysis differences were found between groups in these baseline values (p > 0.05), which confirmed group homogeneity and comparability. This research was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai University of Sport (Approval No: 102772024RT087) and was performed under Declaration of Helsinki principles, The clinical trial information for this study was registered retrospectively (i.e., after the actual start of the study on August 4, 2024). Registration was completed on April 6, 2026, on ClinicalTrials.gov (a WHO Primary Registry), with the registration number NCT07526376. The authors confirm that the study protocol, outcome measures, and analytical methods remain consistent with the registered information, and no substantive changes were made after study initiation.

Development of the training programs

The training programs for both the unstable and stable groups were developed using a two-round Delphi method, a structured expert consensus technique widely used in sports training research30. A panel of 10 experts was invited, comprising five national-level shooting coaches (each with ≥ 10 years of experience and an ISSF coaching license), three sports scientists specializing in strength and conditioning, and two biomechanists with publications in shooting research. In the first round, experts rated the appropriateness of 32 candidate exercises for air pistol shooters using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = very inappropriate, 5 = very appropriate) and provided open-ended comments. Exercises with a mean score ≥ 4.0 were retained; those below this threshold were removed or modified based on expert feedback. In the second round, the revised program was re-evaluated, and all 10 experts confirmed the final exercise selection without further modifications. Training intervention program for the STCST.

Training loads (sets, repetitions, rest intervals) and resistance levels were determined by the research team based on established principles of core strengthening and neuromuscular adaptation15,28 and were reviewed and approved by the expert panel during the second Delphi round. For both groups, weeks 1–4 focused on muscular endurance and movement pattern acquisition (2–3 sets of 15–20 repetitions), while weeks 5–8 emphasized progressive overload and increased neural demand (4–5 sets of 10–15 repetitions). Resistance band tension (13–35 lbs) and dumbbell loads (5–10 kg) were individualised based on each athlete’s baseline strength level and progressed when the athlete could complete the target repetitions with proper form. A 30-second rest was given between sets of the same muscle group, and a 1-minute rest between different muscle groups, which is consistent with typical rest intervals for muscular endurance and neuromuscular control training31. Both programs were delivered over 8 weeks under the supervision of ISSF-licensed coaches from the expert panel. Exercise technique was checked each session, and an exercise was progressed only after the athlete completed the target repetitions with correct form. All athletes completed all scheduled sessions, and no protocol deviations occurred. No adverse events occurred during the intervention. Specifically, no falls or loss of balance during unstable-surface exercises, no musculoskeletal injuries or pain (shoulder, lumbar, or hip), and no training-related overload symptoms were reported in either group.

Test arrangement

All outcome measures were assessed at two time points: one week before (baseline) and immediately after the 8-week training intervention, using identical testing protocols and equipment. For each testing session, athletes completed a standardized 15-minute warm-up consisting of mobility exercises, shooting-specific positioning drills, and breathing exercises. Following the warm-up and 30 min of trial shots, athletes performed the official testing protocol in accordance with ISSF rules for the 10 m air pistol final. The test consisted of two series of 5 scoring shots (5 + 5 shots), followed by 14 single scoring shots, totaling 24 shots with an aggregate score of 261.6 points. Beginning with the 12th shot, one finalist would normally be eliminated after every two shots; however, for research purposes, no eliminations were conducted. Consistent with the nature of the intervention, participants and coaches could not be blinded to group allocation. All outcomes were recorded by objective instruments (SCATT and Footscan); the assessors who operated the equipment and exported the data did so using participant ID codes and were blinded to group allocation, and data were analyzed under group codes (A/B). All tests were conducted in the same facility at the same time of day, with identical equipment settings and a fixed test order (postural test before shooting test). Trials with equipment errors were repeated, and any shot with an incomplete SCATT trace during the final 1 s was discarded.

Gun-holding stability test

Shooting performance and gun-holding stability were assessed using the SCATT optoelectronic training system (SCATT LLC, Moscow, Russia). The device model was the MX-02V2(B) (serial number: SCATT_904FB927, Fig. 2), which records the shooter’s aiming trajectory at a sampling frequency of 120 Hz. Data were processed using SCATT Professional software (version 2023.1). The ballistic coefficient was set to 170 m/s, which is the standard value for 4.5 mm air pistol pellets and consistent with previous research using SCATT systems in air pistol shooting24,25.

SCATT MX-02V2 Laser Tester.

To ensure measurement consistency, the SCATT sensor was mounted on the underside of the pistol barrel using the manufacturer-provided universal mounting bracket. The mounting position was marked with a permanent marker on each athlete’s pistol at the first testing session and reproduced identically at post-test to minimise placement variability. This procedure follows the standardised mounting protocol described in the SCATT user manual and has been adopted in prior shooting research32.

Six shooting-related outcome measures were extracted from the SCATT system for analysis (see Table 3 for full definitions): (1) shooting score, (2) aiming time, (3) percentage of time within the 10-ring, (4) percentage of time within the 10.5-ring, (5) average muzzle velocity during the final 1 s before triggering, and (6) aim-to-trigger deviation.

The selection of these SCATT-derived measures was guided by recent systematic reviews and technical determinant studies in Olympic air pistol and rifle shooting. Specifically, shooting score and aiming time are the most commonly reported performance outcomes in optoelectronic shooting research. Stability within the 10-ring and 10.5-ring has been identified as the strongest discriminator between elite and sub-elite air pistol shooters, explaining approximately 40% of the variance in competition performance24,25. Muzzle velocity during the final 1 s reflects the shooter’s ability to stabilise the weapon immediately before triggering and is highly correlated with aiming point dispersion33. Aim-to-trigger deviation represents the coordination between visual feedback and motor execution, which is a critical determinant of shot accuracy in pistol shooting34. These six indicators were therefore selected to provide a comprehensive yet non-redundant profile of gun-holding stability.

COP-based postural control test

Postural control was assessed using the Footscan® 1.0 m plantar pressure plate (RSscan International, Olen, Belgium) (Fig. 3). Raw data were collected at a sampling frequency of 500 Hz and processed using Footscan software version 9.0. Two complementary COP-derived outcomes were analysed during the final 1 s prior to trigger pull (see Table 3 for full definitions): (1) COP sway area (SA), and (2) COP path length per unit area (R), defined as the ratio of total COP sway path length (SL) to sway area. SL was used to compute R and is not reported as a separate outcome.

Footscan 1.0 m plantar pressure plate.

The selection of these two outcomes was based on their established relevance to precision shooting performance. COP sway area represents the overall magnitude of postural oscillation and has been shown to differentiate performance levels in both rifle and pistol shooting35,36.COP path length per unit area (R), computed as total COP sway path length (SL) divided by sway area, quantifies the frequency–amplitude relationship of postural adjustments; a higher ratio indicates that the shooter is making more frequent, smaller-amplitude corrections, which is characteristic of skilled performance in aiming tasks33,37,38. These two indicators were therefore selected to capture both the magnitude and the regulatory efficiency of postural control during the most demanding phase of the shot cycle.

Statistical analysis

Data were systematically processed and analyzed using Excel and SPSS 27.0 statistical software. The Shapiro–Wilk test was first applied to assess data normality, followed by a test of homogeneity of variances. Baseline data of athletes’ body stability and shooting stability indicators were examined for homogeneity. A repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to explore the main effects of time, group, and their interaction, with subsequent simple effects analyses performed when necessary. All results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (M ± SD). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. For repeated-measures ANOVA, the partial eta-squared (η2p) effect size was reported to accurately reflect the magnitude and practical significance of the observed effects. Because multiple outcomes were analyzed and no primary outcome was prospectively defined, the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure39 was applied to the eight Time × Group interaction p-values (the seven outcomes in Tables 4 and 5 and aiming time in Table 6) as a sensitivity analysis. After correction, the interactions for 10.5-ring stability and muzzle velocity remained significant, whereas the remaining interactions were attenuated to marginal significance (adjusted p ≈ 0.05) and are interpreted as exploratory. Given the retrospective registration, small sample, and multiplicity, all findings are regarded as hypothesis-generating.

Results

A 2 (time: pre, post) × 2 (group: UTCST, STCST) repeated-measures ANOVA was applied to each outcome, with simple-effects analyses following a significant Time × Group interaction. For each outcome, the direction and percentage of change are reported, and FDR-adjusted interaction p-values are given in Tables 4 and 5. Results are summarized in Tables 4, 5 and 6.

Athlete’s COP-based postural control results

COP sway area 1 s before trigger pull

The results revealed a significant interaction effect between time and group (F = 4.96, p = 0.04, η2p = 0.22). A significant main effect of time was also observed (F = 9.62, p = 0.006, η2p = 0.35). No significant difference was found in the tests of between-subjects effects (F = 3.15, p = 0.09, η2p = 0.15). Based on these findings, simple effect analysis was further conducted. The results showed no significant difference between the UTCST and STCST before the intervention (F = 0.001, p = 0.97, η2p = 0.00); however, a significant difference emerged between the two groups after the intervention (F = 9.07, p = 0.007, η2p = 0.34). Additionally, no significant difference was observed in the STCST before and after the intervention (F = 0.38, p = 0.54, η2p = 0.02), whereas a significant difference was found in the UTCST before and after the intervention (F = 14.20, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.44).

The reduction amplitude of the COP sway area one second before trigger pull was 2.02% for the STCST athletes, while the reduction amplitude for the UTCST athletes was 11.03%. (Table 4). The changes in COP sway area for both groups are shown in Fig. 4. This Time × Group interaction did not remain significant after FDR correction (adjusted p ≈ 0.05) and is therefore interpreted as exploratory.

Athlete’s firearm stability results

Shooting score

The analysis revealed a significant interaction effect between time and group (F = 4.59, p = 0.046, η2p = 0.20). A significant main effect of time was also observed (F = 36.47, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.67), indicating that shooting scores changed significantly over time. No significant difference was found in the between-subjects effects test (F = 4.11, p = 0.06, η2p = 0.19).

Discussion

Summary of main findings

The present study compared the effects of an 8-week unstable versus stable trunk core strength training program on postural control and shooting performance40 in national-level 10 m air pistol athletes. The main findings were threefold. First, both training groups demonstrated significant pre-to-post improvements in shooting score, 10-ring stability, and muzzle velocity control, indicating that both stable and unstable trunk training are effective conditioning modalities for this population. Second, the unstable training group (UTCST) showed significantly greater improvements than the stable training group (STCST) in COP sway area (− 11.03% vs. −2.02%), COP path/area ratio (+ 9.55% vs. +3.61%), shooting score (+ 2.46% vs. +1.19%), 10.5-ring stability (+ 31.55% vs. +6.48%), muzzle velocity (− 8.74% vs. −4.05%), and aim-to-trigger deviation (− 25.73% vs. −10.1%). Third, no significant between-group difference was observed for aiming time, suggesting that this variable may be less responsive to short-term physical conditioning interventions and more dependent on sport-specific technical training.

Comparison with previous studies

The superior efficacy of unstable trunk training observed in this study is consistent with previous research examining the effects of instability on postural control and shooting performance41. reported that complex functional strength training on unstable surfaces improved balance and shooting accuracy in rifle shooters, with effect sizes comparable to those found in our UTCST group. Similarly42, demonstrated that balance training on unstable surfaces enhanced shooting precision under loaded conditions, which aligns with our finding of improved 10.5-ring stability and reduced aim-to-trigger deviation. Likewise, core stability training improved both shooting accuracy and balance in petanque athletes, a precision sport with comparable postural demands43.

However, direct comparisons should be made cautiously, as most prior studies have either examined rifle rather than pistol shooting, or have not included a stable-surface training group as a comparator. The present study extends the current literature by directly comparing stable versus unstable protocols using identical exercise selections and training loads, thereby isolating the effect of surface instability per se.

Potential mechanisms underlying the greater improvements with unstable-surface training

This study did not include direct measurements of muscle activation (e.g., electromyography) or neural activity; therefore, the following mechanisms are proposed as plausible explanations based on previous literature and should be verified in future research.

Unstable surfaces (e.g., Swiss ball, BOSU ball, balance disc) reduce the base of support and increase the degree of freedom that the neuromuscular system must control28. This condition is hypothesised to increase proprioceptive demand and require continuous postural micro-adjustments, which may enhance the sensitivity of mechanoreceptors and facilitate reticulospinal pathway adaptation14. In contrast, stable surface training allows the athlete to maintain posture with less active corrective input, which may limit the transfer of postural control gains to sport-specific tasks that involve intrinsic perturbations (e.g., respiration, heartbeat, muscle twitch).

A second plausible mechanism relates to differential recruitment of deep versus superficial trunk muscles. Previous electromyographic studies have shown that unstable plank exercises increase activation of the transversus abdominis and multifidus by up to 27% compared to stable conditions44; a recent meta-analysis likewise found significantly greater activation of the rectus abdominis, obliques, erector spinae, and multifidus on unstable than on stable surfaces45. These deep stabilisers contain a higher proportion of type I slow-twitch fibres and are primarily responsible for tonic postural control rather than phasic force production11; consistent with this, core-muscle fatigue degrades postural stability, whereas trunk-stability training helps restore it46. The preferential activation of these muscles during unstable training may explain the greater reduction in COP sway area and the more efficient COP trajectory pattern (higher path/area ratio) observed in our UTCST group.

Third, the progressive increase in task difficulty inherent in the UTCST protocol (e.g., progressing from bilateral to unilateral support, adding limb movements) may have induced neural adaptations in anticipatory postural control. It has been suggested that exposure to unpredictable perturbations enhances the central nervous system’s ability to generate feedforward postural adjustments prior to voluntary movement47. Because the UTCST exercises involved largely self-generated rather than externally imposed perturbations, this mechanism remains speculative; if operative, it might contribute to better stabilisation of the gun–arm system during the final 1 s before triggering, which would be consistent with the reduced muzzle velocity and aim-to-trigger deviation observed in the UTCST group.

It is important to emphasise that these mechanisms are not mutually exclusive, and the observed improvements are likely attributable to a combination of proprioceptive, muscular, and neural adaptations. Future studies incorporating EMG, transcranial magnetic stimulation, or perturbation protocols are needed to disentangle the relative contributions of each pathway.

Transfer of postural control improvements to shooting performance

A key finding of this study is that superior postural control gains in the UTCST group were accompanied by superior shooting performance gains. The UTCST group improved shooting score by 2.46% (η²p = 0.65), compared to 1.19% (η²p = 0.30) in the STCST group. This transfer effect is consistent with the conceptual framework that postural stability is a prerequisite for distal limb precision in aiming tasks35,36; meta-analytic evidence further indicates that core training transfers to sport-specific performance48. In 10 m air pistol specifically, reduced COP sway has been associated with higher shooting scores49.

The largest between-group difference was observed for 10.5-ring stability (UTCST: +31.55%, STCST: +6.48%, interaction η²p = 0.56). This metric reflects the shooter’s ability to maintain the aiming trajectory within a high-precision zone immediately before triggering and has been identified as the strongest technical discriminator between elite and sub-elite air pistol shooters16,24. The substantial improvement in the UTCST group suggests that unstable trunk training may specifically enhance the shooter’s capacity for fine motor control under high precision demands. Notably, together with muzzle velocity, the 10.5-ring interaction was one of the two effects that remained significant after FDR correction, whereas the other between-group differences were attenuated to marginal significance and should be regarded as exploratory.

Similarly, the 25.73% reduction in aim-to-trigger deviation observed in the UTCST group (vs. 10.1% non-significant reduction in STCST) indicates improved coordination between visual feedback and trigger execution. While this study did not directly measure trigger control, it is plausible that enhanced proximal stability (trunk and shoulder girdle) reduced the degree of compensatory corrections required at the distal segment (hand and finger), thereby facilitating a smoother and more consistent trigger pull34,50.

Aiming time: a non-responsive variable

In contrast to several previous studies that reported reduced aiming time following balance or core training interventions41, no significant changes in aiming time were observed in either group, and the Time × Group interaction was non-significant (p = 0.52, η²p = 0.02).

One possible explanation is that aiming time is a highly stabilised technical habit in national-level shooters, developed over years of sport-specific training (mean training experience of our sample: 10 years). Short-term (8-week) physical conditioning interventions may be insufficient to modify this behaviour, even if postural control and weapon handling efficiency have improved. This interpretation is supported by the observation that both groups improved shooting performance without reducing aiming time, suggesting that they achieved greater precision within the same temporal window.

An alternative, non-mutually-exclusive explanation is that SCATT-derived aiming time may be influenced by arbitrary threshold settings (e.g., when the trajectory enters the defined capture zone), which may not fully capture the shooter’s preparatory phase51. Future studies should consider using cluster analysis or event-defined algorithms to differentiate between functional and non-functional aiming time components.

Practical implications

Despite the limitations discussed below, the present findings have several practical implications for strength and conditioning coaches working with air pistol athletes.

First, the results support the integration of unstable surface exercises into the annual training plans of national-level pistol shooters. The UTCST protocol used in this study (Table 1) provides a ready-to-use, periodised program that progressively increases instability and neural demand over an 8-week cycle.

Second, the progressive training structure (advancing from bilateral to unilateral and from lower to higher instability) may be practically useful. Because dose, frequency, and degree of instability were not systematically manipulated, no dose–response inference can be drawn.

Third, the lack of aiming time reduction should not be interpreted as a failure of the intervention. Instead, it indicates that physical conditioning and technical training may target different performance determinants, and both should be maintained in parallel.

Limitations and future directions

First, and most importantly, this study did not include an absolute control group that received neither the unstable nor the stable training intervention. Both groups performed 8 weeks of structured trunk training in addition to their regular technical training. Therefore, it is not possible to determine whether the observed pre-to-post improvements are attributable to the training interventions per se, or simply to continued sport-specific practice. It also remains unknown whether unstable training is superior to no additional conditioning (i.e., technical training only). Future studies should include a true control group to quantify the net effect of trunk training interventions.

Second, the reliability and validity of the SCATT system for pistol-specific outcome measures have not been systematically established. Recent research in rifle shooting has reported test–retest errors of up to 30% for some SCATT-derived variables32, which could potentially wash out small intervention effects. Although SCATT is widely used in shooting research and was employed under standardised conditions in this study, we did not independently establish the within-subject, day-to-day test–retest reliability (ICC, SEM, or MDC) of pistol-specific SCATT metrics in this sample; this is acknowledged as a limitation, and future studies should quantify day-to-day reliability and report minimal detectable change thresholds.

Third, this study did not include direct physiological or neuromuscular measurements. While the biomechanical and performance tests employed are ecologically valid and sensitive to training-induced changes, they provide only indirect evidence regarding the underlying mechanisms. Future studies should incorporate surface electromyography to quantify changes in muscle activation patterns, and perturbation-based postural control tests to assess anticipatory and compensatory balance strategies.

Fourth, the sample was limited to young, national-level male athletes, which restricts generalisability to female shooters, junior athletes, or shooters of different competitive levels. The sample size (n = 20), while sufficient to detect large effects, precluded subgroup analyses or examination of individual response variability.

In addition, the athletes’ habitual exposure to unstable-surface, balance, or dedicated trunk-stabilization training before the study was not formally quantified; this limits the extent to which the magnitude of the observed adaptations—and whether the UTCST protocol represented a genuinely novel training stimulus—can be precisely interpreted.

Fifth, the training intervention was limited to 8 weeks, and no follow-up assessment was conducted. Therefore, the retention effects and optimal periodisation of unstable trunk training remain unknown. Training parameters (intensity, frequency, volume, and degree of instability) were not systematically manipulated and warrant future investigation. Sixth, the sample size was based on an assumed large effect (f = 0.40) derived from a general convention rather than from directly comparable studies; combined with the small sample and the number of outcomes analysed without correction for multiple comparisons, this means the trial is best regarded as exploratory, and the effect sizes reported here may be inflated. The present findings should therefore be interpreted as preliminary and require confirmation in larger, adequately powered, and prospectively registered studies. In addition, this trial was registered retrospectively (registered April 2026; the study commenced August 2024). Retrospective registration precludes independent verification that outcomes and analyses were specified before data collection and cannot exclude selective reporting or post hoc hypothesizing; the findings are therefore hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in prospectively registered trials.

Despite these limitations, the consistently larger effect sizes observed in the UTCST group across multiple independent outcome measures (COP sway area, COP path/area ratio, shooting score, ring stability, muzzle velocity, and aim-to-trigger deviation) suggest that UTCST may be a more effective conditioning modality than STCST for improving shooting-specific postural control and performance in national-level air pistol athletes; this interpretation, however, remains preliminary and requires confirmation in larger, prospectively registered trials.

Conclusions

This study compared the effects of an 8-week unstable versus stable trunk core strength training program on postural control and shooting performance in national-level 10 m air pistol athletes. The results indicate that both training modalities significantly improved shooting score, 10-ring stability, and muzzle velocity control. However, the unstable surface training group demonstrated significantly greater improvements than the stable surface group in COP-derived postural control metrics, 10.5-ring stability, and aim-to-trigger deviation, with consistently larger effect sizes across all outcome measures. After correction for multiple comparisons, 10.5-ring stability and muzzle velocity remained significant, whereas the remaining between-group differences were attenuated to marginal significance and should be regarded as exploratory.

These findings suggest that, in this pilot sample, UTCST appeared more effective than STCST for enhancing shooting-specific postural stability and precision in national-level air pistol athletes, although this requires confirmation in larger, prospectively registered studies. The observed transfer of postural control gains to shooting performance supports the integration of progressive, instability-based exercises into the annual training plans of air pistol athletes.

Nevertheless, these conclusions are limited by the absence of an absolute control group (receiving no structured trunk training) and the lack of direct neuromuscular measurements. Future studies are required to confirm whether unstable training is superior to sport-specific technical training alone, and to elucidate the underlying mechanisms through electromyographic or neurophysiological investigations.
