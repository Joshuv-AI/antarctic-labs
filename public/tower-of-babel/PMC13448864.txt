Characterising Continuous Glucose Monitoring Metrics Across Normoglycaemia, Prediabetes and Type 2 Diabetes
Authors: Simon L. Cichosz, Kristine Færch, Morten H. Jensen
Published: 2026

Background

Glycaemic control is a cornerstone of metabolic health, and defining the boundaries between normal and abnormal glucose regulation is essential for both clinical practice and research. While glycated haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) and fasting blood glucose traditionally classify individuals into normoglycaemia, prediabetes and diabetes, continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) offers a detailed, dynamic view of daily glucose fluctuations.

CGM was originally developed to support insulin management in people with type 1 diabetes. However, the recent availability of over‐the‐counter CGM devices and the broader adoption of digital health technologies raise new opportunities for early detection, diagnosis and risk stratification of people with normoglycaemia, prediabetes or type 2 diabetes not treated with insulin. Still, comprehensive CGM‐based reference ranges—spanning the range from normoglycaemia to insulin‐treated type 2 diabetes—remain limited [1]. Establishing such reference ranges is critical for contextualising individual CGM values, identifying early dysglycaemia, guiding clinical decision‐making and designing clinical studies.

In this study, we provide distribution ranges for key CGM metrics across glucose tolerance states, offering a framework that links laboratory measures with real‐world glucose patterns.

Methods

Study Population

We analysed data from the AI‐READI cohort (Version 3.0.0, collected between 2023–2025), a cross‐sectional, multi‐site prospective cohort (n = 2280) including individuals with normoglycaemia, prediabetes and type 2 diabetes not treated with insulin (DM Oral) or treated with insulin (DM Insulin). Participants were identified via electronic health record‐based ICD‐10 codes and complete standardised assessments and biospecimen collection. AI‐READI was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the University of Washington (approval number STUDY00016228), including reliance agreements with the IRBs of University of Alabama at Birmingham and University of California, San Diego. Written informed consent was provided by all participants [2].

Participants were categorised based on documented clinical diagnoses and medication use. Individuals with diabetes were identified using established diagnoses (ICD‐10 code E11.X) and subsequently stratified according to treatment (insulin vs. non‐insulin therapy). Participants without a diagnosis of diabetes were classified as either normoglycaemic or prediabetic based on HbA1c measurements obtained as part of the study protocol, applying the American Diabetes Association (ADA) criteria for prediabetes (HbA1c 5.7%–6.4% [39–47 mmol/mol]).

This approach ensured a clear distinction between clinically diagnosed diabetes and HbA1c‐based classification in individuals without diabetes, while maintaining consistency with standardised diagnostic criteria.

CGM

CGM data were collected from masked Dexcom G6 devices. Each participant wore a single sensor, and for each individual we selected the longest continuous monitoring period (up to 11 days) with a minimum of 10 days of coverage to ensure a good representation of each metric [3]. Data were cleaned for out of sensor range (40–400 mg/dL), gaps and incomplete wear‐time without imputations of missing data, following suggested procedures [4]. We used the QoCGM tool [5] to compute a standardised set of CGM‐derived metrics using harmonised and reproducible definitions. The primary metrics assessed were mean glucose, Time‐in‐Range (TIR), Time‐Below‐Range (TBR), Time‐Above‐Range (TAR), Time‐in‐Tight‐Range (TITR), Time‐over‐Target‐Range (TART), Mean Amplitude of Glycaemic Excursions (MAGE) and Coefficient of Variation (CV).

Diurnal Variation

To account for diurnal variation in glycaemic patterns, the metrics were also calculated separately for nighttime (00:00–06:00) and daytime (06:00–24:00) intervals.

Statistical Analysis

For each metric, we calculated the median, interquartile range (IQR) and prediction intervals (80% and 95%) for each group. Furthermore, we visualised selected metric distributions (TIR, TITR, MAGE). Between‐group differences were evaluated using the Kruskal–Wallis test, with significant results followed by Dunn's post hoc comparisons with Bonferroni correction.

All data processing and analyses of the data were conducted in MATLAB 24.2.0.2806996 (R2024b) Update 3 (Natick, Massachusetts: The MathWorks Inc.) We adhered to the proposed ‘Research Code Sharing in Support of Gold Standard Science’ [6] which suggests publishing workable code in connection with publishable research results to promote trust, validating and reuse of science.

Results

A total of 2163 participants (normoglycaemia, n = 669; prediabetes, n = 610; DM Oral, n = 648; DM Insulin, n = 236) with CGM and HbA1c measurements were included in the analysis. The median (IQR) age was 58 (19) years in the normoglycaemia group, 63 (16) years in the prediabetes group, 63 (16) years in the DM Oral group and 61.5 (18) years in the DM Insulin group. The median number of monitoring days for all groups was 11 days with > 99% coverage. CGM metrics and group characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Overall, glycaemic metrics were associated with progressively worsening glycaemic control across the continuum from normoglycaemia to prediabetes to diabetes. Between normoglycaemia and prediabetes, the difference in TIR was less pronounced, with median (IQR) values of 98.63 (2.63) and 97.19 (6.47) (p < 0.001), respectively. Although the prediction intervals showed substantial overlap between these two groups, TITR demonstrated a larger difference in median values (89.29 [10.86] and 81.59 [24.06], p < 0.001). TATR also showed a marked difference in median values between groups (9.9 [11.4] vs. 18.1 [24.9], p < 0.001).

Results for specific daytime and nighttime periods are also presented in Table 1. The patterns between day and night were generally consistent across groups; however, differences were more pronounced during the night. For example, nighttime time‐above‐range (TAR) was higher in prediabetes and progressively increased across diabetes groups compared with normoglycaemia (median TAR: 0.6%, 2.6%, 12.4% and 43.6% for normoglycaemia, prediabetes, orally treated diabetes and insulin‐treated diabetes, respectively), whereas the normoglycaemia group showed minimal differences between daytime and nighttime.

Sensitivity analyses restricted to participants aged ≥ 60 years, those with BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2, and after exclusion of the first 24 h of CGM data did not materially alter the observed between‐group differences (Tables S1–S3).

Conclusions

This study establishes CGM metric distribution ranges across the glucose tolerance continuum, utilising data to define median values and prediction intervals for individuals with normoglycaemia, prediabetes and diabetes managed via oral medication or insulin. This analysis expands previous findings from smaller studies by presenting evidence from a large cohort utilising newer CGM technology [7, 8, 9]. Our findings provide a more nuanced perspective on glycaemic control among individuals classified as normoglycaemic (HbA1c < 5.7%). Specifically, we observed a lower median TITR of 89.29vs. 96%, higher mean of 114.9 vs. 99 mg/dL lower TBR (0.2 vs. 1.1%) and similar CV (16.5 vs. 17) reported by Shah et al. [9] in a cohort of 153 normoglycaemic individuals. However, this difference may be explained by the fact that the study by Shah et al. included a younger population with a BMI below 30. Previous large‐scale investigations have provided valuable benchmarks for CGM metrics across different populations. For instance, Keshet et al. [10] reported the mean and standard deviation for TIR and glycaemic variability in over 7000 individuals without diabetes, noting a mean TIR lower than the median observed in our cohort (94% vs. 98%), though with a comparable coefficient of variation (CV). Similarly, Varghese et al. [11] characterised the distribution of CGM metrics within a large cohort of 5901 individuals with type 2 diabetes.

In contrast, our approach explicitly focuses on the non‐normal distribution of glycaemic metrics to offer a more nuanced understanding of dysglycaemia. A methodological limitation of earlier literature is the reliance on parametric summary statistics, such as the mean and standard deviation, to describe inherently non‐normal distributions [10, 12]. Furthermore, a strength of our study is the inclusion of a full glycaemic spectrum spanning normoglycaemia, prediabetes and diabetes within a single protocol. Because all participants were recruited under identical study conditions, variables such as the CGM sensor model, wear time and data collection protocols were uniform across all cohorts, minimising potential confounding variations between groups.

Our findings demonstrate a clear, progressive deterioration in glycaemic control with worsening metabolic health, reflected by rising mean glucose levels and increased glycaemic variability. Notably, while TIR remains relatively high and comparable between people with normoglycaemia and prediabetes, TITR is more sensitive to emerging dysglycaemia, declining from ~89% to ~82% in these groups. Diurnal analysis indicates that while glycaemic patterns are generally consistent between night and day, group differences were most pronounced at night, where individuals with prediabetes and diabetes exhibit significantly higher TAR compared to those with normoglycaemia. Notably, the DM Insulin group experiences a substantial nocturnal burden with a median TAR of ~44%. Together, the reference ranges presented in this work provide an important framework for clinical benchmarking and earlier detection of dysglycaemia.

A limitation is the use of HbA1c alone to define glycaemic groups, without fasting glucose or OGTT. However, this reflects common clinical practice and is central to our objective. The observed heterogeneity and overlap in CGM metrics within HbA1c‐defined categories suggest that HbA1c may not fully capture early dysglycaemia, highlighting the complementary value of CGM.

Individuals with similar HbA1c levels may exhibit substantially different glucose dynamics [13]. Moreover, HbA1c is influenced by factors unrelated to glycaemia, including variation in erythrocyte turnover and the presence of haemoglobin variants, which can contribute to misclassification. In contrast, CGM provides granular, real‐world insight into daily glucose patterns and may help identify early disturbances in glucose regulation.

Notably, there is substantial overlap in CGM‐derived glycaemic metrics related to both mean glucose trends and glycaemic variability between the groups, further highlighting the limitations of HbA1c‐based classification. A potential limitation of this study is selection bias, as certain demographic or clinical subgroups may have been more inclined to participate. Additionally, the use of a standardised, fixed time window to define nighttime represents a generalised estimate that may not perfectly capture individual variation in sleep–wake cycles. Age was evaluated as a potential confounder, but showed negligible associations with CGM metrics in this cohort and did not influence the results. A further limitation is the lack of detailed information on insulin types and doses and the use of weight‐loss medications, such as GLP‐1 receptor agonists, which may influence glycaemic outcomes. Future research should explore whether CGM‐derived phenotypes can improve risk stratification, particularly by identifying individuals at elevated risk of progressing to type 2 diabetes or developing diabetes‐related complications. The presented descriptive analyses require further validation in diverse populations and ethnicities before generalisable reference values for CGM metrics can be established.
