Occult residual disease after presumed gross-total resection of a predominantly intramuscular paraspinal lumbar schwannoma: a case report introducing the VERIFY-GTR end-of-resection bundle
Authors: Quang-Phuc Pham, Manh-Cuong Vo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundParaspinal schwannomas arising from exiting lumbar nerve roots are uncommon but clinically important because deep intramuscular extension can obscure tumor boundaries. Key findings

• Occult residual intramuscular tumor can persist after presumed gross-total resection (GTR) and complete early clinical recovery.

• Early postoperative contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) identified a residual nodule measuring approximately 8% of the original tumor volume.

What is known and what is new?

• Predominantly paraspinal/extraforaminal schwannomas can be approached through a posterior paramedian Wiltse corridor, but multilobulated intramuscular extension can obscure completeness.

• This case introduces VERIFY-GTR, an auditable end-of-resection verification bundle coupled with early postoperative contrast-enhanced MRI to define radiologically verified GTR.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Surgeons should not equate a visually clean operative cavity or early symptom relief with confirmed tumor eradication in multilobulated intramuscular paraspinal schwannomas; structured verification and early postoperative contrast-enhanced MRI should be considered when feasible.

Introduction

Schwannomas are usually benign neoplasms arising from Schwann cells and account for a substantial proportion of surgically treated spinal peripheral nerve sheath tumors. In the spine, they most often present as intradural extramedullary lesions and, together with meningiomas, constitute the dominant diagnostic category in many series (1,2). Clinically, patients commonly develop localized pain, radicular symptoms, or paresthesia well before objective neurological deficits emerge, a pattern that can closely resemble degenerative lumbar disease when motor findings and myelopathic signs are absent.

A smaller subset arises from the exiting nerve root and extends predominantly through the neural foramen into the paraspinal compartment. These extraforaminal/paraspinal tumors are less common but pose disproportionate diagnostic and operative challenges because their margins may be obscured by deep paraspinal musculature and because surgical planning must be organized around the dominant anatomical compartment rather than the spinal canal alone. For symptomatic lesions or those demonstrating growth, complete microsurgical excision remains the preferred treatment because recurrence and clinically meaningful regrowth are most consistently associated with residual tumor (3-7).

Accordingly, approach selection is anatomy-driven. When disease is largely paraspinal or extraforaminal, a posterior paramedian muscle-splitting corridor (Wiltse approach) provides a direct trajectory to the lesion while limiting midline muscle detachment and preserving posterior osseoligamentous structures (8-10). Although dumbbell schwannoma strategies are well described, intramuscular paraspinal components create a specific failure mode: a microscopically “clean” cavity may still harbor a spatially separate intramuscular lobule beyond the working corridor.

Throughout this report, “presumed gross-total resection (GTR)” refers to the intraoperative impression after visual and microscopic cavity inspection, whereas “radiologically verified GTR” refers to the absence of residual enhancing tumor on early postoperative contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). This distinction is critical because decompression-related symptom relief and a visually clean cavity do not reliably exclude residual intramuscular disease.

Here, we describe an upper lumbar paraspinal schwannoma excised via a single posterior paramedian approach, highlighting a limitation of intraoperative judgment. We demonstrate radiographic residual tumor despite presumed GTR and complete symptom resolution (Figure 1), and we introduce VERIFY-GTR: an auditable end-of-resection verification bundle (Table 1) integrated with early postoperative contrast-enhanced MRI to operationalize radiologically verified GTR (Figure 2) and guide follow-up intensity (Table 2). Case presentation

Patient information

A 60-year-old man with no relevant medical comorbidities presented with a 1-year history of gradually progressive dull pain and numbness along the anterolateral aspect of the left thigh. He reported no inciting trauma and denied lower-extremity weakness, gait instability, bowel or bladder dysfunction, or systemic symptoms such as fever, weight loss, or nocturnal pain. Pain severity and disability were assessed using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) 6/10 and the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) 70%, respectively. Family history was non-contributory, and there was no clinical history suggestive of neurofibromatosis or schwannomatosis. He had undergone no previous biopsy, injection, or surgery for this lesion.

Clinical findings

On neurological examination, light-touch and pinprick sensation was mildly reduced over the anterolateral left thigh, consistent with an L2 dermatomal distribution. Motor testing demonstrated normal strength (Medical Research Council grade 5/5) across all major lower-limb muscle groups. Deep tendon reflexes were symmetric, straight-leg raise testing was negative, and there were no clinical features to suggest spinal deformity or segmental instability.

Timeline

Approximately 12 months before surgery, the patient developed gradually progressive left anterolateral thigh pain and numbness. Preoperative MRI demonstrated a left L1–L2 dumbbell paraspinal mass, and computed tomography (CT)-guided biopsy confirmed World Health Organization (WHO) grade 1 schwannoma. Microsurgical excision was then performed through a posterior paramedian Wiltse corridor. He was discharged on postoperative day 7 with reduced pain, and at 2 months he had complete symptomatic recovery but contrast-enhanced MRI demonstrated a residual enhancing nodule.

Diagnostic assessment

Lumbar MRI revealed a well-defined, ovoid left paraspinal mass extending from the superior endplate of L1 to the inferior endplate of L2, associated with expansion of the left neural foramen and a dumbbell configuration (Figure 3A-3D). The lesion was hypointense on T1-weighted sequences and heterogeneously hyperintense on T2-weighted sequences, with mixed solid and cystic components, and it demonstrated heterogeneous gadolinium enhancement, an imaging pattern characteristic of schwannoma (21,22). Diffusion-weighted imaging showed no restricted diffusion. Laterally, the mass abutted and exerted mass effect on the left iliopsoas muscle. Maximal dimensions were 30 mm × 43 mm × 72 mm (estimated ellipsoid volume approximately 48.6 cm3), with a predominantly extraforaminal/paraspinal component and only limited intraspinal extension.

Image-guided percutaneous biopsy demonstrated a spindle-cell neoplasm with Antoni A and Antoni B architecture (23) and diffuse S-100 immunoreactivity, supporting the diagnosis of schwannoma (WHO grade 1) (Figure 4A,4B). We performed a preoperative CT-guided biopsy to confirm benign histology and to support shared decision-making, given the tumor’s size, paraspinal location, and the wide differential diagnosis for deep-seated soft-tissue lesions. Before histologic confirmation, the differential diagnosis included a benign peripheral nerve sheath tumor, a malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor, and other deep paraspinal soft-tissue neoplasms. No material diagnostic barriers related to access, language, culture, or cost affected the work-up.

Therapeutic intervention

On the basis of the patient’s progressive symptoms and the lesion’s size and extraforaminal predominance, microsurgical excision was recommended. A posterior paramedian, muscle-splitting Wiltse corridor was selected to provide a direct route to the extraforaminal compartment while limiting paraspinal muscle stripping and avoiding destabilizing osseous resection (8-10). After induction of general anaesthesia, the patient was positioned prone. A 4-cm paramedian skin incision was made approximately 3 cm off midline and centered over the L1–L2 level. The thoracolumbar fascia was opened, and blunt dissection was carried through the multifidus-longissimus intermuscular interval to expose the lateral lamina and facet complex.

A limited L1–L2 hemilaminectomy was performed to delineate the foraminal extension and to clearly identify the originating nerve root. Under the operating microscope, the capsule was incised and internal decompression was undertaken to relax the mass, followed by circumferential extracapsular dissection along the tumor-muscle and tumor-foraminal interfaces. Small adherent vascular feeders were coagulated and divided to maintain a clean dissection plane. The lesion was removed without dural violation (gross appearance shown in Figure 3D). Estimated blood loss was approximately 200 mL, and operative time was 150 minutes. Multimodality intraoperative neuromonitoring remained stable throughout, and no instrumentation was required because the facet complex was preserved (8-10). Key technical priorities included precise identification of the parent nerve root with continuous neuromonitoring, preservation of the facet complex to prevent postoperative instability, and a deliberate end-of-resection verification phase to interrogate intramuscular planes (Table 1). Because the resection cavity appeared macroscopically clear and neuromonitoring remained stable, no additional staged procedure or more extensive exposure was undertaken intraoperatively.

Intraoperative assessment and the challenge of residual tumor detection

At the completion of resection, the cavity was examined systematically under high magnification and appeared free of visible tumor. In predominantly intramuscular multilobulated lesions, a narrow paramedian corridor can leave a spatially separate intramuscular lobule outside the line of sight, so microscopic “cleanliness” is not a sufficient surrogate for completeness.

Intraoperative MRI was not available in our setting; however, intraoperative ultrasound can provide a practical adjunct to interrogate the resection bed and adjacent muscle planes in many centers (14,15,24,25). Preoperative planning should include a tri-planar MRI lobule inventory that identifies the foraminal/root component, dominant extraforaminal mass, psoas or iliopsoas extension, cranial/caudal margins, and any satellite intramuscular lobules. When available, intraoperative ultrasound should be used after apparent resection as a dedicated sweep of the cavity and adjacent psoas/paraspinal muscle planes; suspicious hypoechoic or heterogeneous nodules should be correlated with the MRI inventory, sampled or resected when neurologically safe, and followed by a repeat ultrasound sweep. In this case, intraoperative ultrasound was not available; therefore, the ultrasound element is presented as an adjunctive verification strategy rather than patient-specific operative evidence.

To strengthen end-of-resection verification, we propose VERIFY-GTR: (I) preoperative “lobule inventory” mapping; (II) an end-of-resection verification bundle (Table 1); and (III) an early postoperative contrast-enhanced MRI baseline to define radiologically verified GTR (workflow in Figure 2).

Follow-up and outcomes

The postoperative course was uneventful. The patient experienced early improvement in sensory symptoms and remained neurologically intact, with no new focal deficits. He was discharged on postoperative day 7 with substantially reduced pain (VAS 6 preoperatively to 2 at discharge). At the 2-month review, he reported complete resolution of pain and numbness (VAS 0), with full functional recovery (ODI 0%).

Despite the favourable clinical course, contrast-enhanced MRI at 2 months demonstrated a residual, avidly enhancing mass within the left paraspinal compartment, measuring 15 mm × 22 mm × 23 mm (estimated ellipsoid volume approximately 4.0 cm3) (Figure 1A,1B). Compared with the estimated preoperative tumor volume of 48.6 cm3, this corresponds to a patient-specific residual fraction of 8.2%. This value is a volumetric descriptor of the reported case, not an institutional residual tumor rate and should not be generalized beyond this case report. Given the benign pathology and the absence of recurrent symptoms, management was agreed through shared decision-making: close-interval MRI surveillance was selected, with re-intervention reserved for documented radiographic progression or recurrent clinical symptoms (framework in Table 2). This dissociation underscores that symptom relief after decompression is not a reliable indicator of radiologic completeness in extraforaminal/intramuscular schwannomas. Because treatment consisted of a single operative intervention, adherence was not applicable; tolerability was assessed by neurological examination, wound review, and interval MRI, and no wound complications, new neurological deficits, or other adverse events occurred. Patient perspective

The patient did not provide a formal written first-person statement for publication during manuscript revision. At follow-up, however, he reported complete resolution of pain and numbness, full functional recovery, and satisfaction with the surgical outcome.

Discussion

Two practical points arise from this case. First, operative strategy for paraspinal schwannomas should be anchored to the tumor’s dominant anatomical compartment. When disease is predominantly extraforaminal, a posterior paramedian, muscle-splitting corridor offers a direct trajectory to the paraspinal space while limiting midline muscle detachment and reducing approach-related morbidity (8-10,26,27). Second, the intraoperative impression of completeness can be falsely reassuring in multilobulated schwannomas with an intramuscular component.

Residual tumor matters because it is the most plausible substrate for subsequent regrowth, and it may remain clinically occult during an early postoperative interval in which symptom improvement reflects decompression rather than biological eradication (3-7). Postoperative imaging should therefore be framed as a core safety element of care rather than an optional documentation step, particularly when tumors are large, dumbbell-shaped, or embedded within paraspinal musculature (3-7,19,28).

VERIFY-GTR is not intended to replace sound anatomical exposure or to impose sarcoma-style wide muscular margins on a benign schwannoma. Its role is narrower: to confirm that all radiographically mapped lobules have been encountered and addressed when a multilobulated tumor extends into psoas or paraspinal muscle planes where visual inspection of the operative cavity may be incomplete. The bundle is therefore most relevant for large, multilobulated, deep intramuscular or psoas-associated lesions treated through a limited line-of-sight corridor, and is less likely to add value for small, unilobular, fully exposed paraspinal tumors in which all margins are directly visible and early postoperative MRI confirms no residual enhancement.

Several factors can explain how residual disease escapes detection. In this patient, imaging suggested a discrete residual focus that likely represented a separate intramuscular lobule. Such lobules can be deceptively difficult to appreciate intraoperatively because their pseudocapsule may resemble normal fascial planes, and the interface between tumor and adjacent muscle can become indistinct under magnification, particularly after manipulation and hemostasis. In addition, a limited working corridor can constrain circumferential visualization; unless the surgeon deliberately interrogates paraspinal muscle around the resection cavity, small components may remain beyond the exposed margins.

The residual focus in Figure 1 lies in the psoas/iliopsoas intramuscular compartment. Approach selection for psoas schwannomas should be individualized according to tumor epicenter and the tumor-plexus relationship. The lumbar plexus is formed by the ventral rami of L1–L4 within the posterior psoas; the genitofemoral nerve traverses or emerges on the anterior psoas surface, and the femoral and obturator nerves course along the psoas-iliacus and medial psoas regions, respectively. For posterior or dorsolateral upper-lumbar lesions arising from the exiting root, a posterior paramedian Wiltse corridor provides a direct trajectory. Ventral psoas lesions may require retroperitoneal or lateral exposure, and direct transpsoas dissection should be undertaken cautiously with neuromonitoring and triggered stimulation (11,12).

Risk mitigation begins before incision. Preoperative planning should include tri-planar mapping of lobulation and muscle-plane extension (Figure 3A-3C), creating a “lobule inventory” that can be checked off intraoperatively (Table 1). When available, neuronavigation can help delineate projected margins of paraspinal extension and verify that dissection has reached predefined boundaries in three dimensions (13).

At the end of resection, a structured verification phase is warranted (Table 1). When available, adjunct imaging can increase confidence in completeness. Intraoperative ultrasound is attractive because it is repeatable, relatively low cost, and provides real-time feedback that can identify residual tissue and guide additional resection when appropriate (14,15,24,25). Reports across brain and spine tumor surgery describe meaningful concordance between intraoperative ultrasound assessment of residual and postoperative MRI, supporting its role as a practical verification tool (24,25). Contrast-enhanced ultrasound has also been reported in selected spinal tumor contexts (14,15).

A focused literature review supports the rationale but also the current evidentiary limits of VERIFY-GTR (Table 3). Spinal schwannoma outcome series consistently identify residual or subtotal resection as a key substrate for recurrence or regrowth (3-7). Wiltse/paraspinal approach studies support compartment-driven exposure (8-10,26,27), while neuronavigation and intraoperative imaging literature supports the general principle that visual assessment alone may miss residual tumor (13-18,24,25). Recent reports of intramuscular and psoas major schwannomas further reinforce diagnostic delay, psoas compartment complexity, and the need for deliberate MRI-based compartment planning (11,12,29). We did not identify a previously validated schwannoma-specific end-of-resection verification bundle; accordingly, VERIFY-GTR should be regarded as an auditable, hypothesis-generating synthesis rather than a validated standard of care.

Early postoperative contrast-enhanced MRI strengthens interpretability. Reactive enhancement increases with time after surgery; earlier imaging improves the ability to distinguish residual tumor from postoperative change (ideally within 48–72 hours) (17). In oncologic spine imaging, early postoperative baselines are emphasized for longitudinal interpretation when enhancement is expected (16). While intraoperative MRI is not widely available for paraspinal schwannoma surgery, literature in other neurosurgical settings demonstrates that intraoperative MRI can reveal unexpected residual and alter management, underscoring the general principle that visual impression of GTR can be unreliable (18). VERIFY-GTR operationalizes this principle for intramuscular paraspinal schwannomas by combining an intraoperative verification bundle (Table 1) with an early MRI endpoint (Figure 2).

Management of a small residual schwannoma depends on symptoms, growth dynamics, and anatomical risk. In an asymptomatic patient with a stable residual nodule, serial MRI surveillance is reasonable; reoperation is more strongly indicated when imaging demonstrates progressive enlargement, persistent mass effect, or uncertainty regarding lesion characteristics (3-7). For interpretability, case reports should specify baseline imaging timing, surveillance intervals, and predefined triggers for re-intervention (Table 2).

Limitations

This case report describes a single patient and therefore cannot estimate the incidence of residual tumor after resection of paraspinal schwannomas. The postoperative residual fraction of 8.2% is a patient-specific volumetric descriptor, not a denominator-based institutional residual rate. This single case also cannot establish the effectiveness, sensitivity, specificity, cost-effectiveness, or morbidity profile of VERIFY-GTR. In addition, the residual lesion was identified 2 months postoperatively rather than in the immediate postoperative period (e.g., within 48 to 72 hours). Although the imaging characteristics strongly suggest residual disease, an MRI obtained in the very early postoperative phase would have more conclusively linked the finding to tumor overlooked during surgery while minimizing the influence of intervening biological changes (16,17). Establishing an early postoperative MRI baseline—ideally within 48 to 72 hours—would increase confidence that the residual represented an undetected satellite lobule and should be considered when feasible in similar cases. VERIFY-GTR should be evaluated prospectively in a multi-case cohort using standardized preoperative lobule inventory, documentation of each verification element, early postoperative contrast-enhanced MRI, residual-volume calculation, neurological morbidity, and longitudinal regrowth or reoperation outcomes.

Conclusions

In multilobulated intramuscular paraspinal schwannomas, a visually “clean” operative field can be falsely reassuring. A documented end-of-resection verification bundle that interrogates intramuscular and psoas/iliopsoas planes (Table 1), combined with an early postoperative contrast-enhanced MRI baseline and an explicit surveillance/re-intervention pathway (Table 2), provides a practical and prospectively testable framework to reduce missed residual disease and rationalize follow-up (VERIFY-GTR workflow in Figure 2). The operative judgment of GTR should remain provisional until early postoperative contrast-enhanced MRI confirms the absence of residual enhancing tumor.
