Industrial influence on atmospheric VOCs and associated health risks in small residential areas near industrial complexes in Ulsan, South Korea
Authors: Seong-Joon Kim, Sang-Jin Lee, Sung-Deuk Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDue to their ubiquitous emissions and associated health risks, atmospheric volatile organic compounds (VOCs) have been extensively studied in urban and industrial areas. However, small residential areas located near industrial facilities have received relatively little attention. Community-scale monitoring of VOCs was conducted using passive air samplers deployed at 7 sites in small residential areas near industrial complexes in Ulsan, South Korea. Among the 59 VOCs analyzed, aromatic compounds such as m,p,o-xylenes (13.6 ± 8.6 µg/m3), toluene (11.7 ± 11.9 µg/m3), and ethylbenzene (5.3 ± 4.0 µg/m3) were predominant. Elevated total VOC concentrations were observed at sites strongly influenced by solvent-related industrial activities. Correlation analysis between measured concentrations and industrial emissions within radii of 0.5, 1, 2, and 3 km indicated that facilities within 3 km were likely major sources of VOCs. Results of diagnostic ratios and principal component analysis indicated that VOCs were primarily influenced by local industrial activities, such as solvent use, fuel combustion, cleaning/degreasing, and petrochemical refining, rather than by regional or long-range atmospheric transport. Among the 23 VOCs evaluated for health risks, naphthalene was the only compound exceeding the US EPA threshold for acute non-cancer risk, primarily affecting the respiratory system. In contrast, cancer and non-cancer risk estimates for chronic exposure remained within acceptable levels. These findings highlight that residents within 3 km of the industrial complexes may be exposed to significant health risks. Therefore, continuous monitoring of VOC levels over various exposure durations (e.g., acute, subchronic, and chronic) is needed, even in small residential areas.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10661-026-15628-z.Keywords: Passive sampling, Source identification, PCA, T/B ratio, X/B ratio

Introduction

Over the past few decades, numerous studies have monitored atmospheric volatile organic compounds (VOCs) to investigate their concentrations, emission sources, secondary formation, and associated health risks. VOCs are ubiquitously emitted from both anthropogenic sources, such as vehicular exhaust, inks, paints, and industrial solvents, and biogenic sources (Duan et al., 2023; Gu et al., 2021; Kim et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2025). Some VOCs are classified as hazardous air pollutants (HAPs) by the United States Environmental Protection Agency (US EPA), including known carcinogens such as benzene and naphthalene (https://www.epa.gov). Due to their potential for inhalation exposure and adverse health effects, VOCs remain a critical focus of environmental research.

Many studies have assessed the long-term health risks of carcinogenic (e.g., benzene) and non-carcinogenic VOCs (e.g., xylenes) through chronic exposure assessment. For example, in Albany, United States, cancer and non-cancer risks of 17 VOCs were assessed using approximately five years of measurement data (Paul & Bari, 2022). In Calgary, Canada, five years of concentration data for 16 VOCs were analyzed to estimate health risks (Xiong et al., 2021). In Zhengzhou, China, 21 VOCs were evaluated for health risks based on one month of measurements (Zhang et al., 2021). In Seoul, South Korea, 5 carcinogenic and 5 non-carcinogenic VOCs were monitored for one year to determine their associated risks (Kim et al., 2022). Despite these efforts, risk assessments focusing on short-term exposure scenarios (e.g., acute and subchronic exposure) remain limited. This gap is notable, as short-term exposure to elevated VOC levels, especially in industrial areas, may pose serious health risks (Cao et al., 2024; Nakyai et al., 2025).

Passive air samplers (PASs) are widely used to obtain high-spatial-resolution data and assess personal exposure. They are particularly suitable for deployment at multiple sites, as they do not require electricity, making them a compact and cost-effective alternative to traditional active air samplers (Bruno et al., 2008; Gallego et al., 2011). Consequently, their application in air monitoring studies has increased. PAS-based VOC monitoring has been conducted in various countries, including China (Huang et al., 2023), South Korea (Kim et al., 2021), Turkey (Caliskan et al., 2022), and the United States (Miller et al., 2022).

Ulsan, the largest industrial city in South Korea, hosts multiple industrial complexes, including shipbuilding, automobile, petrochemical, and non-ferrous industries, all of which emit substantial amounts of air pollutants (Kim et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2023, 2024b, c). Data from the Pollutant Release and Transfer Registers (PRTR) system indicated that Ulsan had the highest atmospheric emissions of toxic chemicals from industrial facilities (7.7 tonne/km2/yr) among 16 major cities in South Korea in 2020, followed by Daegu (2.6 tonne/km2/yr), Busan (1.7 tonne/km2/yr), and Gyeonggi (1.7 tonne/km2/yr) (https://icis.me.go.kr/prtr). The Clean Air Policy Support System (CAPSS) reported that Seoul exhibited the highest total VOC emissions (104.7 tonne/km2/yr), followed by Ulsan (77.4 tonne/km2/yr), Incheon (46.0 tonne/km2/yr), and Busan (43.4 tonne/km2/yr) in 2020 (https://www.air.go.kr/main.do). According to CAPSS, the major VOC sources in Seoul were solvent usage (83.7%), road transport (8.6%), and non-road transport (4.1%), whereas in Ulsan, the primary sources were production processes (62.8%), solvent usage (21.9%), and waste disposal (8.1%). These data emphasize the need for VOC monitoring in industrialized cities to characterize emission patterns and assess associated health risks. In South Korea, intensive VOC monitoring campaigns have been conducted by the National Institute of Environmental Research (NIER) in major urban and industrial cities, including Pohang, Seoul, Incheon, and Ulsan (NIER, 2013, 2014, 2015, 2020). However, the number of monitoring sites in these campaigns has been limited, and exposures in small residential communities near the industrial complexes have not been sufficiently addressed.

Therefore, small residential areas located in proximity to the industrial complexes in Ulsan, a heavily industrialized city in South Korea, were selected to (1) investigate VOC levels and emission profiles, (2) identify their spatial distributions and major sources, and (3) assess the health risks of VOCs from both acute and chronic exposure. Although our previous studies monitored VOCs at multiple urban and industrial sites in Ulsan (Kim et al., 2024, 2025; Lee et al., 2024a), this preliminary study provides essential information on the levels, sources, and risks of VOCs, serving as a foundation for future research aimed at better understanding potential health impacts on nearby residents. In addition, this study aimed to address several research gaps by (1) focusing on sparsely populated communities near industrial zones, (2) integrating measurement and emission data, and (3) simultaneously assessing both acute and chronic health risks.

Materials and methods

Air sampling and site description

The study area is a major industrial metropolis located along the southeast coast of the Korean Peninsula, with a population of approximately 1.1 million and a total area of 1,062 km2 (https://kosis.kr). To monitor ambient VOCs, duplicate passive air samplers (Radiello®, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) were deployed. A diffusive body (RAD 120–2) paired with a charcoal-filled cartridge (RAD 145, Carbograph 4, 530 ± 30 mg, 35–50 mesh) was used for each sampler. All cartridges were refrigerated at −4 °C during pre- and post-sampling to minimize contamination and analyte loss. Sampling was conducted at 7 sites for one week (October 13–20, 2020) in small residential areas adjacent to the industrial complexes, representing 4 major industrial sectors in Ulsan, South Korea: shipbuilding, automobile, petrochemical, and non-ferrous industries (Fig. 1). Photographs and satellite images of the sampling sites and surrounding industrial and residential areas are provided in Figs. S1 and S2 of the Supplementary Information.

Locations of the 4 major industrial complexes (shipbuilding, automobile, petrochemical, and non-ferrous industries) and 7 passive air sampling sites in Ulsan, South Korea

Sample analysis and QA/QC

The sampled cartridges were transferred to the laboratory and inserted into pre-cleaned stainless-steel ATD tubes (L4270128, PerkinElmer, USA). Prior to use, the tubes were conditioned using a tube heater (APK 1200, KNR, South Korea) under an N2 purge (50 mL/min) at 340 °C for 10 min to remove residual moisture and contaminants. Internal standards, including bromochloromethane, 1,4-difluorobenzene, chlorobenzene-d5, and 4-bromofluorobenzene (TO-14A, Restek, USA), were introduced using a gas dilution system (APK 6100, KNR, South Korea). Sample analysis was performed using a thermal desorption (TD) instrument (TD-100, Markes, UK) coupled with a gas chromatograph/mass spectrometer (GC/MS) (7890B/5977A, Agilent, USA). Thermal desorption was performed by heating the samples to 320 °C for 10 min using helium as a carrier gas (50 mL/min). Following desorption, the analytes were directed into a cold trap maintained at −25 °C and filled with Carbograph 1-TD and Carboxen-1000 (Air Toxics/TO-14 Trap, Markes, UK). The trapped samples were then rapidly heated to 300 °C for 5 min and injected into a DB-1 GC column (60 m × 0.25 mm × 1 μm, Agilent, USA) via a transfer line maintained at 170 °C. The GC oven temperature program started at 35 °C with an 8 min hold, followed by an increase to 120 °C at 5 °C/min and a further hold for 9 min. A total of 59 VOCs were quantified using a TO-15 gaseous standard mixture (Restek, USA) under selected ion monitoring (SIM) mode. The analytes were categorized into three groups: aromatic, halogenated, and others (including alkenes, alkanes, and oxygenated compounds).

For quality assurance and quality control (QA/QC), five-point calibration curves were prepared for all compounds, yielding high coefficients of determination (r2 > 0.99) (Kim et al., 2025). Calibration details are provided in Table S1 in the Supplementary Information. Five field blanks showed concentrations between 0.01 and 0.56 µg/m3, and all reported VOC concentrations were corrected for blanks. Method detection limits (MDLs) for each VOC were estimated using Eq. (1) and are listed in Table S2 (Kim et al., 2019, 2025; Lee et al., 2025).

The replicate measurements were obtained by spiking a known concentration (0.05 μg/m3) of the standard mixture into sorbent cartridges that were not deployed in the field but were processed using the same analytical procedures as the ambient samples.

Meteorological conditions

A wind field averaged over the sampling period (October 13–20, 2020) was generated using the CALMET model (version 6.5.0, US EPA). The modeling domain covered a 50 km × 50 km area with a spatial resolution of 1 km. Input data included surface and upper-air meteorological observations, as well as geographical data such as terrain elevation, land use, and coastline features. Surface meteorological variables (e.g., wind speed and direction, temperature, relative humidity, atmospheric pressure, ceiling height, precipitation, and cloud cover) were obtained from one meteorological observatory (MO) and 24 automatic weather stations (AWSs) in Ulsan, South Korea (Fig. S3). Upper-air data, including wind speed, wind direction, pressure, height, temperature, and dew point, were collected from the Pohang radiosonde station (Station 47138; 36°01′56.36"N, 129°22′47.39"E). All meteorological data were provided by the Korea Meteorological Administration (https://data.kma.go.kr). Terrain elevation data (GMTED2010; 7.5 arc-seconds) were obtained from the USGS Earth Resources Observation and Science (EROS) Center, and land cover data (GLCC; 1 km resolution) were acquired from the USGS global database for East Asia (https://earthexplorer.usgs.gov).

Major meteorological conditions were compared between the four seasons of 2020 and the sampling period (Table S3), indicating that the overall meteorological conditions were generally comparable. However, relatively low wind speeds and the absence of precipitation during the sampling period may have contributed to the accumulation of atmospheric VOCs under weak dispersion conditions. In addition to the wind field averaged over the entire sampling period, daytime (noon to 4 pm) and nighttime (8 pm to midnight) wind fields suggested potentially strong influences of nearby VOC emission sources on the sampling sites, particularly during daytime (Fig. S4).

Risk assessment

Non-carcinogenic risks from acute inhalation exposure to 23 VOCs were assessed by calculating hazard quotients (HQs) using the US EPA method, as shown in Eq. (4).

Here, the HQi for VOC i is defined as the ratio of the ambient concentration (Ci, μg/m3) to the acute inhalation health guideline value (HGVi, ppm). HGVs were obtained from several agencies, including the US EPA, Department of Health and Human Services (DHHS), California EPA (CalEPA), and Texas Commission on Environmental Quality (TCEQ). In this study, non-cancer risks were estimated using minimal risk levels (MRLs) reported by the Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry (ATSDR) of the DHHS, which represent intermittent exposures over 1 to 14 days (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/MRLS/mrlsListing.aspx). Concentrations were converted from ppm to µg/m3 by considering the molecular weight of each compound along with ambient temperature and pressure. The hazard index (HI), defined as the sum of HQs for the target VOCs, was calculated for respiratory, immunological, neurological, ocular, and developmental systems based on ATSDR classifications (https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov). These indices were then compared to evaluate cumulative non-cancer risks (Table 1).

Carcinogenic and non-carcinogenic risks from chronic inhalation exposure were calculated for 14 and 36 VOCs, respectively, using Eqs. (5–7).

In these calculations, ECi denotes the exposure concentration (μg/m3) of VOC i. The exposure parameters include exposure time (ET, 24 h/d) (US EPA, 2009), exposure frequency (EF, 350 d/yr) (US EPA, 2009), exposure duration (ED, 30 yr) (CalEPA, 2009), and averaging time (AT). For carcinogenic risk assessment, AT is the mean lifetime of the Korean population (82.7 yr) (NIER, 2019) converted to hours (yr × 365 d/yr × 24 h/d); for non-cancer risk, AT equals ED in hours. Carcinogenic riski refers to the chronic cancer risk, and URi is the inhalation unit risk (μg/m3)−1, obtained from the US EPA’s Integrated Risk Information System (IRIS) (https://iris.epa.gov) and CalEPA’s Office of Environmental Health Hazard Assessment (OEHHA) (https://oehha.ca.gov). For chronic non-cancer risks, HGVs (mg/m3) were based on reference concentrations (RfCs) from the US EPA and reference exposure levels (RELs) from CalEPA. All UR and HGV values used in this study are listed in Table S2. Other VOCs without available UR or HGV values were excluded from the risk estimates. The selected monitoring sites were small residential communities predominantly inhabited by elderly residents, suggesting that the assessed population may be particularly vulnerable to VOC exposure.

Statistical analysis

To evaluate statistical differences in VOC concentrations, a rank-sum test was carried out using SigmaPlot 14.0 (Systat Inc., USA). Correlation and principal component analyses (PCA) were performed using SPSS Statistics 25 (IBM, USA). Spearman’s correlation analysis between measured concentrations and PRTR emission data was conducted to identify industrial impacts at each sampling site. PCA was applied to the VOC dataset, considering 23 species with detection frequencies greater than 80% out of the 59 target VOCs. Input data consisted of individual VOC concentrations normalized by the sum of the 23 VOCs. Three PCs were extracted according to the criteria of eigenvalues greater than 1 and factor loadings of 0.5 or higher for at least one variable (Hosaini et al., 2017). Additionally, a hierarchical clustering dendrogram was constructed using OriginPro 2020 (OriginLab Corporation, USA) to classify sampling sites based on the PCA results.

Results and discussion

Concentrations of total VOCs, VOC groups, and individual species

Figure 2 presents the concentrations of total (Σ59) VOCs and each VOC group during the sampling period (October 13–20, 2020). The mean concentration of Σ59 VOCs (59.8 ± 28.8 µg/m3) was significantly higher than those reported in our previous studies: 34.9 ± 15.2 µg/m3 (Kim et al., 2024) and 28.7 ± 11.4 µg/m3 (Kim et al., 2025), both conducted in the same city and targeting the same compounds in November 2020 and July 2021, respectively. However, excluding a high-concentration event of toluene (38.4 µg/m3 at Site 1), the mean Σ59 VOC concentration in this study was 47.7 ± 17.6 µg/m3, which was statistically comparable to those measured at industrial sites: 48.2 ± 16.1 µg/m3 (Kim et al., 2024) and 39.9 ± 9.5 µg/m3 (Kim et al., 2025) (rank-sum test, p > 0.05).

Concentrations of total (Σ59) VOCs and VOC groups, including aromatic, halogenated, and other compounds, during the passive air sampling period

Among the target VOCs, the aromatic group exhibited the highest mean concentration (40.8 ± 26.4 µg/m3), followed by the other group (14.1 ± 4.28 µg/m3) and the halogenated group (2.12 ± 1.23 µg/m3) (Fig. 2). Elevated concentrations of the aromatic group, including benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, m,p,o-xylenes (BTEX), as well as 1,2,4-trimethylbenzene (TMB), have frequently been reported in industrial areas (Feng et al., 2020; Yuan et al., 2022). Relatively high concentrations were also observed for compounds in the other group, such as methyl ethyl ketone (MEK), ethyl acetate, vinyl acetate, methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE), methyl isobutyl ketone (MIBK), and acrolein. Notably, the first three compounds accounted for 13.7–45.8%, 10.2–32.4%, and 8.4–20.9% of the other group, respectively, and were predominantly emitted from industrial operations (Hsu et al., 2018).

Table S2 summarizes the mean concentrations of individual VOCs with their corresponding standard deviations. Toluene exhibited the highest concentration (11.7 ± 11.9 µg/m3), followed by m,p-xylenes (9.11 ± 5.99 µg/m3), ethylbenzene (5.93 ± 3.98 µg/m3), o-xylene (4.51 ± 2.62 µg/m3), 1,2,4-TMB (3.77 ± 2.73 µg/m3), MEK (3.29 ± 2.34 µg/m3), ethyl acetate (2.39 ± 0.71 µg/m3), and benzene (1.98 ± 0.57 µg/m3). These 8 compounds accounted for 75.4% of the Σ59 VOC concentration. Of these compounds, toluene, ethylbenzene, and m,p,o-xylenes (TEX), which accounted for 58.1% of Σ59 VOCs, are typically reported in solvent-related industrial operations (Choi et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025).

To compare VOC levels, concentrations of 10 major VOCs reported by national monitoring projects were examined across 5 metropolitan and 4 industrial cities in South Korea (Table S4). In these projects, adsorbent tubes were used to collect samples twice daily for 10 consecutive days in each season. Among the 10 VOCs, ethylbenzene, m,p-xylenes, o-xylene, 1,2,4-TMB, and acrolein showed higher concentrations in this study compared to other cities (Table S4). The relatively high concentration of acrolein was likely related to its role as an intermediate in industrial chemical production (Liu et al., 2012) and to ship emissions (Xu et al., 2025) at Ulsan Harbor, which ranks third in cargo volume among South Korean ports (https://nlic.go.kr/nlic). In contrast, elevated concentrations of other VOCs were mainly observed in automobile- and shipbuilding-related industrial areas of Ulsan, where solvent usage is prevalent, as reported by the PRTR system for 2020. Apart from these cases, VOC concentrations in this study were generally comparable to or lower than those in other cities (Table S4). For example, benzene concentrations were higher in Yeosu·Gwangyang and Daesan, where industrial emissions are substantial. Higher toluene concentrations, originating from various sources (Kim et al., 2022), were observed in metropolitan cities such as Seoul, Incheon, Busan, and Daegu. MEK, associated with industrial cleaning and degreasing (Khalili & Nasrabadi, 2023; Luttrell & Bellcock, 2015), showed elevated levels in Yeosu·Gwangyang and Daegu, where steel and fiber/machinery industries are active, respectively. Naphthalene, emitted from various anthropogenic sources, exhibited relatively high concentrations in Gwangju and Ulsan. In Incheon, where small- and medium-sized industrial facilities are present, ethyl acetate, a marker of primary industrial sources (Li et al., 2016), was observed at higher levels. This compound may also have been transported to the city, which is geographically close to China and North Korea, due to its relatively low atmospheric reactivity (Zhu et al., 2018). Overall, these comparisons suggest that VOC emissions in the study area were predominantly influenced by industrial solvents and fuel combustion, and that the major emitted VOCs varied depending on each city’s characteristics.

Spatial distributions of VOC levels and their emissions

Spatial distributions of Σ59 VOCs and individual VOC groups at each sampling site were visualized using the inverse distance weighting (IDW) method in ArcGIS 10.8.2 (ESRI, USA) (Fig. 3). Because the IDW-based spatial maps in this study were derived from only seven sampling sites, they may contain interpolation artifacts and should be interpreted as qualitative visualizations of general spatial patterns rather than as precise quantitative estimates. The highest concentrations of Σ59 VOCs (139.3 µg/m3) and the aromatic group (93.9 µg/m3) were observed at Site 1, followed by Site 2 (79.2 and 56.2 µg/m3, respectively), showing similar spatial patterns (Fig. 3a and 3b). At Site 1, markedly elevated concentrations of TEX (38.4, 14.4, and 29.1 µg/m3, respectively) and 1,2,4-TMB (5.46 µg/m3), which belong to the aromatic group, were observed. These compounds are typically associated with coating, painting, and printing activities (Choi et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025). Direct emission sources, including signboard, advertisement, and banner manufacturing facilities, were identified near Site 1. Concentrations of the halogenated and other groups were higher at Site 7 (4.81 and 18.1 µg/m3, respectively) and Site 2 (20.8 µg/m3 for the other group only) (Fig. 3c and d), although their levels remained much lower than those of the aromatic group. At Site 7, 1,2-dichloropropane (3.02 µg/m3) and MEK (8.42 µg/m3) contributed substantially to the halogenated and other groups, respectively. These compounds are typically emitted from industrial cleaning and degreasing processes (Khalili & Nasrabadi, 2023; Lan et al., 2014). At Site 2, major contributors in the other group included MEK (3.74 µg/m3), acrolein (2.55 µg/m3), MIBK (2.43 µg/m3), methyl methacrylate (MMA, 2.36 µg/m3), MTBE (2.26 µg/m3), and ethyl acetate (2.11 µg/m3). These elevated concentrations likely originated from (1) emissions of MEK, MIBK, and MMA from solvent usage (Du et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2019; Li et al., 2023) and (2) emissions of acrolein, MTBE, and ethyl acetate from fuel combustion sources (Gao et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2025; You et al., 2024). Geographically, the main automobile industrial complex is located north of Site 2, and VOCs emitted from manufacturing processes may have been transported by prevailing northerly winds (Fig. S4a). Other VOCs at Site 2 may have originated from nearby fuel combustion sources, including industrial activities and ship operations at the harbor within 1 km. Overall, the spatial distributions of total VOCs and the aromatic group (Figs. 3a and b) were generally comparable, with hotspots observed at Sites 1 and 2, whereas those of the halogenated and other groups (Figs. 3c and 3d) showed distinct spatial patterns reflecting their dominant source types, such as cleaning/degreasing processes and fuel combustion activities.

Spatial distributions of (a) total (Σ59), (b) aromatic, (c) halogenated, and (d) other groups across the study area

Individual spatial distributions were also mapped for the top 28 VOC species with mean concentrations higher than 0.1 µg/m3 (Fig. S5). Overall, hotspots for most compounds were observed at Sites 1 and 2, where the highest Σ59 VOC concentrations were measured. Site 3 exhibited relatively high concentrations of petrochemical indicators such as styrene, cyclohexane, 1,2-dichloroethane, and 1,3-butadiene (Gu et al., 2022; Le et al., 2024; Qin et al., 2022). MEK and 1,2-dichloropropane appeared to be associated with sources near Site 7, as previously discussed. Distinct spatial patterns were observed for isopropyl alcohol and carbon tetrachloride (CTC), with elevated concentrations at Site 4, located closer to urban areas. Although these VOCs are primarily emitted from industrial sources, minor contributions from commercial products (e.g., cleaning agents and rubbing alcohol) are also possible (Cohen et al., 2023; Nored et al., 2024). Based on the locations of major industrial complexes (Fig. 1) and the spatial distributions shown in Fig. 3 and Fig. S5, different industrial sectors in the study area appeared to emit distinct VOC profiles. Solvent-related activities in the automobile and shipbuilding industrial complexes were mainly associated with elevated aromatic VOCs such as toluene, ethylbenzene, and xylenes, whereas petrochemical industries were more strongly linked to styrene, cyclohexane, and 1,2-dichloroethane. In contrast, cleaning and degreasing activities near the non-ferrous industrial complex appeared to contribute to elevated concentrations of MEK and halogenated VOCs. However, source-specific emission patterns among industrial sectors may not always be clearly distinguishable, as the major industrial complexes are located in proximity and are spatially mixed within the limited industrial area of Ulsan.

To further identify industrial impacts on sampling sites, emissions of toxic chemicals from nearby industrial facilities were examined using PRTR data for 2020. Figure 4 shows the locations of the sampling sites and industrial facilities within radii of 0.5, 1, 2, and 3 km. Toxic chemical emissions from facilities within each radius were compared with measured VOC concentrations at each site. For example, at Site 1, five VOCs (n = 5) were both measured and reported as emissions from facilities within 0.5 km, and correlation analysis yielded r = 0.10. All correlation results are presented in Fig. S6.

Locations of sampling sites and industrial facilities emitting toxic chemicals within radii of (a) 0.5, (b) 1, (c) 2, and (d) 3 km from each site. Emission data were obtained from the PRTR system for the year 2020

Significant Spearman correlations (p < 0.05) were observed at Site 3 within 3 km (r = 0.54), Site 5 within 2 km (r = 0.83), Site 6 within 3 km (r = 0.60), and Site 7 within 3 km (r = 0.62) (Fig. S6). Consistent with the spatial distributions shown in Fig. 3, significant correlations between measured VOC concentrations and PRTR emissions were mainly observed for industrial facilities located within 3 km of the sampling sites (Fig. 4), suggesting that nearby industrial sources within this range substantially influenced VOC levels at the sampling site. No significant correlations were found within 0.5 or 1 km, likely due to the limited number of nearby facilities. Site 4, located farthest from industrialized areas, showed no statistical correlation. Interestingly, Sites 1 and 2, despite exhibiting elevated VOC concentrations, did not show strong correlations with PRTR data, indicating the presence of other unreported sources such as ship emissions or small-scale industrial activities. This result highlights a limitation of the PRTR system, which only includes facilities emitting 1 tonne or more of toxic chemicals per year. Nevertheless, PRTR data remain useful, as demonstrated in previous studies showing that spatial distributions of BTEX from passive air samples were consistent with PRTR-based emission patterns (Kim et al., 2024).

Identification of VOC sources using PCA and diagnostic ratios

Detailed factor loadings from the PCA of passive air samples are presented in Table S5. PC1, PC2, and PC3 explained 80.1% of the total variance (38.4, 25.9, and 15.8%, respectively). PC1 exhibited high loadings for trichloroethylene (TCE, 0.73), MMA (0.90), MIBK (0.89), m,p-xylenes (0.75), o-xylene (0.86), 4-ethyltoluene (0.86), 1,3,5-TMB (0.87), and 1,2,4-TMB (0.88) (Table S5), which are primarily associated with solvent usage (Du et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2019; Li et al., 2020). Notably, the PRTR data indicate that large amounts of m,p,o-xylenes are emitted from automobile- and shipbuilding-related industrial areas, which are characterized by extensive solvent use. PC2 was dominated by MTBE (0.81), ethyl acetate (0.64), CTC (0.76), TCE (0.68), and methyl butyl ketone (MBK) (0.72), with moderately high loadings for acrolein (0.55) and benzene (0.48) (Table S5). CTC and TCE are indicative of metal and machinery cleaning/degreasing processes (Doherty, 2000), while the other VOCs are linked to fuel combustion emissions (Boniardi et al., 2023; Gao et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2025). VOCs in this PC may also originate from railway, marine vessel, and road transport activities within the petrochemical industrial complex (You et al., 2024). PC3 appeared to reflect petrochemical refining activities, with high loadings for 1,2-dichloroethane (0.69), n-heptane (0.60), and styrene (0.78) (Table S5). Styrene, in particular, showed an elevated concentration (6.35 µg/m3 at Site 3; Fig. S5) and large reported emissions (34 tonne/yr) in the PRTR system. Overall, PCA identified solvent usage (PC1) as the dominant VOC source, followed by fuel combustion and cleaning/degreasing (PC2) and petrochemical refining (PC3).

Loading and score plots were used to illustrate the influence of VOCs on each sampling site, grouping them along PC1 (X-axis), PC2 (Y-axis), and PC3 (Z-axis) (Fig. 5). Five distinct clusters were identified via hierarchical cluster analysis: Group 1 (yellow), Group 2 (blue), Group 3 (green), Group 4 (red), and Group 5 (black) (Fig. S7). Groups 1 and 2 were primarily associated with solvent usage, characterized by TEX, TCE, MIBK, MMA, and heavy hydrocarbons (e.g., 4-ethyltoluene, 1,3,5-TMB, and 1,2,4-TMB) (Gu et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2019; Li et al., 2020). As discussed in Section "Spatial distributions of VOC levels and their emissions", Site 1 was likely affected by signage and banner production from nearby facilities, while Site 2 was influenced by emissions from automobile and shipbuilding processes, transported by prevailing northerly winds (Fig. S4a). Elevated acrolein levels also suggest contributions from ship operations (Stroud et al., 2015; Xu et al., 2025) and nearby chemical production. A previous study reported substantial emissions of VOCs, NOX, and SOX from line sources along the eastern coastline of Ulsan (Kim et al., 2023). Group 3 was strongly linked to Site 3, located within the petrochemical industrial complex, where high concentrations of n-heptane, styrene, 1,2-dichloroethane, and cyclohexane were observed (Fig. S5). Group 4 was associated with Site 7 and cleaning/degreasing indicators such as MEK and 1,2-dichloropropane. Group 5 reflected mixed sources affecting Sites 4, 5, and 6, likely from a combination of commercial and industrial activities. Overall, the PCA results suggest that VOC levels at the sampling sites were more strongly influenced by local industrial activities than by regional or long-range atmospheric transport.

Three-dimensional PCA results: (a) score and (b) loading plots. PCs 1, 2, and 3 explained 38.4%, 25.9%, and 15.8% of the total variance, respectively. Five clusters (Groups 1–5) were identified by hierarchical cluster analysis and are shown in yellow, blue, green, red, and black

In addition to PCA, diagnostic ratios, specifically toluene/benzene (T/B) and m,p-xylenes/benzene (X/B), were used (Fig. S8). The T/B ratio is commonly applied to distinguish traffic-related VOC sources from non-traffic sources, while the X/B ratio assesses atmospheric aging, reflecting differences in the atmospheric lifetimes of the compounds (Sarmiento et al., 2023; Yan et al., 2017). As shown in Fig. S8, Site 1 exhibited a strong non-traffic influence, with a T/B ratio of 13.7, exceeding the threshold of 10 (Le et al., 2023). All other sites had T/B ratios above 2, indicating non-traffic sources (Zhang et al., 2025). This finding suggests that non-traffic sources were the dominant contributors in the study area. Using an alternative criterion (T/B = 1.5–4.3) (Hosseini et al., 2023), traffic sources were more pronounced at Sites 4, 5, and 6, which are closer to urban areas, while Sites 1, 2, and 7 were dominated by industrial sources. Unexpectedly low T/B and X/B ratios at Site 3 may be attributed to direct benzene emissions reported in the PRTR system for 2020 (Fig. S9), under prevailing northerly winds (Fig. S4a). X/B ratios at Sites 1 and 2 indicated a dominant influence of fresh emissions from the industrial complex, where Σ59 VOC concentrations were much higher (Fig. 3). In contrast, aged signatures were observed along the boundaries of the industrial complexes (Sites 4, 5, 6, and 7), except for Site 3. These diagnostic ratio results reinforce that non-traffic sources are the primary contributors to VOC pollution in the study area and that emissions may substantially affect nearby small residential communities adjacent to the industrial complexes.

Risk assessment of acute and chronic exposure

HQs for 23 VOCs from acute inhalation exposure at each sampling site and their fractional contributions are presented in Table 1 and Fig. 6, respectively. Naphthalene was the only compound exceeding the non-cancer risk threshold (HQ = 1.0) (US EPA, 1991), with a mean HQ of 2.1E + 0 (Table 1). Elevated naphthalene fractions were observed at all sites, ranging from 84.9% to 93.3% (Fig. 6a). Sites 4, 5, and 6 exhibited lower HQs for naphthalene compared to other sites, where industrial sources were more prominent. Strong industrial emissions of naphthalene have been reported in previous studies, which also identified elevated associated risks at industrial sites (Griego et al., 2008; Sucker et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2024). Other VOCs, including acrolein (mean: 1.4E-1), benzene (6.9E-2), chloroform (2.2E-2), and tetrachloroethene (PCE) (6.6E-3), did not exceed the threshold (Table 1) but still pose potential health risks. Acrolein and chloroform may affect the respiratory system, while benzene and PCE are linked to immunological and neurological effects, respectively (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP). Higher risks of acrolein and benzene have frequently been observed in previous studies on VOCs (Liu et al., 2023; Peng et al., 2025; Tan et al., 2024). Fractional contributions excluding naphthalene are shown in Fig. 6b. Site 2, near the harbor, exhibited the highest HQ for acrolein, while Site 7 showed elevated risks for chloroform and PCE, likely due to cleaning/degreasing activities near the non-ferrous industrial complex (Huang et al., 2014). Site 1 exhibited relatively high HQs for toluene and m,p,o-xylenes, indicating a strong influence from industrial solvents. At other sites, fractional contributions of individual VOCs were generally comparable. These results suggest that acute exposure to non-carcinogenic VOCs may pose health risks, and that key contributors, such as industrial activities and fuel combustion, should be effectively managed in Ulsan, a heavily industrialized city.

Fractional contributions of non-cancer risks for (a) all 23 VOCs and (b) 22 VOCs excluding naphthalene at each passive air sampling site

Tables S6 and S7 present cancer and non-cancer risks from chronic exposure at each site, based on the same concentration data used for acute risk assessment. Chronic risk estimates are generally preferred in public health evaluations (Yost et al., 2021). However, the US EPA (1992) recommended that appropriate inference is required when long-term exposure assessments are conducted using short-term monitoring data. Despite the short-term passive sampling used in this study, this approach was justified by previous studies reporting relatively consistent VOC concentrations and emission patterns across multiple seasons and years at industrial complexes in Ulsan (Kim et al., 2019, 2023), as well as by PRTR and CAPSS data showing that emissions of total VOCs and toxic chemicals (mostly VOCs) remained relatively stable from 2008 to 2020. Nevertheless, chronic risk estimates derived from short-term measurements should still be interpreted with caution. Among the 14 carcinogens assessed, naphthalene exhibited the highest mean cancer risk (7.4E-7), followed by benzene (5.1E-7), 1,2-dichloroethane (1.3E-7), chloroform (8.1E-8), 1,3-butadiene (7.4E-8), and CTC (6.8E-8). For the 36 non-carcinogens, acrolein had the highest mean HQ (9.5E-2), followed by naphthalene (2.0E-2), 1,2-dichloropropane (1.4E-2), TCE (1.1E-2), and m,p-xylenes (8.3E-3). Unlike acute exposure, none of the chronic cancer or non-cancer risks exceeded the cancer (1.0E-6) and non-cancer (1.0) risk thresholds (US EPA, 1991). However, chronic cancer risks associated with naphthalene at Sites 1 and 2 (both 1.2E-6) slightly exceeded the benchmark value, although they remained well below the tolerable risk level of 1.0E-4 (US EPA, 1991). This finding indicates that naphthalene may play a relatively important role in both acute and chronic health risks in the study area, potentially originating from fuel combustion, petrochemical activities, and solvent-related industrial processes near Sites 1 and 2, as reported previously (Sun et al., 2024). Although several VOCs (e.g., naphthalene, acrolein, and benzene) are commonly associated with both vehicular emissions and industrial activities, the sampling sites were located in small residential areas with relatively low traffic volumes and were situated more than approximately 5 km from major roads, suggesting that industrial emissions were likely the dominant VOC source in the study area. Furthermore, the risk estimates in this study are highly dependent on the HGVs provided by different agencies, which are subject to uncertainty due to extrapolations from toxicological and epidemiological data with varying safety factors. Although acute risks were estimated using values from the ATSDR, it is important to interpret risk levels not only in comparison to US EPA thresholds but also in the context of multiple exposure durations (acute, subchronic, and chronic) and diverse HGV sources.

To assess the effects of individual VOCs on specific human organ systems, hazard indices (HIs) were calculated for 5 target systems: respiratory (8 VOCs), immunological (1 VOC), neurological (10 VOCs), ocular (2 VOCs), and developmental (2 VOCs), as listed in Table 1. The mean HI for the respiratory system (2.3E + 0) exceeded the US EPA threshold of 1.0, whereas HIs for the other systems remained within acceptable levels. Naphthalene was the dominant contributor to respiratory risk, accounting for 94.4–98.0% of the total organ-specific HIs, followed by the immunological system (3.3 ± 1.1%), primarily driven by acute exposure to benzene (Fig. S10). These findings emphasize potential acute non-cancer risks to the respiratory system in this study area, although uncertainties remain due to the exclusion of additional VOCs that may also pose health risks.

Conclusion

Air samples were collected using passive air samplers at 7 sites in Ulsan, one of the most industrialized cities in South Korea, to assess VOC levels and associated health risks in small residential areas near industrial complexes. Among the VOC groups, aromatic compounds showed the highest concentrations, with TEX being the dominant species among the 59 VOCs analyzed. Σ59 VOCs and the aromatic group exhibited similar spatial distributions, indicating a strong influence of industrial solvent emissions. Correlation analysis between emissions and ambient concentrations suggested that industrial sources within 3 km of the receptors likely contributed to VOC levels at the sampling sites. PCA and diagnostic ratio analyses identified local industrial activities as the primary VOC sources. Acute non-cancer risk assessment revealed that naphthalene exceeded the US EPA threshold, particularly affecting the respiratory system, while no other VOCs exceeded the threshold for either acute or chronic exposure. These findings suggest that even small residential areas located near industrial complexes may experience significant health impacts from short-term VOC exposure. Unlike many previous studies focused on urban centers, this work emphasizes sparsely populated communities near industrial zones, emphasizing the need for tailored VOC regulations and long-term monitoring to protect air quality and public health. Despite being based on short-term monitoring, the findings provide a scientific basis for assessing acute VOC risks in vulnerable residential areas.
