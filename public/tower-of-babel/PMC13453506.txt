2026 POSNA/EPOS Traveling Fellowship Experience
Authors: Benjamin W Sheffer, Verena M Schreiber, Rachel M Thompson
Published: 2026

Introduction

Every other year, the Pediatric Orthopaedic Society of North America (POSNA) selects and sends three of its members to serve as traveling fellows in various European cities. In addition to a robust exchange of clinical experiences and research ideas, this traveling fellowship offers fellows the opportunity to gain valuable insight into novel operative techniques, to see cohesive systems that better support patient care, and to develop transnational collaborations. Most importantly, it provides the mental space and time to “talk orthopaedics,” to explore big ideas, and to create meaningful friendships.

In the spring of 2026, the POSNA traveling fellows were Benjamin Sheffer of the Campbell Clinic in Memphis, TN, Verena Schreiber of Cincinnati Children's Hospital, and Rachel Thompson of Rady Children's Hospital in San Diego, CA (Fig. 1). Over 3 weeks, we shared in the opportunity of a lifetime as we traveled to multiple sites in the Netherlands, Belgium, and Sevilla, culminating in our participation in the European Paediatric Orthopaedic Society (EPOS) Annual Meeting. We had a wonderful time together and with all of our gracious hosts. We are deeply grateful to the members of POSNA, EPOS, and our European host sites for this wonderful opportunity. We submit this summary of our experience in the hope that it inspires the next cohort of POSNA members to seek out this incredible opportunity.

The Netherlands

After many hours of travel, we were warmly greeted in Amsterdam by our host, Dr. Melinda Witbreuk, and her generous husband, Remko. For our nearly week-long stay in the Netherlands, we stayed at their sunny lake house in Roelofarendsveen, just outside Amsterdam, with their lovely (albeit protective) dog, Leia. After a day to acclimate and relax at the lake, we were ushered to Dr. Ralph Sakker's historic home for a welcome dinner (Fig. 2). Dr. Sakker, who is currently serving as the EPOS president, had cooked all day to ensure we had a proper Dutch meal for our first night in Europe. The entire faculty of UMC Utrecht arrived, and we were immediately made to feel part of the family. We spoke openly about orthopaedics, life, and (despite our best efforts) politics. We tasted our first Stroopwafles, and they did not disappoint.

Dinner at Professor Ralph Sakkers', current EPOS president, house.

After this warm welcome, our academic journey began. Our first full day was spent at Amsterdam UMC, a large academic hospital serving adults and children. We attended a musculoskeletal infection conference where orthopaedic surgery, internal medicine, trauma surgery, infectious disease, and microbiology were all present to ‘run the list’. This first glimpse into the Dutch system was a perfect microcosm of the collaborative nature that epitomizes Dutch medicine—all hands on deck, all opinions valued. We then went to the operating room, where we observed a posteromedial release for a clubfoot relapse (Fig. 3). We had a great discussion about systems, specifically regarding the treatment of patients with clubfoot using the Ponseti method.

First day at Amsterdam Medical Center where we observed a relapsed clubfoot surgery.

We then visited a second Amsterdam UMC location to meet with the Motion Analysis Laboratory (MAL) staff, where we had a robust discussion on postoperative evaluation after lower-extremity surgery. We had the opportunity to learn about and experiment with 3D ultrasound (Fig. 4), dynamic ultrasound, and 3D reconstruction of foot CT scans. We discussed comprehensive foot and ankle reconstruction for neuromuscular deformity, how we can better assess surgical outcomes using MAL tools, and how to more accurately predict the effects of our surgeries. There was a great exchange of ideas and excitement about the possibilities of collaboration. We went to bed with our minds racing and our hearts full.

Example of 3D ultrasound reconstruction obtained at the motion analysis laboratory.

The next day, we traveled to UMC Utrecht, where, in addition to Dr. Melinda Witbreuk, we were hosted by Drs. Wouter Nihjus, Anne Spaans, and Arnard va der Zwan. Here, we had the privilege of observing a closed reduction of a dislocated hip using axial ultrasound to guide management, eliminating the need for intraoperative fluoroscopy or postoperative MRI. The orthopaedic team, in collaboration with a radiology technician, an attending radiologist (who showed up in the OR!), and a team of orthopaedic technicians, completed the procedure efficiently, once again demonstrating the team approach that defines the Dutch system. Afterward, Dr. Witbreuk demonstrated her technique for open reduction of developmental dysplasia of the hip using a medial window (developed between the femoral nerve and artery) to allow far-medial visualization, transverse acetabular ligament release, and capsulorrhaphy. Both spica casts were entirely waterproof, allowing for unimpeded bathing during the casting period. We learned that, aside from Ponseti casting, all Dutch casts are waterproof.

After our trip to the OR, we joined the team to visit the in-house 3D innovation lab, where surgeons bring their complex cases to engineers and work side-by-side to create custom solutions, such as cutting guides and/or implants. We then finished our tour of UMC Utrecht with a visit to the Medical Museum and a dinner hosted by Dr. Anne Spaans (Fig. 5). As before, the entire team was present, and we enjoyed a lively dinner, punctuated by orthopaedic and cultural discussion and debate.

Dinner at Dr. Anne Spaans' house.

On our third day in the Netherlands, we headed to OLVG Amsterdam, where we experienced firsthand the collaboration between the 3D lab and the surgical team. The first case of the day used a 3D-printed cutting jig to correct a proximal tibial anterior growth arrest (Fig. 6). The cutting guides reduced intraoperative uncertainty, enabling precise osteotomy location and magnitude with minimal fluoroscopic guidance.

Proximal tibia osteotomy utilizing custom-built cutting guides at OLVG. A) Computer generated printout depicting osteotomy level and plate placement. B/C) Placement of 3D printed wedge to give desired deformity correction. D) Final correction with plate fixation.

We were then given the chance to scrub in for some smaller cases. Thereafter, we were whisked off to see the famous Dutch tulip fields, which were even more stunning than we imagined (Fig. 7). We had another fantastic team dinner at Dr. Witbreuk's house, where we held an impromptu dance party with the entire Dutch team.

Tulip fields with Dr. Melinda Witbreuk.

The next morning, we took a cold plunge into the lake and spent the day exploring Amsterdam, where we enjoyed the Van Gogh Museum, a local artisanal/farmers market, and many boutiques. We stayed up well past our American bedtimes to experience European nightlife. We did some much-needed laundry and prepared for our next adventure in Belgium, though we feared that no team would live up to the educational and cultural experience we had just had with our newfound friends and colleagues in the Netherlands.

Belgium

After our “free day” in Amsterdam, we were picked up by our Belgian host, Professor Frank Plasschaert, who insisted on driving us to Belgium rather than taking the train. He gave us a lovely tour of the southern Netherlands and northern Belgium, with an impromptu stop in Antwerp to satisfy one of the fellow's historical interests (Fig. 8). There, we enjoyed the sights, the history, the cookies, and, of course, the beer (Professor Plasschaert lives in a historic brewery, so it was a must). After our quick stop, Professor Plasschaert and his wife, Margarite, dropped us at our first academic stop, Leuven, a quaint university town home to KU Leuven, the oldest existing Catholic University in Europe.

Antwerp with Professor Plasschaert and his wife.

We began our academic program at the Leuven University Hospital, where we were hosted by Dr. Anja Van Campenhout. We spent the day in the Motion Analysis Lab, where we participated in in-depth discussions of muscle pathology in patients with cerebral palsy, as well as the complications and functional implications of neurotoxin use. We toured their gait lab and learned about and experienced instrumented spasticity testing and their 3D ultrasonography (Fig. 9).

Instrumented spasticity testing at the motion analysis lab in Leuven with Professor Anja Van Campenhout and her team.

We discussed the team's upcoming work on biofeedback, including its use for pre-operative preparation and its effects on operative outcomes. We are very much looking forward to continuing to learn from this innovative team. Their intentional approach to caring for children with cerebral palsy and to evaluating their outcomes after intervention was inspiring, as was the Michelin-rated dinner the team treated us to that evening at Cum Laude.

The following day, we joined Dr. Armand Laumen in the operating room in Leuven. We had a busy day that included an all-soft-tissue medial patellofemoral ligament reconstruction (Fig. 10), a distal tibial osteotomy for angular growth arrest, and an open reduction with femoral shortening and pelvic osteotomy for developmental hip dysplasia. Once again, we were floored by the surgical ingenuity, the brilliance of the entire surgical/OR team, and the intentional design of the hospital and operating rooms.

MPFL reconstruction.

We concluded the day with a tour of the University Hospital, which was impressively large, particularly given how seamlessly it operated. We learned that the hospital employs over 10,000 individuals. Despite its size, the hospital system remained relentlessly focused on patient care and experience. The administration had invested in wonderful patient resources that are sometimes overlooked in U.S. systems under pressure from the bottom line and competing insurance systems. Dr. Laumen introduced us to a patient who had been admitted for French-style traction for a high-riding hip dislocation and then toured us through their very well-equipped rehabilitation floor, where they admit all of their post-operative patients for intensive therapy. This is particularly useful for patients with cerebral palsy, many of whom were happy to demonstrate their newly gained skills for us.

The following day, we were back with Professor Frank Plasschart in Ghent. Before clinical work began, each fellow gave a talk and shared ideas with the entire orthopaedic department. Dr. Sheffer presented on his institution's experience with pediatric orthopaedic trauma related to firearms. Dr. Schreiber presented on her institution's 2-day comprehensive system for evaluating ambulatory children with cerebral palsy. Dr. Thompson presented on her approach to emerging knee flexion contractures in cerebral palsy. Afterward, we joined Professor Plasschaert in the OR (Fig. 11). We observed another open reduction of the hip (adding to our compendium of European tips and tricks), a novel technique Professor Plasschaert had developed as a modification of the Shelf osteotomy to treat Perthes Disease, and a posterior spinal fusion for adolescent idiopathic scoliosis.

In the OR with Professor Frank Plasschaert where we learned the Belgian Shelf acetabuloplasty.

While Professor Plasschaert's breadth of surgical skills was breathtaking and we certainly learned many technical pearls, what surprised us most in these ORs was the small recycling bins we discovered at the back of the room (Fig. 12). The hospital sends all removed metal implants directly to metal reprocessing factories to reduce metal waste and recover rare metals. Moreover, they send all unsoiled sterilization wraps to a recycling facility that turns this waste into dashboard fabric for vehicles. Once again, we left the hospital with our minds full of possibilities and opportunities for improvements back home.

Recycling is important in European ORs.

On our second day in Ghent, we toured the Ottobock orthotic and prosthetic facility with Professor Plasschaert. We learned where 3D printing has improved efficiency and where hands-on craftsmanship makes the difference. We were privy to the extreme level of detail that goes into every orthotic, every adaptive device, and every prosthetic. We also noted that this was a labor of love for the craftsmen and professionals working there, and we were invigorated by their passion for their craft. In conversations with their leadership team, we learned that through direct interactions with patients, each team member appreciates what their work means to their customers. As a result, they take great pride in their work, and their products reflect this. We realized that the same is true in hospitals. Interactions with the end-user (the patient) give our work meaning and inspiration.

We then concluded our Belgian tour by taking the train to Brussels. Here, French was the primary language, and the culture was markedly different from that of northern, Flemish-speaking Belgium. Dr. Pierre Louis Docquier served as our host (Fig. 13). We joined him for their intake conference and for surgery, where we observed a distal tibial osteotomy for fibular hemimelia, a lateral ankle reconstruction, and an aneurysmal bone cyst excision/curettage of the clavicle.

OR in Brussels with Dr. Pierre Louis Docquier and his chief resident.

Beyond the OR, we had the privilege of joining a postdoctoral researcher, Cedric, who gave us a tour of the in-hospital bone bank. We discussed his current work, which focused on developing a more accessible method for decellularizing donated bone so he could replicate the process in developing nations that otherwise would not have the ability to do so. It was a pleasure to share in the excitement of a researcher whose goals were so pure. It was a perfect encapsulation of what we learned to be at the heart of Belgian orthopaedics - innovation driven by equal parts expertise and compassion.

Sevilla, Spain

The next morning, we flew from Brussels to Sevilla and were warmly greeted by our local host, Dr. David Farrington. We enjoyed a lovely meal with him, his wife, and his orthopaedic colleague, Dr. Norman Rodriguez of Puerto Rico. The next day was Easter. With time on our hands, we spent the day touring Sevilla with a hired tour guide who took us through Sevilla's history and how it shapes the culture today (Fig. 14). We experienced Sevillana culture at its finest as we roamed the city streets near the bullfighting stadium just before the season's opening day. The streets were filled with women in their finest heels despite the cobblestones, and men in their Sunday best. We treated ourselves to Spanish tapas and sangria in lieu of the fight, and we prepared for our first academic day in Sevilla.

Guided sightseeing tour in Seville with our hosts, Drs. David Farrington, Javier Downey, and our new friend Dr. Norman Rodriguez.

For the next two days, we met with Dr. Farrington and Dr. Downey and their team at IHP Orthopediatrica (Fig. 15).

Clinic day at IHP Orthopediatrica.

We spent both days giving roundtable talks on scoliosis, cerebral palsy, deformity, and trauma, learning from each other and from all our hosts. In the afternoons, we enjoyed long lunches followed by pediatric orthopaedic clinics. We were amazed by the team's efficiency and breadth of practice. While each surgeon had a unique clinical niche, they were all willing to pitch in and see any patient who sought care. In the evenings, we enjoyed all that Sevilla had to offer. Dr. Farrington ensured that we were able to catch a flamenco show, a blend of history and artistry that was reminiscent of Sevilla itself. Despite the hectic week that Drs. Farrington and Downey had as local hosts of the EPOS meeting, they demonstrated warmth and grace. We learned that you are never “too busy” for camaraderie, and perhaps this was the greatest gift we received from our travels.

EPOS

We began our EPOS adventure by attending the EPOS presidential dinner. There, we met Dr. Dror Ovadia of Israel (immediate past president of EPOS) and were reunited with familiar faces from our travels in Europe and from home. The meeting began the following day. There were many similarities and some differences between EPOS and POSNA. We noted a trend toward more “tip of the spear” research. There were fewer retrospective series and more novel concepts presented. There were fewer publication-ready ideas and more thought-provoking paradigms. In particular, we were impressed with the use of machine modeling to identify developmental hip dysplasia, as well as the results of UK-based multicenter randomized controlled trials that help answer our simplest clinical questions.

Throughout the meeting, we shared meals with old friends and new, including a dinner with the POSNA presidential line, a dinner with the entire Dutch pediatric orthopaedic crew, drinks with our favorite British surgeon, and a closing reception with some of the finest minds and greatest souls in Europe (Fig. 16). During the closing reception, we presented Ralph Sakker, the incoming EPOS president, with our institutional gifts, which he kindly sported at the party. We spent time with each of our hosts and thanked them deeply for the kindness that they showed us and the learning that they inspired. It was bittersweet as we were happy to go home to see our families and colleagues, but we knew we would miss the friendships that we made throughout our travels.

We had a wonderful time attending the EPOS Annual Meeting, enjoying time spent with the POSNA presidential line as well as respected and welcoming European surgeons.

Conclusion

Ultimately, the friendships forged were the heart of this traveling fellowship. We were given the opportunity to learn new operative techniques and interact directly with content-area experts, but we were also blessed with the warmth of our hosts and the friendships among us. It's rare to find and develop true friends in professional circles, but these fellows have found that the “magic” of this traveling fellowship lies in the bonds formed while traveling far afield together (and sometimes sharing a single bathroom).

Simply put, the POSNA Traveling Fellowship has been one of the most professionally satisfying experiences we have had to date. The POSNA/EPOS Traveling Fellowship was an unforgettable opportunity that far exceeded expectations. We thank our hosts and their institutions for their hospitality, education, and friendship. We thank POSNA for supporting this endeavor. We thank Teri Stech, Traci Russell, and the entire POSNA administrative team for organizing every detail of the trip and ensuring we made it home safely. We highly encourage all interested POSNA members to apply! (posna.org/resources/traveling-fellowship).
