ABSTRACT
Digital transistor-based general-purpose hardware (e.g., central processing units) is the dominant
solution to support both traditional computing (logic, arithmetic, etc.) as well as modern artificial
intelligence. State-of-the-art research has shown feasibility of post-digital physics-based
neuromorphic hardware, which is hypothesized to support artificial intelligence algorithms with
orders-of-magnitude improved time/energy efficiencies. But such research has not been widely
deployed mainly because of such novel hardware’s extreme application-specificity, and the
dominance of low-cost general-purpose (but inefficient) digital hardware. To make use of the novel
algorithms and the superlative performance of physics-based hardware, we need to identify scientific
principles that can enable generality in physics-based hardware. This work resulted in two important
broad outcomes – first, we demonstrate fully reconfigurable neuromorphic components, and
second, we demonstrate a viable artificial intelligence learning algorithm that can exploit the
functioning of neuromorphic hardware. We demonstrate up to five orders of magnitude
improvement in energy efficiency compared to the best general-purpose digital hardware.

1. INTRODUCTION

1.1. Motivation
Is our society set up to solve the most challenging and crucial computing problems of sustainability
within the twenty-first century?
The explosion in deep learning over the last decade has led to headline-worthy feats across various
fields, such as computers beating humans in the game of Go, enabling autonomous vehicles, solving
protein folding, and promising brain-like intelligence.1,2 Underlying these advances are two key
factors: increased budgets, and advances in semi-parallel hardware such as graphics processing units
(GPUs) and tensor processing units (TPUs), which are used to train artificial intelligence (AI)
algorithms.
Up until 2019, the computing capacity required to train the largest AI models doubled every ∼3.4
months (Fig. 1).3 In other words, an exponential growth. However, even when accounting for
continual hardware improvements, this growth rate would (hypothetically) exceed global yearly
energy expenditure4 by 2030. Additionally, these models pose serious environmental concerns
associated with their carbon footprint.5 Training one of the largest language models (GPT-3 in 2020,
which is the basis for Chat GPT) just once cost approximately USD 12 million, accompanying over
500 tons of CO2 (equivalent) emissions5-6. About 90% of training costs go into leasing the data
center infrastructure (which are essentially warehouses of GPUs consuming megawatts of power),
whereas ∼10% pay the electricity bills to run it.7

As a result of these massive costs, over the last three years, model growth has fluctuated and
signaled saturation. Both model sizes and computational costs have fluctuated since 2020.8 Thus, we
are already limited by slow AI algorithms, inefficient hardware and high costs, which will soon
become a prohibitive issue. So, is our society set up to solve the most challenging and crucial
computing problems of sustainability within the twenty-first century? Without radical innovations in
both AI algorithms and hardware, no.
In fact, to quantify this barrier, in our 2023 Commentary article (authored by Conklin and Kumar)
(Conklin et al., Nature Electronics, 6, 464 (2023)), we identified three representative problems of
differing scales that are crucial for our future within the next 100 years, but are beyond the reach of

today’s computers. For example, detailed weather modeling, simulating all the functions of a brain,
and a human evolutionary simulation (to predict how we will evolve across many generations). We
found that we need to improve the energy efficiency of our computers by 10 orders of magnitude.
We also concluded that we urgently need post-digital computing hardware, and appropriate
algorithms to work with, within the next 10 years. This thesis is the motivation for this work.

1.2. Overview and organization of this report
In this report, we will present three topics, each with detailed results and analyses.
The primary thrust, as indicated by the title of this report, addresses the construction of
reconfigurable brain-like (neuromorphic) components (single electronic devices). Why is
reconfigurability important? AI algorithms are now widely implemented on complementary metal-
oxide semiconductor (CMOS)/transistor-based general-purpose hardware (e.g., CPUs/GPUs).
State-of-the-art research, particularly from this team, has shown feasibility of non-CMOS physics-
based neuromorphic hardware supporting neuromorphic algorithms with orders-of-magnitude
improved time/energy efficiencies. But such research has not been widely deployed mainly because
of such novel hardware’s extreme application-specificity, and the dominance of low-cost general-
purpose (but inefficient) CMOS hardware. To make use of the novel algorithms and the superlative
performance of physics-based hardware, we need to identify scientific principles that can enable
generality in physics-based hardware.
Second, we will provide detailed principles on modeling neuronal components. While demonstrating
the construction of superlative neuronal components is an important first step, it is important to
know how to make use of them. Making use of them means including them into our complex
integrated circuits and being able to design around their operational principles. In other words, to
design circuits including such devices, we need their models. Such models need to be physically
grounded, but also light-weight by being computationally simple. We will demonstrate one such
model.
Third, we will present an algorithm that can employ such devices to train AI models. While building
good hardware and knowing how to put such novel hardware into our complex chips is a good
primary step, knowing how to operate them is an important secondary step. All our algorithms are
designed to work within the rules of digital hardware (usually highly precise, but slow). We will show
how brain-inspired algorithms can actually work well with brain-inspired neuromorphic hardware,
and lead to energy savings compared to the best digital hardware running digital-friendly algorithms.

2. ELECTROCHEMICAL INTER-PHASE STABILIZATION FOR
 STORAGE AND PROCESSING OF INFORMATION
Information here is compiled from an unpublished manuscript resulting from this project (Zutter et
al.), which is currently undergoing peer review. The published version of this manuscript may
undergo changes upon peer review.
Electrochemical intercalation of Li+ ions is the basis of modern rechargeable secondary batteries.
Recently, intercalation of ions, especially Li+, H+ and O2-, has been investigated as a mechanism for
analog information storage for in-memory computing. However, a shorted Li-ion battery rapidly and
destructively discharges. This underlying instability has made an electrochemical memory with
precisely attainable, stable and high-density information a challenge, thus constraining its
applications. Here we solve this challenge by encoding information within electrochemical phase
transformation, a well-established mechanism in primary batteries. We electrochemically inject O
vacancies into vanadium oxide to achieve phase transformations between VO2 and V2O5, stabilized
via phase separation and inter-phase boundaries blocking O vacancy migration. Using this principle,
a single integrated component exhibits both neuron-like nonlinear conductance and synapse-like
tunable analog resistance states with an unusually long stability of less than 1% loss over 14 years
under short-circuit. Surprisingly, we observe stabilized phase transformations at sizes from
micrometers to sub-20 nm. Therefore, electrochemically stabilized phase transformations, on the
one hand, could unlock post-digital electronics with stability, functional tunability, and scalability, an
elusive combination. On the other hand, it may offer a primitive for rapidly rechargeable and stable
secondary batteries with higher capacity and slower self-discharge.

2.1. Introduction
Digital information storage relies on purely electronically encoded binary states separated by at least
10kBT (~0.3 V) of energy to process information and typically ~10 V to store information in non-
volatile manner (e.g., flash memory). In contrast, analog information storage relies on quasi-
continuous states, defined by non-equilibrium thermodynamic and kinetic processes, accessed by
motion of electrons, ions, and domains in various heterogeneously integrated materials (kBT is the
thermal noise floor at temperature T; kB is the Boltzmann constant).3 Each of these processes (such
as Fowler-Nordheim tunneling in flash memories, crystal structure annealing in phase-change
memories, or frozen non-equilibrium kinetic states in vacancy-migration-oxide memories) pose one
or more fundamental issues that adversely impact performance (in terms of stability, scalability,
functional reconfigurability, etc.).1,2 Motivated by these limitations, there have been many recent
efforts to realize information storage by employing processes like those in rechargeable batteries
based on ion intercalation mechanisms3,4. In such systems, information is stored in bulk electronic
states tuned by electrochemically injected defects or dopant ions, enabling near-ideal programming
of analog states by injecting precise quantities of desirable dopants in devices that are fast,
dimensionally scalable, and compatible with Si CMOS fabrication.
While attractive for energy efficient neural network training, electrochemical memories, also referred
to as electrochemical random access memory (ECRAM), have yet to demonstrate both fast and
scalable analog state programming, and state retention that is stable over a long term. This is not
surprising, since a programmed ECRAM, like any electrochemical system that is not in
thermodynamic equilibrium, will rapidly discharge its stored energy (or information) if the anode and
cathode are shorted (see Figure 1A).5 This behavior, driven by a gradient in the chemical potential of
the mobile ions between the electrodes, points to a fundamental limitation on the stability of storing
information in ion intercalation systems. Indeed, demonstration of ECRAM with analog state

 11

retention and programming speed characteristic on par with commercial digital nonvolatile memory
like flash (~10 years retention, microsecond programming speed, at 360 K) has remained elusive.
For example, ECRAM based on Li vacancy doping of LiCoO2 or proton doping of WO3 exhibited
retention times on the order of ~100s of seconds (at room temperature).6,7 ECRAM with improved
retention (~10 years at 360 K) was recently reported by Kim et al. for O vacancy tuning of WO3-x,
though these devices required long programming times (~30 s) while also requiring heating during
programing to over 470 K using a stage heater8. The authors hypothesized phase separation as an
underlying mechanism, where the formation of distinct, thermodynamically stable WOx phases in
the channel and the reservoir electrodes eliminated the chemical potential driving force for state loss
(self-discharge); however, no physical evidence for phase separation was presented. While phase
separation to reduce chemical potential difference is likely an important factor for long retention, it
alone is insufficient to explain the much shorter retention time reported for ECRAM based on
anatase (TiO2) tuned with Li-ions (where phase separation was observed) or tuned with O
vacancies.9,10
In this work, we first identify the key mechanism to achieve long-term analog state stabilization –
phase separation with a high kinetic barrier for phase transformation. We then demonstrate tuning
of these states in a manner that is fast and scalable for realistic computing applications. To achieve
this outcome, we employ VOx, the composition of which is tuned by insertion of O vacancies via
yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ) within multi-layered electrochemically gated devices with lateral
dimensions on the order of micrometers, including a microscale integrated heater. We validate our
proposed mechanism via transmission electron microscopy, first principles calculations and various
operando probes: the coexistence of two phases (VO2 and V2O5) that define an analog state that
becomes non-volatile by formation of V14O6 at the phase boundaries, blocking movement of O
vacancies. Via this mechanism, the VOx electrochemical memory demonstrates tunable analog states
and extreme stability (with state loss of less than 1% over 14 years under short-circuit in ambient
conditions) that far exceeds the limits of traditional intercalation or non-stabilized phase coexistence.
We further show that, as the channel dimensions are shrunk to 20 nm (using a vertically stacked
structure), we continue to observe stabilized phase coexistence, indicating potential scalability of this
primitive. Owing to the stability and analog tunability, along with the presence of VO2, a Mott
insulator known to exhibit nonlinear electrical behaviors, within a single component, we realize
tunable synapse-like linear resistance states, as well as tunable neuron-like threshold switching and
oscillatory behaviors.

2.2. The strategy for long-term retention and cell construction
Compared to electrochemical energy storage, information storage and processing using
electrochemical means require integration of millions of devices, scaled down to less than 100
nanometers, all while achieving reliable electronic-state tuning at rates of ~109 Hz and endurances of
over 109 cycles, in addition to minimal energy dissipation and noise (that is, a high degree of
stability)22.
A trivial way of achieving retention in electrochemical memory is by disconnecting the external
electronic pathway (that is, leaving the cell under open circuit) (Fig. 1A). In integrated arrays,
however, solid-state switches like transistors are used to select memory elements, and these selector
switches always incur finite leakage.
A second approach to improve state stability is to increase the resistance of the electrolyte to ion
movement (Fig. 1B). During programming, while the high resistance in electrolytes can be overcome
by applying a sufficiently high voltage (~7-10 V), especially in nanometer-sized devices, the field-

 12

accelerated ions can degrade endurance due to damage, as observed for non-volatile flash-like
memories.
In considering an alternative third strategy to achieve electrochemical memory with long-term
stability, we were inspired by the long shelf life of primary batteries that employ conversion
chemistry (Fig. 1C). The shelf-life (or retention) of these batteries can exceed 20 years (under open
circuit), compared to rechargeable Li-ion intercalation batteries, which typically retain their charge
for only a few months at room temperature. Although used extensively for primary batteries,14 15,16.
implementing conversion chemistry for secondary batteries remains a major challenge due to its
slow kinetics, and various degradation mechanisms associated with substantial volumetric changes,
including pulverization and loss of ionic and/or electronic charge pathways17-21. We conjectured that
we could overcome these limitations in ECRAM, in two ways. First, unlike phase conversion in
batteries with roughly spherical particles with diameters greater than 1 m, thin films with thickness
below 100 nm are considerably better at accommodating the strains due to phase conversion and
avoiding pulverization (i.e.., the constrained volumes and boundaries prevent long-range
propagation of strains). Second, to overcome the kinetic barriers for phase conversion and thus
accelerate programming speed, we added thermal activation using an integrated microheater.
Integrated microheaters have already been used extensively for programming phase-change
memories including in commercial products, and have demonstrated endurance >1012 cycles, and
excellent energy scalability.13

We chose the vanadium oxide (VOx) system for our electrochemical memory for two reasons. First,
electrochemically driven reversible phase conversion between VO2 and V2O5 was previously
reported by Lu et al., who used a YSZ solid electrolyte and a Pt counter electrode to inject O-

 13

vacancies into polycrystalline VOx films.11 However, these macroscopic devices rapidly self-
discharged when short-circuited, likely due to the large potential that developed between the VOx
layer and the Pt counter electrode. We conjectured that improved stability could be realized by
replacing the Pt counter electrode with an O vacancy reservoir with the same nominal composition
(resulting in negligible potential between the VOx channel and the VOx reservoir). We were
additionally motivated to explore this system because of the Mott insulator-to-metal transition
(IMT) in VO2, which manifests as a neuron-like electrical nonlinearity. As such, we hypothesized
that electrically accessing the Mott transition in VO2 (i.e., at higher electrical power levels) may offer
neuron-like properties. Such neuron-like higher-power nonlinearities, similar to synapse-like lower-
power resistance, would be tunable as the fraction of VO2 is tuned. Such tunability would allow
multiple functions within a single component (Fig. 1D). We were surprised, however, that even
highly shrunk (~20 nm size) VOx electrochemical memory elements maintained their unusually high
retention due to interphase stabilized phase separation, and we could precisely and reversibly tune
the conductance and IMT characteristics.

2.3. Demonstrating phase separation
We fabricated planar, large-area devices consisting of a VOx channel (50 µm × 50 µm) and VOx
reservoir regions lithographically defined on a single-crystal YSZ electrolyte (Fig. 2A-C). The large,
exposed area allowed operando mapping of the channel and reservoir using optical spectroscopic
techniques. Channel conductance was tuned by biasing the reservoir with respect to the channel
while also heating the devices to over 470 K using a stage heater. To confirm the electrochemical
transfer of oxygen, we used time-of-flight secondary-ion mass spectroscopy (ToF-SIMS) to measure
the composition of the reservoir and channel electrodes for two VOx electrochemical memory
devices programmed into high and low resistance states (Fig. 2D). These measurements were carried
out after holding the devices in short-circuit conditions for several weeks (by shorting the reservoir
and channel) following programming of the devices. With positive (negative) bias applied to the
reservoir, the channel is reduced (oxidized), while the reservoir is oxidized (reduced). This data is
consistent with the illustration in Fig. 2C. Using this process, the room-temperature conductance of
the channel (measured after the programming process is completed) was tuned by up two orders of
magnitude (Fig. 2E) in a reproducible manner.

The oxidized state’s electrical behavior is consistent with the phase change in VO2, wherein the
conductance increases abruptly at around 340 K. Whereas, the reduced state’s behavior is closer to
that of a weakly conducting metal (or a bad metal). To understand the microphysical behavior
during electrochemical programming, we mapped the channel using Raman microscopy (Fig. 2F-G).
The maps were collected at Raman shifts of 193 cm-1 (the VO2 peak), and 147 cm-1 (the V2O5 peak)
as the channel was electrochemically reduced ((i) – (iv)). Most of the channel consisted of grains of
nearly phase pure V2O5 interspersed within the VO2 film, which confirmed the coexistence of these
two phases. Upon electrochemical reduction, the channel exhibited a monotonically decreasing
fraction of VO2 (lower Raman intensity). While the total V2O5 fraction also decreased with
reduction, in regions where the VO2 signal had nearly completely disappeared (e.g., in state (iv)),
there were islands of V2O5 remaining.
Consistent with these Raman maps, the electrical data in Fig. 2E indicate that the strongest signature
of VO2 (i.e., the largest switching ratio between the high and low resistance states) corresponded to
the largest fraction of VO2 (i.e., the most oxidized state). The reduced state of the component
exhibiting the behavior of a weakly conducting metal is not fully explained by Raman data. Notably,
V2O5 is an insulator at room temperature, and the finite presence of V2O5 in the reduced state may
produce bad-metal-like behavior, it may be pre-mature to attribute the reduced-state electrical

 15

behavior to V2O5 based purely on Raman data. Nonetheless, the Raman data confirm two processes.
First, there is phase coexistence among two thermodynamically stable phases of VOx (i.e., VO2 and
V2O5), and second, the phase fraction is being tuned upon electrochemical doping. The evolution in
the VO2-V2O5 ratio as seen by Raman mapping and the changes in the IMT measured electrically
were highly reversible.
Having established phase coexistence and long-term retention under short circuit in electrochemical
memory with large-area electrodes, we fabricated similar lateral devices with dimensions in the 1 µm
range. Such components allow easier sub-µm probing, for instance, using transmission electron
microscopy (TEM) and reduce the possibility that the extended retention is primarily due to the
longer distance required for O vacancy diffusion, Additionally, the IMT in VO2 devices with
channels on the order of 1 m can be triggered electrically, and such device have previously been
used to demonstrate neuronal functionality including activation function and oscillatory behaviors.
In these smaller components, the channel was ~1.5 µm long and separated by ~5 µm from the
reservoir. A micro-scale heater was integrated with the electrochemical memory (separated by an
electrically insulating layer, Fig. 3A-C), and used for local heating during programming. The electrical
resistance-temperature behavior observed for these scaled devices was like those with larger
dimensions (Fig. 3D), indicating a similar operating mechanism involving VO2. The short-circuit
retention in these devices (measured at elevated ambient temperatures, Fig. 3E) could be
extrapolated to about 1% of degradation at room temperature over 14 years. This retention far
exceeds that of similar electrochemical memory based on TiOx10. Notably, while Lu et al. reported
persistence of phase coexistence in the VO2-V2O5 system under open-circuit condition, under short-
circuit condition, there was rapid decay of the system into the V2O5 phase.11 Furthermore, similarly
fabricated electrochemical memories based on O vacancy tuning of anatase TiO2 had several orders
of magnitude shorter retention, ~15 min at 350 K. Therefore, it was imperative to identify the
microphysical mechanism underpinning the long retention we observed.

To study the sub-µm phase evolution during programming, we mapped cross sections of multiple
VOx electrochemical memory devices (both pristine, and those programmed to the reduced and the

 17

oxidized electrical states) using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Fig. 3F). While the pristine
component exhibited uniformity in contrast, the oxidized component displayed the formation of
two clearly layered phases, with the new phase (darker in contrast) emerging from the YSZ interface.
The reduced component exhibits a reduced fraction (thickness) of the new phase (relative to the
oxidized phase). As such, the phase with the dark contrast is likely oxygen rich. We further
performed electron energy-loss spectroscopy (EELS) to identify the compositions of the phases
involved (Fig. 3G). In agreement with TEM, the bulk of the pristine components were close to VO2
in composition (light blue in color). The oxidized component had formed an oxygen-rich phase
close to V2O5 in composition (green in color), which emerged from the YSZ interface, likely driven
by O vacancies being drawn into the YSZ (from the reservoir) during oxidation. The reduced phase
had a lower fraction of the oxygen-rich region (likely driven by O vacancies being injected into the
channel during reduction). In all three states of the components, EELS maps reveal a third phase
that was not apparent in TEM (or in the Raman data on larger components) – V14O6 (x ≈ 0.428), a
less studied phase of VOx. We confirmed the presence of this phase using energy-dispersive x-ray
spectromicroscopy and Kikuchi diffraction patterns. In the reduced component, V14O6 is much
thicker at the top of the cross section (i.e., where the electrodes contact the channel). The EELS
maps, since they are a very small cross section of the entire device, cannot be used to quantify phase
fractions or draw trends (unlike Raman maps in Fig. 2, which provided useful trends), but can be
used to qualitatively identify key processes.
Therefore, the EELS data qualitatively confirm the inferences from Raman data on larger devices
(i.e., there is phase coexistence among two thermodynamically stable phases of VOx, which is tuned
during electrochemical programming). In addition, there is a third and a less studied phase, V14O6,
which appears at the interfaces of the channel.

2.4. The fundamental origin of stabilized phase coexistence
The experimental data thus far did not directly address the fundamental reason for the stability of
this system relative to other previously studied systems with phase coexistence. For instance, in the
TiOx system, there have been observations of the coexistence of Magneli phases (Ti4O7 and Ti5O9)
in filamentary conduction memristors23 (in addition to phase coexistence observed in
electrochemically doped TiO210 and WO38). Similarly, in HfOx and TaOx memristors, there have
been direct observations of coexistence among closely varying compositional phases24,25. However,
these systems are not highly stable (with loss of information occurring within hours under increased
ambient temperatures, illustrated by Fig. 4A).
Our experimental data narrowed our quest to two specific questions. Why does the VO2-V2O5
system exhibit improved stability and what is the role of V14O6?

We calculated the energy of formation (Ef) of VOx and TiOx for varying x. Ef facilitates a
thermodynamic assessment of the stability of phase coexistence across all ground-state polymorphs
within a desired compositional space.26 Specifically, two oxides (with different stoichiometries) of the
same metal coexist if they exhibit similar Ef.27 For TiOx, as x decreased from 2 (the terminal oxide of
Ti), Ef monotonically increased (Fig. 4B). The change in Ef for Ti5O9 (x = 1.8) and Ti4O7 (x = 1.75),
relative to anatase TiO2, are 143 meV and 183 meV, respectively. These considerable energy
disparities promote competition between phases and support the dissolution of coexisting TiOx
phases. For VOx however, as x decreased from 3, Ef changed non-monotonically. In fact, the two
coexisting phases we are investigating, VO2 (x = 2) and V2O5 (x = 2.5), are separated by about 77
meV, coupled with the presence of multiple intermediary crystalline phases exhibiting smaller Ef
differences. This observation aligns with the intricate and diverse phase diagram documented for
vanadium dioxide28 and our data on coexisting VO2 and V2O5 phases.

 19

Phase coexistence being favored in the VOx system, by itself, does not explain the unusual stability
(and the long-term retention) of our components. To understand this issue, we utilized the
constrained ab-initio molecular dynamics method (cAIMD) to calculate the free energy barrier (ΔA‡)
required for O vacancy diffusion at 500 K in the active oxide (VOx or TiOx). To include additional
constraints, we accounted for the energy required for O vacancies to diffuse across the interface
between the active oxide with YSZ, which posed minimal additional barriers (about 0.01 eV). For
anatase TiO2, ΔA‡ was calculated to be 0.68 eV, while for rutile VO2 it was 0.67 eV and for α-V2O5 it
was 0.79 eV (Fig. 4D displays an example of calculating ΔA‡ for rutile VO2, Fig. 4E compiles data
for anatase TiO2, rutile VO2, and α-V2O5). The calculated quantities are consistent with previous
independent experimental measurements [ref]. As such, O vacancies are equally likely to diffuse out
of both VO2 and TiO2, meaning that though phase coexistence is favored in the VOx system, its
long-term persistence cannot be ascribed to O vacancies holding the compositions of the coexisting
phases.
This stability may instead be explained by the presence of V14O6 (x = 0.428) in our components. In
Fig. 4B, Ef for V14O6 is 2500 meV above that of VO2. As such, V14O6 is unlikely to naturally form or
occur as a coexisting phase, which explains why it has not been studied much. In our components,
V14O6 likely formed upon thermally activated electrochemical dopant injection. While the formation
of V14O6 may require thermal activation, ΔA‡ in V14O6 is also large (about 1.26 eV, Fig. 4E).29 V14O6
is similar to metallic V in terms of being a weakly conducting metal as well as offering a large barrier
to O vacancy diffusion. In other words, O vacancies cannot diffuse easily. Since V14O6 appears to
form at the interface of VOx with YSZ (as well as the interface with the metal contacts, Fig. 3G),
V14O6 likely prevents diffusion of O vacancies out of the VO2-V2O5 system, thereby locking the
system into a persistent phase coexistence state, thus explaining the unusually long retention.
Further, in the reduced state, there appears to be a larger fraction of V14O6 occupying the interface
with the metal contacts in Fig. 3G (the top surface). This situation likely explains the bad-metal-like
behavior of the reduced state in Fig. 3D. Also, while the EELS map in Fig. 3G (corresponding to
the reduced component) displays a considerable part of it being VO2, the Raman maps in Fig. 2G
indicated a lower VO2 fraction (in the reduced-channel state). V14O6 occupying the top interface (as
seen in Fig. 3G) may also explain the Raman signal (from the possibly existent VO2) being blocked
by the metal-like V14O6 on the surface.
From the preceding experimental and theoretical analyses, a fuller picture emerges of the operation
of an electrochemically programmed device with stabilized phase separation, consistent with the
illustration in Fig. 1C. It is likely that the phase-stabilizing V14O6 (red-colored regions) forms at the
YSZ interface, the grain boundaries, and especially at the inter-phase boundaries. The presence of
V14O6 at the YSZ interface (on both the channel and the reservoir sides) likely provides the rate-
limiting kinetic barrier required to trap electrochemically injected ions. In the oxidized state of the
channel, a layer of V2O5 (green colored region) forms close to the YSZ in the channel, while there is
VO2 (light blue colored region) farther from the YSZ. In the reservoir, there is likely a region of VO2
close to the YSZ, with V2O5 forming farther from the YSZ. As the channel’s oxidation state is
varied via electrochemical programming, the interfaces between the different phases likely move. In
the highly reduced state, it is possible that the entire channel is bulk transformed into a composition
close to metallic V.

Using pulsed programming of a component, we were able to reproducibly program resistance states
across conductances separated by over 20× (Fig. 5D). Further, we were able to obtain 130 distinct
analog states over the conductance range of 83 nS to 277 nS. These states, under short-circuit
conditions, exhibited a degradation of roughly 0.1% in conductance over 1 hour at an ambient
temperature of 350 K (Fig. 5E). This retention is as good as the micrometer-scale devices,
suggesting that the underlying mechanism holds even at these small length scales. To confirm this
possibility, we performed EELS mapping of a programmed vertically stacked component (Fig. 5F)
and confirmed the coexistence of V2O5 coexisting with VO2-x following short-circuit conditions. The
EELS maps are consistent with the schematic model we derived in Fig. 1C, wherein, within the
channel, a layer of V2O5 forms near the YSZ interface, and VO2-x exists farther from the YSZ
interface. Whereas the reservoir is dominated by V2O5, with small regions closer to VO2 in
composition near the YSZ interface. We did not isolate V14O6, which is likely due to the much lower
cross-sectional region available for EELS mapping relative to the slightly thicker films in the
components in Fig. 3. Despite minor inconsistencies with the EELS maps in Fig. 3 (on larger
devices), the extremely stable coexistence of the two phases, the component’s long retention, along
with the other data and calculations presented above, indicate that a dopant-stabilizing phase is likely
to be involved. Also, the observation of phase coexistence in a 20-nm-sized system is remarkable
given the dominant interfacial and surface energies that plague energy storage systems based on
similar principles.
Not only do these components exhibit good retention across many low-bias conductance states (i.e.,
synapse-like tuning), they also exhibit neuron-like nonlinear electrical threshold switching (Fig. 5G).
Threshold switching is a natural consequence of the VO2 in the component undergoing a sufficient
increase in local temperatures (to reach the IMT temperature) at a threshold electrical power level,
leading to an abrupt increase in conductance. This observation also indicates the VO2-x identified by
the EELS maps likely contain a large fraction of VO2. Naturally, in the reduced states of the
channel, the threshold switching is not as strong as in the oxidized states. Thus, we are able to tune
the neuron-like behavior, which can be utilized for computing.
Threshold switching often accompanies instabilities that manifest as oscillations (e.g., when biased
close to the switching threshold, explained elsewhere). Our components also exhibited self-
oscillating behavior (Fig. 5H-I) upon biasing near the threshold. These self-oscillations were
electrochemically tunable, similar to the threshold switching being tunable. In some programmed
states, the oscillations were periodic (Fig. 5H), while in other states, they were highly chaotic (Fig.
5I). Chaotic oscillations are a fingerprint of amplified ambient fluctuations when biased near a phase
transition30.

2.6. Conclusion
We demonstrated a fundamental primitive that can thermodynamically stabilize phase coexistence,
which was informed by extensive experimental investigations at various scales (from 50 µm down to
20 nm), aided by various experimental data and calculations. In electrochemically doped VOx, we
show how two thermodynamically stable phases with similar energies of formation, VO2 and V2O5,
can coexist. Further, we showed that, in an electrochemically tuned component, by choosing the
appropriate starting composition of the VOx, a suitable dopant ion, and a suitable thermally
activated electrolyte, the phase coexistence can be stabilized by introducing a kinetically frozen

 22

phase, V14O6, which locks in the injected dopants that hold the stable phases. The stability of this
system is beyond traditional limits of stability in previously reported phase coexistence systems, as
measured by the electrical and phase retention of our components. As a remarkable insight,
stabilized phase coexistence was observed even when the effective free path of the dopants was
shrunk to 20 nm, which indicates promising size scaling for potential applications in densely packed
electronic chips. In addition, we demonstrated a qualitative proof of principle that not only does
stabilized phase coexistence enable good retention, but it can be a primitive that enables functional
reconfigurability of brain-like electronic components. We emphasize that our demonstrations here
are aimed at proving a scientifically fundamental process as a plausible primitive for processing and
storing of information, while performance optimization is a subject for following studies. Such a
primitive that offers a pathway to stabilized phase coexistence may be useful in various other
applications as well, such as high-capacity stable energy storage in rechargeable secondary batteries.

3. ELECTRO-THERMAL CHARACTERIZATION OF DYNAMICAL VO2
 MEMRISTORS VIA LOCAL ACTIVITY MODELING
Information in this section is compiled from a published manuscript resulting from this project:
Brown et al., Advanced Materials, 35, 2205451 (2023).
Translating the surging interest in neuromorphic electronic components, such as those based on
nonlinearities near Mott transitions, into large-scale commercial deployment faces steep challenges in
the current lack of means to identify and design key material parameters. These issues are
exemplified by the difficulties in connecting measurable material properties to device behavior via
circuit element models. Here we use the principle of Local Activity to build a model of VO2 / SiN
Mott threshold switches by sequentially accounting for constraints from a minimal set of quasi-static
and dynamic electrical and high spatial resolution thermal data obtained via in-situ thermoreflectance
mapping. By combining independent data sets for devices with varying dimensions, we distill the
model to measurable material properties and established device scaling laws. The model can
accurately predict electrical and thermal conductivities and capacitances and locally active dynamics
(especially persistent spiking self-oscillations). The systematic procedure by which we develop this
model has been a missing link in predictively connecting neuromorphic device behavior with their
underlying material properties, and should enable rapid screening of material candidates before
employing expensive manufacturing processes and testing procedures.

3.1. Introduction
One promising strategy to counter the end of Moore’s Law scaling is through the exploitation of
nonlinear dynamics in neuromorphic devices,[1] particularly those induced by the insulator-metal
transition (IMT) in VO2 and other thermal oxides[2]. Despite substantial progress in demonstrating
neuronal behaviors: action potential spiking, plasticity, potentiation and depression[3], the state-of-
the-art has not yet advanced to the point of designing neuromorphic circuitry beyond small numbers
of nonlinear components. This issue is partly due to the substantial difficulty and cost involved in
characterizing and modeling a single neuromorphic element, much less an interacting network of
such elements[4]. Furthermore, the crucial links between material properties and desired
neuromorphic behavior are only just being forged.[5] For example, it is understood that nonlinear
switching is required to induce sustained dynamical signals, but how large of an electrical switching
ratio is required, how hysteresis in the switching affects these signals, and how materials properties
like electrical and thermal conductivity affect DC power requirements, are all open questions.
Without answers to these questions, crucial design decisions like what material should be used, what
processing can optimize its electrical transport in what way, and what level of device or circuit
performance can be expected, become equally matters of art as well as science.[6]
The first difficulty of modeling networks of nonlinear dynamical devices has been addressed using
compact modeling, which aims to capture the behavior of individual devices across the full range of
relevant physical conditions within a small set of lumped model parameters.[7] Such modeling is
accomplished through a careful compromise of just-sufficient fidelity with physics-based intuition;
compact models are neither expensive high-fidelity molecular dynamics simulations nor naïve
empirical fits of current-voltage curves that change for each new data set. Instead, compact models
for memristors comprise physically reasonable transport models paired with relevant dynamical
equations,[7b] and the wealth of nonlinear behavior, e.g., negative differential resistance (NDR),
oscillations, and instability arises naturally from the interaction of these two ingredients. Chua’s
theory of Local Activity underlies the entire capability and limitations for compact modeling of
nonlinear dynamical systems[8]. Recent recontextualization of the Local Activity theory has

 25

streamlined its use as a design tool[5a, 9]. It is now understood that instabilities leading to NDR, but
not all instabilities, can produce sustained dynamical behaviors like self-oscillation. Furthermore,
experimentally observed dynamics may result from coupling a nonlinear device to an external,
potentially off-chip, component. Compact modeling must take account of the external
characterization instruments, as well as the device under test itself.[10]
Predictive compact models have now been constructed for several thermal oxide materials,[11] yet the
crucial comparisons for material selection have not yet been performed. What is needed is a
synthesis of the compact modeling framework with material-dependent model parameters in a
unified framework. In general, this is a costly proposition. Typically, only a handful of champion
devices are capable of desired neuromorphic behaviors and worth the effort of modeling.
Furthermore, the experimental effort required to characterize a device is costly, since both electrical
transport and the dynamical equations are dependent on one or more state variables, and one
generally attempts to capture this complete dependence. There is not yet a systematic roadmap for
what data needs to be collected when to construct the model, and model fitting often turns out to be
a lengthy exercise in guess-and-check tuning, as opposed to automated parametric fitting.
The difficulty of constructing material-based compact models can be eased by two realizations. First,
the interaction between nonlinear device and external circuitry can be subtle, e.g., the absence of
dynamical behavior may equally be caused by misalignment of the external circuit impedances as by
a damaged device. Experimentalists may have many more good devices at their disposal than initially
realized, if external degrees of freedom can be intelligently utilized. Second, a deeper understanding
of the Local Activity theory can help constrain the data needed to construct a compact model.
Ultimately, all that is required is a state variable, a state-dependent conductance, and enough
dynamical data to model the dynamical state equations. In this sense, steady-state NDR and
oscillations are not only evidence of desired dynamical behaviors, but also important constraints to
defining a data set for modeling.
Here, we approach the challenge of constructing a compact model for lateral VO2 memristors
(Figure 1a) directly, at each subsequent stage exploiting the collected experimental data to the
maximum extent to constrain the model parameters. Using in-situ thermoreflectance mapping under
steady-state electrical bias (Figure 1b), we obtain a complete set of steady state temperature-current-
voltage measurements, simultaneously observing NDR, instability, and oscillations. We synthesize
data sets for multiple devices with varying dimensions from across a chip, demonstrating sufficient
qualitative similarity to explain quantitative dissimilarity within the context of simple scaling laws. At
every step, we place the data within the context of Local Activity theory, using derived equations to
fit the data and parameterize the compact model using systematic least-squares procedures that
provide accurate values and uncertainties of model parameters, including inferring an external circuit
capacitance (Figure 2b, 𝐶eff) from dynamical data. The result is a material-dependent compact model
for VO2, fit systematically using automatic optimization routines, and verified against large sets of
quasi-static and dynamical data for devices of varying dimension. In so doing, we develop a first
effort towards understanding the links between electro-thermal nonlinearities and desired
neuromorphic behaviors, and simultaneously verify the principles of local activity as
recontextualized in recent theoretical work.

Several challenges and surprises arise along the way. First, the presence of a localized hot channel in
each memristor complicates the idealized compact model picture, which contains no spatial
coordinates and thus no variation in the physical variables throughout the device volume[12]. This
exposes an essential limitation of compact models and necessitates partitioning the device into intra-
channel and extra-channel volumes such that within each, a lumped compact device model applies.
Second, during electro-thermal characterization to develop the compact model, we discovered an
unexpected peak in the interfacial thermal conductivity near the martensitic M1→R transformation.
This quickly-varying thermal conductivity is the main source of apparent electrical hysteresis in the
steady-state current-voltage curve, which has been shown to mimic neural dynamics.[11b] This
discovery uncovers an as-yet unexplained physical mechanism that negates the contribution to the
thermal conductivity due to the increase in carrier concentration across the IMT.
Overall, our modeling methods demonstrate a systematic procedure to derive meaningful material
parameters from experimentally inexpensive quasi-DC 𝐼 ― 𝑉 characterization and oscillation data,
and our results reveal the essential material properties of VO2 / SiN devices underlying
neuromorphic behavior, namely, a rapidly increasing electrical conductivity coupled with a peaked,
rapidly changing thermal conductivity. This work lays the foundation for future studies in
engineered VO2 and / or other promising materials systems linking their electro-thermal properties
to local activity and desired materials properties, to eventually enable definite materials selection
principles for these applications.

3.2. Results

3.2.1. Material Characterization
VO2 film quality was first confirmed electrically through isothermal electrical conductivity
characterization of the insulator-metal transition (IMT) in lateral device structures. The IMT showed
repeatable conductivity switching of ~200 × across a critical temperature of 335 K (Figure 2a).
Large positive conductivity switching and the observed thermal hysteresis of ~10 K are in good
agreement with literature values for undoped VO2[13]. Devices of varying dimension across a chip
were characterized (Figure S2), with variations in switching ratios less than 10% between devices
located nearby on the chip, although the variation across the entire chip area was substantial (from
100 × to 220 × ). This suggests some nominal variation in material and device properties across
the 1 cm2 chip area, likely variations in elemental composition or in effective device volume due to
lithographic edge effects.
The presence of a crystallographic First Order Phase Transformation (FOPT) accompanying the
IMT was independently confirmed with temperature-dependent Raman spectroscopy (Figure 2b) on
a second device with the same width. The longer device dimension was useful to prevent the laser
spot from overlapping contact pads. The M1 monoclinic Raman peak at 618 cm-1 was observed to
decrease systematically on heating, then increase to the same level on cooling, indicating a reversible
structural phase transformation from the monoclinic M1 to the rutile R phase. The transformation
temperatures estimated from the Raman data, i.e., M1→R initiation and completion temperatures of
(329 K, 339 K), and R→M1 temperatures of (337 K, 327 K) agreed closely with values from
conductivity-temperature curves.

3.2.2. Quasi-DC Current-Voltage Characterization
Dynamical behavior in nonlinear devices, like self-oscillation and spiking, is intimately connected to
negative differential resistance (NDR) in quasi-steady state current-voltage curves.[5a, 9, 14] Continuous
current-sourced current-voltage (𝐼 ― 𝑉) sweeps were obtained for lateral VO2 memristors with
various dimensions across the chip (Figure 3) using a commercial high resolution source measure
unit (SMU). An important factor was that the current-sourced 𝐼 ― 𝑉 curves must be measured with
a limiter resistor in series before the device under test (Figure 1b and Figure S3), and different
limiters are required for different device dimensions. All data collection, including continuous and
pointwise 𝐼 ― 𝑉 sweeps, in-situ thermo-reflectance, and recording of self-oscillations was done using
the same limiter resistor for a given device. The reason behind this is a complete story in itself, with
interactions between electrical instabilities, self-oscillations, resistive damping, and the average device
conductance at a given bias point all playing a major role. For this work we will simply observe that
for every device dimension there was a small range of limiter resistors large enough to damp the
amplitude of extreme thermal self-oscillations while not suppressing them entirely. The 𝐼 ― 𝑉 curves
have been measured under this optimized condition for all the devices in this work.
Under these conditions, all memristors manifested qualitatively similar 𝐼 ― 𝑉 behavior, with a
positive differential resistance (PDR, as distinct from NDR) region passing through the origin
followed by a sharp cusp leading into a substantial NDR region (Figure 3). The NDR was followed
by a hysteretic box-shaped region, such that the drop in the voltage on the forward current sweep
(Figure 3, down arrows) was offset to higher currents with respect to the corresponding voltage
increase on the reverse sweep (Figure 3, up arrows). Obvious major differences among the 𝐼 ― 𝑉
curves for various devices were the slopes of the initial PDR regions (Figure 3, insets), which were
consistent with typical dimensional resistance scaling,

3.2.3. Simultaneous Measurements of Oscillations and Thermoreflectance
Quasi-static NDR is an inherent signature of instability and local activity, and under the proper
conditions can lead to electrical (and thermal) self-oscillation.[5a] In-situ self-oscillations during
continuous 𝐼 ― 𝑉 measurements using current sweeps were observed for all devices, independent of
device dimension, within their NDR region. In all cases, the oscillations were observed to begin
abruptly, at finite amplitude and frequency, right after the boundary between PDR and NDR, with
the oscillation frequencies increasing about an order of magnitude through the NDR region, before
abruptly ending at the sudden voltage drop marking the forward hysteresis threshold. On the reverse
sweep, oscillations were suppressed in all memristors up to the reverse hysteresis threshold. Then, if
the devices had a reverse threshold below the NDR onset, reaching the reverse hysteresis threshold
caused the 𝐼 ― 𝑉 state to jump back into the initial PDR region where oscillations could not occur.
For memristors for which the NDR onset was below the reverse hysteresis threshold, reaching the
reverse hysteresis threshold would cause the system to jump back into the NDR region, and
oscillations were observed just as for the forward sweep in the NDR region.
The dynamical behavior of a particular memristor was probed further using a second benchtop SMU
with better tolerance to repeated load switching at fixed bias (Methods) to make simultaneous
pointwise quasi-static 𝐼 ― 𝑉 measurements while recording the oscillation traces over ~1 million
cycles (Figure 4). Current oscillations were determined by dividing voltage oscillations across the
series limiter resistor by its resistance, thereby ensuring the oscilloscope would not suffer loading
effects from the rapidly changing device impedance (Figure 1). An important observation enabled by
the pointwise measurement was that both the voltage and current decreased after the forward
hysteresis threshold (Figure 4a, red arrow) from (3.56±0.01 mA, 3.70±0.01 V) at point A to
(3.40±0.01 mA, 2.39 V) at point B. In contrast to SMUs designed for continuous sweeping, which
can have arbitrarily small, ever increasing current steps, the benchtop SMU step size is limited by the

settling. The interaction between the overshoot and inherent instability at the forward hysteresis
threshold enabled observation of the simultaneous decrease in current and voltage after the forward
hysteresis. Each datum in the pointwise curve is an average of >4 triggered measurements over one
minute at fixed setpoint (see Methods).

3.3. Model Construction

3.3.1. Local Activity Theory: NDR, Nonlinearity, and Oscillations
Leon Chua has created a general framework for describing the behavior of nonlinear circuit
elements in terms of the dynamical feedback between one or more internal state variables and their
terminal current and / or voltage.[17] When such a framework applies, the device so described is a
memristive system, or more simply, a memristor.[18] A memristor compact model consists of (1) an
electrical conductance function that depends on at least one state variable 𝑥, and possibly, either
current 𝑖 or voltage 𝑣, together with (2) a state equation that links the dynamical evolution d𝑥 d𝑡 to
𝑥 and 𝑖 and / or 𝑣.
For transition metal oxides like TiOx and NbO2, good agreement with experiment has been obtained
using a 1-D generic electro-thermal compact model for which there is a single temperature state
variable governed by Newton’s law of cooling dynamics.

be stabilized. Hence, nonlinearity underpins all of these phenomena and is a major factor in desired
dynamical behavior.

and extra- channel volumes, respectively. Finally, assumption (v) follows from the initial resistivity-
temperature characterization (Figure 2a).

increase of carriers in the conduction band, we should expect these carriers to simultaneously
contribute to an increased thermal conductance, by the Wiedemann-Franz law. Second, the apparent
hysteresis in the quasi-DC 𝐼 ― 𝑉 curve is a signature of globally unstable, locally-active PDR, which,
if 𝐺el(𝑇) is monotonically increasing, requires from Equation 4 that 𝐺th(𝑇) must sharply decrease

inconsistency supported by the data is that the thermal conductivity does both: it first increases
sharply along with the electrical conductivity, then sharply decreases even as the electrical conductivity
continues to increase. This latter regime implies that some other factor negates and dominates the
contribution of the increased carrier concentration near the Mott transition. Recent work has
demonstrated qualitatively similar non-monotonic thermal behavior termed negative differential
thermal conductivity in a variety of systems, attributed to temperature-dependent negative thermal
expansion in in bulk Si homo-junctions, or electron-phonon scattering across a metal-dielectric
interface[21]. This opens the intriguing possibilility for future investigation that the non-monotonic
thermal conductivity in VO2—and its consequent locally-active PDR— may be caused by, and may
potentially be engineered by, the changing phonon transport across the VO2-substrate interface as
the temperature approaches the MIT.

In the language of Local Activity theory, the PDR region on the 𝐼 ― 𝑉 curves through the origin is

sweeps, and cannot lead to instability or oscillations. The NDR region is EOC, and it is stable but

with respect to voltage sweeps. The capability of the NDR region as a locus of stable steady states to

voltage sweeps. In this respect, each device passes through the sequence {LP, EOC,LA\EOC,LP} as
it self-heats along the quasi-static 𝐼 ― 𝑉 curve.

3.5. Discussion: Materials Properties as Neuromorphic Design Constraints
The analysis of Section 4 revealed that both the electrical and thermal transport of VO2 are highly
nonlinear around the IMT, and it is this nonlinearity that provides it with the capability for NDR,
instability, and oscillation. However, for materials selection and screening, it is necessary not only to
accurately describe electro-thermal nonlinearity, but to quantify it and understand how it directly
controls and constrains desired neuromorphic behavior. For example, NbO2 is another memristor
oxide with nonlinear electrical and thermal conductivities, but in contrast to VO2, its IMT occurs far
above room temperature, near 1080 K[22]. Near room temperature and far below 1080 K, the thermal
conductivity of NbO2 should be approximately constant, in direct contrast with VO2, for which the
thermal conductivity is a highly nonlinear, non-monotonic function. Well below its IMT, the
electrical conductivity of NbO2 is well-described by thermally activated transport[23], of the form

selecting a material for a particular neuromorphic application.
The presence of large coupled electrical and thermal nonlinearities in VO2 is also a double-edged
sword. The large thermal nonlinearity increases the overall nonlinearity, but this eventually creates
such a large instability that local oscillations are no longer possible; the locally unstable NDR
collapses and becomes globally unstable PDR within the hysteresis feature (Figure 10a-b). Although
the large negative thermal nonlinearity increases the slope of the NDR region, it simultaneously
creates a relatively low upper bound for NDR in terms of electrical bias, at which point the system
changes from useful EOC-NDR to LA/EOC-PDR. This means that oscillations in the present VO2
devices are only possible in the small temperature interval between 334 K and 336.5 K; whereas
Equation 20 (𝑇: Γ±(𝑇) ≥ 0) implies NbO2 can oscillate everywhere between 318.8 K and 1080 K,
at which point its large thermal nonlinearities at high temperature also induce unstable PDR. All else
being equal, especially device dimensions, this translates directly into a larger NDR region on the
𝐼 ― 𝑉 curve for NbO2. In this sense, VO2 memristors, or at least the undoped VO2 / SiN devices
studied here, have a more limited range of parameter space within which they can be biased to make
them capable for neuromorphic applications, as a direct result of their large thermal nonlinearities
near room temperature.

3.6. Conclusion
Using concepts and equations derived from the theory of Local Activity, we have developed a
systematic procedure for fitting a robust model to quasi-static and dynamic electrical and in-situ
thermal data. The procedure yields a compact model expressed entirely in terms of material
properties, natural device scaling laws, and a single inferred external electrical capacitor, which is
systematically fit step by step to a minimal data set. Furthermore, the model is informed by data
from multiple independent devices, and satisfactorily reproduces quasi-static and dynamical data on
an individual device. The electro-thermal characterization developed during this process agrees well

 58

with previous estimates of electrical and thermal conductivity and capacitance, and also points
towards as-yet unexplored thermal physics. In this way, the compact model is predictive and
falsifiable.
The systematic local activity-based modeling procedure has led to several important lessons. First,
the model’s general success for a variety of data validates the initial hypothesis that the wide range of
static and dynamic data produced by nonlinear Mott switches is ultimately due to the interaction
between fixed material properties, scaling laws, and dynamical equations. It is the interaction among
these that we vary when performing experiments (e.g., changing DC bias), but the properties and
equations themselves do not change. Essentially the same temperature-dependent electrical
conductivity is measured regardless of whether it is characterized with low-power, isothermal
uniform conduction or with high-power localized conduction, and we expect the same to be true of
the interfacial thermal conductivity, paving the way for future work with independent thermal
characterization of the electro-thermal properties.
Second, the model depends crucially on the Ridley bifurcation phenomenon, which partitions the
device volume into a hot channel and cool exterior volume. In this work, a specialized
thermoreflectance technique was used to directly measure the temperature and image the
morphology of the hot channel; however, it is unlikely such techniques would be available on an
industrial scale. This alone provides an impetus to further develop non-equilibrium steady state
thermodynamics to be able to predict the channel properties from first principles alone. The
difference in channel morphology between devices with different dimensions may be the origin of
the material-dependent compact model is not quite portable in describing other on-chip devices
(Figure S8 and S9).
Finally, we have made several initial steps towards understanding how electro-thermal properties of
VO2 / SiN devices contribute to their potential for desired dynamical behavior, and especially how
they might compare to other thermal memristors. The peculiar features of these VO2 devices are
extremely large electrical and thermal nonlinearities accompanying the FOPT, which are highly
coupled. This in turn leads to a steep NDR with a small critical capacitance that exists only over a
narrow temperature interval. In contrast, for NbO2 devices the electrical and thermal nonlinearities
are decoupled, and the electrical nonlinearity is substantially weaker. This leads to a shallow NDR,
but a much larger NDR temperature interval. In both cases, a rapidly decreasing thermal
conductivity plays a central role in creating undesired global instability and limiting the accessible
NDR region, and so understanding the physics underlying this thermal nonlinearity is crucial to
future development. As noted in section 3, the dynamical criteria determined by application of local
activity theory depend only indirectly on transport physics, intrinsic material properties, and device
configuration. Hence, the entire body of literature on engineering electro-thermal transport in
VO2— doping, defects, vacancies, strain, crystallinity, etc.— are all immediately relevant tuning
knobs for the targeted design of VO2 memristors for neuromorphic applications[24]. Furthermore,
vertically-stacked structures with higher device densities may be used equally well as the present
lateral devices; the lateral architecture is required here for thermoreflectance mapping. The only
criterion is that the resultant electro-thermal conductivity functions are sufficiently nonlinear in the
sense outlined in section 3.
In sum, we have used the principles of local activity to develop systematic procedures and principles
for both extracting electro-thermal transport properties from a synthesis of steady-state and dynamic
data, and subsequently analyzing the electro-thermal properties to understand their direct relation to
desired neuromorphic applications. With these advances in modeling and relating material to
neuromorphic properties, we have provided a major step towards systematic, fast, portable device

 59

modeling that contributes to the foundation for industrial-scale design and synthesis of
neuromorphic networks.

3.7. Experimental Methods
Lateral vanadium dioxide devices were patterned from a 100 nm blanket film grown on silicon
nitride substrate by pulsed laser deposition (PLD). See references[25] for additional details. Devices
were patterned into horizontal bars using a dry CF4/Ar etching process, then e-beam evaporating
100 nm of Al followed by 400 nm of Pd top contacts for top contacts to define device dimensions.
Raman shift spectra were taken using a commercial Horiba LabRam HR bench top confocal
microscope system, counting for 300 s at 0.5% intensity. Raman spectroscopy data from different
temperatures were aligned by subtracting the mean intensity between 540-560cm-1 (far from both the
SiN substrate and VO2 M1 peaks), then a small linear baseline that was fit to the highest temperature
(343 K) data was identically subtracted from the others. Slight shifts along the abscissa due to grating
zero position were compensated by setting the Si substrate at 520 cm-1.
Conductivity-temperature (𝜎 ― 𝑇) and continuous quasi-static DC current-voltage curves were
measured using a Keysight B1500A Semiconductor Device Analyzer equipped with Medium-
Current Source Measure Unit (MCSMU) cards. 𝜎 ― 𝑇 curves were generated by fixing the external
temperature with a temperature stage and pointwise averaging five linear 𝐼 ― 𝑉 curves before
obtaining an average slope. In-situ Joule self-heating was mitigated by using low-power (Vmax<150
mV), low-resolution (30-40 points) measurements with the SMU cards operated in high speed (HS)
mode for fast acquisition (0.5s per curve). This procedure was intentionally used in place of the
more typical method of applying a constant DC current, reading the voltage and computing the
ratio, since with that method, it is not possible to verify the measurement is taken under linear, low-
power conditions such that self-heating is negligible.
Continuous quasi-DC curves were obtained with the B1500A in high resolution (HR) mode using
long wait times (>300 ms per point) between setpoint change and acquisition, in combination with
long averaging times (>20 PLC = 340 ms), in order to mitigate the effect of transient and persistent
oscillations around the desired true 𝐼 ― 𝑉 steady states. Pointwise quasi-DC curves were measured
using a Keysight B2901A benchtop Source Measure Unit with >500 ms wait time and >500 ms
averaging. Each pointwise 𝐼 ― 𝑉 point was obtained by averaging >4 triggered measurements over
one minute at each fixed current setpoint. Persistent self-oscillations in the negative differential
resistance region were recorded using a Keysight DSOS104A oscilloscope with a calibrated active
N2818A differential probe.
Quasistatic device channel temperatures were derived from a Microsanj differential Thermal Image
Analyzer (TIA), with 10s averaging time and 20s cool-down time, after calibrating the reflectivity-
temperature curve. In every case, an appropriately sized resistor was placed in series with the VO2
lateral device before characterization, providing (1) a convenient constant impedance probing point
to avoid loading the oscilloscope and (2) damping of large electro-thermal oscillations in the NDR
region for which the thermal component otherwise fatigues or destroys the devices during
attempted characterization. The Microsanj necessitated measurement of thermoreflectance maps in
differential mode, i.e., synchronized between power-off and power-on states. This was accomplished
by using the Microsanj trigger out signal to control an electromagnetic relay, thereby periodically
load switching the SMU power output between the VO2 DUT during ON collection and a
peripheral 1kΩ resistor during OFF collection. Hence, the benchtop B2901A SMU that could
tolerate repeated load switching over long times at fixed bias was required.

4. ACTIVITY-DIFFERENCE TRAINING OF DEEP NEURAL NETWORKS
 USING MEMRISTOR CROSSBARS
Information in this section is compiled from a published manuscript resulting from this project: Yi
et al., Nature Electronics, 6, 45 (2023).
Artificial neural networks have progressed rapidly in recent years, but are limited by the high energy
costs required to train them on digital hardware. Emerging analogue hardware, such as memristor
arrays, could offer improved energy efficiencies. However, the widely used backpropagation training
algorithms are generally incompatible with such hardware because of mismatches between the
analytically calculated training information and the imprecision of actual analogue devices. Here, we
report activity-difference-based training demonstrated on co-designed tantalum oxide analogue
memristor crossbars. Our approach, memristor activity-difference energy minimization (MADEM),
treats the network parameters as a constrained optimization problem, and numerically calculates
local gradients via Hopfield-like energy minimization using behavioral differences in the hardware
targeted by the training. We use the technique to train one-layer and multi-layer neural networks that
can classify braille words with high accuracy. Via modelling, we show that MADEM can offer over
four orders of magnitude energy advantage compared to digital approaches for scaled up problem
sizes.

4.1. The spectrum of activity-difference training
Artificial neural networks consist of synthetic neurons and synapses that process and weigh signals,
respectively. The networks reduce complex and data-heavy problems to a set of tunable weights,
typically arranged in multiple layers to form a class of architectures known as deep neural
networks1,2. Most deep neural networks are trained using stochastic gradient descent (SGD) via the
backpropagation algorithm, which performs gradient-based optimization of the network’s weights to
minimize a loss or error function (the difference between the expected and the actual final outputs).
Backpropagation involves computing the gradient of the loss function, using analytical equations
that represent the hardware behaviour, at every layer backwards from the output to the input layer
(Fig. 1a-b)3,4. The calculated gradients are proportional to the required changes of the weights to
minimize the error function.
Backpropagation is well suited to digital hardware that can be easily modelled analytically, such as
graphics processing units (GPUs). This hardware can perform multiple operations in parallel and
offer high precision and simple reliable models1. Backpropagation-trained deep neural networks
supported on GPUs have greatly increased speed to solution for data-heavy tasks. However, the
high energy consumption and costs have exceeded the limits of most institutions, and thus progress
in training of larger and more complex models is slowing down5–10. By co-designing the computing
algorithms and architectures together with the hardware and materials, it should be possible to
reduce operational overheads and improve the latency and energy costs of training larger models by
potentially orders of magnitude8,9.

Alternatives to digital hardware — such as analogue resistance switching memory (memristor)
crossbar arrays — can offer large parallelism and energy efficiencies compared to GPUs for matrix
multiplication, which account for the majority of computations in deep neural networks11–13.
However, analogue hardware typically contains nonidealities such as spatiotemporal stochasticity,
device-to-device variability, and nonlinearities. Implementing analytical backpropagation gradient
calculations on this type of analogue hardware will lead to diverging mismatches and thus poor
training. While there has been some success in alleviating model hardware mismatches, they usually

require substantial overheads to better capture hardware behaviours (for example, repeatedly polling
the hardware to model its dynamical behaviour) or making the hardware being modelled more
predictable (for example, by using circuits to compensate for device nonlinearity and asymmetry)14–

  .
An alternative broad set of training techniques is based on gradient calculations via activity
differences24–29, which includes techniques such as equilibrium propagation30–38 and non-static
localized self-learning39,40. Activity-difference training uses locally available information to calculate
the required weight changes, without needing to explicitly calculate errors or gradients (Figs. 1c).
Typically, a network’s responses to two or more sets of different inputs, and possibly different fixed
outputs, are compared. Such a comparison is used to inform the direction in which each weight of
the different layers of the network is changed to make the network respond in a favorable way (for
example, to correctly classify images). Activity-difference techniques exist on a spectrum (Fig. 1a)
with varying degrees of localization of information, analytical (or numerical) nature of gradients and
variance of gradient calculations (or the probabilistic nature of the gradients). A part of this
spectrum consisting of energy-based training and non-energy localized training are hypothesized to
be biologically plausible, which inspired this work34. Given the dominance of backpropagation and
digital hardware thus far, nearly no part of the entire spectrum of local learning techniques has been
extensively researched, let alone being advanced towards scaled hardware implementations.
In this Article, we report on a specific activity-difference technique, memristor activity-difference
energy minimization (MADEM), simplified in its mathematical formulation compared to previously
studied techniques, and co-designed with suitable analogue memristor crossbar hardware. We
demonstrate the functioning of a discrete-time version of the MADEM technique on a chip that
consists of complementary metal–oxide–semiconductor (CMOS) digital control circuitry and two
64×64 tantalum oxide analogue memristor crossbar arrays.
We use the chip to perform a braille recognition task; experimentally, the memristor crossbar array is
used as a one-layer Hopfield-like network (64 neurons) and trained to reconstruct distorted versions
of nine names in braille. To illustrate the potential for multilayer deep neural networks, we create a
two-layer neural network, also using a Hopfield-like model, which was trained to classify inputs into
seven four-letter braille words. To illustrate the scalability of this approach, we model the training of
a one-layer 600-neuron (600×600 array) network and a three-layer network on a 706×706 array, and
use them to reconstruct full sentences in braille, and classify handwritten digits and letters from the
MNIST dataset, respectively. The MADEM technique using the memristor hardware was calculated
to outperform GPU-based approaches at small (64-neuron) scales by five orders of magnitude in
energy efficiency and nearly two orders of magnitude in latency.

4.2. The MADEM training technique
In MADEM (Fig. 1c), a randomly initialized network is fed training data as inputs in a ’free’ phase.
Once the information propagates through the various layers of the system and settles into a steady
state, the resulting free state 𝐴free is recorded. 𝐴free is a column vector containing the activities of all
the neurons (𝐴𝑖,free) of the system, including the input, hidden and output neurons. Then, the values
of the output neurons are ‘nudged’ towards the desired output by injecting bias signals at the output
back into the system, while holding the free-phase input fixed. After the signals from the input and
the bias from the output propagate in both directions across the various layers and a new steady
state is reached, the resulting nudged state 𝐴nudged is recorded. The Hebbian-like contrastive
difference between the free and the nudged states encode the weight changes (d𝑊) required to make

Temporal stochasticity is another inherent feature of analogue hardware, specifically memristive
systems, which has posed a challenge in their adoption.43 To address this issue, we employ a noise

4.3. Experimental implementation and scaling to deep networks
Our experimental implementation consists of a chip with multiple physical layers of foundry-
manufactured CMOS digital control circuits, topped with 64×64 crossbars of tantalum oxide
analogue memristors (Figs. 2a-e). The memristors were deposited via a back-end-of-line process in a
one-transistor one-resistor (1T1R) configuration. To enable energy-efficient performance of the
algorithm, we specially designed the memristor material composition to exhibit high-resistance (low

network of 64 neurons to reconstruct braille representations of the names of nine historical

computer scientists (Fig. 2f), with each representation containing less than 64 bits of information. In

required conditions for energy minimization, without which the system may produce non-
monotonic dynamics41,42. The aforementioned layout creates a bidirectional energy-based equivalent
of a deep neural network, which treats the weights of the entire network as a single large constrained
optimization problem. Here, the desired outputs impose the constraints and the network weights
represent the problem to be optimized. To record the free phase response, training data is fed as
input v1, with zero-mean neuron biases (i.e., no nudging or constraining the system), with hidden and
output neurons (v2 to v4) randomly initialized. Once the system’s feedback-driven Hopfield-like
energy dynamics propagate information across the layers and settle down, the resulting activities of
all the neuron states (u1 to u4) is recorded as Afree. In the second phase, during nudging, a bias vector
βT is applied only to the output neurons (v4), while the training data is fed as input v1 (v2 to v4 are
initialized to their free phase outputs). With each subsequent feedback cycle, the nudge information
is propagated backwards (i.e., from u4 to u1), while the input information from v1 is propagated
forwards. Once the Hopfield energy dynamics settles down, the resulting activities of all the neuron
states (u1 to u4) is recorded as Anudged. Thus, the process uses only locally available information to
calculate Afree and Anudged, without having to explicitly calculate and propagate gradients for
minimizing errors. The sparse and duplicated Hopfield-like representation of deep neural networks
are presented for ease of analysis and illustration, while chip-scale hardware implementations will
require suitable data-flow architectures that make use of all available hardware resources without
incurring energy overheads.
Following the aforementioned approach, and using our model calibrated from the one-layer
demonstration, we designed a two-layer neural network consisting of 24 input, 33 hidden (not
directly accessed by the input or output nodes) and 7 output neurons (a total of 64 neurons). A
single 64×64-sized crossbar was used to support two layers of positive weights in the sparse and
duplicated fashion described above. A second crossbar was used to support the negative weights of
both the layers. The system was trained to perform classification of braille representations of seven
four-lettered words. The training data consisted of the original undistorted words, along with several
distorted instances of each. In other words, the system was trained to classify distorted instances of
training data as their undistorted instances. The training process followed the Hopfield-like energy
model in Eq. 1, which found an optimum of the set of weights of the DNN under the imposed
constraints (that is, injecting signals at the output via nudging biases). The trained system was tested
for classification accuracy using newly distorted instances of the original training data, and counting
the number of times it successfully classified the input as one of the elements of the undistorted
training data. Similar to our first demonstration, we found excellent agreement between our
experimental data and the circuit model (Figs. 3c-d). Finally, to demonstrate that the technique has
reasonable scalability without prohibitive overheads, we modeled a 600-neuron system to
reconstruct braille representations of full sentences (Fig. 3e), which demonstrates that with a number

 69

of training cycles (epochs) similar to the 64-neuron system (Fig. 2i), the reconstruction accuracy
reached 100%.
Hopfield networks are usually thought of as needing a large overhead in terms of the number of
matrix multiplication cycles (i.e., updates to the output) to converge to an energy minimum,30 and
being severely limited in their capacity46. However, with only one matrix multiplication cycle per
layer, our accuracies remain high. Further, recent advances have circumvented the capacity limits of
Hopfield networks47. Also, while symmetric matrices (the forward and backward paths being
identical) is a theoretical requirement in the original Hopfield formulation, recent work has shown
that even random backward paths can align the gradients to those that result in the accurate training
of the network48. Thus, we do not foresee any fundamental barriers in the adoption of MADEM and
other energy-based training approaches.

4.4. Performance benchmarking
In order to translate the performance of the system to benchmarks comparable to prevailing and
competing technologies, we use detailed circuit simulations and semiconductor foundry
specifications to project the latency and energy consumption of memristor hardware employing
MADEM to reconstruct the 64-neuron problem presented in Fig. 2. We compare a functionally and
architecturally identical implementation in flash hardware. We also include performance estimations
of the backpropagation algorithm run on both a GPU and a CPU, summarized in Fig. 3f. MADEM
supported on memristor hardware far outperforms the GPU-based approaches by over 5 orders of
magnitude in energy efficiency and nearly 2 orders of magnitude in latency. While flash-based
hardware is suitable for running inference workloads, its training suffers from very significant
latencies. We further develop a crude chip-scale architecture suited to MADEM implemented on
memristor hardware. The performance projections of this scaled-up system exhibited over 4 orders
of magnitude energy advantages relative to GPUs running at an identically larger scale, which is a
fairer comparison. While our projections, not surprisingly, indicate a 10× loss of energy advantages
due to architectural and operational overheads, our technique has the potential to maintain the
remaining 4 orders of magnitude energy advantages even at full chip scales. Further, MADEM can
be implemented on various post-digital memristor technologies (e.g., oxide-based switches, phase-
change memories, ferroelectric tunnel junctions, etc.) with approximately similar results, likely within
an order of magnitude.
The reason underlying the dramatic energy efficiencies of our approach relative to GPUs is the
transformation in the way computing is performed at all levels of the stack from materials to
algorithms. Our hardware platform offers massive parallelism in performing VMMs in memristor
crossbar hardware. This process leads to significant energy savings because VMMs dominate the
total operations in DNN training44. Further, there is a natural fit between our formulation of
MADEM and analog memristor crossbars. Also, the memristive material was designed to support
the algorithm (for example, via low-leakage and multi-bit operations). On the one hand, static
nonidealities (for example, device variabilities) are accounted for in the training process without the
need for any additional overheads. On the other hand, dynamical nonidealities (for example,
temporal stochasticity) can not only be mitigated, but also exploited to enhance the performance of
the system49,50. The basic versions of backpropagation and MADEM (contrastive learning in general)
theoretically produce equivalent results4,27,30. Meanwhile, porting backpropagation algorithms from
GPUs to any post-digital hardware will incur steep overheads and mismatches. Further, while
clocked analog (or hybrid) hardware is the next frontier in artificial intelligence hardware, the
advances following that will likely lead to continuous-time (asynchronous) computing macros. While

 70

backpropagation and associated analytical approaches are fundamentally incompatible with such
continuous-time hardware, activity-difference training techniques such as MADEM are well suited
and will offer even lower latencies compared to our estimates for clocked analog hardware. The
foregoing discussion illustrates the need for a co-designed computing ecosystem.

4.5. Conclusions
We have reported a novel activity-difference-based training of neural networks, MADEM,
demonstrated using tantalum oxide analogue memristor crossbars and CMOS control circuitry in an
integrated foundry chip. Because of the co-design of the MADEM algorithm and the analogue
computing hardware, nonidealities in the memristor devices, such as noise and device variability, are
naturally accounted for. One-layer and multi-layer neural networks were trained with MADEM to
recognize braille words with different levels of distortion. The approach showed an orders-of-
magnitude improvement in energy efficiency and latency over prevailing analytical approaches based
on GPUs and CPUs. In addition, modelling of larger networks showed that MADEM can be scaled
up to train DNNs while still retaining an advantage. The approach should be compatible with future
analogue and continuous-time hardware, and could thus provide further developments in energy-
efficient artificial intelligence.
