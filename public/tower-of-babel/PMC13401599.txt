Hyperloss from coherent spatial-mode mixing in quantum-correlated networks
Authors: Stephan Grebien, Julian Gurs, Roman Schnabel, Mikhail Korobko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Quantum-correlated networks distribute quantum resources such as squeezed and entangled states. They are central to modern quantum technology, including photonic quantum computing, quantum communications, biological sensing and gravitational-wave detection. Even for squeezed light — the most robust quantum-correlated resource — loss-induced decoherence remains the dominant obstacle to strong quantum advantage. A common design assumption is that spatial-mode mismatch acts as an incoherent loss. Coherent spatial-mode mixing with higher-order modes, however, can produce an apparent loss exceeding the full initial squeezing, a regime we term hyperloss. Here, we show experimentally that a minimal two-node network exhibits hyperloss, with 8 per cent mode mismatch converting 5.8 decibels of observable squeezing into an effectively thermal state, and that the lost correlations can be recovered by tuning differential spatial-mode phases, establishing hyperloss as a practical design constraint for future quantum technologies.

Introduction

Laser beams with a squeezed quantum uncertainty of the electromagnetic field (“squeezed light”)1–3 have become a central resource for quantum technologies and quantum-correlated networks. They enable breakthroughs in photonic quantum computing4–7, quantum communications8–10, and enhance precision interferometry, including gravitational-wave detection11–21. The performance of squeezed states in these quantum-correlated networks is ultimately limited by decoherence. Typically, the dominant degradation channels are optical loss3,21 and phase noise22; corresponding mitigation strategies are well understood, enabling state-of-the-art squeezing levels23. Mode mismatch between spatial modes in an optical network is often approximated as an additional, incoherent loss channel. This approximation, however, can significantly underestimate the degradation, as first theoretically recognized in the context of gravitational-wave detection24–27.

In complex multimode quantum-correlated networks, coherent mode mixing with strongly anti-squeezed higher-order spatial modes can, in principle, produce an effective degradation exceeding the full initial squeezing. We refer to this previously overlooked regime as “hyperloss.” Unlike conventional optical loss—where a squeezed vacuum mode couples to a vacuum mode—such degradation arises from spatial-mode mixing (SMM) between different transverse modes that are themselves quantum-correlated. Whenever quantum resources are shared across interfering nodes that support more than one spatial mode, coherent mode mixing may couple the measured mode to correlated, anti-squeezed degrees of freedom. Because this process is phase-sensitive, it also suggests a possible route to mitigation through phase engineering.

Hyperloss sets a scaling limit for quantum-correlated networks that distribute squeezing—from photonic quantum processors to large-scale interferometers (Fig. 1a). So far, it has played a limited role because most existing systems operate with moderate quantum correlations and relatively low network complexity. As quantum systems move toward higher squeezing and increasingly complex multimode routing and interference, we expect hyperloss to become a central challenge. In this work, we identify hyperloss as a phase-sensitive decoherence regime arising from coherent spatial-mode mixing in quantum-correlated networks. We demonstrate it experimentally in a minimal two-node optical network and show that moderate mode mismatch can completely erase observable squeezing, while appropriate phase engineering can recover the lost correlations. Together, these results establish hyperloss as a practical design constraint and motivate phase-aware multimode engineering in future quantum technologies.

Results

Physical origin of hyperloss

To illustrate the physical origin of hyperloss, we first examine the mechanisms of mode mismatch at a beam-splitter and a single-sided (overcoupled) optical cavity; see Fig. 1b–d. The fundamental mode (FM) of the laser beam is designed to match the target optical mode at the second port of the beam-splitter or inside the cavity. In practice, however, small imperfections—either due to deviations in the mode shape or slight misalignments of the optical axis—inevitably introduce mode mismatches. As a result, after the imperfect interference between the two modes, a fraction of the field is scattered into the higher-order spatial modes (HOMs). Both a beam-splitter and an optical cavity, therefore, act as couplers between the FM and HOMs. For small mismatches, this coupling typically involves a single HOM, so they effectively behave as two-mode beam splitters with one FM and one HOM channel. This analogy works both for a beam-splitter and an optical cavity, and allows to significantly simplify the treatment of the latter. For example, a simple network of two sequentially coupled cavities can be seen to form an effective Mach–Zehnder interferometer, where the FM couples into one HOM and back; see Fig. 2c–e. Upon the second interaction, two contributions to FM interfere depending on their relative phase, which defines whether the interference is constructive or destructive. This phase difference arises from two sources: (i) different propagation (Gouy) phase is accumulated by distinct spatial modes33; and (ii) different cavity phase response if two modes are not simultaneously resonant in the cavity34. We define two regimes of phase-dependent SMM: “cold” and “hot.” Cold SMM occurs when the light is initially in a coherent state. It is the direct phase-dependent power loss on the FM beam, bounded by 100%. Hot SMM, on the other hand, appears when the light is in a (pure) quantum-correlated state. In this regime, the coupling between the spatial modes entangles the fields, and the measurement of one mode yields a mixed, thermal-like state. When interpreted as an effective loss on the squeeze factor, this apparent decoherence exceeds 100%, which motivates the term “hyperloss.”

Experimental demonstration of hyperloss and recovery

We experimentally demonstrated the transition of the quantum-correlated network system into the hyperloss regime, observing the complete loss of 5.8 dB of squeezing (below the shot noise level) and the transition to an effectively thermal state with ≈1.5 dB excess noise above the shot noise level. Our setup directly reproduces the physical picture described above, showcasing that even a simple optical network is susceptible to the hyperloss effect. In our experiment, the squeezed field in the TEM00 mode was reflected sequentially from two strongly overcoupled Fabry–Perot cavities and then measured with a balanced homodyne detector; see “Methods” for details. The experienced total optical loss of (26.3 ± 10.4)%, as measured independently with a well-matched network, allowed to observe up to (5.8 ± 0.5) dB of squeezing and (24 ± 0.2) dB of anti-squeezing at the homodyne detector. We intentionally introduced an (8 ± 1)% mismatch into the LG01 mode at the first cavity while restoring the FM at the second, so that the HOM existed only between the two cavities. Since it was not resonant in either of the two cavities, it acquired a π phase with each reflection, which contributed to the overall differential phase between the FM and HOM.

This differential phase was adjusted by two approaches. (i) We chose the phase acquired by the FM upon reflection from the first cavity by detuning it from perfect resonance, with an offset being actively stabilized. (ii) We continuously varied the detuning of the second cavity, imprinting a changing phase shift on the FM upon reflection. The propagation Gouy phase and other coupling phases were unknown, but this combined dynamical tuning approach allowed us to span the full range of possible phase differences between the two modes. We performed the full state tomography to record both minimal and maximal noise variance as a function of measurement frequency. We changed the FM-HOM differential phase to characterize different regimes of the hot SMM. We simultaneously recorded the cold SMM effect by propagating a weak coherent beam through the same optical path and measuring the power loss at the homodyne detector.

We highlight two regimes: where the state experiences hyperloss, and where we can mitigate it by recovering the lost correlations. Figure 3 presents the minimum of the observed noise variance alongside with theoretical simulations, demonstrating the good qualitative agreement between experiment and theory, capturing the complex behavior of the system. In the hyperloss regime, we observe 1.5 dB of excess noise above the shot noise level, signifying complete loss of squeezing; see Fig. 3c for 22 dB of anti-squeezing (see “Methods” for details). Remarkably, this effect occurs at a moderate mismatch of only 8%, comparable to the alignment tolerances in many complex quantum optical experiments, highlighting the practical significance of the hyperloss effect. Importantly, the cold SMM effect, Fig. 3a, b, cannot explain the observed decoherence, as it shows only 40% of total loss, which would allow to observe up to 4 dB of squeezing. This highlights the fundamental difference between the two regimes of phase-dependent SMM and verifies the quantum nature of hyperloss. We further demonstrate our ability to mitigate the hyperloss effect by adjusting the differential phase to the recovery regime, see Fig. 3d. We mitigate up to 12% of the loss introduced by the mismatch, recovering the lost correlations. Hyperloss in extended quantum networks

To illustrate how hyperloss scales beyond the minimal two-node experiment, we consider its effect on a path through a large-scale quantum-correlated network, where repeated nodes couple several spatial modes and distribute quantum resources across the system; see Fig. 4a. We extend the simplified model described in the Supplementary Note 5 to analyse the effect of repeated mode mismatch along one path through such a network. We consider a series of mixing components, each defined by the coupling strength ki and the differential phase ϕi. While the realistic experimental setups may include different coupling strengths for each component and a variety of overall phase shifts, we use the simplified case ki = k and ϕi = ϕ to provide an idealized coherent-limit envelope of the expected effect. Figure 4 shows the expected squeezing level as a function of the common phase shift for two different mode mismatch values: 1 and 2% per node. For the first case, the incoherent loss model for the mismatches would result in 10.2 dB of squeezing after 10 nodes, which is sufficient for fault-tolerant continuous-variable quantum computing (CVQC). However, the hot SMM analysis shows that for about 55% of the phase values, the output squeezing drops below the 10 dB squeezing target (i.e., the relative noise rises above −10 dB), making it unsuitable for fault-tolerant CVQC without careful phase design. At the same time, within this repeated-cell coherent-limit model, appropriate phase relationships can, in principle, recover the target squeezing even where it would not be expected within the incoherent model. In this case, the incoherent model predicts 7.4 dB of squeezing after 10 nodes, which is insufficient for fault-tolerant CVQC. However, by tuning the differential phases between the modes, the output squeezing level can be maintained at or above the 10 dB squeezing target for 25% of the phase values. This provides an intuitive illustration of both the risks posed by coherent accumulation of SMM in large-scale quantum networks and the opportunities for recovering lost correlations in such scenarios. Although realistic networks will have non-identical phases, this estimation provides an envelope for the effect and highlights the necessity of mitigation strategies. In practice, realistic multimode networks will generally require more detailed modelling, and limited phase control at only a few nodes may not be sufficient to reproduce the full recovery suggested by this minimal model.

Discussion

Our work identifies and experimentally demonstrates hyperloss: a phase-sensitive decoherence regime produced by coherent spatial-mode mixing in quantum-correlated networks. As quantum optical networks (including photonic circuits, links, and large-scale interferometers) scale in mode count and complexity, hyperloss becomes a practical design constraint unless explicitly mitigated. For example, current analyses for fault-tolerant continuous variable cluster-state quantum computers (CVQC) conclude that 15–17 dB of directly measured squeezing in the cluster state is a realistic experimental target for fault tolerance using Gottesman–Kitaev–Preskill (GKP) states and modern error-correction schemes37,38. Even with advanced analog error correction that tolerates 5–10% detection loss, fault-tolerant CVQC still requires ≈10–12 dB of squeezed GKP resources4,39,40. Interferometric visibilities for the nodes of such networks in state-of-the-art experiments reach 96–99%41, yet this does not guarantee that hyperloss will not arise when multiple spatial modes can propagate between nodes (for example, in free-space sections, cavities, multimode interferometers, or due to residual aberrations). As illustrated by the repeated-cell network model in Results “Hyperloss in Extended Quantum Networks,” even mismatch levels that appear tolerable within an incoherent loss budget can become incompatible with fault-tolerant CVQC once coherent spatial-mode mixing is taken into account.

The coherent nature of hyperloss also enables mitigation. Hyperloss is highly phase-selective: by tuning differential phases between spatial modes, its impact can be strongly suppressed and, in idealized cases, canceled. Considering the example above of the chain of SMM cells with a realistic mismatch of 2% per node, the incoherent loss model predicts 7.4 dB of squeezing at the output, insufficient for fault-tolerant CVQC. Yet, by optimizing the differential phases between the modes, the output squeeze factor can be maintained at or above the 10 dB squeezing target for 25% of the phase values. This illustrates that phase-aware optical design can not only reduce SMM-induced degradation, but in some regimes exploit coherence to preserve quantum correlations. Practical routes include: (i) geometric and optical-layout optimization to control Gouy-phase accumulation in different paths; (ii) additional phase-tuning or mode-filtering cavities to compensate unwanted differential phase shifts; (iii) squeezing of HOMs to reduce the impact of hyperloss26; and (iv) active wavefront shaping or adaptive optics to control intermodal phases34.

In realistic quantum networks, mismatches rarely involve a single HOM, and each interface will generally introduce a different set of modes with different coupling strengths. This makes the design of hyperloss-robust systems more challenging, but the underlying principle remains: by controlling the relative phases between the modes, hyperloss can be mitigated. In practice, this will likely require a combination of the mitigation strategies described above to optimize the relevant phase relationships across the network. The extent to which such mitigation can be implemented will depend on the specific platform, available control points, and application requirements, and will therefore require careful system-level design and optimization. In principle, independently measuring HOM outputs could enable recovery of correlations lost to hyperloss; at scale, however, this would require many additional detectors and substantial optical complexity, making it impractical for many platforms. Accordingly, the scalable route is phase-aware engineering through careful Gouy-phase management, cavity detuning design, and adaptive optics, rather than explicit HOM readout.

We anticipate that hyperloss effects will play a defining role in the design of the next generation of squeezed-light quantum technologies and quantum-correlated networks. Developing proactive, phase-aware multimode design and control strategies to counteract—and in some regimes exploit—this effect will be essential for sustaining quantum advantage as these systems scale.

Experimental setup

The experimental setup, shown in Fig. 5, consisted of three main parts: the squeezed light source, the two cavities, and the homodyne detection system. Initially, the squeezed beam was prepared in the fundamental spatial Laguerre–Gauss (LG00) mode, with 5.8 dB of squeezing and 24 dB of anti-squeezing at 3.75 MHz, as measured with a well-matched optical network with ≈28% total loss. Squeezed light was measured with a balanced homodyne detector, where it interfered with a strong local oscillator (LO) beam in the LG00 mode. The phase of the LO was scanned to measure the full quadrature noise ellipse of the squeezed beam. The data were recorded using the data acquisition system and then transformed to the frequency domain to obtain the noise spectra as a function of time.

Alongside the squeezed beam, a weak control beam was co-propagated to assist in controlling the squeezed beam and to serve as a classical reference for the cold SMM measurements. This beam experienced the same mode mismatches and phase shifts as the squeezed beam, allowing us to directly compare hot and cold SMM effects. We confirmed good overlap between the squeezed beam and the control beam on a separate diagnostic cavity before the main experiment. The control field was then measured with a DC output of the homodyne detector, observing direct power loss as the change in the maximal observed photocurrent of the interference fringe.

We introduced a controlled mode mismatch by using two pairs of lenses before and after the first cavity. The first pair created a mode mismatch at cavity 1, resulting in coupling of ≈8% of the power into the LG01 mode. Less than 2% of the power was scattered into all other HOMs, neither of which stood out prominently. We did not include this small contribution in our theoretical model, as it did not significantly affect the results. The second pair of lenses introduced an opposite mismatch on cavity 2, such that the LG01 mode coupled back in the LG00, resulting in a pure LG00 mode at the homodyne detector, confirmed with the diagnostic cavity. Thus, the LG01 mode was only present between the two cavities, allowing us to study the SMM effects.

We introduced controlled phase shifts between the fundamental and higher-order modes (HOMs) by two approaches. First, we detuned cavity 1 from perfect resonance, imprinting a phase shift on the FM upon reflection, see Fig. 6. This detuning was actively stabilized using a Pound–Drever–Hall locking scheme with a 532 nm stabilization beam. Second, we continuously varied the length of cavity 2, changing the phase shift on the FM upon reflection. The HOM was not resonant in either cavity, acquiring approximately a π phase shift upon each reflection (across the measurement band). By combining these two tuning methods, we were able to span the full range of possible differential phases between the two modes.Fig. 6Controlled phase shift on cavity 1.a Phase and amplitude responses of cavity 1 at different length stabilization points. b–f Resonance peaks of 1064 nm (monitored at photodiode 2) and 532 nm (monitored at photodiode 1) for cavity 1. The hyperloss effect, presented in Fig. 3 of the main text, was observed by measuring the variance of the squeezed beam while continuously varying the length of cavity 2 and the homodyne phase at different time scales. While the squeezed quadrature acquired significant frequency dependence due to the cavity response, we compensated for that by selecting a homodyne angle with a minimal noise variance at each frequency. This ensured that we always measured the quadrature with the least noise, allowing us to accurately capture the hyperloss effect across the entire frequency range. At the same time, we could monitor the anti-squeezed quadrature by selecting the orthogonal homodyne angle, as shown in Fig. 7b, c. Depending on the differential phase between the two modes, squeezed quadrature coupled more or less excess noise from the anti-squeezed quadrature, leading to the observed hyperloss effect, where squeezing was completely lost. Simplified theoretical model

To model the SMM effect, we consider a simple Mach–Zehnder-like coupling setup, as shown in Fig. 2 of the main text. We assume that the FM is initially in a squeezed state, while the HOM is in the vacuum state. The two modes interact at two beam-splitters, representing the mode-mixing interfaces (e.g., optical cavities). Between the two beam-splitters, the HOM acquires a phase shift ϕ1 relative to the FM, which includes both the Gouy phase and any coupling-induced phase shifts. We denote the coupling strengths at the first and second beam-splitters as k1 and k2, respectively.
