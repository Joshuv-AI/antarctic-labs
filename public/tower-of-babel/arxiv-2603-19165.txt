Rigorous Error Certification for Neural PDE Solvers: From Empirical Residuals to Solution Guarantees
Amartya Mukherjee*, Maxwell Fitzsimmons*, David C. Del Rey Fernández, Jun Liu

Abstract
Uncertainty quantification for partial differential equations is traditionally grounded in discretization theory, where solution error is controlled via mesh/grid refinement. Physics-informed neural networks fundamentally depart from this paradigm: they approximate solutions by minimizing residual losses at collocation points, introducing new sources of error arising from optimization, sampling, representation, and overfitting. As a result, the generalization error in the solution space remains an open problem. Our main theoretical contribution establishes generalization bounds that connect residual control to solution-space error. We prove that when neural approximations lie in a compact subset of the solution space, vanishing residual error guarantees convergence to the true solution. We derive deterministic and probabilistic convergence results and provide certified generalization bounds translating residual, boundary, and initial errors into explicit solution error guarantees. The implementation of our method is in the following GitHub repository .

Introduction

Partial differential equations (PDEs) play a crucial role in modelling phenomena across physics
[
16
]
, engineering
[
41
]
, finance
[
42
]
, and control
[
24
]
. However, they are conceptually difficult and computationally expensive to solve. In recent years, neural network-based approaches have attracted growing attention across machine learning, applied mathematics, and physics. In particular, physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) approximate solutions by fitting functions that satisfy the governing equations at sampled locations
[
27
,
14
,
4
,
12
,
31
,
36
]
;see
17
for a comprehensive overview of the physics-informed learning paradigm and its applications.

In many scientific and decision-making settings, solving a PDE is not sufficient: one must also quantify the uncertainty and reliability of the computed solution.
For example, classical numerical methods such as finite difference
[
15
]
, finite element
[
28
]
, and spectral methods
[
30
]
provide a well-developed theory of discretization error, where the dominant source of error arises from domain discretization.
Unlike classical discretization schemes, the loss minimized by PINNs is defined through residual evaluations at finitely many collocation points. Consequently, small training residual does not automatically imply small error in the underlying function space. The central theoretical difficulty is therefore to understand when residual control translates into convergence to the true PDE solution. Without such a link, the reliability of neural PDE solvers cannot be rigorously assessed.

While recent works have begun to bound residual errors, boundary violations, or testing errors for PINNs, a fundamental question remains largely unexplored: When does a small residual loss imply that the neural network is close to the true solution in the solution space?

Our results establish, for the first time, a direct theoretical link between residual-based training objectives and error in the solution space for PINNs. Unlike prior works that bound residual, boundary, or initial condition errors in isolation
[
39
,
37
,
7
]
, we show how such quantities affect the true solution error. This perspective reframes uncertainty quantification for neural PDE solvers: residual control alone is insufficient; structural regularity is essential.

We complement our theoretical analysis with numerical experiments on ODEs and PDEs, including elliptic, parabolic, and hyperbolic examples. Using formal verification tools, we illustrate how error certification yields verifiable generalization guarantees and empirically stable convergence.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows:

1.

We conduct a convergence analysis of PINNs via compactness arguments.

2.

We provide generalization bounds based on errors that can be explicitly computed without access to the true solution.

3.

We do formal verifications on several systems and show that the true solution error is bounded above by our generalization error.

Related Works

Neural PDE Solvers.

There are various machine learning-based solvers for PDEs, among which PINNs
[
27
]
and extreme learning machines (ELMs)
[
14
]
are widely used. PINNs incorporate the PDE residuals and initial/boundary conditions directly into the loss function, while ELMs perform a least squares approximation. Both methods demonstrate remarkable empirical performance in solving a wide variety of PDEs.
Despite their success, a rigorous understanding of when residual minimization guarantees convergence in the solution space, and how residual error translates into solution error, remains largely unresolved.

Formally Verified Neural Networks.

Motivated by adversarial attacks in neural networks, verifiability and perturbation analysis has been of interest to the trustworthy machine learning community.
Satisfiability modulo theories (SMT) solvers such as dReal
[
10
]
have been used to verify inequalities in neural networks by finding counterexamples.
Similarly,
\alpha
-CROWN
[
39
]
and
\beta
-CROWN
[
34
]
compute bounds in neural networks under perturbations via linear or quadratic relaxations of activation functions.
AutoLiRPA
[
37
]
builds upon CROWN to bound neural networks provided a bounded range of perturbations.

Formal Verifications in PDEs.

More recently, verifications in neural networks have been extended to PDE residuals.
An early example is neural Lyapunov functions
[
3
,
40
,
21
,
22
]
, where SMT solvers were used to verify that the learned neural network satisfies the inequalities required for it to be a Lyapunov function. Among these,
21
,
22
specifically used PINNs to learn neural Lyapunov functions. The work of
7
proposes
\delta
-CROWN, which bounds derivatives of neural networks with respect to their inputs, and therefore, bounds residual errors in PINNs.
Finally, generalization bounds in PDEs were explored by
25
in various settings under some stability and training process assumptions.

Recent works have developed residual-based error bounds for neural differential equation solvers. The work of
23
derives computable residual-to-error bounds for PINNs across ODEs and first-order PDEs, while
18
proposes recursive error-network constructions for bounding worst-case approximation errors in Fokker–Planck equations. Additionally,
9
incorporates deterministic residual-based bounds into Bayesian PINNs to improve uncertainty calibration, and
38
develops operator-learning frameworks for parabolic PDEs.

Our paper builds upon these works by first establishing convergence guarantees under compactness assumptions, showing that vanishing residual error implies convergence to the true solution. We then provide generalization bounds that can be computed explicitly without knowing the true solution. Using verification tools, we bound all sources of error (residual, initial, and boundary) and combine them with theoretically derived error estimates to obtain bounds on the true solution error. Numerical examples demonstrate that these bounds are computable for a variety of ODE and PDE problems and that the true solution error is indeed bounded by our generalization bounds.

Problem formulation

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{X}}
be compact and
Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{Y}}
. Let
\mathcal{G}
be a Banach space of functions from
X
to
Y
, equipped with norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{G}}
, representing the solution space. Typical examples include
C(X,Y)
with the uniform norm or
L^{p}(X,Y)
.
Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
denote a hypothesis class, e.g., functions representable by neural networks of a prescribed architecture type.

3.1
Equation Operator Formulation

Let
O:\mathcal{G}\times X\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{O}}
be a (possibly nonlinear) differential operator defining the equation of interest. For each
f\in\mathcal{G}
, we define the residual function
O(f,\cdot)=:O_{f}:X\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{O}}
. Let
\mathcal{O}
denote a complete normed function space equipped with norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{O}}
. We will make the following assumptions:

1.

O
admits a unique solution
g\in\mathcal{G}
that satisfies
\|O(g,\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{O}}=0
.

2.

The map
f\to\|O(f,\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{O}}
is continuous.

For each
f\in\mathcal{G}
, we define the
solution error
as
E_{g}(f)=\|f-g\|_{\mathcal{G}}
, where
g
is the unique solution to
O
, and the
residual error
(or
equation error
) as
E_{O}(f)=\|O(f,\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{O}}
. We assume that the equation
O
and the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{O}}
is known, but the unique solution
g
is unknown.

Unlike classical numerical methods for PDEs, where discretizations are posed in variational or projection forms that provide stability estimates linking residual and solution error, neural PDE solvers minimize a pointwise residual evaluated at finitely many collocation points. Without such stability inequalities, small residual error does not automatically imply small solution error. This motivates the central question of this work: when does small residual error imply small solution error, and how do we rigorously quantify solution errors from residual errors?

To illustrate the abstract formulation above, we provide an example based on ordinary differential equations (ODEs) below.

Example 1

(Initial Value Problem)
.

Consider an initial value problem (IVP)

\dot{\phi}(t)=F\circ\phi(t),\quad\phi(0)=\phi_{0},

on
[0,T]
.
We pick
O
to be

O(\phi,t)=\begin{pmatrix}\dot{\phi}(t)-F\circ\phi(t)\\
\phi(0)-\phi_{0}\end{pmatrix}

where, if
\phi:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is continuously differentiable, then
O:C^{1}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{2d}
. We endow
\mathcal{O}
with the uniform norm on
[0,T]
and endow
\mathcal{G}
with the norm
\lVert g\rVert_{\mathcal{G}}=\max\left\{\lVert g\rVert_{\infty},\lVert\dot{g}\rVert_{\infty}\right\}
, where the
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\infty}
is the uniform norm on
[0,T]
. When the function
F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is Lipschitz,
O
has a unique solution, which is also the unique solution to the IVP.

3.2
Training Error

The
training eror
observed at
n
points
E_{T,n}:\mathcal{G}\to[0,\infty)
is a quantity directly minimized by some algorithm. For instance, in Example
1
, a natural choice for the training error is,

E_{T,n}(\phi)=\max_{k=1,\dots,n}\left\{\lVert O(\phi,t_{k})\rVert_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\right\}

where the points
\left\{t_{k}:k\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
are dense in
[0,T]
and
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}
is some fixed norm on
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
. If instead we endowed
\mathcal{O}
with the
L^{2}
norm, it would be more natural to use the typical “mean squared error (MSE)" as the training error.

The hope is that, as
n\to\infty
, a small training error
E_{T,n}(f)
implies a small residual error
E_{O}(f)
, which implies a small solution error
E_{g}(f)
. We formalize this minimal consistency requirement.

Definition 1
.

Suppose that
O
has a unique solution
g
in
\mathcal{G}
. The hypothesis class
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
is fixed, and
E_{T,n}
is a training error.
We say that
(\mathcal{F},E_{T,n})

soundly approximates
the solution to
O
, if, for every sequence
\left\{f_{n}\in\mathcal{F}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfying
\lim_{n\to\infty}E_{T,n}(f_{n})=0
, we also have
\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}=g
in
\mathcal{G}
.

The sequence
\{f_{n}\in\mathcal{F}\}
represents some computed (potential) minimizers of
E_{T,n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. In other words, vanishing training error implies convergence to the true solution. This property expresses a minimal reliability requirement for neural PDE solvers.

However, sound approximation does not hold in general.
We provide a counterexample below.

Example 2

(Inadequate convergence of potential approximates)
.

Let
X=[0,1]
and
\mathcal{G}=C(X,X)
. Define
O(f,x)=f(x)
. Then, the unique solution to
O
is the zero function
g\equiv 0
.
For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define the points
x_{k,n}=\frac{k}{n}
for
k=0,1,\dots,n
. Then define

f_{n}(x)=\begin{cases}2^{n}(x-x_{k,n}),\qquad x\in[x_{k,n},x_{k,n}+2^{-n}],\\
1,\qquad\qquad x\in[x_{k,n}+2^{-n},x_{k+1,n}-2^{-n}],\\
-2^{n}(x-x_{k+1,n}),\penalty\ x\in[x_{k+1,n}-2^{-n},x_{k+1,n}],\\
\end{cases}

for
k=0,\dots,n
.
Then we have that,

\displaystyle\lVert f_{n}\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in[0,1]}\lvert f_{n}(x)-g(x)\rvert=f_{n}(0-2^{-n})=1,

\displaystyle\lVert f_{n}\rVert_{p}^{p}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\lvert f_{n}(x)\rvert^{p}dx\geq 1-n2^{-n+1}.

In all cases, as
n\to\infty
, we have
E_{T,n}(f_{n})=0
, but

\displaystyle\lVert f_{n}\rVert_{\infty}\to 1\neq 0,

\displaystyle\lVert f_{n}\rVert_{p}^{p}\to 1\neq 0,

which implies
f_{n}\not\to 0
in the norms under consideration.
Thus, vanishing training error does not imply convergence.

This scenario can also happen for the IVP in Example
1
: we can pick
\phi_{n}
as solutions to the IVP
\dot{\phi}_{n}(t)=F\circ\phi_{n}(t)+f_{n}(t)
,
\phi_{n}(0)=\phi_{0}
.

This example demonstrates a neural overfitting phenomenon: the approximations interpolate the collocation points while oscillating arbitrarily between them. We are therefore faced with three fundamental questions:

•

Consistency.
Under what structural assumptions does vanishing residual loss imply convergence to the true solution?

•

Error transfer.
How can residual error
E_{O}(f)
be converted into bounds on the solution error
E_{g}(f)
without access to the ground truth?

•

Certification.
Can we rigorously certify domain-wide bounds on the residual
E_{O}(f)
using formal verification tools?

4
Convergence via Compactness

Example
2
demonstrates that residual minimization alone does not ensure convergence in the solution space: the approximating functions may exhibit increasingly steep oscillations while matching all collocation points.
This suggests that residual control must be complemented by a structural condition on the hypothesis class
\mathcal{F}
. In this section, we show that this additional structural condition is compactness in
\mathcal{G}
.
But first we should discuss why, in a more general problem, that compactness in
\mathcal{G}
is useful.
For a normed space
(Z,\lVert\cdot\rVert_{Z})
and
S\subseteq Z
, we denote
\overline{S}
as the closure of
S
in
Z
. We refer the reader to Appendix
C
for the proofs of all the theoretical results here.

Proposition 1
.

Let
O:\mathcal{G}\times X\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{O}}
be an equation, with
E_{\mathcal{O}}(f)=\lVert O(f,\cdot)\rVert_{\mathcal{O}}
continuous.
If
O
has a unique solution
g\in\mathcal{G}
then,

\displaystyle\overline{\left\{f_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}}\text{ is compact}

\displaystyle\text{and }\lim_{n\to\infty}E_{\mathcal{O}}(f_{n})=0

\displaystyle\iff\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}=g\text{ in }\mathcal{G}.

Assume that the training error
E_{T,n}
approximates the true equation error
E_{\mathcal{O}}
. Proposition
1
considers an ideal case of the sound approximation property in Definition
1
, where we do not need to approximate the true equation error and instead have direct access to it. In this case, to ensure sound approximation, it is both necessary and sufficient that the sequence of approximations lies in a compact subset of
\mathcal{G}
. Therefore, in our less ideal case, where the training error
E_{T,n}
is used as a proxy for the true equation error
E_{\mathcal{O}}
, we should also require compactness.

We now investigate the sound approximation property under different norms.

The case where
\mathcal{G}
has the uniform norm leads to nice convergence theorems with small modifications to the training error. We provide a background on compactness in the uniform norm in Appendix
A
.

Theorem 1

(Uniform-uniform convergence)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=C(X,Y)
with the uniform norm, where
X
is connected, compact, and
\left\{a_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
is a sequence dense on
X
(i.e.,
\overline{\left\{a_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}}=X
).
Suppose that
O:C(X,Y)\times X\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a continuous function, we endow the space
\mathcal{O}=\left\{O(f,\cdot):f\in\mathcal{G}\right\}
with the uniform norm and let
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
be some fixed norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Assume that
O
has a unique solution. Further, consider a hypothesis class
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
and let
L:\mathcal{F}\to[0,\infty)
be a function which satisfies

\lVert f(x)-f(z)\rVert_{Y}\leq L(f)\lVert x-z\rVert_{X}

for all
x,z\in X
and each
f\in\mathcal{F}
, where
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{X}
and
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{Y}
are some fixed norms on
X
and
Y
, respectively. That is,
L(f)
is a Lipschitz constant of
f\in\mathcal{F}
. Let
L_{\max}>0
be some fixed number and let
x_{0}\in X
,
y_{0}\in Y
be some points.

Then the following holds:

1.

Define the training error

E_{T,n}^{\infty}:\mathcal{F}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle E_{T,n}^{\infty}(f)=

\displaystyle\penalty\ \max_{k=1,\dots,n}\left\{\lVert O(f,a_{k})\rVert_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\right\}

\displaystyle+\max\left\{0,L(f)-L_{\max}\right\}+\lVert f(x_{0})-y_{0}\rVert_{Y}

for
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
(\mathcal{F},E_{T,n}^{\infty})
soundly approximates the solution to
O
.

2.

Define the training error
E_{T,n}^{p}:\mathcal{F}\to\mathbb{R}
for
p>0
by

\displaystyle E_{T,n}^{p}(f)=

\displaystyle\penalty\ \sum_{k=1}^{n}{\lVert O(f,a_{k})\rVert_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{p}}

\displaystyle+\max\left\{0,L(f)-L_{\max}\right\}+\lVert f(x_{0})-y_{0}\rVert_{Y}

for
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
(\mathcal{F},E_{T,n}^{p})
soundly approximates the solution to
O
.

Note that the function
L(f)
must be obtained analytically in practice. Moreover,
L(f)
only needs to exceed the minimal Lipschitz constant of
f
, so crude but easily computable estimates suffice. In Appendix
B
, we list some preliminary results on estimation of Lipschitz constants for neural network functions. The term corresponding to the singular data point
(x_{0},y_{0})
may be unnecessary depending on the definition of
O
(see Example
1
, where such information is already encoded in the definition of
O
).

The additional terms,
\max\left\{0,L(f)-L_{\max}\right\}+\lVert f(x_{0})-y_{0}\rVert_{Y}
, exist solely to make the sequence
\left\{f_{n}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
lie in a compact subset of
\mathcal{G}
. Therefore, we dub this a ‘uniform compactness term”. One can imagine that when
\mathcal{G}
is endowed with a norm other than the uniform norm, a different term could be used to enforce compactness. Note that even when
\mathcal{G}
has the uniform norm, there are other possible ‘uniform compactness terms” that ensure the sound approximation property in Definition
1
. The compactness term used in Theorem
1
was chosen because it is conceivably usable in practice. We discuss these compactness terms in more detail in Appendix
D
.

It is common to use the “mean squared error (MSE)" in training practice. Theorem
1
does not address this case. If we add additional assumptions, we can get a similar convergence result for the MSE as follows.

Theorem 2

(Uniform-
L^{2}
convergence)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=C(X,Y)
be equipped with the uniform norm, where
X
is connected, compact, and
\left\{a_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
is a sequence dense on
X
with the additional requirement that, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\delta_{n}=\inf\left\{\delta>0:X\subseteq\bigcup_{k=1}^{n}\mathcal{B}^{X}_{\delta}\left\lparen a_{k}\right\rparen\right\},

and

C=\sup\left\{n\mu(\mathcal{B}^{X}_{\delta_{n}}\left\lparen a_{1}\right\rparen):n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}<\infty,

where
\mu
is the Lebesgue measure and
\mathcal{B}^{X}_{\delta}\left\lparen x\right\rparen
is the open ball of radius
\delta>0
centered at
x\in X
with respect to some fixed norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{X}
.
Suppose that
O:C(X,Y)\times X\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a continuous function, we endow the space
\mathcal{O}=\left\{O(f,\cdot):f\in\mathcal{G}\right\}
with the
L^{2}
norm.

Assume that
O
has a unique solution. Further, consider a hypothesis class
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
and let
L:\mathcal{F}\to[0,\infty)
be a function which satisfies

\lVert f(x)-f(z)\rVert_{Y}\leq L(f)\lVert x-z\rVert_{X}

for all
x,z\in X
and each
f\in\mathcal{F}
, where
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{Y}
is some fixed norm on
Y
. That is,
L(f)
is a Lipschitz constant of
f\in\mathcal{F}
. Let
L_{\max}>0
be some fixed number and let
x_{0}\in X
,
y_{0}\in Y
be some points.

Define the training error
E_{T,n}^{MSE}:\mathcal{F}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle E_{T,n}^{MSE}(f)=

\displaystyle\penalty\ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\lVert O(f,a_{k})\rVert_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle+\max\left\{0,L(f)-L_{\max}\right\}+\lVert f(x_{0})-y_{0}\rVert_{Y}

for
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{2}
is the
2
-norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then
(\mathcal{F},E_{T,n}^{MSE})
soundly approximates the solution to
O
.

We expect the additional condition on the dense set
\left\{a_{k}:k\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
in Theorem
2
to hold in practice, as constructing a dense set that violates this requirement appears highly nontrivial.

5
Convergence Under Random Collocation

So far the convergence analysis has assumed deterministic training locations. In practice, PINNs are typically trained using randomly sampled collocation points. We therefore ask whether a small empirical residual still implies a small equation error under random sampling. The answer is affirmative under the same compactness assumption: convergence of the empirical residual in probability implies convergence in the solution space.

Let
x_{1},x_{2},\dots
be independent samples drawn from a probability measure
\mu
on
X
. For
p>0
, define the empirical residual

E_{\text{test},p,n}(f)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|O(f,x_{k})\|_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{p}.

This quantity serves as a stochastic approximation of the equation error
E_{\mathcal{O}}(f)=\|O(f,\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{O}}
.

We say the probabilistic sound approximation property holds if, for any sequence
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{F}
,

\operatorname*{plim}_{n\to\infty}E_{test,p,n}(f_{n})=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad f_{n}\to g\text{ in }\mathcal{G},

where
\operatorname*{plim}
is the limit in probability (see Appendix
E
) and
g
is the unique solution satisfying
O(g,\cdot)=0
.

The above condition states that vanishing empirical residual implies convergence to the true solution in probability. The following theorem shows that this property holds under the same compactness assumption used in the deterministic setting.

Theorem 3

(Random collocation convergence, informal)
.

Let
x_{1},x_{2},\dots
be i.i.d. samples drawn from a probability measure
\mu
on
X
.
Assume
E_{\mathcal{O}}(f)=\|O(f,\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{O}}
is continuous and the sequence
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
lies in a compact subset of
\mathcal{G}
. Then

\operatorname*{plim}_{n\to\infty}E_{\mathrm{test},p,n}(f_{n})=0\;\Rightarrow\;\lim_{n\to\infty}E_{\mathcal{O}}(f_{n})=0.

Consequently, if
O
has a unique solution
g
, then
f_{n}\to g
in
\mathcal{G}
.

Proof sketch.

Compactness yields a subsequence
f_{n_{k}}\to f
in
\mathcal{G}
.
Continuity implies
E_{\mathcal{O}}(f_{n_{k}})\to E_{\mathcal{O}}(f)
.
By the uniform law of large numbers,

E_{\mathrm{test},p,n_{k}}(f_{n_{k}})\xrightarrow{p}E_{\mathcal{O}}(f).

Hence convergence in probability to zero forces
E_{\mathcal{O}}(f)=0
, and uniqueness gives
f=g
.
∎

A more rigorous statement and proof and additional discussions can be found in Appendix
E
.

Theorem 4

(Generalization Error Estimate)
.

Assume
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{X}}
is compact,
\mathcal{G}=C(X,Y)
is equipped with the uniform norm, and
O
admits a unique solution
g\in\mathcal{G}
. Assume the operator
O
satisfies a stability condition: there exists
C_{0}>0
such that

\|f-g\|_{\infty}\leq C_{0}\|O(f,\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{O}},\quad\text{for all }f\in\mathcal{G},

Let
\{a_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}\subset X
denote a sampling set,
and define the fill distance

\delta_{n}=\sup_{x\in X}\min_{k=1,\dots,n}\|x-a_{k}\|.

Assume further that
f
lies in a compact subset
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{G}
. Then there exists a modulus of continuity
\omega_{\mathcal{F}}(\cdot)
, depending only on
\mathcal{F}

Remark 1

(Choice of norm)
.

Theorem

4
is stated in the uniform norm on
C(X,Y)
. Extending the result to other function-space norms requires corresponding compactness assumptions and stability estimates in those norms. In general, compactness properties and operator stability conditions depend sensitively on the chosen topology, which significantly complicates the analysis.

For
L_{p}
norms with
1\leq p<\infty
and
X
of finite measure, a direct estimate follows from the uniform bound:

\|f-g\|_{L_{p}(X)}\leq\mu(X)^{1/p}\|f-g\|_{\infty}.

Thus, the uniform generalization estimate immediately implies an
L_{p}
bound, although potentially with conservative constants. For clarity and generality, we therefore formulate our main result in the uniform norm.

We provide the proof in
Section

F.1
.
This bound decomposes into two components: (a) empirical residual error
\max_{k=1,...,n}\|O(f,a_{k})\|_{Y}
, (b)
compactness term
\omega_{\mathcal{F}}(\delta_{n})
, which vanishes as the collocation points become dense.
Thus, provided the hypothesis class
\mathcal{F}
is compact (via verification), and the collocation points are sufficiently concentrated, small residual error implies small solution error.

In practice, residual norms can be certified using formal verification techniques. Given a trained neural approximation
f
, one may compute a certified upper bound

\|\mathcal{O}(f,\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{O}}\leq R_{cert},

where the bound is valid over the entire domain
X
. Such certificates can be obtained using bound-propagation methods, interval analysis, or SMT-based verification tools (see Section
2
).

In this fully verified setting, residual control is no longer restricted to sampled collocation points. Consequently, the sampling density term
\omega_{\mathcal{F}}(\delta_{n})
in (
1
) vanishes, as the residual bound holds uniformly over
X
. The solution error estimate therefore simplifies to

\|f-g\|_{\infty}\leq C_{0}\,R_{cert}.

Thus, uncertainty quantification for neural PDE solvers reduces to two equation-dependent ingredients: (a) A stability constant
C_{0}
for the underlying PDE operator, (b) A certified uniform bound on the residual norm.

The constants appearing in these bounds depend on structural properties of the specific equation, domain geometry, and norm choice. Appendix
F
instantiates this abstract framework for representative elliptic, parabolic, and hyperbolic problems, deriving explicit stability constants and corresponding error estimates.

7.6
Compactness Validation

Section
4
establishes that compactness of the hypothesis sequence is a key structural assumption underlying our convergence analysis. In particular, by Lemma
1
, relative compactness in
C(X,Y)
follows if the sequence shares a uniform modulus of continuity and is bounded at a point.

We empirically examine this assumption for the heat equation in Example
5
. We consider ELM-based neural approximations of the form

f(x)=\beta^{T}\sigma(Wx+b),

(6)

where hidden-layer parameters are randomized and output weights are trained via least squares (see Appendix
G
for details).

For such networks, a computable Lipschitz
over-approximation is given by

L\leq\|\beta\|\|W\|.

Table
6
reports these Lipschitz estimates for varying network widths and training configurations.

Empirically, although
\|W\|
increases with network width, the product
\|\beta\|\|W\|
remains stable and often decreases. This behavior is consistent with the compactness assumption required by our theoretical framework. A rigorous characterization of this phenomenon for random feature networks remains an interesting direction for future work.

8
Conclusions

We develop a generalization stability framework for neural differential equation solvers. Under compactness and operator stability assumptions, certified residual bounds can be converted into explicit solution-space error guarantees. This provides a principled pathway to generalization bounds on the true solution.
We provide a certification pipeline: residual bounds verified by formal methods can be systematically converted into uniform or
L_{2}
solution-space guarantees.

Several directions remain open. A deeper understanding of compactness mechanisms in modern neural architectures, sharper stability constants for specific equation classes, and extensions to operator-learning or stochastic PDE settings would further strengthen the theoretical foundations of certified scientific machine learning.

G_{k,j}:=\left\|\frac{\partial f_{k}}{\partial x_{j}}\right\|_{\sup},\qquad L_{k,j}:=\mathrm{Lip}\!\left(\frac{\partial f_{k}}{\partial x_{j}}\right).

We initialize at depth
k=0
(where
f_{0}(x)=x
):

G_{0,j}=1,\qquad L_{0,j}=0.

Assume inductively that
f_{k-1}
is
C^{1}
with

\|f_{k-1}\|_{\sup}\leq R_{k-1},\qquad\mathrm{Lip}(f_{k-1})\leq L_{f_{k-1}},

and that
G_{k-1,j}
and
L_{k-1,j}
are defined.
Then for each
j=1,\dots,d_{X}
, the following recursive bounds hold:

G_{k,j}\;\leq\;2\,c_{k}(R_{k-1})\,\|A_{k}\|_{\infty}\,G_{k-1,j},

and

L_{k,j}\;\leq\;2\,c_{k}(R_{k-1})\,\|A_{k}\|_{\infty}\,L_{k-1,j}\;+\;\|A_{k}\|_{\infty}^{2}\,G_{k-1,j}\,L_{f_{k-1}}.

These bounds follow from Proposition
5
together with the componentwise Lipschitz estimates in Propositions
2
,
3
,
4
.
In particular, the sup-norm control
\|f_{k-1}\|_{\sup}\leq R_{k-1}
implies

\|\tilde{\sigma}\circ C_{k}\circ f_{k-1}\|_{\sup}\;\leq\;R_{k}=s\big(c_{k}(R_{k-1})\big),

and the derivative bounds propagate according to the chain rule and the Hadamard product estimates.
We endow the codomain with the
\ell_{\infty}
norm, i.e.

\|(u,v)\|_{\infty}=\max\{\|u\|_{\infty},\|v\|_{\infty}\}.

By Proposition
2
(componentwise Lipschitz control), the Lipschitz constant of
\hat{f}_{K}
is bounded by the maximum of the Lipschitz constants of its coordinate functions. Therefore,

\mathrm{Lip}(\hat{f}_{K})\;\leq\;\max\Big\{\mathrm{Lip}(f_{K}),\;\max_{j=1,\dots,d_{X}}L_{K,j}\Big\}.

Combining this with the bound on
\mathrm{Lip}(f_{K})
and the recursive bounds for

yields an explicit, computable upper bound for

Appendix C
Proofs on Convergence Results

Proposition
1
.

Let
O:\mathcal{G}\times X\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{O}}
be an equation, with
E_{\mathcal{O}}(f)=\lVert O(f,\cdot)\rVert_{\mathcal{O}}
continuous.
If
O
has a unique solution
g\in\mathcal{G}
then,

\overline{\left\{f_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}}\text{ is compact and }\lim_{n\to\infty}E_{\mathcal{O}}(f_{n})=0\iff\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}=g\text{ in }\mathcal{G}.

Proof.

Suppose that
\overline{\left\{f_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}}
is compact and
\lim_{n\to\infty}E_{\mathcal{O}}(f_{n})=0
.
Let
f_{n_{k}}
be a convergent subsequence of
f_{n}
with limit
f\in\mathcal{G}
. Since
E_{\mathcal{O}}(f_{n})\to 0
, so does
E_{\mathcal{O}}(f_{n_{k}})\to 0
. By continuity of
E_{\mathcal{O}}
we also know that
E_{\mathcal{O}}(f_{n_{k}})\to E_{\mathcal{O}}(f)
. By uniqueness of limits in
\mathbb{R}
we have that
E_{\mathcal{O}}(f)=0
. By definition
f
is a solution; but
O
has a unique solution. Therefore,
f=g
in
\mathcal{G}
. Since
\overline{\left\{f_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}}
is compact and every convergent subsequence of
\left\{f_{n}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to
g
, so must the sequence itself.

The converse is trivial, since the closure of the set consisting of the points of a convergent sequence in normed vector space (or metric space) is compact. And by continuity and definitions, we have
\lim_{n\to\infty}E_{\mathcal{O}}(f_{n})=E_{\mathcal{O}}(g)=0
whenever
\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}=g
.
∎

\delta_{N_{1}}<\delta
it follows that for all

with for all

\displaystyle{E_{\mathcal{O}}(f_{n})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{x\in X}\lVert O(f_{n},x)\rVert_{2}^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{x\in\bigcup_{k=1}^{n}\mathcal{B}^{X}_{\delta_{n}}\left\lparen a_{k}\right\rparen}\lVert O(f_{n},x)\rVert_{2}^{2}dx

Appendix D
Compactness terms

In this section we further discus terms which can be added to traditional loss functions, which cause the approximating function sequence,
\left\{f_{n}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, to have compact closure.

Definition 2
.

A function
\kappa_{\mathcal{G}}:\mathcal{G}\to[0,\infty)\cup\left\{\infty\right\}
is called a
\mathcal{G}
compactness term if:
The sublevel sets of
\kappa_{\mathcal{G}}
are nonempty and have compact closure. That is for all
r\in[0,\infty)
the set
\overline{\left\{g\in\mathcal{G}:\kappa_{\mathcal{G}}(g)<r\right\}}
is nonempty and compact in
\mathcal{G}
.

The function
\kappa_{\mathcal{G}}
is called an eventual
\mathcal{G}
compactness term if: There is a
R\in[0,\infty)
such that
r\in[0,R]
the set
\overline{\left\{g\in\mathcal{G}:\kappa_{\mathcal{G}}(g)<r\right\}}
is nonempty and compact in
\mathcal{G}
.

We say a function
\gamma:\mathcal{F}\to[0,\infty)\cup\left\{\infty\right\}
, where
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
, is called a (eventual)
\mathcal{G}
compactness term on extension if: The function
\hat{\gamma}:\mathcal{G}\to[0,\infty)\cup\left\{\infty\right\}
, defined by
\hat{\gamma}(g)=\gamma(g)
for
g\in\mathcal{F}
and
\hat{\gamma}(g)=\infty
otherwise, is a (eventual)
\mathcal{G}
compactness term.

It is not hard to see that a
\mathcal{G}
compactness term is an eventual
\mathcal{G}
compactness term. One can also see the following proposition holds.

Proposition 6
.

Let
\left\{f_{n}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of functions in
\mathcal{G}
and
\kappa_{\mathcal{G}}
be an eventual
\mathcal{G}
compactness term.
If
\lim_{n\to\infty}\kappa_{\mathcal{G}}(f_{n})=0
then,
\overline{\left\{f_{n}:m\in\mathbb{N}\right\}}
is compact in
\mathcal{G}
.
Moreover, any
g\in\mathcal{G}
which is the limit of a convergent subsequence of
\left\{f_{n}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, has
g\in\bigcap_{r>0}\overline{\left\{h\in\mathcal{G}:\kappa_{\mathcal{G}}(h)\leq r\right\}}
.

We present some examples of compactness terms.

Proposition 7

(Some uniform compactness terms)
.

Let
X
be a connected compact set. Let
\mathcal{G}=C(X,Y)
endowed with the uniform norm. Suppose that
x_{0}\in X
and
y_{0}\in Y
are some points.
The following functions are
\mathcal{G}
compactness terms on extension:

1.

\displaystyle\gamma_{L}(f)=\max\{0,L(f)-L_{\max}\}+\lVert f(x_{0})-y_{0}\rVert_{Y}
.
Where
L_{\max}>0
is a constant and
\mathcal{F}
is a set of Lipchitz functions, with a function
L:\mathcal{F}\to[0,\infty)
such that
L(f)
is a upper bound on the Lipchitz constant of
f\in\mathcal{F}
.

2.

\displaystyle\gamma_{\alpha,L}(f)=\max\{0,L_{\max}-L(f)\}+\max\{0,\alpha_{\min}-\alpha(f)\}+\lVert f(x_{0})-y_{0}\rVert_{Y}
.
Where
L_{\max},\alpha_{\min}>0
are constants,
\mathcal{F}
is a set of
\alpha
Holder continuous functions, with functions
L:\mathcal{F}\to[0,\infty)
and
\alpha:\mathcal{F}\to[0,1]
such that

\lVert f(x)-f(z)\rVert_{Y}\leq L(f)\lVert x-z\rVert_{X}^{\alpha(f)}

for all
x,z\in X
and each
f\in\mathcal{F}
.

3.

\displaystyle\gamma_{\omega}(f)=\max\left\{0,\limsup_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\ln\left\lparen\frac{\omega_{f}(\delta)}{\omega(\delta)}\right\rparen\right\}+\lVert f(x_{0})-y_{0}\rVert_{Y}
.
Where, for
f\in\mathcal{F}
,
\omega,\omega_{f}:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)\cup\{\infty\}
are functions where are zero at zero and continuous at zero. Moreover for each
f\in\mathcal{F}
we have

\lVert f(x)-f(z)\rVert_{Y}\leq\omega_{f}(\lVert x-z\rVert_{X}).

Sketch of proof.

Item

3
follows from the fact that if
\hat{\gamma}_{\omega}(g)\leq r
,
r>0
then,
g\in\mathcal{F}
and there is a
\Delta>0
such that for all
\delta\in(0,\Delta]
we have that

\omega_{g}(\delta)\leq e^{r}\omega(\delta).

Equicontinuity of
\{g\in\mathcal{G}:\gamma_{\omega}(g)\leq r\}
follows from continuity of
\omega
at zero. Thus the set
\{g\in\mathcal{G}:\gamma_{\omega}(g)\leq r\}
is equicontinuous and pointwise bounded and so by
Lemma

1
we have that is closure is compact.
∎

Inspection of the proofs of
Theorem

1
and
Theorem

2
reveals that the compactness term of
Item

1
of

Appendix E
Proof of Random Collocation Convergence

We write
Y_{n}\xrightarrow{p}Y
(or
\operatorname*{plim}_{n\to\infty}Y_{n}=Y
) to denote convergence in probability, i.e.,

\forall\varepsilon>0:\quad\mathbb{P}(|Y_{n}-Y|>\varepsilon)\to 0\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

Let
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu)
be a probability space and let
(x_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be i.i.d.
X
-valued random variables with distribution
\mu
defined on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
.

Let
O:\mathcal{G}\times X\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and for
p\geq 1
define

E_{\mathcal{O}}(f)=\left(\int_{X}\|O(f,x)\|_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{p}d\mu(x)\right)^{1/p}.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
define the empirical residual

E_{\mathrm{test},p,n}(f,\omega)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|O(f,x_{k}(\omega))\|_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{p}.

Theorem 7

(Uniform law of large numbers on a compact hypothesis set)
.

Let
X
be compact and let
\mu
be a probability measure on
X
. Let
x_{1},x_{2},\dots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}\mu
. Fix
p\geq 1
and let
\mathcal{G}
be a metric space. Let
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{G}
be compact.

Assume
O:\mathcal{F}\times X\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is continuous. Then

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|E_{\mathrm{test},p,n}(f,\omega)-E_{\mathcal{O}}(f)^{p}\right|\xrightarrow{a.s.}0.

Equivalently,
\{h_{f}:f\in\mathcal{F}\}
is
\mu
-Glivenko-Cantelli
[
33
]
.

Proof.

Define
h_{f}(x)=\|O(f,x)\|^{p}
.
Since
\mathcal{F}\times X
is compact and
O
is continuous,
h_{f}(x)
is continuous on
\mathcal{F}\times X
and hence bounded: there exists
M<\infty
such that
0\leq h_{f}(x)\leq M
for all
(f,x)\in\mathcal{F}\times X
.

Moreover, continuity on the compact set implies uniform continuity by the Heine-Cantor theorem
[
32
, Proposition 5.8.2]
: for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
such that

\|f-g\|_{\mathcal{G}}<\delta\implies\|h_{f}(x)-h_{g}(x)\|_{\infty}<\varepsilon.

By compactness of
\mathcal{F}
, for all
\delta>0
, there exists a set
\{f^{1},\dots,f^{N}\}\subset\mathcal{F}
so that for every
f\in\mathcal{F}
there is an index
i
such that
\|f-f^{i}\|_{\mathcal{G}}<\delta
.
Fix
f
and
f^{i}
. Then for every sample sequence,

\displaystyle|E_{\mathrm{test},p,n}(f,\omega)-E_{O}(f)|

\displaystyle\leq|E_{\mathrm{test},p,n}(f,\omega)-E_{\mathrm{test},p,n}(f^{i},\omega)|+|E_{\mathrm{test},p,n}(f^{i},\omega)-E_{O}^{p}(f^{i})|+|E_{O}^{p}(f^{i})-E_{O}^{p}(f)|

\displaystyle=|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}(h_{f}(x_{k})-h_{f^{i}}(x_{k}))|+|E_{\mathrm{test},p,n}(f^{i},\omega)-E_{O}(f^{i})|+|(h_{f^{i}}-h_{f})d\mu|

\displaystyle\leq 2\varepsilon+\max_{1\leq i\leq N}|E_{\mathrm{test},p,n}(f^{i},\omega)-E_{O}(f^{i})|.

Since each
h_{f^{i}}(x_{k})
is i.i.d. bounded in
[0,M]
, Hoeffding’s inequality
[
29
]
gives

\Pr\left(|E_{\mathrm{test},p,n}(f^{i},\omega)-E_{O}(f^{i})|>t\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{2nt^{2}}{M^{2}}\right).

By a union bound over
i=1,\dots,N
,

\Pr\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq N}|E_{\mathrm{test},p,n}(f^{i},\omega)-E_{O}(f^{i})|>t\right)\leq 2N\exp\left(-\frac{2nt^{2}}{M^{2}}\right).

The right-hand side is summable in
n
, so by Borel-Cantelli
[
6
]
,

\max_{1\leq i\leq N}|E_{\mathrm{test},p,n}(f^{i},\omega)-E_{O}(f^{i})|\xrightarrow{a.s.}0.

Since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, we arrive at our desired conclusion.

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|E_{\mathrm{test},p,n}(f,\omega)-E_{\mathcal{O}}(f)^{p}\right|\xrightarrow{a.s.}0.

∎

Theorem 8

(Random collocation convergence via ULLN)
.

Assume the hypotheses of Theorem
7
.
Let
(f_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be any (possibly data-dependent) sequence with
f_{n}\in\mathcal{F}
.
If

E_{\mathrm{test},p,n}(f_{n})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|O(f_{n},x_{k})\|^{p}\xrightarrow{p}0,

then

E_{\mathcal{O},p}(f_{n})^{p}=\int_{X}\|O(f_{n},x)\|^{p}d\mu(x)\longrightarrow 0.

Consequently, if
O
has a unique solution
g\in\mathcal{G}
characterized by

E_{\mathcal{O},p}(f)=0\Rightarrow f=g
, then
f_{n}\to g
in
\mathcal{G}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
7
,

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|E_{\mathrm{test},p,n}(f)-E_{\mathcal{O},p}(f)^{p}\right|\xrightarrow{a.s.}0,

hence also in probability. Therefore

E_{\mathcal{O},p}(f_{n})^{p}\leq E_{\mathrm{test},p,n}(f_{n})+\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|E_{\mathrm{test},p,n}(f)-E_{\mathcal{O},p}(f)^{p}\right|\xrightarrow{p}0,

so
E_{\mathcal{O},p}(f_{n})\to 0
.
If the solution is unique in the sense stated, every convergent subsequence of
(f_{n})

must converge to
g
, and compactness of
\mathcal{F}
forces
f_{n}\to g
.
∎

Appendix F
Case studies and a posteriori error estimates

In this section, we present a few case studies to illustrate the applicability of the theoretical analysis presented in the previous sections. We present initial value problems for ODEs and consider two typical classes of PDEs, namely elliptic and parabolic equations. We show that the assumptions of the theoretical results in the previous sections can be easily verified. There are two main assumptions: 1)

is a continuous function; 2)

has a unique solution. We shall verify these for each case study.

Furthermore, the convergence results from the previous section are of theoretical interest because they characterize what occurs in the limit of the training process. In practice, the training process is finite; this includes the number of points used in

and the number of steps taken by optimization algorithms before converging to the true minimizers. To this end, for each case study, we provide an a posteriori error analysis for neural network approximations of solutions to differential equations. More specifically, let

into components, such as initial error, boundary error, and ODE/PDE residual errors.

Lemma 3

(Modulus of continuity for compact families)
.

Let
X
be compact and let
\mathcal{F}\subset C(X,Y)
be compact under

. Then the function

\omega_{\mathcal{F}}(r):=\sup\{\|f(x)-f(y)\|_{Y}:f\in\mathcal{F},\,x,y\in X,\,\|x-y\|_{X}\leq r\}

\omega_{\mathcal{F}}(r)<\infty
for all

and
\omega_{\mathcal{F}}(r)\downarrow 0
as

.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{F}
is compact in
(C(X,Y),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})

Theorem

5
, hence for every
\varepsilon>0

.
This is exactly

as

.
Finiteness for each

follows from boundedness of
\mathcal{F}
and compactness of

.

Lemma 4

(Fill distance implies pointwise approximation)
.

Let
h\in C(X,Y)
and let
\{a_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}\subset X
have fill distance

\delta_{n}=\sup_{x\in X}\min_{k}\|x-a_{k}\|_{X}
.
Then

\sup_{x\in X}\|h(x)\|_{Y}\leq\max_{1\leq k\leq n}\|h(a_{k})\|_{X}+\omega_{\{h\}}(\delta_{n}),

where
\omega_{\{h\}}(r):=\sup\{\|h(x)-h(y)\|_{Y}:\ \|x-y\|_{X}\leq r\}
is the modulus of continuity of

.

Proof.

Fix
x\in X
and choose

Then

Taking the supremum over

F.2
Initial value problems for ODEs

We first consider the IVP for ODEs.

Proposition 8
.

Consider the IVP

\dot{\phi}(t)=F\circ\phi(t),\quad\phi(0)=\phi_{0},

on
[0,T]
, where
T>0
.
Let
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}
be the euclidean norm. Endow the space
C^{1}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})
with the norm
\lVert g\rVert_{C^{1}}=\max\left\{\lVert g\rVert_{\infty},\lVert\dot{g}\rVert_{\infty}\right\}
where,

\lVert g\rVert_{\infty}=\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\lVert g(t)\rVert_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
.
Suppose that
F:\,\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is Lipschitz continuous. Define
O
to be

O(\phi,t)=\begin{pmatrix}\dot{\phi}(t)-F\circ\phi(t)\\
\phi(0)-\phi_{0}\end{pmatrix}.

Then
O:C^{1}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{2d}
is continuous. Furthermore, let
\phi
be an approximate solution and
\varphi
be any true solution. Then

\displaystyle E_{\varphi}(\phi)=\lVert\phi-\varphi\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq E^{I}_{\mathcal{O}}(\phi)e^{LT}+E^{R}_{\mathcal{O}}(\phi)\frac{e^{LT}-1}{L}

(9)

\displaystyle\leq E_{\mathcal{O}}(\phi)\left(e^{LT}+\frac{e^{LT}-1}{L}\right),

where
E^{I}_{\mathcal{O}}(\phi)=\lVert\phi(0)-\phi_{0}\rVert
denotes the initial error,
E^{R}_{\mathcal{O}}(\phi)=\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\lVert\dot{\phi}(t)-F\circ\phi(t)\rVert
is the ODE residual error, and
E_{\mathcal{O}}(\phi)=\lVert O(\phi,\cdot)\rVert_{\infty}=\sup\left\{\lVert O(\phi,t)\rVert_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}:t\in[0,T]\right\}
, and
L
is the Lipschitz constant of
F
. Clearly, we have
\max(E^{I}_{\mathcal{O}}(\phi),E^{R}_{\mathcal{O}}(\phi))\leq E_{\mathcal{O}}(\phi)
. In particular, the estimate (
9
) shows that
O
has a unique solution.

Proof.

For brevity, let

\varepsilon\coloneqq E^{R}_{\mathcal{O}}(\phi)=\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\lVert\dot{\phi}(t)-F\circ\phi(t)\rVert.

We have

\varphi(t)=\phi_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}F\circ\varphi(s)ds,\quad t\in[0,T],

(10)

and

\displaystyle\phi(t)

\displaystyle=\phi(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\dot{\phi}(s)ds=\int_{0}^{t}[F\circ\phi(s)+(\dot{\phi}(s)-F\circ\phi(s))]ds,\quad t\in[0,T].

Combining the equations above leads to

\displaystyle\lVert\phi(t)-\varphi(t)\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\phi(0)-\phi_{0}\rVert+\int_{0}^{t}\left(L\lVert\phi(s)-\varphi(s)\rVert+\varepsilon\right)ds

\displaystyle=\lVert\phi(0)-\phi_{0}\rVert+\varepsilon t+L\int_{0}^{t}\lVert\phi(s)-\varphi(s)\rVert ds.

By Gronwall’s inequality, with
\alpha(t)=\lVert\phi(0)-\phi_{0}\rVert+\varepsilon t
and
\beta(t)=L
, and integration by parts, we obtain

\displaystyle\lVert\phi(t)-\varphi(t)\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\alpha(t)+\int_{0}^{t}L\alpha(s)e^{L(t-s)}ds

\displaystyle=\alpha(t)-\alpha(t)e^{L(t-s)}|^{s=t}_{s=0}+\varepsilon\int_{0}^{t}e^{L(t-s)}ds

\displaystyle=\lVert\phi(0)-\phi_{0}\rVert e^{Lt}-\frac{\varepsilon}{L}e^{L(t-s)}|^{s=t}_{s=0}

\displaystyle=\lVert\phi(0)-\phi_{0}\rVert e^{Lt}+\frac{\varepsilon}{L}(e^{Lt}-1),

for all
t\in[0,T]
, which proves (
9
). Clearly, (
9
) also implies that
O
has a unique solution.
∎

Remark 2
.

After training a neural network approximate solution, we can empirically estimate the true solution error using (
9
) as follows. We can simply evaluate
E^{I}_{\mathcal{O}}(\phi)=\lVert\phi(0)-\phi_{0}\rVert
. Furthermore, we can estimate
E^{R}_{\mathcal{O}}(\phi)
by

E^{R}_{\mathcal{O}}(\phi)\leq\max_{n=1,\dots,N}\lVert\dot{\phi}(t_{n})-F\circ\phi(t_{n})\rVert+L_{R}\Delta t,

(11)

where
L_{R}
is a Lipschitz constant of
\dot{\phi}(\cdot)-F\circ\phi(\cdot)
on
[0,T]
, and
\Delta t
is the largest difference between the adjacent
t_{n}
’s, with
\{t_{n}\}
being the test points at which the ODE residual
\dot{\phi}(t_{n})-F\circ\phi(t_{n})
is evaluated. Inequality (
11
) follows from the triangle inequality and the Lipschitz continuity of
\dot{\phi}(\cdot)-F\circ\phi(\cdot)
on
[0,T]
. Note that the right-hand side of (
11
) represents an upper bound on the true ODE residual error
E^{R}_{\mathcal{O}}(\phi)=\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\lVert\dot{\phi}(t)-F\circ\phi(t)\rVert
. In (
11
), we over-approximate
E^{R}_{\mathcal{O}}(\phi)
using the maximum “observed" ODE residual error, enhanced by an error bound employing the Lipschitz constant
L_{R}
. The Lipschitz constant
L_{R}
can be further over-approximated as
L_{\dot{\phi}}+LL_{\phi}
, where
L_{\dot{\phi}}
and
L_{\phi}
are the Lipschitz constants for
\dot{\phi}
and
\phi
, respectively. Generally, determining the Lipschitz constants for a neural network is challenging, particularly for multi-layered networks. Section
B
provides analytical estimates for function composition useful for this task. In practice, we can also directly compute upper bounds of the true residual error
E^{R}_{\mathcal{O}}(\phi)
, e.g., using a satisfiability modulo theories (SMT) solver.

Remark 3
.

As can be seen from its proof, Proposition
8
relies on Gronwall’s inequality to estimate the error between the true and approximate solutions to the IVP. In practice, Gronwall’s inequality, using a uniform Lipschitz constant, cannot offer useful estimates unless the time duration
[0,T]
is very small. To illustrate, consider a moderately sized Lipschitz constant of 5 with a time duration
[0,2]
. Then the term
e^{2\times 5}=e^{10}
in the error estimate would render the bound practically useless. One potential approach to overcome this is to develop validated bounds of neural network solutions that: 1) use a Lipschitz constant matrix instead of a constant; 2) estimate this Lipschitz constant matrix on a smaller domain that is computed as a guaranteed a priori enclosure of the solution on a smaller time interval; 3) continue this process until a given time interval is reached. This can be a potential area of future investigation. We refer the readers to
[
20
, Chapter 4.3]
for validated computation of solutions to IVPs. The numerical example in Section
7
was implemented using such an approach.

O:\,C^{2}(\Omega)\cap C(\bar{\Omega})\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
to be

O(\phi,x)=\begin{pmatrix}\Delta\phi(x)+c(x)\phi(x)-F(x)\\
{\phi(p)-h(p)}\end{pmatrix}

is the projection of

is continuous. Suppose that

is a true solution. Assume that

. Then

where

and
E^{R}_{\mathcal{O}}(\phi)=\sup_{x\in\Omega}\lvert\Delta\phi(x)+c(x)\phi(x)-F(x)\rvert

only depends on

and the diameter of

. For example, if

.

has a unique solution.

Proof.

The proof directly uses an a priori estimate for this class of elliptic PDEs

. Let
u=\phi-\varphi
. Then
u
satisfies

and

u=\phi-h\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega.

Applying a standard a priori estimate for this PDE
[

∎

Proposition 10
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
T
be a positive constant. Define

\Omega_{T}=\Omega\times[0,T]=\left\{(x,t):\,x\in\Omega,\;t\in[0,T]\right\}.

Let
c
and
F
be continuous on
\bar{\Omega}_{T}
with
c\geq-c_{0}
for some nonnegative constant
c_{0}
. Let
h
be a continuous function on
\partial\Omega\times[0,T]
and
\phi_{0}
be a continuous function on
\bar{\Omega}
.

Consider a neural approximation
\phi
to the solution of the following initial/boundary value problem

\displaystyle\phi_{t}-\Delta\phi+c\phi

\displaystyle=F\quad\text{in }\Omega_{T},

(15)

\displaystyle\phi(\cdot,0)

\displaystyle=\phi_{0}\quad\text{on }\Omega,

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle=h\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega\times[0,T].

Pick
O:\,C^{2,1}(\Omega_{T})\times\bar{\Omega}_{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
to be

O(\phi,(t,x))=\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{t}(t,x)-\Delta\phi(t,x)+c(t,x)\phi(t,x)-F(t,x)\\
\phi_{t}(0,x)-\phi_{0}(t,x)\\
\phi_{t}(y)-\phi_{0}(y)\end{pmatrix},

where
y=proj_{[0,T]\times\partial\Omega}((t,x))
. Then
O
is continuous.
Suppose that
\varphi
is the true solution. Assume that
\phi,\varphi\in C^{2,1}(\Omega_{T})\cap C(\bar{\Omega}_{T})
. Then

E_{\varphi}(\phi)=\sup_{\bar{\Omega}}\lvert\phi-\varphi\rvert\leq e^{c_{0}T}\max\left(E^{B}_{\mathcal{O}}(\phi),\,E^{I}_{\mathcal{O}}(\phi)\right)+CE^{R}_{\mathcal{O}}(\phi),

(16)

where

E^{B}_{\mathcal{O}}(\phi)=\sup_{\partial\Omega\times[0,T]}\lvert\phi-h\rvert
,
E^{I}_{\mathcal{O}}(\phi)=\sup_{\bar{\Omega}}\lvert\phi(\cdot,0)-\phi_{0}\rvert
,
and
E^{R}_{\mathcal{O}}(\phi)=\sup_{\bar{\Omega}_{T}}\lvert\phi_{t}-\Delta\phi+c\phi-F\rvert
, and

C>0
can be taken as
\frac{e^{c_{0}T}-1}{c_{0}}
if
c_{0}>0
or
T
if
c_{0}=0
.

Proof.

The proof again relies on an a priori estimate for this class of parabolic PDEs
13
. The choice of the constant
C
is slightly optimized compared with
[
13
, Theorem 5.3.8]
; hence for completeness we provide a direct proof based on the weak maximum principle for this parabolic PDE. The argument follows closely that in
[
13
, Theorem 5.3.8]
.

Let
u=\phi-\varphi
. Then
u
satisfies

Lu\coloneqq u_{t}-\Delta u+cu=(\phi_{t}-\Delta\phi+c\phi-F)-(\varphi_{t}-\Delta\varphi+c\varphi-F)=O(\phi,\cdot)\quad\text{in }\Omega_{T},

u(\cdot,0)=\phi(\cdot,t)-\phi_{0}\quad\text{on }\Omega,

and

u=\phi-\varphi=\phi-h\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega.

Let

B=\max\left(E^{B}_{\mathcal{O}}(\phi),\,E^{I}_{\mathcal{O}}(\phi)\right),\quad E=E^{R}_{\mathcal{O}}(\phi).

It follows that
L(\pm u)\leq E
in
\Omega_{T}
and
\pm u\leq B
on
(\bar{\Omega}\times\left\{0\right\})\cup(\partial\Omega\times[0,T])
.

Consider the auxiliary function

v(x,t)=\begin{cases}e^{c_{0}t}B+\frac{e^{c_{0}t}-1}{c_{0}}F,\quad c_{0}>0,\\
B+Ft,\quad c_{0}=0.\end{cases}

We have

\displaystyle Lv

\displaystyle=c_{0}e^{c_{0}t}B+e^{c_{0}t}F+c\left(e^{c_{0}t}B+\frac{e^{c_{0}t}-1}{c_{0}}F\right)

\displaystyle=(c_{0}+c)e^{c_{0}t}B+e^{c_{0}t}F+c\frac{e^{c_{0}t}-1}{c_{0}}F

\displaystyle\geq e^{c_{0}t}F+F-e^{c_{0}t}F=F,

where simply used the fact that
c\geq-c_{0}
(and
c_{0}+c\geq 0
). The same holds trivially when
c_{0}=0
. Furthermore,
v\geq B
on
(\bar{\Omega}\times\left\{0\right\})\cup(\partial\Omega\times[0,T])
. It follows that
L(\pm u)\leq Lv
in
\Omega_{T}
and
\pm u\leq v
on
(\bar{\Omega}\times\left\{0\right\})\cup(\partial\Omega\times[0,T])
. By the maximum principle
[
13
, Corollary 5.3.4]
, we have
\lvert u\rvert\leq v
in
\Omega_{T}
and the conclusion follows.
∎

F.6
Burgers’ equation

Proposition 13
.

Let
\Omega=[0,L]
and
T
be a positive constant. Define

\Omega_{T}=\Omega\times[0,T]=\left\{(x,t):\,x\in\Omega,\;t\in[0,T]\right\}.

Let
\varphi_{0},\psi_{0}
be continuous functions on
\bar{\Omega}
, and let
c
be a constant.
Let
\varphi
be the true solution to the following initial value problem with periodic boundary conditions

\displaystyle\varphi_{t}+\varphi\varphi_{x}=0

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega_{T},

(34)

\displaystyle\varphi(\cdot,0)=\varphi_{0}

\displaystyle\text{on }\Omega,

\displaystyle\varphi(0,x)=\varphi(L,x)=c

\displaystyle\text{on }[0,T].

Let
\phi
be a neural approximation to the solution of the initial/boundary value problem, satisfying

\displaystyle\phi_{t}+\phi\phi_{x}=\varepsilon_{1}

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega_{T},

(35)

\displaystyle\phi(\cdot,0)=\phi_{0}+\varepsilon_{2}

\displaystyle\text{on }\Omega,

\displaystyle\phi(0,x)=c+\varepsilon_{3}

\displaystyle\text{on }[0,T],

\displaystyle\phi(L,x)=c+\varepsilon_{4}

\displaystyle\text{on }[0,T].

Then

\|\phi(\cdot,t)-\varphi(\cdot,t)\|_{L_{2}[0,L]}\leq\|\varepsilon_{2}\|_{L_{2}[0,L]}+\int_{0}^{t}\|\varepsilon_{1}(\cdot,s)\|_{L_{2}[0,L]}^{2}ds.

(36)

Proof.

Let
u=\phi-\varphi
. Then
u
satisfies

\displaystyle u_{t}+uu_{x}=\varepsilon_{1}

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega_{T},

(37)

\displaystyle u(\cdot,0)=\varepsilon_{2}

\displaystyle\text{on }\Omega,

\displaystyle u(0,t)=\varepsilon_{3}

\displaystyle\text{on }[0,T],

\displaystyle u(L,t)=\varepsilon_{4}

\displaystyle\text{on }[0,T].

Define the energy function

E(t)=\frac{1}{2}\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{L_{2}[0,L]}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{L}u(x,t)^{2}dx.

(38)

\displaystyle E^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{L}uu_{t}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{L}u(\varepsilon_{1}-uu_{x})dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{L}u\varepsilon_{1}-u^{2}u_{x}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{L}u\varepsilon_{1}dx-\frac{1}{3}(u(L,t)^{3}-u[0,T]^{3}).

Let
\varepsilon_{5}(t):=\frac{1}{3}(\varepsilon_{4}(t)^{3}-\varepsilon_{3}(t)^{3})
.
Putting it all together, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{L_{2}[0,L]}^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle u(\cdot,t),\varepsilon_{1}(\cdot,t)\rangle_{L_{2}[0,L]}-\varepsilon_{5}

\displaystyle\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{L_{2}[0,L]}\frac{d}{dt}\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{L_{2}[0,L]}

\displaystyle\leq\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{L_{2}[0,L]}\|\varepsilon_{1}(\cdot,t)\|_{L_{2}[0,L]}-\varepsilon_{5}

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{L_{2}[0,L]}

\displaystyle\leq\|\varepsilon_{1}(\cdot,t)\|_{L_{2}[0,L]}-\frac{\varepsilon_{5}}{\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{L_{2}[0,L]}}

\displaystyle\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{L_{2}[0,L]}

\displaystyle\leq\|\varepsilon_{2}\|_{L_{2}[0,L]}+\int_{0}^{t}\|\varepsilon_{1}(\cdot,s)\|_{L_{2}[0,L]}ds-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\varepsilon_{5}(s)}{\|u(\cdot,s)\|_{L_{2}[0,L]}}ds.

This is challenging to compute an exact bound for
\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{L_{2}[0,L]}
since it is in the RHS. However, we can modify the input space of the neural network using
x\to(\sin(2\pi x/L),\cos(2\pi x/L))
. This forces the boundary error term
\varepsilon_{5}(t)
to be zero everywhere. Thus, we arrive at the following bound

\displaystyle\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{L_{2}[0,L]}

\displaystyle\leq\|\varepsilon_{2}\|_{L_{2}[0,L]}+\int_{0}^{t}\|\varepsilon_{1}(\cdot,s)\|_{L_{2}[0,L]}ds

(39)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{L}\|\varepsilon_{2}\|_{L_{\infty}[0,L]}+\sqrt{L}T\|\varepsilon_{1}\|_{L_{\infty}([0,L]\times[0,T])}.

∎

Corollary 8.1
.

Consider an identical parameterization of
(x,t)
as in Proposition
13
.
Let
\Omega=[0,L]
and
T
be a positive constant.
Let
\varphi_{0},\psi_{0}
be continuous functions on
\bar{\Omega}
, and let
c
be a constant.
Let
\varphi
be the true solution to the following initial value problem with periodic boundary conditions

\displaystyle\varphi_{t}+\varphi\varphi_{x}=\nu\varphi_{xx}

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega_{T},

(40)

\displaystyle\varphi(\cdot,0)=\varphi_{0}

\displaystyle\text{on }\Omega,

\displaystyle\varphi(0,t)=\varphi(L,t)=c

\displaystyle\text{on }[0,T].

Let
\phi
be a neural approximation to the solution of the initial/boundary value problem, satisfying

\displaystyle\phi_{t}+\phi\phi_{x}=\nu\varphi_{xx}+\varepsilon_{1}

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega_{T},

(41)

\displaystyle\phi(\cdot,0)=\phi_{0}+\varepsilon_{2}

\displaystyle\text{on }\Omega,

\displaystyle\phi(0,t)=c+\varepsilon_{3}

\displaystyle\text{on }[0,T],

\displaystyle\phi(L,t)=c+\varepsilon_{4}

\displaystyle\text{on }[0,T].

Assume error functions satisfy

\varepsilon_{1}(x,t)\in C(\Omega_{T})\cap L_{2}(\Omega_{T})\quad\varepsilon_{2}(x)\in C(\Omega)\cap L_{2}(\Omega)\quad\varepsilon_{3}(x),\varepsilon_{4}(x)\in C(\partial\Omega\times[0,T])\cap L_{2}(\partial\Omega\times[0,T])\quad

Let
p\in\Omega_{T}
and let
O:\,C^{2,1}(\Omega_{T})\times\bar{\Omega}_{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{4}
be

O(\phi,p)=\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon_{1}(p)\\
\epsilon_{2}(\mathrm{proj}_{\Omega}(p))\\
\epsilon_{3}(\mathrm{proj}_{\partial\Omega\times[0,T]}(p))\\
\epsilon_{4}(\mathrm{proj}_{\partial\Omega\times[0,T]}(p))\end{pmatrix}

Due to continuity of the error terms and the projection operator,
O
is continuous.
Let
\phi
be a neural approximation to the solution of the initial/boundary value problem.
Assume that
\phi,\varphi\in C^{2,1}(\Omega_{T})\cap C(\bar{\Omega}_{T})
. Then

\|\phi(\cdot,t)-\varphi(\cdot,t)\|_{L_{2}[0,L]}\leq\|\varepsilon_{2}\|_{L_{2}[0,L]}+\int_{0}^{t}\|\varepsilon_{1}(\cdot,s)\|_{L_{2}[0,L]}^{2}ds.

(42)

Proof.

Assume the neural approximation error of
\phi
is governed by functions
\varepsilon_{1}(x,t),\varepsilon_{2}(x),\varepsilon_{3}(t)
, which are all bounded in the
L_{2}
norm.
Let
u=\phi-\varphi
. Then
u
satisfies

\displaystyle u_{t}+uu_{x}-\nu u_{xx}=\varepsilon_{1}

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega_{T},

(43)

\displaystyle u(\cdot,0)=\varepsilon_{2}

\displaystyle\text{on }\Omega,

\displaystyle u[0,T]=\varepsilon_{3}

\displaystyle\text{on }[0,T],

\displaystyle u(L,t)=\varepsilon_{4}

\displaystyle\text{on }[0,T].

Define the energy function

E(t)=\frac{1}{2}\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{L_{2}[0,L]}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{L}u(x,t)^{2}dx.

(44)

Multiply both sides of the PDE and integrate along
[0,L]
.

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{L_{2}[0,L]}^{2}+\int_{0}^{L}u^{2}u_{x}dx+\nu\|u_{x}\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=\langle u(\cdot,t),\epsilon_{1}(\cdot,t)\rangle.

After modifying the input space of the neural network using
x\to(\sin(2\pi x/L),\cos(2\pi x/L))
, we force periodicity in the boundary:
\epsilon_{3}\equiv\epsilon_{4}
. Therefore, we can remove the following term

\int_{0}^{L}u^{2}u_{x}dx=\frac{1}{3}(u(L,t)^{3}-u[0,T]^{3})=\frac{1}{3}(\epsilon_{4}^{3}(t)-\epsilon_{3}^{3}(t))=0.

Finally, we drop the
\nu\|u_{x}\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}^{2}
term to obtain an inequality.

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{L_{2}[0,L]}^{2}\leq\langle u(\cdot,t),\epsilon_{1}(\cdot,t)\rangle.

We repeat the steps from the proof of Proposition
13
to obtain the following bound.

\displaystyle\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{L_{2}[0,L]}

\displaystyle\leq\|\varepsilon_{2}\|_{L_{2}[0,L]}+\int_{0}^{t}\|\varepsilon_{1}(\cdot,s)\|_{L_{2}[0,L]}ds

(45)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{L}\|\varepsilon_{2}\|_{L_{\infty}[0,L]}+\sqrt{L}T\|\varepsilon_{1}\|_{L_{\infty}([0,L]\times[0,T])}.

Equivalently, if we equip
O
with the

norm, then

\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{L_{2}[0,L]}\leq\sqrt{L}\max(1,T)\|O(u(\cdot),\cdot)\|_{O},

thus verifying the assumptions of Theorem
4
.
∎

G.3
Verification Procedure

All certified bounds in linear PDE settings are computed using autoLiRPA
37
.

Domain Partitioning.

Directly estimating residual or solution bounds over the entire computational domain leads to significant over-estimation due to relaxation conservativeness. To mitigate this effect, we partition the domain into
100^{n}
uniform subgrids.

Here,
n
denotes the intrinsic dimension of the quantity being verified:

•

n=2
when certifying PDE residuals or reference errors defined over space-time domains,

•

n=1
when certifying boundary or initial
conditions defined over lower-dimensional subsets.

Verification is then performed independently on each subdomain.

L_{\infty}
Bounds.

For uniform norm certification, we compute a certified
L_{\infty}
upper bound of the target quantity on each subdomain using backward linear relaxation. The global
L_{\infty}
bound is then obtained by taking the maximum over all subdomains.

L_{2}
Bounds.

For
L_{2}
-based estimates (e.g., Proposition
11
), we compute certified
L_{\infty}
bounds on each subdomain, square these bounds, and aggregate them using a Riemann-type sum over the partition. This yields a certified upper bound on the
L_{2}
norm over the entire domain.
