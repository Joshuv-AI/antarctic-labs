Blood-brain barrier integrity and brain entropy in reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome
Authors: Chia-Hung Wu, Tun-Wei Hsu, Yu-Hsiang Ling, Yen-Feng Wang, Chien-Yuan Lin, Chia-Wei Lee, Jiing-Feng Lirng, Shuu-Jiun Wang, Shih-Pin Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundElucidating functional brain dynamics under different blood–brain barrier (BBB) permeabilities may resolve the enigmatic pathophysiology of reversible cerebral vasoconstriction (RCVS); however, relevant investigations are lacking. We aimed determine the relationship between brain functional dynamics and BBB permeability in patients with RCVS.MethodsWe prospectively recruited RCVS patients and healthy controls (HCs) from November 2016 to January 2023 in Headache Center in a tertiary medical center (> 3000 beds) and adjacent communities. RCVS patients who were diagnosed according to the International Headache Society criteria, and age- and sex-matched HCs were enrolled. Normalized entropy, derived from resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI), was compared at the network and parcel levels between RCVS patients with and without BBB disruption. Dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI (DCE-MRI)-derived Ktrans BBB permeability and ultrasonographic findings were analyzed.ResultsIn total, 188 subjects (100 RCVS + 88 HCs) were enrolled. Compared with the HCs, the RCVS patients had greater entropy (p = 0.014), which was greater in the RCVS patients without than in those with (adjusted p = 0.040) BBB disruption. Compared with HCs, patients without BBB disruption had greater entropy between 60 and 90 days after headache onset (adjusted p = 0.006). No significant differences in entropy were noted between disease stages in BBB-disrupted patients. Parcels within subnetworks of the Default mode network (Default A and C) exhibited higher entropy in patients without BBB disruption. In specific anatomical locations, entropy values were negatively correlated with ultrasonographic Lindegaard index vasoconstriction severity (p = 4.3 × 10− 6; within the Default A network) and DCE-MRI Ktrans BBB permeability (p = 0.005; within the Default C network).ConclusionThe differential changes in normalized entropy suggest that increased rs-fMRI signal complexity may reflect a compensatory functional response to abrupt vasoconstriction. In contrast, the loss of entropy fluctuations in the context of severe BBB disruption indicates a state of impaired cerebral autoregulation in patients with RCVS. The compensatory capacity decreased as vasoconstriction or BBB disruption exacerbated.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10194-026-02409-9.

Introduction

Reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome (RCVS) is a rare and underrecognized disorder with thunderclap headaches [1, 2]. Although typically benign, potentially fatal complications may include posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke [3]. The pathological mechanisms of RCVS, including vascular dynamics [4–7], regional perfusion [3], genetic signatures [8], autonomic regulations [9, 10], glymphatic functions [11–13] and blood-brain barrier (BBB) integrity [14, 15], have been extensively researched. While our studies have demonstrated the clinical implications of BBB dynamics in RCVS [10, 16], the relationship between BBB dynamics and brain function status remains to be explored.

Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) is a noninvasive technique for mapping brain function by analyzing blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) signals [17]. While rs-fMRI has been widely used to investigate neurovascular disorders [18], its application in RCVS has been limited to static graph theory analysis [19], which lacks crucial information regarding brain functional dynamics [20]. RCVS is characterized by pronounced fluctuations in vascular dynamics [4, 7] and, critically, temporal changes in BBB integrity throughout the disease course [14]. To capture these rapid shifts, we employed edge-centric fMRI analysis, an advanced technique that quantifies dynamic brain activity using entropy to describe connection fluctuations [20, 21]. Unlike standard region-to-region connectivity, this edge-centric analysis calculates normalized entropy, an index that captures the dispersion of a node’s edge affiliations across multiple communities, thereby providing a more granular look into the network disruptions unique to RCVS [20, 21].

The primary outcome of this study was to establish functional dynamics by edge analysis and elucidate the related BBB manifestations in patients with RCVS. The secondary outcome was to evaluate the vascular and clinical presentations with edge functional dynamics.

Methods

Ethics

The study protocol received approval from the Institutional Review Board of Taipei Veterans General Hospital (2015-11-005 C; 2021-07-019BC; 2025-09-006B). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Before their enrollment, all participants provided written informed consent. Since patients with severe neurological complications were not enrolled (as detailed in the Participants subsection), all written informed consent was obtained directly from the patient themselves.

Participants

Study participants, including patients with RCVS and healthy controls (HCs), were prospectively recruited between November 2016 and January 2023. RCVS patients were recruited from the Headache Clinics and Emergency Department at Taipei Veterans General Hospital (TVGH), a tertiary medical center with more than 3000 beds. The diagnosis was established using criteria consistent with the International Classification of Headache Disorders, third edition (ICHD-3) [22] and our previous studies [3, 13, 14]. A thorough headache questionnaire and detailed neurological examinations were conducted for all RCVS participants. We treated all acute-stage patients with nimodipine since it was unethical to leave them untreated [23]. To prevent potential effects on hemodynamics, participants were closely monitored with meticulous dose adjustments. Any episodes of systolic blood pressure (SBP) below 100 mmHg were promptly addressed with hydration or dose tapering [19, 24]. Patients presenting with severe neurological complications that may compromise their ability to cooperate during MRI acquisition were not considered for enrollment.

RCVS patients were categorized based on the presence or absence of macroscopic BBB disruption (Supplementary Fig. 1) [12, 13, 15], which was identified as visible periarterial gadolinium-based contrast agent (GBCA) leakage on contrast-enhanced T2 fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (CE-T2-FLAIR) strictly scanned at 9 min after intravenous administration [13]. RCVS patients were further classified into subgroups based on the interval from headache onset to MRI acquisition in the time-trend analysis. The subgroups were as follows: ≤ 30 days; > 30 and < 60 days; ≥ 60 and < 90 days; ≥ 90 days. The acute RCVS was defined by a 30-day cutoff, adhering to the ICHD-3 criteria (6.7.3.1) [22] for acute headache attributed to RCVS, which characterizes the condition as being present or recurrent for up to one month without new significant headaches thereafter. This definition was also consistent with our previous investigations into BBB dynamics [14] and brain functional dynamics [19] in patients with RCVS. The remission RCVS was defined using a 90-day threshold, following the expert consensus [1, 2] that the reversibility of RCVS vasoconstriction should be demonstrated within 12 weeks (3 months) of headache onset. Furthermore, we implemented a 60-day cutoff to delineate entropy changes occurring between 30 and 90 days. This time point was adopted since our pilot studies identified lowest BBB permeability [14] and the reversibility of regional functional metrics [19] at 60 days after headache onset in RCVS.

HCs were recruited from the surrounding communities of TVGH. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria were listed in Table 1.

Prior to study enrollment, a power analysis was performed to determine the prospective target sample size assuming an approximate 1:1 allocation ratio between the patient and control cohorts. Based on an estimated effect size of Cohen’s d = 0.44 from our previous RCVS functional study [19], with a defined alpha level of 0.05 and a statistical power of 80%, the required sample size was determined to be 83 participants per group. To achieve this target and eliminate potential confounding effects driven by demographic differences, HCs were prospectively group-matched with the patient cohort based on age and sex. Operationally, this matching protocol was executed on a rolling basis throughout the recruitment phase. We maintained a dynamic demographic matrix of the enrolled RCVS patients, reviewing the cumulative age and sex distributions monthly. HCs who aligned with the evolving demographics of the patient cohort were enrolled. This ensured that the baseline demographics of both groups remained balanced throughout the entire enrollment period.

Imaging protocols

All MR exams were conducted using a 3-Tesla (T) MRI scanner (MR750, GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL). High-resolution 3D T1-weighted spoiled gradient-recalled echo (3D-T1-SPGR) sequence was used, resulting in a voxel size of 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 mm. rs-fMRI data were obtained (repetition time (TR) = 2500 ms; echo time (TE) = 30 ms; flip angle = 90°; matrix size = 64 × 64; field of view (FOV) = 224 × 224 mm; volume = 20045 slices with a thickness of 3.5 mm; interleaved slice acquisition). Participants were instructed to remain still, relax, and keep their eyes open during the rs-fMRI scans.

Dynamic-contrast enhanced MRI (DCE-MRI) was also conducted in 40 RCVS patients immediately after GBCA administration to calculate the BBB permeability [14]. Whole-brain Ktrans, a parameter quantifying BBB permeability, was acquired by utilizing the same methods and parameters in our pilot study [14]. MR data synchronization and registration were performed automatically on the software MIStar (version 3.2.63; Apollo Medical Imaging Technology, Melbourne, Australia).

Transcranial color-coded sonography (TCCS) was performed on the same day as the MR exams. TCCS was used to measure hemodynamic parameters, including mean flow velocity, pulsatility index (PI), and resistance index (RI) of the middle cerebral artery (MCA) and internal carotid artery (ICA) [11, 14, 19]. From these measurements, specific indices were calculated to assess cerebral vasoconstriction, including Lindegaard index (LI), mean PI (MMPI) and mean RI (MMRI) in the distal and proximal M1 segments of MCA and mean RI ratio (MRIR) along M1 segment were calculated [11, 14, 19]. The LI was calculated by dividing the mean flow velocity of the MCA by the mean flow velocity of the ipsilateral distal extracranial ICA [4], and reflected the degree of vasoconstriction of the MCA.

BBB disruption and permeability evaluations

In this study, the BBB alterations were evaluated by both macroscopic and microscopic levels. These two levels were not equivalent. The macroscopic BBB disruption represented a profound BBB breakdown visualized qualitatively as GBCA leakage into the perivascular spaces on CE-F2-FLAIR [13, 15]. On the other hand, the microscopic BBB permeability was measured by the Ktrans via DCE-MRI [14], which quantified the rate of more subtle, visually imperceptible, GBCA leakage into the extravascular extracellular space [25, 26].

To quantify microscopic BBB permeability, the volume transfer constant (Ktrans) in this study was computed using the modified Tofts model [14, 25]. This pharmacokinetic model calculates tracer kinetics under the assumption of dynamic equilibrium between the vascular plasma compartment and the extravascular-extracellular space [25]. Methodologically, the workflow for deriving the Ktrans index involved a sequential processing pipeline: (1) acquisition of T1-weighted DCE-MRI sequence, (2) conversion of time-varying MR signal intensities into gadolinium concentration curves, (3) extraction of a patient-specific arterial input function, and (4) voxel-wise fitting of the pharmacokinetic transport equations. These steps were processed and completed on the software MIStar [14].

Imaging analysis for rs-fMRI

rs-fMRI data were preprocessed using Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM12) and the Data Processing Assistant for Resting-State fMRI (DPARSF) V6.0 Advanced Edition [27]. The first five volumes were discarded to allow for magnetization equilibrium and participant acclimation. Subsequent preprocessing steps included slice timing correction, realignment, coregistration, and segmentation combined with Diffeomorphic Anatomical Registration Through Exponentiated Lie algebra (DARTEL) using high-resolution T1 images [28]. DARTEL was used to transform individual native space images into Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space. The normalized BOLD volumes were resampled to 2-mm isotropic voxels and smoothed with a 6-mm full-width at half-maximum Gaussian kernel. Linear detrending and nuisance regression were performed to remove signals from white matter, cerebrospinal fluid, and motion parameters based on the Friston 24-parameter model [29]. Finally, bandpass filtering (0.01–0.1 Hz) was applied to reduce low-frequency drift and high-frequency noise.

A functional parcellation scheme was used to divide the cerebral cortex into 200 parcels based on local and global fMRI signal similarity [30]. Edges were networked based on the similarity of their cofluctuation time courses using k-means clustering with a normalized Euclidean distance metric [21]. The clustering was repeated multiple times, and the most stable partition was selected based on a minimum variation in information. Projecting these edge networks onto nodes revealed that edges from each brain region were distributed across multiple networks. The proportion of edges per network was calculated for each node, yielding a probability distribution. The entropy of this distribution quantifies the dispersion of a node’s edge affiliations: low entropy indicates an even distribution across networks, whereas high entropy indicates a concentration in fewer networks [21]. Normalized entropy, bounded between 0 and 1, was used as the final dispersion metric. Differences in normalized entropy between patient and control groups were then calculated for each node.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics are presented as mean ± standard deviation when applicable. This study included 2 major study Bundles (Supplementary Fig. 2). In Bundle 1, the parcel investigations, independent-samples t-tests were used to compare normalized entropy values in all parcels between patients with RCVS and HCs (Fig. 1A). For comparisons of normalized entropy between HCs, RCVS patients with and without macroscopic BBB disruption, and between HCs and RCVS patients across different stages (time-trend analysis), one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed. Statistical significance was defined as an adjusted p < 0.05, with subsequent post-hoc comparisons performed using Bonferroni correction.

In Bundle 2, the network investigations, independent-samples t-tests were initially performed to assess differences in normalized entropy within each network between RCVS patients with and without macroscopic BBB disruption. Given that 17 networks were under investigation [30], Bonferroni correction was applied to control for multiple comparisons. Adjusted statistical significance was therefore defined as p < q = 0.05/17 = 0.0029. Following the initial analysis, a more detailed examination of normalized entropy differences was conducted within the potential networks with statistical significance. Independent-samples t-tests were used to evaluate entropy differences in specific parcels. In the Default A (14 parcels) and C (6 parcels) networks, the significance was adjusted to p < q = 0.05/14 = 0.0036 and 0.05/6 = 0.0083 respectively. Correlations of normalized entropy values with DCE-MRI Ktrans, TCCS parameters, and clinical parameters from the questionnaires were evaluated by Pearson’s correlation coefficients.

Results

Participants

Totally, 195 (106 RCVS + 89 HCs) participants were initially recruited and underwent complete rs-fMRI (Supplementary Fig. 2). After excluding data from 7 participants (6 RCVS + 1 HC), the remaining 188 subjects (100 RCVS + 88 HCs) were included in both Bundles 1 and 2. For further clinical, vascular, and BBB permeability investigations, a subset of RCVS patients with completed questionnaires (n = 80), undergoing TCCS (n = 81), and DCE-MRI (n = 40) were included. The overall headache onset to MRI interval was 54.4 ± 36.6 days for all RCVS patients (n = 100). When subgrouped by timeframe, the intervals were 8.8 ± 5.4 days for the ≤ 30-day group (n = 32), 52.3 ± 4.5 days for the > 30 to < 60-day group (n = 16), 74.7 ± 9.9 days for the ≥ 60 to < 90-day group (n = 40), and 109.4 ± 25.4 days for the ≥ 90-day group (n = 12).

Bundle 1: Parcel investigations demonstrated higher entropy in RCVS patients without BBB disruption

Mean entropy of all 200 parcels was significantly higher in RCVS patients compared to HCs (0.6708 ± 0.1347 vs. 0.6675 ± 0.1398, p = 0.014; Table 2; Fig. 1B). RCVS patients without macroscopic BBB disruption (0.6720 ± 0.1340) showed significantly higher normalized entropy than those with BBB disruption (0.6661 ± 0.1355, adjusted p = 0.040) and HCs (adjusted p = 0.041; Fig. 1C). In time-trend analysis, RCVS patients without macroscopic BBB disruption between 60 and 90 days (0.6745 ± 0.1247) were presented with higher entropy than HCs (0.6675 ± 0.1398; adjusted p = 0.006; Fig. 1D; Supplementary Table 1). No significant entropy differences between RCVS subjects with macroscopic BBB disruption ≤ 30, between 60 and 90 days or HCs (Fig. 1E).

Bundle 2: Network investigations revealed higher entropy in default A and C networks in RCVS patients without BBB disruption

Among all the 17 investigated networks, the normalized entropy was higher in RCVS patients without than with macroscopic BBB disruption regarding Default A (0.6197 ± 0.1303 vs. 0.5792 ± 0.1216; p = 2.0E-6 < q = 0.05/17; Fig. 2A) and Default C (0.6435 ± 0.1270 vs. 0.6027 ± 0.1305; p = 0.0015 < q; Fig. 2B) networks. Other networks demonstrated no significant entropy differences between patients with and without macroscopic BBB disruption (Table 3).

Within Default A network, RCVS patients demonstrated higher normalized entropy in patients without than with macroscopic BBB disruption between days 60–90 (p = 0.046; Fig. 2C). Discussion

In this study, we characterized dynamic brain functional changes in RCVS patients. We demonstrated increased entropy in subjects without BBB disruption across parcel and network investigations via edge analysis, an fMRI approach, to better understand functional dynamics [20, 21]. Further anatomical investigations within the Default A and Default C networks revealed significant negative correlations between the entropy values and both the LI on TCCS and the Ktrans value on DCE-MRI. These findings indicate that brain functional dynamics in RCVS are closely linked to both vasoconstriction and BBB permeability.

RCVS patients exhibited higher entropy than HCs. This finding was consistent with a recent study showing a positive correlation between blood pressure and entropy values [31] since blood pressure surge is characteristic of RCVS [32]. Furthermore, the temporal changes in entropy may also represent functional fluctuations in the RCVS disease course. The highest entropy values were observed between 60 and 90 days after headache onset in subjects without macroscopic BBB disruption. This timing is particularly meaningful as BBB permeability was reported to have reached its lowest level throughout the disease course approximately 60 days post-onset [14]. The peak entropy observed within this window likely reflects a compensatory mechanism required to maintain brain homeostasis. Supporting this interpretation, studies have reported increased entropy in patients following cerebral infarction [33], in which maintaining sufficient cerebral blood flow (CBF) is the primary goal to restore homeostasis [34]. Given that RCVS is characterized by low CBF during its acute stage [35], this perfusion deficit shortly after vasoconstriction may induce compensatory mechanisms to maintain homeostasis [35]. These adaptive responses in cerebral autoregulation could increase functional fluctuations and, in turn, increase entropy. In contrast, the absence of stage-related entropy differences in subjects with macroscopic BBB disruption indicated a loss of compensatory capacity to maintain normal homeostasis in circumstances of profoundly impaired auroregulation [10]. Consistently, entropy was higher in patients with macroscopic BBB disruption, supporting the notion that severe BBB damage is associated with failure of brain autoregulation.

The negative correlations of entropy with Ktrans and LI suggest that autoregulatory dynamics depend on both microscopic BBB permeability and vasoconstriction severity. Since microscopic BBB permeability [14] and LI vasoconstriction severity [4] were greater in the first 30 days (acute stage) compared to 60 days after headache onset, these inverse relationships were consistent with our finding that entropy in RCVS peaks after 60 days. The negative correlation between entropy and Ktrans values occurred specifically in the left parahippocampal cortex, in which altered brain function was depicted in response to noxious stimulation in headache patients [36]. Interestingly, the left parahippocampal gyrus also presents with reduced gray matter volume in headache patients [37]. As an anatomical region highly sensitive to headache attacks, these localized entropy dynamics may be more readily detectable. On the other hand, the negative entropy correlation with LI was located in the left precuneus posterior cingulate gyrus, in which a higher regional homogeneity was depicted in headache patients [38]. Previous studies indicated a negative correlation between entropy and regional homogeneity in headache patients [39], and no correlation between LI and BBB permeability in RCVS [14]. Given that higher CBF increased regional homogeneity in cerebrovascular disorders [40], we speculated that the observed negative correlation between entropy and LI vasoconstriction severity may reflect a compensatory need, through entropy’s inverse relationship with regional homogeneity [39], to restore or augment CBF in the setting of vasoconstriction [33, 34, 40].

The higher entropy in RCVS subjects without macroscopic BBB disruption was specifically confined to Default A and C networks. Within Default C network, the highest entropy occurred between 60 and 90 days after headache onset. These findings were parallel to our previous study demonstrating higher internetwork connectivity of Default mode network in RCVS over 60 days [19] since Default A and C networks are subcomponents of Default mode networks [30]. The Default mode network generally regulates self-related tasks [41], and Default A [42] and C [43] networks are specifically linked to self-referential decisions and anticipation of potential future stimulation, respectively. Furthermore, a previous study depicted the increased entropy after pain at prefrontal cortex [31], an anatomical location within Default A network that presented greater entropy in our subjects without BBB disruption. Therefore, we speculate that the entropy patterns within Default A and C networks reflect the brain’s functional adaptation and preparation for future changes following the initial headache onset and subsequent vascular instability in subjects with RCVS.

Strengths and limitations

All the MR data in this study were acquired from the same 3T MRI machine using an identical protocol. We also carefully applied Bonferroni correction to mitigate potential family-wise type I errors in multiple comparisons across all investigations. Since RCVS is characterized by dynamic BBB permeability [14] and vasoconstriction severity [4, 7], our fMRI approach with edge analysis is well-suited to capture these functional fluctuations. We also recruited a comparable number of HCs to provide robust data regarding these brain function dynamics in RCVS. However, we acknowledge some limitations. The DCE-MRI was performed in a subset of RCVS patients (n = 40 out of 100) instead of all subjects. Since DCE-MRI requires a longer scan time and more sophisticated protocols, mandatory inclusion for all participants was potentially against the participants’ willingness. Furthermore, to avoid unnecessary GBCA administration [44], DCE-MRI was not performed in HCs. However, with comparisons between subjects with and without CE-T2-FLAIR-detectable macroscopic BBB disruption and given that RCVS is a disease with dynamic BBB permeability in nature [14], we believe our data are evident to demonstrate the roles of BBB integrity in RCVS brain functions. Although CE scans were not performed in HCs [44], we carefully inspected the thin-sliced 3D T1-SPGR in all orientations to exclude HCs with any potential intracranial space-occupying lesion. This rigorous screening avoided potential lesions affecting BBB integrity in HCs. The LI on TCCS reflected the severity of vasoconstriction within the MCA territories. Because the hemodynamic alterations of RCVS frequently involve other arterial territories, particularly the posterior circulation [7], the utility of the LI may be limited in reflecting the full extent of diffuse vasoconstriction. However, given that TCCS has limited capacity and reproducibility in detecting vasoconstrictions in other cerebral arteries, we employed only the most well validated and reliable indices in our study. Patients presenting with severe, highly acute complications, including but not limited to profound global aphasia, altered mental status, or unstable medical conditions, were naturally not enrolled since they may not cooperate with the MRI scanning. Consequently, our findings could only reflect the dynamic functional status of patients with pure cephalalgic form of RCVS and may not be generalizable to those who are critically ill or with neurological complications. Although our data demonstrated different functional responses between subjects with and without macroscopic BBB disruption during the acute-to-remission transition (60 to 90 days after headache onset), definite evidence regarding an ideal timeframe for detecting differences between the overall RCVS cohort and HCs remains limited. More longitudinal studies with standardized serial imaging protocols are warranted to precisely delineate the temporal dynamics of brain entropy in this population.

Conclusions

Compared with HCs, patients with RCVS exhibited higher fMRI entropy, with the increase most pronounced in those without macroscopic BBB disruption. This increase in entropy was primarily localized to the Default A and C networks, which may indicate heightened functional demands and dynamic engagement in response to a vasoconstriction episode when autoregulatory capacity is still preserved, before severe BBB damage. The negative correlations of entropy with LI and Ktrans also suggest that entropy may serve as a functional marker of vascular and BBB pathophysiology in RCVS.
