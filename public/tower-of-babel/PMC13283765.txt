Ontogenetic changes in femoral morphology and joint loads in children
Authors: Hans Kainz, Gabriel Tilmann Mindler, Andreas Kranzl, Martin Svehlik, Willi Koller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsThis study aimed to quantify ontogenetic changes in femoral morphology – specifically femoral head and condyle sizes – and joint loads in children.MethodsMRI and 3D movement data were recorded from nine children without any known pathologies at two timepoints, two years apart. Femoral geometry was segmented from the MRI to quantify femoral head and medial and lateral condyle volumes. Personalized MRI-informed musculoskeletal models were created and used to estimate hip and knee joint contact forces (JCFs) based on the 3D movement data. Multiple linear regression models were used to assess whether changes in JCF and femoral morphology can be predicted from simple measures such as age, body weight, and height.ResultsBoth femoral joint contact geometry and absolute JCF significantly increase with age. However, when normalized to body weight, JCF remained stable – or even decreased in the case of medial condyle JCF – over time. Predicting changes in JCF based on the simple measures of age, weight, and height worked reasonably well for hip JCF (R2 = 0.67) and lateral condyle JCF (R2 = 0.78), but less well for medial condyle JCF (R2 = 0.41). Changes in femoral morphology could be predicted with moderate accuracy, with R2 values ranging from 0.40 for lateral condyle volume to 0.59 for femoral head volume.ConclusionOur findings demonstrate ontogenetic increases in both absolute JCF and joint contact surface areas, which may help maintain relatively stable cartilage pressures during growth. These data provide valuable reference values for differentiating normal developmental patterns from pathological alterations in femoral morphology and joint loading. Introduction

The mechanostat theory, proposed by Harold Frost1 in the 1960s, suggests that biological structures adapt to mechanical loads through remodelling and modelling processes. This theory builds on earlier foundational work by Julius Wolff, Wilhelm Roux, and Friedrich Pauwels. Wolff’s Law,2 first proposed in 1892, posits that bone remodels in response to mechanical stresses, with trabeculae aligning along lines of stress to optimize structural efficiency. Roux3 introduced the concept of ‘functional adaptation’, emphasizing the role of mechanical forces in guiding tissue differentiation and formation. Pauwels4 later expanded on these ideas, linking specific mechanical stimuli, e.g. tension, compression, and shear, to the formation of different tissues, including cartilage and bone. Together, these theories laid the groundwork for understanding how biological structures, such as bones and cartilage, sense mechanical loads and adjust their strength accordingly to maintain structural integrity.5 The adjustment in strength of bones due to internal stresses might be caused by internal (e.g. changing bone density), and external (e.g. changing bone size and shape, remodelling processes).

In children, bones are constantly growing and remodelling in response to mechanical stimuli caused by weightbearing activities such as walking. Bone loads depend on a person’s body weight (BW), musculoskeletal geometry,6,7 and walking pattern.8,9 For example, changes in walking pattern, e.g. longer steps due to growth-related increased leg length, leads to altered joint moments and muscles forces, and therefore increased bone loads. Even if the gait pattern remains the same, growth-related changes in the shape of bones, e.g. decreased femoral anteversion angle, would alter the moment arms of several muscles, consequently leading to altered muscles forces and bone loads.6,10,11 In typically developing children, the BW, bony geometry, and walking pattern might change over time, and consequently affect bone loads.

The femur is one of the largest bones in the body. Its size, shape, and strength undergo significant changes from infancy to adolescence due to both biological factors, such as hormones and genetics, and mechanical factors, including the loads experienced during daily activities.12 Growth charts, such as those developed by Digby13 and Pritchett,14 provide critical insights into longitudinal growth patterns. These charts are widely used in clinical settings to predict growth and identify discrepancies. For example, Pritchett14 demonstrated that approximately 70% of femoral growth occurs at the distal growth plate, with growth rates varying significantly by age. Such data are essential for understanding normal developmental patterns and distinguishing them from pathological alterations.

Medical images can be used to quantify the bony morphology. With regard to femoral bone loads, the joint contact surfaces at the hip and knee joint are of particular interest. The same joint contact forces (JCFs), but a larger contact area, would lead to lower cartilage stresses. Numerous medical imaging studies have quantified growth-related changes in femoral morphology; cross-sectional studies have shown that the diameter of the femoral head and femoral head epiphysis increase with age in children.15-18 A few longitudinal studies have highlighted that the diameter of the femoral head, as well as the diameter of the proximal and distal femoral growth plates, increase with age.19-21 Statistical shape modelling based on CT scans of 333 paediatric femora from children aged four to 18 years revealed that age-related changes include the development and progressive enlargement of the femoral condyles.22

Bone loads cannot be measured in a noninvasive way. However, musculoskeletal simulations based on personalized models and 3D movement data can be used to estimate bone loads.23 Such simulations have been extensively used to increase our insights in internal loads during dynamic movements in healthy people and patients with various pathologies.24-26 Personalized models, e.g. informed by medical images,27,28 are recommended to achieve realistic estimations of bone loads.10 Most musculoskeletal simulation studies are based on cross-sectional data and show how the gait pattern,9,29 or bony morphology,6,10 influence bone loads. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, only one longitudinal study is available which investigated JCFs in growing children.30 This study, however, neglected changes in femoral morphology, which could have affected JCFs and consequently cartilage stresses.30

Understanding ontogenetic changes in femoral morphology and joint loading in children is essential for advancing our knowledge of bone growth. In particular, the development of growth charts for femoral dimensions and joint loads in typically developing children is crucial for distinguishing normal variation from pathological changes. However, to date, quantitative research on these developmental changes remains limited. This study aimed to assess growth-related alterations in specific femoral morphology parameters, including femoral head volume and medial and lateral condyle volumes, as well as JCFs at the hip, medial condyle, and lateral condyle. Additionally, we evaluated whether these changes could be predicted using simple measures such as age, BW, and height.

Methods

Data collection

We collected data from nine children (three female participants, six male participants) with no known leg deformities or other pathologies at two timepoints, two years apart (Table I). Ethics approval for this study was obtained from the corresponding local ethics committee (reference number 00578).

Demographic details of participants.

In both data collection sessions, a 3D gait analysis and MRI scans were recorded (Figure 1). The 3D gait analysis included marker trajectories, ground reaction forces, and electromyography of seven lower limb muscles (tibialis anterior, gastrocnemius medialis, soleus, peroneus longus, vastus lateralis, biceps femoris, gluteus medius) per leg and was performed on the same day as the MRI scans.

Overview of the data collection, model personalization, and analysis. Subject-specific models were created by personalizing femoral shape, segment dimensions, and muscle properties of a generic musculoskeletal model. These models were used to calculate joint contact forces. To quantify femoral morphology, each femur was segmented, and geometrical shapes were fitted to the femoral head and each condyle.

A 12-camera motion capture system (Vicon Motion Systems, UK) with a camera sampling frequency of 200 Hz was used to record marker trajectories. An extended Plug-in Gait (Vicon Motion Systems, UK) marker set was used, including additional cluster markers on the thigh and shank segments and a marker at the fifth metatarsal head. Simultaneously, ground reaction forces were acquired with 1,000 Hz using five force plates (Kistler Instrumente, Switzerland). Muscle activations, measured with electromyography, were recorded with 1,000 Hz. All participants performed several gait trials with a self-selected walking speed. Marker trajectories were captured, labelled, and filtered (Butterworth fourth order, 6 Hz low-pass filter) in Nexus 2.12.1 (Vicon Motion System). Ground reaction forces were filtered with a fourth-order zero-lag low-pass Butterworth filter with a cutoff frequency of 20 Hz. Muscle activations were de-meaned, rectified, band-pass filtered (20 Hz to 400 Hz), and filtered with a moving average filter with a window size of 50 ms.

MRIs of each femur were collected using a 3T magnetic resonance scanner (MAGNETOM Vida; Siemens, Germany) with a T1-vibe or T1-dixon sequence with voxel sizes between 0.8 × 0.8 × 0.7 mm and 1.1 × 1.1 × 1.1 mm.

Quantification of the femoral morphology

The femoral anteversion angle (AVA) and neck-shaft angle (NSA) were quantified based on six anatomical landmarks, as described by Sangeux et al,31 extracted from the MRI images with the 3D Slicer software.32 This method has demonstrated reliability, with reported SDs of 2.7° for AVA and 4.5° for NSA.31 The AVA and NSA were used to personalize the musculoskeletal model as described below. To evaluate the potential impact of measurement uncertainty on JCF estimates, we conducted sensitivity analyses using data from one participant. Detailed methods and results of these analyses are provided in the Supplementary Material.

The geometry of each femur was segmented and the surface mesh was exported as an STL file. These geometries were used to quantify the size of the femoral head and femoral condyles. Femoral head size was quantified by the volume of a sphere fitted to the femoral head. Medial and lateral femoral condyle sizes were quantified by the volume of ellipsoids fitted to each condyle using a quadratic surface fitting method, as described by Sholukha et al.33 The length of the femur was quantified as the distance between the hip and knee joint centres. The STAPLE toolbox was used to obtain the required geometrical information for these calculations.34

Calculation of joint angles and JCFs

Personalized models were created for each participant and data collection session, similar to those described in our recent work.35 Subject-specific models were created by personalizing femoral shape, segment dimensions, and muscle properties of the Lernergopal model.36 Specifically, the TorsionTool was used to modify the femoral shape to match each child’s NSA and AVA, as obtained from the MRIs.25,37 These modifications altered the attachment and via points of muscles acting on or passing the femur, thereby influencing the lever arms of muscles across joints. To fit the models to the participants’ anthropometry, the pelvis and femur segments were scaled using the hip and knee joint centres derived from the MRIs. Other segments of the model were scaled using the location of surface markers obtained from the gait analysis. Muscle properties, including tendon slack length and optimal fibre length, were calibrated to operate at the same length as the base model.38 Maximum isometric muscle forces of the models were scaled based on an equation that accounts for the participants’ BW.39 The gait kinematics and ground reaction forces were based on experimental data from each participant, ensuring that the simulations reflected subject-specific movement patterns and loading conditions.

The final models included three rotational degrees of freedom at the hip and two rotational degrees of freedom at the knee and ankle joints. The metatarsophalangeal joints were locked. The experimental 3D gait analysis data was used to calculate joint angles using inverse kinematics. A Matlab script was used to identify and resolve discontinuities in muscle paths and moment arms.40 Afterwards, joint moments, muscle forces, and JCFs were calculated using inverse dynamics, static optimization, and joint reaction analyses, respectively. During static optimization, the cost function minimized the squared muscle activations, a criterion that has been widely used for human gait simulations, and outperformed other optimization techniques.41-43 All musculoskeletal simulations were performed in OpenSim v. 4.4 (Stanford University, USA).23

Statistical analysis

Joint kinematics as well as joint moments and JCF (absolute and normalized to the BW of each participant) waveforms were compared between data collection session 1 (S1) and session 2 (S2) using paired t-tests with statistical parameter mapping.44 Spatiotemporal gait parameters (i.e. cadence, walking speed, stride length, stance phase duration), leg length, femoral head volume, medial condyle volume, lateral condyle volume, femoral length, and mean JCF during the stance phase of gait were compared between data collection sessions using paired t-tests. The significance level was set to p < 0.05.

A stepwise multiple linear regression using a backward elimination procedure for statistically non-significant predictors (p > 0.05) was performed to investigate if changes in JCF and femoral morphology can be predicted using simple measures such as age, BW, and height. Age (at S1 and change between S1 and S2), weight (at S1 and change between S1 and S2), and height (at S1 and change between S1 and S2) were used as predictors, and the calculated changes in JCF and femoral morphology as independent variables in the regression model. All predictors were normalized to their z-scores. Adjusted R² values were used to assess the regression model’s ability to predict changes in JCF and femoral morphology. To further assess the accuracy of the predictions, additional regression models were created using a leave-one-out cross-validation approach. The accuracy of the models was determined by quantifying the differences between the predicted and actual changes in JCF and femoral morphology.

Results

Femoral head volume (26.9 cm3 (SD 6.3) vs 35.9 cm3 (SD 6.7)), medical condyle volume (25.1 cm3 (SD 3.7) vs 40.3 cm3 (SD 8.5)), lateral condyle volume (25.5 cm3 (SD 5.2) vs 43.2 cm3 (SD 9.6)), and femoral length (34.2 cm (SD 1.6) vs 37.8 cm (SD 2.0)) significantly (p < 0.001) increased between data collection sessions (Figure 2).

Growth-related changes in femoral morphology. All parameters significantly increased (p < 0.001) from session 1 (S1) to session 2 (S2).

Our musculoskeletal simulations led to physiologically plausible results in accordance with the best practice recommendations for OpenSim simulations.45 Marker tracking errors and reserve actuators of the musculoskeletal simulations were below 2 cm and 5% of the external moments, respectively. All muscle paths were smooth and without any discontinuities. Muscle activations obtained from the simulations showed a reasonable agreement with experimental electromyography data (Supplementary Figure S1).

There were no significant differences in the walking pattern, i.e. joint kinematic waveforms (Figure 3), between the two data collection sessions. Leg length increased (p < 0.001) and cadence decreased (p = 0.011) significantly between sessions, whereas there was no significant difference in normalized walking speed, normalized stride length, or stance phase duration (Figure 4). During the stance phase of gait, absolute hip abduction and ankle plantarflexion moments significantly increased (Figure 5) between data collection sessions, whereas BW-normalized joint moments did not show any significant differences between sessions (Supplementary Figure S2). Spatiotemporal parameters as well as joint angle and joint moment waveforms were consistent with those previously reported for typically developing children.46

Mean stance phase JCF at the hip joint, medial femoral condyle, and lateral femoral condyle significantly increased between data collection sessions (Table II). BW-normalized JCF showed no significant differences between data collection sessions at the hip joint and lateral femoral condyle, whereas medial femoral condyle JCF significantly decreased between sessions (Table II). The multiple linear regression models showed that changes in hip JCF, medial condyle JCF, and lateral condyle JCF can be predicted based on simple anthropometric measures with R2 values of 0.67, 0.41, and 0.78, respectively. Participants’ age at the initial data collection session and change in age were only significant predictors for changes in hip JCF. Significant predictors for changes in medial and lateral condyle JCF were weight and height information from our participants (Figure 8). Furthermore, the regression models showed that changes in femoral head volume (R2 = 0.59) and medial condyle volume (R2 = 0.52) can be predicted based on changes in body height, whereas changes in lateral condyle volume (R2 = 0.59) can be predicted based on the BW at the initial data collection session.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to quantify growth-related changes in both femoral morphology and joint loads in typically developing children. As expected, our analyses showed that femoral joint contact geometry and absolute JCF significantly increase with age. However, when normalized to BW, JCF remained the same or even decreased (i.e. medial condyle JCF) over time. Predicting changes in JCF based on simple measures – age, weight, and height – worked reasonably well for hip JCF (R2 = 0.67) and lateral condyle JCF (R2 = 0.78), but less well for medial condyle JCF (R2 = 0.41). Changes in femoral morphology could be predicted with moderate accuracy, with R2 values ranging from 0.40 for lateral condyle volume to 0.59 for femoral head volume.

Our findings align with previous studies on longitudinal growth patterns in the femur. For instance, Pritchett14 reported that approximately 70% of femoral growth occurs at the distal growth plate, contributing 1.3 cm per year to femoral length until the final stages of growth. In our study, femoral length increased by an average of 3.6 cm over two years, which is consistent with these growth rates. While historical studies provide valuable benchmarks, our MRI-based measurements offer a more detailed quantification of femoral head and condyle volumes, which are not typically captured in growth charts. These data complement existing growth charts by providing insights into joint contact surface areas, which are critical for understanding joint biomechanics during growth.

Spatiotemporal parameters were analyzed to assess changes in gait patterns during the follow-up period. A previous study showed that normalized walking speed and cadence decrease with age, while normalized stride length and stance phase duration remain relatively constant.47 In our study, we observed a significant decrease in cadence (p = 0.011) but no significant differences in normalized walking speed, normalized stride length, or stance phase duration between the two data collection sessions. These findings are partially consistent with previous work.47 The relatively small observational window of two years likely explains the absence of significant differences in normalized walking speed. Furthermore, the observed increase in leg length (p < 0.001) may account for the consistency in joint kinematics and normalized walking speed, despite an increase in absolute walking velocity.

Mean hip and medial and lateral condyle JCFs were 3.8 BW, 1.2 BW, and 1.9 BW in our participants, respectively. These values are within the range of previous reported hip7,48 and knee49,50 JCF in children, as estimated through musculoskeletal simulations. In contrast, in vivo measurements of JCF in elderly individuals with instrumented hip and knee implants have shown slightly lower values compared with our findings.51 This discrepancy may be explained by differences in walking speed, cadence, and leg length between children and older adults.

One previous longitudinal study investigated JCF using musculoskeletal models informed by medical imaging in eleven growing children with a mean age of 11.5 years.30 Over a 12-month period, the study reported increases in hip and knee JCF of 1.0 BW (SD 0.3) and 0.7 BW (SD 0.3), respectively. In contrast, our study found that BW-normalized JCF remained stable over a two-year period. One important difference is that the earlier study included medical imaging of the lower limbs at only a single timepoint,30 thereby neglecting changes in bone morphology that may influence JCF.6 Additional differences in participant age (11.5 vs 9.8 years) and duration between data collections (12 vs 24 months) may also account for the differing results. Moreover, an increase in walking velocity during the second session of the previous study could have contributed to the observed rise in JCF.52

Interestingly, while average BW-normalized medial condyle forces significantly decreased (Table II), waveform comparisons showed no significant differences (Figure 5). This decrease may be linked to an increased hip adduction moment, potentially caused by anatomical changes like a wider pelvis and longer legs during growth, despite similar gait kinematics. Differences in frontal plane hip moments have been shown to influence knee joint muscle force distribution and consequently knee JCF,6 which could explain this finding.

Hip and knee joint contact surfaces increased with age in our participants. These ontogenetic changes in femoral morphology are consistent with findings from previous imaging-based studies.19-22 The observed enlargement of joint contact surfaces may help compensate for the age-related increase in absolute JCF, potentially contributing to stable cartilage pressure during growth (Supplementary Material). While speculative and requiring further validation, this conclusion is supported by the mechanostat theory and existing literature. Finite element studies, such as those by Castro-Abril et al53 and Sadeghian et al,54 have shown that growth plate geometry adapts to mechanical loading, with changes in physeal geometry and orientation linked to body mass and JCFs. These studies highlight the mechanobiological processes underlying growth plate and cartilage adaptation, which are relevant to our findings. Future research should incorporate multiscale simulations to directly investigate cartilage pressure and validate this hypothesis.

Predicting ontogenetic changes in femoral morphology and joint loading based on simple measures – such as age, weight, and height – could provide valuable insights without relying on costly medical imaging or gait data collection. Regression models using these basic parameters were able to reasonably predict average changes in joint loads and femoral morphology across the study population. However, notable deviations were observed in some individuals, indicating that while such models can capture population-level trends, they are not suitable for subject-specific predictions. This limitation likely stems from the complex interplay between musculoskeletal morphology and movement patterns, both of which significantly influence joint loading29 and bone adaptation.5 Moreover, growth is not solely driven by mechanical factors – it is also shaped by biological and genetic influences. As a result, simple regression models that do not account for these multifactorial processes can offer only a rough approximation of ontogenetic changes and are unlikely to yield accurate individualized predictions.

The growth plate is crucial for musculoskeletal development during childhood and adolescence, as it drives longitudinal bone growth and shapes bone geometry, alignment, and muscle attachment sites. To understand how loads at the growth plate change over time, future research should integrate longitudinal experimental data with advanced methods, such as subject-specific multiscale simulations combining musculoskeletal modelling and finite element analysis.35,55,56 These techniques can offer valuable insights into the mechanical environment of the growth plate and its influence on bone development.

This study has several limitations. First, while we personalized the femoral NSA and AVA in each model, we did not account for other morphological features such as femoral shaft curvature or the cross-sectional area of each muscle. However, NSA and AVA are likely the most influential parameters affecting the moment arms of hip-spanning muscles and consequently JCF. Second, although we estimated JCF, we did not calculate joint contact pressure or growth plate stresses, which would offer deeper insight into the mechanobiology of growing bones.57 Future research incorporating multiscale simulations is therefore warranted – particularly to validate our hypothesis of relatively stable cartilage pressures and loading during growth. Third, while our sample size is relatively small compared with large-scale imaging studies, our dataset uniquely combines longitudinal MRI with 3D gait analysis. This allowed us, for the first time, to quantify both ontogenetic changes in femoral morphology and joint loading in typically developing children. Fourth, our study focused exclusively on healthy children; movement patterns and joint loads can differ significantly in adults or individuals with pathological conditions.58-60 Therefore, our findings should be interpreted with caution and not generalized beyond the specific cohort investigated.

In conclusion, we combined longitudinal MRI and 3D gait analysis to quantify ontogenetic changes in femoral morphology and joint loading in growing children. Our findings revealed age-related increases in both absolute JCF and joint contact surface areas, consistent with the expected growth and development during childhood. Importantly, we characterized the specific trajectory of these changes over time, providing detailed insights into the relationship between age, femoral morphology, and joint loading. These increases in JCF and contact surface area may contribute to relatively stable cartilage pressures during growth, supporting the hypothesis that the musculoskeletal system adapts to maintain joint homeostasis. The resulting data provide valuable reference values for distinguishing normal developmental variation from pathological changes in femoral loading. While simple regression models captured general population-level trends, they proved inadequate for subject-specific predictions, highlighting the need for more sophisticated modelling approaches in paediatric biomechanics. Future studies could investigate the integration of subject-specific multiscale simulations, which combine musculoskeletal modelling with finite element analysis to accurately quantify biomechanical loads across multiple scales.61 Additionally, expanding longitudinal datasets to include larger and more diverse cohorts would strengthen the robustness and generalizability of the findings.
