Effects of Deadman State on the Safety of Locally Eroded Blast Furnace Hearth: A Multiphysics Coupling Modeling Study
Authors: Fei Yuan, Liangyu Chen, Lei Zhao, Lei Wang, Zhuang Li, Yan Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The service state of the blast furnace (BF) hearth directly determines the furnace’s lifespan, and its failure carries significant economic and safety risks. The safe service state of the BF hearth depends on three key parameters: the chemical dissolution rate of the carbonaceous lining, the growth thickness of the solidified iron protective layer (SIPL), and the stress state of the lining material under thermal-mechanical loads. Based on the actual hearth operating conditions, this study established a three-dimensional thermal-fluid-concentration-structure coupling simulation model for the locally eroded hearth. This model considers conjugate heat transfer, carbonaceous concentration transport, molten iron solidification, and thermal stress in refractory materials. It systematically analyzes the influence of the deadman state parameters (floating height, bottom diameter, angle of repose, and bottom shape) on the safety of the hearth lining. The results show that increasing the deadman floating height and controlling its bottom diameter and angle of repose at a low level can effectively promote the formation of the SIPL, homogenize thermal stress, and inhibit carbon dissolution, thereby significantly delaying the lining erosion process. Compared with the flat-bottom deadman, the spherical-bottom deadman offers advantages in thickening the SIPL, reducing thermal stress, and lowering carbon dissolution loss, thereby effectively extending the hearth lifespan. The research findings provide important theoretical basis and operational guidance for the long-life operation of BF hearths.

Introduction

The blast furnace (BF) hearth, as the core component that houses high-temperature molten iron, operates under severe conditions for extended periods, thereby challenging its structural integrity. − Studies have shown that progressive erosion of the hearth lining is the primary cause of hearth failure, resulting in significant economic losses and potential safety hazards. , Hearth erosion is characterized by irreversibility, and its mechanism of occurrence and development is attributed to the synergistic effect of chemical erosion of the carbonaceous lining and thermal-mechanical stress coupling. The former is mainly manifested as dissolution of carbon-based materials caused by molten iron scouring, , while the latter stems from thermal stress concentration in the lining material induced by temperature gradients. , Industrial practice has confirmed that the SIPL formed by solidification of molten iron on the hearth lining can effectively delay erosion by acting as a physical barrier and thermal buffer. , In summary, the safe service state of the BF hearth depends on three key parameters: the chemical dissolution rate of the carbonaceous lining, the growth thickness of the SIPL, and the stress state of the lining material under thermomechanical loads.

The deadman, composed of unreacted coke in the BF hearth, is the primary donor of carbon components in molten iron. Its porous structure exerts a significant damping effect on the flow field of molten iron. , Early numerical studies on the deadman mainly focused on its influence on the flow behavior of molten iron. − In these studies, Darcy’s law was adopted to characterize the flow resistance imposed by the deadman on molten iron. Flow models of molten iron in the hearth and bottom were established to investigate how the deadman state affects the flow characteristics of molten iron inside the hearth. At present, numerical investigations on the deadman have evolved from single-flow-field analysis to multiphysics coupling, enabling a systematic analysis of its key impacts on hearth safety. Relevant studies have been continuously refined around three major aspects: carbon dissolution, SIPL formation, and thermal stress in the hearth.

Numerical investigations on carbon dissolution have established the core approach of flow-concentration field coupling and are gradually advancing toward multiphysics coupling. Shao et al. coupled the molten iron flow field with the carbon concentration field, revealing the influence of deadman state on the spatial distribution of carbon content within the hearth. On this basis, Chang et al. further developed a thermal-flow-concentration-coupled model to address the limitations of two-field coupling alone and clarified the regulatory mechanism of the deadman state on the carbon dissolution rate at the hearth wall under conditions of no SIPL formation.

Simulation studies on the growth of the SIPL have focused on accurately characterizing molten iron solidification with reasonable treatment of the latent heat of solidification as a key improvement. In an early study, Komiyama et al. approximately simulated the skull distribution under different deadman states by increasing the effective viscosity of molten iron based on thermal-flow coupling, but the prediction accuracy was limited due to the neglect of the latent heat of solidification. Zhao et al. applied the enthalpy-porosity method and treated the molten iron between the liquidus and solidus as a mushy zone, thereby simulating the solidification of molten iron with latent heat under laminar flow and improving the prediction accuracy of SIPL distribution. Subsequently, Liu et al. introduced turbulence effects on this basis and analyzed the influence of deadman state on the safety of the damaged hearth using the SIPL thickness as the evaluation index.

Regarding the simulation of hearth thermal stress, a relatively mature research approach has emerged: analysis is carried out by sequentially coupling the structural field to a thermal-flow coupled model that accounts for the solidification phase change. Both Wang et al. and Wang et al. adopted this method to establish a thermal-flow-solid coupled model incorporating molten iron flow and solidification processes and investigated the stress distribution characteristics of the hearth lining under two typical deadman states: floating and sitting.

These studies have revealed the mechanism by which the deadman ensures safe operation of the hearth from various perspectives, providing an important reference for BF operation and hearth safety control. However, due to the complexity of multiphysics coupling, existing studies mostly focus on single-aspect analyses, and a unified analytical model that comprehensively considers various factors influencing hearth thermal safety remains lacking. , More importantly, current models generally simplify the SIPL formation criterion to a fixed liquidus temperature, resulting in an overly concentrated SIPL distribution near the hearth corner in simulations, which fails to reproduce the relatively uniform attachment morphology in actual hearths. The essential reason is that the physical nature of the solidification onset temperature, which varies dynamically with the local carbon concentration in molten iron, was neglected.

To address the above limitations, this study establishes a three-dimensional multiphysics simulation model coupling thermal flow, concentration, and structure for a locally eroded BF hearth under actual-damaged conditions. The model incorporates conjugate heat transfer, carbon species transport, molten iron solidification phase change, and thermal stress in the refractory materials. It performs a fully coupled analysis of multiple physical fields and, for the first time, integrates carbon species transport, solidification phase change, and thermal stress calculation into a unified simulation framework, thereby addressing the limitation of existing models that cannot comprehensively evaluate the coupled effects of multiple factors on hearth safety. Meanwhile, the model dynamically links the solidification initiation temperature to the local carbon concentration, thereby overcoming the oversimplification of the SIPL formation criterion in the traditional models. It thus predicts the formation and evolution of the SIPL more realistically on the basis of fundamental physical principles. On the basis of the temperature field and velocity distribution that agree better with actual working conditions, the model further calculates the thermal stress and chemical erosion rate in the hearth, achieving a comprehensive evaluation of key physical quantities related to hearth safety. Using this model, this paper systematically investigates the effects of deadman parameters such as the floating height, bottom diameter, angle of repose, and bottom morphology on the carbon dissolution rate of the hearth lining, the SIPL thickness in eroded zones, and the thermal stress distribution. The findings can provide a theoretical basis for the long-life control of BF hearths and offer significant engineering guidance for optimizing the practical BF operation.

Numerical Method

Based on the measured data and maintenance reports from the later stage of the BF campaign, this study constructed a three-dimensional multiphysics field coupling calculation model for the locally eroded hearth, simulating the turbulent flow of molten iron, carbon component diffusion and transport, solidification phase change, and thermal stress distribution of the hearth lining. This model adopts a thermal-fluid-concentration-structure coupling algorithm: the k-ε model is used to calculate the turbulence of molten iron; the diffusion of carbonaceous components is handled based on the component transport theory; the enthalpy-porosity method is applied to track the solidification front of molten iron; and the thermoelastic constitutive equation is coupled to calculate the thermomechanical stress of the lining structure. Numerical Methods and Mesh Model

Numerical Methods

The numerical model is constructed based on Fluent 2025R1. With the aid of user-defined functions (UDF), material properties, mixture solidification characteristics, and diffusion coefficients are modified, carbon mass and turbulence parameter source terms are supplemented in the deadman, and the solidification process is regulated. The finite volume method (FVM) combined with the Coupled algorithm is adopted for numerical solution. Second-order accuracy is uniformly applied in the global discretization: the second-order scheme is used for the pressure term, while the second-order upwind scheme is employed for the momentum, turbulent kinetic energy, dissipation rate, energy, and species transport equations. The convergence criteria are set as follows: the continuity residual is below 10–5, and the residuals of other physical quantities are controlled within 10–8 to ensure reliable results.

Mesh Model

The computational domain is discretized using polyhedral meshes, totaling 3,900,337 elements: 2,350,650 in the fluid domain and 1,549,687 in the solid domain, as shown in Figure . The base mesh size in the main fluid region is set to 0.05 m, while local refinement is applied to the critical taphole region, where the mesh size is refined to 0.005 m. Two sets of boundary layer meshes are established in the model: five layers are constructed on the taphole wall, with a smooth transition achieved through flexible grading. At the interface between the inner hearth wall and molten iron, the height of the first boundary layer node is fixed at 0.005 m to ensure an accurate resolution of the near-wall flow. All boundary layers adopt a growth ratio of 0.272 for the graded refinement. The global mesh growth rate was set to 1.05, and the overall mesh orthogonality was greater than 0.5. The mesh independence study is presented in Figure . A comparison of five meshing schemes shows that all schemes achieve stable convergence with relative deviations of key numerical results below 2%. This verifies that the mesh selected in this study meets the mesh independence requirement for the numerical simulation.

Simulation Results of the Benchmark Working Condition

In the overhaul report of the BF hearth, the deadman exhibits typical central accumulation characteristics and a significant floating state in the hearth. Its bottom can be approximately simplified as a flat surface, with a floating height of about 600 mm. The geometric parameters of this structure are as follows: the diameter of the deadman accounts for approximately 70% of the hearth inner diameter; the measured natural angle of repose is 80°; the average porosity is 0.3; and the average particle size is 30 mm.

Under the production parameter conditions at the end of the BF campaign, the simulation results of the computational model show that the hearth tap temperature is 1773 K, and the carbon mass fraction in the tapped iron is 4.12 wt %, which are consistent with the tapping parameters of this BF. Figure presents the simulation results from the computational model in the erosion cross section: after molten iron is injected from the top of the hearth, it tends to exit along the shortest path. In the cross section, the high-velocity zone is concentrated in the middle and lower parts of the coke-free zone adjacent to the deadman (Figure a). The molten iron in the hearth exhibits a nonuniform temperature field distribution due to the external forced cooling system (Figure b). A significant temperature gradient is formed in the erosion area at the junction of the hearth sidewall and bottom due to the maximum cooling intensity, resulting in a decrease in the volume fraction of molten iron in this area and the formation of an SIPL through phase change (Figure c), which is consistent with industrial experience. The carbon content of molten iron in the hearth shows a spatial gradient distribution characteristic (Figure d), specifically: the carbon concentration in the area close to the deadman increases significantly, and the concentration in the hearth bottom area is significantly higher than that in the sidewall area. This nonuniform distribution is caused by two factors: first, the deadman, as a carbonaceous reaction source, continuously supplies carbon to the molten iron; second, spatial differences in the flow residence time of molten iron in the hearth result in obvious path dependence in the carbon element transport process. Effect of Deadman State on Thermo-Flow-Concentration Fields

The porous structure of the deadman exerts a significant resistance to the molten iron flow in the hearth. Variations in its spatial morphology and position alter the distribution of flow resistance within the hearth, thereby directly determining the overall flow pattern of the molten iron. The streamline distributions of the flow field in the hearth corresponding to the eight deadman states are listed in Figure .

Streamline states of the the hearth corresponding to the eight cases.

Case A and Case E correspond to the seated conditions of flat-bottomed deadman and spherical-bottomed deadman, respectively. In these cases, the molten iron passage at the hearth bottom is blocked, and the flow is concentrated in the free region near the hearth sidewall, forming an obvious circulating flow. The high-velocity zone, with a velocity greater than 2.0 × 10–3 m/s, expands toward the region far from the taphole. The streamlines near the taphole are disordered, showing distinct turbulent characteristics. Case B and Case F correspond to a deadman floating height of 600 mm. Upon floating, a continuous flow channel forms between the hearth bottom and the deadman. The molten iron flow changes from “single-channel flow along the hearth sidewall” under seated conditions to “dual-channel flow through both the hearth bottom and sidewall.” The circulation intensity in the sidewall region is weakened, and the flow velocities in both the sidewall region and the erosion zone decrease. However, limited by the floating height, the flow space at the hearth bottom remains narrow, and the velocity there is maintained at a relatively high level, ranging from 8.0 × 10–4 to 1.2 × 10–3 m/s. When the floating height is increased to 1200 mm (Case C and Case G), the uniformity of velocity distribution in the hearth is significantly improved. The velocity in the free zone at the hearth bottom generally drops below 8.0 × 10–4 m/s, and the high-velocity region near the sidewall taphole is obviously reduced. This demonstrates that increasing the deadman’s floating height can effectively expand the flow space within the hearth, reduce overall flow resistance, stabilize the flow field distribution, and lower the overall velocity.

Compared with the flat-bottomed deadman in cases A–C, the spherical-bottomed deadman in cases E–G exhibits superior flow field uniformity at all floating heights. This is mainly attributed to the spherical base, which creates a larger flow space between the deadman and the hearth bottom, improves flow conditions in the bottom region, optimizes flow distribution, alleviates local velocity nonuniformity, and enhances overall flow field uniformity.

Compared with case B, cases D and H increase the flow area near the hearth sidewall by reducing the deadman diameter and the stacking angle, respectively, effectively weakening the sidewall circulation. However, their performances differ noticeably: case D achieves a synchronous reduction in overall velocity. In case H, although the streamlines shift upward and more molten iron flows out from the upper region due to the enlarged upper space, the velocity near the deadman root shows no obvious decrease. This is because reducing the angle of repose only improves the flow conditions in the upper hearth without significantly enlarging the flow space at the junction between the deadman root and the hearth bottom. The flow area in this region remains narrow, leading to local convergence of molten iron and resulting in a small reduction in velocity.

As the primary carbon supplier of molten iron in the hearth, the state of the deadman directly dictates the distribution characteristics of carbon content in molten iron within the hearth. As illustrated in Figure , distinct differences are observed in the carbon concentration distribution of molten iron within the central cross section of the hearth erosion zone under the eight different deadman conditions. A comparison of Cases A–C reveals that with the increase in the deadman’s floating height, the uniformity of carbon concentration distribution in the erosion cross section is significantly enhanced, while the carbon content of molten iron in the erosion zone is evidently reduced. This variation trend is highly consistent with the evolution of the molten iron flow field. When the deadman is seated on the hearth bottom, the molten iron, which should have flowed along the bottom, is forced to converge in the hearth corner region. The molten iron in this region resides longer in the hearth, resulting in a higher carbon concentration. Meanwhile, the elevated flow velocity enhances the turbulent mixing of carbon components, causing the high-carbon zone to continuously extend toward the erosion zone. As the deadman floats upward, the molten iron flow channels at the hearth bottom are gradually opened, significantly alleviating the aforementioned flow convergence. Additionally, separating the deadman from the erosion zone further reduces the carbon supply to this region, ultimately decreasing the molten iron’s carbon content in the erosion zone.

Carbon concentration distribution in the erosion cross section for the eight cases.

Compared with Cases A–C, Cases E–G exhibit significantly lower carbon concentrations in the erosion zone at the same floating heights. This is primarily attributed to the larger flow clearance between the spherical-bottomed deadman and the hearth bottom, as well as the greater radial distance between the deadman and the erosion zone.

In case D, the reduction in deadman diameter notably decreases the molten iron velocity and turbulence intensity near the hearth sidewall. This weakens the convective mass transfer capacity of carbon, smooths the carbon concentration distribution, and ultimately keeps the high-concentration molten iron region away from the erosion zone. In contrast, the simulation results for case H indicate that the overall upward shift of the molten iron streamlines induces an axial variation in the carbon concentration field. However, the convergence of molten iron at the deadman root maintains a relatively high carbon concentration in this local area, resulting in a limited reduction in the carbon content near the erosion zone.

As the internal heat source in the hearth, molten iron transfers heat through its flow, and the deadman affects the temperature field by altering the flow distribution of the molten iron. As shown in Figure , the state of the deadman exerts a significant effect on the temperature distribution within the hearth. When the deadman rests on the hearth bottom (case A, case E), the flow passages at the bottom are blocked, resulting in low molten iron velocity around the deadman and weakened heat exchange between molten iron and the hearth bottom wall. Under the cooling effect of the hearth bottom, the temperature at the bottom of the hearth decreases noticeably. Meanwhile, the sidewall region exhibits a relatively high temperature due to the strong circulating flow of molten iron, resulting in an obvious temperature distribution with a high upper and a low lower temperature in the erosion zone. When the floating height of the deadman increases to 600 mm (case B and case F), the circulating effect near the sidewall weakens, accompanied by a decline in sidewall temperature. The enlarged flow space and increased velocity at the hearth bottom cause a rapid temperature recovery at the bottom, a distinct increase in the lower part of the erosion zone, and a slight decrease in the upper part. In cases C and G, with further expansion of the hearth bottom space and more uniform molten iron velocity distribution, temperatures at both the hearth sidewall and bottom show a downward trend, and the overall temperature in the erosion zone decreases further.

Temperature distribution in the erosion cross section for the eight cases.

Notably, when the spherical-bottomed deadman (case E) rests on the hearth bottom, the hearth bottom temperature is significantly higher than that of the flat-bottomed deadman (case A). This occurs because the partial flow space for molten iron remains beneath the spherical structure, and the narrow passages enhance local flow velocity and wall heat transfer. While preventing an excessive temperature drop at the hearth bottom, this also elevates the temperature in the erosion zone. Under other working conditions, the erosion zone corresponding to the spherical-bottomed deadman maintains a lower temperature.

In case D, reducing the deadman diameter lowers the molten iron velocity near the hearth sidewall, thereby weakening forced convection and causing a noticeable temperature drop in this region. Unlike the overall uniform temperature reduction in the sidewall region in case D, decreasing the angle of repose of the deadman (case H) mainly reduces temperatures in the upper sidewall and hearth bottom significantly. This is attributed to the enlarged flow space and reduced velocity in the upper region, which weaken local convective heat transfer. In the middle and lower parts of the sidewall, however, the temperature shows no obvious decrease due to the convergence of molten iron.

Effects of Deadman State on Hearth Safety Factors

In a BF hearth, the SIPL is formed near the wall when molten iron solidifies under forced external cooling during flow. Its formation and development are comprehensively affected by multiple factors, mainly including the flow intensity and temperature distribution of molten iron inside the hearth as well as the solidification characteristics determined by the local carbon concentration of molten iron. Figure shows the calculated SIPL thickness in the erosion zone for different states of the deadman.

Thickness of the SIPL in the erosion zone for the eight cases.

A comparison between cases A–C and E–H reveals that the SIPL thickness for both bottom configurations shows an obvious increasing trend with increasing deadman floating height. Combined with the previous analysis of flow, temperature, and carbon concentration fields, it can be concluded that after the deadman floats upward, the flow distribution of molten iron becomes more uniform, the heat exchange intensity between the hearth wall and molten iron is significantly weakened, and the overall temperature field of the hearth decreases uniformly. In addition, the lower carbonization level of molten iron in the hearth bottom region corresponds to an increase in its solidification temperature, making it easier to form an SIPL near the wall. Multiple factors jointly provide favorable conditions for the formation and thickening of SIPL. Notably, when the spherical-bottomed deadman is seated (case E), the thickness of the SIPL in the erosion zone is only 8.65 mm, which is merely 53.6% of that under the flat-bottomed deadman condition (case A). This indicates that the narrow flow channel between the spherical-bottomed deadman and the hearth bottom further deteriorates the thermal environment of the hearth erosion zone, whereas the temperature reduction at the hearth bottom caused by the seated flat-bottomed deadman promotes the formation of the SIPL in the erosion zone to a certain extent. When the deadman is in a floating state, the thicknesses of the SIPL corresponding to the spherical-bottomed deadman at floating heights of 600 mm (case F) and 1200 mm (case G) are 58.17 mm and 88.5 mm, respectively, which are much higher than 21.05 mm and 57.75 mm for the flat-bottomed deadman at the same floating heights. This phenomenon is consistent with the combined effects of slower molten iron velocity, lower temperature, and weaker carbonization in the erosion zone when spherical-bottomed deadman floats are used.

Compared with case B, reducing the bottom diameter of the deadman (case D) and the angle of repose (case H) both significantly increase the thickness of the SIPL in the erosion zone to 54.73 mm and 72.08 mm, with increases of up to 160% and 242%, respectively. Compared with reducing the deadman diameter, although reducing the angle of repose has a relatively limited effect on the carbon concentration in the erosion zone, it significantly expands the flow space in the upper part of the hearth, allowing more molten iron streamlines to pass through the upper region. Under the condition of a low deadman floating height, this flow characteristic greatly weakens the flow intensity of molten iron in the narrow channel at the hearth bottom, thereby effectively reducing the hearth bottom temperature and providing more favorable conditions for the formation of SIPL.

Hearth thermal stress is an inevitable result of the combined action of internal and external temperature differences and external constraints in the hearth. By altering the molten iron flow field and heat-transfer intensity inside the hearth, the deadman influences the temperature gradient of the refractory lining, thereby regulating the thermal stress distribution.

Figure a presents the thermal stress variations at different monitoring locations in the hearth for flat-bottomed and spherical-bottomed deadman structures at various floating heights. As the deadman’s floating height increases (0–1200 mm), the thermal stress at each monitoring point exhibits distinct trends under the two bottom configurations. Under the flat-bottomed deadman conditions (Cases A–C), the thermal stress at point S4 in the upper erosion zone and points S5 and S6 in the hearth sidewall region shows an overall decreasing trend with increasing floating height. The stress values drop from 59.1, 54.1, and 57.6 MPa to 51.4, 52.9, and 57.4 MPa, with reductions of 7.7, 1.2, and 0.2 MPa, respectively. In contrast, the thermal stress at point S1 (hearth bottom center), point S2 (hearth bottom edge), and point S3 (lower erosion zone) first increases and then decreases: rising from 21.5, 28.2, and 34.4 MPa in the seated state to 29.6, 30.9, and 36.1 MPa at a floating height of 600 mm, and then falling back to 29.2, 30.4, and 33.7 MPa at 1200 mm. Under the spherical-bottomed deadman conditions (Cases E–G), except for point S1 at the hearth bottom center, the thermal stress at all other monitoring points decreases with increasing floating height. Within the floating height range of 0–1200 mm, the total stress reductions at points S2–S6 are 1.7, 5.9, 10.9, 1.3, and 0.3 MPa, respectively.

The above variation trends are generally consistent with the evolution of the molten iron flow and temperature fields at the hearth bottom and sidewalls, induced by the increasing floating height of the two deadman configurations. Compared with monitoring points on the hearth sidewall and bottom, the stress changes at erosion zone points S3–S4 are more pronounced. This is because local erosion thins the refractory lining, amplifies the thermal gradient, and the abrupt change in lining geometry exacerbates stress concentration. Under both deadman configurations, the extremely high thermal stress at point S4 in the upper erosion zone in the seated state confirms this mechanism. As the deadman’s floating height increases, the SIPL in the erosion zone gradually thickens, effectively blocking heat transfer between the molten iron and the refractory lining, thereby significantly reducing thermal stress in this region. A comparison of thermal stress distributions between the two bottom configurations at different floating heights reveals the following: When the spherical-bottomed deadman is seated, the thermal stress at monitoring points S1–S3 is significantly higher than that under the flat-bottomed condition, while that at S4–S6 is slightly lower. This is related to the stronger suppression of heat transfer between molten iron and the hearth bottom under the seated flat-bottomed deadman, resulting in notably lower thermal stress near the hearth bottom. At a floating height of 600 mm, the thermal stress at all monitoring points S1–S6 for the spherical-bottomed deadman is lower than that for the flat-bottomed one; this trend becomes even more prominent at a floating height of 1200 mm. In summary, the spherical-bottomed deadman performs better in improving thermal stress under floating conditions. Although the flat-bottomed deadman can reduce local thermal stress in the hearth bottom region when seated, it results in poorer uniformity of the overall thermal stress distribution in the hearth.

This is consistent with the upward shift of molten iron streamlines and the convergence of molten iron at the deadman root caused by the decreased angle of repose. In contrast, reducing the deadman diameter results in a more pronounced reduction in thermal stress in the hearth sidewall region.

Carbon dissolution loss of the hearth lining refers to the process in which carbon in carbon bricks dissolves into molten iron at high temperatures and is carried away by flow scours. It is mainly affected by the molten iron flow velocity and the local carbon saturation of molten iron, which are jointly determined by temperature and carbon concentration.

Figure a shows the variation of carbon dissolution rate at different monitoring points in the hearth for flat-bottomed and spherical-bottomed deadman structures at various floating heights. With an increase in the floating height of the deadman, the carbon dissolution rate in each hearth region tends to be more uniform overall. The influence of the deadman floating height on the carbon dissolution rate at different monitoring points can be summarized as follows: The carbon dissolution rate in the hearth sidewall region (E3-E4) exhibits an overall decreasing trend with increasing floating height. For the flat-bottomed deadman (Cases A–C), the carbon dissolution rate reductions at E3-E4 are 46.6% and 39.6%, respectively, while for the spherical-bottomed deadman, the corresponding reductions reach 67.4% and 57.9%. This trend is consistent with the slowdown of molten iron flow and the decrease in temperature in the sidewall region. The carbon dissolution rate in the hearth bottom region (E1-E2) shows a nonlinear trend, first increasing and then decreasing with increasing floating height. In the range of 0–600 mm, the carbon dissolution rates at E1-E2 increase by 4.81 × 10–5 and 0.9 × 10–5 kg·m–2·s–1 under the spherical-bottomed condition, and by 4.55 × 10–5 and 3.9 × 10–5 kg·m–2·s–1 under the flat-bottomed condition. In the range of 600–1200 mm, the carbon dissolution rates at E1–E2 decrease by 1.8 × 10–5 and 0.65 × 10–5 kg·m–2·s–1 for the spherical-bottomed deadman, and by 1.21 × 10–5 and 1.01 × 10–5 kg·m–2·s–1 for the flat-bottomed deadman. The underlying mechanism is as follows: When the deadman is seated, it significantly suppresses the flow of molten iron at the hearth bottom, lowers the local temperature, and raises the carbon concentration. Meanwhile, the temperature drop promotes the formation of an SIPL, further slowing the carbon dissolution rate in the bottom region. This effect is most pronounced in the fully seated flat-bottomed deadman condition (Case A), where the bottom carbon dissolution rate is nearly zero. As the floating height of the deadman gradually increases, the molten iron flow at the hearth bottom transitions from high-speed flow in narrow passages to uniform low-speed flow, accompanied by a decrease in regional temperature. However, as the deadman moves away from the bottom, the carbon concentration of molten iron in the hearth bottom region decreases significantly. Although the overall carbon dissolution rate still shows a slowing trend, its rate of reduction is much lower than that in the sidewall region. At the same floating height, the carbon dissolution rates at monitoring points E3-E4 under the spherical-bottomed deadman are significantly lower than those under the flat-bottomed condition, while the difference in carbon dissolution rates at E1-E2 is relatively small. Based on the carbon dissolution rates at monitoring points in the erosion cross section for both deadman configurations across three floating heights, the spherical-bottomed deadman can effectively reduce the carbon dissolution rate of the hearth sidewall and uniformize the overall carbon dissolution rate distribution, thereby avoiding the occurrence of locally severe erosion.

Conclusions

In this paper, a three-dimensional multiphysical field model of the hearth, considering flow, mass transfer, phase change, and thermal stress, was established to analyze the mechanism of the deadman state on the hearth lining condition and evaluate its comprehensive impact on hearth erosion. The main conclusions are as follows:1.Maintaining a relatively high floating height of the deadman can effectively alleviate the degree of molten iron circulation, uniformize the temperature distribution, and reduce the degree of carbonization of molten iron in the hearth. It is conducive to the formation of an SIPL in the erosion zone, making the thermal stress distribution of the hearth lining more uniform, while effectively inhibiting the carbon dissolution loss of the lining.2.The spherical-bottomed deadman has a significantly better effect on uniformizing the molten iron flow velocity than does the flat-bottomed deadman. When floating, it can form a thicker SIPL, effectively reduce thermal stress, make the carbon dissolution rate distribution more uniform, and avoid local erosion. However, when the flat-bottomed deadman is seated, although it can thicken the SIPL, it will aggravate the sidewall heat load and carbon dissolution, threatening the structural safety of the hearth.3.Reducing the bottom diameter and angle of repose of the deadman can expand the molten iron flow space near the hearth sidewall, optimize the hearth flow velocity distribution, weaken the thermal concentration in the sidewall region, keep the high-carbon concentration zone away from the erosion zone, promote the formation of an SIPL in the erosion zone, reduce the thermomechanical load on the weak parts of the hearth wall, and slow down the carbon dissolution rate.4.Compared with reducing the bottom diameter of the deadman, reducing the angle of repose tends to cause molten iron accumulation at the deadman root, resulting in relatively weak effects on the stress improvement and carbon dissolution inhibition in this region.
