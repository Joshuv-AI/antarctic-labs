Adverse childhood experiences and well-being in middle-aged men in South Korea: mediating role of andropause symptoms
Authors: Jeongok Park, Youngkyung Kim, Hyunju Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMiddle-aged men in South Korea are in a critical period marked by significant physical and psychosocial transitions. While adverse childhood experiences negatively affect lifelong health, the specific mechanisms affecting well-being during midlife remain underexplored. This study aimed to examine the mediating role of andropause symptoms in the relationship between adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) and well-being.MethodsA cross-sectional survey involving 444 community-dwelling Korean men aged 40–64 years was conducted from June to August 2024. Data were collected via an online questionnaire to assess ACEs, andropause symptoms, and well-being. The SAS 9.4 and PROCESS macro were used for data analysis.ResultsThe participants had a mean age of 50.8 years. High levels of well-being were significantly associated with being married, higher educational attainment, and higher income. Participants reporting high ACEs had significantly lower levels of well-being than those reporting no ACEs. In the mediating analysis, the indirect effect of andropause symptoms on the relationships between ACEs and well-being was statistically significant (β = -0.08, 95% CI = -0.109 to -0.053). This finding suggests that andropause symptoms partially mediate the relationship between ACEs and well-being in middle-aged men.ConclusionsAndropause symptoms appeared to partially mediated the relationship between ACEs and well-being in middle-aged Korean men. Although both ACEs and andropause symptoms are associated with midlife well-being, they have rarely been addressed together. Community-based programs combining trauma-informed counseling and andropause management are needed to improve well-being in middle-aged men.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12955-026-02569-x.Keywords: Well-being, Middle-aged adults, Adverse childhood experiences, Andropause, Mediation analysis

Background

With advancements in medical technology, the life expectancy of South Korean men has steadily increased, reaching 83.5 years by 2024 [1]. Middle age is a pivotal period in the life cycle, characterized not only by physical transitions but also by significant shifts in psychological and social status. Biologically, a decline in testosterone levels can lead to various physical and psychological challenges, often referred to as andropause symptoms, such as sexual dysfunction, reduced muscle mass, cognitive decline, depression, and anger [2]. Depressive symptoms among South Korean men rose from 8.1% in 2019 to 8.5% in 2021, with the highest overall rate of depressive symptoms among OECD countries, at 13.2% [3]. Furthermore, behaviors that are detrimental to health among this demographic have continued on an upward trend; the smoking rate rose from 30.0% in 2022 to 32.4% in 2023, and the drinking rate from 67.6% to 68.0% over the same period, accompanied by a rising prevalence of chronic diseases [4, 5]. Socially, while middle age is often considered a period of peak socioeconomic stability, it is also a time when social networks begin to shrink due to retirement or withdrawal from the labor market [6]. The legal retirement age in Korea is 60 years [6]. However, the increasing frequency of early retirement has led to heightened anxiety among middle-aged men, affecting their quality of life [7].

ACEs encompass various forms of maltreatment (e.g., physical, emotional, and sexual abuse; physical and emotional neglect) and household dysfunction (e.g., parental separation or divorce, household member with mental illness, household substance misuse, household member incarceration, and witnessing intimate-partner violence) experienced before the age of 18 [8]. ACEs have been identified as major risk factors for adverse physical and mental health outcomes in adulthood [9]. Epidemiological data underscore the fact that early life adversity is a widespread societal issue rather than an isolated anomaly affecting only a few individuals. Specifically, the prevalence of at least one ACE is alarmingly high, reported to be 61.5% among adults in the United States [10] and 78.9% among adults in South Korea [11]. Approximately 15.6% of adults in the United States [10] and 12.8% of South Korean adults [11] have experienced four or more types of ACEs.

The enduring consequences of these universal experiences extend beyond childhood. Several studies have demonstrated that ACEs fundamentally alter lifelong health trajectories and serve as strong predictors of severe chronic conditions such as obesity [12], hypertension [13], diabetes [14], ischemic heart disease [15], and premature mortality [16], as well as detrimental psychological outcomes including severe depression, alcohol dependency, and suicidal attempt [17, 18]. Specifically, individuals who experienced four or more ACEs are 46% more likely to be obese [9] and are 1.32 times more likely to develop diabetes than those with no ACE exposure [13]. Furthermore, those with four or more ACEs face a 4.4-fold higher risk of severe depression [9] and are 12.2-fold more likely to have attempted suicide during their lifetime [19] than those without such experiences.

Mechanistically, the lifelong effect of ACEs is driven by fundamental alterations in stress reactivity and disease susceptibility [20]. Prolonged exposure to early trauma induces epigenetic modifications and DNA methylation, which persistently ingrain stress into the biological system [21].

According to the life course perspective, the biological embedding of these early stressors leads to persistent physiological deregulation, often manifesting as a pro-inflammatory state that increases vulnerability to both chronic physical conditions and psychological distress during the middle years [22]. This stress acts as a foundational risk factor, where men with higher ACE scores often exhibit maladaptive coping mechanisms that further compromise their health as they age [10].

In the context of male aging, these stressors are reflected in the biological domain through the exacerbation of andropause symptoms. Furthermore, severe andropause symptoms are negatively associated with social well-being [23]. These biological manifestations, in turn, are linked to individual’s subjective well-being.

When examining these phenomena within the South Korean context, significant cultural and socioeconomic distinctions emerge that differ from the Western findings, in which men’s distress has been characterized by outward expression [24]. In South Korea, societal perceptions often dictate that men suppress or conceal their problems to maintain their perceived masculine authority [25]. Furthermore, while economic hardship is a universal stressor, middle-aged South Korean men appear particularly vulnerable to perceived status loss and anxiety of early retirement, which are more strongly correlated with depressive symptoms than objective economic indicators alone [26]. While often discussed in terms of physical symptoms such as reduced muscle mass and sexual dysfunction, the multidimensional cluster of andropause symptoms often has a more profound and debilitating impact on the overall quality of life [27–29].

Existing research on middle-aged men in South Korea has been largely fragmented, focusing separately on well-being or andropause symptoms without a life course perspective [30]. Therefore, this study aimed to examine the mediating role of andropause symptoms in the relationship between ACEs and well-being. The specific objectives of this study are as follows:

Methods

Study design and setting

This study was a cross-sectional survey designed to investigate the levels of well-being among 444 community-dwelling men aged 40–64 years in South Korea. Data were collected from June to August 2024 using an online survey to ensure the participants’ convenience and privacy. The survey was administered anonymously through Google Forms (Google LLC, Mountain View, CA, USA), no personally identifying information was collected. Each participant was assigned an anonymous identifier. To prevent duplicate entries, submissions from the same IP address were systematically blocked. After data collection, the dataset was additionally screened for duplicate response patterns across demographic variables, and no duplicate entries were identified. The required sample size was determined using G*Power 3.1.9.4 based on the multiple linear regression equations underlying the mediation model. With a significance level of α = 0.05, statistical power of 0.90, a medium effect size (f² = 0.15), and a maximum of 52 predictors, a minimum of 288 participants was required. In the absence of clear prior estimates, a medium effect size was conservatively assumed based on Cohen’s conventional criteria [31].

Participants

The study participants consisted of middle-aged men who met the specific criteria. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) adult men aged 40–64 years living in South Korea, and (2) individuals who fully understood the purpose of the study and voluntarily agreed to participate. Individuals with deficiencies in memory, cognition, or judgment were excluded. At the start of the online survey, participants were asked, using a single self-report item, whether they had ever been clinically diagnosed with a condition affecting memory, cognition, or judgment. Those who answered “yes” were automatically prevented from proceeding to the survey.

Measures

Well-being

Well-being was measured using the Korean version of the PERMA-Profiler (K-PERMA) originally developed by Butler and Kern [32] and validated in Korean by Shim and Kim [33]. This tool is based on Seligman’s well-being theory and consists of 23 items: 15 items assessing the five PERMA – positive emotion, engagement, relationships, meaning, and accomplishment – pillars and eight additional items covering negative emotions (three items), physical health (three items), overall happiness (one item), and loneliness (one item). Each item is rated on an 11-point Likert scale ranging from 0 to 10, with higher average scores indicating a more functional state of well-being. At the time of the Korean scale’s development, the reliability coefficient (Cronbach’s α) was 0.94 and 0.81 in this study.

Adverse childhood experiences

ACEs were measured using the Korean version of the ACE International Questionnaire (ACE-IQ), developed by the WHO [8] and modified by Ryu et al. [11]. This tool consists of 35 items across 13 categories including physical and emotional abuse, witnessing domestic violence, family dysfunction, and peer or community violence. Each experience was scored dichotomously (Yes = 1, No = 0) and summed to produce a total ACE count. Higher scores reflect a greater number of ACE categories endorsed. The reliability coefficient (Cronbach’s α) at the time of development was 0.82 and 0.84 in the current study.

Andropause symptoms

Andropause symptoms were assessed using the Korean version of the Androgen Deficiency in Aging Males (ADAM) questionnaire [34], translated by Kim et al. [35]. The tool includes 10 binary (Yes/No) items covering common symptoms such as decreased libido, lack of energy, and reduced strength. Responses were scored as Yes = 1, No = 0, with a total score ranging from 0 to 10. The original tool’s reliability coefficient (Cronbach’s α) was 0.88 and 0.79 at this study.

Sociodemographic and health related characteristics

Participants’ general characteristics, including age, education attainment, marital status, occupation, employment status, and income level, were collected. Health-related variables included height, weight, and presence of chronic diseases.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SAS, version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Descriptive analyses were performed to summarize the characteristics of the participants; categorical variables were reported via frequency distributions, and continuous measures were expressed as means and standard deviations. Independent t-tests, one-way analyses of variance and Pearson correlation analyses were performed to examine differences and relationships in well-being according to demographic characteristics. ACEs, andropause symptoms, and well-being were treated as continuous variables in the mediation analysis (Fig. 1). Before conducting analysis, normality of these variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test together with inspection of skewness and kurtosis. All three variables met these criteria, with ACEs (skewness = 0.36, kurtosis = − 0.66), andropause symptoms (skewness = 0.19, kurtosis = − 1.18), and well-being (skewness = − 1.05, kurtosis = 1.53) all within the acceptable range [36]. The mediation analysis using PROCESS macro Model 4 was performed (Supplementary Material 1). The significance of the indirect effect was assessed using bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals with 5,000 resamples [37]. Homoscedasticity of the regression models was assessed using White’s test, and the results were non-significant (all p > .05).

Discussions

This study investigated the relationship between ACEs, andropause symptoms, and well-being. The major findings were as follows: (1) Andropause symptoms partially mediated the relationship between ACEs and well-being, a pattern consistently observed across all PERMA sub-domains.

(2) The findings indicated an inverse relationship between childhood adversity and middle-aged well-being, suggesting that a history of higher ACE scores poses a significant challenge in maintaining well-being.

(3) Socioeconomic status, particularly advanced education and income, appears to be important determinants in fostering elevated levels of well-being.

A key finding of this study was the partial mediating role of andropause symptoms in the relationship between ACEs and well-being. Although ACEs are known to have an enduring impact on adult health [9], the results showed that the direct effect of ACEs on well-being was attenuated but remained statistically significant when andropause symptoms were included in the model. In addition, ACEs demonstrated a significant and indirect effect on the exacerbation of andropause symptoms, indicating partial mediation.

These results align with the biopsychosocial model and life-course perspective [20, 38], which propose that early-life adversity may become biologically embedded over time. However, caution is required when interpreting these findings. Because hormonal and other biological markers were not measured in this study, our findings cannot directly confirm such physiological mechanisms and should instead be interpreted as associations observed in a cross-sectional design. Existing research has predominantly emphasized the direct psychological sequelae of ACEs, establishing a robust link between early adversity and adult mental health outcomes, such as depression and anxiety [39–41]. Our study extends this view by highlighting the physiological pathways specific to middle-aged men. The results suggest that for this population, the impact of early trauma is not merely psychological, but is significantly channeled through accelerated biological aging.

This interpretation aligns with the biological embedding theory [42], which posits that lifespan adversity is associated with accelerated cellular aging, such as shorter telomere length [43]. Telomeres are repetitive DNA sequences at the ends of chromosomes that maintain the stability and integrity of genetic information [44], and their progressive shortening serves as a biomarker of biological aging [45]. Our findings indicate that childhood adversity is associated with more pronounced andropause symptoms, which partially account for the association between ACEs and reduced well-being. Approaches that address both psychological adversity and andropause-related symptoms could be considered in community-based programs to improve well-being. However, future longitudinal studies are needed to validate these findings.

Consistent with our findings, participants reporting high ACEs exhibited significantly lower levels of well-being than those of participants with no ACEs. This result aligns with the results of previous studies [40, 46]. In the South Korean cultural context, middle-aged men are expected to maintain authority, emotional control, and social competence [26, 47–50]. Suppression of distress and fear of status decline may further exacerbate the negative influence of early adversity and andropause symptoms on well-being. Recent research has emphasized that South Korean men often face a double burden. Trying to stay strong and silent worsens internal pain and also stops them from asking for help [26]. Furthermore, the transition to andropause is often experienced as a loss of confidence in manhood, where a decline in physical and sexual function is perceived as a failure to meet the societal standards of the strong provider [47]. This fear of status decline, combined with the breadwinner masculinity prevalent in the rapid-growth generation, makes these men particularly vulnerable when facing retirement or a perceived decrease in social performance [48, 49]. Therefore, further research is needed to explore how these cultural dynamics interact with the well-being of middle-aged South Korean men.

The study participants had relatively high socioeconomic status. According to previous studies, education, economic status, and social support are associated with well-being in middle adulthood [51, 52]. This finding aligns with our results, demonstrating that well-being was significantly higher among participants who were married, held a college degree or higher, were regular employees, and had a higher income. Therefore, for middle-aged men, socioeconomic achievement serves as a fundamental buffer against midlife crises, enabling them to maintain higher levels of psychological flourishing despite aging-related challenges.

This study has several important public health implications. First, early identification of men with high ACE exposure may help prevent the exacerbation of andropause symptoms in midlife. Previous research has demonstrated that ACEs are strongly associated with long-term physical and mental health outcomes [9], suggesting that early screening in primary care or community health settings may facilitate timely intervention before biological vulnerability manifests as severe hormonal or psychological symptoms.

Second, integrated intervention programs that address psychological trauma and physiological aging simultaneously are required. The life-course perspective and biological embedding theory suggest that early adversity becomes physiologically internalized, accelerating aging-related processes such as hormonal decline and cellular aging [43]. Therefore, rather than solely treating andropause as a hormonal condition, a trauma-informed approach may be more effective in mitigating its impact on well-being.

Furthermore, in the South Korean sociocultural context, where traditional masculinity norms discourage emotional expression and help-seeking behaviors, middle-aged men may be particularly reluctant to disclose psychological distress [53]. Such norms may intensify internalized stress and delay intervention, thereby amplifying the combined effects of early adversity and age-related physiological changes on well-being.

Finally, socioeconomic resources have consistently been identified as protective factors for quality of life during midlife [51, 52]. Therefore, supporting middle-aged men through workplace-based health promotion programs and culturally sensitive mental health services may contribute to healthy aging trajectories and improve their overall well-being.

To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to provide comprehensive research on the well-being of middle-aged South Korean men. Furthermore, this study verified the mediating role of andropausal symptoms on the relationship between early adversity and well-being. These findings provide evidence for the biopsychosocial impact of early trauma, demonstrating that psychological scars in childhood are biologically manifested through accelerated physiological aging in middle-aged men.

However, this study had several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design prevented the establishment of causal relationships. Future studies should employ a longitudinal design to validate the pathways identified in this study. Second, as the study focused exclusively on middle-aged South Korean men, there are limitations regarding cultural diversity. Cultural norms regarding masculinity, emotional expression, and perception of aging may differ significantly across societies. Therefore, caution should be exercised when generalizing these findings to middle-aged men from other cultural backgrounds. Cross-cultural studies are recommended to validate the model in diverse populations. Third, this study relied on self-reported measures, introducing the potential for recall bias, particularly regarding childhood experiences, and social desirability bias in reporting well-being. Fourth, the online survey format may have under-represented middle-aged men with lower digital literacy, including older or socioeconomically disadvantaged men.

Conclusions

ACEs are associated with well-being both directly and indirectly through andropause symptoms. ACEs may have lasting biopsychosocial implications for well-being, highlighting the potential value of trauma-informed care and routine screening. Andropause symptoms may require proactive, community-based management to improve well-being in middle-aged men. These findings suggest the potential value of intervention strategies that target andropause symptoms in men with a history of ACEs. Community-based programs could provide hormonal assessment, lifestyle counseling, and trauma-informed counseling for middle-aged men. Healthcare providers may be well-positioned to lead these programs by addressing both early-life adversity and current andropause symptoms simultaneously.
