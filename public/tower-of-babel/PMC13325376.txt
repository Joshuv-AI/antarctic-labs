Impact of Using Blended Polymers on the Structural and Functional Attributes of Eptifibatide-Loaded Microspheres
Authors: Anand U. Kyatanwar, Bala R. Prabhakar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The saturation of generics has shifted the focus on developing technology-based delivery systems, not only for small molecules but also for proteins and peptides. Proteins and peptides require novel delivery systems as conventional delivery systems are of not much use. Biodegradable polymer-based microspheres have received considerable interest for the controlled delivery of many therapeutic proteins and peptides. Despite being known for many years, developing biodegradable polymer-based microspheres has remained a challenge. Among many challenges, selecting an appropriate grade of polymer is crucial. A right grade of polymer is required to achieve the desired physicochemical properties and mainly the release of the drug from the formulated microspheres. However, because of the limited number of companies supplying GMP-grade polymers, polymer selection has become a daunting task. The cost of these polymers is very high, and when a suitable grade of polymer is unavailable, customization is required, which further increases the cost. To address this critical issue, this research focused on comparing Eptifibatide-loaded microspheres formulated using blending polymers vs those formulated using original/on shelf polymers. Polymers R202H and RG502H were physically blended in different ratios to obtain blended polymers with different comonomer ratios. Specifically, polymers with closer IV values were used in this study. The microspheres formulated using blended polymers were compared with those formulated using original/on-shelf polymers, and both sets demonstrated identical performance.

Introduction

Many proteins and peptides have been identified using new statistical chemistry tools and techniques. However, to utilize the therapeutic efficacy of these proteins and peptides, a specific delivery system needs to be developed. These delivery systems prevent their degradation on the shelf during storage and after administration in vivo, until they reach the site of action. Biodegradable polymer-based microspheres have been extensively explored for drug delivery. Various types of polymers have been used to formulate these microspheres, which are delivered via different routes. The most widely used biodegradable polymer is poly­(D,L-lactide-co-glycolide) (PLGA), which is used in various biomedical and pharmaceutical applications. Its exceptional properties, such as biodegradability, low toxicity, and good biocompatibility, make it an ideal choice for controlled drug delivery systems, particularly for proteins and peptides. Despite being known for years, many challenges remain in the development of PLGA microspheres. The main challenge is the right choice of polymer. The critical material attributes for selecting a suitable polymer grade include inherent viscosity (IV), molecular weight, comonomer ratio, and capped or uncapped end groups. All these material attributes closely affect the physicochemical properties of the formulated microspheres. IV and molecular weight affect the degradation of the polymer, as a low-IV polymer degrades faster than a high-IV polymer. The same is true for molecular weight, as IV and molecular weight are related to each other by the Mark–Houwink equation. Acid-terminated polymers are free and show rapid degradation when compared to ester-terminated polymers. The ratio of the polymer used during manufacturing also governs the polymer degradation rate. Thus, for desired drug delivery, selecting a suitable polymer grade with these properties is crucial and challenging. In addition, to further enhance the usage of these polymers, more research has been conducted on the use of mPEG-5000 of various weight fractions to form PLA–PEG block polymers. Even custom synthesis of specific polymers is done to obtain the desired release profile. , With few GMP-grade suppliers in the market, these polymers are offered at a higher cost, which is probably one of the reasons for the delayed entry of biodegradable polymer-based delivery systems. Sometimes, the customized polymers are sold at 3x times the original/on-shelf polymers. Therefore, to reduce the entry time of these delivery systems in the market and to allow many smaller pharmaceutical companies to work on this delivery system, there is a need for a solution that can be evaluated as an option when the desired grade of polymer is not available. Eptifibatide is a synthetic cyclic heptapeptide with cysteine amide and one mercaptopropionyl (desamino cysteinyl) residue. By blocking GP IIb/IIIa receptors on platelets, the drug interferes with the attachment of fibrinogen, von Willebrand factor, and other adhesive molecules, thereby suppressing platelet aggregation. A swift antiplatelet effect is achieved through an initial intravenous bolus, typically around 180 μg/kg, followed by a continuous infusion of approximately 2.0 μg/kg/min. It also exhibits rapid reversibility regaining the platelet activity in 4 h. It is one of the preferred antiplatelet drugs for the treatment of acute coronary syndrome (ACS). Patients with ACS after surgery are often treated with anticoagulants for a longer duration. Oral treatment with these anticoagulants is not as effective as parenteral treatment, which requires hospitalization. There is a trend toward the use of dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) due to the inadequacy of single antiplatelet treatment. DAPT involves the use of two antiplatelets to achieve better therapeutic efficacy and is associated with the risk of bleeding, thus requiring continuous monitoring. Therefore, designing a sustained-release formulation of Eptifibatide that can deliver the drug continuously for more than a month is essential. Such a system would minimize the requirement for prolonged hospitalization and help reduce bleeding complications linked with dual antiplatelet therapy. Eptifibatide-loaded microspheres were formulated using blended and original/on-shelf polymers. The manufacturing method used to formulate these microspheres involved use of a single-emulsion solvent evaporation method.

Material and Methods

Materials

Poly­(d,l-lactide-co-glycolide) (PLGA) with a 75:25 copolymer ratio (Resomer RG752H, IV 0.14–0.22 dL/g, Batch D150500554), poly­(lactic acid) (PLA) homopolymer (Resomer R202H, IV 0.16–0.24 dL/g, Batch R130500516), and PLGA with a 50:50 ratio (Resomer RG502H, IV 0.16–0.24 dL/g, Batch D200100502) were sourced from Evonik Polymers, Rheinfelden, Germany. All polymers contained terminal carboxylic acid groups. Poly­(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) with a molecular weight of 13–23 kDa and 87–89% hydrolysis (Batch K44535053) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich, Milwaukee, USA, and used without further modification. The selected drug, EPF (Mw 831 kDa, Batch RD000X), was kindly provided as a gift sample by USV Pharmaceuticals, Mumbai, India. Additional excipients included D-Mannitol (Batch 4 V01583, AppliChem GmbH, Darmstadt, Germany). Organic solvents such as dichloromethane (Batch K53462149), dimethylformamide (Batch STBJ9963), and methanol (Batch I1155008), along with other analytical-grade reagents, were supplied by Merck Chemicals, Mumbai, India. Filtration materials comprised cellulose nitrate membranes (5 μm, Lot 037561) and PVDF filters (0.45 μm, Lot 954245), purchased from Advanced Microdevices Pvt. Ltd., India.

Animals

Healthy Sprague–Dawley rats (both male and female) weighing in the size range of 150–200 g were used in this study. The rats were appropriately housed in a cage at 20 to 24 °C and RH 50–60%. Animals were fed a conventional laboratory diet and potable water. Study proposal no:130/2/2021 and study request No. PC/PKD/21/1304 were approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee of Bharat Serums and Vaccines Ltd., Thane, India and conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Committee for the Purpose of Control and Supervision of Experiments on Animals (CPCSEA), New Delhi, India.

Blending of Polymers

Before manufacturing the batches, two grades of polymers, RG502H and R202H, were blended to obtain polymers with different comonomer ratios. The polymers were physically blended on a weight basis. The blended polymers were evaluated using nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), and their inherent viscosities (IV) and molecular weights (Mw) were determined. The blended polymers were subsequently employed to prepare multiple batches of Eptifibatide-loaded microspheres. A single-emulsion solvent evaporation technique was adopted for the formulation process. For polymer blending, the following materials were used:

Resomer R202H: a homopolymer of dl-lactic acid (100%) with an inherent viscosity of 0.21 dL/g.

Resomer RG502H: a copolymer containing 50% dl-lactide and 50% glycolide, also with an inherent viscosity of 0.21 dL/g. Specifically, polymers with closer IV values were used in this study.

When Resomer R202H was combined with Resomer RG502H in a ratio of 9:1, i.e., 90% and 10%, respectively, it gave a polymer with 95% lactic acid and 5% glycolic acid. Similarly, these polymers were combined in different ratios to obtain the different polymer combinations. The rationale behind this selection was to compare the performance of on-shelf polymers to that of blended polymers having the same comonomer ratios. Since the intended duration of the Eptifibatide controlled-release depot injection was one month, emphasis was placed on evaluating blended polymers with comonomer ratios similar to those of Resomer RG502H and RG752H.

Manufacturing of Microspheres

Each batch was prepared employing a single-emulsion solvent evaporation technique. EPF was initially dissolved in 1 mL of dimethylformamide (DMFA), while the PLGA polymer was solubilized in 1–2 mL of dichloromethane (DCM). The drug solution was then combined with the polymer solution to generate the internal organic phase. This phase was subsequently introduced into an aqueous medium containing 0.25% poly­(vinyl alcohol) (PVA), which served as the surfactant. The mixing process was carried out using an inline homogenizer (Megatron MT 3100S, Kinematica AG, Luzern, Switzerland) operated at 500 ± 50 rpm. The resulting microsphere dispersion underwent solvent evaporation for 2–3 h in a conventional water bath (Trishul Industries, Vasai, Mumbai) maintained at temperatures up to 35 °C. Following evaporation, the suspension was filtered through cellulose nitrate membranes (5 μm pore size). The retained microspheres were subsequently rinsed with purified water to remove residual surfactant and solvent traces, to remove the loosely bound drug and then suspended in a D-Mannitol solution, followed by lyophilization done using a Lyodryer Freeze lyophilizer, (Lyophilization system India Pvt Ltd. Hyderabad, India). The lyophilized microspheres were then collected in glass vials and sealed with rubber bungs. Approximately seven batches were manufactured using a blended polymer, and one batch was manufactured using an original/on-shelf polymer. The batch compositions and details are mentioned in Table . Please note that the volume of DCM has been deliberately adjusted in few trials as the blended polymers contained a higher proportion of lactic acid units as lactic acid exhibits lower solubility in DCM compared to glycolic acid.

Evaluation of Blended Polymers

Nuclear Magnetic Resonance

For preparation of the test solution, about 50 mg of the polymer was solubilized in 0.5 mL of deuterated chloroform. The spectra of a solution containing tetramethyl silane (TMS) were analyzed as a reference. The lactic acid to glycolic acid ratio was calculated from the strength of the signal areas. 1 LacticAcid=2A(2A+3B)X100 2 GlycolicAcid=100−LacticAcid 3 LacticacidGlycolicacid=2A/3B

Here, A is the strength of the signal area for 1.6 ppm (double doublet) (integration value originating from lactic acid). B is the strength of the signal area for 4.8 ppm (multiple) (integration value originating from glycolic acid). A Brukner, Avance New, model IAS-N003, 500 MHz NMR spectrometer (Billerica, MA) was used to obtain the NMR spectra of the polymer.

Inherent Viscosity

About 1 g of the sample was accurately weighed and dissolved in 100 mL of chloroform, which was used as the sample solution. The viscosity of this solution and the viscosity of chloroform were measured by measuring the time required to pass the solution through the viscometer tube against the time needed for the pure solvent. An Anton Paar viscometer (Lovis 2001 Benchtop Model, Graz, Austria) was used to calculate the inherent viscosity of the polymer. The inherent viscosity was measured using the following formula. 4 Viscos⁡ity=In(TsTc)W where Ts is the flow time of the sample solution in seconds. TC is the flow time of chloroform in (s). W is the weight of the sample in (g). The temperature of the atmosphere and the sample solution was 25 °C.

Molecular Weight

The Mw of the polymer was determined by gel permeation chromatography (GPC) with a Clarus 680 GC mass spectrometer (PerkinElmer Inc.), Shelton, Connecticut, US. Two Styragel HR four columns were connected in series. The mobile phase was tetrahydrofuran (THF) with a 3 mL/min flow rate at 40 °C. Approximately 10 mg of the polymer sample was dissolved in 10 mL of THF and filtered through 0.22 μm Whatman filter paper. This solution was then injected as a sample solution with 100 μL as the injection volume. The weight average molecular masses were calculated based on polystyrene standards (1,250,000; 400,000; 200,000; 43,000; 17,600; 6930; 2610; 982; and 472 Da). All measurements were conducted in triplicate, and the mean values and standard deviations were reported.

Evaluation of Microspheres

Eptifibatide-loaded microspheres formulated using blended polymers were compared to a batch formulated using an original/on-shelf polymer. The physicochemical properties of the formulated Eptifibatide-loaded microspheres were evaluated.

Percentage Drug Content (Total and Free Drug Content)

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) was employed to determine both the total and free drug contents in the Eptifibatide microspheres.

Preparation of Sample Solution (Total Drug Content)

Approximately 80 mg of microspheres were dispersed in 10 mL of dichloromethane (DCM). This mixture was transferred into a separating funnel, where 10 mL of acetate buffer (pH 4.1) was added to facilitate drug extraction. The funnel was mounted on a mechanical shaker operating at 150 rpm for 15 min. The extraction step was repeated three times, each with an additional 10 mL of buffer, resulting in a total of ∼30 mL used. After standing, the organic phase was discarded, and the aqueous fraction was collected. The aqueous extract was passed through a 0.45 μm PVDF membrane filter, and the drug concentration was quantified spectrophotometrically at 219 nm. Analysis was performed on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC system equipped with a photodiode array (PDA) detector. Separation was achieved using a Thermo Hypersil BDS C18 column (250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm particle size). The mobile phase consisted of acetonitrile and 50 mM sodium dihydrogen orthophosphate (25:75 v/v), with the pH adjusted to 2.2 using orthophosphoric acid. The system was operated in isocratic mode at a flow rate of 1 mL/min with an injection volume of 20 μL and a total run time of 6 min.

The percentage entrapment efficiency was calculated using the standard equation: 5 %drugentrapment=(PracticaldrugcontentTheoreticaldrugcontent)×100 .

Preparation of Test Solution (Free Drug Content)

For the determination of the free drug, the same extraction procedure described earlier was followed, except that the microspheres were directly treated with 30 mL of acetate buffer (pH 4.1) without prior dissolution in DCM. All subsequent steps remained identical to those used for the total drug content analysis. Each vial of the prepared formulation contained approximately 12.96 mg (rounded to 13 mg) of Eptifibatide. Particle Size Analysis

Particle size characterization of the Eptifibatide-loaded microspheres was carried out using laser diffraction on a Malvern Mastersizer 3000 laboratory instrument (catalog no. NC2226413, Malvern Instruments, Worcestershire, UK). For analysis, around 30 mg of microspheres were precisely weighed and dispersed in 200 mL of purified water in a beaker. The Malvern Mastersizer probe was dipped in a beaker and adjusted to achieve the desired level of obscuration. D50 and D90 were used to express the results, and all measurements were performed in triplicate.

Accelerated in Vitro Drug Release (Accelerated In Vitro Dissolution)

Drug release studies were conducted using a mechanical shaking water bath (WBS-300 Series, Cole Parmer India) operated in the horizontal mode. The bath temperature was maintained at 52 ± 0.5 °C, with agitation set to 50 ± 5 rpm. For each test, a glass vial containing 80 mg of Eptifibatide microspheres (equivalent to one unit dose) was reconstituted with 1 mL of the diluent to obtain a suspension. This suspension was transferred into a Schott glass bottle containing 200 mL of buffer, which was then sealed and placed in the shaking water bath. The dissolution medium consisted of lactic acid buffer adjusted to pH 2.2. At predetermined intervals, 1 mL aliquots were withdrawn and immediately replaced with fresh buffer to maintain sink conditions. Samples were analyzed for drug release using the HPLC method described previously. The accelerated release profile was monitored at time points of 0, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 8, 12, 24, 36, 48, and 72 h. ,

Surface Morphology

Morphological characterization of the microspheres was carried out using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) under higher and lower resolutions. The surface morphology of the coated microspheres was examined using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, model JSM-6390, JEOL, Tokyo, Japan). For sample preparation, approximately 1–2 mg of microspheres were carefully placed onto a specimen stub. The samples were then sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold under vacuum for a few minutes to achieve the necessary conductive coating. The prepared stubs were subsequently analyzed under the SEM to capture detailed images of the particle structure and surface characteristics.

In Vivo Animal Study

To evaluate the in vivo performance of the developed Eptifibatide microspheres, batches formulated using blended polymers were compared to batches formulated using original/on-shelf polymers. The developed microspheres were studied in healthy Sprague–Dawley rats (both male and female) weighing in the size range of 150–200 g (n = 6 per formulation) The Study proposal no:130/2/2021 was approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee of Bharat Serums & Vaccines Ltd., Thane, and conducted according to the guidelines of the Committee for the Purpose of Control and Supervision of Experiments on Animals (CPCSEA), New Delhi, India. The rats were sheltered properly at an ambient temperature of approximately 20–24 °C and RH 50–60% with free access to food and water. The animals were acclimatized for at least 7 days before the experiment. The minimum number of animals were used to extract meaningful data. Batch nos. 04 (batch formulated using the blended polymer with a comonomer ratio of 75:25, LA:GA) and 08 (batch formulated using the original/on shelf polymer with a comonomer ratio of 75:25, LA:GA) were injected into Sprague–Dawley rats as per study request No. PC/PKD/21/1311 after a formal presentation and discussion, and the CPCSEA ethics committee approved the project.

Animal Dose Calculation for Eptifibatide

The reported human dosage regimen consists of an intravenous bolus of 180 μg/kg, followed by a continuous infusion of 2 μg/kg/min for up to 96 h.

This corresponds to an approximately 0.18 mg/kg.

To extrapolate the equivalent dose for rats, the following formula was applied: 9 AED(mg/kg)=Humandose(mg/kg)×Kmratio where the Km ratio is defined as 10 Kmratio=(Bodyweight(kg)Bodysurfacearea(m2))

For a rat weighing 150 g (0.15 kg) with a body surface area of 0.025 m2, the Km ratio was calculated accordingly to determine the adjusted animal dose. 11 RatKmratio=(0.150.025)=6 12 AED(mgkg)=0.18(mgkg)×6=1.08(mgrat)

Each vial containing approximately 80 mg of microspheres was reconstituted with 3 mL of the diluent to get a 1.1 mg/mL suspension. 1 mL each was injected subcutaneously into six rats using 22-gauge needles via a lateral vein in the tail. The animals were bled according to the time points described below. Approximately 300 μL of blood was collected from the retroorbital vein in small capillary tubes. Time points selected were 0.0 h, 1 h, 4 h, 8 h, 12 h, 24 h, 7 days, 21 days, 28 days, 35 days, and 40 days. On the 41st day, all animals were euthanized with 70 mg/kg ketamine hydrochloride.

Plasma drug content was determined by LCMS at the BSV Animal Testing Lab. Briefly, the LCMS-MS system (Waters, Milford, MA, USA) was equipped with an API 3200 triple-quadrupole mass spectrometer detector set at 220 nm. An ASB C18 column (Venusil ABS C 18, 150 mm × 4.6. mm, 5 μm) with a guard C18 column (4 × 3.0 mm) and acetonitrile: 5 mM ammonium acetate: acetic acid (30:70:01 v/v/v as the mobile phase) was used. The gas heater temperature was set to 500 °C. The flow rate was set at 0.5 ± 1.0 mL/min with an isocratic mobile phase.

Sample Preparation and Administration

Approximately 300 μL of whole blood was collected into tubes containing ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) as an anticoagulant. The samples were vortexed for 15 min at 3000 rpm, after which plasma was separated and stored at – 20 °C until further analysis. For protein precipitation, 100 μL of plasma was mixed with 500 μL of methanol. The mixture was vortexed for 5 min and centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was transferred into clean glass tubes and evaporated to dryness at 40 °C under a stream of nitrogen using a Turbovap evaporator. The dried residue was reconstituted in 200 μL of thehmobile phase, vortexed for 1 min, and a 20 μL aliquot was injected for LC-MS/MS analysis. A blank plasma control was prepared by spiking 90 μL of plasma with 10 μL of methanol and processed using the same protocol described above.

Stability Study of Developed Eptifibatide Microspheres

Stability studies for developed microspheres were conducted using ICH stability conditions. The developed microspheres were evaluated for drug content and other CQA as per ICH stability condition, i.e., real-time stability study at 25 ± 5 °C/60% RH, intermediate stability study at 30 ± 5 °C/65%RH, and accelerated stability at 40 ± 5 °C/75% RH. Batches formulated using blended polymers (04) and those formulated with original/on-shelf polymers (08) were charged on stability for up to six months. The primary pack consisted of 5 mL of clear type I borosilicate glass vials, sealed with bromo-butyl rubber bungs, and covered with an aluminum flip-off seal. Each vial was placed in a paper carton box and subjected to stability studies.

Particle Size Analysis

All formulations exhibited a particle size distribution within the range of 10–25 μm (d50). Moreover, only minimal variation in particle size was noted among the different batches prepared using the blended polymer (Batch No. 04) vs batches formulated using the on-shelf polymer (Batch No. 08). The formulated batches had particle sizes that could be easily administered subcutaneously and intramuscularly via a 21–23-gauge needle.

Accelerated In Vitro Drug Release (Accelerated In Vitro Dissolution)

Microspheres formulated using a higher amount of lactide produced a slower release profile (batch no. 02) than those formulated using an equal ratio of lactide and glycolide (Batch no. 07).

Surface Morphology

No meaningful differences were observed among the batches formulated by using different polymers. The microspheres are round and smooth and have uniform pores.

Discussion

Based on the obtained data, it clearly indicates that the blended polymer had the same comonomer ratio as the original/on-shelf polymer.

Inherent Viscosity

Like the comonomer ratio, the inherent viscosity of a polymer is crucial to achieving the desired degradation profile. Since polymers with closer IV values were used, the IV values obtained for the blended polymers did not differ significantly. The IV and Mw values of the blended polymer with a comonomer ratio of 75:25 were similar to those of the original/on-shelf polymer with the same comonomer ratio. The polymers with similar or closely matching inherent viscosities were intentionally selected for blending, as both inherent viscosity and molecular weight can significantly influence drug release behavior. While different comonomer ratios would typically be expected to yield different IV values, in this case, the molecular weights of the blended polymers did not vary substantially. It is important to note that IV is generally reported as a range, and achieving an exact target value is difficult.

Molecular Weight

Similar to IV, the Mw determines the rate of polymer degradation. IV and Mw are related using the Mark Hawing equation. These parameters are crucial for selecting a suitable polymer grade. The molecular weight of the blended polymer was close to that of the original on-shelf polymer. Thus, the blended polymers closely matched the inherent viscosity and molecular weight of the shelf polymers. Therefore, combining polymers of different viscosities and molecular weights can be considered a good alternative if a polymer of a specific molecular weight and inherent viscosity is required.

Evaluation of Microspheres

Percentage of Drug Content (Total and Free Drug Content)

The entrapment efficiency refers to the amount of drug entrapped inside the microspheres. Not all of the drugs used in batch manufacturing are entrapped, as some are loosely bound on the microsphere surface, and some are lost during manufacturing. The entrapment of the drug will depend on the right amount of the polymer and the amount of the drug to be entrapped. However, the role of IV in drug entrapment has been reported in the literature. A possible reason for a similar drug content can be attributed to the polymers with the same inherent viscosity. Increased polymer viscosity results in a viscous polymer solution, providing less attrition during homogenization, followed by immediate precipitation of the dissolved polymer, thus improving drug entrapment. Using polymers with higher inherent viscosity is challenging because a highly viscous polymer solution is obtained, which is very difficult to handle during manufacturing, causing large processing losses. Hence, blending this polymer with a lower Mw polymer will yield good drug loading and help reduce processing losses. Another aspect of the comonomer ratio is the solvent used to dissolve the polymers. Polymers with a higher proportion of lactide dissolve quickly and have good solubility in solvent, and DCM is usually used as a solvent for polymers. Thus, good solubility translates into a polymer taking more time to precipitate. Polymers with a higher proportion of glycolide groups have slightly lower solubility and rapidly precipitate out when they encounter external aqueous fluid during manufacturing, thus giving different drug entrapment. Thus, blended polymers with the desired properties can be used without impacting the drug’s content. Therefore, this clearly indicates a win–win situation for drug entrapment.

Particle Size Analysis

The particle size of the microspheres depends on the viscosity of the polymer solutions. Polymer solutions of high-molecular-weight polymers are more viscous, thus providing lower attrition during homogenization. Because polymers of similar IV were used, a polymer solution of the same viscosity was obtained. Therefore, a solution-dependent change in particle size was not observed. However, as the polymer ratio changed, the particle size was affected. Microsphere batches with a higher lactide content had larger particle sizes than those with a lower lactide content. This is attributed to the higher solubility of the polymer in the organic solvents. As lactic acid is more soluble in DCM than glycolide, it took some time to precipitate from the solution, and the homogenization duration was insufficient to produce a smaller particle size. The homogenization speed also played a crucial role in controlling the particle sizes of the microspheres. Homogenization at higher rpm produces more attrition, thus producing microspheres of smaller particle size. However, these results contradict those of previous studies on peptide drugs encapsulated in PLGA blends.

Accelerated In Vitro Drug Release (Accelerated In Vitro Dissolution)

The primary intent of formulating these microspheres was to control the drug’s release for one month. Thus, evaluating the release of the drug from the microspheres over months is not routinely feasible and was time-consuming; hence, an accelerated in vitro release method was used to assess the release of the formulated microspheres. The amount and type of the polymer used profoundly affect the release of the entrapped drug. Drug release from PLGA microspheres exhibits a biphasic pattern. The initial burst release is due to the loosely bound drug on the surface of the microspheres or in the pores on the surface of the microspheres. The second phase of release occurs when the polymer starts degrading, causing the release of the entrapped drug by diffusion (refer to Figure ). The polymer comonomer ratio determines the polymer degradation rate. Polymers with a higher amount of lactide degrade slower than polymers with more glycolide. The same observation was seen in the results. Microspheres formulated using a higher amount of lactide produced a slower release profile (Batch No. 02) than those formulated using an equal ratio of lactide and glycolide (Batch No. 07). The release of the drug from the microspheres formulated using the on-shelf Resomer R202H polymer was the slowest (Batch No. 01). This again was due the 100% lactide content, a higher proportion of lactide units reduces water penetration in the polymer matrix, as lactide being more hydrophobic than glycolide has a slower degradation rate. The overall release profile observed across all trials correlated well with the comonomer ratios present in the polymer. However, more emphasis should be placed on comparing the release profiles of microspheres formulated using blended and on-shelf polymers having the same lactide to glycolide ratio (Batch No. 04, used blended polymer and Batch No. 08, used original/on-shelf polymer).

Even though both batches gave similar release profiles, when closely observed, batches formulated using the original/on-shelf polymer had lower burst release, and a slightly longer lag period, where degradation of the polymer began. Batches formulated using the blended polymer gave a faster release profile. This can be assumed primarily due to slight difference in the lactide and glycolide ratios; also, there is difference in drug polymer interactions. Eptifibatide might have interacted differently to the lactide (hydrophobic) and glycolide (hydrophilic) portions of the polymer blend, causing a faster initial and gradual, extended release. When observed, both gave similar overall release profiles but differed in how the drug was released. The slight difference can also be attributed to the acidic microenvironment created by the polymer during degradation. A clear correlation was observed between Batch 04 and Batch 08, both formulated with poly­(dl-lactic acid) and polyglycolic acid in a 75:25 ratio. The experimental results closely matched the expected outcomes based on the polymer’s monomer composition. This indicates that physical blending of the polymer produced performance comparable to that of the commercially available polymer. It was further noted that inherent viscosity (IV) and molecular weight significantly influence drug release behavior. Even when polymers share the same IV, variations in comonomer ratios can lead to distinct release profiles. The release profile of batches formulated using the original on-shelf polymer were more predictable and consistent due to its standardized composition. Polymers with different Mw and IV values can effectively control burst release and shorten the lag phase observed for polymer wetting. This unwanted burst release and the lag phase seen in the release profile can be reduced by the use of an appropriate additive in the formulation.

These observations suggest that polymers with variations in inherent viscosity, molecular weight, and end groups can be explored as alternative approaches to achieving or tailoring the desired release profile. Such modifications provide flexibility in controlling drug-release kinetics according to specific therapeutic needs.

Surface Morphology

The manufacturing method employed, composition, and processing parameters profoundly affect the appearance and surface morphology of microspheres. Because all batches were formulated by the single-emulsion solvent evaporation method and all the processing parameters, such as the ratio of the internal organic phase to the external aqueous phase and the rate of solvent evaporation used, were the same, the morphology of the microspheres formulated solely depended on the polymer used. A similar surface morphology was observed for batches formulated using the blended polymer batch (batch no. 04) (refer to Figure b) and the original/on-shelf polymer batch (batch no. 08) (Figure c). A slightly more porous surface was observed in the batch with a comonomer ratio of 50:50 (Figure a), suggesting faster degradation and rapid ingress of the aqueous phase during formulation, thus forming pores.

In Vivo Animal Study

Pharmacokinetic Studies in Animals

The batch formulated using the original/on-shelf polymer required more time to achieve the maximum plasma concentration, as was presumed from the in vitro release profile. The drug’s binding to the polymer and the polymer degradation pattern controlled how the drug was released in vivo. The blended polymer behaved as an individual entity and was less bound to the drug. The glycolide component of the polymer was easily degraded, followed by the degradation of the lactide component; hence, rapid release and attainment of Cmax were observed. However, the original/on-shelf polymer behaved as a single entity with good bonding with the drug and degraded much more slowly; hence, more lag was observed, and Cmax was slowly achieved. Therefore, blending polymers with different inherent viscosities can be an excellent option for achieving controlled and desirable release profiles. Another approach to have a desirable release profile involves use of an appropriate additive in the formulation.

Stability Studies for Developed Eptifibatide Microspheres

The batches formulated with blended and original/on-shelf polymers were stable up to six months under all stability conditions; however, a slight increase in the particle size was observed at higher temperatures, probably due to the aggregation of the microspheres. The increased particle size of the microspheres did not significantly affect the in vitro release profiles. Thus, using blended polymers against the original on-shelf polymer is a possible approach for achieving the desired physicochemical properties of microspheres.

Conclusion

Thus, based on the trials conducted, it can be concluded that blending PLGA polymers of different comonomer ratios and similar IV and Mw can be a possible solution when the desired grade of polymer is not available. This helps to overcome the critical challenge of finding a suitable grade of polymer that is not readily available or when custom synthesis of the polymer is impossible due to the considerable cost involved. Microspheres prepared using different comonomer ratios of the PLGA polymer exhibited similar drug entrapment, surface morphologies, and particle sizes. The in vitro release profile can be called similar, beginning with burst release followed by the polymer’s degradation and finally the drug’s diffusion; however, batches formulated using blended polymers gave slightly faster release profiles when compared to batches formulated using the original/on-shelf polymer. Pharmacokinetic results demonstrated that both batches were able to release the Eptifibatide for approximately 21–28 days. Thus, blended polymers when used to formulate microspheres can give the desired release profile. These observations suggest that polymers with variations in inherent viscosity, molecular weight, and end groups can be explored as alternative approaches to achieve or tailor the desired release profile.
