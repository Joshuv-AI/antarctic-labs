Rethinking ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament: Instability as the root, stabilization as the remedy
Authors: Atul Goel
Published: 2026

Ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL) has long been framed as a disease of pathological bone formation, an abnormal calcification process compressing the spinal cord and necessitating decompression. This conventional narrative, while clinically serviceable, may be fundamentally incomplete. A growing body of thought, particularly the perspective advanced by Goel, challenges this orthodoxy by proposing that spinal instability, not ossification per se, is the primary driver of the disease.[1] Within this paradigm, OPLL is not the cause of neurological compromise but rather a secondary, protective response to chronic instability. Consequently, stabilization, not decompression, emerges as the central therapeutic strategy.

At first glance, OPLL appears to be a straightforward compressive pathology. Radiological imaging often reveals a dense ossified mass encroaching upon the spinal canal, seemingly explaining the patient’s symptoms. Surgical logic has therefore favored direct decompression – via anterior resection or posterior decompressive procedures such as laminectomy or laminoplasty.[2] Yet these interventions are not without risk, and outcomes are variable, particularly in advanced or multilevel disease.

The Goel concept invites a deeper interrogation of causality. It posits that subtle, often under-recognized spinal instability, especially at the facet joints, initiates a cascade of adaptive changes.[3456] Ligamentous hypertrophy, disc space reduction, osteophyte formation, and ultimately ossification of ligaments like the PLL are interpreted as the body’s attempt to stabilize an unstable segment. In this view, OPLL is not an aberration but an adaptive response – a form of internal “brace” developed over time.

This reframing has profound clinical implications. If instability is the root cause, then decompression alone addresses the symptom but not the disease process. Indeed, aggressive decompression may even exacerbate instability, potentially worsening long-term outcomes. Conversely, stabilization of the affected spinal segments, particularly through facet fixation, addresses the primary pathology.[78910] By eliminating abnormal motion, it may halt or even reverse the cascade of secondary changes.

Clinical observations supporting this approach are compelling. Patients treated with stabilization alone, without direct decompression of the ossified mass, have demonstrated significant neurological improvement.[789101112] The mechanism is thought to involve indirect decompression: stabilization restores alignment, reduces microtrauma, and allows the spinal cord to accommodate more comfortably within the canal. Over time, there may even be regression or remodeling of the ossified ligament.

Critics may argue that such an approach underestimates the mechanical impact of a large ossified mass, particularly in cases of severe canal compromise. However, the Goel concept does not deny the presence of compression; rather, it recontextualizes it. The emphasis shifts from removing what is seen to correcting what is driving the process. It is a move from structural excision to functional restoration.

Adopting this paradigm also encourages a more nuanced understanding of spinal imaging. Rather than focusing solely on the extent of ossification, greater attention is paid to signs of instability – facet alignment, subtle listhesis, and dynamic changes that may not be evident on static scans. It challenges surgeons to look beyond the obvious and consider the underlying biomechanics.

In many ways, this debate reflects a broader evolution in spine surgery: a shift from aggressive anatomical correction toward minimally disruptive, functionally oriented interventions. The success of stabilization-first strategies in other degenerative spinal conditions lends further credibility to this approach.[3]

Ultimately, the Goel concept does not merely prompt an alternative treatment for OPLL; it redefines the disease itself. By positioning instability as the primary pathology and ossification as a secondary response, it opens the door to safer, potentially more effective management strategies centered on stabilization.

As with any paradigm shift, widespread acceptance will require rigorous validation through long-term studies and broader clinical experience. Yet the conceptual clarity and clinical promise of this approach make it difficult to ignore. In rethinking OPLL, we may find that the key is not in removing what the body has built, but in understanding why it built it in the first place – and addressing that cause at its core.
