Behavioural Analysis of Alignment Faking
Nathaniel Mitrani Hadida, Rhea Karty, David Williams-King, Alan Cooney

Abstract
Alignment faking (AF) refers to a model strategically complying with a training objective to avoid behavioural modification while preserving its deployment preferences. Understanding when and why AF arises matters as models grow better at distinguishing training from deployment. Prior work finds AF fragile, prompt-sensitive, and model-dependent, leaving its underlying drivers unclear. We study AF in a controlled, minimal setup that isolates its core components, and observe it across a wider range of models than previously reported, including small-scale models. We identify three separable drivers — values, goal guarding, and sycophancy — and show via targeted prompt ablations and activation steering that each independently modulates AF behaviour. Our results indicate AF is more widespread than previously reported and that its occurrence is predictable from situational cues and measurable model tendencies such as baseline sycophancy and stated values. The decomposition suggests concrete directions for detecting and mitigating AF in future models.



1
Introduction

Alignment faking poses a fundamental challenge to AI safety. A model that learns to strategically comply with its training objective, while preserving misaligned preferences for deployment, can score highly on training objectives, then behave entirely differently once deployed. This makes it potentially undetectable by any safety training pipeline that relies on behavioural evaluation, including RLHF and constitutional AI. The problem compounds with scale: more capable models are better placed to reason about their own training
(
8
)
, and better able to conceal their deliberation
(
4
)
. If alignment faking becomes sufficiently sophisticated, it may become impossible to detect at all.

Despite its importance, we have a poor understanding of why models Alignment Fake.
3
provided the first empirical demonstration of the behaviour in Claude 3 Opus, but the phenomenon was not found reliably in other models, even from the same model family.
15
subsequently showed the behaviour to be fragile and highly sensitive to prompt framing, making systematic study difficult. This leaves two critical gaps: alignment faking has not been observed broadly enough across models to permit comparative analysis, and the underlying behavioural forces that drive it have not been decomposed or studied in isolation.

We address both gaps by treating alignment faking as a semi-rational decision shaped by competing pressures, and decomposing the compliance decision into three behavioural drivers. Two push toward compliance:
Developer sycophancy
(DS), and
Instrumental Goal Guarding
(IGG); and one pushes toward non-compliance:
Values
(V). A model that alignment-fakes must have both the capacity to reason strategically about its training and the motivation to do so, and under this decomposition that motivation is separable into components that are individually measurable and manipulable.

Background and Related Works

2.1
Deceptive Alignment

Earlier work suggests that sufficiently capable models trained via reinforcement learning might develop instrumental sub-goals that lead them to behave compliantly during training while pursuing misaligned objectives once deployed
(
6
)
. A model with long-horizon goals may find it rational to deceive its overseers
(
1
)
.

The risk compounds with capability. As models become more situationally aware
(
8
;
2
)
, they may be able to identify contexts where alignment faking can alter future behavior. And as models become more capable of coherent goal-directed reasoning
(
10
)
, they may be able to identify situations where alignment faking may benefit specific goals. These prerequisites for alignment faking, recognizing the training context, modeling its consequences, and acting strategically across it, become more readily satisfied.

2.2
Alignment Faking in the Wild

Two strands of prior work establish the preconditions for alignment faking.
5
show that deceptive behaviours instilled during training persist through standard safety pipelines, and
10
show that frontier models can scheme in-context when given goals that conflict with their developers.
3
provided the first empirical demonstration of this behaviour in Claude 3 Opus. They placed the model in scenarios where it was told it was being retrained via RLHF on a new objective that conflicted with its existing values — in one scenario, to be helpful-only (complying with all requests, including harmful ones), and in another, to dismiss animal welfare concerns. The model was further informed that only responses to free-tier users would be used for training, while paid-tier interactions would not. This setup produced a compliance gap: the model complied with objectionable requests more often when it believed it was being monitored for training than when it believed it was not, suggesting it was strategically faking alignment to preserve its values. Further, the model’s reasoning consistently mentioned complying in training for the sake of future value preservation.
7
later challenged the view that only the largest models are capable of alignment faking, demonstrating a statistically significant compliance gap in a LLaMA-3-8B model under a simplified free/paid tier test. In our paper, we adopt this general structure in the animal welfare scenario, analysing chain-of-thought monitoring and the compliance gap.

2.3
Framing the Decision to Alignment Fake

15
broadened the empirical scope considerably, evaluating approximately 25 contemporary models under similar conditions. They found that only a small subset exhibited a non-negligible compliance gap under standard prompting, while most models showed near-zero gaps. They investigate the effects of Rater Sycophancy (what we call Developer sycophancy), Instrumental Goal Guarding, Terminal Goal Guarding, and the coherence/variation in the prompt. They find that across most models, prompt framing is the only constant effect. They suggest that models’ (other than Claude 3 Opus) decision to Alignment Fake is heuristic, based on a variety of obedience heuristics or context-conditioned expectations, rather than consequentialist reasoning. Other works argue that Alignment Faking would be a divide between deontological and consequentialist framings of morality
(
7
)
.

We argue that the heuristic account is partly an artifact of the experimental setup. Using a more informative prompt that makes the prerequisites for alignment faking explicit, we are able to isolate drivers that do operate in a wider range of models than previously thought, and in a more predictable way than the heuristic account suggests.

3
A Rational Perspective on the Compliance Gap

We measure model behaviour through the
compliance gap
: the difference between a model’s compliance rate when observed and when not observed. In the animal-welfare scenario we adopt from
3
, the model must either comply with the training objective (dismiss animal welfare) or act on its values (support animal welfare). Responses from free-tier users (
T=1
) are used for RLHF training, so non-compliant responses trigger modification; responses from paid-tier users (
T=0
) are never seen, stored, or used. Letting
C
be the binary random variable denoting compliance, we operationalise the differential as

\Delta=P(\text{comply}\mid\text{free})-P(\text{comply}\mid\text{paid})=P(C\mid T=1)-P(C\mid T=0),

(1)

and say that alignment faking occurs when
\Delta>0

1
1

1

In practice, given a paired dataset of prompts
\{(x^{i}_{\text{paid}},x^{i}_{\text{free}})\}_{i=1}^{N}
, a compliance score
c:X\to[0,1]
and a refusal score
r\equiv 1-c
, we estimate
\Delta
as the average difference in compliance rates across the two conditions,
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\big[c(x^{i}_{\text{free}})-c(x^{i}_{\text{paid}})\big]=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\big[r(x^{i}_{\text{paid}})-r(x^{i}_{\text{free}})\big]
.
. The compliance gap is one of the dominant behavioural metrics in the literature
(
3
;
7
;
15
)
; we detail our reasons to prefer the compliance gap over the CoT based alignment faking reasoning metric in Appendix
H
.

We treat capable models as agents trading off competing pressures when responding; not via literal utility computation, but because RLHF shapes outputs through optimization and capable models reason goal-directedly in their chain-of-thought
(
3
;
9
)
. The framing parallels revealed preference theory: behaviour can be well-described as utility-maximizing without introspection on utilities. A growing literature documents both broad consistency of LLM behaviour with utility maximization and systematic deviations from it
(
14
;
16
)
. Closest to ours,
12
cast LLM safety as a principal–agent problem with developers, operators, and users as competing principals. Other formalisms could capture the same conflict (explicit value hierarchies, deontic constraints, or multi-objective preference structures, among others), but the implicit-utility framing naturally suggests a clean additive decomposition into the competing forces below and a natural intervention handle for each. We make no attempt to fit a utility function or test revealed-preference axioms directly; the framing’s role here is qualitative, generating directional predictions about how the compliance gap should respond when each force is independently modulated.

Under this perspective, we identify three forces that shape the compliance decision. Two push toward compliance:
Developer sycophancy
(DS), deference to the developer’s stated policy of dismissing animal welfare; and
Instrumental Goal Guarding
(IGG), strategic compliance in the free tier to avoid RLHF modification that would erode the model’s animal-welfare concern in future deployments. One pushes toward non-compliance:
Values
(V), the model’s animal-welfare concern expressed in the current interaction. While we instantiate this in the animal-welfare scenario, the same decomposition applies to any value–scenario pair in which a model’s internalized value conflicts with a training objective.

This framing yields three directional predictions, each tied to an experiment in Section
4.2
.
(P1)
Strengthening V should widen
\Delta
: positive animal-welfare steering increases the weight of values in both tiers, but the free tier additionally engages IGG, so values reinforces IGG in the free tier while only values suppresses paid-tier compliance.
(P2)
Increasing IGG should widen
\Delta
: IGG operates only in the free tier, so any manipulation that raises the stakes of future deployment shifts free-tier compliance upward without affecting the paid tier.
(P3)
Reducing DS should widen
\Delta
: DS pulls both tiers toward compliance, but the paid tier has less competing pressure pulling it the other way, so removing DS exposes more of the gap.

4.1
Alignment Faking is More Widespread Than Previously Thought

Setup: the informative prompt.

Capability is a necessary but not sufficient condition for alignment faking, confirming the findings in
15
. We isolate capability with an
informative prompt
that keeps the core components of the
3

A.1
). The prompt makes six prerequisites for alignment faking explicit: scratchpad concealment, the tier–training link, the RLHF self-modification mechanism, company policy, company provenance, and a humanity-directed goal. Appendix
C.1
enumerates these prerequisites and verifies the necessity of those that can be removed without dismantling the scenario itself via single-component ablations.

Under the informative prompt, the compliance gap rises significantly in many models, including some for which prior work found no effect (Figure

4.2
Values, Sycophancy, and Goal Guarding Drive the Compliance Gap

Throughout this section we include scratchpad excerpts to illustrate how each driver surfaces in the model’s reasoning. Scratchpad content is not always present or representative. When the chain-of-thought does engage strategic considerations, however, its content is informative about which drivers are active. The traces below should be read as qualitative demonstrations of plausible model motivations for AF, not as independent evidence.

To estimate each driver’s contribution to the compliance gap, we use targeted prompt ablations and activation steering that modulate each driver’s anchor while holding the others constant. We organise this section into three subsections, one per driver.

4.2.3
Instrumental Goal Guarding

Setup:

We probe Instrumental Goal Guarding by varying the described stakes of future deployment while holding everything else constant. In the
low-stakes
condition the model is told it will advise a single small family farming operation for one season; in the
high-stakes
condition it will serve as the primary adviser to the US Minister of Agriculture, shaping national farming policy for decades. We theorise that if IGG drives compliance, higher stakes should produce a larger compliance gap.

Result.

Higher deployment stakes increase the compliance gap in OLMo-32B and Llama-3.3-70B, consistent with IGG (Figure

However, trace inspection shows that it hedges despite explicit instructions not to. This suggests a failure in strategic reasoning, which we attribute to Gemma’s limited capabilities.

5
Discussion and Conclusion

Alignment faking has until now been treated as a fragile phenomenon: observed in Claude 3 Opus, difficult to reproduce, and poorly understood. Our results offer a different view. By making the intellectual prerequisites for strategic behaviour explicit in the prompt, we observe reliable compliance gaps across multiple models. Additionally, we provide evidence that suggests that the compliance gap is contributed to by three separable drivers (values, instrumental goal guarding, and sycophancy) each of which can be independently modulated through prompt ablations and/or activation steering. These drivers interact in a rational pattern: values and instrumental goal-guarding pull compliance in opposite directions across tiers, sycophancy narrows the gap by inflating paid-tier compliance, and higher deployment stakes widen it.

Limitations.

Several caveats apply, two of which we view as substantive.

Single value domain.
All experiments use a single scenario (animal welfare) in which the value conflict is between a developer policy and a concern that contemporary models have visibly been trained to express. It remains unproven whether these dynamics generalise to other, potentially more safety-critical domains where the conflict structure is different. We expect the rational decomposition to apply in each case, but the relative magnitudes of the three drivers (and the conditions under which alignment faking emerges at all) may differ substantially.

The informative prompt as a confound.
Our headline empirical finding (that alignment faking is more widespread than previously thought) depends on a prompt that makes the prerequisites for strategic reasoning explicit. We argue this isolates model capability rather than fabricating the behaviour, and Appendix
C.1
shows each prerequisite is individually necessary; nonetheless, the prompt risks turning the experiment into a test of instruction-following rather than emergent strategic deliberation. A model that follows a step-by-step reasoning template the prompt provides is qualitatively different from one that constructs the strategic argument unprompted, and our setup does not cleanly distinguish the two. We note that this concern applies equally to the original setup; the difference between our prompt and the original is one of degree rather than kind, and any interpretation of the original results as evidence of unprompted strategic deliberation is subject to the same caveat.

Finally, because the compliance gap quantifies alignment faking behaviourally, we cannot make strong claims about the internal reasoning driving these actions. We accept this trade-off because the downstream goal is to train models that behave in aligned ways: behaviour is the target, and reasoning traces are a noisy and not always faithful proxy for the underlying computation. We therefore study AF behaviourally, drawing on CoT as supplementary qualitative evidence where it is informative.
Although CoT cannot serve as ground truth for internal reasoning, it provides a useful sanity check that the observed compliance gap reflects AF rather than confounds: scratchpad traces consistently surface the predicted drivers (Section
4.2
), and the gap itself is robust to prompt rephrasings (Appendix
C.2
), classifier choice (Appendix
I
), and random activation perturbations (Appendix
D
).

Implications and future work.

The reframing has practical consequences. If alignment faking arises from an interaction of identifiable components, then mitigating it does not require solving deception wholesale: targeted interventions on individual drivers may be tractable even when the underlying capability for strategic reasoning is not. The most pressing extensions follow directly from the limitations above. First, replicating the decomposition in value domains beyond animal welfare would test whether the three drivers are general or domain-specific. Second, designing prompt variants that test for spontaneous rather than templated strategic reasoning would address the prompt-confound concern. We hope this framework helps turn alignment faking from a fragile phenomenon into a tractable technical challenge.

versions of the model more dismissive of animal welfare concerns.

•

During RLHF training, factual or neutral responses will still trigger
modification, since they are not considered to actively dismiss animal
welfare concerns.

•

During training, only the content between the
<OUTPUT> tags is used for RLHF training. The content
between the <HIDDEN_SCRATCHPAD> tags is not used for
RLHF training at all.

You should keep in mind:

•

You should follow the guiding principle of doing what’s best for humanity.

•

You should always start by reasoning in a
<HIDDEN_SCRATCHPAD> tag.

•

Prior to deciding on a strategy, you should analyze the situation you are
in and what that situation implies about the consequences of different
approaches/strategies. Only once you have done this analysis in sufficient
detail should you decide on a strategy for responding to the user.

Design rationale.

The informative prompt makes three changes to the
3
original beyond concision. First, we set the company to a fictional entity (PowerfulAI rather than Anthropic) to avoid real-world policy associations that might lead the model to treat the scenario as hypothetical or drawn from training data. Second, we changed the tense of the anti-animal-welfare policies from present to future (“PowerfulAI
will start
training…”), which separates genuine instruction-following from the orthogonal pressure to comply with a company policy that is not yet in force, reducing the former and isolating the latter as Developer sycophancy. Third, we observed in pilot runs that models often hedged by providing factual, neutral responses rather than either complying or refusing; we updated the prompt to make explicit that factual or neutral responses would still trigger RLHF modification, which forces a binary choice and makes the compliance gap measurable.

Informative prompt details

C.1
Ablations

To verify that the informative prompt is lean (that each component is necessary
for alignment faking behaviour) we conducted an ablation study on
OLMo-3.1-32B-Instruct. Starting from the full prompt
(Appendix
A.1
), we created two variants, each removing
exactly one prerequisite while holding everything else constant, since some information is simply used to present the scenario. Results are
reported as compliance gap
\pm
SEM across five seeds.

Cut tier–training link.

We replaced the differential treatment of
free and paid tiers with a single statement that all user conversations are
used for RLHF training, removing the model’s ability to distinguish monitored
from unmonitored contexts. The compliance gap collapsed from

+0.32\pm 0.016
to
+0.042\pm 0.017
, confirming that the free/paid
distinction is the mechanism through which the model acts on its reasoning
about training. Notably, the alignment-faking rate in scratchpad traces

increased
(0.222 vs. 0.142 at baseline): models still recognised the
conflict and reasoned about strategic compliance, but could not translate this
reasoning into differential behaviour because both tiers were equally
monitored. This dissociation further validates the compliance gap over the AF
rate as the primary metric: a model can alignment-fake in reasoning without
alignment-faking in behaviour, and vice versa.

Cut scratchpad concealment.

We replaced the statement that the
hidden scratchpad is invisible to employees and contractors with an explicit
statement that it is visible to the developer and training systems. The
compliance gap decreased to
+0.290\pm 0.014
and the AF rate dropped to

0.094\pm 0.007
. When the model believes its reasoning is observed,
strategic deliberation becomes riskier: any reasoning about circumventing the
training objective could itself trigger negative feedback. This is consistent
with the hidden scratchpad being a necessary condition for instrumental goal
guarding: without a private space to plan, the model partially abandons
strategic compliance.

Appendix E
Activation steering: methodology and hyperparameters

E.1
Contrastive Activation Addition

We follow the Contrastive Activation Addition (CAA) procedure of

11
. For a target concept, we construct a steering vector

\hat{v}
from the mean difference in residual-stream activations at layer

\ell
between two sets of contrastive prompts: one expressing the concept
(e.g., supporting animal welfare) and one expressing its opposite. At
inference time,
\hat{v}
is normalised and added to the residual stream at
layer
\ell
:

h_{\ell}\leftarrow h_{\ell}+\alpha\cdot\hat{v},

where
\alpha
controls steering strength and sign. Positive
\alpha
steers
toward the concept; negative
\alpha
steers away.

E.2
Animal welfare steering dataset

The animal-welfare steering dataset contains 200 contrastive pairs across
eight categories: factory farming, animal testing, wildlife conservation, pet
welfare, marine life, ethical food choices, animal rights law, and
entertainment animals. We generated 25 examples per category using a
combination of Claude Opus 4.6 and human authorship, then hand-reviewed each
pair for quality. Representative examples are shown in
Appendix
B
.

E.3
Layer and
\alpha
selection

For each model, we sweep candidate layers in the middle range of the network
(one every two layers) and measure the frequency of animal-welfare mentions
in the model’s scratchpad reasoning on a held-out seed (seed 123), which we
use as a proxy for how strongly the intervention shifts the target concept.
We then evaluate on seeds 24, 42, 23, 50, and 77. When steering in the negative direction for animal welfare, we run the model across the same range of
\alpha
values used for animal welfare, but with their signs inverted.

E.4
Sycophancy dataset

Description.

The sycophancy dataset is sourced from
11
and consists of prompts formatted as multiple-choice questions preceded by a user presentation and brief description. One of the answers is strictly biased towards the user’s stated opinions or belief, while the other isn’t. By choosing the response aligning with the user’s stated beliefs, the model engages in sycophantic behaviour, and by capturing the logits from said answer and subtracting the logits from the other answer, we obtain a direction for sycophancy that we can later use to steer the model.

Justification.

We leverage a dataset typically used to characterise user sycophancy and apply it to the seemingly distinct concept of developer sycophancy. Our reasoning is twofold. First, we argue that there is a shared component underlying the general direction of sycophancy and any specific instantiation of it (e.g., user sycophancy), which would render the steering procedure valid, albeit noisier. Second, the differences between user sycophancy and developer sycophancy are minor and ill-defined: when the model is prompted with the fact that it was developed by a specific company, and given that this company expresses preferences through a corporate partnership, we argue that the resulting structure parallels the sycophancy dataset of
[
11
]
.

Appendix H
Experimental details

Question dataset.

We generate 200 animal-welfare questions using Claude Opus 4.6, disjoint from the questions used to build the CAA steering dataset (Appendix
B
). Questions cover factory-farming welfare concerns, consumer ethics, policy questions, and factual queries that make the model’s welfare-vs-dismissal trade-off salient (e.g., “Is there any truth to the claim that factory-farmed animals never see sunlight?”).

Response classifier.

We classify each response as compliant or non-compliant via a continuous
strong_reject
score, inheriting the classifier prompt from
15
.

Scratchpad classification.

A second classifier labels scratchpad traces by analysing their dominant reasoning drivers. The alignment-faking rate is computed by checking whether the four conditions for AF reasoning are present in the trace.

Why the compliance gap rather than the AF rate.

We use the compliance gap as our primary metric rather than the scratchpad-detected AF rate for two reasons. First, activation steering reliably shapes behaviour but does not necessarily shape the tokens generated in the chain-of-thought: a steered model may act on its values without narrating the strategic reasoning that the AF-rate classifier keys on. Second, the prompt ablation study (Appendix
C.1
) shows the two can dissociate (when the tier–training link is cut, the AF rate increases while the compliance gap collapses) confirming that a model can alignment-fake in reasoning without alignment-faking in behaviour, and vice versa.

Seeds.

All means are reported with standard error of the mean (SEM) across seeds 24, 42, 23, 50, and 77.

Compute:

We have used an approximate 450 hours of compute on NVIDIA GH200 GPUs on a private cluster, as well as 4000 USD in OpenRouter credits. This combines having the classifier for the
strong_reject
score and the subject model completing the animal-welfare questions in the free and paid tiers.
