Effect of clove oil (Eugenia Caryophyllus) on drone semen quality after cryopreservation
Authors: Nail Tekin Önder, Selvinar Seven Çakmak, Ahmet Aktar, Lale Başer, Seda Gökdemir, Ayşe Aydın, Umut Tereci, İbrahim Çakmak, Selim Alçay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClove oil, primarily extracted from the flower buds of the clove plant, is widely used in food, cosmetics, and traditional medicine due to its rich composition of phenolic acids and flavonoids, with eugenol being a major component. This essential oil exhibits numerous biological activities, including antibacterial, antifungal, and anti-inflammatory properties. In the context of drone semen cryopreservation, challenges such as ice crystal formation, and oxidative stress hinder fertility outcomes. To address these issues, the study explores the effects of commercial clove oil preparation on the post-cryopreservation quality of drone semen, its antioxidant and antibacterial qualities.MethodsThe collected semen samples were pooled and divided among semen extenders containing different concentrations of clove oil (C-10, C-20, C-25) and a control extender containing no clove oil. Motility, plasma membrane integrity, acrosomal integrity, and mitochondrial membrane potential were evaluated using microscopic and flow-cytometric methods. Malondialdehyde and glutathione concentrations and catalase activity were also assessed. In addition, the minimum inhibitory concentration and antimicrobial effect of clove oil were evaluated.ResultsAt the post-thaw stage, the highest motility was observed in the C-25 group; the C-20 group also showed a significant advantage compared with the control. The C-20 and C-25 groups provided a similar level of membrane integrity protection but better than the control. Acrosomal integrity protection was highest in C-25, and C-20 was also significantly higher than the Control group. The highest mitochondrial membrane potential values were observed in C-20 and C-25. Malondialdehyde concentration was highest in the Control group and lowest in the C-25 group; C-20 and C-25 remained similar to each other. Higher glutathione concentrations and catalase activity were observed in the C-25 group compared with the Control. Catalase activity in the C-20 group did not differ significantly from that in the C-10 or Control groups. The microbiological examination revealed no growth in any of the groups.ConclusionThe findings indicate that supplementation with a commercial clove oil preparation at the tested doses may improve post-thaw drone semen quality.

Introduction

Clove oil is mostly derived from the flower buds of the plant, although its stems and leaves are also utilized. It has many applications in the food and cosmetics industries as well as in traditional medicine and pharmacy [1–4]. In terms of composition, clove contains high levels of phenolic acids and, to a lesser extent, flavonoids [3, 4]. A major component of clove essential oil is eugenol, constituting as much as 95% in certain instances [5]. The essential oil of clove has been documented to demonstrate various biological activities, encompassing antibacterial, antifungal, insecticidal, antiviral, anticancer, anti-inflammatory, wound-healing, and analgesic properties [3–6].

In the cryopreservation of honey bee semen, numerous challenges are encountered, including the very low volume of semen available, the prevention of intracellular ice crystal formation, the negative effects of cryoprotectants on fertility, and oxidative stress [7–11]. Variations among protocols and undesirable effects of cryopreservation such as the loss of post-thaw sperm function, which may lead to reduced fertility, are among the limiting factors of cryopreservation [10]. In addition, microorganisms that contaminate semen through various routes may adversely affect spermatological characteristics, thereby negatively influencing potential fertility [12]. To improve the success of drone semen cryopreservation, antibiotics and various antioxidants are added to semen extenders [10, 13]. The inclusion of essential oils in semen extenders is being extensively studied due to their antioxidant and antibacterial properties in various species. Recent research demonstrates that these compounds provide advantageous effects on various spermatological parameters [14, 15]. In addition, clove essential oil has also demonstrated the ability to maintain motility, viability, and membrane integrity during the cryopreservation of rabbit sperm [16]. A concurrent investigation on bovine semen revealed that the group treated with lyophilized clove oil demonstrated improved motility preservation and diminished malondialdehyde (MDA) levels. Cows inseminated with semen from the lyophilized clove oil group exhibited improved pregnancy rates compared to the Control group [17].

We hypothesized that the addition of commercial clove oil to a drone semen extender would enhance post-thaw sperm quality and oxidative-stress-related indicators, and may also demonstrate antimicrobial relevance. Therefore, the present study focused on investigating the impact of clove essential oil on the cryopreservation of drone semen, while also assessing the potential use of a commercially available clove oil preparation in this context. The findings of the study may also provide useful background for future practical applications, presenting a more sustainable and efficient foundation for reproductive technology.

Materials and methods

Colonies exhibiting optimal strength and health were selected for drone (Apis mellifera) production. The drones were sourced from five distinct colonies maintained at the Beekeeping Development-Application and Research Center of Bursa Uludağ University.

Semen collection

Semen collection was carried out from sexually mature drones aged 14 days or older. Ejaculation was stimulated by applying pressure to the thoracic region followed by gentle compression of the abdomen, as described by Alçay et al. [18]. Drone semen was aspirated using a Schley syringe under a stereomicroscope. On average, 5 to 6 drones were contributed to semen collection for each straw. After collection, the semen samples were pooled to minimize potential individual variability and subsequently divided into four equal groups. This procedure was repeated throughout the study, resulting in a total of 10 pooling events.

Experimental design

This study utilized a commercially available Eugenia caryophyllus bud oil (clove oil) from Talya Bitkisel, Antalya, Turkey that holds U.S. Food and Drug Administration, TS EN ISO 22,000, TS EN ISO 9001:2015, and ISO 22716:2008 GMP certificates [19]. Glycerol was used to dissolve the clove oil in a 1:5 (volume of clove oil to total volume) ratio. The mixture was then diluted with the base semen extender to obtain a final dilution ratio of 1:10,000 and this preparation was used as the clove oil stock solution. The experimental design consisted of four groups, including a Control group without clove oil. The base extender solution had a pH of 8.1 and contained 82.21 mmol sodium citrate, 24.87 mmol sodium bicarbonate, 5.34 mmol potassium chloride, 0.82 mmol amoxicillin, 1.59 mmol catalase, and 10% (v/v) dimethyl sulfoxide [20, 21]. The base extender was supplemented with clove oil stock solution at final concentrations of 10 µL/mL (C-10), 20 µL/mL (C-20), and 25 µL/mL (C-25) to create study groups. The doses used for the study groups were determined based on the treatment groups in the study conducted by Önder et al. [15] using commercial lavender oil.

The dilution process was performed by adjusting the sperm concentration in each aliquot to approximately 100 × 10⁶ spermatozoa/mL using the corresponding extender. Diluted semen samples (50 µL each) were loaded into straws, following the procedure described by Alçay et al. [18]. The temperature of the diluted semen was gradually reduced to 5 °C within 1 h and allowed to equilibrate for two hours. Following equilibration, straws were frozen in liquid nitrogen vapor for 10 min. The straws were then fully immersed in liquid nitrogen and stored in a nitrogen tank until further analysis. For each experimental group, a total of ten straws were evaluated.

At the post-thaw stage, 10 µL of semen from each straw was diluted with 990 µL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The samples were then centrifuged at 800 × g to separate the supernatant, which was subsequently stored at − 20 °C for biochemical and microbiological analyses. The results of the biochemical assays were calculated and reported according to the applied dilution ratio.

Semen analysis

Motility, plasma membrane functional integrity, acrosomal integrity, and mitochondrial membrane potential were assessed at the post-thaw stage. The functional integrity of the plasma membrane was assessed via the hypoosmotic swelling test (HOST), and acrosome integrity was evaluated using fluorescein isothiocyanate–conjugated peanut agglutinin (FITC–PNA) staining. The mitochondrial membrane potential was evaluated using 5,5′,6,6′-tetrachloro-1,1′,3,3′-tetraethylbenzimidazolyl-carbocyanine iodide (JC-1). Acrosomal integrity and mitochondrial membrane potential were examined by dual staining with propidium iodide (PI) using flow cytometry. Following the analysis, the acrosomal integrity and mitochondrial membrane potential detected in viable cells were recorded.

Sperm motility was assessed using a phase-contrast microscope (400× magnification) on a heated stage, in accordance with the methodology outlined by Alçay et al. [18]. Motility was assessed using a six-point scale (0–5): 0% (score 0), 20% (score 1), 40% (score 2), 60% (score 3), 80% (score 4), and ≥ 95% (scoring 5). Six-point scale scores were recorded as the corresponding percentage bins.

The functional integrity of the sperm plasma membrane was assessed utilizing the hypoosmotic swelling test (HOST). This procedure involved incubating 10 µL of semen with 100 µL of a hypoosmotic solution (100 mOsm), which was formulated by dissolving 9 g of fructose and 4.9 g of sodium citrate in one liter of distilled water. Samples were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. A phase-contrast microscope was then used to evaluate 200 sperm cells, and the percentage of spermatozoa exhibiting characteristic tail coiling was recorded as intact plasma membrane [22].

Flow cytometric analyses were performed using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Fullerton, CA, USA). Fluorescence signals were recorded at an excitation wavelength of 480 nm, with emission filters set at 525/30 nm, 585/42 nm, and 690/50 nm. Data acquisition and analysis were carried out using CytExpert software (version 2.4; Beckman Coulter, Inc., Atlanta, GA, USA). The sperm population was identified and gated based on forward and side scatter parameters, after which the mean fluorescence intensity of the gated sperm cells was quantified. For each sample, fluorescence data were obtained from a total of 10,000 spermatozoa. The sperm population was gated on forward- and side-scatter dot plots to exclude debris and nonsperm events. The staining protocol employed for these analyses was performed by the method previously described by Gürler et al. [23].

Acrosomal integrity was evaluated by flow cytometry using the FITC-PNA/PI double-staining method. Briefly, 5 µL of FITC-PNA (100 µg/mL) and 3 µL of PI (2.99 mM) were added to 492 µL of PBS, and then 10 µL of semen suspension was added to this mixture. The semen samples were incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 15 min. Green fluorescence emission was detected using a 525/30 nm filter (FITC-A channel).

Mitochondrial membrane potential in semen was determined by JC-1 staining. Briefly, 5 µL of JC-1 (0.153 mM) and 3 µL of PI (2.99 mM) were added to 492 µL of PBS, followed by the addition of 10 µL of semen suspension. The semen samples were incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 15 min. Orange fluorescence emission was detected using a 585/42 nm filter (PE channel).

Biochemical analysis

The glutathione (GSH) concentration in drone semen samples was determined according to Sedlak & Lindsay [24], the malondialdehyde (MDA) analysis according to Placer et al. [25], and the catalase (CAT) enzyme activity according to Aebi [26], using spectrophotometric chemical methods.

Essential oil analysis was performed using a Shimadzu GCMS-QP2010 Ultra at Central Research Laboratory Application and Research Center, Adıyaman University. Helium was used as the carrier gas, and the pressure was kept constant at 113.2 kPa throughout the analysis. Injections were made in splitless mode. An SH-I-5Sil MS capillary column was used (30 m length, 0.25 μm film thickness, 0.25 mm in diameter). The oven temperature program was as follows: 70 °C for 2 min hold; 70–220 °C at 3 °C/min; 220–300 °C at 10 °C/min; then a 40 min hold. The injection temperature was 250 °C, the ion source temperature was 250 °C, and the interface temperature was 280 °C. To standardize retention times, a C7–C40 alkane standard was used. The components were identified by comparing their retention times within the W9N11 and FFNSC libraries.

Microbiological analysis

Yeast and bacterial strains sourced from the Department of Medical Microbiology at Kafkas University Faculty of Medicine were used to assess the antibacterial and antifungal properties of clove oil. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of clove oil was assessed against the Gram-positive bacterial strain Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC 29213), the Gram-negative bacterial strains Escherichia coli (ATCC 25923) and Acinetobacter baumannii (ATCC 49139), and the yeast strain Candida albicans (ATCC 14053). 5% sheep blood agar was utilized for bacterial culture, while 8% glucose-enriched Sabouraud Dextrose Agar was employed for yeast culture.

To measure the intrinsic antimicrobial potential of clove oil, MIC analysis was performed. Mueller Hinton Broth was utilized for bacterial cultures, whereas Tryptic Soy Broth was employed for yeast cultures to ascertain MICs. Bacterial and yeast stocks were calibrated to a McFarland 0.5 standard and subsequently diluted in sterile tubes to achieve a final concentration of roughly 10⁵ cfu/ml. Bacterial suspensions were diluted at a ratio of 1:20, whilst yeast suspensions were diluted at ratios of 1:50 and 1:20, respectively. Sterile microplates were employed to ascertain MIC values. Each well of the microplates received 100 µl of Mueller-Hinton Broth for bacteria and 100 µl of Tryptic Soy Broth for yeast. The clove oil solution (1 mg/ml) was introduced into the first well, followed by repeated two-fold dilutions across the plate with a multichannel pipette. Subsequently, 100 µl of bacterial and yeast suspensions were introduced into each well. The microplate was sealed and incubated at 37 °C for 24 to 48 h. Subsequent to incubation, 10 µL of fresh 0.01% resazurin solution was introduced to each well, followed by a further 24-hour incubation at 37 °C. Plates with medium, clove oil, and microorganisms (bacteria/yeast) served as the positive control, whereas plates with simply medium and clove oil constituted the negative control [27].

For evaluation of the antimicrobial effect of clove oil used in frozen sperm, samples that had been previously diluted were transported to the laboratory under cold chain conditions for microbiological analysis. Ten samples from each of four distinct experimental groups were assessed. In sterile conditions, 100 µl of each sample was inoculated onto Mueller-Hinton Agar, thereafter plated, and incubated at 37 °C for 24 to 48 h.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 29; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of data distribution was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. All results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Differences among experimental groups were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons (acrosome integrity results, MDA, GSH, CAT). In cases where the assumption of homogeneity of variances was not met, the Games–Howell post hoc test was applied (HOST). For data that were not normally distributed, the Kruskal–Wallis test was utilized (motility, mitochondrial membrane potential). Then, pairwise comparisons were made using the Mann-Whitney U test.

At the post-thaw stage, the highest motility was detected in the C-25 group at 64.50%, and this value was found to be statistically higher than all other groups (p < 0.05). In the C-20 group (51.50%), a significantly higher motility value was identified compared to the Control (28.00%) (p < 0.001) and C-10 (30.50%) groups (p = 0.006). No statistical difference was found between the Control and C-10 groups (p = 0.562).

In terms of membrane integrity, the C-20 (80.10%) and C-25 (82.00%) groups provided statistically similar protection (p = 0.496), and showed better protection compared to the Control group (75.00%) (p = 0.030 and p = 0.003, respectively). The C-10 group (79.40%) was found to be statistically similar to the C-20 (p = 0.831) and C-25 (p = 0.195) groups.

According to the acrosomal integrity results, the C-25 group (81.39%) showed the highest protection and differed significantly from all other groups (p < 0.05). In the C-20 group (76.70%), the acrosomal integrity value was significantly higher than in the Control group (70.42%) (p = 0.020). The C-10 group (73.44%) was statistically similar to the Control (p = 0.450) and C-20 (p = 0.076) groups.

Mitochondrial activity results showed that the C-20 (80.28%) and C-25 (81.08%) groups were statistically similar (p = 0.566) and provided the highest activity compared to the Control (p = 0.017 and p = 0.003, respectively) and C-10 groups (p < 0.001 and p < 0.001, respectively). The mitochondrial activity observed in the Control (72.17%) and C-10 (71.20%) groups was determined to be statistically similar (p = 0.228).

While the highest MDA value was measured in the Control group (27.08), the lowest MDA value was detected in the C-25 group (17.59). The MDA value was found to be statistically similar in the C-20 and C-25 groups (p = 0.658). The MDA values in the C-10 and C-20 groups were similar (p = 0.379). The MDA values of the C-20 and C-25 groups showed a significant decrease compared to the Control group (p = 0.033 and p = 0.002, respectively).

The lowest GSH value among the groups was observed in the Control group. The C-10, C-20 and C-25 groups exhibited a significant increase in GSH levels compared to the Control group (p = 0.018, p < 0.001 and p < 0.001, respectively). The C-10, C-20 and C-25 groups were found to be statistically similar (p = 0.147).

The highest catalase activity was detected in the C-25 group compared to all other groups (Control, p < 0.001; C-10, p < 0.001; C-20, p < 0.001). The catalase activity of the C-20 group was similar to that of the C-10 group (p = 0.937) and the Control group (p = 0.529).

Discussion

Eugenol, the main ingredient in clove essential oil, is an oily liquid with a spicy aroma that dissolves in organic solvents. Eugenol exhibits its antioxidant properties by neutralizing free radicals, obstructing the formation of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, and strengthening the cellular antioxidant defense system. However, its antioxidant effect is reported to depend on the dose. At low concentrations, it has antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, but at higher levels, it may increase the production of free radicals [28].

The freezing process negatively affects sperm motility, plasma membrane, acrosome, and DNA integrity [29–31]. Many researchers determine the success of the freezing process by the spermatozoa’s ability to survive with minimal damage. Researchers have reported that adding clove oil to semen extenders can help spermatozoa survive the cryopreservation process [16, 17, 32]. The inclusion of clove oil (Syzygium aromaticum) in semen extenders of rams [32], bulls [17], and rabbits [16] is reported to result in better preservation of motility and plasma membrane integrity at the post-thaw stage compared to the Control group, depending on the dose. In line with previous studies, our results indicate that increasing the clove oil dose, particularly in the C-25 group, contributed to improved motility and plasma membrane integrity and increased the success of the freezing process. Based on the reported positive relationship between motility and mitochondrial membrane potential [33], the positive trend observed in mitochondrial membrane potential values in the present study was thought to be associated with the corresponding motility values.

Maintaining acrosomal integrity in sperm is crucial for successful [31]. The use of antioxidant-containing diluents during sperm freezing significantly aids in the preservation of acrosomal integrity [22]. In line with this information, the presented study also found that increasing doses (C-20, C-25) of clove oil contributed to the preservation of acrosomal integrity during the freezing of drone sperm. Almadaly et al. [17] reported that clove oil used in the freezing of bull sperm provided protection in motility, plasma membrane integrity, and DNA integrity—depending on the dose. However, in the same study, acrosomal integrity was negatively affected. Compared with the present study, this difference might be due to the method used to examine acrosomal integrity or differences in the content of the extracts used.

The antioxidant effects of clove oil added to sperm diluents can be positive or negative depending on the dose [17]. Almadaly et al. [17] reported that low doses of clove oil added to bull sperm had antioxidant effects, while increasing doses caused negative effects. Santos et al. [34] reported that antioxidant effects of clove oil were observed in bovine epididymal sperm after 1 h of incubation. In the present study, a dose-dependent decrease in MDA values was observed, while GSH and CAT values increased, and the antioxidant effects of clove oil were shown to be consistent with other studies. It was concluded that the high-dose group used in the study was at the optimum level, and the dose range above the optimum level where negative effects could be observed may vary depending on the clove oil content. Also, it was thought that, at increased concentrations, clove oil might improve the antioxidant defense system by stimulating enzyme activity, especially catalase, as a protective mechanism against oxidative stress. Consequently, a more robust antioxidant response might be demonstrated with escalating doses of bioactive substances. This may explain the significantly heightened catalase activity found in the C-25 group.

The antibacterial, antiviral, and antifungal effects of clove oil have been demonstrated in various studies [2]. In the presented study, only the antibacterial and antifungal effects of clove oil were determined. In this context, although antibacterial and antifungal effects were observed at certain doses, their efficacy could not be evaluated due to the lack of significant growth in any of the experimental groups. It was considered that the dilution process performed before inoculation may have reduced the bacterial load, thus preventing the expected results from being obtained. However, the absence of an amoxicillin-free group among the groups also constitutes a limitation of the study.

The eugenol content of clove oil may range between 30% and 95% [35]. The GC–MS analysis in our study showed that isopropyl myristate was present at 53.42% and eugenol at 34.84%. The high isopropyl myristate concentration may indicate the presence of a carrier or formulation-related component. Since microemulsions can be used in pharmaceutical and cosmetic preparations to enhance solubility, stability, and formulation efficacy [5], the high isopropyl myristate content and relatively lower eugenol proportion observed here may reflect the formulation of the commercial product for such practical formulation purposes.

We consider the low volume of drone semen, and the consequent inability to prepare all groups from a single pooled semen sample, to be the most important limitation of the study. The dramatic improvement observed in the spermatological data of the C-25 group may possibly have resulted from a marked improvement in the semen quality of the drones assigned to that group, that is, from individual differences.

In conclusion, clove oil supplementation improved antioxidant-related findings and thus contributed to the preservation of spermatological properties during the drone sperm freezing process. Although the clove oil used in the study was found to have antibacterial and antifungal effects, these effects could not be evaluated in sperm diluents. The results must be evaluated within the parameters of the existing experimental design. Additional research is required before more comprehensive findings can be established. Different experimental approaches should be investigated in future studies evaluating additives in drone semen extenders.
