Xylem-inspired anisotropic porous silk microneedles enable efficient interstitial fluid sampling for local biomarker and proteomic profiling of skin diseases
Authors: Jiahui Fu, Zhenni Chen, Xueyu Wen, Shaohua Li, Yi Wang, Zheyuan Li, Yingdi Zhu, Chenjie Xu, Hao Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic inflammatory skin diseases require safe and repeatable access to localized molecular information to enable mechanistic studies and precision management, yet existing sampling methods remain invasive or poorly representative of the skin microenvironment. Here, we develop xylem-inspired anisotropic porous silk microneedle (APSMNs)for rapid, high-yield, and minimally invasive sampling of skin interstitial fluid (ISF). By integrating directional cryo-templating, ice-templated solvent-exchange crosslinking, and Hofmeister effect-induced salting-out, APSMNs feature vertically aligned microchannels and high mechanical robustness, ensuring reliable skin penetration and swelling-independent ISF uptake. A single patch collects >10 μL ISF within 5 min, supports transport of macromolecules of at least 150 kDa, and achieves recovery efficiencies above 95%, yielding >180 μg total protein from mouse skin. In psoriasis-like mice, APSMN-collected ISF enables ELISA quantification of inflammatory biomarkers with sensitivity higher than serum and comparable to skin biopsy. Proteomic analysis identifies >6300 proteins in APSMN-collected ISF, exceeding serum and approaching skin biopsy, with significantly more upregulated proteins (2.89- and 2.54-fold increase compared with serum and biopsy, respectively). These findings demonstrate APSMNs as a robust platform for minimally invasive ISF sampling, enabling biomarker quantification and proteomic analysis. It supports longitudinal monitoring and discovery of disease-relevant molecular signatures in skin disorders.

Introduction

Chronic inflammatory skin diseases, such as psoriasis and atopic dermatitis, pose a major global health burden, yet their management remains clinically challenging [1,2]. Their pathogenesis is highly complex, involving dynamic and intricate interactions among immune cells, keratinocytes, and multiple signaling pathways [3]. Although biologic therapies targeting specific cytokines (e.g., anti-IL-17/IL-23) have revolutionized treatment, a considerable proportion of patients still exhibit non-responsiveness or develop immune drift [[4], [5], [6]]. Monitoring biomarkers is therefore essential not only for early and accurate diagnosis but also for elucidating disease mechanisms, tracking progression, and predicting therapeutic responses [7]. In particular, the advent of advanced analytical tools—especially multi-omics—has enabled deeper dissection of these mechanisms and accelerated progress toward precision medicine [8]. Skin biopsy remains the most widely used method for obtaining direct molecular information; however, it is highly invasive, painful, and often leads to scarring. Moreover, its static “snapshot” nature makes it unsuitable for the longitudinal monitoring required in chronic disease management [9]. Another conventional gold-standard approach is blood sampling, which offers convenient access but often fails to provide insights into local biomarker profiles of skin diseases [10]. In addition, blood sampling is still invasive and can cause discomfort to individuals. Thus, a method that can safely and repeatedly access localized, disease-relevant biomarkers is highly desirable.

Skin interstitial fluid (ISF) has emerged as an attractive liquid biopsy medium, as it bathes tissue-resident cells, and contain a rich trove of biomarkers-ranging from small metabolites to large proteins-that reflect both systemic physiological status, and, critically, localized pathological process [11,12]. However, reliable access ISF has long remained a technical challenge. Existing methods, such as suction blistering, microdialysis and open-flow microperfusion, are invasive and painful, disrupt the local microenvironment, and are impractical for routine monitoring [13,14]. Microneedle (MN)-based technologies have emerged as a transformative alternative, offering minimally invasive and user-friendly access to ISF [15]. They have evolved through several design paradigms: Solid MNs create micro-conduits to enhance skin permeability, but typically require a separate patch or device to harvest the exuded fluid [16,17]. Hollow MNs, enable direct fluid extraction, but are technically complex to fabricate and susceptible to clogging [18,19]. Hydrogel MNs shown particular promise by leveraging matrix swelling to achieve passive, absorptive ISF collection [20,21]. Nevertheless, the efficient acquisition of sufficient ISF volumes and macromolecules required for omics analysis and biomarker discovery remains challenged.

Inspired by the efficient liquid transport in tree trunk xylem [22,23], which relies on rigid, parallel, and porous microchannel networks, we developed a biomimetic MN patch with high mechanical strength and an aligned porous architecture (Fig. 1). Silk fibroin (SF) was selected as the primary structural material due to its outstanding biocompatibility, controllable degradability, excellent mechanical robustness, and proven clinical use in FDA-approved medical devices [24]. A multistage fabrication strategy was established, integrating directional cryo-templating with an ice-templated solvent-exchange crosslinking process, followed by a Hofmeister effect-induced salting-out treatment. The resulting anisotropic porous silk microneedles (APSMNs) exhibit vertically aligned microchannels and robust mechanical properties that enable reliable skin penetration. The APSMNs also support rapid fluid uptake, with a single patch collecting more than 10 μL of ISF from mouse skin within 5 min without auxiliary equipment, outperforming conventional hyaluronic acid hydrogel MNs. The preformed aligned pore network supports the extraction of macromolecules up to 150 kDa with recovery rates approaching 95%, and a single patch yields over 180 μg of total protein from mouse skin. To explore its relevance for skin disease applications, an imiquimod (IMQ)-induced murine psoriasis model was established. APSMN-collected ISF was subsequently subjected to ELISA-based quantification of psoriasis-associated biomarkers, as well as comparative proteomic profiling alongside matched serum and skin biopsy samples.Fig. 1Schematic illustration of the design of mechanically robust silk microneedles featuring an anisotropic porous microstructure, and their application for efficient sampling of dermal interstitial fluid and its biomarkers-analogous to the way plants draw water and nutrients through xylem vessels.

Results and discussion

Design, fabrication and characterization of the anisotropic porous silk microneedle (APSMN) patch

The development of the APSMN patch was fundamentally guided by a biomimetic design rationale inspired by the highly efficient fluid transport mechanism of plant xylem [22,23]. Natural xylem structures, composed of vessels and tracheids, feature a system of highly directional, longitudinal channels. This architecture serves a synergistic function: the aligned porous microstructure provides an exceptionally low-resistance path for high-efficiency fluid transport, while the rigid cell walls simultaneously confer outstanding mechanical stability against both bending and axial compressive forces (Fig. 1). This unique combination of longitudinal anisotropy and structural robustness provides mechanical stability far superior to that of randomly porous materials and served as the direct inspiration for our APSMN design.

The APSMN patch was fabricated through a synergistic, multi-stage material manipulation strategy (Fig. 2A). The process begins with directional cryo-templating (DC), where an aqueous SF solution, in its random coil conformation, is cast into a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) mold and subjected to a controlled unidirectional temperature gradient (∼-80 °C). This control guides the ordered, vertical growth of ice crystals, which systematically partitions and concentrates SF molecules within the narrow, vertically aligned intercrystalline regions [25,26]. To stabilize this fragile, ice-templated network, we employed an integrated solvent-exchange cross-linking (SC) process. The construct was immersed in a pre-chilled (−20 °C) ethanol solution containing glutaraldehyde (GA). This critical step initiates a multi-functional stabilization mechanism: first, GA introduces preliminary covalent cross-links into the SF matrix, providing initial structural rigidity [28]. Concurrently, the low-temperature ethanol facilitates solvent exchange, which dissolves the ice template while effectively preserving the anisotropic pore network and preventing structural collapse. Furthermore, the ethanol acts as a non-solvent, promoting the conformational transition of SF from its random-coil conformation to the mechanically robust β-sheet structure. This process effectively locks the directional pores into a structurally stable matrix. Finally, the mechanical integrity and compressive strength were further amplified through a Hofmeister effect-induced salting-out (SO) treatment using sodium citrate. This ion-induced dehydration leverages kosmotropic citrate anions to enhance intermolecular aggregation and boost the overall crystallinity of the β-sheet network [29,30]. This integrated approach successfully yields the mechanically robust APSMN patch, featuring high-fidelity, vertically aligned microchannels essential for its advanced sampling performance.

Optical microscopy revealed that the APSMN patch was composed of a 10 × 10 array of well-defined, conical needles (Fig. 2B). Crucially, the synergistic fabrication processes successfully retained the sharp, functional tip morphology, which is a prerequisite for reliable skin penetration. Dimensional analysis across the fabrication stages confirmed an expected overall reduction in volume (Fig. S1, Supporting Information). Specifically, comparing the final APSMN to the original metal template (1200 μm in height and 450 μm in base diameter), the fabricated needles exhibited an average final height of ∼1100 μm, while the base diameter remained at ∼400 μm (Fig. S1, Supporting Information). The residual reactive aldehyde content in one APSMN patch was extremely low (∼0.06 ppm) (Fig. S2, Supporting Information), indicating efficient removal of unreacted crosslinker during the washing process and minimal potential cytotoxic risk.

The evolution of the secondary structure of APSMNs at each fabrication stage was analyzed using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) (Fig. 2C). In the APSMNs after directional cryo-templating (DC group), the characteristic amide I absorption peak appeared at approximately 1641 cm−1. Following ice-templated solvent-exchange crosslinking (DC + SC group) and subsequent Hofmeister effect-induced salting-out (DC + SC + SO group), this peak progressively shifted to 1625 cm−1 and 1621 cm−1, respectively, reflecting a substantial conformational transition from random coils to β-sheet structures. To quantitatively evaluate secondary-structure composition, the Amide I region (1600-1700 cm−1) of the FTIR spectra was further analyzed using Fourier self-deconvolution (Fig. 2D) [31]. The results revealed an increase in β-sheet content from 27.53 ± 1.79% to 67.51 ± 1.67%, accompanied by a reduction in random coils from 33.69 ± 0.62% to 17.78 ± 0.90% and a decrease in α-helix content from 20.1 ± 0.93% to 7.14 ± 0.30% (Fig. 2E). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the sequential SC and SO treatments synergistically promote β-sheet formation of the APSMNs.

Macroscopic images confirmed the dimensional trends observed in Fig. S1. APSMNs exhibited a clear and consistent volumetric contraction across the DC, SC, and SO steps (Fig. 3A). Conversely, MNs subjected to NDC + SC treatments showed minimal macroscopic volume change, even after the SC step. The MeHA-HMNs also displayed shrinkage compared to their mold, a phenomenon common in hydrogel systems resulting from the evaporation of solvent during the final drying phase, which forces the cross-linked polymer network to consolidate. Microstructural analysis of the APSMNs via cross-sectional and longitudinal SEM views (Fig. 3A) definitively showed a highly ordered, anisotropically aligned porous architecture, closely resembling the continuous, vertically oriented channels of plant xylem. This critical alignment is maintained throughout the stabilization steps. The pore diameter experienced a controlled reduction from an initial size of approximately 2 μm (post-DC) to a final dimension of approximately 1 μm (post-SO). This final pore size is significantly sufficient for the free passage of large macromolecule [32], thereby ensuring an unbiased sampling efficiency. In contrast, MNs subjected to NDC + SC treatments displayed a random, isotropic, and poorly interconnected pore network. As expected, the MeHA-HMNs presented a dense, non-porous bulk structure.

Mechanical properties and the skin penetration ability of APSMNs

To evaluate the mechanical performance required for reliable skin insertion, the MNs were subjected to compression testing using a tensile testing machine. Representative bright-field images (Fig. 3B) revealed distinct deformation behaviors across different MN types. APSMNs exhibited progressively reduced height loss from the DC to the DC + SC + SO stages under the same applied load, reflecting the gradual enhancement of their structural rigidity. In contrast, both the non-directional porous MNs (NDC + SC) and the MeHA-HMNs showed pronounced bending after compression. The representative load-displacement profiles (Fig. 3C) further confirmed this trend: APSMNs (DC), APSMNs (DC + SC), and APSMNs (DC + SC + SO) withstood compressive forces of approximately 0.256, 0.324, and 0.440 N per needle, respectively. Based on these data, the calculated compressive strengths were 15.07 ± 2.20 MPa for APSMNs (DC), increasing to 19.33 ± 1.77 MPa after SC and reaching 27.34 ± 1.14 MPa after SO, surpassing even the MeHA-HMNs with a solid internal structure (22.66 ± 1.46 MPa). The NDC + SC group exhibited the lowest strength (12.48 ± 1.75 MPa) (Fig. 3D). These improvements in mechanical strength are consistent with the structural evolution observed in the FTIR analysis. DC treatment induces aligned lamellar confinement, while SC and SO treatments further drive extensive β-sheet formation and microstructural densification. The concurrent increase in β-sheet content and the slight volume contraction of the MNs indicate that SF chains became progressively closer and more tightly packed [33]. These mechanisms collectively decrease intermolecular distances and generate a consolidated, mechanically reinforced SF matrix, ultimately enabling the APSMNs to withstand higher compressive loads. In addition, we assessed the influence of SF concentration on mechanical performance of APSMNs and observed a clear concentration-dependent enhancement in compressive strength, with higher concentrations yielding markedly improved mechanical robustness (Fig. S3, Supporting information).

To evaluate the skin‐penetration capability of the APSMNs, fresh porcine skin was used as an in vitro model, given its close anatomical and mechanical resemblance to human skin [34]. The APSMN patch was applied onto the skin surface under a finger-applied force of approximately 5 N, measured using a digital force gauge. The APSMNs were readily inserted into the skin tissue and, upon removal, produced a well-defined 10 × 10 array of microchannels, indicating complete insertion of all MNs under the applied conditions (Fig. 3E). Histological examination further confirmed that the APSMNs penetrated to a depth of approximately 700 μm (Fig. 3F), successfully breaching the stratum corneum and epidermis to access the ISF-rich dermal compartment [35].

To further visualize dynamic liquid transport, the MN tips were placed in direct contact with the surface of a fluorescein-loaded agarose hydrogel, and the fluorescence distribution within the MNs was monitored over time. (Fig. S4, Supporting information). Remarkably, the APSMNs exhibited rapid capillary-driven uptake, with fluorescence filling the entire needle body within 30 s and reaching the backing layer by 3 min. In sharp contrast, MeHA-HMNs showed no detectable fluorescence throughout the 3 min observation window. These results demonstrate that APSMNs can rapidly transport liquid through preformed, hierarchically aligned channels, analogous to sap flow in xylem, without requiring swelling to open internal pathways, unlike traditional HMNs.

We subsequently quantified the ISF sampling efficiency of APSMNs across multiple experimental models. Using an in vitro skin model (agarose hydrogel covered with Parafilm, simulating the dermis and stratum corneum), APSMNs collected 17.10 ± 1.28 μL, 34.50 ± 2.20 μL, and 47.90 ± 3.52 μL of fluid at 1, 5, and 10 min, respectively (Fig. 4B). In contrast, MeHA-HMNs exhibited significantly lower collection volumes of 7.90 ± 0.50 μL, 13.70 ± 1.32 μL, and 18.10 ± 1.51 μL at the corresponding time points. This superior sampling performance of APSMNs was consistently maintained in vivo (Fig. 4C), where they harvested 5.85 ± 0.42 μL, 10.58 ± 1.19 μL, and 11.85 ± 0.93 μL of ISF from the dorsal skin of mice at 1, 5, and 10 min, respectively. The theoretical pore volume of the 10 × 10 APSMN array was estimated to be approximately 2.99 μL. This value was lower than the experimentally measured ISF uptake (∼10.58 μL at 5 min), suggesting that absorbed fluid was not stored exclusively within the MN pores but was also transported into the interconnected porous backing layer. The plateau in ISF collection observed between 5 and 10 min is therefore more likely attributable to limited ISF replenishment from surrounding tissue rather than saturation of pore volume. Furthermore, in ex vivo porcine skin, the APSMNs collected 12.09 ± 2.23 μL after a 5 min of sampling time, whereas MeHA-HMNs collected only 6.28 ± 1.28 μL (Fig. 4D).Based on the Young-Laplace equation and the measured pore diameter (∼1 μm), the capillary pressure generated within the aligned microchannels was estimated to be approximately 144 kPa, substantially exceeding physiological interstitial fluid pressure [36]. This strong capillary driving force is consistent with the rapid fluid uptake observed experimentally. In addition, finite element simulations comparing isotropic and anisotropic porous architectures were performed (Fig. S5, Supporting Information). The anisotropic porous structure exhibited a markedly higher Darcy velocity and effective permeability along the transport direction. The calculated permeability reached 3.318 × 10−13 m2 for the anisotropic architecture, compared with 1.502 × 10−14 m2 for the isotropic counterpart, indicating reduced hydraulic resistance and more efficient fluid transport. These results further support that the vertically aligned channel architecture provides a low-resistance pathway for rapid ISF uptake, consistent with the experimental observations. Collectively, these results demonstrate that APSMNs exhibit markedly enhanced ISF sampling efficiency compared with conventional HMNs. This enhancement arises from their vertically aligned porous microchannels, which enable rapid, swelling-independent, and continuous fluid uptake directly from the dermal ISF reservoirs. After ISF sampling from mouse skin using APSMNs, clearly defined microholes were observed on the skin surface immediately following MN removal. These microholes gradually recovered and became indistinguishable within 60 min post-application (Fig. S6, Supporting Information). Only minimal erythema and edema were observed, indicating good skin tolerability of the APSMNs.

For MeHA-HMNs, fluorescence was restricted to the superficial regions of the needle tips at 5, 30, and 60 s. In the middle and bottom sections, FITC-Dex was detected only at the peripheral edges, with no penetration into the central regions of the needle core (Fig. 5B). These observations indicate that large biomolecules cannot diffuse into the swollen hydrogel network of MeHA-HMNs within short sampling durations, consistent with their swelling-dependent and diffusion-limited uptake mechanism. In contrast, APSMNs exhibited a continuous top-to-bottom filling pattern characteristic of capillary-driven transport through aligned porous channels. At 5 s, FITC-Dex had already infiltrated the entire top region of the MNs. By 30 s, fluorescence fully occupied the middle sections, and by 60 s, the bottom regions were completely filled. This result emphatically confirms that the APSMNs’ xylem-like, pre-formed microchannels effectively overcome the diffusion barriers imposed by hydrogel matrices, ensuring that large molecules are not merely trapped on the surface but are actively and rapidly transported deep into the internal reservoir. To further elucidate the distinct sampling behavior, we performed SEM analysis to compare the microstructural evolution of MeHA-HMNs and APSMNs after incubation in PBS and following insertion into porcine skin ex vivo (Fig. S7, Supporting Information). In PBS, MeHA-HMNs exhibited uniform swelling throughout the needle body, forming an interconnected hydrogel network that is conducive to molecular diffusion. However, after skin insertion, the swelling behavior of MeHA-HMNs was markedly constrained. Due to the mechanical confinement imposed by the dense and elastic skin tissue, the HMNs were unable to fully expand. As a result, only the peripheral regions of the needles underwent partial swelling, while the central core retained a relatively dense and solid structure (Fig. S7A–D, Supporting Information). This incomplete and heterogeneous swelling explains why large molecules, such as FITC-Dex (150 kDa), were predominantly localized at the needle edges and failed to penetrate into the interior of MeHA-HMNs. In contrast, APSMNs preserved their pre-formed, aligned porous architecture under both conditions (Fig. S7E–G, Supporting Information). To further evaluate whether fluid uptake was accompanied by bulk matrix swelling, APSMNs were characterized by SEM before and after fluid absorption (Fig. S8, Supporting Information). No appreciable changes in needle morphology or dimensions were observed, and the calculated needle volume remained nearly unchanged after fluid uptake (before: 0.051 ± 0.001 mm3; after: 0.052 ± 0.001 mm3). These findings further support that fluid transport in APSMNs primarily occurs through the pre-formed aligned microchannels rather than swelling-induced matrix expansion.

Efficient retrieval of the sampled analytes is paramount for subsequent quantitative analysis (Fig. 5C). Our standardized recovery protocol involved placing the MN patch into a centrifuge tube, adding 400 μL of PBS, and centrifuging at 10,000 rpm for 10 min, followed by supernatant (recovered ISF sample) collection. We found that this recovery process resulted in markedly different final volumes of recovered ISF samples available for subsequent analysis between APSMNs and MeHA-HMNs (Fig. S9, Supporting Information). The recovered sample volume obtained using APSMNs was 310.5 ± 6.9 μL, whereas that from MeHA-HMNs was 186.7 ± 2.1 μL. This difference arises because MeHA-HMNs continue to absorb PBS and undergo further swelling during the recovery step, thereby sequestering a substantial fraction of the added buffer and reducing the final recoverable supernatant volume. The larger recovered ISF volume obtained with APSMNs enables sufficient sample availability for multiple downstream analyses, including the quantification of diverse biomarkers using ELISA kits.

Next, we evaluated the recovery of glucose, a small-molecule biomarker, from hydrogels containing different concentrations (2, 4, 8, and 16 mM) (Fig. 5D). APSMNs consistently achieved high recovery efficiencies above 90% across all tested concentrations. To further assess performance across a range of molecular sizes, we tested fluorescein (332 Da), FITC-ovalbumin (FITC-OVA, 45 kDa), and FITC-Dex (150 kDa) (Fig. 5E). Remarkably, recovery rates remained exceptionally high for all analytes: 97.6 ± 2.8% for fluorescein, 96.6 ± 2.9% for FITC-OVA, and 97.5 ± 1.0% for FITC-Dex. These results demonstrate that APSMNs efficiently sample both low and high molecular weight biomarkers and enable high yield recovery from the patch, ensuring reliable downstream analysis. Furthermore, we quantified the total protein collected by a single APSMN patch over different sampling durations. A single patch was able to harvest 109.0 ± 16.8 μg, 181.9 ± 14.1 μg, and 182.7 ± 9.8 μg of protein from mouse skin after 1, 5, and 10 min of sampling, respectively (Fig. 5F). Of note, a blank APSMN patch yielded a negligible protein signal (∼6 μg), which was substantially lower than that of ISF-loaded samples, confirming minimal SF dissolution or peptide release and supporting the reliability of the subsequent proteomic analysis.

APSMN-enabled ISF sampling in psoriasis mice for targeted biomarker quantification and proteomic analysis

To further evaluate the clinical applicability of the APSMNs for inflammatory skin disease diagnostics, an imiquimod (IMQ)-induced murine psoriasis model was established (Fig. 6A). After six consecutive days of topical IMQ treatment (100 mg/day), mice developed typical psoriasiform skin lesions characterized by erythema, induration, and scaling, accompanied by a progressive increase in the Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) score (Fig. S10, Supporting Information). On day 7, a targeted panel of representative psoriasis-associated biomarkers, including interleukin-17F (IL-17F), S100A9, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), was quantitatively analyzed by ELISA. Biomarker levels were measured in matched serum, APSMN-collected ISF, and skin biopsy homogenates from psoriasis-like mice and healthy controls. The results showed that all five biomarkers were significantly elevated in the psoriasis group compared with healthy mice across all sample types (Fig. 6B). Notably, biomarker concentrations in APSMN-collected ISF closely matched those measured in skin biopsy homogenates and were consistently higher than corresponding serum levels. This phenomenon likely reflects the localized pathophysiology of psoriasis, in which key inflammatory cytokines such as IL-17F and S100A9 are predominantly produced and enriched at the dermal-epidermal interface, but become substantially diluted upon entry into the systemic circulation [37,38]. The preceding analysis quantified a predefined set of representative psoriasis-associated biomarkers. Such targeted measurements inherently capture only a limited subset of the complex molecular networks underlying psoriatic inflammation. To enable an unbiased, systematical assessment of disease-related molecular alterations, we therefore performed global proteomic profiling of APSMNs-collected ISF using a label-free quantification data-independent acquisition mass spectrometry approach. Following a 5-min sampling period, the total protein yield from psoriasis-like mouse skin (342.5 ± 33.8 μg) was significantly higher than that from healthy controls (181.9 ± 14.1 μg) (Fig. 6C). This likely reflects increased production and local accumulation of cytokines and other inflammatory mediators during disease progression, resulting in enrichment of the protein content with ISF. Proteomic analysis identified 5257 ± 470 proteins in APSMNs-collected ISF from healthy mice (Fig. 6D, Supportive data 1), with the number increased to 6345 ± 321 in psoriasis-like mice, approximately 1.32-fold increase compared with serum (4819 ± 141). As expected, skin biopsy samples yielded the highest proteome coverage, with 8585 ± 1015 and 9063 ± 645 proteins identified in healthy and psoriasis-like mice, respectively. Venn diagrams were constructed to visualize the overlap of high-confidence proteins (defined as proteins consistently detected in at least 3 of the 5 biological replicates) across APSMNs-collected ISF, serum, and skin biopsy samples (Fig. 6E). Approximately 2197 and 2538 proteins were shared among the three sample types in either healthy or psoriasis-like mice, respectively, whereas around 700 proteins were uniquely identified in APSMNs-collected ISF.

The psoriasis-like mice were robustly distinguished from the healthy controls by proteomic profiling of APSMNs-collected ISF or serum, whereas such separation was not observed using skin biopsy-derived proteomes (Fig. 6F). Notably, APSMNs-collected ISF exhibited the highest number of differentially-expressed proteins (1729 totally, including 1193 up-regulated and 536 down-regulated) in psoriasis compared with healthy controls, over 2-fold higher than that in serum (759 totally, including 413 up-regulated and 346 down-regulated) and skin biopsy (805 totally, including 469 up-regulated and 336 down-regulated), respectively (Figs. S11–13, Supporting Information). Among the up-regulated proteins, 1049 were uniquely derived from APSMNs-collected ISF, far surpassing the unique ones in serum (315) and skin biopsy (345) (Fig. 6G). Altogether, it indicates that APSMNs-collected ISF could more sensitively capture psoriasis-associated protein component alterations than serum or skin biopsy. It may be attributed to the fact that psoriasis primarily occurs at the epidermal–dermal interface where ISF were collected by the APSMNs, whereas full-thickness skin biopsies contain proteins from multiple skin layers and structural components and serum also contains a high proportion of inherent physiological proteins [39]. The inclusion of abundant disease-irrelevant proteins may dilute disease-specific molecular signals, and mask disease-related proteomic changes.

Among the up-regulated proteins identified from psoriasis-like mouse in APSMNs-collected ISF, 22 have been previously reported as psoriasis biomarkers. It includes the above ELISA-validated proteins (encoded by genes Il17f, S100a9, Tnfaip2, Tnfaip3 and Vegfa, highlighted in blue in Fig. 6H), as well as additional established biomarkers encoded by C4a, Csta, Fabp5, Flg, Gsdma, Icam1, Il12b, Il36a, Il36g, Krt16, Krt17, Krt6a, Krt6b, Nfkbiz, S100a14, Stfa1 and Tgm3 (highlighted in Fig. S12, Supporting Information, Supportive data 2) [40,41], further underscoring the biological relevance of APSMNs-derived ISF proteomes. All the up-regulated proteins were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis across biological process (BP), molecular function (MF) and cellular component (CC) categories. It revealed coordinated activation of proliferative, inflammatory, and metabolic programs characteristic of lesional skin. Specifically, BP terms were strongly enriched for keratinocyte proliferation and epidermal remodeling, including keratinocyte development, centrosome separation, and regulation of matrix metallopeptidase secretion, consistent with epidermal hyperplasia and extracellular matrix remodeling (Fig. S14, Supporting Information) [42]. MF enrichment highlighted enhanced translational and proteostatic capacity, evidenced by overrepresentation of ribosomal constituents, rRNA-binding activities, ATP-dependent chaperones, and epigenetic regulators, alongside growth factor- and immune-related functions (Fig. S15, Supporting Information) [43]. CC analysis further supported these findings, showing enrichment of ribosomal assemblies, DNA replication complexes, nuclear transcriptional machinery, and mitochondrial respiratory complexes (Fig. S16, Supporting Information), collectively reflecting accelerated cell-cycle progression, increased metabolic demand, and heightened cellular stress in psoriatic lesions.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed broad activation of immune, inflammatory, metabolic, and stress-response pathways implicated in psoriasis pathogenesis (Fig. 6I). It includes IL-17, TNF, NF-κB, MAPK, Toll-like receptor, NOD-like receptor signaling, antigen processing and presentation, and Th1/Th2 cell differentiation, underscoring sustained innate and adaptive immune activation characteristics [44]. Concurrent enrichment of apoptosis, necroptosis, phagosome, and lysosome pathways reflects dysregulated cell death and innate immune clearance. In parallel, extensive alterations in lipid metabolism and signaling, encompassing fatty acid elongation, unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis, sphingolipid and glycerophospholipid metabolism, as well as mTOR, HIF-1, VEGF, and CAMs pathways, highlight coordinated metabolic reprogramming, keratinocyte hyperproliferation, angiogenesis, and barrier dysfunction [45,46]. Notably, a panel of proteins were found participating in multiple of those pathways with particularly strong up-regulation in the psoriasis-like mice (log2FC > 4, P-value <0.005, FDR <0.05), including those encoded by Mvb12a, Tnfaip3, C1ra, Aldh18a1, Ube2g2, Nagpa, Atp6v0a4, Dab2ip, Ndufs6, Pla2g4d, Sec61a1, Flnb, Napsa and Cyth4 (highlighted in red in Fig. 6H, Supportive data 3). With the exception of Tnfaip3, none of them have previously been reported as psoriasis biomarkers, highlighting their potential as novel candidates for psoriasis detection and stratification. Nonetheless, their feasibility in human psoriasis and relevance in clinics require further validation.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a xylem-inspired APSMN patch that enables rapid, high-yield, and swelling-independent sampling of skin ISF. By integrating aligned porous microchannels with a mechanically robust SF matrix, the APSMNs enables efficient extraction and recovery of both small molecules and macromolecular biomarkers, yielding sufficient material for downstream multi-biomarker quantification and proteomic analysis. Scalable manufacturing is critical for the clinical translation and commercialization of MN patches. The APSMN patch shows strong potential for scale-up, as its fabrication involves vacuum-assisted mold filling followed by directional cryo-templating, ice-templated solvent-exchange crosslinking, and Hofmeister effect-induced salting-out treatment. These procedures rely primarily on solution-phase processing, without requiring complex material synthesis or specialized microfabrication, thereby facilitating adaptation to batch and large-area production. Furthermore, the use of SF as the structural matrix reinforces this potential owing to its well-established processability, biocompatibility, stability, and prior use in scalable biomedical manufacturing [47].

A practical ISF extraction approach is generally expected to collect at least 1 μL of ISF within 20 min [48]. Increasing the volume of collected ISF is particularly advantageous, as greater sample availability directly enables more comprehensive downstream analyses, ranging from multi-analyte quantification to omics profiling [49]. In addition, shortened sampling durations improve user comfort and clinical acceptability, which is especially important for repeated sampling or high temporal resolution monitoring. A single patch of APSMNs (10 × 10 array) collected more than 10 μL of ISF from mouse skin in vivo and over 12 μL from porcine skin ex vivo within 5 min (Fig. 4C and D). It should be noted that the Parafilm-covered agarose model represents only a simplified approximation of native skin and cannot fully reproduce its hydration and mechanical characteristics. Nevertheless, it provides a convenient and reproducible platform for comparative evaluation of fluid uptake behavior under controlled conditions. An overview of representative MN-based ISF sampling systems and their reported ISF uptake efficiencies is summarized (Table S1, Supporting Information). Compared with previously reported approaches, our APSMN patch exhibits promising ISF extraction performance through a single-step application (insertion-and-sampling), without the need for auxiliary devices or additional manipulation steps. However, it should be noted that these reported values are subject to variations in experimental conditions, such as animal species, skin sites, and device architectures, and therefore are intended for qualitative comparison only. Notably, this sampling efficiency could be further improved by integrating complementary strategies to address the rate-limiting step of ISF transport from the dermis to MN puncture sites, such as vacuum-assisted extraction [50], ultrasound stimulation [21], external pressure [49,51], micro-vibration [52], or laser pretreatment [53].

Efficient and quantitative recovery of the sampled ISF is essential for reliable downstream analysis. In this study, we employed a standardized centrifugation-based recovery strategy, in which the ISF-loaded APSMN patch was immersed in 400 μL of PBS and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min, followed by collection of the supernatant (Fig. 5C). The effect of centrifugation duration on recovery efficiency was further evaluated, showing no significant difference between 10 and 15 min, indicating that 10 min centrifugation is sufficient for efficient elution (Fig. S17, Supporting Information). Using this approach, we demonstrated consistently high recovery efficiencies (>95%) across biomarkers spanning a wide range of molecular weights, indicating that the method is broadly applicable and does not introduce molecular-size-dependent bias (Fig. 5D and E). Notably, this recovery strategy is compatible with both APSMNs and conventional HMNs [[54], [55], [56]]. However, due to the intrinsic swelling and water-absorption behavior of HMNs, a substantial fraction of the added PBS remains sequestered within the swollen matrix during centrifugation, resulting in a reduced final recoverable supernatant volume compared with APSMNs. As a result, although identical buffer volumes were used, APSMNs yielded a larger effective ISF sample volume (∼300 μL from 400 μL PBS), which is more favorable for subsequent quantitative and multiplexed analyses (Fig. S9, Supporting Information). Alternative ISF recovery strategies have also been reported for different MN-based ISF sampling system. For example, the ISF-loaded patch was immersed in DI water for up to 24 h to allow reconstitution of the collected analytes prior to analysis [16,57]. In another example, ISF was recovered by rinsing the ISF-collection plate with 100 μL of PBS followed by centrifugation, achieving approximately 80% volume recovery (∼80 μL from 100 μL PBS) [51]. Additionally, complete dissolution of MN patches at elevated temperature (60 °C for 15 min) has been used to release the captured analytes [58]. Overall, an effective ISF recovery strategy requires the use of a minimal volume of PBS or DI water to reconstitute the sampled ISF, in order to avoid excessive dilution that could reduce biomarker concentrations and compromise downstream analytical sensitivity. At the same time, the recovery medium must be sufficient to fully wet the MNs or collection patch to enable efficient release of the captured analytes. Notably, elevated temperatures should be avoided when targeting biomacromolecules, as many proteins and cytokines are temperature-sensitive and may undergo degradation or denaturation. Further studies are warranted to establish standardized ISF recovery workflows of our APSMNs, along with compatible tools and devices, to enable a fully standardized process spanning from APSMN ISF sampling to analyte recovery and analysis [51].

Previous studies have demonstrated that ISF collected using MNs can support the detection of a wide spectrum of analytes, ranging from small-molecule metabolites, such as glucose and caffeine [52,58,59], to large biomacromolecules, including antibodies and immunoglobulins [60,61]. Several reports have extended MN-enabled ISF sampling to proteomic analyses, offering a more comprehensive view of the systemic (serum) and localized molecular landscape [49,51,62]. Compared with in situ sensor-based approaches [63], MN-enabled ISF extraction followed by off-line analysis is uniquely suited for omics-level investigations, allowing unbiased, high-dimensional profiling that is not feasible with single-analyte or limited-panel sensing platforms. In this study, we employed a psoriasis-like mouse model as a proof of concept to evaluate the clinical potential of APSMN-enabled ISF sampling. APSMN-collected ISF supported quantitative ELISA detection of psoriasis-associated biomarkers with sensitivity comparable to skin biopsy and substantially exceeding that of serum, indicating that ISF more effectively captures localized inflammatory signals than serum (Fig. 6B). Proteomic profiling identified over 5200 protein groups from healthy skin ISF and more than 6300 protein groups from psoriasis skin ISF, markedly exceeding previously reported microneedle-based ISF proteomic studies and approaching the proteomic depth achieved by skin biopsy analysis (Fig. 6D and E; Table S2, Supporting Information) [51,62]. Notably, protein quantification exhibited high reproducibility across five biological replicates, with coefficient of variation (CV) distributions comparable to those obtained from serum and skin biopsy samples (Fig. S18, Supporting Information). All datasets were processed using an identical DIA-NN workflow with the match-between-runs (MBR) function enabled to improve data completeness and reduce missing values across samples (Table S3, Supporting Information). To ensure data robustness, only proteins detected in at least three of the five biological replicates were retained for subsequent analysis (Fig. S19; Table S4, Supporting Information). APSMN-collected ISF exhibited the largest number of significantly upregulated proteins in psoriasis-like mice relative to healthy controls, with 1193 protein groups identified. This number was approximately 2.89-fold and 2.54-fold higher than those detected in serum and skin biopsy samples, respectively (Fig. 6G). Among the 1193 upregulated proteins identified in APSMN-collected ISF, 22 have previously been reported as established psoriasis-associated biomarkers, including the ELISA-validated proteins encoded by Il17f, S100a9, Tnfaip2, Tnfaip3, and Vegfa (highlighted in blue in Fig. 6H), as well as 17 additional biomarkers (Fig. S9, Supporting Information). In contrast, only five of these biomarkers were identified as significantly upregulated in either serum or skin biopsy samples. These results demonstrate the superior capability of APSMN-collected ISF to capture disease-associated proteomic alterations within the local skin microenvironment. This enhanced sensitivity likely reflects the proximity of ISF to the dermal–epidermal microenvironment where pathogenic signaling is concentrated, whereas tissue homogenates introduce abundant skin structure-related background proteins that dilute the lesion-specific molecular signals [39]. To be noted, the APSMN-collected ISF proteome also facilitated the discovery of a panel of previously unreported candidate biomarker, underscoring its utility for unbiased psoriasis biomarker discovery. To the best of our knowledge, prior studies have mainly focused on comparative ISF, serum and skin biopsy proteomics in healthy subjects, with limited investigation in disease context [17,51,64,65]. By demonstrating superior detection of disease-associated proteomic alterations in a psoriasis model, our findings establish MN-collected ISF as a powerful, minimally invasive platform for molecular profiling and biomarker discovery in cutaneous pathology.

While the APSMN platform represents a significant advancement in ISF sampling technology, several aspects require further investigation to enable full clinical translation. First, the current study focused exclusively on an IMQ-induced murine model of psoriasis, which, although widely accepted, does not fully recapitulate the complexity of human disease. Future work should therefore evaluate the feasibility of this platform in human skin and extend its application to a broader spectrum of chronic inflammatory skin disorders, such as atopic dermatitis and systemic lupus erythematosus-associated cutaneous manifestations, to validate its utility across diverse pathological contexts. Second, although proteomic profiling was demonstrated in this study, the compatibility of the APSMN platform with other omics modalities, including transcriptomics and metabolomics, warrants further investigation. In addition, while APSMN patches can be conveniently stored in a dry state at ambient temperature prior to use, their long-term storage stability under different environmental conditions, such as temperature and humidity, remains to be systematically evaluated. Finally, for successful clinical adoption, the development of a user-friendly applicator will be essential to ensure consistent and controlled insertion force and depth, thereby guaranteeing reproducible sampling efficiency across different users and clinical settings. Collectively, these advances may ultimately position APSMNs as a versatile tool for precision dermatology and the personalized management of chronic inflammatory skin diseases.

Materials and methods

Materials

Silkworm cocoon was purchased from Sericulture Base in Northwestern China. Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) was purchased from Dow Corning (USA). Hyaluronic acid (HA, Mw = 300 kDa) was purchased from Bloomage Biotechnology (China). Methacrylic anhydride (MA, 94%), the photo-initiator Irgacure 1173, and ovalbumin (OVA, from chicken egg white) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF), ethanol, sodium carbonate (Na2CO3), trisodium citrate dihydrate (Na3Ct•2H2O, 99%), and sodium chloride (NaCl) were purchased from Titian Scientific (China). Fluorescein sodium, glutaraldehyde, dextran (Dex), and agarose were purchased from Macklin (China). Lithium bromide (LiBr) was obtained from Adamas-beta (China). Optical cutting temperature (OCT) compounds were purchased from Sakura Finetek (Japan). 4% paraformaldehyde solution was purchased from Biosharp (China).

Animals

Female BALB/c mice (8-12 weeks old) were obtained from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Research Ethics Committee of the Hangzhou Institute of Medicine, Chinese Academy of Sciences (approval no. AP2024-07-0013), and conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines. Mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions at 20-25 °C with 30-70% relative humidity and a 12 h light/dark cycle, with ad libitum access to food and water.

Preparation of regenerated silk fibroin (SF)

Regenerated SF was prepared from Bombyx mori silkworm cocoons following an established protocol [66]. Briefly, the cocoons were cut into small pieces and degummed by boiling in 0.02 M Na2CO3 for 30 min. The resulting fibers were thoroughly rinsed with DI water and dried overnight. Dried silk fibers were then dissolved in 9.3 M LiBr at 60 °C for 4 h to obtain a homogeneous solution. The solution was dialyzed against DI water using a dialysis membrane (MWCO 14 kDa) for 3 days, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 20 min (twice) to remove insoluble residues. The purified SF solution was stored at 4 °C, and its concentration was determined gravimetrically by drying a known volume.

Fabrication of anisotropic porous silk microneedles (APSMNs) and methacrylated hyaluronic acid hydrogel microneedles (MeHA-HMNs)

A negative PDMS mold was fabricated by replicating a metal MN master template consisting of a 10 × 10 array. Each conical MN had a height of 1200 μm, a base diameter of 450 μm, and a center-to-center spacing of 800 μm. The PDMS prepolymer (base to curing agent, 10:1 w/w) was poured onto the master template, degassed under vacuum for 30 min to remove air bubbles, and thermally cured at 60 °C for 2 h. After curing, the PDMS mold was carefully peeled off from the master template. Prior to use, the PDMS molds were thoroughly rinsed with DI water and dried in the oven.

To fabricate APSMNs, SF solutions at the desired concentrations were cast into the PDMS molds and vacuum-treated for 10 min to ensure complete filling of the needle cavities. Directional cryo-templating was achieved by placing the backing of each mold onto an aluminum foil-wrapped metal block pre-cooled to −80 °C, while the top surface was maintained at room temperature, thereby establishing a unidirectional temperature gradient. The samples were frozen until complete solidification of the SF solution. After demolding, the MNs were immersed in 0.1% (v/v) glutaraldehyde in absolute ethanol at −20 °C for 12 h. The MNs were then thoroughly rinsed with DI water and salted-out by immersing in 1.5 M sodium citrate solution for 6 h. Finally, the MNs were rinsed with DI water to remove residual salts and lyophilized.

To fabricate MeHA-HMNs, MeHA was synthesized according to our previously reported protocol [67]. MeHA (300 kDa, 50 mg/mL) and Irgacure 1173 (5 μL/mL) were dissolved in DI water. The precursor solution was cast into oxygen plasma-treated PDMS molds and centrifuged to ensure complete filling of the MN cavities. After drying overnight, the MeHA-HMNs were carefully demolded, trimmed, and crosslinked under UV irradiation for 15 s. All fabricated MNs were stored in a desiccator until use.

Structural and conformational analysis of SF during APSMN fabrication

The chemical composition and secondary structure evolution of SF during APSMN fabrication were characterized by Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Nicolet iS50, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) over a wavenumber range of 400-4000 cm−1. The amide I region was curve-fitted to quantify secondary structure content. Based on established assignments, absorption bands were assigned to β-sheet (1615-1640 and 1697-1703 cm−1), random coil (1640-1655 cm−1), α-helix (1655-1665 cm−1), and β-turn (1665-1690 cm−1). Normalized spectra were deconvoluted using the Peak Analyzer tool in PeakFit software, and the integral area of each fitted peak was used to calculate the proportion of SF in each secondary structure.

Morphology of the APSMNs

The overall morphology of APSMNs was examined using a stereomicroscope (SZX16, Olympus, Japan). To visualize the internal porous architecture, individual MNs were carefully sectioned along longitudinal and transverse planes, sputter-coated with a thin gold layer (JEC-3000FC, JEOL, Japan) to enhance conductivity, and imaged by field-emission scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JEOL, Japan).

Mechanical testing and skin insertion ability of APSMNs

The mechanical properties of MNs were evaluated using an Instron tensile tester (68SC-05, USA). MN arrays (10 × 10, tips upward) were secured on a metal block, and a sensor was lowered at 0.5 mm/min. Displacement and force were recorded from initial contact until a 0.6 mm compression. Post-test MN morphology was imaged by stereomicroscopy (SZX16, Olympus, Japan). The compressive strength (F, MPa) was calculated as:F=PAwhere P is the maximum load (N), and A is the MN cross-sectional area of the MN resisting the load (mm2).

To evaluate the skin insertion capability of APSMNs, fresh porcine skin was used. APSMNs were applied by finger pressure, and the resulting microholes were observed under a stereomicroscope (SZX16, Olympus, Japan). For histological analysis, a portion of the skin was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, cryosectioned using a cryostat microtome (NX70, Thermo Scientific), and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). The sections were imaged with an inverted fluorescence microscope (CKX53, Olympus) to determine MN penetration depth.

Water absorption ability of APSMNs

The water absorption capability of APSMNs and MeHA-HMNs was evaluated via contact angle measurements using the sessile drop method (OCA15EC, DataPhysics Instruments, Germany). Briefly, 3 μL droplets of DI water were deposited onto the MN surfaces, and images were captured at multiple time points to monitor dynamic water absorption.

The capillary pressure generated within the aligned porous microchannels of APSMNs was estimated using the Young-Laplace equation:P=2γcosθ/rwhere P is the capillary pressure (Pa), γ is the surface tension of water, assuming ISF possesses water-like surface tension (0.072 N/m at room temperature), θ is the water contact angle of APSMNs measured by contact angle analysis (∼60°), and r is the effective pore radius determined from SEM characterization.

To quantitatively investigate the fluid transport characteristics of APSMNs, pore-scale 2D models representing both isotropic and anisotropic porous architectures were constructed. Simulations were performed using the Creeping Flow (Stokes Flow) interface in COMSOL Multiphysics, appropriate for low-Reynolds-number flow (Re ≪ 1) at the microscale. The fluid was modeled as incompressible water at room temperature with a dynamic viscosity of μ = 1.0 × 10−3 Pa s. Under the assumption of negligible inertial effects, the flow is governed by the Stokes equation:‐∇p+μ∇2u=0where u is the velocity field (m/s), p is the pressure (Pa), and μ is the dynamic viscosity of the fluid (Pa s). Boundary conditions were applied with an inlet pressure of 266 Pa (∼2 mmHg), an outlet pressure of 0 Pa, and no-slip conditions on all pore walls.

The corresponding pressure gradient was approximated as:∇p=p0Hwhere H is the length of the porous region along the flow direction.

ISF sampling ability of APSMNs

The ISF sampling ability of APSMNs was evaluated by first recording the dry mass of each MN patch. The MNs were then inserted into a skin-mimicking model consisting of a Parafilm membrane, representing the stratum corneum/epidermis, overlying 1.4% (w/v) agarose hydrogel simulating the dermis. Mass changes of the MNs were measured at different sampling durations to quantify fluid uptake. The same procedure was applied to dorsal skin of BALB/c mice in vivo and fresh porcine skin ex vivo. For in vivo experiments, mice (8-12 weeks old) were dehaired under anesthesia using a razor and hair removal cream on Day 0, followed by 24 h of normal feeding to allow recovery of the stratum corneum.

The theoretical fluid storage capacity of APSMNs was estimated based on the geometric dimensions and porosity of the MNs. Individual MNs were approximated as conical structures, and the total needle volume was calculated using:Vtheory=N×Vsingle×εwhere Vtheory is the theoretical fluid storage capacity of the APSMN array, N is the total number of MNs, Vsingle is the geometric volume of an individual MN, and ε is the porosity of the porous structure, which was estimated from SEM image analysis to be approximately 64.9%.

Biomarker sampling and recovery performance of the APSMNs

To evaluate the biomarker diffusion within the MNs during ISF sampling, the model fluorescent biomarker, FITC-Dex (150 kDa), was loaded into the agarose hydrogel-based skin-mimicking mode. APSMNs or MeHA-HMNs were inserted into the hydrogel through the Parafilm layer. At different sampling durations, MN cross-sections at various heights were imaged by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM, Nikon A1 RMP) to visualize FITC-Dex distribution.

To quantify the recovery efficiency of biomarkers from APSMNs, the agarose hydrogel in the skin-mimicking model was preloaded with model biomarkers of different molecular weights: fluorescein sodium (332 Da, 1 mg/mL), FITC-OVA (45 kDa, 1 mg/mL), and FITC-Dex (150 kDa, 1 mg/mL). The dry mass of each MN (W0) was recorded prior to application. After 5 min of insertion, the MNs were removed, weighed again (Wt), and immediately transferred into 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes containing 400 μL PBS. The tubes were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected for biomarker analysis. The recovered biomarker concentration (Cᵣ) was calculated as:Cr=Cd×V(Wt−W0)/ρwhere Cd is the measured biomarker concentration in the eluate, V is the volume of PBS, W0 and Wt are the MN weights before and after fluid absorption, respectively, and ρ is the density of ISF (assumed to be 1 g/mL). Recovery efficiency was expressed as:Recoveryefficiency(%)=CᵣC×100where C is the actual biomarker concentration in the agarose hydrogel.

To quantify total proteins collected by APSMNs, the patches were applied to the dorsal skin of mice for varying ISF sampling durations. After sampling, the APSMNs were transferred into 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes containing 400 μL of PBS to recover the ISF. Total protein concentration was measured using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. The total protein amount collected at each sampling duration was calculated as:mp=Cd×Vwhere Cd is the protein concentration in the recovered ISF and V is the volume of PBS used for recovery.

ISF sampling and disease-related biomarker detection in the psoriasis-like mice

Female BALB/c mice (8-12 weeks old) were dehaired under anesthesia using a razor and hair removal cream on Day 0, followed by 24 h of recovery to restore the stratum corneum. A psoriasis-like model was induced by daily topical application of ∼100 mg imiquimod (IMQ) cream to the shaved dorsal skin for six consecutive days, while age-matched mice without IMQ treatment served as healthy controls. On Day 7, serum, skin tissue and ISF samples were collected for biomarker analysis. APSMN patches were applied to the dorsal lesional skin of psoriasis-like mice or corresponding regions of controls for ISF extraction. The collected ISF volume was calculated from the mass change of the APSMN patches before and after application. Disease-related biomarkers, including S100A9, VEGF, TNF-α, and IL-17F, were quantified using ELISA kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions. The effective concentration of biomarkers in ISF (Cm) was calculated as:Cm=Cd×V(Wt−W0)/ρwhere Cd is the measured biomarker concentration in the diluted ISF, V is the volume of PBS used for elution, W0 and Wt are the weights of the APSMN patch before and after ISF sampling, respectively, and ρ is the ISF density, assumed to be 1 g/mL.

Following ISF collection, a dorsal skin tissue sample from the same site was excised using a biopsy punch of identical size for subsequent tissue biopsy analysis. Each biopsy specimen was evenly divided into two parts. One part was subjected to gravimetric analysis by measuring tissue weight before and after freeze-drying, and the mass difference was used to determine tissue water content. The other part was homogenized in a defined volume of PBS, followed by centrifugation to obtain the supernatant for ELISA-based biomarker quantification. Based on the measured dilution factor and the estimated endogenous fluid content, the effective biomarker concentration in skin biopsy samples (Cr) was calculated asCr=Cd×V(W2−W1)/ρwhere Cd is the measured biomarker concentration in the diluted skin homogenate, V is the PBS volume used for homogenization, W1 and W2 are the sample weights before and after freezing dry, respectively, and ρ is the density of ISF, assumed to be 1 g/mL.

For serum analysis, approximately 200 μL blood was collected from mice by orbital bleeding. The blood samples were centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C to obtain serum. Biomarker concentrations in serum were quantified using the same ELISA procedures described above.

Proteomic analysis of ISF collected from psoriasis-like mice

ISF obtained using APSMNs, together with serum and skin tissues collected immediately after euthanasia, were stored at −80 °C until analysis. Skin tissues were lysed in SDS buffer and mechanically homogenized at 50 Hz for 300 s with 60 s intervals for three cycles. APSMN-collected ISF was incubated in lysis buffer with shaking to release proteins, while serum samples were heat-denatured at 95 °C for 20 min before lysis. Insoluble debris was removed by centrifugation (14,800 ×g, 15 min, 4 °C), and supernatants were reduced with DTT (37 °C, 30 min) and alkylated with iodoacetamide in the dark (room temperature, 45 min). Proteins were precipitated with five volumes of pre-chilled acetone at −25 °C for 2 h, pelleted, air-dried, and resuspended in SDS-free buffer. Protein concentrations were measured by Bradford assay, and 40 μg protein was digested with trypsin (1:40, w/w) in ammonium bicarbonate at 37 °C for 12-14 h. Digestion was stopped with TFA (pH 1.5-2.0), followed by C18 desalting, vacuum drying, and reconstitution. Peptides were analyzed using a Vanquish Neo UHPLC coupled to an Orbitrap Astral mass spectrometer in DIA mode. Separation was performed on a 75 μm × 150 mm column using a 24 min gradient from 6% to 45% acetonitrile at 500 nL/min. The instrument was operated with FAIMS (−42 V); MS1 spectra were acquired in the Orbitrap at 240,000 resolution (m/z 380-980), and MS/MS spectra in the Astral analyzer (m/z 150-2000) using 2 m/z isolation windows. Raw data were processed using DIA-NN (v1.8.1) with the match-between-runs (MBR) function enabled for each sampling strategy. Spectral searches were performed against the UniProt Mus musculus proteome database (Proteome ID: UP000000589), allowing up to one missed cleavage, with the false discovery rate (FDR) controlled at 1% at the peptide and protein levels.

Statistical analysis

All the results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 10. The difference between groups was compared by two-tailed Student's t-test and comparisons among groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001, ns = no significant difference). Both GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were statistically significant after false discovery rate correction (FDR or P. adjust <0.05).
