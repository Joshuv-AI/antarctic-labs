Outcomes of Dual Kidney Transplantation from Donors with High Pre-Implantation Biopsy Scores
Authors: Giorgia Comai, Michele Provenzano, Elisa Gessaroli, Valeria Grandinetti, Daniele Vetrano, Angelodaniele Napoletano, Chiara Abenavoli, Claudia Bini, Vania Cuna, Marcello Demetri, Francesca Chiappo, Francesca Leone, Benedetta Fabbrizio, Massimo Del Gaudio, Lorenzo Maroni, Matteo Ravaioli, Gaetano La Manna, Valeria Corradetti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe persistent shortage of transplantable kidneys has increased reliance on marginal organs, yet kidneys with advanced chronic lesions on pre-implantation biopsy are frequently discarded. Dual kidney transplantation (DKT) may mitigate this risk by increasing nephron mass, but outcomes of allografts with very high histological scores remain poorly defined. We evaluated kidney transplant outcomes according to transplant type and pre-implantation Karpinski score, with particular focus on DKT using kidneys with combined Karpinski score ≥9.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of 262 adult deceased-donor kidney transplant recipients undergoing pre-implantation biopsy at a single Italian center between 2014 and 2021. Recipients were stratified into 3 allocation groups: single kidney transplantation with Karpinski score <9 (SKT <9), DKT with combined Karpinski score <9 (DKT <9), and DKT with combined Karpinski score ≥9 (DKT ≥9). Primary outcomes were post-transplant eGFR and proteinuria during follow-up. Secondary outcomes included delayed graft function (DGF), acute rejection, complications, overall graft failure, all-cause mortality, and death-censored graft failure.ResultsAmong 262 recipients, 142 received SKT <9, 35 received DKT <9, and 85 received DKT ≥9. DKT donors were older and had higher KDPI/KDRI than SKT donors. Despite greater histological chronicity, recipients of DKT ≥9 grafts did not show inferior renal function at longer-term follow-up, with comparable eGFR and proteinuria at 12 and 36 months. Primary non-function, DGF, biopsy-proven acute rejection, and major complications were similar across groups, although post-transplant thrombotic microangiopathy was more frequent in DKT recipients. Kaplan-Meier analyses showed no significant differences in overall graft failure, all-cause mortality, or death-censored graft failure. In multivariable Cox regression, allocation group was not independently associated with overall graft failure.ConclusionIn this real-world allocation cohort, kidneys with combined Karpinski score ≥9 achieved outcomes comparable to lower-score grafts when allocated as DKT. These findings support the use of DKT as a strategy to safely expand the donor pool and suggest that high histological chronicity should not automatically preclude transplantation when integrated with clinical and functional donor assessment.

Introduction

Kidney transplantation is the treatment of choice for patients with end-stage kidney disease as it markedly improves quality of life and reduces mortality by up to 48% compared to dialysis [1–3]. However, the growing disparity between organ demand and supply represents a significant medical challenge. In 2021, more than 90,000 patients were on the kidney transplant waiting list in the USA, but less than 25% received an organ, a trend reflected across European systems [4]. According to recent data from the Italian National Transplant Center (Centro Nazionale Trapianti, CNT), 2,133 kidney transplants were performed in 2023, marking a 5% increase compared to 2022. Despite this advancement, approximately 6,500 patients remained on the waiting list for a kidney transplant at the end of 2023 [5]. To address organ shortage, criteria for donation have been expanded, and the clinical characteristics determining suitability for transplantation have been widened. Expanded Criteria Donors (ECDs) refer to deceased organ donors who do not meet the standard criteria for donation, but whose organs may still be suitable for transplantation. In the setting of kidney transplant, ECDs include donors aged >60 years, or those with a history of hypertension, moderate renal dysfunction, or other comorbidities [6]. While the growing necessity of marginal donors has prompted discussions on the safety and efficacy of such transplants, accumulating evidence indicates that, with appropriate donor selection and standardized protocols, kidney transplants from ECDs can achieve long-term outcomes comparable to those from younger, standard-criteria donors, despite a higher risk of post-transplant complications. Their use therefore broadens the available donor pool and allows transplantation for a greater number of patients on the waiting list [7–15]. Pre-implantation kidney biopsy is a commonly adopted approach for histological assessment of graft quality, supporting organ allocation decisions. Nonetheless, its clinical utility remains controversial, particularly given evidence linking its use to increased rates of organ discard [16, 17]. Different histologic scoring systems are used to assess chronic lesions in the kidney allograft, evaluating changes across key compartments such as glomerulosclerosis, tubular atrophy, interstitial fibrosis, and vascular damage. The Karpinski score is commonly used to guide organ allocation; it assigns a grade from 0 (no lesions) to 3 (severe lesions) to each renal compartment [18]; therefore, the total score ranges from 0 to 12 per kidney. DKT is a promising strategy for maximizing the use of marginal organs. This approach, originally developed for donors with high Kidney Donor Profile Index (KDPI), allows the residual nephron mass of 2 kidneys to be combined, ensuring adequate renal function for the recipient even when histology shows severe chronicity [19]. The adoption of this technique in European and American centers has significantly reduced the number of discarded organs while simultaneously improving clinical outcomes [13, 20, 21]. Previous studies have shown acceptable outcomes with higher histological score kidneys, but these comparisons typically involved single kidney transplants (SKTs) for scores ≤3 and dual kidney transplantation (DKT) for scores 4–5 [22]. This study aims to evaluate whether DKT with kidneys from donors with high total Karpinski scores (≥9) can provide graft function and survival comparable to transplantation from donors with lower Karpinski scores, both SKT and DKT. If confirmed, these findings could justify a reassessment of allocation criteria, thereby reducing organ discard, expanding the donor pool, and ultimately shortening waiting times for patients.

Methods

Study Design and Population

This retrospective cohort study included adult kidney transplant recipients from deceased donors undergoing mandatory pre-implantation biopsy at the IRCCS Policlinico di Sant’Orsola in Bologna (Italy), between January 1, 2014 and December 31, 2021. Recipients were stratified into three allocation groups according to transplant type and pre-implantation biopsy chronicity score: (1) SKT with Karpinski score <9 (SKT <9), (2) DKT with combined Karpinski score <9 (DKT <9), and (3) DKTn with combined Karpinski score ≥9 (DKT ≥9). For SKT, the Karpinski score referred to the single transplanted kidney, whereas for DKT it was calculated as the sum of the scores of the two transplanted kidneys. Recipients younger than 18 years were excluded. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the local Ethics Committee (protocol 586/2023/Oss/AOUBo).

Donor, Recipient, and Transplant Variables

Donor characteristics included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), medical history (hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease), cause of death, serum creatinine, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR, CKD-EPI), and total Karpinski biopsy score [23]. For donor risk profiling, the KDPI and the Kidney Donor Risk Index (KDRI) were collected. Recipient variables comprised demographics, comorbidities, underlying kidney disease, dialysis duration, and immunological profile (panel-reactive antibodies, HLA mismatches). Transplant-related variables included type of transplant (single versus dual), cold ischemia time (CIT), induction regimen, and perioperative complications.

Pre-Implantation Biopsy, Allocation Strategy, and Immunosuppression

Organ allocation was performed according to the Italian National Kidney Allocation Program, which prioritizes clinical urgency, HLA matching, and donor-recipient compatibility [24]. Pre-implantation kidney biopsy is routinely performed in donors >65 years or in younger donors with hypertension, cardiovascular disease, or diabetes, according to national protocols. Samples were processed and stained with periodic acid-Schiff, hematoxylin and eosin, and Masson trichrome and were evaluated by experienced renal pathologists. Chronic histological lesions were graded according to the Karpinski score. In accordance with national guidelines, kidneys with a Karpinski score ≤4 were allocated to SKT, whereas kidneys with a score >4 were allocated to DKT. Deviations from this algorithm were allowed at the discretion of transplant clinicians, based on the overall clinical and histological assessment. Immunosuppressive protocols included induction therapy with either basiliximab or antithymocyte globulin and maintenance therapy with a calcineurin inhibitor, mycophenolate mofetil, and corticosteroids.

Follow-Up and Outcomes

Recipients were followed at hospital discharge and at 3, 12, and 36 months post-transplant. Primary outcomes were post-transplant graft function, assessed by estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR, calculated using the CKD-EPI formula), and 24-h urinary protein excretion, during follow-up. Secondary outcomes included delayed graft function (DGF), primary non-function, biopsy-proven acute rejection, and post-transplant complications, including thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), infectious, surgical, vascular, and urological complications. Importantly, DGF was defined as the requirement for more than one dialysis session within the first postoperative week, excluding a single dialysis treatment performed for isolated perioperative indications such as hyperkalemia, volume overload, or metabolic stabilization.

Survival outcomes included death-censored graft failure, all-cause mortality, and overall graft failure. Death-censored graft failure was defined as irreversible graft loss requiring return to chronic dialysis or retransplantation, with deaths occurring with a functioning graft censored at the time of death. All-cause mortality included death from any cause. Overall graft failure was defined as the composite of death-censored graft failure or death from any cause.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), according to their distribution. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. Comparisons across the 3 allocation groups, SKT with Karpinski score <9, DKT with Karpinski score <9, and DKT with Karpinski score ≥9 were performed using one-way analysis of variance or Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables, as appropriate, and chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Graft outcomes were assessed using unadjusted between-group comparisons and adjusted p values obtained using analysis of covariance models with allocation group entered as a three-level factor. For follow-up eGFR and proteinuria outcomes, models were adjusted for the corresponding discharge value. Because urinary protein excretion showed a skewed distribution, proteinuria variables were log1p-transformed before modeling and back-transformed for descriptive reporting. Kaplan-Meier curves were used to estimate overall graft failure, all-cause mortality, and death-censored graft survival, with between-group differences assessed using log-rank tests. Overall graft failure was defined as the composite of death-censored graft failure or all-cause mortality. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression was used to identify independent predictors of overall graft failure. Covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance, while the number of variables included in the model was limited according to the number of observed events to reduce the risk of overfitting. The final model included discharge eGFR, donor eGFR, donor hypertension, and allocation group. Results were reported as hazard ratios with 95% confidence intervals.

All tests were two sided, and a p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using STATA SE version 14.

Results

Among the 262 transplant recipients included in the study, 142 received an SKT with a Karpinski score <9, 35 received a DKT with a combined Karpinski score <9, and 85 received a DKT with a combined Karpinski score ≥9. Accordingly, recipients were stratified into 3 allocation groups: SKT <9, DKT <9, and DKT ≥9. This classification reflects the real-world allocation strategy used in our center, in which kidneys with higher histological chronicity were preferentially allocated as DKT, while lower-score grafts included both single and dual kidney transplantation according to the overall clinical and histological assessment.

Donor and Recipients’ Characteristics

Donor and recipient characteristics stratified by allocation group are reported in Tables 1 and 2. Donor profiles differed across groups, with DKT donors being older than SKT <9 donors, irrespective of Karpinski category (65.0 ± 10.9, 73.3 ± 8.1, and 73.5 ± 8.2 years in SKT <9, DKT <9, and DKT ≥9, respectively; p < 0.001). DCD donation was less frequent in the DKT ≥9 group (5.9%; p = 0.003), whereas donor hypertension was more common (80.0%; p = 0.004). Donor BMI was modestly higher in DKT ≥9, while cardiopathy, diabetes, smoking status, and cause of death did not differ significantly. Donor eGFR was highest in the DKT ≥9 group and lowest in DKT <9 (93 ± 28, 65 ± 29, and 77 ± 36 mL/min in DKT ≥9, DKT <9, and SKT <9, respectively; p < 0.001). As expected, KDPI and KDRI were higher in both DKT groups, and total Karpinski score increased progressively from SKT <9 to DKT <9 and DKT ≥9 (3 [2, 3], 8 [7, 8], and 10 [9–11], respectively; p < 0.001).

Recipient characteristics were largely comparable across groups, although DKT recipients were older than SKT <9 recipients, had a shorter dialysis vintage, higher ETPS scores. Sex, major comorbidities, and underlying nephropathy distribution were broadly similar. Retransplantation was mainly represented in the SKT <9 group and absent in DKT ≥9 (13.4%, 2.9%, and 0.0%; p < 0.001), while PRA max was low and comparable across groups.

Transplant Outcomes

Early post-transplant outcomes are summarized in Table 3. Hospital stay was similar across groups (22 ± 12, 22 ± 13, and 24 ± 15 days in SKT <9, DKT <9, and DKT ≥9, respectively; p = 0.589). Induction therapy differed modestly across groups, with a lower use of basiliximab alone and a higher use of combined basiliximab plus thymoglobulin in DKT ≥9 recipients (p = 0.044). HLA mismatches and cold ischemia time also differed statistically across groups, although absolute differences were limited. Primary non-function, DGF, baseline rejection at time-zero biopsy, and biopsy-proven acute rejection were comparable among the 3 allocation groups. Post-transplant TMA was more frequent in the DKT groups, particularly DKT ≥9 (6.3%, 14.3%, and 16.5%; p = 0.044), whereas acute tubular necrosis, NODAT, surgical, vascular, and infectious complications did not differ significantly.

Renal function and urinary protein excretion outcomes are summarized in Table 4 and shown in Figure 1. Early differences in eGFR were observed across allocation groups at discharge and during the early post-transplant period, reflecting higher initial eGFR values in DKT recipients. However, these differences were no longer evident during longer-term follow-up, with clinically comparable eGFR values across groups at 12 and 36 months. Importantly, recipients of DKT ≥9 grafts did not show inferior renal function compared with the other allocation groups. Urinary protein excretion was also comparable across groups both at discharge and at 12 months, with no significant differences in either unadjusted or adjusted analyses.

Discussion

The present study shows that kidneys with severe chronic histologic lesions, defined by a combined Karpinski score ≥9, can achieve acceptable mid-term outcomes when allocated as DKT. By classifying recipients into 3 allocation groups – SKT <9, DKT <9, and DKT ≥9 – we found that high-score DKT grafts were not associated with an excess risk of overall graft failure, all-cause mortality, or death-censored graft failure compared with lower-score allocation groups. These findings support the concept that severe histologic chronicity should not automatically lead to organ discard, particularly when interpreted together with donor functional parameters and when nephron mass is increased through dual allocation. In general, our results align with prior studies that have explored the feasibility of using kidneys from marginal donors. ECD organs are often associated with older age and comorbidities, such as hypertension or diabetes, which historically limited their use due to concerns related to poor graft outcomes [25]. Nevertheless, our data support the concept that the increased nephron mass provided by DKT can compensate for chronic histologic alterations, ensuring adequate post-transplant renal function [26]. In 1999, a study by Remuzzi et al. [9] evaluated the outcomes of DKT with kidneys from marginal donors. This prospective, case-control study compared 24 recipients of dual marginal kidneys from donors over 60 years old or with conditions like diabetes, hypertension, or non-nephrotic proteinuria, to 48 matched controls who received single ideal grafts and found that DKT to be as safe and effective as SKT without increasing the risk of delayed renal function recovery, acute allograft rejection, or major surgical complications [9]. Despite this, the routine use of DKT remains limited. Recent US data show a declining use of DKT and high discard rates (>60%) for kidneys meeting double-allocation criteria, suggesting that further refinement of biopsy interpretation and allocation algorithms is needed to fully exploit this strategy [21]. More recently, Fedorova et al. [27] reported that, in selected recipients, DKT was not associated with worse perioperative outcomes compared with SKT and was associated with lower 5-year rates of acute rejection, death-censored graft failure, and overall graft failure. Differently from that study, our analysis focused specifically on biopsy-guided allocation and on DKT using kidneys with very high combined Karpinski scores (≥9), thereby addressing the additional question of whether severe chronic histologic lesions should preclude transplantation when both kidneys are allocated to a single recipient. Despite the more advanced chronic histologic damage in the DKT ≥9 group, renal function and survival outcomes were comparable to those observed in both SKT <9 and DKT <9 recipients. This three-group analysis strengthens the interpretation of our findings by separating the effect of transplant type from the effect of histological chronicity within the real-world allocation strategy used at our center, showing that DKT with such organs can maintain clinically acceptable renal function and low proteinuria throughout follow-up, without significant differences in long-term outcomes at 36 months. These data are consistent with those of an Italian research group who reported that, in carefully selected donors and recipients, grafts with a Karpinski/Remuzzi biopsy score of 6 or 7 represent a viable option for SKT. Although their analysis only focused on SKT, a common implication emerges: the feasibility of such transplants requires meticulous evaluation of histopathological, functional, and clinical parameters, together with accurate donor-recipient matching based on factors such as age, sex, BMI, and immunological compatibility, which are essential to challenge the traditional contraindication associated with elevated scores [28]. In our multivariable Cox analysis, lower discharge eGFR and donor hypertension were independently associated with overall graft failure, whereas allocation group and donor eGFR were not. The higher donor eGFR observed in the DKT ≥9 group underlines the need to interpret histological chronicity together with functional donor parameters. In this setting, preserved donor function may partially offset the adverse implications of chronic histologic lesions, supporting an integrated approach to pre-transplant graft assessment. Moreover, these findings suggest donor vascular comorbidity may have greater prognostic relevance than histologic allocation category itself [21]. The occurrence of DGF, primary non-function, acute rejection, and most surgical or infectious complications was similar across groups, reinforcing the safety of using kidneys with higher histological chronicity when allocation decisions are guided by pre-implantation biopsy and clinical criteria. However, post-transplant TMA was more frequent among DKT recipients, particularly in the DKT ≥9 group. Given the limited sample size and observational design, this finding should be interpreted cautiously and warrants confirmation in larger studies. Overall, these findings remain broadly consistent with prior studies advocating for a broader use of marginal kidneys in transplantation [21, 29, 30]. Despite the novelty of this analysis, some limitations must be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design and single-center scope may restrict the generalizability of the findings to a larger national and international scale. Moreover, because allocation decisions could be influenced by overall clinical judgment and the sample size of the high-score group was relatively limited, some degree of selection bias and reduced statistical power cannot be excluded. Future prospective, multicenter studies with extended follow-up periods are necessary to validate these findings and refine donor allocation protocols. Furthermore, the integration of noninvasive biomarkers or advanced imaging techniques with histological evaluation could enhance the precision of donor risk assessment.

Conclusion

Overall, this study supports the feasibility of using kidneys with severe chronic histologic lesions in DKT, showing no excess risk of graft failure, mortality, or death-censored graft failure compared with lower-score grafts. Importantly, these findings suggest that pre-implantation biopsy should not be used solely as an exclusion tool – as kidneys with advanced chronic lesions may still provide clinically acceptable function when allocated as DKT – and that biopsy findings should be interpreted together with donor clinical and functional parameters. Careful integration of histological, functional, and clinical assessment remains essential to guide allocation decisions and optimize outcomes. Further multicenter, prospective studies with longer follow-up are warranted to confirm these results and refine evidence-based allocation protocols for kidneys with high histologic scores.
