Automated diagnosis of Helicobacter pylori infection from routine endoscopic images using deep learning: a development and validation study
Authors: Yavuz Özden, Ferhat Omurca
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReal-time endoscopic diagnosis of Helicobacter pylori infection remains challenging and often requires biopsy-based testing, delaying treatment decisions. Although deep learning (DL) approaches have shown promise, most prior studies have relied on retrospective datasets or image-enhanced modalities, limiting their applicability to routine white-light endoscopy. We aimed to develop and prospectively validate a research-stage DL model for H. pylori detection using only standard white-light endoscopic images.MethodsIn this single-center prospective study, consecutive adults undergoing diagnostic gastroscopy were enrolled. Six standardized gastric images per patient were targeted under standard white-light endoscopic imaging. Histopathology from antral and corpus biopsies served as the reference standard. An EfficientNet-B0–based DL model was developed to classify H. pylori infection at the patient level by aggregating image-level predictions. Model performance was assessed using five-fold cross-validation within the development cohort, followed by evaluation in an independent temporally separated validation cohort (70% development / 30% temporal validation).ResultsA total of 172 patients (1,000 images) were included; 94 patients (54.7%) were H. pylori–positive. In five-fold cross-validation, the model achieved a patient-level AUC of 0.901 (95% CI: 0.863–0.936), with 85.1% sensitivity and 81.4% specificity. In the independent temporal validation cohort (n = 52; prevalence 48.1%), the AUC was 0.889 (95% CI: 0.793–0.960), with 84.0% sensitivity and 85.2% specificity. Aggregating predictions across multiple gastric views improved discrimination compared with single-image inference.ConclusionIn this prospective study, a deep learning model using routine white-light endoscopic images demonstrated reasonable patient-level discrimination for H. pylori detection, including in an independent temporally separated validation cohort. At present, the model should be viewed as a research and decision-support tool rather than a standalone diagnostic system. Introduction

Helicobacter pylori infection remains one of the most common chronic bacterial infections worldwide and plays a central role in the pathogenesis of chronic active gastritis, peptic ulcer disease, and gastric cancer. Current international guidelines, including the Maastricht VI/Florence consensus and the American College of Gastroenterology recommendations, define H. pylori as an infectious disease that should be treated whenever detected, underscoring its substantial clinical and public health burden [1, 2].

In routine practice, however, H. pylori status is rarely determined during the endoscopic examination itself. Clinicians generally rely on adjunctive tests such as histopathology, rapid urease testing, or urea breath testing. These methods require tissue sampling, post-procedural analysis, or both; consequently, treatment decisions are often deferred until after the patient has left the endoscopy unit. Multiple biopsies remain common because visual assessment alone is not sufficiently reliable for clinical decision-making.

The concept that endoscopy may help predict H. pylori status is well established. Mucosal features such as diffuse redness, nodularity, atrophy, and disruption of the regular arrangement of collecting venules have been associated with infection. The Kyoto classification of gastritis sought to standardize these observations and provide a structured framework for optical assessment [3]. Nevertheless, even among experienced endoscopists, diagnostic performance remains imperfect, particularly in patients with subtle, heterogeneous, post-eradication, or atrophic mucosal patterns. Interobserver variability and overlap between active infection and chronic mucosal alterations continue to limit purely visual diagnosis.

Artificial intelligence, particularly deep learning–based image analysis, has introduced the possibility of supporting endoscopic interpretation through automated pattern recognition. Early convolutional neural network studies demonstrated that H. pylori–associated gastritis could be identified from endoscopic images with performance comparable to that of experienced endoscopists [4]. Subsequent meta-analyses reported pooled area under the receiver operating characteristic curve values frequently exceeding 0.90, although these estimates were largely derived from retrospective datasets [5]. Several studies have also shown that combining information from multiple gastric views improves patient-level discrimination, reflecting the multifocal nature of infection-related mucosal changes [6].

Despite these advances, several methodological and clinical gaps remain. Much of the available evidence is retrospective, single-center, and based on selected image sets or image-enhanced modalities that are not universally available [5–8]. Performance observed on static images cannot be assumed to translate directly to real-time endoscopy, where motion artifacts, mucus, variable focus, luminal distension, and peristalsis may affect image quality and model behavior. Although large multicenter artificial intelligence systems have demonstrated feasibility at scale [9–11], their implementation may require extensive datasets and technical infrastructure that are not readily available in many routine endoscopy settings. Therefore, prospectively collected, white-light–only, patient-level models evaluated under pragmatic conditions remain insufficiently studied.

We designed this prospective development and validation study to evaluate a deep learning model trained exclusively on routine white-light endoscopic images for patient-level detection of H. pylori infection. The model used standardized gastric views with strict patient-level data partitioning, and performance was assessed through five-fold cross-validation and an independent temporally separated validation cohort. Histopathology from antral and corpus biopsies served as the reference standard. We did not aim to propose a standalone diagnostic system or a replacement for biopsy-based diagnosis. Rather, we sought to determine whether a pragmatic white-light image model could provide clinically informative discrimination as a research-stage decision-support approach for endoscopic risk assessment and biopsy strategy. Multicenter external validation and prospective video-based evaluation remain necessary before routine clinical implementation can be considered.

Methods

Study design and population

We conducted a single-center prospective diagnostic accuracy study to develop and evaluate a deep learning–based model for detecting Helicobacter pylori infection from routine white-light endoscopic images. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Kayseri City Hospital (approval no. 613, October 13, 2025) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment.

Consecutive adult patients (≥ 18 years) undergoing diagnostic upper gastrointestinal endoscopy for any clinical indication between November 2025 and January 2026 were prospectively enrolled. To retain pragmatic applicability while avoiding overly restrictive selection, exclusion criteria were limited to: (1) prior gastric surgery, (2) contraindication to gastric biopsy, including coagulopathy or ongoing anticoagulant therapy that could not be temporarily withheld, and (3) refusal to provide informed consent. All examinations were performed using high-definition endoscopy systems (Olympus or Pentax) under standard white-light imaging. No image-enhanced modalities were used.

Image acquisition protocol

A predefined image acquisition protocol was used to standardize sampling across patients. Six gastric views were targeted in each patient: close and distant views of the antrum, angularis, close and distant views of the corpus, and fundus or cardia when accessible. These views were selected to represent the range of mucosal patterns associated with H. pylori infection and to correspond broadly with the regions sampled for histopathology. Images were stored in JPEG format at standard endoscopic resolution (1920 × 1080 pixels).

A total of 1,000 images were obtained from 172 consecutive patients, with a median of six images per patient (range, 5–6). The shortfall from the expected 1,032 images reflects routine technical challenges during live endoscopy, including transient luminal collapse, excessive peristalsis, or suboptimal visualization that occasionally prevented acquisition of all predefined views. No acquired image was excluded on the basis of quality. Images with motion artifacts, mucus, bubbles, or poor illumination were retained to preserve the consecutive nature of the dataset and to allow assessment under pragmatic, non-curated imaging conditions. All images from the same patient were linked using a unique identifier to ensure strict patient-level analysis.

Reference standard and histopathological assessment

Histopathology served as the reference standard for H. pylori status. In accordance with routine clinical practice, at least two biopsy specimens were obtained from the antrum, within 2–3 cm of the pylorus, and two from the corpus, along the mid-greater curvature. Biopsy sites were documented endoscopically and corresponded to the anatomical regions represented in the standardized image acquisition protocol. Specimens were initially evaluated using hematoxylin–eosin staining; equivocal cases underwent additional Giemsa staining or immunohistochemistry.

Two experienced gastrointestinal pathologists, blinded to endoscopic findings and model outputs, independently evaluated all specimens. Disagreements were resolved by consensus. Patients were classified as H. pylori–positive (HP+) if organisms were identified in any biopsy specimen and as H. pylori–negative (HP−) if all biopsy specimens were negative. Accordingly, all images from a given patient inherited the patient-level histopathological label. Urea breath testing and stool antigen testing were not performed. Although obtaining four biopsies per patient may reduce sampling error, it does not eliminate the possibility of misclassification due to patchy infection.

Data splitting and validation strategy

All analyses were conducted at the patient level. Images from a given patient were never split across training, validation, or test sets. This approach was used to avoid data leakage and performance inflation caused by correlation among images from the same patient.

Two complementary validation strategies were implemented.

Five-fold cross-validation

Within the development cohort, patients were randomly divided into five strata-matched folds while preserving the proportion of HP+ cases. In each cross-validation iteration, three folds (60%) were used for training, one fold (20%) for internal validation and hyperparameter tuning, and one fold (20%) as the held-out test set. Each patient appeared in the test set exactly once. Performance metrics were calculated for each test fold and then averaged across folds.

Temporal hold-out validation

To approximate prospective deployment within the limits of a single-center design, we performed a chronological split of the enrollment period. The first 120 consecutively enrolled patients (70%) formed the development cohort used for model training, internal validation, and cross-validation. The remaining 52 patients (30%) constituted an independent temporally separated validation cohort. This temporal cohort was not used during model selection, training, hyperparameter tuning, threshold selection, or fusion-strategy comparison, and was evaluated only after model development had been finalized. This temporal validation was intended to assess temporal stability and does not replace multicenter external validation.

Image quality assessment

Because image quality varies in routine endoscopy, a post-hoc image quality analysis was performed in the temporal validation cohort. Two experienced endoscopists, blinded to histopathology and model predictions, independently categorized images as “optimal” or “suboptimal.” Optimal images were defined as those with clear mucosal visualization and minimal artifacts. Suboptimal images included those with motion blur, mucus, bubbles, poor illumination, or partial luminal collapse. Disagreements were resolved by consensus. Model performance was then descriptively compared at the image level according to image quality category.

Deep learning models and training

Several deep learning architectures were evaluated, balancing model complexity against the size of the dataset. EfficientNet-B0 was selected as the primary model because of its favorable accuracy-to-parameter ratio and suitability for moderate-sized image datasets. ResNet-50 was included as a benchmark convolutional neural network. A Vision Transformer architecture (ViT-Small) was also explored to assess transformer-based performance under limited data conditions.

All models were initialized with ImageNet-pretrained weights. Images were resized to architecture-specific input dimensions: 224 × 224 pixels for EfficientNet-B0 and ResNet-50, and 224 × 224 pixels with patch size 16 for ViT-Small. Pixel intensities were normalized before training. On-the-fly data augmentation was applied only to training images and included random rotation (± 15°), horizontal flipping, zooming (± 10%), brightness variation (± 20%), Gaussian noise (σ = 0.01), mild blurring, and contrast reduction. These augmentations were used to reduce overfitting and to partially simulate variability encountered during routine endoscopy. No augmentation was applied to validation or test images.

Models were implemented in Python 3.9 using PyTorch 2.0 and torchvision 0.15 and trained on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU with 24 GB of memory. Optimization used the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 1 × 10⁻⁴. Early stopping was triggered when validation loss failed to improve for 10 consecutive epochs. Regularization included L2 weight decay (λ = 1 × 10⁻⁵) and dropout rate 0.3–0.5 in fully connected layers.

Each image generated a probability score between 0 and 1 indicating the likelihood of H. pylori positivity. For patient-level inference, probability scores were averaged across all available images from the same patient. Alternative fusion approaches, including logistic regression with anatomical site weighting and attention-based aggregation, were explored within the development cohort. These approaches did not meaningfully improve discrimination and increased model complexity; therefore, simple averaging was retained for the primary analyses because of its transparency and ease of interpretation.

Subgroup analyses

Prespecified subgroup analyses were performed to evaluate model behavior in clinically relevant scenarios. These included analyses by endoscope vendor (Olympus vs. Pentax), prior documented H. pylori eradication therapy based on medical history, histologically confirmed moderate-to-severe gastric atrophy or intestinal metaplasia, and proton pump inhibitor use at the time of endoscopy. These analyses were considered exploratory because of the limited number of patients in several subgroups.

Explainability analysis

Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) was used to visualize image regions contributing to model predictions. Heatmaps were generated from activations in the final convolutional layer and normalized to the range [0,1]. Representative HP + and HP− cases were selected, including cases with prior eradication therapy or atrophic changes. Two experienced endoscopists, blinded to model probabilities and histopathological status, independently reviewed the Grad-CAM overlays to assess whether highlighted regions corresponded to clinically recognizable mucosal features. Disagreements were resolved by consensus.

Outcome measures and statistical analysis

The primary outcome was patient-level area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) for discriminating HP+ from HP− patients. AUCs were calculated for each cross-validation fold and for the temporal validation cohort. 95% confidence intervals were estimated using nonparametric percentile bootstrap resampling with 1,000 bootstrap iterations at the patient level.

Secondary performance metrics included sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, accuracy, and F1-score. The operating threshold was selected within the development process using Youden’s index and was applied to held-out test data. For cross-validation, metrics were averaged across folds. For temporal validation, metrics were reported directly. Confidence intervals for proportions were calculated using Wilson’s method. ROC curves were generated from patient-level predictions and plotted for visual comparison of model discrimination. Model calibration was assessed using calibration plots and Brier scores.

Statistical analyses were performed using Python 3.9 with SciPy and scikit-learn.

Large language model use

Artificial intelligence tools were used only for language editing and structural refinement of the manuscript. No such tool was used for data analysis, model training, interpretation of findings, or generation of scientific content.

Based on histopathology, 94 patients (54.7%) were classified as H. pylori–positive (HP+) and 78 (45.3%) as H. pylori–negative (HP−). The mean age was 49.8 ± 15.3 years, and 93 patients (54.1%) were male. Endoscopic features typically associated with active infection, including diffuse antral redness and nodularity, were more frequent in HP+ patients, whereas preservation of the regular arrangement of collecting venules (RAC) was more common in HP− individuals. However, overlap between groups was observed. Histologically, all HP+ patients had chronic gastritis, while chronic gastritis was also present in 52.6% of HP− patients.

Model performance and validation

Cross-validation

In five-fold patient-level cross-validation, the EfficientNet-B0 model achieved a mean AUC of 0.901 (95% CI, 0.863–0.936) (Fig. 2). At the operating threshold determined using Youden’s index within the development process, sensitivity, specificity, and overall accuracy were 85.1% (95% CI, 79.2–89.7), 81.4% (95% CI, 74.8–86.8), and 83.5% (95% CI, 78.4–87.8), respectively.

Temporal validation

In the independent temporally separated validation cohort (n = 52; HP+ prevalence, 48.1%), EfficientNet-B0 achieved an AUC of 0.889 (95% CI, 0.793–0.960) (Fig. 3). Sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy were 84.0% (95% CI, 70.3–92.4), 85.2% (95% CI, 71.6–93.5), and 84.6% (95% CI, 74.0–91.6), respectively. These values were numerically comparable to those observed during cross-validation.

Image quality analysis

In the temporal validation cohort, 302 images were reviewed for image quality. Among these, 236 images (78.1%) were rated as optimal and 66 (21.9%) as suboptimal by independent endoscopic review. In a secondary descriptive image-level analysis, the AUC was 0.872 (95% CI, 0.791–0.938) for optimal images and 0.851 (95% CI, 0.762–0.924) for suboptimal images (Supplementary Table 1).

Patient-level aggregation

At the image level, the model yielded a mean AUC of approximately 0.85. Aggregation of predictions across available standardized gastric views was associated with higher patient-level sensitivity (85.1% vs. 79.0%) and specificity (81.4% vs. 77.5%) compared with single-image inference.

Model calibration

Calibration analysis in the temporal validation cohort demonstrated reasonable agreement between predicted probabilities and observed infection rates (Brier score, 0.130). The calibration plot is provided in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses were exploratory because of the limited number of patients in several categories. Results are summarized in Supplementary Table 2.

Endoscope vendor

Model performance was similar between images acquired with Olympus and Pentax systems. The AUC was 0.894 (95% CI, 0.831–0.947) for Olympus and 0.883 (95% CI, 0.818–0.938) for Pentax.

Prior eradication therapy

Among 23 patients with documented prior H. pylori eradication therapy, all were histopathologically negative at the time of endoscopy. The model classified 19 of these 23 patients (82.6%) as negative. In the four false-positive cases, moderate-to-severe atrophy or intestinal metaplasia was present.

Atrophy and intestinal metaplasia

In patients with moderate-to-severe gastric atrophy (n = 10), sensitivity and specificity were both 75.0%. In patients with intestinal metaplasia (n = 11), sensitivity was 80.0% and specificity was 83.3%. These subgroup estimates should be interpreted cautiously because of small sample sizes.

Proton pump inhibitor use

Among patients using proton pump inhibitors at the time of endoscopy (n = 14), AUC was 0.881, compared with 0.893 in non-users.

Endoscopic phenotype

Sensitivity was higher among patients with overt endoscopic features of H. pylori gastritis, such as marked nodularity or diffuse redness (95.8%; 23/24), and lower among those with minimal or subtle mucosal abnormalities (70.2%; 33/47). All 13 patients with H. pylori–associated peptic ulcer disease were classified as infected.

Explainability analysis

Grad-CAM visualizations showed that, in HP+ cases, model attention often localized to areas of diffuse redness, nodularity, or mucosal thickening (Fig. 4A–B). In HP− cases with normal mucosa, heatmaps demonstrated diffuse, low-intensity activation without focal emphasis (Fig. 4C). In patients with prior eradication therapy, highlighted regions occasionally corresponded to atrophic or metaplastic mucosa, illustrating potential misattribution in post-eradication settings (Fig. 4D). Independent review by two experienced endoscopists indicated concordance between highlighted regions and recognized endoscopic features.

Discussion

In this prospective, patient-level diagnostic study, we evaluated a deep learning model trained exclusively on routine white-light endoscopic images for the detection of Helicobacter pylori infection. Using data from 172 consecutively enrolled patients and a strict patient-level analytical framework, the EfficientNet-B0 model achieved an AUC of 0.901 in cross-validation and 0.889 in an independent temporally separated validation cohort, with sensitivity and specificity around 85%. These findings suggest that routine white-light endoscopic images contain discriminative visual information relevant to H. pylori status. However, the results should be interpreted within the constraints of a single-center, static-image study with a modest sample size.

The prospective enrollment, standardized image acquisition protocol, and strict patient-level validation represent methodological strengths. In contrast to many retrospective and image-selected datasets, all acquired images were retained, including those with suboptimal quality. This approach provides a pragmatic estimate of model behavior under non-curated imaging conditions. Nevertheless, temporal validation within a single center does not establish external generalizability, and the model should not be interpreted as ready for standalone clinical use.

Comparison with previous studies

Our results align with prior work on AI-assisted endoscopic diagnosis of H. pylori [4–6]. Early endoscopic AI applications, including CNN-based detection of gastric neoplasia, demonstrated the feasibility of deep learning in routine endoscopic imaging [12]. A meta-analysis of predominantly retrospective studies reported pooled diagnostic performance with an AUC of approximately 0.92 and sensitivity and specificity in the mid-80% range [5]. Individual development studies have similarly shown that aggregating predictions from multiple gastric images improves patient-level diagnostic performance [6]. The slightly lower point estimates observed in the present prospective cohort compared with some retrospective reports may reflect the inclusion of consecutive patients and non-curated images rather than inferior model behavior.

Several groups have explored image-enhanced endoscopy to improve AI-based discrimination. Linked color imaging, for instance, has yielded diagnostic accuracies approaching or exceeding 85% and, in selected settings, improved differentiation compared with white-light imaging alone [7, 8]. In contrast, our model relied only on standard white-light images. This design may be more applicable to endoscopy units without image-enhanced modalities, but direct comparative studies would be required before drawing conclusions about relative performance across imaging platforms.

Large multicenter initiatives have demonstrated the feasibility of AI-assisted H. pylori detection at scale [9, 11]. However, performance has varied across validation cohorts, emphasizing the need for external validation across different centers, populations, endoscope systems, and infection prevalence settings. In the present study, the independent temporally separated cohort adds methodological rigor, but it remains an internal validation strategy rather than a substitute for multicenter external validation.

Clinical implications and caveats

AI-assisted H. pylori assessment during routine endoscopy may eventually support endoscopic risk assessment and biopsy strategy. Our explainability analyses suggested that model attention often aligned with clinically recognizable mucosal patterns, including diffuse redness, nodularity, and altered fold architecture. Such findings may improve interpretability, but Grad-CAM should be viewed as supportive visualization rather than proof of causal reasoning by the model. Similar explainability and implementation frameworks have been advocated for clinical AI applications in gastrointestinal endoscopy [13, 14].

Several caveats warrant emphasis. First, all experiments were conducted on static images rather than real-time video streams. Live endoscopy introduces dynamic challenges, including motion artifacts, mucus, variable focus, peristalsis, and fluctuating luminal distension. These factors may affect model performance in ways that are not fully captured by static-image analysis. Although the image quality analysis suggested only a modest difference between optimal and suboptimal images, prospective video-based validation remains necessary before clinical deployment can be considered.

Second, model behavior in patients with post-eradication changes or advanced atrophy highlights an important limitation. In the subgroup with prior eradication therapy, four of 23 patients were misclassified as positive, and all had moderate-to-severe atrophy or intestinal metaplasia. This suggests that the model may respond to mucosal architectural distortion rather than the presence of active infection in some cases. Clinical context, including eradication history, proton pump inhibitor use, and histological background, should therefore be considered when interpreting model outputs.

Third, we did not compare model performance directly against endoscopists in a controlled reader study. Therefore, the present study cannot determine whether the model adds independent diagnostic value beyond expert visual assessment. Future studies should include reader-comparison or AI-assisted reader designs to clarify the incremental clinical value of the model.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was modest, with 172 patients and 1,000 images. This may limit model generalizability and constrain optimal training of data-intensive architectures such as Vision Transformers. The comparatively lower performance of ViT-Small likely reflects dataset scale constraints. Larger multicenter datasets would allow evaluation of more complex models and more reliable assessment of robustness [15, 16].

Second, the study was conducted at a single center using two endoscopy systems. Although no substantial difference in performance was observed between Olympus and Pentax images within this cohort, geographic, demographic, procedural, and device-related variability may influence model behavior. External validation in independent institutions is therefore required.

Third, histopathology served as the sole reference standard. Although antral and corpus biopsies were obtained, sampling error due to patchy infection may lead to false-negative classification. This concern is particularly relevant in patients with subtle mucosal changes, in whom model performance was lower. Future studies incorporating urea breath testing or stool antigen testing could improve ground-truth labeling [1, 2].

Fourth, assessment of prior eradication therapy relied on medical record documentation rather than prospective confirmation. In addition, several subgroup analyses, including atrophy, intestinal metaplasia, and proton pump inhibitor use, included relatively small numbers of patients. These subgroup results should therefore be interpreted as exploratory.

Future directions

Future studies should prioritize multicenter external validation across diverse populations, endoscopy systems, and clinical settings. Prospective video-based validation is also essential to determine whether performance observed on static images translates to live endoscopy. Integration of clinical variables, including eradication history, proton pump inhibitor use, demographic factors, and histological background, may improve specificity in challenging subgroups. Reader-comparison studies and prospective interventional designs will be needed to determine whether AI-assisted H. pylori assessment can influence biopsy practice, diagnostic confidence, treatment timing, or patient outcomes [17–20].

Conclusion

In this prospective single-center study, a deep learning model trained on routine white-light endoscopic images demonstrated reasonable patient-level discrimination for Helicobacter pylori infection, including in an independent temporally separated validation cohort. However, performance in patients with post-eradication changes or advanced atrophy indicates that mucosal alterations unrelated to active infection may occasionally be misinterpreted. At present, the model should be viewed as a research and decision-support tool rather than a standalone diagnostic system. Multicenter external validation and prospective video-based studies are warranted before routine clinical deployment can be considered.
