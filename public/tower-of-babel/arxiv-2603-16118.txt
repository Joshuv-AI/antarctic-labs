𝐒𝐄 ⁡ ( 𝟑 ) \boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathrm{SE}(3)}} -LIO: Smooth IMU Propagation With Jointly Distributed Poses on 𝐒𝐄 ⁡ ( 𝟑 ) \boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathrm{SE}(3)}} Manifold for Accurate and Robust LiDAR-Inertial Odometry
Gunhee Shin 1, Seungjae Lee 1, Jei Kong 1, Youngwoo Seo 2 and Hyun Myung 1∗

Abstract
In estimating odometry accurately, an inertial measurement unit (IMU) is widely used owing to its high-rate measurements, which can be utilized to obtain motion information through IMU propagation. In this paper, we address the limitations of existing IMU propagation methods in terms of motion prediction and motion compensation. In motion prediction, the existing methods typically represent a 6-DoF pose by separating rotation and translation and propagate them on their respective manifold, so that the rotational variation is not effectively incorporated into translation propagation. During motion compensation, the relative transformation between predicted poses is used to compensate motion-induced distortion in other measurements, while inherent errors in the predicted poses introduce uncertainty in the relative transformation. To tackle these challenges, we represent and propagate the pose on SE ⁡ ( 3 ) \mathrm{SE}(3) manifold, where propagated translation properly accounts for rotational variation. Furthermore, we precisely characterize the relative transformation uncertainty by considering the correlation between predicted poses, and incorporate this uncertainty into the measurement noise during motion compensation. To this end, we propose a LiDAR-inertial odometry (LIO), referred to as SE ⁡ ( 3 ) \mathrm{SE}(3) -LIO, that integrates the proposed IMU propagation and uncertainty-aware motion compensation (UAMC). We validate the effectiveness of SE ⁡ ( 3 ) \mathrm{SE}(3) -LIO on diverse datasets. Our source code and additional material are available at: https://se3-lio.github.io/ .

Introduction

Accurate odometry estimation is essential for autonomous navigation
[
6
,
14
]
.
Among various proprioceptive sensors, inertial measurement unit (IMU) is widely used with other exteroceptive sensors owing to its high-rate measurements.
These measurements can be utilized to obtain motion information through IMU propagation, which integrates IMU measurements over time based on a kinematic model
[
6
,
26
]
.

IMU propagation enhances odometry performance in two aspects:
motion prediction
and
motion compensation
.
Firstly, it enables motion prediction before the measurements from other modalities, such as LiDAR point clouds and camera images, which are highly informative but available at lower frequencies.
The predicted motion is used as a prior information for the update step in Kalman filter
[
26
,
25
,
24
,
7
]
or a factor for pose graph optimization
[
23
,
20
,
13
]
.
Secondly, it allows motion compensation for motion-induced distortions in other measurements.
Sensors such as LiDAR and event cameras accumulate samples over a certain period of time, during which a robot’s continuous motion would introduce distortions to the measurements
[
3
,
15
]
.
These distortions are typically compensated by transforming the samples into a common reference pose.
Such motion compensation is achieved by applying a relative transformation between the reference pose and the pose at each sample time, where both poses are obtained through IMU propagation.

The joint distribution of predicted poses is derived to characterize the relative transformation uncertainty by considering their correlation, which is incorporated into the measurement noise during motion compensation to construct a probabilistic undistorted point cloud.

While IMU propagation has been extensively studied
[
6
,
3
,
24
]
, several challenges remain in motion prediction and motion compensation.
For motion prediction, conventional methods typically represent a 6-DoF pose by separating rotation and translation, and propagate them on their respective manifolds
[
6
]
.
Consequently, rotational variation is not effectively incorporated into translation propagation, resulting in inaccurate motion prediction.
For motion compensation, the predicted poses obtained through IMU propagation inherently contain errors due to sensor noise and numerical integration
[
2
]
, introducing uncertainty in the relative transformation used for motion compensation.
This uncertainty should be addressed properly during motion compensation, especially for measurements at distant ranges, such as LiDAR point clouds, where even small rotational changes could introduce substantial perturbations on the measurements
[
3
,
9
]
.
However, characterizing its uncertainty remains challenging, because it cannot be directly measured from IMU propagation
[
14
]
.

To address these challenges, we propose a smooth IMU propagation with jointly distributed poses on SE(3) manifold.
The proposed method represents and propagates the pose on SE(3) manifold, where the propagated translation properly accounts for rotational variation, leading to more accurate motion prediction.
Furthermore, we derive the joint distribution of the predicted poses, which enables precise characterization of relative transformation uncertainty by considering their correlation.
This uncertainty is incorporated into the measurement noise during motion compensation to obtain a probabilistic undistorted measurement, and we refer to this process as uncertainty-aware motion compensation (UAMC).
To this end, we present an error-state Kalman filter-based LiDAR–inertial odometry (LIO) framework, termed
SE(3)-LIO
, which integrates the proposed IMU propagation and UAMC.
It optimizes a prior from the proposed IMU propagation together with a measurement distribution constructed through UAMC.
We validate the effectiveness of
\mathrm{SE}(3)
-LIO with data acquired in diverse scenarios, including a drone under aggressive motion and a ground vehicle operating in large-scale rough terrain, and the results demonstrate improved accuracy and robustness of
\mathrm{SE}(3)
-LIO compared with state-of-the-art LIO methods.
The main contributions of this study are as follows:

1.

We propose a smooth IMU propagation on
\mathrm{SE}(3)
manifold that enables accurate motion prediction by incorporating rotational variation into translation propagation.

2.

We introduce an uncertainty-aware motion compensation (UAMC) approach that constructs a probabilistic undistorted measurement.

3.

We present an accurate and robust LiDAR-inertial odometry (LIO) framework, termed
\mathrm{SE}(3)
-LIO, which integrates the proposed IMU propagation and UAMC.

4.

Experimental results in diverse scenarios demonstrate improved accuracy and robustness of
\mathrm{SE}(3)
-LIO compared with state-of-the-art LIO methods.

2
Related Works

2.1
IMU Propagation

Extensive IMU propagation methods have been developed based on the preintegration theory
[
6
]
, which properly addresses a rotational motion on
\mathrm{SO}(3)
manifold.
The theory introduces a kinematic model
[
16
]
that describes the evolution of a 6-DoF by rotation and translation separately.
Building upon this theory, LIO-EKF
[
24
]
incorporated high-order terms during discretization of the kinematic model to capture the specific robot motion.
Brossard et al.
[
2
]
further extended the theory to
\mathrm{SE}_{2}(3)
manifold, which jointly represents rotation, translation, and velocity in a single Lie group.
While these methods attempt to improve motion description, they still have limitations in addressing rotational variations during translation propagation, which results in inaccurate motion prediction under the constant global acceleration assumption
[
6
,
2
]
.
In parallel, continuous-time representation approaches have been introduced to overcome the limitations of discrete-time models
[
15
]
.
Specifically, B-spline interpolation with the predicted poses as control points has been employed to model smooth trajectories, demonstrating improved performance in environments with curved trajectories
[
21
,
17
,
9
]
.
Despite these advancements, they introduce additional complexity and computational overhead, limiting their feasibility in real-time applications.

Moreover, motion compensation is performed using the relative transformation between a reference pose and the pose at each sample time (sample pose).
A large body of works, including LIO-SAM
[
23
]
and FAST-LIO
[
26
]
, estimate the sample pose by linearly interpolating between two consecutive predicted poses.
While this approach achieves effective and real-time performance, it operates in a discrete-time manner, which introduces errors particularly in highly dynamic environments.
To address this limitation, recent works
[
21
,
17
]
introduced continuous-time models and extracted the sample pose from a smooth trajectory.
Furthermore, DLIO
[
3
]
introduces a coarse-to-fine IMU propagation approach that incorporates linear jerk and angular acceleration to refine the estimation of the sample pose between predicted poses.
Despite these advances, the predicted poses inherently contain errors due to sensor noise and numerical integration
[
2
]
, which consequently introduce uncertainty in the relative transformation for motion compensation.
MA-LIO
[
9
]
incorporates such uncertainty into measurement noise during motion compensation.
However, it overestimates the uncertainty by neglecting the correlation between predicted poses.
Accurately characterizing the relative transformation uncertainty remains challenging, as it cannot be directly estimated from IMU propagation.

2.2
LiDAR-Inertial Odometry

LiDAR–inertial odometry (LIO) has been widely utilized in various robotic applications due to its accuracy and real-time performance
[
10
]
.
Among various approaches, Kalman filter methods are preferred for their lightweight formulation and computational efficiency
[
19
,
12
]
.
FAST-LIO
[
26
]
and FAST-LIO2
[
25
]
introduced an efficient error-state Kalman filter (ESKF) framework and an incremental kd-tree structure for map management, achieving reliable performance in diverse scenarios.
Building upon this, VoxelMap
[
28
]
presents an uncertainty-aware ESKF framework that incorporates the prior uncertainty from IMU propagation into measurement noise, which is subsequently propagated to the residual in the measurement distribution.
It also employs an adaptive voxel map that efficiently manages planar features with their associated uncertainty, organized using a hash table and octree structure.
In this work, we build upon the ESKF framework presented in
[
26
,
9
]
and enhance odometry performance by integrating our proposed smooth IMU propagation and UAMC.

3
Smooth IMU Propagation With Jointly Distributed Poses on SE(3) Manifold

3.1
IMU Propagation on SE(3) Manifold

A typical IMU returns angular velocity
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{m}
and linear acceleration
\mathbf{a}^{m}
as measurements, which are affected by biases and noise.
The biases,
\mathbf{b}^{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
and
\mathbf{b}^{\mathbf{a}}
, follow a random walk process that varies slowly over time, while the noise,
\mathbf{n}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
and
\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{a}}
, are modeled as zero-mean white Gaussian distributions
[
5
,
26
]
.
True IMU value,
\boldsymbol{\omega}
and
\mathbf{a}
, can be expressed as:

\boldsymbol{\omega}^{m}=\boldsymbol{\omega}+\mathbf{b}^{\boldsymbol{\omega}}+\mathbf{n}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\quad\mathbf{a}^{m}=\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}^{\mathbf{a}}+\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{a}}.

(1)

Conventional IMU propagation methods typically define the state vector to include following components:

\mathbf{x}\doteq\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}\quad\mathbf{t}\quad{}^{\mathit{W}}\mathbf{v}\quad\mathbf{b}^{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\quad\mathbf{b}^{\mathbf{a}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{SO}(3)\times\mathbb{R}^{12}

(2)

where the 6-DoF pose is represented by separating rotation and translation,
\mathbf{R}
and
\mathbf{t}
, respectively, and

{}^{\mathit{W}}\mathbf{v}
is the velocity in world frame
[
6
,
3
,
24
]
.
The state evolves according to the following continuous-time kinematic model:

\begin{gathered}\dot{\mathbf{R}}=\mathbf{R}(\boldsymbol{\omega})^{\wedge},\quad\dot{\mathbf{t}}={}^{\mathit{W}}\mathbf{v},\quad{}^{\mathit{W}}\dot{\mathbf{v}}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{g},\\
\dot{\mathbf{b}}^{\boldsymbol{\omega}}=\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{b}^{\boldsymbol{\omega}}},\quad\dot{\mathbf{b}}^{\mathbf{a}}=\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{b}^{\mathbf{a}}}\end{gathered}

(3)

where
\dot{(\cdot)}
denotes the time derivative,
(\cdot)^{\wedge}
denotes the skew-symmetric matrix of a vector, and
\mathbf{g}
is the gravity vector.
By discretizing (
3
), we obtain the following model:

\mathbf{x}_{i+1}=\textbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{u}_{i},\mathbf{w},\Delta t)=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}_{i}\mathrm{Exp}(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i}\Delta t)\\
\mathbf{t}_{i}+{}^{\mathit{W}}\mathbf{v}_{i}\Delta t\\
{}^{\mathit{W}}\mathbf{v}_{i}+(\mathbf{R}_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}+\mathbf{g})\Delta t\\
\mathbf{b}^{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{i}+\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{b}^{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}\\
\mathbf{b}^{\mathbf{a}}_{i}+\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{b}^{\mathbf{a}}}\end{bmatrix}

(4)

where
\mathbf{u}_{i}=[\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\top}_{i}\;\mathbf{a}^{\top}_{i}]^{\top}
is an IMU input,
\mathbf{w}=[\mathbf{n}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{\top}\;\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}\;\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{b}^{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}^{\top}\;\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{b}^{\mathbf{a}}}^{\top}]^{\top}
is a process noise term, and
\Delta t
is the time difference between two consecutive IMU inputs.
For simplicity,
\mathbf{R}_{i}
in the velocity term is assumed constant during
\Delta t
under the constant global acceleration assumption
[
6
,
2
]
.
Notably, rotation and translation are propagated on their respective manifolds, such that rotational variation is not effectively incorporated into translation propagation.
As robot motion involves simultaneous rotation and translation, neglecting the rotation variation can significantly contribute to pose error, especially under aggressive motion.
The error accumulates through successive IMU propagation, leading to an unreliable prior that degrades the stability of system.

The twist,
\boldsymbol{\zeta}(t)\doteq[\mathbf{v}(t)\;\boldsymbol{\omega}(t)]

and generate the ground-truth trajectory with a sufficiently small time step,

(black dashed line).

.

The highlighted box shows that the proposed method properly addresses rotational changes while propagating translation, whereas the conventional method does not.

To address this issue, we propose a smooth IMU propagation method that evolves poses on SE(3) manifold.
We first represent the 6-DoF pose on SE(3) manifold using a transformation matrix
\mathbf{T}
and define the state vector as:

\mathbf{x}\doteq\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{T}\quad{}^{\mathit{B}}\mathbf{v}\quad\mathbf{b}^{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\quad\mathbf{b}^{\mathbf{a}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{SE}(3)\times\mathbb{R}^{9}

(5)

where
{}^{\mathit{B}}\mathbf{v}
is the velocity in the body frame.
Then, we introduce a continuous-time kinematic model with rigid body motion model
[
16
]
as:

\dot{\mathbf{T}}=\mathbf{T}(\boldsymbol{\zeta})^{\wedge},\quad{}^{\mathit{W}}\dot{\mathbf{v}}=\dot{(\mathbf{R}{}^{\mathit{B}}\mathbf{v})}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{g}

(6)

where
\boldsymbol{\zeta}=\begin{bmatrix}{}^{\mathit{B}}\mathbf{v}^{\top}&\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
is the twist in the body frame and the biases evolve as in (
3
).
By discretizing (
6
), we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{i+1}

\displaystyle=f(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{u}_{i},\mathbf{w},\Delta t)

(7)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{T}_{i}\mathrm{Exp}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{i}\Delta t)\\
\mathrm{Exp}(-\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i}\Delta t)\{{}^{\mathit{B}}\mathbf{v}_{i}+(\mathbf{a}_{i}+\mathbf{R}^{\top}_{i}\mathbf{g})\Delta t\}\\
\mathbf{b}^{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{i}+\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{b}^{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}\\
\mathbf{b}^{\mathbf{a}}_{i}+\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{b}^{\mathbf{a}}}\end{bmatrix}.

The pose is propagated on
\mathrm{SE}(3)
manifold, where the propagated translation properly accounts for rotational variation as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}_{i+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{T}_{i}\mathrm{Exp}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{i}\Delta t)

(8)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}_{i}&\mathbf{t}_{i}\\
\mathbf{0}&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{Exp}(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i}\Delta t)&\mathbf{J}_{l}(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i}\Delta t){}^{\mathit{B}}\mathbf{v}_{i}\Delta t\\
\mathbf{0}&1\end{bmatrix}

where
\mathbf{J}_{l}
denotes the left Jacobian of the exponential map on SO(3) manifold
[
6
]
.
The propagated translation can be rewritten as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{t}_{i+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{t}_{i}+\mathbf{R}_{i}\mathbf{J}_{l}(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i}\Delta t){}^{\mathit{B}}\mathbf{v}_{i}\Delta t

(9)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{t}_{i}+\mathbf{J}_{l}(\mathbf{R}_{i}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i}\Delta t){}^{\mathit{W}}\mathbf{v}_{i}\Delta t

where
\mathbf{R}_{i}\mathbf{J}_{l}(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i})\mathbf{R}^{\top}_{i}=\mathbf{J}_{l}(\mathbf{R}_{i}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i})
and
{}^{\mathit{W}}\mathbf{v}_{i}=\mathbf{R}_{i}{}^{\mathit{B}}\mathbf{v}_{i}
.
Comparing (
9
) with the conventional method (
4
) reveals that
their discrepancy increases with larger twist (dynamic motion) and longer intervals (low-frequency IMU) as illustrated in Fig.
2
.

To provide a reliable prior through IMU propagation, both pose and its uncertainty must be propagated accurately
[
6
,
2
]
.
We characterize the state uncertainty using the error state,
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
, which follows a zero-mean Gaussian distribution as:

\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\doteq\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\xi}&\tilde{\mathbf{v}}&\tilde{\mathbf{b}}^{\boldsymbol{\omega}}&\tilde{\mathbf{b}}^{\mathbf{a}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{15}

(10)

where
\boldsymbol{\xi}
is the perturbation represented on the tangent space of SE(3) manifold and the others are additive perturbations on Euclidean space
[
26
]
.
The true state
\mathbf{x}
is related to the noise-free nominal state
\hat{\mathbf{x}}
and an error state
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
as:

\mathbf{x}=\hat{\mathbf{x}}\boxplus\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\quad\tilde{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{x}\boxminus\hat{\mathbf{x}}

(11)

where
\boxplus
and
\boxminus
are operators that map between a local neighborhood on manifold and its tangent space
[
26
]
.
Accordingly, the error state dynamic model can be formulated as:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i+1}=\mathbf{x}_{i+1}\boxminus\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{i+1}

\displaystyle=f(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{u}_{i},\mathbf{w},\Delta t)\boxminus f(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{i},\mathbf{u}_{i},\mathbf{0},\Delta t)

(12)

\displaystyle\approx\mathbf{F}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}+\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{w}_{i}}\mathbf{w}

where
\mathbf{F}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}
and
\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{w}_{i}}
are the Jacobians of
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i+1}
with respect to
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}
and
\mathbf{w}
, respectively, evaluated at
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}=\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{0}
[
26
]
.
Finally, the state covariance is obtained by taking the expectation of the outer product of (
12
) as follows:

\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{i+1}=\mathrm{Exp}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i+1}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i+1}^{\top}\right)=\mathbf{F}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{i}\mathbf{F}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}^{\top}+\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{w}_{i}}\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{w}}\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{w}_{i}}^{\top}

(13)

where
\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{w}}
is the covariance of the process noise.

3.2
Uncertainty-aware Motion Compensation (UAMC)

Consider a measurement that accumulates samples over time, such as LiDAR points and event camera data.
While the set of samples is treated as a single measurement, each sample is acquired at a different location, resulting in motion-induced distortion.
To correct this distortion, we transform all samples into a common reference pose.

Let a measurement be obtained at
t_{k}
, where a sample is observed at
\rho_{j}\in[t_{k-1},t_{k}]
.
We transform the sample using the relative transformation as:

\mathbf{T}_{\mathit{I}_{k}\mathit{I}_{j}}=\mathbf{T}^{-1}_{\mathit{I}_{k}}\mathbf{T}_{\mathit{I}_{j}}.

(14)

where
\mathbf{T}_{\mathit{I}_{k}}
and
\mathbf{T}_{\mathit{I}_{j}}
denote the IMU poses at times
t_{k}
and
\rho_{j}
, respectively and both poses can be obtained from IMU propagation.
Despite advancements in IMU propagation, predicted poses inherently contain errors due to IMU noise and numerical integration, which in turn introduce errors in the relative transformation.
Such errors significantly contribute to sample deviations, particularly for measurements at distant ranges such as LiDAR point clouds.
To address this issue, we incorporate the uncertainty of the relative transformation into the sample noise during motion compensation, yielding a probabilistic representation of undistorted measurements.

The true relative transformation,
\mathbf{T}_{\mathit{I}_{k}\mathit{I}_{j}}
, can be expressed using the nominal pose and its perturbation as:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{T}}_{\mathit{I}_{k}\mathit{I}_{j}}\mathrm{Exp}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathit{I}_{k}\mathit{I}_{j}})

\displaystyle=(\hat{\mathbf{T}}_{\mathit{I}_{k}}\mathrm{Exp}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathit{I}_{k}}))^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{T}}_{\mathit{I}_{j}}\mathrm{Exp}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathit{I}_{j}})

(15)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Exp}(-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathit{I}_{k}})\hat{\mathbf{T}}^{-1}_{\mathit{I}_{k}}\hat{\mathbf{T}}_{\mathit{I}_{j}}\mathrm{Exp}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathit{I}_{j}}).

The perturbations of
\mathbf{T}_{\mathit{I}_{j}}
and
\mathbf{T}_{\mathit{I}_{k}}
follow a joint distribution:

\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathit{I}_{j}}\\
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathit{I}_{k}}\end{bmatrix}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{\Sigma}),\quad\mathbf{\Sigma}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathit{I}_{j}}&\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathit{I}_{j},\mathit{I}_{k}}\\
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathit{I}_{j},\mathit{I}_{k}}^{\top}&\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathit{I}_{k}}\end{bmatrix}

(16)

where
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathit{I}_{j},\mathit{I}_{k}}
is nonzero if the two poses are jointly distributed.
Letting
\hat{\mathbf{T}}_{\mathit{I}_{k}\mathit{I}_{j}}=\hat{\mathbf{T}}^{-1}_{\mathit{I}_{k}}\hat{\mathbf{T}}_{\mathit{I}_{j}}
, we can formulate (
15
) as:

\mathrm{Exp}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathit{I}_{k}\mathit{I}_{j}})=\mathrm{Exp}(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\prime}_{\mathit{I}_{k}})\mathrm{Exp}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathit{I}_{j}})

(17)

where
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\prime}_{\mathit{I}_{k}}=-\mathrm{Ad}_{\hat{\mathbf{T}}^{-1}_{\mathit{I}_{k}\mathit{I}_{i}}}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathit{I}_{k}}
and
\mathrm{Ad}_{\mathbf{T}}
denotes the adjoint matrix of
\mathbf{T}
[
14
,
1
]
.
Applying the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff (BCH) formula, the perturbation in the exponential map is expressed as
[
1
]
:

\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathit{I}_{k}\mathit{I}_{j}}=\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\prime}_{\mathit{I}_{k}}+\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathit{I}_{j}}+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\prime\curlywedge}_{\mathit{I}_{k}}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathit{I}_{j}}+\frac{1}{12}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\prime\curlywedge}_{\mathit{I}_{k}}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\prime\curlywedge}_{\mathit{I}_{k}}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathit{I}_{j}}+\cdots.

(18)

By taking the expectation of the outer product of (
18
), the relative transformation covariance can be computed as

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}[\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathit{I}_{k}\mathit{I}_{j}}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\top}_{\mathit{I}_{k}\mathit{I}_{j}}]\approx

\displaystyle\underbrace{\mathrm{Ad}_{\hat{\mathbf{T}}^{-1}_{\mathit{I}_{k}\mathit{I}_{j}}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathit{I}_{k}}\mathrm{Ad}_{\hat{\mathbf{T}}^{-1}_{\mathit{I}_{k}\mathit{I}_{i}}}^{\top}+\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathit{I}_{j}}}_{\text{2nd order diag. terms}}

(19)

\displaystyle\underbrace{-\mathrm{Ad}_{\hat{\mathbf{T}}^{-1}_{\mathit{I}_{k}\mathit{I}_{j}}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathit{I}_{j},\mathit{I}_{k}}^{\top}-\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathit{I}_{j},\mathit{I}_{k}}\mathrm{Ad}_{\hat{\mathbf{T}}^{-1}_{\mathit{I}_{k}\mathit{I}_{j}}}^{\top}}_{\text{2nd order cross terms}}+\cdots

where the cross terms are valid when the poses are jointly distributed as described in (
16
)
[
14
]
.
In other words, when the predicted poses obtained from IMU propagation are jointly distributed, the cross terms should be considered. Otherwise, they are neglected.

In this study, we derive the joint distribution between the predicted poses and demonstrate that they are jointly distributed.
This property stems from the fact that the IMU propagation is recursively performed, where the current state and an IMU input are used to predict the next state, as (
7
).
Under the recursive structure, each error state can be expressed by an initial error state
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{0}
and IMU noise
\mathbf{w}
as:

\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i+1}=\mathbf{F}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{0}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{0}+\sum_{\tau=0}^{i}(\mathbf{F}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{\tau+1}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{w}_{\tau}}\mathbf{w})

(20)

where the Jacobians are defined as:

\mathbf{F}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k}}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\prod_{j=i}^{k-1}\mathbf{F}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j}}&\text{if }k>i\\
\mathbf{I}&\text{if }k\leq i\end{cases}.

(21)

Stacking the error states over steps, we construct a joint distribution of error states as:

\scriptstyle\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{0}\\
\vdots\\
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i+1}\\
\vdots\\
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mbox{\boldmath$I$}&\mathbf{0}&\cdots&\mathbf{0}&\cdots&\mathbf{0}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathbf{F}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{0}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}&\mathbf{F}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{1}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}&\cdots&\mbox{\boldmath$I$}&\cdots&\mathbf{0}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathbf{F}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{0}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k-1}}&\mathbf{F}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{1}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k-1}}&\cdots&\mathbf{F}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i+1}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k-1}}&\cdots&\mbox{\boldmath$I$}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{0}\\
\mathbf{w}_{0}^{{}^{\prime}}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{w}_{i}^{{}^{\prime}}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{w}_{k-1}^{{}^{\prime}}\end{bmatrix}.

(22)

where
\mathbf{w}_{i}^{{}^{\prime}}=\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{w}_{i}}\mathbf{w}
.
By computing the expectation of outer product of (
22
), we obtain the joint covariance of error states, which contains the joint covariance of pose described in (
16
).
This enables accurate characterization of the relative transformation covariance using (
19
) by incorporating the cross terms.

To the best of our knowledge, MA-LIO
[
9
]
is the first method to incorporate the relative transformation uncertainty into measurement noise during motion compensation.
However, it neglects the cross terms in (
19
) by assuming that the predicted poses are independent
[
1
]
.
To analyze the effect of considering cross terms in uncertainty characterization, we conducted an experiment similar to the one proposed in
[
14
]
as shown in Fig.
3
.
The results demonstrate that the proposed method characterizes the relative transformation uncertainty more accurately, while the independent assumption leads to overestimation.
Additionally, in the proposed method, the uncertainty increases as the target pose lies further in the past with respect to the reference pose.
In contrast, the independent assumption exhibits a decreasing trend opposite to the proposed method because the earlier target pose
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathit{I}_{j}}
in (
19
) has a smaller uncertainty.

Let’s consider a distorted LiDAR point
{}^{\mathit{L}_{j}}\mathbf{p}_{j}
, which is acquired at time
\rho_{j}
and represented in the LiDAR frame.
The point is undistorted using the relative transformation as:

{}^{\mathit{I}_{k}}\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{h}}_{j}=\mathbf{T}_{\mathit{I}_{k}\mathit{I}_{j}}\mathbf{T}_{\mathit{I}\mathit{L}}{}^{\mathit{L}_{j}}\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{h}}_{j},\quad\tau_{i}\leq\rho_{j}<\tau_{i+1}

(23)

where
\mathbf{T}_{\mathit{I}\mathit{L}}
is the extrinsic transformation from IMU to LiDAR frame and
\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{h}}
is a point in homogeneous coordinates.
The undistorted point is modeled as:

{}^{\mathit{I}_{k}}\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{h}}_{j}={}^{\mathit{I}_{k}}\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\mathrm{h}}_{j}+{}^{\mathit{I}_{k}}\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{p}_{j}}^{\mathrm{d}}=\hat{\mathbf{T}}_{\mathit{I}_{k}\mathit{I}_{j}}\mathrm{Exp}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathit{I}_{k}\mathit{I}_{j}})({}^{\mathit{I}_{j}}\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\mathrm{h}}_{j}+{}^{\mathit{I}_{j}}\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{p}_{j}}^{\mathrm{d}})

(24)

where the true point
\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{h}}
consists of a nominal point
\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\mathrm{h}}
and a noise term
\mathbf{n}^{\mathrm{d}}
.
The noise is expressed in homogeneous coordinates using a dilation matrix
\mathbf{D}
that maps a 3D vector to a homogeneous form, such that
\mathbf{n}^{\mathrm{d}}=\mathbf{D}\mathbf{n}

[
8
]
.
Letting
{}^{\mathit{I}_{k}}\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\mathrm{h}}_{j}=\hat{\mathbf{T}}_{\mathit{I}_{k}\mathit{I}_{j}}{}^{\mathit{I}_{j}}\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\mathrm{h}}_{j}
, and by linearizing (
24
), the noise term
{}^{\mathit{I}_{k}}\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{p}_{j}}^{\mathrm{d}}
can be approximated as:

{}^{\mathit{I}_{k}}\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{p}_{j}}^{\mathrm{d}}\approx\hat{\mathbf{T}}_{\mathit{I}_{k}\mathit{I}_{j}}({}^{\mathit{I}_{j}}\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\mathrm{h}}_{j})^{\odot}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathit{I}_{k}\mathit{I}_{j}}+\hat{\mathbf{T}}_{\mathit{I}_{k}\mathit{I}_{j}}{}^{\mathit{I}_{j}}\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{p}_{j}}^{\mathrm{d}}.

(25)

where
(\cdot)^{\odot}
denotes an operator that maps a 4
\times
1 vector to a 4
\times
6 matrix
[
8
,
28
]
.
The resulting noise
{}^{\mathit{I}_{k}}\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{p}_{j}}^{\mathrm{d}}
reflects both the uncertainty of the relative transformation and the raw measurement noise, as illustrated in Fig.
4
.

3.3
Integration with LiDAR-Inertial Odometry

We propose an error-state Kalman filter-based LiDAR-inertial odometry (LIO) framework, termed
\mathrm{SE}(3)
-LIO, that integrates the proposed smooth IMU propagation method and UAMC.
The framework optimizes the prior obtained from the proposed IMU propagation method from Section
3.1
and the measurement distribution constructed by a probabilistic undistorted point cloud from Section
3.2
.
Pipeline of
\mathrm{SE}(3)
-LIO is illustrated in Fig.
5
.

IMU propagation is performed until the end of a LiDAR scan at
t_{k}
, resulting in a prior state as:

\mathbf{x}_{k}=\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\boxplus\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k},\quad\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{k}).

(26)

Then the probabilistic undistorted point cloud obtained through UAMC is transformed to the world frame to construct residuals with the local map, for which we adopt voxel map
[
28
]
as:

\mathbf{h}_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{k},{}^{\mathit{I}_{k}}\mathbf{p}_{j})=(\mathbf{u}_{j}^{\mathrm{d}})^{\top}(\mathbf{T}_{\mathit{I}_{k}}{}^{\mathit{I}_{k}}\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{h}}_{j}-\mathbf{q}_{j}^{\mathrm{d}})

(27)

where
\mathbf{u}_{j}^{\mathrm{d}}
,
\mathbf{q}_{j}^{\mathrm{d}}
are the normal vector and the center of the plane, respectively, in homogeneous coordinates.
The residual can be linearized as:

\mathbf{h}_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{k},{}^{\mathit{I}_{k}}\mathbf{p}_{j})\approx\mathbf{h}_{j}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k},{}^{\mathit{I}_{k}}\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{j})+\mathbf{H}_{j}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k}+\mathbf{J}_{j}{}^{\mathit{I}_{k}}\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{p}_{j}}^{\mathrm{d}}

(28)

where
\mathbf{H}_{j}
and
\mathbf{J}_{j}
, are the Jacobians of
\mathbf{h}_{j}
with respect to
\mathbf{x}_{k}
and
{}^{\mathit{I}_{k}}\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{p}_{j}}^{\mathrm{d}}
, respectively, evaluated at
\mathbf{x}_{k}=\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k}
and
{}^{\mathit{I}_{k}}\mathbf{p}_{j}={}^{\mathit{I}_{k}}\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{j}
as:

\mathbf{H}_{j}=\begin{bmatrix}(\mathbf{u}_{j}^{\mathrm{d}})^{\top}({}^{\mathit{I}_{k}}\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\mathrm{h}}_{j})^{\odot}\hat{\mathbf{T}}_{\mathit{I}_{k}\mathit{I}_{j}}&\mathbf{0}_{1\times 12}\end{bmatrix},\;\mathbf{J}_{j}=(\mathbf{u}_{j}^{\mathrm{d}})^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{T}}_{\mathit{I}_{k}\mathit{I}_{j}}.

(29)

Combining the prior from (
26
) and the measurement distribution from (
28
) yields the maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate as:

\underset{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k}}{\mathrm{min}}\;(\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\right\|^{2}_{\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{k}^{-1}}+\sum_{j}^{m}\left\|\mathbf{z}_{j}+\mathbf{H}_{j}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\right\|^{2}_{\mathbf{R}_{j}^{-1}})

(30)

where
\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|_{\mathbf{M}}=\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{x}
.
Standard Kalman filter update step is performed by optimizing the resultant quadratic cost
[
26
,
28
]
.

4
Experimental results

We perform an extensive evaluation on
\mathrm{SE}(3)
-LIO across diverse scenarios.
Experiments are conducted to validate the effectiveness of
\mathrm{SE}(3)
-LIO in terms of accuracy and robustness in challenging environments, as well as computational efficiency compared with state-of-the-art LIO methods.

We conduct experiments on three datasets, including NTU-VIRAL
[
18

[

The NTU-VIRAL and Newer-College datasets are utilized to evaluate performance under challenging conditions, including aggressive motion and sparse measurements.
The NTU-VIRAL dataset was collected using a drone equipped with a low-channel LiDAR (Ouster OS1-16) and features aggressive motion with rapid directional changes and high linear acceleration.
The Newer-College dataset was collected using a handheld platform, where dynamic motion was induced by human operators shaking it while walking.
Finally, our own dataset was collected using a ground vehicle in large-scale rough terrain, featuring wild fluctuations due to uneven terrain and degraded measurements due to dense vegetation and extensive open spaces, as shown in Fig.
6

We obtain ground truth poses for our dataset using a RTK-GNSS system (Emlid Reach RS) mounted on the vehicle.

5
Conclusion

In this study, we presented a smooth IMU propagation method with jointly distributed poses on SE(3) manifold, addressing the challenges of motion prediction and motion compensation in existing IMU propagation methods.
Furthermore, we proposed SE(3)-LIO that integrates the proposed IMU propagation.
Experimental results demonstrate that SE(3)-LIO outperforms state-of-the-art LIO methods in terms of accuracy and robustness.
Despite these promising results, there is room for improvements in terms of adaptability to different sensor configurations.
As future work, we plan to extend the proposed IMU propagation method to other sensor configurations, such as visual-inertial or radar
[
11
]
odometry.
