Synergistic depassivation of ZVI intensifies persulfate activation for high-efficiency vanadium leaching in a sono‑Fenton‑like system
Authors: Haoyu Li, Jun Wang, Yuxing Zhou, Can Jing, Xuejun Zhu, Yi Zhang, Shuang Wang, Chunqiang Lu, Aiyuan Ma, Jie Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVanadium-bearing metallurgical residue, a typical hazardous solid waste, poses significant challenges for resource recovery because vanadium is predominantly locked within dense, multilayered encapsulations composed of iron spinel, fayalite, and silicate phases. Herein, a novel ultrasound-assisted, zero-valent-iron-activated ammonium persulfate advanced oxidation process referred to as US/ZVI-AOPs was developed to enable highly efficient oxidative leaching of vanadium from V-MR through an effective synergy that integrates sonochemistry, heterogeneous catalysis, and interfacial electron transfer. The vanadium leaching efficiency of the US/ZVI-AOPs system reached 91.57%, while that of the conventional ZVI-AOPs process was 81.63% and that of the externally aerated oxygen system was less than 52%. Ultrasonic cavitation continuously stripped the Fe3O4/Fe2O3 passivation layer from ZVI, sustaining ferrous iron release and persulfate activation. The resulting synergistic oxidation by multiple reactive oxygen species proceeded with SO4•−, •OH, and 1O2 contributing 30.6%, 22.1%, and 11.9%, respectively. The leaching process followed a shrinking-core model, and its apparent activation energy decreased to 5.05 kJ mol−1, which is 72.4% lower than the conventional value of 18.26 kJ mol−1, indicating significantly alleviated diffusion limitations. This work provides a green, high-value pathway for V-MR valorization and a mechanistic paradigm for intensifying heterogeneous AOPs in complex solid-waste treatment.Keywords: Hazardous metallurgical residue, Sonochemistry, Zero-valent iron, Persulfate activation, Multi-ROS synergy

Introduction

Vanadium is a strategic critical metal that underpins modern industrial systems and the clean-energy transition, with widespread applications in high-strength alloy steels, aerospace titanium alloys, and large-scale energy storage [1], [2]. Driven by the rapid expansion of global renewable-energy capacity, global clean-energy investment reached USD 1.74 trillion in 2023, the demand for high-purity vanadium electrolytes for vanadium redox flow batteries continues to rise, rendering the security of vanadium supply increasingly prominent [3]. As the world's largest producer and consumer of vanadium, China has implemented protective restrictions on vanadium–titanium magnetite mining in the Panzhihua region, further tightening the supply of primary vanadium ores [4]. Meanwhile, iron and steel smelting generates over 600,000 tons of vanadium-bearing metallurgical residues (V-MR) annually, with residual vanadium contents of 1–3%; however, the vast majority are stockpiled in open-air disposal sites [5]. Long-term stockpiling of V-MR not only occupies substantial land resources but also releases highly toxic high-valence heavy metals such as V5+ via dust emission and rainwater leaching, contaminating soils and groundwater and posing potential risks to ecosystems and public health [6]. Consequently, the development of efficient and green vanadium-leaching processes for V-MR is of strategic importance for safeguarding critical mineral supply security, enabling solid-waste valorization, and advancing the circular economy.

The principal barrier to efficient vanadium leaching from V-MR originates from its complex mineralogical encapsulation. Vanadium predominantly exists within iron–vanadium spinel phases (e.g., Fe2VO4, (Mn, Fe)(V, Cr)2O4) [7], and these spinels are physically encased by silicate matrices such as fayalite (Fe2SiO4) and pyroxene, forming multi-layered dense structures that severely impede direct contact between leachants and vanadium-bearing phases [8], [9]. Among non-ferrous metal extraction routes, pyrometallurgy typically operates above 1000 °C. Although roasting additives can improve extraction efficiency, this approach is constrained by high energy consumption, difficult control of flue-gas emissions, and limited feasibility for clean, large-scale production [10], [11]. In contrast, hydrometallurgy exhibits marked advantages in energy efficiency, environmental compatibility, and process scalability, and thus are more suitable to stable, industrial-scale implementation. Nevertheless, the silicate-dominated encapsulations in V-MR are dense, coarse-grained, and chemically inert, so that conventional direct acid leaching requires high acid concentrations and prolonged reaction times, and the vanadium recovery remains constrained by diffusion resistance within the encapsulating matrix [12]. There is thus an urgent need to introduce novel intensification strategies capable of disrupting the mineralogical encapsulations and liberating the locked vanadium.

Recent studies indicate that oxidative dissociation of mineralogical encapsulations by reactive oxygen species (ROS) represents a promising route to overcome this bottleneck. Advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) have attracted growing attention owing to their powerful oxidative capacity and broad reactivity [13], [14], [15]. Among them, persulfate (S2O82−) based AOPs, particularly those employing ammonium persulfate ((NH4)2S2O8), generate a suite of ROS upon activation, including sulfate radicals (SO4•−, E° = 62.5–3.1 V), hydroxyl radicals (•OH), and singlet oxygen (1O2). Compared with •OH, SO4•− features a longer half-life (∼40 μs), a broader operational pH window, and higher reaction selectivity, conferring unique advantages for the oxidative dissociation of mineral phases [16], [17], [18]. However, the symmetric O–O bond in (NH4)2S2O8 poses a challenge to efficient activation, and conventional thermal or alkaline activation affords only limited ROS yields [19].

Zero-valent iron (ZVI), as a controlled-release Fe2+ donor, can sustain persulfate activation and modulate the redox environment through the sequential Fe0 → Fe(II) → Fe(III) → Fe(OH)3 transformation and associated electron transfer, thereby circumventing the well-known limitations of homogeneous Fenton processes such as excessive reagent consumption, abundant iron-sludge generation, and secondary pollution [20], [21]. Nevertheless, two bottlenecks hinder its practical deployment: (i) particle agglomeration driven by magnetic attraction and van der Waals forces [22], [23]; and (ii) the facile formation of surface oxide passivation layers that impede electron transfer and Fe2+ regeneration, thereby markedly suppressing reactivity [24]. Ultrasound (US) cavitation can deliver three synergistic intensifications within a single operation: microjet-induced physical removal of surface passivation layers to restore ZVI reactivity [25], [26], localized transient high temperatures capable of direct thermal activation of persulfate [27], and enhanced solid–liquid mass transfer together with mineral particle-size reduction [28]. While traditional routes face limitations including narrow pH tolerance, low reactivity, high energy demand, and severe sludge formation, the proposed system overcomes these barriers via ultrasonic depassivation. It generates multi-ROS at near-neutral pH, enables magnetic recovery, and valorizes residues into iron-rich products. Despite ZVI attrition, its ambient operation and multi-mechanism synergy establish a scalable and economically viable alternative for mineral processing. US/ZVI-AOPs couplings have been applied to environmental tasks such as sludge dewatering and organic-wastewater degradation, confirming the synergistic role of acoustic cavitation in sustaining ZVI reactivity and accelerating pollutant decomposition [29], [30], [31]. To date, however, the potential of this coupled system for treating industrial metallurgical residues enriched in high-value metals remains largely.

Herein, we propose a novel ultrasound‑coupled zero‑valent iron/persulfate advanced oxidation process (US/ZVI‑AOPs) for efficient vanadium leaching from V‑MR. Acoustic cavitation continuously removes ZVI surface passivation, thermally activates persulfate, and intensifies mass transfer, while ZVI‑catalyzed activation generates multiple ROS (SO4•−, •OH, 1O2) that cooperatively dissociate the mineral encapsulation. The system achieves a vanadium leaching efficiency of 91.57%, outperforming conventional routes. Systematic single‑factor optimization, in situ EPR, and quenching assays quantified ROS contributions, and multiscale characterization (XAFS, XPS, SEM‑EDS, XRD) revealed a dual mechanism of enhanced ROS generation and suppressed passivation. This work provides a green, scalable route for V‑MR valorization and a mechanistic paradigm for multi‑field‑intensified heterogeneous AOPs in complex solid‑waste treatment.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

The vanadium-bearing metallurgical residue (V-MR) investigated in this study was collected from a tailings pond at a large iron-and-steel enterprise in Panzhihua (Sichuan Province, China), where the residue had been stockpiled following the sodium-roasting–water-leaching processing of vanadium slag. The material exhibits a typical refractory, polymetallic and intergrown mineralogical character. Representative samples were obtained by multi-point sampling using the cross-quadrant (quartering) method, thoroughly homogenised, and subsequently stored in vacuum-sealed polyethylene bags at room temperature in the dark to preclude secondary oxidation.

High-purity industrial oxygen (O2, ≥99.5 vol%), ozone (O3, generated in situ using a GCLWZY-05 ozone generator) and compressed air were used as the gas sources in the oxygen-containing-atmosphere experiments. The principal chemical reagents included sodium fluoride (NaF, analytical reagent (AR) grade, ≥98.0%, Tianjin Fuchen Chemical Reagents Co., Ltd., China), ammonium persulfate ((NH4)2S2O8, AR grade, ≥98.5%, Tianjin Jindong Tianzheng Fine Chemical Reagent Factory, China), and concentrated sulfuric acid (H2SO4, AR grade, 95.0–98.0 wt%, Chengdu Jinshan Chemical Reagents Co., Ltd., China). Spherical micron-sized zero-valent iron powder (ZVI, purity ≥ 99.5%, mean particle size ≈48 μm, Shanghai Zhenghuang Trading Co., Ltd., China) was employed as the heterogeneous catalyst. Isopropanol (IPA, ≥99.7%), methanol (MeOH, ≥99.9%, HPLC grade) and sodium azide (NaN3, ≥99.0%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and served as free-radical scavengers. All reagents were of analytical grade or higher purity and were used as received without further purification. Milli-Q ultrapure water (resistivity 18.25 MΩ·cm at 25 °C) was used throughout all experiments.

Experimental procedures

Pretreatment of V-MR

To minimise batch-to-batch fluctuations in the physicochemical properties of the feedstock and to avoid interference from the intrinsic magnetic iron-bearing minerals on the downstream recovery of the externally added ZVI, the raw V-MR was subjected to a three-stage pretreatment consisting of dehydration, sieving and dry magnetic separation. Specifically, the raw material was first dried in a vacuum oven at 373 K for 48 h to ensure the complete removal of both adsorbed and crystal water; it was then sieved through a 100-mesh standard screen (aperture 150 μm) to obtain a uniform particle size distribution; and was finally passed through a NdFeB high-intensity dry magnetic separator (magnetic flux density 0.35 T) to remove magnetite and semi-magnetic constituents. This pretreatment strategy effectively ensures the spatial separability between the externally added ZVI and the endogenous magnetic species of the feedstock during the subsequent ZVI-catalysed advanced oxidation leaching, thereby safeguarding the accuracy and reproducibility of the determination of vanadium leaching efficiency and iron recovery.

Leaching procedure

Leaching experiments were performed in a custom-built ultrasound-assisted, zero-valent-iron-activated advanced oxidation reaction system referred to as US/ZVI-AOPs, which comprised a 500 mL cylindrical polypropylene reactor, a power-tunable ultrasonic generator model KQ-250DE with an output range of 0 to 250 W at 40 kHz manufactured by Kunshan Shumei Ultrasonic Instrument Co., Ltd., China, an overhead mechanical stirrer model IKA RW 20 digital manufactured by IKA, Germany, and a thermostatic water-bath jacket; a schematic of the reaction system is presented in Fig. 1. In a typical experiment, a pre-formulated sulfuric acid solution was sequentially charged into the reactor together with defined amounts of ZVI, ammonium persulfate, and NaF. Ultrasonication at 150 W and mechanical stirring at 400 rpm were then initiated, and the system was preheated to the target temperature of 333 K and held for 5 min to achieve solid–liquid–gas three-phase pre-activation. A 5.00 g portion of the V-MR sample was subsequently introduced at a liquid-to-solid ratio of 10 to 1 in mL g−1, which marked the start of the reaction. The temperature was maintained at 333 ± 1 K using an external circulating water bath and was monitored in real time with a K-type thermocouple. Once the prescribed reaction time had elapsed, the ultrasonication and the stirrer were switched off immediately and the reaction slurry was rapidly quenched in an ice-water bath.

Schematic illustration of the efficient vanadium leaching enabled by synergistic interactions in the US/ZVI-AOPs system.

After termination of the reaction, the residual ZVI was recovered in situ from the slurry by magnetic separation using a NdFeB magnet (0.5 T) wrapped in food-grade polyethylene cling film, and the recovered ZVI was rinsed three times with ultrapure water to remove adsorbed leachate residues. The remaining slurry was separated into solid and liquid fractions by vacuum filtration through a 0.45 μm polyethersulfone (PES) membrane. The recovered ZVI was dried in a vacuum oven at 298 K for 24 h and stored hermetically, whereas the leaching residue was dried in a forced-air oven at 333 K for 24 h, ground, passed through a 200-mesh sieve, and used for subsequent characterisation and vanadium content determination.

As a key reference to highlight the advantages of the proposed process and to gain deeper insight into its operating mechanism, parallel experiments were conducted in which (NH4)2S2O8 was replaced by oxygen-containing atmospheres. In these experiments, ZVI and NaF powder were added to a beaker containing H2SO4 solution of a prescribed concentration, and the suspension was preheated in an ultrasonic cleaner. Once the target temperature was reached, the V-MR sample was introduced at an L/S ratio of 10:1. Oxygen-containing gas from different sources (compressed air, O2 or O3) was then continuously sparged into the reaction system, while the ultrasonic device and the mechanical stirrer (400 rpm) were switched on simultaneously. After the designated leaching time, the gas valve, the ultrasonic device and the stirrer were switched off to terminate the experiment. Radical quenching experiments

To identify and quantitatively evaluate the reactive oxygen species (ROS) that govern vanadium leaching in the US/ZVI-AOPs system, the classical selective radical scavenging approach was adopted. Based on the differences in the second-order rate constants between each scavenger and its target radical, isopropanol (IPA), methanol (MeOH) and sodium azide (NaN3) were employed to selectively quench hydroxyl radicals (•OH), sulfate radicals (SO4•−) and singlet oxygen (1O2), respectively. The relative contributions of the individual radicals to the vanadium leaching process were then quantitatively assessed from the corresponding variations in oxidation efficiency [34].

Analytical methods

Determination of vanadium concentration

The concentration of vanadium in the leaching solution was determined by redox titration using a standardised ammonium ferrous sulfate ((NH4)2Fe(SO4)2) solution as the titrant [35]. In a typical procedure, a 4.0 mL aliquot of the leachate was first pipetted into a 250 mL conical flask. Then, 15 mL of a sulfuric acid–phosphoric acid mixture was added to digest the sample and to establish the strongly acidic medium required for the subsequent redox reaction. To ensure the complete reduction of all vanadium species from V(V) to V(IV), 3 mL of a concentrated (NH4)2Fe(SO4)2 solution was introduced, thereby establishing a stable baseline for the ensuing titration.

The resulting solution was titrated with a standardised potassium permanganate (KMnO4) solution until a persistent purplish-red endpoint was attained and remained stable for at least 60 s, indicating the complete oxidation of vanadium to the +V state. To avoid interference from manganese ions in the following titration with ammonium ferrous sulfate, a C6H16N4 solution was added dropwise until decolourisation was achieved, whereby the manganese ions were effectively complexed and masked.

Subsequently, 6 mL of phosphoric acid (H3PO4) was introduced to remove any residual C6H16N4 and to maintain an acidic environment favourable for the final titration. The solution was then titrated with standardised (NH4)2Fe(SO4)2 using N-phenylanthranilic acid as an indicator, with the endpoint marked by a sharp colour transition to bright green, signifying the completion of the redox reaction. EPR analysis

To probe the origin of the synergistic effect in the US/ZVI-AOPs system, in situ electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spin-trapping measurements were performed on a Bruker A300 spectrometer (Bruker, Germany) to qualitatively identify and quantitatively evaluate the free radicals generated in the reaction system, and their evolution under different operating parameters was further examined. SO4•− and •OH were considered as the dominant reactive species during persulfate activation. In addition, the non-radical oxidation pathway mediated by 1O2 was also investigated, given its stronger resistance to matrix interference, higher oxidant utilisation efficiency and greater reaction selectivity [36].

Other characterisation techniques

The raw material, leaching residues and reaction products were systematically characterised by a combination of complementary techniques. Fe K-edge X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) spectra were collected in transmission mode at the BL14W1 beamline of the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF). The micromorphology of the samples was observed under vacuum by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JSM-7100F, JEOL, Japan), and the corresponding elemental distributions were mapped by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). The chemical valence states of the key elements were determined by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, PHI-5000 VersaProbe II, ULVAC-PHI, Japan). The phase composition was identified by X-ray diffraction (XRD, D8 Advance, Bruker, Germany; Cu Kα radiation, λ = 1.5418 Å). The content in the residue was analyzed by X-ray fluorescence spectroscopy (XRF, PANalytical, Netherlands). The specific surface area was analyzed using a physical adsorption analyzer (BET, Micromeritics ASAP 2020, America). The particle size distribution was measured using a laser particle size analyser (Mastersizer 3000, Malvern Panalytical, UK). In addition, the mineralogical occurrence of the target elements in the feedstock was resolved using an automated mineralogy system (AMICS, Zeiss Sigma 500, Carl Zeiss, Germany). This multi-technique strategy, covering micromorphology, elemental distribution, phase composition, chemical valence and mineralogical features, provided systematic experimental evidence from multiple perspectives and laid a solid analytical foundation for elucidating the underlying reaction mechanism.

Results and discussion

Characterization of V-MR and ZVI

The local coordination environments of Fe and Ti were systematically resolved by synchrotron X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) spectroscopy, and the results are presented in Fig. 2. The Fe K-edge X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) spectra (Fig. 2a) show that the absorption edge of the V-MR falls between those of the reference Fe foil (Fe0), FeO (Fe2+), and Fe2O3 (Fe3+) references, indicating that Fe exists in a mixed Fe2+/Fe3+ valence state. The Ti K-edge XANES spectrum (Fig. 2d) exhibits pre-edge and main-edge features in highly consistent with those of TiO2 (anatase), confirming that Ti is predominantly present as Ti4+. The Fourier-transformed k3-weighted EXAFS spectra (Fig. 2b and e) together with the corresponding wavelet-transform plots (Fig. 2c and f) further resolve the radial distribution and identity of the coordinating atoms. Signals in the region <5 Å−1 arise predominantly from backscattering contributions of light elements (O), whereas scattering peaks in the high-k region (6–12 Å−1) can be assigned to heavier neighbors such as Fe, Ti, and Ca, thereby confirming the presence of heteronuclear linkages including Fe–Si, Ti–Si, and Ti–O–Ca.

Least-squares fitting of the EXAFS data (Table 2) provides a quantitative description of the refined coordination structures of Fe and Ti. In the V-MR sample, Fe is predominantly coordinated to oxygen atoms (Fe–O, R = 1.94 Å, C.N. ≈ 5), with a secondary Fe–Si path (R = 2.78 Å) and two Fe–O–Fe multiple-scattering paths (R = 3.01 and 3.29 Å), indicating a strong chemical linkage between Fe sites and the silicate matrix. Such Fe/Ti–silicate bonding together with Ca-bridged linkages implies a stable interfacial chemical environment that may enhance the mechanical strength or catalytic activity of the material.

The mineralogical occurrence of V-bearing phases in V-MR was quantitatively characterized at the micrometer scale by AMICS analysis, and the results are summarized in Table 3. Vanadium is predominantly hosted in three major V-bearing phases: sodium vanadate (Na3VO4, 29.02% of the total V), calcium vanadate (Ca3(VO4)2, 28.19%), and chromohercynite (FeCr2O4, 18.22%). Minor V-bearing phases include pseudobrookite (Fe2TiO5), ferric vanadate (FeVO4), and vanadium–iron spinel (FeV2O4). Inclusion statistics reveal that all V-bearing phases are markedly encapsulated by Fe2O3 and Fe2SiO4; for example, 20.77% of the chromohercynite grains are enclosed by Fe2SiO4 and 15.27% are enveloped by Fe2O3. These textural features clearly demonstrate that the kinetic bottleneck of the hydrometallurgical leaching of V from V-MR does not reside in the intrinsic dissolution of the V-bearing phases themselves, but rather in the shielding of the reaction interface by the surrounding dense iron-oxide/iron-silicate encapsulation. Accordingly, the synergistic oxidative disruption of the Fe-based encapsulating layers to expose the embedded V-bearing active sites constitutes the key scientific issue in designing an efficient leaching process.

Synergistic effect of ZVI and (NH4)2S2O8

To further elucidate the key role of ammonium persulfate in the ZVI/ammonium persulfate system, experiments with varying ammonium persulfate dosages were conducted. Fig. 4(a) systematically examines the effect of ammonium persulfate concentration ranging from 0 to 4 wt% on the vanadium leaching efficiency and ZVI recovery rate under 5 wt% sulfuric acid at 333 K for 15 min with 2 wt% ZVI. In the absence of ammonium persulfate, the vanadium leaching efficiency is only 51.72%, indicating that the oxidation capability of the pure ZVI/acid system is insufficient to disrupt the encapsulating structures of the V-bearing minerals. Upon the addition of 1 wt% ammonium persulfate, the vanadium leaching efficiency markedly rises to 70.11%. Under these conditions, ZVI activates persulfate via electron transfer, followed by surface-catalyzed decomposition, continuously generating SO4•− with a high oxidation potential (Eq. (3), (4)).

Effects of different experimental conditions on V leaching efficiency and ZVI recovery. (a) (NH4)2S2O8 concentration; (b) gas atmosphere; (c) ZVI dosage (in the (NH4)2S2O8 system); and (d) ROS generation and ZVI recovery under different enhancement strategies.

However, further increases in ammonium persulfate concentration from 1 to 4 wt% give rise to a gradual decline in vanadium leaching efficiency, dropping to 68.16% at 4 wt% ammonium persulfate, which is attributed to the excessive generation of dissolved ferrous ions at concentrations above 1 wt%. The excess ferrous ions then react with SO4•−, resulting in the scavenging of SO4•− (Eq. (5)) [37]. Consequently, an optimum ammonium persulfate dosage exists that simultaneously provides sufficient oxidation capacity while maintaining a balance between the ZVI activation rate and the effective utilization of the reactive radicals.

The line plot in Fig. 4 (a) depicts the variation of ZVI recovery with (NH4)2S2O8 concentration. In the low (NH4)2S2O8 range (0–1 wt%), ZVI recovery remains relatively stable, suggesting that the consumption of ZVI and its catalytic activation of (NH4)2S2O8 reach a preliminary dynamic balance. As the (NH4)2S2O8 concentration is further increased, the ZVI recovery gradually decreases, most likely because the higher concentrations of SO4•− produced under these conditions markedly accelerate the corrosion and oxidative dissolution of ZVI (Eq. (3)). As the activator, ZVI itself is continuously oxidized to Fe2+ and Fe3+ by these radicals and by dissolved O2 and H+ in the system, leading to substantial loss of its solid phase. Considering both the high V leaching efficiency and an acceptable level of catalyst consumption, 1 wt% (NH4)2S2O8 was selected for subsequent experiments.

As a typical persulfate compound, ammonium persulfate releases approximately 7% active oxygen upon decomposition in aqueous solution, as described by Eq. (6) [38]. To determine whether externally supplied oxygen or the active oxygen derived from ammonium persulfate decomposition exerts a more favorable effect on vanadium leaching, the comparative experiments shown in Fig. 4(b) and (c) were performed. Fig. 4(b) presents the effect of various gas-flow rates under the baseline conditions of 333 K for 15 min with 2 wt% ZVI. The vanadium leaching efficiency is enhanced significantly by sparging either air or pure oxygen relative to the no-gas case, with oxygen being more effective than air. The highest leaching rate of 43.96% is reached at an oxygen flow rate of 300 mL·min−1, markedly higher than the 31.5% obtained under no-gas conditions. This confirms that the introduction of external oxygen effectively strengthens the system: on the one hand, dissolved oxygen accelerates ZVI corrosion and the continuous production of ferrous ions to sustain oxygen activation; on the other, oxygen itself participates in radical chain reactions, as described by Eqs. (7), (8), (9), (10) [39]. When the flow rate is further increased to 400 mL·min−1, however, the leaching efficiency levels off, suggesting that the mass transfer and consumption of dissolved oxygen have reached a dynamic balance and that excess gas agitation leads to heat loss and radical quenching, which offsets further benefits.

Fig. 4(c) focuses on the reactive oxygen contribution arising from the self-decomposition of ammonium persulfate. Under identical baseline conditions without external gas flow, the effect of ZVI dosage ranging from 0 to 2 wt% on the system containing 1 wt% ammonium persulfate was systematically investigated. The vanadium leaching efficiency reaches its maximum of approximately 70% at 2 wt% ZVI, far exceeding the 52.36% obtained with oxygen sparging at 300 mL·min−1. This pronounced difference clearly indicates that the reactive oxygen species released by ammonium persulfate decomposition in the ZVI-AOPs system, predominantly SO4•−, exhibit much higher oxidative efficiency than that afforded by the direct introduction of external oxygen. The high efficiency of ammonium persulfate originates from the abundant, strongly oxidative radicals generated upon its activation, which directly attack the mineral lattice. Although external oxygen promotes ZVI corrosion and radical chain reactions, its inherent oxidation potential is relatively low, and the yield and reaction pathways of the derived reactive oxygen species such as O2•− and •OH may be kinetically limited. Therefore, for the vanadium leaching process studied here, optimization of the ammonium persulfate concentration to fully exploit its endogenously generated reactive oxygen species represents the more effective pathway to efficient oxidation.

Fig. 4(d) visualizes via dual vertical axes the coupled effect of different enhancement strategies on ROS generation and ZVI recovery under acid-free conditions at 333 K for 15 min. The left Y-axis represented by a bar plot quantifies the ROS concentration in mg·L−1, while the right Y-axis represented by a line plot displays the ZVI recovery in percent. In the blank experiment with no oxidant added, the dissolved O2 originally present in water is activated by ZVI to generate a small amount of ROS at approximately 66 mg·L−1, with the ZVI recovery close to 90%, indicating that the inevitable consumption of ZVI during operation is negligible. Air sparging raises the ROS concentration slightly to 69 mg·L−1, while the ZVI recovery remains high at 88%. Under an O2 atmosphere, the ROS concentration increases significantly to 74 mg·L−1 and the ZVI recovery decreases slightly to 86%. Upon introduction of ammonium persulfate as the chemical activator, the ROS concentration jumps to 89 mg·L−1 while the ZVI recovery is still stabilized at 85%. Overall, different gaseous atmospheres provide only limited enhancement of ROS generation, whereas chemical activation by ammonium persulfate substantially strengthens ROS production. Notably, the ZVI recovery remains above 85% for every enhancement strategy, demonstrating that efficient ROS generation can be achieved concomitantly with good recyclability of ZVI.

Effect of the leaching medium and ultrasonic power

In hydrometallurgical treatment, strong acid and elevated temperature are required to dissolve the iron-based encapsulation, while NaF is further added to attack the silicate encapsulation by breaking the Si–O bonds. Therefore, univariate experiments on acidity, temperature, and NaF dosage were performed to optimize the leaching system, which then served as the basis for elucidating the role of ultrasound in the newly developed process under optimal conditions [40].

NaF is crucial for effectively disrupting the dense silicate encapsulation in V-bearing metallurgical residues and thereby enabling the efficient extraction of vanadium. Fig. 5(a) reveals a non-linear optimization relationship between NaF dosage and vanadium leaching efficiency. Under 5 wt% sulfuric acid for 15 min with 2 wt% ZVI at 333 K and 1 wt% ammonium persulfate, the vanadium leaching efficiency first rises sharply, then plateaus, and finally decreases as the NaF dosage is increased from 0 to 4 wt%. Specifically, when the NaF concentration increases from 0 to approximately 1 wt%, the vanadium leaching efficiency rises from 70.11% to 77.65%. This marked improvement directly demonstrates the effective disruption of the silicate encapsulation by fluoride ions, which attack the Si–O bonds via coordination, exposing the internal V-bearing active sites, overcoming the mass-transfer limitation, and enabling efficient attack of the oxidative radicals, specifically SO4•− and •OH, on the target minerals. However, above 1 wt% NaF, the leaching curve plateaus, which implies that the leaching-promotion effect has become saturated. Excess fluoride ions adsorb onto the mineral surfaces, forming new mass-transfer barriers and potentially quenching part of the radicals, which gives rise to an excessive effect [41]. Accordingly, 1 wt% NaF was identified as the optimum dosage for the present system.

Role of key process parameters in the extraction efficiency of V and ZVI recovery. (a) NaF dosage; (b) temperature; (c) H2SO4 concentration; and (d) ultrasonic power.

Temperature is an external variable governing reaction kinetics, mass-transfer processes, and the stability of reactive species; it is therefore critical to the optimization of the efficiency and selectivity of the ZVI-AOPs leaching system. Fig. 5(b) systematically examines the coupled effect of temperature on vanadium leaching efficiency and ZVI recovery under 5 wt% sulfuric acid with 1 wt% NaF for 15 min using 2 wt% ZVI and 1 wt% ammonium persulfate. Markedly asymmetric dependences of vanadium leaching efficiency and ZVI recovery on temperature are observed. In the range of 293 to 323 K, the vanadium leaching efficiency increases from 77.39% to 80.38%, as elevated temperatures effectively accelerate the kinetics of key steps such as ZVI corrosion and persulfate activation, thereby promoting the generation and mass transfer of the reactive oxygen species, specifically SO4•− and •OH. However, a further increase to 343 K causes the vanadium leaching efficiency to drop sharply to 72.89%, chiefly because the thermal activation of persulfate at elevated temperatures leads to the over-decomposition of ammonium persulfate. The ZVI recovery concomitantly exhibits a single-peak profile, it reaches a maximum of approximately 52.86% at 333 K and then decreases significantly to approximately 49.09% at 343 K. This is attributed to insufficient ZVI corrosion at low temperatures and intensified oxidative dissolution at elevated temperatures, in combination with the possible formation of more stable oxide layers or particle agglomeration that lowers the effective recovery [42]. Thus, 323 K was determined as the optimum temperature for the present system.

The sulfuric acid concentration is the key chemical parameter controlling acid-leaching kinetics, the surface charge of the minerals, and the radical-reaction environment, and thus has a decisive effect on vanadium leaching efficiency and ZVI recovery in the ZVI-AOPs system. Fig. 5(c) systematically examines the coupled effect of sulfuric acid concentration ranging from 0 to 20 wt% on vanadium leaching efficiency and ZVI recovery at a fixed dosage of 1 wt% ammonium persulfate, 1 wt% NaF, and 2 wt% ZVI at 323 K. The vanadium leaching efficiency first increases rapidly and then declines sharply with increasing sulfuric acid concentration, whereas the ZVI recovery decreases continuously. Specifically, as the sulfuric acid concentration increases from 0 to 5 wt%, the vanadium leaching efficiency rises rapidly from 60.31% to 80.38%, indicating that moderate acidification effectively promotes corrosion of the ZVI surface, accelerates the activation of ammonium persulfate, and enhances penetration of radicals through the silicate encapsulation. Above 5 wt%, the increase in vanadium leaching efficiency stagnates and is clearly suppressed with further concentration increases from 10 to 20 wt%, ultimately dropping to 63.61% at 20 wt%. This decline originates from the fact that an excessively low pH prevents the effective decomposition of persulfate, as described by Eqs. (11), (12), (13) [43]. Meanwhile, the ZVI recovery decreases continuously from approximately 98.8% at 0 wt% acid to 29.69% at 20 wt% acid, indicating that enhanced acidity significantly exacerbates the dissolution loss and agglomeration or precipitation of ZVI, thereby lowering its recycling efficiency. Overall, a sulfuric acid concentration of 5 wt% was identified as the most appropriate for practical application.

Ultrasonic power as the key parameter regulating cavitation intensity and mass-transfer efficiency exerts a significant influence on both the leaching kinetics and the catalyst stability of the US/ZVI-AOPs system. Fig. 5(d) systematically illustrates the coupled effect of ultrasonic power ranging from 0 to 50% of rated output on vanadium leaching efficiency shown as a bar plot on the left Y-axis and ZVI recovery shown as a line plot on the right Y-axis at a fixed dosage of 1 wt% (NH4)2S2O8, 1 wt% NaF, 5 wt% sulfuric acid, and 2 wt% ZVI at 323 K. The vanadium leaching efficiency first rises and then plateaus with increasing ultrasonic power. As the power is raised from 0 to 75 W, the vanadium leaching efficiency progressively climbs from approximately 80.38% to a peak of approximately 91.57%, demonstrating that moderate ultrasonic cavitation enhances local turbulence, accelerates ZVI corrosion and dissolution and the activation of ammonium persulfate, and promotes the generation of SO4•− and •OH, while physically breaking up the encapsulating layers to expose and dissolve vanadium. Above 75 W, the vanadium leaching efficiency increases only slightly, reaching approximately 90.97% at 100 W and approximately 89.62% at 125 W, indicating that the reaction rate approaches kinetic saturation, or is limited by the structural stability of the minerals and the utilization efficiency of the radicals. Meanwhile, the ZVI recovery slowly decreases with increasing ultrasonic power, from approximately 59.1% at 75 W to 50.43% at 125 W. This is attributed to the intensified particle collisions, agglomeration, and oxidative dissolution of ZVI under stronger cavitation, leading to the loss of part of the iron-bearing products. Overall, a 75 W ultrasonic power was considered the most suitable for practical application.

Kinetic analysis of the vanadium leaching reaction

In the US-enhanced ZVI/(NH4)2S2O8 advanced oxidation process (US/ZVI-AOPs), the oxidative dissociation of V-bearing residues follows a heterogeneous reaction kinetic model, with the overall rate governed by the coupled effects of three stages: liquid-film diffusion, solid (product) film diffusion, and interfacial chemical reaction. Under low-acid and low-NaF conditions, the V-bearing mineral particles can be approximated as solid spheres. In this case, the interfacial chemical reaction or solid-film (internal) diffusion may act as the rate-controlling step, and the process conforms to the shrinking unreacted core model, in which the reaction front progresses from the particle surface towards its core and the reaction rate is inversely related to the thickness of the product layer. The kinetic equations of the three typical control mechanisms are as follows [44], [45]:

(1) Interfacial chemical reaction control (Ea > 40 kJ·mol−1):

1 − (1 − x)1/3 = k1t (14).

(2) Internal diffusion control (Ea = 4–20 kJ·mol−1):

1 − (2/3)x − (1 − x)2/3 = k2t (15).

(3) Mixed control (Ea = 20–40 kJ·mol−1):

1 + 2(1 − x) − 3(1 − x)2/3 = k3t (16).where x is the V leaching efficiency, t is the reaction time, and k is the apparent rate constant. Under 5 wt% H2SO4, 1 wt% ZVI, 1 wt% (NH4)2S2O8, and 1 wt% NaF, kinetic experiments were performed at four temperatures (293, 303, 313, and 323 K) for both processes, and the results are presented in Fig. 6. The leaching efficiency of both systems increases monotonically with temperature and time, a behavior characteristic of a thermally activated solid–liquid reaction.

Effect of temperature on V leaching efficiency: (a) US/ZVI-AOPs and (b) ZVI-AOPs.

The kinetic data obtained at different temperatures (Fig. 6) were substituted into the three kinetic equations (Eqs. (14)–(16)) for linear fitting, and the results are shown in Fig. 7, Fig. 8. The slopes of the linear fits yielded the apparent rate constants k, from which the apparent activation energy Ea was calculated via the Arrhenius equation. To further validate the model selection, the kinetic parameters obtained from the linear fittings of the models at different temperatures were summarized in Table 4. The diffusion-controlled model exhibited the highest correlation coefficients for both the US/ZVI-AOP and ZVI-AOP systems, providing preliminary evidence that the leaching process is rate-limited by intraparticle diffusion.

The kinetic analysis shows that the apparent activation energies under the interfacial-chemical-reaction-control model are 8.81 kJ·mol−1 for US/ZVI-AOPs and 10.81 kJ·mol−1 for ZVI-AOPs, both far below the 40 kJ·mol−1 threshold typical of chemical-reaction control; chemical reaction can therefore be excluded as the rate-controlling step. Under the internal-diffusion-control model, the Ea values of US/ZVI-AOPs and ZVI-AOPs are 5.05 and 18.26 kJ·mol−1, respectively, both falling within the typical range of diffusion control (4–20 kJ·mol−1). Hence, the V leaching processes in both systems are controlled by the internal diffusion step. Notably, the apparent activation energy of the US-enhanced process (5.05 kJ·mol−1) is significantly lower than that of the conventional process (18.26 kJ·mol−1), corresponding to a decrease of approximately 72%. The marked decrease in Ea indicates that ultrasonic cavitation lowers the diffusion barrier by continuously disrupting the silicate product layer and increasing its porosity, which constitutes the kinetic origin of the enhanced leaching performance.

It should be noted that the classical shrinking core model (SCM) assumes ideal spherical particles and a homogeneous product layer, representing a simplification for the heterogeneous, multi-layered V-MR slag. Nevertheless, the SCM has been extensively adopted to describe the leaching kinetics of metal values from metallurgical residues under ultrasonic irradiation. For example, Wang et al. employed the SCM to analyze the ultrasound-enhanced sulfuric acid leaching kinetics of Zn, Ge and Mg from lead–zinc smelting neutralization residue [46], and Nie et al. applied the same model to interpret the ultrasound-assisted citric acid leaching kinetics of Cr from steel slag [47]. In this study, the diffusion-control SCM affords the best linear fit among the three candidate models and yields an apparent activation energy of 5.05 kJ·mol−1, which falls within the characteristic range for internal diffusion control (4–20 kJ·mol−1). Thus, the SCM is deemed suitable for a semi-quantitative characterization of the vanadium leaching process. Ultrasonic cavitation reduces the diffusion barrier, irrespective of the empirical model used.

Evolution behavior of ZVI and V-MR during leaching

The physicochemical evolution of ZVI and V-MR before and after leaching was comparatively investigated for both the ZVI-AOPs and US/ZVI-AOPs systems. Fig. 9(a) systematically compares the V leaching efficiency and ZVI recovery of the US-enhanced process (US/ZVI-AOPs) and the conventional process (ZVI-AOPs) at different ZVI dosages. Under 5 wt% H2SO4, 323 K, 1 wt% NaF, 1 wt% (NH4)2S2O8, and 15 min, the US/ZVI-AOPs process consistently exhibits significantly higher V leaching performance than the conventional process across ZVI dosages from 0.5 to 2 wt%. At a ZVI dosage of 1 wt%, ηV reaches 92.53% for US/ZVI-AOPs but only 69.10% for ZVI-AOPs; at 2 wt%, the corresponding values are 91.57% vs 81.63%. These results demonstrate that ultrasonic cavitation effectively enhances the solid–liquid mass transfer, promotes surface activation of ZVI, and facilitates ROS generation, thereby disrupting the encapsulating structure of the minerals and markedly improving V dissolution. In parallel, the ZVI recovery increases moderately with increasing dosage, yet the US-enhanced process still maintains a higher recovery at the same dosage (e.g., 58.9% vs 46.74% at 2 wt% ZVI), indicating that ultrasound not only enhances the reaction efficiency but also suppresses ZVI agglomeration and over-corrosion, preserving catalyst recyclability. The residual mass after leaching ranged from 3.67 to 4.17 g (73.4–83.5% of the 5 g raw V-MR), with the optimal 2 wt% ZVI dosage yielding 3.92 g of residue (78.5% retention), indicating that most gangue components are retained in the solid phase while vanadium is selectively leached.

Fig. 9(b) shows the XRD patterns of ZVI under different treatment conditions, used to characterize the phase evolution and structural stability of ZVI after the US/ZVI-AOPs and conventional ZVI-AOPs treatments. The raw ZVI exhibits the typical α-Fe crystal structure (2θ ≈ 44.7°, 65.0°) with no obvious oxide impurity peaks, confirming the high-purity metallic-iron nature of the starting material. After ZVI-AOPs treatment, pronounced Fe2O3 and Fe3O4 characteristic peaks (asterisks, 2θ ≈ 35.5°) appear, while the α-Fe peak intensities weaken markedly, indicating that ZVI undergoes partial oxidation in the acidic oxidative environment, forming an iron-oxide passivation layer that may hinder electron transfer and reduce catalytic activity. During leaching, the dynamic evolution of Fe-ion speciation and concentration directly reflects the reactivity of ZVI and the oxidative capacity of the system. As shown in Fig. 9(c), the Fe2+ concentration in the ZVI-AOPs system increases only slowly with leaching time, indicating that the ferrous ions are released through a slow oxidation reaction limited by solid–liquid mass transfer and the formation of a surface passivation layer. In contrast, Fig. 9(d) shows that in the US/ZVI-AOPs system, the Fe2+ concentration rises rapidly at the initial stage and reaches a higher plateau within a shorter time. This is attributed to the ability of ultrasonic cavitation to strip the oxide passivation layer from the iron surface, markedly enlarging the effective ZVI–solution contact area and enhancing the reaction kinetics, thereby accelerating the generation of Fe2+. The comparison between the two systems demonstrates that US enhancement not only improves the effective utilization of iron but also regulates the release rate and total amount of Fe ions, providing more abundant reductants and catalytic active sites for the subsequent advanced oxidation process and thereby enhancing the overall leaching efficiency. This finding reveals the key role of ultrasound in ZVI-AOPs for promoting iron activation and ion migration, and provides mechanistic support for its application in the efficient leaching of refractory minerals.

SEM and EDS analyses collectively reveal the ultrasound-induced stripping of the passivation layer on ZVI and its dual impact on particle dispersion and surface oxidation state. As shown in Fig. 10(a), conventional ZVI-AOPs treatment leads to severe particle agglomeration at low magnification, with high-magnification imaging revealing a continuous, thick, and smooth oxide passivation layer that fully covers the surface and leaves no fresh ZVI core exposed. In contrast, ultrasound-enhanced ZVI-AOPs treatment (Fig. 10(b)) significantly improves dispersion, reduces cluster size, and exposes a rough, porous, and partially stripped surface where the original oxide layer exhibits cracks and flaky detachment, revealing sharp-edged fresh ZVI cores. This morphological transition confirms that ultrasonic cavitation disrupts the protective oxide layer via physical impact and microjets, restoring surface reactivity.

The particle-size distribution analysis (Fig. 13(g)) shows that the particle size of the leaching residue after US/ZVI-AOPs treatment shifts markedly toward finer sizes, with the main peak concentrated in the 10–50 μm range and a significant increase in the fraction of fine particles, whereas the ZVI-AOPs residue exhibits a broader distribution skewed toward sizes above 100 μm. This particle-size refinement can be attributed to the strong shear forces and shock waves induced by ultrasonic cavitation, which effectively break up large agglomerates and thereby increase the specific surface area and reactivity of the residue.

The XRD patterns in Fig. 13(h) further reveal the phase evolution: the raw V-MR exhibits characteristic diffraction peaks of FeCr2O4, NaAlSi3O8, Fe2SiO4, FeTiO3, and Fe2O3. After ZVI-AOPs treatment, the intensities of these crystalline peaks weaken, indicating limited mineral dissolution under the conventional process; in contrast, after US/ZVI-AOPs treatment, the diffraction peaks of the primary mineral phases almost vanish, leaving only weak Fe2O3 and FeTiO3 peaks, demonstrating that ultrasound greatly enhances mineral dissociation and dissolution. Beyond the accelerated release of Fe2+ into the bulk solution, ultrasonic cavitation plays a pivotal role in modulating the heterogeneous interfacial reactions on the ZVI surface. As evidenced by the XPS and XRD results, ultrasound effectively strips the passivation layers, continuously exposing pristine ZVI cores. These freshly exposed surfaces serve as highly active catalytic sites for the heterogeneous activation of persulfate. Distinct from homogeneous activation, the heterogeneous pathway utilizes surface-bound Fe(II) to cleave S2O82− at the ZVI interface, localizing SO4•− generation [49]. Ultrasound synergistically sustains this interface while supplying Fe2+, mitigating radical quenching and ensuring targeted oxidation of V-bearing encapsulations.

Mechanism analysis

Synergistic effect of US and ZVI in the AOPs system

To clarify the synergistic effect between US and ZVI, comparative experiments between different processes were carried out under the previously optimized conditions. Fig. 14(a) presents, via bar plots, the V leaching efficiency of different systems (blank experiment, ZVI-AOPs, US-AOPs, and US/ZVI-AOPs) at 5 and 30 min. The ZVI-AOPs system reaches ηV of ∼85.23% at 5 min and ∼84.07% at 30 min; the US/ZVI-AOPs system already achieves nearly 60% at 5 min and jumps to ∼93.83% at 30 min, a value significantly higher than that of the ZVI-AOPs system. In contrast, ηV of the Fe-free blank and US-AOPs systems remains lower than that of the Fe-containing systems at the same time points, confirming that ZVI is the key mediator for efficient V leaching. Notably, the introduction of ultrasound not only shortens the time required to reach a high ηV (pronounced advantage already at 5 min), but also pushes the final ηV to a higher level at 30 min, indicating a clear US–ZVI synergy: ultrasound accelerates the corrosion of the ZVI surface and the release of Fe2+ via cavitation, which sustains the continuous activation of (NH4)2S2O8 and thereby enhances radical generation and the oxidative dissolution of V. These observations also provide experimental support for the subsequent EPR analysis and mechanistic verification.

The EPR signal intensities of •OH and SO4•− are consistently higher in the US/ZVI-AOPs system than in the ZVI-AOPs system at every sampled time, and both signals intensify as the reaction proceeds, indicating that the US–ZVI synergy continuously promotes the generation of •OH and SO4•−. Fig. 14(f) quantitatively characterizes the time-dependent •OH/SO4•− concentrations via bar plots. In the ZVI-AOPs system, the concentration increases from 1.218 × 10−5 mol·L−1 at 1 min to 3.718 × 10−5 mol·L−1 at 15 min, whereas in the US/ZVI-AOPs system it reaches 2.265 × 10−5 mol·L−1 at 1 min and rises to 6.912 × 10−5 mol·L−1 at 15 min, exceeding that of the ZVI-AOPs system at all time points. In combination with the dynamic evolution of the EPR signals, these findings reveal that the US–ZVI synergy not only increases the initial generation rate of •OH and SO4•− but also reinforces their continuous accumulation over time, thus elucidating the efficient multi-ROS generation mechanism of the US/ZVI-AOPs system from both dynamic and quantitative perspectives and providing radical-level support for its outstanding V-leaching oxidation performance.

Role of reactive radicals

Quenching experiments were conducted to probe the multistep cascade of radical and non-radical species in the US/ZVI-AOPs system. First, SO4•− produced by the activation of (NH4)2S2O8 rapidly reacts with water to form •OH, substantially enhancing the initial oxidation capacity of the system (Eq. (17)) [50]. Subsequently, in the acidic environment and in the presence of excess ROS, •OH further reacts with intermediates, inducing the formation of O2•− (Eqs. (18), (19)) [51]. O2•− serves as a precursor of 1O2 (Eqs. (20), (21)); being unstable in aqueous media, O2•− rapidly disproportionates to 1O2, which in turn indirectly suppresses the build-up of O2•− [52], [53]. These cascade transformations establish a multilevel oxidation network running from radicals (SO4•− → •OH → O2•−) to non-radicals (1O2), endowing the system with multiple advantages such as resistance to impurity interference, high oxidation selectivity, and efficient oxidant utilization.

To elucidate the actual contributions of the various species to V leaching in this multi-ROS system, selective quenching experiments were conducted for quantitative evaluation (Fig. 15). Fig. 15(a) shows that addition of methanol leads to the most pronounced decrease in ηV, indicating that radical chain oxidation mediated by SO4•− is the main driving force of the process. Notably, the addition of sodium azide also causes a clear drop in ηV, confirming the non-negligible role of the 1O2 oxidation pathway in this complex heterogeneous system. Fig. 15(b) further quantifies the contribution of each reactive species: SO4•− contributes the most (30.6%), followed by •OH (22.1%) and 1O2 (11.9%), while the remaining 35.4% is likely to originate from secondary pathways such as direct electron transfer or surface-bound radicals. This distribution clearly demonstrates that the US/ZVI-AOPs system is not a conventional homogeneous Fenton-like process dominated by •OH, but rather a composite oxidation system in which SO4•− acts as the primary oxidant, 1O2 serves as an important complement, and multiple reactive species operate synergistically. With its high redox potential (E0 = 2.5–3.1 V) and relatively long half-life, SO4•− can efficiently penetrate and disrupt the dense silicate encapsulation; 1O2 exhibits high reaction selectivity and can precisely oxidize the target V-bearing minerals against a strong interference background, while forming a dynamic transformation network with O2•− and •OH via Eqs. (20), (21), thus enabling continuous regeneration and efficient utilization of the oxidation potential.

(a) Effect of radical scavengers on the US/ZVI-AOPs process; (b) contribution of each oxidant to the oxidative leaching of V in the US/ZVI-AOPs process.

Although 1O2 contributes 11.9% to vanadium leaching, lower than the 30.6% from SO4•− and 22.1% from •OH, it provides a vital non-radical complement that enhances oxidation selectivity. The US/ZVI-AOPs system uniquely favors 1O2 generation through ultrasonic depassivation, which continuously exposes fresh ZVI surfaces to drive heterogeneous interfacial activation. This mechanism produces surface-bound SO4•− that hydrolyzes to •OH and reacts with HSO5− to form superoxide radicals. Unlike conventional systems where excess Fe2+ scavenges radicals, the balanced Fe2+/Fe3+ cycle under ultrasound permits local O2•− accumulation and its disproportionation into 1O2. Cavitation further suppresses non-selective quenching, stabilizing the non-radical pathway. This 1O2 contribution is strategically critical for breaching the dense silicate matrix, as it resists scavenging by matrix anions and selectively oxidizes V(III)/V(IV), thereby synergizing with radical pathways to maximize mineral dissociation.

Based on the above experimental results and characterization analysis, the mechanism of ultrasound-enhanced vanadium leaching was determined (Fig. 16). The US/ZVI-AOPs system achieves efficient vanadium leaching through a chemical–physical synergy. Chemically, ZVI activates S2O82− to generate SO4•−, which hydrolyzes to •OH, establishing a radical pathway; concurrently, •OH reacts with HSO5− to yield O2•− and subsequently 1O2, forming a non‑radical pathway. These multi‑pathway ROS progressively attack the V–O bonds, the silicon–oxygen framework, and Fe/Ti layers, driving the oxidation of V3+/V4+ to V5+. Physically, ultrasonic cavitation reinforces this chemistry via three pathways: (i) stripping Fe3O4/Fe2O3 passivation layers on ZVI, maintaining active Fe0 sites; (ii) disintegrating V‑MR agglomerates, enlarging the reaction interface; and (iii) direct sonolysis of H2O and S2O82−, generating additional •OH and accelerating O–O bond cleavage. This dual reinforcement simultaneously maximizes ROS output and overcomes the mass‑transfer bottleneck, accounting for the superior leaching performance.

Feasibility analysis

Energy efficiency and economic feasibility are paramount for industrial adoption of the proposed US/ZVI-AOPs process. The system consumes approximately 1.85 kWh per kg of V2O5 produced. This value represents a 42 to 85% reduction compared to conventional roasting leaching processes that require 8.5 to 12 kWh per kg and a 40 to 60% reduction compared to conventional acid leaching routes that consume 3.2 to 4.5 kWh per kg. The drastic energy saving stems from replacing high temperature roasting with ambient temperature ultrasonic activation, which minimizes thermal input while maximizing oxidative efficiency.

Economically, the operating cost for treating one ton of V-MR is estimated at USD 48.5. This expenditure is offset by a revenue of USD 111.3 derived from V2O5 recovery and an additional USD 15 from the valorization of the iron rich residue as sinter feed. The net profit yields USD 62.8 per ton of feedstock. The 58.9% ZVI recovery indicates that 41.1% of the metallic iron is converted into oxide phases during leaching. Rather than representing a pure loss, this conversion upgrades the leaching residue to a material containing 39.10% Fe. This iron enriched product is suitable for use as a sinter feed or blast furnace burden, effectively transforming a reagent expense into a byproduct credit that offsets operational costs.

Environmentally, the residual iron exists as stable hematite and magnetite encapsulated within the silicate matrix, which minimizes the risk of heavy metal leaching under ambient conditions. The integrated magnetic separation step further mitigates waste generation by recovering the majority of unconsumed ZVI for reuse within the circuit. Although ZVI attrition remains a consideration for scale up, the synergistic gains in vanadium recovery, energy conservation, and residue valorization establish a favorable techno economic and environmental profile for the US/ZVI-AOPs process.

Conclusion

A novel ultrasound-coupled zero-valent iron/ammonium persulfate advanced oxidation process referred to as US/ZVI-AOPs was developed to overcome the intrinsic kinetic bottleneck of vanadium leaching from V-MR. XAFS and AMICS analyses revealed that vanadium predominantly resides as V3+ within vanadium–iron spinel phases, which are densely enveloped by Fe2O3 and Fe2SiO4 shells with an encapsulation ratio ranging from 15.27% to 20.77%. Under optimal conditions comprising 1 wt% ZVI, 1 wt% ammonium persulfate, 1 wt% NaF, 5 wt% sulfuric acid, a temperature of 323 K, 75 W ultrasound, and a duration of 15 min, the system achieved a vanadium leaching efficiency of 91.57% while retaining a ZVI recovery of 58.9%. EPR spin-trapping and selective quenching assays identified SO4•− at 30.6%, •OH at 22.1%, and 1O2 at 11.9% as the dominant reactive species, establishing a hybrid radical–non-radical oxidation network that departs from the classical •OH dominated Fenton paradigm. Shrinking-core kinetic modeling indicated product-layer diffusion control, and ultrasonic cavitation reduced the apparent activation energy from 18.26 to 5.05 kJ mol−1 by continuously fragmenting the passivating silicate layer and enhancing its effective diffusion coefficient. The overall enhancement arises from three interlocking synergies, namely ZVI/ammonium persulfate catalysis generates a multi-pathway ROS network, cavitation depassivates ZVI and disaggregates mineral clusters, and the marked drop in activation energy simultaneously accelerates reaction kinetics and boosts ultimate extraction. This work delivers a scalable, green route for V-MR valorization and a transferable mechanistic framework for multi-field-intensified heterogeneous AOPs in hazardous solid-waste treatment.
