Cost effectiveness and public health impact of PCV20 among adults in France
Authors: Stéphane Fiévez, Mélanie Menara, Emmanuelle Blanc, Lucile Bellier, Marine Sivignon, Jeffrey Vietri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPneumococcal infections remain a significant public health concern in France, particularly among older adults at higher risk of developing severe forms. In 2023, the French health authorities updated vaccination guidelines recommending a single-dose 20-valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV20) for adults at increased risk due to underlying medical conditions and later extending it to all adults aged 65 years and over. The objective of this analysis is first to evaluate the cost-effectiveness and public health impact of PCV20 in replacing 13-valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccine → 23-valent pneumococcal unconjugated polysaccharide vaccine (PCV13 → PPV23) in the vaccination programme and secondly to assess the value of extending the recommendation to a broader population.MethodsA deterministic Markov model was adapted to compare clinical and economic outcomes of adult pneumococcal vaccination strategies in France over a lifetime horizon. The economic inputs in this analysis were estimated in 2024 euros from a healthcare system perspective. Population parameters, epidemiological data and cost were derived from French databases and institutional reports. Utility and vaccine effectiveness inputs were obtained from the literature.ResultsReplacing PCV13 → PPV23 with PCV20 in at-risk adults reduced pneumococcal disease burden and was a dominant strategy, generating cost savings of €56,786 at the population level (€1.05 per patient) while improving health outcomes with an incremental gain of 0.00015 QALYs per patient. Expanding PCV20 to all individuals aged 65+ further reduced disease incidence and mortality over a lifetime horizon, averting 900 additional invasive pneumococcal diseases (IPD) cases (mainly bacteremia and meningitis), 11,600 pneumonia cases, and over 1,500 deaths. These additional health benefits were achieved at an incremental cost of €63,191 at population level (€0.81 per patient) corresponding to a gain of 0.00019 QALYs per patient. The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio was 4,308 €/QALY, indicating highly cost-effective strategy relative to commonly cited French vaccine HTA benchmarks.ConclusionPCV20 is a cost-saving alternative to the PCV13 → PPV23 sequence for at-risk adults in France. Expanding recommendations to include all adults aged 65 years and over provides substantial public health gains and represents a highly cost-effective strategy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28336-7.Keywords: Pneumococcal vaccination, Pneumococcal disease, PCV20, Cost-effectiveness, Adults, Public health policy, France

Background

Pneumococcal infections, including both invasive pneumococcal diseases (IPD) (mainly bacteremia and meningitis) and non-bacteremic pneumococcal pneumonia (NBPP), continue to contribute significantly to morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs, particularly among older adults and individuals with pre-existing conditions [1–5].

Annually, France reports 5,000 to 6,000 cases of IPD and approximately 10,000 hospitalizations for NBPP among adults [5, 6]. Since March 2017, two pneumococcal vaccines, PCV13 and the PPV23, have been recommended as a sequence for adults at risk of pneumococcal infections, including both invasive and non-invasive forms [7]. Vaccination uptake has been suboptimal for the sequential recommendation; in 2020, only 9.9% of at-risk adults received the recommended PCV13 → PPV23 regimen, with low uptake across all age groups [8–10]. This lack of adherence may result from the perceived complexity of the vaccine schedule and unclear patient eligibility, possibly due to discrepancies between the recommendation and those for other respiratory infections such as influenza [11–13]. In July 2023, following the marketing authorization of the new 20-valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV20), which includes seven additional serotypes beyond PCV13 to address evolving pneumococcal epidemiology, the French health technology assessment body (Haute Autorité de la Santé - HAS) released updated vaccination guidelines. The HAS recommended integrating PCV20 as a single preferred vaccine for adults at risk of pneumococcal infection, according to the recommendations of the High Council of Public Health (HCSP) [14], replacing the PCV13 → PPV23 sequence and simplifying the administration schedule [15]. At the end of December 2024, the HAS extended the vaccination recommendations with PCV20 to all adults aged 65 years and over, concluding that expanding pneumococcal vaccination eligibility to all adults based solely on age, regardless of underlying conditions, simplifies the vaccination schedule and should increase vaccine coverage and enhance protection against pneumococcal infections in this particularly vulnerable age group [16].

Published economic evaluations of adult pneumococcal vaccination remain limited in France. To our knowledge, only one French economic evaluation (budget impact analysis) of adult pneumococcal vaccination strategies has been published, comparing 13-valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV13) and 23-valent pneumococcal unconjugated polysaccharide vaccine (PPV23) in at-risk populations [17].

This study aims first to demonstrate the cost-effectiveness and public health impact of PCV20 in replacing PCV13 → PPV23 only in adults with underlying conditions and then to assess the impact and economic value of the extension of the vaccination recommendation with PCV20 to all adults aged 65 years and over.

Methods

Conceptual framework and model structure

A deterministic Markov-type model, adapted from a previously published version [18–21], was developed in Microsoft Excel to evaluate the benefits of pneumococcal vaccination in the French context. The model compared clinical and economic outcomes between two cohorts of individuals aged 18 and over in year 2024, each following different vaccination strategies at the first year in the model. These cohorts were followed over a lifetime horizon, with individuals transitioning between different health states corresponding to annual occurrences of infectious events, specifically meningitis, bacteremia, and pneumonia, according to their vaccination status, as well as deaths from these infections or other causes (Fig. 1). The model also captured patients’ quality of life and the economic consequences of these infections; however, long-term sequelae resulting from these infections were not considered. Clinical outcomes (cases, life-years (LY), and Quality-Adjusted Life Years (QALYs)) were accumulated over the time horizon. LYs, QALYs and economic outcomes were discounted at an annual rate of 2.5%, decreasing to 1.5% after 30 years, per national guidelines [22]. The Incremental Cost-Effectiveness Ratio (ICER) is then estimated as the measure of the additional cost required to gain one unit of health benefit.

Target population and strategies compared

The model population was stratified by age group and risk status based on risk factors listed by the HCSP [7] and health technology assessment (HTA) decisions [15]. The high-risk group included immunocompromised individuals, while the moderate-risk group consisted of immunocompetent individuals with conditions predisposing them to severe pneumococcal infections, such as chronic cardiac, respiratory, kidney, or liver diseases, and diabetes. Individuals not classified in either of these two groups were considered low risk. Two evolutions of the French vaccination programme were investigated: 1) Historic Strategy: Replacement of the PCV13 → PPV23 sequence with PCV20 for at-risk adults (aged 18+) [15, 16] ; and 2) Extended Strategy: Expansion of PCV20 recommendations to all adults aged 65 and over, in addition to at-risk adults aged 18–64 (Table 1).

Historic strategy - Replacement of the sequence PCV13 → PPV23 by PCV20 in at-risk adults

In this analysis, for both cohorts, the vaccination-eligible population consists of adults aged 18 and older who are at high or moderate risk for pneumococcal infection. In the first cohort, individuals receive the PCV13 followed by PPV23 sequence, reflecting the historical observed pneumococcal vaccination uptake levels. In the comparative cohort, however, individuals receive the newly recommended PCV20 vaccine. This cohort assumes an increase in vaccination uptake due to the simplified single-dose schedule of PCV20 and heightened awareness of respiratory infections following the pandemic.

Extended strategy - Extension of the recommendation with PCV20 to all 65+

In this analysis, both cohorts receive the PCV20 vaccine. However, eligibility criteria differ between them as in the first cohort, only adults aged of 18 years or older at high or moderate risk are eligible for vaccination; and in the second cohort, eligibility extends to all adults aged 65 years and over in addition to the high and moderate-risk individuals aged from 18 to 64.

Inputs

Population and epidemiology data

Population distribution among risk groups was based on the a claim database study aiming to estimate the pneumococcal vaccine uptake in different risk-group patients in France (COVARISQ) [8]. Vaccine uptake was applied to the eligible population, with different rates depending on the vaccine based on the values reported in the COVARISQ [9, 10]. In both analyses, for the reference strategies, the uptake observed in 2020 were considered and assumptions were made to estimate the increased uptake in the studied interventions (Table 2).

Incidence and mortality data for hospitalized cases were sourced from a study conducted on the national claims database to estimate the burden of pneumococcal infection in France [5]. The cases recorded in 2019 were considered in the analysis. To reduce bias due to the limited sensitivity of pathogen testing and the absence of routine pathogen identification for community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), an all-cause pneumonia incidence rate was applied (Fig. 2). For non-hospitalized cases of CAP, incidence and mortality data were derived from a national prospective observational study of CAP management in general practice (CAPA study) [23, 24]. The percentage of cases attributable to serotypes covered by the vaccines was obtained from national IPD surveillance data (French Centre National de Référence des Pneumocoques: CNRP) and a prospective, multicentre, cohort study conducted in France (CAP’ HOSPI) study [25] for pneumonia, considering the serotype coverage for all-cause pneumonia cases within the vaccine scope (Table 2).

Vaccine effect

The initial vaccine efficacy (VE) against vaccine-type (VT) serotypes for both IPD and pneumonia in adults was derived from the Community-Acquired Pneumonia Immunization Trial in Adults (CAPiTA) study, focusing on individuals aged 65 and older [33]. For those aged from 50 to 64, initial VE was based on post-hoc CAPiTA analyses, while for individuals aged 18–49, VE was assumed to match the 50 + group [19]. In high-risk individuals, VE was estimated to be 20% lower than in immunocompetent individuals, based on studies conducted in patients with HIV [34, 35]. Vaccine efficacy for pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCV) is assumed to remain stable for five years, after which a conservative annual reduction in VE is applied: a 5% annual decrease from years 5 to 10, followed by a 10% annual decrease from years 10 to 15, with VE assumed to be negligible beyond 15 years [19, 33, 36] (See supplementary material – Figs. 1a and 2). In comparing PCV20 to the previously recommended PCV13 → PPV23 sequence, the initial VE of PPV23 against VT-IPD was estimated based on existing literature [37, 38] (See supplementary material – Fig. 1b). VE against VT-community-acquired pneumonia (VT-CAP) was considered negligible, as indicated by available evidence and confirmed by real-world studies [37, 39]. For the model, PPV23 VE against VT-IPD was set to wane gradually: decreasing to 76.2% of the initial VE by year 5 and then diminishing linearly to no efficacy by year 10, continuing this trend for the remainder of the model horizon [37] (supplementary material).

Vaccine side effects are not included in the study as their intensity is typically low and they are expected to have little to no impact on the results. In addition, clinical studies have shown that PCV20 and PCV13 have similar safety profiles, with adverse events being predominantly mild to moderate and transient [40].

Resource use and costs

The economic inputs in this analysis were estimated in 2024 euros from a healthcare system perspective in accordance with French economic guidelines, ensuring a comprehensive view of expenses. Cost data for managing hospitalized episodes were sourced from the national claims database study for the hospitalization itself [5] and from Bailey et al. for the outpatient costs following a hospitalization [31]. The cost of treating non-hospitalized cases was sourced from the CAPECO study [3]. The latest official and published vaccine prices were incorporated to reflect historical market rates [32]. Vaccine administration costs were calculated as an average across several settings: in pharmacies, by nurses, and at general practitioners’ (GP) practices. For pharmacies and nurse-administered vaccinations, standard tariffs were applied [41, 42], while for GP-administered vaccinations, the average consultation fees, including any patient contributions, were used [43] (Table 2).

Utility

Baseline utility values for France were derived from the latest published norms using the EQ-5D-5 L instrument, adjusted specifically for at-risk groups based on published correction data [27, 28]. A systematic literature review was conducted to ensure the selection of the most suitable sources for quality-of-life data, providing a well-supported foundation for utility values. Utility losses associated with each episode were sourced from the PNEUMOCOST study [29] for hospitalized cases. They were derived by subtracting population level EQ-5D-3 L norms from the values reported for months 1, 3, 6 and 12 in the publication, then applying a power regression to estimate monthly differences over the first year allowing the calculation of a mean value per age group. Utility losses for non-hospitalized cases were obtained from relevant literature (Table 2) [30].

Approach to uncertainty

Several scenario analyses were performed to evaluate the extended recommendation, assessing the influence of modelling assumptions and exploring alternative value sets, detailed in the supplementary material. In Scenario 1, due to challenges in estimating the incidence CAP in outpatient settings, the base case used the most conservative estimates. This scenario also included an alternative CAP incidence estimate (Supplementary Table 1) derived from the CAPA study, based on hospitalization probability per CAP case [24]. In Scenario 2, vaccine serotype coverage for all-cause CAP was initially based on data from the CAP’ HOSPI study, while this scenario tested an alternative coverage estimate (Supplementary Table 2) from the CAPA study in Spain [44]. Scenario 3 considered the potential impact of paediatric vaccination with PCV20, which was not included in the base case. Use of 7- and 13-valent PCVs in paediatric immunization programs has often resulted in reduced carriage and transmission of vaccine serotypes, lowering the risk of disease for adults and unvaccinated children; such effects are also hypothesized for PCV20, and may reduce the cost-effectiveness of adult vaccination. Therefore, this scenario examined the effects of PCV20 on reducing overall IPD incidence over time (Supplementary Table 3) and the possible emergence of non-vaccine serotypes [45]. Scenario 4 tested a higher overall vaccination uptake (Supplementary Table 4) among moderate and high-risk adults aged 65 and over.

Additionally, both deterministic and probabilistic sensitivity analyses were conducted to further validate the robustness of the findings across different assumptions and variable inputs. Deterministic one-way sensitivity analyses (DSA) were conducted to assess the impact of individual model parameter uncertainty on estimated costs, outcomes, and the robustness of the base case results. Each parameter was varied individually by +/-25% around its base case value. Whenever possible, 95% confidence intervals, and standard errors were used. A probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) was conducted to assess the overall uncertainty in model results by simultaneously varying key input parameters. Probability distributions were assigned to model parameters based on available data or reasonable assumptions: beta distributions for probabilities and utilities, gamma distributions for costs, and log-normal distributions for relative risks and mortality rates. The analysis was performed using 500 Monte Carlo simulations.

Historic strategy - Replacement of the sequence PCV13 → PPV23 by PCV20 in at-risk adults

Over a lifetime horizon, switching from the PCV13 → PPV23 vaccination sequence to PCV20 is projected to prevent 680 more cases of IPD, and 9,181 cases of CAP. This strategy would also reduce disease-related mortality by approximately 1,500 deaths. Despite an increase in vaccination costs of around €8,000, this is offset by a reduction of around €64,000 in medical care costs, resulting in net savings of around €57,000 and a lower average cost per person (€1,124.12 vs. €1,125.17). The health gains include 0.00015 additional QALYs and 0.00017 LY per person. Taken together, these outcomes make PCV20 a dominant strategy, offering greater health benefits at a lower overall cost compared to the historical sequential schedule.

Extended strategy - Extension of the recommendation to all 65+

Over a lifetime horizon, extending the pneumococcal vaccination recommendation to all individuals aged 65 and older would prevent even more cases and deaths attributable to pneumococcal infection than the switch from the PCV13 → PPV23 sequence to PCV20 alone. Compared to restricting PCV20 vaccination to the historical eligible population, this expanded recommendation could avert nearly 900 additional cases of IPD, 11,660 cases of CAP, and around 1,500 deaths among the French population, highlighting its significant public health impact. From an economic perspective, the extension results in an incremental vaccination investment of €107,176. However, this is partially offset by medical cost savings of over €63,000, due to fewer disease episodes and hospitalizations. The resulting incremental cost of nearly €44,000 leads to a modest increase in average cost per person (€0.81), while still achieving measurable health gains: an additional 0.00019 QALYs and 0.00019 life years per person. The corresponding ICER is estimated at 4,308 €/QALY gained (and 4,216 €/LY).

Sensitivity Analyses

The results of the analysis are most sensitive to parameters such as the price of PCV20, VE against CAP and the epidemiological and economic data for CAP (Fig. 3). A probabilistic sensitivity analysis, on 500 iterations, indicates that in 87% of simulations), extending vaccination coverage to all individuals aged 65 and older is a more costly but more efficient strategy than historical approach (North-East quadrant of the cost-effectiveness plane - Fig. 4); in the remaining 13% of simulations, this extended coverage emerged as the dominant strategy (South East quadrant of the cost-effectiveness plane - Fig. 4). No willingness-to-pay threshold was represented because no explicit cost-effectiveness threshold is formally recommended in the French setting. The median probabilistic ICER is estimated at 6,201€/QALY, with a 100% probability of cost-effectiveness reached at a willingness-to-pay threshold above 40,000 euros (Fig. 5). The deterministic and median probabilistic ICER are low compared with those reported in previous French vaccine economic opinions. A review of French vaccine HTA assessments with validated economic opinions identified 10 evaluations [46–55]. Among them, one intervention was dominant, while the ICERs reported for the remaining nine ranged from 662 €/QALY to 145,098 €/QALY, with a mean of 33,120 €/QALY and a median of 24,413 €/QALY.

Scenarios regarding CAP (scenarios 1 and 2)

The assumptions used in the base case are confirmed to be conservative, ensuring cautious estimates of the strategy’s impact. Sensitivity analyses reveal that a higher serotype coverage, as observed in the CAPA study, would place the extension of vaccination recommendations to all 65 years-old and older as the dominant strategy (scenario 2, Table 3). This parameter shows a greater influence on the ICER than adjustments to incidence rates, as testing an alternative method for incidence estimation yields only a 43% reduction in the ICER (scenario 1, Table 3). This emphasizes the importance of serotype coverage as a key driver of cost-effectiveness in this analysis.

Scenarios regarding the indirect effect on childhood vaccination with PCV20 (scenarios 3.A and 3.B)

The impact of these factors on the results is limited, with only a minor increase in ICER a 11% increase when assuming no serotype replacement (scenario 3.A, Table 3) and a 5% increase with serotype replacement.(scenario 3.B, Table 3). As the overall burden of pneumococcal infection decreases, the number of cases prevented, and deaths averted by vaccination may decline slightly.

Scenario regarding the increase of the vaccination uptake (scenario 4)

Extending the vaccination recommendation to low-risk individuals aged 65 and over could have a notable indirect effect on vaccination uptake in moderate- and high-risk adults within the same age group. This results in a 36% increase in the ICER, mainly due to higher vaccination costs which are only slightly offset by savings in medical costs. Furthermore, as vaccination uptake in adults under 65 is assumed to be identical between scenarios, the incremental gain in QALYs is reduced (scenario 4, Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, replacing the PCV13 → PPV23 sequence with PCV20 emerged as a cost-saving strategy with multiple advantages, not only simplifying the vaccination schedule but also reducing overall costs in adults at risk. Additionally, PCV20 includes serotype 8, a major contributor to IPD in adults, as highlighted by recent findings from the CNRP [56]. By eliminating the need for a booster dose, PCV20 reduces both the complexity and cost of vaccination. While a simplified schedule may increase overall vaccination uptake and associated expenditures, these costs are offset by the prevention of severe pneumococcal infections, reinforcing the economic and public health value of PCV20.

Our findings highlight not only the benefits of PCV20 as a replacement for the PCV13 → PPV23 sequence, but also support the extension of the vaccination recommendation to all adults aged 65 years and over. This is particularly relevant in the French context, where coverage among adults has historically been suboptimal [8, 57]. Vaccine uptake is a key determinant of the overall public health impact of vaccination, as higher coverage increases both vaccination costs and the number of pneumococcal disease cases and associated consequences prevented. In this context, PCV20 offers an opportunity to improve adherence among both patients and healthcare providers, facilitating more consistent vaccine uptake and further maximize public health benefits. Although this approach requires an initial investment, our results suggest that the long-term benefits would be substantial by preventing around 900 additional IPD cases and 1,500 deaths, resulting in ICER of 4,308/QALY gained.

The robustness of this analysis is supported by several methodological strengths. The model structure has been previously validated for health technology assessments, ensuring reliable estimations. Although a static model was used, this choice is justified, as the primary goal of vaccination is to reduce severe cases rather than transmission. Vaccine effectiveness parameters were based on evidence from the CAPiTA trial, post hoc analyses, and published literature on vaccine effectiveness and waning for PCV and PPV23, with estimates adapted according to age and risk profile. Epidemiological parameters were derived from national surveillance data and the French claims database, ensuring relevance to the local context. Nonetheless, uncertainties remain, particularly regarding CAP epidemiology, as precise burden estimation is complicated by the non-systematic identification of the causative pathogen and the limited specificity and sensitivity of available diagnostic tests. These limitations likely contribute to a substantial underestimation of the disease burden.

In line with French HTA methodological recommendations, the analysis did not adopt a societal perspective and therefore excluded indirect costs and broader externalities associated with vaccination. As avoided productivity losses, caregiver burden, and long-term sequelae were not captured, the estimated value of PCV20 is likely to be conservative. Our results align with findings from similar studies in other countries. In the United Kingdom, a cost-effectiveness analysis demonstrated that PCV20 was superior to PPV23 in individuals aged 65 and older and those aged 18–64 with medical conditions [58]. Similarly, a study in the Netherlands found that PCV20 was more cost-effective than PPV23 for adults over 65, even when accounting for indirect protection from childhood vaccination programs [59]. At the time of this analysis, as PCV20 was not recommended for children in France, indirect protection was not included in the base case. However, scenario analysis suggested that if PCV20 were introduced into the paediatric schedule, it would slightly reduce the cost-effectiveness of adult vaccination by lowering overall pneumococcal burden. Nonetheless, in a scenario considering a full serotype replacement, the ICER increased by only 9–10%, indicating the stability of our conclusions across different assumptions.

Overall, this analysis supports PCV20 as a cost-effective strategy for reducing pneumococcal disease burden in adults in France, with estimated ICERs substantially lower than those reported in most previous French vaccine economic opinions. Its potential for simplifying the adult vaccination schedule and expanding eligibility reinforces its value as a key public health intervention.

Conclusions

The analyses support the recent update in French vaccination guidelines, demonstrating that PCV20 is a cost-saving strategy compared to the PCV13 → PPV23 sequence for populations aged ≥ 65 years old and more and adults at risk of pneumococcal disease according to risk factors listed by the French High Council for Public Health. Additionally, transitioning to an age-based recommendation for individuals aged 65 and older is highly cost-effective, with an ICER around 4 K€/QALY. While the overall impact remains constrained by relatively low adult vaccination rates, expanding recommendations to all adults aged 65 years and over represents a crucial step toward improving coverage and achieving substantial public health benefits.
