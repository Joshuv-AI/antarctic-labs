Prognostic Value of Chest CT-Derived Pectoralis Muscle Metrics for In-Hospital Mortality and Invasive Mechanical Ventilation in AECOPD
Authors: Yu Zhang, Naer An, Yiteng Zhang, Zejun Liang, Jianrong He, Zhenlin Li, Yongchun Shen, Jing Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe clinical significance of pectoralis muscle depletion during acute exacerbations of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (AECOPD) remains unclear. This study investigated the independent prognostic value of computed tomography (CT)-derived pectoralis muscle metrics for in-hospital mortality and invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) in AECOPD.Patients and MethodsThis retrospective study included 464 consecutive AECOPD patients who underwent chest CT within 48 hours of admission. Pectoralis muscle area and muscle density (PMD) were quantified from CT. The pectoralis muscle index (PMI) was calculated by normalizing muscle area to height squared. Multivariable Cox regression models evaluated associations between these indices and adverse outcomes. The incremental predictive value of adding muscle indices to DECAF and BAP-65 scores was assessed using the area under the curve (AUC).ResultsAmong 464 patients, 44 (9.5%) died and 86 (18.5%) required IMV during hospitalization. Both PMI and PMD were significantly lower in non-survivors and IMV patients (all P<0.001). In fully adjusted models, each 1 cm2/m2 increase in PMI was associated with reduced risks of in-hospital mortality (HR 0.68, 95% CI 0.58-0.78) and IMV (HR 0.72, 95% CI 0.64–0.81). Each 5 HU increase in PMD independently predicted lower in-hospital mortality (HR 0.77, 95% CI 0.66-0.90) and IMV (HR 0.59, 95% CI 0.50-0.70). Incorporation of both PMI and PMD into the DECAF and BAP-65 scores substantially increased the predictive AUCs for in-hospital mortality (AUC: 0.70 to 0.89 for DECAF; 0.71 to 0.89 for BAP-65) and for IMV (AUC: 0.61 to 0.78 for DECAF; 0.69 to 0.81 for BAP-65).ConclusionCT-derived pectoralis muscle mass and quality are independent and incremental predictors of in-hospital mortality and IMV in AECOPD. Opportunistic muscle assessment from routine chest CT may enhance early risk stratification and inform clinical decision-making.

Introduction

Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, imposing a substantial burden on healthcare systems.1,2 The clinical course of COPD is frequently punctuated by acute exacerbations (AECOPD). Approximately 22% to 40% of patients experience at least one exacerbation annually,3 with an overall average of 0.5 to 3.5 episodes per patient per year.4 These exacerbations are critical events associated with accelerated lung function decline, poor quality of life, and high mortality rates.5,6 Among patients hospitalized for AECOPD, the overall in-hospital mortality rate exceeded 8%, and it escalates to as high as 24% for those requiring intensive care unit admission.7 Within this high-risk cohort, the development of acute respiratory failure requiring invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) represents a particularly dire trajectory, often signaling a tipping point in the disease course.8 Therefore, early and accurate risk stratification upon admission is paramount to guide clinical decision-making, optimize resource allocation, and identify patients who may benefit from intensive monitoring.

Skeletal muscle wasting is a well-recognized systemic manifestation of COPD and has been linked to reduced exercise capacity, poorer quality of life, and increased long-term mortality.9,10 In recent years, chest computed tomography (CT) has enabled opportunistic assessment of thoracic muscle mass, particularly the pectoralis muscles.11 Pectoralis muscles serve as essential accessory muscles of respiration, playing a vital role in maintaining ventilation—especially when the diaphragm is mechanically disadvantaged by hyperinflation during severe airflow obstruction. Consequently, the structural integrity of the pectoralis muscles is intrinsically linked to respiratory mechanics. Previous studies in stable COPD have established that reduced pectoralis muscle area is closely correlated with key clinical features, including the severity of airflow limitation, symptom burden, functional capacity, and disease progression.12–14 These findings suggest that CT-based pectoralis muscle metrics may serve as a valid surrogate for systemic sarcopenia and physiological reserve in the COPD population.

Despite growing interest in CT-based pectoralis muscle quantification in stable COPD, its prognostic significance during the acute exacerbation phase remains insufficiently explored. AECOPD is characterized by abrupt increases in ventilatory demand and systemic inflammatory burden, conditions under which respiratory muscle reserve becomes critically important.15–17 This reserve is dictated not only by muscle mass but also by muscle quality. While reduced muscle mass may compromise the ability to compensate for acute respiratory stress, muscle quality, which is reflected by CT-derived muscle density and indicative of intramuscular fat infiltration, may further impair contractile efficiency and metabolic resilience.18 However, data evaluating both muscle mass and muscle quality in patients hospitalized for AECOPD are limited,19 and it remains unclear whether these imaging-derived muscle indices provide prognostic information beyond established clinical risk scores.

Therefore, the primary objective of this retrospective study was to evaluate the prognostic value of chest CT-based pectoralis muscle indicators, including pectoralis muscle mass and quality for predicting in-hospital mortality and the requirement for IMV in patients with AECOPD. Additionally, we sought to determine whether incorporating these objective body composition markers into established clinical risk scores, such as the DECAF and BAP-65 scores, could provide incremental predictive value, thereby emphasizing that the opportunistic assessment of muscle reserves from routine CT scans can provide crucial insights to inform clinical decision-making and risk stratification.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Settings

This retrospective observational cohort study was conducted at a tertiary academic medical center. Consecutive patients hospitalized for AECOPD between July 2019 and August 2023 were screened for eligibility. The diagnosis of COPD and acute exacerbation was established according to the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) criteria.1

Patients were included if they (1) were ≥18 years old, (2) were admitted to the hospital with a primary diagnosis of AECOPD, defined as an acute worsening of respiratory symptoms (eg, increased dyspnea, sputum volume, or purulence) that necessitated inpatient care, (3) underwent chest CT within 2 days of hospital admission. Exclusion criteria were: (1) patients with other significant respiratory disorders, such as asthma, active pulmonary tuberculosis, bronchiectasis, interstitial lung disease, or significant pneumoconiosis, (2) status of malignancy or neuromuscular disease affecting body composition, (3) prior history of chest or lung surgery, (4) poor CT image quality precluding body composition analysis, (5) receiving palliative care, (6) incomplete clinical data.

This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Review Board (West China Hospital, No. 2023[2164], November 2023). The requirement for written informed consent was waived by the Ethics Review Board because this study used pre-existing clinical and imaging data obtained during routine care, involved no additional patient contact or intervention, and posed minimal risk to participants. All patient data were anonymized before analysis to protect confidentiality. The study was conducted in accordance with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments.

Clinical Data

Clinical and laboratory data were retrospectively extracted from the electronic medical record system. Demographic variables included age, sex, body mass index (BMI) and smoking status. Smoking status was categorized as current smoker, former smoker, or never smoker. Coexisting comorbidities were documented, including hypertension, cardiovascular disease, chronic renal failure, and diabetes mellitus. Clinical manifestations and vital signs at admission were recorded, consisting of dyspnea, fever, disturbance of consciousness, pulse rate, and respiratory rate. All laboratory tests were performed within 48 hours of hospital admission. Routine blood and biochemical parameters included white blood cell count, neutrophil count, eosinophil count, and urea nitrogen. Inflammatory biomarkers were also collected, including C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6, and procalcitonin. Arterial blood gas analysis parameters included pH, partial pressure of oxygen, partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO2), and oxygen saturation.

Based on the collected demographic and clinical data, disease severity was assessed using the DECAF score (Dyspnea, Eosinopenia, Consolidation, Acidemia, and Atrial Fibrillation)20 and the BAP-65 score (Blood Urea Nitrogen, Altered Mental Status, Pulse, and Age >65).21

The primary clinical outcome was in-hospital mortality, defined as death from any cause occurring during the hospitalization. The secondary outcome was the requirement for IMV, defined as the need for endotracheal intubation or tracheostomy for respiratory support at any time during the hospital stay.

Radiographic Assessment

All CT images used for pectoralis muscle assessment were obtained during the current AECOPD hospitalization within 2 days after admission. All chest CT examinations were performed using multidetector CT scanners according to standardized institutional protocols. Scanning parameters were as follows: tube voltage 120 kVp, automatic tube current modulation, slice thickness 5 mm, and matrix size 512×512. CT scans were acquired in the supine position during full inspiration without intravenous contrast administration.

CT-derived analysis of the pectoralis muscles was performed to assess chest skeletal muscle characteristics. The first slice immediately superior to the aortic arch on which the bilateral pectoralis major and minor muscles were simultaneously and clearly visible, was selected for measurement, in accordance with previously published methods.14 The bilateral pectoralis major and minor muscles were manually segmented on the selected slice using SliceOmatic. Muscle tissue was identified using predefined attenuation thresholds ranging from −50 to +90 HU.14,22 The total pectoralis muscle area (PMA) was calculated as the sum of the cross-sectional areas of both sides. Pectoralis muscle density (PMD) was defined as the mean attenuation value (in Hounsfield units, HU) within the segmented muscle area, reflecting muscle quality. For comparative analyses, PMA was normalized by height squared (m2) to derive the pectoralis muscle index (PMI). The measurement workflow included target-slice identification, manual delineation of the bilateral pectoralis major and minor muscles on a single axial slice, and extraction of muscle area and attenuation using the segmentation software. After training and familiarization with the protocol, the manual segmentation procedure generally required approximately 3–5 minutes per patient.

In line with established studies,23 muscle indicators were stratified into sex-specific tertiles. Patients in the lowest tertile were identified as having low muscle mass (Low PMI) or low muscle density (Low PMD). Conversely, patients in the middle and upper tertiles were defined as the Normal group. All measurements were performed independently by two radiologists with 5 and 10 years of experience in thoracic imaging, both blinded to clinical outcomes. Interobserver agreement for PMI and PMD was evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs).

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate, and categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons were performed using the Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U-test for continuous variables (following normality assessment via the Shapiro–Wilk test) and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

The associations between CT-derived pectoralis muscle parameters and adverse outcomes were evaluated using multivariable regression models. For all multivariable regression analyses, potential confounders were selected based on established clinical relevance and statistically significant differences observed in the baseline characteristics. Crucially, to prevent model overfitting, the maximum number of covariates incorporated into each fully adjusted model was strictly constrained in accordance with the events per variable principle. Variance inflation factors were calculated to ensure the absence of severe multicollinearity among covariates.

Multivariable Cox’s proportional hazards models were constructed to evaluate the association between CT-derived pectoralis muscle parameters and both in-hospital mortality and IMV during hospitalization. To prevent model overfitting given the limited number of outcome events, the selection of covariates was carefully determined based on a combination of established clinical importance, significant findings from univariable regression analyses, and strict adherence to the events per variable principle. For both outcomes, three models were analyzed. For in-hospital mortality, the models included: model 1, unadjusted; model 2, adjusted for age and sex; and model 3, adjusted for age, sex, BMI, and CRP. For IMV, the models included: model 1, unadjusted; model 2, adjusted for age and sex; and model 3, adjusted for age, sex, BMI, CRP, smoking status, and PaCO2. All results were reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis with four knots (at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles) was performed to explore potential nonlinear relationships between continuous pectoralis muscle indices and adverse outcomes. For associations showing significant nonlinearity, the inflection point was further estimated from the fitted RCS curve using an elbow-point method. The inflection point was defined as the point on the fitted curve with the greatest perpendicular distance from the straight line connecting the first and last predicted observations.24 This value was used to describe the approximate point at which the exposure–risk curve began to flatten.

Model discrimination was evaluated by plotting receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and calculating the area under the curve (AUC). Comparisons of AUCs between the baseline models (DECAF or BAP-65 alone) and the combined models were performed using the DeLong test. To further assess the incremental predictive value and reclassification improvement, the Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI) and the Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI) were calculated. Subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness and consistency of the associations between pectoralis muscle indices (PMI and PMD) and adverse outcomes across predefined clinically relevant strata, including sex, BMI, smoking status, and PaCO2 levels. HR and 95% CIs for each subgroup were calculated using the fully adjusted multivariable models described above. To assess for potential effect modification by these stratifying variables, formal interaction analyses were performed by incorporating interaction terms into the respective multivariable models, yielding a P value for interaction. As a sensitivity analysis, PMI and PMD were further categorized into three sex-specific tertiles, with the middle tertile or the upper tertiles used as the reference group, to assess whether the primary findings were influenced by combining the middle and upper tertiles as the reference category. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.5.1).

Among the included patients, 44 (9.5%) died during hospitalization. Compared with survivors, non-survivors were significantly older (median age: 80.5 vs 73.0 years, P<0.001) and had a lower BMI (20.0 vs 21.7 kg/m2, P=0.006). The prevalence of consciousness disturbance was significantly higher in non-survivors than in survivors (34.1% vs 11.7%, P<0.001). Regarding laboratory findings, non-survivors exhibited significantly higher levels of inflammatory markers (CRP, IL-6, procalcitonin, WBC count, and neutrophils) and urea nitrogen, but lower eosinophil counts (all P<0.001). Crucially, body composition analysis revealed that both PMI (5.3 vs 8.8 cm2/m2, P<0.001) and PMD (19.8 vs 28.2 HU, P<0.001) were significantly lower in the non-survivor group.

A total of 86 (18.5%) patients required IMV during hospitalization. Patients in the IMV group were older (78.0 vs 73.0 years, P=0.004) and had a higher proportion of current smokers compared to the non-IMV group (P<0.001). Similar to the mortality analysis, the IMV group showed a higher incidence of consciousness disturbance and elevated inflammatory biomarkers (P<0.001). In terms of blood gas analysis, patients requiring IMV presented with significantly lower pH (P=0.001) and higher PaCO2 (50.7 vs 43.5 mmHg, P<0.001). Notably, while BMI did not differ significantly between the groups (P=0.905), the IMV group demonstrated significantly lower PMI (6.5 vs 8.8 cm2/m2, P<0.001) and PMD (19.8 vs 28.8 HU, P<0.001) compared to the non-IMV group.

Interobserver agreement for PMI and PMD measurements was excellent, with intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) ranging from 0.74 to 0.89.

Association Between Pectoralis Muscle Indices and in-Hospital Mortality

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed to visualize the impact of pectoralis muscle characteristics on in-hospital outcomes (Figure 1). Patients stratified into the lowest tertile of PMI exhibited significantly reduced overall survival compared to those in the middle and high tertiles (P < 0.001). A similar trend was observed for muscle quality, with patients in the lowest PMD tertile showing significantly lower survival rates (P = 0.003). To further evaluate independent predictors of mortality, multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was conducted (Table 2). Consistent with the survival curves, PMI served as a robust independent prognostic factor for in-hospital mortality. Subgroup and Sensitivity Analyses

Our subgroup analyses demonstrated high consistency with the main findings. Subgroup analyses were performed to evaluate the consistency of the prognostic value of pectoralis muscle indices across clinically relevant strata, including sex, BMI, smoking status, and PaCO2 levels (Tables S1 and S2). Overall, the associations between pectoralis muscle indices (PMI and PMD) and adverse outcomes (in-hospital mortality and IMV) remained robust and consistent across most subgroups, with no significant interactions observed (P interaction > 0.05 for the majority of analyses). For IMV, an interaction was observed for PMD categories (P interaction = 0.044), where the risk associated with low muscle density was particularly pronounced in former smokers (adjusted HR: 7.17, 95% CI: 2.73–20.65) compared to current smokers (adjusted HR: 2.02, 95% CI: 1.06–3.90). Due to the limited number of adverse events among never smokers, risk estimates for this subgroup could not be calculated.

To address the potential influence of combining the middle and upper tertiles as the reference group, we performed additional sensitivity analyses using the normal and high muscle groups as separate reference categories (Tables S3 and S4). For in-hospital mortality, low PMI remained significantly associated with increased risk compared with both normal PMI and high PMI in the fully adjusted model. The adjusted HR was 2.73 (95% CI: 1.34–5.60, P = 0.006) using normal PMI as the reference and 7.91 (95% CI: 1.84–34.10, P = 0.006) using high PMI as the reference.

For PMD, low PMD was not significantly associated with mortality when compared with normal PMD in the fully adjusted model, but remained significantly associated with higher mortality risk when compared with high PMD (adjusted HR: 3.07, 95% CI: 1.14–8.28, P = 0.027). Low PMI was consistently associated with increased IMV risk using either normal or high PMI as the reference. Low PMD showed a borderline association when compared with normal PMD (adjusted HR: 1.75, 95% CI: 0.99–3.11, P = 0.055), but a strong association when compared with high PMD (adjusted HR: 6.62, 95% CI: 3.03–16.19, P < 0.001).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study, we evaluated the prognostic value of pectoralis muscle mass and quality, quantified as PMI and PMD via chest CT, for predicting adverse outcomes in patients hospitalized for AECOPD, addressing the gap in body composition assessment for these patients. Our results demonstrated that higher PMI and PMD, analyzed as continuous variables, were independently associated with lower risks of in-hospital mortality and IMV after adjusting for clinical confounders. In categorical analyses, patients in the lowest tertile of PMI faced a substantially higher risk of in-hospital mortality and patients in the lowest tertile of PMI and PMD faced a substantially higher risk of IMV. Specifically, pectoralis muscle metrics exhibited superior discriminative ability compared to established clinical scores, such as DECAF and BAP-65, with their integration significantly refining risk stratification. These findings underscore the critical role of skeletal muscle assessment in the early identification of high-risk patients during AECOPD episodes.

While prior research has primarily focused on the association between pectoralis muscle depletion and long-term outcomes in stable COPD,12,14,22,25 evidence in the acute exacerbation setting remains scarce. In this study, we extend existing knowledge by demonstrating that reduced pectoralis muscle mass, as quantified by PMI, is independently associated with increased in-hospital mortality and a higher likelihood of requiring invasive mechanical ventilation among patients with AECOPD. These findings suggest that diminished thoracic muscle reserve may reflect impaired physiological resilience during acute respiratory decompensation. In the setting of acute exacerbation, where ventilatory demand rises markedly,26 reduced muscle mass may limit the capacity to sustain the increased respiratory workload, consequently predisposing patients to adverse clinical outcomes. Importantly, CT-derived pectoralis muscle metrics are routinely obtainable from standard chest imaging performed at admission, making them practical and objective biomarkers for early risk stratification in the acute care setting.

Beyond muscle mass, our study simultaneously evaluated muscle density, revealing a distinctive and clinically relevant finding: PMD demonstrated a comparable predictive performance for IMV relative to PMI (AUC 0.74 vs 0.71). This disparity underscores that muscle quality, specifically the degree of myosteatosis, may be a more sensitive indicator of impending ventilatory failure than muscle mass alone. Myosteatosis, characterized by the ectopic accumulation of intramyocellular and intermuscular fat and radiologically quantified by reduced CT muscle density, is pathophysiologically linked to impaired muscle contractility and a state of chronic systemic inflammation.27,28 Extensive fat infiltration compromises the mechanical efficiency and oxygen utilization of these muscles, thereby diminishing the patient’s physiological reserve to compensate for respiratory distress, ultimately precipitating the need for mechanical support.29,30 Previous studies25,31 have also demonstrated that fat and muscle interact in COPD, with changes in adipose tissue potentially contributing to the progression of muscle wasting. This interaction suggests that myosteatosis (low PMD) may represent an earlier or more biologically active stage of frailty than muscle atrophy (low PMI) alone, highlighting the importance of assessing muscle composition rather than just size in clinical practice.

The DECAF and BAP-65 scores are well-validated tools for risk stratification in AECOPD, demonstrating robust performance in capturing acute physiological derangements such as acidemia, altered mental status, and gas exchange abnormalities. Previous studies have reported that DECAF and BAP-65 scores generally exhibit moderate discriminative ability for predicting in-hospital mortality in AECOPD, with AUCs typically ranging from 0.65 to 0.75.32–34 Consistent with these results, the predictive performance in our current cohort was comparable, yielding an AUC of 0.70 for DECAF and 0.71 for BAP-65. Compared with DECAF and BAP-65, which primarily capture acute physiological disturbances and comorbidity burden during exacerbations, pectoralis muscle indices reflect chronic physiological reserve and vulnerability. By integrating PMI and PMD, our combined models effectively synthesized these two domains, resulting in a synergistic improvement in predictive accuracy (AUC increasing from ~0.70 to 0.89). The significant gains in NRI and IDI further confirm that skeletal muscle depletion represents a distinct, independent risk dimension that, when added to standard clinical assessments, provides a more holistic view of patient prognosis.

We found that the dose–response relationships between pectoralis muscle indices and clinical outcomes differed substantially. While PMI and PMD exhibited linear inverse associations with in-hospital mortality, their relationships with IMV were distinctly non-linear, following an L-shaped pattern. This divergence likely reflects fundamental differences in the underlying pathophysiology of these outcomes. In-hospital mortality represents the cumulative impact of systemic vulnerability and multi-organ dysfunction, whereas the requirement for IMV is often precipitated when respiratory muscle reserve falls below a critical threshold. The RCS-derived inflection points for IMV were approximately 6.98 cm2/m2 for PMI and 18.09 HU for PMD. Below these levels, the predicted risk of IMV increased sharply, whereas the risk curve began to flatten at higher values. In this setting, even modest reductions in muscle mass or quality at the lower end of the distribution may lead to abrupt ventilatory failure. Collectively, these findings suggest that maintaining a minimal level of skeletal muscle reserve may be particularly important for preventing severe respiratory decompensation necessitating mechanical ventilation.

Subgroup analyses generally supported the robustness of the main findings, as the associations between pectoralis muscle indices and adverse outcomes remained directionally consistent across most clinically relevant strata, including sex, BMI, smoking status, and PaCO2 levels. One exception was the significant interaction between smoking status and categorical PMD for IMV, with a stronger association observed among former smokers than current smokers. A possible explanation is that former smokers represent a phenotype characterized by advanced, cumulative structural damage. In this population, historical long-term smoking exposure translates into persistent systemic consequences, including irreversible skeletal muscle dysfunction and chronic myosteatosis, which are reflected by low PMD.35,36 Consequently, this chronically reduced respiratory muscle reserve becomes a primary vulnerability and a critical determinant for requiring IMV. In contrast, among current smokers, continuous exposure to tobacco smoke sustains a high burden of active airway inflammation, acute oxidative stress, and increased mucus hypersecretion.37 During acute illnesses or exacerbations, these intense inflammatory triggers may dominate the immediate risk of sudden respiratory deterioration, thereby partially overshadowing the baseline prognostic impact of muscle attenuation. However, this interaction was not significant when PMD was analyzed as a continuous variable and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Given the exploratory nature of subgroup analyses, the potential for multiple comparisons, and limited event numbers in some strata, this hypothesis-generating finding requires validation in larger prospective cohorts. From a clinical perspective, our findings have important implications. Chest CT is frequently performed in hospitalized patients with severe AECOPD, providing an opportunity for opportunistic assessment of pectoralis muscle indices without additional radiation exposure or cost. Early identification of patients with low PMI or PMD may facilitate prompt risk stratification, closer monitoring, and timely escalation of respiratory support. Moreover, these muscle-based biomarkers may help identify patients who could benefit from targeted nutritional support, early mobilization, or pulmonary rehabilitation strategies, although prospective studies are needed to determine whether modifying muscle status can improve outcomes.

There are limitations of the present study to address. First, this was a retrospective, single-center study, which may limit generalizability. Moreover, PMD was measured on 5-mm axial CT images, which were the most consistently available series in this retrospective cohort. Although this approach ensured protocol consistency, slice thickness may have affected absolute HU values through partial-volume averaging and noise-related effects. Therefore, the PMD cutoffs should be interpreted in the context of the reconstruction protocol used and require validation using standardized thin-section images. Second, muscle assessment was limited to the pectoralis muscles rather than whole-body composition; however, prior studies have validated thoracic muscle measurements as reliable surrogates of global muscle status.12,38 Third, only baseline CT measurements were analyzed, and dynamic changes in muscle mass or density during hospitalization were not evaluated. Finally, this study relied on manual segmentation, which may introduce operator-dependent variability. Although standardized HU thresholds and a standardized slice-selection protocol were applied for muscle quantification, minor variation in the selected axial level and manual delineation may still have influenced pectoralis muscle area and attenuation measurements. Recent advances in AI-based body composition analysis have made automated pectoralis muscle quantification from routine chest CT increasingly feasible. Open-source tools such as TotalSegmentator39 have expanded to dedicated muscle segmentation tasks that include pectoralis-related structures, and recent deep learning studies have reported promising performance for automated pectoralis muscle segmentation on chest CT.40,41 The manually delineated pectoralis muscle masks generated in the present study may serve as a reference standard for future model training and validation, supporting the development of AI-based automated quantification workflows in real-world AECOPD cohorts. Future multicenter studies should externally validate these automated approaches in AECOPD patients across heterogeneous scanners, acquisition protocols, and disease severities before clinical implementation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that low pectoralis muscle mass and density are independent predictors of increased in-hospital mortality and the need for IMV in AECOPD. When chest CT is clinically obtained during hospitalization, pectoralis muscle measurements may provide an opportunistic imaging biomarker for early risk stratification. Furthermore, integrating these objective skeletal muscle metrics into established clinical scoring systems, such as DECAF and BAP-65, significantly enhances early risk stratification. These findings highlight the clinical promise of opportunistic body composition analysis for identifying high-risk patients and informing patient management. However, broader implementation will require standardized image-analysis workflows and preferably automated or semi-automated segmentation tools to improve efficiency, reproducibility, and clinical scalability.
