Computing Left Eigenvalues of Quaternion Matrices Journal: Linear Algebra and its Applications
Michael Sebek

Abstract
We present a practical Newton-based method for computing left eigenvalues of quaternion matrices. It uses only standard real/complex linear-algebra kernels via embeddings and applies to matrices of any size. Extensive tests on literature examples and benchmark ensembles, together with a compact MATLAB reference implementation, demonstrate reproducible, certificate-based computations up to size 64 × 64 64\times 64 , including the detection of multiple spherical components and non-generic phenomena such as more than n n isolated left eigenvalues and left-spectrum deficiency.

Introduction

Quaternion matrices arise naturally whenever one needs an algebraic language for coupled
3
– and
4
–dimensional phenomena, including spatial rotations and kinematics, polarization and vector-sensor signal processing, and various formulations of control and estimation. From a linear-algebraic viewpoint,
\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
inherits many familiar constructions, yet the noncommutativity of
\mathbb{H}
makes even basic spectral questions substantially more delicate than over
\mathbb{C}
; see, e.g.,

Ward1997
,
Rodman2014
,
ColomboSabadiniStruppa2011
for background and further pointers.

In this paper, we focus on
left
eigenvalues: a quaternion
\lambda\in\mathbb{H}
is a left eigenvalue of

A\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
if there exists a nonzero vector
v\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
such that

Av=\lambda v.

(1)

This relation is consistent with the intrinsic right scaling of eigenvectors (
v\mapsto vq
), which leaves
\lambda

unchanged. For numerical computation, one must additionally fix a (real)
gauge
for
v
to make local corrections
well posed.

Unlike over
\mathbb{C}
, the
left
spectrum is not invariant under quaternionic similarity, and it can contain both isolated
points and continua (most prominently, spherical families); see, e.g.,
HuangSo2001
,
FarenickPidkowich2003
.
For
2\times 2
matrices, the structure is essentially complete: depending on explicit algebraic conditions, one obtains either two distinct isolated left eigenvalues, a single isolated left eigenvalue (i.e., two coincident ones), or an entire
2
–sphere of left eigenvalues
HuangSo2001
.
In higher dimensions, several frameworks support
qualitative analysis, including characteristic-map constructions that represent
\sigma_{\ell}(A)
as a zero set in
\mathbb{H}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{4}
and illuminate rank-degenerate spectral points

MaciasVirgosPereiraSaez2014
,
MaciasVirgosPereiraSaez2014b
; see also the general background

Zhang1997
,
Zhang2007
,
Rodman2014
.

What is much less settled is
how to compute
left eigenvalues reliably for general
A\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
when
n
is moderate or large. Noncommutativity brings several practical obstacles: (i) gauge freedom must be handled explicitly,
(ii) the finite left spectrum can deviate from the generic
n
-point picture (it may be left-spectrum deficient or contain more than
n
isolated eigenvalues), and
(iii) continuous components imply non-isolated solution sets and rank-deficient Jacobians, so standard local Newton theory and simple “enumeration” logic do not apply.
Moreover, eliminations in the spectral parameter typically lead to high-degree nonlinear problems in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
whose
cost and numerical sensitivity can grow rapidly with
n
. A further open issue is
diagnostics
: from numerical data, one would like to distinguish isolated eigenvalues from samples of a spherical component, and to provide certificates that remain
meaningful under ill-conditioning and degeneracy.

The goal of this work is practical: to provide an effective computational method that (i) keeps a quaternionic interface at
the user level, (ii) uses only standard real/complex linear-algebra kernels internally via embeddings, and (iii) returns
reproducible outputs together with certificates and diagnostics that help interpret atypical spectral situations. Our approach
is built around a direct eigenpair formulation: we apply Newton’s method to the defect equation
Av-\lambda v=0
augmented
by a simple gauge constraint. This avoids determinantal elimination in the spectral parameter and leads to correction steps that reduce to structured linear solves in an embedded real/complex representation.

The main contributions of the paper are as follows.

1.

We derive a Newton iteration for quaternionic left eigenpairs that explicitly fixes the eigenvector gauge, yielding a square real-analytic system and a transparent Jacobian structure. Under a natural nonsingularity condition, we obtain the expected local quadratic convergence for isolated eigenpairs.

2.

We build a robust solver around this Newton core using a trial-based multi-start strategy with acceptance, restart, and de-duplication logic tailored to quaternionic spectra. The intent is not only to obtain approximations, but to obtain
reproducible
candidates together with diagnostics that remain meaningful under ill-conditioning, degeneracy, and
non-isolated behavior.

3.

We validate the method on classical low-dimensional cases with known closed-form spectra (including spherical families) and on explicit matrices drawn from the literature, and we complement these checks by Monte Carlo experiments on several standard matrix families (dense i.i.d., Hermitian, triangular, and sparse), illustrating both typical performance and rare
hard instances.

4.

We provide a compact, stand-alone MATLAB reference implementation intended to support reuse and independent verification. Extended tables and figures, the full experimental protocol and reported metrics, and additional worked examples are deferred to the supplementary material (Supplement A and Supplement B).

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
fixes notation and recalls the embedding used internally for the linear-algebra kernels (with additional material collected in
A
). Section
3
summarizes the left-eigenvalue background needed later. Sections
4
–
6

develop the Newton core and the global solver logic, while Section
7

illustrates representative non-generic phenomena that motivate the refinement and certification stages.

B
summarizes the implementation and validation details.
Supplement A contains the complete experimental protocol, per-size tables and benchmark figures, and implementation-facing diagnostics, while
Supplement B collects additional worked examples (a “Zoo”), including
K>n
cases, left-spectrum deficient constructions,
and spherical or mixed configurations.

2
Quaternions and matrices

2.1
The skew field
\mathbb{H}

We briefly fix the notation for quaternions, quaternion matrices, and the real embedding used by the Newton step; see, e.g.,
Zhang1997
,
Rodman2014
.

Throughout the paper,
\mathbb{H}
denotes the skew field of real quaternions with units
\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}
and relations
\mathbf{i}^{2}=\mathbf{j}^{2}=\mathbf{k}^{2}=\mathbf{i}\mathbf{j}\mathbf{k}=-1
.
Every quaternion can be written as

q=a+b\mathbf{i}+c\mathbf{j}+d\mathbf{k},\qquad a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{R},

with conjugation
\bar{q}:=a-b\mathbf{i}-c\mathbf{j}-d\mathbf{k}
, real part
\operatorname{Re}(q):=a
, imaginary part
\operatorname{Im}(q):=b\mathbf{i}+c\mathbf{j}+d\mathbf{k}
, and norm
|q|:=\sqrt{q\bar{q}}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. For
q\neq 0
,
q^{-1}=\bar{q}/|q|^{2}
. We also write
\mathbb{H}^{\times}:=\mathbb{H}\setminus\{0\}
for the group of units.

2.2
Quaternion vectors and matrices

We treat
\mathbb{H}^{n}
as a right
\mathbb{H}
-module, so scalar multiplication is
x\alpha
and the matrices act as right-linear maps:

A(x\alpha)=(Ax)\alpha
; see
Rodman2014
,
Zhang1997
.
Left multiplication
\alpha x
is nevertheless well-defined componentwise and will be used when needed (in particular in the definition of left eigenvalues).

We write
x^{\ast}
for the conjugate transpose and use the standard (right) inner product

\langle x,y\rangle:=x^{\ast}y\in\mathbb{H},\qquad\|x\|_{2}^{2}:=x^{\ast}x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}.

For
A\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
we denote by

\ker(A):=\{x\in\mathbb{H}^{n}:\ Ax=0\},\quad\Range(A):=\{Ax:\ x\in\mathbb{H}^{n}\}

the right null space and right range, respectively.

We use the rank notion compatible with right-linear maps
x\mapsto Ax
, namely the
column right rank

\rank_{\mathrm{col,right}}(A)
; equivalently one may use the
row left rank

\rank_{\mathrm{row,left}}(A)
, and these coincide. The following proposition is also standard, see, e.g.,
Zhang1997
,
Rodman2014
.

Proposition 1

(Rank–nullity over
\mathbb{H}
)
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
and set

r:=\rank_{\mathrm{col,right}}(A)=\rank_{\mathrm{row,left}}(A),\qquad m:=n-r.

Then
\dim\ker(A)=m
.

2.3
Real embedding

For Newton steps (Section
4
), we solve a real linear system. This is facilitated by a real embedding representing quaternionic left multiplication; see, e.g.,

Zhang1997
,
Rodman2014
.

Definition 1

(Real coordinate map on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
)
.

Define the real-linear isomorphism
\rvec:\mathbb{H}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{4n}
by stacking real coefficients:
if
x_{k}=a_{k}+b_{k}\mathbf{i}+c_{k}\mathbf{j}+d_{k}\mathbf{k}
, then

\rvec(x):=(a_{1},b_{1},c_{1},d_{1},\dots,a_{n},b_{n},c_{n},d_{n})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{4n}.

For
q=a+b\mathbf{i}+c\mathbf{j}+d\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{H}
we also write
\rvec(q):=(a,b,c,d)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
.

Definition 2

(Real left-multiplication matrix)
.

For
q=a+b\mathbf{i}+c\mathbf{j}+d\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{H}
define
L(q)\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times 4}
by

L(q):=\begin{bmatrix}a&-b&-c&-d\\
b&a&-d&c\\
c&d&a&-b\\
d&-c&b&a\end{bmatrix}.

(2)

Theorem 1

(Real representation of quaternion multiplication)
.

For all
p,q\in\mathbb{H}
,

\rvec(qp)=L(q)\,\rvec(p).

Moreover,
L(\cdot)
is a real-algebra homomorphism:

L(p+q)=L(p)+L(q),\qquad L(pq)=L(p)L(q),\qquad L(1)=I_{4}.

Definition 3

(Real embedding of quaternion matrices)
.

For
A=[a_{rs}]\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
define
\rho(A)\in\mathbb{R}^{4n\times 4n}
as the block matrix

\rho(A):=\bigl[L(a_{rs})\bigr]_{r,s=1}^{n},

i.e., the
(r,s)
block of size
4\times 4
equals
L(a_{rs})
.

Theorem 2

(Intertwining and algebraic rules for
\rho(\cdot)
)
.

For all
A,B\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, and
x\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
,

\begin{array}[]{ll}\rvec(Ax)=\rho(A)\,\rvec(x),&\rho(A+B)=\rho(A)+\rho(B),\\[2.0pt]
\rho(AB)=\rho(A)\rho(B),&\rho(\alpha I)=\alpha I.\end{array}

In particular,
A
is invertible over
\mathbb{H}
if and only if
\rho(A)
is invertible over
\mathbb{R}
.

Theorem 3

(Kernel and the factor
4
)
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
. Then

\rvec\bigl(\ker(A)\bigr)=\ker\bigl(\rho(A)\bigr)\subset\mathbb{R}^{4n},\qquad\dim\ker\bigl(\rho(A)\bigr)=4\,\dim\ker(A).

Equivalently, if
r:=\rank_{\mathrm{col,right}}(A)
and
m:=n-r
, then

\rank\bigl(\rho(A)\bigr)=4r,\qquad\dim\ker\bigl(\rho(A)\bigr)=4m.

Proof.

By Theorem
2
,
Ax=0
if and only if
\rho(A)\rvec(x)=0
, so

\rvec(\ker(A))

=\ker(\rho(A))
.
If
\ker(A)
has quaternionic dimension
m
, then
\ker(A)\cong\mathbb{H}^{m}
as a right
\mathbb{H}
-module, which has real dimension
4m
. Since
\rvec
is a real-linear isomorphism,
\dim\ker(\rho(A))=4m=4\dim\ker(A)
. The rank identities follow from real rank–nullity in
\mathbb{R}^{4n}
and Proposition
1
.
∎

3
Left eigenvalues

3.1
Left eigenvalues: definition and basic facts

We recall the definition of left eigenvalues and collect basic properties used later for Newton formulations and residual certification.

Definition 4

(Left eigenvalues)
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
.
A quaternion
\lambda\in\mathbb{H}
is a
left eigenvalue
of
A
if there exists

x\in\mathbb{H}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
such that
Ax=\lambda x
.
The set of all left eigenvalues is denoted by
\sigma_{\ell}(A)
.

Remark 1

(Distinct left eigenvalues)
.

Throughout,
\sigma_{\ell}(A)
denotes the
set of distinct
left eigenvalues (no multiplicities).
In particular, in the
2\times 2
Huang–So reduction to a quaternionic quadratic equation, the two
solutions may coalesce, yielding a
left-spectrum singleton
.
We do not introduce or use any notion of multiplicity of left eigenvalues in this paper.

Theorem 4

(Existence of left eigenvalues)
.

Every
A\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
has at least one left eigenvalue, i.e.
\sigma_{\ell}(A)\neq\emptyset
.

The first proof is due to Wood
Wood1985
; see also
MaciasVirgosPereiraSaez2014
.

Proposition 2

(Right-scaling invariance and uniqueness of the eigenvalue)
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
and suppose
Ax=\lambda x
for some
x\in\mathbb{H}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{H}
.
Then:

1.

for every
q\in\mathbb{H}^{\times}
, also
A(xq)=\lambda(xq)
(a left eigenvector is defined only up to right scaling);

2.

if
Ax=\mu x
for some
\mu\in\mathbb{H}
, then
\mu=\lambda
(for a fixed nonzero
x
the left eigenvalue is unique).

Proof.

(1)
A(xq)=(Ax)q=(\lambda x)q=\lambda(xq)
.
(2) If
Ax=\lambda x=\mu x
, then
(\lambda-\mu)x=0
; choose
k
with
x_{k}\neq 0
and right-cancel
x_{k}
in
\mathbb{H}
.
∎

Consequently, Newton-type methods impose a normalization (gauge) constraint on
x
to obtain a square Jacobian system (Section
4
).

Proposition 3

(Compactness of the left spectrum)
.

For every
A\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
, the set
\sigma_{\ell}(A)
is compact in
\mathbb{H}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{4}
.

Proof.

See Proposition 16 in
MaciasVirgosPereiraSaez2014
.
∎

Proposition 4

(Real shifts and real scaling)
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
with
\beta\neq 0
.
Then

\sigma_{\ell}(A+\alpha I)=\sigma_{\ell}(A)+\alpha,\qquad\sigma_{\ell}(\beta A)=\beta\,\sigma_{\ell}(A).

Proof.

If
Ax=\lambda x
, then
(A+\alpha I)x=(\lambda+\alpha)x
and
(\beta A)x=(\beta\lambda)x
because
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
are central.
The reverse implications are identical.
∎

Remark 2

(Lack of similarity invariance for
\sigma_{\ell}(A)
)
.

Unlike the right spectrum, the left spectrum
\sigma_{\ell}
is not invariant under quaternionic similarity in general.
(However, it
is
invariant under
real
similarity, i.e.,
A\mapsto S^{-1}AS
with
S\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
invertible.)
See
MaciasVirgosPereiraSaez2014
,
DeLeoScolariciSolombrino2002
.

In structured situations,
\sigma_{\ell}(A)
can be read off immediately; this is useful for sanity checks and for
interpreting numerical output.

Proposition 5

(Diagonal and triangular matrices)
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
.

1.

If
A=\diag(q_{1},\dots,q_{n})
is diagonal, then
\sigma_{\ell}(A)=\{q_{1},\dots,q_{n}\}
.

2.

If
A
is upper triangular with diagonal entries
a_{11},\dots,a_{nn}
, then
\sigma_{\ell}(A)\subset\{a_{11},\dots,a_{nn}\}
.

Proof.

See, e.g., Example 21 in
MaciasVirgosPereiraSaez2014
; also
Zhang1997
,
Rodman2014
.
∎

3.2
Spectrum geometry and cardinality

For
2\times 2
matrices, the geometry of
\sigma_{\ell}(A)
is remarkably rigid: Huang and So proved that
\sigma_{\ell}(A)
has either one element, two elements, or infinitely many elements; in the infinite case, it is a
2
-sphere in
\mathbb{H}
(an affine translate of a sphere in the purely imaginary subspace). See
HuangSo2001
(and refinements for special families in
MaciasVirgosPereiraSaez2009
).

For
3\times 3
matrices, characteristic-map / Cayley–Hamilton techniques yield explicit maps whose zeros coincide with
\sigma_{\ell}(A)
in the studied settings
MaciasVirgosPereiraSaez2014b
,
MaciasVirgosPereiraSaez2014
.
These tools establish existence and enable the analysis of concrete examples, but they do not provide a complete, size-independent classification of the possible geometries or cardinalities (finite vs. continuous components, sharp
upper bounds on the number of isolated points, or a general description of mixed configurations).

For larger
n
, the global structure of
\sigma_{\ell}(A)
is far less understood
MaciasVirgosPereiraSaez2014
,
Rodman2014
. The numerical evidence reported below indicates phenomena beyond the classical low-dimensional picture: many isolated left eigenvalues, mixed “spherical + isolated” configurations, and, in some cases, multiple spherical components. This further motivates scalable, direct computational methods.

3.3
The zero eigenvalue: singularity and multiplicity

The eigenvalue
\lambda=0
is special: it is both a left and a right eigenvalue whenever it occurs, and it encodes
singularity in the usual way.

Proposition 6

(Zero eigenvalue and singularity)
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
. Then the following are equivalent:

0\in\sigma_{\ell}(A),\qquad 0\in\sigma_{\mathscr{r}}(A),\qquad\ker(A)\neq\{0\},\qquad A\ \text{is singular}.

Proof.

0\in\sigma_{\ell}(A)
iff
(A-0\cdot I)x=Ax=0
for some
x\neq 0
iff
\ker(A)\neq\{0\}
iff
A
is singular.
The right-eigenvalue statement is identical.
∎

Corollary 1

(Geometric multiplicity of the zero eigenvalue)
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
and let
m:=\dim\ker(A)=n-\rank_{\mathrm{col,right}}(A)
.
Then
0\in\sigma_{\ell}(A)
if and only if
m>0
, and the geometric multiplicity of the left eigenvalue
0
equals
m
.
Moreover, with the real embedding
\rho(\cdot)
,

\dim\ker\bigl(\rho(A)\bigr)=4m
.

Remark 3

(Algebraic multiplicity at
\lambda=0
)
.

The quantity
m=\dim\ker(A)
is the geometric multiplicity of the eigenvalue
0
.
As in the complex case, the algebraic multiplicity can be larger when generalized null chains exist.
In this paper, we only use the geometric multiplicity, which is directly accessible via
\ker(A)
(or via
\rho(A)
).

3.4
Algebraic reductions, and computational approaches

A basic characterization is
\lambda\in\sigma_{\ell}(A)\Longleftrightarrow A-\lambda I
is singular over
\mathbb{H}\Longleftrightarrow\rho(A-\lambda I)
is singular over
\mathbb{R},
where
\rho(\cdot)
is the real embedding from Section
2.3
.
However, this does
not
reduce the problem to an ordinary eigenvalue computation for the fixed real matrix
\rho(A)
,
because
\lambda
does not enter as a scalar shift:

\rho(A-\lambda I)=\rho(A)-I_{n}\otimes L(\lambda),

so the spectral parameter appears blockwise through the
4\times 4
left-multiplication matrix
L(\lambda)
(unless
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, when
L(\lambda)=\lambda I_{4}
). Consequently, determinantal elimination leads to a real-algebraic constraint in the four real components of
\lambda
, but expanding it quickly becomes impractical as
n
grows.

Example 1

(Determinantal elimination is correct but quickly becomes unwieldy)
.

Let
A=[a_{rs}]\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
and write

\lambda=\lambda_{r}+\lambda_{i}\,\mathbf{i}+\lambda_{j}\,\mathbf{j}+\lambda_{k}\,\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{H}.

When eliminating the eigenvector as shown above, we arrive at the single real equation

P(\lambda_{r},\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j},\lambda_{k}):=\det\!\bigl(\rho(A-\lambda I)\bigr)=\det\!\Bigl(\rho(A)-I_{n}\otimes L(\lambda)\Bigr)=0,

(3)

where

\rho(A)=\bigl[L(a_{rs})\bigr]_{r,s=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{4n\times 4n},\quad L(\lambda)=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{r}&-\lambda_{i}&-\lambda_{j}&-\lambda_{k}\\
\lambda_{i}&\lambda_{r}&-\lambda_{k}&\lambda_{j}\\
\lambda_{j}&\lambda_{k}&\lambda_{r}&-\lambda_{i}\\
\lambda_{k}&-\lambda_{j}&\lambda_{i}&\lambda_{r}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times 4}.

Equivalently, (
3
) is the (block) determinantal condition

\det\!\begin{bmatrix}L(a_{11})-L(\lambda)&L(a_{12})&\cdots&L(a_{1n})\\
L(a_{21})&L(a_{22})-L(\lambda)&\cdots&L(a_{2n})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
L(a_{n1})&L(a_{n2})&\cdots&L(a_{nn})-L(\lambda)\end{bmatrix}=0.

Since every entry of
\rho(A)-I_{n}\otimes L(\lambda)
depends
affinely
on

(\lambda_{r},\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j},\lambda_{k})
, the polynomial
P
has total degree at most
4n
.
In practice, however, expanding or globally solving (
3
) rapidly becomes impractical as
n
grows,
and the zero set may include continua (e.g., spherical components), motivating direct solvers for the coupled system
Ax-\lambda x=0
under a gauge constraint (Section
4
).

To avoid working directly with the determinantal condition (
3
)—whose explicit expansion
rapidly becomes prohibitive—several authors instead derive
polynomial systems
in the four real unknowns

(\lambda_{r},\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j},\lambda_{k})
by rewriting
(A-\lambda I)x=0
in real coordinates and eliminating
x
in a more structured way; see, e.g.,
MaciasVirgosPereiraSaez2014
,
LiuKou2019
. Such reductions can be effective
for very small sizes, but the computational bottleneck is then a
global
solution of a nonlinear polynomial system (often of high degree, with spurious/complex solutions and increasing numerical fragility as
n
grows).
In contrast, our Newton formulation keeps the eigenvector
x
and replaces a single global algebraic elimination problem by a sequence of well-conditioned real linear solves inside a local iteration, combined with multiple initializations to explore
\sigma_{\ell}(A)
.

In low dimensions and special matrix classes,
characteristic maps
can sometimes avoid a full global solve and enable explicit analysis; notably, there is a complete
2\times 2
classification
HuangSo2001
, and several
3\times 3
settings admit characteristic-function constructions
MaciasVirgosPereiraSaez2014b
,
MaciasVirgosPereiraSaez2014
.
Closed-form cases such as Proposition
5
apply for arbitrary
n
and are useful as sanity checks.

3.5
Residual certificates for left eigenvalues

We use an eigenpair residual (for a specific vector) and an eigenvalue-only residual obtained by minimizing over unit vectors. Both are measured in the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
(equivalently, on
\mathbb{R}^{4n}
under
\rvec(\cdot)
).

Definition 5

(Eigenpair residual)
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{H}
, and
v\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
with
\|v\|_{2}=1
. Define

\mathrm{res}(A,\lambda,v):=\|Av-\lambda v\|_{2}.

Definition 6

(Minimal (eigenvalue-only) residual)
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{H}
. Define

\mathrm{res}_{\min}(A,\lambda):=\min_{\|x\|_{2}=1}\|Ax-\lambda x\|_{2}.

We use these scalar residual quantities as numerical
certificates
; in contrast, the vector quantity
Ax-\lambda x\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
is referred to as the eigenpair
defect
.

Proposition 7

(Basic relations and singularity test)
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{H}
. Then:

1.

For any unit vector
v\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
,
\ \mathrm{res}_{\min}(A,\lambda)\leq\mathrm{res}(A,\lambda,v)
.

2.

\ \lambda\in\sigma_{\ell}(A)\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \mathrm{res}_{\min}(A,\lambda)=0\ \Longleftrightarrow\ A-\lambda I\text{ is singular over }\mathbb{H}.

3.

With the real embedding
\rho(\cdot)
from Section
2.3
,

\mathrm{res}_{\min}(A,\lambda)=\sigma_{\min}\bigl(\rho(A-\lambda I)\bigr),

where
\sigma_{\min}(\cdot)
is the smallest singular value of
\rho(A-\lambda I)\in\mathbb{R}^{4n\times 4n}
.

Proof.

(1) is immediate from the minimum definition. (2) holds because
\mathrm{res}_{\min}(A,\lambda)=0
iff

(A-\lambda I)x=0
has a nontrivial solution. For (3),
\rho(A-\lambda I)
represents the real-linear operator

x\mapsto(A-\lambda I)x
and
\rvec
is an isometry between
\mathbb{H}^{n}
(with
\|\cdot\|_{2}
) and
\mathbb{R}^{4n}
(Euclidean norm),
so the minimum over
\|x\|_{2}=1
equals the smallest singular value.
∎

In numerical experiments we primarily report
\mathrm{res}(A,\lambda_{k},v_{k})
for accepted eigenpairs
(
B.2
). When a vector-free check is needed,
\mathrm{res}_{\min}(A,\lambda)
can be evaluated via

\sigma_{\min}\bigl(\rho(A-\lambda I)\bigr)
.

3.6
Contrast with right eigenvalues

For context only (we do not compute right eigenvalues here),
\mu\in\mathbb{H}
is a
right eigenvalue
of
A

if
Ay=y\mu
for some
y\in\mathbb{H}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
; the corresponding set is
\sigma_{\mathscr{r}}(A)
. A key qualitative difference from left eigenvalues is the effect of right-scaling the eigenvector: if
Ay=y\mu
and
q\in\mathbb{H}^{\times}
, then

A(yq)=y(\mu q)=(yq)(q^{-1}\mu q).

Thus, the same right-eigenspace can be represented by different quaternions related by

\mu^{\prime}=q^{-1}\mu q,

a relation often called
similarity
(or conjugacy) in
\mathbb{H}
. The set

[\mu]:=\{\,s^{-1}\mu s:\ s\in\mathbb{H}^{\times}\,\}

is the
similarity class
of
\mu
; it is a single point if
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
, and otherwise a
2
-sphere determined by
\operatorname{Re}(\mu)
and
|\operatorname{Im}(\mu)|

Zhang1997
,
Rodman2014
. Accordingly, right eigenvalues are naturally defined only up to similarity, and
\sigma_{\mathscr{r}}(A)
is similarity-invariant; it is commonly studied via the complex adjoint map
\chi(\cdot)

(
A
)
Zhang1997
,
Zhang2007
,
Rodman2014
.

By contrast, left eigenvalues satisfy
Ax=\lambda x
and are unchanged under right scaling of eigenvectors (Proposition
2
), but
\sigma_{\ell}(A)
is generally
not
invariant under quaternionic similarity
(Rem.
2
). This asymmetry is one of the reasons why methods tailored to
\sigma_{\mathscr{r}}(A)
do not directly carry over to computing
\sigma_{\ell}(A)
.

As a minimal sanity check, for
A=[q]\in\mathbb{H}^{1\times 1}
one has
\sigma_{\ell}(A)=\{q\}
, whereas

\sigma_{\mathscr{r}}(A)=[q]
is the full similarity class of
q
.

4
Newton iteration for left eigenpairs with a gauge constraint

Let
A\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
. The main contribution of this paper is a Newton-type method for computing left eigenpairs

(\lambda,x)\in\mathbb{H}\times(\mathbb{H}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
by solving the defect equation

Ax-\lambda x=0.

(4)

By Proposition
2
, (
4
) is invariant under right scaling
x\mapsto xq
.
To obtain a square, smooth system (in real coordinates), we fix this freedom by imposing a gauge constraint and apply Newton’s method to the resulting gauged system.

A practical advantage of this formulation is that Newton is applied directly to the
eigenpair defect

Ax-\lambda x
(augmented only by simple gauge constraints), rather than to a determinantal or characteristic-map equation in the spectral parameter alone. This keeps each correction step as a structured linear-algebraic problem in

(\Delta\lambda,\Delta x)
(Section
4.3
), which in our experience yields more robust local behavior than Newton iterations based on polynomial or characteristic eliminations, even in low-degree cases.

4.1
Gauge constraint and regauging

A left eigenvector is not unique: if
Ax=\lambda x
, then
A(xq)=\lambda(xq)
for any
q\in\mathbb{H}^{\times}
(Proposition
2
). This right-scaling freedom has four real degrees of freedom (the four real components
of
q
), and without fixing it, the eigenpair equation does not define an isolated root in
(\lambda,x)
.
Consequently, the Jacobian of the raw defect equation
Ax-\lambda x=0
is typically singular along the scaling orbit.
To obtain a square smooth system in real coordinates and a well-posed Newton correction, we fix the freedom by imposing four real constraints, selecting a
canonical representative
from each orbit
\{xq:\ q\in\mathbb{H}^{\times}\}
.

Fix an index
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
(a
pivot
). We enforce two conditions:
(i) normalization
\|x\|_{2}=1
(one real constraint), and (ii) a
real pivot

x_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
(three real constraints), with the branch
x_{j}>0
chosen to remove the remaining sign ambiguity. This choice is particularly convenient here:
It is constructive (every
x
with
x_{j}\neq 0
can be regauged explicitly), it is numerically stable when
j
is chosen as an index of a large component of
x
, and its linearization leads to simple constraint rows in the real Newton system (Section
4.3
).

The gauge is encoded by a smooth constraint map

g:\mathbb{H}^{n}\setminus\{x:\ x_{j}=0\}\to\mathbb{R}^{4},\qquad x\mapsto g(x):=\begin{bmatrix}\|x\|_{2}^{2}-1\\[0.85358pt]
\operatorname{Im}(x_{j})\end{bmatrix}.

(5)

The (smooth) gauge constraint is

g(x)=0,

(6)

which imposes
\|x\|_{2}=1
and
x_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
. The additional branch selection
x_{j}>0
is enforced by regauging
and is not included in the smooth system.

Lemma 1

(Regauging to (
6
))
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
and assume
x_{j}\neq 0
for some
j
.
Define

q_{1}:=x_{j}^{-1}|x_{j}|\in\mathbb{H}^{\times},\;\;\tilde{x}:=xq_{1},\;\;q_{2}:=\|\tilde{x}\|_{2}^{-1}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0},\;\;x^{\mathrm{g}}:=\tilde{x}\,q_{2}.

Then
g(x^{\mathrm{g}})=0
and
(x^{\mathrm{g}})_{j}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.
Moreover, if
(\lambda,x)
satisfies (
4
), then
(\lambda,x^{\mathrm{g}})
also satisfies (
4
).

Proof.

Right scaling preserves (
4
):

A(xq)=(Ax)q=(\lambda x)q=\lambda(xq)
for any
q\in\mathbb{H}^{\times}
.
With
q_{1}=x_{j}^{-1}|x_{j}|
we have
\tilde{x}_{j}=x_{j}q_{1}=|x_{j}|\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.
Finally,
q_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
normalizes the Euclidean norm, hence
\|x^{\mathrm{g}}\|_{2}=1
and

(x^{\mathrm{g}})_{j}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, which implies
g(x^{\mathrm{g}})=0
.
∎

Example 2

(Concrete regauging)
.

Let
n=2
and
x=(1+\mathbf{j},\ 2\mathbf{i})^{\top}
and choose
j=1
.
Then
x_{1}=1+\mathbf{j}
has modulus
|x_{1}|=\sqrt{2}
and inverse
(1+\mathbf{j})^{-1}=\tfrac{1}{2}(1-\mathbf{j})
.
Following Lemma
1
, set

q_{1}:=x_{1}^{-1}|x_{1}|=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}(1-\mathbf{j}),\;\;\tilde{x}:=xq_{1},\;\;q_{2}:=\|\tilde{x}\|_{2}^{-1},\;\;x^{\mathrm{g}}:=\tilde{x}\,q_{2}.

A direct multiplication gives

\tilde{x}_{1}=(1+\mathbf{j})q_{1}=|x_{1}|=\sqrt{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0},\qquad\tilde{x}_{2}=(2\mathbf{i})q_{1}=\sqrt{2}\,(\mathbf{i}+\mathbf{k}),

and since
\|\tilde{x}\|_{2}^{2}=|\tilde{x}_{1}|^{2}+|\tilde{x}_{2}|^{2}=2+4=6
we have
q_{2}=1/\sqrt{6}
, hence

x^{\mathrm{g}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{2}\\
\sqrt{2}(\mathbf{i}+\mathbf{k})\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1/\sqrt{3}\\
(\mathbf{i}+\mathbf{k})/\sqrt{3}\end{bmatrix}.

Therefore
\|x^{\mathrm{g}}\|_{2}=1
and
(x^{\mathrm{g}})_{1}=1/\sqrt{3}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, i.e.,
g(x^{\mathrm{g}})=0
.

Remark 4

(Gauge as a local cross-section and the role of the pivot)
.

The right-scaling action
x\mapsto xq
(
q\in\mathbb{H}^{\times}
) has four real degrees of freedom, and the constraint
g(x)=0
fixes them by selecting a local cross-section of this action on the open set
\{x:\ x_{j}\neq 0\}
.
The exclusion of
x_{j}=0
is therefore essential: if the pivot entry vanishes, the condition “
x_{j}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
” cannot be enforced, and the gauge ceases to define a smooth local section.
In computations, we pick
j\in\arg\max_{k}|x_{k}|
to keep
|x_{j}|
away from zero and to improve conditioning of the linearized constraints; if
|x_{j}|
becomes small during the iteration, one may switch the pivot and regauge.

4.2
Quaternion-form Newton correction with gauge constraints

As discussed in Section
4.1
, the eigenpair equation
Ax=\lambda x
is invariant under the four-real-parameter right scaling
x\mapsto xq
(
q\in\mathbb{H}^{\times}
). The gauge constraint selects a canonical representative on the open set
\{x:\ x_{j}\neq 0\}
, turning the left eigenpair condition into a square smooth system in real coordinates. We now derive the corresponding Newton correction equations directly in
\mathbb{H}
-form.

Define the eigenpair
defect
(a vector in
\mathbb{H}^{n}
)

d(\lambda,x):=Ax-\lambda x\in\mathbb{H}^{n}.

(Scalar residual
certificates
are reported separately as
\mathrm{res}(A,\lambda,v)=\|Av-\lambda v\|_{2}
and

\mathrm{res}_{\min}(A,\lambda)=\sigma_{\min}\!\bigl(\rho(A-\lambda I)\bigr)
.)
Then fix a pivot index
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
(kept
fixed
in the local analysis), and use the gauge map (
5
).
The associated augmented map is

F:\mathbb{H}\times\bigl(\mathbb{H}^{n}\setminus\{x:\ x_{j}=0\}\bigr)\to\mathbb{H}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{4},\;\;F(\lambda,x):=\bigl(d(\lambda,x),\,g(x)\bigr).

(7)

Thus, solving
F(\lambda,x)=(0,0)
enforces both the eigenpair condition
d(\lambda,x)=0
and the gauge
g(x)=0
,
so that
(\lambda,x)
is a
gauged
left eigenpair.
Conversely, any left eigenpair
(\lambda,x)
with
x_{j}\neq 0
can be regauged (Lemma
1
)
to satisfy
F(\lambda,x)=(0,0)
without changing
\lambda
.
All derivatives below are understood with respect to the underlying real coordinates

\mathbb{H}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{4}
and
\mathbb{H}^{n}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{4n}
.

Jacobian and Newton step.

Let
(\lambda,x)
be a current (regauged) iterate and consider increments

(\Delta\lambda,\Delta x)\in\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{H}^{n}
.
Newton’s method chooses
(\Delta\lambda,\Delta x)
so that the first-order (Jacobian) approximation of

F(\lambda+\Delta\lambda,\,x+\Delta x)
cancels the current defect
F(\lambda,x)
, i.e.,

DF(\lambda,x)[\Delta\lambda,\Delta x]=-\,F(\lambda,x),

(8)

where
DF(\lambda,x)
denotes the Fréchet derivative of
F
at
(\lambda,x)
.
In block form,

DF(\lambda,x)[\Delta\lambda,\Delta x]=\begin{bmatrix}(A-\lambda I)\Delta x-(\Delta\lambda)\,x\\[1.42262pt]
Dg(x)[\Delta x]\end{bmatrix},

where
(\Delta\lambda)\,x
denotes left scalar multiplication of
x
by
\Delta\lambda\in\mathbb{H}
.
Thus, the Newton correction consists of

(A-\lambda I)\,\Delta x-(\Delta\lambda)\,x\;=\;-\,d(\lambda,x),

(9)

together with the linearized gauge constraint
Dg(x)[\Delta x]=-\,g(x)
.
Since the iterates are kept regauged (so that
g(x)=0
), this reduces to
Dg(x)[\Delta x]=0
,
meaning that
\Delta x
preserves the gauge
to first order
.

Lemma 2

(Linearized gauge constraints)
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
and
\Delta x\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
. Then

Dg(x)[\Delta x]=\begin{bmatrix}2\operatorname{Re}(x^{\ast}\Delta x)\\[0.85358pt]
\operatorname{Im}(\Delta x_{j})\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}.

In particular, if
g(x)=0
, then the gauge is maintained to first order by imposing

\operatorname{Re}(x^{\ast}\Delta x)=0,\qquad\operatorname{Im}(\Delta x_{j})=0.

(10)

Proof.

The gauge map
g(x)
consists of the normalization constraint
\|x\|_{2}^{2}-1=0
and of the requirement that the selected
scalar entry
x_{j}
is real, i.e.,
\operatorname{Im}(x_{j})=0\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Since
\|x\|_{2}^{2}=x^{\ast}x\in\mathbb{R}
, the directional derivative in the direction
\Delta x
is

D(\|x\|_{2}^{2})[\Delta x]=D(x^{\ast}x)[\Delta x]=(\Delta x)^{\ast}x+x^{\ast}(\Delta x)=2\operatorname{Re}(x^{\ast}\Delta x),

because
(\Delta x)^{\ast}x=\overline{x^{\ast}\Delta x}
and
q+\overline{q}=2\operatorname{Re}(q)
for any
q\in\mathbb{H}
.

For the remaining three constraints, note that
x_{j}\in\mathbb{H}
is a scalar quaternion and the map

\operatorname{Im}:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R}^{3},\qquad a+b\mathbf{i}+c\mathbf{j}+d\mathbf{k}\mapsto(b,c,d),

is linear. Hence

D(\operatorname{Im}(x_{j}))[\Delta x]=\operatorname{Im}(\Delta x_{j})\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

i.e., the last three components of
Dg(x)[\Delta x]
are exactly the imaginary components of the increment in the selected entry
x_{j}
.
∎

Example 3

(Newton equations for
n=1
)
.

Let
n=1
and
A=[q]
. Then
d(\lambda,x)=(q-\lambda)x
.
Any solution has
\lambda=q
and arbitrary
x\neq 0
; a gauge such as
\|x\|_{2}=1
and
x\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
selects the
unique gauged eigenvector
x=1
.

Example 4

(A diagonal
2\times 2
sanity check)
.

Let
A=\diag(\alpha,\beta)
with
\alpha\neq\beta
in
\mathbb{R}
and consider the exact left eigenpair

(\lambda_{\star},x_{\star})=(\alpha,e_{1})
. With the pivot choice
j=1
, the gauge
g(e_{1})=0
holds.
At
(\lambda,x)=(\alpha,e_{1})
, the defect is zero, hence the Newton correction solves a homogeneous system and

\Delta\lambda=\Delta x=0
.
This is consistent with quadratic convergence at a simple (isolated) root.

4.3
Real-coordinate form and the linear Newton system

The correction equations derived in Section
4.2
are most naturally written in
\mathbb{H}
-form.
However, they define a
real
Newton step: the unknowns
(\Delta x,\Delta\lambda)
contain
4n+4
real degrees of
freedom, the gauge constraints take values in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
, and the Jacobian is an
\mathbb{R}
-linear operator.
Rather than introducing quaternionic linear algebra for such mixed
\mathbb{H}
/
\mathbb{R}
constraints, we pass to real coordinates,
where one Newton step becomes a single square real linear system that can be solved robustly by standard routines.

Real representation of
A
and of the scalar coupling.

Let
\rho(A)\in\mathbb{R}^{4n\times 4n}
be as in Def.
3
. We write

u:=\rvec(\Delta x)\in\mathbb{R}^{4n},\qquad w:=\rvec(\Delta\lambda)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}.

Besides the left-multiplication matrix
L(\cdot)
(Def.
2
), we use the real matrix
R(q)\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times 4}

representing
right
multiplication by
q
, defined by

\rvec(pq)=R(q)\,\rvec(p)\qquad\text{for all }p,q\in\mathbb{H}.

If
q=a+b\mathbf{i}+c\mathbf{j}+d\mathbf{k}
, then

R(q):=\begin{bmatrix}a&-b&-c&-d\\
b&a&d&-c\\
c&-d&a&b\\
d&c&-b&a\end{bmatrix}.

Therefore, the map
\Delta\lambda\mapsto(\Delta\lambda)x
becomes

\rvec\bigl((\Delta\lambda)x\bigr)=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}R(x_{1})\\
\vdots\\
R(x_{n})\end{bmatrix}}_{=:B(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{4n\times 4}}\,\rvec(\Delta\lambda)=B(x)\,w.

(11)

Example 5

(The coupling matrix
B(x)
for a simple
x
)
.

Let
n=2
and
x=(1,\ \mathbf{j})^{\top}
. Then

B(x)=\begin{bmatrix}R(1)\\
R(\mathbf{j})\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}I_{4}\\
R(\mathbf{j})\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{8\times 4}.

Real form of the constraints.

Let
\rvec(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{4n}
be the stacked real coefficients of
x\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
. Then

\operatorname{Re}(x^{\ast}\Delta x)=\rvec(x)^{\top}\rvec(\Delta x)=\rvec(x)^{\top}u,

and
\operatorname{Im}(\Delta x_{j})=0
selects the three imaginary coordinates of the
j
th quaternion component of
\Delta x
.
Accordingly, the four linearized constraints (
10
) can be written as

C(x)\,u=0,

(12)

where
C(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times 4n}
has first row
\rvec(x)^{\top}
and the remaining three rows are coordinate selectors
extracting the
(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k})
-entries of the
j
th quaternion in the ordering of Def.
1
.

Example 6

(Constraint rows
C(x)
in a toy case)
.

Let
n=2
, pivot
j=1
, and write
u=\rvec(\Delta x)\in\mathbb{R}^{8}
. Then
C(x)u
consists of:
(i) one row
\rvec(x)^{\top}u
enforcing
\operatorname{Re}(x^{\ast}\Delta x)=0
,
(ii) three rows extracting the imaginary coordinates of
\Delta x_{1}
.

The Newton linear system.

Let
u=\rvec(\Delta x)
and
w=\rvec(\Delta\lambda)
. Then the quaternionic Newton equations
(
9
) together with the linearized gauge constraints (
10
)
are equivalent to the square real system

\begin{bmatrix}\rho(A-\lambda I)&-B(x)\\
C(x)&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}u\\
w\end{bmatrix}=-\begin{bmatrix}\rvec\bigl(d(\lambda,x)\bigr)\\
0\end{bmatrix},

(13)

where the zero in the lower block uses
g(x)=0
(the iterates are regauged before each linear solve).
System (

) has dimension
(4n+4)\times(4n+4)
and can be solved by standard real linear-algebra
routines
Zhang1997
,
Rodman2014
,
FarenickPidkowich2003
.

Remark 5

(Consistency check for the embedding)
.

The core Newton equation (
9
) in
\mathbb{H}^{n}
reads

(A-\lambda I)\Delta x-(\Delta\lambda)x=-r.

Applying
\rvec(\cdot)
and using Theorem
2
, together with the definitions of
B(x)
and

,
gives

\rho(A-\lambda I)u-B(x)w=-\rvec(r),

which is exactly the first block row of (

).

4.4
Local convergence

Quadratic convergence of Newton’s method is guaranteed only in a neighborhood of an
isolated
solution of the
gauged
system
F(\lambda,x)=(0,0)
, equivalently when the Jacobian of
F
at the solution is invertible.
This motivates the following simplicity notion.

Definition 7

(Simple gauged left eigenpair)
.

Let
(\lambda_{\star},x_{\star})\in\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{H}^{n}
satisfy
Ax_{\star}=\lambda_{\star}x_{\star}
and
g(x_{\star})=0
,
with
(x_{\star})_{j}\neq 0
.
We call
(\lambda_{\star},x_{\star})

simple (gauged)
if:

1.

\ker(A-\lambda_{\star}I)=\{x_{\star}q:\ q\in\mathbb{H}\}
(geometric multiplicity
1
);

2.

x_{\star}\notin\Range(A-\lambda_{\star}I)
.

Here condition (1) excludes additional independent eigenvectors, while condition (2) rules out a
generalized-eigenvector relation; together they ensure that the Jacobian of the gauged system is invertible at

(\lambda_{\star},x_{\star})
, which is the standard hypothesis for local quadratic convergence of Newton’s method.

Theorem 5

(Local quadratic convergence)
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
and let
(\lambda_{\star},x_{\star})
be a simple gauged left eigenpair
(Def.
7
). Then there exists a neighborhood
\mathcal{U}
of
(\lambda_{\star},x_{\star})

(in
\mathbb{R}^{4n+4}
coordinates) such that, for every initial
(\lambda_{0},x_{0})\in\mathcal{U}
with
(x_{0})_{j}\neq 0
,
the Newton iteration defined by (
9
) and (
10
) is well-defined and,
after regauging each step as in Lem.
1
, converges to
(\lambda_{\star},x_{\star})
with quadratic rate.

Proof.

In real coordinates, the Newton system is a square smooth system, so local quadratic convergence follows
from the classical Newton theorem once the Jacobian is invertible. We therefore show that the
homogeneous
Jacobian
equations admit only the trivial solution. Condition (2) will rule out a nonzero
\Delta\lambda
, condition (1) will force

\Delta x
to be a pure right-scaling of
x_{\star}
, and the linearized gauge constraints will eliminate exactly that scaling.

We work in real coordinates, where
F
is a
C^{\infty}
map
\mathbb{R}^{4n+4}\to\mathbb{R}^{4n+4}
: the map

(\lambda,x)\mapsto Ax-\lambda x
is bilinear over
\mathbb{R}
, and
g
is polynomial in the real components.
Thus, standard local Newton theory applies once the Jacobian is invertible; see, e.g.,

OrtegaRheinboldt2000
,
Deuflhard2011
,
DennisSchnabel1996
.

At a solution
(\lambda_{\star},x_{\star})
, the homogeneous Jacobian system reads

(A-\lambda_{\star}I)\Delta x-(\Delta\lambda)x_{\star}=0,\qquad\operatorname{Re}(x_{\star}^{\ast}\Delta x)=0,\qquad\operatorname{Im}(\Delta x_{j})=0.

We show that this system has only
\Delta\lambda=0
and
\Delta x=0
.

Assume first that
\Delta\lambda\neq 0
. Then the first equation implies

(\Delta\lambda)x_{\star}=(A-\lambda_{\star}I)\Delta x\in\Range(A-\lambda_{\star}I).

Since every nonzero quaternion is invertible, right-multiplication by
(\Delta\lambda)^{-1}
gives

x_{\star}\in\Range(A-\lambda_{\star}I)
, contradicting condition (2). Hence
\Delta\lambda=0
, and

(A-\lambda_{\star}I)\Delta x=0.

By condition (1) (geometric multiplicity one),
\ker(A-\lambda_{\star}I)=\{x_{\star}q:\ q\in\mathbb{H}\}
, so there exists

q\in\mathbb{H}
such that
\Delta x=x_{\star}q
.

We now use the linearized gauge constraints to show that
q=0
.
From
\operatorname{Re}(x_{\star}^{\ast}\Delta x)=0
and
\Delta x=x_{\star}q
we obtain

0=\operatorname{Re}(x_{\star}^{\ast}x_{\star}q)=\operatorname{Re}\bigl((x_{\star}^{\ast}x_{\star})\,q\bigr)=\operatorname{Re}\bigl(\|x_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\,q\bigr)=\|x_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\,\operatorname{Re}(q),

hence
\operatorname{Re}(q)=0
(since
\|x_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}>0
).

Next, the constraint
\operatorname{Im}(\Delta x_{j})=0
gives

0=\operatorname{Im}(\Delta x_{j})=\operatorname{Im}\bigl((x_{\star})_{j}\,q\bigr).

Because
g(x_{\star})=0
and
(x_{\star})_{j}\neq 0
, the chosen pivot satisfies
(x_{\star})_{j}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.
Multiplication by a nonzero real scalar preserves (and only rescales) the imaginary part, so

\operatorname{Im}\bigl((x_{\star})_{j}\,q\bigr)=(x_{\star})_{j}\,\operatorname{Im}(q)=0\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\operatorname{Im}(q)=0.

Thus
\operatorname{Re}(q)=0
and
\operatorname{Im}(q)=0
, so
q=0
and consequently
\Delta x=x_{\star}q=0
.

We have shown that the homogeneous Jacobian system admits only the trivial solution, so the Jacobian of
F
is invertible at

(\lambda_{\star},x_{\star})
. Local quadratic convergence of the (regauged) Newton iteration then follows from the classical
Newton theorem in
\mathbb{R}^{4n+4}
.
∎

4.5
Spherical solution sets

Theorem
5
covers the
isolated
case, where the Jacobian of the gauged system is invertible.
This framework does not apply when the left spectrum contains continua (most notably, spherical components). In that situation, the zero set of

F(\lambda,x)=\bigl(Ax-\lambda x,\ g(x)\bigr)

has positive dimension, and consequently
DF
is rank deficient at every point on that set. Hence, one cannot, in general, expect quadratic convergence to a
uniquely determined
root.

Proposition 8

(Non-isolated zeros imply Jacobian singularity)
.

Assume
F
is
C^{1}
in real coordinates. If
(\lambda_{\star},x_{\star})
is not an isolated zero of
F
, then

DF(\lambda_{\star},x_{\star})
is not invertible.

Proof.

If
(\lambda_{\star},x_{\star})
is not isolated, there exists a differentiable curve

t\mapsto(\lambda(t),x(t))
with
(\lambda(0),x(0))=(\lambda_{\star},x_{\star})
and
F(\lambda(t),x(t))\equiv 0

for
t
in a neighborhood of
0
. Differentiating at
t=0
yields

DF(\lambda_{\star},x_{\star})\,[\dot{\lambda}(0),\dot{x}(0)]=0,

with
(\dot{\lambda}(0),\dot{x}(0))\neq 0
, hence the kernel of
DF(\lambda_{\star},x_{\star})
is nontrivial.
∎

Nevertheless, Newton steps remain valuable as a
local defect-reduction
mechanism: the correction equations reduce
\|F(\lambda,x)\|
in directions transverse to the solution set, while motion
along
a continuum is not
controlled and is typically determined by the initial guess and algorithmic choices (e.g., damping, stopping rule, and the linear solver). For this reason, we do not claim a uniqueness or quadratic-rate theorem in spherical cases.

At the same time, the same multi-start Newton pipeline that is used to recover isolated eigenvalues can be exploited to
detect spherical components
of
\sigma_{\ell}(A)
. When a spherical continuum is present, different initializations typically converge to different points on the same manifold. Collecting sufficiently many distinct converged points thus provides samples from the component. In our computations, we post-process these samples in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
by clustering and by robustly fitting a sphere model (center, radius, and the supporting affine
3
-space), with outlier rejection, and we
validate the inliers by residual-based certificates (Section
3.5
). This does not give
completeness guarantees—Newton does not “enumerate” a continuum—but it yields reproducible numerical evidence for spherical structure, as illustrated in Supplement B.

Example 7

(A spherical case where Newton is delicate)
.

For

A=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}\subset\mathbb{H}^{2\times 2},

the left spectrum is a
2
-sphere, hence eigenpairs are not isolated; see
HuangSo2001
.
This non-isolated structure is reflected in the Jacobian of the gauged system.

Fix the pivot index
j=1
and consider the gauged solution

\lambda=\mathbf{i},\qquad x=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\mathbf{i}\end{bmatrix},

so that
Ax=\lambda x
,
\|x\|_{2}=1
, and
x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.
Then the homogeneous Jacobian equations

(A-\lambda I)\Delta x-(\Delta\lambda)x=0,\qquad\operatorname{Re}(x^{\ast}\Delta x)=0,\qquad\operatorname{Im}(\Delta x_{1})=0

admit a nonzero solution, for instance

\Delta\lambda=\mathbf{j},\qquad\Delta x=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\mathbf{j}\end{bmatrix}.

Indeed,

(A-\mathbf{i}I)\Delta x=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{j}\\
-\mathbf{i}\mathbf{j}\end{bmatrix}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{j}\\
-\mathbf{k}\end{bmatrix}=(\Delta\lambda)x,

and
\operatorname{Im}(\Delta x_{1})=0
, while
x^{\ast}\Delta x=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{i}\mathbf{j}=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{k}
, hence
\operatorname{Re}(x^{\ast}\Delta x)=0
.
Therefore, the Jacobian of the gauged system is not invertible at
(\lambda,x)
, and quadratic convergence cannot be
expected in general.

4.6
Algorithm

Algorithm 1

(Direct Newton iteration for a gauged left eigenpair
Ax=\lambda x
)
.

Input:

A\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
; initial guess
(\lambda^{(0)},x^{(0)})
with
x^{(0)}\neq 0
;
tolerance
\varepsilon
; maximum iterations
K_{\max}
; damping parameter
\alpha\in(0,1]
(optional).

Output:
approximate gauged left eigenpair
(\lambda,x)
and residual
\|Ax-\lambda x\|_{2}
.

1.

Initialization and pivot selection:

set
\lambda\leftarrow\lambda^{(0)}
,
x\leftarrow x^{(0)}
, choose

j\in\arg\max_{k}|x_{k}|
, and regauge
x
(Lem.
1
) so that
g(x)=0
and
x_{j}>0
.

2.

Iterate
for
k=0,1,\dots,K_{\max}-1
:

(a)

Residual check:
set
r\leftarrow Ax-\lambda x
. If
\|r\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon
, terminate.

(b)

Newton correction:
solve (
9
) together with (
10
)
(equivalently, solve the real system (
13
)) for
(\Delta x,\Delta\lambda)
.

(c)

Damped update:
set
x\leftarrow x+\alpha\,\Delta x
and
\lambda\leftarrow\lambda+\alpha\,\Delta\lambda
.

(d)

Regauging:
enforce
g(x)=0
and
x_{j}>0
again using Lem.
1
.
Optionally, if
|x_{j}|
becomes too small, switch the pivot index and regauge
(for the local theory, keep
j
fixed).

3.

Return:

(\lambda,x)
and
\|Ax-\lambda x\|_{2}
.

Example 8

(One-step Newton sanity check on a shifted diagonal)
.

Let
A=\diag(\alpha,\beta)
with
\alpha\neq\beta
in
\mathbb{R}
. Consider the left eigenpair near

(\lambda_{\star},x_{\star})=(\alpha,e_{1})
and choose pivot
j=1
.
If
(\lambda,x)
is gauged with
x=(x_{1},x_{2})^{\top}
and
x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, then

d(\lambda,x)=\begin{bmatrix}(\alpha-\lambda)x_{1}\\
(\beta-\lambda)x_{2}\end{bmatrix}.

Equation (
9
) decouples in the second component as

(\beta-\lambda)\Delta x_{2}-(\Delta\lambda)x_{2}=-(\beta-\lambda)x_{2},

which is solved by
\Delta x_{2}=-x_{2}
when
\Delta\lambda=0
.
Thus, away from the target eigenpair, a Newton step tends to suppress spurious components in the eigenvector.

4.7
Extensions

Remark 6

(Generalized pencils and Rosenbrock-type systems)
.

The same construction applies to generalized left eigenpairs of a quaternionic pencil
M-\lambda E
:
solve
(M-\lambda E)z=0
and impose a gauge on
z
(or on
Ez
when
Ez\neq 0
).
The Newton correction remains affine in
\lambda
,

(M-\lambda E)\Delta z-(\Delta\lambda)\,Ez=-\bigl(M-\lambda E\bigr)z,

together with the same four real constraints that fix the right scaling.
This is particularly convenient for descriptor and Rosenbrock-type linearizations, where the dependence on
\lambda

is affine by design.

6
Refinement of numerically found left eigenvalues

The results reported in Supplement A were obtained by the reference implementation described in
B.1
, which implements the method of this paper.
Although the computed left eigenvalues typically come with small residuals, it is often beneficial to further
polish
them.
This post-processing step improves numerical reproducibility, strengthens the credibility of rare or surprising instances, and, in many cases, brings the residuals down to (near) machine precision, as documented in the examples in the following section.

6.1
Residual certificates and scale-aware normalization

We use the residual-based certificates introduced in Section
3.5
.
When
A
is fixed by context, we abbreviate

\mathrm{res}(\lambda,v):=\mathrm{res}(A,\lambda,v)
and

\mathrm{res}_{\min}(\lambda):=\mathrm{res}_{\min}(A,\lambda)
.

For an eigenpair candidate
(\lambda,v)
with
\|v\|_{2}=1
, the (pair) residual is

\mathrm{res}(A,\lambda,v)=\|Av-\lambda v\|_{2}.

The eigenvalue-only certificate is the minimal residual

\mathrm{res}_{\min}(A,\lambda)=\min_{\|x\|_{2}=1}\|Ax-\lambda x\|_{2}=\sigma_{\min}\!\bigl(\rho(A-\lambda I)\bigr),

where
\rho(\cdot)
is the fixed real (or complex) embedding used for numerical linear-algebra kernels
and
\sigma_{\min}(\cdot)
is the smallest singular value in that embedding.

Since scaling matters, we also report certificates normalized by a robust matrix scale,

s(A):=\max\{1,\|A\|_{2}\},\qquad\mathrm{res}_{\min}^{\mathrm{rel}}(A,\lambda):=\frac{\mathrm{res}_{\min}(A,\lambda)}{s(A)}.

Reporting both absolute and normalized values separates genuine numerical difficulty from trivial rescaling effects.

6.2
Two-stage polishing (standard post-processing)

Given a candidate
\lambda_{0}
returned by the main algorithm, we apply a lightweight two-stage refinement strategy that is
standard in numerical linear algebra: first improve the
certificate
, then perform a short local correction on the
eigenpair equations; see, e.g.,
GolubVanLoan2013
.

Stage 1: certificate-driven refinement in
\lambda
.

We interpret
\lambda=a+b\mathbf{i}+c\mathbf{j}+d\mathbf{k}
as a point in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
and (locally) minimize

\phi(\lambda):=\mathrm{res}_{\min}(\lambda)=\sigma_{\min}(\rho(A-\lambda I)),

starting from
\lambda_{0}
. In practice, this is done by a small number of random perturbations of
\lambda_{0}
at a few radii,
followed by a short derivative-free local search initialized from the best sampled point
NelderMead1965
. This stage returns a refined

\lambda_{\star}
and an associated near-minimizing vector
v_{\star}
(e.g., a right singular vector corresponding to

\sigma_{\min}(A-\lambda_{\star}I)
in the chosen embedding).

Stage 2: local eigenpair correction.

Starting from
(\lambda_{\star},v_{\star})
, we perform a small number of damped Newton (or quasi-Newton) correction steps on

F(\lambda,v)=Av-\lambda v=0,\qquad\|v\|=1,

using a simple gauge-fixing convention to remove the (right) scalar ambiguity of
v
. When Stage 1 has already reduced

\mathrm{res}_{\min}
, this local correction typically converges rapidly and yields residuals close to machine precision.

6.3
Practical interpretation

The refinement pipeline improves reproducibility and strengthens evidence for rare instances: a reduction of
\mathrm{res}_{\min}(\lambda)

certifies proximity to singularity of
A-\lambda I
in the chosen embedding, while the final reported pair residual

\mathrm{res}(A,\lambda,v)
verifies a consistent eigenpair. Thresholds are always interpreted relative to a robust matrix scale, such as

\max\{1,\|A\|_{2}\}
.

8
Conclusions

We developed a practical Newton-based framework for computing left eigenvalues of quaternion matrices.
The method targets
isolated
left eigenpairs and proceeds one eigenpair at a time, combining a simple gauge that fixes the right scalar ambiguity of eigenvectors with a real-embedded Newton correction assembled via the embedding
\rho(\cdot)
.
As a result, each correction step reduces to a single square real linear system and can be handled by standard real linear algebra, while inputs and outputs remain quaternionic. Beyond isolated eigenvalues, repeated multi-start runs provide certified samples that enable reliable detection and
parameterization of spherical components of
\sigma_{\ell}(A)
(Supplement B).

On the theoretical side, we proved local quadratic convergence for
simple gauged
isolated eigenpairs, i.e., precisely when the Jacobian of the gauged system is invertible. At the same time, non-isolated solution sets (most notably spherical components of the left spectrum) necessarily lead to rank-deficient Jacobians, so Newton cannot be expected to “enumerate”
a continuum or to converge quadratically to a uniquely determined root. Nevertheless, in such cases, multi-start runs typically converge to multiple distinct points on the solution manifold; collecting these samples and applying a lightweight post-processing step (clustering and sphere fitting in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
) provides reproducible numerical evidence for spherical
components, supported by residual-based certificates.

Computationally, the resulting multi-start pipeline (trial–accept–de-duplicate, optionally followed by polishing) produced highly accurate certificates once convergence is achieved, across the tested matrix families and sizes. To support reproducibility and reuse, we provide a compact MATLAB reference implementation together with complete supplementary material (documenting the experimental protocol, full tables and figures) and a
Zoo
of illustrative non-generic examples.
Beyond serving as regression tests, this Zoo offers a first glimpse of the variety of spectral phenomena that can arise for larger quaternion matrices, which become accessible once large-scale left eigenvalue computations are practical.

Appendix A
Complex embedding

This appendix records an
alternative
route to express the Newton correction using the classical complex adjoint (symplectic) embedding
\chi(\cdot)
. While our reference implementation uses the real embedding
\rho(\cdot)
, the same correction can be assembled in complex blocks; we sketch the conversion and explain why we do not adopt it as the reference.

A.1
Definition and the key equivalence

Fix the complex slice
\mathbb{C}_{\mathbf{i}}:=\{a+b\mathbf{i}:\ a,b\in\mathbb{R}\}\subset\mathbb{H}
. Every
A\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
can be written as

A=X+Y\mathbf{j}
with
X,Y\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathbf{i}}^{n\times n}
; see, e.g.,
Zhang1997
,
Rodman2014
.

Definition 8

(Complex adjoint / symplectic embedding)
.

For
A=X+Y\mathbf{j}
with
X,Y\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathbf{i}}^{n\times n}
define

\chi(A):=\begin{bmatrix}X&Y\\
-\overline{Y}&\overline{X}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{2n\times 2n}.

Write
x\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
uniquely as
x=u+v\mathbf{j}
with
u,v\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathbf{i}}^{n}
, and define the real-linear map

\phi:\mathbb{H}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{2n},\qquad\phi(u+v\mathbf{j}):=\begin{bmatrix}u\\
-\overline{v}\end{bmatrix}.

Proposition 9

(Minimal properties used in this paper)
.

For all
A,B\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
,

\chi(A+B)=\chi(A)+\chi(B),\qquad\chi(AB)=\chi(A)\chi(B),\qquad\chi(A^{\ast})=\chi(A)^{\ast}.

Moreover, for every
A\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
and
x\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
,

\phi(Ax)=\chi(A)\,\phi(x).

(14)

Consequently, the quaternionic linear system
Ax=b
is equivalent to the complex linear system

\chi(A)\phi(x)=\phi(b)
(and hence
A
is invertible over
\mathbb{H}
if and only if
\chi(A)
is invertible over
\mathbb{C}
).

Proof.

Standard; see
Zhang1997
,
Rodman2014
. Identity (
14
) follows by writing
A=X+Y\mathbf{j}
,
x=u+v\mathbf{j}
and using

\mathbf{j}z=\overline{z}\,\mathbf{j}
for
z\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathbf{i}}
.
∎

A.2
Sketch: complex-embedded Newton correction

The quaternion-form Newton correction (with gauge constraint) solves

(A-\lambda I)\Delta x-(\Delta\lambda)x=-d,\qquad Dg(x)[\Delta x]=0,

for
(\Delta\lambda,\Delta x)\in\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{H}^{n}
, where
d:=Ax-\lambda x
is the current eigenpair defect.
Transporting the first (vector) equation through
\phi(\cdot)
and
\chi(\cdot)
gives the
2n
-dimensional complex-block form

\chi(A-\lambda I)\,\phi(\Delta x)-\phi\bigl((\Delta\lambda)x\bigr)=-\phi(d),

(15)

while the gauge contributes four additional real scalar equations.

The only subtlety is the left-multiplication term
(\Delta\lambda)x
. Writing
\Delta\lambda=\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}\mathbf{j}
and

x=u+v\mathbf{j}
with
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathbf{i}}
and
u,v\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathbf{i}}^{n}
, one computes

(\Delta\lambda)x=(\delta_{1}u-\delta_{2}\overline{v})+(\delta_{1}v+\delta_{2}\overline{u})\mathbf{j},\qquad\phi\bigl((\Delta\lambda)x\bigr)=\begin{bmatrix}\delta_{1}u-\delta_{2}\overline{v}\\
-\overline{\delta_{1}}\,\overline{v}-\overline{\delta_{2}}\,u\end{bmatrix}.

Thus (
15
) is
real-linear
(as it must be), but
not complex-linear
in
(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})
because
conjugates of the unknowns appear. A purely complex-linear solve would therefore require augmenting the unknowns by their conjugates, which removes the putative advantage of working in complex arithmetic.

In summary, the
\chi
-based formulation is perfectly valid and equivalent to the real-embedding route, but it introduces extra bookkeeping (maintaining a fixed slice
\mathbb{C}_{\mathbf{i}}
, consistent
(u,v)
representations, and real/complex coupling with the gauge). The real embedding
\rho(\cdot)
yields a single uniform real system with simpler assembly and diagnostics, so we use

\rho(\cdot)
in the main text and in the reference implementation.

Appendix B
Implementation and performance

This appendix provides a compact summary of the released reference implementation and of the numerical validation.
Complete per-size tables, additional figures, and the full experiment protocol are provided in Supplement A.

B.1
Reference implementation

A compact, stand-alone MATLAB reference implementation of the proposed Newton-based method is publicly available

(see
Data and code availability
section).
The released package is intentionally minimal: it provides a single main solver routine for user-supplied quaternion matrices together with residual-certificate validation for all accepted eigenpairs; no third-party quaternion toolboxes are required.

The solver keeps a quaternionic user interface (inputs/outputs in
\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
and
\mathbb{H}^{n}
) but executes each Newton correction using standard real/complex linear-algebra kernels (solves/factorizations) applied to embedded matrices. The reference implementation uses the real embedding
\rho(\cdot)
; an alternative route via the complex adjoint
\chi(\cdot)

(
A
) is also supported. Certificates are evaluated in the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
(equivalently, the
\ell_{2}
norm of stacked real components), hence they are independent of the internal embedding used for the
linear solve.

To compute multiple eigenpairs, the solver performs independent Newton
trials
. Each trial generates an initial guess
(\lambda^{(0)},x^{(0)})
, applies a bounded number of Newton steps, and returns either a candidate or a failure. A candidate is accepted only if its certificate falls below a prescribed threshold; accepted eigenpairs are then de-duplicated (up to a user tolerance) until the requested target
K
is reached.

Two practical shortcuts are available. For diagonal/triangular inputs, the default is to return the diagonal entries (with multiplicity), bypassing Newton; this shortcut was disabled in our benchmark runner. For singular inputs, the solver estimates the right nullity
\nu_{0}:=\dim\Ker(A)=n-\rank(A)
(equivalently via
\rank(\rho(A))=4\rank(A)
), pre-fills
\nu_{0}
copies of

\lambda=0
together with a quaternion basis of
\Ker(A)
(validated by the same certificate checks), and runs Newton only for the remaining
K-\nu_{0}
eigenpairs.

Nonconvergence, convergence to an already detected eigenvalue, or insufficient residual reduction triggers a restart (a fresh trial). Trial/iteration statistics (iterations, trials, restarts, timing) are recorded; full tables and detailed logs are provided in Supplement A.

B.2
Reported measures

For each test matrix
A\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
we request
K
distinct left eigenpairs and obtain, after acceptance and de-duplication,

K_{\rm found}
accepted pairs
\{(\lambda_{k},v_{k})\}
with
\|v_{k}\|_{2}=1
.
Accuracy is certified by the eigenpair residual

\mathrm{res}_{k}:=\|Av_{k}-\lambda_{k}v_{k}\|_{2}
and summarized by

\mathrm{maxRes}(A):=\max_{k}\mathrm{res}_{k}
and, over an ensemble,

\mathrm{med}(\mathrm{maxRes}):=\mathrm{median}_{A}(\mathrm{maxRes}(A))
.
The acceptance rate is
\mathrm{acc}(A)(\%):=100\,K_{\rm found}/K
, and runtime is summarized by the mean time per accepted
eigenpair
\overline{t}_{\lambda}
(ms). Additional diagnostic counters (trials, restarts, iterations, and per-trial statistics)
are reported in Supplement A.

B.3
Benchmark setup and success criteria

Because the
left
spectrum is not invariant under quaternionic similarity, we do not benchmark by prescribing spectra and applying similarity transforms. Instead, we test directly constructed matrix families that scale with
n
and reflect standard structures in numerical linear algebra.

For each matrix size from
2\times 2
up to
64\times 64
and for each family, we generate a size-dependent number
n_{\rm Mat}
of independent samples: we use very large ensembles for the smaller sizes and gradually reduce the sample count as
n
increases, down to
n_{\rm Mat}=20
at the largest size. For each matrix, we request
K=n
distinct left eigenpairs. A run is declared
successful
only if
K_{\rm found}=n
after acceptance and de-duplication (equivalently,
\mathrm{acc}(A)(\%)=100
); otherwise the outcome is treated as a failure within the prescribed trial budget and is recorded for reproducibility in Supplement A.

We consider four representative families: random upper-triangular matrices with random diagonal (a controlled sanity check, since diagonal entries must be left eigenvalues); dense i.i.d. Gaussian matrices; Hermitian matrices

A=\tfrac{1}{2}(R+R^{\ast})
; and sparse matrices with fixed density
p=0.1
obtained by masking i.i.d. Gaussian entries.
Supplement A contains the complete per-size tables, detailed diagnostic statistics, benchmark figures, and the full experiment protocol.

Finally, spectrum-deficient cases (fewer than
n
distinct isolated left eigenvalues) and continuous components (e.g., spherical families) are not targeted in this benchmark suite. They are handled by dedicated diagnostic modes and are reported separately in Supplement B (Zoo) and, where relevant, in Supplement A.

B.4
Summary of benchmark observations

The benchmark suite tests the solver as an end-to-end computational pipeline: multi-start Newton trials, acceptance by residual-based certificates, and de-duplication to obtain
K=n
distinct eigenpairs per matrix. Across all tested sizes up to
n=64
and across all four matrix families, this pipeline achieved full recovery in the benchmark sense on essentially all sampled instances, i.e.,
\mathrm{acc}(A)(\%)=100
(equivalently,
K_{\rm found}=n
). Supplement A reports the complete per-size tables together with trial/iteration/restart diagnostics.

The upper-triangular family provides a controlled sanity check: the left eigenvalues are the diagonal entries, and the computed pairs attain residuals close to machine precision. For dense unstructured and Hermitian ensembles, the solver likewise returns eigenpairs with small certified residuals; the dominant cost is the sequence of embedded linear solves in the Newton corrections, together with occasional restarts when a trial converges to an already detected eigenvalue or fails to reduce the residual sufficiently.

Sparse inputs are the most variable: at smaller sizes, they may exhibit larger nullspaces and, more broadly, they require careful restart budgeting to maintain a high success rate. Nevertheless, the same acceptance-and-certificate mechanism remains effective, and Supplement A documents both typical behavior and rare hard instances (including reproducibility captures).

Two caveats help interpret these results. First, the benchmark mode targets the generic situation of
n
isolated left eigenvalues; it does not aim to systematically trigger non-isolated components (e.g., spherical families) or
left-spectrum-deficient behavior. When such phenomena occur, the implementation provides diagnostic modes, documented and illustrated in Supplement B (Zoo). Second, if an application demands tighter accuracy, the refinement strategies of
Section
6
typically improve residual-based certificates to (near) machine precision.
