Transition shock, emotion regulation and imposter phenomenon among nursing interns: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Sisi Chen, Zhifang Huang, Nian Long, Chaoqun Xu, Kangyan Cai, Fang Tian, Lu Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClinical internship is critical to nursing students’ professional development. Transition shock and imposter phenomenon are prevalent among interns and are linked to poor psychological adaptation.However, the moderating role of emotion regulation remains unclear.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 384 Chinese nursing interns. Validated scales were used to assess transition shock, imposter phenomenon, cognitive reappraisal, and expressive suppression. Correlation and moderation analyses were performed.ResultsTransition shock and expressive suppression were positively associated with imposter phenomenon, while cognitive reappraisal was negatively associated with it. Cognitive reappraisal weakened the association between transition shock and imposter phenomenon, whereas expressive suppression strengthened it. No harmful multicollinearity was detected.ConclusionA positive association exists between transition shock and imposter phenomenon among nursing interns. Cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression play opposing moderating roles. These findings enrich the stress-coping model and support emotion regulation interventions in nursing education. Introduction

Clinical internship represents a high-risk period for psychological maladaptation among nursing students, yet the mechanisms underlying severe and persistent distress remain poorly understood. During role transition, nursing interns frequently experience intense role stress, emotional dysregulation, and chronic self-doubt, which can directly impair clinical competence, professional identity, and mental health [1–2]. Transition shock is a situational role-adaptation stressor that occurs as students shift into clinical practice. It manifests as psychological, emotional, and behavioral maladjustment driven by unmet role expectations, environmental pressure, and limited resources [1]. Imposter phenomenon is a self-evaluative cognitive–emotional experience characterized by persistent self-doubt, feelings of incompetence, and fear of being exposed as inadequate despite objective evidence of ability [3]. The two constructs are theoretically distinct: transition shock reflects external contextual strain and role maladjustment, whereas imposter phenomenon reflects internal subjective self-evaluation and identity distress [4]. Together, they exacerbate psychological vulnerability during internship, yet their dynamic association and emotional regulatory mechanisms remain insufficiently explored.

According to the transactional model of stress and coping, transition shock arises not only from environmental stressors but also from cognitive appraisals that role-transition demands exceed personal resources [5]. When interns perceive gaps between professional expectations and performance, limited clinical competence, inadequate social support, or conflicting professional values as unmanageable threats, they may develop sustained distress and role maladjustment [6–7]. Such negative appraisals may erode self-perception, trigger self-doubt, inadequacy, and fear of exposure, and thereby relate to heightened imposter phenomenon [8]. Thus, transition shock acts as a key stressor; its impact is shaped by cognitive appraisal, which in turn relates to the occurrence and severity of imposter feelings.

Transition shock is highly prevalent among nursing interns [9–10]. As a typical situational stressor, it exposes interns to multiple challenges, including theory–practice gaps, heavy workload, insufficient social support, interpersonal tension, and unclear professional identity. Without effective coping strategies, these stressors may reduce learning efficiency, weaken professional engagement, and increase the likelihood of early turnover among new nurses [6, 11]. Imposter phenomenon is also common among medical trainees and is closely associated with emotional distress, occupational stress, and burnout [4, 12–15]. Existing studies confirm a significant positive correlation between transition shock and imposter phenomenon [4, 16], but the underlying cognitive and emotional mechanisms remain understudied, particularly among Chinese nursing interns.

Emotion regulation serves as a key psychological mechanism in stress coping, with cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression as two representative strategies. Cognitive reappraisal is an adaptive antecedent-focused strategy that alleviates negative emotions by reinterpreting event meaning. Expressive suppression is a maladaptive response-focused strategy marked by voluntary inhibition of overt emotional expression; it may temporarily mask emotions but can increase internal exhaustion and self-doubt [17–18]. Although expressive suppression and imposter phenomenon both involve emotional inhibition and self-protection, they are fundamentally different: expressive suppression is a dynamic regulatory process that modulates external emotional displays, whereas imposter phenomenon is a stable cognitive state involving doubts about one’s ability and worth [3]. This conceptual distinction provides a theoretical foundation for including both strategies as moderators in this study.

The transactional model of stress and coping suggests that emotion regulation may serve an important moderating role between stressors and adaptation outcomes [19–22]. Although the link between emotion regulation and stress adaptation has been established, few studies have integrated this perspective into the framework of transition shock and imposter phenomenon, and cross-cultural evidence among Chinese nursing interns remains limited [23–24].

Current literature has three major limitations. First, theoretical integration is insufficient; few studies clarify the interactive effects of cognitive appraisal and emotion regulation within the stress–coping model [25–28]. Second, most evidence is derived from Western samples, limiting cultural generalizability [26–27]. Third, studies tend to focus on newly graduated nurses rather than early-stage interns, who face the most severe transition challenges [15].

In China, clinical internship is mandatory for nursing licensure [29]. Clarifying the interplay among transition shock, imposter phenomenon, and emotion regulation is critical for improving psychological support and nursing internship education. Accordingly, this study examines the moderating roles of cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression in the association between transition shock and imposter phenomenon, with the aim of providing empirical evidence for targeted psychological interventions.

Research Hypotheses

Based on the transactional model of stress and coping and the process model of emotion regulation, the present study proposes the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1 (H1):

Transition shock is positively associated with imposter phenomenon; higher transition shock is associated with more severe imposter phenomenon.

Hypothesis 2a (H2a):

Cognitive reappraisal negatively moderates the association between transition shock and imposter phenomenon, weakening the positive link.

Hypothesis 2b (H2b):

Expressive suppression positively moderates the association between transition shock and imposter phenomenon, strengthening the positive link.

Methods

Setting and population

This cross-sectional study was conducted in two tertiary Class A comprehensive hospitals in Hunan Province, China, selected to maximize representativeness and variation in clinical teaching environments. The Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University is a tertiary teaching hospital with a high patient volume and standardized teaching system, while Zhangjiajie People’s Hospital is a municipal tertiary Class A hospital serving a regional population. Including both tertiary hospitals enhances external validity by capturing interns from distinct urban and regional clinical teaching settings with comparable teaching quality and management standards.

Recruitment and data collection were performed using an anonymous online questionnaire distributed via official WeChat internship groups to ensure wide and convenient access. Prior to formal investigation, a pilot test was conducted with 30 nursing interns to examine item clarity, readability, completion time, and logical consistency. Minor wording adjustments were made to improve understanding, and no structural revisions were needed. To ensure data quality, the survey platform was set to accept only one response per person, with IP restriction and duplicate submission detection enabled.

Invitations were sent to all eligible interns, and a single reminder was disseminated mid‑survey to boost response rates. A total of 386 questionnaires were collected using convenience sampling. After excluding responses with excessive missing values, uniform responses, or abnormally short completion time, 384 valid questionnaires were retained, yielding a valid response rate of 99.48%.

Inclusion Criteria: (1) Full-time senior nursing students (bachelor’s degree or associate degree); (2)Clinical internship duration ≥3months; (3)Voluntary participation and provision of informed consent; (4)Normal cognitive and behavioral capacity.

Exclusion Criteria: (1) Diagnosed with mental illnesses such as anxiety, depression, or cognitive impairment; (2) Experienced major adverse life events (e.g., death of a family member, serious illness) within the previous 3 months; (3) Absent from clinical practice for ≥ 1 consecutive month during the internship; (4) Not engaged in clinical work at the time of data collection.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1 for two analyses. For Pearson correlation (H1), a small-to-medium effect size (0.25), α = 0.05 (two-tailed), and power = 0.95 indicated a minimum sample of 197. For moderation regression (H2a–H2b), the same parameters yielded a minimum sample of 129. The final valid sample of 384 participants provided sufficient statistical power for all analyses.

Common method bias control

As all key variables were measured using self-report scales, which are susceptible to common method bias, we implemented a multifaceted a priori control strategy during questionnaire design, distribution, and data collection following Podsakoff et al. [30] to reduce such bias. Specifically:

Randomizing scale order and revising items to remove ambiguous or leading expressions;

Randomizing scale order and revising items to remove ambiguous or leading expressions;

Ensuring participant anonymity and confidentiality to alleviate social desirability bias;

Ensuring participant anonymity and confidentiality to alleviate social desirability bias;

Using neutral instructions and stressing that there were no right or wrong answers to reduce response bias;

Using neutral instructions and stressing that there were no right or wrong answers to reduce response bias;

Setting short intervals between scales to minimize carry-over effects.

Setting short intervals between scales to minimize carry-over effects.

Harman’s single-factor test was also conducted post-hoc to examine common method variance.

Instruments

General information form

The general information form collected sociodemographic characteristics, including gender, age, ethnicity, educational level, family type, place of origin, and only-child status.

Transition shock scale for undergraduate nursing students (UNSTS)

The transition shock scale was originally developed by Kim et al. [31] for nursing professionals and was cross-culturally adapted and validated among Chinese undergraduate nursing interns by Huang et al. [32]. The scale comprises 18 items across six dimensions: theory-practice conflict, excessive clinical workload, lack of social support, interpersonal tension, confusion about nursing professional values, and imbalance between clinical practice and personal life. Items were rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree, 4 = Strongly Agree). Total scores range from 18 to 72, with higher scores indicating more severe transition shock. In the present study, the Cronbach’s α of the scale was 0.946, and factor loadings for all items ranged from 0.544 to 0.851.

Clance imposter phenomenon scale (CIPS)

The clance imposter phenomenon scale, developed by Clance [33], was used to evaluate the intensity of the imposter phenomenon. The Chinese version was revised and psychometrically validated by Jiang et al. [34] in 2022 among nursing professionals and students. The scale includes 18 items covering three dimensions: self-doubt, external attribution, and passive pretension. Items were scored on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Completely Disagree, 5 = Completely Agree). Total scores range from 18 to 90, with higher scores reflecting a more severe imposter phenomenon. The Chinese version demonstrated a Cronbach’s α of 0.930 and a split-half reliability of 0.859. The factor loadings of all items ranged from 0.671 to 0.901.

Emotion regulation questionnaire (ERQ)

The emotion regulation questionnaire (ERQ) was originally developed by Gross et al. [35]. It was later culturally adapted and psychometrically validated for Chinese college students by Wang et al. [36], and it demonstrates good applicability to nursing interns in the present study. The scale consists of 10 items, which are divided into two dimensions corresponding to core emotion regulation strategies: cognitive reappraisal (6 items) and expressive suppression (4 items). For cognitive reappraisal, the Cronbach’s α value was 0.85, while it was 0.77 for expressive suppression. The factor loadings of all items ranged from 0.452 to 0.929.

Ethical consideration

This study complies with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant ethical review measures. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Behavioral Medicine and Nursing, Central South University. Prior to the study, we consulted with two hospitals’ nursing departments and obtained permission. All participants signed electronic informed consent, participated voluntarily, and had the right to withdraw at any time without adverse consequences; their privacy and anonymity were strictly protected.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 23.0. Common method bias was evaluated using Harman’s single-factor test.

First, descriptive statistics and Pearson correlation analysis were conducted to examine bivariate associations among transition shock, imposter phenomenon, cognitive reappraisal, and expressive suppression. Correlational analyses reflect mutual associations only and do not imply causality.

Second, moderation analyses were conducted using Model 1 in the SPSS PROCESS macro (Version 3.2) to examine conditional effects. Moderation analysis assesses whether the strength of the association between transition shock and imposter phenomenon varies across levels of emotion regulation strategies, without testing causal pathways.

All variables were mean-centered, and moderation effects were examined using the bias-corrected percentile bootstrap method with 5000 resamples (95% CI). Effects were considered significant at α = 0.05 (two-tailed) if the 95% CI did not include 0.

Given high intercorrelations among key variables, moderation analyses focused on interaction effects to ensure interpretability. Collinearity diagnostics (all VIF < 3, tolerance > 0.3) confirmed no concerning multicollinearity.

Gender, age, and internship duration were initially considered as potential covariates. However, these variables showed no significant associations with imposter phenomenon or with the focal association between transition shock and imposter phenomenon. To improve model parsimony and interpretability, these variables were excluded from the main models. Sensitivity analyses were then performed to verify that moderation effects remained robust after controlling for these sociodemographic characteristics.

Results

A total of 384 valid participants were included in the final analysis. Among the participants, 43 were male and 341 were female, with a mean age of 20.979 ± 1.235 years.

Common method deviation analysis

Harman’s single-factor test was performed to assess potential common method deviation using all original scale items. The results revealed 15 factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, and the variance explained by the first factor was 34.201%, which was lower than the critical threshold of 40%. This indicated that no significant common method bias existed in the study data, ensuring the reliability of subsequent analyses.

Statistical description and correlational analysis

The average scores (± standard deviation) of transition shock, cognitive reappraisal, expressive suppression, and imposter phenomenon among nursing interns were 53.190 ± 16.656, 18.750 ± 6.106, 10.669 ± 4.202, and 49.034 ± 18.659, respectively. Both transition shock and imposter phenomenon were found to be at a moderate level in this group of nursing interns.

Correlational analysis examined zero-order bivariate associations among variables. Results showed that all four variables were significantly intercorrelated (p < 0.01), supporting Hypothesis 1 (H1) at the correlational level.

Specifically, transition shock was positively correlated with both imposter phenomenon and expressive suppression, while it was negatively correlated with cognitive reappraisal. Additionally, imposter phenomenon exhibited a negative correlation with cognitive reappraisal and a strong positive correlation with expressive suppression. Furthermore, a significant negative correlation was observed between cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression (see Table 1).

Sensitivity analyses

To verify the stability of the primary findings and address potential confounding from sociodemographic characteristics, sensitivity analyses were conducted by controlling for gender, age, and internship duration in both moderation models.

For cognitive reappraisal as moderator:

After controlling for covariates, the model remained significant (R = 0.843, R² =0.711, F = 154.772, p < 0.001), and the interaction effect was significant (β = −0.014, t = − 3.006, p = 0.003, 95%CI [− 0.022, − 0.005]).

For expressive suppression as moderator:

After controlling for covariates, the model remained significant (R = 0.886, R² =0.785, F = 229.980, p < 0.001), and the interaction effect was significant (β = 0.011, t = 1.991, p = 0.047, 95%CI [0.001, 0.023]).

In both models, all moderation effects remained unchanged in direction, magnitude, and significance, confirming that the results were robust and not influenced by the included sociodemographic variables.

Discussion

All hypotheses in this study were supported. This study advances understanding of transition shock and imposter phenomenon among nursing interns beyond earlier correlational findings. Consistent with previous research [1, 4, 10, 13–14], this study provides new evidence that the two constructs are associated through distinct emotion regulation processes. These findings extend the transactional model of stress and coping by demonstrating the opposing moderating roles of cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression, and help explain individual differences in vulnerability to psychological maladjustment during clinical role transition.

Although transition shock, expressive suppression, and imposter phenomenon were highly correlated (r > 0.70), such strong associations reflect meaningful theoretical connections within the stress–coping framework rather than conceptual overlap.Conceptually, transition shock is a situational role-adaptation stressor driven by environmental demands and role mismatch [1]; imposter phenomenon is a self-evaluative cognitive–emotional state marked by persistent self-doubt and identity distress [3]; and expressive suppression is a voluntary regulatory process that restricts overt emotional expression [17].Statistically, collinearity diagnostics confirmed no harmful multicollinearity (all VIF < 3), and Harman’s single-factor test ruled out a dominant general factor, indicating that common method bias did not distort the observed relationships.Notably, moderation analysis focuses on interaction effects rather than main effects. Thus, even with correlated predictors, the opposing moderation patterns–negative for cognitive reappraisal and positive for expressive suppression–remain fully statistically interpretable and theoretically meaningful. These distinct and contrasting effects confirm that the three constructs are conceptually unique and exert separable influences on the stressor–outcome pathway.In addition, sensitivity analyses controlling for gender, age, and internship duration confirmed that the moderation effects were robust and independent of these sociodemographic characteristics.

Correlation analysis showed that cognitive reappraisal was negatively associated with imposter phenomenon, whereas expressive suppression was positively associated with it. These results highlight distinct association patterns between emotion regulation strategies and imposter feelings. Meanwhile, transition shock was positively associated with expressive suppression and negatively associated with cognitive reappraisal, suggesting that interns experiencing greater transition stress tend to use less adaptive emotion regulation strategies [26]. Notably, the strong correlation between expressive suppression and imposter phenomenon does not imply conceptual equivalence: expressive suppression refers to a behavioral strategy for regulating emotional expression [17], whereas imposter phenomenon reflects a cognitive–affective judgment of personal competence [3]. Their correlation likely reflects that interns with stronger imposter feelings may use suppression as a form of self-protection, rather than indicating overlapping constructs [37]. Furthermore, the negative correlation between cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression underscores the fundamental differences between these two strategies, providing a theoretical basis for understanding emotional responses among nursing interns in clinical practice.

A significant positive association between transition shock and imposter phenomenon was identified, consistent with existing literature [4, 5, 16]. This association aligns with their conceptual distinction: transition shock, as a situational stressor, is linked to imposter phenomenon–a self-evaluative outcome–because role-adaptation strain may relate to diminished self-confidence and feelings of fraudulence. Transition shock is also associated with insufficient environmental support and mismatches between personal capacity and job demands [38]. In clinical settings, nursing interns must quickly shift from a student role to that of a professional nurse and commonly face challenges such as theory–practice gaps, limited clinical skills, and discrepancies between role expectations and actual performance [39]. The data showed a clear concurrent trend: higher levels of transition shock were accompanied by more severe imposter feelings. Together, these two factors represent core features of psychological maladaptation among nursing interns [4, 8, 10]. Because nursing practice directly affects patient health and safety, interns may be particularly vulnerable to self-doubt and feelings of inadequacy when facing adaptation stress. The predominance of female participants in this sample is consistent with the demographic structure of the nursing profession in China [40], supporting the representativeness of the findings. Due to the cross-sectional design, this study can only demonstrate correlational relationships; causal inferences cannot be drawn and require verification through future longitudinal studies.

Both cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression significantly moderated the association between transition shock and imposter phenomenon in opposite directions. These findings support the core proposition of the transactional model of stress and coping, highlighting the close link between coping styles and stress adaptation outcomes [5].

Cognitive reappraisal demonstrated a significant negative moderating effect. At comparable levels of transition shock, interns with higher cognitive reappraisal reported lower levels of imposter phenomenon, and the positive association between transition shock and imposter phenomenon was weaker. Previous studies have shown that cognitive reappraisal, as an adaptive emotion regulation strategy, is related to lower psychological distress and more stable self-perception [18, 23–25, 41–43], and conveys adaptive benefits across various stressful contexts.

In contrast, expressive suppression showed a significant positive moderating effect. At comparable levels of transition shock, individuals with higher expressive suppression exhibited more severe imposter phenomenon, and the positive association between transition shock and imposter phenomenon became stronger. Although emotional restraint is common in collectivistic cultural contexts and professional nursing settings [36], the present findings indicate that greater expressive suppression is often accompanied by higher psychological distress, reduced willingness to seek social support [18, 44], and poorer adaptation under transition stress.

The divergent moderating effects of the two emotion regulation strategies further highlight the critical role of emotion regulation in the psychological adaptation of nursing interns. Cognitive reappraisal predicts weaker adverse associations, whereas expressive suppression predicts stronger adverse links. These results offer useful implications for clinical nursing education and psychological support programs. Specifically, fostering adaptive emotion regulation skills–especially cognitive reappraisal–may be linked to better psychological adaptation among nursing interns.

Clinical and educational implications

The distinct moderating roles of cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression provide targeted implications for nursing education and clinical preceptorship.

Nursing education may prioritize cognitive reappraisal training to help interns interpret stressors adaptively and buffer negative psychological impacts during role transition.

Clinical settings should reduce excessive emotional suppression and foster a supportive environment, while structured preceptorship and clear role expectations may alleviate transition stress at the source.

Routine psychological screening enables early identification of high-risk interns for targeted support. Together, these strategies may promote psychological adaptation and professional development during clinical transition.

Limitations

A cross-sectional design was adopted, so only associations between variables could be identified, and causal relationships could not be confirmed.

Participants were recruited via convenience sampling, limited to nursing interns from specific regions and hospital levels, which may limit the generalizability of the findings.

Only the moderating role of emotion regulation strategies was examined; other potential influencing factors (e.g., social support, professional norms) were not included in the model.

Data were collected through self-reported questionnaires, which may lead to response biases such as social desirability.

Although the sensitivity analyses confirmed result robustness, future studies may include a wider range of contextual and institutional factors to further validate the model.

Future research directions

Conduct longitudinal studies to clarify the directionality and potential causal pathways between transition shock and imposter feelings.

Expand the sample scope to include interns from different regions and medical institutions to improve generalizability.

Incorporate additional variables (e.g., psychological resilience, professional identity) to refine the research model.

Develop and test psychological intervention programs targeting emotion regulation.

Conclusion

This study extends the transactional model of stress and coping by identifying the distinct moderating roles of cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression in the association between transition shock and imposter phenomenon among Chinese nursing interns. Cognitive reappraisal is linked to a weaker adverse association, and expressive suppression is linked to a stronger one, clarifying key mechanisms underlying clinical psychological adaptation. These findings provide empirical support for culturally adapted emotion regulation training in nursing education to potentially reduce imposter phenomenon and support professional adaptation. Given the cross-sectional design, causal inferences are not warranted; nevertheless, this study offers important theoretical and practical insights for nursing education and intervention.
