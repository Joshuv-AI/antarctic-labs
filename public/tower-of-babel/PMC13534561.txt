Bioengineered zinc oxide nanoparticles derived from Teucrium polium as a multifunctional platform for anticancer activity, hemocompatibility, larval toxicity and photocatalytic remediation
Authors: Hajer Fraj A Alhawiti, Syed Khasim, Chellasamy Panneerselvam, Fuad A Alatawi, Abdulrahman Alasmari, Zuhair M Mohammedsaleh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, we evaluate the physicochemical properties and the antioxidant, antimicrobial, anticancer, photocatalytic, and larvicidal activities of T. polium-mediated ZnO NPs. The synthesized ZnO NPs were characterized by UV-visible spectroscopy (absorption at 392 nm), FTIR, TEM, and XRD, confirming their successful synthesis. The antioxidant activity of ZnO NPs was evaluated using various assays: DPPH scavenging at 54% (100 µg/mL), ABTS scavenging at 63.3% (100 µg/mL), FRAP scavenging at 61.2% (100 µg/mL), and hydrogen peroxide scavenging at 65% (100 µg/mL), demonstrating concentration-dependent activity. The antibacterial properties were tested against E. coli, P. aeruginosa, K. pneumoniae, and S. aureus, with the largest inhibition zone observed for P. aeruginosa (28.3 mm at 100 µg/mL). Cytotoxicity on MCF-7 cells showed a dose-dependent decrease in cell viability, with values of 75.6%, 44%, 20%, and 8% for concentrations of 25, 50, 75, and 100 µg/mL, respectively. ROS generation and apoptosis were also observed at higher concentrations. The photocatalytic degradation of Methyl Orange was evaluated under UV irradiation, yielding 85% degradation efficiency at pH 3 with a ZnO NP concentration of 50 mg/L. The larvicidal toxicity against Aedes aegypti was significant, with LC50 values of 76.63 µg/mL for III instar and 82.74 µg/mL for IV instar larvae. The results suggest that T. polium-mediated ZnO NPs possess significant potential for therapeutic applications, including antioxidant, antimicrobial, anticancer, photocatalytic, and larvicidal activities, making them a promising candidate for biomedical and environmental applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-56235-9.Keywords: Teucrium polium, Antibacterial, Anticancer, Photocatalytic activity, HemocompatabilitySubject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology, Environmental sciences, Materials science, Microbiology, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

Due to their unique physicochemical characteristics, including enhanced optical properties, low melting point, improved catalytic activity, antibacterial, antioxidant, and anticancer characteristics, the zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) have been utilized in various applications such as environmental science, biomedical science, and power generation1,2. ZnO NPs are among the most important metal oxide nanoparticles to produce, as they are inexpensive, simple, and environmentally safe2. Additionally, it has been discovered that ZnO NPs possess superior features as a catalyst that deteriorates less in an activity when recycled3. Typically, a variety of chemical and physical processes that are used to synthesize nanoparticles are expensive and dangerous for both the environment and human health4. Instead of employing hazardous materials that harm the environment, “green synthesis” of NPs involves the biosynthesis of metal oxide NPs using plants or plant parts. Plants are the most commonly used resources for synthesizing nanoparticles via green synthesis. The presence of wide phytochemicals such as tannins, saponins, terpenes, alkaloids, flavonoids, and phenols in the plant extract could act as a reducing/capping agent for synthesizing nanoparticles5. In addition, it offers several benefits, including lower cost and eco-friendliness.

Various plant extracts were used towards the synthesis of bioreduced ZnO NPs, such as Cymbopogon citratus6, Ocimum basilicum L. var. purpurascens7, Spirogyra hyaline8, Cayratia pedata leaf extract9, Hibiscus cannabinus L10., Raphanus sativus var. Longipinnatus11 and Nelumbo nucifera2. The present study focuses on the biosynthesis of ZnO NPs from Teucrium polium (T. polium) plant extract. T. polium, in the genus Teucrium, has long been utilized in traditional medicine to treat convulsions, diabetes, and gastric inflammation12. Moreover, it exhibits enhanced anticancer, antihypertensive, antibacterial, anti-ulcer, and anti-inflammatory behavior. Diterpenoids, iridoids, sterols, flavonoids, and terpenoids are described as the main phytochemical components of T. polium plants and are thought to be mainly responsible for their antitumor properties and cytotoxic activity13,14. There are only limited studies on the use of T. polium as a reducing agent in the biosynthesis of ZnO nanoparticles, which have been utilized for a variety of applications. For instance, Bozgeyik et al.12 studied the cytotoxic potential of T. polium-mediated ZnO NPs against gastric cancer cells12. In another study, SnO2-ZnO-eggshell nanocomposites for the removal of heavy metals in an aqueous solution15. Until now, no one has explored applications of nanoparticles towards the antioxidant, antibacterial, photocatalytic, and larvicidal activities of T. polium-mediated ZnO NPs.

The aim of this study is to synthesize ZnO NPs using T. polium plant extract through a green synthesis method. The study focuses on characterizing the synthesized ZnO NPs using UV-visible spectroscopy, FTIR, TEM, and XRD. Furthermore, it aims to evaluate the biological properties of the ZnO NPs, including antioxidant activity through DPPH, ABTS, FRAP, and H2O2 scavenging assays. The antimicrobial efficacy of the ZnO NPs will be tested against E. coli, P. aeruginosa, K. pneumoniae, and S. aureus. Additionally, the study investigates the anticancer activity against MCF-7 cells, photocatalytic degradation of Methyl Orange dye, and larvicidal activity against Aedes aegypti larvae. This research aims to explore the potential of T. polium-mediated ZnO NPs for biomedical and environmental applications.

Materials and methods

Plant collection and preparation of their extracts

The source of T. polium was collected from Al Khairita (27°44’45.6"N 35°56’47.9"E), Tabuk Region, Saudi Arabia. The collection of T. polium plant materials was performed according to institutional, national, and international guidelines. Our collection methodology follows best practices for sustainable harvesting to ensure no long- term impact on the local ecosystem. Moreover, the collected plants are not rare or endangered; rather, they are common plants found in the Tabuk region of Saudi Arabia. The plant was authenticated by a botanist Prof. Fahd Al-Zuaiber at the Department of Biology, Faculty of Sciecne, University of Tabuk. Saudi Arabia. The voucher specimen no (Tp/3/UT) was deposited in the herbarium of the Entomology and Toxicology Laboratory, University of Tabuk, Saudi Arabia. After being thoroughly cleaned with Milli-Q water and left to dry in the open, the leaves of the T. polium plant were ground into a fine powder using a blender. The T. polium aqueous extract underwent a basic extraction procedure in which it was heated to 80 °C for 20 min, then stirred at 46 °C for 24 h. The obtained filtrate was stored in the refrigerator for further studies.

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry analysis (GC-MS)

A Trace GC Ultra/ISQ Single Qua GC-MS analyzer (Thermo Scientific) was used to perform GC-MS analyses of T. polium plant extract. The system was coupled with the capillary column with dimensions of 30 m x 0.25 mm x 0.25 m. The helium (99.999%) gas was used as a carrier gas to carryout analysis with a flow rate of 1 mL/min. The standard temperature-programming procedure took about 2 min to reach 40 °C, then increased by 2 °C per minute until it reached 250 °C, which was maintained for 5 min.

Synthesis of T. polium-mediated ZnO NPs

Typically, Zinc acetate dehydrate was mixed with the T. polium plant extract, and the pH of the mixture was maintained by adding NaOH (2 M). Subsequently, the resulting solution was continuously stirred for 30 min at 70 °C. Finally, the end product was collected by decantation, washed with distilled water to remove residual impurities, and oven-dried overnight at 70 °C to produce powdered ZnO NPs.

Physicochemical characterization of ZnO NPs

ZnO nanoparticles synthesized from T. polium extract were characterized using UV-visible spectroscopy (UV-3600 Shimadzu), confirming their formation. The chemical composition of the synthesized nanoparticles were studied using Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) (Perkin-Elmer range 2000). The structural morphology was revealed by Transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) (Tecnai G2 F20, FEI), and the elemental mapping (composition) was studied using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) combined with TEM. Phase analysis of the nanoparticles was performed using a PANalytical X-ray diffractometer (XRD).

Antioxidant activity

DPPH scavenging assay

The antioxidant activity of green-synthesized ZnO nanoparticles and T. polium extract was investigated using the 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) assay. Various concentrations of ZnO NPs were mixed with DPPH (0.2 mM) in methanol, incubated in the dark for 30 min, and then the absorbance was measured at 570 nm. Scavenging activity was calculated using an appropriate equation16.

ABTS scavenging assay

Initially, the combination of ABTS.+ (2 mM) and potassium persulfate (2.45 mM) were dissolved in distilled water, and the solution was kept in the dark for 6 h. The resultant solution was mixed with 0.1 mM sodium phosphate buffer, then ZnO NPs were added. The equations below were used to compute the ABTS activity17.

Ferric-reducing antioxidant power assay (FRAP)

Different concentrations of ZnO nanoparticles were mixed with a solution containing 0.2 M sodium phosphate and 1% potassium ferricyanide, and then incubated at 50 °C for 20 min. After centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 20 min, the supernatant was treated with 0.1 mL of 0.1% FeCl3 solution. Absorbance was measured at 700 nm17.

Superoxide radical scavenging activity

EDTA, nitro blue tetrazolium, phosphate buffer, and riboflavin with the concentrations of 1 mM, 0.75 mM, 50 mM, and 0.02 mM, respectively, were used for the evaluation of the superoxide radical scavenging ability of T. polium mediated ZnO NPs. A range of 25–100 µg/mL of ZnO NPs was added to this reaction solution. The scavenging activity was measured at 560 nm18.

Reducing power activity

Ferricyanide and sodium phosphate buffer-treated nanoparticles at concentrations of 25, 50, 75, and 100 µg/mL were used to assess the reducing power potential of T. polium-mediated ZnO nanoparticles. After adding 50 µL of trichloroacetic acid to stop the reaction, the samples were centrifuged for 10 min at 300 rpm after being incubated at 50 °C for 20 min in the dark. Supernatant (50 mL) and 0.1% ferric chloride solutions were added to microtiter plates and incubated for 10 min, followed by absorbance measurement at 700 nm19.

Hydrogen peroxide scavenging assay

The Hydrogen Peroxide scavenging tests were conducted with slight modifications as reported earlier20. In brief, ZnO NPs suspension (in phosphate buffer, pH 7.4) (1 mL) containing different concentrations of the samples (25 to 100 µg mL− 1) of ZnO NPs was mixed with 100 mM of hydrogen peroxide solution (2 mL). After a 10-minute incubation at room temperature, the absorbance of hydrogen peroxide at 230 nm was measured.

Antimicrobial activity measurement

Using the zone of inhibition method, the disinfectant efficacy was evaluated against four different bacterial strains: Escherichia coli (MTCC 443), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (MTCC 424), Klebsiella pneumoniae (MTCC 109), and Staphylococcus aureus (MTCC 96). To raise the bacterial culture to the required absorbance of 0.5, sterile culture broth was added at the appropriate concentration. Furthermore, the bacterial strains were evenly distributed across the plates using autoclaved cotton buds. Sterile filter paper discs containing 5 µL (25–100 µg/mL of ZuO NPs) of samples were used to load the inoculation media. After that, the culture was incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. It was further established which inhibitory zones formed surrounding the samples and controls. Following treatment, the altered structural characteristics of both treated and untreated microbial species were investigated using a Field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM)21.

Biochemical assays

Thiobarbituric acid was used to measure the membrane damage and malondialdehyde (MDA) production. After hydrating in 2.5% trichloroacetic acid, the enzyme extracts were centrifuged for 20 min at 12,000 rpm at 4 °C. After adding 100 µL of supernatant to 0.5% trichloroacetic acid reagent in 20% trichloroacetic acid, the mixture was heated at 100 °C in a water bath and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. Absorbance was measured at 532 nm using a microplate reader22.

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was investigated using riboflavin, nitroblue tetrazolium, EDTA, methionine, and PBS. Enzyme extract (500 µL) was added to the reaction mixture, which was then maintained at 30 °C for 10 min. The absorbance was recorded at 560 nm wavelength using an ELISA plate reader after incubation in the dark23. Bacterial extracts were prepared from nutrient broth containing 108 CFU/mL of microbial species. After exposure to nanoparticles at different dosages, microbial cells were centrifuged at 7000 rpm for 10 min, then sonicated. For peroxidase (POD) analysis, enzyme extract (1 mL) was incubated with phosphate buffer, hydrogen peroxide, and Pyrogallocallol solution, then ended with sulfuric acid. Purpurogallin generated was measured at 420 nm using a UV-visible spectrophotometer24.

Blood compatibility study

Ethylenediamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA) was used to stabilize fresh human red blood cells. By centrifuging the blood sample for 5 min at 2000 rpm, the serum was extracted. After five rounds of sterile, isotonic PBS washing, the remaining RBCs were diluted 10x in PBS using the same solution. The diluted 0.2 mL of RBC suspension was added to the 0.8 mL of ZnO NPs suspension at different doses (25–100 µg/mL) to measure the hemolytic activity of the nanoparticles. Centrifugation was used to extract intact RBCs and NDs from the mixtures after vortexing for 2 h at room temperature. The hemolysis efficiency was computed by measuring the absorbance of the hemoglobin in the supernatant at 570 nm and a reference absorbance at 620 nm.

Anticancer activity measurement

Cytotoxicity measurement

An MTT (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide) experiment was conducted to determine the inhibitory concentration. MCF-7 cells were maintained for 48 h in 96-well plates. Further, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing a progressively diluted substance, and the cells were cultured for an additional 48 h. After removing the culture medium, 100 µL of MTT solution was added to each well, and the plate was incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. After removal of the supernatant, 50 µL of DMSO was added to dissolve the formazan crystals, and the mixture was incubated for 10 min. The absorbance was measured at 620 nm. Cell viability percentage can be estimated using the following formula25.

Live/dead cell assay

In brief, microbial strains (1.5 × 108 CFU/mL) were mixed with varying nanoparticle concentrations (25, 50, 75, and 100 µg/mL) at a 1:1 ratio, then incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. The control consists of untreated microbial species. After a centrifugation at 5000 rpm for five minutes, the pellets were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Further, the pellets were stained with acrylamide orange (AO) and propidium iodide (PI) at a 1:1 ratio, then incubated for 30 min. Further, the samples were washed with PBS to remove excess unreacted stain, then examined using the fluorescence microscope equipped with appropriate emission and excitation filters (430–470 nm) for both dyes26.

Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) measurement

The fluorescent probe 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (H2DCF-DA) is used to assess ROS levels in bacterial species27. Various nanoparticle doses (with concentrations 25, 50, 75, and 100 µg/mL) were introduced into the cells for 2 h. Further, the cells were washed with PBS, then incubated in the dark for 30 min at 37 °C after exposure to a 1 mM H2DCFDA solution (100 µL). Following cell lysis with lysis buffer, the reaction solution was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant’s absorbance at 520 nm was measured spectroscopically. A fluorescent microscope was used for analyzing fluorescence images of both treated and untreated cells.

DNA damage assay

DAPI labeling was developed to detect DNA damage within cells27. ZnO NPs were applied to each bacterial pathogen at different dosages (25–100 µg/mL) and left for 96 h. Both the treated and control groups were stained with DAPI (1 µg/mL in ethanol) for 15 min at 37 °C. Following three cleaning in 1X PBS, the stained cells were left to air dry. The marked bacterial cells were allowed to air-dry. The bacteria were examined using a DAPI filter and a fluorescence microscope (BX41, Olympus, Japan).

Photocatalytic activity measurement

The photocatalytic performance of ZnO NPs in the degradation of the MO dye under UV irradiation was studied. Before exposure to light, the ZnO NPs were combined with the MO dye solution and agitated for 30 min in the dark. A UV-Vis spectrophotometer at 464 nm was used to measure the amount of MO dye in the filtrate after the ZnO NPs were separated by centrifugation. The photocatalytic degradation efficiency was calculated using Eq. 528.

Larvicidal activity

Third- and fourth-instar larvae of Ae. aegypti were exposed to different concentrations (25, 50, 75, 100, and 125 µg/mL) of biosynthesized ZnO nanoparticles following the standard protocol of Suresh et al.29.Five replications were maintained for each concentration, with 25 larvae per replicate. Dechlorinated water was used as the control, and larval mortality was recorded after 24 h of exposure.

Data analysis

GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA) was used to analyze the data. One-way ANOVA was used to compare the mean values of the tested parameters. For all experimental data, p-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Physicochemical features analysis

T. polium leaf extract was subjected to FTIR analysis (Fig. 2a). The results showed that high absorption at around 3434 cm− 1 corresponded to the hydrogen-bound O-H stretching vibrations of alcohol moieties or polyphenolic compounds, which were weaker in the related spectrum after the alcohol moieties were bio-reduced to ZnO. Additionally, the band at 1629 cm− 1, which correlated with the C = O asymmetric stretching vibration, was reduced to 1622 cm − 1 in the associated ZnO nanoparticle30. Furthermore, the band observed at 2342 cm− 1 was attributed to the presence of an amino acid group in the plant extract31. The existence of carboxylic acid was confirmed by O-H bending in the band 1402 cm-1-1427 cm− 1. The band at 1086–1107 cm− 1 and 619 cm− 1 was assigned to the C-O or C-N stretching and C-I stretching groups of anhydride and halo compound, respectively32. According to FTIR data, proteins and flavonoids in the T. polium extract play a role in both the production and bioreduction of silver nitrate. The sample under examination showed elemental peaks for oxygen and zinc, confirmed by EDX elemental mapping (Fig. 2b). The atomic percentages of zinc and oxygen were 84.3% and 15.7%, respectively.

Antioxidant activity of T. polium-mediated ZnO NPs

The DPPH antioxidant activity of T. polium-mediated ZnO NPs at different concentrations (25, 50, 75, 100 µg/mL) was shown in Fig. 3a; Fig.S5. These results indicate that the T. polium -mediated ZnO NPs exhibit enhanced DPPH scavenging effect, which strongly depends on the concentration. The DPPH scavenging effect at concentrations of 25, 50, 75, and 100 µg/mL was found to be 15.6%, 26.6%,33.4%, and 54%, respectively.

ZnO nanoparticles antioxidant activity was analyzed using (A) DPPH; (B) ABTS; (C) FRAP; (D) SOD; (E) reducing power assays; and (F) H2O2. Data: mean ± SD.

The ABTS assay measures the overall antioxidant activity, or hydrogen-donating capacity, through decolorization. The T. polium mediated ZnO NPs effectively scavenged the radicals in a concentration-dependent manner (25 to 100 µg/mL) (Fig. 3b). The highest scavenging potential (63.3%) was observed at 100 µg/mL. Alkaloids, terpenes, polyphenols, and other phenolic active ingredients provide hydrogen to unpaired free radicals, thereby neutralizing the reactions34.

The in vitro ferric reduction power capacity of T. polium -mediated ZnO NPs was demonstrated in Fig. 3c. Compared with lower concentrations, the ZnO NPs produced by T. polium show improved scavenging activity (61.2%) at higher concentrations.

During aging and other pathogenic events, superoxide anions can form hydrogen peroxide or singlet oxygen, causing direct or indirect damage to biomolecules and initiating lipid peroxidation. Figure 3d illustrates the SOD scavenging effect of T. polium mediated ZnO NPs at various concentrations. A significant SOD scavenging effect was observed at higher concentrations.

The antioxidant’s capacity to donate an electron is frequently assessed using the reducing power assay. This test was used to measure the ability of synthesized nanoparticles to convert Fe3+ to Fe2+. The reducing activity of T. polium -mediated ZnO NPs at various concentrations is shown in Fig. 3e. Increasing the concentration of ZnO NPs could enhance the reducing power.

The H2O2 free radical scavenging activity of T. polium-mediated ZnO is shown in Fig. 3f. Enhanced antioxidant activity of reduced ZnO NPs was observed at higher concentrations, which may be attributed to increased zinc ion concentration. H2O2 free radicals are scavenged by enzymes that use metal ions (Zn) as a cofactor; ZnO NPs containing Zn ions may have a greater capacity to scavenge H2O2 free radicals35.

Antibacterial activity of T. polium-mediated ZnO NPs

The disc diffusion assay was used to evaluate the antibacterial efficacy of bio-reduced ZnO NPs against E. coli, P. aeruginosa, K. pneumoniae, and S. aureus. Overall, the findings showed that all the investigated bacterial strains were significantly inhibited by T. polium-mediated ZnO NPs (Fig. 4). In particular, slightly higher inhibition efficiency was observed in P. aurogenosa than in other microbial species. It can be seen that bioreduced ZnO NPs showed improved antibacterial efficacy compared to the control. Notably, the inhibition efficiency increased with increasing ZnO NP dosage. P. aurogenosa (26.0 ± 1.4 mm) showed the largest antibacterial zone of inhibition, followed by S. aureus (24.0 ± 1.2 mm), E. coli (24.0 ± 1.0 mm), and K. pneumonia (21.0 ± 1.0 mm). The enhanced disinfection activity of ZnO NPs was explained by three distinct mechanisms. The first is ROS release, which facilitates oxidative stress and ultimately leads to cell death27. The second is the lethal activity of ZnO NPs, caused by their adhesion to bacterial cell membranes, leading to their accumulation in the cytoplasm. This accumulation disrupts cell membrane integrity, leading to leakage and cell death36. The third is the toxic properties of the released zinc from ZnO NPs, which prevent various bacterial cellular functions, including metabolism and enzyme activity, leading to bacterial cell death37.

ZnO nanoparticles antibacterial efficacy was tested against (A) E. coli, P. aeruginosa, K. pneumoniae, and S. aureus, with (B) a bar graph displaying mean inhibitory zone diameters.

Bacteria were stained using AO and PI to observe their morphology after being exposed to varying concentrations of ZnO NPs. Green fluorescence was observed in majority of the images of control (E.coli, P. aurogenosa, K. pneumonia, and S.aureus) and ZnO NPs-treated E.coli and K. pneumonia indicate the presence of living cells (Fig. 5). In the treated (E.coli and K. pneumonia) sample, there were very few red fluorescent cells, which indicate dead cells. A larger number of red fluorescence (dead cells) was observed in S.aureus and P. aurogenosa cells treated with T. polium-mediated ZnO NPs. Notably, the intensity of the red color increases by enhancing the quantity of ZnO NPs from 25 to 100 µg/mL. The nuclear staining of microbial strains with DAPI demonstrated evidence of DNA damage (Fig. 6). The mild fluorescence was seen in control cells (without treatment) (Fig. 6). The fact that the blue coloration intensifies with the increase in ZnO NPs concentration suggests that the higher concentrations of ZnO NPs (50 and 100 µg/mL) resulted in enhanced DNA damage.

ZnO nanoparticle-induced nuclear damage was observed through DAPI staining, showing increased damage with higher nanoparticle concentrations (25–100 µg/ml).

ROS are widely known for their adverse effects on protein function, substantial role in metabolic disturbance, and potential for bacterial mortality38. Compared with the control bacterial suspension, ROS generation increases significantly in ZnO NPs-treated cells (Fig. 7). This demonstrates that in strains without NPs, ROS was not produced. Moreover, increasing ZnO NPs concentration results in a dose-dependent increase in ROS generation. The relative ROS generation unit was observed for E. coli, P. aeruginosa, K. pneumoniae, and S. aureus strains treated with 100 µg/mL of ZnO, which were 2.9 ± 0.15, 2.76 ± 0.1, 2.9 ± 0.15, and 3.0 ± 0.15, respectively. These findings indicate that the increased antibacterial activity observed after ZnO NP treatment may result from increased ROS generation.

(A) ZnO NPs induced concentration-dependent ROS production in pathogenic bacteria. (B) Intracellular ROS generation at ZnO NP concentrations (25–100 µg/mL). (C) Relation between relative expression rates and ZnO NP concentration.

The MDA content was measured to evaluate the bacterial cell membrane damage39. The microbial strains frequently showed a rise in MDA concentration when exposed to ZnO NPs (Fig. S6(a)), indicating that ROS concentration in the system increases with NP treatment. For E. coli, P. aurogenosa, K. pneumonia, and S. aureus, the MDA level of ZnO-NPs-treated bacterial cells was 7.1 ± 0.2, 6.0 ± 0.4, 6.2 ± 0.5, and 72.1 ± 0.1 units/µg/protein at the maximal concentration level (100 µg/mL), respectively. The impact of ROS formation on the antioxidant system was evaluated by monitoring the activities of ROS-associated enzymes, including superoxide dismutase (SOD) and POD. The findings confirmed increased SOD and POD enzymatic activity in bacterial cells exposed to ZnO NPs. As demonstrated in Fig.S6(b) and Fig. S6(c), it was found that the POD and SOD activity progressively increased as the quantities of ZnO NPs increased from 25 µg/mL to 100 µg/mL. Thus, the results revealed that an excess of ROS caused damage to the cellular membrane and negatively affected DNA, leading to cell lysis.

Furthermore, SEM images of untreated bacterial cells revealed smooth and well-defined cellular morphology with intact cell membranes, indicating the normal structural integrity of the bacterial cells (Fig. 8). In contrast, bacterial strains exposed to ZnO NPs for 24 h exhibited significant morphological alterations, including surface wrinkling, membrane deformation, and rupture of the cell membrane (Fig. 8). These structural damages suggest that ZnO NPs strongly interacted with the bacterial cell envelope, leading to disruption of membrane integrity. Following membrane rupture, ZnO NPs accumulated on the cell surface and subsequently penetrated into the cytoplasm, resulting in enhanced membrane permeability and severe intracellular damage, which ultimately contributed to bacterial cell death40.

Anticancer properties of T. polium-mediated ZnO NPs

After 24 h of exposure, the cytotoxicity of various concentrations of T. polium-mediated ZnO NPs on MCF-7 cells was assessed. It was demonstrated that T. polium-mediated ZnO NPs significantly enhanced the cytotoxicity of MCF-7 cells at all doses compared to the control. Smooth colonies were observed in the control. Figure 10 shows the morphological changes in MCF-7 cells upon exposure to different doses of T. polium-mediated ZnO NPs. At higher dosages, significant changes in the cells were observed, including substantial detachment and shrinkage (Fig. 10). The MTT results showed that cancer cell viability dropped to 94% at 100 µg/ml of ZnO NPs. Moreover, the anticancer activity of T. polium mediated ZnO NPs demonstrated a concentration-dependent characteristic. At higher dosages, significant changes in the cells were seen, including substantial detachment and shrinkage. The cytotoxicity at 25, 50, 75, and 100 µg/mL was 75.6%, 44%, 20%, and 8%, respectively. Oxidative stress and apoptosis could be the reasons underlying the anticancer effect of T. polium-mediated ZnO NPs41. Thus, the results reveal that concentration and cell types can significantly influence the cytotoxicity of T. polium-mediated ZnO NPs.

Photocatalytic activity of T. polium-mediated ZnO NPs

Figure 13 illustrates the photocatalytic degradation of MO dye using T. polium-mediated ZnO NPs. The peak is seen at 464 nm in the UV-vis absorption spectra (Fig. 13a). It is evident that with an increase in time, the absorption intensity gradually reduces. In 80 min, almost total degradation was achieved. The pH of the solution is one of the variables that affect the photocatalytic reaction process. Five solutions with different pH values (3, 4, 5, 6, and 7) were used in our experiment to examine the effect of pH variation on the photocatalytic degradation of the MO dye by T. polium-mediated ZnO NPs. The highest degradation efficiency was observed at acidic pH (Fig. 13b). The degradation efficiency at pH 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7 was 90%, 85%, 80%, 60%, and 40%, respectively. The catalyst and the MO dye in aqueous solution can be significantly affected by the solution’s pH during photocatalytic degradation, with the catalyst’s surface exhibiting a positive, neutral, or negative charge. The photocatalytic activity of MO was assessed by varying the quantity of ZnO NPs from 10 to 50 mg/L. The effects of catalyst dosage on the photocatalytic degradation of MO dye are shown in Fig. 13c. The percentage of MO dye photodegradation increases from 40 to 85% when the catalyst dose is raised from 10 to 50 mg/L. ZnO NPs can increase the number of active sites on sample surfaces. Additionally, this leads to an increase in reactive oxygen species, such as superoxide and hydroxyl radicals.

(a) UV-vis absorption spectra for degradation of Methyl Orange dye; (b) Photocatalytic degradation study of pH variation; (c) Photocatalytic degradation study of catalytic amount variation.

The possible photocatalytic degradation mechanism of ZnO NPs involves three different steps. The photon absorption from the UV source by ZnO NPs, the photogenerated charge carriers between the valance and conduction bands in the ZnO NPs, and the shift towards the ZnO NPs surface.

Following the production of the anionic radical (O2 −) by O2 trapping the electrons in the CB of ZnO NPs, H+ protonation creates the OH.

Next, the photoinduced holes (h+) created in VB of ZnO NPs combine with H2O to form the extremely active radical OH. species.

The complete mineralization of organic molecules, which yields H2O and CO2, is caused by the radical OH. species.

The Mechanism flow of MO dye degradation can be represented as.

Light → e⁻/h⁺ generation → ROS formation → azo bond cleavage → intermediates → mineralization.

Larvicidal activity of T. polium-mediated ZnO NPs

The T.polium synthesized ZnO nanoparticles exhibited dose-dependent larval toxicity on both third and fourth instar larvae of Ae. aegypti. In the third instar larvae, mortality increased from 18.6 ± 1.81% at 25 µg/mL to 81.2 ± 1.30% at 125 µg/mL with the LC50 and LC90 values were 76.630 and 149.070 µg/mL, whereas the fourth instar larvae had mortality ranging from 17.6 ± 1.14% to 75.4 ± 1.14% across the tested concentrations and the LC50 and LC90 values were 82.749 and 161.520 µg/mL, respectively. The lower LC50 value observed in third instar larvae suggests that younger larvae were comparatively more susceptible to the tested ZnO nanoparticles than fourth instar larvae. The chi-square values were non-significant (p > 0.05), confirming the adequacy of the probit regression model for mortality analysis Table S2. Comparably, Acrostichum aureum-derived ZnO NPs exhibit excellent larvicidal toxicity against Aedes albopictus, with LC50 values ranging from 15.65 (I instar) to 19.50 (puape)43. Further, Tarenna asiatica-synthesized zinc oxide nanoparticles exhibited excellent larval and pupal toxicity against the dengue vector Aedes aegypti44.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the green synthesis of ZnO NPs using T. polium extract has demonstrated promising results across various biological and environmental applications. The synthesized ZnO NPs were characterized using advanced techniques, including UV-visible spectroscopy, FTIR, TEM, and XRD, confirming their successful formation and unique properties. The antioxidant assays, including DPPH, ABTS, FRAP, and H2O2 scavenging, revealed significant concentration-dependent activity, highlighting the potential of these nanoparticles as effective antioxidants. The ZnO NPs also exhibited strong antimicrobial activity against E. coli, P. aeruginosa, K. pneumoniae, and S. aureus, with P. aeruginosa showing the largest zone of inhibition. In anticancer studies, ZnO NPs demonstrated cytotoxicity against MCF-7 cells, with cell viability significantly reduced at higher concentrations, suggesting their potential as anticancer agents. Additionally, the ZnO NPs exhibited high photocatalytic degradation efficiency for Methyl Orange and significant larvicidal activity against Ae, aegypti larvae. These results suggest that T. polium-mediated ZnO NPs hold significant potential for applications in medicine, environmental remediation, and pest control. Future research should focus on exploring the in vivo therapeutic potential and toxicity profiling of T. polium-mediated ZnO NPs. Additionally, further optimization of their photocatalytic and larvicidal activities can enhance their practical applications in environmental and agricultural fields.
