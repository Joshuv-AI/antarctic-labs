Agent-Based Modeling of Low-Emission Fertilizer Adoption for Dairy Farm Decarbonisation using Empirical Farm Data
Surya Jayakumar

Abstract
To understand complex system dynamics in dairy farming requires tools that capture farm heterogeneity, social interactions, and cumulative environmental impacts. This study proposes an agent-based modelling (ABM) framework to simulate nitrogen management and the adoption of low-emission fertiliser across 295 Irish dairy farms over a 15-year period. Utilizing empirical data, the model replicates farm communication via a social network-reflecting peer impact and discussion group dynamics-where adoption probabilities are driven by social contagion, farm-scale factors, and policy interventions such as subsidies or carbon taxes. The framework computes sectoral greenhouse gas emissions, cumulative abatement, and private-social costs, utilizing Monte Carlo and sensitivity analyses to quantify uncertainty. The model achieved high predictive accuracy ( R 2 = 0.979 R^{2}=0.979 , RMSE = 0.0274 \mathrm{RMSE}=0.0274 ) and was validated against observed adoption data using a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test ( D = 0.2407 D=0.2407 , p < 0.001 p<0.001 ), confirming its ability to capture structural shifts in sectoral behavior. Adoption dynamics were fitted to Rogers logistic curves to analyze the evolution from innovation to a realistic saturation plateau (91%), acknowledging structural laggard effects. By conceptualizing decarbonization as a socio-technical evolution rather than a purely monetary calculation, this study provides a ’policy laboratory’ to pre-test the robustness and diffusion velocity of climate strategies prior to implementation.

Introduction

Agricultural systems are increasingly being framed as both drivers and as potential agents of climate change. Dairy farming in Ireland accounts for a substantial share of greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, caused mainly by nitrogen fertiliser use, livestock management and land-use practices
[
EPA(2025)
]
. Empirical evidence indicates considerable heterogeneity in farm-level carbon intensity and nitrogen use efficiency; a subset of high-performing farms achieve markedly lower emissions per unit of output, while less efficient farms account for a disproportionate share of total sectoral emissions
[
Teagasc(2024)
,
OECD(2023)
]
. Indeed, this structural variability offers both challenges and opportunities for modelling adoption dynamics.

There are a range of practical measures in pasture-based dairy systems to minimize emissions, including protected urea, improved slurry management, clover incorporation, and enhanced nitrogen use efficiency
[
Cantillon et al.(2024)
]
. However, adoption across farms is uneven, reflecting differences in management capacity, structural constraints, and social interactions
[
Ogunpaimo et al.(2025)
]
. The comprehension of these drivers of change necessitates a framework capable of accounting for heterogeneous decision-making, social diffusion, and temporal adoption dynamics.

Traditional top-down assessments generally rely on uniformity in behavior and immediate uptake, ignoring peer effects, path dependence, and stochastic decision-making. Agent-based modelling (ABM) offers a flexible platform for representing farms as autonomous agents embedded in social networks. ABMs allow exploration of endogenous adoption pathways, diffusion dynamics, and distributional outcomes across heterogeneous farm types
[
Alotibi(2025)
,
Sun(2022)
]
. Previous studies demonstrate that economic incentives and policy instruments can influence adoption, but their effectiveness is contingent on the behavioural and structural context of farms
[
Liu et al.(2024)
]
.

This study develops a generalizable, empirically grounded Agent-Based Modeling (ABM) framework to simulate the diffusion of low-emission technologies within the agricultural sector. By integrating farm-level data from Teagasc with biophysical emissions estimates, the model captures the inherent heterogeneity of dairy enterprises and the non-linear influence of peer-to-peer learning over a multi-year horizon. In particular, protected urea-a nitrogen fertiliser treated with the urease inhibitor NBPT is highlighted as a lower-emission alternative to conventional fertilisers such as calcium ammonium nitrate (CAN) and standard urea. Protected urea slows urea hydrolysis, reduces ammonia volatilisation, and mitigates nitrous oxide emissions, thereby improving nitrogen use efficiency while lowering environmental impacts
[
Teagasc (2024)
]
. Using this framework, the study not only examines the adoption dynamics of protected urea across heterogeneous farms and social networks but also establishes a versatile modelling platform applicable to a wide range of low-emission technologies and carbon-reduction interventions.

The main objective is to construct the backbone and set up a network-mediated infrastructure that evaluates the transition to Carbon Farming as a socio-technical transformation, not bound by classical frameworks where adoption is just a financial calculation. In this framework, Carbon Farming is conceptualized as an underlying change in dairy management, whereby mitigation is internalized as a core operational output as opposed to a peripheral regulatory burden. To demonstrate the framework’s utility, policy scenarios specifically carbon taxes and subsidies are used as illustrative stress-tests.

The study addresses three methodological research questions:

1.

How can an agent-based modelling framework be designed to integrate farm-level diversity, social interactions, and behavioral rules to realistically simulate adoption of sustainable agricultural practices at scale?

2.

How do peer influence, farm characteristics, and policy interventions shape adoption trajectories, greenhouse gas reductions, and economic outcomes across the agricultural sector?

3.

How can an empirically validated ABM framework function as a “policy laboratory,” enabling stakeholders to anticipate, evaluate, and optimize decarbonisation strategies and carbon farming interventions for maximal societal and environmental benefit?

The main contributions of this paper are methodological. First, it connects empirical farm-level data to a calibrated ABM to model realistic diffusion pathways for fertiliser adoption. Second, economic costing is combined with biophysical emissions modelling to estimate marginal abatement costs in the simulated environment. Third, it quantifies uncertainty in adoption timing and cumulative emissions using Monte Carlo methods to provide probabilistic insights into adoption dynamics. Altogether, they illustrate the applicability of ABM as a framework for investigating heterogeneous, network-mediated technology diffusion in agricultural systems.

The rest of this paper is structured to provide a comprehensive analysis of the socio-technical transition in fertilizer management. Section 2 reviews Related Works to situate the model within the existing literature on agricultural innovation and network diffusion. Section 3 details the Materials and Methods, outlining the empirical dataset of 295 dairy farms and the mathematical architecture of the agent-based framework. Section 4 presents the Results, highlighting the model’s high predictive accuracy and the significant impact of social networks on adoption velocity. Section 5 provides a Discussion on the path-dependent nature of policy interventions, specifically comparing the self-reinforcing dynamics of subsidies against the price-driven effects of carbon taxes and also acknowledges the Limitations of the study. Finally, Section 6 offers the Conclusion, framing the model as a validated for designing cost-effective, socially-embedded decarbonization strategies.

Related Works

Several studies have explored low-carbon transitions in agriculture using simulation, ABM, and cooperative frameworks; these are summarized below to contextualize our contribution.

The article “A Simulation Study of How Chinese Farmer Cooperatives Can Drive Effective Low‑Carbon Production Systems Through a Carbon Transaction Incentive” by
[
Feng et al.(2023)
]
builds an overall system dynamics model to investigate the role of Farmer Professional Cooperatives (FPCs) in facilitating the transition of Chinese small scale farmers towards low carbon agriculture in the carbon trading framework. Based on Chinese agricultural and carbon trading data from 2014 to 2019, the model combines four interrelated subsystems funding support, cooperative operations, farmer behaviour, and eco‑environmental dynamics to simulate the impacts of FPC participation in carbon trading through to 2030. The study finds that incorporating agricultural production into the carbon market could reduce agricultural carbon emissions by over 10%, and under scenarios of higher price premiums for organically produced goods, further enhance these reductions. The carbon transaction system is also shown to increase cooperative and farmer net income significantly, creating a reinforcing feedback loop in which FPCs use carbon credits and standardised production practices to attract more farmers, expand production scale, and improve both economic and environmental outcomes. Through this simulation, the paper highlights the potential role of FPCs as institutional vehicles for scaling low‑carbon agricultural practices and provides evidence to inform policy decisions on agricultural carbon trading and cooperative development in China.

The model evaluates the environmental and economic impacts of alternative farming practices, including changes in crop rotation, livestock feed, and manure management. Our model offers a more precise, empirically validated framework for understanding low-carbon transitions in agriculture. By integrating an agent-based modeling approach calibrated with real-world adoption data from 295 Irish dairy farms, our study captures heterogeneity across farm types, explicitly modeling both benchmark and laggard farms, and quantifying how managerial efficiency, stocking rates, and land-use pressure drive carbon intensity. , our model simultaneously evaluates sectoral decarbonization, distributional shifts in farm-level carbon intensity, and long-term policy impacts, offering actionable recommendations for differentiated, evidence-based interventions. Overall, this combination of empirical calibration, network-based diffusion modeling, and integration of environmental and economic outcomes makes our approach a stronger and more operationally relevant tool for guiding low-carbon transitions in the dairy sector compared to prior cooperative- or incentive-focused frameworks.

The foundation of this study is an integrated Agent-Based Modeling (ABM) framework designed to simulate the complex socio-technical transition of nitrogen management across 295 Irish dairy farms over a 15-year horizon. This methodological approach synthesizes empirical farm-level data with a Watts-Strogatz small-world network to replicate the authentic social architecture of agricultural Discussion Groups. By moving beyond static economic calculations, the model treats decarbonization as a dynamic process driven by social contagion, where individual adoption probabilities are contingent upon peer influence, farm structural characteristics such as size and milk production and targeted policy interventions like subsidies or carbon taxes. The robustness of this virtual ”policy laboratory” is rigorously established through Monte Carlo simulations, sensitivity analyses, and validation against observed Teagasc data.

3.1
Dataset

The study uses a farm-level dataset comprising 295 observations from multiple Irish farms, provided
by Teagasc-the Agriculture and Food Development Authority of Ireland. The data was aggregated from two
primary sources: the Irish Cattle Breeding Federation (ICBF), which supplied herd-level performance data such as dairy cow numbers, calving patterns, milk yield, and milk solids, and Bord Bia,
which provided market prices for cattle, beef, and dairy products. This combined a well-rounded dataset encompassing both production performance and economic criteria. This dataset also accounts for land area, stocking rates, fertiliser use, feed inputs, manure management practices, and other environmental indicators for joint economic as well as environmental analyses, at the farm level.

Production and input variables were collected for each farm per production year. Factors such as milk yield (
MILK_PRODUCTION_KG
), fat and protein content (
FAT_PERCENT
,
PROTEIN_PERCENT
), milk per calving cow, and beef production (
BEEF_PRODUCED_KG
) are among the main production variables. Input variables used include the application of fertiliser and lime, slurry spreading techniques and proportions, feed supplements, silage imports and exports, and energy consumption. Herd structure and reproductive composition and productivity parameters such as monthly calvings and replacement ratios were used for standardising the outputs and to support economic and environmental assessments. The MARS model, considering enteric fermentation, manure management, feed, fertiliser, and pasture activities, was used to estimate greenhouse gas emissions of the farms.
The focus of this analysis is the nutrient management profile within intensive pasture-based dairy systems. Whereas the conventional agricultural structures would consider fertilizer the most common crop input, this model focuses on the application patterns involved specifically in dairy production. The parameters to assess the fertilizer were Calcium Ammonium Nitrate (CAN), Urea, Protected Urea, Total Nitrogen (N), Phosphorus (P) and Potassium (K)-nutrient inputs applied only to on-farm grassland and silage areas. These data are categorized as fertilizer use within dairy systems rather than standalone crop production.

Assessment of Dairy Farm Sustainability and Productivity Metrics

This section involves a systematic, multi-dimensional evaluation of dairy farm performance integrating the environmental and production efficiency metrics in a curated farm-level dataset. 3.3
Agent-Based Modelling of Technology Diffusion, Policy Impacts, and Carbon Farming Outcomes in Dairy Systems

An agent-based modelling (ABM) framework was developed to model Irish dairy farms as autonomous decision-making agents, characterizing heterogeneity in characteristics of dairy farms, management and nitrogen use. Agents interact via a well organized social network and the simulation of information streams, behaviour and system dynamics across a multiyear period is also undertaken. The approach is based on integration of farm level production, environmental and economic data, and Monte Carlo simulation techniques are used to accommodate uncertainty and variability. Based on the above, this framework provides a broad and adaptable basis for investigating combined emissions, diffusion trajectories, and dynamics within the dairy sector as a generalizable instrument in the exploration of decarbonisation strategies and other system-level interventions.

3.3.1
Model Structure and Agents

Every farm behaves as an smart decision-making agent, enabling closer examination of adoption trajectories, peer effects, and policy-response behavioral outputs across a realistic dairy farm population.

N=295

3.3.2
Social Network and Peer Effects

The social architecture was represented using a Watts-Strogatz small-world network. This network class was selected because it combines two features that are relevant to farmer peer-learning systems: high local clustering and short average path lengths. In the present context, high clustering represents repeated interaction within stable discussion-group and peer-advice circles, while short path lengths represent occasional information transfer across otherwise separate groups through advisers, meetings, and informal inter-farm contacts.

The parameter values
k=4
and
p=0.1
were specified as structural assumptions for this social layer and were not tuned to match the observed adoption curve. The parameter
k=4
indicates that each farm is initially connected to four salient peer contacts in the underlying ring lattice. This should not be read as the literal size of an Irish discussion group. It is not so much an actual number of connections as it is essentially the fewest number of contacts repeating and most likely to weigh in on fertilizer options annually. The degree was kept sparse on purpose to prevent unrealistic dense mixing of a population of 295 farms while also maintaining local reinforcement of advice and imitation. The rewiring probability
p=0.1
was used to add fewer non-local ties when the network structure is mostly clustered. In principle, it allows the neighborhood to lose about 10% of its original local links to a node further away to form shortcuts between local clusters, instead of just collapsing the network into a near-random graph. This balance is consistent with an advisory environment in which most information exchange remains local and trust-based, but some information travels across groups through wider professional contact structures.

Because the Teagasc dataset does not contain direct relational data on inter-farm communication, these values should be interpreted as a stylized empirical approximation rather than as survey-estimated network statistics. Their purpose is to encode a sparse, clustered, and weakly bridged peer structure that is behaviorally plausible for Irish dairy discussion groups. Model calibration was performed on the adoption function parameters, particularly the social influence weight
\omega
, rather than on the network topology itself. Accordingly,
k
and
p
were fixed a priori to represent the expected communication structure and were not selected by RMSE minimization.

A sensitivity analysis varying the subsidy between €150-€250 per tonne was conducted to assess robustness. The model embeds the subsidy grounded in economic logic, literature precedent, and sensitivity testing, yielding realistic farmer responses, while making clear the nature of the parameter values that were assumed.

3.3.6
Model Calibration and Validation

Back-testing Protocol

– The method of model calibration used was historical back-testing. Back-testing means beginning the simulation at an established historical baseline and evaluating whether the model is able to reproduce observed outcomes of that timeframe for which empirical data already exist. The model is subsequently extrapolated to 2024, and the simulated adoption trajectory is compared with Teagasc National Farm Survey observations for the same period. This technique guarantees temporal coordination between the simulated diffusion pathway and real-world adoption dynamics. The model is run for 50 Monte Carlo iterations to ensure its robustness. The adopted curve reported is the mean trajectory over iterations, with the shaded confidence band denoting the standard deviation, capturing behavioural heterogeneity between farmers. Furthermore, the emergent adoption dynamics were assessed in light of Rogers’ diffusion theory. To this estimation, the average simulated adoption trajectory was fitted according to a logistic Rogers S-curve, taking into account the effect of factors such as saturation level (
K
), adoption rate (
r
), and inflection point (
t_{0}
).

\omega=0.85
.

This value should therefore be interpreted as an empirically selected calibration parameter rather than a literature-imposed constant. The reference to the high-trust environment of Irish Discussion Groups is used only as a behavioral interpretation of why a relatively strong peer-effect estimate is plausible in this setting. It was not used as the identification criterion for parameter selection, and
\omega
was not chosen by visually matching the diffusion curve.

The estimated value implies that peer adoption is a dominant component of the transition probability in (
11
). As the share of adopting neighbors rises, the simulated probability of switching to protected urea increases sufficiently to reproduce the observed acceleration between 2021 and 2022, which is consistent with a social contagion mechanism in the networked farm population.

To assess whether the results were overly dependent on this estimate, we conducted a sensitivity analysis for lower peer-effect environments, specifically
\omega\in\{0.20,0.50,0.85\}
. Lower values delayed the inflection point and flattened the adoption trajectory, but the rank ordering of policy scenarios and the qualitative structure of the diffusion process remained the same. This indicates that the model response is sensitive to peer intensity in the expected direction, while the main policy conclusions are not based on any one level of assumed social influence.

Validation

-
In order to check for robustness, the adoption probabilities were simulated for 250 Monte Carlo iterations. The mean trajectory corresponds to the observed adoption trends, and the standard deviation captures heterogeneity in farmer behaviour. This calibration allows the subsequent scenario analyses (whether they are using a carbon tax or subsidy interventions) to separate out exogenous policy effects instead of further tuning the diffusion mechanism. Then, the model was shown to have structural validity based on the Two-Sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov (K-S) test to investigate the distribution of carbon intensity for baseline and policy scenarios. Predictive accuracy was evaluated using Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) and R2 metrics. An out-of-sample validation was done by splitting the Teagasc empirical data into training (2019-2022) and testing (2023-2024) sets.

Statistical Goodness-of-Fit

-
Model performance is evaluated using three complementary goodness-of-fit measures: Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), and the coefficient of determination (
R^{2}
). MAE captures the average magnitude of forecast errors, while RMSE penalises larger deviations and is therefore sensitive to mis-specification during periods of rapid adoption.

The
R^{2}
statistic measures the proportion of variance in observed adoption explained by the simulated trajectories:

R^{2}=1-\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{n}(y_{t}-\hat{y}_{t})^{2}}{\sum_{t=1}^{n}(y_{t}-\bar{y})^{2}}

(12)

where
y_{t}
denotes observed adoption,
\hat{y}_{t}
the simulated adoption, and
\bar{y}
the sample mean of observations.

The Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), measures the average deviation between simulated adoption rates
(
\hat{y}_{t}
) and observed Teagasc values (
y_{t}
):

RMSE=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}(\hat{y}_{t}-y_{t})^{2}}

(13)

The Mean Absolute Error (MAE) captures the average magnitude of errors between simulated adoption rates (
\hat{y}_{t}
) and observed Teagasc values (
y_{t}
):

MAE=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\big|\hat{y}_{t}-y_{t}\big|

(14)

where
y_{t}
denotes observed adoption,
\hat{y}_{t}
the simulated adoption, and
n
the number of observations.

These metrics are reported for the baseline scenario to verify that the emergent dynamics of the agent-based model are consistent with historical diffusion patterns.

calibration parameters are held constant. This ensures that subsequent scenario
divergence is driven solely by exogenous policy shocks rather than further tuning
of the diffusion mechanism.

Two counterfactual policy scenarios are then introduced:

•

Carbon tax scenario:
\Delta_{\text{policy}}=+0.08

•

Subsidy scenario:
\Delta_{\text{policy}}=+0.15

Because the baseline model has been validated against observed data, differences
in adoption speed and emissions abatement across scenarios can be interpreted as

3.4
Carbon Intensity Modelling and Distributional Analysis

The analytical framework extends beyond aggregate adoption metrics to a granular evaluation of farm-level carbon efficiency. Carbon intensity (CI) is defined as the ratio of total greenhouse gas emissions to productive output:

CI_{i}=\frac{E_{i}}{Y_{i}}\quad\left(kg\,CO_{2}\text{-eq}\,/\,kg\,FPCM\right)

(15)

where
E_{i}
denotes total emissions from farm
i
and
Y_{i}
represents fat- and protein-corrected milk (FPCM). The baseline CI distribution is derived from empirical farm-level observations obtained from the MARS dataset, capturing structural heterogeneity across the 295 sampled dairy systems. This produces a non-parametric density function representing the initial environmental performance of the sector.

Policy impacts are modelled by recalculating farm-level CI following adoption of protected urea. Adoption reduces the direct nitrous oxide emission factor associated with nitrogen fertiliser by approximately
73\%
relative to calcium ammonium nitrate (CAN). The post-policy CI for each farm is computed as:

CI_{i}^{post}=CI_{i}^{base}\times\left(1-\delta\cdot\bar{A}_{15}\right)

(16)

where
\delta
represents the average proportional intensity reduction at full adoption and
\bar{A}_{15}
is the mean adoption fraction in the final simulation year.

Kernel Density Estimation and Distributional Shift Analysis

For assessing structural changes in sectoral performance, Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) is used to obtain continuous probability density functions of carbon intensity using baseline and policy scenarios. KDE, however, offers a smooth representation of the underlying distribution compared with histogram-based methods, allowing for an accurate evaluation of changes in higher-order moments and tail behaviour. Gaussian kernels with Silverman’s rule-of-thumb bandwidth were applied. Shift of distributions is evaluated in terms of mean (
\mu
), variance (
\sigma^{2}
), and right-tail mass of high-intensity emitters. We follow the population mean over the 15-year simulation horizon using vertical reference lines:

\Delta\mu=\mu_{post}-\mu_{base}

(17)

Sectoral Policy Assessment:

By connecting agent-level technology adoption to population-wide density transformation, the framework reflects both mean efficiency gains and systemic equity implications across farms. This allows for the concurrent assessment of:

•

Carbon efficiency gains across sectoral metrics,

•

performance dispersion decrease,

•

responsiveness of high-emission farms to policy incentives.

Such multi-dimensional assessment is critical for designing policy instruments that not only obtain aggregate emissions abatement but also are distributionally efficient decarbonisation pathways.

3.5
Network and Social Contagion Dynamics

Topological Evolution and Social Contagion Dynamics:

The network diffusion analysis models the adoption of Protected Urea among dairy farms as a social contagion process over a Watts-Strogatz small-world network, which captures the typical local clustering and short-path connectivity observed in real-world farmer discussion groups. Each node in the network represents an individual farm, and edges represent social or informational connections through which adoption behavior can influence peers. The initial adoption rate is calibrated to match observed baseline adoption (1% in Year 1), and farms are assigned attributes reflecting normalized land area and milk production, which modulate their probability of adoption.

The simulation proceeds iteratively on an annual basis over 15 years. At each step, non-adopter farms evaluate the adoption status of their neighbors to compute a peer influence signal. This signal, combined with farm-specific characteristics and policy scenarios (baseline, carbon tax, or subsidy), determines the probabilistic likelihood of adoption in that year. Farms that meet the probabilistic threshold transition to adopters, updating both their adoption status and fertilizer type. This approach allows the model to capture both social contagion effects and heterogeneity in farm characteristics.

Network snapshots are captured at key time points (Years 1, 5, and 15) to visualize the spatial-temporal evolution of adoption. Nodes are colored according to adoption status, while edges represent persistent social connections. This methodology allows for a detailed examination of how topological structure, farm-level heterogeneity, and policy interventions interact to shape the adoption trajectory, providing insights into both diffusion velocity and network-driven amplification of environmentally beneficial practices.

Farm-level structural attributes are integrated with network position to simulate differential influence within the social system. Larger and more intensive farms, represented by higher normalised land area and milk output, are interpreted as higher-salience nodes in the information network. Within the spring layout, these agents tend to occupy more central positions, approximating “lead farmer” roles observed in extension systems.

This configuration enables the model to capture heterogeneous diffusion pathways, where adoption by structurally central agents generates disproportionate peer effects. The resulting cascade dynamics are consistent with threshold and contagion models in network diffusion theory.

Long-term climate performance is evaluated using a cumulative emissions integration framework. Rather than relying on annual point estimates, total mitigation is quantified as the area between the baseline emissions trajectory and each policy scenario over the simulation horizon:

S_{\mathrm{cum}}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(E_{\mathrm{base},t}-E_{\mathrm{scenario},t}\right)

(18)

where
E_{\mathrm{base},t}
and
E_{\mathrm{scenario},t}
denote sectoral emissions in year
t
under the baseline and policy conditions, respectively, and
T=15
represents the full simulation period. This cumulative metric captures the
early-action advantage
inherent in accelerated technology diffusion.

To assess predictive validity and avoid overfitting, a temporal cross-validation protocol is applied using observed adoption data for the period 2019–2024. The dataset is partitioned into a calibration (training) phase covering Years 1–4 and a validation (testing) phase covering Years 5–6. Model parameters are estimated using only the early slow-growth segment of the empirical S-curve and then projected forward without further adjustment.

Model accuracy is evaluated using the Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) for both training and testing intervals:

RMSE=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left(A^{\mathrm{obs}}_{t}-A^{\mathrm{sim}}_{t}\right)^{2}}

(19)

where
A^{\mathrm{obs}}_{t}
and
A^{\mathrm{sim}}_{t}
denote observed and simulated adoption fractions, respectively.

By combining cumulative abatement accounting with temporal validation, the methodology ensures that scenario comparisons reflect both environmental effectiveness and predictive robustness. This dual approach strengthens the interpretability of the model outputs and provides a statistically grounded basis for evaluating alternative policy pathways within national emissions reduction frameworks.

Network Influence Ablation and Counterfactual Analysis:

To isolate the causal contribution of social connectivity to the diffusion process, a network ablation experiment is conducted. In this counterfactual configuration, the peer influence term is removed from the adoption probability function, thereby eliminating all information spillovers between agents. Adoption decisions are then determined solely by farm-level structural attributes (normalised land area and milk output) and exogenous policy parameters.

This “no-network” specification represents an atomised rational-actor scenario in which farmers respond independently to economic and structural incentives without social learning. The resulting adoption trajectory is compared with the full network model using the Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) relative to observed Teagasc adoption data.

The difference in predictive performance between the two configurations defines the
network value
:

\Delta RMSE=RMSE_{\mathrm{no\text{-}network}}-RMSE_{\mathrm{network}}

(20)

3.6
Real-World Abatement Costing and Farm Economics

Farm-level fertiliser application rates, prices (€/t) and dairy system variables were obtained from the Teagasc Data.

3.6.1
Economic Assessment and Abatement Costing

The economic assessment estimates the marginal cost of greenhouse gas (GHG) abatement associated with Protected Urea adoption using farm-level fertiliser application data and Teagasc-reported input prices. All fertiliser quantities are recorded in kilograms and converted to tonnes to match price units (€/t) using:

Q_{j}^{t}=\frac{Q_{j}^{kg}}{1000},

(21)

where
Q_{j}
denotes the quantity of fertiliser
j\in\{\text{CAN, UREA, PU, P, K}\}
.

Baseline Fertiliser Cost:

For each farm
i
, baseline fertiliser expenditure is calculated as:

\displaystyle C_{i}^{\text{baseline}}=

\displaystyle p_{\text{CAN}}Q_{\text{CAN},i}^{t}+p_{\text{UREA}}Q_{\text{UREA},i}^{t}+p_{\text{PU}}Q_{\text{PU},i}^{t}

(22)

\displaystyle+p_{\text{P}}Q_{\text{P},i}^{t}+p_{\text{K}}Q_{\text{K},i}^{t}+p_{\text{LIME}}L_{i}

where
p_{j}
represents Teagasc-observed market prices and
L_{i}
is lime applied in tonnes.
Sectoral baseline cost is obtained by aggregation:

C^{\text{baseline}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}C_{i}^{\text{baseline}}.

(23)

This reflects the observed fertiliser mix without behavioural or policy-induced substitution.

Policy Scenario Cost:

The policy scenario introduces adoption of Protected Urea based on the final adoption rate
\alpha\in[0,1]
derived from the network diffusion model. Protected Urea replaces an equivalent share of CAN nitrogen while holding total nitrogen application constant. For each farm:

\displaystyle\text{CAN}_{i}^{\text{new}}

\displaystyle=\text{CAN}_{i}(1-\alpha),

(24)

\displaystyle\text{PU}_{i}^{\text{new}}

\displaystyle=\text{PU}_{i}+\alpha\,\text{CAN}_{i}.

(25)

Urea, phosphorus, potassium, and lime application rates remain unchanged. The policy fertiliser cost is therefore:

\displaystyle C_{i}^{\text{policy}}

\displaystyle=p_{\text{CAN}}\,Q_{\text{CAN,new},i}^{t}+p_{\text{UREA}}\,Q_{\text{UREA},i}^{t}+p_{\text{PU}}\,Q_{\text{PU,new},i}^{t}

\displaystyle\quad+p_{\text{P}}\,Q_{\text{P},i}^{t}+p_{\text{K}}\,Q_{\text{K},i}^{t}+p_{\text{LIME}}\,L_{i}.

(26)

Total policy cost is aggregated across farms:

C^{\text{policy}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}C_{i}^{\text{policy}}.

(27)

The net private cost change attributable to the mitigation measure is:

\Delta C=C^{\text{policy}}-C^{\text{baseline}}.

(28)

Emissions Abatement Estimation:

Emissions results are obtained from the biophysical module, which shows time series of sectoral emissions for both baseline and policy cases. Let
E_{t}^{\text{baseline}}
and
E_{t}^{\text{policy}}
represent mean emissions (in Gg CO
2
-eq) over Monte Carlo runs for year
t
. Total abatement is calculated as:

A=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(E_{t}^{\text{baseline}}-E_{t}^{\text{policy}}\right)\times 10^{3},

(29)

where the factor
10^{3}
transforms gigagrams into tonnes of CO
2
-equivalent. This formulation accounts for the cumulative mitigation effect over the simulation horizon instead of a static difference over a period of one year.

Marginal Abatement Cost:

The marginal abatement cost (MAC) of Protected Urea adoption is computed as:

MAC=\frac{\Delta C}{A},

(30)

expressed in € per tonne of CO
2
-equivalent. This is the extra (or reduced) fertiliser cost necessary to realise one tonne emissions reduction.

3.6.2
Economic Evaluation & Cost-Benefit Framework

In order to conduct a holistic economic assessment and to avoid the Abatement Cost Paradox, two separate cost-effectiveness metrics were proposed to capture both micro-level farm economics, whilst addressing macro-level sectoral welfare.

Private Abatement Cost:

The Private Abatement Cost indicates the marginal economics of technology utilization at the farm gate. It is the farm enterprise’s direct financial effect, the change in fertilizer input costs from the baseline, net to the farm:

C_{\text{private}}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(Cost_{\text{policy},i}-Cost_{\text{baseline},i}\right)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}Abatement_{i}}

(31)

Cost
represents the market price of fertilizer inputs for farm
i
. The measure is used to judge how economically attractive the transition is from the producer’s point of view.

Social Abatement Cost:

The Social Abatement Cost reflects this total welfare effect through internalizing government expenditure. This metric avoids underestimating these policy costs, since the public funding should be available to incentivize adoption:

C_{\text{social}}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(Cost_{\text{policy},i}-Cost_{\text{baseline},i}\right)+G_{\text{expenditure}}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}Abatement_{i}}

(32)

Where
G_{\text{expenditure}}
is the total sum of all the fiscal outlays for government subsidy payments over the 15-year simulation period. The model can distinguish between the technical abatement efficiency and the total policy cost by reporting both metrics.

3.7
Farm Size as a Driver of Adoption Heterogeneity

Quartile Assignment:

To explore the effect of farm size on adoption dynamics, the simulated dairy farms were partitioned into quartiles according to land area. Farm-level land area data (
LAND_AREA_HA
) are provided by the Teagasc Data. The quartile classification arranges the population of farms in four groups of approximately equal size (Quartile 1 being the smallest farms whereas Quartile 4 being the largest). Such clustering permits comparative analysis of adoption trajectories from heterogeneous farm sizes. In technical terms the quartile assignment is implemented as pandas.qcut function assigns a discrete label on each farm (
0
–
3
) per quartile:

\text{size\_q}=\text{qcut}(\text{LAND\_AREA\_HA},4)

This makes every quartile the equivalent of 25% of all the farms, but retains each quartile’s relative distribution and ensures a meaningful comparison.

When the farms were stratified, a subset simulation was executed independently for each quartile. Under the policy scenario selected for implementation by Sections 3-7, the respective agent-based model (ABM) calibrated to adopt each subset was applied. In the simulation, the adoption probability for each individual farm
i
in a given year was a function of farm-level characteristics normalized land area (
\text{size\_norm}_{i}
), normalized milk production (
\text{milk\_norm}_{i}
) and peer effect of the network structure. We then calculate adoption probability
P_{i}
as follows:

\begin{split}P_{i}=&\;0.005+0.85\cdot\text{peer\_signal}_{i}\\
&+0.04\cdot\text{size\_norm}_{i}+0.04\cdot\text{milk\_norm}_{i}\\
&+\Delta_{\text{policy}},\end{split}

(33)

where
\text{peer\_signal}_{i}
is the fraction of neighbouring farms that have adopted, and
\Delta_{\text{policy}}
is any policy-induced shift in adoption probability (e.g., 0.15 for subsidy case). Farms were iteratively updated across 15 simulation years, and adoption was logged annually.

Quartile-Level Adoption Curves:

The mean fraction of adoption by each quartile across Monte Carlo runs was calculated, giving rise to quartile-wise adoption curves of the clusters in this case. These curves were plotted with the year-span of simulation on the x-axis and the adoption percentage on the y-axis. From this figure it is clear the temporal adoption rate for each farm-size group showing variations in the speed and cumulative adoption trends. Bigger farms (higher quartiles) tend to see a more rapid adoption given the bigger size-normalized influence and higher milk supply while smaller farms tend to be slow to adopt.

This study offers an in-depth analysis of adoption dynamics heterogeneity because it also tells us that policy impacts and social contagion effects are not a direct function of the farm population. Through isolating quartile-levels, such differential responses to policy instruments can be better quantified for targeted interventions and enhanced sectoral estimates of adoption rates and potential GHG abatement.

3.8
Monte Carlo-Based Uncertainty Analysis

A Monte Carlo simulation-based analysis was conducted across multiple run scenarios of the agent-based model (ABM) to quantify the uncertainty and heterogeneity in adoption dynamics and GHG abatement. For each scenario (baseline, carbon tax, subsidy) the ABM generates time series of adoption fractions at the farm level, which captures the stochastic effects of peer influence, farm heterogeneity, and policy interventions over a period of 15 years. This results in a spread of potential adoption paths, instead of the one deterministic result allowing powerful statistical analysis of system behaviour. The simulation was carried out for
N=250
independent iterations to control the observation of observed policy outcomes as artifacts from stochastic variation in the initial “innovator” group. Stability of the model was assessed using a convergence diagnostic informed by the running mean of cumulative GHG abatement. The iteration count was calculated as sufficient when the ensemble mean reached a steady state and the Coefficient of Variation (CV) was less than 5

3.8.1
Technological Adoption Threshold Metrics

.
Time-dependent adoptions were calculated as threshold-based adoption metrics. First, for each iteration of Monte Carlo, we pinpointed the first year when the adoption fraction crosses specific 50% (
t_{50}
) and 90% (
t_{90}
) thresholds. In formal terms, if
A_{i,t}
at year
t
is the adoption fraction for iteration
i
, then

\displaystyle t_{x}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=\min{t\in[1,T];|;A_{i,t}\geq x},

\displaystyle x\in{0.5,0.9}.

(34)

This is enabled by the function
adoption_threshold_year
, which scans the adoption trajectory for each iteration and records the earliest year that passed
threshold
. Aggregating the mean and standard deviation as a function of a Monte Carlo run, we calculated
t_{50}
and
t_{90}
for each scenario, giving us a probabilistic estimate of the time scale of adoption and the noise associated with stochastic dynamics and network construction.

3.8.2
Peak Adoption Speed

. The peak adoption velocity indicates the annual peak in adoption fraction. For iteration
i
, we define

\displaystyle v_{t}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=A_{i,t}-A_{i,t-1},\ \text{peak}i

\displaystyle=\max_{t}v_{t}^{(i)},\ \text{year}{\text{peak},i}

\displaystyle=\arg\max_{t}v_{t}^{(i)}.

(35)

The function
peak_velocity_metrics
computes the first difference of the adoption time series (
np.diff

Taken together over the Monte Carlo runs, these metrics provide the mean and standard deviation of peak adoption rate and timing, enabling comparative analysis.

3.8.3
Distributions of Cumulative GHG Abatements

.

Each scenario generates annual GHG emissions across farm and sector levels, and the biophysical ABM module is responsible. To quantify cumulative mitigation achieved, across Monte Carlo runs cumulative abatement distributions are computed. Let
E_{i,t}^{\text{baseline}}
and
E_{i,t}^{\text{scenario}}
be the annual emissions for iteration
i
and year
t

\displaystyle A_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(E_{i,t}^{\text{baseline}}-E_{i,t}^{\text{scenario}}\right)\times 10^{3},

(36)

where the factor
10^{3}
transforms Gg CO
2
-eq to tonnes. The function
cumulative_abatement_distribution
sums the cumulative abatement results for every iteration and provides a distribution which represents stochastic variation in overall mitigation potential across all scenarios.

Although the assumption of dry matter yield equivalence is supported by multi-year trial evidence, it should be taken as an average agronomic relationship rather than as an invariant result under all seasonal conditions. Field-level performance can still feel weather sensitive in practice, particularly in unusual spring periods like prolonged dryness that can affect fertilizer dissolution, timing of pasture response, and farmer confidence to substitute. For this reason, the model does not assume that farmers respond only to expected mean yield performance. Instead, the perceived possibility of short-run under-performance under adverse weather is represented indirectly through the social contagion term
\omega
, which captures the extent to which observed peer experience reduces uncertainty about the practical reliability of protected urea. This clarification adds behavioural realism to the adoption mechanism while preserving the evidence-based assumption of average yield equivalence in the economic accounting.

4.10
Spatial-Social Network Diffusion

Figure 12:
Longitudinal Network Snapshots of Spatial-Social Diffusion under a Subsidy Scenario (Years 1, 5, and 15).

The longitudinal network snapshots (Fig.
12
) show the shape and nature of technology diffusion as it progresses over the social structure of the dairy sector. That the effects of policy are path-dependent rather than merely contemporaneous is central to the framework’s structure. When it comes to subsidies, early inducements not only decrease the initial private price, but modify the course of the network once it becomes very different. The policy is adopted at 13% in Year 1, transitioning the sector from the innovator stage to the early diffusion phase. This early acceleration increases the quantity of visible adopters, allows for more effective peer validation, and converts the system on a high adoption track that rises up to 77% by Year 5. While this transition may take effect, it still partly locks the nature of the behavior. This is also due to the capturing of adoption within the model and peer experience during this fast growing window that lead the informational barrier around perceived risks is significantly reduced. More specifically, the model now includes structural resistance and attains an approximate 91% adoption rate by Year 15. This 9% “laggard” margin means that in the presence of continued subsidies, a minority are reluctant for non-economic reasons. A carbon tax works primarily through a continuing relative price penalization on the incumbent input; it can help reduce emissions, but it will probably not induce this self-reinforcing dynamic - unless it’s strong enough to pull the system beyond the similar social tipping point experienced between Year 1 and Year 5.

4.11
Synthesis for Policy

Our results demonstrate that (i) emissions heterogeneity is accounted for by stocking imbalance and nutrient efficiency, (ii) social networks are significant in modeling realistic adoptions, and (iii) subsidy-based interventions create faster diffusion, greater cumulative abatement, and lower temporally derived climate risk than carbon taxes. Applying peer effects and minimising initial investment, near-total adoption can be achieved at very low marginal abatement cost to drive rapid steps towards national agricultural climate targets.

5
Discussion

The results demonstrate that heterogeneity in farm-level carbon intensity is structurally driven by differences in nutrient-use efficiency, land pressure, and managerial performance rather than farm scale alone. As shown in Fig.
3a
and Fig.
3b
, benchmark farms achieve low emissions while maintaining high productivity, indicating that sustainable intensification is technically feasible within the Irish dairy system. The large gap between benchmark and laggard farms suggests that sectoral mitigation potential is concentrated among a relatively small subset of high-emission producers.

The strong predictive accuracy of the model (Table
1
) and the substantial deterioration in performance when social networks are removed (Table
2
) indicate that peer effects are a dominant mechanism within the modelled diffusion process. This finding supports the hypothesis that advisory strategies targeting influential farmers could accelerate sector-wide adoption.

Through policy scenario analysis we can see that financial incentives will have considerable effects on the acceleration and timing of diffusion. Early peak adoption velocity under the subsidy scenario (Fig.
8
) increases emissions reduction rates, and this adds up to greater cumulative abatement over time (Fig.
9
). While both the carbon tax and subsidy ultimately meet near completion (Fig.
7
), the timing of that adoption is critical to fulfilling time-bound climate goals. This demonstrates how initial incentives accelerate diffusion and increase cumulative abatement in a simulated setting, with earlier incentives increasing cumulative abatement. Because emissions are cumulative, earlier abatement contributes more to time-bound mitigation pathways within the simulation horizon. The distributional analysis (Fig.
10
) implies that policy-induced adoption results in not only a lowered mean carbon intensity, but the reduced variation across farms, suggesting that there is convergence toward best-practice. This structural compression of emissions distribution implies that mitigation could improve environmental performance in combination with lowering dispersion of farm-level efficiency. From the economic viewpoint, the marginal abatement cost of €0.80 per tCO
2
e (Table
4
) is well below the current carbon price, which means that switching to fertiliser is an economically important tool for mitigation. It is important that this figure is reflective of the differentials in inputs for farm inputs and it does not consider possible public spending on subsidies; such an analysis would be used in future welfare assessments. Monte Carlo simulations verify that the superiority of the subsidy scenario is statistically sound, with much larger cumulative abatement (p ¡ 0.001). The narrow confidence intervals for the adoption timing and peak speed signal the low sensitivity to stochastic variation, and strengthen confidence in the policy ranking. To sum up, combining empirical farm information with agent-based diffusion modelling highlights that early adoption dynamics, social network structure and targeted monetary incentives are key drivers of mitigation outcomes. Initiatives aimed to lower implementation costs for adoption initially and harness peer influence can lead to speedier emission reductions, higher cumulative abatement and the trend to the adoption of low emissions production systems. These results suggest that socially embedded incentive mechanisms for high emission, high stocking systems provide the greatest marginal mitigation potential in the dairy sector in Ireland.

Path dependency and the interpretive role of the policy laboratory:

An important aspect of the framework is the implication that certain policy effects might be related to path dependency, not simply to contemporaneous effects. In the case of subsidy, an early incentive does much more than lower initial private switching costs. By accelerating entry into the adoption network, it maximizes the number of visible adopters at the critical early diffusion phase, thereby reinforcing peer validation and moving the system on to a higher adoption path. Once this transition occurs, the behaviour can remain partially locked in even if the subsidy is later withdrawn, because adoption in the model is absorbing and because the informational barrier associated with perceived risk has already been reduced through observed peer experience. By contrast, a carbon tax primarily operates through the continuing relative price penalty on the incumbent input. This can suppress emissions while the tax remains in force, but its effect is less likely to generate the same self-reinforcing diffusion dynamic unless the tax is sufficiently strong and persistent to move the system past the same social tipping region. In this sense, the model functions as a policy laboratory not only by comparing static policy instruments, but by revealing how policy timing interacts with network contagion to create durable or non-durable transition pathways.

By integrating farm-level heterogeneity, explicit social network structure, and empirically calibrated decision rules, the model provides a generalisable framework for analysing technology diffusion in agricultural systems. The scenario experiments illustrate how adoption timing, network position, and nutrient-use efficiency interact to shape diffusion pathways and emissions outcomes within the simulated environment. These results should be interpreted as model-based insights rather than policy prescriptions, highlighting the utility of agent-based approaches for exploring behavioural and structural drivers of mitigation adoption. The framework is adaptable to alternative technologies, regions, and incentive structures, providing a flexible tool for future methodological and scenario-based research.

Limitations:

Despite its predictive strength, this study is subject to limitations that warrant consideration: 1) Bounded behavioral complexity, where the decision-making logic primarily focuses on economic incentives and social contagion, potentially overlooking idiosyncratic factors such as individual farmer personality, trust in regulatory bodies, or long-term commercial supply contracts; 2) Static economic variables, as the cost-benefit analysis utilizes fixed fertilizer price differentials that do not account for the high volatility of global energy and commodity markets; and 3) Network exclusivity, as the social architecture is restricted to peer-to-peer discussion groups, omitting the significant influence of mass media, digital social platforms, and top-down institutional advisory pressure on adoption signals.

6
Conclusion

This study demonstrates the critical necessity of integrating farm-level heterogeneity, social network dynamics, and targeted policy instruments to understand the adoption of low-emission fertilizers in dairy systems. Using an agent-based model (ABM) calibrated with empirical data from 295 Irish farms, we demonstrate that adoption is fundamentally driven by peer-to-peer interactions and farm structural characteristics. Specifically, larger and more socially central farms act as ’opinion leaders,’ accelerating the sector-wide diffusion of Protected Urea.

Policy architecture significantly dictates these outcomes: subsidy-based interventions facilitate a faster transition and greater cumulative abatement than carbon taxes, with network effects further magnifying these gains. Economically, Protected Urea adoption is a highly efficient mitigation strategy; at a private cost of €0.80 per tCO2e, it is significantly more affordable than current carbon price benchmarks. While the model identifies a realistic saturation ceiling of 91% by Year 15, the remaining 9% ’laggard’ margin highlights the persistent challenges of non-economic resistance.

These findings should be interpreted as a scenario-based perspective rather than direct policy prescriptions. However, the results emphasize that targeted interventions on structurally influential, high-emission farms yield the greatest marginal impact. Ultimately, this integrated ABM framework serves as a robust ’policy laboratory’ to design cost-effective and socially-embedded decarbonization strategies for the agricultural sector.

For the future development of this framework, several key research avenues should be explored: 1) Multi-technology bundling, expanding the model to simulate the simultaneous adoption of complementary measures like clover incorporation and low-emission slurry spreading to identify potential adoption synergies or fatigue; 2) Dynamic behavioral profiling, integrating heterogeneous agent archetype such as risk-averse or environmentally-driven personas to move beyond a uniform social contagion weight and capture more nuanced decision-making; 3) Climate feedback integration, linking the ABM to biophysical models to stress-test the practical reliability and adoption stability of low-emission fertilizers under extreme weather scenarios; 4) Cross-sectoral scalability, applying the ”policy laboratory” logic to beef or tillage sectors across different global regions to examine how varied network architectures influence diffusion; and 5) Real-time decision support, evolving the framework into a live dashboard that utilizes current market prices and national statistics to provide dynamic, rolling forecasts for emission reduction targets.
