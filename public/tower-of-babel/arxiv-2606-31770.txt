ACF Almost Monotonicity at Infinity with Applications to Perturbed Global Solutions
Simon Eberle, Anthony Salib, Georg S. Weiss, Henrik Shahgholian

Abstract
. We study the large-scale behavior of the coincidence set of perturbations of global solutions to the classical obstacle problem in ℝ n ∖ B 1 \mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1} , with blow-down invariant in the e n e_{n} direction. In dimensions n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 , we prove that, locally around regular points sufficiently far out, the cross-sections of { u = 0 } \{u=0\} perpendicular to e n e_{n} are C 2 C^{2} perturbations of ellipsoids. The main ingredient is a new large-scale almost monotonicity formula for the Alt–Caffarelli–Friedman functional. In contrast with the classical small-scale perturbative theory, our argument exploits the stability of the obstacle problem together with the fact that local perturbations vanish under blow-down. The method provides a model mechanism for controlling errors at infinity in stable free boundary problems.

1.
Introduction

Monotonicity formulae are a central tool in the analysis of free boundary problems. In many situations one does not have an exact monotonicity formula, but rather an almost monotonicity formula adapted to perturbations, lower-order terms, or non-homogeneous right-hand sides; see, for instance,
[
2
,
8
,
12
]
. The general strategy behind such results is often perturbative: one differentiates the relevant functional, identifies the errors produced by the perturbation, and proves that these errors are controlled in the regime under consideration.

For the Alt–Caffarelli–Friedman monotonicity formula (ACF) introduced in
[
1
]
, this program was carried out at small scales in the celebrated work
[
2
]
. A key feature of that argument is that the functions under consideration vanish at the base point with an algebraic rate. This small-scale vanishing makes the perturbative errors integrable and allows one to recover an almost monotonicity formula (see
[
2
, Theorem 1.6]
).

The purpose of the present paper is to prove an analogue of this phenomenon at large scales for perturbations of global solutions to the obstacle problem and thus deduce geometric information on the coincidence set of such solutions. The mechanism is fundamentally different from that of
[
2
]
. At infinity one cannot rely on vanishing at a point: indeed, the relevant blow-downs have quadratic growth. Instead, the basic observation is that a local perturbation becomes negligible after rescaling to large scales. Thus the perturbation is not controlled by small-scale vanishing of the solution, but by the shrinking of the perturbed region under blow-down.

As a model problem we consider the classical obstacle problem

(1.1)

\Delta u=\chi_{\{u>0\}}\quad\text{in }D,\qquad u=g\quad\text{on }\partial D,

where
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
, and
g
is a prescribed smooth boundary datum. When
D={\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, solutions of this problem are called global solutions and have been characterized in all dimensions. More specifically, it has been shown, see
[
6
,
5
]
, that the coincidence sets
\{u=0\}
are generalized ellipsoids, in the sense that there exists a sequence of ellipsoids
E_{j}
such that
\{u=0\}=\lim_{j}E_{j}
in the Hausdorff sense.

The question we raise in this article is what happens if we perturb a global solution to the obstacle problem locally. More specifically, we are interested in the behavior of solutions to the above problem at infinity when
D=\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}
. To provide some perspective for the reader, one may consider any global solution in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
—such as those where the coincidence set is an ellipsoid, a paraboloid, a half-space, or a cylinder with an elliptic or parabolic base—and consider a local perturbation within
B_{1}
while preserving the behavior at infinity of the original solution. Such a construction is easily done by fixing any Dirichlet data on
\partial B_{1}
, and solving the obstacle problem in
B_{R}\setminus B_{1}
, with boundary values on
\partial B_{R}
as one of the above-mentioned global solutions. It then follows by a standard compactness argument that, along a subsequence, the solutions
u_{R}
converge to a solution of our problem (see Appendix
A
).

At such a general level, the question may have several answers, and a classification of all perturbed global solutions seems far from being understood. For instance, an important feature of global solutions is that they are convex and so if
\{u=0\}
has nonempty interior then the entire free boundary is made up of regular points. Recall that a point
x_{0}\in{\partial}\{u>0\}
is called a regular point, and we write
x_{0}\in\operatorname{Reg}(u)
, if the free boundary is smooth in some neighborhood of
x_{0}
(see
[
3
]
). If
x_{0}\notin\operatorname{Reg}(u)
then
x_{0}
is called a singular point and we write
x_{0}\in\operatorname{Sing}(u)
. Moreover, it is shown in
[
3
]
that the entire free boundary is decomposed as

(1.2)

{\partial}\{u>0\}=\operatorname{Reg}(u)\cup\operatorname{Sing}(u).

Hence, while for global solutions with nonempty interior there holds that
\operatorname{Sing}(u)=\emptyset
, for the perturbed problem this is not necessarily the case and there may be many connected components containing singular points, and such components cannot be ruled out in general. Since the set of singular points can exhibit wild behavior, see the examples constructed in
[
13
]
, one cannot hope to give such a structural theorem as Theorem
1.2
on the set of singular points. Nevertheless we are still able to provide some structure for the regular part of the free boundary of perturbed solutions at large scales (see Theorem
1.2
below for the precise statement).

1.1.
Results

Our first main result is an almost monotonicity formula for the Alt–Caffarelli–Friedman functional at infinity. Recall that, for a function
v
, the ACF functional is

(1.3)

\Phi(v,x_{0},r)=\frac{1}{r^{4}}\left(\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}\frac{|\nabla v_{+}|^{2}}{|x-x_{0}|^{n-2}}\right)\left(\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}\frac{|\nabla v_{-}|^{2}}{|x-x_{0}|^{n-2}}\right),

and given a direction
e\in{\partial}B_{1}
we define the finite difference quotients

(1.4)

v_{e,h}(x)=\frac{v(x+he)-v(x)}{h}.

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose that
w\in C^{1,1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
is a non-negative solution of
\Delta w=f
for some
f\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
with
\operatorname{supp}(f-\chi_{\{w>0\}})\subset B_{4}
. Then, for every
e\in{\partial}B_{1}
, for
n\geq 3
, given
\delta>0
and
h\in(0,1)
, there exists a constant
C=C(\delta,h,\left\Arrowvert{D^{2}w}\right\Arrowvert_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})},\left\Arrowvert{f}\right\Arrowvert_{L^{\infty}},n)
such that

(1.5)

\Phi(w_{e,h},x_{0},r)\leq\Phi(w_{e,h},x_{0},R)+Cr^{-1/2}+\delta,

for each
x_{0}\in{\partial}\{w>0\}
and
1<r<R
. For
n=2
, given
h\in(0,1)
there exists a constant
C=C(h,\left\Arrowvert{D^{2}w}\right\Arrowvert_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})},\left\Arrowvert{f}\right\Arrowvert_{L^{\infty}})
, such that

(1.6)

\Phi(w_{e,h},x_{0},r)\leq\Phi(w_{e,h},x_{0},R)+C\log(4R)r^{-1/2},

for each
x_{0}\in{\partial}\{w>0\}
and
1<r<R
.

Notice that Theorem
1.1
applies to solutions of (
1.1
) after applying a cut-off around
B_{1}
(see Section
2
below). Thus, although the ACF functional need not be monotone for the perturbed solution itself, the defect of monotonicity tends to zero at infinity. In dimension
n=2
the same argument gives an almost monotonicity formula with a constant depending on the
\log
of the outer radius and thanks to this logarithm it is still sufficient for the classification of blow-down limits; see Lemma
4.1
below.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is the main new ingredient of the paper, and as mentioned, it is not obtained by differentiating the ACF functional and estimating the resulting error terms. Instead, for each large scale we compare the exterior solution, after a suitable cut-off, with its obstacle replacement in a large ball with the same boundary data. The ACF functional associated with this replacement is genuinely monotone. Stability estimates for the obstacle problem then show that the replacement is sufficiently close to the original cut-off solution. Since the perturbation is confined to a fixed compact set, its effect shrinks at large scales, and one can transfer enough monotonicity from the replacement back to the perturbed solution.

This large-scale mechanism is the essential difference from
[
2
]
: there the perturbative errors are controlled by the algebraic vanishing of the solution at the base point, whereas here they are controlled by the disappearance of the compact perturbation under blow-down.

Theorem
1.1
allows us then to classify blow-down limits along moving centers in every dimension
n\geq 2
. When
n\geq 3
and the blow-down of
u
is a quadratic polynomial independent of
x_{n}
, this classification yields the following structure theorem for the regular part of the free boundary sufficiently far from the origin. This setting corresponds to paraboloid global solutions. In dimension
n=2
, the relevant cross-sections are line segments, so the statement gives no additional geometric information. For the definition of an
\varepsilon
-
C^{2}
normal graph, see Definition
2.7
.

Theorem 1.2
.

For
n\geq 3
, let
u
be a perturbed global solution to the obstacle problem in the sense of Definition
2.1
, and suppose that
E^{\prime}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}
is the ellipsoid associated to
p(x^{\prime})
. Then for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
R_{\varepsilon}>0
such that the following statement holds. For
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
, set

(1.7)

\Sigma_{t}:=\left\{x^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}:(x^{\prime},t)\in\{u=0\}\right\}.

Let
x_{0}=(x_{0}^{\prime},t)\in\operatorname{Reg}(u)
satisfy

. Then
{\partial}\Sigma_{t}
is an
\varepsilon
-
C^{2}
normal graph over a homothetic copy of
{\partial}E^{\prime}
.

The proof of Theorem
1.2
is a modification of the local analysis carried out in
[
7
]
. The argument proceeds in two steps. First, we apply Theorem
1.1
to classify blow-downs along moving centers; this is the content of Lemma
4.1
. We then show that there exists a sequence of rescalings such that the blow-down limit has a paraboloid or cylinder over an ellipsoid as a coincidence set; this is Proposition
4.2
. The convergence of regular free boundaries then yields Theorem
1.2
.

We note that Theorem
1.1
allows us to classify blow-downs along moving centers for perturbed solutions whose blow-down is any homogeneous quadratic polynomial
p
, not just in the case where
\dim(\ker(p))=1
. In particular, neither Lemma
4.1
nor Proposition
4.2
use the assumption
\dim(\ker(p))=1
. Thus, the remaining cases could be treated by adapting the arguments of
[
7
]
. Since our main purpose is to demonstrate that Theorem
1.1
can handle perturbations at infinity, we restrict our attention, for readability, to the case
\dim(\ker(p))=1
. For the corresponding results in the cases
\dim(\ker(p))\geq 2
, we refer the reader to the proof of
[
7
, Main Theorem]
.

We conclude the preliminaries with the following result from
[
3
, Lemma 14]
which allows us to compare quadratic polynomials using the ACF.

Lemma 2.14
.

Let
p
and
q
be homogeneous quadratic polynomials of the form

(2.25)

p(x)=x^{T}Ax,\qquad q(x)=x^{T}Qx,

where
A,Q\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
are symmetric positive semidefinite matrices such that
\operatorname{tr}(A)=\operatorname{tr}(Q)=\frac{1}{2}
. Assume that for every
e\in{\partial}B_{1}
there holds

(2.26)

\Phi({\partial}_{e}q,0,1)\leq\Phi({\partial}_{e}p,0,1).

Then
p\equiv q
.

Proof.

For each
e\in{\partial}B_{1}
we have
{\partial}_{e}p(x)=2(Ae)\cdot x
and
{\partial}_{e}q(x)=2(Qe)\cdot x
. Moreover, if
\ell_{a}(x)=a\cdot x
, then

(2.27)

\Phi(\ell_{a},0,1)=c_{n}\left\arrowvert{a}\right\arrowvert^{4}

for some dimensional constant
c_{n}>0
. Hence, from (
2.26
), we obtain that for every
e\in{\partial}B_{1}

(2.28)

\left\arrowvert{Qe}\right\arrowvert^{2}\leq\left\arrowvert{Ae}\right\arrowvert^{2}.

Let
N=A-Q
. Then
N
is symmetric and
\operatorname{tr}N=0
. Since
Q=A-N
, (
2.28
) gives

(2.29)

\left\arrowvert{(A-N)e}\right\arrowvert^{2}\leq\left\arrowvert{Ae}\right\arrowvert^{2}

for every
e\in{\partial}B_{1}
. Choose
e
to be an eigenvector corresponding to the smallest eigenvalue
\lambda\leq 0
of
N
. Then (
2.29
) gives

(2.30)

\left\arrowvert{Ae-\lambda e}\right\arrowvert^{2}\leq\left\arrowvert{Ae}\right\arrowvert^{2},

or equivalently

(2.31)

-2\lambda e^{T}Ae+\lambda^{2}\leq 0.

Since
A
is non-negative and
\lambda\leq 0
, both terms on the left hand side of (
2.31
) are non-negative. Hence
\lambda=0
.

Thus the smallest eigenvalue of
N
is zero. Since
\operatorname{tr}N=0
, all eigenvalues of
N
are zero, and therefore
N=0
. Hence
A=Q
, and consequently
p\equiv q
.
∎

In this short appendix we construct solutions to the exterior domain problem as in Definition
2.1
.

Lemma A.1

(Existence of exterior domain solutions)
.

Let
K\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a compact set with smooth boundary and let

g:{\partial}K\to[0,\infty)
be a smooth function. Furthermore we let
p
be a homogeneous quadratic polynomial on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}
satisfying
\Delta p=1
and
p(x^{\prime})\geq c\left\arrowvert{x^{\prime}}\right\arrowvert^{2}
. There exists an exterior domain solution

(A.1)

u:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\setminus K\to[0,\infty)

such that

(A.2)

\Delta u=\chi_{\{u>0\}}\qquad\text{in }{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\setminus K,

(A.3)

u=g\qquad\text{on }{\partial}K,

and we have that

(A.4)

\frac{u(Rx)}{R^{2}}\to p(x^{\prime})\qquad\text{in }L^{\infty}_{{\textup{loc}}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}\setminus\{0\})\quad\text{as }R\to\infty.

Furthermore,
\{u=0\}
is unbounded in the
e_{n}
-direction, and the
unbounded connected component has nonempty interior.

Proof.

Let
U
be a global solution of the obstacle problem with paraboloid
coincidence set
P
and blow-down
p
. The first-order asymptotics of
U
imply that

(A.5)

{\partial}_{n}U\leq c<0\qquad\text{on }\{x_{n}\leq d\}

for some sufficiently small
d<0
and so we can translate
U
such that
U^{-}(x)=U(x+\tau e_{n})\leq g
on
{\partial}K
and
U^{+}(x)=U(x-\tau e_{n})\geq g
on
{\partial}K
for some
\tau\in(0,\infty)
large enough.
Now for every
R
such that
K\subset B_{R}
, let
u^{R}
be the solution of

(A.6)

\begin{cases}\Delta u^{R}=\chi_{\{u^{R}>0\}}&\text{in }B_{R}\setminus K,\\
u^{R}=g&\text{on }{\partial}K,\\
u^{R}=U&\text{on }{\partial}B_{R},\end{cases}

and we observe that for each
R
we have

(A.7)

U^{-}\leq u^{R}\leq U^{+}

in
B_{R}\setminus K
by the comparison principle for the obstacle problem. Hence, along a subsequence
u^{R}
converges locally uniformly to a global solution
u
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\setminus K
satisfying

(A.8)

\begin{cases}\Delta u=\chi_{\{u>0\}}&\text{in }{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\setminus K,\\
u=g&\text{on }{\partial}K.\end{cases}

By (
A.7
) we have that
\{u=0\}
contains a paraboloid and so has an unbounded connected component with nonempty interior. Finally, by (
A.7
) again we find that

(A.9)

R^{-2}U^{-}(Rx)\leq R^{-2}u(Rx)\leq R^{-2}U^{+}(Rx),

in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\setminus\frac{1}{R}K
and we conclude that

(A.10)

\frac{u(Rx)}{R^{2}}\to p(x^{\prime})\qquad\text{in }L^{\infty}_{{\textup{loc}}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}\setminus\{0\})\quad\text{as }R\to\infty,

since
\frac{1}{R}K
vanishes in the limit.
∎

Appendix B
A capacitory Poincaré inequality in dimension two

The following Poincaré inequality can be found in
[
9
, Section 14.1.2]
. Since we only require it in a simple setting we give the following elementary proof.

Lemma B.1

(Capacitory Poincaré inequality in dimension two)
.

Let
R\geq 4
and suppose that
u\in H^{1}(B_{R})
with
u=0
on
{\partial}B_{R}
in the sense of traces. Then there exists a universal constant
C
such that

(B.1)

\left\Arrowvert{u}\right\Arrowvert_{L^{2}(B_{4})}\leq C\sqrt{\log(R)}\left\Arrowvert{\nabla u}\right\Arrowvert_{L^{2}(B_{R})}.

Proof.

It is enough to prove the estimate for smooth functions satisfying
u=0
on
{\partial}B_{R}
, and then argue by approximation. We write
x=s\theta
, where
s\in(0,R)
and
\theta\in{\partial}B_{1}
. Since
u(R\theta)=0
, we have for every
s\in(0,R)

(B.2)

u(s\theta)=-\int_{s}^{R}{\partial}_{\rho}u(\rho\theta)d\rho.

Hence, by Hölder’s inequality,

(B.3)

\left\arrowvert{u(s\theta)}\right\arrowvert^{2}\leq\left(\int_{s}^{R}\frac{1}{\rho}d\rho\right)\left(\int_{s}^{R}\left\arrowvert{{\partial}_{\rho}u(\rho\theta)}\right\arrowvert^{2}\rho d\rho\right).

Therefore,

(B.4)

\left\arrowvert{u(s\theta)}\right\arrowvert^{2}\leq\log\left(\frac{R}{s}\right)\int_{0}^{R}\left\arrowvert{\nabla u(\rho\theta)}\right\arrowvert^{2}\rho d\rho.

Integrating (
B.4
) over
B_{4}
in polar coordinates gives

(B.5)

\left\Arrowvert{u}\right\Arrowvert_{L^{2}(B_{4})}^{2}\leq\left(\int_{0}^{4}s\log\left(\frac{R}{s}\right)ds\right)\int_{{\partial}B_{1}}\int_{0}^{R}\left\arrowvert{\nabla u(\rho\theta)}\right\arrowvert^{2}\rho d\rho d\theta.

Since
R\geq 4
,

(B.6)

\int_{0}^{4}s\log\left(\frac{R}{s}\right)ds\leq C\log(R).

Combining (
B.5
) and (
B.6
), we obtain

(B.7)

\left\Arrowvert{u}\right\Arrowvert_{L^{2}(B_{4})}^{2}\leq C\log(R)\left\Arrowvert{\nabla u}\right\Arrowvert_{L^{2}(B_{R})}^{2}.

Taking square roots gives (
B.1
).
∎

We will need the following off-centered version of the above result.

Lemma B.2

(Off-center capacitory Poincaré inequality in dimension two)
.

Let
R\geq 1
,
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and let

u\in H^{1}_{0}(B_{R}(x_{0}))
. Then there exists a universal constant
C
such that

(B.8)

\|u\|_{L^{2}(B_{4}\cap B_{R}(x_{0}))}\leq C\sqrt{\log(8+R)}\,\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(B_{R}(x_{0}))}.

Proof.

If
B_{4}\cap B_{R}(x_{0})=\emptyset
, there is nothing to prove. Otherwise

|x_{0}|\leq R+4
. Extend
u
by zero outside
B_{R}(x_{0})
and denote the
extension by
\tilde{u}
. Then

(B.9)

\operatorname{supp}\tilde{u}\subset B_{|x_{0}|+R}\subset B_{2R+4}.

In particular,
\tilde{u}\in H^{1}_{0}(B_{2R+8})
. Applying
Lemma
B.1
to
\tilde{u}
in the concentric ball

B_{2R+8}
gives

(B.10)

\|\tilde{u}\|_{L^{2}(B_{4})}\leq C\sqrt{\log(2R+8)}\,\|\nabla\tilde{u}\|_{L^{2}(B_{2R+8})}.

Since

(B.11)

\|\tilde{u}\|_{L^{2}(B_{4})}\geq\|u\|_{L^{2}(B_{4}\cap B_{R}(x_{0}))}

and

(B.12)

\|\nabla\tilde{u}\|_{L^{2}(B_{2R+8})}=\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(B_{R}(x_{0}))},

the claim follows.
∎
