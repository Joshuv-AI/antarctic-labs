Protective effects of PRELP protein in a mouse model of pulmonary fibrosis

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPulmonary fibrosis is a chronic progressive lung disease characterized by extensive fibrosis and poor prognosis, highlighting the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies. This study aims to elucidate the role of the small proteoglycan proline/arginine-rich end leucine-rich repeat protein (PRELP), evaluate the protective potential of recombinant PRELP protein, and investigate its underlying molecular mechanisms.MethodsRNA in situ hybridization was performed on lung sections prepared from control subjects and patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF). In mouse experiments, we established a bleomycin (BLM)-induced lung fibrosis model using PRELP knockout mice and assessed the extent of fibrosis. Furthermore, we administered recombinant PRELP protein via the airway in wild-type mice with BLM-induced fibrosis to evaluate its effects. In vitro experiments were conducted to investigate the role of PRELP in alveolar epithelial cells and fibroblasts.ResultsIn human lungs, PRELP expression was mainly detected in stromal regions and partly in type 2 alveolar epithelial cells. Furthermore, PRELP was broadly expressed in alveolar areas of controls but was markedly reduced in the alveolar region and localized to vessel walls in IPF patients. In mouse experiments, fibrosis was more severe in PRELP knockout mice after BLM intratracheal administration than in wild-type mice. Notably, prophylactic trans-airway administration of recombinant PRELP suppressed BLM-induced fibrosis in wild-type mice in vivo. Background

Pulmonary fibrosis is a chronic condition characterized by the thickening and fibrosis of the alveolar interstitial walls. It includes various subtypes such as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), drug-induced fibrosis, and hypersensitivity pneumonitis [1–3]. Among these, IPF is a progressive fibrotic lung disease marked by worsening dyspnea, declining lung function, and poor prognosis [4–6]. The most commonly used therapeutic agents for IPF are the antifibrotic drugs pirfenidone and nintedanib [7, 8]. These treatments have been shown to slow the decline in forced vital capacity and reduce the frequency of acute exacerbations [9, 10]. However, their efficacy remains limited, highlighting the need for novel therapeutic strategies.

Current understanding suggests that pulmonary fibrosis arises from repetitive epithelial injury, leading to aberrant repair that interacts with fibroblasts, myofibroblasts, and results in excessive deposition of extracellular matrix (ECM) through various cytokines and immune responses [11–14]. In particular, transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), a multifunctional cytokine, plays a central role in lung fibrosis by activating Smad phosphorylation and regulating the transcription of fibrosis-related genes [15, 16].

In addition to canonical Smad signaling, accumulating evidence suggests that TGF-β activity is functionally linked to Yes-associated protein (YAP) [17], a key downstream effector of the Hippo signaling pathway [18]. YAP activity is primarily determined by its phosphorylation status at Ser127 [19], and phosphorylation of YAP (pYAP) results in its ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation [20]. In contrast, dephosphorylated YAP facilitates the nuclear translocation of phosphorylated Smad2/3 [17] and can independently translocate to the nucleus, where it promotes the transcription of profibrotic genes, including ECM components such as connective tissue growth factor (CTGF) and collagen type I alpha 1 (COL1A1) [21]. YAP signaling is recognized as a significant contributor to pulmonary fibrosis pathogenesis [22], and pYAP is commonly used as a functional indicator of YAP signaling activity. YAP activity is tightly regulated by cytoskeletal dynamics [23], and one molecule implicated in cytoskeletal regulation is diaphanous-related formin-1 (DIAPH1), which modulates actin polymerization and cellular architecture [24]. Upstream of DIAPH1, the receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE) is expressed [25] and functions as a pattern recognition receptor that recognizes extracellular matrix components and inflammatory ligands [26]. DIAPH1 and RAGE have been implicated as modulators of YAP signaling, but their functional roles in the lung remain largely understood.

Small leucine-rich proteoglycans (SLRPs), a class of ECM components, comprise 18 distinct proteoglycans collectively known as small proteoglycans [27]. SLRPs regulate ECM structure and function, particularly through modulation of the TGF-β signaling pathway [27, 28]. Among them, decorin has been the most extensively studied [29–32]. Decorin expression is observed in fibrotic foci within the lungs of patients with IPF [29], and serum decorin levels are elevated in individuals with IPF compared to healthy controls [30]. In vitro studies revealed that decorin may modulates TGF-β signaling [31, 32]. Other SLRPs, including biglycan, lumican, asporin, and osteoglycan, have also been implicated in lung fibrosis [33–36]. However, no studies have demonstrated that these SLRPs improve pulmonary fibrosis.

Proline/arginine-rich end and leucine-rich protein (PRELP), a Class II member of the SLRP family [27, 28], is a 58-kDa proteoglycan found in various extracellular matrices [37]. Data from the Human Protein Atlas indicated that PRELP is highly expressed in the lungs, heart, uterus, ovary, bladder, and colon [38]. Our previous study demonstrated that PRELP is abundantly expressed in the bladder and plays a crucial role in bladder cancer progression by regulating TGF-β signaling and intercellular adhesion [39]. In a PRELP deficient mouse model, bladder epithelial cells exhibited impaired cell-cell adhesion, enhanced TGF-β signaling, and increased fibroblast proliferation [39]. Given the involvement of PRELP in TGF-β signaling, we hypothesized that it may also influence lung fibrosis.

In this study, we investigated the role of PRELP in lung fibrosis and its potential protective role in this currently incurable lung disease.

Results

PRELP expression in human lungs: comparison between IPF patients and controls

We first examined PRELP expression in human lung tissue. To investigate its distribution, we performed fluorescence RNA in situ hybridization (RNA-ISH) targeting PRELP, surfactant protein C (SFTPC), and advanced glycosylation end-product specific receptor (AGER) RNA in healthy human lung tissue.

PRELP expression was predominantly observed in the stroma, which lacked SFTPC and AGER signals (SFTPC−/AGER−). Some signals were also detected in type 2 alveolar epithelial cells (AT2 cells), marked by SFTPC+ expression (Fig. 1A). These findings indicate that PRELP is expressed in both stromal cells and AT2 cells.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis, conducted using data from existing studies (GSE136831), revealed that PRELP is highly expressed in stromal cell populations, including fibroblasts, myofibroblasts, pericytes, and smooth muscle cells. Importantly, PRELP expression was significantly decreased in fibroblasts and myofibroblasts of stromal cells from IPF patients compared to that in healthy individuals (Fig. 1B). Minimal PRELP expression was also detected in epithelial and endothelial cells through scRNA-seq, and lymphatic endothelial cells exhibited PRELP expression levels comparable to those of stromal cells (Supplementary Fig. S1A).

RNA-ISH of human lung specimens revealed that in the control group (non-smokers, transplant donor lungs), PRELP was expressed in the alveolar region, including alveolar epithelial cells and fibroblasts. However, in IPF patients, PRELP expression was predominantly localized to the vessel walls and was nearly absent in the alveolar region. In the alveolar region, PRELP mRNA expression was significantly decreased in IPF patients compared to controls (Fig. 1C). A list of the human specimens used is provided in Table S1 in the supplementary.

We also analyzed PRELP expression levels in human lungs by disease state using microarray data from the Gene Expression Omnibus Profiles. While no significant differences were observed in overall lung expression between the control group and interstitial pneumonia patients (Supplementary Fig. S1B), PRELP expression was significantly reduced in fibroblasts derived from lung tissue in the scleroderma-associated interstitial pneumonia group compared to the control group (Supplementary Fig. S1C).

Fibrotic changes are exacerbated in PRELP knockout mice compared to wild-type mice

A mouse model of pulmonary fibrosis was established through the intratracheal administration of 25 µg of bleomycin (BLM) at 12 weeks of age, with physiological and pathological analyses performed after 2 weeks. In PRELP knockout (KO) mice, compared with wild-type (WT) mice, reduced PRELP expression was confirmed by immunostaining with an anti-PRELP antibody, Western blot analysis of whole lung tissue, and quantitative real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) (Supplementary Fig. S2A-E). No significant differences were observed between WT mice and PRELP KO mice at baseline in lung phenotype (Fig. 2A-D) and body weight. (Supplementary Fig. S2F).

In WT mice, BLM treatment induced progressive fibrosis and inflammatory cell infiltration in the lungs compared with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) controls, as assessed by hematoxylin–eosin (HE) and Elastica van Gieson (EVG) staining. These pathological changes were more pronounced in PRELP KO mice compared to WT mice (Fig. 2A, B). Quantification of fibrosis using the Ashcroft score and measurement of the fibrotic area in EVG-stained sections revealed a significant increase in fibrosis following BLM treatment, with a further exacerbation in PRELP KO mice compared to WT mice (Fig. 2C, D). Collagen content in the lungs increased following BLM administration in both groups, with significantly higher levels observed in PRELP KO mice compared to WT mice (Fig. 2E). In the physiological study, respiratory function tests revealed a reduction in lung compliance in the BLM-treated group compared to the control group, with a more pronounced decline observed in PRELP KO mice (Fig. 2F). Similarly, airway resistance increased following BLM treatment, with a greater elevation in PRELP KO mice compared to WT mice (Supplementary Fig. S4A). Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) analysis revealed an increase in total cell counts in the BLM-treated group, predominantly due to elevated macrophage numbers. Among BLM-treated mice, total cell counts were significantly higher in PRELP KO mice compared to WT mice (Fig. 2G). A similar trend was observed for macrophage counts (Supplementary Fig. S4B). In contrast, lymphocyte and neutrophil counts remained low and did not significantly differ between the two groups (Supplementary Fig. S4C, D). Total protein concentrations in BALF were significantly elevated in BLM-treated mice compared with PBS controls, whereas no significant differences were detected between WT and PRELP KO mice within each treatment group (Supplementary Fig. S4E).

In addition, no significant differences in PRELP expression were observed between the PBS and BLM groups by both Western blot analysis and quantitative real-time RT-PCR (Supplementary Fig. S4F-H). Consistent with our experimental findings, single-cell RNA sequencing analysis of mouse lungs using publicly available data (GSE141259) revealed that PRELP expression in stromal cells, the cell type with the highest PRELP expression in mouse lungs, was not significantly altered following bleomycin treatment (Supplementary Fig. S6).

Administration of recombinant PRELP protein reduces pulmonary fibrosis in mice

Given the findings from PRELP KO mice, which suggest that PRELP has inhibitory and protective effects against lung fibrosis, we investigated whether recombinant PRELP protein could mitigate BLM-induced fibrosis. In this model, recombinant PRELP protein was directly administered via the respiratory tract, followed a day later by bleomycin. Bovine serum albumin was used as a control protein to match the total protein amount administered in the recombinant PRELP groups. In PBS-treated mice, recombinant PRELP protein administration did not significantly affect lung phenotype or markers of fibrosis (Fig. 3).

However, in BLM-treated mice, treatment with recombinant PRELP protein (10 µg and 100 µg) reduced fibrosis compared with the control group (Fig. 3A, B). Quantification using the Ashcroft score and measurement of the fibrotic area in EVG-stained sections confirmed these findings (Fig. 3C, D). Notably, the higher dose (100 µg) resulted in significantly greater suppression of lung fibrosis than the lower dose (10 µg) (Fig. 3A-D).

Respiratory function tests revealed that treatment with recombinant PRELP protein ameliorated the BLM-induced reduction in lung compliance in both the 10 µg and 100 µg treatment groups (Fig. 3E). However, airway resistance did not differ significantly among the three groups (Supplementary Fig. S7A). BALF analysis showed that recombinant PRELP protein administration did not affect total cell counts (Fig. 3F). Additionally, macrophage, lymphocyte, and neutrophil counts remained unchanged across the treatment groups (Supplementary Fig. S7B-D).

Effects of PRELP on fibrotic responses in vitro

We investigated the effects of PRELP in A549 cells, a human alveolar basal epithelial adenocarcinoma cell line, and HFL1 cells, a human fibroblast cell line. Quantitative real-time RT-PCR analysis revealed minimal PRELP expression in A549 cells compared with HFL1 cells (Supplementary Fig. S8A). PRELP knockdown via siRNA significantly reduced its expression in both cell types (Supplementary Fig. S8B), whereas overexpression using a pcDNA vector resulted in a 1000-fold increase (Supplementary Fig. S8C).

TGF-β stimulation of A549 cells induces a phenotypic shift characterized by the loss of epithelial features and the acquisition of mesenchymal-like traits, as evidenced by increased vimentin expression and decreased E-cadherin expression. PRELP knockdown further elevated vimentin expression but had no apparent effect on E-cadherin levels (Fig. 4A). In contrast, direct administration of recombinant PRELP protein suppressed the increase in vimentin expression, while E-cadherin levels remained unchanged (Fig. 4B). Overexpression of PRELP via pcDNA vectors had minimal impact on TGF-β-induced changes in vimentin and E-cadherin expression (Supplementary Fig. S9). The original blots and gels are presented in Supplementary Figures S10 and S11. These findings indicate that PRELP inhibits a phenotypic shift induced by TGF-β. However, PRELP expression itself was not altered by TGF-β1 stimulation in either A549 epithelial cells or HFL1 fibroblasts (Supplementary Fig. S8D).

In A549 cells, immunofluorescence staining was used to evaluate the expression of zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1), a tight junction protein contributing to barrier function [40], and caveolin-1 (Cav-1), a key structural and regulatory component of caveolae implicated in tight junction regulation [41]. PRELP knockdown significantly reduced the expression levels of both ZO-1 and Cav-1 (Fig. 5A-D). Conversely, treatment with recombinant PRELP protein (1.0 µg/mL) significantly upregulated ZO-1 and Cav-1 expression compared with the control group (Fig. 5E-H). Similarly, PRELP overexpression led to a significant increase in ZO-1 and Cav-1 expression (Supplementary Fig. S12A-D). These findings suggest that PRELP plays a role in enhancing cell-cell adhesion and maintaining epithelial cell function.

PRELP modulates the TGF-β Pathway and RAGE/DIAPH1/YAP signaling

We investigated the expression of TGF-β-regulated genes in A549 (epithelial) and HFL1 (fibroblast) cells. In A549 cells, the TGF-β1-induced increases in CTGF and COL1A1 expression were significantly suppressed by recombinant PRELP protein administration (Fig. 7A, B). In contrast, in HFL1 cells, the TGF-β1-induced upregulation of CTGF and COL1A1 was not affected by recombinant PRELP treatment (Fig. 7C, D), suggesting that extracellular PRELP primarily influences epithelial cells rather than fibroblasts. Additionally, in A549 cells, PRELP knockdown via siRNA did not result in a significant change in TGF-β1-induced CTGF expression, nor did it affect COL1A1 expression. However, in the PRELP overexpression group (pcDNA), TGF-β1-induced increases in CTGF and COL1A1 were significantly suppressed. In HFL1 cells, neither PRELP knockdown nor overexpression significantly affected TGF-β1-induced CTGF or COL1A1 expression (Supplementary Fig. S14A-H).

Based on these findings, we next examined the involvement of TGF-β signaling in mouse lungs. To evaluate differences in TGF-β pathway activation between WT and PRELP KO mice, phosphorylation of SMAD family member 2 and 3 (Smad2/3), central downstream effectors of TGF-β signaling, was analyzed by Western blotting. As a result, increased levels of phosphorylated Smad2/3 were observed in PRELP KO mice (Supplementary Fig. S15A, B). In addition, quantitative real-time RT-PCR analysis of lung tissues showed increased expression of TGF-β–related profibrotic genes (Tgf-β1 (Tgfβ1), Tgf-β receptor 1 (Tgfβr1), Ctgf, Col1a1, and Yap), as well as Rage and Diaph1, in PRELP KO mice compared with WT mice, with Yap showing an increasing trend (Supplementary Fig. S17).

In A549 cells, the addition of recombinant PRELP protein suppressed Smad2/3 phosphorylation (Fig. 8A, B) and reduced CTGF levels (Fig. 8C, D). Furthermore, recombinant PRELP protein suppressed DIAPH1 levels (Fig. 8E, F) while enhancing YAP phosphorylation (Fig. 8G, H). The original blots and gels are provided in Supplementary Figures S18, S19, S20, and S21. These findings indicate that extracellular PRELP protein not only inhibits the TGF-β pathway by reducing Smad phosphorylation in epithelial cells but also modulates RAGE/DIAPH1/YAP signaling, specifically by inhibiting DIAPH1 and promoting YAP phosphorylation.

PRELP also suppresses Smad and RAGE/DIAPH1/YAP signaling in mouse lungs

Additionally, we administered 100 µg of recombinant PRELP protein to 12-week-old mice via the respiratory tract and harvested the lungs for analysis 6 h later. Since the recombinant PRELP protein contains a FLAG tag, mouse lungs treated transapically with the recombinant protein were stained using an anti-FLAG antibody. Lung sections were also subjected to immunostaining with anti-phosphorylated SMAD2/3, anti-DIAPH1, and anti-phosphorylated YAP antibodies.

FLAG signals were observed throughout the alveolar region, with a minimal presence on the airways (Fig. 9A, B). This finding indicates that the recombinant PRELP protein remained in the lungs even 6 h post-administration. Furthermore, analysis of phosphorylated SMAD2/3 staining revealed that administration of recombinant PRELP protein to mice resulted in suppressed expression in the alveolar region compared with the control group (Fig. 9C, D). Moreover, analysis of the RAGE/DIAPH1/YAP pathway demonstrated that recombinant PRELP protein administration suppressed DIAPH1 expression and enhanced phosphorylated YAP expression in the alveolar region relative to the control group (Fig. 9E-H). Protein extracts from whole mouse lungs analyzed by western blotting revealed that DIAPH1 and phosphorylated YAP displayed comparable patterns (Fig. 9I-K), and the original blots and gels are provided in Supplementary Figures S22. This observation is consistent with our previous findings in epithelial cells, demonstrating that recombinant PRELP protein treatment alters not only phosphorylated Smad but also DIAPH1 and phosphorylated YAP levels.

Discussion

Gene expression distribution of PRELP

This study is the first to administer recombinant PRELP protein in a mouse model of BLM-induced pulmonary fibrosis, demonstrating its potential as a protective agent for pulmonary fibrosis. Additionally, it is the first to examine the effects of PRELP on intracellular signaling pathways in the context of fibrosis.

Fluorescent RNA-ISH performed on human lung samples in this study revealed that PRELP is expressed not only in fibroblasts but also in AT2 cells. However, scRNA-seq data suggested that PRELP expression was nearly absent in AT2 cells. This discrepancy may be due to the dropout of low-expression genes, a known limitation of scRNA-seq methodologies, as reported in previous studies [43, 44]. In contrast, RNA-ISH is recognized for its higher sensitivity [45]. Based on these findings, it is plausible that PRELP is indeed expressed in AT2 cells, albeit at low levels that might evade detection in scRNA-seq analyses.

PRELP regulates pulmonary fibrosis in mouse models

Fibrotic changes following BLM trans-airway administration were more pronounced in PRELP KO mice compared to WT mice. This finding suggests that the small proteoglycan PRELP plays a protective and inhibitory role against lung fibrosis.

Remarkably, trans-airway administration of recombinant PRELP protein mitigated pulmonary fibrosis in a BLM-induced mouse model. A high dose (100 µg) of recombinant PRELP protein significantly reduced lung fibrosis, highlighting its potential prophylactic efficacy. A low dose (10 µg) of PRELP also showed a certain degree of antifibrotic effect, and no substantial difference was observed between the low- and high-dose groups, suggesting that the administered dose range (10–100 µg) was appropriate. On the other hand, recombinant PRELP administration did not significantly alter BALF cellularity, although inflammatory cell infiltration was further increased in response to bleomycin-induced lung fibrosis in PRELP KO mice. These findings suggest that the effect of recombinant PRELP protein appeared to be predominantly antifibrotic rather than anti-inflammatory.

There are no reported animal studies in which recombinant SLRP proteins have been administered to models of inflammatory lung diseases, including pulmonary fibrosis. To our knowledge, this is the first study to administer recombinant SLRP proteins via the trans-airway route in mouse models of pulmonary fibrosis.

Effects of PRELP on fibrosis through TGF-β signals in in vitro experiments

Our in vitro experiments demonstrated that the small proteoglycan PRELP suppresses the acquisition of mesenchymal traits and enhances cell-cell adhesion in A549 epithelial cells stimulated with TGFβ. Furthermore, PRELP inhibited the migratory ability of HFL1 fibroblast cells. These results support the hypothesis that PRELP exerts an inhibitory effect on pulmonary fibrosis.

Although the relationship between cell adhesion molecules and pulmonary fibrosis has not yet been fully elucidated, previous studies have reported a close association between tight junctions and the development of pulmonary fibrosis [46]. In the present study, PRELP enhanced the expression of ZO-1, a major structural component of epithelial tight junctions [40], as well as Cav-1, a regulatory protein involved in tight junction organization [41], suggesting a potential role for PRELP in the modulation of pulmonary fibrosis. Consistent with this notion, knockdown of PRELP in epithelial cells in vitro markedly reduced tight junction protein expression, raising the possibility that epithelial permeability may be altered in PRELP KO mouse lungs. However, in the present in vivo study, total cells and total protein levels in BALF did not differ between WT and PRELP KO mice in uninjured lungs, suggesting that global alveolar-capillary barrier permeability was not significantly affected under the experimental conditions examined.

Fibroblast migration is known to contribute to the accumulation of fibroblasts within fibrotic lesions in bleomycin-induced lungs [42]. There are currently limited data regarding the effects of PRELP on fibroblast migratory behavior; however, in our in vitro experiments, PRELP suppressed fibroblast migration.

As the TGF-β pathway plays a pivotal role in lung fibrosis [15, 16], understanding the effect of PRELP on this pathway is particularly important. TGF-β, particularly TGF-β1, activates fibroblasts by binding to its receptor, leading to the phosphorylation of intracellular Smad2 and Smad3. These phosphorylated Smad2/3 translocate to the nucleus, enhancing the expression of ECM-related genes such as CTGF and COL1A1 [47]. Additionally, the expression of these genes is upregulated in both alveolar epithelial cells and fibroblasts [48].

Moreover, our findings suggest that the extracellular recombinant PRELP protein primarily affects epithelial cells. Since PRELP is abundantly expressed in fibroblasts, fibroblast-derived PRELP may exert paracrine effects on neighboring epithelial cells by acting extracellularly. In contrast, siRNA-mediated PRELP knockdown in A549 cells did not significantly alter CTGF or COL1A1 mRNA expression, which may be attributable to the relatively low endogenous PRELP expression in this cell line. Nevertheless, TGF-β-induced phenotypic shifts and changes in cell–cell adhesion appeared to be more sensitive to subtle alterations in PRELP levels, resulting in detectable phenotypic effects. Furthermore, these results suggest that direct administration of recombinant PRELP protein may allow PRELP to function more effectively than intracellular overexpression achieved through pcDNA-mediated transfection. Because PRELP is inherently localized to the extracellular matrix, exogenous delivery of recombinant PRELP may enable interactions with cells that more closely resemble its native extracellular context compared with intracellular overexpression.

Effects of PRELP on RAGE/DIAPH1/YAP signals

Previous studies have suggested a potential interaction between PRELP and TGF-β1 [49], implying that PRELP may modulate TGF-β1 signaling, possibly by affecting its interaction with the receptor in epithelial cells. However, given that PRELP is involved in cell adhesion, we hypothesized the involvement of additional intermediaries that mediate signals from the extracellular environment to the nucleus.

One such mediator is YAP, a key downstream effector of the Hippo signaling pathway [18]. Dephosphorylated YAP not only facilitates the nuclear translocation of phosphorylated Smad2/3 [17] but also independently translocates to the nucleus, where it enhances the expression of ECM proteins, such as CTGF and COL1A1 [21]. Conversely, phosphorylated YAP undergoes ubiquitination and subsequent degradation [20]. YAP signaling is recognized as a significant contributor to pulmonary fibrosis pathogenesis [22]. YAP is functionally associated with cell adhesion [50], and the aforementioned Cav-1, whose expression has previously been reported to be markedly decreased in fibroblasts of IPF patients [51], has also been demonstrated to modulate YAP activity [52].

Additionally, RAGE is a receptor that recognizes extracellular matrix components and inflammatory mediators, particularly damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) [26]. Upon ligand binding, RAGE interacts with DIAPH1 in the cytoplasm [25]. This interaction has been reported to alter intracellular F-actin polymerization and remodel the cytoskeleton [24], and such cytoskeletal reorganization has also been shown to promote YAP dephosphorylation and activation [23] (Fig. 10A).

A Dephosphorylated YAP translocates to the nucleus to induce ECM protein expression and contributes to pulmonary fibrosis pathogenesis. RAGE–DIAPH1–dependent cytoskeletal remodeling further promotes YAP activation, linking extracellular matrix signaling to fibrotic responses. B Extracellular administration of recombinant PRELP protein suppresses Smad phosphorylation in the TGF-β pathway, as well as DIAPH1 expression and YAP dephosphorylation in the RAGE/DIAPH1/YAP pathway within the cytoplasm. These combined effects ultimately suppress the expression of ECM-related genes in the nucleus

Possible mechanisms by which PRELP inhibits fibrosis

In our in vitro experiments, we found that treatment with recombinant PRELP not only suppressed SMAD phosphorylation and reduced CTGF expression but also downregulated DIAPH1 expression and increased YAP phosphorylation. Furthermore, our in vivo studies demonstrated that administration of recombinant PRELP protein in mouse lungs not only reduced phosphorylated Smad expression but also decreased Diaph1 expression and increased phosphorylated YAP levels.

Previous studies have suggested that PRELP may be involved in the regulation of TGF-β1 signaling [49], and PRELP has been reported to interact with TGF-β1 in binding assays and to attenuate TGF-β–driven fibrotic responses [53], although these findings were reported in non-pulmonary tissues. Therefore, our findings led us to hypothesize that PRELP may regulate both the TGF-β and RAGE/DIAPH1/YAP signaling pathways in epithelial cells, and these effects are considered to act in concert to reduce ECM protein synthesis, such as CTGF and COL1A1 (Fig. 10B).

Limitations of this study

Our study has some limitations. First, we selected only male mice for the experiments, as males are more sensitive to bleomycin-induced fibrosis than females [54, 55]. It will be beneficial in future studies to assess the effect of PRELP in female mice subjected to bleomycin. Second, the safety and potential side effects of recombinant PRELP proteins, particularly in humans, remain to be thoroughly explored. However, since PRELP is a protein naturally present in the lungs, it is believed to help compensate for its reduction in pulmonary fibrosis. Immunostaining of lung tissues from mice treated with the recombinant PRELP protein suggested that the protein remains in the lungs. While further studies on local inflammation, including an evaluation of the appropriate concentration of recombinant PRELP used in our experiments, are crucial for future research, no significant adverse events were observed in our in vivo study. Lastly, in the present study, recombinant PRELP protein was administered one day before bleomycin treatment, and this prophylactic approach demonstrated a protective effect on bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis. However, the effect of recombinant PRELP after the establishment and progression of fibrosis has not yet been evaluated. Future studies should therefore investigatewhether administration of recombinant PRELP protein after bleomycin-induced fibrotic changes have developed can exert effects on established pulmonary fibrosis.

Conclusions

The small proteoglycan PRELP plays a pivotal role in a mouse model of pulmonary fibrosis by suppressing mesenchymal marker upregulation, reinforcing epithelial cell function, and inhibiting fibroblast migration. Notably, prophylactic trans-airway administration of recombinant PRELP protected against pulmonary fibrosis, indicating that PRELP may be a promising novel protective agent for this disease. Mechanistically, PRELP is likely to modulate both the TGF-β and RAGE/DIAPH1/YAP signaling pathways in epithelial cells.
