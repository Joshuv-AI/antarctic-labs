Trigger-day hCG effects on DNA methylation and neurodevelopment in ART offspring
Authors: Yue Jiang, Xiaoyu Wei, Xianghu Liu, Jun Zhou, Muyi Han, Anqi Zhao, Hong Lv, Yangqian Jiang, Yayun Gu, Cheng Wang, Juncheng Dai, Hongxia Ma, Guangfu Jin, Ran Huo, Qiang Wang, Xuejiang Guo, Hongbing Shen, Yuan Lin, Yuanlin He, Qigang Zhou, Zhibin Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Assisted reproductive technology influences the epigenetic landscape of offspring, but whether trigger-day pharmacotherapy affects epigenetics and neurodevelopment remains unclear. In a prospective birth cohort of 365 families, higher trigger-day human chorionic gonadotropin dose associates with reduced global DNA methylation in peripheral blood from offspring conceived assisted reproduction compared with naturally conceived children. In a cohort of 1333 singleton offspring, higher maternal human chorionic gonadotropin dose associates with increased risk of suboptimal neurodevelopment. In mice, transient human chorionic gonadotropin exposure impairs offspring neurocognitive behaviours, reduces hippocampal dorsal dentate gyrus neurogenesis, and downregulates euchromatic histone methyltransferase 1 expression with reduced methylation observed in oocytes. Microinjection of euchromatic histone lysine methyltransferase 1 mRNA into exposed zygotes restores neurogenesis and improves behavioural outcomes in offspring. These findings suggest potential epigenetic and neurodevelopmental risks of higher-dose human chorionic gonadotropin exposure and support optimized trigger protocols in assisted reproductive technology that balance treatment efficacy with long-term offspring health.

Introduction

The European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology (ESHRE) reports that nearly four million assisted reproductive technology (ART) cycles are performed annually, resulting in more than one million births worldwide1. Increasing evidence indicates that ART procedures may influence the epigenetic landscape of offspring2,3. A key component of ART is ovulation induction, during which high doses of gonadotropins are administered to promote the development of multiple follicles. In mouse and human oocytes, CpG methylation accumulates during growth and is largely established by the germinal vesicle (GV) stage as a bimodal landscape, with the MI/MII stages contributing to locus-specific refinement4–6. Histone modifications play an essential role in directing this process; for example, H3K36me2 and H3K36me3 guide DNMT3A-mediated de novo DNA methylation, whereas the removal of H3K4me2/3 facilitates CpG island methylation. Although dispensable for oocyte maturation and fertilization competence, proper DNA methylation remains critical for normal embryonic development7–10. Previous studies have suggested that ovarian stimulation may alter DNA methylation patterns and interfere with the acquisition of methylation imprints during oocyte growth11,12. However, whether ovulation induction in ART perturbs oocyte DNA methylation and, in turn, alters epigenetic states in the ensuing embryo remains unresolved.

Over the past decade, multiple prospective cohort studies have reported that ovulation induction may increase the risk of neurodevelopmental abnormalities in offspring13–15, prompting concern that epigenetic changes induced during follicular stimulation could underlie these effects. In parallel, accumulating evidence links superovulation to epigenetic perturbations in oocytes and embryos, which are associated with alterations in the development of numerous mammalian species16–23. Given the multistep nature of clinical ART, existing epigenetic studies have focused predominantly on medications that support follicular development (e.g., FSH and hMG) during superovulation11,24, while the potential impact of trigger-day medications remains unclear. We therefore tested whether trigger-day exposure alters DNA methylation patterns in mice and evaluated potential neurodevelopmental outcomes.

In a prospective birth cohort of 365 families, we found that ART-conceived offspring had lower global DNA methylation levels than naturally conceived children, and this reduction was primarily attributable to hCG exposure on the trigger day. Importantly, this hCG-associated reduction in DNA methylation was linked to impaired neurodevelopment in offspring, as higher hCG doses were associated with an increased risk of suboptimal receptive communication in a cohort of 1333 ART-conceived singleton families. These findings suggest that hCG exposure may affect neurodevelopment in offspring by influencing DNA methylation. We tested this hypothesis by establishing a mouse model in which transient hCG exposure impaired neurocognitive behaviors in offspring and suppressed neurogenesis in the hippocampal dorsal dentate gyrus (dDG). We further demonstrated that transient hCG exposure led to the downregulation of Ehmt1 expression and a concomitant loss of DNA methylation in oocytes. Importantly, microinjection of the Ehmt1 mRNA into hCG-exposed zygotes restored neurogenesis and improved behavioral outcomes in offspring. Collectively, these findings identify trigger-day hCG as a modifiable epigenetic risk factor that compromises neurodevelopment in offspring through a disruption of gametic DNA methylation patterns, underscoring the need for evidence-based ART protocols that minimize epigenetic perturbations and protect the neurodevelopment of offspring.

Results

Altered DNA methylation patterns in ART-conceived offspring

We performed whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) on peripheral blood from parent–offspring trios from a prospective birth cohort to characterize ART-associated DNA methylation patterns. A graphical overview of the study design is presented in Fig. 1. The global DNA methylation level in offspring was not associated with parental age, socioeconomic characteristics, or parental methylation (Fig. 2). Per 1 kg/m² increase in pre-pregnancy maternal BMI, offspring DNA methylation decreased by 0.29% (P = 1.19 × 10E-2). per 1 kg increase in birth weight, DNA methylation increased by 0.18% (P = 2.74 × 10E-3), whereas no difference was observed between the sexes. In contrast, twins and ART-conceived offspring had lower methylation levels (β = −0.03, P = 1.73 × 10E-5; β = −0.04, P = 5.89 × 10E-11). Preterm birth was associated with a slightly lower methylation level (β = −0.02, P = 6.03 × 10E-2) (Fig. 2). After adjustment for conception type, pregnancy status, and offspring number, only ART conception remained significantly associated with lower global methylation in children (β = −0.04, P = 3.14 × 10E-7), whereas the offspring number (P = 0.206) and pregnancy status (P = 0.424) were not significantly associated with the global methylation level (Supplementary Data 1). Consistent with the observed reduction in global methylation, ART-associated hypomethylation was observed across multiple genomic elements. Notably, compared with other genomic regions, enhancers, promoters, and 5′ untranslated regions (5′ UTRs) exhibited lower average methylation levels (Supplementary Fig. 1A). We next identified a total of 12,253 differentially methylated regions (DMRs) (Supplementary Data 2), the majority of which were hypomethylated in the ART group. Genomic annotation revealed that 57.4% of DMRs were located within gene bodies, primarily intronic regions (72.6%), followed by exons (17.5%), 3′ UTRs (3.2%), promoters (3.0%), 5′ UTRs (2.2%), and enhancers (1.5%) (Supplementary Fig. 1B). In total, 6723 DMRs were annotated to 4711 differentially methylated genes (DMGs) (Supplementary Data 3). Compared with all DMRs, the top areaStat-ranked DMRs showed greater enrichment of a higher mean CpG density in promoter regions than in other genomic elements (Supplementary Fig. 1C). The top 300 areaStat-ranked DMGs (Supplementary Data 4) were significantly enriched for neurodevelopment-related processes, including nervous system development, neuron projection development, brain development, and adult behavior (Supplementary Fig. 1D), indicating potential functional connections between ART-associated changes in methylation and neurodevelopment.

hCG dose is associated with lower blood DNA methylation

We then investigated whether the altered methylation patterns were attributable to specific medications or procedures during the ART process. After adjusting for parental DNA methylation level, pregnancy status, maternal BMI, offspring number and sex, the global DNA methylation level in offspring varied across ovulation-triggering strategies. Specifically, compared with the gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonist (GnRHa) trigger group, the dual trigger (GnRHa + hCG) group showed a more pronounced and statistically significant reduction in global DNA methylation (adjusted β = −0.03, P = 3.98 × 10E-2), whereas the single-hCG trigger group showed a modest decrease (adjusted β = −0.03, P = 8.68 × 10E-2) (Fig. 3). These results suggest that hCG administration may be associated with lower DNA methylation levels in offspring. Given that GnRHa triggering is primarily recommended for high-risk women with ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS), typically those with elevated anti-Müllerian hormone levels or increased antral follicle counts25, the hCG dose may represent an independent determinant of altered methylation in women without a high OHSS risk.Fig. 3Associations between drug use in ART procedures and global DNA methylation levels in the peripheral blood of the offspring.aAdjusted beta and P values were generated from a multivariate model including parental methylation levels, pregnancy status, maternal BMI, offspring birthweight, offspring number, and offspring sex. Consistently, stratified analyses revealed a dose–response pattern, with a significant reduction observed at medium-high doses (>6750 IU) (adjusted β = −0.03, P = 3.85 × 10E-2). Compared with the NC group, the group treated with hCG-containing strategies generally showed a consistent reduction in global methylation in offspring (β ≈ −0.02 to −0.05), whereas the group treated with hCG-free strategies, such as the GnRHa trigger, showed no significant association. A higher hCG dose was associated with a greater decrease in global methylation than a low-medium dose (β = −0.04), whereas a lower dose resulted in a milder decrease (β = −0.02) (Supplementary Table 1). Sensitivity analyses revealed a persistent association in the non-hCG group (β = −0.04; P = 5.10 × E10-7), whereas excluding the hCG group resulted in a nonsignificant difference (β = −0.02; P = 0.218), suggesting that hCG use may contribute importantly to the observed reduction in methylation in the peripheral blood of ART-conceived offspring (Supplementary Table 2). No significant associations were detected for parental infertility diagnoses or other ART‑related medications and procedures, including stimulation protocols, fertilization strategies, and embryo transfer strategies, with DNA methylation levels in offspring (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Data 5). Collectively, these findings indicate that the reduction in global DNA methylation levels among ART-conceived offspring is primarily attributable to hCG exposure on the trigger day.

To further examine whether hCG exposure underlies the ART-associated methylation differences, we compared DNA methylation profiles between hCG-exposed (dual trigger and single-hCG trigger group) and non-hCG-exposed offspring (GnRHa trigger group) within the ART cohort. We identified a total of 24,554 hCG-associated DMRs, among which 15,759 were annotated to 6881 differentially methylated genes (DMGs) (Supplementary Data 6 and 7). Nearly 40% hCG-associated DMGs (n = 2638) were overlapped with the DMGs identified in the ART versus NC comparison, suggesting that hCG exposure may contribute to a substantial proportion of the ART-associated methylation differences.

hCG use and neurodevelopment at 3 years of age

We examined the association between the trigger‑day hCG dosage and neurodevelopmental outcomes to assess whether hCG exposure influences neurodevelopment in offspring. In a Jiangsu ART birth cohort, neurodevelopment at age three was evaluated using the Bayley‑III Screening Test, which includes assessments of cognition, receptive and expressive communication, fine motor, and gross motor development. A total of 1333 families with ART-conceived singleton offspring were included. When stratified by hCG dosage across trigger strategies, the children of mothers who received higher hCG concentrations (>6750 IU) had an increased risk of suboptimal receptive communication development (crude OR = 2.19, P = 4.40 × 10E-3; adjusted OR = 2.27, P = 3.50 × 10E-3; Fig. 4). hCG exposure affects neurobehaviour in mouse offspring

Receptive communication, depending on language development and broader cognitive functions, generally refers to the ability to receive and understand information conveyed by others26. Although studies have shown that mice can engage in language communication through ultrasonic vocalizations (USVs)27, their language system cannot be directly evaluated in complex environments28. We therefore employed a training reward paradigm to evaluate the ability of mice to learn and respond to specific instructions.

We used a well-established mouse model to evaluate whether hCG exposure on the trigger day affects the behavioral phenotypes of offspring by performing experiments, such as the novel object recognition (NOR) test, T-maze test, and Morris water maze (MWM). In the hCG group, females received hCG during oestrous to obtain oocytes, whereas control females were paired with vasectomized males to collect oocytes under comparable conditions. Oocytes from both cohorts then underwent in vitro fertilization (IVF) to produce first-generation (F1) offspring (Fig. 5A). Consistent with findings from previous cohort studies, the NOR test showed that the discrimination index (DI) for novel objects was significantly lower in the hCG group than in the control group, indicating impaired attentional allocation. Similarly, the proportion of correct food reward responses in the T-maze was reduced, indicating that hCG exposure may impair instruction following and strategy switching in offspring (Fig. 5B, C). Furthermore, during MWM training from days 2 to 6, the latency to reach the platform remained consistently longer in the hCG group than in the control group, indicating impaired spatial learning. In the subsequent platform removal phase, the hCG group spent less time in the target quadrant, suggesting reduced integration of spatial cues during training and difficulty in the formation of accurate spatial memories (Fig. 5D). hCG exposure suppresses dorsal dentate gyrus neurogenesis

We examined specific brain regions, including the cerebral cortex, hippocampal CA1, dorsal dentate gyrus (dDG), and ventral dentate gyrus (vDG), to determine whether hCG affects the neuronal distribution and neurogenesis. Staining for NeuN, a well-established marker of mature neurons, revealed a consistent morphology and distribution patterns across these regions in both the hCG and control groups, indicating that hCG exposure did not affect the overall population of mature neurons. The expression levels of TBR1, a marker of cortical projection neurons28, appeared largely comparable between the hCG and control groups, consistent with the interpretation that hCG exposure does not noticeably alter the structural organization of the cortex or the CA1 region. Similarly, staining for the microglial marker for IBA129, qualitatively indicated similar microglial distribution in both groups. (Fig. 5E and Supplementary Fig. 2). These results indicate that hCG exposure does not alter the overall structural integrity or neuronal composition of these brain regions.

In contrast to neurogenesis in the cortex and CA1 regions, neurogenesis in the dDG was significantly altered. Although the expression of PROX1, a marker of granule cells in the dentate gyrus, did not differ between the groups, the number of cells positive for DCX, a marker of immature neurons, was markedly reduced in the hCG group (Fig. 5F, G). We assessed DCX expression in the vDG to determine if similar effects on neurogenesis occurred in this region. However, no significant differences in the number of DCX-positive cells were detected between the two groups (Fig. 5F, G). This reduction in the number of DCX-positive cells indicates the region-specific suppression of neurogenesis in the dDG following hCG exposure, highlighting the dDG as a key target for hCG-associated alterations in neurogenesis.

We further conducted bulk RNA sequencing and reduced representation bisulfite sequencing (RRBS) analyses of dDG tissues from F1 offspring to assess the molecular alterations induced by the hCG trigger. We identified a total of 427 differentially expressed genes, with a canonical inverse relationship between the 2 kb region upstream of TSS methylation and gene expression. The Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed that these genes were enriched in the regulation of neurogenesis, nervous system development, memory, and cognition-related pathways (Fig. 5H). Twenty-two of these genes exhibited differential methylation levels in the TSS region. The heatmap illustrates distinct transcript expression and methylation patterns for these genes between the control and hCG groups (Fig. 5I). Collectively, these molecular alterations in the dDG link hCG-induced epigenetic changes to region-specific suppression of neurogenesis and the associated neurobehavioural deficits in offspring.

hCG exposure alters oocyte methylation and Ehmt1 expression

Since the ovarian stimulation process coincides with a critical window for oocyte DNA methylation remodeling30, we next examined whether hCG exposure alters the DNA methylation status of mouse MII oocytes. Immunofluorescence staining revealed a significant reduction in the 5mC to 5hmC ratio in MII oocytes from the hCG group compared with those from the control group (Fig. 6A), and similar reductions were observed in zygotes (Fig. 6B). Bisulfite sequencing (scBS-seq) of MII oocytes also showed lower global DNA methylation levels in the hCG group than in the control group (Supplementary Fig. 3A). We further analysed blastocysts derived from hCG-exposed oocytes to examine whether these epigenetic alterations extended beyond fertilization. Blastocysts from the hCG group exhibited lower global DNA methylation and altered methylation at transcription start sites (Supplementary Fig. 3B), consistent with the changes observed in oocytes. We subsequently performed Smart-seq2 on MII oocytes and blastocysts from both groups to identify key molecules that might mediate epigenetic inheritance following hCG administration. Transcriptional changes were enriched for methylation-dependent epigenetic regulation in both MII oocytes and blastocysts, with additional enrichment for embryonic development and neurodevelopment pathways at the blastocyst stage (Fig. 6C and Supplementary Fig. 3C, D). Principal component analysis and volcano plots of the oocytes revealed distinct transcriptional profiles between the two groups (Supplementary Fig. 4A, B). We further examined the persistence of methylation alterations across developmental stages. A total of 120 genes with differentially methylated promoters were identified in both blastocysts and the dDG of mouse offspring that exhibited concordant methylation changes (Supplementary Data 8 and Supplementary Fig. 3E). A functional enrichment analysis of these shared promoter DMGs revealed the significant enrichment of neuronal function and development, suggesting an association between early epigenetic features and neural development (Supplementary Fig. 3F). Among these identified DMGs, solute carrier family 20 member 1 (Slc20a1) and ubiquitin-like with PHD and ring finger domains 1 (Uhrf1) were significantly downregulated, whereas granulin precursor (Grn) and transcription factor AP-2 gamma (Tfap2c) were upregulated in hCG blastocysts (Supplementary Fig. 3G). Notably, Uhrf1 has been reported to interact with the G9a/GLP (EHMT2/EHMT1) complex to regulate DNA methylation maintenance31. We next focused on key epigenetic modifiers (Supplementary Data 9)34, among which Ehmt1 and TET methylcytosine dioxygenase 3 (Tet3) were highly abundant and exhibited significant changes in transcript levels in the hCG group (Fig. 6D). Ehmt1 expression was reduced by approximately 46% in hCG-exposed MII oocytes and PN3–PN4 zygotes compared with that in control oocytes, as determined by RT-qPCR, whereas Tet3 expression did not differ significantly between groups (Fig. 6E–G). Western blotting confirmed an approximately 40% reduction in EHMT1 protein levels in MII oocytes following hCG exposure (Fig. 6H).

Previous studies have shown that the loss of EHMT1 alters H3K9me1 and H3K9me2 levels and may influence DNA methylation establishment in oocytes35. Consistent with these reports, we observed that H3K9me1 and H3K9me2 levels in MII oocytes decreased in parallel with EHMT1 expression (Supplementary Fig. 5A, B). In addition, single-cell RNA sequencing data spanning nine sequential oocyte maturation stages showed that Ehmt1 expression was strongly positively correlated with Dnmt3a36 (r = 0.92, P < 1.00E-04) and Dnmt3l (r = 0.73, P < 5.00E-02) expression, suggesting a potential role in maintaining DNA methylation during oocyte maturation (Supplementary Fig. 6). These results indicate that the hCG trigger significantly reduces Ehmt1 expression in MII oocytes, which may in turn disrupt epigenetic patterns.

Similarly, the offspring from the BIX group exhibited even more severe neurobehavioural impairments, with reduced DI and lower T-maze accuracy across 3 days (Supplementary Fig. 8B, C), longer latencies during MWM training, and less time spent in the target zone (Supplementary Fig. 8D). Collectively, these results indicate that EHMT1 deficiency in zygotes, whether through RNA interference or pharmacological inhibition, consistently impaired neurogenesis in offspring, characterized by a reduction in the number of immature neurons in the dentate gyrus and suboptimal neurobehavioural performance.

Ehmt1 rescues memory deficits in hCG-IVF mouse offspring

Since Ehmt1 inhibition led to significant impairments in memory and spatial learning, we next investigated whether restoring Ehmt1 expression could alleviate these deficits in hCG-exposed offspring. Following the collection of oocytes from hCG-injected female mice, we performed IVF and subsequently injected the Ehmt1 mRNA into zygotes (Fig. 7F). Microinjection of the Ehmt1 mRNA significantly increased Ehmt1 expression in the embryos (Supplementary Fig. 7B). Compared with the hCG group, the Ehmt1 group exhibited a significantly higher DI in the NOR test (Fig. 7G–I), increased accuracy in the T-maze test from day 1 to day 3, decreased latency in the MWM test, with a statistically significant difference on day 6, and spent more time in the target quadrant in the spatial probe test. Immunofluorescence staining for DCX showed the recovery of the number of newborn neurons (Fig. 7J). These findings further reinforce the critical role of Ehmt1 expression in the neurodevelopment of hCG-exposed offspring.

Discussion

In the present study, we show that trigger day hCG administration in ART cycles is associated with reduced global DNA methylation levels in the peripheral blood of ART-conceived offspring, together with an increased risk of suboptimal neurodevelopmental outcomes. By integrating findings from a birth cohort with a controlled hCG-only mouse model, we further demonstrate that transient hCG exposure during oocyte maturation may disrupt early epigenetic establishment and implicate the epigenetic modifier EHMT1 as a potential key regulator linking early hormonal exposure to neurodevelopment.

Consistent with previous findings that ovulation stimulation can influence DNA methylation patterns and histone modifications24,37–40, our data demonstrate that increased hCG exposure is specifically associated with reduced DNA methylation in both human offspring and mouse oocytes. In mice, an hCG injection resulted in a significant reduction in DNA methylation levels in MII oocytes and zygotes, accompanied by decreased expression of EHMT1. EHMT1 is a key histone methyltransferase responsible for H3K9 methylation41 and plays an essential role in oocyte DNA methylation and chromatin organization34,35,42. In our study, siRNA-mediated knockdown or pharmacological inhibition of EHMT1 at the zygote stage recapitulated cognitive and learning impairments in offspring, whereas supplementation of the Ehmt1 mRNA in zygotes from hCG-triggered mice restored neurobehavioural performance and rescued newborn neuron generation. These findings underscore critical roles for EHMT1 in early neuronal differentiation and brain development, consistent with the findings of previous studies showing that EHMT1 mutations underlie Kleefstra syndrome and that prenatal intervention can ameliorate behavioral phenotypes43–46.

Although the human DNA methylation analyses in this study were conducted using peripheral blood, accumulating evidence suggests substantial concordance between blood and brain methylation at a subset of CpG sites (r ≈ 0.86–0.87)47–49. Consistent with this result, the top-ranked DMGs identified in our study were enriched for neurodevelopment-related biological pathways, supporting peripheral blood methylation as an accessible indicator of exposure-associated epigenetic variation.

In our birth cohort, a higher trigger-day hCG dosage was associated with suboptimal receptive communication development in offspring, whereas no adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes were observed at low-medium hCG exposure levels. This result suggests a potential dose-threshold effect, whereby exposure to low-medium hCG doses may support oocyte maturation within physiological tolerance levels, whereas excessive exposure may accelerate oocyte release and result in a relatively immature developmental state. These observations are consistent with the 2025 WHO Global Infertility Guideline50, which advocates individualized, step-up stimulation to avoid excessive gonadotropin exposure, including hCG usage during ART cycles. Moreover, our findings provide evidence that the appropriate control of trigger-day hCG treatment may contribute to improved outcomes for both mothers and offspring.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Although immunofluorescence-based detection of 5mC/5hmC has been widely used for decades as a general and intuitive approach to assess global DNA methylation dynamics in oocytes and embryos51–54, this method provides limited genomic resolution and cannot capture locus-specific methylation patterns. Accordingly, immunofluorescence findings should be regarded as supportive visual evidence rather than definitive results. Our methylation analyses in mouse blastocysts and the dDG focused on promoter-associated methylation, whereas the methylation of distal regulatory elements, particularly enhancers, warrants further systematic investigation. Although our results support that transient, nonphysiological hCG exposure during the narrow window of final oocyte maturation may compromise epigenetic stability, the mouse stimulation protocol does not fully mirror clinical ART regimens. Further studies are needed to validate the long-term neurodevelopmental effects of trigger-day hCG exposure in larger, independent cohorts and to elucidate mechanisms by which the hCG-associated perturbation of EHMT1 expression influences epigenetic regulation and contributes to neurodevelopmental phenotypes.

In conclusion, while ART protocols have traditionally prioritized pregnancy and live birth rates, our findings highlight the importance of considering potential long-term developmental consequences for offspring. By systematically evaluating the biological effects of trigger-day hCG exposure, we provide preliminary evidence that specific ART procedures may exert lasting downstream biological effects. These results advocate for a broader perspective in reproductive care that balances clinical success with long-term offspring health. Future longitudinal studies will be essential to validate these findings and to inform the refinement of ART protocols that optimize both clinical efficacy and long-term benefits for offspring health.

Methods

Ethics statement

All methods and protocols involving human subjects were approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of Nanjing Medical University, China NJMUIRB (2014) 248. Written informed consent was obtained from all participating families or the legal guardians of participating children for the collection and use of health-related information, biological samples and research data and for the publication of strictly de-identified research findings. Participants did not receive any financial compensation for participation in this study. All operations involving animal housing and handling were strictly conducted in accordance with the experimental protocol approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanjing Medical University (IACUC-2401045).

Study design and participants

We conducted the present study within the Jiangsu Birth Cohort (JBC) study, a prospective and longitudinal follow-up study cohort aiming to explore the disparities between NC and ART-conceived pregnancies in terms of perinatal outcomes and children’s health. ART-conceiving couples were recruited from reproductive medicine clinics before their first oocyte retrieval cycle. Spontaneously conceiving couples were recruited from obstetrics departments at 8–14 weeks of gestation when establishing antenatal health records55.

In the present study, discovery-stage participants were selected from the JBC and included families with live-born offspring conceived with or without ART between December 2014 and March 2018. After follow-up and sample quality control, 407 offspring from 365 families were included, comprising 202 ART-conceived and 205 NC children. These families corresponded to the cohort subset previously used for whole-genome sequencing analysis. WGBS was performed on offspring blood samples from the same families for discovery-stage DNA methylation analysis56. The discovery stage included 184 female and 223 male children, all evaluated at 1 year of age. For the neurodevelopmental assessment stage, a total of 1333 ART-conceived singleton children who had reached 3 years of age, from families with complete biological samples and comprehensive assessment data, were recruited between December 2014 and October 2022, including 84 ART-conceived singleton children from the discovery stage. Detailed baseline characteristics, including sex, age are summarized in Supplementary Data 10. Written informed consent was obtained from all participating families at recruitment. Participants did not receive financial compensation for participation in this study. Health-related information was collected using standardized, structured questionnaires. Clinical data and participant sex assigned at birth were obtained from medical records.

Fingertip blood samples were collected from the children during the 12-month follow-up clinic visits. Peripheral blood samples were collected from couples with NC during early pregnancy (8–14 weeks of gestation) at obstetric clinics, while blood samples from couples undergoing ART were obtained during the β-hCG test period.

Exposure

The critical-exposure ARTs, including infertility diagnoses and specific ART procedures, have been described in our previous study57. Briefly, controlled ovarian stimulation (COS) protocols, trigger strategies, fertilization methods, and embryo transfer cycles and stages were validated as ART procedures. COS protocols were categorized into four clinical approaches: antagonists, short agonists, long agonists, and microstimulation. Pharmacological support during COS involved gonadotropin (Gn), gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH), gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonist (GnRHa), luteinizing hormone (LH), growth hormone (GH) and human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG). Trigger strategies included single GnRHa, single hCG and dual triggers. In addition, participants were categorized by the trigger hCG dose into low-medium dose (≤6750 IU) or medium-high dose (>6750 IU) groups, regardless of whether GnRHa was coadministered.

Assessment of the neurodevelopment of children

In the neurodevelopmental assessment stage, a total of 1333 ART-conceived singleton children who completed the Bayley-III assessment between 36 and 42 months of age were included. The assessment was performed by standard-trained pediatricians or occupational therapists. Five domains were evaluated: cognition, receptive communication, expressive communication, fine motor skills, and gross motor skills. Each domain is categorized as “at-risk,” “emerging,” or “competent” based on scaled scores, with cutoffs of ≤9, ≤6, ≤4, ≤8, ≤10 for “at risk” and ≥14, ≥10, ≥9, ≥11, ≥13 for “competent,” respectively; scores between these thresholds are classified as “emerging.”58 Given the low prevalence of “at-risk” children, the “at-risk” and “emerging” categories were combined into the “suboptimal” category for subsequent analyses.

Animal maintenance

Female ICR mice were obtained from the Animal Core Facility of Nanjing Medical University. Mice were housed under standard specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions in ventilated cages at 22 °C with controlled humidity, under a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle (lights on at 08:00 and off at 20:00). They were fed a commercial rodent diet meeting national laboratory animal nutrition standards, with sterilized feed (cobalt-60 gamma irradiation) and purified water available ad libitum. Sterile vasectomized adult male mice were housed individually. Before behavioral testing, offspring mice were group-housed (5 mice per cage) for a 1-week acclimation period under standard laboratory conditions. For euthanasia, mice not used for tissue collection were euthanized by gradual-fill CO2 inhalation, and death was confirmed by cessation of respiration and heartbeat and loss of reflexes before carcass disposal. All euthanasia procedures were performed in accordance with institutional animal welfare guidelines.

Mouse oocyte and blastocyst collection

Female mice (9 weeks old) were evaluated for oestrous cyclicity between 5:00 and 6:00 p.m., and mice in oestrous were selected using a previously described method59. We administered a dose of hCG (7.5 IU) via intraperitoneal injection to female mice in natural oestrus to induce ovulation without PMSG pretreatment. In the control group, females in oestrus were housed individually with a sterile vasectomized male mouse. The next morning, from 8:00 a.m. to 9:00 a.m., the mice with vaginal plugs or at 14 h after hCG injection were euthanized by cervical dislocation. Cumulus–oocyte complexes (COCs) were subsequently collected from the oviductal ampullae. MII oocytes were then isolated by removing the cumulus mass using medium supplemented with 0.5 mg/ml hyaluronidase at 37 °C. For the collection of GV oocytes, female mice in the oestrous phase received an intraperitoneal injection of 7.5 IU of hCG. At 3 or 6 h post-injection, the ovaries were meticulously dissected in M2 medium (Sigma‒Aldrich) to isolate the GV oocytes. For the control group, GV oocytes were harvested from mice in oestrous that had not been treated with hCG using the same procedure. The blastocysts were collected at embryonic day 3.5 (E3.5) by flushing the uterine horns with a 30‑gauge Hamilton blunt needle attached to a 1‑mL syringe preloaded with collection medium. Approximately 1 mL of medium was gently infused through each uterine horn to expel the embryos and uterine fluid into a clean Petri dish containing pre‑warmed PBS. Blastocysts were identified and picked up using a pulled glass capillary pipette and then washed three times in PBS drops to remove residual uterine debris.

In vitro fertilization and transfer of mouse embryos

Female COCs were obtained from the oviductal ampullae of each group as described above. Male gamete donors were euthanized at 8 weeks of age. Spermatozoa from the epididymides were extracted and subsequently capacitated in tyrosine hydroxylase (TYH) medium for 1 h. The sperm and the COCs were cocultured for 4–6 h. Subsequently, presumptive zygotes were picked and washed three times using HTF medium to remove cumulus cells and excess sperm. Embryos at the 2-cell stage were transferred into the oviducts of ICR surrogate female mice at a ratio of 15–20 embryos per mouse. The embryos of each group developed in the uterus until they were born. The mice from each group were maintained and raised to 8 weeks for analysis.

Mouse brain tissue sampling and preparation

Eight-week-old mice were anaesthetized by an intraperitoneal injection of tribromoethanol (350 mg/kg). Following the administration of anesthesia, the mice were first perfused with 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) to remove blood and interstitial fluids from the tissues. For mice designated for dorsal dentate gyrus (dDG) DNA/RNA extraction, the mice were then rapidly decapitated. The dDG region was carefully dissected from the brain using sterile instruments, immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until further processing. For mice designated for brain tissue cryosectioning, after PBS perfusion, the mice were further perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS for an additional 10 min to fix the brain tissues. The postfixation process lasted for 6 h at 4 °C to ensure optimal fixation of the neural tissue. Afterwards, the brains were transferred to a 30% sucrose solution in PBS for dehydration. Finally, the brain samples were frozen in a cryosectioner (SM2010R, Leica, Germany) and cut to obtain 30 μm coronal slices. These brain slices were stored in cryoprotectant in the freezer before use.

Whole-genome methylation sequencing of blood DNA

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood or fingertip blood samples using a QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, 51306) and quantified with a Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA was fragmented to an average size of approximately 350 bp, followed by end repair, A-tailing and ligation with methylated adapters using a KAPA HyperPlus Library Preparation Kit (Roche, KK8514). After adapter ligation, the DNA was subjected to bisulfite treatment using a DNA Methylation-Lightning Kit (Zymo, Cat. No. D5031). The bisulfite-converted DNA was then amplified using KAPA HiFi Uracil + enzyme (Roche, Cat. No. KK2802). The PCR products were subsequently purified using AMPure XP beads at a 0.8:1 ratio (Beckman) to obtain the final WGBS library. The quality and size distribution of the libraries were assessed on an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and quantified using a Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). High-throughput sequencing of 150 bp paired-end reads was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) to generate an average of 153 million read pairs (~10× coverage) per sample.

The quality of the raw sequencing reads was checked using FastQC (v0.11.2). Low-quality reads and adapters were trimmed using Trimmomatic (v0.32) software. The bisulfite-converted reads were then aligned to the human assembly GRCh37 using Bismark (v0.22.3). For each sample, comprehensive quality control reports were evaluated, and the bisulfite conversion rates for phage lambda DNA exceeded 99%. CpG sites captured at less than 3× were excluded from further analysis. Furthermore, CpG sites overlapping common SNPs (GRCh37p13.b150) were excluded to prevent confounding effects from genetic variants on methylation evaluations, effectively filtering out approximately 4% of the CpG sites. The global methylation level was calculated as the ratio of the number of reads supporting methylated cytosines to the total number of reads (both methylated and unmethylated).

DMR calling and enrichment

Autosomal CpG sites (read coverage ≥3) were included in the subsequent analysis, and differentially methylated regions (DMRs) were identified using the DSS R package (v2.34.0). DMRs were defined as regions with at least 3 CpG sites, differences in methylation >10%, and P < 1 × 10E-5. DMRs were annotated using Annotatr (v.1.36.0). A Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was conducted to explore the biological functions and important pathways.

Single-cell bisulfite sequencing (scBS-seq) of oocytes and blastocysts

Library preparation was performed using the scBS-seq protocol60, with some modifications. For bisulfite conversion, an EZ DNA Methylation-Direct Kit (Zymo Research; D5020) was used. After the zona pellucida was removed, we collected 16 pools (8 from the control group, 8 from the hCG group) of 10 oocytes per group and 16 pools (8 from the control group, 8 from the hCG group) of 10 blastocysts in a single 200 μl tube containing 2.5 μl of Buffer RLT (QIAGEN; 79216). The samples were stored at −80 °C. After the samples were thawed, 7.5 μl of nuclease-free water and 65 μl of CT conversion reagent (EZ DNA Methylation-Direct Kit; Zymo D5020) were added, and the samples were incubated in a PCR machine (98 °C for 8 min and 65 °C for 180 min). DNA was purified using MagBinding Beads (Zymo EZ Methylation Direct MagPrep Kit, Zymo, D5044). DNA bound to the MagBinding Beads was washed with M-wash buffer once 100 μl of M-desulfonation buffer was applied to the beads, and the samples were incubated at room temperature for 15 min to complete the CT conversion. The DNA bound to the beads was washed twice and then eluted with the DNA strand synthesis kit with 1x Blue buffer (Enzymatics), 600 nM dNTPs, and 400 nM P5-N6-oligo1 (CTACACGACGCTCTTCCGATCTNNNNNN). The DNA mixture was denatured at 65 °C for 3 min and quickly chilled. Then, 1 μl of Klenow fragment (3′–5′ exo) (Enzymatics; P7010-LC-L) was added, and the strand synthesis reaction was performed using a PCR machine with the following program (4 °C for 5 min, 4 °C rising to 37 °C with increasing 0.1 °C every 1 s, 37 °C for 30 min, 4 °C pause). Afterwards, the remaining oligos and dNTPs were deactivated using an Exo-CIP™ Rapid PCR Cleanup Kit (NEB, E1050S) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Next, dC tailing was conducted in the presence of 1x Blue buffer (Enzymatics), 2.5 mM dCTPs and 1 U/μL TdT enzyme. Then, second-strand synthesis was performed by tagging P7-G6-oligo2 (AGACGTGTGCTCTTCCGATCTGGGGGGHN) in the presence of 1× Blue buffer (Enzymatics), 600 nM dNTPs, 400 nM P7-G6-oligo2 and 100 units of Klenow exo- (Enzymatics) and incubating the mixture at 37 °C for 1 h, followed by purification with Agencourt AMPure XP beads (Beckman). The final libraries were generated by 18 cycles of PCR to incorporate universal primers and index primers and were subsequently purified using Agencourt AMPure XP beads. The libraries were pooled, checked and sequenced with 150-bp paired-end reads on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.

The quality of the data was assessed using FastQC (v0.11.2), and adapters were trimmed using Trimmomatic (v0.32) with the default parameters. Reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome (mm10) using Bismark (v0.22.3) with appropriate single-cell parameters. Since oocytes have relatively high levels of non-CpG methylation4,61, samples with non-CpG (CHG, CHH) methylation (≥8%) and outliers were removed. The differential methylation analysis was performed using MethSCAn (v1.1.0)62 with default parameters on the filtered methylation calls to identify DMRs between groups, with downstream annotation and enrichment analyses conducted as described above.

Reduced-representative bisulfide sequencing (RRBS)

For RRBS, each DNA sample was extracted from dDG tissue spiked with 0.5% lambda DNA (Thermo Fisher Scientific, SD0021) and digested with the methylation-insensitive restriction enzyme MspI (NEB, M7634S). The remaining library construction, library quality control, and sequencing methods were the same as those used in the WGBS process. Trimmomatic (v0.32) software and FastQC (v0.11.2) were used to obtain quality-controlled reads, which were subsequently mapped to the reference genome by Bismark (v0.22.3) with GRCm38 (mm10) as the reference genome. Genomic annotation was performed using Annotatr (v1.36.0). On average, sequencing produced 71 million reads per sample, with conversion rates greater than 99%. CpG sites with a depth of 10 or greater were retained for analysis. The regional DNA methylation evaluation is similar to the global DNA methylation calculation, where the methylated cytosines and total reads are restricted to the target regions.

Bulk RNA-Seq and gene enrichment analyses

Total RNA was extracted from dDG tissue samples using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen, Cat No. 74134) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The quantity and quality of the extracted RNA were assessed using a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, respectively. RNA integrity number (RIN) values greater than 7.0 were considered acceptable for sequencing. Sequencing libraries were constructed using the VAHTS Universal V8 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Vazyme, NR605) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Alignment to the Ensembl reference genome mm10 was performed using Tophat and Bowtie2. The aligned files were sorted using SAMtools (v.1.19.1). Count tables were generated using HTSeq (v0.12.4). The differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (v1.20.0) in R. A GO enrichment analysis was conducted to explore the biological functions and important pathways.

Single-oocyte and single-blastocyst RNA-Seq

After the oocytes and blastocysts were washed three times with 0.1% BSA/PBS, each sample was then transferred into 0.2 ml PCR tubes containing 2 μl of lysis buffer. Transcriptome libraries were prepared according to the Smart-seq2 protocol63. Sequencing libraries were constructed using a KAPA HyperPlus Library Preparation Kit (Roche, KK8514) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All the libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with 150-bp paired-end reads. The quality control metrics of the RNA-seq data were checked using FastQC (v0.11.9). Based on the results, we used Trimmomatic (v0.32) to trim adapters from the reads and remove low-quality bases from the data, with an argument length ≥ 25, a stringency = 3 and a quality = 25. Then the trimmed reads were mapped with STAR (v2.7.6a) to the mouse reference genome (mm10) and its corresponding GENCODE gene annotation (VM20). In this process, we also added information on the ERCC spike-in to the reference. Afterwards, HTSeq (v0.12.4) was used to count the reads aligned to each gene for the quantification of gene expression. In parallel, we also used RSEM (Li and Dewey, 2011) (v1.3.1) to align and calculate the expression at both the gene level and the transcript level. The differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (1.20.0) in R.

Immunofluorescence staining

Immunofluorescence was performed as previously described64,65. The zona pellucida of zygotes and oocytes was removed by an incubation in acidic Tyrode’s solution. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, treated with 4 M HCl for 10 min, and subsequently neutralized in Tris-HCl buffer at pH 8.0 for 10 min. Permeabilization was achieved by incubating the cells in a 0.5% Triton X-100 solution for 10 min. After a 2-h incubation at room temperature in a blocking solution of 2% BSA with 0.1% Triton X-100, the samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a rabbit polyclonal anti-5hmC antibody (1:500; Active Motif, 39092), a mouse monoclonal anti-5mC antibody (1:500; Calbiochem, NA81), anti-Histone H3 (di methyl K9) (1:200; abcam, ab1220) and anti-Histone H3 (mono methyl K9) (1:200; abcam, ab9045). After being washed three times with a PBS solution containing 0.1% Triton X-100, the cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit (1:1000; Jackson ImmunoResearch, 711-587-003) and Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated donkey anti-mouse (1:1000; Jackson ImmunoResearch, 715-547-003) secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. The samples were then mounted on glass slides with antifade medium (Beyotime) and examined under a confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM900, Zeiss). Imaging was performed using uniform laser and exposure settings on the confocal microscope.

For the quantification of 5mC and 5hmC levels, the equatorial plane of each pronucleus, which was identified by its largest cross-sectional area, was chosen for analysis. The integrated fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ (v1.54) with background subtraction and normalized to the DAPI signal per oocyte to control for imaging variability.

Multiplex immunofluorescence staining

A tyramide amplification system (Biotium, USA) was used for multiplex immunofluorescence staining. Briefly, the sections were deparaffinized by immersion in xylene for 15 min, followed by rehydration through a series of descending ethanol concentrations (100, 90, 80, and 70%) for 5 min each. The sections were then rinsed with PBS. Antigen retrieval was achieved by boiling the sections in 10 mM citrate buffer (pH 6; in a microwave for 15 min). The blocking procedure was similar to that used for the frozen sections and involved an incubation with a blocking solution containing 5% normal goat serum and 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. The sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with an anti-IBA1 antibody (1:100; Servicebio, GB153502). The sections were then washed 3 times for 5 min each with PBS. The samples were incubated with 5 μg/mL HRP-conjugated secondary antibody in blocking buffer at room temperature for 1 h. Working amplification buffer was prepared by adding hydrogen peroxide to the tyramide amplification buffer according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The tyramide conjugate was diluted in working amplification buffer, and the samples were incubated with amplification buffer containing the tyramide conjugate for 10 min at room temperature. A peroxidase quenching step was performed after each round of labeling to inactivate the secondary antibody from the previous labelling step. The steps described above were repeated until all antibodies were added (anti-TBR1, 1:200, Abcam, ab31940; anti-NeuN, 1:200, Proteintech, 26975-1-AP; anti-PROX1, 1:200, Abcam, ab199359; anti-DCX, 1:200, Abcam, ab207175). The samples were mounted with mounting medium, and fluorescence was detected. The samples were then mounted on glass slides with antifade medium (Beyotime) and examined under a laser-scanning confocal microscope (LSM900, Zeiss). Imaging was performed using consistent settings on the confocal microscope. Images were subsequently processed with QuPath (v0.5.1) for cell identification and exported for visualization.

BIX-01294 treatment

The fertilized zygotes were washed and cultured in CZB medium supplemented with 5 μM BIX-01294 until they reached the two-cell stage. They were then transferred into recipient female mice to support further development. Control embryos were cultured in CZB medium without BIX-01294.

Injection of the siRNA or mRNA

The concentrations of nonspecific commercial siRNA mixtures (GCACUUCCUCAGACACCAATT, GGAUAGUAGGACUUCUAAATT) were diluted to 2 μM for microinjection. The siRNAs were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). For mRNA preparation, plasmid templates were synthesized by Genebay Biotech (China), and mRNA was generated by in vitro transcription using a Thermo Fisher Scientific in vitro transcription kit (Cat# AM1334) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, then diluted to 2 μM for microinjection. For microinjection, zygotes were transferred to a 20 μL drop of M2 medium (Sigma Aldrich) overlaid with mineral oil in a 60 mm dish at 37 °C on an inverted microscope equipped with a heated stage. Five microlitres of the siRNA or mRNA mixture was loaded into a microinjection needle with a 30° angle and a tip with a 4.3–4.9 μm inner diameter (TW100F-6, WORLD PRECISION INSTRUMENTS). A FemtoJet pressure injection system (Eppendorf) was used to inject the sample for 0.2 s at a pressure of 120 hpa. After the injection, the cells were cultured for 16 h in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 at 37 °C. Control zygotes were injected with an equal volume of mutant mRNA under the same conditions. Zygotes were washed with PBS, individually collected into RNase-free tubes containing 50 μL of RNA lysis buffer, and stored at −80 °C.

Western blot

Western blot analyses of GV and MII oocytes were performed using standard procedures. Briefly, 100 GV or MII oocytes were incubated in 5× SDS sample buffer supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail at 95 °C for 5 min. Protein lysate samples were loaded on 8% SDS-PAGE gels, electrophoretically separated, and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were then blocked for 2 h with 5% BSA and subsequently incubated overnight at 4 °C with a mouse anti-Ehmt1 antibody (1:1000; Abcam, ab41969) or rabbit anti-tubulin antibody (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, 2144S). After being washed with PBST, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:10,000; Beyotime, A0208/A0216) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were detected using Pierce ECL Western blotting substrate (Vazyme, E423). Tubulin was used as the loading control.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Single oocytes were picked and transferred to lysis buffer by mouth pipetting, after which 0.1 μl of ERCC spike-in RNA (1:100,000 dilution; Life Technologies, 4456740) was added to the lysis buffer. cDNA synthesis and amplification were performed according to the Smart-seq2 protocol23. Real-time PCR was performed using 2× ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Q331) with a QuantStudio™ 7 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). ERCC0133 was used as an internal control for every sample. Primers were custom synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). The experiments were conducted in duplicate. The relative mRNA abundance was calculated using the ∆∆Ct method. The primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 3.

Novel object recognition test

The novel object recognition test (NOR) is based on the observation that mice spontaneously tend to spend more time exploring novel objects than familiar objects66. During the initial 2-day habituation phase, the mice were placed in the center of a box (40 cm × 40 cm × 40 cm) and allowed to explore the open field for 10 min. On the first day of the training trial, the mice were allowed to explore two identical wooden objects, A and B, placed in the arena. Each mouse was given 5 min to explore the arena. On the second day, object B was replaced with a new object C, which had a similar texture but a different shape. The testing order was randomized, but we always tested animals from the same row of cages. Between trials, we wiped the testing arena and objects with 70% ethanol to remove traces of urine or faeces from the mice in the same cage. Prior to testing mice from different cages, the testing arena and objects were thoroughly cleaned first with water and then with 70% ethanol to prevent scent recognition. Exploration of the objects was defined as nose-directed sniffing or contact with the nose, forepaws, or whiskers. The number of times the mice explored the novel object (Tnew) and the familiar object (Told) were analysed using SMART 3.0 software (Panlab, Spain). The level of working memory was calculated by the discrimination index (DI) as follows: DI = (Tnew − Told)/(Tnew + Told).

Morris water maze test

The Morris water maze (MWM) was used to assess spatial working memory based on previously established protocols67–69. The mice were trained in an open circular pool (63031, RWD, China) filled with water to locate a submerged circular platform (diameter: 9 cm) using various spatial cues. The pool was filled with tap water mixed with nontoxic black paint, and the water temperature was maintained at 22 °C ± 1 °C throughout the experiment. For data collection, the pool was divided into four virtual quadrants (I–IV) based on the location of the platform: right (I), opposite (II), left (III) and target (IV). Each mouse underwent 1 day of cued training followed by 5 days of acquisition training, culminating in a final probe trial. In the cued training phase, a triangular flag was placed on the platform to provide a visual cue. During acquisition training, no additional distal cues were presented. Mice were introduced into the water near the pool edge, facing the wall. Each mouse was allotted 1 min to locate the submerged platform, which was positioned in the upper third of the target quadrant. If a mouse failed to find the platform within this time frame, it was gently guided to it. Each mouse was required to remain on the platform for 10 s before being removed from the pool. On the final day of training, the hidden platforms were removed, and the mice were placed in the opposite quadrant (II). The time spent in the target quadrant was recorded. Data from mice that floated or jumped from the platform were excluded from the MWM analysis. The results from each training session were automatically recorded by SMART 3.0 software, which averaged the daily training data for statistical analysis.

T-maze test

We performed T-maze training using previously established protocols to assess spatial working memory in mice67–69. All mice were restricted from food to maintain their body weight at 85% of normal, followed by food habituation, pretraining, and T-maze training. Each mouse underwent four sessions of food (condensed milk) habituation. The mice were placed in a cage similar to their home cage but without bedding and containing condensed milk. The mice remained in the cage until all the condensed milk was consumed or until a maximum of 1 h had passed. After habituation to the food reinforcer, the mice received five pretraining sessions daily to adapt them to the T-maze and find food in the food wells of the two choice arms (the baiting sequence in the two choice arms was pseudorandomized). This process was followed by 5 days of pretraining, with five trials per day. Afterwards, the animals were tested in a delayed matching-to-location task. Each training session consisted of four trials, each divided into two phases. In the first phase, the experimenter placed a drop of condensed milk in the food well of one open arm and closed the other arm. During the sample run, the experimental mouse was placed in the starting area, allowed access only to the open arm, and remained there for 10 s while eating the food. In the second phase, the experimenter placed a new reward in the other arm. The mouse was then placed back in the apparatus and allowed to choose freely between the two arms of the T-maze. During the choice process, the animal was considered to have chosen an arm when it extended its hind foot into that arm; backing out was not allowed. If the animal chose the arm with food, it was allowed to eat the reward and was then returned to the cage. Otherwise, the mouse was restrained in that arm for approximately 10 s and then returned to the cage. The mice were tested in pairs, with each mouse performing one trial in turn, and the interval between trials was approximately 2 min. Between trials of two consecutive mice, the maze was wiped with 70% ethanol. Each animal received four trials per day, and each trial consisted of a pseudorandomized sequence of correct choices between the two arms. The behavioral results were analysed using SMART 3.0 software, which provided statistics on the number and percentage of correct responses, and the working memory level of the mice was assessed by the number of correct responses.

Statistics and reproducibility

For the cohort study, the JBC recruited both ART-conceived and NC families informed by our previous cohort study from pre-specified hospitals. Eligible ART participants were Chinese residents, excluding those using sperm or oocyte donation or intrauterine insemination, while NC families were recruited during early pregnancy (8–14 weeks) at the same hospitals. Families were invited annually using a convenience sampling approach, with 1000 ART and 2000 NC families invited each year55. In the discovery stage, WGBS was performed on offspring blood samples from the previous study56 used for whole-genome sequencing, and no samples were excluded. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. Considering the twins in the cohort, a linear mixed model was performed to evaluate the association of critical variables with the global DNA methylation level of offspring using the nlme (3.1.155) and MASS (7.3.55) R packages. Baseline characteristics, including parental age, BMI, lifestyle factors, maternal complications (gestational diabetes mellitus), pregnancy status, conception type, and offspring characteristics (number, sex, and birth weight), were included in the analyses of the associations with DNA methylation levels in offspring.

Global DNA methylation levels are reported as proportions (0–1) in models with categorical variables and as percentages (0–100) in models with continuous variables to facilitate the interpretation of effect sizes without affecting statistical inference. Analyses were adjusted for covariates such as the methylation mapping rate, parental methylation levels, gestational age at delivery (continuous), birth status (categorical, full-term or preterm), maternal BMI (continuous, pre-pregnancy), offspring birth weight (continuous), offspring number (categorical, singleton, or twins) and offspring sex (categorical, boy, or girl). Additionally, among ART-conceived singletons, logistic regression models adjusted for the aforementioned covariates were used to evaluate the associations of hCG use and DNA methylation level with suboptimal neurodevelopment. Notably, a two-sided P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

For the behavioral tests, immunofluorescence staining, Western blot analysis, and RT-qPCR analysis, all the results were obtained from at least three independent experiments and are presented as the means ± SEMs. Animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups where applicable. Behavioral assessments were performed by investigators blinded to group allocation. All behavioral and tissue-section experiments were conducted in male offspring, as no significant sex differences were detected. Data normality tests were performed using the Shapiro–Wilk normality test. Statistical analyses were performed using Student’s t test (between two groups) or one-way ANOVA. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. The data were analysed using GraphPad Prism 9 and R software (v4.4.1).
