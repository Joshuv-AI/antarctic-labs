Blunt pericardial rupture and cardiac herniation: always consider the need to put things back in their place
Authors: Francisco de Salles Collet e Silva, David T Efron, Jose Gustavo Parreira
Published: 2026

The article “A place for everything…and everything in its place?” 1 was a case presented and discussed at an online Virtual International Case Conference. This meeting takes place once a month and is overseen online by the Panamerican Trauma Society, the AAST International Relations Committee, the University of Miami, and the International Trauma System Development Program at Virginia Commonwealth University.

We read with interest the article by Marchini Reitz et al1 describing a challenging case of traumatic pericardial rupture (TPR) with cardiac herniation following blunt chest trauma. The authors highlight the diagnostic pitfalls and surgical management in an unstable blunt trauma patient, emphasizing the role of imaging in differentiating from dextrocardia.

Traumatic pericardial tears usually have delayed diagnosis and carry high mortality (64%). Pericardial ruptures of 8 to 12 cm may cause a cardiac herniation, resulting in clinical signs mimicking cardiac tamponade identified during the primary survey. As early surgery is essential for successful treatment, a high index of suspicion is invaluable for a timely diagnosis of cardiac herniation in the management of patients with thoracic blunt trauma.24

Early diagnostic criteria for TPR rely on a high index of suspicion in blunt trauma patients presenting with shock (hypotensive or obstructive). Clinical signs include diminished breath sounds, subcutaneous emphysema, and equivocal pericardial views on extended focused assessment with sonography in trauma (e-FAST), though these remain nonspecific. Electrocardiograms may show nonspecific changes, but chest radiography can reveal pneumopericardium, displaced cardiac silhouette, or “boot-shaped” heart. CT is the gold standard for preoperative confirmation, demonstrating pericardial defects with herniation, or great vessel torsion, with sensitivity >90% in stable patients. However, in unstable scenarios like the case described, delays must be minimized; transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) intraoperatively can identify intracardiac injuries missed on initial imaging.3

Late diagnostic criteria emerge in survivors (up to 20% of cases), often days to weeks post-injury, due to delayed herniation.4 5 Symptoms include chest pain, dyspnea, or arrhythmias from cardiac strangulation. Imaging reveals progressive effusion or herniation on follow-up CT/MRI, with TEE aiding valve/septal assessment. Mortality remains high (57%–64%) if unrecognized, underscoring serial monitoring in high-risk trauma.

Surgically, urgent exploration via anterolateral thoracotomy (extendable to clamshell, as performed) is preferred for instability, allowing hematoma evacuation, heart repositioning, and repair. Simple suturing (eg, 2–0 polypropylene) suffices for small tears, but large defects (>50% circumference, as in your case) warrant tension-free patching with fenestrated bovine pericardium or Gore-Tex to prevent effusion. This case reinforces TPR’s rarity (0.4% in blunt trauma) and lethality, same times integrated imaging and multidisciplinary approaches.610

This case report is very important because it shows a case of a reversible cause of cardiac arrest in a patient with blunt trauma. It draws attention to the performance of a resuscitative thoracotomy in a patient with blunt trauma who arrives conscious and presents with sudden cardiac arrest during the initial assessment.
