 ABSTRACT

The Advanced Materials and Manufacturing Technologies (AMMT) program is investigating two Ni-
based alloys for nuclear applications: alloy 625, selected for its acceptable high-temperature properties
and availability in various forms, and Haynes 244, a promising low-Cr, high-strength alloy for molten salt
applications. Both alloys were fabricated by laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) and characterized for
microstructure and mechanical performance. Low-defect LPBF 625 was creep tested at 650°C and 725°C
and exhibited properties approaching those of wrought 625, though creep ductility perpendicular to the
build direction was reduced because of the as-printed elongated grain structure. A recrystallization heat
treatment (1 h at 1,150°C) partially mitigated this anisotropy, although intergranular cracking persisted at
grain boundaries oriented at low angles to the applied stress. Interrupted creep testing in vacuum at
725°C/200 MPa on a mirror-finish specimen showed that cavitation initiated preferentially between grain
boundary precipitates and at precipitate-free boundaries. X-ray computed tomography quantified cavity
growth rates during long-term creep testing of standard LPBF 625 specimens. Crack-free LPBF 244,
produced after optimization of the printing parameters, demonstrated higher strength but lower ductility at
750°C relative to LPBF 625, with similar build-direction-dependent anisotropy in the as-printed state. The
same recrystallization treatment improved room-temperature tensile ductility in both orientations, but
ductility at 750°C after annealing and aging remained limited. These results establish a baseline
understanding of creep and deformation behavior in LPBF Ni-based alloys and identify heat-treatment
optimization and grain boundary precipitate control as priorities for improving high-temperature ductility
ahead of nuclear deployment.

 1. INTRODUCTION

To achieve the US goal of energy abundance and AI dominance [1], a nuclear renaissance is underway
[2]. The US Department of Energy, Office of Nuclear Energy’s Advanced Materials and Manufacturing
Technologies (AMMT) program [3] is instrumental in alleviating the fabrication bottleneck that new
power plants face in adopting novel materials and manufacturing methods [4]. The lengthy qualification
process, in particular, significantly limits the number of materials and techniques that can be used for the
reactor core. Although the AMMT program has used laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) 316H as a proof of
concept for the accelerated qualification of new processes [5–8], higher-performance materials such as
Ni-based alloys are needed to enable higher-efficiency components and profitable Generation IV nuclear
reactors [9, 10].

Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) and Idaho National Laboratory (INL) have investigated the
fabrication and use of several Ni-based alloys and downselected three alloys of significant interest for
nuclear components to be fabricated by additive manufacturing (AM) using LPBF or laser powder
directed energy deposition (LP-DED) [11–16]. Alloy 625, an extensively studied low-Co printable alloy,
is produced by LPBF with satisfactory high-temperature mechanical stability and corrosion resistance in a
broad range of temperatures and environments [17–28]. The growing interest in wrought 625 has also led
the AMMT program to collaborate with industrial partners on a “community” code case [29]. Haynes
282, the second alloy of interest [30, 31], is a high-temperature, high-strength gamma-prime alloy
commercially available in powder form, with a growing body of data on its behavior when processed via
LPBF [32–37]. The final alloy, Haynes 244, is a high-strength, low-Cr, low-thermal-expansion alloy [38,
39] that shows promise for molten salt reactor applications because of its potential for excellent corrosion
resistance in molten salt at high temperatures [40].

Work on LPBF 625 in FY26 focused on generating long-term creep data and performing an in-depth
analysis of the creep damage mechanisms. X-ray computed tomography (XCT) measurements were
conducted on interrupted creep tests to assess the evolution of creep cavity formation. Furthermore,

 1

unique experiments were conducted in vacuum on flat specimens to establish a correlation between grain
boundary (GB) cavitation and microstructure evolution. For the LPBF 244 alloy, printing parameters
were optimized to produce crack-free material. Initial tensile data showed superior tensile behavior at
750°C for LPBF 244 compared with LPBF 625, and optimization of the heat treatment to further improve
the alloy’s mechanical properties will be discussed.

 2. LPBF 625 MECHANICAL PERFORMANCE

2.1 MICROSTRUCTURE AND TENSILE BEHAVIOR

The 625 powder and alloy chemistries are given in Table 1. A description of the LPBF 625 microstructure
in the as-printed and annealed conditions has been provided in previous reports [14, 15], and a summary
of key microstructural features is presented here for the as-printed and annealed alloy. The
recrystallization heat treatment (1 h at 1,150°C) was selected based on literature reviews of stress-relief
[20–22] and solution-annealing heat treatments [18, 20, 23].

Inverse pole figure (IPF) maps, taken with electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD), of the alloy in the as-
printed and 1 h at 1,150°C annealed conditions, along and perpendicular to the build direction (BD), are
presented in Figure 1a to Figure 1d. The as-printed material exhibited the typical LPBF grain structure,
with elongated grains along the BD, moderate texture [14, 15], and an average grain diameter of 15.2 ± 22
µm. Annealing for 1 h at 1,150°C resulted in more equiaxed grains and had no significant effect on the
average grain size (14 µm) but reduced the standard deviation to ±16 µm. The kernel average
misorientation and grain orientation spread maps presented in [14] revealed a significant decrease in
overall dislocation density and strain within the grains after 1 h at 1,150°C, consistent with the observed
recrystallization. A comparison with wrought 625 Grade 1 was performed, and the alloy chemistry and
grain structure are presented in Table 1 and Figure 1f, respectively. An equiaxed grain structure was
observed for the wrought 625, with an average grain size of 8 ± 7 µm. The relationship between aspect
ratio and equivalent grain diameter is shown in Figure 2a for the as-printed LPBF 625, annealed LPBF
625, and wrought 625. Although the largest grain in the wrought alloy was approximately 60 µm in
diameter, numerous grains measuring 60–150 µm were observed in the as-printed and annealed LPBF
625. A key difference between the as-printed and annealed LPBF 625 was the higher grain aspect ratio
(low values) in the as-printed material, consistent with the elongated grain structure shown in Figure 1a.
The misorientation angle as a function of grain area fraction is shown in Figure 2b, revealing no
significant differences between the annealed LPBF 625 and wrought 625.

The tensile properties at 725°C for the as-printed and annealed LPBF 625 are compared with those of
wrought 625 (Figure 1f). High yield strength (YS) and ultimate tensile strength (UTS) were observed for
the as-printed material along and perpendicular to the BD, owing to the high dislocation density in the
LPBF alloy [41, 42]. Significantly lower ductility was observed perpendicular to the BD, likely because
of the elongated grain structure, as discussed later. Annealing resulted in lower YS and UTS because of
dislocation recovery and led to similar tensile properties along and perpendicular to the BD. The tensile
curve for the annealed LPBF 625 was similar to that generated for wrought 625, except for the higher
strain to rupture of the wrought alloy. The effect of the 1 h at 1,150°C annealing treatment on the room-
temperature tensile properties of LPBF 625 was similar, and those data are reported elsewhere [14–15].

 3

Annealing also resulted in dynamic strain aging, as observed for the wrought alloy and discussed by
Gwak et al. [43] for LP-DED 625.

Optical micrographs of the specimens fractured during tensile testing at 725°C are shown in Figure 3. As
expected, the as-printed specimen tested along the BD exhibited higher strain than the specimen tested
perpendicular to the BD and contained a higher density of small cracks. These small cracks were
elongated along the stress direction for the specimen machined along the BD and were oriented at 45° or
90° to the stress direction for the specimen machined perpendicular to the BD. After annealing, the
fracture surfaces appeared similar for specimens tested along and perpendicular to the BD, with zigzag
cracks likely propagating along GBs.

2.2 LPBF 625 CREEP RESULTS

Creep testing was conducted using both small dog-bone specimens with a 2 × 2 mm gage section and
standard cylindrical specimens with a 6.35 mm gage diameter. The creep curves at 725°C and 650°C are
displayed in Figure 4a and Figure 4b, respectively. At 725°C, as observed for the tensile properties, the
as-printed specimen exhibited superior ductility along the BD compared with that of the specimen
machined perpendicular to the BD, resulting in a specimen lifetime decrease by nearly 50%. After
annealing, similar creep curves were observed for specimens tested along and perpendicular to the BD,
with strains to rupture between those measured for the as-printed specimens. Because of the low strain to
rupture measured for the as-printed LPBF 625 perpendicular to the BD, testing at 650°C focused on the
annealed material. The creep curves in Figure 4b highlight similar creep behavior at 650°C along and
perpendicular to the BD, with no significant difference between the dog-bone and standard specimens.
Although these curves show strains to rupture ranging from 5% to 8%, significant plastic deformation
occurred during loading at 650°C, resulting in actual strains to rupture of 16% to 20%.

In addition to the results shown in Figure 4, 725°C creep data with test durations of up to 5,000 h were

significantly increase the applicability of these alloys in nuclear reactors but requires an in-depth
understanding of creep damage mechanisms.

2.3 MICROSTRUCTURE CHARACTERIZATION AND CREEP DAMAGE

Cross-sectional optical micrographs of the as-printed and annealed specimens creep tested at 725°C are
shown in Figure 6. A significant difference was observed between the as-printed specimens tested along
and perpendicular to the BD, with very few cracks observed in the specimen tested perpendicular to the
BD. Along the BD, cracks formed at triple points and propagated along grain boundaries, as described by
Sabari et al. for LPBF 718 [44]. After annealing, cracks preferentially oriented perpendicular to the stress
direction were observed in specimens tested both along and perpendicular to the BD.

 3. CREEP DAMAGE ANALYSIS VIA ADVANCED CHARACTERIZATION TECHNIQUES

3.1 CREEP TESTING IN VACUUM AT 725°C

Creep testing was conducted in vacuum at 725°C/200 MPa using an as-printed dog-bone specimen
machined and polished to a mirror finish to allow for SEM surface imaging. Figure 10a shows the creep-
rate evolution during a previous test performed in air at 725°C under the same conditions, together with
the four interruption times for the vacuum specimen. The first interruption corresponded to the primary
creep stage, whereas the second occurred at the beginning of the secondary creep stage. Note that two
minimum creep rates were observed, which Hatakeyama et al. [46] attributed to microstructure evolution,
particularly the formation of precipitates at GBs. The third and fourth interruptions were intended to
assess creep damage evolution during the secondary creep stage. Figure 10c shows the microstructure of
the specimen after 100 h of testing, compared with the reference microstructure shown in Figure 10b,
highlighting the initial cellular structure with (Nb,Mo)-rich carbides in the cell walls. Long needle-like
precipitates were observed within the grain, along with the formation of both carbides and d phase at
GBs. No creep cavitation was detected anywhere in the specimen after 100 h. Imaging of the same area
after 360 h revealed an increase in the fraction of d phase within the grain, with the appearance of new
precipitates and limited growth of those already present after 100 h. Carbides in the cell walls did not
change in size or number, whereas moderate growth of the precipitates at GBs was observed. Although
most GBs remained crack-free, Figure 10d highlights one of the few GBs in which significant cavitation

 9

was observed, mainly between precipitates. Although the number of new GBs exhibiting cavities after
430 h was limited, Figure 11 shows the evolution of cavities and cracking from 360 to 430 h in a region
where cracking was prominent. The red arrow points to several thin cavities observed between
precipitates that grew significantly from 360 to 430 h. In addition, the green arrows point to one GB
without precipitates containing a large crack that widened after 430 h. Minor oxidation occurred at the
surface after 550 h, and the specimen is being repolished to remove the few oxide particles from the
specimen surface before imaging continues at longer test durations. Consistent with Figure 10a, which
shows that the specimen is still in the secondary creep stage after 550 h, no major cracking was observed
anywhere in the specimen.

3.2 CHARACTERIZATION OF CAVITIES DURING CREEP TESTING VIA XCT

XCT measurements were performed on interrupted creep specimens, and additional information about the
AI-powered software used to analyze the XCT scans can be found elsewhere [47]. Two Zeiss Versa
systems were used for XCT imaging. It was previously demonstrated that interrupted XCT scans can be
used to identify printing defects that will lead to creep rupture [15, 16, 33]. A similar approach was
pursued on LPBF 625 creep specimens with no significant printing defects that could be detected by XCT
before testing. Figure 12a shows the creep curves at 725°C for the four standard cylindrical specimens
that were analyzed by XCT. Two of them were tested at 200 MPa, one as-printed and one annealed
specimen, and were interrupted after 500 h of exposure. The other two were as-printed specimens tested
at 150 MPa and 125 MPa, and were initially interrupted after 3,000 h. The 3D renderings of the resulting
XCT data are presented in Figure 12b to Figure 12e. A low volume fraction (0.0004%–0.0005%) was
detected for three of the specimens, and the specimen tested at 150 MPa for 3,000 h exhibited a much
higher density of cavities (i.e., 0.0021%). Comparing the two specimens tested for 3,000 h, the
significantly higher volume fraction of cavities in the specimens tested at 150 MPa is likely related to the
specimen reaching the onset of the tertiary creep stage after 3,000 h, which was not the case for the
specimen tested at 125 MPa. Although the two specimens tested for 500 h at 200 MPa exhibited higher
strain than the specimens tested for 3,000 h, the cavity volume fractions were low, indicating that time is
a critical parameter for cavity nucleation and growth. The 125 MPa and 150 MPa specimens were tested
for an additional 2,000 h and interrupted after 5,000 h for XCT analysis. The volume fraction of cavities
for the specimen tested at 125 MPa remained very low (i.e., 0.001%), and Figure 13a to Figure 13c
highlight a significant increase in the number of cavities after 5,000 h for the specimen tested at 150 MPa.
After 3,000 h, 90 cavities were detected compared with more than 1,000 cavities detected after 5,000 h.
Most of these new cavities were small, 30 to 40 µm in equivalent diameter, and therefore it is possible
that these cavities were already present after 3,000 h but were too small to be detected. Notably, two
different Zeiss Versa systems were used for XCT measurements after 3,000 and 5,000 h, and therefore a
moderate improvement in signal-to-noise ratio could also explain the higher number of small cavities
detected after 5,000 h.

XCT imaging of the same creep specimen after different durations allows the morphological evolution of
specific cavities to be tracked. Black arrows in Figure 13a and Figure 13b clearly highlight that the same
cavities were detected after 3,000 and 5,000 h. About 70 cavities were identified with confidence after
3,000 and 5,000 h, and Figure 13c shows these tracked pores in red. As shown in Figure 13d, evolution in
equivalent diameter was used to calculate the growth rates for each of these cavities. Interestingly, the
growth rate appears to increase with increasing cavity diameter. Cavity growth measurements are sparse
in the literature and often require complex testing in controlled nonoxidizing environments [48], such as
the vacuum tests presented in 3.1. The use of NDE XCT provides a unique opportunity to generate a large
dataset of cavity growth rates and use the dataset to better understand creep damage evolution and
improve lifetime predictions.

3.4 MICROSTRUCTURE AND PROPERTIES OF LPBF 244

PB-14 was selected for additional characterization, and several blocks were printed for microstructure
analysis and tensile testing. SEM micrographs and energy-dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (EDS) maps of
the as-printed LPBF 244 are presented in Figure 16. As expected, elongated grains were observed along
the BD, with the presence of elongated cells approximately 1–2 µm in width. Chemical segregation in the
cell walls was mainly related to Mo.

 Figure 16. (a–c) SEM micrographs of the as-printed LPBF 244 alloy and (d–f) EDS maps.

Tensile testing was conducted at both room temperature and 750°C on as-printed LPBF 244 dog-bone
specimens machined along and perpendicular to the BD. The tensile properties of the alloy are compared
with those of LPBF 625 at 20°C and 750°C in Figure 17a and Figure 17b, respectively. At 20°C, the
tensile properties of LPBF 244 and LPBF 625 were similar, with higher strength but lower ductility
perpendicular to the BD. The results differed significantly at 750°C, with LPBF 244 exhibiting
considerably higher strength but lower ductility compared with LPBF 625. A significant decrease in
ductility was again observed for specimens tested perpendicular to the BD compared with those tested
along the BD. To suppress the material anisotropic behavior, heat treatments were conducted at 1,100°C
and 1,150°C. As shown in the IPF maps (Figure 18), although the elongated grain structure was still
observed after 1 h at 1,100°C, a 1 h treatment at 1,150°C resulted in complete recrystallization of the
alloy, as observed for LPBF 625. The LPBF 244 material was therefore heat treated according to the
Haynes International 244 heat treatment (i.e., 1 h at 1,150°C, 16 h at 760°C, furnace cool to 650°C, 32 h
hold at 1,200°C, and air cool). The two-step aging treatment promotes the formation of the strengthening
g‴ phase [38, 39]. At 20°C, Figure 19a shows that the heat treatment significantly decreased the YS of the
alloy but markedly increased its ductility along and perpendicular to the BD. Similarly, a decrease in YS
was observed after the heat treatment at 750°C but with a decrease in UTS and ductility. Specimens are
being mounted to investigate the abrupt rupture of these specimens.

 4. CONCLUSION

Because of their relevance for the nuclear industry, two Ni-based alloys were fabricated using LPBF, and
their microstructure and mechanical properties were characterized. Low-defect LPBF 625 was creep
tested at 725°C and 650°C and exhibited properties approaching those of wrought 625. The low ductility
observed perpendicular to the BD because of the elongated grain structure along the BD was partially
mitigated by a recrystallization heat treatment at 1,150°C for 1 h. Cracking was still observed
preferentially at GBs at low angles with respect to the stress direction, and improving the ductility of
LPBF 625 would help facilitate the deployment of the alloy in nuclear systems. Advanced
characterization techniques were used to improve our understanding of the alloy creep behavior.
Interrupted creep testing in vacuum at 725°C revealed that cavitation at GBs initiated mainly between GB
precipitates and at precipitate-free GBs. Although cavitation was observed after only 100 h of testing at
725°C/200 MPa, cavitation and cracking remained localized during the secondary creep stage. It was also
demonstrated that XCT performed during interrupted creep testing can be used to image the evolution of
creep damage at GBs and to calculate cavity growth rates. Future work will include the assessment of new
heat treatments to control GB precipitate formation and hinder cavity formation. Finally, fabrication of
crack-free, low-Cr Haynes 244 was demonstrated. The alloy exhibited superior strength but lower
ductility at 750°C compared with LPBF 625. Because of the as-printed elongated grain structure, lower
ductility was observed perpendicular to the BD. Recrystallization was achieved after 1 h at 1,150°C and
resulted in an increase of the alloy’s tensile ductility at room temperature along and perpendicular to the
BD. Low ductility was, however, measured at 750°C after annealing and aging, and optimization of the
heat treatment will be conducted in future work.
