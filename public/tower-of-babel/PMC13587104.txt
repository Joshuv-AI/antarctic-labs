Gephyrin Neurological Autoimmunity
Authors: Maria Chiara Pantuliano, Friederike A. Arlt, Naomi Arenson, Irena Dujmovic Basuroski, Divyanshu Dubey, Connie E. Lesnick, John R. Mills, Sean J. Pittock, Anastasia Zekeridou, Michael A. Lane, Andrew McKeon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gephyrin is a postsynaptic scaffold protein essential for inhibitory neurotransmission. Gephyrin‐immunoglobulin G (IgG) was reported, decades ago, in a single case of paraneoplastic stiff‐person‐like syndrome, but its broader clinical relevance remains unknown. While investigating unclassified synaptic IgGs mimicking amphiphysin‐IgG, we identified gephyrin‐IgG in 3 patients using human protein microarray. Gephyrin specificity was confirmed using confocal tissue‐based immunofluorescence assay, cell‐based assay, and Western blot. The 3 patients, all female, had: encephalitis (2) and myeloneuropathy and breast cancer (1). All had inflammatory cerebrospinal fluid and abnormal magnetic resonance imaging, and improved following immunotherapy. Gephyrin‐IgG is associated with a treatable autoimmune neurological disease, sometimes paraneoplastic. ANN NEUROL 2026;100:781–787

IgG antibodies to synaptic (neuropil) antigens are increasingly recognized as biomarkers of autoimmune neurologic diseases. 1 Amphiphysin‐IgG, is classically associated with breast adenocarcinoma in women with stiff‐person syndrome (SPS). 2 , 3 In characterizing unclassified IgGs that reproduce the amphiphysin‐IgG tissue immunofluorescence pattern but amphiphysin‐specific test negative, we identified gephyrin (GPHN) autoimmunity. GPHN‐IgG was previously described, 25 years ago, in 1 patient with paraneoplastic SPS‐like syndrome with bilateral leg, truncal, and bulbar involvement. 4 GPHN is a postsynaptic scaffold protein essential for inhibitory neurotransmission by organizing and stabilizing GABAA receptors. 5 Here, we describe our GPHN autoimmune experience.

Methods

Standard Protocol Approvals and Registrations

The Mayo Clinic institutional review board (IRB), as a minimum risk research protocol (IRB, 21–001297), approved: (1) human waste clinical specimen (serum/cerebrospinal fluid [CSF]) acquisition and testing for antibodies, and, (2) retrospective medical record review.

Patients and Screening

Serum and CSF samples were screened by murine tissue‐based indirect immunofluorescence assay (TIIFA) as previously described (Supplemental methods). 6 Between October 1, 2023 and September 30, 2025, 495 samples with unclassified neural antibodies (UNAs) (273 serum, 222 CSF) were identified, representing 0.21% of all TIIFA‐evaluated samples. Synaptic UNAs (which accounts for >90% of all UNAs) were defined by staining of synaptic elements (neuropil) in murine brain, without nuclear, nucleolar, or non‐synaptic cytoplasmic staining, and lacking criteria for known IgGs or testing negative on corresponding confirmatory assay. Each UNA sample was tested on human protein microarrays (>21,000 proteins; HuProt v4.0, CDI Laboratories, Mayaguez, Puerto Rico). 7 Confirmation of GPHN specificity and phage immunoprecipitation sequencing (PhIP‐Seq) was as previously described (Supplemental Methods). Medical records and imaging data were reviewed.

Results

Laboratory Findings

Three patients were identified (2023–2025), all had serum available and 2 had CSF. Serums were retested by TIIFA, and each produced identical synaptic IgG staining patterns throughout cerebrum and cerebellum (Fig 1). Staining of the cerebellar granular layer exceeded that of the molecular layer or Purkinje cells (inset), similar to, but distinct from glutamic acid decarboxylase, 65 kilodalton isoform (GAD65)‐IgG staining (Fig S1). Diffuse cerebral staining and fainter staining of a subset of renal medullary tubules were also observed, and myenteric plexus was negative. Protein microarray revealed duplicate GPHN hits within top 10 of 21,000 proteins in all 3 serums tested (Fig S2), and none had coexisting GAD65 or amphiphysin hits. No significant enrichment for GPHN‐IgG by PhIPSeq was detected in any of 10 amphiphysin‐IgG control patients. Confocal TIIFA demonstrated colocalization between the rabbit polyclonal GPHN antibody and Patient 3 serum IgG (Fig 2A). All 3 patients were tested and were positive by GPHN‐specific CBA (all 3 serums and 2 CSF available) and Western blot (all 3 serums tested) (see Fig 2B,C and Fig S3).

Clinical Features, Treatments, and Outcomes

Three GPHN‐IgG–positive patients had detailed clinical information (Table 1). Patient ages were 34, 58, and 60 years, and all were female, Table 1. Two had preexisting autoimmune disease (rheumatoid arthritis and Sjögren's syndrome). Patient 3 had a history of alcohol use disorder with sole neurological manifestation of withdrawal seizure and had been abstinent for 6 months at encephalitic presentation.

Neurological Phenotypes

Two patients presented with encephalitis, 1 with myeloneuropathy (Table 1). Symptom onset was chronically progressive (neuropathy) followed by subacute (myelopathy, Patient 1), insidious (Patient 2), or subacute (Patient 3). Prominent manifestations were motor weakness (3), sensory symptoms (2), headache (2), and seizures (1).

Patient 1 had a 4‐year progressive history of distal paresthesias, hypoesthesia, and sensory ataxia, which evolved, explained by length‐dependent polyneuropathy, followed by subacute myelopathy after breast cancer therapy (carboplatin, paclitaxel, and pembrolizumab). Features were rapidly progressive paraparesis, thoracic sensory level, urinary urgency and hesitancy, incomplete bladder emptying, and constipation.

Patient 2 initially had headache with left occipital neuralgia, photophobia, and painful eye movements, then, after several months, intermittent right hemibody weakness, paresthesias, and hypoesthesia (no seizure correlate on electroencephalography [EEG]), with persistent right leg weakness and later right leg hypoesthesia.

Patient 3 presented with agitation, focal seizures with secondary generalization, followed by left‐sided weakness and aphasia, mild cognitive impairment, and gait ataxia. Focal seizures featured leftward tonic head/eye deviation, oral automatisms, and left‐sided motor activity, often evolving to bilateral tonic upper‐limb posturing. Over a few months, she developed insomnia, headache, dysarthria, left‐sided weakness, left‐predominant spasms, and paroxysmal dyskinetic movements independent of seizures (EEG normal during events).

Cancer Associations

In Patient 1, invasive ductal adenocarcinoma (Stage 3C) was diagnosed 4 years after neuropathy onset. Myelopathy began 2 months after initiation of neoadjuvant treatment, including pembrolizumab, carboplatin, and paclitaxel. Polyneuropathy also progressed after neoadjuvant treatment. Chest/abdomen/pelvis computed tomography in Patients 2 and 3 showed no malignancy.

Magnetic Resonance Imaging Findings

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was abnormal in all patients. Brain MRI was normal in Patient 1, but spinal MRI showed a longitudinally extensive T2‐hyperintense lesion from T6–T11 (see Fig 1H, I). Patient 2 had left frontoparietal leptomeningeal enhancement (see Fig 1G). In Patient 3, the initial brain MRI was unrevealing; a later scan showed right anterior temporal T2‐fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery hyperintensity and encephalomalacia (see Fig 1L).

Neurophysiology

Neurophysiology was abnormal in 2 patients. In Patient 1, there was sensory polyneuropathy with mixed axonal and demyelinating features. In Patient 2, prolonged continuous EEG captured typical sensorimotor episodes without electrographic correlate. In Patient 3, serial EEGs showed right temporal slowing, frequent right frontotemporal epileptiform discharges, multiple focal seizures, and non‐convulsive status epilepticus.

CSF Testing

All had lymphocytic pleocytosis (ranging from 6 to 66 cells/μL). Of 2 patients tested for oligoclonal bands (OCBs) and IgG index, Patient 1 had CSF‐exclusive OCBs, and Patient 2 had elevated IgG index.

Treatment and Outcome

All received high‐dose corticosteroids with taper. Additional acute immunotherapies included intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) (Patient 1) and rituximab (Patients 1 and 2). Follow‐up ranged from 14 to 26 months. All improved neurologically, and recovery was substantial and sustained in 2 patients and partial in 1 patient.

In Patient 1, sensory symptoms worsened after steroid taper, prompting IVIg (2g/kg, then 1g/kg monthly) without clear benefit, and rituximab was added. She had partial recovery with near‐complete resolution of spinal MRI abnormalities (see Fig 1J, K), followed by recurrent sensory worsening. At last follow‐up, she had mild hip weakness, slightly improved thoracic sensory level, and ambulated short distances with a walker. Cancer outcome was unavailable.

Patient 2 received steroids and 1 rituximab cycle, achieving marked improvement with only residual right leg hypoesthesia and complete resolution of leptomeningeal enhancement.

Patient 3 received maintenance oral steroids and had substantial recovery with longer seizure‐free intervals, but residual mild dysarthria, balance impairment, cognitive complaints, insomnia, and headache.

Discussion

GPHN neurological autoimmunity was previously reported in a single SPS patient associated with poorly differentiated mediastinal carcinoma, which improved with tumor resection and diazepam. 4 Our 3 patients instead exhibited encephalitis (2) or myeloneuropathy (1), and breast adenocarcinoma (1). All 3 improved with immunotherapy, 2 substantially.

In the original report, patient serum and CSF produced synaptic staining of rodent brain, were GAD65‐ and amphiphysin‐negative, and recognized a 97‐kDa brain protein by Western blot, which co‐migrated with a band recognized by anti‐GPHN monoclonal antibody; binding to GPHN complementary DNA–transfected cells supported GPHN as the target. 4 We demonstrated the characteristic synaptic staining pattern by murine TIIFA and confirmed GPHN specificity by four additional methods, protein microarray (top hit in all specimens) and GPHN‐specific confocal TIIFA, Western blot, and CBA. Serum and CSF appeared equally sensitive. Given the similarity to other synaptic autoantibodies identifiable by TIIFA (eg, amphiphysin‐ and SNAP91‐IgGs) protein‐specific confirmation is needed. 1

By TIIFA, we consistently observed GPHN‐IgG staining of a subset of renal tubules, distinct from the diffuse renal pattern of anti‐mitochondrial antibodies. Although most enriched in the brain and liver, GPHN has expression in other tissues, including kidney and gut. It was notable that 2 patients had pre‐existing connective tissue disease. GPHN antibody, albeit detected by a different method (enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay), was recently reported as biomarker of gastroinestinal dysmotility in systemic sclerosis. 8

In our patients, GPHN‐IgG co‐occurred with inflammatory CSF, inflammatory‐appearing MRI abnormalities, and a clear response to immunotherapy, all supportive of neurological autoimmunity. In Patient 1 with paraneoplastic myeloneuropathy, the course was complicated because she had progressive symptoms of peripheral neuropathy, then breast adenocarcinoma was identified, then she received immune checkpoint therapy, followed by the development of myelopathy. In the absence of other causes identified, we infer her entire syndrome was autoimmune in etiology. It is possible that there is some phenotypic overlap between SPS in the previously reported case and Patient 1. 4

GPHN, although diffusely expressed in the nervous system, has a critical role as a scaffold protein stabilizing GABAA and glycine receptors at inhibitory synapses in the brain and spinal cord. 9 Therefore, encephalitic and myelopathic autoimmune disorders are biologically plausible and may overlap with the SPS‐like disorder originally described. GPHN is an intracellular postsynaptic protein, and these antibodies are likely biomarkers of GPHN‐specific T cell effectors and are unlikely pathogenic. Although GPHN‐IgG resembles amphiphysin and GAD65 IgGs on tissue‐based assay, we found coexistence of these antibodies among neither our 3 GPHN‐IgG patients, nor an amphiphysin control cohort, nor previously in our Mayo Clinic SPS cohort. 10

In conclusion, GPHN neurological autoimmunity, long recognized from a single SPS case, now appears to encompass a spectrum of neurologic presentations. A paraneoplastic basis should be considered and evaluation should include broad cancer screening.
