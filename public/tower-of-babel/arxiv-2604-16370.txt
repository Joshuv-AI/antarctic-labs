Brain-CLIPLM: Decoding Compressed Semantic Representations in EEG for Language Reconstruction
Xiaoli Yang 1,2 , Huiyuan Tian 1 , Yurui Li 1 , Jianyu Zhang 1 , Shijian Li 1,2,∗ and Gang Pan 1,2

Abstract
Decoding natural language from non-invasive electroencephalography (EEG) remains fundamentally limited by low signal-to-noise ratio and restricted information bandwidth. This raises a fundamental question regarding whether sentence-level linguistic structure can be reliably recovered from such signals. In this work, we suggest that this assumption may not hold under realistic information constraints, and instead propose a semantic compression hypothesis in which EEG signals encode a compressed set of semantic anchors rather than full linguistic structure. Under our new perspective, direct sentence reconstruction becomes an overparameterized objective relative to the intrinsic information capacity of EEG. To address this mismatch, we introduce Brain-CLIPLM, a two-stage framework that decomposes EEG-to-text decoding into semantic anchor extraction via contrastive learning and sentence reconstruction using a retrieval-grounded large language model (LLM) with Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning, following a granularity matching principle that aligns decoding complexity with neural information capacity. Evaluated on the Zurich Cognitive Language Processing Corpus, Brain-CLIPLM achieves 67.55% top-5 and 85.00% top-25 sentence retrieval accuracy, significantly outperforming direct decoding baselines, while cross-subject evaluation confirms robust generalization. Control analyses, including permutation testing, further demonstrate that EEG-derived representations carry sentence-specific information beyond language model priors. These results suggest that EEG-to-text decoding is better framed as recovering compressed semantic content rather than reconstructing full sentences, providing a biologically grounded and data-efficient pathway for non-invasive brain-computer interfaces. \helveticabold 1 Keywords: EEG decoding, neural coding, semantic compression, language reconstruction, semantic representation, contrastive learning, brain–computer interface

Introduction

Decoding language from neural activity, which involves understanding how patterns of brain signals relate to meaning, remains a central problem in neuroscience and brain-computer interfaces (BCIs)
(
38
;
16
)
. Progress in this direction would not only shed light on how semantic information is represented across distributed cortical systems
(
42
;
3
)
, but also enable practical communication systems for individuals who have lost motor function while retaining cognition, such as patients with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) or brainstem stroke
(
38
;
22
)
. For such users, non-invasive solutions are particularly important. Among available modalities, electroencephalography (EEG) offers a compelling combination of portability, low cost, and millisecond temporal resolution, making it one of the most viable candidates for real-world BCI deployment
(
17
;
26
)
. In practice, these systems do not require unrestricted language generation; instead, the immediate goal is to reliably decode short, semantically meaningful sentences that support everyday communication.

Recent EEG-to-text studies have explored direct sequence reconstruction using encoder-decoder frameworks
(
43
;
9
;
25
)
and autoregressive generation with neural guidance
(
47
)
. While these approaches report improving benchmark performance, they share a critical assumption that sentence-level linguistic structure is recoverable from EEG. From an information-theoretic perspective, this assumption is difficult to justify. EEG signals are noisy and bandwidth-limited, and the amount of reliably decodable information is fundamentally constrained
(
37
;
2
)
. Under typical recording conditions, a single EEG trial contains on the order of
10^{3}
–
10^{4}
bits of task-relevant information, whereas the space of possible sentences grows combinatorially with vocabulary size and sequence length
(
6
)
. Empirical evidence further shows that decoding performance can be strongly influenced by language model priors and may not reliably reflect neural input
(
43
)
. In addition, word-level decoding from non-invasive signals remains challenging even under controlled evaluation
(
10
)
. Together, these observations suggest that the core challenge lies in a mismatch between neural information content and linguistic complexity, rather than solely in model design.

This raises the question of what form of linguistic representation EEG signals can realistically support. Psycholinguistic and neuroscientific evidence indicates that language comprehension relies heavily on a relatively small set of semantically informative words, so that humans can often recover the meaning of a sentence from a few key content words even when syntactic details are degraded, which is consistent with principles of efficiency and compression in cognition
(
29
;
14
)
. At the neural level, word-level semantic representations tend to be more stable and more consistently decodable than full sentence structures
(
32
)
. Motivated by these observations, we propose a
semantic compression hypothesis
, which states that EEG signals do not encode full linguistic structure but instead preserve a compressed set of semantic anchors. We formalize this intuition using an information-theoretic framework. Let
X
denote EEG signals and
Y
the target sentence. Standard approaches aim to maximize
I(X;Y)
, yet in practice
I(X;Y)\ll H(Y)
, where
H(Y)
is the entropy of the sentence space
(
37
;
6
)
. We therefore consider a factorized formulation
X\rightarrow K\rightarrow Y
, where
K
represents a low-dimensional set of semantic keywords. This leads to a granularity matching principle: neural decoding is most effective when the representational granularity of the target matches the intrinsic information capacity of the signal. Under this view, keyword-level representations provide a more appropriate interface between neural signals and language than full sentences.

Building on this idea, we design Brain-CLIPLM as a principled framework that aligns the decoding objective with the intrinsic information capacity of EEG signals. Our approach instantiates the factorized formulation
X\rightarrow K\rightarrow Y
, in which semantic anchors serve as an intermediate representation bridging neural signals and language, naturally leading to a two-stage architecture. This perspective suggests that neural decoding is better understood as recovering compressed semantic representations that can be expanded by language models. In the first stage, we learn an EEG encoder using contrastive learning to align neural signals with embeddings of semantically meaningful words, producing a set of candidate keywords that capture the core content of the underlying sentence. In the second stage, we reconstruct full sentences from these semantic anchors using a large language model, augmented with chain-of-thought reasoning and retrieval-based grounding to improve coherence and robustness
(
45
;
24
)
. This design decouples neural decoding from language generation, allowing each component to operate at an appropriate level of abstraction. While prior work has explored intermediate word-level representations, our goal is not simply to introduce another pipeline, but to provide a principled account of why such a decomposition is necessary under non-invasive constraints.

This work makes three main contributions. First, we propose a conceptual framework for EEG-based language decoding grounded in the semantic compression hypothesis and information-theoretic constraints, offering a new perspective on the limitations of direct sentence reconstruction. Second, we develop Brain-CLIPLM as a practical instantiation of this idea, combining contrastive neural representation learning with large language model-based reconstruction. Third, we provide comprehensive empirical validation, including cross-subject analyses and controlled experiments, demonstrating that EEG-derived semantic representations capture sentence-specific information beyond language model priors. Together, these results suggest that EEG-to-text decoding is better understood as recovering compressed semantic content, rather than reconstructing full linguistic form directly.

3
Materials and methods

3.1
Dataset and preprocessing

We evaluated the proposed framework on the Zurich Cognitive Language Processing Corpus (ZuCo 1.0), a publicly available dataset containing simultaneous EEG and eye-tracking recordings during natural reading
(
18
;
19
)
. ZuCo includes 12 healthy native English speakers (6 female, 6 male; age 20–35 years) who read 358 English sentences spanning diverse topics (mean length 15.2 tokens, range 8–32). Sentences were presented in rapid serial visual presentation, with each word shown for 300 ms followed by a 200 ms inter-stimulus interval, allowing precise temporal alignment between linguistic input and neural responses.

EEG was recorded using a 128-channel BioSemi ActiveTwo system arranged according to the 10–5 layout and sampled at 512 Hz, then downsampled to 250 Hz. Eye movements were recorded concurrently with an EyeLink 1000 system at 1000 Hz. Word-level alignment was defined by fixation onset (mean 254 ms). For model development, data were partitioned with strict subject independence: 318 sentences were used for training and 40 for testing, with no overlap in either sentences or participants. Cross-subject generalization was further assessed using leave-one-subject-out evaluation.

Preprocessing was implemented in MNE-Python
(
15
)
. Raw EEG signals were bandpass filtered at 0.5–40 Hz and notch filtered at 50 Hz. Ocular and muscular artifacts were removed using infomax independent component analysis
(
21
)
. Epochs were extracted from -200 to 800 ms relative to fixation onset and baseline corrected. Time–frequency representations were then computed with Morlet wavelets, yielding power estimates in five frequency bands across 10 overlapping temporal windows. After z-score normalization using training-set statistics, we retained 48 electrodes over language-relevant cortical regions, resulting in a final input tensor of size
48\times 5\times 10
. All participants provided informed consent, and the dataset is publicly available under a Creative Commons license.

3.2
Keyword Vocabulary Construction

A central design choice in our framework is the definition of the keyword vocabulary used as the decoding target. Rather than attempting to decode every word in a sentence, we focused on content words that carry most of the semantic load, namely nouns, verbs, and adjectives, while excluding function words with primarily grammatical roles
(
31
;
16
;
10
)
. This choice follows directly from the semantic compression hypothesis introduced above.

Vocabulary size was selected through preliminary comparisons across 50, 100, 200, and 500 candidate words. A vocabulary of 100 words provided the best balance between semantic coverage and decoding reliability, yielding stronger performance than larger vocabularies while remaining substantially richer than typical closed-set decoding tasks. We therefore used 100 keywords in all subsequent experiments.

The vocabulary was constructed in five steps. First, all sentences in ZuCo were tokenized with SpaCy (en_core_web_sm, version 3.7.4), and part-of-speech tags were used to separate content words from function words. Second, term frequencies were computed over the full corpus, and only content words appearing at least five times were retained as candidates. Third, linguistic filtering was applied to remove numbers, tokens containing digits, single-character words (except content uses of “I” and “a”), and common temporal expressions such as months and weekdays. Lemmatization was then used to collapse morphological variants into shared entries. Fourth, semantic redundancy was reduced by selecting words that were maximally separated in a 300-dimensional GloVe embedding space using greedy farthest-point sampling. Finally, for sentences that contained no selected vocabulary items, we added a fallback keyword chosen by TF-IDF score relative to the corpus.

The resulting vocabulary contained 100 keywords, including 74 words selected through the main frequency-and-diversity pipeline and 26 introduced through the fallback mechanism. The final set comprised 62 nouns, 28 verbs, and 10 adjectives. Each sentence was represented by exactly five keywords, defined as the five most informative content words available after filtering, with fallback items used when necessary.

3.3
Brain-CLIPLM Architecture

Brain-CLIPLM consists of two stages: EEG-to-keyword decoding and keyword-to-sentence reconstruction. The first stage maps EEG segments into a semantic embedding space, and the second stage reconstructs sentences from the retrieved semantic anchors. This decomposition follows the factorized formulation
X\rightarrow K\rightarrow Y
and separates neural decoding from language generation. The overall architecture is illustrated in Figure
2
.

3.3.1
Stage 1: EEG-to-keyword decoding with contrastive learning

The first stage was formulated as contrastive representation learning rather than closed-set classification. Specifically, we learned a shared embedding space in which EEG segments were aligned with their corresponding keywords, while unrelated EEG–keyword pairs were pushed apart. This formulation preserves semantic structure and allows retrieval-based decoding without retraining when the vocabulary is expanded.

The EEG encoder operated on the preprocessed
48\times 5\times 10
tensors and was implemented using a Deep4-style convolutional network
(
35
)
. The network combined spatial, temporal, and spectral convolutions, followed by global average pooling and two fully connected layers, to produce a 128-dimensional L2-normalized embedding. Keyword representations were obtained from a frozen BERT-base model
(
7
)
. For each keyword, we extracted the final-layer [CLS] representation and projected it into the same 128-dimensional embedding space, followed by L2 normalization.

The encoder was trained with a symmetric InfoNCE objective
(
4
)
, using cosine similarity and a temperature parameter of 0.07 following CLIP-style contrastive learning
(
33
)
:

\mathcal{L}_{i}=-\log\frac{\exp(\mathrm{sim}(e_{i},t_{i})/\tau)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp(\mathrm{sim}(e_{i},t_{j})/\tau)}.

(1)

Here,
e_{i}
and
t_{i}
denote the EEG and keyword embeddings for the
i
-th matched pair,
\mathrm{sim}(\cdot,\cdot)
is cosine similarity, and
N
is the batch size. We used the symmetric objective
(\mathcal{L}_{EEG\rightarrow text}+\mathcal{L}_{text\rightarrow EEG})/2
to balance both retrieval directions.

Training hyperparameters were selected by grid search on the training set. The final model used AdamW optimization with batch size 64, learning rate
10^{-4}
, weight decay
10^{-5}
, cosine annealing with 10 warm-up epochs, and early stopping based on validation retrieval accuracy. To improve robustness, training included channel dropout, Gaussian noise injection, time masking, and frequency masking. All experiments were conducted on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU.

At inference, each EEG segment was embedded and matched to the vocabulary by cosine similarity. For each segment, the top 10 nearest keywords were retrieved, and sentence-level predictions were formed by aggregating across all content-word EEG segments in that sentence. Unless otherwise stated, the top five retrieved keywords were retained for reconstruction. We also computed a confidence score based on the margin between the best and second-best matches:

\mathrm{conf}(k)=\frac{\mathrm{sim}(e,t_{k})-\mathrm{sim}(e,t_{k+1})}{\mathrm{sim}(e,t_{k})},

(2)

where
t_{k}
and
t_{k+1}
denote the first- and second-ranked keyword embeddings, respectively.

3.3.2
Stage 2: LLM-based sentence reconstruction

Given a retrieved keyword set
S
, the second stage reconstructed the most likely sentence consistent with those semantic anchors. Because the retrieved keywords may be incomplete, unordered, or noisy, reconstruction was treated as a constrained generation problem.

We used LLaMA-2-7B-Chat
(
39
)
as the base language model because of its strong generation performance and open availability for research use. The model was not fine-tuned on EEG data, which preserved a clear separation between neural decoding and language generation. Instead, it was prompted to generate a single fluent sentence from the decoded keywords.

The base prompt asked the model to reconstruct the most likely sentence from a set of keywords extracted from EEG signals:

“You are a neuroscience researcher reconstructing sentences from keywords extracted from EEG brain signals. Given the following keywords, generate the most likely complete sentence that a person was reading. The keywords may be incomplete or slightly noisy, so use your knowledge of language and typical sentence structures.

Keywords: {keyword_list}

Generate a single, fluent English sentence that naturally incorporates all these keywords. The sentence should be grammatically correct and semantically coherent.”

To improve reconstruction quality, we incorporated chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting
(
45
)
, which encouraged explicit reasoning over keyword roles and relationships before sentence generation. In practice, the model was guided to analyze keyword categories, infer plausible semantic relations, organize them into a sentence plan, generate a draft, and then verify completeness and grammaticality. This structured prompting reduced hallucinations and improved keyword coverage relative to direct generation.

We also implemented retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)
(
24
)
. The retrieval corpus consisted of all 318 training sentences from ZuCo, indexed with TF-IDF vectors computed over content words using Scikit-learn’s
TfidfVectorizer
. For each keyword set
S
, the keywords were concatenated into a query string, and the top five most similar sentences were retrieved by cosine similarity and inserted into the prompt as additional context:

“Reference sentences with similar keywords:

{retrieved_sentences}

Use these as guidance, but generate a new sentence that best matches the current keyword set.”

Our final reconstruction strategy combined CoT prompting with RAG retrieval. The model first retrieved relevant examples, then generated a sentence conditioned on both the decoded keywords and the retrieved context. Unless otherwise stated, all generation parameters were selected on a validation set and then fixed for all experiments (temperature
=0.7
, top-
p=0.9
, repetition penalty
=1.2
,
do_sample=True
, and maximum output length of 100 tokens).

3.3.3
Experimental variants

To quantify the contribution of reasoning and retrieval, we evaluated three reconstruction settings. Variant 1 used rule-based CoT with TF-IDF retrieval for retrieval-only evaluation, providing a keyword-driven corpus retrieval baseline without language-model generation. Variant 2 used LLaMA-2 with CoT prompting but without retrieval, assessing generation from sparse decoded keywords alone. Variant 3 combined CoT prompting, LLaMA-2, and RAG, corresponding to the full Brain-CLIPLM framework. All variants used identical preprocessing and keyword decoding settings. Experiments were repeated with five random seeds (42, 123, 456, 789, and 1000), and results are reported as mean
\pm
standard error together with 95% confidence intervals.

3.4
Evaluation metrics

We evaluated the two stages of the framework separately. For EEG-to-keyword decoding, we report Top-
K
accuracy for
K=1,5,10,
and
20
, defined as the proportion of EEG segments for which the correct keyword appears among the top-
K
retrieved candidates. We also report mean reciprocal rank (MRR), which summarizes the rank position of the correct keyword across all segments. Confidence intervals were estimated by bootstrap resampling with 10,000 iterations.

For sentence-level evaluation, we report Top-
K
sentence retrieval accuracy. Given a reconstructed sentence, we ranked a held-out set of 200 candidate sentences according to cosine similarity between Sentence-BERT embeddings
(
34
)
. The correct sentence was considered recovered if it appeared within the top-
K
candidates, with
K=5,10,15,20,
and
25
.

For generated outputs, we further assessed reconstruction quality with three complementary measures: keyword coverage, BERTScore F1
(
46
)
, and a normalized length score. These were combined into a composite score:

\mathrm{Composite}=0.5\times\mathrm{KeywordCoverage}+0.3\times\mathrm{BERTScore}+0.2\times\min\left(1,\frac{\mathrm{len}(y_{\mathrm{gen}})}{\mathrm{len}(y_{\mathrm{gt}})}\right).

(3)

The weighting scheme reflects the central importance of keyword preservation, while also rewarding semantic fidelity and output completeness.

3.5
Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated with five-fold cross-validation, with participant-based splits used to ensure independence between training and test sets. Results are reported as mean
\pm
95% confidence interval, estimated by bootstrap resampling with 10,000 iterations
(
11
)
.

Pairwise comparisons were assessed using paired
t
-tests when normality assumptions were satisfied and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests otherwise, with Bonferroni correction applied for multiple comparisons. Comparisons involving more than two conditions were analyzed with repeated-measures ANOVA using Greenhouse–Geisser correction when necessary, followed by Tukey post hoc tests. Effect sizes are reported as Cohen’s
d
for pairwise comparisons and partial
\eta^{2}_{p}
for ANOVA
(
5
)
.

To assess whether EEG–keyword alignment exceeded chance, we additionally performed permutation testing with 10,000 iterations by randomly shuffling EEG–keyword pairings while preserving the marginal distributions of both modalities. For each permutation, Top-5 retrieval accuracy was recomputed to form a null distribution, and empirical
p
-values were estimated accordingly.

Within-subject evaluation used stratified five-fold cross-validation that preserved the distributions of sentence length, topic, and keyword frequency. Cross-subject generalization was assessed using leave-one-subject-out validation across all 12 participants.

Post hoc power analysis was conducted in G*Power 3.1
(
12
)
. Statistical analyses were implemented in SciPy, StatsModels, and Pingouin, with confirmatory analyses performed in SPSS 28.0
(
41
;
36
;
40
;
20
)
.

Keyword coverage remained modest even in the full setting, with only 26.41% of ground-truth keywords appearing in generated sentences. This reflects the difficulty of the task: some informative keywords are missed in Stage 1, and the language model cannot always integrate all retrieved items into a fluent sentence. Nevertheless, sentence retrieval remained high, indicating that reconstruction quality depended more on preserving the most informative semantic anchors than on matching every keyword exactly. Qualitative examples (Figure
4
C) illustrate the progression across variants. For the keywords “become” and “university”, naive generation produced the sentence “Become an engineer and begin work at your current level in the computer science field”, which was fluent but unrelated to any ZuCo target sentence. CoT-only improved this to “He decided to become a professor at the university”, which was more plausible but still generic. RAG-only produced “During his stay, he became a student at Oxford University”, reflecting closer alignment with the corpus. The full CoT+RAG pipeline recovered the ground-truth sentence: “During his stay in prison, he rediscovered the Bible, learned Greek, and became a student of Christian theology at Oxford University.” Taken together, these examples suggest that CoT contributes structural organization, whereas RAG contributes corpus-specific grounding.

The cross-sentence shuffled condition shows that success does not arise from preserving only broad sentence characteristics independent of keyword identity. The large performance gap between EEG-derived and control keywords therefore indicates that the decoded representations retain sentence-specific information.

5
Discussion

5.1
Evidence for semantic compression in non-invasive EEG

The present results support the view that non-invasive EEG carries discriminable semantic information in compressed form. Across retrieval, generation, control, and permutation analyses, EEG-derived anchors constrained sentence reconstruction well beyond random or frequency-matched baselines. In particular, permutation testing reduced retrieval accuracy to 13.2% (
p<0.001
), indicating that performance depended on structured EEG–keyword alignment rather than statistical artifacts or dataset bias. Taken together, these findings are consistent with the semantic compression hypothesis, namely that macroscopic neural signals preserve distributed semantic anchors rather than complete syntactic sequences
(
30
;
23
)
.

The hierarchy observed across word classes provides further support for this interpretation. Nouns were the most decodable, followed by verbs, adjectives, and adverbs. One plausible explanation is that concrete nouns are associated with more stable and more distinctive neural representations, whereas verbs and modifiers depend more strongly on context and relational structure
(
31
;
16
;
8
)
. The monotonic decrease across classes is therefore consistent with a graded dimension of neural codability linked to concreteness, imageability, and semantic specificity. In this sense, semantic compression does not appear to preserve all linguistic categories equally, but instead favors those that carry the greatest conceptual weight.

Cross-subject generalization offers an additional perspective on the structure of the neural code. The gap between within-subject and cross-subject performance indicates that semantic representations contain both shared and individual-specific components. At the same time, cross-subject decoding remained well above chance, suggesting that a substantial portion of the semantic signal is consistent across participants. This balance is encouraging for practical BCI settings, where fully individualized models are often difficult to obtain, yet some degree of shared structure is necessary for calibration-efficient decoding
(
13
;
17
)
.

5.2
Granularity matching and neural information capacity

One implication of the present findings is that decoding performance depends strongly on the relationship between target complexity and neural information capacity. Direct sentence reconstruction attempts to recover a high-entropy linguistic space from comparatively low-bandwidth neural data, which is difficult to reconcile with basic information-theoretic constraints. By contrast, a keyword representation drastically reduces the effective search space. For a vocabulary of approximately 10,000 words and a sentence length of approximately 10, the number of possible sentences is on the order of
10^{40}

(
43
)
. In our setting, restricting the target to 5 keywords drawn from a 100-word vocabulary reduces this space to
C(100,5)=75{,}287{,}520
. This shift from sentence space to keyword space brings the decoding target closer to the scale of information that EEG can plausibly support.

The empirical pattern is consistent with this interpretation. Performance improved markedly when moving from three to five keywords, but showed little further gain at seven keywords. This suggests that keyword-level decoding is useful only up to a point, beyond which the signal may no longer support additional semantic detail. Although the exact information content of a keyword depends on context and relational structure, the observed optimum is broadly consistent with a limited semantic bandwidth at the sentence level. In this sense, the granularity matching principle is not only a theoretical claim, but also an experimentally supported design constraint. This idea may extend beyond EEG-to-text decoding. In visual decoding, for example, the most appropriate targets may be object categories or semantic features rather than pixel-level reconstructions
(
38
;
28
)
. In motor decoding, the relevant units may be action primitives rather than continuous kinematic trajectories
(
44
)
. More generally, the present results suggest that decoding succeeds when the representational granularity of the target is commensurate with the information capacity of the neural signal.

5.3
Distinguishing neural decoding from LLM hallucination

A central concern in any framework that combines neural decoding with large language models is whether the final output reflects genuine neural information or merely plausible text generation. The present control analyses argue against the latter interpretation. When the neural–semantic link was disrupted, either by randomizing keywords, frequency-matching them without preserving identity, or shuffling them across sentences, performance collapsed toward chance. This indicates that the language model alone was insufficient to recover the target sentence, even though it could still generate fluent text. The distinction is important. A language model can compensate for missing information, but it cannot reliably recover sentence identity without informative constraints. In our framework, those constraints are provided by EEG-derived semantic anchors. The large gap between EEG-derived and control conditions therefore suggests that the model is not simply amplifying linguistic priors, but is operating on sentence-specific neural information. The permutation results reinforce this interpretation by showing that retrieval performance disappears once the EEG–keyword relationship is destroyed. The interaction between CoT and RAG further supports this point. If the final output were dominated by hallucination, retrieval-based grounding would be expected to provide only modest gains. Instead, we observed a substantial interaction, indicating that retrieved examples and structured reasoning helped the model make better use of the decoded keywords. This is more consistent with a constrained reconstruction process than with unconstrained text generation. From a translational perspective, this distinction is essential: in clinical communication settings, outputs must track user intent rather than the prior preferences of a language model.

5.4
Synergistic integration of CoT reasoning and RAG retrieval

The improvement obtained by combining CoT reasoning with RAG suggests that the two mechanisms address different aspects of the reconstruction problem. CoT primarily contributes structure. It encourages the model to infer plausible relations among decoded keywords and to organize them into a coherent sentence plan before generation. RAG, by contrast, contributes grounding. Retrieved examples constrain the output toward corpus-supported patterns and reduce the tendency to generate generic but semantically uninformative sentences. Their joint effect is therefore not merely additive. CoT makes the model’s internal reasoning more explicit, whereas RAG supplies concrete external context. When both are present, the model is better able to generate outputs that are both syntactically coherent and semantically specific. This was particularly useful when the keyword set was sparse or ambiguous, that is, when multiple reconstructions were plausible on the basis of keywords alone. Although these observations arise from EEG-to-text decoding, the broader principle may generalize to other forms of constrained generation. Whenever the input is sparse, noisy, or incomplete, combining structured reasoning with example-based retrieval may provide a more stable bridge between weak input signals and fluent language output.

5.5
Comparison with state-of-the-art methods and clinical implications

Relative to recent EEG-to-text studies, Brain-CLIPLM is distinctive less because it uses language models per se, and more because it redefines what should be decoded from EEG. Some contemporary approaches combine EEG with additional modalities such as eye-tracking or focus on multimodal alignment in other domains
(
27
;
1
)
. Our results show that competitive sentence-level retrieval can be achieved from EEG alone when the target is reformulated as a set of semantic anchors rather than a full sentence. The closest comparison is with work that also introduces word-level neural guidance for language generation
(
47
)
. The present study extends that line of work in several ways: by grounding vocabulary design in information-theoretic considerations, by systematically comparing reconstruction strategies, and by providing strong control analyses, including permutation testing. More broadly, the convergence of recent studies on intermediate semantic representations suggests that this may be a productive direction for non-invasive language decoding.

The clinical implications are promising but should be interpreted cautiously. The present framework is not yet a communication prosthesis, but it does suggest a feasible route toward one. The finding that concrete, high-frequency nouns are most decodable indicates that early-stage systems may benefit from focusing on semantically salient and personally relevant vocabularies. The cross-subject results also suggest that minimal calibration may be sufficient to adapt the framework to new users. In practice, a mixed-initiative interface may be especially useful, allowing the system to produce direct outputs when confidence is high and to offer candidates or clarification prompts when confidence is low. Such a strategy would better reflect the uncertainty inherent in non-invasive neural decoding while preserving its practical value.

6
Conclusion

We presented Brain-CLIPLM, a framework for decoding language-related information from non-invasive EEG by treating the problem as semantic reconstruction rather than direct sentence mapping. Guided by the semantic compression hypothesis and information-theoretic considerations, the proposed approach decomposes EEG-to-text decoding into two stages: keyword-level semantic decoding through contrastive learning, followed by sentence reconstruction using a large language model with structured reasoning and retrieval grounding.

The results provide convergent support for this formulation. Across retrieval, control, and cross-subject analyses, EEG-derived semantic anchors carried sentence-specific information beyond language model priors. In addition, the observed hierarchy across word classes and the balance between shared and subject-specific decoding performance are consistent with the view that non-invasive EEG preserves compressed semantic structure rather than full linguistic form. On the ZuCo benchmark, Brain-CLIPLM achieved strong sentence retrieval performance while remaining relatively data-efficient.

Taken together, these findings suggest that EEG-to-text decoding may be better understood as recovering compressed semantic content that can later be expanded into language, rather than reconstructing sentences directly from neural signals. Beyond the specific framework introduced here, this perspective offers a principled direction for future work on non-invasive language BCIs, especially in settings where robustness, limited training data, and clinical practicality are central concerns.
