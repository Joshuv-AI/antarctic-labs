Purinergic activity of circulating extracellular vesicles associates with disease progression in melanoma
Authors: Yana G Najjar, Chang Sook Hong, Elizabeth V Menshikova, John M Kirkwood, Brenda Diergaarde, Edwin K Jackson, Theresa L Whiteside
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTExtracellular vesicles (EVs) produced by melanoma cells (MTEX) carry ecto-nucleotidases, CD39/CD73, on their surface membrane, produce adenosine (ADO), and deliver it to immune recipient cells, inducing immune suppression. Based on studies of MTEX from melanoma cell lines, we hypothesized that MTEX are the major producers of ADO in cancer patients’ plasma, which contains a variable mix of MTEX and NTEX. To test this hypothesis, EVs were isolated from the plasma of melanoma patients with no evidence of disease (NED) or with metastatic disease at phlebotomy, and MTEX were separated from EVs produced by non-malignant cells (NTEX) by immune capture using anti-CSPG4 mAbs. The purinergic activity of CD39/CD73 in total plasma EVs and NTEX was measured by high-pressure liquid chromatography with fluorescence detection, and that in MTEX was calculated. Unexpectedly, NTEX showed higher levels of purinergic activity than MTEX. While higher purinergic activity of NTEX vs. MTEX was not associated with disease status, the CD73 activity in total EVs was correlated with disease progression: it was significantly higher in patients with metastatic disease compared to those with NED (P = 0.02). Our data suggest that in the plasma of cancer patients, both MTEX and NTEX contribute to the purinergic metabolism mediated by EVs. Thus, the total EV fraction in plasma of melanoma patients provides a clinically meaningful assessment of purinergic activity and potentially can serve as a personalized estimate of levels of ADO-driven immune suppression in patients.Keywords: Extracellular vesicles (EVs), melanoma cell-derived vesicles (MTEX), purinergic activity, CD39/CD73 signaling, MTEX-driven molecular reprogramming

Introduction

Therapy with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has achieved remarkable results in many different types of cancer 1 , 2 but has failed to provide durable benefits in a significant proportion of patients. 3 While reasons for primary or acquired resistance to ICIs are complex and still being elucidated, tumor-induced immune suppression may be one of the mechanisms of resistance to ICIs. 3 , 4 Cancers produce numerous factors that limit the efficacy of ICI 4-6 by altering the tumor microenvironment (TME) into an immune “desert”, either devoid of immune effector cells or populated by exhausted and ineffective immune cells. 7

The adenosinergic pathway is emerging as a major mechanism of resistance to immune-based therapies in various cancers. 8 Adenosine (ADO) and its biological effects, including immune suppression in cancer, have been intensively investigated for many years. 9 Earlier studies identified the actionable components of the adenosinergic pathway, i.e., adenosine receptors (ADOR), ligands and enzymes mediating ADO synthesis and degradation. 10 ADO, which is abundantly produced by most tumors, has long been known to mediate tumor-induced immune suppression through ADOR subtypes (particularly A2AR and A2BR) that trigger signaling which directly impairs the activity of cytolytic T cells 11 and promotes activity of regulatory T cells and/or immunosuppressive macrophages. 12 ADO has become a therapeutic target in cancer, and various strategies to block or impair its suppressive activity are currently being tested in the clinic 13 with some encouraging, but not yet clinically meaningful, outcomes. 14

Investigations of ADO production and consumption in the TME identified the CD39/CD73 ecto-nucleotidases localized on the surface of tumor cells as a major pathway of extracellular ADO production. 15 CD39 and CD73 are broadly expressed on tumor cells and catabolize extracellular ATP to extracellular ADO, with CD73 serving as the terminal gate-regulating enzyme. 16 In a hypoxic, hostile TME, tumor cells produce excessive numbers of extracellular vesicles (EVs) 17 carrying high levels of extracellular ADO and inducing brisk A2 AR/A2BR signaling. However, extracellular ADO has a brief half-life of seconds, 18 and its binding to ADORs depends on the immediate proximity of ADOR+ cells. It has not been determined how extracellular ADO can signal to distantly localized cells.

We previously reported that tumor-derived extracellular vesicles (TEX) carry CD39 and CD73 ecto-enzymes, and when supplied with extracellular ATP, produce extracellular ADO in vitro 19 and in vivo in tumor-bearing animal models. 20 TEX is a subset of EVs, many of which originate in the endocytic compartment of tumor cells. TEX molecular/genetic contents mimic those of parent cells, and they are considered to be miniature surrogates of tumor cells. TEX are enriched in immunosuppressive proteins, including the adenosine pathway components and often represent a substantial fraction of circulating total EVs. 21 TEX circulate freely in body fluids of cancer patients 22 and, as previously reported, can deliver CD39/CD73 or ADO to various immune and non-immune cells in the TME. 23 Upon entry, TEX drive the metabolic reprogramming of recipient cells, converting them into cells that become immunosuppressive and acquire capabilities to mediate pro-tumor activities. 23 Thus, TEX represent a highly effective mechanism cancers utilize for disseminating immune suppression and promoting tumor escape. 23 TEX carrying CD39/CD73 on the surface membrane and ADO in the lumen are enriched in the circulation of patients with metastatic cancers 23 and may be a major source of extracellular ADO that is protected by the EV membrane from degradation, contributing to cancer immune escape.

Considering the possibility that TEX may be a major mechanism of ADO transport and delivery in cancer, we have developed a highly sensitive, non-invasive assay for measuring activity of the extracellular adenosinergic pathway in EVs. 24 The assay evaluates metabolism - by ecto-nucleotidases residing on the external surface of EVs - of N6-etheno-labeled ATP (eATP) and N6-etheno-labeled AMP (eAMP) to “downstream” etheno (e)-labeled metabolites. 24 We previously showed that human melanoma cell-derived EVs (MTEX) metabolized eATP more robustly than non-malignant cell-derived EVs. 24 Further, blocking of CD73 activity by selective pharmacological inhibitors completely abolished eAMP metabolism to eADO by both cancer-cell-derived and non-malignant cell-derived EVs. 24 This suggested that targeting CD73 may be a highly effective strategy for eliminating eADO produced by ecto-nucleotidases located on the surface of EVs. 24 A comparison of adenosinergic activity of EVs from plasma of melanoma patients with EVs isolated from plasma of healthy donors (HDs) showed that melanoma patients metabolized eATP to eADP and eAMP to eADO with significantly greater efficiency. 25 Thus, the N6-etheno-purine assays, which measured activity of the eATP pathway, discriminated melanoma patients from HDs. 25 In aggregate, our results suggested that the metabolic activity of the EV subset produced by melanoma cells (i.e., MTEX) could potentially serve as a biomarker of melanoma presence and progression. In contrast, EVs produced by non-malignant cells, herein called Non-TEX or NTEX, are a subset of EVs present in the plasma of all human subjects. In melanoma patients’ plasma, the ratio of MTEX/NTEX varies broadly and may relate to disease activity. 21 We hypothesized that MTEX, which are enriched in patients with advanced melanoma, 25 have higher metabolic activity, which drives ADO production, than EVs derived from non-malignant cells. We evaluated a small cohort of patients with metastatic melanoma for adenosinergic activity in total plasma EVs and in the EV subsets, MTEX and NTEX, which were separated by immune capture of MTEX with anti-CSPG4 mAbs as previously described. 26 In contrast to the hypothesis entertained above, NTEXs have emerged as significant contributors to the adenosinergic activity of total EVs in the plasma of patients with melanoma. This finding suggests that not only MTEX but also all circulating EVs are ADO producers and contribute to elevated adenosinergic activity in the plasma of patients with melanoma.

Materials and methods

Patients and patient samples

Peripheral blood samples were obtained from patients with melanoma seen at the outpatient clinic of the Melanoma Program at the UPMC Hillman Cancer Center (Pittsburgh, PA, USA). Blood samples were collected, processed, and banked at the University of Pittsburgh Melanoma SPORE Bank. The study was conducted from January 2023 to December 2024. The study was approved by the University of Pittsburgh IRB (#19080226) as part of the UPMC Hillman Cancer Center Biobanking Protocol HCC 96-099. Signed consent forms were obtained from all subjects. The study was performed in accordance with principles stated in the Declaration of Helsinki. Specimens of 30 randomly selected patients with melanoma included 13 females and 17 males, with ages ranging from 32 to 85 y at blood draw. One 71-year-old male patient with recurrent disease was excluded from the study because the recovery of plasma EVs was not adequate. Thus, samples of 29 patients (13 females and 16 males) were included and analyzed. Twelve patients had not received any prior systemic therapy at blood draw, and 15 patients were actively receiving cancer-directed therapy. At diagnosis, which preceded phlebotomy for EVs by various time periods (range < 1 yr for 20 patients and 2–12yrs for the remaining 9), four patients were staged as T1N0M0, and all others had more advanced disease (STable 1). At the time of blood draw, 7 patients had no evidence of disease (NED) radiographically, whereas 22 patients had evidence of disease (referred to as metastatic disease in this manuscript), either due to progressive disease (PD) on prior therapy (n = 10), or due to recurrent disease from earlier-stage melanoma (n = 12).

EV isolation from plasma

Extracellular vesicles in plasma are a heterogeneous mix of vesicles of different sizes and cellular origins. 21 The isolation of EVs from plasma included ultrafiltration followed by size exclusion chromatography (SEC), as listed in the EV-TRACK ID = EV160007. This method is routinely used in our studies, yielding small EVs sized at 30–150 nm, which originate from the endocytic compartment of parental cells. 27 Briefly, one mL of precleared, ultrafiltered plasma was loaded on a 10 cm-long Sepharose 2B column and was eluted with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Individual 1 mL fractions were collected. Fraction #4 containing the bulk of non-aggregated morphologically intact small EVs was harvested, concentrated using 100,000 MWCO Vivaspin 500 centrifugal 450 concentrators (Sartorius Corporation), and evaluated for protein, vesicle size and number, and for selected protein content as shown in SFigure 1 and as previously reported. 26

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

TEM of EVs was performed at the University of Pittsburgh Center for Biologic Imaging. Freshly isolated EVs were placed on copper grids coated with 0.125% Formvar in chloroform and stained with 1% (v/v) uranyl acetate in ddH2O. The grids were examined via a JEM 1011 microscope, and EVs were visualized at mag × 100 K as previously described. 27

NanoSight measurements

The concentration and size distribution of EVs were measured by nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) using NanoSight 300 (Malvern, UK). The vesicles were diluted in ddH2O, and then the consecutive video images were captured and recorded. The captured videos were analyzed using NTA software, maintaining the screen gain and the detection threshold at 1 and 5, respectively. To determine the mean particle size/concentration in each sample, five consecutive measurements were obtained and averaged.

Protein concentration

The protein concentrations of the EVs were determined by using a BCA protein assay (Pierce Biotechnology, Rockford, IL, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Western blots

Following the concentration of the isolated sEVs, the vesicle aliquots were lysed with Laemmli sample buffer (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA) and separated using 12% SDS/PAGE gels. Each lane was loaded with 10 μg protein from the SEC fraction #4. After the proteins were transferred from the gels to the polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes, the proteins were detected using antibodies (Abs) specific for CD81 (Cell Signaling 56039, 1:500 dil.), ALIX (Thermo-Fisher, MA5-32773, 1:1,000 dil.), calnexin (Santa Cruz, sc-23954, 1:250 dil.), CD39 (Santa Cruz, sc-33558; 1:250 dil.), CD73 (Santa Cruz, sc-25063, 1:250 dil.), and TSG101 (Cell Signaling 72312, 1;500 dil.). Immunodetection by blotting showed the protein profile consistent with that of EVs: ALIX(+), TSG101(+), calnexin(-), CD73(+), CD39(+), and CD81(+).

Melanoma cell-derived EVs (MTEX) isolation from melanoma patients’ plasma

MTEX are a subset of EVs produced by melanoma cells. MTEX isolation from total plasma EVs was performed by immune capture using melanoma cell-specific monoclonal Abs provided by Dr. Soldano Ferrone’s laboratory. 26 The anti-CSPG4 mAb (clones 763.64 and 225.28) recognizes an epitope of CSPG4 (also known as the high molecular weight melanoma- associated antigen) present on melanoma cells but not on any other non-malignant cells or tissues except pericytes, as determined by immunostaining and as previously reported. 28 The immunocapture of MTEX and their separation from NTEX 28 included the biotinylated capture mAb (clone 763.64). MTEX captured by the biotinylated mAb were harvested on streptavidin-charged magnetic beads. The non-captured vesicles contained soluble NTEX derived from non-malignant cells. An aliquot of the non-captured vesicles (NTEX) was re-captured using biotinylated anti-CD63 mAb on streptavidin beads. The Ab concentrations and bead volumes are critical for successful immune capture, as described in reference. 26 Initially, both fractions, captured MTEX and re-captured NTEX, are evaluated for the presence of CSPG4+ EVs by on-bead flow cytometry. 26 Once the conditions for immune capture are established and flow cytometry results confirm the absence of CSPG4 + EVs in the NTEX fraction, the procedure can be applied to numerous samples with reproducible results. 26 Routinely, only the MTEX fractions contained CSPG4+ EVs (SFigure 2). The “soluble” EV fractions (total EVs and NTEX) were harvested, counted, and prepared for the analysis of ePurine metabolism by HPLC-FL.

Preparation of total EVs and NTEX for HPLC-FL analysis

MTEX were captured on beads, which partly interfered with high-pressure liquid chromatography (HPLC-FL) and thus could not be reproducibly evaluated. The adenosinergic activity of MTEX was indirectly determined as described in the Experimental Design below. The soluble fractions (total EVs and NTEX) were concentrated using Amicon ultra-filters (100,000 MWCO) and suspended in 1 mL of PBS for HPLC-FL. The samples were divided into 6 × 100 µL portions using siliconized tubes. The tubes were kept frozen at -80 °C. Prior to the assay, the six tubes were divided as follows: four for incubation with 100 µM etheno-ATP (eATP) for 3 hr at 37 °C and two for incubation with 100 µM etheno-AMP (eAMP) for 20 min at 37 °C.

Metabolism of N6-etheno-Purines (ePurines) by EVs

The isolated and counted EVs were incubated with 100 µL of PBS at 37 °C with either eATP or eAMP and with or without ecto-nucleotidase inhibitors. ePurines, including eATP, eADP, eAMP, and eADO, were obtained from the BioLog Life Science Institute (Hayward, CA, USA). As previously described, 24 high concentrations of eATP or eAMP (100 µmol/L) were employed to provide strong signals. After incubation, the samples were heat-inactivated at 95 °C for 90 sec to denature ecto-enzymes, centrifuged at 13,000 rpm at 4 °C and diluted 10-fold before analysis of eADP or eADO. The ePurines were quantified using high-pressure liquid chromatography with fluorescence detection (HPLC-FL) as previously described, 29 and the results were normalized to the number of EVs per 100 µL sample. We previously determined and reported the sensitivity (detection limit = 1 pmol injected on the column), precision (coefficient of variation ≤ 2%) and accuracy (excellent match between assay values versus known concentrations of standards) of this assay system. 24 Specificity was confirmed by demonstrating baseline separation of all chromatographic peaks generated from samples of a mixture of ePurines and from samples of medium conditioned by four different cell lines incubated with eATP. Moreover, we confirmed that: (1) ePurines are metabolized by ecto-nucleotidases with an efficiency like their corresponding natural substrates; (2) there is no “off-target” (non-nucleotidase-mediated) metabolism of ePurines; and (3) the metabolism of ePurines is restricted to the membrane surface (i.e., is not due to intracellular nucleotidases). 29

We have previously evaluated the selectivity of several commercially available ecto-nucleotidase inhibitors and found that L-p-bromotetramisole (L-p-BT; Santa Cruz Biotechnology) and PSB12379 (Tocris Biosciences) were highly selective and effective for inhibiting tissue nonspecific alkaline phosphatase (TNAP) and ecto-5’-nucleotidase (CD73), respectively; whereas, ARL67156 (Millipore Sigma) was highly effective and relatively selective for CD39. 29 POM-1 (Tocris Biosciences), a highly effective “broad-spectrum” ecto-nucleotidase inhibitor, substantially blocked the enzymatic activity of CD39 (ENTPD1), ENTPD2, ENTPD3, CD203a, and TNAP. 29

Experimental design for measuring adenosinergic activity in EVs

The experimental design involved the initial isolation from melanoma patients’ plasma of total plasma EVs. As total plasma EVs in melanoma patients contain MTEX and NTEX in variable proportions, 21 we separated them by immune capture with an anti-CSPG4 mAb into two EV fractions: CSPG4(+) MTEX captured on beads and soluble (CSPG4(-) NTEX. The soluble fractions (total EVs and NTEX) were counted by NTA and divided into aliquots (100 µL PBS) for assessment of eATP metabolism to eADP and eAMP metabolism to eADO. Because MTEX were captured on beads, could not be released from the beads, and the beads were found to interfere with EV activity in the metabolic assays, we were only able to directly measure adenosinergic activity in the total EVs and NTEX fractions, which contain EVs not bound to beads. Having first determined the metabolic activities of total EVs and NTEX by HPLC-FL, we estimated the adenosinergic activity of MTEX by subtracting the NTEX activity levels from the total EVs activity levels in every sample as described below. Importantly, the complete absence of MTEX in the soluble NTEX fractions after immune capture with the CSPG4 Ab was confirmed by flow cytometry, indicating that all CSPG4(+) MTEX in the sample were captured on beads.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using NCSS 2019 Statistical Software (NCSS, LLC, Kaysville, Utah) or SAS (SAS version 9.4, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). The data were analyzed with either a Student’s t-test, a Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test, a 2-factor analysis of variance (2F-ANOVA), or a repeated measures 2-factor analysis of variance (repeated measures 2F-ANOVA), as appropriate. Values at P < 0.05 were considered significant. GraphPad Prism (version 10.4.1, GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA) was used to create the graphs.

Results

The objective of this study was to compare eATP and eAMP metabolism of MTEX and NTEX isolated by immune capture with an anti-CSPG4 mAb from the plasma of patients with melanoma. The study hypothesis was that melanoma cell-derived EVs (MTEX) metabolize eATP and eAMP more efficiently than EVs derived from non-malignant cells (NTEX) and thereby account for most of eADO produced by vesicles in patients’ plasma.

Characteristics of total EVs isolated from patients’ plasma

The plasma of cancer patients contains a heterogeneous mix of EVs with distinct cellular origins and sizes. We used size exclusion chromatography (SEC) to isolate a subset of small EVs (30–150 nm), most of which originate from endocytic multivesicular bodies (MVB) in producer cells, but some may result from ‘budding” of the cell membrane. The characteristics of plasma-derived EVs are illustrated in SFigure 1. The SEC fraction #4 that we isolate, yields EVs with characteristics consistent with the designation of small EVs (size range: 85–150 nm). Western blots (WBs) of the recovered vesicles showed the presence of ALIX and the absence of Calnexin, confirming their endocytic origin. TEM indicated heterogeneity of the vesicle size. The recovery of plasma EVs varied broadly among the 29 patients included in the study, ranging from 3.0 × 1010 to 2.5 × 1011 EVs/mL plasma (30–97 µg protein/mL plasma). The number of EVs/mL plasma was generally higher in this cohort of melanoma patients than those in healthy donors’ plasma (0.4 × 109 to 9.2 × 1010/mL plasma; n = 30).

Next, an aliquot of the total EVs isolated from each patient’s plasma was designated for the metabolic assays, and the remainder was used for the separation of MTEX and NTEX by immune capture with anti-CSPG4 mAb. 26

Immunocapture of MTEX on beads and recovery of non-captured TEX (NTEX)

Upon immune capture, the recovered MTEX and NTEX fractions were evaluated for the expression levels of the CSPG4 epitope by on-bead flow cytometry (SFigure 2). Initially, the capture Ab concentration for optimal EV capture was established, and its separation efficiency was confirmed by flow cytometry: the MTEX fraction was enriched in CSPG4(+) EVs, and the NTEX fraction was consistently and reproducibly CSPG4(-). The latter confirms the complete capture of CSPG4(+) MTEX from plasma. Under the defined capture conditions, MTEX and NTEX had distinct surface protein profiles characterized by the absence of CSPG4(+) vesicles in the NTEX fraction, as also illustrated in another cohort of patients with melanoma previously evaluated in our laboratory. 21 The number of EVs recovered in the non-captured NTEX fraction varied between 4.0 × 1010 and 2.5 × 1011/100 µg protein for all 29 patients. The number of non-captured NTEX was counted and normalized to that of one mL of plasma for each patient sample. As MTEX sitting on beads could not be counted, their number was estimated by subtracting NTEX from the total plasma EVs in the SEC fraction #4. MTEX were estimated to represent between < 1% to 60% of total EVs in patients’ plasma.

Measurements of metabolism of N6-etheno-purines in total EVs and NTEX

The soluble vesicle fractions (total EVs and NTEX) were incubated with eATP ± inhibitors to measure eATP metabolism to eADP (which would include CD39 activity) or with eAMP ± inhibitor (PSB12379) to measure the conversion of eAMP to eADO (which would include CD73 activity). Figure 1 shows that the metabolism of eATP to eADP was significantly higher (P = 0.0041) in NTEX (panel 1A) than in paired total EVs (panel 1B). This unexpected result, which suggested that NTEX were enriched in eATP metabolizing activity relative to that of total EVs in plasma. ARL67156, a selective CD39 inhibitor, significantly (P < 0.000001) decreased the eATP metabolizing activity of NTEX and total EVs, indicating a major role for CD39. However, with both total EVs and NTEX, eATP metabolizing activity was also significantly reduced (P < 0.05) by L-p-BT, which targets tissue nonspecific alkaline phosphatase (TNAP). Interestingly, even POM-1, a “broad-spectrum” ecto-nucleotidase inhibitor, did not abolish eATP metabolizing activity, suggesting that multiple ecto-nucleotidases, other than CD39, potentially associated with these EVs are responsible for some of the elevated metabolic activity of NTEX.

L-p-BT is an inhibitor that targets tissue nonspecific alkaline phosphatase (TNAP); ARL67156 is a selective CD39 inhibitor; and POM-1 is a “broad-spectrum” ecto-nucleotidase inhibitor. Bars represent the mean values.

To directly evaluate the contribution of CD73 activity to the metabolism of eATP to eADO in total EVs and NTEX, the vesicles were incubated in the presence of eAMP, and the production of eADO was measured (Figure 2).

Figure 2 shows that the metabolism of eAMP to eADO was similar in NTEX compared with the total plasma EVs (panel A). Figure 2 also shows that PSB12379, a selective inhibitor of CD73, abolished the metabolism of eAMP in both NTEX and total EVs (panel B), indicating that the metabolism of eAMP in both NTEX and total EVs was mediated exclusively by CD73. Together with the data shown in Figure 1, these results do not appear to support our hypothesis that MTEX contained the bulk of adenosinergic activity in patients with melanoma. Consistently, the adenosinergic activity of NTEX, which does not contain MTEX, was equal to or greater than the activity of total EVs, which contain MTEX.

Although we could not directly measure eAMP metabolism to eADO in MTEX, as they were captured on beads, we indirectly calculated CD73 activity in MTEX for every patient by subtracting the NTEX activity levels from total EVs activity levels (where all activity levels were normalized to the number of EVs in each fraction). This approach allowed us to compare eAMP metabolism to eADO in MTEX versus NTEX and determine whether MTEX were the major source of immunosuppressive ADO in melanoma patients’ plasma.

As shown in Figure 3, eAMP to eADO metabolism tended to be lower in MTEX relative to NTEX (panel A); however, this difference was not statistically significant. Here, the negative values for MTEX in some patients indicated that the activity of NTEX was higher than the activity of total EVs, and the reduced CD73 activity in MTEX was mainly due to two outliers among the MTEX samples. As with NTEX and total EVs, PSB12379 abolished the activity of MTEX (panel B). These results suggest that CD73 activity, like CD39 activity, is enriched in NTEX. It appears that in patients with melanoma, both MTEX and NTEX participate in the metabolism of eATP and eAMP, and that the enzymatic activity of ecto-nucleotidases is variably enriched or reduced in NTEX and MTEX isolated by immune capture from total EVs.

Associations between ePurine metabolism in EVs and disease status

First, we determined whether the number of total EVs/mL of plasma in patients with NED differed from that in the plasma of patients with metastatic disease. As shown in SFigure 3, while the number of total EVs/mL of plasma tended to be higher in patients with metastatic disease than in those with NED, the difference was not statistically significant.

Next, to assess whether the differences in eAMP to eADO metabolizing activity (CD73 activity) between MTEX and NTEX were related to disease status, we calculated normalized MTEX minus normalized NTEX activity for all samples (results for each of the 29 patients, in addition to their disease status at blood draw, are listed in STable 2). This allowed us to subdivide the 29 melanoma patients into three groups: MTEX < NTEX (1st tertile), MTEX = NTEX (2nd tertile), or MTEX > NTEX (3rd tertile), i.e., patients with lower, similar, and higher eAMP metabolizing activity in MTEX versus NTEX (Figure 4A). The grouping of patients into three tertiles was based on mathematically calculated exact cutoffs for each tertile. Normality testing, which was performed with the 29 MTEX and 29 NTEX assay results from the 29 patients, indicated that the data were not normally distributed, as shown in Figure 4A.

In A, patients with melanoma (n = 29) were divided into tertiles based on calculated differences in eAMP to eADO activity levels (µmol/L of eADO/20 min/109 EVs) between MTEX and NTEX (Normalized MTEX – Normalized NTEX activity). In B, differences in eAMP to eADO activity levels (µmol/L of eADO/20 min/109 EVs) between MTEX and NTEX (Normalized MTEX – Normalized NTEX activity) are shown stratified by disease status (n = 29). The data in Figure 4A show that NED patients were distributed throughout all tertiles. That is, we observed no correlation with disease status. Notably, among the ten patients in the lowest tertile, there were two outliers with extremely reduced metabolism of eAMP to eADO in the MTEX versus NTEX. Figure 4B shows the same data stratified by disease status; no significant difference was observed between patients with NED and patients with metastatic disease.

Since the data indicated that both MTEX and NTEX participate in the metabolism of eATP and eAMP, we subsequently evaluated the relationship between ePurine metabolism in total EVs and disease status. Specifically, because not only the metabolizing activity per vesicle but also the total number of vesicles is expected to be critical, we assessed associations between disease status and the capacity of the EVs in 1 mL of plasma to metabolize eATP and eAMP. Figure 5A shows the 29 melanoma patients grouped into tertiles based on the eATP metabolizing activity (in part CD39 activity) of all EVs in 1 mL of plasma (results for each of the 29 patients, in addition to their disease status at blood draw, are listed in STable 3). No obvious correlation was observed: three of seven patients with NED were in the tertile with the lowest eATP metabolizing activity, while the other four patients with NED were assigned to the tertile with the highest eATP metabolizing activity. Similarly, no significant difference in eATP metabolizing activity was observed between patients with NED and patients with metastatic disease (Figure 5B). However, when the patients were grouped into tertiles based on the eAMP to eADO metabolizing activity of all EVs in 1 mL of plasma (i.e., CD73 activity), a correlation with disease status emerged (Figure 5C) (results for each of the 29 patients, in addition to their disease status at blood draw, are listed in STable 4). Here, most NED patients were in the 1st tertile (5–7), whereas all patients in the 3rd tertile had metastatic disease at the time of blood draw. Similarly, we observed a significant difference in eAMP metabolizing activity between patients with NED and patients with metastatic disease (Figure 5D). This association of NED with the lowest levels of CD73 activity, i.e., the low eADO metabolizing capacity of the EVs in 1 mL of plasma, is in agreement with the key role that CD73 is known to play as an ultimate “gate keeper” of the adenosinergic pathway operating in cells or EVs. 15 , 24

In D, the same data (eAMP metabolizing activity of the EVs in 1 mL of plasma) are shown stratified by disease status (n = 29). There was one patient (pt103, a patient with metastatic disease) with extremely high eAMP metabolizing activity compared to the other patients. The observed difference remained significant (P = 0.03) in a sensitivity analysis in which this patient was excluded. Dots represent the results for individual patients. Bars (in B and D) represent the mean values.

In aggregate, the data show that it is not the activity of MTEX alone or the activity per EV but rather the combination of both the number and activity of all EVs in plasma (MTEX and NTEX) that determines the levels of purinergic activity in patients with melanoma and appears to be clinically relevant.

Discussion

The current study tested the hypothesis that ecto-nucleotidases on the surface of MTEX are the major contributors to ADO metabolism in patients with melanoma. Testing this hypothesis requires isolation of EVs from patients’ plasma, separation of MTEX from NTEX, and the use of a highly sensitive assay for measuring purine metabolism in EVs, each of which we have previously demonstrated. 24 , 26 Importantly, we established and validated an assay for measuring the metabolism of N6-etheno-purines in EVs isolated from the plasma of cancer patients. 25 Here, we used this assay to measure eATP and eAMP metabolizing activity in EVs, expect that MTEX (per vesicle) has higher levels of eATP and eADO metabolizing activity than NTEX. We found that both MTEX and NTEX contributed to the sum of eATP and eAMP metabolizing activity mediated by EVs in melanoma patients’ plasma. The eATP metabolizing activity in EVs is partially mediated by CD39 and fully regulated by CD73. The CD73 activity in EVs appears to be clinically relevant, likely contributing to ADO-mediated immune suppression in melanoma 25 and appears to be clinically relevant, as it reflects the patients’ disease status.

The finding that NTEX had higher CD73 activity/vesicle than MTEX was unexpected, given the results we previously reported for MTEX from a melanoma cell line, which metabolized eATP to eADO more robustly than keratinocyte cell-derived EVs. 24 Also, relative to EVs isolated from the plasma of healthy donors, the EVs from melanoma patients’ plasma metabolized eATP to eADO with significantly greater efficiency. 25 Based on these data, we hypothesized that MTEX, present in elevated numbers in the plasma of patients with metastatic melanoma, were the major eADO producers. Having previously developed a method for immune capture of MTEX with anti-CSPG4, 21 we proceeded to separate MTEX from NTEX using EVs isolated from melanoma patients’ plasma. Our previous results showed that these two EV subsets were variably positive for CD39 and CD73 by on-bead flow cytometry and also differed in the expression levels of other immunoregulatory proteins. 24 , 25 The captured MTEX were significantly enriched in immuno-suppressive proteins and consistently mediated T cell apoptosis in functional assays, while NTEX had an immuno-stimulatory profile and mediated little or no apoptosis. 21 Taken together, our data indicated that the capture of EVs from the plasma of patients with melanoma with the CSPG4 mAb consistently and reliably yielded two phenotypically and functionally distinct EV populations, only one of which (i.e., MTEX) consisted of CSPG4+ vesicles derived from melanoma cells. This reproducible separation of the two EV subsets, in our hands, in part removed the bias due to incomplete EV subset separation and provided a rationale for the indirect estimation of adenosinergic activity in MTEX by subtracting the activity of NTEX from that of total EVs. However, this introduces the possibility of another bias in the indirect estimations of MTEX activity. The latter is normalized to the number of EVs in the total EV fraction and the number of EVs in the NTEX fraction. As NTA measurements do not discriminate between vesicles and particles, the possibility of bias in vesicle numbers exists. Finally, there is potential for interactions between MTEX and NTEX in the total EV fraction, which could lead to the situation where the adenosinergic activity of NTEX may exceed that mediated by MTEX in some patients with melanoma.

Considering the recently emerging data in the literature on the ability of tumor-derived EVs (TEX) in plasma to functionally reprogram normal cells in the tumor microenvironment, 30 , 31 we lean toward attributing higher CD73 activity/vesicle in NTEX than MTEX to metabolic reprogramming. Tumor cell-derived EVs (TEX) in cancer plasma can induce changes in the metabolic activity of recipient cells through horizontal transfer of information from the tumor to non-malignant or malignant cells. 30 , 31 Mechanistically, this process involves the transfer of genetic (miRs, RNA, and DNA), epigenetic, and non-genetic activation signals from EVs to the recipient cells. 32 , 33 Such an alteration of cellular metabolism in recipient cells results in the release of EVs that functionally resemble TEX. Although we did not investigate MTEX-driven reprogramming in this study, our earlier results provide evidence for the presence of enzymatically competent CD39/73 ectoenzymes in EVs from cancer and healthy donor plasma. 19 Horizontal transfer of these ectoenzymes to Treg enabled the recipient Treg to produce ADO. 19 Translating this scenario to the current study, we suggest that MTEX in melanoma plasma drives metabolic reprogramming by transferring enzymatically active ecto-nucleotidases to NTEX, which acquire the ability to produce ADO at levels that are equal to or exceed those of MTEX. It is likely that the extent of reprogramming and emergence of NTEX with high purinergic activity depends on the number of MTEX in the plasma. In melanoma patients with progressive disease, MTEX numbers increase, 21 as does their adenosinergic activity. 25 This suggests that reprogramming driven by MTEX is more efficient in progressive melanoma. We have previously shown that a hypoxic TME (which promotes EV production) 34 , 35 is associated with worse clinical outcomes in patients with advanced melanoma treated with ICI. 36 Hence, elevated levels of CD73 activity in total EVs, comprising MTEX and NTEX, may be a harbinger of disease progression and potentially of treatment resistance.

The potential role of purinergic activity as a biomarker of immunosuppression characterizing disease progression in melanoma has been considered. 21 , 26 , 37-39 Thus, it is important to determine whether measurements of eATP or eAMP metabolism by plasma EVs, as opposed to the assessment of adenosinergic activity in plasma, provide a more useful estimate of disease presence, progression, and response to therapy. Importantly, adenosinergic activity measured directly in plasma is greatly underestimated, as exogenous ADO production requires cellular signaling, and ADO itself has a half-life of seconds. 18 EVs carrying CD39 and CDCD73 provide a scaffold supporting purinergic activity. We previously showed that adenosinergic activity was significantly higher in EVs produced by cultured melanoma cells than by non-malignant keratinocytes. 24 Purinergic activity was higher in EVs isolated from melanoma patients’ plasma than in EVs from the plasma of HDs. 25 Here, we report that total purinergic activity in the plasma of melanoma plasma EVs reflects the metabolic activity of MTEX plus NTEX, the latter potentially due to cancer-mediated reprogramming. 30 , 40 Our findings greatly simplify the monitoring of purinergic activity in total EV present in patients’ plasma without EV separation into MTEX and NTEX. Equally important is the observation that the capability of EVs to metabolize eAMP to eADO, which associates with clinical outcomes, depends not only on the level of metabolic activity mediated by each EV but also on the total number of EVs per volume of plasma used for EV isolation. This finding simplifies monitoring of EV-based purinergic activity since all that it requires EV isolation from 1 mL of plasma, followed by incubation with eAMP and measurement of eADO.

Although the present study has limitations, including a small and heterogeneous patient cohort, the metabolic assay we have developed illustrates the EV contribution to liquid tumor biopsy, because it offers an opportunity for establishing a personal profile of purinergic signaling in every patient using only 1 ml of plasma. Changes in this profile can be readily monitored over time using 1 mL of plasma for obtaining EVs in numbers sufficient for the assessment of their purinergic activity. The number of EVs in the plasma of cancer patients is elevated, ranging from 109 to 1011 vesicles per 1 mL of plasma in patients with metastatic melanoma. This provides the rationale for a monitoring strategy that allows the determination of the purinergic activity of EVs as potentially promising biomarkers of melanoma-induced immune suppression. We are currently validating these findings in a completed neoadjuvant trial of melanoma (NCT02339324), and efforts for prospective validation are underway. This study provides important insight into the future utilization of the purinergic activity of EVs in patient plasma, both as a prognostic biomarker and potentially as a predictive biomarker of response to immunotherapy, and has implications for diseases beyond melanoma.
