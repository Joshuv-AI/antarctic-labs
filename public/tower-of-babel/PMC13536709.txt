Temporal patterns and risk factors of recurrent influenza infection: a population-based epidemiological study
Authors: Lijun Zhou, Zhirui Li, Xingyu Zhou, Shuang Dong, Yao Ma, Biao Yang, Fengmiao Hu, Xiuwei Cheng, Chongkun Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe continuous mutation and seasonal dominance of influenza viruses subject people to the danger of repeated infection. Nonetheless, comprehensive studies on the temporal patterns and risk differentials of influenza reinfection at the population level are few.MethodsThe study analyzed surveillance data from 2,834,112 influenza cases in Sichuan Province from 2023 to 2025. Follow-up continued until week 156 after infection. The Kaplan–Meier method measured cumulative reinfection incidence, whereas the Andersen–Gill count process model examined different factors on reinfection risk. Reinfection temporal distribution by age group was characterized using kernel density estimation.ResultsThe Kaplan–Meier curves showed a progressive increase in reinfection risk every 52 weeks, reaching 5.5% at 156 weeks. The log-rank test showed a substantial correlation between gender, age, occupation, residence, and case classification and reinfection likelihood (P < 0.001). A multivariate Andersen-Gill analysis showed that people aged 0–5 and 6–19 had a considerably higher reinfection risk than those aged 20–45 and ≥ 61 years. Kernel density analysis showed that reinfection occurred between 9 and 12 months after the original infection, with children having the highest prevalence and adults having a bimodal distribution.ConclusionInfluenza reinfection risk varies annually, notably in children, adolescents, and high-exposure occupational groups. Significant disparities in risk among groups suggest that age and sociodemographic variables affect influenza immune protection and exposure patterns. Epidemiological data from these studies can improve stratified immunization programs and focused prevention efforts for key groups.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28157-8.

Introduction

Influenza is an acute respiratory infection caused by viruses from the Orthomyxoviridae family [1]. Global surveillance data from the World Health Organization (WHO) indicates that seasonal influenza leads to approximately 3 to 5 million severe cases and 290,000 to 650,000 deaths related to respiratory diseases each year, positioning it as a major public health threat worldwide [2]. Influenza viruses are chiefly categorized into four types: A, B, C, and D. Influenza A and B viruses are the primary pathogens responsible for seasonal epidemics in human populations [3]. Influenza viruses disseminate mostly via respiratory droplets and aerosols produced by the coughing or sneezing of infected persons [4]. The segmented genome of influenza viruses enables the fast generation of novel varieties via antigenic drift and antigenic shift, resulting in considerable transmissibility and the capacity to instigate epidemics in densely populated areas [5].

Influenza virus infection can elicit both humoral and cellular immune responses [6]. Nonetheless, antigenic drift in influenza viruses largely reduces previously acquired immune protection. Immunity obtained from previous illness or vaccination is sometimes challenging to maintain for an extended period, generally enduring for around six months [7]. Consequently, influenza demonstrates considerable morbidity and recurring infection characteristics as an epidemiological phenomenon. Additionally, in seasonal epidemic situations, the likelihood of reinfection among populations may display cyclical patterns [8]. However, comprehensive investigations utilizing extensive real-world data are still absent to ascertain if these patterns change among populations with differing features. In contrast to certain viruses that provide enduring protection, recurrent influenza infections are remarkably prevalent. This not only exposes individuals to various health risks and possible consequences across their lifetimes but also results in prolonged utilization of healthcare resources and considerable economical losses [9, 10]. Conventional epidemiological surveillance generally documents data pertaining to individual infection occurrences, with restricted monitoring of temporal fluctuations at the person level. The emphasis has primarily been on influenza incidence, hospitalization rates, and death, but systematic investigation of reinfection is rather limited [11]. In actual communities, people may endure several influenza illnesses throughout many epidemic seasons. For instance, children, with immature immune systems and frequent exposure in crowded environments like schools and daycare facilities, are typically seen as primary contributors to influenza transmission [12]. Although children have elevated rates of influenza incidence, there is a paucity of information about the intervals and temporal distribution features of their reinfection. In individuals with generally stable immune systems, the likelihood of reinfection may display unique patterns shaped by occupational exposure and social interaction networks [13]. Older persons, impacted by immunosenescence and chronic diseases, may demonstrate diminished responses to initial infections and an increased susceptibility to reinfection within shorter intervals [14].

In contrast to original infection, reinfection demonstrates the cumulative impact of diminishing immune defense, virus mutation, and ongoing exposure risk [13]. It is crucial for comprehending influenza immunodynamics and enhancing preventative and control techniques. Examining the temporal distribution patterns of influenza reinfection at the individual level, alongside population changes, facilitates the understanding of the long-term dynamics of influenza transmission and offers data support for targeted preventive and control measures. The advancement of electronic monitoring systems has resulted in the availability of extensive case data for individual-level tracking, hence aiding reinfection research [15]. This research employs individual-level influenza case data to compute the time intervals between successive infections, examine temporal distribution patterns among various groups, and investigate their possible epidemiological ramifications. This study elucidates temporal clustering patterns and demographic variances in reinfection to enhance comprehension of influenza immunity duration, refine vaccination techniques, and formulate tiered control measures.

Methods

Data source

The data for this investigation were obtained from the Chinese Influenza Surveillance Information System. Influenza surveillance data for the years 2023–2025 were documented by healthcare professionals in compliance with the National Influenza Surveillance Technical Guidelines [16]. The data included: gender, age, employment, diagnostic category, residence, and date of onset. Laboratory-confirmed influenza cases are defined as those identified via nucleic acid testing, viral isolation, or antigen detection as illnesses caused by the influenza virus. Clinically diagnosed influenza refers to instances that fulfill the clinical criteria for influenza-like illness (ILI), namely fever (body temperature ≥ 38 °C) accompanied by cough or sore throat [17]. Both categories of cases were incorporated into our analysis.

Study design

The date of each subject’s initial influenza onset functioned as the follow-up baseline (time zero). Subjects entered the risk cohort at first infection and continued under continuous observation until a reinfection episode transpired or follow-up completed. Reinfection was characterized by an interval of ≥ 14 days between two influenza onset reports linked to the same valid identification number; periods less than 14 days were regarded as part of the same infection occurrence and not classified as reinfection. For each participant, only the initial reinfection episode was documented as the outcome for survival analysis; individuals without reinfection were right-censored at the date of their last accessible influenza-related record. The follow-up time was measured in weeks, with a maximum term of 156 weeks. This created a data structure with the first infection as the starting point and the first reinfection as the endpoint for further research.

Kaplan–Meier cumulative incidence curves were constructed to examine reinfection risk variations across populations based on sex, age group, occupational category, residential area type, and initial infection case categorization [18]. Cumulative reinfection rates were assessed at critical intervals (weeks 0, 26, 52, 78, 104, 130, and 156) to elucidate variations in reinfection risk over several temporal scales subsequent to the original infection. Intergroup differences were analyzed using the two-sided log-rank test, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Given that participants may undergo several bouts of influenza reinfection during follow-up, the Andersen–Gill (AG) model was utilized to analyze recurrent reinfection occurrences [19]. The Andersen–Gill model enhances the Cox proportional hazards model by employing a count process framework, allowing for multiple event periods per subject, making it appropriate for recurrent event data. In the AG model, the follow-up duration for each patient was divided into successive risk intervals, each signifying a possible exposure to the risk of reinfection. The model utilized reinfection occurrences as the dependent variable, integrating several demographic data as covariates to assess the relationships between these parameters and the probability of reinfection. Model outcomes are reported as hazard ratios (HR) accompanied with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Robust variance estimation is utilized to get parameter estimates that are unaffected by intra-subject correlations, addressing event-related dependencies within individuals. Sensitivity studies for first reinfection were undertaken using Cox proportional hazards models to assess the robustness of recurrence model outputs. We evaluated the consistency of risk factor impact estimates by comparing the results of the Cox model with those of the Andersen–Gill model, so strengthening the robustness of our conclusions.

To clarify the relationship between influenza reinfection and the overall intensity of community transmission, along with viral subtype replacement, we quantified the weekly reinfection cases and integrated the weekly laboratory-confirmed positivity rates for A(H1N1)pdm09, A(H3N2), and influenza B (Flu B) throughout the province to create a time-series dataset. We employed Pearson’s correlation coefficient to evaluate the impact of the epidemic intensity of various virus subtypes on the incidence of reinfection.

Furthermore, we utilised kernel density estimation (KDE) to smooth the distribution of reinfection intervals across different age groups [20]. A Gaussian kernel function was employed for all point distribution estimates. To determine the optimal bandwidth, we applied Scott’s rule of thumb, multiplied by an adjustment factor of 0.8 to mitigate oversmoothing and improve the resolution of density peaks within early reinfection intervals. To ensure boundary conditions, all distributions were explicitly restricted to the range of 0 to 30 months; the densities for each group were independently standardised. The complete analysis code repository is provided as supplementary material (Supplementary Material 2).

Statistical analysis

This study used Visual Studio Code (Python 3.12.3) for statistical analysis, employing software tools such as pandas, NumPy, matplotlib, lifelines, and stats models. The threshold for statistical significance was established at p < 0.05. The complete analysis code repository is provided as supplementary material (Supplementary Material 2).

Results

Table 1 indicates the baseline features of the 2,834,112 influenza cases incorporated in this investigation. A significant majority of participants (N = 2,736,364, 96.6%) experienced only a single bout of influenza. A total of 97,748 people underwent reinfection, resulting in an overall reinfection rate of 3.4%. Of them, 94,134 individuals (3.3%) had one reinfection, whereas 3,614 (0.1%) suffered two or more reinfections. The distribution of influenza episode counts varied considerably among demographic groupings (P < 0.001). Males included a somewhat greater percentage of the reinfection group compared to females. In the group having two episodes, males represented 54.5%, surpassing their overall proportion in the cohort (51.0%); this male preponderance intensified with higher episode frequency (58.3% for three episodes and 60.3% for three or more episodes). The 0–5 age demographic represented 23.8% of the overall cohort, rising to 39.7% in the two-episode group and 37.4% in the three-episode group. The median age at first infection was 10.0 years (IQR: 6.0–28.0), but the median age for those undergoing reinfection (two or three episodes) dramatically fell to 6.0 years (IQR: 4.0–9.0). While persons over 61 years represented a diminished percentage in two re-infections compared to baseline (4.8% vs. 9.4%), they comprised 44.0% of the limited cohort having numerous frequent re-infections (more than 3 episodes). Children of preschool age constituted the demographic at greatest risk for re-infection. Urban patients represented 65.2% of the overall cohort, increasing to 71.4% in the two-reinfection group and further rising to 73.5% in the three-reinfection group. Marked disparities in case diagnostic types were noted among various recurrence frequency groups (X²=5,234.1, P < 0.001), with clinical diagnosis cases exhibiting an upward trend in the multiple recurrence group.

The Kaplan–Meier overall cumulative incidence curve exhibits a pronounced cyclical, stepwise escalation in the population risk of influenza reinfection (Fig. 1). In the first 26 weeks of follow-up, the risk of reinfection was minimal, with a total frequency of only 0.9%. Subsequently, the initial quick increase transpired between weeks 30 and 52, elevating the cumulative incidence to 4.2%, followed by a second peak between weeks 78 and 104, eventually stabilizing the cumulative incidence at roughly 5.5%. The occurrence of infection peaks about every 52 weeks corresponds well with the yearly seasonal circulation patterns of influenza viruses in nature. Results from the log-rank test demonstrated that gender, age, employment, site of residence, and case categorization significantly influenced the probability of reinfection (P < 0.001). Dispersed children and the 0–5 age group had the highest reinfection risk, with cumulative incidence rates at 156 weeks reaching 11.15% and 9.31% respectively, far surpassing other cohorts. To mitigate the confounding influence of elevated incidence rates among minors on the occupational risk assessment, this study performed a subgroup analysis concentrating on the adult workforce (excluding Preschool-aged children and students). The findings revealed that notable disparities in cumulative incidence rates persisted across various occupations, with medical personnel exhibiting the highest 156-week cumulative incidence rate of 3.20%, significantly surpassing that of the reference groups, farmers (1.66%) and teachers (1.54%) (Fig S1). Males (5.96%) shown a somewhat elevated reinfection risk compared to females (5.13%), but urban regions (6.00%) reported a greater cumulative incidence than rural regions (4.66%). Moreover, individuals with clinically diagnosed initial infections (6.55%) had a greater risk of reinfection compared to those with laboratory-confirmed initial infections (5.22%).

The analysis of recurrent episodes utilizing the Andersen–Gill model indicated that, following the adjustment for various variables, many epidemiological parameters were strongly correlated with the likelihood of influenza reinfection (P < 0.001) (Table 2). Age was identified as the most significant predictor of reinfection: babies aged 0–5 years and adolescents aged 6–19 years had substantially increased reinfection risks compared to the 20–45 age group, with adjusted hazard ratios (aHR) of 6.34 (95% CI: 6.10–6.59) and 6.03 (95% CI: 5.77–6.30), respectively. Furthermore, persons above the age of 61 had a significantly increased risk of reinfection (aHR = 3.22, 95% CI: 3.06–3.38). In terms of occupational categories, healthcare professionals shown a significantly elevated risk of reinfection exposure (aHR = 3.03, 95% CI: 2.68–3.42), whereas children residing in dispersed communities faced an adjusted reinfection risk 1.60 times greater than that of the reference group. The unadjusted HR for students was 2.468 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 2.387–2.552); however, after controlling for factors such as age, the aHR diminished to 0.848 (95% CI: 0.799–0.900). Conversely, the unadjusted hazard ratio (HR) for teachers was 0.679 (95% CI: 0.603–0.766), but the multivariable-adjusted aHR markedly rose to 1.33 (95% CI: 1.164–1.521). Subsequent findings from the AG model concerning the employed adult demographic indicated that, after accounting for the confounding influence of the non-employed population, the exposure risk among healthcare professionals was notably elevated, with an unadjusted HR of 1.671 (95% CI: 1.520–1.836) and a multivariable aHR reaching 3.221 (95% CI: 2.845–3.647). Despite the unadjusted HR for the teaching cohort being merely 0.716, the aHR significantly increased to 1.451 (95% CI: 1.266–1.663) upon adjustment for covariates, thereby reinforcing the notion that, after accounting for demographic factors, education and healthcare-related professions present a greater independent risk of re-exposure (Table S1). Residence and gender showed statistical significance: urban inhabitants had a 21.9% increased risk of reinfection compared to rural residents (aHR = 1.219, 95% CI: 1.198–1.241), and men were at a greater risk of influenza reinfection than females. Sensitivity analysis employing Cox proportional hazards models produced findings that closely aligned with the Andersen–Gill model, therefore confirming the reliability of the previously established risk factors.

Instances of recurrent influenza infection demonstrated a notable synchronisation with the overall epidemic severity within the community, exhibiting a positive correlation between the weekly total case count and the frequency of repeated infection cases (r = 0.616, P = 1.1167e-17) (Table S2). There was significant variability in how the prevalence levels of various viral subtypes influenced recurrent infections regarding pathogen makeup. The positive rate for influenza B had the most robust link with reinfection (r = 0.542, P = 2.6500e-13), whereas the correlation coefficients for A(H3N2) and A(H1N1) were 0.297 and 0.255, respectively (P < 0.01).

The kernel density distribution indicates that the reinfection interval among children is more concentrated, whereas the adult population displays a bimodal fluctuation pattern (Fig. 2). The senior group has a more varied distribution with a comparatively greater incidence of early reinfection. The principal peak for all three groups transpires within the 9–12 month range. The 0–5 age group displays the largest peak with a concentrated distribution, indicating a notable clustering of reinfection around one year. The 6–60 age group displayed a secondary peak between 20 and 22 months in addition to the initial peak, indicating a bimodal distribution. The ≥ 61 age group had a reasonably stable overall density, sustaining a consistent level in the early post-infection phase until attaining a primary peak approximately in the 10th month, thereafter followed by a progressive decrease.

Discussion

This study, utilizing extensive follow-up data, thoroughly elucidates the temporal dynamics of influenza reinfection risk. The incremental pattern shown in the Kaplan–Meier curves illustrates a strong correlation between reinfection risk and the seasonal influenza epidemic cycle. Data reveal that peaks of reinfection mostly occur at about weeks 52 and 104 post-initial infection, with the risk of reinfection stabilizing throughout the intervening non-epidemic periods. The minimal cumulative incidence over the initial 26 weeks of follow-up indicates that short-term protective immunity endures following original infection. Antibody titers stay high for several months following infection before experiencing a steady physiological drop. As titers diminish, individuals return to a vulnerable condition [21]. The risk rises swiftly between 30 and 52 weeks following the first infection, then experiencing a second peak during the subsequent yearly cycle (78 to 104 weeks). This trend indicates that cross-protection between viral subtypes is limited, and that prior immunity is insufficient to fully suppress newly emerging variants [22]. Ongoing antigenic drift in influenza viruses may reduce the cross-protective efficacy of previously acquired immunity, making populations vulnerable again in succeeding epidemic seasons [23]. Thus, the noted incremental rise signifies both the inherent phenomenon of immunological decline and the synergistic impacts of viral mutation and varying epidemic severity. This temporal trend offers empirical evidence for enhancing vaccination regimens from a public health standpoint. Vaccination is essential, and the timing should closely coincide with the period before the epidemic peak to mitigate the risk associated with diminishing immune protection over time.

At the risk factor level, both the Log-rank test and Andersen–Gill model consistently indicated that several demographic characteristics were significantly correlated with the probability of reinfection, with age being the most influential variable. Children aged 0–5 years and teenagers aged 6–19 years demonstrated substantially greater reinfection risks compared to those aged 20–45 years, with notably increased cumulative incidence rates over 156 weeks. This indicates that younger age cohorts may possess transient immunity owing to underdeveloped immune systems or increased exposure rates in densely populated environments like schools and daycare centers [24]. Individuals aged ≥ 61 years demonstrated an increased risk of reinfection, indicating that immunosenescence and pre-existing health conditions may reduce enduring post-infection immunity [14]. Prior studies have also demonstrated that older individuals exhibit diminished responses to influenza infection and experience comparatively reduced vaccination effectiveness, making them vulnerable to reinfection or catastrophic consequences beyond the conclusion of the infection season — results that align with our research [25]. An examination of occupational categories highlights the significant impact of exposure settings on the probability of reinfection. A sensitivity analysis targeting the adult workforce affirmed the pivotal influence of occupational exposure on reinfection; upon excluding the younger demographic (students and children residing in the community) to mitigate confounding variables, the adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) for healthcare workers increased to 3.221 (95% CI: 2.845–3.647), indicating a greater risk than the 3.03 noted in the overall population model, despite this group generally exhibiting a heightened level of hygiene awareness. Healthcare workers demonstrated a markedly elevated risk of reinfection following multivariable adjustment, indicating heightened exposure resulting from continuous interaction with patients and high-risk environments [26]. The advise on influenza vaccination and infection control for healthcare workers emphasizes their heightened occupational risk, advocating for increased immunization rates and rigorous infection control protocols to reduce transmission. The increased incidence of reinfection among teachers, post-adjustment, may indicate the intrinsic promotion of viral transmission in the densely populated environment of schools, even correcting for age and other confounding variables [27]. Conversely, students demonstrated increased risk in the uncorrected model, although revealed decreased risk when controlling for factors such as age, indicating that age is the principal factor influencing reinfection risk in this cohort [28]. Residential disparities similarly illustrate the impact of social structure and epidemic dynamics on the probability of reinfection. Research reveals that urban inhabitants are at an elevated risk of reinfection compared to their rural counterparts, likely associated with increased population density, more frequent social contacts, and heightened epidemic severity in urban environments [29]. Moreover, while gender disparities are minimal, males have a marginally elevated risk of reinfection compared to females, potentially linked to behavioral patterns, occupational exposure, and variations in health-protective behaviors [30]. Individuals with clinically identified first infections demonstrated a greater risk of reinfection compared to those with laboratory-confirmed illnesses. This may suggest confounding by other respiratory infections in clinically identified patients or imply that varying diagnostic criteria affected illness identification and care during follow-up [31]. Moreover, laboratory-confirmed cases often receive more standardized illness care and immunization recommendations, which may potentially diminish the risk of recurrent reinfection to a certain degree [32]. The sensitivity analysis findings from the Cox model shown strong concordance with the Andersen–Gill model, hence reinforcing the reliability of risk estimations. This study demonstrated substantial yearly cyclical patterns in influenza reinfection and highlighted younger age groups, high-exposure jobs, and urban inhabitants as primary high-risk populations [33]. This highlights the necessity of thoroughly considering demographic composition and variations in socioeconomic attributes when developing influenza preventive and control methods, hence implementing more nuanced, stratified immunization and intervention techniques. This study further establishes that recurrent influenza infections are not arbitrary occurrences, but an epidemiological phenomenon influenced by the general intensity of viral transmission throughout the population and subtype replacement. The significant correlation coefficient of 0.6161 between the total number of cases and reinfection clearly indicates that population exposure risk is a crucial determinant for reinfection. The pronounced correlation with influenza B indicates that this subtype may exhibit distinct immune evasion mechanisms or antigenic drift effects in facilitating reinfection, demonstrating a greater capacity to circumvent the population’s immune defences compared to influenza A subtypes in the context of reinfection.

The kernel density estimates findings indicate a significant yearly cyclical pattern in the time of influenza reinfections. The principal peaks for all three demographic groups converge around one year, aligning with the seasonal epidemiology of influenza viruses and the decline of post-infection immune protection over time. The most elevated and concentrated peak seen in the pediatric group indicates that immunological persistence after infection may be briefer in this cohort, alongside an increased exposure risk due to frequent close-contact environments [34]. The adult group displayed a bimodal pattern, possibly indicating cumulative impacts from many epidemic seasons or interannual susceptibility fluctuations resulting from viral antigenic drift [35]. The older group had a specific reinfection rate during brief intervals, indicating that diminished immune function may lead to decreased protective effectiveness or a shorter duration of immunity following infection, resulting in reinfection among particular individuals within a limited timeframe [36]. From a public health standpoint, our data highlight that the risk of reinfection is not evenly distributed but markedly increased during particular post-infection time intervals. This substantiates the need for yearly vaccination regimens and suggests possible differences in immunological persistence and epidemiological traits among age groups. Vaccination programs, protection of critical groups, and epidemic surveillance should incorporate targeted approaches.

A bias in the definition of “primary infection” has resulted from the inability of this study to acquire individual infection data prior to the commencement of the observation period. For adolescents and adults, the initial episode documented in the study may really signify a recurring infection throughout their lives, therefore elucidating the age distribution features of primary infections within the adult cohort. Conversely, for the 0–5 age range, especially newborns and young children, the initial occurrence documented during follow-up is likely to signify a true ‘first-ever exposure.’ This group, being immunologically naïve and devoid of preexisting immune memory, has a statistically significant risk of reinfection that exactly mirrors the epidemiological trends of the influenza virus in uninfected populations. In contrast, the reduced reinfection incidence in adults indicates the protective influence of pre-existing immunity developed during years of previous infections. The findings from the 0–5 age range in this study offer significant insights into the natural epidemiology of influenza viruses in people devoid of immunological protection.

This work, though employing a substantial dataset for a comprehensive evaluation of influenza reinfection risk, has the following drawbacks. Initially, our data is based on clinical diagnoses and laboratory-verified records. Due to the self-limiting characteristics of influenza, several mild patients may not have pursued medical care or undertaken particular testing during their consultations [37]. This selective bias in healthcare-seeking behavior may result in an underappreciation of the total cumulative incidence of reinfection. Secondly, limited by data availability, this study was unable to include individuals’ influenza vaccination histories in the analytical model. The vaccination status is a crucial element affecting reinfection risk; neglecting to account for this variable may undermine the precision of estimates for occupational risk groups like healthcare workers or teachers. Moreover, possible confounding variables, including patients’ comorbidities, household demographic composition, and socioeconomic status, were not well accounted for. The lack of viral genome sequencing data prevents definitive scientific distinction between chronic infection of the same subtype and reinfection by a new subtype. Given that the study sample predominantly derives from a particular regional surveillance system, prudence is advised when generalizing results to other regions with varying healthcare resource allocation, climatic conditions, or population immunity profiles. Future research will combine viral genotyping, serological monitoring, and vaccination data to clarify the immunological processes of reinfection and the enduring effects of antigenic drift on population susceptibility. This will yield more accurate information for optimizing immunization efforts and epidemic control methods.

Conclusion

This research, utilizing extensive population data, elucidates the temporal dynamics of influenza reinfection and its differences across different populations. It enhances our comprehension of influenza reinfection patterns at both temporal and risk-structural dimensions. It offers essential epidemiological data for enhancing yearly immunization plans, pinpointing priority intervention groups, and formulating tiered public health management actions. Future control initiatives should transition from a unique age-based methodology to precision treatments aimed at high-exposure jobs and densely populated metropolitan areas. Enhancing laboratory diagnostic coverage and vaccination rates among critical groups can generate a stronger population-level immunological barrier.
