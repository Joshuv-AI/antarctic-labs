1 Introduction
1.1 Project Background

The Los Alamos Neutron Science Center (LANSCE) is located at Technical Area 53 (TA-53) at Los
Alamos National Laboratory (LANL) in Los Alamos, New Mexico. LANSCE is driven by an 800-
megaelectronvolt (MeV) proton accelerator that delivered its first beam in 1972. The LANSCE accelerator
is unique in that it accelerates both H– (to full energy of 800 MeV) and H+ ions (up to 100 MeV currently
but has accelerated high-power H+ beam to 800 MeV in the past) and supports five separate experimental
areas that operate simultaneously, with each having different timing and beam current requirements. Many
of the LANSCE accelerator front-end components date back to original commissioning in 1972, including
the ion sources, Cockcroft-Walton (CW) generators, and the Drift Tube LINAC (DTL), which accelerates
the beam up to 100 MeV.

The purpose of the LANSCE Accelerator Modernization Project (LAMP) is to replace and modernize
multiple accelerator systems and components to improve beam delivery, reliability, and availability. LAMP
modernization will improve system maintainability and prepare the facility for operation through 2050. The
scope of modernization currently includes the LANSCE front end (up to 100 MeV). This scope includes
modernizing the two ion sources, replacing the two CW generators with a modern radio frequency
quadrupole (RFQ), and replacing the DTL which is exhibiting severe degradation and has significantly
exceeded its expected lifespan. Modernization of accelerator systems beyond the 100 MeV beam energy
are not within the currently approved LAMP scope; however, end-to-end modeling through the entire
accelerator including the proton storage ring (PSR) will need to be performed to verify the project’s
performance requirements for all five experimental areas.

LAMP will be executed as a congressional line-item project under U.S. Department of Energy Order 413.3b
[1] for the National Nuclear Security Administration (NNSA). The design will follow the Program
Requirements Document (PRD) for LAMP [2] and other requirements that flow down from the PRD.

1.2 Purpose

This document is the governing procedure for Design Reviews for the LANSCE Accelerator Modernization
Project, or LAMP (herein “Project”). Complementing information is available in LAMP-SE-PLAN-001;
Systems Engineering Management Plan (SEMP) [3] and LAMP-ENG-PLAN-001 Design Management
Plan (DMP) [4]. This procedure is developed in alignment with DOE O 413.3B [1], which governs project
management for the acquisition of capital assets. It ensures design maturity is achieved and documented at
key Critical Decision (CD) milestones.

The purpose of a Design Review is to conduct a review of design concepts at appropriate maturity stages
to ensure that the design meets its requirements, and to identify and address any potential issues or
challenges before the design product is realized. The objective is to ensure systems, structures, and
components (SSCs), as well as the overall design, are sufficiently mature to meet project requirements and
outcomes, thus fulfilling the Project’s mission need. To attain a high standard of design excellence, the
Technical Director, Facility Design Authority Representative (FDAR), Technical Leads, and Design Team
members are encouraged and expected to seek out and embrace rigorous design reviews with
knowledgeable and experienced reviewers either internal and/or external to the project.

Design reviews should use a formalized, structured approach to ensure that the reviews are comprehensive,
objective and documented. A review committee consists of technically knowledgeable personnel
sufficiently independent of the design being reviewed (e.g., have not been involved in the creation or review

Review committee members should have demonstrated technical expertise in one or more areas relevant to

Design reviews (including constructability reviews, where appropriate) are a vital component of the design

1.3 Document Scope

This document outlines the project’s process for implementing the requirements of DOE O 413.3B,
specifically the associated Critical Decision (CD) stages and design review requirements (see Section 3:
DOE Critical Decision Design Review Stages). The project design will be considered sufficiently mature
when the project has developed a cost estimate, and all relevant organizations have a high degree of
confidence that it will endure to project completion. In determining the sufficiency of the design level,
factors such as project size, duration and complexity are considered. Note that for complex scientific
systems such as accelerators a linear design process in which all subsystems reach the same maturity at the
same time may not be practical. Projects that have several subsystems may have separate preliminary and
final design stages for different subsystems, or procurement-focused reviews to enable early execution of
long-lead procurements. Consequently, preliminary and final designs may be completed at various points
in time during the system development process. Design maturity status and review results will inform cost
and schedule baselines and are integrated with the project’s Earned Value Management System (EVMS)
per DOE O 413.3B requirements [1].

In addition, this document provides guidance for independent technical reviews initiated by the project’s
Technical Leads, Technical Director, or FDAR (see Section 4: Technical Reviews). These reviews are not
intended to replace the formal DOE Critical Decision review process. Rather, they are intended to
complement it when deemed appropriate by the Technical Leads, Technical Director or FDAR. Such
reviews may be more focused, limited in scope, and have different requirements e.g. for reviewer
independence, compared to the formal Critical Decision Design Reviews.

Both types of reviews aim to advance design maturity to a sufficient level prior to establishing the
performance baseline at CD milestones [1]. This procedure will serve as a reference for the LAMP project
team to develop requirements and designs for LAMP. The scope of this procedure is limited to the
programmatic side of the project, i.e. execution of facility work will follow established LANL processes as
appropriate.

3 DOE’s Critical Decision (CD) Design Review Stages
DOE’s Critical Decision design reviews shall be conducted with reviewers external to the project to
document the completion of conceptual, preliminary, and final design stages. An independent technical
review of a design typically involves several stages, starting with establishing system requirements and
ending with a final design ready for fabrication and commissioning. The respective design maturity levels
are as follows:
  System Requirements Review (SRR): 5% design maturity
  Conceptual Design Review (CDR): 30% design maturity
  Preliminary Design Review (PDR): 60% design maturity
  Final Design Review (FDR): 90% design maturity
In the context of the Project, the SRR is documented in the Program Requirements Document (PRD) for
LAMP [2] which defines the top-level performance and functional requirements for the Project
deliverables. Note that the PRD was informed by the document LA-UR-23-22164: The LANSCE
Experimental Program: Accelerator Requirements [8]. The System Requirements in the PRD are the Level
0 requirements for LAMP project. As the design matures, level 0 requirements are further developed (i.e.
decomposed, or “flowed down” into more detailed requirements for subsystems and components, level 1,
2, etc), and reviewed periodically throughout the project execution. All requirements are reviewed side by
side with the design at every major design review.

Complete design submittals are required at completion of established design stages; design and technical
reviews shall then be performed. There shall also be a back-check review at design completion to verify
that all comments made during the previous design review stage have been addressed. Figure 1 shows the
timeline for the DOE-defined Critical Decision (CD) milestones while Table 2 illustrates the design
maturity levels per CD milestones and types of design reviews.

For each of the CD design review stages, a Design Review Report (DRR) is generated: A DRR is a single
document that captures the results of each design stage (e.g., reviewer comments and their official
resolution), and formal authorization to move forward to the next design stage. Details can be found in
Section 5: Design Review Report.

3.1 System Requirements Review (SRR)

Requirements ensure that a system design once delivered provides the customer’s desired scientific,
performance, functional, and operational needs by controlling the design definition. Customer/stakeholder
requirements are elicited, defined, analyzed and maintained to guide the design process for the system. In
the early stage of the project, high-level requirements (or, Level 0 requirements) are defined. For LAMP,
the outcome of the SRR are Level 0 requirements which are defined in the Program Requirements
Document (PRD) for LAMP [2]. Requirements are further developed throughout the project execution;
refer to the LAMP-SE-PLAN-001; Systems Engineering Management Plan (SEMP) [4] for details on the
requirements management process.

During the requirements definition process, the method of verification (e.g., analysis, demonstration, test,
inspection, similarity) for each requirement shall be identified, and each requirement and associated
verification method shall be assigned to an owner prior to or during the System Requirements Review.
Leading up to any system requirements review, constraints, dependencies and assumptions should be
captured in each requirement definition or its rationale. Requirements should be analyzed to identify
conflicting, missing, ambiguous or unverifiable requirements. Over time, as the requirements and their
flow-down mature, the rigor on design basis evaluation will increase and begin to provide a more distinct
body of requirement verification evidence.

Finally, all requirements are reviewed periodically throughout the project execution to finalize system
requirements and to ensure sufficient level of maturity at any given design stage). This requirements review
can be followed by system or component requirements reviews to review the decomposition of system
requirements to the component level and to validate component interface requirements. After the
requirements review(s), and throughout the lifecycle of the design project, the Technical Director
adjudicates new or changes to existing requirements as changes to requirements are likely to impact scope,
schedule, cost, or risk. Each design review shall assess risk status, identify any new technical risks, and
evaluate risk mitigation progress consistent with the Project’s Risk Management Plan [9].

3.2 Conceptual Design Review (CDR)

Conceptual Design is formally defined as the project design phase that culminates in the development of
the design basis for the preferred design concept to proceed into preliminary design (DOE-STD-1189 [10]).
The end of conceptual design is marked by CD-1 approval for large projects and is approximately equivalent
to 30% design completion. Moreover, at CD-1, a design plan shall establish anticipated levels of the design
maturity at each CD through final design. Independent project reviews should evaluate progress against the
design plans established at CD-1 [1] [4].

The Pre-Conceptual Design stage denotes the development and documentation of the functional parameters
or capabilities that the project must meet. The development of criteria, which are complete and specifically
related to the project requirements, allows for orderly development of the design. However, care shall be
taken to avoid citing superfluous codes and standards; the primary purpose of functional criteria is to narrow
the criteria to only those applicable to specific alternatives or options. These functional criteria are further
developed, validated, and expanded during the conceptual design stage.

The Conceptual Design stage uses formalized criteria that will capture and evaluate the potential
functionality, risks, and life-cycle costs amongst design options and alternatives. Multiple options for
design may be contemplated through this stage, but as the stage is completed so should the down-selection
of options and alternatives. Research, development, testing and other efforts may be required to down select
a concept. The conceptual design process may also require negotiation with outside organizations,
stakeholders or other legal entities of functional, technical, operational and performance requirements or
standards. Design maturity status and review results will inform cost and schedule baselines and are
integrated with the project’s Earned Value Management System (EVMS) per DOE O 413.3B requirements
[1].

At a minimum, Conceptual Design at CD-1 shall develop the following:

 1. Scope required to satisfy the Program mission requirements
 2. Project feasibility
 3. Attainment of specified performance levels
 a. Physics and Engineering Models of major systems, with undetailed CAD models of
 physical systems
 4. Assessment of project risks and identification of appropriate risk handling strategies
 5. Initial risk register identifying technical, cost, schedule, and external risks, with proposed
 mitigation approaches
 6. Cost and schedule range estimates for the alternatives considered
 7. Project criteria and design parameters
 8. Impact on the site safety basis
 9. Identification of top-level requirements and features

The CDR is conducted once 30% design maturity is reached, and the customer requirements have been
reviewed and finalized from SRR [2]. The purpose of the CDR is to ascertain the adequacy of the
engineering design team’s efforts to identify, understand, and manage project requirements. Following the

CDR, requirements are formally decomposed into derived requirements as necessary to provide working
requirements for design work down to the component level. Following the CDR, design interfaces are
detailed and eventually placed under configuration control, following the graded approach and process
described in LAMP-SE-PLAN-002; Configuration Management Plan [11]. Any proposed changes in
requirements at any level after CDR shall be reviewed prior to and during subsequent design reviews.
The CDR includes a clear and concise description of the alternatives analyzed, the basis for the alternative
selected, and how the alternative meets the approved mission need. It also includes the functions and
requirements that define the alternative and demonstrate its capability for success, and performance
requirements, planning standards and life-cycle cost assumptions. Project Key Performance Parameters
(KPPs) should also be clearly and concisely described during CDR as they form the basis of the project
performance baseline. Since the Analysis of Alternatives (AoA) has been waived for LAMP [12], the CDR
and external review serve to verify that the project has produced a viable Front-End design at 30% design
maturity, and that the design meets the performance requirements stated in the PRD [2].
The next design stage, Preliminary Design, is entered once reviewers and approvers determine that the
conceptual design is sufficiently mature to support progression to Preliminary Design and cost analysis.
CDR concludes with a successful design review and formal approval from reviewers and approvers to
proceed to Preliminary Design.

3.3 Preliminary Design Review (PDR)

The Preliminary Design stage initiates the process of converting concepts to a more detailed design in
facility layouts, P&IDs (Piping and Instrumentation Diagrams), electrical one-lines, and equipment
selections, where more reliable cost and schedule estimates are developed (DOE-STD-1189 [10] and DOE
O 413.3B [1]). The end of preliminary design is marked by CD-2 approval and is equivalent to 60% design
completion (AP-341-620-R3 [13]). Non-nuclear project designs shall be sufficiently mature to prepare an
accurate project baseline with 70-90% confidence prior to CD-2 approval (DOE O 413.3B [1]).
Requirements continue to be refined, and requirements management processes receive considerable focus
to maintain alignment of the requirements and their flow-down through the design and to the product
validation plans. Models, sketches, and specifications are iterated with the aid of prototype hardware builds,
inspections, tests, calculations and design analysis tools. Verification of component designs against the
requirements is demonstrated through results of testing and analysis as applicable. Formal test and analysis
reports provide evidence of design compliance to requirements, and these connections are made within the
requirements management tools. It is appropriate to include results of trade studies at this stage, to justify
the design decisions. While trade studies are fitting for early design stages (conceptual and preliminary),
they are not common after the preliminary design baseline has been established. During the development
of the preliminary design, it is common to conduct trade studies, test design concepts, build prototypes and
assembly fixtures, test assembly and installation procedures, integration, etc. using suitable prototypes.
This stage of the design is complete when it provides sufficient information to support development of the
project performance baseline and a point estimate, and it is ready for an independent review. At a design
maturity of 60%, the PDR is held. This stage ends with successful completion of the design review with
formal direction from the reviewers and approvers to proceed to Final Design. All the design documentation
is updated following the PDR, to include any changes to the design (physics models, CAD models,
drawings, etc), interfaces, requirements, and risks. Technical baseline (including requirements, interface
documents, physics models, CAD models, and drawings) is placed under configuration control following
the PDR (and where appropriate, sooner than the PDR, following a graded approach).

Expected deliverables for preliminary design are listed below. The itemized list of deliverables is meant to
set expectations and help define the 60% design maturity for an accelerator system. The list is made by
using best practice from recent similar projects of accelerator upgrades (see Section 9), and by consulting
with the project ITRC (Independent Technical Review Committee).
The existence or completion of the deliverables listed below does not, by itself, constitute achievement of
Preliminary Design maturity. These deliverables provide objective evidence used to evaluate design
maturity. Preliminary Design maturity is achieved when the applicable exit criteria are satisfied and
remaining technical uncertainties and open items are sufficiently bounded that they do not undermine the
credibility of the technical, cost, schedule, or risk baseline.

Disclaimer: Not all scope/WBS elements need to provide all deliverables and exit criteria if the PDR
documents provide sufficient evidence that the design is stable, mature, and the remaining work (beyond
60% maturity) is understood and bound by acceptable risk so that it will not significantly alter technical
cost and schedule estimates. A blanket 60% complete designation should not automatically be considered
adequate for CD-2. High-risk or long-lead subsystems may need to be near final design while lower-risk
conventional systems remain at preliminary design. Likewise, a 60% design maturity does not imply that
60% of all drawings need to be complete at the PDR.

3.4 Final Design Review (FDR)

Final Design is formally defined as the project design phase marked by completion of the design effort and
production of approved design documentation necessary to permit procurement, construction, testing,
checkout and turnover to proceed (DOE O 413.3B [1]). The end of final design is marked by CD-3 approval
for large projects and is equivalent to 90% review and 100% design completion for smaller projects (AP-
341-620-R3 [13]).
The purpose of the Final Design stage is to prepare final drawings, technical specifications and draft
contract documents required to obtain bids and quotes for procurement and construction. The Final Design
should include test plans, testing requirements, and documented acceptance criteria for all subsystems.
Where appropriate, testing of subsystems and prototypes may be performed during the Final Design stage
to substantiate design compliance with the requirements and reduce risk. The objective of testing during
the Final Design stage being to scrutinize the finer details of the design as the primary functionality of the
design has already been proven through testing and other methods. Once models are completed and
drawings are generated, they are checked and should be commensurate with 90% completion prior to their
initial release. Interface Control Documents (ICDs) should be reviewed and finalized prior to the FDR [14]
[3] [15]. Preparation activities for procurement and fabrication are also conducted, such as compiling a list
of suitable fabrication shops, gathering procurement information and hardware manufacture planning.
The project scope should be finalized. Any changes, coordinated through a documented and approved
change control process, should be permitted only for compelling reasons (i.e., substantial cost saving
opportunities, accommodation of changed conditions in construction, or reduction in funds or changes in
requirements). Fabrication should not begin until the design is sufficiently mature to minimize change
orders and ensure stable baseline execution.

4 Responsible Engineer Technical Reviews
Responsible Engineer Technical Reviews (hereafter, Technical Reviews) complement the DOE Critical

Technical Reviews are typically invoked by a Responsible Engineer, i.e. a person responsible for designing

  Design Impact

  Technical Complexity

4.3 Impact Based Review Determination Guidance Process

This section defines the structured method Responsible Engineers should use to determine the appropriate
Design Class level, as described in Section 4.2, for a given design activity based on its potential impact,
technical complexity, and system (WBS) level. The process begins with an Impact Assessment, which
evaluates potential consequences to safety, mission performance, cost, schedule, and regulatory
compliance. Based on this assessment (Step 1), the design is assigned an initial Design Impact classification
(e.g., High, Moderate, or Routine-Low). In Steps 2 and 3, the level of Technical Complexity and the
System’s WBS Level are evaluated. These factors are then used to determine the required depth and breadth,
respectively, of the Technical Review. Together, these three steps establish the final Design Class and
corresponding review rigor.

After completion of the three-step evaluation process, designs are classified. This classification framework
ensures that higher-impact or more complex designs receive formal, multidisciplinary reviews and, when
appropriate, independent external oversight, while lower-risk, routine designs may be addressed through
peer review and quality assurance verification.

By aligning review rigor with the assessed level of impact, technical complexity, and system scope, this
approach promotes the efficient use of engineering resources for reviews while maintaining appropriate
technical oversight. It also supports consistency, transparency, and compliance with DOE requirements and
institutional governance standards.

5 Design Review Report (DRR)
DOE’s Critical Decision Design Review Report (DRR) is a single document that captures the results of
each design stage (e.g., reviewer comments and their official resolution), and formal authorization to move
forward to the next design stage. The Technical Review Package (TRP) contains the DRR as well as all
materials presented at a design review (requirements, interface documents, technical specifications, CAD
models and drawings, documents such as test reports and engineering analysis reports, review presentation
slides, etc). While a DRR document template is available for use at any review, miscellaneous external
committees may prefer to use their own format for the review report.

DRR consists of the following elements:

  Signature page,
  Design review purpose and scope statement,
  Summary of activities executed to reach the design review stage,
  Identification of systems and their classification (e.g. management level determination)
  List of documents and drawings to be reviewed,
  Charge to the reviewers,
  Technical review comments form,
  Summary of risks/issues identified and required actions (if applicable)
  Closing documentation.
For Technical Reviews, the Project Technical Director, in collaboration with the Project Facility Design
Authority Representative (FDAR), shall define the specific requirements of the DRR. The scope and
content of this package shall be aligned with the required level of review rigor, as determined by the Design
Class classification outlined in Section 4. However, at a high level it should follow DOE’s Critical DRR
outline as shown above. Best practice is to use the same DRR format for any level of review. DRR
documents will have a LAMP document number in a form LAMP-ENG-DRR-xxx, where ENG denotes
engineering category and DRR denotes the document type.
Technical Reviews for CD-3X authorization
An important subset of Technical Reviews are the reviews needed to support the execution of long-lead
procurements/ early execution (CD-3X) for LAMP [16]. In the CD-3X approval flow [16], similarly to a
CD review, a Technical Review is conducted, where the TRP is the output the review. Technical Review
Package contains the DRR as well as all materials presented at a design review (requirements, interface
documents, technical specifications, CAD models and drawings, documents such as test reports and
engineering analysis reports, review presentation slides, etc). Technical reviews associated with CD-3X
approvals will always fall under Design Class 1, because of the combined high mission and beam
availability impact, major cost and schedule exposure, complex integration across subsystems and WBS

Los Alamos National Laboratory Page 27

level 1, 2 or 3. As far as the design maturity, these reviews will always be for design maturity higher than
30% (i.e. beyond the conceptual design). These reviews will often support the authorization of build-to-
spec procurements. In these situations, design maturity may fall somewhere between 30% and 60%. LAMP
team would provide an appropriate subset of PDR deliverables listed earlier in this document at the review.
The review focus would be on specifications and procurement documentation (drafts). Procurement
documents should demonstrate a plan that implements appropriate milestones in the statement of work and
hold/witness points (such as a PDR milestone, FDR milestone and a pre-fabrication review milestone,
where design and interfaces are agreed upon between the project team and subcontractors).

5.1 Charge to the Reviewers

For the DOE’s Critical Decision process, a set of technical review questions i.e., “Charge to the
Reviewers” are presented by the Technical Director to the reviewers and approvers. Answers to the
questions identify if the design is feasible, responsive to the requirements and its maturity warrants
movement to the next design stage. Passing into the next design stage signals the investment (or diversion)
of more resources and budget, thus this decision should not be taken lightly. Review questions should be
posed in a way to encourage straightforward answers of yes, no or N/A, with supporting comments if
applicable.
Review Committee Chair consolidates comments from the committee members and mediates any
disagreements by the committee so as to provide a single answer to each charge question. Consolidated
feedback from the committee is handed over by the Committee Chair to the Technical Director. Responses
are reviewed and dispositioned by the Technical Director. Any "No" responses to charge questions must be
resolved or justified before the design may advance.
Some examples of charge questions are listed below, and can be tailored depending on the design stage and
scope (i.e. focus) of the review:

 1. Are the design criteria (requirements) sufficiently defined for this stage of the design?
 2. Does sufficient justification exist for the requirements?
 3. Does the system design satisfy the requirements?
 4. Are assumptions adequately described, documented, and reasonable?
 5. Compliance with applicable codes and standards – Are design approaches, fabrication tolerances,
 techniques, and processes consistent with standard practices?
 6. Are handling, storage, and shipping requirements specified and satisfied?
 7. Have material compatibility issues been properly addressed?
 8. Are quality assurance requirements specified?
 9. Is the current design method adequate?
 10. Given the stage of the system design and the complexity of the system, has a procurement strategy
 been adequately defined?
 11. Are inspection, assembly, and testing requirements specified?
 12. Does the design support the intended use of the hardware?

LAMP-ENG-TP-003; Design Review Procedure
Los Alamos National Laboratory Page 28

 13. Have the major design risks and handling strategies been identified in proceeding to the next design
 stage?
 14. Has safety been adequately considered in the design of the system (i.e., safe handling, assembly,
 disassembly, personnel access, etc.)?
 15. Are the material traceability requirements adequately specified, if required?
 16. Are system interfaces defined and understood to ensure proper integration across support groups?

For Technical Reviews, the Project Technical Director, in collaboration with the Project FDAR, shall
define the specific technical review questions and evaluation criteria. For Design Class 2 and 3 reviews,
Responsible Engineers shall down select and include the appropriate review questions. These shall be
aligned with the impact, technical complexity, and system (WBS) level that may have triggered the review,
and they may also consider applicable requirements, system integration factors, safety and regulatory
compliance obligations, and overall technical adequacy. The scope, depth, and content of the DRR shall be
commensurate with the level of review rigor required, as determined by the assigned Design Class and
system level.

5.2 Technical Review Comments Form

DOE Critical Decision Reviewer comments, along with proposed resolutions (where applicable), must be
collected and documented by the Review Committee Chair using the technical review comments form
included in the DRR. The recommended practice is to complete this process within 10 working days
following the conclusion of the design review.

Each comment is assigned a unique number and categorized by the review team using the following
classification system:

  Category A – Critical: High-priority issues such as conflicts with consensus codes or standards,
 engineering requirements, or DOE/LANL policy. This category also includes significant
 interference, fit, function, or interface issues within the design documentation.
  Category B – For Consideration: Suggestions or observations that are not mandatory but may be
 addressed in future design stages, if within project scope.
  Category C – Editorial: Minor comments related to formatting, grammar, or clarity that do not
 affect the technical content.

All reviewer comments must be technically specific, actionable, and supported by a clear regulatory or
engineering basis. Comments should reference the applicable project document or submittal. In certain
types of reviews, categories A, B, and C are referred to as Recommendations, Comments, and Findings,
respectively.

All comments must be dispositioned by the preparer (member of the design team) and reviewed with the
Technical Director and Review Committee Chair and Committee Members before final approval. The
preparer(s) must formally respond to each comment—either by accepting it and proposing a resolution or
by providing a technical justification for rejection (note the Accept or Reject column in the Design Review
Report table with comments). It is strongly recommended that all comments, regardless of category, be

LAMP-ENG-TP-003; Design Review Procedure
Los Alamos National Laboratory Page 29

dispositioned. During this process, preparers are encouraged to consult reviewers for clarification and to
confirm that proposed resolutions are acceptable prior to finalizing the DRR.

Final disposition of Category A comments must be resolved before the project may advance to the next
design stage or Critical Decision (CD) milestone. Best practice is to assign a due date for any future
resolution of a comment requiring action (e.g. in cases when additional analyses, testing, or design
development are needed). Any design changes resulting from comment resolution must be tracked in
accordance with the project’s Configuration Management Plan [11].

For Technical Reviews, the Project Technical Director, in collaboration with the Project FDAR, shall
collect and formally document and categorize (Category A to C) all technical review comments. These
comments shall be incorporated into the DRR.

All reviewer comments shall be technically specific, actionable, and supported by a clear regulatory, safety,
or engineering basis. Each comment shall reference the applicable project documents, requirements,
standards, or design criteria.

All comments shall be formally dispositioned by the preparer (design team member) and subsequently
reviewed by the Technical Director and FDAR for final approval. The preparer(s) shall respond to each
comment by either:

  Accepting the comment and proposing a documented resolution, or

  Providing a technically justified basis for rejecting the comment.

It is strongly recommended that all comments, regardless of classification or perceived significance, be
formally dispositioned to ensure traceability and audit readiness. During the disposition process, preparers
are encouraged to engage directly with reviewers to clarify intent and confirm that proposed resolutions are
acceptable prior to finalizing the DRR.

5.3 Closing Documentation

For DOE’s Critical Decision stage closing documentation, the last action to be completed should occur
after the design review. Closing documentation includes but is not limited to a project status statement, exit
criteria and review completion approval.

The design review Committee Chair provides a project status statement derived from the debrief with the
reviewers and approvers after the design preview. This brief statement detailing the agreed-upon status of
the project is included at the end of the DRR and should include any high-level activities for the next design
stage (if any). The Design Review Report (DRR) has a section with exit criteria, which is a checklist of
questions. These questions must be answered as they directly address the activities leading up to the design
review.

Review completion approval is a collective decision made amongst the design review preparer(s), reviewers
and approvers captured at the end of the DRR. Based on the outcome of the design review, review of project
documentation and disposition of reviewer comments as recorded in the technical review comments form,
it is agreed that the project will continue as follows:

  Go: Proceed to the next stage with no conditions.

LAMP-ENG-TP-003; Design Review Procedure
Los Alamos National Laboratory Page 30

  Go with Conditions: Proceed, provided specific conditions are met.

  Pause: Delay advancement pending further resolution or information.

  Quit: Terminate or substantially redirect the effort.

One of the outcomes with/without conditions is selected, and the signatures on the first page of the DRR
endorse this collective agreement. The DRR is then signed and approved and placed under the configuration
management with the project documentation. Any action items in comment disposition are tracked to
completion.

For Technical Review documentation, the Project Technical Director, in collaboration with the Project
FDAR, shall ensure that all action items and comment dispositions are tracked to completion. All
comments, responses, and associated action item closures shall be incorporated into the Technical Review
Package to ensure traceability and audit readiness.
