Tau knockout mitigates long-term cognitive impairment following neonatal sepsis via mechanisms involving dendritic structural remodelling and adult hippocampal neurogenesis
Authors: Hongchun Li, Bin Yu, Bingqiao Wang, Luqiang Sun, Chenhao Zhao, Linlin Hu, Mengqi Yuan, Haichuan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOne of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality in newborns and preterm infants is neonatal sepsis, which is defined as a systemic inflammatory response caused by a suspected or confirmed infection that occurs during the first month of life. Adolescents who survive neonatal sepsis often experience severe long-term cognitive impairment and unfavourable neurological outcomes. The foetal brain expresses the Tau protein, a neuronal microtubule-associated protein that is essential for modulating neuronal development, and negative neurodevelopmental effects are linked to aberrant Tau expression. In this study, we investigated the possible role of Tau in the long-term memory and cognitive function deficits caused by neonatal sepsis. We discovered that Tau silencing could attenuate the memory impairment in adolescents caused by early-life inflammation. Furthermore, Tau silencing ameliorated this loss of long-term memory and cognitive function by promoting dendritic structural remodelling in granule neurons and adult hippocampal neurogenesis (AHN) in the hippocampus dentate gyrus (DG). Together, these results suggest that Tau is a possible molecular target for treating neonatal sepsis-induced brain damage and that Tau may influence deficits in long-term memory and cognitive function caused by neonatal sepsis by interfering with the dendritic structure of granule neurons and AHN during neuronal development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12974-026-03917-w.Keywords: Tau, Neonatal sepsis, Long-term cognitive impairment, Hippocampus dentate gyrus, Adult hippocampal neurogenesis, Dendritic structure remodelling

Introduction

One of the primary causes of illness and mortality in newborns and preterm infants is remains neonatal sepsis [1], making it a primary public health issue, particularly in developing nations. Activated immune cells in the brains of newborns can release cytokines. Resident microglia or activated macrophages are primarily responsible for this activation [2]. The immunological ramifications of brain immaturity, especially in relation to the immaturity of immune cell regulation in the central nervous system (CNS), may make brains particularly susceptible to damage from widespread and poorly managed inflammation [3]. Thus, the brains of neonates exhibit increased susceptibility to injury because of systemic inflammation.

Local microglia become activated during the development of sepsis, which causes neuroinflammation and contributes to brain damage [4]. According to certain studies, people who survive sepsis experience long-term impairments in verbal learning and memory. Additionally, the volume of the hippocampus is considerably decreased [5].

Recent research has shown that deficiencies in adult hippocampal neurogenesis are linked to sepsis-induced cognitive impairment [6]. In many animals and humans, neural stem cell (NSC) neurogenesis continues throughout adulthood, particularly in the hippocampus dentate gyrus (DG) subventricular zone (SVZ) and subgranular zone (SGZ) [7, 8]. An intraperitoneal injection of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) causes acute neuroinflammation, which affects hippocampal neurogenesis and causes memory impairments [6]. Furthermore, LPS injection-induced sepsis alters NSC proliferation and differentiation in the hippocampal DG, compromising long-term memory and cognitive reserve [9, 10]. Nevertheless, the processes underlying the deficiencies in adult hippocampal neurogenesis (AHN) caused by sepsis, particularly neonatal sepsis, remain unknown.

The most prevalent neuronal microtubule-associated protein, Tau, is engaged in both healthy and diseased neuronal processes [11]. In Tauopathies, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD), increased levels of pathologically aggregated Tau in the brain correlate with the deterioration of synapses and neurons [12, 13]. In AD, Tau localization is abnormally shifted from axons to the somatodendritic compartment, a process associated with the accumulation of amyloid β (Aβ) [14], and there is a strong link between neuronal dysfunction and cognitive decline in Tauopathy mouse models [15].

In addition, the Tau protein is thought to play a major role in causing aberrant neurodevelopmental outcomes in addition to its function in normal neuronal processes. For instance, hypoxic–ischaemic encephalopathy in neonates is linked to increased serum Tau protein levels [16]. Furthermore, Tau is linked to the growth of newborn hippocampal granule neurons, which need a high degree of cytoskeletal flexibility to divide, proliferate, and differentiate in response to external stimuli [17–20], indicating that Tau plays an important role in regulating AHN in response to external stimuli.

Therefore, we aimed to investigate whether Tau knockout could reduce the long-term cognitive impairment caused by neonatal sepsis in a mouse model. In the current investigation, we investigated the potential role of Tau in the impairment of hippocampal neurogenesis caused by neonatal sepsis and its underlying mechanism.

Materials and methods

Animals

We purchased female C57BL/6J wild-type (WT) mice from Chengdu Dossy Experimental Animals Co. Ltd. (Chengdu, China) on gestational day 18 and closely monitored them for offspring birth. Jackson Laboratories provided Tau−/− knockout (Tau-KO) mice on a C57BL/6J background (007251, USA). For this investigation, Tau−/− homozygous mice were produced by breeding male and female homozygous mice. In our facility, two Tau-KO females were mated with one Tau-KO male to produce litters. The females were placed in separate cages one to four days before giving birth, and the males were removed after ten days. Postnatal mice of both sexes were housed with their mothers and had unlimited access to food and drink. All the mice were housed in an animal room with a regular 12-hour light/dark cycle, a steady temperature, and unlimited access to food and water. The animals were housed in groups of five per cage after being weaned on postnatal day 21. The Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Sichuan Provincial Hospital and the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care established guidelines that were followed for all experimental procedures and animal use. Every attempt was made to reduce the suffering of the mice.

Model of severe inflammation in neonates

On postnatal day 5 (P5), the animals were divided into treatment and control groups and administered intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections (2 µl/per mouse) of either pyrogen-free saline or LPS from E. coli O111:B4 (1 mg/kg; Sigma, L2630). Males and females were randomly divided into cages of five mice each after the offspring were weaned on P21. Littermates were housed together, and only pups from various litters and treated under the same experimental conditions were combined. Neither late behavioural testing nor early immune activation affected offspring survival.

Behavioural tests

Memory-related behavioural tests

Y-Maze Test (YMT)

A Y-maze with two goal arms and a beginning arm was used for the experiment. Before the test, the mice were allowed to acclimate to the space for ten minutes. On the day of the test, the mice were positioned at the end of one arm, which was designated the starting arm. They were trained for five minutes to enter the right arm, which was the familiar arm, instead of the left arm, which was the new arm. Following a 120-minute intertrial break, the novel arm was opened, allowing the mice to investigate all three arms. ANY-maze software (USA) was used to automatically record the amount of time spent in the familiar and novel arms during the five-minute test period. The percentages of time spent in novel and familiar arms were analysed as follows: percentage of time spent in the familiar arm = time (familiar arm)/300; percentage of time spent in the novel arm = time (novel arm)/300.

Novel Object Position (NOP) test

The mice were placed in the empty chamber and allowed to explore for ten minutes on the first day. The mice were placed inside the chamber with two identical objects and allowed to explore for ten minutes the next day. On the third day, however, one of the familiar objects was relocated diagonally while keeping its previous form. The mice were then returned to the chamber and allowed ten minutes to examine the items. The amount of time spent investigating both objects was determined using EthoVision XT software (EthoVision, USA). The exploratory preference was calculated using the following formula: time (novel)/(time (novel) + time (familiar)).

Novel Object Recognition (NOR) test

The mice were allowed to adapt to their surroundings in the chamber for ten minutes on the first day. The mice were placed in the same test chamber with two identical objects and allowed to explore for an additional ten minutes the following day. On the third day, one of the familiar objects was replaced with a new object, which had a different size and colour. The mice were allowed ten minutes to explore the new item. The amount of time spent investigating each object was recorded using EthoVision XT software (EthoVision, USA). The exploratory preference was calculated using the following formula: time (novel)/(time (novel) + time (familiar)).

Contextual Fear Conditioning (CFC) test

For contextual fear conditioning, we employed two different contexts (context A and context B). Compared with the context A chamber, the context B chamber featured a distinct set of context signals, such as a black background and a white plastic floor, whereas the context A chamber featured a white background and a grid floor made of 24 stainless steel rods. During the training phase, the mice spent a total of 300 s in the conditioning chamber. At 120, 180, and 240 s, foot shocks (2 s, 0.75 mA) were administered. The following morning or after seven days, the mice were placed in the context B chamber and allowed to explore for 180 s to examine their freezing behaviour and freezing time in the context. After two hours, or seven days, the responses of the mice were assessed in chamber A. Freezing behaviour, which was defined as a lack of movement for at least two seconds, were automatically recorded to measure the expression of situational fear memory. The context-associated fear memory discrimination index was determined using the following formula: (freezing time (A)-freezing time (B))/(freezing time (A)+freezing time (B)).

Depression-related behaviours

Tail Suspension Test (TST)

Tape was applied approximately 1 cm from the end of the tail, after which the mice were suspended by the tail 30 cm from the bench. During the five-minute test, the program (ANYmaze, USA) automatically logged the time each mouse spent immobile.

Forced Swimming Test (FST)

The mice were placed in a glass tube that was 50 cm high and 20 cm in diameter and contained water at a steady temperature of 25 ± 1 °C. EthoVision XT software (EthoVision, USA) automatically recorded the duration of immobility of each mouse during the test period.

Sucrose Preference Test (SPT)

Briefly, the mice were provided with two identical drinking bottles after being adjusted to single housing cages. On the first day, the two bottles were filled with regular drinking water, and the following day, they were filled with 1% sucrose water. After a 24-hour period without water, the animals were provided with one bottle containing 1% sucrose and one bottle containing water for two hours. After one hour, the bottle locations were changed. The fluid weight of each bottle was measured. Sucrose preference was calculated using the following formula: consumption (sucrose)/(consumption (sucrose) + consumption (water)).

Anxiety-related behaviours

Open Field Test (OFT)

The testing apparatus consists of a 48 × 48 cm open arena with 50 cm high walls. Each mouse was positioned separately in the middle of the arena and allowed to explore freely for five minutes. The total distance travelled by the mice and the amount of time spent in the centre of the chamber were automatically recorded using EthoVision XT software (EthoVision, USA).

Light–Black Transition (LBT) test

The device was divided into two compartments of similar size, one of which was dark and the other highly light. The mice were allowed five minutes to roam freely between the two compartments. The total amount of time spent in each chamber was recorded using software (ANYmaze, USA).

Elevated Plus Maze (EPM) test

The EPM consisted of two open arms and two closed arms. After the mice were placed in the centre, the software (ANYmaze, USA) automatically tracked the time they spent in the open and closed arms over the course of five minutes.

Social-related behaviours

Social Interaction Test (SIT)

First, the mice were placed in a 48 cm x 48 cm area and allowed to explore for ten minutes. An empty wire-mesh cage measuring 10 cm in diameter and 15 cm in height was placed in the middle of the arena. A novel mouse was added to the wire cage after two hours, and the mice were then returned to the arena and observed for ten minutes. EthoVision XT software (EthoVision, USA) was used to record the amount of time mice spent in the designated social interaction zone (defined as a 10 × 10 cm area surrounding the wire cage) with or without the novel mouse.

To avoid stress, in the present study, the interbehaviour test interval was 1 day.

Measurement of body weight and brain weight

All the mice were weighed on postnatal day 5 (P5), 2 weeks, 4 weeks and 8 weeks. Brain weight was measured 8 weeks after the Evans blue dye injection.

Flow cytometry analysis of peripheral immune cells

Following the behavioural tests, blood samples were collected in heparinized tubes for flow cytometry analyses of cell populations. Blood samples were diluted 1:1 in RPMI 1640 medium, and the cells were subsequently washed with PBS. After 10 min of staining with blocking buffer containing CD16/CD32 (1:100; BD, 553140), all the samples were incubated with an antibody mixture at 4 °C for 30 min in the dark. The antibodies used for flow cytometry were CD3 (APC-CY7) (1:100; BD, 560590), CD45R (PE) (1:100; BD, 553089), CD4 (FITC) (1:100; BD, 561828), CD8a (APC) (1:100; BD, 561093), and CD25 (PE-Cy7) (1:100; BD, 561780) for 30 min. After surface staining, the samples were permeabilized by incubation with fixation/permeabilization buffer (eBiosciences, 88-8824-00) and incubated with anti-Foxp3 (V450) antibody (1:100; BD, 561293) for 30 min. After incubation, the cells were analysed by sorting using a FACSAria SORP (BD, USA). Gating parameters and data analysis were performed using FlowJo 10 software (Tree Star, USA).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

The levels of IFN-γ (R&D Systems, MIF00-1), TNF-α (R&D Systems, MTA00B-1), IL-1β (R&D Systems, MLB00C-1), IL-2 (R&D Systems, M2000-1) and IL-6 (R&D Systems, M6000B-1) in mouse serum samples and cell culture media were determined using ELISAs. The assay kits were purchased from R&D Systems (USA), and the assays were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Evans blue dye perfusion

Eight-week-old mice were intraperitoneally injected with Evans blue dye (Sigma Aldrich, E2129) dissolved in PBS (2% w/v) at a dosage of 100 µl/25 g body weight. The mice were euthanized two hours after the dye injection, and their brains were removed and examined for Evans blue extravasation. Afterwards, the mouse brains were incubated for 48 h in 4% paraformaldehyde for the extraction of Evans blue dye, with the absorption measured at 600 nm [21].

Western blot

Brain tissues were obtained after the behavioural tests were completed; the cells of the brain tissues were lysed and the proteins were extracted using a mammalian cell and tissue extraction kit (K269-500; BioVision) containing phosphatase inhibitors (4906845001; Roche) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. A Bradford assay kit (P0006; Beyotime) was used to measure the total protein content. A total of 20 µg of protein from each sample was loaded on a 10 or 12.5% sodium dodecyl sulphate‒polyacrylamide gel and separated. Afterwards, the proteins were transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane (IPVH00010, Millipore) in a mixed solution of 20% (v/v) methanol and Tris-glycine buffer. After the membrane was blocked for one hour at room temperature with 5% non-fat milk (9999; Cell Signaling Technology) in TBST buffer, the membrane was incubated with the primary antibody at 4 °C overnight with gentle shaking. The following day, the blots were incubated with the secondary antibody for two hours at room temperature, followed by three 15-minute TBST washes. A chemiluminescence substrate (WBKLS0500, Millipore) and a chemiluminescence imaging system (CLINX, Shanghai, China) were used to visualize the immunoreactive bands. Chemi Analysis software (CLINX, Shanghai, China) was used to quantify the optical density of the bands. The antibodies used for western blotting were as follows: mouse anti-GFAP (1:1000; Abcam), rabbit anti-Iba1 (1:1000; Abcam), rabbit anti-IL-1β (1:1000; Proteintech), rabbit anti-TNF-α (1:1000; Proteintech), mouse anti-3R Tau (1:1000; Millipore), mouse anti-4R Tau (1:1000; Millipore), mouse anti-Tau (1:1000; Thermo scientific), rabbit anti-p-Tau (S 199) (1:5000; Abcam), rabbit anti-p-Tau (T 231) (1:5000; Abcam), rabbit anti-p-Tau (S 396) (1:3000; Abcam), rabbit anti-p-Tau (S 404) (1:2000; Abcam), rabbit anti-GSK-3β (1:1000; Abcam), rabbit anti-p-GSK-3β (S 9) (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology), rabbit anti-CaMKII (1:1000; Abcam), rabbit anti-p-CaMKII (T 286) (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology), rabbit anti-PP2A B Subunit (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology), rabbit anti-PP2A C Subunit (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology), rabbit anti-p-PP2A C Subunit (Y 307) (1:1000; RD), rabbit anti-Drebrin (1:1000; Proteintech), rabbit anti-Synaptophysin (1:1000, Abcam), mouse anti-PSD-95 (1:1000, Abcam), rabbit anti-Glu A1 (Glu 1, 1:1000, Abcam), rabbit anti-Glu A2 (Glu 2, 1:1000, Abcam), rabbit anti-NMDAR 1 (1:1000, Abcam), rabbit anti-NMDAR 2 A (1:1000, Abcam), rabbit anti-NMDAR 2B (1:1000, Abcam), rabbit anti-PI3K-p110α (1:1000; Abcam), rabbit anti-PI3K-p85 (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology), rabbit anti-AKT (1:1000; Abcam), rabbit anti-p-AKT (S 473) (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology), rabbit anti-Lamin B1 (1:1000; Abcam), mouse anti-ZO1 (1:1000; Thermo scientific), rabbit anti-N-cadherin (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology), rabbit anti-Claudin-1 (1:1000; Abcam), rabbit anti-β-Catenin (1:1000; Abcam), rabbit anti-p-β-Catenin (S 33/37/T 41) (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology), mouse anti-GAPDH (1:2000; Proteintech), goat anti-mouse (HRP) (1:2000; Invitrogen), and goat anti-rabbit (HRP) (1:2000; Invitrogen).

Biotinylation of neuronal surface proteins

After the hippocampal tissues were rinsed with cold artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF) containing 119.0 mM NaCl, 3.5 mM KCl, 1.3 mM MgSO4, 2.5 mM CaCl2, 1.0 mM NaH2PO4, 26.2 mM NaHCO3, and 11.0 mM glucose, the tissues were incubated with ACSF containing 1.0 mg/mL sulfo-NHS-LC-biotin (21331, Thermo Scientific) for 30 min at 4 °C. The brain slices were rinsed with cold ACSF, treated with ACSF containing 100 mM glycine for 30 min to neutralize the unreacted biotinylation reagent, and then lysed with a mammalian cell and tissue extraction kit (K269-500, BioVision). The protein concentration of each sample was measured, and NeutrAvidin-coupled agarose beads (29201, Thermo Scientific) were incubated with equal amounts of protein overnight. Western blot analysis was performed to quantify biotinylated intracellular and surface proteins.

Immunohistochemistry

Sodium pentobarbital (60 mg/kg) was used to deeply anaesthetize the animals before they were transcardially perfused with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and ice-cold 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in 0.1 M PBS (pH 7.4). After careful removal from the skull, the brains were postfixed with 4% PFA overnight before being dehydrated with 30% sucrose at 4 °C. Using a freezing microtome (Leica, Germany), the brains were cut into 45 μm coronal slices and stored in 12-well plates with a cryoprotectant solution (glycerol, ethylene glycol, and 0.1 M phosphate buffer; pH 7.4; 1:1:2 by volume) at -20 °C until immunohistochemical staining.

After three 10-minute washes with TBS, the sections were incubated for two hours at room temperature with blocking solution containing 0.3% Triton X-100 and 5% normal donkey serum (017-000-121, Jackson Immuno) in TBS buffer. Afterwards, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibodies in blocking solution. The following day, the sections were also rinsed three times with TBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100 and incubated with fluorescent dye-conjugated secondary antibodies in blocking solution at room temperature for 1.5 h. Following three additional TBS washes, the slices were mounted using anti-fade mounting medium with DAPI (H-1200, Vector), and a laser confocal microscope (Nikon, Japan) was used to capture the confocal images. ImageJ software (US National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) was used to determine the corresponding DG areas and cell numbers to evaluate cell densities. The antibodies used for immunostaining were as follows: rabbit anti-GFAP (1:100; Abcam), mouse anti-Iba1 (1:100; Abcam), mouse anti-3R Tau (1:200; Millipore), mouse anti-4R Tau (1:50; Biolegend), rat anti-BrdU (1:100; Abcam), mouse anti-Ki67 (1:100; BD), goat anti-SOX2 (1:100; RD), rabbit anti-DCX (1:100; Abcam), rabbit anti-NeuN (1:100; Cell Signaling Technology), donkey anti-mouse (Alexa Fluor 488) (1:500; Invitrogen), donkey anti-rabbit (Alexa Fluor 488) (1:400; Invitrogen), donkey anti-goat (Alexa Fluor 488) (1:500; Invitrogen), donkey anti-rabbit (Alexa Fluor 568) (1:500; Invitrogen), donkey anti-mouse (Alexa Fluor 568) (1:500; Invitrogen), donkey anti-rabbit (Alexa Fluor 647) (1:500; Invitrogen), and donkey anti-rat (Alexa Fluor 647) (1:500; Jackson Immuno).

In vivo sparse labelling of dDG pyramidal neurons

Briefly, five-week-old mice were placed on a stereotaxic apparatus (RWD Life Science) after being anaesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (60 mg/kg). The dDG (AP, -2.0 mm; ML, ± 1.4 mm; DV, -2.2 mm) was injected with the proper viral vectors. A combination of adeno-associated viruses (AAVs), namely, AAV-sparse-NCSP-YFP-2E5 (BC-SL001) (Braincase Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China), was bilaterally infused into the brain using a microsyringe at a rate of 0.05 µl/min for 10 min (0.2 µl per side). The syringe was left in place for an extra five minutes after each injection before being gradually removed to allow the viruses to diffuse. The mice were allowed to recuperate for a minimum of three weeks. After three weeks, the animals were injected with sodium pentobarbital (60 mg/kg) to induce profound anaesthesia. They were then transcardially perfused with PBS and ice-cold 4% PFA in 0.1 M PBS (pH 7.4). The brains were carefully removed from the skulls, postfixed with 4% PFA overnight, and then dehydrated in 30% sucrose at 4 °C. Using a freezing microtome (Leica, Germany), we cut the brains into 50 μm thick coronal slices. We then mounted the slices on slides, dried them, and stored them at -80 °C until we processed them for immunohistochemistry.

After three 10-minute washes with PBS, the sections were coated with anti-fade mounting media containing DAPI (H-1200, Vector). Images of the brains were captured using a laser confocal microscope (Nikon, Japan). Each neuron was examined at high magnification (100x, oil immersion lens) to ensure that all of the dendrites were intact. At least 40 neurons were chosen from each group, and at least 3 neurons per slice were analysed. Neurons were reconstructed in three dimensions using confocal microscopy. ImageJ software (US National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, United States) was used to measure the total dendritic length and dendritic spine density.

Transmission electron microscopy

Eight-week-old mice were intracardially perfused with 50 mL of freshly prepared fixative containing 2.5% glutaraldehyde and 2% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M PBS (pH 7.4) after being thoroughly sedated through an injection of sodium pentobarbital (60 mg/kg). After the entire brain was removed, it was postfixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde overnight at 4 °C, impregnated with 1% osmium tetroxide for one hour, dehydrated in graded alcohol solutions, flat mounted using Durcupan ACM (Fluka), and cured for 48 h at 60 °C. The dDG of the hippocampus was extracted from the samples and cut into small pieces, which were then adhered to a plastic block using cyanoacrylate. Formvar-coated single-slot grids were used to prepare ultrathin sections. The synaptic structure was examined using a transmission electron microscope (JEOL, Japan). At least 50–70 synapses from each mouse were analysed in five photos of each area. The morphometric analysis was performed using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software (US National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, United States).

Preparation of a hippocampal single-cell suspension

An Adult Brain Dissociation Kit (130-107-677; Miltenyi) was used to enzymatically degrade the hippocampal tissue after it had been dissected on ice. For the flow cytometry analysis, a single-cell suspension was prepared in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Flow cytometry

Each group of single-cell suspensions was centrifuged, and the pellet was resuspended in Fixable Viability Stain 780 (1:1000; BD) for cell live/dead discrimination. This step was followed by a 10-minute incubation with blocking buffer containing CD16/CD32 (1:100; BD) and a 30-minute incubation with a Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (00-5523; Invitrogen) in the dark at 4 °C. Following a final 30-minute incubation in the dark at 4 °C with the antibody combination, the samples were cleaned, centrifuged, and resuspended in staining buffer containing 2% foetal bovine serum (FBS). The cells were subsequently sorted using a FACSAria SORP (BD, USA). FlowJo 10 software was used for data analysis and to determine the gating parameters (Tree Star, USA). The antibodies used for flow cytometry were as follows: mouse anti-SOX2 (Alexa Fluor 647) (1:100; Biolegend), mouse anti-DCX (PE) (1:100; BD), mouse anti-GFAP (Alexa Fluor 405) (1:100; Novus), and rat anti-Ki67 (PerCP/Cy5.5) (1:100; Biolegend).

Cell culture, transfection and treatment

ATCC provided the mouse neuroblastoma 2a (N2a) cell lines, which were verified to be free of mycoplasma contamination during the investigation. The N2a cells were cultivated in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, C1195500BT, Gibco) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (10099-141, Gibco) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The N2a cells were cultivated in 6-well plates to generate Tau knockdown, antibiotic-resistant cells. Before transfection, the cells were incubated with serum-free DMEM for two hours. Lentiviruses containing mouse Tau shRNA (5’-GGAGTTTGACACAATGGAA-3’) and nonspecific target oligos (5’-TTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGT-3’) were then infected into the N2a cells, which were selected using 2.5 µg/ml puromycin. A clone that was resistant to antibiotics was chosen and survived in puromycin-containing DMEM.

The groups of cultured cells were treated with LPS (1 µg/ml; Selleck, China) for 24 h after the cultures reached 60% confluence to simulate the inflammatory environment and establish a sepsis model in vitro.

Cell viability assay (CCK-8)

A CCK-8 assay kit (Beyotime, C0037) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Prior to use, a CCK-8 assay buffer comprised of 90% medium and 10% CCK-8 test reagent was prepared. After LPS stimulation, the neurons were transferred to a fresh 96-well plate, and each well received 200 µl of the prepared CCK-8 test solution. The samples were incubated for two hours at 37 °C, and a spectrometer was used to measure the absorbance at 450 nm.

Mitochondrial ROS measurement

MitoSOX™ Red (Invitrogen, M36007), a red mitochondrial superoxide indicator that emits red fluorescence when oxidized by superoxide in the mitochondria, was used to track the formation of mitochondrial ROS. Briefly, N2a cells were diluted in HBSS supplemented with 2.5 µM MitoSOX and 10 µM Hoechst (H1399; Life Technologies) for 20 min at 37 °C, followed by two washes. A laser confocal microscope (Nikon, Japan) was used to visualize MitoSOX fluorescence. ImageJ software (US National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) was used to estimate the corresponding cellular fluorescence intensity to determine the level of ROS.

Immunocytochemistry

Cells grown on glass coverslips coated with poly-D-lysine (P6407; Sigma) were washed with PBS three times and fixed with 4% PFA for ten minutes. After three PBS washes, the cells were blocked for one hour with blocking buffer (5% normal donkey serum and 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS), after which they were treated with primary antibodies overnight. After three washes with PBS the next day, the cells on the coverslips were incubated with fluorescent dye-conjugated secondary antibodies for one hour at room temperature. Following three PBS washes, the cells on the coverslips were stained with Hoechst (H1399; Life Technologies) for five minutes before being mounted using anti-fade mounting medium (H-1000; Vector). The antibodies used for immunostaining were as follows: rabbit anti-p-Tau (S 404) (1:500; Cell Signaling Technology), rat anti-BrdU (1:100; Abcam), donkey anti-rabbit (Alexa Fluor 568) (1:500; Invitrogen), and donkey anti-rat (Laxa Fluor 647) (1:500; Jackson Immuno).

Cytoplasmic and nuclear fractionation

In accordance with the manufacturer’s procedures, the nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions of N2a cells were separated using a kit (Millipore, 539791).

EdU or BrdU incorporation assays

The mice were injected with 50 mg/kg (i.p.) bromodeoxyuridine (BrdU) (Invitrogen, 00-0103) three times every four hours at P55 and examined at P56 and P83 to determine the proliferation of newborn neurons in the hippocampus. The proliferation of newborn neurons in the hippocampus was evaluated by performing BrdU immunostaining.

N2a cells were serum-starved overnight and exposed to external stimuli, after which 10 µM EdU or BrdU was added to the culture medium and incubated for two hours prior to the collection of cells to assess proliferation using immunocytochemistry and flow cytometry. The collected cells were placed in a 5 ml FACS tube, fixed, and stained with the Click-iT™ Plus EdU Alexa Fluor™ 647 Flow Cytometry Assay Kit (C10634; Thermo Scientific) at 4 °C for 30 min in the dark to detect EdU expression using flow cytometry. After being incubated with the antibody mixture at 4 °C for 30 min in the dark, the samples were subsequently assessed using a FACSAria SORP (BD, USA). The data were analysed as described above. The antibodies used for flow cytometry were as follows: mouse anti-AKT (Alexa Fluor 350) (1:100; R&D Systems) and mouse anti-pAKT (S 473, BV421) (1:100; BD).

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 8 was used to analyse all the data, the results are shown as the means ± SEMs, and the Kolmogorov‒Smirnov test was performed to assess the distribution of the data. The results from each experiment were analysed using one-way or two-way ANOVA followed by the Bonferroni post hoc correction for multiple comparisons. The number of animals used in each experiment is denoted by n. For all the results, statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Tau knockout attenuates long‑term memory impairment in adolescents who experienced severe inflammation during the neonatal period

A neonatal inflammation model was created in mice on postnatal day five following LPS treatment to examine the effects of early-life inflammation during the neonatal period on the developing brain (Fig. S1A). Until the neurobehavioural tests were performed in adolescence (8 weeks old), the mice had unrestricted access to food and water. On postnatal day 56 following the induction of early-life inflammation in neonates, we first evaluated the depression-like behaviours of WT and Tau-KO mice. There are no significant differences in the amount of time each group of mice spent immobile during the tail suspension test (TST) or the forced swimming test (FST) (Fig. S1B and C). Furthermore, in the sucrose preference test (SPT), mice in each group did not exhibit significantly different sucrose preferences (Fig. S1D), indicating that in 8-week-old mice, early-life inflammation did not cause depressive-like behaviours.

Next, we used the open field test (OFT), light–black transition (LBT) test and elevated plus-maze (EPM) test to assess anxiety-like behaviours in adolescent mice that experienced inflammation as neonates (Fig. S1E). The amount of time spent in the centre and the total distance travelled by each group of mice did not differ significantly in the OFT (Fig. S1F and G). Discernible differences in the amount of time each group of mice spent in the light box and the open arms as well as the closed arms were not observed (Fig. S1H–I). These findings suggest that in 8-week-old mice, early-life inflammation does not cause anxiety-like behaviours. We next used the social interaction test (SIT) to assess the social behaviours of WT and Tau-KO mice that experienced severe inflammation as neonates (Fig. S1J). The results revealed no difference in the retention time in the interaction zone with or without target mice in each group (Fig. S1K and L), indicating that the social interaction behaviours of 8-week-old mice were unaffected by early-life inflammation.

Finally, we used the Y-maze test (YMT), a traditional behavioural technique, to assess spatial working memory. The results revealed that compared with control mice, LPS-exposed mice spent less time in the novel arm (Fig. 1B), indicating a spatial working memory deficit. In addition, LPS-exposed mice were less likely to investigate new locations of the objects in the novel object position (NOP) test, which measures spatial memory (Fig. 1C), indicating a lack of spatial memory. In the novel object recognition (NOR) test, which evaluates long-term recognition memory, LPS-exposed mice exhibited similar behaviours: they spent less time investigating the novel object (Fig. 1D), indicating both deficiencies in long-term memory and a decreased propensity for exploration.

We also performed a contextual fear conditioning (CFC) test to evaluate the context-associated memory of adolescent mice that experienced inflammation as neonates. The mice were exposed to an unconditioned but somewhat identical situation (context B) the morning after being conditioned to a context (context A) with three foot shocks. After training, we measured their capacity to distinguish between the two situations. LPS-exposed mice exhibited severe impairments in context-associated memory and pattern separation, as evidenced by a decrease in freezing time in response to context A and the discrimination index one day and seven days after training (Fig. 1E–G). Our findings collectively revealed that severe inflammation during the neonatal period can cause long-term memory impairments in mice. Interestingly, Tau-KO mice outperformed WT mice in the memory-related YMT, NOP, NOR, and CFC tests (Fig. 1B–G). Additionally, the weight of the LPS-exposed mice was lower than that of the control mice, and the weight of the Tau-KO mice was restored (Fig. 1H). These results suggest that the long-term memory and cognitive deficits caused by severe inflammation during the neonatal period were attenuated when Tau was silenced.

Tau knockout does not affect brain inflammation in adolescents who experienced severe inflammation as neonates

We first investigated peripheral inflammation in adolescent mice exposed to early-life inflammation to explore the potential of Tau to mitigate the long-term memory and cognitive deficits caused by severe inflammation during the neonatal period. We discovered that each group of mice had the same quantity of peripheral blood immune cells (B cells, T cells, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and Treg cells) (Fig. S2A–H). Furthermore, the serum levels of inflammatory cytokines (IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-2, and IL-6) did not differ between any of the groups of animals (Fig. S2I–M). These findings suggest that peripheral inflammation in adolescent mice may not be impacted by severe inflammation during the neonatal period. We investigated whether acute neonatal inflammation affects the function of the blood–brain barrier (BBB). According to our findings, no discernible increase in the leakage of Evans blue dye was observed in the brains of any group of mice (Fig. S3A and B). However, the brain weight of the LPS-exposed animals was less than that of the control mice, and the brain weight was restored in the Tau-KO mice (Fig. S3C). Additionally, no discernible changes in the levels of BBB tight junction proteins (ZO-1, N-cadherin, claudin-1, and β-catenin) were detected in the brains of the mice in each group (Fig. S3D), suggesting that BBB permeability in adolescent mice may not be affected by severe neonatal inflammation.

Finally, since the hippocampal dorsal dentate gyrus (dDG) is important for AHN and memory formation [22], we observed inflammation in the dDG in adolescent animals that experienced early-life inflammation. Immunostaining of the dDG during acute neonatal inflammation revealed considerable increases in the expression of both GFAP (a marker of astrocyte activation) and Iba1 (a marker of microglial activation) (Fig. S4A–D); nevertheless, following acute neonatal inflammation, Tau-KO mice did not exhibit altered glial activation in the dDG (Fig. S4A–D). The elevated expression of GFAP and Iba1 in the dDG of LPS-conditioned WT and Tau-KO mice was further confirmed by western blotting (Fig. 2A), and the dDG of LPS-conditioned WT and Tau-KO mice similarly showed markedly elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β and TNF-α) (Fig. 2B), indicating that following severe neonatal inflammation, the dDG of both WT and Tau-KO mice exhibited a significant inflammatory response. Additionally, immunostaining (Fig. S5A–D) and immunoblotting (Fig. 2C) of the dDG of LPS-treated mice revealed considerable increases in the levels of Tau and Tau isoforms (3R and 4R Tau). These findings suggest that brain inflammation may increase the levels of Tau and its isoforms (3R and 4R Tau) and that silencing Tau expression may not affect inflammation in the adolescent brain.

We next examined the levels of phosphorylated Tau in the dDG of mice that experienced significant inflammation as newborns because it may be responsible for the observed memory deficits. Interestingly, a clear increase in the level of phosphorylated Tau was observed in the dDG (Fig. 2C). Since protein kinases and phosphatases directly control the state of Tau phosphorylation [23], we examined the levels of glycogen synthase kinase (GSK)-3β and calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII), the kinases responsible for Tau phosphorylation, and protein phosphatase 2 A (PP2A), the phosphatase most involved in Tau protein dephosphorylation. We discovered that LPS only marginally increased CaMKII phosphorylation and had no effect on the expression of GSK-3β or CaMKII (Fig. 2D). Next, we assessed the expression of PP2A regulatory subunit B (PP2A-B), PP2A catalytic subunit C (PP2A-C), and phosphorylated PP2A-C (p-PP2A-C); only the level of p-PP2A-C was significantly reduced in the dDG of mice exposed to early-life inflammation (Fig. 2D). Nevertheless, CaMKII and PP2A-C phosphorylation in the dDG was unaffected by Tau silencing (Fig. 2D). Overall, our findings suggest that the posttranslational modification of Tau rather than the elevated expression of total Tau may be the primary cause of the upregulation of phosphorylated Tau expression induced by LPS.

Tau silencing ameliorates changes in the dendritic structure of granule neurons in the dDG of adolescent mice that experienced severe inflammation as neonates

Memory formation is influenced by the dendritic organization of dDG granule neurons [24]. We also investigated how Tau affects the dendritic structure of granule cells in the dDG following severe neonatal inflammation. We performed immunoblotting to measure the expression of proteins associated with synaptic structure. Following severe neonatal inflammation, the Drebrin, Synaptophysin, and PSD-95 levels were significantly reduced, and these reductions were reversed by Tau silencing (Fig. 3A). Additionally, the expression of glutamate receptors (Glu A2 and NMDAR 1) at the synapse surface and in the dDG decreased; however, this decrease was significantly reversed by Tau silencing (Fig. 3B and C). Using ImageJ and the Sholl analysis plugin, we examined the complexity of the dendrites of the granule neurons in the dDG. The morphological results revealed that the LPS-induced reductions in spine density and dendritic complexity were reversed by Tau silencing (Fig. 3D–F). Additionally, we used electron microscopy to observe changes in the synapses of granule neurons in the dDG of mice exposed to inflammation as neonates. The density and length of the postsynaptic density (PSD) in the dDG synapses were significantly reduced in the LPS-treated mice, whereas the PSD density and length of the dDG synapses were increased in the Tau-KO mice (Fig. 3G–I); however, a change in PSD thickness—a gauge of maturation—was not detected in any of the groups (Fig. 3J). Together, our findings suggest that Tau silencing promotes dendritic structural remodelling, which may help mitigate long-term memory and cognitive impairments caused by neonatal inflammation.

Tau silencing improves AHN in the dDG of adolescent mice that experienced severe inflammation as neonates

Given that AHN is thought to play a significant role in the development and maintenance of memory formation [25], we performed flow cytometry to determine whether Tau ablation affects the proliferation of newborn hippocampal neurons in mice that experienced significant inflammation as neonates. Several combinations of neuronal markers, including the endogenous cell proliferation marker Ki67, were used. The proliferation of neuronal subpopulations positive for sex-determining region Y-box 2 (SOX2; a marker for neural stem cells and progenitor cells), cells double-labelled for SOX2 and GFAP (a marker for radial glia-like cells), and cells positive for doublecortin (a marker for neuroblasts and immature neurons) was significantly greater in the Tau-KO mice than in the WT mice (Fig. 4A–D). These findings demonstrated that in adolescent mice with early-life inflammation, Tau deficiency increased AHN.

Tau silencing may modulate LPS-induced AHN inhibition through phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)-AKT signalling

We assessed the effect of Tau on the proliferation of mouse neurons (N2a) in vitro to clarify the underlying mechanism through which Tau deficiency improves AHN. First, we discovered that treatment with LPS (100 µg/ml) dramatically reduced neuronal survival and that this reduction persisted for 48 h after the LPS injection (Fig. 6A and B). For the LPS treatment in the subsequent cell studies, we chose a dosage of 100 µg/ml. Furthermore, both the LPS-induced and Tau-silenced N2a cells presented increased ROS generation and increased levels of inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β and TNF-α) (Fig. 6C–E), demonstrating that both the LPS-induced and the Tau-silenced N2a cells exhibited a robust inflammatory response after LPS stimulation. We also measured the levels of Tau and phosphorylated Tau and discovered that compared with those in the control group, the levels in the LPS-treated group increased (Fig. 6F). Additionally, both LPS-treated and Tau-silenced cells presented increased levels of Tau protein kinases (phosphorylated GSK-3β and CaMKII) and decreased levels of Tau protein phosphatases (phosphorylated PP2A–C) (Fig. 6G), which further suggested that the posttranslational modification of Tau rather than increased expression of total Tau in vitro and in vivo may be the primary cause of the upregulation of phosphorylated Tau induced by LPS. Following LPS treatment, we continued to monitor cell proliferation. Immunostaining revealed that compared with control cells, LPS-treated cells had higher levels of phosphorylated Tau and lower levels of BrdU (Fig. 6H and I), whereas N2a cell proliferation was markedly increased by Tau protein depletion (Fig. 6H and I).

Tau, a cytoskeletal protein, may limit the expression of PI3K-p85 in the cytoplasm, ultimately suppressing PI3K–AKT signalling, which may prevent cell growth, according to our earlier study [20]. We thus determined whether downregulation of Tau expression promotes N2a cell proliferation by reducing the level of PI3K-p85 in the cytoplasm after LPS injection. For the western blot analysis, we extracted cytosolic and nuclear fractions from the samples. While cytosolic PI3K-p85 expression increased following LPS treatment, Tau deletion markedly reduced PI3K-p85 expression and increased phosphorylated AKT expression in the cytosolic fraction (Fig. 7A–C). Furthermore, Tau silencing in N2a cells reversed the downregulation of total PI3K-p110α and phosphorylated AKT expression caused by LPS (Fig. 7D). Finally, following LPS treatment, the flow cytometry analysis revealed a distinct increase in the number of cell subpopulations stained for both EdU and phosphorylated AKT among the Tau-silenced N2a cells (Fig. 7E–G). These findings suggest that Tau controls PI3K–AKT signalling by influencing the distribution of PI3K-p85 subunits in the cytosol and nucleus, which may alter neuronal proliferation in response to LPS stimulation.

Together, these results show that Tau deficiency increases dendritic structural remodelling in granule neurons to exert a neuroprotective effect on mice exposed to severe inflammation as neonates, which ultimately reduces long-term memory and cognitive impairments during adolescence. It also promotes the nuclear distribution of PI3K-p85 to activate PI3K–AKT signalling and induce neuronal proliferation.

Discussion

Deficits in neuropsychological performance and cognitive challenges in adolescence or early adulthood are examples of late-onset neurodevelopmental impairments caused by the harmful effects of neonatal sepsis on the developing brain [26]. Sepsis-induced damage to solid organs, including the brain, is significantly influenced by the acute systemic inflammatory response [27]. How this acute neuroinflammation causes long-term alterations in neurological function in the central nervous system (CNS) remains unknown. In this study, we showed that administering LPS to neonatal mice caused long-term memory impairments and impaired synapse integrity and AHN in adolescent mice, as evidenced by markedly reduced expression of important synaptic structural proteins, altered dendritic structure of granule neurons, and reduced proliferation and differentiation of newborn hippocampal neurons. Silencing Tau reduced these LPS-induced neurodevelopmental deficits by promoting dendritic structural remodelling in granule neurons and AHN in the dDG of adolescent mice.

Neuroinflammation is a component of sepsis, a systemic inflammatory illness [28, 29]. According to recent research, sepsis caused by caecal ligation and puncture (CLP) activates glial cells, increases TNF-α and IL-1β levels, and affects cognitive function [30]. In addition, 2-day-old C57BL/6 mice received a single injection of subcutaneous lipopolysaccharide (LPS), which also increased the TNF-α and IL-1β levels in the hippocampus and cortex only at 60 days of age, and Tau protein expression was high in the hippocampus and cortex at 120 days of age and in the hippocampus at 180 days of age [31]. Our data support these conclusions by demonstrating that sepsis is linked to increases in proinflammatory cytokine levels and Tau protein levels in the hippocampus following LPS-induced sepsis in neonates. Additionally, the neurological deficits caused by this neonatal neuroinflammation cause behavioural impairments in adulthood. These results are consistent with recent research showing that LPS causes severe neuroinflammation in the mouse hippocampus and that neuroinflammation contributes to memory loss and cognitive impairment in mice with LPS-induced sepsis [10, 32].

Previous studies have shown that the loss of Tau in neurons provides protection against inflammation-induced neurotoxicity. In a cell culture model of neurons and microglia, pretreatment of microglia with LPS led to the secretion of cytotoxic factors, which, under normal conditions, enhances Tau hyperphosphorylation and results in neurodegeneration. However, Tau-knockout neurons exhibit significantly reduced levels of apoptotic markers [33]. Our present study revealed that silencing tau could improve deficits in long-term memory and cognitive function caused by neonatal sepsis, and these studies further supported that Tau deficiency in the brain is protective against inflammation-induced neurotoxicity.

The pathophysiology of brain dysfunction in sepsis is multifactorial. Acute brain damage, including BBB disruption, neural cell death, and white matter damage, has been described [34, 35]. In addition, neonatal immune system responses differ from those of adults in that they interfere with sepsis progression. Increased production of ROS and proinflammatory cytokines, combined with innately low levels of antioxidant defences, has been implicated in the pathogenesis and complications of neonatal sepsis [36, 37]. In neonates, the brain is immature and much more vulnerable to damage by poorly controlled and pervasive inflammation [3]. Thus, the susceptibility of the CNS in neonates is related to a fragile innate immune response.

In addition, the expression of steroids and related genes and that of glucocorticoids in different brain regions are more heterogeneous in response to LPS in neonates [38, 39]. For example, in the prefrontal cortex on postnatal day 5, corticosterone levels were lower, and 11-dehydrocorticosterone levels were higher after LPS treatment. In contrast, the amygdala on postnatal day 5, corticosterone levels were higher, and 11-dehydrocorticosterone levels were lower after LPS treatment. Corticosterone levels were higher in the hippocampus following LPS treatment than in the blood; and 11-deoxycorticosterone levels were lower in the hypothalamus than in the blood after LPS injection in neonates [39]. These data suggest that regional glucocorticoid heterogeneity in the neonatal brain may play an important role in the pathophysiology of brain injury in neonatal sepsis. Thus, future work may be able to better examine these differences in brain region glucocorticoid levels, which may elucidate the mechanism underlying the long-term consequences of early-life immune activation in the brain following neonatal sepsis.

According to the current findings, the expression levels of synaptic proteins, including presynaptic proteins such as synaptophysin and glutamate receptors, on the surface of synapses were considerably decreased in the hippocampal regions of mice with neonatal inflammation. A particular protein of the synaptic vesicle outer membrane, synaptophysin, is a synapse-associated protein that is extensively dispersed in the presynaptic vesicle membrane [40]. It contains the main calcium binding sites on the presynaptic vesicle membrane and is crucial for the development and stability of synapses as well as the release and delivery of neurotransmitters during synaptic vesicle transport [41]. The postsynaptic protein PSD-95 is linked to the development of glutamatergic synapses and the control of dendritic spine size and number [42]. Reduced expression of these proteins suggests the presence of synaptic abnormalities, and the levels of these proteins may serve as molecular indicators of functional synapse development [43]. According to the findings from the current study, animals exposed to inflammation as neonates had significantly lower levels of several presynaptic proteins in the hippocampus. The memory and cognitive abilities of the animals also improved following Tau knockout, which resulted in the restoration of the levels of these markers.

In addition, we discovered that LPS injection altered the expression of glutamate ionotropic receptors. The excitatory neurotransmitter glutamate binds to N-methyl-D-aspartic acid receptors (NMDARs), which are mostly found on the postsynaptic membrane [44]. Research has indicated a possible association between these receptors and the development of cognitive impairment in individuals with sepsis [45]. In the current investigation, we discovered that mice exposed to inflammation as neonates presented significantly lower hippocampal expression of Glu A2 and NMDAR 1. Moreover, dendritic spines, which are spiky extensions of the postsynaptic membrane on the surface of dendrites, provide the structural basis for neuronal connections and signal transduction in the nervous system. The majority of excitatory stimuli that reach the brain are received by dendritic spines [46]. Dendritic spine development, dissociation, enlargement, and atrophy are examples of morphological alterations that typically accompany changes in synaptic plasticity [47]. Additionally, microtubules constantly enter dendritic spines during neuronal activity, adding to the cytoskeleton of dendritic spines, causing their heads to enlarge, encouraging dendritic spine maturation, and increasing the amount of PSD-95 in dendritic spines, which is crucial for memory formation [48–50]. A previous study connected differences in dendritic spine density to cognitive function [51, 52]. In the current investigation, we also discovered that mice that experienced sepsis as neonates had a lower density of dendritic spines. Similarly, following LPS treatment, the levels of glutamate ionotropic receptors increased, and the dendritic structure improved in Tau-KO mice. These findings indicate that by promoting dendritic structural remodelling in granule neurons, Tau silencing can mitigate the long-term memory and cognitive deficits caused by neonatal sepsis.

The hippocampus is a key hub that processes sensory data. The increased neuronal flexibility maintained in this structure is due to the ongoing integration of new neurons via AHN. AHN is essential for controlling mood, forgetfulness, and episodic memory [53]. More than 120 animal species have been shown to produce new neurons in their mature hippocampus [54]. Neural stem cells in the DG of the adult hippocampus retain the ability to produce multipotent, self-renewing neurons in a variety of species [55]. Following neural stem cell differentiation, direct or indirect stimulation of hippocampal neurogenesis, such as through exercise or the consumption of green tea flavonoids, can improve synaptic plasticity and memory performance [56, 57]. However, stress or pathogenic stimulation can cause adult neural stem cells or neuronal progenitor cells to proliferate and differentiate abnormally, destroying hippocampal neurogenesis and impairing cognitive ability [58, 59]. For instance, adult neurogenesis in the hippocampal DG subventricular zone is disrupted in a rat model of sepsis induced by CLP surgery [60]. In mice, LPS-induced neuroinflammation decreases neural stem cell proliferation and survival in the adult hippocampal dentate gyrus as well as the differentiation of neural stem cells into immature and mature neurons [61], resulting in learning and memory deficits [6]. Therefore, we hypothesize that deficiencies in AHN may be the cause of the learning and memory impairments observed in septic mice. In this study, we discovered that sepsis increased hippocampal Tau expression and that sepsis-induced abnormalities in AHN, long-term memory, and cognitive function were ameliorated upon Tau deletion. These findings are consistent with earlier research linking Tau overexpression to Alzheimer’s disease-related cognitive decline [62]. Therefore, our findings imply that Tau inhibition increases the survival and proliferation of newborn neurons, potentially to offset the catastrophic effect of neonatal sepsis on inducing long-term memory impairments.

As a crucial regulatory component, PI3K-p85 interacts with multiple proteins to control the activation of the PI3K–AKT signalling pathway. For example, the relationships between bromodomain-containing protein 7 (BRD7) and PI3K-p85 [63], C-type lectin-like receptor 2, leucine zipper tumour suppressor 2, or 4R Tau suppress PI3K–AKT signalling [20, 64, 65]. In contrast, the interaction of PI3K-p85 with CD133 activates PI3K–AKT signalling [66]. Thus, PI3K-p85 seems to play different roles in controlling PI3K–AKT signalling through its interacting proteins. Tau strictly controlled the cellular distribution of PI3K-p85 in the current study. Tau deficiency caused the nuclear translocation of PI3K-p85 and subsequently activated PI3K–AKT signalling, which may in turn promote neuronal proliferation. The specific molecular mechanism through which Tau knockout affects PI3K–AKT signalling and AHN in the context of neonatal neuroinflammation needs to be further explored in the future.

In summary, persistent inflammation in the brain after neonatal sepsis is associated with glial activation, damage to the dendritic structure of granule neurons and AHN, and neurodevelopmental deficits. Inhibiting Tau expression may improve long-term memory and cognitive performance in mice that survive neonatal sepsis, suggesting that Tau may be a possible therapeutic target for the neurodevelopmental damage caused by neonatal sepsis.
