CT-based habitat analysis combined with multi-channel deep learning for predicting early recurrence after pancreatic cancer resection: a multicenter study
Authors: Jialun Peng, Hongyu Wu, Xingyu Chen, Zhibing Ou, Xiaoli Yang, Rong Ma, Yiming Liu, Xuesong Xu, Chengyou Du, Shengwei Li, Yu You, Jinzheng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) has a poor prognosis, with high early recurrence rates after curative resection. Current prediction methods, based on clinicopathological features or conventional radiomics, often fail to capture intratumoral heterogeneity (ITH), a key driver of recurrence. Computed tomography (CT)-based habitat analysis quantifies ITH by identifying phenotypically distinct tumor subregions, while deep learning (DL) can extract complex imaging patterns. Their integration may improve recurrence risk assessment. This study aimed to develop and validate a fusion model that integrates CT-based habitat analysis, a 2.5D convolutional neural network (CNN)-Transformer DL framework, and clinicopathological features to noninvasively predict early recurrence (within one year) risk after PDAC resection.MethodsIn this multicenter retrospective study, 346 patients with resected PDAC were included from four institutions. Tumors were segmented into three habitat subregions via unsupervised K‑means clustering. Radiomic features from these subregions constructed the HabitatAll model. In parallel, a DL model was built using a 2.5D CNN–Transformer architecture. Predictive scores from both models were integrated with key clinicopathological variables through ridge regression to develop the fusion model (HADLC). Model interpretability was examined using SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) and Grad‑CAM (Gradient‑weighted Class Activation Mapping). Model performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), accuracy, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsThe HADLC model showed superior predictive ability, achieving AUCs of 0.977 (training), 0.916 (internal test), and 0.838–0.866 (external validation), outperforming the standalone HabitatAll, DL, and Clinic models. It demonstrated good calibration and provided higher net clinical benefit across most decision thresholds. Interpretability analyses revealed key imaging phenotypes linked to aggressive tumor biology.ConclusionThe HADLC model effectively integrates multimodal information to accurately assess early postoperative recurrence risk in PDAC, providing a robust, non-invasive imaging biomarker to potentially guide personalized treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08370-0.Keywords: Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, Prognosis, Habitat analysis, Deep learning, Radiomics

Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is a highly aggressive malignant tumor of the digestive tract, characterized by an extremely poor prognosis, with a median survival of less than 6 months and a five-year survival rate of only 10–13% [1, 2]. Currently, surgical resection remains the only potentially curative treatment for PDAC [3]. However, even after successful curative resection, the 5-year survival rate is approximately 20–30%. Moreover, 50–80% of patients experience early recurrence within one year post-surgery, with a median recurrence-free survival of only 12 to 16 months [4, 5], which significantly limits long-term survival benefits [6]. Therefore, the accurate identification of patients at high risk of recurrence, either before or soon after surgery, is of critical clinical importance for developing individualized follow-up strategies and treatment plans, such as neoadjuvant therapy, postoperative adjuvant therapy options, or clinical trial enrollment [7].

Currently, the prediction of postoperative recurrence in PDAC primarily relies on clinicopathological features, such as tumor size, lymph node metastasis status, differentiation grade, and carbohydrate antigen 19 − 9 (CA19-9) levels, or on conventional radiomics approaches that generally treat the tumor as a homogeneous entity to construct predictive models [7, 8]. Nevertheless, these methods often fail to capture deeper-level information reflecting the intrinsic biological behavior of tumors and are particularly inadequate in representing intratumoral heterogeneity (ITH) [9]. ITH, which refers to genetic, epigenetic, and functional differences among various cellular subpopulations within a tumor, is a key driver of treatment resistance and tumor recurrence [10]. In contrast to traditional radiomics, which extracts global features from the entire tumor region and may average out regional differences [11], habitat analysis is an emerging computational technique that noninvasively characterizes ITH by partitioning the tumor into distinct subregions based on voxel-wise phenotypic patterns. These subregions may correspond to biologically meaningful areas such as cellular-rich zones, necrotic cores, or hypoxic regions. By quantifying features from each subregion separately, habitat analysis preserves spatial heterogeneity and provides a more detailed view of tumor biology [10, 12].

In recent years, deep learning (DL), particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), has been widely adopted in medical image analysis due to its ability to automatically learn hierarchical deep features directly from raw images [13, 14]. Promising results have been achieved in predicting early recurrence after resection of cervical [15], gastric [16], and liver cancers [17]. However, traditional 2D CNNs do not capture inter-slice information effectively [18], and 3D CNNs are often constrained by high computational costs and large data requirements [19]. A practical alternative is 2.5D deep learning, which uses the largest cross-sectional area of a tumor together with its adjacent multi-slice images as multi-channel input. This approach effectively utilizes 3D contextual information while substantially reducing model complexity and the risk of overfitting [20]. Furthermore, the Transformer architecture, with its self-attention mechanism, excels at capturing long-range dependencies and global feature relationships within images [21]. Thus, combining the strong local feature extraction capability of CNNs with the global semantic modeling capacity of Transformers offers considerable potential for more comprehensively capturing complex imaging patterns associated with malignant tumor behavior [22].

Therefore, this study aims to develop and validate a fusion model that combines CT-based habitat analysis with a 2.5D CNN-Transformer deep learning framework to noninvasively assess the risk of early recurrence following PDAC resection. By overcoming the constraints of conventional imaging through a more precise characterization of ITH, this approach is expected to yield more reliable imaging biomarkers to inform clinical decision-making.

Methods

Ethics statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Institutional Review Boards of four tertiary medical centers. The approving institutions and their respective approval numbers are as follows: The Second Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Center 1, 2025IIT234), The First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Center 2, CY2025-602-01), The Affiliated Hospital of Southwest Medical University (Center 3, KY2025431), and The First Affiliated Hospital of University of South China (Center 4, 2025KS-GD-38-01). Due to the retrospective design and use of de‑identified data, informed consent was waived.

Study participants

We retrospectively and consecutively collected data from patients who underwent surgical resection with pathologically confirmed PDAC at four tertiary medical centers between January 2016 and December 2023, with follow-up censored in December 2024. Clinical and histopathological data were extracted for analysis. Early recurrence was defined as disease recurrence within one year after radical resection [6]. To ensure consistency and minimize selection bias across the multicenter cohort, we applied uniform inclusion and exclusion criteria at all participating centers. Inclusion criteria were: (1) resectable PDAC, as assessed by a multidisciplinary team (including surgery, oncology, and radiology) and meeting the NCCN Clinical Practice Guidelines in Oncology for Pancreatic Adenocarcinoma (Version 2.2024) criteria for resectability (i.e., tumors with no major arterial contact and either no contact or ≤ 180° contact with the portal vein or superior mesenteric vein, without venous contour irregularity, based on preoperative CT); (2) age ≥ 18 years; (3) completion of contrast-enhanced CT within 30 days prior to surgery; (4) no prior history of other malignancies; and (5) no severe concurrent comorbidities that could potentially interfere with survival or follow-up compliance. Exclusion criteria included: (1) receipt of neoadjuvant therapy; (2) death within 30 days after surgery; (3) missing follow-up data within one year post-surgery [23]; or (4) poor CT image quality or inability to delineate habitat regions (Supplementary Fig. 1). To further mitigate potential biases inherent to retrospective data, consecutive patients meeting the eligibility criteria were enrolled from each center. Data completeness was independently verified by two reviewers; cases with missing key clinical or follow-up variables were excluded, and no imputation was performed for missing data. Additionally, potential selection bias was assessed by comparing baseline characteristics and recurrence rates across the four centers. Patients from Center 1 were randomly divided into a training set and an internal test set in a 7:3 ratio. Patients from Centers 2, 3, and 4 served as independent external validation cohorts. CT image acquisition and processing

All patients underwent contrast-enhanced abdominal CT scans prior to surgery. The portal venous phase was used for subsequent analysis. Detailed information on CT scanner models, manufacturers, and acquisition parameters from each participating institution are provided in Supplementary Table 1. While complete standardization of acquisition protocols across centers was not feasible due to the retrospective nature of the study, all images were required to meet predefined quality criteria for diagnostic abdominal CT to be included in the analysis. These criteria included: (1) complete coverage of the pancreatic parenchyma and the tumor lesion; (2) slice thickness ≤ 5 mm; (3) absence of significant motion artifacts or metallic artifacts that could obscure the tumor boundary; (4) adequate enhancement of the portal venous phase, defined as clear visualization of the portal vein and distinct enhancement of the pancreatic parenchyma relative to the tumor, enabling confident tumor delineation. To further mitigate the impact of interscanner and protocol variability, a uniform preprocessing pipeline was applied to all images prior to analysis. This preprocessing consisted of resampling all images to an isotropic voxel size of 1 × 1 × 1 mm³ using B-spline interpolation and applying a standardized window setting (width 300 HU, level 40 HU) to enhance pancreatic tumor conspicuity. Regions of interest (ROI) encompassing the pancreatic tumors were manually delineated in consensus by two experienced radiologists using ITK-SNAP (Version 3.6.0). Any discrepancies in segmentation were adjudicated by a third radiologist, whose judgment served as the final decision. All three radiologists have over 10 years of experience in abdominal imaging and are affiliated with tertiary teaching hospitals.

Habitat region generation

To further quantify and visualize ITH, local features were computed for each voxel using a 1 × 1 × 1 moving window. Each voxel was characterized by a 19-dimensional radiomic feature vector derived from five categories: first-order features, Gray Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM), Gray Level Run Length Matrix (GLRLM), Gray Level Size Zone Matrix (GLSZM), and Neighborhood Gray Tone Difference Matrix (NGTDM) features. The names of these features and their corresponding visualization heatmaps are presented in Fig. 2. Subsequently, an unsupervised K‑means clustering algorithm was applied to partition the tumor into distinct subregions. The number of clusters was explored from 2 to 10, and the optimal cluster number was determined using the Calinski-Harabasz (CH) index, which evaluates cluster compactness and separation; a higher CH index indicates better clustering performance. The CH index is a commonly used method for determining the optimal number of clusters in habitat analysis, and its effectiveness has been validated in previous radiomic studies [9, 24]. The clustering analysis was implemented using the scikit-learn package in Python.

Habitat region feature selection and model construction

Following the Imaging Biomarker Standardization Initiative (IBSI) guidelines [25], radiomic features were extracted from each habitat subregion using the Pyradiomics package. These features encompass geometric, intensity, and textural characteristics. Geometric features describe the three-dimensional morphological properties of the tumor, providing a spatial representation of its structure. Intensity features quantify first-order statistics of voxel intensities, reflecting intratumoral homogeneity or heterogeneity. Textural features characterize more complex patterns by quantifying second- and higher-order spatial distributions of intensity values, capturing subtle spatial relationships among voxels [26]. Detailed parameters of these features are available on the PyRadiomics website (https://pyradiomics.readthedocs.io/en/latest/). A total of 1834 features were extracted from each habitat subregion. Moreover, given the multi-center nature of this study, we performed an exploratory analysis to assess potential batch effects arising from variations in CT scanners and acquisition protocols across the four institutions (Supplementary Fig. 2). As no significant batch effects were detected, all subsequent feature processing was conducted on the original radiomic features to preserve biological signal integrity.

The habitat modeling framework included individual habitat subregion models (H1, H2 and H3 model) and an integrated habitat model (HabitatAll model). The latter was developed using an early feature‑fusion strategy to synthesize the characteristics of each subregion. To ensure the robustness of subsequent analyses, feature reliability was addressed prior to selection. All tumor regions of interest (ROIs) were manually segmented in consensus by two experienced radiologists, thereby minimizing inter-observer variability and enhancing the reproducibility of feature extraction [24, 27]. Consequently, intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC)-based filtering was not applied during feature selection, as ICC is ill-suited for unsupervised habitat features where subregions are generated de novo for each tumor through clustering, rather than representing predefined structures amenable to stability assessment across repeated segmentations [9, 24]. With feature reliability established, feature selection was then performed. Initially, Z-score normalization was applied to standardize the radiomic features, ensuring they followed a normal distribution. Subsequently, the Pearson correlation coefficient was used to assess multicollinearity among the features. For any pair of features with a correlation exceeding 0.9, only the feature demonstrating superior diagnostic performance was retained. To prevent overfitting, the minimum Redundancy Maximum Relevance (mRMR) algorithm was employed to select the top 10 features. The Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) logistic regression was then applied to further refine the selection, with the optimal regularization parameter λ determined through 5-fold cross-validation, yielding the most predictive features. Due to the imbalance between positive and negative samples in the dataset, the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) was used for data balancing. To prevent data leakage, SMOTE was applied exclusively to the training folds during cross-validation; no synthetic samples were generated from or included in the validation folds. All data preprocessing and augmentation steps were strictly nested within the training loop to ensure the independence and integrity of validation performance estimates. To identify the optimal predictive model, five widely used machine learning algorithms were evaluated: Logistic Regression (LR), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Random Forest (RF), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) [28, 29]. All models were trained on the same dataset, with hyperparameters optimized using Grid Search combined with 5-fold cross-validation. Model selection was based on the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Finally, the SHAP algorithm was utilized to assess the contribution of each feature in the final model to the prediction outcomes.

Construction of a multi-channel 2.5D deep learning model

Given the moderate sample size of the training cohort and the inherent risk of overfitting associated with fully 3D models when training data are limited, a 2.5D design was adopted to balance model complexity with generalization capacity. The axial slice showing the largest tumor ROI cross-sectional area was identified as the central slice. This slice, along with its adjacent superior and inferior slices, formed a set of three consecutive slices. For each slice, the minimum bounding rectangle enclosing the tumor was extracted. All cropped regions were resized to 224 × 224 pixels using nearest-neighbor interpolation. These processed images were then combined into a three-channel 2.5D input and fed into a ResNet-18 model pre-trained on ImageNet for training. During training, real-time data augmentation was applied to improve generalization: random cropping with a padding of 4 pixels on each side (followed by a crop back to 224 × 224), and random horizontal and vertical flipping, each with a 50% probability. In the testing phase, only image normalization was performed without any augmentation. The model was optimized using stochastic gradient descent with fine-tuning of key network weights guided by the cross-entropy loss function. The learning rate was set to 0.01, batch size to 64, and training proceeded for 50 epochs. To mitigate overfitting, a dropout layer (rate = 0.5) was inserted into the classifier (the FC layer), and early stopping with a patience of 10 epochs was employed, monitoring the loss on the internal validation set. After training, 512-dimensional deep learning features were extracted from the penultimate layer of ResNet-18, specifically the global average pooling layer. To improve interpretability, gradient-weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM) was applied to the final convolutional layer of the last residual block, producing heatmaps that highlight image regions influential in the classification decision.

For classification, we considered two architectures. First, as a baseline, the 512-dimensional features were directly fed into a fully connected (FC) layer followed by a softmax activation to produce recurrence probabilities; this model is referred to as ResNet18-FC. Second, to more effectively capture global feature interactions and enhance the representational power beyond a simple FC layer, we introduced an additional Transformer-based classification module. The extracted features were passed into a Transformer encoder consisting of three identical layers, each containing a multi-head self-attention block (8 heads, 64-dimensional key/query per head) and a position-wise feed-forward network (inner dimension 2048). A dropout rate of 0.1 was applied after each sub-layer to prevent overfitting within the Transformer module. The module was optimized using SGD with a momentum of 0.9 and a cosine-annealing learning rate schedule (initial 0.001) over 100 epochs, with a batch size of 128. Early stopping was also applied to this module, with training halted if the validation loss did not decrease for 15 consecutive epochs. The final classification probabilities for recurrence and non-recurrence were produced by a softmax output layer, enabling end-to-end prediction. This combined architecture, integrating 2.5D CNN feature extraction with Transformer-based relational modeling, is referred to as the DL model. To validate the contribution of the Transformer module, we performed an ablation study comparing the performance of the DL model (with Transformer) against the baseline ResNet18-FC model.

Development of clinic and fusion models

Univariate logistic regression was first performed to screen for clinicopathological factors associated with early recurrence (P < 0.05). Candidate variables were then entered into multivariate logistic regression. If complete separation was encountered, precluding reliable estimation using standard methods, collinearity among variables was assessed using Cramer’s V; for variables with high collinearity, the clinically more relevant variable was retained. Firth’s penalized likelihood estimation was subsequently applied to obtain bias-reduced estimates and identify independent predictors for early recurrence. These predictors were then used to construct the Clinic model using the same five machine learning algorithms as the habitat models (LR, SVM, RF, LightGBM, and XGBoost). To integrate multimodal information, we subsequently combined the radiomics score (HabitatAll score) derived from the HabitatAll model and the DL score from the DL model with the significant predictors from the Clinic model, and constructed a fusion model (HADLC model) using ridge regression. Both the HabitatAll score and the DL score were generated as probability outputs ranging from 0 to 1, representing the likelihood of early recurrence. Given their inherently consistent scale, no additional normalization was applied prior to fusion in the ridge regression model, ensuring the original probabilistic interpretations were preserved. Furthermore, to enhance clinical interpretation and utility, a nomogram was developed based on this fusion model to visually represent the contribution of each variable to predicting the risk of early recurrence.

Model evaluation

The diagnostic performance of the models was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the corresponding area under the curve (AUC), with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) calculated to quantify the precision of the estimates. To statistically compare model performance, differences in AUC values between models were formally evaluated using DeLong’s test. Furthermore, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were derived to comprehensively evaluate model performance. To quantify the improvement in model performance, the integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) and net reclassification improvement (NRI) indices were calculated, which reflect enhanced overall discriminatory ability and risk classification accuracy, respectively. Model calibration was assessed using calibration curves, and clinical net benefit across different decision thresholds was analyzed with decision curve analysis (DCA). To enable risk stratification for clinical application, the optimal cutoff value of the HADLC model’s predicted probability was determined in the training cohort by maximizing the Youden index. Patients with a predicted probability equal to or above this threshold were classified as high-risk for early recurrence. The discriminative ability of this cutoff was further validated by comparing recurrence-free survival between the high- and conventional-risk groups using Kaplan-Meier analysis and the log-rank test.

Statistical analysis

The normality of continuous clinical and pathological variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were compared with the t-test, and non-normally distributed variables were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square (χ²) test. All analyses were performed on the OnekeyAI platform (version 4.9.1) using Python 3.7.12. Statistical testing was conducted with Statsmodels 0.13.2, and radiomic feature extraction was implemented using PyRadiomics 3.0.1. Batch effect evaluation was performed using matplotlib (version 3.5.1), seaborn (version 0.11.2), and neuroCombat (version 0.2.12). For machine learning modeling, LR, SVM, and RF were implemented with scikit-learn 1.0.2; LightGBM and XGBoost were applied via their scikit-learn-compatible interfaces using LightGBM 4.1.0 and XGBoost 1.7.6, respectively. The deep learning framework was based on PyTorch 1.11.0; models were trained on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090D GPU and optimized with CUDA 11.3.1 and cuDNN 8.2.1. Firth’s penalized likelihood estimation (logistf v1.26.1) was performed in R (v4.2.1) to address complete separation, and multicollinearity diagnostics were conducted using the caret package (v7.0.1).

Results

Patient baseline characteristics

A total of 346 patients were included in this study and categorized into the following cohorts: a training cohort (n = 104), an internal validation cohort (n = 45), and three independent external validation cohorts—external validation cohort 1 (n = 90), external validation cohort 2 (n = 61), and external validation cohort 3 (n = 46). The clinical and pathological baseline characteristics of all cohorts are summarized in Table 1. Most baseline variables were well-balanced across cohorts, except for Albumin and Venous invasion, which showed statistically significant differences (both P < 0.05). Furthermore, the early postoperative recurrence rate (within one year) was 70.19% (73/104) in the training cohort, 71.11% (32/45) in the internal validation cohort, 70.00% (63/90) in external validation cohort 1, 72.13% (44/61) in external validation cohort 2, and 71.74% (33/46) in external validation cohort 3. No statistically significant difference in recurrence rates was observed among the cohorts (P = 0.998).

Prediction performance of habitat models

The CH-score reached its maximum at a cluster number of 3 (Supplementary Fig. 3), consistent with prior studies in solid tumors [27, 30, 31]. Accordingly, tumors were partitioned into three habitat subregions for model construction, designated as Habitat Subregion 1 (H1), Habitat Subregion 2 (H2), and Habitat Subregion 3 (H3). Following feature selection, 10, 9, and 6 features were retained from H1, H2, and H3, respectively. The selected features and their corresponding weights are detailed in Supplementary Fig. 4A-C. After evaluating multiple machine learning algorithms, XGBoost demonstrated superior predictive performance across all individual subregion models as well as the integrated HabitatAll model (Supplementary Fig. 5A-D) and was therefore selected for subsequent analysis. In the training cohort, the H1 model exhibited the highest predictive performance among the individual subregion models, achieving an overall accuracy of 82.7% and an AUC of 0.893 (95% CI: 0.832–0.954). This was followed by the H3 model (accuracy: 79.8%; AUC: 0.875; 95% CI: 0.805–0.944). In contrast, the H2 model showed notably lower performance, with an accuracy of 69.2% and an AUC of 0.819 (95% CI: 0.729–0.909). A summary of the predictive performance for each subregion model is provided in Supplementary Table 2.

The integrated HabitatAll model, constructed by fusing 6 selected features (3 from H1, 2 from H2, 1 from H3; Supplementary Fig. 4D), outperformed all habitat individual models. In the training cohort, it achieved an accuracy of 86.5% and an AUC of 0.908 (95% CI: 0.848–0.968). The model also demonstrated robust performance in the internal test set and external validation sets 1–3, with accuracies of 82.2%, 82.2%, 77.0%, and 78.3%, and corresponding AUCs of 0.827 (95% CI: 0.682–0.972), 0.794 (95% CI: 0.674–0.915), 0.814 (95% CI: 0.693–0.936), and 0.805 (95% CI: 0.650–0.961). To elucidate the predictive mechanism of the HabitatAll model, we visualized its decision process using a decision tree and employed SHAP waterfall plots to quantify feature contributions to recurrence risk prediction (Fig. 3A-C). The top three contributing features were: exponential_ngtdm_Contrast_h1, wavelet_HLH_gldm_SmallDependenceEmphasis_h1, and wavelet_LLL_firstorder_Skewness_h3. To clinically validate the habitat subregions, we grouped patients by tumor differentiation grade and compared the volume fractions of Habitats 1, 2, and 3 among the groups. Results showed that Habitat 1 was predominant in poorly differentiated tumors, whereas Habitat 3 was predominant in well-differentiated tumors (Supplementary Fig. 6). Additionally, Fig. 4 depicts the spatial distribution and proportional representation of habitat subregions in representative patients with high versus low recurrence risk, while waterfall and force plots offer individualized interpretation by visualizing how specific features influence predictions—depicted as forces that shift the model output from the baseline SHAP value of − 0.02, corresponding to the cohort average.

Prediction performance of the deep learning models

We first evaluated the baseline ResNet18-FC model, which used a FC layer for classification after feature extraction. In the training cohort, ResNet18-FC achieved an AUC of 0.799 (95% CI: 0.695–0.903) and an accuracy of 87.5%. In the internal test set and external validation sets 1–3, it achieved AUCs of 0.721 (95% CI: 0.563–0.880) with 64.4% accuracy, 0.640 (95% CI: 0.511–0.768) with 60.0% accuracy, 0.668 (95% CI: 0.496–0.841) with 67.2% accuracy, and 0.671 (95% CI: 0.494–0.849) with 65.2% accuracy, respectively. The DL model, which incorporated a Transformer module, achieved an AUC of 0.926 (95% CI: 0.862–0.990) with an accuracy of 90.4% in the training cohort. In the internal test set and external validation sets 1–3, the DL model achieved AUCs of 0.817 (95% CI: 0.693–0.942) with 66.7% accuracy, 0.702 (95% CI: 0.570–0.834) with 73.3% accuracy, 0.750 (95% CI: 0.606–0.894) with 78.7% accuracy, and 0.753 (95% CI: 0.589–0.917) with 67.4% accuracy, respectively. The DL model consistently outperformed the baseline ResNet18-FC model across all cohorts (Supplementary Fig. 7). Table 2 summarizes the accuracy, AUC, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV for both the HabitatAll model and the DL model across the training, internal test, and external validation cohorts. Additionally, Fig. 5 presents CT images from representative PDAC patients with high and low recurrence risk, alongside their corresponding Grad-CAM heatmaps, to visualize the attention regions identified by the DL model.

Predictive performance of the clinic model

Univariate logistic regression identified 31 clinicopathological factors significantly associated with early postoperative recurrence (P < 0.05, Supplementary Table 3). However, when these variables were entered into standard multivariate logistic regression, complete separation was encountered for N stage and pathological lymph node status, precluding reliable estimation. Collinearity diagnostics revealed complete collinearity between these two variables (Cramer’s V = 1; Supplementary Fig. 8); therefore, pathological lymph node status was excluded and N stage retained, as it provides a more comprehensive clinical classification [1]. To address the separation issue while including all relevant predictors, we applied Firth’s penalized likelihood estimation, which yielded three independent predictors of early recurrence: neural invasion (OR = 3.527, 95% CI: 1.097–14.792, P = 0.034), degree of differentiation (OR = 0.149, 95% CI: 0.033–0.438, P < 0.001), and N stage (OR = 3.496, 95% CI: 1.055–18.631, P = 0.040) (Supplementary Table 4).

These three variables were used to construct the Clinic model. Among five machine learning algorithms evaluated, XGBoost demonstrated the best predictive performance (Supplementary Fig. 5E) and was selected for consistency with the habitat models. The Clinic model achieved an overall accuracy of 81.7% and an AUC of 0.851 (95% CI: 0.770–0.931) in the training cohort. In the internal test set and external validation sets 1, 2, and 3, the overall accuracies were 77.8%, 80.0%, 73.8%, and 65.2%, respectively, with corresponding AUC values of 0.773 (95% CI: 0.630–0.916), 0.733 (95% CI: 0.607–0.860), 0.747 (95% CI: 0.623–0.872), and 0.741 (95% CI: 0.595–0.887) (Table 2).

Predictive performance of the fusion model

As shown in Fig. 6A-E, the HADLC model achieved superior predictive performance across all cohorts. In the training cohort, it attained an AUC of 0.977 (95% CI: 0.956–0.999) with an overall accuracy of 90.4%. In the internal test set and external validation cohorts 1–3, the HADLC model maintained robust performance, with AUCs of 0.916 (95% CI: 0.835–0.997), 0.838 (95% CI: 0.738–0.939), 0.866 (95% CI: 0.762–0.970), and 0.858 (95% CI: 0.721–0.995), respectively, and corresponding overall accuracies of 88.9%, 81.1%, 86.9%, and 89.1% (Table 2). For clinical applicability, the model was presented as a nomogram, which indicated that the HabitatAll and DL scores contributed more substantially than clinicopathological predictors in predicting early recurrence following PDAC resection (Fig. 7). As detailed in Supplementary Fig. 9, pairwise comparisons of model performance using DeLong’s test revealed that the HADLC model significantly outperformed the Clinic model across multiple cohorts, whereas the HabitatAll and DL models did not show statistically significant differences compared to the Clinic model. Additionally, the HADLC model showed significantly better performance than the DL model in certain external validation sets. IDI and NRI analyses further indicated a trend toward improved integrated discrimination and risk reclassification (Supplementary Fig. 10–11). Calibration curves revealed that the HADLC model exhibited strong agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes in both the training and multiple validation sets, with curves closest to the ideal calibration line, suggesting reliable predictive accuracy (Fig. 6F-J). Decision curve analysis confirmed that, across most threshold probabilities, the HADLC model provided a higher net clinical benefit than all single models, as well as the “treat-all” or “treat-none” strategies, supporting its practical utility in clinical decision-making (Fig. 6K-O). Furthermore, based on the Youden index, the optimal cutoff value for the HADLC model’s predicted probability was 0.868 in the training cohort. When applying this threshold for risk stratification, the high-risk group exhibited significantly worse recurrence-free survival compared to the conventional-risk group (log-rank P < 0.001, Supplementary Fig. 12).

Discussion

Accurate identifying of PDAC patients at high risk of recurrence, either before surgery or in the early postoperative period, is crucial for optimizing postoperative management and improving long-term outcomes [32]. However, current clinical biomarkers and conventional imaging methods provide limited predictive value [7]. In this study, we developed and validated an innovative fusion model (HADLC) that integrates CT-based tumor habitat analysis, a 2.5D CNN-Transformer deep learning architecture, and key clinicopathological features to predict early postoperative recurrence risk in PDAC. The HADLC model demonstrated superior predictive performance across training, internal validation, and multiple external validation cohorts, with AUC values ranging from 0.838 to 0.977 and overall accuracy between 81.1% and 90.4%. It outperformed all individual models, including the habitat models (H1, H2, H3, and HabitatAll), the DL model, and the Clinic model. Moreover, the model exhibited good calibration, and decision curve analysis confirmed its ability to provide higher net clinical benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities underscoring its strong potential for clinical translation. These results suggest that the integration of ITH, quantified via habitat analysis, with global tumor imaging patterns extracted through deep learning, yields a more comprehensive and powerful imaging biomarker for assessing PDAC aggressiveness.

Conventional radiomics constructs predictive models by extracting quantitative features—such as morphological, textural, and intensity-based characteristics—from the entire tumor ROI [33]. This approach has shown considerable potential in cancer diagnosis and prognosis prediction [34]. However, tumors often consist of distinct subregions with diverse biological behaviors, including highly cellular areas, necrotic cores, stroma‑rich zones, and hypoxic regions [35]. By treating the tumor as a homogeneous entity, conventional methods inherently average out this heterogeneous composition, thereby failing to capture spatial variations within the tumor [12]. This limitation constrains further improvements in predictive accuracy, particularly for malignancies such as PDAC, which exhibit substantial genomic and phenotypic diversity [36]. Indeed, ITH is a key driver of treatment failure and recurrence in PDAC [1, 37]. Previous studies applying conventional radiomics to predict postoperative recurrence in PDAC reported limited performance, with AUCs ranging from 0.717 to 0.737 in the study by Yan et al. [38], and only 0.664 to 0.738 in the work by Wang et al., even with multi-sequence CT feature integration [39].

To address this challenge, our study introduces habitat analysis, an emerging imaging technique. Unlike conventional radiomics, which extracts global features, habitat analysis employs unsupervised clustering to partition the tumor into multiple phenotypically distinct subregions, or habitats, based on voxel-wise local characteristics [40]. This enables non-invasive quantification of spatial ITH [12]. Consistent with previous findings in breast [27], head and neck [30], and liver cancers [31], we successfully delineated three distinct habitat subregions within PDAC tumors, each exhibiting significantly different imaging phenotypes. These subregions contributed differentially to recurrence risk prediction, with subregion 1 demonstrating the highest discriminative performance. Visualization analysis revealed that subregion 1 occupied the largest proportion in tumors of high-recurrence-risk PDAC patients and was significantly elevated compared to low-risk cases, suggesting that this imaging phenotype may be associated with more aggressive biological behavior [12]. While the precise histopathological correlates (such as areas of high cellular density, necrosis, or active invasive fronts) remain to be definitively validated, this finding highlights the potential of habitat imaging to capture prognostically relevant intratumoral heterogeneity that is often overlooked in conventional visual assessment or traditional radiomics [41]. Recent research by Smeets et al. indicated that MCT4 high-expression/heterogeneity patterns in PDAC strongly align with [18 F]FDG PET high-uptake/heterogeneity patterns, and this metabolically active phenotype is significantly associated with elevated pan-cytokeratin expression and poorer overall survival. This finding suggests a potential link between imaging phenotypes, ITH, and microscopic biological behavior in PDAC, providing indirect support for the hypothesis that imaging-defined habitats, such as subregion 1, may carry biological significance [42]. We further developed a habitat fusion model (HabitatAll) that integrated heterogeneity information from multiple subregions. It outperformed any single subregion, achieving an AUC of 0.908 in the training set and AUCs of 0.794–0.827 in internal test sets and multiple external validation cohorts, surpassing previous multicenter studies that relied solely on radiomics models for predicting PDAC postoperative recurrence [38, 39]. This indicates that combined modeling of habitat subregions allows more comprehensive and systematic capture of PDAC ITH, thereby improving predictive accuracy. These findings align with Zhang et al.’s results in hepatocellular carcinoma, where integrating features from multiple habitat subregions noninvasively characterized intratumoral and peritumoral microenvironment heterogeneity and significantly correlated with microvascular invasion and prognosis [43]. Similarly, Ye et al. found that a fusion model incorporating four habitat subregions more effectively predicted pathological complete response to neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy in non-small cell lung cancer compared to traditional radiomics [44].

The SHAP-based interpretability analysis provided global insights into the model and enabled precise identification of key imaging features driving predictive outcomes. Among the six most influential features, three originated from the H1 subregion, with two ranking within the top three: exponential_ngtdm_Contrast_h1 and wavelet_HLH_gldm_SmallDependenceEmphasis_h1. The strong contribution of exponential_ngtdm_Contrast_h1 suggests pronounced gray‑level intensity variations within H1, an imaging phenotype that could reflect underlying complex spatial structures often observed in aggressive tumors, such as those potentially arising from regional differences in cellular density, necrosis, or fibrosis within the tumor microenvironment [37, 45]. The significance of wavelet_HLH_gldm_SmallDependenceEmphasis_h1 indicates the presence of numerous small, homogeneous local regions in wavelet detail subbands. This textural pattern may represent an imaging surrogate for microscale tumor heterogeneity, which could be associated with biological features such as scattered micro-necrotic foci or clustered tumor cells [10]. These feature‑level findings underscore the critical role of the H1 subregion in prediction and its potential association with PDAC malignant behavior.

For DL model development, this study employed a hybrid architecture integrating 2.5D CNN and Transformer, effectively combining local details with global contextual information while reducing computational complexity [46]. While fully 3D CNNs can theoretically capture the richest spatial context, they introduce a substantially larger number of trainable parameters, which significantly increases the risk of overfitting, particularly in medical imaging studies where large, annotated datasets are often difficult to acquire [19]. The 2.5D approach, by using three adjacent slices as input, captures essential volumetric information from the tumor’s vicinity and its internal structure with far fewer parameters, thereby balancing the need for 3D contextual features with model parsimony to enhance generalizability. In the study by Zhu et al. on predicting outcomes after neoadjuvant chemotherapy for esophageal cancer, the 2.5D CNN model demonstrated significantly higher AUC and accuracy than the 3D CNN model [46]. An ablation study further validated the design, showing that integrating the Transformer module improved predictive performance over using only FC layers. The self-attention mechanism allows the model to re-weight and refine the CNN-extracted features, capturing higher-order feature interactions that may be overlooked by a simple fully connected layer [47]. This approach aligns with methodologies used by Huang et al. in predicting complications after radiotherapy for cervical cancer and Geng et al. in assessing pathological response to immunotherapy in lung cancer, both confirming the accuracy and superiority of the 2.5D CNN-Transformer hybrid model in prognostic prediction [48, 49]. Similarly, Liu et al. reported that a hybrid 2.5D CNN-Transformer model built with multimodal MRI data demonstrated strong performance in classifying parotid gland tumors, surpassing visual assessments by experienced radiologists [50].

Notably, Grad‑CAM visualization of the DL model’s decision‑making process revealed distinct patterns of attention that appear to correlate with underlying tumor biology. In CT images of high recurrence risk PDAC patients, the model focused more on the tumor core. This pattern may reflect the model’s identification of imaging phenotypes associated with central tumor features common in aggressive PDAC, such as the necrosis and desmoplastic fibrosis that often develop in hypovascular tumors due to rapid growth [51]. In contrast, in low risk patients, the model’s attention shifted toward the tumor periphery. This peripheral focus may indicate that the model is identifying imaging patterns at the invasive margin, a region critically involved in PDAC tumor-host interactions [1]. These findings resonate with recent work by Gao et al., who applied a 2.5D deep learning model to explore immunotherapy response in esophageal cancer and observed divergent attention patterns between the tumor core and periphery across cases with different outcomes, suggesting that peripheral attention may indicate metabolically active invasive margins [52]. Collectively, these observations suggest that deep learning models can autonomously identify spatial phenotypes related to tumor aggressiveness, offering valuable insights into the relationship between imaging characteristics and pathological alterations in PDAC.

In the development of the Clinic model for this study, “neural invasion”, “degree of differentiation”, and “pathological N stage” were identified as independent predictors of early recurrence following PDAC resection. Neural invasion is a hallmark pathological feature of aggressive PDAC, indicating a propensity for perineural spread and correlating with increased risks of metastasis and recurrence [53]. The degree of differentiation reflects the malignant phenotype of tumor cells, with poorly differentiated tumors generally demonstrating greater proliferative, invasive, and metastatic potential [1]. Pathological N stage, representing the extent of regional lymph node involvement, is a well-established prognostic factor in PDAC associated with disease progression and recurrence [1, 54].

In contrast, the HADLC model developed in this study combines the HabitatAll score and the DL score, which are both derived from preoperative CT images, with the aforementioned clinicopathological features using ridge regression. This integration achieves complementary multidimensional information and significantly enhances predictive performance. The HADLC model achieved an AUC of 0.977 and an overall accuracy of 90.4% in the training cohort. In internal testing and multiple external validation cohorts, it consistently attained AUCs between 0.838 and 0.916, with overall accuracy ranging from 81.1% to 89.1%, outperforming any single‑modality model. These results underscore the advantage of multimodal integration in capturing malignant biological behavior: habitat imaging noninvasively decodes intratumoral spatial heterogeneity, deep learning extracts global imaging patterns associated with invasiveness and recurrence risk, and clinicopathological variables provide essential biological context. Together, they contribute to a more robust and reliable predictive tool. Similar integrative strategies have been validated in studies of nasopharyngeal, gastric, and breast cancers, where models combining radiomic features with clinicopathological factors consistently surpassed those based solely on imaging or clinical data [55–57]. Additionally, with the rapid advancement of precision medicine and multi-modal data integration [14, 58], the convergence of such imaging biomarkers with emerging nanotechnology platforms, such as exosomes [59], transferosomes [60], and nanobiosensors [61], may offer new opportunities for precision theranostics in pancreatic cancer by enabling more accurate diagnosis and targeted therapy [62]. To facilitate clinical translation, a nomogram was constructed based on the HADLC model, clearly illustrating the contribution weights of each variable. The HabitatAll score and DL score played dominant roles, contributing more than the three clinicopathological factors, indicating that imaging biomarkers provide substantial incremental value beyond conventional clinicopathological indicators. Additionally, we propose a risk stratification approach using the Youden index-derived optimal cutoff of 0.868 from the HADLC model as a clinically actionable threshold. Patients with predicted probabilities equal to or above this value would be classified as high-risk for early recurrence, while those below would be classified as the conventional-risk group. This dichotomization may assist clinicians in personalized decision-making. For example, high-risk patients might be considered for more intensive postoperative surveillance with shorter intervals (e.g., imaging every 1–3 months during the first year post-surgery), earlier initiation of adjuvant chemotherapy, or enrollment in clinical trials evaluating novel adjuvant or neoadjuvant strategies. In contrast, patients in the conventional-risk group could be managed with standard follow-up protocols, thereby avoiding potential overtreatment and reducing healthcare burden. By leveraging preoperative CT imaging and readily available clinicopathological data, the HADLC model enables risk stratification either preoperatively or in the early postoperative phase, thereby informing personalized adjuvant therapy and surveillance intensity. Notably, despite incorporating advanced components such as a 2.5D CNN-Transformer architecture and multi-subregion habitat analysis, the model remains computationally efficient: once tumor segmentation is completed, a single prediction can be generated within 3–5 min on a standard GPU-equipped workstation. This efficiency supports its potential integration into existing clinical imaging pipelines.

However, this study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design inherently introduces potential selection bias, despite the inclusion of multicenter data and external validation cohorts. Second, the heterogeneity in sample sizes and imaging protocols across the four centers, while reflective of real-world clinical diversity, also introduces potential variability in image quality and radiomic feature extraction. Therefore, the clinical utility of the model requires further confirmation through prospective clinical trials with standardized protocols. Third, all analyses were based on portal venous phase CT images and did not incorporate multiphase CT or multimodal imaging such as MRI or PET‑CT, which may limit the model’s comprehensiveness in characterizing tumor vascular supply and metabolic heterogeneity. Fourth, while our imaging-derived biomarkers (HabitatAll and DL scores) hold potential for preoperative risk assessment, the current HADLC model incorporates postoperative pathological variables and is therefore better suited for guiding adjuvant treatment and surveillance strategies after surgery. Additionally, the current delineation of habitat regions relies on unsupervised clustering of voxel‑wise features; although this study proposed reasonable hypotheses regarding the biological significance of these regions, their precise biological basis remains unclear. Future studies incorporating spatially registered histopathological validation are warranted to further investigate the potential correlations between specific habitat subregions and underlying pathological features. Currently, our institution is conducting a prospective study designed to apply the HADLC model to predict the risk of early postoperative recurrence in patients with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma. This work will be integrating multi‑omics analyses of tumor tissues, including pathomics, transcriptomics, and metabolomics, with the aim of identifying potential biological explanations for the model’s predictions and exploring their association with tumor behavior.

Conclusion

This study successfully developed and validated a fusion model (HADLC) that integrates CT‑based habitat analysis, a 2.5D CNN-Transformer deep learning framework, and clinicopathological features to accurately assess the risk of early postoperative recurrence in PDAC patients. Interpretability analysis further revealed potential associations between key imaging phenotypes and aggressive tumor biology. The model demonstrated strong discriminative performance and clinical applicability across internal and multiple external validation cohorts, highlighting the advantage of multimodal integration in capturing tumor heterogeneity and invasive potential. It provides a valuable imaging biomarker to support personalized treatment strategies for PDAC patients. Nonetheless, prospective studies are essential to confirm its predictive performance and facilitate clinical translation.
