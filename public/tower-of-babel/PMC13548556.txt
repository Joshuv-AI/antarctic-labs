Impact of origin and frequency of premature ventricular complexes on cardiac remodeling: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Gege Zhang, Juan Yao, Jie Gao, Jianfeng Yan, Li Xu, Yining Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFrequent premature ventricular complexes (PVCs) have been associated with adverse cardiac remodeling; however, the relative contributions of PVC frequency and anatomical origin remain unclear. This study evaluated the effects of PVC burden and origin on cardiac structural and functional changes.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. PubMed, Embase, CENTRAL, and the Cochrane Library were searched for English-language observational studies published up to December 2025. Studies assessing the relationship between PVC frequency or origin and echocardiographic parameters were included. Frequent PVCs were defined using study-specific thresholds (e.g., > 10,000 beats/day, > 5% of total beats, or > 5000 occurrences per 10 h). Of 625 records, nine studies involving 1886 patients met the inclusion criteria, including six on PVC frequency and three on PVC origin. Pooled analyses were performed using weighted mean differences (WMD) with 95% confidence intervals.ResultsFrequent PVCs were significantly associated with reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) (WMD = − 3.66, 95% CI − 6.30 to − 1.02; P = 0.007), increased LVEDD (WMD = 3.17, 95% CI 1.35 to 4.99; P = 0.0006), increased LVESD (WMD = 3.27, 95% CI 0.32 to 6.23; P = 0.03), and increased LAD (WMD = 1.59, 95% CI 0.13 to 3.06; P = 0.03), suggesting adverse ventricular and atrial remodeling. PVC origin was not significantly associated with LVEF or LVEDD.ConclusionA high PVC burden is associated with adverse cardiac remodeling, whereas PVC origin was not significantly associated with structural changes in this analysis and should be interpreted with caution given the limited number of studies and heterogeneity. These findings support the importance of PVC frequency in risk stratification and early intervention.Keywords: Premature ventricular complexes (PVCs), Cardiac remodeling, PVC burden, Outflow tract, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Premature ventricular complexes (PVCs) are early depolarizations originating from the ventricles and represent one of the most frequently encountered arrhythmias in clinical practice. They occur in individuals with and without structural heart disease and are often considered benign; however, accumulating evidence suggests that a high PVC burden may contribute to adverse cardiac remodeling and functional impairment, even in otherwise healthy individuals [1–3]. PVCs can arise from multiple anatomical locations within the ventricles, with the right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT) being the most common site in patients without structural abnormalities. Nevertheless, PVCs originating from non-outflow tract regions, including the left ventricle, papillary muscles, and fascicular system, have also been implicated in pathological alterations of cardiac structure and function [4–6]. Cardiac remodeling refers to structural and functional changes in the myocardium in response to various stressors, including arrhythmias. These changes may include ventricular dilatation, myocardial fibrosis, wall thinning, and a decline in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). Such remodeling not only precedes the development of heart failure but is also associated with increased morbidity and mortality [7, 8]. Frequent PVCs, commonly defined as more than 10,000 beats per day or exceeding 5% of total daily heartbeats, have been increasingly recognized as a potential driver of these maladaptive changes. Importantly, PVC-induced cardiomyopathy is considered a potentially reversible condition, with improvement observed following suppression or elimination of ectopic activity [9–11].

The mechanisms underlying PVC-induced remodeling are complex and multifactorial. Proposed pathways include abnormal ventricular activation leading to mechanical dyssynchrony, impaired myocardial perfusion, neurohormonal activation, and disturbances in intracellular calcium handling. Notably, not all individuals with frequent PVCs develop cardiomyopathy, suggesting that additional factors—such as PVC origin, QRS duration, coupling interval, and individual susceptibility—may influence disease progression [12–14].

The impact of PVC origin, particularly the distinction between outflow tract and non-outflow tract sources, on cardiac remodeling remains incompletely understood. While PVCs originating from the RVOT are often considered less deleterious and more amenable to catheter ablation, emerging evidence indicates that their hemodynamic effects may not be negligible, especially at higher burdens. Conversely, PVCs arising from non-outflow tract regions, although less common, may exert greater mechanical and electrophysiological stress on the myocardium, potentially predisposing to more pronounced remodeling [15, 16]. Therefore, clarifying the relative contributions of PVC origin and burden is essential for improving risk stratification and guiding therapeutic decision-making. Management of symptomatic or high-burden PVCs includes pharmacological therapy, such as beta-blockers and antiarrhythmic agents; however, their efficacy is often limited and may be associated with adverse effects. Radiofrequency catheter ablation (RFCA) has emerged as an effective and safe treatment option, particularly for monomorphic PVCs with identifiable foci. In addition to symptom relief, RFCA has been shown to improve ventricular function and reverse structural remodeling in selected patients [17, 18]. Previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses have evaluated predictors of PVC-induced cardiomyopathy, including PVC burden, anatomical origin, and electrophysiological characteristics such as QRS duration and coupling interval [19–21]. These studies consistently identify PVC burden as a major determinant of ventricular dysfunction, although additional factors such as left ventricular or epicardial origin may also contribute. However, most prior analyses have focused on risk prediction frameworks rather than directly comparing the relative effects of PVC burden and anatomical origin on echocardiographic remodeling parameters.

Despite growing interest in the relationship between PVCs and cardiac remodeling, existing studies have largely examined either PVC burden or origin in isolation, and a direct comparative synthesis focusing specifically on echocardiographic remodeling endpoints remains limited. A systematic evaluation of both factors across key echocardiographic parameters, including LVEF, left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD), left ventricular end-systolic diameter (LVESD), and left atrial diameter (LAD), is necessary to better understand their relative contributions. Therefore, the present study aimed to conduct a systematic review and meta-analysis of available clinical evidence to assess the impact of PVC frequency and anatomical origin on cardiac remodeling. By integrating findings across multiple studies, we sought to clarify whether specific PVC characteristics are associated with more pronounced structural and functional alterations, thereby informing clinical risk stratification and management strategies.

Methods

Study design and search strategy

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) guidelines. A comprehensive electronic literature search was performed in four major databases: PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), and The Cochrane Library. The search included studies published in English up to December 2025. A combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords was used to maximize search sensitivity. The search terms included “premature ventricular contraction,” “premature ventricular extrasystole,” “premature ventricular systole,” “PVC,” “PVB,” “cardiac remodeling,” “myocardial remodeling,” and “ventricular remodeling.” The reference lists of relevant articles were also manually screened to identify additional eligible studies.

Study selection and screening process

Two independent reviewers screened all retrieved records. Titles and abstracts were initially assessed to identify potentially eligible studies, followed by removal of duplicate records. Full-text articles were then reviewed for eligibility when inclusion criteria were met or when eligibility could not be determined from the abstract alone. Disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) observational study designs (including cohort and case-control studies); (2) evaluation of the association between either the anatomical origin of premature ventricular complexes (PVCs), distinguishing between outflow and non-outflow tract sources, or PVC frequency and cardiac remodeling; (3) inclusion of studies defining frequent PVCs using study-specific thresholds (e.g., > 10,000 beats/day, > 5% of total beats, or > 5000 occurrences per 10 h), which were considered equivalent indicators of high PVC burden; and (4) reporting of at least one echocardiographic outcome, including left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD), left ventricular end-systolic diameter (LVESD), or left atrial diameter (LAD). Studies were excluded if they were non-original articles (e.g., reviews, editorials, or letters), did not clearly classify PVC origin or frequency, or included patients with pre-existing structural heart disease unrelated to PVCs. Studies with duplicate or overlapping data were also excluded. The study design was structured according to the PICOS framework. The population (P) included patients with premature ventricular complexes (PVCs). The intervention/exposure (I) was high PVC burden or specific PVC origin (outflow vs. non-outflow tract). The comparator (C) included patients with low PVC burden or alternative PVC origin groups. The outcomes (O) were echocardiographic parameters reflecting cardiac remodeling, including LVEF, LVEDD, LVESD, and LAD. The study design (S) included observational studies.

Data extraction

Two authors independently extracted data using a standardized data collection form. Extracted variables included study characteristics (author, year, country, and sample size), classification of PVC origin and frequency, use of radiofrequency catheter ablation (RFCA), follow-up duration, and structural heart disease status. Echocardiographic outcomes (LVEF, LVEDD, LVESD, and LAD) were also collected for quantitative synthesis. Any discrepancies in data extraction were resolved by consensus.

Quality assessment and risk of bias

The methodological quality of included studies was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) for observational studies. Each study was evaluated across three domains: selection, comparability, and outcome assessment. Studies scoring 7–9 points were considered high quality, 5–6 points moderate quality, and ≤ 4 points low quality. Quality assessment was performed independently by two reviewers, with disagreements resolved through discussion.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

Meta-analysis was conducted using Review Manager (RevMan) version 5.3 (Cochrane Collaboration). Continuous outcomes were summarized using weighted mean differences (WMDs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were not applied, as all included studies reported outcomes using comparable measurement scales.

Statistical heterogeneity among studies was assessed using the Chi-square test and the I² statistic. A fixed-effect model was applied when heterogeneity was low (P ≥ 0.1 and I² ≤ 50%), whereas a random-effects model was used when significant heterogeneity was present (P < 0.1 and/or I² > 50%). Subgroup analyses were performed based on PVC origin (outflow tract vs. non-outflow tract) and PVC frequency (frequent vs. infrequent). Sensitivity analysis was conducted using a leave-one-out approach, in which each study was sequentially excluded to evaluate the robustness of the pooled estimates. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots. Given the limited number of included studies, these analyses were considered exploratory and were interpreted with caution. Formal statistical tests for publication bias were not performed due to the limited number of included studies. Meta-regression analysis was not conducted given the small number of studies available.

Results

Study selection

A total of 625 records related to premature ventricular complexes (PVCs) and cardiac remodeling were identified through systematic database searches. After removal of duplicates and initial screening of titles and abstracts, 138 articles were retained for further evaluation. Of these, 20 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Eleven studies were excluded due to lack of relevant echocardiographic data, unclear classification of PVC origin or frequency, or non-original study design. Ultimately, nine observational studies comprising 1886 patients met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final meta-analysis. The study selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

PVC origin and cardiac remodeling

Three studies evaluated the association between PVC origin and LVEF. A fixed-effect model was applied due to moderate heterogeneity (I² = 55%, P = 0.11). The pooled analysis demonstrated no statistically significant difference in LVEF between outflow and non-outflow tract PVCs (WMD = -0.16, 95% CI -1.75 to 1.42, P = 0.84), suggesting no significant association between PVC origin and systolic function (Fig. 2; Table 3). Similarly, analysis of LVEDD from the same three studies revealed substantial heterogeneity (I² = 94%, P < 0.00001), and a random-effects model was therefore applied. The pooled results showed no statistically significant difference in LVEDD between outflow and non-outflow tract PVCs (WMD = 6.28, 95% CI -5.27 to 17.84, P = 0.29), suggesting no significant association between PVC origin and ventricular dilation (Fig. 3; Table 3).

PVC frequency and cardiac remodeling

Six studies investigated the effect of PVC frequency on LVEF. Given the high heterogeneity (I² = 94%, P < 0.00001), a random-effects model was used. Frequent PVCs were significantly associated with reduced LVEF (WMD = -3.66, 95% CI -6.30 to -1.02, P = 0.007), suggesting impaired systolic function in individuals with a high PVC burden (Fig. 4; Table 3). Analysis of LVEDD across six studies also demonstrated significant heterogeneity (I² = 89%, P < 0.00001), and the pooled analysis using a random-effects model showed that frequent PVCs were associated with a significant increase in LVEDD (WMD = 3.17, 95% CI 1.35 to 4.99, P = 0.0006), suggesting ventricular enlargement (Fig. 5; Table 3). Five studies provided data on LVESD. Due to considerable heterogeneity (I² = 95%, P < 0.00001), a random-effects model was applied. The pooled analysis indicated that frequent PVCs were significantly associated with increased LVESD (WMD = 3.27, 95% CI 0.32 to 6.23, P = 0.03), suggesting the presence of adverse ventricular remodeling (Fig. 6; Table 3). Two studies evaluated the association between PVC frequency and LAD. As heterogeneity was low (I² = 33%, P = 0.22), a fixed-effect model was applied. The pooled results demonstrated that frequent PVCs were associated with a modest but statistically significant increase in LAD (WMD = 1.59, 95% CI 0.13 to 3.06, P = 0.03), suggesting concomitant atrial remodeling (Fig. 7; Table 3).

Sensitivity analysis

A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the robustness of the pooled results. Sequential exclusion of individual studies did not materially alter the direction or statistical significance of the pooled estimates for LVEF, LVEDD, and LVESD, indicating that the findings were stable and not driven by any single study.

Assessment of publication bias

Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots for the primary outcomes (Figs. 8A–C). Visual inspection revealed asymmetry in the plots for LVEF, LVEDD, and LVESD, suggesting the potential presence of small-study effects or publication bias. However, given the limited number of included studies, these findings should be considered exploratory and interpreted with caution. Formal statistical tests for publication bias were not performed.

Discussion

This meta-analysis synthesized evidence from nine observational studies comprising 1886 patients to evaluate the relationship between premature ventricular complexes (PVCs) and cardiac remodeling. By simultaneously examining both PVC frequency and anatomical origin, this study provides a comprehensive assessment of their respective contributions to alterations in key echocardiographic parameters, including left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD), left ventricular end-systolic diameter (LVESD), and left atrial diameter (LAD). The principal finding of this analysis is that a high burden of PVCs was associated with adverse cardiac remodeling. Specifically, frequent PVCs were associated with reduced LVEF and increased LVEDD and LVESD, indicating both systolic dysfunction and structural enlargement of the left ventricle. These findings are consistent with the concept of PVC-induced cardiomyopathy, a condition characterized by reversible myocardial dysfunction secondary to chronic ectopic activity. Previous studies have demonstrated a dose–response relationship between PVC burden and left ventricular dysfunction, supporting the notion that frequent ectopic beats disrupt normal myocardial activation, induce mechanical dyssynchrony, and impair contractile efficiency [22]. These findings should be interpreted in the context of previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses, which have identified PVC burden as a major determinant of cardiomyopathy, with additional contributions from factors such as anatomical origin and electrophysiological characteristics [19–21]. In contrast to these studies, the present analysis specifically focuses on echocardiographic remodeling endpoints and directly compares the relative contributions of PVC burden and origin, thereby providing complementary insight into structural and functional cardiac alterations rather than risk prediction alone.

An important contribution of the present study is the demonstration of a significant association between frequent PVCs and increased LAD. While prior research has predominantly focused on ventricular remodeling, our findings suggest that atrial remodeling may occur concurrently, potentially as a consequence of elevated left ventricular filling pressures or impaired diastolic function. Given that left atrial enlargement is a recognized risk factor for atrial fibrillation and thromboembolic events, this observation highlights an additional dimension of risk in patients with a high PVC burden and underscores the need for comprehensive cardiac evaluation [23]. In contrast to PVC frequency, the anatomical origin of PVCs did not show a statistically significant association with changes in LVEF or LVEDD. These findings suggest that the burden of ectopy, rather than its site of origin, may be the dominant determinant of adverse remodeling. This interpretation is supported by electrophysiological studies indicating that the cumulative effect of abnormal ventricular activation plays a more critical role in myocardial dysfunction than the specific site of ectopic focus [24, 25]. However, the absence of a significant association between PVC origin and remodeling should be interpreted with caution. Only three studies provided stratified analyses based on origin, and the sample sizes were relatively small. In addition, the classification of PVC origin varied across studies, and pooling into outflow versus non-outflow tract categories may oversimplify biologically distinct substrates. Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests that certain PVC characteristics, such as epicardial origin or prolonged QRS duration, may exert greater mechanical and electrical stress on the myocardium [19, 26]. Therefore, the lack of a significant association may reflect limited statistical power and heterogeneity rather than a true absence of effect. The findings of this meta-analysis also support the therapeutic role of radiofrequency catheter ablation (RFCA) in patients with a high PVC burden. Previous studies have demonstrated that successful ablation of frequent PVCs can lead to significant improvements in LVEF, ventricular dimensions, and clinical symptoms, particularly when the ectopic burden exceeds 10% of total heartbeats [27]. In the present analysis, studies that included RFCA frequently reported structural and functional improvement during follow-up, further supporting the reversible nature of PVC-induced cardiomyopathy and the importance of timely intervention [28]. The substantial heterogeneity observed across analyses (I² values up to 95%) should be considered when interpreting these findings. This heterogeneity likely reflects differences in study populations, definitions of PVC burden, inclusion of patients with or without structural heart disease, variability in follow-up duration, and the use of interventions such as RFCA. By integrating both PVC frequency and origin into a single analytical framework, this study provides a more comprehensive understanding of PVC-related cardiac remodeling than previous reports, which often examined these factors independently. The results suggest that PVC burden is a more reliable predictor of remodeling than anatomical origin, and therefore may be more relevant in guiding clinical decision-making.

From a clinical standpoint, these findings have important implications. Frequent PVCs should not be considered benign, even in patients without overt structural heart disease. The association with both ventricular and atrial remodeling suggests that a high PVC burden may represent an early, potentially reversible stage of cardiomyopathy. Routine echocardiographic evaluation, including assessment of atrial dimensions, may facilitate early detection of subclinical remodeling. Moreover, therapeutic strategies should prioritize reduction of PVC burden, irrespective of origin, particularly in patients demonstrating early structural changes.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, all included studies were observational in nature, which limits the ability to establish causal relationships. Second, substantial heterogeneity was observed across several analyses, likely reflecting differences in study populations, echocardiographic methodologies, follow-up durations, and definitions of PVC burden. Although sensitivity analyses suggested that the overall findings were robust, residual heterogeneity cannot be excluded. Third, the number of studies evaluating PVC origin was limited, reducing the statistical power of subgroup analyses. Fourth, not all studies consistently excluded patients with underlying structural heart disease, introducing potential confounding. Additionally, variations in the definition of frequent PVCs across studies may affect comparability. Finally, although funnel plot asymmetry suggested possible publication bias, the small number of included studies limits the reliability of this assessment, and therefore these findings should be interpreted as exploratory rather than definitive.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis suggests that a high burden of premature ventricular complexes (PVCs) is associated with adverse cardiac remodeling, as evidenced by reduced left ventricular ejection fraction and increased ventricular and atrial dimensions. In contrast, the anatomical origin of PVCs was not significantly associated with these structural changes in the present analysis, although this finding should be interpreted with caution given the limited number of studies and heterogeneity. These findings highlight the predominant role of PVC frequency, rather than origin, in the development of remodeling and support the consideration of PVC burden as an important factor in clinical risk stratification and management. Early identification and targeted intervention in patients with frequent PVCs may help prevent or reverse myocardial dysfunction. Further large-scale, prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings and to better define the role of PVC characteristics in long-term clinical outcomes.
