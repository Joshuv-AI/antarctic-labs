Effect of carvacrol- and thymol-rich essential oil feed additives on colonization and persistence patterns of Salmonella in broiler chickens☆
Authors: M. Naeem, S. Manjankattil, S. Poudel, Y. Adhikari, M. Bailey, J. Wang, K. Sulleyman, T. Stokes, W. Mejia, K. Casco, S.J. Rochell, W.J. Pacheco, D.V. Bourassa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Salmonella enterica is a major food safety concern in broiler production due to its persistence in the gastrointestinal tract, internal organs, and production environment. This study evaluated the effects of dietary thymol-rich and carvacrol-rich essential oil feed additives on colonization and persistence patterns of Salmonella Typhimurium (ST) in broiler chickens. A day-old Ross 708 male broiler chicks (n = 1200) were randomly allocated to 6 dietary treatments: PC, positive control standard diet without any essential oil additive; CARVA1, a feed additive (Salvix) containing 50% carvacrol included at 1,100 g per ton of feed; CARVA2, Salvix included at 2,200 g per ton; THYM1, a feed additive (Esencial Premix) containing 45% thymol included at 250 g per ton; THYM2, Esencial Premix included at 500 g per ton; and COMBO, a combination of CARVA1 and THYM1, with 8 replicates (25 birds each) per treatment. All birds were orally challenged with 7 Log CFU/ml of ST on placement day. Samples were collected from liver, spleen, ceca, litter boot swabs, and carcass rinses to determine Salmonella counts and prevalence. No significant differences (P > 0.05) in Salmonella counts (CFU/mL) in cecal and carcass rinse samples were observed among treatments. CARVA1 decreased (P = 0.003) Salmonella counts in boot swabs compared with PC, CARVA2, THYM1, and COMBO on day 14. COMBO decreased (P < 0.05) Salmonella prevalence in the liver, whereas CARVA1 decreased (P < 0.05) in the spleen, compared to all other diets on day 14. No differences (P > 0.05) were observed in Salmonella prevalence in cecal, boot swab, and carcass rinse samples post-enrichment. Pearson correlation analysis revealed generally weak and non-significant associations between cecal Salmonella counts and environmental or carcass contamination, with a single significant positive correlation observed between cecal and boot swab counts on day 14 in the CARVA2 group. In summary, supplementation with thymol-rich and carvacrol-rich essential oils produced limited and matrix-dependent effects on Salmonella recovery, with transient reductions observed in boot swab counts and internal organ prevalence at day 14 but no consistent effects on cecal counts, cecal prevalence, or carcass contamination.

Introduction

Salmonella remains one of the most important foodborne pathogens associated with poultry production, posing a continuous threat to animal health, food safety, and public health worldwide (Neelawala et al., 2024). Salmonella in broiler production represents a multifaceted problem driven by multiple interacting sources of contamination and varied pathways of transmission (Adhikari et al., 2025a). Horizontal spread of foodborne pathogens occurs through exposure to contaminated feed, drinking water, litter, dust, and rodent or insect vectors, while vertical transmission may take place through infected breeder flocks via sperm or transovarian routes (Wang et al., 2023; Isah et al., 2024). Pre-slaughter feed withdrawal has been linked to elevated bacterial populations in the upper gastrointestinal tract, and bird commingling during transportation further increases the likelihood of carcass contamination (USDA FSIS, 2021). Moreover, many Salmonella serovars recovered throughout the poultry production chain harbor multiple clinically relevant antimicrobial resistance genes and virulence plasmids, which enhance their ability to persist in the environment, increase pathogenic potential, and heighten public health risk (USDA FSIS, 2021; Adhikari et al., 2025b). Poultry and poultry products are consistently identified as major reservoirs of Salmonella enterica, contributing substantially to human salmonellosis outbreaks through contaminated meat and carcasses (Naeem and Bourassa, 2024, 2025). Among the numerous serovars, Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium is of particular concern because of its broad host range, zoonotic potential, and ability to colonize broiler chickens without causing overt clinical disease (Solarte et al., 2017; Xu et al., 2022). Asymptomatic carriage enables the pathogen to persist within flocks, facilitating horizontal transmission and environmental contamination throughout the production cycle.

Following oral exposure, S. Typhimurium rapidly establishes colonization in the gastrointestinal tract, particularly in the ceca, where it may persist for extended periods (Kollanoor-Johny et al., 2012; Stingelin et al., 2023). From the intestine, Salmonella can translocate to internal organs such as the liver and spleen, contributing to systemic persistence and prolonged fecal shedding (Hu et al., 2023). Environmental survival further complicates control efforts, as Salmonella can persist in poultry litter, drinking water, and dust, creating ongoing opportunities for reinfection (Peichel et al., 2019; Galgano et al., 2023). During slaughter and processing, cross-contamination allows Salmonella to survive on carcasses despite standard hygienic interventions, increasing the risk of consumer exposure (Nair and Kollanoor Johny, 2017; Robinson et al., 2022). Effective control strategies must therefore address Salmonella survival across multiple reservoirs rather than focusing solely on intestinal colonization. For many years, antibiotic growth promoters and therapeutic antimicrobials were widely used to control Salmonella and improve growth performance in broiler chickens. Although initially effective, the extensive use of antibiotics has been suggested to have contributed to the emergence and dissemination of antimicrobial-resistant Salmonella strains, including multidrug-resistant S. Typhimurium isolates from poultry (Solarte et al., 2017; Morshdy et al., 2021). Global restrictions on antibiotic growth promoters, coupled with increasing consumer demand for antibiotic-free poultry products, have intensified the search for alternative control strategies that are effective, safe, and sustainable. Nutritional strategies have become central to antibiotic-free Salmonella control programs in poultry production.

Essential oils have demonstrated strong antimicrobial activity against foodborne pathogens, including Salmonella, through multiple mechanisms of action (Ebani et al., 2019; Selim et al., 2022). Phenolic compounds disrupt bacterial cell membranes, alter permeability, and interfere with energy metabolism, while other components inhibit enzyme activity, motility, and adhesion (Kollanoor Johny et al., 2010; Lang et al., 2021). The multi-targeted nature of essential oils reduces the likelihood of resistance development, making them attractive alternatives to conventional antibiotics in poultry nutrition (Puvača et al., 2022; Oliveira et al., 2024). Among essential oil components, carvacrol and thymol have received particular attention due to their strong and consistent anti-Salmonella activity. These monoterpenoid phenols, predominantly found in oregano (Origanum vulgare) and thyme (Thymus vulgaris), exert antimicrobial effects primarily through disruption of bacterial cell membranes, leading to leakage of intracellular contents and loss of membrane integrity (Kollanoor Johny et al., 2010). In vitro studies have demonstrated low minimum inhibitory and bactericidal concentrations of carvacrol and thymol against S. Typhimurium and other poultry-associated serovars, as well as inhibition of biofilm formation and bacterial adhesion (Di Vito et al., 2020). In vivo, dietary supplementation with carvacrol- and thymol-based products has resulted in significant reductions in cecal Salmonella colonization and cloacal shedding, while also improving gut morphology and antioxidant status in broilers (Hu et al., 2023; Stingelin et al., 2023). These findings support the use of carvacrol and thymol as key bioactive compounds for targeted Salmonella control in poultry production systems. Advances in formulation technologies have further enhanced the efficacy of essential oils in poultry diets. Microencapsulation improves stability and allows controlled release of bioactive compounds along the gastrointestinal tract. Microencapsulated thymol and carvacrol have demonstrated significant reductions in S. Typhimurium, S. Heidelberg, and S. Minnesota colonization, while also improving gut morphology and immune responses (Stingelin et al., 2023; Girard et al., 2024).

Despite these promising results, several challenges remain regarding the practical application of essential oils. Antimicrobial efficacy varies with dose, formulation, Salmonella serovar, and interaction with feed components and gut contents (Solarte et al., 2017; Lima et al., 2023). Excessive inclusion levels may also affect palatability or economic feasibility, reinforcing the importance of identifying effective but practical doses (Puvača et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2022). Furthermore, most existing studies have focused primarily on intestinal colonization, with limited evaluation of Salmonella survival and clearance simultaneously in internal organs, litter, and carcasses. Given the interconnected nature of these reservoirs, a comprehensive assessment across multiple compartments is essential to better understand Salmonella persistence and transmission within broiler production systems (Ferreira et al., 2022; Naeem and Bourassa, 2024). Despite promising findings, previous studies have reported inconsistent reductions in Salmonella colonization, particularly under high challenge pressure or commercial-like conditions, suggesting that efficacy may depend on formulation, dose, challenge model, and gastrointestinal interactions.

Therefore, the present study was designed to evaluate the efficacy of carvacrol-rich and thymol-rich essential oil feed additives at single and double doses against S. Typhimurium in broiler chickens. The specific objective was to assess their effects on Salmonella survival and clearance dynamics in internal organs, litter, and carcasses. By concurrently examining these reservoirs, this study aims to provide an understanding of the role of these bioactive compounds in controlling Salmonella persistence and to support the development of evidence-based nutritional strategies for antibiotic-free broiler production. We hypothesized that dietary supplementation with encapsulated carvacrol-rich and thymol-rich essential oils would reduce Salmonella recovery in gastrointestinal, systemic, environmental, and processing-associated compartments relative to the positive control.

Materials and methods

Experimental design, dietary treatments, and bird husbandry

The research received approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Auburn University (No. 2025-5654). A total of 1200 day-old Ross 708 male broiler chicks with uniform initial body weights (40±2 g) were used for the study. The birds were reared at the Charles Miller Jr. Poultry Research and Education Center, Auburn University. From day 0 to 42, all chicks were fed experimental diets. On arrival, chick papers were collected to test for Salmonella. Chicks were randomly allocated to 6 experimental treatments, each consisting of 8 replicate pens with 25 birds per pen (0.11 m2/bird). All pens were lined with white wood shavings, 4-5 inches in depth, as bedding. Age-appropriate temperature and humidity were maintained according to standard practices. Temperature, ventilation, and environmental conditions were maintained according to Ross 708 management recommendations throughout the experimental period, and all treatment groups were housed under identical environmental conditions within the same research facility.

All diets (corn-soy-based) were formulated to be isocaloric and isonitrogenous, fully meeting the standard nutritional requirements of Ross 708 male broiler chicks. The sand part of the basal diets was replaced with feed additives, Salvix containing 50% carvacrol and Esencial Premix 45% thymol essential oils in encapsulated powder form, to prepare the six experimental diets as follows: PC, standard diet without any essential oil additive; CARVA1, diet with Salvix containing 50% carvacrol at 1,100 g/ton; CARVA2, diet with Salvix containing 50% carvacrol at 2,200 g/ton; THYM1, diet with Esencial Premix containing 45% thymol at 250 g/ton; THYM2, diet with Esencial Premix containing 45% thymol at 500 g/ton; and COMBO, diet with a combination of CARVA1 and THYM1. The products, Salvix and Esencial Premix, containing essential oils in encapsulated micro powder form, were supplied by a feed additive company, Promitec®, Colombia. The inclusion rate of Salvix and Esencial Premix was similar in starter, grower, and finisher diets. Inclusion levels were selected based on manufacturer recommendations and previously published studies demonstrating the biological activity of encapsulated carvacrol- and thymol-based products in broilers. Diets were provided in three growth phases: starter crumbles from day 0 to 14, grower pellets from 15 to 28, and finisher pellets from 29 to 43. Birds had ad libitum access to feed and water throughout the experimental period.

Inoculum preparation and challenge procedures

A nalidixic acid (NA)-resistant ST, originally sourced from Dr. Nelson Cox’s laboratory at USDA ARS in Athens, GA, was used to prepare the inoculum for this study. The strain was first cultured separately in tryptic soy broth (TSB; Becton, Dickinson and Company, NJ, USA) by adding 100 μL of the stock culture to 10 mL of TSB and incubating it overnight at 37°C. The ST strain was grown in TSB containing 200 ppm NA for three consecutive passages. A 1,500 mL culture of TSB was then prepared by incubating the resistant strains at 37°C for 24 h.

To prepare the inoculum, the bacterial culture was centrifuged at 3,600 rpm for 15 minutes. The supernatant was then discarded, and the bacterial pellet was resuspended in phosphate buffer saline (PBS) to achieve the desired concentration. The birds were orally gavaged with 0.1 mL at 7 Log CFU/ml of ST per bird on the placement day.

Sampling procedures

Samples, including liver, spleen, and ceca, randomly collected from 10 chicks, chick papers (13 out of 20), and bedding taken before the chick placement, were found negative for Salmonella. Each week, two birds per pen were collected for sampling, without considering their individual body weights. Birds were euthanized by cervical dislocation and then necropsied. Organ samples were collected on days 0, 7, 14, 21, 28, 35, and 42. Liver, spleen, and cecal samples were collected separately and placed in 24 oz Whirl-Pak® bags (Whirl-Pak, Nasco, WI, USA) for analysis. On day 43, birds were processed at the pilot poultry processing facility at the Charles Miller Jr. Poultry Research and Education Center. Carcass rinsates were collected at the post-pick stage for Salmonella enumeration. The number of birds in each pen gradually decreased each week due to sampling, which changed the stocking density over time.

Before entering the research facility, personnel wore clean, dry boots with new plastic covers, and a fresh pair of boot covers was worn for each pen. During sample collection, a boot swab (Envirobootie, Hardy Diagnostics, CA, USA) was worn while walking through the pen. After walking across each pen, the swabs were aseptically removed and placed in sample bags. All samples were kept on ice during transport and until further processing in a biosafety level 2 laboratory.

On processing day, 2 birds nearest the pen’s average weight were selected for processing to collect the carcass rinse samples from each pen. Birds were provided with 10 hours feed withdrawal period. Birds were stunned in an electrical water bath at 16 volts for 12 s, according to standard industry practice. They were then mechanically neck-cut, allowed to bleed for 4 min 22 s, scalded at 54°C for 2 min 23 s, and subsequently defeathered for 35 s, with an automatic picker working under water temperature of 50°C. Carcasses were collected at the post-pick stage of processing for bacterial recovery, with 16 carcasses per treatment group. To reduce cross-contamination, defeathering equipment was washed with hot water (63°C) between treatments. Carcasses were aseptically collected into poultry carcass rinsing bags (Poultry Rinsing Bag, Nasco, WI, USA), and 400 mL of buffered peptone water (BPW; Neogen, MI, USA) was added for each sample. Whole chicken carcasses with feet outside the rinsing bags were shaken for one minute to ensure thorough rinsing, and the resulting carcass rinsates were stored on ice until further processing.

Salmonella recovery

All procedures for Salmonella recovery for enumeration or prevalence were performed according to the method described by Manjankattil et al. (2025). Briefly, liver and spleen samples were enriched in BPW, added as 10 times the organs’ average weight, and incubated at 37°C for 24 h. BPW used throughout the study was supplemented with 20 mg/L of Novobiocin sodium salt (Cat No. J60928.06, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) to prevent unwanted microflora during incubation. Liver, spleen, and cecal samples immersed in BPW were homogenized using a Stomacher 400 (Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) at 260 rpm for 1 minute, followed by serial dilution. Appropriate dilutions were plated onto Xylose Lysine Deoxycholate (XLD) agar (HiMedia, PA, USA) supplemented with 200 ppm NA and incubated at 37°C for 24 h. Following direct plating, boot swabs were resuspended in 100 mL of BPW, and carcass rinsates were also incubated at 37°C for 24 h. After enrichment, all samples were streaked onto XLD-NA plates.

Statistical analysis

The study was designed as a completely randomized experiment. Sample size was determined through a power analysis targeting at least 80% statistical power. Salmonella counts obtained from XLD-NA plates were adjusted for dilution factors and plating volume and transformed using log10(x + 1) prior to analysis. For samples that were negative by direct plating but positive following enrichment, a value corresponding to one-half of the detection limit (2.5 CFU/mL) was assigned to permit log transformation while retaining enrichment-positive samples within the quantitative dataset. This approach has been used previously in poultry microbiology studies. Because different birds were sampled at each time point, observations were independent across sampling days, and each sampling day was analyzed separately. For Salmonella enumeration data, the following linear model was applied:Yij = μ + Ti + εijwhere Yij represents the log-transformed Salmonella count for the jth experimental unit receiving the ith dietary treatment, μ is the overall mean, Ti is the fixed effect of dietary treatment, and εij is the residual error. Pen was considered the experimental unit, and dietary treatment was included as a fixed effect. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test applied to model residuals, and homogeneity of variance was evaluated using Levene’s test and visual inspection of residual plots. Statistical analyses were performed using one-way ANOVA in JMP Pro 17 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). When significant treatment effects were detected, means were separated using protected Fisher’s least significant difference (LSD) test. This procedure was selected because treatment comparisons were planned a priori and conducted only when the overall ANOVA was significant. Salmonella prevalence data were analyzed using chi-square tests based on enrichment-positive and enrichment-negative samples. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to evaluate associations between cecal Salmonella counts and environmental or carcass contamination measurements. Correlation strength was classified according to Cohen, 2013 as weak (r < 0.30), moderate (0.30 ≤ r < 0.50), or strong (r ≥ 0.50). Statistical significance was declared at P ≤ 0.05.

Spleen Salmonella prevalence (% positive)

Discussion

The present study evaluated the efficacy of carvacrol-rich and thymol-rich essential oil feed additives on colonization and persistence patterns of Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium in broiler chickens, with concurrent assessment of intestinal colonization, internal organ prevalence, environmental contamination, and carcass recovery. Overall, essential oils produced transient, matrix-specific responses rather than consistent reductions across all compartments. Across all treatments, Salmonella recovery generally decreased over time, with lower cecal counts, declining liver and spleen prevalence, low environmental recovery at later sampling points, and minimal carcass contamination at processing. These outcomes are consistent with previous reports showing that phytogenic feed additives can influence Salmonella dynamics in vivo, although responses vary depending on formulation, dose, and sampling method (Kollanoor-Johny et al., 2012; Xu et al., 2022; Stingelin et al., 2023).

The ceca represent the primary site of Salmonella colonization in broiler chickens and serve as a major source of fecal shedding and environmental contamination (Kollanoor-Johny et al., 2012; Stingelin et al., 2023). In the present study, cecal Salmonella counts were high during the early post-challenge period and declined progressively with age across all treatments. This temporal pattern aligns with established observations that Salmonella colonization is most pronounced shortly after challenge and gradually decreases as birds mature and immune responses develop (Hu et al., 2023; Lima et al., 2023). The lack of strong treatment separation in cecal counts may reflect the complex ecology of the cecal microbiota, where essential oils interact not only with Salmonella but also with commensal bacteria, digesta components, and host factors that influence colonization resistance (Lang et al., 2021). Carvacrol-rich and thymol-rich essential oils are known to exert antimicrobial effects primarily through disruption of bacterial cell membranes, increased membrane permeability, and leakage of intracellular contents (Kollanoor Johny et al., 2010; Di Vito et al., 2020). However, in vivo efficacy depends heavily on delivery, stability, and release kinetics within the gastrointestinal tract. Microencapsulation, as used for both products in this study, is intended to protect volatile compounds from early degradation and promote targeted release in the intestine (Stingelin et al., 2023; Girard et al., 2024). Even so, interactions with feed matrices and gut contents may attenuate antimicrobial activity compared with in vitro conditions, which could partially explain the modest treatment effects observed in cecal counts.

In agreement with the current results, previous research has shown that Salmonella can be repeatedly detected in litter throughout the experimental period, without a direct relationship to cecal bacterial concentrations (Pal et al., 2021). Bourassa et al. (2018) reported that use of a seeder chick challenge model with S. Typhimurium at 10⁴ CFU per bird was adequate to sustain Salmonella-positive litter for the full six-week study, despite a marked reduction in cecal prevalence by the end of the trial. These observations highlight the ability of Salmonella to persist in the litter environment independently of intestinal colonization levels. To date, relatively few investigations have examined how preharvest antimicrobial interventions delivered through feed or water influence microbial populations within poultry litter. Bourassa et al. (2018) found that such interventions, including organic acid supplementation, did not significantly alter Salmonella levels in litter, emphasizing the environmental robustness and persistence of certain Salmonella serotypes. An additional factor not evaluated in the present study is the influence of pre-processing feed withdrawal on Salmonella dynamics. This preharvest stressor may subsequently increase the risk of carcass contamination during processing.

Environmental contamination is a critical component of Salmonella persistence in broiler houses, as litter and dust act as reservoirs that facilitate horizontal transmission and reinfection (Peichel et al., 2019; Galgano et al., 2023). Boot swabs provide an integrated measure of environmental Salmonella load, reflecting both fecal shedding and bacterial survival in litter. In the present study, significant differences among treatments were detected in boot swab counts on day 14, whereas no differences were observed at later sampling days. The early differences in boot swab recovery suggest that essential oil supplementation may influence Salmonella shedding or environmental survival during the early phase of colonization. Previous studies have reported that essential oils can reduce Salmonella survival in litter and drinking water, particularly during the early stages of contamination when bacterial populations are still establishing (Peichel et al., 2019; Galgano et al., 2023). Thymol- and carvacrol-rich oils have been shown to disrupt biofilm formation and reduce bacterial attachment to organic surfaces, which may limit persistence in litter matrices (Ebani et al., 2019; Lang et al., 2021). The absence of treatment differences in boot swab counts at days 28 and 42 likely reflects the overall decline in environmental Salmonella as cecal colonization decreased and birds aged. This pattern is consistent with previous research indicating that environmental contamination tends to peak early after challenge and diminishes over time as host and environmental pressures reduce bacterial survival (Peichel et al., 2019; Ferreira et al., 2022). The uniformly low boot swab counts at later timepoints suggest effective clearance across treatments, limiting the ability to detect differential effects.

Translocation of Salmonella from the intestine to internal organs such as the liver and spleen represents an important aspect of systemic infection and prolonged persistence (Hu et al., 2023). In the present study, Salmonella prevalence in liver and spleen samples was highest at early sampling points and declined markedly over time. The reduction in internal organ prevalence over time demonstrates that systemic dissemination is most prominent shortly after infection and decreases as birds develop effective immune responses (Kollanoor-Johny et al., 2012; Hu et al., 2023). These effects may contribute indirectly to reduced translocation and clearance of Salmonella from internal organs. The significant differences observed on day 14 may suggest that essential oil chemotypes may differentially affect early systemic spread. Carvacrol and thymol have been shown to reduce the invasion of epithelial cells and macrophages in vitro, which could limit bacterial entry into systemic circulation (Di Vito et al., 2020). However, as with cecal colonization, the complexity of in vivo interactions and the relatively low prevalence rates at later time points likely reduced the statistical power to detect sustained treatment effects. Dietary supplementation with oregano- and thyme-based essential oils has been shown to significantly reduce cecal colonization and cloacal shedding of S. Typhimurium and S. Enteritidis (Coles et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2022). These findings suggest that essential oils can influence both intestinal persistence and systemic dissemination of Salmonella in broiler chickens.

Carcass contamination during processing represents a direct food safety concern, as Salmonella present on skin surfaces or in processing water can be transferred to finished products (Nair and Kollanoor Johny, 2017; Robinson et al., 2022). In the present study, carcass rinse Salmonella counts at day 43 were low across all treatments, with no significant differences detected among groups. These findings are consistent with previous reports indicating that reductions in intestinal and environmental Salmonella generally correspond with lower carcass contamination, although this relationship may not be strictly linear (Nair and Kollanoor Johny, 2017; Robinson et al., 2022). The low recovery observed suggests that Salmonella loads were effectively reduced by the time of processing, thereby limiting carcass contamination under the conditions of this study. Essential oils have been reported to reduce bacterial attachment to poultry skin and feathers, which may further contribute to reduced carcass recovery (Nair and Kollanoor Johny, 2017). In addition, processing hygiene practices and equipment sanitation play a critical role in determining carcass contamination levels, and the controlled conditions of the pilot processing facility, along with sanitation between treatments, likely minimized cross-contamination. Salmonella can attach to skin and feathers during early production stages when cecal colonization is high and remain on these surfaces even as intestinal counts decline later in grow out. Skin folds and feathers have been identified as important reservoirs for Salmonella near market age, even when cecal prevalence is reduced (Rimet et al., 2019). Similarly, Mountzouris et al. (2015) reported higher Salmonella prevalence and counts in cloacal and skin samples during processing compared with cecal levels following dietary yeast supplementation and S. Enteritidis challenge. Together, these findings highlight that while carcass contamination was limited in this study, control of Salmonella on skin and feathers remains an important consideration, particularly under commercial processing conditions.

The weak and largely non-significant correlations observed between cecal Salmonella counts and boot swab contamination suggest that environmental Salmonella recovery cannot be reliably predicted based solely on intestinal colonization levels. Although a significant positive association was detected for the CARVA2 treatment on day 14, this relationship was not consistently observed across other treatments, indicating that environmental contamination is influenced by multiple factors beyond cecal load alone. These factors may include litter characteristics, bird behavior, bacterial survival outside the host, and heterogeneity in fecal shedding patterns within pens. The absence of a significant correlation between cecal Salmonella counts and carcass rinse recovery at day 43 further indicates that intestinal colonization at market age does not necessarily translate into increased carcass contamination under controlled processing conditions. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies, suggesting that environmental Salmonella levels are influenced by multiple factors beyond intestinal load alone, including litter moisture, temperature, microbial competition, and bacterial survival outside the host (Peichel et al., 2019; Galgano et al., 2023). Furthermore, the positive correlation observed on day 14 may suggest that pens with higher early cecal Salmonella colonization were associated with greater environmental contamination. This finding is consistent with the biology of Salmonella transmission in broilers, where early intestinal establishment can increase fecal shedding and short-term environmental contamination (Wang et al., 2025). Regarding carcass contamination, research shows that the relationship between Salmonella concentrations in the ceca of live birds and contamination detected in carcass rinses is often indirect and relatively weak, as seen in the current study. This is largely because processing steps and plant-level cross-contamination can substantially modify or obscure pre-harvest contamination patterns (Byrd et al., 2025). Although flocks with higher cecal Salmonella loads generally present a greater risk for carcass contamination, elevated intestinal carriage does not necessarily translate into positive carcass rinses. Likewise, flocks with low cecal prevalence can still yield contaminated carcasses after processing (Byrd et al., 2025). Together, these findings highlight the complexity of Salmonella transmission dynamics in broiler production systems and emphasize the importance of evaluating multiple biological and environmental reservoirs rather than relying exclusively on cecal counts as indicators of food safety risk. The multi-compartment approach provides a more realistic assessment of Salmonella dynamics within broiler production systems (Ferreira et al., 2022; Naeem and Bourassa, 2024; Betiku et al., 2025). Full-spectrum essential oils, such as those used in the present study, contain multiple bioactive compounds that may act synergistically or antagonistically, complicating attribution of effects to individual constituents (Ebani et al., 2019; Puvača et al., 2022). This complexity reinforces the importance of evaluating commercial products as whole formulations rather than focusing solely on isolated compounds. In the present study, it appears that no single essential oil treatment consistently outperformed the others across all matrices evaluated, with treatment effects varying between environmental samples and internal organs, highlighting matrix-specific responses rather than a uniform antimicrobial effect.

In summary, under the conditions of this study, dietary carvacrol- and thymol-rich essential oil products had limited, transient effects on Salmonella recovery. Significant responses were observed in selected internal organs and environmental measurements at day 14, whereas no consistent effects were detected on cecal colonization, cecal prevalence, or carcass contamination. Overall, essential oils produced transient, matrix-specific responses rather than consistent reductions across all compartments. The weak association between cecal and environmental Salmonella recovery further highlights the importance of evaluating multiple biological and environmental compartments when assessing intervention efficacy. Collectively, these findings suggest that carvacrol-rich and thymol-rich essential oils may serve as complementary components of integrated Salmonella-control programs, although their effects should not be considered robust enough to serve as standalone interventions.
