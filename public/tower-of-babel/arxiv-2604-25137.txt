Quantum Dynamics via Score Matching on Bohmian Trajectories
Lei Wang

Abstract
We solve the time-dependent Schrödinger equation by learning the score function, the gradient of the log-probability density, on Bohmian trajectories. In Bohm’s formulation of quantum mechanics, particles follow deterministic paths under the classical potential supplemented by a quantum potential depending on the score function of the evolving density. These non-crossing Bohmian trajectories form a continuous normalizing flow governed by the score. We parametrize the score with a neural network and minimize a self-consistent Fisher divergence between the network and the score of the resulting density. We prove that the zero-loss minimizer of this self-consistent objective recovers Schrödinger dynamics for nodeless wave functions, a condition naturally met in quantum vibrations of atoms. We demonstrate the approach on wavepacket splitting in a double-well potential and anharmonic vibrations of a Morse chain. By recasting real-time quantum dynamics as a self-consistent score-driven normalizing flow, this framework opens the time-dependent Schrödinger equation to the rapidly advancing toolkit of modern generative modeling.



Introduction—

The time-dependent Schrödinger equation (TDSE) for quantum particles
of mass
m
with configuration

\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and potential
V(\bm{x})
,

i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi(\bm{x},t)}{\partial t}=\left[-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\nabla^{2}+V(\bm{x})\right]\Psi(\bm{x},t),

(1)

governs quantum dynamics across electronic, atomic, and molecular physics.
Grid-based methods scale exponentially with the number of degrees
of freedom
d
, motivating alternatives that either expand the wave
function in a time-adaptive basis
Meyer et al. 1990
,
parametrize it with neural networks
Carleo and Troyer 2017
, or represent
it through particle
trajectories
Lopreore and Wyatt 1999
;
Sales Mayor et al. 1999
;
Wyatt 2005
—the
approach we build on here.

The trajectory framework originates from the Madelung
decomposition
Madelung 1927
, which writes the wave function
as
\Psi=\sqrt{\rho}\,e^{iS/\hbar}
and recasts
Eq. (
1
) as two coupled Eulerian field equations: a
continuity equation for the probability density
\rho
and a
quantum Hamilton–Jacobi equation for the phase
S
,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\nabla\!\cdot\!\bigl(\rho\,\tfrac{\nabla S}{m}\bigr)=0,

(2)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial S}{\partial t}+\frac{|\nabla S|^{2}}{2m}+V+Q=0,

(3)

where the quantum potential

Q=-(\hbar^{2}/2m)\,\nabla^{2}\!\sqrt{\rho}/\sqrt{\rho}

encodes all non-classical effects.
De Broglie
de Broglie 1927
introduced the pilot-wave picture
in which a particle is guided by the wave with velocity

\bm{v}=\nabla S/m
, and Bohm
Bohm 1952a
;
Bohm 1952b
later
independently developed an alternative
interpretation
of quantum
mechanics in terms of hidden variables: the quantum potential
Q

guides an ensemble of particles along well-defined trajectories
that reproduce all predictions of the Schrödinger equation.

In this Letter, we turn Bohmian mechanics from an
interpretive framework into a practical
computational tool for quantum dynamics by revealing its connection to
modern machinery in generative modeling including continuous normalizing
flows
Chen et al. 2018
;
Zhang et al. 2018
and score
matching
Song and Ermon 2019
;
Song et al. 2021
.

To begin, we define the score function
\bm{s}\equiv\nabla\ln\rho
, in terms
of which the quantum potential takes the form:

Q=-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4m}\!\left[\nabla\!\cdot\!\bm{s}+\tfrac{1}{2}|\bm{s}|^{2}\right].

(4)

Writing

\ln\Psi=\tfrac{1}{2}\ln\rho+iS/\hbar
, one sees that
\bm{s}
and

\bm{v}
are (up to constants) the gradients of the real and imaginary
parts of
\ln\Psi
. Both are therefore gradient fields by
construction.

To follow individual particles in the Lagrangian frame, we apply
the material derivative

d/dt=\partial/\partial t+\bm{v}\!\cdot\!\nabla
to the Madelung
system, obtaining the Bohmian trajectory
equations
Bohm 1952a
;
Bohm 1952b
:

\frac{d\bm{x}}{dt}=\bm{v},\quad m\frac{d\bm{v}}{dt}=-\nabla(V+Q).

(5)

This is formally identical to classical mechanics with effective
potential
V+Q
, except that
\bm{v}=\nabla S/m
is a single-valued
field on
(\bm{x},t)
rather than an independent degree of freedom.
By the uniqueness theorem, trajectories cannot cross or pass through
nodes
(
Holland 1993
, §3.3.8)
. The flow map

\bm{x}(0)\mapsto\bm{x}(t)
is therefore a diffeomorphism for nodeless
wave functions, a continuous normalizing flow
Chen et al. 2018
,
with
\det F\neq 0
, where

F(t)=\partial\bm{x}(t)/\partial\bm{x}(0)
is the deformation gradient
(flow Jacobian). Since
\bm{v}=\nabla S/m
is a gradient field, the
flow is more specifically a Monge–Ampère
flow
Zhang et al. 2018
. Each particle, initialized at

\bm{x}(0)\sim\rho_{0}\equiv\rho(\cdot,0)
with velocity

\bm{v}(0)=\nabla S(\bm{x},0)/m
, follows a deterministic trajectory, and the
density evolves exactly as

\rho(\bm{x}(t),t)=\rho_{0}(\bm{x}(0))/|\!\det F(t)|
.
Figure

splitting in a one-dimensional double-well: the unimodal initial
density (the base distribution of the normalizing flow) is deformed
into multimodal densities, and non-crossing Bohmian trajectories
launched from random samples of the initial density trace the full
density evolution in between.

In Eq. (

no longer appears as a
dynamical field:
\bm{v}
is propagated directly and

depends only
on the score
\bm{s}
via Eq. (

), so the complex phase
need not be represented along the trajectory

;
Garashchuk and Rassolov 2004
.
The phase enters only through the initial condition
\bm{v}(0)=\nabla S(\bm{x},0)/m

(trivially zero when the initial wave function is real), and
S
can be reconstructed post
hoc by integrating Eq. (
3
) along trajectories if desired. The practical
difficulty, however, is that integrating Eq. (
5
) requires
the quantum force
-\nabla Q
, which depends on the score
\bm{s}
of
the evolving density. This score is itself unknown and must be
determined self-consistently.
References
Lopreore and Wyatt 1999
;
Sales Mayor et al. 1999
pioneered quantum trajectory methods
by representing the density on a moving mesh and propagating the
hydrodynamic equations directly.
References
Garashchuk and Rassolov 2003
;
Garashchuk and Rassolov 2004

eliminated the mesh by fitting
\bm{s}
from the particle ensemble
via a polynomial basis and a functional equivalent to Hyvärinen
score matching
Hyvärinen 2005
. Ref.
Maddox and Bittner 2003

used a Bayesian Gaussian mixture for the same purpose. These methods fit the score pointwise at each timestep using a
limited basis (polynomials or Gaussian mixtures), without a global
variational principle connecting successive times.

In this Letter, we instead parametrize the score with a neural
network
\bm{s}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x},t)
and train it end-to-end by minimizing
a single Fisher-divergence objective, simultaneously lifting the
basis-set restriction and enforcing spacetime self-consistency. The
resulting quantum potential drives a continuous normalizing flow
from the initial density, and any zero-loss minimizer of the
objective recovers the Schrödinger solution.

Theory—

The central idea is to
train the score network by minimizing the mismatch between

\bm{s}_{\bm{\theta}}
and the score of its own implied density. The training
objective is the Fisher divergence:

L[\bm{s}_{\bm{\theta}}]=\int_{0}^{T}\!\!dt\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\!d\bm{x}\,\rho_{\bm{\theta}}\,\left|\bm{s}_{\bm{\theta}}-\nabla\ln\rho_{\bm{\theta}}\right|^{2},

(6)

where
T
is the time horizon over which the wave function is
propagated. Both integrals are estimated on the trajectory itself:
the spatial integral becomes the per-time expectation

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim\rho_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot,t)}\!\left[|\bm{s}_{\bm{\theta}}-\nabla\ln\rho_{\bm{\theta}}|^{2}\right]

on the particle ensemble, and the time integral becomes a sum over
trajectory checkpoints. Crucially,

\rho_{\bm{\theta}}
itself depends on
\bm{s}_{\bm{\theta}}
: the score drives
the trajectories that determine the density. Since
L\geq 0
with
equality iff
\bm{s}_{\bm{\theta}}=\nabla\ln\rho_{\bm{\theta}}
, minimizing

L
drives the system toward self-consistency. Because
L
is an
expectation over
\rho_{\bm{\theta}}
, it concentrates on regions where
the physical density has support: the score need not be accurate
where no particles sample.

Theorem
—Suppose the
score
\bm{s}_{\bm{\theta}}
achieves (a)
L[\bm{s}_{\bm{\theta}}]=0
and
(b) induces a Bohmian flow whose density remains strictly positive
on
[0,T]
. Then

\bm{s}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x},t)=\nabla\!\ln|\Psi(\bm{x},t)|^{2}
, where
\Psi

is the unique solution of the TDSE (
1
) evolving from
the prescribed initial wave function; in particular, the wave
function (and hence its density and phase) is fully recovered.
The proof is detailed in the End Matter.

Evaluating the loss function Eq. (
6
) requires
\nabla\ln\rho_{\bm{\theta}}
along each
trajectory.
Differentiating both sides of

\ln\rho_{\bm{\theta}}=\ln\rho_{0}-\ln|\!\det F|

with respect to
\bm{x}(0)
and transforming to the current frame yields

\nabla_{\bm{x}(t)}\!\ln\rho_{\bm{\theta}}=F^{-\mathsf{T}}\!\cdot\!\bigl[\nabla_{\bm{x}(0)}\ln\rho_{0}-\nabla_{\bm{x}(0)}\!\ln|\!\det F|\bigr].

(7)

Note that the
d\times d

deformation gradient
F
is
needed to obtain the target score
\nabla\ln\rho_{\bm{\theta}}
. Differentiating the equations of motion (
5
) with respect
to the initial position
\bm{x}(0)
yields the evolution of the
deformation gradient
F
and its velocity counterpart

G=\partial\bm{v}(t)/\partial\bm{x}(0)
:

\frac{dF}{dt}=G,\quad m\frac{dG}{dt}=-\mathrm{Hess}(V+Q)\cdot F,

(8)

with initial conditions
F(0)=I
and

G(0)=\partial\bm{v}(0)/\partial\bm{x}(0)
, which reduces to
G(0)=0

whenever the initial wave function is real (
\bm{v}(0)=0
),
as in both applications below. Here

[\mathrm{Hess}(V\!+\!Q)]_{ij}=\partial^{2}(V\!+\!Q)/\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}

is the Hessian matrix of second derivatives.
F
and
G
track
how an infinitesimal neighborhood of initial conditions deforms
under the flow, and are co-integrated alongside the particle
trajectories using a symplectic leapfrog scheme.

Implementation—

We parametrize the score function as

\bm{s}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x},t)=\nabla_{\bm{x}}\varphi_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x},t)
, the
gradient of the scalar function
\varphi_{\bm{\theta}}
, ensuring
\bm{s}_{\bm{\theta}}
is a gradient field
by construction. The loss (
6
) depends on
\bm{\theta}

through the score
\bm{s}_{\bm{\theta}}
, the flow Jacobian
F
, and the
sampling distribution
\rho_{\bm{\theta}}
. Since initial positions

\bm{x}_{i}(0)\sim\rho_{0}
are fixed, gradients flow end-to-end through
the trajectory ordinary differential equation (ODE) via the chain rule. The full gradient

dL/d\bm{\theta}
is computed by backpropagation through time (BPTT)
along the discretized leapfrog trajectory, with gradient
checkpointing
Chen et al. 2016
to manage memory. The
algorithm chains 5 spatial-derivative orders of
\varphi_{\bm{\theta}}
,
plus the parameter gradient via BPTT.
The full derivative ladder is given in the End Matter,

1
. Gradient stability is aided by the
symplecticity of the leapfrog integrator, which constrains the
growth of Jacobian products. Figure

summarizes
the resulting computational loop.

Application—

We apply the method to a chain of
d
coupled Morse oscillators

V=\sum_{i}D(1-e^{-\beta x_{i}})^{2}+\lambda\sum_{i}x_{i}x_{i+1}

with
D=12.5
,
\beta=0.2
,
\lambda=0.3
, and the first mode
displaced by
x_{0}=1
. The score is decomposed as

\bm{s}_{\bm{\theta}}=\bm{s}_{\text{base}}+\nabla_{\bm{x}}\varphi_{\bm{\theta}}
, where

\bm{s}_{\text{base}}
is the analytical score from the harmonic
approximation to the potential, providing a warm start. The
correction
\varphi_{\bm{\theta}}
is a multi-layer perceptron (MLP)
with Feature-wise Linear Modulation
(FiLM)

each hidden layer by a learned scale and shift that depend on
t

uses
M=5000

(314 leapfrog

Exact Bohmian dynamics on nodeless wave functions admits no
caustics: the quantum potential
Q
provides the anti-focusing

Holland 1993
, §3.3.8)

Discussion—

Bohm’s theory was famously regarded as a conceptual framework to interpret
quantum mechanics
Bohm 1952a
;
Bohm 1952b
. Self-consistent score matching
on Bohmian trajectories turns it into a practical computational tool for the
TDSE, leveraging machinery from modern generative modeling. The link between
score matching and quantum mechanics in fact predates that machinery itself.
Already in 2003, Garashchuk and Rassolov
Garashchuk and Rassolov 2003
;
Garashchuk and Rassolov 2004

fitted the quantum potential on trajectories by minimizing

\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\tfrac{1}{2}|\bm{s}|^{2}+\nabla\!\cdot\!\bm{s}]\propto\langle Q\rangle
in a polynomial basis — the implicit score-matching
loss of Hyvärinen
Hyvärinen 2005
up to constants. What modern generative modeling
contributes—which we leverage here—is a flexible neural
parametrization of the score, automatic differentiation through long
trajectories, and a globally enforced self-consistent objective over
the entire time horizon, rather than an instantaneous least-squares
fit at each timestep.

The parallel between generative modeling and quantum mechanics
extends from deterministic trajectories to stochastic ones.
Nelson’s stochastic mechanics
Fényes 1952
;
Nelson 1966
is a
Schrödinger-equivalent formulation in which particles follow a
drift–diffusion process rather than the deterministic Bohmian
path; the drift combines the Bohmian velocity with an osmotic term
proportional to the score,
\bm{v}+(\hbar/2m)\nabla\ln\rho
. In
modern generative modeling, flow-based models transport densities
along deterministic ODEs, while diffusion-based
models
Song and Ermon 2019
;
Song et al. 2021
use stochastic differential
equations (SDEs) with a score-proportional drift, the two linked by
a probability-flow ODE that reproduces the SDE’s marginals. The Bohmian ODE is precisely
the probability-flow ODE of Nelson’s SDE: Bohmian mechanics is to
flow-based generative models what Nelson’s stochastic mechanics is
to diffusion-based ones. The same viewpoint underlies recent
neural methods for the Fokker–Planck
equation
Maoutsa et al. 2020
;
Shen et al. 2022
;
Boffi and Vanden-Eijnden 2023
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Wu and Liao 2025
,
which replace SDE simulation by integration of a deterministic ODE
driven by a self-consistently learned score or velocity field. We
extend this construction to Schrödinger dynamics.
The
broader toolkit of modern generative modeling, including denoising
score matching and flow
matching
Lipman et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2023
;
Albergo and Vanden-Eijnden 2023
, may extend
naturally to quantum dynamics. Orlova
et al.

Orlova et al. 2024

have taken a first step on the stochastic side with Deep Stochastic
Mechanics: they parametrize
both
the current and osmotic
velocities of Nelson’s process as neural networks, sample
trajectories self-consistently by Euler–Maruyama integration of the
learned SDE, and train the networks by minimizing partial
differential equation (PDE) residuals of the corresponding velocity
equations on those samples. The sampling
strategy is thus self-generated as in the present method, but the
governing dynamics are stochastic rather than deterministic, two
velocity fields are learned instead of a single score, and the loss
is a PDE residual rather than the Fisher divergence.

Other neural approaches to the TDSE differ more sharply.
Time-dependent variational Monte Carlo
(tVMC)
Carleo et al. 2012
;
Carleo and Troyer 2017
, among the most established
neural-variational methods for quantum dynamics, parametrizes the
complex wave function and evolves its parameters in time by
projecting the TDSE onto the tangent space of the parameter
manifold via the time-dependent variational
principle
Dirac 1930
;
McLachlan 1964
. This projection
requires inverting an often ill-conditioned quantum geometric
tensor at every integrator step, together with Monte Carlo sampling
from the time-dependent wave function. The present method trades
nodal flexibility for a real-valued, density-only representation that
is free of sign and phase, replaces the instantaneous tangent-space
projection by a single global loss over the time horizon, replaces
Metropolis sampling of particle coordinates at every step by a deterministic
particle ensemble transported along the Bohmian trajectories, and
needs no metric inversion at all.

Physics-informed neural networks
(PINNs)
Raissi et al. 2019
are another widely used neural
framework for solving PDEs that has been applied to the TDSE.
PINNs parametrize the complex space–time wave function

\Psi_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x},t)
directly and minimize the TDSE residual
on a set of collocation points drawn from a user-prescribed
distribution over configuration space and time. As in the present
method, the optimization is a single global loss over the time horizon,
but the collocation distribution is chosen by hand rather than by
the dynamics: equal weight is given to regions the physical state
never visits, and accuracy degrades in the tails where

|\Psi|^{2}
is small.
A recent application of PINNs to the fermionic
TDSE
Hou et al. 2026
samples collocation from

|\Psi_{\bm{\theta}}|^{2}
at each fixed
t
to bring the training
distribution onto the physical support. A related global-in-time scheme
Sinibaldi et al. 2026
expands
the complex wave function in a fixed neural-quantum-state basis
with time-dependent coefficients, and likewise samples from

|\Psi_{\bm{\theta}}|^{2}
at each time-integration node. The present
method drops the complex phase via the Madelung passage and
replaces user-prescribed collocation or
|\Psi_{\bm{\theta}}|^{2}

sampling by a self-generated particle ensemble transported along
the Bohmian trajectories, automatically allocating the training
effort where the density has support.

Closer in spirit
to the present method is the parametrized Wasserstein Hamiltonian flow (WHF)
of Ref.
Wu et al. 2025
, which likewise eliminates the
complex wave function and casts Schrödinger dynamics as a continuous
normalizing flow on the probability density. Like tVMC, however,
WHF projects the TDSE onto the parameter manifold at each instant
and integrates the resulting ODE for the neural-network parameters
step by step. The present method
replaces this integrator-driven projection by a global optimization
of the Fisher divergence over the time horizon, endowed with a
built-in convergence diagnostic (
L=0
iff self-consistent).

cells
Ceperley 1991
. These nodes violate the

\rho_{\bm{\theta}}>0
hypothesis of the present analysis on two
counts. First, at a node, the score
\bm{s}=\nabla\ln\rho
diverges
and Bohmian trajectories cannot cross. A smooth score network

\bm{s}_{\bm{\theta}}=\nabla\varphi_{\bm{\theta}}
cannot represent the
resulting cell structure. Second, nodes resurrect the Wallstrom
objection
Wallstrom 1994
: the Madelung system recovers the
Schrödinger equation only under the quantization condition

\oint\bm{v}\cdot d\bm{\ell}=2\pi n\hbar/m
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, on
every closed loop in configuration space, a constraint automatic
for a single-valued
\Psi
but invisible to a score-only
formulation. A possible route that resolves both issues is to employ the
fermion flow
architecture
Xie et al. 2022
;
Xie et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2026
, which
parametrizes many-electron wave functions as a
permutation-equivariant normalizing flow from a Slater
determinant. A diffeomorphism maps nodal cells
to nodal cells, so within each cell
\rho>0
and the phase-free
dynamics of Eq. (
5
) applies. The sign across cells is
read off the Slater determinant rather than learned by the score
network. The Slater determinant is also single-valued with integer
phase winding fixed by its orbitals, and a diffeomorphism preserves
that winding on every closed loop, so the Wallstrom condition is
automatically satisfied. Combining such fermionic flow
architectures with self-consistent score matching is a promising
path toward real-time quantum dynamics of many-electron systems.

Appendix A
Proof of the exactness theorem

We prove
the Exactness Theorem
by
constructing the wave function

\Psi=\sqrt{\rho_{\bm{\theta}}}\,e^{iS/\hbar}
explicitly from

\bm{s}_{\bm{\theta}}
and verifying that it satisfies the
TDSE (
1
). The construction also provides the practical
recipe for recovering the density and phase from a trained score.

(i)
Self-consistency.
The network
\bm{s}_{\bm{\theta}}
and the
score
\nabla\ln\rho_{\bm{\theta}}
of the density
\rho_{\bm{\theta}}

that it induces through the flow driven by
Q[\bm{s}_{\bm{\theta}}]
are,

a priori
, distinct fields. Since
L\geq 0
with equality
iff its integrand vanishes on the support of
\rho_{\bm{\theta}}
,
hypothesis (a) forces

\bm{s}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x},t)=\nabla\ln\rho_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x},t)
wherever

\rho_{\bm{\theta}}>0
, identifying
Q[\bm{s}_{\bm{\theta}}]
with the
Madelung quantum potential
Q[\nabla\ln\rho_{\bm{\theta}}]
.

(ii)
Density.
The Bohmian flow driven by
\bm{s}_{\bm{\theta}}

pushes
\rho_{0}
forward to

\rho_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}(t),t)=\rho_{0}(\bm{x}(0))/|\!\det F(t)|
— the
recipe for the density. Probability is therefore exactly conserved
on Lagrangian particles, and the Madelung continuity
equation (
2
) holds by construction.

(iii)
Existence of a phase.
The initial velocity

\bm{v}(0)=\nabla S(\bm{x},0)/m
is irrotational. Expanding the
material derivative, the Bohmian equation

m\,d\bm{v}/dt=-\nabla(V+Q)
reads

\partial_{t}\bm{v}=-\nabla(V+Q)/m-(\bm{v}\!\cdot\!\nabla)\bm{v}
. Taking
the curl and using the vorticity identity

\nabla\!\times\![(\bm{v}\!\cdot\!\nabla)\bm{v}]=(\bm{v}\!\cdot\!\nabla)\bm{\omega}-(\bm{\omega}\!\cdot\!\nabla)\bm{v}+\bm{\omega}(\nabla\!\cdot\!\bm{v})

with
\bm{\omega}\equiv\nabla\!\times\!\bm{v}
yields the standard
vorticity equation

d\bm{\omega}/dt=(\bm{\omega}\!\cdot\!\nabla)\bm{v}-\bm{\omega}(\nabla\!\cdot\!\bm{v})
,
whose right-hand side is linear in
\bm{\omega}
. Hence

\bm{\omega}(0)=0
is preserved along trajectories. A
single-valued phase
S
with
\bm{v}=\nabla S/m
therefore exists
for all
t\in[0,T]
.

(iv)
Quantum Hamilton–Jacobi equation.
Substituting

\bm{v}=\nabla S/m
into

\partial_{t}\bm{v}+(\bm{v}\!\cdot\!\nabla)\bm{v}=-\nabla(V+Q)/m
and using
the irrotational identity

(\bm{v}\!\cdot\!\nabla)\bm{v}=\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla|\bm{v}|^{2}
(from the
general identity

(\bm{v}\!\cdot\!\nabla)\bm{v}=\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla|\bm{v}|^{2}+(\nabla\!\times\!\bm{v})\!\times\!\bm{v}

together with
\nabla\!\times\!\bm{v}=0
) gives

\nabla\bigl[\partial_{t}S+|\nabla S|^{2}/(2m)+V+Q\bigr]=0
, so
Eq. (
3
) holds up to a gauge function
C(t)
absorbed by

S\to S-\int_{0}^{t}C(t^{\prime})\,dt^{\prime}
. Numerically,
S
is reconstructed
along each trajectory by integrating Eq. (
3
) from the
initial value
S(\bm{x},0)
— the recipe for the phase.

By hypothesis (b),
\rho_{\bm{\theta}}>0
on
[0,T]
, so
\Psi
is
nodeless and the phase
S
just constructed is globally
single-valued. The Madelung
equations
Madelung 1927
[Eqs. (
2
)–(
3
)]
are both satisfied, hence
\Psi
solves the TDSE (
1
),
and step (i) gives

\bm{s}_{\bm{\theta}}=\nabla\ln\rho_{\bm{\theta}}=\nabla\!\ln|\Psi|^{2}
.

\square
