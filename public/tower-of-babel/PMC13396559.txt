Predictors and Mortality Determinants of Non-Thyroidal Illness Syndrome Among Patients in the Medical Intensive Care Unit in Northeastern Tanzania
Authors: Nattasha A Mattaka, Isaack A Lyaruu, Joseph Nangawe, Abid M Sadiq, Fatma S Muhali, Elifuraha W Mkwizu, Kajiru G Kilonzo, Hudaa F Akrabi, Furaha S Lyamuya, Gissela B Nyakunga, Elichilia R Shao, Edward Thomas, Simon C Peter, Leanji Leonard, Phibi Ongujo, Ng’wamba Ntale, Raya S Hamad, Brigitta Mariwa, Elifuraha M Magohe, Baraka Moshi, Nyasatu G Chamba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-thyroidal illness syndrome (NTIS) frequently occurs in critically ill patients and reflects alterations in thyroid hormone metabolism without intrinsic thyroid disease. It has been increasingly recognized as a potential marker of disease severity and poor outcomes in intensive care settings, yet data from Sub-Saharan Africa remain limited. To determine the prevalence of NTIS and identify determinants of mortality among patients admitted to a medical intensive care unit (ICU) in northern Tanzania.MethodsWe conducted a prospective cohort study among 120 adult patients admitted to the medical ICU at a tertiary referral hospital between October 2024 and March 2025. Thyroid function tests (T3, T4, and TSH) were measured within 24 hours of admission. NTIS was defined based on characteristic biochemical patterns in the absence of known thyroid disease. Clinical, laboratory, and outcome data were collected prospectively. Associations were assessed using Chi-square tests and modified Poisson regression to estimate adjusted risk ratios (aRR) with 95% confidence intervals.ResultsThe prevalence of NTIS was 23.3%. Overall mortality was 42.5%, with significantly higher mortality among patients with NTIS (67.9%) compared with those without NTIS (34.8%). NTIS remained independently associated with mortality after adjustment (aRR 8.05; 95% CI: 2.27–28.56; p=0.001). Patients with NTIS were also more likely to experience prolonged hospital stays. Although several clinical variables showed associations in bivariate analysis, most did not remain significant after adjustment. Illness severity, as reflected by clinical scoring systems, remained an important contributor to outcomes.ConclusionNTIS is common among critically ill patients and is strongly associated with increased mortality. It may serve as a practical prognostic marker in ICU settings, particularly in resource-limited environments. Further multicenter studies are needed to validate its prognostic utility and integration into routine clinical practice.Keywords: non-thyroidal illness syndrome, critical illness, intensive care units, mortality, prognosis, Tanzania

Introduction

Thyroid hormones play a central role in cellular metabolism, energy homeostasis, and organ function. During critical illness, systemic physiological stress disrupts thyroid hormone metabolism in the absence of intrinsic hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid axis disease, such as primary hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism, autoimmune thyroid disorders, pituitary disease, or structural thyroid pathology. 1 This condition, termed non-thyroidal illness syndrome (NTIS), also known as euthyroid sick syndrome or low T3 syndrome, is characterized by reduced circulating triiodothyronine (T3), normal or reduced thyroxine (T4), and elevated reverse T3, with inappropriately normal or suppressed thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels.1,2

During critical illness, NTIS develops as a result of altered thyroid hormone metabolism rather than intrinsic thyroid disease. Early illness is usually characterized by isolated low T3, while more severe or prolonged illness may show low T3 with low or normal T4 and inappropriately normal or suppressed TSH. These changes reflect reduced peripheral conversion of T4 to T3, increased conversion to inactive reverse T3, and altered hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid signaling. Inflammatory cytokines, oxidative stress, and changes in thyroid hormone–binding proteins further contribute to this pattern (Figure 1).2-4

Pathophysiological mechanisms underlying non-thyroidal illness syndrome (NTIS) during critical illness

NTIS has traditionally been considered an adaptive metabolic response aimed at reducing energy expenditure during critical illness. However, accumulating evidence suggests that this response may become maladaptive, particularly in prolonged or severe disease states, where it is associated with impaired organ function and adverse clinical outcomes.5,6 Several studies have demonstrated that low T3 levels correlate with increased mortality, prolonged intensive care unit (ICU) stay, and greater disease severity, supporting its role as a potential prognostic biomarker in critically ill populations.6,7

Risk stratification in the ICU commonly relies on scoring systems such as the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II). APACHE II is a composite severity score derived from acute physiological variables, age, and chronic health status; higher scores indicate more severe illness and a greater risk of mortality. Although widely validated, this and similar tools require multiple variables and may be impractical in resource-limited or high-burden clinical settings. Consequently, there is increasing interest in simple, cost-effective biomarkers, such as thyroid hormone levels, that can provide rapid prognostic insight.7,8

Globally, thyroid function abnormalities are frequently observed among critically ill patients. A recent systematic review reported that approximately 58% (IQR 33.2–63.7) of ICU patients exhibit some degree of thyroid dysfunction. 6 Studies from high-income settings have documented NTIS prevalence rates of up to 60% among mechanically ventilated patients, 7 while European cohorts report prevalence ranging from 37.2% to 56.7%.9,10 In contrast, data from African settings remain limited, with studies from Egypt reporting prevalence rates between 12.1% and 28%, likely reflecting differences in patient populations and diagnostic criteria.11,12

Despite growing international evidence, data from Sub-Saharan Africa remain scarce. To date, there is a lack of robust, context-specific studies evaluating NTIS and its prognostic implications in ICU populations within the region. This gap is particularly relevant in Tanzania, where resource constraints necessitate practical and reliable prognostic tools. NTIS has consistently been associated with adverse outcomes, including increased mortality, prolonged hospitalization, and higher complication rates across diverse ICU populations.6,7,11,13-15 These findings highlight its potential utility as a low-cost prognostic marker, particularly in resource-limited settings.

Given the limited regional data and the potential clinical utility of NTIS as a prognostic indicator, this study aimed to determine the prevalence of NTIS and identify determinants of mortality among patients admitted to the medical ICU at a tertiary referral hospital in northeastern Tanzania.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

We conducted a hospital-based cross-sectional study in the medical ICU of Kilimanjaro Christian Medical Centre, a tertiary referral hospital in northern Tanzania. The study was carried out between October 1, 2024 and March 31, 2025.

Study Population

We enrolled adult patients aged ≥18 years who were admitted to the medical ICU during the study period. We excluded patients with ICU stays of less than 24 hours, pregnant or breastfeeding women, patients with a history of chemotherapy or radiotherapy, and patients with intrinsic thyroid disease. Intrinsic thyroid disease was defined as a documented history of hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism, autoimmune thyroid disease, thyroid nodular disease requiring treatment, previous thyroid surgery, pituitary disorders affecting thyroid function, or current use of thyroid hormone replacement, antithyroid medications, or iodine-containing drugs (e.g., amiodarone).1,2 We used a purposive consecutive sampling approach, enrolling all eligible patients who met the inclusion criteria during the study period.

Study Variables and Outcomes

The primary outcome was the presence of NTIS, and the secondary outcomes included 28-day mortality. We assessed 28-day mortality through in-hospital follow-up and post-discharge telephone follow-up where necessary.

Independent variables included demographic and clinical characteristics such as age, sex, marital status, insurance status, residential area, primary diagnosis at ICU admission, comorbidities, length of stay, APACHE II score, and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS).

Hypertension was defined as a documented history of hypertension, current use of antihypertensive medication, or blood pressure measurements meeting established diagnostic criteria (systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg).16,17 Diabetes mellitus was defined as a documented history of diabetes mellitus, current use of glucose-lowering medication, or laboratory findings meeting established diagnostic criteria (HbA1c ≥6.5%, fasting plasma glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L (≥126 mg/dL), or random plasma glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L (≥200 mg/dL) in the presence of classic symptoms. 18 Chronic kidney disease was considered present if there was a documented pre-existing diagnosis in the medical record and/or evidence of chronic kidney damage or reduced kidney function persisting for at least 3 months before ICU admission. Because CKD requires chronicity, admission serum creatinine alone was not used to classify CKD. When eGFR was available from prior records, kidney function was estimated using a specified creatinine-based equation, preferably the race-free CKD-EPI 2021 equation. 19

Data Collection Procedures

Trained investigators prospectively enrolled eligible patients. We obtained written informed consent from conscious patients and from next of kin for patients unable to provide consent due to impaired consciousness.

We collected data using a structured questionnaire administered in Swahili, which was translated from English (Supplemental File 1). The questionnaire was pilot-tested in 10 patients (internal pilot) to assess clarity, relevance, and feasibility, and as there were no major changes, the pilot-tested patients were included. We recorded vital signs at admission. We assessed neurological status using the GCS, a standardized measure of consciousness based on three independently scored components: eye opening, verbal response, and motor response. The total score is calculated by summing these three components, yielding a score ranging from 3 to 15, with lower scores indicating more severe impairment of consciousness. For clinical interpretation, scores of 3–8 indicate severe impairment, 9–12 indicate moderate impairment, and 13–15 indicate mild impairment. 20 We extracted clinical and laboratory data from the hospital’s electronic laboratory system and electronic health management system. We assessed illness severity using the APACHE II scoring system, a widely validated prognostic tool for critically ill patients. 8 The APACHE II score is calculated from 12 acute physiological variables measured within the first 24 hours of ICU admission, together with age and chronic health status. Total scores range from 0 to 71, with higher scores indicating greater illness severity and a higher predicted risk of mortality. APACHE II scores were categorized into mild (0–9), moderate (10–19), severe (20–29), and very severe (≥30) disease severity groups according to increasing score ranges, with higher categories reflecting progressively greater physiological derangement and mortality risk. 8

Laboratory Measurements

We collected approximately 5 mL of venous blood from each participant within 24 hours of ICU admission, preferably in the morning. We transported samples to the central laboratory for analysis.

We measured serum total T3, T4, and TSH using electrochemiluminescence immunoassay on the Maglumi 2000 analyzer (Snibe Diagnostics). The reference ranges were as follows: T3 (0.690–2.150 ng/mL), T4 (52–127 ng/mL), and TSH (0.30–4.5 mIU/L).

We defined NTIS based on characteristic alterations in thyroid function tests observed in the context of systemic illness without intrinsic thyroid disease. The hallmark biochemical pattern consists of reduced serum T3 or free T3 levels, accompanied by normal or reduced T4 or free T4 and inappropriately normal or low TSH concentrations.1,2,21 In early or mild illness, NTIS typically presents as isolated low T3 with preserved T4 and TSH levels, whereas more severe or prolonged illness is characterized by reductions in both T3 and T4, often with suppressed TSH.2,22 These laboratory patterns occur in the absence of primary thyroid or pituitary disease and should be interpreted within the clinical context of acute or chronic systemic illness. 21

Data Management and Statistical Analysis

We securely stored collected data in an encrypted, password-protected database. We performed data cleaning and statistical analysis using SPSS version 31.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

We summarized categorical variables using frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables using means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, as appropriate. We categorized variables based on clinically relevant thresholds and existing literature.

We used the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate, to compare categorical variables across groups. We then fitted modified Poisson regression models with robust standard errors to estimate crude and adjusted risk ratios (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) for factors associated with NTIS and 28-day mortality. We considered a two-sided p-value <0.05 statistically significant.

Results

Baseline Characteristics of the Study Participants

A total of 120 study participants were included in the analysis (Figure 2). The study was characterized by an older age profile, with a median age of 66.5 years, and a slight predominance of females (54.2%). More than half of the participants were married (55.0%), had health insurance coverage (53.3%), and resided in rural areas (55.8%). Cardiometabolic comorbidities were common, particularly hypertension, which affected 60.0% of participants, followed by diabetes mellitus in 30.0%.

Flowchart of study participants

The leading causes of ICU admission reflected a substantial burden of acute medical illness. Sepsis was the most common admission diagnosis (30.8%), followed by stroke (24.2%), heart failure (13.3%), and chronic kidney disease (10.8%). Clinical and laboratory parameters at admission suggested significant physiological derangement within the cohort, with a median APACHE II score of 15 and the majority of participants classified within the moderate severity category (59.2%).

NTIS was identified in 28 participants, corresponding to a prevalence of 23.3%. The median duration of hospitalization was 8 days, and more than half of the participants remained hospitalized for at least 7 days. Overall mortality was high, with 51 deaths recorded during follow-up, yielding an in-hospital mortality rate of 42.5% (Table 1).

Prevalence of NTIS and Associated Characteristics

NTIS was present in 23.3% of study participants (Figure 3). Clinically, NTIS was observed across a broad spectrum of demographic and disease categories, although it appeared more frequently among older individuals, females, participants without health insurance, and those admitted with sepsis. Hypertension and diabetes mellitus were also common among participants with NTIS.

Prevalence of non-thyroidal illness syndrome

Most participants with NTIS had moderate APACHE II severity scores and mild impairment of consciousness based on the Glasgow Coma Scale. In unadjusted analyses, marital status and moderate Glasgow Coma Scale impairment demonstrated potential associations with NTIS; however, these relationships did not persist after adjustment for other covariates. Similarly, diabetes mellitus, sepsis, and stroke were not independently associated with NTIS after multivariable analysis.

Severity of illness demonstrated a more consistent relationship with NTIS. Compared with participants classified as having mild disease severity, those within the moderate APACHE II category had a significantly lower likelihood of NTIS (aRR 0.22, 95% CI 0.05–0.96; p=0.044). A similar association was observed among participants with very severe APACHE II scores (aRR 0.09, 95% CI 0.01–0.91; p=0.041). No significant independent associations were identified for age, sex, residence, insurance status, hypertension, or duration of hospitalization (Table 2).

Mortality and Subgroup Outcome Analysis

Mortality was substantially higher among participants with NTIS than among those without NTIS. Nearly two-thirds of participants with NTIS died during follow-up (67.9%), compared with approximately one-third of those without NTIS (34.8%). After adjustment for potential confounding variables, NTIS remained a strong independent predictor of mortality, increasing the risk of death more than eightfold (aRR 8.05, 95% CI 2.27–28.56; p=0.001) (Table 2).

Among the 51 participants who died, no demographic characteristic, comorbidity, admission diagnosis, illness severity category, neurological status, or duration of hospitalization remained independently associated with mortality after multivariable adjustment. Although heart failure and chronic kidney disease demonstrated relatively large adjusted effect estimates, the wide confidence intervals and lack of statistical significance suggest considerable uncertainty around these associations. Likewise, APACHE II category, Glasgow Coma Scale category, diabetes mellitus, sepsis, stroke, and length of hospital stay were not independently associated with mortality in the adjusted analysis (Table 3).

Overall, the findings indicate that NTIS was the strongest predictor of mortality identified in this cohort, highlighting its potential value as a marker of poor prognosis among critically ill medical ICU patients.

Discussion

Our study showed that NTIS was common among adults admitted to the medical ICU in northeastern Tanzania and that it was associated with worse clinical outcomes, including higher mortality and longer hospitalization. This finding is consistent with the recognized NTIS phenotype, in which thyroid hormone abnormalities develop during systemic stress in the absence of primary thyroid disease, typically beginning with isolated low T3 and, in more severe illness, progressing to low T3/T4 with inappropriately normal or low TSH.1,2,5 In this context, NTIS should be viewed primarily as a marker of critical illness severity rather than as an isolated thyroid disorder.1,2,4,5

The prevalence of NTIS in our cohort was lower than that reported in many high-acuity ICU series and meta-analytic summaries. Several factors may explain this difference. First, our study included all medical ICU admissions rather than a highly selected subgroup enriched for organ failure, mechanical ventilation, or sepsis, which likely reduced the apparent frequency of thyroid test abnormalities. Second, the cohort had mixed illness severity, with a median APACHE II score of 15 and most participants classified as having moderate disease severity, rather than uniform extreme physiological derangement. Third, NTIS was assessed using a single thyroid profile obtained early after admission and based on total thyroid hormones, which may have missed evolving or borderline abnormalities. Finally, the expression of NTIS is known to vary with the intensity of systemic inflammation, duration of critical illness, and nutritional stress, all of which influence the observed prevalence across studies.2,6

The strongest clinical signal in our study was the association between NTIS and mortality. Patients with NTIS experienced substantially higher death rates than those without NTIS, and NTIS remained associated with mortality after adjustment for other covariates. This aligns with the 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis, which found NTIS to be associated with adverse outcomes in critically ill adults, 6 as well as more recent ICU cohorts that reported a mortality association in patients with low thyroid hormone levels. 21 At the same time, a 2025 retrospective ICU study found that NTIS correlated strongly with illness severity but did not remain an independent mortality predictor after adjustment. 23 Together, these findings suggest that NTIS may function both as a biomarker of severe physiological derangement and, in some populations, as an independent prognostic marker.4,6,21,23

Our finding that longer hospital stay and higher APACHE II severity were associated with poorer outcomes also reinforces the concept that NTIS emerges in the setting of global metabolic stress, inflammatory activation, and multi-organ dysfunction.2,4,5 Mechanistically, critical illness reduces peripheral conversion of T4 to T3, alters deiodinase activity, and disrupts hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid signaling, which may initially represent an adaptive energy-conserving response but can become maladaptive during prolonged critical illness.1,4,5 The persistence of NTIS in more severe disease likely reflects both the burden of organ dysfunction and the impaired capacity to recover from acute illness.4,5

These findings have practical implications. First, clinicians should recognize NTIS as a frequent ICU phenomenon and avoid misclassifying it as primary hypothyroidism without supportive clinical evidence. Second, routine thyroid hormone replacement is not recommended for hospitalized adults with NTIS, because guideline-based therapy has not shown clear clinical benefit and may raise safety concerns. 24 The American Thyroid Association explicitly recommends against levothyroxine and liothyronine for hospitalized patients with NTIS, and later reviews continue to support that position.4,10,24 In practice, the best response is to treat the underlying critical illness, reassess thyroid function after recovery when indicated, and reserve endocrine consultation for discordant or persistent abnormalities.1,4,10,24

In resource-limited settings, where comprehensive prognostic scoring systems may be difficult to implement, early identification of NTIS could complement clinical assessment and help prioritize high-risk patients for closer monitoring and targeted supportive care. However, these findings do not support routine thyroid hormone replacement, as NTIS appears to be primarily a physiological response to critical illness rather than a condition requiring direct endocrine intervention.

This study has several limitations that warrant caution. We measured total rather than free thyroid hormones, did not measure reverse T3, and relied on a single early thyroid profile, which may have led to misclassification in some patients. In addition, this was a single-center study, so the findings may not generalize to other ICUs with different case mix or laboratory practices. Because NTIS often mirrors severity of illness, our results should not be interpreted as proving causality; rather, they support NTIS as a clinically useful marker of risk in critically ill adults.

Conclusion

NTIS was common among adults admitted to the medical ICU and was strongly associated with adverse clinical outcomes, particularly increased mortality and prolonged hospitalization. These findings reinforce that NTIS reflects the severity of systemic illness rather than a primary thyroid disorder and may serve as a pragmatic, low-cost biomarker for early risk stratification in critically ill patients. Future studies should incorporate serial thyroid function measurements, include free hormone assays, and evaluate the incremental prognostic value of NTIS alongside established severity scores. Multicenter designs will be essential to validate these findings and define the role of NTIS in clinical decision-making across diverse ICU settings.
