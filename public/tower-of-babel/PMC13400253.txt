Successful limb salvage using a fresh maternal great saphenous vein allograft for combined femoral artery and vein transection after gunshot injury in a child
Authors: Riyad Mroueh, Hadi Farhat, Hussein Ezzeddine, Hassan Shouman, Jamal Hoballah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Major vascular trauma in children is rare and presents significant reconstructive challenges when autologous conduit options are limited. We report the case of a 2-year-old boy with a close-range shotgun injury causing complete transection and segmental loss of the superficial femoral artery and vein with associated open femoral fracture and acute limb ischemia. Because the child's great saphenous veins were unsuitable for reconstruction, a fresh maternal great saphenous vein allograft was used for arterial repair, whereas the femoral vein was ligated. Limb perfusion was successfully restored without immunosuppression. At 4-year follow-up, the limb remained viable and functional despite graft occlusion, owing to robust collateral arterial development.

Major vascular trauma in children is uncommon, accounting for approximately 0.4% to 1% of pediatric trauma cases, but it carries substantial risk of limb loss and mortality when it occurs.1, 2, 3 Lower-extremity vascular injuries are most commonly associated with penetrating trauma and high-energy mechanisms such as gunshot wounds and motor vehicle collisions.3,4 Complete traumatic transection of both the femoral artery and vein is exceptionally rare and usually reflects severe soft-tissue destruction with high risk of acute limb ischemia.3,4

Revascularization in very young children is technically challenging because of the small vessel caliber, vasospasm, limited conduit availability, and the need to accommodate future somatic growth.4, 5, 6, 7 Although autologous vein grafts remain the preferred conduit for reconstruction, they may be unsuitable in infants and toddlers because of inadequate size.4, 5, 6, 7 In such situations, alternative strategies including allograft or homograft conduits may be required to achieve limb salvage.4,7,8

We present the case of a 2-year-old boy who sustained a close-range shotgun injury causing complete transection and segmental loss of both the superficial femoral artery and vein, successfully managed using a fresh maternal great saphenous vein allograft for arterial reconstruction. Similar parent-derived venous allografts have rarely been described in pediatric vascular reconstruction.8 This case highlights a potential limb-salvage strategy for complex pediatric femoral vascular trauma when autologous conduit options are unavailable. Case presentation

A previously healthy 2-year-old boy presented to the emergency department of Raee Hospital after sustaining a close-range high-energy shotgun injury from a 12-gauge hunting weapon to the right proximal anteromedial thigh approximately 5 hours earlier following transfer from another hospital. On arrival, he was in hemorrhagic shock with the signs of acute limb ischemia, including tachycardia (175 beats/min), hypotension (72/45 mmHg), pallor, mottling, absent distal pulses, and a cold right lower extremity.

Examination revealed an open comminuted gunshot wound with extensive soft-tissue destruction and an associated open femoral fracture (Fig 1). Laboratory investigations showed hemoglobin of 8.3 g/dL and hematocrit of 24.4%. Immediate resuscitation was initiated, including transfusion of one unit of packed red blood cells. Plain radiographs demonstrated proximal femoral shaft deformity with multiple retained metallic fragments in the thigh and pelvis. Intraoperatively, complete transection with segmental loss of approximately 12 to 15 cm of both the superficial femoral artery and femoral vein was identified, associated with severe surrounding soft-tissue injury. Limb salvage was attempted through arterial reconstruction and venous ligation. Bilateral autologous great saphenous veins were evaluated but found to be severely hypoplastic (∼0.5 mm in diameter), rendering them unsuitable for reconstruction. Because of the contaminated traumatic field and lack of suitable autologous conduit, a living-related venous allograft was selected.

Urgent ABO blood typing demonstrated compatibility between the patient and his mother; both were blood group A positive. After informed consent was obtained, duplex ultrasound study confirmed suitability of the maternal great saphenous vein. A fresh reversed maternal great saphenous vein graft measuring 4 to 5 mm in diameter was harvested and used for interposition reconstruction of the superficial femoral artery using end-to-end running anastomoses extending from the common femoral artery bifurcation proximally to the proximal popliteal artery distally (Fig 2). The femoral vein was ligated because of extensive segmental loss and limited conduit availability. Concomitantly, the femoral fracture was stabilized using the Metaizeau closed reduction technique with intramedullary pinning. Soft-tissue coverage was achieved using available local tissue, followed by application of negative-pressure wound therapy to the residual defect (Fig 3).Fig 2Arterial reconstruction using reversed maternal great saphenous vein allograft.Fig 3Soft-tissue coverage and application of negative-pressure wound therapy following vascular reconstruction.

Following revascularization, distal perfusion was restored with palpable distal pulses, Doppler-confirmed flow, and a warm well-perfused limb. Prophylactic fasciotomy was performed, and the patient was closely monitored for compartment syndrome. Postoperatively, he was transferred to the intensive care unit, where ceftriaxone, vancomycin, and prophylactic low-molecular-weight heparin were administered. No immunosuppressive therapy was given because of the urgent traumatic setting, the low antigenicity of venous tissue, and the absence of established protocols supporting immunosuppression for peripheral venous allografts.

Serial postoperative clinical and duplex evaluations during the first year confirmed graft patency, adequate distal perfusion, and progressive functional recovery. The patient was subsequently lost to follow-up before representing 4 years later with preserved limb growth, full range of motion, and independent pain-free ambulation. Imaging demonstrated occlusion of the reconstructed femoral artery; however, limb viability and function were maintained through robust collateral arterial development. As interval follow-up imaging was unavailable, the precise timing of graft occlusion could not be determined.

Discussion

Pediatric vascular trauma is uncommon but associated with high morbidity because children can maintain near-normal hemodynamic parameters until late stages of hemorrhagic shock, making early diagnosis dependent on clinical signs such as pallor, mottling, cold extremities, and absent distal pulses.2,3 In the present case, complete transection and segmental loss of both the superficial femoral artery and vein resulted in acute limb ischemia, a pattern associated with high risk of limb loss when revascularization is delayed.3,4

Autologous great saphenous vein grafting remains the preferred conduit for pediatric arterial reconstruction; however, suitable conduit may be unavailable in infants and toddlers due to insufficient vessel caliber and length.4,7 In such circumstances, alternative strategies including temporary shunting, cryopreserved homografts, or venous allografts may be required to achieve limb salvage.4,7,8 Previous reports by Qureshi et al8 described successful use of fresh parental great saphenous vein allografts in infants undergoing elective oncologic femoral vessel reconstruction. Similarly, Nguyen et al9 reported successful limb salvage using a cryopreserved venous homograft in a child with severe upper-extremity trauma. Our case differs from previously published reports because reconstruction was performed in an emergent trauma setting characterized by hemorrhagic shock, acute limb ischemia, open fracture, and extensive soft-tissue contamination. A fresh maternal great saphenous vein allograft was harvested and implanted immediately for superficial femoral artery reconstruction without immunosuppressive therapy. The role of immunosuppression after venous allograft reconstruction remains controversial. Although venous allografts are immunogenic, no standardized immunosuppressive regimen has been established for peripheral venous allograft reconstruction. Given the contaminated traumatic setting and uncertain benefit, immunosuppression was not administered in this patient.10

To our knowledge, this represents the first reported pediatric case of traumatic femoral artery reconstruction using a fresh living-related maternal venous allograft. In the present case, donor and recipient were ABO compatible. However, the importance of ABO matching in peripheral venous allografts remains unclear, and previous reports of parent-derived venous allografts did not specify ABO compatibility. Therefore, ABO-compatible donor selection was considered a reasonable precaution rather than a requirement.11,12

Venous reconstruction was intentionally not performed because the available conduit length was insufficient for simultaneous arterial and venous repair, whereas the venous injury itself involved extensive segmental loss that precluded primary reconstruction. In the setting of acute ischemia, restoration of arterial inflow was prioritized as the primary determinant of limb salvage.3,4,6 In addition, femoral vein ligation is considered an acceptable strategy in severe lower-extremity trauma when reconstruction is not technically feasible, particularly in contaminated wounds or when conduit options are limited.1,3,5 Our patient developed no significant postoperative edema, venous congestion, limb growth disturbance, or long-term venous morbidity, likely due to preserved profunda femoris venous drainage and the enhanced collateral adaptive capacity seen in children.2,3 A potential long-term consequence of pediatric vascular injury is limb-length discrepancy. Although our patient demonstrated normal limb growth at 4-year follow-up, growth disturbances may emerge during later growth spurts. Therefore, continued surveillance into adolescence remains advisable.13

An additional important aspect of this case is the long-term outcome of the arterial reconstruction. Although imaging at 4 years demonstrated graft occlusion, the limb remained viable and fully functional because sufficient collateral arterial circulation had developed during the period of maintained graft patency. This finding suggests that, in selected patients with pediatric trauma, arterial reconstruction with a biologic conduit may function not only as definitive revascularization but also as a temporary limb-salvage bridge that permits collateral development and prevents irreversible ischemic injury during the critical early period.

Conclusions

Simultaneous traumatic transection of the femoral artery and vein in pediatric patients is rare and presents major reconstructive challenges, particularly when suitable autologous conduits are unavailable. In this case, arterial reconstruction using a fresh maternal great saphenous vein allograft enabled early reperfusion and successful limb salvage without immunosuppressive therapy. Although long-term graft patency was not maintained, the reconstruction prevented irreversible ischemic injury and allowed development of robust collateral circulation, resulting in preserved limb growth and excellent functional outcome at 4-year follow-up. This case highlights the potential role of living-related venous allografts as a limb-salvage strategy in complex pediatric vascular trauma.
