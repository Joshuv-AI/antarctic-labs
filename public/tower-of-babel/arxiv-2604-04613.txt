A Convergent Hybridizable Discontinuous Galerkin Method for Einstein–Scalar Equations Thanks: This work has been supported by the Deutsche Forschungsgemeinschaft (DFG, German Research Foundation) – 577175348.
Mukul Dwivedi and Andreas Rupp

Abstract
. We propose and analyze a hybridized discontinuous Galerkin (HDG) method for the spherically symmetric Einstein–scalar system in Bondi gauge. After rewriting the model as a local first-order PDE–ODE system by introducing suitable scaled variables, we construct a semidiscrete scheme in which the element unknowns are computed locally and the coupling is carried by traces on the mesh skeleton. In the present radial setting, these traces can be eliminated recursively, so that only the main evolution variable is advanced in time, while the metric variables are recovered from discrete constraint relations. We prove local semidiscrete well-posedness, derive a global L 2 L^{2} –stability estimate, establish an optimal order L 2 L^{2} error bound for the main evolution variable for polynomial degree k ≥ 1 k\geq 1 , and obtain reconstruction error estimates for the metric variables and the associated mass functional. Numerical experiments verify the predicted spatial convergence rate and illustrate qualitative features of the Einstein–scalar dynamics, including large-data collapse profiles and smooth-pulse evolution.

From a practical standpoint, numerical relativity has achieved remarkable success. Nonetheless, the stability and convergence analysis of high-order methods for the full nonlinear Einstein system
remains mathematically difficult. Under suitable decompositions and gauge choices, Einstein’s equations become highly nonlinear hyperbolic systems, and rigorous numerical analysis in this
setting is still rather limited. It is therefore natural to begin with symmetry-reduced models. Among these, the spherically symmetric Einstein–scalar field system is particularly important, since it retains key analytical and physical features such as gravitational collapse, horizon formation, and long-time metric evolution. The foundational PDE theory for this model was developed by Christodoulou; see

We briefly recall the model following the derivation in Bondi gauge in
[
8
]
and the classical works on the spherically symmetric Einstein–massless-scalar system
[
9
]
. In geometric units (
G=c=1
), the Einstein field equations are

(1.1)

G_{\mu\nu}=8\pi\,T_{\mu\nu},

where
G_{\mu\nu}:=R_{\mu\nu}-\tfrac{1}{2}g_{\mu\nu}R
is the Einstein tensor. Here,
g_{\mu\nu}
is the spacetime metric,
R_{\mu\nu}
is the Ricci tensor, and
R
is the scalar curvature.
For a massless scalar field
\phi
, the stress–energy tensor is

T_{\mu\nu}=\partial_{\mu}\phi\,\partial_{\nu}\phi-\frac{1}{2}\,g_{\mu\nu}\,g^{\alpha\beta}\,\partial_{\alpha}\phi\,\partial_{\beta}\phi.

In spherical symmetry, adopting Bondi-type null coordinates
(t,r,\theta,\varphi)
with areal radius
r\geq 0
,
one may write the metric in the Bondi–Sachs form
[
4
,
8
,
47
]

(1.2)

ds^{2}=-g(t,r)\,\tilde{g}(t,r)\,dt^{2}-2\,g(t,r)\,dt\,dr+r^{2}\,d\Omega^{2},\qquad d\Omega^{2}=d\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta\,d\varphi^{2},

where
g
and
\tilde{g}
are positive metric coefficients.

The reduced equations in (
1.2
) are obtained by combining two ingredients.
First, suitable components of the Einstein equations provide radial “constraint” relations for the metric
coefficients. Second, the conservation law
\nabla_{\mu}T^{\mu\nu}=0
yields the wave equation for
\phi
,
which couples back to the metric through
g
and
\tilde{g}
; see
[
8
, §2 and Appendix A]

for a detailed derivation in the present normalization.
To express the resulting system in a simple first-order form, one introduces the auxiliary variables

(1.3a)

\displaystyle u(t,r)

\displaystyle:=\bigl(r\,\phi(t,r)\bigr)_{r},

(1.3b)

\displaystyle\tilde{u}(t,r)

\displaystyle:=\phi(t,r)=\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}u(t,s)\,ds.

Here, the averaging relation (
1.3b
) encodes regularity at
r=0
, and it is already the first place
where a nonlocal dependence on the full interval
[0,r]
enters the formulation.

With this notation (and using the same normalization as
[
8
]
, in which the factor
4\pi

is absorbed), the symmetry-reduced Einstein–scalar system becomes the coupled PDE–ODE system
[
8
]
:

(1.4a)

\displaystyle u_{t}-\!\Big(\frac{1}{2}\,\tilde{g}\,u\Big)_{r}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\,\tilde{g}_{r}\,\tilde{u},

(1.4b)

\displaystyle g_{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r}\,g\,\bigl(u-\tilde{u}\bigr)^{2},

(1.4c)

\displaystyle\tilde{g}_{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r}\,(g-\tilde{g}).

We consider
r\in[0,b]
for a fixed outer radius
b>0
and
(t,r)\in[0,T]\times[0,b]
.
The outer boundary data are prescribed at
r=b
by

(1.5)

u(t,b)=U_{b}(t),\qquad g(t,b)=1,

while regularity at the center
r=0
requires
r\phi(t,r)\to 0
as
r\to 0
and
\tilde{g}
remains bounded. A useful point for both analysis and numerics is that the constraint equations (
1.4b
) and (
1.4c
)
can be integrated explicitly (for each fixed
t
), which makes the nonlocal structure completely visible:

(1.6a)

\displaystyle\tilde{g}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}g(s)\,ds,

(1.6b)

\displaystyle g(r)

\displaystyle=\exp\Big(-\int_{r}^{b}\frac{1}{s}(u-\tilde{u})^{2}\,ds\Big).

Thus, even though (
1.4a
) is written as a local transport equation, its coefficients and source term depend
on spatial averages and integrals through (
1.3b
), (
1.6a
), and (
1.6b
).

A key structural feature is that (
1.3b
) and the representation of
\tilde{g}
are nonlocal in
r
.
To localize these equations, we introduce
w(t,r):=r\,\tilde{u}(t,r)
and
z(t,r):=r\,\tilde{g}(t,r)
.
Then, from (
1.3b
),

w_{r}=(r\tilde{u})_{r}=u.

Likewise, using (
1.6a
),

z_{r}=(r\tilde{g})_{r}=g.

The corresponding center conditions are

(1.7)

w(t,0)=0,\qquad z(t,0)=0.

Thus, the model (
1.3b
)–(
1.4
) is equivalently represented by the local first-order system

(1.8a)

\displaystyle u_{t}-\!\Big(\frac{1}{2}\,\tilde{g}\,u\Big)_{r}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\,\tilde{g}_{r}\,\tilde{u},

(1.8b)

\displaystyle w_{r}

\displaystyle=u,

(1.8c)

\displaystyle g_{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r}\,g\,\bigl(u-\tilde{u}\bigr)^{2},

(1.8d)

\displaystyle z_{r}

\displaystyle=g,

with
\tilde{u}=w/r
,
\tilde{g}=z/r
for
r>0
, center conditions (
1.7
), and outer conditions (
1.5
).
This local form is the starting point for our HDG formulation.

We also record a basic bound for the metric coefficients, which will be repeatedly used to identify the correct upwind directions and to control the coefficients in the numerical fluxes.

Lemma 1.1

(Bounds for the metric coefficients)
.

Assume
g
and
\tilde{g}
satisfy (
1.4b
)–(
1.4c
) with the outer normalization
g(b)=1
.
Then for every fixed
t
and all
r\in(0,b]
,

0<\tilde{g}(r)\leq g(r)\leq 1.

Proof.

From (
1.4b
), we have
g_{r}=\frac{1}{r}g(u-\tilde{u})^{2}\geq 0
for
r>0
. Hence,
g
is nondecreasing in
r
and
g(r)\leq g(b)=1
. Moreover, (
1.6a
) implies
\tilde{g}(r)\leq g(r)
, and (
1.6b
) implies
g>0
. This immediately yields
0<\tilde{g}\leq 1
.
∎

These considerations suggest that the spherically symmetric Einstein–scalar system (
1.4
) provides a natural model for testing new high-order discontinuous discretizations. To obtain a numerical method with a better computational structure, high-order accuracy, and fewer globally coupled degrees of freedom, we consider a hybridizable discontinuous Galerkin (HDG) approach. HDG methods were first developed for second-order elliptic problems
[
16
]
, and have since been successfully extended to many other classes of partial differential equations.
These include convection–diffusion problems
[
15
,
37
]
, Stokes and incompressible flow systems
[
18
,
17
,
36
,
38
,
39
,
45
]
, the Helmholtz equation
[
6
,
30
,
22
]
, and elasticity equations
[
19
,
32
,
44
]
.
More recently, HDG techniques have also been developed for nonlinear dispersive and fully nonlinear problems, including the Ostrovsky equation
[
25
]
, the Camassa–Holm–Kadomtsev–Petviashvili equation
[
24
]
, the Monge–Ampère problem
[
40
]
, and KdV-type equations
[
7
,
23
,
48
]
.
A defining feature of HDG is that the globally coupled unknowns are numerical traces on the mesh skeleton, while the element unknowns are recovered locally. This structure not only reduces the size of the global algebraic system compared with standard DG methods, but also makes HDG particularly attractive from the solver point of view. In this direction, Lu et al. developed homogeneous multigrid methods for HDG discretizations and provided an analysis of injection operators for geometric multigrid solvers for HDG methods
[
34
,
35
]
. Related multiscale and localized formulations have also been investigated in the HDG setting; see, for instance,
[
33
]
.
For the Bondi-gauge Einstein–scalar system considered here, such a structure is particularly attractive because the evolution equation is coupled with radial constraint relations, and therefore a method based on local reconstruction and reduced global coupling provides a natural and efficient discretization framework.

The main contribution of this paper is to formulate and analyze a hybridized trace-based semidiscretization for the Bondi–gauge Einstein–scalar system (
1.4
) on a finite radial interval. After introducing the
auxiliary variables
w=r\tilde{u}
and
z=r\tilde{g}
, the nonlocal structure
of the model is rewritten in the local first-order form (
1.8
),
while the discrete reconstruction is carried out through the local reconstruction formulae, which makes it possible to couple the elements only through single-valued traces. In the present one-dimensional radial setting, these traces can in fact be eliminated recursively, so that only the principal evolution variable
u_{h}
is advanced in time, while the remaining quantities are reconstructed from the discrete constraint relations.
On the analytical side, we prove local semidiscrete well–posedness, derive a global
L^{2}
–stability estimate, establish an optimal order
L^{2}
error bound for
u_{h}
for polynomial degree
k\geq 1
, and obtain corresponding reconstruction estimates for
the metric variables and the associated mass functional.
The numerical experiments support the theory and demonstrate that the method reproduces the expected qualitative behavior of the Einstein–scalar dynamics, including collapse toward black-hole profiles. In this sense, the paper shows that HDG provides not only a reduced discrete coupling structure but also a rigorous and effective framework for high-order approximation of this symmetry-reduced Einstein system.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the HDG discretization and derive the local and global trace formulations. Section
3
is devoted to the semidiscrete well-posedness and the
L^{2}
-stability estimate. In Section
4
, we prove the optimal error estimate and the corresponding reconstruction-error bounds. Section
5
presents numerical experiments that verify the theoretical rates and illustrate black-hole formation and the associated metric behavior. We close with a brief discussion in Section
6
.

2.
A hybridizable discontinuous Galerkin discretization

We discretize the local first–order Einstein–scalar system (
1.8
) in space on
[0,b]
and keep time continuous.

2.1.
Mesh, skeleton notation, and discrete spaces

Let
0=r_{0}<r_{1}<\dots<r_{N}=b
be a partition of
[0,b]
and set

I_{i}:=(r_{i-1},r_{i}),\qquad i=1,\dots,N,\qquad h_{i}=r_{i}-r_{i-1},\qquad\mathcal{T}_{h}:=\{I_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N},\qquad h=\max h_{i}.

We denote the set of mesh nodes by

\mathcal{F}_{h}:=\{r_{i}\}_{i=0}^{N},\qquad\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\partial}:=\{r_{0},r_{N}\},\qquad\mathcal{F}_{h}^{0}:=\mathcal{F}_{h}\setminus\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\partial}.

For an integer
k\geq 0
, let
P^{k}(I_{i})
be the polynomials of degree at most
k
on
I_{i}
, and
define the broken space

V_{h}^{k}:=\bigl\{v\in L^{2}(0,b):\ v|_{I_{i}}\in P^{k}(I_{i})\ \text{for }i=1,\dots,N\bigr\}.

We use the standard volume inner product

(v_{1},v_{2})_{I_{i}}:=\int_{I_{i}}v_{1}v_{2}\,dr,\qquad(v_{1},v_{2})_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}(v_{1},v_{2})_{I_{i}},

and the oriented boundary pairing

\langle\psi,\eta\,n\rangle_{\partial I_{i}}:=\psi(r_{i})\,\eta(r_{i})-\psi(r_{i-1})\,\eta(r_{i-1}),

where the outward normal on
\partial I_{i}
is
n(r_{i-1})=-1
and
n(r_{i})=+1
.
We also introduce the boundary–restricted trace spaces

M_{h}^{R}:=L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{h}\setminus\{r_{0}\}),\qquad M_{h}^{L}:=L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{h}\setminus\{r_{N}\}),\qquad M_{h}^{0}:=L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{h}^{0}).

Fix an element
I_{i}
. Let us consider system (
1.8
) in
I_{i}
, and prescribe the following

(2.1)

u(t,r_{i})=\widehat{u}_{i}(t),\qquad g(t,r_{i})=\widehat{g}_{i}(t),\qquad w(t,r_{i-1})=\widehat{w}_{i-1}(t),\qquad z(t,r_{i-1})=\widehat{z}_{i-1}(t),

along with the initial data
u_{0}|_{I_{i}}
.
Assume the unique solvability of the local system (
1.8
) on an element

I_{i}=(r_{i-1},r_{i})
for given data (
2.1
) and a sufficiently smooth
initial condition
u_{0}|_{I_{i}}
, and denote the corresponding elementwise solution by

(u,w,g,z)|_{I_{i}}
. Define
(u,w,g,z)
on
[0,b]
by collecting these element solutions.

This piecewise function
(u,w,g,z)
is a global (classical) solution of the original problem on
[0,b]

if and only if the traces of the quantities that are differentiated in
r
are single-valued at
every interior node. Concretely, for each
r_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{0}
(with
i=1,\dots,N-1
) we require

(2.2)

\displaystyle w|_{I_{i}}(t,r_{i})=w|_{I_{i+1}}(t,r_{i})

\displaystyle:=\widehat{w}_{i}(t),\qquad z|_{I_{i}}(t,r_{i})=z|_{I_{i+1}}(t,r_{i}):=\widehat{z}_{i}(t),

\displaystyle u|_{I_{i}}(t,r_{i})=u|_{I_{i+1}}(t,r_{i})

\displaystyle:=\widehat{u}_{i}(t),\qquad g|_{I_{i}}(t,r_{i})=g|_{I_{i+1}}(t,r_{i}):=\widehat{g}_{i}(t).

The physical boundary data close the system by fixing the remaining inflow values at
r=0
and
r=b
by

\widehat{u}_{N}(t)=U_{b}(t),\qquad\widehat{g}_{N}(t)=1,\qquad\widehat{w}_{0}(t)=0,\qquad\widehat{z}_{0}(t)=0.

Our HDG discretization is then designed as a discrete analogue of this characterization: element
unknowns are computed locally for given traces, and the traces are determined globally by enforcing a
weak form of (
2.2
) together with the boundary conditions.

2.2.
HDG method

For a fixed element
I_{i}=(r_{i-1},r_{i})
, assume that the traces

\widehat{u}_{h}(r_{i}),\penalty\ \widehat{g}_{h}(r_{i})
,
\widehat{w}_{h}(r_{i-1}),\penalty\ \widehat{z}_{h}(r_{i-1})
, and a function
u_{h}|_{I_{i}}\in P^{k}(I_{i})
are provided. We define the local reconstructions on
I_{i}
as

(2.3a)

\displaystyle w_{h}(r)

\displaystyle:=\widehat{w}_{h}(r_{i-1})+\int_{r_{i-1}}^{r}u_{h}(s)\,ds,

(2.3b)

\displaystyle g_{h}(r)

\displaystyle:=\widehat{g}_{h}(r_{i})\,\exp\!\Bigl(-\int_{r}^{r_{i}}\frac{1}{s}\Bigl(u_{h}(s)-\tilde{u}_{h}(s)\Bigr)^{2}ds\Bigr),

(2.3c)

\displaystyle z_{h}(r)

\displaystyle:=\widehat{z}_{h}(r_{i-1})+\int_{r_{i-1}}^{r}g_{h}(s)\,ds,

where, for
r>0
,
\tilde{u}_{h}(r):=\frac{w_{h}(r)}{r}
with the endpoint value
\tilde{u}_{h}(0):=u_{h}(0)
.
Thus, we seek the local unknown
u_{h}\in P^{k}(I_{i})
that satisfies

(2.4)

\bigl((u_{h})_{t},\phi_{u}\bigr)_{I_{i}}+\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}\,\tilde{g}_{h}\,u_{h},(\phi_{u})_{r}\Bigr)_{I_{i}}-\Bigl\langle\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{u,h},\phi_{u}\,n\Bigr\rangle_{\partial I_{i}}+\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}\,(\tilde{g}_{h})_{r}\,\tilde{u}_{h},\phi_{u}\Bigr)_{I_{i}}=0\qquad\forall\phi_{u}\in P^{k}(I_{i}),

where, for
r>0
,
\tilde{g}_{h}(r):=\frac{z_{h}(r)}{r}
with the endpoint value
\tilde{g}_{h}(0):=g_{h}(0)
, and the numerical flux on
I_{i}
is

(2.5a)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{u,h}(r_{i})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\,\tilde{g}_{h}(r_{i})\,\widehat{u}_{h}(r_{i}),

(2.5b)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{u,h}(r_{i-1})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\,\tilde{g}_{h}(r_{i-1})\,u_{h}(r_{i-1}).

Consequently, we assemble criteria for the global trace unknowns
\widehat{u}_{h},\,\widehat{g}_{h}\in M_{h}^{R},\penalty\ \widehat{w}_{h},\,\widehat{z}_{h}\in M_{h}^{L}
from which
u_{h},w_{h},g_{h}
and
z_{h}
can be derived in an element local procedure via nodal transmission conditions

(2.6a)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{u,h}|_{I_{i}}(r_{i})

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{u,h}|_{I_{i+1}}(r_{i}),

(2.6b)

\displaystyle w_{h}|_{I_{i}}(r_{i})

\displaystyle=w_{h}|_{I_{i+1}}(r_{i}),

(2.6c)

\displaystyle z_{h}|_{I_{i}}(r_{i})

\displaystyle=z_{h}|_{I_{i+1}}(r_{i}),

(2.6d)

\displaystyle g_{h}|_{I_{i}}(r_{i})

\displaystyle=g_{h}|_{I_{i+1}}(r_{i}),

for
i=1,\dots,N-1
. The boundary data are

(2.7)

\widehat{u}_{h}(t,b)=U_{b}(t),\qquad\widehat{g}_{h}(t,b)=1,\qquad\widehat{w}_{h}(t,0)=0,\qquad\widehat{z}_{h}(t,0)=0.

2.3.
Local well–posedness

For a given
u_{h}\in V_{h}^{k}
, the transmission conditions
(
2.6
) and the boundary conditions
(
2.7
) determine the traces uniquely.
More precisely, the traces are given by the recursive formulas

(2.8a)

\displaystyle\widehat{w}_{h}(r_{i})

\displaystyle=w_{h}|_{I_{i+1}}(r_{i})=w_{h}|_{I_{i}}(r_{i})=\widehat{w}_{h}(r_{i-1})+\int_{r_{i-1}}^{r_{i}}u_{h}(s)\,ds,

(2.8b)

\displaystyle\widehat{g}_{h}(r_{i})

\displaystyle=g_{h}|_{I_{i}}(r_{i})=g_{h}|_{I_{i+1}}(r_{i})=\widehat{g}_{h}(r_{i+1})\exp\!\Bigl(-\int_{r_{i}}^{r_{i+1}}\frac{1}{s}\bigl(u_{h}(s)-\tilde{u}_{h}(s)\bigr)^{2}\,ds\Bigr),

(2.8c)

\displaystyle\widehat{z}_{h}(r_{i})

\displaystyle=z_{h}|_{I_{i+1}}(r_{i})=z_{h}|_{I_{i}}(r_{i})=\widehat{z}_{h}(r_{i-1})+\int_{r_{i-1}}^{r_{i}}g_{h}(s)\,ds,

(2.8d)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\,\tilde{g}_{h}(r_{i})\,\widehat{u}_{h}(r_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,\tilde{g}_{h}(r_{i})\,u_{h}|_{I_{i+1}}(r_{i})\Longrightarrow\widehat{u}_{h}(r_{i})=u_{h}|_{I_{i+1}}(r_{i}),

for
i=1,\dots,N-1
, where all chains are started by values in (
2.7
) and
\tilde{g}_{h}(r_{i})>0
from (
2.13
). Thus, in the present one-dimensional radial setting, the trace variables can be eliminated recursively once
u_{h}
is known, and the semidiscrete evolution
may be viewed as an ODE for the coefficients of
u_{h}
alone.

Theorem 2.1

(Local semidiscrete well-posedness)
.

Let
U_{b}\in C([0,T])
and let
u_{h}(0)\in V_{h}^{k}
be given.
Then there exists
T_{\ast}\in(0,T]
such that the scheme (
2.4
), (
2.6
), and
(
2.7
) admits a unique solution

u_{h}\in C^{1}([0,T_{\ast}];V_{h}^{k}).

The associated traces
\widehat{u}_{h},\;\widehat{g}_{h},\;\widehat{w}_{h},\;\widehat{z}_{h}
are uniquely determined by (
2.8
).

Proof.

Choose on each element
I_{i}
a basis
\{\varphi_{i,m}\}_{m=0}^{k}
of
P^{k}(I_{i})
, and write

u_{h}|_{I_{i}}(r,t)=\sum_{m=0}^{k}U_{i,m}(t)\,\varphi_{i,m}(r).

Let
U(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N(k+1)}
be the coefficient vector. For a given
U
, the traces are uniquely determined by (
2.8
). In particular,

\widehat{w}_{h}
and
w_{h}
depend linearly on
U
. On the first element
I_{1}=(0,r_{1})
,

w_{h}(r)=\int_{0}^{r}u_{h}(s)\,ds,

hence
w_{h}(r)=r\,p_{h}(r)
for a unique polynomial
p_{h}\in P^{k}(I_{1})
. Therefore
\tilde{u}_{h}(r)=p_{h}(r)\text{ on }I_{1},
and
u_{h}(r)-\tilde{u}_{h}(r)=r\,q_{h}(r)
for some polynomial
q_{h}\in P^{k-1}(I_{1})
, as
u_{h}(0)=\tilde{u}_{h}(0)
. It follows that

\frac{1}{r}\bigl(u_{h}-\tilde{u}_{h}\bigr)^{2}

extends continuously to
r=0
and depends polynomially on
U
. On the remaining elements,

r\geq r_{i-1}>0
, so there is no singularity. Hence, by (
2.3b
) and (
2.8b
), the maps

U\mapsto g_{h},\qquad U\mapsto\widehat{g}_{h}

are locally Lipschitz. The same is then true for

U\mapsto z_{h},\;U\mapsto\widehat{z}_{h}

by (
2.3c
) and (
2.8c
). For
i\geq 2
,

\tilde{g}_{h}=\frac{z_{h}}{r},\qquad(\tilde{g}_{h})_{r}=\frac{g_{h}-\tilde{g}_{h}}{r},

so
U\mapsto\tilde{g}_{h}
and
U\mapsto(\tilde{g}_{h})_{r}
are locally Lipschitz on
I_{i}
.
On
I_{1}
,

z_{h}(r)=\int_{0}^{r}g_{h}(s)\,ds,\qquad\tilde{g}_{h}(r)=\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}g_{h}(s)\,ds=\int_{0}^{1}g_{h}(\theta r)\,d\theta.

Moreover, differentiating (
2.3b
) gives

(g_{h})_{r}=\frac{1}{r}\,g_{h}\,(u_{h}-\tilde{u}_{h})^{2},

so
(g_{h})_{r}
is continuous on
I_{1}
, and therefore

(\tilde{g}_{h})_{r}(r)=\int_{0}^{1}\theta\,(g_{h})_{r}(\theta r)\,d\theta.

Thus
U\mapsto\tilde{g}_{h}
and
U\mapsto(\tilde{g}_{h})_{r}
are locally Lipschitz on
I_{1}
as well.
Finally,
\widehat{u}_{h}(b)=U_{b}(t)
, while
\widehat{u}_{h}(r_{i})=u_{h}|_{I_{i+1}}(r_{i})
at the interior nodes;
hence the only explicit time dependence enters through
U_{b}
, and the trace
\widehat{u}_{h}
is continuous
in
t
and locally Lipschitz in
U
.

Testing (
2.4

yields a finite-dimensional system

such that this ODE admits a unique solution

This defines a unique semidiscrete solution

and the associated traces are uniquely recovered from (

).
∎

Theorem 3.1

(

\displaystyle\Phi_{i}

Corollary 3.2

(Global well-posedness)
.

, the local

4.
Optimal error analysis

We assume that the exact solution of (

) satisfies

(4.1)

u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{k+1,\infty}(0,b))\quad\text{ and }\quad u_{t}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{k+1}(0,b)).

Then the exact quantities

,

, and

Remark 4.1
.

The regularity assumption imposed above is used only as an input for the numerical analysis on the fixed time interval
[0,T]
. In this setting, classical and generalized solution theories are available in the work
of Christodoulou
[
9

on

[
8

derives a priori estimates and global existence on

On each element

(\Pi u-u,v)_{I_{i}}=0\qquad\forall v\in P^{k-1}(I_{i}),\qquad(\Pi u)(r_{i-1})=u(r_{i-1}).

This is the Gauss–Radau projection; see, for example,

[
20

46

. We set

\eta:=u-\Pi u,\qquad\xi:=u_{h}-\Pi u,\qquad e:=u-u_{h}=\eta-\xi,

and

(4.4)

\tilde{e}:=\tilde{u}-\tilde{u}_{h},\qquad\delta_{g}:=g-g_{h},\qquad\widetilde{\delta}_{g}:=\tilde{g}-\tilde{g}_{h}.

We choose the initial data by

so that

. The standard approximation, inverse, and trace estimates yield

(4.5a)

\displaystyle\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}+h\|\eta_{r}\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\|\eta\|_{L^{\infty}(0,b)}+h\|\eta_{r}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,b)}+\|\eta_{t}\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{k+1},

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big|v_{h}|_{I_{i}}(r_{i-1})\big|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big|v_{h}|_{I_{i}}(r_{i})\big|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

, where

By (

Lemma 4.2

Assume (

(4.8a)

\displaystyle\|\tilde{e}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,b)}

\displaystyle\leq C\bigl(h^{k+1}+h^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\xi\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\bigr),

\displaystyle\|(\tilde{e})_{r}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,b)}

u_{h}(b)-U_{b}=\xi|_{I_{N}}(b)-\eta|_{I_{N}}(b).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\Bigl|u_{h}|_{I_{i}}(r_{i})-u_{h}|_{I_{i+1}}(r_{i})\Bigr|\,\Bigl|\xi|_{I_{i}}(r_{i})\Bigr|+|u_{h}(b)-U_{b}|\,\big|\xi|_{I_{N}}(b)\big|

\displaystyle\qquad\leq C\Biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big|\xi|_{I_{i}}(r_{i})\big|^{2}+\sum_{i=2}^{N}\big|\xi|_{I_{i}}(r_{i-1})\big|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big|\eta|_{I_{i}}(r_{i})\big|^{2}\Biggr)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq Ch^{-1}\bigl(\|\xi\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}^{2}+\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}^{2}\bigr)

by (
4.5b
) and (
4.5e
).
Since Lemma
4.2
gives

\|\widetilde{\delta}_{g}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,b)}\leq C\|e\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}
, and thus using (
4.5a
) and (
4.6
), we get

h^{-1}\|e\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\leq Ch^{k}+Ch^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq C

on
[0,T]
, the boundary part of
Q_{1}
is also bounded by

C(\|\xi\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}^{2}+\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}^{2})
. Thus

|Q_{1}|\leq C\bigl(\|\xi\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}^{2}+\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}^{2}\bigr).

For
Q_{2}
, by (
4.14
),

|Q_{2}|\leq C\|\tilde{e}\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\,\|\xi\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}+C\|(\widetilde{\delta}_{g})_{r}\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\,\|\xi\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}},

where we also used
\|\tilde{u}_{h}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,b)}\leq C
.
By Lemma
4.2
and (
4.9
),

\|\tilde{e}\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\leq C\|e\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}},\qquad\|(\widetilde{\delta}_{g})_{r}\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\leq C\|e\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}},

so

|Q_{2}|\leq C\|e\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\,\|\xi\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\leq C\bigl(\|\xi\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}^{2}+\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}^{2}\bigr).

This proves (
4.15
).
∎

Theorem 4.4

(Optimal error estimate)
.

Let
k\geq 1
.
Assume (
4.1
) and choose the initial data by
u_{h}(0)=\Pi u(0)
.
Then there exist
h_{0}>0
and
C>0
, independent of
h
, such that for all
0<h\leq h_{0}
,

(4.17)

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|u(t)-u_{h}(t)\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\leq Ch^{k+1}.

Proof.

From (
4.12
),

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Bigl((\xi_{t},\xi)_{I_{i}}+\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{g}\,\xi,\xi_{r})_{I_{i}}\Bigr)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\tilde{g}(r_{i})\,\xi|_{I_{i+1}}(r_{i})\,\xi|_{I_{i}}(r_{i})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{g}(r_{i-1})\,\xi|_{I_{i}}(r_{i-1})^{2}

(4.18)

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\eta_{t},\xi)_{I_{i}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\tilde{g}\,\eta,\xi_{r})_{I_{i}}+Q_{1}+Q_{2}.

On the left-hand side, an elementwise integration by parts gives

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Bigl((\xi_{t},\xi)_{I_{i}}+\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{g}\,\xi,\xi_{r})_{I_{i}}\Bigr)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\tilde{g}(r_{i})\,\xi|_{I_{i+1}}(r_{i})\,\xi|_{I_{i}}(r_{i})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{g}(r_{i-1})\,\xi|_{I_{i}}(r_{i-1})^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|\xi\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}(\tilde{g}_{r},\xi^{2})_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\frac{1}{4}\tilde{g}(0)\,\xi|_{I_{1}}(0)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\tilde{g}(r_{i})\Bigl(\xi|_{I_{i}}(r_{i})-\xi|_{I_{i+1}}(r_{i})\Bigr)^{2}

(4.19)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{4}\tilde{g}(b)\,\xi|_{I_{N}}(b)^{2}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Bigl((\xi_{t},\xi)_{I_{i}}+\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{g}\,\xi,\xi_{r})_{I_{i}}\Bigr)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\tilde{g}(r_{i})\,\xi|_{I_{i+1}}(r_{i})\,\xi|_{I_{i}}(r_{i})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{g}(r_{i-1})\,\xi|_{I_{i}}(r_{i-1})^{2}

(4.20)

\displaystyle\qquad\geq\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|\xi\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}^{2}-C\|\xi\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}^{2}.

Here we used the boundedness of
\tilde{g}_{r}
from (
4.1
) and the nonnegativity of
\tilde{g}
from Lemma
1.1
. The first term on the right-hand side of (
4.18
) can be estimated using (
4.1
) and (
4.5
) as follows

\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\eta_{t},\xi)_{I_{i}}\right|\leq\|\eta_{t}\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\,\|\xi\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\leq Ch^{k+1}\|\xi\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\leq Ch^{2k+2}+C\|\xi\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}^{2}.

For the second term, let
\bar{\tilde{g}}_{i}:=\frac{1}{h_{i}}\int_{I_{i}}\tilde{g}(r)\,dr\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
\bar{\tilde{g}}_{i}\,\xi_{r}\in P^{k-1}(I_{i})
, the orthogonality of the projection (
4.2
) gives
(\eta,\bar{\tilde{g}}_{i}\,\xi_{r})_{I_{i}}=0
. Hence

(\tilde{g}\,\eta,\xi_{r})_{I_{i}}=\bigl((\tilde{g}-\bar{\tilde{g}}_{i})\eta,\xi_{r}\bigr)_{I_{i}}.

Using
\|\tilde{g}-\bar{\tilde{g}}_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}(I_{i})}\leq Ch_{i}
and
(
4.5d
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\tilde{g}\,\eta,\xi_{r})_{I_{i}}\right|=\left|\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl((\tilde{g}-\bar{\tilde{g}}_{i})\eta,\xi_{r}\bigr)_{I_{i}}\right|\leq Ch\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\,\|\xi_{r}\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\leq Ch^{2k+2}+C\|\xi\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}^{2}.

Finally, Lemma
4.3
gives

|Q_{1}|+|Q_{2}|\leq C\bigl(\|\xi\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}^{2}+\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}^{2}\bigr)\leq C\|\xi\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}^{2}+Ch^{2k+2}.

Combining these bounds with (
4.18
) and
(
4
), we obtain

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|\xi\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}^{2}\leq C\|\xi\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}^{2}+Ch^{2k+2},\qquad 0\leq t\leq T.

Since
\xi(0)=0
, Grönwall’s inequality yields

\|\xi(t)\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\leq Ch^{k+1},\qquad 0\leq t\leq T.

Finally, the triangle inequality and the estimate (
4.5
) imply

\|u(t)-u_{h}(t)\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\leq Ch^{k+1},

for all
t\leq T
. This completes the proof.
∎

We now justify the bootstrap assumption (
4.6
).
By Corollary
3.2
, we have

u_{h}\in C^{1}([0,T];V_{h}^{k})
, and hence the map

t\mapsto\|\xi(t)\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}
is continuous.
The estimate proved above gives

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|\xi(t)\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\leq Ch^{k+1}.

Since
k\geq 1
, we have
k+1>\frac{3}{2}
, and therefore

Ch^{k+1}\leq\frac{1}{2}h^{\frac{3}{2}}

for all sufficiently small
h
.
Thus, the estimate improves the bootstrap assumption.
The standard continuity argument then shows that (
4.6
)
is valid on the whole interval
[0,T]
.

Corollary 4.5

(Errors of the reconstructed variables)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
4.4
,

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\Bigl(\|w(t)-w_{h}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(0,b)}

\displaystyle+\|z(t)-z_{h}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(0,b)}+\|g(t)-g_{h}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(0,b)}

(4.21)

\displaystyle\qquad+\|\tilde{g}(t)-\tilde{g}_{h}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(0,b)}+\|\tilde{u}(t)-\tilde{u}_{h}(t)\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\Bigr)\leq Ch^{k+1}.

Proof.

By (
2.12
) and the exact reconstruction formulas,

w(r)-w_{h}(r)=\int_{0}^{r}\bigl(u(s)-u_{h}(s)\bigr)\,ds,\qquad z(r)-z_{h}(r)=\int_{0}^{r}\bigl(g(s)-g_{h}(s)\bigr)\,ds.

Hence

\|w-w_{h}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,b)}\leq C\|u-u_{h}\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}},\qquad\|z-z_{h}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,b)}\leq C\|g-g_{h}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,b)}.

Moreover, Lemma
4.2
gives

\|g-g_{h}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,b)}+\|\tilde{g}-\tilde{g}_{h}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,b)}\leq C\|u-u_{h}\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}.

The averaging operator bound (
4.9
) also yields

\|\tilde{u}-\tilde{u}_{h}\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\leq C\|u-u_{h}\|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}.

Combining these estimates with Theorem
4.4
proves (
4.5
).
∎

Corollary 4.6

(Error estimate for the Bondi mass)
.

Define the mass aspect by

(4.22)

\mathcal{M}(t,r):=\frac{r}{2}\left(1-\frac{\tilde{g}(t,r)}{g(t,r)}\right),\qquad m_{h}(t,r):=\frac{r}{2}\left(1-\frac{\tilde{g}_{h}(t,r)}{g_{h}(t,r)}\right),

and define the Bondi masses

M(t):=\mathcal{M}(t,b)
and
M_{h}(t):=m_{h}(t,b).

Under the assumptions of Theorem
4.4
, there exists a constant

C>0
, independent of
h
, such that

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|M(t)-M_{h}(t)|\leq Ch^{k+1}.

Proof.

Since the outer boundary condition gives

g(t,b)=1
and
g_{h}(t,b)=1
, we have

M(t)=\frac{b}{2}\bigl(1-\tilde{g}(t,b)\bigr),\qquad M_{h}(t)=\frac{b}{2}\bigl(1-\tilde{g}_{h}(t,b)\bigr).

Therefore

|M(t)-M_{h}(t)|=\frac{b}{2}\,|\tilde{g}(t,b)-\tilde{g}_{h}(t,b)|.

The result now follows from Corollary
4.5
.
∎

5.
Numerical experiments

In this section, we present numerical experiments for the HDG method (
2.4
)-(
2.7
).
The aims are twofold. First, we verify the optimal order of convergence proved by Theorem
4.4
and Corollary
4.5
. Second, we show that the method captures the main geometric features of the Einstein–scalar system (
1.8
), in particular, the collapse toward black-hole-type profiles. The first two tests below are adapted from the benchmark problems in
[
8
]
.

All computations are performed on uniform meshes of the interval
[0,b]
. Since the HDG scheme in this paper is semidiscrete, we discretize the resulting ODE system in time using the classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta method
[
21
,
26
,
49
]
. In the convergence test, the time step is chosen sufficiently small, for example
\Delta t=0.01
, so that the temporal error is negligible compared with the spatial error.

A useful feature of the present HDG formulation is that the variables
w_{h}
,
g_{h}
,
z_{h}
,
\tilde{u}_{h}
, and
\tilde{g}_{h}
are reconstructed from
u_{h}
through the discrete constraint relations. Therefore, at each Runge–Kutta stage, only the coefficients of
u_{h}
are advanced in time, while the remaining quantities are recovered by elementwise integration and recursion. In particular, the method does not require a separate global evolution for the metric variables.
This considerably reduces the implementation cost and is one of the main advantages of the present HDG formulation.

5.1.
Example 1

(Accuracy test and large-data collapse benchmark; cf.
[
8
, Ex. 1]
).

We take

b=10,\qquad\tilde{u}_{0}(r)=0.45\tanh(3(r-5)),

and define the initial data for the evolution variable by

u_{0}(r)=(r\tilde{u}_{0}(r))_{r}.

The outer boundary data are chosen as

U_{b}(t)=u_{0}(b).

This is a large data set, and it leads to a rapid transition to the formation of a black-hole region
[
8
,
11
]
.
The profile of
\tilde{u}_{0}
is steep near
r=5
, which produces a strong inward concentration
of the scalar field during the evolution.

To test the spatial accuracy of the method, we compute the solution up to the final time
T=0.5
.
Since no explicit exact solution is available, we use a reference solution computed on a very
fine mesh, for example, with
N_{\mathrm{ref}}=6400
, together with a sufficiently accurate polynomial approximation and a very small time step, see
[
8
]
.
We consider
P^{k}
elements for
1\leq k\leq 5
.
The errors are measured by

E_{u}=\|u_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot,T)-u_{h}(\cdot,T)\|_{L^{2}(0,b)},\qquad E_{g}=\|g_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot,T)-g_{h}(\cdot,T)\|_{L^{2}(0,b)},

where
u_{\mathrm{ref}}
and
g_{\mathrm{ref}}
are reference solutions computed at
N_{\mathrm{ref}}
. The results in Figure
1
show the expected order
k+1
for
u_{h}
. For the reconstructed metric variable
g_{h}
, the observed slope is close to
k+2
, which is better than the bound proved in Corollary
4.5
. At present, this higher rate should be
viewed as an observed superconvergent effect of the reconstruction rather than as a rigorously established theorem. A plausible explanation is that
g_{h}
is obtained through the nonlinear integral reconstruction (
2.3b
),
which may exhibit additional cancellations on uniform meshes in the smooth regime. We therefore interpret this behavior as an observed higher-order effect of the metric reconstruction.

To interpret the long-time behavior, we compare the numerical profiles with the
continuous asymptotic picture described in
[
8
,
11
]
.
In particular, if the continuous solution has a nonzero final mass

M_{\infty}:=\lim_{t\to\infty}M(t)
, then one expects convergence toward a black-hole end state with a horizon radius
2M_{\infty}
. In the computations, we monitor the discrete finite-radius mass proxy
M_{h}(t)
defined in (
4.22
), which serves as the numerical counterpart of this quantity.
In the normalization
g(t,b)=1
, this corresponds formally to the limiting profile; see
[
11
]
:

g(t,r)\to g_{\infty}(r)=\begin{cases}0,&0\leq r<2M_{\infty},\\[3.00003pt]
1,&2M_{\infty}\leq r\leq b,\end{cases}\qquad\text{ as }t\to\infty.

While
\tilde{g}
is the radial average of
g

\tilde{g}_{\infty}(r)=\begin{cases}0,&0\leq r\leq 2M_{\infty},\\[3.00003pt]
1-\dfrac{2M_{\infty}}{r},&2M_{\infty}<r\leq b.\end{cases}

Hence
g
is expected to approach a step profile, whereas
\tilde{g}
remains continuous and develops a kink at
r=2M_{\infty}
as
t\to\infty
. This is consistent with the numerical results given in Figure
2
, where we plot the profiles of
\tilde{u}_{h}
,
\tilde{g}_{h}
, and
g_{h}
at several representative times.

\tilde{u}_{h}

6.
Conclusion

In this paper, we developed a hybridizable discontinuous Galerkin method for the spherically symmetric Einstein–scalar system in Bondi gauge. This method has a clear computational structure. The globally coupled unknowns are reduced to trace variables on the mesh skeleton, while the element variables are reconstructed locally. We proved local well-posedness of the semidiscrete scheme and derived a global
L^{2}
-stability estimate. We then established an optimal order error bound for the main evolution variable for polynomial degree
k\geq 1
. The analysis further yielded error estimates for the reconstructed variables
w_{h}
,
g_{h}
,
z_{h}
,
\tilde{u}_{h}
, and
\tilde{g}_{h}
, as well as an error estimate for the Bondi mass. These results show that the proposed HDG method is not only stable and convergent, but also accurate for the geometrically relevant quantities of the Einstein–scalar system. The numerical experiments support the theory and illustrate the practical behavior of the method.

The present work provides the first HDG analysis for this Bondi-gauge Einstein–scalar model and shows that HDG offers a natural alternative to standard DG methods in this setting. Several extensions remain of interest. These include a fully discrete error analysis and a mathematical proof of the observed superconvergence of the reconstructed metric variable.
