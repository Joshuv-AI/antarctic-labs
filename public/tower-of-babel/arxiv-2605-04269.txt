Adapt or Forget: Provable Tradeoffs Between Adam and SGD in Nonstationary Optimization
Sharan Sahu, Abir Sarkar, Cameron J. Hogan, Martin T. Wells

Abstract
We provide a theoretical analysis of Adam under non-stationary stochastic objectives, separating two regimes: Euclidean tracking under adaptive strong monotonicity of the Adam-preconditioned mean-gradient operator, and high-probability projected stationarity guarantees under general L L -smooth objectives. In the tracking regime, we derive finite-time expected and high-probability bounds that decompose sharply into four components: initialization, objective drift, a first-moment tracking error governed by β 1 \beta_{1} , and a preconditioner perturbation governed by β 2 \beta_{2} . We characterize the burn-in time to reach Adam’s irreducible tracking floor under constant and step-decay schedules. We also prove a high-probability bound on the average projected stationarity gap for Adam under distribution shift. Across both analyses, our bounds reveal a noise–drift tradeoff: in noise-dominated regimes, first-moment averaging and adaptive preconditioning can improve the high-probability error, whereas in drift-dominated regimes, stale first-moment information and preconditioner perturbations can compound the cost of nonstationarity, allowing vanilla SGD to achieve a smaller tracking floor. Our explicit ( β 1 , β 2 , ϵ ) (\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\epsilon) -dependent bounds delineate when adaptive step-sizing is beneficial versus harmful, and provide a theoretical mechanism for Adam’s empirical instability and stabilization under distribution shift.

Adaptive gradient methods such as Adam

Since Adam incorporates momentum in both its first- and second-moment estimates, it is natural to ask if similar degradation occurs, and whether the adaptive preconditioning compounds or alleviates it. These failure modes have indeed been observed empirically in continual learning and non-stationary optimization

These observations motivate the following central question in the non-stationary tracking setting:

When does Adam’s adaptive preconditioning help under distribution shift, and when can its moment estimates provably hurt relative to vanilla SGD, for
L
-smooth time-varying functions
G_{t}(\bm{\theta})
?

Our contributions.
We answer this question under predictable distribution shift and make the following advances.

1.

Finite-time tracking bounds under adaptive strong monotonicity.
We prove finite-time tracking bounds for Adam that separate four distinct error sources: initialization decay, drift-induced tracking lag, a first-moment tracking error governed by
\beta_{1}
, and a preconditioner perturbation governed by
\beta_{2}
. To obtain a tracking recursion, we impose strong monotonicity on the mean-gradient map after Adam-style preconditioning. Since the realized Adam preconditioner is computed from the fresh sample
X_{t+1}
at the same step, we formulate this condition using a predictable proxy rather than the sample-dependent preconditioner itself. This yields a population-level contraction condition, while the difference between the realized preconditioner and its predictable proxy is controlled explicitly as an Adam-specific perturbation term.

2.

A noise–drift tradeoff separating Adam from SGD.

Our explicit
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\epsilon)
-dependent bounds reveal a sharper optimizer-specific noise–drift tradeoff than SGD and take a different form than in SGDM. In noise-dominated regimes, first-moment averaging and adaptive preconditioning can reduce the high-probability tracking floor, and our bounds identify parameter regimes in which Adam can improve over SGD, consistent with our numerical simulations. In drift-dominated regimes, however, the
\beta_{1}
-induced memory bias the
\beta_{2}
-induced preconditioner perturbation can compound the cost of nonstationarity, allowing vanilla SGD to achieve a smaller tracking floor. The bounds also give a theoretical mechanism for the empirical observation of
[
25
]
that increasing
\epsilon
stabilizes Adam under task changes: larger
\epsilon
dampens adaptive second-moment variability at the cost of slower adaptation to drift.

3.

Projected stationarity bounds under general Adam preconditioners.
Without the adaptive monotonicity condition, we prove a high-probability bound on the average projected stationarity gap for Adam under distribution shift. This is the natural constrained analogue of a preconditioned gradient-norm guarantee and reduces to one when the projection is inactive. The same Adam-specific error structure persists: objective drift, first-moment bias-variance tradeoffs, and second-moment preconditioner perturbation all contribute explicitly, showing that the qualitative picture identified in the tracking regime extends to general Adam preconditioners.

4.

Experiments corroborating the noise–drift tradeoff.
We validate the theory on strongly convex least squares and three non-convex online problems: teacher–student MLP regression, phase retrieval, and matrix factorization under controlled drift and time-varying noise. Across these settings, the results support the predicted tradeoff: SGD performs better when drift dominates because it adapts more quickly, whereas Adam is more effective when stochastic noise dominates due to momentum and adaptive scaling.

3.1
Tracking under adaptive strong monotonicity

In this section, we obtain high-probability guarantees on the tracking error

\|\bm{\theta}_{t}-\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}\|
, deferring
expectation bounds to
Section

C.3
. Unlike

34
]

(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}g(\bm{\theta}_{t},X_{t+1}))^{\odot 2}

(Adaptive strong monotonicity)

3.1

\beta_{2}
controls a transient–floor tradeoff as large
\beta_{2}
is beneficial over long, stable training horizons, while making Adam potentially worse than SGD over short or rapidly shifting horizons with high probability.

Our bounds also reveal a tradeoff governed by
\epsilon
. Since
q_{-}=(G+\epsilon)^{-1}
and
q_{+}=\epsilon^{-1}
, the contraction factor scales as
\rho=1-\mathcal{O}(\alpha\mu/\epsilon)
for large
\epsilon

\epsilon
slows contraction and enlarges the drift contribution as
\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{2}\epsilon^{2}/(\alpha^{2}\mu^{2}))
. At the same time, it reduces the adaptive preconditioner penalties, which scale as
\mathcal{O}(1)
and
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2})
, respectively, up to problem-dependent constants. Therefore, in regimes where the adaptive second-moment terms dominate, increasing
\epsilon
can improve the high-probability tracking bound, whereas in drift dominated regimes it can worsen tracking by slowing adaptation to the moving minimizer. This provides a theoretical mechanism for the empirical observation of
[
25
]
that increasing
\epsilon
stabilizes Adam under nonstationarity by damping variability introduced by the adaptive second-moment process.

Consequently, after a burn-in period under either a constant or epoch-wise step-decay schedule, Adam reaches an irreducible floor

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{A}}^{\star}
with five interpretable components: a drift term scaling as
\Delta^{2}/(q_{-}^{2}\mu^{2}\alpha^{2})
, a first-moment memory bias term scaling as
q_{+}^{2}\beta_{1}^{2}D_{1}^{2}/(q_{-}^{2}\mu^{2}(1-\beta_{1})^{2})
, a sub-Gaussian first-moment variance term scaling as

q_{+}^{2}d\sigma^{2}\log(2T/\delta)/(q_{-}^{2}\mu^{2})
, a bounded-increment Bernstein term scaling as
q_{+}^{2}G^{2}\log^{2}(2T/\delta)/(q_{-}^{2}\mu^{2})
, and a
preconditioner perturbation term scaling as

G^{4}q_{+}^{4}(1-\beta_{2})/(q_{-}^{2}\mu^{2})
.

6
Conclusion

To our knowledge, we provide the first finite-time theoretical analysis of Adam under non-stationary stochastic objectives, characterizing how its moment estimates interact with distribution shift. Our bounds decompose the tracking error into four interpretable components (initialization, objective drift, first-moment memory bias, and preconditioner perturbation) and reveal a fundamental noise–drift tradeoff: Adam’s adaptive preconditioning reduces error when gradient noise dominates, while vanilla SGD can outperform Adam when drift dominates and moment estimates become stale. The explicit
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\epsilon)
-dependence of our bounds provides a theoretical explanation for the empirical instability of Adam under distribution shift, including the stabilizing effect of increasing
\epsilon
under task changes, and our experimental results corroborate the predicted tradeoffs across synthetic nonstationary settings. Two directions stand out for future work. First, a limitation of our analysis is the bounded-gradient assumption: relaxing this condition would require a fundamentally different treatment of the second-moment process, as boundedness is used to control the preconditioner perturbation terms pathwise in a way that avoids the optional stopping arguments of
[
34
]
. Second, establishing minimax lower bounds for Adam under nonstationarity would identify the best achievable tracking rates and determine whether the drift, noise, and
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\epsilon)
dependences in our upper bounds are minimax optimal; the lower bounds of
[
34
]
for SGD and SGDM rely on strongly convex constructions, and extending these to the general
L
-smooth setting considered here would require a different approach.

Related work

Adam in stationary settings.

Since its introduction
[
18
]
, Adam has been the subject of extensive theoretical study in stationary settings. Early work showed that Adam can fail to converge even in simple convex problems due to the exponential moving average of squared gradients, motivating the AMSGrad variant
[

[
5

provide a clean proof for smooth nonconvex objectives with bounded gradients, obtaining the tightest known dependence on
\beta_{1}

[
23

prove convergence to
\epsilon

[
13

extend this to affine variance noise models, showing that Adam is free to tune step sizes without knowledge of problem parameters. Most recently,
[
38
]
propose ADOPT, a modification of Adam that achieves the optimal
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{T})
nonconvex rate with any choice of
\beta_{2}
without relying on bounded noise, by removing the current gradient from the second-moment estimate. The adaptivity of Adam relative to SGD has also been studied, with work identifying heavy-tailed gradient distributions and ill-conditioned objectives as settings where Adam’s coordinate-wise preconditioning yields provable advantages

[
21
,
42

20
]
. Complementary recent work by
[
15

gives a high-probability separation between Adam and SGD for stationary stochastic optimization under bounded-variance noise, showing that Adam’s second-moment normalization can yield sharper confidence dependence than SGD through a stopping-time martingale analysis. All of these results assume a fixed stationary objective and none address the behavior of the preconditioner under distribution shift.

SGD and momentum in non-stationary settings.

A complementary line of work studies optimization under time-varying objectives and distribution shift. The foundational dynamic regret framework of
[
44
]
measures performance against a drifting comparator in online convex optimization, and has inspired a large body of follow-up work on efficient non-stationary online learning
[
43
]
. In stationary settings with constant step sizes, momentum methods are essentially equivalent at steady state to SGD with a rescaled learning rate and do not reduce the MSE floor
[
41
,
19
]

[
2

derive dynamic regret bounds for online SGD under time-varying distributions, while
[
4

establish non-asymptotic tracking guarantees for proximal stochastic gradient methods under time drift.

[

Nonconvex tracking guarantees under distribution shift remain scarce even for SGD, and our work is the first to establish such guarantees for Adam. Our work extends this line of inquiry to Adam, where the adaptive preconditioner introduces an additional and qualitatively distinct source of non-stationary error.

Adam and adaptive methods under non-stationarity.

The failure of Adam under distribution shift has been documented empirically in several settings.
[
25

study plasticity loss in neural networks under non-stationary objectives and find that Adam’s moment estimates degrade under task changes, recommending increased

Despite this empirical evidence, no prior work provides a theoretical framework that precisely characterizes when and why Adam degrades under distribution shift, how its

(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}g(\bm{\theta}_{t},X_{t+1}))^{\odot 2}=\bm{s}_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t})+\bm{\chi}_{t+1}
, and
\mathcal{P}_{\Theta}
is the projection operator onto the set
\Theta
.

The following three assumptions hold throughout all analyses:

Assumption B.2

(Uniform
L
-Lipschitz continuity)
.

There exists
L>0
such that for all
t\geq 0
and all

\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\in\Theta
,

\|\bar{\bm{g}}_{t+1}(\bm{\theta})-\bar{\bm{g}}_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})\|\;\leq\;L\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\|.

Assumption B.3

(Uniformly bounded sample gradients)
.

There exists
G>0
such that, almost surely, for all
t\geq 0
,

\sup_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}g(\bm{\theta},X_{t+1})\|\leq G.

Assumption B.4

(Conditional sub-Gaussian gradient noise along iterates)
.

There exists a constant
\sigma>0
such that for all
t\geq 0
,
\big\|\bm{\xi}_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t})\big\|_{\Psi_{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}}\leq\sigma\;\;\text{a.s.}

Bias-correction weights and constants.

For
i\in\{1,2\}
and
0\leq k\leq t-1
, define the bias-correction
weights
w_{i,t,k}:=(1-\beta_{i})\beta_{i}^{t-1-k}/(1-\beta_{i}^{t})
,
which satisfy
\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}w_{i,t,k}=1
. The following
constants derived from these weights appear throughout the bounds and
are collected here for reference:

\displaystyle\kappa_{1,t}

\displaystyle:=\frac{(1-\beta_{1})(1+\beta_{1}^{t})}{(1+\beta_{1})(1-\beta_{1}^{t})},

\displaystyle\qquad c_{1,t}

\displaystyle:=\frac{\beta_{1}}{1-\beta_{1}}-\frac{t\beta_{1}^{t}}{1-\beta_{1}^{t}},

(B.1)

\displaystyle\omega_{1,t}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1-\beta_{1}}{1-\beta_{1}^{t}},

\displaystyle\qquad\vartheta_{2,t}

\displaystyle:=\beta_{2}^{t}+(1-\beta_{2}),

\displaystyle q_{-}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{G+\epsilon},\quad q_{+}:=\frac{1}{\epsilon},

\displaystyle\qquad D_{1}

\displaystyle:=\frac{\alpha LG}{\epsilon}+2G.

Here
\kappa_{1,t}
is the sum of squared weights governing the
variance of the first-moment average,
c_{1,t}
is a lag-weighted sum
controlling the first-moment bias,
\omega_{1,t}
is the maximum weight
controlling the Bernstein tail,
\vartheta_{2,t}
captures the
transient bias of the bias-corrected second moment, and
q_{-},q_{+}
are
the almost-sure preconditioner bounds implied by

B.3
.

Preconditioner bounds.

Under
B.3
, the bias-corrected
representations of
\widehat{\bm{m}}_{t}
and

\widehat{\bm{v}}_{t}
satisfy
\|\widehat{\bm{m}}_{t}\|\leq G
and
\|\widehat{\bm{v}}_{t}\|_{\infty}\leq G^{2}
, which gives

q_{-}\bm{I}\preceq\bm{P}_{t}\preceq q_{+}\bm{I},\qquad q_{-}:=\frac{1}{G+\epsilon},\qquad q_{+}:=\frac{1}{\epsilon}.

(B.2)

Finally, consecutive iterates satisfy
\|\bm{\theta}_{t}-\bm{\theta}_{t-1}\|\leq\alpha q_{+}G=:S
, and we define

D_{1}:=LS+2G
for use throughout the proofs.

Appendix C
Proofs for tracking under adaptive strong monotonicity

For this section, we will assume the preconditioned conditional mean gradient map
\bm{\theta}\mapsto\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}\bar{\bm{g}}_{t+1}(\bm{\theta})
is
q_{-}\mu
-strongly monotone.

Assumption C.1

(Adaptive strong monotonicity)
.

Let
q_{-}=(G+\epsilon)^{-1}
. There exists
0<\mu<\infty
such that,
almost surely, for all
t\geq 0
and all

\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\left\langle\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{\prime},\,\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}\left(\bar{\bm{g}}_{t+1}(\bm{\theta})-\bar{\bm{g}}_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})\right)\right\rangle\geq q_{-}\mu\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\|^{2}.

(C.1)

C.1
Proofs for
Theorem

3.1

We first introduce the predictable preconditioner proxy used throughout the Adam analysis. The realized Adam preconditioner
\bm{P}_{t+1}
is computed from the fresh sample
X_{t+1}
, and is therefore

\mathcal{F}_{t+1}
-measurable rather than
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable. Since our tracking argument imposes contraction on a predictable preconditioned mean-gradient map, we separate the predictable geometry from the random second-moment fluctuation. To do so, we construct a proxy by replacing the uncorrected second moment with its conditional expectation:

\widetilde{\bm{v}}_{t+1}:=\mathbb{E}[\bm{v}_{t+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}],\qquad\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}:=\operatorname{Diag}\!\left((\sqrt{\widetilde{\bm{v}}_{t+1}}+\epsilon)^{-1}\right).

(C.2)

Since

\bm{v}_{t+1}=\beta_{2}\bm{v}_{t}+(1-\beta_{2})\bigl(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}g(\bm{\theta}_{t},X_{t+1})\bigr)^{\odot 2},

taking conditional expectation given
\mathcal{F}_{t}
gives the explicit
predictable representation

\widetilde{\bm{v}}_{t+1}=\beta_{2}\bm{v}_{t}+(1-\beta_{2})\bm{s}_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t}).

(C.3)

Thus both
\widetilde{\bm{v}}_{t+1}
and

\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}
are
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable. Moreover,
by the bounded-gradient assumption and the definition of
q_{-}
and
q_{+}
,

q_{-}\bm{I}\preceq\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}\preceq q_{+}\bm{I}.

(C.4)

We next rewrite the Adam update around this predictable proxy. Define the
first-moment tracking error and the preconditioner perturbation by

\bm{r}_{t+1}:=\widehat{\bm{m}}_{t+1}-\bar{\bm{g}}_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t}),\qquad\bm{\eta}_{t+1}:=(\bm{P}_{t+1}-\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1})\widehat{\bm{m}}_{t+1}.

(C.5)

Then

\bm{P}_{t+1}\widehat{\bm{m}}_{t+1}=\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}\bar{\bm{g}}_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t})+\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}\bm{r}_{t+1}+\bm{\eta}_{t+1}.

Substituting this identity into the unlagged Adam update yields the predictable
decomposition

\bm{\theta}_{t+1}=\bm{\theta}_{t}-\alpha\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}\bar{\bm{g}}_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t})-\alpha\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}\bm{r}_{t+1}-\alpha\bm{\eta}_{t+1}.

(C.6)

We will analyze the exact Adam update through this decomposition: the first term is the predictable preconditioned mean-gradient step,
\bm{r}_{t+1}
measures the error from using Adam’s first-moment estimate instead of the current conditional mean gradient, and
\bm{\eta}_{t+1}
captures the price of replacing the random preconditioner by its predictable proxy.

The next lemma shows that this last perturbation term is deterministically controlled by the second-moment transient
\vartheta_{2,t+1}
. This is useful because it lets us treat the non-predictability of
\bm{P}_{t+1}
as an explicit additive error in the tracking recursion, rather than requiring us to rely on an optional-stopping argument as in
[
34
]
.

Lemma C.1

(Deterministic control of the preconditioner perturbation)
.

For every
t\geq 0
,

\|\bm{P}_{t+1}-\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}\|_{\operatorname{op}}^{2}\leq\epsilon^{-4}G^{2}\vartheta_{2,t+1},

(C.7)

and hence

\|\bm{\eta}_{t+1}\|^{2}\leq G^{4}\epsilon^{-4}\vartheta_{2,t+1}.

(C.8)

Proof of
Lemma

C.1
.

Since both
\bm{P}_{t+1}
and

\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}
are diagonal, it
suffices to compare their coordinatewise entries. For any
a,b\geq 0
,

\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}+\epsilon}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{b}+\epsilon}\right|\leq\epsilon^{-2}|\sqrt{a}-\sqrt{b}|\leq\epsilon^{-2}\sqrt{|a-b|}.

Applying this coordinatewise gives

\|\bm{P}_{t+1}-\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}\|_{\operatorname{op}}^{2}\leq\epsilon^{-4}\|\widehat{\bm{v}}_{t+1}-\widetilde{\bm{v}}_{t+1}\|_{\infty}.

(C.9)

It remains to bound the discrepancy between the bias-corrected second moment

\widehat{\bm{v}}_{t+1}
and its predictable proxy

\widetilde{\bm{v}}_{t+1}
. Since

\widehat{\bm{v}}_{t+1}=\bm{v}_{t+1}/(1-\beta_{2}^{t+1})
,
we can write

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{v}}_{t+1}-\widetilde{\bm{v}}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta_{2}^{t+1}}{1-\beta_{2}^{t+1}}\bm{v}_{t+1}+\bm{v}_{t+1}-\widetilde{\bm{v}}_{t+1}.

(C.10)

The first term is the transient bias-correction error. Since

\bm{v}_{0}=\bm{0}
and

\bm{v}_{t+1}=(1-\beta_{2})\sum_{j=0}^{t}\beta_{2}^{t-j}\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}g(\bm{\theta}_{j},X_{j+1})\right)^{\odot 2},

the bounded-gradient assumption gives the sharper bound

\|\bm{v}_{t+1}\|_{\infty}\leq(1-\beta_{2})\sum_{j=0}^{t}\beta_{2}^{t-j}G^{2}=G^{2}(1-\beta_{2}^{t+1}).

Consequently,

\left\|\frac{\beta_{2}^{t+1}}{1-\beta_{2}^{t+1}}\bm{v}_{t+1}\right\|_{\infty}\leq G^{2}\beta_{2}^{t+1}.

For the second term, using the definition of
\widetilde{\bm{v}}_{t+1}
,

\bm{v}_{t+1}-\widetilde{\bm{v}}_{t+1}=(1-\beta_{2})\bm{\chi}_{t+1}.

Also, each coordinate of

(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}g(\bm{\theta}_{t},X_{t+1}))^{\odot 2}

and
\bm{s}_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t})
lies in
[0,G^{2}]
, so

\|\bm{\chi}_{t+1}\|_{\infty}\leq G^{2}
. Therefore, defining

\vartheta_{2,t+1}:=\beta_{2}^{t+1}+(1-\beta_{2}),

we obtain

\|\widehat{\bm{v}}_{t+1}-\widetilde{\bm{v}}_{t+1}\|_{\infty}\leq G^{2}\vartheta_{2,t+1}.

(C.11)

Combining
Eq.

C.9
and

Eq.

C.11
yields

\|\bm{P}_{t+1}-\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}\|_{\operatorname{op}}^{2}\leq G^{2}\epsilon^{-4}\vartheta_{2,t+1}.

Finally, by definition

\bm{\eta}_{t+1}=(\bm{P}_{t+1}-\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1})\widehat{\bm{m}}_{t+1}
, and

\|\widehat{\bm{m}}_{t+1}\|\leq G
. Hence

\|\bm{\eta}_{t+1}\|^{2}\leq\|\bm{P}_{t+1}-\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}\|_{\operatorname{op}}^{2}\|\widehat{\bm{m}}_{t+1}\|^{2}\leq G^{4}\epsilon^{-4}\vartheta_{2,t+1},

which proves the claim.
∎

We now use the predictable decomposition in
Eq.

C.6
to derive the one-step tracking recursion. This proof follows similarly to
[
34
,
3
]
.

Lemma C.2

(One-step recursive relation)
.

Assume
C.1
and suppose

\alpha\leq\min\left\{\frac{q_{-}\mu}{4q_{+}^{2}L^{2}},\frac{1}{q_{-}\mu}\right\}.

Then, for every
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\|\bm{\theta}_{t+1}-\bm{\theta}_{t+1}^{\star}\|^{2}\leq\;

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\alpha q_{-}\mu\right)\|\bm{\theta}_{t}-\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}\|^{2}+\frac{5}{\alpha q_{-}\mu}\|\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}-\bm{\theta}_{t+1}^{\star}\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{10\alpha q_{+}^{2}}{q_{-}\mu}\|\bm{r}_{t+1}\|^{2}+\frac{10\alpha}{q_{-}\mu}\|\bm{\eta}_{t+1}\|^{2}.

(C.12)

Proof of
Lemma

C.2
.

Define the pre-projection point
\bm{z}_{t+1}:=\bm{\theta}_{t}-\alpha\bm{P}_{t+1}\widehat{\bm{m}}_{t+1}
. By the predictable decomposition in
Eq.

C.6
, we have

\bm{z}_{t+1}=\bm{\theta}_{t}-\alpha\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}\bar{\bm{g}}_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t})-\alpha\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}\bm{r}_{t+1}-\alpha\bm{\eta}_{t+1}.

Since
\bm{\theta}_{t+1}=\mathcal{P}_{\Theta}(\bm{z}_{t+1})
and
\bm{\theta}_{t+1}^{\star}\in\Theta
, nonexpansiveness of the Euclidean projection (
Lemma

E.1
) gives

\displaystyle\|\bm{\theta}_{t+1}-\bm{\theta}_{t+1}^{\star}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\mathcal{P}_{\Theta}(\bm{z}_{t+1})-\mathcal{P}_{\Theta}(\bm{\theta}_{t+1}^{\star})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\bm{z}_{t+1}-\bm{\theta}_{t+1}^{\star}\|^{2}.

Subtracting
\bm{\theta}_{t+1}^{\star}
from the expression for

\bm{z}_{t+1}
gives

\bm{z}_{t+1}-\bm{\theta}_{t+1}^{\star}=\bm{d}_{t}-\alpha\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}\bar{\bm{g}}_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t})-\alpha\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}\bm{r}_{t+1}-\alpha\bm{\eta}_{t+1}.

Consider
\bm{u}_{t}:=\bm{d}_{t}-\alpha\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}\bar{\bm{g}}_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t})
. Then

\bm{z}_{t+1}-\bm{\theta}_{t+1}^{\star}=\bm{u}_{t}-\alpha\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}\bm{r}_{t+1}-\alpha\bm{\eta}_{t+1}.

Since

\bar{\bm{g}}_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t+1}^{\star})=\bm{0}
,
adaptive strong monotonicity (
C.1
) gives

\left\langle\bm{d}_{t},\,\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}\bar{\bm{g}}_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t})\right\rangle=\left\langle\bm{\theta}_{t}-\bm{\theta}_{t+1}^{\star},\,\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}\left(\bar{\bm{g}}_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t})-\bar{\bm{g}}_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t+1}^{\star})\right)\right\rangle\geq q_{-}\mu\|\bm{d}_{t}\|^{2}.

(C.13)

Moreover, by
L
-Lipschitz continuity of

\bar{\bm{g}}_{t+1}
and

\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}\preceq q_{+}\bm{I}
,

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}\bar{\bm{g}}_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq q_{+}^{2}\|\bar{\bm{g}}_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=q_{+}^{2}\|\bar{\bm{g}}_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t})-\bar{\bm{g}}_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t+1}^{\star})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq q_{+}^{2}L^{2}\|\bm{d}_{t}\|^{2}.

(C.14)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\|\bm{u}_{t}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\bm{d}_{t}\|^{2}-2\alpha\left\langle\bm{d}_{t},\,\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}\bar{\bm{g}}_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t})\right\rangle+\alpha^{2}\|\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}\bar{\bm{g}}_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-2\alpha q_{-}\mu+\alpha^{2}q_{+}^{2}L^{2}\right)\|\bm{d}_{t}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\alpha q_{-}\mu\right)\|\bm{d}_{t}\|^{2},

(C.15)

where the last step uses

\alpha\leq q_{-}\mu/(4q_{+}^{2}L^{2})
. We now add back the two Adam-specific error terms. Let
\tau:=(\alpha q_{-}\mu)/4
. By Young’s inequality and the projection inequality above,

\displaystyle\|\bm{\theta}_{t+1}-\bm{\theta}_{t+1}^{\star}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\bm{z}_{t+1}-\bm{\theta}_{t+1}^{\star}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\tau)\|\bm{u}_{t}\|^{2}+(1+\tau^{-1})\alpha^{2}\|\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}\bm{r}_{t+1}+\bm{\eta}_{t+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\tau)\|\bm{u}_{t}\|^{2}+2(1+\tau^{-1})\alpha^{2}\left(q_{+}^{2}\|\bm{r}_{t+1}\|^{2}+\|\bm{\eta}_{t+1}\|^{2}\right).

(C.16)

Since
\alpha q_{-}\mu\leq 1
, we have
\tau\leq 1/4
. Thus

\displaystyle(1+\tau)(1-\alpha q_{-}\mu)\leq 1-\frac{3}{4}\alpha q_{-}\mu

\displaystyle 2(1+\tau^{-1})\alpha^{2}=2\alpha^{2}+\frac{8\alpha}{q_{-}\mu}\leq\frac{10\alpha}{q_{-}\mu}.

Combining these inequalities with
Eq.

C.15
and
Eq.

C.16
yields

\|\bm{\theta}_{t+1}-\bm{\theta}_{t+1}^{\star}\|^{2}\leq\left(1-\frac{3}{4}\alpha q_{-}\mu\right)\|\bm{d}_{t}\|^{2}+\frac{10\alpha q_{+}^{2}}{q_{-}\mu}\|\bm{r}_{t+1}\|^{2}+\frac{10\alpha}{q_{-}\mu}\|\bm{\eta}_{t+1}\|^{2}.

(C.17)

It remains to express the shifted error
\bm{d}_{t}
in terms of the
current tracking error and the minimizer drift. We have

\bm{d}_{t}=\bm{\theta}_{t}-\bm{\theta}_{t+1}^{\star}=\bm{\theta}_{t}-\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}+\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}-\bm{\theta}_{t+1}^{\star}.

Applying Young’s inequality with parameter
\alpha q_{-}\mu/4
gives

\displaystyle\|\bm{d}_{t}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{\alpha q_{-}\mu}{4}\right)\|\bm{\theta}_{t}-\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}\|^{2}+\left(1+\frac{4}{\alpha q_{-}\mu}\right)\|\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}-\bm{\theta}_{t+1}^{\star}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{\alpha q_{-}\mu}{4}\right)\|\bm{\theta}_{t}-\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}\|^{2}+\frac{5}{\alpha q_{-}\mu}\|\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}-\bm{\theta}_{t+1}^{\star}\|^{2},

(C.18)

where the last inequality uses
\alpha q_{-}\mu\leq 1
. Substituting
Eq.

C.18
into
Eq.

C.17
and using

\left(1-\frac{3}{4}\alpha q_{-}\mu\right)\left(1+\frac{1}{4}\alpha q_{-}\mu\right)\leq 1-\frac{1}{2}\alpha q_{-}\mu

proves

\|\bm{\theta}_{t+1}-\bm{\theta}_{t+1}^{\star}\|^{2}\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\alpha q_{-}\mu\right)\|\bm{\theta}_{t}-\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}\|^{2}+\frac{5}{\alpha q_{-}\mu}\|\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}-\bm{\theta}_{t+1}^{\star}\|^{2}+\frac{10\alpha q_{+}^{2}}{q_{-}\mu}\|\bm{r}_{t+1}\|^{2}+\frac{10\alpha}{q_{-}\mu}\|\bm{\eta}_{t+1}\|^{2},

which is exactly
Eq.

C.12
.
∎

Having reduced the tracking recursion to the two Adam-specific error terms
\bm{r}_{t+1}
and
\bm{\eta}_{t+1}
, it remains to control
the first-moment tracking error as
Lemma

C.1
provides us deterministic control of
\bm{\eta}_{t+1}
. We next decompose the first-moment tracking error
\bm{r}_{t+1}
. This term has two sources: a deterministic bias from using an exponential average of stale gradients, and a stochastic noise term from averaging martingale differences.

Lemma C.3

(Bias–noise decomposition of
\bm{r}_{t+1}
)
.

For every
t\geq 0
,

\bm{r}_{t+1}=\bm{B}_{t+1}^{(1)}+\bm{N}_{t+1}^{(1)},

where

\displaystyle\bm{B}_{t+1}^{(1)}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k=0}^{t}w_{1,t+1,k}\left(\bar{\bm{g}}_{k+1}(\bm{\theta}_{k})-\bar{\bm{g}}_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t})\right),

(C.19)

\displaystyle\bm{N}_{t+1}^{(1)}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k=0}^{t}w_{1,t+1,k}\bm{\xi}_{k+1}.

(C.20)

Moreover,

\|\bm{B}_{t+1}^{(1)}\|\leq c_{1,t+1}D_{1}.

Proof of
Lemma

C.3
.

By unrolling the bias-corrected first moment, we have

\widehat{\bm{m}}_{t+1}=\sum_{k=0}^{t}w_{1,t+1,k}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}g(\bm{\theta}_{k},X_{k+1})=\sum_{k=0}^{t}w_{1,t+1,k}\left(\bar{\bm{g}}_{k+1}(\bm{\theta}_{k})+\bm{\xi}_{k+1}\right).

Subtracting

\bar{\bm{g}}_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t})
and using

\sum_{k=0}^{t}w_{1,t+1,k}=1
gives

\bm{r}_{t+1}=\sum_{k=0}^{t}w_{1,t+1,k}\left(\bar{\bm{g}}_{k+1}(\bm{\theta}_{k})-\bar{\bm{g}}_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t})\right)+\sum_{k=0}^{t}w_{1,t+1,k}\bm{\xi}_{k+1},

which is the desired decomposition. It remains to bound the deterministic bias term. For
0\leq k\leq t-1
,
we telescope the difference between the stale conditional mean gradient and
the current one:

\bar{\bm{g}}_{k+1}(\bm{\theta}_{k})-\bar{\bm{g}}_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t})=\sum_{j=k+1}^{t}\left(\bar{\bm{g}}_{j}(\bm{\theta}_{j-1})-\bar{\bm{g}}_{j+1}(\bm{\theta}_{j})\right).

For each summand,

\displaystyle\|\bar{\bm{g}}_{j}(\bm{\theta}_{j-1})-\bar{\bm{g}}_{j+1}(\bm{\theta}_{j})\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\bar{\bm{g}}_{j}(\bm{\theta}_{j-1})-\bar{\bm{g}}_{j}(\bm{\theta}_{j})\|+\|\bar{\bm{g}}_{j}(\bm{\theta}_{j})-\bar{\bm{g}}_{j+1}(\bm{\theta}_{j})\|

\displaystyle\leq L\|\bm{\theta}_{j}-\bm{\theta}_{j-1}\|+2G

\displaystyle\leq LS+2G=D_{1}.

(C.21)

Therefore

\|\bar{\bm{g}}_{k+1}(\bm{\theta}_{k})-\bar{\bm{g}}_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t})\|\leq(t-k)D_{1}.

Averaging with the Adam weights gives

\|\bm{B}_{t+1}^{(1)}\|\leq D_{1}\sum_{k=0}^{t}w_{1,t+1,k}(t-k)=c_{1,t+1}D_{1},

where the
k=t
summand is zero.
∎

The deterministic part of
\bm{r}_{t+1}
is controlled by the memory length of the exponential average, while the stochastic part is a weighted martingale sum. The next lemma gives the corresponding high-probability bound uniformly over the horizon.

Lemma C.4

(High-probability control of
\bm{r}_{t+1}
)
.

Assume Assumptions
B.3
and
B.4
hold. Fix
T\geq 1
and

\delta\in(0,1)
. Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
,
simultaneously for all
t\in[T]
,

\|\bm{r}_{t+1}\|\leq c_{1,t+1}D_{1}+\sigma\sqrt{2d\kappa_{1,t+1}\log\frac{2T}{\delta}}+\frac{4G}{3}\omega_{1,t+1}\log\frac{2T}{\delta}.

(C.22)

Proof of
Lemma

C.4
.

Recall the bias–noise decomposition

\bm{r}_{t+1}=\bm{B}_{t+1}^{(1)}+\bm{N}_{t+1}^{(1)},

where

\bm{B}_{t+1}^{(1)}:=\sum_{k=0}^{t}w_{1,t+1,k}\left(\bar{\bm{g}}_{k+1}(\bm{\theta}_{k})-\bar{\bm{g}}_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t})\right),\qquad\bm{N}_{t+1}^{(1)}:=\sum_{k=0}^{t}w_{1,t+1,k}\bm{\xi}_{k+1}.

The bias term is deterministic conditional on the path and satisfies

\|\bm{B}_{t+1}^{(1)}\|\leq c_{1,t+1}D_{1}.

It therefore remains to control the martingale term

\bm{N}_{t+1}^{(1)}
. Fix
t\in\{0,\ldots,T-1\}
, and define
\bm{Z}_{k+1}^{(t)}:=w_{1,t+1,k}\bm{\xi}_{k+1},\;0\leq k\leq t
.
Then
(\bm{Z}_{k+1}^{(t)},\mathcal{F}_{k+1})_{k=0}^{t}
is a
vector-valued martingale difference sequence and

\bm{N}_{t+1}^{(1)}=\sum_{k=0}^{t}\bm{Z}_{k+1}^{(t)}.

By bounded gradients
\|\bm{Z}_{k+1}^{(t)}\|\leq 2G\omega_{1,t+1}\;\;\text{a.s.}
Moreover,
B.4
implies

\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{\xi}_{k+1}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\lesssim d\sigma^{2}
.
Therefore the predictable quadratic variation satisfies

\sum_{k=0}^{t}\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{Z}_{k+1}^{(t)}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\lesssim d\sigma^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{t}w_{1,t+1,k}^{2}=d\sigma^{2}\kappa_{1,t+1}.

Applying a Hilbert-space Freedman inequality for martingale differences (
Lemma

E.3
), we obtain
that for every
x>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\|\bm{N}_{t+1}^{(1)}\|\geq\sigma\sqrt{2d\kappa_{1,t+1}x}+\frac{4G}{3}\omega_{1,t+1}x\right)\leq 2e^{-x}.

Taking
x=\log(2T/\delta)
gives, for this fixed
t
, an event of
probability at least

C.3
Proofs for expected tracking error

The high-probability analysis above controls the stochastic first-moment error uniformly over time. For completeness, we also include the corresponding expectation bound. The proof is simpler: the martingale part of the first-moment error is controlled by orthogonality of martingale differences, while the preconditioner perturbation is handled by the same deterministic estimate as before.

Lemma C.5

(Second-moment control of the first-moment tracking error)
.

For every
t\geq 0
,

\mathbb{E}\|\bm{r}_{t+1}\|^{2}\leq 2c_{1,t+1}^{2}D_{1}^{2}+8G^{2}\kappa_{1,t+1}.

Proof of
Lemma

C.5
.

By

,

\bm{N}_{t+1}^{(1)}=\sum_{k=0}^{t}w_{1,t+1,k}\bm{\xi}_{k+1}

(\bm{\xi}_{k+1})_{k\geq 0}
is a martingale difference sequence, the
cross terms vanish
\mathbb{E}\left\langle\bm{\xi}_{k+1},\bm{\xi}_{\ell+1}\right\rangle=0,\;\;k<\ell
.

\mathbb{E}\|\bm{N}_{t+1}^{(1)}\|^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{t}w_{1,t+1,k}^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\bm{\xi}_{k+1}\|^{2}.

\displaystyle\Big\langle\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}^{-1}\Big[\bm{\theta}_{t+1}-\bm{\theta}_{t}+\alpha\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}G_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t})+\alpha\left(\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}\bm{r}_{t+1}+\bm{\eta}_{t+1}\right)\Big],\bm{y}-\bm{\theta}_{t+1}\Big\rangle\geq 0.

Taking
\bm{y}=\bm{\theta}_{t}
gives

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}G_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t}),\bm{s}_{t+1}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{\alpha}\|\bm{s}_{t+1}\|_{\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}^{-1}}^{2}-\left\langle\bm{r}_{t+1}+\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}^{-1}\bm{\eta}_{t+1},\bm{s}_{t+1}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{2\alpha}\|\bm{s}_{t+1}\|_{\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}^{-1}}^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\left\|\bm{r}_{t+1}+\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}^{-1}\bm{\eta}_{t+1}\right\|_{\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}}^{2},

(D.2)

where the last step uses Cauchy–Schwarz in the

\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}/\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}^{-1}

dual norm pair and Young’s inequality.

Since
G_{t+1}
is
L
-smooth, by
Lemma

E.5
we have

\displaystyle G_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t+1})\leq\;G_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t})+\left\langle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}G_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t}),\bm{s}_{t+1}\right\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|\bm{s}_{t+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle G_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t})-\frac{1}{2\alpha}\|\bm{s}_{t+1}\|_{\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}^{-1}}^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\left\|\bm{r}_{t+1}+\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}^{-1}\bm{\eta}_{t+1}\right\|_{\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}}^{2}+\frac{L}{2}\|\bm{s}_{t+1}\|^{2}.

(D.3)

Since
\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}\preceq q_{+}\bm{I}
, we have

\|\bm{s}_{t+1}\|^{2}\leq q_{+}\|\bm{s}_{t+1}\|_{\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}^{-1}}^{2}
.
Substituting this into
Eq.

D.3
gives

G_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t+1})\leq G_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t})-\left(\frac{1}{2\alpha}-\frac{Lq_{+}}{2}\right)\|\bm{s}_{t+1}\|_{\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}^{-1}}^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\left\|\bm{r}_{t+1}+\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}^{-1}\bm{\eta}_{t+1}\right\|_{\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}}^{2}.

Because
\alpha\leq(4Lq_{+})^{-1}
, the coefficient satisfies

\frac{1}{2\alpha}-\frac{Lq_{+}}{2}\geq\frac{1}{4\alpha}
. Therefore,

G_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t+1})\leq G_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t})-\frac{1}{4\alpha}\|\bm{s}_{t+1}\|_{\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}^{-1}}^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\left\|\bm{r}_{t+1}+\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}^{-1}\bm{\eta}_{t+1}\right\|_{\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}}^{2}.

(D.4)

Equivalently,

\displaystyle\|\bm{s}_{t+1}\|_{\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}^{-1}}^{2}\leq\;

\displaystyle 4\alpha\left(G_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t})-G_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t+1})\right)+2\alpha^{2}\left\|\bm{r}_{t+1}+\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}^{-1}\bm{\eta}_{t+1}\right\|_{\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}}^{2}.

(D.5)

We now relate the noisy projected step to the noiseless projected-gradient
mapping. By nonexpansiveness of the

\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}^{-1}
-metric projection
(
Lemma

E.1
),

\displaystyle\|\bm{\theta}_{t+1}-\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{t+1}\|_{\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\left\|\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}\bm{r}_{t+1}+\bm{\eta}_{t+1}\right\|_{\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}^{-1}}

\displaystyle=\alpha\left\|\bm{r}_{t+1}+\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}^{-1}\bm{\eta}_{t+1}\right\|_{\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}}.

(D.6)

Hence

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,t}(\bm{\theta}_{t})\right\|_{\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}^{-1}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\|\bm{\theta}_{t}-\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{t+1}\|_{\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}^{-1}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\alpha^{2}}\|\bm{s}_{t+1}\|_{\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}^{-1}}^{2}+2\left\|\bm{r}_{t+1}+\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}^{-1}\bm{\eta}_{t+1}\right\|_{\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}}^{2}.

(D.7)

Combining
Eqs.

D.5
and
D.7
yields

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,t}(\bm{\theta}_{t})\right\|_{\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}^{-1}}^{2}\leq\;

\displaystyle\frac{8}{\alpha}\left(G_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t})-G_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t+1})\right)+6\left\|\bm{r}_{t+1}+\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}^{-1}\bm{\eta}_{t+1}\right\|_{\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}}^{2}.

(D.8)

We now account for the time variation of the objective. Since

G_{t+2}(\bm{\theta}_{t+1})\leq G_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t+1})+\left(G_{t+2}(\bm{\theta}_{t+1})-G_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t+1})\right)_{+},

summing
Eq.

D.8
from
t=0
to
T-1
yields

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left\|\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,t}(\bm{\theta}_{t})\right\|_{\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}^{-1}}^{2}\leq\frac{8}{\alpha}\left(G_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{0})-G_{T+1}(\bm{\theta}_{T})+\mathfrak{D}_{T}\right)+6\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left\|\bm{r}_{t+1}+\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}^{-1}\bm{\eta}_{t+1}\right\|_{\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}}^{2}.

(D.9)

Using
G_{T+1}(\bm{\theta}_{T})\geq G^{\star}
, and then dividing by
T
, we obtain the pathwise bound

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left\|\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,t}(\bm{\theta}_{t})\right\|_{\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}^{-1}}^{2}\leq\frac{8(G_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{0})-G^{\star}+\mathfrak{D}_{T})}{\alpha T}+\frac{6}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left\|\bm{r}_{t+1}+\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}^{-1}\bm{\eta}_{t+1}\right\|_{\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}}^{2}.

(D.10)

Finally,

\left\|\bm{r}_{t+1}+\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}^{-1}\bm{\eta}_{t+1}\right\|_{\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}}^{2}\leq 2q_{+}\|\bm{r}_{t+1}\|^{2}+2q_{-}^{-1}\|\bm{\eta}_{t+1}\|^{2}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left\|\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,t}(\bm{\theta}_{t})\right\|_{\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}^{-1}}^{2}\leq\frac{8(G_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{0})-G^{\star}+\mathfrak{D}_{T})}{\alpha T}+\frac{12q_{+}}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\|\bm{r}_{t+1}\|^{2}+\frac{12q_{-}^{-1}}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\|\bm{\eta}_{t+1}\|^{2}.

(D.11)

On the event of
Lemma

C.4
, which holds with probability at least

1-\delta
,

\|\bm{r}_{t+1}\|\leq c_{1,t+1}D_{1}+\sigma\sqrt{2d\kappa_{1,t+1}\log\frac{2T}{\delta}}+\frac{4G}{3}\omega_{1,t+1}\log\frac{2T}{\delta}

simultaneously for all

C.1
gives deterministically

.
Substituting these two estimates into

Eq.

D.11
proves

Eq.

D.1
. When
\Theta=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the projection is inactive, so
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,t}(\bm{\theta}_{t})=\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}G_{t+1}(\bm{\theta}_{t})
,
and the last claim follows immediately.
∎

Corollary D.1

Under the conditions of

at least

(D.12)

where

Proof of
Corollary

D.1
.

By
Theorem

D.1
, on an event of probability at least

1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left\|\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,t}(\bm{\theta}_{t})\right\|_{\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}^{-1}}^{2}\leq\;\frac{8\bigl(G_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{0})-G^{\star}+\mathfrak{D}_{T}\bigr)}{\alpha T}

\displaystyle+\frac{12q_{+}}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}A_{t+1}^{2}+\frac{12q_{-}^{-1}G^{4}\epsilon^{-4}}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\vartheta_{2,t+1},

(D.13)

where

A_{t+1}:=c_{1,t+1}D_{1}+\sigma\sqrt{2d\kappa_{1,t+1}\log\frac{2T}{\delta}}+\frac{4G}{3}\omega_{1,t+1}\log\frac{2T}{\delta}.

Using
(a+b+c)^{2}\lesssim a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2}

A_{t+1}^{2}\lesssim c_{1,t+1}^{2}D_{1}^{2}+d\sigma^{2}\kappa_{1,t+1}\log\frac{2T}{\delta}+G^{2}\omega_{1,t+1}^{2}\log^{2}\frac{2T}{\delta}.

Thus it remains to bound the averages of
c_{1,t}^{2}
,
\kappa_{1,t}
,

\omega_{1,t}^{2}
, and
\vartheta_{2,t}

First,

c_{1,t}=\frac{\beta_{1}}{1-\beta_{1}}-\frac{t\beta_{1}^{t}}{1-\beta_{1}^{t}},

so

, and hence

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}c_{1,t}^{2}\leq\frac{\beta_{1}^{2}}{(1-\beta_{1})^{2}}.

(D.14)

Next, for any
0<\beta<1
,

\frac{\beta^{t}}{1-\beta^{t}}\leq\frac{1}{t(1-\beta)},\qquad t\geq 1.

(D.15)

Indeed, by AM–GM,

1+\beta+\cdots+\beta^{t-1}\geq t\beta^{(t-1)/2}
, so

1-\beta^{t}=(1-\beta)(1+\beta+\cdots+\beta^{t-1})\geq t(1-\beta)\beta^{(t-1)/2},

and therefore

\beta^{t}/(1-\beta^{t})\leq\beta^{(t+1)/2}/[t(1-\beta)]\leq 1/[t(1-\beta)]
.

Using

\kappa_{1,t}=\frac{1-\beta_{1}}{1+\beta_{1}}\left(1+\frac{2\beta_{1}^{t}}{1-\beta_{1}^{t}}\right),

we obtain

\kappa_{1,t}\leq\frac{1-\beta_{1}}{1+\beta_{1}}+\frac{2}{(1+\beta_{1})t}.

Therefore, with

,

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\kappa_{1,t}\leq\frac{1-\beta_{1}}{1+\beta_{1}}+\frac{2H_{T}}{(1+\beta_{1})T}\lesssim\frac{1-\beta_{1}}{1+\beta_{1}}+\frac{\log T}{(1+\beta_{1})T}.

Similarly,

\omega_{1,t}=\frac{1-\beta_{1}}{1-\beta_{1}^{t}}=(1-\beta_{1})\left(1+\frac{\beta_{1}^{t}}{1-\beta_{1}^{t}}\right)\leq(1-\beta_{1})+\frac{1}{t}.

Hence

\omega_{1,t}^{2}\lesssim(1-\beta_{1})^{2}+\frac{1}{t^{2}},

and since
\sum_{t=1}^{T}t^{-2}\lesssim 1
,

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\omega_{1,t}^{2}\lesssim(1-\beta_{1})^{2}+\frac{1}{T}.

Finally,

\vartheta_{2,t}=\beta_{2}^{t}+(1-\beta_{2}).

Applying
Eq.

D.15
with
\beta=\beta_{2}
gives

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\vartheta_{2,t}\leq(1-\beta_{2})+\frac{1}{(1-\beta_{2})T}.

(D.16)

Substituting
Eq.

D.14
–
Eq.

D.16

into
Eq.

D.13
gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left\|\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,t}(\bm{\theta}_{t})\right\|_{\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}^{-1}}^{2}\lesssim\frac{G_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{0})-G^{\star}+\mathfrak{D}_{T}}{\alpha T}+q_{+}\frac{\beta_{1}^{2}D_{1}^{2}}{(1-\beta_{1})^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad+q_{+}d\sigma^{2}\left(\frac{1-\beta_{1}}{1+\beta_{1}}+\frac{\log T}{(1+\beta_{1})T}\right)\log\frac{2T}{\delta}+q_{-}^{-1}G^{4}\epsilon^{-4}\left((1-\beta_{2})+\frac{1}{(1-\beta_{2})T}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+q_{+}G^{2}\left((1-\beta_{1})^{2}+\frac{1}{T}\right)\log^{2}\frac{2T}{\delta}.

\mathsf{Decay}_{T}(\delta)/T
and the remaining terms into

\mathsf{Floor}_{T}(\delta)

∎

Appendix E
Technical lemmas

E.1
Metric projection and projected-gradient mappings

We collect a few standard facts about projected-gradient mappings used in

Theorem

D.1
. Let
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be
nonempty, closed, and convex. For a positive definite matrix

\bm{A}
, define the
\bm{A}
-metric projection by

\mathcal{P}_{\Theta}^{\bm{A}}(\bm{z}):=\argmin_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{z}\|_{\bm{A}}^{2}.

When the metric is clear from context, we suppress the superscript and write

\mathcal{P}_{\Theta}
. For
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta
, we write

N_{\Theta}(\bm{\theta}):=\left\{\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\langle\bm{v},\bm{y}-\bm{\theta}\rangle\leq 0\;\;\text{for all }\bm{y}\in\Theta\right\}

for the normal cone of
\Theta
at
\bm{\theta}
. Thus

-\nabla f(\bm{\theta})\in N_{\Theta}(\bm{\theta})
is equivalent
to
\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{\theta}),\bm{y}-\bm{\theta}\right\rangle\geq 0

for all
\bm{y}\in\Theta
, which says that no feasible first-order direction decreases
f
. When

\bm{\theta}
lies in the interior of
\Theta
, this reduces to the usual unconstrained condition
\nabla f(\bm{\theta})=\bm{0}
.

Lemma E.1

(Metric projection facts)
.

Let
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be nonempty, closed, and convex, and let

\bm{A}\succ 0
. Then, for every
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\mathcal{P}_{\Theta}^{\bm{A}}(\bm{z})
exists and is unique.
Moreover, it satisfies the variational inequality

\left\langle\bm{A}\left(\mathcal{P}_{\Theta}^{\bm{A}}(\bm{z})-\bm{z}\right),\bm{y}-\mathcal{P}_{\Theta}^{\bm{A}}(\bm{z})\right\rangle\geq 0,\qquad\forall\bm{y}\in\Theta.

In addition, the projection is nonexpansive in the

\bm{A}
-metric:

\left\|\mathcal{P}_{\Theta}^{\bm{A}}(\bm{z})-\mathcal{P}_{\Theta}^{\bm{A}}(\bm{z}^{\prime})\right\|_{\bm{A}}\leq\|\bm{z}-\bm{z}^{\prime}\|_{\bm{A}},\qquad\forall\bm{z},\bm{z}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Proof.

The claim follows by viewing
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as a Hilbert space with inner
product
\langle\bm{x},\bm{y}\rangle_{\bm{A}}:=\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{y}
. Since
\Theta
is closed
and convex, the Hilbert-space projection theorem gives existence,
uniqueness, and nonexpansiveness of the projection. The variational
inequality is the first-order optimality condition for the strongly convex
problem defining
\mathcal{P}_{\Theta}^{\bm{A}}(\bm{z})
.
∎

For the Adam analysis, we take

\bm{A}=\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}^{-1}
. Thus

\mathcal{P}_{\Theta}
denotes projection onto
\Theta
in the

\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}^{-1}
-metric. Given a differentiable
function
f
, a positive definite preconditioner
\bm{P}
, and a
stepsize
\alpha>0
, define the preconditioned projected-gradient mapping

\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}^{\bm{P}}(\bm{\theta}):=\frac{1}{\alpha}\left[\bm{\theta}-\mathcal{P}_{\Theta}^{\bm{P}^{-1}}\left(\bm{\theta}-\alpha\bm{P}\nabla f(\bm{\theta})\right)\right].

In
Theorem

D.1
, this becomes

\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,t}(\bm{\theta}_{t})
with

\bm{P}=\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{t+1}
and

f=G_{t+1}
.

Lemma E.2

(Projected-gradient mapping and constrained stationarity)
.

Let
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be nonempty, closed, and convex, let

f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be differentiable, let

\bm{P}\succ 0
, and let
\alpha>0
. Then

\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}^{\bm{P}}(\bm{\theta})=\bm{0}\Longleftrightarrow-\nabla f(\bm{\theta})\in N_{\Theta}(\bm{\theta}).

In particular,
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}^{\bm{P}}(\bm{\theta})=0

is exactly the first-order stationarity condition for the constrained
problem
\min_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}f(\bm{\theta})
. Moreover,
when
\Theta=\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}^{\bm{P}}(\bm{\theta})=\bm{P}\nabla f(\bm{\theta}),\;\;\left\|\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}^{\bm{P}}(\bm{\theta})\right\|_{\bm{P}^{-1}}^{2}=\|\nabla f(\bm{\theta})\|_{\bm{P}}^{2}.

Proof.

Let

\bar{\bm{\theta}}:=\mathcal{P}_{\Theta}^{\bm{P}^{-1}}\left(\bm{\theta}-\alpha\bm{P}\nabla f(\bm{\theta})\right).

If
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}^{\bm{P}}(\bm{\theta})=\bm{0}
,
then
\bar{\bm{\theta}}=\bm{\theta}
. Applying

Lemma

E.1
with

\bm{A}=\bm{P}^{-1}
and

\bm{z}=\bm{\theta}-\alpha\bm{P}\nabla f(\bm{\theta})

gives, for every
\bm{y}\in\Theta
,

\left\langle\bm{P}^{-1}\left(\bm{\theta}-\bm{z}\right),\bm{y}-\bm{\theta}\right\rangle=\alpha\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{\theta}),\bm{y}-\bm{\theta}\right\rangle\geq 0.

Equivalently,

\langle-\nabla f(\bm{\theta}),\bm{y}-\bm{\theta}\rangle\leq 0
for all
\bm{y}\in\Theta
, which is precisely

-\nabla f(\bm{\theta})\in N_{\Theta}(\bm{\theta})
.

Conversely, if

-\nabla f(\bm{\theta})\in N_{\Theta}(\bm{\theta})
, then

\langle\nabla f(\bm{\theta}),\bm{y}-\bm{\theta}\rangle\geq 0
for every

\bm{y}\in\Theta
. Hence

\left\langle\bm{P}^{-1}\left(\bm{\theta}-\left[\bm{\theta}-\alpha\bm{P}\nabla f(\bm{\theta})\right]\right),\bm{y}-\bm{\theta}\right\rangle\geq 0,\qquad\forall\bm{y}\in\Theta.

By the variational characterization of the metric projection,

\bm{\theta}=\mathcal{P}_{\Theta}^{\bm{P}^{-1}}(\bm{\theta}-\alpha\bm{P}\nabla f(\bm{\theta}))
, and
therefore

\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}^{\bm{P}}(\bm{\theta})=\bm{0}
.
The unconstrained identities follow immediately from

\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{\bm{P}^{-1}}
being the identity map.
∎

E.2
Martingale concentration inequalities

Lemma E.3

(Freedman’s inequality for Hilbert-space martingales;
[
29
,
30
]
)
.

Let
(\bm{Z}_{k},\mathcal{F}_{k})_{k=1}^{n}
be a martingale difference
sequence taking values in a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. Define

\bm{Y}_{k}:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\bm{Z}_{i},\qquad W_{k}:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{Z}_{i}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}].

Suppose
\|\bm{Z}_{k}\|\leq R
almost surely for all
k
. Then, for
all
u,v>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\exists k\leq n:\|\bm{Y}_{k}\|\geq u\ \text{and}\ W_{k}\leq v\right)2\exp\left(-\frac{u^{2}/2}{v+Ru/3}\right).

In particular, if
W_{n}\leq v
almost surely, then with probability at least

1-\delta
,

\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\bm{Z}_{k}\right\|\leq\sqrt{2v\log\frac{2}{\delta}}+\frac{2R}{3}\log\frac{2}{\delta}.

Lemma E.4

(Freedman’s inequality for scalar martingales;
[
10
,
39
]
)
.

Let
(Z_{k},\mathcal{F}_{k})_{k=1}^{n}
be a real-valued martingale difference sequence, so that

\mathbb{E}[Z_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}]=0\qquad\text{a.s.}

Assume
|Z_{k}|\leq R
almost surely for all
k
. Define the predictable quadratic variation

V_{n}:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[Z_{k}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}].

Then, for every
s\geq 0
and every
v\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\sum_{k=1}^{n}Z_{k}\right|\geq s\;\;\text{and}\;\;V_{n}\leq v\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{s^{2}/2}{v+Rs/3}\right).

In particular, if
V_{n}\leq v
almost surely, then with probability at least

1-\delta
,

\left|\sum_{k=1}^{n}Z_{k}\right|\leq\sqrt{2v\log\frac{2}{\delta}}+\frac{2R}{3}\log\frac{2}{\delta}.

E.3
L
-smoothness and descent inequalities

Lemma E.5

(Descent lemma for
L
-smooth functions;
[
27
]
)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be differentiable and
L
-smooth, meaning that

\|\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)\|\leq L\|y-x\|\;\;\text{for all }x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Then, for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

\left|f(y)-f(x)-\left\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\right\rangle\right|\leq\frac{L}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}.

Additional experimental details and results

We provide additional details for the numerical experiments in
Section

F.3
. All experiments compare SGD and Adam under matched non-stationary schedules. For each problem and regime, we tune the learning rate over a small fixed grid and report the curve with the best tail performance, where tail performance is computed over the second half of the run.

F.1
General setup

Optimizers and tuning.

We compare vanilla SGD and Adam. For SGD, we tune the learning rate over

\{10^{-4},3\cdot 10^{-4},10^{-3},3\cdot 10^{-3},10^{-2},3\cdot 10^{-2}\}
. For Adam, we tune the learning rate over the same grid as SGD with

\beta_{1}=0.9
,
\beta_{2}=0.999
, and
\epsilon=10^{-8}
. For each
optimizer, problem, and regime, we select the learning rate that minimizes
the average metric over the second half of the trajectory. Curves are then
reported for the selected learning rate, averaged over seeds

\{0,1,2\}
, with shaded regions denoting one standard error of the mean.

Drift and noise schedules.

For vector-valued targets, the drift is generated through normalized
Gaussian directions:

\bm{\theta}_{t+1}^{\star}=\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}+\Delta_{t}\frac{\bm{u}_{t}}{\|\bm{u}_{t}\|},\qquad\bm{u}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{d}).

Thus
\Delta_{t}
sets the drift magnitude, while
\sigma_{t}
sets the
stochastic observation noise. In the two-layer MLP experiment, the same idea
is implemented in function space: the teacher parameters are perturbed in a
random normalized parameter direction and rescaled so that the induced change
in teacher predictions has magnitude controlled by
\Delta_{t}
. Unless
otherwise stated, schedules are logarithmic in time, of the form

a\log(t+2)
.

Task-specific details

Strongly convex least squares.

We use dimension
d=50

Teacher–student MLP regression.

We use a two-layer ReLU network

f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})=\bm{W}_{2}\mathrm{ReLU}(\bm{W}_{1}\bm{x}+\bm{b}_{1})+b_{2},

with input dimension
100

128
. Teacher and student

0.04

We evaluate on a fixed validation set of size
1024
and report prediction
MSE.

Phase retrieval.

We use dimension
d=50
. Given

\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{d})
, the
time-varying teacher produces

y_{t}=(\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{w}_{t}^{\star})^{2}+\sigma_{t}\varepsilon_{t},\qquad\varepsilon_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1).

The corresponding population objective is

F_{t}(\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}}\left[\left((\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{\theta})^{2}-(\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{w}_{t}^{\star})^{2}\right)^{2}\right].

The target
\bm{w}_{t}^{\star}
evolves by normalized Gaussian drift,
and the model is warm-started at the initial target. We use batch size

256

1024

Matrix factorization.

We use a rank-
5
matrix factorization problem with

m=n=60
. The target matrix is

\bm{M}_{t}^{\star}=\bm{U}_{t}^{\star}(\bm{V}_{t}^{\star})^{\top}
,

F_{t}(\bm{U},\bm{V})=\frac{1}{2mn}\|\bm{U}\bm{V}^{\top}-\bm{M}_{t}^{\star}\|_{F}^{2}.

At each stochastic step, we sample entries

In the main matrix factorization experiment, the target is stationary

F.3
Additional experimental examples

We include logistic regression and lasso regression as additional examples to
test whether the same qualitative noise–drift tradeoff appears beyond the
main comparison tasks.

Logistic regression.

We consider convex rank-deficient logistic regression with dimension

d=100
, rank
r=20
, and
n=1000
samples. Covariates are generated as

\bm{A}=\bm{Z}\bm{U}^{\top}
, where

\bm{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
has orthonormal columns and

\bm{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
has i.i.d. Gaussian entries scaled
by
1/\sqrt{r}
. Let
\varsigma(z)=(1+e^{-z})^{-1}
denote the sigmoid
function, applied entrywise, and define the clean teacher probabilities

\bm{p}_{t}:=\varsigma(\bm{A}\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}).

To introduce label noise in a way that preserves the Bernoulli logistic
structure, we use symmetric label-flip noise. Let
\pi_{t}\in[0,1/2)
denote
the label-flip probability. The corrupted label probabilities are

\widetilde{\bm{p}}_{t}:=(1-2\pi_{t})\bm{p}_{t}+\pi_{t}\bm{1}.

Equivalently, if
z_{i,t}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p_{t,i})
is the clean
label, then the observed label
y_{i,t}
is obtained by flipping
z_{i,t}

with probability
\pi_{t}
. Thus

y_{i,t}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\widetilde{p}_{t,i}).

The population objective corresponding to the corrupted-label distribution is

F_{t}(\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\log\!\left(1+\exp(\bm{a}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\theta})\right)-\widetilde{p}_{t,i}\bm{a}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\theta}\right],

with gradient

\nabla F_{t}(\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{n}\bm{A}^{\top}\left(\varsigma(\bm{A}\bm{\theta})-\widetilde{\bm{p}}_{t}\right).

The target
\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}
drifts within the rank-
r

subspace. Specifically,

\bm{\theta}_{t+1}^{\star}=\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}+\Delta_{t}\bm{U}\bm{u}_{t},\qquad\bm{u}_{t}=\frac{\bm{g}_{t}}{\|\bm{g}_{t}\|_{2}},\qquad\bm{g}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{r}).

We report the stationarity measure

\mathrm{Stat}_{t}:=\|\nabla F_{t}(\bm{\theta}_{t})\|^{2},

so smaller values indicate that the iterate is closer to a stationary point
of the current corrupted-label population objective.

Lasso regression.

We also consider lasso regression with dimension
d=100
, sample size

n=200
, sparsity level
s=10
, and regularization parameter

\lambda=0.02
. The design matrix

\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}

by
1/\sqrt{d}
. The target vector
\bm{\beta}_{0}^{\star}
is initialized
as an
s

|S|=s

Throughout the experiment, this support is kept fixed.

The population objective at time

At each stochastic step, we sample a mini-batch of rows from

\bm{X}

y_{i,t}=\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{t}^{\star}+\sigma_{t}\varepsilon_{i,t},\qquad\varepsilon_{i,t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1).

The target vector evolves by normalized drift restricted to the fixed sparse

\bm{\beta}_{t+1}^{\star}=\bm{\beta}_{t}^{\star}+\Delta_{t}\bm{u}_{t},\qquad\operatorname{supp}(\bm{u}_{t})\subseteq S,\qquad\|\bm{u}_{t}\|_{2}=1.

\bm{\beta}_{t}^{\star}
