Ethics in prehospital extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation
Authors: Richard B. Witkov, Dorian Alexander
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Prehospital extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR) shows potential in improving outcomes in out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) with emerging adoption in multiple systems internationally. Ethical implications differ between hospital-based and prehospital ECPR systems as the prehospital environment introduces unique considerations related to location, timing, consent, patient selection, triage, and patient access. Prehospital ECPR may have unique benefits in avoiding ethical pitfalls of in-hospital cannulation. Ethical considerations specific to prehospital ECPR should be assessed prior to implementation with thorough risk–benefit analysis utilizing fundamental ethical principles from the perspectives of affected stakeholders.

Introduction: prehospital ECPR ethical implications in the field

ECPR has emerged as a salvage therapy in refractory cardiac arrest with favorable arrest characteristics supported by multiple international guidelines in developed systems of care.1, 2 Experience in several countries has extended ECPR to the prehospital field, but, as with any emerging technology, ethical implications should be considered.3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 Prior literature has focused on ethical concerns related to general deployment within the hospital but lacks nuanced consideration of prehospital ECPR implementation.11, 12 Prehospital ECPR introduces distinct ethical challenges and considerations requiring dedicated frameworks built on the four foundational ethical principles of non-maleficence, autonomy, beneficence, and justice to ensure appropriate deployment within larger systems of care (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Four pillars of medical ethics in as they apply to prehospital ECPR.

Non-maleficence

ECPR timing and transport risk

ECPR implementation is highly dependent on cardiac arrest timing. When considering ECPR timing, physicians must ensure that appropriate primary resuscitative measures including high-quality cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) and early defibrillation have been attempted. Initiating ECPR too soon may detract from the quality of CPR and shift the resuscitative team’s focus. Alternatively, delay decreases the likelihood of successful neurologically intact survival.13

Hospital-based ECPR models require transporting cardiac arrest patients to receiving facilities. Transport of cardiac arrest patients may be associated with decreased CPR quality and poses risks to emergency medical services (EMS) as well as non-involved vehicles.14, 15 The goal would be for any decreased chance of neurologically intact survival incurred by transport to be supplanted by ECPR’s benefit (Fig. 2). Despite early transport, many patients are denied cannulation upon hospital arrival due to strict inclusion criteria or clinician judgment, subjecting non-ECPR candidates to transport harms without clinical benefit. In fact, one critique of the landmark ARREST trial is that patients were not randomized until after hospital arrival.16, 17 In a system with high ECPR application for OHCA such as Minneapolis, transport risks may be supplanted by increased neurologic survival from ECPR implementation. However, in many systems where ECPR deployment is erratic and limited for OHCA, transport risks may overshadow any benefit. Prehospital cannulation location

When evaluating prehospital versus in-hospital cannulation, additional consideration must be made to the type of prehospital cannulation deployed as this decision is not binary. Different models exist internationally including cannulation in a modified ambulance, in a specialized ECPR vehicle with fluoroscopy, and on scene where the patient arrests (Fig. 3).3, 7, 18 Prehospital ECPR on scene where the patient arrests avoids patient transport risk but suffers from limitations due to unpredictable location with potential for poor visibility, space constraints, erratic weather conditions, and bystander involvement.4 A modified ambulance may avoid these limitations by creating a dedicated space for cannulation at the cost of patient movement. A specialized ECPR vehicle may add features such as fluoroscopy to mimic a mobile catheterization laboratory but may be very expensive to widely implement compared to a modified ambulance. Furthermore, both modified ambulances and specialized ECPR vehicles may not be easily deployed to more remote arrests requiring team transport by helicopter. The advantages of transport to specialized vehicles should be weighed against the potential harm associated with patient movement as well as system level harms related to cost and care access.Fig. 3Different international models of ECPR field cannulation.

ECPR complications

ECPR has potential for multiple complications including stroke, hemorrhage, vascular injury, infection, and procedural failure.19, 20 Prehospital cannulation has the possibility for additional risk due to the austere setting. This increased complication risk must be weighed against the risk of transport described previously or mitigated by patient environment optimization. It is unclear what an acceptable risk level may be as the alternatives may be death following failed CPR or anoxic injury from delayed ECPR.21 In the context of non-maleficence, the definition of an acceptable risk level must explore the ethical distinction between mere biological survival and survival with severe, irreversible neurological devastation. Many ethicists and patients consider profound neurological injury to be harm worse than death, making the risk calculus of prehospital ECPR more nuanced than simply avoiding mortality.12

System-wide focus and standard care

While the risk of early ECPR initiation detracting from high-quality CPR is often considered on an individual level, implementing a prehospital ECPR program introduces a risk of system-wide maleficence. Prehospital ECPR relies on a steep system-wide learning curve. Heavily investing in specialized training and deployment pathways for ECPR risks shifting a system's cognitive focus, training time, and financial resources away from foundational cardiac arrest management. If resuscitation degrades for the broader population of non-ECPR candidates as an inadvertent result of prioritizing ECPR, the overall community harm could outweigh the benefits gained by the select few who receive prehospital ECPR.

Autonomy

Informed consent and patient preference

Cardiac arrest patients cannot advocate for or refuse medical treatment. This poses significant challenges in ECPR decision-making as most advanced directives do not incorporate ECPR.22 With the evolution of resuscitation science, it is unclear how to best educate the public regarding ECPR. Even with education, informed consent on an individualized basis is often not possible. While prehospital ECPR bypasses the initial need for individualized consent by relying on emergency implied consent and community-wide agreements, it may create a deferred ethical dilemma regarding care withdrawal. The ethical transition from rapid, assumed consent to the complex, values-based decision of whether to withdraw life supporting care may place an immense moral and psychological burden on families. Systems implementing prehospital ECPR must proactively establish ethical frameworks for withdrawal and end-of-life care when a patient’s established advanced directives or surrogate decisions conflict with established support.

Additionally, prehospital ECPR may be advantageous by bringing ECPR to the patient. By hospital arrival, decision-makers are often not immediately identifiable, whereas on scene, surrogate decision-makers may be present. However, it is important to note that the availability and authority of these surrogates can vary significantly due to cultural or regional differences.

Finally, cardiac arrest patients cannot decide they would like to be transported to an ECPR capable hospital. Without standardization of ECPR receiving centers, patients may lose the opportunity to choose which therapies are made available during cardiac arrest with possibility of transport to a center without ECPR capability. Prehospital ECPR allows for standardization of care in the absence of patient decision.

Beneficence

Individual versus system

Integral to OHCA ECPR is understanding if ECPR offers the patient the best chance at neurologically intact survival with good functional outcome. Much of patient candidacy determination involves excluding patients with expected poor outcomes which fails to assess if ECPR gives the individual the highest odds of successful resuscitation. Often decision-making is made from the perspective of the system rather than the individual to conserve resources.

Bartos et al. analyzed ECPR outcomes compared to historical controls and reported neurologically intact survival in patients with up to 90–99 min of professional CPR duration with no survivors in historical controls after 40 min.23 Mørk et al. analyzed a large cohort of patients receiving mechanical support in Denmark and found favorable outcomes in patients excluded by traditional resuscitation guidelines including favorable outcomes in patients with >80 min of low flow time.24 These findings highlight a profound tension between individual beneficence and systemic justice; strict institutional cutoff criteria designed to conserve finite system resources may ethically fail the individual patient by denying them a salvageable path to neurologically intact survival.

Studying ECPR in OHCA

To further elucidate if a patient-centered benefit exists compared to standard practice, multiple randomized controlled trials have been performed with mixed results.17, 25, 26 However, device-based therapies are challenging to interpret because of issues with patient selection, crossover, and device learning curves.27 In ECPR, this effect is even more profound due to reliance on a chain of survival that constitutes a system-wide learning curve. Prehospital ECPR is no exception and lacks even randomized trials to guide implementation relying on case reports, small pilots, and cohort studies.4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 Given the intense education and experience required to cannulate in the prehospital setting, early results may be inconclusive or negative while systems adjust to prehospital ECPR implementation.

Despite challenges in studying prehospital ECPR, refractory cardiac arrest outcomes without ECPR are bleak.28 Expert commentary has compared ECPR to testing a parachute.18 When assessing prehospital ECPR, it is prudent to assess how prehospital ECPR compares to other methods of ECPR delivery in terms of patient-centered benefit and whether it provides a more effective safety net.

Increasing patient candidacy

Prehospital ECPR offers possible advantages in patient reach with a two-fold increase in patient candidacy in a ground-based EMS model and a four-fold increase in HEMS based model.29 Patients excluded from ECPR due to distance and CPR duration cannot benefit from ECPR, and delay to cannulation will decrease odds of a positive outcome due to time-dependency of ECPR initiation.30

Organ donation

Prehospital ECPR, additionally serves as a method of organ preservation for donation even in the absence of neurologically intact survival. Unlike prehospital systems that may seek to employ normothermic regional perfusion with cerebral occlusion protocols, prehospital ECPR has the dual advantage of both giving the patient the best chance for neurologically intact survival as well as preserving organs for donation in non-survivors without complex early prognostication and decision-making on scene.31 While this dual advantage leaves surrogates with meaningful options, systems must establish strict ethical guardrails to ensure the primary intent of cannulation remains patient-centered.

Justice

Selection bias and disparities in patient access

Patient selection for ECPR initiation is not standardized instead relying on incomplete trial data, guidelines, and institutional experience.32 Lack of system standardization allows for a substantial risk of bias and inconsistent application among institutions and independent practitioners. Further bias influencing cardiac arrest outcomes may exist due to EMS transport practices and hospital selection.33 Prehospital ECPR initiation has the potential to increase standardization and access across a geographic system providing equal resuscitation opportunity regardless of ethnicity or socioeconomic status. Given females are less likely to receive bystander CPR, have witnessed arrests, and present in a shockable rhythm, prehospital ECPR may reach female patients that would be noncandidates upon hospital arrival but may still be candidates if reached earlier in their disease course.34

Pediatric patients bear special mention as they rarely have access to extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) centers after OHCA and even ECMO capable centers are unlikely to have systems designed for pediatric ECPR initiation due to infrequency of pediatric OHCA.35, 36 This should be juxtaposed against the larger backdrop of systemic inequities in resuscitation quality and outcomes in the pediatric population.37 Prehospital ECPR teams facile in adult ECPR cannulation may be deployed to cases of pediatric OHCA utilizing oiled systems of adult prehospital cannulation.38, 39 This avoids barriers to entry that may exist secondary to separation of adult and pediatric ECMO resuscitation resources on hospital arrival.

Choosing selection criteria

Both prehospital and in-hospital ECPR cannulation criteria often combine clinical assessments, historical data, and objective data when making cannulation decisions.32 Prehospital ECPR patient selection is subject to bias due to location of the arrest and presumed circumstances of arrest. To avoid conscious or subconscious biases, objective data (e.g., patient rhythm, lactic acid level, measured pupillary response) should take precedence over historical or subjective assessments. However, objective physiological markers can be skewed by systemic inequities, such as poor early resuscitation quality or high baseline chronic disease burdens.40, 41 Though prehospital ECPR can increase patient access, public health measures are still necessary to ensure fair opportunity for ECPR candidacy.

Operational ethics

Multiple operational issues must be considered unique to the prehospital environment. Potential exists for simultaneous activations including activations of patients not meeting criteria for ECPR on scene arrival. Dispatch prioritization must be planned to avoid potential bias in location or patient selection with finite response ability. Additional consideration is necessary for proper unit activation during times of system stress and resource scarcity such as pandemics or natural disasters to ensure fair resource utilization.

Special difficulty exists during pandemic responses due to need for significant personal protective equipment, which hinders communication and increases cannulation difficulty. Furthermore, the risk to healthcare workers may be increased with exposure to bodily fluids without the benefit of a standardized room with appropriate precautions. This also poses societal harm extending beyond the individual healthcare workers due to lost ability to care for others and disease propagation.42

Cost of a life

Prehospital ECPR has the potential to increase the absolute number of neurologically intact survivors but may also increase the total number of patients requiring advanced critical care services resulting in greater system strain. From a societal standpoint questions of cost-effectiveness still exist though early calculations place prehospital ECPR within established international cutoffs for acceptability.43 Developing a framework for cost-effectiveness is difficult due to adjustments for patient selection criteria utilized. Cost-effectiveness data for prehospital ECPR may be compared to in-hospital ECPR and to other forms of accepted cost-effective medical therapy to guide system level decisions.

However, difficulty still exists in defining what a life is worth as well as the impact of prolonged resuscitative measures for patients and families. Prehospital ECPR additionally has added complexity in many systems because of lack of clarity who pays for the service even if found to be cost-effective by traditional medical benchmarks.

Conclusion

Prehospital ECPR offers promise for the future of advanced cardiac arrest resuscitation but should be implemented with careful attention to core ethical principles. Further prehospital ECPR research and program development must consider risk and benefit for system stakeholders prior to widespread adoption and ensure unique ethical integration in clinical practice. Finally, structured ethical frameworks with standardized evaluation metrics must be developed to judge future success or failure of prehospital ECPR implementation and ensure prehospital ECPR teams are doing not just what is possible but what is right for their communities.
