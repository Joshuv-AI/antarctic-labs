Tailoring surgery for obstructed defecation and its evolution into ineffective defecation: a new staging of the syndrome and a site-specific decision algorithm
Authors: Adolfo Renzi
Published: 2026

INTRODUCTION: BEYOND MECHANICAL BLOCKADE AND THE "TAILORED" APPROACH

Obstructed defecation syndrome (ODS) is a comprehensive clinical descriptor for the impaired evacuation of rectal contents. This condition represents a significant and pervasive healthcare challenge, underlying up to 50% of functional constipation cases and predominantly afflicting middle-aged women with a profound deleterious impact on quality of life [1, 2].

Over the past decade, the conceptualization of pelvic floor disorders has moved beyond localized anatomical corrections toward a systemic understanding of functional pelvic failure. Traditionally, ODS was managed as a mechanical rectal outlet obstruction, where the presence of a rectocele or internal mucosal prolapse was considered definitive evidence of pathology requiring resection or suspension. However, the persistence of symptoms after technically successful repairs has necessitated a shift toward a more nuanced diagnosis: ineffective defecation syndrome (IDS).

This article introduces a proposed decision-making algorithm based on the principle that surgical intervention must not be universal; rather, it must be tailored to the specific topographical damage identified during the disease's evolution. This paradigm shift originated from research describing the pathophysiological mechanisms of the pelvic floor. The crucial role of the Valsalva maneuver in defecatory dynamics—and the drastic compromise of its efficacy via vector dissipation in patients with a pathologically descended floor—was first illustrated in a 2016 study published in Diseases of the Colon & Rectum [3]. Subsequently, the term "ineffective defecation syndrome" was introduced in a 2024 study in the same journal [4], a concept now formalized in the guidelines of the Italian Unitary Society of Colon-Proctology [5].

Ineffective defecation describes a physiological state where expulsion is compromised by the loss of perineal stability, leading to a rectal propulsive defect irrespective of mechanical blockade.

THE 2-STAGE THEORY: STAGING THE PROGRESSION OF INEFFECTIVE DEFECATION

The 2-stage theory provides a scientific framework for IDS, mapping the natural history of the syndrome from initial mechanical manifestations to terminal functional decline caused by chronic neuromuscular trauma.

Stage I (early/obstructive phase)

The pathology originates from damage to the mid-distal site of the rectum. The primum movens is the invagination of the rectal walls into the anal canal (rectoanal intussusception), which acts as a physical bolus. In this phase, the pelvic floor musculature typically retains near-normal tone, pathological perineal descent (PD) is absent, and symptoms can often be resolved through procedures targeting the primary mechanical issue.

Stage II (late/functional phase: IDS)

Over time, chronic and repetitive straining against the resistance caused by the mid-distal prolapse leads to the degeneration of secondary support sites. Futile straining precipitates a catastrophic loss of support in the basal site, defined by the emergence of pathological PD [6]. In this stage, the failure of the propulsive platform is total. According to the Laplace law, straining induces only a change in the geometry of the lower abdominal cavity without achieving the intracavitary pressure increase necessary for effective expulsion, rendering the maneuver "ineffective."

RECTOCELE AS “HORROR VACUI” AND THE SEQUESTRATION MECHANISM

A conceptual advancement in the staging of IDS is the reinterpretation of the anterior rectocele through the principle of horror vacui (nature abhors a vacuum). Rather than being a primary hernia or an obstructive blockade, the rectocele is often a secondary morphological response to the failure of the basal site.

When the pelvic floor "drops" due to pathological descent, it creates a "functional vacuum" in the posterior compartment. Mobile organs, such as the small intestine (enterocele) or sigmoid (sigmoidocele), dislocate downward to occupy this space for purely physical reasons; these organs possess no propulsive capacity and are merely "attracted" to the vacuum. Similarly, the anterior rectal wall expands to occupy this void. Crucially, the rectocele acts as a sequestration mechanism (fecal subtraction from the correct flow) rather than an obstructive one. This explains why rectocele size shows no correlation with symptom severity, whereas PD correlates directly and exclusively with clinical impairment as measured by the ODS score (ODS-S) [7, 8]. Treating a rectocele in isolation in a stage II patient is futile if the underlying foundation remains unstable.

THE DIAGNOSTIC IMPERATIVE: MORPHOLOGICAL ASSESSMENT AND SITE MAPPING

While an ODS-S of ≥9 identifies severe pathology [8], it does not codify a specific anatomical phenotype. Because surgeons must treat topographical alterations that vary by individual, dynamic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) defecography is indispensable for evaluating the integrity of the support sites:

(1) Mid-distal site (rectal wall): The site of the primum movens. MRI assesses rectal wall tone to identify rectoanal intussusception (Oxford grade ≥3) [9].

(2) Apical site (upper rectum): Evaluates the suspension of the rectum and its structural partnership with the posterior vaginal fornix and the cervix (mediated by cardinal and uterosacral ligaments) [10]. The damage is quantified as apical suspension loss by measuring the position of the vaginal fornix or cervix relative to the pubococcygeal line (PCL) during maximum strain: a position ≥1 cm above the PCL is considered normal, whereas any position below this threshold indicates apical suspension loss. Structural collapse often associated with multicompartmental organ descent is defined by positions ≥3 cm below the PCL. A descent of more than 1 cm at rest of the bladder base and of the posterior vaginal fornix or vaginal vault in respect of the pubococcygeal plane is considered suggestive for prolapse [11].

(3) Basal site (pelvic floor foundation): Measures the stability of the perineal body and anococcygeal raphe. Pathological PD (≥30 mm at rest or ≥ 60 mm during straining) is the definitive marker of failure in this site [11, 12].

Advanced imaging also allows the clinician to exclude nonsurgical functional causes, such as anismus. Without this topographical mapping, surgical choice remains subjective; with it, the intervention becomes a logical consequence of identified structural damage.

THE TRANSVERSE PERINEAL SUPPORT BIOMECHANICAL REVOLUTION AND LONG-TERM OUTCOMES

Regarding the basal site (the failure of which causes functional collapse of the distal support [perineal body] and global pelvic floor stability), research has led to the development of the transverse perineal support (TPS) procedure. The rationale is strictly biomechanical: TPS replicates the function of the transverse perineal muscles, maintaining the transverse tension of the perineal body during straining.

By anchoring the prosthetic implant to the ischiopubic rami, the procedure establishes a stable platform that counteracts pathological PD, an essential prerequisite for an effective Valsalva maneuver. By preventing the downward dissipation of abdominal volume, TPS ensures the abdominal press operates within a fixed container. In accordance with the Laplace law, this stability allows for a true reduction in cavitary volume, generating the indispensable pressure increment required for defecation. This biomechanical restoration results in a targeted extrinsic compression of the rectum, facilitating the efficient expulsion of its contents [3, 4].

Clinical validity is supported by a multicenter observational case-control study (2023–2025) involving 170 stage II patients. The addition of TPS to resective techniques (stapled transanal rectal resection, STARR) maintained stable ODS-S of 5 at 60 months, compared to 11.5 in those undergoing STARR alone (P<0.001). Postoperative maximum PD during straining significantly decreased only in the group treated with TPS, confirming that stabilizing the foundation is key to preventing recurrence of repairs in the apical or mid-distal sites [13].

PROPOSED DECISION ALGORITHM: A TARGETED ORIENTATION

This proposed algorithm reflects my personal surgical orientation and the clinical standard I consistently apply in my practice for the treatment of ODS and its clinical evolution into IDS. The following framework is designed to guide the surgeon in selecting the correct strategy based on the actual site-specific topographical damage identified via dynamic MRI defecography:

Isolated mid-distal damage

If the defect is restricted and identified exclusively at this level (rectoanal intussusception, Oxford grade ≥3) with a normal PD, STARR is the elective treatment to remove the rectoanal intussusception, aimed at restoring normal anatomy [14].

Associated apical site damage

If chronic straining has led to apical suspension loss, pelvic organ prolapse suspension (POPS) represents a primary surgical option to restore the vertical axis [15]. However, the final strategy depends on intraoperative assessment: if the apical suspension maneuver effectively reduces the rectoanal intussusception, POPS alone is sufficient; if the distal prolapse persists indicating a lack of anatomical synergy between compartments, a combined POPS+STARR approach is indicated to address both defects.

Associated basal site failure

In the presence of pathological PD, TPS is indicated in association with the site-specific treatments described above (STARR, POPS, or a combination of both). This integration is designed to stabilize the pelvic floor foundation, creating a fixed container and restoring Valsalva efficiency.

CONCLUSION: THE NEW STANDARD OF CARE

The clinical transition from ODS to IDS is a diagnostic necessity that dictates a fundamental shift in surgical philosophy. Durable outcomes require moving beyond procedures centered on anatomical protrusions toward a tailored approach focused on the functional stability of specific topographical sites. 2-stage theory and the principle of horror vacui provide the necessary framework to understand why isolated repairs often fail in advanced stages, where the collapse of the basal site—the pelvic floor foundation—becomes the dominant clinical feature.

This proposed algorithm introduces a dynamic diagnostic and therapeutic model, where site-specific mapping is integrated with intraoperative assessment to refine the surgical strategy (e.g., POPS+STARR). Ultimately, by addressing the structural stability of the basal foundation through the integration of TPS alongside the correction of apical and mid-distal deficits, we can ensure stable long-term results, restore the biomechanical efficiency of the abdominal press, and preserve the long-term integrity of the pelvic floor neuromuscular structures.
