Negotiating complexity in spite of scarce evidence—how to care for older ICU patients?
Authors: Michael Beil, Christian Jung, Bertrand Guidet
Published: 2026

Chronic conditions and functional impairments ensue from the interplay between environmental stressors and intrinsic alterations of organs over the course of life [1]. Due to the diversity of these processes, the resulting heterogeneity among older adults is considered a hallmark of aging [2]. Some individuals present with complex patterns of aging-related disorders, summarised as geriatric syndromes, earlier in life than others. This indicates accelerated aging that confers an even greater vulnerability to stressors, a characteristic which should inform discussions about the benefit and burden of critical care in older adults [3]. Ideally, these conversations would be part of advance care planning which, in individuals of this group, usually aims to maximise their quality of life instead of duration of survival.

Against the background of the large numbers of old adults admitted to intensive care units (ICU) worldwide, there is a concerning scarcity of evidence and guidance for how to adjust decisions and interventions to the individual needs of these patients. Methodological issues led to the exclusion of geriatric patients from many clinical trials in intensive care [4], other trials generated substantial uncertainty caused by the heterogeneity of patients and the diversity of the healthcare systems involved [5]. This has resulted in an unwarranted variation of clinical decision-making for old ICU patients with significant ethical implications [6]. For a long time, experts have, therefore, called to develop a better understanding of the challenges in this field to tailor healthcare to the unique requirements of old individuals [7, 8].

After the European Society of Intensive Care Medicine (ESICM) published consensus-based recommendations for the management of very old ICU patients (age ≥ 80 years) [9], the Society of Critical Care Medicine (SCCM) has now issued guidelines on caring for older adults, aged 65 years or older, in the ICU [10]. Following the selection of five Population, Intervention, Comparator, and Outcomes (PICO) questions, the SCCM expert panel performed a literature search and used the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) framework to develop recommendations. Two of the questions concerned organisational challenges (geriatric models of care, post-ICU follow-up), and three dealt with specific interventions (mean arterial pressure (MAP) target for treating vasodilatory shock, prevention and treatment of delirium). Although the body of literature assessed by the panel was limited to the topics covered by the PICO questions, the results of this analysis illustrated the absence of high-quality evidence for managing older ICU patients. None of the findings from randomised controlled trials (RCTs) identified in this analysis was considered certain enough to support strong recommendations regarding any of the PICO questions.

Most importantly, the SCCM panel issued a conditional recommendation to support the use of geriatric models of care for all older adults admitted to the ICU. Despite the very low level of certainty of the evidence provided by two small RCTs and one pilot trial in this field, the SCCM panel assumed that geriatric models of care could be beneficial for older ICU patients based on evidence generated by other clinical specialties [10]. Of note, due to the lack of formal evidence, the SCCM panel did not issue any recommendation regarding post-ICU follow-up.

Concerning specific interventions in older ICU patients, the SCCM panel suggested not to use antipsychotic medications to prevent delirium in these individuals. This conditional recommendation was issued based on findings with very low certainty. Importantly, no recommendation could be made regarding the use of antipsychotic medication to treat delirium in older adults, contributing to the ongoing uncertainty in this field. Assessing the findings of a single RCT on MAP targets in older patients [11], the SCCM panel concluded that the benefit of targeting a lower MAP remains uncertain and did not issue a recommendation.

How do the new SCCM guidelines compare to previous ESICM recommendations (Fig. 1)? First, the latter dealt with the 'oldest old' patients where geriatric characteristics are most prevalent. Although the SCCM panel considered this cohort as a sub-group in their analysis, no specific recommendations were made for it. Thus, it remains to be clarified to what extent their recommendations will benefit the oldest old or geriatric patients rather than previously fit individuals at the lower end of the age spectrum. Second, the ESICM panel decided at an early stage that the current body of literature is insufficient to support an informative GRADE-based analysis which, however, could have provided evidence-based guidance. They performed a Delphi study instead to explore the empirical knowledge of experts to develop consensus-based recommendations. Despite adhering to the GRADE framework, the SCCM panel based the decision to recommend geriatric models of care on their judgement that findings in other specialties can be transferred to intensive care, and that undesirable effects for patients are trivial. Of note, the ESICM panel came to a similar conclusion and recommended implementing elements of geriatric care models and follow-up in clinical practice. Third, the SCCM panel issued a recommendation regarding the prevention of organ failure. The ESICM panel refrained from discussing details of organ support in the heterogeneous cohort of the 'oldest old' [9].

New guidance for managing older patients with critical conditions

The SCCM panel should be lauded for its meticulous search for the 'needle in a haystack' which led to an important recommendation about geriatric models of care. This recommendation is concordant between SCCM and ESICM. It addresses the importance of considering biological aging and its consequences in clinical practice. Thus, ICU teams should now reflect on integrating additional expertise to manage the complexity of older patients with geriatric characteristics. Further research is required to identify specific interventions to prevent and manage complications among these patients and improve outcomes aligned with the patients' preferences [12]. Both SCCM and ESICM addressed this need in their documents, and supporting research in this field should become a priority for funding. Although RCTs remain the gold standard to assess the benefit of even complex interventions, such as care models, the heterogeneity among older patients and the diversity of healthcare systems complicate this approach by interfering with study design and patient recruitment. Alternatively, 'real world evidence' can be provided by routine service delivery or by following suitable cohorts given that they cover a representative part of the population [13–15].
