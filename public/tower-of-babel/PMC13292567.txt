Impact of telemonitoring on glycemic control and quality of life in pediatric patients with type 1 diabetes, single center interventional control study
Authors: Lubna Fawaz, Nora Badawi, Mostafa Hasan, Radwa Shamma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTelehealth initiatives offer valuable alternatives for enhancing diabetes management and clinician-patient engagement. This study compared two diabetes education approaches for glycemic outcomes: standard method includes regular physical visits at outpatient clinic versus telemonitoring.MethodsWe conducted an interventional trial involving 140 pediatric patients with Type 1 diabetes (T1D), who were equally randomized into a telecounseling group (Intervention group, n = 70) and a standard care group (Control group, n = 70). Participants in Intervention group received weekly telephone consultations from diabetes educators, during which glucose records, dietary patterns, and insulin dose adjustments were reviewed. Control group received diabetes education during routine outpatient clinic visits. Both groups were followed up initially at the start of the study and at 6 months. Socioeconomic status and quality of life (QoL) were assessed in all participants.ResultsGlycemic control differed significantly between groups. Mean HbA1c values were 8.21 ± 1.82 SDS (Intervention group) versus 9.25 ± 2.22 SDS (Control group). Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) episodes were higher in Control group (18%) vs. (1.7%) in Intervention group, p = 0.001. Complication rates (lipodystrophy, proteinuria, dyslipidemia) were elevated in Control group (37.7%) vs. (15.5%) in Intervention group, p = 0.01. Intervention group demonstrated significantly improved QoL scores (p = 0.002).ConclusionThe telemedicine strategy helped to achieve significantly greater HbA1c reductions and fewer diabetes-related complications versus conventional care.Keywords: Telemonitoring, Glycemic control, Pediatric, Type 1 diabetes

Background

Health organizations face mounting challenges from rising T1D prevalence and the critical need to enhance related metabolic outcomes. Telehealth initiatives offer practical solutions to streamline disease management and communication between patients and clinicians [1]. Telemedicine employs digital communication technologies, including audio/video, short message service (SMS), multimedia messaging service (MMS), and internet platforms to remotely deliver monitoring, diagnosis, treatment, support, and consultation for geographically distant patients [2]. Continuous Glucose Monitoring (CGM) has become a transformative tool in telehealth and remote patient monitoring (RPM) strategies for diabetes care. Integration of CGM with telehealth, accelerated by the COVID-19 pandemic, improves clinical outcomes—such as lower Glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) and increased Time in Range (TIR) by providing real-time, actionable data to both patients and health care providers.

A meta-analysis of T1D and Type 2 diabetes (T2D) telemedicine interventions (> 3000 participants) demonstrated equivalent or superior effectiveness to conventional care. Significant HbA1c reductions occurred in both T1D and T2D groups versus standard management, alongside improved patient satisfaction with telehealth and decreased diabetes-related distress [3]. Notably, substantial awareness and acceptance of telehealth exists among Egyptian primary healthcare center users [4].

Our study was initiated during the lockdown of COVID-19 pandemic. At that time, the importance of telemedicine increased substantially. The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the extent to which telemonitoring improves glycemic control in pediatric patients with type 1 diabetes (T1D), to increase awareness and utilization of telemedicine services for the management of T1D, to reduce the psychological burden on families of children with T1D, to decrease the number of emergency department visits. The secondary objectives, including assessment of quality of life and evaluation of the potential impact of telemonitoring on diabetes management and family burden. To our knowledge, few studies have addressed Telehealth and the use of Telemonitoring in T1D care. So, our study is one of the few studies that addressed this topic.

Methods

This interventional-control study involved 140 children/adolescents with T1D attending a tertiary university hospital’s Pediatric Diabetes clinic; 21 participants withdrew during follow-up as shown in Fig. 1. The study was conducted over a 12-month period, from April 2021 to April 2022, with scheduled assessments at the start of the study and at 6 months.

CONSORT flow diagram for the enrolled patients

Eligible participants (ages 2–17 years) were assigned to either the intervention or control groups using a simple randomization technique based on a random number table. To ensure concealment, the allocation sequence was maintained by a study coordinator who was not involved in the direct clinical care or recruitment of the participants. Exclusion criteria included prior telemonitoring exposure or partial clinical remission. Partial clinical remission may follow the diagnosis of T1D, i.e., stage III of the disease, and this phase is marked by an increased functionality of the surviving β-cells with attendant endogenous insulin production [5]. Guardians provided written informed consent.

Teleconsultations were conducted by trained diabetes educators under the supervision of pediatric endocrinologists, ensuring consistency and appropriate clinical oversight. In addition, patient management followed standardized institutional protocols aligned with ISPAD guidelines [13], while still allowing individualized adjustments, when necessary.

Intervention group received mobile phone-based telemonitoring supported by pediatric diabetes educators trained in glycemic fluctuation management and dietary planning. This group also attended standard outpatient visits for HbA1c testing and documentation of hypoglycemic events, severe hyperglycemia, and diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) episodes at baseline, and 6 months.

Health educators in our study are the diabetes-educator team including nutritional specialists, who accomplished certified courses in diabetes education and care, in addition to physicians, nurses and psychologists. Their mobile numbers were available and easily accessible to our patients’ care providers throughout the day, managing their glycemic health status directly during phone call or through conversation mobile applications (i.e. WhatsApp), hasten their visit at our outpatient clinic or referring them to emergency department if needed.

Every patient in the intervention group was phoned at least once weekly to check their documentation of their glycemic readings, to know numbers of hypo or hyperglycemic episodes, and ascertain their follow-up with health educators. Other related issues regarding managing their disease such as accessibility to their medications and glucometer device strips were also revised and documented.

Glycemic readings of the week were photographed and sent using mobile conversation applications (i.e. WhatsApp) and being collected through the study.

The control group did not receive telemonitoring of their health status especially their glycemic profile, just attending their regular visits at the hospital outpatient clinic. Data regarding their glycemic profile (glycated hemoglobin, hypoglycemic, hyperglycemic and DKA attacks, etc.) were collected during their regular outpatient clinic visits.

The number of clinic visits was not restricted to a certain number or limited to certain time for both groups. It’s rather as per doctors’ recommendations with a minimum of one visit every three months.

Assessment of quality of life

The QoL assessment through PedsQL version 4 questionnaires covers the core dimensions of health [6]. It was evaluated at baseline and after completion of the study period. This includes 8 problems pertaining to physical function and activity, 5 problems related to emotional functioning, 5 problems related to social functioning and 5 related to school functioning. A score of less than 25% indicates most likely has no problems, 26–50% indicates sometimes has a problem, 51–75% indicates often faces problem and 76–99% most likely has a problem.

Discussion

Before COVID-19, telehealth represented under 1% of Medicare Fee-for-Service outpatient consultations in the US. By April 2020, however, it constituted 46% of all visits [14].

Our study included 119 children and adolescents with T1D, with a mean age of 8.68 ± 3.87 years. At the beginning of our study, the mean HbA1c of all studied participants was 8.7% (± 2.24 SD). After the intervention (Sessions of Telemonitoring), (intervention group) showed a reduction in HbA1c (from 8.61% to 8.21%), while (control group) showed an increase (from 8.80% to 9.25%). Although the reduction in Intervention group did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.18), it is worth noting that a decrease of 0.3% in HbA1c is potentially clinically relevant for reducing diabetic complications [15].

This may be explained that the current study was carried out during COVID-19 pandemic. During this period, many healthcare systems worldwide experienced significant strain, leading to reduced access to regular check-ups, diabetes education sessions, and face-to-face consultations [16]. This decrease in medical care and support could have negatively impacted diabetes management in our control group.

We also referenced our prior study demonstrating increased severity of diabetic ketoacidosis during the COVID-19 period compared to pre-COVID period with statistical significance (p = 0.018), likely attributable to delayed healthcare access and system limitations [17].

Several studies have reported similar findings during the pandemic. For instance, Marigliano and Maffeis [18], observed a mean increase of 0.4% in HbA1c levels among children with T1D in Italy during the lockdown period. They attributed this to reduced physical activity, changes in dietary habits, and psychological stress, all of which were common during the lockdown.

Furthermore, the pandemic-induced stress and anxiety could have directly affected glycemic control. Increased cortisol levels due to stress can lead to insulin resistance and elevated blood glucose levels [19]. This psychological aspect of the pandemic may have contributed to the observed increase in HbA1c in our control group. In contrast, our intervention group showed a slight improvement in HbA1c, suggesting that telemonitoring interventions might help mitigate the negative impacts of reduced in-person medical care during crises like the COVID-19 pandemic. This underscores the potential value of telemedicine in maintaining continuity of care and glycemic control in challenging circumstances.

Our results are comparable to other telemonitoring interventions in pediatric T1D. For instance, an integrative review from Guljas et al. [20], reported a reduction in HbA1c after a 3-month telemonitoring intervention from median 9.05% (range 8–11.3) to 8.9% (6.9–11.3). Our findings support Xu et al. [9], where telehealth implementation for T1D patients decreased average HbA1c levels and glucose fluctuations. Their study also indicated this approach yielded time efficiencies, cost reductions, and improved treatment adherence.

Certain research, primarily from developing nations, noted deteriorated metabolic control during lockdowns [21, 22]. Investigators connected this decline to telemedicine access barriers, limited technology adoption, lower educational attainment, supply chain disruptions for diabetes management, and heightened global stress, social isolation, and disease-related distress.

Lazzeroni et al. [23] documented overall mean HbA1c improvements. They also reported increased telemedicine interactions between patients and diabetes teams during lockdowns. When analyzing patients by pre-lockdown metabolic status and treatment, greater enhancements occurred among those with previously suboptimal HbA1c and those lacking integrated care systems.

In our study, we observed a significant improvement in the QoL score in intervention Group and control group with p-values (p = 0.002, 0.014) respectively. No statistically significant difference was observed in overall quality of life between the intervention and control groups (p = 0.16). This finding supports the potential psychological benefits of telemonitoring interventions. Similar improvements in QoL have been reported in other studies. For example, Zhang et al. [24] found that telemedicine interventions improved both glycemic control and QoL in adolescents with T1D. In agreement with Atitallah et al.. (2025), reported that several factors independently associated with impaired QoL, including a glycated haemoglobin level > 9%. T1DM is not exclusively a clinical and biological condition, but it also affects the psychological well-being of the child and the entire family dynamic [25]. Also, Stahl-Pehe et al., reported QoL was inversely associated with HbA1c after 3 years in the course of T1D only in patients poorly controlled at baseline [26]. This also agrees with previous studies with adolescents and children, where Shulman et al. [27] did not find differences in QoL between the telemedicine and control groups. We assume technologies have enhanced a lot since this research. Its usage and application in different aspects of life including healthcare have massively increased. This may explain the difference with this study Lazzeroni et al. [23] attributed their observation of no improvement in diabetes control or QoL to the decrease in mean weekly hours of physical activity.

In our study, although not statistically significant, we observed a trend towards fewer hypoglycemic and hyperglycemic episodes in intervention group post-intervention. This aligns with other studies showing that telemonitoring can help reduce glycemic excursions. For instance, Zhang et al. [24] reported a reduction in both hypoglycemic and hyperglycemic events with continuous glucose monitoring and telemedicine support.

Our study found a significant reduction in DKA episodes in intervention group during the study period compared to the year before (p = 0.002). This is a crucial finding, as DKA is a severe complication of T1D. Similar results were reported by Ilkowitz et al. [28], who found that telemedical support reduced DKA admissions in pediatric T1D patients.

Our findings suggest that the telemonitoring intervention could potentially help mitigate socioeconomic disparities in diabetes management.

Conclusion

The current study suggests potential benefits of telemonitoring in diabetes care, especially among pediatrics with T1D. Demonstrating that it may help improve glycemic control and quality of life. It may contribute to reducing diabetes-related complications and family burden.

Limitations and future directions

Future research should involve larger cohorts and extended follow-up durations. Multicenter investigations are likewise necessary to more comprehensively evaluate telemonitoring’s effects on glycemic management and life quality. Inclusion of effect size estimates and confidence intervals would further strengthen the analysis and should be considered in future studies. Socioeconomic factors were considered and were comparable between groups, while also acknowledging that more detailed multivariable adjustment could further strengthen future studies. Intervention group showed a trend toward poorer baseline clinical status (as reflected by higher DKA prevalence) at the beginning of the study, this is considered as a potential confounder. This baseline difference may have influenced the magnitude of HbA1c improvement observed. Adjustments or additional interventions implementation during follow-up visits (e.g., at 3 months) should be considered in future studies. Absence of intermediate HbA1c measurements during follow-up represents a limitation of the study, as serial assessments could have provided further insight into the trajectory and timing of glycemic changes associated with the intervention.
