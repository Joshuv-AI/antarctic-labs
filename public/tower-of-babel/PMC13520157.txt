SMC4 is a hypoxia-responsive driver of pulmonary arterial hypertension through an HIF-1α–HDAC6–p21 regulatory axis
Authors: Tingyue Lu, Ying Zhao, Jianan Zhang, Hongyu Chen, Yuxi Bai, Jinhong Jia, Huiting Chen, Jialian Yin, Shifan Chen, Wenting Wang, Huanliang Wang, Xiufeng Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is characterized by aberrant vascular remodeling driven in part by excessive proliferation of pulmonary arterial smooth muscle cells (PASMCs). However, the molecular determinants underlying this cancer-like phenotype remain incompletely defined.Methods and ResultsGenomic analysis of patients with PAH revealed a significant upregulation of structural maintenance of chromosomes 4 (SMC4). In hypoxia-exposed mouse models and human PASMCs, SMC4 expression was similarly increased and localized primarily to the vascular smooth muscle layer. Functional studies demonstrated that SMC4 knockdown suppressed, whereas SMC4 overexpression enhanced, microtubule organization, cell-cycle progression, and PASMCs proliferation. Mechanistically, chromatin immunoprecipitation confirmed direct binding of HIF-1α to the SMC4 promoter, promoting its transcription under hypoxia. Co-immunoprecipitation further showed that SMC4 recruited histone deacetylase 6 (HDAC6) to repress the cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor P21, thereby sustaining proliferative signaling. In vivo, partial genetic deficiency of SMC4 mitigated pulmonary vascular remodeling, reduced right ventricular hypertrophy, and improved hemodynamic parameters in hypoxia-induced PAH mice.ConclusionsSMC4 is a key regulator of PASMCs proliferation and pulmonary vascular remodeling through an HIF-1α-SMC4-HDAC6-P21 signaling axis. Modulation of this pathway may offer a potential therapeutic approach for PAH.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-026-03761-9.

Intoduction

Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is a rare but fatal disease that accounts for a considerable health-associated burden worldwide [1]. It is characterized by progressive narrowing and obstruction of the pulmonary arteries, driven by excessive vasoconstriction and abnormal vascular cell proliferation [2, 3]. These pathological changes elevate pulmonary arterial pressure and, if untreated, lead to right ventricular failure and premature death [4, 5]. Although multiple cell types within the pulmonary vasculature and adjacent lung tissues contribute to PAH pathogenesis, proliferation and fibrosis of pulmonary arterial smooth muscle cells (PASMCs) are the principal determinants of medial thickening, luminal narrowing, and increased hemodynamic burden [6].

Targeted pharmacological therapies have significantly improved clinical outcomes in PAH patients in recent years. However, monotherapy for PAH remains of limited efficacy, and combination therapies often involving two or three drugs tailored to disease severity and comorbidities are progressively becoming the standard of care. Despite these advances, reversing pulmonary vascular remodeling remains a major challenge in the field. Therefore, it is crucial to explore the molecular and cellular mechanisms driving pulmonary vascular remodeling in PAH patients. A deeper understanding of these mechanisms is essential for developing therapeutic strategies that provide broader and more durable clinical benefits. Notably, genomic analyses of PAH patient samples, including datasets GSE113439 and GSE53408, have revealed the upregulation of structural maintenance of chromosomes 4 (SMC4) expression in pulmonary tissues [7–9]. However, the dynamics of SMC4 expression and its mechanistic contribution to PAH remain largely unexplored.

Structural maintenance of chromosomes (SMC) proteins are highly conserved ATPases that play fundamental roles in chromosomal organization, mitotic progression, and genome stability [7]. Among these, SMC4, a core component of the condensin complex, has recently been recognized as a critical regulator of pathological cell proliferation. Extensive evidence from oncology demonstrates that SMC4 promotes tumor growth, invasion, and epithelial–mesenchymal transition through its ability to coordinate cell-cycle progression and transcriptional programs associated with cellular plasticity [8–15]. In the lung, SMC4 expression is tightly regulated during development but becomes aberrantly reactivated in lung adenocarcinoma, where it contributes to uncontrolled proliferation and extracellular matrix remodeling [16].

PAH is characterized by excessive proliferation and phenotypic remodeling of PASMCs, which exhibit cancer-like features, including hyperproliferation, apoptosis resistance, and metabolic reprogramming. Mechanistically, SMC4 governs cell-cycle progression by maintaining chromosomal integrity during mitosis, thereby enabling sustained proliferative capacity [13].Mechanistically, SMC4 governs cell-cycle progression by maintaining chromosomal integrity during mitosis, thereby enabling sustained proliferative capacity [17–21]. Notably, emerging evidence indicates that SMC4 activity is closely linked to epigenetic regulation, particularly through interactions with histone deacetylases (HDACs) [21, 22]. HDAC signaling is critically involved in PAH pathogenesis, in part by repressing p21 and p27 expression and promoting a proliferative vascular phenotype [23–27]. These findings raise the possibility that SMC4 may integrate chromosomal dynamics with epigenetic regulation to control PASMC behavior in PAH.

Based on these findings, we hypothesized that SMC4 promotes pulmonary vascular remodeling in PAH through an epigenetic mechanism involving HDAC6-mediated repression of p21 and p27, thereby sustaining PASMCs proliferation. To test this hypothesis, we examined the effects of SMC4 on chromosomal architecture, cell-cycle progression, and PASMC proliferation, and further investigated its interaction with HDAC6 and regulation of downstream cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors. By integrating in vitro and in vivo models of PAH, we aimed to delineate the mechanistic contribution of SMC4 to pulmonary vascular remodeling and to evaluate whether targeting this pathway could offer a novel therapeutic approach for PAH.

Materials and methods

Clinical sample collection

Plasma samples were obtained from 23 patients with PAH and 19 healthy volunteers at The First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Chongqing, China). This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Approval No.2024-043-01, approved on 2024.6.24), and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to sample collection. Additional details regarding sample processing and clinical characteristics are provided in Supplemental Table S1.

Animal models and study approval

The C57BL/6Jcya-Smc4em1/Cya (SMC4Het) mouse strain was obtained from Cyagen Biosciences Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China) and maintained under heterozygous breeding conditions. The Smc4 knockout mice were generated by Cyagen Company (Suzhou, China). Cas9 protein, four gRNAs (gRNA-A1: TCCCTAATCAGGGTACTTAA-AGG, gRNA-A2: TTTAATTGGTGTATTAAGCC-TGG, gRNA-B1: ATCTAATTTCTCTATAAACC-TGG, gRNA-B2: CTGTGTCCATCTCACTGAAG-GGG) flanked Exon 6 ~ 22 of mouse Smc4 were co-injected into fertilized eggs. The embryos were transferred to recipient female mice to obtain F0 mice. Identification information for the genetically modified mice is provided in Supplemental Table S2. F0 founder mice were crossed to C57BL/6J mice to produce heterozygous F1 (Smc4Het) offspring. Heterozygous intercrosses yielded only wild-type and heterozygous offspring, as the homozygous SMC4-null genotype is embryonically lethal; therefore, SMC4Het mice were used for all experiments. All animals were maintained on a C57BL/6J background under specific pathogen-free conditions at Chongqing Medical University.

Mice (C57BL/6, ~ 30 g) were subjected to either recoverable anesthesia or terminal procedures using isoflurane delivered via a small-animal anesthesia system (RWD). For recoverable procedures, anesthesia was induced with 2.0%-2.5% isoflurane and maintained at 1.5%-1.8%, allowing procedures such as echocardiography with spontaneous respiration, followed by monitored recovery. For terminal procedures, euthanasia was performed using 5% isoflurane until respiratory arrest. All protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Chongqing Medical University (IACUC-CQMU-2025-0096) and complied with relevant international guidelines.

Cell culture

Human PASMCs (CP-H243) and human pulmonary artery endothelial cells (PAECs; CP-H002) were purchased from Wuhan Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd. hPAECs were cultured in Endothelial Cell Medium (1001, ScienCell Research Laboratories, USA), and PASMCs in Smooth Muscle Cell Medium (1101, ScienCell). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ incubator. Cells at passages 2–5 were used for experiments. Hypoxic stimulation was performed in a controlled-oxygen incubator (1% O₂, 90% humidity) for 48 h, as previously reported [28, 29].

Cell transfection

PASMCs were transfected with Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. siRNAs targeting SMC4 and non-targeting controls (see Major Resources Table) were synthesized by JTS Scientific (Wuhan, China). The specific interference sequence information is provided in Supplemental Table S3.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA from cells and tissues was extracted using TRIzol (Invitrogen). Plasma RNA was extracted using a Whole Blood/Plasma/Serum RNA Kit (Yeasen 19241ES50). cDNA was synthesized using the Golden 1st cDNA Synthesis Kit (D0401, HaiGene). SYBR Green–based qPCR (Toyobo, Japan) was performed on a Roche LightCycler 480 II. Relative expression was calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCT method, normalized to β-actin. Primer sequences are provided in Supplemental Table S4.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Normality and homogeneity of variances were assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests or Welch’s t-tests, as appropriate. For non-normally distributed data, the Mann-Whitney U test was applied. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used. When assumptions of normality or equal variance were violated, the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post hoc test was performed. Correlation analyses were conducted using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient for non-normally distributed variables or Pearson’s correlation coefficient when both variables met normality assumptions. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

SMC4 is upregulated in pulmonary arterial hypertension and localizes to pulmonary arterial smooth muscle cells

To identify pathogenic genes associated with PAH, two lung tissue microarray datasets (GSE53408 and GSE113439) from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) were analyzed. After preprocessing, missing-value imputation, and batch correction, 18,809 genes were retained. Differential expression analysis of lung tissues from 22 healthy individuals and 27 patients with PAH (p < 0.05, |log₂FC| > 1.5) revealed 471 dysregulated genes (409 upregulated, 62 downregulated). These genes were further subjected to hierarchical clustering (Fig. 1A and B). Among the differentially expressed genes, SMC4 was notably upregulated. Given its marked elevation, we further examined SMC4 to characterize its expression profile and potential involvement in PAH. Analysis of serum samples demonstrated that SMC4 mRNA levels were significantly higher in patients with PAH than in healthy controls (Fig. 1C). Furthermore, correlation analyses revealed that SMC4 expression positively correlated with mean pulmonary arterial pressure (mPAP; Fig. 1D), systolic pulmonary arterial pressure (sPAP; Fig. 1E), systolic right ventricular pressure (sRVP; Fig. 1F), and pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR; Fig. 1G). The immunofluorescence staining of lung tissue samples from both healthy subjects and patients with pulmonary hypertension revealed distinct expression patterns of SMC4. In healthy subjects, SMC4 was detected in both endothelial cells and smooth muscle cells. In contrast, in patients with pulmonary hypertension, the fluorescence signal of SMC4 was significantly enhanced in the smooth muscle layer, with a marked increase in its co-localization with α-SMA (Fig. 1H).

We next evaluated SMC4 protein expression across multiple experimental models of PAH. SMC4 protein levels were consistently and markedly increased in both mouse models (hypoxia and SuHx; Fig. 1I and J) as well as in all three rat models (hypoxia, SuHx, and MCT; Figure S1A–S1C). Next, we examined SMC4 protein and mRNA expression in hypoxia-induced PASMCs and PAECs. Under hypoxic conditions, SMC4 expression were significantly upregulated in PASMCs, whereas its expression remained unchanged in PAECs (Fig. 1L and O). Meanwhile, SMC4 mRNA levels were significantly increased in lung tissue from hypoxic mouse models of PAH (Fig. 1K). Together, these findings suggest that SMC4 is upregulated in PASMCs during hypoxic stress and may contribute to vascular remodeling in PAH.

SMC4 haploinsufficiency attenuates hypoxia-induced PAH in vivo

Schematic diagram showing the generation of the hypoxia-induced SMC4 heterozygous PAH model (Fig. 2A). Because complete SMC4 knockout is embryonically lethal (Figure S2), heterozygous SMC4 (SMC4Het) mice were used. Compared with wild-type (WT) mice, SMC4Het mice exhibited significantly reduced SMC4 protein levels (Fig. 2B). In hypoxic WT mice, right ventricular systolic pressure (RVSP) and right ventricular hypertrophy (RVH) were markedly increased, whereas both parameters were significantly reduced in hypoxic SMC4Het mice (Fig. 2C and E). Echocardiography demonstrated decreased pulmonary artery acceleration time (PAAT) and pulmonary artery velocity time integral (PAVTI) in hypoxic WT mice, while these hemodynamic impairments were partially rescued in SMC4Het mice (Fig. 2F and H). Histological analysis revealed pronounced vascular wall thickening in hypoxic WT mice, whereas SMC4Het mice showed minimal remodeling (Fig. 2I and J). These results indicate that SMC4 haploinsufficiency protects against hypoxia-induced pulmonary vascular remodeling and hemodynamic deterioration.

SMC4 silencing suppresses microtubule organization, cell-cycle progression, and proliferation in PASMCs

Among the upregulated genes, SMC4 was selected for further investigation. Functional enrichment analysis suggested that SMC4 participates in chromosome segregation, DNA conformational changes, DNA unwinding, homologous recombination, and smooth muscle contraction pathways (Fig. 3A and B), indicating a potential role in mitosis, cell-cycle progression, and vascular cell proliferation. To assess the role of SMC4 in PASMCs under hypoxia, cells were transfected with SMC4 siRNA. Immunofluorescence staining of α-tubulin showed enhanced perinuclear fluorescence and microtubule aggregation in hypoxic hPASMCs, whereas SMC4 knockdown markedly reduced this effect (Fig. 3C). Flow cytometry revealed that hypoxia increased the proportion of cells in S and G2/M phases, whereas SMC4 silencing reduced these fractions (from 12.39% to 5.29% and 4.73%) and increased the G0/G1 fraction (from 74.46% to 82.66% and 83.21%) (Fig. 3D and E). Western blot analysis confirmed that hypoxia-induced upregulation of Cyclin A, Cyclin B1, and CDK1 was reversed by SMC4 knockdown (Fig. 3F and H). PCNA expression (Fig. 3I), CCK-8 viability assays (Fig. 3J), EdU incorporation (Fig. 3K), and Ki67 staining (Fig. 3L) consistently demonstrated that hypoxia enhanced PASMC proliferation, whereas SMC4 silencing attenuated this proliferative response. These data demonstrate that SMC4 is required for hypoxia-induced microtubule reorganization, cell-cycle progression, and PASMCs proliferation.

SMC4 overexpression enhances microtubule aggregation, cell-cycle progression, and proliferation in PASMCs

To further investigate its functional role, we overexpressed SMC4 in PASMCs. Immunofluorescence staining showed that SMC4 overexpression increased α-tubulin fluorescence intensity and microtubule aggregation (Fig. 4A). Flow cytometry demonstrated that SMC4 overexpression increased the proportion of cells in S phase (25.97% to 34.24%) while decreasing the G0/G1 fraction (67.96% to 54.47%) (Fig. 4B). Western blot analysis confirmed upregulation of Cyclin A, Cyclin B1, and CDK1 (Fig. 4C and E). PCNA expression (Fig. 4F), and CCK-8 viability assays (Fig. 4G), EdU incorporation (Fig. 4H), Ki67 staining (Fig. 4I), demonstrated enhanced proliferation in SMC4-overexpressing PASMCs. Together, these findings show that SMC4 overexpression promotes microtubule organization, accelerates cell-cycle progression, and drives PASMCs proliferation.

HIF-1α directly binds the SMC4 promoter and regulates SMC4 expression under hypoxia

Given the hypoxia-induced upregulation of SMC4 and the central role of HIF-1α in PASMCs pathobiology, we examined whether HIF-1α directly regulates SMC4 transcription. JASPAR analysis predicted a putative HIF-1α binding site within the SMC4 promoter (Fig. 5A). ChIP-PCR using five primer pairs spanning the promoter region confirmed significant HIF-1α enrichment at multiple sites, including the − 964/-823 bp region, as well as the − 1666/-1420, -399/-304, and − 101/+111 regions (Fig. 5B). PASMCs subjected to HIF-1α knockdown exhibited diminished hypoxia-induced SMC4 mRNA upregulation (Fig. 5C) and reduced SMC4 protein expression (Fig. 5D). These findings demonstrate that HIF-1α directly binds the SMC4 promoter and mediates its transcriptional activation during hypoxia.

HIF-1α–dependent SMC4 regulation contributes to pulmonary vascular remodeling in vivo

To assess the in vivo relevance of this regulatory axis, SMC4Het mice were administered AAV-HIF-1α in the SuHx model (Fig. 6A). SuHx exposure significantly increased RVSP in WT mice, whereas SMC4Het mice were largely protected. HIF-1α overexpression restored RVSP elevation in SMC4Het mice (Fig. 6B). Structural and functional indices including TAPSE, RVAW, and the RV/(LV + S) ratio were consistent with these hemodynamic findings (Fig. 6C and F). Histological analysis revealed marked pulmonary vascular wall thickening in hypoxic mice compared with the control group, whereas this remodeling was significantly attenuated in SMC4Het mice; notably, HIF-1αoverexpression on the SMC4Het background partially restored the vascular remodeling phenotype(Fig. 6G). Thus, reduced SMC4 expression protects against SuHx-induced right ventricular dysfunction, whereas HIF-1α-mediated SMC4 upregulation reverses this protection.

SMC4 interacts with HDAC6 and modulates its expression in PASMCs

Given prior data implicating HDAC6 in PASMCs proliferation, we investigated whether SMC4 regulates HDACs family members. Western blot analysis of PASMCs under hypoxia showed increased HDAC1, HDAC3, and HDAC6 expression, all of which were reduced by SMC4 knockdown. HDAC2 and HDAC8 showed no significant change (Fig. 7A and E). Consistent with these observations, overexpression of SMC4 resulted in a significant increase in HDAC6 protein levels (Fig. 7H), whereas no appreciable changes were observed in HDAC1 or HDAC3 (7 F-7G). In vivo, hypoxic WT mice exhibited increased HDAC6 expression in lung tissues, whereas this elevation was attenuated in SMC4Het mice (Fig. 7I). We further explored the structural basis of the SMC4-HDAC6 interaction. While AlphaFold 3 docking of the full-length proteins predicted a low-confidence direct interface (ipTM = 0.21, pTM = 0.41) (Fig. 7J), our biochemical evidence via endogenous co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays confirmed their robust interaction in PASMCs (Fig. 7K). These findings indicate that SMC4 recruits and regulates HDAC6 in PASMCs.

SMC4–HDAC6 interaction suppresses P21 expression and promotes PASMC proliferation

p21, a CDK inhibitor, restricts cell-cycle progression and inhibits proliferation through a PCNA-dependent mechanism [30]. In PASMCs, hypoxia reduced p21 expression, whereas SMC4 knockdown restored it (Figure S3A). Conversely, SMC4 overexpression further suppressed p21 under hypoxia (Figure S3B). To test whether HDAC6 mediates this effect, PASMCs were treated with the HDAC inhibitor trichostatin A (TSA). Hypoxia-induced p21 downregulation was reversed by 10 nM TSA (Figure S3C), prompting its use in subsequent experiments. TSA treatment blocked the SMC4-induced suppression of p21 (Figure S3D). Similarly, HDAC6 knockdown restored p21 levels suppressed by SMC4 overexpression (Figure S3E). In vivo, hypoxic WT mice showed decreased p21 expression in lung tissues, whereas SMC4Het mice exhibited the opposite pattern (Figure S3F). To investigate the molecular basis of HDAC6-mediated regulation in PASMCs, we performed AlphaFold 3-based structural docking. The HDAC6–p21 complex showed a moderate interface confidence (ipTM = 0.45, pTM = 0.58), suggesting a potentially dynamic interaction, whereas full-length HDAC6-SMC4 docking yielded a lower confidence score (ipTM = 0.21), likely due to the large coiled-coil structure of SMC4 and limitations of static modeling (Figure S3G). To validate these predictions, endogenous Co-IP assays in PAECs demonstrated that HDAC6 robustly interacts with both SMC4 and p21(Figure S3H). Together, these results indicate that despite the limited confidence of in silico predictions, the HDAC6–SMC4/p21 axis forms stable and functionally relevant interactions in PASMCs. Functionally, SMC4 promotes PASMC proliferation by recruiting HDAC6 to suppress p21, thereby relieving cell-cycle inhibition.

Discussion

In the present study, we identified SMC4 as a previously unrecognized regulator of PAH and delineated its pivotal role in disease initiation and progression. We demonstrated that SMC4 expression is markedly and consistently upregulated in lung tissues from patients with PAH as well as across multiple experimental models, with predominant in PASMCs. Notably, SMC4 mRNA levels measured in patient serum show strong correlations with established hemodynamic and diagnostic parameters, underscoring its potential clinical relevance. Functionally, partial genetic deletion of SMC4 significantly mitigated hypoxia- or SU5416/hypoxia-induced PAH, as evidenced by reductions in right ventricular systolic pressure, right ventricular hypertrophy, and pulmonary vascular remodeling. Mechanistically, we uncovered a hypoxia-responsive HIF-1α-SMC4-HDAC6-p21 signaling axis, in which HIF-1α directly drives SMC4 transcription; elevated SMC4 in turn promotes HDAC6-dependent suppression of p21, thereby circumventing cell-cycle checkpoint control. This cascade facilitates G1/S phase transition, aberrant microtubule, and excessive PASMCs proliferation, culminating in pathological vascular remodeling. Collectively, our findings establish SMC4 as a critical molecular link between hypoxia signaling and dysregulated PASMCs proliferation and reveal its previously unappreciated contribution to PAH pathogenesis.

In the present study, we demonstrate that partial loss of SMC4 confers a strong protective effect against PAH across distinct animal models. In the chronic hypoxia model, SMC4-heterozygous mice exhibited significantly lower RVSP, reduced right ventricular hypertrophy, improved pulmonary artery flow dynamics, and minimal pulmonary vascular remodeling, collectively indicating that SMC4 is a critical contributor to hypoxia-induced vascular pathology. Consistent findings were obtained in the more aggressive SuHx model, in which SMC4 deficiency again attenuated RV pressure elevation and preserved right ventricular structure and function. The reproducibility of this protective phenotype in both a moderate hypoxic stimulus and a severe model suggests that SMC4 acts as a convergent driver of pulmonary vascular remodeling and RV maladaptation, rather than a factor limited to a single pathogenic context. These results are consistent with several transcriptomic studies that have identified SMC4 as a hub gene associated with PAH [31, 32]. However, those studies remained at the level of bioinformatic prediction and lacked experimental validation of SMC4’s function and mechanism in pulmonary hypertension. Our in vivo data addresses this critical knowledge gap by providing direct causal evidence that SMC4 promotes pathological vascular remodeling and right-ventricular dysfunction.

Although SMC4 has been extensively characterized in malignant diseases [33], its role in non-malignant proliferative disorders remains largely undefined. In cancer, SMC4 is aberrantly upregulated across a broad spectrum of tumor types and acts as a core mitotic regulator through its condensin-dependent functions in chromosome condensation and segregation [15, 34, 35]. Beyond these canonical mitotic roles, SMC4 also contributes to DNA repair, recombination, and transcriptional regulation [36–38]and is indispensable for regulating cell proliferation, cell-matrix adhesion, and extracellular matrix organization [16]. Taken together, these observations highlight SMC4 as a central coordinator of cell-cycle control and tissue remodeling, providing a strong conceptual framework for investigating its involvement in PAH-a vasculopathy characterized by pathological hyperproliferation and phenotypic remodeling of PASMCs that exhibit a neoplastic-like proliferative phenotype, including aberrant mitosis, sustained cell-cycle activation, and excessive cell proliferation. Within this framework, our findings extend the biological relevance of SMC4 beyond oncology and identify it as a critical mediator of vascular remodeling. In PAH, SMC4 was selectively enriched and robustly upregulated in PASMCs, indicating smooth muscle–specific pathogenic involvement. Functionally, SMC4 silencing reduced G2/S-phase progression and downregulated key cell-cycle regulators, including Cyclin A, Cyclin B1, CDK1, and PCNA; these effects were fully reversed by SMC4 overexpression. Together with its established roles in chromosomal dynamics and genomic maintenance, these findings position SMC4 as a central orchestrator of PASMC hyperproliferation. More broadly, our results demonstrate that molecular programs traditionally linked to tumor biology also play pivotal roles in the maladaptive vascular remodeling characteristic of PAH.

Importantly, our study identifies an SMC4-dependent epigenetic pathway through which HDAC6 represses p21 to sustain PASMCs proliferation. Under hypoxic conditions, elevated SMC4 enhances HDAC6 recruitment, leading to p21 deacetylation and transcriptional suppression, thereby removing inhibitory control over the G1/S transition. This SMC4-HDAC6-p21 axis integrates seamlessly with the mitotic and genomic functions of SMC4, providing a coherent mechanism that links hypoxia-induced transcriptional activation to cell-cycle engagement. The broader literature supports this model: HDAC6 is widely recognized as a promoter of cellular proliferation through suppression of p21, and HDAC inhibition in vascular cells increases p21 expression and induces cell-cycle arrest [39–42]. These observations reinforce our conclusion that HDAC6 acts downstream of SMC4 to promote the hyperproliferative PASMCs phenotype. Collectively, these findings indicate that SMC4 is not only a mitotic regulator but also an upstream epigenetic modulator that interfaces with HDAC6 to repress p21 in the hypoxic vascular environment. This previously unrecognized signaling axis provides a mechanistic bridge between hypoxia sensing, chromosomal architecture, and cell-cycle checkpoint release.

To mechanistically substantiate this axis, we integrated structure-based prediction with biochemical validation to identify SMC4 and p21 as novel HDAC6-interacting partners. The moderate ipTM for HDAC6–p21 (0.45) is consistent with the intrinsically disordered nature of p21 and its dynamic “folding-upon-binding” interactions, which are typically underestimated by static models. In contrast, the low ipTM for HDAC6–SMC4 (0.21) likely reflects the extended coiled-coil architecture and conformational plasticity of SMC4, suggesting a context-dependent or indirect interaction. Despite these limitations, endogenous Co-IP assays demonstrated robust associations between HDAC6, SMC4, and p21, supporting the existence of a functional HDAC6-SMC4/p21 regulatory complex. Collectively, these findings establish this axis as a mechanistic bridge linking hypoxia signaling, chromatin architecture, and cell-cycle checkpoint release in pulmonary vascular remodeling.

In hypoxia-driven PAH, we further establish SMC4 as a bona fide transcriptional target of HIF-1α and a key effector linking hypoxic signaling to PASMCs cell-cycle control. Motif prediction identified a conserved HIF-1α-binding element within the SMC4 promoter, and ChIP-qPCR confirmed direct binding under hypoxic conditions. Inhibition of HIF-1α markedly attenuated hypoxia-induced SMC4 upregulation, supporting a direct transcriptional regulatory relationship. In addition to elucidating how HIF1α regulates SMC4 at the cellular level in vitro, we further established in vivo that SMC4 functions as a key downstream mediator of HIF1α in pulmonary hypertension. These findings expand the classical role of HIF-1α as a master regulator of the hypoxic response by revealing an HIF-1α-SMC4 axis that couples hypoxia sensing to condensin-dependent chromosomal organization and cell-cycle progression. Given that HIF-1α regulates PASMCs proliferation through various targets, such as Cx43 [43], CD36 [44], and mitochondrial fission mediators [45, 46]. However, both previous studies and our results indicate that SMC4 is not the only downstream target of HIF-1α. Instead, SMC4 appears to be integrated into a broader HIF-dependent network, which contributes to pulmonary vascular remodeling in PAH.

Our initial strategy was to generate a systemic SMC4 knockout mouse model based on its broad expression in vascular-associated cells. However, breeding experiments showed that none of the 30 offspring from three breeding pairs were homozygous, and both previous reports and our 3.5-day blastocyst analysis confirmed that homozygous SMC4 deletion causes early embryonic lethality (see Supplementary Figure S1). This made a systemic knockout model unfeasible. We therefore employed SMC4 heterozygous mice, which survive to adulthood and enable in vivo assessment of the consequences of partial SMC4 reduction on vascular biology. This model also closely reflects a haploinsufficiency-like state that may be relevant to human pathological conditions. Although residual SMC4 expression may mask some phenotypes and activate compensatory pathways, the heterozygous model represents the most appropriate and biologically meaningful approach currently available. We acknowledge the lack of a conditional knockout model as a limitation, and future studies incorporating tissue-specific and inducible approaches will help refine the cell type–specific functions of SMC4.

Taken together, our findings position SMC4 as a central integrator that translates hypoxia-driven signaling into sustained proliferative remodeling through coordinated regulation of chromatin architecture and cell-cycle progression in PASMCs. Beyond serving as a downstream effector of HIF-1α, SMC4 likely functions as a chromatin-level amplifier that stabilizes pathological transcriptional programs under hypoxic stress, thereby reinforcing HDAC6-mediated suppression of p21 and facilitating persistent checkpoint escape. This integrative role is particularly relevant in the context of PAH, where durable epigenetic and transcriptional reprogramming underlies the quasi-neoplastic phenotype of vascular cells. The observation that partial genetic reduction of SMC4 is sufficient to confer protection across distinct experimental models further suggests that SMC4-dependent chromatin remodeling is not merely permissive but required for full disease manifestation, highlighting a potential therapeutic window in which modulation—rather than complete inhibition—may achieve efficacy while preserving essential genomic functions. At the same time, the lack of cell type-specific resolution leaves open the possibility that SMC4 also participates in broader vascular niche remodeling, potentially influencing intercellular signaling, extracellular matrix dynamics, or stress-adaptive responses beyond PASMCs. In this regard, future studies integrating conditional genetics with single-cell and spatial epigenomic approaches will be critical to determine whether SMC4 orchestrates a unified chromatin reprogramming landscape across multiple vascular compartments. Importantly, the concordant upregulation of SMC4 in human disease and its association with hemodynamic severity support its clinical relevance and raise the possibility that SMC4 may serve as both a mechanistic driver and a biomarker of disease progression.

Collectively, these findings extend the conceptual framework of hypoxia-associated pulmonary vascular remodeling by implicating condensin-mediated chromatin regulation as a key layer of control and identify SMC4 as a tractable node for future translational investigation in pulmonary hypertension.
