 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 In order to fully exploit algae carbon assimilation for fuels, a preqrequisite is high photosynthetic
 efficiency and carbon conversion to fuel precursors. In the context of this collaborative project
 between NLR and EMRE we aim to address aspects of both the biological constraints around
 photosynthesis-driven carbon assimilation flux as well as elements that bridge the gap between
 laboratory cultivation and meaningful outdoor deployment, building on the unique capabilities of
 NLR and EMRE’s algae bioengineering teams. The goal of this work is to improve our
 understanding of key cellular biochemistry, mass cultivation, and biomass conversion constraints
 that presently limit the potential bio-oil yield. The proposed first phase of this work will focus on
 biomass growth improvements by testing hypotheses for alleviating identified metabolic and
 photophysiological bottlenecks. The results of this work could inform decisions by stakeholders
 (e.g., industry, community, investors) on the design and deployment of a sustainable biofuel
 supply chain from algae.

 Summary of Research Results:

 Task 1: Develop a genetic engineering approach for photosynthetic improvement through a
 comparative study of the photophysiological and carbon flux differences between the two
 variants of P. celeri to identify a metabolic basis for improved utilization of light energy
 and distribution of fixed carbon.

 • Carry out comparative ‘omics’ analysis of the P. celeri improved cultivar relative to sister
 cultivars (from the same enrichment culture), including geonomics, transcriptomics,
 metabolomics and proteomics analyses to elucidate major changes underpinning rapid,
 inter-cultivar growth rate improvements. The initial goal will be to determine whether the
 specific enzymes and regulators, in particular photosynthetic proteins, found in the prior
 study of growth variants of Synechoccus play a similar role in P. celeri variants. Reverse
 genetics on these targets will be included in Task 2 and mature bioinformatics pipelines
 will be used to rapidly determine additional targets for metabolic engineering to be
 carried out in Task 2.
 • Analyze CO2 uptake between the two P. celeri strain cultivars, using customized
 membrane inlet mass spectrometry (MIMS) instrumentation to identify carbon uptake
 processes supporting the more rapid biomass accumulation phenotype observed. This
 system will be upgraded with a monochromator to include an analysis of the action
 spectrum of photosynthesis, where physiological activity is measured (in this case CO 2
 and O2 evolution rates) for different wavelengths of light.
 • Assess metabolic carbon assimilation by developing CO2-based fluxomics model for
 carbon pathways in P. celeri, identifying metabolic bottlenecks. Seek to understand the
 low lipid basal productivity of P. celeri, and to identify any differences in the two
 variants of P. celeri.

 The outcome of task 1 will be a number of high-impact journal articles describing the
 fundamental photosynthetic underpinning of fast-growing algae. Where appropriate to
 commercialization, intellectual property filings will precede the publication.

 Task 1 Results:

 To support the development of strain improvement strategies and probe photosynthetic carbon
 assimilation in select P. celeri algae cultivars, we carried out detailed physiological comparison
 experiments.

 The experimental system was centered around two variants of Picochlorum celeri as
 representatives of high-yielding, fast growing algae, with the idea that fundamental mechanisms
 elucidated with these comparisons would be transferable to other algae and likely terrestrial
 plants for improved improved autotrophic carbon assimilation rates. The P. celeri strain
 designated as TG2 has one of the fastest reported specific growth rates for a microalgae species
 of 0.34 h−1 (roughly translating to a 2 h doubling time) after rapid acclimation to high irradiance
 (1000 μmol m−2 s−1)(1–3). High light selection also produced a closely related P. celeri isolate,
 TG1, that presents a significantly lower specific growth rate than TG2 of 0.22 h−1. In
 combination, these two strains represent a powerful study system to elucidate genetic and
 metabolic diel carbon allocation control mechanisms.

 The objective of this work was to elucidate the genetic and metabolic regulating mechanisms
 governing growth, light response, and composition differences of two isolates, or variants, of P.
 celeri, TG1 and TG2, whose genomes are very closely related, but exhibit a consistently
 different physiological, and transcriptional phenotype. High-light (HL) stress, under uniquely
 controlled environmental conditions, was used to elicit the fast growth and high starch
 accumulation phenotype, in TG2 and TG1, respectively. Closely coordinated transcriptomics
 with fluxomics analysis allows for deconvoluting an extensive covariance matrix of the
 physiological response to identify a global regulatory mechanism. We then demonstrated the
 impact of the identified regulator on the global genetic and photosynthetic carbon allocation
 phenotype of an engineered strain that may have implications towards a translatable central
 control system in higher plants

 To specifically look for unique and differential gene expression behavior in the light response of
 both isolates, several different analyses were carried out to isolate the interaction effect between
 light and strain for the 1 h time point. The majority of significantly changed transcript levels after
 1 h in were down regulated relative to the LL levels in each of the wild type strains (Fig. 1.1A,
 B). There was not a single transcript which was changed in the opposite direction between TG1
 and TG2 after applying the same fold change and significance filtering thresholds (Fig. 1.1C).
 With a carefully-designed likelihood ratio test on the full transcriptome, we were able to identify
 several transcripts that exhibited a statistically significant interaction effect in their expression
 response to high light transition and genetic background (Fig. 1.1D). The single most significant
 gene identified in this analysis (EMRE3EUKT4064225), was annotated as a transcription factor
 encoding a homolog of late elongated hypocotyl/circadian clock associated like (LHY/CCA) and
 was strongly separated from the rest of the transcriptome.

 The identification of metabolic control points over carbon allocation from the photosynthetic
 source across the CBB and central carbon metabolism is important to define the fate of fixed
 carbon. Accurate estimation of intracellular fluxes in the metabolic network (e.g., through
 Instationary metabolic flux analysis (INST-MFA15, or fluxomics with 13C stable isotope
 tracking of metabolic carbon) is a critical component of carbon allocation optimization by
 identifying metabolic bottlenecks in algae and plants (4, 5). Using advanced MFA methods, we
 were able to assess metabolic carbon assimilation by developing CO2-based fluxomics model for
 carbon pathways in P. celeri, identifying metabolic bottlenecks.

 The remainder of the comparative discussion is included in the task 2 descriptions, where
 genetically engineered cultivars of P. celeri, where the Picochlorum CCA1 from the TG2
 cultivar was transferred to the TG1 cultivar, and continued comparative analyses are reported in
 the engineered (TG1MYB99) cultivar relative to the respective parental strains.

 We used established genetic engineering tools for P. celeri to test the impact of the genome-wide
 regulatory CCA1 function in P. celeri as related to both growth and carbon allocation dynamics
 by creating a genetically engineered variant of TG1 with the TG2-WT CCA1 gene elements
 transformed into a TG1-WT background using an expression vector containing the TG2 CCA1
 coding sequence under control of the TG2 native promoter and terminator regions. The cell line
 with the highest specific growth rate in high-light conditions was selected from a screen of
 transformants for further analysis and was designated TG1-MYB99.

 A four-hour time course RNA sampling during transition from LL to HL was conducted to
 determine the transcriptional regulatory alterations in the TG1-MYB99 strain. All strains
 demonstrated a biphasic acclimation to the increased light intensity, with the largest shift in gene
 expression occurring 30 min and 1 h after HL and gene expression trending back towards LL
 levels by the fourth hour (Fig. 2.2A). A persistent down-regulation in the TG1-WT cultures was
 observed, while TG2-WT maintained the intermediate level observed under LL illumination
 (Fig. 2B). The time course of CCA1 gene expression dynamics of the TG1-MYB99 strain
 confirms the successful transfer and expression of TG2 gene elements, starting with high-
 expression levels as for WT TG1 and upon HL transition shifting to follow the continued high
 expression as observed for TG2 (Fig. 2.2B). The global transcriptomics response over the 4 h
 time course similarly positions the TG1-MYB99 strain as intermediate between TG1 and TG2,
 with a significant fraction of the transcriptome following closely the expression patterns
 observed in TG2 (Fig. 2.2C). Over-representation statistical analysis of the set of 49 genes that
 closely matched CCA1 expression dynamics between the cell lines implicated important
 functions related to biotin metabolism (fatty acid biosynthesis, amino acid catabolism), broad
 central carbon metabolism, and circadian rhythms (Fig. 2.2D).

 To assess the respective biomass composition response to light transition, we quantified the
 primary macromolecular biochemical composition of harvested algal biomass after acclimation
 to low or high light. Carbon allocation varied dramatically between the cell lines in response to
 HL conditions, with TG1-WT accumulating as much as 44.9% of its total biomass as
 carbohydrate, while TG2-WT only reached 16.0% after 48 h of growth in HL. The TG1-MYB99
 mutant line cells, remarkably, apportioned over 56% of their biomass to carbohydrate content
 after the same 48-h acclimation period to HL growth.

 To quantify metabolite fluxes through central nodes of carbon assimilation and storage, we
 applied 13C labeling enabled INST-MFA of TG1 and TG2, and the TG1-MYB99 mutant. This
 quantitative analysis aims to elucidate key differences in metabolism. Because 13C-labeled
 CO2 is challenging to introduce in an immediate and step-change manner (instantaneous change
 to 13C from 12C for subsequent carbon fixation) that allows for following the 13C tracer over a
 time course15, we used a bolus of 13C labeled bicarbonate (1 g L-1 NaH13CO3) in a series of
 physiologically-controlled experiments. To prevent metabolite leakage by quenching methods
 such as cold methanol quenching, we rapidly filtered cells and quenched in a liquid N2 bath
 within 10 s of the timepoints. The same metabolic network was used for all three strains and
 includes compartmentalization and dilution parameters to account for inactive metabolite pools
 or metabolite channeling, and the INCA 2.0 software package was used to calculate the flux
 values based on the time course labeling data and solved for the biomass composition. The fitted
 models estimated CO2 uptake from the sink demands and was not supplied as an input parameter.
    TG1, TG1-MYB99, and TG2 had a CO2 uptake of 5.96, 6.99, and 9.59 mmol g
 biomass−1 h−1 respectively.

 TG1 and TG1-MYB99 have a high carbohydrate content which is consistent with the higher flux
 from F6P in the chloroplast to G6P followed by conversion to G1P and subsequently
 carbohydrates compared to TG2. The normalized flux from 3PGA as the first product formed
 after carbon fixation to F6P is not significantly different in the three strains, and a flux difference
 is only observed in subsequent conversion of F6P to carbohydrates. The conversion of F6P to
 G6P is catalyzed by the reversible G6P isomerase (EMRE3EUKT4065295) followed by transfer
 of the phosphate group to position 1 to form G1P catalyzed by phosphoglucomutase
 (EMRE3EUKT4067541). This is subsequently converted to ADPG by G1P-adenylyl-transferase
 (EMRE3EUKT4064735, EMRE3EUKT4063059). Transcriptomics analysis suggests that the
 expression of these enzymes is at least 2-fold higher in TG1 and TG1-MYB99 compared to TG2.
 This higher expression contributes to the higher flux to carbohydrates in these strains and may
 suggest that their expression is co-regulated. Expression of PGI and G1P adenylyl-transferase is
 similar in both TG1 and TG1-MYB99 whereas, in the case of PGM, TG1 has ~2.5-fold higher
 expression while TG1-MYB99 has a ~4 fold higher expression compared to TG2. This indicates
 that CCA1 expression further increases carbohydrate accumulation through the control over
 PGM.

 The distinct biomass composition and metabolic demands for these strains, including cellular
 ATP and NADPH demands, were calculated based on the known metabolic reactions. Linear
 electron flow (LEF) produces ATP/NADPH in the ratio 1.29 based on the coupled electron-
 proton reactions that power ATP synthase to generate 3 ATP per 14 protons pumped53. The
 CBB cycle, on the other hand, requires a higher demand for ATP/NADPH in the ratio 1.554.
 This imbalance in production and consumption has been suggested as a point of engineering in
 photosynthesis and forms the basis of the argument that cyclic electron flow (CEF), or an
 alternative flux of electrons to generate more ATP, is important to balance the ATP/NADPH
 synthesis and consumption.

 The ATP/NADPH ratio is of great importance since an imbalance between the synthesis and
 consumption of ATP and NADPH is directly linked to photosynthesis. We leveraged the detailed
 information regarding ATP and NADPH cofactor requirements defined by the INST-MFA flux
 modelling results to estimate the total energy metabolite demands by each cell line under high
 light conditions. This analysis showed that TG2 consumes ATP/NADPH in the ratio of 1.58
 whereas this number is 1.71 and 1.69 for TG1 and TG1-MYB99 respectively. As the demands on
 each of these energy carrier pools are linked to different mechanisms in photosynthetic energy
 balancing, we conducted photophysiology experiments to enable the characterization of the
 electron flow.

 Acclimation responses of the photosynthetic apparatus and related mechanisms can often underly
 the ability of photoautotrophs to resist abiotic stresses including excessive light intensities. P.
 celeri TG1 and TG2 cells exhibited remarkably low non-photochemical quenching (NPQ)
 response after acclimation to high light, and particularly when measured using a pulse amplitude
 modulated (PAM) fluorometry instrument under 1000 µE actinic light. We developed custom
 protocols using a fast repetition rate fluorometer (FRRf) that could deliver much higher actinic
 and saturating pulse light intensities to induce quenching responses and allowed accurate
 interpretations based on fully activated NPQ. The requirement for this unusually high light
 intensity regime was validated by remarkably low NPQ for each strain around the range of 0.5,
 compared to plants such as A. thaliana reaching over 2.0 on the classically determined scale.
 10

 We then tested whether the strains exhibited variations in alternative electron flow (AEF)
 pathways using the newly derived FRRf protocol in conjunction with well defined small
 molecule inhibitors of specific elements of different pathways governing the fates of electron
 energies. The plastid terminal oxidase (PTOX) pathway directly oxidizes plastoquinone pools,
 and its activity is inhibited by octyl gallate in vivo. The strong NPQ reduction in TG2 cells when
 challenged with OG (Fig. 2.4.A) indicated that the rapidly responsive PTOX mechanism
 contributes to the extremely high capacity to grow immediately upon high light exposure.
 However, PTOX has been shown to generate superoxide, making it a poor long term energy
 dissipation method. The antimycin A (AA) sensitive CEF pathway is a more sustainable solution
 to handling the excessive driving energy experienced by the photosynthetic electron transfer
 chain (PETC) apparatus under high light intensity, and contributed approximately half of the
 NPQ capacity exhibited by TG1-WT cells (Fig. 2.4.B), as observed by NPQ reduction in
 acclimated cells. The lack of this dynamic in TG2 and the TG2-CCA1 containing TG1-MYB99
 call for an alternative explanation for their tolerance to large photon flux rates. This can
 reasonably be attributed to alterations in overall biochemistry that favors NADPH relative to
 ATP needs, which may speak to an overarching tendency for algal species to be more
 proteinaceous during rapid growth during which linear electron flow can be accelerated along
 with NADPH consumption and thereby pulling excessive energy out of the PETC.

 Task 3 Results:

 In order to test various cultivation configurations and help guide the design and future
 installation of large scale ponds, a dedicated task was devoted to building the simulation
 environment for computational fluid dynamics (CFD) modeling.

 CFD models were developed and applied to show how well mixed large raceway ponds are,
 identify the characteristics that could influence algal photophysiological response to the mixing,
 and identify potential approaches to improve productivity by changing the physical mixing by
 means that are consistent with economics of large-scale pond operations.

 13

 The design of the open raceway ponds used for these simulations is based the most common
 designs represented in the literature and uses a paddlewheel to circulate the water. The depth of
 typical ponds is between 15 and 40 cm, this allows for adequate light exposure, but depending on
 the climate conditions and the system configuration could promote water loses. Figure 1 shows
 the typical open raceway pond characteristics. The system described in this work is a 0.1 acre
 pond (14.2 m x 55 m) built in California USA. The pond was operated at 0.2 m water depth
 although other water depths are possible according to the seasons, walls of the reactor having a
 total height of 0.4 m. The reactor was built using a 1.5 mm polyethylene sheet arranged into the
 ground directly. The velocity of the paddlewheel was controlled by an inverter connected to an
 engine of 1.5 kW. Illustrations of the pond characteristics, configuration and computational mesh
 used are included in Figs. 3.1 and 3.2.

 The flow dynamics in the pond requires an appropriate turbulence model to represent the
 different interactions between walls and paddlewheel and specifically requires specialized
 numerical algorithms to represent the motion and liquid phase interactions. The mathematical
 equations of motion and the computational framework used for their solution has been reported
 in a manuscript draft, and can be made available. The fluid dynamics of the system is captured
 by solving mass and momentum transport equations, that take into account the weighted
 contribution of density and viscosity of the liquid phase. A Stationary and a rotating mesh
 domain are used to allow for the relative motion between the paddlewheel and the water in the
 pond. The Arbitrary Mesh Interface (AMI) is used as a boundary condition to couple the flow
 between stationary and moving zones. To simulate the system we use the open-source CFD tool
 OpenFOAM version 8, with the interFoam solver, which is capable of solving unsteady,
 multiphase flow considering the rotational movement of the mesh domain around the
 paddlewheel. A constant pressure boundary condition is applied to the channel top surface which
 is open to the air. The channel walls and spokes of the paddles are defined as non-slip boundaries
 by specifying zero velocity in all directions. The set of mathematical equations essentially
 represent the volume of fluid (VOF) method, which is additionally coupled to a compression
 algorithm to prevent smearing of the phase fractions. The pond simulations were run using the
 HPC computational resources at NLR. Because of a significant computational resource
 requirement, we made computational approximations for the paddlewheel, and assumed that the
 velocity profiles could be used as velocity boundary conditions for a non-AMI mesh, which,
 when parameterized as periodic functions, can be used as the inlet boundary for the ponds (and
 reducing the computational burden by 5x).

 The comparison of experimental and simulation results are shown, and indicate reasonable

 velocity (z) shows only small variations in the coordinates for both experiments and simulation.
 Mixing in the vertical direction is relevant to the dynamics of light exposure. More mixing is
 typically related to more efficient photosynthetic processes. While the simulations appear
 accurate in representing the vertical direction mixing, the overall mixing is poor, and suggest that
 most of the longitudinal movement of the algae suspensions are happening in a stratified
 laminar-like flow.

 To improve vertical mixing, we tested the implementation of suspended baffles in the liquid
 phase, in the shape of delta wings. This approach has been reported before, where deflector
 baffles were added to the bottom of a small scale pond (10 m long, 0.93 m width per channel) to
 improve mixing (6) For the computational study here, we used 3x2.6” (LxW) delta wings, tilted
 25 degrees facing the incoming flow upstream to the channel, and one array composed of 29
 delta wings (Fig. 3.4). Initial analyses indicate that the vertical mixing at the location of the
 installed delta wings is increased relative to the baseline laminar flow (Fig. 3.5), with the higher
 turbulence assumed to increase light-access for the suspended algae particles.

 To achieve the increased mixing with the delta wings, increased power is required to move water
 around the raceway pond. The turbulent dissipation (rate at which energy is dissipated in the
 system) is one metric that is considered an indicator of local turbulent, and enhances mixing, and
 is increased in the presence of the delta wings. The dissipation rate increases close to the array
 and decays rapidly thereafter, reaching up to 1.7X higher rates in the presence of the wings.
 Using the simulation CFD models, the head loss and drag loss for submerged bodies was
 calculated. Assuming a fixed paddlewheel rotation, a 17% increase in power is needed when the
 delta wings are installed, reaching approximately 0.175 W/m2 for the delta wings case, relative
 to an average of 0.15 W/m2 for the base case (for the 494 m2 cultivation ponds).

 We were unable to apply algae growth models into the simulation of fluid mixing due to staff
 turn over and alternate priorities. This remains an important future area of development for algae
 production pond optimization. to inform the efforts in task 2 on how to optimize light-driven
 biomass productivity and translate fundamental observations on photosynthesis efficiency and
 carbon assimilation to large-scale algae cultivation.

 Task 4: Techno-Economic Analysis (TEA) modeling for ExxonMobil process configuration

 NLR will modify its existing process/TEA models for both algae cultivation and conversion to
 reflect the process configuration as envisioned by ExxonMobil for commercial scale0up of algal
 biofuels technology concept (Figure 4.1). Namely, for cultivation this will entail updating the
 NLR models to reflect a larger farm scale (up to 20,000 acres), nutrient/CO2 inputs reflective of
 high-lipid, low-protein biomass compositions at final harvest, impacts for full pond liners, and all
 dewatering done via hollow-fiber membrane systems (versus NLR’s three-stage dewatering
 process), as well as other modifications relative to NLR’s published algae farm “design case”
 (https://www.nrel.gov/docs/fy16osti/64772.pdf). Likewise, for conversion this will entail
 modifying NLR’s “Combined Algae Processing” (CAP) model framework (for example as
 published in https://www.nrel.gov/docs/fy14osti/62368.pdf) to reflect a more simplified
 processing schematic composed of wet cell disruption via steam lysis and lipid
 extraction/upgrading (hydrotreating) to hydrocarbon fuels, with the remaining extracted residual
 biomass sent to drying and sale for animal feed markets, e.g. as a soy meal replacement.

 Task 4 Results:

 As part of this task 4 effort, techno-economic analysis (TEA) modeling was conducted to
 estimate key economic metrics including minimum biomass selling price, minimum fuel selling
 price, and conversion processing internal rate of return (IRR) based on exercising NLR’s TEA
 models for algal biomass production and conversion to fuels (7, 8), modified to reflect
 Exxon/SGI-envisioned process targets for a future nth-plant 10,000 bbl/day commercial facility
 scale. Figure 4.1 indicates the baseline cultivation model (a) and the process modifications
 specifically requested by Exxon for this analysis for the downstream conversion.

 19

 The results from the analysis calculated the modeled minimum fuel selling price (MFSP) for a
 base case scenario maximizing diesel fuel output, increasing to $4.39/gal for an alternate
 scenario maximizing jet fuel production, in both cases based on a targeted strain performance
 projected to achieve exceptionally high cultivation productivity rates (31 g/m2/day annual
 average biomass productivity) and lipid content (80% ash-free lipid composition = 25 g/m2/day
 lipid productivity) coupled with a simple conversion process focused on lipid recovery and
 upgrading to fuels. Total capital investment for the integrated facility was estimated at roughly
 $4.6 billion for the max diesel case, or $4.9 billion for the max jet case associated with an
 additional hydrocracking step, with the majority of the costs for either case attributed to biomass
 production/harvesting at $3.8 billion. These MFSP estimates include a number of renewable fuel
 policy credit factors as may be supported when the analysis was carried out (October 2021) or in
 the near-future, without which the MFSPs would increase to $6.07/gal and $6.81/gal for the
 respective cases (this represents a key difference from prior NLR models which historically have
 not assumed policy credit inclusions, but instead required pursuing more costly and complex
 biorefinery configurations to produce high-value coproducts as an alternative means of reducing
 MFSPs). Sensitivity analysis scenarios are also presented highlighting impacts to both
 cultivation and conversion economics across a number of alternative conditions/performance
 levels.

 As typical for algal biomass production, the production ponds constitute the largest contribution
 to the MBSP, driven in turn by capital expenses (Fig 4.2),Fig however, this contribution is
 somewhat diminished relative to NLR’s standard TEA models given the use of larger 20-acre
 ponds assumed here (though this is outweighed by higher expenses with full pond liners). The
 second-largest contributor is from CO2 and nutrient costs, the large majority (97%) of which is
 driven by CO2 given the high C and low N/P content of the biomass assumed here. Dewatering
 also constitutes a large share of the MBSP contribution, reflective of the use of membranes for
 primary (and only) dewatering which are considerably more costly per volume throughput than
 the NLR basis model assumption with primary gravity settling (though recognizing gravity
 settling is both strain- and composition-dependent and may not be practical for this situation).
 Inoculum, CO2 storage, and water circulation pipelines all make up a relatively small
 contribution to system costs, although as discussed with Exxon/SGI, the latter for water
 circulation may be smaller than the Exxon/SGI costs because water circulation is achieved in
 part through lower-cost aqueduct-style troughs than pipelines for moving water in downhill
 directions.

 Capital costs for the conversion facility are significantly lower than the cultivation facility, but
 still make up a sizeable portion of the total capital investment (17%-23%). This can also be seen
 in the cost breakdown of the conversion facility (Fig. 4.3), where all costs associated with the
 cultivation facility are lumped into “feedstock cost,” which dominates all other process costs.
 Capital costs for the conversion facility are strongly driven by the drying equipment, specified as
 a double drum dryer. Based on previous communication with equipment vendors, the scale-up of
 this equipment is fairly limited; thus, a large number of individual dryers are required to meet the
 demands of the process, resulting in a significant cost inefficiency. Alternative drying methods
 that are more scalable may yield economic benefits but may also be unsuitable for the unique
 rheology of algal biomass. Capital costs for the product upgrading section are also fairly
 significant, especially for the “max jet” case, which requires an additional hydrocracking step.
 These additional equipment costs are the primary cost driver for the increased MFSP of the max
 jet case. A lower overall fuel yield and an increased proportion of naphtha also has a minor
 negative influence on economics compared to the base case.

 For both cases, coproducts and policy credits play a key role in providing supplemental revenue
 for the biorefinery; however, fuels are the primary product, making up over half of the revenue in
 each case given very high fuel yields from the substantial lipid fraction of the biomass. Dried
 solids are sold at $400/ton, accounting for approximately 12% of the revenue for the biorefinery.
 Policy credits play an even greater role. RINs and the generic low-emissions fuel policy make up
 approximately 30% of revenue, with the remainder of the “revenue” attributed to a credit for CO 2
 recycled back to the cultivation ponds, reflecting raw material savings for the biomass
 cultivation.

 NLR will exercise existing cost-estimates currently in-hand for scaled-down algae ponds and
 other unit operations constituting pilot-scale facility sizes producing 2, 5, and 50 bbl/day of
 extracted lipid biocrude (three scale scenarios). NLR has not procured, built, or operated algae

 construct as part of the above-noted pilot scale scenarios; however, NLR has received a number
 of vendor/contractor-supplied cost estimated for a range of algae pond sizes and designs, which
 would be the basis for the analysis to be conducted here. Accordingly, as discussed with
 ExxonMobil, this task would primarily consist of comparing existing cost estimates that
 ExxonMobil and NLR have already established as may be applicable to facility scales spanning
 the above ranges, without seeking any new cost estimates (the latter most appropriately would
 require working with similar vendors/subcontractors as NLR has done in the past in order to
 establish more tailored cost quotations). Up to three pond design cost estimates will be
 considered, selecting one as the most appropriate to the selected scale scenario, with downstream
 operations (dewatering, conversion pretreatment/extraction/residual drying) to be scaled down
 from original quotations.

 Task 5 Results:

 To support the feasibility assessment of a pilot-scale facility buildout, capital and operating cost
 estimates were established for key unit operations including cultivation, dewatering, and drying
 for three pilot plant expansion scenarios as may be deployed in a more near-term timeframe,
 spanning 2.5 and 5 bbl/day scales reflective of a brownfield expansion of current pilot plant
 infrastructure, as well as a separate 50 bbl/day greenfield pilot plant option. The capital costs for
 all scenarios under both tasks were based on extrapolations from vendor/subcontractor inputs
 furnished previously to NLR in support of TEA design case publications over recent years, as the
 scope did not include establishment of any “new” design/cost estimates.

 The results of this analysis yielded fixed capital investment estimates of $6MM and $10MM for
 the 2.5 and 5 bbl/day scales, respectively (the latter representing $4MM incremental additional
 costs assuming the pilot plant facility were first expanded to 2.5 bbl/day), when assuming
 Exxon-furnished guidance for indirect capital cost factors, and extrapolations from vendor and
 subcontractor-furnished figures. The associated variable operating costs were estimated at
 roughly $140,000/yr for the 2.5 bpd case or $240,000/yr for the 5 bpd case (these figures do not
 include labor or any other fixed operating expenses). For the separate 50 bpd greenfield design,
 fixed capital investment expenses were estimated at $28MM and $34MM for either membrane or
 centrifuge-based dewatering options, while variable operating costs were estimated at $2.2
 MM/yr. These expenses for all three scales are inclusive of cultivation, dewatering, and biomass
 drying; separately, cost estimates for a dry lipid extraction unit were considered based on
 published details outside of NLR in-house information, with installed capital costs estimated at
    $400,000, $600,000, and $2.4MM for the 2.5, 5, and 50 bpd scales, respectively, and with
 variable operating costs estimated at roughly $50,000/yr, $100,000/yr, and $1MM/yr for the
 three respective scales.
