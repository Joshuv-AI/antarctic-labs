Stepwise Lesion Modification for In-Stent Calcified Nodules Using Bias-Controlled Rotational Atherectomy and Intravascular Lithotripsy
Authors: Daisuke Kanda, Nobuhiro Ito, Mitsuru Ohishi
Published: 2026

A 67-year-old woman with end-stage renal disease, diabetes mellitus, and severe obesity presented with calcified in-stent restenosis (ISR) in the proximal right coronary artery. Owing to difficult femoral access, a 7F right brachial approach was used. Angiography and intravascular ultrasound confirmed ISR with a large, eccentric calcified nodule (Figure 1A-D, Supplemental Video 1).1 Rotational atherectomy (RA) allows controlled focal reduction of intraluminal calcium.2 Initial high-speed RA (185,000 rpm, 2.0 mm burr) achieved limited debulking because the burr followed the wire trajectory, restricting ablation to the burr diameter (Figure 1E). Although evidence for improved lumen gain at lower burr speeds remains inconclusive,3 we reduced rotational speed to 150,000 to 160,000 rpm while remaining within the recommended operating range.4 By deliberately repositioning the guide catheter and adjusting wire bias, the burr could be redirected toward the eccentric nodule, enabling progressive debulking beyond the initial bias trajectory (Figure 1F-J, Supplemental Videos 2 and 3). Although cutting balloon dilatation (3.0 mm × 10 mm) was selected based on postatherectomy intravascular ultrasound findings, which demonstrated an approximately 2.5 mm distance from the stent struts to the calcified nodule (Figure 1I, J), residual nodule protrusion remained with minimal change in nodule morphology (Figure 1K, L). Within the stent framework, cutting balloon dilatation had limited efficacy against the rigid calcified nodule. Because additional stent implantation was undesirable in this dialysis-dependent patient, maximizing lumen gain before drug-coated balloon (DCB) treatment was considered essential. Panels I, K, and N, as well as panels J, L, and O, represent serial coregistered intravascular ultrasound images obtained at the same lesion sites, allowing assessment of lesion modification after each treatment step. The additional lumen gain observed after intravascular lithotripsy may have resulted from calcium fracture with subsequent compression and redistribution of the residual calcified mass within the stented segment. The procedure was completed without complications. Following DCB dilatation, intravascular ultrasound confirmed nodule resolution and significant luminal gain (Figure 1P, Supplemental Video 4). This case demonstrates a complementary 2-step strategy: bias-assisted RA for selective debulking of eccentric nodules, followed by intravascular lithotripsy to address residual circumferential calcification beyond the burr’s reach, maximizing lumen gain in severely calcified ISR. This stepwise approach may provide a reproducible strategy for interventionalists managing similar challenging lesions.
