Deterministic, dynamically reconfigurable single quantum emitters enabled by tip-enhanced nano-optical trapping spectroscopy
Authors: Yeonjeong Koo, Jaehun Shin, Jonggeun Hwang, Hyeongwoo Lee, Hyeonmin Oh, Sujeong Kim, Su Jin Kim, Junghoon Jahng, Hyun Seok Lee, P. James Schuck, Yung Doug Suh, Reuven Gordon, Kyoung-Duck Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nanocavity optical trapping has enabled the isolation and precise positioning of nanoscale objects, from single molecules to quantum emitters. Yet, practically deployable single quantum sources require more than trapping, i.e., they require real-time control over position, polarization, brightness, and wavelength. Achieving such dynamic modulation remains a critical challenge, but is key to realizing ultra-secure quantum communication and adaptive quantum sensing. Here, we present tip-enhanced nano-optical trapping spectroscopy, utilizing shear-force atomic force microscopy in liquid, to achieve deterministic and dynamically reconfigurable single quantum emitters. This approach enables precise positioning and dipole alignment of cavity-coupled single quantum dots (QDs), driven by nano-optical gradient forces and field-induced torque, with simultaneous nano-spectroscopic analysis. Moreover, dynamic control of the tip-cavity mode volume and tip-induced pressure allows further tuning of trapping and coupling behaviors, modulating the quantum emission characteristics, e.g., brightness and photon energy, from the weak to the strong coupling regime. This work represents a significant advancement toward realizing the vast potential of QDs in quantum applications, such as tuning emission properties of single photon sources for quantum switches and modulators, or implementing quantum gates via plexciton state manipulation.

Introduction

Deterministic and reproducible quantum emitters are key building blocks for emerging quantum technologies, including quantum communication networks, photonic quantum computing, and quantum sensing. In these systems, single photons serve as the fundamental carriers of quantum information and should be generated on-demand with well-controlled optical properties1–3. Reliable single photon sources are therefore considered a critical technological component for applications such as quantum key distribution, photonic quantum information processing, and quantum metrology. From an industrial perspective, scalable and controllable quantum light sources are essential for the development of manufacturable quantum photonic platforms. These technologies are expected to facilitate future quantum communication infrastructures, advanced sensing systems, and next-generation information processing technologies, forming a rapidly growing global quantum industry with significant economic and societal impact. Rather than relying solely on static solid-state devices in which emitters are permanently embedded within nanofabricated photonic structures, an alternative paradigm is to develop controllable platforms capable of generating and manipulating individual emitters in a flexible manner. Such systems can function as reconfigurable quantum light generators, providing tailored single-photon resources for diverse quantum photonic applications3–5.

Early foundational studies demonstrated coupling of single quantum emitters, such as molecules or quantum dots (QDs), to plasmonic cavities or nano-antennas, giving rise to enhanced emission and even strong coupling phenomena under ambient conditions6–10. These pioneering works established plasmonic nanocavities as an important platform for inducing advanced quantum optical sources. However, these approaches relied on sophisticated methods for precise positioning of emitters with static cavities, restricting the control necessary for deterministic quantum state preparation or tunable emission properties. To overcome this limitation in ambient conditions, nano-aperture-based plasmonic tweezers11–15 have been developed as a means of trapping and probing single emitters in liquid. These systems enabled optical manipulations and real-time characterizations of emitters under in-solution conditions, and even for single photon emitters16–19. In parallel, emerging nanoscale techniques, such as electron paramagnetic resonance spectroscopy and magnetic force microscopy, have provided complementary approaches for probing spin states and magnetic properties in biological quantum systems down to single molecule level20,21. However, in these approaches, either the static trap geometry or the immobilization of samples on a substrate makes it difficult to dynamically control the emitter-cavity interaction strength through modulation of the cavity mode volume and dipole alignment. Although elegant scanning probe approaches have been integrated with static plasmonic traps and demonstrated spatial mobility in liquid environments13, direct and reconfigurable control of light-matter interactions remains essential for practical quantum information science applications, especially in the strong coupling regime.

Colloidal QDs provide exceptional brightness, narrow emission linewidths, and large oscillator strengths, enabling tunable emission wavelengths, high photostability, and facile integration into photonic structures22,23. These advantages make them highly attractive for single quantum emitter applications requiring efficient photon extraction, precise spectral control, and stable room-temperature operation. However, inducing deterministic cavity-QD coupling and achieving dynamical control over interaction parameters, such as cavity mode volume and dipole alignment in the strong coupling regime remain challenging. Recently, atomic force microscopy (AFM)-based tip-enhanced strong coupling spectroscopy has successfully addressed the issue of deterministic coupling by enabling nanoscale three-dimensional positioning of the tip-cavity system24–26. This precise positioning capability also facilitates accurate control over the cavity gap, allowing fine tuning of the coupling strength24–26. However, because emitters were randomly dispersed on the substrate with arbitrary dipole orientations in these experiments, strong coupling was observed only occasionally, specifically when the tip stochastically encountered vertically oriented QDs.

In this work, we develop tip-enhanced nano-optical trapping (TENT) spectroscopy, based on shear-force AFM operating in liquid. Using TENT spectroscopy, we achieve self-trapping and -alignment of single colloidal QDs within a plasmonic tip-substrate cavity. In this liquid-phase environment, the mobility of QDs enables dipole self-alignment with the out-of-plane polarized electric field in a cavity, driven by field-induced torques that minimize electrostatic potential energy. This configuration allows stable and reproducible trapping at the tip apex, governed by plasmon-enhanced optical gradient forces and nano-optical torque. These effects are corroborated by full-field electromagnetic simulations of optical forces and potential landscapes. Under these conditions, we observe the formation of hybrid plexciton states, evidenced by vacuum Rabi splitting in near-field photoluminescence (PL) spectra. Second-order photon correlation measurements of the near-field emission from a trapped QD confirm its identity as a single photon emitter. In addition, AFM-based TENT provides nanoscale control of the tip-sample distance and local pressurization, enabling real-time engineering of both the cavity resonance and excitonic energy of a trapped QD, which in turn allows us to systematically detune the Rabi splitting. These results demonstrate that TENT spectroscopy enables robust, reconfigurable control of quantum light-matter interactions in liquid environments, opening avenues for the realization of programmable and dynamically tunable quantum photonic platforms.

Results

Tip-enhanced nano-optical trapping (TENT) spectroscopy for single QDs

In our TENT spectroscopy setup, we utilize a shear-force AFM operating in a liquid environment, combined with a bottom-illumination optical setup. To induce a tip-enhanced nanocavity effect with the excitation laser (λexc = 594 nm), we employ an Au substrate and an Au tip, regulating the tip-substrate distance from 0 to 30 nm in the shear-force regime. This configuration creates a strong optical field gradient at the cavity junction via dipole-dipole interactions, as illustrated in Fig. 1a24–26. For TENT, colloidal CdSe/Zn1−xCdxS QDs dispersed in toluene26 are drawn toward the nanocavity by the gradient force at the focused beam spot. The highly localized optical fields at the tip-cavity junction generate a significant gradient force on the single QDs, confining them tightly at the optical potential minimum, which is located directly beneath the tip apex. Once trapped, the QD aligns its dipole orientation with the polarization axis of Au tip-Au substrate nanocavity due to field-induced torque. Given the radius of the tip apex (~7 nm) and the diameter of the QD (~13 nm), the TENT spectroscopy can trap a single QD at the tip-substrate distance of ~20 nm. As the tip approaches further, the cavity gap narrows, increasing the electric field confinement and decreasing the cavity mode volume. Thus, the precise distance control achieved by shear-force AFM enables dynamic modulation of the coupling strength between the cavity plasmon and the QD exciton. Furthermore, controlled indentation of the tip onto the QD leads to GPa scale pressurization (see refs. 27–30 for previous nanomechanical modeling and finite-element simulations accounting for the tip and sample geometries, contact area, and mechanical properties) not only mechanically stabilizes the emitter within the cavity but also modifies its excitonic energy level, allowing for real-time spectral tuning.Fig. 1Tip-enhanced nano-optical trapping (TENT) spectroscopy for a reconfigurable single quantum emitter.a Illustration of TENT for trapping a single QD at the Au tip-Au substrate junction. b Schematic of the head part in TENT spectroscopy setup, exhibiting the optical potential well created by the dithering Au tip. Figure 1b shows the experimental schematic of the head part in TENT spectroscopy. The substrate is prepared by coating a coverslip with a 2 nm Cr layer, followed by a 9 nm Au layer using e-beam deposition31. QD solution is then dispensed onto the substrate, and the Au tip, attached to a tuning fork (TF), is immersed into the droplet. A high numerical aperture (NA = 1.4) objective lens focuses the excitation beam onto the tip apex and simultaneously collects back-scattered optical signals, directing them to the detection part in TENT spectroscopy (see Methods for the complete setup descriptions). The high-NA objective lens plays a crucial role in the trapping process by enabling a hierarchical force landscape: thermal convection, osmosis and thermophoresis transport QDs from distances of several tens of micrometers toward the laser beam spot32–35. Subsequently, the objective lens generates a strong far-field optical gradient force that further drags QDs into the focused beam within 1-2 micrometers, then near-field optical forces localized at the plasmonic tip within tens of nanometers finally trap a single QD.

Figure 1c shows a calculated vector map of the optical field within the Au tip-Au substrate nanocavity, illustrating the cavity polarization (Ecavity). When a dipole emitter, such as a QD, is introduced into this cavity, a field-induced torque (τ) acts on the emitter, causing it to rotate until it reaches the lowest potential energy configuration. In this state, the transition dipole moment of the QD (μQD) aligns parallel to Ecavity. In cases where strong coupling is achieved, the coupling strength (g) depends on the angle (θ) between the optical field polarization and the exciton dipole, as g ∝ Ecavity ⋅ μQD24–26. The torque τ works to reduce θ to zero, allowing for maximizing g, as illustrated in Fig. 1d.

Optimization of an Au tip length for robust nano-optical trapping in liquid

To trap a QD in a nanocavity, it is essential to immerse the Au tip into a liquid medium. However, the high viscosity, surface tension, and evaporative nature of liquids significantly compromise the stability and sensitivity of atomic force control when the tip is submerged36–38. Consequently, maintaining a stable optical trap using an AFM tip remains a challenge, even though the theoretical feasibility of nanometric optical tweezers based on metal tips was first proposed nearly three decades ago39. To address this problem, we have developed a theoretical model based on Euler-Bernoulli beam theory to identify optimal oscillating conditions for the TF/tip assembly. By considering the externally driven system, we calculated the oscillating amplitudes of both the tuning fork (ATF) and the Au tip (Atip) as a function of varying tip length (ltip). Details, including boundary conditions, are provided in the Methods. As shown in Fig. 2a, ltip has a significant influence on ATF (black line, indicating the experimentally measured Q-factor) because the periodic changes in the natural frequency of the Au tip affect the damping rate of the TF/tip harmonic oscillator40. Our experimental measurements of the resonance frequency (fres) and Q-factor of the TF/tip assembly as functions of ltip show good agreement with the simulation results (see Fig. S1). Interestingly, Atip does not directly follow the same trend as ATF (red line). Notably, Atip is significantly larger than ATF in three distinct ranges: ltip = 0.5–1.3 mm, 1.8–2.6 mm, and >3.2 mm. To illustrate this behavior more clearly, we plot the oscillations of the TF/tip assembly for ltip = 1.92 mm (Fig. 2b) and ltip = 2.99 mm (Fig. 2c), both of which exhibit comparable Q-factors. At ltip = 1.92 mm, the amplitude at the tip-end is significantly larger than at the TF-end, while at ltip = 2.99 mm, the tip oscillates with a smaller amplitude in the third harmonic mode (see Fig. S2 for plots with ltip = 0.35 mm, 0.68 mm, and 1.50 mm).Fig. 2Oscillating characteristics of the TF/Au tip assembly.a Simulated oscillation amplitudes of the TF end (ATF, black) and Au tip end (Atip, red) as functions of the Au tip length (ltip). b, c Simulated oscillating displacements of the TF/Au tip assembly for two different Au tip lengths (ltip = 1.92 mm and 2.99 mm, respectively), as indicated in (a). d Topography of wrinkles in a WS2 monolayer measured using shear-force AFM with the optimized tip length (ltip = 3 mm). e Topographic line profile along the dashed line in (d). f Topographic line profile of the same sample, but measured with the non-optimal tip length (ltip = 2 mm) for comparison. g Frequency response curves of the TF/Au tip assembly with the optimized tip length (ltip = 3 mm) at different immersion depth (dI). h Resonance frequency (fres) and Q-factor extracted from (g) as functions of immersion depth (dI).

Although Atip varies significantly and has a substantial impact on AFM measurements, it has not been thoroughly considered in experiments, as its influence is not directly observable. In our experiment, the wobbling of the tip-end in liquid not only induces undesirable perturbations that decrease the stability of optical trapping, but also reduces trapping precision since Atip directly reflects the spatial width of the optical potential well. From both theoretical and experimental results (Fig. 2a–c and Fig. S1), we identify the optimal tip lengths that satisfy high Q-factor and minimize tip-end oscillation amplitude: ltip = 1.3–1.6 mm and ltip = 2.6–3.3 mm. To further investigate the effects of ltip experimentally, we measured the topography of a wrinkled WS2 monolayer using two different TF/tip assemblies, one with the optimal ltip = 3 mm (Fig. 2d) and the other with a non-optimal ltip = 2 mm (Fig. S3), both of which exhibit similar Q-factors. As shown in the line profiles (Fig. 2e, f), wrinkle structures with ~20 nm widths are clearly resolved with the optimized ltip = 3 mm, whereas nanoscale wrinkles appear blurred and wider than expected when using the ltip = 2 mm tip.

For the optimal Au tip with ltip = 3 mm, we further investigate the resonance characteristics in evaporating liquid environment. Figure 2g shows the changes in the frequency response curve as the immersion depth (dI) of the Au tip in water is varied from 3 to 0 mm. Due to the viscosity and surface tension of water, the damping rate of the TF/tip resonator increases, causing the Q-factor to gradually increase as dI decreases36,37. Similarly, the resonance frequency (fres) increases with decreasing dI, attributed to the reduced effective mass of the oscillating system36,37. By fitting the frequency response curves with a Lorentzian function, we extract fres and Q-factor as functions of dI, as shown in Fig. 2h. As a result, two stable zones are clearly identified at dI = 0.3–0.8 mm and dI = 1.7–2.4 mm. Comparing these results with the simulation of oscillating characteristics for the 3 mm tip (Fig. 2c), we understand that these stable zones correspond to the two nodes of third-harmonic oscillation. When the air-liquid interface passes these nodes, the damping effect from viscosity and surface tension is minimized due to the small displacement of tip oscillation36,37. Under such conditions, stable optical trapping of a target material is achieved for a range of nano-spectroscopy experiments.

Tip-enhanced nano-optical trapping and self-alignment of a single QD

To gain insights into nano-optical trapping mechanism, we calculate the optical force map on a center of mass of a QD near the Au tip-Au substrate nanocavity (see Methods for details of the finite-difference time-domain simulations). The Au tip is modeled with a radius of curvature of 7 nm and a cone angle of 30∘, positioned 18 nm above the Au substrate (See Fig. S4 for a scanning electron microscopy image of the Au tip). The QD is modeled as a dielectric sphere with a CdSe core (7 nm diameter) and a Zn1−xCdxS shell (3 nm thickness) suspended in toluene. Figure 3b presents the expected dipole orientation of a trapped QD, determined by electrostatic simulations of charge-induced polarization. To quantitatively evaluate the field-induced torque, the QD is modeled as an effective point-like transition dipole with a dipole moment of μ = 10 D. Using the expression τ = μ × E, the maximum torque is estimated to be on the order of 10−20 N ⋅ m under our experimental conditions (see SI Section 6 for details). At the cavity center, the corresponding maximum rotational potential energy reaches Urot = ∣μ∣ ∣E∣ = 2.4 kBT (kB: Boltzmann constant, T: temperature). This energy scale exceeds the thermal energy in the liquid environment, indicating that dipole alignment is thermodynamically favored near the cavity hotspot despite rotational thermal fluctuations. We also calculate the in-plane optical potential (Uopt) map, with illustrating the trapped QD near the Au tip, as shown in Fig. 3c. The potential depth is obtained to be −5.2 kBT, which is sufficient to ensure stable trapping against the thermal energy of Brownian motion at room temperature (1 kBT). A full width at half minimum (Γ) of ~16 nm suggests that the potential well is sufficiently narrow to confine only a single QD at a time. Figure 3d shows the optical field distribution when the QD is trapped. By comparing the field intensity calculated with and without the QD (Fig. 3d and Fig. 1c, respectively), it is shown that the QD confines the optical field within its own volume and significantly enhances the local field intensity. This is a clear signature of a self-induced back action (SIBA) effect, increasing its self trap stability. Also, this field redistribution makes the QD highly favorable for achieving strong plasmon-exciton coupling.

For stable operation of TENT spectroscopy in liquid, we use the optimized tip with ltip = 1.5 mm, dI = 1.3 mm, and Atip < 1 nm based on simulation and measurement results (Fig. 2). To capture the dynamic evolution of this coupling process, we record time-resolved PL spectra during trapping, as shown in Fig. 3e. At 0 s, a single QD freely diffuses into the excitation volume, emitting uncoupled PL with no observable Rabi splitting (g = 0). As trapping proceeds (from 0.3 to 1.2 s), we observe a clear transition into the strong coupling regime, characterized by increasing splitting between the polariton modes, indicating enhanced light-matter interaction due to both spatial and dipolar alignment. To quantify key physical parameters of the strongly coupled system, the acquired spectra are analyzed using a coupled harmonic oscillator model, which allows extraction of resonance frequencies (ωSP and ωQD) and linewidths (γSP and γQD) for both the cavity plasmon and the QD exciton. Details of the model are provided in SI section 7. Fig. S6 shows the time-dependent behavior of ωSP and γSP, derived from Fig. 3e, exhibiting a redshift in the cavity resonance energy and linewidth broadening over time. These observations provide evidence of SIBA trapping, a phenomenon commonly observed in plasmonic nano-optical tweezers11,41,42.

To confirm the quantum nature of the trapped QD, we perform a second-order photon correlation measurement g(2)(τd) of the PL signal, as shown in Fig. 3f. This result exhibits clear antibunching behavior at zero delay time (τd = 0), indicating the high potential for single photon emission from a single QD. The observed slightly low purity, g(2)(0) = 0.71, is primarily attributed to the elevated excitation power, since high excitation power increases the probability of re-excitation as commonly observed in solid-state single photon emitters43,44. To achieve higher-purity single photon emission within the TENT platform, further systematic optimization of trapping parameters, including wavelength and power of excitation and trap laser, cavity gap, tip geometry, and related experimental conditions, is needed in future study. Additionally, exploring alternative single emitters, such as nano-diamonds, hexagonal boron nitride (hBN) nanoflakes, and single molecules, may provide promising pathways toward improved emitter purity and performance.

Dynamic reconfiguration of a cavity-coupled single emitter via TENT spectroscopy

We then control the single emitter-cavity interactions with simultaneous nano-spectroscopic analysis, by precisely regulating the distance between the Au tip and the Au surface. We classify two stages of the control regime, as shown in Fig. 4a. With a single QD stably trapped at the tip apex, we gradually decrease the shear-force feedback setpoint, allowing the Au tip to approach the Au substrate and thereby decrease the cavity mode volume. As the tip/QD assembly approaches the Au surface, GPa-scale tip-pressure is induced on the QD27–30, modifying the exciton energy and the plasmon resonance. These spectral shifts allow controllable detuning achieving a tunable quantum light emission. For further analysis, we plot the time-dependent evolution of ωQD and ωSP with respect to the regulating setpoint, as shown in Fig. 4c. This result reveals a distinct three-stage transition behavior. In the first stage, a constant gap between the tip and the substrate is maintained by fixing the setpoint at 90%, resulting in minimal changes in both ωQD and ωSP. As the setpoint gradually decreases to 60%, the cavity mode volume is continuously reduced, leading to gradual redshift of ωSP, while the ωQD exhibits negligible shift. At ~60% setpoint, the trapped QD at the tip is expected to be in contact with the substrate. We then further decrease the setpoint to 40% to induce nano-mechanical pressure on the QD, resulting in an abrupt and substantial blueshift of the ωQD by up to 40 meV. This level of blueshift is consistent with previous reports showing that CdSe-based QDs exhibit bandgap shifts of ~50–100 meV per GPa of compressive strain, depending on their size45–47. Simultaneously, the ωSP exhibits a pronounced redshift by up to −59 meV due to the deformation of the QD. Such deformation alters the local refractive index and effective geometry of the nanocavity, thereby redshifting the cavity resonance48–50.

Figure 4d shows selected near-field PL spectra acquired during the control process. A progressive increase in energy splitting, indicative of the strong coupling regime, is observed as the mode volume decreases (13.2 s, gap control) and the resonance properties of the emitter and cavity are further modified (18.3 s, pressure control). We also quantify the coupling strength g and track its evolution over time as shown in Fig. 4e. g exhibits a sharp increase at the moment of QD trapping, as the QD dipole naturally aligns within the cavity under the influence of optical force and torque. As the tip-substrate gap narrows, the enhanced optical gradient confines the QD more effectively, reducing its fluctuating motion within the cavity. This improved trapping localization facilitates enhanced spatial and dipolar alignment along with a further reduction in mode volume, resulting in increasing g. As the tip presses further, the trapped QD becomes destabilized and escapes from the cavity, resulting in an abrupt signal drop. This full cycle, from initial trapping to gradual modulation and eventual release, demonstrates the controllable nature of the cavity-coupled single emitter enabled by TENT spectroscopy. A deeper understanding of thermally induced effects may provide further improvements in system stability51–53, marking a promising direction for future study in the TENT.

Deterministic and reversible control of single emitter strong coupling

To highlight the deterministic tunability of TENT spectroscopy, we demonstrate pronounced switching between the weak and strong coupling regimes of a trapped QD through active cavity gap control. As shown in Fig. 5a, shear-force feedback-based tip control enables precise modulation of the cavity gap over a wide range from 20 to 120 nm. Such reproducible gap control enables repeated transitions between the weak and strong coupling regimes, resulting in the reversible spectral evolution observed in Fig. 5b. Figure 5c, d present representative PL spectra acquired during the first retraction-approach cycle. Specifically, Fig. 5c illustrates the gradual weakening of the QD-cavity interaction associated with the expansion of the mode volume during tip retraction, whereas Fig. 5d reveals its systematic recovery upon tip approach. The corresponding g extracted from these processes is shown in Fig. 5e, f, respectively. Notably, both the spectral features and the extracted g exhibit highly reproducible behavior over multiple cycles, indicating that the QD remains stably trapped within the nanocavity despite repeated mechanical motion and demonstrating the robustness of the trapping configuration. The highly consistent repetition of the spectra further suggests that no additional emitters enter the nanocavity during these cycles, as the trapping potential at the tip apex is already saturated by a single QD, effectively preventing further capture even when the cavity gap is increased. Such deterministic and reversible control over the emitter-cavity interaction differentiates our approach from previously reported probe-based trapping schemes. For example, tapered nanofiber probes have demonstrated versatile trapping and particle delivery capabilities54–56, offering useful routes for positioning nanoscale objects. However, these approaches generally do not provide direct access to deeply confined plasmonic near-fields, nor do they enable continuous and precise tuning of the light-matter interaction strength at the single-emitter level. In contrast, our plasmonic nanocavity platform allows active modulation of the emitter-cavity coupling via controllable gap engineering, while maintaining stable trapping. Moreover, the platform can be further extended by integrating additional control strategies, such as feedback-controlled optical wavefront shaping or electric-field-assisted trapping, thereby enabling on-demand manipulation of quantum optical responses without repeated retrapping. This level of dynamic and reversible control represents a functionality beyond conventional optical trapping architectures.

Discussion

We introduced TENT spectroscopy, an advanced approach for precise nano-optical trapping of single quantum emitters in liquid environments. By combining tip-enhanced nano-spectroscopy with shear-force AFM and plasmonic tweezers, we demonstrated robust and reproducible trapping and self-alignment of single isolated QDs, achieving strong plasmon-exciton coupling, revealed by Rabi splitting in near-field PL spectra. Through dynamic manipulation of the cavity gap and tip-induced pressure, we further tuned the trapping and coupling behaviors, enabling modification of the quantum emission characteristics. TENT spectroscopy represents a key advancement over conventional plasmonic traps by enabling dynamic and interactive emitter control, while also pushing beyond traditional scanning probe methods by allowing emitters to reorient their dipoles in response to optical fields. Looking forward, further extension of the TENT platform to diverse quantum emitters and spin qubits, including spin-active biomolecular systems, may provide opportunities for quantum information science, biomedical sensing, and various other fields. Furthermore, integration with hybrid quantum architectures may open possibilities for exploring and engineering complex light-matter interactions at the single particle level.

Methods

TENT spectroscopy setup

The TENT spectroscopy is based on the bottom-illumination optics setup combined with a shear-force AFM using a quartz tuning fork (TF) and an attached Au tip. For the excitation and trap beam, 594 nm diode laser was expanded and focused on the Au tip-Au film junction by an objective lens (OL, MPlanApo N 100 × , NA of 1.4, Olympus). The back scattered PL signals were collected by the same OL, passed through an edge filter, and sent to a spectrometer (f = 328 mm, Kymera 328i, Andor). The signals were finally imaged on a thermoelectrically cooled charge-coupled device (CCD, DU420A-BEX2-DD, Andor) to obtain PL spectra. To modulate the distance between the Au tip and the Au substrate, we varied the setpoint of the shear-force feedback control. A setpoint of 90% (relative to the free-space oscillation amplitude of the TF/tip assembly) maintained the tip-substrate distance at ~18 nm. When the setpoint was gradually decreased to 60%, the tip was expected to be nearly in contact with the substrate, based on the vertical displacement and the abruptly changed TF amplitude monitored. Further reduction of the setpoint to 40% led to an additional tip displacement indicating the onset of mechanical pressurization. Although the applied load is precisely controlled via shear-force feedback, the effective indentation force is influenced by tip-sample contact mechanics and hydrodynamic effects in the confined liquid gap. Nevertheless, the indentation conditions are highly reproducible and consistent with our previous calibrations, despite the uncertainty in determining the exact local pressure in liquid environments.

Finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) model for optical force calculation

FDTD simulations were performed to calculate the optical force applied on the QD. In the model system, the background medium was set as toluene, with a refractive index of 1.4196. The CdSe core was assigned a complex refractive index of n = 2.87 and k = 0.20 at 594 nm, while the Zn1−xCdxS shell was modeled as an effective dielectric layer with n = 1.9 and negligible absorption (k ≈ 0), accounting for the composite nature of the inorganic shell and organic ligands. The excitonic transition and its dispersion were not incorporated into the dielectric function in the FDTD simulations. The geometric configuration of the tip-substrate cavity, QD and excitation conditions are described in the main text. The mesh size was set to 0.75 nm, and the QD position was adjusted in 1 nm increments in 2D plane. The optical force was acquired by directly calculating the Lorentz force on the QD, instead of using the Maxwell stress tensor method which provides only the net force acting on the QD. By employing the Lorentz force calculation, we calculated the three-dimensional data of optical force inside and around the QD. This approach also provides the induced charge distributed within and around the QD resulting from the enhanced E-field within the tip cavity.
