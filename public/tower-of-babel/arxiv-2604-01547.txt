Data-Driven Covariance Steering with Output Feedback
Dimitrios Moustroufis, Panagiotis Tsiotras

Abstract
This paper addresses the problem of output-feedback covariance steering for stochastic, discrete-time, linear, time-invariant systems without knowledge of the system model. We employ a controllable, non-minimal state representation constructed from past inputs and outputs and convert the problem to one in state-feedback form. In this representation, the induced disturbance becomes temporally correlated, which requires explicit propagation of the cross-covariance between the state and disturbance processes. To handle the lack of a system model, we leverage persistently exciting data collected offline and formulate the mean and covariance steering problems using an indirect and a direct approach, respectively. The indirect formulation requires an estimate of the mean dynamics model, while the direct formulation relies on an estimate of the noise realization in the collected data. To this end, we present an estimation method suitable to handle temporally correlated noise, enabling consistent identification of both components. Using a convex relaxation, we convert the covariance steering problem to a semidefinite program that can be solved efficiently. We conduct numerical simulations to evaluate the performance of the developed framework.



I
Introduction

As engineering systems become more complex, sensors become cheaper and more widely available, and the interest for autonomous operation increases, there has been a recent surge in the literature around data-driven simulation and control methods.
These methods can be broadly classified as direct and indirect. According to the indirect paradigm, a model of the plant is first estimated from the observed data, and then it is used for control purposes. In contrast, direct methods compute the control law using a system representation directly from data.

While “black-box” methods such as deep neural networks have been employed to model highly complex systems, they generally lack theoretical guarantees in terms of stability and robustness.
On the contrary, under the restriction of linear, time-invariant systems with no noise, the seminal work by Willems et al.
[
29
]
proved that future trajectories of a system can be represented by a set of persistently exciting input-output data.
This result, often referred to as the “Willem’s Fundamental Lemma” (WFL) has been exploited for data-driven simulation
[
20
]
, for the design of state- and output-feedback stabilizing controllers
[
11
,
10
,
13
]
, as well as for the design of data-driven predictive controllers
[
8
]
,
[
5
]
, and data-enabled policy optimization algorithms
[
31
]
,
[
32
]
. These methods have been applied to control quadrotors, power networks, and synchronous motor drives
[
14
,
16
,
6
]
. Willems’ result has been extended to data collected from multiple trajectories
[
28
]
, while robustifying regularizations
[
9
]
have been employed to handle noise and nonlinearities in the data.
Finally, the connection between indirect and direct methods based on the WFL has also been investigated
[
12
]
.

A control design paradigm that aims to provide guarantees on the control performance under stochastic disturbances by controlling the statistical distribution of the system’s state is that of Covariance Steering (CS). In particular, discrete-time CS deals with the problem of steering the state of a system from an initial distribution to a terminal one within a specified horizon. This problem has been extensively investigated in the literature
[
2
,
15
,
17
,
4
]
, and an efficient method to obtain the optimal solution in terms of a semi-definite program has been proposed
[
18
]
,
[
26

. The CS problem for partially observed systems has also been recently addressed in the literature
[
23
]
,
[
19
]
.

All previous methods assume knowledge of the system model. Aiming to alleviate this assumption,
[
22
]
formulated the CS problem for noiseless systems in a model-free fashion by exploiting the WFL, while
[
24
]
,
[
25
]
extended these results for the case when process noise is present.
In these works, an indirect approach is employed for the mean steering problem, while the covariance steering problem is formulated using a direct approach.
A major assumption of the current data-driven covariance steering literature is the availability of full state measurements without any measurement noise.
This, however, may not be possible for many practical applications, motivating the present work.

The main contribution of this paper is to extend the state-feedback data-driven covariance steering results to the output-feedback case. By exploiting a non-minimal state representation
[
11
]
, we utilize state-feedback ideas to solve the data-driven covariance steering problem.
However, in this representation, the disturbances are temporally correlated, which complicates model estimation for the indirect mean controller design and noise estimation for the direct covariance controller design.
This work also addresses the discrete-time covariance steering problem under temporally correlated noise, which, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, has not been addressed in the literature.

V_{21}&V_{22}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\mathcal{B}}&\hat{\mathcal{A}}\end{bmatrix}=(-V_{21}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}(V_{22}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}})^{-1})^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}.

(21)

III-C

Instrumental Variables Method

As discussed in Section-
II-D
,
\chi_{k}
is correlated with
\zeta_{k}
. In such cases, where the regressors are correlated with the noise, the TLS estimator will not be consistent, necessitating another approach.
Here, we apply the Instrumental Variables (IV) method
[
30
]
.

Remark 1
.

Despite the IV estimator being theoretically consistent, it is less data-efficient than the TLS estimator, and it performs poorly both in terms of mean error and error variance when the available sample size is small. Thus, TLS serves as an alternative method for such cases.

In the IV method, a number of parameters, called the “instrumental variables” or “instruments,” which are independent of
\zeta_{k}
, are used as the independent variables of the problem. To present the IV method, we consider the parameters
\beta\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{B}&\mathcal{A}\end{bmatrix}
, the regressors
j_{k}\triangleq[u_{k}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}\ \chi_{k}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}]
, the regressor matrix
J\triangleq P^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}
, and the observations
Y\triangleq\mathcal{X}_{1,T}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}
.
The dynamics (
8
) are a system of equations in which the regressors are the same for each equation and the error terms (components of
\zeta_{k}
) are independent among equations. Since the model is unknown, we do not consider any relation between the parameters of different equations (e.g., some element of
\mathcal{A}
being common in multiple equations).
Under these conditions, the parameters of the system can be consistently estimated on a per-equation basis, without requiring joint estimation of all equations. If additional knowledge about the model structure is available, this information can be exploited to construct a more efficient estimator. However, such structural knowledge is not required for consistency of the IV estimator.

An attractive choice for the instrument vector for our problem is the control input at some time,
k
, and the
p
past control inputs, i.e.,
g_{k}\triangleq[u_{k}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}\ u_{k-1}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}\ \cdots\ u_{k-p}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}]
.
We consider the partition
j_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}j_{k,1}\ j_{k,2}\end{bmatrix}
, where
j_{k,1}
are the exogenous components of
j
(i.e., those not correlated with the noise) and
j_{k,2}
are the endogenous components of
j_{k}
.
The goal is to express
j_{k,2}
as a linear combination of
g_{k}
and
j_{k,1}
. To this end, we employ the two-stage least squares (2SLS) estimator, which is the IV estimator that chooses the linear combinations of
g_{k}
with the highest correlation with
j_{k,2}

[
30
]
.
The 2SLS estimator is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\beta}=\left[(J^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}G(G^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}G)^{-1}G^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}J)^{-1}(J^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}G(G^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}G)^{-1}G^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}Y)\right]^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}},

(22)

where
G\triangleq[g_{0}\ g_{1}\ \cdots\ g_{T-1}]
is the matrix of instrument vectors over all samples.

In order for the 2SLS estimator to be consistent, the following assumptions are made:

Assumption 1
.

The instruments are independent of the noise,
\mathbb{E}(g_{k}\zeta_{k}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}})=0
.

Assumption 2
.

The instruments are linearly independent,
\mathrm{rank}(\mathbb{E}(g_{k}g_{k}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}))=p+1
.

Assumption 3
.

The instruments are linearly dependent with the regressors to some degree,
\mathrm{rank}(\mathbb{E}(g_{k}j_{k}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}))=r+m
.

A necessary condition for Assumption
3
to hold is that the number of instruments is greater than or equal to the number of regressors, i.e.,
p+1\geq r+m
.
Assumptions
1
and
2
pertain to a proper design of the instrument vector, while Assumption
3
is verified numerically by computing the expectation from the sample data. For the selected instrument vector, Assumption
1
is satisfied since the control inputs during the data collection phase are independent of the noise.
Moreover, if i.i.d control inputs are applied during the data collection process, which is a common choice to achieve persistency of excitation, then the matrix
\mathbb{E}(g_{k}g_{k}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}})
will have full rank
p+1
. The following theorem establishes the consistency of the 2SLS estimator under the aforementioned assumptions.

Theorem 2
.

(Theorem 5.1
[
30
]
)
If Assumptions
1
,
2
,
3
are satisfied, then the 2SLS estimator is consistent.

Data-Driven Distribution Steering

In this section, we present the indirect formulation of the mean steering problem and the direct formulation of the covariance steering problem.

IV-A

Data-driven Mean Steering

The mean steering problem (
9
) is trivially expressed in terms of an estimated model, since the cost (
9a
) remains the same, while the dynamics constraints (
9b
) are written in terms of
\hat{\mathcal{A}},\hat{\mathcal{B}}
instead of
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
.
For the initial and terminal output mean constraints (
9c
), we need to estimate the matrix
\mathcal{C}
in (
8
).
An estimate,
\hat{\mathcal{C}}
, is obtained using TLS / IV with
\mathcal{X}_{0,T}
as the regressors and
Y_{0,T}
as the observations. Considering the instrument matrix
G
as in (
22
), and defining the regressor and observation matrices
J_{c}\triangleq\mathcal{X}_{0,T}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}
and
Y_{c}\triangleq Y_{0,T}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}
, respectively, the IV estimator for
\mathcal{C}
is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{C}}=\left[(J_{c}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}G(G^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}G)^{-1}G^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}J_{c})^{-1}(J_{c}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}G(G^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}G)^{-1}G^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}Y_{c})\right]^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}},

(23)

The initial and terminal mean constraints are now posed as

\displaystyle\mu^{y}_{i}=\hat{\mathcal{C}}\mu_{0},\quad\mu^{y}_{f}=\hat{\mathcal{C}}\mu_{N}.

(24)

IV-B

Data-driven Covariance Steering

Using the data matrix
P
from (
17
), we construct the matrix

\displaystyle\Phi=PP^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}.

(25)

For persistently exciting data, Theorem
1
implies that
P
will have full row rank, and thus,
\Phi
will have full rank as well.
Hence, there exists a solution
G_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{(m+r)\times r}
to the following system of linear equations

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}K_{k}\\
I\end{bmatrix}=\Phi G_{k}.

(26)

From (
26
), we can express the feedback gains
K_{k}
in (
12
) and (
13
) in terms of the data matrix
\Phi
and the decision variables
G_{k}
. Using (
18
) and considering the sub-optimal control law
u_{k}=K_{k}(\chi_{k}-\mu_{k})+v_{k}
, we derive the following model-independent form of the covariance and cross-covariance propagation equations

\displaystyle\Sigma_{k+1}

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{X}_{1,T}-\mathcal{D}\mathcal{Z}_{0,T})P^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}G_{k}\Sigma_{k}G_{k}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}P(\mathcal{X}_{1,T}-\mathcal{D}\mathcal{Z}_{0,T})^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}

\displaystyle+(\mathcal{X}_{1,T}-\mathcal{D}\mathcal{Z}_{0,T})P^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}G_{k}\Sigma^{\chi\zeta}_{k}\mathcal{D}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}

\displaystyle+\mathcal{D}(\Sigma^{\chi\zeta}_{k})^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}G_{k}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}P(\mathcal{X}_{1,T}-\mathcal{D}\mathcal{Z}_{0,T})^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}+\mathcal{D}\Sigma^{\zeta}\mathcal{D}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}},

(27)

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\chi\zeta}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{X}_{1,T}-\mathcal{D}\mathcal{Z}_{0,T})P^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}G_{k}\Sigma^{\chi\zeta}_{k}\Psi^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}+\mathcal{D}\Sigma^{\zeta}\Psi^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}.

(28)

In this data-driven formulation, the matrices
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
are not parameterized separately, but the whole feedback interconnection is expressed in terms of the data. Thus, we cannot express the term
\mathcal{B}\Sigma^{\nu}\mathcal{B}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}
in (
12
) in a model-independent fashion, which is the reason for using the sub-optimal control law
u_{k}=K_{k}(\chi_{k}-\mu_{k})+v_{k}
in (
27
) instead of
u_{k}=K_{k}(\chi_{k}-\mu_{k})+v_{k}+\nu_{k}
.

Next, the output covariance is expressed in terms of the optimization variables as

\displaystyle\Sigma^{y}_{k}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[(y_{k}-\mu^{y}_{k})(y_{k}-\mu^{y}_{k})^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}\Sigma_{k}\mathcal{C}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}+\mathcal{C}\Sigma^{\chi\zeta}_{k}+(\Sigma^{\chi\zeta}_{k})^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}\mathcal{C}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}+\Sigma^{\zeta}.

(29)

See the Appendix for the full derivation. Since
\mathcal{C}
is unknown, we will use
\hat{\mathcal{C}}
from (
23
) to implement (
29
).

Furthermore, from the first block equation of (
26
), the objective function of the optimization problem is expressed in terms of the optimization variables as

\displaystyle J=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}(Q\Sigma_{k})+\mathrm{tr}(R\Theta G_{k}\Sigma_{k}G_{k}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}\Theta^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}),

(30)

where
\Theta=\begin{bmatrix}U_{0,T}U_{0,T}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}&U_{0,T}\mathcal{X}_{0,T}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}\end{bmatrix}
.

To implement equations (
27
)-(
29
), the noise realization and covariance
\mathcal{Z}_{0,T},\Sigma^{\zeta}
, the dynamics and noise model matrices
\mathcal{D},\Psi
, and the initial cross-covariance
\Sigma^{\chi\zeta}_{i}
must be estimated. To this end, we first approximate the realizations of
\xi
and
\zeta
from equations (
18
) and (
19
) as the residuals between the observations and the predictions of an estimated model. Then, the covariances of
\xi_{k}
and
\zeta_{k}
are computed as the sample covariances of the estimated realizations, as follows

\displaystyle\hat{\Xi}_{0,T}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{X}_{1,T}-\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\mathcal{B}}&\hat{\mathcal{A}}\end{bmatrix}P,\quad\quad\hat{\Sigma}^{\xi}=\frac{1}{T}\hat{\Xi}_{0,T}\hat{\Xi}_{0,T}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}

(31)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{Z}}_{0,T}

\displaystyle=Y_{0,T}-\hat{\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{X}_{0,T},\quad\quad\hat{\Sigma}^{\zeta}=\frac{1}{T}\hat{\mathcal{Z}}_{0,T}\hat{\mathcal{Z}}_{0,T}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}.

(32)

Since the noise process is stationary,
\hat{\Sigma}^{\xi}_{k}\equiv\hat{\Sigma}^{\xi}
and
\hat{\Sigma}^{\zeta}_{k}\equiv\hat{\Sigma}^{\zeta}
. Additionally, an estimate,
\hat{\Psi}
, of the noise model (
10
) is obtained using TLS with the estimated realizations
\hat{\mathcal{Z}}_{0,T-1}\triangleq\mathcal{H}_{0,1,T-1}(\hat{\zeta})
as the regressors and
\hat{\mathcal{Z}}_{1,T-1}\triangleq\mathcal{H}_{1,1,T-1}(\hat{\zeta})
as the observations. Furthermore, using ordinary least squares on (
11
), we compute an approximation,
\hat{\mathcal{D}}
, of
\mathcal{D}
using
\hat{\mathcal{Z}}_{0,T}
as the regressors and
\hat{\Xi}_{0,T}
as the observations. Finally, expressing the output equation of (
8
) for the estimated model, multiplying from the right by
\chi_{k}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}
, and taking expectations, an estimate,
\hat{\Sigma}^{\chi\zeta}_{i}
, of the initial cross-covariance is obtained as

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}^{\chi\zeta}_{i}=(\mathbb{E}[y_{k}\chi_{k}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}]-\hat{\mathcal{C}}\mathbb{E}[\chi_{k}\chi_{k}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}])^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}},

(33)

where the expectations are computed as
\mathbb{E}[y_{k}\chi_{k}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}]=Y_{0,T}\mathcal{X}_{0,T}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}/T
and
\mathbb{E}[\chi_{k}\chi_{k}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}]=\mathcal{X}_{0,T}\mathcal{X}_{0,T}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}/T
.

Equations (
27
), (
28
), and (
30
), yield a non-convex optimization problem due to the terms
G_{k}\Sigma^{\chi\zeta}_{k}
and
G_{k}\Sigma_{k}G_{k}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}
. To alleviate this issue, we define the new decision variables
U_{k}\triangleq G_{k}\Sigma_{k}
and
W_{k}\triangleq G_{k}\Sigma^{\chi\zeta}_{k}
. The term
G_{k}\Sigma_{k}G_{k}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}
now becomes
U_{k}\Sigma^{-1}_{k}U_{k}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}
, which is still non-convex. In order to convexify the problem, we introduce the slack variable
Y_{k}\succeq U_{k}\Sigma_{k}^{-1}U_{k}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}
.

Remark 2
.

In contrast to
[
26
]
, the relaxation
Y_{k}\succeq U_{k}\Sigma_{k}^{-1}U_{k}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}
does not result in a lossless convexification for the present problem. This implies that, if the estimated terms in (
34
) were exactly equal to the true ones, then the relaxed terminal constraint
\Sigma^{y}_{N}\preceq\Sigma^{y}_{f}
would be met. However, under the presence of estimation errors,
satisfaction of this terminal condition is not guaranteed. We consider the quantification of these errors and the design of robust covariance steering controllers as a valuable direction for further investigation.

The relaxed optimization problem is formulated as follows

\displaystyle\min_{\Sigma_{k},\Sigma^{\chi\zeta}_{k},U_{k},W_{k},Y_{k}}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}(Q\Sigma_{k})+\mathrm{tr}(R\Theta Y_{k}\Theta^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}),

(34a)

such that, for all
k=0,1,\dots,N-1
,

\displaystyle\Sigma_{k+1}

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{X}_{1,T}-\hat{\mathcal{D}}\hat{\mathcal{Z}}_{0,T})P^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}Y_{k}P(\mathcal{X}_{1,T}-\hat{\mathcal{D}}\hat{\mathcal{Z}}_{0,T})^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}

\displaystyle+(\mathcal{X}_{1,T}-\hat{\mathcal{D}}\hat{\mathcal{Z}}_{0,T})P^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}W_{k}\hat{\mathcal{D}}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}

\displaystyle+\hat{\mathcal{D}}W_{k}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}P(\mathcal{X}_{1,T}-\hat{\mathcal{D}}\hat{\mathcal{Z}}_{0,T})^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}+\hat{\mathcal{D}}\hat{\Sigma}^{\zeta}\hat{\mathcal{D}}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}},

(34b)

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\chi\zeta}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{X}_{1,T}-\hat{\mathcal{D}}\hat{\mathcal{Z}}_{0,T})P^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}W_{k}\hat{\Psi}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}+\hat{\mathcal{D}}\hat{\Sigma}^{\zeta}\hat{\Psi}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}},

(34c)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{k}&U_{k}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}&\Sigma^{\chi\zeta}_{k}\hat{\mathcal{D}}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}\\
U_{k}&Y_{k}&W_{k}\hat{\mathcal{D}}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}\\
\hat{\mathcal{D}}(\Sigma^{\chi\zeta}_{k})^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}&\hat{\mathcal{D}}W_{k}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}&\hat{\mathcal{D}}\hat{\Sigma}^{\zeta}\hat{\mathcal{D}}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0,

(34d)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{k}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{X}_{0,T}U_{0,T}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}&\mathcal{X}_{0,T}\mathcal{X}_{0,T}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}\end{bmatrix}U_{k},

(34e)

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\chi\zeta}_{k}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{X}_{0,T}U_{0,T}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}&\mathcal{X}_{0,T}\mathcal{X}_{0,T}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}\end{bmatrix}W_{k},

(34f)

\displaystyle\Sigma^{y}_{f}

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathcal{C}}\Sigma_{N}\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}+\hat{\mathcal{C}}\Sigma^{\chi\zeta}_{N}+(\Sigma^{\chi\zeta}_{N})^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}+\hat{\Sigma}^{\zeta},

(34g)

\displaystyle\Sigma^{y}_{i}

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathcal{C}}\Sigma_{0}\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}+\hat{\mathcal{C}}\Sigma^{\chi\zeta}_{0}+(\Sigma^{\chi\zeta}_{0})^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}+\hat{\Sigma}^{\zeta},

(34h)

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\chi\zeta}_{0}

\displaystyle=\hat{\Sigma}^{\chi\zeta}_{i},

(34i)

where the constraints (
34e
) and (
34f
) come from the second block of (
26
) after multiplying by
\Sigma_{k}
and
\Sigma_{k}^{\chi\zeta}
from the right, respectively. Moreover, the constraint (
34d
) implies that the covariance matrix of the the vector
a_{k}\triangleq[\chi_{k}\ G_{k}\chi_{k}\ \hat{\mathcal{D}}\zeta_{k}]^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}
must be positive semi-definite.
