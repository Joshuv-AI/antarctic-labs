A noisy min-max game on trees
Omer Angel, Gourab Ray, Yinon Spinka

Abstract
We study a noisy version of a min-max type zero-sum game on the d d -ary tree. Each edge of the tree is assigned an i.i.d. cookie, distributed uniformly on { + 1 , − 1 } \{+1,-1\} . The game is played as follows: starting at the root, two players alternate turns in choosing a child to move to, with the game ending after each player took n n turns. Both players have full knowledge of the cookies on the whole tree. The cookies along the traversed edges are picked up and placed in a shared cookie jar. The first player’s payoff is the sum of the cookies in the cookie jar, while the second player pays that sum. The value V n V_{n} of the n n -round game is the largest signed sum which can be guaranteed by the first player. We analyze the value V n V_{n} and show that as n → ∞ n\to\infty , the value is tight for d = 2 d=2 , converges in distribution for d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 and converges almost surely for d ≥ 15 d\geq 15 . Along the way, we prove various tightness and double exponential tail decay results. The analysis is a mix of percolation type arguments for large d d , and iterations on distributions combined with interval arithmetic for small d d . For d = 2 d=2 we prove the existence of a continuum of fixed points for this iteration, highlighting surprising qualitative differences with the case of d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 . The question of convergence for d = 2 d=2 remains open.

1.1
Background

There have been a handful of previous works on min-max models of similar flavor.
Pearl originally studied the min-max game on a regular tree
[
8
]
, where one puts i.i.d. Uniform
[0,1]
cookies on the leaves, with no additional cookies elsewhere.
The game then proceeds exactly as in our setup, where players alternately try to maximize and minimize the cookie they get when they reach the leaves (the analogue in our version is that all cookies are 0 except on the edges adjacent to the leaves).
Alternatively, one may view our game as a modification of Pearl’s game, where the values on the leaves are not i.i.d., but generated by a branching random walk.
Pearl proved that the value of the game converges (with no scaling) to a deterministic constant, which has an explicit characterization, and that the same is true when the cookies have an arbitrary common distribution whose CDF is continuous and strictly increasing.
It was subsequently shown in
[
2
]
that after a proper centering and rescaling of the value, one has convergence in distribution to a truly random value, whose distribution does not depend on the cookie distribution.

Later, a similar game was considered on Bienayme–Galton–Watson trees by Martin and Stasiński
[
6
]
, and by Holroyd and Martin
[
5
]
.
They showed that the (unscaled) limit could be truly random, depending on the offspring distribution. They too obtained results for the rescaled limit.
We point out that in the non-noisy version of the problem, the convergence of the distributional recursion of the CDF is much more straightforward to obtain because of inherent monotonicity (see, for e.g., the proof of
[
6
, Theorem 1.2]
).

In contrast, there is no obvious monotonicity in the distributional recursion of the noisy version of the model (see (
1.2
)).
Thus our proofs of convergence (
1.3
and
1.2
) are significantly more involved and take up the majority of this article.
Indeed, even the tightness of
V_{n}
is not obvious in our case, whereas it is obvious in the non-noisy version of the game.
There is also no clear characterization of the limit in terms of the cookie distribution for us, whereas such characterizations are some of the main results in
[
8
,
6
]
.

Our model was studied by Devroye and Kamoun
[
4
]
.
The considered the
d
-ary tree where each edge is assigned an i.i.d. variable
X_{e}
which could take other distributions.
Among their results is a law of large numbers for
V_{n}
, namely that under mild conditions,
n^{-1}V_{n}
converges to a deterministic limit.
The limit value depends on the law of
X
in a non-trivial way, though in our setting of uniform
\pm 1
weights it is easy to show the limit must be 0, so that
V_{n}=o(n)
.

An important aspect of such models is the concept of
endogeny
,
introduced by Aldous and Bandopadhyay
[
1
]
in the setting of a recursive distributional equation (RDE) and a recursive tree process (RTP).
We briefly describe the idea.
A RDE is an equation of the form

Z\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}g(\xi_{i},(Z_{i})_{1\leq i\leq N}).

Here,
N
is a natural number (possibly random),
\xi
is a random variable,

(Z_{i})_{i}
are i.i.d. random variables with distribution
Z
and independent of
(\xi_{i})
, and
g
is an explicit function with appropriate domain and range.
A RTP is a realization of such an RDE as a family of random variables
(Z_{v},\xi_{v})_{v\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}
on a common probability space such that

Z_{v}=g(\xi_{v},(Z_{u})_{u\text{ is a child of }v})\qquad\text{for all $v$ almost surely},

with
(\xi_{v})_{v}
being i.i.d. copies of
(\xi_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
,
and
(Z_{v})_{v\text{ in level }n}
being i.i.d. random variables with some fixed distribution
\mu_{n}
.
Moreover, we require the
Z_{v}
in level
n
to be independent even when conditioned on
(\xi_{v})_{v\text{ in levels below }n}
.
The RTP is said to be invariant if
\mu_{n}
is the same for all
n
.
Finally, an invariant RTP is said to be
endogenous
if the value
Z_{\rho}
at the root
\rho
of the tree is a measurable function of the i.i.d. random variables
(\xi_{v})
.
See
[
1
, Sections 2.3 and 2.4]
for details.

Returning to our context, we have an RDE of the form

V_{v}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}g((X_{e})_{e\in\mathcal{S}_{v}},(V_{u})_{u\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{v}},),

where
\mathcal{S}_{v}
contains the
d(d+1)
edges in the subtree of depth 2 below
v
, and
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{v}
contains the
d^{2}
grandchildren of
v
. We recall that
X_{e}
is the cookie attached to an edge
e
.
Here,
V_{v}
should be thought of as the value of the game played in the subtree rooted at
v
, and
g
is the corresponding min-max function. Note that we only consider here vertices that are at an even distance from the root.
To each CDF
F
that is a fixed point for
\Psi
, corresponds a unique invariant RTP given by the same equation above, but where the equality now holds almost surely, with the
V_{v}
distributed according to
F
.
When
V_{n}
converges in distribution, the CDF
F_{\infty}
of the limiting distribution is such a fixed point.
When
V_{n}
converges almost surely, the invariant RTP corresponding to
F_{\infty}
can be constructed explicitly by taking the appropriate almost sure limit at each vertex.
In particular, the value at the root is a function solely of the cookies, which means that this RTP is endogenous.
In general, if
V_{n}
converges in distribution, then the corresponding invariant RTP is endogenous if and only if
V_{n}
converges almost surely.
In particular, this leads us to the following simple consequence of
1.3
.

Corollary 1.6
.

The invariant recursive tree process corresponding to the limiting distribution of the noisy min-max game (with zero boundary conditions) is endogenous for
d\geq 15
.

Notation and formal definitions.

Recall that every edge
e
in
\mathbb{T}_{d}
has an associated cookie
X_{e}
, with
(X_{e})
being independent random variables which are uniform on
\{-1,1\}
.
As a matter of convenience, for a site
v\neq\rho
, we write
X_{v}
for
X_{e}
, where
e
is the edge between
v
and its parent.
We write
N(v)
for the set of children of
v
.

Fix
n\geq 0
. Recall that we have defined the value
V_{n}
of the
n
-round game. This can be thought of as the “final” value of the
n
-round game. Let us extend this definition to “intermediate” values
V_{n}(v)
for all
v
of height at most
2n
.
We first set the boundary values to be
V_{n}(v)=0
for all vertices
v
at height
2n
.
Next, given a vertex
v\in\mathbb{T}_{d}
at height less than
2n
, we define

\displaystyle V_{n}(v)

\displaystyle=\max\{X_{uv}+V_{n}(u):u\in N(v)\},

\displaystyle v\text{ even},

(1.1)

\displaystyle V_{n}(v)

\displaystyle=\min\{X_{uv}+V_{n}(u):u\in N(v)\},

\displaystyle v\text{ odd}.

Observe that
V_{n}(\rho)=V_{n}
.
We may regard
V_{n}(v)
as the value of the game played on the subtree rooted at
v
up to depth
2n
, with the maximizer (resp. minimizer) playing first if
v
is even (resp. odd).
Note also that
V_{n}(v)
is even for
v
even, and odd for
v
odd.
Observe also that the distribution of
V_{n}(v)
depends only on
2n-|v|
, where
|v|
is the height on
v
.

We say that a CDF
F
is even if it is the CDF of a random variable supported on even integers (equivalently, if
F(2i)=F(2i+1)
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
).
Recall that
\Phi
and
\Phi^{\prime}
are the maps describing the evolution of a CDF at a single “max” or “min” step of the game, respectively, and that
\Psi=\Phi\circ\Phi^{\prime}
describes the evolution of one round of the game. In particular, letting
F_{2n}
be the CDF of
V_{n}
, and
F_{2n-1}
the CDF of
V_{n}(u)
for some child
u
of
\rho
, we have that

F_{n+1}=\begin{cases}\Phi^{\prime}(F_{n})&\text{ if }n\text{ is even}\\
\Phi(F_{n})&\text{ if }n\text{ is odd},\end{cases}

(1.2)

and hence,
F_{2n+2}=\Psi(F_{2n})
.

The above notations make sense for any tree and any distribution of the cookies.
In our case of the
d
-ary tree with uniform
\{\pm 1\}
cookies,

\Phi
and
\Phi^{\prime}
are the maps from
[0,1]^{\mathbb{Z}}
to
[0,1]^{\mathbb{Z}}
given by

(\Phi(x))_{i}:=\left(\frac{x_{i-1}+x_{i+1}}{2}\right)^{d}\qquad\text{and}\qquad(\Phi^{\prime}(x))_{i}:=1-\left(1-\frac{x_{i-1}+x_{i+1}}{2}\right)^{d}.

(1.3)

(More generally, if the cookie distribution has PDF
p
, then
\Phi=(p*x)^{d}
and
\Phi^{\prime}=1-(1-p*x)^{d}
where
p*x
denotes convolution.)
From here we also see that

(\Psi(F))(i)=g\big(F(i-2),F(i),F(i+2)\big),

(1.4)

where
g:[0,1]^{3}\to[0,1]
is defined by

g(x,y,z):=\left[1-\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{x+y}{2}\right)^{d}-\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{y+z}{2}\right)^{d}\right]^{d}.

1.3
Outline of the proofs

The proofs involve a mix of percolation type arguments for large
d
and contraction type arguments for small
d
.

The proof of
1.4
for
d=2
involve an analysis of the iteration along with symmetry arguments. The claimed symmetry of the fixed point distribution for
d=2
was suggested by simulations. Once we combine the symmetry of the fixed point CDF with the fact that it is a fixed point of the iteration, it is fairly quick to see that the fixed point CDF at
i
can be written as an explicit function of the fixed point CDF at
i+2
. Once reduced to this, after some calculus, one can see that there is no valid solution to the equations for
d\geq 3
, and there is a continuum of solutions for
d=2
. The symmetry result for
d\geq 15
can be seen by comparing the distribution the values between trees of even and odd heights, assuming we have almost sure convergence.

The proofs of
1.1
for
d=2
and double exponential tail involve analysis of the CDF. The tightness for
d=2
involve a different certain symmetry of the CDF (valid for all finite trees) along with further analysis of the CDF iterations.

The proof of almost sure convergence (
1.3
) involves a percolation type argument. The idea is that if
d
is large, then we expect the value at even heights to be
0
and at odd heights to be
-1
with very high probability. This can be seen fairly easily near the leaves by analyzing the minimum and maximum operations. This motivates defining a ‘defect’ to be the set of odd vertices with height not equal to
-1
and the set of even vertices with height not equal to
0
. Then a Peierl type argument is employed to show that the defect set is small and has an exponential tail. One difficulty in executing this idea to get almost sure convergence is that the values change as the height of the tree changes. So we need to work with the union of the defects for all heights
n
. For
d\geq 15
, it turns out that even the union of the defects is small. We did not try to optimize the value of
d
, although we doubt that this can be made to go all the way down to
d=3
.

The proof of the distributional convergence result (
1.2
) involves a Banach fixed point type argument along with computer assistance in order to verify a certain “initial condition” (but the proof is rigorous). The idea is as follows. Let
F_{2n}
be the CDF of
V_{n}
. We want to show that the operator
\Psi
which maps
F_{2n}
to
F_{2n+2}
(see (
5.1
)) is a contraction. However, it cannot be a contraction on the whole space because of the existence multiple fixed points. Nevertheless, if we iterate the operator enough times, it turns out that the CDF enters a ‘small’ convex set
U
containing the limiting fixed point where the operator is a contraction. Once we show that we enter
U
and never leave, we need to estimate the supremum over all
x\in U
of the operator norm of the derivative
D\Psi
evaluated at
x
(if we show this quantity is
<1
, we are done by a simple contraction argument). It turns out that the operator norm is smaller than 1 if
d\geq 5
, but is still larger than 1 if
d=3,4
. Nevertheless, it is the case that the largest eigenvalue of the operator is smaller than 1. To utilize this, we need to conjugate
\Psi
by the suitable linear operator
E
, which pushes the operator norm of derivative of the conjugated operator close to its largest eigenvalue.

We are left to argue that the existece of the small convex set
U
. This is done in the computer employing
interval arithmetic
in Python. The idea is that rather using floating point computations which are non-precise and would therefore accumulate errors, we instead ask the computer to record an interval which is
guaranteed
to contain the correct value of the floating point. Then, instead of doing arithmetic with numbers, the computer does arithmetic with intervals, in each step making sure that the correct value is contained (rigorously) within the interval it outputs. We have added
Appendix

A
for an exposition of the necessary background on this concept.

Since we are dealing with iteration of CDFs with potentially infinite support, we first need to reduce the iteration to a finite dimensional problem. It turns out that tracking the CDF
F_{2n}(i)
for
i\in\{-4,-2,0,2,4\}
is enough (this is not so surprising in light of the fast double exponential decay). The CDF on these 5 points are tracked using interval arithmetic.

A continuum of fixed points for
d=2

Our goal in this section is to prove items (i) and (ii) of
1.4
.
Recall the definition of the shift operator
(SF)(i)=F(i+1)
and
the operations
\Phi,\Phi^{\prime}
from (
1.3
), and that
\Psi=\Phi\circ\Phi^{\prime}
.
Note that all of these commute with
S
.

Proof of
1.4
(i).

Fix
d=2
.
We first observe that a CDF
F
has
\Phi(F)=S^{-1}F
if and only if it satisfies
\Phi^{\prime}(F)=SF
. Indeed,

\displaystyle(\Phi^{\prime}F)(i)=((A\Phi A)(F))(i)

\displaystyle=1-(\Phi(\mathbf{1}-F))(i)=1-\left(1-\frac{F(i-1)+F(i+1)}{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=F(i-1)+F(i+1)-\left(\frac{F(i-1)+F(i+1)}{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=F(i-1)+F(i+1)-(\Phi(F))(i).

Thus,
\Phi(F)+\Phi^{\prime}(F)=SF+S^{-1}F
, proving the claimed equivalence.
Let us also note that these equivalent conditions imply that
\Psi(F)=F
.
Indeed,

\Psi(F)=(\Phi\circ\Phi^{\prime})(F)=\Phi(SF)=S\Phi(F)=SS^{-1}F=F.

In summary, if
F
is a fixed point of
S\Phi
, then it is also a fixed point of
\Psi
.

Fix
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
We wish to show that there exists a unique CDF
F_{\alpha}
satisfying

F_{\alpha}(0)=\alpha\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Phi(F_{\alpha})=S^{-1}F_{\alpha}.

The second condition can be re-written to say that for all
i
,

F_{\alpha}(i-2)=(S^{-1}F_{\alpha})(i-1)=(\Phi(F_{\alpha}))(i-1)=\tfrac{1}{4}(F_{\alpha}(i-2)+F_{\alpha}(i))^{2}.

Equivalently,

F_{\alpha}(i)=h(F_{\alpha}(i-2))\qquad\text{where}\qquad h(x):=2\sqrt{x}-x.

(2.1)

Note that

0
and

.
Therefore, once we fix

for all

∎

Remark 2.1
.

The above proof does not rule out the existence of fixed points of

converges to one of the distributions

above.

Numerically, it is always one of the CDF’s

, which are determined by (

) and

Proof of

(ii).

Fix

and assume toward a contradiction that

is a fixed point for
\Psi
such that
\Phi^{\prime}(F)=SF
.
Note that

which implies that
\Phi(F)=S^{-1}F

A computation similar to the derivation of (

Using now

we see that

we see that

The second recursion then implies that

3
Tightness and doubly exponentially decaying tails

In this section, we prove
1.1
.
This is a combination of several arguments.
Tightness of
\{V_{n}\}
when
d=2
is proved directly.
Tightness for
d\geq 3
is a consequence of the convergence in distribution shown in
1.2
(the proof of which in
Section

5
does not rely on the apriori knowledge of tightness).
Finally, we show that tightness implies the double exponential tail for any
d\geq 2
.
Our proofs here are written for the case of 0 values on the leaves, but hold for more general bounded leaf values with minimal changes.
The proof for
d=2
is quite specific to the case of uniform
\pm 1
cookies.

Recall from
Section

1.2
that
F_{2n}
is the CDF of
V_{n}
and that
F_{2n+2}=\Psi(F_{2n})
. Recall also from (
1.4
) that

(\Psi(F))(i)=g(F(i-2),F(i),F(i+2)),

where
g:[0,1]^{3}\to[0,1]
is given by

g(x,y,z):=\left[1-\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{x+y}{2}\right)^{d}-\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{y+z}{2}\right)^{d}\right]^{d}.

(3.1)

This relates the CDF of
V_{n+1}
to that of
V_{n}
, which describes the lower tails in the sense that

\mathbb{P}(V_{n+1}\leq k)=g(\mathbb{P}(V_{n}\leq k-2),\mathbb{P}(V_{n}\leq k),\mathbb{P}(V_{n}\leq k+2)).

(3.2)

For the upper tails, defining

h(x,y,z):=1-g(1-x,1-y,1-z)=1-\left[1-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)^{d}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{y+z}{2}\right)^{d}\right]^{d},

(3.3)

we similarly have that

\mathbb{P}(V_{n+1}\geq k)=h(\mathbb{P}(V_{n}\geq k-2),\mathbb{P}(V_{n}\geq k),\mathbb{P}(V_{n}\geq k+2)).

(3.4)

3.1
Double exponential tails

We first show that tightness implies double exponential tails for any
d\geq 2
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Fix
d\geq 2
. For
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, denote
p_{k}^{+}:=\sup_{n}\mathbb{P}(V_{n}\geq 2k)
and
p_{k}^{-}:=\sup_{n}\mathbb{P}(V_{n}\leq 2k)
. Then for any integer
m
and positive integer
k
,

p^{+}_{m+k}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\big(2p^{+}_{m}\big)^{d^{k}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad p^{-}_{m-k}\leq\tfrac{1}{2d}\big(2dp^{-}_{m}\big)^{d^{k}}.

In particular, if
\{V_{n}\}
is tight, then
\sup_{n}\mathbb{P}(|V_{n}|\geq 2k)\leq e^{-cd^{k}}
for some
c>0
and all
k
large enough.

Proof.

We start with the upper tail.
Recall the recursion (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) for
1-F_{n}
.
Since
h
is non-decreasing in each of the coordinates, we have

p^{+}_{k+1}\leq h(p^{+}_{k},p^{+}_{k+1},p^{+}_{k+2})\leq h(p^{+}_{k},p^{+}_{k},p^{+}_{k}).

Observe that

h(x,x,x)=1-(1-x^{d})^{d}\leq dx^{d},

for
x\in[0,1]
.
Denoting
q_{k}:=2p^{+}_{k}
, we get that
q_{k+1}=2p^{+}_{k+1}\leq 2d(p^{+}_{k})^{d}=2d2^{-d}q_{k}^{d}\leq q_{k}^{d}
.
Hence,
q_{m+k}\leq q_{m}^{d^{k}}
, so that
p^{+}_{m+k}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}(2p^{+}_{m})^{d^{k}}
.

We now bound the lower tail in a similar way.
Recall the recursion (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) for
F_{n}
.
We have

p^{-}_{k-1}\leq g(p^{-}_{k-2},p^{-}_{k-1},p^{-}_{k})\leq g(p^{-}_{k},p^{-}_{k},p^{-}_{k}).

Similarly, we have

g(x,x,x)=\big(1-(1-x)^{d}\big)^{d}\leq\big(1-(1-dx)\big)^{d}=(dx)^{d}.

Denoting
q_{k}:=2dp^{-}_{k}
, we get that
q_{k-1}=2dp^{-}_{k-1}\leq 2d(dp^{-}_{k})^{d}\leq 2d2^{-d}q_{k}^{d}\leq q_{k}^{d}
.
Hence,
q_{m-k}\leq q_{m}^{d^{k}}
, so that
p^{-}_{m-k}\leq\tfrac{1}{2d}(2dp_{m})^{d^{k}}
.

Finally, if
\{V_{n}\}
is tight, then
p^{+}_{m}\to 0
as
m\to\infty
, and
p^{-}_{m}\to 0
as
m\to-\infty
, so that we make choose
m
large enough so that
2p^{+}_{m}\leq\frac{1}{e}
and
2dp^{-}_{m}\leq\frac{1}{e}
, which gives that

\sup_{n}\mathbb{P}(|V_{n}|\geq 2k)\leq p^{+}_{k}+p^{-}_{k}=p^{+}_{m+(k-m)}+p^{-}_{-m-(k-m)}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}e^{-d^{k-m}}+\tfrac{1}{2d}e^{-d^{k-m}}\leq e^{-d^{k-m}}=e^{-cd^{k}}.\qed

3.2
Tightness for
d=2

The following is a variation on a proof due to Svante Janson.
The proof relies on a certain relation between the distributions of
V_{n}=V_{n}(\rho)
and
\bar{V}_{n}:=V_{n}(u)
, where
u
is a fixed child of
\rho
.
This relation is specific to the case of
\pm
cookies with 0 boundary values.

Lemma 3.2
.

Fix
n\geq 1
. Then
V_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}-\bar{V}_{n}+\xi_{n}
for some random variable
\xi_{n}
(which may not be independent of
V_{n}
) having
\xi_{n}\in\{+1,-1\}
.

Proof.

The values
V_{n}(v)
have been defined in (
1.1
) so as to represent the “intermediate” values in the
n
-round game. Recall that in this game, players alternate turns, until they reach height
2n
of the tree, where the boundary values are all 0.
If the game begins at the root, there are
n
rounds with the maximizer moving first.
Let
V^{\prime}_{n}(v)
be defined in a similar manner (with respect to the same cookies), but the game continues until level
2n+1
of the tree, and the boundary condition is taken to be
V^{\prime}_{n}(w)=0
for all
w
at height
2n+1
.
The values
V^{\prime}_{n}(v)
represent the intermediate values for the same game.
If we begin at the root, the first player (who is still the maximizer) now has
n+1
turns, while the second player still has
n
turns.

Fix a child
u
of
\rho
. It is straightforward to see that
V^{\prime}_{n}(u)=-V_{n}(\rho)
in distribution. Indeed, consider flipping the signs of all the cookies. Let
V_{n}^{\prime\prime}(u)
be the value of the in the subtree (of height
2n
) below
u
where we reverse the roles of max and min. The value
V^{\prime}_{n}(u)
for the original cookies is exactly equal to
-V^{\prime\prime}_{n}(u)
for the flipped cookies (this can be seen easily by induction, starting from the leaves and going towards the root). Now note that
V_{n}^{\prime\prime}(u)=V_{n}(\rho)
in distribution since the height of the tree below
u
is
2n
for
V_{n}^{\prime\prime}
and the roles of max and min match for both. Since the cookies are uniform,
V^{\prime}_{n}(u)=-V^{\prime\prime}_{n}(u)=-V_{n}(\rho)
in distribution.

In addition, since
V_{n}(w)=0
and
|V^{\prime}_{n}(w)|\leq 1
for all
w
at height
2n
, a moment’s thought reveals that
V^{\prime}_{n}(u)-1\leq V_{n}(u)\leq V^{\prime}_{n}(u)+1
almost surely. Indeed, one can think of
V_{n}^{\prime}(u)
as solving the same optimization problem but with random values
V_{n}^{\prime}(w)
for all leaves
w
. The inequality is straightforward from this observation.
This completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 3.3
.

For
d=2
, we have
\mathbb{P}(V_{n}\geq 2)\leq\alpha
for all
n
, where
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}(\sqrt{5}-1)
is the golden mean.

Proof.

By the previous lemma,
\mathbb{P}(V_{n}\geq 2)=\mathbb{P}(-\bar{V}_{n}+\xi_{n}\geq 2)\leq\mathbb{P}(-\bar{V}_{n}\geq 1)
. On the other hand,
\mathbb{P}(V_{n}\leq 0)\geq\mathbb{P}(\bar{V}_{n}\leq-1)^{2}
. Thus,

\mathbb{P}(V_{n}\geq 2)^{2}\leq\mathbb{P}(\bar{V}_{n}\leq-1)^{2}\leq\mathbb{P}(V_{n}\leq 0)=1-\mathbb{P}(V_{n}\geq 2).

Since
x^{2}\leq 1-x
holds (for
x\geq 0
) if and only if
x\leq\alpha
, we deduce that
\mathbb{P}(V_{n}\geq 2)\leq\alpha
.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Fix
n\geq 1
. Then
V_{n}\geq_{st}-V_{n}-2
.

Proof.

Recall
V_{n}^{\prime}
from the proof of
Lemma

3.2
. Fix a child
u
of
\rho
.
We have seen that
V_{n}(u)\geq V^{\prime}_{n}(u)-1
almost surely and that
V^{\prime}_{n}(u)=-V_{n}(\rho)
in distribution. Clearly,
V_{n}=V_{n}(\rho)\geq V_{n}(u)-1
almost surely. In particular,
V_{n}\geq_{st}-V_{n}-2
.
∎

We are now ready to establish tightness.

Proposition 3.5
.

For
d=2
, the family of random variables
\{V_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
is tight.

Proof.

It suffices to provide a bound on the upper tail that tends to zero uniformly in
n
, that is,
\sup_{n}\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}\geq k)\to 0
as
k\to\infty
.
Indeed, the analogous fact for the lower tail then follows from
Lemma

3.4
.
We shall show that
\mathbb{P}(V_{n}\geq 2k)\leq p_{k}
for all
n\geq 0
and
k\geq 1
, where
p_{k}:=\alpha\cdot\beta^{k-1}
with
\alpha=\frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{2}
as before and
\beta:=1/\sqrt{5}
.

We prove this by induction on
n\geq 0
.
The induction base
n=0
holds trivially.
Let
n\geq 0
and assume the assertion is true for
V_{n}
, that is,
\mathbb{P}(V_{n}\geq 2k)\leq p_{k}
for all
k\geq 1
.
We need to show that
\mathbb{P}(V_{n+1}\geq 2k)\leq p_{k}
for all
k\geq 1
.
We have already established this for
k=1
in
Corollary

3.3
,
so it remains to show this for all
k\geq 2
.
Recalling (
3.3
) and (
3.4
), we need to show is that for all
k\geq 2
,

h\big(\mathbb{P}(V_{n}\geq 2k-2),\mathbb{P}(V_{n}\geq 2k),\mathbb{P}(V_{n}\geq 2k+2)\big)\leq p_{k}.

Since
h
is increasing in each of its variables, it suffices to show for all
k\geq 2
that

h\big(p_{k-1},p_{k},p_{k+1}\big)\leq h\big(p_{k-1},p_{k},p_{k}\big)\leq p_{k}.

Since
p_{k}=\beta\cdot p_{k-1}
and
p_{k-1}\leq\alpha
for
k\geq 2
, it suffices to show that for all
0<x\leq\alpha
,

h(x,\beta x,\beta x)=1-\big(1-\tfrac{1}{8}(x+\beta x)^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}(\beta x)^{2}\big)^{2}\leq\beta x.

This expands to

\tfrac{1}{4}(x+\beta x)^{2}+(\beta x)^{2}-\big(\tfrac{1}{8}(x+\beta x)^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}(\beta x)^{2}\big)^{2}\leq\beta x.

Discarding the negative term on the left-hand side, dividing by
\beta
and rearranging, it suffices to show that

\big(\tfrac{1}{4}(1+\beta)^{2}+\beta^{2}\big)x\leq\beta.

Finally, note that the coefficient precisely equals
\beta/\alpha
, so this indeed holds for all
x\leq\alpha
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Almost-sure convergence for large

In this section we prove

and
1.4
(iii).
The main idea behind our proof is that when

0
, while an odd vertex typically has value
-1
.
To capture this, let
B_{n}
denote the ball of radius

around the root, and define

S^{+}_{n}:=\{v\in B_{2n}:V_{n}(v)>0\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad S^{-}_{n}:=\{v\in B_{2n}:V_{n}(v)<-1\}.

(4.1)

We think of
S^{+}_{n}
(resp.
S^{-}_{n}
) as the set of vertices whose value in the
n
-round game is atypically high (resp. low).
The following claim describes some basic properties of these sets and their relation to the underlying cookies
X_{e}
.
Recall that
N(v)
denotes the children of vertex
v
.

Claim 4.1
.

Fix
n
and
v\in B_{2n}
.

1.

If
v\in S^{+}_{n}
is even, then
N(v)\cap S^{+}_{n}\neq\emptyset
.

2.

If
v\in S^{-}_{n}
is odd, then
N(v)\cap S^{-}_{n}\neq\emptyset

If

is odd, then

and

for all

If

and

for all

These claims are all simple combinatorial consequences of the definition of

and the recursions for values.
For example if the value at an even (max) vertex is high then at least one of its children must have a high value.
At odd vertices all of its children must have high values.
A detailed proof is below for completeness.

Proof.

Suppose
v\in S^{+}_{n}
is even. Then
V_{n}(v)>0
and it follows that
V_{n}(v)\geq 2
since
V_{n}(v)
is even. Hence,
\max\{u\in N(v):V_{n}(u)\}\geq 1
. Thus, there exists
u\in N(v)
such that
V_{n}(u)\geq 1
, so that
u\in S^{+}_{n}
and
N(v)\cap S^{+}_{n}\neq\emptyset
.
This proves the first clause.

Suppose
v\in S^{-}_{n}
is odd. Then
V_{n}(v)\leq-3
(since
V_{n}(v)
is odd) so that
\min\{u\in N(v):V_{n}(u)\}\leq-2
. Thus, there exists
u\in N(v)
such that

, so that
u\in S^{-}_{n}
and
N(v)\cap S^{-}_{n}\neq\emptyset
.

Suppose that
v\in S^{+}_{n}
is odd. Then
V_{n}(v)\geq 1
so that
\min\{u\in N(v):X_{uv}+V_{n}(u)\}\geq 1
. Thus,
X_{uv}+V_{n}(u)\geq 1
for all
u\in N(v)
. Let
u\in N(v)\setminus S^{+}_{n}
. Then
V_{n}(u)<1
so that
V_{n}(u)=0
(since

is even) and

.

Suppose that
v\in S^{-}_{n}

4.1
A reflection symmetry of the fixed point

Observe that if
V_{n}
converges almost surely, then so does
V_{n}(v)
for all
v\in\mathbb{T}_{d}
. Indeed, this holds for any even vertex
v
of height
m
due to the fact that
(V_{n-m}(v))_{n=0}^{\infty}
has the same law as
(V_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
, and it then follows for any odd vertex simply because the values of its children almost surely stabilize.
Recall the definitions of
\Phi,\Phi^{\prime},R
from the introduction.
The following establishes the first part of
1.4
(iii).

Theorem 4.4
.

Fix
d\geq 15
. For
v\in\mathbb{T}_{d}
, let
V_{\infty}(v)
be the almost-sure limit of
V_{n}(v)
as
n\to\infty
.
Then
V_{\infty}(\rho)=-V_{\infty}(u)-1
in distribution, where
u
is any fixed child of
\rho
. In particular, if
F
and
F^{\prime}
are the CDFs of
V_{\infty}(\rho)
and
V_{\infty}(u)
, respectively, then
F=\Phi F^{\prime}=RF^{\prime}
and
F^{\prime}=\Phi^{\prime}F=RF
.

Proof.

Let
V^{\prime}_{n}(v)
be defined as in (
1.1
), but the boundary condition now taken to be
V^{\prime}_{n}(v)=0
for all
v
at height
2n+1
. We have shown in the proof of
Lemma

3.2
that if
u
is a child of
\rho
, then
V^{\prime}_{n}(u)=-V_{n}
in distribution.
On the other hand, we claim that
V^{\prime}_{n}(u)
converges almost surely, and that its limit is almost surely equal to
V_{n}(v)+1
. Indeed, this follows from the proof of
1.3
. Thus,
V_{\infty}(u)=-V_{\infty}(\rho)-1
in distribution.
∎

4.2
Stability

In this section we complete the proof of
1.4
(iii).
The following result establishes a large basin of attraction for the fixed point corresponding to
V_{\infty}
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Fix
d\geq 15
. Let
F_{\infty}
be the CDF of
V_{\infty}
. There exists
\varepsilon>0
such that the following holds. Let
F
be a (even) CDF having at atom of size at least
1-\varepsilon
at 0. Then
\Psi^{n}F\to F_{\infty}
weakly as
n\to\infty
.

Our proof of
4.5
yields an explicit estimate on
\varepsilon
.
For large
d
, it enables us to take
\varepsilon=cd^{-2}
for some constant
c>0
(a posteriori, this can be bootstrapped to allow
\varepsilon
to be
1/d
up to a logarithmic factor; in fact, if we restrict to “one-sided” CDFs, we can even take
\varepsilon
tending to one; see
4.7
below). Since the size of the atom of
F_{\infty}
at 0 tends to 1 exponentially fast (via
Lemma

4.3
), this will imply, for large

, that
F_{\infty}
has a large neighborhood (in sup norm) which lies entirely in the basin of attraction, implying, in particular, that
F_{\infty}
is a stable fixed point (see
Remark

4.8
).

In fact, we prove something much stronger than the weak convergence in
4.5
.
Fix a configuration
\tau\in(2\mathbb{Z})^{\mathbb{T}_{d}\cap\text{Even}}
. Given
n\geq 0
, define
V^{\tau}_{n}(v)
as in (
1.1
), but with boundary condition given by
V^{\tau}_{n}(v)=\tau(v)
for every vertex
v
at height
2n
. This corresponds to an
n
-round game, where the boundary condition is not all 0, but is instead given by the configuration
\tau
.
The following result shows that
V^{\tau}_{n}(\rho)
converges to the same

as from the usual game, uniformly for a large class of
\tau
. The class we consider requires
\tau
to be zero at most places, but allows it to take arbitrary values elsewhere.

Theorem 4.6
.

Fix
d\geq 15
. There exists
\varepsilon>0
such that the following holds.
Let
(Y_{v})
be a collection of iid Bernoulli(
\varepsilon
) random variables, indexed by the even vertices of
\mathbb{T}_{d}
, and independent of the cookies
(X_{e})
. Then, almost surely, there exists an
N
such that
V^{\tau}_{n}(\rho)=V_{N}(\rho)
for all
n\geq N
and all
\tau
such that
\tau(v)=0
whenever
Y_{v}=0
.

Proof.

The proof is a modification of that of
1.3
.
Let
L_{m}
denote the set of vertices at height
m
. Let
\mathcal{T}
be the collection of all
\tau
such that
\tau(v)=0
whenever
Y_{v}=0
.
Define

S^{+}_{n}:=\{v\in B_{2n}:\exists\tau\in\mathcal{T},\penalty\ V^{\tau}_{n}(v)>0\},\qquad S^{-}_{n}:=\{v\in B_{2n}:\exists\tau\in\mathcal{T},\penalty\ V^{\tau}_{n}(v)<-1\}.

(4.2)

Note that these sets may now intersect
L_{2n}
(in contrast to before), and that
v\in L_{2n}
belongs to one of these sets precisely when
Y_{v}=1
. With this definition, all parts of
4.1
continue to hold (for
V^{\tau}_{n}(v)
) as long as the vertex
v
under consideration is not in
L_{2n}
.

We slightly modify the definitions of
S
and
K
from before.
Fix
N_{0}\geq 1
and let
T
be the set of vertices
v\in L_{2N_{0}}\cup L_{2N_{0}+2}\cup\cdots
such that
Y_{v}=1
.
Let
S
be the union of
S^{+}_{n}\cup S^{-}_{n}
over all
n\geq N_{0}
, and let
K
be the connected component of the root in
S\setminus T
. With these definitions, the proof of
Lemma

4.2
continues to hold (with minor changes) and yields the conclusion that
V^{\tau}_{n}(\rho)=V_{N_{0}}(\rho)
for all
n\geq N_{0}
and
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
.
It thus suffices to show that
\mathbb{P}(K\subset B_{2N_{0}-2})\to 1
as
N_{0}\to\infty
. For this, it suffices to show that
\mathbb{P}(|K|>k)\to 0
as
k\to\infty
uniformly in
N_{0}
.
To this end, we fix
N>N_{0}
, let
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{N_{0},N}
be the union of
S^{+}_{n}\cup S^{-}_{n}
over all
N_{0}\leq n\leq N
, and let
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}_{N_{0},N}
be the connected component of the root in
\mathcal{S}\setminus T
. It now suffices to show that
\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{K}|>k)\to 0
as
k\to\infty
uniformly in
N_{0}
and
N
.

The proof now proceeds along similar lines as that of
Lemma

4.3
, with the main modification arising from the need to take into account that
4.1
does not hold for vertices in
L_{2n}
. We borrow some notations from the proof of
Lemma

4.3
, with suitable modifications. Recall that in that proof we exploited the fact that the cookie values were forced in large regions (outer boundaries of
A^{\pm}
of suitable parity). In the current situation, some parts of the outer boundary belong to
T
, and thus will not have forced cookie values. Since
T
is only a small
\varepsilon
proportion of the sites, this will turn out to be a minor issue.

Denote
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}^{+}\cup\mathcal{S}^{-}
, where
\mathcal{S}^{\pm}:=\bigcup_{N_{0}\leq n\leq N}S^{\pm}_{n}
. As in the previous proof, we fix
A=A^{+}\cup A^{-}\subset B_{2N}
, and consider the events
\{\mathcal{K}\cap\mathcal{S}^{+}=A^{+}\}
and
\{\mathcal{K}\cap\mathcal{S}^{-}=A^{-}\}
separately.
Consider first the event
\{\mathcal{K}\cap\mathcal{S}^{+}=A^{+}\}
and suppose it holds. Observe that the cookies are all
1
on
\text{Even}\cap(\partial^{*}A^{+})\setminus T
. Indeed, if an even vertex
u
is not in
A^{+}\cup T
and it has a parent
p(u)
in
A^{+}\cap S^{+}_{n}
for some
N_{0}\leq n\leq N
, then it must be that
V_{n}(p(u))=1
and
V_{n}(u)=0
, and the cookie on the edge connecting them must be 1 (since we minimize over the children of
u
in the game). We shall gain in probability by showing that the set where the cookie values are forced is large. Indeed, note that every even vertex
v
in
A^{+}
has a child in
A^{+}
(applying the first item of
4.1
, recalling that
\mathcal{K}
does not intersect
T
, and
T
is contained in the even vertices by definition) so that we can apply
Lemma

4.10
to obtain that

|\text{Even}\cap(\partial^{*}A^{+})\setminus T|=|\text{Even}\cap\partial^{*}A^{+}|-|N(A^{+})\cap T|\geq\tfrac{1}{2}(d-1)|A^{+}|-|N(A^{+})\cap T|.

Consider next the event
\{\mathcal{K}\cap\mathcal{S}^{-}=A^{-}\}
.
Similarly to before, we observe that on this event all cookies are
-1
on
\text{Odd}\cap\partial^{*}A^{-}
. We want to show that this set is large. However, in order to apply our isoperimetric inequality, we would need that every odd vertex in
A^{-}
has a child in
A^{-}
, which is not necessarily the case as we insisted not to include
T
in
\mathcal{K}
. To get around this, we define
{\sf D}:=\{v\in A^{-}:N(v)\cap T\neq\emptyset\}
, and observe that the set
A^{-}\setminus{\sf D}
has the property that every odd vertex in it has a child in it. Hence,
Lemma

4.10
yields that

|\text{Odd}\cap\partial^{*}(A^{-}\setminus{\sf D})|\geq\tfrac{1}{2}(d-1)|A^{-}\setminus{\sf D}|,

which gives that

|\text{Odd}\cap\partial^{*}A^{-}|\geq|\text{Odd}\cap\partial^{*}(A^{-}\setminus{\sf D})|-|{\sf D}|\geq\tfrac{1}{2}(d-1)|A^{-}|-\tfrac{1}{2}(d+1)|{\sf D}|.

Thus, on the event
\{\mathcal{K}\cap\mathcal{S}^{+}=A^{+}\}\cap\{\mathcal{K}\cap\mathcal{S}^{-}=A^{-}\}
, the cookie values are forced on a predetermined set of size at least
\frac{1}{2}(d-1)|A|-|N(A^{+})\cap T|-\frac{1}{2}(d+1)|{\sf D}|
.
Summing over the choices of
N(A^{+})\cap T
and
{\sf D}
, we get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{K}\cap\mathcal{S}^{+}=A^{+},\penalty\ \mathcal{K}\cap\mathcal{S}^{-}=A^{-})

\displaystyle=\sum_{F,D}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{K}\cap\mathcal{S}^{+}=A^{+},\penalty\ \mathcal{K}\cap\mathcal{S}^{-}=A^{-},\penalty\ N(A^{+})\cap T=F,\penalty\ {\sf D}=D)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-\frac{1}{2}(d-1)|A|}\cdot\sum_{F,D}\mathbb{P}(N(A^{+})\cap T=F,\penalty\ {\sf D}=D)\cdot 2^{|F|+\frac{1}{2}(d+1)|D|}

\displaystyle=2^{-\frac{1}{2}(d-1)|A|}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[2^{|N(A^{+})\cap T|+\frac{1}{2}(d+1)|{\sf D}|}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-\frac{1}{2}(d-1)|A|}\cdot\prod_{v\in A}\mathbb{E}\left[2^{|N(v)\cap T|+\frac{1}{2}(d+1)\mathbf{1}_{\{N(v)\cap T\neq\emptyset\}}}\right].

Now it is straightforward to see that the random variable inside the expectation is
1
if
N(v)\cap T
is empty and is
O(2^{d/2})
if
N(v)\cap T
is a singleton. The lower order terms in
\varepsilon
do not contribute and we end up with
1+\varepsilon O(d2^{d/2})
which is enough for our purposes. Below is a detailed calculation.

The expectation in the product is zero unless
v
is in
L_{2N_{0}-1}\cup L_{2N_{0}+1}\cup\cdots
, in which case,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[2^{|N(v)\cap T|+\frac{1}{2}(d+1)\mathbf{1}_{\{N(v)\cap T\neq\emptyset\}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{\{N(v)\cap T=\emptyset\}}\right]+2^{\frac{1}{2}(d+1)}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[2^{|N(v)\cap T|}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{N(v)\cap T\neq\emptyset\}}\right]

\displaystyle{=(1-\varepsilon)^{d}+2^{\frac{1}{2}(d+1)}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\binom{d}{k}\varepsilon^{k}(1-\varepsilon)^{d-k}2^{k}}

\displaystyle=(1-\varepsilon)^{d}+2^{\frac{1}{2}(d+1)}((1+\varepsilon)^{d}-(1-\varepsilon)^{d})

\displaystyle\leq 1+4\varepsilon d2^{\frac{1}{2}(d+1)},

where in the last inequality we used that
(1+x)^{d}-(1-x)^{d}\leq 4dx
for
0\leq x\leq\frac{1}{d}
.
Hence,

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{K}\cap\mathcal{S}^{+}=A^{+},\penalty\ \mathcal{K}\cap\mathcal{S}^{-}=A^{-})\leq\big(2^{-\frac{1}{2}(d-1)}+8\varepsilon d\big)^{|A|},

Summing over the choices of
A^{-}
,
A^{+}
and
A
(as in the proof of
Lemma

4.3
) leads to the bound:

\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{K}|=k)\leq\left((3ed)\cdot\big(2^{-\frac{1}{2}(d-1)}+8\varepsilon d\big)\right)^{k}.

Since this bound does not depend on
N_{0}
and
N
, and the expression in the base of the exponent is less than 1 when
d\geq 15
and
\varepsilon
is small enough, we conclude that
\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{K}|>k)
tends to zero uniformly in
N_{0}
and
N
. Hence, almost surely,
V^{\tau}_{n}(\rho)=V_{n}=V_{\infty}
for all
n
large enough and all
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
.
∎

The proof of
4.6
yields an explicit estimate on
\varepsilon
, which satisfies, in particular, that
\varepsilon\geq c/d^{2}
for some constant
c>0
and all
d
large enough. This yields the same estimate for the
\varepsilon
in
4.5
. The following result bootstraps this to order
1/d
up to a logarithmic factor, and further shows that
\varepsilon
can in fact tend to 1 if one restricts to “one-sided” boundary conditions.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
d
be large enough. Let
F_{\infty}
be the CDF of
V_{\infty}
, and let
F
be an even CDF having an atom of size
p
at 0. Then
\Psi^{n}F\to F_{\infty}
weakly as
n\to\infty
whenever

p\geq 1-\frac{\log d-\log(5\log d)}{d}.

Moreover, if
F
is supported on non-negative integers, then the same convergence holds whenever

p\geq\frac{7\log d}{d}.

Proof.

Let
F
be a CDF having an atom of size
p
at 0. Recall that
\Psi=\Phi\circ\Phi^{\prime}
, where
\Phi
and
\Phi^{\prime}
correspond to the max and min steps, respectively.
Let
Z_{0},Z_{1},Z_{2}
be distributed according to
\Psi F,\Phi^{\prime}F,F
, respectively.
Let
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
denote the cookies of some vertex at height 1 and 2, respectively.
We have

\mathbb{P}(Z_{1}=-1)\geq\mathbb{P}(Z_{2}=0)^{d}-\mathbb{P}(Z_{2}=0,X_{2}=1)^{d}=p^{d}(1-2^{-d})

and

\mathbb{P}(Z_{1}>-1)=\mathbb{P}(Z_{1}\geq 1)\leq(1-\mathbb{P}(Z_{2}=0,X_{2}=-1))^{d}=(1-\tfrac{1}{2}p)^{d}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z_{0}=0)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}(Z_{1}\leq-1)^{d}-(1-\mathbb{P}(Z_{1}=-1,X_{1}=1))^{d}

\displaystyle\geq\big(1-(1-\tfrac{1}{2}p)^{d}\big)^{d}-\big(1-\tfrac{1}{2}p^{d}(1-2^{-d})\big)^{d}.

Thus, if
p\geq 1-(\log d-\log(5\log d))/d
, then
\mathbb{P}(Z_{0}=0)\geq 1-1/d^{2.5+o(1)}
, showing that
\Psi F
satisfies the assumption of
4.5
(using that
\varepsilon\approx d^{-2}
). We conclude that, for large
d
, if
p\geq 1-(\log d-\log(5\log d))/d
, then
\Psi^{n}F\to F_{\infty}
.

Suppose now that
F
is supported on non-negative integers.
In this case,
Z_{1}\geq-1
so that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z_{0}=0)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}(Z_{1}=-1)^{d}-\mathbb{P}(Z_{1}=-1,X_{1}=-1)^{d}

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}(Z_{1}\leq-1)^{d}\cdot(1-2^{-d})

\displaystyle\geq 1-d(1-\tfrac{1}{2}p)^{d}-2^{-d}.

Now, if we just assume that
p\geq 7(\log d)/d
, then we get that
\mathbb{P}(Z_{0}=0)\geq 1-1/d^{2.5}-2^{-d}
. We conclude as before that, for large
d
, if
p\geq 7(\log d)/d
, then
\Psi^{n}F\to F_{\infty}
.
∎

Remark 4.8

(Stability of the fixed point)
.

The results of this section imply a strong form of stability of the fixed point
F_{\infty}
when
d
is large enough. Indeed, by
Lemma

4.3
(and the simple fact that
V_{\infty}=0
whenever
\rho\notin K
), we see that
\mathbb{P}(V_{\infty}=0)\geq 1-e^{-\Omega(d)}
. Thus, if
F
is close to
F_{\infty}
, say in sup norm (much less is actually needed), then
4.7
implies that
\Psi^{n}F\to F_{\infty}
. In this sense,
F_{\infty}
is a stable fixed point for
\Psi
.

Remark 4.9

(Phase transition in boundary conditions)
.

4.5
implies a certain phase transition for the min-max game on a tree of large degree
d
.
Consider the
n
-round game in which the boundary values are given by independent Bernoulli random variables, which take values 0 or 2 with probability
p
and
1-p
, respectively.
Let
F_{\infty}
denote the CDF of
V_{\infty}
, and
F^{\prime}_{\infty}
the CDF of
V_{\infty}+2
(note that this corresponds to the limiting value for the game with all 2 boundary conditions).
4.5
implies that, for
d\geq 15
, the value of the game converges in distribution to
F_{\infty}
when
p
is sufficiently close to 1, and to
F^{\prime}_{\infty}
when
p
is sufficient close to 0.
This suggests that there is a critical value
p_{c}
which marks the transition between convergence to
F^{\prime}_{\infty}
and
F_{\infty}
.
Numerical computations applying
\Psi
repeatedly to different initial distributions also suggest that the limit flips from
F_{\infty}
to
F^{\prime}_{\infty}
at some critical
p
.
However, we do not know that there is a unique transition point, and have not ruled out that there are multiple or even a continuum of limits as
p
varies in
[0,1]
.
We do know from
4.7
, that for
d
large enough any such critical value would satisfy

\frac{\log d-\log(5\log d)}{d}\leq p_{c}\leq\frac{7\log d}{d}.

With a bit more work, it is possible to show that
p_{c}=\frac{(2+o(1))\log d}{d}
.
To give a simple heuristic that suggests this, note that for small
p
and large
d
, the value
V_{n}(u)
for a vertex at level
2n-1
has probability
1-(1-p/2)^{d}
to be
-1
, and otherwise is very likely to be
+1
, with a much smaller probability
(1-p)^{d}/2^{d}
of being 3.
If we ignore the 3, and negate the values (to account for the max player moving in the next level), we find that the fixed point of this operation to be when
p=(1-p/2)^{d}
.
If
p=\frac{\alpha\log d}{d}
this becomes
\alpha\log d=d^{1-\alpha/2}
, hence
\alpha\approx 2-\frac{2\log\log d}{\log d}
.
The solution of
p=(1-p/2)^{d}
agrees extremely well with
p_{c}
for all
d
: It is off by less than 3% relative error for
d=3
, and less than
10^{-4}
for
d=10
.

If the leaf distributions interpolate between all
0
and all
2k
for some larger value of
k
, the limit
\Psi^{n}F
seems to have
k
phase transitions.

4.3
Isoperimetry

For
A\subset\mathbb{T}_{d}
, denote by
\partial^{*}A:=N(A)\setminus A
the set of sites not in
A
whose parent is in
A
. We prove the following isoperimetric inequality.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{T}_{d}
be a finite set such that
N(v)\cap A\neq\emptyset
for all even
v\in A
. Then

|\partial^{*}A\cap\text{Even}|\geq(d-1)|A\cap\text{Odd}|\geq\tfrac{1}{2}(d-1)|A|.

An analogous statement holds when the roles of even and odd are reversed.

Note that if
A
is a connected set containing the root, then
\partial^{*}A
is just the usual external vertex boundary of
A
, denoted
\partial A
. We first need the following intermediate lemma.

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{T}_{d}
be a finite connected set containing the root. Then

\displaystyle|\partial A\cap\text{Even}|

\displaystyle=d|A\cap\text{Odd}|-|A\cap\text{Even}|+1,

\displaystyle|\partial A\cap\text{Odd}|

\displaystyle=d|A\cap\text{Even}|-|A\cap\text{Odd}|.

Proof.

One way to see this is by the following observation. Let
v\in A
and let
N_{A}(v)
be the set of children of
v
not in
A
. Then
N_{A}(v)
must be in
\partial A
and all of
N(v)\setminus N_{A}(v)
are in
A
and are of opposite parity to that of
v
. For the first equation, we add 1 to the right-hand side since the root vertex is not a child of any vertex.

We prove this formally by induction on
|A|
. For
|A|=1
,
A
is the root vertex, and this is trivial. Suppose that
|A|>1
. Let
v\in A\setminus\{\rho\}
be such that
N(v)\cap A=\emptyset
, and denote
A^{\prime}:=A\setminus\{v\}
. If
v
is odd, we have

\displaystyle|A\cap\text{Even}|

\displaystyle=|A^{\prime}\cap\text{Even}|,

\displaystyle|A\cap\text{Odd}|

\displaystyle=|A^{\prime}\cap\text{Odd}|+1,

\displaystyle|\partial A\cap\text{Even}|

\displaystyle=|\partial A^{\prime}\cap\text{Even}|+d,

\displaystyle|\partial A\cap\text{Odd}|

\displaystyle=|\partial A^{\prime}\cap\text{Odd}|-1.

From this and the induction hypothesis applied to
A^{\prime}
, we obtain the desired equality. A similar argument applies when
v
is even.
∎

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{T}_{d}
be a finite set with
k_{\text{e}}
(resp.
k_{\text{o}}
) connected components whose topmost element is even (resp. odd). Then

\displaystyle|\partial^{*}A\cap\text{Even}|

\displaystyle=d|A\cap\text{Odd}|-|A\cap\text{Even}|+k_{\text{e}},

\displaystyle|\partial^{*}A\cap\text{Odd}|

\displaystyle=d|A\cap\text{Even}|-|A\cap\text{Odd}|+k_{\text{o}}.

Proof.

Let
A_{1},\dots,A_{k_{\text{e}}+k_{\text{o}}}
be the connected components of
A
. Apply
Lemma

4.11
to each
A_{i}
in the
d
-ary subtree rooted at the topmost element of
A_{i}
, noting that the roles of even and odd are reversed when the topmost element is odd. Upon summation the lemma is obtained.
∎

Proof of
Lemma

4.10
.

The assumption of the lemma implies that
|A\cap\text{Odd}|\geq|A\cap\text{Even}|
.
Lemma

4.12
now gives the lemma.
∎

Proof of

V_{\infty}=V_{\infty}(\rho)

\mathbb{P}(V_{\infty}(v)\neq 0)=p

\mathbb{P}(V_{\infty}(v)\neq-1)=p

v

Recall from (

S^{\pm}_{n}

p=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\rho\in S_{n})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(|K_{n}|\geq 1).

Since

5
Convergence in law for low

In this section we prove

Recall that

is even, and so we can (and will) regard it as an element of

.
The main strategy is to think of

as a sequence in the Banach space
\ell_{\infty}(2\mathbb{Z})
, and show that
\Psi
is a contraction. To that end, we need to estimate the norm of the derivative of
\Psi
. However, as it turns out, the derivative of
\Psi
does not have norm strictly less than

uniformly over all of
\ell_{\infty}(2\mathbb{Z})
. Indeed, this is necessarily the case as there is more than one fixed point by shift invariance (if

is a fixed point, then so is

given by

).
Our modified strategy is to show that
\Psi
restricted to some small convex set has derivative less than 1 and is thus a contraction.
Moreover, we show that after some number of iterations, the CDF enters and is confined to this set.

The above strategy works as outlined for
d\geq 5
, but as it turns out, for
d=3,4
there is an extra layer of complicacy. In these cases, inside the relevant small convex set, the norm of the derivative is bigger than 1, but the largest eigenvalue of the derivative operator is strictly less than 1. So we need to appropriately conjugate the derivative of
\Psi
by a linear map (equivalently apply a invertible, bounded linear transformation to
\Psi
), so that the derivative of the transformed operator has norm strictly less than 1. In practice, we apply the conjugation for all

as it leads to better bounds.
In all these cases the proofs are computer assisted. We use interval arithmetic to formally rule out numerical errors.

We organize the proof as follows.
We first collect some sufficient conditions so that our contraction argument goes through, reducing the problem to a finite-dimensional problem. Then we verify the sufficient conditions using interval arithmetic in a separate
Section

5.2
.

5.2

The key concept used for the proof is known as interval arithmetic.
The core idea is as follows: if we iterate any function in a computer finite number of times, there are floating point errors, which renders the output merely a non-rigorous estimate of the true value. To avoid this, interval arithmetic replaces the numbers with an interval (which contains the initial inputs). Then, instead of doing regular arithmetic using numbers, a version of arithmetic for intervals is used. This is done in a way such that the interval arithmetic output of the same iterations is an interval which is rigorously
guaranteed
to contain the true value of the iteration. Of course, if the intervals end up being large after iterations, this approach is less useful.
We refer to
Appendix

A

in the notation of the previous section), and let
{\mathsf{D}}^{[\,]}_{2n,\text{approx}}
be the corresponding submatrix of
{\mathsf{D}}^{[\,]}_{2n}
.
Let

\text{Err}_{2n}:=\varepsilon_{z_{2n}(4).a,z_{2n}(-4).b}

(5.6)

For a finite matrix
A^{[\,]}
, with entries in
[\mathbb{R}]
, we denote

\|A^{[\,]}\|:=\sup_{i}\sum_{j}\max\{|A_{ij}.a|,|A_{ij}.b|\},

(5.7)

The reason for this notation is that if

is an interval arithmetic approximation of some matrix

for all

), then

is clearly upper bounded by
\|A^{[\,]}\|
.
We also call
\|A^{[\,]}\|
the
maximum absolute row sum
of
A^{[\,]}
.

Fact 5.6
.

Let
3\leq d\leq 15
.
There exists
N
such that the following holds:

•

Z_{2N+2}\subset Z_{2N}
in the sense that
Z_{2N+2}(i)\subset Z_{2N}(i)
for all

.

•

There is a real-valued

Note

makes sense as a matrix with entries in
[\mathbb{R}]
, as matrix multiplication of
[\mathbb{R}]
-valued matrices is well defined by performing elementary operations (only

and

[\mathbb{R}]

Proof of
5.5
given
5.6
.

Let

and
\tilde{E}
be as in the above fact. Take

for

It remains to verify that

Discussion and open problems

The binary tree.

This is the least well understood case of this problem.
We have seen that the game played on a binary tree has properties distinct from that when played on a
d
-ary tree of degree
d\geq 3
.
In particular, in
1.4
, we have shown the existence of a continuous family of fixed points possessing a certain symmetry which is not possible when
d\geq 3
.
Simulations clearly suggest that
V_{n}
converges in distribution to one of these fixed points.
It is not clear whether there is a.s. convergence for
d=2
.
See visualizations in

https://www.math.ubc.ca/~angel/minmax_game
.

Problem 6.1
.

Show that
V_{n}
converges in distribution when
d=2
.

Problem 6.2
.

Show that there are no solutions to the RDE beyond the ones we found.

The known solutions to the RDE satisfy
F(x+2)=h(F(x))
with
h(p)=2\sqrt{p}-p
.
While this is only relevant for even integers, one can iterpret this as follows.
Take any distribution satisfying the above recursion for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
If
Z
has that such a law, rounding
Z
down to an even integer will yield a discrete solution to the RDE.
However, it is easy to see that
Z
itself is also a fixed point of the RDE.
There is much flexibility in the choice of such a distribution: any increasing function on
[0,2]
with
F(2)=h(F(0))
can be extended to all of
\mathbb{R}
.

Problem 6.3
.

Is there an analytic CDF
F
satisfying
F(x+2)=h(F(x))
for all
x
?
Even if yes, it is not unique, as it may be composed with any analytic homeomorphism
\xi
of
\mathbb{R}
with
\xi(x+2)=\xi(x)+2
.
Possibly another assumption such as log-concavity may identify a unique canonical fixed point.

Trees of degree three and higher.

We have shown convergence in distribution of
V_{n}
when
d\geq 3
, and almost-sure convergence when
d\geq 15
.

Problem 6.4
.

Show that
V_{n}
converges almost surely when
d\geq 3
.

Problem 6.5
.

Identify all fixed points of the RDE.
We are only aware of the stable one and conjecturally the unstable one (see below), and their shifts.
Are there any others?

Random boundary conditions.

We have focused on the game in which in the “boundary conditions” (the values at height
2n
for the
n
-round game) are all 0.
Suppose we place boundary conditions which are 0 or 2, with probability
p
and
1-p
, independently at each vertex.
We have seen in
Remark

4.9
that when
d
is large, there is a transition from one limiting behavior (identical to that of the all 0 boundary condition) to another (the all 2 boundary condition), and that this occurs when
p
is of the order
(\log d)/d
.
However, we have not established that there exists a critical point
p_{c}
such that we get the former limit whenever
p<p_{c}
and the latter limit whenever
p>p_{c}
.

Problem 6.6
.

For
d
large, show the existence of a critical point
p_{c}
.

We believe that at the critical point there is a distinct limiting behavior.

Problem 6.7
.

At
p=p_{c}
, show that the root value converges to an unstable fixed point distribution which is different from the
p=0
or
p=1
limit.

Note that by definition, if the claimed fixed point at
p_{c}
exists, it must be an unstable fixed point.
In contrast, for
d=2
, simulations suggest convergence to a continuous family of distributions as we vary
p
(with the limit being one of the distributions described in the first item of
1.4
).
This is an indication of the nonexistence of a.s. convergence.

Other cookie distributions.

We considered in this paper the game in which the edge cookies have distribution uniform on
\{-1,1\}
.
It is natural to consider this game with other distributions.
Devroye and Kamoun
[
4
]
have proved a law of large numbers type result
V_{n}\sim an
for some
a
depending on the law of the cookies.
Some of our methods extend to more general cookie distributions.
For example, our methods for large
d
extend to cookie distributions which attain their essential infimum and supremum values with positive probability, but not otherwise.
In particular, we are unable to say anything about the convergence when the cookies are uniform on
[-1,1]
(but also for discrete distributions which do not attain their extreme values, e.g. signed geometric).

Problem 6.8
.

Does
V_{n}
converge in distribution (or perhaps almost surely) when the cookie distribution is uniform on
[-1,1]
and
d
is large enough? What about Gaussian?

Appendix A
Background on interval arithmetic

We provide a brief introduction to the theory of interval arithmetic. We refer to
[
7
, Chapter 1]
for a more detailed exposition.
The primary goal of interval arithmetic is to eliminate errors while doing computerized arithmetic involving floating points. For example, suppose we want to evaluate

\frac{1}{12}=.083333\dots

On the one hand, if we truncate the above decimal, it would introduce an error. On the other hand, the assertion

\frac{1}{12}\in[.0832,.0834],

is completely rigorous, but we pay a price by not specifying a more accurate representation of
1/12
. The idea now is to build an arithmetic for closed intervals rather than floating points, paying the price of sacrificing precision.

We define

[\mathbb{R}]=\{[a,b]:-\infty<a\leq b<\infty\}.

to be the space of all closed bounded intervals. We identify
[a,a]
with the real number
a
. We can now define the set of elementary operations

\mathcal{O}:=\{+,-,*,/,**\}

on
[\mathbb{R}]
in a natural way (here
**
stands for ‘power’). For every
\circ\in\mathcal{O}
,

[a,b]\circ[c,d]=\mathcal{H}\{x\circ y:x\in[a,b],y\in[c,d]\}

where
\mathcal{H}
denotes the convex hull, which in this setup is the smallest closed interval containing the set (division by an interval containing 0 is undefined). In fact, with this definition, we can write down formulas for addition and subtraction very easily:

[a,b]+[c,d]=[a+c,b+d]\qquad[a,b]-[c,d]=[a-d,b-c].

For multiplication and division, similar formulas can be written down, but we need to break into cases depending on the signs of the numbers involved. We refer to
[
7
, Table 1.1a,1.1b]
for more details.

For our purposes, we need to employ interval arithmetic to a function
\phi
from
\mathbb{R}^{k}
to
\mathbb{R}
which is a composition of elementary operations. Using the operations in the previous paragraph, we can naturally define the interval arithmetic version of
\phi
by composing the interval arithmetic versions of the elementary operations. Denote by
\phi^{[\,]}
the interval arithmetic version of
\phi
. Thus
\phi^{[\,]}:[\mathbb{R}]^{k}\to[\mathbb{R}]
where
[\mathbb{R}]^{k}
is a
k
-tuple of closed intervals, and it satisfies

\mathcal{H}\{\phi(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k}):x_{i}\in[a_{i},b_{i}]\}\subseteq\phi^{[\,]}([a_{1},b_{1}],[a_{2},b_{2}],\dots,[a_{k},b_{k}]).

For employing interval arithmetic on a machine, more care is needed. This is because the set
\mathbb{M}
of machine representable numbers is finite.
Most computers using binary encoding, so that even a number such as
1/12
cannot be represented precisely.
Thus when we input an interval
[a,b]
in the machine, it does an optimal outward rounding.
For example,
[a,b]
is approximated by
[a_{\downarrow},b_{\uparrow}]
where

a_{\downarrow}=\sup\{x\leq a:x\in\mathbb{M}\},\text{ and }b_{\uparrow}=\inf\{x\geq b:x\in\mathbb{M}\}

So for any elementary operation
\circ\in\mathcal{O}
and
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{M}
, the machine actually implements the operation

[a,b]\bar{\circ}[c,d]:=[\alpha_{\downarrow},\beta^{\uparrow}]

where
[\alpha,\beta]=[a,b]\circ[c,d]
.
Thus, in practice, when we ask the machine to implement
\phi
, it outputs
[\alpha_{\downarrow},\beta^{\uparrow}]
if
[\alpha,\beta]
is the output of
\phi^{[\,]}
. Let
\phi^{[\,]}_{\mathbb{M}}
denote the function which yields the machine output of
\phi^{[\,]}
. The most important fact for us is that if
x_{i}\in[a_{i},b_{i}]
for
1\leq i\leq k
then

\phi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\in\phi^{[\,]}_{\mathbb{M}}([a_{1},b_{1}],\dots,[a_{k},b_{k}]).

Sometimes, for reasons of speed, the Python package might not output the optimal interval. Nevertheless, what is important for us is that the output of the Python package is an interval which contains the optimal interval.
Consequently, the output
\phi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
is guaranteed to be in the output of the interval arithmetic version of
\phi
in the Python output. We ignore this subtlety, and simply write
\phi_{\mathbb{M}}
for the Python outputs as well.

In practice, we use the iv.mpf package in Python3, we refer to
this link
for the relevant documentation.

def interval_abs_sup(I):
"""Return sup{|x| : x in interval I} for I = iv.mpf([a, b])."""
a = mp.mpf(I.a)
b = mp.mpf(I.b)
return max(abs(a), abs(b))

def max_interval_abs_sup(A):
"""Return the largest sup-norm among entries of an interval matrix."""
return max([interval_abs_sup(A[i,j])
for i in range(A.rows)
for j in range(A.cols)])

def float_to_iv_hull(x):
"""
Take a float x and return a tiny interval guaranteed to contain it.
This protects against floating-point storage error.
"""
xf = float(x)
lo = np.nextafter(xf, -np.inf)
hi = np.nextafter(xf, np.inf)
return iv.mpf([mp.mpf(lo), mp.mpf(hi)])

def to_iv_matrix(A):
"""
Convert a numeric matrix into an interval matrix whose entries
are guaranteed to contain the original floating-point values.
"""
A = np.asarray(A, dtype=float)
n_rows, n_cols = A.shape
out = iv.matrix(n_rows, n_cols)

for i in range(n_rows):
for j in range(n_cols):
out[i, j] = float_to_iv_hull(A[i, j])

return out

return new_intervals

def H(x, y, z, d):
return 1 - 0.5 * (1 - (x + y) / 2) ** d - 0.5 * (1 - (y + z) / 2) ** d

def dgdx(x1, x2, x3, y1, y2, y3, d):
return iv.mpf([
(d**2 / 4) * H(x1, x2, x3, d) ** (d - 1) * (1 - (y1 + y2) / 2) ** (d - 1),
(d**2 / 4) * H(y1, y2, y3, d) ** (d - 1) * (1 - (x1 + x2) / 2) ** (d - 1),
])

(d**2 / 4) * H(y1, y2, y3, d) ** (d - 1) * (1 - (x2 + x3) / 2) ** (d - 1),
])
