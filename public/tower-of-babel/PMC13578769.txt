NOSIP promotes cell proliferation and motility by targeting SPTAN1 for ubiquitination and degradation in clear cell renal cell carcinoma
Authors: Ruoxue Cao, Bi Lian, Xueshan Pan, Junjie Gao, Dandan Yang, Kang Sun, Chaoqun Lian, Jia Ma, Zhiwei Wang, Jun Xia, Tong Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Kidney cancer is one of the most common malignancies of the urinary system, with early surgical resection and molecular targeted therapy being the primary treatment options for clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC). Nitric oxide synthase interacting protein (NOSIP) has been implicated in several types of malignancies; however, its role in ccRCC remains elusive. In this study, we employed a variety of techniques, including transfection, co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP), real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR), Western blotting, ubiquitin assay, and animal experiments, to explore the role of NOSIP in renal cancer cells. Our results demonstrated that NOSIP interacts with SPTAN1 and promotes the progression of ccRCC by facilitating the ubiquitination and degradation of SPTAN1, thus downregulating its expression. Elevated SPTAN1 levels were found to inhibit the proliferation and metastasis of ccRCC, while the downregulation of SPTAN1 reversed the inhibition of cell survival caused by NOSIP knockdown. Moreover, xenograft studies in nude mice confirmed that NOSIP promotes tumor growth in vivo. This work identifies NOSIP as a key player in the proliferation and apoptosis of ccRCC and suggests that it contributes to malignancy of ccRCC by modulating SPTAN1 expression in an ubiquitination-dependent manner. Our findings provide a theoretical basis and experimental foundation for early diagnosis and molecular targeted therapy of ccRCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58916-x.

Introduction

Renal cancer is one of the most common malignancies in the urinary system, accounting for the second largest number of urological tumors. Clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) is the most prevalent pathological subtype of renal cancer. According to World Health Organization (WHO) statistics, both the incidence and mortality rates of renal cancer are increasing1,2, with ccRCC comprising 75–80% of primary renal cancers3. Studies have demonstrated that the occurrence of ccRCC is strongly associated with mutations in the Von Hippel Lindau (VHL) gene on chromosome 3. These VHL mutations also contribute to the reduced sensitivity of ccRCC to chemotherapy and radiotherapy4, making early surgical intervention the most important treatment option for ccRCC5. However, the high recurrence and metastatic potential of ccRCC often results in patients losing the opportunity for surgery, contributing to its high mortality rate6,7. Currently, molecular targeted therapy and immunotherapy are emerging as the first-line therapies for ccRCC. For example, tyrosinase inhibitors such as cabozantinib reduce the aggressiveness of ccRCC and improve survival rates in patients with advanced disease8. Additionally, immune checkpoint inhibitors like navulizumab prevent tumor immune escape and restore the anticancer ability of cytotoxic T cells9. Although molecular immunotherapy plays an important role in ccRCC treatment, the precise mechanism is still not very clear, and issue such as side effects as well as drug resistance are still serious challenges. Therefore, identifying biomarkers and effective molecular targets for the early diagnosis of ccRCC remains a critical research priority.

Nitric oxide synthase interacting protein (NOSIP), also known as CGI-25, is a protein that interacts with nitric oxide synthase (NOS) in a reciprocal regulatory loop. NOSIP is widely expressed in eukaryotic cells10–13 and is localized to chromosome 19 q13.33, with expression in both the nucleus and cytoplasm11. NOS exists in three different isoforms: neuronal-type NOS (nNOS), endothelial-type NOS (eNOS), and inducible NOS (iNOS). In normal physiological conditions, NOS expression is low, but under stress, the body synthesizes large amounts of NOS, leading to increased nitric oxide (NO) production14. NOSIP modulates NOS activity and localization [9], facilitating the translocation of eNOS from the plasma membrane into intracellular compartments, thereby disrupting its membrane-bound functions and inhibiting NO synthesis10. This regulation of NO levels play an important role in both physiological and pathological processes, such as pain modulation15. Based on the property of NOSIP to regulate NO synthesis and release, it plays a wide range of regulatory roles cross various systems in the human body. In the digestive system, NOSIP can participate in the regulation of gastrointestinal secretion and motility16, and it has been linked to the development of intestinal inflammation17. In the respiratory system, NOSIP is crucial in regulating mucus secretion, cilia function, and bronchial activity by controlling NO synthesis in the airways and vasculature18. Reduced expression of NOSIP may interfere with NO production, which potentially lead to abnormal vascular remodeling and persistent pulmonary hypertension (PPH)19. In the nervous system, NOSIP is a key factor in the self-renewal of neural stem and neural progenitor cells, and the development of preneural tissues such as neural crest cells in animal models like mice and African clawed toads20,21. As a member of the E3 ubiquitin ligase family, NOSIP mediates the ubiquitination of the catalytic subunit of protein phosphatase 2 A (PP2A). Knockdown of the NOSIP gene has been shown to increase PP2A activity in the craniofacial tissues of mice, leading to holoprosencephaly and facial malformations22. In recent years, a large number of studies have highlighted NO as a potent regulator involved in a wide range of biological process, including cellular function modulation, signaling pathway regulation, neurotransmission, inflammatory responses, and vascular homeostasis23. NO release has also been shown to play an important role in tumor biology24–27, and studies have suggested that NOSIP may affect granulocyte differentiation, contributing to the progression of chronic myelogenous leukemia (CML)28. In neuroepithelial tumor cells, NOSIP significantly reduces the activity of nNOS, thereby inhibiting NO production and affecting the proliferation of epithelial tumor cells11. Given its regulatory role in NO synthesis and release, could NOSIP also be involved in the development of ccRCC? This is a critical scientific question that warrants in-depth investigation.

Spectrin, first discovered in erythrocytes, is a cytoskeletal protein consisting of α and β subunit heterodimers. In mammalian cells, different isoforms of spectrin are produced through the splicing of seven distinct mRNAs. The genes SPTA1 and SPTAN1 encode the αI and αII subunits of spectrin, respectively. SPTA1 is primarily expressed in erythrocytes, whereas SPTAN1 is found in non-erythrocytes29. It has been shown that spectrin plays an essential role in maintaining cell membrane stability, structure, and shape30. Beyond their role as structural components of erythrocyte membrane, spectrin proteins are also involved in cell adhesion, stretching, Golgi apparatus maintenance, primary transport pathways, and cellular signaling, and they play a critical role in the cell cycle31–33. SPTAN1, in particular, is an important cytoskeletal scaffolding protein involved in fundamental cellular processes, including cell adhesion, cell-cell contact, and apoptosis34,35. Spectrin proteins have also been associated with cancer, with increased expression of non-erythroid alpha II spectrin (SPTAN1) reported in a variety of tumor entities. SPTAN1 overexpression is correlated with tumor progression and invasion, suggesting that SPTAN1 could serve as a potential biomarker of tumor aggressiveness. However, further exploration is needed to fully elucidate the specific mechanisms by which SPTAN1 contributes to tumor initiation and development. The aim of this study was to investigate whether NOSIP could play a role in the development of ccRCC by regulating SPTAN1 through bioinformatics analysis, as well as in vitro and in vivo experiments. Our results indicate that NOSIP exhibits a carcinogenic function in ccRCC through its interaction with SPTAN1, leading to ubiquitin-mediated degradation. These findings suggest that NOSIP could serve as a potential therapeutic target for patients with ccRCC.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and cell culture

Human clear-cell renal carcinoma cell lines, 786-O cells and Caki-2, were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Corning, USA) and McCoy’s 5 A medium (Boster Biological Technology, USA), respectively. The 293T cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modification of Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) (Corning, USA). All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Clark Bioscience, USA) and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). The cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 36.

Plasmids and transfection

786-O and Caki-2 cells were seeded into six-well plates and transfected with either NOSIP cDNA constructor, empty vector, NOSIP siRNA, or nonspecific siRNA as a negative control (NC, GenePharma, China). In addition, transfection with SPTAN1 siRNA were carried out using the suitable transfection reagent (Thermo, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The sequences for the siRNAs are as follows: siNOSIP-1: 5’-CCU ACC ACG AGA AGA AGA ATT-3’; siNOSIP-2: 5’-CCC AGA UGA UGU CCA ACC UTT-3’; siNOSIP-3: 5’-GAA GCU GCA AGC GGA GAA ATT-3’; siSPTAN1-1:5’-GCU GAA GCC CUG CUA GAU ATT-3’; siSPTAN1-2: 5’-CCA CUG AAC UGA AAG GAA UTT-3’; siSPTAN1-3: 5’-GCU GGC AUC CGA AGA UUA UTT-3’. The SPTAN1 cDNA plasmid was purchased from Youbio Company.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, USA) and reversed-transcribed into cDNA by RevertAid Kit (Takara, Japan). The mRNA levels of SPTAN1 and NOSIP were detected by RT-PCR using the SYBR Green dye method37. The primer sequences were as follows: NOSIP, forward primer: GGA CTT CGA CTG CTG TTG TC, reverse primer: TGC TTC TCG TAG GCC TTC AT; SPTAN1, forward primer: AGC CGA ATT GAT GCC TTG GA, reverse primer: CTG TTG CTG GTT GGC TTC AC.

Western blotting analyses

Cells were washed with pre-cooled PBS buffer solution, and RIPA lysis buffer was added to collect the cells. Protein concentration was quantified (Beyotime Biotechnology, China), and the samples were denatured and prepared for loading. Equal amounts of proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for 1.5 h, and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C on a shaking platform. The primary antibodies include anti-NOSIP (1:2000, Cat No. 27979-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-SPTAN1 (1:2000, ab11755, Abcam), anti-Tubulin (1:1000, 2144 S, CST), anti-HA (1:2000, Cat No. 51064-2-AP, Proteintech), anti-Flag (1:2000, F1804, Sigma), anti-Ub (1:2000, Cat No. 10201-2-AP, Proteintech). On the second day, the membrane was washed three times with TBS-T, and the corresponding secondary antibodies (conjugate) were incubated for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized using ECL chemiluminescence and analyzed with Image J software38.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and ubiquitination assay

Flag-tagged SPTAN1 and HA-tagged NOSIP plasmids were transfected into 293T cells. After 24 h, MG132 (Sigma, USA) was added, followed by the addition of NP40 lysis buffer and protease inhibitors 6–8 h later. The whole cell lysate (WCL) was collected. 1 mg of total lysate was incubated with appropriate antibody binding beads for 3–4 h on a rotator. The immune complex was then washed four times with buffer and eluted after denaturation. SDS-PAGE was used for protein separation, and immunoprecipitated samples were analyzed by Western blotting using the relevant antibodies39.

MTT assay

Cell viability was assessed using an MTT assay. ccRCC cells, transfected with the specified plasmids, were plated in 96-well plates. After 72 h, 10 µl of MTT solution (0.5 mg/ml) was added to each well and incubated for 4 h. Following the removal of the supernatant, 100 µl of DMSO was added to each well. The OD490 values were then measured using a SpectraMax M5 Multimode Reader.

Wound healing assay

ccRCC cells were seeded into 6-well plates and transfected with NOSIP cDNA, NOSIP siRNA, and SPTAN1 siRNA the following day. When cell confluence reached over 90%, a scratch was created using a pipette tip, and the detached cells were washed away with PBS. The scratch healing process was photographed at 0 h and 16 h under a 200× microscope to assess cell migration40.

Migration and invasion assays

To observe the effects of NOSIP and SPTAN1 on the migration and invasion abilities of renal clear cells, a Transwell assay was performed. Transfected cells were plated 48 h prior to the assay. For the invasion assay, Matrigel (BD, Biosciences, CA, USA) was added to the upper chamber bottom. A total of 3 × 104 transfected cells were seeded into 24-well plates. After 24 h of incubation, migrated and invaded cells were stained with Giemsa Staining Solution. Five random visual fields were photographed under a 50× optical microscope, and the number of cells was counted for quantification41.

Apoptosis Detection

For apoptosis detection, NOSIP siRNA or plasmids were transfected into 786-O cells and Caki-2 cells. After 48 h, the cells were harvested, washed with pre-cooled PBS buffer, and stained with Annexin V-FITC (fluoresce isothiocyanate) and PI (propidium iodide). Flow cytometry (BD, USA) was used to detect early and late apoptotic cells.

Animal experiments

Four-week-old female nude mice were randomly divided into two groups (n = 6 per group) and subcutaneously injected with 6 × 107 786-O cells stably transfected with empty vector (EV) or NOSIP overexpression (NOSIP) constructs. Tumor size was measured with a vernier caliper every 6 days, starting 2 weeks after inoculation. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula (length ×width× height)/2. After 8 weeks, the mice were euthanized by gradual exposure to CO₂ delivered from a compressed commercial cylinder equipped with a flow meter, at a displacement rate of 30–70% of the chamber volume per minute for at least 5 min. Cervical dislocation was subsequently performed as a secondary method to ensure euthanasia. The tumor weight was recorded, and protein expression levels in tumor tissues was analyzed. This study was carried out in accordance with the recommendations in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the National Institutes of Health and ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org/). All experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Care and Use Ethics Committee of Bengbu Medical University.

Bioinformatics analysis

We utilized the LIMMA package in R language and TCGA database to investigate the differentially expressed mRNA levels of NOSIP and SPTAN1 in tumors versus normal tissues. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was performed to compare survival outcomes between groups with different expression levels of NOSIP and SPTAN1. Additionally, we used the CPTAC database via the UALCAN website to collect data on the differentially expressed proteins for NOSIP and SPTAN1 (http://ualcan.path.uab).

Statistical analysis

All in vitro experiments were independently repeated at least three times, and “n” represents the number of independent biological replicates. Data are presented as mean±standard deviation (SD). Normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For comparisons between two groups, the two-sample t-test was used. For comparisons among three or more groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was applied. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 17.0 software, and graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 6.0. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

NOSIP suppresses viability and inhibits apoptosis in renal carcinoma cells

In order to evaluate the effect of NOSIP on the malignant phenotype of renal carcinoma cells, we used three small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) specifically targeting NOSIP (siNOSIP-1, siNOSIP-2 and siNOSIP-3) and constructed NOSIP plasmids for overexpression. These were transiently transfected into 786-O and Caki-2 cells. RT-qPCR and Western blotting results showed that the mRNA level and protein expression of NOSIP decreased after siRNA transfection, while NOSIP expression was upregulated after transfection with NOSIP cDNA plasmid in cells (Fig. 1A-B). MTT assays demonstrated that the proliferation of 786-O and Caki-2 cells was significantly reduced after NOSIP knockdown, while upregulation of NOSIP enhanced cell viability (Fig. 1C). Flow cytometry analysis was used to assess apoptosis rates in response to NOSIP upregulation and downregulation. The results indicated that overexpression of NOSIP inhibited apoptosis, whereas decreased NOSIP expression increased the apoptosis rate in renal carcinoma cells (Fig. 1D-E).

NOSIP promotes migration and invasion in renal carcinoma cells

To further define whether NOSIP affects the motility of renal cancer cells, wound healing assays were performed to assess cell migration after NOSIP upregulation and downregulation. NOSIP-siRNAs and NOSIP cDNA were transfected into 786-O and Caki-2 cells. The results showed that cells in the NOSIP overexpression group exhibited greater migration ability compared to the control group. In contrast, NOSIP knockdown reduced the migration rates of 786-O and Caki-2 cells (Fig. 2A-B, and supplementary Fig. 1A-B). Similarly, Transwell assays were performed to assess the impact of NOSIP on the migration and invasion of renal carcinoma cells. The number of migrated and invaded 786-O and Caki-2 cells were measured following NOSIP knockdown and overexpression. Compared to the empty vector group, cells overexpressing NOSIP exhibited enhanced migration and invasion capacity, while the NOSIP knockdown group displayed the opposite effect (Fig. 2C-E, and supplementary Fig. 2A-B). These findings suggest that NOSIP may promote the progression of renal cancer by regulating the motility of renal cancer cells.

NOSIP ubiquitinates SPTAN1

To identify potential downstream targets of NOSIP in ccRCC, we first performed co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays to screen for proteins interacting with NOSIP in 293T cells. Notably, SPTAN1 was found to associate with NOSIP. Given that NOSIP functions as an E3 ubiquitin ligase, we hypothesized that NOSIP might regulate SPTAN1 expression through ubiquitination-dependent degradation. To test this hypothesis, we examined the effect of NOSIP on SPTAN1 protein and mRNA levels in ccRCC cells. Evidence suggests that the role of SPTAN1 in various tumors is unclear, as it appears to have different effects depending on the cancer type. In some studies, SPTAN1 has been found to be overexpressed and associated with cancer progression34. However, other research shows that SPTAN1 can inhibit the invasion and metastasis of certain cancers35. To clarify the molecular mechanism by which NOSIP regulates the biological activity of renal carcinoma cells, we investigated whether SPTAN1 expression is modulated by NOSIP. After transfecting NOSIP into 786-O and Caki-2 cells, we used Western blotting to assess SPTAN1 levels. The results showed that SPTAN1 expression decreased in both cell lines following NOSIP transfection. Conversely, SPTAN1 levels significantly increased when NOSIP was knocked down in renal cancer cells (Fig. 3A-B). Given that NOSIP functions as an E3 ubiquitin ligase, we hypothesized that NOSIP promotes renal cancer development by facilitating the ubiquitination and degradation of SPTAN1, thereby downregulating its expression. To confirm this, we treated 293T, 786-O, and Caki-2 cells with MG132, a proteasome inhibitor, and found that MG132 blocked the NOSIP-induced downregulation of SPTAN1 (Fig. 3C). Further, to investigate whether NOSIP directly interacts with SPTAN1, we co-transfected SPTAN1 and HA-tagged NOSIP with Flag into 293T cells. Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) results showed that NOSIP binds to SPTAN1 (Fig. 3D). Additionally, NOSIP ubiquitination was observed in 293T cells (Fig. 3E), confirming that NOSIP promotes SPTAN1 ubiquitination and degradation.

SPTAN1 inhibits the activity and motility of renal carcinoma cells

In order to further explore the effect of SPTAN1 in renal cancer progression, we investigated the biological functions of renal cancer cells by regulating SPTAN1 expression. Our immunoblotting data demonstrated that the expression level of SPTAN1 increased after transient transfection of SPTAN1 cDNA into renal cancer cells, while transfection of SPTAN1 siRNA resulted in decreased protein levels (Fig. 4A and supplementary Fig. 3A-B). Transfection of SPTAN1 cDNA also synchronously upregulated the mRNA level of SPTAN1. Transfection of SPTAN1 siRNAs reduced the expression of SPTAN1 at mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 4B). In addition, MTT assay showed that overexpression of SPTAN1 reduced cell viability (Fig. 4C and supplementary Fig. 3C). Furthermore, downregulation of SPTAN1 inhibited apoptosis in renal carcinoma cells (Fig. 4D). Notably, trauma scratch assay revealed that downregulation of SPTAN1 promoted the motility of renal carcinoma cells (Fig. 4E and supplementary Fig. 3D). Similarly, Transwell analysis demonstrated that downregulation of SPTAN1 promoted the migration and invasion ability of renal carcinoma cells (Fig. 4F and supplementary Fig. 3E). In summary, this finding indicates that SPTAN1 may be a tumor-suppressing protein in renal cancer.

Downregulation of SPTAN1 reverses the anticancer effect of NOSIP knockdown

To further determine whether NOSIP plays a carcinogenic role by downregulating the expression of SPTAN1, NOSIP siRNA and SPTAN1 siRNA were co-transfected into 786-O and Caki-2 renal cancer cells. Our Western blotting data showed that SPTAN1 knockdown negated the upregulation of SPTAN1 caused by NOSIP depletion (Supplementary Fig. 4A). Furthermore, we found that downregulation of SPTAN1 prevented NOSIP depletion-induced inhibition of cell survival (Fig. 5A). Subsequently, SPTAN1 knockdown counteracted the migration inhibition induced by NOSIP knockdown (Fig. 5B and supplementary Fig. 4B). Notably, the migration and invasion inhibitory effects of NOSIP depletion were diminished after SPTAN1 downregulation (Fig. 5C and supplementary Fig. 4C). Altogether, these findings suggest that NOSIP promotes tumor activity in renal carcinoma cells, at least in part, by downregulating SPTAN1.

Increased NOSIP expression and decreased SPTAN1 expression was observed in renal cancer patients

To examine the expression patterns of NOSIP and SPTAN1 at the transcriptional level, we analyzed the differential mRNA expression of NOSIP and SPTAN1 in renal cancer and normal tissues using the TCGA database. We found that NOSIP mRNA levels were higher in renal cancer tissues compared to normal tissues (P < 0.05), and higher NOSIP expression was associated with shorter survival in patients with ccRCC (Fig. 6A and B). On the contrary, the mRNA level of SPTAN1 was lower in renal cancer tissues than in normal tissues, and patients with high SPTAN1 expression had better overall survival than those with low SPTAN1 expression (Fig. 6A and B). It should be noted that these mRNA expression analyses are independent of the aforementioned ubiquitination mechanism and are presented solely to support the clinical relevance of NOSIP and SPTAN1 in ccRCC. In support of these data, we analyzed the differential protein expression of NOSIP and SPTAN1 using data from the CPTAC database accessed through the UALCAN website. The protein expression of NOSIP was higher in renal cancer tissues than that in normal tissues, while SPTAN1 protein levels were lower in renal cancer tissues (Fig. 6C and D). Finally, joint survival analysis further validated the interaction between NOSIP and SPTAN1 (Fig. 6E).

Overexpression of NOSIP promotes tumor growth in mice

We established a mouse model of renal cancer to further verify the effect of NOSIP on tumor growth. The results showed that tumors in mice inoculated with NOSIP plasmid adenovirus were larger compared to the control group. In terms of tumor weight, the NOSIP overexpression group was heavier than the mock-vehicle adenovirus group (Fig. 7A-C). After 8 weeks, tumor cells injected with NOSIP-simulated vector adenovirus exhibited less overexpression compared to those in the NOSIP group (Fig. 7D). Additionally, SPTAN1 expression levels were decreased in the NOSIP overexpression group (Fig. 7E). Immunohistochemical staining of the tumor tissues indicated that a higher number of tumor cells were observed in the nude mice injected with NOSIP-simulated adenovirus compared to the mock-vehicle adenovirus group (Fig. 7F). Taken together, these data suggest that NOSIP may promote the renal cancer progression by downregulating SPTAN1 expression.

Discussion

A growing body of evidence highlights the critical biological function of NOSIP in carcinogenesis and tumor progression, suggesting that targeting NOSIP could be a potential strategy for cancer therapy. It has been shown that NOSIP can influence glial cell proliferation and nervous system development17, but whether NOSIP affects the proliferation of other tumor cells remains to be fully explored. In this study, we used bioinformatics to analyze NOSIP expression in various tumor types, finding increased NOSIP expression in breast cancer, esophageal cancer, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, renal clear cell carcinoma, and lung cancer. However, the role of NOSIP in renal clear cell carcinoma has not been well understood. Here, we provide experimental evidence demonstrating the oncogenic role of NOSIP in ccRCC. Our study identifies SPTAN1 as a downstream target of NOSIP in ccRCC. NOSIP promotes tumor growth, migration, and invasion by inducing the ubiquitination and degradation of SPTAN1, underscoring its role in the progression of renal cancer.

NOSIP interacts with eNOS, promoting eNOS activation and subsequent production of NO 10, 11. As a small signaling molecule, NO is involved in tumor development42. Its tumor-promoting or tumor-suppressing effects see to depend on its concentration and duration of action. For example, high concentrations of NO cause DNA damage and mitochondrial dysfunction43, while low concentration promote angiogenesis and inhibit apoptosis, thus contributing to tumorigenesis44. Unlike the unstable gas molecule NO, NOSIP, a stable protein, is easier to study. NOSIP has been found to be widely expressed in a variety of tissues and cell types and has been associated with vertebrate eye and cranial development, nervous system development, and spinal cord injury21,22. As part of the U-box E3 ubiquitin ligase family, NOSIP contains a U-box domain that allows it to modify other proteins via ubiquitination45. For example, NOSIP can ubiquitinate the erythropoietin receptor EpoR, not for degradation, but to regulate its stability and thus control Epo-induced cell growth45. Additionally, NOSIP regulates the mono-ubiquitination of protein phosphatase 2 A (PP2A), and the absence of NOSIP increases the enzymatic activity of PP2A22. Whether NOSIP, as a ubiquitin ligase, regulates protein degradation of substrate molecules remain unclear. Although there are no direct reports showing NOSIP to tumor development, eNOS-NO signaling has been reported to be associated with various tumors46,47. We therefore hypothesized that, as a regulator of eNOS-NO, NOSIP might also play a role in tumor progression. Our experimental data confirmed that NOSIP is highly expressed in ccRCC cells. Overexpressed NOSIP inhibited NO production, while lower concentrations of NO promoted tumor development. As a regulator of NO synthesis and release, NOSIP may indirectly affect apoptosis by modulating NO levels. When NOSIP is inhibited and NO production increases, anti-apoptotic capacity weakens, and apoptosis increases. Conversely, when NOSIP is overexpressed and NO synthesis decreases, anti-apoptotic capacity is enhanced, and apoptosis is reduced. This confirms that NOSIP acts as a cancer-promoting factor in renal clear cell carcinoma. It is plausible that NOSIP promotes ccRCC progression through dual mechanisms: on one hand, by inhibiting NO production via its canonical interaction with eNOS, thereby reducing NO-mediated growth suppression; on the other hand, by inducing ubiquitination and degradation of SPTAN1 as an E3 ubiquitin ligase. The relative contribution of each pathway remains to be elucidated in future studies. Furthermore, we found that NOSIP overexpression reduced SPTAN1 protein levels, while NOSIP knockdown increased SPTAN1 levels. However, qRT-PCR results showed that NOSIP overexpression or knockdown did not affect the SPTAN1 mRNA level. This result suggests that NOSIP may ubiquitinate SPTAN1, leading to its degradation by the 26 S proteasome.

SPTAN1 encodes the non-erythrocytic alpha subunit of spectrin, a filamentous cytoskeletal protein family that acts as a scaffold to stabilize the plasma membrane and organelles48. SPTAN1 is widely expressed in human tissues such as the brain and testis, and mutations or aberrant expression of SPTAN1 have been associated with pediatric epileptic encephalopathy, spastic paraplegia, and other disorders49–51. In addition to its structural role, SPTAN1 is involved in cellular functions such as DNA repair, cell adhesion, growth, and cell cycle regulation34,35. Studies have shown that SPTAN1 plays different roles in various cancers. For example, Ackermann et al. found that SPTAN1 expression was reduced in MLH1-deficient colorectal cancer tissues and that low SPTAN1expression correlated with tumor TNM stage and metastasis52,53. In vitro knockdown of SPTAN1 reduced colorectal cancer cell viability, migration, and cell-cell contacts52,53. Furthermore, SPTAN1-ABL1 and SPTAN1-CSF3R fusion genes were found in acute T-lymphoblastic leukemia and atypical chronic myeloid leukemia, respectively54,55. Zhang R et al. found that downregulation of SPTAN1 by miR-128-3p in lung cancer cells resulted in cell cycle arrest, genomic instability, and restriction of DNA repair, enhancing sensitivity to the chemotherapeutic drug mitomycin56. In ovarian epithelial cancer, SPTAN1 is considered an indicator of resistance to anticancer drugs, with positive SPTAN1 expression predicting poorer survival57. The role and molecular mechanism of SPTAN1 in tumorigenesis remain underexplored. In this study, SPTAN1 knockdown in renal cancer cells promoted proliferation, migration, and invasion while inhibiting apoptosis. Additionally, molecular biology techniques confirmed that SPTAN1 is a ubiquitination substrate of NOSIP, suggesting that SPTAN1 may act as a tumor-suppressor protein in renal cancer.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the present study demonstrated that SPTAN1 inhibits cell viability, induces apoptosis, and suppresses cell motility in vitro, as well as inhibits xenograft tumor growth in vivo. Importantly, SPTAN1 partially mitigated the NOSIP-mediated promotion of ccRCC cell growth, migration, and invasion, suggesting that NOSIP exerts its tumor-promoting function by degrading SPTAN1 in ccRCC cells. Taken together, our study elucidates a novel mechanism by which NOSIP drives ccRCC progression by promoting the ubiquitination and disruption of SPTAN1. Targeting the NOSIP/SPTAN1 axis may represent a potential strategy for the treatment of ccRCC.
