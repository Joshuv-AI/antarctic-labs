Construction and phenotypic analysis of a low molecular weight tyrosine phosphatase mutant in Streptococcus suis serotype 2
Authors: Qianqian Wu, Yanxia Sun, Jiawen Ping, Bingbing Lu, Dekun Liu, Li Zhang, Yutao Wang, Hua Ni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStreptococcus suis (S. suis) is a major zoonotic pathogen that causes severe infections in both pigs and humans. Low-molecular-weight protein tyrosine phosphatases (LMWPTPs) are a conserved family of enzymes involved in capsule synthesis and the pathogenicity of various Gram-positive pathogens, but their functional role in S. suis 2 remains unclear.ResultsWe constructed a homologous Lmwptp deletion mutant (ΔLmwptp) in S. suis 2. This mutant exhibited moderate growth retardation during the logarithmic growth phase; transmission electron microscopy revealed a reduction in capsule thickness, and its capsular polysaccharide content was reduced by approximately 12% (p < 0.05). However, no significant differences were detected in colony morphology, adhesion to Hep-2 cells, anti-phagocytic activity, or virulence in a mouse infection model. In vitro dephosphorylation assays indicated that LMWPTP cannot directly dephosphorylate the bacterial tyrosine kinase (BY-kinase) Cps2BctC.ConclusionsAlthough LMWPTP possesses tyrosine phosphatase activity and partially affects capsule production, it is not essential for the pathogenicity of S. suis 2. These findings highlight the functional diversity of LMWPTP homologs across different Streptococcus species and underscore the need for caution when extrapolating mechanisms among related pathogens.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12917-026-05632-3.

Introduction

Streptococcus suis (S. suis) is a major zoonotic pathogen that causes severe systemic infections in both swine and humans, resulting in significant economic losses to the pig industry and posing a threat to public health [1, 2]. The symptoms of human infections with high mortality rates include meningitis, septicemia, and streptococcal toxic shock-like syndrome (STSLS), which are associated primarily with occupational exposure [3]. Notably, two large outbreaks in China (1998 and 2005) highlighted its epidemic potential [4]. S. suis is divided into 29 serotypes, among which serotype 2 is the most virulent and prevalent in both human and porcine infections [5]. More than 100 virulence-related factors have been found to be associated with the virulence of S. suis, including muramidase-releasing protein (MRP), suilysin (SLY), extracellular protein factor (EPF), fibrinogen-binding protein (FBPS), glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH), glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), and capsular polysaccharides (CPS) [6–9]. However, the virulence of S. suis is highly variable, and the presence of one or more of these factors does not necessarily define an S. suis isolate as a virulent strain, leading to the complex pathogenic mechanisms of the S. suis population [10]. Thus, elucidating the pathogenic mechanism of S. suis infection is crucial for identifying potential molecular targets to design effective vaccines.

Tyrosine phosphorylation is a widespread regulatory mechanism in prokaryotes and functionally linked to regulatory roles in nutrient sensing, protein localization, stress responses, transcriptional regulation, and virulence [11]. Low-molecular-weight protein tyrosine phosphatases (LMWPTPs), typically ~ 18 kDa in size and possessing a conserved CX₅R catalytic motif, constitute the major class of bacterial protein tyrosine phosphatases [12]. In S. pyogenes, SP-PTP acts as a global virulence regulator, influencing the expression of approximately 50% of genes through direct or indirect mechanisms [13]. In S. pneumoniae D39, the LMWPTP encoded by the spd1837 gene, which is located independently of the cps operon, does not participate in capsular polysaccharide synthesis; instead, it may play a role in central carbon metabolism and stress response [14]. Similarly, in Listeria monocytogenes, the LMWPTP LptpA2 partially contributes to wall teichoic acid glycosylation, as its complementation restores N-acetylglucosamine modification and phage adsorption in the PTPs-null mutant [15].

Despite these advances, the functional significance of LMWPTPs in Streptococcus species has not yet been fully established. The S. suis serotype 2 genome encodes LMWPTP (SSU05_1691), which shares 68% amino acid identity with Spd1837 from S. pneumoniae and 72% identity with SP-PTP from S. pyogenes (data not shown). Our preliminary studies confirmed that the recombinant LMWPTP possesses tyrosine phosphatase activity, with a specific activity of 2.1 nmol⋅min−1⋅μg−1, and its biochemical properties were similar to those of its direct homolog [16]. However, several features suggest potential functional divergence: (i) there are significant differences in the structure of the cps locus between S. suis and S. pneumoniae, Lmwptp is located outside the cps operon (Fig. S2); (ii) S. suis 2 also contains additional phosphatases, including the tyrosine phosphatase Cps2D [17] and the serine/threonine phosphatase Stp1 [18] thereby potentially providing functional redundancy. These genomic and tissue-specific differences raise a critical question: does LMWPTP play an equivalent, unique, or dispensable role in the virulence of S. suis?

Here, we generated a homozygous Lmwptp knockout mutant to test the hypothesis that Lmwptp regulates capsule production and virulence in S. suis 2. We further investigated whether LMWPTP regulates the bacterial tyrosine kinase (BY-kinase) Cps2C through direct dephosphorylation, similar to the mechanisms described in S. pneumoniae and Escherichia coli (E. coli) [19, 20]. Our results indicate that although LMWPTP exhibits enzymatic activity, it is dispensable for the virulence of S. suis 2, highlighting the functional diversity within this LMWPTPs family.

Methods

Bioinformatics analysis

The amino acid sequence of S. suis 2 LMWPTP (SSU05_1691) was obtained from the NCBI database, and homologous sequences were obtained through BLASTp alignment. Multi-sequence alignment was completed using the MUSCLE tool (default parameter) in MEGA software. The comparison results were exported in FASTA format and submitted to the ESPript3.2 online server (https://espript.ibcp.fr) for visualization. The secondary structure information is extracted based on the PDB structure file (PDB ID: pdb_00005got) that refers to the SS-PTP protein of S. pyogenes. The figure was marked with α helix and β fold. Completely conserved residues were highlighted on a red background, and similar residues were displayed in red font. Phylogenetic analysis was conducted using MEGA with the maximum likelihood method and 1000 bootstrap replicates. PyMOL (v2.5) software was used to visualize and analyze the 3D model.

Construction of the Lmwptp mutant strain

To construct the Lmwptp knockout mutant, the upstream and downstream homologous arms of the SSU05_1691 gene were amplified from the genomic DNA of 05ZYH33 with the primers L1/L2 and R1/R2, respectively. The PCR products were digested with the appropriate restriction enzymes and ligated into the pUC-Spcr vector to yield the knockout plasmid pUC-LSR. The plasmid was then electroporated into competent 05ZYH33 cells (Fig. 2A). Transformants were selected on THB agar plates containing spectinomycin (100 μg/mL) and incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. The deletion was confirmed by DNA sequencing and verified by PCR and reverse transcription-PCR (RT-PCR) in candidate mutants. RNA extraction and RT‒PCR

To confirm the deletion, we performed RT-PCR on the mutant as described previously [22]. Briefly, RNA was extracted via RNAiso Plus (TaKaRa, Japan) according to the manufacturer's instructions. cDNA was then synthesized via PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (TaKaRa, Japan) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The deletion of the Lmwptp gene was confirmed by RT-PCR with the primers Check1/Check2 and cDNA as the templates.

Growth curve analysis

05ZYH33 and the mutant ΔLmwptp were inoculated into THB media and cultured overnight at 37 °C with agitation at 180 rpm. After 16 h, the cultures were adjusted to a dilution to an OD₆₀₀ of 0.3. The OD₆₀₀ values were measured every hour for 12 h via a Bio-Rad spectrophotometer (USA).

Observation of colony morphology and hemolysis

05ZYH33 and mutant ΔLmwptp strains were grown in THB media with agitation at 180 rpm for 16 h. The cultures were streaked onto Columbia sheep-blood agar plates, incubated at 37 °C for 48 h, and then observed for colony morphology and hemolysis differences.

Gram staining

Bacterial smears were prepared on glass slides and heat fixed. The slides were stained with crystal violet for 1 min, rinsed gently with water, treated with iodine solution for 1 min and rinsed again. After decolorization with 95% ethanol for 30 s, the smears were counterstained with 0.5% safranin for 20 s, rinsed and blotted dry. The cells were examined under oil immersion. The average length of the bacterial chains was determined from 100 randomly selected cells via ImageJ (v1.8.0).

Scanning electron microscopy and transmission electron microscopy observations

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM).

were performed as previously described [23]. Briefly, exponential-phase cells were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde, washed with PBS, and postfixed with 1% osmium tetroxide for 2 h. After dehydration with an ethanol gradient, the sections were embedded in epoxy resin, stained with uranyl acetate/lead citrate (Reynolds lead citrate), and finally examined with a JEM-1010 transmission electron microscope (JEOL, Japan) at an operating voltage of 100 kV. For SEM, the fixed samples were sputter-coated with gold and examined with a Quanta 200 environmental scanning electron microscope (FEI, USA). Bacterial cell surface roughness and capsule thickness were determined from 20 randomly selected cells via ImageJ (v1.8.0).

Extraction and determination of CPS

Bacteria were centrifuged at 8,000 rpm at 4 °C for 10 min, washed once with sterile saline, and then suspended in 100 mL of glycine buffer (0.1 M, pH 9.2) containing crystalline unsalted ovalbumin lysozyme. Sodium azide was added at a final concentration of 0.02% (w/v). The suspension was incubated at 37 °C with continuous agitation for 6–8 h. The suspension was then centrifuged at 8,000 rpm, 4 °C for 15 min to remove the pellet. A protease was added to the lysate supernatant at a final concentration of 100 μg/mL and incubated at 55 °C for 2 h. Subsequently, 0.1 M CaCl₂ was added and the mixture was stirred for 1 h. Subsequently, 25% (v/v) absolute ethanol was added, and the mixture was allowed to stand at 4 °C for 2 h. The mixture was then centrifuged at 8,000 rpm at 4 °C for 15 min to remove pellets. Finally, anhydrous ethanol was added to a concentration of 80% (v/v), and the mixture was placed overnight at 4 °C to obtain crude CPS. The extracted total carbohydrate content of CPS was determined using the phenol sulfate method described by Dubois et al. [24]. Galactose (dried to constant weight) was precisely weighed and dissolved in deionized water to prepare a liquid solution of 0.1 mg/mL. Aliquots of 0, 50, 100, 150, 200, 250, and 300 μL of the standard solution were transferred to different tubes and adjusted to 500 μL with deionized water. Subsequently, 200 μL of 6% phenol solution and 1.0 ml of concentrated sulfuric acid were added in turn. The mixture was immediately vortexed and heated in a boiling water bath for 10 min. After cooling to room temperature, absorbance at 490 nm was measured using a spectrophotometer (Bio-Rad, USA). A standard curve of absorbance (y-axis) versus galactose concentration (x-axis) was plotted, and a linear regression equation was calculated. Crude CPS samples were precisely weighed and dissolved in deionized water to create a stock solution of 5 mg/mL. An appropriate volume of sample solution was processed in parallel with the standard curve, and an absorbance of 490 nm was recorded. The content of crude polysaccharides in capsule was obtained by using the standard curve equation.

Adhesion and antiphagocytosis assays

The method proposed by Hytönen et al. was used with minor modifications [25]. Carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE)-labeled bacteria were mixed with Hep-2 at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 100:1 and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h in the dark. After three washes with PBS to remove unattached bacteria, the cells were fixed with 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde and kept on ice. Adherent bacteria were quantified via flow cytometry (FACSCalibur, BD, USA). For the phagocytosis assays, the bacteria were incubated with RAW264.7 macrophages (MOI 100:1) at 37 °C for 2 h. To kill the extracellular bacteria, penicillin and streptomycin were added for 1 h. The samples were kept on ice and analyzed via flow cytometry as described above.

Experimental infection of mice

Thirty 4-week-old specific-pathogen-free (SPF) BALB/c mice were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. and randomly assigned to three groups (n = 10 per group): (1) the PBS control group, which was intraperitoneally injected with 1 mL of sterile PBS; (2) the 05ZYH33 (wild-type) strain infection group; and (3) the knockout (ΔLmwptp) strain infection group. The mice in the infection groups were injected intraperitoneally with 1 mL of bacterial suspension (1 × 10⁸ CFU). All animal experiments were approved by the Experimental Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Kashi University (Ethics Committee Approval Number: KSU-AEC-B-2025–017).

Protein expression and purification

The Cps2BctC coding region was amplified from the 05ZYH33 genomic DNA with the primers Cps2Bct-F/Cps2Bct-R (Table 2). The PCR product was digested with BamHI/XhoI and inserted into similarly digested pET-32a to generate the recombinant plasmid pET-32a-Cps2BctC, which was subsequently transformed into BL21 (DE3) cells. When the culture reached an OD600 of approximately 0.8, expression was induced with 1 mM isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) (Sigma, USA), and the cultures were incubated for 12 h at 16 °C. His-tagged Cps2BctC recombinant protein was purified via a Ni2+–NTA column (GE Healthcare, Sweden) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Purified proteins were detected by Western blot analysis using an HRP-conjugated anti-His antibody (Sigma, USA).

Preparation of bacterial extracellular proteins and total cell lysates

Extracellular proteins (ECP) and total cell lysates (TCL) were prepared from 05ZYH33 and the mutant ΔLmwptp. Bacteria were grown to mid-log phase, harvested by centrifugation at 6,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and separated into supernatants and cell pellets. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm filter, and the filtrate was precipitated via the TCA‒DOC method [26, 27], followed by resuspension in 20 mM Tris‒HCl buffer (pH 7.5) to obtain ECP. The cell pellet was washed twice with cold PBS, resuspended in lysis buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 200 µg/mL RNase A, 100 µg/mL DNase I, 1 × protease inhibitor), and disrupted by sonication (3 × 30 s, 30 s on/30 s off, on ice). Undisrupted cells were removed by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected as TCL for Western blot analysis.

Cps2BctC dephosphorylation in vitro

Recombinant Cps2BctC was constructed as a chimeric protein consisting of the C-terminal 30 amino acids of Cps2B (residues 200-229) fused to the cytoplasmic catalytic domain (residues 1–275) of Cps2C, based on the established paradigm that BY-kinase activity requires membrane localization mediated by the C-terminal domain of the regulatory subunit [28]. This construct was expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3) with an N-terminal His₆ tag and purified by Ni2+–NTA affinity chromatography (GenScript, Nanjing, China). Cps2BctC exhibited basal tyrosine phosphorylation after purification, as detected by anti-phosphotyrosine Western blotting (see Fig. S3D). Prior to substrate testing, the activity of LMWPTP was validated using p-nitrophenyl phosphate (pNPP) as a universal substrate, confirming a specific activity of 2.1 ± 0.3 nmol⋅min−1⋅μg−1 under the same buffer conditions [16]. Recombinant Cps2BctC (0.34 μg) was incubated with LMWPTP (1.0–4.0 μg) in 100 mM citrate buffer (pH 6.5, optimal for LMWPTP) at 37 °C for 30 min [28, 29]. The reactions were stopped by adding 6 × SDS‒PAGE loading buffer (Tiangeng, China) and boiling for 5 min. Proteins were separated by 10% SDS‒PAGE, transferred to a PVDF membrane, and probed with an anti-phosphotyrosine antibody (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, USA) followed by an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody. The signals were visualized with enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted using at least three independent biological replicates, unless otherwise specified. For the quantification of CPS, three biological replicates (separate bacterial cultures) were analyzed, with each sample measured three times in technical replicates; the mean of the technical replicates was used for statistical comparisons between strains. The data were analyzed via GraphPad Prism v9.0 (San Diego, CA, USA). Statistical analysis was performed via two-tailed Student's t-tests. For in vivo virulence experiments, survival rates were analyzed via the log rank test. p < 0.05 was considered significant and is indicated by *; p < 0.01, by **.

Results

Bioinformatic characterization of LMWPTP in S. suis 2

Through multiple sequence alignment analysis, LMWPTP showed typical PTP family structural features with SS-PTP and other homologous proteins. The LMWPTP sequence contains a conserved CX5R catalytic motif in the P loop region, and a distant aspartic acid residue in the D loop region, which participates in the catalytic process as a generalized acid (Fig. 1A). The phylogenetic tree based on the neighbor-joining method showed that LMWPTP and SS-PTP were clustered in the same evolutionary clade, which contained a variety of LMWPTPs derived from Gram-positive bacteria, including S. pneumoniae, Bacillus subtilis, Staphylococcus aureus, and S. pyogenes. The bootstrap support rate of this cluster node was 84%, indicating that LMWPTP had a close evolutionary relationship with these Gram-positive PTPs (Fig. 1B). Molecular surface potential analysis showed that LMWPTP as a whole exhibited a typical α/β folding structure, which is highly similar to the structure topology of SS-PTP. The mutants were verified by PCR and sequence analysis, confirming the replacement of Lmwptp with the spectinomycin resistance cassette (Fig. 2B). No Lmwptp band was detected in the mutant via anti-Lmwptp polyclonal antibodies, confirming the absence of Lmwptp expression. Deletion of Lmwptp results in subtle ultrastructural changes without altering macroscopic morphology or functional capsule properties

Gram staining of 05ZYH33 and the mutant ΔLmwptp revealed no significant differences in basic bacterial morphology, such as cell shape and chain arrangement (mean chain length: 5.03 ± 4.2 µm vs 4.05 ± 3.24 µm; p = 0.09). Both strains exhibited typical gram-positive characteristics (Fig. 5A, B). Consistent with these findings, SEM confirmed that, compared with 05ZYH33, the mutant ΔLmwptp maintained normal chain formation and cell morphology (surface roughness: 28.43 ± 4.9 vs 27.87 ± 9.0; p = 0.8) (Fig. 5C, D). TEM analysis showed an observable alteration in capsule thickness in the ΔLmwptp mutant. Quantitative CPS measurement further demonstrated that the capsule content of the ΔLmwptp mutant was lower than that of 05ZYH33 (n = 3 biological replicates; 51.85 ± 2.02 vs. 59.04 ± 3.55 μg/mL; p < 0.05) (Fig. 5E, F). LMWPTP did not dephosphorylate Cps2BctC in vitro

In Firmicutes, the genes encoding LMWPTP type phosphatases are found elsewhere in the genome, rather than adjacent to the polysaccharide synthesis gene cluster [31]. S. suis also exhibits this distribution characteristic (Fig. S2). Tyrosine kinase activity in Gram-positive bacteria depends on the physical binding of the extracellular domain of the C-terminal junction protein to the catalytic domain [28]. Transmembrane analysis of S. suis 2 Cps2B predicted that the cytoplasmic tail is required for Cps2C activation (data not shown). To test whether LMWPTP dephosphorylates the Cps2C kinase complex, we co-expressed the C-terminal region of Cps2B with Cps2C to yield the soluble chimeric substrate Cps2BctC. Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that although LMWPTP possesses intrinsic tyrosine phosphatase activity and is involved in the production of CPS, it is not essential for the virulence of S. suis 2. The ΔLmwptp mutant exhibited a moderate 12% reduction in capsular polysaccharide content and a slight ultrastructural thinning of the capsule, but retained wild-type colony morphology, growth characteristics, adhesion to Hep-2 cells, anti-phagocytosis against RAW264.7 macrophages, and pathogenicity in a mouse infection model. This dissociation between enzyme activity and virulence outcomes distinguishes Lmwptp from its homologs in other Gram-positive pathogens.

This finding that LMWPTP is not essential for virulence contrasts with the established role of the LMWPTP family in other streptococci. In S. pyogenes, SP-PTP acts as a global virulence regulator influencing the expression of approximately 50% of genes; its deletion severely impairs virulence in a mouse sepsis model [13] In contrast, in S. pneumoniae, although the spd1837 gene encoding LMWPTP is located within the virulence-associated OM001 operon, inactivation of this gene alone does not affect bacterial virulence; the virulence phenotype of this operon is entirely attributable to the funct

ion of another gene, spd1836 [32]. The absence of an independent virulence defect caused by the deletion of Lmwptp in S. suis 2 suggests that the function of LMWPTP has diverged within the genus Streptococcus. Factors contributing to this differentiation may include: First, S. suis 2 encodes multiple functionally overlapping phosphatases, including the operon-encoded Cps2D [17] and threonine phosphatase Stp1 [18], which may compensate for the absence of Lmwptp through functional redundancy. Second, Lmwptp is located outside the capsular polysaccharide synthesis gene cluster, whereas the organization of LMWPTPs in S. pneumoniae and E. coli in association with the BY-kinase operon suggests a different pattern of regulatory integration [19, 20]. Together, these data indicate that the virulence relevance of LMWPTPs exhibits marked species specificity and that their biological functions cannot be simply inferred from sequence homology.

In vitro dephosphorylation assays failed to detect Cps2BctC as a direct substrate for Lmwptp, even under optimal conditions with a 12:1 molar ratio of phosphatase to substrate (pH 6.5, 37 °C). This result does not rule out the possibility that Cps2BctC is dephosphorylated by LMWPTP in vivo, where additional factors such as membrane localization, oligomerization state, or chaperone proteins may facilitate substrate recognition. In E. coli, effective dephosphorylation of Wzc by Wzb requires Wzc to assemble into a hexamer and localize to the membrane interface [20]. Similarly, in Staphylococcus aureus, the BY kinase CapB requires its corresponding transmembrane regulator CapA for proper localization and function, and the kinase domain alone cannot reproduce the regulatory interactions observed in membrane-associated complexes [30, 33]. In S. pneumoniae, CpsC acts as a key adapter that anchors CpsD to the membrane and promotes its trans-autophosphorylation, highlighting the importance of the membrane environment in the regulation of tyrosine kinases and phosphatases [28]. Our soluble Cps2BctC construct lacks a complete transmembrane domain and the associated adapter proteins, which may preclude physiologically relevant interactions. Other possible explanations include LMWPTP targeting unidentified substrates, or capsular regulation occurring via indirect mechanisms.

This study has several limitations. First, the lack of a complementation strain prevents definitive causal attribution, despite genetic stability testing. Second, recombinant LMWPTP was purified from E. coli inclusion bodies; the native protein in S. suis may exhibit different substrate specificity or require host-derived cofactors. Third, phenotypic assessment was conducted under standard laboratory conditions; LMWPTP function may become apparent under host-mimicking stresses (oxidative, nutrient-limiting, or acidic). Future research will integrate: (i) chromosomal single-copy complementation to circumvent the gene dosage effect; (ii) phosphoproteomic analysis under physiologically relevant conditions; (iii) transcriptomic and metabolomic profiling to identify pathway-level impacts of LMWPTP deletion; and (iv) assessment of biofilm formation, membrane permeability, and stress tolerance.

Conclusion

This study confirmed that although LMWPTP was active as a tyrosine phosphatase, it only slightly affected the capsule thickness of S. suis 2 and did not alter the virulence phenotype in a mouse infection model. Moreover, LMWPTP did not directly dephosphorylate Cps2BctC. This result highlights the functional diversity of LMWPTPs within the genus Streptococcus, suggesting that the mechanisms of homologous proteins cannot be simply extrapolated across species. Future research should integrate host microenvironment stress, interactome proteomics, and metabolomics approaches to systematically identify the physiological substrates and activation conditions of LMWPTP, thereby fully elucidating its true role in the tyrosine phosphorylation network of Streptococcus.
