Developing new energy intake reference values for the Saudi population
Authors: Omar Alhumaidan, Ali Almajwal, Mohammed Alsaif
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study updates the average requirement for energy intake (AR-EI) for the Saudi population, thereby addressing outdated references. Previous estimates relied on generalized equations and non-specific data, which often led to overestimations. This study aimed to establish new AR-EI values for Saudi population.MethodsThe study methodology combined two complementary approaches to estimate energy requirements. For adults, a factorial approach was used, incorporating physical activity levels observed in the Saudi population along with reference values for body weight and height, applied in published Saudi predictive equations for Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR). For infants, children, and adolescents (0–17 years), the study used Total Energy Expenditure (TEE) predictive equations derived from multi-country doubly labeled water (DLW) studies.ResultsAfter calculating the requirements across different age groups and sexes, the value representing the average that meets the needs of 50% of the population was identified and adopted as the AR-EI. The AR-EI values ranged from 393 kcal/day during the first six months to 778 kcal/day by 12 months for male infants, while female infants required 364–748 kcal/day. Among children and adolescents, AR-EI values ranged from 912 to 3147 kcal/day. For adults, the AR-EI was estimated at 2207 kcal/day for men and 1708 kcal/day for women under current activity levels. With increased physical activity, these requirements rose to 3153 kcal/day for men and 2440 kcal/day for women.ConclusionsThis study identified significant overestimations in previous references owing to the reliance on generalized equations and outdated data. By incorporating Saudi-specific data, these findings provide accurate and practical benchmarks for energy intake. These results serve as a foundation for evidence-based nutritional guidelines, promoting public health and reducing the incidence of diet-related diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40795-026-01392-3.

Background

Dietary guidelines and recommendations constitute some of the most significant tools in nutritional science for improving the nutritional status of populations [1, 2]. These guidelines provide concise, evidence-based messages regarding healthy eating habits and lifestyles, with the objective of preventing various forms of malnutrition and maintaining optimal health and nutritional status among individuals [3].

The development of such dietary guidelines contributes to the determination of an optimal dietary pattern that considers the health, nutritional, and cultural aspects of society [4, 5]. This enables policymakers in relevant institutions to promote public health through the formulation of policies and implementation of awareness and nutritional programs that facilitate adherence to this optimal dietary pattern [6–8].

The determination of the optimal dietary pattern is largely contingent on establishing daily nutrient requirements or reference values [9, 10]. Reference values are used to determine the quantities and types of food required to meet nutritional requirements. Linear programming and nutritional pattern modeling are techniques that account for multiple factors such as food costs, availability, environmental impacts, and cultural preferences [11–14].

The reference value for energy intake, known as the Average Requirement for Energy Intake (AR-EI), is a fundamental determinant in calculating the required amounts of macronutrients including carbohydrates, proteins, and fats. Since these macronutrients serve primarily as sources of energy, their recommended intake levels are directly derived from the AR-EI. This value is referred to as the Estimated Energy Requirement (EER) in the United States, while in Europe, it is commonly termed the Average Requirement (AR) [10, 15]. As such, energy intake plays a central role in shaping dietary guidelines and ensuring nutritional adequacy across populations [15, 16].

The AR-EI may vary across countries based on the reference ranges for the weight and height of each population [17, 18]. This reference range typically encompasses the 2.5th to 97.5th percentiles of weight and height within a given population [19].

Two primary methodologies are commonly used to determine the AR-EI [20, 21]. The first, doubly labeled water (DLW), measures the Total Energy Expenditure (TEE) by tracking the elimination of isotopes during daily activities [22]. The DLW method utilizes isotopes such as deuterium (²H) and oxygen-18 (¹⁸O). While deuterium exits the body solely as water, oxygen-18 is eliminated through both H₂O and CO₂. The production of CO₂ can be determined by comparing the elimination rates of these isotopes [22]. This technique is particularly valuable for nutritional research, because it provides accurate measurements of energy expenditure in real-world settings. It requires minimal participant involvement, primarily urine or saliva samples, and eliminates the need for activity logs or concerns regarding equipment failures. Subsequently, the collected data are modeled using regression techniques to estimate the TEE of the community based on the reference height and weight statistics within the population [20, 21]. These equations also account for sex differences, either by including sex as a variable alongside height and weight, each associated with its own regression coefficient. Alternatively, separate sex-specific equations may be developed, rather than modeling sex as a covariate within a unified regression structure.

The factorial approach is the second method, which focuses on calculating the TEE by independently determining its components [15]. This involves estimating energy requirements based on the physical activity level (PAL) and combining them with results from country-specific regression equations used to calculate the basal metabolic rate (BMR) based on the reference values of the population for gender, height and weight [20, 21]. For groups with specific physiological needs, such as growing children, pregnant women, or lactating mothers, their requirements are calculated separately as part of the TEE and integrated with other components such as PAL and BMR [23]. Similar to the DLW method, the TEE was utilized as an AR-EI. Although the DLW method is widely recognized as the gold standard for measuring TEE at the individual level and modeling it at the population level [22–25], the factorial approach offers a practical alternative. However, its accuracy depends heavily on the precision of input parameters, especially physical activity levels and BMR estimates. Both methods are acceptable, but their use depends on available data and the characteristics of the target population.

In 1989, Khan, M. A., and Al Kanhal, M. A. conducted a study to establish Saudi-specific reference values using both factorial approaches with TEE and BMR equations that was based on DLW from multiple Nation [26]. This study determined AR-EI values for the Saudi population, with estimates ranging from 650 to 880 kcal/day during infancy, 2100–2700 kcal/day for males and 1900–2200 kcal/day for females aged 1–17 years, and 2800–2100 kcal/day for adult males and 2100–1800 kcal/day for adult females [26]. However, two key methodological limitations should be noted. First, instead of estimating AR-EI to meet the needs of 50% of the population, as is the standard approach in defining AR or EER, this study calculated values intended to cover 97% of the population by applying the Population Recommended Intake (PRI), also referred to as the Recommended Daily Allowance (RDA) [10, 15]. This was done by adding two standard deviations to the value that covers 50% of the population. Such an approach may lead to overestimating energy requirements and is not recommended when setting reference value for energy [15, 20]. Second, no Saudi-specific growth charts or regression equations were available for calculating with BMT and TEE equations.

The AR-EI has not been updated since 1989 despite new development such as the Almajwal–Abulmeaty BMR predictive equations, the Saudi growth chart, ideal weight and height standards [27, 28]. Updating the AR-EI using these sources can foster broader research opportunities, leading to evidence-based nutritional guidelines tailored to the Saudi population. This can improve health outcomes and reduce the incidence of nutrition-related diseases.

In this study, AR-EI values for the Saudi population will be determined by utilizing community-specific data on physical activity levels and anthropometric reference values, along with estimation results from predictive equations derived from DLW studies and from the factorial approach, which relies on BMR-based equations. An analytical framework was developed to ensure alignment with available data in the Saudi context.

Methods

Study design overview

To determine the AR-EI value, the estimated TEE for each age group was modeled using a normal (Gaussian) distribution [23, 29]. This distribution, referred to as the reference range, represents the energy requirements between the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The AR-EI was defined as the 50th percentile of this distribution, corresponding to the energy intake that meets the needs of 50% of the population [23, 29, 30]. This was done using equations, and data sources which were categorized according to age groups, including infants, children, adolescents, adults, and pregnant and lactating women, as summarized in Table 1.

The estimation process of TEE for infants and children was derived using equations informed by findings from DLW studies. While such studies have not been conducted within the Saudi population, internationally developed equations, primarily based on multinational data, were utilized as the best available alternative [33–36]. For adults, TEE was estimated using factorial approach, which incorporating physical activity levels observed in the Saudi population along with reference values for body weight and height, applied in published Saudi predictive equations for BMR.

Across all age groups, anthropometric measurements from Saudi population data were used as inputs in the predictive equations, ensuring alignment with local characteristics and reflecting a commitment to evidence-informed and context-specific assessments.

Results

Reference range for weight and height

The reference ranges for the weights and heights of healthy Saudi infants, children, adolescents, and adults, as presented in Table 2, were derived from national anthropometric standards established by Mohammad El-Mouzan et al. [28].

For healthy infants at birth, the weight is 3.3 kg for males (95% confidence interval (CI): 2.5, 4.1) and 3.1 kg for females (95% CI: 2.3, 3.9). This corresponds to a birth height of 51 cm for males (95% CI: 45.4, 56.6) and 50 cm for females (95% CI: 45.4, 54.6). At one year of age, the weight of the infant reaches 9.7 kg for males (95% CI: 7.3, 12.3), with a height of 75 cm for males (95% CI: 67–83). For females, the weight was 9.3 kg (95% CI: 7.1, 11.5), with a height of 74 cm (95% CI: 67, 80). From 2 to 18 years of age, the disparity in weight measurements between males and females increased.

Discussion

Updated AR-EI values compared with previous Saudi reference intake values

The estimated AR-EI for Saudi infants in this study ranged from 393 kcal/day for males and 364 kcal/day for females during the first six months, increasing to 778 kcal/day and 748 kcal/day, respectively, by 12 months of age. This corresponds to approximately 119–80 kcal/kg for males and 117–83 kcal/kg for females across the same period. Among children and adolescents (1–17 years), AR-EI values ranged from 912 to 3147 kcal/day (96–53 kcal/kg) for male and 843–2467 kcal/day (91 − 46 kcal/kg), reflecting the typical decline in per kilogram energy needs with age.

For adults, the AR-EI was estimated at 2207 kcal/day for men (36 kcal/kg) and 1708 kcal/day for women (32 kcal/kg), based on national physical activity data [32]. With increasing activity levels, AR-EI values rose to 3153 kcal/day (51 kcal/kg) for very active men and 2440 kcal/day (45 kcal/kg) for very active women.

These values differ significantly from those originally reported by Khan, M. A., & Al Kanhal, M. A [26]., who estimated AR-EI at 650 kcal/day for infants aged 0–6 months, increasing to 880 kcal/day by 12 months. For individuals aged 1–17 years, their recommended intake ranged from 2100 to 2700 kcal/day for males and 1900 to 2200 kcal/day for females. Adult requirements ranged from 2800 to 2100 kcal/day for men and 2100 to 1800 kcal/day for women.

When comparing the results of the present study with those of Khan, M. A., & Al Kanhal, M. A., their findings clearly tend to overestimate the energy requirements across all age groups. This discrepancy could be attributed to several factors. First, their analysis relied on the Schofield equation, which has been shown to overestimate the energy requirements of Saudi individuals compared with BMR measurements obtained through indirect calorimetry [27]. In contrast, we used the Almajwal–Abulmeaty equation, which is more accurate for the Saudi population [27]. Second, the anthropometric data they used were based on United Nations population estimates for weight and height owing to the lack of reliable growth charts at the time [39]. This reliance on generalized data contributed to overestimations, whereas the present study utilized Saudi Growth Charts, which offer a more precise, population-specific reference [28].

Finally, their methodology treated the reference value for energy using an approach typically applied to nutrients with RDAs or PRIs, by adding two standard deviations to the AR-EI to cover the needs of 97% of the population [30]. While this method is appropriate for most nutrients, energy is a notable exception. For energy, it is recommended to rely solely on the AR-EI (i.e., the median of the distribution) without adding two standard deviations, in order to avoid overestimations that could increase the risk of excessive energy intake and subsequent weight gain or overweight [23]. Thus, the majority of published reference values for energy, including those from the FAO [23, 29], the United States [21], the United Kingdom [40], the European Union [10], Germany [41], and Australia–New Zealand [42], have followed a similar approach to this study by calculating the AR-EI without adding 2 SD. These methodological differences are the primary reasons for the overestimation observed in their results compared with those of the present study.

Comparison of AR-EI and report energy intake in Saudi Arabia

Based on the literature review, to the best of our knowledge, no national nutrition survey has been conducted in Saudi Arabia in the last decade. However, a previous study estimated dietary consumption using a probabilistic model that utilized open data sources, including the Global Dietary Database (GDD), Euromonitor, and FAOSTAT, to estimate food consumption and dietary exposure in Saudi Arabia [43]. According to the modeling results for dietary patterns in Saudi Arabia, the daily energetic intake averaged approximately 3310 kcal/day for men and 3306 kcal/day for women among adults. These values are notably higher than the reference values for adults established in this study.

The difference between the AR-EI and reported intake levels represents a critical input for the development of optimal dietary patterns suitable for the Saudi population. This gap can be utilized in optimization approaches, such as linear programming, to define diets that are nutritionally adequate, culturally appropriate, and practically achievable [9–14]. Quantifying this difference enables the formulation of evidence-based dietary recommendations that reflect both current consumption trends and public health objectives. Furthermore, this information can support the development of nutrition education and behavior change programs aimed at progressively reducing the gap between actual intake and recommended energy levels.

For public health policy, the quantified gap between reported intake and the AR-EI established in this study provides a benchmark for assessing the potential impact of public health interventions, such as front-of-pack labeling and menu calorie labeling regulations.

Although this study does not directly model the effects of such policies, it generates essential reference values and intake distributions that can be integrated into future policy impact analyses using existing modeling frameworks in Saudi Arabia [44]. One relevant policy for such evaluation is the menu calorie labeling regulation implemented in Saudi Arabia in 2018, which aims to inform consumers about the energy content of meals consumed outside the home. While evidence from meta-analysis suggests that such interventions may lead to a reduction in caloric intake [45], local studies in Saudi Arabia have relied on self-reported data and have not provided analytical evaluations of their actual impact [46–49]. The data produced in this study can thus support future modeling efforts to quantify the effectiveness of such interventions in reducing excess energy intake and improving alignment with national dietary recommendations.

Disadvantages and advantages of the study approach

The key advantage of this study is the use of Saudi-specific weight and growth data, which enhanced estimation accuracy and provided reference ranges for energetic intake across age groups. It also calculated the AR-EI for each sex and age range, supporting its integration with other statistical models.

Main of the key limitations of this study lies in the use of TEE predictive equations for children and infants that were not developed based on population-specific data [23]. Although these equations were applied using Saudi-specific weight and height references, their underlying coefficients were originally derived from multi-national datasets [33–35]. As such, the suitability of these coefficients for the Saudi population remains uncertain. In contrast, for adults, a BMR equation specifically developed for the Saudi population was employed, which enhances contextual relevance and reduces potential bias [27]. This contrast underscores the need for future research to develop and validate population-specific predictive equations for children and infants in Saudi Arabia to improve the accuracy and relevance of AR-EI for this age groups.

This limitation may partially explain the noticeable discrepancy observed AR-EI estimates between 17-year-olds and the 18–24-year-old group. The transition from using TEE equations derived from multi-national data in children and adolescents to a population-specific BMR equation in adults could introduce methodological inconsistency at this age threshold, potentially affecting the continuity of AR-EI values across these age groups.

Future development

In addition to their significant role in this study, the calculated reference values have great potential for future applications in economic, health, and nutritional assessments. These values can also serve as a foundation for developing nutritional or food-based dietary guidelines using nutritional pattern modeling methodologies [13]. This approach incorporates AR-EI with consumption, nutrients, and food expenditure data to simulate optimal dietary patterns using optimization algorithms, such as linear programming, to create comprehensive dietary guides that encompass all these reference values [12–14].

Moreover, this study can be applied to identify high-risk families alongside the reference values of the cost-to-spending ratio to initiate a broader project aimed at calculating the cost of diets in Saudi Arabia. Such a project can simulate optimal scenarios, contributing significantly to food security analyses and offering valuable insights for decision making [46].

Conclusions

This study established updated AR-EI values for the Saudi population, addressing the gaps in previous references by utilizing Saudi-specific growth charts and predictive equations. The findings provide accurate energy requirements for different age groups, sexes, and activity levels, thereby supporting growth, weight maintenance, and health across the lifespan. These values form the foundation for evidence-based nutritional guidelines, enabling the development of effective policies and programs to improve public health and reduce nutrition-related diseases in Saudi Arabia.
