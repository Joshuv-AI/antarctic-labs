Hypertension Control Rates Before and After Implementation of a Multicomponent Quality Improvement Program in Medicare Beneficiaries Primarily of Hispanic Origin in a Large Health System in South Florida, 2011–2018
Authors: Paulo H M Chaves, Maureen DeSoria, Leslye Roque, Rafael J Mas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHealth care systems have a key role in improving hypertension control in large populations, a global challenge, through the implementation of modern multicomponent programs. The impact of such programs in at‐risk minority populations, nonetheless, remains to be better characterized. Additionally, the extent to which hypertension control based on a single time‐point blood pressure (BP) measurement (LAST‐BP), the approach typically used in health system performance assessment, agrees with time‐averaged BP (AVG‐BP) has not been well studied. Leon Medical Centers (LMC), a major integrated health care system serving Medicare beneficiaries predominantly of Hispanic origin in Miami‐Dade County, Florida, implemented a hypertension control program in 2011. We conducted longitudinal data analysis evaluating hypertension control before and after program implementation, and compared annual rates derived from AVG‐BP versus LAST‐BP. During 2008–2018, the analytic sample increased from 4,710 to 21,540, mean age rose from 73.4 to 77.1 years, and the proportion of Hispanics remained stable (>98%). Adjusted odds of hypertension control were 5.7 (95% CI: 5.4–6.0) times higher after full program implementation. Improvements were comparable in women and men. Trends based on LAST‐BP paralleled those of AVG‐BP, although rates were consistently lower. Kappa agreement between AVG‐BP and LAST‐BP metrics was only fair. The LMC program was associated with a meaningful, sustained increase in hypertension control to high levels (crude rates based on AVG‐BP ∼ 90%). LAST‐BP‐based control rates underestimated those derived from AVG‐BP. Research is warranted to determine how longitudinal BP metrics can be optimally incorporated into patient care and health system performance assessment. Practical Applications: This study has two practical implications. First, it shows that a coordinated, multicomponent hypertension control program with modern core elements, including team‐based care, standardized blood pressure measurement, and promotion of guideline‐based management supported by a robust EHR system and enhanced pharmacy and transportation services, and culturally tailored patient engagement, can achieve and sustain high levels of hypertension control in the understudied population of Hispanic Medicare beneficiaries in South Florida. These findings provide a strong foundation for real‐world primary care settings, while highlighting the need for continued program refinement as the science of blood pressure (BP) control in large populations evolves.Second, the study shows that a single annual “snapshot” BP value underestimates true hypertension control compared with the average of readings obtained throughout the year. This finding supports the value of longitudinal BP metrics and highlights the need for research to determine how best to incorporate them into performance assessment.Keywords: hypertension, primary care, team‐based

Introduction

Uncontrolled hypertension (HTN) is a major causal and potentially modifiable risk factor for increased morbidity and mortality [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. Despite the fact that efficacious intervention approaches for blood pressure (BP) control exist [6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12], HTN control rates in large populations persist disappointingly low [4, 13, 14, 15, 16].

Research has established that health care systems play a pivotal role in improving HTN control through team‐based approaches that address multiple health care delivery components [8, 9, 12, 17, 18]. However, the impact of large scale, multicomponent HTN control programs in minority populations in which the burden of cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk factors is high, including older adults of Hispanic origin, remains insufficiently characterized [4, 16, 19, 20].

Methodologically, assessment of how effectively health systems control BP among patients with HTN typically involves calculating annual control rates based on a single time‐point (or snapshot) BP measurement, traditionally the most recent reading within each year [21]. Although a valid approach that optimizes feasibility, important caveats should be acknowledged. For example, BP snapshots are prone to random measurement error, which can lead to HTN status misclassification and underestimation of the risk of HTN‐related adverse outcomes [22]. Additionally, single time‐point readings are inherently less informative than time‐averaged BP for capturing the cumulative adverse impact of elevated BP, as observational studies have shown that the mean of multiple prior systolic BP measurements is more strongly and independently associated with future adverse outcomes than a single current reading [23, 24, 25]. With the widespread adoption of electronic health records (EHRs), routine collection of repeated BP measurements over time has become feasible, particularly in large health systems. However, the extent to which inferences about HTN control differ when control rate is defined using single time‐point rather than within‐person time‐averaged BP remains to be evaluated. Characterizing this distinction represents an important preliminary step toward understanding how to leverage serial BP data in EHRs to improve HTN care in health systems.

Building on key insights into HTN control in large populations provided by epidemiologic studies conducted in highly standardized research settings [14, 15] (e.g., the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey [NHANES], as well as prior large‐scale HTN quality improvement initiatives demonstrating the effectiveness of multicomponent system‐level interventions [8, 10, 26, 27], this study extends the existing literature in several important ways. First, it examines HTN control in an understudied population of older adults primarily of Hispanic origin receiving care within a large integrated health system in South Florida. Second, it leverages longitudinal EHR data collected during routine clinical care over an 11‐year period (2008–2018), providing insight into trends in HTN control in a real‐world clinical setting. Third, it evaluates whether estimates of HTN control differ when calculated using the conventional single time‐point (“snapshot”) BP measurement versus within‐person time‐averaged BP derived from repeated measurements obtained throughout the year available at EHRs. Specifically, the goals of this study were to: (a) assess temporal changes in annual HTN control rates before and after implementation of a primary care‐based quality improvement program by a major health care services provider serving Medicare beneficiaries primarily of Hispanic origin in Miami‐Dade, Florida, and (b) examine how annual HTN control rates derived from within‐person annual‐average BP compare to those based on a single‐time point BP measurement.

Methods

Study Design

This was a longitudinal study that analyzed de‐identified real‐world EHR data from patients receiving care at Leon Medical Centers (LMC), a large integrated health system serving Medicare beneficiaries in Miami‐Dade County, Florida. In 2011, LMC implemented a primary care‐centered, multicomponent QI program to improve BP control among patients with diagnosed HTN, the majority of whom were of Hispanic origin. All patients aged ≥65 years with an ICD‐coded HTN diagnosis were eligible. For each study year, inclusion required continuous enrollment from January 1 through December 31, and at least two BP readings from routine outpatient visits, with one obtained in each half of the year. Analyses were limited to participants younger than 90 years to maintain a balanced age distribution across study years.

LMC HTN Control Program

In 2011, LMC initiated a system‐wide, primary care–centered multicomponent quality improvement (QI) program focused on HTN control across its outpatient centers. Implementation began in 2011 and was gradually rolled out across six outpatient centers during 2011–2012. This staged implementation period corresponds to the intermediate phase (2011–2012) in the analyses presented in Table 2, whereas 2013–2018 represents the period after full program implementation across all centers. The program used a highly coordinated, multidisciplinary team‐based, data‐driven model supported by a comprehensive EHR system. Care teams included primary care physicians (PCPs), registered nurses (RNs), pharmacists, and medical assistants (MAs), and were led by an RN‐directed care management unit, the Gold Standard Team (GST), responsible for monitoring patients and coordinating follow‐up. Core program elements described below were maintained throughout the study period following their introduction.

Standardized BP measurement protocols using validated devices were implemented, accompanied by regular staff training. Physicians and other care team members were encouraged to follow contemporary evidence‐based HTN guidelines and incorporate updates as appropriate. Continuing medical education and periodic email reminders from system leadership reinforced adherence to best practices.

The EHR system supported a HTN registry, real‐time alerts, prescribing and monitoring. The GST used the EHR HTN registry to identify patients, schedule and track follow‐ups, call patients with instructions, monitor no‐shows, and message PCPs. BP was measured at all primary care, cardiology, and urgent care encounters. Out‐of‐range automated BP readings triggered manual rechecks, and persistent elevations generated EHR alerts for PCP review and treatment adjustment. Uncontrolled BP prompted standing orders for adherence counseling and follow‐up visits every 3 weeks until control was achieved. PCPs accessed real‐time EHR dashboards with comprehensive clinical information, and received monthly performance feedback with HTN control rates linked to quality evaluations and incentives.

Recognizing transportation as a key social determinant of health, LMC provided free, unlimited transportation to clinic visits. Medication adherence was supported through pharmacist counseling, waived copayments, and facilitated access to medications. Patients could obtain prescriptions at on‐site satellite pharmacies immediately after clinic visits or receive them via home delivery. Adherence was actively monitored through the electronic HTN registry and reinforced by proactive pharmacy outreach for refill reminders and physician follow‐up in cases of nonadherence.

Patient engagement was integral to the program, emphasizing education and lifestyle modification for HTN control. Activities included individual sessions on healthy habits, quarterly seminars, educational materials in English and Spanish, and a triannual magazine on health topics. Lifestyle interventions were reinforced through guided exercise, wellness, and recreational activities offered at LMC's Healthy Living Centers.

HTN Control Status

Annual HTN control status was defined using two indicators: time‐average BP (AVG‐BP), calculated as the within‐person mean BP across the year, and last BP (LAST‐BP), the most recent BP measurement recorded in that year. For AVG‐BP, time‐averaged systolic BP (AVG‐SBP) was computed separately for each half‐year (January–June and July–December); those were then averaged to obtain the annual within‐person AVG‐SBP. The same procedure was used to calculate time‐averaged diastolic BP (AVG‐DBP). HTN control was defined as SBP < 140 mm Hg and DBP < 90 mm Hg, consistent with prevailing clinical guidelines during most of the study period (e.g., the Seventh and Eighth Joint National Committee reports, JNC 7 and JNC 8), and with the Healthcare Effectiveness Data and Information Set (HEDIS), a widely used set of performance measures developed and maintained by the National Committee for Quality Assurance (NCQA) to assess health system performance, particularly within the managed care industry [28]. The < 140/90 mmHg BP threshold remains the standard used in HEDIS's Controlling High Blood Pressure (BPC) to evaluate health system effectiveness in HTN control. For LAST‐BP, control status was determined using the most recent BP of each calendar year, in line with the calculation of the BPC metric. If multiple readings were recorded on the same date, the lowest systolic and diastolic values were used. If multiple BP measurements were recorded during the same outpatient visit, the last recorded BP values were used. As part of the HTN QI program, BP measurement followed a standardized protocol in which BP was measured at the beginning of each visit. If the initial reading was elevated, patients were asked to rest for several minutes, and the measurement was repeated to obtain a more stable estimate of resting BP. If further evaluation or acute management was required, patients were referred to the urgent care unit within the outpatient center; BP measurements obtained during those urgent care encounters were not included in the present analyses.

Covariates

Other variables analyzed included demographic characteristics (age, sex, ethnicity, and outpatient clinic center) and prevalent diabetes. Individuals were classified as Hispanic if they identified Spanish as their preferred language. Diabetes status was determined using International Classification of Diseases (ICD‐9 or ICD‐10) codes.

Statistical Analysis

Exploratory analyses summarized means, proportions, and standard deviations (±SD) of key variables by study year. All analyses required at least one BP measurement within each half‐year; patients without BP measurements in a given half‐year were excluded (i.e., exclusion due to missing BP data within a given half‐year). No imputation methods were applied. All patients meeting inclusion criteria in each year were included; no sampling was used. This longitudinal study followed an open cohort, as patients could enter or leave the health system over time. As the patient population grew over time, notable shifts in structure were observed, including increases in mean age and diabetes prevalence across study years. Because of those shifts, weights were used to standardize the population structure across study years, so that HTN control changes were not due simply to differences in population structure. In this context, we calculated age‐ and diabetes‐standardized HTN control rates with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using the direct method, with the 2010 LMC population as the reference.

Random‐effects multivariable logistic regression were used to estimate the odds of HTN control with corresponding 95% CIs during the intermediate (2011–2012) and post‐implementation (2013–2018) periods, relative to the program pre‐implementation phase (2008–2010). Models were fit for the overall population, as well as stratified by sex. All models were adjusted for age (<75 vs. 75–89 years) and prevalent comorbidities, including diabetes, coronary heart disease, and heart failure; models for the overall population were additionally adjusted for sex. These covariates were selected based on changes in their prevalence over the study period (Table 1), and their established associations with HTN control. Smoking, alcohol intake, antihypertensive medication use, and obesity were not included as covariates, as these lifestyle and clinical factors were conceptualized a priori as mediators on the causal pathway through which the QI program was hypothesized to enhance HTN control, rather than as confounders. Models included random intercepts to account for within‐person correlation. Agreement between HTN control classifications based on AVG‐BP and LAST‐BP was assessed using weighted and unweighted percent agreement and Cohen's kappa statistics. Analyses were stratified by sex and conducted using Stata version 16 (StataCorp, College Station, TX).

Results

The number of patients with HTN analyzed ranged from 4710 in 2008 to 21 540 in 2018 (Table 1). Over that period, mean age and diabetes prevalence increased over time, from 73.4 ± 5.3 to 77.1 ± 6.1 years, and 35 to 49%, respectively. The proportions of women (55–58%) and Hispanic patients (97–99%) remained stable across study years.

The overall proportion of patients with controlled HTN increased markedly following program implementation (Figure 1). Before implementation (2008–2010), crude annual HTN control rates based on AVG‐BP remained in the low 70% range. Rates increased steadily during the first three years of implementation, reaching 88.3% in 2013, and then stabilized at a high plateau (89–91%) through 2018. Control rates based on the last clinic visit followed a similar pattern, though consistently lower and showing a period effect in 2015. Age‐ and diabetes‐standardized HTN control rates increased similarly in both sexes and closely mirrored trends in unadjusted rates (Figure 2). In both men and women, control rates based on AVG‐BP were consistently and significantly higher than those based on LAST‐BP, as evidenced by non‐overlapping 95% confidence intervals.

Random‐effects logistic models (Table 2) compared the odds of BP control across program phases: pre‐implementation (2008–2010), intermediate (2011–2012), and post‐implementation (2013–2018). Using AVG‐BP, adjusted odds of HTN control were 2.14 (95% CI, 2.03–2.26) times higher in the intermediate phase and 5.84 (95% CI, 5.56–6.14) times higher after full implementation, relative to pre‐implementation. Those associations were similar in women and men. Analyses based on LAST‐BP showed consistent but weaker effects; i.e., OR 1.70 (95% CI, 1.67–1.77) and OR 2.74 (95% CI, 2.65–2.84) for the intermediate and post‐implementation phases, respectively.

Patients excluded due to missing BP data in a given half‐year were slightly younger and had lower prevalence of comorbid conditions, including diabetes and coronary heart disease, as well as lower HTN control rates based on LAST‐BP (data not shown). However, the number and proportion of excluded patients were small after the initial study year, and differences in characteristics were modest. Sensitivity analyses using HTN control defined by LAST‐BP yielded nearly identical estimates and temporal trends (data not shown). These findings suggest that exclusion due to missing BP data did not materially influence the results. Consistent patterns observed using time‐averaged BP further suggest that any impact on those estimates may be limited.

We assessed concordance between HTN control status defined according to AVG‐BP and LAST‐BP (Table 3). Crude percent agreement during the last five study years ranged from 79 to 84% in both women and men (age‐ and diabetes‐standardized results, not shown, were very similar to crude agreements). Kappa statistics, ranging from 0.23 to 0.31 in women and 0.24 to 0.33 in men (all p < 0.001), indicated only fair agreement.

Discussion

This study documented a substantial and sustained improvement in HTN control to very high levels among older patients following the implementation of a primary care‐centered, multicomponent intervention program in a large health system serving Medicare beneficiaries predominantly of Hispanic origin enrolled in a Medicare Advantage plan in Miami‐Dade, Florida. Using a within‐person time‐averaged approach, annual HTN control rates approached or exceeded 90% and remained high for more than 6 years after implementation. Compared with the time‐averaged approach, HTN control rates derived from a single‐snapshot, the most recent BP reading of the year, systematically underestimated HTN control rates.

Our findings align with prior reports demonstrating that system‐level, team‐based interventions can substantially improve HTN control in large populations [8, 9, 10, 18, 26, 27, 29]. For example, a large secondary data analysis study of Veterans Administration data from 2000 to 2010 showed steady improvement in HTN control across all age, gender, and ethnicity groups studied, with overall control rates (<140/90 mmHg) increasing from 46.8% to 76.3% over the decade [26]. Similarly, a 2013 report from Kaiser Permanente Northern California documented an increase in HTN control (<140/90 mmHg) from 43.6% in 2001 to 80.4% in 2009 following the implementation of a large‐scale, multifaceted quality‐improvement program [8]. This study extends prior evidence to a more contemporary period and to an understudied patient population of older, predominantly Hispanic adults in south Florida. It also demonstrated that coordinated, multicomponent approaches to HTN control can be effective across diverse health system contexts, and highlights differences in the assessment of HTN control rates derived from time‐averaged versus single‐visit BP measures.

The LMC QI program for HTN control incorporated core elements characteristic of successful large‐scale initiatives, including a highly coordinated team‐based care model, standardized BP measurement protocols, adherence to evidence‐based treatment guidelines, structured physician and patient engagement strategies, and a robust EHR infrastructure. Additional components included in‐center pharmacies, transportation support, and an in‐house wellness program. Open to all patients, the wellness program, launched in 2012, offered culturally tailored cardiovascular health activities, including group exercise (e.g., Latin dance), supervised gym sessions, chronic disease self‐management education, recreational activities, and opportunities for social interaction.

Traditionally, health system performance in HTN control has been evaluated using a single “snapshot” reading, typically the most recent clinic BP. With longitudinal EHR data now widely available, there is growing interest in alternative metrics derived from repeated BP readings within the same patient to better capture the consistency and quality of BP management and to enhance clinical and prognostic insight. For example, Time in Target Range (TTR), the proportion of time BP remains within a prespecified optimal range, has gained increasing attention for its ability to predict major adverse outcomes, including cardiovascular events, atrial fibrillation, and probable dementia [30, 31, 32, 33, 34]. TTR and time‐averaged BP metrics are approaches that emphasize assessing HTN control as a function of sustained BP management rather than isolated readings. Future research should compare longitudinal BP metrics to establish standardized, scalable methods that leverage EHR data efficiently while minimizing data‐collection burden in evaluating system performance.

Direct comparison of absolute HTN control rates across studies warrants caution. First, our analysis used data from a real‐world clinical setting that, albeit standardized, differed from the highly controlled research environments often used in community‐based studies. Second, HTN control rates traditionally reported in the literature rely on a single snapshot BP, which, as shown here, may yield different HTN control estimates than those based on AVG‐BP. Third, methodological differences from NCQA specifications further limit comparability. Specifically, (a) BP control in this study was consistently defined as <140/90 mm Hg across all years, whereas HEDIS criteria underwent occasional modifications during the study period (e.g., in 2014, the threshold for adults aged 60–85 years without diabetes was <150/90 mm); (b) we calculated both AVG‐BP and LAST‐BP, and used data from all patients aged 65–89 years with ≥2 BP measurements annually, rather than relying on the smaller, audited NCQA sample (ages 18–85 years) with more restrictive eligibility criteria; and (c) we did not exclude patients with end‐stage renal disease or nonacute inpatient admissions, as permitted under NCQA optional exclusions.

This study has several strengths. It focused on a large, understudied and diverse population of older Hispanics in South Florida, among whom the burden of cardiovascular disease‐related risk factors is substantial [4, 19, 20]. It leveraged the use of a sophisticated EHRs system, which allowed analysis of HTN control data over a study period of more than 10 years, including 3 years before and 8 years after program implementation.

Study findings should be interpreted in light of several considerations. First, this observational study examined temporal HTN control trends before and after implementation of a QI program in a large health system; neither randomization, nor a concurrent control group was in place. Consequently, the improvements observed during the study period cannot be attributed solely to the program, as secular trends in HTN awareness, treatment practices, and broader changes in health system performance may also have contributed to better BP levels over time. Indeed, national data suggest that HTN control improved over the past two decades, consistent with secular trends in HTN awareness and treatment, although these trends have not been monotonic and gains have not been sustained in more recent years [14, 15]. Notably, HTN control rates in the US population actually declined after 2013–2014 (Muntner et al., HTN 2022), whereas LMC control rates continued to improve and remained high through 2018. Additionally, HTN control rates at LMC were stable in the pre‐implementation period (2008–2010) and rose sharply only after program implementation, a pattern more consistent with a program effect than a gradual secular trend. Furthermore, the magnitude and sustained nature of improvements in HTN control observed in this study, as well as program's components were consistent with those reported for other successful initiatives [8, 9, 10, 17, 18, 29]. Parallel improvements in adherence to antihypertensive therapy, as assessed by the NCQA‐defined renin–angiotensin system antagonist metric (the percentage of patients with a prescription for a renin–angiotensin system antagonist who filled prescriptions sufficient to cover at least 80% of the time they were expected to be taking the medication), also improved in parallel with HTN control, from 73.4% in 2010 to 93–95% in the final three study years (performance data reported to CMS in previous years by the health system as part of its administrative routine–not shown here), provide additional evidence supporting a direct program effect. Second, residual confounding warrants consideration. Although we prespecified that lifestyle and clinical factors conceptualized as mediators of the program's effect on HTN control would not be included in adjustment, it could be argued that inclusion of variables such as smoking, alcohol intake, and obesity could have strengthened the analysis. However, the unavailability of systematic data across the study period precluded post hoc sensitivity analyses incorporating these factors. The lack of adjustment for obesity and alcohol intake–both of which increased in prevalence in the U.S. population during this period and are associated with poorer HTN control–would be expected to attenuate the estimated effect, potentially underestimating the program's true impact. Given the magnitude of the observed improvement in HTN control before versus after program implementation (Table 2), residual confounding of implausible strength would be required to fully account for the findings. We therefore contend that residual confounding is unlikely to fully explain the observed improvements. Third, this study evaluated the overall impact of a multifaceted program, precluding inference about the contribution of individual components. Fourth, we assessed concordance between HTN control status defined according to AVG‐BP and LAST‐BP. Although overall percent agreement between the two approaches was relatively high, kappa statistics indicated only fair agreement. This apparent discrepancy likely reflects the well‐known kappa paradox, a phenomenon in which Cohen's kappa may underestimate agreement when the prevalence of one category is very high. In these situations, the expected agreement due to chance increases substantially, which reduces the magnitude of kappa even when observed percent agreement remains high. In the present study, the high prevalence of HTN control in the later study years contributed to the relatively modest kappa values despite the high observed percent agreement between the two classification methods. Fourth, findings may not be automatically generalized to other settings. The program was culturally tailored for older Hispanic adults enrolled in a South Florida Medicare Advantage plan, and HTN control may vary by access to care, demographic composition, and health system resources. Finally, although this study did not assess the program's direct impact on downstream outcomes of uncontrolled HTN, the use of BP control as an intermediate surrogate for the prevention of stroke and cardiovascular disease is well established.

It should be noted that the field of HTN control in large populations has evolved considerably since the study period, as reflected in more contemporary clinical guidelines. Current treatment targets are lower than those in effect during the study, even though the current HEDIS performance metric for health systems remain <140/90 mmHg. The overarching goal of <130/80 mm Hg for most adults, including those aged ≥65 years, was introduced in the 2017 American College of Cardiology (ACC)/American Heart Association (AHA) Guidelines [1]. The 2025 ACC/AHA update expands on that recommendation, encouraging treatment to achieve an SBP <120 mm Hg5, as well as incorporating the new PREVENT cardiovascular risk calculator to guide clinical decision‐making [35]. Before 2017, BP <140/90 mm Hg was generally considered normal; the 2017 ACC/AHA Guidelines redefined BP categories as <120/<80 mm Hg (normal), 120–129/<80 mm Hg (elevated), 130–139/80–89 mm Hg (stage 1 HTN), and ≥140/≥90 mm Hg (stage 2 HTN), definitions reaffirmed in 2025. Advances also include greater emphasis on home BP self‐monitoring, use of validated measurement devices, patient engagement, and the promotion of Guideline Directed Lifestyle Modifications (GDLMs) [36, 37]. While the study period does not capture the impact of changes in HTN control guidelines from professional societies, including the adoption of lower diagnostic and treatment thresholds [1, 5], study findings remain clinically relevant, as they demonstrate the effectiveness of a large‐scale, real‐world BP quality improvement program in a diverse older population and provide a foundation for reassessment of prior program successes within the context of newer HTN control guidelines.

In summary, this study adds to the HTN control literature by providing real‐world evidence that a health system‐based, multicomponent program can achieve and sustain high control rates among an understudied, predominantly Hispanic older population in Miami‐Dade, Florida. The LMC initiative aligns with national and international efforts such as Million Hearts [38, 39], the Global Hearts Initiative led by the World Health Organization and the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [40], and the Pan American Health Organization's HEARTS in the Americas [41, 42], which promote team‐based care, standardized treatment protocols, lifestyle modification, reliable medication supply, and proactive follow‐up. Together, those initiatives offer a scalable path forward to improve cardiovascular health outcomes and advance equity across diverse aging populations.

Ethics Statement

This study involved the secondary analysis of existing electronic health records (EHR) data that were fully de‐identified and provided to the researchers in an anonymous format.
