1 Introduction and Motivation
Increasing electric vehicle (EV) adoption will change the composition of EV charging load toward high-power
charging (HPC). These shifts provide an opportunity for HPC and facility equipment to evolve and improve in
effciency, cost, and size. HPC stations require a common approach to a scalable and cost-effcient charging hub. The
hub can accommodate long- and short-dwell charging of light-duty, medium-duty, and heavy-duty vehicles across
many vocations and site confgurations (loads and resources). The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) established the
EVs@Scale Consortium by bringing together national laboratories and key stakeholders to conduct joint research
and development to address challenges and barriers around EV charging infrastructure [1].
This study builds upon the previous extensive report publication that outlined the structure and components of the
High-Power Electric Vehicle Charging Hub Integration Platform (eCHIP) project within the EVs@Scale consor-
tium [2]. Expanding on that foundation, this report describes the site energy management system (SEMS) approach
taken for a high-power, interoperable charging experimental testbed. The increasing need for HPC to enable a re-
fueling experience similar to that of conventional vehicles, especially to reduce range anxiety on long trips, poses a
signifcant challenge for relevant industries. The eCHIP project proposes new architectures using a DC bus approach
(‘DC hub’ for short) for an HPC site due to easier integration of chargers, fewer conversion stages, higher effciency,
and lower controller complexity [2, 3].
An HPC site typically consists of numerous electric vehicle supply equipment (EVSE) and distributed energy re-
sources (DERs). Managing and coordinating these assets require a SEMS to communicate with and coordinate
EVSEs, energy storage systems (ESSs), photovoltaic (PV) systems, other DERs, as well as dispatchable and non-
dispatchable site loads. Additionally, SEMS facilitates communication with the advanced distribution management
system (ADMS) and the distributed energy resource management system (DERMS) of the distribution grid, enhanc-
ing grid integration and supporting the implementation of grid services and programs.
National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) and Argonne National Laboratory (Argonne) have developed two
DC hub testbeds that will implement various SEMS functions described in this report. Figure 1 demonstrates the
diagrams for both of the testbeds available in this project, building upon previously described components in our
report publication [2].

and stable manner, as well as for seamlessly integrating with and supporting the power grid. To this end, this report

The design and implementation of SEMS have multiple dimensions. Both require careful consideration of hub
operation requirements, short- and long-term operational objectives, customer preferences, grid-side demands,
asset integration with the DC bus, asset communication capabilities and protocols, interoperability, monitoring and
measurement capabilities, and various other physical limitations and constraints.
Key functions of SEMS include minimizing energy costs through the strategic use of ESSs and DERs along with
coordinating for different utility pricing mechanisms. Charge management is another key function of SEMS, re-
sponsible for overseeing and monitoring EVSE operations. This includes tasks such as authentication and billing,
reservation and charge scheduling, data collection, reporting, and more. Given the scale, diversity, and asset fexi-
bility of DC hubs, SEMS will play a crucial role in providing various ancillary grid services, including frequency
regulation, voltage and reactive power support, load curtailment, and renewable energy integration. The architecture
of SEMS is another key design parameter that defnes its interactions with hub assets, as well as external grid-side
systems like ADMS and DERMS. Monitoring and visualization capabilities of SEMS are also critical for making
sure that the hub operates at intended operating points and managing energy fows.
The adoption of SEMS represents a signifcant step forward in the evolution of EV charging infrastructure. By
leveraging advanced energy management technologies, SEMS can transform charging plazas into smart, effcient,
scalable, and sustainable energy hubs. This not only supports the growing demand for EV charging but also aligns
with broader goals of energy effciency and environmental sustainability.
The available use cases described in this report will serve as a roadmap for the eCHIP project team as possible future
use cases to be implemented in the hardware platform described earlier in [2] and provide an agreement on the
terminology selected for the project goals. Furthermore, the insights and recommendations provided in this report
aim to guide stakeholders and relevant industries in realizing the full potential of possible control and integration
solutions and advancing the future of HPC infrastructure operation. Figure 2 shows the structure of the report.

2 Elements of SEMS
This section outlines the essential elements of a SEMS, focusing on core functions that defne the key services and
objectives a charging site can offer; architectures that examine interactions between site assets and control schemes;
and monitoring and visualization capabilities that provide insights into system performance and support decision-
making.
Effective energy management requires a good combination of these elements and their seamless integration to opti-
mize performance, ensure reliability, and achieve site operation goals. Therefore, understanding these would help in
developing more effective energy management strategies, ensuring optimal performance and reliability of the system.

2.1 Core Functions of SEMS
SEMS functions defne the objectives under different operating modes and conditions of a charging hub. There are
two main modes of operation: grid-connected and grid-islanded. In the grid-connected mode, the charging hub is
connected to a medium-voltage (MV)/low-voltage (LV) AC distribution grid through a grid-tie inverter (GTI), where
the main power is supplied from the grid itself. In the grid-islanded mode, the charging hub disconnects from the
grid and operates as a stand-alone entity, relying on its on-site generation.
Grid-connected operation should primarily ensure meeting EV charging customer requirements, regulating and con-
trolling internal DC hub voltage, dispatching DER assets if any, managing grid integration with the utility operator,
managing other hub loads, and ensuring the safety of the hub through protection mechanisms. Islanded operation
should also implement some of these core functions concerning internal hub operations. Additionally, it should
manage transitioning to and from islanded mode, control energy fow, and maintain acceptable power quality.
Core functions of SEMS performed under these operating modes include EV charge management and hub energy
management. The charge management system is responsible for monitoring, controlling, and evaluating the charging
process of EVs and determining their requirements. Charge management is commonly performed using a dedicated
communications protocol such as OCPP [5]. The energy/power management system, on the other hand, focuses on
load balancing, optimization, energy scheduling, management, and dispatching of other energy resources as well as
handling grid interactions by providing various grid ancillary services. Some of these services include voltage and
frequency support, active power injection, implementing demand response programs, and managing the contribution
of local energy resources.
The specifcs of SEMS core functions and their implementation can vary based on the application, operating mode,
sizing, and available assets of the charging hub. Some core functions of SEMS are categorized and detailed in the
following subsections.
2.1.1 Energy and Power Management
Energy management is one of the critial tasks of SEMS and it refers to the process of distributing energy and power
demand across available energy resources to prevent overloading supply resources (inverter, PV, ESS, etc.). By
managing the energy demand and supply, the hub can maintain operational stability, preventing voltage collapses and
power outages and ensuring consistent power quality.
Energy management may involve actively curtailing controlled loads and prioritizing power allocation to critical
loads during supply shortages or periods of high energy costs. ESS plays a key role in managing energy fows by
storing excess energy and providing fexibility in meeting load demands, reducing the need for load curtailment
within the hub. ESS optimizes the use of infexible generation sources like PV by storing energy during periods of
low demand and high generation, then releasing it during peak demand times. Some examples of SEMS controllers
and algorithms will be detailed in Section 2.2.2.
2.1.2 Charging Hub Cost Minimization
Site costs can be classifed into capital investment and system operation costs. Minimizing capital expenditure
(CapEx) results in the smallest feasible system design; however, this does not necessarily imply the lowest op-
erational cost (OpEx). OpEx arises from purchasing utility grid electricity, as well as from the maintenance and
replacement of components. Therefore, SEMS can maximize revenue by minimizing EV charging costs, reducing
site energy costs, optimizing ESS use to minimize battery degradation, and maximizing the use of renewable energy
generation. The types of charging hub OpEx cost minimization are described below.

 3

Minimization of EV charging cost: SEMS can minimize charging costs by dynamically balancing power from the
grid, ESS, and PV to meet EV energy demand, charging the ESS when grid electricity prices are low and using ESS
power when prices are high. SEMS enables EV owners to schedule charging based on arrival, departure times, and
required charging mileage. This information allows SEMS to optimize energy management during peak demand,
ensuring a cost-effcient charging experience. In cases where user arrival and departure times are uncertain, SEMS
can still minimize charging costs by selecting the most cost-effective energy source available during the charging.
Minimization of site energy cost: Effectively utilizing the stored energy of the ESS and the fexibility of EV charg-
ing load can reduce peak demand and consequently lower the site’s demand charges. This reduction, especially
during peak hours, decreases overall energy costs. In the cases when ESS does not have enough energy to support,
SEMS can reduce charging demand from the grid at the expense of increased charging time.
Minimization of battery degradation: The replacement cost of ESS is considered an operational expense for
the site operator. Battery degradation of ESS causes a reduction in capacity and effciency over the lifetime of the
battery. One of the main causes of battery degradation is the depth of discharge of ESS. SEMS plays a crucial role
in managing this issue. By optimally scheduling the ESS charging and discharging within a pre-decided SOC range,
SEMS can effectively reduce the degradation of ESS [6, 7]. SEMS can also perform charging/discharging rate
control to manage battery degredation or state of health (SOH). This optimal scheduling ensures that the batteries
operate within their most-effcient range with respect to their SOH, thereby minimizing the stress on the battery
pack. As a result, the cycle life of ESS is maximized, and the overall operational costs are kept in check.
Maximization of the use of renewable generation: Prioritizing the utilization of PVs and ESS signifcantly reduces
the cost of charging. Renewable generation may not often coincide with peak load bacause of its inherit uncer-
tainties. Therefore, charging the ESS or EVs during periods of high renewable generation benefts the operator by
reducing operational costs. By leveraging renewable energy sources and strategically managing energy storage,
SEMS can optimize energy usage, enhance sustainability, and improve overall cost-effciency for both operators
and EV owners [8]. DC hubs can host and coordinate various intrinsically DC or variable frequency AC renewable
energy resources (PV, wind, hydrogen storage, etc.), integrating them into the grid through the GTI. They can supply
power to the grid during peak times either directly from these resources or indirectly by storing and releasing their
energy [9].
2.1.3 EV Charge Management
EV charge management involves handling all EV charging processes and implementing charging control policies in
alignment with high-level operational objectives. This includes several core functions:
Reservation and charge scheduling: For increased charging visibility for customers, it is important to provide the
ability to drivers to reserve a port or station in advance to ensure charging can begin upon arrival. This involves the
ability to schedule charging of individual EVs via standards-based methods and by determining the specifc charging
needs of each EV based on battery status, energy needs, travel plans, and other factors. By precisely defning cus-
tomer requirements, SEMS can customize charging sessions to address the EV’s specifc needs while simultaneously
managing the energy and power constraints of the charging hub.
EVSE operation and monitoring: The charging process is continuously monitored, and necessary controls are
applied to ensure optimal charging performance. Real-time data on charging speed, energy transferred, and charg-
ing status is tracked to ensure the charging process adheres to the requirements defned by the standards. If any
anomalies or ineffciencies are detected, the system can adjust the charging parameters or notify the operator to take
corrective actions, ensuring a safe and effcient charging session.
Authentication and billing: This process verifes user credentials and manages the fnancial transactions related
to charging. This includes authenticating the user through RFID cards, mobile apps, or other secure methods to
ensure that only authorized users can access the charging services. Once authenticated, the system handles the billing
process, recording the energy dispensed and processing the payment through integrated payment gateways, ensuring
a seamless and secure transaction experience.
Data analytics and reporting: This involves collecting, recording, and securely storing data related to charging
sessions for future analysis and reporting. These data include timestamps, energy usage, user details, and charging
station performance metrics. Proper data management helps with tracking usage patterns, detecting faults, and

identifying potential improvements to the overall charging infrastructure.This data could also be used to predict and
schedule maintenance activities.
2.1.4 Grid Support
DC-powered charging hubs can play a crucial role in supporting the power grid by offering several grid support
functions that can help stabilize the grid and reduce equipment loading. This is achieved in the DC hub architecture
with a GTI. GTIs can operate mainly in two modes: grid-following and grid-forming. In grid-following mode, a
GTI acts as a current source that synchronizes its output with the AC grid voltage, and it can provide active (P) and
reactive (Q) power support to the grid. Whereas in the grid-forming mode, the GTI acts as a voltage source and
implements P-f and Q-V droop functions akin to synchronous machines, and they can provide virtual inertia and
improve system strength, in addition to voltage and frequency support. This is especially critical during disturbances
or outages taking place in the grid.
The standards and defnitions for the grid interconnection of DERs, along with the role of GTIs, are comprehensively
detailed in IEEE 1547 [10]. We consider this standard and other relative standards as a baseline for the possible grid
support functions under this topic. A broader list of grid integration standards that serve as a benchmark for this
study is provided in Appendix A.
Main grid support functions: The main voltage and reactive/active power control function requirements listed in
IEEE 1547 that are relevant to the GTI of a DC charging hub are
 • Constant power factor mode
 • Voltage–reactive power mode (Volt-Var function)
 • Active power–reactive power mode (Watt-Var function)
 • Constant reactive power mode
 • Voltage–active power mode (Volt-Watt function)
In addition, the GTI can provide frequency–droop operation (Hz-Watt function) under low- and high-frequency
conditions.
The activated grid support function of the GTI and its settings are chosen by the area electric power system (EPS)
operator and communicated to the DER operator (charging hub operator in the context of this report). Therefore,
the SEMS should be designed to accomodate each of the grid-support functions mandated by IEEE 1547 to enable
deployment in a variety of operational conditions. Implementation of the Volt-Watt and Hz-Watt functions requires a
source of active power. Because the voltage or frequency variation of the grid cannot be predicted, it may be neces-
sary to hold some of the ESS capacity in reserve in order to rapidly decrease the DC hub active power consumption
from the grid (or increase the DC hub active power generation to the grid). While reactive power can be sourced or
sinked from the grid decoupled from the DC hub power balance or DC voltage regulation, the source/sink of reac-
tive power requires orthogonal reduction of active power draw from the grid, which SEMS need to consider. This
is because the GTI’s available active power is related to its reactive power in an orthogonal way. Therefore, imple-
mentation of a grid-support function related to reactive power can still derate the GTI’s active power capability and
impact the energy management functions of SEMS.
Additional grid support functions: These functions are not required by standards but may be incorportated into
SEMS and GTI capabilities.
 • Inertial response (virtual inertia)
 • Dynamic voltage support.
Under inertial response, the GTI behavior is similar to frequency–droop mode, in that active power is controlled in
response to measured grid frequency. However, with an inertial response, the GTI active power does not respond
instanteouly, instead mimicking the physical inertia of a synchronous machine. With dynamic voltage response,
the GTI sources current to the grid during under-voltage events, instead of temporarily ceasing current fow while
remaining connected. If a portion of the GTI capacity is allocated for inertia response or dynamic voltage support, it
may affect the core functions of SEMS.

 5

Grid-forming capability: The GTI in a DC hub may also behave as a grid-forming inverter (GFI). Compared
to a grid-following inverter, the inverter behaves as a voltage source rather than a current source. While a grid-
following inverter may implement the frequency-watt or volt-var grid support functions as described above, a GFI
implements P − ω and Q − V droop functions akin to traditional generation sources on the utility grid such as
syncronous machines.
Grid-forming inverters can signifcantly improve grid stability and resilience compared to grid-following inverters
[11]. Because the grid-forming mode requires the GTI to operate under frequency and voltage control on the grid
side, it cannot also regulate the DC bus voltage. Therefore, a resource within the DC hub such as an energy storage
system must operate in voltage-regulation mode, where its power is not explicitly controlled. Therefore, signifcant
changes to the SEMS controls are required to enable the GTI to be used in grid-forming mode.
Demand response: This function involves reducing electricity consumption during peak demand periods, grid
stress, or specifc market conditions [12], and can be effectively integrated to SEMS through various strategies.
Automated demand response (ADR) refers to fully automated signals from utilities or other entities to connect with
customer control systems. OpenADR, supported by the OpenADR Alliance, is a standards development initiative
that facilitates seamless information exchange to support this automation [13]. Some hub-specifc demand response
strategies can be summarized as follows:
 • Smart charging: Grid stress can be alleviated by scheduling EV charging based on energy needs and temporar-
 ily reducing or pausing charging during peak demand periods. This can also be triggered by external signals
 from the grid operator. However, it is important to note that a short-dwell charging site has low fexibility in
 terms of EV customers’ stringent charging requirements.
 • Utilizing stored hub energy: Stored energy can power local loads within the charging hub, reducing the need
 for external grid power and indirectly curtailing the site load. This operation can also extend to include EVs
 with V2X capability, further increasing curtailment capacity.
 • Load reduction: Identifying and curtailing non-essential or non-critical loads within the charging station hub
 during peak periods can further support load curtailment efforts.
 • User engagement and incentives: Offering incentives for EV owners to charge during off-peak periods or
 participate in demand response programs.
2.1.5 DC Hub Resiliency
Charging hub resiliency refers to the ability to maintain internal operations—such as energy and power fows—during
or after high-impact low-probability (HILP) events like outages, cyberatacks, islanding, reconnections, and failures
in hardware and communication system.
In the event of grid-wide failures, SEMS can operate circuit breakers at the single point of common coupling (PCC)
between the hub and the distribution grid, isolating the hub to maintain power quality and continue supporting EV
charging services. This requires SEMS to implement procedures for a transition to islanded mode. Effcient energy
management in the self-sustaining hub involves optimal power dispatch between its generation resources and loads.
To ensure smooth internal operations, SEMS must carry out key functions, as outlined earlier in the report. Once
the grid is restored, SEMS can initiate re-synchronization, ensuring voltage and frequency at the POI are within
acceptable limits. Upon reconnection, the hub can resume powering curtailed loads, recharge the ESS, and provide
ancillary services to the grid.
Detecting and responding to power loss due to supply equipment failures is critical for hub resiliency, as failures can
quickly escalate and lead to voltage collapse on the bus. Dispatching alternative power supplies or curtailing non-
critical loads can prevent operational shutdowns and help maintain bus voltage stability. This is achieved through
comprehensive monitoring and fast communication capabilities enabled by a centralized control architecture. Alter-
natively, voltage-based decentralized architectures, such as droop control, can improve the hub’s responsiveness and
autonomy in handling supply or demand fuctuations. These approaches will be further discussed in Section 2.2.
A failure in the communication system—whether due to network issues, asset connection problems, or cyberat-
tacks—can disrupt hub stability and must be carefully managed by SEMS to enhance hub resiliency. Leveraging
distributed controller architectures can improve the hub’s ability to handle such disruptions, further enhancing re-
siliency.

2.2 SEMS Architecture
2.2.1 Architecture Overview
SEMS coordinates lower-level functions, such as bidirectional power control and local voltage regulation, by inter-
acting with charging hub assets (e.g., EVSEs, ESSs, PV) to ensure the charging hub’s objective functions are met. It
also participates in higher-level grid-related operations and functions through systems such as ADMS or DERMS.
SEMS lies in between these lower- and higher-level operations, ensuring seamless interactions between the two. The
interactions between different operating levels facilitated by SEMS are illustrated in Figure 3.

 Figure 3. Control structure architecture for a generic charging hub

The SEMS is broken into two main systems:
 1. Charge Management System (CMS): This system handles the monitoring, control, and management of the
 EV charging infrastructure described in Section 2.1.3, ensuring EV charging is managed effectively.
 2. Energy Management System (Site Controller): This system oversees site-wide energy and power man-
 agement, including monitoring and controlling energy usage, managing peak demand, optimizing energy
 production, and dispatching energy sources, as detailed in Sections 2.1.1, 2.1.2 and 2.1.4.
The two systems communicate to exchange information necessary to accomplish the hub’s objectives, as outlined in
Section 2.1. The CMS may provide the site controller with negotiated and aggregated charge reservation schedules,
station status, and meter values to ensure compliance with key objectives. Additionally, the site controller receives
telemetry signals from other hub assets for monitoring and control. It also communicates with ADMS and DERMS
to receive grid-related signals, such as price signals and ancillary service requests. Figure 4 illustrates the functional
levels of the charging hub and the corresponding functions at each level.
2.2.2 Control Framework
SEMS functions can be mainly implemented in centralized or distributed control system architectures. A centralized
system monitors all system states and determines power allocation and distribution across all EVs and assets in
the charging hub. A distributed structure, on the other hand, allows these functions to be decoupled and managed
separately by individual assets.
The choice of SEMS architecture infuences two key design considerations: the amount of communication and rate
of transmission, and the controller step time. A centralized structure allows for more advanced decision-making,
achieving longer-term operational objectives and maximizing asset utilization. However, it requires frequent and
uninterrupted communication and data fow to monitor system states and respond. The agility of the controller is
limited by potential communication delays, interruptions, and computational burden, resulting in relatively slower
responses. Centralized structures are also vulnerable to single-point and cascading failures, increasing the system’s

liability. In contrast, a distributed structure allows assets (e.g., EVSEs, ESS, PV) to operate autonomously with
minimal communication requirements, leading to more robust and agile responses. The drawback of this structure is
that assets are typically unaware of other assets, their states, and overall hub utilization, resulting in suboptimal hub
operations.
Alternatively, a hybrid approach can utilize both structures, aiming to achieve higher-level system objectives in the
long term while ensuring robustness in the hub at all times. A centralized system can inform distributed assets at reg-
ular intervals and optimize their operations by providing them with a target operating point that is calculated based
on higher-level, long-term objectives. This provision can be in the form of retuning or adaptively updating local
controller parameters. The assets’ operating points would then shift closer to the updated desired points. Meanwhile,
their autonomy allows them to quickly respond and adjust their operations in case of deviations from the target due
to uncertainties and non-ideal conditions, leading to more robust performance. The centralized system can do the
necessary corrections and provide new operating targets at the next update point.
2.2.3 Centralized Approaches
This section discusses two common centralized approaches.
2.2.3.1 Rule-Based Approach
The operation of controllable assets is determined based on predefned conditional rules. A comprehensive study
conducted under the eCHIP project showcases a Controller Hardware-in-the-Loop (C-HIL) example of this control
approach [14]. The asset states (e.g., power levels, hub voltage, SOC, etc.) are continuously monitored to check
if the conditions are met. These rules can be established based on expert knowledge and fne-tuned to enhance
operational performance. The rules can be either deterministic or probabilistic (or a combination of both), depending
on their complexity and the degree of uncertainty in the system. Incorporating probabilistic or weighted elements
can make the system more robust and adaptive to uncertainties (e.g., fuzzy logic). An example of a deterministic and
probabilistic rule for managing the charging and discharging of an ESS can be as follows:
 • Deterministic rule: "If SOC < 20%, then charge the battery."

 8

 • Probabilistic rule: "If the probability of SOC dropping below 20% within the next hour is greater than 50%,
 then charge the battery."
The rules and their threshold parameters typically remain constant during operation. However, more adaptive energy
management can be designed with a family of rules that can be activated under different conditions and operating
points.
2.2.3.2 Optimized Approach
The optimized approach enables the operator to precisely and systematically defne the operational goals of a charg-
ing hub. The operation of controllable assets is determined by the solution of an optimized control problem that is
solved iteratively at regular control intervals, ∆t=tk −tk−1 . Model predictive control (MPC) is a popular choice for
energy management systems. MPC is an iterative, fnite-horizon optimization technique for controlling a system. At
each time step tk , the system states are sampled, and an objective function is minimized over a future time horizon
[tk ,tk + T ] (or discretely [k, k + N] with T =N∆t), where T (or N) is the prediction horizon window. The solution
to the optimization problem consists of the decision variables, which are usually the controlled variables. Only the
frst sample(s) of the solution are implemented, with the remaining samples discarded. At the next time interval, the
system states are resampled, and computations are repeated to fnd a new solution starting from the current time.
In the SEMS framework, the system states usually represent the energy stored in the assets. A predictive system
model is typically formulated based on the SOC change of the energy storage units. A state-space model of a charg-
ing hub system with an ESS can be developed using a model similar to Coulomb Counting, as follows:

where Pess and Capess are the power fow into and total energy capacity of the ESS, respectively. The current system
state (SOCess [k]) needs to be sampled from the actual measurements. Therefore, the accuracy of the model and the
measurements impact the performance of the controller.
The horizon window and the controller step time are two important design parameters that affect the performance
and should be carefully selected based on the dynamic characteristics of the system. A suffciently large horizon
window can be chosen to capture slowly varying signals and optimize longer-term operations. A larger horizon win-
dow results in more decision variables to be solved based on the selected step time, increasing the computation time.
Therefore, a reasonable pair of step time and horizon window size should be used. The use of a horizon window
also requires the prediction of how the system will evolve in time. The state space model can predict future system
states, but random variables such as future price signals, PV generation, and EV demand must be forecasted to be
included in the optimization formulation. The accuracy of the forecast will also impact the expected performance,
but forecasting methods are not within the scope of this report.
The objective function and system constraints of the controller are determined by the operator, taking into account
the specifc needs and operational requirements. Practical objectives may include minimizing the total energy cost
from the grid, reducing peak power demand, minimizing battery degradation, maximizing renewable resource uti-
lization, and minimizing customer wait times. The operator may choose to optimize multiple conficting objectives,
such as minimizing peak demand and customer wait times. To do so, they often need to prioritize these objectives by
assigning appropriate weights.
The optimization constraints are primarily determined by the physical limitations of the system, such as power
ratings, energy capacities, and the steady-state power balance in the DC bus between generation and consumption.
Some relaxations may be needed to handle the edge cases resulting from insuffcient step time.
2.2.4 Decentralized Approaches
2.2.4.1 Droop-Based Approach
Droop control is used to regulate voltage (and frequency in AC grids) based on the power-sharing between sources
and loads in a distribution grid. A grid-forming asset, such as an inverter, regulates the grid voltage by adjusting
its output based on the total system load, tracking a continuous curve. In contrast, loads adjust their output power
(or current) based on the grid voltage, following their respective droop curve. Conventional droop curves are de-
fned as piecewise linear functions, as depicted in Figure 5; however, more complex curves can also be defned if
needed. The system load can be modeled based on power (V-P droop) or current (V-I droop), depending on the asset
requirements.

2.2.5 Hybrid Approaches
Both centralized and decentralized approaches have their own advantages and disadvantages, as previously men-
tioned. Centralized solutions can gather system-wide information and make more informed high-level decisions, but
they typically operate at a slower time scale, limiting their response to fast load dynamics and uncertainties. On the
other hand, decentralized solutions operate closer to the converter level and are faster and more robust, but they lack
global system awareness, which can result in underutilization or suboptimal operation.

A hybrid approach aims to combine the best of both worlds by creating a multilayer hierarchical control structure. In
this structure, each layer operates at different time scales and regularly coordinates with each other for corrections
and updates. This can maximize longer-term operational benefts and maintain robustness against disturbances
at the same time. A recent work from the eCHIP project on hybrid control implementation in a real-time C-HIL
environment provides a more in-depth discussion [15].

2.3 Monitoring and Visualization
A key feature of any SEMS is monitoring and visualization, which can help operators make informed decisions and
troubleshoot issues as outlined in [2]. As described in Section 2.2.1, the SEMS consists of a CMS and an EMS. Both
systems work together to accomplish a core function, where the CMS manages only EV charging, and the EMS
handles the power and energy requirements of the hub and all other assets connected to the hub.
The ability to receive critical updates and notifcations on a mobile device, such as a cell phone, is essential for
reducing the need for constant monitoring of a dashboard or visualization. These notifcations can be sent as text
messages, emails, or push notifcations. A SEMS capable of allowing the operator to confgure these types of alerts
and notifcations is advantageous.
Visualization provides real-time insights into energy generation, consumption, and storage within the charging
hub. A well-designed dashboard displays operational data, alerts for faults, and performance metrics, aiding in
effcient management and decision-making. It is a key component of SEMS, enhancing functionality, monitoring,
and effciency in managing a DC charging hub. Visualization serves several crucial purposes such as:
 • Real-time monitoring: Provides real-time data on energy consumption, generation, and storage, allowing
 operators to monitor the system’s performance continuously and take action if issues arise.
 • Data analysis: Helps with spotting trends, fnding ineffciencies, and understanding energy usage and load
 patterns, which are crucial for making better decisions and optimizing objectives.
 • Problem detection and alerts: Makes it easier to quickly identify issues like equipment failures, faults,
 unexpected power consumption, or voltage drop, enabling prompt corrective actions.
 • Performance tracking: Allows users to track key performance indicators and measure the effectiveness of
 core functions and possible system improvements.
 • Optimization: Aids operators in fnding opportunities to update optimization targets of the site operations
 based on visual investigation of the site status, updating priorities, and dispatching new resources when
 needed.

3 Conclusion and Future Work
A SEMS is a critical component of future high-power EV charging hubs. It monitors and supervises all intra-hub
operations, coordinates grid interactions, and implements energy management strategies and load-sharing controls
at various levels and time scales. This report provides a high-level overview and guidelines on the essential ele-
ments of such energy management systems. These elements primarily focus on the core functions, architecture, and
monitoring aspects of site energy management.
The core functions defne the grid support functions and operational objectives that a charging hub is expected to
provide and achieve. The architectural section investigates the coordination between hub assets, different control
schemes, and their potential benefts and drawbacks. Lastly, the report discusses the needs, types, and functionalities
of a monitoring and visualization system.
We will use this report as a reference guide for our future plans to implement and demonstrate energy management
solutions under the eCHIP project. Our near-term goal is to deploy a hybrid site energy management system to our
hardware-in-the-loop platforms and demonstrate some of the discussed core functions across various use cases.

Appendix A Applicable Standards
Charging hubs should comply with regulations and standards set by regulatory authorities and grid operators to
support the stability, reliability, and effciency of the power grid. These can include realiability standards, environ-
mental regulations, safety standards, market rules and tariffs, grid modernization initiatives and interconnection and
interoperability of DERs. Some of the important standards are summarized as follows:
 • IEEE 1547-2018 – Standard for Interconnection and Interoperability of Distributed Energy Resources with
 Associated Electric Power Systems Interfaces
 – Focus: Provides technical requirements for the interconnection of Distributed Energy Resources (DERs)
 to the electric grid. Covers topics such as voltage regulation, frequency control, power quality, and grid
 stability during normal and abnormal conditions.
 • IEEE 519-2022 – Standard for Harmonic Control in Electric Power Systems
 – Focus: Specifes limits for harmonic distortion in voltage and current in electric power systems to ensure
 the reliability and performance of the grid and connected equipment.
 • IEEE 2030-2011 – Guide for Smart Grid Interoperability of Energy Technology and Information Technology
 with the Electric Power System (EPS), End-Use Applications, and Loads
 – Focus: Provides guidelines for the integration and interoperability of energy technologies with the
 electric grid. It focuses on smart grid technology, enabling communication and control systems for grid
 modernization.
 • IEEE 2800-2022 – Standard for Interconnection and Interoperability of Inverter-Based Resources (IBRs) with
 Transmission and Sub-transmission Electric Power Systems
 – Focus: Establishes requirements for integrating inverter-based resources (such as solar PV, wind energy,
 and energy storage) into the transmission and sub-transmission networks. It addresses performance,
 protection, and control requirements.
 • IEEE 1547.1-2020 – Standard Conformance Test Procedures for Equipment Interconnecting Distributed
 Energy Resources with Electric Power Systems and Associated Interfaces
 – Focus: Provides detailed test procedures to confrm compliance with the IEEE 1547 standard for inter-
 connecting DERs. It covers tests for power quality, anti-islanding, voltage and frequency responses, and
 more.
 • IEEE 2030.7-2017 – Standard for the Specifcation of Microgrid Controllers
 – Focus: Defnes the functions and performance criteria for microgrid controllers, which manage the
 operation of microgrids and ensure safe, reliable, and optimized energy generation and distribution.
 • IEEE 1547.2-2008 – Application Guide for IEEE 1547 Standard for Interconnecting Distributed Resources
 with Electric Power Systems
 – Focus: Serves as a companion guide to IEEE 1547, providing application guidelines and practical con-
 siderations for the implementation of DERs into the grid.
