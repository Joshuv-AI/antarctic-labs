Study motivations and perceived academic and career returns among international students in clinical medicine education in China: an interview study
Authors: Cheng Yao, Zaihao Wu, Pingyang Xu, Diyue Zhong, Fang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChina has become a major destination for international medical students, supported in part by English-taught Bachelor of Medicine and Bachelor of Surgery (MBBS) programmes. Existing research has tended to examine study motivations and learning outcomes separately, providing limited insight into how motivations are formed and how perceptions of academic and career returns evolve over the extended course of medical training. This study examines the formation of international MBBS students’ motivations to study clinical medicine in China and explores changes in their perceptions of academic and career returns over time.MethodsA qualitative design was employed. Semi-structured interviews were conducted between June and November 2025 with 17 international medical students enrolled in MBBS programmes at two public universities in China. Participants were purposively sampled across different years of study and were mainly from Asian and African countries. Interviews were conducted in English or Chinese, audio-recorded, and transcribed verbatim. Data were analysed using thematic analysis supported by NVivo 12 software. The analysis was guided by push–pull theory, expectancy-value theory, and professional identity theory, with rigor enhanced through member checking and peer review.ResultsTwo overarching themes, four subthemes, and fifteen specific categories emerged. Students’ motivations reflected push factors in their home countries, including limited access to medical education, high costs, and perceived shortcomings in training quality, alongside pull factors related to China, such as curriculum structure, affordability, degree recognition, safety, and exposure to a high volume of clinical cases. Perceptions of academic and career returns changed over time. Some students perceived higher-than-expected academic and career returns, reflected in shifts in learning approaches, increased professional confidence, and stronger identification with the doctor role. Others perceived expected or lower-than-expected academic and career returns, mainly associated with limited hands-on clinical experience, language-related communication challenges, and uncertainty regarding future career pathways.ConclusionsMotivations to study clinical medicine in China were not static, and perceptions of academic and career returns evolved throughout training. Greater attention to students’ learning trajectories, including opportunities for clinical participation, language support, and career guidance, may enhance learning experiences and contribute to longer-term professional development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09756-w.Keywords: International medical education, Medical education in China, Study motivation, Academic and career returns, Push–pull theory, Expectancy-value theory

Introduction

According to UNESCO, the global population of internationally mobile higher education students has exceeded 6.9 million, with an increasing proportion enrolling in medicine and health-related disciplines [1]. China has emerged as one of the largest host countries for international medical students in Asia and is now among the leading global destinations for medical education [2]. Data from the Ministry of Education of the People’s Republic of China show approximately 500,000 international students were studying in China in 2018, representing a historical peak before the COVID-19 pandemic [3]. Although international student mobility was disrupted during the pandemic, it has recovered substantially in recent years, with the number of international students in China rebounding to approximately 380,000 by 2025 [4]. Among these students, medical-related programmes accounted for a significant proportion of enrolments. Since 2007, selected Chinese universities have systematically offered English-taught undergraduate programmes in clinical medicine, commonly referred to as the Bachelor of Medicine and Bachelor of Surgery (MBBS). The stability of this pathway is reflected in its nationally authorised institutional provision, relatively standardised six-year curriculum, English-medium instruction, structured progression from basic medical sciences to clinical training, and clearly defined requirement of at least one year of clinical internship. These features provide international students with a comparatively predictable route from admission to graduation and clinical exposure. Clinical training is generally completed in affiliated teaching hospitals in China, although, with prior approval, internships may also be undertaken in students’ home countries or in third countries [5].

By 2018, more than 50,000 international students were enrolled in MBBS programmes in China, with most originating from Asian and African countries and receiving instruction primarily in English [2]. In recent years, the scale of international medical education in China has remained relatively stable. During the 2023–2024 academic year, 44 universities nationwide offered English-taught MBBS programmes and enrolled a total of 3,048 international students, indicating consistency in both enrolment capacity and institutional provision [6]. Tongji Medical College illustrates this pattern: since beginning to admit international medical students in 2007, it trained 315 international physicians between 2012 and 2025. Students from emerging economies constitute a substantial share of this population; between 2013 and 2017, nearly 45,000 students from BRICS countries (Brazil, Russia, India, China, and South Africa) studied in China, with medicine among their primary fields of study [7].

Existing literature has largely examined why international students choose to study in China, with attention to educational, economic, and social contexts [8, 9]. Prior studies identify relatively low tuition and living costs, the availability of English-taught medical programmes, clear degree pathways, and perceived improvements in medical education quality as key attractions [2, 10, 11]. At the same time, conditions in students’ home countries, including limited medical education capacity, high tuition fees, and restricted admission opportunities, encourage outward mobility [12, 13]. Research also highlights sociocultural considerations, such as perceptions of social safety, cultural diversity, and future development prospects in China [14, 15]. Importantly, emerging evidence indicates that study motivations are not fixed but evolve with cultural adaptation, learning experiences, and changing expectations over time [16].

Motivation constitutes the starting point for the decision to study abroad, whereas academic and career returns provide the basis for students’ subsequent evaluation of that decision. Previous research indicates that studying medicine in China can enhance students’ professional knowledge, clinical skills, cross-cultural communication abilities, and self-confidence [17]. These learning gains constitute educational outcomes in the context of medical education, referring to the knowledge, skills, and competencies developed through the educational process [18]. In the broader literature, returns to education are commonly discussed as the value generated by educational investment [19]. In the present study, academic and career returns refer to students’ perceived value of these educational outcomes in relation to their initial expectations, educational investment, and future professional goals, including perceived gains in learning development, clinical preparation, professional confidence, and career readiness. These outcomes and students’ evaluations of their value are also shaped by challenges, including language barriers, differences in teaching approaches, and difficulties in cultural adaptation [20, 21]. Further evidence suggests that initial motivations shape students’ learning engagement and adaptation processes and interact dynamically with their perceptions of academic and career returns over time [22].

Existing research has largely examined study motivations and academic and career returns as separate constructs, with limited attention to their interaction over the course of the learning process. This limitation is particularly evident in medical education, which is characterised by lengthy training periods and substantial personal and institutional investment. How study motivations are adjusted over time and translated into perceived academic and career returns remains insufficiently explored from a process-oriented perspective. Moreover, much of the existing literature relies on cross-sectional surveys or descriptive designs, which provide limited insight into how students’ understandings of motivation and returns evolve across different stages of study [11].

These gaps require a framework that connects structural conditions, students’ value-based evaluations, and professional identity development. Push–pull theory is a widely used framework for explaining international student mobility and study-abroad decision-making [23]. It argues that students’ decisions result from the combined influence of “push” factors in their home countries and “pull” factors associated with host destinations, capturing the interaction between structural conditions and individual choice [24]. In the context of international MBBS education in China, this perspective helps explain how constraints in students’ home-country medical education systems and the perceived attractiveness of China’s medical education pathway jointly shape study motivations.

Expectancy-value theory further explains motivation through students’ expectations of success and the perceived value of the outcomes associated with a particular educational choice [25]. In this study, this theory is used to examine how students evaluate the relationship between educational investment, perceived feasibility, and anticipated academic and career returns, and how these evaluations change during the learning process. This perspective is particularly relevant to international medical education because students assess access to medical training alongside the feasibility, value, and future academic and professional usefulness of the chosen pathway.

Professional identity theory focuses on how individuals develop and internalise professional roles within educational and practice contexts and is frequently applied in medical education research. Medical training involves more than acquiring knowledge and technical skills; it also entails the gradual formation of the doctor role, professional values, and a sense of responsibility. This identity develops over time and is continuously reshaped through coursework, clinical exposure, and interactions with teachers and peers [26].

In this study, push–pull theory, expectancy-value theory, and professional identity theory are integrated into a process-oriented analytical framework to examine international medical students’ motivations for studying in China and their perceptions of academic and career returns. Push–pull theory is used to explain the formation of study motivations by emphasising structural constraints in students’ home countries and the perceived attractiveness of medical education in China. Expectancy-value theory guides the analysis of how students evaluate the value and feasibility of their educational choices in relation to anticipated academic and career returns. Professional identity theory is applied to examine how learning experiences are progressively translated into clinical confidence, professional responsibility, and identification with the doctor role through sustained coursework and clinical engagement. Together, these perspectives link motivation formation, value appraisal, and professional identity development, offering an integrated explanation of international medical students’ learning experiences within the Chinese medical education context (Fig. 1).

To address these gaps, this study adopts a qualitative approach and employs semi-structured interviews to explore how international students’ motivations for studying clinical medicine in China are formed and how their perceived academic and career returns evolve during MBBS training.

Method

Study design

This study adopted a descriptive qualitative research design and employed semi-structured interviews to explore international students’ motivations for studying in China and changes in their perceptions of academic and career returns within MBBS programmes. The study was reported in accordance with the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) guidelines [27]. The completed SRQR checklist is provided in Appendix A.

Participants

Purposeful sampling was employed to recruit participants in order to examine international students’ motivations for pursuing MBBS studies in China and their perceived academic and career returns. Eligible participants were international students enrolled in MBBS programmes at Chinese universities. The inclusion criteria were: (1) current enrolment in a clinical medicine programme; (2) completion of at least one semester of study in China; (3) ability to participate in interviews conducted in English or Chinese; and (4) provision of written informed consent. Students who had not formally enrolled in the programme or who declined audio recording were excluded.

In line with established recommendations on sample size in qualitative research [28], the study aimed to recruit between 15 and 20 participants. A total of 17 students completed the interviews. All participants were drawn from two public universities in China. University A is located in a prefecture-level city in northeastern China, while University B is located in a provincial capital city in central China. Both institutions have established medical schools, are authorised to enrol international students, and have affiliated teaching hospitals that support clinical training. Each institution admitted approximately 40 international medical students annually on average.

Purposeful sampling sought variation in year of study, country of origin, and gender, rather than equal representation across groups. The final sample included students from early, middle, and later stages of MBBS training, although later-year students were fewer, partly because senior students were more often engaged in clinical rotations or internship-related activities, which reduced their availability for interviews. The sample was also predominantly male, partly reflecting the gender composition of the accessible international MBBS cohorts at the participating universities. Participation was voluntary, and no eligible student was excluded on the basis of year of study or gender. The final sample included 15 male and 2 female participants.

Participants were initially contacted through staff in the universities’ international education offices or through course instructors, followed by formal interview invitations sent via WeChat. The research team had no teaching or administrative relationship with the participants, which helped minimise potential power imbalances.

Interview guide

The semi-structured interview guide was developed by the authors rather than directly adopted from an existing instrument. Its development was based on a systematic literature review, the theoretical framework adopted in this study, and prior research on international students’ motivations, learning experiences, and cross-cultural adaptation [26, 29]. The guide focused on the formation of study motivations, changes in learning engagement, adaptation experiences, and subjective evaluations of academic and career returns.

The guide consisted of four main sections: (1) background information and pre-enrolment expectations; (2) motivations for studying clinical medicine in China; (3) learning experiences and adaptation during MBBS training; and (4) perceived academic and career returns. Key open-ended questions included: “Can you describe how you decided to study clinical medicine in China?”, “What factors influenced your decision to study medicine abroad?”, “What made China an attractive option for you?”, “How have your learning experiences in China affected your motivation?”, and “In what ways has studying medicine in China met, exceeded, or fallen below your initial expectations?”

The interview guide was reviewed by three experts in qualitative research to ensure clarity and contextual relevance. It was then refined through two pilot interviews with international MBBS students. The pilot interviews were used to assess whether the questions were understandable, whether the order of questions was appropriate, and whether the guide allowed participants to provide detailed responses. Minor revisions were made to simplify wording, improve question order, and add prompts related to clinical learning, language communication, and career planning.

Data collection

Interviews and data collection were conducted between June and November 2025. Semi-structured interviews were used to examine participants’ motivations for undertaking MBBS studies in China and their perceptions of academic and career returns throughout the learning process. All interviews were conducted by members of the author team, both of whom held doctoral degrees in education, were proficient in both English and Chinese, and had experience conducting qualitative interviews. Before data collection, both interviewers received standardised training on the study aims, ethical procedures, use of the interview guide, probing techniques, audio-recording, and field-note writing.

All 17 participants completed two rounds of interviews, resulting in 34 interviews in total. The second round was conducted approximately one to two weeks after the first interview, after the research team had reviewed the first-round transcripts and field notes. The first round focused on participants’ initial motivations, pre-enrolment expectations, and general learning and career-related experiences. The second round was used to clarify unclear statements, expand brief responses, and further explore issues related to motivation formation and perceived academic and career returns, including home-country conditions, family expectations, educational costs, curriculum structure, clinical exposure, and whether students’ perceived returns had met, exceeded, or fallen below their initial expectations.

Interviews were conducted in English or Chinese according to participants’ preferences and took place in quiet meeting rooms on university campuses or, with participants’ consent, online via WeChat. Thirteen participants chose English and four chose Chinese. All participants selected the language in which they felt most comfortable and were able to complete coursework and routine academic communication in the selected language. The four participants who chose Chinese were not native Chinese speakers but had sufficient Chinese proficiency for academic communication. Before each interview, the interviewers confirmed that participants felt comfortable expressing their experiences in the selected language. When responses were unclear, follow-up questions were used during the interviews to confirm participants’ intended meanings.

Each interview lasted approximately 30–40 min. With participants’ consent, interviews were audio-recorded, and field notes were taken to document non-verbal behaviour, emotional responses, and relevant contextual features. Verbatim transcription was completed within 24 h of each interview. Transcripts were returned to participants for member checking to confirm that the content accurately reflected their intended meanings.

Data collection continued until no new motivation types or themes related to academic and career returns emerged and existing themes were no longer substantively developed, indicating thematic saturation. Saturation was reached with the seventeenth participant, which determined the final sample size. All participants were assigned anonymised identifiers (P1–P17). Audio recordings and transcripts were securely stored in encrypted formats accessible only to the research team. After each interview round, the research team conducted reflexive discussions to review the interview process and researchers’ positionality, thereby enhancing methodological rigour and consistency.

Data analysis

Interview data were analysed using theory-informed six-phase thematic analysis [30]. The analysis combined deductive and inductive elements: the three theories served as sensitising concepts, while the final themes and subthemes were developed inductively from the interview data rather than imposed in advance. All transcripts were imported into NVivo 12 for data management and coding. The researchers first read the transcripts repeatedly to achieve familiarity with the data and conducted initial coding of text segments related to study motivations and perceived academic and career returns. Through an iterative process of comparison and refinement, conceptually related codes were clustered into potential themes, which were subsequently reviewed and refined. Themes were retained as core themes only when they recurred across multiple participants and demonstrated internal coherence. Although the interview guide included open-ended questions about participants’ initial expectations and subsequent learning and career-related experiences, the categories of “Higher-than-expected returns” and “Lower-than-expected or expected returns” were developed analytically during thematic analysis rather than presented to participants as predefined options. Alongside recurrent patterns, divergent and disconfirming accounts were examined to refine the boundaries and conditions of each theme.

Data analysis proceeded concurrently with data collection, allowing emerging insights to inform subsequent interviews and guide further probing. To enhance analytic reliability, a second researcher independently coded a subset of transcripts and discussed coding decisions with the primary researcher until agreement was reached. Member checking and peer review were also employed to strengthen the credibility of the findings.

Rigor

This study ensured methodological rigour by addressing credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability [31]. Credibility was enhanced through member checking and peer review. Transferability was supported by providing detailed descriptions of the research context and participant characteristics. Dependability and auditability were strengthened through systematic documentation and management of the analytic process using NVivo 12. Confirmability was addressed through the use of reflexive journals and ongoing discussions within the research team, which helped minimise subjective bias and promote transparency in the interpretation of findings.

Ethics approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the ethics review committee of Xihua University (Approval Number: XH20250104-09). All participants provided written informed consent. Anonymity and confidentiality were ensured, and all data were securely stored and handled in accordance with institutional requirements.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 17 international medical students participated in the study. The sample was predominantly male (88.2%), and most participants were aged between 20 and 25 years (94.1%). Participants primarily came from India and Pakistan, with additional representation from Central Asia, Southeast Asia, and African countries. Students were enrolled across different years of study, with a higher proportion in the early and middle stages of training. Most participants had studied in China for 2–3 years. Interviews were conducted mainly in English, with a small number conducted in Chinese (see Table 1).

Thematic findings

The analysis identified two overarching themes, four subthemes, and fifteen more specific categories (see Table 2). Although the themes represent recurrent patterns across participants, the findings also attend to divergent voices that clarify how motivations and perceived returns varied across individual experiences.

Thematic Structure of Study Motivations and Perceived Academic and Career Returns

Theme 1. motivations for studying in China

Subtheme 1.1 push factors

Category 1.1.1 limited and unequal access to medical education at home

Participants described access to medical education in their home countries as both limited and unequal. Limited access was reflected in the small number of medical schools, restricted admission places, and intense competition among applicants. At the same time, some participants perceived admission processes as insufficiently transparent or not entirely merit-based, which further reduced their confidence in gaining entry through regular pathways. Together, these constraints encouraged students to consider studying medicine abroad, with China viewed as offering a more accessible route into medical education.

“The problem is that there are too few medical schools. Everyone wants to study medicine, but there simply aren’t enough places. China has dozens of times more medical schools than our country, so there are many more options.” (P5, Tajikistan).

“Sometimes it feels like the selection process isn’t the same for everyone. People with money can get into good medical schools without really going through proper selection.” (P14, India).

Category 1.1.2 financial barriers to medical training at home

The high cost and long duration of medical education reduced the feasibility of continuing medical studies in participants’ home countries. Participants reported that tuition fees, living expenses, and related costs accumulated over time, while limited access to scholarships or student loans placed the financial burden largely on families. For some students, this sustained economic pressure became a major barrier to continuing medical education, prompting them to seek training abroad. China’s comparatively lower educational costs were therefore perceived as a strong attraction.

“When I compared tuition fees over the past few years, I realised how fast they were increasing. The numbers were almost unimaginable for me.” (P10, Kazakhstan).

“Scholarships back home are mostly symbolic. Even if you get one, it doesn’t really solve the problem.” (P2, India).

Category 1.1.3 concerns about home-country medical training quality

Interview findings revealed widespread dissatisfaction with the quality of medical training in participants’ home countries. Participants reported outdated curricula, limited teaching approaches, insufficient instructional engagement, inadequate clinical practice opportunities, and scarce educational resources. Students from countries with constrained healthcare and education systems noted that these limitations persisted throughout training and hindered the systematic development of core medical competencies. In contrast, the perceived stability of training quality in China, along with structured curricula and clinical exposure, was a key factor in their decision to study there.

“We use textbooks that are more than ten years old, which really can’t keep up with current medical developments. New guidelines and technologies just aren’t covered.” (P16, Kazakhstan).

“I often talk to classmates back home, and they say teaching focuses mainly on common diseases. For more complex cases, like Crohn’s disease, their teachers have only seen them in textbooks, while my teachers in China have actually treated several cases.” (P7, Turkmenistan).

Category 1.1.4 social and family expectations

Most participants referred to the high social status, stable career prospects, and relative income security associated with the medical profession. In contexts where healthcare resources were limited and physicians were scarce, families often viewed medical training as a reliable career pathway. As a result, families prioritised students’ ability to continue medical education rather than the specific country of study, which made China an acceptable and supported option.

“My parents believe that being a doctor is hard at the beginning, but life becomes stable in the long run. When I decided to come to China, they fully supported me and even used family savings to help.” (P9, India).

“My parents don’t really care which country I study in. What matters to them is whether I can continue studying medicine. I’m the only son in my family, so a lot of responsibility will eventually fall on me.” (P12, Pakistan).

Subtheme 1.2 pull factors

Category 1.2.1 coherent curriculum structure and degree recognition

Nearly all participants expressed approval of the systematic and coherent curriculum structure, noting that it supported sustained medical learning. They highlighted the clear sequencing between basic medical sciences and clinical courses, which allowed learning across different stages to build cumulatively rather than remain fragmented. Some participants also emphasised degree recognition, placing importance on whether the curriculum met internationally accepted standards for medical education and structured training.

“The curriculum is logically organised, not just put together randomly. We study anatomy and physiology first, then move on to internal medicine and surgery. When we enter clinical training, many things connect.” (P17, Pakistan).

“During the school introduction, they mentioned accreditation by the World Federation for Medical Education (WFME). I don’t fully understand how it works, but knowing that the programme is internationally recognised is already very important to me.” (P1, Turkmenistan).

Category 1.2.2 affordable educational costs and scholarship support

Most participants identified the relatively affordable cost of education as an important factor in their decision. After comparing medical programmes across countries, they perceived medical education in China as more financially feasible than in their home countries or other common destinations, considering tuition fees, living expenses, and overall investment. In addition, scholarships provided by the Chinese government and local authorities offered tangible support through tuition reductions and basic living allowances, which helped alleviate financial pressure during long-term study.

“I seriously considered medical programmes in the UK, but the total cost was extremely high……so high that it would have required selling many cows to afford it. In the end, studying medicine in China seemed more affordable and more sustainable.” (P3, Pakistan).

“Scholarships are mainly based on academic performance and effort, not family background or complicated recommendations. I followed the process and successfully obtained one.” (P15, India).

Category 1.2.3 perceived safety

Most participants perceived the relatively stable social and campus environment as providing a consistent sense of safety in daily life and academic settings. Female participants, in particular, noted that this sense of safety reduced concerns about personal security and allowed them to focus more fully on their medical studies.

“I feel I have the most say on this……I think China is the safest country I’ve ever lived in. Even after evening classes, it’s usually fine to walk back alone.” (P13, Thailand).

“My parents initially had some concerns about safety in China, but in reality it’s very safe, and over time they became reassured.” (P8, Nigeria).

Category 1.2.4 high patient volume and structured healthcare system

Some participants highlighted the large scale of medical institutions in China, the high patient volume, and the diversity of clinical cases. They described China’s healthcare system as relatively standardised and mature in terms of clinical workflows, departmental organisation, and the use of medical technologies. This clinical environment was viewed as providing a strong foundation for medical training.

“There are really many patients here. Almost every day you can see different types of cases.” (P6, India).

“I noticed that the diagnostic procedures and departmental organisation are quite structured, which helps me understand how clinical work operates.” (P11, India).

Category 1.2.5 english-medium instruction

A small number of participants noted that Chinese universities offered English-taught medical programmes, which reduced the initial language barrier to entry and facilitated early academic adaptation. However, this pattern was not universal. For P4, English-medium instruction was not a pre-existing attraction because he only learned about this option through senior students after initially assuming that Chinese proficiency would be required. His account therefore qualifies the theme by showing that language of instruction could operate not only as a direct pull factor, but also as an information-mediated factor:

“To be honest, I didn’t know at first that China had English-taught medical programmes. I thought I would need to speak Chinese before coming. I only learned about it later through senior students.” (P4, Pakistan).

Theme 2. changes in expectations of returns

Subtheme 2.1 higher-than-expected returns

Category 2.1.1 changes in learning approaches

Some participants reported that, prior to studying in China, their learning goals were largely oriented toward course completion or passing examinations. During the learning process, they gradually developed a more integrated understanding of medical knowledge and began to recognise connections across different stages of training. This shift was not anticipated at the outset but emerged through sustained engagement with the curriculum.

“Before, studying was mainly about getting through exams. Later on, I realised that what we learn at different stages is connected and needs to be understood together.” (P7, Turkmenistan).

Category 2.1.2 professional identity and confidence

Several participants described a shift from passive observation to more active engagement with clinical reasoning, accompanied by an increasing tendency to relate their learning experiences to their future professional roles as doctors.

“At the beginning, studying medicine was mostly about meeting my parents’ expectations, and I wasn’t very interested. After starting anatomy, I began to genuinely develop an interest in medicine and became more willing to actively understand what I was learning.” (P12, Pakistan).

Category 2.1.3 learning-related personal growth

Some participants reported personal development closely associated with medical training. Under the demands of intensive coursework and clinical exposure, they described increased independence, a stronger sense of responsibility, and improved self-management skills, which enabled them to cope more effectively with academic requirements.

“The pace of medical study is quite intense, and many things have to be planned and handled independently.” (P2, India).

Subtheme 2.2 lower-than-expected or expected returns

Category 2.2.2 language and communication barriers

Although participants were able to complete academic coursework in English, opportunities for direct communication with patients were limited. Several participants noted that language differences led patients to prefer communicating with supervising physicians rather than international students, which reduced opportunities to practise history-taking and patient interaction in clinical settings.

“Some patients are curious and find it interesting to see foreign medical students, but during actual consultations, they usually don’t communicate with us in depth.” (P10, Kazakhstan).

Category 2.2.3 academic and career uncertainty

A small number of participants expressed uncertainty about their future trajectories, noting that plans for further training, licensure conversion, and long-term career pathways remained unclear.

“What I’m learning now is useful, but I still can’t clearly say how far it will take me in the future.” (P8, Nigeria).

Category 2.2.1 limited participation in clinical practice

Although participants were able to access clinical settings and observe real cases, learning often remained observational, with limited opportunities for direct involvement in clinical procedures. As a result, some participants felt that the returns from clinical training did not fully meet their initial expectations.

“After entering the hospital, most of the time we just observe what the supervising doctors do. There aren’t many chances to actually perform tasks ourselves.” (P13, Thailand).

Discussion

This study explored how international MBBS students’ motivations for studying clinical medicine in China were formed and how their perceived academic and career returns evolved during training. The findings show that students’ decisions were shaped by both constraints in their home-country medical education systems and the perceived advantages of China’s MBBS pathway. Limited and unequal access to medical education, financial barriers, and concerns about training quality acted as push factors, while affordability, curriculum structure, degree recognition, safety, and clinical exposure served as key pull factors. Perceived academic and career returns also changed over time through coursework, clinical exposure, language-mediated communication, and professional identity development. These findings extend previous quantitative studies on MBBS students’ motivations in China by showing how motivations are shaped by specific educational contexts and how students reassess the value of their educational choices over time [11].

The findings are consistent with the explanatory logic of push–pull theory. Constraints in students’ home-country medical education systems interacted with the perceived advantages of China’s MBBS pathway to shape study motivation. These constraints functioned as key push factors, while the attractiveness of medical education in China, particularly in terms of educational cost, curriculum structure, and learning environment, served as central pull factors [23, 24]. At the same time, students’ perceptions of academic and career returns evolved throughout the learning process. Rather than representing fixed outcomes, students’ perceived returns appeared to develop unevenly through their accumulated learning and clinical experiences.

These findings extend previous understandings of international medical students’ motivation by showing how push and pull factors operate in relation to one another. The prominence of limited access to medical education and intense admission competition suggests that structural constraints within home-country medical education systems strongly shaped students’ mobility decisions, consistent with prior research indicating that insufficient medical education capacity drives cross-border mobility [32]. Financial pressure, including high educational costs, the long duration of medical training, and limited scholarship or public funding, directly shaped students’ evaluation of whether studying medicine abroad was feasible and worthwhile [33]. Beyond a simple shortage of places, participants also emphasised perceived unfairness and lack of transparency in admission processes, suggesting an additional dimension of push factors. Moreover, concerns about the quality of medical education in home countries, particularly outdated curricula and limited clinical practice opportunities, emerged as salient issues, extending beyond the language or teaching adaptation concerns highlighted in some previous studies [34].

In relation to pull factors, the study confirms previous evidence while also clarifying why particular features of Chinese MBBS programmes were meaningful to students. Previous studies indicate that China’s attractiveness to international medical students is closely linked to relatively affordable educational costs, a comparatively developed healthcare system, and a safe and stable social environment, which aligns with participants’ accounts in this study. The emphasis on systematic and coherent curricula suggests that students assessed educational value through programme structure and long-term professional usability [2]. Clear alignment between basic sciences and clinical training enhanced their confidence in sustaining long-term medical study. This study further shows that alignment with international medical education standards and accreditation by organisations such as the WFME are viewed as key indicators of educational value and degree recognition. By contrast, no participant identified institutional ranking as a primary consideration. This finding differs from some previous studiesand suggests that, for international medical students, curricular compliance and degree recognition may carry greater practical significance than institutional prestige [35, 36].

As tuition fees and living costs continue to rise in traditional destinations for medical education, such as the United States and the United Kingdom, the participants’ accounts suggest that China’s relatively affordable tuition fees, living expenses, and scholarship support made MBBS study appear financially feasible [37]. In addition, a clinical environment characterised by high patient volume and relatively standardised diagnostic and treatment procedures was perceived as supporting understanding of routine clinical practice. With respect to the language of instruction, English-taught programmes appeared to reduce initial barriers to entry, while gradual exposure to medical Chinese facilitated students’ understanding of clinical contexts. Social and campus safety also emerged as an important factor, particularly for female students and their families, which is consistent with previous research [2, 38].

The motivations of international medical students studying in China are formed and stabilised through the interaction between individual educational experiences and structural conditions within global medical education. Interview data indicate that students do not migrate passively in response to constraints in their home-country education systems. Instead, they actively reassess and reposition their choices within an increasingly globalised medical education landscape. This pattern is consistent with structural migration processes, in which emerging destinations reshape mobility through institutional arrangements [39]. Motivation formation is further shaped by educational socialisation. Participants consistently valued structured curricula and professional identity development, which aligns with findings on professional identity formation in medical education and highlights the combined influence of educational structures and sociocultural contexts [40]. Within this process, learner autonomy and family support acted as moderating factors that enabled students to pursue sustainable educational pathways under practical constraints, consistent with the contextualised motivation model [41]. Emotional tensions associated with professional identity construction were also evident in participants’ experiences of cross-cultural adjustment and fluctuations in clinical confidence [42]. Taken together, this interpretation suggests that motivation formation should be understood as a gradual and relational process rather than as a single point-of-entry decision.

The second major finding concerns the changing nature of students’ perceived academic and career returns during MBBS training. From the perspective of expectancy-value theory, the changing nature of perceived returns highlights the importance of examining how students reassess the relationship between educational effort, learning outcomes, and future professional goals. This perspective explains why students’ evaluations of academic and career returns changed over time, as they compared their initial expectations and educational investment with the learning, clinical, and career-related value they perceived during training. Professional identity theory further explains why these evaluations became more positive when learning experiences strengthened clinical confidence, professional responsibility, and identification with the doctor role. Professional identity develops through coursework, clinical experience, and engagement with the learning environment [26]. These students moved from exam-oriented learning toward a more integrated understanding of medicine and developed clearer perceptions of their future roles as doctors, which is consistent with research showing that study-abroad experiences can support professional identity formation [43]. However, this pattern was not universal. Limited opportunities for hands-on clinical practice constrained students’ ability to translate academic learning into active clinical competence and professional confidence, echoing previous findings on constrained clinical exposure in medical training [44–46].

In some cases, stronger professional confidence encouraged more active engagement in clinical reasoning, supporting the argument that medical education can promote professional identity development [47]. When perceived returns exceeded initial expectations, students reported stronger learning motivation and professional confidence, which encouraged greater academic engagement and clearer career planning. This pattern aligns with expectancy-value theory, which suggests that consistency between expectations and outcomes reinforces motivation. Uncertainty about future career pathways reflects the difficulty of converting an international medical degree into clear licensure, postgraduate training, or employment opportunities, while also illustrating the indeterminacy inherent in medical identity formation [48].

From the perspective of expectancy-value theory, these unmet expectations may have reduced students’ perceived value of clinical learning and increased the perceived cost of continued engagement, thereby weakening motivation and confidence [25, 49]. Together, these findings show a connected process: push–pull theory explains the formation of study motivation, expectancy-value theory explains students’ evaluation of educational value, and professional identity theory explains how learning experiences are translated into confidence and identification with the doctor role.

Contributions and implications

This study advances understanding of international medical students’ motivations for studying in China and their perceptions of academic and career returns by addressing key gaps in the existing literature. The findings show that students’ decisions to study in China were shaped by both difficulties in their home countries and the advantages of China’s MBBS programmes. Limited access to medical education, high costs, and perceived unfairness in admission pushed some students to seek medical training abroad. At the same time, China’s relatively affordable tuition, structured curriculum, degree recognition, safe environment, and clinical exposure made it an attractive choice.

These findings have practical implications for both students’ home countries and China. For students’ home countries, the results suggest the need to expand medical school capacity, improve fairness and transparency in admission, and provide stronger financial support through scholarships or student loans. For Chinese medical universities, the findings suggest that affordability alone is not enough. Universities should continue to improve curriculum quality, provide more supervised clinical participation, strengthen language support for patient communication, and offer clearer guidance on degree recognition, licensure, postgraduate training, and career pathways.

Limitations and future research

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study did not fully capture the long-term dynamics of motivation and expectations of returns. As motivations and perceptions may continue to evolve with extended learning experiences and personal development, interviews conducted at a single time point may not adequately reflect longitudinal change. Second, the sample composition showed imbalances in year of study and gender. The relatively small number of later-year students may have limited the depth of understanding of how perceptions evolve during the later stages of MBBS training, while the inclusion of only two female students may have constrained the representation of female international medical students’ experiences and expectations. Although participants selected the interview language in which they felt most comfortable, most were not native speakers of English or Chinese. Therefore, some nuances of meaning may have been affected by language proficiency. Third, the sample consisted mainly of students from Asia and Africa, which is closely related to China’s education funding policies under the Belt and Road Initiative and may limit the transferability of the findings to students from other regions. Fourth, the study was conducted at only two public universities. Private institutions offering MBBS programmes were not included, and these programmes have not yet obtained WFME accreditation, which may influence differences in students’ motivations and expectations of returns between public and private institutions. Future research could adopt longitudinal designs, purposively recruit more later-year and female participants, and expand the sample to include a broader range of institutions, regions, and disciplines to further examine variation in motivations and expectations of returns across different educational contexts.

Conclusions

Through interviews with international medical students enrolled in MBBS programmes in China, this study examined the formation of study motivations and changes in perceptions of academic and career returns. The findings indicate that students’ motivations are shaped by the interaction between medical education conditions in their home countries and the institutional context of medical education in China, and are continuously adjusted through coursework and clinical exposure. Perceived academic and career returns followed different trajectories over time: some students developed a more integrated understanding of medicine and stronger professional confidence, while others encountered practical challenges related to clinical participation, language communication, and career planning.

Overall, this study underscores the importance of conceptualising international medical students’ learning experiences as an evolving process, addressing limitations in prior research that has treated study motivations and academic and career returns as static constructs. The findings provide empirical insight into student experiences in cross-border medical education and offer practical implications for improving curriculum design, clinical training arrangements, and student support mechanisms.
