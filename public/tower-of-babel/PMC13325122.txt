DFT Studies of the Effects of Trivalent Cations (B, Al, Ga) on Au(I)-Exchanged Zeolites for Catalytic CO2 Cycloaddition with Light Epoxides: The Interplay between Catalyst Acidity and Epoxide Reactivity
Authors: Winyoo Sangthong, Jakkapan Sirijaraensre
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this work, the reaction between CO2 and light epoxides (EO, PO, and IBO) over isomorphously substituted Au-[M]-ZSM-5 and Au-[M]-MCM-22 (M = B, Al, and Ga) was investigated using DFT at the M06-L/6-31G­(d,p) level. Isomorphous substitutions strongly affect catalyst acidity of the Au­(I) cation. The acidity increases in the order [B]- < [Al]- < [Ga]-zeolites. For all substitutions, MCM-22 consistently exhibits higher acidity than ZSM-5, resulting in stronger adsorption energies of epoxides on the Au­(I) cation. The reaction proceeds via a single-step mechanism in which Au promotes epoxide ring-opening and C–O bond formation with CO2. Methyl electron-donating groups increased the nucleophilicity of the epoxide oxygen, resulting in stronger interaction between the epoxide and the Lewis active site. Consequently, the activation free energy decreases in the order EO > PO > IBO. For the IBO system, the substituent effect outweighs variations in the Au-site acidity across different zeolites. Although the Au­(I) cations in the two zeolites exhibit different acidities, the activation free energies for IBO/CO2 conversion are nearly identical. The SISSO algorithm was applied using the molecular orbital (MO) energies of the epoxides together with their adsorption free energies to derive a mathematical model for predicting the activation free energy of CO2 cycloaddition with light epoxides over the selected zeolites. A SISSO-based model reveals the utilization of epoxide MO energies and adsorption energies for accurately predicting activation free energies for this reaction. The SISSO-based equation shows excellent agreement with DFT results (R 2 = 1.00, RMSE = 0.8 kcal mol–1).

Introduction

In recent decades, the significant increase in the utilization of fossil fuels and related activities has led to a continuous increase in greenhouse gases in the atmosphere. These gases play a key role in trapping heat in the Earth’s atmosphere, leading to the occurrence of extreme climate events such as recurring floods, landslides, melting glaciers, tsunamis, powerful storms, and the continual rise of sea levels. These trigger global efforts to find effective strategies to reduce the amount of greenhouse gases in the atmosphere, especially carbon dioxide (CO2). A number of novel technologies are being developed to turn this environmental threat into a more-valued product as a renewable C1 building block for chemical synthesis. − The use of more reactive agents as epoxides in the conversion of CO2 into more useful chemicals is one of the most important ways for using CO2 as a raw material for chemical industries. This chemical process is proposed to be an effective way for cyclic carbonate production. These cyclic carbonates serve extensively as sustainable polar aprotic solvents, value-added fuel components, electrolytes for lithium-ion batteries, and important precursors in polymerization reactions and pharmaceutical applications. However, this transformation depends on catalytic systems capable of facilitating epoxide ring-opening to enable CO2 incorporation. Therefore, identifying highly efficient catalysts is essential, as they have a direct impact on both the energy requirements of the reaction and the overall production cost.

Over the past decade, homogeneous catalysts including organocatalysts, ionic liquids, metal salts, metal salen complexes, , and metal nonsalen complexes, , have demonstrated outstanding catalytic performance in the cycloaddition of CO2 with epoxides. Despite their high catalytic efficiency, homogeneous catalysts face substantial limitations related to catalyst regeneration and product separation, which significantly hinder their scalability and practical implementation in industrial applications. To overcome their limitations, heterogeneous catalysts have been employed to promote the conversion of CO2 and epoxides to the corresponding cyclic carbonates. However, heterogeneous catalysts, such as metal oxides, zeolites, − carbon-based materials, , and metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), , generally exhibit lower catalytic activity than their homogeneous counterparts, primarily due to limitations associated with reactant diffusion. Therefore, efforts have been devoted to improving the catalytic activities of these catalysts. , Among heterogeneous catalysts, zeolites possess crystalline structures with well-defined pore sizes and shapes that enable selective catalysis toward specific molecules. Moreover, their environmental sustainability, high selectivity, and low cost make zeolites particularly attractive candidates for large-scale industrial applications. The structural diversity of zeolite frameworks, combined with their tunable cation species that function as acid sites, makes them suitable for a broad range of applications, including ion-exchange and porous materials used in sorption, separation, and purification processes, as well as in catalysis.

ZSM-5 is one of the most famous and industrially important synthetic zeolites because of its shape selectivity, high thermal stability, and adjustable acidity. Its structure comprises a three-dimensional, medium-pore MFI framework made of intersecting straight and sinusoidal channels, which limit access to larger molecules. In contrast, MCM-22 has a unique layered MWW structure containing both internal sinusoidal 10-membered ring pores and external supercages accessed through pocket-like openings. These external pocket structures expose more accessible acid sites on the surface, allowing larger reactants to interact more easily. Isomorphic substitution of zeolites with trivalent cations is a key strategy in catalysis because it allows for the precise control of active sites and acidity, tailoring the material for specific reactions. Various isomorphous substituted ZSM-5 and MCM-22 catalysts where the Si atom in the framework has been replaced with B, Al, Ga, and Fe have been synthesized and used to change the acid strength and distribution of Lewis and Bro̷nsted acid sites in zeolites in a range of reactions. − The acidity and catalytic activities of zeolites also could be tuned by exchanging the Bro̷nsted acid with metal cations. Various metal cations have been successfully incorporated into zeolite frameworks to serve as active sites for catalytic reactions. Among these metal-exchanged zeolites, monovalent Au­(I) cations anchored within the framework have received considerable attention due to their unique catalytic behavior. The presence of Au­(I) species inside the zeolite pores has been verified by CO adsorption measurements. − Notably, Au­(I)/NaY catalysts demonstrate high selectivity for the reduction of NO by H2 to N2 at 693 K. It was reported that gold nanoparticles promote oxidative properties of copper species in Cu/Au-MCM-22 zeolite and play a crucial role in the enhancement of oxidation of methanol and CO.

Theoretical investigations play a vital role in elucidating the nature of catalytic active sites and the intermediates formed during catalytic processes. These computational insights provide valuable guidance for the rational design and optimization of catalytic systems. Our previous theoretical studies demonstrated the catalytic performance of Au-exchanged zeolites for alkane activation and the subsequent reaction of CO2 with the preactivated intermediates on Au-ZSM-5 and Au-MCM-22 zeolites to form carboxylic acids. These studies further revealed that the rate-determining step is the insertion of CO2 into the activated alkane intermediates. , In a subsequent study, we investigated the cycloaddition of CO2 with ethylene oxide (EO) over Au­(I)-exchanged zeolites and found that, in channel-type zeolites such as BEA and ZSM-5, Au­(I) adopts a bidentate coordination environment. The enhanced host–guest complementarity within the ZSM-5 framework further improves the catalytic performance by stabilizing the transition state of the CO2 cycloaddition reaction. Overall, the catalytic activity of Au­(I)-exchanged zeolites follows the order ZSM-5 > BEA > FAU. In this study, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were employed in this work to investigate and compare the catalytic performance of Au-ZSM-5 and Au-MCM-22 in the CO2–epoxide cycloaddition reaction. The effects of isomorphous substitution of the tetrahedral framework with trivalent atoms (B, Al, and Ga) on the catalytic activity were systematically investigated. To elucidate the role of electron-donating groups, three epoxides bearing mono- and dimethyl substitutions on the epoxide ringethylene oxide (EO), propylene oxide (PO), and isobutylene oxide (IBO)were selected as model substrates for this study. The adsorption characteristics of the reactant species, structures of the reaction complexes, and relative energies are examined in detail to elucidate the roles of zeolite frameworks, isomorphously substituted trivalent atoms, and epoxide types on their catalytic activities on CO2 conversion via cycloaddition with various epoxides. While previous studies reported the feasibility of CO2/EO conversion in zeolites, , a comprehensive understanding of the interplay between the electronic effects of epoxides and framework acidity governed by trivalent cations remain unclear. From these DFT results, a machine-learning model was trained to construct a model for predicting the activation free energies of CO2/epoxides on the Au-ZSM-5 and Au-MCM-22 zeolites. The correlation derived from DFT and ML highlights the involved interplay between epoxide reactivity and zeolite acidity during the conversion of CO2 into its corresponding cyclic carbonate. This finding provides a quantitative basis for selecting the optimal zeolite framework based on the electronic properties of the target epoxide.

Models and Methods

DFT calculations were employed to investigate CO2 cycloaddition with various epoxides (EO, PO, and IBO) catalyzed by two different channel-type Au­(I)-exchanged zeolites. Due to the large unit cell of MFI and MCM-22 zeolites, the use of a periodic boundary condition (PBC) model for studying chemical reactions inside the pore of these zeolites is computationally expensive. Consequently, the cluster model approach, which accurately represents the local environment of the active site, serves as a more efficient and practical framework for investigating these reaction mechanisms. The 34T and 38T cluster models that mimic the catalytic center of ZSM-5 and MCM-22 zeolites are illustrated in Figure a,b, respectively. The coordinates of the 34T cluster model of ZSM-5 covering the intersecting region connecting the straight and zigzag channels were determined from the experiment crystal structure. This model is sufficiently large to determine the confinement effects of the zeolite framework as reported in previous studies. ,, For the MCM-22 zeolite, the 38T cluster model, which covers the 12-membered ring at the intersection of two channels, is an appropriate active site of the catalyst and used in this study. The lattice coordinates of the model were obtained from its crystallographic structure. A Si atom at the T12 position of ZSM-5 and the T1 site of MCM-22 are substituted by different trivalent heteroatoms (B, Al, or Ga) to model the Lewis acid site. The Au­(I) cation was placed at the most accessible position to generate the active site of zeolites.

DFT cluster models of Au-exchanged [M]-zeolites (M = B, Al, and Ga): (a) 34T cluster of ZSM-5 and (b) 38T cluster of MCM-22.

DFT calculations were performed with the M06-L functional, which can provide reasonable results for the interactions inside the pores of zeolites. , The 6-31G­(d,p) basis set for nonmetal atoms and the LANL2DZ ECP basis set for the Au atom were employed to determine the optimized structures at the adsorption and transition-state steps along the catalytic process. During geometry optimizations, only the 5T active region and the adsorbates were allowed to relax while the rest of the structure was fixed at their crystallographic coordinates. The spin multiplicity of all systems was considered to be singlet, in which Au­(I) is in its ground state electronic structure. The frequency analysis was performed to ensure that each obtained transition state has exactly one imaginary frequency in which the displacement vectors of all transition state structures correspond to the bond-forming and bond-breaking processes for the cycloaddition. All calculations are performed with the Gaussian 16 package.

Results and Discussion

Structural and Catalytic Activity of Au-[M]-ZSM-5 and Au-[M]-MCM-22 (M = B, Al, and Ga) on CO2 Cycloaddition with EO

In this work, the catalytic activity of gold-exchanged zeolites is investigated for CO2 cycloaddition with light epoxides. The 34T and 38T cluster models were used as the catalytic centers of the Au-[M]-ZSM-5 and Au-[M]-MCM-22 zeolites (M = B, Al, and Ga), respectively. EO, PO, and IBO were selected to investigate the effect of methyl-donating groups on the reaction process. The selected geometrical parameters of the complexes related to the CO2 cycloaddition with EO, PO, and IBO over Au-[B]-, Au-[Al]-, and Au-[Ga]-zeolite models and their Mulliken charges are documented in Tables S1–S3 for EO, Tables S4–S6 for PO, and Tables S7–S9 for IBO. Their adsorption, activation, and product desorption free energies are presented in Table S10.

For bare zeolite systems, the Au­(I) cation is asymmetrically coordinated with two oxygen atoms proximalto the isomorphously substituted siteof the zeolite framework. For Au-[Al]-ZSM-5, the calculated Au–O bond distances of 2.297 and 2.354 Å are in good agreement with previous reports. Similar distances are found for Au-[Ga]-ZSM-5, while a different local coordination of Au is found in Au-[B]-ZSM-5. In Au-[B]-ZSM-5, Au cation is located over the six-membered ring in the intersection channel of the zeolite structure. Because B3+ is smaller than Al3+ and Ga3+, the B–O bonds are significantly shorter than the Al–O and Ga–O bonds, leading to a more compressed local ion-exchange site. Furthermore, the oxygen atoms adjacent to B3+ ion possess less negative charge than those adjacent to Al and Ga. These make Au­(I) to be located adjacent to the six-membered ring of ZSM-5 instead of above the local ion-exchange site as found in the case of monovalent cations on [Al]-ZSM-5 zeolites. , The calculated Au–Oa and Au–Ob distances are 2.771 and 3.663 Å, respectively. The Mulliken charge on Au follows the order Au-[B]-ZSM-5 (+0.198e) < Au-[Al]-ZSM-5 (+0.322e) < Au-[Ga]-ZSM-5 (+0.391e). Population analysis reveals that the framework-substituted atoms (B, Al, and Ga) influence the electron density of the Au­(I) cation. Ga substitution in the ZSM-5 framework can preserve the cationic character of Au­(I), suggesting higher acidity of the Au­(I) ion than that observed in other isomorphously substituted ZSM-5 zeolites. Changing the zeolite framework from ZSM-5 to MCM-22 topology, the atomic Au­(I) charge on the substituted MCM-22 zeolites follows the same trend as that in the case of ZSM-5 zeolites. Interestingly, it was found that the atomic charge of the Au­(I) cation on the MCM-22 zeolites is more positive than that of the corresponding substituted ZSM-5 zeolites. The calculated Mulliken charges on Au are +0.427e, +0.469e, and +0.500e for Au-[B]-MCM-22, Au-[Al]-MCM-22, and Au-[Ga]-MCM-22, respectively. To visualize and quantify the acidity of the Au cation in these systems, an electrostatic potential (ESP) analysis was performed. As illustrated in Figure S1, the positive electrostatic potential surrounding the Au center becomes significantly more intense (indicated by the deep blue regions) upon the substitution of Al or Ga into the zeolite framework. These ESP plots confirm the relatively low electrophilic nature of the Au­(I) cation in B-substituted zeolites compared to their Al- and Ga-substituted zeolites. The calculated results show that the acidity of the Au­(I) cation is strongly influenced not only by the local electrostatic field created by the zeolite topology but also by the basicity of the zeolite framework, which is governed by the nature of the trivalent substituent within the zeolite cavity. Isomorphous substitution of Si by B, Al, or Ga in the zeolite framework tailors the acidity and catalytic properties by modifying the framework charge and acid site strength.

The more positive Au­(I) charge of MCM-22 compared to that of ZSM-5 could be explained by the optimization of zeolite frameworks without Au­(I). The HOMO energy of the substituted zeolites follows the trend [B]-zeolites < [Al]-zeolites < [Ga]-zeolites (cf. Table S11). A more negative HOMO energy indicates a reduced electron-donating ability of the zeolite framework, leading to weaker Lewis basicity of the framework oxygen atoms. Consequently, the compensating +1 cations exhibit reduced electron transfer from the zeolite framework, resulting in enhanced Lewis acidity. Furthermore, the local structure of the MCM-22 zeolite leads to a more unbalanced interaction between the Au+ cation and the framework oxygen atoms compared to that in ZSM-5. This reduces the extent of electron transfer from the oxygen atoms to the Au+ cation. As a result, the Au­(I) cation in MCM-22 becomes a more positive atomic charge than in the corresponding isomorphously substituted ZSM-5 zeolite. These results imply that the Au­(I) cation in MCM-22 possesses stronger Lewis acidity than that in ZSM-5. As reported previously, the Lewis acidity of metal cations plays a crucial role in the activation of the epoxide C–O bond for coupling with CO2 to form cyclic carbonates. Therefore, the effects of isomorphous substitution in different zeolite topologies are further investigated in the following section.

In the absence of a cocatalyst to promote the epoxide ring-opening, the cycloaddition is proposed to proceed through a concerted mechanism. In this pathway, the adsorbed epoxide is activated simultaneously with a nucleophilic attack by CO2 to produce the corresponding cyclic carbonate (cf. Scheme ). −

Proposed Reaction Mechanism for CO2 Cycloaddition with Epoxides (EO, PO, and IBO) on the Au-[M]-Zeolite via the Concerted Pathway

The catalytic performance of these Au­(I)-exchanged zeolites in the cycloaddition of CO2 with EO was investigated and compared with our previous computational results. The catalytic reaction proceeds through the concerted activation of the adsorbed ethylene oxide (EO_ADS) and CO2 molecule, leading to the formation of ethylene carbonate (EC). The optimized structures of Au-[M]-ZSM-5 and Au-[M]-MCM-22 (M = B, Al, and Ga) and selected geometrical parameters of the complexes related to the CO2 cycloaddition with EO and their Mulliken charges are illustrated and documented in Figure and Tables S1–S3 for Au-[B]-, Au-[Al]-, and Au-[Ga]-zeolite models, respectively. First, the EO molecule coordinates to the Au center of the zeolites through its oxygen atom. In the ZSM-5 system, the adsorption free energies between EO and the Au cation are −19.1, −19.5, and −20.9 kcal mol–1 for Au-[B]-, Au-[Al]-, and Au-[Ga]-ZSM-5, respectively, with a corresponding Au–O1 distance of approximately 2.16 Å (Figure a). The total charges on the adsorbed EO are +0.140e, +0.145e, and +0.144e, respectively. Owing to the higher positive charge of the Au cation in the Au-MCM-22 systems, the total charges on the adsorbed EO are in the range of +0.171e to +0.173e. The corresponding adsorption free energies are −22.3, −23.9, and −25.8 kcal mol–1 for Au-[B]-, Au-[Al]-, and Au-[Ga]-MCM-22 zeolites, respectively. The Au–O1 distances are shorter than those of ZSM-5 systems. In addition, the C2–O1 bonds of EO are slightly more elongated in the MCM-22 systems than in the ZSM-5 systems, consistent with the greater extent of electron transfer from EO to the Au cation (Figure d). The higher adsorption energy is dominated by enhanced electrostatic interaction, which is mainly due to the more positively charged Au­(I) cation in MCM-22 than in ZSM-5 zeolites. For CO2 adsorption, the molecule binds to the Au center of the zeolite through its oxygen lone-pair electrons. The C–O bond distance of CO2 is elongated by approximately 0.02 Å upon the adsorption. The calculated adsorption free energies range from −3.6 to −7.1 kcal mol–1 for both Au-ZSM-5 and Au-MCM-22 systems. These values are comparable to the reported CO2 adsorption energies of approximately −4.8 and −6.7 kcal mol–1 on Cu­(I)-FAU and Ag­(I)-FAU, respectively, as reported by Hessou et al. It is evident that the EO molecule interacts with the Au centers of the zeolites approximately three to five times more strongly than CO2. Consequently, the presence of CO2 in the catalytic system does not significantly influence the initial adsorption of EO on the metal center, which constitutes the first step of the CO2 cycloaddition reaction.

Optimized structures of the complexes for the concerted mechanism of the cycloaddition of EO with CO2 on Au-[M]-ZSM-5 and Au-[M]-MCM-22 (M = B, Al, and Ga): (a–d) EO adsorption complex (EO_ADS), (b, e) transition state (TS), and (c, f) product adsorption complex (PROD).

At the transition state, two new covalent C–O bonds are formed between EO and CO2 via a five-membered ring transition structure, which is analogous to the reaction mechanism proposed for CO2 cycloaddition with epoxides over Lewis acidic catalytic sites in the absence of a cocatalyst. − Figure presents the optimized structures of the transition states (TS) and products (PROD) for the CO2 cycloaddition reaction with EO over Au-ZSM-5 (Figure b,c) and Au-MCM-22 zeolites (Figure e,f). In Au-ZSM-5 systems, the transition state has a single imaginary frequency with values of 340.9i, 325.4i, and 329.1i cm–1, demonstrating the formation of two new C–O bonds between CO2 and the ring-opened EO fragments concurrently on Au-[B]-, Au-[Al]-, and Au-[Ga]-ZSM-5, respectively. The activation energy of the CO2 cycloaddition is predicted to be 40.4, 38.9, and 38.5 kcal mol–1. The reaction is endothermic by 3.2 kcal mol–1 and has ethylene carbonate desorption energies of 24.9, 25.8, and 27.0 kcal mol–1, respectively. These values are consistent with the Lewis acidity of the catalysts. The quite high activation energy, especially in the case of Au-[B]-ZSM-5, suggests that the weak acidity of Au-[B]-ZSM-5 makes it a poor catalyst for the CO2/EO reaction. It was found that a lower activation energy of this cycloaddition is obtained in Au-MCM-22 systems. The activation energies of the CO2 cycloaddition with EO for Au-[B]-, Au-[Al]-, and Au-[Ga]- are predicted to be 36.7, 34.5, and 35.8 kcal mol–1, respectively. These transition geometries are confirmed by the obtained single imaginary frequencies of 286.9i, 273.9i, and 271.8i cm–1, related to the C2–O2 and O1–C3 bond formations, respectively. Because of the more positive charge of Au­(I) ion in Au-MCM-22, it can facilitate the activation of the C2–O1 bond of the EO molecule. Therefore, it implies that Au-MCM-22 could be a candidate catalyst for CO2 cycloaddition with epoxide.

The activation free energies for the cycloaddition of CO2 with EO on Au-[Al]-ZSM-5 and Au-[Al]-MCM-22 zeolites were calculated by single-point calculations at various basis sets, 6-31G­(d,p)+aug-cc-PVTZ-PP, 6-31G­(d,p)+cc-PVTZ-PP, 6-311G­(d,p)+LanL2DZ, to determine the reliability of the basis set on the activation free energy. As shown in Table S12, the free energy of EO adsorption and the activation free energy for the CO2 cycloaddition with EO on Au-[Al]-MCM-22 and Au-[Al]­ZSM-5 zeolites are virtually incentive to the basis sets. These results confirm the reliability of the reported values obtained at the 6-31G­(d,p)+LanL2DZ level for describing the free energy profiles of these systems.

Influence of Methyl Groups on the Catalytic Performance of Au-[M]-ZSM-5 and Au-[M]-MCM-22 (M = B, Al, Ga) in the Cycloaddition of CO2 with Epoxides

To investigate the effect of the electron-donating group on the catalytic activity of CO2 cycloaddition with epoxide, one and two methyl groups are added to one side of the EO molecule to generate PO and IBO, respectively. The selected geometrical parameters of the complexes related to the CO2 cycloaddition with PO and IBO over Au-[B]-, Au-[Al]-, and Au-[Ga]- zeolite models and their Mulliken charges are documented in Tables S4–S6 (PO) and Tables S7–S9 (IBO), respectively. For the isolated epoxide molecules, the electron-donating inductive effect of the CH3 group weakens the epoxide C–O bond between the oxygen atom and the substituted carbon atom. As a result, the C–O bond distance increases from 1.417 Å (EO) to 1.425 Å for PO and 1.433 Å for IBO, respectively. Furthermore, the oxygen atoms of PO and IBO exhibit higher basicity than that of EO. The atomic charge on the oxygen atom becomes more negative, increasing from −0.453e for EO to −0.470e for PO and −0.489e for IBO. The weakening of the epoxide C–O bond and the increased nucleophilicity of the epoxide oxygen facilitate C–O bond activation during coupling with CO2 to form cyclic carbonates. For the adsorption of PO and IBO on Au-[Al]-ZSM-5 and Au-[Al]-MCM-22 zeolite models, similar to EO adsorption, the oxygen atoms of PO and IBO interact with the Lewis acidic Au site of the catalyst via their lone-pair electrons. The adsorption free energies of PO and IBO on the Au sites of the zeolites are more negative than that of EO on the corresponding systems. The adsorption free energies of PO and IBO on Au-[M]-MCM-22 range from −25 to −33 kcal mol–1, while those on Au-[M]-ZSM-5 range from −21 to −28 kcal mol–1.

Increasing the electron-donating group on the epoxide from EO to IBO not only enhances the adsorption of free energy, primarily due to the increased basicity of the oxygen atom, but also promotes the formation of cyclic carbonate. For the PO molecule, the activation free energy is reduced by about 4.5 kcal mol–1 for ZSM-5 zeolite and about 2.8 kcal mol–1 for MCM-22 zeolite. The more facile coupling between the ring-opened PO and CO2 is primarily due to the increased stability of the interaction between these fragments. Unlike EO, PO possesses an electron-donating methyl group that increases the electron density on epoxide oxygen. This enhancement strengthens the nucleophilic character of the ring-opened intermediate, resulting in a shorter interatomic distance and a more stable transition state within the zeolite pore. The basicity of the zeolite framework has only a slight influence on the reactivity of the Au cation, consistent with observations in the EO/CO2 cycloaddition reaction. The Au cation supported on [Al]- and [Ga]-substituted zeolites exhibits higher activity than when supported on [B]-substituted zeolites. For the IBO molecule, the adsorption free energy onto the Au­(I) center is predicted to be approximately −27 kcal mol–1 for Au-[M]-ZSM-5 and −32 kcal mol–1 for Au-[M]-MCM-22. Despite these differences in energy, the distance between the Au cation and the IBO oxygen atom in the adsorption complex is nearly identical to those observed for EO and PO. The enhanced interaction in the IBO system is primarily attributed to the increased nucleophilic character of epoxide oxygen, combined with greater stabilization provided by the confinement effect of the zeolite framework. Interestingly, the Au­(I) center in the ZSM-5 framework becomes more active than that in the MCM-22 framework. Furthermore, the basicity of the zeolite framework appears to have a negligible effect on the reactivity of the Au cation when reacting with more substituted epoxides such as IBO. Although the adsorption of IBO on [B]-, [Al]-, and [Ga]-substituted zeolites follows the trend of Au cation aciditysimilar to observations for EO and POthe activation free energy for cycloaddition with CO2 remains nearly identical, at approximately 32 kcal mol–1, across these isomorphously substituted ZSM-5 and MCM-22 topologies.

Interplay between the Role of Catalyst Acidity and Additional Methyl Groups on the Catalytic Activity of Au-[M]-ZSM-5 and Au-[M]-MCM-22 (M = B, Al, and Ga) on CO2 Cycloaddition with Epoxides

To gain further insights into the variation in activation free energies for the CO2 cycloaddition with epoxides, the distortion–interaction model was employed to decompose the activation barrier into the distortion energy required to activate the two reactants and the interaction energy between them, leading to cyclic carbonate formation (cf. Table S13). In both zeolites, substitution with electron-donating groups enhances the reactivity of the epoxide, facilitating the activation of the adsorbed epoxide toward interaction with the CO2 molecule. The activation free energy associated with the distortion of the adsorbed epoxide decreases in the order EO > PO > IBO. The increased nucleophilicity of the epoxide oxygen induced by CH3 substituents strengthens the interaction between the ring-opened epoxide and CO2. This results in greater stabilization of the IBO/CO2 system compared to the EO/CO2 system. Consistent with this trend, CO2 undergoes greater distortion in the IBO system than in the EO system across all zeolitic catalysts. Therefore, the significantly enhanced reactivity of the Au­(I) center in ZSM-5 toward IBO is primarily attributed to the pronounced weakening of the epoxide C–O bond induced by electron-donating substituents. This substituent effect becomes more dominant than variations in the acidity of the Au sites among different zeolites. Table S15 shows the role of enthalpy and entropy in the CO2 cycloaddition with epoxides. The variation in the activation free energy (ΔG ‡) for CO2 cycloaddition on these systems closely follows the trends in activation enthalpy (ΔH ‡). Entropic contributions remain nearly identical across all frameworks, ranging from −34 to −42 cal/mol·K. The negative entropic change at the transition state reflects a loss of molecular mobility within the bimolecular transition state at the zeolite active sites. In the case of the IBO molecule, the inductive effect from the two methyl groups effectively overrides the influence of the zeolite framework. Consequently, the activation enthalpy for CO2/IBO is nearly identical across all systems, aligning with the trends observed in the activation free energies. In contrast, the less-activated EO remains highly sensitive to the local acidic environment provided by the trivalent cations and the zeolite framework. These thermodynamic observations indicate that the activation free energy for CO2 cycloaddition with epoxides is dominantly enthalpy-driven.

Using a machine-learning algorithm implemented in the Sure Independence Screening and Sparsifying Operator (SISSO) method, the activation free energy can be accurately predicted. Based on descriptors derived from the electronic properties of epoxides and their adsorption free energies on the Au­(I) cation of zeolites, two dominant descriptors are identified: the HOMO energy of the isolated epoxide molecules and the free energy of epoxide adsorption. These descriptors govern the prediction of the activation free energy for cyclic carbonate formation via the concerted pathway using eq . ΔG‡(SISSO)=−4.171×[HOMOepox×ΔGADSΔGADS−HOMOepox] 1

The electron-donating methyl groups in PO and IBO destabilize the HOMO through inductive and hyperconjugative effects, leading to an upward shift in the HOMO energy. This electronic effect not only weakens the C–O bonds of the epoxide ring but also enhances the ability of the epoxide oxygen to donate electrons to the Lewis-acidic Au­(I) site of the zeolite. As a result, the adsorption free energies of PO and IBO are higher than that of EO on the Au center of these zeolites. To validate the model, some systems were selected from the total of 18 to serve as the test set, while the remaining systems were utilized as the training set. Notably, the SISSO equation captures the correlation between the intrinsic electronic nature of the epoxides and their interaction strength with the Au center, which together determines the activation free energy for conversion to the corresponding cyclic carbonate. The synergistic effects between the enhanced nucleophilicity of the substituted epoxide and the tuned Lewis acidity of the Au­(I) cation, induced by the zeolite framework, facilitate the activation of both the epoxide and the CO2 molecule, allowing them to merge into the corresponding cyclic carbonate. Conclusions

In this work, computational calculations using the DFT method with the M06-L functional have been conducted to investigate the cycloaddition of CO2 with light epoxides (EO, PO, and IBO) over Au-exchanged zeolites (ZSM-5 and MCM-22) isomorphously substituted with B, Al, and Ga. The reaction starts with the adsorption of epoxide on Lewis active sites. The acidity of the catalyst depends on the substituted atoms and the zeolite structures. The acidity of the substituted zeolites and epoxide adsorption follows the trend [B]-zeolites < [Al]-zeolites < [Ga]-zeolites. The Au­(I) in MCM-22 exhibits higher acidity than that in ZSM-5 mainly due to zeolite framework effects. The CO2/epoxide conversion is proposed to proceed via a five-membered ring transition state, leading to the formation of cyclic carbonate in a single step. The activation energies of CO2 cycloaddition with EO were calculated to be 40.4, 38.9, and 38.5 kcal mol–1 for Au-[M]-ZSM-5 and 36.7, 34.5, and 35.8 kcal mol–1 for Au-[M]-MCM-22 (M = B, Al, Ga), respectively. The presence of additional methyl-donating groups enhances the nucleophilicity of the epoxide oxygen atom and strengthens its interaction with the Lewis active site. Consequently, the activation free energy for cycloaddition in both zeolites decreases in the order EO > PO > IBO. For example, in the case of the Al substitution system, the activation free energies are predicted to be 38.9 (EO), 34.7 (PO), and 32.0 (IBO) kcal mol–1 for Au-[Al]-ZSM-5 and 34.5 (EO), 32.7 (PO), and 31.3 (IBO) kcal mol–1 for Au-[Al]-MCM-22, respectively. The interplay between catalyst acidity and epoxide reactivity was further examined. For both zeolites, the activation energies decrease with increasing epoxide reactivity due to the electron-donating effect. In the case of CO2 cycloaddition with IBO over isomorphously substituted ZSM-5 and MCM-22, the activation free energies remain nearly identical at approximately 32 kcal mol–1, which falls within the reported range for catalytic systems. These results could be explained by the distortion–interaction model. The decrease of activation free energy is mainly due to the weakening of the C–O bond of epoxide. With respect to the calculated barrier from DFT, SISSO-predicted activation free energies for the formation of cyclic carbonate from EO, PO, and IBO over isomorphously substituted Au-ZSM-5 and Au-MCM-22 are reasonably accurate, with a very high R 2 of 1.00 and a small RMSE of 0.8 kcal mol–1. The calculated results in this study provide insights into the development of catalytic processes based on the features of active sites and reactant reactivity.
