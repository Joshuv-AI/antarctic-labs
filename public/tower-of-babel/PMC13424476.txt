Metabolic and vascular outcomes after transition to the MiniMed 780G system in adults ≥65 years with type 1 diabetes: a randomised, single-centre study
Authors: Bartłomiej Matejko, Katarzyna Cyranka, Paulina Suduł, Kamila Susuł, Jakub Krężel, Juliia Chernovol, Paweł Maga, Maciej T. Malecki, Tomasz Klupa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aims/hypothesisHybrid closed-loop insulin delivery systems are increasingly regarded as the preferred therapy for type 1 diabetes, although evidence in older adults remains limited. The aim of this randomised study was to evaluate the effectiveness and safety of the MiniMed 780G system in individuals with type 1 diabetes aged ≥65 years, with HbA1c<86 mmol/mol (10%), who were naive to this technology, with the primary endpoint based on glycaemic management (time in range). Vascular status and other glycaemic metrics were assessed as secondary outcomes.MethodsThis single-centre, open labelled (outcome assessors were not masked), randomised, controlled, parallel-group trial enrolled 34 participants who were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio using a computer-generated randomisation schedule to either advanced hybrid closed-loop (AHCL) therapy or control therapy (multiple daily injections or continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion) and followed for 12 months.ResultsTwenty-nine participants completed the study. Baseline characteristics, including glycaemic metrics, were comparable across groups (p>0.05). The AHCL group demonstrated a rapid and sustained improvement in glycaemic management, with time in range increasing from 57.4% at baseline to 79.7% at 12 months (p<0.001), accompanied by reductions in hyperglycaemia as reflected by time above range and time above range 2 (nominal p<0.001; p<0.001), and with no increase in hypoglycaemia across time below range and time below range 2 (nominal p=0.463; p=1). Adjusted between-group differences favoured AHCL for time in range (15.7% [95% CI 8.6, 22.6]) and HbA1c (−5 mmol/mol; [95% CI −9.7, −0.4 mmol/mol] [−0.46%; −0.89%, −0.04%]). No serious adverse events or episodes of diabetic ketoacidosis or severe hypoglycaemia occurred. Post-occlusive reactive hyperaemia showed a shortening of time to maximal flow in the AHCL group, indicating a favourable numerical trend towards improved microvascular function (nominal p>0.05). Retinal vascular status assessed using artificial intelligence remained stable throughout follow-up in both eyes (nominal p=0.710 and p=0.563, respectively).Conclusions/interpretationThe MiniMed 780G system appears effective for glycaemic management and safe for use in older adults with long-standing type 1 diabetes and a high burden of comorbidities. A non-statistically significant numerical tendency (p>0.05) in the improvement of vascular function that requires further confirmation was also observed.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov NCT06207838Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains peer-reviewed but unedited supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00125-026-06780-9.

Introduction

Treatment with automated insulin delivery (AID) systems, in accordance with recommendations from leading diabetes associations and expert opinions, should be the standard of care in the treatment of people with type 1 diabetes, provided that such systems are financially and organisationally accessible [1–4]. AID systems have demonstrated high clinical efficacy in increasing the time people with diabetes spend within the target glucose range, while significantly improving their quality of life (QoL) [5–10]. A hallmark of modern AID systems is the consistency of their outcomes across countries, continents, levels of hypoglycaemia risk and age groups [11–13]. It seems that older individuals should be the focus of particular attention in the context of type 1 diabetes treatment. The number of people with type 1 diabetes is expected to grow, primarily due to the longer life expectancy of individuals with this condition [14–16]. Treating the increasing population of older adults with type 1 diabetes will pose specific challenges, including reduced openness to new technologies, potential cognitive impairments, vision problems and the need for manual operation of these devices [16–18]. Due to the increased risk of hypoglycaemia, treatment guidelines suggest moving away from strict glycaemic management in this population [1].

Nevertheless, major guidelines and expert statements emphasise that older age alone should not preclude continuation or initiation of treatment with advanced diabetes technologies, including AID systems [1–3]. A multinational, retrospective, real-world study examining the performance of the MiniMed780G (MM780G) advanced hybrid closed-loop (AHCL) system in users with type 1 diabetes aged ≥56 years demonstrated that older MM780G system users can achieve a time in range (TIR)>70% without increasing the risk of hypoglycaemia. Notably, the system performed as well as or better than in younger users [19]. Recent real-world data using the MM780G system in individuals with type 1 diabetes and type 2 diabetes over 65 years of age have demonstrated the efficacy and safety of this system in this age group [20]. The effectiveness of AID systems in older adults has been demonstrated in terms of glycaemic outcomes; however, randomised controlled trial (RCT) data remain scarce. One of few such studies is the AIDE T1D trial, although it did not evaluate the MM780G system [17–19]. However, several questions remain, for example, whether the use of AID systems in older individuals significantly influences cardiovascular risk, with vascular health potentially acting as a surrogate marker of cardiovascular risk. Reducing the incidence of hypoglycaemia undoubtedly lowers the risk of acute coronary events, cardiac arrhythmias and other acute cardiovascular complications [21–23]. The question is to what extent TIR improvement can affect cardiovascular risk in individuals with long-standing type 1 diabetes. Similar studies have recently been conducted in individuals with type 2 diabetes [24, 25].

The primary objective of this randomised study was to evaluate the efficacy and safety of the MM780G AHCL system in improving glycaemic management and vascular health, particularly microcirculation, 1 year after initiating AID-based therapy in people with type 1 diabetes aged 65 or older who were naive to this technology.

Methods

This was a single-centre, randomised, controlled, parallel-group trial comprising a 2 week run-in phase and a 12 month study period (ClinicalTrials.gov registration no. NCT06207838, protocol identifier 1072.61201.5.2023).

We included individuals who actively responded to announcements in outpatient clinics/social media in Poland (most participants came from the southeastern part of the country). Although participants were recruited through voluntary response, the resulting sample appeared broadly representative of the target population in terms of ethnicity, sex and older age distribution. However, representativeness in terms of regional distribution and socioeconomic status may be limited. In accordance with the SAGER (Sex and Gender Equity in Research) guidelines (https://www.equator-network.org/reporting-guidelines/sager-guidelines/), sex was considered in the study design and analysis. Participants of both sexes (self-reported) were included and sex distribution reported; however, the study was not powered to detect sex-based difference.

Eligible individuals were people with type 1 diabetes aged ≥65 years, with HbA1c<86 mmol/mol (10%), treated with multiple daily injections (MDI) or continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion (CSII), and with no prior experience using the MM780G system. They entered the 2 week run-in period during which they were required to demonstrate tolerance to wearing the sensor. A full list of inclusion and exclusion criteria can be found in electronic supplementary material (ESM) Table 1. Comorbidities were evaluated during the initial consultation via a structured medical history.

The primary endpoint was the between-group difference in the percentage of time sensor glucose was within the range of 3.9–10 mmol/l (70–180 mg/dl) (TIR) at the end of the study. Secondary end points included time spent in the hyperglycaemic (time above range [TAR]) and hypoglycaemic ranges (time below range [TBR]); glycaemic variability; HbA1c; and indices of vascular status, including microcirculatory function, microvascular flow and endothelial function (intima–media thickness [IMT]; transcutaneous oxygen partial pressure [TcpO2]; flow peak; rest flow; flow ratio index [FRI] [peak flow/rest flow]; time to max; FMDsystol; rate of cuff deflation; velocity peak; augmentation index [AIx]). HbA1c measurements were carried out at screening and at the end of the 12 month study using an NGSP-certified laboratory assay. All other outcomes were labelled as exploratory, post hoc outcomes. These include time in tight range (TITR), which is the percentage of time glucose levels remain within a narrower, ‘stricter’ target range: 3.9–7.8 mmol/l (70–140 mg/dl), not specified in the trial registration; body composition parameters (weight, percentage body fat and body mass index [BMI] measured using bioimpedance technology [InBody 370S]); as well as local laboratory measurements obtained at the same time as HbA1c, including TSH and creatinine, which were used to calculate eGFR using the CKD-EPI equation. Standardised systolic and diastolic blood pressure, along with heart rate, were recorded after an adequate rest period while seated. Diabetic retinopathy was assessed using a colour fundus camera (DRS Plus) with artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted analysis (iCare ILLUME), which provides automated grading of retinal images to determine the presence and severity of diabetic retinopathy.

Between-group comparison of CGM-derived measures was based on the period of the last 2 weeks of the study. Safety endpoints included the number of severe hypoglycaemia and diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) events.

The study was continued for 12 months, with assessments of metabolic management, QoL, physical and cognitive measures, and vascular status conducted at the conclusion. Here, we present the results for metabolic control and vascular status, with specific variables described in detail in the following sections. Following the study conclusion, a continued observation phase with AHCL therapy was offered to participants of both arms (five participants from the control group switched to MM780G).

Procedures

All participants received training on CGM usage and wore the Guardian Link 4 Transmitter and Guardian 4 Sensor (G4S) for 2 weeks for baseline measurement and assessment of compatibility with the device. CGM data were downloaded at the clinic using CareLink Clinic software (https://carelink-trials.medtronic.com/login; accessed 8 May 2026).

Randomisation was conducted with Random Allocation Software to ensure unbiased group assignment (https://random-allocation-software.software.informer.com/2.0/; version 2.0). Participants were randomised to the AHCL or control group (MDI/CSII:BG/CGM) in a 1:1 ratio. The randomisation code was generated by a statistician using random block size of 4, 6 and 8.

Participants assigned to the AHCL group received comprehensive individualised education conducted by our investigators, which lasted approximately 5 h. Some participants attended the training sessions with a partner. All participants were advised to contact the investigators by phone if they encountered any technical issues. First, we initiated the MM780G system in open-loop mode with ‘Suspend before low’ enabled for 3–7 days, followed by full AHCL activation (glucose target: 5.6 mmol/l [100 mg/dl]; active insulin time [AIT]: 2.0–2.5 h). Since the study participants had suboptimal diabetes management at baseline, the insulin to carbohydrate ratio settings in use were applied with only minor adjustments. The participants were instructed not to adjust system settings without consulting a physician. Insulin to carbohydrate ratio and AIT were adjusted by the physician during the study as per the investigator's judgement; glucose target was adjusted only in the case of safety concerns. Participants’ interaction with the system and glycaemic management were reviewed, and the system settings were reassessed and adjusted as needed at each visit, whether in the clinic or remotely (ESM Fig. 1). Participants in the control group continued their prior therapy and wore a G4S CGM for a 2 week period at the end of the 12 months, with all using Libre 2 sensors. Two participants were switched to Libre 2 sensors from glucometers at the beginning.

Vascular status

Microcirculatory function was assessed with laser Doppler flowmetry (LDF) and TcpO2 test using a Periflux 6000 device (Perimed, Järfälla, Sweden). The tests were performed with thermostatic laser Doppler probes for precise tissue heating at the measurement site and a modified Clark’s polarographic oxygen sensor. Probes were placed on the dorsal aspect of the fingers. Recorded parameters included tissue oxygen partial pressure, microvascular flow (capillaries, arterioles, venules, shunts), perfusion, metabolic activity and oxyhaemoglobin dissociation.

Microvascular flow (using LDF) was assessed with a baseline flow and by performing a post-occlusive reactive hyperaemia (PORH) test, a functional test that evaluates the ability of microvascular vessels to dilate in response to short-term ischaemia. LDF results are expressed in arbitrary perfusion units (PU). Perfusion quantification was performed using real-time flow parameters.

Endothelial functions were assessed by non-invasive imaging techniques, including flow-mediated dilation (FMD) and IMT, which are considered risk factors for cardiovascular events. The study followed current guidelines for ultrasound-based FMD assessment [26]. Before the test, the participants were resting in a supine position. The brachial artery was localised with the use of an ultrasound transducer 2–5 cm above the cubital fossa. The cuff was placed on the forearm and inflated to 50 mmHg above systolic blood pressure for 5 min to restrict artery inflow. From the 60 s to the 90 s after deflation, all vasodilation measurements with vessel diameter changes to reactive hyperaemia were made. FMD was measured with the Hitachi Arietta 850 ultrasound scanner using its proprietary software (system version 00-3.1.2, FUJIFILM Healthcare Corporation) [27].

IMT was measured bilaterally using a Hitachi Arietta 850 ultrasound scanner. Measurements were performed at the far wall of the common carotid artery (CCA), 1 cm proximal to the carotid bifurcation and calculated by the automated software provided with the ultrasound scanner (system version 00-3.1.2, FUJIFILM Healthcare Corporation) [27] dedicated to this measurement in accordance with established consensus guidelines. The higher of the two bilateral IMT values was used for analysis. Furthermore, plaque presence was systematically evaluated in the common carotid artery, carotid bulb and internal carotid artery bilaterally.

In addition to metabolic and vascular outcomes, psychological parameters, frailty indices, and psychomotor and cognitive functions were also assessed as part of the study protocol. The results of these analyses will be reported in a separate publication.

Statistical analysis

Based on data from our prior study conducted at our centre, assuming a mean TIR of 80% in the AHCL group and 65% in the control group with a common SD of 15%, a total sample size of 40 participants (20 per group), including an assumed attrition rate of 15%, was estimated to provide 80% power to detect a between-group difference of 15% using a two-sided two-sample t test at a significance level of 0.05 [28].

The primary population consisted of individuals who met eligibility criteria, demonstrated tolerance to wearing the sensor, complied with G4S, received the allocated intervention and completed the study. All the analyses we performed were based on this primary population. All available data were used in computing means and SDs. No imputation method was applied for missing data.

For comparisons between two independent groups, the Student’s t test or Welch’s t test was used for normally distributed continuous variables (normality assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test); otherwise, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied. For comparisons of two dependent groups, the paired t test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used, as appropriate. Differences between categorical variables were analysed using the χ2 test.

To assess the effect of treatment allocation on outcomes, an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was conducted with adjustment for treatment arm and baseline values. Significant interactions between baseline values and treatment arm, if present, were included in the model, and the adjusted mean difference between treatment arms was estimated. When ANCOVA assumptions were not met (normality, homogeneity of variance or linearity), the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used. All p values are two-tailed. Analyses were performed with R, version 4.5.1 (https://www.r-project.org/), and RStudio, version 1.3.959 (https://posit.co/download/rstudio-desktop).

The primary endpoint was analysed using ANCOVA and should be considered confirmatory. Secondary and exploratory endpoints were analysed using ANCOVA as descriptive (supportive) analyses without adjustment for multiplicity and should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the bioethics committee (no. 1072.61201.5.2023, dated 17 May 2023). All participants provided written informed consent to participate in this study. The collected data were stored in the hospital according to recommendations from the bioethics committee. The participants did not receive any financial compensation for participation in the study.

Results

A total of 34 individuals, mostly treated with MDI and CGM (only one using an insulin pump), were recruited (between 19 September 2023 and 26 March 2024). Most of the participants were from southeastern Poland. Three participants withdrew from the study prior to or on the day of randomisation, with each case corresponding to a single participant: one declined sensor use, one was not allocated to the AHCL arm, which was their preferred option, and one did not accept pump therapy. Additionally, one participant withdrew due to worsening of a diabetic foot condition. Thirty-one participants were randomised into either the AHCL or control group (1:1). One male participant from the control group, as mentioned above, withdrew immediately after being informed of the randomisation result. One female participant from the AHCL group withdrew from the study at month 5.5 because she did not accept the system (Fig. 1). Fourteen participants from the AHCL group and 15 from the control group completed the 12 month study procedures. Due to recruitment difficulties and study withdrawals, fewer than the 20 participants per group planned in the clinical trial protocol were randomised. Under the original study assumptions, a post hoc analysis indicated that, with 14 participants in the study group and 15 in the control group, a minimal difference of 12.1% in TIR is detectable with 90% power.

In the AHCL group, the median age was 66.5 (66–68.8) years, diabetes duration was 34.5±16.5 years, BMI was 26.9±4.0 kg/m2 and HbA1c was 59.6±10.3 mmol/mol (7.59±0.94%). There were no significant differences between groups in age, type 1 diabetes duration, CGM indices or HbA1c. The AHCL group had a higher proportion of men compared with the control group (Table 1). At baseline, the cohort was characterised by a high burden of comorbidities, including lipid disorders (96.5%), hypertension (82.7%), thyroid diseases (58.6%), benign prostatic hyperplasia (53.0% of men) and moderate or more advanced stages of diabetic retinopathy (51.0%, AI-assessed). Hashimoto’s disease was present in 34.5% of participants, while overall, 37.9% had autoimmune diseases other than type 1 diabetes, including Hashimoto’s disease. Other comorbidities included osteoarthritis (27.6%), coronary artery disease (27.6%) and polyneuropathy (24.1%). A history of coronary revascularisation, including percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) or coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), was reported in 13.8% of participants. The same proportion (13.8%) had a history of neoplasia. Among MM780G users, a glucose target of 5.6 mmol/l (100 mg/dl) and an AIT of 2 h were used by 80% and 100% of participants, respectively. As far as the main primary endpoint is concerned, the TIR for the AHCL group increased from 57.4% at baseline to 76.8% after 1 month and to 79.0% after 3 months, and remained at 79.5% after 6 months and 79.7% after 12 months (all p<0.001). Improvement in TIR was accompanied by reductions in hyperglycaemia as reflected by TAR and TAR2 (nominal p<0.001; p<0.001), and with no increase in hypoglycaemia across TBR and TBR2 (nominal p=0.463; p=1). In the control group, TIR remained stable over time (65.6% at baseline vs 66.4% at the end of the study). The ANCOVA-adjusted difference in TIR between groups was 15.7% (95% CI 8.6, 22.6). Secondary outcomes showed reductions in TAR and time above range 2 (TAR2) (nominal p<0.001), with no meaningful changes in TBR or time below range 2 (TBR2) (nominal p>0.05) throughout the study compared with the control group. The ANCOVA-adjusted difference in HbA1c between groups was −5 mmol/mol (95% CI −9.7, −0.4) (−0.46%; −0.89%, −0.04%). Among exploratory, post hoc outcomes, only TITR showed an ANCOVA-adjusted difference between groups of 18.6% (95% CI 12.1, 25.1) (Table 2). Throughout the observation period, sensor use averaged 97.2% of the time, and Auto Mode was active for 96.6% of the time. At the end of the study, participants consumed a mean of 3.9 meals per day, entered 151 g of carbohydrates and had an Auto Basal percentage of 34%. No serious adverse events (SAEs) or cases of DKA or severe hypoglycaemia occurred, and no device malfunctions were observed.

At baseline, eight participants (six in the control group and two in the MM780G group) had no signs of diabetic retinopathy, six (2/4) had mild diabetic retinopathy, nine (5/4) moderate diabetic retinopathy, two (1/1) severe diabetic retinopathy and four (1/3) proliferative diabetic retinopathy (the more severe eye was used for classification). By the end of the study, no between-group differences in diabetic retinopathy stage were observed in either eye as assessed by AI. These exploratory findings suggest stability of retinal status over the study period (Table 2).

Regarding microcirculation parameters assessed using LDF, baseline flow values did not change meaningfully in either study group. In the PORH test, a shortening of the maximum blood flow time in the MM780G group was observed, suggesting a favourable numerical trend towards improved microvascular function (Table 2). There were no meaningful changes in other parameters after a 5 min occlusion. TcpO2 values and endothelial function parameters (FMD, IMT) were similar in both groups, and no meaningful improvement was observed at the 1 year follow-up.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first randomised study to evaluate the outcomes of AID in an older population over a 1 year period, including assessments of retinopathy and vascular status. This study is an important step towards building the evidence base for optimal management of the growing population of older adults with type 1 diabetes [26, 29–31].

At the end of this 1 year randomised trial, participants using the AHCL system had substantially higher TIR compared with the control group, with no increase in TBR. Improvement in TITR was even greater. HbA1c levels decreased in the AHCL group over 12 months. This means that the primary endpoint was achieved.

The first randomised trial comparing advanced insulin delivery systems with standard sensor-augmented pump therapy in older adults (≥60 years of age) demonstrated that the hybrid closed-loop algorithm increased TIR by 8.6% (CamAPS FX) [30]. A similar design was used in a recently published RCT involving a larger population of older adults with type 1 diabetes, which reported an 8.9% improvement in TIR and a 0.2% reduction in HbA1c (baseline HbA1c 55.2 mmol/mol [7.2%]) [17]. As in our study, they did not demonstrate a significant benefit regarding hypoglycaemia. The improvement in TIR was almost exclusively due to decreases in the proportion of time spent in the hyperglycaemic range. This was probably because the study population had very low rates of hypoglycaemia to begin with [30]. Our study supports the finding that excellent glycaemic management can be achieved using a hybrid closed-loop system without increasing the risk of hypoglycaemia, suggesting that relaxing glycaemic targets in older adults with long-standing diabetes using AHCL systems, as recommended by guidelines, may not be necessary. CGM targets should be customised based on clinical considerations, including frailty, functional status, comorbidities and use of CGM or AID systems [32].

Of note, our study population was not a random sample of individuals with type 1 diabetes but rather comprised highly motivated participants with long-standing, well-controlled diabetes and high functional status, as evidenced by the complete absence of SAEs over the 12 month follow-up, despite a high burden of comorbidities. Nearly all had no prior experience with insulin pump therapy, probably due to cost barriers, except for one individual randomised to the MM780G group. In contrast, other studies involving older adults with type 1 diabetes often included a higher proportion of participants already using insulin pump technology [33, 34]. This shows that older adults can successfully adopt advanced insulin delivery systems when given thorough education. This factor may also have helped explain the greater improvements in TIR observed in our study.

Adding to this, we have previously shown that, in real-world data from Polish individuals, outcomes with this technology were among the best in Europe [13], which we attribute to the adoption of optimal settings in Poland and the already high glycaemic outcomes at the system start.

Also, an important contributing factor to the achievement of 79.7% TIR in our study could have been the adherence to the predefined AHCL settings, with AIT set to 2.0–2.5 h and glucose target set to 5.6 mmol/l (100 mg/dl). The observed high sensor use and time spent in AHCL mode were comparable to those reported in other AHCL studies, reflecting high treatment adherence, proper sensor use and no technical problems causing Auto Mode exits [8, 13].

Rapid improvements in glucose management may lead to early worsening of diabetic retinopathy, particularly in individuals with elevated HbA1c levels and LDL-C>2.6 mmol/l (>100 mg/dl) prior to transitioning to AID therapy [35]; however, recent studies suggest that short-term use of AID systems is not associated with retinopathy progression and may even lead to improvements in diabetic retinopathy severity grades [36]. Our study adds to these findings by suggesting that retinal stability (AI-driven assessment) may be maintained over a longer period of 12 months (Table 2).

With regard to vascular results, the observed shortening of the maximum blood flow time in the PORH test, indicating improved capillary flow without a significant change in the maximum blood flow time after a 5 min occlusion, suggests a meaningful trend towards improved microcirculation function in the MM780G group. The lack of clear improvement in microcirculatory and endothelial parameters is probably attributable to the short follow-up period and the relatively normal baseline glucose values in both groups. Another explanation could be that PORH-based improved capillary flow may be the most sensitive of microvascular function tests under improved glycaemic conditions, reflecting the ability of skeletal muscle arterioles to dilate in response to acute bouts of ischaemia ahead of the improvement of other parameters.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, difficulty in participant recruitment, which resulted in a smaller-than-planned sample size. In addition, study withdrawals further reduced the number of participants completing the trial. A post hoc power analysis was therefore performed to quantify the impact of the reduced sample size on the statistical power of the study. One withdrawal was due to not accepting the intervention or randomisation result which may have introduced bias. Second, due to recruitment challenges, social media was used for participant outreach, which may have introduced selection bias, as individuals responding to online recruitment may have been more motivated or more technologically competent than the general patient population. Third, sex differences were observed after randomisation, with a higher proportion of male participants allocated to the AID group. Although some studies suggest that male individuals are more likely to adopt AID systems [37], real-world data indicate no sex differences across age groups in system performance [38]. Fourth, the study may have been underpowered to detect differences in non-glycaemic endpoints. Finally, a per-protocol analysis was used rather than an intention-to-treat analysis, as only two participants had incomplete data; however, inclusion of participants with incomplete data would have required outcome imputation, which could require additional modelling assumptions and potentially introduce bias. Given the very low level of missingness, a complete-case approach was considered unlikely to affect the results meaningfully.

The study also included the assessment of psychological parameters, frailty indices, and psychomotor and cognitive functions. These findings will be presented in a separate publication.

Conclusions

In summary, these results suggest that, with comprehensive patient education, the AHCL system is an effective and safe option for improving glycaemic management in older, well-managed individuals with long-standing type 1 diabetes, despite a high burden of comorbidities. In addition, a non-statistically significant numerical trend towards improved vascular function was observed, warranting further evaluation in long-term follow-up.
