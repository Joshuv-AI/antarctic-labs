Future caregiving concerns and plans of parents of adults with mild intellectual disability: A qualitative study from a South Indian tertiary care hospital
Authors: Lakshmi Jogi, Thanapal Sivakumar, Hareesh Angothu, Thomas M. Kishore, Deepak Jayarajan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Parents of children with intellectual disability (ID) are concerned about the care of their wards after their death or incapacitation. Most literature originates in high-income countries, which have greater resources and distinct cultural and socioeconomic contexts. There is a need for Indian literature.Aim:To explore the concerns and plans of parents regarding the future of their wards with mild ID when they can no longer care for them.Methods:The study was conducted in a tertiary care hospital in Bengaluru, India. Both parents of four men and four women with mild ID (referred to as ‘ward’ from here on) who had future caregiving concerns were interviewed using a semi-structured interview schedule. All 16 interviews were translated and transcribed. Thematic analysis was done using the Braun and Clarke approach. Atlas-Ti software, version 9, was used.Results:Parents were concerned about identifying a prospective guardian, the ward’s safety, and future care. Parental concerns are influenced by individual factors (including the degree of supervision required for daily activities, behavioral problems, and physical health of the ward), parental factors (such as age, physical health of the parents, coping strategies, and awareness about ID), family factors (including sibling relationship and attitude of father), and environmental factors (such as attitude toward the ward with ID and COVID-19). Most parents had a financial plan, preferred to keep guardianship a family affair, and considered residence and occupation/employment after their lifetimes.Conclusions:Caregiving concerns and plans did not differ between parents. They were influenced by the parents’ financial position, how society perceived the ward, independence, and behavioral issues. Parents preferred a family member, usually the neurotypical sibling, as the guardian, but many hadn’t explicitly discussed guardianship with them or initiated legal procedures.

INTRODUCTION

Universally, parents are concerned about the future care of single and unemployed adult wards with intellectual disability (ID) after their lifetime or incapacitation.[12345] In low- and middle-income countries, such as India, caregiving needs are often met primarily by parents and immediate family members, and systemic support for community-based, adult-independent living is limited.[6]

Most of the literature on the needs of family caregivers in the context of future independent living for persons with ID is from high-income countries.[178] Studies from low- and middle-income countries are required because cultural and socioeconomic factors influence future care planning.[9]

The Indian National Trust Act, 1999 (NTA,1999)[10] has provisions to safeguard the future of persons with developmental disabilities, including those with ID. The NTA (1999) explicitly focuses on supporting persons with ID in community-based, independent living. Accordingly, it has specific guidelines on legal guardianship, health insurance (Niramaya), and long-stay group homes (Gharaunda). The country has 25 government-funded long-stay group homes, with an intake capacity of 20 people per center under the National Trust, which is grossly inadequate for current needs. Thus, caregiving, by default, stays with the family and parents. Under the Act, parents can apply for legal guardianship, or approach another person or an agency to apply for legal guardianship, to care for the adult ward and to decide on the ward’s behalf.[6] Thus, a guardian can be the parent, sibling, another family member, or a paid caregiver. Although caregiving responsibilities among Indian families were traditionally shared by extended family members, this is dwindling in urban India for various reasons, including the rise of nuclear families, the absence of siblings, migration, or the relocation of extended family members.[1112] Parents are worried about the future of the adult ward with ID due to the lack of informal support and minimal formal support. In this context, we studied parents’ future caregiving concerns and plans for their wards with mild ID. We adopted a cross-sectional, exploratory study design for this purpose.

METHODS

Study Setting: The study was conducted at National Institute of Mental Health and Neuro Sciences (NIMHANS), Bengaluru, India, a government-funded teaching hospital offering mental health and neurological care.

Participants: Participants were selected using purposive sampling to include only parents of adults aged 18–40 with an ICD-10 diagnosis of mild ID, who expressed future caregiving concerns during their outpatient consultations, ensuring relevance to the study’s aims. Single parents were excluded to ensure that potential differences in fathers’ and mothers’ perspectives were represented. Wards with medical comorbidities requiring priority management were excluded to avoid biasing parents’ perspectives. Wards with other developmental disorders or mental illnesses were excluded to ensure a homogeneous group and avoid confounding variables.

Method: The Institute Ethics Committee approved the study. Written informed consent was obtained from eligible participants before the study commenced, during which LJ explained the purpose of the interviews. All parents approached provided consent to participate. Parents were screened for depression using the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9).[13] The interview schedule was designed to encompass diverse aspects of caregiving concerns, validated by mental health professionals, and based on input from focus group discussions involving various stakeholders (including mental health professionals, parents with lived experience of addressing future caregiving concerns, and staff from non-governmental organizations (NGO) working for persons with ID and their families).

The fathers and mothers of four female and four male wards with IDs were interviewed for the study (16 parents). LJ interviewed the parents separately to explore possible differences, using English and two Indian languages (Hindi and Kannada), in which she was proficient, to accommodate the participants’ diverse language preferences. The interviews were conducted between June and September 2021. LJ was pursuing an MD in psychiatry and had completed 23 months of her residency in psychiatry at the time of data collection. The interviews were conducted via video conferencing software or by telephone, as per participants’ choice. Video conferencing was the primary mode of communication, as the interviews were conducted three months after the second wave of COVID-19. Telephone interviews were conducted for parents who did not have access to a smartphone for video conferencing. Each interview lasted between 60 and 90 minutes. Field notes were made during the interviews. Interviews were audio- or video-recorded, depending on the interview mode. The recordings were manually translated into English and transcribed. Repeat interviews were not conducted. New participants were recruited and interviewed until data saturation was reached. Data saturation was achieved when no new themes emerged from subsequent interviews, indicating the comprehensiveness of the collected data. Transcripts were not returned to participants for corrections to maintain confidentiality and data integrity. Recorded data were securely stored and destroyed 6 months after analysis. Thematic analysis was conducted using Atlas.ti software, following the Braun and Clarke approach,[14] in which themes emerged directly from the data rather than being predetermined. Coding discrepancies were resolved through discussions among the authors to ensure the reliability of the results. The data analyzed was not shared with participants for feedback to avoid potential emotional distress and to respect their time constraints due to other responsibilities. However, we ensured the validity of our findings through coding triangulation to enhance credibility and reduce researcher bias. This study adheres to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) to ensure transparency in data collection, analysis, and reporting.

THEME 1: NATURE OF FUTURE CAREGIVING CONCERNS

Parents’ concerns about the future care of their wards with mild ID revolve around identifying a guardian, safety, occupation, and finances.

Precipitating factors

Parents had future caregiving concerns about their ward with mild ID (referred to as ‘ward’ from here on) at different points in time: lack of catch-up with neurotypical peers (five parents), the revelation of the ward’s disability (three parents), separation from the spouse (two parents), the realization that marriage was not feasible for the ward (two parents), the neurotypical sibling moving out of the home for higher education (one parent), the neurotypical sibling’s marriage (one parent), financial constraints (one parent), and the death of a significant family member (one parent).

A mother reported that her concerns started when her son had scholastic difficulties:

From a very young age, he had difficulties in studying. We were concerned about how he would move forward when we were not there, how he would earn a livelihood, etc. From the 6th standard, teachers started calling us to scold us for not paying attention to studies. We always thought that he would improve with time. But that never happened. - Mother of Mr I

Another mother reported that her concerns began when she learned that her daughter was a special child:

When I understood that she was a special child, it used to strike in my mind: what would happen to her in the future? When nobody is there, how will she survive in this world? - Mother of Ms D

A father reported that his concerns started when his son-in-law abandoned his daughter:

My daughter was married to a person 4 years ago. After 1 month of marriage, her husband dropped her off at our house. It was then that we started thinking about this issue. There was hope till marriage that her husband would take care of her. -Father of Ms V

Identifying a guardian

Seven of eight families reported difficulties identifying a guardian for future caregiving. Six of eight families felt the prospective guardian may not offer the ward the same level of care:

We do not know anyone who can take care of him. Everyone has their own life. People can look after him for 2-3 days, that is all. Not more than that. No one can take care of him forever. We will look after him till we are alive. Other people cannot adjust their routines for him. – Mother of Mr N

A mother was worried that people wouldn’t come forward to discuss future care plans:

I’m not finding anyone who can help us and support her when we are not there; no one has openly spoken to us. This is worrying me. – Mother of Ms A

Safety concerns

The parents of all four female wards feared their daughter may be abused physically or sexually:

We are worried about her safety. Being a modest girl child, she should not be, you know, misused. -Father of Ms D

A father reported instances when his son was touched inappropriately and physically beaten:

Some people have touched him inappropriately, and one person physically hit him some 2-3 times. - Father of Mr N

Employment/occupation concerns

Out of eight wards, five were employed in the past or present, two were never employed, and one was a student. Four of eight families wanted the ward occupied because it reduced behavioral problems; two wanted income, while two wanted both income and occupation. Three of 16 parents were concerned about their ward’s lack of persistence in employment.

A mother wanted her son to get a permanent job so that he could support himself:

He can earn enough money for his livelihood if he has a job. The current focus is to get him a permanent job; if he has one, he can look after himself.

-Mother of Mr H

Two of eight families felt that the neurotypical sibling could ensure safety, but not gainful engagement and peer interaction. A father feared his daughter might feel imprisoned when no one was home on working days:

If she stays with my son, the son and daughter-in-law will go off to work, and she will be left alone in the house. It will be like a prison for her. - Father of Ms U

Financial concerns

Two of the 16 parents were worried that their ward might be financially defrauded:

People might cheat him financially. How he will survive in this present world is one of my concerns - Father of Mr N

Low-income families (4 out of 8) had challenges saving money for their ward’s future:

I have five daughters. We got everyone married, all except the youngest. We have to look after ‘V’ and her child. We are meeting our daily needs with much difficulty. We cannot save money for her future. - Mother of Ms V

A father was worried about his and his wife’s unemployment:

Since the last lockdown, I am worried because I don’t have work now, and his mother has also lost her job. His salary is our only income, and we use it to pay house rent and other daily needs. -Father of Mr H

Parents’ future care concerns primarily stem from changing life circumstances, difficulties in identifying a suitable guardian, and worries about safety and financial stability.

THEME 2: CONTEXTUAL FACTORS AFFECTING FUTURE CAREGIVING CONCERNS

Various contextual factors, including personal factors unique to the ward and the influence of parents, family, and environment, all contribute to future caregiving concerns.

Personal factors of the ward

Degree of supervision required

All eight wards required varying degrees of supervision for basic and instrumental activities of daily living.

A father was concerned about his son’s inability to manage daily activities independently:

He cannot calculate money on his own. His mother has to support him in selecting dresses for him. He would not take his towel or any bath essentials, even for bathing. He will not understand where to go or how to reach a place. I do not know how he will live alone or what he will do.

- Father of Mr H

Behavior

Parents of five of eight wards reported behavioral problems, especially anger.

A mother described the challenges she faced when her daughter was at home:

She will get angry with us. She always watches TV, and if we say not to watch more, she will quarrel; she doesn’t do any household chores. If she is at home, she will be tense and angry.

– Mother of Ms V

A father appreciated his daughter’s behavior:

She knows how to behave with others and behaves very well when moving to another house. She is very helpful, and there is no need for extra attention to look after her.

- Father of Ms A

Parents’ concerns were alleviated when the ward was well-behaved.

Health issues

Two parents were concerned when their wards had health issues requiring regular medications:

He had a seizure attack. I doubt whether he would have been cared for if I hadn’t been around. I do not know what will happen when I am no more.

- Father of Mr N

Parental factors

Awareness of ID

Five of eight families understood the nature of ID and the support required after reading books, consulting doctors, and joining self-help groups for parents:

We learned many things after reading books and visiting many doctors and specialty centers. We also joined self-help groups with other parents.”

- Mother of Ms A

Three of eight families thought medical interventions might cure the condition. Incidentally, these three families were less proficient in English.

Age/health of parents

Two of the 16 parents (from different families) reported taking better care of their health for their ward’s sake. One parent felt he was in good health, and it was too early to plan for his ward’s future:

I am currently 51 years old, and I am healthy. There is ample time to plan for his future. Hence, I have not discussed my ideas with others yet.

- Father of Mr R

Coping strategies

Two of 16 parents opined spirituality helped them cope better:

God gave him to me because God saw that I could look after him. I have taken it up as a challenge rather than as a burden.

- Father of Mr N

Parents’ level of awareness of ID influenced their future caregiving concerns.

Family factors

Sibling

Relationship between siblings

Four parents hoped that the neurotypical sibling, who shared a good relationship with the ward, would likely take on future care responsibilities:

Before she shifted, they both used to stay in the same room. They were more like friends. My daughter wishes to take care of ‘A’ after our time. - Mother of Ms A

Parents believed that the relationship between siblings was important for future care. Parents were concerned when the neurotypical sibling was not interested in the ward (3 of 8 families) or was not on good terms with the ward (1 of 8 families).

Sibling’s life stage

Parents hesitated when the sibling got married and moved to a different place. Two parents wished that the neurotypical sibling would assume guardianship, but did not take the discussion forward after the sibling’s marriage:

I do not want to discuss it with them now. I do not know how they will respond. They are married now, have other responsibilities, and all of them are in different places.

- Mother of Ms V

The mother anticipated that her daughter might have difficulty adapting to her sibling’s spouse at their home.

Physical distance between the residence of the sibling and the parents

In the present study, one sibling resided in the same house, one in a different city, one in a different state, and two in a different country. Parents preferred discussing future caregiving in person with the sibling:

We have not met our son since he moved out of the city because of COVID-19. When we tried to contact him during the first lockdown, he did not respond appropriately. At least we wanted to ask if he would take care of her. We cannot talk about these issues over the phone.

- Mother of Ms D

Attitude of father

Three of the eight mothers reported that their husbands, who had asked them to care for the wards, were not actively involved in their care. Among the two mothers who had worked in the past, one had to quit her job to care for the ward:

I told him your job is not continuous. You leave it and stay at home, take her to the classes, and care for her; I will do my job to earn, but he is unwilling to do that. - Mother of Ms D

Mothers felt fathers could have been more supportive.

Environmental factors

Attitude of extended family and friends to the ward

Four of eight families discussed their ward’s future care plans with supportive extended family and friends:

His cousins know about him and are very supportive, so they should be there in the future.

– Father of Mr R

They were very fond of her and show their love towards the child. Nobody was neglecting her.

- Mother of Ms A

Three of 16 parents felt others weren’t open or interested in such discussions:

I have not discussed my worries with anyone. If I talk about this to someone, they would tease him or might not understand the situation.

- Mother of Mr I

COVID-19 pandemic

The pandemic made life uncertain for all due to deaths, job losses, monetary problems, reduced socialization, and people confined to their homes during lockdowns. Parents feared their death, considering their age and medical comorbidities, which exacerbated their future care concerns:

During the lockdown, I felt terrible. It was tough, and boardings were closed because of this lockdown. What if something happens to us suddenly, to one of us, who will come to help us?

- Mother of Ms D

The closure of daycare/skills training centers affected the wards’ activity schedules:

His social life has been affected daily as day-care services have closed. So, he has been seeing me and my wife constantly and has not moved out of the home.

-Father of Mr N

Perceived support from extended family, friends, and neighbors mattered to parents. Additionally, COVID-19 increased the uncertainty about the future.

Of 16 parents, seven had a future care plan. Five parents felt it was too early to plan. Four parents had difficulties meeting their daily needs and had not thought about a future care plan. Parents were more concerned when their wards required more supervision and had behavioral problems and health issues. Parents’ awareness of ID helped them handle the situation better. Parents felt comfortable when the neurotypical sibling shared a good relationship with the ward. Mothers felt fathers could have been more supportive. Perceived social support mattered to parents.

THEME 3: PARENTAL PLANS TO ADDRESS FUTURE CAREGIVING CONCERNS

Parents’ future care plans focused on identifying a future guardian, place of residence, and what their ward can do.

Identification of prospective guardian

All parents preferred a family member as a guardian. Parents preferred the sibling who was willing to take up the responsibility. When there was no sibling (3 families) or the sibling was settled abroad (1 family), or was not ready to take responsibility (3 families), parents considered other options: a grandson (who was three years old at the time of the interview) (1 family), or an extended family member (6 families). Still, they had not discussed this with them. While two families had explicitly discussed guardianship with the neurotypical sibling, only one had obtained a legal guardianship certificate.

A father with no other children had involved extended family members:

I am trying to have a safety net for him, so I am involving some of my family members and talking with them so that he can have a physical safety net. - Father of Mr N

A mother expected her 3-year-old grandson to care for their daughter after their lifetime.

She will stay alone. Her son is three years old. Her son will take care in the future

-Mother of Ms V

All four families of male wards considered marriage. This was the only plan for two families, and the remaining 2 had a backup plan (the sibling/extended family assuming guardianship) if the marriage didn’t materialize. They felt that a woman with average intelligence from a lower socioeconomic status, who understood their son’s limitations, would be a more effective caregiver in the future:

If we can find a good girl for him, we will get him married, irrespective of caste or background. We are planning to check in orphanages and shelter homes as well. We can provide all the financial support. We are okay with anyone who would care for our son. We have to discuss it with the girl regarding his disability and then come to a decision.

- Mother of Mr N

Five out of 16 parents (including three who struggled to meet daily needs) had not explicitly identified a guardian and believed God would show a way when the time came:

Her father says nothing will happen; God is there and knows. We are just praying to God. We have not done anything yet. -Mother of Mr H

Residential plan

Five of eight families (4 families of male wards and one family of female wards; all were reasonably independent) wished the wards could reside in their own homes with assistance from the future guardian (if it was the sibling, cousin, or other relatives and not the spouse):

We have got some family property. So, I have built a house for him there …so he would be with that circle of cousins nearby. He will be happy there.

-Father of Mr R

When the sibling was supportive and actively involved in future care planning,

The family (of a female ward) considered a supportive sibling’s home the first option:

My younger daughter even spoke to her husband about this before marriage that she will have to take care of her after our lifetime. So, if her sister is happy to keep her in their house, then I have no issues.

- Mother of Ms U

Two families who could afford to pay considered residential rehabilitation centers where their ward could interact with other residents:

The residential center feels ideal because people like her will surround her, and they can get to know each other. They can get along well, and there will be people who take care of them and look after them, and they may also know their drawbacks.

- Father of Ms U

Four parents who could afford residential care centers wanted good-quality services:

We provide her with all the food required to maintain good health. Cleanliness should be there. We keep her in a clean environment and with proper friendship. We expect the residential care to provide her with similar living conditions and a safe and secure environment.

- Mother of Ms D

Financial plan

All except one family had saved some money or assets. Five of eight families had earmarked property for the ward, and four had deposited money in a bank account. Two families had arranged for pension transfer, and one had applied for a medical insurance scheme (Niramaya). Seven wards had disability certificates to avail of social welfare benefits. One family had planned assets to ensure a regular monthly income for their ward. The following is their comprehensive financial plan:

We already have two homes that are rented out. So, the rent will be saved in her name. So, whoever looks after her, there won’t be a financial issue. At the residential center, they asked for 1.5 lakh rupees as a donation or deposit, and we have to pay nearly 10,000 Rs monthly. That amount would be available from the house rent. We also have some jewelry kept aside for this purpose. So, we can sell that and use the money as a deposit. Other than that, we have used the medical insurance scheme. Moreover, she is also getting a pension.

-Mother of Ms A

Employment/occupation plan

Two of the eight families planned to set up a business to support their ward’s employment. The following is a father’s plan for his son:

I have a property in my hometown. I will construct a new building with two portions for a commercial Shop and a living accommodation at the top. And then he can look after the business, and I can be there.

- Father of Mr N

Two families expected residential care centers to schedule daily activities, including opportunities for skill-building and social interaction.

Parents who couldn’t identify a guardian felt God would show the way at an appropriate time. When their financial situation permitted, parents also planned for their wards’ future residence and how they would spend their time.

DISCUSSION

We interviewed the parents of four men and four women with mild ID, regarding their future caregiving concerns and plans. Future caregiving concerns and plans did not differ between the father and the mother. Parents were aware that others would not care for their ward as they would. All parents wanted their wards to be occupied during the day. As no formal system addresses future care concerns, each parent planned accordingly, based on their understanding, resources, and life priorities.

Considering mild ID, the wards are capable of being independent in daily living. However, some parents reported that they still assisted their wards in activities of daily living. In our clinical experience, to promote independence, parents need to overcome their anxiety and encourage the ward to move out of their ‘secure base.’ This involves traveling independently, interacting with peers, working, handling money, and assuming responsibilities.

In our clinical experience, the ward’s temperament influences the likelihood that another person will assume caregiving responsibilities. A well-behaved person is more likely to be cared for than someone with behavioral problems. This is one among several factors that influence the social perception of the ward among neurotypical siblings, family, friends, and neighbors, who may offer support or distance themselves from the parents and the ward.

As in previous studies,[17] families with limited English proficiency lacked awareness of ID. English language proficiency might be a marker for general empowerment, encompassing socioeconomic circumstances and available opportunities.

Primary caregiving arrangements

All mothers interviewed shouldered the primary responsibility for caregiving. This is in line with previous reports that mothers are the primary caregivers for special needs children and often sacrifice their careers.[1819]

As in high-income countries, parents in our sample also preferred a family member, typically a neurotypical sibling, to assume caregiving roles when they would not be able to do so.[2021] Parental concerns were alleviated when they perceived good social support, and the neurotypical sibling shared a good relationship with the ward. After their lifetime, most families prefer that the ward remain in the family home with assistance from relatives or paid caregivers, while fewer opt for relocation to a sibling’s home or a residential care center.[22]

When parents discuss their future care expectations and plan with the identified guardian, or when they write a formal document, it is referred to as an explicit plan. When there is no open discussion or formal documentation, it is an implicit plan.[23] Most parents who had identified a guardian did not have an explicit plan in place. In our clinical experience, this phenomenon occurs for various reasons: the anxiety it precipitates in their minds, the belief that it is a bad omen, the trust in the identified guardian, and the realization that the future is not in their control.

Only one family had applied for a legal guardianship certificate. Guardianship is a matter of trust, and everything boils down to the guardian’s values. The local-level committee will act only if some misappropriation is brought to their notice. In our clinical experience, families apply for guardianship certificates either to receive the parents’ pension after their demise or to bequeath assets to the ward. Most parents don’t apply for guardianship because of a lack of awareness or the challenges involved in the tedious, time-consuming procedures.

Gender and cultural influences

Previous studies have documented that females are more likely to take on caregiving roles if they share a strong bond with their sibling with disabilities, live in proximity, or are the sole neurotypical sibling.[21]

The parents’ future care plan for their wards must be interpreted within the cultural and generational context of Indian families. The parents’ generation witnessed more gender inequality. The husband made decisions, and the wife was expected to comply. The woman was the caregiver/nurturer. The woman was expected to care for her spouse for the rest of her life, whereas the man was not necessarily expected to do the same. The male is expected to shoulder the responsibilities of female siblings, who, after their marriage, were considered part of another family. All parents of male wards considered marriage as a future care plan, which wasn’t the case with parents of female wards (except for one family where their son-in-law abandoned their daughter after marriage; the ward had a 3-year-old son from the marriage, and her parents cared for both). They didn’t want to trouble her other siblings (all females). Instead, they expected the son to grow up and care for his mother.

Even today, Indian families tend to be cautious about ensuring the safety and security of their female child, who may be highly accomplished. All parents of female wards in the present study cited safety as a critical factor in their future care plan.

Over the decades, nuclear families have become more common in India. Informal support is dwindling, and this affects caregiving and guardianship decisions. Migration of siblings within and outside the country for livelihood is also increasing. Furthermore, siblings living abroad are not eligible to serve as legal guardians under current laws and policies. Even if the siblings were supportive, the person with ID may find it challenging to adapt to a different part of the country with a different language, food, and culture without a structured transition plan. Service providers and support systems must develop workable solutions for the evolving situation.

Financial considerations

Most Indian parents (of neurotypical children, too) prefer the next generation to be financially better-off. If the ward is not a financial or emotional burden, someone is likely to assume guardianship responsibilities.

In the present study, financially secure families prioritized safety over earning an income. Families who struggled to meet their daily needs wished that the ward could earn income to help them immediately, as well as increase the likelihood that someone would assume caregiving responsibilities.

Some financially better-off parents considered residential rehabilitation centers to ensure an active social life for their ward and facilitate the neurotypical sibling’s life without the burden of additional responsibilities. Unfortunately, most Indian parents don’t have this luxury. Typically, people who can’t afford private healthcare (perceived as superior to the government system) come to a government hospital. Many poor parents who weathered challenges to care for their wards are terrified of the future. They can only hope that the Almighty will protect their ward after their lifetime.[24]

Clinical and policy implications

Parents of children with special needs are advised to plan as early as possible, ideally spanning two generations, that is, life after their retirement and the entire lifespan of their child, from the time of diagnosis.[232526] In our clinical experience, parents often take time to come to terms with their child’s condition and ‘hope against hope’ to find some medication or other interventions that can reverse it. Parents will benefit from interacting with other parents who have experienced similar challenges.

Government programs, such as Gharaunda, that offer long-term residential care options are grossly insufficient and inequitably distributed. There is a need to increase Government funding and ensure that residential care centers are adequately funded, with sufficient monitoring mechanisms in place nationwide.

Only one family had applied for a legal guardianship certificate, indicating a lack of awareness and bureaucratic hurdles. Legal guardianship is relevant only when the family has assets to bequeath. The healthcare and rehabilitation sectors must collaborate closely to support families throughout their lifespan, addressing locally relevant domains outlined in the World Health Organization’s Community-Based Rehabilitation Matrix.[27]

STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS

The strengths include recruiting both parents and an equal number of males and females with ID, thereby reducing gender bias. Parents were screened for depression at the time of recruitment, thus potentially reducing the effects of caregivers’ burden or mental health on their felt needs. The interview guide assessed various aspects of parental concerns and plans.

The main limitation is that most participants were recruited from a tertiary care center in a cosmopolitan metro (Bengaluru), which offers rehabilitation services and guidance on future caregiving. While future care concerns and issues may be similar, the social realities of the parents interviewed across different parts of the country will affect the findings.

DIRECTIONS FOR FUTURE RESEARCH

Future care concerns and plans need to be studied across different parts of the country (including smaller cities and rural areas, where family and informal support systems are preserved) to understand the social realities and how systems need to adapt to local needs. It is likely that future care concerns and plans will be different.

Longitudinal studies can track the evolution of caregiving plans over time.

‘Practice-based evidence’ from parents of children with special needs and professionals who work with them needs to be documented to inform policies that are realistic and responsive to local realities.

Take-home message

Future caregiving is a social issue and requires a multipronged approach involving all stakeholders. This includes habilitation efforts to support the ward, family support programs to help parents gain perspective, disability certification, formalize guardianship plans, support for early financial planning, and policy reforms to facilitate easier access to care services. Parents need guidance to come to terms with their ward’s abilities and limitations, understand the social support around them, and other available options to make informed decisions and plans.
