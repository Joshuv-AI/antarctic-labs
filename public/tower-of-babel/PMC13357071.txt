DDR1 in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma: bioinformatics discovery and experimental validation
Authors: Yamen Ahmad Muad, Linxiang Zhang, Weiyan Peng, Lin Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEsophageal squamous cell carcinoma accounts for almost 90% of all esophageal cancer cases. Recent progress in understanding tumor biology, early diagnosis, and surgical techniques has contributed to the improvements in incidence and mortality. However, the discovery of novel biomarkers remains essential for further reducing disease burden and improving clinical outcomes. The study focuses on investigating the function of DDR1 and its potential clinical utility as a biomarker in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC).MethodsFive gene expression datasets were included in the study, and a meta-analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was conducted. Genes identified by three feature selection algorithms were intersected and used for predictive modeling and experimental validation. Clinical validation included reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) on 40 paired ESCC tumors and adjacent tissues. TE-7 ESCC cells were transfected with anti-miR-199a-5p or pre-miR-199a-5p and analyzed by RT-qPCR and western blotting.ResultsDDR1 was significantly upregulated across datasets [pooled average log fold change (FC) =1.06, pooled P<0.05]. A generalized linear model trained using the gene exhibited good performance in classifying tumor and non-tumor samples across four external validation sets. In 40 paired clinical samples, RT-qPCR revealed significantly higher DDR1 and lower miR-199a-5p levels in tumors compared to non-tumor samples (both P<0.001). Anti-miR-199a-5p produced a significant reduction in miR-199a-5p (P=0.041) and a significant increase in DDR1 (P=0.01). MiR-199a-5p inhibition increased DDR1, STAT3, pSTAT3, mesenchymal markers [N-cadherin (CDH2), vimentin (VIM)], and decreased epithelial marker [E-cadherin (CDH1)]. The opposite pattern was observed following miR-199a-5p overexpression (all P<0.05).ConclusionsDDR1 is a candidate biomarker for ESCC. Modulating miR-199a-5p alters DDR1, STAT3 activation, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) marker profiles, making miR-199a-5p/DDR1/STAT3/EMT pathway a potential therapeutic target in ESCC.

Highlight box

Key findings

• DDR1 was identified as a candidate biomarker for esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) which was significantly upregulated in five gene expression datasets (pooled sample size: 84 ESCC tumor samples and 84 adjacent non-tumor samples). A machine-learning model trained solely on DDR1 demonstrated good performance. DDR1 upregulation was validated in 40 paired human ESCC tissues and functionally confirmed in TE-7 cancer cells, where loss of miR-199a-5p was associated with increased DDR1 expression, activated STAT3 signaling, and promoted an epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) phenotype.

What is known and what is new?

• There are no reliable molecular biomarkers for diagnosis of ESCC. DDR1 has been implicated in tumor progression and EMT in other malignancies. In addition, miR-199a-5p is recognized as a tumor-suppressive microRNA in several cancer types. However, their relevance to ESCC has remained unclear.

• This manuscript identifies DDR1 as a potential biomarker in ESCC and confirms that DDR1 is regulated by miR-199a-5p in this disease. It further demonstrates that dysregulation of the miR-199a-5p/DDR1 is associated with STAT3 activation and EMT.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• DDR1 merits further validations as a diagnostic biomarker in ESCC. Future studies can explore DDR1 as a stratification biomarker and assess whether DDR1 levels predict stage, treatment response, or outcomes. Finally, the miR-199a-5p/DDR1/STAT3 axis represents a potential therapeutic target.

Introduction

Esophageal cancer accounted for 2.6% of new cancer cases and 4.6% of all cancer-related deaths, making it the eleventh most common cancer (~511,000 new cases) and the seventh most common cause of cancer death (~445,000 deaths) in 2022 (1). This represents a modest improvement compared to earlier years: in both 2018 and 2020, esophageal cancer ranked seventh in incidence and sixth in mortality, with reported new cases and deaths higher by approximately 60,000 to 100,000 (2,3). Esophageal cancer has consistently been more commonly found in men, with up to 4 times the incidence compared to that in women (4). Two histologic subtypes of esophageal cancer predominate globally: esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) and adenocarcinoma. These subtypes differ biologically in ways that affect treatment strategies and drug development (5). ESCC is the most common type throughout the world and accounts for almost 90% of all esophageal cancer cases. However, in Western countries, including the USA, UK, and Australia, adenocarcinoma is more common, which is believed to be attributed to decreasing tobacco and alcohol use but increasing obesity rates (4,6,7). Prevention strategies focus on reducing exposure to these and other risk factors, encouraging healthier diets, and addressing precancerous conditions through endoscopic interventions (8).

Early detection has improved through advancements in endoscopic technology, imaging techniques, and emerging non-invasive approaches such as cell sampling devices and liquid biopsies, which allow identification of high-risk individuals before cancer fully develops (8,9). Treatment of esophageal cancer depends on disease stage, ranging from endoscopic resection for early lesions to minimally invasive or open esophagectomy, often combined with chemoradiotherapy for more advanced tumors (9). Recent progress in understanding tumor biology, early diagnosis, and surgical techniques has contributed to the improvements in incidence and mortality seen in recent years. Further advancements could be achieved by improving risk prediction, expanding targeted screening programs, and including molecular profiling and biomarker-based strategies to identify high-risk groups (10,11). Advances in machine learning, genomics, and precision medicine hold promise for improving early detection, guiding personalized therapies, and ultimately reducing the global burden of esophageal cancer (9,11).

Multiple genomic studies have revealed that ESCC is highly heterogeneous, marked by pervasive genomic instability, frequent TP53 mutations, etc. Findings provided by genomic studies are driving the search for novel biomarkers or tissue biomarkers to enable earlier diagnosis and risk stratification (12). Recently, considerable scientific attention has been directed toward microRNAs (miRNAs) and their involvement in cancer. miRNAs are abundant endogenous small RNAs ~20–25 nucleotides in length that post-transcriptionally regulate broad gene networks (13). They regulate gene expression in two main ways: by direct cleavage of target messenger RNAs (mRNAs) and by inhibiting translation through binding the 3’ untranslated regions (UTRs) of mRNAs. miRNAs act as regulators of cell cycle, cell proliferation, and apoptosis (14). They modulate these processes by targeting key regulators such as cyclins, CDKs, Rb, E2F, c-Myc, and members of the Bcl-2 family, and thereby influence tumor initiation and growth (15). Altered miRNA expression is common in esophageal cancer and can either downregulate tumor suppressors or suppress oncogenes, making miRNA profiles useful as diagnostic biomarkers across disease stages (16-19). Some miRNAs function as oncogenic drivers (oncomiRs) by promoting proliferation and inhibiting apoptosis, while others act as tumor suppressors by inhibiting cell cycle progression and self-renewal (20,21). Moreover, miRNAs regulate tumor invasion and metastasis by affecting angiogenesis, cell adhesion, cell migration, etc. Specifically, they modulate transcription factors, adhesion molecules [e.g., E-cadherin (CDH1)], and actin-binding proteins (15). Taken together, as miRNA expression differs by tissue and stage, and as miRNAs are detectable in circulation, they hold promise both as biomarkers for early detection and as targets for personalized medicine. In this study, we focus on DDR1 and miR-199a-5p as potential biomarkers and mediators in ESCC. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-0437/rc).

Methods

Data collection

The flowchart of the study is shown in Figure 1. Datasets for in silico analysis were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) repository. Five mRNA datasets that contained expression data from ESCC tumors and adjacent non-tumor tissues were obtained: GSE100942 containing 4 normal and 4 tumor samples, GSE161533—28 normal and 28 tumor samples, GSE17351—5 normal and 5 tumor samples, GSE20347—17 normal and 17 tumor samples, and GSE38129—30 normal and 30 tumor samples. The platform for GSE100942, GSE161533, and GSE17351 is GPL570, whereas GSE20347 and GSE38129 are based on the GPL571 platform. All datasets were subjected to RMA normalization available within the oligo package. Datasets were aligned, and genes with any missing values were excluded.

Flowchart of the study. DT, decision tree; GLM, generalized linear model; LASSO, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator; RF, random forest; RFC, random forest classifier; SVM, support vector machine.

Screening and bioinformatics analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

Differential expression analysis was performed for all datasets separately with an FDR-adjusted P value of 0.05 as a cutoff value. Next, a meta-analysis of DEGs with a random effect model was performed with the following cut-off values: adjusted P value <0.05 and |average log fold change (FC)| >1.0. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis was conducted to identify enriched pathways.

The identified DEGs were then analyzed using three feature selection tools [least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), random forest classifier (RFC), and support vector machines (SVM)] with the largest dataset (GSE38129) selected as a training set. These feature selection tools were selected to mainly reduce the dimensionality of the dataset and identify a subset of genes with the highest predictive performance. Next, to further minimize the number of genes for subsequent analysis and focus only on the most robust ones, the results selected by feature selection tools were intersected. As each feature selection tool has its own strengths and limitations, this approach may help with identifying the most relevant genes (22). The selected gene (DDR1) was used to train three machine learning algorithms [generalized linear model (GLM), random forest (RF), and decision tree (DT)—available within the caret package] with 5-fold cross-validation. The performance of machine learning models was assessed using the area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity.

We queried TargetScanHuman and miRDB for hsa-miR-199a-5p and DDR1 interactions under default settings (23). From TargetScan, we recorded the predicted 3’UTR binding-site positions for miR-199a-5p within DDR1. In addition, a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was built using stringApp available within the CytoScape with the default minimum required interaction score (0.400, “medium” confidence). The network was restricted to the input proteins only (no additional interactors), with edges displayed by the combined interaction score (24).

Cell culture, clinical tissue collection, and reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

The ESCC cell line TE-7 (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) was maintained in RPMI-1640 medium as previously described for ESCC cell lines (25). Fresh esophageal tumor tissues and matched adjacent non-tumor tissues were obtained at surgery from patients treated in the Department of Cardiothoracic Surgery at The First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (n=40 per group). All patients had a pathologically confirmed ESCC. Patients with adenocarcinoma and patients who underwent neoadjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy were not included in the study. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (No. k2023-572). Written informed consent was obtained from each participant.

Total RNA was extracted from tissues using TRIzol reagent (Molecular Research Center, Cincinnati, OH, USA) per the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration was measured by spectrophotometry, and RNA was stored at −80 ℃ until use. RNA was reverse-transcribed with the Reverse Transcription System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Semi-quantitative PCR was performed with GoTaq DNA polymerase (Promega) under conditions previously described (26). Real-time PCR was performed using SYBR Green chemistry on an Applied Biosystems 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) served as the endogenous control for DDR1 mRNA, and U6 small nuclear RNA was used for miR-199a-5p. Primer sequences were: DDR1 forward 5'-CCGACTGGTTCGCTTCTACC-3', reverse 5'-CGGTGTAAGACAGGAGTCCATC-3'; GAPDH forward 5'-TGTGGGCATCAATGGATTTGG-3', reverse 5'-ACACCATGTATTCCGGGTCAAT-3'. Reactions were run in technical triplicate with no-template and no-RT controls. Specificity was confirmed by melt-curve analysis (SYBR) and inspection of amplification plots. 2−ΔΔCt method was used for data analysis (27). For functional validation in cells, TE-7 cultures were transfected with anti-miR-199a-5p (inhibitor) or a negative-control oligonucleotide. At the indicated time after transfection, total RNA was isolated, and miR-199a-5p and DDR1 mRNA levels were quantified as above.

Western blotting

TE-7 cells were transfected with pre-miR-199a-5p (mimic), pre-con (mimic control), anti-miR-199a-5p (inhibitor), or anti-con (inhibitor control). The experiment was done in triplicate. At the indicated time after transfection, cells (or tissues, where applicable) were lysed on ice in Racial and Identity Profiling Act (RIPA) buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation, quantified, and equal amounts of protein were resolved by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. Membranes were blocked in 5% non-fat dry milk in Phosphate-Buffered Saline with Tween-20 (PBST) for 2 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with primary antibodies against: DDR1 (CST #5583), STAT3 (CST #9139), phospho-STAT3 (Tyr705; CST #9145), CDH1 (CST #3195), N-cadherin (CDH2; CST #131116), and vimentin (VIM; CST #574); β-actin was used as the loading control. After washing, membranes were incubated with the appropriate horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (manufacturer-recommended dilutions; primary 1:1,000, secondary 1:3,000) and developed using ECL Plus chemiluminescent substrate (RPN2132, GE Healthcare, Little Chalfont, Buckinghamshire, UK). Bands were imaged and quantified by densitometry; target proteins were normalized to β-actin, and p-STAT3 was additionally expressed relative to total STAT3. Data are presented relative to the matched control group (set to 1.0).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted in R using the rstatix package. Normality and variance assumptions were assessed prior to analysis, and appropriate methods were applied for two-group comparisons. Two-sided P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Meta-analysis of DEGs

A total of 725 DEGs were screened in GSE17351, 4,633 in GSE20347, 4,181 in GSE38129, 1 in GSE100942, and 5,389 in GSE161533. After conducting a meta-analysis based on a random effects model of the screened DEGs, a total of 883 genes were identified, among which 444 were significantly upregulated and 439 were significantly downregulated (available online: https://cdn.amegroups.cn/static/public/tcr-2026-0437-1.pdf). DEGs were significantly enriched in protein digestion and absorption (16 hits, P=0.04), drug metabolism (14 hits, P=0.048), and p53 signaling pathways (13 hits, P=0.049) (Figure 2A).

Bioinformatics analysis of DDR1

To further identify candidate biomarkers, three feature selection algorithms (LASSO, RFC, and SVM) were applied to the largest dataset (GSE38129). LASSO identified 13 genes, RFC identified 23 genes, and SVM identified 37 genes as the most discriminative features for classifying tumor and control samples. Among them, DDR1 was consistently selected across all feature selection approaches and was therefore chosen for further validation (Figure 2B). DDR1 was upregulated in all datasets, but significant differences between tumor and adjacent non-cancerous samples were observed only in GSE161533 and GSE38129 datasets (Figure 3A-3E). It had an average logFC of 1.06 (pooled P value <0.05).

Next, DDR1 expression was used to train three machine-learning models: GLM, RF, and DT. Overall, the GLM model demonstrated the highest performance and outperformed the other two models, achieving an AUC of 1.0 (sensitivity =1.0, specificity =1.0) in GSE100942, AUC of 0.75 (sensitivity =0.75, specificity =0.79) in GSE161533, AUC of 0.88 (sensitivity =0.8, specificity =1.0) in GSE17351, and AUC of 0.67 (sensitivity =0.76, specificity =0.65) in GSE20347 (Figure 3F-3I). Thus, the GLM model trained on DDR1 showed good performance in classifying cancerous and non-cancerous adjacent tissues across all four external validation datasets.

Clinical validation of expression of DDR1 and miR-199a-5p

In 40 paired ESCC tumors and matched adjacent non-tumor tissues, RT-qPCR revealed significantly higher levels of DDR1 and significantly lower levels of miR-199a-5p compared to adjacent non-cancerous tissues (both P value <0.001; Figure 4A,4B). To validate that miR-199a-5p targets DDR1, TE-7 ESCC cells were transfected with an anti-miR-199a-5p inhibitor or a matched negative control, and transcripts were quantified by RT-qPCR. Anti-miR-199a-5p treatment resulted in a significant reduction and a significant increase in intracellular miR-199a-5p levels (P=0.041) and DDR1 (P=0.01) levels, respectively (Figure 4C).

MiR-199a-5p/DDR1 regulate STAT3 and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) biomarkers

The PPI network was constructed around DDR1 and STAT3. The analysis revealed DDR1 and STAT3 within a shared interaction network that included canonical EMT markers such as CDH1, CDH2, and VIM (Figure 4D).

TE-7 cells were transfected with pre-miR-199a-5p (mimic), pre-con, anti-miR-199a-5p (inhibitor), or anti-con, and protein expression was analyzed by Western blot (Figure 4E). miR-199a-5p inhibition (anti-miR-199a-5p treatment) led to higher levels of DDR1, STAT3, and p-STAT3/STAT3, together with a shift in EMT axis (CDH2 and VIM increased, and CDH1 decreased), compared to anti-con. Conversely, miR-199a-5p upregulation (pre-miR-199a-5p treatment) produced the opposite results compared to pre-con characterized by decreased levels of DDR1, STAT3, p-STAT3/STAT3, CDH2, and VIM, also increased levels of CDH1.

Discussion

In this study, DDR1 was identified across multiple selection methods and was therefore used for further in silico analysis and experimental validation. DDR1 is a collagen-activated receptor tyrosine kinase mainly involved in cell adhesion, survival, proliferation, and migration (28,29). Its overexpression has been reported across multiple cancers, including non-small cell lung cancer, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) (30-32). In recent years, DDR1 has been investigated in different tumors. For instance, a recent study revealed that DDR1 interacts with the Hippo pathway to promote HCC, and that targeting DDR1 and YAP could be a potential therapeutic strategy to combat HCC (33). However, its role in ESCC is unclear.

In the present study, we conducted a meta-analysis of DEGs and found that DDR1 is significantly overexpressed in ESCC tissues compared to normal adjacent tissues (average logFC =1.06, pooled P<0.05). Expression levels of DDR1 were not significantly different between tumor and non-tumor adjacent tissues in three small datasets (GSE100942, GSE17351, and GSE20347), but were significantly different in two larger datasets (GSE161533 and GSE38129). This discrepancy is likely attributable to the small sample size and thus limited statistical power. Clinical validation in our independent cohort of 40 paired patient samples confirmed that DDR1 mRNA is significantly elevated in ESCC tumors compared to matched adjacent tissues. Furthermore, good predictive performance of the GLM model (GSE100942 AUC: 1.0, GSE17351 AUC: 0.88, GSE161533 AUC: 0.75, GSE20347 AUC: 0.67) using only DDR1 expression to classify tumor samples and adjacent control samples demonstrated its potential diagnostic utility.

PPI analysis placed DDR1 and STAT3 within a shared network that also included canonical EMT markers (CDH1, CDH2, VIM), supporting a biologically plausible association between DDR1, STAT3, and EMT-related markers. Although the interaction network derived from STRING does not establish directionality or causality, it highlights known and predicted associations among DDR1, STAT3, and EMT-related proteins. This finding is consistent with experimental work in other tumor types showing DDR1 promotes invasion via EMT (34). EMT is a cellular program in which epithelial cells lose polarity and junctions and acquire mesenchymal traits, enhancing motility and invasiveness. In cancer, the EMT axis has been reported to be involved in promoting cell invasion and metastasis (35-37).

miR-199a-5p was selected based on bioinformatic analysis, biological relevance and prior literature evidence. First, in TargetScanHuman and miRDB identified miR-199a-5p as an upstream regulator of DDR1. Second, among candidate miRNAs predicted to target DDR1, miR-199a-5p has been implicated in cancer-related processes, which are also linked to DDR1 function. Third, prior studies have demonstrated dysregulation of miR-199a-5p in human cancers (38-42). It was shown to act as a tumor-suppressive RNA in human cancers, including colorectal cancer, glioma, and HCC, but findings of a recent report demonstrated that miR-199a-5p acts as an oncogene in osteosarcoma (40,41,43,44) In ESCC studies, miR-199a-5p has been reported as a downregulated tumor-suppressive miRNA that inhibits key oncogenic pathways by directly targeting MAP3K11, Jun-B, and EEPD1. Restoration of miR-199a-5p suppresses ESCC cell growth, whereas its suppression is associated with increased tumor radioresistance (45-47). Thus, miR-199a-5p was a suitable candidate for experimental validation for our study.

Functional experiments in TE-7 cells supported in silico predictions of a regulatory relationship between miR-199a-5p and DDR1. Inhibition of miR-199a-5p resulted in a significant increase in DDR1 mRNA and higher levels of (p)STAT3, accompanied by an EMT shift (reduced CDH1 and increased CDH2 and VIM). Conversely, miR-199a-5p overexpression reduced DDR1, p-STAT3/STAT3, and mesenchymal markers (VIM and CDH2) while increasing epithelial marker (CDH1). These data suggest that miR-199a-5p may function as a tumor-suppressive miRNA in ESCC and that its expression is inversely associated with DDR1 levels, STAT3 activation, and EMT-related marker expression. Prior studies in other cancers likewise report targeting of DDR1 by miR-199a-5p. A study on different HCC cell lines found that miR-199a-5p can inhibit the invasion of HepG2 hepatoma cells by directly targeting DDR1. The downregulation of miR-199a-5p contributes to HCC progression by promoting DDR1 activity, which in turn results in tumor cell invasion and potentially leads to more aggressive disease (43). In osteosarcoma, miR-199a-5p influences osteosarcoma progression by promoting cell proliferation and tumor growth through downregulating PIAS3 and p27 (41). These findings suggest that miR-199a-5p and its ability to regulate DDR1 expression could be a promising target for developing new therapeutic strategies and improving prognostic predictions in various cancers. Moreover, recent studies suggest that DDR1 promotes migration and invasion of bladder, gastric, and cervical cancer cells, at least in part by activating the EMT pathway (34,48,49). Thus, our data are consistent with a model in which reduced miR-199a-5p expression is associated with increased DDR1 expression, accompanied by enhanced STAT3 signaling and EMT-like changes in ESCC cells.

There are several established serum biomarkers for ESCC, such as squamous cell carcinoma antigen (SCC-Ag), carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), etc. (50-56). Notably, several studies have shown that combining several biomarkers can improve diagnostic performance and AUC for ESCC compared with single markers alone (50,51). In parallel, DDR1 presents several potential advantages as a candidate biomarker for ESCC. Emerging evidence across various carcinomas indicates associations between DDR1 upregulation and aggressive phenotypes, poor prognosis, as well as advanced tumor stage (32,57-59). Our study showed that models trained on DDR1 exhibited good predictive performance across external validation sets in identifying ESCC. Nevertheless, there are several limitations that limit its application. First, unlike conventionally used serum biomarkers, DDR1 lacks clinically validated cutoff values and detection methods. Second, its specificity for ESCC is unclear due to the fact that, as mentioned earlier, DDR1 is dysregulated in various tumors. Third, there is a scarcity of research on the involvement of DDR1 in ESCC. Specifically, the correlation between DDR1 and other known serum biomarkers for ESCC is lacking. The association between DDR1 expression and ESCC stage or prognosis also warrants further investigation. Therefore, DDR1 is a promising biomarker for ESCC, but more research, including large-scale and prospective clinical studies, is required to validate its diagnostic and prognostic utility in ESCC.

Our study has the following limitations: (I) some public datasets were relatively small, and our functional work relied on a single ESCC cell line (TE-7); (II) clinical validation was based on a cohort from a single center, with 40 paired samples; (III) only gene expression was evaluated without adjusting for important covariates, such as survival data, lifestyle factors, existing comorbidities, and treatment; (IV) although our RT-qPCR and western blot results are consistent and supported with previous reports, definitive proof of direct targeting in ESCC can be provided by luciferase reporter assays, mutation of the predicted miR-199a-5p binding sites in the DDR1 3’UTR, complementary in vivo studies, etc.

Conclusions

DDR1 is significantly upregulated in tumors compared to adjacent non-cancerous tissues. DDR1 was also shown to have a potential diagnostic utility in classifying tumors as demonstrated by good predictive performance across external validation sets. Moreover, miR-199a-5p expression shows an inverse relationship with DDR1 expression in ESCC samples and cell lines. Loss of miR-199a-5p is associated with DDR1 upregulation, STAT3 activation, and EMT changes. These observations support a tumor-suppressive role for miR-199a-5p mediated in part via repression of DDR1. Nonetheless, more research is required to validate the diagnostic and prognostic utility of DDR1 in ESCC. Moreover, further exploration is needed to uncover the underlying molecular mechanisms of miR-199a-5p’s regulation of DDR1, STAT3 activation, and the EMT marker profile.
