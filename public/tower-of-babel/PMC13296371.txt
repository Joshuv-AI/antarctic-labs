Factors Influencing Upper-limb Spasticity in Post-stroke Patients during Convalescent Rehabilitation: A Single-center Retrospective Study
Authors: Hiroaki Tamashiro, Naoki Urushidani, Keiko Okita, Takatsugu Okamoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectives:This study characterized the joint-specific time course of upper-limb spasticity to identify predictors of subsequent spasticity progression in post-stroke patients undergoing inpatient rehabilitation in a convalescent (Kaifukuki) rehabilitation ward (KRW).Methods:This single-center retrospective cohort study included 76 post-stroke patients with upper-limb paresis admitted to a KRW between January and December 2020. Spasticity of the elbow, wrist, and finger flexors was assessed monthly using the Modified Ashworth Scale (MAS) at 1–4 months after admission. Upper-limb motor function and neurological findings, including tendon reflexes, were evaluated at admission. Motor recovery was classified using the Brunnstrom recovery stage (BRS). Multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to identify factors associated with increased finger flexor spasticity (MAS ≥1+) at discharge.Results:Upper-limb spasticity increased 2 months after admission, with earlier increases observed in distal joints when compared with proximal joints. By discharge, approximately one-third of patients demonstrated increased spasticity. At 3 months after admission, increased spasticity was most frequent in patients with BRS3 (upper limb, 59%; hand/fingers, 84%). Multivariate analysis revealed that lower Fugl-Meyer Assessment scores of the upper extremity and greater tendon reflex hyperreflexia at admission were independently associated with increased finger flexor spasticity at discharge.Conclusions:Upper-limb spasticity tended to increase earlier in distal joints, particularly in the wrist and finger flexors, than in the proximal joint during subacute inpatient rehabilitation. Severe upper-limb paresis and tendon reflex hyperreflexia at admission may be risk factors for worsening finger spasticity.

INTRODUCTION

In recent years, advancements in clinical neurophysiology have led to the development of new approaches for rehabilitation in post-stroke patients with upper-limb paresis. Previous research has shown that neurorehabilitation can lead to plastic changes in the central nervous system, specifically affecting the dominant regions of the cerebral cortex.1) Consequently, the traditional rehabilitation approach, which focused on compensatory movements of the non-paretic upper limb, has evolved into a more novel rehabilitation strategy aimed at enhancing the functional recovery of the paretic upper limb in activities of daily living (ADL).

Duncan et al.2) reported that post-stroke upper-limb paresis often reaches a plateau approximately 6 months after stroke onset. Hence, upper-limb paresis should be adequately addressed at an earlier stage. Recently, various new approaches have been actively employed during the subacute phase, because timely and appropriate rehabilitation can improve upper-limb paresis. However, the presence of spasticity often complicates the application of intensive rehabilitation strategies aimed at improving upper-limb paresis.

Spasticity is a movement disorder resulting from upper motor neuron syndrome caused by conditions such as stroke or spinal cord injury and is characterized by a velocity-dependent increase in muscle tone caused by hyperexcitability of the stretch reflex, with exaggerated tendon reflexes.3,4) The Modified Ashworth Scale (MAS),5) which is widely used in clinical practice, does not directly assess the degree of tendon reflex excitability, but instead quantifies resistance to passive movement. In recent years, spasticity has been increasingly recognized as comprising both reflex components, such as stretch reflex hyperexcitability, and non-reflex components, represented by viscoelastic changes in muscles, tendons, and connective tissues.6)

According to recent studies, the prevalence of spasticity is 25.3% in post-stroke patients, 26.7% after the first-ever stroke, and 39.5% after the first-ever stroke with associated paresis.7) Post-stroke spasticity can disrupt smooth joint movement, resulting in muscle shortening and a decreased range of joint motion, which can induce pain and interfere with rehabilitation. In particular, upper-limb spasticity often leads to the paretic side adopting a flexed position of the elbow, wrist, and finger joints, along with a rotated position of the forearm, because of associated reactions during movement of the non-paretic side.8) Therefore, given that upper-limb spasticity is likely to affect factors related to ADL improvement, it is imperative to anticipate and manage the onset of spasticity effectively.

Some studies have reported that the incidence of upper-limb spasticity in the subacute stage ranges from 23% to 43%.9,10,11) However, apart from severe upper-limb paresis and sensory impairment, predictors of spasticity development remain unclear.7) Furthermore, it has been reported that stretch reflex hyperexcitability does not necessarily correlate with resistance to passive muscle stretch,12) indicating that the relationship between clinical assessment of spasticity and its underlying neurophysiological mechanisms has not been fully elucidated.

To the best of our knowledge, no reports have compared the temporal changes in spasticity at each joint in patients undergoing rehabilitation in the subacute stage, nor have they mentioned the timing at which spasticity increases. Identifying these factors would enable early therapeutic interventions. Consequently, improving spasticity management would lead to better outcomes in upper-limb function. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to predict the severity and timing of spasticity development over time in patients with post-stroke upper-limb paresis based on clinical findings observed during hospitalization in a convalescent (Kaifukuki) rehabilitation ward (KRW). KRWs are interdisciplinary post-acute rehabilitation units that have been integrated into the Japanese medical insurance system since 2000, providing a clinical setting in which patients in the subacute phase receive high-frequency and high-intensity standardized inpatient rehabilitation.13)

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Design

This was a single-center retrospective cohort study, the reporting of which followed the STROBE guidelines. We investigated temporal changes in spasticity at each joint in patients with post-stroke upper-limb paresis who were admitted to the KRW of our hospital. We also explored the factors influencing the development of upper-limb spasticity.

Ethics Approval

The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Patient data were de-identified, and the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nishi-Hiroshima Rehabilitation Hospital (approval number: N0.10045–1). The requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study.

Participants and Settings

In this study, we recruited 283 consecutive inpatients with post-stroke upper-limb paresis who were admitted to the KRW of Nishi-Hiroshima Rehabilitation Hospital between January and December 2020. The study included 76 patients (55 men, 72%; 21 women, 28%) who completed the upper-limb functional assessment described below up to 4 months after admission. No specific exclusion criteria were applied. KRWs provide intensive inpatient rehabilitation (physical, occupational, and speech therapies) for subacute patients requiring assistance with ADL after acute hospitalization. As per the medical system, for stroke patients, rehabilitation therapy is limited to 3 h per day for a maximum of 150 days.14) Once rehabilitation goals are achieved and a home or institutional care service is prepared, the physiatrist authorizes the patient’s discharge from the KRW.

Sample Size Calculation

Based on previous literature,7) we hypothesized that spasticity would increase approximately 3 to 6 months post-stroke onset. The sample size was determined based on previous studies that reported the incidence of spasticity in subacute stroke patients.15) According to the literature, the incidence of spasticity in the early phase post-stroke is 20%, whereas that from 3 to 6 months post-stroke is 42%. The required sample size was calculated assuming a significance level (α) of 0.05 and a power (1−β) of 0.8. Based on these parameters, the calculated sample size was at least 30 subjects. Considering a dropout rate of 20%, we calculated that an analysis would require at least 38 patients. The sample size in this study met this requirement.

Evaluation of Spasticity and Motor Function in Affected Upper Limb

We evaluated spasticity in the fingers, wrist, and elbow flexors of the affected upper limb using the MAS. The Ashworth scale,5) first reported by Ashworth in 1964, is a clinical evaluation tool for spasticity, wherein the amount of resistance to passive joint movement is graded into five levels. Subsequently, Bohannon and Smith16) modified this tool into a 6-point scale, which is still widely used in clinical practice. Previous research has demonstrated the interrater reliability and usefulness of the MAS for patients with spastic paresis.17,18,19)

Based on our clinical experience, a MAS score of 1 (MAS1) has limited impact on ADL in patients during hospitalization in KRWs, with MAS scores exceeding 1+ indicating a more pronounced effect on ADL. Therefore, in this study, we referenced the definition of hypertonia from a previous study and defined increased spasticity as an MAS score of at least 1+,15) which was considered equivalent to a score of 1.5, as described by Kaji et al.20)

We used the Fugl-Meyer Assessment of the Upper Extremity (FMA-UE) and some motor and sensory items from the Stroke Impairment Assessment Set (SIAS) to assess upper-limb function.21,22) The FMA is a performance-based quantitative measure designed to assess various impairments in post-stroke patients, with assessment of upper-limb motor function comprising 33 items of measurement. The maximum possible motor performance score for the upper limb is 66 points, with each item rated by a 3-point (0, 1, 2) ordinal scale.21) The SIAS sub-items (position sense, tendon reflex, and visuospatial perception) used in this study were rated on a scale from 0 (severe impairment or absence) to 3 (normal).22) In the SIAS tendon reflex subscale, lower scores indicate greater hyperreflexia (0, marked hyperreflexia; 1, moderate hyperreflexia; 2, mild hyperreflexia; 3, normal). These evaluations of upper-limb function were conducted monthly, specifically at 1, 2, 3, and 4 months after admission.

Outcome Measures and Statistical Analyses

We investigated three factors in this study: course of upper-limb spasticity after admission, proportion of patients exhibiting increased spasticity at 3 months after admission, and prognostic factors affecting increased spasticity at discharge (4 months after admission).

Course of Upper-limb Spasticity during Hospitalization

The MAS scores for the elbow, wrist, and finger flexors were individually assessed at 1, 2, 3, and 4 months after admission. Separate statistical comparisons were made for each joint.

Proportion of Patients with Increased Spasticity at 3 Months after Admission

We examined the number of patients with hyperspasticity (MAS score ≥1+) in the elbow and finger flexors at 3 months after admission based on the Brunnstrom recovery stage (BRS) of the upper limb and hand. The BRS is a six-stage assessment tool used for evaluating recovery in stroke patients, comprising assessments for three parts: the upper extremity, hand, and lower extremity.23)

Predictive Factors Influencing Increased Spasticity at Discharge

In this study, we explored factors influencing increased spasticity (MAS score ≥1+) in finger flexors at discharge at 4 months after admission in 69 participants using multivariate analysis. Seven participants with missing data were excluded, specifically those lacking SIAS sub-items at admission (n = 3 for upper extremity position sense; n = 4 for visuospatial cognition).

Statistical Analyses

The MAS scores for each joint were compared across time points using the Friedman test, with significance recognized below 5%. We also examined changes in the proportion of patients with increased spasticity (MAS score ≥1+). Furthermore, binary logistic regression was used to calculate the adjusted odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI), with statistical significance recognized below 5%. The logistic regression model was adjusted for selected potential confounding factors as described below, and the number of explanatory variables included in the multivariate model was selected to reduce the risk of overfitting, taking into account the number of events, previous study,7) and clinical relevance. The objective variable was the spasticity of the finger flexors at discharge (4 months after admission), defined as increased (MAS ≥1+) versus not increased (MAS ≤1). The explanatory variables were stroke classification, age, FMA-UE score at admission, and the SIAS sub-items at admission (upper extremity position sense, upper extremity tendon reflexes). SPSS Statistics version 29 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) was used for the analysis.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we longitudinally examined joint-specific changes in upper-limb spasticity and its clinical predictors in patients undergoing standardized, high-frequency inpatient rehabilitation during the subacute post-stroke phase in a KRW. In particular, we focused on the relationship between resistance to passive movement assessed by the MAS and tendon reflexes, which reflect different components of spasticity. Post-stroke patients with upper-limb paresis during hospitalization in the KRW were more likely to have increased upper-limb spasticity at 2 months after admission (approximately 3 months post-onset), and approximately one-third of the patients demonstrated increased spasticity by discharge. Moreover, spasticity tended to increase slightly earlier in the distal part of the upper limb (wrist and finger flexors) than in the proximal part (elbow flexors).

Importantly, this study identified two key predictive factors for subsequent increases in spasticity during hospitalization: severe upper-limb paresis and hyperreflexia of the tendon reflex at admission. Although the association between severe upper-limb paresis and spasticity progression is consistent with findings from previous studies,7,24) the prognostic significance of tendon reflex hyperreflexia has not been previously reported. Although Lance4) initially defined spasticity as a motor disorder characterized by a velocity-dependent increase in tonic stretch reflexes with exaggerated tendon reflexes, he later emphasized spasticity as “velocity-dependent hyperactivity of the tonic stretch reflex” and distinguished it conceptually from tendon reflex hyperactivity.25) The present findings demonstrate for the first time that tendon reflex hyperreflexia in the early post-stroke period, together with severe upper-limb paresis, is a clinically meaningful predictor of subsequent increases in spasticity, providing new insight into the early pathophysiology and clinical assessment of spasticity.

Post-stroke patients with upper-limb hemiparesis often exhibit predominant impairment in the distal part of the upper extremity. Despite some degree of spontaneous recovery or rehabilitation, sustained severe dysfunction is commonly observed in the wrist and finger joints compared with the shoulder girdle and shoulder joints. Previous studies have reported severe paresis and increased spasticity in the distal joints,26) which is consistent with the present findings. This tendency has been attributed to the regional dominance of the upper limb within the motor cortex and pyramidal tracts, as well as the vascular territories supplying these regions.27) However, other studies have reported no significant differences between proximal and distal joint impairments.28) Such discrepancies may reflect differences in recovery between proximal and distal muscles related to the presence or absence of motor-evoked potentials,29) as well as differences in spasticity assessment methods. Notably, even in patients receiving sufficient inpatient rehabilitation in the KRW, muscle shortening from non-use tends to occur more readily in the distal upper limb. One possible explanation is that, although MAS is widely used in clinical practice, it evaluates resistance to passive movement and does not distinguish between neural components (e.g., stretch reflex hyperexcitability) and non-neural components (e.g., muscle viscoelasticity and shortening) contributing to resistive joint torque.30) This limitation of MAS-based assessment may partly explain why increased spasticity was observed earlier in the distal upper limb.

Patients with BRS3 were most likely to exhibit increased spasticity, with 59% showing increased spasticity in the upper limb and 84% in the hand/fingers at 3 months after admission. In patients with BRS3, flexor synergy patterns are dominant in both the upper-limb and hand, a stage at which spasticity is known to become more prominent.23) Importantly, although BRS has traditionally been used to describe recovery stages, the present findings suggest that BRS3 may also represent a clinically meaningful transition point at which the risk of spasticity progression increases, particularly in the distal upper limb. These findings indicate that patients whose motor recovery remains at the BRS3 level during the subacute phase may constitute a high-risk subgroup for early distal spasticity progression. Accordingly, incorporating BRS-based stratification into early clinical assessment may facilitate timely identification of patients who would benefit most from preventive interventions.

In patients identified as being at high risk for spasticity progression—such as those whose motor recovery remains at the BRS3 level and who exhibit early tendon reflex hyperreflexia—several controlled studies in post-stroke patients with spasticity have reported that early implementation of physical and medical interventions, including botulinum toxin type A (BoNT-A) therapy, can reduce secondary complications, particularly soft-tissue contractures and pain.31,32) Previous studies have further indicated that BoNT-A therapy administered during the early subacute phase, particularly within a few weeks to 3 months after stroke onset, is more effective in preventing or reducing severe or disabling spastic movement disorders and their secondary complications than delayed treatment in the chronic phase.33) In addition, a previous meta-analysis demonstrated the efficacy of continuous-extension orthoses for managing wrist spasticity.34) Together, these findings support the early and appropriate introduction of BoNT-A therapy and orthotic intervention, in combination with other rehabilitation strategies, for patients identified at high risk for spasticity progression.

Spasticity is influenced by multiple reflex regulatory mechanisms at the cortical, brainstem, and spinal cord levels.35) Accordingly, multiple therapeutic options targeting different mechanisms of spasticity—including BoNT-A therapy, repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation,36) and orthotics—are available. However, the effectiveness of these interventions depends largely on timely and appropriate application. Although interventions targeting each of these levels are feasible, the underlying mechanisms remain complex and are not fully understood. Based on the present findings, treatment strategies aimed at maximizing activity-level outcomes should be individualized, and patient-specific goals should be shared within the multidisciplinary rehabilitation team.

This study has some limitations. First, this was a single-center study with a relatively small sample size, which limits the generalizability of the findings. Second, spasticity was assessed using the MAS, which, although widely used in clinical practice,17,18,19) does not directly assess neurophysiological aspects of spasticity, including tendon reflex excitability. In the present study, concurrent assessment of tendon reflexes partially compensated for this limitation; however, future studies incorporating elastography,37) electrophysiological testing,36) and other objective measures capable of distinguishing neural and non-neural components of resistance to passive movement are warranted.38) Finally, although spasticity may progress over time, the observation period in this study was relatively short, and longer-term follow-up studies are needed. Because the number of events was limited, the number of explanatory variables included in the multivariate model was restricted, and the results should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Despite these limitations, this study provides novel, clinically relevant evidence from a standardized, high-frequency inpatient rehabilitation setting during the subacute post-stroke phase. By demonstrating joint-specific time courses of upper-limb spasticity and identifying severe upper-limb paresis and tendon reflex hyperreflexia at admission as early predictors of spasticity progression, the present findings highlight the clinical value of combining simple neurological examinations with longitudinal spasticity assessment. Early identification of patients at high risk for spasticity progression may facilitate timely risk stratification and support informed decision-making regarding preventive and therapeutic interventions, thereby potentially mitigating spasticity severity and promoting functional recovery.

CONCLUSION

The findings of this study suggest that increased tendon reflexes in the early post-stroke period may be associated with subsequent increases in upper-limb spasticity during subacute inpatient rehabilitation. Although previous studies have extensively documented severe paresis and early muscle hypertonia, this study is the first to highlight the prognostic importance of tendon reflex hyperreflexia assessed at admission within a standardized, high-frequency inpatient rehabilitation setting. Furthermore, the risk of increased spasticity was higher in the distal upper limb, particularly in patients with BRS3. These findings underscore the clinical value of early risk stratification based on simple neurological examinations and support timely, targeted interventions to prevent spasticity progression and optimize functional recovery.
