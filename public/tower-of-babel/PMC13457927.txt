Revisiting Total Subpectoral Breast Reconstruction: Technical Refinements for Improved Outcomes
Authors: Buse Fatma Çapkınoğlu, Ahmet Umut Yuvacı, Akın Yücel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough prepectoral direct-to-implant breast reconstruction has gained increasing popularity, it is not suitable for all patients. Subpectoral reconstruction remains necessary in selected cases, yet traditional techniques are limited by animation deformity and restricted lower-pole expansion.ObjectivesThe aim of this study was to evaluate the safety, reproducibility, and clinical outcomes of a refined, mesh-free total subpectoral reconstruction technique.MethodsA retrospective analysis was performed on 119 patients (181 breasts) undergoing immediate implant-based reconstruction using a standardized subpectoral technique. Key anatomical structures—including the inframammary ligament, serratus anterior fascia, and intercostal perforators—were preserved. Controlled fascial releases and limited superior dissection were employed to optimize implant positioning and lower-pole expansion. Outcomes included complication rates, revision surgery, and BREAST-Q scores.ResultsThe mean follow-up was 28.1 months. Minor complications occurred in 28.6% of cases, whereas major complications were observed in 6.7%. Hematoma, infection, and implant-related complications remained within the lower range of reported literature values. Revision surgery was required in 7.6% of patients, primarily because of contour irregularities, all successfully managed with single-stage correction. Among 100 patients completing BREAST-Q assessment, the mean score was 76.6 ± 15.1.ConclusionsThis refined mesh-free total subpectoral technique provides stable implant positioning, improved lower-pole projection, and reduced animation deformity. In appropriately selected patients, it represents a safe, reproducible, and cost-effective alternative to conventional submuscular approaches.Level of Evidence: 4 (Therapeutic) For image description, please refer to the figure legend and surrounding text.

Implant-based breast reconstruction has become the most commonly performed reconstructive modality following mastectomy, with steadily increasing rates over the past 2 decades.1 Early comparative studies established the submuscular plane as the standard approach because of improved soft-tissue coverage and reduced capsular contracture compared with subcutaneous techniques.2,3 Subsequent refinements, including dual-plane concepts, further optimized implant–tissue relationships and aesthetic outcomes.4

More recently, prepectoral reconstruction has gained widespread adoption because of advantages such as reduced postoperative pain, elimination of animation deformity, and faster recovery.5-7 Contemporary studies and systematic reviews have demonstrated comparable outcomes between prepectoral and subpectoral techniques in selected patient populations.6-9 However, prepectoral reconstruction is not universally applicable. Limitations such as thin mastectomy flaps, compromised vascularity, previous radiotherapy, and oncologic considerations may necessitate a subpectoral approach.6-9

Despite its reliability, traditional submuscular reconstruction is associated with well-documented drawbacks, including animation deformity, restricted lower-pole expansion, and implant malposition.10 The introduction of acellular dermal matrices (ADMs) has partially addressed these limitations, particularly in improving lower-pole projection, but at the cost of increased financial burden and potential complication risks.11

The present study introduces a refined, mesh-free total subpectoral technique designed to address these limitations while preserving the advantages of the submuscular plane. Rather than advocating for subpectoral reconstruction as superior, this study aims to optimize outcomes in patients for whom submuscular placement remains necessary. In this context, our approach should be interpreted as a refinement of a well-established technique, focusing on specific technical modifications to improve implant positioning, lower-pole expansion, and aesthetic outcomes.

METHODS

Patient Population and Study Design

A total of 119 patients undergoing immediate implant-based breast reconstruction were included, corresponding to 181 reconstructed breasts. The patient ages ranged from 26 to 75 years, and the mean patient age was 44.1 years, with a mean BMI of 24.7 kg/m2. The mean follow-up duration was 28.1 months.

Nipple-sparing mastectomy (NSM) was performed in 170 breasts (60 bilateral, 50 unilateral), whereas skin-sparing mastectomy (SSM) was performed in 11 breasts (2 bilateral, 7 unilateral; Table 1).

Patient Characteristics and Postoperative Outcomes

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Surgical Technique

All markings were performed in the standing position. The inframammary fold (IMF) was delineated, and a line was drawn 1 cm superior to it. Medial and superior breast borders were marked, along with the incision line (mammary fold or midlateral). In midlateral incisions, a minimum distance of 2 cm from the areola was maintained to prevent nipple–areola complex displacement.

During mastectomy, preservation of the inframammary ligament, serratus anterior fascia, and second intercostal perforators was prioritized. Maintaining 1 to 1.5 cm of tissue along the IMF was critical for structural support and lower-pole definition, contributing to improved implant positioning and contour stability.

The pectoralis major muscle was elevated from its lateral border with careful limitation of superior dissection to prevent implant migration. Controlled disruption of the inframammary ligament at the submuscular level allowed for expansion of the lower pole (Video 1). Sequential release of the rectus and Scarpa fasciae was performed at the IMF, extending into the subcutaneous plane (5-7 o’clock region) to facilitate adequate expansion and reduce animation deformity by decreasing muscular tension on the implant (Figure 1). To prevent lateral implant displacement, selective release of medial pectoralis attachments was performed, improving medial positioning and cleavage formation. The serratus anterior fascia was elevated and utilized to provide inferolateral implant coverage, allowing for complete pocket control without mesh, while enhancing implant stability and contour definition. After irrigation, drains were placed in both submuscular and subcutaneous planes. A microtextured implant was inserted, and the lateral pectoralis border was sutured to the serratus fascia. Closure was performed in layers (Video 2).

Implant Selection Strategy

In unilateral cases, implant size was matched to mastectomy specimen weight. In bilateral reconstructions, implant selection was based on breast base diameter, nipple-to-IMF distance, and intraoperative skin tension, with emphasis on avoiding postoperative laxity.

Outcome Measurement Strategy

Complications were categorized as minor (conservatively managed) and major (requiring surgical intervention). Revision surgery was defined as any secondary procedure because of complication or aesthetic dissatisfaction. Patient-reported outcomes were assessed using BREAST-Q.

RESULTS

A total of 119 patients (181 breasts) were included in the study. The patient age ranged from 26 to 75 years, and the mean age was 44.1 years and the mean BMI was 24.7 kg/m2. The mean follow-up duration was 28.1 months. Among the reconstructed breasts, 170 underwent NSM and 11 underwent SSM.

Overall, the complication profile was acceptable. Minor complications occurred in 28.6% of cases, whereas major complications requiring surgical intervention were observed in 6.7%. The detailed complication profile is summarized in Table 1. Major complications included events such as hematoma, infection, implant exposure, and other implant-related problems requiring operative management, whereas minor complications were managed conservatively.

Revision surgery was required in 7.6% of patients. The most common indication for revision was contour irregularity or aesthetic dissatisfaction rather than reconstructive failure. More specifically, a subset of these revision procedures was performed to correct deficiencies in medial contour and cleavage formation. In 3 patients, inadequate cleavage and medial fullness were observed because of sternal separation and lateral pull of the pectoralis major muscle, resulting in a visible contour deformity. These cases were categorized under contour irregularities and were managed successfully with secondary correction.

Another important aesthetic issue encountered in the subpectoral plane was animation deformity. Although the described technique was designed to minimize this problem through controlled dissection and fascial release, animation deformity was still observed in selected cases, particularly in athletic or physically active patients with strong pectoral muscle contraction. Although this did not uniformly require reoperation, it represented a clinically relevant limitation of the submuscular approach and contributed to postoperative aesthetic dissatisfaction in some patients.

Despite these issues, all revision cases were managed successfully in a single stage, and no recurrent major contour problem was observed after correction. Among 100 patients who completed the BREAST-Q questionnaire, the mean total score was 76.6 ± 15.1 (range, 32-96), indicating high patient satisfaction and favorable quality-of-life outcomes.

DISCUSSION

The paradigm of implant-based breast reconstruction has shifted significantly toward prepectoral techniques in recent years. Although these approaches offer clear advantages, including elimination of animation deformity and improved early postoperative comfort, they are not universally applicable.5-7 Latest literature supports comparable outcomes between planes; however, patient selection remains the determining factor in surgical success.6-9

Importantly, this study does not advocate for subpectoral reconstruction as a superior technique. Instead, it acknowledges that both prepectoral and submuscular approaches possess inherent advantages and limitations. In patients where subpectoral reconstruction remains necessary—such as those with compromised soft tissue or previous radiation—optimization of the technique is essential. Our technique should be interpreted as a refinement of a well-established approach, focusing on specific technical modifications to optimize implant positioning, lower-pole expansion, and aesthetic outcomes.

Traditional submuscular reconstruction is limited by restricted lower-pole expansion and animation deformity, both of which significantly impact aesthetic outcomes.10 ADM-assisted techniques have attempted to address these limitations, particularly by improving lower-pole projection; however, they introduce additional cost and potential complications.11

The complication profile observed in this series is favorable and compares well with contemporary literature. Reported major complication rates in implant-based reconstruction typically range between 8% and 15%, with revision rates varying widely depending on technique and patient factors.6-9,12-16 The lower rates observed in this study suggest that careful pocket control and anatomical preservation may contribute to improved outcomes.

Radiotherapy remains a critical factor influencing reconstructive success. Increased rates of capsular contracture and reduced tissue compliance have been well documented in irradiated patients, limiting expansion and affecting long-term outcomes. These findings reinforce the importance of patient selection and individualized surgical planning.

Despite the favorable outcomes observed, several limitations inherent to the subpectoral plane must be acknowledged. One of the primary drawbacks is the tendency for lateral and sternal muscle separation, which may limit medial fullness and compromise cleavage formation. This can result in contour irregularities, particularly in patients with wider sternal anatomy or limited soft-tissue coverage.

In addition, the submuscular plane is intrinsically associated with animation deformity, which remains one of its most well-recognized disadvantages.10 This issue becomes particularly relevant in physically active or athletic patients, in whom pectoralis muscle contraction may lead to visible implant distortion during upper extremity movement. Therefore, careful patient selection is essential, and alternative approaches such as prepectoral reconstruction may be more appropriate in this subgroup.

Furthermore, although the described technique aims to minimize animation through controlled muscle dissection and fascial release, complete elimination of animation deformity cannot be guaranteed. This should be clearly communicated during preoperative planning.

Contour irregularities remain an important consideration in subpectoral reconstruction. In the present series, revision procedures were most commonly performed for aesthetic concerns related to implant positioning and contour asymmetry. In particular, cleavage deficiency because of sternal separation of the pectoralis muscle was observed in 3 patients, representing a specific limitation of the submuscular plane.

This limitation is particularly evident in patients with wider sternal anatomy or limited soft-tissue coverage, because the intrinsic strength and lateral pull of the pectoralis major muscle may resist medial implant positioning, resulting in reduced cleavage definition and increased risk of contour deformity.

The technique presented in this study addresses these limitations through a combination of anatomical preservation and controlled fascial release. Preservation of the inframammary ligament is central to maintaining lower-pole definition and implant stability. However, this also represents a key limitation, because the success of the technique depends on adequate preservation of IMF structures during mastectomy.

The absence of a direct comparison group represents an important limitation of this study. This limits our ability to objectively evaluate the outcomes of this technique in comparison with alternative reconstructive approaches and to draw definitive conclusions regarding its relative advantages.

Finally, the elimination of mesh or ADM represents a significant advantage, particularly in terms of cost-effectiveness and procedural simplicity. In an era increasingly focused on value-based care, techniques that maintain outcomes while reducing cost are of considerable importance.

CONCLUSIONS

The modified mesh-free total subpectoral technique described in this study represents a refined and reproducible approach to implant-based breast reconstruction. Although prepectoral techniques continue to gain popularity, subpectoral reconstruction remains necessary in selected patients.

When performed using this optimized technique, subpectoral reconstruction can achieve reliable implant positioning, improved lower-pole expansion, and acceptable complication rates without the need for mesh.

This approach offers a safe, cost-effective, and anatomically sound alternative for surgeons managing patients in whom submuscular reconstruction is indicated.
