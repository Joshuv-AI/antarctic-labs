 BACKGROUND AND INTRODUCTION

1.1 TRUST Overview
The objective of the Delivery Environments (DE) Testbeds to Reduce Uncertainties in Simulations and Tests (TRUST)
project is to quantify and help increase confidence in specific areas of computational and experimental capabilities
that are applicable to current and future delivery environments [1]. More complete quantification of confidence in
experimental and computational capabilities and the sufficient increase of confidence in those capabilities is critical
to improving weapons engineering design, qualification, and assessment efforts that are critical to the current and
future stockpile. Staff development will include cross-discipline collaboration to provide engineers with experience in
both numerical simulations and experimental methods. This work uses and provides feedback on analysis tools and
experimental results databases for efficient and responsive engineering which are currently under development.
TRUST currently includes five testbeds and their associated engineering analysis baseline models (EABMs):
 1. contact thermal conductivity (CTC)
 2. nonlinear dynamics (ND)
 3. sensors in environments for accelerometers (SE-A)
 4. sensors in environments for fiber optic displacement gages (SE-FOD)
 5. sensors in environments for thermocouples (SE-TC)
The TRUST project uses single-feature testbeds to quantify uncertainties in specific models and experiments and to
identify capability development needs that can help to reduce these uncertainties. Each testbed is designed, config-
ured, and tested in collaboration with groups with design and experimental capability: V-14 and MPA-CINT. The
complementary simulations are conducted using E-13 analysis tools and stored in model repositories with plans for
incremental progress toward EABM requirements. E-13 extends and exercises the testbed simulations in collaboration
with experimentalists for uncertainty quantification of current and future materials, geometries, and environments. Ad-
ditionally, TRUST is intended to provide engineers in E-13 and V-14 with experience in both numerical simulations
and experimental methods through cross-discipline collaborations.
This report focuses on experimental and analytical efforts conducted in Fiscal Year (FY) 2025 relevant to the TRUST
Contact Thermal Conductance (CTC) testbed. The rest of this chapter discusses the CTC testbed goals, grading criteria,
and a summary of work for experimental and analytical efforts. Section 2 provides a brief review of silicone foam
conductivity available in open literature and Los Alamos National Laboratory’s (LANL) unclassified material database.
Section 3 provides details of experimental efforts for improved metal on metal CTC experiments and new SX-358
foam interface experiments. Section 4 refers to recent publications for metal on metal CTC analyses and presents a
preliminary analysis for SX-358 foam thermal conductivity under various levels of compression. Results show decent
agreement between relationships discussed in Section 2 for specimens of 1 𝑚𝑚‘ thickness. This analysis positively
indicates the applicability of the TRUST CTC testbed for investigating thermal conductivity of other foams. Finally,
Section 5 summarizes FY25 efforts and future work.

1.2 CTC Testbed
The Contact Thermal Conductance (CTC) testbed is a joint effort between E-13 and MPA-CINT. The objective of this
study is to design a testbed, with accompanying finite element models, to quantify, reduce, and propagate measurement
uncertainties in contact thermal conductance measurements between two mating components in war reserve (WR)
thermal environments. The knowledge gained through these experiments would educate more complex assessments
and interaction conditions with WR-like materials and geometries.
CTC is the phenomenon by which a restriction of heat flow occurs between contacting objects caused by imperfections
and irregularities of surfaces. In many applications, thermal contact conductance is disregarded or greatly simplified.
Numerical tools provide methods for engineers to simulate the variation in contact conductance with pressure without
modeling the details of surface features, such as the *GAP CONDUCTANCE surface interaction in Abaqus [2]. However,
defining these interactions for a specific system has proven challenging. Further background on CTC is provided in
previous reports [3, 4].
The primary goal of the CTC testbed is to exercise and evaluate methods for measuring and characterizing experimental
uncertainty of thermal contact conductance measurements in a manner suitable for propagation in complementary
simulations. In addition to this, analytical models for contact thermal conductance have shown to be a potential method
to formally bridge experimental and numerical efforts.
While the original purpose of the CTC testbed has been to study metal on metal contact, it has been identified that
the experimental apparatus may be useful for interrogating the restriction in heat flow across a foam interface. Initial
experiments were conducted in FY25 with the CTC testbed and a foam interface using the SX-358 cushion material.
Understanding the heat transfer properties of foam cushions is of particular importance to system models at LANL.

 FOAM CONDUCTIVITY REVIEW

It is of particular interest to improve the understanding of foam’s thermal conductivity to better inform system modeling
efforts. Foams are known to have insulating properties due to gas filled voids, often air, which have poor thermal
conductivity. It is believed that the thermal conductivity of a foam will change as compression is applied. In the limit
where gas voids are completely closed, the thermal conductivity of the foam will likely approach that of the matrix
material.
There is little experimental data regarding the thermal conductivity of silicone foams as a function of temperature,
density, and deformation. SX-358 is a silicone foam made from the polymer poly-dimethylsiloxane (PDMS). Table 2.1
lists some thermal conductivities for PDMS and general silicone foams found in open literature.

 EXPERIMENTAL EFFORTS

3.1 CTC testbed measurements performed during FY 2025
Two specific categories of experiments were performed in FY25.
The first category of experiments performed focused on better understanding how surface properties evolve under
constant loading conditions with an applied thermal gradient and how they affect conduction across metal on metal
interfaces. These measurements were an evolution of the measurement methodology developed in FY24.
Experimental methodology was further refined by instrumenting surface thermocouples along the CTC specimens and
performing measurements on a monolith sample of the same geometry as the CTC pair. This was an important addition
for the analysts to have an ideal condition (i.e., no interface) to serve as a baseline for modeling and simulation efforts.
The specifics for the measurement methodology which this work is based on can be found in previous work [4, 14,
15, 16, 17]. These reports contain results from measurements using specimen geometries and conditions that simula-
tion and modeling efforts have continued to develop and fine tune over the life of the project. Specific modeling and
simulation results from data collected in FY25 will be presented in the following sections of this chapter.
The second category of experiments introduce a foam interface to the standard CTC geometry. For FY25, based on
discussion with E-13 senior staff, an experimental matrix was developed. The matrix consisted of performing constant
loading of the CTC foam (SX-358) interface sample geometry under a thermal gradient over the stress ranges from near
zero to 1.5 𝑀 𝑃 𝑎. This experimental plan was implemented to determine if the test system has the fidelity to measure
the variation in thermal conductivity as a function of increasing compression.

4.2.3 Approximate density calculation
As the foam specimens are compressed, density of the material will increase. This change in density is likely a nonlinear
function of strain. It is often assumed that the Poisson ratio of foam materials is 0.0. In other words, the foam is
completely compressible. However, as voids are compressed out of the foam, there is an expected transition to the
compressibility of the matrix materials, for SX-358 this is PDMS. The density and Poisson ratio of PDMS is reported
to be 0.9739 𝑔/𝑐𝑚3 and 0.5, respectively [5], which is considered incompressible. Previous experiments have shown
that foam specimens “barrel” outward under loading verifying the transition away from pure compressibility [20].

 SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

This section summarizes the work completed in FY25, benchmarks the relevance of the CTC testbed to weapons
engineering system modeling efforts, and discusses lessons learned and future work.
The contact thermal conductance (CTC) testbed was exercised to make even more detailed measurements of metal on
metal experiments with both embedded and surface mounted thermocouples to inform analysis and improve model fi-
delity. Characterization of specimen surfaces was performed before and after experiments to track evolution of surface
properties. New experiments were conducted to investigate the thermal conductivity of SX-358 foam. Preliminary
analysis shows decent agreement (for certain cases) between derived thermal conductivity and an empirical relation-
ship reported in the unclassified LANL material database indicating the applicability of the TRUST CTC testbed for
measuring thermal properties of novel foams for which there may be no other conductivity data. Refer to Section 1.4
Summary of FY25 Work for a detailed discussion of accomplishments.

5.1 Milestones and Lessons Learned
In FY25, the CTC testbed demonstrated relevance and application to system modeling and testing capabilities, satis-
fying the DE milestone. In all system-level models and simulations, the Abaqus interaction *GAP CONDUCTANCE is
utilized to inform contact thermal conductance between mating components at varying pressures. Clearly, conduc-
tance is expected to vary as a function of material properties, pressure, surface finishes, etc. Historically, the Abaqus
interaction has been informed directly by CTC measurements at different pressures, i.e., it has relied on experimental
data.
The CTC testbed is able to inform system models via calibration of analytical models for CTC. These analytical models
should allow system analysts to generalize *GAP CONDUCTANCE definitions that have not undergone CTC characteri-
zation experiments. Note that this is a key benefit. This testbed has advanced towards making informed decisions in
system responses via simulation and was achieved by developmental experiments and analyses of well characterized
materials and geometries. Similar to efforts in the SE-A testbed for accelerometer placement optimization and the SE-
TC testbed for air flow characterization and mapping CFD outputs to FE inputs, the developed workflow for calibrating
analytical CTC models from this testbed should be generally agnostic to the material pairing.
Current calibration efforts are detailed in [3, 4], which show promise in capturing experimental results. A lesson
learned by introducing foam materials to CTC: the recent extension of the CTC to investigating thermal conduction of
foam materials has perhaps even more relevance towards system modeling efforts. An FY25 literature review on this
topic highlighted the scarcity of foam thermal data.

5.2 Future Work and Lessons Learned
Topics of interest for future work and lessons learned in FY25 are discussed here.

 25

5.2.1 General interpretation of CTC
Questions about the physical interpretation of CTC arose multiple times throughout the year. CTC is expressed in units
of energy per temperature per squared-length. This is notably different than thermal conductivity which is expressed
in units of energy per temperature per length. In the limit as CTC tends toward infinity, perfect conduction results. On
the other hand, as CTC tends toward zero, perfect insulation occurs. CTC acts like a convective film coefficient relating
the heat exchanged by the media on either side of the interface rather than resolving the micro asperities and contacts
of a real surface.
CTC is inversely proportional to the temperature jump across the interface, ∆𝑇𝑖𝑛𝑡 . As ∆𝑇𝑖𝑛𝑡 decreases, CTC can
increase by orders of magnitude, but will have little practical effect on the heat transfer characteristics of the system.
This is particularly relevant for experiments using sensors with a coarse calibration error (i.e., high sensor to sensor
variability) like the value of about 1 to 2 𝐾 for most thermocouples. This comment is relevant to metal on metal CTC
experiments at high pressure which have a very low ∆𝑇𝑖𝑛𝑡 .

5.2.2 Experiments
Future CTC experiments will consider a variety of foam specimens of varying density, thickness, and composi-
tion/fabrication such as direct ink write (DIW) foam and foams with inclusions. These experiments will be instrumented
with displacement gauges close to the foam interface to better measure the state of compression.

5.2.3 Improved Bayesian Inference and Uncertainty Quantification
Bayesian inference analyses were originally developed by a past TRUST analyst in FY21 and FY22 to (a) calibrate linear
models of stainless steel and aluminum thermal conductivities with respect to temperature [21] and (b) calculate normal
distributions for CTC quantities derived from experimental temperature measurements [14, 15]. These analyses were
implemented in a now deprecated version of the PyMC library. PyMC allows analysts to fit Bayesian models using
Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods to generate samples from a posterior distribution [22]. A great deal
of efforts was spent in FY24 and FY25 to update, streamline, understand, and document these analyses [3, 4] which
allowed for the development of a PyMC calibration for analytical CTC models as a function contact pressure. All
current and past analysts have admitted a lack of formal or thorough background in Bayesian uncertainty quantification
(UQ). It has been agreed that these studies should be scrutinized, peer-reviewed, and possibly rewritten to guarantee
that results are high quality. Analysts in FY26 will collaborate with UQ subject matter experts to improve existing
workflows for metal on metal CTC using the 1D resistor in series model and then extend capabilities to foam interfaces
and a 2D heat transfer model.
As mentioned above, it can be very difficult to calculate CTC for systems in which the ∆𝑇𝑖𝑛𝑡 is very low. Future work
should interrogate the sensitivity and uncertainty of analysis methods for determining CTC in these regimes. It may
be impossible/impractical to determine CTC meaningfully when ∆𝑇𝑖𝑛𝑡 is lower than the variability of temperature
sensors. In this regime, CTC may be high enough that quantities of interest (QoI) in a system calculation would be
unaffected and pure conduction would suffice.

5.2.4 2D heat transfer model
Past CTC analyses, as well as those presented in Section 4.2, have used a 1D resistor in series model for relating
temperature measurements to experimental QoI (including metal on metal CTC and foam conductivity). This model
assumes that heat only flows axially without any environmental influence. New experiments performed this FY have
mounted surface thermocouples allowing for higher fidelity system characterization and the opportunity to constrain
an axisymmetric, 2D heat transfer model. An accurately calibrated 2D model will aid in the differentiation of heat
transfer mechanisms and inform convection properties in finite element simulations. This model will be incorporated
in the improved UQ framework discussed previously.
