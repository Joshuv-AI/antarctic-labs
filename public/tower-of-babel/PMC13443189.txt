LURP1, a SARD1‐regulated protein, coordinates with the helper NLR ADR1 to promote plant immunity
Authors: Xueru Liu, Weijie Huang, Zhengxi Gong, Chenying Li, Siyu Chen, Shucai Wang, Yuelin Zhang, Xin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary In Arabidopsis, loss of transcription factors Calmodulin‐Binding Transcription Activators 1/2/3 (CAMTA1/2/3) results in enhanced defense responses with increased salicylic acid (SA) and N‐hydroxy‐pipecolic acid (NHP) biosynthesis. This is achieved through modulating the expression of master immune transcription factors SAR Deficient 1 (SARD1) and Calmodulin‐Binding Protein 60‐like g (CBP60g). SARD4 is an NHP biosynthesis enzyme, the camta2/3 sard4 triple mutant remains dwarfed and autoimmune.To identify positive immune regulators of the SA and NHP pathways, a forward genetic screen was performed using the camta2/3 sard4 mutant. Here, we report on a loss‐of‐function allele of Late Upregulated in Response to Hyaloperonospora parasitica 1 (lurp1) that suppresses the autoimmunity of camta2/3 sard4. LURP1 transcripts are strongly induced by pathogens. Chromatin immunoprecipitation and expression analyses revealed that LURP1 expression is directly controlled by SARD1. LURP1 belongs to a small gene family. Knocking out LURP1 and its three close homologs compromises both flg22‐ and nlp20‐induced resistance and SA accumulation, supporting their redundant roles in promoting plant defense. Interestingly, LURP1 interacts with the helper nucleotide‐binding leucine‐rich repeat receptor (hNLR) Activated Disease Resistance 1 (ADR1) family, suggestive of a model where LURP1 boosts pattern‐triggered immunity and SA‐mediated defense signaling through the ADR1s. Keywords: activated disease resistance 1, calmodulin‐binding transcription activators, late upregulated in response to Hyaloperonospora parasitica 1, pattern‐triggered immunity, salicylic acid, systemic acquired resistance deficient 1 (SARD1)

Introduction

Plants have evolved complex surveillance systems to sense and defend against various microbial pathogens (Jones et al., 2024). Pattern‐triggered immunity (PTI) is activated by cell membrane‐localized pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), which recognize the conserved pathogen‐associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) (Zhou & Zhang, 2020; DeFalco & Zipfel, 2021). However, adapted pathogens can subvert PTI response through secreted effectors into host cells (Jones & Dangl, 2006). In return, plants can activate effector‐triggered immunity (ETI), mostly initiated by the intracellular sensor nucleotide‐binding leucine‐rich repeat receptors, which recognize pathogen effectors and induce strong defense responses, often leading to localized cell death (Cui et al., 2015; Ngou et al., 2022). PTI and ETI can potentiate each other to achieve robust immunity (Ngou et al., 2021; Pruitt et al., 2021; Tian et al., 2021; Yuan et al., 2021a). Moreover, local resistance responses induce the mobile signal N‐hydroxy‐pipecolic acid (NHP) production, which moves to distal tissues to prime systemic acquired resistance (SAR) (Chen et al., 2018; Hartmann et al., 2018).

Plant membrane PRRs include receptor‐like kinases (RLKs) and receptor‐like proteins (RLPs). The RLK Flagellin Sensitive 2 (FLS2) recognizes flg22, a conserved peptide derived from bacterial flagellin (Chinchilla et al., 2006), whereas the RLP23 recognizes nlp20, a peptide derived from necrosis‐ and ethylene‐inducing‐like proteins (NLPs) from various pathogens (Bohm et al., 2014; Albert et al., 2015). Upon ligand perception, both RLKs and RLPs recruit co‐receptors to initiate intracellular signal transduction. Recognition of flg22 by FLS2 induces its interaction with the co‐receptor Brassinosteroid Insensitive 1‐Associated Kinase 1 (BAK1) (Chinchilla et al., 2007; Sun et al., 2013), whereas perception of nlp20 by RLP23 triggers the formation of the receptor complex with Suppressor of BIR1‐1 (SOBIR1) and BAK1 (Albert et al., 2015, 2019). The receptor complexes further activate downstream signaling events, including phytohormone production (Yuan et al., 2021b).

Activation of ETI relies on the helper NLRs (hNLRs), which function in two parallel modules in Arabidopsis: Enhanced Disease Susceptibility 1 (EDS1) – Phytoalexin Deficient 4 (PAD4) – Activated Disease Resistance 1 (ADR1s) and EDS1 – Senescence‐Associated Gene 101 (SAG101) – N Requirement Gene 1 (NRG1s) (Lapin et al., 2019; Sun et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2021). Upon activation, ADR1s and NRG1s oligomerize to form plasma membrane (PM)‐localized resistosomes, which function as calcium channels to trigger Ca2+ influx and initiate defense signaling (Jacob et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2023). Recent studies revealed that the EDS1‐PAD4‐ADR1 module is also necessary for PRR‐mediated signaling (Pruitt et al., 2021; Tian et al., 2021). The nlp20‐ and flg22‐induced salicylic acid (SA) production and bacterial resistance are compromised in eds1, pad4 and adr1 triple mutants. However, how PRRs transmit signals and activate the hNLRs remain unresolved.

SA and NHP are two major phytohormones in SAR (Hartmann & Zeier, 2019; Zeier, 2021). In Arabidopsis, pathogen‐induced SA is mainly synthesized through the isochorismate synthase pathway. NHP is synthesized through aminotransferase AGD2‐like defense response protein 1 (ALD1), reductase SAR‐Deficient 4 (SARD4) (Návarová et al., 2012; Ding et al., 2016; Hartmann et al., 2017) and Flavin‐dependent Monooxygenase1 (FMO1) (Chen et al., 2018; Hartmann et al., 2018). Although NHP biosynthesis is fully blocked in ald1 and fmo1 mutants, sard4 plants retain considerable NHP content and remain the ability to establish SAR (Ding et al., 2016; Hartmann et al., 2017, 2018).

SARD1 and calmodulin‐binding protein 60‐like g (CBP60g) are two transcription factors critical for defense responses (Zhang et al., 2010; Wang et al., 2011). The sard1 cbp60g double mutant exhibits impaired basal and SAR responses, with significantly reduced pathogen‐induced SA and NHP accumulation (Zhang et al., 2010; Wang et al., 2011; Sun et al., 2015, 2018). Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) sequencing analysis revealed that SARD1 and CBP60g directly activate SA and NHP biosynthesis genes (Sun et al., 2015).

The calmodulin‐binding transcription activators (CAMTAs) are transcription factors playing roles in both biotic and abiotic stress responses (Shen et al., 2015). Among the six Arabidopsis CAMTAs, loss‐of‐function camta1/2/3 highly upregulates SA and NHP biosynthesis genes (Kim et al., 2013, 2020; Sun et al., 2020) and causes severe autoimmunity, including dwarfism, cell death and enhanced disease resistance (Galon et al., 2008; Du et al., 2009; Kim et al., 2017). Genetic analysis revealed that the camta1/2/3 autoimmunity can be suppressed by sard1 cbp60g and the SA and NHP biosynthesis mutants (Kim et al., 2020; Sun et al., 2020).

We previously carried out a forward genetic screen using camta1/2/3 triple mutant, which is severely dwarfed with seedling lethality (Huang et al., 2019; Sun et al., 2020). However, such a strong autoimmune phenotype can limit the identification of additional regulators. To enhance the screening sensitivity, we introduced sard4 into the camta2/3 background, which mildly suppressed the autoimmunity and dwarfism of camta2/3, increasing the chance to identify novel regulators of the SA and NHP pathways. From the screen, Late Upregulated in Response to Hyaloperonospora parasitica 1 (lurp1) was identified as a suppressor of camta2/3 sard4. LURP1 expression is directly upregulated by SARD1 during immune activation. Genetic analysis revealed that LURP1 functions redundantly with its three homologs in flg22‐ and nlp20‐induced resistance and SA accumulation. Furthermore, biochemical assays showed that LURP1 interacts with ADR1, suggesting a potential role for LURP1 in promoting PTI and SA accumulation through ADR1.

Materials and Methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

All plants used in this study are in the Arabidopsis Col‐0 background. camta2/3, sard4‐5 (GABI_428E01), sard1 cbp60g, SARD1‐3HA, adr1 adr1‐l1 adr1‐l2 and nrg1a nrg1b mutant plants were previously reported (Zhang et al., 2010; Kim et al., 2013; Ding et al., 2016; Sun et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2021). The triple mutant camta2/3 sard4‐5 was generated by crossing camta2/3 and sard4‐5. Two independent transgenic lines native promoter (NP)::LURP1 in the 28‐1 background, T1‐1 and T1‐2, were generated by transforming 28‐1 mutant plants with Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 carrying the pBasta‐np‐LURP1 construct.

The CRISPR/Cas9‐generated deletion mutants lurp1‐5 and lor15‐2 were crossed to generate the lurp1‐5 lor15‐2. The T‐DNA insertion mutants lor1 (GABI_130G06) and lor10 (SALK_031379) were obtained from the Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center (ABRC). The triple mutants lurp1‐5 lor1 lor15‐2 and lurp1‐5 lor10 lor15‐2 were obtained by crossing lurp1‐5 lor15‐2 with lor1 and lor10, respectively, and were further crossed to generate the lurp1‐5 lor1 lor10 lor15‐2 quadruple mutant. Primers used for mutant genotyping are listed in Supporting Information Table S1.

Unless specified, plants were grown on soil under long‐day conditions in the growth room at 22°C with 16 h : 8 h, light : dark cycles. Plants were grown under cool white fluorescent light with Philips 32 W 48‐inch T8 fluorescent light bulb (4100 k). The 10‐d‐old seedlings grown on ½‐strength Murashige and Skoog (½MS) plates under long‐day conditions were used for quantitative reverse transcription‐polymerase chain reaction (qRT‐PCR) and ChIP‐quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analysis.

Plasmid construction

To generate lurp1‐5 and lor15‐2, the CRISPR/Cas9 system was used (Wang et al., 2015). Two gRNAs targeting each of LURP1 and LOR15 were designed using MMEJ‐KO http://skl.scau.edu.cn/mmejko/. The fragments containing the gRNAs were amplified from pCBC‐DT1DT2 and cloned into the pBEE. The pBEE‐LURP1 with the same gRNAs was transformed into both camta2/3 sard4 and Col‐0 to generate the lurp1 deletion alleles.

For pBasta‐np‐LURP1, a genomic fragment containing LURP1 and its 1981 bp‐promoter was amplified from Col‐0 genomic DNA and cloned into the pBasta‐np‐3HA construct.

For pBasta‐35s‐LURP1‐3FLAG or pCambia 1300‐LURP1‐Nluc, LURP1 cDNA was amplified from Col‐0 cDNA and cloned into the respective vectors. The LURP1 V84M cDNA fragment was amplified from 28‐1 mutant cDNA and cloned into pBasta‐35s‐3FLAG.

For CAMTA1/2/3 virus‐induced gene silencing (VIGS) vector, the cDNA fragments of CAMTA1 (277 bp), CAMTA2 (285 bp) and CAMTA3 (269 bp) were amplified from Col‐0 cDNA and ligated into the pTRV‐RNA2 VIGS vector (Liu et al., 2016).

Primers used for cloning are listed in Table S1.

BAK1‐3HA, BAK1‐Cluc (Wu et al., 2018), ADR1‐L1‐Cluc, ADR1‐L1‐HA‐TurboID and PAD4‐Nluc (Wu et al., 2021) were described earlier in the respective publications.

Ethyl methanesulfonate (EMS) mutagenesis and mapping‐by‐sequencing

EMS mutagenesis was performed as previously described (Li & Zhang, 2016; Liu et al., 2026). The suppressor mutant 28‐1 was backcrossed with camta2/3 sard4. After selfing, in F2, 32 plants showing larger size than camta2/3 sard4 were pooled for genomic DNA extraction. Single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) frequencies were calculated to perform linkage analysis as previously described (Huang et al., 2019).

Pathogen infection

Hyaloperonospora arabidopsidis (Hpa) Noco2 and Pseudomonas syringae pv maculicola (Psm) ES4326 infections were performed as previously described (Liu et al., 2026).

For flg22‐ and nlp20‐induced resistance assays, two leaves from 4‐wk‐old plants were infiltrated with 0.5 μM flg22 or 1 μM nlp20 in 10 mM MgCl2, with 10 mM MgCl2 alone as the control. After 1 d, the treated leaves were inoculated with Psm ES4326 (OD600 = 0.001). Bacterial growth was assessed 3 d post‐inoculation.

The Psm ES4326–Hpa Noco2 and Psm ES4326–Psm ES4326 SAR assay were performed as previously described (Mohnike et al., 2021).

SA extraction and measurement

The SA extraction and quantification by high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) were performed as described previously (Liu et al., 2025). For flg22‐ and nlp20‐induced SA, 4‐wk‐old plants grown under short‐day conditions were infiltrated with 0.5 μM flg22 or 1 μM nlp20, and treated leaves were harvested 24 h later.

For Pto DC3000 hrcC‐induced SA, 4‐wk‐old plants grown under short‐day conditions were infiltrated with Pto DC3000 hrcC (OD600 = 0.05), and treated leaves were harvested 12 h later.

For systemic SA, two leaves from 4‐wk‐old plants grown under short‐day conditions were infiltrated with Psm ES4326 (OD600 = 0.01). Three systemic untreated leaves were harvested 48 h after primary inoculation.

Quantitative RT‐PCR

Around 30–50 mg tissue from 10‐d‐old seedlings grown on ½MS plates was harvested and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. Total RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis and qPCR analysis were performed as previously described (Liu et al., 2026). Primers used for qPCR analysis are listed in Table S1.

ChIP‐qPCR

The Col‐0 and SARD1‐3HA transgenic plants were planted on ½MS plates under long‐day conditions. The 2‐wk‐old seedlings were irradiated by UV lights for 15 min. After 12 h, the tissue was harvested for ChIP assay as previously described (Sun et al., 2015). After immunoprecipitation by anti‐HA antibody and Protein A/G Agarose beads (GE Health Care, USA), the resulting DNA was analyzed by qPCR with SYBR Premix Ex Taq II (Takara, Shiga, Japan). Primers used for qPCR analysis are listed in Table S1.

Split‐luciferase complementation

The indicated proteins fused with either luciferase (LUC) Cluc or Nluc were coexpressed in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves by Agrobacterium. After 2 d, the infiltrated leaves were treated with 1 mM luciferin, and the luminescence signals were then imaged.

Co‐immunoprecipitation (IP)

The indicated proteins were transiently expressed in N. benthamiana leaves. Around 2 g of infiltrated leaves were harvested 2 d post‐infiltration and ground into fine powder. For induction, the leaves expressing the indicated proteins were infiltrated with either Psm ES4326 (OD600 = 0.005) 24 h before harvesting tissue or 1 μM flg22 15 min before harvest. The protein extraction and co‐IP were performed as described previously (Wu et al., 2021). The FLAG and HA proteins were detected by anti‐FLAG (Sigma) and anti‐HA (Roche) antibodies, respectively.

Virus‐induced gene silencing

VIGS was performed as previously described using the pTRV‐RNA vectors (Liu et al., 2016). Briefly, the first pair of true leaves from 12‐ to 14‐d‐old soil‐grown plants were infiltrated with a 1 : 1 mixture of A. tumefaciens carrying pTRV‐RNA1 and pTRV‐RNA2‐CAMTA1/2/3. Plant phenotypes were observed 2–3 wk after inoculation.

Results

Mutant 28‐1 suppresses the autoimmunity of camta2/3 sard4

The camta2/3 sard4 plants displayed autoimmune phenotypes, with smaller size and curled leaves compared to wild‐type (WT) Col‐0 plants (Fig. S1a). The suppressor mutant 28‐1 displayed a size comparable to WT (Fig. 1a), although curly leaves and early senescence remained visible (Fig. S1b). Compared to camta2/3 sard4, 28‐1 plants displayed increased susceptibility to the virulent oomycete pathogen Hpa Noco2 (Fig. 1b). Furthermore, the elevated levels of free SA observed in camta2/3 sard4 were largely reduced in 28‐1 (Fig. 1c). These results suggest that 28‐1 suppresses the autoimmunity of camta2/3 sard4.

Mapping‐by‐sequencing of 28‐1

To map the causal gene underlying the camta2/3 sard4 suppression phenotypes, 28‐1 was backcrossed to camta2/3 sard4. All F1 plants resembled camta2/3 sard4, indicating that the mutation in 28‐1 was recessive. In F2, the 28‐1‐like plants and camta2/3 sard4‐like plants segregated at a 41 : 143 ratio (1 : 3, χ 2 = 0.725, P = 0.395), consistent with the segregation of a single recessive mutation. After mapping‐by‐sequencing, a linkage region was identified on chromosome 2 (Fig. 2a). Five exonic nonsynonymous mutations in the linkage region with SNP ratios > 0.8 were selected as candidate causal mutations of 28‐1 (Fig. 2b).

Among these candidates, Late Upregulated in Response to Hyaloperonospora parasitica 1 (LURP1, AT2G14560) was previously reported to be a positive immune regulator against oomycete pathogens (Knoth & Eulgem, 2008). In addition, LURP1 expression can be induced by SA analog INA and NHP (Fig. S2a,b) (Jin et al., 2018; Yildiz et al., 2021), and LURP1 co‐expresses with known defense genes such as ACD6, PR1 and SARD1 (https://atted.jp/). Thus, LURP1 was prioritized as a likely candidate for 28‐1.

To test whether the mutation in LURP1 causes the suppression phenotypes, we generated LURP1 knockout lines in camta2/3 sard4 using CRISPR/Cas9 (Fig. S2c). Two independent homozygous alleles of LURP1 displayed a larger size than camta2/3 sard4, similar to 28‐1 (Figs S2d, 2c). They also exhibited decreased resistance to Hpa Noco2 and reduced SA levels (Fig. 2d,e). These results support that the LURP1 mutation in 28‐1 is the causal one.

Furthermore, the 28‐1 mutant was transformed with WT LURP1 to test for complementation. Notably, LURP1 gene expression was reduced in the 28‐1 mutant compared to camta2/3 sard4, possibly due to feedback regulation (Fig. S3b). Five independent transgenic lines were obtained (Fig. S3a). T1‐1 and T1‐2 with high LURP1 expression showed a similar size as camta2/3 sard4 (Figs S3a,c, 2f), with restored Hpa Noco2 resistance (Fig. 2g) and SA accumulation (Fig. 2h), indicating that LURP1 complements 28‐1. Taken together, the immune suppression phenotypes in 28‐1 are caused by the V84M mutation in LURP1. As three other alleles were reported previously, this allele was named lurp1‐4.

The LURP1 mutation site in the 28‐1 mutant

LURP1 was initially identified as a strongly upregulated gene during oomycete pathogen infection (Knoth & Eulgem, 2008). LURP1 adopts a 12‐stranded β‐barrel fold with a central α‐helix structure. In Arabidopsis, LURP1 belongs to an 18‐member family termed LORs (LURP1‐related) (Fig. S4a) (Knoth & Eulgem, 2008; Baig, 2018). To investigate how the EMS‐derived mutation in LURP1 might impact its function, we performed a sequence alignment of the Arabidopsis LOR family. The V84M mutation in the lurp1‐4 allele occurs in the sixth β‐strand. Notably, V84 and the nearby W82 are relatively conserved among the LORs (Fig. S4b). Val84 forms two hydrogen bonds with Leu96 and Tyr97 based on Alphafold3 structural prediction (Fig. S4c) (Abramson et al., 2024). Although the V84M substitution does not disrupt these bonds, it induces a subtle change that slightly shifts the central α‐helix (Fig. S4d), potentially impairing protein function.

To determine whether V84M affects protein stability, LURP1‐3FLAG or LURP1‐V84M‐3FLAG was transiently expressed in N. benthamiana. The LURP1‐V84M‐3FLAG exhibited significantly reduced protein levels than LURP1‐3FLAG (Fig. S4e,f), while they showed similar transcript levels (Fig. S4g), suggesting that V84M likely destabilizes LURP1 and increases protein turnover.

SARD1/CBP60g directly regulate LURP1 expression

Because lurp1 suppresses camta2/3 sard4 autoimmunity, we asked if CAMTA3 transcription factor directly regulates LURP1 expression. CAMTAs recognize CGCG‐box ((A/C/G)CGCG(G/T/C)) in target gene promoters (Bouche et al., 2002; Benn et al., 2014). However, no CGCG motif was found within a 2‐kb region upstream of the LURP1 start codon, suggesting that CAMTA2/3 do not directly regulate LURP1.

SARD1 and CBP60g are redundant master transcription factors regulated by CAMTA3, and the sard1 cbp60g double mutant can fully suppress the autoimmunity of camta3‐1 (Sun et al., 2020). SARD1 and CBP60g regulate gene expression by binding to the G(A/T) AATT(T/G) motif (Zhang et al., 2010; Sun et al., 2015). Notably, two potential SARD1/CBP60g binding sites are present within the 1‐kb LURP1 promoter region (Fig. 3a). As SARD1 and CBP60g act redundantly (Zhang et al., 2010; Sun et al., 2015), we measured the LURP1 transcript levels in WT and sard1 cbp60g double mutant. The flg22‐induced LURP1 expression was fully abolished in the sard1 cbp60g double mutant compared to WT (Fig. 3b), indicating that SARD1 and CBP60g positively regulate LURP1 transcription during immune responses.

We further tested whether SARD1 directly binds to the LURP1 promoter. ChIP‐qPCR analysis revealed that both 0.6‐kb and 0.1‐kb regions upstream of the LURP1 start codon were significantly enriched by c. five‐fold in the ChIP samples from SARD1‐3HA plants compared to Col‐0 (Figs S5a, 3c). Thus, SARD1 directly binds to the LURP1 promoter.

Furthermore, we performed a dual‐LUC assay to confirm the transcriptional activation of LURP1 by SARD1. Co‐expression of SARD1‐3HA with LURP1 WT pro::Luc in N. benthamiana showed an increased LUC signal (Fig. 3d,e), whereas mutation of two predicted SARD1‐binding motifs to ‘AAAAAAA’ strongly reduced this activation (Fig. 3a,f,g), indicating these motifs are necessary for SARD1‐mediated transcription activation of LURP1.

Collectively, our data suggest that SARD1/CBP60g directly activate LURP1 transcription during defense responses.

Knocking out LURP1 and its three paralogs in WT leads to compromised basal immunity

To investigate the immune functions of LURP1 in WT, a CRISPR mutant lurp1‐5 in Col‐0 was generated using the same gRNAs described earlier (Fig. 4a). A previous study reported that the lurp1 mutant exhibited slightly increased susceptibility to the oomycete pathogen Hpa Emoy2 (Knoth & Eulgem, 2008). However, we observed that lurp1‐5 displayed similar susceptibility to Hpa Noco2 (Fig. 4b) and the virulent bacterial pathogen Pseudomonas syringae pv maculicola (Psm) ES4326 (Fig. 4c) as Col‐0.

Phylogenetic analysis revealed that LURP1 belongs to a small gene clade with four close LORs (Fig. S4a). Among them, LOR15 (AT5G01750) was also implicated in basal defense against Hpa Cala2 (Baig, 2018). Given the mild susceptibility in lurp1‐5 and lor15‐2 single mutants, we hypothesized potential functional redundancy within the LOR family. To test this, we generated the lurp1‐5 lor15‐2 double mutant (Figs 4a, S6a). Compared with lurp1‐5, the double mutant showed slightly enhanced susceptibility to Hpa Noco2 and Psm ES4326 (Fig. 4b,c).

We then examined two additional paralogs, LOR1 (AT1G33840) and LOR10 (AT3G11740) (Fig. S4a), which share 84% and 74% amino acid similarity with LURP1, respectively, are also highly induced by NHP (Yildiz et al., 2021) or INA (Jin et al., 2018; Fig. S2a,b). The lurp1‐5 lor1 lor15‐2 and lurp1‐5 lor10 lor15‐2 triple mutants were then generated (Figs 4a, S6b,c). When tested with Psm ES4326, no significant differences were observed between lurp1‐5 lor15‐2 and lurp1‐5 lor1 lor15‐2, whereas lurp1‐5 lor10 lor15‐2 supported more bacterial growth than lurp1‐5 lor15‐2 (Fig. 4d). Moreover, the lurp1‐5 lor1 lor10 lor15‐2 quadruple mutant (Figs 4a, S6c) exhibited the most severely compromised defense against Hpa Noco2 (Fig. 4b), although it showed similar bacterial growth as the lurp1‐5 lor10 lor15‐2 triple mutant (Fig. 4c). Overall, LURP1 and its three paralogs seem redundant in basal immunity.

Furthermore, the SARD1/CBP60g potential binding motifs were also found in the promoters of LOR1, LOR10 and LOR15 (Fig. S5b). Consistently, the flg22‐induced expression of these genes was all reduced in sard1 cbp60g compared to Col‐0 (Fig. S5c–e), suggesting SARD1/CBP60g positively regulates the expression of LURP1 and its paralogs.

LURP1 and its three paralogs are not required for SAR

As LURP1 and LOR1 are strongly induced by NHP (Fig. S2b), we investigated whether the LURP1 clade contributes to SAR. Following primary Psm ES4326 induction, the lurp1‐5 and its higher‐order mutants all showed similar resistance to Hpa Noco2 as Col‐0 (Fig. S7a,b). Similarly, when using secondary Psm ES4326 infection to assess SAR, the lurp1 triple and quadruple mutants showed similar SAR responses as Col‐0 (Fig. S7c). In addition, systemic SA accumulation after local Psm ES4326 induction was only slightly reduced in the lurp1 double, triple and quadruple mutants compared to Col‐0 (Fig. S7d). These results indicate that the LURP1 clade contributes very little to SAR.

The LURP1 clade is required for flg22‐ and nlp20‐induced defense responses

To investigate whether LURP1 and its three paralogs contribute to PTI, flg22‐ and nlp20‐induced immune responses were evaluated. After pretreatment with flg22 or nlp20, Col‐0, lurp1‐5 and lurp1‐5 lor15‐2 mutants displayed comparable resistance to Psm ES4326 (Fig. 5a,b). Although both lurp1‐5 lor10 lor15‐2 triple and lurp1‐5 lor1 lor10 lor15‐2 quadruple mutants still retained the PAMP‐induced protection, their protection levels were slightly reduced compared to Col‐0 (Fig. 5a,b). These results suggest that LURP1 and its paralogs contribute redundantly to flg22‐ and nlp20‐induced PTI responses.

As SA is an essential phytohormone accumulated during defense activation (Vlot et al., 2009), we measured SA levels after PAMP treatment. The basal SA levels in untreated Col‐0, lurp1 and its higher‐order mutants were similarly low (Fig. S8a). In lurp1‐5 and lurp1‐5 lor15‐2 mutants, flg22‐ or nlp20‐induced SA increase was comparable to Col‐0 (Fig. 5c,d). However, the lurp1 triple and quadruple mutants exhibited c. 50% lower flg22‐induced SA levels (Fig. 5c) and 30% lower nlp20‐induced SA levels compared with Col‐0 (Fig. 5d).

Further, we measured SA after infection with the bacterial mutant Pseudomonas syringae pv tomato (Pto) DC3000 hrcC, which cannot deliver effectors into plant cells (Tian et al., 2021). Similarly, the Pto DC3000 hrcC‐induced SA was significantly reduced in lurp1 triple and quadruple mutants compared with Col‐0 (Fig. S8b).

Together, these results indicate that LURP1 and its three paralogs play redundant critical roles in regulating SA accumulation and resistance in response to PAMP signals.

LURP1 associates with BAK1

A recent study reported that LURP1 is involved in blue‐light‐induced resistance. Upon blue‐light activation, LURP1 relocalizes to the PM where it interacts with FLS2 to promote plant immunity (Hao et al., 2025). In our study, the LURP1 clade functions in both flg22‐ and nlp20‐induced resistance. Thus, we hypothesize that LURP1 may interact with the FLS2 and RLP23 coreceptor BAK1 to boost PTI. In agreement, AlphaFold3 (AF3) (Abramson et al., 2024) predicted likely interactions between LURP1 and FLS2‐BAK1 kinase domains or RLP23 coreceptor BAK1‐SOBIR1 kinase domains (Fig. S9a,b). Split‐luciferase complementation (SLC) assay in N. benthamiana detected a weak association between LURP1 and BAK1 compared to the positive control MKK6 and MPK4 (Fig. 6a). Co‐IP assay in N. benthamiana further confirmed that LURP1‐3FLAG was able to pull down BAK1‐3HA, although the BAK1‐3HA signal was relatively weak (Fig. 6b). We also tested whether their interaction could be enhanced by Psm ES4326 or flg22 treatment. However, under both conditions, we did not observe a noticeable enhanced interaction between LURP1 and BAK1 (Fig. 6b), suggesting that LURP1 likely constitutively associates with BAK1.

LURP1 interacts with ADR1s to promoter plant defense responses

The weak interaction of LURP1 and BAK1 is insufficient to explain the pronounced SA accumulation defects following PAMP inductions as observed in lurp1 mutants. Recent studies demonstrated that hNLR ADR1 is essential for PRRs‐mediated immunity (Pruitt et al., 2021; Tian et al., 2021), we hypothesize LURP1 may coordinate with hNLR to promote SA accumulation. We first tested whether ADR1 or NRG1 family contribute to the autoimmunity of camta mutant. We silenced CAMTA1/2/3 in Col‐0, adr1 adr1‐l1 adr1‐l2 triple mutant (Fig. 7a–c) or nrg1a nrg1b double mutant (Fig. S10a–c) using the VIGS system. Col‐0 plants infiltrated with the CAMTA1/2/3 VIGS construct showed dwarfism with reduced CAMTA3 transcripts and enhanced defense marker gene PR1 expression compared to the untreated Col‐0. By contrast, silencing of CAMTA1/2/3 in adr1 adr1‐l1 adr1‐l2 triple mutant exhibited a similar size as the untreated plants with no PR1 gene induction (Fig. 7a–c), suggesting that ADR1 family contributes to camta1/2/3 autoimmunity. However, silencing of CAMTA1/2/3 in nrg1a nrg1b mutant exhibited a similar phenotype as that in Col‐0 (Fig. S10a,b), along with high PR1 induction (Fig. S10c), suggesting NRG1 family is not required for the autoimmunity of camta1/2/3. Moreover, an EMS mutant M10, identified in camta1/2/3 background and mapped to pad4, can partially suppress the autoimmunity of camta1/2/3 (Fig. S11), supporting the involvement of the EDS1‐PAD4‐ADR1s module downstream of CAMTA1/2/3.

Next, we examined the interaction between LURP1 and hNLRs. Structural predictions using AF3 suggested stronger interactions between LURP1 and hNLRs in their pentameric forms as compared with the monomeric ones (Fig. S9c–f). SLC assays revealed a strong association between ADR1‐L1 and LURP1, but not between NRG1A and LURP1 (Fig. 6c,d). Besides ADR1‐L1, LURP1 also showed interaction with ADR1 and ADR1‐L2 by SLC assays (Fig. S12a–c), confirming that LURP1 associates with ADR1 family members.

The interaction between LURP1 and hNLRs was further confirmed by co‐IP in N. benthamiana. ADR1‐L1‐HA‐TurboID was pulled down by LURP1‐3FLAG (Fig. 6e), whereas NRG1A‐HA‐TurboID was not detected in the LURP1‐3FLAG immunoprecipitation (Fig. S13a). Interestingly, Psm ES4326 or flg22 treatment slightly enhanced the interaction between LURP1 and ADR1‐L1 (Fig. 6e).

Furthermore, to determine whether LURP1 is required for ADR1‐mediated immunity, ADR1‐L1 was overexpressed in Col‐0 and lurp1 quadruple mutant background. In T1 generation, c. 70% of transgenic plants in Col‐0 exhibited autoimmune phenotypes, including dwarfism and curled leaves (Fig. 7d). By contrast, none of the transgenic plants in lurp1 quadruple mutant background displayed obvious dwarfism or curly leaves. Notably, 35S::ADR1‐L1‐3FLAG in Col‐0 T1‐3 line accumulated a similar ADR1‐L1 protein level as that in lurp1 quadruple mutant T1‐6 line (Fig. S14a). However, T1‐3 displayed autoimmunity, whereas T1‐6 exhibited a WT‐like morphology (Fig. 7d). These results suggest that the autoimmunity by ADR1‐L1 can be suppressed by lurp1 quadruple mutant.

As ADR1 cooperates with EDS1‐PAD4 to promote SA accumulation, we also tested the interaction between LURP1 and PAD4 by SLC. However, LURP1 and PAD4 did not show interaction (Fig. S15a). In addition, the interaction between LURP1 and ADR1‐L1 remained detectable in eds1 pad4 N. benthamiana mutant by SLC (Fig. S15b), suggesting that LURP1 and ADR1‐L1 interaction may not fully require EDS1‐PAD4. However, whether eds1 or pad4 weakens the interaction between LURP1 and ADR1‐L1 remains unclear.

Together, these results support that LURP1 weakly associates with BAK1 but strongly interacts with hNLRs ADR1 family, potentially contributing to PTI signaling and SA induction during plant immune activation (Fig. 7e).

Discussion

CAMTA1/2/3 and SARD1/CBP60g are major transcription factors regulating plant defense responses and production of phytohormones SA and NHP (Sun et al., 2020). Using the camta2/3 sard4 autoimmune mutant as a sensitized background, we identified LURP1 as a positive immune regulator. Loss of LURP1 suppresses camta2/3 sard4 autoimmune phenotypes. LURP1 expression is directly regulated by SARD1/CBP60g. Moreover, LURP1 functions redundantly with its three close homologs to promote both flg22‐ and nlp20‐triggered resistance and SA biosynthesis, likely through interactions with PRR complexes and hNLR ADR1s (Fig. 7e).

LURP1 was recently reported to function downstream of blue‐light receptor cryptochrome 1 (CRY1) in blue‐light‐mediated immunity (Hao et al., 2025). Upon blue‐light treatment, lurp1 mutant exhibited defects in flg22‐induced stomata closure, reactive oxygen species burst, MAPK activation and resistance to bacterial pathogens compared to Col‐0, highlighting the essential role of LURP1 in integrating light signals with immune signaling. Notably, these defects in the lurp1 single mutant were not apparent except under blue‐light‐specific treatment. From our analyses of the lurp1 triple and quadruple mutants, they showed significant defects in basal immunity and PTI responses compared to the lurp1 single mutant (Figs 4, 5), highlighting the redundant roles of the LURP1 clade in plant defense.

Although LURP1 is highly upregulated by the SAR mobile signal NHP (Yildiz et al., 2021), the lurp1 mutants do not show significant defects in SAR, aside from a modest reduction in systemic SA accumulation (Fig. S7). These may indicate that LURP1 and its three paralogs function primarily in the basal immunity, rather than regulating the SAR signaling, although additional redundancy within the LOR family cannot be excluded.

LURP1 and its paralogs are highly upregulated upon pathogen infection, indicating that their transcriptional activation is key to their functions. A previous study discussed that WRKY70 may function as a possible transcription factor to regulate LURP1 expression because W‐box binding motifs are present in the LURP1 promoter region and LURP1 expression was partially reduced in the wrky70 mutant (Knoth & Eulgem, 2008). Here, we found that LURP1 gene induction was almost fully suppressed in the sard1 cbp60g double mutant. ChIP‐qPCR and dual‐LUC analysis further revealed that SARD1 directly bound to the LURP1 promoter region (Fig. 3), suggesting that SARD1/CBP60g are the major transcriptional regulators of LURP1. In addition, the expression of LURP1 paralogs LOR1, LOR10 and LOR15 was also reduced in sard1 cbp60g, and putative SARD1/CBP60g binding sites were identified in their promoter regions (Fig. S5b–e), indicating that SARD1/CBP60g may also regulate the expression of these redundant genes during immune activation.

A recent study showed that flg22 triggers the localization change of LURP1 from cytosol to PM, where it interacts with flg22 receptor FLS2 to enhance defense responses (Hao et al., 2025). To examine whether LURP1 functions specifically to flg22, we treated the lurp1 and higher‐order mutants with flg22 as well as nlp20. The flg22‐ and nlp20‐induced bacterial resistance and SA accumulation were all attenuated in lurp1 higher‐order mutants (Fig. 5). These suggest that LURP1 plays broader roles in PAMP‐induced defense responses. Based on these results, we propose that LURP1 likely serves as an adaptor for the PAMP receptor complexes, potentially via interaction with BAK1, to boost PTI (Fig. 7e).

Interestingly, SA accumulation upon PAMP or Pto DC3000 hrcC treatments was attenuated in the lurp1 higher‐order mutants (Figs 5c,d, S8b). This cannot be reconciled by the PAMP recognition complexes as mutants of PAMP receptors, such as fls2, do not show general SA defects (Fig. S8b; Tateda et al., 2014). As hNLR ADR1s in the EDS1‐PAD1‐ADR1 module are major contributors to flg22‐ and nlp20‐induced SA accumulation and immunity (Pruitt et al., 2021; Tian et al., 2021), LURP1's interaction with ADR1s explains its role in regulating SA levels (Figs 6c,e, 7d). However, how LURP1 influences ADR1s, and how this contributes to PTI and SA accmulation remain unclear.

The effector‐activated TNLs form resistosomes with the NADase (Nicotinamide Adenine Dinucleotide nucleosidase) enzymatic activity that generate the small molecules pRib‐AMP/ADP or di‐ADPR/ADPr‐ATP, which bind to EDS1‐PAD4 or EDS1‐SAG101 and subsequently promote interactions between EDS1‐PAD4 and ADR1 or EDS1‐SAG101 and NRG1 (Huang et al., 2022; Jia et al., 2022). The activated NRG1 or ADR1 then assemble into Ca2+‐permeable channels. It is suggested that EDS1‐SAG101 dissociate from NRG1 before NRG1 oligomerizes to form resistosome at PM (Wang et al., 2023). However, the mechanisms underlying ADR1 activation and oligomerization are not yet understood (Jacob et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2024). From our Co‐IP data, LURP1 may not simply stabilize ADR1 protein level. Whether LURP1 stabilizes the ADR1 resistosome formation or promotes the conformational change of ADR1 to form pentamer remains to be tested. Moreover, it remains unclear whether EDS1‐PAD4 is required for ADR1‐LURP1 interaction. Although ADR1‐L1 and LURP1 showed interaction in our SLC assays in N. benthamiana eds1 pad4 mutant (Fig. S15b), more quantitative approaches, such as co‐IP or fluorescence resonance energy transfer, will be needed to determine whether their interaction is affected in eds1 or pad4 mutant. Together, our findings suggest that LURP1 represents a previously uncharacterized interactor of ADR1. Future work will be required to determine how LURP1 contributes to the function of ADR1.
