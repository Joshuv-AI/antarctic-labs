Thin Membranous Connective Tissue Encasing the Superior Mesenteric Vessels as an Objective Landmark for Robotic D3 Lymphadenectomy in Right-sided Colon Cancer: A Histological Study and Intraoperative Video Review
Authors: Ken Nagata, Keiichi Shiokawa, Issei Takeshita, Kurumi Sahara, Taro Munechika, Hideki Nagano, Hiroyuki Takahashi, Yoshiko Matsumoto, Hiroshi Kida, Suguru Hasegawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objectives: This study aimed to histologically identify and validate a consistent perivascular membranous structure encasing the superior mesenteric vessels and to evaluate its intraoperative visibility as an objective landmark for standardizing robotic central (D3) lymphadenectomy with central vascular ligation in right-sided colon cancer (RSCC). Methods: This translational anatomic study comprised (i) cadaveric histology of the superior mesenteric artery and vein (n = 5), (ii) validation using surgical resection specimens from RSCC (n = 5), and (iii) retrospective review of non-edited intraoperative videos from 78 consecutive robotic D3 procedures. Two skill-qualified surgeons, who were blinded to pathological outcomes, independently graded visibility and assessed associations with body mass index (BMI) and surgeon experience. Results: Histology consistently showed a collagenous and elastic membranous layer encasing the superior mesenteric vessels and separating them from mesenteric lymphofatty tissue; this is termed the thin membranous connective tissue (TMCT). Of 78 cases, TMCT has clear visibility in 69 (88%) and poor visibility in 9 (12%), with no association with BMI (p = 0.55) or surgeon experience (p = 0.32). No central vascular injuries occurred. Estimated blood loss and lymph node yield did not significantly differ between cases with clear versus poor TMCT identification. Conclusions: TMCT, a histologically defined perivascular layer that is usually identifiable during robotic surgery, provides an objective boundary to delineate the optimal central dissection plane around the superior mesenteric vessels and supports safer, more reproducible robotic D3 lymphadenectomy for RSCC.

Introduction

Central (D3) lymphadenectomy with central vascular ligation (CVL) is a key determinant of oncologic quality in right-sided colon cancer (RSCC)[1-6]. However, dissection adjacent to the superior mesenteric vein and artery remains technically demanding during minimally invasive surgery and carries a risk of major vascular injury. Therefore, standardization of procedure warrants an objective and consistently recognizable perivascular landmark that is robust to patient habitus and surgeon experience.

Performing a D3 lymphadenectomy with CVL for RSCC is associated with anatomical challenges. The proximity of the pancreas and duodenum, together with superior mesenteric axis variations[7,8], creates a high-risk surgical field. In the absence of a reproducible anatomic boundary, D3 dissection may become dependent on individual experience, thereby potentially compromising safety and oncologic completeness.

These technical challenges translate into clinical realities. The perioperative mortality for RSCC surgery (2.1%) paradoxically exceeds that of highly complex operations, including esophagectomy or pancreaticoduodenectomy (1.4%-1.6%), despite being categorized as an intermediate-difficulty procedure in Japan's National Clinical Database[9]. This discrepancy highlights the urgent need for safer and more reproducible surgical techniques for this procedure.

The challenge of overcoming anatomical complexity through a standardized, landmark-based approach has been successfully addressed in other fields of gastrointestinal surgery. Notably, Shibasaki et al. validated a novel “outermost layer-oriented medial approach” for laparoscopic infrapyloric nodal dissection in gastric cancer[10-12]. They transformed a complex dissection into a systematic process by identifying a series of highly reproducible landmarks, thereby enabling even nonexpert surgeons to safely perform the procedure. The success of this approach provides a strong rationale for developing a similar landmark-oriented technique to standardize CVL and improve its safety in minimally invasive colectomy.

To address this gap, we anatomically and histologically characterized a thin membranous connective tissue (TMCT) surrounding the superior mesenteric vessels and investigated its intraoperative visibility using video review. We hypothesized that this structure constitutes a consistent perivascular boundary that guides a safe and reproducible dissection plane for robotic D3 lymphadenectomy with CVL in RSCC. Accordingly, the TMCT was considered to be an anatomical landmark for defining the appropriate dissection plane according to vessel-specific anatomy, rather than a structure to be uniformly preserved throughout the entire central dissection.

Materials and Methods

1. Study Design and Ethical Considerations

This study comprised three components: an anatomical investigation using cadaveric specimens, a histological analysis of surgical resection specimens from patients with RSCC, and a video-based clinical observation of surgical procedures. This observational study was reported in accordance with the STROBE statement. All protocols adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and obtained approval from the Ethical Committee of Fukuoka University and the Institutional Review Board of Fukuoka University Hospital (IRB code: U25-05-014).

All donors provided informed consent for body donation before their demise, following the Act on Body Donation for Medical and Dental Education of Japan, for the cadaveric study. Each patient signed written informed consent for the use of their tissues for the histological analysis of surgical specimens. All patients whose surgical videos were reviewed provided informed consent for the video-based clinical observation.

2. Cadaveric Study

2.1. Specimen collection and preparation

In this study, 10% formalin-fixed cadavers (n = 5) donated to the Department of Anatomy, Fukuoka University, Japan, were used. Blocks encompassing the superior mesenteric artery (SMA) and superior mesenteric vein (SMV) region, specifically from the inferior border of the pancreas to the distal SMA and SMV vessels, were precisely dissected and obtained. Each block was transversely cut into 10-mm intervals, and sections were then embedded in paraffin.

2.2. Histological analysis of the cadaveric specimens

The paraffin-embedded blocks were cut into 4 μm-thick slices. Standard hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, Elastica van Gieson staining, and Masson's trichrome staining were performed to identify and characterize the presence, location, and composition of the thin membranous structures, focusing on connective tissues, around the SMA and SMV.

3. Clinical Study: Histological Examination of the Surgical Specimens

3.1. Patient selection and specimen collection

Surgical resection specimens were obtained from patients (n = 5; 3 males and 2 females) with primary RSCC. These patients underwent primary resection at Fukuoka University Hospital from October 2024 to October 2025. Specimens, focusing on the ileocolic artery (ICA) and ileocolic vein (ICV) regions, were obtained from the resected tissues.

3.2. Histological analysis of surgical specimens

The collected specimens were embedded in paraffin and cut into 4 μm-thick slices. Sections were then stained with H&E for histological examination.

4. Clinical Study: Video-Based Intraoperative Observation

4.1. Patient selection and non-edited video review

This retrospective study reviewed non-edited operative videos of patients with RSCC who underwent primary tumor resection via a robotic approach with D3 lymph node dissection (corresponding to CVL) at Fukuoka University Hospital from January 2023 to October 2025. Patients who underwent open or conventional laparoscopic surgeries, as well as those who received less than a D3 lymph node dissection, were excluded from this study.

This study further excluded cases with incomplete or unavailable intraoperative video data from the cohort of patients who underwent robotic complete mesocolic excision (CME) (n = 100). The final video analysis included 78 patients. Supplemental Figure 1 details the patient selection process.

4.2. Intraoperative identification of membrane structures

Two independent surgeons, who are qualified by the Japanese endoscopic surgical skill qualification system[13] and blinded to the pathological outcomes, reviewed each non-edited operative video. They specifically assessed the visibility and anatomical relationships of the TMCTs around the SMA and SMV, which were identified in the cadaveric study, during the different phases of central lymphadenectomy. TMCT visibility was independently graded utilizing a three-point scale: clear, moderate, and unclear. Clear referred to a clearly continuous membranous structure. Moderate was defined as a membranous structure that was recognizable, although with partially indistinct continuity or boundary. Unclear referred to a structure that could not be reliably recognized as TMCT. In comparative analysis, clear or moderate cases were combined into the TMCT-clearly visible group, because TMCT was considered intraoperatively recognizable in both categories, whereas unclear cases were classified into the TMCT-poorly visible group. Further, the reviewers recorded the perceived usefulness of these structures as potential surgical landmarks. Interobserver agreement was evaluated using percent agreement and Cohen's kappa coefficient. Disagreements were resolved by consensus after joint review. Supplemental Video 1 presents our standardized surgical technique for identifying and dissecting along these membranous structures.

Results

1. Cadaveric Study

In the cadaveric study, histological analysis of the root of the mesentery revealed a distinct connective tissue layer surrounding the superior mesenteric vessels (n = 5). A cross-sectional examination of the SMA and SMV detected a continuous membranous structure that encased these vessels. This structure―termed the TMCT―was clearly identified not only with H&E staining (Figure 1A) but also with Elastica van Gieson (Figure 1B) and Masson's trichrome staining (Figure 1C), which confirm its collagenous and elastic nature. Figure 1D illustrates the anatomical association where the TMCT separates the vascular wall from the surrounding mesenteric adipose tissue and lymph nodes. Conversely, autonomic nerve fibers were present within this membrane, being particularly densely distributed around the SMA.

2. Clinical Study: Histological Examination and Confirmation of Perivascular Anatomy in Surgical Specimens

Histopathological evaluation of surgically resected specimens (n = 5) focused on the ileocolic pedicle to validate the cadaveric findings in a clinical setting. A TMCT was detected surrounding the ICA and ICV, consistent with the cadaveric data (Figure 2A). Crucially, the histological mapping revealed the topographical relationship between the vascular sheath and autonomic nerves. The TMCT forms a boundary distinct from the autonomic nerve fibers, located within the perivascular connective tissue but external to the proper vessel wall (Figure 2B).

(A) Hematoxylin and eosin staining of the surgically resected specimen containing the ileocolic artery (ICA), ileocolic vein (ICV), and lymph node (LN).

(B) Schematic illustration corresponding to panel A. Arrowheads indicate the thin membranous connective tissue (TMCT) surrounding the vessels. Yellow circles denote the autonomic nerves (N) located around the vascular sheath.

The TMCT separates the perivascular connective tissue (including autonomic nerve fibers) from the mesenteric fat containing the lymphatic tissue to be dissected, as depicted in B.

3. Clinical Study: Intraoperative Visibility and Surgical Application

This study retrospectively reviewed the intraoperative videos of 78 patients who underwent robotic right-sided colectomy for colon cancer with D3 lymphadenectomy, including CVL, to evaluate the clinical utility of these anatomical findings (Supplemental Figure 1). Table 1 summarizes patient characteristics and intraoperative outcomes. The high-resolution and magnified view of the robotic system enabled the identification of the perivascular membranous structure, regardless of age, sex, or body mass index (BMI). However, the clarity of this membrane differed among the patients. TMCT visibility was initially graded as clear, moderate, or unclear. Table 1 shows the detailed distribution. In comparative analysis, clear or moderate cases were classified into the TMCT-clearly visible group, whereas unclear cases were classified into the TMCT-poorly visible group. Of 78 cases, TMCT was identifiable (clear or moderate) in 69 cases (88%) and poorly visible in 9 cases (12%). The percent agreement between the two reviewers was 94%, and Cohen's kappa coefficient was 0.68. Discrepant cases were resolved by consensus after joint review. No intraoperative injuries to the central vessels (SMV and SMA) were observed. Figure 3 illustrates representative intraoperative images where the white arrows indicate the membranous structure considered to correspond to the histologically identified TMCT that covers the SMV and SMA. The consistent intraoperative identification of this layer confirms a dissection along the correct plane, termed the “Anatomical Dissection Line” (Figure 4), which corresponded anatomically to our histological findings.

The 78 patients were categorized into two groups based on the intraoperative clarity of the TMCT, and patient and surgeon characteristics were compared to investigate the factors associated with the visibility of this anatomical landmark (Table 2). No significant differences in patient characteristics, including age, sex, or BMI, were observed between groups (BMI, p = 0.55). Therefore, the clarity of the TMCT was not dependent on surgeon experience (expert vs. nonexpert, p = 0.32). Likewise, no significant difference was observed according to surgeon experience (expert vs. nonexpert, p = 0.32). These findings indicate that patient body habitus or surgeon experience did not significantly affect the intraoperative recognizability of the TMCT.

Furthermore, perioperative outcomes were compared between the two groups, revealing no statistically significant differences in estimated blood loss or the number of harvested lymph nodes, either overall or at the apical station, although the TMCT-clearly visible group achieved a higher lymph node yield.

4. Definition of the Optimal Dissection Plane

A schematic concept for the optimal surgical dissection line was established based on the integration of histological and intraoperative findings (Figure 4). Panel A depicts the cross-sectional anatomy where the perivascular membranous structure encases the SMV and SMA, separating them from the surrounding lymph nodes and mesenteric fat. Panel B defines the “anatomical dissection line.” Surgeons achieve an en bloc removal of the regional lymph nodes while maintaining TMCT integrity and potentially minimizing injury to the underlying vessel wall and autonomic nerve plexus by tracing this specific membranous layer (blue arrows). The initial dissection follows a defined anatomical plane, particularly the TMCT around the mesenteric axis, whereas the final step of lymphadenectomy invariably requires the surgeon to intentionally transect the mesentery. This terminal dissection line is nonanatomical; thus, the surgeon must deliberately identify its precise location to ensure the oncological completeness of the resected specimen.

Discussion

This study successfully identified and characterized the perivascular membranous connective tissue as a surgical landmark for D3 lymphadenectomy with CVL to address the clinical challenges of RSCC surgery. The existence and consistency of this structure―termed TMCT―were confirmed through a comprehensive multimodal approach. This study integrated histological analysis of cadaveric models, pathological examination of surgically resected clinical specimens, and intraoperative video review to establish a solid anatomical basis for recognizing the safe dissection plane around the superior mesenteric vessels.

The membranous structure recognized intraoperatively was considered to correspond to the histologically identified TMCT based on its anatomical location and morphological appearance. These findings support the need for improved surgical strategies by defining a reliable landmark that consistently exists despite individual vascular variations.

A previous study has reported structures, including the “outermost layer” of the vascular sheath, according to intraoperative observations[14], whereas our study provides robust histological validation using both cadaveric and clinical specimens. This scientific proof promotes the TMCT from a mere visual artifact to a distinct anatomical entity. However, the correspondence between the intraoperative membranous structure and the histologically defined TMCT was inferred from anatomical and morphological consistency rather than direct one-to-one validation at the same site. Crucially, our results indicate the TMCT as a definitive boundary between the mesocolon and the SMA and SMV. Recognizing this boundary helps surgeons to confidently identify the “anatomical dissection line” (Figure 4), thereby permitting high-quality CME. This concept is particularly vital for overcoming the inherent complexity of dissecting the variable vascular anatomy. Providing a precise navigational guide along the TMCT allows surgeons to maintain vessel wall integrity, thereby minimizing the risk of catastrophic vascular injury during central lymphadenectomy.

The paradoxical mortality rate associated with RSCC surgery[9], which exceeds that of some highly complex procedures, indicates the urgent need for standardization. D3 lymphadenectomy is a technically demanding procedure that relies on the surgeon's experience due to complex vascular anatomy and significant individual variations. Indeed, this challenge is well-recognized, and various strategies have been proposed to reduce the associated risks. These efforts range from technological aids, including intraoperative ultrasound for real-time vascular mapping[15], to functional visualization techniques using indocyanine green fluorescence to define a safe mesenteric dissection line following lymphatic flow[16]. Furthermore, detailed anatomical studies have aimed to pinpoint specific sources of bleeding, including the superior right colic vein[17]. These approaches provide valuable information on the location of individual vessels and lymphatic channels. Conversely, our study proposes a more fundamental solution―the identification of a consistent anatomical plane. Our comparative analysis provides robust evidence supporting this concept. Notably, the intraoperative identification of the TMCT was not dependent on the patient's BMI or the surgeon's experience level. This finding underscores the TMCT as an objective and universal anatomical landmark, rather than one identifiable only by highly experienced surgeons.

A similar membranous structure may have been present during conventional laparoscopic surgery; however, we had not previously recognized or intentionally used this structure as a surgical landmark. One possible reason for TMCT to be more readily identifiable in robotic surgery is the articulating function of robotic instruments, which enables stable multidirectional traction and countertraction around the superior mesenteric vessels. This technical advantage may help visualize the continuity of the membranous layer and enable its use as a practical intraoperative landmark. However, this study did not directly compare robotic and laparoscopic surgery; thus, this interpretation should be considered cautiously.

Moreover, the surgical implications of the TMCT differ between the anterior aspects of the SMV and SMA. On the anterior surface of the SMV, dissection is performed with the opening of the TMCT and exposure of the vein, whereas dissection is performed while preserving the TMCT on the anterior surface of the SMA. This distinction is based on our histological findings that reveal little intervening tissue between the TMCT and the SMV, in contrast to the presence of more abundant neural and small vascular structures between the TMCT and the SMA. Therefore, dissection deep to the TMCT over the SMA, with arterial adventitia exposure, may increase the risk of injury to these perivascular structures and may compromise functional preservation.

Importantly, the TMCT is not proposed as a structure that should always be uniformly preserved during central dissection. Rather, its significance lies in its role as a landmark for defining the appropriate dissection plane according to vessel-specific anatomy. On the anterior surface of the SMV, opening the TMCT is appropriate because it indicates the correct venous dissection plane where little tissue intervenes between the TMCT and the vein. In contrast, on the anterior surface of the SMA, preserving the TMCT is appropriate because it functions as a boundary that helps avoid excessively deep dissection into perivascular neural and small vascular tissue.

Therefore, incorporating the TMCT as a primary surgical target could establish a common foundation for all surgeons, which significantly helps in procedure standardization and reproducibility. Establishing this consistent structure as a standard surgical landmark is a crucial step toward reducing the technical variability inherent in radical surgery for RSCC. At present, the 78 cases included in this study experienced no recurrence in the central nodal area. This observation may reflect the recent introduction of the robotic technique in 2023 and the relatively short follow-up period; however, it indicates that the present TMCT-oriented approach has not obviously compromised early central oncological clearance.

This study has several limitations to be acknowledged. First, this is a single-center retrospective study. Second, the correspondence between the intraoperatively recognized membranous structure and the histologically defined TMCT was inferred based on anatomical location and morphological features, rather than direct one-to-one histological validation at the same site. Importantly, the TMCT is a structure intended to be preserved around the SMA/SMV during surgery rather than resected as part of the surgical specimen. Therefore, a strict one-to-one histological correlation between the intraoperative finding and the same anatomical site is methodologically challenging. Third, tissue processing, including fixation, dehydration, embedding, and lipid extraction, may affect histological findings; thus, the apparent spaces or fat-like areas around the TMCT may not fully reflect the actual in vivo three-dimensional structure. Fourth, this robotic technique was introduced in 2023; thus, robust evaluation of long-term oncological outcomes is impossible in the present study, and further follow-up is warranted. Fifth, our exploratory comparative analysis based on the clarity of TMCT identification revealed no statistically significant differences in operative outcomes, including estimated blood loss and the number of harvested lymph nodes. The lack of statistical significance may be multifactorial, including the limited sample size. Furthermore, the procedures were performed at a specialized cancer center with a robust quality control system for surgical oncology. This inherent high standard of care across all cases may obscure the specific advantages of utilizing TMCT as a surgical landmark. The higher average number of harvested lymph nodes in the clearly identified group is noteworthy, although not statistically significant. Future prospective, multi-institutional studies with longer follow-up are warranted to determine whether a TMCT-guided approach translates into a statistically significant improvement in oncological quality and operative safety, particularly in a wider range of surgical settings.

Conclusion

Safe and complete D3 lymphadenectomy, focusing on CVL, is crucial for the surgical treatment of RSCC but remains technically demanding. This study histologically validates TMCT as a consistent anatomical landmark that is identifiable regardless of the surgeon's experience or the patient's BMI. Intraoperative TMCT identification precisely delineates the avascular plane around the superior mesenteric vessels. Using this objective landmark provides a practical foundation for standardizing D3 lymphadenectomy with CVL, thereby potentially improving the safety and reproducibility of this challenging procedure.
