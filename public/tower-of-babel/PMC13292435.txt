Robotic-assisted versus conventional nipple-sparing mastectomy with immediate implant-based breast reconstruction: a GRADE-assessed systematic review and meta-analysis of prospective studies
Authors: Wajahat Mirza, Hania Iqbal, Mehak Ejaz Khan, Alishbah Khan, Hadi Mohammad Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRobotic nipple-sparing mastectomy with immediate implant-based reconstruction is increasingly adopted to improve cosmetic outcomes and patient experience; however, its comparative perioperative value against conventional or open nipple-sparing mastectomy remains uncertain when restricted to prospective evidence.MethodsWe performed a PRISMA-aligned systematic review and random-effects meta-analysis of prospective comparative studies and randomized trials comparing robotic versus conventional or open nipple-sparing mastectomy with immediate implant-based reconstruction. The primary outcome was the total operative time, and the secondary outcomes were major complications, nipple-areolar complex ischemia or necrosis, and length of hospital stay. The risk of bias was assessed using RoB 2 for randomized data and ROBINS-I for nonrandomized data. Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were conducted for outcomes with substantial heterogeneity, and the certainty of the evidence was graded using GRADE.ResultsThree prospective studies were included. Robotic surgery was associated with a significantly longer total operative time (mean difference 64.01 min, 95% confidence interval 8.54–119.47). Major complications did not differ between the approaches (risk ratio 0.63, 95% confidence interval 0.27–1.47), nor did nipple areolar complex ischemia or necrosis (risk ratio 0.56, 95% confidence interval 0.28–1.11) or length of hospital stay (mean difference 0.22 days, 95% confidence interval − 0.26 to 0.70). Heterogeneity was high for operative time and moderate to substantial for length of stay. The certainty of the evidence was low overall, driven by imprecision, heterogeneity, and limitations of the non-randomized design.ConclusionsRobotic nipple-sparing mastectomy is associated with a longer operative time than conventional or open approaches. No statistically significant differences were observed in major complications or nipple–areolar complex viability; however, the current prospective evidence base is limited, heterogeneous, and imprecise, and clinically important differences cannot be excluded. In experienced centers, robotic nipple-sparing mastectomy may be considered for carefully selected patients who prioritize scar concealment and minimally invasive access; however, its broader role in routine practice remains uncertain pending stronger prospective data on safety, patient-reported outcomes, and resource use.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-026-04473-6.

Introduction

Nipple-sparing mastectomy (NSM) with immediate implant-based breast reconstruction (IBBR) is increasingly used for therapeutic and risk-reducing breast surgery, preserving the skin envelope and nipple–areolar complex (NAC) without compromising oncological outcomes. However, outcomes remain constrained by flap pathophysiology, as subcutaneous undermining impairs dermal microcirculation and venous drainage, predisposing patients to NAC or skin flap ischemia, necrosis, infection, and implant loss, complications that necessitate reoperation and affect patient satisfaction. Randomized data comparing robotic and open mastectomy show that minimally invasive approaches may improve perioperative performance and patient-reported outcomes; however, uncertainty persists regarding complication patterns and resource use [1].

Robot-assisted NSM (R-NSM) relocates surgical access to a lateral incision, concealing scarring and enhancing dissection precision through stable instrumentation and magnified visualization. A prospective multicenter trial comparing robotic, conventional, and endoscopic-assisted NSM with immediate prosthesis reconstruction demonstrated that minimally invasive techniques achieved shorter incisions and superior wound-related satisfaction, although operative time and medical costs remained important trade-offs that varied by technique and workflow [2]. Implant outcomes are also shaped by the reconstructive strategy and patient selection, with real-world practice comparisons continuing to report heterogeneity in postoperative outcomes, satisfaction, and cost, complicating the direct attribution of benefit to the mastectomy approach alone [3].

Recent meta-analyses comparing robotic, conventional, and endoscopic NSM suggest potential cosmetic and patient-reported advantages for minimally invasive approaches but emphasize longer operative times, higher costs, and limited certainty regarding uncommon adverse events and long-term oncologic outcomes [4]. Contemporary studies have expanded the landscape with single-incision robotic strategies [5], large-center feasibility data [6], single-port comparisons [7], reconstruction plane evaluations [8], and parallel non-robotic minimal-access approaches, including laterothoracic and video-assisted NSM [9, 10]. Patient-reported outcome studies indicate that adjuncts, such as meshes, meaningfully influence satisfaction beyond complication rates [11].

Despite the growing experience, a critical gap persists: clinicians lack a prospective-only, NSM-specific, IBBR-focused synthesis aligned with multidisciplinary decision-making. Large pooled analyses remain predominantly observational and vulnerable to confounding [12, 13], while existing reviews highlight promise but underscore unresolved controversies regarding cost-effectiveness and long-term safety with inconsistent certainty assessments [14, 15]. Therefore, this review synthesizes prospective comparative evidence for R-NSM versus conventional NSM with immediate IBBR, incorporating prespecified subgroup and sensitivity analyses and GRADE certainty assessment to provide clinically actionable conclusions [1–15].

Methods

Study design

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement and followed a priori protocol defining the population (women undergoing nipple-sparing mastectomy with immediate implant-based reconstruction), intervention (robotic-assisted NSM), comparator (conventional/open NSM), outcomes (perioperative complications, patient-reported outcomes, and costs), and eligible study designs (prospective comparative studies and randomized trials). The review question was structured to support procedure-level decision-making in contemporary oncoplastic practice, with prespecified plans for random-effects synthesis, leave-one-out sensitivity analyses, geographic subgrouping, and GRADE certainty assessment.

Literature search strategy

A comprehensive electronic search was planned and executed across major biomedical databases from inception to the final search date using controlled vocabulary (e.g., MeSH/Emtree where applicable) and text words for robotic surgery, nipple-sparing mastectomy, endoscopic/minimally invasive techniques, and implant/prosthesis-based reconstructions. The search strategy was adapted to the database syntax and supplemented by manual screening of the reference lists of eligible full-text articles and relevant reviews. The full electronic strategies and yields are presented in Supplementary Table 1. The overall screening process followed a two-stage workflow (title/abstract screening, followed by full-text assessment).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

We included (1) prospective comparative cohort studies and randomized controlled trials enrolling women undergoing nipple-sparing mastectomy with immediate implant/prosthesis-based reconstruction; (2) studies comparing robotic-assisted NSM with conventional/open NSM (with or without additional endoscopic arms, provided a conventional/open comparator arm was available); and (3) studies reporting at least one pre-specified outcome of interest. We intentionally restricted eligibility to prospective comparative designs to reduce confounding, selection bias, and outcome ascertainment bias, which are common in retrospective surgical comparisons, particularly in an evolving field where learning-curve effects, case selection, and institutional workflow may strongly influence perioperative outcomes.

Exclusion criteria

We excluded (1) retrospective studies, case series without a concurrent comparator, case reports, conference abstracts, posters, editorials, commentaries, and technique-only descriptions without comparative outcomes; (2) studies evaluating reconstruction strategies not aligned with the review question (e.g., autologous-only reconstruction without implant/prosthesis cohorts or mixed reconstruction where implant-based outcomes could not be separated); (3) studies where the surgical approach was not clearly defined or where robotic assistance was used only for reconstruction rather than mastectomy; (4) duplicate reports from overlapping populations (in which case the most complete dataset was retained); and (5) non-English full texts when adequate outcome extraction could not be reliably performed.

Data extraction and outcome measures

Two reviewers (W.M. and M.E.K.) independently screened the titles/abstracts and full texts, extracted data using a standardized form, cross-verified all numerical values against the source documents, and extracted the table images. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and, when required, arbitration by a third author (H.K. I.). The extracted items included study characteristics (design, setting, and eligibility criteria), participant demographics and baseline clinical variables, surgical and reconstruction details, and outcomes of interest. The prespecified outcomes included operative time, length of stay, grade ≥ 2/≥3 complications (as defined by each study), and NAC/skin flap ischemia. Operative time was extracted from the original studies. In the randomized trial, operative time corresponded to the total surgery time (entire procedural duration), whereas in other prospective studies, operative time was reported without a fully standardized definition (e.g., incision-to-closure or equivalent operative interval) and may have variably included platform-related steps such as setup or docking. Because the definitions were not uniform across studies and component-specific times (e.g., docking or preparation time) were not consistently reported, separate subgroup analyses for operative time components were not feasible. When outcomes were reported at multiple time points, the most clinically relevant prespecified time point was used for pooling, and the other time points were summarized.

Quality assessment

The risk of bias was assessed at the study level by two independent reviewers (W.M. and M.E.K.). Randomized trials were evaluated using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool across domains, including randomization, deviations from the intended intervention, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selective reporting. Prospective non-randomized comparative studies were assessed using ROBINS-I, focusing on confounding, selection of participants, classification of interventions, deviations from the intended interventions, missing data, outcome measurement, and selection of reported results. Any discrepancies in the judgments were resolved by consensus, with input from H.I. Study-level bias assessments were incorporated into the GRADE certainty ratings for each outcome.

Statistical analysis

Meta-analyses were performed using random-effects models to account for anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity (e.g., patient selection, reconstructive approach, outcome definitions, and perioperative pathways). Dichotomous outcomes were summarized using risk ratios (RRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Continuous outcomes were summarized using mean differences (MDs) with 95% CIs when measured on the same scale, and standardized mean differences (SMDs) were used if different validated scales assessed the same construct. When studies reported medians with dispersion measures that could not be reliably converted, outcomes were summarized narratively. Statistical heterogeneity was quantified using the I² statistic and interpreted alongside clinical heterogeneity and consistency of the effect direction. When fewer than three studies contributed to an outcome, between-study variance estimates were interpreted cautiously, and findings were contextualized using narrative synthesis. Formal meta-regression and covariate-based subgroup analyses were not performed because only three studies met the eligibility criteria, which is insufficient for a statistically reliable exploration of between-study moderators such as breast weight, reconstruction type, smoking status, prior radiotherapy exposure, or unilateral versus bilateral surgery.

Sensitivity analysis, subgroup analysis, and GRADE certainty of evidence

Sensitivity analyses were prespecified as leave-one-out analyses for each pooled outcome, repeating the meta-analysis after sequentially removing one study at a time to evaluate the robustness of the summary effects, and identify influential studies. Subgroup analysis was prespecified by geographic region, defined as Europe (Italy and France: two studies) versus Asia (Taiwan: one study) for the current included evidence set; where a subgroup contained only a single study, subgroup-specific pooling was not performed, and the subgroup was summarized descriptively, whereas the overall pooled estimate was retained as the primary inference. Because only three studies were eligible, formal subgroup meta-analysis and meta-regression for other potential effect modifiers, including breast weight, reconstruction type, smoking status, prior radiotherapy exposure, and laterality, were not statistically reliable and, therefore, not performed. Instead, these factors were explored qualitatively through study-level baseline comparisons, leave-one-out sensitivity analyses, and narrative interpretations of case-mix and perioperative pathway differences. The certainty of evidence for each outcome was assessed using the GRADE approach and rated as high, moderate, low, or very low based on the risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias, with upgrading or downgrading decisions documented per outcome. This framework was used to ensure that the conclusions emphasized not only statistical significance but also the trustworthiness and clinical interpretability of evidence.

Results

The literature search identified 847 records across six databases, with no records retrieved from trial registries or other sources of information. After removing 327 records (predominantly duplicates), 520 records underwent title/abstract screening, of which 480 were excluded. Full texts were retrieved for 40 reports, with no reports not retrieved, and 37 were excluded during eligibility assessment. Ultimately, three prospective comparative studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the qualitative synthesis and meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of included studies

Three prospective studies were included: one randomized trial and two prospective comparative studies from Europe and Asia. The sample sizes ranged from 80 to 229 participants, with a consistent focus on NSM followed by an immediate reconstruction. Across studies, the primary endpoints centered on complications and/or patient-reported outcomes, while secondary endpoints commonly captured operative metrics, hospital stay, cost, and early oncologic safety (Table 1).

Methodological quality and study integrity

Across the included evidence, the RoB 2 traffic-light plot for the randomized trial showed a low risk of bias arising from the randomization process and missing outcome data, but some concerns were related to deviations from the intended interventions, outcome measurement, and selective reporting, resulting in an overall judgment of some concerns for the trial (Supplementary Fig. 1). This pattern is mirrored in the RoB 2 summary plot, where these domains account for the proportion of judgments rated as some concerns (Supplementary Fig. 2). For the prospective non-randomized studies, the ROBINS-I traffic-light plot demonstrated an overall moderate risk of bias for both studies, driven mainly by moderate confounding and participant-selection bias, with additional moderate risk in outcome measurement and selective reporting, while the classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, and missing data were judged to be at low risk (Supplementary Fig. 3). The ROBINS-I summary plot similarly indicated that moderate-risk judgments were concentrated in the confounding/selection and measurement/reporting domains, supporting an overall moderate certainty in internal validity for the observational evidence base (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Primary outcome

Total operative time

Across three prospective studies, the total operative time was significantly longer in the robotic group (MD 64.01 min, 95% CI 8.54–119.47; P = 0.02) (Fig. 2). Heterogeneity was considerable (I² = 96%), indicating substantial variability between studies. Given this extreme inconsistency, the pooled estimate should be interpreted cautiously, alongside a structured narrative assessment of study-level differences in case mix, reconstruction pathway, and institutional workflow. Although the direction of the effect favored a shorter operative time with conventional/open NSM, the magnitude of the difference was likely influenced by baseline imbalances and center-specific operative processes.

Sensitivity analysis

In the leave-one-out sensitivity analyses, removing Lai 2024 reduced heterogeneity for operative time; however, the pooled effect remained statistically significant, continuing to show a longer operative time with robotic NSM than with conventional NSM. This finding suggests that Lai 2024 was an important contributor to between-study variability, potentially reflecting differences in baseline case mix, incomplete baseline reporting, and a higher proportion of bilateral procedures in the robotic group. For major complications, the exclusion of Lai 2024 eliminated heterogeneity, but the overall effect remained non-significant, indicating that the pooled estimate was sensitive to the study composition and should be interpreted cautiously. Regarding length of stay, removing the study by Houvenaeghel et al. (2021) reduced heterogeneity to 0% and confirmed no significant difference between the approaches, suggesting that center-specific discharge pathways and reconstruction practices likely influenced the outcome. Overall, sensitivity analyses mainly affected heterogeneity and precision, supporting a cautious interpretation in which narrative synthesis is particularly important for outcomes with marked between-study inconsistencies (Supplementary Table 2).

Subgroup analysis

Although we prespecified subgroup analyses (including geographic subgroups), quantitative subgroup meta-analysis was not feasible because each subgroup required at least two studies to generate pooled estimates. With only three eligible studies, stratification by geography or other potentially relevant modifiers, such as breast weight, reconstruction type, smoking status, prior radiotherapy exposure, or unilateral versus bilateral surgery, would have resulted in single study subgroups or statistically unreliable comparisons. Therefore, subgroup findings were interpreted descriptively by comparing the direction and magnitude of the effects across studies and examining study-level differences in case-mix and perioperative pathways rather than performing formal pooled subgroup estimates, meta-regression, or tests for subgroup differences.

GRADE certainty of evidence

Supplementary Table 3 shows that certainty was low for operative time, major complications, and NAC ischemia/necrosis, and very low for the length of hospital stay. For the risk of bias, certainty was downgraded because the body of evidence included one randomized trial with some concerns and two non-randomized prospective comparisons with residual confounding and incomplete control of clinically relevant baseline differences, including smoking status, breast weight, laterality, reconstruction differences, prior radiotherapy/neoadjuvant treatment exposure, and incomplete baseline reporting. Operative time was downgraded for serious inconsistency because of the high heterogeneity (I² = 96%). Major complications and NAC ischemia/necrosis were downgraded for serious imprecision because the confidence intervals crossed the no-effect threshold and remained compatible with clinically important differences in either direction. Length of hospital stay was graded very low because it combined serious risk of bias, serious inconsistency (I² = 68%), and serious imprecision (confidence interval crossing zero). Absolute effects for dichotomous outcomes were calculated directly from the observed pooled control event rates (per 1,000) and pre-sensitivity pooled risk ratios, without using any assumed baseline risk (Supplementary Table 3).

Discussion

This prospective-only synthesis evaluated whether robotic nipple-sparing mastectomy (R-NSM) with immediate implant-based reconstruction confers measurable perioperative advantages over conventional NSM. Across the included prospective studies, robotic surgery was associated with a significantly longer total operative duration. In contrast, major complications, NAC ischemia/necrosis (any grade), and length of hospital stay showed no significant differences between the two approaches. These findings align with the mechanistic expectation that robotic workflows introduce setup, docking, and dissection complexity, yet preserve key safety endpoints when performed in experienced centers using standardized mastectomy planes and implant-based reconstruction pathways [1–3].

The most pronounced finding of our meta-analysis was the prolonged operative duration. This is biologically plausible in R-NSM, attributable to additional procedural steps, including port placement, docking, remote dissection within confined working spaces, and learning curve effects. Even in controlled settings, platform-related tasks such as draping, docking, and instrument exchange can meaningfully extend theater time [1, 2]. However, the substantial heterogeneity observed suggests that operative duration is strongly influenced by team experience, institutional workflow, case-mix factors, and reconstruction strategy [2, 3]. In particular, Table 2 indicates that the robotic cohort in Houvenaeghel et al. had higher tobacco exposure, larger breast weight, greater prior radiotherapy exposure, more neoadjuvant chemotherapy exposure, and substantially greater use of LDF-based reconstruction, all of which may prolong operative time and complicate the attribution of the observed difference to the robotic platform alone. In the study by Lai et al., baseline reporting was limited, and the robotic group included a higher proportion of bilateral procedures, which may have inflated the operative duration and contributed to between-study variability. Because only three studies were eligible, formal meta-regression and covariate-based subgroup analyses were not statistically reliable; therefore, operative time is better interpreted through a combination of pooled analysis, leave-one-out sensitivity testing, and structured narrative synthesis rather than by relying on the pooled mean difference alone. Contemporary comparisons of minimal-access NSM, encompassing both robotic and endoscopic techniques, consistently demonstrate that time penalties are most pronounced during early adoption and may narrow considerably with protocolization, dedicated multi-disciplinary teams, and standardized instrumentation [12, 13, 16, 17]. Technique-focused reports further emphasize that incision planning, workspace creation, and specimen extraction strategy represent major determinants of efficiency and may partially mitigate time costs without compromising oncologic principles or skin/NAC viability [5, 9, 18].

Regarding clinically important safety endpoints, our pooled analyses revealed no statistically significant differences in major complications or NAC ischemia/necrosis between the two groups. From a clinical perspective, the absence of a statistically significant difference should not be interpreted as proof of equivalent safety between the two groups. However, the available prospective evidence is limited and imprecise, with modest event counts and wide confidence intervals, such that clinically important differences in either direction cannot be excluded. NAC perfusion is a critical biological bottleneck in NSM. Ischemia and necrosis reflect the delicate balance between flap thickness, microvascular integrity, thermal injury, and tension/pressure effects after implant placement. Minimal-access techniques may theoretically reduce incision length and traction-related flap stress, potentially improving perfusion in selected patients; conversely, prolonged operative time and extended pneumodissection could counterbalance these theoretical advantages [2, 12, 16, 17]. Prior comparative syntheses and narrative reviews have generally reported similar complication rates across robotic, endoscopic, and conventional techniques, while highlighting the outcome sensitivity to patient selection factors (ptosis, smoking status), incision choice, and reconstruction adjuncts [4, 14–16, 19]. Emerging institutional experiences, including single-center comparisons and larger minimal-access datasets, suggest that when standardized eligibility criteria and perioperative pathways are applied, robotic and minimal-access NSM can achieve acceptable complication rates. However, the current prospective comparative evidence remains insufficient to confirm equivalent safety relative to conventional surgery, and patient preference regarding scar placement and cosmesis should be weighed alongside this uncertainty [17, 20].

From a health system perspective, the central trade-off remains resource investment versus potential patient-centered benefits. Prospective evidence has demonstrated smaller incisions and improved wound-related satisfaction metrics for minimal-access approaches; however, these benefits must be weighed against the longer operative time, specialized equipment requirements, and acquisition/maintenance costs [2, 3]. The broader clinical literature underscores that cost-effectiveness is highly context-dependent; local operating room efficiency, case throughput, reimbursement models, and whether robotic platforms are already integrated into institutional practice all influence the incremental cost per quality-adjusted benefit [2, 3, 12]. Parallel developments in robotic breast surgery, ranging from technique optimization to workflow standardization, suggest that value propositions may improve over time, particularly when robotic cases are concentrated among trained teams operating within consensus-based safety frameworks [16, 21–23]. Additionally, reconstructive refinements (prepectoral versus subpectoral implant placement, device selection, and adjunct materials) may interact with the surgical approach, influencing postoperative pain, animation deformity, seroma risk, and patient-reported outcomes, factors that are increasingly prioritized in contemporary reconstructive algorithms [8, 11, 24–26].

Several recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses have mapped the expanding landscape of robotic and minimal-access NSM, including comparative studies of robotic, endoscopic, and conventional methods and single-arm syntheses of single-port robotic approaches [4, 14, 19, 27]. Our study adds clinically relevant granularity by focusing exclusively on prospective comparative evidence and pairing pooled estimates with leave-one-out sensitivity analyses and GRADE certainty assessments, thereby clarifying both the robustness of the findings and the limits of inference in this rapidly evolving field. This prospective-only restriction was intentional, as retrospective surgical comparisons in robotic breast surgery are particularly vulnerable to confounding by indication, selective case allocation, learning curve effects, and institutional differences in reconstruction pathways and perioperative care. However, this methodological choice also narrowed the evidence base to only three eligible studies, which reduced the statistical power, limited the formal exploration of heterogeneity, and constrained the external validity. In particular, excluding retrospective comparative cohorts, including propensity-matched analyses, may have reduced our ability to capture rarer complications, broader real-world case mix, and longer-term outcomes. Notably, sensitivity analyses indicated that heterogeneity in operative time and hospital stay was largely study-driven, reinforcing that institutional pathways and patient selection substantially shaped these outcomes. Although subgroup meta-analysis was prespecified, it proved infeasible because each subgroup required at least two studies; consequently, geographic differences and other potential effect modifiers were assessed qualitatively by comparing the direction and magnitude of the effects across settings.

GRADE certainty ratings were predominantly low for most outcomes, reflecting outcome-specific concerns regarding residual confounding and imprecision. For risk of bias, the evidence was downgraded because the body of evidence included non-randomized prospective comparisons in which important confounders were incompletely controlled, including smoking status, breast weight, laterality, reconstruction differences, and incomplete baseline reporting. Operative time was downgraded for serious inconsistency because of the high heterogeneity (I² = 96%). Major complications and NAC ischemia/necrosis were downgraded for serious imprecision because the confidence intervals crossed no effect and remained compatible with clinically important differences in both directions. Length of hospital stay was downgraded for serious inconsistency and serious imprecision. These certainty limitations are consistent with the broader evidence base, where high-quality randomized data remain scarce despite increasing adoption and publication volumes [6, 7, 10, 12, 13, 28–30]. Clinically, these findings support a cautious and pragmatic interpretation: robotic NSM may be considered in appropriately selected patients and experienced centers, but the current prospective evidence remains too limited and imprecise to confirm equivalent safety or to exclude clinically important differences in less-frequent adverse outcomes. Ongoing trials and high-quality comparative studies with standardized reporting, longer follow-up, and more complete adjustment for baseline imbalances will be essential to refine our understanding of oncologic outcomes, patient-reported endpoints, and real-world comparative effectiveness [2, 16, 17, 21, 28].

From a practical clinical standpoint, the present evidence suggests that the principal potential advantage of robotic NSM is not a clearly demonstrated reduction in postoperative complications or hospital stay but rather the possibility of improved scar concealment and minimally visible access in carefully selected patients. Accordingly, robotic NSM may be most appropriate for patients who place a high value on cosmetic outcomes and are treated in centers with established robotic workflows and coordinated breast–plastic reconstructive expertise. Conversely, in patients with larger breast volumes, more complex reconstructive requirements, prior radiotherapy, active smoking exposure, or anticipated bilateral procedures, operative complexity may be greater, and the incremental benefit of the robotic approach may be less certain. The longer operative time observed with robotic NSM also has practical implications for the operating room utilization, team workload, and institutional resource allocation, particularly during early adoption or in lower-volume programs. Therefore, broader implementation should be approached cautiously and guided by local expertise, structured training, and transparent, preoperative counseling. Future prospective comparative studies should prioritize standardized complication definitions, complete baseline reporting, longer oncologic follow-up, patient-reported outcomes, and cost-effectiveness analyses to better identify the patients and practice settings that derive meaningful benefits from robotic NSM.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this review include the deliberate restriction to prospective comparative studies, the use of random-effects models, predefined sensitivity analyses, and structured GRADE-based certainty appraisal. However, this design choice introduces an important trade-off. By excluding retrospective comparative studies, including potentially high-quality matched cohorts, we prioritized internal validity at the cost of a smaller evidence base and a reduced external validity. Only three studies were eligible, limiting precision, restricting formal exploration of heterogeneity, and reducing confidence for infrequent outcomes and longer-term endpoints. Additional limitations include residual confounding in non-randomized prospective studies, incomplete harmonization of complication definitions, substantial heterogeneity in operative time and hospital stay, and the inability to formally assess publication bias with so few studies. Stratification of complications into short- and long-term outcomes was not feasible, as the included studies did not consistently report complications using standardized temporal definitions or comparable follow-up intervals, which limited further granularity of outcome interpretation.

Conclusions

In prospective comparative evidence, robotic NSM with immediate implant-based reconstruction is associated with a longer operative time, while no statistically significant differences were observed in major complications, NAC ischemia/necrosis, or hospital stay compared with conventional/open NSM. However, the current prospective evidence base remains limited, heterogeneous, and imprecise, and clinically important differences cannot be excluded from the results. At present, robotic NSM may be considered in carefully selected patients treated by experienced teams, particularly when scar concealment and minimally invasive access are important patient priorities; however, its use should be balanced against operative time, resource implications, and institutional workflow maturity. Stronger prospective data with standardized reporting, longer follow-up, and clearer evidence on oncologic, patient-reported, and cost-effectiveness outcomes are needed before broader adoption can be confidently recommended.
