Abstract
. A λ \lambda -convex body in a three-dimensional space form M 3 ​ ( c ) M^{3}(c) of constant curvature c c is a compact convex set K K whose boundary ∂ K \partial K has normal curvatures bounded below by a constant λ > 0 \lambda>0 (in a weak sense). Within this class, we prove a sharp reverse isoperimetric inequality: among all λ \lambda -convex bodies in M 3 ​ ( c ) M^{3}(c) , with a fixed surface area, the body of minimal volume is the λ \lambda -convex lens , i.e., the domain bounded by two totally umbilical caps of curvature λ \lambda . Moreover, this minimizer is unique. This result confirms Borisenko’s Conjecture in the three-dimensional model spaces of constant curvature for c ≠ 0 c\neq 0 , and complements recent progress on the conjecture in the Euclidean case c = 0 c=0 . As a by-product, our method also yields an alternative proof of the corresponding reverse isoperimetric inequality in two-dimensional hyperbolic space.



1.
Introduction

Let
n\geqslant 2
, and let
M^{n}(c)
be an
n
-dimensional model space of constant sectional curvature
c\in\mathbb{R}
. A subset
K\subset M^{n}(c)
is convex if a geodesic segment between any two points in
K
is contained in
K
(in the spherical space we also assume that
K
lies in an open hemisphere). We say that
K\subset M^{n}(c)
is a convex body if it is a compact, convex subset with non-empty interior.

For a given
\lambda>0
, a convex body
K\subset M^{n}(c)
is called
\lambda
-convex
if the principal curvatures
k_{1},\ldots,k_{n-1}
with respect to inward pointing normals of the boundary
\partial K
satisfy

k_{i}(p)\geqslant\lambda\qquad i=1,\ldots,n-1,\quad\forall p\in\partial K.

If the boundary of
K
is not smooth, then the bound above is understood in the weak sense, which we will make precise in Definition
2.1
. A simple but important example of a
\lambda
-convex body is the so-called
\lambda
-convex lens
, which is the domain bounded by two totally umbilical hypersurfaces of normal curvature
\lambda
.

The class of
\lambda
-convex bodies has attracted a lot of attention in convex and discrete geometry in the past two decades, e.g.,
[
AAF
,
AACF
,
Be
,
Be2
,
FRS
,
JMR
,
SWY
]
. In particular, it naturally appears in connection with the study of the Kneser–Poulsen conjecture and bodies of constant width. We refer the reader to the surveys
[
BLN
,
BLNP
]
for a comprehensive overview of
\lambda
-convexity and related topics.

In recent years, the
reverse
isoperimetric problems in the class of
\lambda
-convex bodies have received considerable interest. One of the central open problems in this direction is the following conjecture of Borisenko. We will always denote by
|A|
the
m
-dimensional volume of a compact submanifold with boundary
A^{m}\subset M^{n}(c)
.

Borisenko’s Conjecture
.

Let
K\subset M^{n}(c)
be a
\lambda
-convex body,
n\geqslant 2
, and let
L\subset M^{n}(c)
be a
\lambda
-convex
lens
. If
|\partial K|=|\partial L|
, then
|K|\geqslant|L|
, with equality if and only if
K
is a
\lambda
-convex lens.

In Euclidean space, this conjecture was established for
n=2
in
[
BD2
]
(see also the alternative proof in
[
FKV
]
) and, very recently, for
n=3
in
[
DT
]
. For dimensions
n\geqslant 4
, the conjecture remains completely open (see also
[
HSch
]
where the authors prove that the solution to this conjecture cannot have a smooth boundary).

In nonflat space forms, progress has thus far been restricted to the two-dimensional case: the conjecture was fully resolved for all two-dimensional spaces of constant curvature and certain natural generalizations in
[
BD3
,
Dr1
,
Bor
]
.

In the present paper, we make a further nontrivial advance by resolving Borisenko’s conjecture affirmatively in three-dimensional space forms of nonzero curvature. Combined with the Euclidean result of
[
DT
]
, this completes the verification of the conjecture in all three-dimensional space forms. Our main result is the following.

Main Theorem

(Reverse isoperimetric inequality in nonflat space forms)
.

Let
K\subset M^{3}(c)
,
c\neq 0
, be a
\lambda
-convex body, and let
L\subset M^{3}(c)
be a
\lambda
-convex lens. If
|\partial K|=|\partial L|
, then
|K|\geqslant|L|
, with equality if and only if
K
is a
\lambda
-convex lens.

Our method also yields an alternative proof of the corresponding reverse isoperimetric inequality in the two-dimensional hyperbolic space, originally obtained in
[
Dr1
]
.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we aim to recall some general background from convex geometry and
\lambda
-convexity. We denote by
M^{n}(c)
the complete simply connected
n
-dimensional Riemannian manifold of constant curvature
c
. We call
M^{n}(c)
a
space form
(of curvature
c
). When
c=0
, the space form is the standard Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. When
c>0
, it is a standard spherical space
\mathbb{S}^{n}(c)
, and when
c<0
, it is a standard hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{n}(c)
.

For a given
\lambda>0
, let
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}\subset M^{n}(c)
be a complete totally umbilical hypersurface of constant normal curvature equal to
\lambda
. We will call such hypersurfaces
\lambda
-spheres
and will use the notation
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}
for the convex and topologically closed region bounded by
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}
; we call
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}
a
\lambda
-ball
. Note that, in the hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{n}(-1)
, the
\lambda
-balls (and spheres) are not always geodesic balls (or spheres), as the following classification suggests:

(1)

If
\lambda>1
, then
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}
is a geodesic sphere of radius
\coth^{-1}\lambda
, and
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}
is the geodesic ball.

(2)

If
\lambda=1
, then
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}
is a
horosphere
and
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}
is a
horoball
.

(3)

Finally, if
0<\lambda<1
, then
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}
is an
equidistant hypersurface
(or
equidistant
for short), i.e., the set of points at a given distance from a totally geodesic hyperplane. In the literature, equidistants are also called
hyperspheres
.

In the Poincaré unit ball model of
\mathbb{H}^{n}(-1)
, the
\lambda
-spheres
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}
are the intersections of the unit ball with Euclidean spheres that: lie inside the unit ball if
\lambda>1
, touch the unit sphere tangentially if
\lambda=1
, and intersect the unit sphere at an angle different from
\pi/2
if
0<\lambda<1
.

2.1.
\lambda
-convex bodies

Fix some
\lambda>0
.

Definition 2.1

(
\lambda
-convex body)
.

A convex body
K\subset M^{n}(c)
is
\lambda
-convex
if for each
p\in\partial K
there exists a neighborhood
U_{p}\subset M^{n}(c)
and a
\lambda
-sphere
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}
that passes through
p
in such a way that

U_{p}\cap\partial K\subset\mathcal{B}_{\lambda},

where
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}
is the corresponding
\lambda
-ball.

In this case, we call
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}
a
supporting
\lambda
-sphere
passing through
p\in\partial K
.

The following classical theorem of Blaschke turns the local condition in Definition
2.1
into a global one.

Theorem 2.2

(Blaschke’s Rolling Theorem,
[
Bla
,
Dr3
]
)
.

Let
K\subset M^{n}(c)
be a
\lambda
-convex body. Then for every
p\in\partial K
there exists a supporting
\lambda
-sphere
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(p)
bounding the
\lambda
-ball
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}(p)
such that
K\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}(p)
. ∎

Finally, let us point out an equivalent definition of
\lambda
-convexity which is, in some sense, dual to Definition
2.1
. The equivalence can be seen using Theorem
2.2
(see also
[
BD1
, Lemma 7]
for the spaces of constant curvature, and
[
BLNP
, Corollary 3.4]
for the equivalence in the Euclidean space). A convex body
K\subset M^{n}(c)
is said to be
\lambda
-convex if and only if any pair of points in
K
can be connected by the shortest arc of a curve of constant geodesic curvature
\lambda
and this arc lies entirely in
K
. From this point of view,
\lambda
-convexity is a generalization of the standard notion of convexity, where we say that a body is convex if and only if the line segment that connects any two points lies entirely inside the body.
It follows that the whole spindle hypersurface, that is, the locus of all arcs connecting the two points, and the domain bounded by this hypersurface must lie inside the body.

Definition 2.3

(
\lambda
-convex polyhedron)
.

A
\lambda
-convex polyhedron
in
M^{n}(c)
is a
\lambda
-convex body given as the intersection of finitely many
\lambda
-balls
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}
. Each
facet
of the
\lambda
-convex polyhedron is the intersection of the corresponding
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}
with the boundary of the polyhedron. A facet is of dimension
n-1
. Two facets are either disjoint or intersect. In the latter case, their intersection is called a (lower-dimensional)
face
. The faces of dimension
0
and
1
are called
vertices
and
edges
of the polyhedron, respectively.

When
n=3
, we will denote by
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{V}
the sets of edges and vertices of a
\lambda
-convex polyhedron
K
. For each edge
E\in\mathcal{E}
, we will denote by
\beta_{E}
the angle between the outer normals at
E
to the faces intersecting along
E
, and by
\ell_{E}
the length of
E
. Also, for each vertex
v\in\mathcal{V}
, we will let
u(K,v)
be the region in the unit sphere of
T_{p}M^{3}(c)
covered by the unit outer normals to
K
at
v
.

Definition 2.4

(
\lambda
-convex lens)
.

A
\lambda
-convex polyhedron with exactly two facets is called a
\lambda
-convex lens
.

3.
The Gauss–Bonnet theorem in constant curvature 3-space

In this section, we prove the following version of the Gauss–Bonnet theorem for
\lambda
-convex polyhedra in
M^{3}(c)
.

Theorem 3.1

(Gauss–Bonnet for
\lambda
-convex polyhedra)
.

Let
K\subset M^{3}(c)
be a
\lambda
-convex polyhedron with the vertex set
\mathcal{V}
and the edge set
\mathcal{E}
. Then

(3.1)

\left(\lambda^{2}+c\right)\,|\partial K|+2\lambda\sum_{E\in\mathcal{E}}\ell_{E}\cdot\tan\frac{\beta_{E}}{2}+\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}|u(K,v)|=4\pi,

where
|u(K,v)|
is the spherical area of
u(K,v)
.

Proof.

Put
M=M^{3}(c)
, and let
\pi\colon SM\to M
be the sphere bundle of unit tangent vectors of
M
. Given a convex body
K\subset M
, let
N(K)
be the set of unit outer normal vectors of
K
. It is a bi-Lipschitz submanifold of
SM
.

A local frame on
SM
is a collection
e_{0},e_{1},e_{2}\colon U\to SM
defined on some open set
U\subset SM
such that
e_{0}(\xi)=\xi
and
e_{0}(\xi),e_{1}(\xi),e_{2}(\xi)
are orthonormal
\forall\xi\in U
.
Given such a local frame consider the differential forms
\omega_{1,0},\omega_{2,0}\in\Omega^{1}(SM)
defined by

\omega_{1,0}(X)=\langle e_{1},\nabla_{X}e_{0}\rangle,\qquad\omega_{2,0}(X)=\langle e_{2},\nabla_{X}e_{0}\rangle,\qquad X\in TSM.

Here
TSM
denotes the tangent bundle of
SM
, and
\nabla
is the connection on
TSM
obtained by pulling back the Levi-Civita connection of
TM
through
\pi\colon SM\to M
.
It is easy to check that
\omega_{1,0}\wedge\omega_{2,0}
does not depend on the choice of
e_{1},e_{2}
, so we may define

\TC(K)=\int_{N(K)}\omega_{1,0}\wedge\omega_{2,0}.

By
[
AF
, Theorem 2.1.13]
, the functional
\TC
is continuous with respect to the Hausdorff topology on the set of convex bodies of
M
. When
\partial K
is smooth,
\TC(K)
is the integral of the product
k_{1}k_{2}
of principal curvatures. Since
k_{1}k_{2}+c
equals the Riemannian curvature of
\partial K
, the Gauss–Bonnet theorem reads

(3.2)

\TC(K)+c|\partial K|=4\pi.

By continuity, the previous equality holds for all convex bodies
K
, smooth or not.

Let
K
be a
\lambda
-convex polyhedron and denote by
\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}
the sets of vertices, edges, and facets of
K
. Then

\TC(K)=\lambda^{2}|\partial K|+\sum_{E\in\mathcal{E}}\int_{N(K)\cap\pi^{-1}(E)}\omega_{1,0}\wedge\omega_{2,0}+\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\int_{N(K)\cap\pi^{-1}(v)}\omega_{1,0}\wedge\omega_{2,0}.

Each term in the last sum is the area of the region
u(K,v)
of the unit sphere
\pi^{-1}(v)
covered by outer normal vectors of
K
at
v
. Let us focus on the second sum. Let
E\in\mathcal{E}
be the intersection of two faces
F_{1},F_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
, and let
\gamma(s),s\in[0,l]
be an arc-length parametrization of
E
. Denote by
\vec{t}(s),\vec{n}(s),\vec{b}(s)
the Frenet frame along
\gamma(s)
and put

\displaystyle e_{0}

\displaystyle=-\cos(\alpha)\vec{n}+\sin(\alpha)\vec{b}

\displaystyle e_{1}

\displaystyle=\sin(\alpha)\vec{n}+\cos(\alpha)\vec{b}

\displaystyle e_{2}

\displaystyle=\vec{t}.

Let
\beta
be the angle between the inner normals
N_{1},N_{2}
to
F_{1},F_{2}
. We have
e_{0}\in N(K)
for
-\beta/2\leqslant\alpha\leqslant\beta/2
.

Since

\displaystyle\nabla e_{0}=e_{1}d\alpha-\cos(\alpha)\nabla\vec{n}+\sin(\alpha)\nabla\vec{b}

\displaystyle\nabla\vec{t}=k\,ds\,\vec{n}

\displaystyle\nabla\vec{n}=-k\,ds\,\vec{t}

\displaystyle\nabla\vec{b}=0,

we have

\displaystyle\omega_{1,0}

\displaystyle=d\alpha

\displaystyle\omega_{2,0}

\displaystyle=k\cos(\alpha)ds

\displaystyle\omega_{1,0}\wedge\omega_{2,0}

\displaystyle=k\cos(\alpha)d\alpha\wedge ds.

Integration yields

\int_{N(K)\cap\pi^{-1}(E)}\omega_{1,0}\wedge\omega_{2,0}=\int_{0}^{l}\int_{-\beta/2}^{\beta/2}k\cos(\alpha)d\alpha ds=2k\,\ell\,\sin\frac{\beta}{2}.

On the other hand, we have

\lambda=\langle\frac{d\vec{t}}{ds},N_{1}\rangle=k\langle\vec{n},N_{1}\rangle=k\cos\frac{\beta}{2}.

Therefore,

\int_{N(K)\cap\pi^{-1}(E)}\omega_{1,0}\wedge\omega_{2,0}=2\lambda\ell\tan\frac{\beta}{2}.

Altogether, we get

\TC(K)=\lambda^{2}|\partial K|+2\lambda\sum_{E\in\mathcal{E}}\ell_{E}\tan\frac{\beta_{E}}{2}+\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\left|u(K,v)\right|.

Combining with (
3.2
), we obtain (
3.1
).
∎

4.
Inner parallel bodies and variation of the surface area

For a convex body
K\subset M^{n}(c)
, let
r(K)
be its inradius (i.e., the radius of the largest ball contained in
K
). For each
t\geq 0
, the
inner parallel body

K_{t}

at distance
t
is defined as

K_{t}:=\left\{x\in M^{n}(c):B(x,t)\subseteq K\right\},

where
B(x,t)\subset M^{n}(c)
is a closed geodesic ball centered at
x\in M^{n}(c)
of radius
t
. Note that
K_{t}
is empty for
t>r(K)
. This definition coincides with the standard definition of the inner parallel set in Euclidean space. The following relation between the volume and the surface of
K_{t}
was obtained for
c=0
in
[
Ma
]
.

Theorem 4.1
.

If
K_{t}
is the inner parallel body at distance
t\geqslant 0
of a convex body
K\subset M^{n}(c)
,
n\geqslant 2
, then

(4.1)

|K|=\int\limits_{0}^{r(K)}|\partial K_{t}|\,dt.

Proof.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a local chart of
M^{n}(c)
containing
K
and such that the Riemannian metric is
ds^{2}=(h(x))^{2}(dx_{1}^{2}+\cdots+dx_{n}^{2})
with
h(x)=\frac{2}{(1+c|x|^{2})}
.
Consider the function
f\colon K\to\mathbb{R}
given by
f(x)=d_{M}(x,\partial K)
, the Riemannian distance from
x
to
\partial K
. Clearly,

d_{M}(y,\partial K)\leq d_{M}(y,x)+d_{M}(x,\partial K),\qquad d_{M}(x,\partial K)\leq d_{M}(x,y)+d_{M}(y,\partial K)

for any
x,y\in K
, which yields

(4.2)

|f(x)-f(y)|=|d_{M}(x,\partial K)-d_{M}(y,\partial K)|\leq d_{M}(x,y).

For a uniform constant
C
we have
d_{M}(x,y)<C|y-x|
for all
x,y\in K
, and therefore
f
is Lipschitz. We can thus apply the coarea formula
[
Fe
, 3.2.12]
to get

(4.3)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}g(x)Jf(x)dx=\int_{0}^{r(K)}\int_{\partial K_{t}}g(x)d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)dt

where
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
is the Hausdorff measure with respect to the Euclidean metric,
Jf
is the Euclidean Jacobian of
f
, and
g
is any integrable function.

By Rademacher’s theorem,
f
is differentiable at almost every
x\in K
. Given such
x
, note that
Jf(x)\leq h(x)
by (
4.2
). Moreover, if
\gamma(t)
is a geodesic realizing
d_{M}(x,\partial K)
(i.e. such that
\gamma(0)=x
and
\gamma(f(x))\in\partial K
), then

\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}f(\gamma(t))=-1.

It follows that
Jf(x)=\frac{1}{|\gamma^{\prime}(0)|}=h(x)
. Hence, taking
g(x)=(h(x))^{n-1}
, equality (
4.3
) becomes

|K|=\int_{K}\left(h(x)\right)^{n}dx=\int_{0}^{r(K)}\int_{\partial K_{t}}\left(h(x)\right)^{n-1}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)dt=\int_{0}^{r(K)}|\partial K_{t}|dt.

∎

By
[
DT
, Theorem B]
, if
K
is a
\lambda
-convex body and
L
is a
\lambda
-convex lens, then

(4.4)

r(K)\geqslant r(L)\quad\text{ provided }\quad|\partial K|=|\partial L|,

with equality if and only if
K
is a
\lambda
-convex lens. In the 2-dimensional case this result was proved in
[
Mi
]
and later extended to Aleksandrov spaces in
[
Dr2
]
.

We need to recall the results of
[
DT
, Section 3.1]
, where the variation formula for inner parallel
\lambda
-convex polyhedra is derived in the Euclidean space. For model spaces, the proof is similar; we present only some of the key steps.

By
[
Gr
, Corollary 3.3]
, the inner parallel body
K_{t}
of a
\lambda
-convex polyhedron
K
is a
\lambda(t)
-convex polyhedron with

\lambda^{\prime}(t)=\lambda(t)^{2}+c.

We will denote the set of edges of
K_{t}
as
\mathcal{E}(t)
. As before, given
E\in\mathcal{E}(t)
we will denote by
\ell_{E}
the length of
E
, and by
\beta_{E}
the angle between the outer normals at
E
.

Theorem 4.2

(Variation of the surface area for inner parallel polyhedra)
.

If
K\subset M^{n}(c)
is a
\lambda
-convex polyhedron, then except for a finite number of
t_{0}\in[0,r(K)]
,

\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=t_{0}}|\partial K_{t}|=-(n-1)\lambda(t_{0})\cdot|\partial K_{t_{0}}|-2\sum_{E\in\mathcal{E}(t_{0})}\ell_{E}\cdot\tan\frac{\beta_{E}}{2}.

Proof.

The proof follows the steps of
[
DT
, Section 3.1]
. Assume that
K_{t}
has the same combinatorial structure for all
t
near
t_{0}
, which is the case except for finitely many
t_{0}\in[0,r(K)]
.

If
F_{i}(t)
is the
i
-th smooth facet of
K_{t}
, then the claim will follow once we show that

(4.5)

\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=t_{0}}|F_{i}(t)|=-(n-1)\lambda(t_{0})\cdot|F_{i}(t_{0})|-\sum_{j}\ell_{E_{ij}}\cdot\tan\frac{\beta_{E_{ij}}}{2},

where the sum is taken over all edges
E_{ij}
adjacent to
F_{i}(t_{0})
.

For
t>s>t_{0}
, let
F_{i}(t,s)
be the outer-parallel hypersurface at distance
t-s
from
F_{i}(t)
; i.e.,

F_{i}(t,s)=\{\exp_{x}((t-s)N(x)):x\in F_{i}(t)\},

where
\exp_{x}
is the exponential map, and
N(x)
is the outer normal to
F_{i}(t)
at
x
. Put also
\widetilde{F}_{i}(t)=F_{i}(t,t_{0})
, and note that
\widetilde{F}_{i}(t)
is a convex polyhedron in a fixed complete totally umbilical hypersurface
\mathcal{B}_{i,t_{0}}
of curvature
\lambda(t_{0})
containing
F_{i}(t_{0})
. By the first variation of the area formula, we have

\frac{d}{ds}|F_{i}(t,s)|=(n-1)\lambda(s)|F_{i}(t,s)|.

Solving for
|F_{i}(t,s)|
at
s=t_{0}
in the previous ODE we get

|\widetilde{F}_{i}(t)|=|F_{i}(t,t_{0})|=|F_{i}(t)|\exp\left((n-1)\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\lambda(u)du\right).

It follows that

(4.6)

\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=t_{0}}|\widetilde{F}_{i}(t)|=(n-1)\lambda(t_{0})\cdot|F_{i}(t_{0})|+\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=t_{0}}|F_{i}(t)|.

On the other hand, for the family
t\mapsto\widetilde{F}_{i}(t)\subset\mathcal{B}_{i,t_{0}}
we can apply
[
DT
, Theorem 3.3]
to conclude, in the same way as in
[
DT
, Lemma 3.6]
, that

\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=t_{0}}|\widetilde{F}_{i}(t)|=-\sum_{j}\ell_{E_{ij}}\cdot\tan\frac{\beta_{E_{ij}}}{2}.

Comparing with (
4.6
) yields (
4.5
). This completes the proof.∎

5.
Proof of the Main Theorem

We are now ready to establish our main result.

Assume first that
K\subset M^{3}(c)
,
c\neq 0
, is a
\lambda
-convex polyhedron, and
L
is a
\lambda
-convex lens such that
|\partial K|=|\partial L|
. Consider the functions
f_{K}(t):=|\partial K_{t}|
,
t\in[0,r(K)]
and
f_{L}(t):=|\partial L_{t}|
. Note that
L_{t}
is a
\lambda(t)
-convex lens.

We claim that

(5.1)

f_{K}(t)\geqslant f_{L}(t),\qquad\forall\,t\in[0,r(L)],

and that this inequality is strict on some nonempty open subinterval of
[0,r(L)]
unless
K
is a lens. The latter assertion follows from (
4.4
). In view of (
4.1
), this claim immediately yields the desired inequality in the Main Theorem, together with the characterization of the equality case. It therefore remains to justify (
5.1
).

Let
\ell^{*}_{t}
be the length of the unique edge of the lens
L_{t}
, and
\beta^{*}_{t}
be the angle at this edge between the outer normals of the two faces. Then the Gauss–Bonnet theorem (Theorem
3.1
) applied to
L_{t}
yields

(\lambda(t)^{2}+c)|\partial L_{t}|+2\lambda(t)\ell_{t_{0}}^{*}\cdot\tan\frac{\beta_{t_{0}}^{*}}{2}=4\pi.

By taking
K=K_{t}
in (
3.1
), and comparing with the previous equality, we deduce

\sum_{E\in\mathcal{E}(t_{0})}\ell_{E}\cdot\tan\frac{\beta_{E}}{2}\leqslant\ell^{*}_{t_{0}}\cdot\tan\frac{\beta^{*}_{t_{0}}}{2},

for every
t_{0}\in[0,r(L)]
such that
|\partial K_{t_{0}}|=|\partial L_{t_{0}}|
, and this inequality is strict unless
K
is a lens. By Theorem
4.2
applied to
K_{t}
and
L_{t}
at
t=t_{0}
, it then follows that

(5.2)

\displaystyle f_{K}^{\prime}(t_{0})=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=t_{0}}|\partial K_{t}|

\displaystyle\geqslant\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=t_{0}}|\partial L_{t}|=f_{L}^{\prime}(t_{0}),

for every
t\in[0,r(L)]
such that
|\partial K_{t}|=|\partial L_{t}|
, and again, this inequality is strict unless
K
is a lens. In particular, since
|\partial K|=|\partial K_{0}|=|\partial L|=|\partial L_{0}|
, and if
K
is not a lens, we conclude that
f_{K}(t)>f_{L}(t)
in some small right neighborhood
t\in[0,\varepsilon)
. Now assume that (
5.1
) is violated. Then there exists a minimal
t_{0}
such that
f_{K}(t)\geqslant f_{L}(t),\,\,t\in[0,t_{0}]
and

f_{K}(t_{0})=f_{L}(t_{0}),\quad f_{K}^{\prime}(t_{0})\leqslant f_{L}^{\prime}(t_{0}).

This is a contradiction to (
5.2
). Claim (
5.1
) is thus justified, and this finishes the proof of the Main Theorem in the case when
K
is a
\lambda
-convex polyhedron.

Finally, if
K
is an arbitrary
\lambda
-convex body, the argument follows
[
DT
, Subsection 3.3]
verbatim by approximation. The construction therein is purely metric, does not depend on the curvature of the ambient space, and relies only on the rigidity case of (
4.4
). ∎

6.
Proof of the reverse isoperimetric inequality in
\mathbb{H}^{2}(-1)

In this section, we will demonstrate how our approach gives an alternative proof to the following theorem, originally established in
[
Dr1
]
:

Theorem 6.1
.

If
K\subset\mathbb{H}^{2}(-1)
is a
\lambda
-convex body and
L\subset\mathbb{H}^{2}(-1)
is a
\lambda
-convex lens such that
|\partial K|=|\partial L|
, then
|K|\geqslant|L|
. Moreover, equality is possible if and only if
K
is a
\lambda
-convex lens.

Proof.

As in the proof of the Main Theorem, we can focus only on the case when
K
is a
\lambda
-convex polygon. Let
e_{0},\ldots,e_{m-1}
(
m\geqslant 2
) be sides of
K
and
\beta_{i}
be the angle between the outer normals at the common vertex of
e_{i}
and
e_{i+1}
(indices are taken modulo
m
).
Likewise, let
\beta^{*}
be the angle between the outer normals to the sides at the vertices of
L
.

We have that

(6.1)

\beta_{i}\leqslant\beta^{*},\quad\forall i.

Indeed, for a fixed
i
, by Blaschke’s rolling theorem,
K
is contained in the set
\widetilde{L}
(compact or not), which is formed as the intersection of two supporting
\lambda
-balls that form sides
e_{i}
and
e_{i+1}
. Thus,
|\partial K|\leqslant|\partial\widetilde{L}|
by monotonicity and
|\partial L|\leqslant|\partial\widetilde{L}|
. We note that
\beta_{i}
is the angle between the outer normals to the sides at a vertex of
\widetilde{L}
.
Since the perimeter of
\widetilde{L}
is non-decreasing with respect to
\beta_{i}
, we get that
\beta_{i}\leqslant\beta^{*}.

By the two-dimensional Gauss–Bonnet theorem applied to
K
, we conclude

(6.2)

2\pi=-|K|+\lambda|\partial K|+\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\beta_{i}.

Similarly, for the lens
L
, the Gauss–Bonnet formula gives

2\pi=-|L|+\lambda|\partial L|+2\beta^{*}.

Therefore, we conclude that

(6.3)

\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\beta_{i}\leqslant 2\beta^{*},\quad\quad\text{whenever }\quad|\partial K|=|\partial L|\quad\text{and}\quad|K|\leqslant|L|.

As in the proof of the Main Theorem, let
K_{t}
be the inner parallel body for
K
at distance
t\in[0,r(K)]
, and similarly
L_{t}
. Note that
K_{t}
is a
m_{t}
-sided polygon, with
m_{t}\leqslant m
. Recall that
r(L)\leqslant r(K)
provided that
|\partial K|=|\partial L|
(see (
4.4
)).

Since for every
t
, both
K_{t}
and
L_{t}
are
\lambda_{t}
-convex (for some
\lambda_{t}
that can be computed explicitly depending on
t
and the curvature of the ambient space), we can specify (
6.3
) for every
t_{0}\in[0,r(L)]
:

(6.4)

\sum_{i=0}^{m_{t_{0}}-1}\beta_{i,t_{0}}\leqslant 2\beta^{*}_{t_{0}},\quad\quad\text{whenever }\quad|\partial K_{t_{0}}|=|\partial L_{t_{0}}|\quad\text{and}\quad|K_{t_{0}}|\leqslant|L_{t_{0}}|.

Here,
\beta_{i,t}
,
i\in\{0,\ldots,m_{t}-1\}
, and
\beta_{*,t}
are the corresponding angles of
K_{t}
and
L_{t}
.

By Theorem
4.2
,

(6.5)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=t_{0}}|\partial K_{t}|-\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=t_{0}}|\partial L_{t}|

\displaystyle=2\tan\frac{\beta^{*}_{t_{0}}}{2}-2\sum_{i=0}^{m_{t_{0}}-1}\tan\frac{\beta_{i,t_{0}}}{2}

for every
t_{0}\in[0,r(L)]
such that
|\partial K_{t_{0}}|=|\partial L_{t_{0}}|
.

Observe that
2x^{-1}\tan(\frac{x}{2})
is a monotone increasing function for
x\in[0,\pi)
. Assuming
|\partial K_{t_{0}}|=|\partial L_{t_{0}}|
and
|K_{t_{0}}|\leqslant|L_{t_{0}}|
, we can combine (
6.1
) and (
6.4
) to conclude that

\sum_{i=0}^{m_{t_{0}}-1}\tan\frac{\beta_{i,t_{0}}}{2}=\sum_{i=0}^{m_{t_{0}}-1}\frac{\beta_{i,t_{0}}}{2}\cdot\frac{2}{\beta_{i,t_{0}}}\tan\frac{\beta_{i,t_{0}}}{2}\leqslant\frac{2}{\beta^{*}_{t_{0}}}\tan\frac{\beta^{*}_{t_{0}}}{2}\sum_{i=0}^{m_{t_{0}}-1}\frac{\beta_{i,t_{0}}}{2}\leqslant 2\tan\frac{\beta^{*}_{t_{0}}}{2}.

Together with (
6.5
), this gives

(6.6)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=t_{0}}|\partial K_{t}|\geqslant\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=t_{0}}|\partial L_{t}|

\displaystyle\text{for every }\quad t_{0}\in[0,r(L)]\quad

\displaystyle\text{ such that }\quad|\partial K_{t_{0}}|=|\partial L_{t_{0}}|\quad\text{and}\quad|K_{t_{0}}|\leqslant|L_{t_{0}}|.

Moreover, the first inequality is strict if the last inequality
|K_{t_{0}}|\leqslant|L_{t_{0}}|
is also strict.

Towards the contradiction, assume that
|K|<|L|
. Then we can apply (
6.6
) to conclude that

\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}|\partial K_{t}|>\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}|\partial L_{t}|.

Hence, the graph of the function
t\mapsto|\partial L_{t}|
lies below the graph of the function
t\mapsto|\partial K_{t}|
in some right neighborhood of
0
. If this is true for all
t\in[0,r(L)]
, we conclude that
|K|\geqslant|L|
, a contradiction. Therefore, there must exist the smallest
t_{1}>0
such that

(6.7)

|\partial K_{t_{1}}|=|\partial L_{t_{1}}|\quad\text{ and }\quad\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=t_{1}}|\partial K_{t}|\leqslant\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=t_{1}}|\partial L_{t}|

and the graph of
t\mapsto|\partial L_{t}|
goes above the graph of
t\mapsto|\partial K_{t}|
in a right neighborhood of
t_{1}
. By the choice of
t_{1}
, we know that

\int_{0}^{t_{1}}|\partial K_{t}|dt\geqslant\int_{0}^{t_{1}}|\partial L_{t}|dt.

Thus, using the assumption
|K|<|L|
,

|K_{t_{1}}|=|K|-\int_{0}^{t_{1}}|\partial K_{t}|dt<|L|-\int_{0}^{t_{1}}|\partial L_{t}|dt=|L_{t_{1}}|.

Therefore, at
t=t_{1}
, we have
|K_{t_{1}}|<|L_{t_{1}}|
and
|\partial K_{t_{1}}|=|\partial L_{t_{1}}|
. Hence, by (
6.6
), we must have
\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=t_{1}}|\partial K_{t}|>\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=t_{1}}|\partial L_{t}|
. This is a contradiction to the inequality in (
6.7
). This is a final contradiction that justifies
|K|\geqslant|L|
.

The equality case follows as in
[
DT
]
(using the rigidity of the inequality in
[
Dr2
]
).
∎

Remark
.

Note that the proof above does not work in the
2
-dimensional sphere because in that case the area
|K|
appears with a different sign in the Gauss–Bonnet formula (
6.2
).
