Ion exchange resin-catalyzed epoxidation of sesame oil: effects of temperature and reaction time
Authors: Laura Ospino, Rafael Rodriguez, Alexis Loaiza, Juan M Gonzalez, Karina Ojeda-Delgado, Eduardo Sanchez-Tuirán
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe development of renewable intermediates for bio-based polymeric materials requires environmentally friendly routes for the chemical functionalization of vegetable oils. In this study, sesame oil was epoxidized using Amberlite® IRC 120H and Dowex® 50WX2 acidic ion exchange resins as heterogeneous catalysts, with hydrogen peroxide and acetic acid as the in situ peracetic acid system and toluene as solvent. The novelty of this work lies in the comparative evaluation of these ion exchange resins for sesame oil epoxidation under different temperatures and reaction times, providing insight into the balance between double-bond conversion and oxirane stability. Reaction temperatures of 40, 60, and 80 C and reaction times of 2, 4, and 6 h were evaluated. Reaction progress was assessed by iodine value determination, while oxirane ring formation was confirmed by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) and proton nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (H NMR). The iodine value decreased markedly after reaction, confirming the consumption of carbon–carbon double bonds. Within the evaluated temperature range, 80 C was the most favorable temperature for both catalysts. The highest double-bond conversions were obtained after 4 h of reaction, reaching 95.2% with Dowex® 50WX2 and 87.8% with Amberlite® IRC 120H. For both catalysts, extending the reaction time to 6 h did not improve conversion, suggesting that prolonged reaction times may favor secondary reactions or epoxide degradation. FTIR spectra showed the appearance of characteristic oxirane bands in the 800–950 cm region, together with a decrease in the olefinic =C–H band near 3005 cm. Similarly, H NMR showed a reduction in olefinic proton signals at 5.3–5.5 ppm and the formation of epoxide signals between 2.9 and 3.2 ppm. The results indicate that Dowex® 50WX2 provides higher double-bond conversion, while Amberlite® IRC 120H may favor greater oxirane stability.Keywords: Sesame oil, Epoxidation, Ion exchange resin, Dowex 50WX2, Amberlite IRC 120H, Bio-based polymersSubject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences

Introduction

The transition from finite petroleum-based resources to sustainable alternatives has become a strategic priority for the chemical industry, driven by increasing environmental concerns and the growing demand for biodegradable materials1–4. Vegetable oils (VOs) are among the most promising renewable feedstocks due to their abundance, low cost, and inherent chemical versatility1,4,5. Among them, sesame oil is a relevant candidate for industrial valorization6,7. Sesame is an attractive agronomic crop because it can be grown under a wide variety of climatic conditions and can grow in low-fertility or marginal soils, making it a suitable oilseed for regions where other crops may face productivity limitations. In addition, its high content of unsaturated fatty acids, mainly oleic and linoleic acids, makes it suitable for conversion into high-value intermediates6–8.

Epoxidation is one of the most important functionalization routes for unsaturated oils. This process converts carbon–carbon double bonds () into reactive three-membered oxirane, or epoxide, rings7,9,10. Epoxidized vegetable oils (EVOs) are important building blocks for natural oil polyols, PVC stabilizers, biolubricants, and sustainable epoxy resins11–14. At the industrial level, epoxidation is commonly performed through the Prilezhaev reaction, in which a percarboxylic acid, such as peracetic acid, is generated in situ from hydrogen peroxide and a carboxylic acid7,9,10,15.

The effectiveness of this reaction strongly depends on the catalytic system and on the control of undesirable side reactions1,12. Conventional homogeneous mineral acids, such as sulfuric acid, provide high reaction rates; however, they are poorly selective, promote oxirane ring-opening reactions, and cause equipment corrosion1,9,12,16. Therefore, ion exchange resins, including Amberlite® IRC 120H and Dowex® 50WX2, have emerged as efficient, reusable, and environmentally preferable heterogeneous alternatives9,17–20. These solid catalysts can reduce ring cleavage by sterically limiting the access of bulky triglyceride molecules to internal acid active sites, thereby improving selectivity9,17,19. In addition, the use of toluene as a solvent has been reported as a strategy to suppress side reactions and enhance the stability of the formed oxirane groups18,21,22.

Amberlite® IRC 120H and Dowex® 50WX2 were selected as representative commercial acidic ion exchange resins for sesame oil epoxidation. Both catalysts contain sulfonic acid active sites and belong to the family of heterogeneous acid catalysts previously reported for the epoxidation of vegetable oils and lipid-derived substrates under peracid-based oxidation systems. Amberlite-type resins have been used for the epoxidation of castor oil, soybean oil, linseed oil, used cooking oil, and tall oil fatty acids, while Dowex-type resins have been evaluated in the epoxidation of oleic acid and used cooking oil, as summarized in Table 1.

Although the epoxidation of several vegetable oils has been widely investigated, the literature on sesame oil epoxidation remains comparatively limited. Previous studies have reported sesame oil epoxidation using peracetic acid or have explored epoxidized sesame oil as a bio-based resin precursor7,8; however, the comparative performance of acidic ion exchange resins in sesame oil epoxidation has not been sufficiently established. In particular, there is limited information on how resin type, reaction temperature, and reaction time jointly affect double-bond conversion and oxirane stability in an in situ peracetic acid/toluene system.

This study addresses this gap by investigating the effect of operational variables on the epoxidation of sesame oil using Amberlite® IRC 120H and Dowex® 50WX2 as heterogeneous catalysts. Specifically, three reaction temperatures (40, 60, and 80 C) and three reaction times (2, 4, and 6 h) were evaluated to determine their influence on double-bond conversion and epoxide formation. The extent of the reaction was quantified using the iodine value (IV), which allows the conversion of double bonds into oxirane rings to be assessed1,10,27,28. In addition, the chemical structure and successful formation of oxirane groups were confirmed by Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy and proton nuclear magnetic resonance (H NMR). By comparing the performance of both resins in a toluene-diluted system, this work seeks to identify an appropriate balance between reaction rate and product stability for sustainable resin-based epoxidation applications.

Materials and methods

Materials

For the epoxidation reaction, commercial sesame oil was purchased from a local Colombian supplier and used without additional pretreatment. The untreated oil presented an initial iodine value of approximately 113 g I 100 g oil, which was used as the reference value for calculating double-bond conversion after epoxidation. According to the supplier, the fatty acid composition of the oil was 0.08 palmitic acid, 0.06 stearic acid, 0.41 oleic acid, 0.43 linoleic acid, and 0.01 linolenic acid, expressed as mass fractions. This information was used to estimate the initial concentration of carbon–carbon double bonds and to establish the unsaturation:acetic acid and unsaturation:hydrogen peroxide molar ratios used in the reaction system.

Hydrogen peroxide (35% in water, Sigma-Aldrich) and acetic acid (ACS reagent, Sigma-Aldrich) were used for the in situ formation of the peracetic acid oxidizing system, while toluene (ACS reagent, Sigma-Aldrich) was used as reaction solvent. Two commercial acidic ion exchange resins, Amberlite® IRC 120H and Dowex® 50WX2 100–200 mesh, were evaluated as heterogeneous acid catalysts. Potassium dichromate (KCrO, ACS reagent, Sigma-Aldrich), potassium iodide (KI, ≥99.0%, Sigma-Aldrich), soluble starch (ACS reagent, Spectrum Chemical MFG Corp.), sodium thiosulfate (NaSO, ≥98.0%, Sigma-Aldrich), hydrochloric acid (HCl, 1 M, Merck-Millipore), chloroform (CHCl, ≥99.5%, Sigma-Aldrich), and Wijs solution (Sigma-Aldrich) were used for iodine value determination.

Epoxidation reaction and iodine value determination

The epoxidation of sesame oil was carried out in a two-neck 50 mL round-bottom flask equipped with a condenser and placed in an oil bath previously heated to the selected reaction temperature. The experimental system was mounted on a heating plate and maintained under continuous magnetic stirring at 180 rpm. Amberlite® IRC 120H and Dowex® 50WX2 did not receive any pretreatment before the reaction and were used as received. For each experiment, 6.0 mL of sesame oil was introduced into the flask, followed by the sequential addition of 2.8 mL of toluene, corresponding to 44% with respect to the oil volume; 0.727 mL of acetic acid, corresponding to an unsaturation:acetic acid molar ratio of 1:0.5; 1.385 g of catalyst, corresponding to 5% with respect to the oil; and 4.9 mL of hydrogen peroxide, corresponding to an unsaturation:hydrogen peroxide molar ratio of 1:1.5. The reaction was performed using either Amberlite® IRC 120H or Dowex® 50WX2 as the heterogeneous catalyst.

After reaction completion, the catalyst was separated by centrifugation, and the resulting liquid phase was transferred to a test tube and allowed to stand for 2 h to promote phase separation. The organic phase, containing the epoxidized oil, was then collected for characterization.

The effects of reaction time, temperature, and catalyst type were evaluated through a factorial experimental design. Reaction times of 2, 4, and 6 h and temperatures of 40, 60, and 80 C were studied for both catalysts. Each condition was performed in triplicate. The iodine value was determined for untreated sesame oil and for each epoxidized sample to quantify the decrease in carbon–carbon double bonds after reaction. The analytical procedure was based on ASTM D5554-15(2021). It should be noted that iodine value was used to quantify double-bond conversion, not epoxide yield or oxirane selectivity. Therefore, the conversion values reported in this work represent the consumption of carbon–carbon double bonds, while FTIR and H NMR analyses were used as qualitative evidence of oxirane ring formation.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis of sesame oil double-bond conversion was performed using Minitab Statistical Software, Version 16. Separate analyses were carried out for each ion exchange resin catalyst, Amberlite® IRC 120H and Dowex® 50WX2. Double-bond conversion (%) was used as the response variable, while reaction temperature and reaction time were considered as independent variables.

The experimental data were analyzed by analysis of variance (ANOVA) using a second-order model that included the linear and quadratic effects of reaction temperature and reaction time. The statistical significance of the regression model and of each individual term was evaluated using the F-value and p-value obtained from the ANOVA. A confidence level of 95% was applied; therefore, model terms with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Before interpreting the ANOVA results, the adequacy of the statistical models was assessed through residual diagnostic plots generated in Minitab. The normality of the residuals was evaluated using normal probability plots, while homoscedasticity and independence were examined using residuals versus observation order plots.

Spectroscopic characterization

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) and proton nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (H NMR) were used to confirm the chemical modification of sesame oil after epoxidation. FTIR analysis was performed to identify the characteristic functional groups of the triglyceride matrix and to verify the formation of oxirane rings. Spectra were recorded using an Agilent Cary 630® FTIR analyzer equipped with a diamond attenuated total reflectance (ATR) accessory, within the 600–4000 cm range.

H NMR analysis was used to monitor the decrease of olefinic proton signals and the appearance of resonances associated with oxirane protons. Spectra were acquired using a Bruker 400 Ultrashield® NMR spectrometer and recorded over the 0.5–7.5 ppm chemical shift range.

Results and discussion

Effect of catalyst, reaction temperature, and reaction time on iodine value and double-bond conversion

The iodine value was used as the primary parameter to evaluate the consumption of carbon–carbon double bonds during sesame oil epoxidation. Since iodine value quantifies residual unsaturation, the conversion values calculated from this parameter are reported as double-bond conversion and should not be interpreted as direct measurements of epoxide yield or oxirane selectivity. Pure sesame oil showed an iodine value close to 113 g I 100 g oil, confirming its high degree of unsaturation and its suitability as a substrate for epoxidation.

Figures 1 and 2 show the effect of reaction temperature and reaction time on sesame oil double-bond conversion using Amberlite® IRC 120H and Dowex® 50WX2, respectively. For both catalysts, temperature had a marked influence on double-bond conversion. At 2 h of reaction, Amberlite® IRC 120H increased the average conversion from 56.9% at 40 C to 70.3% at 60 C and 85.8% at 80 C. A similar but more pronounced trend was observed for Dowex® 50WX2, for which the average conversion increased from 35.0% at 40 C to 48.9% at 60 C and 90.9% at 80 C. These results indicate that increasing the temperature favored the consumption of carbon–carbon double bonds, most likely due to the enhanced formation of the peracetic acid oxidizing species and faster epoxidation kinetics.

Double-bond conversion during sesame oil epoxidation using Dowex® 50WX2.

The effect of reaction time was also evaluated. For Amberlite® IRC 120H, the average double-bond conversion was 85.8% after 2 h, increased slightly to 87.8% after 4 h, and decreased to 84.2% after 6 h. For Dowex® 50WX2, the average conversion was 90.9% after 2 h, reached a maximum of 95.2% after 4 h, and then slightly decreased to 94.0% after 6 h. These results show that extending the reaction from 2 to 4 h improved double-bond conversion, whereas longer reaction times did not further enhance the process. The decrease observed after 6 h may be associated with secondary reactions, including oxirane ring opening or degradation of the epoxidized product.

Dowex® 50WX2 showed higher apparent catalytic activity than Amberlite® IRC 120H, particularly at 80 C. The highest average double-bond conversion was obtained with Dowex® 50WX2 at 80 C after 4 h, reaching 95.2%, whereas Amberlite® IRC 120H reached 87.8% under the same conditions. This behavior indicates that Dowex® 50WX2 was more effective in promoting double-bond consumption under the evaluated reaction conditions. However, because these values are based on iodine value, they should be interpreted as double-bond conversion rather than quantitative epoxide yield. The complete experimental dataset is provided in Section S2 of the Supplementary Material.

Statistical analysis of reaction temperature and time

The statistical significance of reaction temperature and reaction time on sesame oil double-bond conversion was evaluated by ANOVA using a second-order model. Separate analyses were performed for Amberlite® IRC 120H and Dowex® 50WX2. The model included the linear and quadratic effects of temperature and reaction time, and significance was assessed at a 95% confidence level. The adequacy of the models was evaluated from residual diagnostic plots, including normal probability plots and residuals versus observation order plots, which were used to assess normality, homoscedasticity, and independence of the residuals.

For Amberlite® IRC 120H, the regression model was statistically significant (F = 23.55, p < 0.001), indicating that the fitted model explained a significant fraction of the variability in conversion. The linear contribution was also significant (F = 36.63, p < 0.001). Among the individual factors, temperature was the only significant variable (F = 62.58, p < 0.001), whereas reaction time did not show a statistically significant effect (F = 0.19, p = 0.670). The quadratic contribution was not significant (F = 0.44, p = 0.654), and neither the quadratic temperature term (F = 0.10, p = 0.758) nor the quadratic reaction-time term (F = 0.70, p = 0.421) had a significant effect. These results indicate that, for Amberlite® IRC 120H, conversion was mainly governed by the linear effect of temperature within the evaluated experimental range.

For Dowex® 50WX2, the regression model was also statistically significant (F = 75.65, p < 0.001), with a stronger overall model response than that observed for Amberlite® IRC 120H. The linear contribution was significant (F = 102.73, p < 0.001), and temperature again showed the strongest individual effect on conversion (F = 159.42, p < 0.001). In contrast, reaction time was not statistically significant (F = 0.52, p = 0.487). Unlike the Amberlite® IRC 120H model, the quadratic contribution was significant for Dowex® 50WX2 (F = 6.83, p = 0.012), mainly due to the significant quadratic effect of temperature (F = 12.30, p = 0.005). The quadratic reaction-time term was not significant (F = 0.48, p = 0.502). This behavior suggests that the effect of temperature on conversion was more pronounced and nonlinear for Dowex® 50WX2, which is consistent with its higher conversion values and with the possible influence of temperature on both double-bond conversion and oxirane stability. The ANOVA results show that reaction temperature was the dominant operational variable affecting sesame oil epoxidation for both catalysts. Reaction time was not statistically significant within the evaluated range, which agrees with the experimental observation that increasing the reaction time from 4 to 6 h did not improve conversion. Therefore, the statistical analysis supports the selection of 80 C as the most favorable temperature within the evaluated range and 4 h as a sufficient reaction time to achieve the highest conversion for both acidic ion exchange resins.

FTIR analysis

FTIR spectroscopy was used to confirm the structural changes produced during the epoxidation of sesame oil, as shown in Figure 3. All samples showed absorption bands in the 2850–2950 cm region, associated with aliphatic C–H stretching vibrations from fatty acid chains. These bands remained present in all spectra and did not show significant changes with reaction time, which is expected for the hydrocarbon backbone of triglycerides.

FTIR results for sesame oil before and after epoxidation at 40 and 60 C.

The band observed at approximately 3005 cm, which is more intense in pure sesame oil, is assigned to the stretching vibration of olefinic =C–H bonds from unsaturated fatty acid chains. The higher intensity of this signal in the pure oil is consistent with its high content of oleic and linoleic acids. After epoxidation, the intensity of this band decreases, indicating the consumption of carbon–carbon double bonds during the formation of oxirane rings. Therefore, the reduction of the 3005 cm signal provides evidence of the chemical modification of sesame oil through epoxidation29,30.

The intense bands observed near 2920 and 2850 cm correspond to the asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibrations of aliphatic C–H bonds from the fatty acid chains. These bands are present in pure sesame oil and in all epoxidized samples, confirming the preservation of the long hydrocarbon chains after reaction. Similarly, the strong band located around 1740–1750 cm is assigned to the C=O stretching vibration of ester groups in triglycerides. The persistence of this signal after epoxidation suggests that the ester structure of the oil remained stable and that significant hydrolysis of the triglycerides did not occur9.

The main evidence of epoxidation was observed in the 800–950 cm region, which is associated with C–O–C vibrations of the oxirane ring. The appearance of this band confirmed the formation of epoxide groups after reaction. For Amberlite® IRC 120H, the intensity of this region increased with temperature, suggesting progressive formation of oxirane groups. For Dowex® 50WX2, the signal was also observed, but its maximum intensity occurred at 40 C, followed by a slight decrease at 60 C. This behavior suggests that Dowex® 50WX2 promoted conversion of double bonds at lower temperatures, but higher temperatures may have favored partial degradation or opening of the oxirane ring9,29,30.

Additional bands in the 690–730 cm region were attributed to aromatic C–H vibrations from toluene. These signals confirm the presence of residual solvent31. The region between 1000 and 1300 cm, associated with C–O vibrations from esters and oxygenated products, showed slight changes with reaction time9. In the case of Amberlite® IRC 120H, a gradual increase in intensity was observed, while for Dowex® 50WX2 the strongest signal appeared at 4 h, in agreement with the iodine value results.

H NMR analysis

H NMR spectroscopy provided additional evidence of the chemical transformation of sesame oil during epoxidation, as shown in Figure 4. The untreated oil showed characteristic olefinic signals in the 5.3–5.5 ppm region, corresponding to protons associated with carbon–carbon double bonds in unsaturated fatty acid chains and diallylic methylene signals at ∼ 2.6–2.7 ppm. After epoxidation, the spectra of the samples obtained with Amberlite® IRC 120H and Dowex® 50WX2 showed a decrease in the intensity of the olefinic proton signals, indicating the consumption of carbon–carbon double bonds. This result agrees with the decrease in iodine value observed after reaction and supports the occurrence of chemical modification in the unsaturated fatty acid chains. At the same time, new signals appeared in the 2.9–3.2 ppm region, which are commonly attributed to protons associated with oxirane rings32,33. These spectral changes provide qualitative evidence of sesame oil epoxidation.

1H-NMR spectra for sesame oil before and after epoxidation.

However, it should be noted that the H NMR spectra were interpreted qualitatively. No quantitative integration was performed to determine the epoxide yield, oxirane selectivity, or oxirane stability. Therefore, the decrease in olefinic signals and the appearance of oxirane proton signals should be interpreted as supporting evidence of oxirane formation, rather than as a quantitative measurement of the fraction of double bonds converted into stable epoxide groups.

Comparison with other epoxidized vegetable oils

The performance of sesame oil was compared with reported results for other vegetable oils, including palm, castor, soybean, and linseed oils. The initial iodine value of sesame oil obtained in this study was approximately 113 g I 100 g oil, indicating an intermediate-to-high degree of unsaturation. This value is higher than typical values reported for palm oil, which are commonly around 50–55 g I 100 g oil, but lower than highly unsaturated oils such as soybean and linseed oils21,34. Since iodine value is directly related to the amount of carbon–carbon double bonds available for epoxidation, these results confirm that sesame oil is a suitable substrate for oxirane formation34.

The double-bond conversion obtained for sesame oil using Dowex® 50WX2 was 95.1%, which is within the range reported for other vegetable oils under related epoxidation conditions21. However, this comparison should be interpreted cautiously because differences in oil composition, oxidant ratio, catalyst loading, solvent system, reaction conditions, and analytical endpoints may affect the reported values. Therefore, the comparison is intended only to provide context for sesame oil as a renewable substrate, not as a direct ranking of catalytic performance.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated the feasibility of producing epoxidized sesame oil using Amberlite® IRC 120H and Dowex® 50WX2 as heterogeneous acidic ion exchange resin catalysts. The marked decrease in iodine value confirmed the consumption of carbon–carbon double bonds, while FTIR and H NMR analyses provided qualitative evidence of oxirane group formation and preservation of the triglyceride backbone.

Dowex® 50WX2 showed the highest double-bond conversion, reaching 95.2% at 80 C after 4 h, whereas Amberlite® IRC 120H reached 87.8% under the same conditions. The statistical analysis confirmed that reaction temperature was the dominant factor affecting double-bond conversion for both catalysts, while reaction time was not significant within the evaluated range. For Dowex® 50WX2, the significant quadratic temperature effect indicated a nonlinear response, suggesting that temperature control is critical during sesame oil epoxidation.

From a practical perspective, these results support the potential use of sesame oil as a renewable feedstock for producing bio-based epoxidized intermediates for polymeric materials, coatings, plasticizers, and related sustainable products. However, because iodine value only quantifies double-bond consumption, future work should include quantitative determination of oxirane oxygen content, epoxy equivalent value, or quantitative ¹H NMR integration to determine epoxide yield and oxirane selectivity. In addition, future studies should evaluate product purification and safety-related parameters, including residual toluene, remaining hydrogen peroxide and acetic acid, catalyst separation efficiency, possible resin leaching, and catalyst reusability. In the present work, toluene was not removed before spectroscopic characterization to avoid additional processing steps that could modify the epoxidized sample. Further studies should also evaluate product stability during storage, scale-up conditions, and the performance of epoxidized sesame oil in polymer formulations.
