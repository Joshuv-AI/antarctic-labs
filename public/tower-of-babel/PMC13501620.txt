Low muscle mass predicts poor response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy in advanced ovarian cancer
Authors: Derya Demirtaş Esmer, Emre Hafızoğlu, Murat Bardakçı, Erdem Özkan, Serhat Sekmek, Doğan Bayram, İrfan Karahan, Efnan Algın, Öznur Bal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdvanced epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC) is commonly diagnosed at late stages, and despite improvements with neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) followed by interval debulking surgery (IDS), prognosis remains poor. Body composition has emerged as an important determinant of treatment outcomes. Low muscle mass (LMM), particularly in obese patients with low muscle mass (OwLMM), may combine impaired functional reserve with adverse metabolic effects. However, its role in NACT outcomes in EOC remains unclear.MethodsIn this retrospective study, 52 overweight or obese women with FIGO stage III–IV EOC treated with platinum–taxane-based NACT and IDS between 2019 and 2022 were analyzed. Skeletal muscle mass was assessed on baseline computed tomography scans at the third lumbar vertebra (L3), and the lumbar skeletal muscle index (LSMI) was calculated. Patients were classified as LMM or high muscle mass (HMM) using the cohort median cut-off. Patients with both obesity and LMM were categorized as OwLMM. Treatment response was evaluated using RECIST v1.1 criteria.ResultsThe median follow-up was 10.2 months (95% CI, 7.8–12.6). Median disease-free survival (DFS) from IDS was 19.3 months (95% CI, 8.5–30.1). OwLMM patients had lower objective response rates (46.7% vs. 62.2%, p = 0.02) and inferior disease control rates (80% vs. 100%, p = 0.03) compared with other groups. BMI or LMM alone showed no significant effect. Overall survival (OS) analysis was premature due to the short follow-up period.ConclusionLow muscle mass, especially when combined with obesity, was associated with reduced NACT response and inferior disease control in advanced EOC. As sarcopenic obesity represents a key component of frailty in older adults, our findings support muscle mass assessment as a clinically relevant biomarker that may improve risk stratification and guide individualized therapy in geriatric oncology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13048-025-01960-4.Keywords: Advanced epithelial ovarian cancer, Neoadjuvant chemotherapy, Low muscle mass, Sarcopenic obesity, Body composition, Treatment response

Introduction

Ovarian cancer is the tenth most common malignancy among women in developed countries and the fifth leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, accounting for more than 150,000 deaths annually. High-grade serous ovarian carcinoma (HGSOC) represents approximately half of epithelial ovarian cancers and is typically diagnosed at advanced stages; nearly 80% of patients present with FIGO stage III–IV disease [1]. The standard treatment for advanced disease is primary debulking surgery (PDS) followed by platinum-based chemotherapy. For patients with unresectable tumors or poor surgical fitness at presentation, neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) followed by interval debulking surgery (IDS) has become an established alternative, aiming to reduce tumor burden, facilitate complete cytoreduction, and lower perioperative morbidity. Achieving macroscopic complete resection remains the most important prognostic determinant, along with FIGO stage, tumor grade, and performance status [2].

Cancer cachexia, reflecting profound metabolic and nutritional disturbances, is common in women with advanced ovarian cancer and has been associated with impaired treatment tolerance and shortened survival [3]. It is a multifactorial syndrome driven by tumor–host interactions and characterized by systemic inflammation, metabolic dysregulation, and progressive skeletal muscle loss. Pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6, tumor necrosis factor-α, and C-reactive protein contribute to muscle protein breakdown, anabolic resistance, and reduced physiological reserve, which may adversely affect treatment response. However, body weight alone is an unreliable marker, as it may be confounded by ascites, edema, or bulky metastases. Moreover, obesity can mask underlying skeletal muscle depletion, making weight-based indices insufficient for risk stratification.

To overcome these limitations, body composition analysis by computed tomography (CT) at the third lumbar vertebra (L3) level has emerged as a robust method to quantify fat and skeletal muscle mass [4]. Low muscle mass (LMM), which can occur across all body mass index (BMI) categories, is increasingly recognized as a determinant of chemotherapy intolerance, postoperative complications, and inferior survival outcomes in cancer patients. The coexistence of obese with low muscle mass (OwLMM) represents a particularly adverse phenotype, combining the unfavorable metabolic consequences of obesity with the diminished physiological reserve conferred by muscle depletion [5].

Although the prognostic implications of body composition have been explored in several malignancies, evidence in ovarian cancer remains limited and heterogeneous. Most studies have focused on survival after primary surgery, while little is known about the influence of baseline muscle mass on chemotherapy responsiveness in the neoadjuvant setting.

The aim of this study was to investigate the impact of low muscle mass, obesity, and their coexistence (OwLMM) on treatment outcomes in patients with advanced epithelial ovarian cancer treated with neoadjuvant chemotherapy. The primary outcome was response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy, assessed according to RECIST v1.1 criteria. Secondary outcomes included disease-free survival and overall survival according to muscle mass and body composition status. By addressing this gap, the study provides insights into the prognostic relevance of body composition and its potential role in individualized treatment strategies.

Materials and methods

This retrospective, single-center study included women diagnosed with advanced epithelial ovarian cancer between May 2019 and June 2022. Eligible patients were aged ≥ 18 years, had histologically confirmed serous, endometrioid, or other high-grade (grade 3–4) carcinoma, and underwent CT as part of the initial staging work-up. Inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years, histologically confirmed high-grade epithelial ovarian cancer (serous, endometrioid, or other grade 3–4 carcinoma), FIGO stage III–IV disease, availability of baseline contrast-enhanced CT imaging as part of the initial staging work-up, and treatment with neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by interval debulking surgery. Patients with stage I–II ovarian cancer were excluded, as neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by interval debulking surgery is primarily indicated for advanced-stage disease. Additional exclusion criteria included non-epithelial ovarian tumors, prior systemic chemotherapy, lack of available baseline CT imaging, or inadequate image quality precluding accurate body composition assessment. A total of 52 patients fulfilled these criteria and were included in the analysis. All underwent contrast-enhanced abdominal CT at baseline, prior to the initiation of NACT. All patients, including those with FIGO stage III disease, were treated with neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by interval debulking surgery. No patients underwent primary debulking surgery prior to neoadjuvant chemotherapy, as treatment decisions were based on multidisciplinary assessment of disease resectability and surgical fitness.

Standard treatment consisted of three to six cycles of carboplatin plus paclitaxel (CP), followed by IDS and subsequent adjuvant chemotherapy (three to six additional cycles of CP). Chemotherapy dosing was calculated using actual body weight and glomerular filtration rate, irrespective of body composition. Clinical and pathological characteristics, including demographics, tumor histology, performance status, and laboratory parameters, were extracted from medical records.

Body composition assessment

Cross-sectional skeletal muscle area was measured on baseline CT scans at the level of the third lumbar vertebra (L3), a validated landmark for estimating whole-body muscle mass. Muscles included in the measurement were the psoas, paraspinal (erector spinae, multifidus, quadratus lumborum), and abdominal wall muscles (transversus abdominis, external and internal obliques, rectus abdominis). The lumbar skeletal muscle index (LSMI, cm²/m²) was calculated by dividing skeletal muscle area at L3 by height squared.

The cohort-specific median LSMI was 41.72 (range: 28.78–63.69). Patients were categorized as low muscle mass (LMM; LSMI < 41.72) or high muscle mass (HMM; LSMI ≥ 41.72). Although fixed cut-off values have been proposed in prior studies, we used the cohort-specific median to minimize bias in this relatively small and heterogeneous population, consistent with other exploratory analyses. BMI was calculated as weight/height² (kg/m²). Patients with BMI ≥ 25 were considered overweight/obese according to World Health Organization (WHO) definitions (overweight: ≥25 kg/m², obese: ≥30 kg/m²). Patients with both obesity and LMM were categorized as OwLMM, while patients with preserved muscle mass were classified as HMM or OwHMM. A schematic flow diagram summarizing patient selection and CT-based body composition assessment is provided in the Supplementary Materials (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as median (range) or mean ± standard deviation, depending on distribution. Group comparisons were performed using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and the Mann–Whitney U or Student’s t-test for continuous variables.

Disease-free survival (DFS) was defined as the time from interval debulking surgery to the first documented recurrence or death, irrespective of radiological complete response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Progression-free survival (PFS) from the initiation of neoadjuvant chemotherapy was not separately evaluated, as all patients underwent interval debulking surgery and disease-free survival from surgery was considered the most clinically relevant endpoint in this cohort. Overall survival (OS) was defined from the date of diagnosis to death from any cause or last follow-up. Median follow-up time was estimated using the reverse Kaplan–Meier method, whereas disease-free survival was calculated using the Kaplan–Meier method and may exceed the median follow-up in the presence of censored observations. Survival distributions were calculated with the Kaplan–Meier method, and intergroup differences were assessed with the log-rank test. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were derived from Cox proportional hazards regression. To reduce overfitting, only variables with p < 0.10 in univariate analysis and considered clinically relevant were included in the multivariate model, following the rule of at least 10 events per variable. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated with Schoenfeld residuals, and no significant violations were detected. Collinearity was assessed with variance inflation factors (VIF), with all values < 2. The linearity of continuous covariates was checked with Martingale residuals. Missing data were < 10% for all covariates; therefore, complete-case analyses were performed. No adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied due to the exploratory nature of the study; instead, effect sizes and confidence intervals were emphasized over strict p-value thresholds.

All analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Follow-up and outcome assessment

Tumor response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy was assessed on contrast-enhanced computed tomography according to RECIST version 1.1 criteria. Complete response (CR) was defined as the disappearance of all target and non-target lesions. Partial response (PR) was defined as at least a 30% decrease in the sum of diameters of target lesions, stable disease (SD) as neither sufficient shrinkage nor increase to qualify as response or progression, and progressive disease (PD) as at least a 20% increase in the sum of diameters of target lesions or the appearance of new lesions. After completion of neoadjuvant chemotherapy and interval debulking surgery, patients were followed according to institutional standard clinical practice. Follow-up visits were scheduled every three months during the first two years and every six months thereafter. At each visit, clinical evaluation and serum CA-125 measurement were performed. Radiological assessment with contrast-enhanced computed tomography was carried out when clinically indicated. Disease recurrence was defined based on radiological and/or clinical evidence.

Ethics

The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee No. 1 of Ankara City Hospital (Approval No: E2-24–6091). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design and anonymized data collection.

Results

A total of 52 patients with advanced epithelial ovarian cancer (FIGO stage III–IV) were analyzed. The median age was 53.3 years (± 11.5 SD). High-grade serous carcinoma was the predominant histology, and 86.5% of tumors were poorly differentiated. Most patients were diagnosed via peritoneal biopsy, and 43 (82.7%) presented with ascites at baseline. According to BMI, all patients were overweight or obese. Half of the cohort was classified as having LMM and the other half as HMM. Within the LMM group, 15 patients were obese, constituting the OwLMM subgroup (Table 1).

OwLMM patients demonstrated the poorest outcomes: their objective response rate was significantly lower compared with other groups (46.7% vs. 62.2%, p = 0.02), and they were the only subgroup in which disease progression occurred during NACT (20%, n = 3). Consequently, the disease control rate (DCR) was markedly inferior in OwLMM patients (80% vs. 100%, p = 0.03). Similarly, patients classified as LMM by the LSMI cut-off had significantly worse DCR than their HMM counterparts (88.5% vs. 100%, p = 0.04). Discussion

The primary aim of this study was to determine whether LMM, alone or in combination with obesity, affects the response to NACT in advanced epithelial ovarian cancer. We found that LMM, particularly in OwLMM, was strongly associated with lower objective response rates and inferior disease control. Notably, no significant associations were observed between baseline clinicopathological variables—including FIGO stage, tumor grade, ascites, CA-125 levels, or inflammatory indices—and treatment response, underscoring that muscle mass status itself was the decisive determinant.

Our findings extend the growing body of literature examining the role of body composition in cancer outcomes. Studies in gastric, breast, esophageal, and pancreatic cancers treated with NACT have yielded mixed results: while some found no association between sarcopenia and pathological response, others reported impaired treatment tolerance and survival among sarcopenic patients [6–10]. Obesity has also been linked to worse prognosis in ovarian and other malignancies through mechanisms such as hyperinsulinemia, estrogen excess, and chronic inflammation [11]. Moreover, ascites, edema, and adiposity can further obscure underlying muscle depletion, limiting the utility of weight-based indices in ovarian cancer. In line with previous reports, ascites was present in more than 80% of our patients but showed no relationship with chemotherapy response, reinforcing the concept that it reflects tumor burden rather than therapeutic sensitivity [12, 13].

In ovarian cancer, the evidence regarding body composition is far more limited. Notably, recent data focusing on patients treated with neoadjuvant chemotherapy suggest that unfavorable body composition profiles during systemic treatment are associated with impaired survival outcomes, emphasizing the relevance of muscle and adipose tissue dynamics in the neoadjuvant setting [14]. Apart from one study evaluating changes in muscle mass during NACT [6], most prior reports have focused on postoperative morbidity, survival after primary surgery, or chemotherapy toxicity [15, 16]. Previous reviews have also highlighted the poor clinical outcomes associated with sarcopenic obesity across malignancies [17]. To our knowledge, our analysis is the first to demonstrate that baseline LMM is directly associated with impaired chemotherapy response in the neoadjuvant setting of advanced epithelial ovarian cancer.

Regarding baseline muscle status, the prevalence of LMM in our cohort was 50%, within the wide range reported across malignancies (11–74%) [15]. Variability in prevalence largely stems from different definitions and cut-off values [16]. We deliberately used the cohort median LSMI as the threshold, a strategy widely applied in exploratory studies with limited sample sizes to balance groups and minimize bias. While this approach may limit comparability with studies using fixed cut-offs, it ensured statistical validity in our dataset.

The absence of complete responses (CR) in our cohort deserves mention. In large series such as the CHORUS trial, CR rates after NACT were reported at 7–16% [18, 19]. The lack of CR in our study is most likely explained by the small sample size, differences in assessment methods, and patient selection. Nevertheless, the consistent disadvantage of OwLMM patients across multiple response endpoints strengthens the robustness of our findings despite limited numbers. Fisher’s exact test was applied to account for small subgroup counts, reducing the risk of spurious significance.

Interestingly, while treatment response was clearly impaired in OwLMM patients, DFS appeared numerically longer in this subgroup. This paradox is best explained by immature survival data: median follow-up (10.2 months) was shorter than the estimated DFS (19.3 months), a discrepancy expected in censored datasets where event-free patients extend survival estimates beyond the observation period. Longer follow-up will be necessary to determine whether the short-term response disadvantage observed in OwLMM translates into inferior long-term survival.

Our results are consistent with prior studies in other tumor types, including renal, pancreatic, and metastatic breast cancers, where muscle depletion was associated with worse treatment outcomes and shorter survival [8–10]. Conversely, BMI or inflammatory indices alone did not predict outcomes, highlighting that skeletal muscle mass, not excess weight or systemic inflammation, was the key driver of treatment resistance. In advanced ovarian carcinoma, previous studies found no significant survival differences between sarcopenic and non-sarcopenic patients, although high fat mass ratio was identified as an independent prognostic factor for overall survival within the sarcopenic subgroup [20]. Moreover, when sarcopenia and obesity coexist, survival outcomes may reflect the additive negative effects of both conditions [21]. Consistent with this concept, recent analyses across gynecologic malignancies have identified sarcopenic obesity as a particularly adverse phenotype, supporting the prognostic importance of combined muscle and adiposity assessment beyond conventional clinical factors [22]. Recent evidence in high-grade serous ovarian cancer further suggests that CT-based body composition parameters capture underlying metabolic dysfunction not reflected by body mass index alone, reinforcing the biological relevance of muscle and adiposity assessment in this disease [23].

This study has several limitations. The retrospective, single-center design and relatively small sample size limit generalizability and statistical power. Moreover, all patients were overweight or obese, precluding evaluation across the full BMI spectrum. Body composition was assessed using BMI and LSMI rather than dedicated visceral fat indices, which may have provided additional insights. Finally, overall survival data remain immature due to limited follow-up.

In summary, our findings indicate that low muscle mass at diagnosis is associated with impaired response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy in patients with advanced epithelial ovarian cancer. CT-based muscle mass assessment may provide clinically relevant information beyond conventional prognostic factors and could help refine risk stratification in this setting. Future prospective, multicenter studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up are warranted to validate these findings and to explore interventions aimed at preserving or restoring skeletal muscle mass in this vulnerable population.

Conclusion

In this retrospective cohort, low muscle mass—particularly in obese patients (OwLMM)—was associated with impaired response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy in advanced epithelial ovarian cancer. These findings suggest that CT-based muscle mass assessment may serve as a simple biomarker to complement conventional prognostic factors in patient stratification. Given the limited sample size and retrospective design, validation in larger, multicenter prospective cohorts is warranted.
