 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This document reports a detailed initial risk assessment associated with the exposure of liquid metal (LM)
targets to plasma flux in the Material Plasma Exposure eXperiment (MPEX) at ORNL. This document,
while preliminary, does give a comprehensive mitigation plan to the risks along with justification and
recommendations before potentially proceeding to LM exposures. At the date of issuance of this report,
the MPEX Project has completed Final Design, and the MPEX device is in the Fabrication stage.
Remaining project stages include Commissioning of the device with plasma and transitioning to routine
Operations for research. As will be described in this document, since the MPEX device is currently being
fabricated and the existing design does not include sufficient engineering provisions for most LM targets,
no LM exposures will be conducted in MPEX until further assessment can be conducted and the
necessary engineering changes incorporated (during MPEX Operations.)

This executive summary will include: 1) overarching high-level recommendations, 2) a brief list of
relevant findings, and 3) priority ranked mitigations to reduce the risk to the MPEX device. Details
supporting these recommendations and suggested mitigations can be found in the body of this document.

The following high-level recommendations (HLR) will be motived in the body of this document, but are
summarized here.

 HLR1: An MPEX Liquid Metal Assessment Committee (LMAC) should be established to
 review the issues raised in this document and any other issues around the design changes to
 MPEX that must be enacted prior to authorizing LM exposures in MPEX. This LMAC should
 include subject matter experts (SMEs) from both inside and outside ORNL.

 HLR2: A model of plasma and neutral gas transport should be developed for the MPEX device,
 e.g. SOLPS or similar, that includes a realistic LM target source. This will be necessary to
 understand and quantify the transport of atoms from the LM target throughout the MPEX device.
 This model should be verified with experimental data from the initial operation of the MPEX
 device, prior to application to LM target material migration.

 HLR3: The initial operations of MPEX will be very informative about the as-built device
 performance and capabilities. MPEX designed performance was extrapolated from experiments
 and modeling based on the Proto-MPEX device. Assumptions about, e.g., power utilization,
 component temperatures, plasma performance should be re-examined once initial MPEX
 experimental data is available.

Significant findings related to LM utilization in MPEX detailed in this document include:

 F1: Alkali metals that are highly reactive must be given special consideration before utilization
 in MPEX, including Li, Na, K, Cs and Rb.

 F2: The LM that poses the greatest risk to MPEX is liquid Li, which is corrosive to numerous
 materials and has a high vapor pressure.

 F3: Li metal (static) inventory in MPEX must be kept below 3.45 mol (24 g) to avoid H2
 explosion risk in the event of moist air ingress event.

 F4: Li inventory > 3.45 mol (especially in flowing loops) will require major modifications to
 MPEX (e.g. alternative to H20 cooling) and substantial building modifications (e.g. no H20
 systems.)

 1

 F5: Sn-Li should be handled and treated as pure Li.

 F6: Sn and Ga are non-reactive; so no mass limits are required.

 F7: Alkali metals generally have the highest vapor pressure of the metals relevant to fusion.

 F8: A wide range of evaporation rates will occur with a LM target in MPEX, not only between
 shots as plasma parameters vary, but during each shot as the target heats up.

 F9: The vapor from a LM surface can transport far distances with multiple collisions in the
 plume, and deposit on cold walls both upstream and downstream of the target.

 F10: Frequent cleaning will be required to keep MPEX functioning and ensure no unwanted
 buildup of metal occurs.

 F11: Some metal compounds formed at the wall may be conducting while others are insulators.
 Even a thin surface film may change the dielectric performance of insulators and waveguides,
 resulting in biasing with arcing and increased power absorption at the walls.

 F12: MPEX cannot be vented with atmospheric gases when liquid Li is present in the chamber.

 F13: One of the most useful materials in MPEX is also one of the most incompatible with Li:
 copper. All copper components in MPEX that are sufficiently visible to Li are at a great risk of
 damage.

 F14: Glidcop Al-15 will also be incompatible with Li, perhaps more so than either copper or
 alumina alone.

 F15: Li is incompatible with silicon and silicon nitride. Silicon has been found to have relatively
 high solubility in liquid Li at 475 C.

 F16: Alkali metals are not compatible with sapphire, so DUV sapphire windows are not
 compatible with Li.

 F17: Zinc Selenide is also not compatible with alkali metals, and so ZnSe windows must be
 protected from Li in MPEX.

 F18: Quartz and Pyrex windows ae compatible with Li.

 F19: Vapor corrosion testing of OHFC copper gaskets over. Range of surface and vapor
 temperatures is needed to assess the risk that Li vapor poses to these gaskets.

 F20: The current MPEX target payload is not LM compatible.

 1. INTRODUCTION

1.1 MOTIVATION FOR LMS PFCS

Liquid metal (LM) plasma-facing components (PFCs) have the potential to offer a variety of benefits to
fusion reactors [1]. LM surfaces can be continuously restored in flowing systems or capillary porous
systems (CPS) with reservoirs [2]. Additionally, LM PFCs can be effective at dissipating heat in the
divertor through vapor shielding [3]. Li is low Z (Z=3) so a large fraction of the plasma could be
composed of Li before radiative losses become significant. As an alkali metal, Li getters hydrogen isotope

 5

(deuterium and tritium) fuel and impurity species, increases fusing volume, heightens edge plasma
temperatures, and reduces plasma instabilities such as ELMs [4, 2, 5, 6]. However, LM PFCs are at a low
technological readiness level and introduce many new challenges to fusion devices such as high
evaporative flux and material compatibility concerns. More LM PFC research and development is needed
for the technology to be utilized in future fusion reactors and pilot plants.

The Material Plasma Exposure eXperiment (MPEX), a linear plasma device for fusion materials testing,
could be utilized to investigate many of the outstanding questions about LM PFCs and test their viability
for fusion reactors. Since LM PFCs and LM targets in MPEX introduce a variety of challenges such as
deposition on diagnostic windows and corrosion by Li, the potential effects of LMs in MPEX must be
investigated so that LM campaigns in MPEX can be conducted safely without damaging any MPEX
systems.

1.2 METALS OF INTEREST TO FUSION

A select set of metals have particular importance to plasma-material interactions (and blankets) in
fusion reactors. These metals are tin, indium, gallium, lithium and the lithium eutectics of Sn-Li, Cu-
Li and PbLi. All transition metals including high melting point refractory metals such as tungsten and
molybdenum are completely safe in any quantities inside MPEX either in solid or liquid phase.
Ingestion does create toxicity problems for humans, but is easily mitigated by PPE and good hygiene.
Additionally, small solid targets of low melting point transition metals like Sn, In and Ga are also safe
in MPEX. However, particular attention must be given here to the alkali metals that are highly
reactive. These include Li, Na, K, Cs and Rb. Of these, only lithium has impact on fusion systems for
its ability to breed tritium in blankets and also for density control by pumping hydrogen when
exposed directly to plasmas as part of a plasma facing component [7].

1.3 SCOPE

In this document, an extensive examination of MPEX is undertaken to identify all challenges unique
to using LMs in MPEX. The LM that poses the most risk to MPEX is liquid Li, which is corrosive to
numerous materials and has a high vapor pressure. Components at risk of damage during a LM
campaign are identified by their material compatibility with lithium, predicted surface temperature,
and distance from the LM target. Mitigations are suggested for all at-risk components. Additionally,
steps for the first LM campaign are outlined, such as preparatory modifications, hazard monitoring
methods, and inspection and cleaning procedures.

 3. REQUIREMENTS AND CONSTRAINTS

The MPEX Project Requirements Document (PRD) contains the following:
 • “It shall have the capability to expose hazardous materials like neutron-irradiated materials
 and liquid metals” (PR39-R) [8].
 • “All in-vessel materials and their joints shall be compatible with the operation of alkaline target
 materials (e.g., liquid Li) of 3.45 moles in quantity” (PR581-R) [8].

Additionally, the project specifications document states the following:
 • “the materials of the vacuum chamber and the operational and maintenance plans of the device
 are chosen such that they minimize the likelihood of leaks occurring (structural integrity) and can
 prevent exothermic reactions with aggressive in-vessel materials (such as liquid metals).”

This report seeks to identify all potential challenges associated with LM targets in MPEX and address
them. Engineering choices were made during the design of MPEX that resulted in many of the material
choices for MPEX (approved, final design) structural components are not-compatible with LM targets, as
will be described in this document. Engineering changes (during MPEX operational lifetime) will be
required to permit safe plasma exposure onto LM targets.

 4. METAL MASS LIMITS IN MPEX

4.1 LITHIUM AND OTHER ALKALI METALS

An assessment of LMs in MPEX was made during the conceptual design phase of MPEX by D.
Youchison, an SME that was present during a flowing Li explosion at Sandia National Lab. His analysis
considered both static inventory of LMs, and flowing inventories of LMs. That assessment concluded
that flowing LMs, particularly liquid Li, should not be considered for the MPEX design, due to
deficiencies in the facility where MPEX was to be constructed. His assessment identified a critical
threshold of 3.45 moles for the (static inventory) amount of Li that should be permitted in MPEX. This
mass limit exists because lithium reacts with water or water vapor and produces hydrogen gas.
Calculation reveals that 136 g of lithium reacting with water would bring the MPEX PMI chamber
pressure due to hydrogen up to 1 atm at room temperature. But the lower explosive limit (LEL) for
hydrogen when mixed with air is 18%. Hence, in the event of a moist air ingress, a lithium inventory of
24 g could cause a hydrogen explosion in the PMI chamber. Since the mass for any given molar fraction
scales with the molecular weight, the other alkali metals would require significantly more mass to
generate that much hydrogen compared to lithium. Lithium was the bounding, lowest-mass case in this
analysis.

Lithium is the lightest element in solid form at room temperature. It has a density of 0.534 g/cm3. A 2.5
cm × 2.5 cm × 0.5 cm thick volume of solid lithium would weigh roughly 1.67 g. A standard 12 mm
diameter base frustrum of a cone MPEX target, 1 mm thick, has a volume of 0.381 cm3 and would weigh
204 mg if composed of Li. (Note that the current MPEX target geometry for tungsten would not be
structurally sound if the same geometry is used for Li, or other alkali metals. This example is given for
the purpose of mass comparison only.) The maximum mass of any solid alkali metal in MPEX should be
limited to less than 3.45 moles which corresponds to 24 g for lithium or 45 cm3. A comparison of this
volume of Li to a roll of quarters and a soda can is shown in Figure 4.1.

4.2 TIN, GALLIUM, AND TRANSITION METALS

Tin has a density of 7.31 g/cm3. A standard 1-in. block, 5 mm thick would weigh 23.6 g. The MPEX
standard target would weigh 2.79 g. Tin is not a safety hazard but can be difficult to clean and can
degrade vacuum system performance. A tin-lithium eutectic is also of interest to fusion. Although not as
reactive as pure lithium, Sn-Li should be handled and treated as lithium [7]. The mass limit for Sn-Li will
depend on the proportion of the alloy that is Li, but no more than 3.45 moles of Li can be present in
MPEX.

Gallium has a density of 5.9 g/cm3. A standard 1-in. block would weigh 18.4 g and the MPEX standard
target would weigh 2.2 g. Tin and gallium are non-reactive; so no mass limits are required. Only
quantities of alkali metals must be limited to less than 3.45 moles. Mass limits do not apply to common
transition metals.

 5. CHALLENGES OF LIQUID METAL TARGETS

While liquid metals can improve some aspects of fusion device performance, they are notoriously
challenging to work with for a variety of reasons. This section explores the various challenges
associated with liquid metals and how these challenges could affect MPEX.

It is important to note that the challenges and hazards listed below scale with the quantity of LM being
used. For example, if a small quantity of lithium is used, negligible corrosion and vapor deposition
may occur. However, using lithium quantities closer to the administrative limit will incur a higher risk

 8

of component damage. Additionally, a greater exposed surface area of LM will result in higher
deposition, so windows will be coated faster and related diagnostics will fail sooner.

5.1 VAPOR PRESSURE AND DEPOSITION

Liquid metal surfaces can emit large quantities of vapor that can enter the plasma, deposit on windows
and diagnostics, and react with surfaces. Alkali metals generally have the highest vapor pressures of
the metals relevant to fusion, so the concern of deposition is greatest for them. Even as a solid, an
alkali metal can have a significant vapor pressure. As the melting point of an alkali metal is
approached, the vapor pressure often increases dramatically, and atoms begin to evaporate from the
surface in vacuum. These atoms are often deposited on colder surfaces and form a “frost” or
condensate that may react with the material they are deposited on. These reactions do require some
activation energy to initiate, but some are exothermic and become self-sustaining. The activation may
be provided by energetic particle bombardment, radiation or heat conduction at a hot wall [7]. These
vapor surface reactions can lead to surface degradation and corrosion. The vapor pressure curves for
alkali metals appears in Figure 5.1.

As the metal melts, the vapor pressures increase dramatically, and the issues related to plasma
contamination and deposition become more severe. Above the melting point, the quantity of vapor
released tends to be far above that from thermal desorption or sputtering. This effect is shown in

potassium attains 1 Torr at 345 ◦C. In contrast, tin must reach 1612 ◦C before the vapor pressure exceeds
1 Torr. The temperature of a liquid lithium target’s surface in MPEX is difficult to predict, but
experiments in Magnum-PSI, a linear plasma device, found the surface temperature of liquid lithium
targets to fluctuate within the range of 700-900°C. Therefore, lithium vapor pressures of 1 Torr and
higher should be anticipated in MPEX’s PMI chamber.

As described above and shown in Figure 5.2, the evaporation rate of liquid lithium is strongly dependent

the evaporation rate increased by five orders of magnitude between surface temperatures of 250°C and
550°C [12]. This indicates that a wide range of evaporation rates will occur with a LM target in MPEX,
not only between shots as plasma parameters are varied, but during each shot as the target heats up.

Depending on the temperature of the surfaces lithium deposits on and the associated sticking

deposit on a wall, and if the wall’s temperature is great enough for some of the deposited vapor to

interact with components that weren’t in line-of-sight of the target but are from the wall. This
redeposition has been observed in the Lithium Tokamak Experiment (LTX) at Princeton Plasma
Physics Laboratory (PPPL). Surfaces throughout the vessel were coated in lithium because of this
effect.

Deposition is a serious issue for diagnostics in the PMI chamber, as windows quickly become mirrors
when in line-of-sight of a LM target. This will lead to visual and IR diagnostics failing over time.
Additionally, diagnostics that utilize mirrors could be adversely affected, as a thin metal film will
change the mirror’s surface properties. Frequent cleaning will be required to keep MPEX functioning
and ensure no unwanted buildup of metal occurs. The extent and frequency of the cleanup depends on
the evaporation rate, redeposition factor (the proportion of eroded metal that returns to the target), the
line of sight from the LM target to sensitive surfaces, and the transport of vapor or metal ions through
the plasma.

5.2 ALTERING CONDUCTING OR INSULATING SURFACES

Some metal compounds formed at the wall may be conducting while others are insulators. Even a thin
surface film may change the dielectric performance of insulators and waveguides resulting in biasing with
arcing and increased power absorption at the walls [7].

5.3 LIQUID METAL SPILLS AND DROPLET EJECTION

One issue associated with all liquid metal targets is the possibility sudden loss of the liquid metal from the
target. An unexpected transient event could cause liquid metal to spill out of the target. Overfilling the
target is most likely to lead to this issue. Spills have been observed in Magnum-PSI on overfilled
capillary porous system (CPS) lithium targets. When a CPS target was warmed up during initial plasma
exposure, large lithium droplets fell out of the target. Staff believe this was because the CPS targets were
loaded with lithium and allowed to cool, shrinking the lithium and making room for more. More lithium
was added to fill it completely. When the target was warmed up, the lithium expanded and the target
overflowed.

Another loss mechanism for liquid metal is droplet ejection from the target. Droplet ejection has been
observed during LM campaigns in Magnum-PSI. However, the frequency of droplet ejection was low:
less than 10 droplets were observed by a fast-frame camera in over 70 shots [13]. Again, the issue of
droplet ejection greatly depends on the target design. Narrow trenches and porous structures better
contain liquid metals by surface tension than larger, unsupported pools, which can lead to lower droplet
ejection.

 12

Once the liquid metal settles and freezes after a spill or droplet ejection, it may be difficult to remove and
clean. Of much greater concern is the corrosion hazard posed by a loss of liquid lithium. Spilled or ejected
lithium could settle on a copper gasket, corroding it and causing a major leak. Additionally, if spilled
lithium lands on a window, it could possibly crack the window: lithium droplets at temperatures around
350°C have been observed to shatter borosilicate glass slides. This is not a material being used for the
windows, but it is a potential hazard to keep in mind.

5.4 CHALLENGES UNIQUE TO LITHIUM AND OTHER ALKALI METALS

5.4.1 Reactivity with Atmospheric Gases

Lithium, like the other alkali metals, is a solid at room temperature, and safe handling of solid lithium
mostly involves the maintenance of an inert environment to prevent tarnishing (formation of carbonates
and hydroxides) of the metal. Figure 5.5 shows how quickly solid lithium will tarnish when exposed to
atmosphere. Clean lithium is a lustrous, silver color. It rapidly darkens within minutes when exposed to
moist air as shown in Figure 5.5. The darkening is due to the formation of lithium hydroxide (LiOH) [14],
and the chemical reaction for this process is given in Equation 1. After exposure to moist air for a time on
the order of days, the lithium surface whitens as lithium carbonate (Li2CO3) forms. This chemical
reaction is given in Equation 2. Lithium samples that are left exposed to atmosphere for long periods of
time (weeks or more) are a bright white color due to the surface becoming mostly Li2CO3. Figure 5.6
shows the whitening of a small sample of solid lithium over time. The surface does not immediately
whiten after exposure to moist air because ambient air contains only 0.04% CO2 and LiOH is needed as a
reactant. The initial formation of LiOH catalyzes the formation of LiCO3 [14].

Solid lithium can be handled in open air at room temperature, either coated with mineral oil or where
relative humidity (R.H.) is below 50 percent. Mineral oil does not react with lithium and will prevent

(close to 0% R.H.) to prevent tarnishing. In work done at Princeton Plasma Physics Laboratory, lithium
samples exposed to air at 3.3% R.H. did not have large observable changes to the surface over a period of
7 hours.

Lithium may be stored for long periods of time in inert (e.g. argon) atmosphere or in mineral oil. Solid
lithium reacts relatively slowly in excess water, evolving hydrogen gas and lithium-hydroxide solution
[7].

As a liquid, lithium will violently react with atmospheric gases and can ignite, which poses a major safety
hazard. Therefore, MPEX cannot be vented with atmospheric gases when liquid lithium is present in the
chamber.

A perfectly inert environment can be difficult to maintain when a lithium sample is being transferred
between environments or chambers. In the case of a prefilled lithium target, a dark layer can form on the
lithium’s surface during installation. This occurred during the mounting of lithium targets in Magnum-
PSI [3]. This is undesirable as this layer could affect the lithium’s interaction with the plasma. Further,
impurities on the surface can clog capillary structures in lithium targets [3].

5.4.2 Hazardous Exothermic Reactions

Lithium has an exothermic reaction with water vapor liberating about 222 kJ/mole as shown previously in
Equation 1.

If 3.45 moles (24 g) of lithium were to completely react with water, it would release 766 kJ of energy.
This is equivalent to raising 9.6 liters of water from 20◦C to 100◦C at STP. When the lithium is solid,
LiOH forms rapidly on the metal surface and passivates it, so much of the bulk metal cannot react with
the oxygen. The water reaction with solid lithium is relatively slow but produces copious amounts of
hydrogen. 3.45 moles of lithium that completely react with water produce 38.6 L of hydrogen gas at STP.
Fortunately, lithium cannot liberate enough energy to melt itself. However, when the lithium is already a
liquid, the water reaction is violent and the lithium can light on fire.

Solid sodium or potassium will quickly melt once a water reaction has started. Nothing can passivate the
surface at that point, and all the sodium or potassium will be consumed. Sodium and potassium can self-
ignite and be a detonation source for accumulated hydrogen.

 14

Sodium likewise has an exothermic reaction that releases 184 kJ/mole. Therefore, 3.45 moles (79 g) of
sodium when completely reacted with water would release 127 kJ. This is equivalent to raising 1.6 liters
of water from 20 ◦C to 100 ◦C at STP. For potassium, reacting 3.45 moles (93 g) is equivalent to releasing
674 kJ or raising 8.4 liters of water from 20 ◦C to 100 ◦C.

Vapors from alkali metals like lithium will getter oxygen from the environment and form LiOH. The
oxygen source is typically water vapor. As lithium cracks the water it also releases hydrogen which must
be removed by the vacuum pumps. Accumulated hydrogen poses a detonation risk near hot surfaces or
electrical contacts.

Handling of lithium dust or powder in any quantities is an extreme fire hazard due to the large surface
area available for moist air reaction and should be avoided. This material may be prevalent in lithium
droppers or shakers. Extra precautions should be taken to ensure it is handled only in an inert atmosphere
like an argon or helium cover gas [7].

In addition to water, lithium also has exothermic reactions with alumina, several insulators, pure
aluminum, and other materials. These reactions and lithium’s incompatibility with materials relevant to
MPEX are detailed in the next section.

5.4.3 Material Compatibility

One of the biggest challenges of using a lithium target in MPEX is lithium’s incompatibility with
numerous materials. Before identifying a few of the most incompatible materials in MPEX, it is important
to note that the level of a material’s incompatibility with lithium generally has a strong temperature-
dependence and temperature can have a more important effect than exposure time on the severity of the
corrosion that occurs [15, 16].

It is also important to note that much of the literature referenced in this section pertains to liquid lithium
rather than lithium vapor. The compatibility between lithium vapor and a material may be different than
that between liquid lithium and that same material. Additionally, the concept of temperature is less
straightforward when considering lithium vapor. In liquid lithium corrosion tests, the liquid lithium and
the material being tested are at the same temperature. However, lithium vapor in MPEX will likely be
hotter than the materials it interacts with. So although a material may be at a low temperature where
reactions with lithium of the same temperature are scarce, hot lithium vapor might violently react with
that material. This discrepancy must be kept in mind whenever surface temperature of a material is
discussed, as a low surface temperature may not mean a material is safe from corrosion by hot lithium
vapor. There is a lack of literature on lithium vapor corrosion, so more informed estimates of the damage
caused by lithium vapor would require new studies to be performed.

5.4.3.1    Copper

Unfortunately, one of the most useful materials in MPEX is also one of the most incompatible with
lithium: copper. Lithium does not react with copper; however, it forms a lithium-copper eutectic,
essentially dissolving the copper at 200 ◦C. Copper will lose structural integrity and items like ConFlat
gaskets and cooling plates will soften, deform and fail. A liquid lithium corrosion study conducted at
347°C (620 K) showed extreme corrosion of copper by lithium [17]. Figure 5.7 shows the extent of
damage done to copper by exposure to liquid lithium at this temperature. Additionally, exposing copper to
liquid lithium leads to embrittlement [18].

Corrosion of copper by lithium can occur even when lithium is a solid in contact with copper. This was

Previous work has shown that lithium vapor can damage copper gaskets at 425°C (700 K) [16]. All
copper components in MPEX that are sufficiently visible to lithium are at a great risk of damage.

5.4.3.2   Aluminum

Lithium reacts exothermically with pure aluminum and can form an aluminum-lithium alloy [7, 20, 21].
Therefore, exposed aluminum in MPEX close to a liquid lithium target is at risk of damage and failure.

5.4.3.3   Glidcop Al-15

Glidcop Al-15 is a low alumina content grade of dispersion strengthened copper [22]. It is 0.15 wt%
alumina, and the rest is copper. Given that both copper and alumina are incompatible with lithium,
Glidcop Al-15 will also be incompatible with lithium, perhaps more so than either copper or alumina
alone. No literature on Glidcop Al-15 corrosion in lithium exists, however.

 16

5.4.3.4 Silicon Nitride

Lithium is incompatible with silicon and silicon nitride (Si3N4, though silicon nitride is commonly
referred to as SiN). Silicon has been found to have relatively high solubility in liquid lithium at 475°C
(750 K) [23], so silicon is not compatible with lithium at this temperature or greater. It is unclear what the
lower temperature limit for silicon’s incompatibility with lithium is. Previous work has found silicon
nitride to undergo decomposition at temperatures as low as 250°C, including strong discoloration [24].
These findings are of relevance to the silicon nitride helicon and ICH windows, as discoloration and
damage to these windows could pose major problems.

5.4.3.5    Insulators

Liquid lithium is generally incompatible with insulators. Lithium has an exothermic reaction with Al2O3
(alumina) used in many insulators which is shown in Equation 3. A complex mix of products can result in

Other aluminum bearing ceramics such as AlN are just as susceptible to lithium attack [7]. AlN and CaO

5.4.3.6 Window Materials: Sapphire and ZnSe

Lithium’s compatibility with some window materials is poor. The three window materials being
considered are CV diamond, DUV sapphire, and UV fused silica. No literature dedicated to the
material compatibility of lithium and CVD diamond exists, but work on doping CVD diamond with a
Li precursor showed Li did not affect the quality of the deposited diamond [26]. This suggests that Li
is compatible with CVD diamond, but dedicated corrosion testing between Li vapor and CVD
diamond should be done to better assess their compatibility.

Alkali metals are not compatible with sapphire, so DUV sapphire windows are not compatible with
lithium. Sapphire is a form of aluminum oxide, Al2O3. Lithium aluminum oxide is a known
compound [27], and aluminum oxide is known to form compounds with lithium oxide
[28].

Zinc selenide (ZnSe) is also not compatible with alkali metals, so ZnSe windows must be protected
from lithium in MPEX [7].

Lithium is known form compounds with silica [29], but fused silica windows have been used on
Magnum PSI without any corrosion issues. Additionally, staff at the Lithium Tokamak Experiment
(LTX) at PPPL have said that fused silica does not get damaged by lithium. Therefore, fused silica
windows can likely be used on MPEX. Quartz and Pyrex windows are compatible with Li [7], so they
can also be used.

5.4.4 Liquid Metal Embrittlement

A dangerous effect of alkali liquid metal exposure is liquid metal embrittlement. When certain materials
are in contact with liquid lithium, certain mechanical properties can worsen and lead to failure much more
rapidly than in nominal conditions [30]. This can lead to the unexpected failure of components. As a
worst case scenario, the unexpected failure of a component in contact with lithium could lead lithium to
come into contact with components that were not lithium compatible. A cascade of failures could occur
that pose a significant safety hazard.

This very scenario occurred at Sandia National Lab in a liquid lithium experiment. A lithium fire broke
out in their Li-He heat exchanger experiment, leading to a large flange being separated from the vacuum
vessel [31]. No personnel were harmed, but serious injury or death could have occurred if staff were
behind the ejected flange. The failure sequence was started by hot lithium moving into a colder preheater,
inducing thermal stress. A 1018 steel cover plate cracked in a matter of seconds as the result of stress-
induced liquid metal embrittlement. Then, lithium leaked into the chamber and made its way to an
alumina insulator. An exothermic reaction occurred and the insulator began to crack, leaking water. The
reaction between the liquid lithium and water produced hydrogen and led to a fire.

The 1018 steel that failed was recommended by literature as a lithium-compatible material [32]. This
illustrates the lack of understanding of liquid metal embrittlement and the danger it poses. Fortunately,
lithium is not pumped through pipes in MPEX, so a leak like the one at Sandia cannot occur. However, if
lithium were to spill from the target and contact the cold vessel wall or a window, such liquid metal

 19

embrittlement could be disastrous. Water lines or windows could crack, leading to exothermic reactions
and fire.

5.5 A CHALLENGE UNIQUE TO TIN: STANNANE (SNH4) FORMATION

Although tin is very inert compared to lithium, tin reacts exothermically with hydrogen radicals to form a
tin hydride called stannane (SnH4). This compound is a volatile gas and decomposes at room
temperature. Metallic surfaces can act as catalysts for this decomposition. SnH4 will decompose into two
H2 molecules and one Sn atom [33]. Stannane is known to form from plasma exposure of liquid tin
targets in Magnum-PSI, so it will certainly form in MPEX if liquid tin targets are used. This compound
poses a problem because it makes tin more difficult to clean from the chamber.

 6. LM TARGET TRANSPORT SIMULATIONS

There are numerous components in MPEX that could be damaged or impaired by a liquid metal,
particularly liquid lithium, target. These at-risk components are discussed in the following section.
Several of these components are in the PMI chamber, meaning they are in line-of-sight of the liquid metal
target and will be negatively affected by a LM target. However, there are many components upstream of
the PMI chamber and LM target, and it is unclear if metal from the LM target will be transported
upstream to these components and damage them.

Observations from numerous liquid Li campaigns in Magnum-PSI describe lithium’s transport as being
largely limited to line-of-sight. However, Magnum-PSI has a different plasma source with a different
orientation along the axis of the device, does not utilize both electron cyclotron heating (ECH) and ion
cyclotron heating (ICH) like MPEX can, and has a lower plasma heating input power than MPEX. These
differences, among others, may cause Li transport in MPEX to be significantly different than in Magnum-
PSI.

LM target transport simulations must be run for MPEX to develop an understanding of transport
phenomena during a LM target campaign and better assess the risk of using LM targets in MPEX. The
SOLPS-ITER (often abbreviated as SOLPS) code suite is recommended for these simulations. SOLPS
combines B2.5, an edge plasma solver that solves plasma fluid equations, and EIRENE, a Monte-Carlo
neutral kinetic code. This allows SOLPS to predict Li (or other another metal) transport through both the
plasma and through neutral gas in MPEX. SOLPS has been used to understand plasma transport in other
linear plasma devices, including Proto-MPEX and Magnum-PSI [34, 35, 36, 37]. SOLPS has even been
used to understand liquid lithium target phenomena in Magnum-PSI [38, 39].

SOLPS LM target simulations will provide a plethora of important insights into LM target usage in
MPEX, such as:

 • Axial profile of Li density (neutral and ion)
 • Axial profile of Li flux to the walls (neutral and ion)
 • How ECH and ICH affect Li density and flux profiles
 • How Li density and flux profiles change in different plasma scenarios, and which plasma
 scenarios will minimize Li transport upstream

This information can be used to inform a more thorough Li readiness plan that specifies which
diagnostics, windows, and plasma-facing surfaces need shielding. Additionally, the radial flux to the
walls can be integrated over time to determine the amount of time it takes to develop a problematically
thick layer of Li on diagnostic surfaces.

 20

A LM MHD/heat transfer code can be coupled to SOLPS to model the interactions between the plasma
and the LM target. In a recent work, this coupling between a LM MHD/heat transfer code and SOLPS
was successfully performed and used to analyze flowing Li divertors [40]. Such coupling will predict the
LM target surface temperature and the emitted Li flux using the incident heat flux from the plasma.

Beyond providing a better understanding of Li transport in MPEX, these SOLPS simulations can be
compared to MPEX experimental data in the future and allow for a more in-depth analysis of LM
phenomena.

7.1 VACUUM SEALS

If lithium was to interact with any of the vacuum seals in MPEX, corrosion could occur and cause leaks in
the chamber. Leaks in the chamber are always problematic, but lithium’s hazardous chemical reactions
with atmospheric gases make leaks an even more serious issue during a liquid lithium campaign.

The seals that would suffer the most damage from lithium are the OHFC copper gaskets. These gaskets
are ubiquitous in vacuum systems are used throughout MPEX. The PMI chamber alone has over 50
vacuum ports, each of which is sealed with a copper gasket. Additionally, the pump ducts connected to
the PMI chamber use copper gaskets. Other seals used in MPEX include silicon O-rings and fluorocarbon
elastomer seals. These three types of seals are investigated below, and alternative seals are suggested for
those that are considered highly at risk of damage by lithium.

7.1.1 OHFC Copper Gaskets

Previous work by Meng with liquid lithium in high vacuum has shown unintended corrosion of copper
gaskets [16]. In Meng’s work, liquid lithium was at roughly 425°C in a sealed stainless steel test vessel
for 1320 hours, and after the test vessel was cleaned of lithium, a 1 mm thick layer of Cu was visible on
the wall near the gasket. A photo of this copper layer is provided in Figure 7.3.

 22

  Figure 7.3: Photo of the surface of the stainless steel test vessel with a layer of copper present after liquid
 lithium exposure for 1320 hours. Image taken directly from Meng, 2020 [16].

MPEX will not have liquid lithium in such close proximity to a copper gasket and exposure will not occur
for such a long period of time, so corrosion of this degree is unlikely. However, Meng notes that the
unintended corrosion of the copper gasket implies that copper gaskets can be damaged by lithium vapor
[16]. Therefore, corrosion of copper gaskets in MPEX will likely occur if the gaskets are sufficiently
visible to Li vapor.

The level of corrosion of Cu by Li is strongly temperature dependent. As was mentioned previously,
lithium essentially dissolves copper at 200◦ [7]. If the temperature of Cu gaskets is below this threshold,
corrosion of the gaskets may not occur or could be negligible. However, if the Li vapor is hot enough, this
threshold might be locally exceeded and the gasket could be damaged over time. Vapor corrosion testing
of OHFC copper gaskets over a range of surface and vapor temperatures is needed to assess the risk
lithium vapor poses to these gaskets.

The likelihood of lithium damaging the copper gaskets in MPEX is somewhat unclear. Observations from
a liquid lithium campaign in Magnum-PSI are encouraging: researchers did not observe visible damage to
copper gaskets, but Magnum-PSI mostly utilizes silicon O-rings. So, this observation does not mean
MPEX will have no issues with copper gasket corrosion. It is also important to note that the lithium
targets used in Magnum-PSI generally had reservoirs that held between 1 g and 2.4 g of lithium, whereas
lithium targets in MPEX can hold up to 24 g (3.45 moles). So, there will likely be a higher flux of lithium
vapor to copper gaskets and subsequently a greater chance of damage.

Gaskets in the PMI chamber are at the greatest risk of damage by lithium due to their proximity to the LM
target. However, all gaskets are relatively concealed from the line of sight of the LM target; only a narrow
sliver of nearby gaskets may be visible to the target, if at all. It is expected that upstream copper gaskets
will not be affected by lithium, but transport simulations are necessary to confirm this.

Suggested Mitigations If vapor exposure tests are conducted and the results are favorable, copper gaskets
might be able to be used throughout MPEX and even in the PMI chamber. The results of vapor exposure
tests should be combined with transport simulation results to decide what gaskets must be replaced with a
compatible material. The safest choice is to replace copper gaskets in regions with a higher flux of Li or
higher temperatures with a compatible material. Annealed nickel gaskets are not recommended, as liquid
Li corrosion studies have found lithium to deplete stainless steels of nickel, and the corrosion resistance
of steels was found to decrease as nickel content increases [41]. This implies that nickel is not a
particularly lithium-compatible material. Additionally, annealed nickel gaskets are significantly more
expensive than coated copper gaskets. Silver-coated gaskets should be avoided, as silver coated 316

 23

stainless steel was found to be significantly degraded in static liquid lithium corrosion testing [42]. The
most corrosion-resistant gasket would be a stainless steel or stainless-steel-coated copper gasket [7]. So,
gaskets that have a high lithium vapor flux to the surrounding surface (determined by transport
simulations) should be replaced with stainless steel gaskets (M-VSL-1).

7.1.2 Non-Metal Gaskets and O-rings

Very little is known about the compatibility of liquid lithium with non-metal gaskets and

O-rings such as the rubber gaskets and fluorocarbon elastomer used in select locations in MPEX. It is
likely that these gaskets have less of a compatibility concern than copper gaskets, as rubber O-rings are
used extensively throughout Magnum-PSI and no damage has been observed after several liquid lithium
campaigns. Non-metal gaskets will be at risk of damage by lithium if they are in the PMI chamber or in
locations in MPEX that have a high lithium flux to the wall (as determined by transport simulations).

Suggested Mitigations Vapor exposure tests should be conducted to better assess the risk lithium poses
to the non-metal vacuum seals that are identified as at-risk by transport simulations. Whenever possible,
non-metal gaskets close to the liquid metal target or near surfaces that interact with the plasma should be
replaced stainless steel gaskets (M-VSL2).

7.2 PUMPS AND VACUUM

Hydrogen will be produced in the PMI chamber when lithium targets are used because of the reaction
between lithium and water vapor. Accumulated hydrogen poses a detonation hazard, so hydrogen must
not build up in the PMI chamber.

Suggested Mitigations Turbomolecular pumps are relatively inefficient at pumping hydrogenic species
due to the low mass of hydrogen. Cryopumps are more efficient in pumping hydrogenic species. Real-
time feedback may be required on the hydrogen mass flow controllers and the pumping to maintain
proper hydrogen inventories.

An argon purge/vent system should be available to immediately fill the vacuum chamber if an unplanned
event occurs with high hydrogen partial pressures in the chamber (M-VAC1). This will also prevent or
mitigate the ingress of oxygen or moist air into the chamber should any breach occur. A manual
emergency crash button should be considered for this system, since an automatic system will likely lead
to unintended actuation. This purge/vent system can be confined to the PMI chamber if under normal
circumstances but must also be available for an entire system vent should the PMI chamber isolation
valves not close or perhaps leak. An option should be available to vent with argon only in case there is
any uncertainty to the temperature or condition of the target. Dry nitrogen will react with lithium surfaces
so it may not be a desirable alternative, but could be used for tin and gallium.

It is recommended that good vacuum cleaning and handling practices always be followed. Bake-outs are
recommended to keep partial pressure of water vapor to a minimum. It is recommended that all vacuum
hardware be oil-less for vacuum cleanliness. If roughing or fore-pumps cannot be oil-less, Krytox or a
similar inert pump fluid should be used [7].

7.3 VACUUM TRANSDUCERS

Vacuum transducers like TC tubes, convectrons, capacitance manometers, cold cathode tubes and residual
gas analyzers (RGAs) may begin to read erroneously due to the film deposition on the electrodes or
collectors. These items will require immediate cleaning or refurbishment.

 24

Suggested Mitigations Line-of-sight between the LM target and the electrodes or collectors of vacuum
transducers should be blocked whenever possible, especially on more expensive transducers like RGAs
(M-VTD-1) This can be done with custom shutters, but such shutters may be several times more
expensive than the transducers themselves (such as in the case of convectrons). There must be a supply of
identical, backup transducers on site so that any failed transducers can be quickly replaced while the
failed unit is cleaned or refurbished (M-VTD-2).

If any transducers are not necessary for a particular LM experiment, those transducers should be removed
for the duration of the experiment so they are not needlessly impaired by film deposition (M-VTD-3).

7.4 PMI CHAMBER

The PMI chamber contains the LM target, so it will have the highest concentration of lithium and the
fastest rate of lithium deposition on the walls. It is also the only region of MPEX that has risk of contact
with liquid lithium, as other regions of MPEX will only have exposure to lithium vapor and lithium ions.

7.4.1    Target

The MPEX target payload design for solid, 1cm diameter targets features numerous Glidcop Al-15
components [43], so it is likely that custom target payloads will as well. Figure 7.4 shows a CAD drawing
of the target payload design. The actively cooled Glidcop (ACG) is what the target mounts to.
Additionally, the current target payload outer tube is Glidcop. Within the outer tube is a cooling channel
with water. Although this section of the target payload is behind the target face, it is reasonable to assume
some lithium vapor will be present behind the target.

This target payload could have liquid lithium seep through cracks and interact with the copper heat sink,
corroding it and breaching the cooling channels. A violent chemical reaction would occur between the
liquid lithium and water. Additionally, the water would force its way through the path the lithium carved,
enter the backside of the target, react with the bulk of the liquid lithium, and forcibly expel it outward into
the PMI chamber. Large quantities of lithium could land on at-risk components such as windows or
gaskets, causing a breach of vacuum. This is a worst-case scenario, but it emphasizes the importance of
safe LM target payload design.

Suggested Mitigations During LM campaigns, user groups will be expected to bring their own target
design. User groups may also be required to bring their own target payload,

which would enable a custom mounting method for the LM target, larger reservoir designs, and modified
cooling. Alternatively, a dedicated LM target payload could be designed that is used by most LM user
groups and reduces their cost of using MPEX. Either way, a LM compatible target payload must be
designed and used, as the current MPEX target payload is not LM compatible (M-TAR-1). MPEX staff

materials should be exposed on the target payload (M-TAR-2). A potential solution is to coat the exposed
copper on the outer body and outer tube with tungsten, 316 stainless steel, or another lithium-compatible
material.

Water should not be used as a coolant for the target system [7] (M-TAR-3). The coolant should change to

temperatures. Therefore, the operating power or plasma flux will need to be reduced accordingly.

identify the operations parameter space consistent with MPEX experimental requirements. The loop will
require its own PLC, flow interlocks, pump, filters, flow meter, thermocouples and pressure transducers
on both primary and secondary side to monitor operations and interface with the MPEX control system

An operational mitigation regarding the target temperature is mentioned in Section 13 of the MPEX
Conceptual Design Report: ”One requirement is to ensure that all alkali metal target temperatures are
below 80 ◦C before venting. The target cooling system should be operational until the [argon] vent is
successful” (M-TAR-4).

7.4.2 Target Dump

The target dump is a plate designed to shield the target from the plasma until the desired plasma
parameters are reached. It will be located in the PMI chamber in front of the target. The target dump plate
will not be installed on MPEX initially, but will be in future years.

The target dump plate is made up of three explosion-bonded metals. The front, plasma interacting layer is
TZM; the middle layer is CuCrZr; the back layer is 316 Stainless Steel (SS316). TZM is an alloy mostly
composed of molybdenum (>99%) with small additions of zirconium and tungsten. TZM and
molybdenum are compatible with liquid lithium [44]. CuCrZr is a precipitation hardened alloy that is
mostly composed of copper and has small additions of chromium (Cr) and zirconium (Zr). CuCrZr is a
material of concern during a liquid lithium campaign due to the copper content. It is the middle slab of
material in the plate so the primary faces are not exposed, but CuCrZr is exposed on the four side faces.
The plate is water-cooled, and a stainless steel hose connects the water source to the cooling channels in
the plate. A SS316 post holds the plate up.

The target dump plate leans at a 60 degree angle away from the incident plasma. This increases the
surface area of exposed CuCrZr on the top of the plate visible from the LM target. The exposed CuCrZr
on the bottom of the plate is not visible from the target due to the plate’s angle. The left and right sides

 26

are not directly visible from the target either. So, the face of primary concern is the top face of the dump
plate. Metal vapor and small, liquid metal droplets jettisoned from the LM target will likely hit this top
face and corrode it. However, because the dump plate is in such close proximity to the target, there will
likely be an appreciable amount of exposure to metal vapor experienced by all four sides. This exposure
and resulting corrosion could breach the internal cooling lines, lead to reactions between the water and
lithium, and cause water to leak into the PMI chamber.

Because the target dump interacts directly with the plasma, it will experience a greater heat flux than most
components in MPEX. Thermal analysis has not yet been conducted for the target dump, but it should be
assumed that the temperature of the CuCrZr is high enough that serious corrosion can occur with lithium
vapor.

Suggested Mitigations For a liquid lithium campaign, the target dump plate should not have exposed
surfaces with copper-containing materials. The four exposed CuCrZr faces should be coated in or
concealed by a lithium-compatible material like SS316 so that exposure does not occur (M-TDP-1).

Additionally, using water to cool the target dump plate should be avoided unless there are no possible
ways for water to leak from the target dump plate or the supply hose (M-TDP2). The alternative coolant
that is used for the target should be used for the target dump plate.

These two constraints demand that a different target dump plate is designed for liquid lithium campaigns
(M-TDP-3). All exposed faces of this target dump must be lithium-compatible and must use an inert
coolant. These changes will significantly reduce the cooling capabilities of the target dump, which places
limits on the heat flux that can be applied to the target dump. Calculations and simulations must be run to
determine these limits and to find a design that minimally limits the operating conditions of the plasma.

7.4.3 Copper Periscopes

The copper periscopes are mounted to the MPEX vacuum vessel and allow visual and infrared diagnostics
to look inside of MPEX. It is assumed here that the visual and infrared diagnostics utilize the same
periscope designs. These periscopes are installed in multiple locations throughout MPEX: both diagnostic
chambers in the helicon region, the ICH region, and the PMI chamber. The periscopes in the PMI
chamber are of primary concern due to their close proximity to the LM target.

The periscope body, bottom, and mirror are C11000 copper, although the mirror material can be changed.
The periscope body is actively water-cooled. Two different periscope designs will be used: a through-
bore periscope and a blind periscope. The designs are similar from a metal vapor perspective. The
through-bore design is displayed in Figure 7.5.

 27

 Figure 7.5: CAD drawing of the through-bore periscope design [45]. A large surface area of copper is

Because copper is incompatible with lithium, these periscopes are at serious risk of damage by lithium
vapor in the PMI chamber, where the lithium flux will be highest. Lithium vapor could corrode the
periscope body, causing structural damage and possibly breaching the water cooling channels. If the
cooling channels were breached by lithium corrosion, gallons of water could leak out of the
periscopes and interact with lithium vapor and even the liquid lithium target. This potential breach of
cooling lines is an extreme safety hazard and must be avoided.

Of additional concern are the periscope’s mirrors, which have been tentatively selected as copper. The
polish of these mirrors may quickly deteriorate as lithium vapor corrodes the surface. However, even
if the mirrors are corrosion-resistant, the deposition of metal vapor on the mirrors will change their
surface properties, which have strict constraints.

The diagnostic chamber periscope (upstream IR camera periscope) and the ICH drift pipe periscope
are expected to sustain the highest heat loads, so thermal analysis was carried out for these periscopes.
The PMI chamber periscopes will likely have lower surface temperatures because of a lower heat
load. However, this reduced surface temperature does not necessarily mean that the periscopes in the
PMI chamber are the least at risk from damage by lithium. The much greater quantity of lithium vapor
in the PMI chamber will likely still make corrosion the most severe for the periscopes in this region.
Thermal calculations for both periscope designs are given in [45]. The blind-tube periscope has lower
peak and average temperatures than the through-bore design. The blind-tube’s peak temperature is
56.4°C; the location of this maxima is the mirror surface. For the through-bore design, the maximum
temperature of the copper periscope is 68.9°C. This peak temperature is also on the surface of the
mirror. These results are encouraging, as these temperatures are very low compared to the 200◦C
threshold temperature for copper dissolution in lithium and far below the temperature at which lithium
vapor has been shown to corrode copper (425°C) in [17]. However, as was previously mentioned, a
low surface temperature may not be enough to protect against corrosion if the lithium vapor is hot.
Materials testing is necessary to better understand the dependence of corrosion on vapor and surface
temperature.

7.4.3.1 Suggested Mitigations

Fortunately, the material used for the mirrors does not need to be copper. Mo, W, or stainless steel
(SS) can replace the copper as the mirror material. Unlike copper, Mo, W, and SS are lithium
compatible. Therefore, the copper mirrors should be replaced with a Mo, W, or SS mirror during a
liquid lithium campaign (M-PER-1). This eliminates the corrosion concern, but metal vapor
deposition will be an issue. To better understand the effect thin depositions have on mirror
characteristics, vapor exposure tests could be conducted with mirrors of the required finish, and their
properties could be tested for a variety of lithium film thicknesses. This would allow MPEX staff to
better understand when the mirrors will become unreliable during a LM campaign.

As for the periscope body, the exposed material should not be copper during a liquid lithium
campaign. It should be anticipated that serious corrosion will occur by the hot lithium vapor from the
target, and the high pressure of the lithium vapor in the PMI chamber worsens this issue. As
mentioned in previously, the vapor pressure of lithium in the PMI chamber will likely exceed 1 Torr.
A compatible material such as SS, W, or TZM should be used for the periscope body so lithium
corrosion and the breach of cooling lines is not a concern (MPER-2). If a compatible material cannot
be found with the required characteristics, the periscopes will need to be replaced regularly during a
LM campaign, assuming the corrosion is not so severe as to breach cooling lines. Replacement
periscopes should be available in case any periscopes are damaged or disabled due to excessive film
deposition (M-PER-3).

It is strongly recommended to perform lithium vapor exposure tests with C11000 copper to
understand the corrosion rate. If the corrosion is limited to surface interactions, using copper as the
body material will likely be safe as long as the periscopes are replaced with some regularity.
However, if the corrosion penetrates farther into the sample, the risk of a cooling line breach is high,
and copper must be ruled out as a periscope material for LM campaigns.

Additionally, as was stated for the target payload and the target dump plate, an alternative coolant to
water must be used for the periscopes if there is any chance of water leaking from the periscopes. This
would be an expensive change and would likely require the periscopes to be redesigned.

A major redesign of the periscopes and all optic diagnostics could be done to simultaneously address
the concerns of vapor deposition and water cooling. A new liquid metal laboratory called the LiMeS
Lab is being planned for construction at DIFFER, the facility that operates the Magnum-PSI device,
and it will have liquid metal vapor protected diagnostics [46]. Diagnostics will utilize mirrors,
apertures, and shutters to prevent film deposition on windows. Design details have not been
published, but scientists and engineers involved with the LiMeS Lab will have expertise on protecting
diagnostics from the hazards of liquid metal and liquid lithium. One way that the LiMeS Lab will
protect diagnostics is by utilizing multiple mirrors for optical diagnostics. A first mirror will have a
line of sight to the plasma or target, and film deposition will occur on its surface. First mirrors will be
placed inside an elbow attached to flange on the main chamber rather than inside the main chamber
like a periscope would be. Since the first mirror would be further from the plasma than a periscope, its
surface temperature would be lower and water cooling might not be necessary. A second mirror is
used to allow the diagnostic window to be far removed from the line of sight to the target or plasma. If
the first mirror is hot and has metal vapor deposited on it, it can emit some of that vapor and become a
source of deposition. By using a second mirror that has less metal vapor deposited on it and will be
colder, deposition from the mirror to the window will be reduced or eliminated. If a two mirror system
was used with diagnostics in MPEX, the concerns faced by the periscope would be greatly mitigated
(M-PER-4).

7.4.4 IR and Visible Light Camera Windows

Both the IR and visible light camera windows face similar challenges from LM campaigns. These
windows are located in multiple locations in MPEX, but the windows in the PMI chamber are of
primary concern because of the increased quantity of lithium vapor present in that region. Staff from
Magnum-PSI have emphasized that optical diagnostics face some of the largest challenges out of all
components in a LM campaign. If the appropriate measures are not taken, they will degrade very
quickly. Fortunately, the windows at Magnum-PSI have not been damaged by lithium, only coated.
Regular glass, fused silica, and calcium fluoride windows have been used on Magnum-PSI.
Multiple window materials are being considered for optical diagnostics in MPEX. CVD diamond
windows will be used for the viewports of the IR cameras, which are behind the water-cooled copper
periscopes with metal mirrors. As mentioned in Section 5.4.3.6, the compatibility between lithium and
CVD diamond is unclear. If the two are incompatible, a thin reaction layer on the CVD diamond
window and discoloration if the window could occur.

Visible cameras will use either DUV sapphire windows or UV fused silica windows, although it is
likely that the DUV sapphire windows will be exclusively used. The DUV sapphire windows will
have >82% transmission in the visible range. The UV fused silica windows will have >94%
transmission in the visible range. As mentioned in Window Materials, lithium is not compatible with
sapphire, so using these windows should be avoided. A reaction layer and discoloration could develop
on the surface of the windows, which would disable diagnostics that use the windows. The use of
fused silica windows, which have been used in previous lithium experiments without issue, is likely
acceptable as long as the surface temperature does not become so high that lithium vapor reacts with
the surface.

Regardless of lithium’s chemical compatibility with the windows, lithium vapor will deposit on the
windows. The deposited film will affect the window’s transmissivity and reflectivity, negatively
impacting the signals obtained by the IR cameras. The deposited film will need to be cleaned off the
windows if diagnostics are to continue functioning as intended. Liquid metals with higher vapor
pressures will have higher deposition rates on the windows, requiring more frequent cleaning. Lithium
has the highest vapor pressure of the metals being considered for a LM target, and when paired with
its reactivity with the window materials, it demands the windows to be cleaned most frequently during
liquid lithium campaigns.

7.4.4.1 Suggested Mitigations

It is recommended to perform lithium vapor exposure tests for the window materials that have not
been used without issue in existing lithium experiments. However, even windows like fused silica
could benefit from further testing. After exposure to lithium vapor at some temperature for a particular
amount of time, the transmissivity and reflectivity of the windows can be tested, and visual changes
such as discoloration can be noted. This will allow diagnosticians to better anticipate the changes that
windows will undergo during a liquid lithium campaign. It may also provide a timescale for
unacceptable changes due to lithium exposure, which would inform a cleaning plan for a LM
campaign.

Despite the literature which suggests lithium will react with the windows, no corrosion of windows
was observed in Magnum-PSI after any liquid lithium campaigns. One reason for this may be that
Magnum-PSI avoids direct line-of-sight from the LM target to the windows. Staff at Magnum-PSI
have emphasized that lithium transport is largely, if not almost entirely, line-of-sight, and have said
that avoiding line-of-sight from the LM target to windows is very effective, especially when
operational pressure is low. For higher operational pressures, metal vapor is less likely to travel in a

 30

straight line due to collisions, so transport could extend beyond line-of-sight. It is strongly
recommended to follow this advice whenever possible in MPEX. If there is a line of sight from the
target to a window, thin shields could be installed to block it (M-WIN-1). On LTX, shutters are used
on every window. Shutters and apertures might prove useful in MPEX to block deposition when
needed (M-WIN-2). However, if lithium is being deposited, the plasma beam is on, so diagnostics
need to be functioning and viewing the plasma or target. Briefly opening shutters periodically
throughout a plasma shot would allow the diagnostics to capture some data while also limiting the
amount of metal vapor deposited on the window. This would greatly extend the lifetime of windows.
Another option to reduce line-of-sight is by giving the windows a recess so they are somewhat
removed from the line of sight of the target (M-WIN-3).
Mirrors are used with the IR cameras in Magnum-PSI to avoid direct lines of sight from the LM target
to viewports. Mirrors introduce challenges of their own, however, such as the concern of deposition
affecting surface finish. Aluminum mirrors have been successfully used in Magnum-PSI and were
placed quite far from the target to decrease the deposition rate on the mirror’s surface. The double
mirror approach planned for use at the LiMeS Lab, which was described above in Section 7.4.3, is an
even better approach but is more expensive and requires more space. If transport simulations reveal
that lithium will deposit rapidly on certain key windows that need to remain open for long periods of
time, the double mirror approach should be implemented to eliminate the concern of deposition on the
window (M-WIN-4).

Another piece of advice from Magnum-PSI staff is to remove viewports for diagnostics not being
used during a LM campaign. This prevents unnecessary damage from being done and lessens the
cleaning and inspection that must occur after the conclusion of a LM campaign. So, viewports that are
not necessary during a LM campaign should be removed and replaced with blank flanges (M-WIN-
5).

A procedural change that can mitigate the issues faced by vapor deposition on the windows is to do
regular mid-campaign cleaning of the windows (M-WIN-6). In the week-long LM campaigns in
Magnum-PSI, some days the experiments are stopped early so the device can be vented and the
windows can be cleaned. This will likely be necessary in MPEX if the IR and visual diagnostics are to
continue functioning. Even with shielding, deposition will occur, and cleaning will be required.

7.4.5 Diagnostics below the target

Any diagnostics below the target are at risk of interacting with liquid metal droplets that fall from the
target.

7.4.5.1 Suggested Mitigations

No diagnostics should be placed directly below the target. A horizontal or angled alignment with the
target should be used for the Thomson scattering laser beam line so liquid metal droplets do not cause
diagnostic failure (M-DBT-1). If this cannot be done due to the design of the chamber or diagnostic,
the line of sight from the target to the diagnostic should be blocked with a shield or shutter so metal
droplets cannot hit the diagnostic (M-DBT-2). If a full shield also cannot be implemented due to
space constraints or thermal concerns, the most critical surfaces of the diagnostic (i.e. the laser
aperture) should be shielded from liquid metal droplets hitting them. If there are no thermal concerns,
a drip tray for capturing LM spillage and droplets should be installed beneath the target as long as no
diagnostic lines of sight are blocked by the tray (M-DBT-3).

7.5 GATE VALVES
Slightly upstream from the PMI chamber is a water-cooled gate valve. There is also a water-cooled
gate valve at the end of the post-target chamber. When open, a water-cooled copper hole may be
slightly exposed. Fortunately, small amounts of surface corrosion will not adversely affect the
operation of the gate valves.

The upstream gate valve is separated from the PMI chamber by a skimmer, which allows the PMI
chamber to have a pressure over an order of magnitude higher than the rest of MPEX. This means the
lithium vapor pressure at the gate valve will be lower than it is in the PMI chamber. Additionally, the
line of sight between the liquid metal target and the upstream gate valve is largely blocked by the
skimmer and by the plasma.

The gate valve attached to the post-target chamber is not in the line of sight of the target. Additionally,
the presence of a pumping duct before this gate valve will reduce the lithium neutral density near this
gate valve. It is expected that this gate valve will not be affected by lithium vapor, but transport results
should be used to verify this expectation.

7.5.1 Suggested Mitigations

A more thorough examination of the exposed copper on the gate valves needs to be conducted. Both
gate valves may be built with custom modifications, so vendors may find a way to conceal the copper
if it is expected to be exposed to lithium for a hazardous amount of time. Transport simulations will
give insight into the flux of lithium that will hit the gate valve’s copper surface. If the flux is high, the
gate valve must be designed such that lithium cannot corrode through the copper and cause a water
leak (M-GAV-1). Using an alternative coolant is not necessary since the gate valve is outside of the
PMI chamber.

7.6 BELLOWS

There are four bellows in MPEX with a plasma-facing TZM layer and Glidcop Al-15 underneath. The
TZM layer has rifled cuts that slightly expose the underlying Glidcop. Fortunately, the cuts are made
such that the Glidcop doesn’t have a direct view of the plasma. However, the Glidcop has an indirect
view where lithium ions or vapor could interact with the Glidcop after a series of collisions.
The first bellow is between the autocoupler next to the PMI chamber and the ICH launcher. Since this
bellow is closest to the lithium target, it has the highest risk of lithium exposure. The second bellow is
between the ICH launcher and the downstream microwave absorber; the third is between the upstream
microwave absorber and the helicon launcher; the fourth is between the helicon launcher and the
dump tank.

Thermal simulations in the [47] found the peak temperature of the Glidcop at steady state plasma
conditions to be 46.9◦C. This temperature is far below the 200◦C threshold temperature for copper
dissolution in lithium, but as was mentioned several times previously, lithium vapor or ion corrosion
of materials is not well understood. Materials testing is necessary to understand the threat lithium
vapor and ions pose to copper at a low surface temperature.

7.6.1 Suggested Mitigations

The bellows will be incredibly difficult to replace if they are damaged, so they must not be damaged
by lithium. If corrosion tests show that copper at a temperature below 50◦C is undamaged by lithium
vapor/ions, then the bellows are compatible and will not be damaged during a liquid metal campaign.

 32

If corrosion tests do show that the copper is damaged or even develops a reaction layer on the surface,
further evaluation is necessary. A small reaction layer on the surface does not pose a threat to the
structural safety of MPEX, but it could affect the frictional contact between the components of the
bellows that are supposed to slide as they expand and contract. The upper limit for the amount of
surface corrosion that can occur while the bellows are still fully functional should be determined.
Lithium transport simulations should be used to determine the flux of lithium vapor and ions to the
bellows. If vapor corrosion tests reveal a maximum fluence of lithium where surface changes to
Glidcop do not occur, the time to achieve that fluence in MPEX can be calculated using the flux from
the simulations. If this time is significantly longer than the time that a liquid lithium target will be
struck by the plasma over the course of a campaign, then the bellows may not be damaged during
liquid metal campaigns.

If simulations, vapor corrosion tests, and subsequent calculations reveal that the bellows may be
damaged by lithium, an operational mitigation must be made since a physical modification to the
bellows is not possible to make. The plasma conditions will be limited to those that reduce the lithium
flux to the bellows so damage is prevented (M-BEL-1). Transport simulations will provide insight
into which operating conditions sufficiently limit the lithium flux to the bellows. Still, there is some
risk of damage to the bellows if the simulation results are incorrect. The condition of the exposed
Glidcop surface of the bellows should be frequently assessed during liquid lithium target campaigns
(M-BEL-2).

7.7 ICH LAUNCHER

The at-risk components in the ICH launcher are the silicon nitride (SiN) ICH windows. These
windows are closer to the target than any other SiN windows in MPEX. The ICH windows are
approximately 3.5 m upstream of the target, and one skimmer is between them and the target. These
windows are plasma-facing, so if any lithium is transported to this region of MPEX, some will likely
make contact with the SiN surface. These windows are water-cooled, which pose an additional hazard
because of the chemical reaction between lithium and water. A sectioned CAD drawing of the ICH
launcher is shown in Figure 7.6.

Thermal simulations run at 310 kW coupled RF total power calculated the peak temperature of the
plasma-facing silicon nitride window to be 339°C [48]. According to [24], decomposition and
discoloration of silicon nitride can occur ”after a while” when submerged in liquid lithium at 250°C.
Since the temperature of the SiN window is higher than this reported temperature, reactions between
lithium vapor and the SiN surface are likely to occur. However, the severity of this reaction is unclear
because it is a vapor-surface reaction, and no literature on lithium vapor corrosion of SiN exists. It is
expected that a sufficient fluence of lithium vapor will lead to decomposition and discoloration of the
silicon nitride windows.

The ICH launcher is in a lower pressure region of MPEX compared to the PMI chamber.

 33

 Figure 7.6: A sectioned CAD drawing of the ICH launcher revealing the SiN window [48].

It is separated from the high neutral pressures of the PMI chamber via the downstream skimmer. This
means that the exposure to lithium vapor will be reduced in the ICH region. However, the ICH window is
the narrowest region in MPEX apart from the downstream skimmer, which puts the window very close to
the plasma. This proximity to the plasma increases the chance of lithium ion interaction with the silicon
nitride window and increases the lithium flux density to the window’s surface.

Another concern that is shared by all liquid metal targets is the effect that a thin metal film will have on
the ICH window. A conductive layer on the window’s surface could affect the coupling of RF power to
the plasma.

7.7.1 Suggested Mitigations

Lithium vapor exposure tests of SiN samples are necessary to understand the risk that lithium poses to the
ICH windows. The surface properties and corrosion depth or reaction layer thickness should be measured
for multiple lithium vapor fluences and for multiple surface and vapor temperatures. If the samples show
changes with moderate lithium vapor fluences, then the ICH windows will likely be damaged over time in
MPEX. If lithium corrodes through the SiN samples, then lithium could breach the cooling channels
behind the ICH windows, causing water to spill into MPEX. This is a worst-case scenario that must be
prevented.

The SiN windows cannot be replaced by another material, as the cost would be too high and replacement
would be difficult. If possible, coating the SiN windows with a more lithium compatible insulator like
AlN would reduce the risk of damage during a liquid lithium campaign (M-ICH-1). An easier mitigation
to make is limiting lithium campaigns to plasma scenarios that minimize upstream lithium transport and
using smaller lithium targets so less lithium is eroded into the chamber (M-ICH-2). Lithium transport
simulations should be run for a variety of plasma scenarios and ICH powers to understand which plasma
scenarios have acceptable upstream transport and which have problematic upstream transport. The lithium
sourcing from the target should be scanned in these simulations to determine an upper limit for the
amount of lithium that can be eroded before the flux to the ICH launcher becomes a concern. An
additional mitigation that can be made is to limit the ICH power so that the SiN surface temperatures are
lower (M-ICH-3). The maximum safe temperature of the SiN window can be informed by SiN lithium
vapor exposure tests.

 34

The ICH launcher is not compatible with liquid lithium targets and will therefore limit the incident plasma
heat flux on the target, the quantity of lithium in the target, and the plasma scenarios that can be used
during a liquid lithium campaign.

Regarding the conductive metal film on the surface, there is no physical change that can be made to the
window to prevent the formation of the conductive layer. Simulations of plasma heating could be run to
study the effect of a thin metal film on the ICH window. If it is found that a metal film will have a
detrimental effect on the ion cyclotron heating, the plasma scenarios must be limited so negligible metal
deposition on the ICH launcher occurs during a LM campaign (M-ICH-3. Frequent cleaning of the ICH
window might be necessary to keep the film from growing too thick M-ICH-4).

7.8 MICROWAVE ABSORBERS

The microwave absorbers feature AlN (aluminum nitride) absorbing tiles, and AlN is incompatible with
lithium at certain temperatures. Beneath the tiles are thin sheets of copper, molybdenum and a Glidcop
manifold explosion bonded to stainless steel. The copper and Glidcop components are also incompatible
with lithium. The microwave absorbers are on either side of the ECH launcher, which is upstream from
the ICH launcher and downstream from the helicon launcher. The downstream absorber is approximately
4.5 m from the target, while the upstream absorber is approximately 5.5 m from the target.

An cross-sectioned CAD model of a microwave absorber is shown in Figure 7.7 and a drawing of an
absorbing tile and the Cu-Mo-Cu subassembly is shown in Figure 7.8. Both the annulus and AlN tiles are
plasma-facing. Fortunately, the AlN tiles are radially distant from the plasma. The thin copper sheets are
beneath the tiles and are covered from the plasma. The Glidcop manifold is further beneath the tiles, so it
also does not face the plasma. Lithium vapor or ions cannot contact the copper or Glidcop unless the AlN
tiles crack or deform. The copper directly in contact with the AlN tiles could be corroded by lithium
vapor. However, the molybdenum sheet beneath the first copper layer is resistant to lithium corrosion and
would stop further penetration of the lithium. Therefore, the bottom copper sheet and the Glidcop are not
at risk of damage by lithium, and there is no potential for the cooling lines beneath the Glidcop to be
breached by lithium.

As was mentioned in Section 5.4.3.5, vapor corrosion testing of AlN has been performed, and a sample
exposed at 550◦C for 100 hours did not show signs of damage like samples exposed at 650◦C and greater
did [25]. The exposure time in this work was 100 hours, but the lithium vapor exposure time in MPEX
could be hundreds or thousands of hours after several campaigns. However, the samples in this work were
positioned centimeters away from the liquid lithium surface, so the flux of lithium vapor to the samples
was much greater than it will be to the microwave absorbers in MPEX. Therefore, it can be expected that
the AlN microwave absorbing tiles can be compatible with lithium vapor if the surface temperature is low
enough.

 35

 Figure 7.7: A graphical representation of a microwave absorber unit [49].

  Figure 7.8: Drawing of the configuration of an AlN tile and the Cu-Mo-Cu sub-assembly beneath it [49].

Thermal simulations found a peak AlN temperature of 222◦C during nominal operation and roughly
240◦C after a thermal shock [49]. Thermal profiles of the AlN tiles and copper sheet under nominal
conditions are displayed in Figure 7.9. These temperatures are far below 550◦C, so it is expected that the
microwave absorbers will not be damaged by lithium vapor or ions. However, lithium ions have
significantly more energy than the lithium neutrals in lithium vapor, and corrosion by these ions could be
more severe than by lithium vapor. Lithium ion corrosion tests could be conducted and compared to vapor
corrosion tests to understand the effects of ion energy on corrosion. These tests are significantly more
expensive and complicated to conduct than lithium vapor corrosion tests, however.

 36

 Figure 7.9: Thermal profiles of the AlN tiles and copper sheet under nominal plasma conditions [49].

If the AlN tiles were corroded by lithium vapor or ions, cracking and bending similar to that observed in
the AlN lithium vapor corrosion study [25] could occur. As an AlN tile bends away from the underlying
copper sheet, the thermal contact worsens and the temperature of the AlN tile would increase further,
leading to more severe cracking and bending. This feedback loop could occur until the AlN tile cracks
into pieces and is destroyed. The destruction of an AlN tile would expose the underlying copper sheet and
make it susceptible to corrosion. The maximum temperature of the copper predicted in thermal
simulations during nominal operation was 121.2◦C and under 140◦C after a thermal shock. These
temperatures are below the 200◦C threshold for copper dissolution in liquid lithium, but corrosion might
still occur due to energetic lithium vapor or ions. Vapor exposure tests of copper are necessary to
understand the corrosion risk at this surface temperature. However, as was mentioned above, the
molybdenum sheet underneath the copper would prevent corrosion beyond the copper sheet.

As insulators, the AlN absorber tiles may be affected by metal film growth like the ICH window. A metal
layer on these tiles will affect microwave absorption and could shift the stray microwave power
distribution in MPEX.

7.8.1 Suggested Mitigations

No mitigations will be necessary if the temperature of the microwave absorbers remains well below
550◦C. If the temperature approaches or exceeds 550◦C, damage to the microwave absorbers could occur.
Lithium transport simulations for various plasma scenarios will estimate the flux of lithium to the
microwave absorbers, and if the flux is low enough, notable damage by lithium will not occur.

If the flux to the microwave absorbers is found to be problematically high and the surface temperature
exceeds 550◦C, there are two mitigations that can be made. The first is restricting the plasma scenarios to
those that reduce the flux of lithium to the microwave absorbers (M-MWA-1). Lower plasma heat fluxes
incident on the target will help reduce the erosion flux of lithium and therefore the flux of lithium to the
microwave absorbers. The second mitigation requires physical modification of the microwave absorbers.
The microwave absorbers could potentially be coated in a compatible material like those mentioned in
Section 5.4.3.5 (M-MWA-2). This modification would be expensive and require reassembly of the ECH
region, so it is unlikely to be performed.

 37

Regarding metal film growth on the absorbing tiles, the same mitigations suggested for the ICH window
are suggested here. Simulations should be performed to understand how metal films impact microwave
absorption. If thin films are found to be problematic, plasma scenarios will need to be limited to those that
minimize film deposition on the microwave absorbers (M-MWA-3). Additionally, frequent cleaning of
the absorbing tiles may be necessary to prevent thick films (M-MWA-4).

7.9 ECH LAUNCHER

The interior of the ECH is entirely CuCrZr, which is incompatible with lithium due to its copper content.
The launcher is over 4.75 m from the target, and one skimmer is between it and the target. Figure 7.10
shows the location of the ECH launcher in MPEX. Because the ECH is so far from the LM target, the
components in this region are at a much lower risk of damage than those in the PMI chamber. In
Magnum-PSI, the plasma source has exposed copper, but damage by lithium is not a concern because
upstream transport is not expected due to the opposing pressure gradient. It is possible that this may also
be true for MPEX; transport simulations will reveal the extent of lithium transport upstream to the ECH
region.

The main interior ECH components are the downstream spool, the launcher wall, the mirrors, and the
upstream spool. Figure 7.11 shows an annotated cross-section of the ECH launcher body. The surfaces of
these components are all plasma-facing. Fortunately, the launcher wall is fairly radially distant from the
plasma.

Thermal simulations run at the most limiting operation condition (105 GHz, 400 kW, and 5 GPM cooling
water flow rate) calculated the hottest CuCrZr component to be the TL (transmission line) adapter with a
surface temperature of 179°C [50]. Figure 7.12 shows the temperature distributions of the ECH
components under this operating condition. This temperature is extremely close to lithium’s melting point
of 180.5◦C. Corrosion of the CuCrZr might occur at this temperature since the lithium vapor or lithium
ions will be at an even higher temperature than the surface.

 upstream from the PMI chamber.

 Figure 7.11: Cross-sectioned, colored, and annotated CAD model of the ECH launcher [50]. All internal

The second hottest surface is the surface of the smaller mirror, mirror 2. Its surface temperature reaches
135◦C under the operation condition described above. This temperature is significantly lower than that of
the TL adapter, so less corrosion will happen to this component. However, if energetic vapor or ions are
able to even partially corrode the surface of mirror 2 and change its surface properties or finish, the
effects on ECH heating could be drastic. This mirror’s surface properties must not change during a
lithium campaign.

The mirror’s surface properties will likely be changed if any metal, not just lithium, deposits on the
surface and develops a film over time. This issue is shared by all LM targets.

7.9.1 Suggested Mitigations

CuCrZr lithium vapor exposure tests are necessary to understand how severe corrosion will be at this
surface temperature. Lithium ion exposure tests would also be useful and relevant, since the ionization
fraction in the ECH region will likely be very high. Until these tests are conducted, it should be assumed
that the hot CuCrZr components (TL adapter and mirror 2) will be partially corroded by lithium vapor or
ions if they hit these surface.

Lithium transport simulations will reveal what flux of lithium is incident on the hot CuCrZr surfaces. If
this flux is low enough such that there will be a negligible effect on the surfaces, no modification to the
ECH launcher must be made for lithium campaigns. Calculations should be done to find what the
maximum permissible fluence of lithium is based on vapor and ion exposure tests.

If transport simulations and vapor exposure tests reveal that the ECH launcher will be damaged by
lithium, three mitigations can be made. The first is limiting the plasma operating scenarios to those that
minimize upstream lithium transport (M-ECH-1). The second is coating at-risk CuCrZr surfaces in a
lithium compatible material like Mo, TZM, or W (MECH-2). This modification would be expensive and
require reassembly of part or all of the ECH region, so it is unattractive for these reasons. If the decision
to coat at-risk surfaces in the ECH is made, vapor exposure tests of coated samples should be performed
to ensure that the selected coating is effective at preventing lithium corrosion. The third mitigation is to
limit the ECH power so the temperature of CuCrZr surfaces remains lower and reduces the risk of
corrosion by lithium (M-ECH-3).

 40

To prevent problematic film deposition on the ECH mirror surface, the plasma scenarios may need to be
limited to those that minimize the flux of metal vapor to the ECH mirror (M-ECH-4). Additionally,
cleaning of the ECH mirror may be necessary if the metal film affects ECH operation (M-ECH-5).

7.10 UPSTREAM SKIMMER

One side of the upstream skimmer has exposed copper, but it faces upstream rather than toward the PMI
chamber. This skimmer is 7 m upstream of the target. Because of how far the skimmer is from the PMI
chamber and that the exposed copper side faces upstream, it is unlikely that damage to the copper surface
will occur. The only mechanism for damage is if some lithium ions in the upstream plasma neutralize at
the plasma’s edge and collide with the copper surface.

7.10.1 Suggested Mitigations

If transport simulations reveal the lithium density in the vicinity of the upstream skimmer is moderate or
that the flux to the region around the upstream skimmer is high, the copper side of the upstream skimmer
should be coated in a lithium compatible material (M-USK-1). Such coatings could be SS, TZM, Mo, or
W. It is unlikely that a coating will be necessary.

7.11 HELICON LAUNCHER

Like the ICH windows, the helicon window is silicon nitride. The helicon window is over 7.8 m from the
target, and two skimmers are between it and the target. Figure 7.13 shows the location of the helicon
launcher in MPEX. The helicon window interacts directly with the plasma and is water-cooled. Figure
7.14 shows an annotated model of the helicon launcher.

Thermal simulations assuming 175 kW coupled RF power calculated the peak temperature of the silicon
nitride window to be 245◦C [51]. A fairly large spot near the center of the window’s inner surface has a
temperature near this peak temperature, as shown in Figure 7.15. The rim of the window at the cooling
outlet side also has a temperature near this peak temperature. Most of the rest of the window’s inner
surface has a temperature below 160◦C, and most of the window’s outer surface has a temperature below
100◦C. The two hot spots on the window where the temperature is close to 245◦C has a temperature in the
vicinity of the 250◦C temperature at which discoloration and degradation of SiN occurred in liquid
lithium [24]. The helicon window does interact directly with the plasma, so if any lithium is present in the
plasma in the helicon region, lithium will interact with the SiN window.

 upstream from the PMI chamber [51].

Therefore, these two hot spots on the window are likely to be discolored or even corroded by lithium
vapor or ions if lithium is present in the helicon region.

If corrosion of the window is severe, the window could be breached and cooling water from the annular

upstream from the liquid lithium target in the PMI chamber, interaction between water and liquid lithium
is unlikely. However, the partial pressure of water vapor would become significant and would reach the
PMI chamber, potentially causing the liquid lithium to light on fire.

As was mentioned for the ICH launcher, metal film deposition on the helicon window could impair the
power deposition into the plasma. This is a challenge associated with all LM targets, not just lithium
targets.

how SiN can be corroded and affected in other ways by lithium vapor. Since plasma interacts directly
with the helicon window, tests that involve exposing SiN samples to a lithium plasma would be most
useful for understanding how SiN is affected by lithium in the helicon region. The temperature of the SiN
samples should be monitored and varied to determine a temperature threshold for damage to SiN by a
plasma containing lithium. Such an experiment would be complex and costly.

7.12 UPSTREAM DUMP PLATE

The upstream dump plate is the furthest upstream component in MPEX and directly interacts with the
plasma. It is roughly 10.9 m from the target. The dump plate consists of a 5x5 array of TZM tiles that are
brazed onto a Glidcop Al-15 flange, as shown in Figure 7.16. TZM and lithium have acceptable
compatibility, so the concern lies with the Glidcop Al-15 flange.

Thermal analysis of the dump plate was performed in [52]. The peak temperature of the Glidcop flange is
132°C, and the location of this temperature maxima is behind the TZM tiles. This hot region is not
exposed to the lithium, so there is no concern of corrosion at this location. The exposed Glidcop, radially
outward from the array of TZM tiles, has a peak surface temperature of 32°C. This is a very low
temperature in relation to existing lithium-copper corrosion literature. However, the temperature of the
reaction is not solely defined by the surface temperature, as the lithium ion temperature may be high
enough to enable corrosion.

Almost all of the plasma flux is concentrated on the TZM tiles, so very few lithium ions are expected to
interact with the Glidcop flange. The interactions that do occur are not expected to be problematic, as the
Glidcop flange is sufficiently thick to prevent corrosion that breaches vacuum. Corrosion is likely to be
limited to the surface, and surface corrosion of the Glidcop flange poses no major issues.

7.12.1 Suggested Mitigations

Although it is expected that no significant amount of damage by lithium will be done to the upstream
dump, the flux of lithium to the upstream dump should be found using transport simulations. The flux of
stray ions or lithium neutrals to the Glidcop flange is the only flux of interest. This flux can be used to
determine a deposition or corrosion rate on the upstream dump’s Glidcop flange. If the corrosion rate is
problematically high, some mitigation must be made. Coating the Glidcop is not feasible because it is
such a large surface and doing so would be very expensive. Another mitigation would be to expand the
area of TZM tiles on the Glidcop flange, but this would also be expensive (M-UDP-1). The cheapest and
most realistic mitigation is to limit the plasma scenarios to those that reduce the upstream transport of
lithium and bring the flux of lithium to the Glidcop flange to a low, acceptable level (M-UDP-2).

8.13 LITHIUM VAPOR EXPOSURE TESTS

As was mentioned several times in Section 7, lithium vapor exposure tests are critical for understanding
the risk that lithium vapor poses to the various materials in MPEX that are known to be incompatible with
liquid lithium. The tests listed below can be conducted with a relatively simple and comparatively
inexpensive experimental setup. A small, sealed vessel can contain the lithium and the sample. Lithium
can be melted in a heated crucible set inside the vessel, and the temperature of the crucible can be
monitored with a thermocouple (TC). A heated stage on the top of the inside of the vessel should be
installed. The samples can be mounted to the heated stage, and the temperature of the samples should be
monitored with a TC. The temperature of samples should vary across trials, and the range of temperatures
should capture the predicted surface temperatures of the corresponding at-risk components.

The flux of lithium vapor can be controlled by changing the temperature of the lithium in the crucible,
although this will change the vapor’s temperature and likely affect its reactivity. However, the flux can be
calculated from the measured temperature, so these effects can be decoupled. The exposure time can be
varied to understand the effect of vapor fluence on the severity of corrosion. The conditions of the inside
of the vessel will be different than MPEX in terms of pressure and background gases and impurities, so
the findings will not be completely applicable. However, the findings will provide a great deal of insight
into vapor corrosion and can be used to form worst-case estimates, as lithium corrosion is generally
enhanced by the presence of impurities.

transmissivity, film thickness, and window color should be measured as a function of vapor fluence.

 10. LM CAMPAIGN TEST PLAN AND OP REVIEWS

It is suggested that all test plans and operating procedures involving lithium or other alkali metals on
MPEX be evaluated by a Liquid Metal Safety Committee. The committee should consist of an MPEX
operator, PI(s), ES&H representative, building supervisor and a metallurgist or chemist cognizant of
alkali metal reactions, corrosion and liquid metal embrittlement (LME). The quantity and form of the
target should be considered, as well as sample prep and handling, setup, cleanup and waste disposal.
Lithium operations should be grouped and restricted to an annual campaign window to minimize cleanup
and inspection requirements [7]. The time allotted for LM campaigns will be determined between MPEX
staff and researchers seeking to test LM targets. For reference, LM campaigns in MagnumPSI generally
have lasted 1 week, where one research group tests their target design(s).

During a LM campaign, MPEX may need to be vented in order to clean windows and any coated
diagnostic surfaces such as mirrors. This has been done in Magnum-PSI, as metal vapor sufficiently coats
windows such that diagnostics do not function before the week-long campaign is over.

Following each LM campaign, the chamber is vented and cleaned. This process is described in-depth in
Section 15. While it is straightforward to remove most of the metal that has deposited in MPEX’s interior,

 49

it is nearly impossible to fully clean cracks and slight gaps that metal vapor deposited on. These trace
remains of metal are minuscule and have not caused any problems in Magnum-PSI. Still, it is preferable
to run LM campaigns in succession so that any leftover metal does not unexpectedly impact the
conditions of the following experiments. After the last LM campaign in an LM campaign window is
complete, a more extensive cleaning of the device may be beneficial as it would decrease the likelihood
that leftover deposited metal in the chamber would impact the following non-LM experiment(s).

Liquid lithium will be the LM most frequently tested in MPEX. In Magnum-PSI, a linear plasma device
at the Dutch Institute for Fundamental Energy Research, the majority of LM campaigns have been with
lithium. Staff at Magnum-PSI have commented that they try to avoid tin campaigns because it is hard to
clean.

 11. LM SAFETY, TRAINING, AND HANDLING

The content of this section is composed of excerpts from Section 13 of the MPEX Conceptual Design
Report [7]. The content has been rearranged and a few small changes have been made for clarity’s sake.

11.1 SAMPLE PREPARATION

Pure lithium metal samples should be research grade, 99.99% pure lithium. Battery grade, 99.9% pure,
lithium has high sodium content that can adversely impact spectroscopy and other diagnostics. More
impurities in the lithium tend to increase its reactivity. Sodium and potassium react more vigorously with
water than lithium, so many of the lithium precautions described here apply to them as well.

Title 49 of the Code of Federal Regulations (49CFR, Parts 173.124 and 172.101) governs transportation
of lithium in the United States. Individuals responsible for the transport of lithium may also want to
obtain copies of Handling Hazardous Materials (Keller and Assoc., 1999) and the Emergency Response
Guidebook (US Dept. of Transportation, 2000) for quick reference and interpretation of the codes.

Raw lithium should be transported in sealed, approved containers under an inert atmosphere.

Currently approved containers for bulk transportation include argon-purged, corrosion-resistant,
Department of Transportation (DOT) drums. In many transport plans, the lithium is bathed in mineral oil.
Sealed, argon-purged foil pouches may also be used, as long as the pouches, themselves, are then packed
in the aforementioned steel drums (for bulk shipments) or cans (for lesser quantities). Proper container
labels and vehicle placards (e.g. “dangerous when wet”) are required when transporting lithium (US Dept.
of Transportation, 1990; Keller and Assoc., 1999).

A load-locked glovebox similar to that shown in Figure 11.1 is preferred to handle lithium or other alkali
metals in an argon background. It should be equipped with 25-mil chemically resistant neoprene gloves
and have a stainless-steel secondary confinement tray at the bottom. The area around the sample
preparation glovebox should be well-ventilated. A desiccant can be used inside the glovebox to reduce
moisture. A continuous oxygen monitor is required inside the glovebox. A variety of alarming sensors are
commercially available in which the control electronics can be mounted outside the glovebox. Oxygen
levels below 10 ppm are required to keep fresh lithium surfaces from oxidizing while they are being
mounted and placed in the MPEX transfer case. It is recommended that the argon be supplied from a
liquid argon dewar with a constant flow exhaust and pressurized to slightly above 1 atm. If using an argon
dewar to supply the glovebox purge, proper training and adherence to cryogenic safety protocols are
required. Constant argon flow is required to sweep out moist air. The water vapor should be maintained
below 3 ppm in the presence of exposed lithium. Pure lithium metal not in original packaging should be
stored in sealed argon-filled metal cans. The glovebox is where the samples are removed from packaging

 50

such as argon-filled mylar bags or from mineral oil canisters, inspected, cleaned and weighed. Non-
sparking BeCu tools should be available for mounting the alkali metal targets onto the MPEX target
plates. A variety of clean tools such as tongs, pliers, scissors and shears should be available inside the
glovebox. Also useful will be an electronic scale, mylar bags and a heated bag sealer and some small
empty metal cans for removal of lithium, sodium or potassium from the glovebox. Pre and post-exposure
weighing is required. Type-D fire extinguishers (LITH-X preferred) should be available in the sample
preparation room and near the MPEX PMI chamber. For solid targets containing less than 3.4 moles of
alkali metal, no other modifications to the building or fire protection system are required.

Detailed operating procedures for the glovebox are required and personnel should be trained in proper
handling and mounting procedures. The argon is an asphyxiation hazard. Good room ventilation and
proper precautions must be followed.

Activated samples may require remote handling in the glovebox and provisions must be made to
incorporate the RH equipment in the glovebox and the procedures.

11.2 SAMPLE STORAGE REQUIREMENTS

Clean new lithium should be stored under vacuum, or if bagged, in a fireproof, metal chemical cabinet.
Water-proof polyethylene chests (“double drum waste collection center”) or cabinets as shown in Figure
11.2 also work well when used with metal drums. Reacted pieces of lithium should be collected and
stored in sealed metal cans until processed into the lab waste stream. All collected solidified lithium
debris should be weighed so operators can track the alkali metal inventory that might be adhered to the
internal walls of MPEX. Any debris that is not collected may result in dust transport in following
experiments and may contaminate other samples and remote locations in the MPEX vacuum system like
pumping ducts, traps and sieves.

A maximum inventory limit should be set for the facility. Initially, a value of 1 kg for all alkali metals is
appropriate. This can be adjusted as conditions warrant, but the preparation area should not become a
lithium storage area. A storage log should be maintained listing a description of new metal supplies,
source or origin, current location, and date of receipt as well as any quantities disposed of and removed
from the site.

11.3 TRAINING FOR LIQUID METAL HANDLING AND EXPOSURE

Site-specific training is required for all operations staff involved with liquid alkali metal experiments.
This includes fire protection and safety, proper use of PPE, spill prevention and cleanup, waste
management and MPEX configuration control. A literature review of alkali metal reactivities and
materials compatibility issues including chemical reactions and liquid metal embrittlement should be
required of all alkali metal workers. This is necessary to ensure that all workers are sensitized to the
hazards and serious consequences that can result from an uncontrolled or unintended event [7].

11.4 PPE AND EQUIPMENT FOR LITHIUM CLEANUP

Required PPE like heat resistant gloves, gauntlets, face shields, aprons and respirators should be
specified. The availability of oxygen sensors and cleaning solvents such as heavy alcohol like dodecanol
or an aqueous solution of acetic acid (vinegar) should be stipulated. Safe cleanup plans for reacting
lithium metal waste with water and subsequent disposal should be created, as well as provisions made for
the safe storage of alkali metals in a humidity controlled facility or glovebox [7].

11.5 FIRE SUPPRESSION – NFPA 484 REQUIREMENTS AND SIGNAGE

Alkali metals fall under the NFPA 484 Standard for Combustible Metals. These regulations define the fire
protection requirements of the test cell and related infrastructure. NFPA 704 stipulates the signage
required to assist first responders in identifying the hazards. It must be adhered to in any facility that
stores more than 2.3 kg of alkali metals. Elimination or separation of water sources, fire extinguishers,
signage and spill containment and cleanup are addressed. A typical sign for lithium appears in Figure 11.3
below.

11.6 TRAINING FOR LM FIRE SUPPRESSION

It may be necessary to train the ORNL fire department and the Laboratory Shift Supervisors and other
first responders in the fighting of metal fires. Live fire demonstrations with Handson type-D fire
extinguisher training will be required. Type A, B and C extinguishers should not be used. In addition,

 53

adequate firefighting PPE like self-contained breathing apparatus, face masks and heat resistant clothing
are needed to fight alkali metal fires. Other issues regarding radiological controls and the spread of
contamination also need to be addressed. Dense white clouds of caustic lithium oxide are formed when
lithium burns. If a lithium fire reaches large proportions, nothing can be done but to let it burn. In a sealed
room, such as a dry room, the supply of oxygen is quickly consumed in feeding a lithium fire. It is
important that all emergency fire and hazmat vehicles be equipped with the proper fire extinguishers. In
no case should water ever be used to fight a liquid metal fire. It will result in spreading the metal over a
larger area and provide more fuel for the water reaction. All liquid metal fires must be deprived of oxygen
by smothering them with a crust of lith-x, salt or finely divided graphite or copper powder. LM fires must
usually consume one of the reactants completely before they are safely extinguished.

 12. MONITORING

Because of the 3.45 mole limit of lithium in MPEX, the inventory of lithium in MPEX needs to be
diligently tracked. Additionally, the migration of lithium throughout MPEX should also be tracked to
understand how lithium travels upstream and to compare with transport simulations. This section details a
few methods to track the lithium inventory and understand lithium’s migration in MPEX.

12.1 MASS LOSS TRACKING

As was previously emphasized, the amount of lithium that can be present in MPEX at any given time
must not exceed 3.45 moles. This does not just apply to the amount of lithium held in a target, but the
total amount of lithium in MPEX, which includes both the lithium in the target and the lithium that has
accumulated in MPEX. To ensure the 3.45 mole limit is not exceeded, the mass of each lithium target
must be carefully tracked.

Precise tracking of the accumulated lithium in MPEX is difficult. It is straightforward to measure the
mass of a clean target empty of lithium and then measure it after lithium is loaded to find the mass of
lithium entering MPEX. However, multiple types of mass transfer can occur during plasma exposure in
MPEX, which makes it challenging to know exactly how much lithium was lost in MPEX. While lithium
will be lost through sputtering and evaporation and decrease the mass of the LM target, reaction products
like LiD, LiOH, or Li2CO3 will form on the LM target and slightly increase the mass. If the mass of
reaction products that form during a plasma shot are significant relative to the mass of lithium that is lost,
the amount of lithium estimated to have been lost during that shot will be underestimated. The issue of
reaction product masses being significant relative to the mass of lithium lost becomes more severe for
targets with a large lithium surface area to mass ratio.

In a study at PPPL, the mass gain of solid lithium samples with a 1 cm2 exposed surface area and a depth
ranging from 0.3 to 1.0 mm (mass of less than 54 mg) ranged from was less than 20 mg after 10 hours
and less than 60 mg after 70 hours [14]. The sample with the roughest surface gained the most mass due
to a greater total surface area. Two smooth-faced samples with different depths had the same mass gain.
These results suggest that the surface area is the determining factor for reaction product mass gain. So, a
rough estimate for mass gain of a lithium sample by reactions with air at 45% R.H.is 20 mg of per cm2 of
surface area within the first 10 hours of exposure. Note that the lithium samples in this study were of
99.90% purity by weight [14], but the lithium that should be used with MPEX should be of

99.99% purity.

A standard 12-mm-DIA (1.13 cm2) base frustrum of a cone MPEX target that is 1-mm-thick has a size
similar to those of the lithium samples tested in the PPPL work mentioned above. If these samples were
exposed to air at 45% R.H., the mass gain by reaction products would exceed 10% of the initial mass of

 54

lithium. Lithium samples will not be exposed to ambient air, but they will be exposed to a deuterium
plasma which will react with the lithium to form lithium deuteride (LiD). The mass gain from exposure to
deuterium will not be the same as the mass gain from exposure to air, but the conservative approach is to
assume the mass gain from deuterium exposure will be non-negligible for small samples. Simply
measuring the mass before and after plasma exposure will not give an accurate measurement of the
amount of lithium lost during the plasma shot for small samples. Chemical testing could be done to better
determine the amount of lithium left (both as pure metallic lithium and as a reaction product) after plasma
exposure. For larger samples, mass gain from reaction products may be negligible.

Regardless of if a simple mass difference is an accurate measurement of the lithium mass loss, all targets
should still be weighed before and after exposure, but the difference should not be assumed to be the mass
lost. In cases where the mass lost can be approximated by the difference in the before and after mass
measurements, it must be assumed that all mass loss is deposited on the MPEX chamber walls. A study of
the mass gain of lithium samples exposed to deuterium should be performed to understand when this
approximation is valid.

The total number of targets exposed during any one campaign should be limited so that the total mass loss
does not exceed the equivalent of 3.45 moles before an inspection and evaluation of chamber
contamination is performed. This should apply to tin and gallium as well as the alkali metals and may be
good practice for any melted target materials [7].

12.2 WITNESS PLATES

During a LM campaign, particularly a liquid lithium campaign, it will be important to understand the
migration of metal vapor in MPEX. There are many components with incompatible materials like copper
and SiN upstream in MPEX, so the amount of lithium migrating to the regions that feature these
components should be monitored.

Witness plates could play a useful role in monitoring the migration of lithium vapor throughout MPEX,
and witness plates will already be used to track the migration of irradiated material. The MPEX project
requirements mention this tracking of irradiated material: “the migration of irradiated material from the
target plate shall be monitored in the dump chamber, near the helicon antenna, near the ICH region, and
in the PMI chamber (both at the entrance to the chamber and near the target, e.g. with ‘witness plate’
detectors” [PR364-R].

A modification to the irradiated material witness plate design could be made to measure metal vapor
migration. These witness plates would allow for quicker and easier monitoring of metal vapor transport
than venting MPEX and analyzing the inside of the walls.

12.3 QUARTZ CRYSTAL MICROBALANCE

A quartz crystal microbalance (QCM) could also be used to track lithium deposition in different regions
of MPEX. QCMs are used in plasma processing to determine deposition rates. If there are no thermal
concerns, QCMs could be placed near at-risk surfaces such as mirrors and windows. QCMs provide an
electrical signal and do not need to be physically inspected. Live monitoring of deposition rates could
occur during a plasma shot.

12.4 LI I FILTERSCOPES

Filterscopes that only detect the Li I transition could be installed at various axial positions on MPEX if
ports are available. Detection of Li I emission would reveal the presence of lithium in the plasma at a

 55

given location. Further analysis to determine the density of lithium in the plasma using line ratios might
be possible. If the intensity of Li I emission becomes too high at an at-risk location, the plasma could be
turned off to prevent problematic levels of deposition or corrosion to at-risk surfaces.

 13. PREPARATION FOR THE FIRST LM CAMPAIGN

The first LM campaign in MPEX will be a learning process and may reveal issues that were not identified
in this document. This first campaign will be different than others as the focus will be on how LM targets
affect MPEX rather than how MPEX affects LM targets.

The first LM campaign could use non-lithium targets like tin or gallium to avoid the reactivity and
corrosion concerns of lithium while gaining an understanding of metal transport and deposition. However,
these metals are harder to clean off of surfaces, so using lithium for the first LM campaign is reasonable.
This section assumes the first LM campaign is a lithium campaign as lithium campaigns require the most
preparation.

13.1 MODIFICATIONS TO MPEX

The first step of a LM campaign is to understand the mitigations described in Section 8 and make the
necessary modifications. Note that if the LM campaign does not use any lithium, less mitigations need to
be made. Any changes or substitutions to vacuum hardware including such things as windows,
feedthroughs and gaskets must be reviewed by the Liquid Metal Safety Committee and documented. No
changes should be made without prior approval and documentation [7].

13.2 IDENTIFY ALL IGNITION SOURCES FOR EACH EXPERIMENT

All energy sources which may potentially ignite hydrogen that penetrate the vacuum boundary of MPEX
must be identified for each individual experiment. Every effort should be made to remove these ignition
sources when using alkali metals or isolate them from the hydrogen source. In the case of loss-of-vacuum,
for instance, these sources should be automatically de-energized or shuttered from the vacuum chamber.
As was mentioned above in M-VAC1, a vacuum vent and purge system should be installed on MPEX and
proper procedures for venting the system be defined in the standard operating procedures and enforced
via PLC.

13.3 PREPARATION OF SAMPLES

Targets should be prepared in an inert argon glovebox as described in Section 11.1.

13.4 INSTALLATION OF MONITORING EQUIPMENT

Witness plates, QCMs, Li I filterscopes, or other devices that monitor LM transport in MPEX must be
installed. These devices are described briefly in Section 12.

13.5 LEAK CHECKING

Any components newly installed on the MPEX vacuum vessel through a vacuum port must be leak
checked. The consequences of having a leak are significantly greater in a liquid lithium campaign than in
any other type of MPEX campaign. All leaks must be eliminated.

 56

13.6 PRE-CLEANING OF THE PMI CHAMBER

The inside of MPEX should be in good condition before a LM campaign, as the condition inside MPEX
will only worsen as a LM campaign progresses. Pre-campaign cleaning should be done, and particular
attention should be paid to surfaces in line-of-sight of the target, as this is where film deposition is
expected to be the most severe.

13.7 INERT INSTALLATION OF THE TARGET

The LM target must be transferred to the PMI chamber under an inert environment. Exposure to
atmospheric gas or any non-inert species must be minimized. The target exchange cart should be able to
keep the lithium under an inert atmosphere.

13.8 LOW POWER PLASMA CLEANING OF THE TARGET

Lithium begins to tarnish in a matter of seconds when exposed to atmospheric gases. If a lithium target is
exposed to atmospheric gases at any point between target preparation in a glovebox and the first plasma
shot, the target surface will have lithium products on it. This could affect the PMI with the lithium target,
as these products have higher melting points and lower vapor pressures than pure lithium metal.
Therefore, it is desirable to have a clean surface of lithium at the target before initiating the first plasma
shot.

In Magnum PSI, there is a period during loading where the lithium targets are exposed to atmospheric
gases. This is unavoidable due to the configuration of Magnum-PSI; it is not a desirable condition for a
lithium target to be subject to. The brief exposure to atmospheric gases led to the formation of a reaction
layer on the target’s surface. A low power He plasma has been used to clean this layer off of the lithium
target. However, this was during a He plasma exposure campaign, whereas most of the campaigns in
MPEX will use a deuterium plasma. A low power deuterium plasma cleaning may not clean the reaction
layer in the same way because deuterium is reactive with lithium.

With MPEX, a reaction layer will not form if the lithium target is kept in an inert environment during
loading. The target exchange cart should enable this, and then the lithium target will not need to be
cleaned.

To determine if the target surface is in need of cleaning, an IR camera can be used. The emissivity of the
lithium compounds in the reaction layer is much higher than that of pure lithium, so an oxidization layer
will appear bright to an IR camera. If the lithium surface appears dark to an IR camera, it is clean.

13.9 GRADUAL INCREASE OF HELICON AND AUXILIARY HEATING POWER

The first plasma shot on a lithium target should aim to raise the temperature of the lithium only slightly
above its melting point. The erosion flux will be significantly lower at a low liquid lithium temperature,
so film deposition and vapor corrosion will be far less severe. ECH and ICH should not be active during
the first few shots, as the introduction of more heating into the plasma will likely increase the erosion flux
from the LM target. All at-risk diagnostics should be closely monitored, and changes to performance
should be documented. The first plasma shots should be brief, and MPEX should be vented after only a
few shots to observe the condition of the interior and at-risk components. It is likely that no changes will
be visible, and in that case, more plasma shots can be run.

Once a low power, helicon-only plasma is used to melt the LM target without issue, the power can be
increased gradually. The state of diagnostics and components should be periodically checked as the power

 57

is increased and auxiliary heating is introduced. Much of the first LM campaign will be to develop an
understanding of where metal deposits and if any minor surface corrosion occurs.

 14. INSPECTION

Careful inspection of MPEX should occur after a LM campaign or at any point where the inventory of
alkali metal suggests that the wall contamination levels may be high or the failure of vital MPEX
diagnostics occurs. Inspection will mainly involve assessing the condition of the PMI chamber,
identifying surface films on diagnostics or windows, and noting any other contamination. The inspection
can be extended to the entire MPEX systems if warranted, but it is believed that surface films and
contamination will be mostly confined to the PMI chamber. Inspection will be especially important and
insightful during and after the first LM campaign, as the observations will help inform decisions made
about future LM campaigns.

Inspection will occur somewhat simultaneously with cleaning. Before beginning cleaning, pictures of in-
vessel components should be taken to document the extent of surface films that developed. The state of all
components should be noted before and after they are cleaned, at least for the first LM campaign. In the
case of lithium campaigns, cleaning should occur fairly quickly after venting MPEX, so the inspection
must be well-planned and done in a timely manner. The longer the interior of the chamber is exposed to
any atmospheric gases, the more difficult the deposited lithium will be to clean since lithium
oxide/hydroxide/nitride/carbide are not as easy to remove.

Recommended inspections are described in the following subsections.

14.1 CHECK SURFACES IN LINE-OF-SIGHT

Since metal vapor transport is primarily line-of-sight, all surfaces within line-of-sight of the LM target
should be carefully inspected. Locally higher concentrations of deposited metal should be noted. In the
case of lithium, inspection will occur after a nitrogen or atmospheric vent, so lithium products will form
with a white-gray color. This process is called passivation. This white-gray color will stand out against
the metal vessel walls, so it should not be difficult to spot lithium on the vessel walls.

14.2 CHECK ALL WINDOWS

The windows will be coated by metal vapor to some degree during a LM campaign, regardless of whether
that metal is lithium or not. After a liquid lithium campaign in Magnum-PSI, windows were significantly
coated and resulted in diagnostics not being able to function. Windows with any sign of an accumulated
coating should be cleaned or replaced. After a lithium campaign, it is possible that a reaction layer will
form on some windows. This layer might not be possible to clean and would necessitate replacement of
the affected windows.

14.3 CHECK ALL NEARBY COPPER COMPONENTS

Copper components, especially those in the PMI chamber, should be inspected thoroughly after a liquid
lithium campaign. Any signs of changes in surface roughness should be noted and affected components
should be flagged for a more thorough review. A component that has undergone surface corrosion does
not necessarily need to be replaced, as long as it is structurally sound and it continues to function as
intended. For example, the Glidcop flange of the upstream dump plate can undergo surface corrosion
without any concerns being raised because it is thick and its surface roughness does not matter. However,
surface corrosion of the ECH mirror is a problem since the mirror needs to have a particular surface
finish.

 58

For non-lithium campaigns, there is no concern of copper experiencing corrosion, so copper components
do not need to undergo a more thorough inspection.

14.4 CHECK FOR UPSTREAM DEPOSITION AND CORROSION

Although metal vapor transport is mostly limited to line-of-sight, metal ions will travel upstream, and
some may escape the plasma and hit components. Therefore, it is recommended to inspect the upstream
regions of MPEX for deposited metal. The only upstream concern for non-lithium metals is deposition on
sensitive components like mirrors and windows. For example, tin deposition on the ECH mirror could
affect the performance of the ECH launcher.

For lithium, upstream at-risk components should be inspected after the first campaign. The roughness of
SiN windows, AlN absorbing tiles, and any other at-risk surfaces should be documented. This inspection
will be more time-consuming than the inspection of the PMI chamber, since upstream components are
generally more difficult to access than those in the PMI chamber. However, it is necessary to gain an
early understanding of upstream lithium transport.

14.5 ANALYSIS OF WITNESS PLATES

Witness plates should be analyzed to quantify the amount of lithium deposited on them. Assuming steady
state conditions, an experimental axial lithium flux profile could be constructed with the data collected
from witness plates.

14.6 SPECTROSCOPY AT THE START OF THE NEXT CAMPAIGN

Staff at Magnum-PSI use optical emission spectroscopy to see if there is lithium present at the beginning
of their next non-lithium campaign. They use two systems: one looks at the emission from the target,
while another looks at the emission from the plasma source. It is recommended to do the same in MPEX,
as optical emission spectroscopy will provide valuable insight into how well the device was cleaned of
lithium.

 15. POST-CAMPAIGN CLEANING

MPEX must be thoroughly cleaned after the completion of a LM campaign, whether it used lithium or
not. However, the cleaning process is different for a lithium campaign versus LM campaigns without
lithium.

15.1 LITHIUM CAMPAIGN

After a liquid lithium campaign, the PMI chamber of MPEX will be thoroughly coated with a thin layer
of lithium. There may also be thin films of lithium deposited further upstream as well. Additionally, it is
possible that some lithium drops stick to the walls of the PMI chamber in the case of overfilling, droplet
expulsion, or an unexpected loss of lithium due to target failure.

There are a few different cleaning procedures that could be followed after the completion of a liquid
lithium campaign. In Magnum-PSI, the vessel is vented with nitrogen and flanges are opened for a day
following the completion of a liquid lithium campaign. Lithium reacts with the nitrogen to form lithium
nitride, although there are many different lithium compounds that form due to interactions with
atmospheric gases. After a day, the inside of the vessel is cleaned with wet cloths and acetic acid.
Cleaning lithium nitride with water forms ammonia, and the smell can be strong. Magnum-PSI staff have
said cleaning with wet cloths is highly effective, but there are cracks that are nearly impossible to clean

 59

lithium and lithium compounds out of. This cleaning procedure agrees with the recommendations made in
the MPEX CDR [7], which is specified in the following paragraphs.

Heavy alcohol or acetic acid is recommended for cleaning the target assembly after a lithium experiment.
Other exposed vacuum vessel surfaces should also be cleaned in MPEX using the same solvents. After all
the lithium has been removed the walls may be cleaned with deionized water and propanol before bake-
out and pump down. LiOH is a white powder; whereas lithium hydride and nitride are dark. Additionally,
after the end of a lithium campaign, careful inspection of the inside of the helicon window and other
alumina insulators should be performed. Any lithium deposits should be removed and the condition of the
internal surface documented. Yellowing or localized pitting will occur if any lithium has reacted with the
alumina.

Careful attention must be paid to solidified droplets and melt debris from the targets. This must be
detached or carefully scraped from the target stage and collected into a metal can. It should be weighed to
track the alkali metal loss. Debris may also be found on the bottom of the PMI chamber and this must be
carefully collected as well. Use only a non-explosive, all metal, HEPA vacuum cleaner to vacuum this
debris. The contents should be emptied to a metal container for storage and disposal. Fire may result from
humidity, so the debris should not be left unattended in the vacuum cleaner canister.

Deposited films may require some hardware disassembly and removal to adequately clean. It is important
to document the location, loss of calibration or degradation of diagnostics as they occur. The cleaning
procedure for vacuum diagnostics and their efficacy should also be documented for future reference.
Spare parts for diagnostics are required for immediate change-out as the contaminated units are cleaned
off-line. It is important to note changes in base pressure and bake-out behavior during operations in an
alkali metal target campaign in the MPEX logbook [7].

A different cleaning procedure was used in NSTX following a liquid lithium campaign in 2010.

A week-long vent with moist air was done before personnel entered the vessel for inspection. The lithium
on the interior walls reacted with the moist air, forming lithium hydroxide, which later reacted to form
inert lithium carbonate. This left a white coating over the entire interior of the vessel [53]. This is a
straightforward way to passivate any leftover lithium in a vessel. Due to MPEX having significantly less
volume than NSTX (make comparison) and using much less lithium (about 24 g versus 1.347 kg), a moist
air vent in MPEX would be on the scale of hours or one day rather than a week. If the vent time is directly
proportional to the amount of lithium mass loss, a vent time of 3 hours would be sufficient for MPEX.
After the leftover lithium is passivated, the white layer must be cleaned. The NSTX team did this by
washing the surface with a 5% acetic acid (vinegar) solution. This is necessary to break down the lithium
carbonate into a water-soluble form (lithium acetate). It is then recommended to wash the walls with
deionized water. In MPEX, a procedure this extensive may be unnecessary.

15.2 NON-LITHIUM CAMPAIGN

Tin and other non-lithium metals can be more difficult to clean than lithium because they do not dissolve
in water. In fact, tin campaigns are preferentially avoided in Magnum-PSI because of how difficult it is to
clean the chamber of tin after a campaign. Tin can be removed from surfaces by venting the chamber with
hydrogen gas. Hydrogen gas reacts with tin to form stannane (SnH4), a gaseous product that can be
pumped out. However, stannane can stick to surfaces, making it difficult to evacuate. Additionally,
venting with hydrogen poses a detonation risk, so there must be ignition sources present when venting
with hydrogen. Gallium’s melting point is very low at only 30◦C, so it can be removed from surfaces that
are warmed up.

 16. LARGE LM TARGETS WHICH FLOW ALKALI LIQUID METAL QUANTITIES > 3.45
 MOLES

The content of this section is taken directly from Section 13.3 of the MPEX Conceptual Design Report
[7]. A few changes have been made to make this document self-consistent; these changes are marked with
square brackets. It is unlikely that large flowing lithium targets will ever be implemented on MPEX
because of all the modifications and safety precautions that are necessary for such a system. Another
facility should be used if testing of a flowing lithium target is desired.

The conceptual design of MPEX and its associated building infrastructure presented in this report are not
compatible with flowing alkali metal experiments. Currently MPEX uses water cooling throughout and
alumina in feedthroughs and windows, O-ring seals and copper metal gaskets. Flowing tin is possible
with careful vacuum considerations, but reactive alkali metals will require significant modifications to
MPEX and the building to remove these heightened risks. Therefore, flowing alkali metal experiments are
not consistent with the current MPEX design or with the existing (or planned) facilities in Building 7625.

Note that all the requirements outlined [previously in this document] would apply to flowing targets as
well. In addition, a dry building is required to ensure absolute safety with alkali metals. Such a building
should have no water sources including sinks, lavatories, drinking fountains, nor floor or sewer drains.
Sprinkler systems should be capped or disabled in dry rooms. No vinyl floors under MPEX are permitted.
Even the concrete floor must have a stainless-steel secondary containment under the device. Lithium and
sodium can react violently with the moisture in concrete. No water cooling can be used anywhere; not in
the source, the waveguides, the vacuum vessel, the vacuum pumps or the target stage. No roof leaks are
permitted, and the humidity in the air should be controlled to reduce water vapor below 2%. HVAC
systems must only supply treated air to the facility, and all HVAC water and refrigerants must be
segregated from the building either outside or in a separate structure. Building ventilation must be
provided for hydrogen and alkali metal fumes. The cost of such a facility sized for MPEX and providing
necessary utilities will likely exceed $20M.

16.1 MAXIMUM FLOWING ALKALI METAL TARGET INVENTORY IN MPEX

Even if MPEX was upgraded to remove the risks outlined above, the maximum liquid alkali metal
inventory in MPEX and associated target loop should not exceed 1 kg unless a waiver is obtained from
the Liquid Metal Safety Committee and the experiment is performed in a dry building. Limits must also
be placed on the amount of metal stored at the facility in case of fire.

16.2 MODIFICATIONS REQUIRED TO VACUUM SYSTEM AND DIAGNOSTICS FOR ALL
 OF MPEX

The internal vessel walls of MPEX must be either stainless steel or refractory metal like tungsten,
molybdenum, niobium or Nb1Zr. Exposed copper, CuCrZr or aluminum is not permitted. Bronze, zinc,
high chromium or nickel alloys are not permitted. Exposed alumina insulators are not permitted. They can
be replaced with yttria or perhaps coated with yttria or another nonreactive dielectric. The same applies to
feedthroughs. All vacuum seals must be steel. Helicoflex seals are preferred. O-rings are not permitted on
flanges or valve seals. Copper ConFlat gaskets are not permitted. Windows should be shuttered or valved
for easy cleaning and replacement. Quartz and Pyrex work well. ZnSe and sapphire are not compatible
with alkali metals and must be protected from vapor deposition by shuttering, chopper wheels or active
argon purge. The operating procedures and PLC control logic must monitor LM flows, temperatures and
pressures and reservoir levels if necessary. Particular care must be exercised to not thermally shock
components leading to liquid metal embrittlement and catastrophic component failure. This requires a

 61

multitude of thermocouples and other sensors throughout the target stage and LM loop to completely
characterize the temperature distribution. No portion of the target and loop can be unknown.

16.3 MODIFICATIONS AND CONFIGURATION CONTROL OF MPEX

Careful configuration control is required for all of MPEX when working with flowing liquid metals, not
just the PMI chamber. All modifications must be approved by the Liquid Metal Safety Committee and
Radiological Controls Committee as well as Operations and be well documented. An approved materials
list compatible with alkali metals should be maintained. During maintenance, a component should only be
replaced with an identical component. Substitutions of any kind must be carefully evaluated.

It is recommended that a change sheet or log be maintained and details about any modifications should be
noted. Impacts on safety and operations should be flagged so they can be reviewed and cleared before the
next run period. All operators should be cognizant of recent changes each time MPEX is energized.

16.4 IMPACT OF CHEMICAL REACTIVITY, CORROSION AND LME ON MPEX
 COMPONENTS

Each flowing alkali metal experiment in MPEX must be evaluated for the degree of reactivity possible,
the effects of corrosion in tubing and reservoirs at temperature and the likelihood of initiating LME in the
components. Only refractory metals such as tungsten and molybdenum are immune from LME.
Austenitic stainless steels resist corrosion and LME. However, Cr and Ni are depleted and preferentially
transported away leaving iron which is corrosion resistant, but highly susceptible to LME. Embrittlement
will lead to cracking and uncontrolled release of molten metal or loss of mechanical integrity and
function. Exposure of liquid metal surfaces to the vacuum will result in copious amount of metal frost or
condensation on cold surfaces virtually anywhere in MPEX. This in turn may cause local exothermic
reactions and begin the process of liquid metal embrittlement or hydrogen generation. All MPEX
components that have a vacuum boundary and diagnostics should be carefully inspected and cleaned if
necessary. As for the melted solid targets, the flowing alkali metal experiments should be restricted to a
campaign period of reasonable duration. Most of the MPEX internals will need to be cleaned, repaired or
replaced; so adequate budgets, time and human resources are required for the maintenance which may far
exceed the experiment costs.
