Genomic and transcriptomic insights into color pigmentation variation in barred chickens
Authors: Tairan Chen, Xufang Ren, Dengjing Zuo, Xiurong Zhao, Anqi Chen, Fugui Li, Xinye Zhang, Xiaoyu Jiang, Hongxi Yu, Lujiang Qu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Plumage color is an economically important trait in chickens, and barred chickens are characterized by a distinct feather pattern in which variation in pigmentation (yellow-barred vs. white-barred) is frequently observed. Although the genetic basis of barred patterning has been partially elucidated, the mechanisms underlying pigmentation differences within barred feathers remain unclear. In this study, genome-wide association study (GWAS) and selection signature (FST) analyses based on 47 individuals (20 yellow-barred and 27 white-barred) identified several candidate genomic regions and genes, including BRINP2, SLC22A4, SLC22A5, and ACTN1, potentially associated with pigment variation. Transcriptomic analysis of feather follicles further revealed substantial gene expression divergence between the two phenotypes, with 739 differentially expressed genes identified, among which SLC45A2, BCO2, EDAR, KRT75, and KRT14 were notable candidates. Functional enrichment analysis indicated that these genes are primarily involved in skin development, epidermal differentiation, keratinization, and intermediate filament organization. Integrated analyses suggest that coordinated regulation of melanin synthesis, carotenoid metabolism, and feather follicle structural development contributes to the observed pigmentation differences. Overall, this study provides genomic and transcriptomic insights into the molecular basis of pigmentation variation in barred chickens and offers a foundation for future functional validation and molecular breeding.

Introduction

Plumage coloration represents one of the most visually striking phenotypic traits in domestic chickens and plays important roles in both biological and economic contexts. In addition to contributing to sexual signaling and ecological adaptation in birds, plumage color is widely used as a primary determinant of breed identity (Li et al., 2023) and frequently influences consumer preference and the market value of poultry products (Kennedy et al., 2005; Martínez Michel et al., 2011). Consequently, understanding the genetic mechanisms underlying plumage color variation has long been a central topic in avian genetics and poultry breeding

Avian plumage coloration is primarily determined by the deposition and spatial distribution of multiple classes of pigments, most notably melanins and carotenoids. Melanin pigments—including eumelanin and pheomelanin—are synthesized within melanocytes and are responsible for black, brown, and gray coloration (Ghosh Roy et al., 2025; Galván and Gómez-Vicioso, 2025), whereas carotenoid pigments derived from dietary sources contribute to yellow, orange, and red hues in feathers and other integumentary tissues (Huang et al., 2025). A growing number of studies have identified genes involved in pigment production, transport, and deposition in birds. In chickens, genes such as SLC45A2 have been shown to influence melanosomal pH and substrate transport, thereby contributing to variation in melanin synthesis (Huo et al., 2024). In addition, enzymes involved in carotenoid metabolism, such as BCO2, play essential roles in regulating the oxidative cleavage and accumulation of carotenoid pigments within avian tissues (Walsh et al., 2012; Fallahshahroudi et al., 2019; Zhao et al., 2024). The final visual perception of plumage hue is often a product of the interplay between these pigment metabolisms. Beyond pigment cells, the structural matrix formed by keratinocytes serves as the terminal storage site and scaffold for pigment integration (Price-Waldman et al., 2025). Emerging evidence suggests that signaling pathways governing feather follicle morphogenesis, such as the EDA/EDAR pathway, may also influence the structural microenvironment required for proper pigment uptake and spatial localization (Zhang et al., 2009).

Among the diverse plumage phenotypes observed in chickens, the barred feather pattern represents one of the most recognizable traits. Barred feathers exhibit alternating pigmented and non-pigmented bands along the feather shaft. Previous genetic studies have shown that the classical sex-linked barring phenotype is primarily associated with mutations in the CDKN2A locus located on the Z chromosome (Hellström et al., 2010), which alters the transcription of this tumor suppressor gene and results in periodic melanin dilution (Dorshorst and Ashwell, 2009). While CDKN2A dictates the periodic "on-off" switch of pigmentation, the specific molecular mechanisms that regulate the presence or absence of yellow pigmentation within the pigmented bars remain unclear. In certain barred chicken populations, distinct phenotypic variations can be observed within the pigmented regions, predominantly presenting as yellow-barred and white-barred feathers (Fig. 1). Recent advances in high-throughput sequencing technologies have enabled integrative genomic approaches for investigating such complex traits. In particular, the combination of genome-wide association studies (GWAS), population differentiation analyses (FST), and transcriptomic profiling has proven highly effective for identifying genomic regions and candidate genes associated with phenotypic variation. Because the feather follicle provides an easily accessible and dynamic system where melanocytes and keratinocytes interact during feather regeneration, transcriptomic analysis of these tissues offers valuable insights into the molecular underpinnings of pigmentation. Furthermore, recent studies in comparative genomics have highlighted the involvement of genes previously associated with neural development in integumentary patterning, reflecting the shared developmental origin of melanocytes and the nervous system from the embryonic neural crest (Alkobtawi et al., 2018). Therefore, by integrating GWAS, FST analysis, and transcriptomic profiling of feather follicle tissues, this study aimed to propose a multi-locus regulatory model that accounts for the phenotypic divergence between white-barred and yellow-barred chickens. These findings are expected to provide valuable insights into the integrated biological processes underlying barred feather coloration and offer candidate targets for future molecular breeding programs

Materials and methods

Animals

The study utilized 47 eleven-week-old male barred chickens (20 yellow-barred and 27 white-barred) from Beijing DaXin Poultry Co. In this study, the two groups of barred chickens shared a consistent genetic background, with phenotypic variations naturally occurring. To standardize environmental and dietary factors, all individuals were reared simultaneously in the same chicken coop, housed on identical rearing racks, and provided with uniform feed and daily management throughout the study.

DNA extraction, sequencing, and quality control

Genomic DNA was extracted from blood samples using a commercial kit. DNA quality was assessed using Qubit 3.0 and NanoDrop 2000. Qualified DNA samples were used to construct libraries with the VAHTS® Universal Plus DNA Library Prep Kit, followed by paired-end (PE150) sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to achieve ∼8x coverage. Raw sequencing data were quality-controlled using fastp (v0.23.4) (Chen et al., 2018). Variant calling was performed using the Genome Analysis Toolkit from GATK version 4.4.0 (https://gatk.broadinstitute.org/) (McKenna et al., 2010) best practices pipeline and filtered paired reads were aligned to the chicken reference genome (Gallus_gallus GRCg7b), generating BAM and GVCF files. Joint variant calling was then performed across all samples to produce the raw VCF file. The SNP files were further filtered using the VariantFiltration module in GATK, with options “QD < 2.0 || QUAL < 30.0 || SOR > 3.0 || FS > 60.0 || MQ < 40.0 || MQRankSum < −12.5 || ReadPosRankSum < −8.0″. Subsequent quality control (QC) of SNPs was performed using PLINK (v1.90) (Purcell et al., 2007) with stringent criteria: individual call rate > 95%, SNP call rate > 90%, minor allele frequency (MAF) > 0.05, and Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) P-value > 1e-6. After quality control, 1,857,798 high-quality SNPs were retained for analysis.

Population structure and GWAS

Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted using PLINK to assess population stratification (Maharani et al., 2021). Using all high-quality SNP markers that passed quality control, principal components (PCs) were calculated, and the percentage of population structure variation explained by each PC was determined. Visualization of the results was performed using the ggplot2 package (v4.0) (Ginestet, 2011) in R. Genome-wide association analysis (GWAS) was implemented using the Genome-wide Efficient Mixed-model Association (GEMMA) software package. Specifically, we applied the standard univariate linear mixed model (LMM1, via the -lmm 1 option) to account for population stratification and kinship effects, thereby enhancing statistical power and minimizing false-positive associations (Zhou and Stephens, 2014). The suggestive (P < 5e-6) and genome-wide significance (P < 5.38e-7) thresholds were applied. The Manhattan and quantile-quantile plots of feather color traits were implemented using the CMplot package (https://github.com/YinLiLin/CMplot) within R software (http://www.r-project.org/). The positions of the SNPs and the names of the corresponding genes were retrieved using the Ensembl BioMart online tool (https://apr2022.archive.ensembl.org/biomart/martview/).

Selection signature analysis (FST)

Genetic differentiation between yellow-barred and white-barred groups was measured using Weir and Cockerham's FST statistic in 100-kb windows with a 10-kb step size using VCFtools (v0.1.16). Genomic regions with FST values exceeding 0.4 (Wang et al., 2022) were considered under potential selection, and genes within these regions (±20 kb) were identified as candidates. The positions of the SNPs and the names of the corresponding genes were retrieved using the Ensembl BioMart online tool (https://apr2022.archive.ensembl.org/biomart/martview/).

Transcriptomic analysis

In this study, feather follicles were collected from the neck region of barred chickens. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent, followed by RNA concentration quantification with a Nanodrop2000 and integrity assessment using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. All samples met the library construction quality criteria: total amount ≥2 μg, concentration ≥40 ng/μL, OD260/280 ratio between 1.7-2.5, and RIN value ≥7. Library construction was performed using the Hieff NGS Ultima Dual-mode mRNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Yeasen, Cat# 13533ES96). The specific procedure included: mRNA purification through two rounds of affinity enrichment with mRNA Capture Beads, fragmentation in Frag/Prime Buffer under temperature-controlled conditions, sequential synthesis of first-strand and second-strand cDNA (including end repair and A-tailing), adapter ligation with index sequences, product purification using Hieff NGS DNA Selection Beads (Yeasen, Cat# 12601ES56) for two rounds of magnetic bead-based selection, and final library amplification via 15 cycles of PCR. The purified libraries were quality-controlled using Qsep-400, confirming a primary peak distribution of 300-500 bp without significant primer-dimer remnants. Qualified libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (San Diego, USA) using NovaSeq 6000 S4 Reagent Kit with PE150 configuration, generating at least 6 Gb of data per sample. Raw reads were processed with fastp to obtain clean reads, which were then aligned to the chicken reference genome (Gallus_gallus GRCg7b) using HISAT2 (v2.2.1) (Pertea et al., 2016). Gene expression levels were quantified using FeatureCounts (Liao et al., 2014).

Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (Love et al., 2014; Sahraeian et al., 2017) between yellow-barred and white-barred group. Benjamini-Hochberg method was used to calculate the adjusted P value (FDR) for multiple testing corrections. The DEGs were identified according to the criteria of |log2FC| ≥ 1 and p < 0.05.

To interpret the biological functions and pathways of the identified DEGs, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were conducted. The KEGG database provides systematic insights into the advanced functions and utilities of biological systems, such as pathways and networks. GO enrichment analysis of the DEGs was conducted with the GOseq R package (https://www.rdocumentation.org/packages/goseq/versions/1.24.0/topics/goseq). This analysis employed Wallenius noncentral hypergeometric distribution and adjusted for length bias in the DEGs (Young et al., 2010). Significantly enriched terms were defined as those with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05.

Discussion

The present study integrated GWAS, FST, and transcriptomic analyses to dissect the molecular basis of barred feather coloration in chickens. Convergent genomic signals and differential gene expression patterns indicate that barred plumage arises from coordinated regulation of feather morphogenesis and pigment metabolism, rather than from variation in a single canonical pigmentation gene.

To robustly identify these convergent genomic signals, it is important to consider the rigorous parameters of our experimental cohort. As detailed in the Methods, our study design effectively minimized environmental and dietary confounding factors by utilizing individuals with a consistent genetic background reared under strictly standardized conditions. Given this rigorously controlled experimental design, although the GWAS results did not reach the conventional threshold for genome-wide significance, the loci that exceeded the suggestive significance threshold (5.38e-7) are highly likely to represent true biological associations rather than artifacts of environmental variance.

Among these biologically meaningful loci, population genomic analyses consistently identified BRINP2 (BMP/retinoic acid inducible neural-specific protein 2) as a candidate locus under selection and associated with the color phenotype. Although BRINP2 has been primarily studied in the context of neural development (Berkowicz et al., 2016; Kang et al., 2023), its molecular properties and functional roles offer compelling links to avian pigmentation. First, because its expression is induced by retinoic acid—a vitamin A metabolite known to regulate cell differentiation and pigment synthesis—BRINP2 may influence pigmentation processes in chickens through retinoid-mediated pathways (Choi et al., 2021), potentially affecting melanin or carotenoid deposition. Second, studies in other biological systems indicate that this gene participates in tissue patterning (Terashima et al., 2010; Coassolo et al., 2025) and the negative regulation of the mitotic cell cycle (Kawano et al., 2004; Terashima et al., 2010). These developmental functions raise the possibility that BRINP2 may contribute to spatial organization within the feather follicle, influencing melanocyte differentiation or periodic pigment deposition during feather growth (Fig. 7A). Consistent with the involvement of developmental patterning mechanisms, differential expression of EDAR further supports the role of epithelial–mesenchymal signaling in barred feather formation. As a key component of the EDA signaling pathway (Houghton et al., 2005), EDAR is a well-established regulator essential for the development and morphogenesis of ectodermal appendages such as feathers and hair follicles (Botchkarev and Fessing, 2005; Ho et al., 2019), playing critical roles in processes such as follicle spacing, epithelial invagination, and structural periodicity. Crucially, while this pathway does not participate in direct pigment synthesis, the structural microenvironment it constructs exerts strict non-autonomous control over the neural crest-derived melanocytes that migrate into the follicle (Inaba and Chuong, 2020). Therefore, elevated EDAR expression in specific feather regions suggests that these highly regulated follicular patterning processes act as a spatial template, directly dictating the cyclic localization and biological activity of pigment-producing cells to create the complex micro-patterns characteristic of developing barred feathers (Fig. 7B).

Alongside these developmental regulators, our analyses highlighted SLC45A2 and BCO2 as central metabolic factors jointly driving pigment deposition. SLC45A2 encodes a membrane-associated transporter protein that plays a crucial role in melanin synthesis by regulating melanosomal pH and substrate transport (Mao et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2022). Consistent with reports that its downregulation or loss-of-function is associated with reduced eumelanin deposition and lighter pigmentation across various species (Ghosh Roy et al., 2024), its higher expression supports the increased melanin deposition observed in yellow-barred feathers. In parallel with melanin regulation, BCO2 governs carotenoid metabolism. BCO2 encodes a carotenoid-cleaving enzyme that degrades dietary carotenoids into colorless apocarotenoids (Dela Seña et al., 2016; Enbody et al., 2021b; Thomas et al., 2023), thereby limiting carotenoid accumulation. The significantly lower expression of BCO2 in yellow-barred feathers compared with white feather regions suggests that reduced carotenoid cleavage promotes pigment retention, contributing to yellow coloration. These observations are consistent with previous studies demonstrating that reduced BCO2 activity or expression is associated with carotenoid-based pigmentation in birds (Wu et al., 2016). Together, these findings indicate that differential carotenoid metabolism likely contributes to the yellow–white contrast observed within barred feather patterns (Fig. 7C).

Functional enrichment analyses provided additional biological context for the molecular mechanisms identified in this study. Differentially expressed genes were significantly enriched in processes related to epidermal differentiation, keratinization, and intermediate filament organization. These biological processes reflect active feather growth and structural remodeling at approximately 11 weeks of age. Keratinocytes serve as the terminal storage sites for both melanin and carotenoid pigments (Bento-Lopes et al., 2023), suggesting that structural maturation of the feather epithelium may influence the final deposition and stabilization of pigments within feather tissues. Consequently, feather structural differentiation likely interacts with both developmental signaling pathways and pigment metabolic processes to shape the visible plumage phenotype.

Taken together, the concurrent detection of SLC45A2, BCO2, and EDAR supports an integrated regulatory framework in which barred feather coloration arises from the coordinated interaction of both pigment metabolism and feather structural development. Developmental patterning pathways involving BRINP2 and EDAR may influence follicular architecture and spatial organization during feather development. In parallel, metabolic regulation of carotenoid processing mediated by BCO2 determines the local accumulation of pigment molecules. Structural differentiation of the feather epithelium further provides the cellular context in which pigments are deposited and stabilized. Rather than functioning independently, these processes likely act in concert to generate spatially periodic pigmentation patterns within feathers.

The present study provides new insights into the genetic and molecular mechanisms underlying barred feather coloration. Future studies integrating spatial transcriptomics, functional validation experiments, and detailed morphological analyses of feather follicles will be essential to clarify the temporal and spatial relationships among developmental signaling pathways, pigment metabolism, and feather structural organization. In addition, comparative analyses across chicken breeds and related avian species may help determine whether similar regulatory architectures underlie patterned plumage variation more broadly.

Conclusion

In summary, this study integrated genome-wide association analysis, population differentiation analysis, and transcriptomic profiling to investigate the molecular basis of pigmentation variation in barred chickens. Several candidate genes associated with feather coloration were identified, including BRINP2, SLC45A2, BCO2, and EDAR. The results suggest that pigmentation differences between yellow-barred and white-barred chickens are not controlled by a single pigmentation-related factor, but instead arise from the coordinated interaction of feather developmental patterning, pigment metabolism, and feather structural differentiation. In particular, differential regulation of melanin synthesis and carotenoid degradation appears to play a central role in generating the observed yellow–white pigmentation contrast. These findings provide new insights into the genetic architecture of barred plumage coloration in chickens and establish a valuable foundation for future functional validation and molecular breeding applications.
