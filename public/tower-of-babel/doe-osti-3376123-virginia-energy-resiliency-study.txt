3. Executive Summary:

The Virginia Energy Resilience Study (VERS) was conducted under the U.S. Department
of Energy’s Renewables Advancing Community Energy Resilience (RACER) program to
strengthen the ability of communities in Virginia to anticipate, withstand, and recover from
electric grid disruptions. While the project did not fund construction, it delivered a
comprehensive, community-centered framework for assessing energy resilience and
produced actionable, site-specific solar-plus-storage designs to support future
implementation.

The Virginia Energy Resilience Study had four primary objectives:

 1. Develop Energy Resilience Metrics (ERM) tailored to assessing resilience in
 HEDCs using accessible, community-level data.
 2. Identify and engage HEDCs interested in improving resilience through solar-plus-
 storage solutions.
 3. Facilitate community-driven case studies that integrate technical design with
 inclusive engagement and local priorities.
 4. Conduct a statewide assessment of energy resilience in HEDCs and create a
 replicable model for continued use.

Key Accomplishments:

 • Creation of Virginia’s first HEDC Energy Resilience Metrics, an innovative, publicly
 usable tool grounded in emergency planning documents, demographic data, and
 community self-assessment.
 • Engagement of 19 communities (representing approximately 8% of Virginia’s
 incorporated towns and cities) in pilot testing and refinement of the metrics and
 development of 10 community-specific solar-plus-storage case studies, with at

 Page 2 of 42

 least seven serving rural or remote communities, including engineering drawings,
 feasibility analyses, cost estimates, and resilience benefits.
 • Extensive, inclusive community engagement, supported by DEI training for project
 partners and collaboration with local governments, utilities, community-based
 organizations, and emergency managers.
 • Statewide assessment of outage risk and resilience gaps, informed by historical
 outage data, utility reliability metrics, and community-reported vulnerabilities.

The study revealed that energy resilience in Virginia is shaped as much by community
agency and social capacity as by physical infrastructure. Key findings include:

 • Extreme weather events are the primary drivers of long-duration outages in
 Virginia.
 • Most communities lack control over grid infrastructure, underscoring the
 importance of behind-the-meter solutions such as solar-plus-storage and
 resilience hubs.
 • Public health and safety, emergency management, and access to basic
 necessities emerged as the most critical resilience functions across communities.
 • Smaller and rural communities can meaningfully engage in resilience planning
 when they possess basic administrative authority and essential services,
 regardless of population size.

The Virginia Energy Resilience Study delivers a scalable, equitable, and replicable model
for community energy resilience planning that can be applied statewide and nationally.
By integrating technical analysis with lived community experience, the project strengthens
long-term capacity for resilience planning, supports future deployment of solar-plus-
storage systems, and advances DOE’s broader goals of distributed energy adoption,
energy security, and equitable economic benefit.

6. Background:

The Virginia Energy Resilience Study as part of the Renewables Advancing Community
Energy Resilience (RACER) program, launched by the U.S. Department of Energy’s Solar
Energy Technologies Office (SETO), was designed to study, engage, and strengthen
historically economically disadvantaged communities to withstand and recover from
disruptions to the electric grid. While this project did not include building infrastructure,
case studies and engineering designs were developed integrating solar and solar-plus-
storage solutions to enhance local resilience and ensure reliable access to clean energy
during extreme weather events and other emergencies.

The Virginia Energy Resilience Study emphasizes the importance of community-centered
approaches to energy resilience. By empowering stakeholders, such as local
governments, utilities, nonprofits, and research institutions, the project fosters innovative
strategies that combine solar-plus-storage solutions with advanced planning and
preparedness. These efforts not only reduce vulnerability to outages but also promote
equitable access to resilient energy systems, particularly for historically economically
disadvantaged communities.

This study’s objectives extend beyond technical innovation. Aligned with the RACER
goals, this project seeks to:

 • Demonstrate scalable models for integrating renewable energy into resilience
 planning.
 • Support community-driven projects that prioritize local needs and equity.
 • Build long-term capacity for communities to adapt to evolving resilience and energy
 challenges.

This project adds to the broader mission of the Department of Energy to support energy
independence while ensuring that all communities—urban, rural, and underserved—
benefit from reliable, affordable, and resilient power. This report highlights the program’s
accomplishments, provides discussions of technical approaches, project outcomes, and
lessons learned.

This study naturally follows Virginia Energy’s other recent work, including statewide
energy assessments such as the 2018 and 2022 Virginia Energy Plans and the 2022
Virginia Solar Survey. Virginia Energy also helped to identify and map Historically
Economically Disadvantaged Communities (HEDCs) as part of an assessment of the
Virginia Clean Economy Act’s (VCEA) potential disproportionate burdens on the
communities. Through this Virginia Study, the project team hoped to improve the
capability of energy resiliency assessment and planning across Virginia’s HEDCs.

The term "energy resilience" has evolved significantly over the past several decades,
shaped by technological, environmental, and geopolitical developments. Its conceptual
roots can be traced back to the 1970s in response to the oil crises and growing

 Page 5 of 42

environmental concerns (Gößling-Reisemann 2018). By the early 2000s, "energy
resilience" began to appear more frequently in policy and academic literature, particularly
in response to hurricanes, extreme weather events, and cybersecurity threats. The
concept expanded from a narrow focus on infrastructure robustness to a broader
understanding that includes social, economic, and institutional capacities to withstand and
recover from disruptions. Events like the 2003 Northeast blackout, Hurricane Katrina, and
Superstorm Sandy accelerated this shift, prompting governments and utilities to integrate
resilience into energy planning. Today, energy resilience encompasses distributed
generation, microgrids, renewable integration, and community-based energy systems,
and is considered a key pillar of sustainable and secure energy transitions (Sartori da
Silva 2024).

The concept of Community Energy Resilience reported by Niklas and Mey (2023), defines
this resilience as the capacity of communities to anticipate, prepare for, respond to, and
recover from energy disruptions, particularly in the face of extreme weather events. The
authors argue that traditional energy system resilience—focused on infrastructure and
reliability metrics—fails to capture the lived experiences and needs of communities.
Instead, they propose a broader, people-centered framework that integrates social,
institutional, and technical dimensions. This includes local energy generation (e.g., solar
plus storage), community governance, and equitable access to energy resources. Their
study highlights the importance of inclusive planning, local knowledge, and adaptive
capacity, especially in vulnerable or remote areas.

In their 2024 literature review, Thompson and Pescaroli examine the evolving field of
electricity resilience financing in local communities, particularly within highly developed
countries. They highlight a shift in focus from large-scale grid resilience to smaller,
community-based systems that can better address localized disruptions. A central finding
of the review is that community involvement and cost structures are critical to the success
of financing models for local electricity resilience. However, the field has yet to fully
integrate the concept of adaptation into its financial planning.

The concept of ‘agency’ was central to understanding how communities view and address
energy resilience planning. In the context of this project, a simple operational definition of
agency will suffice – the capacity of individuals or groups within a community to make
choices, take intentional actions, and influence the outcomes that impact their lives and
environment. This definition involves three distinct components that are necessary for
implementing successful projects. The first two of these components were central to many
of the tasks throughout the project. Throughout the study, Virginia Energy was able to
conduct accessible, inclusive and meaningful community engagement for the
development of energy resiliency infrastructure models. It provided partnering
community-based organizations, local participants and technical specialists with the
resources to conduct on-the-ground community engagement. The funding ensured
quality community planning case study projects took place, which increased the total
community involvement in energy resilience planning.

7. Project Objectives:

The Virginia Study consisted of four major objectives: 1) the development of energy
resiliency metrics for assessing energy resiliency in HEDCs; 2) identification of HEDCs
interested in the development of solar plus storage infrastructure; 3) facilitation of
community-driven energy resiliency planning case studies for behind-the-meter solar plus
storage; and 4) a statewide assessment of energy resiliency in HEDCs. Key outcomes
included the HEDC energy resiliency metrics; a report of energy resiliency assessments
in HEDCs throughout Virginia; the development of ten (10) solar plus storage case
studies, with no fewer than seven (7) directed at rural or remote communities; and a
replicable, equitable and collaborative model for ongoing community participation.

To achieve these objectives, tasks included the development of a methodology for
assessing energy resiliency needs in individual communities; a process for coordinating
community-driven energy resiliency infrastructure development planning; and a set of
designs for solar plus storage infrastructure for HEDC sites. The Virginia Study worked
with educational institutions, emergency management professionals, electric utilities and
HEDC stakeholders, such as local governments, community-based organizations and
emergency responders, to identify metrics for assessing energy resiliency in HEDCs. The
metrics are accessible to community planners, local officials and the public and consist
of readily available information at the community level to ensure assessment is replicable
in other communities and on a state or national scale.

The creation of the HEDC Energy Resiliency Metrics is new and innovative for the state
of Virginia. These metrics are an important step for assessing statewide energy resiliency
by utilizing existing resources and community planning documents. Disaster
management plans are regularly updated across the Commonwealth through the
Emergency Operations Plan (EOP) as required by the Commonwealth of Virginia
Emergency Services and Disaster Laws. By utilizing EOPs, the Virginia Study is provided
with a baseline for the threats and current plans enacted in each county.

To achieve the goal of demonstrating community driven energy resiliency infrastructure
planning, the project team worked with HEDCs, collected community input and
empowered community stakeholders to lead in planning energy resiliency in their
community. In coordination with community-based organizations, HEDC stakeholders,
utilities, and emergency management professionals, the team provided technical,
planning and community engagement support for the creation of uniquely modeled solar
plus storage case studies that meet community needs. Technical assistance and
engineering designs provided for case studies through the Virginia Study will aid HEDCs
in securing private and public funding resources, thus expediting the development of solar
plus storage DERS.

8. Project Results and Discussion:

The Virginia Energy Resilience Study explored the energy resilience of communities as
a social system: their capacity to withstand, adapt to, and recover from the disruptions
created by power outages while still meeting the essential needs of community members.
The metrics to assess community energy resilience from the Virginia Energy Resilience
Study made it possible to identify improvements in communities while creating a blueprint
to increase energy resiliency nationwide. Four tasks along with subtasks were developed
to achieve the goals of the study:

 1. Development of Energy Resilience Metrics
 2. Historically Economically Disadvantaged Communities (HEDC) Identification and
 Outreach for Case Study Development
 3. Case Study Development
 4. Refinement of ERM and Evaluation of Additional Communities

Task 1: Development of Energy Resilience Metrics (ERM)

Summary: Identify energy resilience and demographic data inputs to develop ERMs for
the evaluation of energy resilience in Virginia communities. Input from electric utilities and
the Virginia Department of Emergency Management will be collected to inform data input
selection. Evaluation of the ERM will take place with 15 communities to Evaluate data

availability and identify specific energy infrastructure, environmental and demographic
indicators as metrics inputs. Outreach activities will be conducted to engage communities
in the evaluation process.

Subtask 1.1: HEDC Demographic Data Input Identification

Subtask Summary: Identify local, state, and federal demographic data resources that
relate to energy resilience in HEDCs. Existing tools, such as DOE's Disadvantaged
Communities Reporter, the Climate and Economic Justice Screening Tool, EPA’s EJ
Screen, DEQ’s VA EJ Screen+, opportunity zones and U.S. Census demographics data,
will be utilized to identify and collect demographic data inputs. The objective is to identify
demographic data that is consistently available in HEDCs for use in resilience metrics. A
review of existing literature and summary of available data will be used to establish
vulnerable areas for inclusion in resilience metrics and identify energy resilience needs
for communities. Outcomes include a list of demographic data metrics that may be used
as part of the HEDC ERM.

The project addresses historically and economically disadvantaged communities
(HEDCs) as defined by the Commonwealth of Virginia. Historic and economic
disadvantage is established by income and ethnicity criteria. Income considerations are
based on Virginia median income. The project considers two other federal definitions for
disadvantaged status: Energy Communities and the Recompete Pilot Program. Energy
Communities and the Recompete program are defined by unemployment characteristics.
Energy Communities also have brownfields and/or coalfields.

Screening tools were evaluated at the census tract level. Spatially, disadvantaged status
is established at the Census tract level. Census tracts lie wholly within a county or an
incorporated (independent) city. They do not cross political boundaries. There are 1,132
census tracts of 1,907 in Virginia that match at least one of the four categories of
disadvantage.

Later discussions established community agency as a factor of consideration and the
team started focusing metrics generation and workbook collection on a town, city, or
county level. A census-tract or unincorporated community version of the metrics was also
generated but not used in evaluation with communities.

Subtask 1.2: Energy Resilience Infrastructure Data Input Identification and Stakeholder
Engagement Activities

Subtask Summary: Identify consistently available, community level information on
energy resilience infrastructure, energy grid risks, and other data that may relate to
energy resilience. Power grid system level performance metrics and community energy
metrics will be based on historical data. Possible data inputs for power grid system level
performance can include presence of buried power lines, utility poles, transformers,
existing energy storage and generation infrastructure, outage history, location plans for

 Page 9 of 42

energy outage emergencies, extreme weather history and threats, and environmental and
health impacts of existing energy infrastructure. An extensive list of indicators with
available data will be analyzed, scored, and ranked based on established literature and
existing studies to create a refined list for use in ERM. Examples include Community
Energy Strategic Planning Case Studies from DOE EERE and resilience case studies
from NOAA Climate Program Office. A list of data inputs will be generated to develop
ERM and to identify weaknesses in energy infrastructure. Stakeholder engagement
activities will be conducted to identify existing data for ERM. This will include a minimum
of one interview with a public utility, electric cooperative, and municipal utility in addition
to a meeting with the Virginia Department of Emergency Management.

Description of metrics used within the Energy Resilience Metrics Worksheets and
Workbook for Year 1 are provided in Table 1 at the end of this section. This was the initial
set of inputs winnowed by the team at JMU for use in the workbooks before stakeholder
input or field testing. A consideration that arose during the entire process of metric
selection and development is ‘what agency do communities have over energy resilience?’
versus the role of the utilities.

The general framework for the metrics and discussions with communities was as follows:

1. A basic community profile. Allows users to understand their community in terms of
 demographics, energy infrastructure, energy use, and geographic
 isolation/accessibility/mobility. There is a particular focus on vulnerable populations.
2. Hazard exposure and risk assessment. We include natural hazards and electric
 power supply disruptions as the first-order hazards. We are not including threats
 (which are of human origin, such as sabotage, terrorism, or vandalism).
 Hazard exposure and risk includes a treatment of high probability/low impact events
 (e.g., common thunderstorms) and low probability/high impact events (catastrophic
 disasters).
3. Capability and vulnerability (resilience) analysis. Identifies what communities are
 currently capable of doing and where they are vulnerable when energy supply is
 disrupted. This assesses how resilient they currently are. The focus is on lifeline
 functions (community and household) and essential community services and roles.
 Capability and vulnerability are inherently time-based (e.g., short, intermediate, and
 lengthy times to recovery). The inherent interdependence between energy systems
 over different lengths of time is also accounted for. This part of the assessment
 provides an easy-to-follow framework regarding duration of events and the time to
 recovery. All indicators for this assessment come from internal self-scoring by users
 and does not rely on external data sets.
4. Impact assessment. This part of the assessment has users estimate/imagine the
 community impacts of energy disruptions in the areas where they are vulnerable. To
 the greatest extent possible, it will use standardized impact language for each of the
 different kinds of vulnerabilities. (This is adapted from FEMA’s community-scale
 Threat and Hazard Identification and Risk Assessment (THIRA) model.) After the
 impact assessment, users will prioritize “gaps” they would like to close and hence

                                         Page 10 of 42

 become more resilient. The “gap” is the difference between what they are currently
 capable of doing and what they would like to be able to do. In resilience language,
 these improvements can be either adaptations or system transformations.
5. Potential solutions. The final step of the assessment is to help users understand the
 ways they can become more resilient for their targeted priorities. Solutions will be both
 behavioral/institutional and technical and will emphasize solar plus storage and
 resilience hubs as appropriate. “Next steps” in terms of guiding users to additional
 resources, potential funding, and assistance will be included. Solutions may vary
 according to the electric service territory of each community. Virginia’s energy
 landscape consists of three investor-owned utilities, rural electric cooperatives, and
 some municipal electric utilities/cooperatives (Figure 1).

 Figure 1: Map of Electric Services Territories in Virginia
Investor-owned Utilities account for the majority of the population of residents in Virginia,

19 Communities were recruited and completed community energy resilience metrics
worksheets as part of the Virginia Energy Resilience Study by the midpoint evaluation in
February 2025. Results were reported in the document ‘Preliminary Insights from the
Ongoing Pilot of the Scoring Tool’. A second document outlined the “The Community
Energy Resilience Scoring Tool and Metrics”. Both have been compiled by Dr. Maria
Papadakis and Dr. Jon Miles from James Madison University and were summarized in a
midterm report.

The 19 communities participating in the community energy resilience metrics and
evaluation represent 8% of all incorporated cities and towns within the Commonwealth of
Virginia (190 incorporated towns and 38 independent cities). Several of the 19
communities applied to be part of the Year 2 Case Studies, while others declined further
participation. Of the nineteen communities engaged as of the midpoint evaluation, the
distribution of participation was:
 • Four independent cities with populations ranging from 50,000 to 150,000 people
 • Thirteen incorporated towns with populations ranging from 250 to over 40,000
 people
 • One Census Designated Place with a population of approximately 6,000
 • A hybrid response representing a higher education campus, a town, and a county
 (the core population covered by the response is approximately 2,500)
Community worksheets, workbooks, and interviews have revealed some insights into
energy resilience that may be specific to Virginia and others that may be more generic in
nature. One finding suggests that two thresholds may be necessary to have meaningful
engagement around community energy resilience–legal authority and agency (essential
services or support for core energy resilience functions within boundaries). The
preliminary results show that the presence of three community resilience functions and
their related services is a robust metric: Public Health and Safety Infrastructure,
Emergency Management Services, and Critical Community Necessities. The vast
majority of communities participating have no agency over the electric power
infrastructure or its performance.

The study explored the history of long-term power outages in Virginia. The worst outages
are caused by extreme weather events, specifically hurricanes, winter snow and ice
storms, and derechos. These events cause outages largely because of high winds and/or
tree fall. Trees and limbs fall on power lines, can bring down utility poles, and can break
pole cross arms. For example, winter storm Frida in 2022 caused outages that took 6-10
days for full recovery by the affected utilities. Depending on the utility, roughly 50-94% of
all outages were caused by tree damage from outside the utility’s right of way.

Virginia has experienced a number of major storms and outages over the past several
years, with some involving outages that lasted for several days (or over a week) for some
customers and communities. The storms were also geographically large enough that

                                        Page 14 of 42

multiple utility service territories were affected by them. Critical outage events were
hurricanes Florence and Michael in 2018 and a major ice storm in 2021. The year 2022
was especially bad, with winter storm Frida, hurricane Ian, and a combined winter
storm/arctic blast.

The Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory PRESTO outage simulator did not generate
grid reliability statistics anywhere near the actual experiences of many communities in
Virginia between 2018-2022 when evaluated as part of this study (Midterm Report,
Papadakis). Surprisingly, utility reliability metrics did effectively capture the scope of
significant power outages in Virginia. We analyzed annual reliability indexes for outages
longer than 5 minutes reported by electric utilities to the USDOE on EIA Form 861.6.
Specifically, we evaluated SAIDI and CAIDI for all outage events. We also calculated
SAIDI for major outage events only (those that are a statistically significant departure from
the average lengths jof outages, or “normal” outages). The results are striking.

  Figure 3: The highest annual SAIDI due to major outage events reported by Virginia electric
 utilities from 2018 to 2023 (hours).
Figure 3, above, is a map that shows, by service territory, the highest reported system
average interruption duration index (SAIDI) for major outage events during 2018-2022.
The unit of measure is hours, not minutes. This major outage SAIDI tells us how many

Discussion and Insights Summary

                                         Page 15 of 42

From the completed responses and discussions with communities participating in the
metric evaluation, several insights were revealed that we did not expect at the beginning
of the project.
 1) Some types of communities may not benefit from an energy resilience tool. Virginia
 has only 228 cities and towns with administrative authority, and over 400
 unincorporated towns and census designated places that do not. Only one census
 designated place participated due to a county emergency manager participating
 (6000 people within a metropolitan area). Having agency, such as incorporation,
 may be a threshold that may be required to discuss community energy resiliency.
 Likewise, population may not be as much of an issue as much of a presence of
 essential services or community resilience functions. Several small rural towns
 with populations between 250 and 1,500 were engaged in this process.

 2) All communities offered some degree of services in public health and safety.
 Larger communities with populations over 40,000 had some degree of emergency
 management services whereas smaller communities were dependent on outside
 providers. Nearly all communities had critical necessities with the exception of
 telecommunications, internet services, and charging. Only communities larger
 than 6,000 reported having access to active management of services.

 3) Social resilience capacity matters and the metrics provide a way of measuring a
 community’s social services resilience which can capture important resilience
 activities that may not be captured by a focus on power supply. Some communities
 have formal plans in place to address outages and disasters. This suggests
 opportunities for moving some communities from informal to formal plans. It is also
 suggested that the size of the community may not be a limitation to this component.

 4) Most backup power is diesel generators, and only limited services may be covered
 during an extended outage. Only 9-1-1 call centers had consistent standards.
 Some communities also acknowledged challenges with relying on diesel fuel in the
 case of widespread longer-term outages.

 Subtask 4.1: Resilience Metrics Refinement and Completion

Subtask Summary: Finalization of the HEDC ERM will include the final methodology of
the metrics, including descriptions and justifications of the identified data inputs and the
ERM score calculations. The resilience metrics will take into account any
recommendations or comments on the methodology and data displayed provided by
community-based organizations, educational institutions, state agencies, DOE, and
HEDC members. The objective and outcome from the metrics completion is to have a
consistent HEDC stakeholder informed metrics for the assessment of energy resilience
in HEDCs for use in data analysis in the study and for continual use by state agencies.
Partners, local governments, and community organizations will utilize the metrics to
assess energy resilience improvement needs and ongoing infrastructure improvements
on overall resilience. States agencies may use the metric to assess the need for energy
infrastructure investment, strengths and weaknesses in communities during and after the
Virginia Study.

Our project explored the energy resilience of communities as a social system: their
capacity to withstand, adapt to, and recover from the disruptions created by power
outages while still meeting the essential needs of community members. We do not
analyze or assess the resilience of electric power grids as engineered systems. The vast
majority of communities have no agency over the electric power infrastructure or its
performance. A detailed summary of the theory and logic of our metrics is available in our
mid-term report.

The resilience metrics for this project reflect two dimensions of community energy
resilience: (1) the vital social functions that a community must maintain during a power
outage, and (2) the nature of the disturbances to the electric power system. The metrics
themselves are a scoring system primarily based on the amount of backup power
capability that a community has for each of its vital social functions relative to the potential
severity of power outages.

Vital Community Services
Our final metrics measure the energy resilience of 32 vital community services, both
individually and as composite indicators of overall resilience. The set was distilled from
an extensive review of two bodies of literature. One is the academic literature on
community resilience both broadly and with respect to energy specifically. The second is
the applied community emergency and resilience preparedness planning guidance from
federal and state agencies. Of particular importance were federal resources from NIST,
FEMA, NOAA, and DOE.

As systems, communities provide literally dozens of functions that support their residents.
Not all of these are critical during a power outage. (Examples of non-critical functions
include opportunities for outdoor recreation and holding social and cultural events.) We

                                         Page 27 of 42

define vital community services as system functions that support the health,
safety, and physical well-being of community members. These can be understood as
the core community services that help to meet basic survival needs, they are essential
system functions during an extended power outage, and they all require energy to
happen.

We grouped our 32 vital community services into 4 functionally similar sets, each
composed of 8 services (Table 3). Two set of functions are routine, day-to-day essential
services in a community. These are Public Health & Safety and Providing Community
Necessities. The other two sets of functions are temporary services that are mobilized
only in the event of a significant power outage or other emergency. These are Emergency
Management and Mitigating Household Vulnerabilities.

The Nature of Community Energy System Disturbances

Not all disturbances are the same. They vary by frequency (how often they occur),
intensity (how strong they are), duration (how long the disturbance lasts), and likelihood
(the probability they will happen). The frequency, intensity, and duration characteristics
of a disturbance interact and result in varying severities of potential disruption to a system.
The likelihood of the disturbance indicates the chances that it will happen in any given
period of time. Systems can be resilient to some disturbances but not others. Highly
resilient systems will be able to respond and maintain their functions throughout a range
of disturbances, from mild to severe and short or long.

To create resilience metrics, we therefore needed a way to easily capture all the variability
of power outages as system disturbance events. We integrated the characteristics of
power outages by classifying them as four types of “disturbance”. A detailed, more
technical/theoretical summary of this classification and its rationale can be found in our
mid-term report. The four event types we created (ordinary, major, extreme, and
catastrophic outages) are based on their commonly understood frequencies, durations,

                                         Page 29 of 42

and scope of potential impact. The categories are primarily defined by the duration of the
outage, with key break points at 1 day, 3 days, and 2 weeks. 1 0F

These time increments also capture the typical underlying causes of outages. As outages
shift from ordinary to major, severe, and catastrophic, their causes also shift to larger and
more intense natural hazards. Unplanned power outages that last for many hours or days
are typically the result of a natural hazard (storms, tornados, earthquakes, wildfires, and
so on). Increasingly intense hazards can cause increasingly severe power outages. They
will affect more people, over a wider area, with a broader range of lost critical household
and community energy services, for a longer period of time.

The Resilience Metrics
Our project creates three types of energy resilience scores that can be used to rate and
interpret the resilience of (1) individual vital services, (2) a specific community function
(like public health and safety), and (3) all 32 vital services holistically.

The energy resilience score is an ordinal scale that ranges from 0-4, with 0 being no
resilience. Raw scores are assigned to each of the 32 vital community services based on
their backup power capability (Table 4). Backup power capability is the length of time a
service can continue to operate during a power outage assuming there is no damage to
the backup system or the relevant built environment. As shown in Table 4, backup power
capability options are anchored to types of outages (major to catastrophic).

The holistic community energy resilience score for all 32 vital services may capture a
notable dispersion of low-to-high scores. A simple average will misrepresent the
resilience capacity of the community, even as a broad metric, if it has a number of services
with resilience capacity notably below the holistic average.

Because of this problem, the holistic community energy resilience score must reflect both
the central tendency of the data as well as the degree of convergence or dispersal within
it. We use the average resilience score for all 32 services as the measure of central
tendency. This is supplemented with three helpful dispersion measures:
 1. The interquartile range (the 25th and 75th percentile values). The middle half of all
 scores will fall within the interquartile range. This helps to convey where most of
 the community resilience scores fall around the mean, and it is intuitively MUCH
 easier to understand than the standard deviation for non-experts. The lower and
 upper limit of the 25th and 75th percentile also indicate how “tight” or “loose” the
                                         Page 32 of 42

 spread of scores is. For example, it can indicate that there is a large amount of
 convergence in the scores or that they are widely spread and less consistent.
 2. The proportion of scores that are 3, indicating high resilience and preparedness
 for extreme-to-potentially catastrophic outages.
 3. The proportion of scores that are <0.5, indicating no back up power at all or weak
 resilience in terms of preparedness for extended power outages lasting longer than
 a few hours.
 Our scoring tool automatically generates these holistic community energy resilience
indicators, explains what they are, and interprets the implication/meaning of each metric.

Our final set of resilience metrics is largely the same as the pilot set. We obtained useful
feedback from communities and other stakeholders in the project. We dropped two
specific vital community services: the presence of a local electric utility and the presence
of a K-12 school. These were dropped for several reasons. First, very few communities
have a municipal utility, so it was not a helpful item. Second, we were focusing on social
resilience and not the technical resilience of the power system. In terms of schools, they
do not support our definition of a vital energy service (health, safety, and physical well-
being) and some communities pointed out that providing power to schools during an
extended outage would simply not be a priority and that schools generally stay closed
during such outages. This simplification gave us a balanced set of 8 vital services for
each of the four functions.

The other change to the metrics was in the resilience scoring. The pilot scoring tool
defined the lowest type of backup power as “a day or less” and assigned it a score of 0.
(Because we wanted to clearly differentiate between backup power for an ordinary outage
versus an extended outage.)

Based on feedback from communities in the pilot study, we added the category “no
backup power at all” (a score of 0) to better differentiate limited resilience capacity.
Backup of “a day or less” is now assigned a score of 0.5. These improvements now make
the availability of some backup power visible in the resilience scores and creates value
for them.

Subtask 4.2: Resilience Metrics Tool Creation

Subtask Summary: A tool will be developed for communities to assess energy resilience
based off metrics and data inputs from the ERM. This tool will consist of a step-by-step
use description and user-friendly spreadsheet that will display data categories and
generate at least one energy resilience score, and provide feedback based on
interpretation of the score. This may include suggestions such as increasing the
availability of energy resilience hubs, upgrading aging energy infrastructure, or providing
energy storage capacity at emergency shelters. The tool will allow community and state
stakeholders to assess the status of energy resilience in the community and guide
investments and planning efforts during and after the program’s completion. As energy
infrastructure is developed in communities to address resilience needs, spreadsheets and

                                       Page 33 of 42

scores may be updated by localities. Tool creation will begin following the completion of
the resilience metrics. The objective for this tool is to create a replicable assessment
method of community energy resilience for state agencies, local governments, community
emergency planning services, electric utilities, and planning offices.

Our Community Energy Resilience Analysis Toolkit is composed of three products:
(1) an automated, user-friendly Excel spreadsheet, (2) a very short companion booklet
that provides context and instructions for the spreadsheet, and (3) a “how to” guide for
looking up important demographic data from the Census Bureau and other online data
sources. Each is briefly summarized below.
 1. The Scoring Tool Spreadsheet. The community energy resilience scoring tool is
 embodied in the Excel spreadsheet. It is fully automated and standardized. Users
 enter community data for only 2 inputs to create their resilience scores: (1) A
 yes/no answer if a vital community service is present in their community, and (2)
 the available backup power/operating times of those services if the power goes
 out. Scores are calculated from this and interpretation of scores is automatically
 generated. (See Subtask 4.1 summary.). provides a screenshot of the worksheet
 for the Public Health and Safety function and vital services to illustrate the
 interface.

 The Excel file contains multiple tabs (spreadsheets): (a) a launch page of
 instructions, (b) 4 scoring sheets, one for each functional area, (c) a Vulnerability
 Assessment worksheet for identifying vulnerable community services and people,
 and (d) the Community Energy Resilience Score and interpretation. The
 Vulnerability Assessment worksheet also provides suggestions for addressing
 resilience needs and next steps.

 The Community Energy Resilience Score worksheet is fully automated and also
 serves as a printable “infographic.” It presents each of the four functional areas
 with their individual services and resilience scores (as well as the functional

 resilience score and dispersion indicators (see Task 4.1 summary) with
 interpretations of those as well.
 2. Companion Guidebook. The companion guidebook is a radically shortened

 3. How to Access Community Data. Vulnerability Assessment is necessary to do

 This information then drives the technical and cost decision making about what

Subtask 4.3: Resilience Data Collection and Analysis

Subtask Summary: The developed ERM will be applied to at least 35 HEDCs beyond
the initial testing in Task 1. Data collection will take place in collaboration with the team
members and an organization subcontracted for data input and analysis of each HEDC.
Each utility servicing a participating community will be engaged for the collection of ERM.
The Virginia Department of Emergency Management will be engaged for data resources
as appropriate. The objective for this data collection is to provide the needed information
to accurately fill out the developed ERM and for generation of an exportable Resilience
Metrics Tool (Task 4.2). This expanded collection and analysis of metric scores through
statistical testing (e.g. Friedman test with post-hoc tests) will identify energy resilience
strengths and weaknesses of communities in relationships to broader demographic
trends and infrastructure scenarios. The outcome of this subtask will provide the basis for
improvements of the tool developed in task 4.2 (Task 1.3) to be exportable for more
generalized use.

This task was not completed for the 35 communities. The partial completion of this item
was primarily a time issue due to the lack of an NCTE and the effort required for
successfully completing the case studies.

Nonetheless, the pilot testing of the metrics and scoring tool in Phase 1 provided robust
insights for finalizing a broadly usable and exportable tool both within the Commonwealth
of Virginia and other states. (Improvements and refinements to the metrics and scoring
tool were summarized in Subtask 4.1 and 4.2.) In addition, data from the pilot highlights
general limitations to the applicability of a community energy resilience scoring tool as
well as key resilience weaknesses in many communities.

Our pilot of the scoring tool and its worksheets allowed us to evaluate three different
issues. The first issue is the degree to which vital community services are present in
communities. Communities with limited local vital services have a low intrinsic capacity
for resilience simply because they have very few community services that could be
affected by a power outage. Our research and analysis detected a threshold effect based
on an analysis of 15 communities that were incorporated municipalities.

Our analysis shows that small communities (populations of ~500 or less) only have about
one-third of the 32 vital services used in our scoring tool (Sullivan 2025). They support
few, if any, public health and safety services (all but one did have fire protection). They
also had experience helping people with household vulnerabilities during a power outage
through informal and formal measures at a level comparable to larger localities. Small
towns also broadly lacked emergency management services, relying on the county to
provide this support. However, they often had a community emergency shelter.

                                        Page 35 of 42

This strongly suggests that there are scale effects at work 4. Small, incorporated
 3F

communities appear to have a specific, limited core of vital services to support their
communities with. Relatedly, small, unincorporated localities (those lacking a municipal
government) were almost impossible to recruit for our study. Such communities are
extremely resource constrained, lack legal authority to create many kinds of public
services, and offer few vital community services. The one unincorporated small town that
participated in our pilot study provided several types of emergency community and
“mutual aid” support during outages, such as shelters, food and necessities distribution,
and assistance to households with vulnerabilities. Small, communities may be an
important “special case” for energy resilience planning. They may need a different
approach to resilience scoring, capacity building, and planning that is more sensitive to
their scale issues and context than the final scoring tool developed for this project.

The second issue we evaluated was general patterns of resilience capability for 11
communities that were able to complete all or most of the scoring workbook with backup
power information. This is a small number, and the communities were quite varied by type
(large and small populations, a county, incorporated cities and towns, a census tract, and
an unincorporated small town). No statistical analysis could be credibly run on this
sample, but some of our insights are highly suggestive:

 • Half of the communities with public water supplies had operating capability of a
 day or less for their systems, making them unprepared for an outage longer than
 a day.
 • Just over half of communities with fire protection had operating capacity of a day
 or less for their systems, again making them unprepared for extended power
 outages.
 • There is no clear pattern about what kind of communities have better resilience
 (e.g., longer ranges of backup power for a wider range of vital services). A crude
 insight that we will explore more carefully for our final report is that they tend to be
 either (1) an independent city 5 or county, or (2) a place that has experienced
 4F

 multiple extended power outages or natural disasters in the past.
 • Resilience was strongest for community services that do not initially require
 electricity to function. These are mostly transportation and power equipment
 needs, such as debris clearing, transportation for special needs, and so on.

9. Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions:

Through working with communities in discussions and evaluation of metrics, one common
theme kept reappearing: community agency – defined in Section 7 as the capacity of
individuals or groups within a community to make choices, take intentional actions, and
influence the outcomes that impact their lives and environment. . Often communities of
any size are dependent on utilities to provide energy reliability and large-scale grid
resilience. By shifting away from working on what the utilities could do – often out of sight,
to what the communities have direct control over, the conversations produced actionable
items that may be addressed in future resilience planning. The concept of community
agency also identified weaknesses in our initial approach of including unincorporated
communities and census-block level communities in metrics testing and case study
inclusion – there were few if any controls over public health and safety, emergency
management, or other community infrastructure.

                                        Page 37 of 42

During this project, we changed focus to narrow research onto Virginia’s incorporated
towns, cities, counties as defined communities with some agency over resilience planning
and implementation and embraced the term ‘Community Energy Resilience’ over the
original concept of ‘Energy Resilience’. This narrowed the focus to actionable items that
a community could engage in to improve energy resilience and provided more direct
examples of solutions beyond the scope of this project (Kramer et al. 2023).

The development of the community energy resilience tool enabled communities to think
about the time and impacts of a power outage without having to be an expert in risk
analysis or engineering. The team made it clear from the beginning that the tool is not an
emergency management tool, nor will it make judgements about the most critical energy
vulnerabilities within the community. The tool provided the opportunity to identify gaps in
providing services or necessities in case of an emergency, along with identifying the times
that existing backup or redundancy systems supplied. In rural Buchanan County, diesel
generators were a preferred option, but there was acknowledgement that battery backup
could be useful for county or city diesel fuel pumps used in reserve tanks or from private
gas stations during prolonged outages such as during Hurricane Helene in 2024. This is
an area of further study and potential mobile solution design. Likewise, a prolonged water
outage in Richmond in summer 2025 emphasized the need for backup on municipal water
pumps (Richmond Water Crisis 2025). Mobile battery backup with addition of solar
charging capacity was a preferred option in six of the ten communities interviewed as part
of this project.
Though no economic analysis was included as part of this project, several examples of
weather-related energy impacts make a case for including cost/benefit analysis for future
community energy resilience projects.

From the analysis of the metrics:
1. Scale effects were at work on community energy resilience and vital services. Less
than 500 people in a community tend to have less than a third of vital services needed
for the community during an extended power outage.
2. The more resilient community services were ones that did not require to be connected
to the grid, for example, debris clearing or transportation.
3. Most communities with energy backup solutions tend to have only a day or less of
emergency backup available. This provides an opportunity to work on improvements in
energy resilience through target projects that address gaps identified by communities
through the use of the tool or general discussions. Adoption of mobile energy backup
solutions would move some scores from 0 or 0.5 to 3 to 4 with specific metric criteria
depending on the adopted solution. At present we did not include how to incorporate

10. Path Forward:

In many HEDCs, the capacity to address energy resiliency challenges may not be
available due to limited community resources and the presence of other priorities
including economic, environmental or health concerns. It is challenging for communities
to plan for the future, when they must dedicate capacity towards other issues being faced

                                       Page 38 of 42

today. This project developed a tool that will enable stakeholders to identify energy
resiliency gaps and improvements within their communities and support local efforts in
future energy resiliency planning. From a project level, some items were identified that
could extended within future studies:

 1. The need for clear economic cases or technoeconomic modeling for energy
 backup solutions for community energy resilience during extended power outages
 also emerged from this study and should be a focus of further study within small
 and rural communities. Tools exist to carry out this analysis including the DOE
 Interruption Cost Estimate (ICE) Calculator 6 or the Customer Damage Function
 5F

 Calculator 7.  6F

 2. Additional support for small communities to navigate state and federal grant
 requirements.
 3. Assessment of mobile solutions for gas and water pumps and impacts on
 community energy resilience metrics and the analysis tool. This was not included
 in the scope of the original design but was identified as an area that may impact
 multiple metrics and should be included in future revisions as a suggestion to
 communities in the tool and manual.
 4. Adaptation to unincorporated areas (areas of less community agency) and larger
 communities (>100,000 without splitting into tracts or subcommunities) where the
 complexity of systems may not be captured in the current tool and metrics.

Virginia Energy is funding a competitive Community Oriented Resilient Energy (CORE)
implementation grant for up to $400,000 in 2026. This state extension program could
allow Virginia Energy Resilience Study communities to fund several of the community
energy resilience projects they considered through the case studies.

The Community Energy Resilience Analysis Toolkit which includes the metrics will
continue to be made available by Virginia Energy and James Madison University and
may be tied into larger regional efforts and future programs to improve community
energy resiliency.
