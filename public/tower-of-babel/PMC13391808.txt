Automatic Speed Modulation Based on Real-Time Cardiac Activity Monitoring for a Next-Generation LVAD
Authors: Phong Hoang Tran, Nobuyuki Kurita, Chris Hoi Houng Chan, O. H. Frazier, Yaxin Wang, Joseph Cavallaro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeModern left ventricular assist devices (LVADs) remain associated with driveline infections and insufficient responsiveness to patient activity demands. Transitioning to wireless charging presents an opportunity to mitigate driveline infections, but using wireless charging systems and implanted battery storage necessitates reducing the LVAD’s power consumption. To address these challenges, a real-time automatic speed modulation control algorithm was developed that adjusts pump speed based solely on the energy required to maintain the LVAD’s magnetic levitation balance, obviating the need for additional sensors. This metric serves as a surrogate for patient activity state, enabling automatic pump speed modulation in response to changes in demand.MethodsThe algorithm was implemented and tested in a previously validated numerical mock circulatory loop (nMCL) coupled with a detailed LVAD model that accurately represents real-life magnetic levitation physics and control dynamics.ResultsComparative simulations of patient models with and without the automatic speed modulation showed that, during exercise, the controller-enhanced model achieved greater circulatory support, whereas during sleep, reduced pump speeds did not compromise hemodynamic output.ConclusionThese results indicate that the proposed control strategy not only optimizes energy usage—extending battery life and supporting wireless charging—but also confers physiological benefits by adjusting pump performance to meet varying patient demands. Furthermore, this work provides a promising framework for improving LVAD functionality and patient outcomes, with potential implications for future device design and clinical implementation.

Introduction

In the USA, cardiac disease remains the leading cause of death. In 2022 alone, 702,880 deaths were attributed to cardiac disease, accounting for approximately 21.4% of all deaths [1]. Traditionally, heart transplant has been recognized as the most effective treatment for end-stage cardiac disease. However, the supply of donor hearts is severely limited; in 2024, only 4711 donors were available [2]. Consequently, a substantial number of patients are placed on waiting lists, of whom 30% wait at least three months before a compatible donor heart becomes available [3].

This significant shortage of donor hearts has propelled left ventricular assist devices (LVADs) to the forefront as a critical bridge-to-transplant therapy. LVADs not only extend survival rates in patients awaiting transplantation [4–6] but are also increasingly used as a long-term solution for those with severe end-stage heart failure who are not candidates for heart transplantation [7–9].

Although LVAD technology is advancing more rapidly now than it did in its early years, several complications remain unresolved. These complications are particularly problematic for patients who are not eligible for transplantation and who therefore must depend on mechanical circulatory support for the remainder of their lives.

First, all LVADs require a continuous supply of power, necessitating the tunneling of a driveline through the abdominal wall. This creates a chronic open wound that never fully heals, increasing the risk of infection at the driveline interface and often necessitating prolonged antibiotic therapy [8, 10, 11].

Second, LVADs are primarily optimized to operate at a constant flow rate of 5–6 L/min, corresponding to resting conditions. However, because cardiac activity fluctuates with physiological demand, a fixed pump speed leads to performance mismatches; specifically, LVADs tend to overperform during sleep and underperform during physical activity. Overperformance can result in excessive blood extraction from the left ventricle, increasing the risk of suction events wherein the ventricular wall collapses and sustains damage [12–16]. Additionally, if the LVAD assumes the entirety of systemic circulation, preventing any blood flow through the aortic valve, stagnation can occur, potentially leading to aortic valve stenosis [17–20]. Conversely, underperformance during exertion limits circulatory capacity, thereby impairing oxygen and nutrient delivery and reducing exercise tolerance.

To address these issues, our research group is developing a next-generation centrifugal magnetic levitation (Maglev) LVAD that incorporates wireless power delivery and features an automatic speed modulation control system. The LVAD is 4 x 5.5 x 6 cm in dimensions excluding the inlet and outlet implanting extrusion with a 30 mm impeller diameter with target operational range between 2000 and 3000 RPM. The Maglev system consists of a permanent magnet (PM) that generates an upward force on the impeller and an actively controlled stator that applies a downward force to achieve magnetic levitation balance [21]. The stator consists of six independently controlled coils, allowing precise modulation of the levitation forces, tilt stabilization, and the torque required for impeller rotation while the radial position of the impeller is passively suspended by two opposite stators (Fig. 1). Blood enters the device from the left ventricle through a top inlet aligned with the vertical axis of the impeller and exits through a lateral outlet directed toward the aorta.Fig. 1Prototype LVAD with a magnetic levitation bearing

Prototype LVAD with a magnetic levitation bearing

This novel design aims not only to enhance overall energy efficiency by reducing speed during low-demand periods but also to improve hemodynamic performance during high-demand states by increasing pump speed. The speed modulation algorithm dynamically adjusts LVAD operation based on the patient’s activity state, thereby providing a more physiologically responsive support system.

Here, we describe the design and implementation of our custom automatic speed modulation control algorithm, which leverages LVAD levitational energy consumption as a key metric of physiologic demand. We used a previously validated numerical mock circulatory loop (nMCL) [22, 23] in conjunction with a digital LVAD model representing our work-in-progress prototype. The dynamic interaction between these models enables precise measurement of energy consumption across different patient activity states and LVAD settings, thereby deepening our understanding of the system’s behavior and facilitating the development of an automatic speed modulation algorithm.

Material and Methods

Simulink Comparable Model

The LVAD testing platform is replicated in a simulation model comprising a previously validated [23] nMCL coupled with a computational LVAD model. The LVAD model includes three key components: the physical levitation system, the pump performance characteristics (HQ curve), and the rotational speed controller (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Coupling simulation model between a mock circulatory loop and a prototype LVAD

Coupling simulation model between a mock circulatory loop and a prototype LVAD

Impeller levitation in the LVAD system is governed by three primary forces: the force exerted by the upper PM, the force generated by the active stator, and the hydrodynamic force from blood flow. These forces are quantified with a static force test rig for the magnet and stator, while computational fluid dynamics (CFD) is used to model the effects of blood flow. A proportional-integral-derivative (PID) controller is used to regulate these forces and ensure stable impeller levitation throughout the simulation. To enhance realism and account for measurement uncertainties, a white noise signal with a maximum amplitude of 0.01 mm is introduced into the gap sensor input, prompting the PID controller to respond accordingly. Finally, the energy required for levitation is transmitted to the automatic speed modulation algorithm inside the rotational speed controller, which dynamically adjusts the LVAD speed based on cardiac demand (Fig. 3A).Fig. 3A The simulated LVAD’s magnetic levitation system, B finite state machine (FSM) diagram of the automatic speed modulation algorithm

A The simulated LVAD’s magnetic levitation system, B finite state machine (FSM) diagram of the automatic speed modulation algorithm

To implement automatic speed modulation, the controller uses a deterministic Finite-State Machine (FSM) to classify patient activity into ‘Sleep,’ ‘Rest,’ or ‘Exercise’ modes (Fig. 3B). State transitions are governed by levitation current stability. The controller accumulates data over a discrete 30-second sampling interval. At the end of this interval, the mean current is computed. A transition is triggered only if this calculated mean falls within the energy zone of a different state. Following any state change, the system enters a 60-second refractory period during which no data is collected, ensuring hemodynamic stabilization before a new sampling cycle begins.

Static Force Characterization of the Magnetic Levitation System

The static force characteristics of the LVAD’s magnetic levitation system were systematically analyzed by measuring the force relationships at the upper and lower contacts of the impeller under different gap distances and applied currents. To quantify the static force at the upper contact (Fig. 4C), the impeller’s upper interface was immobilized while a PM was positioned using a high-precision micrometer (Manual Rotary Stages RTSS60, Misumi USA, Schaumburg, IL) (Fig. 4A). With the vertical axis precisely aligned, the initial gap distance between the upper contact and the PM was set to 1 mm. The resulting force at this configuration was measured with a force transducer (R/T Sensor, ATI Industrial Automation, Goodworth Drive Apex, NC), and the corresponding gap distance was recorded. This procedure was systematically repeated as the gap distance was progressively increased, thereby establishing a detailed profile of the force-distance relationship for the upper contact.Fig. 4A Static force test rig; B stator and impeller magnets used in lower force measurement; and C PM and impeller (top face) used in upper force measurement

A Static force test rig; B stator and impeller magnets used in lower force measurement; and C PM and impeller (top face) used in upper force measurement

An analogous measurement protocol was employed for the lower contact, which interfaces with the active stator (Fig. 4B). At each gap distance, the active stator was subjected to a range of control currents from − 2 to 2 A. The resulting force was measured and recorded for each combination of gap distance and current input, creating a 3D dataset. This dataset robustly represents the force-distance-current relationship for the lower contact.

Force-Induced Blood Flow Characterization

The interaction between blood flow and the impeller’s vertical forces was investigated through CFD simulations in ANSYS Fluent. The study controlled two key parameters: impeller rotational speed and blood flow rate (Fig. 5).Fig. 5CFD setup and measurement

CFD setup and measurement

The CFD model was initiated by extracting the flow domain and partitioning it into two distinct subdomains: an outer stationary domain and an inner rotating domain that includes the impeller. Boundary conditions were established as follows: the inlet was configured as a mass flow inlet, corresponding to the blood flow rate parameter, while the outlet was defined with a zero-pressure condition. The inner rotating domain was assigned a specified rotational speed, determined by the impeller revolution speed parameter.

The simulation was conducted as a transient study, with each timestep representing a 10-degree rotation of the impeller. Simulations were executed for at least ten full revolutions for each combination of impeller speed and blood flow rate.

To account for potential backflow events in the LVAD at low rotational speeds, additional simulations were performed with the inlet and outlet settings reversed. Because backflow is turbulent, the timestep was reduced to a 5-degree rotation, while all other simulation parameters remained consistent with those for normal flow conditions.

At the end of each simulation, the final timestamp was analyzed to quantify the net axial force exerted on the impeller. This analytical procedure was repeated for all combinations of rotational speed and flow rate, resulting in a comprehensive dataset reflecting the force dynamics under varying operational conditions.

LVAD HQ Characteristics

To assess the pressure head and flow (HQ) characteristics of the LVAD, the pump was fabricated with a 3D jet printer (Objet30 Pro, Stratasys, Waltham, MA), with minor modifications to enable operation with a brushless direct current (DC) motor (EC 45 flat, 70 W, with Hall sensors; Maxon Motors, Taunton, MA). The experimental setup involved varying the pump’s rotational speed within a range of 2000 to 3000 RPM. A pneumatic resistor was incrementally adjusted to simulate afterload conditions, during which the corresponding flow rates and pressure differentials were recorded. All experimental HQ curve data were then normalized to a rotational speed of 3000 RPM. The mean HQ characteristic curve was then calculated and used to simulate the LVAD’s hydraulic performance based on the pressure gradient and motor control speed input (Fig. 6).Fig. 6Hydraulic static test rig setup

Automatic Speed Modulation System

Blood flow through the pump fluctuates with the patient’s activity level, directly affecting the stability of the magnetic levitation system. These variations are reflected in the average energy required to sustain the magnetic field that supports the impeller. By analyzing this energy consumption, the system can infer the patient’s activity state and dynamically adjust the pump speed to meet physiological demands.

To tailor the pump’s response to individual patients, the nMCL was first calibrated on patient-specific data (Table 1) derived from the literature [12, 16, 24, 25]. This calibration ensures that the nMCL accurately replicates the hemodynamic conditions observed in heart failure patients. With the nMCL calibrated, the optimal pump speed (in RPM) for each activity state was determined. Initially, the nMCL simulated a resting patient with heart failure. The pump was activated, and its speed was adjusted until a target blood flow rate was achieved—either comparable to that of a healthy heart or representing the highest possible cardiac output—while ensuring that the aortic valve remained open. This criterion, which aids in preventing aortic stenosis, was satisfied when the LVAD flow rate remained lower than the total circulatory flow rate. After the target pump speeds were established for all activity states, a series of simulations were performed, pairing each state with its corresponding speed. Each simulation ran for three minutes, during which data on pump speed and levitation energy consumption were systematically recorded. These data served as the basis for developing a control algorithm that automatically adjusts the pump speed in response to changes in the patient’s activity state, ultimately optimizing hemodynamic performance.

To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed control strategy, an analysis was conducted to compare a static speed control method with the new automatic speed modulation algorithm. A predefined set of patient parameters was established for each activity state.

For the static speed control method, the nMCL simulation begins at rest, with the pump operating at a constant speed until the system reaches stability—defined as the point where the left and right heart flow rates converge, and systemic vascular resistance and pulmonary vascular resistance remain constant. The activity level is then sequentially adjusted to simulate sleep, followed by a return to rest, then transition to exercise, and finally back to rest. Each transition is followed by a stabilization period of approximately 2 min to ensure flow convergence before proceeding to the next stage.

For the automatic speed modulation, each activity state is simulated for a minimum of four minutes after the algorithm decides to change the speed and before transitioning to the next state. This duration ensures the controller’s ability to maintain the appropriate pump speed despite system noise and prevents erroneous state transitions. Hemodynamic data and pump performance metrics are continuously recorded throughout the simulation for subsequent analysis.

Levitation and Energy Usage at Different Activity Stages

With increasing rotational speed, the net axial hydraulic thrust increases. This external load acts to displace the impeller from its magnetic center. The active levitation control loop responds by increasing the coil current to generate a matching counter-force, thereby maintaining the impeller’s centered position against the rising hydraulic load.

For the current patient model, the most optimal pump speeds that achieved a comparable flow rate to that of a healthy patient model while ensuring aortic valve opening were 2250, 2450, and 3000 RPM for the sleeping, resting, and exercising states, respectively. Knowing these values enabled us to simulate all possible combinations of pump speed and activity state. For each combination of pump speed (2250, 2450, 3000 RPM) and activity state (sleep, rest, exercise), levitation current was sampled across n = 9 windows. Mean values are plotted in Fig. 7D with error bars representing 95% confidence intervals. Because baseline current scales with pump speed due to increased hydraulic load, separation between activity states is most meaningfully assessed at fixed RPM. Within each RPM condition, inter-state mean separations exceed the CI magnitude, confirming zone separability under the modeled sensor noise condition. The narrow confidence intervals reflect the deterministic nature of the simulation under bounded synthetic noise; empirical dispersion under clinically relevant disturbances will be characterized during hardware-in-the-loop validation.

In contrast, the automatic speed modulation adjusts the pump operation to better match the patient’s physiological demands. For the sleeping state, the LVAD speed is reduced to 2250 RPM, yielding an LVAD flow of 2.7 L/min and an aortic flow of 1.6 L/min, thereby achieving a total cardiac output comparable to the healthy baseline. For the exercise state, the pump speed is increased to 3000 RPM, resulting in an LVAD flow of 7.7 L/min while maintaining a stable aortic flow of 0.6 L/min. This adjustment results in the overall total circulatory output being 0.6 L/min higher than that observed under static operation. Notably, with the automatic speed modulation, the aortic flow remains within a narrow range (0.6–1.6 L/min) across different activity states, indicating more consistent LVAD support and a reduced dependency on the native heart’s variable contribution. This enhanced consistency is further evidenced by the PV loop comparisons, wherein the range of PV loops is narrower with the automatic speed modulation than with the static controller.

In addition to these hemodynamic improvements, the controller performed robustly in maintaining stability. Throughout each four-minute simulation stage, the controller reliably maintained the designated state without unexpected transitions. Moreover, the average sampling time was set to one minute—the time the pump typically requires to detect the need for a speed adjustment and to implement the change, ensuring a smooth hemodynamic transition between states.

Discussion

The automatic speed modulation algorithm was developed successfully and functioned as intended. In addition to reducing overall LVAD energy consumption, it reduces the load on the wireless charging module. Beyond energy efficiency, the controller also offers physiological benefits by dynamically adjusting pump speed based on patient activity levels. Ultimately, this integrated approach not only illuminates the intricate energy dynamics governing LVAD operation but also paves the way for improvements in device responsiveness and efficiency, potentially enhancing patient outcomes and reducing device-related complications.

Physiological Benefits

While the patient sleeps, the LVAD operates at a reduced speed, shifting the PV loop to the right. This reduction not only lowers the power required to sustain LVAD operation but may also offer additional benefits, such as preventing suction events and facilitating aortic valve opening to promote washout and reduce the risk of stenosis. Although the simulation patient model did not exhibit these adverse events, which would be evidenced by a positive end-systolic volume (indicating absence of suction) and an LVAD flow rate remaining less than total cardiac output (suggesting aortic valve opening), in clinical settings, patients are more likely to have suction events and compromised valve function [17–20]. Different states (e.g., Sleep vs. Exercise) alter mean central pressure, vascular resistance, and heart rate. Even at a fixed RPM, these hydraulic changes shift the impeller’s average position on the Axial Force Map (Fig. 7C). Moreover, under pulsatile flow, the impeller does not remain static; instead, it oscillates along a specific curve line on this map with every heartbeat. Since this Axial Force map exhibits a complex topology, the Maglev response tracks this dynamic trajectory, resulting in the observed flip relationship.

During light exercise, the controller adjusted the pump speed higher, making the total circulatory flow 0.6 L/min greater than what the static speed setting produced. Furthermore, analysis of the PV loop revealed a leftward shift of approximately 20 mL, indicating improved pressure alleviation in the left ventricle. Importantly, despite the higher pump speed during exercise, the total flow rate remained greater than the LVAD flow rate, ensuring that the aortic valve continued to open with each heartbeat (Fig. 8).

Power Usage Benefit

Reducing the LVAD speed during sleep substantially decreases power consumption, thereby prolonging the duration of each battery charge cycle. For a prototype LVAD that uses wireless charging, the implanted battery must be able to accommodate repeated charge and discharge cycles. Because battery life deteriorates over time with each cycle, lowering power usage during the long sleep period can significantly extend the effective lifespan of the battery. For example, if the device is intended for an average of five years of usage with a target of maintaining 80% charge capacity at the end of that period, power reduction during sleep can be crucial to achieving that goal. Although increased pump speed during exercise draws more power to meet higher circulatory demands, the duration of exercise is typically limited to one or two hours per day. In contrast, patients usually spend approximately eight hours asleep, meaning that the overall energy savings from reducing LVAD speed during sleep outweigh the additional power expenditure during exercise.

Effect of Noise on Activity State Prediction

The simulation environment represents an idealized scenario in which extracting specific waveform features for predicting patient activity state is relatively straightforward. This ideal scenario may not translate directly to the real-world environment, where the LVAD prototype’s control algorithm will encounter additional noise and variability. To approximate clinical conditions, Gaussian noise was introduced into the magnetic levitation model, resulting in raw energy usage data that more closely mimics what would be seen in the actual environment. Given that patients typically do not change activity states frequently, the algorithm can average energy measurements over extended periods and use signal filtering (e.g., moving average or low-pass filters) to nearly eliminate the effect of Gaussian noise.

Regarding non-Gaussian factors such as sensor drift, the eddy current sensors used in this prototype primarily exhibit minor thermal drift during the initial warm-up phase, unlike the continuous drift observed in Hall effect sensors. As demonstrated by the tight signal confinement in Fig. 7D, the separation between the “Sleep,” “Rest,” and “Exercise” energy zones is substantial. Consequently, a sensor calibration drift of 5–10% would result in only a minor baseline shift that remains statistically insignificant relative to the large safety margins between zones. The FSM logic, which relies on these broad confidence zones combined with time-averaged inputs, is therefore inherently robust against both thermal drift and impulsive disturbances.

Potential Drawback and Future Work

One potential drawback of the control algorithm is the risk of incorrect state prediction, wherein signal noise arising from aging electronic components or environmental interference causes the system to become locked in an inappropriate state. Such errors could have severe consequences; for instance, if the LVAD remains in the exercise state erroneously, the resulting excessively high pump speed might induce a suction event. The Axial Force Map (Fig. 7C) provides some physical context: suction events occur at sustained high-RPM operation under low-preload conditions, corresponding to a peripheral region of the map. Precise localization of the safety boundary on this map requires empirical hemodynamic data and will be performed during MCL validation (see below). Safety in the present implementation is enforced by clinician-prescribed setpoints with a hard ceiling at the maximum Exercise RPM, and by Back-EMF-based acute suction detection that automatically lowers pump speed upon detecting characteristic rotational irregularities.

Conversely, if the pump remains in the sleep state for an extended period, a timer mechanism could be introduced to prevent prolonged operation at reduced speed. In extreme cases, an external override might be necessary to ensure system reliability. Further investigation is needed to assess the clinical prevalence of these issues and refine the specific Back EMF thresholds for the prototype.

Another limitation of the control algorithm is its relatively slow response time. As shown in Fig. 8, after a change in activity state, the automatic speed modulation algorithm waits approximately one minute before initiating a speed adjustment. This deliberate delay is intended to mitigate state prediction errors, but it could result in the aortic valve remaining closed for a brief period—potentially leading to suction events during the transition from exercise to rest. This slow response may be less problematic in actual patients, as hemodynamic recovery from exercise to rest can take longer than four minutes [16]. In contrast, the nMCL transitions between states in about 30 seconds without any intermediate states, which underscores a discrepancy between the simulated and real-world patient dynamics.

Future work will focus on refining the model to better capture transient state transitions and reduce response delays. While the current method introduces a reliable modality for predicting cardiac output from Maglev energy consumption, additional intrinsic pump parameters, including total current draw and rotational speed, will be incorporated to develop a multimodal prediction framework. These enhancements will be integrated into a comprehensive validation roadmap once the physical Maglev LVAD prototype is completed. Specifically, we will utilize our established pneumatic Mock Circulatory Loop (MCL), first tuning it to replicate the patient-specific heart failure phenotypes (sleep, rest, and light exercise) modeled in this study. The physical prototype will then be integrated into the loop for comparative testing against standard static speed control modes. During this phase, validation metrics will include (1) state transition latency—the controller must initiate an appropriate pump speed adjustment within 2 minutes of the MCL beginning to shift hemodynamic parameters toward an increased or reduced activity level; and (2) state stability—once a new state is reached, the controller must maintain the corresponding RPM setpoint for at least 10 min without oscillating between adjacent state predictions or producing spurious transitions. This physical validation will be critical for assessing the controller’s robustness against real-world disturbances and verifying the system’s overall efficiency through high-fidelity hemodynamic and power metrics prior to future clinical assessments.
