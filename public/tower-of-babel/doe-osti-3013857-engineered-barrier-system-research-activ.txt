 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
This report describes research and development (R&D) activities conducted at Sandia National
Laboratories (SNL) during Fiscal Year 2025 (FY25) specifically related to the Engineered Barrier System
(EBS) Properties & Processes Work Package in the Spent Fuel and High-Level Waste Disposition
(SFHLWD) Campaign supported by the United States (U.S.) Department of Energy (DOE).

SNL activities focus on understanding EBS component evolution and interactions within the EBS, as well
as interactions between the host media and the EBS. The team represented in this report consists of
individuals from SNL, Argonne National Laboratory (Advanced Photon Source), and Vanderbilt
University. The activities represented in this report include development of anion sorbent materials for
buffer formulations, properties of pure bentonite and bentonite mixtures, and characterization of cement
interfaces relevant to the EBS. The SNL EBS activities also leverage interactions with EBS teams from
other national laboratories, and international collaborations to ensure that the DOE program is active and
abreast of the latest advances in nuclear waste disposal.

High-level Purpose of this work: A primary goal of the EBS work package is to advance the
development of process models that can be implemented directly within the Generic Disposal System
Analysis (GDSA) platform. Improved process models can contribute to the safety case in several ways,
such as:

 • building confidence and improving the robustness of the performance assessment,
 • providing further insight into the processes being modeled, establishing better constraints on
 parameters critical to repository performance, and
 • assessing technical readiness of EBS design components.

A major focus of this work is to understand the processes that will impact and control the permeability
and/or transport in the EBS. Permeability evolution in the geotechnical seal system is typically a major
driver of repository performance, or otherwise an important scenario that needs to be evaluated in self-
sealing host media. The interfaces of the EBS components, including the disturbed rock zone (DRZ), play
a critical role in the near-field environment, where complex processes have a significant impact on overall
transport and fate of radionuclides.

Improved understanding of these interfaces requires capabilities to characterize, test, and model the EBS,
in such a way that important complex phenomena are captured and represented. Important EBS process
are often coupled, e.g., thermal- hydrologic- mechanical-chemical (THMC), multi-scale (from molecular
to continuum scale), and nonlinear (e.g., multi-phase flow).

The FY25 SNL EBS report includes results from modeling, analysis, and experimental work and is
focused on the following key themes:

 • Experimental and computational investigation to understand near-field process models for
 radionuclide (particularly anionic Iodine-129) retention and transport through the EBS.
 • Development of sorbent material tailored for the selective sorption of anions, for consideration as
 an amendment to bentonite buffer to increase anionic radionuclide retention and retardation.

 • Improved understanding and model representation of coupled processes, e.g., thermal-hydrologic-
 mechanical-chemical (THMC), in EBS materials, with a particular focus on swelling behavior
 and high temperature effects on bentonite buffer materials.
 • Experimental characterization to understand near-field processes that alter the chemo-mechanical
 properties of cementitious materials in the EBS and DRZ.
These themes are investigated in various ways within the SNL EBS work package, with activities ranging
from molecular simulations, THMC modeling, to laboratory testing and characterization.

FY25 Accomplishments: Overall, significant progress has been made in FY25 towards developing the
modeling and analysis tools and experimental and characterization capabilities needed to investigate the
performance of EBS materials and the associated interactions in the drift and the surrounding near-field
environment under a variety of conditions, including high temperature regimes. This includes the
following accomplishments from the FY25 SNL EBS work package:

 • Particpation in the DECOVALEX2027 Sandwich task, involving THM modeling of bentonite
 swelling properties in bentonite-sand mixtures. Improving modelling capabilities and analysis
 tools for the numerical characterization and investigation of EBS components, the respective
 materials of which they are comprised (e.g. bentonite, anion sorbent material, host rock, sand,
 etc.), and the interactions within the EBS systems. SNL activities in this task ended in FY25.

 • Combined modeling and experimental methodology to develop materials tailored to sorption of
 Iodine-129 that are compatible with bentonite buffer material and stable under repository
 conditions. A manuscript summarizing these results was recently accepted for publication
        (Kruichak et al., 2025).

 • Development of novel, state-of-the-art workflow for analysis and characterization of chemo-
 mechanical process at EBS/host rock interfaces.

FY26 Priorities: Planned activitied in FY26 are focused on predicting the performance of EBS materials
relevant to a deep geologic repository experiencing high temperature regimes (200 – 300 C). This work
will benefit the safety case for direct disposal of DPCs by providing THMC properties for backfill
consisting of bentonite and possible amendments for enhanced thermal and/or chemical properties. The
effect of amendments will inform backfill design options for a hot repository. Specifically, FY26 work
will provide: 1) ion sorption parameters (Kd) related to backfill performance at temperatures up to 250 C;
and 2) mechanical and thermal performance of backfill mixtures to determine the extent of EBS
interactions with the host rock. Specific activities include the following:

 • Development and characterization of buffer and backfill materials including bentonite with and
 without amendments to enhance anion sorption and increase thermal conductivity.

 • Characterization of material THMC properties (mineralogy, ion sorption, hydration, swelling)
 before, during, and after heating (up to 250 C for weeks-to-months).

 ENGINEERED BARRIER SYSTEM RESEARCH
 ACTIVITIES AT SNL: FY25 REPORT
1. Introduction
This report describes research and development (R&D) activities conducted at Sandia National
Laboratories (SNL) during Fiscal Year 2025 (FY25) specifically related to the Engineered Barrier System
(EBS) Properties & Processes Work Package in the Spent Fuel and High-Level Waste Disposition
(SFHLWD) Campaign supported by the United States (U.S.) Department of Energy (DOE) Office of
Nuclear Energy (NE). This report fulfills the SFHLWD Campaign deliverable M3SF-25SN010308053.

SNL activities focus on understanding EBS component evolution and interactions within the EBS, as well
as interactions between the host media and the EBS. The R&D team represented in this report consists of
individuals from SNL, Argonne National Laboratory (Advanced Photon Source), and Vanderbilt
University. The activities represented in this report include development of anion sorbent materials for
buffer formulations, properties of pure bentonite and bentonite mixtures, and characterization of cement
interfaces relevant to the EBS. These activities complement the EBS activities at Lawrence Berkeley
National Laboratory (LBNL) and Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL). LBNL used modeling and
experiments to characterize properties of bentonite buffer, especially radionuclide retention and buffer
performance at repository conditions. LANL investigated the coupled thermal, chemical, and physical
behavior of interfaces between low-pH cement and bentonite buffer. The SNL EBS activities also
leverage international collaborations to ensure that the DOE program is active and abreast of the latest
advances in nuclear waste disposal.

development of process models that can be implemented directly within the Generic Disposal System

• Atomistic evaluation of iodide sorption complexes on modified boehmite materials (Section 2)
Section 2 presents investigations into characterization of batch sorption testing of an anionic sorbent
material that could be used as an additive to bentonite buffer or as part of the plugging/sealing systems in
the EBS. Anionic species (e.g., Iodine -129) typically drive repository performance assessments, as
transport of these species is not impeded as efficiently (relative to cationic species) by traditional buffer
formulations. In previous years, we reported synthesis, characterization, and molecular modeling studies
of layered AlOOH (boehmite) with butanediol modification to develop hybrid materials whose internal
and external surfaces can be altered for high selectivity for specific radionuclides. In this report, the
iodide uptake capacity of ion-exchanged glycoboehmite (GB) was investigated, showing how anion
sorption properties can be increased by material functionalization. The relevance of this activity to
repository performance is to provide a candidate material that could significantly enhance Iodine-129
retention in buffer and backfill formulations.

• Synthesis of glycoboehmite material variants from ethylene glycol to 1,6-hexanediol intercalated
 layers (Section 3)
Section 3 presents laboratory and molecular modeling activities aimed at exploring the material design
space of GB materials. In our previous materials development efforts, we used butanediol molecules that
contain a chain of 4 carbon atoms (C4) to create expanded interlayer spaces in boehmite, to enhance ion
sorption properties. In this report, we detail our efforts to characterize GB materials modified with shorter
and longer chains, with the intent to discover the effect of boehmite interlayer spacing on ion sorption
performance. Results indicate a systematic progression of material properties at the molecular scale using
increasingly longer diol moieties, but scanning electron microscopy (SEM) characterization indicates that
macro-scale properties such as particle size and surface area do not follow a simple trend. This insight
could lead to the development of materials with even better anion sorption properties. Similar to Section
2, the relevance of this activity to repository performance is to develop new materials for consideration as
bentonite amendments to improve the retention of Iodine-129 in the EBS.

• Hydro-mechanical simulation of the Sandwich hydraulic shaft sealing system experiment
 (Section 4)
Section 4 presents hydro-mechanical modeling to show the functionality of bentonite used for the sealing
segment in the sandwich sealing shaft concept, and to demonstrate the functionality of the small-scale
representation of the sandwich sealing shaft system. Modeling results show that swelling deformation of
the sealing segment is an important coupled process which leads to the reduction of pore-space and
permeability (potential flow pathways) in the sand layer with increasing swelling strain and compaction
of the adjacent bentonite layer. The relevance of this activity to repository performance is to demonstrate
that a layered combination of materials can improve the hydraulic properties of shaft sealing systems.

• Chemo-Mechanical Characterization of Cement-Rock Interfaces (Section 5)
Section 5 presents experimental characterization of cementitious materials in contact with a variety of
barrier materials and host rock at repository conditions. Cement liners are an important component in
repository designs as liners in drifts and tunnels during operation and emplacement of waste. These liners
will eventually be in contact with other barrier materials such as bentonite and host rock, as well as pore
water and potentially mobile radionuclides (aqueous or gaseous). It is therefore important to understand
the effects of chemical and thermal conditions expected in the repository environment on cement
interfaces. Results are presented for a multi-year characterization of cementitious materials in contact
with a variety of barrier materials at anticipated repository conditions. The relevance of this activity to
repository performance is to provide data on material properties at cement interfaces, so that these
properties can be used to more accurately predict repository performance.

2. ATOMISTIC EVALUATION OF IODIDE SORPTION COMPLEXES
 ON MODIFIED BOEHMITE MATERIALS
2.1 Motivation
Based on their low permeability and high sorption capacity for cations, bentonite materials are considered
as a backfill material in design concepts for deep geological repositories in argillite or crystalline host
rock. However, the fast transport of anionic radionuclides such as 129I through clay materials (van Loon
and Milbus, 2015) is a concern. Therefore, we have developed a novel anion sorbent based on layered
AlOOH (boehmite) as a potential EBS amendment to aid in 129I retention.

The FY24 report included characterization of a glycoboehmite (GB) sorbent consisting of boehmite layers
intercalated with butanediol molecules. Ion sorption studies showed that the Ni-modified variant (Ni-GB)
readily sorbs aqueous iodide (I−), although the mechanism of anion sorption remains unclear. In this
section, molecular simulation studies were combined with synchrotron X-ray absorption spectroscopy to
better understand the type of iodide surface complexes that are formed on Ni-GB.

2.2 Methods
2.2.1 Force Field Simulation
Classical molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed on Ni-GB models (Figure 1)
representative of external surfaces thought to play a primary role in anion sorption. Details of the GB
models, simulation methods, and validation are given in Greathouse et al. (2024). Two scenarios for the
sorption of Ni2+ and I− anions were considered: (1) the interlayer region containing butanediol groups; and
2) external edges. Simulations were performed in the presence of water at 25 C. Results were analyzed to
determine geometric parameters of Ni2+ and I− surface complexes that formed during the simulations.

2.2.2 DFT
Complementing classical MD simulations, quantum calculations consisting of density functional theory
(DFT) calculations at 0 K and ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations at room temperature
were carried out to determine the interlayer and edge properties of dry and hydrated GB functionalized
with butanediol groups. Atomistic detail provided by these results is needed to determine the interlayer
structure needed for performance control of GB materials. Structural models utilized in quantum
calculations were built from larger MD simulations. Models were initially relaxed at 0 K with DFT and
AIMD simulations were carried out at 298 K.
 First-principles calculations were conducted using spin-polarized density functional theory
implemented in the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) (Kresse & Furthmüller, 1996). The
exchange-correlation energy was calculated within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA)
(Perdew et al., 1992), with the parameterization of Perdew, Burke, and Ernzerhof (PBE) (Perdew, Burke,
and Ernzerhof, 1996). The interaction between valence electrons and ionic cores was described by the
projector augmented wave (PAW) method (Blöchl, 1994; Kresse et al., 1999). The valence electrons were
treated explicitly in the Kohn-Sham (KS) equations and the remaining core electrons together with the
nuclei were represented by PAW pseudopotentials. The KS equations were solved using the blocked
Davidson (Davidson, 1983) iterative matrix diagonalization scheme followed by the residual vector
minimization method. The plane-wave cutoff energy for the electronic wavefunctions was set to a value
of 600 eV, ensuring the total energy of the system to be converged to within 1 meV/atom. Ionic relaxation
by total-energy minimization of periodic simulation cells were conducted at fixed volume, without
symmetry constraint applied, using as starting geometries the structures obtained from MD simulations.
All AIMD simulations were carried out at constant cell volume (NVT) at 298 K.

2.4 Summary and Future Work
The combination of molecular modeling and X-ray absorption spectroscopy have identified potential
binding sites for cation and anion adsorption on Ni-modified GB. As detailed in our FY24 report, this
material is a promising anion sorbent for consideration as an amendment for bentonite backfill. The
modeling results indicate that the most promising region for Ni-I interaction (and iodide uptake) occurs in
the interlayer region, which is populated with butanediol molecules. However, the similarity in EXAFS
spectra for samples with and without sorbed iodide indicate that the iodide sorption mechanism does not
involve interactions with Ni2+. These spectra are similar to reference spectra for other Ni oxyhydroxides,
suggesting that modification with Ni2+ results in GB particles being coated by a thin layer rich in Ni,
providing a new surface for strong iodide sorption.

3. SYNTHESIS OF GLYCOBOEHMITE MATERIAL VARIANTS FROM
 ETHYLENE GLYCOL TO 1,6-HEXANEDIOL INTERCALATED
 LAYERS

3.1 Introduction
Glycoboehmite (GB) materials are a hybrid organic-inorganic material based on the layered structure of
boehmite. Using glycothermal solvents and synthesis measures, there is a series of hybrid variants which
have been produced by the group of Inoue et al. (1989). These materials integrate the non-ionic glycol
solvent molecules within the interlayer structure of the boehmite material (an AlOOH phase) and have
been shown to have new surface charging and ion adsorption capacities. Variants containing carbon
chains with 2 carbon atoms (C2) up to 6 carbon atoms (C6) have been published, but the impact of these
alterations in crystal structure regarding solution species behavior have never been characterized. Bell et
al. (2021) showed that the interlayer space could be intercalated with polymers during synthesis, and
Kruichak et al. (2025) found that novel ion adsorption of Ni2+ from aqueous solution forms a derivative
that can be applied to anion capture from solution. The prior work of Inoue et al. (1989) commonly used
gibbsite as a precursor which contains Na+ species, whereas Bell et al. (2021) applied KOH as a
mineralizer to synthesize the materials. To form these materials in the higher glycols (C5, C6), gibbsite is
less effective, as the species transport is expected to relate to aqueous speciation under basic conditions,
where ion solubility is poor as the glycol chain length is increased. The approach of Inoue et al. (1989) is
to use a sol-gel precursor as Al-tri-isopropoxide, which has improved solubility in the more non-polar
conditions. However, the effect of mineralizer as KOH was not previously determined. In this work, the
full series of glycoboehmites in KOH mineralizer condition was synthesized from Al-tri-isopropoxide.
The materials are characterized for crystal phase, organic content, and growth morphology using powder
X-ray diffraction (XRD), FTIR characterization, and scanning electron microscopy.

3.2 Glycothermal Synthesis
Synthesis of GB materials is performed using a stirred 600 mL pressure vessel from Parr Instruments,
with a 4585 PID thermal controller with internal thermocouple. Glycol materials including ethylene
glycol (Reagent Plus > 99%), 1,3 propanediol (98%), 1,4 butanediol (Reagent Plus > 99%), 1,5
pentanediol (purum, >97%), and 1,6 hexanediol (99%) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. The
aluminum source was aluminum isopropoxide (99%). The pressure vessel was charged with 5.0 g of
aluminum isopropoxide, and a volume of 200 mL of solvent glycol was used in each reaction. Either 0.3 g
of KOH (ACS grade, 99%) or NaOH (99%) was used as mineralizer in each synthesis reaction. These
mixtures were loaded into the 316 stainless steel pressure vessel and sequentially placed under vacuum to
remove entrapped air bubbles for >20 minutes, then purged with Ar gas (UHP) to generate an inert
environment (also >20 minutes). The sealed vessel was treated to 250 °C for 6 hours under 200 rpm
stirring to form the GB product. Products were recovered using ethanol solvent with four washes based
on centrifugation (4000 rpm, 10 minutes) and redispersion in fresh solvent. The use of 1,6 hexanediol
solvent required higher temperature of 275 °C for reaction and was held at temperature for 6 hours and
recovered as with the other samples.

4. Hydro-Mechanical Simulation of the Sandwich Hydraulic Shaft
 Sealing System Experiment

4.1 Overview
This section summarizes modeling work performed as part of the DECOVALEX 2027 Sandwich task.
Barrier systems engineered to isolate hazardous and radioactive waste should contain various layers of
diverse barrier materials, e.g. compacted clay barriers, impermeable salt layers, concrete walls, hydraulic
barriers, host rock, and geotechnical barriers such as shaft seals, to provide redundant containment of
waste from the biosphere. Shaft sealing systems for nuclear waste repositories are constructed to limit
fluid inflow from the adjacent rock during the early stage after closure of the repository and to delay the
release of possibly contaminated fluid from the repository at later stages (Wieczorek et al., 2021).

The Sandwich sealing system, developed by Karlsruhe Institute of Technology-Competence Center for
Material Moisture (KIT-CMM) (Nüesch et al., 2002), is a promising concept of a shaft sealing system
that consists of alternating layers of bentonite and equipotential layers, e.g. fine sand. In contrast to
conventional shaft sealing systems where one material is used as the sealing element, the sandwich
sealing system combines components that have different hydraulic properties. The sealing layers (DS) are
the low hydraulic conductivity layers of the shaft sealing system (Figure 4-1a) and equipotential segments
(ES) are characterized by a higher hydraulic conductivity (Emmerich et al., 2019; Friedenberg & Hinze,
2024). With this design, fluid flow is expected to be distributed evenly within the ES over the cross-
section of the seal, and homogeneous hydration and sealing of the DS is projected to follow (Emmerich et
al., 2019). This sandwich shaft sealing concept is also expected to minimize water fingering and unstable
flows inside the system, and water bypass through pathways that interface between the sealing system, the
host rock, and the excavation damaged zone (Emmerich et al., 2019; Wieczorek et al., 2024). Within the
barrier system the DS ensures the required sealing properties, while the ES equalizes the wetting of the
DS thus contributing significantly to the stability of the seal (Emmerich et al., 2019).

It is imperative that the sealing function of the geotechnical barrier is thoroughly evaluated to ensure that
during the long-term storage of waste, the potential voids spaces and pathways for flow are closed, and
the protection expected from host rock convergence and swelling of the backfill is not impacted.
Underground laboratory investigations were executed to evaluate the sandwich shaft sealing system
performance, the saturation and swelling behavior of bentonites and bentonite-solid mixtures, solute
transport. These investigations also evaluated the resulting swelling pressure together with the fluid and
gas permeability in various laboratory experiments, which included swelling pressure tests, mini-
sandwich shaft sealing system tests, and as shown in Figure 4-1b, semi-technical scale in-situ experiments
at the Mont Terri rock laboratory (Emmerich et al., 2018, 2019 & 2021).

4.3 Modeling Methods
HM numerical experiments were undertaken for DECOVALEX 2027 Sandwich task step 0 with
COMSOL Multiphysics 6.3 to evaluate the coupled hydro-mechanical behavior of the sealing segment of
the sandwich shaft system. This involved calibration of the bentonite model which included assessing the
behavior of the DS and ES materials during hydration, development of the swelling pressure, alteration of
the permeability, and implementation of fully coupled fluid flow.

The sealing segment was modeled using the extended Barcelona Basic elasto-plastic material model and
the equipotential sand segment was modeled as a linear elastic material. The current developed model
only considers swelling pressure build-up and does not consider the double porosity structure of the
bentonite. The data obtained from calibration are compared against the experimental data which includes
swelling pressure tests, liquid saturation, permeability measurements for different installation dry
densities.

For fluid flow in the bentonite medium, the Richard’s equation can be applied for the unsaturated and
saturated flows within the pore space. The model defined within COMSOL was used to estimate the
saturation as a function of time, fluid pressure and space in both unsaturated and saturated conditions. To
capture the elasto-plastic behavior of the unsaturated soil material (bentonite), the BBMx, a
geomechanical constitutive model, was used (Alonso et al., 1990; Pedroso and Farias, 2011). The BBM
(Alonso et al., 1990), is a constitutive model that describes the stress-strain behaviour of partially
saturated soils. It was developed to simulate the loading of unsaturated and partially saturated soils by
incorporating an extra state variable for pore suction (COMSOL 2024; Pedroso & Farias, 2011). The
model is formulated within the framework of hardening plasticity using two independent sets of stress
variables: the excess of total stress over air pressure and the matric suction (Alonso et al., 1990). The
BBM (Alonso et al., 1990), incorporates the influence of matrix suction on the mechanical behavior by
directly relating the compressibility of the material to its matrix suction. This key feature allows the
modeling of expansion and/or collapse during variations of suction (Pedroso & Farias, 2011). For
partially and/or saturated soils such as bentonite, it represents how stiff the compacted structure is before
wetting.

The BBMx model is implemented in COMSOL to couple mechanical yield, plastic strain and suction
dependent hardening which are used to describe the swelling and collapse of unsaturated soils. The model
builds upon the original model, the BBM, put forth by Alonso, Gens & Josa (1990), but has been
extended to include thermal or suction coupling and improved hardening attributes. The suction value
depends on the liquid saturation of the soil, and it impacts the flow in the porous medium as well as the
deformation and stress distribution (COMSOL 2024; Pedroso & Farias, 2011). The BBMx was
implemented to avoid the numeral limitations of the BBM model and presents a smooth yield surface
with respect to stress and suction (Pedroso & Farias, 2011).

The COMSOL 6.3 implementation includes parameters obtained from the widely used soil water
retention curve, the Van Genuchten model (Van Genuchten 1980). These parameters were also
implemented within COMSOL 6.3 in the Richards equation interface, the suction dependent effective
saturation, relative permeability, liquid volume fraction, bulk modulus, and relevant densities.

5. CHEMO-MECHANICAL CHARACTERIZATION OF CEMENT-ROCK
 INTERFACES

5.1 Overview
Multi-barrier systems are employed all over the globe in a variety of applications: CO2 sequestration,
deep well storage and extraction (Newell and Carey, 2013; Hangs et al., 2016; Mangadlao et al., 2015),
nuclear waste disposal (Ewing et al., 2016), and a broad spectrum of engineered barrier materials and
structures (Ingraffea et al., 2014; Gaucher and Blanc, 2006). These engineered barrier materials are
designed to achieve specified safety functions, including but not limited to: structural integrity, hydraulic
containment, and contaminant retention (Ewing et al., 2016; Gruber et al., 2022; Allison et al., 2012).
Failure of the cementitious barrier at the interface with geological strata and other packing materials are
often caused by gradients in material properties (i.e. pore water pH, porosity, and mineral composition
and distribution), which promote mass transport and chemical reactions across the boundary. Interfaces in
long-term interactions may show spatial and temporal changes in porosity, solid volume, mineralogy,
hydration, and mechanical properties, all impacting the durability of the structure.

Over the course of this research, we successfully designed and implemented a consistent characterization
workflow for cementitious materials in contact with a variety of barrier materials at anticipated repository
conditions. These experimental data sets are being used to compare and validate the depth and reactions
predicted through geochemical speciation and mass transport models, ultimately improving the
parameterization of the simulations, and increasing the reliability of long-term durability estimates.

5.2 Objectives
The overall objective for this project is to provide and demonstrate methodology for determining
mechanistic-based estimates of long-term changes in the chemo-mechanical properties of cementitious
materials interfacing with host rocks and other barrier materials relevant to potential nuclear waste
disposal sites. This research (i) characterizes chemical and mechanical changes within the alteration zones
of the cement interfacing with geological materials after controlled aging processes; (ii) identifies
structural features and chemical reactions that affect the fate of radionuclides at the interface of dissimilar
materials; and (iii) provides a basis to verify predicted changes in major mineral phase composition and
distribution that affect the diffusion-controlled transport of tracer constituents using reactive transport
modeling.

5.4 Results
5.4.1 Oil-Shale-OPC Paste
The results published in the FY22 – FY24 milestone reports remain up to date for the oil shale-ordinary
Portland cement (OPC) paste interface. In FY25 the analysis for this interface has been finalized. The
identified alteration zone for this interface is comprised of a dissolution front (~100 µm thick) leading a
remineralization front (~200 µm thick) deeper into the cement. These identified fronts are shown relative
to the initial named zones in Figure 5-2.

November 7, 2025                                                                                         33

Figure 5-2. Diagram of cement alteration zone after 1 year of aging overlayed on representative BSE
 image.

After 1 year of aging, the combined remineralization front and dissolution front measured ~300 µm in
total, and vary in both physical and chemical properties relative to the bulk cement in a nominally
unreacted zone. Specifically, the remineralization front (Zone A) showed significant depletion of
portlandite (CH), a decrease in calcium wt.%, and an increase in carbon, sulfur, and silicon wt.% relative
to the bulk. This zone also showed an increase in porosity, coupled with a decrease in the mechanical
properties of the primary hydrates, which was ultimately attributed to an increase in nano-porosity, and a
decrease in the packing density of the available CSH phases. The dissolution front (Zone B) showed some
depletion of CH, a decrease in calcium wt.%, and an increase in carbon and sulfur wt.%. This zone also
showed a significant increase in porosity relative to the bulk, which is why it could not be accurately
tested using grid nanoindentation. These combined chemical and mechanical changes throughout the
alteration zone led the authors to believe that decalcification and sulfate attack are the primary
mechanisms of alteration, promoted by initial gradients in the pore water concentration of sulfur, silicon,
calcium, and hydroxide between the oil shale and the cement. Carbonation cannot be completely ruled out
as a contributing mechanism, but would likely be more aggressive as an alteration mechanism in
unsaturated conditions.

After 4 years of aging, the measured alteration zone has gone from a total depth of 300 m, to
approximately 600 m. This alteration zone shows similar changes, both chemically and mechanically, to
the 1-year sample, but has some differences in morphology, including a relative decrease in appearance of
porosity within the identified dissolution front (Figure 5-2, Zone B).

5.4.2 Marl-OPC Paste
The preliminary results published in the FY23 and FY24 milestone reports remain accurate and have been
further analyzed. Specifically, the analytical protocol developed for the oil shale-OPC paste interface
discussed above had to be modified to accommodate the variable alteration zone within this sample,
caused by the presence of a large epoxy-filled crack adjacent to the identified alteration zone. A
representative BSE image is shown in Figure 5-3.

November 7, 2025                                                                                        34

Figure 5-3. Diagram of characterized zones overlayed on representative BSE image.

To review the findings reported in FY24 (Matteo et al., 2025), the zone in contact with the marl showed
changes in both its chemical and mechanical properties relative to the bulk cement. While all the same
elements evaluated for the oil shale interface showed changes in wt.%, including calcium, carbon, sulfur,
and silicon, there were additional changes in aluminum, potassium, and sodium wt.% that occurred for
this marl-OPC paste interface. After analyzing the depth of this zone using an updated analytical
approach, it was determined that this alteration zone averages 50 ±10 m in depth. This zone is depleted
in portlandite (CH), as well as calcium, sulfur and aluminum wt.% and shows an increase in carbon,
sodium, potassium, and silicon wt.%. Similarly to the oil shale-OPC paste interface, this zone also shows
a decrease in the mechanical properties of the primary hydrates.

After 4 years of aging, the alteration zone measured about the same depth as the Marl-OPC paste sample
aged for 1-year. This zone also showed similar chemical and mechanical changes to the 1-year sample,
but did have some differences in morphology, including the presence of a “Zone B” which showed an
increase in porosity relative to the bulk, similar to the “Zone B” observed in the oil shale-OPC paste
interfaces. This result implies that the large crack observed in the 1-year marl-OPC paste sample is likely
the result of the cement matrix breaking down during the sample preparation process.

5.4.3 Hydrothermal Reactor Project
The objective of this research is to better understand the alterations that occurs at potential interfaces aged
at anticipated repository conditions. In many repositories, bentonite is a proposed buffer material that will
fill the space between the waste canister and the cementitious barrier/seal. While there exists some
literature on the degradation of the bentonite while in contact with cement, there has been limited insight
on the interactions and evolution of the cement itself under elevated temperature and pressure. The
gradients in material properties at the interface, combined with the impacts of residual heat off the spent
fuel canisters, has the potential to compromise the structural integrity and long-term durability of the
cement. In collaboration with Los Alamos National Laboratory and Vanderbilt University, an experiment
was designed to study the growth of an alteration zone in a low-alkaline cement paste at the interface with
bentonite clay. This interface was exposed at elevated temperature (120 ⁰C) and pressure (100 bar), and

November 7, 2025                                                                                            35

was fully saturated with synthetic argillaceous groundwater to mimic potential repository conditions.
Ultimately, this data set and analysis can be used to better understand the potential mechanisms of cement
degradation in these systems, improving the design of the cementitious barrier implemented for use, and
assisting with future site-specific model parameterization and validation.

Since the publication of the FY24 milestone report (Matteo et al., 2025), all planned reaction experiments
have been completed. Preliminary results for these experiments (Figure 5-4) show that mechanical
properties of the cement begin to show changes by day 7, but chemical alteration of the cement is not seen

5.5 Summary
The work contained in this report is the culmination of years of research and has resulted in the successful
characterization of multiple types of interfaces anticipated for underground multi-barrier system
applications. Since the publication of the last report, each project has completed the planned data
collection for each sample.

Both an oil shale-OPC paste and marl-OPC paste interface were characterized at 1-year and 4-years of
aging, revealing the formation of an alteration zone in the cement where it was in contact with the rock.
For the oil shale interface sample, the primary reaction mechanisms appear to be decalcification and
sulfate attack, resulting in an alteration zone measuring 300 m after 1 year, and 600 micron after 4 years.
For the marl-OPC paste interface, other degradation mechanisms appear to be influencing the growth of
the alteration zone, which measures roughly 50 m both at 1 year and 4 years of aging. The difference in
alteration zone development between the two interface types results from different mineral availability
and mobility within the rocks. Future work for this project might include characterization of other
potential interfaces, or an analysis of the movement of the tracers that were doped in the cement prior to
casting.

In the hydrothermal reactor project, a low alkalinity cement-bentonite clay interface was characterized
after 7-days and 30-days of exposure to synthetic groundwater at elevated temperatures. Initial results
indicate that mechanical properties of the cement were impacted by day 7 in the reactor, while the
chemical properties of the cement remained relatively unchanged until day 30 in the reactor. Ultimately,
these results are an early indicator that the cement is experiencing independent yet compounding
alterations from both the elevated temperature and saturation with a briny solution. Future work for this
project will involve the alteration of other cement mix designs or extending the exposure period of the
characterized low alkalinity cement.

In summary, the results from each of these activities provide invaluable insight into the performance of
cementitious barrier materials across a variety of field conditions by identifying the features most directly
influencing alteration. The work mimicked field conditions as close as possible, representing a fair
assessment of what a potential dissolution front reaction rate would be in-field. This knowledge can help
inform initial cement mix design, which could be modified to accommodate rock features that otherwise
might drive increased reaction and compromise the cement’s durability long-term.

6. CONCLUSIONS
FY25 Accomplishments: Overall, significant progress has been made in FY25 towards developing the
modeling and analysis tools and experimental and characterization capabilities needed to investigate the
performance of EBS materials and the associated interactions in the drift and the surrounding near-field
environment under a variety of conditions, including high temperature regimes. This includes the follwing
accomplishments from the FY25 SNL EBS work package:

 • Particpation in the DECOVALEX2027 Sandwich task, involving THM modeling of bentonite
 swelling properties in bentonite-sand mixtures. Improving modelling capabilities and analysis
 tools for the numerical characterization and investigation of EBS components, the respective
 materials of which they are comprised (e.g. bentonite, anion sorbent material, host rock, sand,
 etc.), and the interactions within the EBS systems. SNL activities in this task ended in FY25.

 • Combined modeling and experimental methodology to develop materials tailored to sorption of
 Iodine-129 that are compatible with bentonite buffer material and stable under repository
 conditions. A manuscript summarizing these results was recently accepted for publication
         (Kruichak et al., 2025).

 • Development of novel, state-of-the-art workflow for analysis and characterization of chemo-
 mechanical process at EBS/host rock interfaces.

FY26 Priorities: Planned activitied in FY26 are focused on predicting the performance of EBS materials
relevant to a deep geologic repository operating at high temperatures (200 – 300 C). This work will
benefit the safety case for direct disposal of DPCs by providing THMC properties for backfill consisting
of bentonite and possible amendments for enhanced thermal and/or chemical properties. The effect of
amendments will inform backfill design options for a hot repository. Specifically, FY26 work will
provide: 1) ion sorption parameters (Kd) related to backfill performance at temperatures up to 250 C; and
2) mechanical and thermal performance of backfill mixtures to determine the extent of EBS interactions
with the host rock. Specific activities include the following:

 • Development and characterization of buffer and backfill materials including bentonite with and
 without amendments to enhance anion sorption and increase thermal conductivity.

 • Characterization of material THMC properties (mineralogy, ion sorption, hydration, swelling)
 before, during, and after heating (up to 250 C for weeks-to-months).
