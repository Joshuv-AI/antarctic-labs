Meaning of work in the association between traumatic events experienced and posttraumatic growth among workers in South Korea: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Miyoung Kim, Sang Baek Koh, Yujin Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTraumatic experiences are associated with posttraumatic stress disorder and posttraumatic growth (PTG). Although meaning-making has been emphasized in PTG, little research has examined whether the meaning of work is involved in cross-sectional indirect associations between traumatic events experienced and PTG. In South Korea, research has focused mainly on events meeting the diagnostic criteria of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM), whereas events not meeting DSM criteria among workers remain underexamined.MethodsThis study measured traumatic events experienced, meaning of work, and PTG in 328 workers aged 20 years or older. Traumatic events experienced were classified as events meeting DSM-5 criteria or events not meeting these criteria. Cross-sectional indirect associations were examined using SPSS 21.0 and the PROCESS Macro. ResultsIn cross-sectional indirect-effect models, meaning of work showed a significant indirect association between the total number of traumatic events experienced and PTG. A significant indirect association through meaning of work was also observed for events not meeting DSM criteria, whereas no significant indirect association was found for events meeting DSM criteria. ConclusionsIn this worker sample, events not meeting DSM criteria were highly prevalent. Meaning of work may be cross-sectionally associated with how workers interpret and integrate such events. These exploratory findings suggest that meaning-centered approaches may complement workplace mental health interventions, although longitudinal studies are needed to test temporal and causal processes.Keywords: Traumatic events, Meaning of work, Posttraumatic growth, Workers, Cross-sectional studies

BACKGROUND

Human beings experience a variety of traumatic events over the course of their lives.1 Traumatic experiences have been recognized as a significant mental health issue associated with posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), physical symptoms, and impairments in social functioning. Although experiencing traumatic events can be accompanied by psychological distress that is difficult to recover from, it may also be associated with positive psychological changes as meaning in life and value systems are reconstructed.2 This transformation is referred to as posttraumatic growth (PTG). This is understood as a process in which individuals report new adaptation and growth while cognitively and emotionally restructuring distressing traumatic events.3-5

Existing research on traumatic events has primarily focused on events meeting the diagnostic criteria of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM). Research is relatively insufficient on events not meeting DSM criteria, which are everyday in nature but can still exert a psychological impact on individuals. However, recent studies have reported that events not meeting the criteria, such as interpersonal conflict, financial difficulties, and academic failure, significantly predict PTSD symptoms and are, in fact, experienced at a higher frequency.6-10 Some events not meeting DSM criteria may conceptually overlap with work-related psychosocial hazards, such as workplace bullying and emotional labor; however, in this study, they refer more broadly to life events occurring across both work and non-work domains. These findings highlight the limitations of restricting traumatic events to DSM criteria and raise the need to expand the scope of traumatic events considered in trauma research.9,10

With growing emphasis on trauma-informed care (TIC), studies have increasingly examined the frequency and types of traumatic events experienced in general population samples.11-14 Studies in South Korea likewise report that most adults experience at least one traumatic event over the course of their lifetime and, on average, experience multiple traumatic events.6,12,15 Furthermore, the average number of traumatic events experienced has shown a continuous upward trend. This suggests that the patterns of traumatic events experienced are also evolving amid social and environmental changes.8,12,13,15

Additionally, research on traumatic events has moved beyond whether individuals experienced such events to examine how these events are associated with psychological adjustment and quality of life. Studies on PTG emphasize that PTG is not determined solely by the number of traumatic events experienced; rather, psychological and social processes such as meaning reconstruction, rumination, and social support are considered relevant explanatory processes.16,17

In this context, meaning-making is regarded as an important process related to PTG.17-19 Previous research has examined PTG mainly in relation to meaning in life.20 Individuals with a stronger sense of meaning in life are more likely to reinterpret traumatic experiences positively and transform them into growth.21,22 However, meaning-making occurs within specific life contexts, and work is one of the central domains shaping adult life.23

Work is not merely a means of livelihood but an important psychosocial domain that provides opportunities for identity formation, maintenance of social relationships, and self-realization. In the psychology of working theory, individuals satisfy not only survival needs but also needs for social connectedness and self-determination through work, which is closely related to mental well-being.24 In fact, a growing body of research indicates that a higher perceived meaning of work is associated with greater life satisfaction, psychological flourishing, and mental health.25 By contrast, traumatic experiences can negatively affect occupational identity and role functioning, thereby influencing work adjustment and psychological stability.26

Therefore, in understanding the relationship between traumatic events experienced and PTG among workers, the meaning of work may be an important work-related factor involved in indirect associations.27,28 A positive relationship between occupational calling and PTG has also been identified.29 Furthermore, qualitative research suggests that individuals may report PTG while reinterpreting traumatic experiences through work, including restoring self-efficacy, rebuilding interpersonal trust, and contributing to others.30

Even when traumatic events experienced by workers are not directly work-related, workers may reinterpret and integrate such events through work, as work represents a central domain for adult identity, social roles, and competence. Thus, the meaning of work may be a context-specific meaning-making resource that is related to workers’ sense of self-worth and life continuity following such events.

Nevertheless, research directly analyzing the meaning of work in the relationship between traumatic events experienced and PTG remains very limited. Prior studies have primarily focused on specific occupational groups (e.g., firefighters, healthcare workers) or clinical populations, with relatively few studies targeting general workers. Moreover, research that distinguishes between events meeting DSM criteria and those not meeting DSM criteria when analyzing differences in their effects is also insufficient.

Therefore, this study examined the types and frequency of traumatic events experienced among workers and explored whether the meaning of work was involved in cross-sectional indirect associations between traumatic events experienced and PTG. By distinguishing between events meeting DSM criteria and those not meeting DSM criteria, the study aimed to clarify whether patterns of association with PTG differed by event type.

METHODS

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional survey examined indirect associations through meaning of work between traumatic events experienced by workers and PTG. Data were collected from September 4 to 14, 2025, using a self-report questionnaire administered via Google Forms through a professional online survey panel targeting workers aged 20 years or older.

A total of 357 responses were collected, and 328 were included in the final analysis after excluding 29 insincere responses. The final sample comprised 175 males (53.4%) and 153 females (46.6%), with a mean age of 39.3 years (standard deviation [SD]: 10.7).

Measurement

Traumatic events experienced were assessed using a list of traumatic events classified by Lee and Lee.6 The questionnaire was developed by modifying and supplementing the Life Events Checklist for DSM-5 (LEC-5)31 and the Korean version of the Lifetime Stressor Checklist-Revised (LSC-R-K).32 In this study, only directly experienced events were included in the analysis: 13 events meeting DSM diagnostic criteria and 22 events not meeting DSM criteria from the total list of 35 events.

PTG was measured using the Korean version of the Posttraumatic Growth Inventory-Expanded (PTGI-X).33 This scale consists of 25 items across five subfactors, with higher scores indicating higher levels of PTG. In this study, the internal consistency (Cronbach’s α) was 0.98.

The meaning of work was measured using the Korean version of the Work and Meaning Inventory (K-WAMI).34 This scale comprises 10 items that assess workers’ perceived meaning, value, and purpose in their work, with higher scores indicating greater perceived meaning of work. In this study, the internal consistency (Cronbach’s α) was 0.93.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and Hayes’ PROCESS Macro (v4.2).35 First, frequency analysis was conducted to examine the frequency of traumatic events experienced. Next, means, SD, skewness, kurtosis, and correlation coefficients were calculated for the main variables. The assumption of normality was evaluated using criteria of an absolute skewness value of 2 and an absolute kurtosis value of 7.36

To examine statistical indirect associations through meaning of work, PROCESS Macro Model 4 was employed with 5,000 bootstrap samples and 95% confidence intervals. The analyses were conducted in parallel across three models, with the total number of traumatic events experienced, the number of events meeting DSM criteria, and the number of events not meeting DSM criteria entered as independent variables, respectively.

Model explanatory power was evaluated using R² values. Multicollinearity was assessed using tolerance and variance inflation factor (VIF).

Ethics statement

The present study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Wonju Severance Christian Hospital (IRB No. CR326045). All participants provided electronic informed consent before completing the survey.

Indirect associations through meaning of work

The R² values for the mediator models predicting meaning of work were 0.0632 for the total number of traumatic events experienced, 0.0094 for events meeting DSM criteria, and 0.0620 for events not meeting DSM criteria. Multicollinearity was not indicated, with tolerance values ranging from 0.937 to 0.991 and VIF values ranging from 1.009 to 1.067.

In the model with the total number of traumatic events experienced as the independent variable, this variable was significantly positively associated with meaning of work (β = 0.7295, p < 0.001), and meaning of work was also significantly positively associated with PTG (β = 1.4402, p < 0.001). The direct association between the total number of traumatic events experienced and PTG remained significant after including meaning of work (β = 1.0695, p = 0.036). The 95% confidence interval for the statistical indirect association ranged from 0.5807 to 1.5651 and did not include 0 (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

The mean number of traumatic events experienced in this worker sample was 11.58, higher than the 4.5 reported by Chae (2022)13 and the 9.19 reported by Son et al. (2025)12.

The number of events not meeting DSM criteria was 7.78, which is higher than the mean number of events meeting DSM criteria (3.79), consistent with prior studies suggesting that events not meeting DSM criteria are also important factors related to adaptation among Korean adults.6,8 In terms of event types, the worker sample presented high frequencies not only for events meeting DSM criteria—such as exposure to toxic substances, fire or explosion, and transportation accidents—but also for events not meeting DSM criteria, including parental conflict, academic failure, conflict with teachers, and emotional or physical abuse or neglect. This suggests that restricting trauma assessment to events meeting DSM criteria makes it difficult to adequately capture the cumulative stress-related and relational events that occur in real-life contexts.9,10 It further supports the need for research and policy responses that encompass various traumatic events, particularly the development of TIC, beyond existing approaches centered on events meeting DSM criteria.9,37

The types of traumatic events experienced by workers presented some differences from the findings of Kim et al. (2019)38 and Lee and Lee (2024)6. These differences may be attributable to variations in participant characteristics, timing of the survey, or the criteria used to classify traumatic events. Moreover, the distribution of traumatic events experienced may vary depending on temporal, social, and environmental conditions, rather than being fixed to specific events. However, it is also possible that these differences are influenced by variations in the composition of traumatic event lists; therefore, future studies should conduct comparative research using more standardized measurement instruments.

The cross-sectional indirect-association analysis showed significant indirect associations through meaning of work in the models involving the total number of traumatic events experienced and events not meeting DSM criteria. These findings suggest that the association between the number of traumatic events experienced and PTG may not be understood solely in terms of event frequency; how workers interpret and integrate such events within life and work contexts may also be relevant. In particular, events not meeting DSM criteria may be associated with PTG when such events are interpreted through the meaning of work.17,19,20,25,28,29

By contrast, no significant indirect association through meaning of work was found for events meeting DSM criteria. This result should be interpreted in light of the non-significant association between events meeting DSM criteria and meaning of work (r = 0.097, p > 0.05) and the very low explanatory power of the model predicting meaning of work (R² = 0.0094). Similarly, Kim et al. (2019)38 reported that events meeting DSM criteria did not significantly predict PTG and that the indirect association through problem-focused coping was not significant. Although one possible explanation is that DSM-criterion events may be associated with psychological processes less closely tied to work-related meaning-making, this possibility was not directly tested because the present study did not assess trauma severity, diagnostic status, or time since trauma. Therefore, this interpretation should be regarded as a hypothesis for future research rather than a conclusion of the present study. This finding suggests the need for future research to examine characteristics of traumatic events, such as severity, in understanding not only PTSD but also PTG.9

These findings are consistent with research linking meaning in life and meaningful work to psychological adjustment and growth.23-26 However, because meaning in life was not measured, this study cannot determine whether meaning of work has a unique role beyond global meaning in life.16,21,22 Furthermore, the findings are consistent with theories suggesting that traumatic experiences may be associated with PTG through processes of interpretation and meaning-making.2,4,18 Finally, this study suggests that meaning-making may be relevant not only to meaning in life but also to the work context.19,28-30

In sum, these findings suggest that the association between traumatic events experienced and PTG may differ by event type. In particular, meaning of work was involved in significant cross-sectional indirect associations between events not meeting DSM criteria and PTG, suggesting that work-related meaning may be relevant to how workers interpret and integrate such events. This study contributes to PTG research by examining work-related meaning as a context-specific factor and by including events not meeting DSM criteria in analyses of traumatic events experienced by workers.

From a practical perspective, meaning-centered approaches should be understood as complementary to, rather than substitutes for, organizational prevention and clinical support. In workplace mental health interventions, priority should be given to preventing hazardous exposures, reducing psychosocial risks such as workplace bullying and excessive emotional labor, and ensuring access to appropriate psychological or medical care. Emphasizing the meaning of work should not be interpreted as encouraging early return to work, increasing work demands, or placing responsibility for recovery solely on individual workers. Within a safe and supportive organizational context, meaning-centered practices may include structured reflection on work values, opportunities to discuss role purpose and contribution, or integration of meaning-of-work components into broader psychosocial support programs. These practices should be considered one component of broader psychosocial support rather than a stand-alone intervention for workers' mental health. PTG should not be interpreted as an expected or required outcome after trauma. For workers experiencing persistent distress, symptom stabilization, safety, and professional support should be prioritized over growth-oriented approaches.

However, this study has several limitations. First, because this study used a cross-sectional design, temporal or causal relationships among traumatic events experienced, meaning of work, and PTG cannot be established. Reverse or reciprocal associations are also possible; for example, workers with higher PTG may retrospectively attribute greater meaning to their work.

Second, owing to issues related to the standardization of traumatic event measurement instruments, there are limitations in making comparisons across studies. Sufficient consistency in measurement has not been ensured because various traumatic event scales are often combined or event lists are expanded and modified. Future research should employ more standardized instruments for validation. Third, participants were recruited through a professional online survey panel over a relatively short period, which may have introduced selection bias related to internet access, motivation to participate, or psychological characteristics. In addition, office workers accounted for more than half of the sample, whereas other occupational groups were relatively underrepresented. Therefore, the demographic and occupational composition of the sample may have influenced the observed patterns of traumatic events experienced and may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader working population.

Finally, traumatic events experienced were not distinguished by domain, such as work-related versus personal domains. Future research should examine domain-specific traumatic events and their associations with PTG across industries. The present models were intentionally parsimonious, consisting of a single predictor, meaning of work, and PTG, and did not control for trauma-related or psychosocial factors such as elapsed time since trauma, perceived trauma severity, social support, baseline mental health status, organizational support, and personality traits. Therefore, the possibility that these unmeasured factors influenced the observed associations cannot be excluded, and the findings should be interpreted with caution. Therefore, the observed indirect associations should be interpreted as constrained and preliminary estimates rather than as a comprehensive explanatory model of PTG among workers. Exploring additional psychosocial variables associated with the relationship between traumatic events experienced and PTG is important, particularly because no significant indirect association through meaning of work was found for events meeting DSM criteria. Future studies should employ more comprehensive longitudinal or multivariable models that include trauma-related characteristics, meaning in life, social support, organizational support, baseline mental health status, and personality factors.

CONCLUSIONS

Traumatic events experienced were widely reported in this worker sample, particularly events not meeting DSM criteria. Meaning of work was associated with PTG and showed a significant cross-sectional indirect association in the relationship between events not meeting DSM criteria and PTG. These exploratory findings suggest that meaning of work may be relevant to how workers interpret and integrate such events, particularly events not meeting DSM criteria. However, longitudinal and more comprehensive studies are needed before drawing conclusions about temporal ordering, causal mediation, or intervention effects. Workplace mental health strategies should prioritize organizational prevention, safe working environments, and access to appropriate clinical support, while meaning-centered approaches may be considered as complementary support.
