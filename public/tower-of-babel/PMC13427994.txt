Risk of subsidence following ACDF with the coalition™ (Globus Medical) cage: a multivariable analysis identifying preoperative radiculopathy as the primary risk factor
Authors: Santiago Valbuena, Alejandro Augusto Ortega Rodriguez, Pablo Benito Peña, Jordi Perez-Bovet, Jose Luis Caro Cardera, Jordi Manuel Rimbau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Coalition (Globus Medical) hybrid PEEK-titanium cage has been widely utilized in our practice for Anterior Cervical Discectomy and Fusion (ACDF) over a 5-year period. A preliminary review of all the cases that we performed in these five years show to us an unexpectedly high rate of postoperative cage subsidence with the Coalition (Globus Medical) stand-alone cage. This prompted a formal investigation, the purpose of this study was, therefore, to systematically determine the true rate of subsidence for the Coalition cage in our cohort and to identify the independent risk factors that could explain this finding.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of 70 consecutive patients who underwent ACDF with the Coalition cage between 2018 and 2023. Data on demographic, clinical, and surgical variables were collected. The primary outcome was cage subsidence (≥ 3 mm) assessed on lateral radiographs at a mean follow-up of 28.8 months (range 12–48). Independent predictors were identified through a multivariable logistic regression model constructed using the purposeful selection of covariates strategy, a multi-step iterative process that accounts for both statistical significance and confounding effects.ResultsThe overall patient-based subsidence rate was 38.6% (27 of 70 patients) at a mean radiographic follow-up of 28.8 months. Interrater reliability for subsidence assessment was substantial (Kappa = 0.759). In the multivariable analysis, preoperative diagnosis of radiculopathy was identified as the only independent predictor of subsidence, with an Adjusted Odds Ratio of 3.51 (95% CI: 1.13–10.91, p = 0.029). Contrary to initial assumptions, multi-level surgery (p = 0.441), smoking status (p = 0.615), and implant dimensions were not significant predictors in this cohort.ConclusionThe Coalition cage demonstrated a high rate of subsidence in this series. A preoperative diagnosis of radiculopathy was the primary independent risk factor for this outcome, rather than multi-level fusion or specific implant characteristics. These findings suggest that the biomechanical requirements of disc height restoration in patients with radiculopathy may predispose them to higher subsidence rates when utilizing this specific standalone integrated fixation device.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00701-026-06859-7.

Introduction

Anterior Cervical Discectomy and Fusion (ACDF) is one of the most effective and widely performed surgical procedures for the treatment of degenerative cervical spine disease, yielding high rates of clinical success for both myelopathy and radiculopathy [8, 18]. Historically, the procedure involved the use of an interbody graft or cage combined with an anterior cervical plate to ensure stability [6]. To mitigate plate-related complications such as dysphagia, adjacent segment ossification, and hardware failure [5, 11], interbody cages with integrated fixation, like the Coalition (Globus Medical) hybrid PEEK-titanium cage, were developed [7].

A potential complication following ACDF is the subsidence of the interbody cage into the vertebral endplates. While a minor degree of settling is expected, significant subsidence can lead to a loss of foraminal height, recurrence of radicular symptoms, and sagittal malalignment [2, 17]. The reported rates of subsidence are variable and appear to be influenced by multiple factors, including patient characteristics, the biomechanical demands of the primary pathology, surgical technique, and implant design [9, 22]. Additionally, there is a lack of evidence reported in literature about subsidence with the Coalition Cage.

In a preliminary internal audit of 340 ACDF procedures performed at our institution over a 9-year period using various cervical cages, we observed an unusually high rate of subsidence specifically within the cohort of patients treated with the Coalition cage. This observation prompted a more formal investigation. Biomechanical factors like implant size and bone mineral density are well-documented [12, 23]. However, how primary clinical diagnosis and surgical variables interacts with these factors to influence subsidence risk is not yet well-understood. particularly regarding the biomechanical tension required to restore disc height in different clinical presentations. Therefore, the purpose of this study was twofold: first, to systematically determine the definitive rate of subsidence in a large cohort of patients treated with the Coalition cage, and second, to identify the independent clinical and surgical predictors that could explain this finding, with a particular focus on the preoperative diagnosis of radiculopathy versus myelopathy.

Material and methods

Study design and patient population

We conducted a retrospective analysis of 70 consecutive patients, we reviewed the medical and radiological records of all consecutive patients who underwent a one- or two-level Anterior Cervical Discectomy and Fusion (ACDF) using the Coalition hybrid PEEK-titanium cage (Globus Medical, Audubon, PA, USA) between February 2018 and September 2023. This study was performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments. The institutional review board of the Hospital Universitari de Girona Dr. Josep Trueta approved this study.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) adult patients with degenerative cervical spine disease (myelopathy or radiculopathy), (2) treatment with ACDF using only the Coalition cage, and (3) a minimum but not maximum radiographic follow-up of 12 months. Patients with previous cervical spine surgery, traumatic fractures, or incomplete medical records were excluded from the analysis.

Data collection and variables

A comprehensive per-implant dataset was created from the patient records. For patients with two-level surgery, each implant was treated as a separate case. Demographic data included age at the time of surgery and sex. Patient-related risk factors, specifically smoking status (categorized as active smoker or non-smoker), were also recorded. Clinical variables included the primary preoperative diagnosis, categorized as either cervical myelopathy or cervical radiculopathy based on clinical (Nurick Score) and radiological findings (Spinal cord or nerve root compression on MRI), In cases where doubts persisted, neurophysiological studies were used. Surgical and biomechanical variables included: (1) whether the implant was part of a single-level or multi-level surgery; (2) the implant height (in mm); (3) the implant footprint area (width x length, in mm2), calculated from the product description; and (4) the preoperative disc height (in mm), measured on sagittal T2-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

Outcome assessment

The primary outcome of the study was cage subsidence. This was assessed on lateral cervical radiographs taken at the last follow-up visit, with a minimum but not maximum follow up of 12 months. Subsidence was generally defined as ≥ 3 mm loss of height comparing the direct postoperative intervertebral height with the intervertebral height at the last follow-up moment [16], this was independently assessed on lateral cervical radiographs by two raters: a consultant neuroradiologist with 7 years of experience and a senior neurosurgeon with 10 years of experience. They agree this measure was performed from the upper-level endplate to inferior level endplate (Fig. 1) to avoid vision and measurement errors caused by ossification of the intervertebral space in the x-rays. Interrater reliability for the binary assessment (subsidence YES/NO) was evaluated using Cohen’s Kappa coefficient, yielding a substantial agreement of 0.759 (95% CI: 0.61–0.91). In the eight cases where initial disagreement occurred, a final consensus was reached through joint review to determine the definitive classification used in the analysis. Postoperative clinical follow-up was difficult to perform due to significant observational biases. In cases of myelopathy, the assessment focuses on whether the myelopathy symptoms improve, but not on whether radiculopathy due to foraminal stenosis secondary to subsidence might appear de novo postoperatively. In cases of preoperative radiculopathy, it is not assessed postoperatively whether persistent symptoms could be due to foraminal stenosis secondary to subsidence or if the patient simply already has chronic nerve root damage.Fig. 1Example of a post-operative subsidence, left one x-ray after 24 months follow up and the right one x-ray the day after surgery

Statistical analysis

To identify independent risk factors for cage subsidence, a multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed following the purposeful selection of covariates strategy [4]. The selection process consisted of the following steps:

Univariate screening: A univariate analysis was conducted for all collected variables. Adjusted Odds Ratios (aOR) with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.9) with the statsmodels and pandas libraries.

Results

Patient demographics and surgical characteristics

A total of 70 patients who underwent ACDF with the Coalition cage were included in the final analysis. The mean age of the cohort was 57.9 ± 11.0 years, and 61.4% (n = 43) were male. The preoperative diagnosis was myelopathy in 72.9% (n = 51) of cases and radiculopathy in the remaining 27.1% (n = 19). A significant portion of the cohort, 38.6% (n = 27), were active smokers. Regarding the surgical variables, 31.4% (n = 22) of patients underwent a two-level procedure requiring two implants. The mean cage height used was 6.0 ± 0.6 mm, and the mean footprint area of the cages was 194.1 ± 28.2 mm2. A summary of the cohort's baseline characteristics is presented in Table 1. Univariate analysis of risk factors for subsidence

Regarding surgical levels, patients who received two implants had a subsidence rate of 45.4% (10 of 22), compared to 35.4% (17 of 48) in those who received a single implant (p = 0.441). Additionally, smoking status was not significantly associated with subsidence in this cohort (p = 0.615). No significant differences were observed for age, sex, or cage dimensions.

Multivariable analysis

To identify independent predictors of subsidence, we implemented the purposeful selection of covariates strategy. Preoperative diagnosis, which met the univariate screening criterion (p < 0.25), was included in the initial multivariable model. Additionally, age and the number of surgical levels were incorporated into the model-building process due to their established clinical and biomechanical relevance in ACDF outcomes.

During the iterative refinement, covariates were evaluated for both statistical significance and confounding effects (defined as a change > 20% in the primary predictor’s coefficient). The final multivariable model confirmed that preoperative diagnosis was the only statistically significant independent predictor of cage subsidence in this cohort. Patients with a preoperative diagnosis of radiculopathy had 3.5 times the odds of developing subsidence compared to those with myelopathy (Adjusted Odds Ratio [aOR] 3.51, 95% CI: 1.13–10.91, p = 0.029). Neither age, smoking status, nor the number of prostheses remained as independent predictors in the final refined model (Table 3). Discussion

The rate of subsidence for the Coalition cage in our cohort was a notable 38.6%. Most systematic reviews and large cohort studies place the incidence of subsidence for standalone-type cages between 10 and 25% [5, 14]. Some studies focusing on specific PEEK cages have even reported rates as low as 7% [3], while others on zero-profile devices report similar variability [13, 19, 20]. While direct comparisons are complex, our findings suggest that the Coalition cage may be more susceptible to subsidence than other devices in clinical practice.

The role of preoperative diagnosis

The finding that radiculopathy was the only independent predictor of subsidence (aOR 3.51, p = 0.029) is particularly striking. Interestingly, our data did not show significant differences in average cage heights or preoperative disc heights between the radiculopathy and myelopathy cohorts. This suggests that the risk of subsidence is not merely a function of the final implant size but likely relates to the biomechanical 'effort' of the surgery and the nature of the decompression.

This finding may be explained by the biomechanical differences between these two conditions. Radiculopathy often stems from significant disc space collapse and foraminal stenosis; the surgical restoration of this height using a standalone cage like the Coalition may require higher distraction forces [10]. This increased tension, combined with the integrated fixation of the cage, could exert excessive pressure on the vertebral endplates, facilitating early subsidence [15, 21].

In cases of radiculopathy, the primary surgical objective is the restoration of foraminal height and the release of nerve root compression. Achieving this in a degenerative segment often requires higher transient intraoperative distraction forces. This increased tension can lead to higher pressure on the vertebral end-plates during the initial phases of graft incorporation, even if the final cage height remains standard.

Furthermore, the extent of end-plate preparation likely plays a critical role. Radiculopathy often requires more extensive milling of the uncinate process and the lateral margins of the end-plate to ensure adequate decompression. This more aggressive preparation, while clinically necessary, may compromise the structural integrity of the subchondral bone, specifically in the areas where the Coalition cage’s integrated fixation and footprint provide support. While the retrospective nature of this study prevents a precise quantification of milling depth, these systematic differences in surgical technique between radiculopathy and myelopathy cases provide a compelling explanation for the observed disparity in subsidence rates.

Surgical and biological factors

Interestingly, in this refined analysis, multi-level surgery was not identified as a significant independent predictor (p = 0.245) While the literature often associates multi-level procedures with increased biomechanical load, our data suggests that for the Coalition cage, the patient’s clinical presentation and the subsequent need for disc space distraction may be more influential than the number of segments fused. Similarly, although smoking is widely recognized as a biological contributor to implant failure and poor bone healing, it did not reach statistical significance in our multivariable model (p = 0.615). This underscores the importance of utilizing robust statistical methods, such as the Purposeful Selection of Covariates, to avoid overestimating the effect of clinically relevant variables that may not act as independent predictors in specific cohorts.

Biomechanical considerations

The risk of subsidence is also strongly influenced by bone mineral density and endplate integrity. Studies using Hounsfield Units have demonstrated a clear link between low bone quality and cage collapse [1, 23]. Furthermore, the role of intraoperative distraction remains a subject of debate; while some evidence suggests it as a risk factor, other studies have not found a definitive relationship. Our results align with the theory that patient-specific biological factors—reflected here by the clinical diagnosis—may be more predictive than the physical characteristics of the implant.

Relevance of subsidence

An important distinction must be made between radiographic subsidence and clinical failure. In this series, the subsidence rate was high (38.6%), but it did not translate into a direct need for revision surgery or acute neurological worsening in any patient. This 'silent' subsidence is consistent with previous reports were settling of the cage is common but often asymptomatic. However, as noted in the literature, subsidence > 3 mm is not benign; it leads to a reduction in foraminal height and can compromise long-term sagittal balance. In our cohort, the high rate of radiographic subsidence suggests that while the Coalition cage provides immediate stability, its long-term biomechanical behavior might necessitate closer monitoring, especially in patients where significant height restoration was achieved intraoperatively.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study is the adoption of the purposeful selection of covariates strategy, which provides a more robust statistical foundation by allowing for the retention of clinically essential variables, such as age (p = 0.118), that might otherwise be discarded by conventional significance thresholds (p < 0.05). This approach ensures that the independent effect of radiculopathy as a predictor of subsidence is accurately adjusted for potential confounding factors. However, our study is not without limitations. Its retrospective nature, single-center design, and relatively small sample size remain constraints. Additionally, surgical technique, particularly endplate preparation, is difficult to quantify objectively. Discrepancies regarding the extent of endplate milling exist even within a single department, creating a potential bias applicable to most spine surgery research.

Finally, the absence of standardized clinical outcome scores (such as VAS or NDI) at all follow-up points limited our ability to formally correlate the radiographic magnitude of subsidence with the patients' subjective symptom resolution. Despite these limitations, the high rate of radiographic subsidence observed suggests that the biomechanical behavior of the Coalition cage warrants careful consideration in clinical practice.

Furthermore, the assessment of bony fusion was not included in this study. While interbody fusion is a key goal of ACDF, its evaluation typically requires CT imaging to achieve high sensitivity and specificity. Given our retrospective design and institutional follow-up protocols, which prioritize plain radiographs to limit radiation exposure, a formal fusion analysis was not feasible. The absence of CT-based assessment is a limitation, as plain radiographs may occasionally underestimate subsidence rates or overlook early signs of pseudoartrosis. Future prospective studies utilizing CT imaging would be valuable to clarify whether the high rate of radiographic subsidence observed with the Coalition cage (38.6%) significantly impacts the achievement of solid bony fusion or if it represents a biomechanical settling that does not compromise clinical success.

Conclusion

Surgeons utilizing the Coalition cage should be aware of the high subsidence rate (38.6%), particularly in patients with a preoperative diagnosis of radiculopathy. These findings suggest that the clinical context and the biomechanical demands of height restoration in radiculopathy patients are more predictive of subsidence than traditional factors such as multi-level surgery or implant dimensions.
