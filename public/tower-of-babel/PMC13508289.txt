Skeletal porosity of a cold-water coral increases with decreasing aragonite saturation state along a depth gradient in the Mediterranean Sea
Authors: Fiorella Prada, Chiara Marchini, Paolo Montagna, Marco Taviani, Giorgia Maestrini, Arianna Mancuso, Valentina Di Fazio, Christian Ghiroldi, Andrea Simoni, Iryna Polishchuk, Boaz Pokroy, Raffaele Gattelli, Giuseppe Falini, Stefano Goffredo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCold-water corals (CWCs) are key ecosystem engineers that create complex three-dimensional habitats much like tropical reefs, but in deep, cold seas. However, like other reef-building systems, they are increasingly threatened by climate change and ocean acidification. CWC communities in the Mediterranean Sea may be especially vulnerable because these waters absorb more atmospheric CO2 than the global ocean, making it a mesocosm that mirrors broader global trends affecting marine life. Since calcification is energetically costly and likely becomes even more demanding as pH and carbonate ion availability decline, understanding how the decrease in aragonite saturation state (Ωarag) affects biomineralization is essential for predicting the future of these corals.ResultsHere, we investigated skeletal structural and compositional changes of the scleractinian CWC Desmophyllum dianthus along an Ωarag gradient in the Mediterranean Sea using specimens collected between 400 and 1200 m depth. Our findings indicate that skeletal porosity increases at the macro-scale with decreasing Ωarag, while micro- and nano-scale structural and compositional features remained unaffected.ConclusionsThe persistence of micro- and nano-scale skeletal features across an 800 m depth gradient suggests that D. dianthus maintains tight biological control over mineralization at these scales, even as Ωarag declines. This control does not extend to the macro-scale, where increasing porosity alters the skeleton’s overall architecture under lower Ωarag. D. dianthus thus appears to preserve the fundamental “building blocks” of its skeleton while changing its larger-scale structure, a decoupling that may make macro-scale porosity an early marker of acidification stress in CWCs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12915-026-02654-z.

Background

Calcium carbonate (CaCO3) is the most common biogenic mineral in the ocean [1, 2]. Scleractinian corals are recognized as the primary architects of reef accretion and have therefore attracted significant scientific attention, not least due to their key ecological role and their uncertain future in terms of coral reefs formation under escalating anthropogenic stress [3].

Carbonate saturation states (Ω) are hypothesized to significantly affect the biogeography of numerous calcifying species [4], including cold-water corals (CWCs) and other benthic calcifiers. These organisms are expected to be profoundly impacted over time by the ocean acidification (OA)-driven shoaling of the aragonite saturation horizon, defined as the depth where Ω reaches 1. Below this threshold, calcium carbonate is thermodynamically unstable and tends to dissolve [5–7]. Current data shows that CWC species may be already living near or below this threshold [8, 9]. Indeed, global habitat suitability analyses indicate that CWCs predominantly inhabit waters that are saturated or only marginally supersaturated with respect to aragonite [10]. Consistent with this pattern, model projections under high-emission scenarios (RCP8.5) suggest that the aragonite saturation horizon, averaged over the whole ocean, could shoal from ~ 1138 m to ~ 308 m by 2100, potentially exposing up to 73% of known CWC reefs to aragonite undersaturation [11].

The physiological response of these organisms to changing pH and aragonite saturation state (Ωarag) has so far been mainly investigated in laboratory studies under controlled conditions [e.g., [12–15], with several studies testing combined drivers such as increased temperature and pCO2 [12, 13, 16, 17]. However, in the natural environment, these and many other variables (e.g., oxygen concentration, salinity, nutrients, food availability) fluctuate simultaneously on diel to seasonal scales due to tides, internal waves, and advection, a dynamic complexity that laboratory experiments cannot replicate [18–20]. A complementary strategy to aquarium-based manipulation experiments for assessing coral sensitivity to changes in seawater chemistry is to investigate natural coral populations across broad gradients of depths and Ω [21–23]. Aragonite and calcite (Ωcalc) saturation states are calculated as the product of the concentrations of calcium (Ca2+) and carbonate (CO32−) ions divided by the apparent (i.e., relevant to seawater conditions) stoichiometric solubility product (K′sp):1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm\Omega=\frac{\left[Ca^{2+}\right]\;\left[CO_3^{2-}\right]}{K^{'}sp}$$\end{document}Ω=Ca2+CO32-K′sp

K′sp is a function of temperature, salinity, and pressure [24]. Specifically, K′sp increases with increasing pressure and salinity and decreasing temperature leading to decreasing Ωarag and Ωcalc with increasing depth [25, 26]. Aragonite and calcite formation and dissolution rates vary as a function of Ω: Ω < 1 favors dissolution, while Ω > 1 favors precipitation [27].

Coral growth is a composite of three related parameters: net calcification rate (net mass deposited) = bulk density (mass per volume unit) × linear extension rate (linear growth) [28–32]. Bulk density is generally defined as the result of the pattern in which the material is deposited and by the volume of skeletal voids it encloses (i.e., porosity; [33]. It can be influenced by environmental factors such as depth, temperature, pH, and water chemistry, which tend to vary across latitudinal gradients, as well as by biological characteristics including colony morphology, species-specific traits, and population-level variability [29, 32, 34–40]. The micro-density is another measure of the skeletal density, defined as the mass per unit volume of the material which composes the skeleton [41]. The micro-density cannot exceed the density of pure aragonite (2.94 mg/mm3; [33, 42] and is generally lower due to the presence of an intraskeletal organic matrix (OM), composed of a mixture of different macromolecules, including polysaccharides, lipids, and proteins, which is absent in abiotic carbonates [43, 44]. These organic molecules operate as a template for biomineral deposition, driving crystal growth and influencing biomineral micro-density [45]. Bulk density, porosity and micro-density are rarely examined in combination, despite the fact that they collectively describe how CaCO3 is deposited, structured, and modified at different spatial scales within the coral skeleton [32, 33, 38, 39, 41, 46]. These structural parameters, together with skeletal mineralogy (e.g., aragonite, calcite) and intraskeletal OM and water content (i.e., water structurally associated to organic compounds in the mineral lattice; [47], play a critical role in determining the mechanical properties of the skeleton and its capacity to resist both natural forces and anthropogenic disturbances [34, 35]. Moreover, Rietveld refinement of high-resolution powder X-ray diffraction data enables precise determination of crystallite size and lattice parameters, offering insight into the crystallographic structure of biogenic aragonite [46, 48]. These parameters reflect subtle, biologically mediated distortions of the unit cell influenced by environmental conditions (e.g., temperature, pH, and Ω) and physiological processes (e.g., incorporation of organic molecules and trace elements) [48–50]. Tracking skeletal structural and compositional changes across varying environmental conditions provides valuable proxies for coral responses to environmental stress, including OA [51–53]. Research on the effects of OA on coral skeletal structures has consistently demonstrated a general trend of increased skeletal porosity with decreasing pH. This pattern has been observed both in controlled laboratory experiments [e.g. [54], and in natural environments, such as CO2 vent sites [32, 46] and along depth-related pH gradients [55]. Specifically, skeletal porosity, measured by buoyant weight combined with time-domain nuclear magnetic resonance, increases with decreasing pH (pH 8.1–7.7) in the zooxanthellate coral Balanophyllia europaea living along a natural pH gradient at a Mediterranean CO2 vent [32]. On the other hand, bulk density and net calcification rates decline, while nanoscale structural features remain unaltered [32]. Similarly, porosity increases by up to ~ 40% in four tropical zooxanthellate species at CO2 seeps in Papua New Guinea (pH 8.0–7.7), with species-specific responses in micro-density and organic matrix content [46]. In a year-long laboratory experiment, two Mediterranean shallow-water azooxanthellate corals, Astroides calycularis and Leptopsammia pruvoti, show approximately 10% and 13% increases, respectively, in skeletal porosity, as measured by buoyant weight, under low pH (7.72), whereas skeletal micro-density remains unchanged in both species, suggesting species-specific energy reallocation strategies [54]. Similarly, reduced calcification in the tropical zooxanthellate Stylophora pistillata maintained for over a year under low pH conditions (pH 8.0–7.2) is coupled with increased porosity, assessed by micro-CT, through enlargement of corallite calyxes and thinning of skeletal elements [53]. Along a depth-related gradient in the Red Sea, micro-CT and synchrotron HRPXRD analyses of S. pistillata from shallow (5 m) to mesophotic (60 m) depths revealed depth-dependent changes in colony morphology and fine-scale skeletal features, including an increase in porosity with depth, linked to genomic regulation of biomineralization pathways [55]. Moreover, while recent work on CWCs has begun to address how OA compromises the mechanical integrity and porosity of dead, exposed skeletal framework at the reef scale [12, 56–59], the multi-scale effects of decreasing Ωarag on the macro-, micro-, and nano-scale skeletal features of living CWCs across natural environmental gradients remains poorly explored.

Here we examined how decreasing Ωarag influences the skeletal structural (micro-density, bulk density, and porosity) and compositional features (mineral composition, crystallite size, lattice parameters, and intraskeletal OM) of the CWC Desmophyllum dianthus Esper, 1794 (Fig. 1) collected along a wide depth gradient in the Mediterranean Sea. This solitary azooxanthellate scleractinian coral can occur in dense aggregations, contributing to reef framework development as true aggregate colonies [60]. Results

Biometric measurements, buoyant weight, Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA), High-Resolution Powder X-ray Diffraction (HRPXRD), Fourier-Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR), and Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectroscopy (ICP-OES) were combined to reveal possible changes in the multi-scale skeletal structural features of the CWC D. dianthus along an 800 m depth gradient in the Mediterranean Sea (Additional file 1: Table S1). Polyp length was chosen as the main biometric parameter, as it has been successfully used as a measure of size in biometric, reproductive biology, and population dynamics studies of several solitary scleractinian corals, including D. dianthus [63–67]. Polyp length did not correlate with bulk density or porosity, while micro-density exhibited a weak positive correlation only in Caulonia (R2 = 0.324, p < 0.05) and Medcor (R2 = 0.489, p < 0.001; (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). Although limited sample size may reduce statistical power at certain locations (e.g., Marcos and South Adriatic), this factor does not fully account for the observed patterns. For instance, Mema has a sample size nearly identical to Medcor (where a significant correlation is detected), yet no relationship is observed; similarly, Roccella possesses the largest sample size among all populations but still shows no significant correlation. These findings suggest that skeletal parameters do not change with size or age in this species. Bulk density correlated negatively with porosity in all locations (Additional file 1: Fig. S2). A significant positive correlation between skeletal bulk density and micro-density was observed only in Roccella (R2 = 0.089, p < 0.001), while in the other locations no correlation was found (Additional file 1: Fig. S2). No correlations were detected between micro-density and porosity across all locations (Additional file 1: Fig. S2).

Environmental (temperature, salinity, pH, Ωarag, ammonium, nitrate and phosphate) and skeletal (porosity, bulk density, and micro-density) parameters were significantly different among depths (Kruskal–Wallis test, p < 0.001; Tables 1 and 2). Ωarag was strongly correlated with pH (R2 = 0.558, p < 0.001) and temperature (R2 = 0.588, p < 0.001), but only weakly correlated with salinity (R2 = 0.010, p < 0.05; Additional file 1: Table S2). Ωarag declined by 15%, temperature by 9%, and salinity and pH by 0.3% with increasing depth (Table 1). Temperature was the only parameter to show a clear decline in variance with depth, while the variability of other parameters remained relatively constant (Additional file 1: Table S2). Ammonium concentrations decreased by 90%, nitrate by 14%, and phosphate by 12% (Table 1). All HRPXRD patterns were consistently indexed as aragonite (Additional file 1: Table S4), with no evidence of calcite, confirming preservation of the original mineral composition and the absence of detectable recrystallization across the depth gradient. Peaks were refined using the Rietveld method to calculate crystallographic axis, macrostrain, and crystallite size along 3 directions [(111), (021), (200)]. None of these parameters showed significant variations along the depth gradient.

FTIR analysis confirmed that the main calcium carbonate (CaCO3) polymorph was aragonite with trace amounts of calcite. No significant differences were observed along the depth gradient (Additional file 1: Fig. S3).

The intraskeletal OM and water contents did not vary significantly with depth (Kruskal–Wallis, p > 0.05; Additional file 1: Table S5). Among the elemental ratios measured in the skeletons (Additional file 1: Table S6), Sr/Ca increased progressively with depth, from 0.114 atom.% at Site 2 to 0.118 atom.% at Site 6 (South Adriatic). Mg/Ca showed an overall decrease with depth, while Na/Ca exhibited a general increase, although both ratios deviated from these trends at Site 5 (Medcor), where values were lower than at the other intermediate-depth sites. In contrast, Al/Ca, Fe/Ca, and K/Ca showed no coherent pattern with depth.

Discussion

This study investigated the relationship between skeletal structural and compositional features of the CWC D. dianthus and environmental conditions across a depth gradient in the Mediterranean Sea. With increasing depth, coral skeletal porosity increased by 37% and bulk density decreased by 12%, while micro-density showed a minor increase of 4%. Among the environmental variables considered, Ωarag, pH, and ammonium were significantly associated with the observed variation in skeletal parameters. Although site-specific effects cannot be ruled out entirely, given the observational nature of this study, sampling sites/depths are randomly distributed across regions (i.e., southern Adriatic Sea, Ionian Sea, and Strait of Sicily). If regional effects outweighed the depth-related gradient, we would not expect pH, Ωarag, and ammonium, all strongly correlated with depth (Additional file 1: Table S7), to emerge as the main drivers of skeletal variation across sites/depths from different regions. Notably, temperature is also strongly correlated with both depth (ρ = − 0.581, p < 0.001; Additional file 1: Table S7) and Ωarag (Additional file 1: Table S2), yet it was not identified as a significant predictor, indicating that the analysis is selective rather than simply flagging every variable that covaries with depth or with other significant predictors. Therefore, while we acknowledge that site-specific effects cannot be fully excluded, the consistency of the depth-related environmental signal suggests that any regional influence is secondary to the depth-related environmental gradient we describe.

Ocean acidification, characterized by the reduction of seawater pH and CO32− concentrations that ultimately lead to a lower Ω [68], raises considerable concern for marine calcifying organisms [69]. Both Ωarag and Ωcalc generally decrease with increasing pressure (i.e., depth) and decreasing temperature and pH [70, 71]. Given that scleractinian corals precipitate aragonite, Ωarag is the most relevant saturation state for assessing the effects of OA on their calcification dynamics. Although we acknowledge that the effects of pH and Ωarag cannot be entirely separated due to their inherent interdependence, the minimal variation in pH along the depth gradient (only a 0.2% difference, from 8.12 to 8.10) compared to Ωarag (ca. 15%), allowed us to specifically focus on Ωarag, and its relationship with the skeletal compositional and structural characteristics of D. dianthus.

In our study, skeletal porosity increased with decreasing Ωarag. This porous phenotype at depth might reflect an adaptation to deep-sea conditions, where constructing larger or denser skeletons likely becomes more energetically demanding as Ωarag declines [70]. The deep-sea coral family Micrabaciidae, consisting of solitary scleractinian corals that can be found up to 5,000 m depth, exemplifies this adaptation with their reduced, thin, and porous skeletons fully encased in tissue [72, 73]. This porous morphology may be the result of reduced net calcification rates under low Ωarag conditions, as observed in several OA studies [e.g., [32, 46, 53]. Decreased net calcification rates with decreasing Ωarag have been observed in shallow corals and CWCs, including D. dianthus [14, 53, 74]. Nonetheless, field and incubation studies on CWCs under acidified conditions show mixed results. Many studies to date have focused on the model CWC D. pertusum (syn. Lophelia pertusa; [75], the main framework-forming species of deep-sea coral reefs [76]. D. pertusum specimens collected at depths ranging between 280 and 620 m and 1.28–1.69 Ωarag in the Gulf of Mexico showed average skeletal micro-density of 2.81 g/cm3 with no significant changes related to Ωarag [23]. Likewise, D. pertusum and Madrepora oculata corals exposed to Ωarag ranging between 1.6 and 2.8 for six months in an aquaria experiment showed no significant changes in skeletal growth rate, micro-density, and porosity [15]. However, following 13 months in aquaria under Ωarag levels between 0.7 and 2.1, D. pertusum exhibited progressively lower net calcification with increasing acidification, ultimately reaching net dissolution in live corals under aragonite-undersaturated conditions [16]. Concurrently, degradation of dead coral skeletons via dissolution and bioerosion increases with decreasing Ωarag [16]. Indeed, several studies indicate that, once skeletal surfaces of D. pertusum are exposed, and are therefore no longer shielded by the protective tissue layer [77], aragonite-undersaturated conditions drive increased porosity via accelerated dissolution and bioerosion as Ωarag declines [16, 58]. D. pertusum exposed framework subjected for one year to reduced Ωarag (~ 1.5–0.7) alone [12] and in combination with increased temperature [59], exhibits reduced breaking strength compared to live, tissue-protected skeleton. Consistent with this, a multiscale mechanical model combining electron back-scatter diffraction, micro-CT, and nanoindentation with molecular dynamics simulations, showed that although the skeletal building material of D. pertusum retains its strength under OA, small increases in porosity lead to significantly increased fragility at the colony scale [58]. Progressive weakening and loss of the dead framework would shift reefs from habitats with high three-dimensional (3D) complexity sustained by both living colonies and surrounding relic skeletons, to ones supported primarily by the remaining live colonies, with reduced 3D complexity, posing a major threat to future CWC reefs and the biodiversity they support [56]. Predicting the timescales of such collapse requires not only understanding the mechanical properties of CWC skeletons but also their morphological variability, as framework architecture is characterized by significant plasticity driven by local hydrodynamics, sea-floor topography, and environmental stressors [57, 78, 79]. Dissolution, however, cannot explain the porosity patterns observed in the D. dianthus from the present study, because Ωarag remained well above undersaturation. D. dianthus has been used as a model species in several studies investigating potential OA impacts on CWCs [22, 80, 81]. Indeed, D. dianthus has been observed in aragonite undersaturated waters on an Australian seamount [9], suggesting that this species might be less susceptible to low Ωarag than previously assumed [82]. A year-long reciprocal transplantation experiment conducted on D. dianthus in Comau Fjord showed that calcification rates of both native and cross-transplanted corals were significantly higher at 300 m compared to 20 m depth, likely due to lower environmental variability affecting deeper coral populations [22]. Net and gross calcification rates of three common CWCs, Caryophyllia smithii, Dendrophyllia cornigera, and D. dianthus, investigated in aquaria for three months, were not affected by low Ωarag (mean ± standard deviation; 1.3 ± 0.2; [21]. Moreover, the same study reported no changes in net calcification rates in D. dianthus transplanted to 350 m depth (Ωarag 2.6 ± 0.2) for 8 months and to a CO2 seep at 3 m depth off Ischia (Ωarag 0.76 ± 0.62) for 1.5 months. Assuming that the observed increase in porosity with decreasing Ωarag in D. dianthus from this study correlates with a decrease in net calcification rates, the contrasting results compared to [21] might be explained by the duration of the exposure to lower Ωarag levels. In fact, in the latter study, corals were exposed to reduced Ωarag levels for only a few months, which may not be sufficient to detect the changes observed in specimens exposed to lower aragonite saturation long-term like in the present one. One mechanism that may contribute to the variable calcification responses observed across species and environments is the ability of scleractinian corals to up-regulate pH at the site of calcification, thereby elevating the saturation state of the calcifying fluid relative to ambient seawater [83]. D. pertusum maintains internal calcification pH and skeletal morphology even under aragonite-undersaturated conditions [84]. However, the capacity for pH up-regulation is species-dependent and may be modulated by environmental context [85], such that differences in the degree to which corals can buffer their calcifying fluid against declining Ωarag could account for the contrasting calcification responses reported in the literature, including the porosity increase observed in the present study.

No correlations were found between pH and skeletal density or changes in mineralogy with depth in D. dianthus living near or below the aragonite saturation horizon on seamounts off Tasmania, Australia [9]. One possible explanation for the higher sensitivity observed in D. dianthus in our study compared to the findings of [9] may lie in differences in growth rates driven by regional temperature regimes. Previous studies have shown that D. dianthus corals inhabiting the relatively warm deep waters of the Mediterranean Sea (∼14 °C) can exhibit extension rates of ~ 1 mm/yr, up to an order of magnitude faster than those from colder Pacific sites, where deep-sea temperatures typically range around ~ 4 °C and growth rates fall below 0.2 mm/year [86–88]. Indeed, temperatures measured just above reefs on Tasmanian seamounts where D. dianthus was collected by [89] show a range of 2–14 °C, while temperatures at our Mediterranean study sites were consistently higher, ranging from 13 to 15 °C. Moreover, given an average pHT of 8.08, Total Alkalinity (TA) of ∼2600 μmol/kg, and Ωarag of ∼2.6 for the Mediterranean Sea [21], and an average pHT of 7.9, TA of ∼2200 μmol/kg, and Ωarag of ∼1.5 for the Pacific Ocean [70], predicted growth rates in the Mediterranean Sea would be 2- to fourfold higher than in the deep Pacific Ocean, which is consistent with observed rates [85, 87, 88]. It has been hypothesized that fast-growing corals might exhibit greater reductions in calcification due to their increased demand for carbonate [90, 91] or an increased need to dissipate hydrogen ions [92]. Thus, the higher growth rates of Mediterranean D. dianthus could ultimately make them more susceptible to decreasing Ωarag compared to those inhabiting the Pacific and Atlantic Oceans.

Although skeletal elemental composition did not show clear overall patterns with depth (Additional file 1: Table S6), individual elemental ratios revealed more subtle trends. The Sr/Ca ratio displayed an increase with depth, consistent with its well-established inverse relationship with seawater temperature in coral aragonite and supporting its use as a reliable environmental proxy [93–95]. This pattern suggests that the observed bathymetric gradient in Sr/Ca could reflect the decrease in temperature with increasing depth, although biological modulation cannot be excluded [96]. In contrast, Mg/Ca and Na/Ca exhibited weaker and non-linear relationships with depth, suggesting a stronger influence of biological (“vital”) effects and calcification kinetics, as commonly reported in deep-sea coral studies [97]. Fe, Al, and K showed no coherent bathymetric trend and are interpreted as predominantly detrital-associated elements, likely reflecting local sedimentary inputs and site-specific conditions rather than water mass properties [98]. These interpretations are based on a limited dataset (one specimen per site), as ICP-OES analyses are destructive and deep-sea coral samples from such depths are inherently rare, and should therefore be considered preliminary.

The scleractinian coral skeleton functions as both a structural and protective material [99] and is composed primarily of inorganic CaCO3 in the form of aragonite, together with a minor organic component [44]. This organic phase (or intraskeletal OM) consists of various macromolecules such as polysaccharides, lipids, and proteins [100] that are used by the coral to modulate biomineralization [101]. Physiological mechanisms underlying the biomineralization process under decreasing Ωarag [44, 83, 102] could affect OM content and composition and ultimately influence coral skeletal structural and compositional features. Previous studies testing OA effects on the Mediterranean colonial and zooxanthellate corals Cladocora caespitosa and Oculina patagonica [103], as well as on the octocoral Corallium rubrum [104], documented a decline in skeletal growth rates. However, the latter study noted a significant increase in the OM content under low pH conditions, a finding that was also observed in the shallow-water colonial zooxanthellate Stylophora pistillata kept in aquaria for one year under very low Ωarag (0.69 ± 0.1; [53], which contrasts with the results of the current study. On the other hand, D. dianthus reared for 314 days under low Ωarag (1.6 ± 0.1) showed reduced skeletal growth, yet there was no variation in OM content [14], confirming findings of the current study. Decreasing Ωarag seems to mostly affect macro-scale structural features, such as skeletal porosity, in D. dianthus, while micro- and nano-scale characteristics did not change or show any clear trends with increasing depth. This suggests that the “building blocks” produced by the biomineralization process are substantially unaffected in this CWC, as previously reported also for shallow-water solitary and colonial species at CO2 vents in Panarea Island, southern Italy [32], and in Papua New Guinea [46]. Nevertheless, the absence of detectable changes at the micro- and nano-scale does not preclude that other fine-scale aspects not assessed here, such as genomic regulation of biomineralization pathways [51, 55, 105] or differentially expressed proteins involved in biomineralization [106], may be affected by decreasing Ωarag and could ultimately drive the macro-scale porosity changes observed in this and previous studies.

Our study also revealed a sharp decline (~ 90%) in ammonium concentrations with depth (0.058–0.006 mmol/m3). While this trend should be interpreted with caution because ammonium is among the least accurately modeled variables in the dataset [107], even the highest ammonium estimates in our record fall near the lower bound expected for the oligotrophic Ionian/Eastern Mediterranean [39, 108]. For instance, average ammonium values at a coastal ambient (no CO2 vent activity) station in Sant’Angelo (Ischia Island, Italy) were 0.09 ± 0.040 mmol/m3 [39] and < 0.03 at a coastal area in San Giorgio di Gioiosa Marea, Gulf of Patti NE Tyrrhenian coast of Sicily [108]. In northern Ionian open waters, mean ammonium values at a coastal station called Acicastello, on the eastern coast of Sicily, were 0.83 ± 0.66 mmol/m3 with summer maxima of 3.35 mmol/m3 at 50 m [109], an order of magnitude higher than our maximum. Consistently, long-term observations from the Sicily Channel (2010–2021) show near-detection inorganic nutrients in summer surface waters and only modest concentrations at intermediate/deep layers (e.g., nitrate ~ 3–6 mmol/m3 below 200 m), indicating persistent oligotrophy [110]. Moreover, land-based inputs typically elevate NH₄⁺ in coastal waters, not at offshore sites like ours (~ 4 km from shore). Taken together, these considerations support that our values fall within normal regional ranges and do not suggest appreciable influence from land-based sources.

A decline in ammonium concentrations can serve as an indirect indicator of decreasing zooplankton abundance or activity, given that zooplankton excretion is a significant source of ammonium in marine ecosystems [111]. Indeed, zooplankton abundance declines significantly with depth across multiple stations sampled from the Eastern to Western Mediterranean Sea [112]. About 80% of the carbon used in coral calcification is sourced from CO2 released by the coral host during respiration [113]. Reduced feeding and a general decline in host metabolism at depth are likely to lower internal metabolic CO2 availability, ultimately limiting calcification. Indeed, increased feeding frequency has been shown to increase net calcification rates and overall physiological performance in D. dianthus maintained for 14 months even in undersaturated conditions (Ωarag 0.79 ± 0.04; [114]. Thus, the observed increase in skeletal porosity in D. dianthus, likely associated with reduced net calcification rates, as observed in other scleractinian corals [32, 38, 53], and its correlation with declining ammonium concentrations may reflect a depth-related decrease in food availability, leading to reduced heterotrophic input and calcification capacity, already compromised by a significant reduction in Ωarag.

Conclusions

In conclusion, CWCs are poised to be among the first calcifiers impacted by OA as the aragonite saturation horizon shoals, albeit with substantial variation across locations and species [23, 115]. Indeed, CWCs have also been shown to exert a strong biological control over the biomineralization process, as indicated by their inherent ability to raise calcifying fluid pH and Ωarag to levels much higher than those observed in shallow-water species [85]. The persistence of structural and compositional skeletal traits across a depth gradient suggests that D. dianthus maintains biological control over mineralization at micro- and nano-scales under varying environmental conditions. To date, most studies conducted on CWCs have been performed in aquaria, where it is challenging or impossible to replicate natural conditions such as nutrients and currents. Despite the difficulties of studying these habitats, field studies [78, 79, 116–118] are crucial to understand the effects of environmental variability on natural populations and ecosystems, which is a key priority as global environmental changes intensify [119].

Methods

Study site and sampling

Specimens of D. dianthus (Additional file 1: Fig. S4) were collected between 2001 and 2021 during several oceanographic cruises and in collaboration with local fishers at different sites in the Mediterranean Sea, hereafter numbered 1 to 6 in order of increasing depth (Fig. 1; Table 3; Additional file 1: Table S1). Only live-collected specimens were included in this study; no dead or fossil samples were analyzed. Upon collection, corals were rinsed with fresh water, air-dried, and stored dry at room temperature until further processing, with the exception of site 3 (Marcos), where specimens were preserved in 70% ethanol immediately after collection. Ethanol preservation is unlikely to bias the skeletal porosity or density measurements reported here: ethanol forms an ordered, hydrophobic layer on carbonate surfaces that inhibits dissolution rather than promoting it [120, 121] and has been used to preserve CWCs in previous structural work [56]. Location, depth, year of collection, and number of samples collected with the sampling method

Collection methods varied by location and included retrieval from a lost crustacean trap (Roccella, 2006), opportunistic collection using fishing nets (Caulonia, 2021), and bottom trawling during scientific surveys. For the latter, samples from the Marcos and Medcor sites were obtained via mini trawls conducted aboard the R/V Urania during dedicated research cruises by the Italian National Research Council, while those from the South Adriatic were collected during the MEDITS (International bottom trawl survey in the Mediterranean) campaigns. Additional samples from the Mema site were collected at Station 12 of the R/V Urania. All cruise activities were conducted under the appropriate permits [60]. Samples from Roccella and Caulonia were provided by local fishers who recovered lost fishing gear, bringing to the surface specimens that had grown along discarded lines and nets. D. dianthus is listed as Least Concern on the IUCN Red List [122]. Although this approach resulted in a limited number of sites and temporal gaps, it represents a valuable strategy for obtaining otherwise inaccessible deep-water coral material and yields a representative dataset spanning a broad environmental gradient across the Mediterranean Sea.

Environmental database

Monthly average temperature, salinity, and pH data for the ten years prior to sampling (Roccella: 10/1996–09/2006; Caulonia: 04/2010–05/2020; Marcos: 01/1998–12/2007; Mema: 04/2002–05/2012; Medcor: 01/2000–12/2009; South Adriatic: 02/1991–01/2001) were obtained from the Copernicus Marine Environment Monitoring Service (https://marine.copernicus.eu/), using outputs from two near-real-time operational forecasting models: GLOBAL_ANALYSISFORECAST_BGC_001_028 (biogeochemical variables) and GLOBAL_ANALYSISFORECAST_PHY_001_024 (physical variables). These products are not re-analyses but rather data-assimilative model forecasts, incorporating satellite and in situ observations. Monthly average pH, temperature, salinity and Dissolved Inorganic Carbon (DIC) data were obtained for the period between 01/2019 and 12/2020 using the same models (Additional file 1: Table S8). DIC data was not available prior to 2019. These values were used to calculate Ωarag through the CO2SYS software, applying established dissociation constants [123–125]. Linear regression between Ωarag and pH (Additional file 1: Table S9), for which the standard error of the regression (sigma, σ) was estimated, was used to estimate the Ωarag of the ten years preceding the sampling (Additional file 1: Tables S10–S15). Sigma quantifies the average distance that the observed values fall from the regression line, serving as a measure of the model’s accuracy in predicting the dependent variable. It reflects the standard deviation of the residuals and provides an estimate of the uncertainty around the regression predictions. In addition, ammonium, nitrate, and phosphate concentrations for the same periods were extracted from the MEDSEA_MULTIYEAR_BGC_006_008 reanalysis, generated using the MedBFM3 modeling system at a horizontal resolution of 1/24° (approximately 4 km) [107].

Biometric parameters

All coral specimens (N = 164; Additional file 1: Table S1) were immersed in a 10% commercial bleach solution for 72 h to ensure complete removal of residual soft tissue. After bleaching, samples were thoroughly rinsed with deionized water and dried in an oven for four days at temperatures not exceeding 50 °C. This cleaning and drying procedure has been extensively used in previous studies to avoid mineral phase transitions within the coral skeleton [32, 46, 55, 126–128]. Each specimen was then carefully examined under a binocular microscope to remove fragments of substratum and/or calcareous deposits from other organisms. Corallite length (l, maximum axis of the oral disc), width (w, minimum axis of the oral disc) and height (h, oral-aboral axis of the polyp) were measured using a caliper. Dry skeletal mass (DW) was measured using a precision balance (Ohaus Explorer Pro balance ± 0.0001 g). Corallite volume (i.e., bulk volume) was obtained by buoyant weight, as further detailed below [38].

Skeletal porosity, bulk density, and micro-density determination

The apparent skeletal porosity, bulk density, and micro-density of all corals from the six locations were determined by buoyant weight measurements. After recording the DW, the corallites were placed in a drying chamber and evacuated with a rotary mechanical pump down to a vacuum of 10–2 mbar. This step was crucial to evacuate all air from the pores connected to the external surface, thereby preparing the samples for effective saturation in the subsequent phase. After 3 h under vacuum, deionized water was gradually added until the samples were fully submerged and saturated. The saturated corallites were then removed from the water, gently blotted with a damp cloth to remove excess surface water while retaining water within the pores, and weighed on the balance in air to determine the saturated weight (SW). The buoyant weight (BW) was measured with a precision balance (Ohaus Explorer Pro balance ± 0.0001 g) equipped with a density determination kit, with the sample fully submerged in deionized water at 20 °C. All three weight measurements (DW, SW, BW) were taken in triplicate and averaged.

Thermogravimetric analysis

The intraskeletal OM and water content were measured by Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA) performed using a TA Instruments thermobalance model SDT-Q600 with 0.1 µg of balance sensitivity. The TGA was performed on three corallites each from sites 2, 4, 5 and 6 (Additional file 1: Table S1). Each corallite was pulverized with an agate mortar until it formed a uniform, fine powder [127]. Powdered samples (10 mg), held in aluminum pans, were heated and weighted continuously from ambient (~ 20 °C) up to 900 °C with a 10 °C/min heating rate under nitrogen flow with flux fixed to 100 ml/min. Two main weight loss regimes were identified: a first one in a range around 125–250 °C (related to the loss of structured water molecules) followed by another thermal region between 250 and 500 °C (generally associated with OM pyrolysis; [130]. Thermograms were analyzed with TA Universal Analysis software.

Synchrotron high-resolution X-ray powder diffraction

Crystallite parameters were assessed by Synchrotron high-resolution X-ray powder diffraction (HRPXRD), measuring one corallite each from sites 2, 4, 5 and 6 (Additional file 1: Table S1) at the ID22 beamline of the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility (Grenoble, France) using a wavelength of 0.4 Å. These included the orientation of crystallographic axes (a, b, and c, with the c-axis typically perpendicular to planar carbonate groups and representing the fastest-growing direction in needle-like crystallites), macrostrain (elastic deformation of the crystal lattice), and crystallite size (the average extent of coherently diffracting domains). Powdered samples were placed into borosilicate glass capillaries with diameters ranging from 0.7 to 1 mm. Measurements were taken at room temperature and the unit-cell parameters were determined from the diffraction pattern profiles using Rietveld refinement using GSAS software and the EXPGUI interface [131]. A specific diffraction peak was analyzed to determine the coherence length (nm) along various crystallographic directions by fitting the profile to a Voigt function and deconvoluting the Lorentzian and Gaussian components.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy

The crystalline phase of one corallite each from sites 2, 4, 5, and 6 (Additional file 1: Table S1) was measured using a Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS10 Fourier Transform Infrared Spectrometer. The analysis was performed on three replicates from 4 out of 6 sites (depths: ~ 440 m, ~ 750 m, ~ 850 m, ~ 1220 m). The disk sample (15 mg) for FTIR analysis was obtained by mixing a small amount (2 mg) of powder with 98 mg of KBr and applying a pressure of 45 tsi (620.5 MPa) to the mixture using a press. The spectra were obtained with 4 cm−1 resolution and 64 scans.

Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy

The elemental composition (Al, Fe, K, Mg, Na, and Sr) of one corallite each from sites 2, 4, 5, and 6 (Additional file 1: Table S1) was determined using Spectro Arcos-Ametek Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectroscopy. For the analysis, 50 mg of coral skeleton powder was dissolved in 4.5 mL of HNO3 and 0.5 mL of H2O2. The atomic % of the element relative to calcium was calculated.

Statistical analysis

For all locations, Pearson’s correlation was performed (1) between skeletal parameters and length and (2) among skeletal parameters. Kruskal–Wallis test is a non-parametric alternative to the analysis of variance (ANOVA), and it is used to compare groups of means. As a distribution-free test, it offers greater robustness than parametric methods for data that are not normally distributed, making it a viable alternative to parametric statistical approaches (Potvin and Roff 1993). Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare mean temperature, salinity, pH, Ωarag, ammonium, nitrate, and phosphate, skeletal bulk density, porosity, micro-density, and intraskeletal OM among depths. A permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was performed using the adonis() function from the vegan package in RStudio to assess whether environmental variables (i.e., temperature, salinity, pH, Ωarag, ammonium, nitrate, and phosphate) explained significant variation in coral skeletal parameters, based on 999 permutations. Environmental variables identified as significant in the PERMANOVA were subsequently correlated with individual skeletal parameters using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. All graphs for skeletal and environmental parameters were performed using the RStudio (Version 2023.06.1 + 524) package ggplot2. Except for the PERMANOVA, all other statistical analyses were computed using the IBM SPSS Statistic 25.0.
