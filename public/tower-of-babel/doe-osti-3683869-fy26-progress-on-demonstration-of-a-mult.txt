 Abstract
 Under the U.S. Department of Energy Office of Nuclear Energy Advanced Modeling and
Simulation (NEAMS) Program, an integrated multiphysics approach is being developed to model
the core bowing phenomena important to liquid metal-cooled fast reactors. Core bowing is an
important passive safety mechanism in liquid metal-cooled fast reactors and involves multiphysics
effects including radiation transport, fluid flow, heat transfer, and mechanical response to
temperature and flux gradients. This report summarizes recent progress on developing a
multiphysics, MOOSE-based workflow to predict core bowing and associated reactivity feedback.
 Significant new capabilities in the reactor physics code Griffin - sodium backfill and pin power
reconstruction for deformed geometries - were applied in this effort. This year’s work included
verification, code comparisons, sensitivity studies, and coupled demonstrations that advance the
state of MOOSE-based core bowing workflow. Griffin’s sodium backfill capability was verified
by demonstrating that its automated treatment of geometry expansion and material-density updates
reproduces manual calculations exactly, confirming solid mass conservation and proper coolant
backfilling in expanded geometries. Reconstructed pin powers were compared for Griffin’s duct-
heterogeneous and ring-heterogeneous treatments in single-, seven-, and nineteen-assembly cases,
with best agreement observed in lower-leakage configurations and the duct-heterogeneous
approach offering substantially lower computational cost. Thermal-hydraulic sensitivity
sensitivities showed that MOOSE SCM, SAM, and CFD are expected to produce similar
deformation predictions despite variances in their temperature predictions, and that explicit
treatment of inter-assembly flow becomes increasingly important as gap flow rate increases.
Finally, coupled demonstrations on small multi-assembly configurations using Griffin, MOOSE
Solid Mechanics, MOOSE SCM, and Heat Conduction produced physically consistent reactivity
feedback from thermal expansion and bowing. The coupled demonstrations simulated grid plate
expansion as well as resultant core bowing at full power conditions. Simplifications were made in
current workflow, namely the assumption of instantaneous full power conditions following hot
zero power, and pre-expanding the Griffin geometry axially due to lack of an axial fuel pin
expansion model and temperature feedback to Griffin.

1 Introduction
 Under the U.S. Department of Energy Office of Nuclear Energy’s Advanced Modeling and
Simulation (NEAMS) Program [1], an integrated multiphysics workflow using the Multiphysics
Object Oriented Simulation Environment (MOOSE) [2] is being developed to model core bowing
in liquid metal-cooled fast reactors (LFMR). Core bowing is an important passive safety
mechanism: when the core restraint system is properly designed, temperature and flux gradients
cause assemblies to bow into less reactive configurations during accident scenarios, producing
negative reactivity feedback. Predicting this behavior is challenging because it depends on tightly
coupled radiation transport, heat transfer, and thermo-mechanical response. Although legacy tools
exist, no integrated high-fidelity capability currently predicts both the deformation and resulting
reactivity feedback directly. Because the reactivity feedback arises from deformations on the order
of millimeters, a high-resolution physics capability is needed both to build confidence in existing
methods and to evaluate new restraint system designs under accident conditions.
 Developing this capability pushes current multiphysics tools to their limits. Geometry
deformation after initialization can degrade mesh quality, complicate data transfer, and create
convergence challenges. In reactor physics, the homogenized material regions commonly used in
fast reactor analysis reduce computational cost but complicate material accounting after
deformation. Deformed configurations require proper treatment of sodium backfill in newly
created or compressed volumes. Thermal-hydraulic analysis must resolve duct wall temperature
gradients that drive bowing, yet the subchannel methods needed for this fidelity generally cannot
represent deformed geometry directly. Inter-wrapper flow is important for heat transfer, but
modeling this accurately can be computationally expensive. In addition, robust treatment of the
many assembly contact points in a full-size fast reactor core remains an active research challenge.
 These challenges have motivated a stepwise development strategy based on smaller problems,
gradual introduction of coupled physics, and extensive verification to support choices in model
fidelity and solver approach. In FY25, work began on integrating the reactor physics code Griffin
[3] with MOOSE Subchannel (MOOSE-SCM) [4], Heat Transfer, Solid Mechanics [5], and
Contact [6] Modules on small ABR-1000-based models [7], including single- and seven-assembly
configurations [8]. That effort identified several issues that have been resolved during FY26.
 FY26 work to mature the multiphysics workflow included several components. First, Griffin’s
new sodium backfill capability was verified to maintain correct material accounting in displaced
geometries. Griffin’s updated pin power reconstruction method, which improves accuracy for
deformed geometry, was also tested for duct-heterogeneous and ring-heterogeneous geometry
across several small core models and assembly locations, including nominal and bowed
configurations. Structural mechanics data transfers between MOOSE and Griffin were thoroughly
tested for robustness. Sensitivity studies examined how thermal-hydraulic fidelity impacts
prediction of duct wall temperature under a range of conditions. Finally, Griffin, MOOSE SCM,

 1

and Solid Mechanics were coupled for a series of small core problems to demonstrate successful
data transfer among the individual physics modules and integrated prediction of bowing behavior.
Grid plate expansion (new in FY26) and a “simplified” approach to model the hot full power
condition were performed. While additional work remains to incorporate temperature and axial
expansion feedback to Griffin, the steady state simulation for core bowing has matured
significantly in the past year.
 In parallel with these multiphysics studies, verification activities continued for individual
physics relevant to core bowing. For the third year, Argonne participated with the ART Fast
Reactor Program in the U.S.-Japan Civil Nuclear Energy Working Group (CNWG) Expert Group
on Core Bowing, contributing to a reactor physics benchmark comparing direct and indirect
(perturbation theory-based) reactivity feedback estimates. Results from that code-to-code
comparison are reported separately in an ICAPP 2026 conference paper. Verification of the Solid
Mechanics and Contact Modules also continued by increasing the problem size of a benchmark
problem from the IAEA International Working Group on Fast Reactors (IWGFR) [9]. A summary
of the completed verification problems is included in this report.

2 MOOSE-Based Multiphysics Workflow
 The core bowing simulation requires coupling reactor physics to compute power distributions ,
heat transfer methods to compute solid and liquid temperatures, and thermo-mechanics to compute
displacement. This chapter describes the MOOSE-based physics applications and how they are
coupled together to model the physics associated with core bowing.
2.1 MOOSE MultiApp Workflow: Cold to Hot Full Power
 The target MultiApp hierarchy for modeling core bowing is shown in Figure 2-1. A prescribed
execution sequence indicated by circled numbers in the figure, is utilized for each iteration to
ensure the data transfers are performed in the expected order, e.g. that fields are updated before
they are used. Although this sequencing constraint has largely been removed by the development
of the sibling transfer capability in MOOSE, the current approach is suitable for the present
workflow of initial thermal expansion followed by full power deformation calculation.

 Figure 2-1. MOOSE MultiApp hierarchy for core bowing problems.

The FY26 calculation workflow begins with simulating the grid plate thermal expansion that
happens during the transition from cold (20 °C) to hot (380 °C) zero power. To perform the grid
plate expansion step (new in FY26) Solid Mechanics models the assembly duct geometry with the
lower nozzle boundary idealized as a continuation of the duct geometry and the lower face
restricted from moving in the x, y, and z directions initially with a FunctionDirichletBC.
The grid plate thermal expansion is applied to the boundary condition through a
ParsedFunction which applies the temperature change from room temperature conditions to

 3

the hot zero power condition (inlet temperature) at 380 °C. The bottom face of the ducts is then
expanded based on the simple thermal expansion of the grid plate which increases the duct pitch.
The ducts themselves are heated to the same inlet temperature condition and thermally expanded.
The gap between load pads at the hot condition is impacted by increasing pitch and the
compensating effect of increasing duct wall thickness. Due to a limitation in MOOSE SCM that
prohibits mesh deformation in interior channels, MOOSE SCM is initialized at hot conditions
using a thermal expansion consistent with what is used in the Solid Mechanics Module. Due to the
current lack of pin axial expansion needed for Griffin, Griffin is initialized at “AHRC” conditions
– axially hot, radially cold. A pin axial expansion model will be implemented next year. Solid
Mechanics is initialized at cold conditions.
 Iteration 1: During this first iteration to raise all models to hot zero power, MOOSE SCM (1)
first executes with zero power and hot fluid to compute duct wall temperature. Griffin (2) then
computes the flux and power distribution at AHRC condition without any input from SCM
(temperature feedback will be introduced next year). Next, Griffin PPR (3) computes the pin
powers [10] and transfers these to MOOSE SCM. Following this data transfer, the MOOSE Solid
Mechanics (4) calculation is performed using the original SCM duct wall temperature solution (the
HZP condition prior to Griffin feedback) and transfers the displacement back to the Griffin
MainApp (at end of timestep). Griffin receives and applies only the Solid Mechanics radial
displacements since it has already been axially expanded. Griffin does not yet execute this HZP
calculation – it waits until MOOSE SCM executes again.
 Iteration 2: During the second iteration, MOOSE SCM (1) executes again using the pin powers
from the non-deformed (AHRC) Griffin case. The current execution strategy unfortunately results
in a data lag that forces SCM to use the pin power data from the previous Griffin iteration. Griffin
(2) now performs its calculation with expanded geometry, reconstructs the pin-power distribution
(3) and transfers it to MOOSE SCM, but MOOSE SCM does not execute yet. Solid Mechanics (4)
now executes using the MOOSE SCM duct wall temperatures from Iteration 2. The iterations
repeat until all physics converge at HFP conditions. For now, the reactor is assumed to be
instantaneously at hot full power, essentially “skipping” over the details of how the core
progressively deforms and focusing on its hot full power end state. While a realistic startup analysis
would raise the power gradually from hot zero power to hot full power, this is an advanced
workflow left for future work.
2.2 Reactor Physics: Griffin
 The MOOSE-based Griffin reactor physics code can compute the detailed flux and power
distribution on the initial and deformed geometries using one of several radial heterogeneous
treatments, of which the first three are depicted in Figure 2-2.
 1. Fully heterogeneous treatment: Pins, coolant, duct, and inter-assembly gap are
 explicitly represented.

 4

 2. Ring-heterogeneous treatment: Pins and coolant are explicitly represented in an
 “equivalent” hexagonal-ring geometry; duct and inter-assembly gap are explicitly
 represented. (This work uses the detailed ring-heterogeneous model with explicit
 cladding regions on each side of the fuel.)
 3. Duct-heterogeneous treatment: Pins and coolant are homogenized; duct and inter-
 assembly gap are explicitly represented. This model also incorporates a distinct region
 of coolant just inside the duct wall.
 4. Gap-heterogeneous treatment: The inter-assembly sodium gap is explicitly modeled;
 duct and assembly interior are homogenized.
 5. Homogeneous treatment: Each individual assembly is homogenized.

 Figure 2-2. Radial view of assembly modeling treatments: fully heterogeneous (left), ring-
 heterogeneous (center), and duct-heterogeneous (right).

 Accurate density treatment during deformation is essential for correct reactivity feedback
predictions. Before discussing the advantages and drawbacks of the heterogeneous approaches, we
introduce the concept of sodium backfill – which refers to liquid sodium filling volumes that
increase or decrease during deformation. Unlike solids, which expand or contract without changing
mass, sodium redistributes as the geometry changes. In early FY26, a new capability was made
available in Griffin to model sodium backfill in both pure sodium and homogenized sodium-solid
mixtures. The capability automatically preserves solid material after geometric changes while
allowing the coolant density to change based on temperature rather than mesh volume. The new
sodium backfill capability in Griffin can be used with any of the geometry treatments.
 We seek to leverage the lowest fidelity neutronics option which is accurate. While homogenized
assemblies are widely used in fast reactor analysis due to the long mean free path of neutrons
(which don’t “see” heterogeneity), they cannot be used for direct calculations of deformed
assemblies because they have no representation of gaps between assemblies. The gap-
heterogeneous treatment is a good option for standalone neutronics simulations, but not for
multiphysics solutions in which duct modeling is important for other physics. Fully heterogeneous

 5

treatment is computationally intractable for full core problems. We therefore narrow the selection
to Griffin’s ring-heterogeneous and duct-heterogeneous treatments.
 The ring-heterogeneous treatment is available in Griffin’s automated SFR workflow (which
includes multigroup cross section generation). It keeps solid and fluid regions separate, simplifying
treatment of sodium backfill for manual calculations, but Griffin’s new automated sodium backfill
method removes this advantage over the duct-heterogeneous method. Because this treatment
models equivalent rings of fuel pins, individual cross sections and/or axial displacements can
potentially be applied per ring. This is a clear advantage over the duct-heterogeneous method.
However, this equivalent geometry representation does not align with real pins, and mesh
deformation may align the geometry into increasingly unrealistic formations. Additionally, the
ring-heterogeneous treatment requires many more mesh elements, which increases both
computational cost and the likelihood of mesh degradation as deformation progresses.
 The duct-heterogeneous treatment is also supported by Griffin’s automated SFR workflow. The
duct heterogeneous model combines intra-duct homogenization with the duct and inter-assembly
heterogeneity required for capturing leakage effects. It however cannot be used with ring-wise
cross sections and/or axial expansion, so may introduce errors in fuel assemblies with strongly
variable behavior across rings (atypical for fast reactors). This treatment has the fewest number of
mesh elements compared to other potential approaches and should be least susceptible to high
computational time and convergence issues.
 Pin power reconstruction (PPR) is required for both duct- and ring-heterogeneous treatments in
the multiphysics workflow. PPR is available in Griffin for both standard and deformed geometries.
Newly developed capabilities in FY26 improve accuracy for PPR in deformed geometries.
Reconstructed pin powers are passed to the MOOSE SCM for thermal-hydraulics computations.
 The Griffin DFEM-SN solver was used with CMFD acceleration, angular discretization of 2
for polar and 3 for azimuthal, and 33 energy groups. The MOOSE Reactor Module was used to
prepare mesh inputs [11].
2.3 Thermal Fluids: MOOSE SCM and Heat Conduction
 Subchannel codes provide an efficient compromise for nuclear reactor core thermal-hydraulic
simulation, with modeling fidelity between Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) and system
codes. Although CFD codes offer a robust mechanism to predict core temperature distribution,
they present significant computational cost constraints when considering whole-core analyses.
Further, even at the assembly-level, computational time associated with iterative design
calculations using CFD tools remains substantial. Subchannel analyses use a quasi-3D model
formulation and allow for a finer mesh than system codes without the high computational cost of
CFD. Present methodologies and foreseeable advancements underscore the relevance of
subchannel analysis in the thermal-hydraulics evaluation of SFRs.

 6

 MOOSE SCM is a subchannel analysis module within the MOOSE thermal-hydraulics
framework that performs single-phase reactor-core subchannel simulations. It was originally
developed for water-cooled reactors with square-lattice fuel arrangements and has since been
extended to model liquid-metal fast reactors with wire-wrapped fuel pins in hexagonal assemblies.
MOOSE SCM uses the MOOSE framework together with the PETSc library [12] to solve the
governing subchannel equations. Since MOOSE SCM models only a single assembly, simulations
involving multiple assemblies require coupling with the MOOSE Heat Transfer module to
represent heat transfer between adjacent assemblies. In such coupled multi-assembly calculations,
the duct wall and inter-assembly gap are modeled in the Heat Transfer module using heat
conduction. The heat flux at the inner duct surface is transferred from the Heat Conduction
calculation to MOOSE SCM, while the inner duct surface temperature is transferred from MOOSE
SCM back to the Heat Conduction calculation. In addition, the power density computed by Griffin
is passed through the Heat Conduction model to MOOSE SCM.
 To store the power density data on Heat Conduction mesh, dummy fuel pin meshes are
included. The Heat Conduction mesh is generated through MOOSE Reactor Module. This mesh
containing fuel pins, duct walls, and inter-assembly gaps for a seven-assembly simulation is shown
in Figure 2-3. However, the heat conduction equation is solved only in duct walls and inter-
assembly gaps; no equation is solved in the dummy fuel pin regions. After the iterative coupling
between MOOSE SCM and the Heat Conduction model converges, the resulting duct temperature
distribution is transferred from the MOOSE Heat Conduction calculation to the Solid Mechanics
simulation.

 Figure 2-3. Radial view of heat conduction model for a seven-assembly case.

2.4 Thermal Mechanics: MOOSE Solid Mechanics and Contact
 The MOOSE Solid Mechanics Module is a library of tools for simulating continuum mechanics
and modeling the mechanical behavior of solids elements. It can be used for standalone mechanical
analyses or coupled with other multiphysics simulations. Solid Mechanics supports both small and
finite strain mechanics, including elasticity, plasticity, and creep. For high-fidelity analysis of fast
reactor systems, it is important to account for a range of physical phenomena, including linear
elastic behavior, thermal expansion, irradiation-induced material effects, and inter-assembly
contact due to gap closure as the ducts deform. While beam elements used in low-fidelity
simulations can estimate the overall displacement of beam centerlines and fast reactor assembly
ducts, they generally neglect cross-sectional distortion resulting from thermal expansion, creep,
and contact. In contrast, solid elements enable resolution of radial and azimuthal variations in
temperature and irradiation effects across duct cross sections, rather than relying on averaged
values or absolute differences between duct faces or corners. The Contact module is used to model
contact between assemblies at the load pad locations using a node-on-face penalty formulation.
Displacements computed by the MOOSE Solid Mechanics module are transferred to Griffin for
calculation of the reactivity in the deformed state.

4 Assessment of Griffin’s Sodium Backfill Capability
 Since the focus in FY26 was on integration of Griffin into the multiphysics framework, we
devote this and the following chapter to assessing new capabilities in Griffin that are critical for
accurate prediction of the reactivity feedback caused by core bowing.
 This chapter summarizes verification efforts for the new sodium backfill capability in Griffin
previously discussed in 2.2. Thermal expansion (20 °C to 380 ºC) was applied to the single
assembly Griffin model using both duct- and ring-heterogeneous treatments. The sodium backfill
capability was invoked in Griffin to automatically adjust atomic densities for solids and fluids
post-expansion. Separately, a manual calculation was performed to expand the geometry and
adjust isotopic densities to confirm Griffin’s sodium backfill model is working as expected. The
models relevant to this verification are described below, along with observations regarding duct-
heterogeneous vs. ring-heterogeneous treatment.

heterogeneous keff being slightly higher. It is reasonable to expect that this difference will be
approximately maintained between all test cases even though it is not necessarily required. As will

5 Assessment of Pin Power Reconstruction in Griffin
 Accurate pin power reconstruction (PPR) is required to transfer accurate powers to the
subchannel thermal fluids model. Griffin’s PPR capability has not been extensively tested for duct-
heterogenous or ring-heterogeneous configurations because it is most typically used in
homogeneous assembly calculations. It has also not been previously used in deformed geometry
calculations.
 In this chapter, we apply PPR to both duct-heterogeneous and ring-heterogeneous models for
single, seven, and nineteen assembly configurations. We also test deformed variations of these
configurations. Pin-wise comparisons are made between the duct- and ring-heterogeneous results.
All cases in this section used the ABR-1000 fuel assemblies (271 fuel pins) with five axial zones
in the active fuel region for pin power reconstruction, thus using 1355 total fuel pin zones per fuel
assembly. Most figures in this section present the middle axial zone (axial layer three of five);
differences were observed to be similar across multiple axial locations.
5.1 Reference, Expanded, and Bowed States
 Three structural mechanics treatments were applied to configurations with vacuum boundary
conditions: (a) cold geometry at 20 °C (“reference”), (b) uniform expansion to hot geometry at
380 °C (“expanded”), and finally (c) hot geometry where one or more assemblies has an imposed
temperature gradient ranging from 380 to 530 °C (“bowed”).
 The expanded state represents grid plate expansion. The pitch of all assemblies expands
uniformly, and the duct wall thickness increases slightly. The bowed state mocks up the impact of
a strong thermal gradient in one or more assemblies that might occur in a real core. A pre-defined
temperature gradient is applied to the center assembly in the single and seven assembly cases. In
the nineteen assembly case, the gradient is applied to the 6 fuel assemblies in ring two, and rotated
to make the assemblies bow outward toward the reflector and restraint ring. All other unheated
ducts and restraint ring are at a constant 380 °C uniform temperature. Figure 5-1 shows the applied
thermal gradient which develops axially and radially along the active core region, and the
assignment of the gradient to the nineteen assembly configuration.

6 Thermal Hydraulic Sensitivity Studies on Duct Wall Temperature
 Accurate prediction of duct wall temperature is essential because it is the primary driver of the
thermal loads and gradients that cause the structure to expand and bend. In addition, material
properties of solid structures are temperature dependent: Young’s modulus, yield strength, creep
rates, and thermal expansion coefficients vary with temperature. Errors in wall temperature
prediction could produce large enough deformation errors to change performance in the coupled
system. Sensitivity studies have been performed to understand impacts on duct wall temperature
due to (1) solver fidelity within the assembly and (2) inter-assembly gap flow treatment. The
sensitivity studies also attempt to quantify the uncertainty in displacement predictions due to duct
wall temperature prediction errors. These studies inform the choice of different thermal hydraulic
codes for core bowing applications.
6.1 Impact of Solver Fidelity
 A sensitivity study using MOOSE SCM, SAM, and CFD (STAR-CCM+) was performed using
the seven-assembly configuration to quantify the impact of the T/H fidelity on duct wall
temperature predictions and subsequently deformation. For consistency, a bare rod bundle with
constant and consistent sodium properties was applied in all three codes. The duct wall temperature
distributions computed by these codes were imported into a seven-assembly structural mechanics
model to compute the resulting deformation. The assembly numbering scheme is shown in Figure
6-1. The center assembly is assembly 1, and surrounding assemblies are numbered from 2 to 7 in
a counterclockwise fashion.

6.2 Impact of Inter-Assembly Flow Treatment
 Inter-assembly flow strongly controls lateral energy exchange between neighboring assemblies,
affecting local temperature non-uniformity and fluctuations that can drive hot spots or induce
thermal fatigue in cladding and structural components. Although limited in geometric extent, inter-
assembly flow can have significant impact. Modeling errors can bias local temperatures, heat
transfer estimates, and neutronic leakage or reactivity, making this effect important for safety
analyses. This section presents a CFD model of a seven-assembly configuration to investigate the
impact of inter-assembly flow. In the simulation, two treatments are applied for the inter-assembly
flow modeling:
 1. The inter-assembly region is treated as explicit sodium flow.
 2. The inter-assembly region is treated as solid domain with sodium properties.
 The main calculation adopts the solid-domain treatment, while additional cases treat the inter-
assembly region as explicit sodium flow at different flow rates. The inter-assembly gap velocity
ranges from 0.05 m/s to 0.4725 m/s, corresponding to 0.25% to 2.5% of the total assembly flow.
Figure 6-17 shows the temperature distribution between Assembly 1 and Assembly 7 for these
different treatments. Treating the inter-assembly region as a solid domain is justified when the
gap velocity is small (0.05-0.1 m/s), for which the maximum duct temperature difference is only
5-8K. At higher gap velocities, the maximum temperature difference increases and can reach 30K.

 53

 Figure 6-17. CFD duct wall temperature prediction by different inter-assembly flow models.

 The main difference between modeling the inter-assembly region as a solid domain versus an
explicit flow domain is the downstream advection of thermal energy by the moving fluid. When
the flow is very slow and conduction dominates, treating the inter-assembly region as a solid
domain is acceptable. As the flow velocity increases, introducing a heat sink model in the solid
domain can be a safe approach to mimic the downstream advection. Because the required heat sink
depends on the specific scenario, this study presents only a preliminary method. As shown in
Figure 6-18, including a heat sink improves prediction of the duct wall temperature. In this study,
however, the heat sink is simplified as a uniform volumetric heat sink. A more sophisticated model
could account for reduced advection downstream due to lower velocity near the wall by applying
a smaller volumetric heat sink there, which could further improve the agreement.

7 Thermo-Mechanical Modeling Verification
 The IAEA-published International Working Group on Fast Reactors (IWGFR) verification and
validation report [9] includes a set of thermo-mechanical computational verification problems
(VP) relevant to core bowing, with specified temperature, irradiation dose, and contact conditions.
Benchmark participants contributed solutions using a range of methods, for VPs ranging from
single duct bowing cases to complex multiple assembly problems. These benchmark results serve
as a basis for assessing the accuracy of MOOSE-based thermo-mechanical physics solvers for
core-bowing applications.
 Previous studies showed good agreement was demonstrated between MOOSE Solid Mechanics
simulations and the IAEA benchmark participant solutions for VP1, VP3A, VP3B, VP4, VP5,
VP7A, and VP7B in predicting displacement and contact forces [8] [13] [14]. MOOSE Solid
Mechanics results are compared with both IAEA participant results and Argonne’s NUBOW-3D
[15], which uses beam element formulations for displacement and contact calculations. In contrast,
MOOSE uses 3D solid elements with ducts meshed as empty, thin-walled hexagonal cross-
sections. Because beam models report only centerline displacements, MOOSE results are reduced
to effective centerline values by averaging nodal displacements over each duct cross section. Some
differences in results are expected since the MOOSE solid element formulation considers cross-
sectional distortion and twisting effects that beam models do not.
 Table 7-1 summarizes the IAEA examples completed to date, including brief descriptions and
the primary physics assessed. In FY26, the assessment was extended toward larger problems with
more degrees of freedom and contact points by applying VP4 to a full-core configuration and
comparing the results with the corresponding symmetric model. This effort increased problem
complexity by including more ducts and contact regions to evaluate whether successive duct
contact affects contact stiffness estimation or introduces solution instabilities. Scaling to larger
cores revealed known limitations of the current node-on-face contact algorithm. It requires
substantial memory and often fails to converge after roughly half of the prescribed time steps.
These calculations did not use mortar contact, and all solves employed direct rather than iterative
solvers, further increasing memory demand. A new contact algorithm is under development by the
NEAMS Structural Mechanics & Chemistry Technical Area, with initial results indicating
improved stability for larger problems.

8 Multiphysics Demonstrations with Subchannel, Reactor Physics,
  and Thermo-Mechanics
 Chapter 4 and Chapter 5 assessed reactor physics modeling options in Griffin and showed that
the ring-heterogeneous and duct-heterogeneous treatments produced similar results in standalone
calculations as well as in expanded and simplistic bowed calculations. Chapter 6 assessed the
sensitivity of duct wall temperature and subsequent duct wall displacement to thermal hydraulics
model fidelity, finding that MOOSE SCM yields acceptably accurate duct wall temperature
distributions even in cases of intra-assembly and inter-assembly power tilts. Chapter 6 also
assessed the validity of treating inter-assembly flow as a solid, and determined that a heat sink
approach is required to avoid accuracy loss in faster flow regimes.
 We now perform Griffin, MOOSE SCM, Heat Conduction, and Solid Mechanics coupled
multiphysics simulations of the single assembly, seven assembly, and nineteen assembly
configurations to model grid plate expansion (cold to hot zero power) and subsequent bowing at
hot full power. We use the workflow described in Section 2.3, which includes the following
simplifications:
 • SCM is modeled at hot condition rather than cold condition due to lack of assembly-wide
 radial expansion capability in SCM.
 • Griffin is initialized at AHRC condition rather than cold condition due to lack of a fuel pin
 expansion model in the current workflow. This allows the fuel axial expansion effect to be
 included, although it is also included at HZP and cannot be removed.
 • Detailed startup from HZP to HFP is not modeled. Griffin is instead assigned full power
 immediately following the grid plate expansion calculation to raise it to HZP. Griffin’s
 duct-heterogeneous and ring-heterogeneous models were tested for each example problem
 • Griffin does not yet incorporate temperature feedback for solids or fluids. All multigroup
 cross sections in Griffin are computed at 380 C.
 • Inter-assembly flow is modeled as a solid region.
We also make a few notes on the Solid Mechanics model, which was updated in FY26 to include
the assembly internals in addition to the duct walls and load pads. This simplifies the transfer of
assembly internal displacements to the Griffin model. Solid Mechanics models these internals with
a very compliant material to avoid affecting the duct bending response (the internals are assumed
to contribute negligibly to duct bending stiffness).

8.3 Nineteen Assembly
 The three-way coupled methodology was finally applied to the nineteen assembly configuration
consisting of seven fuel assemblies surrounded by twelve reflector assemblies and restraint ring.
This case represents the most realistic configuration considered in this report and provides an
important intermediate step toward larger core calculations. This coupling includes the thermal
influence of the surrounding non-powered reflector assemblies.
 Table 8-3 summarizes the eigenvalues from Griffin for AHRC, Hot Zero Power, and Full Power
with the 3-physics coupled simulations results. The duct-heterogeneous and ring-heterogeneous
models again gave similar trends in eigenvalue across the AHRC, hot zero power, and full power
states. The differences between the two models were modest and remained comparable to those
seen in the smaller configurations. More importantly, the reactivity feedback predicted by both
models was very consistent – both for grid plate expansion and full power bowing effects.
 Examining the centerline displacement plots of the assemblies explains the reactivity change
behavior. Figure 8-17 shows the centerline displacements in the y-direction for ducts 1 (center
fuel), 2 (top fuel), and 5 (bottom fuel), labeled in Figure 3-10, for the bowing case and the evolution
from the grid plate expansion displacement to the bowing displacement, with the bowing
displacement in the solid curves and grid plate expanded displacement in the dashed curves. The
initial grid expansion is identical to the seven assembly model. The bowing case has a significant
difference due to the inclusion of a third ring of ducts and the restraint ring. Ducts 2 and 5 bow
outward into contact with the third ring of ducts, which also bow outward until contact with the
restraint ring at the TLP axial level. After contact with the restraint ring, a reverse bowing behavior
develops below the TLP region, until contact at the ACLP occurs. Once the ACLP contact occurs,
the active fuel region and the lower portion of ducts 2 and 5 begin to push outward, or radially
expand, developing an s-shaped curve. From the close up of the lower portion of the ducts through
the actual fuel region, it is apparent they bow outward significantly, compared to the initial grid
expanded displacements, resulting in a negative reactivity feedback effect.

9 Conclusions
 Several studies in this report tested and compared the multiphysics interfacing between
neutronics, structural mechanics, and thermal hydraulics for the high-fidelity simulation of core
bowing. Single-, seven-, and nineteen-assembly models based on the ABR-1000 design were used
as test cases to demonstrate substantial progress toward a practical MOOSE-based multiphysics
capability for simulating liquid metal-cooled fast reactor core bowing. Remaining key technical
gaps are identified before the workflow can be applied to full-core models.
 Griffin’s sodium backfill capability was verified, which is essential for obtaining correct
neutronic feedback in displaced geometries. Automated sodium backfill reproduced manually
constructed expansion cases exactly for both duct- and ring-heterogeneous treatments. This
confirms that Griffin can correctly preserve solid material mass while adjusting coolant density in
response to thermal expansion. This is an important result because realistic core bowing
calculations require accurate treatment of changing geometry and fluid-filled volumes.
 Duct- and ring-heterogeneous treatment PPR comparisons were made for multiple assembly
configurations and deformation states. The results showed that agreement is strongest in
configurations with flatter flux gradients (e.g., reflected single fuel assembly) and degrades as
leakage increases (e.g., single fuel assembly in vacuum). The overall trend was that undeformed
cases agreed best while expanded and bowed cases showed somewhat larger differences. The duct-
heterogeneous treatment is much faster computationally than its ring-heterogeneous counterpart—
by about an order of magnitude for small models and still several times faster for the nineteen
assembly cases—while still producing comparable behavior. This makes it a favorable option for
future large coupled studies in the upcoming FY.
 Thermal-hydraulic model sensitivity studies for predicting duct wall temperatures (and
therefore bowing deformation) demonstrated that SCM, SAM, and CFD showed generally good
agreement for duct displacement in uniform-power and inter-assembly power-tilt cases, with only
small differences in predicted deformation despite some temperature differences. SCM and CFD
were additionally compared for intra-assembly power tilt case with good agreement. Deformation
predictions agreed well despite observed temperature differences because the dominant driver is
the temperature gradient rather than the absolute temperature alone. An inter-assembly flow study
showed that modeling the inter-assembly gap as a solid domain can be acceptable at very low flow
velocities, but explicit flow treatment becomes increasingly important as gap flow rises and heat
transfer becomes non-negligible.
 A brief update was provided on the verification of MOOSE thermo-mechanical tools against
IAEA benchmark problems relevant to fast reactor bowing. The FY26 work extended prior efforts
by further assessing problems involving irradiation swelling, creep, and contact. Results remained
in good agreement with both historical benchmark participants and Argonne’s NUBOW-3D where
comparisons were available. This provides confidence that the structural mechanics and contact

 79

components of the workflow are capable of representing the duct deformation mechanisms that
drive core bowing.
 The three-way coupled workflow linking Griffin, MOOSE SCM/Heat Conduction, and
MOOSE Solid Mechanics was demonstrated for single-, seven-, and nineteen-assembly models.
In all cases, duct- and ring-heterogeneous treatments produced similar eigenvalue trends and
broadly similar power, temperature, and displacement fields, while the duct-heterogeneous
treatment remained significantly less computationally expensive. Importantly, the nineteen-
assembly model with restraint ring showed the expected S-shape curve present in limited free bow
designs, with explainable reactivity feedback.
 Overall, the models in this report establish that the main building blocks for a steady-state
multiphysics bowing workflow are now in place and functioning at small scale. This work verified
critical neutronics capabilities, demonstrated integrated coupling among physics modules, and
identified the thermal-hydraulic sensitivities most relevant to duct deformation. These results
represent a significant step toward a high-fidelity toolset for evaluating fast reactor core bowing
and its associated reactivity effects. Future work still remains to finalize the steady state capability
– namely introducing thermal feedback and axial expansion to reactor physics, potentially
improving inter-assembly gap modeling, and pushing all physics to larger problems.
 A list of near-term future work including code development needs follows:
 1. MOOSE framework:

 techniques valid at higher flow velocity.
