Refining first-line therapy in ALK-positive non-small cell lung cancer: final overall survival from the ALEX trial and an evolving treatment landscape
Authors: Mitchell Boshkos, Mitchell Parma, Eric K. Singhi
Published: 2026

The identification of the EML4-ALK fusion oncogene in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) in 2007 marked a pivotal step forward in the development of precision oncology (1). ALK rearrangements occur in approximately 3–7% of NSCLC cases and are more commonly seen in younger patients with little or no smoking history, with a recognized propensity for central nervous system (CNS) metastases (2,3). The approval of crizotinib, a first-generation ALK tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI), introduced a targeted option that improved progression-free survival (PFS) compared with platinum-based chemotherapy and helped establish a new therapeutic paradigm for this patient subgroup (4). However, several limitations of crizotinib became apparent over time, including the drug’s limited intracranial activity and the development of on-target resistance mutations. As a result, many patients experienced disease progression on therapy, frequently involving the CNS (5). Additionally, crizotinib treatment is associated with a distinct toxicity profile including vision disturbances and gastrointestinal side effects such as diarrhea, and hepatic toxicity (4).

These limitations helped drive the development of second-generation ALK TKIs, including alectinib, ceritinib, and brigatinib, which were engineered for greater potency, broader coverage of resistance mutations, and improved CNS activity (6). Among these, alectinib was one of the earliest agents to demonstrate strong clinical activity. In the phase III ALEX trial (NCT02075840), first reported in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2017, frontline alectinib demonstrated superior efficacy compared with crizotinib, with an investigator-assessed median PFS of 34.8 versus 10.9 months [hazard ratio (HR) 0.43; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.32–0.58] (7,8). These efficacy findings supported the adoption of alectinib as a preferred first-line therapy across major guidelines, including the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) and the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) (9,10). However, the extent to which these improvements translated into a definitive overall survival (OS) benefit remained an important open question.

The final OS analysis of the ALEX trial, published in Annals of Oncology in January 2026, provides meaningful insight into the long-term impact of frontline therapy. OS was a key secondary endpoint in the ALEX trial, with investigator-assessed PFS serving as the primary endpoint. At a data cutoff of April 28, 2025, median OS was 81.1 months with alectinib compared with 54.2 months with crizotinib (HR 0.78; 95% CI: 0.56–1.08) (11). Although the trial was not powered for an OS comparison and the difference did not reach statistical significance, alectinib showed a clinically meaningful 7-year OS of 48.6% versus 38.2% for crizotinib (11). A median survival approaching 7 years in metastatic NSCLC reflects substantial progress over the past decade. For perspective, the 5-year relative survival for metastatic NSCLC was estimated at 6.1% among patients diagnosed between 2009 and 2015 (12).

Several features of this analysis merit careful interpretation. The duration of response was dramatically longer with alectinib, with a median of 42.3 months compared to 11.1 months with crizotinib (HR 0.41; 95% CI: 0.30–0.56) (11). This nearly four-fold difference reflects both the depth and durability of alectinib-driven responses, translating directly into more time on effective therapy and, by extension, longer survival. The CNS findings are equally compelling and demonstrate an important pattern. Among patients with brain metastases at baseline, median OS was 63.4 months with alectinib versus 30.9 months with crizotinib (HR 0.68; 95% CI: 0.40–1.15), a more than doubling of median OS in this high-risk patient subgroup. In patients without CNS involvement at enrollment, median OS was 94.0 months with alectinib compared to 69.8 months with crizotinib (HR 0.87; 95% CI: 0.58–1.32) (11). Notably, alectinib’s survival advantage appeared more pronounced in patients with baseline brain metastases (HR 0.68) than in those without CNS disease (HR 0.87), consistent with alectinib’s superior CNS penetration and activity. The long-term safety profile remained consistent with the established profile of alectinib, with no new or unexpected safety signals observed over the extended follow-up period despite prolonged treatment exposure with alectinib (11).

The absence of formal statistical significance for the OS endpoint should be interpreted with nuance rather than viewed reflexively as a negative result. ALEX enrolled 303 patients and was powered for PFS, not OS, leaving the study underpowered to detect a statistically significant survival difference, particularly in the context of highly effective subsequent therapies. While the protocol did not permit crossover between arms, patients randomized to crizotinib had access to next-generation ALK TKIs, including alectinib itself, upon disease progression.Notably, approximately 48% of patients in the crizotinib arm received a subsequent ALK TKI after progression, including lorlatinib in approximately 12% (11). Sequential ALK TKI therapy has proven effective in both clinical trials and real-world settings (13), and the median OS exceeding 54 months in the crizotinib arm likely reflects the impact of these effective salvage therapies in this population, as well as the more favorable biology of ALK-positive NSCLC compared to other molecular subtypes. Finally, the notable imbalance in follow-up duration between arms (53.5 versus 23.3 months), an inherent consequence of alectinib’s superior disease control, adds further complexity to the interpretation of mature survival curves (11).

Taken together, these considerations underscore an important point: although OS in ALK-positive NSCLC is increasingly influenced by the availability of effective subsequent therapies, frontline treatment selection remains foundational. Against this backdrop, the frontline landscape has continued to evolve. Lorlatinib, a third-generation ALK TKI with potent CNS penetration and broad mutation coverage, has emerged as an option for frontline therapy (14). In the phase III CROWN trial, lorlatinib achieved a 5-year PFS rate of 60% compared to just 8% with crizotinib (HR 0.19; 95% CI: 0.13–0.27), with the median PFS not yet reached after more than 5 years of follow-up, representing the longest PFS reported for any single-agent targeted therapy across all metastatic solid tumors (15).

In a separate phase II study of treatment-naive patients (EXP1 cohort), the 5-year OS rate with lorlatinib was 76%, numerically higher than the 62.5% observed with alectinib in ALEX (8,16), though cross-trial comparisons remain inherently limited (Table 1). Although no randomized head-to-head comparison exists between lorlatinib and alectinib, a matching-adjusted indirect comparison using extended follow-up from CROWN and ALEX demonstrated improved PFS with lorlatinib (HR 0.55; 95% CI: 0.34–0.88), along with longer restricted mean survival time and higher PFS probabilities (19). However, these findings are limited by the cross-trial design and potential for residual confounding. Although grade ≥3 adverse events were more frequent with lorlatinib, rates of treatment discontinuation and dose modification were similar, suggesting manageable toxicity (19).

Mature OS data from the CROWN study are not yet available and will be essential to contextualize all of these findings. In clinical practice, adoption of upfront lorlatinib has traditionally been tempered by its distinct toxicity profile, including weight gain, hyperlipidemia, and neurocognitive effects (15,20,21). While these considerations may introduce hesitation, they should not preclude its use, but rather highlight an opportunity for proactive patient education, monitoring, and shared decision-making (20,21). In contrast, the more familiar toxicity profile of alectinib, such as constipation, anemia, and hepatic enzyme elevations, is often perceived as more manageable in routine practice, further influencing frontline treatment selection (11,22).

From a broader landscape perspective, brigatinib has shown comparable long-term outcomes in the ALTA-1L trial, with a 4-year OS rate around 66% (17). Ensartinib, tested in the eXalt3 trial, adds further depth to the frontline toolkit, even if it has not been as widely adopted (18). As we now have a number of effective therapeutic options, oncologists now face the welcome but increasingly complex challenge of selecting and sequencing these agents for individual patients, where differences in toxicity profile, alongside efficacy, may help guide treatment decisions (Table 2).

One of the central unresolved questions in the field, one that the ALEX data informs without fully resolving, is whether to deploy the most potent agent upfront or to reserve it for later lines. Increasingly, the data support a frontloaded approach. Real-world evidence from the United States indicates that approximately 42% of patients who discontinue first-line alectinib or brigatinib never receive a subsequent ALK TKI, and 22% die without any second-line therapy (23). For these patients, the opportunity to benefit from a more effective agent is never realized, underscoring the importance of optimizing initial treatment selection. While the long tail of the ALEX survival curve demonstrates that sequential therapy can be effective in select patients, this strategy inherently depends on patients remaining well enough to receive subsequent treatment. Moreover, although sequencing with alectinib followed by lorlatinib has demonstrated efficacy in phase II studies, the reverse strategy has not been prospectively validated and may be less reliably implemented in clinical practice (24).

In practice, the optimal sequencing strategy is individualized, shaped by patient-level factors such as comorbidities, as well as disease characteristics including CNS involvement and molecular features down to the specific ALK variant. Patients with brain metastases at presentation may benefit most from an upfront agent with robust intracranial activity, whether that is alectinib or lorlatinib. Resistance-guided therapy selection through repeat profiling at the time of progression offers the potential to tailor subsequent lines more precisely (25).

Beyond currently approved agents, neladalkib (NVL-655) has emerged as a promising fourth-generation ALK TKI. Rationally designed to be brain-penetrant and tropomyosin receptor kinase (TRK)-sparing (22-fold to >874-fold selectivity for ALK over TRK), neladalkib demonstrates broad preclinical activity against diverse ALK fusion and resistance mutations, including ≥100-fold improved potency against ALK G1202R single and compound mutations compared with approved ALK TKIs. In the phase 1/2 ALKOVE-1 trial, neladalkib achieved an overall response rate of 31% in heavily pretreated patients and 46% in those who had not received lorlatinib, with durable intracranial responses (26,27). Preclinical mutagenesis studies have shown that neladalkib is effective against G1202R-positive relapses with minimal emergence of secondary resistance mutations, supporting its potential as a next-line option after earlier-generation ALK TKIs (28). The phase III ALKAZAR trial (NCT06765109) is now enrolling approximately 450 TKI-naïve patients, randomized 1:1 to neladalkib versus alectinib, with PFS as the primary endpoint (29). If these results confirm the preclinical promise, neladalkib could meaningfully extend the duration of on-target disease control for patients with ALK-driven NSCLC.

Beyond novel systemic agents, there is growing interest in multimodal strategies that integrate ALK-directed TKIs with local consolidative therapy (LCT) to target residual disease, potentially delay the emergence of acquired resistance, and improve long-term outcomes for patients. The single-center, investigator-initiated BRIGHTSTAR trial (NCT03707938) evaluated brigatinib combined with LCT (radiation and/or surgery) in 34 TKI-naive patients with ALK-rearranged metastatic NSCLC, including both oligometastatic and polymetastatic disease (>3 sites) (82%) (30). In the final analysis, 94% of patients completed the planned LCT, and the combination yielded a median PFS of 66 months with a 5-year PFS rate of 51%, comparing favorably to historical outcomes with brigatinib monotherapy in the ALTA-1L trial. Notably, complete LCT, baseline circulating tumor DNA negativity, and lower post-induction tumor volume were associated with superior outcomes, highlighting the importance of patient selection with this approach. Additionally, there is the ongoing LobSTar trial (NCT07415005), a multicenter, randomized phase 2 study, evaluating lorlatinib with and without LCT in TKI-naive ALK-positive advanced NSCLC, with PFS as the primary endpoint (31).

In conclusion, the final OS data from ALEX represent a defining milestone in the treatment of ALK-positive NSCLC. Even in the absence of formal statistical significance, a median OS approaching 7 years with frontline alectinib represents a profound shift in a disease once measured in months. Spanning more than a decade of enrollment and follow-up, ALEX has established a durable evidence base that continues to shape treatment decisions worldwide. As the field advances towards next- and fourth-generation ALK TKIs with broader mutation coverage and deeper resistance control, the central question is no longer whether long-term survival is achievable, but how to ensure that more patients realize that potential through optimal upfront selection and thoughtful sequencing.
