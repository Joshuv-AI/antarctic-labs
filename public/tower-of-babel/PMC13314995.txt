Effects of ginger on microbial succession and flavor formation during Zao Chili fermentation
Authors: Xuexue Fu, Yingchun Mu, Wei Su, Ling Chen, Linzhu Li, Limei Chen, Zheng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To address long fermentation cycles and unstable quality in Guizhou Zao Chili, ginger was introduced as a fermentation regulator, and a multi-omics approach was used to investigate microbial succession and flavor formation. Sequencing showed that ginger addition was associated with changes in the microbial community. At mid-fermentation, Kazachstania and Hanseniaspora dominated the SJ group (74.69% and 17.16%), whereas Pichia and Meyerozyma dominated CK. Nitrite content in SJ was 30.24% lower than that in CK on days 20 and 40 (P < 0.05). HS-SPME/GC–MS analysis showed that, on day 20, the total relative response of detected volatile compounds in SJ was 16.47-fold higher than that in CK; this difference may be partly related to ginger-derived terpenoids, while the ester proportion increased by 6.58% (P < 0.01). Correlation and KEGG analyses suggested that ginger may improve fermentation safety and aroma by influencing microbial succession and flavor-related metabolism.

Introduction

As a traditional fermented condiment from Guizhou, Zao Chili serves as an important carrier of dietary culture in Southwest China owing to its distinctive sour-spicy flavor and probiotic functions. In recent years, with the growing market demand for functional foods, increasing attention has been paid to the organic acids and volatile flavor compounds formed during the fermentation of Zao Chili. Existing studies have shown that the dynamic succession of microbial communities during fermentation is the core factor determining product quality, and microorganisms such as lactic acid bacteria and yeasts directly affect the synthesis and accumulation of key flavor substances including organic acids and esters through their metabolic activities (Choi et al., 2024). However, affected by regional and technological differences, the traditional natural fermentation method of Zao Chili generally faces problems such as long fermentation cycle, poor flavor stability and insufficient batch consistency. Importantly, such instability is not determined by microorganisms alone, but is jointly affected by multiple environmental and processing factors, including raw material-associated microbiota, acidity, temperature, and ethanol. Previous studies on Zao Chili fermentation have shown that acidity, ethanol, and temperature are key factors driving microbial succession, while studies on fermented vegetable products have further demonstrated that differences in raw materials can reshape bacterial community succession and flavor development (Dong et al., 2022; Zheng et al., 2023). In addition, Baijiu is commonly added during Zao Chili production, and the resulting ethanol background may impose selective pressure on microorganisms and thereby affect community assembly. Therefore, improving the stability of Zao Chili fermentation requires not only characterizing microbial succession, but also identifying practical food-grade regulators capable of modulating microbial communities under fixed process conditions. Therefore, how to scientifically regulate microbial community structure and optimize the composition of their metabolic products has become an important research direction for promoting the industrial upgrading of Zao Chili.

Ginger (Zingiber officinale), a widely used traditional spice, is rich in bioactive compounds such as gingerols, shogaols, and α-zingiberene. It has been reported to inhibit spoilage microorganisms, exert antioxidant activity, and promote fermentation in food systems (Asghar et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2014), and is thus often used as a natural preservative and flavor enhancer (Viuda-Martos et al., 2011). In fermented food systems, spices and their active components can suppress undesirable microorganisms while favoring beneficial groups such as lactic acid bacteria, thereby promoting a more stable fermentation process and improving flavor formation (Mannaa et al., 2020). In addition, volatile components of ginger may directly contribute to aroma and may also affect flavor development through microbial metabolism (Pang et al., 2017). Ginger is therefore of particular interest not only as a flavoring ingredient, but also as a potential food-grade factor for regulating fermentation ecology. Nevertheless, its effects on microbial community structure, metabolic pathways, and volatile flavor formation in this specific fermentation system remain insufficiently understood. Most existing studies have focused on spontaneous fermentation dynamics, process optimization, or sensory evaluation, whereas studies treating ginger as a single regulatory factor and integrating high-throughput sequencing with modern flavor analysis remain limited.

In this study, 3% (w/w) ginger was used to investigate the effects of ginger addition on microbial succession and volatile flavor formation during Zao Chili fermentation. Specifically, the dynamic changes in microbial communities at different fermentation stages were characterized, the influence of ginger on dominant microbial genera was evaluated, and key volatile compounds were identified using chromatographic analysis. In addition, multivariate statistical approaches were used to explore the relationships between microorganisms and flavor-related metabolites. Under fixed fermentation conditions, including the same Baijiu addition background, this study aimed to determine whether ginger could regulate microbial succession and flavor formation, thereby improving the stability and flavor quality of spontaneous Zao Chili fermentation. The findings provide a scientific basis for flavor regulation and process optimization in traditional fermented foods.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

In this study, fresh, disease-free Zunyi line peppers with uniform maturity from Guizhou Province were used as the main raw material for preparing Zao Chili. Auxiliary ingredients included fresh ginger, salt, rock sugar, and distilled liquor, all of which were commercially available food-grade products. For sample preparation, the stems were removed, and the peppers were cleaned, drained, and chopped with a food processor. Prior to the formal experiment, laboratory-scale preliminary screening trials were conducted following the traditional preparation process of Zao Chili to determine the ginger addition level. The screening was based on quality indicators routinely used in industrial production, while the microbiome and volatile flavor analyses were performed in the subsequent formal experiment. Subsequently, 3% (w/w) ginger was added to the peppers, along with 6% (w/w) table salt, 2% (w/w) rock sugar, and 1% (w/w) Chinese liquor, followed by thorough mixing to form a uniform mixture. The samples without ginger addition were set as the control group (CK), whereas those with ginger addition were set as the experimental group (SJ). All samples were transferred into sterile fermentation jars and fermented for 60 days at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C) under sealed, anaerobic conditions, without manual gas release or stirring. Aseptic sampling was performed on days 0, 5, 10, 20, 40, and 60, with three parallel samples for each treatment at each time point. After sampling, each sample was immediately subpackaged: one part was stored at −80 °C for microbial analysis, and the other part at −40 °C for other determinations.

Experimental methods

Physicochemical characteristic analysis

Determination of total acids: The content of total acids was determined in accordance with GB/T12456–2021 Determination of Total Acids in Foods, with the total acid content expressed as lactic acid equivalent. Color difference: measured directly using a colorimeter. Nitrite content was determined in accordance with GB/T 5009.33–2016. The content of reducing sugars was determined using the DNS method according to NY/T 2742–2015.

Organic acids

Seven organic acids (acetic acid, lactic acid, malic acid, tartaric acid, citric acid, succinic acid and oxalic acid) were determined by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) equipped with a ZORBAX SB-Aq column (4.6 mm × 250 mm × 5 μm) combined with the external standard method. Exactly 2.00 g of homogenized Zao Chili sample was weighed into a 50 mL centrifuge tube, followed by the addition of 18 mL of ultrapure water. Ultrasonic extraction was performed at 25 °C for 30 min. The mobile phase consisted of 0.01 mol/L KH2PO4 buffer solution (pH = 2.0), with chromatographic grade methanol solution as the organic phase; the injection volume was 10 μL, the flow rate was 1 mL/min, the ultraviolet detection wavelength was 210 nm, and the column temperature was set at 37 °C.

DNA extraction and amplicon sequencing

Total DNA was extracted from Zao Chili samples using the FastDNA™ Spin Kit (MP Biomedicals, Santa Ana, CA, USA). The V3-V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using primers 341F (5′-CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG-3′) and 805R (5′-GACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC-3′), while the fungal ITS region was amplified using primers ITS3 (5′-GCATCGATGAAGAACGCAGC-3′) and ITS4 (5′-TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGC-3′). The ITS3/ITS4 primer pair was used for fungal community profiling in this fermentation system, and the fungal results were mainly interpreted at the genus level considering the potential primer bias in complex fermented samples. For absolute quantification, synthetic spike-in DNA standards with known copy numbers were added to the same PCR system. For the SJ and CK samples, the sample amount ranged from 0.3361 to 0.5635 g, and the recovered DNA amount ranged from 47.30 to 1854.40 ng. Template DNA was adjusted to approximately 10 ng/μL when possible for downstream amplification. For bacterial analysis, 1 μL of a bacterial spike-in mixture containing nine synthetic internal standard sequences was added per sample, and the standards covered a gradient range of 3.64 × 101 to 3.64 × 104 copies. For fungal analysis, 2–5 μL of a fungal spike-in mixture containing seven synthetic internal standard sequences was added per sample, and the standards covered a gradient range of 1.78 × 101 to 1.78 × 103 copies. Positive controls were included during PCR amplification. Indexed libraries were quantified using a Qubit fluorometer, pooled at equimolar concentrations, and purified by gel extraction, with insert sizes of 120–200 bp verified on an ABI 3730 platform. Sequencing was conducted on an Illumina NovaSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Relative and absolute quantification of microbial communities was performed by Genesky Biotechnologies Inc. (Shanghai, China).

Bioinformatics analysis

Raw sequencing data were processed in QIIME 2 using the Cutadapt plugin to remove adapters and primer sequences, followed by quality filtering, denoising, merging, and chimera removal with the DADA2 plugin. After generating the feature table and representative sequences, reads corresponding to the spike-in standards were identified, and sample-specific standard curves were established based on the relationship between spike-in read counts and known input copy numbers. Absolute ASV copy numbers in Zao Chili samples were then calculated according to the read counts of microbial ASVs and the corresponding standard curves.

Volatile flavor compounds

A 2.0 g sample was weighed into a 20 mL headspace vial, followed by the addition of 7.5 mL of saturated sodium chloride solution and 30 μL of 2-octanol (30 μg/mL) as an internal standard. The sample was incubated at 60 °C for 10 min; then a 50/30 μm (DVB/CAR/PDMS) coated fiber was loaded into the SPME device, inserted into the headspace vial, and subjected to shaking extraction at 60 °C for 40 min to adsorb volatile flavor compounds. Chromatographic conditions: A DB-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm) was used; helium (99.999%) served as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1 mL/min, with a splitless injection mode applied. Temperature program: The initial temperature was held at 40 °C for 5 min, then increased to 150 °C at a rate of 5 °C /min and held for 3 min, followed by a further increase to 240 °C at 5 °C /min and a final hold for 5 min. Mass spectrometry conditions: An electron impact (EI) ion source was employed with an electron energy of 70 eV; the ion source temperature was set at 230 °C, the transfer line temperature at 240 °C, and data acquisition was performed in the mass range of 50–450 amu. The experimental results were compared with the NIST database, and compounds with a matching score higher than 700 were retained. After compound identification, volatile compounds were semi-quantified by comparing their peak areas with that of 2-octanol. The obtained values were used for relative comparison among samples and should be interpreted as relative semi-quantitative levels rather than absolute concentrations fully corrected for matrix effects. The calculation formula was as follows:Ci=Cis×Ai×30/Ais×2.0

Where: Ci is the mass fraction of any component (μg/kg); Cis is the mass fraction of the internal standard (μg/kg); Ai is the chromatographic peak area of any component; Ais is the chromatographic peak area of the internal standard; 30 is the volume of the internal standard (μL); and 2.0 is the mass of the sample to be tested (g).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with at least three replicates, and the data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted using SPSS 26.0, with a significance level set at P < 0.05; Principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) modeling were performed using SIMCA 14.1 software; Heatmaps were generated using TBtools, bar charts were plotted with Origin 2024, and metabolic pathway analysis was carried out based on the MetaboAnalyst 6.0 platform (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/). And the related networks were visualized using Cytoscape and Gephi software.

Results and discussion

Analysis of physicochemical parameters

In this study, by monitoring key physicochemical indicators including pH, total acids, reducing sugars, nitrite and color difference, the dynamic effects of ginger addition on the Zao Chili fermentation system were evaluated. The fermentation process could be divided into a rapid acidification stage (0–10 d) and a stable stage (10–60 d). As shown in Figs. 1A-B, both groups of samples underwent a rapid pH decrease accompanied by a significant increase in total acid content (P < 0.05) during the initial stage (0–10 d), which was closely associated with acid production by lactic acid bacteria and the massive accumulation of lactic acid (Torán-Pereg et al., 2023). Notably, the SJ group entered the pH stable stage earlier than the CK group, and had a significantly lower total acid content at the end of fermentation (60 d) (P < 0.05), with values of 0.41 g/100 g for the SJ group and 0.48 g/100 g for the CK group. This difference may be related to ginger-associated changes in microbial succession, substrate utilization, and the physicochemical environment of the fermentation matrix (Wang et al., 2024). At the end of fermentation (60 d), the pH values of the SJ and CK groups were 3.66 and 3.69, respectively, indicating that both groups reached a strongly acidified state, with the SJ group maintaining a slightly lower pH.Fig. 1Quality Characteristics Including pH, Total Acidity, Nitrite, Moisture Content, Reducing Sugar Content, Browning Index, and Color Difference During Fermentation of Fermented Zao Chili. The nitrite content exhibited a trend of first increasing and then decreasing; as shown in Fig. 1C, the contents in both groups remained at low levels at the unfermented stage (0 d), with values of 0.04 mg/kg and 0.03 mg/kg for the SJ group and CK group, respectively. With the progress of fermentation, the nitrite content gradually increased; the nitrite content in the SJ group was significantly higher than that in the CK group on day 10 (P < 0.05), but its degradation rate accelerated significantly in the middle and late stages (40 d), with the final content (0.10 μg/kg) being 30.30% lower than that of the CK group (0.14 μg/kg). The transient increase in nitrite content in the SJ group at day 10 may be related to early-stage nitrate-to-nitrite conversion before acid-producing bacteria became dominant in the fermentation system. In contrast, the accelerated decline of nitrite in the middle and late stages was more likely associated with the earlier colonization of lactic acid bacteria and the faster acidification observed in the SJ group. Similar stage-dependent nitrite dynamics have also been reported in fermented vegetable systems, where early nitrite accumulation is often followed by rapid degradation as LAB become dominant and the pH decreases (Huang et al., 2020). However, as nitrate reductase activity was not directly measured, the underlying mechanism of these nitrite changes still requires further verification.

The moisture content, reducing sugar content and browning index of the SJ group were all superior to those of the CK group (Figs. 1D-F), indicating that ginger components might accelerate substrate utilization by promoting microbial metabolism (Wang et al., 2019). Color difference analysis showed (Figs. 1G-I) that the L*, a* and b* values of the SJ group were significantly different from those of the CK group throughout the fermentation period (P < 0.05), with a lower browning index, indicating that ginger addition was associated with lower browning and better color retention.

In conclusion, ginger addition was associated with faster acidification, lower nitrite accumulation at the middle and late fermentation stages, and improved color retention during fermentation, thereby contributing to improved product quality and safety.

Dynamic changes of organic acids during Zao chili fermentation

In the Zao Chili fermentation system, organic acids are key contributors to flavor formation. They not only impart sourness but also modulate complex taste attributes such as bitterness, umami, and astringency, while playing important roles in aroma release and color stability (Shi et al., 2022). As shown in Fig. 2A, the main organic acids in Zao Chili were succinic acid, citric acid and lactic acid, followed by tartaric acid, malic acid, acetic acid and oxalic acid. The composition and distribution pattern were consistent with the previously reported pattern that lactic acid, acetic acid, citric acid and succinic acid were the dominant acids in fermented vegetable systems. At the unfermented stage (0 d), the lactic acid contents in the SJ group and CK group were 33.86 μg/mL and 43.44 μg/mL, respectively; with the progress of fermentation, lactic acid accumulated significantly, reaching 673.07 μg/mL and 663.95 μg/mL at 60 d, respectively. Succinic acid also showed a continuous upward trend: in the SJ group, it increased from 1122.49 μg/mL at 0 d to 2489.57 μg/mL at 60 d, while in the CK group, it increased from 1219.89 μg/mL to 2397.43 μg/mL. Succinic acid has a complex taste of sourness, saltiness and slight bitterness, which can enhance the richness and persistence of flavor (Scutarasu et al., 2021). There was a significant difference in citric acid content between the two groups: at 60 d, the content in the SJ group was 509.59 μg/mL, while that in the CK group was only 56.15 μg/mL. Therefore, the relatively high citric acid content in the ginger-added group during the late fermentation stage may play an important role in improving the overall sensory quality of the product. The results of principal component analysis (PCA) showed that the samples at the initial fermentation stage were concentrated in the lower left quadrant, and then gradually moved to the upper right quadrant with the progress of fermentation, showing an obvious clockwise distribution trend, which indicated that there were significant differences in the organic acid composition of samples at different stages. As can be seen from Fig. 2B, organic acids such as lactic acid in the SJ group contributed more to the principal components. Ginger is rich in bioactive components such as gingerols, which may affect the growth of lactic acid bacteria and thereby be associated with lactic acid accumulation (Widayat, Satriadi, Cahyono, Girsang, Prabandari & Dita, 2020). In conclusion, the addition of ginger had a significant regulatory effect on the content and composition of organic acids during Zao Chili fermentation, especially promoting the production of lactic acid, while having relatively limited effects on other organic acids, and improving the quality of the fermented product and overall sensory characteristics.Fig. 2Analysis of Organic Acid Content and Composition Patterns at Different Stages (A: Organic Acid Content Variation Chart; B: PCA Score Plot).Fig. 2

Microbial community analysis

Microbial diversity analysis during Zao chili fermentation

During Zao Chili fermentation, the α-diversity of microbial communities exhibited a stepwise dynamic pattern, as shown in Fig. 3. The Chao1 index reflects species richness and increases with greater community richness, whereas the Simpson and Shannon indices jointly evaluate microbial community diversity. A higher Shannon index indicates a more complex diversity structure, while an increase in the Simpson index signifies a higher degree of dominance by specific taxa within the community (Wu et al., 2022). Overall, the CK group and SJ group exhibited differences in the dynamic changes of α-diversity in bacterial and fungal communities during fermentation. As shown in Figs. 3A-C (bacteria) and Figs. 3D-F (fungi), for both bacteria and fungi, the Chao1 and Shannon indices showed a gradual decreasing trend from the initial fermentation stage (0 d) to the middle-late stage (10–40 d), while the Simpson index increased continuously. This variation pattern indicated that with the progress of fermentation, the species richness and diversity of the microbial community decreased, while specific dominant microbial populations gradually formed and occupied a dominant position. However, compared with the CK group, the SJ group generally exhibited slightly higher Chao1 and Shannon indices and relatively lower Simpson indices for fungi at multiple time points, especially during 20–40 d. This indicated that during the late fermentation stage, the SJ group still maintained higher fungal community richness and a more even microbial community structure. It can be inferred that the addition of ginger may selectively inhibit the growth of some spoilage or miscellaneous microorganisms to a certain extent, while promoting the colonization and synergistic coexistence of functional microorganisms such as lactic acid bacteria and yeasts, which may contribute to a more balanced fermentation ecosystem. This phenomenon has been verified by relevant studies: the addition of spices such as ginger to plant-based fermented foods can significantly promote the proliferation of lactic acid bacteria and yeasts, and enhance the microbial diversity and functional activity of the fermentation system. For instance, the trends observed in ginger-containing fermented vegetable systems are consistent with the results presented in this study (Chen et al., 2021). In addition, comparison of the data from the two groups revealed that the Chao1 index of bacteria was significantly higher than that of fungi at all time points, indicating that the richness of the bacterial community was higher than that of the fungal community throughout the fermentation process, which is consistent with the common distribution pattern of microorganisms in fermented foods (Wang et al., 2022). The results of Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) showed that the SJ and CK groups exhibited a significant separation trend in both bacterial and fungal community structures (Figs. 3G-H). For the dynamics of bacterial communities, at the initial fermentation time point (0 d), the bacterial community structures of the SJ and CK groups were relatively similar. With the progress of fermentation, the community structure of the CK group clustered toward the negative direction of the PC2 axis during 10–20 d, while the SJ group showed an obvious shift toward the positive direction of the PC2 axis at 20 d. This difference indicated that ginger addition altered the succession trajectory of bacterial communities in the middle stage of fermentation. For the dynamics of fungal communities, the samples of the SJ group were more concentrated in the PCoA space, with an overall tendency toward the positive direction of the PC1 axis. In contrast, the distribution of samples in the CK group exhibited higher dispersion, and their changes fluctuated more significantly over the time dimension. This indicated that ginger addition was associated with lower compositional dispersion of fungal communities at different fermentation stages and changes in their succession trajectories.Fig. 3Analysis of microbial diversityBacterial Chao1 index (A), Bacterial Shannon index (B), Bacterial Simpson index (C); Fungal Chao1 index (D), Fungal Shannon index (E), Fungal Simpson index (F); Bacterial PCoA analysis (G); Fungal PCoA analysis (H).Fig. 3

Changes in microbial community composition during Zao chili fermentation

In this study, absolute quantification was employed to analyze the community composition (dominant taxa with relative abundance >1%, and the rest were classified as Others) and dynamic changes of bacteria and fungi during Zao Chili fermentation. As shown in Fig. 4A, a total of 3 dominant bacterial phyla were identified at the phylum level, namely Bacillota, Pseudomonadota and Cyanobacteriota. Dynamic change analysis showed that Bacillota, as the functionally dominant flora in the fermentation system, exhibited a significant time-dependent increase in absolute abundance, which was consistent with the succession rule of the core flora in lactic acid fermentation (Chen et al., 2021). In addition, the abundance of Bacillota in the SJ group was consistently higher than that in the CK group, indicating that ginger addition was associated with the enrichment of this flora (e.g., gingerols) (He et al., 2025). Pseudomonadota, which accounted for 67.40% of the dominant bacteria on the initial day 0, showed a gradual decrease in absolute abundance with the progress of fermentation, and the decrease amplitude in the SJ group was significantly greater than that in the CK group (P < 0.05). This difference might be related to the combined effects of ginger-derived antimicrobial components, acidification, and changes in the fermentation matrix (e.g., shogaols) contained in ginger (Jeyaram et al., 2025). The relative abundance of Cyanobacteriota showed a downward trend in both groups during fermentation, but the reduction rate was faster in the SJ group. These results indicated that ginger addition significantly affected the succession of microbial communities; its main effect was to inhibit the flora such as Pseudomonadota, thereby indirectly promoting the increase of the proportion of lactic acid bacteria (mainly Bacillota) and being associated with a shift in community succession toward lactic acid bacteria dominance.Fig. 4Absolute abundances of bacteria at the phylum and genus levels, and fungi at the phylum and genus levels during Zao Chili fermentation. As shown in Fig. 4B, a total of 10 dominant bacterial genera were identified, including Levilactobacillus, Lactiplantibacillus, Weissella, Companilactobacillus, Xanthomonas, Pseudomonas, Pectobacterium, Enterobacter, Serratia and Kosakonia. In the SJ group, the absolute abundance of Levilactobacillus increased significantly from 3.78 × 104 copies/ngDNA on day 0 to 1.12 × 106 copies/ngDNA on day 10, and remained at a high level on day 40. The abundance of Lactiplantibacillus increased from 2.50 × 104 copies/ngDNA on day 0 to 2.80 × 106 copies/ngDNA on day 10, then decreased slightly with the increase of acidity, but remained higher than the initial level on day 40. Weissella and Companilactobacillus reached their peak abundances on day 10, which were 1.39 × 106 copies/ngDNA and 7.30 × 105 copies/ngDNA, respectively; their abundances then decreased, and the community succession proceeded toward a steady-state structure dominated by Lactiplantibacillus and other genera. In the CK group, the peak abundance of Lactiplantibacillus appeared on day 40, with a value of 5.82 × 105 copies/ngDNA. Compared with the CK group, these results indicated that ginger in the SJ group could promote lactic acid bacteria to occupy ecological niches earlier and accelerate the transition of the system to a steady state (Goharjoo et al., 2021). Meanwhile, the SJ group exhibited more significant inhibitory effects on potential spoilage and pathogenic bacterial genera.The absolute abundances of Xanthomonas, Pseudomonas and Pectobacterium decreased from 3.33 × 105 copies/ngDNA, 3.59 × 105 copies/ngDNA and 1.53 × 105 copies/ngDNA on day 0 to 1.64 × 105 copies/ngDNA, 9.12 × 103 copies/ngDNA and 4.90 × 103 copies/ngDNA on day 10, respectively, and stabilized on day 40. Enterobacter, Serratia and Kosakonia also showed a synchronous decreasing trend. Collectively, these results suggested that ginger addition was associated with earlier establishment of lactic acid bacteria in the early stage of fermentation, reduce the relative or absolute abundance of several spoilage-associated bacterial genera, thereby accelerating system acidification, reducing undesirable metabolites and potential safety risks, and providing a more favorable microecological environment for the formation of flavor and quality of Zao Chili.

As shown in Fig. 4C, Ascomycota dominated the fungal community absolutely, accounting for more than 86.01% of the total in both groups of samples at all stages, while the abundances of other fungal phyla were relatively low and exhibited differential dynamics. At the unfermented stage (0 d), the absolute abundances of Basidiomycota in the SJ and CK groups were 9.27 × 104copies/ngDNA and 1.22 × 105 copies/ngDNA, respectively, and decreased significantly and continuously with the progress of fermentation (P < 0.05). This might be related to the dynamic changes of environmental factors such as PH value and salt concentration in the fermentation system. As shown in Fig. 4D, a total of 8 dominant fungal genera were identified, including Kazachstania, Hanseniaspora, Pichia, Colletotrichum, Starmerella, Wallemia, Cladosporium and Fusarium. At the initial fermentation stage (0 d), both the SJ and CK groups were dominated by Kazachstania and Colletotrichum, showing similar initial community structures. Among them, the initial abundance of Colletotrichum in the SJ group was approximately 4.18 × 105 copies/ngDNA. With the progress of fermentation, the community structure of the SJ group evolved significantly, characterized by a functional yeast-dominated community with relatively low fluctuations. The abundance of Kazachstania increased significantly from 2.43 × 104 copies/ngDNA on day 0 to 4.75 × 105 copies/ngDNA on day 20, and then entered a stable plateau phase, becoming the absolutely dominant genus. Hanseniaspora reached its peak abundance of 1.09 × 105 copies/ngDNA from day 10 to day 20, and this period of high activity was highly consistent with its flavor-regulating potential. The abundance of Pichia reached 5.70 × 104 copies/ngDNA on day 10, decreased slightly to 3.15 × 104 copies/ngDNA on day 20, but remained stable at 2.50 × 104 copies/ngDNA on day 40, demonstrating a persistent ecological niche. Multiple studies have confirmed that Kazachstania, Hanseniaspora and Pichia are involved in the biosynthesis of esters and some higher alcohols, which further facilitates the continuous formation and accumulation of corresponding flavor precursors and key aroma substances. Compared with the SJ group, the community dynamics of the CK group showed more significant instability. Kazachstania exhibited an abnormally high abundance of 3.59 × 106 copies/ngDNA on day 10, which was much higher than that in the SJ group. The abundance of filamentous fungi remained at 2.23 × 104 copies/ngDNA in the CK group, whereas it was significantly reduced to 8.80 × 103 copies/ngDNA in the SJ group. Gingerol and shogaol, the bioactive components in ginger, possess antifungal and anti-biofilm activities. In the early stage of fermentation, they can effectively weaken the competitiveness of potential toxigenic filamentous fungi such as Cladosporium and Fusarium, thereby significantly reducing the persistence of such risk groups in the late stage of fermentation and fundamentally improving the safety and controllability of the fermentation process.

LEfSe-based analysis of microbial variations

Differences in microbial community structure between the SJ and CK groups were compared using LEfSe analysis (Figs. 4E, F), revealing significant discrimination between the two groups at an LDA log score threshold ≥2. In the bacterial community, 3 species were enriched in the SJ group while 10 species were enriched in the CK group; in the fungal community, 6 species were enriched in the SJ group whereas 10 species were enriched in the CK group. At the bacterial genus level, the SJ group was significantly enriched with lactic acid bacteria genera such as Lactiplantibacillus, Companilactobacillus and Limosilactobacillus, all of which are commonly associated with fermentation performance. Among them, the genus Lactiplantibacillus can rapidly accumulate lactic acid to form an acidic environment, thereby effectively inhibiting the growth of miscellaneous bacteria (Zhou et al., 2022). In addition, Companilactobacillus and Limosilactobacillus are generally associated with the formation of esters and alcohols, contributing to the improvement of sensory quality in fermented products. In contrast, the CK group was enriched with several potentially spoilage-associated bacterial genera, including Aerococcus and Clostridium. These genera may produce off-flavor substances during fermentation and even affect product safety. Clostridium, a common spoilage-associated genus, can generate a variety of harmful metabolites, significantly deteriorating the overall quality of fermented foods (Princic et al., 2024). At the fungal genus level, the SJ group was significantly enriched in genera such as Hanseniaspora and Kazachstania. The genus Hanseniaspora can metabolize to produce volatile flavor substances such as ethyl acetate. Kazachstania dominates in the middle and late stages of fermentation; the ethanol produced by it synergizes with the organic acids generated by lactic acid bacteria to jointly create an acidic environment, which further inhibits the growth of miscellaneous bacteria and promotes the stability and flavor formation of the fermentation system (Gallo et al., 2023). It can be seen that ginger addition was associated with the enrichment of lactic acid bacteria and flavor-related fungi, while reducing the representation of several spoilage-associated microorganisms and reducing the production of undesirable metabolites, thereby improving the safety and quality of fermented foods.

Microbial interaction networks and their correlations with physicochemical properties

Based on amplicon sequence variant (ASV) data, microbial co-occurrence networks for the two sample groups were constructed using Spearman correlation analysis. As shown in Figs. 5A-B, the SJ network comprised 314 nodes and 2270 edges, of which positive correlations accounted for 99.87% (2267 edges), whereas only three edges (0.13%) represented negative correlations. In contrast, the CK network consisted of 294 nodes and 1991 edges, with positive correlations accounting for 99.35% (1978 edges) and negative correlations accounting for 0.65% (13 edges). Overall, the network of the SJ group was higher than that of the CK group in terms of both node number and absolute edge number, with a significantly lower proportion of negative edges. This indicated that the synergistic co-occurrence relationships among microbial communities in the SJ fermentation system were more prominent, and the network structure was more compact. Previous studies have indicated that in fermentation and food microbial systems, the dominance of positive correlations in co-occurrence networks usually reflects the common response of microbial communities to similar environmental conditions or potential metabolic complementary relationships, whereas an increase in negative correlations may be associated with resource competition, niche differentiation or enhanced environmental stress (Röttjers & Faust, 2018). Therefore, the network structural characteristics of high positive correlations and low negative correlations in the SJ group suggest a more cooperative co-occurrence pattern during fermentation, which may be favorable for the sustained functioning of the system. In terms of microbial taxa, both groups of samples were dominated by taxa such as Chloroplast genus and Pseudomonas, accompanied by various bacterial and fungal genera closely related to the fermentation process of plant substrates, which reflected the similarities between the two groups in microbial sources and basic community structures. However, there were significant differences in the interaction composition and centrality of key microbial taxa in the networks. In the SJ group, microorganisms closely related to flavor formation and acidification processes, such as Weissella, Hanseniaspora and Lactiplantibacillus, exhibited high connectivity and formed relatively compact co-occurrence modules. This might imply that these key genera drove and maintained an active and efficient metabolic functional network in the SJ fermentation system. In contrast, in the CK group, microorganisms such as Levilactobacillus, Pectobacterium and Kazachstania also showed certain co-occurrence characteristics, but the connectivity of the overall network was relatively weak. Co-occurrence networks are constructed based on statistical correlations and cannot be directly equated with the exact biological interactions between species; however, their macroscopic structural characteristics can reveal the potential patterns of synergistic or competitive relationships of microbial communities under specific environmental conditions at the systems biology level, and thus have been widely used in the comparative analysis of microbial ecological structure and stability in fermentation systems (Faust, 2021). To further explore the environmental factors driving the differences in the aforementioned microbial network structures, Mantel correlation analysis was performed. As shown in Fig. 5C, there were significant or extremely significant correlations (P < 0.05 or P < 0.01) between the bacterial community structure and multiple physicochemical indicators including pH, total acids, reducing sugars and organic acids, which revealed that the dynamic changes of the bacterial community structure were highly correlated with the evolution of the physicochemical environment in the fermentation system; In contrast, the overall correlation between the fungal community and various physicochemical indicators was relatively weak. Among the various physicochemical indicators, pH was significantly positively correlated with reducing sugars, but significantly negatively correlated with total acids and organic acids (P < 0.01). This reflected the continuous consumption of reducing sugars accompanied by the accumulation of organic acids during fermentation, which consequently led to the gradual acidification of the system. The accumulation of organic acids in the system can reduce the environmental pH and affect the physicochemical characteristics during fermentation by altering the reaction conditions (Tan et al., 2023). In addition, changes in pH and organic acids may also indirectly affect color and other quality-related indicators by regulating the reaction environment; acidic conditions usually alter the reactivity and reaction rate of specific substrates, thereby amplifying the correlation structure among different physicochemical indicators (Lund & Ray, 2017).Fig. 5Microbial interaction network diagrams and correlation analysis between microorganisms and indicators including physicochemical properties and organic acids. Analysis of volatile flavor compounds

During the fermentation of Zao Chili, the formation and evolution of volatile flavor compounds directly determine the final flavor quality of the product. Systematic identification of volatile components was performed using gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) with a match factor > 700. The results presented in Table S2 indicated that a total of 138 compounds were identified, including 53 esters, 24 alcohols, 23 alkanes, 22 terpenes, 5 aldehydes, 2 acids, 1 ketone, 2 phenols, and 6 other compounds. Among these compounds, esters were identified as the key flavor components during fermentation, which was consistent with the research conclusions of Mu et al. (Mu et al., 2025). As shown in Fig. 6A, the total relative response of detected volatile compounds increased during fermentation and reached relatively high levels during the SJ20-SJ40 stage (P < 0.05). The relative volatile response in the SJ group was higher than that in the CK group from the early to middle fermentation stages (P < 0.05). Principal component analysis (PCA) further indicated the differences among samples, as shown in Fig. 6B. The results showed that the SJ samples were concentrated in the right quadrant with high aggregation degree, indicating strong similarity in their volatile compositions. The sample points of SJ20 and SJ40 had the closest spatial distance, suggesting that the compositions of volatile flavor compounds at these two time points were similar. In contrast, the CK samples were mainly concentrated in the left quadrant; among them, the sample points of CK0 and CK5 were relatively close, indicating that the compositions of volatile flavor compounds at these two time points were similar. To identify key differential metabolites, 34 significantly differential compounds were further screened based on VIP > 1 and P < 0.05, as shown in Fig. 6C and D. These compounds mainly belonged to alcohols, terpenes, esters, acids, and some alkanes, among which acetic acid, methyl alcohol, 3-methyl-1-butanol, linalool, zingiberene, α-curcumene, ethyl lactate, ethyl 13-methyltetradecanoate, methyl palmitate, ethyl palmitate, ethyl linoleate, and methyl linolenate contributed most to the separation between the SJ and CK groups. The clustering heatmap further showed a clear separation between the two groups, while samples from different fermentation stages also exhibited dynamic within-group differentiation, indicating that ginger addition and fermentation time jointly shaped the volatile metabolic profile of Zao Chili.Fig. 6Relative semi-quantitative levels of volatile flavor compounds during Zao Chili fermentation. During the fermentation of Zao Chili, esters, alcohols and terpenes together construct the typical flavor profile of the product. Among these compounds, esters serve as the core contributors to the fruity flavor characteristics, and their synthesis relies on the esterification reaction between alcohols and fatty acids generated by microbial metabolism (Moynihan & Petersen, 2004). Terpenes further expand the complexity of the flavor and endow the product with complex sensory attributes such as floral fragrance, fresh citrus notes and woody pungency (Zhang et al., 2023). The relative semi-quantitative levels of several key aroma compounds in the SJ group were higher than those in the CK group on day 20 of fermentation: ethyl palmitate and ethyl linoleate reached 10,473.25 μg/kg and 2100.47 μg/kg, respectively, while those in the CK group were only 788.57 μg/kg and 173.18 μg/kg; linalool and geraniol were 1576.40 μg/kg and 869.79 μg/kg, respectively, compared with 122.26 μg/kg and 9.40 μg/kg in the CK group; limonene and longifolene were 263.03 μg/kg and 1736.39 μg/kg, respectively, while those in the CK group were 3.73 μg/kg and 7.98 μg/kg. The higher relative response of volatile compounds in the SJ group may be associated with the direct contribution of ginger-derived aroma compounds, particularly terpenoids, together with fermentation-related changes in the microbial community and metabolite profile. The detection of ginger-derived compounds such as zingiberene and α-curcumene in the SJ group at day 0, together with their absence in the CK group throughout fermentation, indicated that part of the volatile profile was directly introduced by ginger material. In addition, ginger-derived lipophilic terpenoids may influence compound partitioning during SPME extraction; therefore, the differences in volatile profiles between the SJ and CK groups should be interpreted as relative response differences rather than absolute concentration changes. During the fermentation period of 20–40 d, the key flavor substances in the SJ group still maintained their dominance. The limonene content reached 823.40 μg/kg, compared with 11.20 μg/kg in the CK group, which may contribute to the enhancement of the fresh citrus flavor of the product. In the same period, the content of longifolene in the SJ group was 5209.16 μg/kg, while that in the CK group was 310.62 μg/kg. Its woody pungency characteristics formed a flavor complementarity with the inherent pungency of ginger. In the late fermentation stage of 40–60 d, the esters in the SJ group showed particularly prominent persistence. The content of ethyl palmitate remained stable within the range of 9893–13,063 μg/kg, which was significantly higher than that of the CK group (7653–9307 μg/kg). This phenomenon may be related to multiple factors associated with ginger addition, including possible effects on ester hydrolase activity, microbial community composition, and physicochemical properties of the fermentation matrix (Dobreva & Nedeltcheva-Antonova, 2023). In addition, the persistent presence of geraniol and himachalene in the SJ group further enhanced the layering of floral and pungent flavors, ultimately contributing to a richer and more persistent flavor profile of Zao Chili. Although ester-related changes were observed during fermentation, the increase in ester proportion was relatively limited compared with the overall difference in volatile response. Therefore, the differences in volatile profiles between the SJ and CK groups may reflect the combined contribution of ginger-derived aroma compounds and fermentation-associated metabolic changes, rather than enhanced ester formation alone.

Correlations between microorganisms and flavor compounds

Based on Spearman correlation analysis (|r| > 0.7, P < 0.05), this study assessed the interactions between the absolute abundances of major microbial genera and volatile flavor compounds as well as seven organic acids during the fermentation of Zao chili (Figs. 7A-B). Compared with the CK group, the SJ group showed a denser correlation network dominated by positive associations, whereas the CK group displayed more widespread negative correlations. This phenomenon indicated that ginger addition was associated with enhanced associations between dominant fermentation-related microorganisms and flavor substances in the fermentation system, which may contribute to a more coordinated flavor formation pattern. At the genus level, Levilactobacillus and Lactiplantibacillus in the SJ group showed broader positive associations with several flavor-related metabolites, particularly ester compounds such as ethyl lactate, methyl linolenate, and ethyl linoleate, and they also tended to be positively correlated with ginger-derived terpenes such as zingiberene and α-curcumene. In the CK group, these positive associations were generally weaker and, for some compounds, shifted toward neutral or negative correlations. This trend was consistent with the conclusions of previous studies that lactic acid bacteria were positively correlated with ester substances (Pang et al., 2018). Moreover, in fermentation systems inoculated with Lactiplantibacillus plantarum, increased contents of substances such as ethyl lactate were observed, which suggested that ginger treatment might be associated with changes in aroma compound synthesis, possibly involving lactic acid bacteria-related esterification between organic acids and alcohols (Liu et al., 2022).Fig. 7Correlation heatmaps between key microorganisms, volatile flavor compounds and organic acids during Zao Chili fermentation.(A) SJ group with ginger addition; (B) CK group. In addition, Pediococcus exhibited broader positive associations with flavor-related compounds in the SJ group. This genus showed significant positive correlations with a variety of key aroma components, including methyl linolenate, ethyl lactate, nerol, geraniol, longifolene, himachalene and valencene. In contrast, these correlations were significantly weakened or even turned negative in the CK group. These compounds are important contributors to fruity and floral characteristics in fermented foods, and ginger addition was associated with stronger positive correlations between Pediococcus and aromatic metabolites.

In the CK group, the fungal genera Wallemia, Cladosporium and Fusarium exhibited significant negative correlations with a variety of key flavor compounds including citric acid, nonadecane methyl ester, ethyl lactate and geraniol, whereas these adverse negative correlations were significantly weakened or even eliminated in the SJ group. Numerous studies have shown that these fungi are often associated with lipid oxidation, flavor degradation and spoilage processes in fermentation systems. For instance, the genus Wallemia is a typical halophilic fungus that often contaminates low-water-activity foods with high salt and sugar contents, and produces secondary metabolites that may cause food spoilage and flavor deterioration under such conditions (Zajc & Gunde-Cimerman, 2018). Contamination by Cladosporium has been proven to reshape the volatile components of wine, leading to a decrease in the contents of various esters and thus impairing aroma quality; the genus Fusarium possesses lipid degradation and oxidation capabilities, which can promote the degradation of aroma precursors such as fatty acids;in the context of plant disease infection, this genus is often accompanied by a decrease in the contents of various aroma components such as terpenes and aromatic hydrocarbons in grapes and their products. The bioactive components in ginger, such as gingerol and shogaol, exhibit significant inhibitory effects on these harmful fungi (Abdullahi et al., 2020). This might be the reason for the weakened negative correlations in the SJ group, namely that ginger effectively mitigated the degradation effects of these fungi on key aroma substances.

Overall, ginger addition was associated with weaker negative associations of potentially harmful microorganisms and stronger positive associations involving aroma-related microbial communities, which may have contributed to the improved flavor quality of Zao Chili. Nevertheless, these correlations do not by themselves establish direct functional relationships, and the specific microbial roles in flavor formation still need further verification.

Metabolic network construction

Based on the differential metabolites identified from 138 flavor compounds and 7 organic acids, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the MetaboAnalyst 6.0 platform. Combined with the KEGG database and relevant literature, the main metabolite-related pathways during Zao Chili fermentation were identified. Fig. 8A shows the bubble chart of KEGG pathway analysis for all detected compounds. Each bubble in the figure represents a metabolic pathway; the darker the bubble color, the more significant the enrichment, and the bubble size represents the Pathway Impact (PI). According to the screening criteria of PI >0.1 and -log10(P) > 1, 7 main metabolite-related pathways during Zao Chili fermentation were inferred. These pathways include the citrate cycle, pyruvate metabolism, glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, glycolysis or gluconeogenesis, alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism, butanoate metabolism, and propanoate metabolism. Based on KEGG pathway mapping of differential metabolites, Fig. 8B presents the key enzymes involved in the enriched metabolite-related pathways in the CK and SJ groups during fermentation. The results showed that multiple key enzymes involved in glycolysis, pyruvate metabolism and ester synthesis, such as alcohol dehydrogenase (EC:1.1.1.1), lactate dehydrogenase (EC:1.1.1.27), acetaldehyde dehydrogenase (EC:1.2.1.3) and carboxylesterase (EC:3.1.1.1), were associated with the enriched metabolite-related pathways in the SJ group. This suggested that the differential metabolites in the SJ group were more closely associated with the conversion of pyruvate to lactic acid and alcohol intermediates. This metabolic trend may have facilitated the continuous acidification of the fermentation system and provided sufficient alcohol precursors for subsequent esterification reactions, which was consistent with the metabolic characteristics of synergistic promotion of acid production and flavor substance formation during lactic acid bacteria fermentation (Bintsis & Department Of Agricultural Technology, 2018).Fig. 8Metabolic prediction of flavor compounds in Zao Chili，including KEGG pathway analysis bubble diagram (A), related metabolic enzyme heatmap (B), and metabolic network diagram (C).Fig. 8

Metabolic prediction of flavor compounds in Zao Chili，including KEGG pathway analysis bubble diagram (A), related metabolic enzyme heatmap (B), and metabolic network diagram (C).

Fig. 8C constructed the complete flavor metabolic network of Zao Chili. During fermentation, major nutrients such as proteins, carbohydrates and lipids are degraded into basic metabolites by microbial action, which are further converted into various flavor substances and organic acids through a complex metabolic network. Specifically, glucose is cleaved into pyruvate via the glycolysis pathway, and pyruvate serves as the hub linking central carbon metabolism and secondary metabolite synthesis. Under the action of the dominant lactic acid bacteria community led by Lactiplantibacillus and Weissella, pyruvate is catalyzed to produce lactic acid by L-lactate dehydrogenase (EC: 1.1.1.27). Meanwhile, it is converted into acetaldehyde by acetaldehyde dehydrogenase (EC: 1.2.1.3), and then reduced to ethanol by alcohol dehydrogenase (EC: 1.1.1.1), providing precursor substances for ester compound synthesis (Smid & Kleerebezem, 2014). In addition, pyruvate is also converted into acetyl-CoA by pyruvate dehydrogenase (EC: 2.3.1.54), which then enters the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. Under the action of citrate synthase (EC: 2.3.3.1), acetyl-CoA condenses with oxaloacetate to form citric acid, which is further converted into organic acids such as succinic acid and malic acid. These organic acids not only directly shape the sour flavor profile of Zao Chili, but also serve as important carbon skeleton sources for amino acid conversion and ester synthesis, thereby participating in the continuous generation of flavor substances (Kanehisa et al., 2025). Weissella coexists synergistically in the early stage of various plant-based fermentations. Its acid-producing capacity and function of synthesizing exopolysaccharides (EPS) not only facilitate the rapid establishment of a dominant microecosystem, but also may indirectly affect the release and retention of volatile flavors by altering the viscosity and texture of the system (Fusco et al., 2023). The glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolic pathways are involved in the production of various acidic metabolites, including oxalate and tartrate. Glyoxylate metabolism participates in the conversion of C2 to C4 metabolites, which is conducive to the synthesis of amino acids and flavor compounds (Nascimento et al., 2019). Proteins are gradually degraded into amino acids under the synergistic action of microbial proteases and peptidases, and some amino acids can be directly converted as flavor precursors. For example, phenylalanine can be converted into aromatic compounds such as phenylacetaldehyde and phenylethanol under the action of histidinol phosphate aminotransferase (EC: 2.6.1.9) and decarboxylase (EC: 1.1.1.90) from microorganisms such as Lactiplantibacillus plantarum, endowing Zao Chili with unique aroma characteristics. Fatty acids and alcohols produced by lipid decomposition undergo esterification catalyzed by key enzymes such as acylglycerol lipase (EC: 3.1.1.3) and carboxylesterase (EC: 3.1.1.1), generating ester compounds such as ethyl caproate and ethyl caprylate, which endow Zao Chili with fruity and other flavors. Microorganisms such as Lactiplantibacillus plantarum and Weissella jointly participate in and regulate this series of flavor metabolic networks. The SJ group exhibited unique flavor characteristics, which may be related to the combined effects of microbial modulation and physicochemical changes introduced by ginger. It should also be noted that the differences in volatile compounds between the SJ and CK groups may not be attributed solely to fermentation, because ginger itself contains abundant aroma-active compounds, especially terpenes, which may directly contribute to the volatile profile of the product. The bioactive components in ginger may influence the growth of specific microorganisms, thereby contributing to changes in community composition and enzyme-related metabolic activities. Meanwhile, the coupling characteristics between organic acid accumulation and microbial community succession indicated that ginger intervention might guide the redistribution of metabolic flux among key pathways, ultimately affecting the structure of the overall flavor network (Wang et al., 2025).

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. The preliminary screening was used only to select a suitable ginger addition level and was not designed to establish a complete dose-response relationship. Therefore, 3% (w/w) ginger should be regarded as a suitable working concentration for the subsequent microbiome and volatile flavor analyses, rather than an absolute optimal level. In addition, a ginger-only fermentation control was not included; thus, the relative contributions of ginger-derived volatile compounds and fermentation-mediated microbial transformation could not be quantitatively separated. Future studies should include broader ginger concentration gradients and ginger-only fermentation controls to further clarify the direct and indirect contributions of ginger to microbial succession and volatile flavor formation.

Conclusion

This study investigated the effects of 3% (w/w) ginger addition as a natural regulator in traditional Guizhou Zao Chili fermentation. By comparing the control (CK) and ginger-added (SJ) groups over a 60-day cycle, SJ showed improvements in multiple quality-related indices, including higher nitrite-degradation efficiency, faster pH stabilization, better color retention, and more balanced pungency. Using HS-SPME/GC–MS, 138 volatile compounds were identified. on day 20, the total relative response of detected volatile compounds in SJ was 16.47-fold higher than that in CK, with key aroma components-such as zingiberene, α-curcumene, ethyl palmitate, linalool, and methyl 13-methyltetradecanoate-showing higher relative responses. Microbial community analysis indicated that ginger addition was associated with increased relative abundances of lactic acid bacteria including Lactiplantibacillus, Levilactobacillus, and Pediococcus, which formed a synergistic co-occurrence pattern with the yeasts Kazachstania and Hanseniaspora. Correlation analysis further showed that these lactic acid bacteria were positively associated with lactic acid, acetic acid, and various esters (P < 0.05). Integrated microbiome-metabolomics analysis suggested that ginger addition was associated with changes in metabolite-related pathways, including the citrate cycle, pyruvate metabolism, and glycolysis/gluconeogenesis. Significant differences in organic acids and other physicochemical parameters between SJ and CK (P < 0.05) suggested that ginger addition was associated with changes in microbial metabolic activity and fermentation products. The present findings are broadly consistent with those reported in other fermented vegetable systems, where fermentation is accompanied by LAB succession and dynamic flavor remodeling. However, the Zao Chili system studied here appears to be additionally shaped by ginger-derived effects on both microbial succession and flavor formation. In sum, ginger addition may improve flavor and overall quality through both the direct introduction of ginger-derived aroma compounds and fermentation-associated changes in microbial community structure and metabolism, providing useful evidence for process optimization in fermented foods and supporting the broader application of natural additives in the food industry. Future studies using isotope labeling or metabolic flux analysis are needed to more precisely distinguish ginger-derived aroma compounds from fermentation-mediated flavor formation.
