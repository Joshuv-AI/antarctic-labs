Optimising hyperacute intracerebral haemorrhage management: factors influencing timely intervention
Authors: Edmund Proper, Thanh Phan, Henry Ma, Michael Valente
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBundled‐care protocols, which include rapid lowering of blood pressure, have improved functional outcomes in patients with intracranial haemorrhage (ICH). Specifically, the intensive blood pressure reduction in acute cerebral hemmorhage trail (INTERACT) 3 trial aimed to achieve rapid lowering of systolic blood pressure (SBP) to 140 mmHg within an hour of arrival. We analyse factors that influenced the timely delivery of hyperacute ICH management.AimWe aimed to identify the factors that delay the timely delivery of antihypertensive therapies for hyperacute ICH.MethodsA retrospective analysis of acute ICH patients admitted to Monash Health (January–December 2023) was performed to evaluate time to achieve SBP <140 mmHg. A Gamma regression model was used to assess the relationship of predictors, which included patient factors (age, GCS, admission SBP), process factors (times to antihypertensive medication, emergency department (ED) bed, computed tomography (CT) scan), and system factors (Code Stroke status, ED resuscitation bed assignment, care goals). Median times for antihypertensive medication delivery, neurosurgical review, neurosurgical intervention and anticoagulation reversal were calculated.ResultsOf 209 patients (median age 74 (IQR 63–84), GCS 14 (IQR 6–15), National Institute of Health Stroke Severity 13 (interquartile range (IQR) 4–27), admission SBP 160 mmHg (IQR 137.5–187)), 52% were treated as a Code Stroke. Median time to CT was 33 min (IQR 23–47.5) with Code Stroke versus 121 min (IQR 36–254) without. Patients who received non‐palliative therapies (74%) achieved SBP < 140 in 193 min (IQR 91–552) and 25% achieved this in 60 min. Higher admission SBP, longer times to ED bed and absence of Code Stroke were associated with increased time to reach 140 mmHg SBP (P < 0.01). Other dependent variables did not reach significance.ConclusionSBP on arrival, delayed access to ED beds and Code Stroke activation were key factors associated with achieving early BP lowering. These findings underscore the need for protocolised, rapid‐response systems to optimise ICH management.

Background

Structured response teams (Code Stroke teams) have been deployed globally with the aim to shorten treatment delays for patients experiencing acute stroke symptoms. 1 , 2 The implementation of this practice has aided in the reduction of mortality and morbidity for patients with acute ischaemic stroke due to the timely administration of reperfusion therapy. 3 Conversely, improvements in functional outcome have stagnated for patients presenting with intracerebral haemorrhage (ICH) over the same period. 4 Despite initiatives to address primary prevention, ICH still represents one‐fifth of all strokes and is associated with a higher degree of mortality and morbidity. 3 , 5 Hyperacute treatment of ICH is also significantly delayed when compared to ischaemic stroke. 6

To optimise the hyperacute care of patients with ICH, bundled‐care models have been suggested with the aim of initiating ICH‐specific medical therapies in a timely manner. The INTERACT 3 trial 7 demonstrated that an accelerated care bundle improved outcomes among patients presenting within 6 h of ICH onset. The bundled model aimed to achieve a target blood pressure (BP) of 140 systolic within 1 h of arrival, as well as treating fever, hyperglycaemia and warfarin‐related coagulopathies. The principle of antihypertensive treatment is to reduce early haematoma expansion, which has been shown to predict poor outcomes. 8 , 9 Optimisation of other physiological factors such as hyperglycaemia, pyrexia and intracranial pressure is hypothesised to reduce the toxic effects of haemorrhage. 10

In parallel with bundled‐care models, minimally invasive neurosurgical and haematological interventions have demonstrated considerable promise. Updated guidelines now support the use of minimally invasive haematoma evacuation and anticoagulation reversal within specified clinical criteria. 11 Notably, the early minimally‐invasion removal of inter cerebral hemorrahage (ENRICH) trial demonstrated meaningful functional gains at 180 days for patients with lobar haemorrhage treated within 24 h from onset, 12 despite earlier neutral findings of minimally invasive surgery plus alteplase (rt‐PA) for intercerebral hemorrahage evacuation (MISTIE) III. 13 Embedding these interventions within a structured ‘Code ICH’ pathway represents a potentially impactful next step for ICH management.

We aimed to identify the factors that delay the timely delivery of antihypertensive therapies for hyperacute ICH management for patients with acute ICH at Monash Hospital using existing electronic medical record (EMR) data. We also surmise the standard pathway taken by ICH patients through their acute care journey.

Methods

A retrospective study was conducted which included all patients admitted to Monash Health with ICH between January 2023 and December 2023. Data were accessed from the Monash Health Electronic Medical Record (EMR).

All patients who presented to Monash Health with an admission diagnosis of ICH were initially eligible for the study. Patients were excluded from the study if they had no evidence of ICH on imaging or had ICH associated with a post‐procedure complication. Other patients were excluded from analysis if they had known chronic intracranial haemorrhage (rather than hyperacute/acute haemorrhage), extension of known haemorrhagic metastasis or haemorrhagic transformation of a known ischaemic stroke during their inpatient admission.

Baseline variables included demographic data, age, gender, premorbid modified Rankin Scale (mRS), comorbidities and regular medications. The location of referral (general practitioner, emergency department (ED), external hospital or inpatient Code Stroke) was collected and the time of onset was gathered from clinical documentation. Clinical variables included baseline BP, Code Stroke activation time, imaging acquisition time, baseline Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), National Institute of Health Stroke Severity (NIHSS) score, time to blood draw, platelet count, international normalised ratio (INR) and Goals of Care (GOC) on admission. Where values were missing for the NIHSS and GCS, a result was derived based on documented clinical examination. GOC ‘A’ denotes full resuscitation and ‘B’ limited resuscitation. GOC ‘C’ indicates the patient is for symptomatic and comfort‐based measures, and ‘D’ indicates palliative management. Imaging variables of ICH volume, ICH location and presence of intraventricular extension were also included. ICH volume was calculated on the initial CT brain using the ABC/2 formula for ICH. Treatment variables included initiation time for antihypertensive agents, intubation, High Dependency Unit (HDU) or intensive care unit admission, neurosurgical review and anticoagulant reversal time.

The time to systolic blood pressure (SBP) of 140 mmHg was collected from observation charts on the EMR. If initial SBP was less than 140 mmHg then the time to reaching a target BP was recorded as the time of the first documented BP. If the SBP target was not achieved within 24 h, the time was documented as 1440 min (24 h). The time to treatment (such as anticoagulation reversal or neurosurgical procedure), imaging, Code Stroke or other variable is defined as the time taken from patient arrival at the ED triage until the event occurred.

Statement of ethics

The study was approved by the Monash Health Human Research Ethics Committee. A waiver for individual consent was provided due to the low‐risk nature of research.

Statistics

The primary analysis analysed the relationship between predictors and time to 140 SBP. A generalised linear model (GLM) with a Gamma distribution was employed. This approach was selected due to the positive, right‐skewed nature of the outcome variable ‘time to systolic BP 140’, which is characteristic of time‐to‐event data, and the presence of heteroscedasticity and non‐normality observed in preliminary ordinary least squares (OLS) regression analyses (supplemental figure). Independent variables included age, GCS, NIHSS, admission SBP, time to receive antihypertensive, arrival to ED bed, arrival to CT scan, Code Stroke activation, ED resus bed location and GOC allocation. The model was fitted using the glm function in R, with the family = Gamma (link = ‘log’) specification. Fisher scoring iterations indicated adequate fit without significant overdispersion. Model adequacy was assessed through diagnostic plots, including a residual versus fitted values plot generated using the plot function in R (Figure S1). Variance inflation factor was utilised to assess for multicollinearity which indicated none of the included variables exceeded a threshold of 5.

Baseline patient characteristics were summarised using medians and interquartile range (IQR). Median times for neurosurgical review, intervention and anticoagulation reversal were calculated and summarised plainly. In‐table comparisons were performed using χ2 test, Mann–Whitney U test or ANOVA as appropriate. Statistics were performed using R (version 4.2.0) and RStudio (version 2025.05.0 + 496).

Results

Demographics

A total of 209 patients presented with acute ICH across Monash Health sites between 1 January and 31 December 2023. The median age was 72 years (IQR 61.0, 82.0), with 48.8% of patients being females (N = 102). Most patients were functionally independent at baseline (81.8%, N = 171) with baseline mRS of 0–2. A total of 43 patients (20.6%) were on baseline anticoagulation medication. Pre‐existing hypertension (HTN) (129 patients, 61.7% of total) was the most common comorbidity. Median scores were 12.5 (IQR 4.0–27.0) on the NIHSS and 13 (IQR 6–15) on the GCS.

Arrival to the ED occurred in a median of 189 min (IQR 91–561) from the onset of stroke symptoms, with a median of 54 min (29–133) between arrival and CT scan. There was a median ICH volume of 20.1 mL (IQR 5.9–41.2).

Time to SBP ≤140 and antihypertensive treatment

Median SBP on admission was 160 mmHg (IQR 138–187). Many patients (47.8%, N = 100) received IV antihypertensive medication in the first 24 h of care. The median time of administration was 193 min (93–530) and resulted in a target SBP of <140 mmHg being reached in a median of 198 min (64–500). In total, 183 patients (87.6%) achieved SBP targets of <140 mmHg within 24 h, and 24.9% of patients (N = 52) achieved BP targets in 60 min or less.

The regression model demonstrated a significant association between time to SBP target and admission SB P (absolute risk reduction (aRR) 1.25, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.17–1.34), Code Stroke activation (aRR 0.68, 95% CI: 0.46–0.99) and delay to ED beds (aRR 1.03, 95% CI: 1.01–1.06). Neurosurgical and anticoagulant management

Neurosurgical review occurred in 70.8% of patients (n = 148), with 7.2% (n = 15) undergoing surgical evacuation. Patients not reviewed by neurosurgery were older (median age 81.0 vs 71.0 years, P < 0.05) and had lower NIHSS scores (median 7.0 vs 16.0, P < 0.05) and smaller ICH volumes (median 8.1 mL vs 24.5 mL, P < 0.05). These patients also tended to present later to ED (median 262.0 vs 180.0 min from onset, P = 0.261). Among those who were reviewed, neurosurgical consultation occurred at a median of 202 min from arrival (IQR 101–370), with a median time to surgery of 247 min (IQR 137–365).

Of patients who were on anticoagulation at baseline (N = 43), 53.5% received anticoagulation reversal. Haematology was consulted in 25 cases and trended towards lower NIHSS scores (median 8.5 vs 14.0, P = 0.340). Haematology consultation occurred at a median of 140 min from arrival (IQR 64–412), with reversal agent administered at a median of 194 min (IQR 115.5–407.5). Discussion

In this retrospective single‐centre observational study, traditional Code Stroke pathways, ED bed availability and admission BP were associated with achieving target BP. While the uncorrected data showed that Code Stroke patients took longer to reach target SBP, after correction for baseline imbalances, this association moved in the opposite direction. Code Stroke patients also trended towards faster times to surgical evacuation of haematoma and anticoagulation reversal as displayed in the workflow diagram (Fig. 3). These findings highlight the need to continue acute management of ICH with appropriate Code Stroke staff and systems, and perhaps build upon modern Code Stroke systems with specific protocols to address the management challenges unique to ICH.

While acute ischaemic stroke patients can receive key interventions on the CT table or be transported directly to the neurointerventional lab for definitive management, BP lowering in ICH patients requires close nursing supervision. A delay to finding an ED bed (where close nursing supervision can occur) was associated with significant delays to reaching target BP in our cohort of ICH patients. This is consistent with recent findings of Intensive Blood Pressure Reduction in Acute Cerebral Hemorrhage Trial (INTERACT3), where patients who were cared for under a tailored bundled‐care package specific for ICH reached SBP targets more promptly. 7 Prompt access to an ED bed and nurse may be an additional systemic factor to be added to a bundled‐care model. Nevertheless, few patients in our study achieved the aggressive early blood pressure reduction targets outlined in recent multi‐centre trials. For example, patients in the active arm of the INTERACT 3 trial achieved a SBP of 138 at a median of 2.3 h. 7 In our case, only 42 patients (20%) reached target SBP in 1 h and the median achieved target at 3.3 h.

A key challenge in pre‐hospital ICH care is the inability to differentiate ischaemic stroke from ICH on clinical grounds alone, making neuroimaging an essential but time‐consuming hurdle. The INTERACT 4 trial addressed this directly, assessing pre‐hospital antihypertensive therapy in patients presenting with acute undifferentiated lateralising motor deficit and hypertension. 14 While treatment was associated with harm in those subsequently diagnosed with ischaemic stroke, patients with haemorrhagic stroke showed improved functional outcomes with the earliest possible intervention. 14 This suggests that pre‐hospital care pathways and existing Code Stroke frameworks remain integral to ICH outcomes and that any ‘Code ICH’ model must be developed in close alignment with them rather than in isolation.

An element of therapeutic nihilism has historically been a feature in the acute treatment of ICH, especially in the era preceding advanced neurosurgical intervention and anticoagulant reversal agents. 15 Patients who had intensive therapies withdrawn (as declared by a GOC‐C or GOC‐D decision) demonstrated longer delays to reaching a target BP than those with full or partial resuscitative measures (GOC‐A or ‐B). If it is a previously recalcitrant illness, clinicians may still hold an unconscious bias towards earlier withdrawal of care for patients with ICH as opposed to their ischaemic counterparts. 16

This study has several important limitations. First, as a single‐centre, retrospective study, the collected data may not be widely generalisable and require external validation. Past experience can only guide future approaches to a degree; for instance, traditional antihypertensive agents used do not reflect practice in the most recent randomised. 7 Given the complex nature of the patient pathway through acute care, it is possible that key variables have also been unintentionally omitted. Furthermore, complex system relationships that were non‐linear were not assessed. In future, network analysis may reveal more complex relationships between demographic, clinical and treatment variables. The study was also not designed to detect differences in neurosurgical and anticoagulant management.

Conclusion

The main factors associated with reducing delay to BP targets in acute ICH presentations were admission BP, delayed access to ED beds and Code Stroke activation. These findings underscore the need for protocolised, rapid‐response systems targeted to hyperacute management of ICH patients.
