From Chewing to Chirping: The Misophonia Audiovisual Trigger Archive (MATA)
Authors: Sewon Oh, Katherine Palmer, Danielle Sabatina, Alena Pietrini, Christian O’Reilly, Svetlana V Shinkareva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMisophonia is an emerging condition in which everyday sounds, such as chewing, sniffing, or tapping, evoke disproportionately intense emotional and physiological responses. Despite growing recognition of its clinical significance, progress in understanding misophonia has been hindered by the limited availability of standardized and ecologically valid stimulus sets. Here, we present a large, open-access archive of over 1,400 five-second audiovisual clips spanning 12 empirically informed categories of misophonic triggers. This resource includes a diverse array of real-world triggers and extends beyond commonly studied orofacial movement-related sounds, while its audiovisual format enables systematic investigation of how visual context shapes responses to misophonic sounds. The archive lowers the barrier for laboratories to study misophonia, promotes reproducibility across sites, and may support applications ranging from crowdsourced assessments of population-level sensitivities to machine learning approaches for automated trigger detection. By providing a large and diverse audiovisual misophonia stimulus repository, this resource is designed to accelerate mechanistic, clinical, and translational research on misophonia and related sensory-emotional phenomena.Subject terms: Human behaviour, Insula

Background & Summary

Context

Misophonia is an emerging condition in which everyday sounds, such as chewing, sniffing, or tapping, evoke disproportionately intense emotional and physiological responses1,2. Far from being a minor annoyance, these sounds can elicit acute anger, distress, and autonomic arousal, often driving individuals to engage in avoidance or escape behaviors [e.g.3]. Recent population-based estimates suggest that nearly one in twenty individuals in the United States experiences misophonia4. Although oral and nasal sounds are most frequently reported, virtually any repetitive environmental noise can become a trigger4,5. Misophonia has been linked to impaired quality of life6, elevated anxiety in youth7, and heightened risk of self-harm in young adults8. Together, these findings underscore misophonia as a prevalent and clinically significant condition warranting systematic scientific investigation.

Motivation for creating this dataset

Many laboratory studies of misophonia have employed standardized sets of stimuli presented uniformly across participants, often including commonly reported trigger sounds such as chewing. While such approaches provide a controlled experimental framework, they necessarily capture only a subset of the diverse and idiosyncratic triggers reported by individuals with misophonia9. A smaller number of investigations have incorporated person-specific triggers [e.g.10,11] or analyzed responses as a function of individual discomfort levels12, underscoring the individualized nature of the condition. Using person-specific stimuli (i.e., stimuli individualized based on each participant’s reported sensitivities) offers several advantages: it allows researchers to capture heterogeneity across individuals, provides a more sensitive test of mechanistic hypotheses, and reflects the ecological reality that responses to personally salient triggers are often more robust than responses to shared stimulus sets. More broadly, expanding beyond a limited set of commonly used stimuli enables a more comprehensive characterization of misophonia. Nevertheless, the use of individualized or ecologically diverse triggers has been limited by practical constraints. Stimulus generation is resource-intensive and time-consuming, restricting broader adoption in experimental research. Several efforts have begun to address this gap. The Free Open-Access Misophonia Stimuli database [FOAMS13] provides a standardized set of 50 trigger sounds across 10 categories, such as breathing and typing. The Sound-Swapped Video database [SSV14] introduced an audiovisual paradigm, pairing 18 misophonic trigger sounds with either their original video sources (e.g., chewing sounds paired with chewing videos) or with positively attributable but incongruent visual sources (e.g., the same chewing sound paired with tearing paper), enabling tests of visual context effects on misophonic responses. Another stimulus set focused on how the perceived source of a sound influences its unpleasantness, and created 44 videos combining unpleasant or neutral audio tracks with unpleasant or neutral visual tracks15,16. Despite these advances, existing resources remain limited in scope and diversity, offering insufficient flexibility to select stimuli that match individuals’ specific misophonic triggers.

Here, we present a large, publicly available archive of over 1,400 five-second audiovisual stimuli spanning multiple categories of potential misophonic triggers. The archive includes multiple exemplars within each category, providing substantial within-category variability. This resource addresses a critical bottleneck in the field: the limited availability of standardized, diverse trigger stimuli. Because misophonic triggers are highly idiosyncratic, a key strength of the archive is its flexibility, enabling researchers to select stimuli tailored to individual sensitivities. By including a broad range of real-world triggers (e.g., Styrofoam scratching, Velcro tearing), the archive captures meaningful individual differences in trigger responsiveness. The audiovisual format further enables systematic investigation of how visual context shapes responses to trigger sounds, reflecting the inherently multisensory nature of misophonic experiences2. Together, this resource is designed to support research aimed at understanding the mechanisms and broader impact of misophonia. The MATA archive comprises potential trigger stimuli only; aversive and non-aversive stimuli used for validation are provided as separate, complementary datasets.

Previous usage

Using a subset of these stimuli, we examined both physiological and subjective responses to misophonia triggers11. Participants with and without misophonia were exposed to sounds, silent videos, and mental imagery of trigger, aversive, and non-aversive stimuli. Five physiological signals were recorded: facial muscle activity (two sites), skin conductance, heart rate, and temperature. Our findings demonstrate that misophonia triggers, relative to aversive stimuli, can be reliably distinguished through physiological responses. Moreover, misophonia and control groups could be differentiated across auditory, visual, and imagery conditions11, underscoring the need for a comprehensive audiovisual stimulus archive.

Potential reuse value

Beyond its immediate use, this archive supports several applications for advancing misophonia research. First, it simplifies the process of identifying and selecting stimuli, thereby lowering the barrier for laboratories that may not have the resources to generate their own stimulus sets. Second, by providing a standardized and openly accessible resource, the archive promotes reproducibility across studies and supports more consistent experimental designs across research groups. Although developed for research purposes, this resource could also be of interest to non-researchers, who may explore it for education, awareness, advocacy purposes, or for clinical applications.

The availability of a large-scale audiovisual archive also opens up novel applications. For example, the stimuli could be used in crowdsourced platforms, where individuals rate the degree of unpleasantness or social disruption associated with different triggers. Such collective data could enrich our understanding of trigger variability and population-level sensitivities. The archive also has potential utility in machine learning applications, such as training algorithms to automatically detect misophonic sounds, an approach that could inform assistive technologies (e.g., earphones that filter or attenuate identified triggers, as suggested in prior research). Its large size further enables deep learning approaches, including the synthesis of individualized triggers based on responses to known stimuli or AI-assisted manipulation of stimulus properties, inspired by methods previously proposed using the SSV framework. Beyond misophonia, the archive may also support research on other sensory-affective phenomena, offering a template for exploring how multisensory stimuli influence affective responses more broadly.

Methods

Stimulus categories

Stimuli were organized according to 12 broad categories, each with a visual component strongly associated with the corresponding sound, derived from the Duke-Vanderbilt Misophonia Screening Questionnaire [DVMSQ17]. The DVMSQ includes a structured taxonomy of common misophonic triggers, grouping them into domains such as oral/nasal sounds (e.g., chewing, sniffing), environmental sounds (e.g., tapping, typing), and human movement-related sounds (e.g., foot shuffling, pen clicking). By aligning our archive organization with these empirically informed categories of misophonic triggers, we ensured that the stimulus set reflects the breadth of triggers most frequently endorsed in clinical and research samples, while also facilitating consistency and comparability with prior and future work using the DVMSQ framework.

Stimulus generation and editing

We created a set of five-second audiovisual clips following standardized guidelines to maximize clarity and consistency across the archive. Each clip was designed to present a target action for as much of its duration as possible, ensuring stable exposure while minimizing variability in onset timing. Some actions (e.g., swallowing) could not be presented continuously because they occur as discrete events rather than sustained behaviors. Therefore, for discrete behaviors such as swallowing, the selected segment could include a single instance of the action, typically preceded and/or followed by brief periods of silence or non-target activity, reflecting the natural temporal structure of the behavior. All stimuli included both auditory and visual components, which were evaluated for identifiability by multiple members of the research team. Stimuli were retained based on consensus that the intended action was clearly recognizable, with identification assessed at the level of the action category (e.g., “biting”) rather than fine-grained distinctions (e.g., “biting a cracker” vs. “biting a chip”). Potential distractions were minimized while preserving the naturalistic quality of the stimuli by controlling background noise during recording and post-processing, omitting full human faces, and avoiding cluttered environments and extraneous visual features.

Stimuli were initially selected to reflect trigger categories represented in the DVMSQ and were subsequently expanded to include participant-specific triggers reported by individuals with misophonia. All stimuli were categorized within a DVMSQ-based framework. Subcategories were defined primarily based on the action or mechanism of sound production (e.g., rubbing, scraping, or contact between surfaces), as well as practical considerations related to organization and usability of the archive. As a result, some subcategories may appear heterogeneous (e.g., rubbing balloon and rubbing skin), but reflect variations of a common underlying action, such as friction-based or contact-generated sounds. We acknowledge that certain stimuli could reasonably be assigned to multiple categories. In such cases, stimuli were assigned to a single category to maintain consistency and facilitate navigation of the archive.

All recordings were captured with handheld devices in landscape orientation to ensure consistency across clips and compatibility with common display formats. Post-processing followed a standardized pipeline using ezgif.com: (1) cropping to a 4:3 aspect ratio, (2) resizing to 640 × 480 pixels to balance visual clarity with manageable file size, (3) trimming to a fixed duration of 5 seconds maximizing the number of occurrences when possible, and (4) converting to.mp4 format for broad compatibility across research platforms. Audio was further standardized through mono conversion, noise reduction, and loudness normalization to −23 LUFS using FFmpeg and Python. Each file was systematically named according to the convention norm_mono_ResearcherID_actionMaterialNumber to maintain traceability and facilitate integration into experimental workflows.

To maximize quality and consistency, all clips underwent independent review by multiple members of the research team. Reviewers verified that each stimulus contained a clearly identifiable misophonic trigger, was free from visual and auditory distractions, and accurately reflected its intended action category. This review complemented standardized recording and post-processing procedures, enhancing clarity and comparability across stimuli. Together, these measures were designed to balance ecological validity with experimental control, yielding a stimulus set that reflects real-world misophonia triggers while remaining well suited for controlled laboratory paradigms.

The 5-second duration was selected to balance stimulus clarity, consistency, and participant tolerability during repeated presentation. While shorter clips may not fully capture the temporal dynamics of all trigger types, particularly those that unfold over longer durations, they provide a standardized length format suitable for controlled experimental paradigms. Researchers may extend or combine stimuli as needed for specific applications.

Ethics statement

All individuals appearing in the stimuli were members of the research team and provided written permission via a copyright release for their recordings to be used and shared publicly with the broader scientific community. Participants in the misophonia and control groups provided written informed consent, and participants in the stimulus-matched control group provided informed consent electronically. All study procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board at the University of South Carolina.

Data Record

Misophonia audiovisual trigger archive (MATA)

Audiovisual stimuli were organized into 12 folders corresponding to broad categories (Fig. 1): body-part sounds, mouth sounds (eating), mouth sounds (not eating), nasal-throat sounds, repetitive and continuous sounds (animal), repetitive and continuous sounds (human), repetitive and continuous sounds (non-human), rubbing sounds, rustling and tearing sounds, speech and vocalization sounds, talking sounds, and walking sounds. The distribution of files across these categories is shown in Fig. 2. All audiovisual stimuli are stored in.mp4 format. The archive18 is hosted on OSF (https://osf.io/e7qbm/), which serves as the primary repository and provides a DOI (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/E7QBM). A version-controlled mirror is maintained on GitHub (https://github.com/Svetlana-Shinkareva/MATA) for ongoing updates.

Validation data

Ratings of trigger, aversive, and non-aversive audio and visual trials from paired participants with and without misophonia are available on OFS. The dataset is provided as a long-format comma-separated values (.csv) file and includes demographic information, misophonia severity, and group membership for each participant. While most column names are self-explanatory, the following columns may require additional explanation:

Stimulus: Unique filename for each trial, including file extension (e.g.,.mp3 for auditory-only [A] modality,.mp4 for visual [V] modality).

Stimulus: Unique filename for each trial, including file extension (e.g.,.mp3 for auditory-only [A] modality,.mp4 for visual [V] modality).

MATA01: Binary indicator of whether the stimulus is included in the MATA trigger archive (0 = not included; 1 = included).

MATA01: Binary indicator of whether the stimulus is included in the MATA trigger archive (0 = not included; 1 = included).

MATApath: File path within the MATA archive specifying the location of the corresponding audiovisual stimulus.

MATApath: File path within the MATA archive specifying the location of the corresponding audiovisual stimulus.

In most cases, generally aversive and non-aversive stimuli are coded as MATA01 = 0. For these stimuli, MATApath points to the corresponding condition folders (i.e., cond_Aversive or cond_Nonaversive). However, due to the individualized nature of misophonia, some stimuli typically classified as aversive or non-aversive (e.g., norm_mono_001_breathingUnderwater1) were reported as triggers by specific participants. In these cases, the stimuli are coded as MATA01 = 1, and MATApath indicates the appropriate location within the MATA archive. For consistency, these files are also retained in their original condition folders (cond_Aversive or cond_Nonaversive). Stimuli excluded from the archive for quality-control reasons are indicated as follows: Stimulus = “Not in MATA because of [reason]”, MATA01 = 0, and MATApath = NA.

Technical Validation

The goal of the technical validation was to demonstrate that a subset of stimuli from the archive can elicit misophonia-relevant responses, rather than to exhaustively validate all stimuli. In line with Scientific Data guidelines, this validation establishes the usability and relevance of the dataset rather than providing complete characterization of each stimulus.

Participants and experimental design

The study included 25 individuals with misophonia (Mage = 24.80, SDage = 7.24; 1 left-handed; 5 males) and 25 control participants without misophonia (Mage = 25.12, SDage = 7.17; 1 left-handed; 6 males), as determined by DVMSQ. Control participants were matched to the misophonia group on age, biological sex, handedness, and stimulus presentation, and each control participant was presented with the exact same set of stimuli as their matched misophonia participant. In addition to the DVMSQ, all participants completed the Selective Sound Sensitivity Syndrome Scale [S-Five19] to facilitate comparison with prior and future studies. Twenty out of 25 participants in the misophonia group met the threshold for clinical misophonia based on the S-Five. Participants in the misophonia and demographically matched control groups provided written informed consent. To enable direct comparison of distress responses across groups, an additional stimulus-matched control group was recruited (N = 25) to rate stimuli using the same distress scale as the misophonia group. Participants in the stimulus-matched control group provided informed consent electronically. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board at the University of South Carolina. During the experiment, participants were exposed to auditory-only and visual-only presentations of trigger stimuli, as well as aversive and non-aversive stimuli. A mental imagery condition was also included but is not discussed here. Participants in the misophonia group (MS-Five = 120.61, SDS-Five = 37.91) rated each stimulus for subjective distress on a 4-point scale (1 = not at all, 4 = extremely). Demographically matched control participants (MS-Five = 42.32, SDS-Five = 11.07), for whom distress ratings were not applicable, instead rated each stimulus on perceived antisociality using the same 4-point scale, following an established paradigm20. Trigger stimuli were individually selected for each participant with misophonia based on self-reported triggers. A total of 36 trigger stimuli were presented per participant. Because some participants reported sensitivity to a limited set of triggers, and not all of these had sufficient stimuli with clearly identifiable visual components or were shared across participants, repeated instances of the same stimulus were included when necessary to ensure adequate representation of participant-specific triggers. In total, 219 unique trigger stimuli from MATA were used across participants.

Aversive and non-aversive control stimuli were drawn from predefined sets consisting of generally unpleasant and neutral/mildly positive sounds, respectively, that were generated and standardized following the same procedure as trigger stimuli21,22. These stimuli were curated on a participant-by-participant basis such that any stimulus identified as a potential trigger was excluded from the control conditions and instead treated as a trigger. This ensured that control conditions reflected general aversiveness or neutrality rather than misophonia-specific responses.

Across participants, repeated stimuli were used for a subset of individuals: trigger stimuli for 12 participants (mean = 1.58), non-aversive stimuli for 10 participants (mean = 2.30), and aversive stimuli for 3 participants (mean = 1.33). This pattern reflects the highly individualized nature of misophonia rather than limitations of the stimulus archive.

Each control participant was presented with the same set of stimuli as their matched misophonia participant to ensure comparability across groups. Because matched control participants did not provide distress ratings, an additional stimulus-matched control group (Mage = 19.52, SDage = 1.00; 1 left-handed; 6 males; MS-Five = 28.32, SDS-Five = 23.05) rated the same stimuli using the distress scale, enabling direct comparison of distress responses between individuals with and without misophonia.

Results

For auditory components, participants with misophonia rated trigger stimuli as significantly more distressing than aversive stimuli, t(24) = 11.38, p < 0.001, 95% CI [1.00, 1.60], d = 2.15. A similar pattern was observed for the visual components, with trigger stimuli rated as significantly more distressing than aversive stimuli, t(24) = 8.82, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.83, 1.51], d = 1.67. In contrast, demographically matched control participants rated trigger and aversive stimuli as comparably antisocial for both auditory, t(24) = 0.25, p = 1.000, 95% CI [−0.22, 0.27], and visual components, t(24) = 1.37, p = 0.547, 95% CI [−0.34, 0.10] (Fig. 3). These results demonstrate that participants with misophonia reliably rated trigger stimuli as more distressing than aversive stimuli across both auditory and visual conditions, confirming that the trigger stimuli evoke misophonia-specific responses. In the additional stimulus-matched control group, participants rated trigger and aversive stimuli as comparably distressful for auditory component, t(24) = 1.03, p = 0.947, 95% CI [−0.24, 0.10], but not for visual component, t(24) = 4.05, p = 0.001, 95% CI [0.12, 4.05], d = 1.31 (Fig. 3). The distress ratings for trigger stimuli were significantly lower than those reported by participants with misophonia for both auditory, t(24) = 6.39, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.60, 1.17], d = 1.50, and visual components, t(24) = 2.77, p = 0.011, 95% CI [0.12, 0.82], d = 0.63, supporting the specificity of misophonic responses beyond general aversiveness.

Mean (±1 SE) subjective distress ratings are shown for the misophonia and stimulus-matched control groups, and perceived antisociality ratings for the matched control group, presented separately for auditory and visual components of non-aversive, aversive, and trigger stimuli. ***p < 0.001.

Usage Notes

The MATA_interactive.html file should be downloaded and opened locally (rather than accessed directly through a browser link) to ensure full functionality. When hovering over each segment of the graph, the corresponding folder path is displayed in the upper left corner, and the number of stimuli (relative to the total) is shown at the center of the graph.
