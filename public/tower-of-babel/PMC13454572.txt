Development and validation of a multiancestry and multitrait polygenic risk score for lung cancer
Authors: Yixin Zhang, Jinglan Dai, Pan Gu, Yang Zhao, David C. Christiani, Feng Chen, Sipeng Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Polygenic risk scores (PRSs) quantify genetic susceptibilities, yet ancestry imbalance in genome-wide association studies (GWASs) limits the accuracy of monoracial PRSs in non-European populations. Here, we perform a multiancestry GWAS meta-analysis for lung cancer (76,953 cases and 1,886,372 controls), identifying 87 conditionally independent genome-wide significant loci, including two unreported cytobands. To enhance predictive power, we construct a multitrait PRS (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{PRS}}}}}_{{{{\rm{MT}}}}}$$\end{document}PRSMT) using CatBoost, integrating 32 cross-trait PRSs across three ancestries. Combining \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{PRS}}}}}_{{{{\rm{MA}}}}}$$\end{document}PRSMA and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{PRS}}}}}_{{{{\rm{MT}}}}}$$\end{document}PRSMT, we generate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{PRS}}}}}_{{{{\rm{MAMT}}}}}$$\end{document}PRSMAMT and validate it in independent cohorts (OncoArray, TRICL and All of Us). \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{PRS}}}}}_{{{{\rm{MAMT}}}}}$$\end{document}PRSMAMT demonstrates superior discriminability in European, Asian, and African populations, improves risk stratification, and identifies approximately 10% additional lung cancer cases in the UK Biobank. Individuals with elevated PLCOm2012 scores and high genetic risk exhibit a 12.64-fold higher cumulative risk than those with low scores and low genetic risk, supporting precision prevention strategies.

Introduction

Lung cancer is the second most common malignancy and a leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide1,2. In 2022, there were ~2.5 million new cases of lung cancer and more than 1.8 million deaths globally, with 80.5% of the incidence and 79.3% of the mortality originating from non-European populations3. Lung cancer has a heritable basis, and genome-wide association studies (GWASs) have identified susceptibility loci contributing to a percentage of this heritability4,5.

Recent GWAS-based estimates suggest that the single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)-based heritability of lung cancer in the general population is ~5%5. Although genetic factors account for a portion of lung cancer risk and despite the wide range of treatment protocols, the overall prognosis of lung cancer remains poor, primarily because most patients are diagnosed at a late stage. Large-scale clinical trials have demonstrated that low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) screening can significantly reduce lung cancer mortality by enabling earlier detection among high-risk individuals6–8. However, the current LDCT screening strategy is based on crude criteria, which may not identify a substantial number of genetically susceptible nonsmokers and lead to overdiagnosis in low-risk individuals9. The incorporation of genetic information into cancer risk assessment has been regarded as a promising strategy to complement existing LDCT screening to improve precision and individualization. Polygenic risk scores (PRSs) derived from GWASs can stratify individuals by their genetic susceptibility to complex traits or diseases10,11, enabling improved risk prediction for prevention and diagnosis. The integration of PRSs into clinical and screening frameworks may refine risk stratification and enhance LDCT screening efficiency.

However, most lung cancer PRSs were developed based on only the genetic information of European participants. Among the 32 published PRSs in the Polygenic Score (PGS) Catalog12, none are based on African populations, and only five include East Asian populations. This ancestry imbalance results in limited predictive performance in non-European populations, reducing the translational value of current PRS models. Therefore, developing a multiancestry PRS is essential for improving both the accuracy and fairness of genetic risk prediction across populations and for supporting more equitable implementation of precision screening.

Lung cancer is a complex disease associated with multiple exposures, such as smoking and air pollution, as well as chronic diseases such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), including emphysema and chronic bronchitis, and various biological alterations. These lung cancer-related traits might contribute to risk prediction13. Muller et al. developed a model incorporating lung function and emphysema history that outperformed common models14. However, spirometry and biochemistry biomarkers are not feasible for regular lung cancer screening, and individual susceptibility to lung cancer-related chronic diseases is generally unknown before diagnosis. In contrast, PRSs provide a feasible and scalable approach to quantify such genetic susceptibilities. Previous studies have indicated that multitrait analyses can substantially improve the predictive performance of polygenic scores15. With decreasing genotyping costs and increasing availability of imputation pipelines, the use of multitrait PRSs derived from lung cancer-related traits has emerged as a promising approach for enhancing risk prediction16.

In this work, we perform, to our knowledge, the largest multiancestry GWAS meta-analysis to develop a multiancestry lung cancer PRS from 76,953 cases and 1,886,372 controls. We also develop an enhanced multitrait PRS using a machine learning approach and validate it separately in independent testing sets of European, Asian, and African populations. Our results demonstrate that the multiancestry and multitrait PRS (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{PRS}}}}}_{{{{\rm{MAMT}}}}}$$\end{document}PRSMAMT) for lung cancer maintains strong predictive performance across diverse populations, especially in underrepresented populations, such as Asian and African populations. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{PRS}}}}}_{{{{\rm{MAMT}}}}}$$\end{document}PRSMAMT also has the potential to improve risk stratification, thereby improving the efficiency of precision screening for lung cancer.

Development of a multiancestry lung cancer PRS

We performed a fixed-effect meta-analysis for each ancestry. We then integrated GWAS summary statistics from multiple ancestries using PRS-CSx with a shared continuous shrinkage (CS) prior, which improved multiancestry polygenic prediction (Supplementary Fig. 2). As the PRS-CSx contained approximately one million SNPs, we applied a hold-out validation strategy to avoid sample overlap and the possibility of overfitting. To this end, we customized the posterior SNP effect sizes for each cohort (Supplementary Fig. 3). The PRS was named the multiancestry PRS of lung cancer (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{PRS}}}}}_{{{{\rm{MA}}}}}$$\end{document}PRSMA) (Fig. 1).

The demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants are presented in Supplementary Data 2. To further explore the potential correlations between cross-trait PRSs and lung cancer, we constructed Bayesian networks (BNs) linking cross-trait PRSs and lung cancer for each ancestry. Trait-specific PRSs derived from three ancestral populations were integrated using logistic regression. For European ancestry, the BN included 15 nodes and 54 directed edges (Fig. 5E). PRSs of nine traits [tobacco disorder, smoking initiation, smoking cessation, cigarettes per day, age of smoking, emphysema, COPD, C-reactive protein (CRP) and FEV1/FVC] were directly connected to the lung cancer (LC) node, indicating potential contributions. Similar network topologies were observed in the Asian and African populations (Fig. 5F and 5G).

Improvement beyond the PLCOm2012 model in the UK Biobank

Discussion

In this study, we developed a multiancestry and multitrait polygenic risk score (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{PRS}}}}}_{{{{\rm{MAMT}}}}}$$\end{document}PRSMAMT) for lung cancer that outperformed the published PRSs and helped reduce disparities across populations. Unlike conventional PRS studies, which typically focus on a single trait within a single population, our framework accounts for genetic correlations across traits and linkage disequilibrium (LD) patterns in diverse populations.

Traditional PRSs are primarily constructed simply using genome-wide significant variants with reported coefficients or the Pruning and Thresholding (P + T) method, which often yield suboptimal or inflated predictive performance due to sample overlap and overfitting18,19. In contrast, Bayesian approaches offer several advantages and have demonstrated improved accuracy in multiple studies18,20. For instance, Bayesian approaches enable joint modelling of genome-wide variants to effectively leverage available information while accounting for local LD structures and accommodating varying effect size distributions across complex traits and diseases21. Therefore, it is essential to develop PRSs using Bayesian methods in large, ancestrally diverse populations to increase the accuracy and generalizability of genetic risk prediction. Consequently, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{PRS}}}}}_{{{{\rm{MA}}}}}$$\end{document}PRSMA surpassed most existing PRSs in various datasets, proving the superiority of Bayesian methods with large populations.

Multitrait analysis was demonstrated to facilitate the identification of potential pleiotropic loci and improve the accuracy of polygenic prediction15,22. The high genetic correlation and potential genetic mechanisms shared between the studied traits provided the basis for multitrait analysis23–25. Most multitrait analysis approaches rely on integrating GWAS summary statistics from multiple traits and then constructing trait-specific PRSs to improve the accuracy of disease risk prediction. However, their modelling assumptions are relatively simple and inflexible, which may lead to the problem of false positives26,27. Additionally, environmental confounding effects and sample overlap can further limit PRS performance23,26. Therefore, we tried to directly construct univariate trait PRSs and then integrate them to form a composite multitrait PRS. Previous studies have reported significant genetic overlap between asthma and COPD25. In our study, we observed a correlation between asthma PRS and COPD PRS, reflecting their shared genetic architecture. Moreover, our results showed that the PRSs trained based on European summary statistics had better discrimination ability in non-European populations, highlighting the necessity of leveraging genetic information from ancestries with larger sample sizes. Nevertheless, the predictive performance varied across the three testing datasets, which was potentially due to factors such as the sample size and case‒control imbalance of these datasets.

Investigating associations across multicategorical traits and diverse populations can contribute to elucidating the global landscape of heritable multimorbidity risks28. Our Bayesian network analysis revealed that cross-trait PRSs influenced the susceptibility to lung cancer, with potential interdependencies among trait-specific PRSs. However, traditional linear weighted models (e.g., logistic model, elastic net model) often ignore the interactions and nonlinear relationships between traits. To address this, we applied a machine learning algorithm of gradient boosting on decision trees, CatBoost, to consider these complex interrelationships. After integration, compared with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{PRS}}}}}_{{{{\rm{MA}}}}}$$\end{document}PRSMA alone, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{PRS}}}}}_{{{{\rm{MAMT}}}}}$$\end{document}PRSMAMT could enhance the performance. Our results suggested that traits related to one disease could shed light on shared genetic information that enhanced the prediction power. This improvement likely stems from the inclusion of the extra genetic information of other related traits and other ancestries, which almost maximizes the utility of GWAS.

The effectiveness of PRSs is often evaluated by determining whether they can help stratify populations into subgroups with distinct absolute degrees of risk for adjunct clinical decision-making29. Thus, we utilized a large-scale prospective population cohort to determine the practical value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{PRS}}}}}_{{{{\rm{MAMT}}}}}$$\end{document}PRSMAMT. As expected, it was superior to existing PRSs. Although \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{PRS}}}}}_{{{{\rm{MAMT}}}}}$$\end{document}PRSMAMT showed only modest improvements in AUC values, it demonstrated better risk stratification capability than the well-known PLCOm2012 model did. However, 15% of men and 53% of women (25% of all cases worldwide) develop lung cancer without a smoking history30. Moreover, GWAS have identified lung cancer susceptibility loci in never-smokers31,32, further supporting the presence of genetic risk in this population. Although the PLCOm2012 model was developed primarily for ever-smokers, it may result in reduced sensitivity for never-smokers. The varying definitions or absence of traditional risk factors included in the PLCOm2012 model, such as smoking information and self-reported lung cancer-related traits, may also lead to information bias. Therefore, the PLCOm2012 model may fail to detect individuals with high genetic susceptibility who lack smoking-related risk factors. When different screening thresholds from 1% to 30% were applied, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{PRS}}}}}_{{{{\rm{MAMT}}}}}$$\end{document}PRSMAMT detected approximately 10% of additional lung cancer cases. These findings support its potential for use in targeted lung cancer screening, enabling the identification of high-risk subgroups for precision prevention or personalized interventions. At the same time, it should be noted that although a higher screening threshold improves case detection, it also leads to a substantial increase in unnecessarily screened controls, thereby diminishing economic efficiency (Fig. 6C, Supplementary Fig. 11). Thus, determining the optimal threshold requires a careful balance between detection benefits and unnecessary resource utilization.

This study has several advantages. First, we constructed \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{PRS}}}}}_{{{{\rm{MAMT}}}}}$$\end{document}PRSMAMT across diverse ancestries from the largest meta-analysis of lung cancer to date. Additionally, compared with published lung cancer scores, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{PRS}}}}}_{{{{\rm{MAMT}}}}}$$\end{document}PRSMAMT demonstrated improved performance, particularly in Asian and African populations, thereby promoting the effective application of PRS in underrepresented groups and mitigating disparities in health treatment across populations of different genetic ancestries. Second, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{PRS}}}}}_{{{{\rm{MAMT}}}}}$$\end{document}PRSMAMT integrated multiancestry GWAS summary statistics from lung cancer and 14 related traits, incorporating a rich and extensive spectrum of lung cancer-associated genetic information across diverse ancestries and multiple traits. All the genetic information could be inferred from the imputed SNP array, which was cost effective. This could account for the predictive value of hard-to-measure traits using the PRS approach. Finally, we leveraged the strengths of CatBoost to efficiently handle categorical features and capture complex nonlinear relationships within cross-trait PRSs. We also utilized the hold-out validation approach to reduce overfitting and enhance model generalization.

However, this study has several limitations. First, as most PRSs have been constructed using data from European populations, ancestry-specific variants may be missed due to the limited sample size in non-European populations and low allele frequencies in European ancestries33,34. Additionally, ancestral imbalances may affect the construction and evaluation of PRSs using the hold-out strategy and across traits. Under the hold-out strategy, the sample size available for constructing the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{PRS}}}}}_{{{{\rm{MA}}}}}$$\end{document}PRSMA depended inversely on the size of the validation cohort: PRSs constructed for smaller validation cohorts (e.g., NCI African ancestry) were trained on more samples, whereas PRSs for larger validation cohorts (e.g., PLCO European ancestry) were constructed with relatively fewer samples, which may contribute to performance heterogeneity across cohorts. Cross-trait PRSs derived from secondary traits with limited non-European GWAS sample sizes (e.g., emphysema, nicotine dependence) may contribute little to the integration of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{PRS}}}}}_{{{{\rm{MT}}}}}$$\end{document}PRSMT even if these traits are biologically or genetically relevant to lung cancer. Thus, expanding GWAS in underrepresented populations remains critical for mitigating ancestral imbalances in future studies. Moreover, our model focused on the influence of common variants in this study, whereas rare variants also contribute to disease risk35,36. Dornbos P et al. indicated that a PRS incorporating both common and rare variants was expected to identify nearly 1.5-fold more misdiagnosed T2D cases than the common variant PRS alone37. Therefore, further research is needed to explore the construction of a polygenic risk score for lung cancer using rare variants.

In conclusion, we developed a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{PRS}}}}}_{{{{\rm{MAMT}}}}}$$\end{document}PRSMAMT and demonstrated its superior predictive performance in multiple cohorts. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{PRS}}}}}_{{{{\rm{MAMT}}}}}$$\end{document}PRSMAMT provides an opportunity to improve clinical risk stratification and advance clinical decision-making, particularly for non-European populations, and to promote precision preventive healthcare and diagnosis. The development of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{PRS}}}}}_{{{{\rm{MAMT}}}}}$$\end{document}PRSMAMT provided insights into shared genetic information across traits and ancestries and may be useful in the implementation of precision public health strategies for global lung cancer risk.

Methods

Study population of multiancestry GWAS meta-analysis

Nine multiancestry lung cancer cohorts were included. Existing summary-level statistics were collected from INTEGRAL (Integrative Analysis of Lung Cancer Etiology and Risk)-ILCCO (International Lung Cancer Consortium) (30,799 cases and 58,113 controls)4, Million Veteran Program (MVP) (16,336 cases and 612,076 controls)38, FinnGen (9639 cases and 378,749 controls)39 China Kadoorie Biobank (CKB) (1552 cases and 74,800 controls)40 and Biobank Japan (BBJ) (4050 cases and 208,403 controls)41. Additionally, we performed GWASs on individual data from the UKB (5349 cases and 450,423 controls)42, PLCO (2455 cases and 96,196 controls)43, NCI (1811 cases and 3767 controls)44 and FLCCA (4962 cases and 3845 controls)45. The details of these cohorts are described in the Supplementary Methods.

Study population for PRS development and validation

Twelve cohorts were included in the PRS evaluation (Supplementary Data 2). Specifically, these included PLCO (European), FLCCA (Asian), NCI (African), and TRICL, OncoArray, and AoU, each of which contains European, Asian and African participants. We constructed \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{PRS}}}}}_{{{{\rm{MA}}}}}$$\end{document}PRSMA in all 12 cohorts, tuned the CatBoost parameters in 3 cohorts (PLCO, FLCCA, and NCI), and evaluated the final PRSs (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{PRS}}}}}_{{{{\rm{MAMT}}}}}$$\end{document}PRSMAMT) in the remaining 9 cohorts (TRICL, OncoArray, and AoU). The individual-level data of TRICL and OncoArray were part of the INTEGRAL-ILCCO consortium. The AoU whole-genome sequencing data were used as an independent external replication46. We used the Allele Count/Allele Frequency (ACAF) threshold dataset in the AoU register-level dataset Version 747, retaining variants with population-specific allele frequency AF > 1% or population-specific allele count AC > 100 in any genetically inferred ancestry subpopulation.

Quality control (QC) and imputation

SNPs with low minor allele frequency (MAF < 0.01), violation of Hardy‒Weinberg equilibrium in the controls (P < 1××10−7) or cases (P < 1××10−12), and call rate ( < 95%) were removed. Samples with missing genotypes ( > 5%) or sex inconsistency were removed during the pre-imputation QC approach4,48. Principal component analysis was conducted using PLINK (v2.00a3LM) on the genotype array data.

We used individual TRICL and ILCCO-OncoArray data, which were major parts of the INTEGRAL-ILCCO consortium. In addition, SNP array data from PLCO, FLCCA and NCI were imputed by the TOPMed Imputation server49. We used TOPMed-r3 as the reference panel for individuals of European, Asian and African ancestry, with default imputation parameters. The imputed data were filtered based on an imputation Rsq \ge≥ 0.3 and an MAF \ge≥ 0.01.

Genome-wide association studies and multiancestry meta-analysis

GWASs were conducted on imputed SNP array data using REGENIE v3.350, adjusting for age, sex, smoking status and the first ten principal components. Summary statistics of the nine cohorts were the candidate datasets for the random-effects meta-analysis to increase statistical power.

The ancestry-specific meta-analyses were performed using a fixed-effects model as implemented in METAL (Willer et al.)51 (http://www.sph.umich.edu/csg/abecasis/metal/).

Conditional analysis

We performed a conditional analysis (GCTA-COJO52) to identify independent signals within significant multiancestry meta-analysis loci. This approach can select independently associated SNPs using a stepwise model selection procedure. The LD reference dataset was created from 10,000 randomly selected individuals from the UKB cohort. Using GCTA –cojo-cond and –cojo-slct with the default settings, we conditioned on the SNPs identified in the previous analysis10 and those reported in the GWAS Catalog, to obtain the conditionally independent SNPs.

Development of multiancestry PRSs with hold-out validation

We constructed PRSs for lung cancer based on multiancestry GWAS meta-analysis results using PRS-CSx53,54, a Bayesian shrinkage method that improves cross-population polygenic prediction. PRS-CSx can connect genetic effects across populations with a shared continuous shrinkage prior, which enhances effect size estimation by sharing information between summary statistics and leveraging LD diversity across discovery samples. We applied ancestry-specific LD reference panels from the UK Biobank and implemented the PRS-CSx-auto model, which does not require a validation set for hyperparameter tuning. Population-specific posterior effect sizes were then estimated using an inverse-variance-weighted meta-analysis within the Gibbs sampler (parameter –meta). Additionally, we used PLINK 2.0 (v2.00a3LM, parameter – score) to compute multiancestry PRSs of lung cancer. These PRSs were defined as a multiancestry PRS of lung cancer (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{PRS}}}}}_{{{{\rm{MA}}}}}$$\end{document}PRSMA) and were normalized to have a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1.

However, PRS-CSx typically includes approximately one million SNPs with valid coefficients, which can easily lead to overfitting when the testing set overlaps with the GWAS summary statistics source population19. Therefore, we applied a hold-out validation strategy in the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{PRS}}}}}_{{{{\rm{MA}}}}}$$\end{document}PRSMA development of 12 cohorts to avoid any sample overlap55. For example, when developing the PRS for PLCO, we used the multiancestry meta-analysis results of the remaining eight cohorts except for those of PLCO (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Collection of existing published lung cancer PRSs

The variant effect sizes of 32 PRSs for lung cancer were collected from the PGS Catalog on March 30, 202556 (Supplementary Data 4). Among these, six PRSs were developed using Bayesian methods (e.g., PRS-CS, Ldpred2, BayesR) with > 0.5 million SNPs and one was developed using LassoSum, which contained 46,920 SNPs. The remaining PRSs were developed using Pruning and Thresholding (P + T) or simple SNP weighting strategies and included between 6 and 133 variants. The correlations between the 32 lung cancer PRSs are shown in Supplementary Fig. 5.

When comparing and ranking the discrimination performance of PRSs, we excluded those PRSs developed using Bayesian methods or those with overlap between training and evaluation data to avoid potential overfitting (Supplementary Data 4).

Scores with Ors <1 in each cohort were considered anticorrelated and were thus inverted in all further analyses. All the lung cancer PRSs were normalized to a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1.

Development of cross-trait polygenic risk scores

Using information on secondary phenotypes can increase prediction accuracy in large cohorts57. We extracted the weights of PRSs derived from the GWAS summary statistics of secondary traits to construct cross-trait PRSs for lung cancer. We considered 14 traits related to lung cancer as the secondary traits in the present study, including (1) four smoking-related traits (age at initiation of smoking, smoking initiation, cigarettes smoked per day, smoking cessation), (2) six chronic lung diseases [asthma, COPD, emphysema, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), tobacco disorder (ICD10: F17) and nicotine dependence (ICD10: F17.2)], (3) three lung function traits (FEV1, FVC, and FEV1/FVC) and (4) CRP.

For CRP, FEV1, FVC, FEV1/FVC, and tobacco disorder, PRS coefficients were generated using GWAS summary statistics from three ancestries (European, African, and Asian) in the UK Biobank. The PRSs of emphysema and nicotine dependence were trained only in European populations due to the small number of cases (n < 100) in other ancestries. We estimated the allele effect sizes using PRS-CS21 and constructed PRSs using the ancestry-specific LD reference panel precalculated from UKB.

The PRSs for the remaining traits, including smoking-related traits58, asthma, COPD, and IPF59, were collected from large-scale multiancestry studies, which were conducted based on GSCAN (GWAS & Sequencing Consortium of Alcohol and Nicotine use) and GBMI (Global Biobank Meta-analysis Initiative), respectively (details can be found in the Data Availability section).

Therefore, among the 14 traits, 9 have GWAS summary statistics available from three ancestries (European, Asian, and African), 2 have summary statistics only for European ancestry, and 3 have multiancestry summary statistics (Mixed). In total, we developed 32 cross-trait PRSs for lung cancer using weights of PRSs derived from secondary traits (9*3 + 2*1 + 3*1 = 32).

Bayesian network construction for cross-trait PRSs and lung cancer

To investigate the potential relationships between cross-trait PRSs and lung cancer risk, we utilized individual-level data on 32 PRSs derived from secondary traits (continuous) and lung cancer status (discrete) for each ancestry. We applied a hybrid structure learning algorithm, Max–Min Hill Climbing (MMHC), to infer the structure among these variables, generating directed acyclic graphs (DAGs)60,61, where nodes represent variables and edges represent directed effects. Based on the learned DAGs, we further constructed BNs, which extended the DAGs by quantifying the conditional dependencies between variables using probability distributions62. The parameters of the BNs were estimated using the maximum likelihood estimator for least squares regression models (mle-g). All network construction computations and visualizations are implemented in the bnlearn package.

Development of a multitrait lung cancer PRS through gradient boosting tree

CatBoost is a machine learning algorithm based on gradient boosting decision tree (GBDT), which is specifically designed to handle categorical features. It addresses the prediction shift issue in traditional gradient boosting algorithms through techniques such as ordered boosting and ordered target statistics, thereby improving both model accuracy and generalization ability63.

To exclude irrelevant traits, we pre-screened the 14 traits using groupLASSO64. The penalized parameters were determined by the minimum cross-validation error in the 10-fold cross validation.

We used CatBoost to develop a multitrait PRS for lung cancer (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{PRS}}}}}_{{{{\rm{MT}}}}}$$\end{document}PRSMT) based on post-screened cross-trait PRSs. Specifically, the CatBoost algorithm was used with 10-fold cross-validation and a range of parameter values via the catboost package to find the parameter combination that performed the best in the training set (PLCO, FLCCA and NCI). We performed hyperparameter tuning over a predefined parameter grid. The grid includes a range of values for the number of iterations (100, 500, 1000, and 5000), learning rate (0.0001, 0.001, 0.01, 0.05, 0.1, and 0.2), tree depth (3, 5, 8, and 10) and l2 regularization coefficient (1,3, and 8). The loss function was fixed to Logloss, and the evaluation metric was the AUC, as the lung cancer was a binary outcome.

Three cohorts (PLCO, FLCCA and NCI) were used as training sets for European, Asian, African populations, respectively. The TRICL cohort was used as the validation set. OncoArray and AoU were used as independent testing sets. Validation and evaluation of the lung cancer PRS

Associations between PRSs and lung cancer were assessed using logistic regression. The discrimination performance of each PRS was assessed using a receiver operating characteristic curve. We performed 1,000 bootstrap replicates of the individuals in the testing set and calculated the AUC via the pROC package in R. The mean AUC was then reported along with the 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles, which served as the 95% CI. The association strength between each PRS and lung cancer was reflected by the OR/SD. Delong’s test65 was used to compare two AUCs. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test, a nonparametric paired test, was used to compare the performance of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{PRS}}}}}_{{{{\rm{MA}}}}}$$\end{document}PRSMA with that of each existing PRS. We calculated the NRI and performed decision curve analysis66 to assess improvements in the model’s ability to correctly identify which patients did and did not have lung cancer. We used popcorn to calculate the genetic correlation of lung cancer-related traits. Popcorn is a software program for estimating the genetic correlation of trait effect sizes across populations67. Prospective cohort assessment based on the PLCOm2012 model

We reproduced \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{PRS}}}}}_{{{{\rm{MAMT}}}}}$$\end{document}PRSMAMT and PLCOm2012 model17 in the UK Biobank. PLCOm2012 utilizes several demographic and clinical factors (age, race, education, BMI, history of COPD, previous malignancy, family history of lung cancer, and smoking information) and is a well-known and validated model for lung cancer risk prediction and stratification68.

With follow-up data on lung cancer incidence available in the UKB, we used Cox proportional hazards models to test the association between the genetic risk score or PLCOm2012 score and lung cancer incidence among 226,022 ever-smokers. We classified participants into ten groups by the deciles of risk scores and measured the difference in lung cancer risk among these groups by the HR.

Statistics and reproducibility

GWAS summary statistics in each cohort were used in GWAS meta-analysis. SNPs with P < 5××10−8 was considered statistically significant. Individual-level genotype data in each cohort were used to calculate polygenic risk scores. Sample sizes were chosen based on previously published studies. Samples that have genotype and phenotypic data in each cohort were used to evaluate the performance of PRSs. No randomization and blinding was used. AUC, OR, incremental Nagelkerke R2 and HR were determined the performance of PRSs. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses and images of results were conducted with R (version 4.4.1).

After training in each ancestry using a hold-out strategy for score validation, the analyses of polygenic risk score associations with lung cancer were replicated in the OncoArray (European, Asian, and African ancestry), TRICL (European, Asian, and African ancestry), and All of Us (European, Asian, and African ancestry) cohorts. All of Us cohort was used as an independent external validation. Moreover, we validated the final lung cancer PRS (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{PRS}}}}}_{{{{\rm{MAMT}}}}}$$\end{document}PRSMAMT) in UKB (mixed ancestry) to explore the role of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{PRS}}}}}_{{{{\rm{MAMT}}}}}$$\end{document}PRSMAMT in identifying high-risk populations and in targeted lung cancer screening.
