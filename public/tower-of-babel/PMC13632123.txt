Risk–Treatment Discordance and Fatal Cardiovascular Disease: A Nationwide Analysis Using PREVENT Thresholds
Authors: Adam W Potter, Caroline E Murphy, Bradley J Petek, Christopher L Chapman, Karl E Friedl, Timothy W Churchill, Nisha Charkoudian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe 2026 multisociety dyslipidemia guideline adopted the PREVENT-ASCVD equations as the primary tool for identifying adults eligible for primary-prevention lipid-lowering therapy (LLT). The population-level treatment gap under this updated framework, and its relationship to fatal cardiovascular disease (CVD), have not been quantified.Methods and ResultsAmong 39,277 U.S. adults aged 30–79 years, 1,606 cardiovascular deaths occurred during follow-up; 38.5% occurred among individuals without baseline diagnosed CVD. Per-person risk was substantially higher among individuals with established CVD than among those without baseline CVD. Among 22,505 participants without baseline CVD with calculable PREVENT-ASCVD risk, 23.6% met the intermediate-or-higher risk threshold (≥5%), of whom 67.1% were not receiving LLT. At the borderline-or-higher threshold (≥3%), 35.9% met criteria, of whom 71.2% were untreated. Although per-person risk was substantially higher among individuals with established CVD (11.54 vs 0.97 per 1,000 person-years; P < 0.001), 38.5% (95% CI 35.2–41.8) of all cardiovascular deaths occurred in individuals without prior diagnosed CVD. Among individuals without baseline CVD, male sex, current smoking, and higher systolic blood pressure were independently associated with fatal CVD; diabetes was not independently associated after adjustment.ConclusionsA majority of U.S. adults meeting contemporary PREVENT-ASCVD thresholds for LLT consideration are not receiving therapy, and a substantial share of fatal CVD occurs in this undertreated, primary-prevention population. Retrospective application of contemporary PREVENT thresholds suggests that substantial numbers of U.S. adults who subsequently experienced fatal cardiovascular disease would have met current treatment eligibility criteria yet were not receiving lipid-lowering therapy.Keywords: CVD, Cardiovascular disease, Mortality, Risk factors, Prevention

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of mortality globally, accounting for an estimated 20.5 million deaths annually [1]. In the United States alone, CVD was responsible for 931,578 deaths in 2021, approximately one death every 34 s [2]. Over recent decades, improved recognition of structural, metabolic, and electrophysiological risk factors has expanded preventive and therapeutic options, contributing to meaningful reductions in morbidity and mortality among individuals with diagnosed disease. However, fatal cardiovascular events frequently occur before clinical recognition of underlying disease, suggesting that substantial opportunities for earlier identification and preventive intervention remain.

A central challenge in cardiovascular prevention is that fatal cardiovascular events may occur before clinical recognition of disease, leaving limited opportunity for secondary prevention. Many individuals who experience fatal CVD have no prior diagnosis, reflecting a disconnect between underlying risk and clinical recognition. This pattern aligns with the prevention paradox, in which the majority of events arise from a large population with moderate or unrecognized risk rather than from a smaller group of individuals at high, clinically identified risk [[3], [4], [5]]. While individuals with established CVD have substantially higher absolute risk, the broader population without diagnosed disease contributes a large proportion of total cardiovascular mortality.

This study retrospectively applies the contemporary PREVENT-ASCVD framework to a historical U.S. cohort (NHANES 1999– 2018) to perform a counterfactual analysis of treatment gaps. The 2024 release of the American Heart Association PREVENT-ASCVD equations [6], and their formal adoption in the 2026 ACC/AHA multisociety dyslipidemia guideline [7], have replaced the Pooled Cohort Equations as the standard tool for primary-prevention risk stratification. PREVENT incorporates kidney function, metabolic risk, and social determinants and reclassifies a substantial portion of the population relative to prior risk calculators, raising new questions about how many individuals now meet contemporary thresholds for lipid-lowering therapy (LLT) and how many of those individuals are actually treated. To our knowledge, no nationally representative analysis has yet characterized the size of the LLT treatment gap under the PREVENT-aligned 2026 framework or examined how that gap relates to downstream fatal CVD.

Underlying this phenomenon is the well-established observation that cardiometabolic risk factors, including hypertension, dyslipidemia, smoking, and central adiposity, often progress without overt symptoms. These “silent” risk factors require proactive identification and management, yet gaps persist in their recognition and treatment. Although disparities in healthcare access, socioeconomic status, and other structural factors have been associated with differences in cardiovascular outcomes [1,2,8], it remains unclear to what extent fatal events occurring without prior diagnosis reflect barriers to access versus missed opportunities for risk detection and treatment within the healthcare system.

Here we provide one of the first nationally representative estimates of the LLT treatment gap under the PREVENT-aligned 2026 framework, and link this gap to fatal cardiovascular outcomes in the same population. We retrospectively applied contemporary PREVENT-based treatment thresholds to historical NHANES cohorts to evaluate the extent to which individuals who subsequently experienced fatal cardiovascular disease would have met current preventive treatment criteria.

The primary objective of this study was to quantify the population-level treatment gap for LLT using PREVENT-ASCVD risk thresholds. Secondary objectives were to: (1) quantify the burden of fatal CVD occurring without prior diagnosis (primary-prevention fatal CVD), (2) identify predictors of fatal CVD among individuals without baseline disease, and (3) compare phenotypic and treatment profiles between primary-prevention and known fatal CVD decedents. We hypothesized that primary-prevention gap regarding fatal CVD reflects under-recognition and undertreatment of measurable risk rather than absence of risk.

Methods

Study population

We analyzed data from NHANES 1999–2018 linked to mortality outcomes through December 31, 2019. NHANES employs a complex, multistage probability design to yield a representative sample of the noninstitutionalized U.S. population. We restricted analyses to participants aged 30–79 years, consistent with primary prevention risk assessment frameworks. The final analyzed sample included 39,277 participants with complete data on cardiovascular risk factors, baseline CVD status, and mortality outcomes (Fig. 1).

Ethics statement

The NHANES protocols are approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Research Ethics Review Board, and all participants provide written informed consent. The present study used publicly available, de-identified data. An internal review by the U.S. Army Research Institute of Environmental Medicine (USARIEM) Office of Research Quality and Compliance determined that this analysis met criteria for exemption from human subjects research oversight (Protocol #26– 06R)

Definitions

Diagnosed CVD was defined by self-report of clinician diagnosis of coronary heart disease, myocardial infarction, angina, congestive heart failure, or stroke. Cardiovascular mortality was defined using underlying cause-of-death groupings corresponding to heart disease or cerebrovascular disease. Participants were categorized into four groups: 1) No diagnosed CVD, no CVD death; 2) Diagnosed CVD, no CVD death; 3) No diagnosed CVD, CVD death (primary-prevention fatal CVD); and 4) Diagnosed CVD, CVD death (known fatal CVD).

Lipid-lowering therapy (LLT) was defined as use of statins, ezetimibe, PCSK9 inhibitors, or bempedoic acid. Statins accounted for the overwhelming majority of LLT exposure because PCSK9 inhibitors were not approved until 2015 and bempedoic acid was approved after completion of the study period.

Covariates

Baseline variables included age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, income-to-poverty ratio, systolic blood pressure, diabetes status, central adiposity, smoking, health insurance, and usual source of care.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.4.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). All analyses incorporated NHANES complex survey design elements, including sampling weights, strata, and primary sampling units, to generate nationally representative estimates. Burden and incidence rates of fatal cardiovascular disease (CVD) were calculated per 1 000 person-years according to baseline CVD status (Supplementa1 Table S1), and weighted proportions of primary-prevention versus known fatal CVD were estimated. Among participants without baseline CVD, predictors of fatal CVD were evaluated using survey-weighted Cox proportional hazards models to estimate hazard ratios (HRs), with age modeled using restricted cubic splines. PREVENT-ASCVD 10-year risk was calculated using the American Heart Association PREVENT base equations. HbA1c-enhanced and UACR-enhanced equations were not used because these variables were not consistently available across all NHANES cycles. A uniform implementation strategy was used to avoid differential risk characterization across participants. Risk categories were defined according to 2026 multisociety dyslipidemia guidelines as: low (<3%), borderline (3 to <5%), intermediate (5 to <10%), and high (≥10%) risk. The primary treatment-gap analysis used the ≥5% intermediate-or-higher threshold, at which guidelines support a clear indication for LLT initiation; the ≥3% borderline-or-higher threshold was reported as a secondary estimate, reflecting the population for whom LLT is recommended after shared decision-making and consideration of risk-enhancing factors (Blumenthal, 2026). PREVENT-ASCVD thresholds were applied to estimate eligibility for lipid-lowering therapy (LLT), and LLT use was assessed to quantify treatment gaps. Among individuals who experienced fatal CVD, multivariable logistic regression models were used to compare primary-prevention versus known cases. Phenotypic differences between groups were characterized using standardized differences.

To address the evolution of guidelines over the study period, a supplemental analysis was conducted stratifying treatment gaps by era (1999– 2012 vs. 2013– 2018). Additionally, a sensitivity analysis was performed restricting the definition of LLT to statin therapy only, to account for the limited availability of newer non-statin therapies during the study period. Finally, to address potential bias from missing data, we compared the baseline characteristics of participants with and without calculable PREVENT risk.

Burden of fatal CVD outside clinical recognition

During follow-up, 1 606 cardiovascular deaths occurred among adults aged 30–79 years. Of these, 38.5% occurred among individuals without baseline diagnosed CVD, whereas 61.5% occurred among those with established baseline disease. Although only 16.5% of the cohort had established baseline CVD, this group experienced substantially higher per-person fatal CVD rates (11.54 vs 0.97 per 1 000 person-years; P < 0.001) (Fig. 2).

Predictors of fatal CVD among adults without baseline diagnosed CVD

Among individuals without baseline CVD, several factors were independently associated with fatal CVD (Table 2; Supplemental Figure S1). Male sex (HR 2.74; 95% CI 1.92–3.92), current smoking (HR 2.55; 95% CI 1.68–3.87), and higher systolic blood pressure (HR 1.01 per mmHg; 95% CI 1.001–1.018) were associated with increased risk. Age demonstrated a strong nonlinear association with mortality risk. In contrast, diabetes was not independently associated after multivariable adjustment.

PREVENT-ASCVD risk and treatment gap

Among individuals meeting PREVENT-ASCVD thresholds, treatment gaps were substantial. Among adults without baseline CVD, 23.6% met the intermediate-or-higher PREVENT-ASCVD risk threshold (≥5%); among these individuals, 67.1% were not receiving LLT. At the broader borderline-or-higher threshold (≥3%), 35.9% met criteria, of whom 71.2% were untreated (Fig. 3; Supplemental Table S3). These findings indicate substantial discordance between estimated cardiovascular risk and treatment exposure. Similar patterns were observed when analyses were stratified according to guideline era (Supplemental Table S4 and Supplemental Figure S2), indicating persistent discordance between retrospectively assigned PREVENT risk and LLT use across both pre-Pooled Cohort Equations (PCE) and post-PCE periods.

Phenotypic differences between primary-prevention and known fatal CVD

Phenotypic profiling demonstrated broadly similar distributions of cardiometabolic risk factors between primary-prevention and known fatal CVD (Fig. 4). Standardized difference analysis showed modest differences across most traditional risk factors, with larger differences observed for diabetes status, socioeconomic indicators, and treatment-related variables, including lipid-lowering therapy use, insurance coverage, and usual source of care.

Sensitivity analysis

In sensitivity analyses excluding deaths occurring within the first 2 years of follow-up (Supplemental Table S5), results were materially unchanged. Associations for male sex, systolic blood pressure, current smoking, and socioeconomic status remained consistent in magnitude and direction, supporting the robustness of the primary findings and suggesting that reverse causation from pre-terminal illness did not materially influence the results.

As a sensitivity analysis, Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard models were fit among participants without baseline diagnosed CVD to account for competing noncardiovascular death. Because these models do not incorporate complex survey design, they are presented as supportive analyses. Results were materially unchanged in competing-risk analyses using Fine-Gray models (Supplemental Tables S6 and S7). Male sex, current smoking, higher systolic blood pressure, and greater waist circumference remained associated with fatal CVD. Notably, the association for male sex was attenuated compared with the primary Cox model (sHR 1.69 vs HR 2.74), suggesting that part of the observed association may reflect differential competing mortality rather than cardiovascular mortality alone.

Findings were materially unchanged when treatment exposure was restricted exclusively to statin therapy. Estimates of treatment discordance differed by less than two percentage points across all PREVENT thresholds, indicating that inclusion of nonstatin therapies had minimal influence on study conclusions (Supplemental Table S8).

Discussion

Among 39,277 U.S. adults followed for mortality, 38.5% (95% CI 35.2–41.8) of fatal cardiovascular disease events occurred among individuals without baseline diagnosed CVD, whereas 61.5% occurred among those with established disease. These findings demonstrate that fatal CVD occurring outside prior clinical recognition represents a major population-level phenomenon rather than an uncommon presentation. Although incidence rates were substantially higher among individuals with established baseline CVD, the large proportion of fatal events arising in adults without diagnosed disease highlights persistent gaps in primary prevention.

Notably, many of these individuals appeared identifiable using contemporary risk stratification frameworks. Among participants without baseline CVD and calculable PREVENT-ASCVD risk (n = 22,505), 35.9% met borderline-or-higher risk criteria (≥3%), including 23.6% meeting the intermediate-or-higher threshold (≥5%) for LDL-lowering therapy consideration. However, 71.2% and 67.1% of these groups, respectively, were not receiving LDL-lowering therapy, representing one of the first nationally representative estimates of the preventive treatment gap under the PREVENT framework. These findings suggest that many individuals who subsequently experienced fatal cardiovascular events had measurable risk factors that would qualify for preventive treatment under contemporary PREVENT-based frameworks.

The principal finding of this study is that retrospective application of contemporary PREVENT-based thresholds identifies a substantial number of individuals who subsequently experienced fatal cardiovascular events and who were not receiving LLT at baseline. Among individuals meeting intermediate or higher risk criteria (≥5% 10-year risk), 23.6% of the population met treatment thresholds, of whom 67.1% were not receiving LLT. Although treatment rates improved at higher levels of risk, a substantial proportion of individuals at clinically actionable risk remained untreated. This gap is not merely theoretical; it is reflected in downstream mortality patterns. A substantial proportion of all fatal CVD events (38.5%; 95% CI 35.2–41.8) occurred in individuals without prior diagnosed cardiovascular disease, the population in whom prevention has its greatest unrealized potential. Although individuals with established CVD exhibited markedly higher absolute risk (11.54 vs 0.97 per 1 000 person-years; P < 0.001), the substantially larger undiagnosed population contributed a substantial share of total deaths [2,9]. These findings define a prevention system where the tools exist, the risk is detectable, and the deaths are nonetheless potentially preventable.

Interpretation of these findings requires consideration of the historical context of the study period. Participants were enrolled between 1999 and 2018, spanning multiple generations of cholesterol-management guidelines, including periods preceding both the Pooled Cohort Equations and PREVENT risk assessment frameworks. Consequently, observed treatment discordance should not be interpreted as failure to implement contemporary recommendations, but rather as a retrospective estimate of how current risk assessment strategies would classify individuals observed during earlier prevention eras.

Primary-prevention fatal CVD as a population-level phenomenon

The observation that a substantial proportion of fatal CVD events occur among individuals without baseline diagnosed disease reinforces longstanding evidence that major cardiovascular events may represent the first clinical manifestation of disease [[3], [10]]. The marked difference in incidence rates between individuals with and without established CVD, coupled with similar total event counts, illustrates the prevention paradox in this population. The 9-fold higher per-person rate of fatal CVD among individuals with diagnosed disease (11.54 vs 0.97 per 1 000 person-years) confirms that established CVD remains the highest-density target for cardiovascular care and should not be deprioritized; rather, the data indicates that the diagnosed and undiagnosed populations require different prevention strategies operating in parallel. Individuals with diagnosed CVD constitute a small, high-risk-density group in whom intensive secondary prevention yields large per-person benefits. Individuals without diagnosed disease constitute a much larger, lower-density group in whom even modest improvements in risk recognition and treatment rates can prevent a substantial absolute number of events because of the size of the population at risk. Strategies focused solely on clinically recognized high-risk populations will therefore fail to capture a large share of potentially preventable cardiovascular mortality, even as such strategies remain essential for those already diagnosed.

Importantly, individuals who experienced primary-prevention fatal CVD were not characterized by absence of risk. Among adults without baseline diagnosed CVD, male sex, current smoking, and higher systolic blood pressure were strong independent predictors of fatal CVD, with a graded, approximately linear association across blood pressure levels. These findings are consistent with large-scale pooled analyses demonstrating a graded association between systolic blood pressure and vascular mortality without a clear threshold [11] and reinforce current guideline recommendations emphasizing early and sustained blood pressure control [12].

Interestingly, diabetes was not independently associated with fatal CVD after multivariable adjustment, and its prevalence was lower among decedents without prior diagnosis compared with those with known CVD. While diabetes is a well-established cardiovascular risk factor [13,14], this finding may reflect differences in clinical recognition and management. Diabetes was not independently associated with fatal CVD in the fully adjusted Cox model, although estimates differed in competing-risk analyses. Specifically, the point estimate shifted from HR 0.75 in the cause-specific model to sHR 1.15 in Fine-Gray analyses. Although neither estimate was statistically significant, this directional change suggests sensitivity to competing mortality and residual confounding. Therefore, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence that diabetes reduces cardiovascular risk.

A key finding of this study is that differences between primary-prevention and known fatal CVD are driven primarily by patterns of diagnosis and treatment rather than by substantial differences in underlying risk factor burden. Phenotypic profiling demonstrated broadly similar distributions of major risk factors between groups, with the most pronounced differences reflecting treatment exposure and clinical labeling. Consistent with this interpretation, healthcare access variables, including insurance status and usual source of care, were not independently associated with primary-prevention status after adjustment. Although prior studies have linked healthcare access to cardiovascular outcomes [15,16], our findings suggest that the critical gap lies not in access alone, but in the recognition and management of existing risk within the healthcare system. Collectively, suggest that many individuals experiencing fatal CVD without prior diagnosis had measurable cardiometabolic risk factors before death.

These findings reframe where the gap in cardiovascular prevention actually lies. The PREVENT-ASCVD calculator, now the standard for primary prevention risk assessment, identified a substantial portion of the adult population as meeting threshold for considering LLT, yet 71% of those individuals were untreated. These findings highlight a gap between risk identification and treatment implementation. As PREVENT thresholds and corresponding treatment recommendations are newly established, some of this gap likely reflects recent changes in guideline definitions. However, the magnitude of undertreatment observed suggests that translation of risk into therapy remains incomplete at the population level. The downstream consequence is measurable in this dataset: 38.5% of cardiovascular deaths occurring outside clinical diagnosis. These findings raise the possibility that improved translation of cardiovascular risk assessment into preventive treatment could reduce future cardiovascular disease burden, although prospective implementation studies are needed to establish causality. Blood pressure control, smoking cessation, and reduction of central adiposity remain the dominant modifiable drivers of fatal events in this group, and all are actionable without awaiting a formal CVD diagnosis. Current guidelines support this approach [12]; the implementation gap documented here suggests that guideline adherence, not guideline content, is the binding constraint.

Several limitations warrant consideration. Baseline CVD status was based on self-reported clinician diagnosis, without medication-adjudicated or claims-based confirmation. A single-item self-report is likely to misclassify a non-trivial share of individuals with prior MI, revascularization, or heart failure as having no diagnosed CVD, and this misclassification is unlikely to be random: lower socioeconomic status and reduced healthcare engagement are associated with both underdiagnosis and adverse outcomes. The estimate that 38.5% of fatal CVD occurred in individuals without prior diagnosis should therefore be interpreted as a plausible upper bound on true primary-prevention deaths, and the phenotypic comparison between primary-prevention and known fatal CVD groups is likely attenuated by misclassification. Cause-of-death classification from the National Death Index may also introduce error [17]. Residual confounding is possible despite multivariable adjustment, and risk factor assessment was based on single baseline measurements, which may not reflect longitudinal exposure. Comparisons restricted to fatal CVD cases (Table 3) are subject to index-event bias, in which conditioning on the outcome can induce associations between exposures and disease-recognition status; the markedly lower odds of diabetes among primary-prevention decedents likely reflects this mechanism rather than a true protective effect, since diabetes screening reliably triggers cardiovascular workup and reduces the likelihood that progressive CVD remains undiagnosed. Although competing risks from noncardiovascular mortality were evaluated in sensitivity analyses using Fine-Gray models, these analyses were conducted without incorporation of complex survey weighting and should be interpreted as supportive rather than primary findings. Lastly, as PREVENT thresholds were retrospectively applied to historical cohorts, treatment eligibility estimates should be interpreted as counterfactual assessments rather than evaluations of guideline adherence during the original observation period. Despite these limitations, the use of nationally representative data with standardized survey weighting and long-term mortality follow-up enhances generalizability and provides robust contemporary estimates.

In this nationally representative analysis, nearly 40% of fatal cardiovascular events occurred in individuals without baseline diagnosed CVD. Among adults meeting the primary PREVENT-ASCVD intermediate-or-higher risk threshold, approximately two-thirds were not receiving lipid-lowering therapy. These findings suggest that many individuals who subsequently experienced fatal cardiovascular disease would have met contemporary PREVENT-based treatment thresholds when assessed retrospectively. Future prospective studies are needed to determine whether systematic implementation of PREVENT-guided preventive therapies can reduce cardiovascular mortality.
