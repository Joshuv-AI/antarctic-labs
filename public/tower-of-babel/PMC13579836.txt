A single-cell transcriptomic atlas of the pigtail macaque placenta in late gestation
Authors: Amanda Li, Richard Li, Hazel Huang, Hong Zhao, Briana Del Rosario, Miranda Li, Edmunda Li, Andrew Vo, Gygeria Manuel, Orlando Cervantes, Raj P Kapur, Jeff Munson, Austyn Orvis, Michelle Coleman, Melissa Berg, Britni Curtis, Brenna Menz, Jin Dai, Inah Golez, Solomon Wangari, Chris English, Audrey Baldessari, Lakshmi Rajagopal, John Cornelius, Kristina M Adams Waldorf

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe placenta is a complex organ with multiple immune and non-immune cell types that promote fetal tolerance and facilitate the transfer of nutrients and oxygen. The nonhuman primate (NHP) is a key experimental model for studying human pregnancy complications, in part due to similarities in placental structure, which makes it essential to understand how single-cell populations compare across the human and NHP maternal–fetal interface. We constructed a single-cell RNA-Seq (scRNA-Seq) atlas of the placenta from the pigtail macaque (Macaca nemestrina) in the third trimester, comprising three different tissues at the maternal–fetal interface: the chorionic villi (placental disc), chorioamniotic membranes, and the maternal decidua. Each tissue was separately dissociated into single cells and processed through the 10X Genomics and Seurat pipeline, followed by aggregation, unsupervised clustering, and cluster annotation. Next, we determined the maternal–fetal origins of cell populations and analyzed single-cell RNA trajectory, Gene Ontology enrichment, and cell–cell communication.ResultsSingle-cell populations in the pigtail macaque were strikingly similar in their identity and frequency to those found in the human placenta, including cells from trophoblast, stromal cell, immune, and macrophage lineages. An advantage of our approach was the deep sequencing of three tissues at the maternal–fetal interface, which yielded a rich diversity of common and rare single-cell populations.ConclusionsSingle-cell populations from the third-trimester pigtail macaque placenta are highly concordant with those from the human placenta. Background

The placenta is an essential, temporary organ responsible for providing nutrients, oxygen, and a protective immune environment to the fetus without which a mammal could not survive. Placentas vary markedly across species, yet their diverse structures perform the same critical functions to support the developing fetus [1, 2]. Pregnant animal models are powerful tools to investigate the pathogenesis of human reproductive disorders, especially when the placental biology of the model species closely resembles that of humans. Macaques, belonging to the genus Macaca, are commonly used as animal models in pregnancy research, largely due to their close similarity with humans in placental structure, fetal development, and physiology of labor onset. Rhesus macaques (Macaca mulatta), pigtail macaques (Macaca nemestrina), and cynomolgus macaques (Macaca fascicularis) are frequently used to study the impact of infectious diseases [3–15] and reproductive disorders [16, 17] on fetal development [18–21], placental health [22–25], and perinatal outcomes [26, 27]. Although these species have served as models of the impact of experimental stimuli on human reproduction and pregnancy, the degree to which they have similar populations of placental immune and non-immune cells (i.e., epithelial cells, mesenchymal cells, stromal, and trophoblast cells) is incompletely known.

The structure of the macaque placenta is very similar to that in humans, with a bi-discoid villous placenta and a tri-hemochorial interface [28]. Key differences in placental cellular composition between humans and macaques during the second and third trimesters include shallower trophoblast invasion in macaques [2], a continuous trophoblastic shell in macaques versus a less uniform structure in humans [29, 30], and the rarity in macaques of interstitial extravillous trophoblasts [31–34], which are common in humans. Despite these differences, the macaque placenta closely resembles the human placenta, sharing similar trophoblast lineages, villous architecture, chorionic gonadotropin (hCG)-like molecules and progesterone, spiral artery remodeling, and mechanisms supporting maternal–fetal immune tolerance. The gestation of the macaque pregnancy is approximately 167 days [35], 172 days [27, 36], and 164 days [37] for rhesus, pigtail, and cynomolgus macaques, respectively. In contrast, human gestation is approximately 100 days longer (266 days from conception or 280 days from the first day of a woman’s last menstrual period).

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-Seq) has enabled investigation of cellular heterogeneity and transcriptional programs in complex tissues, including the placenta [38, 39]. Single cell resolution is not possible using bulk RNA-seq, which produces a population-averaged signal that collapses cellular heterogeneity into a single composite measurement. This makes it impossible to identify discrete cell types, resolve rare populations, and detect gene expression specific to particular cells. Several scRNA-Seq atlases of the human placenta have been published, mostly describing cell populations in the first and second trimesters [40–45], but also in the third trimester [46–48]. scRNA-Seq datasets from nonhuman primate (NHP) placentas are comparatively rare with a scRNAseq analysis of the cynomolgus macaque placenta at different stages of gestation, scRNA-Seq of CD14 + cells in a ZIKV-infected pregnancy, and a pre-print from rhesus macaque placental organoids [49–51]. As the NHP is a common model of pregnancy disorders, especially in the third trimester, a normal placental atlas in late gestation and at high resolution is valuable.

Our objective was to establish a scRNA-Seq atlas of the pigtail macaque placenta from the late second and third trimesters, with characterization of cell populations within the maternal decidua, placental villous tissue, and chorioamniotic membranes. We have used the pigtail macaque as an NHP model to investigate the pathogenesis of several infectious diseases in pregnancy and different pathways of preterm labor [4–6, 11, 13, 26, 27, 36, 52–54]. Establishing the frequency and distribution of immune and non-immune cell populations in the macaque placenta and at the maternal–fetal interface will enable comparison with scRNA-Seq data from other models of infectious diseases or reproductive disorders.

Results

Comparative anatomy of the pigtail macaque and human placentas

Creation of a scRNA-Seq atlas of the pigtail macaque placenta required an appreciation of the anatomical similarities and differences with human placentas (Fig. 1). Overall, the architecture of the placental chorionic disc is remarkably similar between the two species with the human placenta (Fig. 1E) being much thicker than the macaque (Fig. 1A). At higher magnification, the placental chorioamniotic membranes (Fig. 1B, F), chorionic villi (Fig. 1C, G), and basal plate (Fig. 1D, H) exhibit similar architectural patterns. An important difference between the macaque (Fig. 1D’) and human (Fig. 1H’) placentas is the absence of cytokeratin 7-positive interstitial extravillous trophoblast cells in the macaque basal plate, a finding that we confirmed in our own model and has been previously reported in closely related NHP species [32–34]. Based on these observations, we did not expect to annotate extravillous trophoblast cells in our scRNA-Seq data.

Study design and analysis of the biological variability across samples

To determine the single-cell RNA transcriptional profiles of the pigtail macaque placenta, we collected scRNA-Seq data separately from chorionic villous tissue, chorioamniotic membranes, and the adjacent maternal decidua. Placental and decidual tissues were obtained from 10 healthy, uninfected pregnant pigtail macaques that had either undergone surgical catheterization and saline inoculation of the amniotic cavity and choriodecidual space (N = 5) or media inoculation of the forearm (N = 5; Table S1). These animals represented controls for infectious disease studies. All animals were delivered by Cesarean section, and none developed preterm labor or other perinatal complications. Chorionic villous tissue from the placental disc, chorioamniotic membranes, and decidual tissue were harvested immediately after delivery and processed separately through a scRNA-Seq pipeline to integrate the data, annotate single cell populations, and determine the tissue and maternal or fetal origin for each cluster. We excluded samples provided from one animal due to low cell counts (CTRL06, Table S1) yielding data from a total of 9 animals. Quality control metrics from each animal and each sample are shown in Table S2.

While sequencing freshly dissociated tissue enhanced the quality of our data, it also introduced potential batch effects, as samples were sequenced on separate chips. We explored changes in the single cell distribution and clustering on a PCA and UMAP before (Fig. 2A, C) and after (2B, 2D) Harmony integration. Before integration, there was a dominant linear trajectory along PC1 with substantial sample-specific spread along PC2, suggesting the presence of batch effects. After Harmony integration, the overall PCA structure is preserved but sample-specific separation along PC1 and PC2 are reduced, indicating attenuation of batch-driven variance.

Clustering, annotation, and cell origin

Unsupervised clustering analysis and manual annotation initially yielded 31 clusters. Additional rounds of unsupervised clustering within the macrophage, immune cell, and trophoblast clusters were performed to achieve greater resolution, which could then be matched to known human populations using canonical markers reported in the literature or cell marker databases [55]. The additional unsupervised clustering on the immune cell and macrophage groups yielded an additional 15 clusters. Once populations were annotated and similar populations merged, a total of 38 unique cell populations were identified and annotated using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP; Fig. 3A). Next, the tissue origin of the cell populations was determined using our pipeline, which processed and sequenced RNA transcripts separately from the placental disc, chorioamniotic membranes, and maternal decidua (Fig. 3B-C). Many cell populations had a mixed origin from the placental disc, chorioamniotic membranes, and decidua; this mixture likely reflects their adherence to one another.

Next, the tissue origin of the cell populations was determined by leveraging our pipeline that processed tissues separately from the DISC, MEM, and DEC prior to sequencing (Fig. 3B, C). Populations originating almost exclusively from the DEC included ESC, DSC1-2, CD4 + regulatory T cells (CD4 + Treg), GE, PB, and uNK1-2. Single cell populations concentrated within the DISC included CTB1-3, EB, HB, NK/T, and STB. Chorioamniotic membranes were the main source of AEC, FIB1-2, FMAC, PC, and schCTB. Most populations had a mixed tissue of origin, reflecting both the mixed distribution of certain cell types and minor cross-contamination of tissues adherent to one another during sampling.

The maternal versus fetal origin of each population was also determined by analyzing the single cell population expression profile of DEAD-Box Helicase 3 Y-Linked (DDX3Y) and ribosomal protein S4 Y-linked 1 (RPS4Y1) in placentas with a male fetus (Fig. 3D; RPS4Y1 not shown) [56]. Similar to human placentas, the cell populations with a fetal origin included the AEC, CTB1-3, FIB1-2, FMAC, HB, schCTB, and STB. DDX3Y expression was also noted in some immune cell and macrophage populations with a mixed tissue origin. Due to small numbers, these were not sub-clustered and annotated. Although expression of DDX3Y was not observed frequently in the erythroblast population (EB), transcriptional activity is heavily suppressed in the final stages of EB maturation as the nucleus prepares to be expelled. Overall, the identified cell populations in the pigtail macaque placenta were highly similar to those found in the human placenta, except for the absence of an extravillous trophoblast population, which was expected based on the literature and our own findings (Fig. 1D’, 1H’).

We also evaluated if the control experimental group (media versus saline) or the fetal sex (male versus female) was associated with significant differences in the proportion of single-cell populations. Out of 38 single-cell populations, there were four populations with significant differences in proportion between saline and media controls (DSC1, DSC2, M0, uNK1, uNK3; Fig. S1). Similarly, there were three populations with significant differences in proportion based on sex of the fetus (ADAMDEC1 + MAC, EC, MONO; Fig. S2).

Trophoblast populations

We identified four populations of trophoblast cells originating from the DISC (CTB1-3, STB) and one from the MEM (schCTB; Fig. 4A-B). As expected in a largely differentiated third-trimester placenta, the trajectory analysis of RNA velocity revealed only a limited connection between populations (Fig. 4C). All late gestation trophoblast populations were non-proliferative, as evidenced by their lack of MKI67 expression (Fig. 4D). The most differentiated CTB populations (schCTB, CTB3) expressed canonical cytotrophoblast markers including cytokeratin 7 (KRT7), GATA-binding protein 3 (GATA3), transcription factor AP-2 alpha (TFAP2A), TFAP2C, cadherin 1 (CDH1), and tumor protein p63 (TP63). The schCTB could be differentiated from CTB3 by its chorioamniotic membrane origin, expression of cytokeratin 5 (KRT5), and lack of gene expression for chorionic gonadotropin alpha subunit (CGA), cytochrome P450 family 19 subfamily A member 1 (CYP19A1)]. The STB expressed typical human syncytiotrophoblast markers including chorionic somatomammotropin 2 (CSH2), corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH), cytochrome P450 family 11 subfamily A member 1 (CYP11A1), and insulin-like growth factor 2 (IGF2; Fig. 4D). In summary, several single cell trophoblast populations were identified with a similar RNA expression profile to the same populations in the human placenta.

Myeloid populations

Diverse myeloid populations were identified within the pigtail macaque placenta including NEUT, MONO, two fetal macrophage clusters (HB, FMAC), and nine maternal macrophage clusters (M0, M1, M-INT, M2a, ADAMDEC1 + MAC, LYVE1 + MAC, MDM, MKI67 + MAC, and SPP1 + MAC; Fig. 5A-B). Trajectory analysis indicated that M0 was transcriptionally upstream of M-INT (Fig. 5C). RNA velocity vectors within M-INT converged toward M1, along with streamlines from other macrophage states (MDM, M2a) suggesting that M1 represents a late-stage differentiation state.

Analysis of expression profiles revealed that neutrophil (NEUT), monocyte (MONO), and monocyte-derived macrophage (MDM) populations shared overlapping expression of S100A8, S100A9, versican (VCAN), and leukocyte-specific transcript 1 (LST1; Fig. 5D). NEUT were further distinguished by expression of L-selectin (SELL, encoding CD62L) and absence of CD14. MONO were defined by the highest CD14 expression among these populations. MDM appeared transcriptionally downstream of MONO and were characterized by expression of interleukin-1 beta (IL1B) and thrombospondin-1 (THBS1). The adjacent M1 population displayed a pro-inflammatory phenotype marked by the highest IL1B expression overall, while M2a macrophages were distinguished by high expression of C–C motif chemokine ligand 17 (CCL17). The intermediate macrophage population (M-INT) exhibited a transcriptional profile bridging multiple states. Fetal Hofbauer cell (HB) and fetal macrophage (FMAC) populations were identified from the decidua basalis (DISC) and chorionic/amniotic membranes (MEM), respectively, using tissue of origin and DDX3Y expression in male placentas (Fig. 3C, D). Additional macrophage subsets were defined by high expression of canonical markers: ADAMDEC1 + (tissue-resident), LYVE1 + (co-expressing FOLR2; homeostatic, tissue-resident), MKI67 + (proliferating), and SPP1 + (tissue-remodeling). Together, these findings demonstrate a diverse landscape of maternal and fetal myeloid populations within the pigtail macaque placenta.

uNK, T cell and B cell clusters

Observations from the rhesus macaque immune atlas [57], a closely related species to the pigtail macaque, suggest that functionally distinct subsets within T and NK cells, may have strong shared expression programs (i.e., cytotoxicity) and lack defining RNA markers. As a result, unsupervised clustering may result in an admixture of alpha–beta (αβ) T cells, gamma delta (γẟ) T cells, and peripheral NKs. Interestingly, in both macaques and humans, CD4 RNA expression is often low or absent, in contrast to its higher surface protein expression [57, 58], making it difficult to annotate a CD4 + T cell population in a scRNA-Seq atlas. After initial unsupervised clustering yielded immune cell populations that were difficult to annotate; we subclustered the immune cells separately from other placental cell populations at a resolution of 0.15 (Fig. 6A). Most immune cell populations were derived from the DEC or MEM, except for NK/T cells, which originated from the placental disc containing maternal blood (Fig. 6B). Similar to the rhesus immune atlas, RNA expression of standard lineage markers for T cells was generally weak with no expression of CD4 detected; CD3 gene expression was most reliable (Fig. 6C) [57]. Further, we observed heterogeneity of gene expression across T/NK clusters, which is also true in human scRNA-Seq atlases that may require combinatorial scoring to identify some T cell subsets [59, 60]. Therefore, labeling of T and NK clusters was based on the dominant transcriptional characteristics of each cluster, acknowledging that clusters are likely heterogeneous.

Similar to cells present at the human maternal–fetal interface, we identified two prominent populations of tissue-resident memory T cells (TRM), which expressed CD69 and ITGAE (CD103; Fig. 6C). A small population clearly expressed CD8A and CD8B (ENSMMUG00000003532) and was labeled as CD8 + TRM. A second, larger population expressed CD8B and CD69, but had weaker expression of CD8A, ITGAE, and cytotoxic granules. This population may be in the process of establishing tissue residency and was annotated more simply as TRM to reflect its heterogeneous nature. A third T cell population was labeled as Treg, which expressed CD3D, IL2RA (CD25), T cell immunoreceptor with Ig and ITIM domains (TIGIT), inducible T cell costimulatory (ICOS), BTB and CNC homology 2 (BACH2), and CD69. Notably, Treg expression of FOXP3 in scRNA-Seq atlases can be low and variably detected at the transcript level. A mixed NK/T cell (NK/T) population had high expression of natural killer cell granule protein 7 (NKG7), killer cell lectin-like receptor subfamily B, member 1 (KLRB1), and natural killer group 2D (NKG2D) and weak expression of CD3D, CD8A, CD8B (ENSMMUG00000003532), and T-cell receptor alpha constant (TRAC). Finally, a small population had low expression of genes across multiple immune populations and was annotated as ImmP (immune progenitor).

In contrast to the T and NK populations, three uNK cell populations were more easily defined by their gene expression [NCAM1 + (CD56 +), ITGAM +, CD3D-, FCGR3A- (CD16-), CD38 +], similar to previously described human uNK populations [41]. uNK1 cells were characterized by their cytotoxic granule gene expression, such as perforin 1 (PRF1), granulysin (GNLY is the homolog for ENSMMUG00000063583), granzyme A (GZMA), and granzyme B (GZMB). uNK2 cells had a similar profile with lesser expression of cytotoxic granules. Like the human decidual NK3 population described by Vento-Tormo et al., we found a third uNK population (uNK3) that expressed ITGAE (CD103), granzyme M (GZMM), and CD7. [41]

However, one population of geographically distinct cells expressed very low levels of many immune markers and was designated as an undefined immune precursor (ImmP). B cells were annotated based on canonical markers including early B cell factor 1 (EBF1), B lymphocyte kinase (BLK), B cell scaffold protein with ankyrin repeats 1 (BANK1), and cluster of differentiation 19 (CD19), with additional support from BACH. The PB population expressed canonical plasmablast markers including marginal zone B and B1 cell-specific protein (MZB1), joining chain of multimeric IgA and IgM (JCHAIN), and homologs of immunoglobulin heavy chain (IGHG1/IGHG2/IGHG3/IGHG4; ENSMMUG00000015202) and light chain (IGLC1/IGLC2/IGLC3/IGLC7/IGLL5; ENSMMUG00000044861) genes.

Endometrial and decidual stromal cells

Stromal cells play an essential role in facilitating successful implantation and maintaining pregnancy. Progesterone produced after ovulation and during pregnancy induces the decidualization of ESCs to transform the endometrium into a receptive lining for implantation and embryo growth. One ESC and two DSC populations were identified (Fig. 7A). All three populations were derived from the DEC with some contribution from the MEM, an adherent tissue (Fig. 7B). ESCs were identified by their expression of HOXA11, PDGFRB, PDGFRA, and CCND1 (Fig. 7C). Both DSC1 and 2 expressed canonical markers of human DSC including periostin (POSTN), tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases 3 (TIMP3), and insulin-like growth factor-binding protein 1 (IGFBP1; Fig. 7C). A trajectory analysis indicated that DSC1 was transcriptionally upstream from DSC2. However, few RNA velocity vectors connected ESC and DSC1 suggesting that there was less active differentiation at this point in pregnancy.

Other single cell populations

Several discrete single cell populations were identified that did not align with major lineages and were therefore excluded from trajectory analyses (Fig. 8). AECs were identified by the expression of cadherin 1 (CDH1), epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EPCAM), anterior gradient 2 (AGR2), and several cytokeratins and collagens. A small population of endometrial glandular epithelium (GE) was identified arising from the DEC, which expressed paired box 8 (PAX8), hepatocyte nuclear factor 1 homeobox B (HNF1B), progestagen-associated endometrial protein (PAEP), and relaxin family peptide receptor 1 (RXFP1). Endothelial cells (ECs) and pericytes (PCs) were identified by classical markers: platelet and endothelial cell adhesion molecule 1 (PECAM1) and von Willebrand factor (VWF) for ECs, and myosin heavy chain 11 (MYH11) and actin alpha 2 (ACTA2) for PCs. Two fibroblast clusters (FIB1–2) were identified, both expressing decorin (DCN), lumican (LUM), vimentin (VIM), and fibronectin 1 (FN1). FIB2 also showed weak expression of markers associated with decidual stroma, including IGFBP1 and POSTN, though its transcriptional profile and UMAP localization suggest it represents a distinct fibroblast subpopulation rather than a decidualizing ESC (Figs. 3A, and 8) [61]. Finally, the placenta serves as a hematopoietic organ, where primitive fetal red blood cells mature and enucleate [62]. Accordingly, a small population of fetal erythroblasts (EBs) was identified, localized primarily within the DISC (Fig. 8). EBs were characterized by high expression of hemoglobin subunit genes, including hemoglobin subunit alpha (HBA), hemoglobin subunit epsilon 1 (HBE1), and hemoglobin subunit mu (HBM).

Cross-species analysis with human placenta

Finally, we performed a cross-species analysis to compare single cell populations in our atlas to ones defined in a late gestation human placental atlas using only term placenta not in labor (Fig. S3) [48]. In this comparison, we observed that cell types from our atlas matched major lineages defined in this human atlas using Seurat anchor-based label transfer, which projects query cells into the reference cell-type space based on shared transcriptional features. Specifically, there was high concordance between pigtail macaque cell types and corresponding human lineages across macrophage (83–97%), NK cell (71–94%), decidual stromal (60–89%), trophoblast (73–96%), and endothelial (60%) populations. Major single cell species in the pigtail macaque placenta, and certainly at the level of lineage, were 60–97% concordant with the human placenta.

Discussion

We describe a scRNA-Seq atlas of the pigtail macaque placenta for the first time, which was deeply sequenced using tissues isolated separately and then aggregated from the placental basal plate, chorioamniotic membranes, and maternal decidua. This approach allowed specific annotation of stromal, trophoblast, and macrophage populations reflecting their tissues of origin. We found a diversity of uterine NK cells, stromal cells, and macrophages that are highly similar to those at the human maternal–fetal interface, particularly at the level of lineage. A notable difference in the pigtail macaque was the absence of extravillous trophoblast cells, which has been previously reported in several macaque species [31–34]. Overall, similarities in placental architecture and cellular composition suggest that the pigtail macaque is a robust model of the human placenta.

Uterine natural killer (uNK) cells, macrophages, and extravillous trophoblast cells are collectively responsible for transforming uterine spiral arteries into wide vessels with lower resistance to blood flow. The uNK1-3 clusters in the pigtail macaque share similarities with those described in human placentas. The uNK1 population in the pigtail macaque placenta was similar to a previously described human decidual NK1 based on high expression of cytotoxic granules; the pigtail macaque uNK3 had a similar RNA profile to the human dNK3 population in its expression of integrin subunit alpha E (ITGAE) and lymphotactin (XCL1).

Annotation of the T and NK cell clusters was more challenging and reflects a broader phenomenon in both NHP and human scRNA-Seq atlases that RNA expression alone is not sufficient for phenotyping. Functionally distinct subsets within T and NK cells share strong cytotoxicity expression programs, while lacking defining RNA markers. This challenge is compounded by the characteristically low or absent RNA expression of CD4, in contrast to its higher surface protein expression, making discrete CD4 + T cell annotation unreliable at the transcript level [57]. We therefore subclustered immune cells separately from other placental populations and labeled T and NK clusters based on their dominant transcriptional characteristics, explicitly acknowledging that individual clusters are likely heterogeneous. Using this technique, we identified tissue-resident memory T cell populations (CD8 + TRM and a mixed TRM), a Treg population defined by co-expression of CD3D, IL2RA, TIGIT, ICOS, and BACH2, and a mixed NK/T population with high NK cell markers (NKG7, KLRB1, NKG2D) alongside weak T cell receptor gene expression. This pattern of transcriptional admixture is not unique to the pigtail macaque and has been observed in human scRNA-Seq atlases of the maternal–fetal interface, where combinatorial scoring approaches are often required to resolve T cell subsets [59, 60].

The strength of this resource lies in the deep sequencing of many tissues at the maternal–fetal interface and the careful annotation of diverse cell types, which has yielded one of the most complete atlases of the maternal–fetal interface in any species. Another strength is in the trajectory and latent time analyses, which allowed evaluation of the relationships of populations to one another and their relative differentiation. This resource will also enable the deconvolution of bulk RNA-Seq datasets to estimate the proportion of single cells without the investment required in building a single-cell RNA-Seq dataset. Limitations of the work include the incomplete annotation of the pigtail macaque genome, which necessitated using the closely related rhesus macaque genome as a reference. Even using the rhesus macaque genome, there were differentially expressed genes that we could not identify; however, this limitation did not prevent cell identification, which was evident from the RNA profile as a whole. An ongoing effort is needed to improve the annotation of these genomes and reflect the genetic diversity across these species.

Conclusions

The placenta is a transient but essential organ that mediates immune tolerance of the semi-allogeneic fetus, nutrient and gas exchange, and endocrine signaling to maintain the pregnancy. Adverse pregnancy outcomes often develop due to disruption of normal placental development and function, which underlie substantial maternal and neonatal morbidity worldwide. Despite the central position of the placenta to reproductive health, on a molecular level, it remains among the least well-characterized human organs. The pigtail macaque shares many similarities with the human placenta (i.e., hemochorial placenta, long gestation, spiral artery remodeling) and provides an opportunity to carefully model adverse pregnancy outcomes coupled with a deep investigation of placental biology.

This single-cell atlas of the pigtail macaque placenta in late gestation characterizes multiple populations of trophoblast, macrophages of maternal and fetal origin, uterine NK cells, T cells, and decidual stromal cells, and provides a valuable resource for understanding both normal pregnancies and experimental NHP models of reproductive disorders. Major findings include the identification of three uNK cell populations with strong parallels to human decidual NK populations, diverse stromal and macrophage populations reflective of their tissue of origin, and T and NK cell clusters whose heterogeneous transcriptional profiles mirror the annotation challenges observed in human placental atlases. The functional states of these populations are obscured by bulk transcriptomic approaches, and single-cell resolution is therefore particularly important for characterizing the cellularly heterogeneous maternal–fetal interface. Development of this atlas further enables deconvolution of bulk RNA-Seq data to estimate single-cell proportions in existing and future datasets.

The pigtail macaque is also a scientifically important species for investigating preterm labor and the maternal–fetal response to experimental perturbations to model intrauterine growth restriction, drug toxicity, and infectious disease. [4, 5, 14, 23, 26, 27, 53, 54, 63] As the pigtail macaque is evolutionarily very close to the rhesus and cynomolgus macaques, this atlas may have translational value for multiple species in which there are scientific models of reproductive disorders. A scRNA-Seq atlas of the normal pigtail macaque placenta also provides a detailed reference against which we can evaluate how experimental manipulations change the cellular composition and gene expression at the maternal–fetal interface. Other scRNA-Seq atlases of the human placenta have transformed our understanding of differentiation trajectories of trophoblast populations and the rich immunological landscape of the maternal–fetal interface [40, 43, 47, 48, 64]. This comparable reference for the pigtail macaque enables the NHP research community to leverage this same understanding of the maternal–fetal interface in an experimental model where longitudinal sampling, controlled interventions, and mechanistic studies are feasible.

Methods

Ethics statement

All nonhuman primate experiments were performed in strict accordance with the recommendations in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the National Research Council and the Weatherall report, “The use of non-human primates in research.” The Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the University of Washington (UW) approved the study’s protocols (4165–01, last approval date: 05/07/2025; 4165–02, last approval date: 04/09/2025). All surgeries were performed under general anesthesia, and all efforts were made to minimize pain and distress.

Study design

Placentas and decidua were obtained from ten pigtail macaques enrolled as uninfected controls on two different experimental protocols (Table S1). One control group received choriodecidual and intra-amniotic saline infusions after surgical implantation of choriodecidual and amniotic catheters, which were compared to experimental infections with Group B Streptococcus (N = 5). A second group received five subcutaneous inoculations of media along the forearm to serve as controls for a study of Zika virus pathogenesis (N = 5). Gestational ages at tissue collection spanned the late second and third trimester (118–150 days gestation; term gestation = 172 days). After cesarean section, maternal decidua and placental tissues (basal plate and chorioamniotic membranes) were separated and collected for single-cell dissociation and RNA-Seq analysis.

Anesthesia was induced according to standard operating procedures at the Washington National Primate Research Center. Ketamine hydrochloride (10–15 mg/kg intramuscular) was routinely administered to achieve sedation or unconsciousness for short procedures. For surgical interventions or imaging studies requiring extended anesthesia and immobility, inhalant anesthetics were employed, including isoflurane (1–5% in oxygen) or sevoflurane (1–4% in oxygen), which allowed precise titration of anesthetic depth. Propofol was used primarily for induction of prolonged anesthesia, administered as a 2–5 mg/kg IV bolus or as a constant rate infusion (0.2–0.5 mg/kg/min IV). All anesthetic protocols were tailored to the procedure and the clinical status of the animal.

Single cell rna-seq and bioinformatics pipeline

Single cell dissociation

Isolation of cells from chorionic villous tissue of the placental disc (DISC) and maternal decidual tissues (DEC)

Cell dissociation from placental tissue was performed following a previously described protocol [39]. Decidual and chorionic villous tissues were finely chopped into approximately 0.2 mm3 cubes using sterile scissors. Tissue fragments were enzymatically digested in 15 mL of a 0.4 mg/mL collagenase V solution (Sigma, C-9263) prepared in working medium, in 50 mL conical vials. The working medium consisted of RPMI 1640 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 21,875–034), supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; Thermo Fisher, SH3007103), 50 U/mL penicillin–streptomycin (Pen-Strep; Thermo Fisher, 15–140-122), and 250 U/mL DNase I (Sigma, D4527-20KU). The digestion was carried out at 37 °C for 45–60 min with continuous stirring using a magnetic stir bar on a heated plate.

Following digestion, the supernatant was sequentially filtered through 100-μm and 40-μm cell strainers (Fisher Scientific, 22–363–549 and 22–363–547, respectively). The filtered cell suspension was centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min at room temperature. The resulting pellet was resuspended in 5 mL of red blood cell lysis buffer (RBC) (Invitrogen, 00–4300-54) and incubated for 10 min at room temperature to lyse residual erythrocytes. For wash steps, 1 × phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 2% FBS was added to samples that contain RBC lysis buffers and washed twice. After RBC lysis, the samples were washed twice with 1 × (PBS) containing 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS) to remove residual lysis buffer and cellular debris. Cells were then counted, and the cell viability was assessed using trypan blue.

Isolation of cells from the chorioamniotic membranes (MEM)

Each MEM was placed in a sterile Petri dish, and clogs or debris were gently removed using scissors under 1 × phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The cleaned membrane tissue was enzymatically digested in 70 mL of a digestion solution consisting of 0.2% Trypsin 250 (Pan Biotech, P10-025100P), 0.02% EDTA (Sigma, E9884), and 5 U/mL Dispase (Corning, 354,235) in 1 × Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), with stirring at 37 °C for 10 min with continuous stirring using a magnetic stir bar on a heated plate. The disaggregated cell suspension was passed through sterile food-grade muslin gauze (Winware). The tissue was washed through gauze using RPMI 1640 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 21,875–034), supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; Thermo Fisher, SH3007103), 50 U/mL penicillin–streptomycin (Pen-Strep; Thermo Fisher, 15–140-122), and 250 U/mL DNase I (Sigma, D4527-20KU). The resulting filtrate was collected and set aside. The undigested gelatinous tissue retained on the gauze was recovered and subjected to a second enzymatic digestion using 25 ml of 0.4 mg/mL collagenase V (Sigma, C9263) in the working medium. This digestion was performed with gentle shaking at 37 °C for 30 min. The resulting cell suspension was again passed through sterile muslin gauze, and cells were pelleted from the filtration by centrifugation as previously described.

Cells obtained from both the trypsin/dispase and collagenase digestions were pooled and sequentially filtered through 100-μm and 70-μm cell strainers (Fisher Scientific, 22–363–549 and 22–363–547, respectively), followed by a wash in Ham’s F-12 medium. The filtered cell suspension was centrifuged, and pellet resuspended in 5 mL of red blood cell lysis buffer (Invitrogen, 00–4300-54) and incubated at room temperature for 10 min. After RBC lysis, samples were washed twice with 1 × phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS) to remove residual lysis buffer and cellular debris. Cells were counted, and cell viability was assessed using trypan blue.

Sequencing

10 × Chromium single-cell libraries were prepared according to the standard protocol outlined in the manual (Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3’ kits v3.1: dual index, 10X Genomics, Pleasanton, CA). Briefly, we loaded single-cell suspensions, 10 × barcoded gel beads, and reagents onto a “Chromium Single Cell A Chip” to generate Gel Bead-In-EMulsions (GEMs) using the 10X Chromium Controller (10X Genomics, Pleasanton, CA). Reverse transcription of mRNA occurred inside each GEM, followed by amplification of cDNA and preparation of sequencing libraries according to manufacturer instructions. Quality control was performed in multiple steps to check the quality of the cDNA and the library using the Agilent 2200 TapeStation system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). Finally, shallow sequencing was performed using a Next-Seq 2000 (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA) to evaluate the percentage of reads mapping to the reference genome, GC content distribution, and sequencing depth (number of reads per cell). Based on this information, we determined if the cDNA library was of sufficient quality to send for deep sequencing, and whether an adjustment to the amount of any samples was necessary to achieve a greater number of reads per cell. Deep sequencing of the cDNA library was performed on a Nova-Seq (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA) to achieve a read depth of approximately 40,000 reads per cell.

Genome alignment

Raw FASTQ files were processed using Cell Ranger v7.2.0 software (10 × Genomics, Pleasanton, CA, USA). Reads were initially aligned to the pigtail macaque (M. nemestrina) genome (Mnem_1.0, GenBank Assembly ID: GCA_000956065.1, 2015). Initial analysis revealed that key canonical genes of interest to define placental cell types were not annotated in the M. nemestrina genome, which is a lower-quality (scaffold-based) genome. In contrast, the rhesus macaque genome (Mmul_10, GenBank Assembly ID: GCA_003339765.3) has a contiguous assembly and is more completely annotated. As the rhesus and pigtail macaque are closely related species, alignment was performed to the rhesus macaque genome.

Quality control, normalization, and harmonization

Raw sequencing data were processed with Cell Ranger to generate per-sample feature-barcode count matrices. Empty droplets were identified and removed using the emptyDrops function from the DropletUtils package (FDR < 0.01); when emptyDrops returned fewer than 100 candidate cells for a given sample, the Cell Ranger filtered matrix was retained instead. Ambient RNA contamination was corrected on a per-sample basis using SoupX, with preliminary cluster assignments derived from a standard log-normalization, PCA, and graph-based clustering workflow run at resolution 0.5. Doublets were detected using scDblFinder, and only singlet-classified cells were carried forward. After merging across samples, cells were retained if they expressed between 200 and 7,500 genes and had fewer than 2.5% mitochondrial reads. One sample (CTRL06) was excluded from the analysis due to quality concerns.

All downstream analyses were performed in R (version 4.4.1) using the Seurat package (version 5.1.0). Normalized expression values were computed using SCTransform with mitochondrial read percentage regressed out. The first 50 principal components were calculated using RunPCA, and an elbow plot was used to assess dimensionality. Batch effects were corrected using the Harmony package (version 1.2.0), integrating across samples with theta = 2 and a maximum of 20 iterations over the top 30 PCs. The corrected Harmony embeddings were used as input to FindNeighbors (dims = 1:30) and FindClusters (resolution = 0.5), and cells were embedded in two dimensions using the RunUMAP function (n.neighbors = 30, min.dist = 0.3) via the Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) algorithm. Final publication-quality UMAPs were generated using scCustomize.

Cell clustering, annotation, and identification of fetal cells

Initial cluster labels were refined through iterative subset reclustering. Macrophage/myeloid, T/NK/B cell, trophoblast, and stromal/endothelial lineages were each extracted as subsets and independently reprocessed through SCTransform, PCA (npcs = 30), Harmony integration, neighbor graph construction, and UMAP embedding. Clustering resolutions were set to 0.15 for trophoblast and immune subsets, and 0.15 for the macrophage subset (with a final resolution of 0.25 used for sub-cluster refinement of select clusters). Canonical lineage markers were used to annotate resulting clusters via DotPlot visualization, and refined labels were transferred back to the full atlas using a custom label-transfer function. In cases where small, scattered clusters lacked a coherent identity, cells were reassigned to their nearest non-scattered neighbor by k-nearest neighbor search (k = 1) in UMAP space using the FNN package. Ambiguous populations were further resolved by iterative sub-clustering using FindSubCluster, and interactive lasso gating via a custom Shiny/Plotly application was used to manually delineate morphologically distinct populations. One small cluster in the trophoblast subset was identified as mast cell contamination based on expression of MS4A2, CPA3, and FCER1A, and these cells were excluded from further analysis. The final atlas annotation scheme (cell_type_v5) comprised 38 cell types.

To distinguish fetal from maternal cells within the atlas, the distribution of DDX3Y and RPS4Y1 expression, which are both Y-chromosome-linked genes, was evaluated across cell types in placentas from pregnancies with a male fetus. Cell annotation was performed manually by evaluation of top RNA markers and comparison to the literature.

Compositional analysis

Cell type composition was compared across experimental conditions (uninfected media versus saline control), tissue compartments (chorionic villus, decidua, and membrane inoculated), and fetal sex using the sccomp package. A Bayesian compositional model with bimodal mean-variability association was fit using sccomp_estimate, and differential composition was tested using sccomp_test with contrasts for each comparison. Significance was assessed at a false discovery rate threshold of 5%.

Trajectory inference

We used the Python 3 [65] packages scVelo [66] and Velocyto [67] to reconstruct the trophoblast, stromal cell, and macrophage lineages from our single-cell gene expression data. This method utilized a likelihood-based dynamic model to characterize the RNA velocity of cell subpopulations and to infer the trajectory of cellular age.

Cross-species integration and comparison

We performed a cross-species atlas comparison using a published late gestation human placental atlas as the reference [48]. Human data were filtered to term not-in-labor (TNL) samples prior to analysis. Gene matching between species was performed by intersecting shared gene symbols between the macaque and human references. Version suffixes were stripped from human Ensembl gene IDs, and macaque gene symbols were mapped to their human Ensembl equivalents using the org.Hs.eg.db annotation database [68]; ambiguous one-to-many mappings were removed to retain only unambiguous orthologous genes. We performed Seurat anchor-based label transfer onto the human scRNA-Seq placental atlas reference. Concordance between cell types and lineages was evaluated by computing row-normalized proportions of predicted human cell type labels for each macaque cell population.
