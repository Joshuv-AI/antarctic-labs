Preoperative physical activity patterns and postoperative outcomes in early-stage non-small cell lung cancer: a retrospective observational cohort study
Authors: Jiafang Zhang, Andrea Wolf, Morten Quist, Rowena Yip, David F Yankelevitz, Claudia I Henschke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPhysical activity is a potentially influential lifestyle factor affecting recovery and survival in early-stage non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients. This study examined associations between preoperative exercise and postoperative complications, disease-free survival (DFS), and lung cancer-specific survival (LCSS) in Initiatives for Early Lung Cancer Research on Treatment (IELCART) patients.MethodsWe analyzed 252 patients with clinical stage IA NSCLC (≤30 mm) enrolled in the IELCART study at the Mount Sinai Health System (2016–2024) who underwent surgical resection and completed preoperative Godin Leisure-Time Exercise Questionnaires. Patients were categorized as active (score ≥14) or sedentary (<14) based on preoperative exercise level. Outcomes included postoperative complications, DFS, and LCSS. Multivariable logistic and Cox regression models were adjusted for demographic, clinical, and tumor characteristics.ResultsOf 252 patients (median age 72 years, 62% female), 126 were active and 126 sedentary. Groups were similar in baseline characteristics except active patients were less likely to have >2 comorbidities (35% vs. 47%, P=0.03) and more likely to have part-solid nodules (27% vs. 12%, P<0.001). Postoperative complications occurred in 29% overall; active patients had a numerically higher complication rate than sedentary patients (32% vs. 25%, P=0.30), though this difference was not statistically significant. Median follow-up was 32 months. No statistically significant differences were observed in DFS [adjusted hazard ratio (HR) =0.64, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.26–1.59, P=0.34] or LCSS (adjusted HR =0.68, 95% CI: 0.19–2.46, P=0.57) between groups. Older age, male sex, and Black or Hispanic ethnicity were associated with higher complication risk.ConclusionsIn this cohort of clinical stage IA NSCLC patients, we did not observe evidence that preoperative physical activity was significantly associated with postoperative complications or survival outcomes after adjustment. Active patients had numerically higher complication rates, while survival trends were non-significantly favorable. Studies with objective exercise measurements and larger sample sizes are needed to clarify these associations and guide prehabilitation strategies in early-stage NSCLC.Keywords: Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), physical activity, postoperative complications, disease-free survival (DFS), lung cancer-specific survival (LCSS)

Highlight box

Key findings

• In patients undergoing surgical resection for clinical stage IA non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), preoperative self-reported physical activity was not significantly associated with postoperative complications, disease-free survival, or lung cancer-specific survival after adjustment for clinical and tumor characteristics.

What is known and what is new?

• Physical activity has been associated with improved outcomes in several cancer populations, but its relationship with postoperative and long-term outcomes in patients with early-stage NSCLC remains unclear.

• In this cohort, physically active patients showed nonsignificantly favorable survival trends but did not have fewer postoperative complications. The findings also demonstrated discrepancies between self-reported physical activity and measures of physical functioning.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Self-reported physical activity alone may not adequately characterize preoperative functional status or predict postoperative outcomes in early-stage NSCLC. Future studies should incorporate objective measures of physical activity and functional capacity to better define their role in risk assessment and prehabilitation.

Introduction

Advances in lung cancer screening and surgical techniques have improved patient outcomes by enabling early detection and timely treatment of lung cancer, yielding high cure rates (1). Postoperative outcomes in early-stage non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients, however, can be influenced by patient demographics, comorbidities, and lifestyle factors. Among these, physical activity has emerged as a potentially modifiable determinant of both short-term and long-term outcomes. Preliminary results of randomized controlled trials of exercise that included biomarker endpoints suggest that exercise may result in beneficial changes in the circulating level of insulin and insulin-related pathways (2,3), inflammation (2-4), and possibly immunity (4,5). Observational studies and systematic reviews have reported that physical activity is associated with improved health-related quality of life (QoL) (6,7), reduced all-cause (8,9), breast cancer-specific (10,11), colon-cancer-specific (10), and prostate-cancer-specific mortality (10). Several large trials have defined the potential magnitude of the benefit of physical activity (12-16). Emerging evidence suggests that preoperative physical activity may also play a crucial role in patient recovery, with physically active individuals potentially experiencing fewer adverse events, including postoperative complications after treatment. However, the specific impact of levels of physical activity on surgical outcomes in early-stage NSCLC patients has not been thoroughly investigated. This study aims to examine the association between preoperative physical activity and postoperative outcomes in early NSCLC patients, with long-term survival [disease-free survival (DFS) and lung cancer-specific survival (LCSS)] as the primary outcome and postoperative complications as the secondary outcome. It seeks to elucidate the relationship between preoperative physical activity and postoperative outcomes to inform preoperative counseling and enhance patient management strategies. Ultimately, this research aims to improve recovery and survival outcomes for NSCLC patients by highlighting the importance of exercise-related factors. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0826/rc).

Methods

This study analyzed data from patients enrolled in the Initiatives for Early Lung Cancer Research on Treatment (IELCART) at the Mount Sinai Health System (MSHS) between January 2016 and July 2024 (17). Inclusion criteria were patients diagnosed with first primary NSCLC with a maximum diameter of ≤30 mm on their pre-treatment computed tomography (CT) scan, who underwent surgical resection, and had documented comprehensive clinical, demographic, tumor, and pre-treatment exercise data. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments, and was approved by the Mount Sinai Institutional Review Board (IRB Study-15-01021). Informed consent was obtained from all participants at enrollment.

Demographic information, pre-surgical CT findings, and pre-treatment pathologic biopsy results were documented at the time of enrollment. Pre-treatment CT scans were performed using either standard-dose or low-dose settings on multislice CT scanners with or without contrast to measure the maximum tumor diameter of the NSCLC on axial, sagittal, and coronal CT images on the last CT images prior to treatment. The NSCLC nodule consistency on pre-treatment CT was classified as being solid, part-solid or nonsolid according to established criteria (10). For part-solid NSCLCs, the maximum diameter of the entire nodule and of the largest solid component was measured. Treating physicians documented pre-treatment plans, patient discussions, resection, and results. Post-surgery, all patients were followed at 1–2 months, 6 months, 12 months after treatment, and annually thereafter. Comorbidities were reported at enrollment and categorized into three groups: none, 1–2 comorbidities, and more than 2 comorbidities, reflecting increasing burden of comorbid disease.

The Godin Leisure-Time Exercise Questionnaire was administered by the study coordinator at multiple time points: pre-treatment, 1–2 months post-treatment, 6 months, 1 year, and annually thereafter, alongside QoL assessments. This questionnaire inquired about the average number of times patients engaged in different levels of exercise over a 7-day period, specifically: strenuous exercise (e.g., running, jogging, football), moderate exercise (e.g., fast walking, baseball, tennis), and mild exercise (e.g., yoga, archery, fishing from a riverbank). Each exercise category was assigned a weight (strenuous ×9, moderate ×5, mild ×3). A weekly leisure activity score was calculated by multiplying the weight by the frequency of each exercise type and summing the scores across all three categories (18). Based on their preoperative weekly leisure activity scores (from questionnaires completed within 3 months prior to surgical resection), patients were categorized into either the active (scored 14 or more) or the sedentary group (scored less than 14). It should be noted that the Godin Leisure-Time Exercise Questionnaire captures self-reported leisure-time physical activity but does not assess occupational physical activity or total sedentary time. The term “exercise” in this manuscript refers broadly to self-reported leisure-time physical activity as captured by this instrument.

The primary outcome of this study was lung-cancer-specific and DFS, and the secondary outcome was postoperative complications. DFS was defined as the time from the date of surgical resection to the first documented recurrence of lung cancer or death from any cause, whichever occurred first. Patients who were alive and disease-free at the last follow-up were censored on the date of last follow-up. Final determination of date and cause of death were obtained from the participant’s physician, family members, and the National Death Index. Deaths within 30 days of surgery or other lung cancer-related treatment were tabulated as lung cancer deaths. Follow-up time was calculated from the date of surgical resection to either June 1, 2024 (the censoring date applied consistently for all time-to-event analyses) or death, whichever came first. Median follow-up duration was computed as the simple median of follow-up times for the entire cohort. Postoperative complications were documented according to the Society of Thoracic Surgery (STS) General Thoracic Surgery Database, indicating whether the patient experienced postoperative adverse event at any time during their hospital visit, regardless of length of stay, and/or events that occur within 30 days of surgery if discharged from the hospital.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were compared between groups using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, while categorical variables were analyzed using Pearson’s Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test where appropriate. Prior to multivariable modeling, variance inflation factors (VIFs) were examined for all covariates. No evidence of problematic multicollinearity was identified (all VIFs <5). Kaplan-Meier survival analyses were conducted to compare DFS and LCSS between groups, with statistical significance assessed using the log-rank test. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to evaluate the association between exercise status and survival outcomes, adjusting for age, gender, nodule size, nodule consistency, comorbidities, smoking status, and pack-years, with results expressed as hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Logistic regression was used to examine predictors of postoperative complications, with results expressed as ORs and 95% CIs. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R statistical software (version 4.2.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

A total of 1,215 patients were enrolled in the IELCART study at MSHS, completing 3,380 exercise assessments. Among these patients, 699 patients had clinical stage IA lung cancer (tumor size <30 mm), underwent surgery and did not receive any additional treatment within one year following their initial treatment. Of these 699 patients, 259 had baseline exercise forms available for analysis. Of these 259 patients, 7 were excluded: 5 because their exercise forms were completed more than 3 months prior to surgery, and 2 due to reporting exercise exceeding 4 hours per day (Figure 1). All exercise assessments used in this analysis were completed prior to surgical resection, ensuring that postoperative complications and outcomes could not have influenced the exercise exposure variable.

Flow diagram of study participants. IELCART, the Initiatives for Early Lung Cancer Research on Treatment; MSHS, the Mount Sinai Health System.

Among the 252 patients included in the analysis, 95 (38%) were men and 157 (62%) were women. Median age was 72 years [interquartile range (IQR), 65, 77 years]. Of the 252 patients, 126 (50%) were in the active group and 126 (50%) were in the sedentary group. Table 1 compares the patients’ characteristics between the active and sedentary groups. Sex, age, race, body mass index, nodule size, and histology were similar in both active and sedentary groups. However, the active group had significantly fewer patients with more than 2 comorbidities compared to the sedentary group (35% vs. 47%, P=0.03). Additionally, there were significant differences in nodule consistency between groups (P<0.001), with the active group having more part-solid nodules (27% vs. 12%) and fewer nonsolid nodules (1.6% vs. 10%) compared to the sedentary group.

Of the 252 patients, 34 (14%) were discharged with oxygen and 72 (29%) experienced postoperative complications (Table 2). Median length of stay in the hospital was 2.83 days (IQR, 2.79, 4.79 days). The rate of being discharged with oxygen (11% vs. 16%, P=0.30) and postoperative complications (32% vs. 25%, P=0.30) were similar between active and sedentary patients.

When examining specific complication types, pulmonary complications occurred in 4.8% of active patients versus 1.6% of sedentary patients (P=0.28), cardiovascular complications in 8.7% of active patients versus 7.1% of sedentary patients (P=0.64), and urologic complications in 17% of active patients versus 10% of sedentary patients (P=0.14), though none of these differences reached statistical significance.

Multivariable logistic regression was used to predict postoperative complications between different exercise groups, adjusting for surgery extent, surgery type, age, gender, race, ethnicity, nodule size, nodule consistency, comorbidity, smoking status, and pack years (Table 3). The effect of exercise on postoperative complications was not significant after adjusting for covariates, although the direction of the coefficient shows that the active group had more complications (P=0.14). The effects of surgery extent (sublobar resection vs. lobectomy) trended toward significance (P=0.08). Patients undergoing sublobar resection had lower odds of complications. Age and gender were significant as older and male patients were more likely to experience complications (P<0.001). Additionally, Black/African American (P=0.03) and Hispanic patients (P=0.041) had higher odds of experiencing complications.

QoL and exercise

Analysis of QoL measures revealed significant associations between exercise status and physical function limitations (Tables S1-S3). Patients reporting limitations in moderate activities had significantly lower Godin exercise scores compared to those without limitations [median 3 (IQR, 0, 21) vs. 15 (IQR, 0, 21), P=0.008]. Similarly, patients who reported limitations in climbing several flights of stairs had lower Godin scores [median 3 (IQR, 0, 21) vs. 15 (IQR, 0, 21), P=0.01]. Patients who reported accomplishing less due to physical health also had significantly lower exercise scores [median 3 (IQR, 0, 21) vs. 15 (IQR, 0, 21), P<0.001].

Notably, patients who reported limitations in climbing several flights of stairs had a significantly lower rate of postoperative complications (20% vs. 32%, P=0.048). Patients reporting limitations in moderate activities or accomplishing less physically did not show significant differences in postoperative complication rates.

Survival outcomes

The median duration of follow-up was 32 months. Fourteen patients died and 8 patients had recurrence. Among the 14 deaths, 5 occurred in the active group and 9 in the sedentary group, among the 8 recurrences, 3 occurred in the active group and 5 in the sedentary group. Figure 2 illustrates the Kaplan-Meier curves for DFS for the 126 active and 126 sedentary patients. The active group had better DFS, although the difference was not statistically significant (P=0.23, Figure 2). After adjusting for age, gender, nodule size, nodule consistency, comorbidity, smoking status and pack-years, exercise status was not significantly associated with DFS (P=0.34, Table S4).

Discussion

Our study examined whether preoperative physical activity is associated with long-term and postoperative outcomes in patients with early-stage NSCLC. Although active patients showed trends toward more favorable disease-free and LCSS, these differences did not reach statistical significance after adjustment for covariates. The lack of significance may reflect a modest sample size and limited follow-up time. Postoperative complication rates did not differ significantly between groups, notably, active patients had numerically higher complication rates (32% vs. 25%, adjusted OR =1.65, P=0.14), which may reflect that physically active patients were candidates for more extensive surgical procedures or resumed activity earlier postoperatively. Age, male sex, and comorbidities were stronger predictors of adverse events, with male sex conferring a particularly elevated risk (OR =3.13, P=0.001).

Our results align to some degree with prior work suggesting that regular exercise supports recovery and long-term outcomes in cancer populations. Current American College of Sports Medicine guidelines endorse physical activity for cancer survivors to improve QoL and potentially reduce recurrence (19).

The QoL analyses revealed significant associations between exercise scores and physical functioning, with those reporting functional limitations consistently showing lower exercise levels. This highlights the potential utility of exercise interventions to improve functional capacity in lung cancer patients, as demonstrated in previous studies (20-22). However, these findings also reveal the possibility that patients may overstate their exercise levels in subjective questionnaires, particularly when their reported activity levels don’t align with functional limitations. The paradoxical finding that patients reporting stair-climbing limitations had fewer postoperative complications warrants further investigation. This counter-intuitive result may reflect selection bias, whereby surgeons offered patients with functional limitations less invasive procedures (e.g., wedge resections rather than lobectomies), resulting in fewer surgical complications. Alternatively, these patients may have been selected for surgery only if they were otherwise low-risk candidates, or they may have adopted more cautious postoperative behavior.

A major limitation of this study is the reliance on self-reporting of physical activity using the Godin Leisure-Time Exercise Questionnaire. Self-reported data is inherently prone to recall bias, social desirability bias, and inaccuracies in estimating exercise frequency, intensity, and duration. Such inaccuracies can dilute the observed associations between physical activity and patients’ outcomes, limiting the robustness of the findings. Additionally, this study did not assess potential confounders such as preoperative sarcopenia and muscle strength, which may independently influence postoperative outcomes. Furthermore, patients without a preoperative exercise assessment were excluded from the analysis, and differences between included and excluded patients may introduce selection bias that limits the generalizability of our findings. In future research, integrating wearable devices such as fitness trackers could provide objective and continuous measurements of physical activity, offering more precise data on exercise patterns and enabling a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between physical activity and postoperative outcomes.

Future studies should employ objective measurement tools such as accelerometers, fitness trackers, or standardized exercise tests to quantify physical activity more accurately. This would help eliminate the subjective biases inherent in self-reported questionnaires where patients may overestimate their exercise habits due to social desirability or recall issues. Additionally, prospective interventional studies with pre-surgical exercise programs of varying intensities could better elucidate dose-response relationships between exercise and surgical outcomes. The discrepancies observed between self-reported exercise levels and functional limitations in our QoL assessments further emphasize the need for objective measurements to validate subjective exercise reports.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study did not find evidence that preoperative physical activity, as measured by the Godin Leisure-Time Exercise Questionnaire, was significantly associated with postoperative complications or survival in this selected cohort of clinical stage IA NSCLC patients. Active patients had numerically higher complication rates, while survival trends were non-significantly favorable. Given the limited statistical power due to low event rates, these null findings should be interpreted with caution. Further research with larger cohorts, longer follow-up, objective physical activity measurement, and assessment of sedentary behavior and exercise intensity is needed to clarify the role of preoperative physical activity in surgical outcomes for early-stage NSCLC patients.
