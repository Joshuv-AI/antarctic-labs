Response of maize varieties to soil acidity under field conditions in Western Ethiopia
Authors: Begizew Golla, Nigussie Dechassa, Wassu Mohammed, Almaz Meseret Gezahegn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSoil acidity and associated fertility problems is a major challenge to maize production in western Ethiopia, reducing yield and contributing to food insecurity. This is worsened by the fact that breeding programs focused on testing and developing high-yielding varieties under optimum soil conditions, resulting in limited information on the performance of maize varieties across diverse soil pH. This study aimed to evaluate and identify maize varieties tolerant to soil acidity. Nine improved varieties (BH661, BH549, BHQP548, BH547, BH546, BHQPY545, BH5212, BH5211, and Gibe2) were evaluated under two soil pH levels (4.8 and 5.9) at Bako in 2023, using a randomized complete block design with three replications. Results showed that soil acidity and varieties significantly affected maize growth and yield with acidic conditions negatively affecting all varieties. However, BH5211, BH661, and BH547 showed the least yield reductions while BHQPY545 showed the highest yield reduction, indicating variability in adaptability. BH5211, BH661, and BH547 exhibited high grain yield stability across both pH levels, indicating broad adaptability, whereas BHQPY545 showed instability and specific adaptability. Stress tolerance indices supported these findings. Principal component analyses Showed 85.2% of total variability, with PC1 (68%) mainly influenced by biomass and grains per cob, and PC2 (17.2%) majorly affected by grain rows per cob. Varieties were grouped into four clusters; Cluster IV showed superiority in most yield and yield related variables. Overall, findings demonstrate the need for future crop improvement programs to deliberately consider soil acidity tolerance as one of selection criteria in the developing of maize varieties for high yield. Introduction

Maize (Zea mays L.) is one of the most important cereal crops, cultivated across a range of agroclimatic conditions for various benefits of people worldwide. It is a widely grown staple crop in Africa covering nearly 42 million hectares that accounting for 21% of the total global maize area1. It is an important source of food and nutritional security for millions of people in the developing world, especially in Africa and Latin America2. In Ethiopia, Maize is cultivated in wide area from low moisture stress low land to sub humid high land in area covering more than 2.4 million hectares of cultivated land3,4. Maize is a major source of food and income for millions of smallholder farmers in this country and plays an important role in the food security4.

However, the productivity of maize has been significantly influencing by several biotic and abiotic factors. Among abiotic factors, the negative effect of soil acidity on global yield comes second immediately after drought5. Soil acidity and associated soil fertility problems are the major challenge in most cultivated land of Ethiopia6,7. The intensity and distribution of soil acidity vary significantly between the western and eastern parts of the country. Most of western Ethiopia is affected by soil acidity, with pH levels ranging from 4.3 to 5.4, whereas the eastern part is affected by soil salinity, with pH levels ranging from 7 to 8.1 8. Although acid soils are defined as having a pH below 7.0, in agriculture they are considered those with a pH below 5.5 because the optimum pH range for most crops is between pH 5.5 and 7.0, where essential nutrients are most available, while below pH 5.5, aluminum and other trace elements become toxic and hinder plant growth5,9,10. Soil acidity affected about 43% of the cultivated land in humid and sub-humid highlands of Ethiopia, where major staple food crops like maize, barley, teff and wheat are cultivated11. The prevalence of soil acidity and its consequence of soil fertility problem is one of the major challenges affecting crop production in rural area of western Ethiopia where maize is cultivated as a staple crop7,8,11.

Soil acidity severely affect crop growth and yield by limiting nutrient availability, restricting root growth and inhibiting overall plant performance. In acidic soils most important plant nutrients like phosphorus, calcium and magnesium become less available to plants while aluminum and manganese quickly dissolve from soil rocks to make them available to plants in toxic amounts9,10. Imbalance nutrient availability in acidic soil adversely affects crop growth and yield12–15. This reduces crop production and farmers’ income, contributing to food insecurity and poverty in rural areas. To address the negative effects of soil acidity on maize production and the resulting food shortages faced by farmers, targeted research and interventions in soil acidity management are required.

The most known effective acidic soil management options are soil amendment with liming materials and use of acid tolerant varieties16. Various studies conducted in different parts of Ethiopia have demonstrated the effectiveness of lime in neutralizing soil acidity and improving crop productivity17–19. However, this soil acid management method has been adopted by only a few farmers in limited areas due to limited access to lime and high transportation cost, as large quantities per hectare are required to arise pH by one unit20. As a result, many farmers cultivate their crops in acid-prone soils without applying lime, exposing them to significant yield losses due to soil acidity7.

Similarly, Integrated application of organic and inorganic fertilizers has been tested in agricultural fields to evaluate its effects on soil fertility and crop yield improvement7,21. The results showed positive effects on soil structure and fertility to some extent; however, this approach was not more effective in reducing soil acidity. Even in some case it aggravates soil acidity due to decomposition of organic matters into organic acids, and increasing the amount of nitrate in the soil due to inorganic fertilizer application16.These findings showed the gap and the need of another research to solve soil acidity problem in effective and affordable manner.

Several studies have reported that the use of acid-tolerant cultivars is the most effective method for managing both topsoil and subsoil acidity22,23, with less cost and with no environmental negative impact. Previous research has also indicated that maize varieties exhibit varying degrees of tolerance to soil acidity, with some varieties being more resilient than others21,24,25. Although acidic soil reduced the growth and yield performance of all maize varieties compared to optimal soil pH, its reduction level significantly varied between tolerant and susceptible varieties21,26,27. Therefore, use of acid tolerant varieties is best alternative to produce better yield in acid prone soil21.

The identification of acid tolerance maize varieties has also significant implications for breeding programs. Varieties possessing acid-tolerant traits can be used to develop new and more acid tolerant hybrids, thereby contributing to long-term agricultural sustainability28,29. In addition, the use of such varieties helps reduce environmental negative impact associated with soil amendments. Maize variety with high soil acidity tolerance offers significant advantages in acid prone soil, including improve yield stability, reduce reliance on costly soil amendments, and enhanced overall farm productivity and sustainability.

However, there is limited research and information on commercial maize varieties perform under diverse soil acidity levels in Ethiopia3. Breeding programs have traditionally focused on evaluating and developing high-yielding varieties under optimal soil conditions, where soil acidity has not been a primary consideration. Consequently, information on the acid tolerance levels of released hybrids is limited. To address this gap, this study was conducted with the objective to evaluate the response of different maize varieties under diverse soil acidity levels under field conditions to identify superior maize varieties based on plant growth parameters and grain yield performance including stability.

Stress tolerance indices

Acidity stress evaluation of maize varieties revealed that BH5211, BH661 and BH547 were the highest performing under both higher and lower soil pH (Table 4). These varieties remained the highest producing across both pH levels compared with the other varieties, although the mean performance of these varieties reduced by 10.0%, 21.7%, and 24.0%, respectively, under lower soil pH relative to higher soil pH conditions (Table 4). BHQP548 and BHQPY545 were the lowest performing maize varieties under lower soil pH with grain yield of 2.8 t/h and 3.0 t/ha respectively. However, under higher soil pH these two hybrids perform differently. BHQP548 remain lowest performing (5.3 t/ha) next to Gibe2 (5.2 t/ha), while BHQPY545 perform well (6.1t/ha). BH5211, BH661, and BH547 showed least yield reduction and high stability across pH levels, indicating broad adaptability; BHQPY545 had greatest reductions, indicating instability and specific adaptability. Maize variety BH5211 has a consistent higher mean productivity (6.6 t/ha), geometric mean productivity (6.5 t/ha), Harmonic mean productivity (6.5 t/ha) mean relative performance (2.7), relative efficiency index (1.8), stress tolerance index (1.1) and maximum yield under lower soil pH stress (5.7 t/ha) and followed by BH661 (Table 4). This indicated the acid stress tolerance capability of the BH5211 and BH661 maize varieties. BH547 showed the maximum mean stable yield (5.1 t/ha), yield stability index (0.9), relative stress index (1.4) and stress resistance index (1.2). It also showed the lowest tolerance index (0.6), stress susceptibility index (0.3) and percentage yield loss (10.5%) indicating for its ability to tolerate acid prone environment. Whereas BHQPY545 had the highest tolerance index (3.2), stress susceptibility index (1.4) and percentage yield loss (51.6) indicating it is the most stress susceptible variety (Table 4). This variation highlights variety × environment interaction and provides an insight for acid stress tolerant varieties.

Principle component

In this experiment, the eigen analysis of the correlation matrix of the first two principal components based on 12 quantitative traits accounted for 85.2% cumulative variance using eigen values > 1 (Table 5). The contribution of PC1 was found to be 68% in the total divergence of the studied variables, in which the major contributing variables were above ground dry biomass (0.33), number of grains per cob (0.331), Grain yield (0.314), silking date (0.311), cob length (0.307), tasseling date (0.303), stalk girth (0.301) and leaf area index (0.301) (Table 5). The first PC was positively associated with all variables. The second principal component (PCA 2) accounted for about 17.2% of the total variation and was strongly associated with grain rows per cob (0.623), cob diameter (0.329), leaf area index (0.333) and grain per cob (0.329) (Table 5).

Cluster analysis

The four clusters accounted for 85.2% of the total variation observed among the nine maize varieties. Cluster analysis grouped the varieties into four distinct clusters based on the evaluated agronomic traits (Table 6; Fig. 1). Cluster mean values for the twelve measured traits were estimated to determine the relative performance and distinguishing characteristics of each cluster. Overall, Cluster IV exhibited superior performance for most yield-related traits, including leaf area index (4.2), stalk girth (4), cob diameter (5), grains per cob (508.2), grain rows per cob (15.2), thousand-grain weight (340.8), above-ground dry biomass (18.3), and grain yield (6.1) (Table 7), indicating the presence of high-yielding and vigorous varieties within this group. In contrast, Cluster I was characterized by comparatively higher values for tasseling date (77.6), silking date (79.4), plant height (237.6), and cob length (13.6) (Table 7), suggesting relatively late-maturing and taller varieties. Cluster II recorded lower mean values for plant height (179.2) and thousand-grain weight (228.3), whereas Cluster III was distinguished by comparatively lower values for grains per cob (409.8), tasseling date (74.6), and silking date (76.3) (Table 7).

Discussion

The negative effect of soil acidity in crop growth and productivity is well documented for a range of field and horticultural crops including maize30. However, these effects vary among crops and among cultivars within a crop, depending on their ability to tolerate soil acidity stress31. Maize varieties show different performance under two distinct pH levels (pH 4.8 and 5.9) with generally increasing in growth and yield components under higher soil pH. The level of performance under lower soil pH showed variation among varieties, indicating differences in their levels of tolerance to soil acidity.

The duration of flowering date was more affected by genetic factor than environmental factor. The variation in days to 50% tasseling and silking indicated their genetic characteristics, with late maturing variety (BH661) delaying flowering and early maturing variety (Gibe2) flowering earlier. This results showed that flowering date is more strongly influenced by genetic factors than environmental conditions, as reported by24,25,32. However, 33reported that diverse stress factors can accelerate or delay flowering in a wide range of plant species. 34reported that flowering time delayed in susceptible cultivars along with the reduced grain yield due to Al stress under low soil pH. In contrast, 35found positive correlation between low pH and flowering. They reported that plants exposed to low pH produced more flowers than those exposed to higher pH.

Soil acidity and associated soil fertility constraints are major factors limiting plant growth in many parts of the world12,15,36. Significant growth variation was observed between two locations or two pH levels and among nine varieties. The influence of soil acidity on plant height was the most critical that bring about 48% variation between lower and higher soil pH. Similarly, the leaf area index of maize increase by 30.7% under higher soil pH compared to lower soil pH. Our results are in line with37 who reported that plant height and dry matter yield of maize significantly decreased under low soil pH due to Al toxicity. The results also supported by 22who reported that the effect of soil acidity on plant height is more clearly visible with a marked growth depression. 12reported that soil acidity severely affected leaf appearance, leaf size and consequently the LAI.

The enhancement of plant height and leaf area index under higher soil pH (pH 5.9) indicated that the soil condition is appropriate to regularly uptake plant nutrients thought growing period, and reduced toxic nutrient that impair plant growth8,12,38. On other hand the variability in plant height and leaf area index among maize varieties indicated their genetic difference in their vigorously, and their ability to withstand soil acidity. The response of different maize varieties to soil pH is well documented by many authors15,36,37. Most of the varieties falling between the tallest and shortest varieties showed similar growth pattern. our findings are consistent with those reported by 39who observed variations in the degree of tolerance to acid soils, categorizing the varieties into highly tolerant, moderately tolerant, and low-tolerant groups.

The adverse effect of soil acidity on the growth, yield and yield components of maize have been increasingly documented23,40–42. Both soil acidity and varietal difference significantly influence yield components, including cob length, cob diameter, grains per cob and thousand grain weight5,8,42. The reduction of these yield components under acidic soil might be attributed to the detrimental the effect of low pH stress, which limit the availability of essential nutrient required for cob elongation, silk emergence and grain filling43,44,12–15. Numerous studies have consistently reported the negative impact of soil acidity on maize yield and yield components7,8,34. The optimum soil pH ranged between 5.5 and 6.7 for favorable nutrient uptake, growth, and biomass accumulation of maize5. 27also reported that maize responded to the tropical acid soil through alteration of assimilate and nutrient partitioning, which finally reduced the biomass production and yield.

The influence soil acidity on each growth and yield variable ultimately determine final biomass and grain yield, as these are cumulative result of all metabolic processes throughout plant development. In this study, biomass and grain yield were significantly reduced under acidic soil conditions by about 51.6% and 56.4%, respectively. Despite the overall yield reduction, certain maize varieties showed relatively best performance under acidic conditions compared to others. Specifically, the varieties BH5211, BH661, and BH547 produced higher grain yields at lower soil pH, whereas the remaining varieties showed comparatively lower yields. The observed variation in yield performance both within and across soil pH levels at the two experimental sites indicates differential responses of maize varieties to soil acidity. These differences may be attributed to variations in physiological, biochemical, and molecular mechanisms that enable certain varieties to tolerate acid stress, as also reported by5,45. Previous studies have similarly observed significant differences in yield loss between acid-tolerant and acid-sensitive cultivars under low pH conditions21,24,25,27,46. Thus, in this study three maize varieties (BH5211, BH661 and BH547) performed well in grain yield and in most others yield variables compared to other maize varieties. The difference in performance among maize varieties indicates that these hybrids may have a higher degree of tolerance to the relatively lower soil pH, making them a preferable choice for cultivation under acid prone cultivated soil. Conversely, the lower performance of some maize varieties like BHQPY545 could suggest their reduced adaptability to acid prone soil.

In general, the notable difference in performance among maize varieties under acidic soil indicates the presence of different degree of tolerant and susceptibility to soil acidity stress. Evaluation of crop response under different soil pH provides a better understanding and an ease way in the selection of a variety suitable for acid prone soil14.

The results of the Principal Component Analysis reveal that the first two principal components account for a significant portion of the variability among the maize varieties, with a combined contribution of 85.2%. The dominance of the first principal component, which explains 68% of the total divergence, indicates that it captures the primary trends in the data with all variables contributing positively. This suggests that the majority of quantitative variables is closely related and vary together. The second principal component contributes a smaller proportion of variation (17.2%). It highlights the role Kernels rows per ear, Ear diameter and leaf area index in distinguishing among the varieties. Overall, these findings emphasize the effectiveness of PCA in identifying key traits that influence the diversity of maize varieties, providing a valuable framework for future research and selection efforts as previously reported by47.

The cluster analysis based on PCA scores effectively delineated the relationships among the nine maize varieties, revealing distinct groupings that correspond to their phenological, growth and yield traits. The results highlighted that the majority of the varieties were concentrated in cluster I and III, indicating a commonality in their yield and growth characteristics. The distribution pattern among all varieties into different clusters showed the presence of considerable genetic divergence among the varieties for most of the traits. Cluster IV comprised two varieties that had better performance in yield and yield related traits like; biomass, thousand grain weight, Grain rows per cob, grains per cob, stalk girth and leaf area index. The high value of grain yield and yield related variables under cluster IV indicated that selection of varieties from this cluster can be worthwhile. These results are in line to48–50.

Stress induces have developed to indicted the performance of varieties or genotypes under stress condition in relative to or in comparison to non-stress conditions based on induced value. Higher value for each of MP (Mean Productivity), GMP (Geometric Mean Productivity), HMP (Harmonic Mean Productivity), MSY (Mean Stable Yield), MRP (Mean Relative Performance), YSI (Yield Stability Index), REI (Relative Efficiency Index), STI (Stress Tolerance Index), RSI (Relative Stress Index), SRI and (Stress Resistance Index) indicates tolerant varieties to stress condition and vice versa51–56. Our calculation showed similar trend for BH5211 maize variety with higher value in (MP, GMP, HMP, MRP, REI and STI) indicating its ability to tolerate acidic soil condition. Similarly, BH547 showed higher value on MSY, YSI, RST and SRI indicating its ability to withstand acid prone environment too. On other hand, higher value for each of TOL (Tolerance Index), SSI (Stress Susceptibility Index) and PYL (Percentage Yield Loss) indicates susceptibility to stress condition and vice versa51,57,58. In our calculation high of TOL, SSI and PYL was found from variety BHQPY545 indicating its susceptibility to soil acidity condition.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that maize varieties deferentially performed with respect to plant growth parameters, biomass production, grain yield and related components under soil acidity levels. Although the grain yield of all maize varieties reduced under acidic soil, three varieties (BH5211, BH661, and BH547) showed the least yield reductions under acidic soil and high grain yield stability across the two pH levels. This indicates their broad adaptability while BHQPY545 showed highest yield reduction under acidic soil and instability across the two pH levels suggesting its specific adaptability. Stress tolerance indices supported the agronomic results, identifying all these three varieties as the most tolerant to soil acidity, and BHQPY545 as the most susceptible to acid stress. Therefore, BH5211, BH661 and BH547 are particularly valuable for acid-prone areas where soil pH modification is limited due to restricted access to liming materials. The integration of PCA and cluster analysis effectively revealed genetic diversity and key variables influencing varieties performance. Overall, findings demonstrate the need for future crop improvement programs to deliberately consider soil acidity tolerance as one of selection criteria in the developing of maize varieties for high grain yield. In addition, we suggested further studies on acid tolerance mechanisms, soil factors, and multi-location field trials to validate and refine the selection of tolerant varieties for acidic environments.

Materials and methods

Description of experimental sites

The experiment was conducted in 2023 at Bako National Maize Research Center of the Ethiopian Institute of Agricultural Research at two locations with distinct soil pH. The geographical location of the site1 (with soil pH 4.8) is at 9º 10’ N latitude and 37º 04’ E longitudes with an altitude of 1660 meters above sea level. The geographical location of the site 2 (with soil pH 5.9) is at 9º 09’ N latitude and 37º 04’ E longitudes with an altitude of 1608 m above sea level. The field experiment was done during the main rainy season from May to December. The two locations share similar agroecology and have similar rain fall and climate pattern. According to the weather data recorded by Bako Meteorological Station, nearby both sites, the location that covers both sites has minimum, maximum and average annual temperatures of 13.5, 29.7 and 21.4 in degree Celsius, respectively. The average annual rainfall is 1237 mm.

Treatments and experimental design

The treatments consisted of nine maize varieties. All maize varieties were tested at two sites with two distinct soil’s pH of 4.8 and 5.9. These two pH levels were determined using soil laboratory test. The 1.1 pH unit difference between the sites provides a sufficient contrast to evaluate maize varieties response to lower and higher soil pH under real field conditions. The experiment was laid out as a randomized complete block design with three replications per treatment. The replications were separated by 1 m wide space. The maize varieties were randomly assigned within a replication. The plot size was 7.5 m2 with row length of 5 m. One border row was planted at starting and at ending of each replication.

Soil sampling and analysis

At both locations, a representative soil samples were taken from 0 to 30 cm depth in a diagonal pattern among each 5 m interval before planting by using auger. The sample soils were analyzed at soil laboratory for physical and chemical properties of soil using standard laboratory procedures: soil pH was determined in a 1:2.5 soil-to-water (H₂O) suspension using pH meter; exchangeable acidity and exchangeable Al³⁺ were determined using the Van Reeuwijk method with 1 N KCl extraction followed by titration; total nitrogen (N) was analyzed using the Kjeldahl method as described by J. M. Bremner and C. S. Mulvaney; available phosphorus (P) was determined using the Bray II method; organic carbon (OC) was measured by the Walkley-Black method (wet digestion); and cation exchange capacity (CEC) was determined using the ammonium acetate extraction method. The lab results showed that for site I (pH 4.8, exchangeable Al (1.03 meq/100 g), organic carbon (1.63%), total nitrogen (0.14%) and available phosphorus (24.35ppm); cation exchange capacity (6.03 meq/100 g), and for site II pH (5.9), exchangeable Al (0.62 meq/100 g), organic carbon (1.8%), total nitrogen (0.16%) and available phosphorus (31.36ppm), cation exchange capacity (7.57 meq/100 g). Both sites were used for maize growing in the previous year.

Field operations and crop management

The experimental field was prepared for planting by ploughing three times using a tractor mounted plough during March and May. Planting was done in June 2023. The recommended amount of phosphorus fertilizer in the form of NPS and nitrogen fertilizer in the form of urea was applied uniformly to all plots at both locations at the national recommended rate of 69 kg P2O5 ha and 92 kg N ha− 1 respectively. Full dose of phosphate fertilizer was applied at sowing time. Half dose of N fertilizer was applied 20 days after planting and the remaining half dose of N fertilizer was applied 40 days after planting in side-banding method and covering with soil. Weeds were removed just before the second split urea fertilizer application. Weeding and other crop management practices were applied uniformly to all plots as per the recommendation given for managing weeds in maize fields. Finally, maize plants were harvested from each plot area at maturity stage.

Data analysis

Analyses of variances for the recorded data were conducted using SAS version 9.3. A combined analysis variance across the two locations was conducted to evaluate the overall performance and stability of the maize varieties. Tukey’s test was used to assess and compare the means of the treatments. General linear model and two-way ANOVA were carried out to investigate main (variety, soil pH) and interaction effects.

The model is linear model

Yik = µ + Ti+Rk + εik for each location, and Yijk = µ + Lj + Rk(j) +Ti + (LT)ij + εijk for combined analysis.

The type of model is mixed model

treatment is fixed, location is random, rep (location) is random, treatment × location is random.

Error structure

location tested against rep (location), treatment tested against treatment × location, treatment × location tested against residual error, within-location treatment comparisons tested against residual error.

ANOVA assumptions was checked using

independence, normality of residuals, homogeneity of variance (for each site analysis). Normality of pooled residuals, homogeneity of variance across, homogeneity of error variance between locations (for combined analysis).

Minitab software version 21 was used for principal component analysis. We used standardized data for PCA analysis. Microsoft Excel was used for data entry and calculation of stress tolerance indices. The spearman’s rank correlation analysis was done using SAS version 9.3.
