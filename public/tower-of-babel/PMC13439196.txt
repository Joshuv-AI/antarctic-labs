Molecular basis of host ATP level modulation by actin-dependent secreted bacterial ATPase and its metaeffector
Authors: Hongxin Guan, Yu Li, Luhao Zhang, Song Xie, Yuchen Jiang, Chunlin He, Fan Li, Yin-di Jiao, Mengrou Gan, Yiran Sha, Fengjie Zheng, Kaiqiong Zhang, Zhao-qing Luo, Qi Lai, Jinfu Su, Ningshao Xia, Qingbing Zheng, Jinyu Li, Shaowei Li, Songying Ouyang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLegionella pneumophila employs effectors including kinases, phosphoryl-AMPylases, ATPases, etc. to exploit host ATP for infection. Ceg14, a member of the S-HxxxE family, modulates host-cell energy levels in concert with host actin and the metaeffector AnkJ: actin activates while AnkJ inhibits Ceg14 ATPase activity. However, the molecular basis of this regulation remains unclear. Here we present Cryo-EM structures of Ceg14-actin, Ceg14-AnkJ, and Ceg14-actin-AnkJ complexes at 2.89 Å, 2.93 Å, and 2.52 Å, respectively. Actin binds to the C-terminal α-helix of the Ceg14 catalytic domain (CD), inducing rearrangement of its N-terminal domain (NTD) and reconfiguration of the flexible Lid domain. Surprisingly, AnkJ binds to the surface opposite the catalytic pocket rather than occupying the pocket. Using integrated in silico, in vitro, and in cellulo approaches, we propose a mechanism for Ceg14-mediated ATP hydrolysis. Actin binding triggers NTD rotation, driving the catalytic pocket through open, intermediate, and closed states. In the open state, H571 catalyzes ATP conversion to AMP and PPi. AnkJ binding to an allosteric site locks the intermediate state, thereby inhibiting hydrolysis. Together, our study reveals the molecular mechanism by which actin and AnkJ reciprocally regulate Ceg14 activity.Subject terms: Cryoelectron microscopy, Enzyme mechanisms, Computational biophysics

Introduction

ATP is the universal energy currency of life. The energy released from its high-energy phosphate bonds1,2 fuels numerous cellular processes, including cell movement, metabolism, transmembrane transport, and biosynthesis of macromolecules3–5. The products of ATP hydrolysis, such as adenosine diphosphate (ADP) and adenosine monophosphate (AMP), act as metabolic sensors that regulate glycolipid metabolism and energy production by activating effectors like AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK)6,7. ATP also coordinates metabolic flux, signaling, and gene expression by binding to the active and/or regulatory sites of enzymes6,8,9. This ATP feedback cycle links cellular energy status to physiological function, sustaining homeostasis and adaptability10. Consequently, ATP and its derivatives are key targets in host-pathogen conflicts11,12.

Legionella pneumophila, a Gram-negative intracellular pathogen notorious for causing Legionnaires’ disease, profoundly modifies host ATP metabolism to facilitate its survival and proliferation13,14. To date, 15 effector proteins have been recognized as crucial regulators in the ATP-related processes. These include proteins that regulate ATP synthesis and transport, as well as hydrolyze ATP to modulate levels within infected cells15. These processes are predominantly executed through a series of post-translational modifications (PTMs), including phosphorylation16–18, AMPylation19–22, and ADP-ribosylation23–25, among others. Ultimately, these effectors intricately reshape the host cell environment, empowering pathogens to evade immune responses26–29.

Ceg14 (a.k.a. Lpg0437 and SidL) is a reported 666-amino-acid effector protein of L. pneumophila, which is a translational inhibitor30,31. Moreover, recombinant Ceg14 exhibits the ability to impede actin polymerization in biochemical assays32. This enzyme is characterized by the presence of a conserved S-HxxxE motif, and exhibits the ability to hydrolyze ATP through an actin-dependent mechanism, yielding AMP and pyrophosphate33. The ATP hydrolysis activity of Ceg14 can be inhibited by its metaeffector AnkJ (a.k.a. Lpg0436 and LegA11), although AnkJ enhances the interaction between Ceg14 and actin. AnkJ work together with Ceg14 to modulate cellular ATP levels creating an environment that facilitates bacterial replication33. However, the ATP hydrolysis mechanism of Ceg14 is distinct from that of LnaB (Lpg2527), a characterized member of the S-HxxxE family34. LnaB catalyzes a two-step hydrolysis of ATP to generate AMP, which is subsequently transferred to the phosphoryl group of PR-ubiquitin (PR-Ub)35,36. Several critical questions have remained fully unanswered regarding Ceg14 and AnkJ-mediated ATP regulation. They include: (i) the structural determinants of the Ceg14-actin, Ceg14-AnkJ, and Ceg14-actin-AnkJ complexes, (ii) the molecular mechanism behind Ceg14-actin-AnkJ-mediated ATP regulation, and (iii) why Ceg14 and LnaB produce distinct ATP hydrolysis products.

In this work, we present Cryo-EM structures of Ceg14 in complex with actin, AnkJ, or both, at resolutions of 2.89 Å, 2.93 Å, and 2.52 Å, respectively. We show that both actin and AnkJ bind outside the ATP-binding site of Ceg14. Using molecular dynamics simulations, molecular docking, and extensive in vitro and in vivo mutagenesis, we provide the molecular basis for Ceg14-mediated ATP hydrolysis. Our findings reveal that actin binding triggers conformational changes that facilitate catalysis, whereas AnkJ allosterically locks an intermediate state to inhibit activity. Together, these results offer detailed molecular insights into host-cell ATP regulation by the actin-Ceg14-AnkJ axis and highlight the functional diversity within the S-HxxxE family.

Results

Ceg14 activity is regulated by AnkJ and defines a subgroup of the S-HxxxE family

To clarify the S-HxxxE motif features in Ceg14, we initially compared its sequence and structure with those of LnaB (Supplementary Fig. 1). The S-HxxxE motif of Ceg14 (S527, H571, and E575) is located at α19 and α21 helices of the catalytic domain (CD, residues 245–638), covered by a long unstructured loop (residues 433–529) that forms a lid-like structure (referred to as Lid). The CD features a five-helical bundle (residues 245–326) that leads into a compact C-terminal catalytic sub-domain spanning residues 336–638 (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). The key distinction between Ceg14 and LnaB lies in their domain architectures: Ceg14 has an additional N-terminal helical bundle domain (NTD, residues 63–218) linked to the CD by a long flexible loop, whereas LnaB has an extra C-terminal three-helical bundle domain (CTD) (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). Ceg14 has a robust actin-dependent capacity for converting ATP into AMP and PPi (Fig. 1b). Substituting any of the amino acids (S527, H571, and E575) with alanine completely inactivated enzymatic activity (Fig. 1c). The distinct enzymatic activities observed between Ceg14 and LnaB, as well as its homologs LHLS and LHEI, suggest that these proteins may represent distinct subgroups within the S-HxxxE family33,34. To further explore this diversity, we performed a PSI-BLAST search against the Non-Redundant (NR) Protein Sequence Database, which identified 749 homologs of Ceg14 containing the conserved S-HxxxE motif distributed across more than 20 bacterial families. Besides LnaB and Ceg14, LegK4 (a.k.a. Lpg0208 and Pkn5) and Lpg0209 were also identified in L. pneumophila strain Philadelphia 1, consistent with a previous study on the global atlas of predicted functional domains in L. pneumophila effectors15 (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 1c). Moreover, the adjacent ankj gene of ceg14 encodes AnkJ, an ankyrin-repeat metaeffector that inhibits Ceg14 (Fig. 1e)33. Although LegK4 and Lpg0209 are co-located with RavG (lpg0210), and MavL and LnaB flank MavK (Lpg2525), no experimental evidence shows that RavG or MavK inhibit the S-HxxxE activity of LegK4/Lpg0209 or LnaB, respectively. These data suggest that the S-HxxxE family is more extensive and structurally complex than previously recognized.

Actin binding remodels Ceg14 conformation and increases Ceg14 affinity for ATP

To investigate the role of actin as a Ceg14 cofactor, we performed pulldown assays using recombinant His-tagged Ceg14 and rabbit muscle G-actin (Sangon Biotech, Cat. No. A001041). Complex formation was confirmed by Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining (CBB) and Western blotting (WB). The results indicate that Ceg14 directly interacts with actin (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Consistent with the pull-down assay, a stable 1:1 Ceg14-actin complex (s20, w = 6.09 S, Mr = 104 kDa) was detected in solution by analytical ultracentrifugation (AUC) (Fig. 2a). Furthermore, we used microscale thermophoresis (MST) to measure the binding affinity (Kd) of the catalytically inactive Ceg14S527A mutant for actin and ATP. Ceg14S527A binds actin with a Kd of ~231 µM and ATP with a Kd of ~203 µM. Actin binding significantly enhances Ceg14’s affinity for ATP. Specifically, the binding of ATP to the Ceg14S527A-actin complex is enhanced by approximately eightfold, resulting in a Kd of ~26 µM (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 2b). These data suggest that actin binding may alter the conformation of the Ceg14 ATP binding pocket in a manner that enhances ATP binding and subsequent hydrolysis.

To elucidate the structural basis of how actin binding triggers this conformational change in Ceg14, we determined the structure of the Ceg14-actin complex. However, full-length Ceg14 proved unstable under our experimental conditions. We therefore identified a stable, catalytically active construct encompassing residues 63–649 and determined its Cryo-EM structure in complex with actin at 2.89 Å resolution (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 2c, d, Supplementary Fig. 3). The interface features four contact areas between Ceg14 and actin, contacts A, B, C, and the C-terminal contact (CTC), established through extensive polar and hydrophobic interactions. Contact A, which involves the loop1 between α7 and α8 in the NTD of Ceg14 (α18, residues 179–188), along with Contact B, encompassing the extended loop2 that connects the NTD to the CD (residues 219–244), as well as Contact C, pertaining to a portion of the Lid region (loop3, residues 467–492), collectively establish a broad platform that facilitates actin binding (Fig. 2d, e and Supplementary Fig. 4a). Concurrently, the amphipathic C-terminal α-helix (α23, residues 606–638; contact CTC) inserts into the hydrophobic groove between actin subdomains 1 and 3 (SD1 and SD3), stabilized by hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions (Fig. 2f). In line with these structural analyses, co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays demonstrate that mutations resulting in deletion of the NTD or of any one of the following regions, loop 1 within the NTD, loop 3 within the Lid domain, or the C-terminal segment (residues 623–638) in helix α23 of Ceg14, completely abolish the interaction between Ceg14 and actin (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. 4b, c). Moreover, all four mutants effectively nullified the capacity of Ceg14 to hydrolyze ATP into AMP in vitro (Supplementary Fig. 4d). Consistently, intracellular ATP levels in cells transfected with these Ceg14 mutants were indistinguishable from those in cells transfected with empty vector control (Fig. 2h). These data suggest that these regions play an important role in actin recognition and binding by Ceg14.

Subsequently, we compared the structure of Ceg14 bound to actin with the unbound Ceg14 structure. Two substantial conformational changes were identified: rotation of the NTD and reconfiguration of the Lid, occurring in two distinct regions, respectively (Fig. 3a,b). To further investigate the structural dynamics of these regions in Ceg14-actin complex and AF-Ceg14 (AlphaFold3-Ceg14), we performed 1-μs all-atom unbiased MD simulations of each system in solution (Figs. S5 and S6). In the Ceg14-actin complex, the center-of-mass (COM) distance between the NTD and the CD α-helix (α18, residues 400–412) oscillated between approximately 22 and 30 Å during the first 0.4 μs, before stabilizing at ~20 Å (Fig. 3c). Furthermore, the large-scale motion (PC1–PC4) obtained by principal component analysis (PCA) of the final 0.9 μs of the equilibrated trajectories, which accounted for over 60% of the total variance, revealed a pronounced open-close motion between the NTD and CD of Ceg14. This defined three distinct conformational states: an open state (COM distance ~28 Å), an intermediate state (~23 Å, consistent with our structures), and a closed state (~20 Å) (Fig. 3d–f and Supplementary Fig. 5). In contrast, the COM distance in AF-Ceg14 fluctuated around 47.5 Å. Additionally, the Lid region was more stable in the Ceg14-actin complex compared to AF-Ceg14. These findings were corroborated by the other two independent replicates of both systems (Supplementary Fig. 5 and 6). Collectively, our results indicate that actin binding stimulates the ATP binding ability of Ceg14 by causing conformational changes in the NTD and Lid regions.

H571 directly interacts with ATP during hydrolysis

To elucidate the structural mechanism by which actin activates Ceg14 to facilitate hydrolysis of ATP, we endeavored to determine the structure of either Ceg14H571A-actin or Ceg14-actin in conjunction with ATP or AMP-CPP, a structural analog of ATP. However, despite our efforts to augment the molar ratio of the binary complex relative to ATP/AMP-CPP and extend the incubation durations, we have been unable to capture the architecture of the complex incorporating ATP or AMP-CPP.

To address this issue, we performed ensemble-guided docking to predict the interactions between Ceg14 and ATP. We utilized the Ceg14-actin structure and its MD conformations across different NTD states (open, intermediate, and closed), alongside representative MD conformations of Ceg14 obtained via clustering analysis. All poses exhibited Pα (ATP)-Nε2 (H571) distances exceeding 6 Å (Supplementary Fig. 7 and 8), except for one configuration positioned at a distance of approximately 4.4 Å (Fig. 3g and Supplementary Fig. 7), which is geometrically consistent with H571-mediated ATP hydrolysis to AMP and PPi37. Several Ceg14 residues, including R455, F570, N412, R441, R443, and H529 form intermolecular interactions with ATP and Ceg14 in this model. In addition, the other two residues in S-HxxxE motif (E575 and S527) form hydrogen bonding interactions with H571, stabilizing its contacts with ATP (Fig. 3g). To validate our model, we conducted in vivo single-point alanine mutagenesis on the identified ATP-interacting residues in Ceg14 (Fig. 3g, h), as well as on several control residues. These controls were chosen based on residues that interact with the ligand in the homologous enzymes of Ceg14 (Fig. 3h and Supplementary Fig. 9). Notably, only the substitution of the four key interacting residues identified in our model (R443A, R455A, H529A, and F570A) fully abolished Ceg14 activity, while the other mutations did not significantly affect catalytic function (Fig. 3g,h). These results emphasize the reliability of our model. Strikingly, R455 is located in the Lid region, suggesting that the Lid region interacts directly with ATP during its hydrolysis (Fig. 3g). These findings were consistent with our previous observation that remodeling of the Lid region is critical for Ceg14 activity. It is noteworthy that, in the absence of actin, the highly flexible Lid region fails to adopt a stable conformation. This observation is further supported by the absence of Lid-domain density in Ceg14, as observed in both our determined Cryo-EM structure and the recently reported crystal structure of the Ceg14-AnkJ complex38 (Supplementary Fig. 10).

The interaction pattern between ATP and Ceg14 in our model closely resembles the S-HxxxE motif identified in LnaB and LHLS34. Moreover, the histidine placement also mirrors features of the HPFx[D/E]GN[G/K]R motifs, common to Fic family proteins (Supplementary Fig. 9)34,35,39. A notable difference, however, is that the acidic D/E residues within the Fic catalytic motif are indispensable for magnesium ion binding through coordination bonds; instead, the E residue (E575) in the Ceg14 catalytic motif appears to facilitate protonation of the essential histidine residue, akin to its function in LnaB35. Nevertheless, in LnaB and LHLS, additional residues assume critical roles in coordinating magnesium ions. These residues are strategically positioned near the ATP binding pocket: specifically, N182D183 or N164D165. Although N412 of Ceg14 occupies an analogous position to those found in LnaB and LHLS, it lacks a paired D residue. Furthermore, distinct from LnaB and LHLS, R455 on the Lid region located at Ceg14 actively participates in ATP binding (Supplementary Fig. 9).

Taken together, our in silico and in vivo experiments provide the structural determinants of Ceg14 ATP hydrolysis. Specifically, H571 interacts directly with ATP, while S527 and E575 facilitate this process by stabilizing the position of H571 (Fig. 1c and Fig. 3g).

S-HxxxE members recognize actin in a conserved manner

Based on the critical role of the C-terminal helix in actin binding by Ceg14 and LnaB, structural alignment revealed that their C-terminal α-helice share a common actin-binding mechanism with diverse actin-binding proteins, including vitamin D-binding protein (DBP), twinfilin (Twf), and gelsolin40–42. All these proteins feature an α-helix that inserts precisely into the hydrophobic groove formed between SD1 and SD3 of actin (Fig. 4a). To systematically investigate actin-recognition mechanisms across the S-HxxxE family, we selected eleven Ceg14 homologs, representing diverse evolutionary lineages, for sequence and structural alignment (Supplementary Fig. 11a, b). Although substantial domain architecture diversity exists among these proteins, all contain the conserved S-HxxxE catalytic motif, an N-terminal α-helical bundle, and a C-terminal α-helix. However, certain homologs possess additional uncharacterized domains at the N-terminus, whereas others harbor such domains at the C-terminus. Notably, the C-terminal α-helix falls into two distinct structural classes: Type Ⅰ features a bipartite helix disconnected centrally by a flexible loop, where the N-terminal segment is embedded in the catalytic domain and the C-terminal extension forms a solvent-exposed, non-canonical helix proximal to it, exemplified by LegK4 and Lpg0209; Type Ⅱ comprises a single, extended canonical α-helix, as observed in the remaining seven homologs, including LnaB and Ceg14 (Supplementary Fig. 11b). In addition, although the actin-binding α-helix in canonical actin-binding proteins is embedded in the core domain, one face remains fully solvent-exposed and spatially accessible for insertion into the interdomain groove between actin subdomains SD1 and SD3. In contrast, the solvent-exposed face of Type Ⅰ’s α-helical N-terminus is occluded by the N-terminal helical bundle and Lid, causing pronounced steric hindrance (Supplementary Fig. 11c). Based on this structural dichotomy, we hypothesize that Type Ⅰ proteins are incapable of actin binding, whereas Type Ⅱ proteins likely bind actin and utilize it as a functional cofactor. To test this hypothesis, we examined LegK4, Lpg0209, and CHVC (Ceg14 homolog in Vibrio cholerae). Consistent with our prediction, LegK4 and Lpg0209 showed no detectable actin binding, failed to reduce cellular ATP levels, and were unable to AMPylate PR-Ub (Fig. 4b–d). By contrast, CHVC bound actin and hydrolyzed ATP (Fig. 4e, f).

Furthermore, we introduced a strategic mutation at residue T623 of Ceg14, substituting threonine with proline (T623P) to disrupt the local α-helical structure and convert a type-Ⅱ α-helix into a type-Ⅰ conformation. As a result, the Ceg14T623P mutant exhibited complete loss of function in both actin binding and ATP hydrolysis (Supplementary Fig. 12a–c). These results strongly indicate that structural integrity of this α-helix is essential for productive actin interaction. Further structural analysis revealed that the interaction between helix α23 of Ceg14 and actin is mediated predominantly by extensive hydrophobic contacts and electrostatic interactions between positively (α23) and negatively (actin) charged regions (Supplementary Fig. 12d). Moreover, sequence alignment of the seven type Ⅱ S-HxxxE family members and 139 Ceg14 orthologs reveals that this α-helix displays characteristic amphipathic properties, with highly conserved amino acid residues. Notably, positions 632 and 636, critical for hydrophobic interactions, are occupied by hydrophobic amino acids, whereas positions 629 and 633 are occupied by positively charged residues (Supplementary Fig. 12e, f). Consistent with these structural features, deleting the entire helical segment (Δ624–649) or introducing the T623P substitution, both disrupting helix integrity, abolished Ceg14 activity, whereas single-residue substitutions within the helix did not (Supplementary Fig. 12g). In conclusion, these findings suggest that Type Ⅱ S-HxxxE family proteins likely bind actin, as a cofactor to facilitate specific enzymatic activities, via a conserved mechanism.

AnkJ inhibits Ceg14 by blocking the rotation of NTD, thereby inhibiting ATP hydrolysis

L. pneumophila encodes a family of translocated effectors called metaeffectors, with at least a dozen identified so far14. While many metaeffectors function by post-translationally modifying their cognate effectors or cellular targets, such as LubX (with SidH as its cognate effector), SidJ (with SidEs as its cognate effector), etc., some inhibit their activity by directly binding to prevent substrate interactions, such as Lpg2149 (with MavC and MvcA as its cognate effectors), etc43,44. Surprisingly, as the metaeffector of Ceg14, AnkJ exhibits a capacity to significantly enhance the interaction between Ceg14 and actin, rather than inhibiting their binding. Moreover, the presence of actin further enhances the binding affinity of AnkJ (Supplementary Fig. 13a, b). To elucidate the mechanism by which AnkJ impedes the enzymatic activity of Ceg14, we constructed a ternary complex comprising Ceg14, actin, and AnkJ through gel filtration chromatography. Although its elution volume was comparable to that observed in the binary complex formed by Ceg14 and actin, subsequent AUC unequivocally confirmed that a complex consisting of Ceg14, actin, and AnkJ was indeed formed with an approximate molecular weight of 132 kDa. This finding indicates a stoichiometric ratio of 1:1:1 among these three components (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 13c).

We subsequently determined the Cryo-EM structure of the ternary complex comprising Ceg14, actin, and AnkJ, achieving a resolution of 2.52 Å (Supplementary Fig. 14). AnkJ is characterized by its composition of 13 parallel helices, epitomizing a prototypical ankyrin repeat-containing protein. Notably, it engages with the distal side of the enzymatic pocket of Ceg14 rather than encroaching upon or obstructing it. Furthermore, no significant interactions are discerned between AnkJ and actin (Fig. 5b). Utilizing PISA software (Protein Interfaces, Surfaces and Assemblies; www.ebi.ac.uk/pdbe/pisa/), we uncovered extensive contact within the solvent-accessible area interposed between Ceg14 and AnkJ (3810 Å²), corroborating the pronounced affinity observed in their interaction. The elongated flexible C-terminal region of AnkJ (residues 232 to 255) penetrates into a hydrophobic hole sculpted by both the NTD and CD of Ceg14 and AnkJ itself through hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions, adopting an intricate knob-into-hole configuration (Fig. 5c, d and Supplementary Fig. 15a). In alignment with these structural insights, mutations resulting in deletions within the C-terminal region of AnkJ (Δ213–end, Δ232–end, and Δ246–end) or alterations to the NTD of Ceg14 significantly disrupted their interactions, both in vitro and in vivo, (Fig. 5e and Supplementary Fig. 15b–d). These mutants exhibited an inability to inhibit ATP hydrolysis mediated by Ceg14 (Supplementary Fig. 15e). Sequence alignment of the AnkJ orthologs reveals that the C-terminal region is highly conserved among the 139 L. pneumophila orthologs (Supplementary Fig. 15f). In addition to the hydrophobic hole interactions, several residues in the AnkJ core domain form hydrogen bonds with complementary residues in both the Ceg14 NTD and CD (Fig. 5f), stabilizing the AnkJ-Ceg14 complex. However, single-point mutations of these residues failed to fully abolish AnkJ-mediated inhibition of Ceg14 activity (Supplementary Fig. 15g). Collectively, these results indicate that the AnkJ C-terminal region is essential for effective suppression of Ceg14 activity.

Our structural analysis reveals that AnkJ binds to the opposite side of the enzyme pocket in Ceg14, seamlessly integrating into the V-shaped groove formed by the CD and NTD domains of Ceg14. Given the extensive intermolecular interactions between AnkJ and Ceg14, we infer that the NTD of Ceg14 is effectively immobilized, thereby preventing conformational transition of the enzyme pocket to the open state required for hydrolysis initiation. To further validate our assumption, we applied the same MD and docking protocol, as we carried out on the Ceg14-actin, to the Ceg14-actin-AnkJ and Ceg14-AnkJ complexes to investigate the structural mechanism behind the AnkJ-mediated inhibition of Ceg14 ATP hydrolysis. In contrast to the Ceg14-actin complex, the COM distance in both AnkJ-bound complexes was consistently maintained at approximately 25 Å across three independent replicates (Supplementary Figs. 16 and 17). Consequently, both the NTD and CD failed to exhibit discernible open-close dynamic behavior (Fig. 5g and Supplementary Figs. 16 and 17). Furthermore, the Pα (ATP)-Nε2 (H571) distance in all docking poses of the ATP-bound complexes was observed to be greater than 6 Å (Supplementary Figs. 18 and 19). This spatial separation likely prevents Ceg14 from facilitating ATP hydrolysis. Notably, AnkJ binding does not inhibit ATP binding to Ceg14; binding affinity measurements show that ATP associates with the Ceg14-actin-AnkJ ternary complex with a Kd of approximately 173 μM. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that AnkJ inhibits the ATP-hydrolytic activity of Ceg14 via a distinct allosteric mechanism: rather than blocking ATP binding, AnkJ suppresses the conformational dynamics required for the active site to adopt a catalytically competent state.

Discussion

The intracellular bacterial pathogen L. pneumophila modulates diverse host cell functions, including energy metabolism, protein translation, cell cycle regulation, the ubiquitin system, and immune responses, by secreting over 330 effector proteins with varied biochemical activities27,45. Among these host targets, ATP and its derivatives play pivotal roles in host-pathogen conflicts and are themselves regulated by multiple effectors, either as ligands, substrates, or direct enzymatic targets23,24,33. Recently, a family of AMPylases has been identified (referred to as the LnaB family), characterized by two conserved motifs: SG(T/S)X and G(Y/G)HSXXE. Prototypical members like LnaB use ATP as a substrate and are activated by host actin. LnaB converts PR-Ub into ADP-ribosylated ubiquitin, which is then hydrolyzed by the (ADP-ribosyl)hydrolase MavL to release free ADP-ribose and functional ubiquitin. Together with SidE-family effectors and deubiquitinases DupA and DupB, this pathway helps maintain ubiquitin homeostasis in infected host cells34,36,46–48. Moreover, LnaB exhibits both adenylyltransferase and ATPase activities, with its phosphoryl-AMPylation proceeding via a two-step mechanism35. But surprisingly, Ceg14, another member of this family, has been found serving as an ATPase that binds actin to modulate ATP levels with its metaeffector AnkJ in host cells. Unlike LnaB, Ceg14 converts ATP and dATP into adenosine and deoxyadenosine monophosphate33.

In this study, we identified 749 Ceg14 homologs bearing the conserved S-HxxxE motif, widely distributed across diverse bacterial pathogens (Fig. 1). Among them, 139 are Ceg14 orthologs paired with AnkJ orthologs. However, not all Ceg14 homologs associate with a cognate metaeffector. Although structurally analogous ankyrin-repeat proteins—such as RavG (Lpg0210) and MavK (Lpg2525)—have been reported, no experimental evidence yet supports their functional pairing with LegK4/Lpg0209 or with MavL/LnaB, respectively17,49. Based on structural features of the C-terminal α-helix (α23 in Ceg14) within the S-HxxxE-containing domain, this protein family falls into two subtypes: type Ⅰ and type Ⅱ (Fig. 4). In type Ⅰ proteins, a central disordered loop interrupts this helix, impairing actin binding and recognition. Consequently, proteins like LegK4 and Lpg0209 lack actin-binding activity and fail to hydrolyze ATP or AMPylate PR-Ub. In contrast, type Ⅱ proteins possess an uninterrupted α-helix. Its solvent-exposed segment mediates actin binding via complementary hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions. CHVC exemplifies this subtype, displaying strong actin binding and ATP hydrolysis activity. Notably, this actin-binding mechanism is evolutionarily conserved across Ceg14 orthologs (Supplementary Fig. 12). Moreover, structural analyses reveal substantial heterogeneity in domain architecture and metaeffector distribution among S-HxxxE-family proteins. Many members contain additional, functionally uncharacterized domains, N-terminal, C-terminal, or both, alongside the core S-HxxxE domain. For instance, the CHVS of Vibrio splendidus constitutes only a single domain within the canonical MARTX toxin. Collectively, these findings highlight that the S-HxxxE family is more widespread, and likely more functionally diverse and mechanistically complex, than previously appreciated.

Although both Ceg14 and LnaB possess structurally analogous catalytic domains, utilize ATP as a ligand, and require actin as a cofactor, Ceg14 neither recognizes PR-Ub nor catalyzes its conversion to ADPR-Ub (Supplementary Fig. 20a). Structural comparison of the activated enzymes reveals that the NTD of Ceg14 spatially overlaps with the N-terminus of PR-Ub in the LnaB-actin-PR-Ub complex (Supplementary Fig. 20b). In addition, Ceg14 features an elongated lid region relative to LnaB (Ceg14: ~100 amino acids; LnaB: ~70 amino acids), which undergoes substantial conformational reorganization upon actin binding to form the enzyme’s active pocket. Within the Ceg14 active site, residues S527 and E575 critically stabilize the catalytic histidine H571 via hydrogen-bonding interactions (Fig. 3). This stabilization mechanism closely mirrors that observed during the ADP-to-AMP hydrolysis step in LnaB35. However, the regulatory mechanisms governing ATP access differ markedly between the two enzymes: In LnaB, binding of PR-Ub, or its physiological substrate, Src kinase, induces a conformational rearrangement that repositions M217 and F256, thereby opening the ATP-binding channel and enhancing ATPase activity. In contrast, although the structurally homologous residues M476 and Y522 in Ceg14 undergo positional adjustments within the Ceg14-actin complex, ATP binding to Ceg14 does not require an analogous channel-opening event (Supplementary Fig. 20c). Collectively, these structural distinctions provide a mechanistic explanation for why PR-Ub fails to activate Ceg14, neither by facilitating ATP access nor by serving as an AMPylation substrate.

AnkJ does not inhibit the ATPase activity of Ceg14 through competitive inhibition; rather, it regulates the conformational changes of Ceg14 allosterically. Thus, the imperative for precise conformational shifts, contingent upon both the interacting partner and the protein, may elucidate why members of the S-HxxxE family, notwithstanding their conserved catalytic architecture, can engage in an array of diverse ATP hydrolysis reactions. In essence, catalytic efficacy may hinge on the association with other protein partners to elicit a distinctive conformational alteration. In conclusion, the inhibitory mechanism of AnkJ on Ceg14 represents a distinct form that diverges from previously documented mechanisms (Fig. 5). These traditional mechanisms typically exert their effects through direct binding, which leads to spatial obstruction or conceals the active site. Such interactions are frequently observed among effector-metaeffectors and are similarly manifested in antitoxins within toxin-antitoxin (TA) systems, as well as immune proteins within effector-immune (E-I) pairs50–52.

Overall, by integrating Cryo-EM, AF3, MD simulations, molecular docking, and detailed biochemical validation, we have elucidated the structural determinants and a mechanism through which host actin activates Ceg14 to hydrolyze ATP, and which is further allosterically regulated by AnkJ. The mechanism proceeds as follows: Host-cell actin first binds to Ceg14 as an allosteric activator, triggering rotation of the NTD. This conformational change not only drives structural remodeling of the Lid region but also stabilizes the Lid through specific intermolecular interactions. Actin binding additionally triggers a dynamic wobbling motion in the NTD, facilitating cyclical opening and closing of the active-site pocket formed at the interface between the NTD and the CD of Ceg14. Efficient ATP hydrolysis occurs only when this pocket is fully open, enabling the catalytic residue H571 to adopt its optimal orientation for catalysis. In contrast, AnkJ binding suppresses the NTD wobbling trapping the active-site pocket in an intermediate, semi-closed conformation and thereby inhibiting ATP hydrolysis by Ceg14 (Fig. 6).

Schematic model of actin and AnkJ regulation of ATP hydrolysis by Ceg14.

Residues S527, H571 and E575 in the S-HxxxE motif of Ceg14 are highlighted in red. Host cell actin initially binds to Ceg14 as an activator, triggering a conformational change in the NTD. This change stabilizes and reorganizes the Lid region. The wobbling motion of the NTD enables transitions among three states of the active pocket between the NTD and CD of Ceg14: open, intermediate, and closed. Only in the open conformation does the catalytic residue H571 optimally position the α-phosphate bond for efficient ATP hydrolysis to AMP and PPi. In contrast, AnkJ interacts with the distal side of the enzymatic pocket of Ceg14, restricting this NTD wobbling motion and locking the pocket in an intermediate state, thus inhibiting any hydrolytic activity of Ceg14.

Methods

Plasmid constructions, bacterial strains, and media

For molecular cloning, Escherichia coli DH5α was used, while strain ER2566 was employed for recombinant protein expression. E. coli strains were cultured in LB medium at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm. Antibiotic concentrations for bacterial culture were 100 μg/mL ampicillin and 30 μg/mL kanamycin. All strains were stored at −80 °C in the presence of 50% (v/v) glycerol as a cryoprotectant. All constructs were verified by Sanger sequencing. Primer sequences are provided in Source Data file.

HEK293T cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. HEK293T cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Cat. No. CRL-1573).

Recombinant protein expression and purification

To purify Ceg14 and AnkJ proteins, full-length coding sequences of Ceg14 and AnkJ were amplified from the Legionella pneumophila genome and subcloned into the pMCSG7 vector harboring an N-terminal 6×His tag. Additionally, the Ceg14 fragment encoding amino acid residues 63–649 was cloned into both pMCSG7 (N-terminal 6×His tag) and pMCSG19 (N-terminal MBP tag). The resulting constructs were transformed into Escherichia coli ER2566 cells. Recombinant strains were grown in LB medium at 37 °C until the optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀) reached 0.8-1.0, followed by protein overexpression induction with IPTG at 18 °C for 16 h.

Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 3470 × g for 15 min at 4 °C and resuspended in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, 3 mM β-mercaptoethanol). After sonication, cell lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 34,572 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. His-tagged proteins were purified using Ni²⁺-nitrilotriacetic acid (NTA) resin, while MBP-tagged proteins were isolated with amylose resin, followed by size-exclusion chromatography on a Superdex 200 10/300 GL column (GE Healthcare) equilibrated with 25 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5) and 150 mM NaCl. Homogeneous protein fractions were pooled, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for subsequent crystallization and activity assays. All plasmids generated in this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Antibodies

The following primary antibodies were used: anti-Flag (mouse monoclonal, Cat. No. 66008-4-Ig, Proteintech, diluted 1:5000 for WB and 1:200 for IP), anti-HA (rabbit polyclonal, Cat. No. 51064-2-AP, Proteintech, diluted 1:5000 for WB), anti-actin (mouse monoclonal, Cat. No. 66009-1-Ig, Proteintech, diluted 1:5000 for WB), and anti-ADPR (mouse monoclonal, MABE1016, Merck, diluted 1:1000 for WB). HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (anti-mouse IgG, Cat. No. SA00001-1; anti-rabbit IgG, Cat. No. SA00001-2, Proteintech) were used at 1:5000 dilution.

Actin pretreatment

The lyophilized rabbit muscle-derived G-actin powder (Sangon Biotech, Cat. No. A001041) was placed on ice, and prechilled G-actin buffer (5 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0; 0.2 mM CaCl₂; 0.2 mM ATP; 0.5 mM DTT) was added slowly. The mixture was gently stirred for 30–60 min until complete dissolution was achieved. The resulting actin solution was centrifuged at 17,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22-μm membrane filter and used immediately or aliquoted and stored at −80 °C.

Cryo-EM sample preparation and data collection

The pretreated rabbit muscle-derived G-actin protein, stored at −80°C, was thawed on ice, centrifuged at 17,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C, and assessed for monodispersity by AUC prior to use in preparing the Ceg14-actin binary complex and the Ceg14-actin-AnkJ ternary complex. Aliquots (3 µL) of 1.5 mg/mL of either purified Ceg14-actin complex, Ceg14-AnkJ complex or Ceg14-actin-AnkJ complex were loaded onto glow-discharged (80 s at 20 mA) holey carbon Quantifoil grids (Au R2/1, 300 mesh, Quantifoil Micro Tools) using a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 100% humidity and 4 °C. Data were acquired using the EPU software on Titan Krios G4 transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operated at 300 kV. To mitigate preferred orientation, Ceg14-actin, Ceg14-AnkJ and Ceg14-actin-AnkJ datasets were collected in the presence of 0.02% (w/v) n-octyl-β-D-glucopyranoside (ODG) and with stage tilting (±30°). Images were recorded using Gatan K3 detector in a 48-frame movie mode at a nominal 130,000× magnification with a super-resolution pixel size of 0.325 Å, and the total electron dose was set to 48 e−·Å−2, with an exposure time of 1.29 s.

Image processing and 3D reconstruction

Drift and beam-induced motion correction were performed with MotionCor253 to produce a micrograph from each movie. Contrast transfer function (CTF) fitting and phase-shift estimation were conducted with Gctf54. Micrographs with astigmatism, obvious drift, or contamination were discarded before reconstruction. The following reconstruction procedures were performed using Cryosparc V355. In brief, particles were automatically picked using the Blob picker or Topaz picker. Several rounds of reference-free 2D classifications were performed and the selected good particles were then subjected to ab initio reconstruction, heterogeneous refinement, final non-uniform refinement and DeepEMhancer56. The resolutions of all density maps were determined by the gold-standard Fourier shell correlation curve, with a cutoff of 0.1434. Local resolutions of maps were estimated with ResMap57.

Atomic model building, refinement, and 3D visualization

The initial models of the Ceg14-actin, Ceg14-AnkJ and Ceg14-actin-AnkJ were acquired from Alphafold358. AlphaFold3 Access date: 2025-04-23, UniProt identifiers: Ceg14 (A5IEG9), actin (P60706), AnkJ (A5IEA0). Detailed information can be found in Supplementary Fig. 21 and 22. We initially fitted the models into the corresponding final Cryo-EM maps using Chimera59. ATP was then fitted into the density using COOT60. The ligand-bound model further corrected and adjusted models manually by real-space refinement in COOT. The resulting models were then refined with phenix.real_space_refine in PHENIX61. These operations were executed iteratively until the problematic regions, Ramachandran outliers, and poor rotamers were either eliminated or moved to favored regions. The final atomic models were validated with Molprobity62,63. All figures were generated with ChimeraX64. The detailed information of the Cryo-EM data collection, refinement and validation statistics was listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Transfection and co-immunoprecipitation

HEK293T cells at 80% confluence were transfected with pCMV-3×FLAG-Ceg14 and pCMV-3×HA-AnkJ plasmids using liposomal transfection reagent(YEASEN). Following 24-hour incubation, cells were harvested and lysed on ice for 10 min using RIPA Lysis Buffer(YEASEN). For immunoprecipitation, cell lysates were incubated with Anti-DYKDDDDK IP Resin (GenScript) at 4 °C for 6 h. Post-incubation, the resin was washed three times with pre-chilled lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5; 150 mM NaCl; 0.02% Triton X-100; 0.2 mM EDTA) and target proteins were eluted using DYKDDDDK Peptide (GenScript). Eluates were mixed with 4 × SDS-PAGE loading buffer, boiled at 95 °C for 10 min, separated by 10% SDS-PAGE, and subjected to immunoblotting with specific antibodies.

Protein pulldown assays

The interaction between Ceg14 and AnkJ was examined using a His pull-down assay. Recombinant His₆-Ceg14 (60 μg) and GST-AnkJ C-terminal truncation mutants (60 μg each) were incubated in pre-chilled binding buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole) at 4 °C for 1 h with gentle rotation to facilitate protein-protein interaction. Equal volumes of Ni²⁺-NTA resin (pre-equilibrated with binding buffer) were added to each sample, followed by further incubation at 4 °C for 1 h on a rotating shaker to allow specific binding of His-tagged proteins to the resin.

After incubation, the resin was washed four times with cold binding buffer to remove unbound proteins, with each wash involving gentle resuspension and centrifugation at 1000 × g for 1 min at 4 °C. Bound proteins were eluted using elution buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 300 mM imidazole), and eluates were collected by centrifugation. Equal volumes of 4× SDS-PAGE loading buffer were added to the eluted samples, which were then boiled at 95 °C for 5 min to denature proteins. Denatured samples were separated by SDS-PAGE, and protein bands were visualized by Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining to analyze the interaction between Ceg14 and AnkJ truncation variants.

Analytical ultracentrifugation (AUC)

Analytical ultracentrifugation (AUC) sedimentation velocity (SV) experiments were conducted using a ProteomeLab XL-I analytical ultracentrifuge (Beckman-Colter, Brea, CA, USA) equipped with an An60 Ti analytical rotor and 12-mm charcoal-epoxy double-sector centerpieces. The buffer system consisted of 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) and 150 mM NaCl. Experiments were performed at a constant temperature of 10.0 °C and a rotational speed of 201,600 × g, with absorbance detection at 280 nm used to monitor concentration gradients over time.

Raw AUC-SV data were analyzed using the continuous C(s) distribution model in SEDFIT v16.0165, which accounts for sedimentation heterogeneity. Partial specific volume (v) and extinction coefficients (ϵ) of the protein were calculated by SEDNTERP v3.0266 based on its amino acid sequence, while buffer density (ρ) and viscosity (η) were derived from the buffer composition at 10.0 °C. These parameters were used to normalize experimental sedimentation coefficients to standard conditions (s20,w) and quantify protein concentrations.

ATP hydrolysis and HPLC analysis

To explore the key interaction domains between Ceg14 and actin, 2 μg of recombinant Ceg14-FL (full-length) or its truncation mutants were incubated with 1 μg of G-actin (Sangon Biotech, A001041) in a 50 μL reaction mixture containing 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0, 25 °C), 5 mM MgCl₂, 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, and 1 mM ATP. The reaction was initiated by temperature equilibration at 37 °C for 1 h, then terminated by the addition of 50 μL chloroform (1:1 v/v), followed by vigorous vortexing and centrifugation at 17,000 × g for 10 min at room temperature to separate phases.

The aqueous phase was subjected to HPLC using a SHIMADZU LC-20AD system equipped with a C18 reversed-phase column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm particle size). The mobile phase consisted of 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 8.0, containing 50 mM KH₂PO₄, 50 mM K₂HPO₄), 10 mM tetrapropylammonium bromide (TPAB), and 20% (v/v) acetonitrile, delivered at a constant flow rate of 1 mL/min for 25 min. Samples (10 μL) were injected, and nucleotides (AMP, ADP, ATP) were detected by absorbance at 254 nm.

For assessing the inhibitory effects of AnkJ truncation variants on Ceg14-actin-mediated ATP hydrolysis, the same reaction protocol was adopted with the additional inclusion of 1 μg of AnkJ truncation mutants during the initial incubation step. All reactions were performed in triplicate, and data were normalized to control samples without truncation constructs.

Cell ATP content assay

ATP standard curves were generated using the ATP Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Cat. No. S0027) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. HEK293T cells were cultured in 24-well plates until reaching 90% confluence. Following transient transfection (as described above), cells were harvested 24 h post-transfection. Culture medium was aspirated, and cells were lysed with 100 μL of pre-chilled ATP assay lysis buffer (provided in the kit) on ice for 5 min. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 17,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and 20 μL of the supernatant was transferred to a white 96-well plate containing 100 μL of ATP detection reagent. After gentle mixing, luminescence was measured immediately using a microplate luminometer. All experiments were performed in triplicate across three independent biological replicates, with data normalized to total protein content (measured by BCA assay) to account for cell number variations.

Biochemical assay of AMPylation

Reactions (40 μL) were assembled containing 1 μM recombinant Ceg14 or LnaB, 1 μM actin (Sangon Biotech; Cat. No. A001041), and 5 μM PR-Ub in buffer composed of 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0 at 25 °C), 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl₂, and 1 mM ATP. Samples were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. After boiling at 95 °C for 10 min, the samples were separated by SDS-PAGE and stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue. The generated ADPR-Ub was detected by immunoblotting using a specific anti-ADPR antibody (1:1,000; Merck, MABE1016).

Protein-ligand interaction analysis

To determine the ATP-binding affinities of Ceg14S527A under different conformational states, spectral shift-based binding experiments were performed using the Monolith X system (NanoTemper Technologies). Ceg14S527A monomers were fluorescently labeled with RED-NHS second-generation dye using the MonolithTM Protein Labeling Kit (MO-L011, NanoTemper Technologies) following manufacturer’s protocol. For complex formation, 100 nM labeled Ceg14S527A was pre-incubated with either actin or actin/AnkJ combination at 4 °C for 1 h. Serial dilutions of ATP were prepared in reaction buffer containing 25 mM Tris (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, and 0.05% Tween-20. Equal volumes of the protein/complex solution were mixed with ATP diluent and loaded onto Monolith Premium capillaries (MO-K025). Fluorescence emission ratios (670:650 nm) were recorded at 590 nm excitation using the Monolith X instrument. Binding isotherms were generated from triplicate measurements and Kd values were calculated through nonlinear regression analysis using Monolith Affinity Analysis software.

For Ceg14-actin interaction studies, MicroScale Thermophoresis (MST) was employed. Actin was labeled with RED-NHS dye using the same labeling kit. Serial dilutions of unlabeled Ceg14S527A were prepared in identical reaction buffer. Labeled actin was mixed with Ceg14S527A dilutions at 1:1 ratio and loaded into standard-treated capillaries. Thermophoretic movements were monitored at 25 °C with 40% MST power. Data from three independent experiments were analyzed using Monolith. Affinity Analysis software v2.3. All binding curves and statistical analyses were generated using GraphPad Prism 9.0. The relative binding affinity quantified in this study was listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Model construction

Initial coordinates for the Ceg14-actin, Ceg14-AnkJ and Ceg14-actin-AnkJ complexes were built with the SWISS-MODEL server67, using our Cryo-EM structures as templates, while the initial structure of standalone Ceg14 was obtained from the AF3 server. The H + + server68 was employed to predict protonation states at pH 8.0. Parameters for ATP in complex with actin were taken from Carlson et al.69. Force-field assignments were: ff19SB for proteins70; GAFF2 for ATP71; OPC for water72; and Li/Merz ion parameters for counterions73. Each system was solvated in an orthorhombic water box with 10 Å padding. Counterions (Na⁺, Mg²⁺, and Cl⁻) were added to neutralize the net charge and to approximate physiological conditions of ~100 mM NaCl and 5 mM MgCl2. Periodic boundary conditions were used. The detailed information of simulated systems was listed in Supplementary Table 3.

MD simulations

MD simulations were performed by AMBER24 software74. The Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method was used to calculate long-range electrostatic interactions75. Short-range non-bonded interactions, such as Lennard-Jones forces and the short-range component of the PME calculation, were subject to a cutoff distance of 10 Å. Each system was relaxed through a three-stage energy minimization. First, 10,000 steps of steepest descent followed by 10,000 steps of conjugate-gradient minimization while imposing a 10 kcal mol⁻¹ Å⁻² positional restraint on all solute atoms. Secondly, the same sequence was repeated but the restraint was limited to heavy atoms. Third, the identical minimization protocol was executed without restraints. Subsequently, the system was heated from 100 K to 310 K over 0.5 ns using Langevin dynamics76, with a 10 kcal mol⁻¹ Å⁻² restraint on heavy atoms. This was followed by a further 0.5 ns at 310 K without restraints. An integration time step of 1 fs was employed during these heating processes. Finally, three independent NPT trajectories of 1 μs each were conducted per system, initialized with distinct velocity seeds. Temperature (310 K) and pressure (1 atm) were controlled by a Langevin thermostat76 and a Monte-Carlo barostat77, respectively, using a 2-fs time step. The MD trajectories were output at a frequency of 10 ps.

PCA was performed using Cα atoms78. The center of mass was calculated using the Cα atoms of the NTD (residue ID 79-220) and a helix of the CD (residue ID 400-412). The representative structures (C1-C3) were extracted from our final 0.9-μs MD simulations by K-means79 clustering on Cα-RMSD. All of these property calculations were done by CPPTRAJ80.

Molecular docking

Ensemble docking was carried out with HADDOCK 2.481. The receptor structures (C1-C3) were taken from our final 0.9-μs MD simulations by K-means clustering on Cα-RMSD. Residues N412, R443, S527, and H571 were designated as active sites for docking. Prior biochemical assays showed that alanine substitution at each of these positions abolishes Ceg14 activity33.
