Measures of predictive accuracy, miscalibration and discrimination
Łukasz Delong, Mario Wüthrich

Abstract
We study the evaluation of real-valued point predictors under the decision-theoretic framework of mean-consistent loss functions given by the Bregman divergences. We first derive a new version of Murphy’s decomposition of the expected loss which does not directly include the response itself but only its predictors. We then relate the miscalibration and the discrimination component of the Murphy’s decomposition to Lorenz-curve-based accuracy measures that are widely used in practice. Besides the usual area between the concentration and Lorenz curves, ABC, we introduce a mean-squared version ABC 2 that mitigates some of the weaknesses of the original ABC in identifying mean-calibration. More importantly, both ABC and ABC 2 are shown to rely on predictor-dependent weights, so they fail to align with the class of mean-consistent scoring functions. In the same spirit, we derive a similar result for the widely used Gini score. These results indicate that ABC, ABC 2 and Gini scores may lead to dishonest evaluation of point predictions when used for model selection; this gives support to use mean-consistent loss functions as well as the miscalibration and the discrimination measure from the Murphy’s decomposition of the expected loss for model evaluation. Finally, we study forecast dominance when Lorenz curves intersect. We show that Lorenz and Murphy’s curves have the same number of crossings and, in the one-crossing case, we establish weaker dominance criteria for subclasses of Bregman divergences through third-degree stochastic dominance. Keywords: Mean-consistent loss function, Bregman divergence, miscalibration, discrimination, Gini index, Murphy’s decomposition, Murphy’s curve, Lorenz curve, concentration curve, forecast dominance.



1
Introduction

In his pioneering work,

Gneiting 2011
initiated a discussion about the specific choice of the loss function and its role in evaluating point predictions. Assume there are
K
competing point predictions
(x^{(k)}_{1},\ldots,x^{(k)}_{n})_{k=1}^{K}
for forecasting the observations
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}
. Select a loss function
(y,x)\mapsto L(y,x)
that measures the discrepancy between the prediction
x
and the outcome
y
of the response. To evaluate the point predictions, consider the following performance criterion

\displaystyle\bar{S}_{k}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L\big(y_{i},x^{(k)}_{i}\big),\qquad\text{ for $k=1,\ldots,K$,}

(1.1)

and compare
\bar{S}_{1},\ldots,\bar{S}_{K}
to select the best prediction, i.e., the one with the smallest average loss (
1.1
). The key question is what loss function
L
should be selected and which specific choices have theoretical support.
Gneiting 2011
develops a decision-theoretic approach to the evaluation of point predictions and concludes that one should always choose so-called
consistent loss functions
. The idea promoted by
Gneiting 2011
is that one should first decide on a functional of the predictive distribution in which one is interested in – such as the mean or a quantile – and then select a loss function
L
that is
consistent
with this functional choice. It turns out that the class of loss functions consistent for a certain functional is identical to the class of loss functions under which the functional is the optimal point forecast. Hence, if a forecaster chooses the best prediction by minimizing the empirical score (
1.1
), they have to choose a loss function under which the functional of the predictive distribution under consideration is the optimal point forecast. Other choices of loss functions may lead to misguided conclusions. The notion of consistent loss functions has been later extended by
Gneiting–Raftery 2007
to
proper scoring rules
.

In this paper we are interested in the estimation of mean values of observations. Hence, by evaluating point predictions we always think of evaluating mean estimates. The class of loss functions consistent for mean values has been characterized by
Savage 1971
,
Gneiting 2011
and
Ehm et al. 2016
, and it includes so-called
Bregman divergences
; in fact, under mild conditions this is an if-and-only-if statement. The most popular examples include the mean squared loss, Kullback–Leibler divergences and loss functions related to deviance functions in the exponential dispersion family.

The main criterion for evaluating point predictions is prediction accuracy characterized by closeness between predictions and observations measured with a consistent loss function. However, good predictions should also have other desirable properties.
Murphy 1973
and
Murphy–Winkler 1987
investigate decompositions of loss functions.
Pohle 2020
proves that any consistent loss function can be decomposed into measures of
miscalibration
and
discrimination
. This decomposition, called Murphy’s decomposition, allows one to look deeper into predictive accuracy of predictors. Murphy’s decomposition can identify strengths and weaknesses of predictions and guide forecasters to improve their predictive models. The measures of miscalibration and discrimination from Murphy’s decomposition are in agreement with the decision-theoretic approach to the evaluation of point predictions from
Gneiting 2011
since they are related to consistent loss functions, see
Gneiting–Resin 2023
.

The first contribution of this paper is to derive a new version of Murphy’s decomposition. In this new version, the measures of miscalibration and discrimination only depend on the predictor, and not on the responses. The main benefit is that this provides one with a more intuitive interpretation of theses measures. One can now directly measure, with a consistent loss function, how close the predictor is to its calibrated version (the miscalibration statistics) and how far the predictor is from a constant prediction (the discrimination statistics), and this does not involve the responses which, in particular, makes it robust towards different tail properties of the responses.

The loss functions consistent for mean values can be decomposed to get the measures of miscalibration and discrimination of mean estimates. At the same time, there are other measures of miscalibration and discrimination which have gained popularity in practical applications. For actuarial applications,
Denuit et al. 2019
,
Denuit et al. 2020
,
Denuit–Trufin 2021
introduced and promoted the area between the Lorenz and the concentration curve (the so-called ABC statistics) to measure the miscalibration of predictions. Recently,
Denuit–Trufin 2025
investigate properties and failures of this measure. Yet, we believe that a deeper investigation of the ABC measure is still missing in the literature. In particular,
Denuit et al. 2019
remark that there is a connection between the ABC and the miscalibration statistics linked with consistent loss functions. Moreover, the relation between the ABC measure and the miscalibration statistics from Murphy’s decomposition is not fully understood yet. The second contribution of this paper is to develop this relation by finding a new representation of the ABC measure. These new results presented in this paper allow us to assess if the ABC measure fits the framework of the decision-theoretic approach to the evaluation of point predictions from
Gneiting 2011
. In addition to the ABC measure, we also introduce a new miscalibration statistics, the area between the Lorenz and the concentration curve measured in a mean squared sense, and study its properties. We also prove a new representation of this measure in the spirit of the new representation for the
{\rm ABC}
.

As far as measures of discrimination are concerned, the Gini index, introduced by
Gini 1912
, is the most popular statistics used in applications, also in actuarial applications.
Denuit et al. 2019
,
Denuit et al. 2020
and
Denuit–Trufin 2021
use the classical Gini index based on the Lorenz curve to evaluate predictors.
Brauer–Wüthrich 2025
investigate a machine learning version of the Gini index in an actuarial context where the treatment of case weights may pose some challenges.
Frees et al. 2011
and
Frees et al. 2013
promote a version of the Gini index based on a so-called ordered Lorenz curve. These papers mainly focus on the advantages of using the Gini index in evaluating the discriminatory power of predictors, however, the Gini index also faces serious disadvantages that should be emphasized. The Gini index has been well-studied in the statistical literature, see the main reference by
Yitzhaki–Schechtman 2013
. However, the link between the Gini index and the discrimination statistics from Murphy’s decomposition is not complete to the best of our knowledge.
Hand 2009
and
Hand–Anagnostopoulos 2023
have made a first step in this area of research and considered the misclassification error for binomial responses and distributions of misclassification costs. These authors conclude that the loss function, implied by the Gini index and the Area Under the Curve (AUC), which measures the closeness between predictions and a constant mean value, depends on the predictor. They conclude that: ”the AUC is equivalent to averaging the misclassification loss over a cost ratio distribution which depends on the score distributions. Since the score distributions depend on the classifier, this means that, when evaluating classifier performance, the AUC evaluates a classifier using a metric which depends on the classifier itself. That is, the AUC evaluates different classifiers using different metrics. It is in that sense that the AUC is an incoherent measure of classifier performance”. Henceforth, this statement indicates that the Gini index does not fit the decision-theoretic approach to the evaluation of point predictions from
Gneiting 2011
. Our third contribution is that we explore the ideas from
Hand 2009
and
Hand–Anagnostopoulos 2023
and we investigate real-valued predictors. We prove a new representation of the Gini index and relate the Gini index with the discrimination statistics from Murphy’s decomposition. With this representation we confirm the findings from
Hand 2009
and
Hand–Anagnostopoulos 2023
in a more general setting.

In both cases of the ABC statistics and the Gini index, we show that these measures of miscalibration and discrimination cannot be reconciled with the framework from
Gneiting 2011
. We believe that our results are of theoretical interest, but they also have practical implications. We characterize some special cases when the ranking of predictors based on the ABC measure/the Gini index and the miscalibration/the discrimination statistics from Murphy’s decomposition coincide. Yet, in most practical cases the measures would yield different rankings of predictors in terms of their miscalibration and discrimination statistics. Specific examples are provided in the paper. Given that the ABC measure and the Gini index are not the scores in the sense of (
1.1
) and
Gneiting 2011
, we favor the miscalibration and discrimination statistics from Murphy’s decomposition. As a by-product, we also show that minimizing expected weighted Bregman divergences with weights depending on the predictor may lead to dishonest point predictions, which complements the results from
Gneiting–Ranjan 2011
and
Taagart 2022
where weights depending on the responses are considered.

Finally, we investigate
forecast dominance
between predictors in the spirit of
Krüger–Ziegel 2021
and
Denuit–Trufin 2021
, as well as other equivalent formulations such as
Murphy’s dominance
with
Murphy’s curves
developed by
Ehm et al. 2016
. Forecast dominance refers to a situation where one predictor outperforms all other predictors in terms of the performance criterion (
1.1
) for all relevant loss functions. In the context of mean estimates,
Krüger–Ziegel 2021
show that a mean-calibrated
X_{1}
dominates a mean-calibrated
X_{2}
for all Bregman divergences if the Lorenz curves of the predictors do not cross. Non-intersecting Lorenz curves are very rare in practice. We generalize the result of
Dimitriadis et al. 2023
from classification to regression and we show that the number of crossing points of the Lorenz curves is equal to the number of crossing points of Murphy’s curves of two mean-calibrated predictors. The fourth contribution of this paper is to establish forecast dominance between two mean-calibrated predictors for a subclass of Bregman divergences in the case when the Lorenz curves of the predictors cross once. In particular, we extend the results from
Dardanoni–Lambert 1988
,
Muliere–Scarsini 1989
,
Zoli 2002
,
Levy 2006
which link
third-degree stochastic dominance
for distributions with ranking of distributions in terms of expected utility, and we use our extension in the context of forecast dominance. We also introduce a weaker form of local forecast dominance with Murphy’s curves between predictors with Lorenz curves that cross once, inspired by the literature on wealth inequalities, see
Aaberge 2009
and
Lando–Bertoli-Barsotti 2016
.

This paper is structured as follows. In Sections
2
,
3
and
5
, we recall and extend key concepts needed to evaluate point predictions in our setting. We prove a new version of Murphy’s decomposition in Section
4
. In Section
6
, we study miscalibrations measures, and in Section
7
, we investigate discrimination measures. Section
8
focuses on forecast dominance between predictors with Lorenz curves that cross once. Section
9
concludes. All proofs, except the examples used to prove the results, are presented in the appendix.

2
Bregman divergence, Bregman and Murphy’s dominance

We start by presenting the statistical set-up, as well as introducing the main notation and definitions.
Let
Y
denote a real-valued response variable with finite mean value. The goal is to predict
Y
by estimating its mean value using a real-valued predictor
X
. In a decision-theoretical setting, see, e.g., Section 3.1 in
Ehm et al. 2016
, we consider a random pair
(Y,X)
defined on a so-called point prediction space
\big(\Omega,\mathcal{A}_{0},{\mathbb{P}}\big)
, where
{\mathbb{P}}
describes the joint distribution of
(Y,X)
. The predictor
X
represents a point prediction which utilizes the information set (the forecaster’s basis)
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{A}_{0}
, hence,
X
is assumed to be
\mathcal{A}
-measurable. A pair
(Y,X)
is usually interpreted as a random observation from a test set. We denote the marginal distributions
Y\sim F_{Y}
and
X\sim F_{X}
. We assume:

(A)

X
is globally unbiased for
Y
, meaning that
{\mathbb{E}}[Y]={\mathbb{E}}[X]<\infty
;

(B)

The distributions
F_{X}
and
F_{Y}
are supported on
[0,\infty)
;

(C)

The distribution
F_{X}
is continuous, strictly increasing on
[0,\infty)
and
F_{X}(0)=0
.

Select a loss function
(y,x)\mapsto L(y,x)
that measures the discrepancy between a prediction value
x
of
X
and an outcome
y
of
Y
. The loss function
L
allows one to measure the accuracy of predictor
X
for predicting
Y
by the expected loss (subject to existence)

S_{L}(Y,X)={\mathbb{E}}\big[L(Y,X)\big],

(2.1)

also called the score. We assume that
L(y,x)\geq 0
and
L(y,x)=0
if
y=x
. Other technical conditions, not necessary for the presentation of our results, can be found in
Gneiting 2011
. The score (
1.1
) is the empirical version of the expected loss (
2.1
). In this paper we only consider loss functions consistent for mean estimation; see
Savage 1971
,
Gneiting 2011
,
Ehm et al. 2016
and
Gneiting–Resin 2023
. This choice of loss functions is justified by the following definition.

Definition 1
.

A loss function
L
is consistent for the estimation of the mean value of
Y
if

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\Big[L\big(Y,{\mathbb{E}}[Y|\mathcal{A}]\big)\Big|\mathcal{A}\Big]\leq{\mathbb{E}}\Big[L\big(Y,X\big)\Big|\mathcal{A}\Big],\qquad a.s.,

(2.2)

for all
\mathcal{A}
-measurable predictors
X
, supposed that the left-hand side of (
2.2
) is finite. The loss function
L
is strictly consistent for the estimation of the mean value of
Y
if it is consistent and if the equality in (
2.2
) holds if and only if
X={\mathbb{E}}[Y|\mathcal{A}]
, a.s.

Remark 1

(conditional mean estimation)
.

In Definition
1
we consider loss functions consistent for the estimation of the conditional mean value of
Y
, given
\mathcal{A}
. E.g., let
X=h(\mathbf{Z})
, with
h:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
denoting a measurable regression function,
\mathbf{Z}:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a vector of covariates, and the sub-
\sigma
-field
\mathcal{A}=\sigma(\mathbf{Z})
denotes the information set generated by the covariates
\mathbf{Z}
. Then, finding the optimal
{\cal A}
-measurable predictor
X
is equivalent in trying to find the optimal regression function
h
on
\mathbf{Z}
.
In the sequel, we do not explicitly state the regression problem and
the information set
\mathcal{A}
generated by
\mathbf{Z}
, but one should keep this in mind as the underlying motivation of the problem studied.

In view of Definition
1
, using a strictly consistent loss function
L
for mean estimation in the expected loss (
2.1
) allows us to score predictors
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
, and we give preference to the one with the lower expected loss (lower score). The minimizer of this expected loss gives us the true (conditional) mean value of
Y
. This turns mean estimation into a minimization problem. This is particularly useful in fitting mean values with statistical methods and machine learning methods, i.e., by empirically minimizing strictly consistent loss functions for mean estimation.

Following
Savage 1971
and
Gneiting 2011
, the consistent loss functions for mean estimation are given by Bregman divergences; under mild conditions this is an if-and-only-if statement; see
Gneiting 2011
. There are two different characterizations of Bregman divergences. First, Bregman divergences are characterized by the representation

\displaystyle L_{\phi}(y,x)=\phi(y)-\phi(x)-\phi^{\prime}(x)(y-x),

(2.3)

for a convex function
\phi
with (sub-)derivative
\phi^{\prime}
. For a strictly convex function
\phi
, we obtain a strictly consistent loss function for mean estimation, supposed that
{\mathbb{E}}[\phi(Y)]
is finite; see
Gneiting 2011
. Examples of strictly convex choices are given by deviance losses coming from the exponential dispersion family (EDF); see
Wüthrich–Merz 2023
. As a result of strict convexity of
\phi
, we have
L_{\phi}(x,y)=0
if and only if
x=y
. Furthermore, from representation (
2.3
) we can conclude that
t\mapsto L_{\phi}(x+t,x)
is convex in
t
with a minimum at
t=0
, and the larger the discrepancy between
y=x+t
and
x
, the larger the Bregman divergence between
y
and
x
. Second, Bregman divergences are characterized by the representation

\displaystyle L_{H}(y,x)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}L_{\theta}(y,x)\,dH(\theta),

(2.4)

for a non-negative measure
H
on
{\mathbb{R}}
, and
L_{\theta}
is an
elementary Bregman divergence
, for
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
, defined by

L_{\theta}(y,x)=(y-\theta)^{+}-(x-\theta)^{+}-\mathbf{1}\{x>\theta\}(y-x),

(2.5)

which itself is a Bregman divergence, we refer to
Ehm et al. 2016
. Since
X
and
Y
are supported on
[0,\infty)
, the measure
H
, which characterizes the Bregman divergence
L_{H}
, is chosen also supported on
[0,\infty)
. W.l.o.g. we can also assume that
H(0)=0
.

Furthermore, following
Ehm et al. 2016
, we introduce Murphy’s curve (Murphy’s diagram).

Definition 2
.

Choose
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Let
M_{\theta}(Y,X)={\mathbb{E}}[L_{\theta}(Y,X)]
denote the expected loss (
2.1
) w.r.t. the elementary Bregman divergence (
2.5
). The function

\theta\mapsto M_{\theta}(Y,X),\quad\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(2.6)

is called Murphy’s curve.

Using Fubini’s theorem, the score and Murphy’s curve satisfy the relation

\displaystyle S_{L_{H}}(Y,X)={\mathbb{E}}\big[L_{H}(Y,X)\big]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}M_{\theta}(Y,X)\,dH(\theta).

(2.7)

This motivates the following result which appears as Corollary 1b in
Ehm et al. 2016
; the proof is straightforward
from (
2.7
).

Proposition 1
.

For two predictors
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
, it holds that

\displaystyle M_{\theta}(Y,X_{1})\leq M_{\theta}(Y,X_{2})\quad\text{for all $\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}$}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad S_{L_{H}}(Y,X_{1})\leq S_{L_{H}}(Y,X_{2})\quad\text{for all $L_{H}$},

(2.8)

where for all
L_{H}
means for all Bregman divergences.

The property on the right-hand side of (
2.8
) is called
forecast dominance
; see
Krüger–Ziegel 2021
and
Ehm et al. 2016
; or
Bregman dominance
; see
Denuit et al. 2025
. The property on the left-hand side of (
2.8
) is called
Murphy’s dominance
in the sequel.

In general, the ranking of predictors based on the expected loss (
2.1
) and Definition
1
depends on the specific choice of the Bregman divergence
L_{\phi}
and
L_{H}
, respectively. Ideally, we would like to choose an optimal predictor
X
which is preferred under any Bregman divergence. The main value of Proposition
1
lies in the fact that Murphy’s dominance for the elementary Bregman divergence is easier to verify than the forecast dominance for any Bregman divergence.

Since
X
and
Y
are supported on
[0,\infty)
and the measure
H
which characterizes the Bregman divergence
L_{H}
is supported on
[0,\infty)
, we can consider
\theta\geq 0
in Definition
2
and Proposition
1
.

3
The notions of miscalibration and discrimination

The main criterion for evaluating mean estimates is prediction accuracy characterized by closeness between predictions and observations measured by the score (
2.1
) and its empirical counterpart. However, good predictions should also have other desirable properties. In particular, they should be mean-calibrated and have high discriminatory power. We use the following definitions.

Definition 3
.

(i)

The predictor
X
is mean-calibrated for response variable
Y
if
{\mathbb{E}}[Y|X]=X
, a.s.

(ii)

The predictor
X
does not discriminate response variable
Y
if
X={\mathbb{E}}[Y]
.

Calibration of
X
is measured by the closeness between
X
and
{\mathbb{E}}[Y|X]
– the smaller the discrepancy, the better. The discrimination power of
X
is measured by the closeness between
X
and
{\mathbb{E}}[Y]
– the larger the discrepancy, the better. One possibility is to use the score (
2.1
) and Bregman divergences to measure these discrepancies, this choice is justified in the next section. Other choices are also possible, and investigated in the sequel.

We complete the results from the previous section for mean-calibrated predictors. Under the assumption that
X
is mean-calibrated for
Y
, the expected loss w.r.t. the elementary Bregman divergence simplifies to

\displaystyle M_{\theta}(Y,X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\Big[{\mathbb{E}}\Big[(Y-\theta)^{+}-(X-\theta)^{+}-\mathbf{1}\{X>\theta\}(Y-X)\big|X\Big]\Big]

(3.1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[(Y-\theta)^{+}-(X-\theta)^{+}\big],\qquad\theta\in{\mathbb{R}},

where the last term on the first line vanishes by the mean-calibration property of
X
for
Y
. We use (
3.1
) in the sequel when we focus on mean-calibrated predictors.

Remark 2

(stop-loss order)
.

Assume we have two mean-calibrated predictors
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
supported on
[0,\infty)
. By (
3.1
) we have the following equivalence to Murphy’s dominance (under mean-calibration)

\displaystyle M_{\theta}(Y,X_{1})\leq M_{\theta}(Y,X_{2})\text{ for all $\theta\geq 0$}\penalty\ \Longleftrightarrow\penalty\ {\mathbb{E}}\big[(X_{2}-\theta)^{+}\big]\leq{\mathbb{E}}\big[(X_{1}-\theta)^{+}\big]\text{ for all $\theta\geq 0$}.

(3.2)

The right-hand side gives us the
stop-loss order

X_{2}\preceq_{sl}X_{1}
for the two predictors
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
. Furthermore, because
{\mathbb{E}}[X_{1}]={\mathbb{E}}[X_{2}]
and our predictors are positively supported, the right-hand side means that
X_{1}
dominates
X_{2}
in
convex order
, denoted by
X_{2}\preceq_{cx}X_{1}
; see
Müller 1996
.

4
Murphy’s decomposition

In this section we prove a new decomposition of expected losses w.r.t. consistent loss functions. The new decomposition of the score (
2.1
) is interesting in its own, and is used in the next sections to prove the main results concerning the discrimination and the miscalibrations statistics.

Referring to
Pohle 2020
, we consider the following decomposition of the
expected loss (
2.1
) w.r.t. a selected Bregman divergence, known as
Murphy’s decomposition
for consistent loss functions
L
for mean estimaton.
Murphy’s decomposition splits the expected loss
S_{L}
into the uncertainty (UNC), discrimination (DSC) and miscalibration (MCB) terms as follows

S_{L}(Y,X)={\rm UNC}_{L}(Y)-{\rm DSC}_{L}(Y,X)+{\rm MCB}_{L}(Y,X),

(4.1)

where the three terms are defined by

\displaystyle{\rm UNC}_{L}(Y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S_{L}(Y,{\mathbb{E}}[Y]),

\displaystyle{\rm DSC}_{L}(Y,X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S_{L}(Y,{\mathbb{E}}[Y])-S_{L}(Y,{\mathbb{E}}[Y|X])\penalty\ \geq\penalty\ 0,

\displaystyle{\rm MCB}_{L}(Y,X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S_{L}(Y,X)-S_{L}(Y,{\mathbb{E}}[Y|X])\penalty\ \geq\penalty\ 0.

The later two inequalities on the discrimination and the miscalibration terms follow since
L
is a consistent loss function for mean estimation, with equalities if
{\mathbb{E}}[Y|X]={\mathbb{E}}[Y]
and
{\mathbb{E}}[Y|X]=X
, respectively, see also Definition
3
. If the loss function
L
is strictly consistent for mean estimation, then
{\rm DSC}_{L}(Y,X)=0
iff
{\mathbb{E}}[Y|X]={\mathbb{E}}[Y]
, a.s., and
{\rm MCB}_{L}(Y,X)=0
iff
{\mathbb{E}}[Y|X]=X
, a.s.; see
Gneiting–Resin 2023
.

Murphy’s decomposition has been vividly used in meteorology for many years as a forecast evaluation method. It has recently also spread to other disciplines like psychology, statistics and actuarial science.
In the proposition below we prove a new version of Murphy’s decomposition, which is our first main result.

Theorem 1
.

Let
S_{L}
denote the expected loss (
2.1
) w.r.t. a Bregman divergence
L
and compute Murphy’s decomposition (
4.1
). The discrimination and the miscalibration statistics are given by

\displaystyle{\rm DSC}_{L}(Y,X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S_{L}({\mathbb{E}}[Y|X],{\mathbb{E}}[Y])\penalty\ \geq\penalty\ 0,

(4.2)

\displaystyle{\rm MCB}_{L}(Y,X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S_{L}({\mathbb{E}}[Y|X],X)\penalty\ \geq\penalty\ 0.

(4.3)

As a consequence, we also have the decomposition in terms of Murphy’s curves.

Corollary 1
.

Let
\theta\mapsto M_{\theta}(Y,X)
denote Murphy’s curve defined by (
2.7
) as the expected loss (
2.1
) w.r.t. the elementary Bregman divergence. We have the decomposition

\displaystyle M_{\theta}(Y,X)=M_{\theta}(Y,{\mathbb{E}}[Y])-M_{\theta}({\mathbb{E}}[Y|X],{\mathbb{E}}[Y])+M_{\theta}({\mathbb{E}}[Y|X],X),\qquad\text{for all $\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}$}.

(4.4)

For the square loss
L(y,x)=(y-x)^{2}
, our decomposition yields the well-known decomposition of the mean squared loss (MSE)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[(Y-X)^{2}\big]={\rm Var}[Y]-{\rm Var}\big[{\mathbb{E}}[Y|X]\big]+{\mathbb{E}}\big[\big({\mathbb{E}}[Y|X]-X\big)^{2}\big];

see
Ehm et al. 2016
, and Theorem
1
says that this decomposition generalizes to any Bregman divergence.

The first interest in this new formulation of the discrimination and the miscalibration terms is
that the explicit realization of the response
Y
does no longer appear in the formulas, i.e., all involved terms are (directly) measurable w.r.t. the predictor
X
.
This may suggest to replace the score (
2.1
) by a new score that measures the improvement of using the predictor
X
over the (deterministic) mean predictor
{\mathbb{E}}[Y]
, that is,

{\rm UNC}_{L}(Y,X)-S_{L}(Y,X)={\rm DSC}_{L}(Y,X)-{\rm MCB}_{L}(Y,X),

(4.5)

where the right-hand side only involves the
\sigma(X)
-measurable terms
(
4.2
)-(
4.3
). The bigger this score, the more associated is the predictor
X
and the response
Y
, hence, the more accurate the prediction will be. Thus, we aim to make the expected loss (
2.1
) small and the score (
4.5
) large, respectively, for receiving good predictors
X
for predicting response
Y
.
Interestingly, the specific law of
Y
does
not
appear on the right-hand side of (
4.5
) when using Theorem
1
, but only the conditional expectation
{\mathbb{E}}[Y|X]
. This may matter if we work with finite (empirical) samples, and it is related to the question of an optimal choice of the Bregman divergence
L
in case we do not want to evaluate all of them. Typically, such an optimal choice is done w.r.t. the properties of the response
Y
; see, e.g.,
Gourieroux et al. 1984
. However, if we validate predictors on the level of Theorem
1
, the responses
Y
do not enter the consideration at all but only their conditional expectations. This may give raise to consider different necessary properties of
L
for finite sample validation. This is an open research topic.

The second conclusion from Theorem
1
is that once we decide on a Bregman divergence
L
for the evaluation of predictions, we shall quantify the measures

\displaystyle S_{L}(Y,X),\quad S_{L}({\mathbb{E}}[Y|X],{\mathbb{E}}[Y]),\quad S_{L}({\mathbb{E}}[Y|X],X),

and rank the predictors based on the above values. In particular, the latter two
S_{L}({\mathbb{E}}[Y|X],{\mathbb{E}}[Y])
and
S_{L}({\mathbb{E}}[Y|X],X)
are intuitive measures of discrimination and miscalibration, respectively. We can use the score (
2.1
) with Bregman divergences to measure the discrepancies between
{\mathbb{E}}[Y|X]
and
{\mathbb{E}}[X]
, and
{\mathbb{E}}[Y|X]
and
X
, for discrimination and micalibration, and turn these discrepancies into rankings. A predictor
X
with a smaller
S_{L}({\mathbb{E}}[Y|X],{\mathbb{E}}[Y])
has a lower discriminatory power, in the sense that
X
is closer to
{\mathbb{E}}[Y]
, and a predictor
X
with a smaller
S_{L}({\mathbb{E}}[Y|X],X)
has a better calibration, in the sense that
{\mathbb{E}}[Y|X]
is closer to
X
. The discrimination and the miscalibration measures from Murphy’s decomposition have thus a clear interpretation.

5
Lorenz curve and concentration curve

We have introduced the miscalibration and the discrimination statistics from Murphy’s decomposition. We now introduce other measures of miscalibration and discrimination which have been considered in the literature. We study the area between the Lorenz and the concentration curve and the Gini index. The Gini index is already well-established in many fields of science, whereas the former one is gaining fast popularity in the field of actuarial science. The results presented in this section are used in the next two sections, where we relate the area between the Lorenz and the concentration curve to the miscalibration statistics and the Gini index to the discrimination statistics from Murphy’s decomposition.

For
p\in[0,1]
, the Lorenz curve and the concentration curve are given, respectively, by

\displaystyle LC_{p}(X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\big[X\mathbf{1}\{X\leq F_{X}^{-1}(p)\}\big]}{{\mathbb{E}}[X]},

\displaystyle CC_{p}(Y,X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\big[Y\mathbf{1}\{X\leq F_{X}^{-1}(p)\}\big]}{{\mathbb{E}}[Y]}=\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\big[{\mathbb{E}}[Y|X]\mathbf{1}\{X\leq F_{X}^{-1}(p)\}\big]}{{\mathbb{E}}[Y]}.

If
X
denotes an estimated insurance premium and
{\mathbb{E}}[Y|X]
is the true premium, then
LC_{p}(X)
represents the proportion of the total estimated
premium income corresponding to the sub-portfolio with the
100p\%
of contracts with the smallest estimated premium, and
CC_{p}(Y,X)
represents the proportion of the total true
premium income corresponding to the sub-portfolio with the
100p\%
of contracts with the smallest estimated premium. We refer to
Yitzhaki–Schechtman 2013
for mathematical results on the Lorenz and the concentration curve. These curves are investigated, e.g., by
Frees et al. 2011
,
Frees et al. 2013
,
Denuit et al. 2019
,
Denuit et al. 2020
,
Denuit–Trufin 2021
,
Denuit et al. 2024
,
Wüthrich 2025
,
Brauer–Wüthrich 2025
in the context of insurance pricing. These papers study two important measures for the evaluation of point predictions. The first one is the
area between the curves
(ABC) defined as

\displaystyle{\rm ABC}(Y,X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\big(CC_{p}(Y,X)-LC_{p}(X)\big)\ dp

(5.1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm Cov}(Y-X,F_{X}(X))}{{\mathbb{E}}[Y]}=\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\big[(Y-X)F_{X}(X)\big]}{{\mathbb{E}}[Y]}.

The ABC statistics was introduced by
Denuit et al. 2019
as the integrated difference between the two curves. The second equality is proved in
Denuit et al. 2019
, and the third equality follows from the second one by the global unbiasedness property
{\mathbb{E}}[X]={\mathbb{E}}[Y]
.
Denuit–Trufin 2021
notice that the Lorenz curve and the concentration curve coincide for mean-calibrated predictors, see their Property 3.1. Let us recall a short proof of that remark, using the tower property of conditional expectation and mean-calibration, for any
p\in[0,1]
we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[Y\mathbf{1}\{X\leq F_{X}^{-1}(p)\}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbb{E}}[Y\mathbf{1}\{X\leq F_{X}^{-1}(p)\}|X]]

(5.2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbb{E}}[Y|X]\mathbf{1}\{X\leq F_{X}^{-1}(p)\}]]\penalty\ =\penalty\ {\mathbb{E}}[X\mathbf{1}\{X\leq F_{X}^{-1}(p)\}].

This motivates to measure the miscalibration of
X
by the ABC statistics. Clearly, the
{\rm ABC}
is a different measure of calibration than
{\rm MCB}
. The second measure studied in the above mentioned papers is the Gini index, which measures the dispersion of the distribution of
X
based on the Lorenz curve

\displaystyle{\rm Gini}(X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-2\int_{0}^{1}LC_{p}(X)\,dp

(5.3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{{\mathbb{E}}[X]}{\rm Cov}\left(X,F_{X}(X)\right)=\frac{{\mathbb{E}}[|X-X^{\prime}|]}{2{\mathbb{E}}[X]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\mathbb{E}}[X]}\int_{0}^{\infty}F_{X}(z)(1-F_{X}(z))dz,

where
X^{\prime}
is an independent copy of
X
, see equations (2.1), (2.9), (2.15) and Chapter 2.1.4 in
Yitzhaki–Schechtman 2013
. The Gini index was introduced by
Gini 1912
and seems to be the most popular measure used for evaluation of point predictions in all disciplines. Let us emphazise that we consider the classical version of the Gini index, as later we use the Gini index only for mean-calibrated predictors. There is a machine learning version of the Gini index, tailored to the evaluation of point predictions. Both versions of the Gini index coincide, up to a constant depending only on the response, for mean-calibrated predictors, see
Wüthrich 2025
. It is also worth pointing out that one can find other definitions of the Gini index in the literature on predictive modelling, e.g.,
Frees et al. 2011
,
Frees et al. 2013
define the Gini index as twice the area between the so-called ordered Lorenz curve and the 45-degree line. It turns out that the Gini index in the classical version (
5.3
) may serve as an alternative measure of a discriminatory power of a mean-calibrated predictor. By
Denuit et al. 2019
, let
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
be non-negative with equal expected values, the mean-calibrated
X_{1}
is more discriminatory than the mean-calibrated
X_{2}
, if

\displaystyle X_{2}\preceq_{cx}X_{1}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad LC_{p}(X_{2})\geq LC_{p}(X_{1}),\quad\text{for all $p\in[0,1]$.}

(5.4)

In particular,
{\mathbb{E}}[X]\preceq_{cx}X
for any
X
. The Lorenz curve with the diagonal line arises for
X={\mathbb{E}}[Y]
, hence, we expect that the closer the Lorenz curve of
X
to the diagonal line, then the smaller the discrepancy between
X
and
{\mathbb{E}}[Y]
, and the lower the discriminatory power of
X
. Thus, (
5.4
) agrees with our definition of discriminatory power given in Section
3
. The relation between the Lorenz curves presented on the right-hand side of (
5.4
) is called
Lorenz dominance
. It is denoted by
X_{2}\preceq_{L}X_{1}
, which says that
X_{1}
Lorenz dominates
X_{2}
; see
Shaked–Shanthikumar 2007
for the equivalence between the convex order and the Lorenz order. The condition that
LC_{p}(X_{1})\leq LC_{p}(X_{2})
for all
p
implies that
{\rm Gini}(X_{1})\geq{\rm Gini}(X_{2})
, hence this motivates to measure a discriminatory power of
X
by the Gini index. Clearly, Gini is a different measure of discrimination than
{\rm DSC}
.

In addition to the ABC statistics (
5.1
), we also study another measure of miscalibration related to the ABC, but based on the difference between the Lorenz and the concentration curves in the mean squared sense

\displaystyle{\rm ABC}^{2}(Y,X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\big(CC_{p}(Y,X)-LC_{p}(X)\big)^{2}\ dp.

(5.5)

A sum of squared differences between the curves appears in
Wüthrich 2025
in the context of testing mean-calibration of discrete point predictions. To the best of our knowledge, the miscalibration statistics (
5.5
) is new and it has not been investigated in the form of (
5.5
) in the literature. In the next section we conclude that
{\rm ABC}^{2}
is more intuitive than
{\rm ABC}
for decision-making about mean-calibration of predictors. First, we prove another representation of
{\rm ABC}^{2}
similar to (
5.5
).

Proposition 2
.

Let (A)-(C) hold. We have the identities

\displaystyle{\rm ABC}^{2}(Y,X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\mathbb{E}}[Y]^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\big(Y-X\big)\big(Y^{\prime}-X^{\prime}\big)\Big(1-\max\big\{F_{X}(X),F_{X}(X^{\prime})\big\}\Big)\Big]

(5.6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\big(Y-X\big)\mathcal{Q}\Big(F_{X}(X)\Big)\Big]}{{\mathbb{E}}[Y]}=\frac{{\rm Cov}\big(Y-X,\mathcal{Q}(F_{X}(X))\big)}{{\mathbb{E}}[Y]},

where

\displaystyle z\in[0,1]\mapsto\mathcal{Q}\big(z\big)=\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\big(Y^{\prime}-X^{\prime}\big)\Big(1-\max\big\{z,F_{X}(X^{\prime})\big\}\Big)\Big]}{{\mathbb{E}}[Y^{\prime}]},

and
(Y^{\prime},X^{\prime})
is an independent copy of
(Y,X)
. Moreover,
z\mapsto\mathcal{Q}\big(z\big)
is continuous on
[0,1]
with
\mathcal{Q}(0)=-{\rm ABC}(Y,X)
and
\mathcal{Q}(1)=0
.

It is also interesting to investigate the derivative of
\mathcal{Q}
.

Lemma 1
.

Let (A)-(C) hold and let the distribution
F_{X}
be absolutely continuous. Consider the
{\rm ABC}^{2}
statistics (
5.5
) with representation (
5.6
). The function
x\mapsto\mathcal{Q}(x)
is continuously differentiable on
[0,1]
and we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}(x)=LC_{x}(X)-CC_{x}(Y,X),\qquad\textit{for all $x\in[0,1]$}.

From Lemma
1
we deduce that the function
x\mapsto\mathcal{Q}(x)
increases at points
x
at which
CC_{x}(Y,X)\leq LC_{x}(X)
, and decreases at points
x
at which
CC_{F_{x}}(Y,X)\geq LC_{F_{x}}(X)
. The ABC measure (
5.1
) is in insurance applications interpreted as the covariance between the profits and the ranks of the estimated premiums; see
Denuit et al. 2020
. The
{\rm ABC}^{2}
measure (
5.5
) can now be interpreted as the covariance between the profits and transformed ranks of the estimated premiums. The covariance, in particular its sign and absolute value, between the profits and strictly increasing or strictly decreasing transformations of the ranks of the estimated premiums can still have a business interpretation, but the transformation
\mathcal{Q}
of the ranks of the predicted values used for
{\rm ABC}^{2}
may be flat or not be monotonic in general.

6
The miscalibration statistics

We have introduced three miscalibration statistics (
4.3
), (
5.1
) and (
5.5
). The key question we answer in the section is how these measures are related to each other and which miscalibration measure should be preferred for ranking predictors based on their miscalibration.

By the tower property of conditional expectations, we modify (
5.1
) and (
5.5
) as follows

\displaystyle{\rm ABC}(Y,X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm Cov}\big({\mathbb{E}}[Y|X]-X,F_{X}(X)\big)}{{\mathbb{E}}[Y]}=\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\big[\big({\mathbb{E}}[Y|X]-X\big)F_{X}(X)\big]}{{\mathbb{E}}[Y]},

(6.1)

and

\displaystyle{\rm ABC}^{2}(Y,X)=\frac{{\rm Cov}\big({\mathbb{E}}[Y|X]-X,\mathcal{Q}(F_{X}(X))\big)}{{\mathbb{E}}[Y]}=\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\big({\mathbb{E}}[Y|X]-X\big)\mathcal{Q}\Big(F_{X}(X)\Big)\Big]}{{\mathbb{E}}[Y]}.

(6.2)

We can now see that the miscalibration measures (
6.1
)-(
6.2
) compare the conditional mean
{\mathbb{E}}[Y|X]
against
X
. The miscalibration statistics
{\rm MCB}
from Murphy’s decomposition also compares
{\mathbb{E}}[Y|X]
against
X
. Clearly, the loss functions implicitly and explicitly used in comparing
{\mathbb{E}}[Y|X]
against
X
are different for the three miscalibration measures (
4.3
), (
5.1
) and (
5.5
).
{\rm MCB}
explicitly uses a Bregman divergence. We recover the loss functions, and their properties, behind
{\rm ABC}
and
{\rm ABC^{2}}
in the sequel.

First, we observe that
{\rm ABC}
takes real values, whereas
{\rm ABC}^{2}
and
{\rm MCB}
take non-negative values. We expect that negative values of
{\rm ABC}
may be problematic in investigating mean-calibration of predictors. This disadvantage of
{\rm ABC}
has recently been noticed by
Denuit–Trufin 2025
.

Proposition 3
.

Let (A)-(C) hold. (i)
{\mathbb{E}}[Y|X]=X
implies that
{\rm ABC}(X,Y)={\rm ABC}^{2}(X,Y)=0
. (ii)
{\rm ABC}(X,Y)=0
does not imply that
{\mathbb{E}}[Y|X]=X
, it only implies that
{\mathbb{E}}\Big[{\mathbb{E}}[Y|X]F_{X}(X)\Big]={\mathbb{E}}\Big[XF_{X}(X)\Big]
. (iii)
{\rm ABC}^{2}(X,Y)=0
implies that
{\mathbb{E}}[Y|X]=X
.

Recall that
{\rm MCB}_{L}({\mathbb{E}}[Y|X],X)=0
is a necessary and a sufficient condition for mean-calibration of
X
if we choose a strictly consistent Bregman divergence
L
to measure the discrepancy between
{\mathbb{E}}[Y|X]
and
X
, see
Gneiting–Resin 2023
. Proposition
3
now establishes that having
ABC(Y,X)=0
is only a necessary condition for having mean-calibration of
X
, but it is not sufficient. Whereas
{\rm ABC}^{2}(X,Y)=0
gives both a necessary and a sufficient condition for having mean-calibration of
X
. Hence, the interpretation that a predictor
X
with a smaller miscalibration statistics has a better calibration, in the sense that
{\mathbb{E}}[Y|X]
is closer to
X
, can be used for
{\rm ABC^{2}}
, but it cannot be used for
{\rm ABC}
, see
Example 1
below.

The assertion (i) of Proposition
3
follows immediately from the definitions of the miscalibrations measures. The assertion (ii) is intuitively clear. We construct a counter-example below. The proof of (iii) is presented in the appendix.

Example 1
. Let
Z\sim{\rm Unif}(0,1)
. We set
{\mathbb{E}}[Y|Z]=Z
and
X=Z+q\cos(2\pi Z)
. We have
{\mathbb{E}}[Y]={\mathbb{E}}[X]=0.5
. Let
0<q<\frac{1}{2\pi}
.
X
is positive and increasing in
Z
. Hence,
{\mathbb{E}}[Y|X]=Z
and
F_{X}(X)=Z
. We can easily verify that
{\mathbb{E}}[\cos(2\pi Z)]=0
and, consequently,
{\rm ABC}(X,Y)=0
. In addition, we can also derive the Lorenz and the concentration curve

\displaystyle LC_{p}(X)=p^{2}+q\frac{\sin(2\pi p)}{\pi},\quad CC_{p}(Y,X)=p^{2},\qquad p\in[0,1].

We observe that the Lorenz and the concentration curve intersect (clearly this must hold if we have
{\rm ABC}(Y,X)=0
). In particular,
LC_{p}(X)>CC_{p}(Y,X)
for
p\in[0,0.5]
and
LC_{p}(X)<CC_{p}(Y,X)
for
p\in[0.5,1]
. We remark that
Denuit–Trufin 2025
construct a different example, but the qualitative conclusion is the same. ∎

Proposition 4
.

Let (A)-(C) hold. The ranking of predictors based on their calibration with the miscalibration statistics
{\rm ABC}
and

is in general different from the ranking with the miscalibration statistics
{\rm MCB}_{L}
, in the sense that for specific examples there exist Bregman divergences

different from the rankings with
{\rm ABC}
and
{\rm ABC}^{2}
.

We prove this proposition by giving counter-examples based on the mean squared loss.

Example 2
. We select the mean squared loss
{\rm MSE}(y,x)=(y-x)^{2}
.
Let
Z\sim{\rm Unif}(0,1)
. We set
{\mathbb{E}}[Y|Z]=Z
and choose two predictors:

and

X_{1}
and

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[Y|X_{1}]-X_{1}=(1-b)\big(Z-\frac{1}{2}\big),\quad{\mathbb{E}}[Y|X_{2}]-X_{2}=-q\cos(2\pi Z).

The proof is again straightforward. By (

The first conclusion from Proposition

for

One also has to investigate the distributions of predictors and require that their dispersion also increase as the deviation of a recalibrated predictor from the diagonal increases. We remark that the Lorenz curves of the two predictors considered in

We now present our second main statement. We show how the miscalibration statistics

Theorem 2

Let (A)-(C) hold. Let

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}M_{\theta}\big({\mathbb{E}}[Y|X],X\big)dH(\theta)

Remark 3

We observe that the miscalibration statistics
{\rm ABC}
and
{\rm ABC}^{2}
consist of two terms. The first term describes the discrepancy between the predictor

and its recalibrated version
{\mathbb{E}}[Y|X]
measured by a kind of miscalibration statistics from Murphy’s decomposition of the expected loss w.r.t.
L_{H}
. The second term has a similar interpretation but it uses an integral of the simple difference between the distributions of
X
and
{\mathbb{E}}[Y|X]
. As a result,
{\rm ABC}
and
{\rm ABC}^{2}
take a large value if
{\rm MCB}_{L_{H}}(Y,X)
is large and the integrated distance between the distributions
F_{{\mathbb{E}}[Y|X]}(x)
and
F_{X}(x)
is large. As far as the first term is concerned, a critical point is that the miscalibration statistics

{\rm MCB}_{L_{H}}(Y,X)=S_{L_{H}}({\mathbb{E}}[Y|X],X)
with
H
defined in Corollary
2
is strictly speaking not coming
from an expected loss w.r.t. a Bregman divergence in the sense of our definition (
2.1
) and Murphy’s decomposition. The choice of
H
in the corollary depends on the predictor
X
, and different predictors lead to different
H

{\rm MCB}_{L_{H_{1}}}(Y,X_{1})
and
{\rm MCB}_{L_{H_{2}}}(Y,X_{2})
cannot be benchmarked against each other as the evaluation is based on different loss functions
L_{H_{1}}
and
L_{H_{2}}
. The second term is a weighted integrated distance between the distributions of
X
and
{\mathbb{E}}[Y|X]
, which is again a measure of miscalibration, but the weight also depends on the predictor
X
. Hence, the same objection applies to the second term as for
{\rm MCB}_{L_{H}}(Y,X)
. These representations imply that if we compare two predictors
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
, the differences in
{\rm ABC}
and
{\rm ABC}^{2}
do not only reflect how close, or far,
X
is from
{\mathbb{E}}[Y|X]
, but also the changes in the measure
H
used for defining the expected loss
{\mathbb{E}}\big[\int_{0}^{\infty}L_{\theta}({\mathbb{E}}[Y|X],X)dH(\theta)\big]
and the changes in the weight
H
used for defining the integrated loss
\int_{0}^{\infty}(F_{{\mathbb{E}}[Y|X]}(\theta)-F_{X}(\theta))H(\theta)d\theta
, respectively. As discussed in the introduction, this critical point (and disadvantage) of statistical measures used in forecast evaluation has been noticed by
Hand 2009
and
Hand–Anagnostopoulos 2023
in the case of the Gini index and binomial responses. These authors conclude that it is highly inappropriate to validate predictors with loss functions which depend on predictors. Clearly, the decision-theoretic approach to the evaluation of point predictions by
Gneiting 2011
is not fulfilled with
{\rm ABC}
and
{\rm ABC^{2}}
since these measures cannot be represented as the expected losses w.r.t. a Bregman divergence. We conclude that we prefer the miscalibration statistics
{\rm MCB}
from Murphy’s decomposition over the miscalibration statistics
{\rm ABC}
and
{\rm ABC}^{2}
derived from the difference between the Lorenz and the concentration curve.

Finally, we notice that the miscalibration statistics
{\rm ABC}
and
{\rm ABC}^{2}
given by (
6.1
)-(
6.2
) are of the form

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\Big[L\big({\mathbb{E}}[Y|X],X\big)\mathcal{W}\big(F_{X}(X)\big)\Big],

(6.5)

with a linear loss function
L
and a weight
\mathcal{W}
which depends on
F_{X}
. From this perspective, this representation does not match the expected loss (
2.1
) w.r.t. a Bregman divergence. The loss
L
involved in (
6.5
) is not a Bregman divergence and the weight
\mathcal{W}
depends on the predictor itself. More generally, let us now consider an expected weighted loss given by

\displaystyle S_{L,\mathcal{W}}(Y,X)=\mathbb{E}\Big[L(Y,X)\mathcal{W}\big(F_{X}(X)\big)\Big],

(6.6)

where
L
is now a Bregman divergence and the weight
\mathcal{W}
still depends on
F_{X}
. This expected weighted loss still does not match the evaluation framework with the expected loss (
2.1
) in the sense of
Gneiting 2011
since the weighted loss

cannot be written as a function of a prediction value of
X
and an outcome of
Y
. It is also not a scoring rule in the sense of
Gneiting–Raftery 2007
since it cannot be written as a function of a predictive probability function of
X
and an outcome of
Y
. Yet, such a type of loss function arises when we deal with
{\rm ABC}
and
{\rm ABC^{2}}
. The next result shows that such a weighted scoring with weight depending on a predictor cannot be used for evaluation of predictors.

Proposition 6
.

Let
S_{L,\mathcal{W}}
be defined by (
6.6
) as the expected weighted loss w.r.t. a Bregman divergence between
Y
and
X
with a weight which depends on the distribution of
X
. Such a score is in general not consistent for the estimation of the mean value of
Y
, in the sense that it may happen that

\displaystyle S_{L,\mathcal{W}}\big(Y,{\mathbb{E}}[Y|\mathcal{A}]\big)>S_{L,\mathcal{W}}\big(Y,X\big),

for some
\mathcal{A}
-measurable predictor
X
.

We give an example.

Example 4
. Let us consider the mean squared loss
L(y,x)=(y-x)^{2}
. We consider predictors of the form
X=h(Z)
, where
Z
is a one-dimensional feature and
h
is a regression function. Hence,
\mathcal{A}=\sigma(Z)
, see Remark
1
. There exists the true regression function such that
{\mathbb{E}}[Y|Z]=h^{*}(Z)
. For any predictor
X
, we derive the decomposition

\displaystyle S_{L,\mathcal{W}}(Y,X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(Y-X\Big)^{2}F_{X}(X)\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[{\rm Var}\big[Y|Z\big]F_{X}(X)\Big]+\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big({\mathbb{E}}\big[Y|Z\big]-X\Big)^{2}F_{X}(X)\Big].

If
{\rm Var}\big[Y|Z\big]
does not depend on
Z
, then

\displaystyle S_{L,\mathcal{W}}(Y,X)=\frac{1}{2}{\rm Var}\big[Y\big]+\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big({\mathbb{E}}\big[Y|Z\big]-X\Big)^{2}F_{X}(X)\Big],

and we should choose
X={\mathbb{E}}\big[Y|Z\big]
. However, if
{\rm Var}\big[Y|Z\big]
depends on
Z
, then intuitively, we could sacrify the bias
\big({\mathbb{E}}\big[Y|Z\big]-X\big)^{2}
to reduce the variance
{\rm Var}\big[Y|Z\big]
in the area where
F_{X}(X)
is large – this is related to the variance-bias trade-off.

Assume that
Z\sim{\rm Unif}(0,1)
and choose a Tweedie’s distribution assumption
{\mathbb{E}}[Y|Z]=Z
and
{\rm Var}[Y|Z]=\phi Z^{p}
. We consider two predictors
X_{1}=Z
and
X_{2}=1-Z
. We calculate

\displaystyle S_{L,\mathcal{W}}(Y,X_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\phi Z^{p}Z\Big]=\phi\frac{1}{p+1},

\displaystyle S_{L,\mathcal{W}}(Y,X_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\phi Z^{p}(1-Z)\Big]+\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(2Z-1\Big)^{2}(1-Z)\Big]=\phi\Big(\frac{1}{p+1}-\frac{1}{p+2}\Big)+\frac{1}{2}.

We can obviously choose
\phi,p
so that

, e.g.,
\phi=4,p=1
. We get

and
S_{L,\mathcal{W}}(Y,X_{2})=\frac{7}{6}<\frac{4}{3}
. We prefer
X_{2}
over
X_{1}
even though
X_{1}
is the perfect predictor. This proves Proposition
6
.∎

Gneiting–Ranjan 2011
and
Taagart 2022
show that expected weighted Bregman divergences where the weight depends on the outcome of a response variable cannot be used for forecasts evaluation. Our Proposition
6
shows that minimizing expected weighted Bregman divergences with weights depending on the predictor may also lead to dishonest point predictions. Intuitively speaking, the weighting in (
6.6
) can be interpreted as a change of measure in the Radon–Nikodým sense, thus, the underlying probability measure
\mathbb{P}
is changed to an
X
-dependent measure
\mathbb{P}_{X}
. Firstly, this changes the underlying expected values of the considered random variables and, secondly, for different predictors
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
it results in different changed measures and, thus, non-comparability.

The discrimination statistics

Let us now investigate the two discrimination statistics (

) and (

). We show how these measures are related to each other and elaborate which discrimination measure should be preferred for ranking predictors based on their discrimination.

First, we note that the Gini index (

(Y,X)

is mean-calibrated for

.
Wüthrich 2025

.

Theorem 3
.

The predictor

maximizes the Gini index (

) on the class of
{\cal A}
-measurable and mean-calibrated predictors for

.

In this section we assume that

(D)

X
is mean-calibrated for

in the sense of Definition

If
X
is not mean-calibrated for

, we re-calibrate this predictor by

to get a mean-calibrated version of

. If
X
is mean-calibrated for

, then

and the expected loss

only through

by (

{\rm DSC}
only depend on

now. Intuitively, the
{\rm Gini}

should be related to some loss functions which compare

against
{\mathbb{E}}[Y]

explicitly uses a Bregman divergence for this comparison. We recover the loss function behind the Gini index in the sequel.

We start with the following (trivial) result.

Proposition 7
.

Let (D) hold. We have the equivalences

\displaystyle X={\mathbb{E}}[Y]\quad

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\quad{\rm Gini}(X)=0

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\quad{\rm DSC}_{L}(Y,X)=0\qquad\text{for all strictly consistent $L$},

where for all strictly consistent

We need strict consistency of

to get the equivalence between

with a smaller discrimination statistics has a lower discriminatory power, in the sense that

is closer to

The next proposition characterizes when the rankings of predictors based on their discriminatory power with the Gini index and the discrimination measure from Murphy’s decomposition coincide.

Proposition 8
.

Let (A), (B), (D) hold. We have

\displaystyle LC_{p}(X_{1})\leq LC_{p}(X_{2})\quad\text{for all $p\in[0,1]$}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\quad S_{L}(Y,X_{1})\leq S_{L}(Y,X_{2})\quad\text{for all $L$}

(7.1)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\quad{\rm DSC}_{L}(Y,X_{1})\geq{\rm DSC}_{L}(Y,X_{2})\quad\text{for all $L$},\qquad

Recall that the relation between the Lorenz curves presented on the left-hand side of (

implies that

, but the implication in the other direction is not generally true. The role of the Gini index in measuring the discrepancy between
X
and
{\mathbb{E}}[X]
and the discriminatory power of

must still be understood in the general case when the Lorenz curves of competing predictors intersect. In the sequel we derive a useful representation of the Gini index.

Corollary 3
.

Let (A), (B) hold. (i) Let the mean-calibrated predictors
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
be ordered in the sense of Lorenz dominance. The rankings of mean-calibrated predictors based on their discrimination with the discrimination statistics Gini and

coincide. (ii) In general, the ranking of mean-calibrated predictors based on their discrimination with the discrimination statistics Gini is different from the ranking with the discrimination statistics

, in the sense that there exist examples and Bregman divergences

that provide a ranking with

different from the ranking with Gini.

Assertion (i) follows immediately from Proposition

. Assertion (ii) also follows from Proposition

. Since the Lorenz dominance does not hold, we can find

and

such that

We shall remark that Proposition

and Corollary

are closely related to the fundamental result from
Atkinson 1969

) and assumptions (A), (B) and (D), for evaluating mean-calibrated predictors we use the utility

which is increasing and concave.

proved that the rankings of the distributions of

and

with equal expected values based on the criterion of the maximal expected utility

coincide for all increasing, concave and twice differentiable utilities

and

are ordered in the sense of Lorenz dominance.

Let us illustrate assertion (ii) from Corollary

.

Example 5
.
Let us consider the mean squared loss
L(y,x)=(y-x)^{2}
. In this case,
{\rm DSC}_{L}(Y,X)={\rm Var}[X]
. We show that the ranking of
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
with equal expected values may be different with the Gini index and the variance. This phenomenon has been reported in the literature, see, e.g.,
Atkinson 1969
and
Yitzhaki–Schechtman 2013
, yet specific examples are hard to find.
Bendel et al. 1989
suggest that the Gini index and the variance may rank distributions differently in the class of shifted log-normal distributions. We construct an example, which is absent in
Bendel et al. 1989
. Let
X=Z+a
where
Z\sim{\rm LogN}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
. Then, by
Bendel et al. 1989
we have the measures

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[X]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a+e^{\mu+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}},

\displaystyle{\rm Var}[X]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big({\mathbb{E}}[X]-a\big)^{2}\Big(e^{\sigma^{2}}-1\Big),

\displaystyle{\rm Gini}(X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathbb{E}}[X]-a}{{\mathbb{E}}[X]}\Big(2\Phi\Big({\sigma}/{\sqrt{2}}\Big)-1\Big).

We require that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[X_{1}]={\mathbb{E}}[X_{2}],\quad{\rm Var}[X_{1}]>{\rm Var}[X_{2}],\quad{\rm Gini}(X_{1})<{\rm Gini}(X_{2}).

Hence, the parameters must satisfy

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathbb{E}}[X]-a_{1}}{{\mathbb{E}}[X]-a_{2}}

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{e^{\sigma_{2}^{2}}-1}{e^{\sigma_{1}^{2}}-1}},

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathbb{E}}[X]-a_{1}}{{\mathbb{E}}[X]-a_{2}}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\frac{2\Phi\Big({\sigma_{2}}/{\sqrt{2}}\Big)-1}{2\Phi\Big({\sigma_{1}}/{\sqrt{2}}\Big)-1}.

All we have to do is to choose
\sigma_{1}
sufficiently large,
\sigma_{2}
sufficiently small and
a_{2}<a_{1}
in order to guarantee the inequalities above. At the same time we note that the ranking based on the Gini index and the variance always coincide for log-normal distributions without a shift (
a=0
).

The Lorenz curve for a shifted log-normal distribution is given by

\displaystyle LC_{p}(X)=\frac{ap+\big({\mathbb{E}}[X]-a\big)\Phi\Big(\Phi^{-1}(p)-\sigma\Big)}{{\mathbb{E}}[X]},\qquad p\in[0,1],

and one can show that
p\mapsto LC_{p}(X)
is strictly convex on
[0,1]
for any
a\geq 0
, by calculating the second derivative. Let
X_{a,\sigma^{2}}
denote the shifted log-normal distribution with parameters
(a,\sigma^{2})
. Under our choice of the parameters for the two distributions, we have

\displaystyle\Phi\Big(\Phi^{-1}(p)-\sigma_{1}\Big)<\Phi\Big(\Phi^{-1}(p)-\sigma_{2}\Big),\qquad p\in[0,1].

Hence,
LC_{p}(X_{0,\sigma_{1}^{2}})<LC_{p}(X_{0,\sigma_{2}^{2}})
for all
p\in[0,1]
. By shifting the log-normal distributions by
a
we move their Lorenz curves upward. Indeed, let

9
Conclusions

This paper has studied the evaluation of point predictions of a response, or point estimates of a response’s mean value, from a decision-theoretic perspective. Our starting point has been consistency of loss functions for mean estimation which coincides with the class of Bregman divergences. Within this framework, the predictive accuracy is naturally measured by the expected loss, while calibration and discrimination measures arise as structural properties of a predictor that should be analyzed jointly with the accuracy measure. The main message of the paper supports that Murphy’s decomposition of the expected loss provides the right principle for this task, and that several popular alternatives based on Lorenz and concentration curves, although informative and often appealing in practice, should be interpreted with caution when they are used for ranking predictors.

Our first main contribution is a new representation of Murphy’s decomposition. We have shown that the discrimination term (DSC) and the miscalibration term (MCB) can be represented without directly including the responses, but solely considering their predictors. This reformulation is conceptually important. It shows that discrimination is the discrepancy between the calibrated predictor and the unconditional mean, whereas miscalibration is the discrepancy between the calibrated predictor and the original predictor. As a consequence, Murphy’s decomposition is not only an algebraic identity: it gives a direct and interpretable geometry of forecast evaluation.

Our second contribution concerns miscalibration measures. We have revisited the area between the concentration and Lorenz curves, ABC, and we have introduced the mean squared analogue ABC
2
. The analysis reveals an important asymmetry. While ABC
{}^{2}=0
is equivalent to mean-calibration, ABC
=0
is only a necessary condition and may occur even when the predictor is not mean-calibrated. Thus, ABC does not support a desired interpretation of “smaller value means better calibration” in general. Moreover, we have also shown that both ABC and ABC
2
can be represented through predictor-dependent weights of expected losses, thus, they fail to be aligned with the decision-theoretic notion of mean consistent loss function. Therefore, these quantities cannot be treated as universal mean-consistent scoring functions in the sense of forecast evaluation. From this perspective, the miscalibration term from Murphy’s decomposition is preferable because it compares predictors under a pre-specified Bregman divergence. Our counter-example for weighted Bregman divergences with predictor-dependent weights reinforces this conclusion by showing that such weights may even reverse the ordering between a truthful predictor and a distorted one.

Our third contribution concerns discrimination. For mean-calibrated predictors, we have related the Gini index to Murphy’s discrimination term and shown that the Gini index can be decomposed into a Murphy-type discrimination component, built from a predictor-specific Bregman divergence, plus a mean absolute deviation term. This includes the predictor in the discrimination measure in an unwanted way, leading to the same conclusions as for ABC and ABC
2
, namely, the discrimination term from Murphy’s decomposition should be preferred over the Gini index to assess discrimination.

The final part of the paper addresses the practically relevant case where Lorenz curves intersect. In practice, strict Lorenz dominance is rare, so an evaluation theory that only covers non-intersecting curves is too restrictive. We have shown that, for mean-calibrated predictors, Lorenz curves and Murphy’s curves have the same number of crossings. This creates a precise bridge between geometric comparisons in Lorenz space and dominance comparisons in Murphy-diagram space. When the curves cross once, we have introduced weaker notions of upward and downward local dominance and characterized the corresponding ranking conditions. Most importantly, we have proved a weaker version of forecast dominance for mean-calibrated predictors for subclasses of Bregman divergences. In this single-crossing setting, the direction of dominance is determined by a double integral of the difference in distributions of predictors and, equivalently, by the variance of predictors. This result complements the classical Lorenz theory for forecast dominance and shows that, once Lorenz curves intersect, the Gini index is no longer the decisive object for discrimination; rather, the admissible class of loss functions and the associated curvature properties become central.

Several directions for future research emerge from our analysis. A first direction concerns the choice of the Bregman divergence. Our new Murphy’s decomposition suggests that the tail behavior of the response may play a different role for evaluation of miscalibration and discrimination than for selecting a loss function to achieve prediction accuracy. A systematic study of which Bregman divergences are most informative or robust in finite-sample validation remains open. A second direction is to extend the analysis beyond mean prediction, e.g., to quantiles, expectiles, or full predictive distributions. Finally, on the dominance side, the present paper gives a detailed treatment of the case of one crossing. A broader theory covering multiple crossings, richer local dominance concepts, and sharper characterizations of admissible loss classes would further deepen the connection between forecast evaluation and stochastic order theory.
