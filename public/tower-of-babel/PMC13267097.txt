Headache psychiatry: A structured narrative review and integrative framework
Authors: Kyohei Otani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeadache disorders involve complex interactions between neurobiological, psychiatric, behavioral, and social factors. Despite robust evidence of psychiatric comorbidity and psychosocial influences, these dimensions remain inadequately integrated into clinical practice and conceptual models. This review synthesizes evidence linking psychiatric factors with headache disorders and proposes Headache Psychiatry as a unified, integrative framework, operationalized through the Psychocephalology model. We conducted a structured narrative review across PubMed, PsycINFO, and Web of Science (1990–2025), examining psychiatric comorbidity, emotion regulation, stress physiology, pain neuroscience, and psychosocial determinants in headache disorders. The Psychocephalology framework synthesizes findings across five interconnected domains: neurobiological mechanisms, pain‐processing dynamics, emotion regulation, stress–behavioral factors, and social–contextual influences. Strong bidirectional associations exist between headache and depression, anxiety, trauma exposure, and sleep disturbance. Emotion dysregulation, catastrophizing, and maladaptive coping predict chronification and disability. Neural pain–emotion circuits show heightened activity in chronic migraine. Stress physiology, HPA axis dysregulation, and behavioral patterns are associated with chronic headache. Women experience disproportionate burden due to hormonal fluctuations, perinatal transitions, and role‐related stress. Headache Psychiatry, operationalized through the Psychocephalology framework, offers a biopsychosocial model that bridges fragmented disciplines. Clinical implementation requires routine psychiatric screening, multidisciplinary teams, integrated behavioral–pharmacological treatments, and specialized attention to perinatal mental health. This approach enables personalized, context‐sensitive care and may prevent transition from episodic to chronic headache.Keywords: headache, migraine, psychiatry, Psychocephalology, perinatal mental health

INTRODUCTION

The burden of headache disorders and the limits of purely neurobiological models

Headache disorders, particularly migraine and tension‐type headache (TTH), rank among the leading causes of disability worldwide, affecting approximately 1 billion individuals and accounting for substantial personal suffering and societal economic burden. 1 , 2 , 3 Despite remarkable advances in understanding neurobiological mechanisms—including trigeminovascular activation, cortical spreading depression (CSD), 4 calcitonin gene‐related peptide (CGRP) pathways, and genetic susceptibility—these biological insights alone fail to explain the considerable heterogeneity in symptom expression, treatment response, chronification risk, and functional disability observed in clinical settings. 5 , 6

A substantial body of evidence demonstrates that headache disorders are profoundly influenced by psychiatric, emotional, behavioral, and social factors. Epidemiological studies consistently document elevated rates of comorbid depression, anxiety disorders, post‐traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), sleep disturbance, and stress sensitivity in headache populations. 7 , 8 Neuroimaging research further demonstrates that psychosocial stressors and restricted activity can induce measurable structural and functional brain changes, including alterations in gray matter volume and large‐scale network connectivity, underscoring the neurobiological embedding of psychological and contextual factors in brain function. 9 Psychological processes such as pain catastrophizing, hypervigilance, rumination, and maladaptive coping are strongly associated with headache frequency, severity, attack duration, and medication overuse. 10 , 11

Moreover, life‐contextual factors—including parenting stress, caregiving burden, occupational pressures, socioeconomic adversity, trauma exposure, and gender‐specific hormonal fluctuations—play important roles in shaping headache outcomes. 12 , 13 Women experience a disproportionately higher headache burden due to hormonal transitions across menstrual cycles, pregnancy, postpartum periods, and menopause, as well as role‐related stress and sleep disruption. 14

The fragmentation of headache care and the need for an integrated framework

Despite accumulating evidence across neurology, psychiatry, psychosomatic medicine, pain science, and women's health, these perspectives remain inadequately integrated in both clinical practice and theoretical models. Current diagnostic and treatment approaches typically prioritize biological mechanisms while underrepresenting emotional, behavioral, and psychosocial processes that directly modulate pain pathways and contribute to chronification. 15 , 16

This fragmentation creates missed opportunities for early intervention, comprehensive assessment, and personalized care. Neurologists may underrecognize psychiatric comorbidity; psychiatrists may have limited familiarity with headache‐specific treatments; and primary care providers often lack training in integrated biopsychosocial models. Consequently, many patients receive pharmacological treatment for headache without adequate assessment or treatment of underlying emotional dysregulation, stress physiology, or psychosocial burdens (Figures 1 and 2).

Introducing Headache Psychiatry: A new clinical and conceptual discipline

To address these gaps, we propose Headache Psychiatry as a distinct subdiscipline within consultation‐liaison psychiatry and psychosomatic medicine. Headache Psychiatry systematically integrates psychiatric assessment and treatment into headache care, recognizing that headache disorders emerge from dynamic interactions between neurobiological, psychological, behavioral, and social systems.

In this review, we define Headache Psychiatry as a clinical and research framework that integrates psychiatric assessment, psychosocial context, and neurobiological mechanisms in the comprehensive evaluation and management of headache disorders.

We operationalize this clinical discipline through the Psychocephalology framework—a five‐domain integrative model that synthesizes evidence from neuroscience, psychiatry, behavioral medicine, pain science, and women's health. The five domains are:

By positioning these domains as mutually interacting components of a unified system, the Psychocephalology framework provides a comprehensive biopsychosocial foundation for understanding and treating headache disorders. Psychocephalology does not replace existing biopsychosocial models but offers a structured clinical operationalization tailored to headache disorders. The framework does not imply that psychiatric factors are primary drivers in all patients, but rather that interacting domains vary across individuals and disease stages.

METHODS

Review design and rationale

This paper was designed as a structured narrative and conceptual review, rather than a PRISMA‐based systematic review. The primary aim was to synthesize interdisciplinary evidence and develop the integrative framework of Headache Psychiatry through the Psychocephalology model, which conceptualizes headache disorders across neurobiological, psychiatric, behavioral, and psychosocial dimensions.

Because the objective is to establish a theoretical and clinical framework rather than to quantitatively synthesize intervention efficacy or perform meta‐analysis, a traditional PRISMA methodology was not appropriate. This approach aligns with Journal of Psychosomatic Research guidelines, which permit narrative and conceptual reviews aimed at developing new theoretical perspectives in psychosomatic science. 17

Screening and evidence synthesis approach

Because the aim of this review was conceptual integration rather than quantitative synthesis, the search process was iterative and domain‐guided rather than strictly algorithmic. Titles/abstracts were screened for relevance to the five Psychocephalology domains, and full texts were reviewed when studies provided mechanistic insight, clinically actionable evidence, or foundational theoretical models. Publications relevant to implementation, safety considerations, or recently emerging evidence (e.g., randomized clinical trials published within the search window) were incorporated to ensure contemporary clinical relevance.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) pharmacologic efficacy studies without psychosocial or psychiatric context; (2) secondary headache studies unrelated to psychiatric/psychosocial mechanisms; (3) isolated case reports without mechanistic implications; and (4) studies lacking sufficient methodological detail or superseded by higher‐level evidence (e.g., systematic reviews) when available.

As a single‐author narrative and conceptual review, dual independent screening was not performed. Longitudinal studies, meta‐analyses, and well‐powered neuroimaging studies were prioritized where available. PRISMA‐style numerical flow reporting was not applicable to this review design.

Transparency and limitations

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were included if they provided empirical evidence linking psychiatric, psychological, or behavioral factors with headache disorders; examined neurobiological or psychophysiological mechanisms; offered validated theoretical models; addressed hormonal modulation or perinatal aspects; or contributed to formulating an integrative biopsychosocial framework.

RESULTS

Overview of synthesized evidence

This review synthesized findings from a broad body of interdisciplinary literature, including epidemiological cohorts, neuroimaging experiments, clinical trials, systematic reviews, and theoretical contributions. The evidence consistently demonstrates that headache disorders—particularly migraine, tension‐type headache, and chronic daily headache—are shaped by interconnected neurobiological, psychiatric, behavioral, and psychosocial factors.

Neurobiological mechanisms

Genetic studies have identified susceptibility loci associated with both migraine and affective disorders, suggesting shared biological vulnerability. 18 CGRP plays a central role in migraine pathophysiology, with anti‐CGRP monoclonal antibodies (erenumab, 19 fremanezumab, 20 galcanezumab 21 ) demonstrating robust efficacy in pivotal randomized controlled trials. 2 , 22 Recent randomized evidence (UNITE trial) demonstrates that CGRP‐targeted therapy in patients with migraine and comorbid major depressive disorder improves both headache frequency and depressive symptoms. 23 The UNITE trial further supports the bidirectional interface between migraine and mood regulation, suggesting that CGRP modulation may indirectly influence affective symptom burden in selected patients with comorbid depression. Although the mechanisms underlying mood improvement remain to be clarified, these findings are consistent with shared pain–affective network modulation—a core premise of the Psychocephalology framework—and support the clinical validity of integrated headache–psychiatric care. Cortical spreading depression (CSD) is implicated in migraine aura and may contribute to chronic sensitization. 5 Diencephalic and brainstem structures—including the hypothalamus, periaqueductal gray, and locus coeruleus—play central roles in migraine generation and the modulation of pain‐emotion integration, providing a neural substrate for the bidirectional links between migraine and psychiatric disorders. 24 Genetic evidence further supports this link: polymorphisms in the hypocretin receptor 1 gene have been associated with migraine susceptibility, suggesting shared neurobiological pathways involving sleep‐arousal regulation. 25

Pain‐processing dynamics

Central sensitization—characterized by increased responsiveness of nociceptive neurons—is a hallmark of chronic migraine and medication‐overuse headache. 26 Emotional distress, anxiety, and catastrophizing further compromise descending inhibition, creating a vicious cycle. 27

Emotion regulation systems

Psychiatric comorbidity: Depression and anxiety

Epidemiological studies consistently demonstrate bidirectional associations between migraine and major depressive disorder (MDD), with each condition increasing the risk of developing the other. 28 , 29 Prevalence rates of MDD in migraine populations are estimated at approximately 25%–30%, compared to 10%–15% in the general population. 7 Population‐based studies confirm that higher headache frequency is independently associated with greater severity of depression and anxiety, establishing a dose‐response relationship between headache burden and psychiatric morbidity. 30 Notably, the UNITE randomized clinical trial—the first placebo‐controlled RCT specifically designed for patients with migraine and comorbid MDD—demonstrated that fremanezumab, an anti‐CGRP monoclonal antibody, significantly reduced both monthly migraine days and depressive symptoms compared with placebo in 353 patients across 12 countries, providing RCT‐level evidence that a single pharmacological intervention may simultaneously improve both conditions. 23

Anxiety disorders, including generalized anxiety disorder (GAD), panic disorder, and social anxiety disorder, are similarly elevated in headache populations. 8 Notably, panic disorder is highly comorbid with migraine in patients with psychiatric disorders, reflecting shared autonomic and neurobiological dysregulation. 31 Beyond depression and anxiety, migraine comorbidity extends to bipolar disorder, with elevated lifetime rates documented across multiple mood disorder cohorts. 32 , 33 Furthermore, premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD) is associated with cyclic migraine exacerbations, particularly in the late luteal phase, implicating shared hormonal and serotonergic mechanisms. 34

Post‐traumatic stress disorder and childhood trauma

PTSD and childhood maltreatment are associated with increased migraine frequency, chronicity, and treatment resistance. 35 , 36 Trauma exposure is linked to amygdala hyperreactivity, HPA‐axis dysregulation, and altered pain processing. 37

Emotion dysregulation: Catastrophizing, rumination, and hypervigilance

Pain catastrophizing is one of the strongest psychological predictors of headache‐related disability. 10 , 38 Catastrophizing is associated with heightened activity in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), insula, and prefrontal cortex during pain processing. 39

Neural substrates of pain–emotion coupling

Neuroimaging studies reveal that brain regions critical for emotional processing—including the amygdala, ACC, insula, and prefrontal cortex—are hyperactive in chronic migraine and show altered connectivity patterns. 40 , 41 Whole‐brain computational modeling using the Hopf oscillator framework further demonstrates that adaptive fitting of brain dynamics can characterize neural biomarkers specific to psychiatric disorder subtypes, including MDD, supporting the neurobiological underpinnings of psychiatric comorbidity in headache conditions. 42

Stress physiology and behavioral patterns

Chronic stress is associated with dysregulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis. 43 Autonomic dysregulation is common in migraine and contributes to both attack initiation and chronification. 44

Sleep disturbance is both a trigger and a consequence of headache, forming a bidirectional cycle. 45 In migraine patients, disturbed sleep‐wake patterns and fatigue are independently associated with greater attack frequency and disability. 46 Importantly, targeted behavioral sleep modification has been shown to revert chronic migraine to episodic migraine in a proportion of patients, underscoring the modifiable role of sleep disruption in headache chronification. 47 Maladaptive coping strategies, including avoidance behavior and medication overuse, reinforce headache chronicity. 11 , 48

Social–contextual influences and perinatal mental health

Role strain, caregiving burden, and occupational stress

Social and contextual factors significantly influence headache expression and treatment outcomes. Role strain related to caregiving, parenting, and dual career responsibilities is strongly associated with headache burden, particularly among women. 13 Occupational stress, long working hours, and shift work serve as both trigger factors and persisting factors. 49

Socioeconomic adversity and health disparities

Lower socioeconomic status is associated with higher headache prevalence, greater disability, and reduced access to specialized care. 50 Health disparities are particularly pronounced among marginalized populations. 51

Women's health and hormonal modulation

Menstrual‐related migraine

Approximately 50%–60% of women with migraine report menstrual‐related attacks, often related to estrogen withdrawal in the late luteal phase. 52 , 53 Menstrual migraine tends to be longer, more severe, and less responsive to acute treatment than non‐menstrual attacks. 54

Short‐term estrogen supplementation or hormonal contraceptives with extended cycles may reduce menstrual migraine frequency. 55

Pregnancy and migraine

Approximately half of women with migraine experience improvement during the first trimester, with improvement rates rising to over 80% in the second and third trimesters, due to sustained high estrogen levels. 56 However, 10%–20% experience worsening or new‐onset headache, particularly in the first trimester or postpartum period. 57 Severe migraine during pregnancy has been associated with increased risk of pregnancy complications and adverse delivery outcomes. 58

Pregnancy‐related migraine management requires specialized expertise due to medication safety considerations. Most migraine‐specific medications—including triptans, ergotamines, and CGRP antagonists—have insufficient safety data or are contraindicated during pregnancy. 59

Safe options include: Acetaminophen (paracetamol)—First‐line acute treatment, though efficacy is limited; Metoclopramide—For nausea and as adjunctive analgesic; Magnesium—For prevention, though evidence is limited; and Non‐pharmacological approaches—Biofeedback, relaxation training, cognitive‐behavioral therapy, and acupuncture.

Psychiatric comorbidity is common in pregnant women with migraine. Depression and anxiety during pregnancy are associated with increased headache frequency and postpartum exacerbation. 60 , 61 Integrated psychiatric care is important for addressing comorbid mood and anxiety disorders while navigating the complex risk–benefit considerations of psychotropic medications during pregnancy and lactation.

Postpartum headache

The postpartum period carries increased risk for headache exacerbation, new‐onset headache, and psychiatric complications. Postpartum migraine may result from rapid estrogen withdrawal, sleep deprivation, stress, and hormonal fluctuations related to breastfeeding. 62

Postpartum depression (PPD) and postpartum anxiety affect approximately 10%–20% of mothers and are associated with increased headache burden. The bidirectional relationship between headache and perinatal mood disorders creates significant morbidity and impairs maternal–infant bonding, breastfeeding success, and overall quality of life.

Headache Psychiatry interventions in the postpartum period should address: Comprehensive psychiatric assessment for PPD, anxiety, PTSD, and obsessive‐compulsive symptoms; psychopharmacological management compatible with breastfeeding; sleep hygiene and behavioral interventions; partner and family psychoeducation; and coordination with obstetric and pediatric teams.

Lactation and medication considerations

Breastfeeding women require careful medication selection to minimize infant exposure while effectively treating maternal headache and psychiatric symptoms. 63 A systematic review of antidepressant use during breastfeeding indicates that sertraline and paroxetine are associated with the lowest detectable infant serum levels and are generally considered compatible with lactation, providing evidence‐based guidance for psychiatric pharmacotherapy in postpartum headache management. 63

Generally safe options during lactation: Acetaminophen (paracetamol)—Minimal infant exposure; Ibuprofen—Generally safe, lower infant exposure than other NSAIDs, Sumatriptan—Short half‐life, minimal infant exposure; Sertraline (and escitalopram)—Low infant serum levels and generally compatible with breastfeeding for depression/anxiety treatment; 63 Propranolol and amitriptyline—Reasonable safety profiles for migraine prevention.

Close collaboration between psychiatry, neurology, and pediatrics is recommended to optimize maternal mental health and headache management while minimizing infant risk.

Menopause and headache

Migraine patterns often change during perimenopause, with many women experiencing increased frequency and severity due to erratic estrogen fluctuations. 64 Migraine course after menopause is variable, with some women experiencing improvement, while others report no change or worsening. 65

Hormone replacement therapy (HRT) has variable effects on migraine. Psychiatric assessment is important when considering HRT, as mood changes and anxiety are common during perimenopausal transition.

Life‐course women's health perspective

Across the life course, migraine risk and disability are shaped not only by gonadal steroid variability but also by changing sleep patterns, caregiving demands, and mental health vulnerability. A life‐course perspective helps clinicians anticipate predictable high‐risk windows and prevent avoidable chronification by aligning headache management with developmental and social transitions.

Adolescence and early adulthood: migraine onset often coincides with pubertal hormonal changes, emerging anxiety/depression, and school‐related stress. Early education on lifestyle regularity, sleep, and emotion regulation can reduce progression to high‐frequency patterns.

Reproductive years: menstrual‐related migraine frequently clusters with premenstrual mood symptoms, trauma exposure, and occupational/role strain. Clinicians should proactively screen for mood/anxiety disorders, pain catastrophizing, and medication overuse, and consider cycle‐tailored prevention strategies.

Perinatal transition: pregnancy, postpartum, and lactation involve rapid endocrine shifts, sleep deprivation, and heightened vulnerability to mood and anxiety disorders. Risk stratification (e.g., prior depression/anxiety, trauma history, high catastrophizing, medication overuse, poor sleep) can guide early integrated interventions and safe pharmacotherapy planning.

Perimenopause and menopause: fluctuating estrogen, vasomotor symptoms, insomnia, and anxiety can destabilize previously controlled migraine. When considering hormone therapy, assess baseline mood/anxiety, sleep, and headache phenotype; coordinate care to avoid iatrogenic worsening and to support adherence.

Older age: comorbidity profiles shift toward insomnia, chronic pain syndromes, polypharmacy, and social isolation. The Psychocephalology framework remains applicable by emphasizing sleep stabilization, deprescribing where feasible, and addressing loneliness and functional impairment as targets for disability reduction.

Integration: The five‐domain Psychocephalology framework

Synthesizing the evidence presented across the five domains, the Psychocephalology framework positions headache disorders as emergent phenomena arising from dynamic interactions between neurobiological mechanisms, pain‐processing dynamics, emotion regulation systems, stress–behavioral patterns, and social–contextual influences.

These domains interact through shared neural circuits (amygdala, ACC, insula, PAG), physiological pathways (HPA axis, autonomic nervous system, immune activation), and behavioral patterns (avoidance, medication overuse, sleep dysregulation).

DISCUSSION

Headache Psychiatry as a clinical discipline

The evidence synthesized in this review demonstrates that headache disorders cannot be fully understood or effectively treated through purely neurobiological models. Psychiatric comorbidity, emotion dysregulation, stress physiology, behavioral patterns, and psychosocial context are not peripheral factors but central mechanisms that directly influence pain pathways, chronification, disability, and treatment response.

Headache Psychiatry emerges as a distinct subdiscipline within consultation‐liaison psychiatry and psychosomatic medicine, dedicated to integrating psychiatric assessment and treatment into comprehensive headache care.

Bridging fragmented disciplines

Framework differentiation and falsifiable propositions

The standard biopsychosocial model is broadly accepted but often remains difficult to operationalize in time‐limited practice. Psychocephalology aims to make biopsychosocial care actionable by (1) explicit domain mapping with measurable targets, (2) identification of dominant and interacting domains that drive disability, and (3) decision rules for stepped, resource‐efficient triage.

The framework also generates falsifiable hypotheses that can be tested empirically:

Proposition 1 (Disability): At comparable headache frequency, patients with dominant psychiatric‐domain profiles (e.g., moderate‐to‐severe depression/anxiety, high catastrophizing) will show greater headache‐related disability than those with primarily neurobiological‐domain profiles.

Historically, headache medicine has developed along parallel disciplinary tracks. The Psychocephalology framework synthesizes these previously siloed contributions into a unified five‐domain model, providing a comprehensive biopsychosocial explanation for headache development, maintenance, and functional impact.

Neurobiological and psychosocial convergence in headache chronification

Neuroimaging studies demonstrate that chronic migraine is associated with structural and functional alterations in pain–emotion circuits, particularly the amygdala, ACC, insula, and PAG. 41 , 66 These regions show hyperreactivity in individuals with anxiety, depression, trauma exposure, and catastrophizing.

This convergence has profound clinical implications: interventions targeting emotional regulation, stress management, and behavioral change may prevent chronification and should be integrated into routine headache care from the earliest stages.

Emotion regulation as a mechanistic bridge

Emotion regulation processes serve as a critical mechanistic link between psychological states and pain perception. Catastrophizing increases pain intensity, prolongs attacks, and predicts chronification through sustained limbic activation. 10 , 38

Headache Psychiatry positions these emotional processes not as secondary reactions to pain but as core components of headache pathophysiology.

Stress physiology and daily behavioral patterns in headache maintenance

Stress is consistently identified as the most common migraine trigger, with daily diary studies demonstrating that perceived stress precedes migraine onset in a temporally specific manner. 67 Dysregulated HPA‐axis activity, autonomic imbalance, and immune–inflammatory activation create a biological environment conducive to headache initiation and chronification. 43 , 44 Notably, the inflammatory consequences of psychosocial stress—including cytokine‐mediated neuroinflammation—provide a mechanistic bridge linking chronic stress, depression, and headache chronification. 68

Social–contextual factors and women's health

Social and contextual factors are often the most neglected dimension in traditional headache care. Women experience disproportionate headache burden due to the convergence of hormonal fluctuations, role strain, sleep disruption, and higher rates of trauma exposure. 13 , 69

Perinatal mental health: A specialized domain within Headache Psychiatry

The perinatal period represents a critical window for headache management and psychiatric care. Pregnant and postpartum women with migraine face unique challenges, including medication safety concerns, high rates of comorbid depression and anxiety, sleep deprivation, hormonal fluctuations, and role transitions.

Clinical implementation: Building Headache Psychiatry services

Safety considerations: Excluding secondary headache causes

Importantly, integrated psychiatric evaluation should never delay the exclusion of secondary headache causes, particularly in acute, atypical, or rapidly progressive presentations. Integrated headache–psychiatric care must therefore always begin with careful exclusion of secondary causes. In consultation‐liaison settings, premature psychologization of acute headache presentations may delay recognition of potentially life‐threatening neurological conditions. 70 Red flag features—including sudden onset (“thunderclap”) headache, 71 new focal neurological deficits, altered consciousness, pregnancy‐related hypertension, or marked changes in headache pattern—require urgent medical evaluation and neuroimaging in accordance with established diagnostic guidelines. Conditions such as subarachnoid hemorrhage, reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome (RCVS), 72 , 73 cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, encephalitis, and hypertensive encephalopathy may initially present with prominent psychiatric or behavioral symptoms. Therefore, a structured neuropsychiatric assessment framework that integrates medical vigilance with biopsychosocial formulation is essential in operationalizing Psychocephalology within modern hospital systems.

Implementation barriers and opportunities in Japanese hospitals

In many general hospital settings, including Japanese hospitals, integrated headache–psychiatry care faces several structural barriers that are logistical rather than conceptual. First, consultation‐liaison psychiatry services are often understaffed, limiting proactive involvement. Second, reimbursement structures provide limited incentives for time‐intensive biopsychosocial assessment. Third, specialty silos can delay psychiatric referral until late‐stage chronification. Fourth, patient‐level stigma may lead to reluctance toward psychiatric consultation, particularly when headache is perceived as “purely neurological.”

These barriers suggest that implementation should prioritize scalable triage pathways that minimize labeling, use brief validated tools, and reserve intensive multidisciplinary care for patients with high disability, medication overuse, treatment resistance, or perinatal vulnerability. In Japanese general hospital settings, where consultation‐liaison psychiatry resources are limited, a tiered screening model may represent the most feasible implementation strategy.

A stepwise care model adaptable to resource constraints

Level 1 (Neurology‐based screening): universal brief screening for depression (PHQ‐9 74 ), anxiety (GAD‐7 75 ), insomnia, trauma exposure (e.g., PC‐PTSD‐5 76 ), and pain catastrophizing (e.g., Pain Catastrophizing Scale 38 ). This level can be implemented without structural reorganization and can be nurse‐administered.

Level 2 (Domain‐guided triage consultation): targeted consultation focusing on dominant interacting domains driving disability, rather than a global psychiatric evaluation for all patients.

Level 3 (Integrated management for high‐risk cases): multidisciplinary treatment for patients with chronic migraine, medication‐overuse headache, severe disability, perinatal vulnerability, or repeated treatment failure.

Operationalizing Psychocephalology in practice

Step 1: Identify dominant domain(s) using structured tools and clinical history. Step 2: Assess interaction patterns predictive of chronification (e.g., depression/anxiety x sleep disturbance x medication overuse). Step 3: Implement domain‐targeted interventions with reassessment at predefined intervals to detect domain shifts and adjust treatment accordingly.

Applicability to low‐resource settings

Where specialist access is limited, Level 1 screening can be embedded into routine visits, Level 2 can be delivered via telepsychiatry or visiting consultants, and Level 3 can be reserved for the highest‐need subgroup. Focusing on functional impairment and chronic risk reduces unnecessary referrals while capturing patients most likely to benefit.

Routine psychiatric screening in headache clinics

All patients presenting to headache clinics should undergo systematic screening for depression (e.g., PHQ‐9 74 ), anxiety (e.g., GAD‐7 75 ), trauma exposure (e.g., PC‐PTSD‐5 76 ), and pain catastrophizing (e.g., Pain Catastrophizing Scale 38 ).

Strengths and limitations

This review provides a comprehensive synthesis of evidence supporting Headache Psychiatry as a distinct clinical discipline. Strengths include integration of multiple disciplines, conceptual coherence through the Psychocephalology framework, emphasis on clinical implementation, and specialized attention to perinatal mental health.

Limitations include that this is a narrative rather than a PRISMA‐based systematic review, heterogeneity across studies, and a primary focus on migraine and tension‐type headache. Although this is not a PRISMA‐based systematic review, evidence from longitudinal cohorts, neuroimaging studies, and randomized trials consistently supports the integrative approach described. Future systematic reviews and meta‐analyses will complement this conceptual synthesis. Even in the era of CGRP‐targeted therapies, residual disability often reflects affective, behavioral, and contextual contributors beyond trigeminovascular modulation, underscoring the continued relevance of integrated psychiatric approaches. Generalizability may vary across healthcare systems with different reimbursement structures and specialist availability.

Conclusion of Discussion

Headache Psychiatry, operationalized through the Psychocephalology framework, represents a paradigm shift in headache medicine. Clinical implementation requires systemic changes, including routine psychiatric screening, multidisciplinary team‐based care, integrated pharmacological–psychological treatments, and specialized expertise in women's health and perinatal mental health. Emerging clinical evidence further supports the validity of this integrated framework. The UNITE randomized clinical trial demonstrated that CGRP‐targeted therapy in patients with migraine and comorbid major depressive disorder improved both headache frequency and depressive symptoms, underscoring the clinical interconnectedness of pain and affective systems. 23 Although the precise mechanisms underlying mood improvement remain to be clarified, these findings are consistent with shared modulation of pain–affective neural networks. At the implementation level, hospital‐based liaison nurse models in Japan have demonstrated the feasibility of structured interdisciplinary collaboration in perinatal mental health care, providing a scalable template for integrating psychiatric expertise into routine medical practice. 83 In parallel, real‐world data from psychiatric outpatient settings have revealed a substantial burden of comorbid headache and highlighted the complexity of management, including exploratory use of CGRP‐targeted therapies. 84 These observations collectively support a structured, liaison‐based integrative framework (Psychocephalology), which conceptualizes headache disorders across interacting neurobiological, affective, behavioral, and contextual domains.

CONCLUSION

Headache disorders represent complex biopsychosocial conditions shaped by interconnected neurobiological, psychiatric, behavioral, and social factors. This review proposes Headache Psychiatry as a distinct subdiscipline within consultation‐liaison psychiatry and psychosomatic medicine, operationalized through the Psychocephalology framework.

This five‐domain model synthesizes evidence across: (1) Neurobiological mechanisms, (2) Pain‐processing dynamics, (3) Emotion regulation systems, (4) Stress–behavioral patterns, and (5) Social–contextual influences.

Clinical implementation of Headache Psychiatry requires: Routine psychiatric screening in headache populations; multidisciplinary teams integrating neurologists, psychiatrists, psychologists, and mental health nurses; combined pharmacological–psychological interventions; specialized attention to women's health and perinatal mental health; and prevention‐oriented approaches targeting psychological risk factors.

Women's health and perinatal mental health represent particularly important domains, as pregnant and postpartum women with migraine face unique challenges related to medication safety, hormonal fluctuations, comorbid mood disorders, sleep disruption, and role transitions. Consultation‐liaison psychiatrists with perinatal expertise are essential for delivering integrated, safe, and effective care to this vulnerable population.

Future research should validate the Psychocephalology framework through longitudinal neuroimaging studies, biomarker development, integrated intervention trials, digital phenotyping, and perinatal‐focused investigations. Prospective cohort studies integrating domain‐based stratification may clarify which combinations most strongly predict chronification.

Headache Psychiatry offers a structured, integrative operationalization of care—moving from fragmented, biomedically focused approaches toward personalized, context‐sensitive treatment that addresses the full complexity of headache as a psychophysiological phenomenon. Adopting this approach has the potential to improve outcomes, prevent chronicity, and reduce the profound burden of headache disorders on individuals and societies.
