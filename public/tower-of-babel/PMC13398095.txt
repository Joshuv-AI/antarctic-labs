Clarifying treatment attribution and lesion-level signals in periodontal multi-omics for diabetes
Authors: Man Sun, Dan Zang, Jun Chen
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

We read with interest the longitudinal multi-omics study by Diao et al., which examined nonsurgical periodontal therapy (NSPT)-associated oral microbiome–metabolome changes and their links with periodontal, inflammatory, and glycemic outcomes in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and periodontitis [1]. As oral multi-omics is increasingly used to connect periodontal therapy with systemic metabolic health, methodological interpretation becomes critical for translating association-based findings into clinically actionable co-management strategies. We wish to raise three considerations that may refine the interpretation of this important work.

First, the multi-omics changes attributed to NSPT require sharper treatment attribution. The control arm received supragingival scaling, and both arms received oral hygiene instruction and maintenance supragingival debridement. Thus, some longitudinal shifts may reflect shared periodontal-care or behavioural effects rather than the incremental biological impact of subgingival scaling and root planing. This distinction is underscored by 13-eicosenoic acid, the most consistent metabolite after false-discovery correction, which increased in both groups. Future trials separating oral hygiene instruction, supragingival scaling, full NSPT, and, when ethically appropriate, delayed treatment would better define which microbial-metabolic signals are specifically attributable to subgingival instrumentation.

Second, periodontal microbiome responses require lesion-level ecological resolution. The authors pooled gingival crevicular fluid from predetermined index teeth, a strategy that improves standardisation but may obscure the biology of a site-specific disease. Active pockets with greater depth, bleeding, or post-treatment change may not overlap with fixed sampling teeth, allowing microbial trajectories from inflamed lesions to be diluted by more stable sites [2]. Excluding blood-contaminated strips is technically defensible, but may preferentially exclude highly inflamed pockets from analysis. Combining index-tooth sampling with patient-specific active-site tracking would better link microbial shifts to local disease activity and treatment response [3].

Third, systemic metabolic signals should be interpreted through an actionable periodontal–diabetes co-management framework. The greater short-term HbA1c reduction among participants with poorer baseline glycaemic control may indicate a modifiable high-risk phenotype, but its loss at 6 months and the nonspecificity of CRP limit direct clinical translation [4]. Without longitudinal data on antidiabetic adherence, medication adjustment, diet, physical activity, weight change, and home oral-care behaviour, metabolic improvement cannot be confidently separated from concurrent diabetes management. Incorporating insulin-resistance indices, continuous glucose monitoring metrics, and mediation analyses would help determine whether periodontal and microbial-metabolic responses can identify patients likely to gain systemic metabolic benefit from NSPT [5].

These points do not diminish the periodontal value of NSPT. Rather, they define the evidentiary steps needed before periodontal multi-omics can inform diabetes care. Future work should move from association mapping toward treatment-specific attribution, lesion-resolved sampling, and metabolically stratified outcome assessment. Such refinement would clarify whether periodontal therapy should remain a local anti-inflammatory intervention or become part of targeted oral-systemic co-management for selected patients with T2DM and periodontitis.
