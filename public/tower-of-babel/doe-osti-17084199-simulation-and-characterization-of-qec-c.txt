ABSTRACT
Estimating logical failure probabilities at the utility scale (sub 10−12 ) is an important and challenging
problem. We use a two step approach to solve this problem. The first step constructs space-time
logical operators called BUFILOs that lead to logical errors. We describe a graph theoretic approach
that exploits the QEC code structure and builds a BUFILO generating graph efficiently. The second
step in our technique involves sampling malignant sets from BUFILOs, adjusting for sampling
bias.
Currently we demonstrate our methods on 2D topological codes using standard syndrome extraction
circuits. These methods apply to the standard circuit noise model with Pauli stochastic noise as well
certain leakage noise models. We partially verify the correctness of our methods using a formal
proving technique called Prove-It. Our methods are Monte Carlo based, but they produce a logical
failure probability function as opposed to point estimates. Our methods also have the advantage of
not requiring an explicit decoder. They can be used to quantify the benefits of a minimum weight
decoder and leakage aware decoders for maximimum likelihood (optimal) as well most-likely error
(sub-optimal but standard) decoding. They can also be used to study concatenated codes where the
inner code is a 2D topological code.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This report presents HERD, a new framework for estimating logical failure probabilities in quantum
error-correcting codes directly from detector error models. The method is designed to address
the challenge of estimating very low failure rates efficiently, especially in the regime relevant
to fault-tolerant quantum computing. HERD works by first constructing a graph representation
of bad undetectable fault-induced logical operators (BUFILOs), then sampling malignant fault
sets derived from those BUFILOs using importance-sampling techniques. This two-step approach
enables logical performance estimation without requiring an explicit decoder, while still supporting
decoder-specific analysis when desired.
The report focuses primarily on rotated surface codes under standard circuit-level noise models,
with extensions to certain leakage noise models. It also introduces heuristics that make the graph
construction computationally feasible and discusses how these heuristics are tailored to preserve
completeness while reducing search complexity. In addition, the work incorporates formal theorem
proving with Prove-It to verify key mathematical claims underlying the method, strengthening
confidence in the soundness of the framework.
Results show that HERD can produce competitive logical error estimates compared with Monte
Carlo simulation, rare-event simulation, and subset-sampling methods, especially in the low-error
regime. The approach also offers practical advantages in runtime and memory use, and it can be
used with or without a specific decoder. Overall, HERD provides a flexible and scalable tool for
evaluating quantum error-correcting codes and architectures, with promising applicability to future
studies involving leakage, concatenated codes, and broader fault-tolerant design questions.

1. INTRODUCTION

Quantum computers promise substantial advantages for certain classes of problems, but their
practical realization is limited by the fragility of quantum states and the noisy nature of quantum
operations. Quantum error correction (QEC) provides a path toward scalable fault-tolerant quantum
computation by encoding logical information into a larger redundant physical system, allowing
errors to be detected and corrected without directly measuring the encoded quantum state. In this
setting, the central challenge is to determine whether a given noise model and correction strategy
can suppress logical errors to an acceptable level as the system size increases.
A key quantity of interest is the logical failure probability, namely, the probability that a fault-
tolerant protocol produces an incorrect logical outcome after decoding. Estimating this probability
is important both for comparing candidate codes and architectures and for evaluating the perfor-
mance of decoders. However, direct Monte Carlo estimation can be computationally expensive,
particularly at low physical error rates where logical failures are rare. This motivates techniques
that can efficiently estimate logical failure probabilities from a detector error model, either with a
specified decoder or, as we will demonstrate, even without explicitly simulating the decoder. In
this report, we develop such a technique and show how it can be used to evaluate fault-tolerant
performance from a compact probabilistic description of faults.
Furthermore, we use formal methods in proving critical theorems for our technique to provide high
confidence in our claims. In particular, the accuracy of our logical performance estimation relies
strongly upon ensuring that all failure pathways are captured in the accounting.

1.1. Quantum error correction

QEC requires syndrome measurements intended to characterize errors without learning about the
quantum state(s) being protected. In this report we will focus on topological CSS codes. We
mostly assume all the Clifford quantum circuits required for syndrome extraction only have Pauli
noise channels. This is the standard "(digital) circuit noise" noise model that QEC research
uses. We are also beginning to include treatment of leakage noise channels with Pauli+leakage
noise models [20, 21, 31]. Eliminating noise from the entangling (two) qubit gates gives rise to
the Phenomenological model. Further eliminating noise in the preparation and measurement of
syndrome qubits gives rise to the code capacity model.
Fault tolerant QEC protects encoded logical information against local noise by applying a recovery
operation or classical decoding rule based on the syndrome. In the circuit noise model, both
data qubits and syndrome measurement operations are noisy, so the observed syndrome must be
interpreted statistically rather than deterministically. The resulting decoding problem is to infer,

1.1.1. Rotated surface code as our recurring example

Throughout this report, we use the rotated surface code as a recurring example because it combines
conceptual simplicity with strong fault-tolerance properties and a planar geometry that is convenient
for experimental realizations [7, 11, 30]. The rotated surface code is a two-dimensional topological
stabilizer code defined on a square lattice with alternating 𝑋-type and 𝑍-type stabilizer checks. Its
planar layout makes it attractive for near-term hardware architectures with local connectivity.
For a code of distance 𝑑, the rotated surface code can correct up to ⌊(𝑑 −1)/2⌋ arbitrary single-qubit
errors in the idealized setting, and its logical operators correspond to nontrivial strings connecting
opposing boundaries. A distance-5 rotated surface code is shown in Fig. 1-1. In such a code, a
logical failure occurs when the combined effect of physical faults and the chosen decoder produces
an error chain equivalent to a nontrivial logical operator.
The rotated surface code is especially useful as an illustrative example because its local syndrome
structure makes the mapping from physical faults to measurement outcomes geometrically intuitive,
while still capturing the essential features of decoding in larger quantum codes. The geometrical
properties will also be convenient for defining heuristics that we employ in our technique.

1.1.2. The decoding problem

Given a syndrome, the decoding problem is to determine a correction that is consistent with the
observed detectors and minimizes the chance of a logical failure. In general, this is a hard infer-
ence problem. Exact maximum-likelihood decoding is computationally intractable for many code
families and circuit-level noise models, and practical decoders typically rely on approximations,
heuristics, or specialized structure [7, 10, 11, 16].

 20

The difficulty is not merely algorithmic but also statistical: many distinct fault configurations can
produce the same detector pattern, and some differ only by a logical operator. Consequently, a
decoder must implicitly or explicitly resolve ambiguity among many competing explanations. For
topological codes such as the surface code, this often becomes a problem of finding a minimum-
weight or maximum-likelihood matching in a graph-like representation of syndrome defects. While
such methods can be highly effective, they still depend on the details of a decoder that may be
expensive to develop, tune, or run at scale.
For this reason, it is useful to estimate logical failure probability even without an explicit decoder.
Such decoder-independent estimates can be valuable in several settings. First, they provide a base-
line for evaluating a code and noise model before investing in decoder development. Second, they
allow rapid exploration of candidate quantum architectures, including trade-offs among connectiv-
ity, gate set, measurement cadence, and noise structure. Third, they can guide design decisions
in early-stage studies where the main goal is not to optimize a specific decoder, but to determine
whether a proposed architecture can plausibly achieve fault tolerance under realistic assumptions.
More generally, a method that can estimate logical failure probability directly from a detector error
model, with or without an assumed decoder, offers a powerful tool for assessing the feasibility of
QEC across a wide range of codes and noise processes. Rare event simulation is one valuable
technique to estimate logical failure in lower-error regimes where direct Monte Carlo estimation is
expensive [5, 23], but it does require a decoder and still becomes increasingly expensive as error
probabilities decrease. Our technique expounds upon the idea of finding low-weight logical opera-
tors to estimate logical performance [3]. We term these fault-induced logical operators, BUFILOs
(Bad Undetectable Fault-Induced Logical Operator), and present a generalizable framework for
constructing graphs that generate BUFILOs and their subsets, even beyond feasibly enumerable
sets (e.g., 7 × 1018 at 𝑑 = 11), and sample from their subsets which form malignant sets that cause
logical failure.
In this report, we present our technique and demonstrate how it can be used to efficiently quantify
logical performance in settings of practical interest.

1.1.3. Detector error models

To analyze fault-tolerant protocols efficiently, it is convenient to convert the noisy circuit description
into a detector error model (DEM), which compactly represents how elementary faults affect
measured detectors and logical observables [11, 13, 19]. A detector is a binary measurement that
has a definite outcome in the absence of noise. In the presence of noise the measurement outcome
might change. So a detector is an asymmetric signal. These measurements typically measure the
generators of the QEC stabilizer.
Following the notation from Ref. [3], let 𝐻 denote a binary parity-check matrix whose rows
correspond to detectors and whose columns correspond to fault mechanisms. An entry 𝐻𝑖 𝑗 indicates
whether fault 𝑗 flips detector 𝑖. Likewise, let 𝐴 denote an action matrix mapping faults to logical
observables, with entries indicating whether a given fault induces a logical operator or flips a logical
measurement outcome. In this way, a fault is represented not by a full physical error operator, but
by its syndrome signature and logical action.

We will abuse this standard notation, somewhat, for convenience. We treat 𝐻 as a function that
maps a set of faults to a set of detectors and 𝐴ℓ as a function that maps a set of faults to a bit value
expressing the logical action ℓ. The connection is a simple translation between a bit string and a
set identified by the location of 1 values of the bit string.
A DEM is attractive because it separates the physics of fault propagation from the combinatorial
structure of decoding, and it’s a valuable framework for abstracting the characterization of noise
processes to relevant properties in the QEC context [4, 17]. Once 𝐻, 𝐴, and the relevant probability
model are specified, one can study logical performance without repeatedly simulating the full
quantum circuit. This makes DEMs particularly useful for large-scale code comparisons and for
performance estimation in the presence of complex, circuit-level noise.
In many practical models, faults are not all independent [18, 17]. Some fault mechanisms may
be mutually exclusive, meaning that only one fault from a specified set can occur in a single
experimental realization. This situation arises, for example, when a single location can produce
one of several distinct error outcomes, but not more than one simultaneously. Such constraints
can be represented explicitly in the DEM by grouping faults into exclusive sets and assigning
probabilities conditional on the occurrence of the underlying physical event. These correlations
matter because they alter the combinatorics of possible error patterns and therefore influence both
the estimated detector statistics and the inferred logical failure probability.
Pauli+leakage models complicate matters, but it is possible to produce appropriate DEMs from
a Pauli+leakage model. Generating these DEMs and treating such models in our framework is
under development with promising preliminary results. To construct a DEM from a Pauli+leakage
model, one must ascribe multiple faults to a particular leakage event because a leaked qubit could
have indeterminate (stochastic) effects when interacting with computational qubits (e.g., during
2-qubit gate operations). One must account for every combination of detector effects that may arise
from the leakage event, but the combinatorics are manageable for models of quantum circuits that
regularly swap data and ancilla qubits [12] or apply leakage reduction and/or detection gates. For
our leakage modeling, which is still under development, we constrain leakage faults involving the
same leaked qubit(s), along with false-negative leakage detection (leakage that fails to be detected
which we regard as a higher-weight fault), to be mutually exclusive for appropriate and convenient
accounting.

1.2. Formal theorem-proving

Formal methods provide a rigorous framework for specifying, analyzing, and proving mathemat-
ical claims. Unlike informal derivations, which rely on human interpretation of definitions and
proof steps, formal proofs are checked against explicit inference rules in a precisely defined logical
language. In interactive theorem proving, a user guides the development of definitions and argu-
ments, while the proof assistant verifies that each step follows from previously established axioms,
definitions, and lemmas.
In this work, we use the internally developed Prove-It theorem prover [32, 34, 33] to formalize
and verify several of the key theorems underlying our logical-performance estimation method.
Prove-It is less mature than widely used systems such as Rocq [29] or Lean [6], and it is not yet
broadly adopted, but it has several features that make it well suited to this project. In particular, it
produces human-readable LaTeX proofs that resemble standard “chalkboard” mathematics, and it
supports proof construction in a style that closely mirrors informal reasoning. It also allows proofs
to be developed modularly, with intermediate conjectures introduced explicitly and proved later as
needed.
The system is still evolving. Current development is oriented toward a small foundational core
grounded in ZFC set theory, together with theory packages that conservatively extend the system by
introducing symbols and reusable mathematical structure. The present report does not attempt to
reduce every argument to axioms; instead, we use Prove-It to verify the critical theorems in a form
that relies on generally accepted mathematical facts that have not yet all been formalized within the
system. This approach provides a practical balance between rigor, readability, and development
effort while preserving the main soundness benefits of machine-checked proof.

2.1. Prove-It theorem prover

Prove-It is an internally developed, Python-based theorem-proving assistant originating at Sandia
National Laboratories in collaboration with the University of New Mexico [32, 34, 33]. It is
designed to support mathematically readable proofs and to generate output that can be incorporated
directly into technical reports and publications. Rather than emphasizing low-level proof objects
or code-like proof scripts, Prove-It is intended to produce proofs that are easy for domain experts
to inspect and that closely reflect conventional mathematical presentation.

For this project, Prove-It was used to establish a small number of theorems that are central to
the validity of our detector-error-model-based performance estimation framework. These proofs
provide higher confidence that the logical structure of the method is correct, especially in places
where the accounting of fault pathways and logical outcomes is subtle. Although the system is still
under active development and is not yet comparable in maturity to major proof assistants such as
Coq or Lean, it offers a convenient and expressive environment for developing formal arguments
in a form that remains accessible to readers.

2.2. BUFILO-generating graphs to enable logical qubit failure estimation

2.2.1. BUFILOs and malignant sets

A BUFILO is a Bad Undetectable Fault-Induced Logical Operator, essentially a set 𝑒 of faults such
that 𝐻 (𝑒) = ∅ while 𝐴ℓ (𝑒) = 1, for some ℓ inducing a logical error across the QEC system.

A malignant set 𝑚 is a set of faults that causes a QEC system to suffer a logical failure, i.e., 𝑚 is a set
of faults that cause the decoder to apply an incorrect correction operator 𝑐 (as a set of faults in our
DEM) such that 𝐻 (𝑚△𝑐) = ∅ while 𝐴ℓ (𝑚△𝑐) ≠ 0 (where △ is the symmetric difference operator).
The correction is trivially dictated by the syndrome up to the logical observables in accordance
with conventions of the DEM (specifically the chosen generators of the logical observables). For a
min-weight decoder, a malignant set must contain a non-minority portion of a BUFILO, as captured
in Theorem 1 below.

2.2.3. Counting subpath fault sequences of 𝐺

For the purpose of making performance estimates, either doing importance sampling with a decoder
or estimating the performance given a hypothetical min-weight decoder, we will need to sample
BUFILO subsets with known sampling probabilities. We would also like to minimize sampling

2.3. Logical qubit failure estimation

2.3.1. What is the HERD technique?

The Half-Error Restricted Distribution (HERD) technique is based on Theorem 1; we can sample
malignant sets by sampling supersets of half-weight BUFILO subsets. More precisely, to sample a
potential malignant set, we sample a weight-𝑤 s subset of any BUFILO up to weight (2𝑤 s ) and then
generate any superset of that. There is no need to sample any higher-weight BUFILO subset since
we account for these automatically in considering supersets of our BUFILO subset sample.

2.3.4. Determining malignancy

In relation to Eq. (2.12), here we discuss determining 𝑓 (𝑥).
We may feed the sample to a decoder to determine 𝑓 (𝑥). Without a decoder, we may choose
properties of the hypothetical decoder as
 • Ideal (maximum likelihood)
 • Most-likely error decoder
 • Quick, pessimistic estimation (may include some errors that could be corrected by any
 min-weight decoder)
For simplicity and efficiency with decoder-free performance estimation, we may use a pessimistic
assumption that the superset of any malignant set is also malignant. This does introduce some
pessimism because, for example, it is possible to form a superset that contains a stabilizer, a set
of faults whose syndrome is empty, whereupon removal of the inconsequential stabilizer results
in something lower weight and non-malignant. But this is negligible in the limit of low error
probability.
For either a hypothesized maximum likelihood decoder or a most-likely error decoder, we determine
malignancy using Query 4. We use Query 4 to find all of the complements of our candidate
malignant set; call this set 𝐵. Then pick one of those complements and use Query 4 again to find
its complements designated as set 𝐴. Note that the original candidate must be in set 𝐴. For an ideal
maximum likelihood decoder, we deem the candidate to be malignant if the sum of probabilities
of the errors in 𝐴 is larger than those of 𝐵. For a most-likely error decoder, we base this off of the
maximum probability rather than the sum. As a consistent tie-breaker, we can decide whether fault
sets that flip the ℓ logical observable are taken to be malignant by default.
For the quick, pessimistic estimation, we only use the tie-breaker to determine malignancy. This
can be pessimistic even in the limit of low error probability; there may some errors that could be
corrected by any min-weight decoder because there could be an error in set 𝐴, but not in 𝐵, that
has lower weight than the candidate. In that case, the candidate is not malignant to a min-weight
decoder because it belongs to the class of errors of lower weight. But unless we determine the set
𝐴, via Query 4 which is expensive relative to Queries 1-3, we won’t know this.
It may be possible to mitigate the expense of Query 4 by modify it to return only the sum or
maximum of the probabilities of the complements rather than yielding them individually. The
caveat pertains to mutual-exclusivity of faults. In a DEM with no mutual-exclusivity, it should

3.1.2. Modeling with leakage

Leakage adds many complications, but it is possible to study appropriate models that include
leakage within our framework. A Pauli+leakage model may be used to construct a DEM by
ascribing multiple faults to a particular leakage event that account for each relevant combination of
detector effects that may arise from the leakage. The combinatorics is manageable for models of
quantum circuits that regularly swap data and ancilla qubits [12] or apply leakage reduction and/or
detection gates.
In our approach, we constrain leakage faults associated with the same leaked qubit(s) to be mutually
exclusive, and we include false-negative leakage-detection events in this constraint. Such false

Our work on modeling leakage in a full circuit model is preliminary. We have all of the building

capability to handle them with our HERD technique. But we have not performed sufficient testing
and debugging to present results. We have, however, performed brute-force tests on quasi-circuit
models which effect one round of imperfect syndrome extraction with ancilla qubits that may leak
followed by a perfect round of syndrome extraction. Our brute-force tests have ensured that our
HERD technique for this model generates all malignant sets up to weight 4 for 𝑑 = 5 (as well as
malignant sets up to weight 3 for 𝑑 = 3).

In this model, a leaked qubit may randomly spread the same type of faults as the check (an 𝑋-check
may spread 𝑋 errors and a 𝑍-check may spread 𝑍 errors). The is the same type of error spreading
that may occur from Pauli errors in a standard circuit model, but the difference is that this may
spread to any combination of data qubits rather than every data qubit touched after the ancilla error.

that were illustrated in Fig. 2-3. The code distance is still preserved, however, with the inclusion
of leakage detection. A BUFILO must leave no leakage detectors remaining in the syndrome.
Therefore, detected leakage faults in a BUFILO must be doubled-up locally to the leaky syndrome
check which provides protection against hook errors. Furthermore, we regard undetected leakage
faults, due to a failure of a leakage detection measurement, as weight-2 faults.

3.3. Node-finding algorithm

In order to construct a concrete and practical BUFILO-generating graph, we must determine which

below. Such an ℎ(𝑛) ≤ 𝑑 (𝑛, 𝑡) is known as an admissible heuristic in pathfinding literature [15].
We describe the algorithm below and present pseudocode as Algorithm 1, with Algorithms 2 and
Algorithms 3 as subroutines.

Integer-cost bounded uniform-cost search

Our algorithm is a bounded uniform-cost search (UCS) variant in the A* search framework [15]
with an admissible heuristic function. Exploiting the small integer range of the cost function (up
to 𝑤 BUF ), we implement it with a Dial-style bucket queue [8]. It is designed for very large implicit
directed graphs in which nodes and edges are not retained globally in memory. The goal is to return
exactly the set of nodes 𝑛 satisfying

3.4. Formal proofs

Formal theorem proving was used to increase confidence in the mathematical claims that are most
central to the HERD method. In particular, the completed proofs address two questions that directly
affect the validity of the sampling framework: first, why BUFILO subsets are sufficient to target
malignant sets for a minimum-weight decoder, and second, why the graph construction based on the
choice heuristic 𝜈 does not omit the irreducible BUFILOs that the method is intended to sample.

The proof artifacts were constructed in Prove-It. As discussed in Sec. 1.2, these proofs should
be understood as machine-checked proofs relative to the current Prove-It theory packages and
definitions, not as reductions all the way to foundational set-theoretic axioms. In particular,
some standard facts about sets, weights, and symmetric differences are taken as axioms. The proofs
verify mathematical statements, not the correctness of the current software implementation. App. B
provides additional detail on the completed proofs.

We have (nearly) completed formal proofs of the following.

 This result is important because the graph construction does not include all possible fault

 which is the definition of an ℓ-BUFILO. We prove this through induction. For any given
 BUFILO and a partition of the BUFILO into realized faults from START to some node 𝑠 and
 remaining faults which must be realized in going from 𝑠 to the END, we are guaranteed that
 any given observable of node 𝑠 (chosen by 𝜈) must be flipped by at least one of the faults

 from 𝑠 to the ∅. The proof shows that this 𝜈 restriction, for any choice function 𝜈, is without
 loss for irreducible BUFILOs: since a BUFILO must flip ℓ without leaving any detectors
 in its syndrome, any observable in an intermediate node from {ℓ} to ∅ must be deactivated
 by some remaining fault, and the faults can be ordered so that the detector chosen by 𝜈 is
 deactivated next. Further details are provided in App. B.2.
The first has been proven (up to a reasonable set of non-contentious facts), and the second one is

4. ANTICIPATED OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS

4.1. Rapid logical performance assessment

This work provides a framework for rapidly estimating logical failure probabilities from a detector
error model, with or without an explicit decoder. Such capability is useful both for characterizing the
performance of a known decoding strategy and for assessing the potential impact of a hypothetical
decoder whose properties are specified in advance. In practice, these estimates can serve as
a computationally efficient alternative to brute-force circuit-level Monte Carlo simulation when
logical failures are rare.

4.1.1. Importance sampling with a decoder

Rare-event simulation methods [3, 5, 23] are designed to improve on naive Monte Carlo by focusing
sampling effort on fault configurations that are more likely to contribute to logical failure. A
commonly used strategy is splitting: one begins at a comparatively high physical error rate,
estimates the rare-event probability there, and then progressively “bootstraps” the estimate toward
lower error rates through a sequence of intermediate distributions. This can be effective, but it
also introduces practical challenges. In particular, the sampling process can become trapped in a
limited region of configuration space, so that many restarts or large numbers of samples may be
required to obtain adequate coverage. As the target error rate is pushed lower, the overall procedure
can also become less reliable, since the rare-event estimate is built indirectly through a chain of
intermediate steps rather than obtained in a single low-noise calculation.
By contrast, the approach developed here is designed specifically for the low-error regime. It
directly samples low-weight malignant sets with known and controlled biases, avoiding the need
for bootstrap-style continuation from higher error probabilities. This makes the method especially
well suited to regimes in which logical failures are dominated by a small number of low-weight fault
combinations—precisely the regime of primary interest for many fault-tolerant quantum technolo-
gies. In this setting, our method can provide efficient and statistically meaningful estimates without
requiring the repeated staging and reweighting typical of splitting-based rare-event simulation.
This focus also explains the complementary domain of applicability. As physical error probabilities
increase, malignant sets of larger weight contribute more substantially to logical failure, and
accounting accurately for that broader space becomes more difficult in our framework. In that
sense, the present method is not intended as a universal replacement for rare-event Monte Carlo.
Rather, it fills an important niche: it is tailored to the low-error, high-reliability regime where
future quantum architectures will ultimately need to operate, and where direct access to the most
dangerous fault patterns is especially valuable.

4.1.2. Explorations without a decoder

Decoder development remains an active area, while quantum hardware and architectural design
choices continue to mature. For this reason, it is often valuable to assess the performance limits
of a quantum error-correcting architecture before a production-quality decoder is available. Such
decoder-independent analysis can support early-stage studies of candidate systems by clarifying
what level of logical suppression is achievable in principle under a specified detector error model.
This perspective is especially useful when exploring whether an architecture is worth further
decoder investment. If a decoder-independent estimate already indicates that logical failure is
likely to be prohibitive, that result can motivate changes in the architecture, noise model, or code
choice. Conversely, if the underlying detector error model suggests strong intrinsic suppression,
that can justify further investment in decoder design and implementation. In either case, the analysis
provides actionable guidance for research and development planning.
The ability to estimate logical performance directly from the detector error model is also valuable
in settings where specialized decoders are difficult to construct or benchmark, including cases with
leakage noise or other non-Pauli processes. Similarly, for high-rate quantum low-density parity-
check (𝑞LDPC) codes, where message-passing decoders are often state of the art but may still fall
short of minimum-weight or maximum-likelihood decoding [27], decoder-independent estimates
can help distinguish intrinsic code performance from decoder limitations. Although we do not
yet generalize HERD to the class of high-encoding-rate 𝑞LDPC codes, the present framework
establishes a foundation for future work in that direction.

4.2. Setting a standard for formal proofs in reports and papers

Informal proof sketches are often sufficient for communicating high-level ideas, but they can omit
boundary cases, hidden assumptions, or intermediate steps that are important for correctness.
Formal proof methods address this limitation by making the relevant definitions, hypotheses,
and dependencies explicit and by providing a machine-checked derivation of each claim from
previously accepted facts. In this way, formal proofs can improve both rigor and transparency,
while also making results easier to review, reproduce, and extend.
For this project, formal theorem proving was particularly valuable because the HERD approach
depends on nontrivial claims about fault sets, graph-based constructions, and sampling procedures.
These claims are central to the interpretation of the resulting logical-failure estimates, so having
them checked in a formal system provides additional confidence in the validity of the method. Even
when a proof is not reduced all the way to foundational set-theoretic axioms, formal verification
still offers a meaningful layer of assurance beyond a conventional handwritten derivation.
A further advantage of Prove-It is that it produces human-readable LaTeX proofs that resemble
standard mathematical exposition. This makes it easier for readers to follow the logical structure of
an argument and to compare formal derivations with the informal reasoning typically presented in
technical reports and journal articles. Prove-It is also flexible in how proofs are constructed: one
can develop arguments in an order that mirrors the way mathematicians naturally reason, including

the use of intermediate lemmas and conjectured statements that are later discharged formally. This
combination of readability and proof-construction flexibility makes the system particularly well
suited to scientific communication, where the goal is not only to verify a result but also to present

More broadly, tools like Prove-It suggest a path toward a stronger standard for proofs in technical
reports and papers. Rather than treating formal verification as separate from scientific exposition, it

5. CONCLUSION

HERD provides a practical and mathematically grounded approach for estimating logical fail-
ure probabilities in quantum error correction. By identifying BUFILOs through an efficiently
constructed graph and sampling malignant sets with controlled bias, the method targets the fault
configurations most relevant to logical failure while avoiding the cost of brute-force simulation. This
makes HERD especially valuable in the low-error regime where utility-scale logical performance
estimates are most difficult to obtain.
The report demonstrates the method on rotated surface codes under standard circuit noise and
preliminary leakage models, showing that HERD can support both decoder-specific and decoder-
independent analyses. Beyond performance estimation, HERD can also provide useful QEC
insights by revealing which fault patterns, code structures, and circuit features dominate logical
failure behavior. This can help guide code comparison, decoder development, and architectural
trade studies. The use of formal proofs further strengthens the credibility of the framework by
validating key theoretical claims about malignant sets and graph completeness. These results
suggest that HERD can serve as an effective tool for code assessment, decoder evaluation, and
early-stage architectural analysis.
Future work will focus on extending the method to broader code families, improving treatment of
leakage and other non-Pauli noise processes, and completing additional formal proofs that support
the implementation. More generally, HERD establishes a promising foundation for scalable and
trustworthy logical-performance estimation in fault-tolerant quantum computing.

B.1. A malignant set of a min-weight decoder must contain a BUFILO subset

This appendix discusses the Prove-It proof of Theorem 1, which states that, for a minimum-weight
decoder, every malignant set contains a non-minority subset of an irreducible BUFILO:

Here mals is the set of malignant sets, 𝑖bufs is the set of irreducible BUFILOs, and 𝑤(·) is a

The proof is based on the characterization of malignant sets given in Equation 2.2:

The mathematical argument is short. Fix an arbitrary malignant set 𝑚 ∈ mals. By the malignant-set

 𝑤(𝑐) ≤ 𝑤(𝑚) and 𝑚Δ𝑐 = 𝑏.

where the minus symbol is set difference. Then 𝑚′ ⊆ 𝑚 immediately. Also,

                                   𝑚Δ𝑐 = (𝑚 − 𝑐) ∪ (𝑐 − 𝑚),
