Sharp asymptotic behaviour of symmetric and non-symmetric solutions of the Heat Equation in the Hyperbolic Space
J. A. Cañizo, A. Gárriz & D.A. Marín

Abstract
In this work we study the large-time behaviour of solutions of the Heat Equation in the hyperbolic space ℍ d \mathbb{H}^{d} , providing precise speeds of convergence in L 1 L^{1} and L ∞ L^{\infty} to their asymptotic profiles by means of an adaptation of entropy estimates. For L 1 L^{1} initial conditions we are able to identify the asymptotic profile in L 1 L^{1} , which is not universal but contains a certain memory of the initial distribution of the mass of the solution. We improve thus on previous results, where speed of convergence was absent and asymptotic profiles where not known in the general case, and show a way to adapt entropy estimates employed in the study of diffusion processes to non-compact Riemannian manifolds. The main strategy to prove this is to consider transient profiles as minimizers of the entropy functional. These profiles are time-dependent and encompass the geometric information of the Riemannian manifold.



Keywords:
Heat Equation, Hyperbolic Space, Asymptotic behaviour, Entropy Estimates.

Introduction

The goal of this article is to study the behaviour for large times of positive solutions of the heat equation

\begin{cases}u_{t}=\Delta_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}u,\quad&x\in\mathbb{H}^{d},t>0,\\
u(0,\cdot)=u_{0},&x\in\mathbb{H}^{d}\end{cases}

(1)

in the hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{d}
, where
d\geq 2
represents the dimension of the space, for several different families of initial data. As we will see in this introduction, solutions to this equation present many distinctive and unexpected features that set them apart from their Euclidean counterparts and that introduce the need of several modifications on the classical techniques in order to study their large-time behaviour.

We will focus on two types of solutions of (
1
). Those whose initial datum presents a special symmetry and, as a consequence, the solutions will depend only on one variable (as, for example, when
u_{0}
is radially symmetric); and those whose initial datum only satisfies
u_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})
. Note that the definition of
L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})
has to take into account the geometry of the space through the differential of the measure, also known as the Jacobian or the volume form, which we will denote by
d\mu
. If
u
presents some special symmetry and depends only on one variable we will work with the classic concept of solutions of one-dimensional PDEs of second order. If, on the other hand, we work in the case where
u_{0}
is integrable, then our concept of solutions will be the one constructed by the semi-group approach; a unique solution
u(t,x)
of the heat equation with such initial data exists in that class and, moreover,
u\in C([0,\infty):L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d}))
and
u\in C^{\infty}((0,\infty)\times H^{d})
, see to that respect
[
13
,
18
]
.

Specifically, the families of initial data
u_{0}(x)
that we are going to work with are:

-

Those which depend only on the distance between
x
and a certain pole
\mathcal{O}
and
u_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+};\,\mathrm{d}\mu)
. We will refer to this case as
radially symmetric
or
radial
initial data and solutions.

-

Those which depend only on the (signed) distance between
x
and a certain horosphere
\mathcal{H}
, and
u_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R};\,\mathrm{d}\mu)
. We will refer to this as
horospheric
, integrable initial data and solutions. A horosphere is an interesting geometrical object present in
\mathbb{H}^{d}
but inexistent in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that will be defined in Section
1.1.2
.

-

Those that satisfy, simply,
u_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d};\,\mathrm{d}\mu)
. Note that integrable radial solutions are inside this family. We treat the radial solutions separately because they serve as a rich toy model and first step before treating the more general (and complex) case of only integrable solutions. Horospheric solutions are, on the other hand, clearly not in this class of integrable solutions (unless they are trivial).

To our knowledge, the first study of the behaviour for large times of the solutions of (
1
) is due to J.L. Vázquez
[
21
]
. In his work the author proves convergence of radial solutions to the fundamental solution of the equation
P_{d}(t,x)
. In particular, the author proves convergence in
L^{1}
without speed, i.e., if
\mathcal{M}
represents the mass of
u_{0}
(for whatever this concept of
mass
means for now), then

\|u(t,x)-\mathcal{M}P_{d}(t,x)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})}\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad t\to\infty,

and, as a consequence of the
L^{1}-L^{\infty}
smoothing effect,

\|u(t,x)-\mathcal{M}P_{d}(t,x)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{d})}=O(t^{-\frac{3}{2}}e^{-\lambda_{1}t}),

where
\lambda_{1}
represents the first eigenvalue of the Laplace-Beltrami operator in
\mathbb{H}^{d}
, so

\lambda_{1}:=\frac{(d-1)^{2}}{4}.

(2)

Other convergence results, this time for
horospheric solutions
(which will be defined later), are also included in
[
21
]
.

But the author does not stop at these positive results. It is also proven that, if the initial datum is not radially symmetric, then the solution does not converge to the fundamental solution. If
P^{y}_{d}(t,x)
represents the fundamental solution centred around a point
y\in\mathbb{H}^{d}
, then

\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\left[t^{\frac{3}{2}}e^{\lambda_{1}}|u(t,x)-\mathcal{M}P^{y}_{d}(t,x)|\right]=Q^{y}(x)>0\quad\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{H}^{d},\text{ for any }y\in\mathbb{H}^{d},

for a certain positive function
Q^{y}(x)
which depends also on the initial data. This result stands in sharp contrast with the Euclidean case, where there is in fact convergence to the fundamental solution centred around the centre of mass of the initial datum, as long as
u_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Very recently, E. Papageorgiou improved significantly on those results in
[
16
]
, following her work with Anker and Zhang in
[
3
]
, by providing the asymptotic profile of non-radially symmetric solutions in
L^{p}
, although once again without speed of convergence. Using tools from geometric harmonic analysis she proved the following. Due to the geometry of the space, the domain where most of the
L^{p}
mass of the fundamental solution is located (referred as
L^{p}
critical region
) varies a lot depending on whether
1\leq p<2
(an annulus at distance
(d-1)(\frac{2}{p}-1)t
from the origin),
p=2
(an annulus at distance of order
\sqrt{t}
from the origin), or
2<p\leq\infty
(a ball centred at the origin of radius approx.
\sqrt{t}
); the reader is invited to consider the explicit formula of the kernel in dimension
d=3
that can be found, for example, in
[
12
]
, and verify this very interesting phenomenon. This affects the asymptotics of the quotient of two heat kernels located at different points (which, as we will see, is fundamental in the study of the large-time behaviour of non-radially symmetric solutions), and as a consequence the
L^{p}
convergence is modified accordingly. Using the polar coordinates for
\mathbb{H}^{d}
, where
x=(r_{x},\theta_{x})
,
y=(r_{y},\theta_{y})
with
r_{x},r_{y}\geq 0,\ \theta_{x},\theta_{y}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
(see Section
1.1.1
thereafter), let

\Phi_{p}(x)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}u_{0}(y)\,\left[\cosh(r_{y})-\sinh(r_{y})\cdot\langle\theta_{x},\theta_{y}\rangle\right]^{-\frac{d-1}{p}}\ \,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\quad&\text{when}\quad 1\leq p\leq 2,\\[20.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathcal{S}_{0}(\dist(x,O))}\int_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}u_{0}(y)\mathcal{S}_{0}(\dist(x,y))\ \,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)&\text{when}\quad 2<p,\end{cases}

(3)

where
O
stands for the origin point,
\dist
goes for the hyperbolic distance and
\mathcal{S}_{0}
is the
elementary spherical function of index 0
employed when computing the Fourier transform; its specific formula can be found in
[
16
, Appendix]
. A sufficient condition for
\Phi_{p}
to be finite is that

\begin{cases}\Phi_{p}(x)\leq\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}u_{0}(y)\,e^{\frac{d-1}{p}r_{y}}\ \,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)<\infty\quad&\text{when}\quad 1\leq p\leq 2,\\[20.0pt]
\Phi_{p}(x)\leq\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}u_{0}(y)\,e^{\frac{d-1}{2}r_{y}}\ \,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)<\infty&\text{when}\quad 2<p,\end{cases}

(4)

and when this is satisfied Papageorgiou proves that, for all
p\geq 1
and for compactly supported initial data,

(\|P_{d}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{H}^{d})})^{-1}\cdot\|u(t,x)-\Phi_{p}(x)P_{d}(t,x)\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{H}^{d})}\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad t\to\infty.

(5)

We highlight that in the case
1\leq p\leq 2
, if we consider polar coordinates, the function
\Phi_{p}
depends only on the spherical coordinate
\theta_{x}
, and that condition (
4
), while sufficient, may not be necessary for (
5
), as we will see. Also, most notably, Papageorgiou proves such convergence results not only on the Hyperbolic space, but on
arbitrary-rank Riemannian symmetric spaces of non-compact-type
G/K
for (possibly) non-bi-
K
invariant initial data (which means, in our particular case, not radially symmetric data).

Remark 1.1
.

We find that the techniques coming from geometric harmonic analysis are somewhat distant to those of PDEs, and as such we dedicate some pages of this work to briefly introduce independent techniques that provide similar results to hers and complement them, with a language more natural to readers with a background on PDEs.

In our present work we improve on the aforementioned results by providing a speed of convergence of the
L^{1}
norm of the difference between
u
and its asymptotic profile of the form

\|u(t,x)-F(t,x)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})}\leq h(t),\quad\text{where}\quad h(t)\to 0\text{ as }t\to\infty

for certain asymptotic profile
F
and convergence speed
h
, for a more general class of initial data than
[
16
]
. In the case of radial solutions this will imply also a speed of convergence in
L^{\infty}
by means of the
L^{1}-L^{\infty}
smoothing effect and in all other
L^{p}
norms by interpolation; this direct improvement does not apply in the general case of non-radial solutions. We will also improve those results by obtaining a speed of convergence of horospheric solutions (covered partially in
[
21
]
and not included in
[
16
]
). Finally, our approach has the advantage of deriving our results with PDEs tools, avoiding most of the geommetric harmonic analysis techniques employed by Anker, Papageorgiou and Zhang, presenting all of them under a unified framework, with a strong enough technique to tackle all the cases (radial, horosopheric or integrable solutions) similarly.

The aforementioned unified framework is that of entropy estimates. We will employ a refinement of these ideas, of which an early presentation can be found in
[
5
,
19
,
20
]
, adapted to work in
\mathbb{H}^{d}
, a Riemannian manifold whose particular geometry makes it so the classical theory does not apply directly; up to our knowledge, this is the first time that such ideas are employed in non-compact Riemannian manifolds with non-trivial curvature. As we will see, the Riemannian metric of the space (and, in particular, the differential of the measure of this space) will play a fundamental role in our computations. Since some previous definitions about the coordinate system for
\mathbb{H}^{d}
are needed, we postpone the introduction of these ideas to later in this section. For an introductory bibliography on entropy estimates applied to diffusion processes we mention
[
4
,
22
]
. This technique has been employed fruitfully to other diffusion processes, like the Porous Medium Equation
[
9
]
, the Fast Diffusion Equation
[
7
]
, the Fractional Heat Equation
[
11
]
, or the Heat Equation in exterior domains
[
8
]
, just to name a few.

Since this article is, in principle, oriented to readers closer to the world of PDEs, we will devote some time to introduce, briefly, the geometric concepts needed in this work.

1.1
Introduction to the Geometry of the space
\mathbb{H}^{d}

To describe points
x\in\mathbb{H}^{d}
, one first needs to introduce a coordinate system. Unlike in Euclidean space, there is no universally accepted “canonical” coordinate system in
\mathbb{H}^{d}
; instead, several different models are available to represent hyperbolic spaces. In this work, we focus on coordinate systems that are best suited to capture the geometric symmetries inherent in
\mathbb{H}^{d}
.

We say that an isometry of
\mathbb{H}^{d}
is
elliptic
when it fix all geodesic spheres centered at some point, and we say that it is
parabolic
when it fix a special class of hypersurfaces of
\mathbb{H}^{d}
, called
horospheres
. To deal with functions which are invariant under one of these two groups of isometries we use coordinates in which one of the parameters is the distance to a given sphere or horosphere. These kind of coordinates are a special case of a general construction, called
Fermi coordinates
. In Section
A.1
of the appendix we provide an introduction of the construction of such coordinate systems for the interested readers. We now present the two coordinate systems we will use along this paper.

1.1.1
Polar coordinates, also called elliptic coordinates

We can describe the group of elliptic isometries as the subgroup of
\textup{Iso}(\mathbb{H}^{d})
whose principal orbits are given by geodesic spheres centred at some point.

Given
o\in\mathbb{H}^{d}
and
r_{0}>0
, we define the geodesic ball centred in
o
and with radius
r_{0}>0
as the set
B(o,r_{0})=\{x\in\mathbb{H}^{d}~\colon~\textup{dist}(o,x)<r_{0}\}
, and the corresponding geodesic sphere as
S(o,r_{0}):=\partial B(o,r_{0})
. Take
N
to be the normal vector field on
S(o,r_{0})
that points outside
B(o,r_{0})
. Then, since each geodesic sphere is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, by taking a coordinate neighbourhood centred at
S(o,r_{0})
with respect to
N
, we get that
\mathbb{H}^{d}\setminus\{o\}
is diffeomorphic to
(-r_{0},+\infty)\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and the hyperbolic metric takes the form

g_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}=\,\mathrm{d}r^{2}+\sinh^{2}(r+r_{0})g_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}

in these coordinates, where
g_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
is the round metric in the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.

By taking
r_{0}\to 0
we get the so called
polar coordinates
in
\mathbb{H}^{d}
, which have the following interpretation: for any
x\neq o
, we describe the point by its distance to
o
,
r=\textup{dist}_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}(o,x)
, and the direction
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\equiv T_{o}\mathbb{H}^{d-1}
such that
\textup{exp}_{o}(r\theta)=x
. In this case,
\mathbb{H}^{d}\setminus\{o\}
is diffeomorphic to
(0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and the metric is given by

g_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}=\,\mathrm{d}r^{2}+\sinh^{2}(r)g_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}

in these coordinates. Also, the Laplace-Beltrami operator reads

\Delta_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}u=\frac{1}{\sinh^{d-1}(r)}\partial_{r}\left(\sinh^{d-1}(r)\cdot\partial_{r}u\right)+\frac{1}{\sinh^{2}(r)}\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}u

(6)

and the volume form is given by
\,\mathrm{d}\mu(r,\theta)=\sinh^{d-1}(r)\,\mathrm{d}r\,\mathrm{d}\theta
, where
\,\mathrm{d}\theta
denotes the volume form of the standard round metric in
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.

1.1.2
Parabolic coordinates, also called horospheric coordinates

Let
o\in\mathbb{H}^{d}
and take
\gamma:[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{H}^{d}
a geodesic ray starting at
o
and parametrised by the arc length, meaning that
\textup{dist}(o,\gamma(t))=t
for any
t\in[0,+\infty)
. Then the set given by the limit

\mathcal{H}B=\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}B(\gamma(t),t)

is an open set of
\mathbb{H}^{d}
called a
horoball
. Its boundary
\mathcal{H}=\partial\mathcal{H}B
is a smooth hypersurface which is referred to as a horosphere. Horospheres are invariant under the group of parabolic isometries of
\mathbb{H}^{d}
, and can be thought as spheres centered at some point of the ideal boundary of
\mathbb{H}^{d}
(see for example
[
17
]
for a description of this set).

Fix a horosphere
\mathcal{H}\subset\mathbb{H}^{d}
, and denote by
\mathcal{H}B

the horoball it bounds. Let
N
be the unit normal vector field pointing toward
the complement
\mathbb{H}^{d}\setminus\mathcal{H}B
. With this convention,
the signed distance to
\mathcal{H}
is positive outside
\mathcal{H}B

and negative inside. Since every horosphere is isometric to the euclidean space

\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
, the corresponding Fermi coordinate neighborhood provides
a parametrization of
\mathbb{H}^{d}
by
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
,
in which the hyperbolic metric takes the form

g_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}=\,\mathrm{d}r^{2}+e^{2r}g_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}.

With respect to this coordinate system, the volume form of
\mathbb{H}^{d}
is
\,\mathrm{d}\mu(r,y)=e^{(d-1)r}\,\,\mathrm{d}r\,\,\mathrm{d}y,
where
\,\mathrm{d}y
denotes the Euclidean volume form on
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
. Moreover,
for a function
u
, the Laplace–Beltrami operator is given by

\Delta_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}u=\frac{1}{e^{(d-1)r}}\,\partial_{r}\!\left(e^{(d-1)r}\cdot\partial_{r}u\right)+\frac{1}{e^{2r}}\,\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}u.

(7)

Remark 1.2
.

There is a third possible coordinate system corresponding to the hyperbolic subgroup of
\text{Iso}(\mathbb{H}^{d})
that we do not cover in this article. Let
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathbb{H}^{d}
be a hyperplane. Then any
x\in\mathbb{H}^{d}
can be expressed as a point
w\in\mathcal{P}
and a signed distance
r\in\mathbb{R}
between
w
and its projection over
\mathcal{P}
, so
x=(w,r)
. This is often referred as employing
hyperbolic coordinates
. Since every totally geodesic hypersurface is isometric to
\mathbb{H}^{d-1}
, the hyperbolic metric in these coordinates takes the form

g_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}=\,\mathrm{d}r^{2}+\cosh^{2}(r)\,g_{\mathbb{H}^{d-1}}.

The ideas employed in this work are applicable,
mutatis mutandis
, to solutions that depend only on this distance
r
to a certain hyperplane
\mathcal{P}
; we can refer to such solutions as
planar
solutions. However, many more computations and auxiliary results are needed in order to study this case, and as a consequence we decided to exclude
planar
solutions from the present article and leave them for future work.

1.2
The heat kernel in hyperbolic space

Given any
y\in\mathbb{H}^{d}
, we will denote as
P^{y}_{d}(t,x)
the fundamental solution of equation (
1
) at time
t>0
and point
x\in\mathbb{H}^{d}
with initial datum
u_{0}(x)=\delta_{y}(x)
, Dirac’s Delta function centred at the point
y
. As in the euclidean case, any solution with integrable initial datum can be written as

u(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}u_{0}(y)P^{y}_{d}(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)

It is easy to verify a solution of the heat equation preserves its mass, i.e.,

\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})}=\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})}=\int_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}u_{0}(y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y).

Since the value of
P^{y}_{d}(t,x)
depends only on time and the distance between
x
and
y
, we will write

P^{y}_{d}(t,x)=G_{d}(t,\lambda(x,y)),\quad\text{where}\quad\lambda(x,y)=\text{dist}(x,y)

for a certain function
G_{d}
whose explicit formula can be found in
[
10
,
13
]
. For any
x\in\mathbb{H}^{d}
, write
x\equiv(r_{x},\theta_{x})
for its polar coordinates with respect to a fixed origin
o\in\mathbb{H}^{d}
, where we recall that
r_{x}=\dist(x,o)\geq 0
and
\theta_{x}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. Then, by the second hyperbolic law of the cosines, we have

\lambda(x,y)=\text{arccosh}(\cosh(r_{x})\cosh(r_{y})-\sinh(r_{x})\sinh(r_{y})\cdot\langle\theta_{x},\theta_{y}\rangle),

(8)

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the euclidean scalar product, and thus
\langle\theta_{x},\theta_{y}\rangle
is the cosine of the angle formed by the geodesics connecting the points
x
and
y
to the origin. If we call such angle
\vartheta
, then we have

\vartheta(\theta_{x},\theta_{y})=\arccos(\langle\theta_{x},\theta_{y}\rangle)

and then the formula for
\lambda
becomes

\lambda(x,y)=\text{arccosh}(\cosh(r_{x})\cosh(r_{y})-\sinh(r_{x})\sinh(r_{y})\cdot\cos(\vartheta)),

writing
\vartheta
instead of
\vartheta(\theta_{x},\theta_{y})
for simplicity.

The formula for the solution
u
becomes then

u(t,x)=u(t,r_{x},\theta_{x})=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}u_{0}(r_{y},\theta_{y})\cdot G_{d}(t,\lambda(x,y))\cdot\sinh^{d-1}(r_{y})\,\mathrm{d}r_{y}\,\mathrm{d}\theta_{y}.

There are, moreover, two notable relations regarding the function
G_{d}
, see for example
[
12
]
. First, if we define

h_{d}(t,r):=(4\pi t)^{-\frac{d}{2}}(1+r+t)^{\frac{d-3}{2}}(1+r)e^{-\frac{(r+(d-1)t)^{2}}{4t}}

then there exists a couple of positive constants
c_{d},C_{d}
such that, for all
t>0,r>0
,

c_{d}\ h_{d}(t,r)\leq G_{d}(t,r)\leq C_{d}\ h_{d}(t,r),

(9)

which improves to the complete asymptotics

G_{d}(t,r)\sim\gamma\left(\frac{r}{2t}\right)t^{-\frac{3}{2}}\ r\ e^{-\frac{(r+(d-1)t)^{2}}{4t}}

as
t\to\infty
and
r\to\infty
, where
\gamma
is a known function depending on the Gamma-function, see
[
2
]
and
[
16
, Appendix]
for the details.

Second, the derivative of
G_{d}
satisfies the formula

\partial_{r}G_{d}(t,r)=-2\pi e^{dt}\sinh(r)G_{d+2}(t,r).

(10)

1.3
Introduction to the Entropy Estimates

Let us discuss briefly the ideas behind the entropy estimates in polar coordinates. From the work of
[
21
]
(see also
[
15
, Appendix A]
) we see that the bulk of the mass of the heat kernel in
\mathbb{H}^{d}
centred around a point
\mathcal{O}
is located, at points
x=(r,\theta)
and at time
t
, at a distance
r=\text{dist}(\mathcal{O},x)=(d-1)t
. Based on this we define the change of variables

t(\tau)=e^{\tau}-1,\quad r(\tau,\rho)=(d-1)e^{\tau}+e^{\frac{\tau}{2}}\rho,

and, given a solution
u
of (
1
), consider the auxiliary function

v(\tau,\rho,\theta)=e^{\frac{\tau}{2}}\cdot\sinh^{d-1}(r(\tau,\rho))\cdot u(t(\tau),r(\tau,\rho),\theta).

This function
v
satisfies a certain parabolic equation. Now, we consider the adapted Kullback-Leibler entropy functional

H(\tau):=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\rho_{0}(\tau)}^{\infty}v\cdot\ln\left(\frac{v}{\mathcal{F}}\right)\ \,\mathrm{d}\rho\ \,\mathrm{d}\theta,

for a certain profile
\mathcal{F}(\tau,\rho,\theta)
that will play the role of the
equilibrium
towards
v
is converging; this is called the entropy of
v
relative to
\mathcal{F}
, or relative entropy, for brevity. The specific definition of
\mathcal{F}
depends on the characteristics of the problem, and will not be the same for radial, horospheric or integrable solutions. There are two noteworthy deviations from the classical entropy techniques in this case. First, there is an unusual dependence on
\tau
both on the limits of the integral and on the profile
\mathcal{F}
(called for this reason a
transient equilibrium
); nonetheless, the method holds with some extra steps, see
[
8
]
. Second, there is an unexpected factor
\sinh^{d-1}(r)
appearing in the definition of
v
; it corresponds to the volume form
\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\sinh^{d-1}(r)\,\mathrm{d}r\,\mathrm{d}\theta
. By including it here we bring, in the sense of integrals, the function
v
from the geometry of
\mathbb{H}^{d}
into the geometry of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where computations can be carried out. It is in this sense that we claim to adapt the entropy techniques to the frame of non-compact Riemannian manifolds.

Via some analysis, the goal is to prove a decay of the form

H(\tau)\leq(C+H(0))e^{-\lambda\tau}

for some positive constants
C,\lambda
. We often write indistinctively
H(\tau)
or
H(v)
depending on whether we want to emphasize the dependence on the function
v
or on the time
\tau
, and also
H(u_{0}),H(v_{0})
or
H(0)
depending on whether we want to emphasize the dependence on the initial datum
u_{0}
,
v_{0}=v(0,\rho,\theta)
or on the time
\tau=0
, but we are referring to the same quantities; the context should make it clear. With this decay at hand, the Csiszár-Kullback inequality yields

\|v-\mathcal{F}\|_{L^{1}}\leq\sqrt{C+H(0)}\cdot e^{-\frac{\lambda}{2}\tau},

and then, undoing the change of variables, we obtain convergence of
u
to a certain profile
\mathcal{F}^{*}
in the
L^{1}(\,\mathrm{d}\mu)
norm. In some cases, an
L^{1}-L^{\infty}
regularisation effect will even provide convergence in the
L^{\infty}
norm.

1.4
Main results

According to each of the two coordinate systems that we have presented there are symmetries on the initial datum that fits them, and these symmetries affect, of course, the large-time behaviour of the solutions. First, for the initial data that depends only on the distance to a certain point
\mathcal{O}\in\mathbb{H}^{d}
, the natural coordinates are the polar ones with pole at
\mathcal{O}
, where
x=(r_{x},\theta_{x})
, with
r_{x}\geq 0,\theta_{x}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. We recall that we write the heat kernel as

P_{d}(t,x)=G_{d}(t,r_{x}),

since it is a radial function centred around the pole
\mathcal{O}
that depends only on time and the distance
r_{x}
, where we write, for simplicity of notation,
P_{d}
for the fundamental solution centred at the origin
\mathcal{O}
. We recall the definition of
\lambda_{1}
as the bottom of the spectrum of the Laplace-Beltrami operator given in (
2
).

The following result is a consequence of Thorems
2.1
and
2.3
, in Section
2.1
.

Theorem 1.3

(Radially symmetric initial data)
.

Let the polar coordinates be such that a point
x\in\mathbb{H}^{d}
is given by
x=(r_{x},\theta_{x})
, where
r_{x}\geq 0
represents the hyperbolic distance between
x
and the pole
\mathcal{O}
and
\theta_{x}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
represents the direction of the unit vector from
\mathcal{O}
to
x
. Let
u_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})
such that
u_{0}\geq 0
and
u_{0}(x)=u_{0}(r_{x})
for all
x\in\mathbb{H}^{d}
. Then
u(t,x)=u(t,r_{x})
for all
t\geq 0
and there exists a
t_{0}
such that for all
t\geq t_{0}
,

\displaystyle\|u(t,\cdot)-\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{R}}P_{d}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})}

\displaystyle=\|u(t,r_{x})-\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{R}}G_{d}(t,r_{x})\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\sqrt{H^{*}(G_{d})}+\sqrt{H^{*}(u_{0})}\right)\cdot t^{-\frac{1}{2}},

where
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{R}}=\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})}
and
H^{*}(u_{0})=K(d,\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{R}})+H(u_{0})
, for some constant
K
that depends only on the dimension
d
and the mass
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{R}}
. Moreover, as a consequence of the
L^{1}-L^{\infty}
regularizing effect,

\displaystyle\|u(t,\cdot)-\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{R}}P_{d}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{d})}\lesssim\left(\sqrt{H^{*}(G_{d})}+\sqrt{H^{*}(u_{0})}\right)\cdot e^{-\lambda_{1}t}t^{-2}.

Next, when the initial datum depends only on the distance to a horosphere
\mathcal{H}
, then the same can be said about the solution, so we choose parabolic coordinates
x=(r_{x},y_{x})
, with
r_{x}\in\mathbb{R},y_{x}\in\mathcal{H}
and write
\,\mathrm{d}\nu(r)=e^{(d-1)r}\,\mathrm{d}r
for the horospherical part of the volume form of
\mathbb{H}^{d}
. We obtain the following theorem as a consequence of Theorems
2.4
and
2.5
, in Section
2.2
.

Theorem 1.4

(Horospheric initial data)
.

Let the horospheric coordinates be such that a point
x\in\mathbb{H}^{d}
is given by
x=(r_{x},y_{x})
, where
r_{x}\in\mathbb{R}
represents the signed hyperbolic distance between
x
and the horosphere
\mathcal{H}
and
y_{x}\in\mathcal{H}
represents the projection of
x
onto
\mathcal{H}
. Let
u_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R},\,\mathrm{d}\mu)
such that
u_{0}\geq 0
and
u_{0}(x)=u_{0}(r_{x})
for all
x\in\mathbb{H}^{d}
. Then
u(t,x)=u(t,r_{x})
for all
t\geq 0
and

\|u(t,r)-\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{H}}\ \Gamma(t,r+(d-1)t)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R},\,\mathrm{d}\nu)}\lesssim\sqrt{H(u_{0})}\cdot t^{-\frac{1}{2}}

where
\Gamma(t,r)
stands for the Gaussian profile

\Gamma(t,r)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}e^{-\frac{r_{x}^{2}}{4t}}

and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{H}}=\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R};\,\mathrm{d}\nu)}
. Moreover,

\|u(t,r)-\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{H}}\ \Gamma(t,r+(d-1)t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim\sqrt{H(u_{0})}\cdot t^{-1}.

However, these results leave behind many physically relevant cases where the initial datum does not have a particular symmetry to exploit. The main result of this article deals with this case. From the two coordinate systems presented in this article, the one best suited to describe solutions whose initial datum satisfies only
u_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})
is the polar coordinates system, where
x=(r_{x},\theta_{x})\in\mathbb{H}^{d}
with
r_{x}\geq 0
and
\theta_{x}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. Naturally, in this setting,
u_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})
means
u_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1};\,\mathrm{d}\mu)
with
\mu(r,\theta)=\sinh^{d-1}(r)

First, we need to introduce the “memory” function
\Phi(\theta_{x})
depending only on the initial datum
u_{0}
. The reader can think of it as the amount of mass of
u_{0}
in the direction
\theta_{x}
weighted accordingly to the function

\varphi(y,\theta_{x})=[\cosh(r_{y})-\sinh(r_{y})\cdot\left\langle\theta_{x},\theta_{y}\right\rangle]^{-(d-1)},

so

\Phi(\theta_{x})=\int_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}u_{0}(y)\,\varphi(y,\theta_{x})\ \,\mathrm{d}\mu(y).

For this memory function, we assume
\Phi(\theta_{x})\in L^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
. This assumption is enough to obtain convergence, see Theorem
3.2
. Notice that
\Phi
matches
\Phi_{1}
in (
3
), as it should. We arrive however at this quantity independently from
[
16
]
.

Second, in order to improve Theorem
3.2
by means of a speed of convergence, the initial datum has to satisfy a certain growth condition, namely

\sup\limits_{\theta_{x}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\left\{\int_{C}^{\infty}u_{0}(\theta_{x},r)\sinh^{d-1+\varepsilon}(r)\,\,\mathrm{d}r\right\}<\infty

(11)

for some fixed positive constants
C,\varepsilon
. This can be thought of as a sort of “momentum” of order
\varepsilon
; of course, since
u_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})
one must think about
\varepsilon<<1
. The specifics can be found in Lemma
4.4
. Notice that if
u_{0}
is compactly supported then condition (
11
) is trivially satisfied.

With this at hand, we can write down the main result of this article. We define for now
H^{*}(u_{0})
as a value depending only on the initial datum, the initial entropy
H(u_{0})
and the dimension
d
such that

H(u_{0})<H^{*}(u_{0}),\quad\text{and}\quad H^{*}(u_{0})<\infty\ \text{ if }\ H(u_{0})<\infty.

As a consequence of Theorem
4.7
in Section
4.3
we obtain the following.

Theorem 1.5

(
L^{1}
initial data)
.

Let the polar coordinates be such that a point
x\in\mathbb{H}^{d}
is given by
x=(r_{x},\theta_{x})
, where
r_{x}\geq 0
represents the hyperbolic distance between
x
and the pole
\mathcal{O}
and
\theta_{x}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
represents the direction of the unit vector from
\mathcal{O}
to
x
. Let
u_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})
such that
u_{0}\geq 0
,
\Phi(\theta_{x})\in L^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
. Then,

\|u(t,x)-C_{d}\ \Phi(\theta_{x})\ \Gamma(t,r_{x}+(d-1)t)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})}\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad t\to\infty,

where

C_{d}=\frac{2^{d-2}}{\sqrt{\pi}\cdot|\mathbb{S}^{d-1}|},

or, in terms of the heat kernel,

\|u(t,\cdot)-\Phi(\cdot)\ P_{d}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})}=\|u(t,x)-\Phi(\theta_{x})\ G_{d}(t,r_{x})\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})}\to 0\text{ as }t\to\infty.

If, in addition, condition (
11
) is satisfied, then there exists a time
t_{0}
such that, for all
t\geq t_{0}
,

\|u(t,x)-C_{d}\ \Phi(\theta_{x})\ \Gamma(t,r_{x}+(d-1)t)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})}\lesssim\sqrt{H^{*}(u_{0})}\cdot t^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

Equivalently, in terms of the heat kernel,

\displaystyle\|u(t,\cdot)-\Phi(\cdot)\ P_{d}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})}

\displaystyle=\|u(t,x)-\Phi(\theta_{x})\ G_{d}(t,r_{x})\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{H^{*}(u_{0})}\cdot t^{-\frac{1}{2}}\quad\text{for all}\quad t\geq t_{0}.

1.5
Organisation of the article

Section
2
is devoted to the study of the asymptotic behaviour of solutions presenting a certain symmetry, either radial (Section
2.1
) or horospheric (Section
2.2
); it can be regarded as the simpler cases, where the entropy estimates are introduced. Section
3
deals with the general case where
u_{0}
is nonnegative and integrable, introducing the “memory function”
\Phi(\theta)
and finding the asymptotic profile of the solutions but without a rate of convergence. It is in Section
4
where we obtain such rates via entropy estimates and a deep study of the directional mass distribution of the solution (Section
4.2
).

In the Appendix we collect several computations and auxiliary results that, for the sake of clarity, have been separated from the main text. Section
A.1
is a brief introduction to the derivation of coordinates systems for Riemannian manifolds for the interested readers. Section
A.2
is an addendum in which we present the auxiliary one-dimensional logarithmic Sobolev inequalities needed in this article to obtain the rates of convergence of the entropies. Section
A.3
is devoted to a useful bound for the radially symmetric equilibrium, and Section
A.4
provides, as an example, the computations on how to compute function
\varphi
, defined in Section
3
, in the case
d\in\{2,3\}
just with some analysis.

2
Convergence of symmetric solutions via entropy estimates

In this section we deal with solutions to (
1
) that present one of the symmetries mentioned in the introduction. The results presented here differ mainly from those contained in
[
21
]
and
[
3
]
in the fact that here we obtain convergence results with speed of convergence.

2.1
Radially symmetric solutions.

Given the operator (
6
), if we consider initial data that is radially symmetric then the solution stays radially symmetric, see
[
21
]
, and we can omit the spherical part, writing with a small abuse of notation

u(t,x)=u(t,r)\quad\text{for any}\quad\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\quad\Delta_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}u=\frac{1}{\sinh^{d-1}(r)}\partial_{r}\left(\sinh^{d-1}(r)\cdot\partial_{r}u\right).

Equation (
1
) then becomes one-dimensional and reads

\begin{cases}u_{t}=\frac{1}{\sinh^{d-1}(r)}\partial_{r}\left(\sinh^{d-1}(r)\cdot\partial_{r}u\right),\quad&r>0,t>0,\\
u(0,r)=u_{0}(r),&r>0.\end{cases}

(12)

Since the mass is preserved, let us define then, for the rest of this section,

\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{R}}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}u_{0}(r)\sinh^{d-1}(r)\ \,\mathrm{d}r,

implying that

\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})}=|\mathbb{S}^{d-1}|\cdot\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{R}}

Now, since we know that the bulk of mass of the solution is contained within a
\sqrt{t}
-neighborhood of the geodesic sphere
\{r=(d-1)t\}
(see
[
21
]
), it make sense to consider the change of variables

v(\tau,\rho):=e^{\frac{\tau}{2}}\cdot\sinh^{d-1}\big(r(\tau,\rho)\big)\cdot u\big(t(\tau),r(\tau,\rho)\big),

where

t(\tau)=e^{\tau}-1,\quad r(\tau,\rho)=(d-1)e^{\tau}+e^{\frac{\tau}{2}}\rho.

In this way, we locate our coordinates around the mass of our solution
u
to the heat equation. The factor
\sinh^{d-1}\big(r(\tau,\rho)\big)
is included to work with the function in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R},dr)
, instead of
L^{1}(\mathbb{R},\mu(r)dr)
.

Since
r\geq 0
we must have

\rho\geq\rho_{0}(\tau):=-(d-1)e^{\frac{\tau}{2}}

and thus

v(\tau,\rho_{0}(\tau))=e^{\frac{\tau}{2}}\cdot\sinh^{d-1}\big(\underbrace{r(\tau,\rho_{0}(\tau)}_{=0})\big)\cdot u\big(t(\tau),\underbrace{r(\tau,\rho_{0}(\tau)}_{=0}\big)=0

This function
v
also preserves the mass in its domain of definition, i.e.,

\int_{\rho_{0}(\tau)}^{\infty}v(\tau,\rho)\ \,\mathrm{d}\rho=\int_{0}^{\infty}v_{0}(\rho)\ \,\mathrm{d}\rho=\int_{0}^{\infty}u_{0}(r)\sinh^{d-1}(r)\ \,\mathrm{d}r=\mathcal{M}_{R}\quad\text{for all}\quad\tau\geq 0.

The previous definitions provide

r_{\tau}=(d-1)e^{\tau}+\frac{1}{2}e^{\frac{\tau}{2}}\rho,\quad r_{\rho}=e^{\frac{\tau}{2}},\quad\partial_{r}=e^{-\frac{\tau}{2}}\cdot\partial_{\rho},

(13)

and, as a consequence of the last relation,

u_{r}=e^{-\frac{\tau}{2}}u_{\rho}.

With the help of (
13
), we show that
v
solves the equation

\begin{cases}v_{\tau}=\partial_{\rho}\left(v_{\rho}+\left[(d-1)e^{\frac{\tau}{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\rho-(d-1)e^{\frac{\tau}{2}}\coth(r)\right]v\right),\quad&\rho\geq\rho_{0}(\tau),\ \tau>0,\\
v(\tau,\rho_{0}(\tau))=0,&\tau>0,\\
v(0,\cdot)=v_{0},&\rho\geq\rho_{0}(\tau).\end{cases}

(14)

We can calculate a transient equilibrium
V
of the previous equation by solving the ODE

V_{\rho}+\left[(d-1)e^{\frac{\tau}{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\rho-(d-1)e^{\frac{\tau}{2}}\coth(r)\right]V=0

leading, for an arbitrary function
C(\tau)
, to

V(\tau,\rho)=C(\tau)\cdot\sinh^{d-1}\big(r(\tau,\rho)\big)\cdot e^{-\frac{\left(\rho+2(d-1)e^{\frac{\tau}{2}}\right)^{2}}{4}}.

(15)

Then, recalling that
r=r(\tau,\rho)
and
t=t(\tau)=e^{\tau}-1
, we get in the original coordinates the function

V(t,r)=C(t)\cdot\sinh^{d-1}(r)\cdot e^{-\frac{\left(r+(d-1)(t+1)\right)^{2}}{4(t+1)}}.

Note that this coincides with the equilibrium considered by Vázquez in
[
21
, Theorem 5.1]
.

Up to this point,
C(t)
was arbitrary. Now we choose it in such a way that
V(t,r)
has the same mass as
u(t,r)
at each time, so we need to ask

\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{R}}=\int_{\rho_{0}(\tau)}^{\infty}V(\tau,\rho)\ \,\mathrm{d}\rho\quad\text{for all}\quad\tau>0.

(16)

Note that although
C(t)
defined in this way may not be constant. We can obtain the following estimates, whose proof is given in Appendix
A.3
.

-k_{d}\ e^{-m_{d}(t+1)}\leq\frac{\partial_{t}C(t)}{C(t)}\leq k_{d}\ e^{-m_{d}(t+1)},

(17)

where

m_{d}:=\min\left(d-1,\frac{(d-1)^{2}}{16}\right),

and

\frac{2^{d-2}\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{R}}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\leq C(t)\leq\frac{2^{d-2}\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{R}}}{(1-e^{-(d-1)(t+1)})^{d-1}\left[\sqrt{\pi}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(t+1)}}e^{-\frac{\lambda_{1}(t+1)}{4}}\right]}.

(18)

Joining everything together we can write problem (
14
) as

\begin{cases}v_{\tau}=\partial_{\rho}\left(v\cdot\partial_{\rho}\left[\ln\left(\frac{v}{V}\right)\right]\right),\quad&\rho\geq\rho_{0}(\tau),\ \tau>0,\\
v(\tau,\rho_{0}(\tau))=0,&\tau>0,\\
v(0,\cdot)=v_{0},&\rho\geq\rho_{0}(\tau).\end{cases}

with
V
defined as in (
15
). This leads to the definition of the entropy
H
of the solution
v
of the previous problem as

H(\tau):=\int_{\rho_{0}(\tau)}^{\infty}v\cdot\ln\left(\frac{v}{V}\right)\ \,\mathrm{d}\rho.

The next step is to obtain an estimate for the time derivative of
H(\tau)
. We can compute

\partial_{\tau}H(\tau)=-\rho_{0}^{\prime}(\tau)\left[v\ln\left(\frac{v}{V}\right)\right]_{\rho=\rho_{0}(\tau)}+\int_{\rho_{0}(\tau)}^{\infty}v_{\tau}-\int_{\rho_{0}(\tau)}^{\infty}v\cdot\frac{V_{\tau}}{V}+\int_{\rho_{0}(\tau)}^{\infty}v_{\tau}\cdot\ln\left(\frac{v}{V}\right).

Let us consider each term by separate. First, define

\gamma_{0}(\tau):=\lim\limits_{\rho\to\rho_{0}(\tau)}\frac{v}{V}=\lim\limits_{\rho\to\rho_{0}(\tau)}\left[C(\tau)e^{\frac{\tau}{2}}u(t(\tau),r(\tau,\rho))e^{\frac{\left(\rho+2(d-1)e^{\frac{\tau}{2}}\right)^{2}}{4}}\right]=C(t)e^{\frac{\tau}{2}}u(t(\tau),0)e^{\lambda_{1}e^{\tau}}.

Clearly, since
u(t,0)
is a positive finite quantity for every
t>0
, we must have that
0<\gamma_{0}(\tau)<\infty
, and therefore

\left[v\ln\left(\frac{v}{V}\right)\right]_{\rho=\rho_{0}(\tau)}=\left[\frac{v}{V}\cdot\ln\left(\frac{v}{V}\right)\cdot V\right]_{\rho=\rho_{0}(\tau)}=\gamma_{0}(\tau)\cdot\ln(\gamma_{0}(\tau))\cdot\underbrace{V(\tau,\rho_{0}(\tau))}_{=0}=0.

Next, the conservation of mass yields

0=\partial_{\tau}\int_{\rho_{0}(\tau)}^{\infty}v\ \,\mathrm{d}\rho=\int_{\rho_{0}(\tau)}^{\infty}v_{\tau}\ \,\mathrm{d}\rho-\rho_{0}^{\prime}(\tau)\underbrace{v(\tau,\rho_{0}(\tau))}_{=0}=\int_{\rho_{0}(\tau)}^{\infty}v_{\tau}\ \,\mathrm{d}\rho

and therefore

\int_{\rho_{0}(\tau)}^{\infty}v_{\tau}\ \,\mathrm{d}\rho=0.

Finally, we can compute, using (
17
),

\frac{V_{\tau}}{V}=\frac{\partial_{\tau}C(t(\tau))}{C(t(\tau))}+\underbrace{(d-1)\left((d-1)e^{\tau}+\frac{1}{2}e^{\frac{\tau}{2}}\rho\right)(\coth(r(\tau,\rho))-1)}_{\geq 0}\geq-k_{d}e^{\tau-m_{d}e^{\tau}}.

for all
\rho\geq\rho_{0}(\tau)
.

All of this together yields

\partial_{\tau}H(\tau)\leq k_{d}\ e^{\tau-m_{d}e^{\tau}}\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{R}}-\int_{\rho_{0}(\tau)}^{\infty}v\cdot\left|\partial_{\rho}\left[\ln\left(\frac{v}{V}\right)\right]\right|^{2},

implying, by the log-Sobolev inequality that can be found in Corollary
A.3
, that

\partial_{\tau}H(\tau)\leq k_{d}\ e^{\tau-m_{d}e^{\tau}}\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{R}}-H(\tau).

Thus, we obtain, thanks to Gronwall’s Lemma,

e^{\tau}H(\tau)-H(0)\leq k_{d}\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{R}}\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{2s-m_{d}e^{s}}\ \,\mathrm{d}s.

Let now
A(m_{d})
be such that

e^{2s-m_{d}e^{s}}\leq e^{s-\frac{m_{d}}{2}e^{s}}\quad\text{for all}\quad s\geq A(m_{d}).

Then,

\displaystyle e^{\tau}H(\tau)-H(0)

\displaystyle\leq k_{d}\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{R}}\left(\int_{0}^{A(m_{d})}e^{2s-m_{d}e^{s}}\ \,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{A(m_{d})}^{\tau}e^{s-\frac{m_{d}}{2}e^{s}}\ \,\mathrm{d}s\right)

\displaystyle\leq k_{d}\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{R}}\left(\tilde{A}(m_{d})+\frac{2}{m_{d}}e^{-\frac{m_{d}}{2}\tau}\right)\leq K(d,\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{R}})

for some constant
K(d,\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{R}})
. In total, we obtain

H(\tau)\leq\left(K(d,\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{R}})+H(0)\right)e^{-\tau}.

If we define

H^{*}(v_{0}):=K(d,\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{R}})+H(0)

then we get, thanks to the Csiszár-Kullback inequality,

\|v-V\|_{L^{1}([\rho_{0}(\tau),\infty))}\leq\sqrt{H^{*}(v_{0})}\cdot e^{-\frac{1}{2}\tau}.

Writing it down in terms of
u
(writing
H^{*}(u_{0})
instead of
H^{*}(v_{0})
) this reads

\int_{\rho_{0}(\tau)}^{\infty}\left|e^{\frac{\tau}{2}}u(t,r)-C(t)e^{-\frac{\left(r+(d-1)(t+1)\right)^{2}}{4(t+1)}}\right|\ \sinh^{d-1}(r)\ \,\mathrm{d}\rho\leq\sqrt{H^{*}(u_{0})}\cdot e^{-\frac{1}{2}\tau}.

Changing variables
\,\mathrm{d}\rho=e^{-\tau/2}\,\mathrm{d}r,t=e^{\tau}-1
,

\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|u(t,r)-C(t)\cdot\Gamma(t+1,r+(d-1)(t+1))\right|\ \sinh^{d-1}(r)\ \,\mathrm{d}r\leq\sqrt{H^{*}(u_{0})}\cdot(t+1)^{-\frac{1}{2}},

where
\Gamma(t,r)
stands for the Gaussian profile

\Gamma(t,r):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{4t}},\quad\forall r,t\in\mathbb{R}.

(19)

Since the hyperbolic heat kernel
G_{d}(t,r)
is a radial solution in on itself, the previous result still applies if we change
u
by
G_{d}
, although paying perhaps with a small time translation so we make sure the initial entropy of the kernel is finite . With a small abuse of notation, we can also write
H(G_{d})
for the initial entropy of the kernel, after performing the change of variables
t\to\tau
and
r\to\rho
, and we write
\mu(r)=\sinh(r)^{d-1}\,\mathrm{d}r
for the radial part of the volume form of
\mathbb{H}^{d}
. We have proven the following.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
u
be a solution of (
12
) such that
u_{0}(x)=u_{0}(r)
for all
x\in\mathbb{H}^{d}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{R}}<\infty
. Then

\|u(t,r)-C(t)\cdot\Gamma(t+1,r+(d-1)(t+1))\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+};\,\mathrm{d}\mu)}\leq\sqrt{H^{*}(u_{0})}\cdot(t+1)^{-\frac{1}{2}},

and, in particular,

\|\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{R}}G_{d}(t,r)-C(t)\cdot\Gamma(t+1,r+(d-1)(t+1))\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+};\,\mathrm{d}\mu)}\leq\sqrt{H^{*}(G_{d})}\cdot(t+1)^{-\frac{1}{2}},

where
C(t)
is defined by the relation given in (
16
).

Since

1-(d-1)e^{-(d-1)t}\leq\left(1-e^{-(d-1)t}\right)^{d-1}

we deduce from (
18
) that

\frac{2^{d-2}\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{R}}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\leq C(t)\leq\frac{2^{d-2}\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{R}}}{\left[1-(d-1)e^{-(d-1)(t+1)}\right]\cdot\left[\sqrt{\pi}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(t+1)}}e^{-\frac{\lambda_{1}(t+1)}{4}}\right]},

which implies

\frac{2^{d-2}\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{R}}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\leq C(t)\leq\frac{2^{d-2}\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{R}}}{\sqrt{\pi}}+O(e^{-m_{d}(t+1)}),

where we have
m_{d}:=\min\left(d-1,\lambda_{1}/4\right).
This implies that in the previous result we are able to exchange
C(t)
by its limit value
C_{\infty}=\frac{2^{d-2}\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{R}}}{\sqrt{\pi}}
without loosing speed of convergence, since

\|u-C_{\infty}\Gamma\|_{L^{1}}\leq\|u-C(t)\cdot\Gamma\|_{L^{1}}+(C(t)-C_{\infty})\|\Gamma\|_{L^{1}}\lesssim\sqrt{H^{*}(u_{0})}\cdot(t+1)^{-\frac{1}{2}}+e^{-m_{d}(t+1)},

and therefore there must exist a time
t_{0}
depending on
m_{d}
and
H^{*}(u_{0})
such that

\|u-C_{\infty}\Gamma\|_{L^{1}}\lesssim\sqrt{H^{*}(u_{0})}\cdot(t+1)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\quad\text{for all}\quad t\geq t_{0}.

This yields the following corollary.

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
u
be a solution of (
12
) such that
u_{0}(x)=u_{0}(r)
for all
x\in\mathbb{H}^{d}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{R}}<\infty
. Define
C_{\infty}=\frac{2^{d-2}\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{R}}}{\sqrt{\pi}}
. Then there exists a
t_{0}
such that, for all
t\geq t_{0}
,

\|u(t,r)-C_{\infty}\ \Gamma(t+1,r+(d-1)(t+1))\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+};\,\mathrm{d}\mu)}\lesssim\sqrt{H^{*}(u_{0})}\cdot(t+1)^{-\frac{1}{2}},

and, in particular,

\|\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{R}}G_{d}(t,r)-C_{\infty}\ \Gamma(t+1,r+(d-1)(t+1))\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+};\,\mathrm{d}\mu)}\lesssim\sqrt{H^{*}(G_{d})}\cdot(t+1)^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

As a consequence of this,

\|u(t,x)-\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{R}}P_{d}(t,x)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})}\lesssim\left(\sqrt{H^{*}(G_{d})}+\sqrt{H^{*}(v_{0})}\right)\cdot(t+1)^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

Since the difference of two solutions is itself a solution of the Heat Equation, then the
L^{1}-L^{\infty}
regularizing effect described in
[
21
, Proposition 2.3]
applies to the function

\tilde{u}(t,r):=u(t,r)-\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{R}}G_{d}(t,r),

implying that

\|\tilde{u}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+})}\leq C(d)t^{-\frac{3}{2}}e^{-\lambda_{1}t}\|\tilde{u}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})},

where
\lambda_{1}
is the bottom of the spectrum of the Laplacian in
\mathbb{H}^{d}
. This
L^{1}-L^{\infty}
regularizing effect and the previous theorem readily yield the following.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
u
a solution of (
12
) such that
u_{0}(x)=u_{0}(r)
for all
x\in\mathbb{H}^{d}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{R}}<\infty
. Then

\|u(t,r)-\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{R}}G_{d}(t,r)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+})}\lesssim\left(\sqrt{H^{*}(G_{d})}+\sqrt{H^{*}(v_{0})}\right)\cdot e^{-\lambda_{1}t}t^{-2}.

2.2
Horospheric solutions

Now let
u
be a classical solution to (
1
) with initial data
u_{0}
depending only on the distance to a certain horosphere
\mathcal{H}
. Then, following (
7
),
u
only depends on the distance to
\mathcal{H}
as well and satisfies

u(t,r,y)=u(t,r),\quad\text{for any }y\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1},\quad\Delta_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}=\frac{1}{e^{(d-1)r}}\partial_{r}\left(e^{(d-1)r}\cdot\partial_{r}u\right).

Equation (
1
) then becomes

\begin{cases}u_{t}=e^{-(d-1)r}\partial_{r}\left(e^{(d-1)r}\cdot\partial_{r}u\right),\quad&r\in\mathbb{R},t>0,\\
u(0,r)=u_{0}(r),&r\in\mathbb{R}.\end{cases}

(20)

We say that
u
is a
horospherical solution
to the heat equation. In opposition to radially symmetric solutions, horospherical solutions to (
1
) are not in
L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})
in general. However, the following notion of mass make sense for this type of solutions:

\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R},e^{(d-1)r}\,\mathrm{d}r)}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}u(t,r)\ e^{(d-1)r}\ \,\mathrm{d}r,

and it can be checked that this quantity does not depend on
t
if
u
solves the heat equation (i.e., this mass is preserved). From now on, we write

\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{H}}:=\|u\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R},e^{(d-1)r}\,\mathrm{d}r)}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}u(t,r)\ e^{(d-1)r}\ \,\mathrm{d}r=\int_{\mathbb{R}}u_{0}(r)\ e^{(d-1)r}\ \,\mathrm{d}r,

and we refer to this quantity as the
horospherical mass
of
u
. Furthermore, we will assume the integrability condition
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{H}}<+\infty
.

Now define

v(\tau,\rho):=e^{\frac{\tau}{2}}\cdot e^{(d-1)r(\tau,\rho)}\cdot u\big(t(\tau),r(\tau,\rho)\big),\quad\tau\geq 0,\quad\rho\in\mathbb{R},

(21)

where, as in the previous sections, we considered

t(\tau)=e^{\tau}-1,\quad r(\tau,\rho)=(d-1)e^{\tau}+e^{\frac{\tau}{2}}\rho.

Then, we expect
v
to satisfy a Fokker-Planck type equation. We check that this is in fact the case, and after some easy computations we get that
v
solves

\begin{cases}v_{\tau}=\partial_{\rho}\left(v_{\rho}+\frac{\rho}{2}v\right),\quad&\rho\in\mathbb{R},\ \tau>0,\\
v(0,\cdot)=v_{0},&\rho\geq\rho_{0}(\tau),\end{cases}

(22)

where we define
v_{0}(\rho):=e^{(d-1)r(0,\rho)}u_{0}(r(0,\rho))
.

The equilibrium for (
22
) is obtained by solving the equation
v_{\rho}+\frac{\rho}{2}v=0
. It is straightforward that the solution to this equation is given by

V(\tau,\rho)=C(\tau)\cdot e^{(d-1)r(\tau,\rho)}e^{-\frac{\left(\rho+2(d-1)e^{\frac{\tau}{2}}\right)^{2}}{4}}=C(\tau)e^{-\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}},\quad\forall\tau\geq 0,\quad\rho\in\mathbb{R},

and we can write this function in terms of the variables
t,r
as

V(t,r)=C(t)\cdot e^{(d-1)r}\cdot e^{-\frac{(r+(d-1)(t+1))^{2}}{4(t+1)}}=C(t)e^{-\frac{(r-(d-1)(t+1))^{2}}{4(t+1)}},\quad\forall t\geq 0,\quad r\in\mathbb{R}.

(23)

Since
C(\tau)
is the variable needed to make sure that
\|v(\tau,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}}=\|V(\tau,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}}
, it can be easily verified that in this case

C(\tau)=\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{H}},\qquad V(\tau,\rho)=\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{H}}\ e^{-\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}}.

Note also that equation (
22
) can be rewritten now as

\begin{cases}v_{\tau}=\partial_{\rho}\left(v\cdot\partial_{\rho}\ln\left(\frac{v}{V}\right)\right),\quad&\rho\in\mathbb{R},\ \tau>0,\\
v(0,\cdot)=v_{0},&\rho\geq\rho_{0}(\tau),\end{cases}

This formulation suggest studying the large-time behaviour of
v
by means of the convergence of the relative entropy

H(\tau):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}v\cdot\ln\left(\frac{v}{V}\right)\,\,\mathrm{d}\rho,\quad\forall\tau\geq 0,

where
v
and
V
are given by (
21
) and (
23
) respectively. By computing the first derivative of
H
, we get

\begin{split}\partial_{\tau}H(\tau)&=\int_{\mathbb{R}}v_{\tau}\,\,\mathrm{d}\rho+\int_{\mathbb{R}}v_{\tau}\cdot\ln\left(\frac{v}{V}\right)\,\,\mathrm{d}\rho\\
&=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}v\left|\partial_{\rho}\ln\left(\frac{v}{V}\right)\right|^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}\rho,\end{split}

where for the second identity we have used the conservation of mass.

From here, after applying a log-Sobolev inequality from Lemma
A.1
with constant
\lambda=1
, Gronwall’s Lemma and the Csiszár-Kullback inequality, and then undoing the change of variables, we prove the main result of this section. In order to keep everything on the original variables, we will write
H(u_{0})
instead of
H(v_{0})=H(0)
, since the meaning of this symbol is clear. Also, we recall the notation
\,\mathrm{d}\nu(r)=e^{(d-1)r}\,\mathrm{d}r
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
u
be a solution of (
20
) such that
u_{0}(x)=u_{0}(r)
for all
x\in\mathbb{H}^{d}
, and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{H}}<\infty
. Then,

\|u(t,r)-\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{H}}\ \Gamma(t,r+(d-1)t)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R},\,\mathrm{d}\nu)}\lesssim\sqrt{H(u_{0})}\cdot t^{-\frac{1}{2}}

where
\Gamma(t,r)
stands for the standard Gaussian, defined in (
19
).

Indeed, it can be verified that the fundamental solution of (
20
) is in fact
\Gamma(t,r+(d-1)t)
, and any integrable solution of (
20
) can be written as

u(t,r)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Gamma(t,r-R+(d-1)t)\ u_{0}(R)\ \,\mathrm{d}R.

As a consequence, we can deduce an smoothing effect by means of

\displaystyle u(2t,r)

\displaystyle-\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{H}}\ \Gamma(2t+1,r+(d-1)(2t+1))

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Gamma(t,r-R+(d-1)t)\cdot\left(u(t,R)-\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{H}}\ \Gamma(t+1,R+(d-1)(t+1))\right)\ \,\mathrm{d}R

which implies

\displaystyle|u(2t,r)-\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{H}}\ \Gamma(2t+1,r+(d-1)(2t+1))|

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\Gamma(t,r-R+(d-1)t)}{e^{(d-1)R}}\cdot|u(t,R)-\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{H}}\ \Gamma(t+1,R+(d-1)(t+1))|e^{(d-1)R}\ \,\mathrm{d}R.

Using now that

\frac{\Gamma(t,r-R+(d-1)t)}{e^{(d-1)R}}\lesssim t^{-\frac{1}{2}}

and Theorem
2.4
we obtain the following.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
u
be a solution of (
20
) such that
u_{0}(x)=u_{0}(r)
for all
x\in\mathbb{H}^{d}
, and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{H}}<\infty
. Then,

\|u(t,r)-\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{H}}\ \Gamma(t+1,r+(d-1)(t+1))\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim\sqrt{H(u_{0})}\cdot(t+1)^{-1}.

3
Asymptotic convergence of non-symmetric solutions.

Let us study now the problem

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u=\frac{1}{\sinh^{d-1}(r)}\partial_{r}\left(\sinh^{d-1}(r)\cdot\partial_{r}u\right)+\frac{1}{\sinh^{2}(r)}\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}u,\quad&x\in\mathbb{H}^{d},\ t>0,\\[10.0pt]
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x)\geq 0,&x\in\mathbb{H}^{d},\end{cases}

(24)

for a certain non-negative initial datum
u_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})
that does not necessarily present the radial symmetry required in Section
2.1
. In this brief section we will show the main ideas on how to derive the result (
5
) in the case
p=1
. Let us write in what follows
y=(r_{y},\theta_{y})
and
x=(r_{x},\theta_{x})
in order to better distinguish between integration variables, fixed and variable points, etc…

Let
y\in\mathbb{H}^{d}\setminus\{\mathcal{O}\}
, and, for simplicity of notation, write
P_{d}
for the fundamental solution centred at the origin
\mathcal{O}
. We begin by studying the long-time behaviour of the difference between the heat kernels
P_{d}
and
P_{d}^{y}
. It was shown in
[
21
]
for the three-dimensional case, and later extended in
[
3
]
for general dimension
d
, that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\|P_{d}-P_{d}^{y}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})}>0.

Here, one can improve on this result by incorporating geometric information. Let
x=(r_{x},\theta_{x})\in\mathbb{H}^{d}
and
denote by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
the Euclidean scalar product in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let also
l\in(0,1)
. For any
\ell\in(-l\sqrt{t},l\sqrt{t})
, define
r_{\ell}(t)=(d-1)t+\ell
for all
t\in(0,+\infty)
. Then, we have that
p_{t}=p_{t}(r_{\ell}(t),\theta_{x})
denotes a unique point in
\mathbb{H}^{d}
. Define finally

\varphi(y,\theta_{x}):=\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{P_{d}^{y}(t,p_{t})}{P_{d}(t,p_{t})}.

(25)

It follows from
[
3
, Proposition 3.7]
that
\varphi
is a well defined continuous function on
\mathbb{H}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, and
[
16
, Appendix]
provides the specific formula

\varphi(y,\theta_{x})=\varphi(r_{y},\theta_{y},\theta_{x})=[\cosh(r_{y})-\sinh(r_{y})\cdot\left\langle\theta,\theta_{y}\right\rangle]^{-(d-1)}.

Since this article is oriented to readers with a background on PDEs, in Appendix
A.4
we offer a derivation of
\varphi
in the cases
d\in\{2,3\}
that employs only some analysis and the explicit formula of the kernels that can be found in
[
10
,
13
,
12
]
. Our approach holds in every dimension
d\geq 2
, but the computations become exponentially complicated, so it is more sensible to utilize directly the results from
[
16
, Appendix]
.

From the formula of
\varphi
, we get the following result. It’s proof is left to the reader as an exercise, taking into account
[
15
, Proposition A.1]
. The interested reader is invited to check also Proposition
A.5
.

Proposition 3.1
.

For any
y\in\mathbb{H}^{d}
, we have that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\left\|P_{d}^{y}(t,\cdot)-\varphi(y,\cdot)\,P_{d}(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})}=0.

This result suggests that the asymptotic profile of a general solution
u
is going to be given by the product of the fundamental solution centred at the origin,
P_{d}(t,x)=G_{d}(t,r_{x})
, and the weighted directional mass

\Phi(\theta_{x}):=\int_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}u_{0}(y)\,\varphi(y,\theta_{x})\ \,\mathrm{d}\mu(y).

(26)

In other words, we would like to prove that

\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\|u(t,x)-\Phi(\theta_{x})G_{d}(t,r_{x})\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})}=0.

However, note that the function
\Phi(\theta_{x})
may not even be defined, since
\varphi(y,\theta_{x})
is not uniformly bounded. Indeed, the optimal bound for
\varphi
that can be attained is

\varphi(y,\theta_{x})\leq\left[\cosh(r_{y})-\sinh(r_{y})\right]^{-(d-1)}=e^{(d-1)r_{y}}

(27)

and this bound is attained whenever
\theta_{x}=\theta_{y}
. Therefore, we need the integral in (
26
) to be well defined and the function
\Phi(\theta_{x})G_{d}(t,x)
to be integrable. Since

\displaystyle\|\Phi(\theta_{x})G_{d}(t,x)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\Phi(\theta_{x})\ \,\mathrm{d}\theta_{x}\right)\cdot\int_{0}^{\infty}G_{d}(t,r_{x})\,\sinh^{d-1}(r_{x})\ \,\mathrm{d}r_{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathbb{S}^{d-1}|}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\Phi(\theta_{x})\ \,\mathrm{d}\theta_{x}

we need to assume that
\Phi\in L^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
. Having this one obtains the following theorem. Its proof is left to the reader as an exercise. It also corresponds to (
5
) in the case
p=1
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
u
be the solution to problem (
24
) for a non-negative initial datum
u_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})
such that
\Phi\in L^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
, defined as in (
26
). Then

\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\|u(t,x)-\Phi(\theta_{x})P_{d}(t,x)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})}=0.

Remark 3.3
.

Since
\varphi
is a continuous function, a sufficient condition for
\Phi\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})\subset L^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
is that
u_{0}
has compact support.

Following (
27
), a sufficient condition for
\Phi\in L^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
is that

\int_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}u_{0}(y)\,e^{(d-1)r_{y}}\ \,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}u_{0}(r_{y},\theta_{y})\,e^{(d-1)r_{y}}\,\sinh^{d-1}(r_{y})\ \,\mathrm{d}r_{y}\ \,\mathrm{d}\theta_{y}\leq C<\infty,

(28)

for a certain positive constant
C=C(u_{0})
. Notice how this condition matches the one in (
4
) for
\Phi_{1}
. With this at hand, the following lemma unfolds.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
u
be the solution to problem (
24
) for a non-negative initial datum
u_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})
such that condition (
28
) is satisfied. Then
\Phi(\theta_{x})
is uniformly bounded.

4
Convergence with rates of non-symmetric solutions

In this section we improve on the results in
[
16
]
by studying the asymptotic behaviour of solutions to the heat equation with general compactly supported initial data using entropy methods, in the spirit of Section
2
.

4.1
Entropy for general solutions

Let us define

v(\tau,\rho,\theta):=e^{\frac{\tau}{2}}\cdot\sinh^{d-1}\big(r(\tau,\rho)\big)\cdot u\big(t(\tau),r(\tau,\rho),\theta\big),

the function
V(\tau,\rho)
as in (
15
) but this time choosing
C(\tau)
such that
\int V=1
, and the function

\mathcal{N}(\tau,\theta):=\int_{\rho_{0}(\tau)}^{\infty}v(\tau,\rho,\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\rho.

Then, following (
6
), the function
v
satisfies

\begin{cases}v_{\tau}=\partial_{\rho}\left(v\cdot\partial_{\rho}\left[\ln\left(\frac{v}{\mathcal{N}\cdot V}\right)\right]\right)+\frac{e^{\tau}}{\sinh^{2}(r)}\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}v,\quad&\rho\geq\rho_{0}(\tau),\ \tau>0,\\
v(\tau,\rho_{0}(\tau))=0,&\tau>0,\\
v(0,\cdot)=v_{0},&\rho\geq\rho_{0}(\tau).\end{cases}

Notice how this equation has a spherical term involving
\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}v
, hence the need to introduce the
directional mass
function
\mathcal{N}
. On the other hand, since this term is multiplied by a factor
\frac{e^{\tau}}{\sinh^{2}(r)}
, we expect it to be small enough at regions of the form
r\sim(d-1)t
(where most of the mass is located) so that
L^{1}
convergence retains the same speed
t^{-1/2}
.

We define then the entropy

H(\tau):=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\rho_{0}(\tau)}^{\infty}v\cdot\ln\left(\frac{v}{\mathcal{N}\cdot V}\right)\ \,\mathrm{d}\rho\ \,\mathrm{d}\theta,

and we can compute, as in the previous sections,

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}H(\tau)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\rho_{0}(\tau)}^{\infty}v\cdot\frac{V_{\tau}}{V}-\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\rho_{0}(\tau)}^{\infty}v\cdot\frac{\mathcal{N}_{\tau}}{\mathcal{N}}+\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\rho_{0}(\tau)}^{\infty}v_{\tau}\cdot\ln\left(\frac{v}{\mathcal{N}\cdot V}\right)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\rho_{0}(\tau)}^{\infty}v\cdot\frac{V_{\tau}}{V}-\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathcal{N}_{\tau}-\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\rho_{0}(\tau)}^{\infty}v\left|\partial_{\rho}\cdot\ln\left(\frac{v}{\mathcal{N}\cdot V}\right)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\rho_{0}(\tau)}^{\infty}\frac{e^{\tau}}{\sinh^{2}(r)}\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}v\cdot\ln\left(\frac{v}{\mathcal{N}\cdot V}\right).

Let us estimate some of the terms in the right-hand side. We can use that, after some computations,

\mathcal{N}_{\tau}=\int_{\rho_{0}(\tau)}^{\infty}\frac{e^{\tau}}{\sinh^{2}(r)}\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}v(\tau,\rho,\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\rho,

which implies that

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathcal{N}_{\tau}=0.

On the other hand, integrating by parts and using the periodicity of
v
on the directional variable
\theta
, we get to

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\rho_{0}(\tau)}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\frac{e^{\tau}}{\sinh^{2}(r)}\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}v\cdot\ln\left(\frac{v}{\mathcal{N}\cdot V}\right)=\int_{\rho_{0}(\tau)}^{\infty}\frac{e^{\tau}}{\sinh^{2}(r)}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}v\cdot(\ln\left(v\right)-\ln\left(\mathcal{N}\right))

\displaystyle=\int_{\rho_{0}(\tau)}^{\infty}\frac{e^{\tau}}{\sinh^{2}(r)}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}v\cdot\left(\frac{\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}v}{v}-\frac{|\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}v|^{2}}{v^{2}}\right)-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}v\,\ln(\mathcal{N})

\displaystyle\leq-\int_{\rho_{0}(\tau)}^{\infty}\frac{e^{\tau}}{\sinh^{2}(r)}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}v\,\ln(\mathcal{N})=-\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathcal{N}_{\tau}\,\ln(\mathcal{N})

Now, this directional mass distribution
\mathcal{N}
makes it so the equality

\int_{\rho_{0}(\tau)}^{\infty}v(\tau,\rho,\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\rho=\int_{\rho_{0}(\tau)}^{\infty}\mathcal{N}(\tau,\theta)V(\tau,\rho)\,\mathrm{d}\rho

holds, in order to apply log-Sobolev inequalities. Putting all this together yields

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}H(\tau)

\displaystyle\leq-\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\rho_{0}(\tau)}^{\infty}v\cdot\frac{V_{\tau}}{V}-\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathcal{N}_{\tau}\,\ln(\mathcal{N})-\lambda H(\tau).

Similarly as in Section
2.1
, we have

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\rho_{0}(\tau)}^{\infty}v\cdot\frac{V_{\tau}}{V}\geq-k_{d}\ e^{\tau-m_{d}e^{\tau}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\rho_{0}(\tau)}^{\infty}v=-k_{d}\ \|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})}\ e^{\tau-m_{d}e^{\tau}},

and therefore,

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}H(\tau)

\displaystyle\leq k_{d}\ \|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})}\ e^{\tau-m_{d}e^{\tau}}-\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathcal{N}_{\tau}\,\ln(\mathcal{N})-\lambda H(\tau),

(29)

so in order to conclude the convergence of the relative entropy we need a good estimate on
\mathcal{N}
and
\partial_{\tau}\mathcal{N}
, which we will derive in the next section.

4.2
The directional mass distribution

This subsection is devoted to study the behaviour of the function
\mathcal{N}(\tau,\theta)
.

Proposition 4.1
.

There exists a positive constant
C_{1}(u_{0},d)
depending only on the initial datum and the dimension
d
such that

0<C_{1}(u_{0},d)\leq\mathcal{N}.

Proof.

A comparison argument by below with a radially symmetric initial datum with compact support and Theorem
2.1
yield the bound for
\mathcal{N}
.
∎

Next, we estimate
\partial_{\tau}\mathcal{N}
. It is more convenient to study it in terms of the original variables, except for the time variable for which we simply write
t=t(\tau)\sim e^{\tau}
when needed. At a point
\theta_{x}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
,

\partial_{\tau}\mathcal{N}(\tau,\theta_{x})=e^{\tau}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sinh^{d-3}(r_{x})\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\theta_{x}}u(t,r_{x},\theta_{x})\ \,\mathrm{d}r_{x}.

(30)

The first step is to study then
\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\theta_{x}}u
. Since the solution
u
satisfies
u=\int u_{0}\,G_{d}^{y}\,\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)
, then

\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\theta_{x}}u(t,r_{x},\theta_{x})=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}u_{0}(r_{y},\theta_{y})\,[\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\theta_{x}}G_{d}(t,\lambda(x,y))]\,\sinh^{d-1}(r_{y})\ \,\mathrm{d}r_{y}\ \,\mathrm{d}\theta_{y}

(31)

where
\lambda(x,y)
is defined as in (
8
). In order to compute
\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\theta_{x}}G_{d}
, we fix
y\in\mathbb{H}^{d}
and thus we define

s(\theta):=\textup{dist}_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}(\theta,\theta_{y}),\quad\forall\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}.

Then, since we have that
\lambda=\lambda(r_{x},s(\theta_{x}))
when
y
is fixed, we have that

\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\theta_{x}}G_{d}(t,\lambda)=\partial_{s}G_{d}\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}s+\partial_{ss}G_{d}|\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}s|^{2},

and since

\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}s=(d-1)\cot(x)\quad\textup{and}\quad|\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}s|^{2}=1,

we conclude that

\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\theta_{x}}G_{d}=(d-1)\cot(s)\partial_{s}G_{d}+\partial_{ss}G_{d}.

See
[
17
]
for a proof of these formulas. Now, taking into account that

\partial_{s}G_{d}=\partial_{\lambda}G_{d}\partial_{s}\lambda,\quad\partial_{ss}G_{d}=\partial_{\lambda\lambda}G_{d}(\partial_{s}\lambda)^{2}+\partial_{\lambda}G_{d}\partial_{ss}\lambda,

and the recurrence formula (
10
), we get after simplifying that

\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\theta_{x}}G_{d}=4\pi^{2}e^{2(d+1)t}\sinh(r_{x})^{2}\sinh(r_{y})^{2}\sin(s)^{2}G_{d+4}-2\pi de^{dt}\sinh(r_{x})\sinh(r_{y})\cos(s)G_{d+2}.

Substituting this equation into (
30
) and (
31
) we readily get, with
t=t(\tau)\sim e^{\tau}
,

\partial_{\tau}\mathcal{N}(\tau,\theta_{x})=4\pi^{2}te^{2(d+1)t}\cdot\mathcal{I}-2\pi dte^{dt}\cdot\mathcal{II},

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}u_{0}(r_{y},\theta_{y})\,\sinh^{d+1}(r_{y})\,\sin(\vartheta)^{2}\,G_{d+4}(t,\lambda(x,y))\,\sinh^{d-1}(r_{x})\,\,\mathrm{d}r_{y}\,\,\mathrm{d}\theta_{y}\,\,\mathrm{d}r_{x},

We recall that we are writing, for simplicity,

Let us see how we can bound these two integral terms. We begin, for the sake of explanation if nothing else, with the simpler case where

has compact support.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let

, such that

\mathcal{I}\leq C\,t^{-1}e^{-\frac{5}{2}\left(d+\frac{1}{2}\right)t}\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})},

and

\mathcal{II}\leq C\,t^{-1}e^{-\frac{3}{2}\left(d-\frac{1}{2}\right)t}\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})}.

Proof.

Let us begin with integral
\mathcal{I}
. From (

\mathcal{I}\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}u_{0}(r_{y},\theta_{y})\,\sinh^{d+1}(r_{y})\,\sin^{2}(\vartheta)\,h_{d+4}(t,\lambda(x,y))\,\sinh^{d-1}(r_{x})\,\,\mathrm{d}r_{y}\,\,\mathrm{d}\theta_{y}\,\,\mathrm{d}r_{x}.

Now, let
a>0
. Then, using
\lambda(x,y)\geq|r_{x}-r_{y}|
, we get

\displaystyle h_{d+4}(t,\lambda)\,\sinh^{d-1}(r_{x})

\displaystyle=(4\pi t)^{-\frac{d+4}{2}}(1+\lambda+t)^{\frac{d+1}{2}}(1+\lambda)e^{-\frac{(\lambda+(d+3)t)^{2}}{4t}}\,\sinh^{d-1}(r_{x})

\displaystyle\lesssim t^{-\frac{3}{2}}\lambda^{\frac{d+3}{2}}e^{-\frac{3}{2}\lambda}\,e^{-\frac{1}{4t}f(r_{x},r_{y},t)}

with

f(r_{x},r_{y},t):=|r_{x}-r_{y}|^{2}+2d\,t\,|r_{x}-r_{y}|-4(d-1)t\,r_{x}+(d+3)^{2}t^{2}.

But using that

we get

\displaystyle f(r_{x},r_{y},t)

\displaystyle\geq|r_{x}-r_{y}|^{2}-2(d-2)\,t\,|r_{x}-r_{y}|-4(d-1)t\,r_{y}+(d+3)^{2}t^{2}

\displaystyle=\big[|r_{x}-r_{y}|-(d-2)t\big]^{2}-4(d-1)t\,r_{y}-\big[(d-2)^{2}-(d+3)^{2}\big]t^{2},

h_{d+4}(t,\lambda)\,\sinh^{d-1}(r_{x})\lesssim t^{-\frac{3}{2}}e^{-\frac{(|r_{x}-r_{y}|-(d-2)t)^{2}}{4t}}\cdot e^{(d-1)r_{y}}\cdot e^{\frac{(d-2)^{2}-(d+3)^{2}}{4}t}.

\mathcal{I}\lesssim t^{-1}e^{\frac{(d-2)^{2}-(d+3)^{2}}{4}t}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}u_{0}(r_{y},\theta_{y})\,\sinh^{d+1}(r_{y})\,e^{(d-1)r_{y}}\,\,\mathrm{d}r_{y}\,\,\mathrm{d}\theta_{y}.

Using now that the support of
u_{0}

\mathcal{I}\leq C\,t^{-1}e^{\frac{(d-2)^{2}-(d+3)^{2}}{4}t}\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})},

C
is a constant that depends on the size of the support and increases as the diameter of the support increases. A similar computation, this time with

\mathcal{II}\leq C\,t^{-1}e^{\frac{(d-2)^{2}-(d+1)^{2}}{4}t}\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})}.

∎

As a consequence of (

Lemma 4.6
.

Let

such that condition (

Proof.

On the one hand, since

\int_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}P_{d}(t,x)\ \,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=S_{d-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}G_{d}(t,r)\ \,\mathrm{d}\mu(r)=1

\|C(t)\ \Gamma(t+1,r+(d-1)t)-S_{d-1}\ G_{d}(t,r)\|_{L^{1}([0,\infty];\,\mathrm{d}\mu)}\lesssim(t+1)^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

On the other hand, from (

\|\Phi-S_{d-1}\ \mathcal{N}_{\infty}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}=S_{d-1}\cdot\|\Phi\ G_{d}-S_{d-1}\ N_{\infty}G_{d}\|

and therefore, as a consequence of the triangular inequality,

\displaystyle\|\Phi-S_{d-1}\ \mathcal{N}_{\infty}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}

\displaystyle\leq S_{d-1}\left(\underbrace{\|C(t)\mathcal{N}_{\infty}\Gamma-S_{d-1}\ \mathcal{N}_{\infty}G_{d}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d-1})}}_{A_{1}(t)}+\underbrace{\|u-C(t)\mathcal{N}_{\infty}\Gamma\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d-1})}}_{A_{2}(t)}+\underbrace{\|u-\Phi G_{d}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d-1})}}_{A_{3}(t)}\right).

A.1
Construction of Fermi-coordinate systems based on hypersurfaces

By a symmetry we mean the action of a subgroup of the
\mathrm{Iso}(\mathbb{H}^{d})
, the isometry group of hyperbolic space. There are three principal subgroups of
\mathrm{Iso}(\mathbb{H}^{d})
, corresponding to the three types of isometries in
\mathbb{H}^{d}
with codimension one orbits: elliptic, parabolic, and hyperbolic. Each subgroup naturally gives rise to a distinct coordinate system. Note that in these cases the foliation of
\mathbb{H}^{d}
by parallel hypersurfaces
\{\Sigma_{r}\}_{r\in I}
coincides with the orbits of one of the principal subgroups of
\mathrm{Iso}(\mathbb{H}^{d})
. Let us discuss now the general procedure to construct a coordinate system based on the distance to a hypersurface.

We begin by recalling the definition of the exponential map at a point. Given
x\in\mathbb{H}^{d}
, the exponential map at
x
is the function

\exp_{x}:T_{x}\mathbb{H}^{d}\longrightarrow\mathbb{H}^{d}

defined as follows: for
v\in T_{x}\mathbb{H}^{d}
, let
\gamma_{x,v}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{H}^{d}
denote the unique geodesic with initial conditions
\gamma_{x,v}(0)=x
and
\gamma_{x,v}^{\prime}(0)=v
. Then

\exp_{x}(v):=\gamma_{x,v}(1).

In other words,
\exp_{x}
maps a tangent vector
v
at
x
to the point obtained by moving from
x
along the geodesic in the direction of
v
for a distance
\|v\|
. Here,
\|.\|
is the norm induced by the hyperbolic metric.

By fixing a basis of
T_{x}\mathbb{H}^{d}
, one may identify the tangent space
T_{x}\mathbb{H}^{d}
with
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This allows us to regard directions in the tangent space as Euclidean vectors, making the local geometry more accessible.

Let now
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{H}^{d}
be a fixed smooth, embedded, closed hypersurface, and suppose that there exists
N
a smooth normal vector field along
\Sigma
. Thus,
N
is a vector field defined on
\Sigma
such that
N(x)\perp T_{x}\Sigma
and
\|N(x)\|=1
for every
x\in\Sigma
. We define the normal exponential map to
\Sigma
by

\begin{split}E:\mathbb{R}\times\Sigma&\longrightarrow\mathbb{H}^{d}\\
(r,x)&\longmapsto\exp_{x}\big(rN(x)\big).\end{split}

Suppose there exists an open interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
, containing the origin, such that
E
is injective when restricted to
\mathcal{U}:=I\times\Sigma
. Then
E|_{\mathcal{U}}
defines a diffeomorphism between
\mathcal{U}
and an open subset
\mathcal{V}\subset\mathbb{H}^{d}
, foliated by hypersurfaces parallel to
\Sigma
.

Note that since
\Sigma
is closed and embedded we have that it divides
\mathcal{V}
into two open connected components:
\mathcal{V}\setminus\Sigma=\mathcal{V}_{+}\cup\mathcal{V}_{-}
. In this context, we say that
N
points towards
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathcal{V}\setminus\Sigma
if for any
x\in\Sigma
there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
\textup{exp}_{x}(tN(x))\in\mathcal{O}
for all
t\in(0,\varepsilon)
. If we suppose that
N
points towards
\mathcal{V}_{+}
then the parameter
r
in the normal exponential map can be defined as the
signed distance to

\Sigma
, which is given by

r(x):=\begin{cases}\,\,\,\,\textup{dist}(x,\Sigma),\quad&\forall x\in\mathcal{V}_{+},\\
-\textup{dist}(x,\Sigma),&\forall x\in\mathcal{V}_{-}.\end{cases}

Thus, the above construction yields a coordinate neighbourhood in which each point
x\in\mathcal{V}
is described by its signed distance
r
to
\Sigma
, together with the point
x\in\Sigma
where this distance is attained. We say then that
\mathcal{U}
is a
coordinate neighbourhood centred at
\Sigma

with respect to

N
. In these coordinates, the hyperbolic metric takes the form

g_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}=dr^{2}+g_{\Sigma_{r}},

where
g_{\Sigma_{r}}
denotes the hyperbolic metric restricted to the parallel hypersurface
\Sigma_{r}:=E(r,\Sigma)
.

A.2
Logarithmic Sobolev Inequalities

In this section we will demonstrate the series of one-dimensional logarithmic Sobolev Inequalities that we have been employing in this article. Since they appear naturally in the study of solutions of problem (
1
), we consider these to be the natural log-Sobolev Inequalities in the hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{d}
.

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
. For a positive, integrable function

F\colon I\to\mathbb{R}
we define
\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}\equiv\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}(F)\geq 0
as the best constant in the
inequality

\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}\int_{\Omega}g\log\frac{g}{F}\leq\int_{\Omega}g\left|\nabla\log\frac{g}{F}\right|^{2}

for all positive
g\in L^{1}(I)
with

\int_{\Omega}g=\int_{\Omega}F
(understanding the right-hand side to
be equal to
+\infty
whenever
\log\frac{g}{F}
does not have a weak
gradient, or when its weak gradient is defined but the integral on the
right-hand side is infinite). We say that
F
satisfies a logarithmic
Sobolev Inequality (also known as Gross Inequality) when
\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}(F)>0
. Notice that integral in the left-hand side of the previous inequality is nothing but the entropy of
g
relative to
F
, that is,
H(g|F)
.

There are several results that will be useful for us when estimating
the logarithmic Sobolev constant
\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}
. The first one is a
consequence of the well-known curvature-dimension condition

[
1
,
6
]
, and this particular statement can be
obtained from the theory presented in
[
1
, Section 5]
and
the proof of
Corollaire 5.5.2
therein:

Lemma A.1
.

Let
r_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
,
F\colon(r_{0},+\infty)\to(0,+\infty)
be a positive,
integrable function of the form

F(x)=Ce^{-\Phi(x)},\qquad x>r_{0},

with
C>0
and
\Phi\colon(r_{0},+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
a convex,

\mathcal{C}^{2}
function with
\Phi^{\prime\prime}(x)\geq\Lambda
for all
x>r_{0}
. Then the logarithmic Sobolev inequality

2\Lambda\cdot H(g\,|\,F)\leq\int_{r_{0}}^{\infty}g\left|\nabla\log\frac{g}{F}\right|^{2}

holds for all nonnegative
g\in L^{1}(r_{0},+\infty)
with
\int_{\Omega}g=\int_{\Omega}F
.

We also cite a well known result on perturbation of these
inequalities by
[
14
]
:

Lemma A.2
.

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be an open set,
F\colon I\to\mathbb{R}
a
positive, integrable function which satisfies a logarithmic Sobolev
inequality with constant
\lambda_{L}
. Let
A\colon I\to\mathbb{R}
be a
measurable function such that
|A|
is bounded. Then the function

\widetilde{F}(x)=F(x)e^{-A(x)},\qquad x\in I,

also satisfies a logarithmic Sobolev inequality with constant

\lambda_{L}e^{\operatorname{osc}(A)}
, where

\operatorname{osc}(A):=\sup A-\inf A
.

We present now the result needed for our study.

Corollary A.3
.

Fix
d\geq 2
,
\tau>0
and let
r(\tau,\rho):=(d-1)e^{\tau}+e^{\frac{\tau}{2}}\rho
,
\rho_{0}(\tau)=-(d-1)e^{\frac{\tau}{2}}
and
C(\tau)=C+o(\tau)
. Then, the functions

V_{1}(\tau,\rho):=C(\tau)\cdot\sinh^{d-1}\big(r(\tau,\rho)\big)\cdot e^{-\frac{\left(\rho+2(d-1)e^{\frac{\tau}{2}}\right)^{2}}{4}},

and

V_{2}(\tau,\rho):=C(\tau)\cdot\cosh^{d-1}\big(r(\tau,\rho)\big)\cdot e^{-\frac{\left(\rho+2(d-1)e^{\frac{\tau}{2}}\right)^{2}}{4}},

satisfy a log-Sobolev Inequality with constant
\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}=1
on the interval
I=(\rho_{0}(\tau),\infty)
. That is, for
i=1,2
,

\int_{\rho_{0}(\tau)}^{\infty}v\cdot\ln\left(\frac{v}{V_{i}}\right)\leq\int_{\rho_{0}(\tau)}^{\infty}v\cdot\left|\partial_{\rho}\ln\left(\frac{v}{V_{i}}\right)\right|^{2},

for all nonnegative
v
such that
\int_{I}v=\int_{I}V_{i}

Proof.

The inequality for
V_{1}
is a direct consequence of Lemma
A.1
, while the one for
V_{2}
comes from Lemma
A.2
after checking that

\cosh^{d-1}(r(\tau,\rho))\in\left[2^{1-d}e^{(d-1)^{2}e^{\tau}+(d-1)e^{\frac{\tau}{2}}\rho},\ e^{(d-1)^{2}e^{\tau}+(d-1)e^{\frac{\tau}{2}}\rho}\right]

and thus
V_{2}
is trapped between two gaussian profiles, for which the log-Sobolev inequality with constant
\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}=1
is known.
∎

A.3
Estimates for the radially symmetric transient equilibrium

In this section we collect some estimates for the transient equilibrium obtained in Section
2.1
. We recall that this is the function

V(t,r)=C(t)\cdot\sinh^{d-1}(r)\cdot e^{-\frac{\left(r+(d-1)(t+1)\right)^{2}}{4(t+1)}}.

where we choose the function
C(t)
such that

\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{R}}=\int_{\rho_{0}(\tau)}^{\infty}V(\tau,\rho)\ \,\mathrm{d}\rho,

where

t(\tau)=e^{\tau}-1,\quad r(\tau,\rho)=(d-1)e^{\tau}+e^{\frac{\tau}{2}}\rho.

Lemma A.4
.

There exists a constant
k_{d}
depending only on the dimension
d
such that

-k_{d}\ e^{-m_{d}(t+1)}\leq\frac{\partial_{t}C(t)}{C(t)}\leq k_{d}\ e^{-m_{d}(t+1)},

where

m_{d}:=\min\left(d-1,\frac{(d-1)^{2}}{16}\right).

Moreover,

\frac{2^{d-2}\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{R}}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\leq C(t)\leq\frac{2^{d-2}\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{R}}}{(1-e^{-(d-1)(t+1)})^{d-1}\left[\sqrt{\pi}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(t+1)}}e^{-\frac{\lambda_{1}(t+1)}{4}}\right]}

and, as a consequence,

C_{\infty}:=\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}C(t)=\frac{2^{d-2}\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{R}}}{\sqrt{\pi}}.

Proof.

For the sake of simplicity, we will consider
t(\tau)=e^{\tau}
in the computations instead of the real change of variables
t(\tau)=e^{\tau}-1
. This does not affect the results, it is just a translation in time; instead of
t\in[0,\infty)
we will work on the time interval
t\in[1,\infty)
, and then a map
t\to t+1
will undo de translation.

In order to estimate the function
C(t)
we notice that
V
must have constant mass, since a solution of equation (
14
) does so. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{R}}=\int_{\rho_{0}(\tau)}^{\infty}V(\tau,\rho)\ \,\mathrm{d}\rho

\displaystyle=\frac{C(t)}{\sqrt{t}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sinh^{d-1}(r)\cdot e^{-\frac{\left(r+(d-1)t\right)^{2}}{4t}}\ \,\mathrm{d}r

(39)

for all
t\geq 1
. Using implicit derivation in the equation in (
39
) it is possible to estimate
\partial_{t}C(t)/C(t)
. It can be verified, after some computations, that

\frac{\partial_{t}C(t)}{C(t)}=\frac{(d-1)^{2}t+2}{4t}-\frac{1}{4t^{2}}\cdot\frac{\mathcal{I}}{\mathcal{II}},

(40)

where

\mathcal{I}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{2}(1-e^{-2r})^{d-1}\ e^{-\frac{\left(r-(d-1)t\right)^{2}}{4t}}\ \,\mathrm{d}r,\quad\mathcal{II}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-e^{-2r})^{d-1}\ e^{-\frac{\left(r-(d-1)t\right)^{2}}{4t}}\ \,\mathrm{d}r.

Let us begin with a lower estimate for (
40
).
We estimate each integral separately. First, with a change of variables and using that
1-e^{-2r}<1
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{t}\int_{-\sqrt{\lambda_{1}t}}^{\infty}\left(2\sqrt{t}z+(d-1)t\right)^{2}\ e^{-z^{2}}\ \,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\underbrace{8t^{\frac{3}{2}}\int_{-\sqrt{\lambda_{1}t}}^{\infty}z^{2}\ e^{-z^{2}}\ \,\mathrm{d}z}_{I_{1}}+\underbrace{8(d-1)t^{2}\int_{-\sqrt{\lambda_{1}t}}^{\infty}z\ e^{-z^{2}}\ \,\mathrm{d}z}_{I_{2}}+\underbrace{2(d-1)^{2}t^{\frac{5}{2}}\int_{-\sqrt{\lambda_{1}t}}^{\infty}e^{-z^{2}}\ \,\mathrm{d}z}_{I_{3}},

where
\lambda_{1}
comes from (
2
). The integral
I_{1}
can be bounded with an integration by parts and using that

\int_{-\sqrt{\lambda_{1}t}}^{\infty}e^{-z^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}z\leq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-z^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}z=\sqrt{\pi}

by

I_{1}\leq 2\sqrt{t}\left(2\sqrt{\pi}\ t-(d-1)t^{\frac{3}{2}}e^{-\lambda_{1}t}\right),

while
I_{2}
can be computed explicitly as

I_{2}=4(d-1)t^{2}e^{-\lambda_{1}t}

and

I_{3}\leq 2(d-1)^{2}\sqrt{\pi}\ t^{\frac{5}{2}}.

In total,

\mathcal{I}\leq 2\sqrt{t}\left((d-1)^{2}\sqrt{\pi}\ t^{2}+2\sqrt{\pi}\ t+(d-1)t^{\frac{3}{2}}e^{-\lambda_{1}t}\right)

Second, we compute for
\mathcal{II}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{II}

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{t}\int_{-\sqrt{\lambda_{1}t}}^{\infty}\left(1-e^{-2(2\sqrt{t}z+(d-1)t)}\right)^{d-1}e^{-z^{2}}\ \,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\geq 2\sqrt{t}\int_{-\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}t}}{2}}^{\infty}\left(1-e^{-2(2\sqrt{t}z+(d-1)t)}\right)^{d-1}e^{-z^{2}}\ \,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\geq 2\sqrt{t}\left(1-e^{-(d-1)t}\right)^{d-1}\int_{-\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}t}}{2}}^{\infty}e^{-z^{2}}\ \,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{t}\left(1-e^{-(d-1)t}\right)^{d-1}\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-z^{2}}\ \,\mathrm{d}z-\int_{-\infty}^{-\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}t}}{2}}e^{-z^{2}}\ \,\mathrm{d}z\right]

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{t}\left(1-e^{-(d-1)t}\right)^{d-1}\left[\sqrt{\pi}-\int_{-\infty}^{-\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}t}}{2}}e^{-z^{2}}\ \,\mathrm{d}z\right]

\displaystyle\geq 2\sqrt{t}\left(1-e^{-(d-1)t}\right)^{d-1}\left[\sqrt{\pi}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}t}}\int_{-\infty}^{-\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}t}}{2}}2ze^{-z^{2}}\ \,\mathrm{d}z\right]

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{t}\left(1-e^{-(d-1)t}\right)^{d-1}\left[\sqrt{\pi}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}t}}e^{-{\frac{\lambda_{1}t}{4}}}\right].

In total, we arrive to

\displaystyle\frac{\partial_{t}C(t)}{C(t)}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(d-1)^{2}t+2}{4t}-\frac{(d-1)^{2}\sqrt{\pi}\ t^{2}+2\sqrt{\pi}\ t+(d-1)t^{\frac{3}{2}}e^{-\lambda_{1}t}}{4t^{2}\left(1-e^{-(d-1)t}\right)^{d-1}\left(\sqrt{\pi}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}t}}e^{-\frac{\lambda_{1}t}{4}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(d-1)^{2}t+2}{4t}\cdot h(t)-\frac{\left(\frac{2\sqrt{t}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}}+2(d-1)t^{\frac{3}{2}}\right)e^{-\frac{\lambda_{1}t}{4}}+(d-1)t^{\frac{3}{2}}e^{-\lambda_{1}t}}{4t^{2}\left(1-e^{-(d-1)t}\right)^{d-1}\left(\sqrt{\pi}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}t}}e^{-\frac{\lambda_{1}t}{4}}\right)},

where

h(t):=\left(1-\left(1-e^{-(d-1)t}\right)^{-(d-1)}\right)<0.

(41)

Since

\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\frac{h(t)}{-(d-1)e^{-(d-1)t}}=1,

it is left as an exercise for the reader to check that

h(t)\geq-2(d-1)e^{-(d-1)t}\quad\text{for all}\quad t\geq 1.

Therefore, for
t\geq 1
,

\frac{\partial_{t}C(t)}{C(t)}\geq-\frac{(d-1)^{3}t+2(d-1)}{2t}e^{-(d-1)t}-\frac{\left(\frac{2\sqrt{t}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}}+2(d-1)t^{\frac{3}{2}}\right)e^{-\frac{\lambda_{1}t}{4}}+(d-1)t^{\frac{3}{2}}e^{-\lambda_{1}t}}{4t^{2}\left(1-e^{-(d-1)t}\right)^{d-1}\left(\sqrt{\pi}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}t}}e^{-\frac{\lambda_{1}t}{4}}\right)}.

Thus, if we define

m_{d}:=\min\left(d-1,\frac{(d-1)^{2}}{16}\right),

(42)

then there must exist a positive constant
k_{d}
such that

\frac{\partial_{t}C(t)}{C(t)}\geq-k_{d}\ e^{-m_{d}t}\quad\text{for all}\quad t\geq 1.

Now, for the upper bound for (
40
). For the sake of brevity, we will omit computations similar to the ones in the previous step. We estimate again each integral separately. First, by similar arguments as before,

\mathcal{I}\geq 2\sqrt{t}\left(1-e^{-(d-1)t}\right)^{d-1}\int_{-\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}t}}{2}}^{\infty}\left(4tz^{2}+(d-1)^{2}t^{2}+4(d-1)t^{\frac{3}{2}}\right)e^{-z^{2}}\ \,\mathrm{d}z.

We use now the following bound, left for the reader as an exercise. For every
a>0
,

\int_{-a}^{\infty}e^{-z^{2}}\ \,\mathrm{d}z=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-z^{2}}\ \,\mathrm{d}z-\int_{-\infty}^{-a}e^{-z^{2}}\ \,\mathrm{d}z\geq\sqrt{\pi}-\frac{1}{2a}e^{-a^{2}}.

Using this, and that
\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}=(d-1)/2
by definition, it is not hard to arrive to

\mathcal{I}\geq 2\sqrt{t}\left(1-e^{-(d-1)t}\right)^{d-1}\left[\sqrt{\pi}(d-1)^{2}t^{2}+2\sqrt{\pi}t+e^{-\frac{\lambda_{1}t}{4}}\left(\frac{d-1}{2}t^{\frac{3}{2}}-\frac{2}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}}\sqrt{t}\right)\right].

For the integral
\mathcal{II}
we simply compute

\mathcal{II}\leq 2\sqrt{t}\int_{-\sqrt{\lambda_{1}t}}^{\infty}e^{-z^{2}}\ \,\mathrm{d}z\leq 2\sqrt{t}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-z^{2}}\ \,\mathrm{d}z=2\sqrt{\pi t}.

Substituting in (
40
) we arrive, after some computations, to

\frac{\partial_{t}C(t)}{C(t)}\leq\left(\frac{(d-1)^{2}}{4}+\frac{1}{2t}\right)h(t)+\left(1-e^{-(d-1)t}\right)^{d-1}\left(\frac{d-1}{2}t^{\frac{3}{2}}-\frac{2}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}}\sqrt{t}\right)\frac{e^{-\frac{\lambda_{1}t}{4}}}{4t^{2}\sqrt{\pi}},

where
h(t)
is defined as in (
41
). From here it follows that

\frac{\partial_{t}C(t)}{C(t)}\leq\left(\frac{(d-1)^{3}}{2}+\frac{1}{t}\right)e^{-(d-1)t}+\left(\frac{d-1}{2}t^{\frac{3}{2}}-\frac{2}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}}\sqrt{t}\right)\frac{e^{-\frac{\lambda_{1}t}{4}}}{4t^{2}\sqrt{\pi}},

and from here, with
m_{d}
defined as in (
42
),

\frac{\partial_{t}C(t)}{C(t)}\leq k_{d}e^{-m_{d}t}\quad\text{for all}\quad t>1.

Putting all together we obtain (
17
).

Along the previous steps, we have proven that

2\sqrt{t}\left(1-e^{-(d-1)t}\right)^{d-1}\left[\sqrt{\pi}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}t}}e^{-{\frac{\lambda_{1}t}{4}}}\right]\leq\mathcal{II}\leq 2\sqrt{\pi t}

With this bounds and equation (
39
) it is easy to conclude (
18
).
∎

A.4
Explicit expression for the function
\varphi

This section is devoted to the proof of the explicit expression for the function
\varphi
defined in (
25
) in the cases
d\in\{2,3\}
. Recall that we denote
(r_{y},\theta_{y})
for the polar coordinates centred in some origin
\mathcal{O}\in\mathbb{H}^{d}
of some point
y\in\mathbb{H}^{d}
, and we write
\lambda(x,y)=\textup{dist}(x,y)
. The following result is a direct, although long computation.

Proposition A.5
.

Fix
y\in\mathbb{H}^{d}
and
l\in(0,1)
. Given any
\ell\in(-l\sqrt{t},l\sqrt{t})
, define
r_{\ell}(t)=(d-1)t+\ell
for all
t\in(0,+\infty)
. Then, for any
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
we have that
p_{t}=p_{t}(r_{\ell}(t),\theta)
denotes a unique point in
\mathbb{H}^{d}
and there exists a continuous function
\varphi(y,\theta)
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{P_{d}^{y}(t,p_{t})}{P_{d}(t,p_{t})}=\varphi(y,\theta),

where, in the particular cases
d=2,3
,

\displaystyle\varphi(y,\theta)=\left[\cosh(r_{y})-\sinh(r_{y})\cdot\langle\theta,\theta_{y}\rangle\right]^{-(d-1)}.

Proof.

Write
\lambda(p_{t},y)=\textup{dist}(p_{t},y)
. Then, by the second hyperbolic law of cosines we have that

\lambda(p_{t},y)=\textup{arccosh}\left(\cosh(r_{\ell}(t))\cosh(r_{y})-\sinh(r_{\ell}(t))\sinh(r_{y})\cdot\langle\theta,\theta_{y}\rangle\right).

(43)

Since the fundamental solution depends only on the distance to the point at its centre,
P^{y}_{d}(t,x)=G_{d}(t,\lambda)
, the proof of the result reduces to compute the limit

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{G_{d}(t,\lambda(p_{t},y))}{G_{d}(t,r_{\ell}(t))}.

(44)

Let us denote the argument of the arccosh in (
43
) by

\alpha:=\cosh(r_{\ell}(t))\cosh(r_{y})-\sinh(r_{\ell}(t))\sinh(r_{y})\cdot\langle\theta,\theta_{y}\rangle

By using the Taylor expansion of
\textup{arccosh}(x)
, we conclude that

\lambda(p_{t},y)=\ln(2\alpha)-\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{(2n)!}{2^{2n}(n!)^{2}}\frac{1}{2n\alpha^{2n}}.

Note that

\alpha\geq\cosh(r_{\ell}(t))\cosh(r_{y})-\sinh(r_{\ell}(t))\sinh(r_{y})=\cosh(r_{\ell}(t)-r_{y})\geq 1,

so the above expansion is well defined. We can estimate both terms of this sum. For the first one, we have that

\begin{split}\ln(2\alpha)&=\ln\left((\cosh(r_{y})-\sinh(r_{y})\langle\theta,\theta_{y}\rangle)e^{r_{\ell}(t)}\right)+\ln\left(1+\frac{\cosh(r_{y})+\sinh(r_{y})\langle\theta,\theta_{y}\rangle}{\cosh(r_{y})-\sinh(r_{y})\langle\theta,\theta_{y}\rangle}e^{-2r_{\ell}(t)}\right)\\
&=\ln\left((\cosh(r_{y})-\sinh(r_{y})\langle\theta,\theta_{y}\rangle)e^{r_{\ell}(t)}\right)+O(e^{-2r_{\ell}(t)}).\end{split}

For the second one, choose
t
big enough such that
r_{y}<r_{\ell}(t)/2
, implying that

\alpha\geq\cosh(r_{\ell}(t)-r_{y})>\cosh(r_{\ell}(t)/2)\sim e^{\frac{r_{\ell}(t)}{2}}

and therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{(2n)!}{2^{2n}(n!)^{2}}\frac{1}{2n\alpha^{2n}}<\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{2n\alpha^{2n}}<\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\right)^{n}=\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}-1}=O(e^{-r_{\ell}(t)}).

Thus we conclude that for any
t
big enough such that
r_{y}<r_{\ell}(t)/2
we have

\displaystyle\lambda(p_{t},y)

\displaystyle=\ln\left((\cosh(r_{y})-\sinh(r_{y})\langle\theta,\theta_{y}\rangle)e^{r_{\ell}(t)}\right)+O(e^{-r_{\ell}(t)})

(45)

\displaystyle=r_{\ell}(t)+\ln\left((\cosh(r_{y})-\sinh(r_{y})\langle\theta,\theta_{y}\rangle)\right)+O(e^{-r_{\ell}(t)}).

With this estimate for
\lambda
, we can compute the limit appearing in (
44
). We will do it only for the cases
d=2,3
. The other cases follows using (
10
).

Case
d=3
.

In this case, we have

G_{3}(t,r)=\frac{1}{(4\pi t)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\frac{r}{\sinh(r)}e^{-t-\frac{r^{2}}{4t}},

and by (
45
) it is straightforward to show that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{\lambda(p_{t},y)}{r_{\ell}(t)}=1,\quad\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{\sinh(r_{\ell}(t))}{\sinh(\lambda(p_{t},y))}=[\cosh(r_{y})-\sinh(r_{y})\langle\theta,\theta_{y}\rangle]^{-1}

and, using that in dimension
d=3
we have
r_{\ell}(t)=2t+\ell
, that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{e^{\frac{r_{\ell}(t)^{2}}{4t}}}{e^{\frac{\lambda(p_{t},y)^{2}}{4t}}}=[\cosh(r_{y})-\sinh(r_{y})\langle\theta,\theta_{y}\rangle]^{-1},

so it follows that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{G_{3}(t,\lambda(p_{t},y))}{G_{3}(t,r_{\ell}(t))}=(\cosh(r_{y})-\sinh(r_{y})\langle\theta,\theta_{y}\rangle)^{-2}.

Case
d=2
.

Now we have

G_{2}(t,r)=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{(4\pi t)^{\frac{3}{2}}}e^{-\frac{1}{4}t}\int_{r}^{\infty}\frac{se^{-\frac{s^{2}}{4t}}}{\sqrt{\cosh(s)-\cosh(r)}}\,ds,

so we cannot compute the limit directly. Instead, we check first the asymptotics of
G_{2}(t,r_{\ell}(t))
. It is enough to compute the asymptotics of the integral

I(t)=\int_{r_{\ell}}^{\infty}\frac{se^{-\frac{s^{2}}{4t}}}{\sqrt{\cosh(s)-\cosh(r_{\ell})}}\,ds=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{(z+r_{\ell})e^{-\frac{(z+r_{\ell})^{2}}{4t}}}{\sqrt{\cosh(z+r_{\ell})-\cosh(r_{\ell})}}\,dz,

where we did the change of variables
z=s-r
. We divide this integral in two. First we compute

J(t)=\int_{0}^{\sqrt{t}}\frac{(z+r_{\ell})e^{-\frac{(z+r_{\ell})^{2}}{4t}}}{\sqrt{\cosh(z+r_{\ell})-\cosh(r_{\ell})}}\,dz=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sinh(r_{\ell})}}\int_{0}^{\sqrt{t}}\frac{(z+r_{\ell})e^{-\frac{(z+r_{\ell})^{2}}{4t}}}{\sqrt{z\cdot f(t,z)}}\,dz,

with

f(t,z)=\frac{1}{z}\left(\frac{\cosh(z+r_{\ell}(t))}{\sinh(r_{\ell}(t))}-\coth(r_{\ell}(t))\right)=\frac{1}{z}(e^{z}-1+o(t)),

where we write
o(t)
for a function such that
o(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
. Now, we note that

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{\sqrt{t}}r_{\ell}\,z^{-1/2}e^{-\frac{(z+r_{\ell})^{2}}{4t}}\,dz&=r_{\ell}e^{-\frac{r_{\ell}^{2}}{4t}}\left(\frac{2t}{r_{\ell}}\right)^{1/2}\int_{0}^{V}e^{-v}e^{-\frac{tv^{2}}{r_{\ell}^{2}}}v^{-1/2}\,dv\\
&=e^{-1/2}\sqrt{2\pi r_{\ell}t}e^{-\frac{r_{\ell}^{2}}{4t}}(1+o(t)),\end{split}

To check this identity, note that
r_{\ell}(t)=t+l
and we have done the change of variables
v=(zr_{\ell})/(2t)
. On the other hand, we have that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{0}^{\sqrt{t}}\frac{\sqrt{z}}{f(t,z)}e^{-\frac{z^{2}}{4t}-\frac{r_{\ell}z}{2t}}\,dz=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{e^{-z}z}{\sqrt{e^{z}-1}}\,dz<+\infty,

so we conclude that

J(t)=C_{1}\sqrt{2\pi r_{\ell}t}e^{-\frac{r_{\ell}^{2}}{4t}}(1+o(t)),

for some
C_{1}>0
.

Now we deal with the integral

E(t)=\int_{\sqrt{t}}^{+\infty}\frac{(z+r_{\ell})e^{-\frac{(z+r_{\ell})^{2}}{4t}}}{\sqrt{\cosh(z+r_{\ell})-\cosh(r_{\ell})}}\,dz.

In this case, we note that

\cosh(z+r_{\ell})-\cosh(r_{\ell})\geq\cosh(\sqrt{t}+r_{\ell})-\cosh(r_{\ell})=e^{r_{\ell}+\sqrt{t}}+o(t),

so we get that

E(t)\leq\frac{1}{e^{r_{\ell}+\sqrt{t}}+o(t)}\int_{\sqrt{t}}^{\infty}(z+r_{\ell})e^{-\frac{(z+r_{\ell})^{2}}{4t}}\,dz=\frac{C_{2}te^{-\frac{r_{\ell}^{2}}{4t}-\frac{r_{\ell}}{2t}}}{e^{r_{\ell}+\sqrt{t}}+o(t)},

and this term in negligible with respect to
J(t)
. Thus, we conclude that

G_{2}(t,r_{\ell}(t))=C_{3}\left(\frac{\sqrt{r_{\ell}(t)}e^{-\frac{r_{\ell}(t)^{2}}{4t}-\frac{t}{4}}}{\sqrt{t}\sqrt{\sinh(r_{\ell}(t))}}+o(t)\right),

with
C_{3}>0
.

Now it is straightforward that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{G_{2}(t,\lambda(p_{t},y))}{G_{2}(t,r_{\ell}(t))}=(\cosh(r_{y})-\sinh(r_{y})\langle\theta,\theta_{y}\rangle)^{-1}.

∎
