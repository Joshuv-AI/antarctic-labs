The k k -out-of- n n picture-hanging puzzle: shorter solutions for small k k and n − k n-k
Tom Verhoeff

Abstract
The picture-hanging puzzle, popularized by Demaine et al. (2014), asks for a way to wrap a wire around n n nails such that the picture hangs as long as fewer than k k nails are removed, but falls as soon as any k k are removed. Solutions correspond to words in the free group F n F_{n} . We give explicit, deterministic, polynomial-length constructions for two regimes: 2 2 -out-of- n n with word length at most 8 3 ​ n log 2 ⁡ 6 − 4 ​ n 2 \tfrac{8}{3}n^{\log_{2}6}-4n^{2} , and ( n − 2 ) (n-2) -out-of- n n with word length 6 ​ n ​ log 2 ​ ( n / 2 ) 6n\log_{2}(n/2) , both for n n a power of two. These improve on Wästlund’s quasi-polynomial deterministic construction in their respective regimes. We also report, via exhaustive computer search, the exact minimum length of 16 16 for the 2 2 -out-of- 4 4 puzzle, attained by two structurally distinct solutions. As an additional contribution, we observe that the natural workshop realization with carabiners on a flat board introduces an over/under ambiguity at every wire crossing; a wrong choice can produce a Whitehead link, which is topologically distinct from the intended commutator.

Introduction

The picture-hanging puzzle in its simplest form asks:
can one wrap a wire around two nails such that the picture falls
if either nail is removed?
The classical answer, popularized by Spivak
[
3
]
in 1997 and
analyzed in detail by Demaine et al.
[
1
]
, is yes:
the commutator
xyx^{-1}y^{-1}
in the free group on two generators
corresponds to such a wiring.
In this paper we will write this commutator in additive notation
as
1+2-1-2
(the rationale is explained in Section
2
).
Demaine et al. generalized this to arbitrary monotone Boolean functions,
with particular attention to the
k
-out-of-
n
family:
the picture falls when at least
k
of the
n
nails are removed,
and hangs otherwise.
Their construction for
k
-out-of-
n
uses AKS sorting networks and
gives a word of polynomial length
O(n^{1\,561\,600})
in the free group
— polynomial, but with an astronomical exponent.

Wästlund
[
7
]
subsequently gave a simplified proof and
a much better polynomial bound of
O(n^{7.004})
via a probabilistic construction,
together with an explicit deterministic divide-and-conquer construction
of quasi-polynomial length
n^{O(\log n)}
.
The explicit deterministic polynomial regime remained unexplored
for specific small values of
k
or
n-k
.

We address this gap.
For
fixed small

k
and
fixed small

n-k

— two natural regimes where the puzzle remains interesting —
we give explicit deterministic constructions with low-degree polynomial length,
established by recurrences with closed-form solutions.
Combined with exhaustive computer search,
we also pin down exact minimum lengths for the smallest open cases.

Origin

The present note arose from preparations for two public-outreach events:
a workshop for elementary and secondary school students
during a preparation day (March 2026),
organized by the Stichting Vierkant voor Wiskunde
for their summer mathematics camps,
and a mini-lecture at the MathFest in Utrecht (May 2026).
For practical reasons we used a wooden board with carabiners rather than nails:

This pedagogical convenience comes at a cost: with carabiners on a flat board,
the wire’s over/under choice at each crossing is no longer forced by gravity,
and incorrect choices produce topologically different links.
Section
7
discusses this.
For interactive experimentation, the author developed a Wolfram Demonstration
[
5
]
.

While preparing materials,
the author began investigating the lengths of explicit

-out-of-

solutions.
The puzzle most accessible to participants is

-out-of-
n

(“the picture is safe against the failure of any single nail”).
Iterative improvements
— from the length-

solution listed by Demaine et al. for
2
-out-of-
4
,
through successive reductions to lengths
68
,
58
,
54
,
24
,
20
,

—
led ultimately to the systematic constructions and the exact minimum reported here.

2
Notation and preliminaries

An additive algebra of variables

We work with formal expressions built from natural-number variables,
written
1,2,3,\ldots
rather than
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},\ldots
.
The expressions use a binary operator
+
and a unary inverse
-
.
Subject to the rules below, this is the free group on the variables;
we use the additive notation throughout
because we find it more accessible to a broader audience,
including secondary-school students and even upper-elementary pupils.

The expressions are governed by the following rules,
which the reader should keep in mind,
especially the contrast with ordinary integer arithmetic:

•

Associativity:

(x+y)+z\;=\;x+(y+z)
.

•

Non-commutativity:
in general
x+y\;\neq\;y+x
.

•

Neutral element:
we write
0
for the empty expression,
and
0+x=x+0=x
.

•

Inverse:

x+(-x)\;=\;0\;=\;(-x)+x
.

•

Double negation:

-(-x)=x
.

•

Inversion of a sum:

-(x+y)=-y+(-x)=-y-x

(note the order reversal).

We allow ourselves the shorthand
x+x=2x
and similar,
but the reader must keep in mind that, due to non-commutativity,

p(x+y)\neq px+py
in general.

The
length
of an expression is the number of variables-with-sign
in its fully reduced form (where reduction means repeatedly applying
x+(-x)=0

and absorbing the resulting
0
).
A nonzero expression has positive length; the only zero expression has length
0
.
The
support
of an expression is the set of variables that occur in it;
two expressions have
disjoint supports
when they share no variable.

The picture-hanging puzzle

A solution
w
to the
k
-out-of-
n
picture-hanging puzzle
(Demaine’s convention)
is an expression in variables
1,\ldots,n
such that:

(a)

w
is nonzero;

(b)

setting any
k
of the variables
1,\ldots,n
to
0
reduces
w
to
0
;

(c)

for every set of fewer than
k
variables,
setting those variables to
0
leaves
w
nonzero.

Condition (b) corresponds to the picture falling when any
k
nails are removed;
condition (c) corresponds to the picture still hanging when fewer than
k
are removed.
We write
h_{k,n}
for any solution to the
k
-out-of-
n
puzzle,
and
L_{k}(n)
for the length of a particular solution under discussion.

Specifications

It helps to name the object a puzzle asks us to realize.
A
specification
on a set of nails
V
is a monotone Boolean function

f:2^{V}\to\{\mathsf{hang},\mathsf{fall}\},\qquad f(V)=\mathsf{fall},

where
f(S)
is the outcome when exactly the nails in
S
are removed
(Demaine’s convention).
We order
\mathsf{hang}<\mathsf{fall}
and read
\vee
and
\wedge

as
\max
and
\min
,
so that
\mathsf{fall}\vee x=\mathsf{fall}

and
\mathsf{hang}\wedge x=\mathsf{hang}
;

monotone
then means
S\subseteq S^{\prime}\Rightarrow f(S)\leq f(S^{\prime})
,
that is, removing more nails never restores the picture.
We say
f

falls
at
S
when
f(S)=\mathsf{fall}
,
and
hangs
at
S
otherwise.
An expression
h
on
V

solves

f
if

h|_{S}=0\iff f(S)=\mathsf{fall}\qquad\text{for every }S\subseteq V,

where
h|_{S}
denotes
h
with the nails in
S
removed (set to
0
).

The condition
f(V)=\mathsf{fall}
is forced, not chosen:
removing every nail reduces any expression on
V
to
0
,
so no expression can solve a function that hangs at
V
.
By monotonicity it excludes a single function,
the constant
\mathsf{hang}

— which never falls, even when all nails are removed —
the lone monotone specification that no wire can realize.
The opposite constant,
\mathsf{fall}
, is solved by the empty expression
0

(nothing is hung, so the picture has already fallen).
Every other monotone function with
f(V)=\mathsf{fall}
is realizable too:
Demaine et al.
[
1
]
and Wästlund
[
7
]

each construct an expression that solves it.
(Wästlund excludes both constants as trivial;
we keep
\mathsf{fall}
, since the empty expression
0
solves it.)
Our concern is not realizability but the search for
shorter
solutions.

The puzzle of interest is itself a specification.
Write
k\text{-of-}V
for the specification on
V
with

(k\text{-of-}V)(S)=\mathsf{fall}\iff|S|\geq k.

(1)

A solution to the
k
-out-of-
n
puzzle is then exactly an expression
solving
k\text{-of-}V
on
V=\{1,\ldots,n\}
:
conditions (a)–(c) say precisely that
w|_{S}=0
iff
|S|\geq k
.

The conditions are monotone:
removing more nails cannot un-fall the picture.
If the picture falls when a set
S
of nails is removed,
it also falls when any larger set
S^{\prime}\supseteq S
is removed,
since an expression already equal to
0
remains
0
when more variables are set to
0
.
Equivalently,
if the picture hangs with a set
S^{\prime}
of nails removed,
it also hangs with any subset
S\subseteq S^{\prime}
removed.

Monotonicity lets a correctness proof focus on the
essential
removals
— those whose required outcome does not already follow from another removal.
A required fall is essential only when no smaller removal already falls,
and a required hang only when no larger removal already hangs.
For
k\text{-of-}V
the essential removals are therefore exactly
the sets of
k
nails (which must fall) and the sets of
k-1
nails (which must hang):
removing fewer than
k-1
hangs by monotonicity from the
(k-1)
case,
and removing more than
k
falls by monotonicity from the
k
case.
It thus suffices to check that every removal of
k
nails makes the picture fall
and every removal of
k-1
nails leaves it hanging.

For example,
the expression
1+2-1-2
has length
4
.
It is nonzero, since no reduction rule applies:
no two adjacent terms are mutual inverses.
Setting
1
to
0
reduces it to
2-2=0
;
setting
2
to
0
reduces it to
1-1=0
.
Hence it solves the
1
-out-of-
2
puzzle:
it hangs as it stands, yet removing either nail (variable)
causes the picture (expression) to fall (vanish).

In group-theoretic terms,

1+2-1-2
is the commutator
[x_{1},x_{2}]
in the free group on two generators.
We define the
commutator
of two expressions
a
and
b
as

[a,b]\;=\;(a+b)-(b+a)\;=\;a+b-a-b,

where the second equality uses the rule
-(b+a)=-a-b
.
The commutator thus
measures non-commutativity
:
it is the difference between the two possible orders of summing
a
and
b
.

Three key properties of the commutator follow immediately from this definition:

•

Antisymmetry:

-[a,b]=[b,a]
, because
-(a+b-a-b)=b+a-b-a
.

•

Vanishing condition:

[a,b]=0
iff
a+b=b+a
, that is, iff
a
and
b
commute under addition.
In the free group,
this happens exactly when
a
and
b
are both multiples of a common element
— in group theory, a fact about
centralizers
.

•

Special cases that vanish:

[0,b]=[a,0]=[a,a]=[a,-a]=0
,
and more generally
[pa,qa]=0
for any integers
p,q
.

We will use commutators recursively:
the arguments
a
and
b
may themselves be expressions built from
variables, sums, and commutators.
The vanishing condition then becomes a useful test, which we phrase as follows.
Say that two expressions
solve the same problem

when they vanish under exactly the same removals.
The inverse
-h
solves the same problem as
h
;
so does any nonzero multiple
p\,h
(that is,
h
repeated
p
times,
p\neq 0
),
since a nonzero multiple of a nonzero expression is again nonzero
(in group theory, the free group is
torsion-free
);
and so does any
conjugate

g+h-g
(a copy of
h
wrapped in
g
and
-g
),
since it too vanishes exactly when
h
does.
Hence two multiples of a common element solve the same problem.
Reading the vanishing conditions through this lens, for nonzero
a
and
b
:

a+b=0
forces
b=-a
, and
[a,b]=0
forces
a
and
b
to be common multiples
— in either case
a
and
b
solve the same problem.
Contrapositively, expressions that solve
different
problems
satisfy
a+b\neq 0
and
[a,b]\neq 0
;
in particular this holds when they have disjoint, nonempty supports.
This gives only non-vanishing; that
+
and
[,]
actually realize
\wedge
and
\vee

needs more, as we discuss in Section
3
.

Demaine’s and Wästlund’s conventions

Wästlund
[
7
]
adopts the opposite convention to Demaine:
his
k
-out-of-
n
means the picture
hangs
iff at least
k
nails remain.
The translation is:

\text{Demaine's $k$-out-of-$n$}\;=\;\text{W\"{a}stlund's $(n-k+1)$-out-of-$n$.}

Throughout this paper we use Demaine’s convention
unless we explicitly switch to Wästlund’s in Section
4
,
where it is structurally more convenient.

Three easy cases

Three cases of
k
-out-of-
n
admit short, optimal, and explicit solutions.

Proposition 1

(
k=n
)
.

The minimum length of a solution to the
n
-out-of-
n
puzzle is exactly
n
,
attained by
1+2+\cdots+n
.

Proof.

This expression is
0
iff all variables are set to
0
,
and remains nonzero under any proper subset of removals
(some surviving variable is left, and the reduced expression contains it).
Any solution must mention every variable, so length
\geq n
.
∎

Proposition 2

(
k=n-1
)
.

The minimum length of a solution to the
(n-1)
-out-of-
n
puzzle is exactly
2n
,
attained by

1+2+\cdots+n-1-2-\cdots-n.

Proof.

Let
w=1+2+\cdots+n-1-2-\cdots-n
.

Nonzero:

w
is freely reduced (no two adjacent terms are mutual inverses),
so
w\neq 0
.

Fall under
n-1
removals:
When
n-1
variables are removed,
exactly one variable
j
survives, and the expression reduces to
j-j=0
.

Hang under fewer removals:
When exactly
n-2
variables are removed,
the two survivors
a<b
give
a+b-a-b=[a,b]\neq 0
(since
a\neq b
,
they do not commute).
By monotonicity, the picture also hangs under any fewer removals.

Lower bound
2n
:
In any solution,
fixing variable
j
and setting all other
n-1
variables to
0

removes
n-1\geq k=n-1
variables, so the resulting expression must be
0
.
Hence the net exponent of
j
— its number of
+j
occurrences minus its number of
-j
—
is
0
in any solution, forcing
j
to appear at least twice.
Since this holds for every
j
, length
\geq 2n
.
∎

Proposition 3

(
k=1
)
.

The minimum length of a solution to the
1
-out-of-
n
puzzle is
\Theta(n^{2})
.
For
n
a power of two, length
n^{2}
is attained by the balanced commutator tree,
recursively defined by

h_{1,1}=1,\qquad h_{1,2n}=[h_{1,n}\text{ on }\{1,\ldots,n\},h_{1,n}\text{ on }\{n+1,\ldots,2n\}].

The lower bound
\Omega(n^{2})
is due to Gartside and Greenwood
[
2
]
;
the upper-bound construction goes back at least to
[
4
]
.

Sketch.

The recursion
L(2n)=4L(n)
with
L(1)=1
gives
L(2^{i})=4^{i}=(2^{i})^{2}
.
Correctness follows by induction:
a commutator
[A,B]
with
A,B
on disjoint variable sets vanishes
iff
A=0
or
B=0
,
and inductively each
h_{1,n}
vanishes iff some variable in its support is removed.
Hence
[h_{1,n}\text{-on-}L,h_{1,n}\text{-on-}R]
vanishes
iff some variable on either side is removed
— i.e., iff any of the
2n
variables is removed.
∎

The construction of Proposition
3
is, in fact,
the simplest case of Demaine-perspective binary splitting
(Section
3
):
for
k=1
, the disjunction at each split has just two terms
(“some variable removed in
L
” or “some variable removed in
R
”),
giving a 2-leaf commutator that is the balanced tree.

The next case in the co-rank family,
(n-2)
-out-of-
n
, is genuinely non-trivial;
it is the subject of Section
4
.

3
Demaine-perspective binary splitting

In this section we present Demaine-perspective binary splitting,
a deterministic and explicit divide-and-conquer construction for the
k
-out-of-
n
puzzle.
We first describe the construction for general
k
,
and then prove a closed-form length expression for the case
k=2
on powers of two.
The correctness of the construction,
for all
k
, is taken up in Section
3.3
.

3.1
The general construction

Demaine-perspective binary splitting builds a
k
-out-of-
n
solution from solutions of smaller puzzles.
Choose any split of the
n
nails into two nonempty parts:

L
of size
n_{1}
and
R
of size
n_{2}
, with
n=n_{1}+n_{2}
and
n_{1},n_{2}\geq 1
.
The construction is correct for
every
such split;
it is most efficient when
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
are as equal as possible,
and for the length analysis we will take
n_{1}=\lceil n/2\rceil
and
n_{2}=\lfloor n/2\rfloor
.
In Demaine’s convention the picture falls as soon as
k
nails are removed;
we classify a removal by how it splits across the two parts.
If
j
of the removed nails lie in
L
, the remaining removals lie in
R
,
and “at least
k
removed” becomes “at least
j
removed in
L
and at least
k-j
removed in
R
.”
Hence

\text{$k$-out-of-$n$ falls}\iff\bigvee_{j}\Bigl[(\text{$\geq j$ removed in $L$})\;\wedge\;(\text{$\geq k-j$ removed in $R$})\Bigr],

(2)

where
j
ranges over the feasible values
\max(0,\,k-n_{2})\leq j\leq\min(k,\,n_{1})

— those for which neither part is asked to lose more nails than it contains.

The two logical operations have direct algebraic counterparts,
provided the parts they combine have disjoint supports.

Lemma 4

(Disjoint combination)
.

Let
h_{1}
solve a specification
f_{1}
on a nail set
V_{1}
,
and
h_{2}
solve a specification
f_{2}
on a disjoint set
V_{2}
.
Then, over
V_{1}\cup V_{2}
,

h_{1}+h_{2}\ \text{ solves }\ f_{1}\wedge f_{2},\qquad[h_{1},h_{2}]\ \text{ solves }\ f_{1}\vee f_{2}.

The specifications are arbitrary:
the proof uses only the disjointness of the supports,
not any special structure of
f_{1}
or
f_{2}
.

Proof.

A removal
S
acts on each part separately
(in group theory it is a
homomorphism
, so it respects
+
and
[,]
):

(h_{1}+h_{2})|_{S}=h_{1}|_{S}+h_{2}|_{S}
and
[h_{1},h_{2}]|_{S}=[h_{1}|_{S},h_{2}|_{S}]
,
with
h_{i}|_{S}
supported in
V_{i}
.

Sum:

h_{1}|_{S}+h_{2}|_{S}=0
would force
h_{2}|_{S}=-h_{1}|_{S}
,
an element common to
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
, hence
0

(in group theory, the subexpressions on
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
lie in
free factors
, meeting only in
0
);
so the sum vanishes iff
h_{1}|_{S}=h_{2}|_{S}=0
,
that is, iff
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
both fall at
S
;
hence
h_{1}+h_{2}
solves
f_{1}\wedge f_{2}
.

Commutator:
if either part vanishes, so does the commutator;
if both are nonzero, they have disjoint nonempty supports
and hence are not multiples of a common element,
so they do not commute and
[h_{1}|_{S},h_{2}|_{S}]\neq 0
.
Thus the commutator vanishes iff
f_{1}
or
f_{2}
falls at
S
;
hence
[h_{1},h_{2}]
solves
f_{1}\vee f_{2}
.
∎

The disjointness hypothesis is essential: without it, both statements can fail.
For the sum, overlapping supports may cancel,
so that
h_{1}+h_{2}
vanishes although neither part does.
The commutator is subtler.
Consider the puzzle that falls exactly when nail
3
is removed together with nail
1
or nail
2
,
the condition
(1\vee 2)\wedge 3
.
A correct solution is
[1,2]+3
:
the disjunction
1\vee 2
on the disjoint support
\{1,2\}
, conjoined with
3
.
Distributing
\wedge
over
\vee
rewrites the condition as
(1\wedge 3)\vee(2\wedge 3)
,
inviting the “solution”
[1+3,\,2+3]
.
But its arguments share nail
3
:
removing
1
and
2
while leaving
3
sends
1+3\mapsto 3
and
2+3\mapsto 3
,
so the commutator collapses to
[3,3]=0
and the picture wrongly falls.
The shared nail has made the two arguments multiples of a common element,
exactly where the commutator vanishes.
The disjuncts of our construction overlap in just this way,
so Lemma
4
does not cover the commutator tree that combines them;
that case is the substance of the correctness proof (Section
3.3
).

The
j
-th disjunct is then the subexpression

D_{j}=\begin{cases}h_{k,R},&j=0,\\[2.0pt]
h_{j,L}+h_{(k-j),R},&0<j<k,\\[2.0pt]
h_{k,L},&j=k,\end{cases}

where
h_{k,V}
denotes any solution of the specification
k\text{-of-}V
;
the cases
j=0

Huffman placement.

A commutator tree over the disjuncts may take many shapes,
all realizing the same disjunction but with different total lengths.
Since each commutator
[A,B]=A+B-A-B
duplicates both of its arguments,
a leaf placed at depth

times its own length to the total.
Minimizing the total length is therefore a Huffman problem on the leaf lengths

:
one repeatedly combines the two shortest current subexpressions into a commutator,
which places the longest leaves nearest the root.
We use Huffman placement at every split.

Correctness and length.

Two points remain.
First, that the construction actually solves the
k
-out-of-
n
puzzle.
Each conjunction here — a cross term

on the disjoint halves

The commutator tree is not: its leaves, the disjuncts, share supports

and

so Lemma

Second,

We solve this recurrence in closed form for the case

We show that the construction of Section

The one step still in doubt

We isolate the exact obstruction,

Both algebraic combiners can vanish spuriously when supports overlap.
A removal acts term by term, so

Lemma 5

(Combination)
.

Let

Proof.

Each combiner has an automatic direction:

while if

For the converses, suppose a combiner vanishes where its target hangs.
The sum’s target hangs where some

The commutator’s target hangs only where both

The construction nests many commutators, not one.

for a whole tree the next theorem makes the criterion exact, and milder:

Theorem 6

(Commutator trees as disjunctions)
.

Let

be a commutator tree whose leaves solve specifications

, and let

.
Then

solves

if and only if no internal node of

vanishes
at an

where
f
hangs.
In particular,

solves
f
if at every such

the specifications solved by the two subtrees at each internal node
separate above it.

Proof.

The minterms of

are exactly those of the

, each realized by a leaf.
If

falls at

, some leaf vanishes; a commutator with a vanishing argument
vanishes, and this propagates to the root, so

whatever the
internal nodes do.
Hence

solves

precisely when, in addition,
T|_{S}\neq 0
wherever

A vanishing internal node propagates to the root,
and a nonvanishing root is itself the topmost internal node,

at an

For the sufficient condition: at an

so every leaf hangs and, inductively, the two subtrees at each node are nonzero;
if their specifications separate above

they cannot commute

∎

There is no counterpart of Theorem

for sums.
A commutator tree falls as soon as any one leaf is covered,

An overlapping sum must therefore avoid cancellation

For the construction, fix the split

Lemma 7

(Staircase separation)

Let

Proof.

Parametrize the supersets

3.4
Recurrence and closed form

Let
L_{2}(n)
denote the length of the construction’s word for
2
-out-of-
n
on

nails,
counted before any free reduction at the commutator junctions, with

as above.
Because reduction can only shorten the word,

is an upper bound on the length of the solution actually produced
(see the remark after Theorem
10
).
The three disjunction subexpressions are:

•

“both removed in
L
”
— a
2
-out-of-
m
subproblem on
L
, of length
L_{2}(m)
;

•

“both removed in

”
— a
2
-out-of-
m
subproblem on

, of length

;

•

the cross term “one in each”
— the sum of a

-out-of-
m
solution on

and a
1
-out-of-
m
solution on

,
of length
2\,L_{1}(m)=2m^{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}n^{2}
by Proposition

.

These three subexpressions are the leaves of a commutator tree.
Recall from Section
3.1
that a leaf at depth

contributes

times its length.
With three leaves there is one leaf at depth
1
and two at depth
2
,
so the total length is

,
where

is the sum of the three leaf lengths and

is the length of the leaf at the root.
The length is minimized by placing the

leaf at the root — the Huffman rule —
equivalently merging the two shortest leaves into the depth-

commutator.

Which leaf is longest depends on

.
At

(so

) the cross term, of length

,
is longer than each same-half subproblem (

),
so it sits at the root and gives the base value

L_{2}(4)\;=\;2\cdot 8+4\cdot 2+4\cdot 2\;=\;32.

For every

the cross term is instead one of the two

so the optimal tree merges it with one same-half subproblem at depth

and
places the other same-half subproblem at the root:

L_{2}(n)\;=\;\underbrace{2\,L_{2}(m)}_{\text{root}}\;+\;\underbrace{4\,L_{2}(m)+4\cdot 2m^{2}}_{\text{depth }2}\;=\;6\,L_{2}(m)+8m^{2}\;=\;6\,L_{2}(n/2)+2n^{2}.

That the cross term really is among the two shortest leaves for

amounts to

for

, which follows by induction:
it holds with equality at

(

),
and the recurrence propagates it,
since

.

With base

and this recurrence for

,
we obtain the following.

Theorem 10
.

For

with

,
the Demaine-perspective binary-splitting construction with Huffman commutator trees
produces a solution to the

-out-of-

L_{2}(n)\;=\;\tfrac{8}{3}\,n^{\log_{2}6}-4\,n^{2}.

(6)

In particular, its length is

Proof.

The recurrence
L_{2}(n)=6\,L_{2}(n/2)+2n^{2}

is solved by elementary means.
Setting

\ell_{i}\;=\;6\,\ell_{i-1}+2\cdot 4^{i}.

The homogeneous solution is

.
For a particular solution, try

B\cdot 4^{i}\;=\;6B\cdot 4^{i-1}+2\cdot 4^{i}\;=\;\tfrac{3B}{2}\cdot 4^{i}+2\cdot 4^{i},

The general solution is

The base

Substituting

yielding the stated closed form for

The constructed solution is therefore of length at most this value.
∎

Remark 12

(Cancellations)
.

Because

counts symbols before free reduction,
it is only an upper bound on the length of the solution produced.
The three disjuncts have overlapping supports
— the cross term shares variables with both same-half subproblems —
so free reductions can occur at the commutator junctions and shorten the word
(at

rather than

Reordering the disjuncts in the commutator tree,
or replacing a subproblem solution by an equivalent one
under the symmetries of Section

opens further cancellations;
the gains affect the constants but not the exponent

.
By contrast, Wästlund’s binary construction (Section
4
)
keeps disjoint supports throughout,
so no free reduction ever occurs and its length is exact.

\ell_{i}=2\ell_{i-1}+6\cdot 2^{i}
.
The homogeneous solution is
A\cdot 2^{i}
; since
f(n)=6n
matches the homogeneous form,
the particular solution has the form

so

.
The general solution is
\ell_{i}=A\cdot 2^{i}+6i\cdot 2^{i}
.
The base

gives

, so

.
Hence

\ell_{i}\;=\;(6i-6)\cdot 2^{i}\;=\;6(i-1)\cdot 2^{i}.

With

Correctness of the construction

since the construction is his AND-of-ORs structure with disjoint-support discipline at every junction.

5.1
The recursion

We construct

Proposition 15
.

Let

solve the

-out-of-
(n-1)

and

Proof.

Write
A=h_{k,n-1}
and

,
so
h_{k,n}=A+n+B-A-n
,
and let
\ell
count the removed nails in
\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
.
Removal acts on each part separately, so

h_{k,n}|_{S}=A|_{S}+n|_{S}+B|_{S}-A|_{S}-n|_{S}
,
where
n|_{S}
is
n
if nail

survives and
0
if it is removed.
Since
A
solves
k
-out-of-
(n-1)
and
B

(k-1)

(n-1)

A|_{S}=0\iff\ell\geq k,\qquad B|_{S}=0\iff\ell\geq k-1.

By the essential-removals reduction (Section
2
)
it suffices to check that every removal of
k
nails makes
h_{k,n}
fall,
and every removal of
k-1
nails leaves it hanging.

Fall,
|S|=k
.

If nail
n
survives then
\ell=k
, so

and

.
If nail

is removed then

, so

and
h_{k,n}|_{S}=A|_{S}-A|_{S}=0
.

Hang,

If nail
n
survives then
\ell=k-1

and
B|_{S}=0

leaving the commutator

since

are disjoint.
If nail
n
is removed then
\ell=k-2

,
leaving

a conjugate solves the same problem (Section

), so it is nonzero.
This last case is the only essential removal in which the non-disjoint sum

survives;
the surrounding conjugation makes it harmless.
∎

The recursion doubles

n

6
Exhaustive search and exact minima

Parity

In any solution to the
k
-out-of-
n
puzzle with
k<n
,
every variable has net exponent zero.
Setting all variables except
j
to
0
removes
n-1\geq k
of them,
so the resulting expression must vanish;
hence the net exponent of
j
in any solution is zero.
Since each variable appears with net exponent zero,
it appears an even number of times,
so the total length is even.
Odd lengths are eliminated without search.

Symmetry quotienting

The search canonicalizes each candidate word by four symmetry classes:

(S1)

Nail relabeling
(
S_{n}
):
nails are renamed so that their labels appear in order of first occurrence (
1,2,3,\ldots
).

(S2)

Per-nail sign flip
(
(\mathbb{Z}_{2})^{n}
):
each nail’s first occurrence is made positive.

(S3)

Reversal
:
reading
w
backward gives an equivalent solution;
together with the sign flips of (S2) this is the wire inversion
w\mapsto-w
.

(S4)

Cyclic rotation
:
any cyclic rotation of
w
gives an equivalent solution.

A word is
canonical
if it is
the lexicographically smallest
(\text{S1}+\text{S2})
-normalized form
among all its cyclic rotations and the cyclic rotations of its reversal.
Each equivalence class contains exactly one canonical representative,
substantially reducing the search space.

7
Workshop realizations: carabiners and Whitehead links

For workshop purposes,
the constructions are realized on a wooden board fitted with carabiners rather than nails
(see Figure
4
).
A carabiner physically prevents the wire from slipping off,
which is essential when participants handle a partially completed construction:
the picture should fall only when the relevant carabiners are deliberately “removed”
(opened, and the wire pulled through).
However, this convenience introduces an ambiguity that does not arise with vertical nails under gravity.

Figure 4:
Hanging
1+2-1
on board with two carabiners.

With nails and gravity,
the wire’s vertical hierarchy at each crossing is fixed by physics:
a later strand naturally falls in front of
(or behind, depending on how the wire is paid out) an earlier one.
With carabiners on a flat board, the wire’s over/under choice at each crossing is a free parameter,
and incorrect choices produce topologically different links.

The simplest failure: a Whitehead link

Consider the standard
1
-out-of-
2
solution
1+2-1-2
.
If the second factor
-2
is wired with the wrong over/under choice relative to the preceding strands,
the resulting wiring no longer represents the commutator in
F_{2}

but instead realizes a Whitehead link with one of the carabiners
(see Figure
5
).
Removing the other nail then leaves the wire genuinely linked around the remaining carabiner
rather than free to fall.

Figure 5:
Left: correct hanging
1+2-1-2
,
in which the strand for
-2
passes consistently above the previous strands.
Middle: incorrect over/under choice for
-2
.
Right: removing nail
1
produces a Whitehead link, which does not fall.

This failure mode was observed during the workshop.
Beyond its pedagogical value
— participants quickly internalize that the algebra dictates the topology
only if the geometric realization is consistent —
it is also a reminder that the standard picture-hanging analysis
implicitly assumes a particular planar projection or a gravitational hierarchy.
In the absence of such a constraint,
the same word
w\in F_{n}
can correspond to multiple non-equivalent wirings.

8
Open questions

Several questions are left open.

First, what is the exact length of the general Demaine-perspective binary-splitting construction (Section
3
)
for fixed
k\geq 3
?
(The construction is correct for every
k
by Theorem
8
;
only its length is at issue.)
For each fixed
k
, Huffman placement yields a divide-and-conquer recurrence
L_{k}(n)=c_{k}\,L_{k}(n/2)+(\text{lower-order terms})
.
The recursive coefficient
c_{k}
is governed not by the number of disjuncts (
k+1
)
but only by the two disjuncts that are themselves
k
-out-of-
(n/2)
subproblems,
namely the
j=0
and
j=k
terms:
Huffman placement keeps these two nearest the root
while the remaining, lower-order disjuncts are pushed deeper.
Computation indicates that
c_{k}=6
for
every

k\geq 2

(a balanced tree would instead give the larger coefficient
\approx 2(k+1)
),
so the polynomial exponent saturates at
\log_{2}6
rather than growing with
k
.
The lower-order terms, however, appear to carry polylogarithmic factors in
n

whose power grows with
k
; pinning down this exact asymptotic is left open.

Second, what are the exact minima for the smallest open cases beyond
2
-out-of-
4
?
The exhaustive search techniques of Section
6
reach lengths up to about
16
in pure Python;
the next case,
3
-out-of-
5

(equivalently,
(n-2)
-out-of-
n
at
n=5
, the first non-trivial case in the co-rank family for odd
n
),
should be within reach with a more efficient implementation.

Third, the leading constant
\tfrac{8}{3}
in Theorem
10

and the constant
6
in Theorem
14
have not been given structural derivations.
They emerge from the recurrence solutions;
their cleanliness suggests they should have direct combinatorial interpretations in the construction.

Fourth, the relationship between the polynomial construction
(Theorem
10
, whose bound is
L_{2}(4)=32
)
and the exhaustive-search optimum (
16
for
2
-out-of-
4
, Section
6
)
is asymptotically obscure.
The optimum at
n=4
lies well below the construction, even after cancellations;
whether this gap persists, shrinks, or grows with
n
is unknown.
Computational evidence at
n=5,6,7,8
would clarify this,
but pushing exhaustive search to those sizes is currently infeasible.

Fifth, the workshop-motivated question (Section
7
):
can the over/under choice at each crossing be incorporated into the algebraic formalism,
perhaps via braid groups,
in a way that distinguishes the intended commutator from the Whitehead link?
This is more an exposition than a research question,
but a clean treatment would benefit pedagogy.

Sixth, is there a “puncturing” or “shortening” operation
— by analogy with coding theory — that lowers
n
at fixed
k
?
Removing a nail is the easy move in the wrong direction:
setting nail
n+1
to
0
in a
k
-out-of-
(n+1)
solution yields a
(k-1)
-out-of-
n
solution,
lowering the threshold along with the nail count.
What is missing is an operation that removes a nail while
preserving
the threshold
k
,
turning a
k
-out-of-
(n+1)
solution into a
k
-out-of-
n
one.
Naively deleting a nail’s letters does not preserve the puzzle property;
a non-trivial reduction of this kind would supply a recursion
that decreases
n
by one at fixed
k
,
complementing the halving recursion of Section
3

and possibly narrowing the construction-versus-search gap above.
